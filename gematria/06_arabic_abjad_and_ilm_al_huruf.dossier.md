# The Arabic abjad and ilm al-huruf: Research Dossier

## Basic identification

**Primary name:** Arabic **abjad numerals**, **ḥisāb al-jummal** or **ḥisāb al-jumal** (حساب الجُمَّل, “calculation of the total”), and, more broadly, the **science of letters**, **ʿilm al-ḥurūf** (علم الحروف).

**Script:** Arabic and the Persianate scripts derived from it.

**Core period:** Alphabetic number notation is securely documented in early Islamic Arabic manuscripts by the early eighth century CE; letter-number speculation appears in early Abbasid alchemy and philosophy; mature lettrism flourished from the tenth through fifteenth centuries and continued thereafter.

**Regions:** Arabia, Iraq, Syria, Egypt, the Maghrib and al-Andalus; later Iran, Central Asia, Ottoman Anatolia and the Balkans, South Asia, West and East Africa, and Muslim Southeast Asia.

**System type:** A decimal alphabetic numeral system and alphanumeric code. Each of the 28 Arabic letters has a fixed value. Written words can therefore be read as numbers, and numbers can be encoded as letters or meaningful words.

### Evidence labels used below

- **[TEXT]** Directly documented in a surviving text, manuscript, inscription, or artifact.
- **[RECONSTRUCTION]** A modern scholarly historical inference.
- **[TRADITION]** A claim transmitted within a religious, literary, or occult lineage.
- **[DISPUTED]** Supported by some scholars or practitioners but seriously contested.
- **[LEGEND]** A premodern narrative presented without adequate historical evidence.
- **[MODERN CLAIM]** A modern innovation, retrospective interpretation, or internet-era construction.

Johannes Thomann’s manuscript-based reconstruction revises the usual terminology:

- **[OLDER TEXTBOOK ACCOUNT]:** the Eastern system arose about the mid-seventh century, while the Western system was a later Maghrebi variation.
- **[RECONSTRUCTION]:** the “Western” system is actually the older or **archaic** order. It appears in early Qurʾān verse-numbering and reflects an Arabic alphabetic tradition older than the Abbasid scientific translations.
- The Eastern system is better termed **scientific**, because its high-value sequence was regularized in the ninth-century translation culture of Greek and Syriac astronomy.
- “Western” remains a useful regional label for its later survival in the Maghrib and al-Andalus, but it is misleading as an account of origin.

The six divergences are documented in Thomann’s comparative table. Early Andalusian astrolabes use ṣād = 60, ḍād = 90, and sīn = 300; examples include instruments made at Córdoba in 1026 and 1029. [Johannes Thomann, “Scientific and Archaic Arabic Numerals”](https://www.sciamvs.org/files/SCIAMVS_19_167-200_Thomann.pdf).

### Persian, Ottoman, and Urdu additions

Persian adds **پ چ ژ گ**; Ottoman Turkish and Urdu add further signs. These do not belong to the inherited Arabic 28-letter table.

Two practices coexist:

1. **[DOCUMENTED STANDARD]:** ignore letters not in the Arabic 28 and calculate only their Arabic base or conventional equivalent.
2. **[DOCUMENTED VARIANTS]:** assimilate them to a cognate Arabic form:

   - پ → ب = 2
   - چ → ج = 3
   - ژ → ز = 7
   - گ → ك = 20

Encyclopaedia Iranica’s concise account says that the four additional Persian letters have no independent values. Hurufism, by contrast, treated the 32-letter Persian alphabet as cosmologically significant; that is not merely a computational extension but a doctrinal reorganization. [Iranica, “Abjad”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/abjad/), [Iranica, “Horufism”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/horufism/).

# The system in detail

## 1. Ordinary additive calculation

For a word \(L_1L_2\ldots L_n\):

\[
V(\text{word})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}V(L_i)
\]

For a phrase, spaces and punctuation are ignored, and the word totals are added.

### Example: الله, *Allāh*

\[
ا(1)+ل(30)+ل(30)+ه(5)=66
\]

### Example: محمد, *Muḥammad*

\[
م(40)+ح(8)+م(40)+د(4)=92
\]

### Example: علي, *ʿAlī*

\[
ع(70)+ل(30)+ي(10)=110
\]

### Example: علم, *ʿilm*, “knowledge/science”

\[
ع(70)+ل(30)+م(40)=140
\]

This ordinary sum is variously called:

- **ḥisāb al-jummal / al-jumal** — حساب الجمل
- **ḥisāb al-abjad** — حساب الأبجد
- **al-jummal al-kabīr**, “the great total”
- Persian **ḥesāb-e jommal**
- Ottoman Turkish **ebced hesabı**

## 2. Written letters, not spoken phonemes

The safest general rule is: **calculate the letters present in the adopted spelling**. But different manuals interpret exceptional signs differently; there is no single universal convention covering every period and region.

### Short vowels and recitation marks

The following normally have no independent value:

- fatḥa َ
- ḍamma ُ
- kasra ِ
- sukūn ْ
- tanwīn ً ٌ ٍ
- Qurʾānic recitation marks
- punctuation
- spaces

Long vowels written as letters do count:

- ا = 1
- و = 6
- ي = 10

Thus the system is not purely consonantal: *mater lectionis* letters count because they are graphically present.

### Shadda

A shadda marks pronunciation of a doubled consonant but is not normally counted as a second written letter.

Consequently, the written spelling الله contains two lām letters and is 66; it is not necessary to add another lām merely because of pronunciation. Some operative magical manuals count a shadda as doubling in particular procedures. Such a result must be labeled as a variant rather than “the” abjad value.

### Hamza

Hamza is troublesome because it was not one of the inherited 28 alphabetic numeral signs.

Documented conventions include:

- ignore isolated hamza;
- treat a hamza seated on alif as the alif already present, value 1;
- treat every hamza form as alif = 1;
- count the carrier—ا, و, or ي—according to its shape;
- in some Urdu chronogram manuals, exclude hamza because it is absent from the mnemonic alphabet.

A result involving ء أ إ آ ؤ ئ is therefore not reproducible unless the calculator states its convention.

### Tāʾ marbūṭa

Two principal conventions occur:

- **by graphic form:** ة → ه = 5;
- **by grammatical/etymological value:** ة → ت = 400.

Modern calculators commonly use 5, so:

\[
فاطمة = ف(80)+ا(1)+ط(9)+م(40)+ة/ه(5)=135
\]

A chronogram poet may intentionally exploit the alternative 400 reading.

### Alif maqṣūra

ى may be counted as:

- ي = 10, by graphic form;
- ا = 1, by phonetic/orthographic interpretation.

Again, the source’s stated method controls.

### Lām–alif

The ligature لا represents two letters:

\[
ل(30)+ا(1)=31
\]

It is not a special numeral.

### Persianate letters

No independent ancient values exist for پ چ ژ گ. A calculator that assigns them arbitrary new hundreds or thousands is a later convention unless it identifies a premodern source.

## 3. No automatic reduction

Classical abjad calculation ordinarily preserves the full sum. It does not automatically reduce 786 to \(7+8+6=21\), or then to 3.

Digit reduction belongs to such later or specialized procedures as:

- **ḥisāb al-jummal al-ṣaghīr**, “small calculation”;
- reduction by nines;
- casting out nines;
- extracting a digital root;
- division with remainders for astrological, divinatory, or onomantic tables.

These are related arts, not the default rule.

For example:

\[
786 \rightarrow 7+8+6=21 \rightarrow 2+1=3
\]

That reduced 3 is not interchangeable with the standard abjad value 786 unless a source explicitly prescribes the reduction.

## 4. Equivalence

Two expressions are numerically equivalent when they yield the same total under the same:

- alphabetic table;
- spelling;
- treatment of hamza, shadda, tāʾ marbūṭa, and foreign letters;
- inclusion or exclusion of articles and honorifics;
- reduction rule.

Equality of sums does not, by itself, establish historical connection or authorial intention.

## 5. Common named procedures

### Ḥisāb al-jummal

Ordinary total of written letters.

### Kabīr and ṣaghīr

“Great” and “small” calculation. Terminology varies:

- *kabīr* generally means unreduced conventional values;
- *ṣaghīr* may mean values reduced modulo 9, ordinal values, or another abbreviated series.

A table must be supplied before a “small abjad” result is reproducible.

### Mīzān al-ḥurūf

**ميزان الحروف**, “balance of the letters.” In the Jabirian corpus this is not merely adding conventional abjad values. Letters are distributed among four elemental qualities and seven degrees, so the name of a substance discloses its balance of heat, cold, dryness, and moisture.

### Taksīr al-ḥurūf

**تكسير الحروف**, literally “breaking” or permutation of letters. A word, divine name, or verse is decomposed and recombined in prescribed arrays. It resembles anagrammatic procedures but may include numerical and cosmological correspondences.

### Basṭ

**بسط**, expansion. A letter can be expanded into the spelling of its name:

- م becomes ميم.
- Its ordinary value is 40.
- Expanded:

\[
م(40)+ي(10)+م(40)=90
\]

Further recursive expansions are possible.

### Taḍāʿuf and multiplication

Values may be doubled, multiplied, or distributed through cosmological series. These are operative lettrist methods, not basic abjad arithmetic.

### Taṭbīq

**تطبيق**, “correspondence” or matching: relating words, names, Qurʾānic passages, cosmological levels, persons, dates, planets, or events.

### Sīmiyāʾ

A broad category of letter magic and operative manipulation of signs. Its boundaries with ʿilm al-ḥurūf, divine-name magic, talismanry, and jafr shift between authors.

### Jafr

A prophetic or revelatory letter-science, especially in Shiʿi tradition, applied to scripture, transmitted sayings, the names of rulers, and future events.

### Zāʾiraja

A divinatory board or rotating diagram that combines letters, numbers, astrological positions, and questions to generate answers. Ibn Khaldūn describes and criticizes such procedures.

### Mādda-ye tārīkh / tārīkh düşürme

The chronogram: a word, phrase, or verse whose unreduced abjad value gives a Hijri year.

## Worked Arabic examples

## The basmala: 786

The normal unvocalized formula is:

> **بسم الله الرحمن الرحيم**  
> *bi-smi llāhi l-raḥmāni l-raḥīm*  
> “In the name of God, the Compassionate, the Merciful.”

Calculation:

### بسم

\[
ب(2)+س(60)+م(40)=102
\]

### الله

\[
ا(1)+ل(30)+ل(30)+ه(5)=66
\]

### الرحمن

\[
ا(1)+ل(30)+ر(200)+ح(8)+م(40)+ن(50)=329
\]

### الرحيم

\[
ا(1)+ل(30)+ر(200)+ح(8)+ي(10)+م(40)=289
\]

Total:

\[
102+66+329+289=786
\]

Or continuously:

\[
2+60+40+1+30+30+5+1+30+200+8+40+50+1+30+200+8+10+40=786
\]

**Status:**

- **[TEXTUAL ARITHMETIC]:** The sum is correct under the Eastern table and normal unvocalized spelling.
- **[TRADITION]:** 786 became a substitute or protective numerical signature for the basmala, especially in Persianate and South Asian correspondence and popular devotion.
- **[ABSENCE OF EVIDENCE]:** 786 itself does not occur in the Qurʾān or canonical hadith as a divinely assigned number for the formula.
- **[DISPUTED RELIGIOUS PRACTICE]:** Some jurists permit it as an abbreviation; others object that a number is not the sacred formula and should not replace it.

[Farooqi, “The Secret of Letters: Chronograms in Urdu”](https://www.columbia.edu/itc/mealac/pritchett/00urduhindilinks/txt_abjad_farooqi.pdf); the arithmetic is also independently reproducible from the standard table.

## The number 19 in the basmala

Under the ordinary modern spelling the formula contains nineteen written letters:

\[
ب\,س\,م\ |\ ا\,ل\,ل\,ه\ |\ ا\,ل\,ر\,ح\,م\,ن\ |\ ا\,ل\,ر\,ح\,ي\,م
\]

Counts:

\[
3+4+6+6=19
\]

This is a count of graphemes, not an abjad sum. It later became foundational to Rashad Khalifa’s “Qurʾān Code 19.”

## Wāḥid, “one”

\[
واحد = و(6)+ا(1)+ح(8)+د(4)=19
\]

## Wujūd, “existence”

\[
وجود = و(6)+ج(3)+و(6)+د(4)=19
\]

The equality \(واحد = وجود = 19\) became significant in Babi and Bahaʾi numerical symbolism. It is a real arithmetic equality; the metaphysical implication is **[TRADITION]**, not a result of the arithmetic itself. [Iranica, “Jafr”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/jafr/).

## “A clear victory”: 592

Ibn ʿArabī’s biographical tradition connects Qurʾān 48:1, **فتحاً مبيناً**, “a clear victory,” with the Almohad victory at Alarcos in AH 592/1195–96.

Ignoring vocalization and tanwīn:

\[
فتحا = ف(80)+ت(400)+ح(8)+ا(1)=489
\]

\[
مبينا = م(40)+ب(2)+ي(10)+ن(50)+ا(1)=103
\]

\[
489+103=592
\]

**[TEXT/TRADITION]:** Reported in Ibn ʿArabī’s materials as a prospective or revelatory correspondence.  
**[CAUTION]:** Its evidentiary value depends on the date and textual transmission of the report; a chronogram recorded after an event may be retrospective. [Iranica, “Jafr”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/jafr/).

## “Refuge”: AH 736

The Persian/Arabic word **لوذ**, *lawdh*, “refuge,” was associated with AH 736, Timur’s birth year and the death year of the Ilkhan Abū Saʿīd:

\[
ل(30)+و(6)+ذ(700)=736
\]

## “Smoke came up from Khorasan”: AH 898

A chronogram for Jāmī’s death subtracts “smoke,” **دود**, from Khorasan:

\[
دود = د(4)+و(6)+د(4)=14
\]

\[
خراسان = خ(600)+ر(200)+ا(1)+س(60)+ا(1)+ن(50)=912
\]

\[
912-14=898
\]

AH 898 corresponds to 1492–93 CE. This is a deliberately signaled subtractive chronogram, not ordinary additive abjad.

## “Dust of Muṣallā”: AH 791

The traditional date-phrase for Hafez is **خاک مصلی**, *khāk-e Muṣallā*, “the dust of Muṣallā.”

Using kāf for Persian گ-like forms only where the spelling requires, the conventional phrase is reported as totaling 791, Hafez’s death year. The example illustrates an important warning: published spellings and Persian orthographic normalization must be checked against the inscription or manuscript; small spelling changes alter the sum. [Iranica, “Chronograms”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/chronograms-pers/).

## “King Homāyun fell from the roof”: AH 962

> **همایون پادشاه از بام افتاد**

The component totals reported by the source are:

\[
112+313+8+43+486=962
\]

This gives AH 962/1555, the year Humāyūn died after falling down the steps of his library. [Iranica, “Mādda Tārīkh”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/madda-tarik-chronogram/).

## Khayr, “good”: AH 810

For an architectural inscription:

\[
خير = خ(600)+ي(10)+ر(200)=810
\]

The word supplies AH 810/1407–08. [Oxford study of Persian architectural inscriptions](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid%3A4250c2e5-4e27-43c2-b604-80ff8d60e082/files/m7c74b0cc492901d25f292ffbe732975d).

## Comparative calibration examples

These examples belong to Hebrew or Greek systems, not Arabic abjad. They demonstrate the wider family of alphabetic numeration on which Arabic practice should be comparatively situated.

### Eliezer = 318

Hebrew **אליעזר**:

\[
א(1)+ל(30)+י(10)+ע(70)+ז(7)+ר(200)=318
\]

**[TEXT]:** Babylonian Talmud, *Nedarim* 32a, says one interpretation of Abraham’s 318 retainers is that only Eliezer accompanied him, because Eliezer’s name totals 318. Rashi repeats this at Genesis 14:14. [Sefaria, *Nedarim* 32a:14](https://www.sefaria.org/Nedarim.32a.14?with=Talmud).

### The Tetragrammaton = 26

\[
יהוה = י(10)+ה(5)+ו(6)+ה(5)=26
\]

### Ahavah = eḥad = 13

\[
אהבה = א(1)+ה(5)+ב(2)+ה(5)=13
\]

\[
אחד = א(1)+ח(8)+ד(4)=13
\]

### Naḥash = mashiaḥ = 358

\[
נחש = נ(50)+ח(8)+ש(300)=358
\]

\[
משיח = מ(40)+ש(300)+י(10)+ח(8)=358
\]

The arithmetic is exact. The theological conclusion that the Messiah transforms or defeats the serpent belongs to later kabbalistic interpretation.

### Jesus = 888

Greek **Ἰησοῦς**:

\[
Ι(10)+Η(8)+Σ(200)+Ο(70)+Υ(400)+Σ(200)=888
\]

### Dove = 801

Greek **περιστερά**:

\[
Π(80)+Ε(5)+Ρ(100)+Ι(10)+Σ(200)+Τ(300)+Ε(5)+Ρ(100)+Α(1)=801
\]

These equations are reported amid the Marcosian numerology attacked by Irenaeus in *Against Heresies* I.14–16. They document second-century Christian-Gnostic use; Irenaeus transmits them polemically and rejects their reasoning. [Irenaeus, *Against Heresies* I.16](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/0103116.htm).

### Nero Caesar = 666

Hebrew/Aramaic spelling **נרון קסר**, *Nerōn Qesar*:

\[
נ(50)+ר(200)+ו(6)+ן(50)+ק(100)+ס(60)+ר(200)=666
\]

Dropping the final n of the Greek form gives the Latinized **נרו קסר**:

\[
50+200+6+100+60+200=616
\]

616 is an ancient textual variant in Revelation 13:18. The double fit makes Nero an influential modern scholarly solution, but it is not proved beyond dispute. The first clearly traced publication of this exact solution appears in 1831, and critics note that the required spelling of *Caesar* is not the usual rabbinic קיסר. [Oxford, “Concealed Claudian”](https://academic.oup.com/jts/article/76/1/109/8043235), [De Gruyter, “Forward and Reverse Gematria”](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1515/opth-2022-0245/html?lang=en).

### Pompeii: “I love her whose number is 545”

> **φιλῶ ἧς ἀριθμὸς φμεʹ**

The final letters are Greek numeral notation:

\[
\phi(500)+mu(40)+epsilon(5)=545
\]

**[TEXT]:** CIL IV.4861, before Pompeii’s destruction in 79 CE. The woman’s name is not supplied, so the number cannot now be reversed uniquely. [JTS study citing CIL IV.4861](https://watermark02.silverchair.com/flaf015.pdf).

### Sargon II and the wall of Dūr-Šarrukīn

Sargon’s cylinder inscription says the wall measured 16,280 cubits, “the reading” or “numeral” of his name:

\[
4(3600)+3(600)+60+3(6)+2=16{,}280
\]

**[TEXT]:** The numerical assertion is genuinely in the royal inscription, connected with construction of Dūr-Šarrukīn in the late eighth century BCE.

**[DISPUTED INTERPRETATION]:** It is often called the earliest gematria, but no agreed letter-by-letter cipher reconstructs 16,280 from Šarru-kīn. It is evidence for a name-number conceit, not demonstrably for the later alphabetic value system. [RINAP, *Royal Inscriptions of Sargon II*](https://build-oracc.museum.upenn.edu/rinap/downloads/Frame_RINAP_2.pdf), [Israel Museum translation](https://museum.imj.org.il/journal/archive/2018-2019/pdf/cylinder.pdf).

### Agrippa’s Latin table

In *De occulta philosophia* II.20 (complete edition, Cologne, 1533), Agrippa arranged the 23-letter Latin alphabet in decimal classes:

- A–I = 1–9;
- K–S = 10–90;
- T–Z = 100–500, with the period’s Latin alphabet lacking separate J, U, and W.

This is Renaissance adaptation, not an ancient Roman system. Agrippa also printed Greek and Hebrew tables and numerical divination procedures. [Internet Archive/Open Library edition](https://openlibrary.org/books/OL6529841M/Three_books_of_occult_philosophy_or_magic), [1651 English text](https://www.alchemy-texts.com/book/three-books-of-occult-philosophy/).

# Origins: a dated reconstruction

## Before Arabic abjad

### Fourteenth–thirteenth centuries BCE: Ugaritic order

**[TEXT]:** Ugaritic alphabet exercises preserve a consonantal order related to later Northwest Semitic alphabets.

**[ABSENCE OF EVIDENCE]:** Ugaritic letters were not functioning as the later decimal alphabetic numerals; separate number signs were used. Similarity of alphabetic order is not evidence of gematria. [Thomann](https://www.sciamvs.org/files/SCIAMVS_19_167-200_Thomann.pdf).

### Eighth century BCE: Sargon II

As above, Sargon’s wall/name equation is a genuine number-name statement but its code is unrecovered. It does not establish the Arabic, Hebrew, or Greek letter tables.

### Sixth century BCE onward: Greek alphabetic numerals

**[RECONSTRUCTION SUPPORTED BY EPIGRAPHY]:** Greek speakers began employing alphabetic numerals during the sixth century BCE. The developed Milesian system used nine units, nine tens, and nine hundreds, retaining archaic signs for 6, 90, and 900.

This gave the practical condition for *isopsephy*: words already composed of numeral-bearing letters could be summed.

### Second century BCE: Hebrew alphabetic numerals

Secure epigraphic use of Hebrew letters as numerals is later than the Greek evidence. Gematric interpretation then becomes possible, but every alleged early instance must be dated from the text that actually states the equivalence.

### Third century CE: Syriac alphabetic numerals

Syriac documents adopted alphabetic numerals later still. The Syriac and Greek scientific traditions were important for Arabic translation culture.

## The Arabic script and the old order

### Nabataean-Aramaic inheritance

**[RECONSTRUCTION, strong paleographic consensus]:**

- Arabic script developed through Nabataean Aramaic cursive.
- Nabataean had 22 principal letters.
- Arabic required 28 consonantal distinctions.
- Several Arabic letters developed through diacritical differentiation of shared base shapes.
- The modern Arabic teaching order grouped letters by shape.
- The older **abjad** order retained the inherited ʾ-b-g-d sequence and appended or redistributed the additional Arabic consonants.

This is an inheritance of alphabetic sequence and script ancestry. It is not evidence that Nabataeans used the later Arabic abjad values. Nabataean inscriptions used a different, non-alphabetic numeral notation. Thomann explicitly cautions against bridging the gap from script genealogy to numeral genealogy without documents.

### Seventh century: early Qurʾān manuscripts

**[TEXT, with dating qualifications]:**

- Hijazi Qurʾān manuscripts use alphabetic signs at verse intervals.
- Tübingen UB Ma VI 165, radiocarbon-dated with high probability to 649–675, contains ṣād used for 60; whether every numeral was entered by the first hand requires codicological judgment.
- The Parisino-Petropolitanus has later-added verse numbers in the archaic system.
- The Fustat Qurʾān’s layout shows verse-numbering integrated with production associated with the Umayyad canonization project, about 703–705.

Thus early eighth-century use is secure even if some individual marks in seventh-century codices may be later additions. The system used is the so-called Western/archaic one, not the later standard Eastern table. [Thomann](https://www.sciamvs.org/files/SCIAMVS_19_167-200_Thomann.pdf).

### Eighth–ninth centuries: the Jabirian corpus

**[TEXTUAL CORPUS; AUTHORSHIP DISPUTED]:**

Works attributed to Jābir ibn Ḥayyān employ a “balance of letters” within alchemy:

- 28 letters correspond to four natures across seven degrees;
- the written name of a substance is analyzed to infer its natural constitution;
- letters may be morphologically expanded and weighted;
- the framework joins language, quantity, elemental physics, and alchemical transformation.

Modern scholarship does not treat the enormous Jabirian corpus as the straightforward work of one historical eighth-century author. Its composition is commonly placed across a longer late eighth- to tenth-century formation. “Jābir invented gematria” therefore overstates the evidence.

Moreover, at least one study notes that the *Kitāb al-Aḥjār* arranges letters in abjad order but does not simply assign the conventional 1–1000 values used by the Ikhwān. [Syed Nomanul Haq, critical study of *Kitāb al-Aḥjār*](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10125139/1/Haq_10125139_thesis.pdf).

### Early ninth century: the scientific table

**[RECONSTRUCTION]:**

Thomann links the standard Eastern high-value sequence to the translation of Greek and Syriac mathematical and astronomical literature. Ḥabash al-Ḥāsib provided the earliest known technical description. The sequence thāʾ/phi, khāʾ/chi, ẓāʾ/sampi suggests deliberate alignment with Greek numeration.

By the ninth century, abjad signs numbered propositions and tables in Arabic translations of Greek mathematics. An Arabic Euclidean optics translation probably made before 864 uses sīn = 60; Thābit ibn Qurra’s translation of Nicomachus uses the scientific system.

### Tenth century: the Ikhwān al-Ṣafāʾ

**[TEXT]:** The *Epistles of the Brethren of Purity*, produced in the tenth-century Basran intellectual environment, systematically correlated number, geometry, music, cosmology, and revelation.

Their numerical table extends through the standard abjad values. The 28 Arabic letters correspond naturally to structures such as four qualities multiplied by seven levels. Their treatment is philosophically Neopythagorean and Neoplatonic, but adapted to Arabic revelation and an Islamicate cosmology.

### Tenth–eleventh centuries: mathematical and talismanic squares

**[TEXT]:**

- Mathematical treatises by Abū al-Wafāʾ al-Būzjānī and ʿAlī ibn Aḥmad al-Anṭākī show sophisticated construction of magic squares.
- Early mathematical **awfāq** need not be magical; *wafq al-aʿdād* means a harmonious arrangement of numbers.
- Talismanic use subsequently joined squares to planets, divine names, Qurʾānic phrases, angels, and practical intentions.
- The simplest \(3\times3\) square has 1–9 arranged so every row, column, and diagonal totals 15:

|  |  |  |
|---:|---:|---:|
| 4 | 3 | 8 |
| 9 | 5 | 1 |
| 2 | 7 | 6 |

Its corner values 2–4–6–8 can be expressed as:

\[
ب(2)\ د(4)\ و(6)\ ح(8)
\]

giving **بدوح**, *budūḥ*. The sequence became a talismanic pseudo-name.

**[LEGEND]:** Later texts attributed magic squares to Adam, Solomon’s minister Āṣaf ibn Barakhiyā, Apollonius/Balīnās, Archimedes, or ancient Persian kings.

**[SCHOLARLY CAUTION]:** Attribution of the earliest magical square to Jābir is complicated by the layered dating of the Jabirian corpus. A ninth-century medical text of ʿAlī al-Ṭabarī may be the first unambiguous Arabic textual mention. [NLM Islamic manuscripts survey](https://www.nlm.nih.gov/hmd/arabic/astrology1.html), [Sesiano, *Magic Squares in the Tenth Century*](https://link.springer.com/book/10.1007/978-3-319-52114-5).

### Eleventh century: *Ghāyat al-ḥakīm* / *Picatrix*

**[TEXT, disputed dating/authorship]:** The Arabic *Ghāyat al-ḥakīm*, compiled in al-Andalus, synthesizes astral magic, talismans, planetary invocations, images, and inherited Hermetic material. Its date has been argued from the tenth to mid-eleventh century; the former attribution to Maslama al-Majrīṭī is widely rejected or substantially revised.

The Latin *Picatrix* brought this Islamicate astral-magical synthesis into medieval and Renaissance Europe. It is related to the history of letter magic, but it should not be treated as simply an abjad manual.

### Twelfth–thirteenth centuries: Ibn ʿArabī and al-Būnī

#### Ibn ʿArabī, 1165–1240

**[TEXT]:** In the opening chapters and chapter 20 of *al-Futūḥāt al-Makkiyya*, letters are cosmic entities, articulated breaths, divine signs, and constituents of the “Great Book” of existence. Alif signifies unity partly because its value is 1 and its vertical form is undivided. Chapter 20 calls the science of letters the science of Jesus.

His lettrism is not reducible to addition: shape, articulation point, breath, elemental nature, celestial sphere, hierarchy, and divine speech all matter. [Pierre Lory, “The Symbolism of Letters and Language”](https://ibnarabisociety.org/symbolism-of-letters-and-language-pierre-lory/), [Gril, “The Speech of Things”](https://ibnarabisociety.org/the-speech-of-things-denis-gril/).

#### Aḥmad al-Būnī, d. 1225 or 1232–33

**[TEXT, heavily affected by pseudepigraphy]:** Authentic early Būnian works include sophisticated discussions of divine names, letters, prayer, cosmology, and talismanic operations. They helped make ʿilm al-ḥurūf wa-l-asmāʾ widely transmissible.

**[CRITICAL TEXTUAL FINDING]:** The familiar printed *Shams al-maʿārif al-kubrā* is not safely treated as a unitary book written by al-Būnī. The “Būnian corpus” grew through copying, rearrangement, expansion, and attribution. Later material—including some elaborate magical-square chapters—entered under his name. The oldest manuscript witnesses must be distinguished from nineteenth- and twentieth-century Cairo printings.

A Chester Beatty manuscript of *al-Lumʿa al-nūrāniyya* dated 1287 lacks material present in many later witnesses, demonstrating textual growth. [Gardiner dissertation](https://deepblue.lib.umich.edu/bitstream/handle/2027.42/108942/ngardine_1.pdf?sequence=1), [Springer study of Būnian magic squares](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-032-28419-8_9).

### Fourteenth–fifteenth centuries: imperial lettrism and Hurufism

#### Fazlallāh Astarābādī, d. 1394

**[TEXT]:** Fazlallāh developed the Hurufi movement after a revelatory experience dated to Ramadan 775/February–March 1374.

Principal features included:

- the 28 Arabic and 32 Persian letters as structures of revelation;
- correlations between letters and the human face;
- the fourteen distinct muqaṭṭaʿāt letters and fourteen facial hair-lines;
- eschatological and messianic interpretation;
- the *Jāvdān-nāma*, substantially in the Astarabadi dialect.

The full *Jāvdān-nāma* remains incompletely edited; the manuscript tradition is extensive. A reference within it dated 4 February 1394 places at least part shortly before Fazlallāh’s execution on 2 September 1394.

**[TEXT/HISTORICAL]:** Fazlallāh was executed at the behest of Mīrānshāh, Timur’s son. Hurufi missionaries later worked in Anatolia; one group was burned at Edirne, probably in 1444.

**[RECONSTRUCTION]:** Ibn ʿArabī is sometimes named as a forerunner, but Hamid Algar emphasizes the major doctrinal differences. Direct derivation is unproved. [Iranica, “Horufism”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/horufism/), [Iranica, “Jāvdān-nāma”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/javdan-nama/).

#### ʿAbd al-Raḥmān al-Bisṭāmī, d. 1454

Al-Bisṭāmī integrated lettrism with universal history, astrology, dynastic prognostication, and Timurid/Ottoman political expectation. His work represents an “imperial” or learned occult science rather than merely popular magic.

# The muqaṭṭaʿāt

Twenty-nine Qurʾānic suras begin with one to five disconnected letters, such as:

- الم — *alif lām mīm*
- الر — *alif lām rāʾ*
- حم — *ḥāʾ mīm*
- كهيعص — *kāf hāʾ yāʾ ʿayn ṣād*
- طه — *ṭāʾ hāʾ*
- يس — *yāʾ sīn*
- ق — *qāf*
- ن — *nūn*

Fourteen distinct letter types occur.

## Interpretive history

### Early exegetical restraint

**[TEXT/TRADITION]:** One common early position is *Allāhu aʿlam bi-murādihi*—God knows their intended meaning. They were treated among the Qurʾān’s ambiguous or multivalent signs.

### Abbreviations

Exegetes proposed that they abbreviate:

- divine names or attributes;
- names of the Qurʾān;
- oaths;
- sentences;
- names of suras;
- angelic or prophetic names.

No single expansion gained consensus.

### Challenge theory

A major theological interpretation holds that the Qurʾān is made from the same letters available to its hearers, yet remains inimitable.

### Scribal or liturgical hypotheses

Modern scholarship has proposed:

- scribal sigla;
- ownership initials;
- liturgical markers;
- remnants of sura arrangement;
- rhyme or performance devices;
- Syriac abbreviations.

These remain hypotheses.

### Gematric readings

**[TEXT, but not demonstrably the original function]:** Classical exegetical traditions knew a story in which Jews add the values of **الم** and related initials to infer the duration of Muhammad’s community. Muslim transmitters generally do not establish this as the authoritative meaning.

For **الم**:

\[
ا(1)+ل(30)+م(40)=71
\]

Other initials yield different totals. The very multiplicity of combinations frustrates a single lifespan calculation.

Later lettrists associated the fourteen letters with cosmic structures, divine names, facial features, or historical cycles. Fazlallāh’s Hurufi reading is securely documented in the *Jāvdān-nāma* tradition but is a fourteenth-century doctrine, not evidence for the initials’ seventh-century origin.

There remains no scholarly consensus on what the muqaṭṭaʿāt originally meant. [Encyclopaedia of the Qurʾān, “Mysterious Letters”](https://www.ashtoncentralmosque.com/app/uploads/2014/07/Encyclopaedia_of_the_Quran_vol_3.pdf), [Martin Nguyen publication record](https://fairfield.elsevierpure.com/en/publications/exegesis-of-the-%E1%B8%A5uruf-al-muqa%E1%B9%AD%E1%B9%ADa%CA%BFa-polyvalency-in-sunn%C4%AB-tradition/).

# Jafr

## Traditional account

**[TRADITION]:**

- Jafr was first possessed or practiced by ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib.
- Secret knowledge passed through the Imams.
- Jaʿfar al-Ṣādiq, d. 765, possessed the **Kitāb al-jafr**.
- The book or scrolls were kept in a leather container; *jafr* was explained as a skin, lamb, kid, bag, or mysterious depth.
- It contained knowledge of future rulers, wars, calamities, and the coming Mahdi.

## Documentary assessment

Early Shiʿi hadith collections attest beliefs in objects or books called *al-jafr* and *al-jāmiʿa* in the Imams’ possession. That documents belief in an esoteric repository; it does not authenticate later manuals attributed to ʿAlī or Jaʿfar.

Much later jafr literature uses:

- abjad totals;
- letter permutations;
- Qurʾānic initials;
- divine names;
- astrological cycles;
- dynastic lists;
- apocalyptic traditions, *malāḥim*;
- Danielic prophecies;
- magic squares and divinatory boards.

Authorship claims to ʿAlī, Jaʿfar, Daniel, or ancient sages are frequently pseudepigraphic.

Ibn Khaldūn, d. 1406, gives an important skeptical account. He regards many dynastic predictions as obscure, retrospectively manipulated, falsely attributed, or interpretable only after the event. [Iranica, “Jafr”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/jafr/).

# Uses

## 1. Alphabetic numeral notation

Before positional Hindu-Arabic numerals became dominant, abjad letters could label:

- chapters and propositions;
- manuscript quires;
- astronomical tables;
- degrees on instruments;
- lists;
- verse divisions;
- numbered subsections.

This utilitarian practice is the foundation from which numerical wordplay becomes possible. Not every abjad numeral is “mystical gematria.”

## 2. Qurʾānic and theological exegesis

Lettrists analyzed:

- muqaṭṭaʿāt;
- the basmala;
- divine names;
- letters absent from or present in al-Fātiḥa;
- the 28 letters as a complete created order;
- the dot beneath ب;
- the creative command **كن**, *kun*, “Be!”:

\[
ك(20)+ن(50)=70
\]

The famous saying that all scripture is contained in al-Fātiḥa, al-Fātiḥa in the basmala, the basmala in ب, and ب in its dot belongs to Sufi and lettrist tradition in multiple forms. Precise attribution to ʿAlī or an early Imam is generally unproved.

## 3. Alchemy and natural philosophy

Jabirian letter-balances treated names as indexes of material reality. If language was correctly instituted, the name of a substance encoded its natural proportions.

The alphabet’s 28 letters suited a \(4\times7\) scheme:

- four elemental qualities;
- seven grades or divisions.

This is closer to a quantitative theory of language and matter than to modern fortune-telling.

## 4. Sufi metaphysics

Ibn ʿArabī and related thinkers treated letters as:

- differentiated breaths;
- archetypal forms;
- divine articulations;
- cosmic beings;
- intermediaries between unity and multiplicity;
- constituent “words” of the cosmic Qurʾān.

Abjad number is one dimension among shape, sound, articulation, ontology, and scriptural occurrence.

## 5. Divine-name practice

Names of God were:

- counted;
- repeated a prescribed number of times;
- entered into squares;
- matched to planets, angels, elements, days, or supplicants;
- expanded or permuted;
- inscribed on amulets.

A recitation count equal to a name’s abjad value is common in later devotional-occult practice. Its legitimacy was disputed among jurists and Sufis, especially where invocation shaded into coercive magic.

## 6. Amulets and talismans

Surviving artifacts and manuscripts combine:

- Qurʾānic verses;
- divine names;
- abjad letters;
- number grids;
- seal designs;
- angel names;
- astrological signs;
- prayers;
- unintelligible or deliberately concealed characters.

Purposes included protection, healing, childbirth, victory, love, reconciliation, wealth, safe travel, and defense against spirits. Some objects were understood as pious written prayer; others contain procedures classified by critics as forbidden *siḥr*. The categories overlap historically and should not be imposed solely from modern confessional judgments.

## 7. Awfāq and the budūḥ square

In the \(3\times3\) square above, every line totals 15:

\[
4+3+8=15
\]
\[
9+5+1=15
\]
\[
2+7+6=15
\]

and likewise vertically and diagonally.

The corners 4, 8, 2, 6—or another reading order—produce د ح ب و or the conventional talismanic **بدوح**. Budūḥ eventually behaved like the name of a spirit or power.

Higher-order squares were matched to planets in later Islamicate and European magic:

| Planet | Order | Cells | Magic constant for 1…n² |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Saturn | 3 | 9 | 15 |
| Jupiter | 4 | 16 | 34 |
| Mars | 5 | 25 | 65 |
| Sun | 6 | 36 | 111 |
| Venus | 7 | 49 | 175 |
| Mercury | 8 | 64 | 260 |
| Moon | 9 | 81 | 369 |

The neat fixed planetary series is best documented in later Arabic/Latin astral magic; it should not automatically be projected into the earliest mathematical treatises.

## 8. Chronograms

### Persian

From the tenth century Persian poets sometimes wrote dates explicitly in words; by the eleventh century they were encoding dates through letter totals. The form matured into **mādda-ye tārīkh**.

Chronograms marked:

- births;
- deaths;
- accessions;
- victories;
- marriages;
- books;
- mosques;
- madrasas;
- palaces;
- gardens;
- bridges;
- fountains;
- tombs.

Safavid poets produced large collections. Qāsim Kāhī of Kabul, Muḥtasham Kāshānī, Wāʿiẓ Qazvīnī, Najīb Kāshānī, and Kalīm Kāshānī were prolific. Qajar literary journals continued printing chronograms into the twentieth century. [Iranica, “Mādda Tārīkh”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/madda-tarik-chronogram/).

### Ottoman Turkish

Ottoman **tarih düşürme**, “dropping/composing a date,” became an elaborate poetic art. Poets dated:

- imperial births and circumcisions;
- appointments and dismissals;
- campaigns and treaties;
- fires and earthquakes;
- mosques, fountains, baths, mansions, barracks, and schools;
- deaths and tombs.

Yūsuf Nābī, 1642–1712, wrote chronograms, but the reputation of supreme specialist belongs particularly to **Adanalı Sürûrî**, 1752–1814. Other masters included Kandî, Vehbî, Râşid, and numerous court and inscriptional poets. [Study of Sürûrî](https://www.arastirmax.com/en/publication/ataturk-universitesi-sosyal-bilimler-enstitusu-dergisi/13/2/105-120-klasik-turk-edebiyatinda-tarih-dusurme-sanati-bir-ebced-ustasi-adanalı-sururi/arid/1b646992-18e1), [Ottoman Arabic chronogram study](https://dergipark.org.tr/tr/pub/iusarkiyat/article/11441).

### Urdu

Urdu poets inherited Persian conventions but had to decide how to treat hamza, aspirates, and specifically Indic or Persian letters. Shamsur Rahman Faruqi’s survey stresses that Urdu theorists formulated explicit orthographic rules, and that 786 became the most familiar numerical expression in the South Asian Muslim environment. [Faruqi](https://www.columbia.edu/itc/mealac/pritchett/00urduhindilinks/txt_abjad_farooqi.pdf).

### Reliability as dates

A contemporary chronogram may be excellent historical evidence if:

- the text is securely dated;
- the spelling is recoverable;
- the arithmetic works;
- the poet knew the event;
- there is no later rewriting.

A chronogram copied in a late manuscript may be retrospective. Different copies sometimes transmit different phrases or dates, as manuscripts of Nābī’s *Tuhfetü’l-Haremeyn* demonstrate.

## 9. Hurufi and Bektashi uses

Hurufism mapped the alphabet onto the face and body. Fourteen muqaṭṭaʿāt letter types corresponded to fourteen hair-lines; Arabic’s 28 and Persian’s 32 letters became interlocking sacred totals.

Hurufi doctrine entered Anatolia and the Balkans through missionaries and poets, notably:

- ʿAlī al-Aʿlā;
- ʿImād al-Dīn Nesīmī, executed around 1417–18;
- Refîʿî;
- Firişteoğlu;
- later Turkish adapters and translators.

It significantly influenced Bektashi symbolic vocabulary and manuscript culture. Yet “Bektashism is simply Hurufism” is an overstatement: Ottoman Bektashi writers sometimes defended themselves against precisely that accusation. [Iranica, “Horufism”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/horufism/).

## 10. Messianism and political prophecy

Letter science enabled claims that sacred texts foretold:

- the rise and fall of dynasties;
- conquests;
- the end of a century or millennium;
- the coming Mahdi;
- the identity of an eschatological renewer.

It was particularly powerful around the Islamic year 1000/1591–92 and in Timurid, Safavid, Ottoman, Shaykhi, Babi, and Bahaʾi settings.

The same flexibility that made it politically useful made retrospective manufacture easy. Ibn Khaldūn already recognized this problem.

## 11. Modern Qurʾānic “scientific miracles”

Modern apologetic calculations connect abjad values with:

- atomic numbers;
- sura numbers;
- modern scientific constants;
- dates of discoveries;
- word and letter frequencies;
- astronomical measurements.

Example:

\[
حديد = ح(8)+د(4)+ي(10)+د(4)=26
\]

Iron’s atomic number is 26.

With the article:

\[
الحديد = ا(1)+ل(30)+26=57
\]

Sūrat al-Ḥadīd is sura 57.

The arithmetic is genuine. The inference that the Qurʾān intentionally encodes modern atomic theory is a **[MODERN CLAIM]**. It depends on choosing the bare word for 26 and the articulated form for 57, among many available linguistic and numerical targets. No premodern source records the interpretation.

## 12. Rashad Khalifa’s Code 19

### Development

- **1973:** Khalifa published *Miracle of the Quran: Significance of the Mysterious Alphabets*.
- **1974:** he announced a pervasive mathematical code based on 19.
- **1981:** *The Computer Speaks: God’s Message to the World*.
- Later books and his Qurʾān translation expanded the system.

He appealed to Qurʾān 74:30, “over it are nineteen,” the nineteen letters of the basmala, 114 suras \(=19\times6\), and numerous counts of letters, words, initials, verses, and numerical positions. [Khalifa, *Visual Presentation of the Miracle*](https://principles-of-islam.org/publications/books/vp/contents.html).

### What is reproducible

Some elementary relations are exact:

\[
114=19\times6
\]

\[
واحد=19
\]

The basmala has nineteen letters under the standard count.

### Problems

**[DOCUMENTED CONTROVERSY]:**

- Counts depend on orthography: consonantal *rasm*, fully pointed text, modern normalized spelling, and variant treatment of alif do not always agree.
- Selection rules change between claims.
- Words, lemmas, roots, and grammatical forms are sometimes mixed.
- Some reported letter counts are not reproducible from a consistently specified text.
- Multiple comparisons make chance multiples inevitable unless tests and counting rules are defined before inspecting the data.
- Khalifa ultimately rejected Qurʾān 9:128–129 because their inclusion disrupted parts of his system. Those verses are present in the received textual tradition and in early manuscripts; removing them to preserve the hypothesis reverses normal textual-critical method.

Academic analysis treats the movement as a modern conjunction of Qurʾānic apologetics, computers, numerology, and the older cultural prestige of magical or significant numbers. [Naveeda Khan, “Nineteen”](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1463499610365377), [Khalifa primary-text repository](https://www.quraniclabs.com/downloads).

## 13. Internet abjad and “Arabic gematria”

Modern calculators are useful for arithmetic but frequently obscure editorial decisions.

Common problems include:

- silent normalization of أ إ آ to ا;
- inconsistent hamza counting;
- treating ة as ه without disclosure;
- treating ى as ي;
- ignoring or assimilating Persian letters;
- silently selecting the Eastern table;
- conflating ordinary abjad with digit reduction;
- calling every result “ancient Sufi numerology”;
- reverse-searching vast dictionaries until a desired number appears.

A calculator’s output is evidence only for what its algorithm returns. It is not evidence that a historical author made the association.

# Controversies and disputes

## 1. Mesopotamia, Greece, Israel, or Arabia?

The “origin of gematria” question conflates several separate developments.

### Mesopotamian priority

Sargon II’s inscription is the earliest famous number-name statement. It proves that an Assyrian king linked a measurement to the “reading” of his name.

It does not prove a decimal letter-value alphabet. The conversion remains unreconstructed.

### Greek priority

Greek has the earliest secure, systematic alphabetic numeral notation and abundant ancient isopsephy. For the specific mechanism “every letter has a decimal place value and words are summed,” Greek priority is the strongest documentary claim.

### Israelite priority

Hebrew preserves the same old Semitic alphabetic order, but secure Hebrew alphabetic numeral inscriptions are later than Greek ones. Some biblical number/name interpretations may be deliberate, but many are first attested only in rabbinic or Christian exegesis centuries after the biblical text.

### Arabic origin

Arabic inherited the Semitic order through Aramaic/Nabataean script history, but the complete Arabic numeral table also reflects early Islamic adaptation and, in the scientific order, Greek/Syriac influence. There is no secure pre-Islamic Arabic evidence for the entire 1–1000 table.

The most defensible account is therefore plural:

1. ancient Near Eastern number-name symbolism;
2. Greek invention or earliest documentation of systematic decimal alphabetic numerals;
3. Hebrew and Syriac adaptations;
4. Arabic adaptation of the old Semitic order to 28 letters;
5. Abbasid scientific restructuring;
6. independent Islamicate development of letter cosmology, jafr, awfāq, and chronograms.

## 2. The “Pythagorean” claim

Premodern and modern occultists often call letter-number symbolism “Pythagorean.”

- **[TEXT]:** Pythagorean and Neopythagorean traditions assigned metaphysical qualities to numbers.
- **[RECONSTRUCTION]:** Greek philosophical number symbolism helped shape late-antique and Islamicate cosmology.
- **[ABSENCE OF EVIDENCE]:** No surviving text by Pythagoras describes the modern 1–9 name-number table sold as “Pythagorean numerology.”
- **[MODERN INVENTION]:** Repeating A–I = 1–9, J–R = 1–9, and S–Z = 1–8 across the modern English alphabet belongs to modern numerological culture, especially nineteenth- and twentieth-century occult publishing.

“Chaldean numerology” is likewise a modern Western label for a name-number system, not a demonstrated survival of a Babylonian table.

## 3. Nabataean inheritance: script versus numerals

The Arabic script’s Nabataean ancestry is well supported. But Nabataean number signs were not the same as Arabic abjad numerals. A common modern error reasons:

> Arabic script came from Nabataean; therefore Arabic abjad numerals came unchanged from Nabataean.

The conclusion does not follow. Script, letter order, and numeral notation can be transmitted or replaced independently.

## 4. Eastern versus Western priority

Older reference works commonly treated the Western table as a regional late variation. Thomann’s evidence reverses this:

- archaic/Western ṣād = 60 appears in early Qurʾān culture;
- scientific/Eastern sīn = 60 is associated with Abbasid translations;
- both systems coexisted in tenth-century Egypt;
- the archaic system survived especially in the Maghrib.

This remains a reconstruction open to new manuscript evidence, but it is far better documented than the simple regional account.

## 5. The coincidence problem

Let \(A\) be an alphabet of 28 weighted letters. For words of ordinary length, many different spellings inevitably share a sum.

For example, 19 can be represented by:

\[
وحد = 6+8+4=18
\]

with alif added:

\[
واحد=6+1+8+4=19
\]

and:

\[
وجود=6+3+6+4=19
\]

The equality is interesting only if there are independent reasons to compare these particular words.

Sources of flexibility include:

- alternative spelling;
- articles and honorifics;
- hamza rules;
- tāʾ marbūṭa;
- shadda;
- Persian letters;
- grammatical inflection;
- synonyms;
- singular versus plural;
- addition, subtraction, multiplication, or reduction;
- choosing Hijri, solar Hijri, regnal, or Common Era dates;
- searching after the event.

A convincing historical case therefore needs:

1. a stable text;
2. a historically attested table;
3. a rule specified before calculation;
4. an explicit cue such as “seek its date”;
5. a plausible intended target;
6. ideally contemporary testimony.

Chronograms usually satisfy these conditions because the poet signals the operation. Internet miracle claims often do not.

## 6. Pseudepigraphy and forgery

### Jābir

The Jabirian corpus is layered. References to “Jābir’s exact doctrine” must identify the treatise and its manuscript history.

### Jaʿfar al-Ṣādiq and ʿAlī

Later books of jafr, letter magic, alchemy, and talismans were attributed to these authoritative figures. Attribution expresses lineage and legitimacy but rarely proves authorship.

### al-Būnī

The printed *Shams al-maʿārif al-kubrā* is the most consequential case. It contains genuine Būnian themes, but its received form incorporates substantial later compilation. Dating all its contents to 1225 is methodologically untenable.

### *Picatrix*

The Latin title and the traditional attribution to al-Majrīṭī obscure a complicated Arabic compilation. Its contents cannot be used indiscriminately as evidence for a single tenth-century author.

### Modern “ancient” tables

Many web pages present:

- English ordinal;
- English reduction;
- “reverse gematria”;
- “Sumerian” ×6;
- “Satanic” starting at 36;
- “Jewish gematria” applied directly to English letters

as ancient systems. These are modern calculator ciphers. Their labels do not establish Jewish, Sumerian, Pythagorean, or Satanic antiquity.

## 7. Religious legitimacy

Muslim assessments have ranged across:

- permitted mnemonic arithmetic;
- legitimate chronography;
- contemplative symbolism;
- pious divine-name devotion;
- divinely bestowed saintly knowledge;
- dubious prediction;
- forbidden astrology;
- sorcery and association of partners with God.

No single “Islamic view” covers all periods, schools, objects, and procedures.

## 8. Scholarly assessments

### Franz Dornseiff

*Das Alphabet in Mystik und Magie* (1922) remains a foundational comparative collection on letter mysticism, isopsephy, gematria, magical alphabets, and Islamicate speculation. It is indispensable as a dossier of evidence but reflects early twentieth-century categories and must be supplemented by later manuscript work.

### Toufic Fahd

Fahd’s studies and Encyclopaedia of Islam articles framed *jafr*, *ḥurūf*, divination, and indigenous Arabian practices for modern scholarship. Later scholars have refined his broad genealogies.

### Pierre Lory and Denis Gril

They clarified the metaphysical and alchemical dimensions of Islamic letter science, especially Jābir and Ibn ʿArabī, resisting its reduction to “numerology.”

### Noah Gardiner

Gardiner’s codicological work transformed understanding of the Būnian corpus: manuscript communities, textual accretion, and pseudepigraphic authority matter as much as printed titles.

### Liana Saif

Saif traces changing paths of occult knowledge from *Ghāyat al-ḥakīm* to Būnian texts and emphasizes differences between astral, natural, and revelatory models of efficacy.

### Johannes Thomann

Thomann’s reconstruction of “archaic” and “scientific” abjad systems is the most important recent correction to the conventional table history.

### Kieren Barry

*The Greek Qabalah* (1999) assembled Greek isopsephic material for occult and historical readers. It is useful, but claims of direct lineage or unified “Greek Qabalah” should be checked against epigraphic and philological studies.

### Scholem, Idel, Kaplan, Weinreb

These writers concern Jewish gematria more directly. Scholem and Idel distinguished historical kabbalistic schools; Kaplan popularized traditional materials; Weinreb offered modern theological numerology. Their work should not be silently transferred to Arabic lettrism, although comparative influence and shared late-antique environments are legitimate subjects.

# Open questions

1. **When was the full 28-letter archaic Arabic numeral table first fixed?**  
   Early verse numerals prove part of the system; the earliest complete surviving lists are later.

2. **How directly did Greek alphabetic numeration influence the first Arabic table?**  
   Influence on the ninth-century scientific sequence is strong; the relationship between Greek practice and the older Arabic order is less fully documented.

3. **How old are the earliest layers of the Jabirian letter-balance?**  
   This depends on unresolved corpus chronology.

4. **What did the Qurʾānic muqaṭṭaʿāt originally signify?**  
   No interpretation—gematric, scribal, liturgical, structural, or theological—commands consensus.

5. **Which surviving works are genuinely al-Būnī’s?**  
   Manuscript comparison continues to redraw the corpus.

6. **How did mathematical awfāq become operative talismans?**  
   The stages between mathematical recreation, alchemical symbolism, planetary magic, and popular amulets remain unevenly documented.

7. **When did 786 become a common substitute for the basmala?**  
   Its modern South Asian ubiquity is clear, but the earliest securely dated instance of substitution needs a dedicated manuscript and epigraphic census.

8. **How much Hurufi doctrine passed into particular Bektashi communities?**  
   Literary influence is certain; institutional and doctrinal identity varied by region and century.

9. **How should intentional numerical patterning be statistically tested?**  
   Historical intention cannot be inferred from divisibility alone. A rigorous study must preregister the text, orthography, counting rules, hypotheses, and comparison class.

10. **How extensive was everyday abjad literacy?**  
    Astronomers, scribes, poets, artisans, and occult specialists used overlapping but nonidentical systems. Regional documentary studies are still needed.
