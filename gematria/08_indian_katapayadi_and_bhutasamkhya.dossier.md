# Indian letter-number systems: katapayadi, aryabhata's code and the bhutasamkhya: Research Dossier

## Basic identification

### Scope and classification

This dossier concerns three related but structurally different Indian techniques for putting numbers into pronounceable or meaningful language:

1. **Kaṭapayādi** (कटपयादि; Malayalam പരൽപ്പേര് *paralppēr* in Kerala): a decimal positional cipher assigning digits 0–9 to consonants.
2. **Āryabhaṭa’s alphabetic numeration**: an additive, place-bearing syllabic notation in which consonants supply coefficients and vowels supply powers of 100.
3. **Bhūtasaṃkhyā** (भूतसंख्या, “object-numbers,” “concrete numbers,” or “word-numerals”): a decimal positional notation in which culturally familiar objects—moon, eyes, Vedas, seasons, and so forth—stand for numbers.

They belong to the wider category called **akṣarasaṃkhyā**, “letter-number,” or, in modern comparative terminology, **varṇasaṃkhyā**, “phoneme/letter numeration.” This latter label is useful but was not one universally standardized ancient system.

### Is this “gematria”?

**Comparative classification:** only in the broadest sense.

Hebrew gematria, Greek isopsephy, and Arabic abjad calculations normally start with an already existing name or sentence and obtain a number by adding letter-values. Equality of totals can then become an interpretive relationship.

The principal Indian systems discussed here usually work differently:

- Kaṭapayādi encodes a predetermined **sequence of decimal digits**.
- Āryabhaṭa’s notation constructs a number by adding syllabic values distributed across powers of 100.
- Bhūtasaṃkhyā replaces decimal digits with words denoting conventionally counted things.

They are overwhelmingly instruments of:

- mnemonic compression;
- poetic composition;
- transmission of astronomical constants and tables;
- chronological dating;
- classification.

There is little evidence that their historically central use was to discover mystical equivalences between arbitrary pre-existing names by adding all their letters. Calling them “Indian gematria” can therefore be useful for cataloguing comparative letter-number systems, but it obscures their predominantly **encoding**, not exegetical, function.

### Scripts, languages, regions, and periods

| System | Language or script basis | Earliest secure evidence | Principal region or circulation |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Bhūtasaṃkhyā | Sanskrit vocabulary; later Prakrit and regional languages | Early centuries CE, although the identification and date of the alleged *Yavanajātaka* examples are disputed; securely present by the fifth–sixth centuries | Pan-Indian; also Southeast Asian Sanskrit inscriptions |
| Āryabhaṭa numeration | Sanskrit phonemes, transmitted in various Brahmic scripts | *Āryabhaṭīya*, composed 499 CE | Initially north Indian mathematical astronomy; known to later astronomers across India |
| Kaṭapayādi | Sanskrit consonantal inventory; written in Grantha, Malayalam, Devanagari, Telugu, Kannada and other scripts | Haridatta’s *Grahacāranibandhana*, Kerala, 683 CE | Especially Kerala and southern India; occasional northern and western uses |
| Mēḷakarta application | Sanskritized or South Indian rāga names in Telugu, Tamil, Malayalam, Kannada or Roman transcription | Post-1750 nomenclatures, based on a seventeenth-century 72-mēḷa framework | Carnatic music |
| Akṣarapallī and other varṇasaṃkhyā systems | Letters or syllables of particular manuscript scripts | Medieval manuscripts, with considerable regional variation | India, Nepal, Sri Lanka, Burma/Myanmar and related manuscript cultures |

Sanskrit has been written in many scripts. The tables below use modern **Devanagari Unicode** for legibility, but the systems concern phonemic categories and syllables rather than the graphic shapes of Devanagari alone.

# The system in detail

## 1. Kaṭapayādi

### 1.1 Governing rule

A frequently quoted Sanskrit mnemonic is:

> नञावचश्च शून्यानि संख्याः कटपयादयः ।  
> मिश्रे तूपान्त्यहल् संख्या न च चिन्त्यो हलस्वरः ॥

Its wording and division vary in transmitted and modern presentations. Its practical sense is:

- *na* and *ña* stand for zero;
- the digit rows begin with *ka, ṭa, pa,* and *ya*;
- vowels do not alter a consonant’s value;
- in a consonant cluster, the consonant directly joined to or followed by the vowel—the last pronounced consonant of the onset—is counted;
- consonants not bearing the syllabic vowel are numerical fillers.

The existence of variant formulations means the mnemonic should not be treated as a securely dated founding charter. The fully documented system must be reconstructed from rule-verses, commentaries, tables and actual numerical use.

### 1.2 Positional direction

The conventional maxim is:

> अङ्कानां वामतो गतिः  
> *aṅkānāṃ vāmato gatiḥ*

Literally, “digits move toward the left.”

The encoded sounds are normally given **units first**. The sequence obtained while reading the word normally is reversed when written as a modern decimal numeral.

Thus:

- spoken/encoded digits: 4, 4, 6
- place interpretation: \(4\times10^0 + 4\times10^1 + 6\times10^2\)
- ordinary written number: **644**

Sarma emphasizes that the maxim belongs to the convention that number-words state lower places before higher ones. It is not evidence that Indians physically read Sanskrit prose backwards.

### 1.3 Vowels

Attached vowels ordinarily carry no independent value:

- क *ka* = 1
- कि *ki* = 1
- कु *ku* = 1
- के *ke* = 1
- कौ *kau* = 1

This vowel freedom makes it possible to turn almost any digit string into pronounceable syllables.

Independent vowels are normally zero or non-contributing fillers. Manuscripts and regional expositions sometimes formulate this as “vowels have no value,” rather than explicitly calling them zero. Those descriptions produce the same result internally but can differ at the ends of a numeral, where leading or trailing zeroes matter.

### 1.4 Conjunct consonants

In the standard rule, only the consonant immediately carrying the vowel counts.

Examples:

- त्व *tva*: *t* is unvocalized; *va* carries the vowel. Value = व = 4.
- न्त्य *ntya*: *n* and *t* are unvocalized; *ya* carries the vowel. Value = य = 1.
- श्र *śra*: value = र = 2, not श + र = 5 + 2.

This rule creates “null” consonants that poets can use for sense, grammar and metre.

Some descriptions reverse the rule or count consonants differently. Such variants must be tied to a particular text or regional practice rather than silently combined with the Kerala standard.

### 1.5 Calculation is positional, not summative

Suppose a word yields the consonantal digits:

\[
d_0,d_1,d_2,\ldots,d_n.
\]

Its value is:

\[
d_0+10d_1+10^2d_2+\cdots+10^n d_n.
\]

There is normally:

- no summation of all letters into one total;
- no digital-root reduction;
- no special equivalence merely because two independently written words happen to encode the same integer;
- no traditional rule that equal numbers imply equal theological essence.

Two words are numerically equivalent only in the straightforward sense that they encode the same decimal digit string under the same variant of the code.

### 1.6 Worked elementary examples

#### *bhavati* = 644

भवति, *bhavati*, “is/becomes”:

- भ *bha* = 4
- व *va* = 4
- त *ta* = 6

The encountered digit sequence is 4–4–6. Units are on the left:

\[
4 + 4(10) + 6(100)=4+40+600=644.
\]

Stephen Chrisomalis gives this example while classifying kaṭapayādi as an ordinary decimal ciphered-positional notation with several available signs per digit. [Chrisomalis, *Numerical Notation*](https://nzdr.ru/data/media/biblio/kolxoz/M/MPop/Chrisomalis%20S.%20Numerical%20notation..%20A%20comparative%20history%20%28CUP%2C%202010%29%28ISBN%200521878187%29%28O%29%28498s%29_MPop_.pdf)

#### *Mechakalyāṇi* = mēḷakarta 65

The name begins *me-ca-*:

- म *ma* = 5
- च *ca* = 6

Encountered: 5–6. Reverse for ordinary notation:

\[
5 + 6(10)=65.
\]

Therefore Mēcakalyāṇi is mēḷakarta 65.

#### *Dhīraśaṅkarābharaṇam* = mēḷakarta 29

The significant opening syllables are *dhī-ra-*:

- ध *dha* = 9
- र *ra* = 2

Encountered: 9–2. Reverse:

\[
9+2(10)=29.
\]

Therefore Dhīraśaṅkarābharaṇam is mēḷakarta 29.

#### *Kanakāṅgi* = mēḷakarta 1

Opening syllables *ka-na-*:

- क *ka* = 1
- न *na* = 0

Encountered: 1–0:

\[
1+0(10)=1.
\]

#### *Ratnāṅgi* = mēḷakarta 2

Opening *ra-tnā-*:

- र *ra* = 2
- in *tnā*, the vocalized consonant is न *na* = 0

Thus:

\[
2+0(10)=2.
\]

### 1.7 The *Sadratnamālā* value of π

**Documented text:** Śaṅkara Varman’s *Sadratnamālā*, composed in Kerala in 1819.

The relevant end of a verse is transmitted as:

> भद्राम्बुधिसिद्धजन्मगणितश्रद्धा स्म यद् भूपगीः  
> *bhadrāmbudhisiddhajanmagaṇitaśraddhā sma yad bhūpagīḥ*

Applying the final-consonant-of-each-syllable rule and ignoring dead consonants produces the encountered sequence:

\[
4,2,3,9,7,9,8,5,3,5,6,2,9,5,1,4,1,3.
\]

Because the first encoded digit is the units digit, reverse the sequence:

\[
314159265358979324.
\]

The accompanying rule takes the diameter as \(10^{17}\). Hence:

\[
\pi \approx
\frac{314159265358979324}{100000000000000000}
=
3.14159265358979324.
\]

This is π rounded correctly to seventeen decimal places. Śaṅkara Varman’s work is late—1819—but belongs to the continuing Kerala astronomical tradition and is genuinely documented. [M. D. Srinivas, overview of Indian science](https://iks.iitgn.ac.in/wp-content/uploads/2016/02/Indian-Tradition-of-Science-an-Introductory-Overview-MD-Srinivas-Feb-2016.pdf)

#### The “gopībhāgya” π verse is not the *Sadratnamālā* verse

A different and extremely popular stanza begins:

> गोपीभाग्यमधुव्रात-शृङ्गिशोदधिसन्धिग …  
> *gopībhāgyamadhuvrāta-śṛṅgiśodadhisandhiga …*

It is circulated as an “ancient Sanskrit verse” encoding 31 or 32 digits of π, sometimes with elaborate double meanings praising Kṛṣṇa and Śiva.

**Modern invention or modern first attestation:** the earliest located printed source is Bharati Krishna Tirthaji’s posthumously published *Vedic Mathematics* (1965; later editions 1970s). Takao Hayashi explicitly identified it as a recent production in that work. It is not the documented *Sadratnamālā* verse. It also uses the digits in forward order, contrary to standard Kerala kaṭapayādi direction. [Hayashi’s Indology-list note](https://list.indology.info/pipermail/indology/1995-October/003466.html)

Its numerical ingenuity is real; its frequently claimed Vedic or medieval provenance is unsupported.

### 1.8 Freedom and redundancy

Each digit has two to four possible consonants, while vowels and some members of consonant clusters are free. Consequently a number can be encoded by many words.

For a digit string of length \(n\), even a rough model with three consonant choices and several vowel choices per digit gives an exponentially growing number of possible forms. This is a feature for mnemonic composition, but it creates two evidentiary problems:

1. A meaningful phrase can often be manufactured after the desired number is known.
2. A retrospectively decoded phrase proves little unless the text gives a key, a numerical context, or an independently verifiable target such as a planetary period.

This is the Indian counterpart to the “coincidence problem” in gematria, although it concerns freedom of **encoding** more than collisions between additive totals.

## 2. Āryabhaṭa’s alphabetic code

### 2.1 Source and date

**Documented text:** Āryabhaṭa’s *Āryabhaṭīya*, composed in 499 CE. Āryabhaṭa was born in 476, since the text states that at the relevant epoch he was twenty-three years old.

The system is laid down near the beginning of the *Gītikāpāda*, conventionally verse 2:

> वर्गाक्षराणि वर्गेऽवर्गेऽवर्गाक्षराणि कात् ङ्मौ यः ।  
> खद्विनवके स्वराः नव वर्गेऽवर्गे नवान्त्यवर्गे वा ॥

> *vargākṣarāṇi varge ’varge ’vargākṣarāṇi kāt ṅmau yaḥ /  
> khadvinavake svarā nava varge ’varge navāntyavarge vā //*

The compressed wording is difficult without commentary. Bhāskara I’s seventh-century commentary and later scholastic interpretation are therefore indispensable. The operational result is the table given above. [Sanskrit text with Bhāskara I’s commentary](https://gretil.sub.uni-goettingen.de/gretil/corpustei/transformations/html/sa_AryabhaTa-AryabhaTIya-comm.htm)

### 2.2 Structure

Āryabhaṭa divided consonants into:

- **varga letters**, the 25 stops from क *ka* through म *ma*, valued 1–25;
- **avarga letters**, the semivowels, sibilants and ह *ha*, valued 30, 40, …, 100.

A vowel multiplies the consonantal coefficient by a power of 100. Several consonants associated with the same vowel position are added.

This is not a digit-by-digit decimal cipher. It is an **additive alphasyllabic notation with positional multipliers**.

### 2.3 Worked example: *khyughṛ* = 4,320,000

A standard example from the *Gītikā* is conventionally transliterated *khyughṛ*:

- ख *kh* = 2
- य *y* = 30
- उ *u* multiplier = \(10^4\)
- घ *gh* = 4
- ऋ *ṛ* multiplier = \(10^6\)

Therefore:

\[
(2+30)\times10^4+4\times10^6
\]

\[
=32\times10,000+4,000,000
\]

\[
=320,000+4,000,000
\]

\[
=\boxed{4,320,000}.
\]

This is the number of solar revolutions in Āryabhaṭa’s great astronomical period. The numerical words in the opening section encode planetary revolutions and other constants. [Datta and Singh transcription and arithmetic](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Indian_Mathematics/Arithmetical_notations_etc.)

### 2.4 Worked example: lunar revolutions

A second traditional example is transliterated approximately:

> *cayagiyiṅuśuchlṛ*

The components, as normalized in Datta and Singh, are:

\[
6+30+3(10^2)+30(10^2)+5(10^4)+70(10^4)+(50+7)(10^6).
\]

Calculate:

\[
6+30=36
\]

\[
3(100)+30(100)=300+3,000=3,300
\]

\[
5(10,000)+70(10,000)=50,000+700,000=750,000
\]

\[
(50+7)(1,000,000)=57,000,000
\]

Thus:

\[
36+3,300+750,000+57,000,000
=\boxed{57,753,336}.
\]

That is the conventional figure for lunar revolutions in the cycle in this reading.

The exact segmentation of such strings can look opaque in Roman transliteration because Sanskrit conjuncts share vowel signs. The calculation must follow the Sanskrit syllabic structure, not naïvely assign every printed Roman character an independent value.

### 2.5 Zero and empty places

Āryabhaṭa’s code has no separate zero character. A power of 100 with no coefficient is simply absent.

This does not by itself prove that Āryabhaṭa invented zero or possessed every later arithmetical rule for zero. It does show that his notation presupposes an abstract hierarchy of decimal places and can leave a place unexpressed.

Claims that a modern zero glyph is “hidden” in a particular Āryabhaṭīya syllable confuse:

- absence of a coefficient;
- the place-value concept;
- a written zero sign;
- zero as an arithmetic object.

These need distinct evidence.

### 2.6 Direction

Unlike kaṭapayādi, the numerical value is not obtained simply by reversing a string of decimal digits. The written syllables are read normally and their weighted values are added.

Some older descriptions say that the “smaller elements are on the left.” This reflects the general ordering of low-to-high place components, but calling it “right-to-left reading” can be misleading. The mathematical operation is additive over vowel-marked place groups.

### 2.7 Function

Āryabhaṭa employed the system for compressed astronomical information, especially in the *Daśagītikā/Gītikāpāda*. It did not become India’s general commercial notation and was not normally used for written calculations.

Its advantages were:

- enormous numerical range;
- compatibility with Sanskrit metre;
- compact presentation of tables;
- oral memorization.

Its disadvantages were:

- difficult decoding;
- limited ability to create genuinely meaningful ordinary words;
- vulnerability to textual corruption;
- need for specialist commentary.

These disadvantages help explain why the more flexible kaṭapayādi system became dominant in Kerala astronomical mnemonics.

## 3. Bhūtasaṃkhyā

### 3.1 Principle

A noun conventionally associated with a count stands for that number:

- moon = 1;
- eyes = 2;
- Vedas = 4;
- senses = 5;
- seasons = 6;
- sages = 7;
- elephants of the directions = 8;
- planets = 9;
- sky = 0.

The words form a place-value sequence. Conventionally, as with kaṭapayādi, the first word supplies the units place.

Thus:

> वेद-दन्त-चन्द्र  
> *veda-danta-candra*

can encode:

- Veda = 4
- teeth = 32
- moon = 1

This example also shows that a term need not be restricted to one digit. If *danta* is used as the conventional number 32, the components are interpreted compositionally:

\[
4 + 32(10) + 1(1000)=4+320+1000=1324.
\]

In a simpler digit-only expression, four words representing 5–0–7–1 would be read as 1705.

### 3.2 Worked inscriptional chronogram

An eighteenth-century inscription from Kalna uses:

> बाण-व्योम-धराधर-इन्दु-गणिते शाके  
> *bāṇa-vyoma-dharādhara-indu-gaṇite śāke*

The words yield:

- *bāṇa*, Kāma’s arrows = 5
- *vyoma*, sky = 0
- *dharādhara*, mountain = 7
- *indu*, moon = 1

Encountered sequence: 5–0–7–1.

Reading units first gives:

\[
5+0(10)+7(100)+1(1000)=1705.
\]

Hence Śaka 1705:

\[
1705+78=1783\ \text{CE}
\]

(subject to whether a current/expired year and month boundary affect conversion).

### 3.3 *Lokavibhāga* example

The Jain *Lokavibhāga* is securely dated to 458 CE and contains an extensive word-numeral expression conventionally interpreted as:

- five voids;
- two;
- seven;
- sky;
- one;
- three;
- form.

The resulting numeral is:

\[
13,107,200,000.
\]

The example is important because it shows a mature decimal positional word-notation in a securely dated fifth-century Jain text. It should not be conflated with a written zero glyph; the zero positions are supplied by words meaning void or sky.

### 3.4 No exhaustive canonical dictionary

Bhūtasaṃkhyā depends upon what later authors call established or recognized association, *prasiddhi*. But recognition varies by discipline and community.

For example:

- *rasa* can be 6, referring to the six tastes, but other aesthetic or alchemical enumerations may have different totals.
- *veda* normally means 4.
- *ṛṣi* commonly means 7.
- *graha* commonly means 9 in the classical Indian planetary scheme.
- *netra* ordinarily means 2.
- *samudra* may mean 4 or 7.
- *nāga* may mean 7 or 8.

Decoding is therefore philological. One must establish:

1. the date and region of the text;
2. the author’s likely enumerative tradition;
3. whether each word represents one digit or a multi-digit number;
4. the direction convention;
5. the expected numerical range;
6. whether metre, grammar or a parallel manuscript supports the reading.

### 3.5 Mixed notation

Sanskrit authors freely mixed:

- ordinary cardinal number-words;
- bhūtasaṃkhyā expressions;
- figures;
- fractions;
- technical units.

A verse may say “one through nine,” use *rasa* for 6, and specify “one hundred and three” grammatically in the same passage. Modern descriptions that force every word into a hidden cipher can therefore overdecode the text.

Alessandra Petrocchi treats bhūtasaṃkhyā not merely as a substitution trick but as a semantic technology in which mathematical information is integrated with Sanskrit cultural memory, oral transmission and poetic structure. [Petrocchi, “The bhūtasaṃkhyā notation”](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid%3A5babcfd0-ae8d-4eb6-9ce9-5df583dc1951)

# Origins: dated and placed

## Before the three systems

### Letter labels are not automatically letter-numerals

Indian grammatical, ritual and prosodic literature uses letters as:

- abbreviations;
- markers of classes;
- labels in ordered lists;
- technical symbols;
- devices for compressing rules.

Datta and Singh traced letter-label conventions to grammatical literature associated with Pāṇini and Kātyāyana. These may be antecedents to alphanumeric thought, but a letter marking the scope of a grammatical rule is not yet a positional numeral system.

### The alphabet and Brahmic numerals

Indian scripts descend in large part from Brāhmī, but the ordinary Brāhmī numeral signs were initially an additive system with separate signs for units, tens and hundreds. The phonological order of the Sanskrit alphabet supplied a convenient ordered inventory for later codes, but kaṭapayādi digits are not simply the old Brāhmī numerals relabelled as letters.

The evolution of decimal place-value notation is part of the enabling context:

- place position acquires decimal power;
- zero or an empty-place convention marks absent powers;
- poetic encoders can represent a decimal sequence without writing numeral glyphs.

The exact chronology linking spoken decimal organization, written place-value figures and word-numerals remains debated.

## Bhūtasaṃkhyā: early centuries CE

### The *Yavanajātaka* problem

**Older scholarly reconstruction:** David Pingree’s edition dated Sphujidhvaja’s versification of the *Yavanajātaka* to 269/270 CE, deriving it from a prose translation made in 149/150. On that reading, its final chapter contained early bhūtasaṃkhyā expressions and perhaps the earliest Indian decimal place-value reference with a *bindu* for zero.

This reconstruction was repeated widely by historians, including accounts that call the *Yavanajātaka* the earliest surviving bhūtasaṃkhyā witness.

**Documented dispute:** Bill Mak re-examined the passage using a previously unreported manuscript and argued that several crucial readings—including the chronological statements, mathematical chapter and alleged zero—depend upon Pingree’s emendations rather than the manuscripts. Mak therefore challenged both the 269/270 date and the status of the passage as secure early numerical evidence. [Mak, “The Date and Nature of Sphujidhvaja’s *Yavanajātaka* Reconsidered”](https://journals.library.ualberta.ca/hssa/index.php/hssa/article/view/7)

**Finding:** the *Yavanajātaka* remains a candidate for very early word-numeral use, but “269/270 CE” should not be presented as an undisputed manuscript fact.

### Fifth and sixth centuries

More secure evidence includes:

- the Jain *Lokavibhāga*, 458 CE;
- material preserved from astronomical siddhāntas;
- Varāhamihira’s *Pañcasiddhāntikā*, completed around 505;
- later *Sūryasiddhānta* recensions;
- Bhāskara I, Brahmagupta and succeeding astronomers.

Datta and Singh collected numerous examples from the *Pauliśasiddhānta*, *Sūryasiddhānta*, *Pañcasiddhāntikā*, *Mahābhāskarīya*, *Laghubhāskarīya*, *Brāhmasphuṭasiddhānta*, *Triśatikā* and *Gaṇitasārasaṃgraha*. Their dates sometimes reflect early twentieth-century judgments and must be checked against newer text-critical work, but the broad conclusion—that the notation was established in mathematical astronomy by the middle of the first millennium—is sound. [Datta and Singh, *History of Hindu Mathematics*](https://www.rarebooksocietyofindia.org/book_archive/196174216674_10151561604876675.pdf)

### Epigraphic spread

Early inscriptional word-numerals occur in Sanskrit inscriptions in Southeast Asia. In India proper they become more frequent from the ninth century onward, eventually becoming a standard way to embed dates in verse.

An inscriptional date is stronger evidence than a number inferred from a stray word only when:

- the text identifies an era or year;
- the expression yields a historically plausible date;
- the inscription is genuine;
- palaeography and provenance agree with the result.

Spurious copper plates and circular dating remain a significant hazard. Richard Salomon notes that several inscriptions formerly invoked in the history of Indian place-value notation proved spurious or doubtful. [Salomon, *Indian Epigraphy*](https://rapeutation.com/salomonindianepigraphy.pdf)

## Āryabhaṭa, 499 CE

The *Āryabhaṭīya* supplies the first secure occurrence of its particular code, and the system was probably Āryabhaṭa’s invention.

**Scholarly reconstruction:** “probably invented by Āryabhaṭa” is safer than claiming proof of creation ex nihilo. Earlier alphabetic abbreviations existed, but no earlier text presently documents this complete consonant-coefficient/vowel-power system.

It is unique in assigning:

- coefficients 1–25 to the stops;
- 30–100 to the remaining consonants;
- successive powers of \(100\) to vowels.

Later commentators, above all Bhāskara I, preserved its decipherment.

## Kaṭapayādi, 683 CE

### Secure first attestation

The earliest positive and dateable example accepted by Sarma is Haridatta’s:

> *Grahacāranibandhana*, Kerala, 683 CE.

Haridatta founded or reformulated the **Parahita** astronomical system. The code gave Kerala astronomers a more flexible way than Āryabhaṭa’s notation to express constants and tabular material in metrical language. Chrisomalis accordingly describes Haridatta as an intellectual descendant of Āryabhaṭa while distinguishing the structures of their notations. [Sarma](https://archive.numdam.org/articles/10.24033/rhm.167/); [Chrisomalis](https://nzdr.ru/data/media/biblio/kolxoz/M/MPop/Chrisomalis%20S.%20Numerical%20notation..%20A%20comparative%20history%20%28CUP%2C%202010%29%28ISBN%200521878187%29%28O%29%28498s%29_MPop_.pdf)

### Attribution to Vararuci

**Kerala tradition:** kaṭapayādi and the *Candra-vākyas*, lunar mnemonic sentences, are attributed to Vararuci. Traditional Kerala chronological lore places a Vararuci figure in the fourth century CE.

**Evidentiary status:** tradition or legend. The name Vararuci belongs to several literary, grammatical and legendary personae. No securely dated fourth-century manuscript establishes that such an author invented kaṭapayādi.

### Claim that Āryabhaṭa knew kaṭapayādi

Datta and Singh suggested an origin around 500 and proposed that Āryabhaṭa may have known the system. Chrisomalis notes that no textual evidence supports this assertion. Āryabhaṭa’s own fully specified system is structurally different.

**Finding:** possible historical influence from Āryabhaṭa’s example of syllabic number encoding is plausible; knowledge of the actual kaṭapayādi table by Āryabhaṭa is unproved.

### The Musinikuṇḍa-grant controversy

A string *svādita* in the Musinikuṇḍa grant of Viṣṇuvardhana III was formerly treated as an early kaṭapayādi chronogram:

- *sva-di-ta* allegedly yields encountered digits 4–8–6;
- reverse order gives 684;
- interpreted as Śaka 684, this would be approximately 761/762 CE.

That date conflicts with the reign. Attempts were made to emend the word, change the era, or regard the charter as a reissue.

A recent DHARMA edition concludes that the chronogram interpretation should be discarded:

- the reading does not yield an appropriate date;
- the proposed emendation is linguistically implausible;
- the inscription does not label the expression as a year;
- kaṭapayādi epigraphs are otherwise scarce before the fourteenth century;
- the diplomatic context supplies a better textual explanation.

This is an unusually clear example of a numerical reading being used to “correct” a text until it gives the desired history. [DHARMA edition and commentary](https://dharmalekha.info/texts/INSVengiCalukya00080)

# Historical development and uses

## Astronomical constants and tables

### Āryabhaṭa

The *Gītikāpāda* encodes large astronomical parameters:

- planetary revolutions;
- rotations of the Earth;
- lunar and solar cycles;
- sine differences.

The code compresses data into verse, enabling memorization and oral instruction.

### Kerala astronomy

Kaṭapayādi became especially effective in the Kerala astronomical tradition because a decimal digit could be represented by several consonants and almost arbitrary vowels.

Its uses included:

- planetary constants;
- mean and true longitude procedures;
- lunar-position sentences;
- sine tables;
- calendar calculations;
- epochs and day counts;
- computational manuals;
- inscriptions on astronomical instruments.

The **vākya** tradition stores numerical sequences in short sentences or verse-like strings. Some have semantic meaning; others are primarily pronounceable carriers.

### Astronomical instruments

Sarma has documented kaṭapayādi notation on Sanskrit astrolabes and celestial globes. Its presence outside manuscript verse shows that users understood it as a practical notational technology, not merely a literary curiosity.

## Mathematics

Bhūtasaṃkhyā and kaṭapayādi appear in:

- arithmetic rules;
- geometry;
- trigonometric tables;
- combinatorics;
- calendrical computation;
- magic-square constructions;
- numerical examples in didactic verse.

The systems normally encode inputs or results; they do not replace the working notation used on a dust board or writing surface during calculation.

## Chronograms

### Bhūtasaṃkhyā chronograms

A date can be composed as a meaningful Sanskrit verse. Conventional markers such as:

- *śake*—“in the Śaka year”;
- *saṃvatsare*—“in the year”;
- *abde*—“in the year”;
- regnal or calendrical language

tell the reader that the object-words are numerical.

These chronograms occur in:

- temple inscriptions;
- royal grants;
- colophons;
- astronomical works;
- manuscript-copying records;
- monuments.

### Kaṭapayādi chronograms

Kaṭapayādi could also encode:

- Kali-day numbers;
- years;
- authorial dates;
- foundation dates.

But inscriptional examples are much rarer than popular accounts imply. Sarma’s survey finds that it remained heavily concentrated in Kerala; northern authors often knew of it without using it extensively.

### Direction and calendar conversion

After decoding a chronogram one must still determine:

- Śaka, Vikrama, Kollam or Kali era;
- elapsed versus current year;
- calendrical month convention;
- lunisolar intercalation;
- whether the number is a year, day count or epoch constant.

A correct consonantal decoding does not by itself establish a Gregorian date.

## Carnatic music and the 72 mēḷakartas

### The 72-scale framework

Veṅkaṭamakhin’s seventeenth-century *Caturdaṇḍīprakāśikā* developed a systematic framework of 72 possible parent scales. But the claim that he supplied the modern kaṭapayādi names must be qualified.

A musicological account preserved in Bhatkhande University material states:

- the *Caturdaṇḍīprakāśikā* itself names only nineteen mēḷas;
- those names lack the familiar kaṭapayādi prefixes;
- the *Rāgalakṣaṇa* appendix printed with the Music Academy edition is not Veṅkaṭamakhin’s own work;
- the Kanakāṅgi–Ratnāṅgi nomenclature arose after 1750;
- later scholars adjusted names specifically to make their opening syllables encode the correct number. [Bhatkhande source](https://library.bhatkhandeuniversity.ac.in/opac-tmpl/bootstrap/images/bhatkhandepdf/1285.pdf)

### Modern naming rule

For a mēḷakarta name:

1. take the first two numerically operative syllables;
2. convert their consonants to digits;
3. reverse them;
4. obtain a number from 1 to 72.

Examples already shown include:

- Kanakāṅgi: 1–0 → 01 → 1;
- Ratnāṅgi: 2–0 → 02 → 2;
- Dhīraśaṅkarābharaṇam: 9–2 → 29;
- Mēcakalyāṇi: 5–6 → 65.

The number then identifies the scale’s position in a combinatorial ordering.

### Deriving the notes

The 72 mēḷakartas are divided into two sets:

- 1–36: śuddha madhyama, \(M_1\);
- 37–72: prati madhyama, \(M_2\).

They are further divided into twelve **cakras** of six. Within each group, the combinations of ṛṣabha/gāndhāra and dhaivata/niṣāda positions follow a regular order. Thus the encoded name is not merely a catalogue number: with knowledge of the ordering it lets a musician reconstruct the scale.

### Historical correction

**Documented:** Veṅkaṭamakhin’s seventeenth-century theoretical importance.

**Later development:** systematic kaṭapayādi naming of all 72 scales.

**Incorrect popular compression:** “Veṅkaṭamakhin invented the 72 modern names and encoded every one by kaṭapayādi.”

The evidence instead indicates a staged development involving later nomenclatures, including the tradition associated with Govindācārya and *Saṅgrahacūḍāmaṇi*.

## Poetry and literary aesthetics

### Bhūtasaṃkhyā

Bhūtasaṃkhyā allows a date or mathematical constant to participate in a poem’s imagery:

- moon can mean one while remaining a moon in the surface sense;
- eyes can mean two;
- Vedas can mean four;
- seasons can mean six.

This can produce genuine double articulation: a grammatical sentence and a number.

### Kaṭapayādi

Kaṭapayādi’s semantic layer is less intrinsic. A consonant represents a digit because of its place in the code, not because the word’s meaning naturally evokes that number. Nevertheless, its redundancy makes meaningful poetry possible.

Some texts exploit:

- devotional vocabulary;
- royal praise;
- astronomical imagery;
- authorial signatures;
- puns whose numerical and semantic layers reinforce each other.

## Manuscript organization: akṣarapallī

**Akṣarapallī**, “letter series/system,” refers to schemes in which letters or syllables number manuscript folios or sections. Jain scribes sometimes contrasted it with **aṅkapallī/aikapallī**, numeral-figure notation.

These schemes are related by function to varṇasaṃkhyā but are not necessarily kaṭapayādi. A manuscript system might assign:

- consonants in sequence to 1, 2, 3…;
- consonant-plus-vowel combinations to successive ranges;
- altered letter-forms to folio numbers.

The exact table must be recovered from the manuscript tradition concerned. Calling every letter-based foliation scheme “kaṭapayādi” erases important distinctions.

## Jain uses

Jain authors made major contributions to Indian mathematical culture and used word- and letter-based numerical devices.

### *Lokavibhāga*

The securely dated 458 CE *Lokavibhāga* is a foundational witness to decimal word-numerals.

### Mahāvīra and later Jain mathematics

Mahāvīra’s ninth-century *Gaṇitasārasaṃgraha* uses word-numeral conventions alongside extensive mathematical exposition.

### Nemicandra

The tenth-century Digambara teacher Nemicandra wrote the *Gommatasāra*, whose treatment of enormous enumerations of soul-classes and karmic states generated specialized mnemonic and diagrammatic numerical practices.

Claims that Nemicandra employed “the standard kaṭapayādi system” require passage-specific verification. Some modern cryptographic writing treats any syllabic number construction in Jain works as kaṭapayādi, but Jain texts also use:

- bhūtasaṃkhyā;
- ordinary number-words;
- akṣarapallī;
- technical abbreviation;
- enormous named-number hierarchies.

These should not be merged without textual demonstration.

## Tantric letter speculation

Indian Tantric traditions developed elaborate correspondences involving:

- the Sanskrit alphabet as **mātṛkā**, the “Mothers”;
- letters located on bodily centres or lotus petals;
- mantra syllables;
- phonemes as manifestations of cosmic powers;
- permutations and diagrams;
- numerically organized groups of deities or energies.

This is unquestionably letter mysticism, but it is not automatically kaṭapayādi or Āryabhaṭa numeration. A letter can be:

- cosmological;
- phonetic;
- mantric;
- iconographic;
- enumerated by position;
- assigned a deity;

without possessing a stable decimal value used in calculation.

**Finding:** a broad intellectual environment joined sound, enumeration and cosmology, but evidence for systematic gematria-like addition of arbitrary tantric words under the kaṭapayādi table is sparse. Modern “Sanskrit numerology” often fuses historically distinct mātṛkā theory, astrology and kaṭapayādi.

## Magic squares

Sarma records kaṭapayādi’s appearance in discussions of magic squares. Here it functions as numerical notation. A square’s entries can be represented by letters or syllables; numerical properties remain those of the underlying integers.

This differs from assigning supernatural efficacy to a person’s name-number. Magical or ritual deployment must be shown from the individual source rather than inferred merely because a square is “magic” in the mathematical sense.

## Present-day uses

Living and revived applications include:

- Carnatic mēḷakarta nomenclature;
- Sanskrit pedagogy;
- demonstrations in history-of-mathematics courses;
- software encoders and decoders;
- digital humanities work on Sanskrit scientific texts;
- calendrical and astronomical heritage projects;
- mnemonic composition;
- popular “Vedic numerology”;
- online claims that scriptures contain hidden constants.

The IIT Kanpur **Jñānasaṅgrahaḥ** project offers modern tools for kaṭapayādi, Āryabhaṭīya and bhūtasaṃkhyā encoding. These are useful implementations, but their automatically generated results are modern compositions, not historical attestations. [Jñānasaṅgrahaḥ](https://sanskrit.iitk.ac.in/jnanasangraha/)

# Controversies and disputes

## 1. Was kaṭapayādi Vedic?

**Modern popular claim:** kaṭapayādi is “Vedic numerology,” thousands of years old, embedded in the Vedas.

**Evidence:** no Vedic text supplies the kaṭapayādi table, its conjunct rule, or a securely decodable numerical passage governed by it.

**Earliest secure evidence:** Haridatta, 683 CE.

**Assessment:** the Vedas contain numbers, enumerated classes, phonetic speculation and mnemonic organization, but this does not establish kaṭapayādi. “Vedic” in this claim is a modern sacralizing extension, not a demonstrated textual provenance.

## 2. Was it invented by Vararuci?

**Tradition:** yes, often in connection with the *Candra-vākyas* and Kerala legends.

**Documented evidence:** the oldest secure occurrence is Haridatta. The Vararuci attribution is later and complicated by multiple historical and legendary Vararucis.

**Assessment:** preserve the attribution as Kerala tradition; do not use it to date the code to the fourth century.

## 3. Did Āryabhaṭa invent kaṭapayādi?

No surviving passage by Āryabhaṭa uses the standard kaṭapayādi mapping. He created or first documents a different alphasyllabic system.

A line of conceptual development is plausible:

\[
\text{alphabetic ordering}
\rightarrow
\text{Āryabhaṭa’s syllabic coefficients}
\rightarrow
\text{flexible decimal kaṭapayādi}.
\]

But that is a historical reconstruction, not an explicit ancient testimony.

## 4. Is bhūtasaṃkhyā older than decimal place-value notation?

The answer depends upon which kind of “place-value notation” is meant:

- spoken decimal organization;
- positional word-sequences;
- written numeral signs;
- an explicit zero marker;
- arithmetic using written zero.

Datta and Singh argued that decimal place value must have been familiar before it could be transferred to object words. That is plausible, but the relative survival of texts and inscriptions does not preserve every developmental stage.

Bhūtasaṃkhyā cannot by itself prove the prior existence of the modern numeral glyphs. It does demonstrate a decimal conception in which the same digit acquires a different value by position.

## 5. The *Yavanajātaka* and priority

Pingree’s reconstruction made the *Yavanajātaka* central to several priority claims:

- 269/270 CE date;
- early decimal place value;
- a zero or *bindu*;
- early bhūtasaṃkhyā;
- transmission of Babylonian and Greek astral science.

Mak’s manuscript work contests the readings on which some of these claims rested. The text remains evidence for complex Greco-Indian astral transmission, but its precise date, authorial layers and final mathematical chapter cannot be treated as settled.

## 6. Greek or Mesopotamian origin?

The Indian systems arose in a world where Greek, Iranian, Mesopotamian and Indian astronomical traditions interacted.

Possible lines of influence include:

- imported horoscopy;
- sexagesimal astronomical parameters;
- Greek alphabetic numerals;
- indigenous Sanskrit grammatical compression;
- Indian decimal place-value organization;
- oral verse pedagogy.

No surviving evidence derives the kaṭapayādi consonant-to-digit table from Greek isopsephy or Mesopotamian name-numbers. Structurally:

- Greek alphabetic numerals assign successive letters 1–9, tens and hundreds;
- Āryabhaṭa assigns consonantal coefficients and vowel powers of 100;
- kaṭapayādi assigns several consonants to each decimal digit;
- bhūtasaṃkhyā uses semantic objects.

Cross-cultural background is real; direct descent of the specific Indian codes is unproved.

## 7. Pythagorean claims

**Modern claims:** Pythagoras learned Indian numerology, or kaṭapayādi descends from “Pythagorean vibration numbers.”

**Evidence:** no ancient Greek or Indian text connects Pythagoras with kaṭapayādi, which is first securely attested more than a millennium after Pythagoras.

Confusion frequently arises between:

- the geometrical relation called the Pythagorean theorem;
- Pythagorean philosophical speculation about number;
- modern “Pythagorean numerology,” assigning 1–9 cyclically to modern alphabets;
- Indian syllabic encoding.

They are separate historical questions. The existence of early Indian right-triangle rules says nothing about the origin of kaṭapayādi.

## 8. “Chaldean” name numerology

Modern occult charts called “Chaldean numerology” map Latin letters irregularly to 1–8 and reduce personal names. They are products of modern Western occultism, especially nineteenth- and early twentieth-century numerological literature.

No evidence connects those charts to:

- historical Babylonian cuneiform mathematics;
- Āryabhaṭa;
- kaṭapayādi;
- bhūtasaṃkhyā.

Modern Indian numerologists sometimes combine “Chaldean,” “Pythagorean” and kaṭapayādi calculations. Such syntheses are modern inventions even when the kaṭapayādi table embedded in them is historically genuine.

## 9. Is kaṭapayādi cryptography?

It is a cipher in the elementary sense that a mapping converts digits to syllables. It can conceal a number from an uninitiated reader.

But historical uses usually sought:

- memorability;
- metrical fit;
- error control;
- compactness;

rather than military secrecy. Calling it “ancient encryption” can exaggerate its secrecy and import modern cryptographic concepts such as keys, hashes or computational security.

Comparisons with hashing are analogies, not historical descriptions. The mapping is many-to-one when decoding letters to digits and one-to-many when composing words from digits, but it has neither collision resistance nor adversarial security in the modern technical sense.

## 10. The coincidence problem

### Kaṭapayādi

Many words share a number because:

- each digit has several consonants;
- vowels are free;
- non-vocalized consonants can be fillers;
- different languages permit different word forms.

Equal encodings therefore carry no necessary semantic relation.

### Āryabhaṭa numeration

Different syllabic combinations can have the same additive total. A number may be decomposed in several ways among consonantal coefficients and place groups.

### Bhūtasaṃkhyā

Ambiguity arises because:

- several words denote the same number;
- one word may refer to several traditional enumerations;
- compounds may be segmented differently;
- direction may be uncertain;
- expected dates can tempt editors to choose convenient meanings.

Consequently a decoded value has high evidentiary weight only when supported by explicit numerical context and manuscript evidence.

## 11. Forgeries and circular dating

Potential problems include:

- forged or spurious copper plates;
- misread letter forms;
- treating an ordinary word as a chronogram;
- emending the word until it gives a desired royal date;
- dating a manuscript from a numerical phrase and then using that date to validate the phrase;
- mistaking a late copy’s interpolation for the original author’s wording.

The Musinikuṇḍa *svādita* controversy is a model case. Salomon’s warnings about doubtful inscriptions apply more generally.

## 12. “Hidden constants” in scripture

A modern author can search a large text for consonant sequences matching digits of π, \(e\), astronomical distances or modern dates. Given:

- multiple consonant choices;
- free vowels;
- selectable starting points;
- variant spellings;
- optional treatment of conjuncts;
- forward or reverse direction;
- freedom to omit inconvenient syllables,

apparently striking matches become easy to generate.

A serious historical claim needs:

1. a manuscript predating knowledge of the constant at that precision;
2. a rule stated or consistently used in that textual community;
3. non-arbitrary boundaries;
4. correct direction and phonology;
5. no silent normalization chosen after seeing the result;
6. an independent indication that the passage is numerical.

The documented *Sadratnamālā* π verse meets these criteria. Most viral “Vedic π” claims do not.

## 13. The *gopībhāgya* case

**Documented:** the stanza encodes a long π-like digit sequence under a modified or forward application.

**First located source:** Bharati Krishna Tirthaji’s twentieth-century *Vedic Mathematics*.

**Traditional claim:** ancient, Vedic, or a medieval devotional marvel.

**Evidence for that antiquity:** none presently known.

**Conclusion:** a clever modern Sanskrit numerical composition subsequently presented as ancient.

## 14. “Four variants” of kaṭapayādi

Datta and Singh’s influential history listed four variants.

Sarma’s manuscript-based reassessment concludes that:

- some alleged variants are adaptations to a regional alphabet rather than independent systems;
- one supposed variant arose from insufficiently controlled reporting;
- only two genuine variants can be established;
- one of those occurs in a single work of unknown provenance.

This illustrates the difference between a secondary table repeatedly copied in surveys and a variant actually attested in a primary text.

# Relation to Jain and Tantric number symbolism

## Jainism

Jain traditions possessed exceptionally large cosmological numbers, layered taxonomies and combinatorial classifications. Their mathematical works are central to the history of Indian numeration.

Documented intersections include:

- *Lokavibhāga* word-numerals;
- Mahāvīra’s mathematical Sanskrit;
- manuscript letter-numbering;
- mnemonic encodings;
- Nemicandra’s enumerative karmic cosmology.

What should not be assumed is that every numerical arrangement is gematria. Jain cosmological enumeration usually counts real doctrinal categories; it does not infer meaning by adding the letters of their names.

## Tantra

Tantric traditions sacralize the phonemic inventory as a totality of power. Numbers can structure:

- 50 or 51 Sanskrit letters;
- lotus petals;
- mantras;
- deity groups;
- bodily locations;
- ritual diagrams.

This can resemble gematria conceptually because letters and cosmic numbers interact. Yet the evidence generally concerns ordered sets and correspondences, not the kaṭapayādi conversion of whole names.

Modern esoteric authors sometimes construct a hybrid:

\[
\text{mātṛkā letter}+\text{kaṭapayādi digit}+
\text{planet}+\text{chakra}+\text{name total}.
\]

Unless an early text supplies that same chain, it is a modern synthesis.

# Comparison of the three systems

| Feature | Kaṭapayādi | Āryabhaṭa | Bhūtasaṃkhyā |
|---|---|---|---|
| Basic sign | Consonant | Consonant + vowel | Word or concept |
| Mathematical structure | Decimal ciphered-positional | Additive, with vowel-marked powers of 100 | Decimal word-positional |
| Zero | न/ञ; independent vowels often null | No separate zero sign; absent coefficient | Sky, void, zero, hole and similar terms |
| Direction | Units normally first; reverse encountered digits | Add weighted components; not simple reversal | Units normally first; reverse positional order |
| Vowels | Normally no value | Determine power of 100 | Part of ordinary word |
| Conjuncts | Usually last consonant before vowel counts | Parsed as consonantal coefficients sharing vowel positions | Ordinary Sanskrit grammar |
| Meaningful text | Highly flexible | Often artificial mnemonic strings | Naturally meaningful and culturally allusive |
| Primary purpose | Tables, constants, chronology, music | Astronomical constants and sine data | Verse numbers, astronomy, chronology |
| Add all letters? | No | Add syllabic weighted terms, not plain letter totals | No |
| Reduce to one digit? | No | No | No |
| Traditional semantic equivalence of equal totals? | Generally no | No | Not normally |
| Closest modern analogy | Mnemonic decimal cipher | Mixed-radix-looking but decimal alphasyllabic notation | Semantic decimal code |
| “Gematria” in strict sense? | No | No | No |

# Open questions

1. **Pre-Haridatta development:** Was the standard kaṭapayādi table created by Haridatta, inherited from a now-lost Kerala predecessor, or gradually regularized? Surviving evidence cannot decide.

2. **The Vararuci corpus:** Can manuscript stratigraphy separate old *Candra-vākya* material from later attribution and determine when the Vararuci legend became attached to it?

3. **The *Yavanajātaka*:** What date and textual layer should be assigned to its disputed final mathematical material? Further manuscript discoveries may revise both Pingree and Mak.

4. **Bhūtasaṃkhyā vocabulary:** How stable were associations across astronomy, Jain cosmology, ritual, medicine and regional poetry? A dated corpus-based dictionary is still needed.

5. **Orality:** How did teachers mark syllable divisions and vowel-place groups in recitation? Written commentaries reveal rules but incompletely document performance.

6. **Error detection:** Metre can expose missing or added syllables, but how reliably did it preserve the associated digits? Comparative manuscript studies could quantify this.

7. **Regional variants:** Which apparent kaṭapayādi differences reflect genuinely different rules, and which merely adapt Malayalam, Telugu, Tamil, Pali or Sinhala phonology?

8. **Music nomenclature:** Who exactly introduced each set of kaṭapayādi prefixes into the competing 72-mēḷa lists after Veṅkaṭamakhin? The relationship among manuscripts of *Saṅgrahacūḍāmaṇi*, later rāgalakṣaṇa appendices and performing lineages needs continued study.

9. **Epigraphic distribution:** Sarma found very few kaṭapayādi inscriptions outside Kerala. A comprehensive digital epigraphic search may locate additional secure examples, but proposed readings need chronological controls.

10. **Jain letter codes:** Passage-level studies are needed to distinguish kaṭapayādi, akṣarapallī, bhūtasaṃkhyā and other abbreviatory schemes in Jain manuscripts.

11. **Tantric numeration:** Which tantric works, if any, use a stable letter-to-number table to calculate whole words rather than merely enumerating letters and deities?

12. **Modern transmission:** When did Indian-language popular numerology begin combining kaṭapayādi with Western “Pythagorean” and “Chaldean” name-number systems?

13. **The *gopībhāgya* stanza:** Although its first presently located print source is twentieth-century, an exhaustive manuscript and periodical search would be needed to prove that Tirthaji personally composed it rather than adopting a slightly earlier modern verse.

---

# Overall findings

- **Bhūtasaṃkhyā is probably the oldest of the three**, developing in the early Common Era and securely attested in the fifth century, but its frequently cited third-century *Yavanajātaka* witness is textually disputed.
- **Āryabhaṭa’s code is securely dated to 499 CE** and is structurally unique: consonants provide coefficients, vowels powers of 100.
- **The earliest secure kaṭapayādi evidence is Haridatta’s 683 CE Kerala astronomical work.**
- **Kaṭapayādi is decimal and positional, not summative.** The first encoded syllable normally gives the units digit.
- **The systems are principally technologies of encoding and memory, not Indian counterparts of rabbinic gematria in interpretive method.**
- **The 72-mēḷakarta framework is seventeenth-century, but the fully systematic kaṭapayādi names are later, apparently post-1750.**
- **Śaṅkara Varman’s 1819 *Sadratnamālā* genuinely encodes π as 3.14159265358979324.**
- **The famous *gopībhāgya* long-π verse is first located in twentieth-century “Vedic Mathematics,” not an ancient or Vedic source.**
- **No evidence connects these systems historically to Pythagorean name numerology or modern “Chaldean” charts.**
- **Jain and tantric traditions provide important contexts of numerical and letter symbolism, but their particular practices must not be automatically renamed kaṭapayādi or gematria.**
- **Ambiguity is substantial.** A numerical reading is historically convincing only when supported by an explicit key, numerical context, secure manuscript or inscription, and a plausible date.
