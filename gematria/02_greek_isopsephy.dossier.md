# Greek isopsephy: Research Dossier

## Basic identification

| Field | Identification |
|---|---|
| Name | Greek **isopsephy**, Greek ἰσοψηφία (*isopsēphía*), literally “equality of counters/pebbles,” hence “equal numerical value” |
| Script | Greek alphabet, normally the 24 alphabetic letters plus three numeral signs: digamma/stigma, koppa, sampi |
| Base system | The Ionic or Milesian alphabetic numerals |
| Earliest secure word-sum evidence | A numerical annotation to a Greek epigram at the tomb of Petosiris, Hermoupolis Magna, Egypt, probably mid-third century BCE |
| Principal ancient period | Hellenistic and Roman eras, approximately third century BCE–fourth century CE |
| Principal regions | Egypt, Asia Minor, Greece, Italy, and the Greek-speaking eastern Mediterranean |
| Later continuities | Late-antique magic and Christianity; Byzantine manuscript and epigraphic culture; modern Greek “lexarithmic” speculation |
| Basic operation | Replace each Greek letter by its alphabetic-numeral value and add the values |
| Basic equivalence | Two texts are *isopsepha*, “equal in count,” if their unreduced totals are identical |
| Evidentiary status | The practice is securely ancient. Claims that it was invented by Pythagoras, encoded in the original creation of the alphabet, or embedded systematically in all Greek vocabulary are not supported by early evidence |

### Exact standard table

The table is the alphabetic numeral system itself. Ordinary letters and obsolete numeral characters form three groups of nine.

| Units | Value | Tens | Value | Hundreds | Value |
|---|---:|---|---:|---|---:|
| Α α | 1 | Ι ι | 10 | Ρ ρ | 100 |
| Β β | 2 | Κ κ | 20 | Σ σ ς | 200 |
| Γ γ | 3 | Λ λ | 30 | Τ τ | 300 |
| Δ δ | 4 | Μ μ | 40 | Υ υ | 400 |
| Ε ε | 5 | Ν ν | 50 | Φ φ | 500 |
| Ϝ ϝ, later Ϛ ϛ | 6 | Ξ ξ | 60 | Χ χ | 600 |
| Ζ ζ | 7 | Ο ο | 70 | Ψ ψ | 700 |
| Η η | 8 | Π π | 80 | Ω ω | 800 |
| Θ θ | 9 | Ϙ ϙ, later Ϟ ϟ | 90 | Ͳ ͳ, later Ϡ ϡ | 900 |

The three supplementary signs are:

- **ϝ/ϛ = 6**: originally digamma or *wau*; later commonly written stigma.
- **ϙ/ϟ = 90**: archaic koppa and its specialized numeral form.
- **ͳ/ϡ = 900**: the sign conventionally called sampi.

The historical shapes varied considerably. Modern Unicode distinctions can misleadingly imply a uniformity not present in papyri and inscriptions. The conventional modern table is nevertheless an accurate representation of the mature Milesian system. The history of the three signs, particularly sampi, remains technically disputed; numeric sampi is not securely documented as early as the ordinary archaic alphabet ([The Digamma, Koppa, and Sampi as Numerals in Greek](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/8FF93D1FA8CFDE6688AF9175825BF21C/S0009838800004936a.pdf/the-digamma-koppa-and-sampi-as-numerals-in-greek.pdf); [Unicode Standard, Greek](https://unicode.org/versions/Unicode17.0.0/core-spec/chapter-7/)).

## The system in detail

### 1. The relation between numerals and words

**Documented fact.** Greek isopsephy depends on the prior existence of alphabetic numerals. A Greek letter could therefore be read in two ways:

- phonographically, as part of a word;
- numerically, as a value in the Milesian series.

A numeral was ordinarily distinguished by a stroke or *keraia*: αʹ = 1, ιʹ = 10, ρʹ = 100, χξϛʹ = 666. Thousands could be marked with a lower-left sign: ͵α = 1,000.

Isopsephy removes the practical distinction between “letter” and “numeral” and reads every letter of a word as a number. Thus:

\[
\operatorname{value}(L_1L_2\ldots L_n)
=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\operatorname{value}(L_i).
\]

It is therefore a consequence of the alphabetic-numeral table, but it does **not** follow that Greeks began summing words as soon as they began using letters for numerals. The archaeological record contains a chronological gap between early alphabetic writing, the development of the complete Milesian series, and secure word-sum games.

### 2. What counts

For the ordinary method:

- Every written alphabetic letter counts.
- Vowels and consonants both count.
- Repeated letters count repeatedly.
- Uppercase and lowercase forms have the same value.
- Final sigma **ς** and medial sigma **σ** are the same letter and both equal 200.
- Accents, breathings, diaeresis, punctuation, spaces, apostrophes, numeral strokes, and editorial marks have no independent values.
- A diphthong counts as its two written letters.
- An iota subscript presents an editorial problem. If it represents a historically written iota in the chosen text, a rigorous calculation must state whether it has been counted. Ancient inscriptions often omit later editorial diacritics entirely.
- Scribal ligatures normally resolve into their constituent letters. A ligature has no separate standard value merely because it is written with one pen movement.
- Abbreviations and *nomina sacra* must be handled as the text actually presents them or explicitly expanded; the two procedures can give different results.
- Obsolete signs ϝ, ϙ, and ϡ count only if actually present. They are not silently inserted into ordinary words.
- Orthographic variants, dialect, grammatical case, and movable letters change the total.

For example:

- Ἰησοῦς = 888.
- Ἰησοῦ, the genitive, is only 688.
- Ἰησοῦν, the accusative, is 738.

Consequently, “Jesus equals 888” means specifically that the common Greek nominative spelling **ΙΗΣΟΥΣ** equals 888. It is not a value possessed independently of spelling and grammatical form.

### 3. Reduction

**Documented ancient norm.** Ordinary isopsephy compares the complete, unreduced sum. A pair equal at 888 is equal because both totals are 888, not because both reduce to 6 or 24.

Later mathematical and occult writers sometimes use:

- repeated digit addition;
- reduction modulo 9;
- “roots” or bases, Greek **πυθμένες** (*pythmenes*);
- the sum of a word’s component digits;
- letter count in addition to numerical sum;
- products, triangular numbers, or factorization.

These are related numerical operations, but they are not interchangeable with ordinary isopsephy.

The ancient mathematical term *pythmēn*, “base” or “root,” could describe the unit corresponding to a letter’s place in one of the three enneads: α, ι, and ρ all have base 1; β, κ, and σ have base 2; and so forth. Modern “Pythagorean numerology,” however, turns this into a universal A–I = 1–9 name-number system. That modern system is not evidence that Pythagoras practiced Greek word-summing.

### 4. What equivalence means

Two expressions are **ἰσόψηφα**, “equal in numerical value,” when their complete totals match. The equivalence can serve different functions:

- a joke or insult;
- an erotic concealment;
- a poetic constraint;
- an oracle or riddle;
- a theological symbol;
- an amuletic identity;
- a way to conceal a personal name;
- a scribal check or compositional aid.

Numerical equality did not inherently mean semantic identity. The inference “two expressions have the same total, therefore they secretly mean the same thing” is an interpretive addition. Ancient authors sometimes made that inference, sometimes joked with it, and sometimes merely displayed technical virtuosity.

### 5. Ancient terminology

- **ἰσόψηφος**, *isopsēphos*: equal in numerical count.
- **ἰσοψηφία**, *isopsēphia*: equality of count; the modern name of the practice.
- **ψῆφος**, *psēphos*: pebble, counter, vote, or numerical count.
- **ψηφίζω**, *psēphizō*: count or calculate. Revelation 13:18 says **ψηφισάτω**, “let him calculate.”
- **ἀριθμός**, *arithmos*: number.
- **κεφάλαιον**, *kephalaion*: total or sum; used in documentary arithmetic and in the Petosiris annotation.
- **ἰσόψηφα ἐπιγράμματα**: isopsephic epigrams.
- **πυθμήν / πυθμένες**, *pythmēn/pythmenes*: base or bases in some ancient arithmetic discussions, not a synonym for every form of isopsephy.
- **λεξάριθμος**, *lexarithmos*: “word-number,” chiefly a modern Greek term.
- **νεόψηφον**, *neopsēphon*: “a new calculation,” the pun opening the anti-Nero verse preserved by Suetonius.

There is no securely attested ancient Greek catalogue parallel to the later Hebrew lists of dozens of named gematria transformations. Most proliferating “Greek gematria ciphers” on websites are modern taxonomies.

## Worked examples

### ΙΗΣΟΥΣ = 888

**Documented late-antique calculation.** It is explicit in the Marcosian material reported by Irenaeus and in the Sibylline riddle.

\[
ΙΗΣΟΥΣ =
Ι(10)+Η(8)+Σ(200)+Ο(70)+Υ(400)+Σ(200)
=888.
\]

Irenaeus reports that the Marcosians related this to the alphabet’s eight units, eight tens, and eight hundreds: 8 + 80 + 800 = 888. The actual numeral series has nine signs in each band; their symbolism selectively speaks of “eight” in connection with the Ogdoad and Jesus.

### ΠΕΡΙΣΤΕΡΑ, “dove,” = 801

**Documented in Irenaeus’s account of Marcus.**

\[
ΠΕΡΙΣΤΕΡΑ =
Π(80)+Ε(5)+Ρ(100)+Ι(10)+Σ(200)+Τ(300)+Ε(5)+Ρ(100)+Α(1)
=801.
\]

Irenaeus says Marcus connected the dove with Alpha and Omega:

\[
Α+\Omega=1+800=801.
\]

The arithmetic is exact. The theological meaning belongs specifically to the Marcosian system, which Irenaeus reports polemically in *Against Heresies* I.14–16.

### ΝΕΡΩΝ = 1005

\[
ΝΕΡΩΝ =
Ν(50)+Ε(5)+Ρ(100)+Ω(800)+Ν(50)
=1005.
\]

Suetonius, *Nero* 39.2, preserves the Greek lampoon:

> Νέρων ἰδίαν μητέρα ἀπέκτεινε  
> “Nero killed his own mother.”

The joke is that ΝΕΡΩΝ and ΙΔΙΑΝ ΜΗΤΕΡΑ ΑΠΕΚΤΕΙΝΕ each total 1005. Arithmetic for the predicate:

- ΙΔΙΑΝ = 10 + 4 + 10 + 1 + 50 = 75
- ΜΗΤΕΡΑ = 40 + 8 + 300 + 5 + 100 + 1 = 454
- ΑΠΕΚΤΕΙΝΕ = 1 + 80 + 5 + 20 + 300 + 5 + 10 + 50 + 5 = 476

\[
75+454+476=1005.
\]

The transmitted introductory pun **νεόψηφον**, “a new reckoning,” advertises the operation. Suetonius is therefore unusually strong evidence: the text not only contains equal sums but signals the calculation ([Suetonius, *Nero* 39](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/thayer/e/roman/texts/suetonius/12caesars/nero%2A.html)).

### ΛΑΤΕΙΝΟΣ = 666

**Documented proposal, not Irenaeus’s final identification.**

\[
ΛΑΤΕΙΝΟΣ =
Λ(30)+Α(1)+Τ(300)+Ε(5)+Ι(10)+Ν(50)+Ο(70)+Σ(200)
=666.
\]

Irenaeus calls this plausible because the ruling kingdom was Latin, but explicitly warns against certainty.

### ΤΕΙΤΑΝ = 666

\[
ΤΕΙΤΑΝ =
Τ(300)+Ε(5)+Ι(10)+Τ(300)+Α(1)+Ν(50)
=666.
\]

Irenaeus prefers the archaizing spelling **ΤΕΙΤΑΝ**, not the common ΤΙΤΑΝ, precisely because the added epsilon produces the desired sum. He again declines certainty. This is already an ancient demonstration of the spelling-selection problem.

### ΕΥΑΝΘΑΣ = 666

\[
ΕΥΑΝΘΑΣ =
Ε(5)+Υ(400)+Α(1)+Ν(50)+Θ(9)+Α(1)+Σ(200)
=666.
\]

Irenaeus mentions the name to show that many candidates can be made to fit.

### “Nero Caesar” and Revelation 666/616

This famous solution uses **Hebrew values**, not direct Greek isopsephy.

Greek-form Neron Caesar, transliterated into Hebrew as נרון קסר:

\[
נ(50)+ר(200)+ו(6)+ן(50)+ק(100)+ס(60)+ר(200)=666.
\]

Latin-form Nero Caesar, without the final *n*:

\[
נרו\ קסר =
50+200+6+100+60+200=616.
\]

**Scholarly reconstruction.** This is a widely supported modern explanation of Revelation 13:18, especially because both 666 and 616 are ancient manuscript readings. It is not an identification made by Irenaeus. Indeed, Irenaeus knows the 616 variant, rejects it as a subtraction of fifty, and proposes Greek candidates such as ΛΑΤΕΙΝΟΣ and ΤΕΙΤΑΝ. A further objection is that the expected Jewish spelling of Caesar can contain yod, קיסר, changing the sum. Recent scholarship therefore ranges from strong endorsement to renewed skepticism ([The Number of the Beast in Light of Papyri, Graffiti, and Inscriptions](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/2222582X.2016.1218996); [Forward and Reverse Gematria Are Very Different Beasts](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1515/opth-2022-0245/html?lang=en)).

### The Pompeii beloved: 545

CIL IV 4839 is conventionally read:

> Φιλῶ ἧς ἀριθμὸς φμεʹ.  
> “I love her whose number is 545.”

\[
φμεʹ=500+40+5=545.
\]

The woman’s name is concealed. No surviving text supplies it, and lists of candidate names are modern guesses. The graffito documents social use of name-number concealment, not a mystical doctrine. Pompeii’s destruction in 79 CE gives the terminus ante quem.

### Smyrna: ΚΥΡΙΟΣ = ΠΙΣΤΙΣ = 800

A Christian graffito from the basilica beneath the agora at Smyrna explicitly labels the expressions **ἰσόψηφα**:

\[
ΚΥΡΙΟΣ =
Κ(20)+Υ(400)+Ρ(100)+Ι(10)+Ο(70)+Σ(200)=800.
\]

\[
ΠΙΣΤΙΣ =
Π(80)+Ι(10)+Σ(200)+Τ(300)+Ι(10)+Σ(200)=800.
\]

Thus “Lord” and “faith” are declared equal in count. The explicit label makes this more probative than a coincidence noticed only by a modern calculator.

### Leonides of Alexandria

Leonides, active under Nero and probably Vespasian, composed epigrams in which corresponding verses or distichs had equal totals. One programmatic couplet says:

> Εἷς πρὸς ἕνα ψήφοισιν ἰσάζεται, οὐ δύο δοιοῖς …  
> “One line is made equal in counters to one, not two to two…”

Each line is reported as 4,111. A birthday epigram to Caesar balances one distich against another at 5,699. These are not accidental totals: the poet announces the constraint, and papyrus drafts elsewhere show the labor of testing and recomposing equal-valued lines. The surviving poems are collected in the *Greek Anthology* and studied in Max Leventhal’s *Poetry and Number in Graeco-Roman Antiquity* ([Greek Anthology IX](https://www.attalus.org/poetry/anth9a.html); [Leonides of Alexandria’s Isopsephic Epigrams](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/poetry-and-number-in-graecoroman-antiquity/leonides-of-alexandrias-isopsephic-epigrams/394194E47B42C3C3C669B82EBDEDE08B)).

### Petosiris epigram: 8,373

At the tomb of Petosiris at Hermoupolis Magna, a later hand wrote beneath a two-line Greek epigram:

> κεφάλαιον τούτων τῶν ἰαμβείων … δραχμαὶ ͵ητογʹ  
> “The total of these iambics … 8,373 drachmas.”

The annotation then gives 2,720 for a subsidiary portion. The “drachmas” are playful accounting: the letters have been added as numbers and the poem assigned a mock monetary cost. Because the graffito explicitly gives the total, it is presently the earliest clear material instance of Greek word-summing, dated approximately to the middle of the third century BCE. The inscription is IMEGR 125 = SEG 8.624–625 ([PHI inscription record](https://epigraphy.packhum.org/text/343621?hs=37-46)).

### Sibylline Oracles: the 888 riddle

*Sibylline Oracles* I.324–330 describes a name of four vowels and two repeated consonants and gives:

- eight units;
- eight tens;
- eight hundreds.

\[
8+80+800=888.
\]

The intended six-letter name is ΙΗΣΟΥΣ. The passage is Christian and composite; it is not a pre-Christian pagan Sibyl’s prediction. Dating individual strata of Books I–II is difficult, commonly between the late first and third centuries CE. Thus the arithmetic is documented ancient Christianity, while the prophetic voice is pseudepigraphic literary convention ([Sibylline Oracles I](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Sibylline_Oracles_%28Terry%2C_2nd_edition%29/Book_1)).

### ΒΑΥΧΩΩΧ = 3663

The brief’s “Glycon (3663)” conflates two different items.

\[
ΒΑΥΧΩΩΧ =
Β(2)+Α(1)+Υ(400)+Χ(600)+Ω(800)+Ω(800)+Χ(600)=3203,
\]

not 3663, if spelled exactly ΒΑΥΧΩΩΧ. The scholarly article generally cited is W. Brashear, “Βαυχωωχ = 3663: No Palindrome,” and its reading depends on a longer or differently established magical letter-string. The critical point is that **3663 belongs to a magical formula discussed by Brashear, not to the ordinary name ΓΛΥΚΩΝ**.

The name:

\[
ΓΛΥΚΩΝ =
Γ(3)+Λ(30)+Υ(400)+Κ(20)+Ω(800)+Ν(50)=1303.
\]

Lucian’s *Alexander* 18 reports the verse in which the serpent announces himself as Glycon, but the surviving passage does not state “Glycon = 3663.” That association should be rejected as a modern conflation. Lucian nevertheless supplies valuable context for oracular letterplay and religious fraud in the later second century ([Lucian, *Alexander the False Prophet*](https://www.tertullian.org/rpearse/lucian/lucian_alexander.htm)).

## Origins: dated and placed

### Before Greek isopsephy

#### Sargon II, c. 717–706 BCE

**Documented text; disputed reconstruction.** Sargon II’s Dur-Šarrukin inscriptions say that the city wall measured approximately 16,280 or 16,283 cubits, “the reading/number of my name.” The difference reflects textual witnesses and modern editions. The Royal Inscriptions of the Neo-Assyrian Period translates the relevant claim directly ([RINAP 2](https://build-oracc.museum.upenn.edu/rinap/downloads/Frame_RINAP_2.pdf)).

What is certain:

- the royal text deliberately correlates a structure’s measurement and the king’s name;
- the claim predates secure Greek isopsephy by centuries.

What is uncertain:

- precisely how Sargon’s written name generates the number;
- whether the value derives from wedge counts, polyvalent cuneiform signs, logographic readings, or learned wordplay;
- whether this belongs to a continuous “gematria system.”

Friedrich Delitzsch proposed a wedge-count reconstruction in 1878; later Assyriologists have not treated it as proof of a general Assyrian alphabetic gematria. Cuneiform is not an alphabet with the Milesian value table. It is therefore a comparative precursor to name-number thinking, not the origin of Greek isopsephy established by transmission.

### The Greek alphabetic numeral system

**Scholarly reconstruction.** Greeks first used several numerical notations, including acrophonic numerals. The mature Ionic/Milesian system arranged 27 signs into units, tens, and hundreds. Evidence for its components accumulated unevenly; sampi as 900 is considerably less securely attested in the early period than ordinary alphabetic letters.

By the Hellenistic era the alphabetic system had become a widely usable technology across the Greek-speaking Mediterranean. It supplied the necessary condition for isopsephy: a stable mapping from letters to numbers. It does not establish that isopsephy was the system’s original purpose.

### Petosiris, mid-third century BCE

**Earliest secure Greek attestation presently identified.** The Hermoupolis graffito gives the calculated total of an existing epigram. Its Egyptian setting is important: early isopsephy is documented in the multilingual, scribal, mathematical culture of Hellenistic Egypt, although this alone does not prove an Egyptian invention.

### Apollonius of Perga, later third century BCE

Apollonius devised methods for expressing very large numbers through alphabetic notation and multiplication. This is documented Greek mathematical numeration, not necessarily semantic word equivalence. It demonstrates the intellectual environment in which letters could be manipulated numerically.

### First century CE: popular, poetic, and political uses

By the early Roman imperial period isopsephy appears in several independent genres:

- concealed erotic names at Pompeii;
- Nero lampoons;
- Leonides’ highly constrained epigrams;
- papyrus tables used to find matching expressions;
- epigraphic and votive compositions.

This variety argues that the technique was not confined to a mystery school.

### Revelation, late first century CE

Revelation 13:18 instructs:

> “Let the one who has understanding calculate the number of the beast, for it is the number of a human being; and its number is 666.”

The majority textual form is χξϛʹ, 666. The early variant 616 was known by Irenaeus around 180 CE and survives in Codex Ephraemi and P.Oxy. 4499/P115. P115 is late third or early fourth century; it is an early witness, not the autograph.

The wording strongly invites a name-number solution. Whether the intended alphabet is Greek or Hebrew is not stated. The Nero solution is a modern scholarly reconstruction supported by the paired 666/616 spellings but complicated by Hebrew orthography and the absence of an ancient commentator who explains it that way.

### Second century CE: Artemidorus and dream interpretation

In *Oneirocritica* IV, Artemidorus of Daldis explains that one dream-image can signify another expression **because they are isopsephic**. His examples connect otherwise different words through common sums. This is explicit evidence that professional dream interpreters treated numerical equality as a legitimate analogical bridge, though Artemidorus also warns that interpretation depends on context ([Artemidorus, *Oneirocritica*](https://www.attalus.org/translate/artemidorus.html)).

### Marcus and the Marcosians, c. 150–180 CE

Irenaeus’s *Against Heresies* I.14–16 preserves the most elaborate early Christian-Gnostic alphabet theology associated with Marcus:

- divine emanations arranged as tetrads, ogdoads, and dodecads;
- the 24 Greek letters mapped onto the body of **Aletheia**, Truth;
- the six-letter name ΙΗΣΟΥΣ = 888;
- the dove ΠΕΡΙΣΤΕΡΑ = 801;
- Alpha and Omega = 801;
- speculation about vowels, consonants, syllables, and the unpronounceable divine Name.

**Documented report, hostile source.** Irenaeus is near-contemporary but polemical. We possess no surviving book written by Marcus. The calculations are reliable as evidence for what Irenaeus attributed to the school, but some ridicule, framing, and simplification may be his ([Irenaeus, *Against Heresies* I](https://earlychristianwritings.com/text/irenaeus-book1.html)).

### Irenaeus V.30, c. 180 CE

Irenaeus provides both textual criticism and a coincidence warning:

- he says 666 stood in the best and oldest copies known to him;
- he knows manuscripts reading 616;
- he observes that many names can fit 666;
- he offers ΕΥΑΝΘΑΣ, ΛΑΤΕΙΝΟΣ, and ΤΕΙΤΑΝ;
- he warns that one should await fulfillment rather than name the Antichrist rashly.

This is one of antiquity’s clearest acknowledgments of the multiple-solutions problem ([Irenaeus V.30](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/0103530.htm)).

### Third–fourth centuries: magical papyri and Christian-Gnostic texts

The Greek Magical Papyri contain:

- strings of the seven vowels ΑΕΗΙΟΥΩ;
- divine and “barbarous” names;
- alphabetic sequences;
- repeated permutations;
- explicit or suspected equal-valued formulas;
- words whose letter sums correspond to cosmological numbers such as 365 or 9,999.

A frequently discussed formula is ΧΑΒΡΑΧ and longer expansions said to total 9,999. In magical texts, spelling can be unstable and the words may be deliberately nonlexical. An exact claim must therefore cite a specific papyrus, edition, and reading; translating a modern normalized transcription directly into numbers can conceal textual uncertainty.

The *Pistis Sophia* survives in Coptic in the Askew Codex, probably representing Greek compositions of the third to fourth centuries. It is saturated with numbered aeons, mysteries, orders, repentances, and retained Greek names. **What is documented is numerical cosmology.** Claims that extended Greek phrases in it secretly equal 3,177, 1,059, or other theological totals appear chiefly in late nineteenth- and early twentieth-century occult commentary, especially G. R. S. Mead and F. Bligh Bond, rather than as explicit calculations in the Coptic text. Those later calculations should not be cited as the text’s own declarations ([Mead, *Pistis Sophia*](https://gnosis.org/library/pistis-sophia/index.htm); [Parkhouse, “Canon of the Pistis Sophia”](https://academic.oup.com/jts/article/73/2/649/6815670)).

### Late antiquity and Byzantium

Greek alphabetic numerals remained standard in manuscripts, dates, chapter numbers, Easter tables, regnal computations, and inscriptions. Isopsephic Christian expressions and protective number-signs continued, but several phenomena must be distinguished:

- ordinary use of Greek numeral letters;
- chronology by indictions or world eras;
- Christograms and *nomina sacra*;
- acclamatory formulas;
- explicit isopsephic equations;
- later chronograms.

The famous sign **ΧΜΓ** is sometimes interpreted numerically as 643 and sometimes as an abbreviation or protective formula. Its meaning remains debated; a numerical reading is possible but not sufficient by itself.

Greek culture did not develop a chronogram industry as socially pervasive as Persian-Ottoman **tārīkh** composition or the Hebrew chronogram. Individual Byzantine and post-Byzantine numerical texts exist, but broad claims that every Byzantine date or monogram is “gematria” are misleading.

## Uses

### Poetry and constrained composition

Leonides transformed a recreational calculation into formal poetic craft. Equal line sums required control over:

- synonyms;
- word order;
- dialect forms;
- optional particles;
- spelling;
- grammatical inflection.

P.Jena II 15 A–B preserves what scholars interpret as working material: candidate expressions tested, accepted, or rejected according to their sums. This is unusually direct evidence for composition rather than retrospective pattern hunting.

Later poets and inscription-makers employed equal totals in dedications, epitaphs, and praise. Isopsephy could signify balance, symmetry, intellectual refinement, or the value of the person praised.

### Erotic concealment

The Pompeii 545 graffito and related Smyrna graffiti conceal a beloved under her number. Anyone who knew the intended spelling—or the social circle—could attempt recovery. Because many names have the same sum, the number was a hint, not a mathematically unique cipher.

### Political mockery and polemic

The anti-Nero equation at Suetonius *Nero* 39 converts biography into arithmetic: Nero’s own name equals the accusation that he killed his mother.

Revelation’s beast number became an extraordinarily productive polemical device. Ancient interpreters proposed Greek names; medieval and Reformation polemicists recalculated emperors, popes, religious titles, nations, and opponents. The procedure commonly involved selecting:

- a favorable language;
- an advantageous spelling;
- a title rather than a personal name;
- omitted or added letters;
- Roman numerals embedded in Latin text.

Those later papal calculations are historically documented as polemic, but they do not establish the authorial meaning of Revelation.

### Dream interpretation and divination

Artemidorus makes numerical equality one among many analogical tools. Later dream books, onomantic manuals, and divinatory tables correlate:

- names with planetary or zodiacal rulers;
- questioners’ names with days;
- numerical remainders with favorable answers;
- patient and disease names with prognosis.

These operations often use reduction or division and should not all be called strict isopsephy.

### Magic and amulets

Late-antique practitioners treated letters as simultaneously:

- sounds;
- graphic forms;
- numerical powers;
- divine names;
- cosmic correspondences.

The seven Greek vowels could correspond to seven planets, heavens, tones, or archons. Palindromes and wing-shaped text layouts added visual symmetry. Numerical totals sometimes reinforced a solar year, planetary cycle, or divine identity.

Examples include:

- **ΑΒΡΑΣΑΞ**:
  \[
  Α(1)+Β(2)+Ρ(100)+Α(1)+Σ(200)+Α(1)+Ξ(60)=365.
  \]
  The form is associated with “Basilidian” gems and magical texts. The 365 total is exact; whether the name was invented to produce it or only interpreted afterward is uncertain.

- Long *voces magicae* whose alleged totals are 9,999. These require manuscript-specific checking because one added vowel radically changes the result.

Magic was not a single coherent “Greek Qabalah.” The papyri combine Greek, Egyptian, Jewish, Christian, and other names and practices.

### Christian theology

Isopsephy could support christological symbolism:

- ΙΗΣΟΥΣ = 888;
- Alpha + Omega = 801;
- ΠΕΡΙΣΤΕΡΑ = 801;
- 666 as the beast’s counter-number;
- numerical readings of the 24-letter alphabet.

The Marcosian system is the most explicit early example. Orthodox writers such as Irenaeus preserved it to refute it, yet also used the same numerical technique when considering the beast’s name.

### Epigraphy and commemoration

Explicit isopsephic inscriptions occur in Egypt, Greece, and Asia Minor. They can mark equal verses, theological pairs, or a hidden name. Numerical letters also dated buildings and tombstones, although a date written in alphabetic numerals is not automatically a chronogram: a true chronogram derives the date from letters embedded in a verbal composition.

### Modern Greek lexarithms

From the late twentieth century, Greek popular authors promoted **λεξάριθμοι**, “word-numbers,” as proof that the Greek language was mathematically engineered. Common claims include:

- meaningful Greek words were designed to share totals;
- geometry and scientific constants were encoded in vocabulary;
- the alphabet was created principally as a calculating machine;
- prehistoric markings such as the Dispilio tablet encode the same system;
- the practice descends directly from Pythagoras.

Dimitris Psychoyos’s 2005 article, “The Forgotten Art of Isopsephy and the Magic Number 27,” argues for the numerical priority of the alphabet’s 27-sign structure. His historical documentation of isopsephy deserves separation from the stronger origin thesis, which is disputed. Popular writers Eleftherios Argyropoulos and Konstantinos Dovoris developed much more extensive “mathematical language” theories outside mainstream historical linguistics.

### Modern occultism

Nineteenth- and twentieth-century occultists absorbed Greek numerical symbolism through:

- Renaissance Christian Cabala;
- Eliphas Lévi;
- the Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn;
- Aleister Crowley’s *Sepher Sephiroth* and *777*;
- G. R. S. Mead’s Gnostic studies;
- Kabbalistic and Theosophical readings of Greek divine names.

Crowley’s central numerical vocabulary is primarily Hebrew Qabalah, supplemented by Greek, Latin, and newly invented English correspondences. His work is a documented modern reception of isopsephic reasoning, not an unbroken Greek initiatory tradition.

### Internet calculators

Modern calculators commonly offer:

- standard Greek isopsephy;
- “Jewish gematria” applied to Latin letters;
- reverse ordinal;
- primes;
- squares;
- reductions;
- English ordinal A=1…Z=26;
- sixfold or “Sumerian” English values.

Most of those ciphers are modern inventions. Calling an English A–Z mapping “Greek,” “Jewish,” “Chaldean,” or “Sumerian” does not make it historically descended from the ancient system. A calculator is reliable only to the extent that it discloses its table, normalization, spelling, and treatment of punctuation and diacritics.

### Literature

Authors including Borges, Joyce, Georges Perec, and Oulipo writers used numbers, alphabets, constraints, enumerations, and sometimes Kabbalistic imagery. These affinities should not automatically be classified as Greek isopsephy:

- Perec and Oulipo provide genuine constrained writing, but usually lipograms, combinatorics, and formal restrictions rather than the Milesian table.
- Joyce’s texts contain elaborate number play, but proposed hidden gematrias frequently originate with commentators.
- Borges wrote about Kabbalah, divine names, infinity, and cryptographic interpretation; this is reception of letter mysticism, not documented routine use of Greek isopsephy.

Unless an author supplies a table or an explicit equal sum, numerical correspondences discovered after publication remain conjectural.

## Comparative systems: boundaries and classic examples

These examples belong to the wider requested series, not to Greek isopsephy itself.

### Hebrew gematria

#### Tetragrammaton = 26

\[
יהוה =
י(10)+ה(5)+ו(6)+ה(5)=26.
\]

#### אהבה = אחד = 13

\[
אהבה=א(1)+ה(5)+ב(2)+ה(5)=13.
\]

\[
אחד=א(1)+ח(8)+ד(4)=13.
\]

#### נחש = משיח = 358

\[
נחש=נ(50)+ח(8)+ש(300)=358.
\]

\[
משיח=מ(40)+ש(300)+י(10)+ח(8)=358.
\]

The arithmetic is standard; the theological equation between serpent and Messiah is a later interpretive tradition, not an equivalence asserted in the biblical text.

#### Eliezer = 318

\[
אליעזר =
א(1)+ל(30)+י(10)+ע(70)+ז(7)+ר(200)=318.
\]

Genesis 14:14 says Abraham mustered 318 retainers. Babylonian Talmud *Nedarim* 32a interprets this as Eliezer alone, whose name equals 318; Rashi repeats the tradition. This is a documented rabbinic interpretation, not the plain grammatical meaning of Genesis.

### Arabic abjad

The **basmala**, بسم الله الرحمن الرحيم, is conventionally 786 under the common eastern abjad table:

- بسم = 2 + 60 + 40 = 102
- الله = 1 + 30 + 30 + 5 = 66
- الرحمن = 1 + 30 + 200 + 8 + 40 + 50 = 329
- الرحيم = 1 + 30 + 200 + 8 + 10 + 40 = 289

\[
102+66+329+289=786.
\]

This is a later devotional convention; the Qur’an does not designate 786 as a substitute for the formula.

Arabic and Persian **abjad chronograms** select a word or verse whose letter total gives a Hijri year. Persian **tārīkh-gūʾī** and Ottoman Turkish **tarih düşürme** became highly developed poetic arts. Because Persian and Ottoman spelling can be adjusted, elegant chronograms often include a verbal instruction to add or subtract a “dotted” letter or a phrase.

### Latin and Renaissance tables

Agrippa’s *De occulta philosophia*, printed in its expanded form in 1533, supplied Latin readers with Hebrew, Greek, and related alphabet tables and helped integrate letter-number methods into Christian Cabala and ceremonial magic. There is no single ancient Latin alphabetic isopsephy table equivalent to the mature Greek system; Latin chronograms commonly count only letters that are Roman numerals—I, V, X, L, C, D, M.

### Modern “Pythagorean” and “Chaldean” numerology

The familiar English “Pythagorean” table cycles A–I = 1–9, J–R = 1–9, S–Z = 1–8. Its attribution to Pythagoras is modern and unsupported by Greek sources.

The English “Chaldean” name-number table assigns sound-based values 1–8 in a nonalphabetic arrangement. It likewise lacks documentation from ancient Babylonia. These systems emerged through eighteenth- and nineteenth-century European occult numerology and were popularized by writers such as L. Dow Balliett and Cheiro in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries.

## Controversies and disputes

### 1. Mesopotamian, Greek, or Israelite priority?

**Documented:** Sargon’s name-number wall claim is earlier than Greek and Jewish alphabetic gematria.

**Not documented:** a continuous technical line from Assyrian cuneiform to Greek isopsephy.

**Scholarly reconstruction:** Greek isopsephy arose after the establishment of Greek alphabetic numerals, probably in the cosmopolitan eastern Mediterranean. Greek alphabetic numerical notation is earlier securely documented than a complete Hebrew letter-value system.

**Disputed:** whether Jewish gematria was borrowed from Greek practice, developed from shared Northwest Semitic letter-number habits, or emerged independently once Hebrew letters were used numerically.

The safest conclusion is technological rather than ethnic: stable alphabetic numeral assignments make word-summing possible; the surviving evidence does not identify a single inventor.

### 2. Was it Pythagorean?

Pythagorean traditions assigned metaphysical importance to number. That fact is well documented. Direct evidence that Pythagoras calculated Greek names with the Milesian table is absent.

Problems include:

- Pythagoras lived in the sixth century BCE, before secure evidence for the mature 27-sign numeral system and centuries before secure isopsephy.
- Much “Pythagorean” literature was composed pseudonymously in the Hellenistic and Roman periods.
- Later writers retrojected Platonic, arithmological, and magical ideas onto Pythagoras.

Therefore “Pythagorean isopsephy” is best labeled **later attribution or modern legend**, unless a specific post-classical Pythagorean text is being described.

### 3. Was the alphabet designed for isopsephy?

Psychoyos argues that the 27-sign numerical structure had priority and constrained the Greek alphabet. The mainstream reconstruction instead holds that Greeks adapted the Phoenician alphabet for language, while alphabetic numerals developed within that writing culture. The presence of archaic signs in the numeral series is important evidence but does not demonstrate that phonographic writing was secondary to a numerical master-plan.

Claims that the alphabet was designed to encode π, geometry, Egyptian theology, or every word’s “true mathematical meaning” require independent contemporary evidence. Matching modern calculations are not proof of design.

### 4. The coincidence problem

If a lexicon contains thousands of inflected forms and totals are concentrated in a few-thousand-number range, many words must share totals. Freedom to vary:

- spelling;
- dialect;
- case;
- articles;
- word order;
- titles;
- abbreviations;
- transliteration language;
- inclusion of punctuation or iota;
- reduction method

makes matches still easier.

A defensible intentional isopsephism normally has one or more of these features:

1. The ancient text explicitly says it is equal in value.
2. It prints the numerical total.
3. Corresponding lines are systematically equal.
4. A manuscript preserves working calculations.
5. Contemporary testimony explains the joke.
6. The number is integral to an instruction, oracle, or riddle.

A correspondence first found by searching a digitized corpus is evidence of numerical equality, not automatically of authorial design.

Irenaeus himself supplies the ancient version of this criticism: many names fit 666, so one should not choose rashly.

### 5. Nero, 666, and 616

The Nero hypothesis has real explanatory power:

- Revelation asks readers to calculate a human name.
- Nero was a potent symbol of persecuting imperial power.
- Hebrew נרון קסר can total 666.
- dropping the final nun gives 616.
- both numbers are ancient readings.

But its weaknesses are also real:

- the required Hebrew spelling of Caesar without yod is contested;
- Revelation’s Greek-reading audience is not told to transliterate into Hebrew;
- Irenaeus, close to the period, does not know or report the Nero solution;
- many Greek and Hebrew names can be manipulated to fit;
- the 616 variant might have another origin.

It should therefore be called a strong and widely held scholarly reconstruction, not a decipherment proven by an ancient key.

### 6. Irenaeus as source for the Marcosians

Irenaeus is indispensable and hostile. His report is documented evidence for second-century controversy, but not a neutral transcript. The original Greek survives incompletely; much of *Against Heresies* depends on an ancient Latin translation. Modern arithmetic can confirm the sums, but it cannot verify every theological linkage independently of Irenaeus.

### 7. *Pistis Sophia* and retrospective gematria

The text genuinely organizes its cosmos by numbers. It does not follow that every Greek title reconstructed from its Coptic wording was intentionally chosen for a modernly calculated total. Mead and Bond’s gematric appendices are historically important occult reception. They are not equivalent to an explicit ancient statement such as the Smyrna graffito’s **ἰσόψηφα**.

### 8. Magical names and unstable spelling

Magical formulas were copied, transliterated, and altered. A claimed total may depend on:

- a modern editor’s reconstruction;
- deciding whether a damaged character is omicron or omega;
- splitting one magical name into several;
- including or excluding repeated vowels.

Every such total needs a papyrus identifier and critical transcription. “The magical papyri use 9,999” is too broad without them.

### 9. Dornseiff and Barry

Franz Dornseiff’s *Das Alphabet in Mystik und Magie* (1922) remains foundational because it assembled literary, papyrological, magical, Jewish, Christian, and Islamic alphabet symbolism. Its strengths are range and philological learning; its limitations include its age, pre-Nag-Hammadi context, and the volume of subsequent papyrus and inscription discoveries.

Kieren Barry’s *The Greek Qabalah* (1999) made this material accessible and supplied a useful dictionary of values. It is influential in occult circles, but its synthetic claims should be checked against critical editions. “Greek Qabalah” is a modern comparative label, not the ordinary ancient name of a unified Greek institution.

### 10. Forgeries, pseudepigrapha, and false antiquity

Relevant categories include:

- the *Sibylline Oracles*: ancient pseudepigraphy, not a modern forgery;
- pseudo-Pythagorean writings: Hellenistic or Roman works attributed to earlier sages;
- Renaissance “ancient wisdom” genealogies: mixtures of genuine texts and retrospective synthesis;
- modern English “Pythagorean” and “Chaldean” numerology: modern systems carrying ancient brand names;
- calculator ciphers called “Jewish,” “Sumerian,” or “Greek” despite using English letters;
- alleged prehistoric Greek numerical writing on the Dispilio tablet: disputed and unconnected to the Milesian system;
- arbitrary web lists in which spelling is silently changed to achieve a desired total.

### 11. “Glycon = 3663”

This is not supported by Lucian or by ordinary arithmetic. ΓΛΥΚΩΝ = 1303. The 3663 reference belongs to scholarly discussion of a magical formula, not the serpent-god’s name. This is a useful example of how citations drift when secondary lists merge unrelated ancient materials.

## Open questions

1. When and where did the complete 27-sign Milesian numeral system reach stable form?
2. Was word-summing independently discovered more than once after alphabetic numerals became common?
3. Are there earlier Greek isopsephisms still unrecognized because no total or explicit label survives?
4. How should the Petosiris annotation be dated relative to the original epigram?
5. What is the precise relationship between Greek practice and early Jewish gematria?
6. Was Revelation’s 666 calculated in Greek, Hebrew, or through another convention?
7. Is 666 or 616 earlier in Revelation, and was either variant an interpretive correction?
8. Which alleged isopsephisms in the Greek Magical Papyri survive critical textual scrutiny?
9. To what extent do Coptic texts preserve Greek numerical wordplay lost in translation?
10. Which Byzantine inscriptions are genuine word-based chronograms rather than ordinary alphabetic dates?
11. Can statistical models distinguish intentionally composed equalities from lexical collisions while controlling for inflection and orthography?
12. How widely was isopsephy understood outside educated scribal, poetic, magical, and religious circles?
13. Were tables such as P.Oxy. XLV 3239 school exercises, poets’ tools, divinatory aids, or multipurpose reference sheets?
14. How much did modern occult “Greek Qabalah” reconstruct from ancient sources, and how much did it import from Hebrew Kabbalah?
15. What is the earliest secure printed use of the English labels “Greek gematria” and “Greek Qabalah”?
16. Which modern Greek lexarithmic claims were independently proposed, and which derive from Barry, Psychoyos, or internet databases?
