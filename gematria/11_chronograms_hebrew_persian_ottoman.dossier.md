# Chronograms across the letter-number cultures: Hebrew, Persian, Ottoman, Armenian, Cyrillic: Research Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary labels

This dossier distinguishes:

- **[Documented]**: directly attested in a surviving text, manuscript, inscription, object, or early printed book.
- **[Scholarly reconstruction]**: a historical conclusion supported by comparative, palaeographic, linguistic, or contextual evidence.
- **[Tradition]**: a claim transmitted within a religious or esoteric community, whether or not independently demonstrable.
- **[Disputed]**: a serious proposal for which specialists disagree or the evidence is incomplete.
- **[Legend]**: an etiological or sacred-history narrative not supported by contemporary evidence.
- **[Modern invention]**: a demonstrably recent system presented as a new discovery, revival, or—sometimes misleadingly—an ancient practice.

A fundamental distinction governs the whole subject:

1. **Alphabetic numeration** writes an ordinary number with letters: e.g. Cyrillic letters under a titlo, or Armenian letters representing a manuscript year.
2. **Gematria/isopsephy/ḥisāb al-jummal** sums the numerical values of the letters of a word.
3. **A chronogram** is a deliberately chosen meaningful word, phrase, or verse whose letter-sum gives the date of the event it describes.

Every chronogram rests on letter-numerals, but most letter-numerals are not chronograms.

| 6 | ו | vav | 70 | ע | ayin |
| 7 | ז | zayin | 80 | פ, ף | pe |

In ordinary gematria the five final forms equal their medial forms. Under one convention called **mispar gadol**, ך ם ן ף ץ may instead equal 500, 600, 700, 800, and 900. “Mispar gadol” is not perfectly standardized: some authors use the term for expanded letter names rather than the 500–900 final series.

Vowel points, cantillation, punctuation, spaces, and ornamental marks have no value. Matres lectionis—א ה ו י—count when actually written because they are consonantal letters in the spelling. Thus plene and defective spellings can produce different sums.

## 1.4 Arabic and Perso-Ottoman abjad table

The eastern abjad order is memorized in the formula:

**أبجد هوز حطي كلمن سعفص قرشت ثخذ ضظغ**

*abjad hawwaz ḥuṭṭī kalaman saʿfaṣ qarashat thakhadh ḍaẓagh*

| Value | Letter | Value | Letter | Value | Letter | Value | Letter |
|---:|:---:|---:|:---:|---:|:---:|---:|:---:|
| 1 | ا | 8 | ح | 60 | س | 400 | ت |
| 2 | ب | 9 | ط | 70 | ع | 500 | ث |
| 3 | ج | 10 | ي | 80 | ف | 600 | خ |
| 4 | د | 20 | ك | 90 | ص | 700 | ذ |
| 5 | ه | 30 | ل | 100 | ق | 800 | ض |
| 6 | و | 40 | م | 200 | ر | 900 | ظ |
| 7 | ز | 50 | ن | 300 | ش | 1000 | غ |

Persian additions normally take the value of their Arabic base form:

| Persian/Ottoman letter | Normal value | Basis |
|:---:|---:|---|
| پ | 2 | ب |
| چ | 3 | ج |
| ژ | 7 | ز |
| گ | 20 | ك/ک |
| ڭ and related kāf forms | normally 20 | ك |

Common rules:

- Short vowels not written in the consonantal text do not count.
- Written long-vowel letters ا و ي count.
- Diacritics do not count.
- Hamza is variously ignored or treated with alif according to spelling convention.
- Tāʾ marbūṭa ة is normally valued as ه = 5, though local practices vary.
- Alif maqṣūra ى normally equals ي = 10.
- The definite article’s written letters count even where assimilation affects pronunciation.
- Persian ezāfe normally contributes nothing unless a written yā is present.
- Ottoman spelling, not modern Turkish pronunciation, controls the calculation.
- Western or Maghribi abjad assigns different positions to ṣād/ḍād and related later letters; one must identify the regional table before calculating.

Arabic script descends graphically through Nabataean Aramaic, but the abjad numerical series is not simply the inherited Nabataean numeral notation. Nabataean numerals were a separate cumulative system. The history is therefore an inheritance of **alphabet order and letter culture**, not a direct conversion of each Nabataean numeral sign into an Arabic letter-number. [Encyclopaedia Iranica’s “Abjad”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/abjad/) and Stephen Chrisomalis’s comparative work are especially important here.

| Ա | 1 | Ժ | 10 | Ճ | 100 | Ռ | 1000 |

| Գ | 3 | Լ | 30 | Յ | 300 | Վ | 3000 |
| Դ | 4 | Խ | 40 | Ն | 400 | Տ | 4000 |
| Ե | 5 | Ծ | 50 | Շ | 500 | Ր | 5000 |
| Զ | 6 | Կ | 60 | Ո | 600 | Ց | 6000 |
| Է | 7 | Հ | 70 | Չ | 700 | Ւ | 7000 |
| Ը | 8 | Ձ | 80 | Պ | 800 | Փ | 8000 |
| Թ | 9 | Ղ | 90 | Ջ | 900 | Ք | 9000 |

The later letters **Օ** and **Ֆ**, added in the medieval period, are not part of the original 36-place numeral table. Historical notation uses combinations of letters and scribal overlines or punctuation; multiplying conventions for large values occur, but exact practice varies by period.

**Critical distinction:** a manuscript date written as Armenian numeral letters is documented alphabetic numeration. A meaningful Armenian phrase deliberately engineered to total that year would be a chronogram, but this is far less abundantly documented than ordinary Armenian letter-dates.

The Great Armenian Era begins in **552 CE**. A manuscript year normally converts by adding 551, with caution because the Armenian year began in summer; hence catalogues may print a conversion such as 1169/70. This is documented in scholarship on Armenian colophons.

## 1.7 Cyrillic numerals

Cyrillic numeration follows Greek numerical values rather than the modern Cyrillic alphabet’s phonetic order. Historical glyphs vary considerably by place and font.

| Value | Letter | Value | Letter | Value | Letter |
|---:|:---:|---:|:---:|---|:---:|
| 1 | а | 10 | і/ї | 100 | р |
| 2 | в | 20 | к | 200 | с |
| 3 | г | 30 | л | 300 | т |
| 4 | д | 40 | м | 400 | у/ѵ |
| 5 | є/е | 50 | н | 500 | ф |
| 6 | ѕ | 60 | ѯ | 600 | х |
| 7 | з | 70 | о | 700 | ѱ |
| 8 | и | 80 | п | 800 | ѡ |
| 9 | ѳ | 90 | ч | 900 | ц |

A **titlo** is written over numeral letters to distinguish them from text. A thousands sign, conventionally **҂**, placed before a letter multiplies it by 1,000: ҂а = 1000. Order conventions are not wholly uniform; numbers 11–19 commonly follow spoken order, “one-on-ten,” while larger sequences ordinarily place higher values first.

Dates on Orthodox icons, manuscripts, bells, coins, and foundations may use the Byzantine **Anno Mundi** era, traditionally beginning 5508 BCE. Conversion requires attention to whether the date falls before or after the Byzantine New Year on 1 September. Peter I’s reforms gradually substituted Arabic digits; Cyrillic dates nevertheless remained in ecclesiastical and decorative use. Peter’s coins provide documented transitional mixtures such as letter and Arabic-numeral dates.

Again, this is usually **letter-dating**, not the summing of a meaningful Slavonic sentence.

# 2. The system in detail

## 2.1 Ordinary summation and equivalence

For a written string \(L_1L_2...L_n\),

\[
V(\text{string})=\sum_{i=1}^{n} V(L_i).
\]

Two words are numerically equivalent when their sums under the **same alphabet, spelling, and method** agree. Equality of sums does not establish semantic, historical, or causal connection by itself. Traditional exegetes treat equality variously as a hint, mnemonic, homiletic support, or disclosure of an occult affinity.

Ordinary ancient and medieval calculations are normally **additive**, not digital-root reductions. Reducing totals to a digit is characteristic of *mispar qaṭan* and modern numerology, not the default rule of Hebrew gematria, Greek isopsephy, or abjad chronograms.

## 2.2 Named Hebrew methods

- **מספר הכרחי, mispar hekhreḥi**: standard 1–400 table.
- **מספר גדול, mispar gadol**: usually finals ךםןףץ = 500–900; nomenclature varies.
- **מספר קטן, mispar qaṭan**: suppress zeros in letter values—א/י/ק = 1, ב/כ/ר = 2, etc.—or reduce the word total; sources must state which.
- **מספר סידורי, mispar siduri**: ordinal positions 1–22. This is much more visible in modern handbooks and calculators than in classical exegesis.
- **מספר קדמי, mispar qidmi**: cumulative or triangular value of each letter.
- **מספר פרטי, mispar peraṭi**: squares individual letter values.
- **מילוי, millui**, or **מספר שמי, mispar shemi**: spell each letter-name and total it.
- **כולל, kolel**: add one for the word or conceptual whole; expanded modern usage sometimes adds the number of words.
- **אתב״ש, atbash**: substitution א↔ת, ב↔ש, ג↔ר, etc., then interpretation or summation.
- **אלב״ם, albam**: exchange the two eleven-letter halves of the alphabet.
- **תמורה, temurah**: general letter substitution.
- **נוטריקון, notarikon**: acronymic or acrostic expansion; related to letter interpretation but not intrinsically numerical.
- **צירוף אותיות, ṣeruf otiyyot**: combination/permutation of letters, especially in Abraham Abulafia.

The classification of all these operations as “gematria” is itself partly modern. Medieval Kabbalists commonly distinguished gematria, notarikon, and temurah.

## 2.3 Worked Hebrew examples

### Eliezer = 318

**[Documented rabbinic interpretation]** Genesis 14:14 says Abraham armed 318 retainers. Babylonian Talmud, Nedarim 32a, reports the interpretation that only Eliezer went, because his name totals 318:

**אליעזר**

- א = 1
- ל = 30
- י = 10
- ע = 70
- ז = 7
- ר = 200

\[
1+30+10+70+7+200=318.
\]

[Rashi on Genesis 14:14](https://www.sefaria.org/Rashi_on_Genesis.14.14) transmits the same midrashic reading; [Nedarim 32a](https://www.sefaria.org/Nedarim.32a.3-4) is the explicit Talmudic witness.

**Status:** the text’s existence is documented; that Genesis’s author intended this equivalence is a traditional exegetical claim, not established by the narrative itself. Abraham ibn Ezra later criticized reliance on such interpretation, an early Jewish articulation of the arbitrariness problem.

### אהבה = אחד = 13

**אהבה**, *ahavah*, “love”:

\[
א(1)+ה(5)+ב(2)+ה(5)=13.
\]

**אחד**, *eḥad*, “one”:

\[
א(1)+ח(8)+ד(4)=13.
\]

**[Traditional/medieval-modern homiletic equivalence]** The arithmetic is exact. Its theological use—love as unity—is interpretive; it is not by itself proof that either word was coined for the equation.

### נחש = משיח = 358

**נחש**, *naḥash*, “serpent”:

\[
נ(50)+ח(8)+ש(300)=358.
\]

**משיח**, *mashiaḥ*, “messiah”:

\[
מ(40)+ש(300)+י(10)+ח(8)=358.
\]

**[Kabbalistic/occult tradition]** The arithmetic is documented and widely used in later Kabbalistic and Christian-Hermetic literature. The inference that serpent and Messiah are metaphysically interchangeable is sectarian interpretation, not a consequence of arithmetic.

### The Tetragrammaton = 26

**יהוה**:

\[
י(10)+ה(5)+ו(6)+ה(5)=26.
\]

**[Documented traditional calculation]** This underlies extensive Jewish and Christian Kabbalistic symbolism. It should not be confused with a reduced value:

\[
2+6=8,
\]

which is a later reduction operation and not part of the standard total.

### Chai = 18

**חי**, “life”:

\[
ח(8)+י(10)=18.
\]

**[Documented modern custom]** Gifts in multiples of 18 are now common in Jewish communities. The arithmetic is old; evidence for the modern donation convention is comparatively recent.

## 2.4 Greek and Christian examples

### Ἰησοῦς = 888

**ΙΗΣΟΥΣ**:

\[
Ι(10)+Η(8)+Σ(200)+Ο(70)+Υ(400)+Σ(200)=888.
\]

**[Documented late-antique calculation]** The number became a Christian counter-symbol to 666. Irenaeus reports Marcosian numerical theology, though he is a hostile witness and sometimes paraphrases or mocks his opponents.

### περιστερά, “dove” = 801

\[
π(80)+ε(5)+ρ(100)+ι(10)+σ(200)+τ(300)+ε(5)+ρ(100)+α(1)=801.
\]

\[
80+5+100+10+200+300+5+100+1=801.
\]

**[Documented in Irenaeus’s account of the Marcosians]** Irenaeus, *Against Heresies* I.14–16, reports their use of the dove’s number and alphabetic cosmology. The sum 801 is also α + ω = 1 + 800, facilitating Alpha-and-Omega symbolism. See [Irenaeus I.15](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/0103115.htm).

### The Pompeian love graffito

The celebrated Greek graffito is conventionally translated:

> “I love her whose number is 545.”

**[Documented inscription; identity unknown]** It demonstrates socially playful isopsephy: the beloved’s name was omitted but recoverable to persons who knew it. It does **not** permit modern readers to identify her uniquely, because many names can share 545. This is an ancient demonstration of both the charm and the underdetermination of the method.

### Nero Caesar = 666

Using the Hebrew spelling normally proposed from Greek **Nerōn Kaisar**:

**נרון קסר**

\[
נ(50)+ר(200)+ו(6)+ן(50)+ק(100)+ס(60)+ר(200)=666.
\]

Dropping final nun to reflect Latin *Nero*:

**נרו קסר**

\[
50+200+6+100+60+200=616.
\]

**[Influential scholarly reconstruction; disputed in details]** This elegantly explains both Revelation 13:18’s 666 and the ancient variant 616. Yet difficulties remain:

- Revelation is Greek but the proposed calculation is Hebrew.
- The spelling קסר omits the yod found in the common rabbinic קיסר.
- Multiple ancient names total 666.
- One cannot assume that the 616 variant was deliberately recalculated rather than accidentally corrupted.

The Nero interpretation remains widely regarded as plausible, but not demonstrable beyond doubt. A recent critical reassessment emphasizes the spelling problem, while other epigraphic studies argue that consonantal קסר and cross-script calculations are historically defensible. See the [Journal of Early Christian History study](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/2222582X.2016.1218996), the [2025 JTS reassessment](https://academic.oup.com/jts/article/76/1/109/8043235), and Irenaeus, *Against Heresies* V.30, who already knew the 616 variant but preferred 666.

## 2.5 The basmala = 786

**بسم الله الرحمن الرحيم**

Ignoring spaces and vocalization:

- بسم = ب 2 + س 60 + م 40 = 102
- الله = ا 1 + ل 30 + ل 30 + ه 5 = 66
- الرحمن = ا 1 + ل 30 + ر 200 + ح 8 + م 40 + ن 50 = 329
- الرحيم = ا 1 + ل 30 + ر 200 + ح 8 + ي 10 + م 40 = 289

\[
102+66+329+289=786.
\]

**[Documented arithmetic; later devotional convention]** The number 786 became especially prominent as shorthand in South Asian Muslim culture. It is not a Qurʾanic command, canonical ḥadīth, or universally accepted substitute for the basmala. Some Muslim jurists accept it as conventional shorthand; others reject it as innovation or as lacking religious significance.

## 2.6 Persian chronogram mechanics

A mature Persian *mādda-ye tārīkh* usually has:

1. an occasion—death, accession, completion of a book, building, victory;
2. a cue such as “I sought its date,” “reason said,” or “the pen wrote”;
3. a culminating hemistich whose abjad total gives the Hijrī year;
4. semantic aptness: the phrase should praise, mourn, describe, or pun on the event;
5. sometimes an adjustment signaled in the poem: add or remove the value of a named word or letter.

Johannes Thomas Pieter de Bruijn distinguishes:

- **تاریخ صوری, tārīkh-e ṣūrī**: explicit lexical date.
- **تاریخ معنوی, tārīkh-e maʿnawī**: meaningful abjad phrase.
- combined forms;
- **تاریخ تعمیه, tārīkh-e taʿmiya**: riddling or adjusted chronogram.

Example recorded in *Encyclopaedia Iranica*:

**میل بهشت**, *meyl-e behešt*, “inclination toward Paradise,” totals 787 AH.

Reported analysis:

- میل = 80
- بهشت = 707

\[
80+707=787.
\]

This supplies Monday, 21 Ṣafar 787 AH / 3 April 1385 in its source context. [Iranica, “Mādda Tārīkh”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/madda-tarik-chronogram/).

Another Persian riddling example dates the martyrdom of Imam Ḥosayn to 60 AH through the “head” removed from **دین**, *dīn*: the selected remaining letters yā and nūn give

\[
ی(10)+ن(50)=60.
\]

The poetry supplies instructions for what to remove; without those instructions this is not a reproducible calculation.

## 2.7 Ottoman *tarih düşürmek*

Ottoman poets adopted and elaborated Persian chronogram conventions. A successful *tarih* could be a single word, a hemistich, a full line, or an entire poem whose last line concealed the year. Technical descriptions distinguish such operations as:

- **tam tarih**: an exact/full chronogram;
- **noktalı/muʿcem tarih**: only dotted letters count;
- **mühmel tarih**: only undotted letters count;
- **lafzen ve manen tarih**: date both explicitly and through meaning/value;
- **taʿmiyeli tarih**: an indicated addition or subtraction is required.

**Tarih düşürmek** literally pictures the poet as “dropping” or casting the date into language. It became a recognized literary accomplishment and a form of courtly service.

Documented subjects include:

- births and circumcisions;
- appointments and accessions;
- victories and treaties;
- deaths;
- composition or copying of books;
- mosques, schools, palaces, baths, bridges, inns;
- fountains and public waterworks;
- restorations after fires or earthquakes.

On 101 examined Ottoman stelae from the Merdivenköy, Manṣûr Baba, and Gözcü Baba cemeteries, Edhem Eldem found 38 with a chronogram in addition to an explicit date. This is strong object-level evidence that chronograms were not merely anthology games but part of urban funerary practice. [Eldem’s study](https://www.persee.fr/doc/anatm_1297-8094_1991_num_2_1_878).

## 2.8 Hebrew dates and *lifraṭ qaṭan*

Hebrew dates commonly omit the millennium. The formula:

**לפרט קטן**, *lifraṭ qaṭan*, “according to the small enumeration,”

signals that 5,000 is omitted from an *Anno Mundi* date. Thus a printed total 244 means AM 5244, corresponding to 1483/84 CE depending on the month.

The Soncino Talmud chronogram traditionally cited as the earliest printed Hebrew example is:

**גמרא**, *Gemara*:

\[
ג(3)+מ(40)+ר(200)+א(1)=244.
\]

Read *lifraṭ qaṭan*:

\[
5000+244=5244\ \text{AM}\approx1483/84\ \text{CE}.
\]

**[Documented early printing tradition]** Bibliographic descriptions sometimes normalize this as “1484.” Exact conversion requires month and Jewish New Year boundaries.

Hebrew printers highlighted the operative letters by enlarged type, dots, inverted letters, ornamental marks, or biblical phrases. When typography is lost in transcription, identifying which letters were intended to count can become difficult.

The earliest Hebrew literary chronogram is often said to occur in a poem by Judah al-Ḥarizi dated 1205. That statement is widely repeated in reference works, but a critical edition and exact line should be cited before treating it as an unquestioned terminus ante quem.

# 3. Origins: dated and placed

## 3.1 Before alphabetic numerals: Sargon II

Sargon II’s Khorsabad inscriptions say that the wall of Dūr-Šarrukīn was built to a circumference of approximately **16,280 cubits**, corresponding to “the reading/name-number of my name.” The city was inaugurated in 706 BCE; the relevant inscriptions belong to the late eighth century BCE. Modern editions vary between 16,280 and 16,283 because of textual versions and reconstructions.

**[Documented wording; disputed mechanism]** The royal inscription really associates a monumental measurement with the king’s name. What remains uncertain is how the number was obtained. Friedrich Delitzsch proposed in 1878 that values of cuneiform signs or components in Šarru-kīn produced the total. Later Assyriologists have offered other explanations. Calling it “gematria” is a useful analogy but risks importing alphabetic rules into a syllabic/logographic cuneiform setting.

It is therefore:

- the earliest major **name-number/monumental-measure** parallel;
- not securely the ancestor of Greek, Hebrew, or Arabic alphabetic gematria;
- not evidence that a continuous table passed from Assyria to later alphabets.

See the [Royal Inscriptions of Sargon II](https://build-oracc.museum.upenn.edu/rinap/downloads/Frame_RINAP_2.pdf) and the [Israel Museum cylinder translation](https://museum.imj.org.il/journal/archive/2018-2019/pdf/cylinder.pdf).

## 3.2 Alphabetic numerals as the necessary substrate

Northwest Semitic, Greek, Hebrew, Syriac, and Arabic traditions are genealogically related at the alphabetic level, but they did not all use identical numeral notations at identical dates.

The Greek Milesian system supplied 27 signs in three enneads. Hebrew and Syriac used the inherited Semitic consonant order. Arabic preserved the old abjad order for numeration even after its everyday pedagogical alphabet was rearranged by letter shape.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** Gematria becomes possible only after letters have stable numerical values. It need not arise automatically: an alphabet can serve as a numeral system for centuries before writers begin deliberately composing semantic word-sums.

## 3.3 Greek isopsephy

Secure literary and epigraphic evidence for word-sum play becomes visible in the Hellenistic period. A mid-third-century BCE inscription connected with the tomb of Petosiris at Hermoupolis Magna is among the earliest substantial poetic evidence discussed by recent scholarship. Apollonius of Perga in the third century BCE knew sophisticated alphabet-number operations.

By the Roman period isopsephy was socially familiar:

- lovers hid names behind totals;
- poets produced equal-sum lines;
- riddles encoded personal names;
- Christians and Gnostics computed divine names;
- magical texts combined letters, vowels, and numbers.

Leonides of Alexandria, first century CE, composed elaborate isopsephic epigrams in which lines or couplets balance numerically. See the open-access volume [*Poetry and Number in Graeco-Roman Antiquity*](https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/D024A7FBFCE9A2E66B4C4400634D3DBB/9781009123044AR.pdf).

## 3.4 Jewish gematria

The noun גימטריא is probably a Greek loan, most often connected with γεωμετρία, “geometry,” though the semantic path and exact etymology have been debated. The resemblance to γραμματεία has also influenced explanation. Sambursky’s study remains central to this terminological question.

Gematria appears among the **thirty-two hermeneutical rules attributed to Rabbi Eliezer ben Yose ha-Gelili**, traditionally the twenty-ninth rule. The list is preserved in post-Talmudic textual form; attributing every rule and wording directly to the second-century rabbi is therefore a **traditional attribution**, not a securely dated autograph.

The Babylonian and Palestinian rabbinic corpora contain explicit calculations. Nedarim 32a’s Eliezer = 318 is unambiguous. These sources establish late-antique Jewish use, but they do not establish that gematria was a standard compositional device of all biblical authors.

**Priority judgment:** Greek evidence for developed word-isopsephy is earlier and clearer than securely datable rabbinic evidence. Jewish use could reflect Hellenistic cultural contact, an independent exploitation of Hebrew numerals, or both. Direct one-way borrowing cannot be demonstrated case by case.

## 3.5 Marcosians and Irenaeus, c. 180 CE

Irenaeus’s *Adversus haereses* I.14–16 preserves a detailed hostile account of the Marcosians:

- the 24 Greek letters and their sounds;
- alphabetic emanations;
- Jesus = 888;
- dove = 801;
- Alpha and Omega;
- combinations of letters, syllables, and numbers.

**[Documented report, polemical transmission]** Irenaeus is indispensable because much Marcosian literature is lost, but his purpose is refutation. His arithmetic can be checked; his characterization of Marcosian motives cannot be treated as neutral ethnography.

## 3.6 *Pistis Sophia*

The Coptic *Pistis Sophia*, probably compiled in the third–fourth centuries from earlier materials, preserves Greek letter names, numerical invocations, repeated vowels, seals, and cosmic number symbolism.

**[Documented late-antique Christian-Gnostic letter mysticism]** Not every number or vowel sequence in the work is an isopsephic equation. Modern occultists sometimes calculate strings that the surviving text treats as voces magicae or ritual formulae. Such reverse engineering must be labeled modern interpretation unless the text itself instructs the reader to sum them.

## 3.7 Arabic abjad

Arabic script developed from Nabataean Aramaic, while the numerical order preserves the older Semitic alphabet sequence. Because several Arabic consonants emerged by adding dots to a shared base form, the ordinary teaching order was eventually reorganized by shape, but the abjad mnemonic retained numerical functions.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** It is inaccurate to say simply that “the Nabataeans used the modern Arabic abjad table.” Nabataean epigraphy employed separate numeral signs. Early Islamic Arabic evidence must be assessed manuscript by manuscript.

The earliest dated Arabic inscription presently known, the Zuhayr inscription, is dated 24 AH/644–45 CE, but it gives its date lexically; it is not proof of a poetic abjad chronogram. Scholarship reports no secure monumental abjad numeral inscriptions from the seventh through tenth centuries, while an astronomical ephemeris of 931/32 is an early technical example. Literary chronograms appear later. [Thomann’s study](https://www.sciamvs.org/files/SCIAMVS_19_167-200_Thomann.pdf).

## 3.8 Persian origins and flourishing

Persian verse incorporated dates from an early period, initially by spelling out the year. The fully developed meaningful abjad chronogram becomes prominent later; *Iranica* dates its great popularity to the ninth/fifteenth century.

Khaqani (c. 1120–c. 1190) is associated with early sophisticated chronological wordplay, but claims that the entire mature genre was already fixed under the Ghaznavids should be qualified. Ghaznavid Persian monumental poetry is documented from the eleventh century onward; not every dated inscription is an abjad chronogram.

From Timurid and Safavid times the form became an arena of virtuosity. Moḥtašam Kāšānī reportedly embedded **1,128 chronograms** in six quatrains for Shah Esmāʿīl II’s accession in 984 AH/1576—an extreme demonstration made possible by multiple selectable words and adjustment techniques.

More than half of the examples in Ḥājj Ḥosayn Nakhjavānī’s large collection come from Qajar Iran. Chronogram poems continued in twentieth-century literary journals, showing survival rather than abrupt extinction. [Iranica, “Chronograms”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/chronograms-pers/).

## 3.9 Ottoman development

The genre passed from Persian into Ottoman Turkish, where it became exceptionally visible in public inscriptions. Major practitioners included:

- **Nâbî** (1642–1712), poet of moral and social reflection who wrote occasional chronograms;
- **Adanalı Sürûrî** (1752–1814), celebrated as an *ebced* master and prolific chronogrammist;
- **Zîver Paşa** (1793–1862), whose lines appear prominently on nineteenth-century Ottoman monuments and fountains.

The art joined calligraphy, architecture, poetry, and patronage. A poet could commemorate a patron’s public work while displaying technical control, and the inscription could give posterity both praise and date.

## 3.10 Armenian, Georgian, Cyrillic, and Ethiopic comparison

- **Armenian:** Mesrop Mashtots’s alphabet was created in the early fifth century. The complete classical alphabet supports a Greek-style 1–9000 system. Medieval manuscript colophons routinely record era, scribe, patron, place, and hardship. This is a securely documented letter-date culture.
- **Georgian:** the oldest generally accepted Georgian inscriptions are fifth century. The attribution of the Georgian alphabet to King Parnavaz in the third century BCE is Georgian tradition; attribution to Mashtots appears in Armenian tradition; neither is established by contemporary inscriptional evidence. Letter numerals reflect strong Greek structural influence.
- **Cyrillic:** developed in the First Bulgarian Empire in the late ninth–tenth centuries. Numerical values were assigned primarily to letters with Greek equivalents. Titlo dates are documented on manuscripts, icons, bells, coins, and church objects.
- **Ethiopic:** Geʿez numeral signs belong to the wider eastern Christian manuscript world. They furnish exact dates and pagination but do not support claims for an extensive Ethiopic gematria genre without specific textual witnesses.

# 4. Uses

## 4.1 Exegesis

### Talmud and Midrash

Gematria normally serves as an **asmachta**, a support or hint, rather than the sole foundation of a legal ruling. Its rhetorical force depends upon an already meaningful association.

Rabbinic examples range from straightforward equivalence to extracting hidden quantities. The Jewish Encyclopedia judged only a small subset of proposed biblical gematrias highly probable as intentional textual devices; this early scholarly caution remains useful. [Jewish Encyclopedia, “Gematria”](https://jewishencyclopediadev.sefaria.org/articles/4390-cipher).

### Zohar and medieval Kabbalah

The Zohar uses letter symbolism, permutations, divine names, and numerical correspondences as part of a broader symbolic reading of Torah. The essential point is not that every Zoharic doctrine is produced by addition; gematria participates in a network of:

- sefirotic symbolism;
- letter form;
- spelling;
- divine-name expansion;
- notarikon;
- biblical intertext;
- homily.

### Abraham Abulafia

Abraham Abulafia (1240–after 1291) transformed letter work into a disciplined contemplative practice. His **ḥokhmat ha-ṣeruf**, “science of combination,” used:

- letter permutation;
- vocalization;
- recitation;
- breath;
- head movements;
- divine names;
- selected gematrias.

He studied *Sefer Yeṣirah* and its commentaries in Barcelona and credited Baruch Togarmi as a teacher. Gershom Scholem interpreted him primarily through ecstatic or prophetic Kabbalah; Moshe Idel reconstructed the experiential and linguistic techniques in greater detail. It would be misleading to reduce Abulafia to a calculator: numerical equality was subordinate to an embodied program of linguistic meditation.

### The Baal Shem and Hasidism

Israel ben Eliezer, the Baal Shem Tov (c. 1700–1760), and later Hasidic teachers used letter symbolism and gematria homiletically. Many spectacular numerical teachings circulating under his name are preserved only in later disciples’ collections.

**Status:** Hasidic use is documented; attribution of an individual saying to the historical Baal Shem Tov must be checked against the earliest printed witness.

---

## 4.2 Magic, amulets, and talismans

### Greek magical papyri

The Greek magical papyri combine:

- alphabetic sequences;
- repeated vowels;
- divine and angelic names;
- number signs;
- geometrical figures;
- voces magicae;
- occasionally isopsephic interpretation.

It is methodologically unsafe to assume that every apparently numerical string was generated by isopsephy. Some are sound patterns, scribal symbols, or ritual diagrams.

### *Sefer Raziel*

The printed *Sefer Raziel ha-Malʾakh* is a medieval compilation assembled from heterogeneous Jewish magical and cosmological materials, with important connections to the German Pietists and Eleazar of Worms.

**[Legend]** The book says or is said to contain wisdom given by the angel Raziel to Adam.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** Its surviving form is medieval, layered, and pseudepigraphic—not an antediluvian artifact. It includes divine names, angels, seals, zodiacal material, amulet instructions, and gematria.

### Arabic *ʿilm al-ḥurūf* and *awfāq*

In Islamicate letter science, letters could be treated as:

- sounds of revelation;
- numerical entities;
- cosmological principles;
- elements assigned to planets, zodiacal signs, and humors;
- ingredients for talismans.

A **wafq**, plural **awfāq**, is a magic square with a constant row, column, and usually diagonal sum. Letters or divine names may replace numbers through abjad values.

Aḥmad al-Būnī, active around 1225, became the most famous name attached to this literature. Modern scholarship warns that the enormous “Būnian corpus” is composite and that many works were expanded or attributed to him after his death. Treating the modern printed *Shams al-maʿārif al-kubrā* as a unitary thirteenth-century autograph is untenable. [Recent survey of the Būnian corpus](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-032-28419-8_9).

### *Picatrix*

The Latin *Picatrix* derives from the Arabic *Ghāyat al-ḥakīm*, composed in al-Andalus in the eleventh century. It transmits astral magic, planetary correspondences, suffumigations, images, names, and numerical structures.

**[Documented transmission]** It influenced European astral magic.

**Qualification:** it is not simply a “gematria manual,” and its magic squares and names should not all be retroactively classified as abjad chronograms.

---

## 4.3 Buildings, tombstones, fountains, and books

Chronograms were durable social technologies:

- **patronage:** praise the commissioner while dating the commission;
- **dedication:** bind an institution’s founding date to a pious phrase;
- **mourning:** compress an epitaph and year into the same line;
- **memory:** create a memorable verbal key to a date;
- **professional prestige:** exhibit poetic and calligraphic skill;
- **ambiguity management:** an explicit numeral was often added because a chronogram could be miscopied or miscalculated.

### Hebrew

Hebrew chronograms appear in:

- manuscript colophons;
- tombstones;
- synagogue inscriptions;
- title pages;
- printers’ colophons;
- poems marking expulsions, deaths, fires, and communal events.

Printers often used a biblical verse relevant to the book and marked the date-letters typographically. Loss of those distinctions in cataloguing produces false totals.

### Persian

Persian *mādda-ye tārīkh* appears in:

- court histories;
- *dīvāns*;
- saints’ shrines;
- funerary monuments;
- palaces, mosques, and madrasas;
- book completions;
- successions and victories.

### Ottoman

Ottoman chronograms were especially conspicuous on fountains because the final poetic line could link water, beneficence, purity, patron, and date. Istanbul’s streets thus became a public anthology of dated poetry.

### Armenian

Armenian colophons are unusually rich historical narratives. Besides numeral dates they record invasions, famine, plague, exile, prices, local rulers, patrons, binders, illuminators, and female donors. Their chronological evidence should not be conflated with phrase-sum chronograms.

### Cyrillic

Cyrillic dates on icons and bells served both documentary and sacral functions. A titlo made the letters visibly numerical; Anno Mundi dating placed the object within sacred universal history.

---

## 4.4 Persian, Mughal, Urdu, and Sikh material

Persian chronogram practice passed into Urdu. Collections such as *Tārīkh-e Laṭīf* preserve death chronograms for Urdu poets. Shamsur Rahman Faruqi’s discussion emphasizes how abjad competence remained part of Urdu literary culture. [Faruqi study](https://www.columbia.edu/itc/mealac/pritchett/00urduhindilinks/txt_abjad_farooqi.pdf).

### The Taj Mahal

**[Frequently repeated but poorly specified claim]** Popular accounts sometimes assert “the Taj Mahal’s date is a chronogram.” The monument does contain dated calligraphic signatures: Amanat Khan’s inscription on the great gate records completion with divine help in 1058 AH/1648 CE. The surviving date is not automatically a hidden abjad phrase.

A claim for a particular Taj Mahal chronogram must identify:

- the exact inscription;
- its Arabic or Persian spelling;
- which letters count;
- the target Hijrī year;
- whether the date is explicit or concealed.

Without that evidence, “the Taj Mahal was dated by gematria” should remain **unverified**, distinct from the securely documented dated inscription.

### Sikh chronograms

Persian remained an administrative and literary language in Sikh-ruled regions, and Persian inscriptions and manuscripts could employ abjad dating. But this is a Persianate technique used in a Sikh political or Punjabi setting, not evidence of a distinct ancient Gurmukhi gematria unless an actual Gurmukhi value table and primary text are produced.

## 4.5 Prophecy and polemic

Revelation 13:18 invited readers to “calculate” the beast’s number. From antiquity onward candidates multiplied because innumerable names can be made to total 666 through:

- alternative languages;
- titles;
- transliteration;
- spelling variants;
- added honorifics;
- subtraction or reduction.

Irenaeus proposed or reported several Greek candidates and warned against confident identification before fulfillment.

During the Reformation and Counter-Reformation, Protestants calculated papal titles while Catholics and other polemicists calculated the names of reformers. The famous **VICARIVS FILII DEI** computation counts only Roman-numeral letters:

\[
V(5)+I(1)+C(100)+I(1)+V(5)+I(1)+L(50)+I(1)+I(1)+D(500)+I(1)=666.
\]

\[
5+1+100+1+5+1+50+1+1+500+1=666.
\]

**[Documented polemical calculation; disputed title-history]** The arithmetic works under Roman-numeral extraction. The claim that *Vicarius Filii Dei* is the uniquely official or standard papal title is false or misleading. Its use in anti-papal prophecy is a polemical selection procedure, not neutral identification.

---

## 4.6 Poetry

Chronogram poetry is constrained writing before the modern term:

- a target date fixes the numerical total;
- grammar, meter, rhyme, and semantics must remain acceptable;
- the phrase should allude to the event;
- the poet may incorporate their pen name;
- the final hemistich often bears the calculation.

Persian and Ottoman poets were admired not merely for reaching the correct number, but for making the result sound inevitable.

The technique resembles later Oulipian writing because both impose formal constraints, but no direct historical descent should be assumed.

---

## 4.7 Agrippa’s Latin table and European occultism

Heinrich Cornelius Agrippa’s *De occulta philosophia*, substantially circulated in 1531 and printed in complete authorized form in 1533, Book II, places Hebrew, Greek, Latin, and other alphabetic correspondences within a system of number, planets, celestial signs, and magic.

One Latin scheme associated with Agrippan transmission extends values by alphabetic order:

| Letters | Values |
|---|---|
| A–I | 1–9 |
| K–S | 10–90 by tens |
| T–Z | 100–500 or related extended values, depending on the printed alphabet and whether I/J and U/V are distinguished |

The exact table must be read from the edition used because Renaissance Latin alphabets did not consistently distinguish I/J or U/V, and modern calculators silently regularize them.

**[Documented Renaissance system]** Agrippa is a key transmitter.

**[Modern mislabeling]** The calculator cipher often called “Jewish gematria” in English-language websites—A=1, B=2 … I=9, J=600, K=10 … or related Agrippan arrangements—is not the ordinary Jewish Hebrew table. It is a Latin-alphabet adaptation with Renaissance antecedents and modern digital variants.

Primary editions: [1533 scan](https://archive.org/details/DeOccultaPhilosophiaLoc1533) and the [1651 English translation](https://archive.org/details/occvltaphilosoph00herm/).

## 4.8 Golden Dawn and Crowley

The Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn integrated Hebrew letter values with:

- the Tree of Life;
- Tarot trumps;
- astrology;
- divine and angelic names;
- color scales;
- ritual grades.

Aleister Crowley’s:

- *777* organized large correspondence tables;
- *Sepher Sephiroth*, Liber 500, indexed Hebrew words by numerical value;
- writings repeatedly used equivalence, pun, transliteration, and variant spelling.

The first edition of *777* bore the phrase **אחת רוח אלהים חיים**, “one is the Spirit of the Living God,” represented as totaling 777 under its chosen spelling. See [*777*](https://hermetic.com/_media/93beast.fea.st/files/section1/777/liber_777.pdf) and [*Sepher Sephiroth*](https://hermetic.com/crowley/libers/lib500).

Crowley’s work is documented modern occultism, not evidence that all his English or Greek equations belonged to ancient Kabbalah.

## 4.9 Modern popular numerology

### “Pythagorean” name numbers

The familiar table:

- A/J/S = 1
- B/K/T = 2
- C/L/U = 3
- D/M/V = 4
- E/N/W = 5
- F/O/X = 6
- G/P/Y = 7
- H/Q/Z = 8
- I/R = 9

followed by reduction to a single digit, is commonly called “Pythagorean numerology.”

**[Modern invention/misattribution]** No surviving text by Pythagoras teaches this English alphabet table. English did not exist in his period. The system emerged from nineteenth- and early-twentieth-century occult numerology and was popularized by authors including L. Dow Balliett.

Balliett’s *The Philosophy of Numbers* appeared in 1908 and connected numbers with vibrations, colors, names, and character. [Library of Congress edition](https://www.loc.gov/item/08001111/).

### “Chaldean” numerology

The common “Chaldean” table assigning 1–8 according to purported sound vibrations was popularized by Cheiro—William John Warner (1866–1936)—in books on numbers, names, and destiny.

**[Modern occult construction]** It is not a deciphered Babylonian name-number table. “Chaldean” functions as an authority-conferring label drawn from the ancient reputation of Chaldea for astrology and magic.

### Eighteenth-century English numerology

Eighteenth-century occult, Masonic, biblical, and antiquarian writers experimented with number symbolism, but the standardized commercial personality systems now called Pythagorean and Chaldean belong chiefly to the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. Claims for an unbroken English system from Pythagoras should be rejected for lack of texts.

## 4.10 New Aeon English Qabalah

Crowley’s *Liber AL* II.55 called for obtaining “the order & value of the English Alphabet.” Crowley did not establish a universally accepted solution.

James Lees devised the system now called English Qaballa, ALW, or NAEQ in November 1976. Its order begins:

**A L W H S D O Z K V G R C N Y J U F Q B M X I T E P**

with values 1–26 in that order.

**[Documented modern invention interpreted by adherents as discovery]** Ray Sherwin publicly reported it in *The New Equinox* in 1979; Lees and associates published further material in 1981–82. It is not ancient English gematria. [Hadean Press history](https://hadean.press/products/the-new-equinox).

## 4.11 Internet gematria

Modern calculators make thousands of ciphers instantly searchable. Common labels include:

- English ordinal;
- reverse ordinal;
- reduction;
- reverse reduction;
- “Sumerian” = English ordinal × 6;
- “Jewish” = a modern Latin/Agrippan table;
- primes;
- triangular values;
- Baconian variants;
- Satanic additions;
- case-sensitive or keyboard-order systems.

**[Modern invention]** “English Sumerian gematria” has no demonstrated connection to the Sumerian language or cuneiform. “Jewish gematria” on calculators often operates on Latin letters and should not be confused with Hebrew gematria.

Database search introduces a new procedure absent from most traditional practice: instead of beginning with two contextually related words and noticing equality, the user queries millions of stored phrases and selects whatever matches. This drastically increases false discovery.

“Gematrinator” and similar sites helped popularize these practices in conspiracy communities. Their calculations are real under their declared tables; the inference that a matching result proves coordination, prophecy, or culpability is a separate and generally unsupported claim.

## 4.12 Literature: Borges, Perec, Oulipo, Joyce

Numerical structures, constrained writing, cabalistic libraries, and combinatorial language make these authors attractive to gematria interpreters, but attribution requires restraint.

- **Jorge Luis Borges:** demonstrably fascinated by Kabbalah, infinite libraries, divine names, permutations, and textual totality. Claims that a particular story is governed by a concealed gematria must identify manuscripts or authorial evidence.
- **Georges Perec and Oulipo:** documented use of lipograms, combinatorics, grids, and mathematical constraint. This is analogous to chronogram composition, but not automatically gematria.
- **James Joyce:** numbers, dates, puns, and cyclic structures are undeniably important in *Ulysses* and *Finnegans Wake*. Many gematric analyses are reader-generated and become convincing only when corroborated by notebooks, repeated structure, or a uniquely constrained result.

**Finding:** numerical literary interpretation has a high coincidence risk. General numerical interest does not demonstrate a specific letter-value cipher.

# 6. Controversies and disputes

## 6.1 Mesopotamia, Greece, or Israel?

### Mesopotamian priority

**Evidence:** Sargon’s wall explicitly corresponded to his name’s numerical “reading.”

**Problem:** no securely reconstructed alphabetic table lies behind it, and no continuous transmission to Greek or Hebrew has been demonstrated.

**Assessment:** earliest analogous name-number claim, not established ancestor.

### Greek priority

**Evidence:** Greek alphabetic numerals and Hellenistic isopsephic play predate securely datable rabbinic discussions.

**Problem:** Hebrew letters also served as numerals, and Jewish exploitation could arise independently.

**Assessment:** Greece has the stronger early evidence for systematic word isopsephy; cultural transmission into Jewish practice is plausible but not directly documented.

### Israelite/biblical priority

Claims that the Torah was extensively composed through the later standard gematria table rely on retrospective pattern detection.

**Evidence:** biblical numbers, alphabetic acrostics, word counts, and deliberate literary structures are real.

**Missing evidence:** an early biblical instruction to use the full standard gematria table, or a statistically controlled corpus of uniquely recoverable equations.

**Assessment:** individual intentional wordplays remain possible; a universal gematric design is unproved.

---

## 6.2 Was gematria “Pythagorean”?

Ancient Pythagoreans assigned cosmological meaning to number. That is documented broadly through later reports, especially Aristotle.

What is not documented is:

- Pythagoras teaching A=1 through Z=26;
- Pythagoras inventing Hebrew gematria;
- Pythagoras teaching the modern 1–9 English reduction table;
- an autograph by Pythagoras explaining isopsephy.

The earliest Pythagorean evidence is late and difficult to separate from later Pythagoreanism. “Pythagorean numerology” is therefore legitimate as a broad genealogy of number mysticism but misleading as the name of the modern English name-number cipher.

---

## 6.3 The coincidence problem

For an alphabet with \(m\) letters and ordinary additive values, a large vocabulary maps many distinct strings into a much smaller set of totals. Collisions are inevitable.

If a researcher permits:

- multiple languages;
- spelling variants;
- titles and honorifics;
- transliteration choices;
- final-letter variants;
- reduction;
- reversal;
- multiplication;
- omitted letters;
- *kolel* ±1;
- dozens of calculator ciphers;

then the search space expands so rapidly that striking matches become expected.

A valid historical argument therefore needs more than equality:

1. The table must be attested for the period.
2. The spelling must be natural for that period.
3. The text must cue calculation.
4. The referents must be contextually linked.
5. The solution should explain otherwise puzzling features.
6. Competing names and methods should be considered.
7. Ideally the result should be predictive or independently corroborated.

The Pompeian “545” graffito deliberately withholds the name and thereby illustrates non-uniqueness: contemporaries with social context could identify the woman; arithmetic alone cannot.

## 6.4 Reliability of traditional derivations

Traditional exegesis asks, “What further meaning can this equality support?” Modern historical criticism asks, “Did the author deliberately compose the passage to produce it?” Those questions should not be collapsed.

A gematria can be:

- arithmetically correct;
- traditionally authoritative;
- spiritually meaningful to a community;
- historically secondary.

All four can coexist.

Abraham ibn Ezra’s criticism of the Eliezer calculation is especially important: spelling and alternative words can generate numerous totals, so gematria should not override grammar and context.

## 6.5 Scholem, Idel, Kaplan, Weinreb, Barry, Dornseiff

### Franz Dornseiff

*Das Alphabet in Mystik und Magie* (1922; expanded second edition 1925) assembled Greek, Gnostic, Jewish, Christian, and magical alphabet lore. Its enduring strength is the breadth of primary material. Its historical syntheses must be updated in light of subsequent papyrology, epigraphy, and manuscript studies. [Project Gutenberg text](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/73284).

### Gershom Scholem

Scholem established Kabbalah as a historical field and treated gematria as one technique within evolving mystical systems, not a timeless universal cipher. His account of Abulafia emphasized prophetic/ecstatic Kabbalah and its contrast with theosophical Kabbalah.

### Moshe Idel

Idel challenged some of Scholem’s sharp typologies, emphasizing techniques, experience, linguistic practice, and continuities across schools. For Abulafia, permutation, breathing, and vocalization matter as much as totals.

### Aryeh Kaplan

Kaplan presented *Sefer Yeṣirah*, meditation, and Kabbalistic techniques to modern English readers, often sympathetically reconstructing practical systems. His editions are influential but combine scholarship, synthesis, and religious interpretation; they should not replace manuscript-critical studies.

### Friedrich Weinreb

Weinreb’s numerical readings of Torah have influenced modern mystical audiences. They are theological-literary constructions rather than mainstream demonstrations of biblical compositional history.

### Kieren Barry

*The Greek Qabalah* (1999) usefully popularized Greek alphabet mysticism, isopsephy, Gnostic material, and its relation to later Kabbalah. Specialists have valued its synthesis while questioning some large historical connections and speculative continuities. It should be read alongside Dornseiff and current epigraphic scholarship, not as the last word on priority.

## 6.6 Forgeries, pseudepigraphy, and false antiquity

Examples requiring explicit caution include:

- **Sefer Raziel given to Adam:** sacred legend; surviving book medieval and composite.
- **All works attributed to al-Būnī:** false; the Būnian corpus contains later accretions and pseudepigrapha.
- **Pseudo-Ibn Waḥshiyya alphabet books:** some celebrated occult-alphabet works circulated under prestigious ancient names but are later compositions.
- **“Chaldean” English numerology:** modern, not a recovered cuneiform system.
- **Pythagorean English numerology:** modern, not taught by Pythagoras.
- **English “Sumerian gematria”:** calculator-era ordinal multiplication, unrelated to Sumerian language.
- **Calculator “Jewish gematria”:** often a Latin Renaissance/modern cipher, not Hebrew *mispar hekhreḥi*.
- **Universal Taj Mahal chronogram claims:** require the precise inscription; dated calligraphy alone is not a hidden chronogram.
- **Ancient Georgian alphabet claims based on Grakliani:** the signs are ancient, but their identification as Georgian and their relationship to the fifth-century alphabet remain disputed.
- **Hidden modern scientific constants in Genesis:** normally depend on flexible spelling, unit selection, decimal conventions, and post hoc choice; no ancient procedural key is supplied.

# 7. Open questions

1. What precise cuneiform operation generated Sargon II’s wall/name number, and are 16,280 and 16,283 separate textual traditions?
2. Which Greek inscription should count as the earliest indisputable word-isopsephy rather than an ordinary numeral or a modern reconstruction?
3. When did Jewish scribes first use Hebrew letters as numerals, and when did that notation first become semantic wordplay?
4. What is the earliest securely dated Hebrew chronogram for which the operative letters and manuscript context survive?
5. How stable are the titles and classifications of Hebrew sub-methods before modern printed handbooks?
6. When and where did 786 become common shorthand for the basmala, especially in South Asia?
7. What are the earliest Arabic literary abjad chronograms, as distinct from technical alphabetic numerals?
8. How many works in the Būnian corpus can be securely assigned to Aḥmad al-Būnī himself?
9. Which Ghaznavid Persian inscriptions are genuine abjad chronograms rather than explicit verse dates?
10. Can Ottoman chronogram corpora be computationally mapped by patron, building type, district, and poet without losing spelling distinctions?
11. Which alleged Taj Mahal and Sikh chronograms survive in situ, and what exact arithmetic supports each claim?
12. Are meaningful phrase-sum chronograms independently documented in Armenian, Georgian, Cyrillic, or Ethiopic, or are modern writers conflating them with alphabetic numeral dates?
13. How often were chronograms altered by copyists to “correct” a date?
14. Can statistical controls distinguish deliberately constructed word-sums from incidental equality in a morphologically rich language?
15. How did typography—enlarged letters, dots, color, and ornamental strokes—tell historical readers which characters counted?
16. When did “Jewish,” “Sumerian,” and related misleading cipher names first enter internet calculator culture?
17. Which gematric interpretations in Borges, Joyce, Perec, and Oulipo can be tied to notebooks or explicit authorial statements?
