## 5. Ideas and Programs

### The book’s argument

The authors call *The Technological Republic* a political treatise and organize it into four parts: “The Software Century,” “The Hollowing Out of the American Mind,” “The Engineering Mindset” and “Rebuilding the Technological Republic.” **[PR, contents and preface]**

Its central claims are:

- the early technology industry was created through cooperation among government, military institutions, universities and private engineering;
- Silicon Valley subsequently redirected talent toward advertising, social media, delivery services and other consumer products;
- the United States and its allies have entered a “software century” in which AI, autonomous systems and software-defined weapons will be decisive;
- engineers owe a civic or moral debt to the society that enabled their success;
- government procurement and organization should borrow Silicon Valley’s iterative engineering culture;
- liberal democracies require hard military power, national confidence and some substantive common culture, not merely procedural pluralism; and
- Palantir presents itself as a practical example of this theory. **[SC, authors’ text, 2024–25]**

The June 25, 2024 Washington Post essay previewed the military portion of the argument: autonomous weapons will be developed; the important questions are by whom and toward what ends; U.S. technology workers should therefore assist American and allied defense. **[SC, Karp–Zamiska op-ed]**

At Yale in April 2026, Zamiska added several qualifications in his own voice:

- humans should remain in the lead when AI tools are deployed;
- Palantir does not work with geopolitical adversaries;
- world order and strategic equilibrium may be more attainable than total dominance;
- broad federal regulation of all AI may not be useful;
- deeper public-private integration is preferable; and
- because Palantir has a commercial interest in defense procurement, his advocacy should be taken “with a grain of salt.” **[SC, edited interview published May 5, 2026]**

### The April 2026 “22 points”

On April 18, 2026, Palantir’s official X account posted a graphic titled *The Technological Republic, in brief*. The graphic identifies the statements as excerpts from Karp and Zamiska’s book. **[SC]** It is accurately described as a company-published summary or manifesto, but no reviewed record establishes it as a board-adopted charter, bylaw, compliance policy or new work written personally by Zamiska in April 2026.

Paraphrased, the 22 propositions are:

1. Silicon Valley owes the United States a moral debt and should contribute to national defense.
2. Engineers should move beyond the constraining focus on phone applications.
3. Consumer conveniences alone cannot legitimate an elite; economic growth and public security matter.
4. Democratic societies require software-enabled hard power as well as rhetoric and soft power.
5. AI weapons will be built; the decisive issue is who builds them and for what political purpose.
6. Universal national service, including reconsideration of the all-volunteer military, deserves debate.
7. Providing superior weapons and software to deployed service members can be separated from debate over whether a war is wise.
8. Public servants should not be expected to sacrifice competitive compensation as though public employment were a priesthood.
9. Public figures should receive more room for forgiveness and human contradiction.
10. Citizens should not seek personal or spiritual fulfillment through politicians.
11. Defeating political enemies should occasion restraint, not celebration.
12. The authors foresee AI systems replacing nuclear weapons as the central basis of deterrence.
13. Despite its failings, the United States has expanded progressive values and opportunities beyond hereditary elites.
14. American power has helped prevent great-power world war for generations.
15. Germany and Japan should move beyond postwar military restraint.
16. Builders pursuing grand projects where markets have failed—including Elon Musk—should not be reflexively dismissed.
17. Silicon Valley should contribute technological experimentation to reducing violent crime.
18. Aggressive exposure of public figures’ private lives deters capable people from government service.
19. Excessive caution produces public figures who avoid error by saying little of substance.
20. Elite hostility to religious belief should be resisted.
21. Cultures may be judged by their achievements and harms rather than treated as equivalent in every respect.
22. The United States and the West should define the common culture into which inclusion is offered rather than relying on contentless pluralism. **[SC, Palantir graphic, April 18, 2026]**

- John Ganz’s February 21, 2025 Bloomberg review characterized it as both a call to arms and a Palantir sales pitch, and criticized its reliance on an elite engineering class. **[JR/opinion]**
- Mark O’Connell’s September 25, 2025 *New York Review of Books* treatment was sharply dismissive of the style and politics and largely read the work as an expression of Karp’s worldview. **[JR/opinion]**
- Kirkus called the argument provocative and took its government-procurement proposals more seriously. **[JR/review]**

These are critical judgments, not findings of corruption or undisclosed financial misconduct.
