*Record reviewed through September 28, 2026.*

## Evidentiary key and scope

- **[IR] Institutional record:** official university, company, foundation, museum, or professional-society publication.
- **[JR] Journalistic report:** reporting by an identified journalist or publication.
- **[SC] Subject claim:** Hillis’s recollection or account in an interview, essay, or authorized biography; not independently audited unless another tier is also given.
- **[AL] Allegation:** claim in litigation or by a named accuser.
- **[RU] Rumor/unsupported synthesis:** online assertion, conspiracy narrative, or inference not supported by a traceable primary record.
- **[DR] Disproved/retracted:** claim affirmatively contradicted, withdrawn, or retracted.

“Every documented tie” below means every material, identifiable tie located in the reviewed primary, institutional, journalistic, and contested-source corpus. Mere appearance on a mass mailing or an unconsummated invitation is identified as such, not converted into a personal relationship.

## 1. Identification

**[PR/IR]** William Daniel “Danny” Hillis, born September 25, 1956, in Baltimore, Maryland, is an American computer scientist, inventor, company founder, and government technology adviser. He is best known for conceiving the massively parallel Connection Machine; co-founding Thinking Machines Corporation in 1983; serving as a Disney Fellow and vice president of research and development at Walt Disney Imagineering; co-founding Applied Minds, Metaweb Technologies, Applied Proteomics, and the Long Now Foundation; and founding Applied Invention. His institutional biography describes hundreds of patents in parallel computing, disk arrays, touch interfaces, forgery prevention, and mechanical and electronic systems. He has held appointments at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology and the University of Southern California. [MIT thesis, 1985](https://dspace.mit.edu/bitstream/handle/1721.1/14719/18524280-MIT.pdf); [Edge institutional biography](https://www.edge.org/memberbio/w_daniel_hillis?page=1); [Computer History Museum oral history, 2008](https://archive.computerhistory.org/resources/access/text/2012/11/102702101-05-01-acc.pdf).

**[IR]** Hillis was elected a Fellow of the American Academy of Arts and Sciences in 1992 and is listed by professional biographies as a Fellow of the Association for Computing Machinery and a member of the National Academy of Engineering. He received ACM’s Grace Murray Hopper Award in 1989 and the inaugural 2002 Dan David Prize in the “Present—Technology, Information and Society” category. [American Academy membership record](https://www.amacad.org/sites/default/files/academy/multimedia/pdfs/publications/bookofmembers/ChapterH.pdf); [Dan David Prize biography](https://dandavidprize.org/laureates/daniel-hillis/).

**[IR/JR]** Contemporary biographies said that Hillis was married to a woman identified as Pati and had three children, Asa, Noah, and India. A 1997 *Los Angeles Times* profile described him as married and the father of twin boys and a daughter. Later crowd-edited sources claim that he married Taylor Milsal in 2019, but no primary marriage record or reliable contemporaneous report substantiating that later claim was located; it therefore remains **[RU] unsupported** here. [1997 *Los Angeles Times* profile](https://www.latimes.com/archives/la-xpm-1997-dec-28-op-7644-story.html); [reference biography identifying Pati and the children](https://www.encyclopedia.com/arts/educational-magazines/hillis-w-daniel-1956).

## 2. The Record

### Origins and education

**[SC/IR]** Hillis told the Computer History Museum in 2008 that his father was an epidemiologist and that the family’s work took him during childhood to the Congo, Rwanda, Calcutta, and numerous schools. His mother later earned a doctorate in mathematics or biostatistics. Baylor University’s obituary for his father, William “Bill” Hillis, identifies Hillis’s mother as Argye Briggs Hillis and siblings as David and Beth. [Computer History Museum oral history, September 5, 2008](https://archive.computerhistory.org/resources/access/text/2012/11/102702101-05-01-acc.pdf); [Baylor University obituary, April 30, 2018](https://news.web.baylor.edu/news/story/2018/baylor-mourns-passing-bill-hillis-md-longtime-professor-and-administrator-who).

**[PR/SC]** At MIT, Hillis studied mathematics and computer science, receiving a bachelor’s degree in 1978 and a master’s degree in 1981. His dissertation, *The Connection Machine*, was submitted to MIT’s Department of Electrical Engineering and Computer Science in June 1985 and signed May 3, 1985; later biographies give 1988 as the year the doctorate was formally awarded. Gerald Jay Sussman was the named thesis supervisor. Hillis has separately identified Marvin Minsky, Claude Shannon, Seymour Papert, Tom Knight, Patrick Winston, and Sussman as formative influences in and around the MIT Artificial Intelligence Laboratory. [MIT dissertation](https://dspace.mit.edu/bitstream/handle/1721.1/14719/18524280-MIT.pdf); [Computer History Museum oral history](https://archive.computerhistory.org/resources/access/text/2012/11/102702101-05-01-acc.pdf).

**[SC]** Hillis described working in Papert’s Logo group on machines children could use, building computer-controlled construction systems with Brian Silverman, and using MIT’s MOSIS service to fabricate experimental chips. He recalled Brewster Kahle helping with dynamic-memory work and Michael Dertouzos providing approximately $10,000 for an early prototype. These sums and arrangements are Hillis’s recollections, not independently audited financial records. [Computer History Museum oral history](https://archive.computerhistory.org/resources/access/text/2012/11/102702101-05-01-acc.pdf).

### Thinking Machines and the Connection Machine

**[PR/IR]** Hillis’s dissertation proposed a highly parallel architecture whose many simple processors communicated through a routing network. MIT Press published *The Connection Machine* in 1985. Thinking Machines’ CM-1 implemented 65,536 one-bit processors; later CM-2, CM-200, and CM-5 systems expanded the architecture and increasingly used conventional processors. [MIT Press book record](https://mitpress.mit.edu/9780262081573/the-connection-machine/); [MIT historical report on Thinking Machines](https://courses.csail.mit.edu/6.972/TMC%20Corp.html); [Computer History Museum company history](https://www.computerhistory.org/brochures/t-z/thinking-machines-corporation-tmc/).

**[IR]** Thinking Machines Corporation was founded in 1983 by Hillis and Sheryl Handler to commercialize the dissertation work. Hillis was its technical leader/chief scientist; Handler was president and business organizer. Marvin Minsky was identified as a founding scientist. A design object now held by the Museum of Modern Art credits Hillis, Tamiko Thiel, Gordon Bruce, Allen Hawthorne, and Ted Bilodeau for the CM-2’s design. [MIT historical report](https://courses.csail.mit.edu/6.972/TMC%20Corp.html); [MoMA CM-2 collection record](https://www.moma.org/collection/works/200389).

**[SC]** Hillis recalled that Minsky introduced him to Handler and that Handler brought him to CBS founder William S. Paley. Hillis said that after he proposed needing roughly $10 million, Paley offered half. No contemporaneous investment agreement was found, so the amount is **[SC] subject recollection**, not a verified capitalization figure. [Computer History Museum oral history](https://archive.computerhistory.org/resources/access/text/2012/11/102702101-05-01-acc.pdf).

**[PR/JR]** DARPA support took several forms. A 1989 Pentagon award promised Thinking Machines $12 million, to be matched by the company, toward a teraflop-class machine. Separately, the Government Accountability Office examined DARPA’s massively parallel processor placement program. GAO documented 24 Connection Machines placed in government laboratories and research sites, with listed federal contributions totaling roughly $30 million when the table’s disclosed figures are added. The recipients included SRI International, the Naval Research Laboratory, the National Center for Atmospheric Research, Sandia National Laboratories, NASA, and other defense laboratories. These placement purchases should not be double-counted as the same thing as the 1989 development award. [1989 *Washington Post* report](https://www.washingtonpost.com/archive/business/1989/11/29/12-million-grant-for-fast-computer/dae76928-d694-48ee-9e9c-aeec03a60bd2/); [GAO report, May 17, 1993](https://www.gao.gov/products/imtec-93-24); [GAO placement table](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED359940.pdf).

**[PR]** GAO concluded that DARPA had concentrated its support on Intel and Thinking Machines and warned that the selection process could distort the market. It recommended broader access and more emphasis on portable software. GAO did **not** find that Hillis committed fraud, bribery, or procurement crime. [GAO report](https://www.gao.gov/assets/imtec-93-24.pdf).

**[PR/JR]** Thinking Machines systems were used for climate and ocean modeling, aircraft and automotive design, database analysis, imaging, genetics, cryptography, and classified weapons-related work. A contemporary report quoted Los Alamos’s Andy White saying that the machines made classified contributions to weapons problems. NSA’s declassified computing history identifies its CM-5, FROSTBURG, as a $25 million massively parallel system installed in 1991 and later expanded. This is a documented national-security use of Hillis’s company’s technology, not evidence that Hillis was an intelligence officer. [*Washington Post*, August 27, 1994](https://www.washingtonpost.com/archive/business/1994/08/28/thinking-machines-rather-than-markets/db29b5ee-0107-43e2-89b0-c22518a3a02c/); [NSA declassified computer history](https://www.nsa.gov/portals/75/documents/news-features/declassified-documents/nsa-early-computer-history/6586785-nsa-key-role-in-major-developments-in-computer-science.pdf).

**[JR]** The 1994 *Washington Post* reconstruction estimated approximately $12 million in DARPA development money and $120 million from private investors. These figures describe different categories than GAO’s procurement table and cannot safely be collapsed into a single government-subsidy number. The company sold most systems to government and research users, struggled to build portable software and a durable commercial market, and expanded quickly after its only profitable year, 1990. [*Washington Post*, August 27, 1994](https://www.washingtonpost.com/archive/business/1994/08/28/thinking-machines-rather-than-markets/db29b5ee-0107-43e2-89b0-c22518a3a02c/).

**[PR]** Thinking Machines filed a Chapter 11 petition on August 17, 1994. A federal court opinion records that date. The company’s hardware operation went to Sun Microsystems; the surviving software business continued for a time, and data-mining assets ultimately went to Oracle. This was a corporate bankruptcy, not a personal bankruptcy or criminal proceeding against Hillis. [*Thinking Machines Corp. v. New Mexico Taxation and Revenue Department*, 1997](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/district-courts/BR/211/426/1978535/); [contemporaneous *Computerwoche* report, August 26, 1994](https://www.computerwoche.de/article/2777108/unter-chapter-eleven-soll-basis-fuer-restunternehmen-wachsen-sun-kauft-hardware-division-von-thinking-machines-sehr-billig-ein.html).

### Disney

**[IR/JR]** Hillis joined Walt Disney Imagineering in 1996 as the first Disney Fellow and as vice president of research and development, remaining until 2000. His work ranged across theme parks, film, television, consumer products, internet strategy, robotics, and the animatronic dinosaur project. [*Wired*, March 1997](https://www.wired.com/1997/03/disney-fellows/); [*New Yorker*, October 13, 1997](https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/1997/10/20/future-perfect); [*Los Angeles Times*, February 12, 2000](https://www.latimes.com/archives/la-xpm-2000-feb-12-fi-63522-story.html).

**[SC]** Hillis said Disney invited him to become a Fellow and that he accepted only if Alan Kay received the same invitation. Disney’s research circle also included Bran Ferren and Marvin Minsky. [Computer History Museum oral history](https://archive.computerhistory.org/resources/access/text/2012/11/102702101-05-01-acc.pdf).

### Long Now and the 10,000-year clock

**[IR/SC]** Hillis originated the idea of a mechanical clock designed to run for 10,000 years. Stewart Brand argued that the project required a durable organization; Hillis, Brand, and musician Brian Eno founded the Long Now Foundation in 1996. Hillis served for years as co-chair and is currently listed as an emeritus board member. [Long Now institutional history](https://longnow.org/); [Long Now board roster](https://longnow.org/people/board/).

**[IR/SC]** Long Now’s 2024 clock FAQ gives the funding chain: Jacqui Safra funded the first prototype, loaned to London’s Science Museum; Nathan Myhrvold funded two Orreries and donated one back; Jeff Bezos funded the full-scale clock inside a mountain he supplied in West Texas. Hillis says the final cost is still unknown and that Long Now membership money was not used to construct the clocks. A widely repeated $42 million figure came from press reporting, not an audited or Bezos-confirmed budget. [Long Now clock FAQ, February 2024](https://longnow.org/clock/faq/); [IEEE Spectrum account](https://spectrum.ieee.org/engineering-the-10-000year-clock).

### Applied Minds, defense work, and Applied Invention

**[IR/JR]** Hillis and Bran Ferren left Disney and co-founded Applied Minds in 2000. The company describes work in aerospace and defense, command and control, simulation, training, AI, visualization, healthcare, transportation, and rapid prototyping. Some projects and clients are confidential. Its website credits Ferren and Hillis as co-founders; later reports sometimes also identify software entrepreneur Doug Carlston as an early co-founder or backer, but the current company history does not. [Applied Minds company history](https://www.appliedminds.com/project/hmi); [*Los Angeles Times*, February 12, 2000](https://www.latimes.com/archives/la-xpm-2000-feb-12-fi-63522-story.html).

**[PR]** Federal SBIR records identify Hillis as principal investigator on Applied Minds work for the Air Force Research Laboratory, including “People Finder,” software intended to assemble and support ad hoc teams. The documented award period ran during 2007–2012. [SBIR award record](https://www.sbir.gov/awards/18000).

**[SC/IR]** Hillis has said that intelligence organizations used Thinking Machines computers and that he chose to assist intelligence analysis rather than weapons development; he said Applied Minds later built software for intelligence work. The agencies and projects were not identified. The statement documents Hillis’s own characterization, not CIA employment or a clearance. [Hillis interview, “Working with intelligence agencies”](https://webofstories.com/play/57002?o=MS).

**[IR]** Applied Invention, founded by Hillis after the separation of parts of the Applied Minds organization, describes itself as a multidisciplinary technology company working with confidential Fortune 1000 partners in AI, machine learning, cloud systems, sensors, robotics, healthcare, energy, agriculture, manufacturing, and transportation. The company says teams associated with its lineage have produced more than 1,000 issued patents since 2000. Client identities and commercial terms are generally undisclosed. [Applied Invention](https://www.appliedinvention.com/).

### Metaweb and Freebase

**[IR/JR]** In 2005, Hillis and colleagues from Applied Minds created Metaweb Technologies to develop Freebase, an open structured database of entities and relationships. Public accounts identify Hillis, John Giannandrea, and Veda Hlubinka-Cook among the founding group; Robert Cook became chief executive. Financing reports identify Benchmark Capital, Millennium Technology Ventures, DAG Ventures, Goldman Sachs, and Omidyar Network, with disclosed rounds of $15 million in 2006 and $42.5 million in 2008. [Edge biography](https://www.edge.org/memberbio/w_daniel_hillis?page=1); [contemporaneous TechCrunch acquisition report](https://techcrunch.com/2010/07/16/google-acquires-metaweb-to-make-search-smarter/).

**[IR]** Google acquired Metaweb in July 2010. Google said Freebase then contained more than 12 million entities and that the acquisition would improve search; the purchase price was not disclosed. Google subsequently used Metaweb/Freebase technology in the Knowledge Graph. [Google announcement, July 16, 2010](https://googleblog.blogspot.com/2010/07/deeper-understanding-with-metaweb.html?hl=en_GB).

### Cancer research and Applied Proteomics

**[IR]** Hillis and oncologist David Agus co-founded Applied Proteomics in 2007 to develop protein-measurement and biomarker systems. The company described using blood, urine, and saliva samples, mass spectrometry, genome annotation, standardized processing, and computation for diagnostics. [Applied Proteomics description](https://www.appliedproteomics.com/vision.html).

**[IR/JR]** A 2012 Series B financing raised $22.5 million from Domain Associates, Vulcan Capital, and returning angel investors. Later financing reports place disclosed capital at at least $50.5 million, although private-company totals vary by database. Applied Proteomics’ assets were sold to DiscernDx in February 2018 for a reported $1.85–$2 million. [2012 financing announcement](https://www.technologynetworks.com/immunology/news/applied-proteomics-appoints-ceo-following-series-b-financing-186853); [transaction summary](https://mergr.com/discerndx-acquires-applied-proteomics).

**[IR]** Hillis and Agus became co-principal investigators of USC’s Physical Sciences in Oncology Center, one of twelve centers initially established by the National Cancer Institute. USC announced a five-year, $16 million NCI award in 2009 to the multi-university center; this was consortium funding, not a documented $16 million personal payment to Hillis. Their program modeled cancer as a complex system and analyzed networks of proteins and clinical variables. [USC announcement, October 30, 2009](https://hscnews.usc.edu/archives/pdf09/1533.pdf); [USC account, December 5, 2016](https://today.usc.edu/uscs-cancer-revolution-calls-for-computer-whizzes-and-engineers/); [NCI program description](https://www.cancer.gov/about-nci/organization/dcb/research-programs/pson).

**[Finding on human subjects]** Applied Proteomics and the USC oncology program processed human-derived biological specimens and supported medical research. No reviewed record showed Hillis personally conducting a coercive, nonconsensual, or clandestine human-subject experiment. Detailed protocols, individual IRB approvals, consent forms, and participant-level records were not located. It would therefore be unjustified to characterize the work as abusive human experimentation.

### Government advisory power

**[PR]** President Bill Clinton appointed Hillis in February 1997 to the Advisory Committee on High-Performance Computing and Communications, Information Technology, and the Next Generation Internet, later known as the President’s Information Technology Advisory Committee. A 1999 committee document lists him on its Scalable Information Infrastructure panel. [White House announcement, February 12, 1997](https://clintonwhitehouse6.archives.gov/1997/02/1997-02-12-members-of-computing-advisory-committee-named.html); [PITAC panel record, February 17, 1999](https://www.nitrd.gov/historical/pitac/meetings/1999/19990217/sii-17Feb99.pdf).

**[PR]** Secretary of Defense Ash Carter appointed Hillis to the Defense Innovation Board in 2016. Pentagon records show him attending both public sessions and classified closed sessions; minutes through September 2020 record his work on AI testing, uncertainty, and AI ethics. The board advised on a chief innovation officer, cybersecurity review, AI research, software acquisition, and increased DARPA investment. This was an official advisory role to the Defense Department. It is not evidence of an intelligence-agency post. [Defense Department announcement, July 26, 2016](https://www.defense.gov/News/Speeches/Speech/Article/858155/remarks-on-opening-diux-east-and-announcing-the-defense-innovation-board/); [closed-meeting memorandum](https://media.defense.gov/2017/Dec/18/2001857903/-1/-1/0/2017-2566-147624_CLOSED%20MEETING%20SUMMARY_%282017-09-27-03-34-03%29.PDF); [March 2020 minutes](https://innovation.defense.gov/Portals/63/ES%20Signed%20200305%20-%20DIB%20Public%20Meeting%20Minutes_Final.pdf).

### Legal record and archives

**[PR]** No criminal indictment, conviction, civil fraud judgment, SEC enforcement action, or personal bankruptcy involving William Daniel Hillis was located. Thinking Machines was the debtor in Chapter 11 and related lease and tax litigation; Hillis was not the adjudicated debtor. The reviewed Apple–Samsung filing concerned a Hillis co-authored touch-interface patent used by the Patent Office as prior art during reexamination of an Apple patent. It was not an accusation against Hillis. [Thinking Machines tax case](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/district-courts/BR/211/426/1978535/); [Hillis/Ferren patent](https://patents.google.com/patent/US8665239B2/en); [Apple–Samsung filing, December 19, 2012](https://platform.theverge.com/wp-content/uploads/sites/2/chorus/assets/1899031/2202-main.pdf).

**[IR]** Hillis’s dissertation is preserved by MIT; his 2008 oral history is at the Computer History Museum. Connection Machine hardware and design objects are held by the Computer History Museum and MoMA. Long Now holds clock-development material and artifacts. No public finding aid for a comprehensive personal Hillis papers collection was located.

## 5. Ideas and Programs

### Parallelism and brain-inspired computation

**[PR/SC]** Hillis’s central technical proposition was that intelligence and complex computation might emerge from very large numbers of simple processing elements acting in parallel. The Connection Machine’s resemblance to neural organization was an architectural inspiration, not a claim that the CM-1 was literally a human brain. The dissertation developed processor topology, routing, memory, and programming models; later machines moved toward more conventional processors because software availability, numerical performance, and commercial usability mattered as much as processor count.

**Use by others:** Massive parallelism became standard in supercomputing, graphics processors, machine learning, database systems, and cloud computing. The direct lineage is conceptual and personnel-based rather than a claim that every modern parallel system derives technically from Thinking Machines.

### Artificial intelligence and artificial life

**[IR/SC]** Hillis’s early aim was explicitly AI-oriented: to build hardware capable of supporting many interacting computational agents. His later work on artificial evolution explored machines or programs whose designs could be selected rather than fully specified. He edited or contributed to journals including *Artificial Life*, *Complexity*, and *Complex Systems*. He generally treated intelligence as an emergent property of interacting computational mechanisms.

### The Knowledge Web, Metaweb, and machine-readable entities

**[IR]** Hillis proposed organizing knowledge as a graph of entities and relationships rather than a collection of isolated documents. Metaweb and Freebase operationalized that idea through shared identifiers and structured facts. Google’s acquisition placed the approach within commercial search and the Knowledge Graph. This is the clearest documented route by which Hillis’s “knowledge web” ideas entered mass information infrastructure.

### Long-term thinking

**[SC/IR]** The 10,000-year clock is less a timekeeping necessity than an institution-building and cultural device. Hillis says its purpose is to make distant futures imaginable. Brand supplied the organizational logic; Eno contributed the vocabulary and musical dimension; Safra, Myhrvold, and Bezos supplied capital and physical resources.

### Internet fragility and technological dependence

**[SC]** In talks and essays, Hillis has argued that the internet has become critical infrastructure without acquiring corresponding redundancy, governance, and recovery mechanisms. This is a critique of fragile technological dependence, not an anti-technology position comparable to Theodore Kaczynski’s rejection of industrial society.

### Cancer as a complex system

**[IR/SC]** Hillis and Agus framed cancer as a system-level problem involving networks of proteins, organs, environment, and treatment response. Their practical program combined mass spectrometry, computation, biomarkers, and physical-science modeling. No evidence was found connecting this work to covert behavioral experimentation, eugenics programs, or nonconsensual research.

### Cybernetics, “mirror worlds,” and technological determinism

**Assessment:** Hillis belongs to the historical lineage connecting cybernetics, AI, complex systems, brain metaphors, military research, and computation, but he was not a central member of the first-generation Wiener–McCulloch cybernetics movement. “Mirror worlds” is principally associated with David Gelernter, not a core Hillis program. Hillis’s writing often assigns technology strong path-shaping effects, but it also stresses institutional design and human choice; describing him simply as a technological determinist would be interpretive rather than documentary.

## 7. Reception and Afterlife

**[JR]** During the 1980s and early 1990s, profiles cast Hillis as a prodigy attempting to build a brain-like computer. Coverage alternated between technical admiration and skepticism about whether specialized parallel machines had a market.

**[JR]** Thinking Machines’ bankruptcy revised that image. Contemporary retrospectives treated the company as technologically prophetic but commercially undisciplined and overdependent on government research customers. Hillis later called it his first major failure and acknowledged that invention did not substitute for financial sustainability.

**[JR]** The 1997 *New Yorker* and *Los Angeles Times* profiles recast him as a Disney futurist working across entertainment, robotics, computation, and the Millennium Clock. Applied Minds coverage in the 2000s emphasized secretive prototyping for corporations and defense customers.

**[IR]** *The Pattern on the Stone* (1998) became Hillis’s principal popular explanation of computing, moving from Boolean logic through algorithms, information theory, parallelism, cryptography, and emergent behavior. His institutional reputation remains anchored in the Connection Machine, modern parallelism, and the Long Now clock.

**[IR/JR]** The Computer History Museum’s 2008 oral history preserved Hillis’s own retrospective account of MIT, Thinking Machines, Feynman, Disney, and intelligence work. It is invaluable but should be read as recollection rather than an audited corporate history.

**[JR]** After 2019, and especially after the 2025–2026 document releases, Hillis’s public image acquired an Epstein dimension. The record changed from one well-known 2002 quotation to a sequence of post-conviction emails, Edge coattendance, and a 2015 MIT meeting. Responsible reporting has so far distinguished this continued association from proof of funding or criminal conduct; online databases and conspiracy videos often collapse those categories.

**[Finding]** No substantial public statement by Hillis addressing the full Epstein record was located. His 2002 quotation remains his clearest contemporaneous account of the relationship; in 2026, *Scientific American* reported that he had not responded to its inquiry.
