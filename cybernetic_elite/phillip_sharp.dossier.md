# Phillip A. Sharp: Research Dossier

## Evidentiary key and scope

- **P — Primary/documentary record:** SEC filing, government record, court order, patent record, contemporaneous letter, institutional minutes, grant record, or contemporaneous university announcement.
- **J — Named journalism:** reporting by an identified journalist or established publication.
- **H — Historical/scholarly analysis:** later academic reconstruction based on documentary sources.
- **S — Subject or institutional account:** Sharp’s statements, oral history, official biography, or a company’s description of its own history.
- **A — Allegation:** claim made in litigation, an accusation, or an investigation but not established as fact.
- **U — Unsupported/rumor:** an online or popular claim for which no corroborating primary record was located.
- **D — Disproved, contradicted, or identity confusion:** evidence affirmatively contradicts the claim, or the record concerns another person.

This dossier covers every material named tie located in the reviewed records. Sharp has more than 500 scientific publications, so “every documented tie” cannot literally encompass every co-author, journal editor, conference participant, or laboratory member. The network section instead includes every named relationship material to his discoveries, companies, investments, institutional power, public appointments, controversies, and the Kaczynski/Epstein/intelligence framing under examination.

Searches included SEC filings; federal court materials; NIH, NCI, White House, congressional, Nobel, and university records; company histories; nonprofit documents; UNABOM evidence inventories; contemporary news; scholarly histories; and exact-name adverse searches involving Jeffrey Epstein, CIA, MKUltra, lawsuits, indictments, depositions, offshore entities, intelligence agencies, organized crime, scandals, and foundations.

## 1. Identification

Phillip Allen Sharp, usually published as **Phillip A. Sharp** or **Phillip Sharp**, was born June 6, 1944, in Falmouth, Kentucky. He is an American molecular biologist, biotechnology entrepreneur, university administrator, corporate director, investor, and federal science adviser. He earned a B.A. in chemistry and mathematics from Union College in 1966 and a Ph.D. in chemistry from the University of Illinois at Urbana-Champaign in 1969; trained at the California Institute of Technology and Cold Spring Harbor Laboratory; joined the Massachusetts Institute of Technology in 1974; directed MIT’s Center for Cancer Research from 1985 to 1991; headed MIT Biology from 1991 to 1999; became an Institute Professor in 1999; served as founding director of the McGovern Institute for Brain Research from 2000 to 2004; and became Institute Professor Emeritus in 2023. These dates appear in Sharp’s MIT curriculum vitae and the Science History Institute’s oral-history record. **[P/S, MIT CV; oral history, 2003–2023]**

Sharp and Richard J. Roberts received the 1993 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine “for their discoveries of split genes.” Sharp’s laboratory’s decisive 1977 paper, authored by Susan M. Berget, Claire Moore, and Sharp, showed that mature adenovirus messenger RNA could be assembled from separated genomic regions—an observation that established RNA splicing and transformed understanding of eukaryotic gene structure. **[P, PNAS paper, 1977; Nobel announcement, October 11, 1993]**

Sharp also helped create a durable bridge between academic molecular biology and private capital. He co-founded Biogen in 1978 and Alnylam Pharmaceuticals in 2002, served for decades on their boards or scientific advisory bodies, received cash, stock, and options for corporate service, invested through family trusts, and joined or advised several additional biotechnology companies and investment organizations. **[P/S, SEC filings, 2002–2025; company histories]**

In the UNABOM record, Sharp was a **target**, not an associate, of Theodore John Kaczynski. Kaczynski mailed Sharp an individualized warning on April 20, 1995, and separately told *The New York Times* that similar letters had gone to Sharp, Richard J. Roberts, and David Hillel Gelernter. No reviewed record supports transforming that perpetrator–target relationship into collaboration, membership in a shared “cybernetic elite,” or a connection between Sharp and Jeffrey Epstein. **[P/J, UNABOM evidence inventory; contemporary reporting, 1995–1996]**

## 2. The Record

### Origins, family, and education, 1944–1974

Sharp was born to Joseph Walter Sharp and Katherin Colvin Sharp in rural Kentucky. Biographical and documentary accounts describe a farming background rather than inherited scientific, financial, or political status. He attended Union College in Barbourville, Kentucky, where he studied chemistry and mathematics. He met Ann Holcombe there; the couple married in 1964. They later had three daughters, identified in SEC trust documents as Christine S. Carey, Helena S. Gordon, and Sarah S. Brokaw. **[S/P, Science History Institute oral history, 2003; Syros investor agreement, 2014]**

After graduating in 1966, Sharp entered the University of Illinois at Urbana-Champaign and completed his chemistry Ph.D. in 1969. He then worked at Caltech, including with Norman Davidson, and later at Cold Spring Harbor Laboratory, where James D. Watson was director. His early work involved DNA and genome mapping, tumor viruses, and adenovirus molecular biology. Sharp’s institutional biography records service at Cold Spring Harbor from 1971 to 1974 before MIT recruited him. **[S, MIT CV; Science History Institute oral history]**

### MIT and RNA splicing, 1974–1984

Sharp joined MIT as an associate professor in 1974, the year MIT’s Center for Cancer Research became an NCI-designated basic research cancer center. Salvador E. Luria had founded the center; David Baltimore and other leaders of the emerging molecular-biology establishment were also at MIT. Sharp became a full professor in 1979. **[P/S, MIT CV; MIT Koch Institute history]**

The work that produced the Nobel Prize grew from attempts to compare adenovirus RNA with adenovirus DNA. In the 1977 PNAS paper “Spliced Segments at the 5′ Terminus of Adenovirus 2 Late mRNA,” Susan M. Berget, Claire Moore, and Sharp reported electron-microscope structures showing that a mature messenger RNA hybridized to DNA segments separated by intervening loops. The finding meant that a gene’s expressed sequence need not be continuous in genomic DNA. Richard J. Roberts’s Cold Spring Harbor group independently reached the same general conclusion. **[P, Berget–Moore–Sharp, 1977; H, later PNAS history]**

The discovery opened the modern study of introns, exons, alternative splicing, and RNA processing. It also revised the earlier “one gene–one continuous coding region” picture of gene organization. Subsequent work by Sharp’s laboratory addressed RNA-processing machinery, transcriptional regulation, viral gene expression, small RNAs, RNA interference, microRNAs, and noncoding RNA. **[P/S, original article; Nobel materials; MIT profile]**

Funding identified in MIT’s contemporaneous 1993 account included the American Cancer Society, National Institutes of Health, National Science Foundation, and MIT Center for Cancer Research. No evidence was found that the discovery resulted from intelligence funding, military behavioral research, MKUltra, or experimentation on human subjects. The decisive studies used adenovirus nucleic acids, cell systems, and molecular-imaging methods. **[P, MIT announcement, October 1993; negative search finding]**

### Biogen and the first biotechnology network, 1978 onward

Sharp co-founded Biogen in 1978 with Walter Gilbert, Charles Weissmann, Heinz Schaller, and Kenneth Murray. Biogen’s own historical record identifies this scientific founding group. The company’s model joined academic molecular biologists, recombinant-DNA intellectual property, venture financing, licensing, and drug development. **[S, Biogen founders’ history]**

Sharp was not identified in the reviewed records as Biogen’s day-to-day chief executive. His documented roles were scientific founder, director beginning in 1982, scientific adviser, shareholder, and recipient of board and consulting compensation. A 2002 proxy reported:

- a $20,000 annual nonemployee-director retainer plus meeting fees;
- a $20,000 scientific-advisory consulting payment ordinarily available to members;
- a special $75,000 consulting payment to Sharp as scientific advisory board chair;
- an option for 30,000 shares at $56.395 per share; and
- beneficial ownership of 982,000 shares or exercisable options, less than 1% of the company.  
  **[P, Biogen SEC proxy, 2002]**

For 2006, Biogen reported Sharp’s director compensation as $62,250 in cash, $35,210 in stock-award accounting value, and $256,388 in option-award accounting value, totaling $353,848. The filing also disclosed his simultaneous Alnylam roles. When Biogen considered its progressive multifocal leukoencephalopathy–related RNA-interference collaboration with Alnylam in September 2006, Sharp did not participate in Biogen’s discussion or vote and was described as not having participated for Alnylam either. The filing treated his dual role as a conflict requiring recusal, not as concealed conduct. **[P, Biogen proxy, 2007]**

Sharp left Biogen’s board in 2009. The reviewed filings document compensation and equity but do not establish his sale proceeds, total realized wealth from Biogen, or present net worth.

### MIT leadership and the 1993 Nobel Prize

Sharp served as associate director of the MIT Center for Cancer Research from 1982 to 1985 and director from 1985 to 1991. He then became head of the Department of Biology and Salvador E. Luria Professor, holding those positions until 1999. **[P/S, MIT CV]**

On October 11, 1993, the Nobel Assembly announced that Sharp and Richard J. Roberts would share the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for split genes. MIT reported that the award was then worth approximately $825,000, to be divided between them. That report identified Susan M. Berget and Claire Moore as co-authors of the 1977 paper and Margarita Siafaca as Sharp’s long-serving laboratory manager. **[P/S, Nobel and MIT announcements, October 1993]**

The Nobel award later became a case study in disputes over attribution. Historian Pnina Geraldine Abir-Am has argued that the simplified Nobel narrative obscured the contributions of several women in the Sharp and Roberts laboratories, including Berget, Moore, and Louise T. Chow. This is a documented historiographical criticism, not an adjudicated finding of fraud or research misconduct. The original article itself names Berget and Moore as authors. **[H, Abir-Am; P, original paper]**

A more specific online account claims Sharp initially wrote an inadequate recommendation that failed properly to credit Susan M. Berget and revised it after intervention by Nancy Hopkins and David Botstein. The accessible versions encountered during this research were secondary or tertiary retellings rather than the original correspondence. That claim therefore remains **unsupported in the reviewed primary record** and should not be stated as established fact. **[U]**

### Federal and institutional power, 1994–2004

President Bill Clinton appointed Sharp to the President’s Committee of Advisors on Science and Technology in 1994 and to the National Cancer Advisory Board in 1996. White House records also identify him as an Alfred P. Sloan Foundation trustee. Sharp later chaired the National Cancer Advisory Board, giving him a formal role in review and oversight of national cancer policy and grant programs. **[P, White House announcement, September 16, 1996; NCI records]**

These were public advisory positions, not intelligence appointments. No reviewed record showed that Sharp held a security clearance, served in the CIA, NSA, FBI, Defense Intelligence Agency, or an intelligence cutout, or advised an intelligence operation.

In 2000 Patrick J. McGovern Jr. and Lore Harp McGovern pledged what MIT described as an anticipated $350 million over twenty years to establish the McGovern Institute for Brain Research. Sharp became founding director. The institute’s stated program joined neuroscience, genetics, imaging, and computation. Patrick McGovern’s wealth came from International Data Group, a technology publishing, research, and events company. No reviewed source tied the gift or institute to MKUltra, CIA behavioral experimentation, or intelligence operations. **[P/S, MIT announcement, February 2000]**

### Alnylam and RNA interference, 2002–2025

Alnylam was organized in 2002 around RNA-interference science and intellectual property associated with Sharp, David P. Bartel, Thomas Tuschl, Phillip D. Zamore, and Paul R. Schimmel. Sharp became a founder, director, and scientific advisory board chair. **[P/S, Alnylam history and SEC registration statement, 2004]**

Alnylam’s 2004 registration statement reported that Sharp had received an option for 70,000 shares in 2003 and was paid $1,250 per month for board and scientific-advisory service. A 2005 filing documented another option for 100,000 shares at $7.77. A consulting agreement effective March 1, 2006, provided $3,000 per month, subsequently reduced to $2,083.33 per month; it was signed by Sharp and chief executive John M. Maraganore. **[P, Alnylam SEC filings, 2004–2013]**

The 2025 proxy reported that Sharp received $80,000 in cash director fees, an option with $398,790 grant-date accounting value, and $25,000 for chairing the scientific advisory board during 2024, totaling $503,790. At December 31, 2024, he held 74,198 unexercised options. As of January 31, 2025, the filing attributed to him 266,899 shares plus 57,720 shares acquirable through options within sixty days, totaling 324,619 and remaining under 1% of the company. Some holdings were through trusts benefiting his children and co-administered with his spouse. He retired from Alnylam’s board effective May 8, 2025. **[P, Alnylam proxy, March 2025]**

The filings establish substantial equity exposure and paid governance roles. They do not establish an exact lifetime profit or personal net worth.

### Patents and institutional litigation

RNA-interference patents generated litigation among institutions and companies. In *Max-Planck-Gesellschaft zur Förderung der Wissenschaften e.V. v. Whitehead Institute for Biomedical Research*, a 2009 federal decision described Sharp, David Bartel, Thomas Tuschl, and Phillip Zamore as named inventors on the “Tuschl I” patent family. The litigation concerned patent prosecution, institutional rights, inventorship disputes, licenses, and contractual obligations. **Sharp was not personally accused of fraud or named as the principal defendant in the reviewed decision.** **[P/A, federal court order, 2009]**

Later settlement records addressed University of Massachusetts inventorship claims and releases. Separate disputes over “Tuschl II” rights involved Alnylam, the University of Utah, and researcher Brenda Bass. Those cases concerned technology surrounding companies Sharp helped govern, but the reviewed records did not establish personal wrongdoing by Sharp. **[P/A, settlement and company records]**

### CPRIT controversy, 2010–2013

The Cancer Prevention and Research Institute of Texas appointed Sharp to lead its Scientific Review Council. CPRIT controlled a multibillion-dollar, taxpayer-financed cancer-research program. Its 2010 annual report documents Sharp’s review leadership. **[P]**

In 2012 Sharp and other peer reviewers resigned after CPRIT management advanced an approximately $18 million commercialization award without normal scientific peer review. Sharp’s resignation letter warned that “grandiose promises and hucksterism” could displace scientific rigor. Nature reporter Monya Baker and other journalists documented the resignations and the bypassed review. **[P/J, resignation correspondence and reporting, October 2012]**

The broader CPRIT affair led to audits, investigations, administrative changes, and the indictment of former executive Jerry Cobbs over representations surrounding an $11 million Peloton Therapeutics grant. Sharp was not accused or indicted; the record places him among the reviewers who protested the process. **[J/P, Texas Tribune and state records, 2012–2013]**

### Later career and present status

Sharp has held governance or advisory positions involving the Whitehead Institute, Broad Institute, Massachusetts General Hospital, Alfred P. Sloan Foundation, National Academy of Sciences, National Academy of Medicine, American Academy of Arts and Sciences, Royal Society, American Association for the Advancement of Science, Stand Up To Cancer, MIT Museum, MIT Jameel Clinic, and Gairdner Foundation. He served as AAAS president and chaired Stand Up To Cancer’s Scientific Advisory Committee from the initiative’s 2008 launch until becoming chair emeritus in 2024. **[P/S, institutional records]**

He remains alive as of the dossier date and is an MIT Institute Professor Emeritus. There is therefore no death, probate, or estate record to report. No single publicly catalogued “Phillip A. Sharp papers” archive was found. The Science History Institute holds a lengthy oral history; documentary traces are otherwise distributed across MIT, company SEC files, scientific journals, federal advisory records, patent proceedings, and institutional archives.

## 5. Ideas and Programs

### Split genes and RNA splicing

Sharp’s central scientific contribution was showing that eukaryotic genes can be discontinuous: expressed RNA sequences are copied from separated genomic regions and joined by splicing. This enabled later concepts of introns, exons, spliceosomes, alternative splicing, and multiple protein products from one genomic locus. **[P/H, 1977 paper and Nobel materials]**

### Transcriptional control

Sharp’s laboratory studied how proteins recognize enhancer and promoter elements and regulate gene expression. Work associated with David Baltimore, Ranjan Sen, Harinder Singh, and others helped characterize nuclear regulatory factors, including the scientific terrain that produced NF-κB research. **[P, publication record]**

### RNA interference and noncoding RNA

Sharp’s laboratory and entrepreneurial network moved into small-RNA regulation, microRNAs, gene silencing, and RNA interference. Alnylam translated RNAi into a drug-development platform. Sharp’s role was that of scientist-founder, director, adviser, inventor, investor, and governance participant—not the sole discoverer of RNAi and not the sole inventor of the underlying patents. **[P, SEC and patent records]**

### Biotechnology as an institutional program

Biogen and Alnylam embodied Sharp’s view that academic molecular biology could be coupled to venture capital, patent licensing, corporate laboratories, and therapeutic development. This produced substantial personal financial interests and positioned him to influence both academic and commercial research agendas. The record shows disclosed corporate service and equity, not a covert funding channel.

### Convergence and “New Biology”

Sharp co-chaired or participated in policy work advocating integration of biology with physical science, engineering, computation, and medicine. The National Academies’ *A New Biology for the 21st Century* was sponsored by the Department of Energy, NIH, National Science Foundation, and other federal bodies. This was a public science-policy program, not a classified cybernetics project. **[P, National Academies record]**

### Neuroscience and computation

As the McGovern Institute’s founding director, Sharp helped build a program combining neuroscience, brain imaging, genetics, cognitive science, and computation. Later, his Jameel Clinic role connected him to machine learning in medicine. These are genuine institutional links between biology and computing. No reviewed evidence connects either program to coercive behavioral experiments, MKUltra, intelligence interrogation, or surveillance operations.

### Biosecurity and dual-use research

Sharp participated in national biosecurity governance, including early National Science Advisory Board for Biosecurity activity. That role concerned oversight of research with possible beneficial and harmful applications. It is evidence of public biosecurity-policy influence, not evidence of intelligence employment. **[P, federal meeting records]**

### Recombinant-DNA contest

Sharp participated in the Cambridge/MIT recombinant-DNA debate of the 1970s, when residents, officials, and scientists contested laboratory risk, containment, and democratic oversight. Later historical work reconstructs how local concern produced a temporary moratorium and municipal review framework. Sharp and other molecular biologists defended the research while accepting regulated containment. **[H/P, archival historical study and documentary interviews]**

### Human subjects

No Sharp-led human-subject experiment, coercive intervention, nonconsensual medical study, or behavioral-modification program was identified. His foundational work used molecular, viral, cellular, and later animal-model systems. Clinical trials conducted by companies on whose boards he served are corporate human-subject programs governed through separate investigators, protocols, and regulatory processes; the reviewed sources did not identify Sharp as a clinical principal investigator.

## 6. Scandal, Controversy, and Contest

### Kaczynski’s threat

On April 20, 1995, Kaczynski mailed Sharp a short warning stating in substance that it would be beneficial to his health to stop research in biotechnology. A parallel letter went to Richard J. Roberts. A separate letter to *The New York Times* referenced Sharp, Roberts, and Gelernter. The Sharp envelope used Manfred Morari’s address without Morari’s knowledge. **[P, UNABOM evidence record]**

Sharp later told a Boston journalist that the warning produced anxiety and FBI mail screening. He said he had not originally read the physical letter and doubted Kaczynski understood much molecular biology. Sharp acknowledged that technological change can produce social pressure but rejected Kaczynski’s violence as indefensible. **[J/S, Anthony Flint reporting, April 1996]**

**Outcome:** Sharp was not attacked physically. Kaczynski was arrested in 1996, pleaded guilty in 1998, and was imprisoned. There is no evidence Sharp knew, employed, funded, advised, or corresponded voluntarily with him.

### Scientific-credit controversy

The original RNA-splicing paper plainly credits Susan M. Berget and Claire Moore. The Nobel went to Sharp and Richard J. Roberts, reflecting the Nobel system’s limit on laureates and its preference for laboratory leaders. Historians, especially Pnina Geraldine Abir-Am, have argued that the contributions of women in both laboratories were subsequently marginalized. **[P/H]**

**Outcome:** No research-misconduct finding, retraction, or correction of the 1977 paper was found. The controversy concerns recognition, hierarchy, and historical narration.

### Biogen–Alnylam conflict of interest

Sharp simultaneously held roles at Biogen and Alnylam when the companies considered a collaboration. Biogen’s proxy disclosed the dual affiliation and Sharp’s recusal. **[P, 2007 proxy]**

**Outcome:** A documented conflict was managed through nonparticipation. No SEC enforcement action or judicial finding of self-dealing by Sharp was located.

### RNAi patent litigation

Institutions and companies disputed inventorship, prosecution strategy, licensing, and ownership of the Tuschl patent families. Sharp was a named inventor and company insider, but the reviewed decisions did not find him personally liable for fraud or patent misconduct. **[P/A]**

**Outcome:** institutional litigation and settlement; no personal indictment or conviction.

### CPRIT

Sharp resigned from CPRIT’s review leadership in 2012 after management bypassed peer review for a major commercialization award. Subsequent investigations and Jerry Cobbs’s indictment substantiated that the institution had serious governance failures. **[P/J]**

**Outcome:** Sharp was a protester and witness to institutional breakdown, not an accused participant.

### Jeffrey Epstein narrative

The Jeffrey Epstein proposition was tested against MIT’s independent fact-finding report, which documented MIT’s acceptance of ten Epstein donations totaling approximately $850,000 between 2002 and 2017 and identified relevant administrators and faculty contacts. Sharp is not named in that report. **[P, MIT/Goodwin Procter report, January 2020]**

No reviewed source placed Sharp in Epstein’s flight records, residences, meetings, correspondence, donations, companies, foundations, criminal cases, or civil depositions. Two name collisions can manufacture a false appearance:

1. **Charles J. Epstein**, the geneticist bombed by Kaczynski, was unrelated to Jeffrey Epstein.
2. **Mark Epstein**, listed with Sharp in MIT Museum governance records, is not Jeffrey Epstein.

**Status:** the asserted Sharp–Jeffrey Epstein tie is unsupported. It has not been established by the evidence cited in the popular framing.

Exact-name searches produced no relevant declassified file, employment record, grant, testimony, contract, or contemporaneous correspondence. Sharp’s proximity to presidential advising, biosecurity oversight, neuroscience, computing, and well-capitalized biotechnology is documented; converting proximity into intelligence affiliation is not.

**Status:** unsupported rumor or inference. No evidence was located that Sharp participated in MKUltra, coercive human experimentation, an intelligence operation, blackmail, trafficking, organized crime, or illegal surveillance.

Corporate and patent litigation around Biogen, Alnylam, and RNAi should not be restated as criminal allegations against him.

## 7. Reception and Afterlife

Sharp’s public image has passed through several phases:

1. **1970s–1980s: molecular-biology innovator.** The 1977 paper and rapid growth of RNA biology made him a central figure in gene-expression research.
2. **1978 onward: scientist-entrepreneur.** Biogen positioned Sharp among the academics who institutionalized biotechnology and the commercialization of recombinant DNA.
3. **1993: Nobel laureate.** News coverage presented him as the MIT co-discoverer of split genes while naming Berget and Moore as collaborators.
4. **1995–1996: target of anti-technology terrorism.** Kaczynski’s warning inserted Sharp into public arguments over biotechnology, technological power, and violence.
5. **2000s: builder of science institutions and companies.** McGovern, Alnylam, federal advisory work, nonprofit boards, and cancer funding expanded his authority beyond the laboratory.
6. **2010s: governance figure.** His CPRIT resignation made him a defender of peer review; corporate filings also made his financial and fiduciary interests more visible.
7. **Recent historical reassessment:** scholarship on women’s contribution to RNA splicing has complicated the lone-genius Nobel narrative without overturning the documented scientific discovery.
8. **Documentary celebration:** *Cracking the Code: Phil Sharp and the Biotech Revolution* presents a broadly admiring account of his Kentucky origins, scientific work, and biotechnology career. The Science History Institute oral history supplies Sharp’s own detailed version.
9. **Online network narratives:** the 2026 Hidden AmuraKa framing places him in a Kaczynski–Epstein–cybernetics story. The Kaczynski-target component is documented; the Epstein and intelligence extensions were not corroborated.
