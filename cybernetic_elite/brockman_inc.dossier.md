# Brockman, Inc.: Research Dossier

## Evidentiary key and scope

- **[P] Primary record:** government filing, tax return, contract, archived correspondence, flight log, institutional archive, or contemporaneous company document.
- **[P/S] Primary document containing a participant’s claim:** the document is authentic evidence that the statement was made, but does not independently prove the statement’s substance.
- **[R] Reported:** attributed reporting by named journalists or established publications.
- **[A] Alleged:** allegation in litigation or by a named accuser; not adjudicated as fact unless an outcome is stated.
- **[S] Subject claim:** statement by Brockman, Inc., John Brockman, Katinka Matson, Max Brockman, or an affiliated entity.
- **[U] Unsupported/rumor:** circulated without adequate documentary support.
- **[D] Disproved, corrected, or retracted.**

“Brockman” is an ambiguous search term. Robert Brockman, the deceased Texas software executive prosecuted for alleged tax evasion, and Greg Brockman of OpenAI are unrelated to this subject. This dossier distinguishes the private, for-profit literary agency **Brockman, Inc.** from **Edge Foundation, Inc.**, Edge.org, and John Brockman personally. Their personnel, offices, clients, communications, and promotional activity overlapped, but they were not legally interchangeable.

No private agency can be reconstructed literally in full from public evidence: author contracts, advances, commissions, ownership records, and correspondence are normally confidential. The largest accessible client snapshot is John Brockman’s own 18 March 2018 list, preserved in Jeffrey Epstein’s records and later released by the Department of Justice. It is reproduced below entry by entry, while being treated as a self-generated “selected clients” list rather than an independently audited roster.

## 1. Identification

**Brockman, Inc.** is an active New York business corporation and literary agency specializing in serious nonfiction, particularly science, technology, psychology, economics, philosophy, and related public-intellectual writing. It was incorporated on **16 May 1973** as **John Brockman Associates, Inc.**, New York entity number **261456**, and renamed **Brockman, Inc.** by an amendment filed **8 September 1993**. Those dates derive from New York Department of State data reproduced by a corporate-registry aggregator. **[P, as reproduced from the state registry]**

The agency’s own current account says John Brockman and his wife and business partner, **Katinka Matson**, founded it; that their son **Max Brockman** has been chief executive since 2012; and that it now employs a thirteen-person team at **260 Fifth Avenue, New York, NY 10001**. It describes its clientele as including fourteen Nobel laureates, three Pulitzer Prize winners, two National Book Award winners, and more than seventy-five *New York Times* bestsellers over twenty years. These are current corporate claims, not independently audited figures. **[S, Brockman, Inc., current “About Us” page, accessed September 2026]**

Historically, the company moved through three connected businesses:

1. a 1970s literary agency for countercultural writers and serious nonfiction;
2. a highly profitable early-1980s book-and-software rights operation;
3. from the 1990s onward, a dominant commercial broker for popular science and “third culture” authors.

Its importance was not simply representing writers. It assembled a market: recruiting scientists, packaging their work for general readers, auctioning domestic and foreign rights, and placing clients in an overlapping publicity and social network organized partly through John Brockman’s separate Edge Foundation.

## 2. The Record

### Origins and incorporation, 1973–1981

John Brockman’s account is that he entered literary representation after meeting neuroscientist and dolphin researcher **John Cunningham Lilly** and philosopher **Alan Watts** at an American University of Masters conference. Several participants complained that they lacked agents; Brockman sold a Lilly proposal for “tens of thousands of dollars,” according to Stewart Brand’s recollection reported by *Wired*. That became the origin story of John Brockman Associates. **[R/S, Warren St. John, *Wired*, September 1999]**

New York corporate data fixes the legal beginning more precisely: **John Brockman Associates, Inc. was incorporated on 16 May 1973**. **[P]** John Brockman’s later digital archive says Katinka Matson joined the business in 1975; the agency’s present site calls both John Brockman and Katinka Matson founders. **[S, undated retrospective and current corporate page]**

The firm’s early clients grew out of John Brockman’s countercultural and technology circles. **Stewart Brand** became both a friend and client. In a surviving 1983 agency agreement, John Brockman Associates contracted to represent Brand’s literary rights through named foreign co-agents, with a **20% total foreign-rights commission divided between Brockman and the co-agent**. That contract documents one transaction’s commission structure; it should not be generalized to every agency contract. **[P, 1983 contract in Computer History Museum archival material]**

### The software and computer-book boom, 1982–1985

The agency’s first major commercial transformation followed John Brockman’s purchase of an IBM PC in 1982. He began presenting software creators as intellectual-property clients analogous to authors.

A March 1983 *Washington Post* report documented that Brockman represented the software house **Bruce & James** and was among the first literary agents openly entering software representation. It quoted Bruce & James president **Jim Edlin** crediting Brockman with opening otherwise inaccessible corporate doors. **[R, Michael Schrage, *Washington Post*, 15 March 1983]**

The principal documented deals were:

- **Typing Tutor III / Simon & Schuster:** Brockman reportedly sold the software program to Simon & Schuster for **$1 million**. **[R, *Wired*, September 1999, based partly on Brockman’s account]**
- **The Whole Earth Software Catalog / Stewart Brand / Doubleday:** Doubleday advanced **$1.3 million** on a twelve-page outline in 1983. *Time* contemporaneously called it a record-sized wager; *Wired* later reported it as the largest advance then paid for a paperback original. **[R, *Time*, 18 July 1983; *Wired*, September 1999]**
- **Annual volume:** A 1985 *Washington Post* profile reported a 1983 estimate of **$20 million in agency deals** and commissions of approximately one-seventh—about **$2.86 million** if applied literally. This was a reported estimate, not a filed financial statement. **[R, Michele Slung, *Washington Post*, 17 March 1985]**

The boom did not last. *Wired* reported that computer manuals saturated the market, many titles did not earn out, publishers retreated, and Brockman dismissed much of the staff and closed recently opened West Coast offices. **[R, September 1999]**

### Building the popular-science market, late 1980s–2000s

After the computer-book contraction, the agency concentrated on mass-market science. Its model was to recruit researchers before they had decided to write for a general audience, produce proposal-driven auctions, retain or separately exploit foreign rights, and market science internationally.

The most consequential program was **Science Masters**, developed by John Brockman with British publisher **Anthony Cheetham** and launched in 1994 with **Basic Books**. Brockman’s archive identifies the initial writers as including **John D. Barrow, Paul Davies, Richard Dawkins, Martin Rees, Ian Stewart, Daniel Dennett, and Richard Leakey**. Rights to the first twelve books were licensed to twenty-six publishers worldwide. Because this account is maintained by Brockman’s Edge site, it is evidence of the program and its participants but also corporate self-history. **[S, John Brockman Digital Archive]**

Contemporaneous publishing coverage supports the market effect. In a 1997 profile reproduced by Edge, Free Press editor **Susan Arellano** said Brockman had increased scientists’ visibility and pushed science advances to a new level. Editors also complained, often anonymously, about inflated advances, rapidly prepared proposals, and delayed or underdeveloped manuscripts. **[R, James Gorman, *New York Times*, 14 October 1997, reproduced by Edge]**

Documented deals and failures include:

- **Murray Gell-Mann, *The Quark and the Jaguar*:** Brockman secured **$550,000 from Bantam**, but Gell-Mann did not deliver on schedule and returned the advance; the book later appeared with another imprint. **[R, *Wired*, September 1999]**
- **An unnamed American astrophysicist / Knopf:** a 2000 German report reproduced by Edge referred to a **$2 million** contract. The accessible article does not reliably identify the writer, so the figure should not be assigned to Brian Greene or another client without the underlying contract. **[R, 2000 report]**
- **Brian Greene:** Publishers Weekly reported in 2004 that Katinka Matson coordinated domestic and foreign rights and that Norton won *The Elegant Universe* in bidding after Greene signed with Brockman. **[R, *Publishers Weekly*, 9 February 2004]**
- **Richard Dawkins:** *Publishers Weekly* documented John Brockman selling North American rights to Dawkins’s proposed intellectual autobiography to Ecco/HarperCollins in 2012. **[R, 4 June 2012]**
- **Daniel Kahneman, Olivier Sibony, and Cass R. Sunstein, *Noise*:** Little, Brown Spark acquired North American rights in March 2018; William Collins separately paid a seven-figure sum in a UK pre-empt. John Brockman privately described the overall transaction as the largest deal ever for serious nonfiction, but no public record establishes the worldwide total or substantiates the superlative. **[R for the acquisitions; P/S for Brockman’s superlative]**

### Digital rights infrastructure

In 1999 the agency introduced **RightsCenter**, a password-controlled extranet for proposals, sample chapters, publicity material, and international-rights transactions. Publishers Weekly reported that RightsCenter was a separate company in which Brockman, Inc. held a minority interest; sellers were to pay **$250 per title**, plus **$150** if the service prepared the material. Brockman claimed the system produced six-figure savings in printing and shipping and sharply accelerated international sales. **[R/S, *Publishers Weekly*, 2 August 1999]**

This is one of the clearer instances in which the agency changed trade mechanics rather than merely selling an individual book: editors could be directed to a controlled digital auction room, while Brockman retained direct control of proposals and foreign-rights traffic.

### Succession and current operation

Max Brockman graduated from the University of Pennsylvania in 2002 and worked at the agency. A wedding announcement circulated by John Brockman in 2011 described Max as vice president; it also identified Max as the son of John Brockman and Katinka Matson. **[P/S, contemporaneous family announcement preserved in the Epstein records]**

The agency says Max became CEO in **2012**. Katinka Matson remains president. John Brockman is now described as founder but is absent from the current public team list. The site does not say whether he retains equity or a formal board position. **[S, current corporate page]**

As of September 2026, the agency publicly lists thirteen staff members and remains at 260 Fifth Avenue. Solow Literary says that beginning in 2026 its founder, **Bonnie Solow**, is operating a new business in partnership with Brockman, Inc.; Bonnie Solow also appears on Brockman’s staff page. **[S, both firms’ current websites]**

There is therefore no evidence that the agency dissolved after the 2019 Epstein scandal. The public evidence instead shows continuity under Max Brockman, continued rights activity, and an additional agency partnership in 2026.

### Archives and papers

- John Brockman maintains a large, self-curated **Online Digital Archive** at Edge.org. **[S]**
- The University of Edinburgh holds Brockman, Inc. correspondence and draft agreements from **1997–2000** in the papers of **Ian Wilmut**, concerning *The Second Creation* and dealings with Farrar, Straus and Giroux and Headline Books. **[P, archival finding aid]**
- A centralized deposit of Brockman, Inc.’s corporate files, client contracts, ledgers, or post-2019 correspondence could not be identified.
- John Brockman, Katinka Matson, and Max Brockman are living as of the latest sources used here; there is consequently no publicly documented estate or disposition of personal papers.

- **Gatekeeping:** The agency could convert academic status into commercial publishing proposals and could determine which scientists entered major trade auctions.

## 5. Ideas and Programs

Brockman, Inc. was not a research laboratory and is not documented as conducting human-subject experiments. Its role was commercial and editorial: selecting, packaging, and circulating ideas generated elsewhere.

### Cybernetics and “third culture”

John Brockman’s intellectual formation included **Norbert Wiener**, **Claude Shannon**, **John Cage**, and **Marshall McLuhan**. His own account says Cage introduced him to Wiener’s cybernetics. Brockman later defined the “third culture” as scientists and empirically oriented thinkers displacing traditional literary intellectuals in explaining human life. **[S]**

That outlook shaped the agency’s list but does not prove that every client subscribed to a common ideology. Richard Dawkins, Daniel Dennett, Steven Pinker, David Gelernter, Noam Chomsky, Sherry Turkle, and others represented sharply different political and philosophical positions.

### Science Masters

Science Masters transformed established researchers’ fields into short, nontechnical, internationally licensed books. Its significance was commercial standardization: a branded list, repeatable format, and coordinated foreign-rights market. **[S, corroborated by publication history]**

### Edge and the annual questions

Edge Foundation’s annual questions produced short essays that were published online and repackaged into books. That created a loop:

`agency clients → Edge visibility and events → edited books and publicity → publisher interest → more agency business`

This is an inference from shared personnel, client lists, book contracts, and event records. It should not be converted into the stronger unsupported claim that Edge contributors were contractually required to use Brockman, Inc.

### RightsCenter

RightsCenter applied internet technology to proposal distribution and rights auctions. It reduced mailing costs, shortened international deal cycles, and kept bidders inside a controlled digital environment. **[R/S]**

### Human-subject research

No human-subject research funded or conducted by Brockman, Inc. was identified. Some clients performed or wrote about human research at universities, but their institutional studies cannot be attributed to the agency without a specific funding or contractual record. Likewise, Epstein funded scientific work at MIT, Harvard, and elsewhere, but no evidence shows that Brockman, Inc. administered those grants.

## 6. Scandal, Controversy and Contest

### The 2018 sovereign-fund acquisition pitch

On **18 March 2018**, John Brockman emailed Jeffrey Epstein under the subject “An idea – PRIVATE.” He said the idea was inspired by Epstein, referred to an unnamed Middle Eastern friend expected in the United States the next day, and proposed that the friend’s sovereign fund acquire Brockman, Inc. The commercial pitch was that the fund would thereby obtain deep access to the agency’s scientific authors. Epstein replied only, “Ok.” **[P for the correspondence; P/S for Brockman’s characterizations]**

The buyer and fund were not named in the email. Identification is nevertheless possible as a strong contemporaneous inference:

- On **16 March 2018**, the *Los Angeles Times* reported that Saudi Arabia’s sovereign fund was considering a less-than-10% investment in **Endeavor**, valued at $400–500 million.
- On **18 March**, Bloomberg-derived reporting said the **Public Investment Fund of Saudi Arabia** was taking a **$400 million** stake, scheduled to close as soon as **19 March**, immediately before Crown Prince **Mohammed bin Salman** met President Donald Trump.
- Brockman’s email referred to a Middle Eastern visitor, a 10% Endeavor stake, $400 million, and a “Sovereign Fund.”

Accordingly, the intended buyer was almost certainly **Saudi Arabia’s Public Investment Fund**, associated with Mohammed bin Salman. **[R-based inference]** The email does not prove that Mohammed bin Salman personally knew of the Brockman proposal.

Epstein’s documented role was recipient and possible intermediary: Brockman told him the idea was inspired by him and asked to discuss it. No term sheet, valuation, diligence file, board authorization, message forwarding the proposal to Saudi officials, payment, or completed transaction was located. The claim that Epstein “sold” or formally brokered Brockman, Inc. is therefore unsupported. **[Finding]**

After journalist Jamal Khashoggi’s murder, Endeavor returned the separate Saudi investment in 2019. That does not establish anything about the unconsummated Brockman idea.

### Epstein funding and access

Peter Aldhous’s 26 September 2019 BuzzFeed News investigation analyzed Edge Foundation IRS filings and reported that Epstein-linked foundations supplied **$638,000 of roughly $857,000 in donations between 2001 and 2015**, sometimes functioning as Edge’s only donor. It also calculated about **$1.48 million** in Edge “book contract” revenue. **[P-derived R]**

Epstein attended Edge functions both before and after his 2008 Florida conviction. Archived invitations and emails show that he remained in contact with John Brockman through at least 2018. Reporting found that some public Edge pages or captions identifying Epstein were later altered or removed. Yahoo Finance documented missing photographs and a changed aircraft caption in July 2019. **[R]**

The defensible finding is that Edge gave Epstein repeated access to prominent scientists and technology executives and that John Brockman continued to introduce or promote him after knowing he had served jail time. There is no public evidence that Brockman, Inc., John Brockman, Katinka Matson, or Max Brockman participated in Epstein’s sexual abuse or trafficking. None has been charged in connection with Epstein’s crimes. **[Finding]**

### Diversity and conflicts of interest

Aldhous reported that roughly **80% of the more than 900 Edge contributors were men**. Researcher and Brockman client **Kate Darling** wrote in August 2019 that she had raised the lack of diversity with Max Brockman. **[R/named participant]**

The overlap of agency clients, an ostensibly intellectual nonprofit, donor-funded dinners, and book anthologies created potential conflicts: representation, publicity, philanthropy, and elite social access could reinforce one another without transparent separation. That is a structural inference, not evidence of a criminal scheme.

### Murray Gell-Mann and undelivered books

The Gell-Mann transaction became a publishing-industry controversy because the large advance was returned when the manuscript was not delivered on schedule. John Brockman rejected broader criticism that his authors routinely failed to deliver, calling it false and insisting that editing was the publisher’s responsibility. **[R, with subject response, *Wired*, 1999]**

### Gina Kolata proposal

Brockman urged *New York Times* science reporter Gina Kolata to capitalize immediately on her front-page reporting about Judah Folkman’s angiogenesis research. Critics argued that a reporter appeared poised to profit from a story she had helped elevate; she later abandoned the book. **[R, *Wired*, 1999]**

### John Brockman’s 1986 plagiarism controversy

John Brockman—not Brockman, Inc. as a corporate author—asked Viking to withdraw *Einstein, Gertrude Stein, Wittgenstein & Frankenstein* after James Gleick identified passages closely resembling his *New York Times Magazine* writing. Brockman acknowledged inadequate attribution and blamed material prepared by an assistant. The book’s withdrawal is documented; responsibility remains described through Brockman’s account rather than a reported judicial finding. **[R; acknowledged by subject]**

### Kaczynski and David Gelernter

On **24 June 1993**, Theodore Kaczynski’s mailed bomb seriously injured Yale computer scientist **David Gelernter**, later documented as a Brockman author. The FBI records the victim as a prominent Yale computer scientist who lost several fingers. **[P]**

Popular documentaries and online narratives describe this as the Unabomber “attacking Brockman’s network.” That formulation is misleading:

- Gelernter was indeed represented by Brockman. **[R]**
- Kaczynski targeted Gelernter because of Gelernter’s technological work and ideas, including *Mirror Worlds*, according to evidence and later accounts. **[P/R]**
- No record located shows Kaczynski selected Gelernter because of Brockman, attacked Brockman, attacked the agency’s office, or possessed a verified “Brockman client list.” **[Finding]**

The real tie is therefore **client–victim**, not documented agency targeting.

### CIA, MKUltra, intelligence, and military claims

The contested-material search produced three distinct claims that are frequently collapsed:

1. **John Brockman said** that in the late 1960s he consulted for General Electric, Columbia Pictures, Scott Paper, the Joint Chiefs of Staff/Pentagon, and the White House on cybernetics and media. **[S]**
2. Those claimed consulting activities predate the agency’s 1973 incorporation and were personal to John Brockman. No contracts, security-clearance files, procurement records, or declassified agency documents were located to verify their nature or duration. **[Uncorroborated S]**
3. Films and conspiracy-oriented accounts connect Brockman’s cybernetic milieu to MKUltra, the Macy Conferences, military research, and intelligence history. No primary record located shows that **Brockman, Inc.** was a CIA front, received CIA or MKUltra funding, recruited for an intelligence service, conducted mind-control research, or participated in Kaczynski’s Harvard experiments. **[U as applied to the company]**

The absence of an agency record does not resolve John Brockman’s earlier consulting claims, but it does refute treating those claims as already-proven institutional facts about Brockman, Inc.

### Lawsuits, indictments, and sealed matters

Exact-name searches of federal case indexes, government sites, publishing coverage, and corporate sources did not identify a criminal indictment, regulatory prosecution, adjudicated fraud case, or material civil judgment against this Brockman, Inc. The most visible court-search hits concern unrelated people named Brockman or list the agency as a rights contact. **[Finding]**

No conclusion can be drawn about sealed proceedings or private arbitration. No evidence of such proceedings was found.

## 9. Findings

1. **Founding in 1973 is documented.** The exact legal date is 16 May 1973, initially under John Brockman Associates, Inc.; Brockman, Inc. dates as the corporate name from September 1993.

2. **The “commercial engine” characterization is substantially supported.** The firm did more than transmit manuscripts. It recruited scientists, engineered proposal auctions, raised advances, controlled foreign rights, built branded series, created a digital rights exchange, and overlapped with an elite publicity and event network.

3. **Richard Dawkins, Steven Pinker, Daniel Dennett, and David Gelernter are confirmed clients.** Ray Kurzweil’s participation in Edge is documented, but an agency relationship could not be verified.

4. **Katinka Matson and Max Brockman were not peripheral.** Katinka was co-founder/president and a working agent handling domestic and international rights. Max rose through the firm and has been CEO since 2012.

5. **The 2018 acquisition email is authentic and significant, but often overstated.** It was an informal pitch from John Brockman to Jeffrey Epstein, not a completed sale or located term sheet. Saudi Arabia’s PIF is the strongly inferred proposed buyer; it is not named in the email.

6. **Epstein’s role is documented as donor, attendee, correspondent, passenger-provider, prospective intermediary, and beneficiary of introductions.** No evidence found makes him an owner, employee, or agent of Brockman, Inc., or shows that he completed the proposed sovereign-fund approach.

7. **Edge Foundation and Brockman, Inc. must be separated legally but studied together operationally.** They shared family leadership, addresses, intellectual material, authors, publicity, and events. Epstein’s documented donations went to Edge, not demonstrably to the for-profit agency.

8. **The agency survived 2019.** It continues under Max Brockman, with Katinka Matson as president, a current staff and address, ongoing rights activity, and a 2026 partnership with Solow Literary.

9. **No criminal or intelligence-agency case against Brockman, Inc. was found.** Claims that the agency was a CIA/MKUltra operation, protected Epstein’s trafficking, or was directly targeted by Kaczynski remain unsupported.

10. **The evidentiary link to Kaczynski is narrow:** David Gelernter was both a Brockman author and a Unabomber victim. Evidence indicates Kaczynski targeted Gelernter’s technological ideas, not his literary representation.
