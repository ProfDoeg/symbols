## Evidentiary key and principal findings

- **P — Primary record:** contemporaneous publication, official directory, society record, archival finding aid, government document, or correspondence.
- **R — Retrospective institutional record:** memorial minute, university history, professional-society history, or obituary.
- **S — Subject/participant recollection:** later memoir, interview, or autobiographical account.
- **J — Secondary scholarly or journalistic account:** named historian or reporter interpreting records.
- **D — Directory claim:** biographical directory or comparable compilation not independently corroborated.
- **A — Allegation:** lawsuit, accuser, or other formal allegation, not established fact.
- **U — Unsupported/rumor:** asserted without traceable documentary support.
- **X — Contradicted or materially corrected:** inconsistent with stronger evidence.

The defensible record identifies **Arthur Kaskel Solomon (1912–2002)** as an American physical chemist and biophysicist who established Harvard Medical School’s Biophysical Laboratory, initiated Harvard’s interdisciplinary graduate program in biophysics in 1959, conducted membrane-transport and radioisotope research, helped formalize the Biophysical Society, and became the first secretary-general of the International Union for Pure and Applied Biophysics. During the Second World War he worked on British radar, antiaircraft control, and the Servo Panel—not, on the evidence found, the Manhattan Project. **[R/P, 2004–05 Harvard memorial; P/J, control-engineering history]** [Harvard memorial](https://news.harvard.edu/gazette/story/2005/02/arthur-k-solomon/) [control history](https://www.control.lth.se/fileadmin/control/Education/DoctorateProgram/HistoryOfControl/2016/Bissell_history_of_automatic_control.pdf)

## 1. Identification

**Arthur Kaskel Solomon**, ordinarily published as **Arthur K. Solomon** or **A. K. Solomon**, was born in Pittsburgh, Pennsylvania, on November 26, 1912, and died at his Cambridge, Massachusetts, home on November 6, 2002. No genuine alias was found. He was an American physical chemist, biophysicist, research administrator, professional-society organizer, art collector, and—according to a biographical directory—drugstore-company director and president. His documented principal offices were assistant professor of physiological chemistry at Harvard Medical School from 1946, head of its Biophysical Laboratory, professor of biophysics from 1968, chairman or director of Harvard’s biophysics graduate program from 1959 to 1980, and professor emeritus after his 1982–83 retirement. He was elected to the American Academy of Arts and Sciences in 1956. **[R/P; exact academic-rank dates partly D]** [Harvard memorial](https://news.harvard.edu/gazette/story/2005/02/arthur-k-solomon/) [American Academy](https://www.amacad.org/person/arthur-kaskel-solomon) [directory](https://prabook.com/web/arthur.solomon/700607)

Harvard’s surviving institutional history describes him as the initiator—not merely a later chairman—of the **Committee on Higher Degrees in Biophysics** in 1959. The committee still administers Harvard’s biophysics PhD. **[P/R, Harvard program page last updated 2001; current GSAS rules dated July 15, 2026]** [program history](https://arep.med.harvard.edu/biophysics/) [current GSAS policy](https://gsas.harvard.edu/policy/biophysics)

## 2. The Record

### Origins, inheritance, and education

Solomon’s parents were **Mark K. Solomon** and **Hortense Nattans Solomon**. Hortense died during the 1918 influenza pandemic when Arthur was six, after which he lived with his father and an aunt. He attended Shady Side Academy. The Harvard memorial says his maternal grandfather, Baltimore drugstore proprietor **Arthur Nattans**, left him interests in a drugstore chain that gave him substantial financial security at an early age. It supplies no probate record, valuation, ownership percentage, or date of transfer. **[R, 2004–05; amount unverified]**

At Princeton University he studied chemistry, graduated in 1934, and wrote an undergraduate thesis on radioactivity. Photography led to meetings with gallery owner and photographer **Alfred Stieglitz**, through whom Solomon reportedly bought his first paintings. **[R, 2004–05]**

At Harvard he worked first with physical chemist **Oscar K. Rice** and then **George Bogdan Kistiakowsky**, receiving a PhD in 1937 for research on the spectrum of deuterated benzene. Kistiakowsky later became a major Manhattan Project figure; that adviser–student relationship is probably one source of subsequent conflation, but it does not establish Solomon’s participation in the bomb project. **[R; P-negative for Manhattan participation]**

From 1937 to 1939 Solomon worked at the Cavendish Laboratory, Cambridge, after being accepted by **Ernest Rutherford**. He helped construct a cyclotron and worked on radiation counters and scaling circuits. A biographical directory additionally lists a Cambridge PhD in physics in 1947 and DSc in 1964; these degrees were not independently confirmed in a Cambridge registry during this research. **[R for laboratory work; D for degrees]**

Solomon wrote popular-science pieces for *Discovery*, then edited by **Charles Percy Snow**, and developed a lasting friendship with Snow. Those pieces became *Why Smash Atoms?* Bibliographic records identify a revised Harvard University Press edition in 1946, while the Harvard memorial dates an earlier version to 1940 and other catalogs record a British edition in 1945. These are edition differences, not necessarily an error. **[P/R]** [bibliographic record](https://cir.nii.ac.jp/crid/1971149384851246849)

### Prewar metabolism research

After returning to Harvard in August 1939, Solomon joined Kistiakowsky’s laboratory. Harvard president **James Bryant Conant** had asked physiologist **Albert Baird Hastings** whether animals could synthesize glucose from carbon dioxide. Hastings sought Kistiakowsky’s assistance, and Solomon was assigned to the tracer work. A 1941 paper credited Conant, **Richard D. Cramer**, Hastings, **Alexander Klemperer**, Solomon, and **Birgit Vennesland** in the resulting carbon-metabolism work. **[R referring to contemporaneous publication]**

### War service: radar and feedback control, not the Manhattan Project

Solomon’s first wartime work evaluated the permeability of skin to chemical-warfare gases. He then volunteered for British radar work, serving in Britain from approximately 1941 through 1944. Harvard’s memorial describes radar service in England and says the British government and King honored him, but does not identify the decoration. **[R]**

A technical history of automatic control identifies “Solomon,” head of a special radar group at Malvern, as the initiator in 1942 of the British **Servo Panel**, which met roughly monthly from May 1942 to August 1945. The panel disseminated work on feedback, radar tracking, and antiaircraft gun control among government, military, university, and industrial researchers. **Arthur Porter** served as secretary and **Douglas Rayner Hartree** became chairman. **[J based on wartime-control records and Porter’s memoir]**

This work is genuinely adjacent to the history from which cybernetics emerged: radar tracking, prediction, automatic gun laying, feedback, and servomechanisms. It is not evidence of membership in Wiener’s later cybernetics circle, however, and it is distinct from atomic-bomb development.

No Manhattan Project roster, Atomic Heritage biography, Department of Energy history, Harvard memorial, or wartime career account located for Solomon places him at Los Alamos, Chicago’s Metallurgical Laboratory, Oak Ridge, or another Manhattan Engineer District project. Instead, the record consistently puts him in Britain on radar and servo work. The statement “Solomon worked on the Manhattan Project” is therefore **unsupported and likely a conflation with Kistiakowsky or Solomon’s later Atomic Energy Commission research**. **[P/R-negative; X as an affirmative claim]**

Solomon returned to the United States in January 1945 and worked briefly in public relations at the **MIT Radiation Laboratory**. There he met **Gerard Piel** and **Dennis Flanagan**, later becoming their friend and an initial investor in the relaunched *Scientific American*. No amount invested was found. **[R]**

### Harvard Medical School and the Biophysical Laboratory

After approximately one year in an administrative post at the **American Cancer Society**, arranged by Hastings, Solomon became assistant professor of physiological chemistry at Harvard Medical School effective July 1, 1946. He was placed in charge of what became the Biophysical Laboratory in Building D. **[R]**

The laboratory initially:

- distributed and sometimes purified radioactive materials;
- built and maintained radiation counters and scalers;
- counted specimens submitted by other Harvard researchers; and
- helped formulate permissible-exposure guidance for patients and experimental subjects.

**[R, 2004–05 Harvard memorial]**

This was an institutional control point for radioactive tracers at postwar Harvard, but no evidence was found that Solomon controlled all Harvard human-radioisotope research or participated in the later Fernald School or Wrentham State School studies.

From about 1950 onward his central scientific subject was transport across biological membranes: sodium and potassium flux, water permeability, red-cell membranes, renal and intestinal epithelia, and the existence and size of aqueous pores. He used animal preparations, plant cells, isolated human blood, and some in-vivo human studies. **[P/R]**

Solomon formally became professor of biophysics in 1968. He retired in 1983 but kept a laboratory into the mid-1990s and continued teaching or participating in the graduate program until shortly before his death. **[R]**

### Harvard’s graduate program

Harvard’s program history states directly that Solomon initiated the Committee on Higher Degrees in Biophysics in 1959. The program joined Harvard Medical School and the Faculty of Arts and Sciences rather than belonging to a conventional department. Harvard says Solomon ran it through 1980; one Cambridge obituary gives 1981, a minor one-year discrepancy. **[P/R]**

The phrase “founder of Harvard’s biophysics program” is therefore supported. “Founder of biophysics at Harvard” would be too broad: biophysical work existed before the committee and outside Solomon’s laboratory.

### Scientific and international institutions

The Biophysical Society’s own history says the original 1956 organizing “Committee of Four” comprised **Samuel A. Talbot**, **Kenneth Stewart Cole**, **Ernest Charles Pollard**, and **Otto Herbert Schmitt**—not Solomon. Solomon was local-arrangements chairman for the second meeting, held in Cambridge in early 1958, at which the constitution and bylaws were ratified and the Society was formally founded. Thus “founder” is reasonable in the broad institutional sense, but he was not one of the four initiators. **[P/R; correction of an inflated version]** [Biophysical Society history](https://www.biophysics.org/Portals/0/BPSAssets/Current/SocietyHistory.pdf)

Solomon helped organize the body that became the **International Union for Pure and Applied Biophysics**, founded in Stockholm on August 2, 1961, and served as its first secretary-general, generally dated 1961–72. In 1968 he published his own short history of its foundation. **[P/R]** [IUPAB history](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/quarterly-reviews-of-biophysics/article/abs/short-history-of-the-foundation-of-the-international-union-for-pure-and-applied-biophysics/86F6D0FC42ABCD3E1BE624C7BA5FED22)

A directory attributes to him committee or advisory service for the National Research Council, National Institutes of Health, Brookhaven National Laboratory, National Academy of Sciences, UNESCO, and International Council of Scientific Unions. The detailed dates in that directory have not all been independently corroborated; Princeton’s memorial independently confirms that he was a scientific member of United States delegations to UNESCO meetings in 1976 and 1978. **[D, partly R]** [Princeton memorial](https://www.princeton.edu/~paw/archive_new/PAW02-03/12-0326/memorials.html)

### Art, family, death, and archives

Solomon married **Jean Roth** in 1944 after meeting her in Cambridge in 1938; she had served in Britain with the American Red Cross. The disposition of that marriage was not established in the consulted sources. **[R]**

He met **Mariot Fraser “Marny” Matthews** in 1968 and married her in 1972. Harvard’s archival finding aid identifies this as a second marriage for Marny and documents their joint collecting. Solomon’s children were **Mark Solomon** and **Susanna Solomon Van Leuven**; Marny’s obituary calls them her stepson and stepdaughter. **[P/R]** [Mariot Solomon papers](https://hollisarchives.lib.harvard.edu/catalog/art00056) [Mariot obituary](https://www.dignitymemorial.com/obituaries/boston-ma/mariot-solomon-9376795)

His art advisers and contacts included Stieglitz, Harvard connoisseur **Paul Joseph Sachs**, dealer **Justin K. Thannhauser**, Thannhauser’s son **Heinz Thannhauser**, artist **Pablo Picasso**, and Fogg Museum curator **Marjorie “Jerry” Cohn**. The collection ultimately ranged from Old Master prints and nineteenth-century French art to modern sculpture and Color Field painting. **[R/P archival]**

Solomon died on November 6, 2002. No public probate accounting or estate valuation was found. Harvard records that his trust endowed the **Arthur K. Solomon Professorship of Biophysics** and provided fellowship support in the Faculty of Arts and Sciences; the amount was not disclosed. **[R, 2022]** [HMS trust account](https://hms.harvard.edu/sites/default/files/users/user14021h/pulse_fall22.pdf)

The principal located archival body is not a standalone scientific archive but the **Papers of Mariot F. Solomon, 1947–2013**, four boxes donated by her estate in 2021 and processed in 2025. They contain correspondence, photographs, notebooks, appointments, and materials on both Solomons’ art collecting. Some files have access restrictions. Harvard also produced transcripts of oral-history interviews with Arthur Solomon in 1993–95, but an accessible authoritative catalog or full digital transcript was not located. **[P archival finding aid; incomplete-access finding]**

More than 260 works from the Solomon collection reached the Harvard Art Museums by bequest in 2021. Harvard published a collection catalog and mounted an exhibition in 2025. No public monetary appraisal was found. **[P/R]** [Harvard Art Museums catalog](https://shop.harvardartmuseums.org/products/the-solomon-collection-durer-to-degas-and-beyond)

## 5. Ideas and Programs

### Biophysics rather than a general cybernetic doctrine

Solomon’s published work does not present a broad cybernetic social theory. His program was experimental biophysics: bring physical measurement, isotopic tracing, instrumentation, quantitative transport theory, and interdisciplinary training to biological problems. His 1948 article “Physics and Cancer” argued that physics contributed both instruments and conceptual discipline to biology but warned that biological systems contained many poorly controllable variables. **[P, originally May 1948; reprinted 2002]** [“Physics and Cancer”](https://physicstoday.aip.org/features/physics-and-cancer)

His wartime Servo Panel work belongs to the engineering prehistory of cybernetics because it concerned feedback and automatic control. His later membrane research concerned biological regulation and transport. Neither fact establishes adherence to the Macy group’s social, psychological, or philosophical cybernetics.

### Radioactive tracers and human subjects

The record requires distinctions among different studies:

1. **Paid Harvard medical students and radioactive iron, reported 1948.** Solomon wrote contemporaneously that some Harvard Medical School students received small stipends to serve as subjects in studies of uptake of minute quantities of radioactive iron. The article does not identify the principal investigator, protocol, doses, subject names, or Solomon’s own operational role. **[P; direct statement by Solomon, not proof he ran the study]**
2. **Deuterium-oxide body-water study, 1950.** Solomon, Schloerb, Friis-Hansen, Edelman, and Moore measured total body water in human subjects using deuterium oxide. Deuterium is a stable isotope, so this was tracer research but not ionizing-radiation exposure. The paper documents AEC, ONR, American Cancer Society, Eugene Higgins Trust, and Massachusetts Heart Association support. **[P]** [JCI article](https://www.jci.org/articles/view/102366)
3. **Intracranial tumors and phosphorus, 1950.** Selverstone and Solomon published a preliminary report on phosphorus uptake in human intracranial tumors. The bibliographic record identifies human subjects but does not disclose consent procedures. **[P]**
4. **ACTH/cortisone and human erythrocytes, 1954.** Streeten and Solomon studied infusions and then tested blood samples in vitro. The publication record identifies human material and in-vivo infusion. **[P]**
5. **Radioactive sodium, 1955.** Solomon and Gold reported in-vivo sodium-turnover measurements in healthy young adults using radioactive sodium. The abstracted record does not state consent procedures. **[P]**
6. **Human blood and red-cell work.** Numerous other papers used donated or clinically drawn human blood in vitro to study potassium, sodium, water, steroids, and membrane permeability. **[P]**

These publications predate modern institutional-review boards and the comprehensive federal rules issued in the 1970s. The papers reviewed do not supply enough information to establish whether consent was written, oral, or absent. It would therefore be inaccurate either to certify informed consent or to accuse Solomon of nonconsensual experimentation. **[P-negative]**

The Department of Energy’s later human-radiation review found that federal agencies sponsored thousands of tracer experiments during 1944–74, that surviving records are often fragmentary, and that many tracer doses were probably comparable to later research doses. That general finding contextualizes but does not adjudicate Solomon’s individual protocols. **[P, 1995]** [DOE experiment-list methodology](https://ehss.energy.gov/ohre/roadmap/experiments/index.html) [Advisory Committee findings](https://ehss.energy.gov/ohre/roadmap/achre/chap17_1.html)

No evidence was found tying Solomon to the notorious Fernald School cereal studies, the Wrentham iodine studies, plutonium injections, total-body-irradiation programs, or covert behavioral experiments.

### Membrane transport and rehydration

Solomon’s laboratory quantified water and ion movement across red cells, kidney tubules, intestine, gastric mucosa, plants, and artificial or isolated preparations. The laboratory’s work supported the proposition that water crosses membranes through selective aqueous pathways rather than simply dissolving uniformly through lipid. **[P/R]**

Work with Peter Curran on coupled sodium and water transport in intestine contributed to the physiological foundation on which oral-rehydration therapy for cholera was developed. The sources support a scientific contribution, not the claim that Solomon alone invented oral rehydration. **[R/P]**

### Institutional program building

Solomon’s most durable programmatic creation was Harvard’s interdisciplinary biophysics doctorate. Its Committee on Higher Degrees permitted students to work across physics, chemistry, biology, medical departments, hospitals, and later computational disciplines. The committee survives in Harvard’s 2026 degree rules. **[P/R]**

## 6. Scandal, Controversy, and Contest

### Manhattan Project

**Claim:** Solomon worked on the Manhattan Project.

**Finding:** Unsupported. His documented wartime work was British poison-gas permeability, radar, automatic gun control, and the Servo Panel, followed by brief MIT Radiation Laboratory service. His doctoral adviser Kistiakowsky did join the Manhattan Project. **Evidence tier: X/U.**

### Macy conferences

**Claim:** Solomon was a “Harvard biophysics figure of the Macy period” or member of the Macy circle.

**Finding:** “Of the Macy period” is chronologically true but vague. Solomon is absent from the recovered attendee lists for the ten conferences. Brockman later asserted friendship or collegiality with Wiener, but no primary corroboration was located. **Evidence tier: U for attendance; S for Wiener relationship.**

### The 1965 telephone call

**Claim:** Stewart Brand recalled receiving or witnessing Solomon’s call.

**Finding:** The accessible account belongs to John Brockman. Brockman says Solomon and Rosenblith called him after press coverage of the Expanded Cinema Festival and invited him to bring artists to Cambridge. Brand enters Brockman’s narrative later, when Brockman visited him in Menlo Park and they read Wiener together. **Evidence tier: S; X for Brand attribution.**

### Epstein

**Claim:** Solomon is connected to Jeffrey Epstein through the cybernetics/Edge network.

**Finding:** No direct contact, donation, meeting, letter, flight, employment, or institutional transaction was found. Primary email records do document a much later Brockman–Epstein relationship, including invitations and correspondence, but Solomon died in 2002 and the 1965 meeting preceded that relationship by decades. A copied AI book containing the Solomon anecdote appearing in an Epstein-related document dump is not evidence of contact with Solomon. **Evidence tier: P for Brockman–Epstein; P-negative/U for Solomon–Epstein.** [2010 DOJ-released email](https://www.justice.gov/epstein/files/DataSet%2010/EFTA01815334.pdf) [2018 DOJ-released email](https://www.justice.gov/epstein/files/DataSet%2011/EFTA02503969.pdf) [House release notice](https://oversight.house.gov/release/oversight-committee-releases-epstein-records-provided-by-the-department-of-justice/)

### CIA and MKUltra

**Claim:** Solomon belonged to an intelligence or mind-control network.

**Finding:** No CIA employment file, security-clearance record, declassified cable, MKUltra subproject, foundation pass-through, congressional testimony, lawsuit, or named participant account was found. British wartime radar and later AEC biomedical funding were government relationships but are not evidence of CIA or MKUltra activity. **Evidence tier: P-negative/U.**

### Ted Kaczynski

**Claim:** Solomon is part of a network targeted by or connected with the Unabomber.

**Finding:** No direct tie was found. The relevant cultural genealogy runs through Lutz Dammbeck’s 2003 film *Das Netz*, which juxtaposed Kaczynski, cybernetics, Brockman, Brand, and other countercultural figures. Juxtaposition is not a documented relationship. Brockman’s own archive describes Dammbeck’s thesis as the director’s argument. **Evidence tier: S/J for the film’s narrative; U for a Solomon–Kaczynski tie.**

### Human experimentation

**Issue:** Solomon’s laboratory operated during a period of extensive government-sponsored isotope research and before modern consent rules.

**Finding:** Human studies and government funding are documented. Consent practices are generally not stated in the publications reviewed. No source found accuses Solomon personally of coercion, concealment, injury, or unauthorized dosing, and no litigation or official finding against him was located. The proper status is **documented human-subject research with unresolved consent documentation**, not exoneration and not an abuse finding. **Evidence tier: P/P-negative.**

### Legal and personal scandal

No indictment, conviction, civil judgment, settlement, professional-disciplinary action, sexual-abuse allegation, trafficking allegation, fraud case, sealed criminal matter, or documented organized-crime tie involving this Arthur Kaskel Solomon was located. Search results for “Arthur Solomon” frequently concern other people; middle name, Harvard affiliation, birth date, and profession are essential identifiers. **[P-negative]**

## 7. Reception and Afterlife

During his lifetime Solomon’s public image was principally that of a membrane biophysicist, scientific organizer, teacher, and collector. His popular book *Why Smash Atoms?* and 1948 *Physics Today* essay placed him in science communication as well as laboratory work. **[P]**

Harvard’s 2004–05 memorial—written by **John E. Dowling**, Jared Diamond, **Eugene Patrick Kennedy**, **James M. Hogle**, and Marny Solomon—consolidated the image of a “two cultures” figure linking science and art. Because his widow coauthored it, it is richly informed but commemorative rather than independent investigative biography. **[R]**

Brockman’s later autobiographical writing, especially the introduction associated with *Possible Minds* in 2019 and his online archive, repositioned Solomon as an early bridge between cybernetics and avant-garde art. The underlying event is supported only by Brockman’s retrospective narration so far located. **[S]**

Dammbeck’s 2003 *Das Netz* supplied a broader popular template connecting cybernetics, counterculture, the internet, LSD, and Kaczynski. Hidden AmuraKa’s 2026 video, as described in the brief, adds Epstein and assigns the telephone memory to Brand. The precise video could not be recovered through indexed search, so its claims cannot be quoted or audited beyond the description supplied. The Brand attribution conflicts with Brockman’s record. **[U/X]**

The 2025 Harvard Art Museums exhibition and catalog shifted attention back to Solomon and Marny as collectors and benefactors, documenting more than 260 bequeathed works. **[P/R]**
