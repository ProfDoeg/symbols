# Palantir Technologies: Research Dossier

*Cutoff: September 28, 2026.*

## Evidentiary key and scope

- **[P] Primary record:** SEC filing, contract, procurement notice, court opinion, congressional testimony, audit, or government publication.
- **[R] Reported:** reporting by identified journalists, generally based on documents or named sources.
- **[C] Company/subject claim:** statement by Palantir Technologies or one of its officers, not independently established merely by publication.
- **[A] Allegation:** allegation in litigation, testimony, a government complaint, or by a named accuser; not an adjudicated fact unless stated.
- **[U] Unsupported/rumor:** a claim circulated without traceable documentary support.
- **[D] Disproved, contradicted, or materially corrected.**

“Every tie” below means every material tie identifiable in the public records and reporting reviewed, including every tie specified in the brief. Palantir’s classified work, subcontracting, sealed settlements, confidential customer agreements, and undisclosed beneficial owners make a literally exhaustive network impossible. That incompleteness is itself part of the record.

## 1. Identification

Palantir Technologies Inc. is a Delaware-incorporated software company formed on May 6, 2003. Its principal executive office listed in the 2026 proxy is 19505 Biscayne Boulevard, Suite 2350, Aventura, Florida. Its software integrates data, manages permissions, represents entities and relationships through an “ontology,” and supports analysis and operational decisions. Its principal platforms are Gotham, Foundry, Apollo, and the Artificial Intelligence Platform, or AIP. **[P]** The company says it was created to bring software modeled partly on PayPal’s fraud-detection methods to the United States intelligence community. **[C]**

The standard public founding account names Peter Andreas Thiel, Alexander Caedmon Karp, Stephen Cohen, Joseph Todd “Joe” Lonsdale, and Nathan Gettings. Contemporary corporate governance documents, however, define only Alexander Karp, Stephen Cohen, and Peter Thiel as the capitalized “Founders” who participate in Palantir’s unusual Class F voting arrangement. **[P/R]** This is a difference in definition, not proof that Joseph Lonsdale or Nathan Gettings had no founding role.

Alexander Karp has served as chief executive officer since 2005; Stephen Cohen is president and secretary; Peter Thiel has chaired the board since 2003; and Shyam Sankar is chief technology officer and executive vice president. Palantir’s direct listing began on the New York Stock Exchange on September 30, 2020, under the ticker PLTR. **[P]**

The name “Palantir” comes from J.R.R. Tolkien’s fictional seeing stones. That is a naming reference, not evidence of any relationship with Tolkien, his estate, or the literary works’ rights holders.

## 2. The Record

### Formation and early intelligence work, 2003–2009

Palantir was incorporated in Delaware on May 6, 2003. The company’s 2020 registration statement says it was founded in 2003 and initially built software for intelligence-community counterterrorism work. **[P/C]** Peter Thiel has sometimes described the operational start as 2004, reflecting a distinction between incorporation and active development rather than a demonstrated contradiction.

The technology’s intellectual and personnel lineage ran through PayPal. Peter Thiel and early engineers believed PayPal’s combination of algorithms, link analysis, and human investigators could be adapted to intelligence problems. Fortune reported that the initial technology grew out of PayPal’s anti-fraud work; Peter Thiel likewise described a hybrid human-machine system rather than fully automatic detection. **[R/C]**

In-Q-Tel, the CIA-backed venture-capital organization, became Palantir’s first institutional outside investor. Wired reported that In-Q-Tel supplied approximately $2 million and arranged repeated testing with CIA analysts; the exact amount was an estimate, not a publicly filed investment figure. It also reported that Palantir had spent roughly $40 million before obtaining its first substantial revenue. **[R]** Reporting has repeatedly called the CIA Palantir’s first customer or first significant customer, and says intelligence and law-enforcement agencies dominated its initial market. No public CIA procurement record reviewed independently establishes the first-customer chronology or amount, so “CIA was first customer” belongs in the strongly reported tier, not the public-primary-record tier.

Former National Security Adviser and DARPA Total Information Awareness director John Poindexter reportedly advised or tested the early concept. **[R]** This is a documented personal/technical contact. It does not prove that Palantir inherited Total Information Awareness code, contracts, databases, or formal program status.

Gotham, the platform most closely associated with defense, intelligence, and policing, was first released in 2008. **[P]** Joseph Lonsdale left Palantir in 2009 and later founded investment firm 8VC; SEC filings nevertheless show subsequent consulting payments and an investment by an 8VC vehicle. **[P/R]**

### Expansion, litigation, and policing, 2010–2019

A 2020 Justice Department civil-forfeiture complaint alleged that in March 2010 PetroSaudi International co-founder Tarek Obaid used $2 million traceable to funds diverted from 1Malaysia Development Berhad, or 1MDB, to purchase 2.5 million Palantir Series D shares. The complaint identifies a wire to Palantir’s Silicon Valley Bank account. **[P/A]** It does not allege that Palantir or its personnel participated in, or knew of, the alleged laundering. The transaction is therefore evidence of alleged tainted money reaching a Palantir financing round, not evidence that Palantir joined the 1MDB conspiracy.

Competitor i2 Inc. sued Palantir in 2010, accusing Palantir personnel of obtaining i2 software through improper identities and using its trade secrets. **[A]** The case settled confidentially in 2011 without an adjudication of liability. Press reports placed the payment near $10 million, but no public settlement instrument reviewed confirms that figure. **[R/A]**

In 2011, leaked HBGary Federal emails exposed a proposed consortium called Team Themis involving HBGary Federal, Palantir, and Berico Technologies. The proposal, routed through Hunton & Williams for a potential Bank of America engagement, contemplated discrediting WikiLeaks supporters and journalists, including Glenn Greenwald. **[R/A]** The record shows a proposal and internal planning, not proof the operation was executed. Alexander Karp apologized, said the conduct was inconsistent with Palantir’s values, and severed the HBGary relationship. **[C/R]**

From approximately 2012 to 2018, Palantir supplied New Orleans with a pro bono predictive-policing/social-network analysis program. Ali Winston reported that James Carville, then a paid Palantir adviser, helped establish the relationship and that the New Orleans City Council had not been told about the arrangement. **[R]** New Orleans ended it after the investigation became public. The reporting established analysis of police data and social networks, not that Palantir itself arrested or prosecuted anyone.

An internal Palantir document obtained by TechCrunch listed the National Security Agency among government organizations using Palantir-related capabilities. **[R]** Christopher Wylie later told a parliamentary committee that the NSA was Palantir’s largest client, but provided no contract or amount. **[A]** No public direct NSA prime contract or defensible NSA contract figure was located. Moreover, Palantir’s unrelated financial product called “Prism” was confused online with the NSA’s PRISM collection program; Palantir denied a relationship between the two. **[C/D]**

During 2013–2014, according to emails and Palantir’s later acknowledgment, one Palantir employee gave Cambridge Analytica personnel advice in a personal capacity concerning Facebook-derived data and an application intended to collect personality data. Palantir said it had rejected Cambridge Analytica’s approaches and had no contract or formal relationship. **[R/C]** Christopher Wylie testified that senior Palantir employees worked with Cambridge Analytica and that meetings occurred at both companies’ offices. **[A]** No adjudicated finding established an institutional Palantir–Cambridge Analytica contract.

In 2015, Immigration and Customs Enforcement awarded Palantir the FALCON contract for Homeland Security Investigations. The contract describes federated search, analytics, geospatial tools, and reporting over diverse law-enforcement data. **[P]** Later investigative reporting and migrant-rights groups argued that Palantir systems helped identify or locate people for immigration enforcement. Palantir drew a distinction between Homeland Security Investigations’ transnational-crime mission and Enforcement and Removal Operations, but records show that information and systems within ICE could cross organizational boundaries. **[P/R]**

Palantir challenged the Army’s planned Distributed Common Ground System procurement. The Government Accountability Office denied a protest in May 2016. In separate litigation, however, the Court of Federal Claims enjoined the procurement, and the Federal Circuit affirmed in 2018, holding that the Army had not adequately investigated available commercial products as required by procurement law. **[P]** Thus, “Palantir lost at GAO” and “Palantir later prevailed in court” are both true.

In April 2017, Palantir settled Labor Department allegations that its hiring process discriminated systematically against Asian applicants for engineering jobs. The consent decree required $1,659,434 in back pay and other monetary relief, including stock options, and required eight job offers. Palantir denied wrongdoing; settlement ended the case without an admission. **[P/A]**

Foundry, Palantir’s principal commercial and institutional data-integration platform, was released in 2016. **[P]** By 2019 the company reported $742.6 million in annual revenue, 46.5% from government customers.

### Public-company period, 2020–2026

Palantir began trading through a direct listing on September 30, 2020. It sold no new shares in the listing. The stock opened at $10 and closed at $9.50 against a $7.25 NYSE reference price, implying a market capitalization around $20 billion under the contemporary share-count conventions. **[P/R]**

The listing triggered vesting of restricted stock units and approximately $769.5 million in stock-based compensation expense during 2020. **[P]** Palantir’s multiclass structure preserved founder control: a Class F trust associated with Alexander Karp, Stephen Cohen, and Peter Thiel can maintain 49.999999% of voting power while specified ownership conditions remain satisfied. **[P]**

During the COVID-19 response, NHS England used Palantir to build and operate a health-data platform. Early work reportedly began for a nominal £1 arrangement, followed by a roughly £1 million extension and a two-year agreement worth approximately £23 million–£23.5 million. **[P/R]** NHS England was the controller and Palantir the processor; the data was hosted in the United Kingdom on Amazon Web Services infrastructure. **[P]**

In 2021–2022 Palantir bought shares in numerous companies that also committed to purchasing Palantir software. At the end of 2022 the associated customer contracts carried stated value of $492.7 million, including $63.7 million in options; cumulative recognized revenue was $166.6 million. Palantir invested approximately $326 million in 2021 and another $124.5 million in 2022, while recording $159 million in unrealized losses and $113.1 million in realized losses during 2022. **[P]** These circular-looking arrangements were disclosed, but they complicate any claim that all associated revenue represented independent market demand.

AIP began deployment in 2023. Palantir describes it as a controlled means of connecting large language models and AI agents to institutional data, software, and permitted actions. **[P/C]**

In November 2023, NHS England awarded a seven-year Federated Data Platform contract to a consortium led by Palantir UK. The procurement notice lists a base value of £182,242,760 excluding VAT and a forecast potential value up to £330 million. It expressly states that neither figure is a guaranteed expenditure. **[P]** NHS said the first year would cost at least £25.6 million. The consortium includes Accenture, PricewaterhouseCoopers, North of England Commissioning Support Unit, and Carnall Farrar. **[P]**

Initial NHS assurances said Palantir could not access or use patient data for its own purposes. In July 2026, the National Data Guardian clarified that a limited number of external supplier personnel could access identifiable information when technically necessary, subject to NHS direction and controls. It said an earlier data-protection impact assessment was inaccurate, NHS England had apologized and was correcting it, and the Guardian had not independently verified whether the access was technically necessary. **[P/D]** This correction does not establish that Palantir used the data for its own purposes; it does disprove categorical claims that no supplier personnel could ever see identifiable data.

The Army selected Palantir in March 2024 for a $178.4 million Tactical Intelligence Targeting Access Node prototype agreement covering ten TITAN ground stations—five advanced and five basic variants. **[P]** TITAN is intended to combine space, aerial, terrestrial, and other sensor feeds with AI/ML tools for targeting and situational awareness.

In May 2024, the Army awarded a $480 million firm-fixed-price contract for the Maven Smart System prototype through May 2029. **[P]** Maven Smart System uses machine learning and other algorithms to help organize sensor data, identify objects or potential targets, support operational planning, and assess outcomes. A May 2025 Defense Department notice announced a $795 million modification for software licenses, with funding and work determined through individual orders. **[P]** The releases should not be mechanically added and called cash received: contract values, modifications, ceilings, obligations, and recognized revenue are different accounting measures.

In January 2024 Palantir announced a strategic partnership with Israel’s Ministry of Defense in connection with the continuing war. **[P/C]** Amnesty International later asked about alleged links to Israeli targeting systems. Palantir denied building or supplying the named Gospel and Lavender systems, while acknowledging longstanding work with Israeli civil, defense, and intelligence bodies. **[C]** Public records reviewed do not identify Palantir’s exact operational role in individual strikes.

Palantir has also supplied Ukraine with software and services. Reporting describes battlefield mapping, targeting support, war-crimes documentation, and reconstruction applications; Alexander Karp met Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelensky in Kyiv in 2022. **[R/C]** Some services were donated while allied governments reportedly financed others. Exact aggregate value and operational attribution remain undisclosed.

In April 2025, ICE issued a limited-sources justification for “ImmigrationOS.” The document says Palantir’s existing systems already ingested data from ICE, DHS, and external sources and specified three functions: enforcement prioritization, tracking self-deportation, and monitoring the immigration lifecycle. **[P]** Reporting identified an initial order around $30 million and subsequent work bringing the reported total to roughly $60 million by late 2025. **[R]**

In July 2025 the Army announced an enterprise agreement consolidating 75 existing contracts—15 prime contracts and 60 related agreements—with a maximum potential value of $10 billion over ten years. The Army expressly said the figure was a cap, not a commitment or obligation. **[P]**

Palantir remained active at the cutoff. It reported 954 customers, 4,429 full-time employees, $4.475 billion in 2025 revenue, and $1.635 billion in consolidated net income. **[P]**

## 5. Ideas and Programs

### The “map made machine”

Palantir’s core technical idea is not a literal universal map. It is an operational representation of organizations and events: data from separate sources is normalized, connected to objects and relationships, governed through granular permissions, and made available for queries, workflows, models, and authorized actions. In Foundry and AIP, Palantir calls this representational layer an “ontology.” **[P/C]**

“The map made machine” is an interpretive metaphor. It accurately evokes Palantir’s effort to turn relationships among people, places, objects, events, and decisions into a computable interface. It is not a phrase shown to originate in a founding document, nor does it establish a connection to Lutz Dammbeck’s film.

### Gotham

First released in 2008, Gotham is designed primarily for defense, intelligence, and law-enforcement users. It supports data integration, geospatial and link analysis, operational planning, targeting workflows, and collaboration. **[P/C]** Its effects depend on underlying data quality, permissions, and institutional practice; software can preserve audit trails while still amplifying erroneous or unjust agency data.

### Foundry

Released in 2016, Foundry provides a shared data and operations layer for commercial companies, governments, and health systems. It maps source systems into business objects and workflows and permits controlled use by analysts, applications, and models. **[P/C]**

### Apollo

Apollo manages continuous deployment of Palantir software across commercial clouds, government clouds, classified networks, edge devices, and disconnected environments. **[P/C]**

### AIP

Deployed beginning in 2023, AIP connects language models and AI agents to an institution’s ontology, software, and permitted actions. Palantir emphasizes access controls, logging, evaluation, and human authorization. **[P/C]** These are design claims; their real effectiveness depends on customer configuration and operational enforcement.

### TITAN and Maven

TITAN is a ground-station family intended to ingest data from space, high-altitude, aerial, and terrestrial sensors and use AI/ML to support targeting and situational awareness. **[P]**

Maven originated as a Defense Department effort to apply computer vision to large volumes of sensor imagery. Palantir’s Maven Smart System contract concerns a wider operational platform that can combine data, algorithms, target identification, planning, approvals, and battle-damage assessment. **[P]** The documentation establishes support for military targeting workflows; it does not show that software autonomously possesses lawful authority to select and attack targets.

### *The Technological Republic*

Alexander Karp and Nicholas Zamiska published *The Technological Republic: Hard Power, Soft Belief, and the Future of the West* on February 18, 2025. The publisher describes it as a critique of Silicon Valley’s focus on consumer products and a call for closer technology-industry cooperation with democratic governments, particularly in defense. **[C]**

The book presents Palantir’s work as part of a political program: renewed national ambition, technical competence in public institutions, willingness to build weapons, and defense of Western political power. Critics see this as ideological rationalization for surveillance and militarization; that is interpretation, not evidence that every Palantir customer shares the authors’ philosophy.

### The April 2026 22-point post

Palantir’s corporate X account posted a numbered 22-point statement on April 18, 2026; press accounts appeared April 19. The original status identifier is preserved, and Engadget reproduced the full text. **[C/R]** Its propositions, paraphrased rather than quoted, were:

1. Silicon Valley owes a moral debt to the United States and should aid national defense.
2. Technologists should rebel against the narrow consumer-app ecosystem.
3. Free email and consumer convenience are inadequate measures of elite achievement.
4. Democracy requires hard power built increasingly on software.
5. AI weapons are inevitable; the central questions concern who builds and controls them.
6. The United States should reconsider universal national or military service.
7. Debate over war does not remove the duty to build better defensive tools.
8. Public employees should serve citizens rather than become an insulated priestly class.
9. Public life needs greater grace and forgiveness.
10. Reducing political disagreements to psychological diagnosis is misleading.
11. Citizens should not celebrate enemies’ deaths or misfortunes.
12. Nuclear-era deterrence is being replaced or transformed by AI-era deterrence.
13. American power helped advance opportunity and progressive social values.
14. That power also underwrote an unusually long period of international stability.
15. Postwar demilitarization in Germany and Japan should be reconsidered.
16. Builders willing to undertake grand projects where markets hesitate should be defended; Elon Musk was the prominent example.
17. Silicon Valley should accept responsibility for social experiments affecting crime and urban order.
18. Intrusive exposure of private lives repels capable people from leadership.
19. Excessive caution produces empty public speech.
20. Elite intolerance of religion should be resisted.
21. Cultures differ in achievement and some practices or regimes can be regressive and harmful.
22. Pluralism without a defined national culture is politically hollow.

These are corporate political claims, not factual findings. TechCrunch and other outlets characterized the post as a “mini-manifesto.” Philosopher Mark Coeckelbergh and economist Yanis Varoufakis were among those reported as interpreting the broader ideology in terms such as technofascism or technofeudalism. **[R/opinion]**

## 6. Scandal, Controversy, and Contest

### Privacy, surveillance, and responsibility

Palantir commonly argues that it does not collect or own its customers’ data and that customers determine permissible access. **[C]** Contract and privacy records generally support the narrower processor/vendor description. That does not eliminate responsibility for designing, integrating, or maintaining systems used for policing, migration enforcement, intelligence, or targeting.

The dispute is therefore not resolved by the statement “Palantir does not own the data.” Critics focus on the capabilities Palantir makes operational, whether disparate datasets become newly actionable, the visibility of those processes to affected people, and whether audit controls meaningfully constrain the customer.

### Immigration enforcement

FALCON and ImmigrationOS are documented ICE programs. The 2025 ImmigrationOS justification explicitly calls for enforcement prioritization, tracking of self-deportation, and lifecycle monitoring. **[P]** Claims that Palantir has no operational relationship to deportation are therefore too categorical. Conversely, claims that Palantir independently decides whom ICE arrests or deports are not established; governmental personnel retain legal and operational authority.

### NHS data

The strongest early assurance—that supplier personnel could not access identifiable health data—was corrected in 2026. Limited access was possible for specified technical work under NHS direction. **[P/D]** No cited investigation established that Palantir sold NHS patient data or used it for an independent commercial purpose.

### Cambridge Analytica

The record supports personal assistance by at least one employee and documentary discussions about Facebook-derived personality data. **[R/C]** Christopher Wylie alleged a wider relationship. **[A]** Palantir denied a formal corporate relationship and said it had rejected Cambridge Analytica’s proposals. No court or regulator adjudicated Palantir institutionally responsible for Cambridge Analytica’s Facebook harvesting.

### Hiring discrimination

The Labor Department alleged statistically significant discrimination against Asian engineering applicants. The $1.659 million settlement and eight required offers are primary-record facts; the underlying discrimination remained formally denied and unadjudicated because of the settlement. **[P/A]**

### HBGary and Team Themis

Leaked documents showed a proposal for aggressive information tactics against WikiLeaks supporters and journalists. **[R/A]** Alexander Karp’s apology and termination of the relationship corroborate that Palantir personnel participated in problematic planning, but there is no public proof the proposed operation was deployed.

### New Orleans and Los Angeles policing

New Orleans’ program was controversial because of its secrecy and the use of social-network analysis without ordinary public oversight. Los Angeles-area audit findings separately questioned whether data-access arrangements contained or demonstrated adequate privacy protections. **[R/P]** Neither record proves that every model output was used for an arrest, nor that Palantir invented the underlying police intelligence.

### i2 and KT4 litigation

i2’s trade-secret accusations ended in a confidential settlement, preventing a public merits determination. **[A]** KT4’s litigation did establish that an investor was entitled to inspect some company records, while later claims settled confidentially. **[P/A]** Amounts and sealed terms remain unknown.

### Securities and strategic investments

Shareholders alleged Palantir misled investors and that its investment-in-customer strategy served insiders or inflated demand. Those are litigation allegations. The securities complaint was dismissed with prejudice in 2025 and appealed; a derivative action was dismissed with prejudice. **[P/A]** Dismissal means those pleaded claims did not proceed, not that every business criticism was disproved.

### Israel and targeting systems

Palantir acknowledges work with Israel and a 2024 Ministry of Defense partnership. It denies developing or supplying the specifically named Gospel and Lavender targeting systems. **[C]** No public primary document reviewed assigns Palantir responsibility for a particular strike. Claims doing so remain unproven; the broader defense relationship is documented.

### “Cybernetic elite,” *The Net*, Kaczynski, and MKUltra

The narrative advanced by the cited 2026 video appears to connect:

- Cold War cybernetics and behavior research;
- Theodore Kaczynski’s participation in Henry Murray’s Harvard psychological study;
- MKUltra and intelligence experimentation;
- Lutz Dammbeck’s 2003 *The Net*;
- technology financiers and Jeffrey Epstein;
- and Palantir as a contemporary machine for mapping populations.

Some components have independent historical records, but the assembled Palantir chain does not. Specifically:

- Palantir’s incorporation and the film’s release both occurred in 2003. **[P]**
- John Poindexter’s reported consultation provides a genuine personal bridge to an official associated with Total Information Awareness. **[R]**
- Peter Thiel’s later contact with Jeffrey Epstein is documented independently of Palantir. **[R]**
- No Palantir–Dammbeck, Palantir–Kaczynski, Palantir–MKUltra, or corporate Palantir–Epstein transaction was found. **[U]**
- No evidence shows that 2003 was selected because of the film, or that Palantir inherited a cybernetics or human-experimentation program.
- Similarities among link analysis, network diagrams, intelligence fusion, and cybernetic concepts are intellectual comparisons, not proof of organizational continuity.

The “cybernetic elite” story is therefore best classified as a conspiracy or interpretive network narrative built from some true nodes but unsupported connecting edges.

## 7. Reception and Afterlife

Early coverage portrayed Palantir as a secretive CIA-backed company translating PayPal-style fraud analysis into counterterrorism. Later coverage split into three major images:

1. **The intelligence and defense contractor:** Gotham, Army litigation, special operations, TITAN, Maven, Israel, and Ukraine.
2. **The surveillance and governance controversy:** ICE, predictive policing, NHS data, Cambridge Analytica, and the Team Themis proposal.
3. **The ideological corporation:** Alexander Karp’s public persona, *The Technological Republic*, and the 2026 corporate manifesto.

The company’s own account presents privacy-protective access controls and human-machine cooperation as alternatives to either indiscriminate surveillance or fully automated decision-making. Critics respond that fine-grained permissions do not settle whether the institution’s objective—deportation, predictive policing, or military targeting—is legitimate.

The unsupported bin Laden story and the broader Kaczynski/MKUltra narrative demonstrate a recurring feature of Palantir’s public image: classified customers and limited disclosure create a space in which documented capabilities, marketing, inference, and myth are easily merged.

Palantir has no dissolution or estate. Its principal public archive is the SEC filing system, supplemented by procurement databases, court dockets, FOIA releases, congressional records, leaked corporate documents, and investigative journalism. Substantial contract details, customer identities, beneficial ownership, model behavior, and operational outcomes remain classified, confidential, or unavailable.
