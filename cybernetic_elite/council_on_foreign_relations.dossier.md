# Council on Foreign Relations: Research Dossier

## Evidentiary key and scope

- **[P—primary record]** Court filing, government document, audited financial statement, tax return, contemporaneous roster, annual report, archival record, or other documentary evidence.
- **[O—official institutional account]** CFR’s own history, biography, policy, or current roster. This is primary evidence for what CFR says and records, but not independent corroboration of its interpretation.
- **[J—journalism]** Reporting by an identified journalist or publication, preferably with named or documentary sources.
- **[L—litigation allegation]** Allegation in a complaint, indictment, deposition, or other legal filing; not proof unless adjudicated or admitted.
- **[S—scholarship]** Historical or political analysis by an identified scholar.
- **[C—claim by subject]** An assertion by CFR or a person discussed.
- **[R—rumor/conspiracy narrative]** A claim circulated without adequate documentary support.
- **[N—negative finding]** A specifically investigated connection for which no supporting evidence was located.
- **[D—disproved/retracted]** A claim contradicted by reliable evidence or withdrawn by its source.

“Every tie” is necessarily bounded: CFR reported 5,397 members in June 2025 and has interacted with thousands of speakers, donors, authors, fellows, and officials. The network inventory below records every material tie identified in the requested founding, finance, intelligence, cybernetics/technology, Epstein, Kaczynski, controversy, and governance threads. CFR’s linked historical and current rosters remain the authoritative census for thousands of additional formal affiliations.

## 1. Identification

The **Council on Foreign Relations, Inc.**, usually **CFR**, is a New York–based nonprofit membership institution, foreign-policy research organization, convener, and publisher founded in 1921. Its federal tax identifier is **13-1628168**. It publishes *Foreign Affairs*, maintains headquarters at the Harold Pratt House, 58 East 68th Street, New York City, and an office at 1777 F Street NW, Washington, DC. CFR describes itself as “independent” and “nonpartisan”; it does not formally endorse government policies, political candidates, or positions in its institutional name. As of June 2025, it reported **5,397 members**. Its president since 2023 is **Michael Froman** and its chair since 2017 is **David M. Rubenstein**. [O: CFR’s current description and FY2025 report, accessed September 28, 2026.](https://www.cfr.org/about)

CFR is **not a government agency**, intelligence service, political party, bank, foundation, or university. Its power is informal and networked: it selects members, convenes officials and private actors under non-attribution rules, publishes policy arguments, operates fellowships connecting government and scholarship, organizes task forces, and controls substantial financial assets. Its FY2025 audited statements reported **$816.7 million in assets**, including **$672.0 million in investments**. [P: Grant Thornton audit, October 3, 2025.](https://static.cfr.org/sites/default/files/pdf/2025%20Council%20on%20Foreign%20Relations%20FS.pdf)

## 2. The Record

### Origins: the Inquiry, Paris, and incorporation

CFR’s organizational prehistory begins with **the Inquiry**, a group assembled in 1917 under President **Woodrow Wilson** and organized by presidential adviser **Edward Mandell House** to prepare studies for an anticipated postwar settlement. More than one hundred academics and specialists contributed. This was a government-sponsored wartime planning body, not yet CFR. [O: CFR centennial history, 2021.](https://www.cfr.org/articles/council-foreign-relations-100-history)

At the 1919 Paris Peace Conference, British and American participants discussed creating an Anglo-American institute for continuing international-affairs study. The British organization became the **Royal Institute of International Affairs**, commonly called **Chatham House**. The American project was subsequently combined with a preexisting New York dinner group called the Council on Foreign Relations. The resulting organization was incorporated in New York on **July 29, 1921**. [O: George Gavrilis, *The Council on Foreign Relations: A Short History*, and CFR’s centennial history.](https://static.cfr.org/sites/default/files/pdf/cfrcentennialbook.pdf)

Founding officers and directors came overwhelmingly from law, banking, universities, diplomacy, and publishing. Early leadership included **John W. Davis**, **Paul D. Cravath**, **Edwin F. Gay**, **Paul M. Warburg**, **Isaiah Bowman**, **Frank L. Polk**, **George W. Wickersham**, and **Otto H. Kahn**. That composition supports the descriptive proposition that CFR began as an elite foreign-policy organization. It does not, by itself, establish the stronger conspiracy claim that it possessed governmental command authority. [O/P: CFR historical roster, current compilation of corporate records.](https://www.cfr.org/board-directors)

### *Foreign Affairs*

CFR published the first issue of ***Foreign Affairs*** in September 1922, initially under editor **Archibald Cary Coolidge**. The journal became CFR’s principal public platform while meetings and study groups provided more private forms of exchange.

Important publications included:

- **George F. Kennan’s** pseudonymous “X” article, “The Sources of Soviet Conduct,” in 1947, which publicly articulated containment.
- **Henry A. Kissinger’s** *Nuclear Weapons and Foreign Policy* in 1957, developed from a CFR study group.
- A large subsequent body of essays by officials, scholars, financiers, military officers, technologists, and political figures.

These publications influenced debate; publication is not proof that CFR institutionally endorsed every author’s conclusion. CFR says authors speak for themselves and the institution adopts no foreign-policy position. [O: CFR timeline and nonpartisanship account.](https://www.cfr.org/celebrating-a-century/timeline)

Editors have been **Archibald Cary Coolidge** (1922–1928), **Hamilton Fish Armstrong** (1928–1972), **William P. Bundy** (1972–1984), **William G. Hyland** (1984–1992), **James F. Hoge Jr.** (1992–2010), **Gideon Rose** (2010–2021), and **Daniel Kurtz-Phelan** (2021–present). [O: CFR historical roster, accessed September 2026.](https://www.cfr.org/board-directors)

### The War and Peace Studies

After Germany invaded Poland, *Foreign Affairs* editor **Hamilton Fish Armstrong** and CFR staff member **Walter H. Mallory** met Assistant Secretary of State **George S. Messersmith** on September 12, 1939. CFR then created the confidential **War and Peace Studies** project.

According to CFR’s institutional history:

- The **Rockefeller Foundation** supplied nearly **$350,000**.
- Roughly one hundred participants held more than 250 meetings.
- The project produced **682 memoranda** on economic, territorial, political, and security questions.
- Memoranda marked classified were circulated within the US government.
- The work continued through 1945.

[P/O: Peter Grose’s documentary institutional history, drawing on CFR archives, published 1996 and revised 2006.](https://cdn.cfr.org/sites/default/files/book_pdf/Continuing_The_Inquiry.pdf)

This is strong evidence of a privately funded organization supplying planning material to officials. It is not evidence that CFR legally directed the State Department or secretly governed the United States.

In 1945, at the State Department’s request, CFR also provided training for incoming Foreign Service officers. In 1948, study groups chaired by **Dwight D. Eisenhower**, **Allen W. Dulles**, and **Arthur H. Dean** examined European aid, Germany, and Japan respectively. [O: CFR timeline.](https://www.cfr.org/celebrating-a-century/timeline)

### Membership: process, access, and discipline

CFR distinguishes **life membership** from five-year **term membership**:

- Life-membership nominees must be US citizens or permanent residents who have applied for citizenship.
- A candidate is proposed by one member and supported by three or four seconders.
- The board elects new members twice yearly.
- Selection criteria include intellectual achievement, international-affairs experience, future promise, professional standing, potential contribution, and prospective participation.
- Term applicants are generally ages thirty through thirty-six and serve five years.
- CFR reported electing 387 life members and 200 term members during FY2025. [O: FY2025 annual report and current membership rules.](https://www.cfr.org/annual-report-2025)

CFR does not publicly identify a candidate’s nominator as a general rule. This limits outside reconstruction of patronage within the selection process.

The bylaws give the board authority to elect members and to suspend or terminate membership for rule violations or conduct prejudicial to CFR’s interests or reputation. Older published bylaws also treated prolonged dues default as resignation. [P: bylaws reproduced in CFR’s 2011 annual report.](https://www.cfr.org/sites/default/files/report_pdf/AR_2011_for_web.pdf)

### Meetings and non-attribution

CFR’s meeting rule generally permits participants to use information obtained at a meeting but not publicly identify the speaker or another participant without permission. CFR traces the rule to its earliest years; the board reformulated it in 1977 and amended it in 1994. [P/O: CFR 2012 annual report.](https://www.cfr.org/sites/default/files/report_pdf/AR2012_for_Web_1.pdf)

This is a real confidentiality mechanism. It allows candid exchange but impedes public accountability and makes retrospective network tracing difficult. It is not equivalent to a security classification, secrecy oath, or intelligence-agency privilege.

### Expansion, exclusion, and reform

CFR began a corporate membership program in **1953** with twenty-five firms. As of September 2026, more than 120 corporations participated. Current annual contribution levels are **$100,000 Gold**, **$75,000 Silver**, and **$50,000 Bronze**, in exchange for institutional participation and access to corporate programming. [O: current CFR membership materials.](https://www.cfr.org/membership)

CFR excluded women until 1970. Its own history records that attorney and member-candidate **Rita E. Hauser** threatened sex-discrimination litigation; the board narrowly voted in November 1969 to admit women. The first women elected in 1970 included **Katharine Graham**, **Miriam Camps**, and **Julia Henderson**. [O: CFR’s official centennial history.](https://static.cfr.org/sites/default/files/pdf/cfrcentennialbook.pdf)

The **International Affairs Fellowship** began in 1967. Separate military fellowships began with the Air Force in 1962, Army in 1966, and Navy in 1968. These programs deliberately move officials into research and private-sector settings and outside specialists into policy work. [O: CFR timeline.](https://www.cfr.org/celebrating-a-century/timeline)

### Archives

CFR deposited organizational records at Princeton University’s Seeley G. Mudd Manuscript Library. The collection covers approximately 1918–2018. Records more than twenty-five years old are generally opened, but researchers must sign an agreement restricting attribution of unpublished statements or opinions to living persons without consent. [P: Princeton access agreement and collection policy, accessed September 2026.](https://library.princeton.edu/form/cfr-user-agreement-form)

That restriction is not evidence that the archive conceals crimes; it is, however, a material limitation on independent publication and full reconstruction of living participants’ roles.

### Government funding and contractors

CFR states that it accepts no funding from governments and no corporate funding earmarked for research. The FY2025 audit does not identify government grants as a separate revenue source. This supports, but does not independently prove for every transaction, CFR’s current claim of no direct government funding.

The Quincy Institute’s **Think Tank Funding Tracker** found no US or foreign-government donations in its reviewed 2019–2024 disclosures, while identifying at least **$2.445 million** from companies classified as Pentagon contractors. [S/watchdog compilation, accessed September 2026.](https://thinktankfundingtracker.org/think-tank/council-on-foreign-relations/) There is no contradiction between “no government money” and money from private contractors, but contractor membership creates a recognizable conflict-of-interest and access question.

### Forms of power

CFR’s documented power consists of:

1. **Selection:** Board-controlled membership admissions.
2. **Convening:** Private or non-attributable access among officials, financiers, executives, journalists, academics, and military personnel.
3. **Publishing:** Editorial control over *Foreign Affairs* and CFR-branded reports.
4. **Personnel circulation:** Fellowships that move people between government, universities, media, and business.
5. **Agenda formation:** Study groups and task forces determine which questions receive sustained expert attention.
6. **Capital:** A large investment pool and recurring donations support institutional independence and continuity.
7. **Reputational certification:** Membership, speaking invitations, fellowships, and publication can confer elite professional legitimacy.

CFR has no documented power to issue government orders, grant security clearances, prosecute cases, classify information, command intelligence operations, or compel officials to adopt its recommendations.

## 5. Ideas and Programs

### Internationalism and policy planning

From the Inquiry onward, CFR’s dominant institutional premise has been that US power requires sustained engagement with international affairs and an expert infrastructure outside day-to-day electoral politics. War and Peace Studies was its clearest instance of policy planning directly supplied to government.

This orientation has been described differently:

- CFR calls it independent analysis supporting informed US engagement. [C/O]
- Historian **Michael Wala** described wartime CFR as both an adviser and a “listening post” through which officials could gauge elite opinion. [S]
- **Inderjeet Parmar** argues that organizations including CFR mobilized business and intellectual elites behind an internationalist foreign policy. [S]
- **Laurence H. Shoup** and **William Minter**, and later Shoup alone, argue that CFR has functioned as a policy-planning center for internationally oriented corporate and financial elites. [S]

Those scholarly interpretations concern structural influence. They do not claim CFR has statutory command authority.

### Nuclear strategy

Henry Kissinger’s CFR study group produced *Nuclear Weapons and Foreign Policy*, advocating greater attention to limited nuclear-war strategy. The work became influential and controversial. Its use demonstrates CFR’s capacity to turn private expert deliberation into public doctrine; it does not mean CFR alone determined US nuclear policy.

### Cybersecurity and emerging technology

CFR now operates digital and cyberspace programs, publishes cyber-policy assessments, and convenes technology executives, security officials, academics, and investors. The 2022 task-force report *Confronting Reality in Cyberspace* was co-chaired by former CIA intelligence official Jami Miscik and included recommendations on an increasingly fragmented internet. [O: CFR task-force report, July 2022.](https://www.cfr.org/task-force-report/confronting-reality-in-cyberspace/cdn/ff/YHJp0y66Zi59odiVcoNOX_tNCbpX1m9mD8SmegK4wl4/1657115366/public/2022-07/CFR_TFR80_Cyberspace_Full_SinglePages_06212022_Final.pdf)

This is a policy network spanning intelligence and technology. It is not evidence of a direct institutional line from Norbert Wiener’s cybernetics, Harvard behavioral experiments, MKUltra, Epstein, or Kaczynski to a single CFR-controlled program.

### Human-subject research

No evidence was located that CFR itself operated laboratories, served as an institutional review board, recruited experimental subjects, or funded MKUltra or Henry Murray’s Harvard experiments. CFR has discussed behavioral science and intelligence policy, but discussion and publication are not experimental sponsorship. [N]

## 6. Scandal, Controversy, and Contest

### Jeffrey Epstein: membership, money, and institutional failure

**Documented facts:** Epstein was a CFR member from 1995 to 2009, gave approximately $350,000, and attended only two events identified by CFR. He pleaded guilty in Florida in 2008 to state prostitution-related offenses involving a minor. His CFR membership was not terminated following the conviction; it ended in 2009 after he stopped paying dues. [J/P]

In September 2019, Richard Haass acknowledged that CFR had failed to connect public reporting about Epstein’s criminal case with its membership records and said it should have done so. CFR declined to disclose Epstein’s nominator. [J/C]

In November 2019, CFR announced that it would direct an amount equivalent to Epstein’s donations toward anti-trafficking efforts and revise procedures to remove members convicted of serious crimes more promptly. [J: *Washington Post*, November 25, 2019.](https://www.washingtonpost.com/politics/council-on-foreign-relations-redirects-epstein-gifts-to-fight-human-trafficking/2019/11/25/c6e473da-0fc9-11ea-9cd7-a1becbc82f5e_story.html)

**Assessment:** CFR’s failure to review or remove Epstein after his conviction is documented institutional negligence or governance failure. No located evidence shows that CFR protected him from prosecution, facilitated trafficking, knew the details of his crimes while accepting donations, or participated in an intelligence operation. Claims of such participation remain [R/N].

### Sue Mi Terry prosecution

On July 16, 2024, federal prosecutors charged **Sue Mi Terry**, a former CIA analyst, National Security Council official, and CFR senior fellow, with acting as an unregistered agent of the Republic of Korea and conspiring to violate the Foreign Agents Registration Act. The indictment alleges that she disclosed nonpublic US government information, arranged access, and received luxury goods, meals, and funding from South Korean intelligence officers. These are **allegations**, not established facts. [L: DOJ indictment and announcement, July 17, 2024.](https://www.justice.gov/archives/opa/pr/former-cia-and-white-house-official-sue-mi-terry-arrested-acting-unregistered-agent-south)

Terry was placed on leave and subsequently resigned from CFR. CFR was not charged as an institution. As of September 2026, the public docket still reflected active proceedings and sealed motion practice rather than a final adjudication. [P: SDNY order dated September 16, 2026.](https://www.leagle.com/decision/infdco20260918936)

### Leonard Blavatnik funding dispute

CFR’s reported $12 million Blavatnik Family Foundation internship gift triggered a 2019 protest from anti-corruption specialists. Critics argued the gift could buy access and rehabilitate the reputation of wealth accumulated in post-Soviet privatization networks. CFR rejected that characterization. Leonard Blavatnik was not identified in the reviewed sources as a sanctioned person, and no evidence was located that he dictated CFR research. [J/C]

This controversy concerns donor influence and reputational laundering, not an adjudicated criminal offense by CFR.

### Hassan Nemazee and Kenneth Starr

CFR accepted money from Hassan Nemazee before his fraud conviction. It later paid $167,463 to the government after authorities alleged the donated money derived from fraud. That is a concrete instance of criminal proceeds reaching CFR, followed by recovery. [J/P]

Kenneth I. Starr, another donor, was convicted of investment fraud. In 2019 CFR said it had not identified a comparable repayment or redirection involving his gifts. [J] No evidence reviewed establishes that CFR knew either donor’s money was criminally derived when accepted.

### Exclusion of women

CFR’s exclusion of women until 1970 is undisputed. The board changed course only after sustained internal controversy and Rita Hauser’s threatened litigation. [O/C] The eventual admission of women does not erase the discriminatory policy.

### William Bundy and Vietnam

William Bundy’s appointment as *Foreign Affairs* editor in 1972 produced protests because he had participated in Vietnam War policymaking. Critics regarded the appointment as insulation of establishment policymakers from accountability; defenders treated his government experience as editorial qualification. [O/J] No legal misconduct by CFR resulted.

### Archive and transparency dispute

Non-attribution rules, confidential nomination records, and the Princeton living-person restriction impede external verification. These policies are documented. Calling them a “cover-up” is an inference requiring evidence of a concealed act; no such general proof was located.

### Workplace-injury litigation

In **Del Orbe v. Council on Foreign Relations, Inc.**, a window cleaner injured in a fall at the Harold Pratt House obtained summary judgment on a New York Labor Law §240 claim. A later indemnification dispute involving CFR and a contractor was decided in 2017. [P: New York Supreme Court decision.](https://law.justia.com/cases/new-york/other-courts/2017/2017-ny-slip-op-32259-u.html) This is a documented civil-liability matter, not evidence of the geopolitical conspiracy narratives associated with CFR.

### Institutional criminal record

Targeted searches located no indictment or criminal conviction of **Council on Foreign Relations, Inc. itself**. That finding does not cover sealed proceedings or records unavailable to public search. [N]

### Conspiracy narratives

Recurring narratives claim that CFR:

1. secretly governs the United States;
2. chooses presidents and cabinet officers;
3. coordinates with Chatham House to create world government;
4. is controlled by the Rockefeller family or “international bankers”;
5. is an arm or controller of CIA;
6. directed MKUltra and related behavioral research;
7. protected Jeffrey Epstein;
8. forms part of a continuous cybernetic-control project culminating in contemporary technology platforms.

These narratives were promoted in different forms by the **John Birch Society**, **Robert W. Welch Jr.**, **Gary Allen**, **Liberty Lobby**, **Lyndon LaRouche–associated networks**, **Pat Robertson**, and later internet communities. An academic history of American anti-globalism traces the role of the John Birch Society and Liberty Lobby in depicting organizations such as CFR as instruments of a hidden global government. [S: Cambridge doctoral research.](https://api.repository.cam.ac.uk/server/api/core/bitstreams/5211d62e-828a-42ca-bf85-491fd212831a/content)

The narratives draw on documented facts:

- concentrated wealthy and official membership;
- confidentiality;
- Rockefeller funding and leadership;
- government personnel overlap;
- War and Peace Studies;
- intelligence-officer participation;
- large corporate donors;
- Epstein’s membership.

The stronger conclusions do not follow automatically from those facts. No reviewed corporate record, government file, court filing, or contemporaneous correspondence establishes a single CFR chain of command over the US government, CIA, MKUltra, Epstein’s prosecution, or Kaczynski. Those claims remain [R/N], not [D], where they are framed so vaguely as to be unfalsifiable.

## 7. Reception and Afterlife

CFR’s public image has developed through four competing bodies of material:

1. **Institutional histories.** Peter Grose’s *Continuing the Inquiry* and George Gavrilis’s *The Council on Foreign Relations: A Short History* present CFR as a private institution improving foreign-policy knowledge and debate. They are useful documentary guides but institutionally sponsored. [O]

2. **Elite and diplomatic histories.** Scholars such as Michael Wala and Inderjeet Parmar treat CFR as an intermediary joining business, academia, philanthropy, media, and the national-security state. [S]

3. **Structural criticism.** Laurence Shoup and William Minter’s *Imperial Brain Trust*, followed by Shoup’s *Wall Street’s Think Tank*, interprets CFR as an organ of internationally oriented corporate capitalism. This is an argued historical thesis, not a judicial finding. [S: Shoup’s 2015 book record.](https://www.jstor.org/stable/j.ctt15zc7qx)

4. **Conspiracy literature.** John Birch Society material, Gary Allen’s *None Dare Call It Conspiracy*, Pat Robertson’s *The New World Order*, and later online productions depict CFR as a hidden sovereign. [R] These accounts commonly collapse membership, influence, shared class background, and institutional command into a single category.

The Epstein disclosures shifted attention from abstract “globalist” claims to a concrete governance question: why an institution with extensive vetting retained a convicted sex offender until he stopped paying dues. CFR’s acknowledgment and donation redirection answered part of that criticism but did not disclose his nominator.

The 2026 Hidden AmuraKa video named in the brief could not be independently recovered in indexed web sources. Accordingly, only its supplied CFR–Epstein assertion could be evaluated, and that assertion is verified. Any further claims attributed to the video should be treated as unexamined until the actual recording, transcript, citations, and publication metadata are available.
