# The Suit of Cups: Research Dossier

## Method and evidentiary labels

This dossier distinguishes six kinds of statement:

- **Documented fact** — supported by surviving cards, archival records, rules, or a contemporary printed text.
- **Scholarly reconstruction** — an inference advanced by historians from material, stylistic, documentary, or game evidence.
- **Tradition** — a convention transmitted in card-making, game play, or cartomancy, without necessarily claiming ancient authority.
- **Esoteric attribution** — a deliberately constructed occult correspondence.
- **Disputed claim** — proposed but contested, underdetermined, or unsupported by decisive evidence.
- **Legend / modern invention** — historically untenable as an origin claim, although potentially important in later religious, artistic, or divinatory practice.

“Cups” is a fourteen-card suit, not a trump. Trump-number controversies, the Fool’s position, and regional replacements such as Juno and Jupiter therefore affect the pack containing Cups but do not give the suit itself a trump number.

The suit’s ordinary title has never needed a number. Within it, the ranks are one through ten plus four courts. “Minor Arcana,” now ubiquitous in English, belongs to occult classification; Renaissance players possessed *carte comuni*, suit cards, or equivalent ordinary cards, not a consciously opposed philosophical “Minor Arcana.”

The fifteenth-century Milanese pack was neither numbered nor captioned. A modern label such as “Nine of Cups” identifies the count of suit signs rather than transcribing an original title. The Morgan identifies its Visconti–Sforza Ace, Three, Five, and Nine of Cups, and three Cups courts, as parts of a pack made in Milan around 1450–1480. It emphasizes that tarot was then a courtly game, not an occult book. [Morgan Library collection record](https://www.themorgan.org/manuscript/158525)

## Names and linguistic history

### Italy

**Documented fact.** The 1442 Ferrara account uses *chope*, a regional spelling of *coppe*. Fifteenth-century records call the packs *carte da trionfi* or simply *trionfi*. The term *tarocchi* becomes established around the beginning of the sixteenth century. Thus “trionfi Cups” is a useful modern shorthand but not a fixed contemporary card title.

The standard ranks became:

- *Asso di Coppe*
- *Due, Tre … Dieci di Coppe*
- *Fante di Coppe*
- *Cavallo/Cavaliere di Coppe*
- *Regina di Coppe*
- *Re di Coppe*

Regional packs may say *coppe*, *coppa*, or omit printed suit names altogether.

### France

**Documented tradition.** The suit is *Coupes*: *As de Coupe*, *Deux de Coupe*, and so forth; courts are *Valet, Cavalier, Reine,* and *Roi de Coupe*. Tarot de Marseille woodblocks normally carry no pip titles: “Deux de Coupe” is cataloguing and game language.

“Tarot de Marseille” is a later classificatory name. Jean Noblet worked in Paris, Jean Dodal at Lyon, Pierre Madenié at Dijon, and only later makers such as Nicolas Conver worked in Marseille. The BnF calls Noblet the earliest known pack conforming to what is conventionally called the Marseille graphic type. [BnF, Jean Noblet tarot](https://essentiels.bnf.fr/fr/image/4799f2d6-6f88-4f6f-a15c-40a1a943c95e-tarot-jean-noblet)

In French-suited gaming tarot, Cups are replaced by Hearts. They are analogous red suits, but French tarot game cards are not normally called Cups.

### Spain and Spanish-suited packs

The suit is *Copas*: *As, Dos … Diez de Copas; Sota, Caballo,* and *Rey*. Standard Spanish packs usually lack a Queen and commonly have forty or forty-eight cards; occult Spanish tarot restores the fourteen-card tarot structure.

Mathers’s claim in 1888 that the Spanish Ace of Cups preserves an Egyptian pair of serpents is an **esoteric antiquarian conjecture**, not accepted historical evidence.

### German-speaking countries

In divinatory tarot the suit is *Kelche*: *Ass der Kelche, Zwei der Kelche*, etc. In German-suited Tarock games the corresponding ordinary suit is more often Hearts (*Herz*) because the packs use French suits. Cups therefore survives mainly in Italian-suited historical packs and occult/divinatory translations.

### English occult nomenclature

English writers variously used Cups, Chalices, or Goblets. Golden Dawn papers retained “Cups” while expanding the courts:

- “Lord of the Waves and the Waters”
- “Queen of the Thrones of the Waters”
- “Prince of the Chariot of the Waters”
- “Princess of the Waters and Lotus of the Palace of the Floods”

Crowley retained Cups but changed the courts to Knight, Queen, Prince, and Princess. In Waite–Smith usage, Page and Knight correspond structurally to Golden Dawn Princess and Prince; Waite’s King corresponds to the Golden Dawn’s mounted Knight/King rank.

# Iconography deck by deck

## Mamluk antecedents, approximately fourteenth–fifteenth century

### Cups or *tūmān*

**Documented object; reconstructed transmission.** The surviving Mamluk cards conventionally associated with the Topkapı Palace pack have four signs: swords, polo sticks, coins, and stylized cups. Arabic inscriptions name the cup suit *tūmān*. The word apparently means a myriad or ten-thousand in Central Asian usage rather than simply “cup.”

The suit signs are elaborate stemmed vessels, repeated geometrically. Courts are not human portraits: Mamluk cards use inscriptions and rank titles, consistent with Islamic avoidance of human figuration in this luxury context.

The common historical reconstruction is:

> Mamluk swords, polo sticks, coins, cups → Italian swords, batons, coins, cups.

The relationship is compelling at the four-suit structural level, but no surviving document records a particular Mamluk pack being copied into a named Italian workshop. It is therefore a strong **scholarly reconstruction**, not a documented act of transmission. Leo Ary Mayer’s 1939 study remains foundational. [Mayer, “Mamluk Playing Cards”](https://www.persee.fr/doc/bifao_0255-0962_1939_num_38_1_1998)

### Did the cup become the French heart?

**Tradition and reconstruction, not a demonstrated one-step mutation.** Cartomancers routinely equate Cups with Hearts, and Etteilla transferred meanings from Hearts in his thirty-two-card system to Cups in his tarot system. French suit signs, however, emerged through a separate process of stencil-friendly simplification. It is safer to say that Hearts occupy the corresponding red-suit role than that a continuous series of dated images proves “cup → heart.”

## Marziano da Tortona and Michelino da Besozzo, Milan, approximately 1418–1425

Marziano’s manuscript describes a special pack commissioned for Filippo Maria Visconti, with four bird suits and sixteen gods as superior cards. It does **not** contain Cups.

Its relevance is structural: it is an early Milanese experiment in adding permanent superior cards to a suit pack. Historians sometimes call it a “proto-tarot,” but it is not a surviving standard tarot and cannot attest Cups iconography. The pack itself is lost; the explanatory text survives.

---

## Visconti di Modrone / Cary–Yale, Milan, approximately 1440–1445

**Documented surviving cards; disputed dating and original structure.** Yale describes this as the oldest of the three principal surviving Milanese luxury groups. The surviving cards include the Ace through Ten of Cups, King, male Knight, male Page, and female Page; the female Knight and Queen are absent. The inferred original suit contained six courts, making the pack larger than the later 78-card standard.

The pip cards display separate gold-and-blue chalices against worked silver grounds. The chalices are objects of court luxury rather than narrative symbols. Counts, symmetry, heraldic emblems, gilding, and workshop virtuosity dominate.

The Ace is a monumental vessel or architectural fountain-like chalice. It is not demonstrably a church. Descriptions of it as a “church” arise because its upper structure resembles a Gothic façade, shrine, or ciborium. The identification is **disputed**: “architectural cup” or “fountain-shaped chalice” is the secure description.

The Cups courts carry actual vessels. Imperial crowns and Visconti emblems place the suit within aristocratic ceremonial culture, but no contemporary source says that the courts personify emotions or the clergy. [Yale account and digitized-deck link](https://library.yale.edu/news/yale-library-displays-tarot-treasures-new-morgan-library-exhibition-through-oct-4?page=56)

---

## Brera–Brambilla, Milan, approximately 1442–1451

The Brera group preserves nearly all the ordinary cards, including its Cups sequence and Cups Page and Knight. Its pips again show counted chalices on silver grounds, with elaborate punched decoration.

The Ace is a broad, rounded, monumental vessel with blue wave-like ornament. Comparisons with tureen-shaped Spanish Aces and other Milanese fountain Aces are visually plausible; assertions of a particular Egyptian prototype are not documented.

The Italian national catalogue attributes the set to Bonifacio Bembo, dates it approximately 1442–1451, and records forty-eight surviving cards. [Italian cultural-heritage catalogue](https://catalogo.beniculturali.it/detail/HistoricOrArtisticProperty/0300097697-0)

---

## Visconti–Sforza / Pierpont Morgan–Bergamo, Milan, approximately 1450–1460, with later replacements

The surviving total is seventy-four cards divided among the Morgan Library, Accademia Carrara, and Colleoni family collection. Four cards required by a standard reconstruction are missing.

Surviving at the Morgan are:

- Ace of Cups
- Three of Cups
- Five of Cups
- Nine of Cups
- Page, Knight, and Queen of Cups

The pips are isolated chalices in balanced floral fields. They are not scenes. The Morgan’s Ace is a large fountain and explicitly identifies the fountain as a Sforza device. Water is implied by the fountain, but the later metaphysical equation Cups = elemental Water is not stated by the card. [Morgan object labels](https://www.themorgan.org/sites/default/files/pdf/press/MedievalLabels.pdf)

The Cups Page stands and holds a cup; the Knight rides with one; the Queen sits enthroned and presents one. Hierarchy and possession, rather than individualized psychology, organize the court.

Attribution has moved among Bonifacio Bembo, Francesco Zavattari, Benedetto Bembo, Franco dei Russi, and collaborators. The Morgan appropriately labels the workshop attribution as unsettled. [Morgan catalogue discussion](https://www.themorgan.org/manuscript/158525)

## Other fifteenth-century Milanese fragments

Fountain-shaped Aces occur in other Visconti-associated fragments, including a Colleoni card now connected with the Victoria and Albert Museum. Their architecture supports the conclusion that the great fountain-cup was a northern Italian suit convention and sometimes a dynastic device.

“Church” remains an interpretation of shape. No surviving caption calls an early Ace *ecclesia*, church, Eucharist, or Grail.

---

## Ferrarese and Este cards

Several fragmentary Este-associated cards survive, but attribution, grouping, and exact place of manufacture are often contested. Cups generally retain repeated Italian chalices. The February 1442 Ferrara account is more informative than most fragments because it names *chope* as one of the four suits.

The so-called Charles VI, Este, and related northern Italian groups exhibit courtly figures and ornamental pips. They provide no secure evidence for an occult Cups doctrine.

---

## Mantegna “Tarocchi,” approximately 1460s

**Documented prints; irrelevant to the Cups suit.** The Mantegna “Tarocchi” are fifty educational or emblematic engravings arranged in five decads. They are neither a tarot pack nor ordinary playing cards and contain no suit of Cups. Later occultists and publishers juxtaposed them with tarot because of their allegorical sequence; that does not make them part of the suit’s genealogy.

---

## Sola Busca, Venice/Ferrara sphere, engraved 1491; painted approximately 1490s

The Sola Busca is the earliest surviving complete 78-card tarot and the earliest surviving pack with fully scenic numbered suit cards.

Its suit traditionally called Cups uses great vessels as props in strenuous, sometimes comic or enigmatic actions. Figures carry, strike, pour, inspect, or cluster around oversized cups. The Ten of Cups, for example, shows a figure actively engaged among the vessels rather than a decorative ten-pip arrangement.

This is crucial because it proves that scenic minors existed four centuries before Waite–Smith. It does not prove that the Sola Busca scenes carried Waite’s meanings. Their narratives remain difficult and may invoke Roman history, Renaissance politics, alchemy, satire, or workshop invention.

The Brera identifies the deck as complete and dates its Ten of Cups around 1490. [Pinacoteca di Brera exhibition record](https://pinacotecabrera.org/en/news/mostra/il-segreto-dei-segreti-i-tarocchi-sola-busca/)

Pamela Colman Smith almost certainly knew photographs of the Sola Busca cards shown at the British Museum in 1908. Specific Waite–Smith compositions—most famously the Three of Swords and Ten of Wands—show close dependence. For Cups, the relationship is looser: Sola Busca established a model of human action around suit emblems, but Smith did not simply copy its Cups narratives.

---

## Rosenwald and Budapest sheets, late fifteenth or early sixteenth century

These uncut woodcut sheets attest cheaper, reproducible tarot production outside the unique luxury courts. Their chief value for Cups is structural: stylized vessel signs could be printed in repeated symmetrical arrangements alongside courts and trumps.

Because sheets are incomplete, damaged, and sometimes divided among collections, reconstruction of an entire Cups suit is partly inferential. Claims that their ornament encodes a consistent alchemical programme remain disputed. The Rosenwald sheet also demonstrates that early cheap cards could contain crude or erroneous numerals; such errors warn against treating every printed detail as intentional esoteric doctrine.

---

## Tarot de Marseille lineage

### Jean Noblet, Paris, approximately 1650–1680

Noblet’s surviving pack has seventy-three of seventy-eight cards and is the earliest known substantially surviving example of the conventional Marseille type.

The Cups are open chalices in red, blue, yellow, and flesh tones. Number cards are non-scenic. Stems, leaves, flowers, and scrolls fill the spaces and often link the cups vertically. The Ace is a singular architectural chalice or ciborium-like vessel. No printed water, Heh, zodiac, or emotion keywords appear.

### Jean Dodal, Lyon, approximately 1701–1715

Dodal strengthens the bold woodcut outline and floral scaffolding. Chalices alternate with stems and blossoms. The layouts encode number primarily through visual rhythm:

- Two: paired cups with a central plant.
- Three: triangular or vertical relationship.
- Four: stable bilateral field.
- Five: four around a centre.
- Six through Ten: increasingly dense tiers, usually divided by vegetal ornament.

These meanings arise from modern visual reading; historically the arrangements first served legibility and decoration.

### Pierre Madenié, Dijon, 1709, and related Type II packs

Type II conventions regularized faces, floral motifs, and colour placement. Madenié is the oldest dated Type II Marseille-style pack presently recognized. It shows why “Marseille” should be understood as a pattern family, not proof that every early exemplar was made in Marseille.

### Nicolas Conver, Marseille, 1760/1761

Conver’s Cups became the main source for later commercial Marseille packs. The Ace resembles an ornate architectural reliquary or fountain, while the numbered cards remain floral pips.

Its apparent church architecture encouraged later Eucharistic, Grail, and “clergy estate” readings. Those are interpretive developments; the block does not title the Ace a church.

### Grimaud, nineteenth–twentieth century

Grimaud standardized a cleaner, commercially reproducible Conver-derived pattern, simplifying colours and lines. This helped turn one historical branch into the popular idea of “the” Tarot de Marseille.

### Camoin–Jodorowsky, 1997

Philippe Camoin and Alejandro Jodorowsky described their deck as a restoration assembled from old patterns and Camoin printing inheritance. Its Cups preserve Marseille geometry but introduce reconstructed details and colour decisions.

**Controversy.** Calling it the recovery of an original universal tarot exceeds the evidence. It is better classified as a learned modern reconstruction and spiritual reinterpretation. Jodorowsky and Marianne Costa read the suit dynamically as emotional life, love, receptivity, family, and affective development.

---

## Minchiate

Minchiate expanded the trump series to forty-one but retained the four fourteen-card Italian suits. Cups still comprise Ace–Ten and four courts.

In play, Cups behave like one of the two “round” suits and commonly rank King high down toward Ace, although rules vary by period and locality. The suit has no special Grail privilege. Minchiate’s expanded zodiacal and virtue trumps later tempted occult reinterpretation, but the historical game does not assign individual Cups to Golden Dawn decans.

---

## Bolognese tarocchino

The Bolognese game shortened the pack by removing low pip cards; surviving historical forms therefore do not always retain all fourteen Cups. Courts and remaining Cups participate in an Italian trick-taking game.

A handwritten or printed Bolognese cartomancy tradition, known from an eighteenth-century sheet, supplies brief meanings and may preserve or reflect meanings related to Etteilla’s system. Direction of borrowing remains disputed. It is valuable evidence that practical fortune-telling was not confined to Parisian occult theory.

---

## Sicilian tarot

Sicilian tarocchi developed a distinctive reduced pack and suit ranking. It preserves Italian suit signs, including Cups, but not always the complete Ace–Ten sequence familiar from a 78-card pack.

The Fool is a special card in play, not automatically the modern highest trump. Sicilian rules and designs cannot safely be projected back onto fifteenth-century Milan.

---

## Tarot de Besançon

Besançon-pattern packs replace the Papess and Pope with Juno and Jupiter, largely to avoid confessional offence in Protestant or mixed-confessional markets. The substitutions concern trumps II and V, not Cups. The Cups themselves remain Marseille-like ornamental pips.

This pattern flourished chiefly in Switzerland, Alsace, and adjacent areas in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries. Claims that Juno and Jupiter disclose an original pagan tarot are contradicted by the earlier Papess/Pope cards.

---

## Etteilla’s Grand Etteilla, designed 1788; issued around 1789

Etteilla reorganized the entire deck into a deliberately divinatory “Egyptian” system. Cards carry numbers, upright and reversed keywords, astrological references, and newly designed scenes.

His Cups inherit much of their practical meaning from Hearts in his earlier thirty-two-card cartomancy. A surviving illustrated manual even labels the same pip “*Le Dix de Coupe*” and “*Le Dix de Coeur*.” [Grand Etteilla manual](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/2/2f/Le_grand_Etteilla%2C_ou%2C_l%27art_de_tirer_les_cartes_..._Le_tout_recueilli_..._%28IA_b29321220%29.pdf)

Etteilla’s decisive changes were:

1. meanings printed onto cards;
2. explicit reversals;
3. a restructured sequence;
4. Egyptian cosmogony;
5. systematic astrology.

These are documented innovations, not recoveries of a pharaonic original.

---

## Oswald Wirth, 1889 and 1926

Wirth’s 1889 *22 Arcanes du Tarot Kabbalistique* contained only the twenty-two trumps. It therefore had no Wirth-designed Cups suit.

Modern “Oswald Wirth Tarot” packs with seventy-eight cards combine his trumps with publisher-created Marseille-style minors. Their Cups must not be cited as Wirth’s 1889 work. [Description of the original 22-card scope](https://foolmade.art/archive/wirth-tarot)

---

## Golden Dawn manuscript designs, approximately 1888–1900

“Book T” transformed Cups into a complete Hermetic system.

The Ace is a divine hand holding a cup or *stolistes*-like vessel, with a fountain, lotus, and water lilies. The numbered cards show lotus-fed cups whose water, stability, or corruption varies with Sephirah, planet, and decan. The courts stand or travel over water and display aquatic animals:

- Knight/King: mounted warrior; crab issues from cup; Fire of Water.
- Queen: enthroned over flowing water; crayfish and ibis; Water of Water.
- Prince/Knight: eagle-drawn chariot; serpent from cup; Air of Water.
- Princess/Page: on foaming sea; turtle and dolphin; Earth of Water.

The papers survive in copies rather than one authoritative published deck. Israel Regardie published the teachings; Robert Wang’s *Golden Dawn Tarot* and Chic and Sandra Tabatha Cicero’s reconstruction are later attempts to render manuscript specifications. Differences in colour and detail among reconstructions are not necessarily doctrinal disagreements.

[Golden Dawn manuscript transcription](https://www.tarrdaniel.com/documents/Thelemagick/gd/publication/english/golden_dawn_manuscripts.html)

## Waite–Smith, designed 1909; guide published 1910

Arthur Edward Waite supplied the programme; Pamela Colman Smith drew all seventy-eight cards. Smith’s numbered Cups are fully scenic:

| Card | Scene |
|---|---|
| Ace | A hand from cloud bears an overflowing cup; five streams descend; a dove brings a cross-marked wafer; water lilies below |
| Two | A man and woman exchange cups beneath a caduceus and winged lion’s head |
| Three | Three women raise cups in celebration amid fruit and flowers |
| Four | A seated youth ignores or contemplates a fourth cup offered from a cloud |
| Five | A cloaked figure mourns three overturned cups while two remain upright; bridge and house behind |
| Six | A child offers another child a flower-filled cup; old buildings and guard-like figure |
| Seven | A silhouetted person confronts seven cloud-borne visionary contents |
| Eight | A figure abandons stacked cups and walks into mountains under an eclipsed or paired Moon |
| Nine | A satisfied seated figure before nine displayed cups |
| Ten | Couple and children beneath a rainbow of ten cups |
| Page | Youth regards a fish rising from a cup |
| Knight | Armoured rider calmly bears a cup through a watery landscape |
| Queen | Queen by the sea contemplates an ornate closed cup |
| King | King enthroned amid turbulent water, ships, and a leaping fish |

This made Cups into a sequence of relationships, emotional choices, loss, memory, fantasy, departure, satisfaction, and domestic fulfilment. That narrative coherence is partly retrospective: Waite’s printed meanings remain a miscellany drawn from earlier cartomancy, while Smith’s images frequently sharpen one possibility into a memorable scene.

The Ace explicitly combines Golden Dawn fountain-and-lotus imagery with Christian Eucharist. Waite writes that the dove carries a “cross-marked Host” and that the card intimates what may lie behind the Lesser Arcana. [Waite, *Pictorial Key*, Cups](https://vellumandveil.com/tarot/the-pictorial-key-to-the-tarot/pktcuac)

The often-repeated claim that its five streams “are the five senses” is a later interpretive tradition; Waite does not say so in the *Pictorial Key*.

## Paul Foster Case and BOTA

Paul Foster Case adapted Waite–Smith/Golden Dawn doctrine for the Builders of the Adytum. Jessie Burns Parke drew the BOTA deck. The standard BOTA deck concentrates on the major trumps; later BOTA teaching assigns the minors through Tarot, Qabalah, number, element, and colour rather than establishing an independently influential scenic Cups cycle.

Cups = Water = Briah = first Heh is inherited from Lévi and the Golden Dawn. Case emphasizes subconscious receptivity and formative imagination. Claims about a complete early Case-designed pictorial minor deck require caution; many marketed “BOTA” minor cards are later adjuncts or reconstructions.

## Crowley–Harris Thoth Tarot, painted 1938–1943; first complete commercial publication 1969

Frieda Harris replaced human anecdotes with abstract, botanical, alchemical diagrams. Cups become lotus-fed vessels above seas or fields of water. Their flow changes from purity to stagnation and renewed fulfilment.

| Card | Thoth title and principal image |
|---|---|
| Ace | Root of the Powers of Water; Grail-like cup over the dark sea of Binah, lotus, dove, Moon |
| Two | Love; two cups, lotus fountain, intertwined dolphins |
| Three | Abundance; pomegranate cups overflowing from a lotus |
| Four | Luxury; four unstable cups on a ruffled sea |
| Five | Disappointment; empty cups in inverted pentagram, torn lotuses, dead water |
| Six | Pleasure; cups fed by dancing lotus stems under solar influence |
| Seven | Debauch; poisonous tiger-lilies pour slime into cups |
| Eight | Indolence; shallow, broken cups and drooping lotuses in stagnant pools |
| Nine | Happiness; nine symmetrically overflowing cups |
| Ten | Satiety; cups arranged as the Tree of Life, tilted and spilling |
| Knight | Fire of Water; mounted knight, peacock armour, cup and crab |
| Queen | Water of Water; shell-like sea throne, reflective watery forms |
| Prince | Air of Water; eagle-drawn chariot, lotus, cup and serpent |
| Princess | Earth of Water; dancer over water, fish, shell/lotus symbolism |

Crowley calls the Ace “the essential form of the Holy Grail.” This is a documented Thelemic attribution, not a medieval meaning of the suit. His full Cups discussion appears in *The Book of Thoth*, first published in 1944. [Online text, Cups section](https://www.tarrdaniel.com/documents/Thelemagick/publication/english/Book_of_Thoth.html)

The famous Tzaddi/Heh controversy concerns the Emperor and Star trumps. It does not change Cups’ attribution to the first Heh of the Tetragrammaton.

## Notable modern redesigns

- **Aquarian Tarot, Morgan–Greer, Robin Wood, Hanson–Roberts:** retain Waite–Smith scenes while intensifying colour, intimacy, or close-up psychology.
- **Thoth-derived decks:** retain lotus geometry, Crowley’s one-word pip titles, astrology, and Qabalistic structure.
- **Marseille revivals:** restore unillustrated pips and encourage reading number, arrangement, and visual rhythm instead of fixed RWS scenes.
- **Jodorowsky–Camoin:** treats each pip’s vegetal and cup structure as an organic developmental language.
- **Feminist, queer, decolonial, and culturally specific decks:** redesign courts and relationships, often removing gender hierarchy and heteronormativity.
- **Modern “Grail” decks:** sometimes rename Cups as Chalices and build an Arthurian narrative throughout. This is a modern thematic invention drawing on Waite’s earlier Grail synthesis.

# The game

## Original function

**Documented fact.** Tarot was created as a trick-taking game. Cups were an ordinary suit. Their pictures did not outrank trumps and had no divinatory force in play.

A standard historical Italian tarot pack eventually contained:

- 21 permanent trumps;
- the Fool;
- four suits of 14 cards;
- in each suit, ten numeral cards and four courts.

## Rank

In many Italian and central-European traditions, the two “round” suits—Cups and Coins, later Hearts and Diamonds—rank in reverse pip order:

> King, Queen, Knight, Page, Ace, Two, Three … Ten.

Thus the Ace is the highest pip and Ten the lowest. Swords and Batons commonly run King, Queen, Knight, Page, Ten down to Ace.

Modern French tarot instead ranks every ordinary suit King, Queen, Knight, Jack, 10 down to Ace. Sicilian and other regional games differ. One must state the game before giving rank.

## Point value

In many classic tarot-counting systems:

- King: 5 nominal points
- Queen: 4
- Knight: 3
- Page/Jack: 2
- Pip: 1 nominal point

Because points are counted in groups with deductions, these are often described equivalently as King 4, Queen 3, Knight 2, Jack 1, pip 0 intrinsic points. Both formulations can be correct within their counting convention. [Pagat, tarot card counting](https://www.pagat.com/tarot/counting.html)

No Cups card is ordinarily an *oudler* or honour comparable to the Fool, trump I, or trump XXI in French tarot.

## Following suit and trumping

Cups establish the led suit when a Cup is led. Players normally must follow Cups if able; if void they may have to trump, depending on the regional game. A high Cup can win only a Cup-led trick uncontested by a trump.

## Fool’s role

The historical Fool is usually an Excuse: it can be played without following suit and is recovered by its owner in exchange for another captured card. In some later games it becomes the highest trump. Calling it a universal “wild card” is convenient but imprecise.

## Trump orders

Dummett distinguished three principal early Italian order families:

- **Order A:** associated especially with Bologna and Florence.
- **Order B:** associated with Ferrara.
- **Order C:** associated with Milan and transmitted into the Marseille family.

Justice, Fortitude, and Temperance move relative to other trumps. The Fool is generally outside the numbered sequence. These disagreements do not reorder Cups, except insofar as local rules also change the direction of pip ranking.

Regional substitutions—Papess to Juno, Pope to Jupiter; later secularized names; *Maison Dieu* for the Tower—likewise concern trumps, not the suit.

## Colours, directions, and seasons

- **Golden Dawn:** Water, West, blue; Cups operate in Briah.
- **Season:** Autumn appears in some Golden Dawn-derived teaching, especially when the elemental cycle is arranged Fire/Summer, Water/Autumn, Air/Winter, Earth/Spring. Other tarot writers assign Cups to summer, spring, or winter.
- **Finding:** No universal historical Cups season exists. Season tables are esoteric conventions, and authors frequently conflict.
- **Playing-card colour:** Cups are a “round” Italian suit, not intrinsically red in all historical decks.
- **French analogue:** Hearts are red, but that does not prove medieval Cups were conceived as the modern emotional “heart suit.”

# Divinatory meanings by author

Exact quotations from public-domain writers are kept short. Rachel Pollack and Mary K. Greer remain copyrighted; their positions are therefore summarized rather than extensively quoted.

## Etteilla

Etteilla’s suit meanings were not a single timeless list. Editions and followers rearranged numbering and keywords. His Cups substantially inherit the semantic field of Hearts: love, satisfaction, home, union, friendship, feasting, children, and news, alongside reversals producing change, falsity, desire, or disappointment.

The safest reconstruction is edition-specific:

- Ace: table, feast, nourishment, house or domestic contentment; reversed, change or transformation in some Etteilla traditions.
- Two: love, desire, or attachment; reversed, obstacles or crossed desire.
- Three: success or an undertaking’s outcome; reversed, expedition or end.
- Four: weariness, displeasure, or boredom; reversed, new acquaintance or omen.
- Five: inheritance, relation, or family matter; reversed, alliance or arrival.
- Six: past, memory, ancestral or former condition; reversed, future.
- Seven: thought, intention, project; reversed, design or resolution.
- Eight: fair-haired girl/woman, celebration, satisfaction; reversed, respectable or modest woman, depending on recension.
- Nine: victory, satisfaction, advantage; reversed, sincerity or error depending on edition.
- Ten: town, home, family, or native place; reversed, anger or dispute.
- Courts: persons characterized by complexion, age, affection, service, or seduction.

These meanings influenced Mathers and Waite, but neither copied Etteilla consistently. The Grand Etteilla’s printed keywords should be consulted in the exact pattern being read.

## Paul Foster Case

Case’s practical meanings remain Golden Dawn-derived but are reframed through subconscious response and spiritual psychology:

- Ace: power of receptivity, spiritual abundance, the source of emotional imagination.
- Two through Four: attraction, fertility, and stabilization of feeling.
- Five through Eight: emotional disruption, glamour, and withdrawal.
- Nine and Ten: fulfilment, with the warning that completed desire may become inertia.
- Courts: modes through which elemental Water acts—impulse, receptivity, mental direction, and embodiment.

Case did not create a historically independent pre-Golden-Dawn Cups tradition; his importance lies in transmitting and psychologizing that system.

## Rachel Pollack

Pollack’s *Seventy-Eight Degrees of Wisdom* helped establish the modern interpretive arc:

- Ace opens emotional and spiritual possibility.
- Two establishes reciprocal relationship.
- Three enlarges feeling into community.
- Four withholds or turns inward.
- Five grieves.
- Six revisits childhood and memory.
- Seven proliferates fantasies and choices.
- Eight leaves an emotionally exhausted structure.
- Nine enjoys personal satisfaction.
- Ten extends fulfilment into family and shared life.
- Courts represent imaginative, relational temperaments rather than merely literal blond people.

This synthesis is modern and strongly Waite–Smith-based. Pollack treats myth and psychology as living interpretive tools, not proof of ancient authorship.

## Mary K. Greer

Greer’s work makes meanings participatory and autobiographical. Cups concern feeling, love, relationship, imagination, dreams, receptivity, and the ways these become projection or dependency.

Her use of reversals is deliberately plural: blocked, delayed, excessive, deficient, internalized, unconscious, or opposite manifestations may all be considered. She does not reduce every reversed Cup to a single negative keyword. Her workbook also explicitly distinguishes historical playing-card origins from the psyche’s meaningful use of the “ancient wisdom” myth.

# Esoteric and symbolic readings

## Elemental Water

**First secure systematic appearance:** Etteilla’s late-eighteenth-century elemental occultism, more fully formalized by Lévi and the Golden Dawn.

A cup naturally contains liquid, making Water the least strained of the occult suit-element pairings. Nevertheless, obvious analogy is not evidence that fifteenth-century card makers designed the suit as a coded Aristotelian element.

Golden Dawn Water includes receptivity, cohesion, reflection, intuition, feeling, dissolution, gestation, and the astral imagination.

## Heh and the Tetragrammaton

Lévi writes:

> “the Cup is the cteis or primitive HE”

(*Dogme et Rituel*, English translation, vol. I, chapter on the Qabalah; scan p. 66.) He then groups the four Aces, Twos, and so forth under the ten Sephiroth. [Lévi scan](https://www.hermetics.org/pdf/Eliphas_Levi_Dogma%20et%20Rituel%20de%20la%20Haute%20Magie%201.pdf)

The Cup is the first Heh of YHVH: receptive, maternal, formative. Golden Dawn assigns it to Briah, the Creative World. This is a nineteenth-century Christian-Qabalistic construction. It is not an attested Jewish interpretation of medieval playing cards.

## Sephiroth

The ten numbered Cups descend through:

Kether → Chokmah → Binah → Chesed → Geburah → Tiphareth → Netzach → Hod → Yesod → Malkuth.

This gives the suit a developmental logic:

1. elemental root;
2. pure polarity/attraction;
3. abundance and form;
4. stabilized pleasure;
5. disruption;
6. restored harmony;
7. emotional glamour;
8. depletion;
9. fulfilled foundation;
10. complete manifestation and saturation.

Crowley accentuates decay and instability after the harmonious Two and Three.

## Astrology

Golden Dawn assigns the nine numbered pips to the water signs:

- Cancer: Two–Four
- Scorpio: Five–Seven
- Pisces: Eight–Ten

The planetary rulers use the traditional Chaldean decan order. This system first appears in Golden Dawn materials; it should not be retrojected into Marseille floral arrangements.

Etteilla’s earlier astrology distributes planets, signs, nodes, and the Part of Fortune differently. “Etteilla correspondence” and “Golden Dawn correspondence” are not interchangeable.

## Christian and Eucharistic reading

The Cup’s Christian potential arises from actual European vessels: chalice, ciborium, fountain, baptismal source, and Eucharistic cup. Yet the early pips lack captions establishing a sacramental intention.

Waite makes the association explicit in 1909–1910. The dove, Host, water, and overflowing vessel turn the Ace into a channel of grace. His language—“Holy Table” and “that which may lie behind the Lesser Arcana”—is documented.

## The Grail

### Historical finding

No fifteenth-century tarot document identifies Cups with the Holy Grail. No early Ace bears the word Grail, and the four suit objects do not reproduce the complete set of Grail-procession relics in any demonstrable original programme.

### Esoteric development

Waite’s extensive Grail studies connected the tarot suit emblems with the Grail Hallows—cup, sword, lance/wand, and dish/stone. In 1909 he acknowledged correspondences between tarot symbols and the Grail but treated them as secret-tradition analogies rather than straightforward archaeological descent.

Crowley made the identification explicit: the Ace of Cups is “the essential form of the Holy Grail.” His Grail can contain Water, Wine, Blood, and the life-fluid of the Great Mother.

Thus:

- medieval tarot derived from the Grail Hallows — **disputed and unsupported as origin history**;
- Waite used Grail symbolism to interpret Cups — **documented**;
- Crowley’s Ace is the Grail — **documented esoteric attribution**;
- modern Chalice/Grail decks elaborate this — **modern tradition**.

## Alchemy

Golden Dawn lotus fountains describe circulation, mixture, overflow, corruption, and precipitation. Crowley adds:

- Two’s dolphins and “Royal Art”;
- Three’s pomegranate and Persephone;
- Five’s dead sea and inverted pentagram;
- Six’s fertilizing putrefaction;
- Seven’s corrupt copper/Venus;
- Ten’s completed but unstable circulation.

Sola Busca has repeatedly been interpreted alchemically, but a comprehensive decipherment is not established. Its animated vessels make the hypothesis attractive; the absence of an explanatory contemporary text keeps it disputed.

## Jungian reading

“Cups = Jung’s feeling function” is a twentieth-century analogy. Jung did not create the standard tarot-Cups correspondence.

In Jungian tarot practice:

- Water resembles the unconscious.
- The cup is an ego-container or vessel of transformation.
- Overflow may signify affect exceeding containment.
- Empty or broken cups may signify loss of libido, mourning, or failed containment.
- Courts may personify anima/animus figures, complexes, or relational roles.
- The Ten may image belonging; the Seven projection; the Eight withdrawal from outworn attachments.

This approach was spread through post-Jungian writers and the human-potential/New Age tarot revival, especially from the 1970s onward.

# People and works

## Artists, makers, and occultists

- **Michelino da Besozzo and Marziano da Tortona:** important to the structural prehistory, but their pack had bird suits.
- **Bonifacio Bembo and workshop:** conventional attribution for several Milanese luxury packs; exact hands disputed.
- **Francesco Zavattari, Benedetto Bembo, Franco dei Russi:** alternative attributions for parts of the Visconti groups.
- **Nicola di Maestro Antonio / Mattia Serrati da Cosandola:** competing or complementary names in Sola Busca scholarship.
- **Jean Noblet, Jean Dodal, Pierre Madenié, Nicolas Conver:** principal Marseille-pattern makers.
- **Jean-Baptiste Alliette/Etteilla:** professionalized tarot divination and built the first purpose-designed occult pack.
- **Éliphas Lévi:** established Cup–Heh and sephirotic suit logic.
- **Gérard Encausse/Papus:** popularized the synthetic Kabbalistic “Tarot of the Bohemians.”
- **Oswald Wirth:** drew occult trumps, not an original Cups suit.
- **S. L. MacGregor Mathers:** transmitted French occult tarot into English and helped formulate Golden Dawn practice.
- **Arthur Edward Waite:** Christian mystic and designer of the Waite–Smith programme.
- **Pamela Colman Smith:** actual artist of the deck’s revolutionary scenic Cups.
- **Frieda Harris:** transformed Book T into the Thoth deck’s abstract, projective geometry.
- **Aleister Crowley:** supplied Thelemic titles and commentary.
- **Paul Foster Case and Jessie Burns Parke:** BOTA transmission and visual pedagogy.
- **Rachel Pollack and Mary K. Greer:** major modern psychological and experiential interpreters.
- **Alejandro Jodorowsky and Marianne Costa:** influential Marseille visual and psychogenealogical synthesis.

## Literature, cinema, and art

### T. S. Eliot, *The Waste Land* (1922)

Madame Sosostris is described with a “wicked pack of cards.” Eliot names several pseudo-tarot cards but not the Suit of Cups. The passage is evidence of tarot’s modernist cultural presence, not of a particular Cups doctrine.

### W. B. Yeats

Yeats’s Golden Dawn involvement connects him to the occult environment in which Book T was developed. Direct claims that a particular poem encodes the fourteen Cups require textual demonstration and are usually speculative.

### Italo Calvino, *The Castle of Crossed Destinies*

Calvino uses tarot images as a combinatorial language from which mute travellers construct stories. Different editions and critical discussions reference Visconti and Marseille imagery. Cups participate in narrative grammar, but Calvino does not supply a single canonical suit doctrine.

### Jodorowsky

Tarot imagery informs Jodorowsky’s films, theatre, comics, and therapeutic practice. Claims that an individual shot “quotes the Ace of Cups” range from plausible visual comparison to fan interpretation; a production note or direct statement is needed before treating it as documented.

### Ingmar Bergman

*The Seventh Seal* is frequently associated with medieval tarot because of Death, chess, and procession imagery. It is not a documented adaptation of Cups. General tarot resemblance should not be mistaken for a specific quotation.

### Paintings and buildings

Architectural Aces of Cups resemble fountains, reliquaries, ciboria, façades, and baptismal structures. No single building has been established as the prototype common to Cary–Yale, Brambilla, and Marseille Aces. Proposed identifications are comparative iconography, not documented copying.

Pamela Colman Smith’s Ace combines the architectural/fountain tradition with Golden Dawn lotus-water imagery and explicit Eucharistic symbolism. It is therefore a synthesis, not a portrait of a known church.

# Controversies and disputes

## Egyptian origin

**Legend, first securely published in 1781.** Court de Gébelin asserted that tarot was an Egyptian book preserved under the guise of a game. De Mellet named it the Book of Thoth and joined it to Hebrew letters and divination.

Evidence against historical Egyptian tarot authorship:

- Tarot is documented in fifteenth-century northern Italy.
- Its Pope, Emperor, civic virtues, and Christian Last Judgment belong to late-medieval Europe.
- No Egyptian tarot deck, text, transmission document, or matching hieroglyphic manual survives.
- Gébelin wrote before Egyptian hieroglyphs were deciphered.

The Mamluk ancestry of the ordinary suits does supply a real Egyptian geographical connection, but Mamluk Egypt was medieval and Islamic, not pharaonic, and the tarot trumps were added in Italy. The true Mamluk connection cannot rescue Gébelin’s Book-of-Thoth claim.

Court de Gébelin’s original essay is available in French and translation. [*Le Monde primitif*, vol. VIII, pp. 365–410](https://ldysinger.com/@texts2/1776_Court_Masons_Tarot/02_Monde_Primitif.htm)

## Romani or “Gypsy” transmission

**Legend.** Nineteenth-century writers increasingly claimed Romani people carried tarot from Egypt or India. The title *Tarot des Bohémiens* helped spread the association.

Chronology works against it: tarot is securely documented in Italy before evidence that Romani fortune-tellers transmitted the pack as an occult book. The Romani language demonstrates origins in the Indian subcontinent, not ancient Egypt. Romani practitioners later used European cards, but later use does not establish invention.

## Cathar survival

**Modern speculative claim.** Some twentieth-century writers interpreted the Grail, Papess, or dualistic trump imagery as Cathar doctrine concealed from persecution. No Cathar manuscript describes a tarot pack; no provenance chain joins Cathar communities to fifteenth-century card makers. Cups-as-Grail is insufficient evidence.

## Templar survival

**Legend/modern invention.** Similar claims make Cups the Grail guarded by Templars and tarot a vehicle of suppressed Templar wisdom. No Templar inventory, rule, trial record, or manuscript documents tarot. The order was suppressed more than a century before the earliest tarot records.

## Cups as the clergy estate

This is a widely repeated traditional schema:

- Cups — clergy
- Swords — nobility/warriors
- Coins — merchants
- Batons — peasants

It is symbolically intelligible because chalices are liturgical, but evidence that the four suits were deliberately invented as a complete estate allegory is weak. Mamluk predecessors already possessed the four signs without Christian clergy. Court de Gébelin and later occultists helped circulate estate explanations. It should be labelled **later allegorical interpretation**, not documented medieval intention.

## Ace as church, fountain, chalice, or Grail

- **Fountain:** documented in the Morgan’s description and visually secure.
- **Architectural chalice/ciborium:** strong formal description.
- **Church:** plausible resemblance, unconfirmed identification.
- **Eucharist:** explicit only in Waite–Smith and later esoteric interpretation.
- **Grail:** explicit in Waitean/Crowleyan occultism, not in early records.

## Sola Busca as direct source for all Waite–Smith minors

Smith’s access to British Museum photographs and several striking correspondences support direct influence. “Every Waite–Smith minor copies Sola Busca” is an exaggeration. The Cups scenes mostly follow Book T meanings, older cartomancy, Waite’s Christian interests, Smith’s imagination, and everyday genre art.

## Five streams as five senses

Popular modern interpretation; absent from Waite’s card description. It may be used symbolically but should not be attributed to Waite without further evidence.

## Water, colour, and season as ancient meanings

Cups = Water is an occult attribution formalized in the modern period. Blue and West are Golden Dawn correspondences. Autumn is system-dependent. None is printed on fifteenth-century Cups.

## Misprints becoming traditions

Several mechanisms created false traditions:

- crude Roman numerals on early sheets were reinterpreted as mysteries;
- reordered Etteilla cards and variant keywords were copied without edition control;
- Golden Dawn King/Knight/Prince terminology produced modern court-element confusion;
- Thoth printings introduced colour and detail errors, subsequently repeated in commentary;
- later complete “Wirth” packs encouraged the belief that Wirth designed fifty-six minors;
- Conver-derived modern redrawing was sometimes advertised as exact ancient restoration.

The proper remedy is not to declare one modern deck uniquely authentic, but to identify the particular block, edition, manuscript recension, or reconstruction.

---

# Open questions

1. **Exact priority of the earliest surviving Cups.** Cary–Yale and Brera–Brambilla dating remains close and attribution-dependent.

2. **Original size of the Cary–Yale pack.** Six courts per suit are strongly inferred, but the complete original pack does not survive.

3. **Meaning of the architectural Ace.** Fountain, chalice, dynastic emblem, ciborium, and façade all contribute; no contemporary explanatory caption settles the balance.

4. **Exact route from Mamluk cards into Italy.** Structural descent is strongly supported, but the workshops, ports, intermediaries, and dates remain reconstructive.

5. **Meaning of Sola Busca scenes.** Alchemical, historical, political, comic, and initiatory interpretations compete.

6. **Bolognese cartomancy versus Etteilla.** Similar meanings may reflect borrowing, a shared oral tradition, or later contamination; chronology and manuscript dating remain important.

7. **Pamela Colman Smith’s sources card by card.** British Museum Sola Busca photographs, Book T, stage design, contemporary illustration, Waite’s instructions, and Smith’s invention contributed in different proportions.

8. **First unambiguous estate attribution.** The popular “Cups = clergy” formula needs a more precise pre-Gébelin textual genealogy.

9. **First exact seasonal table.** Autumn is common in Golden Dawn-derived writing, but competing tables are early and persistent.

10. **Jungian authorship.** Many modern books call meanings “Jungian” without identifying whether they derive from Jung, post-Jungians, Pollack, Greer, Sallie Nichols, or general therapeutic language.

11. **Grail transmission.** Waite’s published Grail-tarot analogy can be documented; claims of older secret-school transmission cannot presently be independently verified.

---

# Conclusions

The fullest reconstructable history divides into four phases.

First, Cups emerged as one of four Islamic/Italian playing-card signs. By February 1442 the suit is explicitly named in a Ferrara account. Its earliest surviving Milanese forms are luxurious, counted chalices, with monumental fountain-like Aces and aristocratic courts. Nothing in those objects demonstrates divination, elemental Water, emotional psychology, Kabbalah, or the Grail.

Second, printed European traditions stabilized the suit. Marseille packs transformed the chalices into a dense ornamental language of cups, stems, flowers, and symmetry, while regional games determined rank and value. Scenic Sola Busca cards were the exceptional early alternative.

Third, French occultism reinvented the suit. Gébelin supplied Egypt and social estates; Etteilla supplied divination, reversals, astrology, and Hearts-to-Cups meanings; Lévi supplied Heh and the Sephiroth; Papus and the Golden Dawn systematized Cups as Water in Briah.

Fourth, Waite, Smith, Crowley, Harris, Case, Pollack, Greer, and Jodorowsky turned this constructed correspondence into different experiential languages. Smith made Cups a human drama of relationship and feeling. Harris made them the changing hydraulics of cosmic Water. Waite and Crowley made the Ace Eucharistic and Grail-like. Jungian and therapeutic readers made the cup a container of affect and unconscious content.

The Grail, Water, Heh, clergy, Autumn, and emotional life are therefore genuine parts of the suit’s reception history. They are not all parts of its fifteenth-century origin.
