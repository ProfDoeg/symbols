# Ace of Cups: Research Dossier

## Method and evidentiary key

This dossier distinguishes six kinds of claim:

- **Documented fact** — established by a surviving card, archival record, printed rule, or primary text.
- **Scholarly reconstruction** — an inference advanced by historians from surviving objects and documents.
- **Tradition** — a convention transmitted through cardmaking, gaming, or divination, without necessarily claiming ancient authority.
- **Esoteric attribution** — a deliberately constructed occult correspondence.
- **Disputed claim** — a proposal for which specialists disagree or the evidence is inconclusive.
- **Legend / modern invention** — a story or interpretation unsupported by the early record, though potentially important to later practice.

Because this is a pip card, several subjects in the general brief—trump orders, the Papess/Juno substitutions, Tzaddi, and so forth—do not directly govern it. They are addressed where they clarify the distinction between the Ace and the trumps.

## Basic identification

| Field | Finding |
|---|---|
| Standard English name | **Ace of Cups** |
| Italian | **Asso di Coppe**; historically simply a single *coppa* within the suit *coppe* |
| French | **As de Coupes**, singular suit-name *Coupe* |
| Spanish | **As de Copas** |
| German | **Kelch-Ass**, **Ass der Kelche** in divinatory decks; in German-language games the corresponding red-suit card may instead be a Hearts Ace |
| Latin suit ancestry | Italian/Spanish cups belong to the Latin-suit family: cups, coins, swords, batons |
| Number | **One / Ace**. Usually marked by a single suit-sign, not a Roman numeral. It is not trump I. |
| Card class | Pip or numeral card of Cups, one of the 56 ordinary suited cards in a standard 78-card tarot pack |
| Earliest surviving tarot example | **Visconti di Modrone/Cary–Yale Ace of Cups**, Milan, generally dated c. 1440–1445, subject to cataloguing differences; the slightly later Visconti-Sforza Ace also survives |
| Earliest ancestry of the suit | Islamic/Mamluk cup-suit cards, surviving in fragments dated broadly to the thirteenth–fourteenth centuries and in the near-complete fifteenth-century Topkapı pack |
| Golden Dawn title | **“The Root of the Powers of Water”** |
| Golden Dawn structure | Ace = **Kether**; Cups = **Briah**, Water, and the first **Heh** of YHVH |
| Astrology | The Golden Dawn gives the Ace no planet-in-decan attribution. It is the elemental root covering the whole watery quadrant—Cancer, Scorpio, Pisces—rather than one ten-degree decan. Case later gives it the seasonal span Cancer 0° through Virgo 30°. |
| Rank in play | Not a trump. In the older “round-suit” ranking, it is the highest numeral of Cups, below the four courts; in modern French tarot it is the lowest card of the suit. |
| Point value | Normally an ordinary low-value counting card, despite its relatively high trick-taking rank in some Italian games |

**Central correction — documented fact:** “Ace” and trump I are different cards. The Ace of Cups belongs to an ordinary suit; the Magician/Bagatto is the lowest numbered trump. Modern French rules sometimes call trump I *le Petit* or *l’Atout 1*, which can produce misleading English references to a “trump ace.”

# Iconography, deck by deck

## Before tarot: Islamic and early European cup suits

### Mamluk cards, Egypt and Syria, thirteenth–fifteenth centuries

**Documented fact.** Fragmentary Islamic playing cards survive from the thirteenth–fourteenth centuries; LACMA describes them as among the earliest surviving playing cards from the Islamic world. A nearly complete fifteenth-century Mamluk pack discovered at Topkapı Palace has four suits conventionally identified as cups, coins, swords, and polo sticks. Its cards are non-figural, with geometric, vegetal, and calligraphic ornament.

**Scholarly reconstruction.** The close structural relationship between Mamluk suits and the earliest Italian and Iberian suits makes Mediterranean transmission the prevailing explanation. This is ancestry of the ordinary four-suit pack, not proof that tarot’s additional trumps came from Egypt.

The cup suit is sometimes transcribed *ṭūmān*. Its exact etymology and the earlier Asian history of the pack remain debated. Dummett and Kamal Abu-Deeb argued that the Mamluk system itself may have eastern antecedents.

**What changed in Europe:** the abstract court inscriptions became kings and other human court figures; polo sticks became Italian batons; cups were redrawn as European goblets, chalices, or covered vessels.

[LACMA Mamluk fragment](https://collections.lacma.org/object/114976)

## Fifteenth-century Milan

### Visconti di Modrone / Cary–Yale, c. 1440–1445, Milan

**Documented object.** The unusually large, gilded and hand-painted Cary–Yale pack retains six court cards in some suits, rather than the later standard four. Its Ace of Cups displays one sumptuous cup or fountain-like vessel against a decorated ground.

**Interpretive limit.** The surviving image supports identification as the suit-sign. It does not carry a surviving medieval inscription calling it the Grail, Water, love, or Kether. Those are later readings.

**Dating note:** “Visconti di Modrone” and “Cary–Yale” refer to the same group. It is often called the earliest surviving tarot pack, although exact sequencing among the early Visconti commissions remains reconstructed rather than archival certainty.

### Brambilla deck, before 1447, Milan

The Metropolitan Museum identifies the Brambilla as almost certainly made for Filippo Maria Visconti before his death in 1447. Only a small part survives, with many Coins and two trumps.  

**Absence finding:** no securely identified surviving Brambilla Ace of Cups was located; one should not write a reconstruction of it as though it were extant.

[Metropolitan Museum overview](https://www.metmuseum.org/de/perspectives/tarot-2)

### Visconti-Sforza / Pierpont Morgan–Bergamo, c. 1450–1480, Milan

**Documented object.** The surviving Ace of Cups is a large, elaborate golden fountain or covered chalice. Morgan exhibition material expressly identifies it as “a large fountain” and a Sforza device. Water and architectural ornament convert a single utilitarian cup into a dynastic emblem of abundance and magnificence.

The object now commonly called the Visconti-Sforza tarot is divided among the Morgan Library, Accademia Carrara in Bergamo, and the Colleoni collection.

**Scholarly reconstruction:** the fountain’s primary recoverable context is heraldic and courtly, not an encoded Grail mystery. Christian or baptismal resonance is possible in the general culture, but no contemporary explanation of the card survives.

[Morgan medieval-card labels](https://www.themorgan.org/sites/default/files/pdf/press/MedievalLabels.pdf)

## Ferrara and related fifteenth-century material

### Este cards

Fragments associated with the Este court demonstrate Ferrarese experimentation in tarot imagery. Attribution and dating vary among collections.

**Absence finding:** the surviving Este fragments do not provide a well-documented, complete developmental series for the Ace of Cups comparable to the Visconti-Sforza example. Claims that a particular isolated ornate cup proves alchemical intent exceed the surviving evidence.

### Sola Busca, 1491 engraving; painted copy probably c. 1491–early sixteenth century

**Documented fact.** The Sola Busca is the earliest surviving complete 78-card tarot and the earliest complete pack whose pip cards are elaborately illustrated rather than merely arranging suit-signs. Its historical and pseudo-historical figures are unlike the conventional Marseille trumps.

The Ace of Cups shows a single monumental cup integrated into the deck’s dense all’antica visual language. Unlike Waite’s later Ace, it does not show a hand from a cloud, Eucharistic dove, five effluents, or pond of lilies.

**Influence on Waite-Smith:** photographs of the Sola Busca were displayed at the British Museum in 1908. Several Waite-Smith numbered pips—notably the Three of Swords and Ten of Wands—show persuasive visual debts. A specific Sola-Busca source for the Waite-Smith Ace of Cups is not established; its composition is much closer to Golden Dawn *Book T*.

**Disputed claim:** recent esoteric studies interpret the Sola Busca as a coordinated alchemical or initiatory program. The imagery certainly invites learned readings, but there is no surviving fifteenth-century manual decoding it.

### Mantegna “Tarocchi,” c. 1460s

**Documented fact:** these are fifty didactic engravings arranged into five ten-card series—social estates, Apollo and the Muses, liberal arts/sciences, virtues/cosmic principles—not a tarot playing pack.

**Relevance to the Ace:** none directly. They contain no Ace of Cups and no four-suit structure. Their old name “Mantegna Tarocchi” is a cataloguing tradition and a misnomer; neither Mantegna’s authorship nor their identity as tarot cards is accepted.

## Early printed sheets

### Rosenwald sheets, Florence, c. 1480–1500

The Rosenwald sheets preserve early Florentine-style trumps and suited cards and are important evidence for the structure from which Minchiate developed.

**Absence finding:** surviving sheets and reproductions do not provide a clear, independently explained “Ace of Cups doctrine.” They document a playing-card pattern.

### Budapest sheets, probably northern Italy, late fifteenth or early sixteenth century

These fragmentary printed sheets preserve tarot subjects and decorative conventions.

**Absence finding:** no secure card-specific textual interpretation of an Ace of Cups accompanies them. Their relevance is to manufacture and regional pattern transmission, not divination.

---

## Minchiate, Florence and Tuscany

Minchiate expanded the trump series to forty special cards while retaining the four ordinary suits.

**Documented game/iconography:** the Ace of Cups remains an ornate single cup. It does not acquire a special additional trump-like identity merely because Minchiate has more trumps.

One early engraved non-tarot series catalogued by Willshire contains an ace represented as “an ornamental fountain,” accompanied by falconers and cupids. This demonstrates that elaborating an ace into a fountain was part of Renaissance card ornament; it does not by itself identify the Ace as the Grail.

[Willshire, *Descriptive Catalogue of Playing and Other Cards*](https://iapsop.com/ssoc/1876__willshire___descriptive_catalogue_of_playing_and_other_cards.pdf)

---

## Bolognese pattern

The shortened Bolognese *tarocchino* retains Ace and 6–10 in Cups and Coins while eliminating 2–5.

The Bolognese Ace of Cups is a large stylized vessel, often made reversible in modern manufacture. Its practical function is immediately legible as the single surviving low-number denomination of its suit.

**Documented rank:** King, Queen, Knight, Jack, Ace, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10. Thus the Ace is the highest pip but below every court.

**Documented value:** as an ordinary card it counts one raw point before the paired-card subtraction; it is not one of the high-value *tarocchi*.

[Pagat: Bolognese Ottocento](https://www.pagat.com/tarot/ottocento.html)

---

## Sicilian pattern

The traditional Sicilian game is radically shortened. Cups normally retain 5–10 and the courts, not an Ace. The separate Ace of Coins was reintroduced in the nineteenth century to carry the fiscal stamp.

**Absence finding:** there is no traditional Ace of Cups in the surviving 63-card Sicilian game pack. Applying conventional Ace-of-Cups divination to it is therefore an import from another pattern.

[Pagat: Sicilian Tarocchi](https://www.pagat.com/tarot/sicilian.html)

# Tarot de Marseille lineage

## Jean Noblet, Paris, c. 1650

**Documented object.** The Ace is a great gilt, lidded vessel. Its upper structure resembles a little castle, reliquary, tabernacle, or multi-towered crown; vegetal forms spill around the rim.

Calling it a “fountain” is a defensible visual description. Calling it a “church” is an interpretation. Calling it the “Grail Castle” is a much later esoteric-romantic reading for which no statement by Noblet survives.

[Jean Noblet Ace of Cups](https://foolmade.art/archive/noblet-tarot/cups-01)

## Jean Dodal, Lyon, c. 1701–1715

Dodal retains the Type-I Marseille monumental, closed cup: pedestal, bowl, foliage, and an architectural lid. Drawing and coloring differ, but the basic concept remains one oversized ceremonial vessel.

**Tradition:** this is still a suit-sign, not a narrative scene.

## Pierre Madenié, Dijon, 1709, and Type II

Type-II makers regularized the Marseille vocabulary. The cup becomes more symmetrical and architectonic. The earliest dated Type-II example is generally assigned to Madenié, not to Conver.

## Nicolas Conver, Marseille, 1760/1761

Conver’s Ace has the familiar Type-II form: a massive golden cup, foot and stem, bulbous body, foliage or spray at the sides, and a crown-like architectural top. Conver’s pattern became exceptionally influential through later Marseille manufacture.

The alternating red, blue, yellow, flesh/white, and black are stencil colors, not a surviving color-key supplied by Conver.

## Grimaud / Paul Marteau, twentieth century

Grimaud’s *Ancien Tarot de Marseille*, particularly under Paul Marteau, standardized a heavily recolored Conver-derived pattern. The Ace remained a monumental architectural chalice, but industrial line, color, and restoration decisions became widely mistaken for timeless medieval canon.

## Camoin–Jodorowsky, 1997

Philippe Camoin and Alejandro Jodorowsky presented their deck as a restoration assembled from surviving Marseille examples and printing knowledge. Their Ace emphasizes color details and the vessel’s possible bodily, floral, temple, and generative forms.

**Documented fact:** it is a modern reconstruction/redesign.

**Disputed claim:** the assertion that it restores a single original, encoded tarot cannot be demonstrated from one archetypal printing block or explanatory text. Jodorowsky and Marianne Costa interpret the cup as affective life, receptivity, love, spiritual abundance, and a temple or cathedral of feeling. These are modern symbolic readings, not documented seventeenth-century meanings.

---

## Tarot de Besançon

Besançon packs are derived from the Marseille tradition but replace the Papess and Pope with Juno and Jupiter, principally in regions where overt papal imagery was objectionable. That substitution concerns trumps II and V, not Cups.

A useful card-specific difference is that the Besançon Ace of Cups is often rounder and gadrooned, resembling a ciborium rather than the tall Marseille castle-cup.

**Documented regional renaming:** Juno/Jupiter occurs in eighteenth-century eastern France, Switzerland, and neighboring German-speaking production. No equivalent renaming of the Ace is known.

[French overview of Marseille and Besançon types](https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tarot_de_Marseille)

---

## Etteilla’s *Grand Etteilla*, design c. 1788, publication c. 1789

Etteilla reorganized the entire pack as an expressly occult/divinatory *Livre de Thot*. In surviving later printings his Cup Ace is card **49**, titled **Table**: a hand in a red sleeve presents one large golden cup.

- Upright keyword: **Table**
- Reversed keyword: **Changement**

The hand makes the card an offered vessel or hospitality object, anticipating—but not pictorially equaling—Waite’s supernatural hand.

**Documented innovation:** this is among the first deliberately occult tarot packs, not a preservation of a pharaonic deck.

[Grand Etteilla card 49](https://foolmade.art/archive/grand-etteilla/card-049)

## Éliphas Lévi, Papus, and Wirth

### Lévi, 1854–1856

Lévi’s *Dogme et rituel de la haute magie* linked the twenty-two trumps with the Hebrew alphabet and made tarot a master-key of ceremonial occultism.

**Absence finding:** Lévi did not give a developed, card-specific Ace-of-Cups system comparable to *Book T*. His most influential constructions concern the trumps. Later authors sometimes back-project Golden Dawn minor-arcana doctrine into Lévi.

### Papus, *Le Tarot des Bohémiens*, 1889

Papus systematized tarot through number, suit, Tetragrammaton, Kabbalah, and cyclical diagrams. Cups participate in the second term/Heh, receptive or feminine formation.

A meaning circulated under Papus’s name for the Ace is “commencement of a love affair,” but editions and derivative tables should be checked carefully: Papus’s diagrammatic discussion, Etteilla’s keywords, and later English compilations are frequently conflated.

### Oswald Wirth, 1889

**Documented correction:** Wirth’s original *Les 22 Arcanes du Tarot Kabbalistique* contains only the twenty-two trumps. It has no Ace of Cups. Any “Wirth Ace of Cups” belongs to a modern publisher’s 78-card extension.

[British Museum Wirth record](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/P_1904-0511-48-1-22)  
[Wirth archive](https://foolmade.art/archive/wirth-tarot)

# Golden Dawn and its reconstructions

## *Book T*, late nineteenth century

The Golden Dawn transformed the ornamental Ace into a magical image:

> “A White Radiant Angelic Hand, issuing from clouds”

It supports a cup resembling that of a ritual purifier or *Stolistes*. A fountain rises and falls into calm water containing lotuses and water-lilies. The letter **Heh** is traced in the spray.

Its title is **The Root of the Powers of Water**. Its stated qualities include fertility, productiveness, beauty, pleasure, and happiness.

### Exact correspondence

- Number: One
- Sephirah: Kether, the Crown
- Suit-world: Cups = Briah, the Creative World
- Element: Water
- Divine-name letter: first Heh of YHVH
- Zodiacal field: the watery signs collectively, not a single decan
- Color principle: Kether in the Queen Scale/Briah, described as divine white brilliance; reconstructions commonly render the cup, water, or Heh in blue/white
- Court sub-element: not applicable
- Planetary decan: not applicable

**Important distinction:** *Heh* belongs here through Cups/Briah. It is not a Hebrew-letter assignment to the Ace as though the Ace were one of the twenty-two paths.

## Robert Wang, *The Golden Dawn Tarot*, 1977

Wang’s deck, prepared with Israel Regardie, visualizes the surviving Golden Dawn descriptions: hand, chalice, fountain, lotuses, water, and letter Heh. Its geometry and color are a twentieth-century realization, not a surviving order-issued master deck.

## Sandra Tabatha and Chic Cicero, *The Golden Dawn Magical Tarot*, 2000

The Ciceros likewise reconstruct Golden Dawn texts using formal magical color scales and ritual symbolism. It is another legitimate realization of written instructions, not proof that every Golden Dawn temple used an identical painted Ace.

[Regardie, *Complete Golden Dawn System of Magic*](https://scriptaetveritas.com.br/wp-content/uploads/2016/04/ISRAEL_REGARDIE_COMPLETE_GOLDEN_DAWN_SYSTEM_OF_MAGIC.pdf)

# Waite-Smith, 1909

Pamela Colman Smith’s drawing shows:

- a right hand emerging from a cloud;
- an enormous cup marked with a reversed or stylized **M/W**;
- five visible outflows;
- twenty-six falling drops;
- a dove descending with a cross-marked Eucharistic wafer;
- water below with water-lilies.

Waite’s published description says that **four** streams pour from the cup, although the card is commonly perceived as showing five. This description-image mismatch helped produce the later “five senses” interpretation. That interpretation is now widespread but does not occur in Waite’s account.

Waite calls the card:

> “an intimation of that which may lie behind the Lesser Arcana.”

### Sources of the image

- **Documented source:** the hand, cloud, cup, fountain, lilies, and Heh derive closely from Golden Dawn *Book T*.
- **Documented personal context:** Waite was deeply involved in Christian mysticism and Grail literature.
- **Strong scholarly interpretation:** Smith and Waite converted the Golden Dawn Ace into a Eucharistic Grail. The dove, host, chalice, water/wine imagery, and “Holy Table” meaning support this directly.
- **Possible art-historical source:** the hand from heaven is a long-standing Jewish and Christian device for divine action; no single model has been proved.
- **Disputed detail:** the cup’s M/W has been read as *Mem*, Water, Mary, Waite, a printer’s mark, or a reversed M inherited from an earlier Italian/Piedmontese cup. No definitive statement by Smith or Waite settles it.
- **Modern invention:** exactly equating the five streams with the five physical senses is not traceable to Waite’s text.

Waite’s text and reproduced image appear in *The Pictorial Key to the Tarot*, issued 1910 with a 1911 title-page date.

# Paul Foster Case and BOTA

Case preserved the Golden Dawn title but reworked the image in the Knapp/Case and BOTA tradition.

His *Oracle of the Tarot* describes:

- a downward-pointing vesica, symbol of Water and receptivity;
- a radiant crown with ten descending rays;
- a Rose Cross;
- the Ace as Kether in the Creative World;
- the suit as the second letter Heh of YHVH;
- the keyword **Desire-force**.

Case gives the Ace a broad astrological period, Cancer 0° through the end of Virgo, approximately June 21–September 22. This is not the Golden Dawn’s standard pip-decan scheme and should be credited to Case’s presentation.

BOTA’s coloring practice asks students to color cards themselves. Therefore individual cards vary, although the underlying line design is fixed.

[Case, *Oracle of the Tarot*](https://media-01.imu.nl/storage/divona.be/17547/oracle-of-tarot-paul-foster-case.pdf)

# Crowley–Harris Thoth Tarot

## Painting 1938–1943; first full publication 1969

Frieda Harris painted a radically abstract, luminous Ace:

- a blue-green, translucent or crystalline Grail;
- lotus forms supplying life-fluid;
- concentric waves and luminous currents;
- a descending dove or spiritual force;
- lunar and maternal imagery;
- interlaced rings on the vessel;
- no naturalistic hand from a cloud.

Crowley calls it the essential form of the Holy Grail and the feminine complement of the Ace of Wands. Water may be read as water, blood, or wine according to the symbolism being activated. The dark sea is Binah, the Great Mother, though the Ace itself is Kether of Water.

> “This card represents the element of Water in its most secret and original form.”

Crowley retains the Golden Dawn title, sometimes printed in the plural as **“Root of the Powers of Water/Waters.”**

### Tzaddi/Heh clarification

Crowley’s “Tzaddi is not the Star” revision exchanges the traditional Golden Dawn letter assignments of the Emperor and Star. It affects trumps IV and XVII—not the Ace of Cups. The Cup suit remains Heh/Briah/Water. Confusing these two uses of *Heh*—the suit letter versus the Emperor’s revised path letter—is a category error.

[Primary text: Crowley, *The Book of Thoth*](https://otohungary.org/documents/publication/english/Book_of_Thoth.html)  
[Scanned edition](https://www.rahoorkhuit.net/library/crowley/equinox/volume3/vol_3_no_05.pdf)

# Notable modern redesigns

## Thorsons/Marseille restorations and facsimiles

Modern facsimiles tend to preserve the great architectural cup, presenting it without a pictorial divinatory scene. Meaning remains dependent on the reader’s method.

## Aquarian Tarot, David Palladini, 1970

Palladini retains the Waite-Smith Grail/fountain conception but translates it into Art Deco and psychedelic form. This helped normalize the Ace as spiritual and emotional abundance.

## Morgan-Greer Tarot, Bill Greer, 1979

A close-cropped, saturated Waite-Smith interpretation. The emotional immediacy of the overflowing vessel is emphasized.

## Motherpeace, Karen Vogel and Vicki Noble, 1981

The round format and feminist/goddess framework recast the cup as womb, body, intuition, and communal nurture. This is an influential modern spiritual construction, not a recovered medieval meaning.

## Tarot of the Cat People, Renaissance, queer, Afro-diasporic, feminist, and secular decks

Modern artists variously turn the cup into womb, ocean, heart, cauldron, ancestral vessel, cocktail glass, fountain, or an abstract emotional field. These designs demonstrate the semantic freedom produced by Waite-Smith’s scenic minors.

**Historical caution:** “new relationship,” “self-love,” “emotional breakthrough,” and “creative flow” are now conventional meanings, but their current therapeutic wording is mostly a late twentieth- and twenty-first-century development.

# The game

## Original function

**Documented fact:** tarot originated as a family of trick-taking games. The ordinary suit pack was augmented with permanent trumps and the Fool. The Ace of Cups remained a suit card.

The earliest tarot references cluster in northern Italy in the 1440s–1450s. The standard structure eventually stabilized as:

- 21 numbered trumps;
- one Fool;
- four suits of 14 cards;
- four courts per suit;
- ten numerals per suit.

[Metropolitan Museum history](https://www.metmuseum.org/de/perspectives/tarot-2)

## Rank

### Early or “original” Italian ordering

In most older traditions:

- Swords and Batons: King, Queen, Knight, Jack, 10 down to Ace.
- Cups and Coins: King, Queen, Knight, Jack, Ace, 2 upward to 10.

Thus the Ace of Cups is the highest pip in its suit, but fifth overall, below all four courts.

This reversed ranking distinguishes “round/red” suits from “long/black” suits. It survives conspicuously in Bolognese and some Piedmontese traditions.

### Modern French tarot

All suits rank uniformly:

King, Queen, Knight, Jack, 10, 9 … 2, Ace.

Here the Ace of Cups is the lowest card and ordinarily has no point value beyond its contribution to card-group counting.

[French Tarot Federation rules](https://www.letarot.net/en/rules.html)

### German/Austrian Tarock

Most modern Tarock packs use French suits and are shortened to 54 cards. Red suits retain Ace–4; black suits retain 10–7. The retained red Ace is therefore high among the numeral cards, reflecting the old opposed suit order. The card is normally a Hearts Ace, not iconographically an Ace of Cups.

## Counting value

In traditional tarot scoring, Kings and certain trumps are valuable counters. An Ace of Cups is usually an ordinary low-value card:

- Bolognese raw value: one, before pair deductions.
- French tarot: an ordinary *carte basse*, effectively half a point in standard grouped counting.
- It is not an *oudler* or honor.
- It cannot overtrump any tarot merely because it is an Ace.

## Minchiate

The Ace functions as a suit card under the same general round-suit logic. Minchiate’s additional trumps do not alter it into a special card.

## Fool

The Fool has no ordinary suit and commonly cannot win a trick; instead it may be excused and recovered according to regional rules. This has no direct effect on the Ace’s identity.

## Trump-order disputes

Dummett grouped the early Italian trump sequences into three families:

- **A:** Bologna/Florence
- **B:** Ferrara
- **C:** Milan

Differences include the relative position of Justice, Fortitude, virtues, ecclesiastical figures, and highest celestial cards. Milan ultimately favored the World as highest; Bologna and many other Italian games favored the Angel/Judgement.

None of these disputes includes the Ace of Cups. It stays outside the trump sequence.

# Chronology of attestation

## Before 1370: ancestors

- **Thirteenth–fourteenth century — documented objects:** Mamluk card fragments include the ancestral Latin suit system.
- **1370s — documented:** playing cards appear in European records, including Iberian *naipes* and Italian *naibi*.
- **Scholarly reconstruction:** European cup suits descend through Mediterranean contact from Islamic packs.

## c. 1420: Marziano da Tortona

Marziano described a special pack commissioned for Filippo Maria Visconti, painted by Michelino da Besozzo. It had four bird suits and sixteen gods as superior cards.

**Critical distinction:** it is a proto-trump game, not a standard tarot, and contains no Ace of Cups. It matters because it demonstrates the principle of a permanent superior suit before the conventional tarot sequence.

## 1440: Giusto Giusti

A Florentine diary records a specially made pack of *naibi a trionfi* given to Sigismondo Pandolfo Malatesta. This is presently a stronger candidate for the earliest documentary tarot reference than the once-standard 1442 Ferrara entry.

## 1442: Ferrara ledger

The Este court accounts record payment for *carte da trionfi*. This is secure evidence for the game and production at Ferrara, but the entry does not name the Ace of Cups individually.

## c. 1440–1480: surviving Milanese Aces

Cary–Yale and Visconti-Sforza specimens establish that a single elaborate cup belonged to early tarot suits.

## c. 1470–1500: *Sermo perutilis de ludo cum aliis*

The anonymous sermon condemns games and supplies an early ordered list of the twenty-one trumps and Fool.

**Absence finding:** its celebrated list names trumps, not individual pip cards. It does not supply a meaning for the Ace of Cups.

## c. 1480–1500: printed sheets

Rosenwald and Budapest material documents transition from luxury painting to reproducible print.

## 1491: Sola Busca

The dated engraving is the earliest complete 78-card tarot and the earliest complete scenic-pip program.

## c. 1490s–1520s: Boiardo and *tarocchi appropriati*

Boiardo designed a poetic 78-card moral game with nonstandard suits—Love, Hope, Jealousy, Fear. It demonstrates literary appropriation of ranks and suits but is not an attestation of the standard Ace of Cups meaning.

Courtly *tarocchi appropriati* assigned trumps to named people in verses. Again, this normally concerns trumps rather than pips.

## 1505 onward: the word *tarot/tarocchi*

A French form is documented at Avignon in 1505. Italian *tarocchi* becomes common in the early sixteenth century, replacing or supplementing *trionfi*.

## 1527/1546: Teofilo Folengo

Folengo’s *Chaos del Triperuno* incorporates tarot cards into a literary divinatory episode.

**Disputed significance:** it shows imaginative fortune-related use, but it is not a systematic cartomancy manual and does not document the modern meaning of the Ace of Cups.

## 1635/1637: Marolles

The Abbé de Marolles supplies the earliest known printed complete rules for French tarot. Seven especially important cards include Kings, highest and lowest trump, and Fool—not the Ace of Cups.

[Marolles rules reconstructed](https://www.pagat.com/tarot/marolles.html)

## c. 1650–1760: Marseille sequence

Noblet, Dodal, Madenié, and Conver establish the architectural chalice as the durable French form.

## 1781: Court de Gébelin and Comte de Mellet

Tarot is declared an Egyptian book. Their primary concern is the trumps and overall pack. They do not yet produce the Golden Dawn Ace-of-Water doctrine.

## 1783–1789: Etteilla

Etteilla publishes systematic tarot divination and then an occult pack. The Ace becomes **Table/Change**, card 49.

## 1854–1856: Éliphas Lévi

Lévi binds tarot to Hebrew letters and ceremonial Kabbalah, chiefly through the trumps.

## 1863: Paul Christian

Christian popularizes “arcana” and an Egyptian initiation narrative. His account is literary occult historiography, not an ancient source.

## 1888: Mathers

Mathers publishes English divinatory meanings for the cards. The Ace of Cups means gaiety/feasting/joy/pleasure; reversed, change and inconstancy.

## 1889: Papus and Wirth

Papus develops a Tetragrammaton-based system. Wirth issues only twenty-two trumps and therefore contributes no historical Ace design.

## 1890s: Golden Dawn *Book T*

The Ace becomes Kether in Briah and “Root of the Powers of Water.”

## 1909–1911: Waite-Smith and *Pictorial Key*

Smith makes the Golden Dawn description Eucharistic and pictorially memorable; Waite publishes its description and meanings.

## 1938–1943 / 1969: Thoth

Harris paints the card; the full deck is first commercially published in 1969 after limited earlier trials.

## Mid-twentieth century onward

Case/BOTA, Eden Gray, Rachel Pollack, Mary K. Greer, Jodorowsky/Costa, and innumerable deck artists move the Ace toward psychological categories: emotional openness, relationship, intuition, creative receptivity, and self-love.

## Decans and courts: why they do not apply

In *Book T*:

- Numbered pips 2–10 receive planetary decans and titles such as “Lord of Love.”
- Courts receive sub-elemental combinations and zodiacal spans.
- Aces are elemental roots in Kether.

Therefore assigning the Ace of Cups “Venus in Cancer,” “Moon in Pisces,” or another single planet/decan is a later deck-author’s innovation, not standard Golden Dawn doctrine.

# Divinatory meanings by author

Quotations are kept short; capitalization and punctuation differ by edition.

## Etteilla, 1780s

- Upright: **“Table”**
- Reversed: **“Changement”**

The primary sense is hospitality, feast, household table, or welcome; reversed, alteration or upheaval. Later translators sometimes render *table* as banquet or party.

## S. L. MacGregor Mathers, *The Tarot*, 1888

- Upright: **“Gaiety, Feasting, Joy, Pleasure.”**
- Reversed: **“Change, Novelty, Metamorphosis, Inconstancy.”**

These preserve Etteilla’s feast/change contrast while expanding it.

[Mathers scan](https://logoilibrary.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/07/Mathers.pdf)

## Golden Dawn *Book T*, c. 1890s

Book T does not provide a modern upright/reversed pair. It gives an elemental quality:

- fertility;
- productiveness;
- beauty;
- pleasure;
- happiness.

Ill-dignification, rather than physical reversal, modifies the power. A watery root overwhelmed by hostile surrounding cards may become emotional excess, sterility, instability, or corrupted pleasure, but those exact lists vary by later interpreter.

## A. E. Waite, *Pictorial Key*, 1910/1911

- Upright: **“House of the true heart, joy, content”**, followed by abode, nourishment, abundance, fertility, Holy Table, and felicity.
- Reversed: **“House of the false heart”**, mutation, instability, revolution.

Waite’s “house” preserves Etteilla’s domestic/table sense, while “Holy Table” converts it toward Eucharist and Grail.

## Aleister Crowley, *Book of Thoth*, 1944

Crowley gives no independent reversed meaning and generally rejects mechanical reversal. The card is primordial Water, feminine complement to Fire, life-fluid, conception, fertility, and the Holy Grail. Divination is modified by elemental dignity and neighboring cards.

## Paul Foster Case, *Oracle of the Tarot*

Case gives:

- fertility;
- productiveness;
- development and multiplication;
- happiness and pleasure;
- gratification and fruition of desires;
- cheerfulness, geniality, gaiety.

Keyword: **“Desire-force.”**

Case does not make a standard reversed list central to his treatment.

## Rachel Pollack, *Seventy-Eight Degrees of Wisdom*, 1980/1983

**Copyright-sensitive summary:** Pollack reads the Ace as an overflowing beginning in love, feeling, intuition, joy, and spiritual experience. Its abundance can become difficult if the person cannot receive or give it form. Reversal can suggest blocked feeling, emotional withholding, or a love that cannot yet flow.

**Historical status:** influential modern interpretation built on Waite-Smith imagery and depth psychology, not an early card meaning.

## Mary K. Greer

Across *Tarot for Your Self*, *The Complete Book of Tarot Reversals*, and her essays:

- upright: emotional opening, love, receptive imagination, blessing, fulfillment, spiritual nourishment;
- reversed: withholding, emotional flooding, emptiness, unexpressed affection, or a need to turn love inward.

Greer also documents Waite’s Eucharistic/Grail context rather than treating the Grail equation as medieval fact.

[Greer, “Waite’s Eucharistic Ace of Cups”](https://marykgreer.com/2017/12/10/eucharistic-ace-of-cups/)

# Esoteric and symbolic readings

## Elemental Water

- **First systematic tarot appearance:** Golden Dawn, late nineteenth century.
- **Source:** *Book T* and the Order’s four-world/Tetragrammaton system.
- **Spreaders:** Waite, Crowley, Case, Regardie, Wang, the Ciceros.
- **Evidence:** explicit order texts.
- **Status:** esoteric attribution, not Renaissance cardmaking doctrine.

The Ace is Water before differentiation: potential feeling, reflection, dissolution, receptivity, generation, memory, and imagination.

## Kabbalah

- Ace = Kether, Crown, undivided source.
- Cups = Briah, the Creative World.
- Cups = first Heh, the Mother component of YHVH.
- Combined formula = Kether of Briah, the crown/root of Water.

**Correction:** no path on the Tree of Life joins two sephiroth specifically as the Ace of Cups. Paths and Hebrew alphabet letters belong primarily to the twenty-two trumps. The Ace occupies a sephirah/world combination.

## Christian and Eucharistic reading

### Early imagery

Renaissance cups could resemble chalices, ciboria, reliquaries, fountains, and architectural vessels. This permits Christian associations, but resemblance alone does not prove a secret sacramental program.

### Waite-Smith

Here the Christian meaning is explicit:

- dove = Holy Spirit;
- host = Eucharistic body;
- cup and streams = sacramental blood/water;
- “Holy Table” = altar/Eucharistic table;
- Grail = vessel of spiritual life.

Waite’s separate Grail writings state that the Cup corresponds to spiritual life. This is the strongest textual basis for calling his Ace the Grail.

## Grail Castle reading of Marseille

The lidded cup resembles a castle or church.

- **Possible traditional association:** chalice, altar vessel, fountain of life.
- **Disputed claim:** the maker intentionally depicted the Grail Castle.
- **Evidence against certainty:** no Noblet, Dodal, or Conver commentary survives; similar monumental elaboration appears on non-esoteric aces and suit-signs.
- **Modern spreaders:** occult Marseille readers and online symbolic commentary.

## Alchemy

Water is associated with dissolution, the receptive vessel, *solutio*, lunar matter, and the alchemical bath. The cup can be read as the vessel in which transformation occurs.

**First clear tarot systematization:** nineteenth-century occultism and especially Golden Dawn-derived decks. A comprehensive fifteenth-century alchemical code for the Ace has not been documented.

Crowley intensifies the reading through blood, wine, lunar generation, Binah, and the union of complementary forces.

## Hermeticism

The Ace is a vessel receiving an influence from above. The hand/cloud or dove symbolizes descent from an undifferentiated source into form. This becomes a miniature “as above, so below” drama.

**Status:** a legitimate Hermetic interpretation of Golden Dawn, Waite, and Thoth imagery; not securely an explanation of the early Italian pip.

## Divine feminine, womb, and yoni

Crowley explicitly calls the Ace feminine and connects it with the yoni and Moon, complementing the solar/phallic Ace of Wands. Later feminist decks develop the vessel as womb, matrix, body, birth, and embodied intuition.

**Status:** explicit twentieth-century esoteric attribution; subsequently expanded as modern spirituality.

## Jungian reading

A Jungian reader may treat:

- cup as ego or psyche capable of containing affect;
- water as the unconscious;
- overflow as irruption of feeling or numinous experience;
- dove/host as a symbol of the Self;
- lilies as emergence of consciousness from unconscious water.

**Critical correction:** Jung did not leave a canonical interpretation of the Ace of Cups. “Jungian tarot” is a post-Jungian application associated with writers such as Sallie Nichols and later tarot psychologists. Nichols concentrated on the Marseille trumps, not a full pip-card commentary.

## Five streams and five senses

**Modern invention / disputed interpretation.** The common statement that five streams represent the five senses fits modern numerology but conflicts with Waite’s printed description of “four streams.” No statement by Smith has been found confirming the senses.

## Twenty-six drops and YHVH

The Hebrew numerical value of YHVH is 26. Some modern commentators count twenty-six droplets around the Waite-Smith cup and take this as intentional.

- **Observation:** many reproductions appear to show twenty-six yod-like drops.
- **Plausibility:** Waite knew Hebrew numerology.
- **Unresolved:** no design memorandum by Waite or Smith explicitly identifies the count. Print variation and ambiguous marks complicate exact counting.
- **Status:** plausible reconstruction, not documented intention.

# People and works

## Artists and cardmakers directly attached to the card

- **Michelino da Besozzo:** artist of Marziano’s lost proto-trump pack; no Ace of Cups survives.
- **Bonifacio Bembo and workshop:** traditionally associated with Visconti-Sforza cards; individual cards and later replacements complicate attribution.
- **Jean Noblet:** Paris cardmaker, c. 1650.
- **Jean Dodal:** Lyon cardmaker, early eighteenth century.
- **Pierre Madenié:** Dijon cardmaker, 1709 Type-II witness.
- **Nicolas Conver:** Marseille cardmaker, 1760/1761.
- **Jean-Baptiste Alliette/Etteilla:** occult pack designer and cartomancer.
- **Pamela Colman Smith:** drew the Waite-Smith Ace.
- **A. E. Waite:** designer/author of its program.
- **Paul Foster Case and Jessie Burns Parke:** central to the BOTA tarot’s doctrinal and artistic development.
- **Lady Frieda Harris:** painted the Thoth Ace.
- **Aleister Crowley:** supplied its magical/Thelemic program.
- **Philippe Camoin and Alejandro Jodorowsky:** modern Marseille reconstruction.
- **Marianne Costa:** co-author with Jodorowsky of its influential psychological-symbolic exposition.

## Calvino, Eliot, Yeats, Bergman, and Jodorowsky

### Italo Calvino, *The Castle of Crossed Destinies*

Calvino constructs narratives from tarot layouts, principally using the Visconti-Sforza and Marseille packs. The work is fundamental to tarot in modern literature.

**Absence finding:** the Ace of Cups does not have one universally agreed, isolated role equivalent to Eliot’s named cards. Its significance depends on the particular layout and editorial illustration.

### T. S. Eliot, *The Waste Land* (1922)

Eliot names “the drowned Phoenician Sailor,” “Belladonna,” “the man with three staves,” the Wheel, and “the one-eyed merchant,” plus a blank card.

**Absence finding:** the Ace of Cups is not named. Claims that Eliot secretly meant it lack textual support.

### W. B. Yeats

Yeats belonged to the Golden Dawn and worked within its elemental and symbolic environment. His Grail and vessel imagery makes comparison productive.

**Absence finding:** no securely traced Yeats passage was located in which he gives a card-specific interpretation of the Ace of Cups. General Golden Dawn affiliation should not be turned into card authorship.

### Ingmar Bergman

Bergman’s films repeatedly use medieval death, chess, religion, ritual, and existential imagery.

**Absence finding:** no reliable production record or direct quotation of the Ace of Cups in a Bergman film was found. Associative claims appear to be thematic comparison.

### Alejandro Jodorowsky

Jodorowsky’s tarot work informs his cinema’s ritual bodies, sacred objects, transformation, and initiatory structures. He also wrote directly about the Ace with Marianne Costa.

**Qualification:** identifying every chalice in *The Holy Mountain* or another film as a quotation of this specific card requires shot-level or authorial evidence. No comprehensive verified catalogue was found.

## Paintings and buildings

The Marseille Ace has often been compared with:

- Gothic reliquaries;
- ciboria and tabernacles;
- multi-towered church façades;
- baptismal or civic fountains;
- the fountain device of the Sforza.

These are iconographic analogies. No specific medieval building has been conclusively demonstrated as Noblet’s or Conver’s model.

# Controversies and disputes

## Egyptian origin

### Origin of the claim

Court de Gébelin’s 1781 *Le Monde primitif* declared tarot a surviving Egyptian book of wisdom. He wrote before Champollion deciphered hieroglyphs and supplied no Egyptian cards, inscriptions, or continuous provenance.

Etteilla accepted and elaborated the Egyptian story. Paul Christian, Papus, later occultists, and commercial “Egyptian tarot” decks amplified it.

### Historical assessment

The surviving evidence instead places:

- the four-suit ancestors in the Islamic world;
- European playing cards in the late fourteenth century;
- tarot’s trumped pack in fifteenth-century northern Italy;
- systematic occult tarot in late eighteenth-century France.

**Verdict:** legend as history; extremely influential as esoteric mythology.

[Dummett on tarot origins](https://www.nybooks.com/articles/1981/05/14/origins-of-tarot/)  
[Decker, Depaulis and Dummett bibliography](https://books.google.com/books?id=nVyDAAAAMAAJ)

## “Gypsy” or Romani transmission

The historical slur “Gypsy” arose from the mistaken European belief that Romani people came from Egypt. In the nineteenth century, writers including Jean-Alexandre Vaillant helped popularize the idea that Roma carried tarot into Europe.

No chain of evidence connects the invention of tarot to Romani communities. Linguistic and historical research places Romani origins in northern India; tarot is documented in Italy before evidence for a Romani tarot-transmission tradition.

**Verdict:** unsupported origin legend, though Romani and traveler communities have subsequently participated in European fortune-telling cultures.

## Cathar origin

Claims that Cathars encoded dualist theology in tarot have no supporting fifteenth-century cards, Cathar text, workshop document, or transmission chain. The major Cathar communities had already been suppressed before tarot appears in the record.

**Verdict:** modern speculative legend.

## Templar origin

The Templar theory similarly lacks cards or documents connecting the dissolved order with tarot. It usually depends on reading later symbols backward into the fourteenth century.

**Verdict:** modern legend.

## Ancient Kabbalistic origin

No surviving early Italian tarot card or rulebook assigns Hebrew letters, Tree-of-Life paths, or sephiroth to cards. The explicit letter-tarot synthesis is a product of eighteenth- and nineteenth-century French occultism, systematized by Lévi and successors.

**Verdict:** historically unsupported as an origin claim; documented and internally coherent as modern Western esotericism.

## Was the early Ace a church?

Its architectural lid can look like towers, a church, a reliquary, or a crown.

- **Fact:** it is an elaborate cup-shaped suit-sign.
- **Reconstruction:** courtly fountains, liturgical metalwork, and heraldic devices contributed to its form.
- **Disputed:** it deliberately encodes the Church.
- **Legend:** it depicts a specific hidden Grail temple.

## Ace of Coins versus Ace of Cups as maker’s card

The brief’s lead is verified with qualification:

- Aces often offered conspicuous open space and became convenient locations for makers’ names, fiscal stamps, and official marks.
- In several Italian traditions the **Ace of Coins**, not the Ace of Cups, became the standard fiscal-stamp bearer.
- Modern Sicilian packs added an otherwise extraneous Ace of Coins for this purpose.
- Bolognese examples preserve a blank central coin that formerly received the stamp.
- Marseille makers also placed names and production statements on conspicuous cards, especially the Two of Coins.

**Conclusion:** the maker/tax role does not imply that the Ace of Cups was the privileged fiscal card. Its enlargement is primarily ornamental and identificatory.

## “Sola Busca directly inspired the Waite Ace”

The Sola Busca influenced several Waite-Smith pip compositions. But the Golden Dawn description accounts much more closely for the Ace of Cups.

**Verdict:** possible ambient influence; unproved as the direct source of this card.

## Four streams or five?

- Smith’s image appears to show five effluents.
- Waite’s text says four.
- Modern interpreters often say five senses.
- Golden Dawn *Book T* describes a fountain and spray without making five senses its central explanation.

**Verdict:** genuine image/text discrepancy; the five-senses doctrine is later.

## M, W, or Hebrew Mem?

Possible readings include:

- M;
- inverted W;
- Mem, Hebrew Water;
- Mary;
- Waite;
- an inherited maker-like device.

Mathers described an M on an Italian cup before the Waite-Smith deck. Golden Dawn copies vary in whether the letter is upright, inverted, or replaced by Heh in the spray.

**Verdict:** unresolved. Assertions of certainty are unwarranted.

## Thoth publication date

Harris painted the cards from 1938 to 1943. Crowley’s book appeared in 1944. Limited monochrome or trial presentations preceded the first complete commercial color edition, generally dated 1969.

**Correction:** “created in 1969” is false; 1969 is the principal publication milestone.

## Wirth’s Ace

Commercial decks called “Oswald Wirth Tarot” sometimes contain 78 cards.

**Correction:** Wirth’s 1889 deck had only twenty-two trumps. The Ace in those expanded products is a later publisher/artist invention.

## Misprints becoming tradition

Examples relevant to this dossier include:

- inconsistent singular/plural “Power(s) of Water(s)” in Golden Dawn-derived editions;
- four streams in Waite’s prose versus five in Smith’s image;
- later recolorings of Marseille and Waite-Smith mistaken for original symbolic specifications;
- modern Wirth minors presented without prominently explaining that they are additions;
- reversal lists copied between Etteilla, Mathers, Waite, and Papus until their authorship becomes blurred.

# Open questions

1. **Which surviving tarot Ace is earliest?**  
   Cary–Yale is the strongest conventional answer, but workshop chronology among early Milanese cards remains partly reconstructed.

2. **What exact object inspired the architectural Marseille cup?**  
   Fountain, ciborium, reliquary, covered standing cup, heraldic device, and imagined building are all plausible contributors. No maker’s explanation survives.

3. **Did Noblet or Dodal intend a Grail reference?**  
   No direct evidence has been traced. The Grail reading becomes demonstrable with Waite and Crowley.

4. **What did Smith understand the M/W to mean?**  
   No surviving statement settles it.

5. **Were exactly twenty-six droplets deliberately specified?**  
   The YHVH interpretation is plausible but undocumented.

6. **Why does Waite say four streams when the image appears to have five?**  
   Editorial error, differing design stages, symbolic counting, and simple visual ambiguity remain possible.

7. **How much freedom did Smith have?**  
   Golden Dawn descriptions and Waite’s program constrained her, but the precise division of invention between Waite and Smith is incompletely documented.

8. **Can Sola Busca esoteric readings be recovered historically?**  
   Its imagery is certainly learned, but modern alchemical syntheses lack a surviving contemporary key.

9. **What was the earliest specific divinatory meaning of the Ace?**  
   Etteilla’s *Table/Changement* is the earliest securely printed systematic pair identified here. Earlier Italian fortune-telling fragments may eventually alter that chronology, but none examined supplies the modern “new love” formula securely.

10. **Is “Water = emotion” ancient?**  
    Elemental water had long associations with generation, temperament, mutability, purification, and the passions. Its standardized card-reading equation with personal emotional life is chiefly occult-modern and psychological-modern.
