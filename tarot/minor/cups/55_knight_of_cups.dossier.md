# Knight of Cups: Research Dossier

## Method and evidentiary labels

This dossier distinguishes:

- **[Documented fact]** directly supported by a surviving card, archival record, rulebook, first edition, manuscript, or institutional catalogue.
- **[Scholarly reconstruction]** an inference argued from physical, textual, or comparative evidence.
- **[Tradition]** a historically transmitted practice whose original rationale may be uncertain.
- **[Esoteric attribution]** a deliberately constructed occult correspondence.
- **[Disputed claim]** an identification or genealogy accepted by some writers but challenged by others.
- **[Legend]** a story presented historically or popularly without supporting evidence.
- **[Modern invention]** a recent interpretive or artistic development, not an inherited medieval meaning.
- **[Not traced]** a requested association for which no sufficiently early or reliable source was found.

“Knight of Cups” presents a special nomenclatural problem. Ordinary Italian and French tarot places a mounted Knight between Queen and Knave. The Golden Dawn called its mounted, fiery court figure a **King**, and its enthroned or chariot-borne airy figure a **Prince**. Crowley retained the mounted figure but renamed it **Knight**. Waite returned to the ordinary sequence King–Queen–Knight–Page. Consequently:

> **Waite’s Knight of Cups corresponds structurally and iconographically to the Golden Dawn King of Cups and Crowley’s Knight of Cups—not to the Golden Dawn Prince of Cups.**

Confusing Waite’s Knight with the Golden Dawn Prince produces the widespread but historically incorrect assignment **Air of Water** and the wrong zodiacal sector.

### Yod, Heh, and the common mistake

**[Esoteric attribution, Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn, late nineteenth century]**

The Golden Dawn assigned the four court grades to the four letters of the divine name:

| Court principle | Letter | Elemental role |
|---|---:|---|
| Mounted King/Knight | Yod | Fire |
| Queen | first Heh | Water |
| Prince | Vau | Air |
| Princess | final Heh | Earth |

Cups as a suit are Water and therefore belong collectively to **Heh**. The mounted King/Knight is **Yod within Heh**, hence Fire of Water. Calling the card simply “Heh” suppresses its rank; calling it “Air of Water” substitutes the Prince/Vau attribution.

## Iconography deck by deck

## Mamluk antecedents, approximately fourteenth–fifteenth centuries

**[Documented fact]** Surviving Mamluk cards have four suits conventionally translated as cups, coins, swords, and polo-sticks. Each suit contained ten numeral cards and three inscriptional courts: *malik* (king), *nāʾib malik* (deputy king), and *thānī nāʾib* (second deputy). They did not depict mounted knights; court rank was conveyed by titles and ornamental devices rather than human portraits. [International Playing-Card Society](https://www.i-p-c-s.org/wp/an-introduction/).

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** The Latin suits—*coppe, denari, spade, bastoni*—almost certainly developed from this general Islamic four-suit family. The European Knight is not a literal picture copied from a surviving Mamluk “Knight of Cups.” It is a European personification of one of the inherited intermediate court ranks, reshaped within aristocratic European society. The Queen was also a European addition or substitution; therefore no one-to-one equation such as “Mamluk second deputy = Knight” is demonstrable in every early European pack.

**[Disputed detail]** The precise route—through Spain, Sicily, Venice, or several Mediterranean channels—and whether the Topkapı cards represent one pack or mixed packs remain subjects of technical card-history debate. Dummett and Kamal Abu-Deeb notably corrected L. A. Mayer’s treatment of the surviving cards as a single pack.

## Marziano da Tortona and Michelino da Besozzo, approximately 1414–1425

**[Documented fact]** Marziano’s surviving Latin description concerns a special pack made for Filippo Maria Visconti. Its four suits were birds, and its sixteen superior cards represented classical gods. The painted cards themselves are lost.

**[Important negative finding]** There is no identifiable Knight of Cups in Marziano’s text. The pack is relevant as an early experiment with permanent superior cards—a possible structural predecessor to *trionfi*—but it did not yet possess the standard tarot suit of Cups as documented in later decks.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** Historians call it a “proto-tarot” because its special deity cards defeated the ordinary suits. It is not evidence that the later Knight of Cups had a classical, Hermetic, or astrological identity.

## Cary-Yale / Visconti di Modrone, Milan, approximately 1441–1447

**[Documented fact]** The Beinecke Library pack preserves an unusually expanded court organization. Besides King, Queen, Knight, and male Knave, surviving suits indicate female counterparts—female knaves and mounted female figures—within the original conception. A male Knight of Cups survives. The card is an aristocratic mounted courtier carrying the suit vessel against a costly decorated ground.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** The additional female courts may relate to a dynastic marriage, a court festival, or a deliberately balanced male/female hierarchy. None of these explanations has been proved from a commission document.

**[Disputed identification]** Gertrude Moakley and Stuart Kaplan belonged to a twentieth-century tradition of reading the luxury cards as portraits or quasi-portraits of specific Visconti and Sforza family members. Heraldry securely identifies the patronal milieu, but it does not securely name the Knight of Cups. No contemporary label, inscription, account, or portrait comparison proves that he is a particular prince. “A Visconti/Sforza family member” should therefore be reported as a conjecture, not an identification.

## Brambilla / Brera-Brambilla, Milan, before 1447 or mid-fifteenth century

**[Documented fact]** The Pinacoteca di Brera group preserves the Knight and Knave of Cups among its seven surviving court cards. Its commission is associated with Filippo Maria Visconti; institutional and scholarly dates vary, but a date before the duke’s death in 1447 has strong support. The courts occupy tooled gold grounds, while many numeral cards use silver.

The Knight of Cups is a mounted noble or soldier carrying a large cup. The vessel is an unambiguous suit badge; there is no inscription calling it a Grail and no divinatory text.

**[Disputed attribution]** The cards have traditionally been attributed to Bonifacio Bembo or his workshop. Different scholars have reassigned parts of the early Visconti groups among Bembo, Francesco Zavattari, and related Lombard workshops.

## Pierpont Morgan–Bergamo / Visconti-Sforza, Milan, approximately 1450–1480

**[Documented fact]** Morgan MS M.630.17 depicts a youthful armored rider in profile. His horse proceeds at a controlled pace; the rider raises a vessel in his right hand. The background is punch-tooled gold. His clothing and horse furniture participate in the courtly, heraldic vocabulary of the pack. [Morgan Knight of Cups](https://www.themorgan.org/collection/tarot-cards/the-knight-of-cups).

The suit’s courts carry large cups; their clothing uses the radiant-sun device. The entire surviving group combines Visconti and Sforza emblems. The Morgan dates the cards approximately 1450–1480 and notes that three trumps are later replacements.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** The rider belongs visually to the world of processions, tournaments, military retinues, and princely display. The controlled horse and raised cup communicate rank and suit more securely than any later psychological idea of romance.

**[Disputed identification]** No individual family member can be securely named. The deck may have commemorated Francesco Sforza and Bianca Maria Visconti or Francesco’s accession in 1450, but even the exact commission is reconstructed from heraldry and style. Moakley’s and Kaplan’s personal identifications are historically important proposals, not demonstrated portrait identifications.

## Ferrarese and Este cards, late fifteenth century

**[Documented fact]** Ferrara is central to the written history of *carte da trionfi*, and several surviving late-fifteenth-century cards are conventionally called Este or Charles VI cards.

**[Not traced]** A securely proven, surviving Este Knight of Cups with a continuous museum catalogue identity was not found in the consulted records. Consequently, one should not fabricate a card-by-card Ferrarese iconography from reconstructions sold as complete decks.

Where Ferrarese court cards survive, they belong to the same general northern Italian aristocratic vocabulary: mounted rank plus a conspicuous suit emblem. No contemporary Ferrarese source assigns romance, Grail quest, Water, or Pisces to this court.

## Sola Busca, Venice or Ferrara, 1491

**[Documented fact]** The Sola Busca is the earliest substantially complete engraved tarot and the earliest surviving historic pack to give the numeral suits independent figural scenes. Its cards are named after classical or pseudo-classical figures, often soldiers.

**[Clarification]** Its importance to Pamela Colman Smith lies primarily in the dramatic scenes of certain pip cards—especially the Three of Swords and Ten of Wands—not in a demonstrated copying of its Knight of Cups.

**[Not traced]** No convincing direct visual derivation of Smith’s Knight of Cups from the Sola Busca court was found. The shared mounted-warrior format is older and much broader than that deck.

## “Mantegna Tarocchi,” approximately 1460s

**[Documented fact]** The so-called Mantegna Tarocchi are didactic engravings organized into five groups of ten, representing social estates, muses, liberal arts, cosmic principles, and virtues.

**[Category correction]** They are not a tarot pack and have neither suits nor a Knight of Cups. Their relevance is general—Renaissance classificatory imagery—not card-specific.

## Rosenwald and Budapest sheets, late fifteenth or early sixteenth century

**[Documented fact]** These uncut or fragmentary printed sheets are crucial evidence for inexpensive early tarot production, in contrast with luxury painted Visconti cards.

**[Caution]** Published reconstructions differ over sheet arrangement, completeness, and the identification of individual fragments. A mounted cup-court belongs to the standard printed vocabulary where the suit courts survive, but the accessible records consulted did not support a sufficiently secure, card-specific comparison to justify detailed claims about reins, direction, costume, or vessel shape.

**[Not traced]** No contemporary interpretation accompanies either sheet.

## Milanese Castello Sforzesco fragment, approximately 1480–1510

A woodcut fragment catalogued as **Cavallo di coppe** survives in Milan’s Bertarelli collection. It retains yellow, violet, green, and red color. Michael Dummett observed that its rider’s pose is close to the later Tarot de Marseille Knight of Cups. This is important evidence that the Marseille-type mounted composition has late-fifteenth-century northern Italian antecedents. [Fondazione Giorgio Cini catalogue](https://archivi.cini.it/storiaarte/detail/28686/carta-gioco-28686.html).

## Tarot de Marseille lineage

### Jean Noblet, Paris, approximately 1650

**[Documented fact]** Noblet’s *Cavalier de Coupes* is an armored mounted man in profile. He holds a cup forward as the badge of his suit. The horse is shown in lateral movement, the design is compressed into the woodblock frame, and the palette is stencil-limited.

There is no landscape narrative comparable to Waite-Smith. The card names a social and gaming rank, not a “romantic proposal.”

### Jean Dodal, Lyon, approximately 1701–1715

Dodal retains the same essential syntax: mounted courtier, cup held visibly, profile horse, ornamental costume, and title band. Facial, harness, coloring, and carving details differ, but the function remains rank-plus-suit.

### Nicolas Conver, Marseille, 1760

Conver belongs to the later Marseille Type II lineage. The knight is again a mounted bearer of the cup. Changes are graphic regularizations—contour, face, costume, horse, cup, and palette—not a documented change in meaning.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** Type I and Type II are modern scholarly classifications, associated especially with Thierry Depaulis. Type I includes Noblet/Dodal-like features; Type II is represented by Madenié and later Conver. The nineteenth-century phrase *Tarot de Marseille* should not be projected backward as the printers’ own seventeenth-century system name.

### Grimaud / Paul Marteau, 1930s

Marteau standardized a Conver-derived pattern for twentieth-century mass circulation under the title *Ancien Tarot de Marseille*. The Knight continues to carry the open cup while riding.

**[Esoteric interpretation, twentieth century]** Marteau reads the cup’s asymmetrical opening as showing that its contents or “science” can no longer reverse their quality and interprets the Cavalier as the sensitive and affective human element capable of generous enthusiasm and devotion. This is an interpretation of the inherited graphic form, not evidence of the form’s seventeenth-century intention.

### Camoin–Jodorowsky, 1997

**[Documented modern redesign]** Philippe Camoin and Alejandro Jodorowsky produced a reconstructed/restored Marseille deck drawing on old Marseille blocks, Conver-derived imagery, and editorial restoration.

**[Disputed claim]** Its presentation as recovery of an original, complete, sacred iconographic code goes beyond what surviving printing history can establish. Color, line, and small details include modern decisions. In *The Way of Tarot* (French 2004), Jodorowsky and Marianne Costa treat court cards as stages of energy and movement. Their Cavalier carries the attained experience of Cups toward another level or suit. This is a modern organic/numerological reading, not an attested ancien-régime doctrine.

## Tarot de Besançon, eighteenth century onward

**[Documented fact]** Besançon-pattern tarots usually replace the Papess and Pope with **Juno and Jupiter**, probably emerging around Strasbourg in the early eighteenth century. This substitution affects trumps II and V, not the Knight of Cups. The ordinary *Cavalier de Coupes* remains a mounted suit court. [French overview](https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tarot_de_Marseille).

The replacement was likely motivated by confessional or market sensitivities, but the exact cause is not documented. It is therefore a **scholarly reconstruction**, not a known act of censorship.

## Minchiate, Florence and Tuscany, sixteenth–nineteenth centuries

**[Documented fact]** Minchiate expands the trump series to forty-one special cards but retains ordinary suits with King, Queen, Knight, and Knave.

In many surviving Minchiate designs the Knights are hybrid beings—centaurs or sphinx-like mounted bodies. The Knight of Cups can appear as a bearded male torso growing from an equine body and raising a cup. [Minchiate overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minchiate).

**Meaning of the change:** no surviving early rule or explanation proves an occult meaning. The hybrid form differentiates the knight rank visually and participates in the learned grotesque/classical imagery of Florentine print culture. Calling it alchemical or zodiacal is a later interpretation.

## Bolognese tarocchino

The Bolognese pack was shortened to sixty-two cards by removing numeral cards 2–5 from each suit. It retains **Rè, Regina, Cavallo, Fante**.

The cups court is therefore still **Cavallo di Coppe**. Historic and modern Bolognese patterns often use compact, later double-ended court designs rather than narrative scenes.

A manuscript rule text states:

> “il Rè precede la Regina, questa al Cauallo, e questo al Fante”

—the King precedes the Queen, she the Knight, and he the Knave. [Pedini manuscript transcription](https://www.tarocchinobolognese.it/pages/lorenzo_cuppi/Manoscritto_Pedini.php).

## Sicilian tarot

**[Documented fact]** Sicilian tarot preserves an Italian-suited court hierarchy and an unusual regional game. The Knight/Cavalier remains a suit court, though modern Sicilian packs have their own compact costume conventions.

**[Caution]** The Sicilian exception chiefly concerns numeral-card ranking and its distinctive trumps, not a special esoteric identity for the Knight of Cups. No source consulted documented a unique traditional Sicilian divinatory meaning for this card.

## Grand Etteilla, Paris, approximately 1789

**[Documented fact]** Card **38** is titled *Le Chevalier de Coupe*. Its borders print the orientations and meanings:

- upright: **ARRIVÉE**
- reversed: **RENONCÉE** or, in some impressions/transcriptions, a spelling close to *renommée* has been reported.

The examined digitized copy supports *Arrivée* securely; the lower word is typographically less consistent across reproductions and should be checked against a specified impression. [Digitized *Grand Etteilla*](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/2/2f/Le_grand_Etteilla%2C_ou%2C_l%27art_de_tirer_les_cartes_..._Le_tout_recueilli_..._%28IA_b29321220%29.pdf).

The image recasts the traditional rider within Etteilla’s numbered, explicitly divinatory “Egyptian” pack. Its greatest innovation is not the horseman but the fixed number and printed upright/reversed oracle.

## Oswald Wirth, 1889

Wirth’s original tarot consisted of the twenty-two trumps. It has **no Knight of Cups**.

Later “Wirth” seventy-eight-card decks are expansions by publishers and later artists. Their Knight of Cups cannot automatically be attributed to Wirth. Papus reproduced or discussed ordinary minor-card structure, but Wirth’s 1889 contribution was trump-centered.

## Golden Dawn manuscripts / Book T, approximately 1888–1900

**[Esoteric attribution]** The relevant card is called the **King of Cups**, not Knight.

Book T describes a winged warrior mounted on a white horse, carrying a cup and lotus. He belongs to the fiery/Yod court grade and is:

> “Lord of the Waves and the Waters; King of the Hosts of the Sea.”

His realm extends from 20° Aquarius to 20° Pisces. He is active, rapid, and volatile because Fire acts within Water.

The Golden Dawn added:

- elemental hierarchy;
- YHVH letter;
- zodiacal sector;
- three decans;
- color scales;
- magical weapons and sigils;
- personality delineation.

None of this appears on the fifteenth-century cards.

### Robert Wang reconstruction, 1978

Wang, working with Israel Regardie, reconstructed a Golden Dawn deck from published and manuscript instructions. Its **King of Cups** is the mounted Fire-of-Water figure. Wang’s deck is not a surviving Victorian Golden Dawn deck; it is a twentieth-century realization of textual specifications.

### Chic and Sandra Tabatha Cicero reconstruction, 1991; later editions

The Ciceros’ *New Golden Dawn Ritual Tarot*, later issued as *Golden Dawn Magical Tarot*, likewise visualizes the order’s instructions, colors, weapons, Hebrew and astrological signs. Differences from Wang reflect different judgments about manuscript color scales and ritual symbolism. [Deck description](https://www.learntarot.com/gmdesc.htm).

## Waite-Smith, London, 1909

Pamela Colman Smith depicts a youthful knight in silver-gray armor riding a white horse slowly beside a stream or river. His visor is open. Wing motifs appear on his helmet and boots. He holds a golden cup level before him. Distant hills and open ground create a journey or arrival scene.

Waite’s description is:

> “Graceful, but not warlike; riding quietly, wearing a winged helmet, referring to those higher graces of the imagination which sometimes characterize this card.”

He adds that the figure is “a dreamer,” but that the vision may be sensuous rather than spiritual. See *The Pictorial Key to the Tarot*, Part II, “The Lesser Arcana,” Knight of Cups; Part III for divinatory meanings. [Digitized edition](https://www.mr-kaplan.com/Library/Waite/The%20Pictorial%20Key%20to%20the%20Tarot.pdf).

### Sources of Smith’s image

**[Documented visual inheritance]**

- the old mounted *Cavalier de Coupes*;
- the Golden Dawn’s mounted King of Cups;
- the order’s winged and elemental symbolism;
- contemporary medievalist illustration and stage design.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** The deliberate stillness of the horse visually supports “arrival,” “approach,” imagination, and offering. The river makes the Water association explicit in a way Marseille cards do not.

**[Disputed/unsupported]** The cup is frequently called the Holy Grail. Neither the card nor Waite’s description names it as such. A Grail association is plausible within Victorian medievalism but is not textually established for this particular image.

## Paul Foster Case / B.O.T.A., approximately 1920s onward

Case’s deck, drawn by Jessie Burns Parke, follows Waite-Smith closely in its court imagery while restoring Golden Dawn-style instructional correspondences in accompanying teachings. The deck was designed for students to color.

**[Documented fact]** Case criticized Waite for blinds and omissions, but his own minor-card and court practice remains deeply dependent on Waite and the Golden Dawn. B.O.T.A. publications have varied over time, and proprietary lessons are not all publicly verifiable.

**[Caution]** Statements attributed to “Case” online often combine *An Introduction to the Study of the Tarot* (1920), later B.O.T.A. lessons, and modern deck booklets. Only the first is securely public and edition-stable.

## Crowley–Harris Thoth, painted 1938–1943; first full publication 1969

Crowley renames the Golden Dawn mounted King as **Knight**, making the title agree with older tarot imagery while preserving the Golden Dawn elemental role.

Frieda Harris depicts an armored, winged or radiantly crested rider hurtling over water on a white horse. The composition is diagonal and explosive rather than Waite’s quiet horizontal procession. In the right hand he bears a cup from which a crab emerges; in the other hand is a lotus. Peacock-like iridescence and watery curves surround him.

Crowley writes:

> “This card represents the fiery part of Water, the swift passionate attack of rain and springs.”

And:

> “In his right hand he bears a cup from which issues a crab, the cardinal sign of Water, for aggressiveness.”

[*The Book of Thoth*, 1944 edition scan](https://www.rahoorkhuit.net/library/crowley/equinox/volume3/vol_3_no_05.pdf).

The crab is Cancerian in quality—cardinal Water and aggressive emergence—but the card’s actual zodiacal rulership is **20° Aquarius–20° Pisces**, not Cancer. The distinction is between a symbolic emblem and zodiacal jurisdiction.

## Notable modern redesigns

### Rachel Pollack’s Shining Woman / Shining Tribe Tarot, 1980s–1990s

Pollack replaces Cups with **Rivers**, and the conventional courts with culturally broader figures. This deliberately detaches the card from European hereditary rank while retaining emotional, fluid, and relational functions. [Morgan exhibition on Pollack](https://www.themorgan.org/exhibitions/online/tarot/23).

### Jodorowsky–Camoin, 1997

A restored Marseille rider rather than a Waite-style scenic redesign. Its novelty lies mainly in restoration claims, color, and interpretive system.

### Gender-neutral and role-renamed decks

Modern decks commonly replace Knight with Seeker, Child, Son, Explorer, Warrior, Artist, or elemental titles. These are **modern inventions** intended to address hierarchy, gender, colonial imagery, or psychological accessibility. They should not be retroactively described as medieval meanings.

## The game

## Original function

**[Documented fact]** Tarot was created as a trick-taking game. Its ordinary suits were inherited from playing cards; the trumps were an added permanent sequence. Fifteenth-century luxury cards were not originally labelled “Major” and “Minor Arcana,” terms arising from occult tarot.

The Knight of Cups was simply:

1. a Cups-suit card;
2. a court or honor;
3. ranked between Queen and Knave;
4. unable to beat a trump.

No documented fifteenth-century rule assigns it romance, Water, a Grail, Pisces, or a messenger.

## Rank in traditional Italian tarot

In most old Italian games the round suits, Cups and Coins, run in reverse numeral order:

**King > Queen > Knight > Knave > Ace > 2 > 3 … > 10**

In long suits, Swords and Batons:

**King > Queen > Knight > Knave > 10 > 9 … > Ace**

This opposite ordering is an old feature shared by many tarot traditions.

## Bolognese value

In Ottocento:

- King = 5 nominal points
- Queen = 4
- **Knight = 3**
- Knave = 2
- others = 1 before the game’s pairing deductions

The historical rule says “Ogni Regina quatro; ogni Cauallo trè; ogni Fante due.” [Pedini manuscript](https://www.tarocchinobolognese.it/pages/lorenzo_cuppi/Manoscritto_Pedini.php).

## Modern French tarot

The French-suited game replaces Cups with Hearts, so a modern tournament pack technically has a **Cavalier de Cœur**, not a Cavalier de Coupes.

Rank:

**Roi > Dame > Cavalier > Valet > 10 … 1**

Counting value:

- King or *bout*: 4½ points
- Queen: 3½
- **Knight: 2½**
- Jack: 1½
- other card: ½

[French tarot rules](https://www.pagat.com/tarot/frtarot.html).

## Minchiate

The Knight retains ordinary court status. Its centaur form changes recognition, not game function. Trump-order disputes do not alter its suit rank.

## Tarock

Central European Tarock normally uses French-suited packs. The extra court is usually a **Caval/Kavall**, again ranking below Queen and above Jack. Cups as an iconographic suit generally disappear in favor of Hearts.

## Trump-order disputes: relevant context, but not this card

Historians reconstruct three principal early Italian trump-order families, conventionally called A, B, and C. They differ in such matters as:

- the relative positions of Justice, Strength, and Temperance;
- placement of the virtues against papal and imperial figures;
- ordering of celestial trumps;
- whether some lowest dignities are equal;
- treatment of the Fool.

The Knight of Cups is outside all three trump orders.

The Fool was commonly unnumbered and served as an “excuse”: it could be played without following suit and was normally retained rather than winning the trick. Its exact compensation rules varied. Modern occult numbering as 0 or sometimes 22 does not apply to the Knight.

Similarly, Papess→Juno, Pope→Jupiter, Tower→*Maison Dieu*, and the placement of Justice/Strength concern trumps. They do not rename or renumber the Knight of Cups.

## Chronology of attestation

### Before tarot

- **Approximately fourteenth century — Mamluk world:** Cups and three ranked courts are documented, but no mounted Knight of Cups.
- **1370s — Europe:** ordinary playing cards are documented in several cities. Latin-suit and court development follows quickly.

### Formation of *trionfi*

- **Approximately 1414–1425 — Milan:** Marziano describes a special trump-like pack for Filippo Maria Visconti. No Cups suit or Knight of Cups is named in the surviving text.
- **16 September 1440 — Florence:** Giusto Giusti records *naibi a trionfi* given to Sigismondo Pandolfo Malatesta. This is now earlier than the once-standard 1442 Ferrara “first mention.”
- **February and July 1442 — Ferrara:** Este accounts record painted and purchased *carte da trionfi*. One entry refers to a pack for Ercole and Sigismondo d’Este. [Le Tarot/Andrea Vitali association](https://letarot.it/Le-origini-italiane-del-Ludus-Triumphorum_pag_pg439_ita.aspx).
- **1440s–1450s — Milan:** Cary-Yale, Brambilla, and Morgan–Bergamo cards give the earliest surviving Knight/Cavallo of Cups images.
- **1449 — Jacopo Antonio Marcello:** a letter accompanying a special pack to Isabelle of Lorraine describes the game and recalls Marziano’s earlier pack.

### Lists and literary evidence

- **Approximately 1470–1500 — *Sermo perutilis de ludo cum aliis*:** a Dominican sermon supplies an early list of tarot trumps while condemning play. It does not individually name the ordinary Knight of Cups; its importance is evidence for the standard trump series.
- **Late fifteenth century — Boiardo:** Matteo Maria Boiardo devised poetic verses for a moralized card pack. It demonstrates literary appropriation of card structure but is not a source for the later standard Knight-of-Cups meaning.
- **Approximately 1500 onward:** *tarocchi appropriati* assigned trumps to people in courtly verse. Again, this normally concerned trumps, not the Cups knight.
- **Early sixteenth century:** the word *tarocchi* replaces or competes with *trionfi*.
- **1527 — Teofilo Folengo, *Caos del Triperuno*:** tarot cards appear in a literary/divinatory episode and five sonnets. It is suggestive evidence for imaginative consultation of cards but not a Knight-of-Cups manual.
- **Sixteenth–seventeenth centuries:** printed playing patterns spread through France, Switzerland, and central Europe; *tarot*, *Tarock*, and related forms become regional game names.
- **1637 — Marolles rules:** among the earliest printed French tarot rules, naming *Roy, Royne, Cheualier,* and *Faon/Fante*.
- **Approximately 1650 — Noblet:** earliest substantially surviving Parisian Marseille-type *Cavalier de Coupes*.
- **Approximately 1701–1715 — Dodal:** Lyon.

### The three decans

Under Book T the mounted King/Knight of Cups rules:

| Zodiacal decan | Minor card | Golden Dawn title |
|---|---|---|
| 20°–30° Aquarius | Seven of Swords | Lord of Unstable Effort |
| 0°–10° Pisces | Eight of Cups | Lord of Abandoned Success |
| 10°–20° Pisces | Nine of Cups | Lord of Material Happiness |

This produces a deliberately mixed character: Aquarian strategy or instability, Piscean withdrawal, and Piscean satisfaction.

### Tree of Life

**[Esoteric attribution]** The Knight has no single path comparable to a trump’s Hebrew-letter path. Courts are more accurately located through:

- the four worlds;
- YHVH;
- the four elements;
- the ten Sephiroth as expressed through a suit;
- zodiacal quadrants.

Any modern chart assigning “Knight of Cups = one Hebrew letter/path” is a simplification or a different system.

### Tzaddi/Heh controversy

Crowley’s statement “Tzaddi is not the Star” led him to associate **Tzaddi with the Emperor** and **Heh with the Star**, reversing the Golden Dawn’s Emperor=Heh and Star=Tzaddi linkage while retaining the visible trump order.

This controversy has **no direct bearing on the Knight of Cups**. The card’s Heh is the elemental letter for Water in YHVH, not the trump-letter Heh under dispute. Conflating the two is another common correspondence error.

## Divinatory meanings by author

Quotations are kept brief and tied to editions. Historical spelling and punctuation vary between scans.

## Etteilla, approximately 1789

Card 38 prints:

- **Upright:** “ARRIVÉE” — arrival.
- **Reversed:** the lower word is variously transcribed; some later Etteilla manuals give renunciation, refusal, or a related social qualification.

**[Documented fact]** *Arrivée* is physically printed on surviving cards.

**[Caution]** Longer modern meanings—lover, gift-bringer, invitation—are expansions by later Etteilla readers unless tied to a specified edition.

## S. L. MacGregor Mathers, 1888

In *The Tarot: Its Occult Signification, Use in Fortune-Telling, and Method of Play*, the Knight of Cups receives:

- **Upright:** “Arrival, approach—sometimes that of a messenger; advances, proposition, demeanour, invitation, incitement.”
- **Reversed:** “Trickery, artifice, subtlety, swindling, duplicity, fraud.”

[Mathers scan](https://www.howardnema.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/12/the-tarot-1888-by-macgregor-mathers-1.pdf).

Mathers is an important bridge between French cartomancy and Waite: Waite’s list closely follows this vocabulary.

## A. E. Waite, 1910

Waite gives:

- **Upright:** “Arrival, approach—sometimes that of a messenger; advances, proposition, demeanour, invitation, incitement.”
- **Reversed:** “Trickery, artifice, subtlety, swindling, duplicity, fraud.”

His image-description adds the higher imaginative possibilities of the “graceful” and “not warlike” rider.

**[Documented relationship]** The near-verbatim agreement with Mathers shows textual dependence or common dependence on the same cartomantic list. The scenic artwork turns the abstract word “arrival” into a visible approach.

## Crowley, 1944

Crowley does not organize the courts into simple upright/reversed lists. He gives character potentials:

Positive expression:

- graceful;
- sensitive;
- amiable;
- innocent or pure;
- capable of enthusiasm.

Negative or ill-dignified expression:

- sensual;
- idle;
- untruthful;
- prone to depression;
- unable to sustain effort.

His central formula is “the fiery part of Water.” Crowley generally preferred elemental dignity and card interaction to mechanically reversing cards.

## Paul Foster Case

Case broadly preserves:

- arrival or approach;
- a proposal or invitation;
- imagination and emotional idealism;
- the danger of seduction, unreliability, or deception when adversely placed.

**[Source caution]** A definitive Case “upright/reversed quotation” could not be verified from the public 1920 text alone. Many online lists silently import Waite or later B.O.T.A. lessons. These should not be quoted as Case without edition and lesson number.

## Rachel Pollack

In *Seventy-Eight Degrees of Wisdom*, Pollack develops the knight as:

- romantic and poetic;
- devoted to an ideal;
- following imagination and feeling;
- potentially unable to distinguish dream from reality;
- a person who offers love yet may love the condition of being in love.

Her reading helped spread “romantic proposal,” “quest for beauty,” and “idealistic dreamer” beyond Waite’s terse arrival/proposition language.

**[Copyright/source note]** No open, pagination-stable edition supporting a longer verbatim quotation was found. The wording above is a close summary, not presented as Pollack’s exact text.

## Mary K. Greer

Greer treats courts flexibly—as people, roles, parts of oneself, styles of action, or situations. For the Knight of Cups this supports:

- pursuing what one loves;
- acting through imagination, affection, or receptivity;
- asking what ideal is worthy of devotion;
- testing promises against action.

In reversal practice, Greer’s general method includes:

- opposite;
- blocked;
- delayed;
- excessive;
- unconscious/private expression;
- refusal to enact the court’s role.

Thus a reversed Knight of Cups may be emotional avoidance, false seduction, blocked creativity, excessive idealization, or a private imaginative life rather than one fixed antonym. [Greer on court cards](https://marykgreer.com/2016/03/23/using-the-court-cards/).

## Esoteric and symbolic readings

## Elemental and Hermetic

**First securely developed card-specific appearance:** Golden Dawn Book T, late nineteenth century.

Fire of Water can mean:

- rain driven by storm;
- boiling, steaming, or surging water;
- emotional initiative;
- passion acting through receptivity;
- enthusiasm that forms quickly and disperses;
- active imagination;
- seduction, volatility, or emotional overstatement.

Crowley was the most influential public disseminator through *The Book of Thoth*.

## Kabbalistic

The card is **Yod of Heh**:

- Yod: seed, force, paternal or fiery impulse;
- Heh: matrix, receptivity, Water, the Cup;
- synthesis: initiating force within the receptive element.

The card is not assigned an independent Hebrew letter or one of the twenty-two paths. Its Kabbalah is tetragrammatic and elemental.

## Astrological

The verified Golden Dawn span is:

> **20° Aquarius through 20° Pisces**

It is not:

- all of Pisces;
- Cancer;
- Scorpio;
- 20° Libra–20° Scorpio, which belongs to a different Cups court in some tables;
- Air of Water, which belongs to the Golden Dawn Prince/Thoth Prince.

The crab in Crowley’s cup symbolizes cardinal Water/aggressiveness but does not change the card’s Aquarius–Pisces rulership.

## Alchemical

**[Modern occult development]** Fire in Water naturally invited comparisons with:

- heating and distillation;
- steam;
- solution activated by heat;
- volatile tincture;
- the passionate spirit moving in the watery matrix.

No fifteenth-century alchemical gloss of the Cavallo di Coppe is known. The reading derives from Golden Dawn elemental combination and later occult commentary, not from the early card makers.

## Christian

The cup may evoke:

- Eucharistic chalice;
- Christ’s cup at the Last Supper;
- sacrificial offering;
- charity;
- baptismal or living water.

These are culturally plausible associations in Christian Europe. But no early card labels the Knight’s vessel Eucharistic.

## Grail

**[Tradition/modern esoteric reading]** A mounted knight bearing or seeking a cup easily becomes a Grail knight in post-Waite interpretation.

Possible parallels include:

- Perceval approaching the Grail;
- Galahad’s purity;
- quest for a spiritual ideal;
- the Fisher King’s wounded emotional realm;
- bearing a sacred vessel or message.

**[Historical finding]** The Grail is not named by Bembo, Noblet, Dodal, Conver, Waite, or the surviving Book T text for this card. The specific “Knight of Cups = Grail knight” formula is therefore a later mythopoetic reading.

Jessie Weston’s *From Ritual to Romance* (1920) popularized a broader correspondence between Grail hallows and tarot suits. T. S. Eliot acknowledged Weston in his notes to *The Waste Land*. This helped create a twentieth-century environment in which Cups could be read as Grail symbolism, but it does not prove that medieval card makers intended it.

## Jungian

**[Modern invention/synthesis]** Jung did not publish a card-by-card Knight of Cups system.

Later Jungian tarot interpreters variously construe it as:

- differentiated or developing feeling;
- anima-mediated imagination in a male figure;
- animus carrying a feeling-message;
- *puer aeternus*, the eternal youth;
- projection of an ideal lover;
- the ego’s quest for the Self through desire;
- emotional inflation when fantasy is mistaken for relationship.

Sallie Nichols’s *Jung and Tarot* (1980) primarily treats the trumps. Court-card Jungian schemes were extended by later writers, including Cooke, Sharpe, Pollack, Greer, and numerous deck creators. Myers-Briggs equations such as “Knight of Cups = ENFJ” are late twentieth-century inventions, not Jung’s own attribution.

## People and works

## Artists, printers, and occultists

- **Bonifacio Bembo and workshop:** traditional attribution for Visconti court cards.
- **Francesco Zavattari:** alternative attribution proposed for parts of the Morgan group.
- **Jean Noblet:** Paris cardmaker, approximately 1650.
- **Jean Dodal:** Lyon cardmaker, early eighteenth century.
- **Nicolas Conver:** Marseille cardmaker, 1760.
- **Jean-Baptiste Alliette/Etteilla:** first systematic professional tarot occultist; numbered the card 38.
- **Éliphas Lévi:** joined tarot to Kabbalah and ceremonial magic but left no important Knight-of-Cups-specific discussion.
- **Papus:** systematized tetragrammatic and “Bohemian” tarot theories.
- **S. L. MacGregor Mathers:** published the divinatory list and led Golden Dawn tarot development.
- **Pamela Colman Smith:** created the most influential scenic Knight.
- **A. E. Waite:** supplied the program and published description.
- **Paul Foster Case and Jessie Burns Parke:** B.O.T.A. adaptation.
- **Aleister Crowley and Frieda Harris:** transformed the figure into the explosive Thoth Knight.
- **Robert Wang and Israel Regardie:** 1978 Golden Dawn reconstruction.
- **Chic and Sandra Tabatha Cicero:** later ritual reconstruction.
- **Rachel Pollack and Mary K. Greer:** major psychological and reader-centered interpreters.
- **Alejandro Jodorowsky, Philippe Camoin, Marianne Costa:** modern Marseille restoration and organic/numerological reading.

## Italo Calvino

*The Castle of Crossed Destinies* uses tarot images to generate interlocking stories. One part uses Visconti imagery; another uses a Marseille pack.

**[Documented literary method]** Characters tell stories by arranging cards.

**[Not traced]** No uniquely canonical episode centered on the Knight of Cups was verified in the editions consulted. It is safer to describe Calvino as attached to tarot combinatorics generally, not to claim a specific Knight-of-Cups identification without a chapter and card sequence.

## T. S. Eliot

*The Waste Land* names invented and transformed tarot figures including the drowned Phoenician Sailor, Belladonna, the man with three staves, the one-eyed merchant, the Wheel, and the Hanged Man.

**[Important negative finding]** Eliot does not name the Knight of Cups. A Cups/Grail atmosphere can be constructed through Jessie Weston, water, Fisher King, and quest motifs, but calling Eliot’s figure a Knight of Cups is interpretive, not textual.

## W. B. Yeats

Yeats wrote about occultism, the Golden Dawn, Celtic treasures, and symbolic cycles. His *Stories of Red Hanrahan* compares the four Irish treasures with tarot-suit objects.

**[Not traced]** No primary Yeats passage securely making the Knight of Cups a named symbol was found.

## Jodorowsky

Jodorowsky’s films repeatedly use initiatory, alchemical, and tarot-like imagery, and he co-created a Marseille reconstruction.

**[Not traced]** A building, costume, or shot in his films directly documented by Jodorowsky as a quotation of the Knight of Cups was not found. Visual resemblance alone is insufficient.

## Ingmar Bergman

*The Seventh Seal* uses a medieval knight and a game with Death.

**[Correction]** The game is chess, not tarot. There is no cup-bearing rider and no documentary basis for treating Antonius Block as a Knight-of-Cups quotation. Any such identification is a modern associative reading.

## Paintings and buildings

No premodern painting or building was found that can be proved to “quote” the Knight of Cups as a tarot card. Equestrian portraits, cupbearers, Grail processions, and chivalric frescoes constitute its visual environment but are not necessarily quotations.

Modern murals, tattoos, album covers, comics, and installations regularly reproduce the Waite-Smith image. Without an artist’s statement or unmistakable formal duplication, these should be described as resemblance rather than documented quotation.

## Controversies and disputes

## Egyptian origin

Court de Gébelin announced in 1781 that tarot was a surviving Egyptian book. His opening conceit called it an ancient Egyptian work escaped from the burning of libraries. The accompanying essay attributed to the Comte de Mellet connected the twenty-two trumps with Hebrew letters and called the tarot the Book of Thoth.

**Status:** **[Legend/esoteric attribution]**

Evidence against:

- tarot is documented in northern Italy in 1440–1442;
- its courts reflect late-medieval European hierarchy;
- its trumps include unmistakably medieval Christian and civic figures;
- Gébelin supplied no Egyptian manuscript or archaeological chain;
- his Egyptian etymologies preceded decipherment of hieroglyphs and are linguistically false.

Dummett summarized the occult historical tradition as a constructed false history while distinguishing its cultural influence from factual origin. [Dummett, “Origins of Tarot”](https://www.nybooks.com/articles/1981/05/14/origins-of-tarot/).

## Romani or “Gypsy” transmission

Papus’s 1889 preface states:

> “Le jeu de Tarots, transmis par les Bohémiens de génération en génération, est le livre primitif de l’antique initiation.”

“The Tarot pack, transmitted by the Bohemians from generation to generation, is the primitive book of ancient initiation.” [Papus first-edition scan](https://www.arbredor.com/ebooks/TarotBohemiens.pdf).

**Status:** **[Legend]**

Court de Gébelin, J.-A. Vaillant, Papus, and later popular occultists spread versions of the claim. It exploited the mistaken European belief that Roma came from Egypt.

No documentary evidence connects Roma to tarot’s invention in northern Italy or to the development of the Knight of Cups. Roma did practice fortune-telling in later Europe, but later use is not evidence of invention.

## Cathar tarot

Claims that tarot was:

- a Cathar catechism;
- a secret anti-papal book;
- preserved at Montségur;
- encoded to evade the Inquisition,

are **[modern legends/disputed claims]**. They emerge from nineteenth- and twentieth-century Grail, Cathar, and occult revival literature, not medieval tarot records.

The Knight of Cups becomes a Cathar Grail guardian only by combining separate traditions.

## Templar tarot

The Knights Templar were suppressed in the early fourteenth century, before tarot’s documented appearance. Later writers connected Templars, Grail guardianship, Freemasonry, and tarot.

**Status:** **[Legend]**

No Templar deck, rule, manuscript, or image genealogy leads to the Cavallo di Coppe.

## Grail-hallows theory

Jessie Weston and later esoteric writers compared the four tarot suits with the Grail procession’s cup, sword, lance, and dish/stone.

**Status:** **[Comparative mythic theory, disputed as history]**

The resemblance is real at the level of object sets, but no medieval document says tarot suits were derived from Grail hallows. Cups, swords, coins, and sticks already have a much stronger comparative relationship with Islamic playing-card suits.

## Portrait identifications

The Visconti court cards are often named as specific dukes, duchesses, or children.

**Status:** **[Disputed]**

Heraldry proves patronage and dynastic context. It does not automatically prove portrait identity. Moakley and Kaplan remain historically important for proposing family readings, but museum catalogues increasingly phrase patron and artist identifications cautiously.

## “Waite’s Knight is Air of Water”

**Status:** **[Misattribution that became common tradition]**

It arises by matching the ordinary word Knight to the Golden Dawn/Thoth **Prince**, which is Air. But Waite’s mounted Knight occupies the old and Golden Dawn mounted court position:

- Golden Dawn King = Fire/Yod;
- Crowley Knight = Fire/Yod;
- Waite Knight = same mounted position.

Air of Water properly belongs to:

- Golden Dawn Prince of Cups;
- Crowley Prince of Cups;
- structurally, Waite’s seated King of Cups more nearly occupies this displaced court slot.

Modern authors may intentionally use Knight = Air; that is a new court convention, not Book T’s.

## Cancer versus Aquarius–Pisces

Crowley’s crab leads some readers to call the Knight of Cups Cancer.

**Status:** **[Iconographic inference, incorrect as Book T rulership]**

The crab signifies cardinal Water or aggressive emergence. The stated sector remains 20° Aquarius–20° Pisces.

## Etteilla’s reversed word

Reproductions disagree in transcription of the lower word on card 38.

**Status:** **[Textual/printing uncertainty]**

“Arrivée” upright is secure. The reversed term should always be cited from an identified printing rather than normalized from a modern list. Recut blocks, degraded impressions, spelling variation, and modern redrawings may have turned an uncertain word into a fixed later tradition.

## Tarot de Marseille “restoration”

Grimaud/Marteau and Camoin–Jodorowsky both present forms of restoration.

**Status:** **[Documented redesign with disputed reconstruction claims]**

Neither is a transparent facsimile of a single pristine medieval original. Each combines historical evidence with choices about color, missing lines, symmetry, and legibility.

## Open questions

1. **Which surviving Knight of Cups is absolutely earliest?**  
   The Cary-Yale, Brambilla, and Morgan groups overlap chronologically, and dating continues to depend on heraldry, workshop attribution, material analysis, and reconstructed commissions.

2. **Who painted each Milanese group?**  
   Bembo remains traditional, but Zavattari and multiple-workshop hypotheses complicate a single-author story.

3. **Does any Knight portray a named Sforza or Visconti?**  
   No identification reaches documentary certainty.

4. **How exactly did Mamluk courts become King, Knight, and Knave?**  
   Structural influence is strong; exact rank-by-rank conversion is not documented.

5. **What is the secure reading of the reversed legend on every early Grand Etteilla impression?**  
   Comparison of high-resolution exemplars and recut editions is needed.

6. **Did Smith intend the cup as the Grail?**  
   No surviving Waite description or Smith statement located here says so.

7. **How much of Smith’s Knight came from unpublished Golden Dawn sketches?**  
   Book T supplies the mounted, winged, cup-bearing ruler, but the precise division of invention between Waite, Smith, and manuscript models remains partly reconstructive.

8. **When did “romantic suitor” become dominant?**  
   It grows naturally from Etteilla’s arrival, Mathers/Waite’s approach and proposition, Smith’s graceful rider, and later romantic/Grail readings. A full genealogy would require comparing nineteenth- and twentieth-century fortune-telling manuals edition by edition.

9. **When was the phrase “Grail Knight” first printed specifically for this card?**  
   It was not traced to Waite, Crowley, Case, or the historic Marseille literature consulted.

10. **Can a card be both Fire and Water without contradiction?**  
    Within Golden Dawn doctrine this is intentional: rank supplies Fire/Yod, suit supplies Water/Heh. Outside that system the formula has no historical necessity.

## Conclusions

The reconstructable history has four distinct stages:

1. **Playing-card inheritance:** an intermediate Cups court developed from the broader Mamluk–European card family.
2. **Renaissance personification:** fifteenth-century Milanese artists depicted that court as an aristocratic mounted cup-bearer or soldier.
3. **Printed continuity:** Italian and Marseille traditions preserved the rider-with-cup formula for centuries as a game card.
4. **Occult transformation:** Etteilla gave it a number and oracle; the Golden Dawn made it Fire of Water and 20° Aquarius–20° Pisces; Smith turned “arrival” into a quiet visual narrative; Crowley and Harris transformed the rider into an elemental storm.

The cup was historically a suit sign before it was Water, the psyche, love, Eucharist, or Grail. Those later meanings are culturally powerful, but they entered at different dates and through identifiable writers. The most consequential technical correction is that the Golden Dawn’s mounted equivalent is its **King of Cups**, not its Prince: this makes the Knight **Fire of Water, Yod of Heh**, rather than Air of Water.
