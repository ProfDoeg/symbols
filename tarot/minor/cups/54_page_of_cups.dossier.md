# Page of Cups: Research Dossier

## Method and evidentiary labels

This dossier distinguishes:

- **[Documented fact]** Directly supported by a surviving card, archival record, published rule, or primary text.
- **[Scholarly reconstruction]** A conclusion argued from material, documentary, or comparative evidence by historians.
- **[Tradition]** A durable convention whose historical origin may be only partly recoverable.
- **[Esoteric attribution]** A correspondence deliberately assigned by an occult author or order.
- **[Disputed claim]** A claim with competing scholarly interpretations.
- **[Legend]** A historical narrative contradicted by the available chronology or unsupported by contemporary evidence.
- **[Modern invention]** A recent interpretation or redesign that should not be projected backward.

“Page of Cups” is a court card, not a trump. Consequently, much of the requested trump material—Hebrew-letter sequences, numbered trump orders, the Tzaddi/Heh controversy, Papess/Juno substitutions, and the Fool’s numbering—does not directly attach to this card. It is included only where necessary to prevent systems from being conflated.

## Basic identification

| Category | Identification |
|---|---|

| French | **Valet de Coupe** or **Valet de Coupes** |
| Spanish | **Sota de Copas** |
| German | **Kelchbube**, **Bube der Kelche**; occult decks may retain English “Page” or use **Prinzessin der Kelche** for Golden Dawn/Thoth systems |

| Ordinary tarot rank | Lowest of four courts: Page/Valet below Knight, Queen, King |
| Card number | **None historically.** Modern catalogues or deck sequences may assign an ordinal—often 53 in Waite’s book arrangement—but that is not a face value or traditional card number |
| Golden Dawn element | **Earth of Water** |
| Golden Dawn letter formula | Final **Heh (ה)** of Yod-Heh-Vav-Heh as the Princess/daughter principle—not an individual trump letter |

| Earliest surviving securely identified example | The Visconti-Sforza/Colleoni-Baglioni **Page of Cups**, Milan, approximately 1450, or within the Morgan’s broader ca. 1450–1480 dating; Morgan Library MS M.630, no. 18 |
| Probable remote structural ancestor | The Mamluk cup court ranks, especially the *nāʾib* and subordinate deputy—not a pictorial “Page of Cups” in the European sense |

The Morgan identifies its surviving card as “The Page of Cups,” attributed to Bonifacio Bembo and dated ca. 1450–1480. Its catalogue explains that the original pack contained ten numeral cards and Page, Knight, Queen, and King in each suit. Thirty-five cards are now at the Morgan, twenty-six at the Accademia Carrara, and thirteen in the Colleoni collection. [Morgan object record](https://www.themorgan.org/collection/tarot-cards/the-page-of-cups); [Morgan manuscript catalogue](https://www.themorgan.org/manuscript/158525).

### What the card is not

- **[Documented fact]** It is not one of the twenty-one trumps and therefore does not participate in the historical trump sequence.
- **[Documented fact]** It has no Roman numeral in the Visconti-Sforza, Marseille, Golden Dawn, Waite-Smith, or Thoth systems.
- **[Esoteric attribution]** Golden Dawn manuscripts sometimes call it a “Princess,” a “throne,” and the final Heh. Those are initiatory classifications, not evidence that fifteenth-century players understood it that way.
- **[Modern convention]** Online lists sometimes number it 53, 47, 11, or 14 according to deck order. These are catalogue positions, not stable identities.

## The names

### Italian

**Fante di Coppe** literally means the foot-soldier, servant, attendant, or youthful court retainer of Cups. *Fante* developed as the normal Italian name for the lowest court card. *Coppe* means cups or goblets.

**[Documented fact]** Fifteenth-century account books usually describe whole packs, suits, or “figures,” not individual court cards by names equivalent to “Page of Cups.” The surviving picture therefore precedes any presently located individual written attestation of the formula *Fante di Coppe*.

**[Tradition]** Modern Italian tarot packs use *Fante di Coppe*. Regional games may vary the court vocabulary but retain *fante* as the lowest court.

### French

**Valet de Coupe(s)** is the Marseille title. *Valet* means a male servant, attendant, or young retainer and later became the ordinary French Jack.

**[Documented fact]** Seventeenth- and eighteenth-century Marseille-pattern cards print variations such as **VALET DE COUPE**. Singular and plural fluctuate with maker and transcription.

### English

Early English occult authors used both **Knave** and **Page**. Mathers and Waite helped establish “Page” in English-language esoteric tarot. Modern English gaming descriptions often say Jack, while divinatory decks overwhelmingly say Page.

**[Tradition]** “Page” is a romanticizing translation of *valet/fante*, not proof that the depicted person held the narrowly defined medieval office of page.

### Spanish

**Sota de Copas** is the standard Spanish-suited cognate. The *sota* is the lowest court, below *caballo* and *rey*.

**[Important distinction]** Ordinary Spanish packs normally have three courts—Sota, Caballo, Rey—whereas a 78-card Italian/French tarot pack has four, adding a Queen. Thus *Sota de Copas* is linguistically and visually comparable but is not always a tarot card.

### German

**Kelchbube** or **Bube der Kelche** translates “Jack/Knave of Cups.” In Crowley translations, **Prinzessin der Kelche** translates Princess of Cups.

German-suited Tarock decks usually employ Hearts, Bells, Acorns, and Leaves rather than the Italian cups; consequently there may be no literal *Kelchbube* in a gaming Tarock pack.

### Golden Dawn and Thoth renaming

**[Esoteric attribution, late nineteenth century]** The Golden Dawn renamed the ordinary court sequence:

- King → Knight
- Queen → Queen
- Knight → Prince
- Page/Knave → Princess

Crowley retained this scheme in the Thoth deck. It is therefore misleading simply to equate the Thoth “Knight” with the Marseille Knight: the whole hierarchy has shifted.

## Iconography deck by deck

## Mamluk antecedents, fourteenth to early sixteenth century

The nearly complete Topkapı Mamluk pack has suits of coins, cups, swords, and polo sticks. Each suit has ten numeral cards and three inscribed courts: King, Deputy King, and Second Deputy.

**[Documented fact]** The court cards use ornament and calligraphy rather than human portraits. The Cup courts do not depict a youthful person holding a cup.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** The European *fante/valet* probably occupies a structural position descended from one of the Mamluk deputy ranks, especially the subordinate deputy. The word *nāʾib* is also associated with Italian *naibi* and Spanish *naipes*. The exact one-to-one evolution from Second Deputy to European Page is not directly documented.

**[Correction]** Calling the Mamluk card itself “the Page of Cups” is anachronistic. It is an ancestor of the rank-and-suit structure, not the same named or pictured card. See the [International Playing-Card Society overview](https://i-p-c-s.org/faq/history_2.php) and [Mamluk card survey](https://www.wopc.co.uk/egypt/mamluk-playing-cards).

## Visconti di Modrone/Cary-Yale, Milan, approximately 1440s

Also called the Cary-Yale deck. Sixty-seven cards survive at Yale’s Beinecke Library.

**[Documented fact]** This exceptional pack includes female Pages and female Knights in addition to male counterparts. That gives it six courts per suit in its surviving/reconstructed organization, rather than the later standard four.

**[Significance]** The extra female courts may reflect a special courtly commission, dynastic marriage imagery, or a unique experiment. They do not establish a general early rule that the Page of Cups was female.

**[Disputed]** Dating, original deck size, and exact occasion remain debated. A connection with the Visconti-Sforza marriage culture is reasonable, but assigning a particular figure to a named family member is speculative. Yale’s conservation account stresses the female Page/Knight peculiarity and continuing scholarly uncertainty. [Yale Library account](https://library.yale.edu/news/cards-library-partners-work-together-solve-mysteries-rare-tarot-deck).

## Brambilla deck, Milan, approximately 1440s

The Brambilla cards are preserved principally at the Pinacoteca di Brera.

**[Documented fact]** The deck belongs to the earliest Milanese luxury-card group and is generally associated with the workshop of Bonifacio Bembo.

**[Open point]** A securely catalogued surviving Brambilla Page of Cups was not found in the accessible Brera material consulted here. Descriptions that casually reconstruct every court should not be mistaken for proof that the individual card survives.

## Visconti-Sforza/Colleoni-Baglioni, Milan, approximately 1450

The Morgan Page of Cups shows a youthful male courtier standing against a tooled gold background. He wears sumptuous fitted clothing and hose, turns partly to the side, and presents or carries a large golden cup.

**[Documented fact]** Unlike Waite’s Page, there is no fish, seashore, or narrative incident. The cup is a suit emblem and courtly object.

**[Documented fact]** Visconti and Sforza devices elsewhere in the deck identify a dynastic commission. The Morgan cautiously says it was made for “some member” of the family, perhaps Francesco Sforza after becoming Duke in 1450.

**[Disputed attribution]** The main cards have traditionally been given to Bonifacio Bembo. Francesco Zavattari has also been proposed. Six replacement trumps are assigned to Antonio Cicognara around 1480, but the Page of Cups is not one of those replacement trumps.

### Is it a named Visconti or Sforza family member?

**[Tradition/disputed]** Gertrude Moakley and later Stuart Kaplan discussed the court figures as reflecting members or ideals of the Visconti-Sforza court.

**Finding:** No inscription names the Page of Cups. Heraldry establishes patronage more securely than portrait identity. A specific identification—such as a particular Sforza child or young courtier—cannot presently be treated as documented. The figure is better described as an idealized Milanese court youth unless a source explicitly marks a proposed identification as conjectural.

## Este and Ferrarese cards, later fifteenth century

Surviving Ferrarese cards display a different, often more animated court culture. The Este court’s account books document painted *trionfi* packs in 1442.

**[Documented fact]** The ledger names the four suits—cups, swords, coins, and batons—and “all the figures,” but it does not individually describe a Page of Cups.

**[Open point]** Fragmentary Este groups do not preserve a universally agreed, individually identified Page of Cups that can support a continuous visual genealogy.

## Mantegna “Tarocchi,” approximately 1460s–1470s

**[Documented fact]** The so-called Mantegna Tarocchi are fifty educational engravings arranged in five series of ten. They are neither a tarot pack nor the work of Mantegna.

**Relevance:** None directly. They contain social estates, virtues, liberal arts, cosmic principles, and celestial spheres, but no suit of Cups and no Page of Cups. Their inclusion in tarot histories arises from the later misnomer and broad iconographic comparison.

## Sola Busca, 1491; painted approximately 1490s

**[Documented fact]** The Sola Busca is the earliest surviving substantially complete tarot whose pip cards have full figurative scenes. It is crucial to the history of illustrated minor cards and probably influenced Pamela Colman Smith’s treatment of several Waite-Smith pips.

**[Qualification]** Its courts and trumps have invented or classical-sounding names and military-antiquarian imagery. Its suit corresponding to Cups does not supply a straightforward Marseille-style “Page contemplating a cup,” and the Page-of-Cups fish cannot presently be traced to it.

**[Disputed]** General Sola Busca influence on Smith is strong, particularly through photographs in the British Museum, but a direct visual derivation for the Page of Cups has not been demonstrated.

## Rosenwald and Budapest sheets, late fifteenth/early sixteenth century

These woodcut sheets are invaluable for reconstructing inexpensive popular tarot patterns.

**[Documented fact]** They mainly clarify early trump iconography and pack production.

**Finding:** They do not provide a clear, secure, card-by-card Page of Cups lineage comparable with the Morgan card. Claims based on reconstructed cutting or incomplete sheet identification should be marked tentative.

## Minchiate, Florence, sixteenth century onward

Minchiate expanded the trump series to ninety-seven cards but retained four Italian suits and their courts.

**[Documented fact]** The cup Page remains a suit court; it is not one of the additional zodiacal, elemental, or virtue trumps.

**Game significance:** Its ordinary court rank and scoring depend on the particular Minchiate rules, but it remains the lowest court beneath Knight, Queen, and King.

**[Correction]** Minchiate’s explicit elemental trumps do not make its Page of Cups “Earth of Water.” That is a much later Golden Dawn formula.

## Bolognese tarot

Traditional Bolognese packs shortened the pip structure and used distinctive trump numbering and names.

**[Documented fact]** The lowest court of Cups persists as a *fante*. Its function is that of an ordinary suit court, not an occult Princess.

**[Qualification]** Bolognese cartomantic lists exist from the early modern period, but evidence for a stable, card-specific Page-of-Cups meaning before Etteilla is sparse and localized.

## Sicilian tarot

Tarocco Siciliano has a distinctive shortened pack, regional suit imagery, renamed trumps, and altered ranking conventions.

**[Documented fact]** It is not a direct witness to the Waite or Golden Dawn Page. Its lowest Cup court belongs to a regional gaming system.

**Finding:** No evidence located makes the Sicilian Cup Page a primary source for the fish, Grail, or Earth-of-Water interpretations.

## Jean Noblet, Paris, approximately 1650

The Noblet is the earliest substantially surviving French deck generally placed in the Tarot de Marseille lineage.

The **Valet de Coupe** is a standing, youthful male retainer in fashionable dress, carrying a conspicuously oversized cup. The image is emblematic rather than narrative.

**Change from Milan:** Gold-ground court luxury becomes bold woodcut outline and restricted stencil color. The cup remains an attribute held by the court figure.

## Jean Dodal, Lyon, approximately 1701–1715

Dodal retains the youthful standing Valet and enlarged cup. The posture and costume are simplified into the graphic conventions of Lyon cardmaking.

**[Tradition]** Later interpreters read the large cup as receptivity, offering, feeling, or a message. Those meanings are not printed on the original playing card.

## Nicolas Conver, Marseille: original pattern 1760; surviving witnesses complicated

The Conver pattern shows the Valet moving or turning while holding a tall open cup; in common impressions he also holds or gestures toward its lid. Floral forms appear around or in his hair.

**[Documented fact]** “Conver 1760” normally names the design or original production date. Some digitized BnF exemplars were printed later, including nineteenth-century impressions. Dating the physical scan and dating the pattern are not always the same thing.

**[Later tradition]** Paul Marteau interpreted the figure’s movement, open cup, and four-petalled flowers as announcing or transmitting a beneficent psychic or spiritual influx. That is a twentieth-century reading, not an eighteenth-century caption.

## Besançon pattern, eighteenth–nineteenth centuries

Besançon tarot chiefly differs from Marseille in replacing controversial Catholic trumps: the Papess and Pope become **Juno** and **Jupiter**.

**Relevance to this card:** The Valet of Cups ordinarily remains a Valet of Cups. Juno/Jupiter are regional trump substitutions and do not rename or renumber this court.

## Grimaud/Paul Marteau, twentieth century

The Grimaud *Ancien Tarot de Marseille* regularized Conver-derived imagery and coloring. Marteau’s influential commentary gave every detail an occult or moral function.

**[Tradition]** Here the Valet becomes a messenger or transmitter of emotional, psychic, or spiritual content. This helped move the Marseille court from neutral gaming figure to psychological emblem.

## Camoin–Jodorowsky, 1997

Philippe Camoin and Alejandro Jodorowsky marketed their deck as a restoration assembled from surviving Marseille evidence.

The Valet retains the large cup, lid, floral hair ornament, and active stance. Jodorowsky and Marianne Costa read Pages as beginners and as unrealized or exploratory potential; Cups concern emotional life and love.

**[Disputed claim]** The deck’s claim to recover a single hidden, primordial “original Tarot” goes beyond what the heterogeneous historical witnesses can prove. It is more accurately a modern synthetic reconstruction based on selected Marseille conventions.

## Etteilla and the Grand Etteilla, 1780s–1789

Etteilla reorganized tarot into an explicitly divinatory and purportedly Egyptian system.

**[Documented fact]** His deck differs radically from Marseille in numbering, ordering, captions, and interpretive inscriptions.

**Important complication:** Etteilla’s court meanings vary between his early books, the Grand Etteilla deck families, later “Book of Thoth” editions, and translations. It is unsafe to transfer a Marseille Valet directly into a modern Etteilla table without identifying the edition.

**[Esoteric attribution]** Court cards become human types and divinatory personae rather than simply game pieces. Etteilla also institutionalized separate upright and reversed meanings.

## Oswald Wirth, 1889

Wirth’s original twenty-two-card deck illustrated only the trumps.

**Finding:** There is no original 1889 Wirth Page of Cups. Any “Wirth Page of Cups” belongs to later publishers who expanded or paired his trump designs with a full deck.

## Golden Dawn manuscript designs, approximately 1888–1900

The Golden Dawn’s *Book T* recast the lowest Cup court as the **Princess of Cups**, “Princess of the Waters” and “Lotus of the Palace of the Floods.”

The design instructions place a winged or robed young woman amid water symbolism. She holds a cup from which a tortoise emerges; a swan or related water bird, lotus, and aquatic forms occur in reconstructions.

**[Esoteric attribution]**

- Rank element: Earth
- Suit element: Water
- Compound: **Earth of Water**
- Formula: final Heh
- Function: **Throne of the Ace of Cups**
- Spatial domain: the Cup quadrant around the north celestial pole, associated with the celestial regions of Libra, Scorpio, and Sagittarius

**Critical correction:** Unlike Knights, Queens, and Princes, Princesses do not rule a simple thirty-degree zodiacal span made from three consecutive decans. They govern quadrants associated with the four cardinal directions/celestial fields and gather the relevant decanal forces. Modern charts that give the Princess of Cups a neat “20° Libra–20° Scorpio” span have confused her with the Prince or another court.

### Wang and Cicero reconstructions

Robert Wang’s *Golden Dawn Tarot* and Chic and Sandra Tabatha Cicero’s *Golden Dawn Magical Tarot* are twentieth-century visual realizations of textual and manuscript instructions.

**[Modern reconstruction]** They are evidence for how modern Golden Dawn practitioners visualize *Book T*, not surviving nineteenth-century cards personally approved in every detail by Mathers.

## Waite-Smith, 1909; *Pictorial Key*, 1910

Pamela Colman Smith depicts a youthful figure standing at the seashore. The Page wears a blue floral tunic, elaborate hat and scarf, and holds a cup. A fish unexpectedly rises from the cup and looks at the Page.

Waite’s description is explicit:

> “A fair, pleasing, somewhat effeminate page … contemplates a fish rising from a cup.”

He explains the incident as “the pictures of the mind taking form.” [Waite, *Pictorial Key*, Page of Cups](https://sacred-texts.com/book/the-pictorial-key-to-the-tarot/read/page-of-cups).

### What changed

- Marseille’s neutral standing Valet becomes an event: Page and fish encounter one another.
- The sea makes the Cups/Water association explicit.
- The fish makes an inner image visible, matching Waite’s account of mental imagery taking form.
- The card remains a Page, not a Golden Dawn Princess; Waite suppresses much of the overt *Book T* nomenclature.

### Sources of Smith’s design

**[Documented fact]** Smith had access to Golden Dawn concepts through Waite and to early decks and reproductions available in London.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** Her illustrated pips as a whole owe something to the Sola Busca photographs in the British Museum.

**[Open question]** No primary sketch, instruction sheet, or older card has been located that conclusively identifies the source of the fish emerging from this particular cup. It may be Smith’s imaginative literalization of Water, psychic contents, or Waite’s “pictures of the mind.” Claims that it specifically represents the Christian fish, Jonah, a Grail fish, or the Fisher King are later symbolic readings unless further documentary evidence is produced.

## Paul Foster Case and BOTA, twentieth century

Case and Jessie Burns Parke produced a deck closely related to Waite-Smith in the trumps, while BOTA’s commonly issued minors are comparatively schematic and intended for student coloring.

**[Documented fact]** Case retained Golden Dawn-derived elemental and Qabalistic ideas but rejected or revised what he regarded as Waite’s blinds.

**Finding:** Published BOTA material accessible for this research does not establish a Page-of-Cups scene as historically independent and influential in the way Smith’s or Harris’s image is. Case’s private/order lessons require edition-specific checking; generic online “BOTA Page” meanings are often later summaries.

## Thoth, painted 1938–1943; first complete publication 1969

Frieda Harris’s **Princess of Cups** is a pale, flowing female figure, apparently dancing or floating above water. She is surrounded by fluid, crystalline, shell-like and aquatic forms. A swan spreads above or behind her. She carries a covered cup from which a tortoise emerges; a lotus is also present.

Crowley writes that she represents:

> “the earthy part of Water; in particular, the faculty of crystallization.”

He calls her “Womanhood in its ultimate projection” and emphasizes the apparent inconsistency of water becoming definite form. [Crowley, *Book of Thoth*, court cards](https://hermetic.com/crowley/book-of-thoth/court-cards); [1944 PDF witness](https://www.rahoorkhuit.net/library/crowley/equinox/volume3/vol_3_no_05.pdf).

### Meaning of the tortoise and crystallization

**[Esoteric attribution]** The tortoise gives bodily, slow, enduring form to watery potential. The Princess is manifestation: the last stage of the elemental formula in which the suit’s power becomes material enough to seed the next cycle.

**[Comparative observation]** Smith’s fish rises as a surprising image from a cup; Harris’s tortoise issues from the cup as crystallized elemental life. Both animate the vessel, but the surviving sources do not demonstrate direct derivation of Harris’s tortoise from Smith’s fish.

## Notable modern redesigns

- **Crowley-Harris derivatives:** substitute Princess for Page and stress feminine embodiment, crystallization, intuition, and elemental Earth of Water.
- **Marseille revivals:** retain the male Valet, large cup, lid, flowers, and undecorated background.
- **RWS derivatives:** overwhelmingly preserve the fish, often turning it into a speaking fish, dream messenger, intuitive surprise, or emerging emotion.
- **Feminist and queer decks:** commonly make the Page gender-fluid, female, nonbinary, or deliberately youthful without fixed gender. **[Modern invention]**
- **Jungian decks:** interpret the fish as autonomous unconscious content entering awareness. **[Modern psychological adaptation]**
- **Grail-themed decks:** recast the cup as the Holy Grail and the Page as novice seeker or cup-bearer. **[Modern medievalist synthesis]**

## The game

## Rank

In the usual 78-card Italian/French tarot structure, suit cards rank:

**King > Queen > Knight/Cavalier > Page/Valet > 10 … Ace**

The exact pip direction may change in some historical games and suits, but the Page is normally the lowest court and above the numeral cards.

## Modern French Tarot value

**[Documented rule]**

- King: 4½ points
- Queen: 3½
- Knight: 2½
- Valet: **1½**
- Ordinary pip: ½

A Valet is paired with a low card when counting, producing two points for the pair. [Pagat French Tarot rules](https://www.pagat.com/tarot/frtarot.html).

## Italian tarocchi and Tarock

Values vary by game:

- In many point-trick systems, Kings and higher courts carry the greater values; the Page/Fante is the least valuable court.
- Some games count by subtraction or grouped tricks rather than assigning the isolated modern French values.
- German/Austrian Tarock decks may have French suits and call the lowest court Jack/Bube; the historical Cup Page then has no literal equivalent.
- In shortened packs, low pips may be removed while all courts remain.

No single “tarocchi value” applies across five centuries and dozens of games.

## Minchiate

The Cup Page remains an ordinary suit court. The expanded set of trumps does not change it into a trump. Its capture value varies with the rule tradition.

## Trump-order disputes are not Page-of-Cups disputes

Historians group early trump sequences into approximately three regional orders, conventionally associated with Milan, Ferrara, and Bologna. Justice, Fortitude/Strength, and Temperance move relative to other trumps. The Fool may be unnumbered and function as an Excuse rather than an ordinary highest or lowest trump.

None of these order questions gives the Page a number. Likewise:

- Papess → Juno and Pope → Jupiter are Besançon trump substitutions.
- *Maison Dieu* is a French Tower title.
- Waite’s Strength VIII/Justice XI exchange concerns trumps.
- Crowley’s Tzaddi/Heh exchange concerns Emperor and Star.
- The Page/Princess’s “Heh” is a rank formula, not the Emperor/Star Hebrew-letter controversy.

## Chronology of attestation

### Approximately 1370s–early 1400s: ordinary cards reach Europe

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** European Latin-suited cards derive their suit structure substantially from Islamic, especially Mamluk, models: cups, coins, swords, and polo sticks/batons. European makers humanized the courts and added a Queen.

### Approximately 1420: Marziano da Tortona

Marziano described a special card game designed for Filippo Maria Visconti, with sixteen classical gods divided among four symbolic orders.

**[Documented fact]** This is an early custom trump game, but not the standard tarot pack and not an attestation of the Page of Cups. Its suit organization differs.

### 1440: Florence

A newly recognized archival entry records two packs of *trionfi* made by Giusto Giusti for Sigismondo Pandolfo Malatesta.

**[Documented fact]** This predates the long-cited Ferrara entries.

### January 1441: Ferrara

Sagramoro was paid for fourteen painted “figures” connected with Bianca Maria Visconti’s visit.

**[Disputed]** The entry does not say *trionfi*. It may concern tarot-related cards, but it cannot by itself prove a standard pack or Page of Cups.

### 10 February 1442: Ferrara ledger

Sagramoro was paid for painting cups, swords, coins, batons, and all the figures of four packs of triumph cards.

**[Documented fact]** This is direct evidence for four-suited *trionfi* packs and their figure cards. [Ferrara transcript and discussion](https://www.oocities.org/autorbis/ferrara1442.html); [Farley’s documentary summary](https://e-isomero.com/pdf/book/1611846398.pdf).

### 28 July 1442: Ferrara

Marchione Burdochi supplied *carte da trionfi* for Ercole and Sigismondo d’Este.

**[Documented fact]** Their relatively modest cost suggests that ordinary commercial packs existed alongside luxury painted commissions. [Le Tarot essay](https://letarot.it/Le-origini-italiane-del-Ludus-Triumphorum_pag_pg439_ita.aspx).

### Approximately 1450: Morgan Page of Cups

The Visconti-Sforza Page provides the earliest secure surviving physical witness to the card.

### Approximately 1470–1500: *Sermones de ludo cum aliis*

The anonymous Dominican sermon gives the earliest surviving complete or nearly complete list of the standard trump subjects and condemns gambling.

**[Documented fact]** Its concern is play and moral danger, not tarot divination. Because it lists trumps, it does not individually name the Page of Cups.

### Late fifteenth century: Sola Busca

Provides a complete scenic minor-arcana precedent but not the RWS Page’s fish.

### Approximately 1500: tarot poems

Poetic works attached moral, social, or personal descriptions to tarot cards. The Boiardo-associated tarot scheme and other poem cycles show cards functioning as literary prompts.

**[Qualification]** These are not proof of a secret ancient divinatory code.

### 1520s: Teofilo Folengo

Folengo’s *Chaos del Tri per uno* includes sonnets constructed from tarot-card names.

**[Documented fact]** This is literary play with card imagery, not a Golden Dawn-style correspondence system.

### Seventeenth century: Marseille Valet stabilizes

Noblet gives a clear popular-print form to the youthful cup-bearing Valet.

### 1781: Court de Gébelin and Comte de Mellet

Court de Gébelin declares tarot the remains of an Egyptian Book of Thoth. De Mellet attaches the twenty-two trumps to Hebrew letters and proposes cartomantic meanings.

**Finding:** Neither supplies a distinctive Hebrew letter, planet, or decan for the Page of Cups. Their central constructions concern the trumps and the deck as a whole.

### 1783–1789: Etteilla

Etteilla publishes systematic tarot divination, upright/reversed meanings, astrology, and an Egyptianized deck.

### 1854–1856: Éliphas Lévi

Lévi joins tarot trumps to Hebrew letters and develops the magical-tool/element vocabulary that later encouraged Cup = Water.

**Finding:** Lévi does not yet provide the fully developed Golden Dawn “Princess of Cups = Earth of Water, final Heh, throne of the Ace” formula.

### 1863–1870: Paul Christian

Paul Christian develops an elaborate Egyptian initiation narrative and popularizes *arcana* language.

**[Modern invention relative to antiquity]** His initiatory corridor is a nineteenth-century literary-occult construction, falsely given ancient authority.

### 1888: Mathers

Mathers publishes English tarot meanings shortly before or during the Golden Dawn’s formation.

### 1889: Papus and Wirth

Papus publishes *Le Tarot des Bohémiens*; Wirth issues twenty-two occult trumps.

**Finding:** Wirth’s original deck contributes no Page. Papus discusses the four court levels in a generative Qabalistic scheme but predates or differs from the mature *Book T* title and imagery.

### Approximately 1888–1900: *Book T*

The Golden Dawn completes the Princess/Earth/final-Heh/throne/quadrant synthesis.

### 1909–1910: Waite-Smith and *Pictorial Key*

Smith creates the fish-in-cup scene; Waite publishes its description and meanings.

### 1938–1943/1944/1969: Thoth

Harris paints the Princess; Crowley publishes *The Book of Thoth* in 1944; the full deck is commercially issued in 1969.

### Late twentieth century onward

Psychological, Jungian, feminist, queer, Grail, inner-child, and creativity-centered readings proliferate.

### The four quadrants and zodiacal decans

The Golden Dawn distributes Knights, Queens, and Princes over thirty-degree arcs composed of the last decan of one sign and the first two of the next. Princesses are different: they occupy four celestial quadrants around the north pole and serve as thrones of the Aces.

For the Princess of Cups, modern diagrams commonly associate the quadrant with the Water-zone sequence Libra, Scorpio, Sagittarius. This does not mean she “rules 20° Libra to 20° Scorpio.” That span belongs to a different court calculation.

### Tzaddi and Heh

Crowley’s statement “Tzaddi is not the Star” led him to exchange the Emperor and Star’s letter assignments. This is unrelated to the Princess’s final Heh:

- **Heh as Princess:** grammatical/familial place in YHVH.
- **Heh as trump letter:** a separate path attribution affected by Crowley’s Emperor/Star revision.

Conflating these creates the misleading statement that the Page of Cups “has the Hebrew letter Heh” in the same sense as a trump.

## Divinatory meanings by author

Short quotations are used where primary texts were accessible. Modern copyrighted authors are paraphrased unless an edition could be verified directly.

## Etteilla

**[Documented tradition, edition-sensitive]** Etteilla established fixed upright and reversed readings for every card. Different Grand Etteilla recensions reorganize the Marseille courts, so a single universal English “Page of Cups” gloss cannot safely be quoted without naming the particular deck or table.

Commonly transmitted Etteilla-family readings associate the youthful Cup court with a fair-haired youth, affection, service, news, or a young person; reversed forms shift toward inclination, flattery, seduction, or deception.

**Finding:** I did not locate a clean facsimile page conclusively aligning all of those English glosses with one numbered 1780s Etteilla card. They should therefore be treated as an Etteilla-derived tradition, not quoted as a single uncontested sentence by Etteilla.

## S. L. MacGregor Mathers, 1888

Mathers gives the Knave/Page of Cups meanings approximately as a fair young man, intelligence or news, application, study, reflection, meditation, and business; reversed, taste, inclination, attachment, seduction, deception, or artifice.

**Textual relationship:** Waite’s later list is extremely close. Mathers was drawing on French cartomantic vocabulary, probably including Etteilla-derived lists, rather than describing the Golden Dawn Princess design later called *Book T*.

Edition: S. L. MacGregor Mathers, *The Tarot: Its Occult Signification, Use in Fortune-Telling, and Method of Play* (London: George Redway, 1888), approximately pp. 25–30 depending scan pagination. [1888 scan](https://www.howardnema.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/12/the-tarot-1888-by-macgregor-mathers-1.pdf).

## A. E. Waite, 1910

Upright:

> “Fair young man … a studious youth; news, message; application, reflection, meditation.”

He adds that these may be directed toward business.

Reversed:

> “Taste, inclination, attachment, seduction, deception, artifice.”

Waite’s visual gloss is “the pictures of the mind taking form.” Edition: *The Pictorial Key to the Tarot* (London: William Rider & Son, 1910), Part III, Page of Cups. [Primary text](https://sacred-texts.com/book/the-pictorial-key-to-the-tarot/read/page-of-cups).

## Aleister Crowley, 1944

Crowley does not give a simple upright/reversed dictionary. The Thoth system normally reads dignities and context rather than mechanically reversing cards.

Positive expression: sweetness, gentleness, kindness, tenderness, romance, imagination, graciousness, and the ability of feeling to assume beautiful form.

Ill-dignified expression: indolence, selfish luxury, fantasy, dependence, or emotion lacking strength and practical coherence.

His primary ontological definition is:

> “the earthy part of Water; in particular, the faculty of crystallization.”

Edition: Aleister Crowley, *The Book of Thoth*, Equinox III:5 (London: O.T.O., 1944), “The Court Cards,” Princess of Cups. [Online text](https://hermetic.com/crowley/book-of-thoth/court-cards); [PDF](https://www.rahoorkhuit.net/library/crowley/equinox/volume3/vol_3_no_05.pdf).

## Paul Foster Case

Case’s teaching is Golden Dawn-derived but usually emphasizes practical occult psychology: the Page/Princess level is the material embodiment of Cup/Water forces—imagination, receptivity, emotion, and subconscious response.

**Finding:** A primary, openly accessible Case passage giving a discrete upright and reversed Page-of-Cups list was not verified. BOTA’s course literature is often republished without stable pagination, and some lessons are restricted. It would be irresponsible to manufacture a quotation.

**Probable Case-derived convention:** news or a young person of emotional temperament; imagination made concrete; passivity, fantasy, or emotional suggestibility when adversely placed.

## Rachel Pollack

In *Seventy-Eight Degrees of Wisdom*, Pollack treats Pages as beginnings, messages, youthful openness, or the first practical emergence of a suit’s energy. For the Page of Cups she emphasizes imagination, sensitivity, fantasy, and openness to surprising inner experience—the fish as an image arising from the unconscious.

**Modern Jungian development:** Rather than reducing the card to “a fair young man,” Pollack allows it to signify a psychic stance or event.

**Reversed/adverse:** emotional immaturity, escapism, excessive fantasy, blocked intuition, or difficulty accepting what emerges from within.

No extended direct quotation is supplied because the work remains copyrighted and an edition-specific page was not directly available. Consult Rachel Pollack, *Seventy-Eight Degrees of Wisdom*, Part II, the court cards of Cups.

## Mary K. Greer

Greer’s court-card method is interactive and role-oriented. Pages commonly concern messages, learning, experimentation, embodiment, and the inexperienced or exploratory phase of a suit.

For Cups this becomes experimenting with emotional expression, intuition, relationship, dream, or art. Reversed or shadow expressions include emotional dependency, coyness, manipulation, fantasy without follow-through, and difficulty communicating feelings.

**Qualification:** Greer frequently encourages readers to derive meanings through personal experience, elemental dignities, questions, and card combinations rather than treating one list as canonical.

Consult Mary K. Greer, *Tarot for Your Self* and *Understanding the Tarot Court*. A primary edition-specific Page-of-Cups quotation was not verified in the openly accessible material.

## Esoteric and symbolic readings

## Elemental: Water and Earth of Water

**First systematic form:** late nineteenth-century Golden Dawn.

Water represents emotion, receptivity, imagination, reflection, dissolution, dream, and relationship. The Princess contributes Earth: form, body, result, stabilization, and manifestation.

Thus Earth of Water can be read as:

- ice or crystallization;
- sediment or minerals deposited by water;
- the body as vessel for feeling;
- art giving stable form to imagination;
- an emotional message becoming communicable;
- fertility and gestation.

These are Hermetic analogies, not fifteenth-century game meanings.

## Tetragrammaton and Kabbalah

Golden Dawn courts enact YHVH as a family:

- Knight/King: Yod, Father
- Queen: first Heh, Mother
- Prince: Vav, Son
- Princess: final Heh, Daughter

The Princess is both completion and threshold: she receives the preceding forces and becomes the throne on which the Ace’s pure elemental power can manifest.

**[Esoteric attribution]** “Throne of the Ace” is not a historical rank in play. It arises from Golden Dawn cosmology.

The card has no unique Tree-of-Life path. Courts may be distributed across the four Qabalistic worlds or Sephiroth in particular teaching diagrams, but this is different from the twenty-two paths assigned to the trumps.

## Astrology

The Page/Princess has no classical astrological attribution.

**Golden Dawn:** Princess of Cups governs the watery celestial quadrant and the lands or regions placed beneath it. The quadrant is commonly diagrammed through Libra, Scorpio, and Sagittarius around the north celestial pole.

**Conflict:** Popular handbooks sometimes assign the Page to Pisces, Cancer, Scorpio, Venus, Moon, or a specific birth range. These are later simplifications or alternative systems, not the unambiguous *Book T* rule.

## Alchemy

Crowley’s “crystallization” is the most explicit influential alchemical formulation. Water’s indefinite potential acquires Earthly form. Harris’s crystalline geometries, shell, tortoise, lotus, and moving water express generation through condensation.

**First appearance:** The exact Princess-of-Cups crystallization doctrine is Golden Dawn/Crowley-era, not Renaissance evidence.

## Christian and Grail interpretations

The cup naturally permits comparison with:

- Eucharistic chalice;
- cup of salvation;
- Annunciation or sacred offering;
- Holy Grail;
- Fisher King and Grail quest;
- fish as *ichthys*, baptism, Christ, or the miraculous catch.

**[Tradition/modern synthesis]** Such comparisons are symbolically available but are not documented explanations of the Morgan or Marseille Page. In those cards, the cup is first of all the suit emblem.

**[Modern invention]** Identifying Waite’s fish specifically as Christ or the Page specifically as Parsifal lacks direct evidence from Waite or Smith. It is a later Grail reading.

## Hermetic reading

The cup is a receptive magical instrument. The Page/Princess makes the invisible visible: vision becomes image, feeling becomes gesture, and water becomes a bounded offering.

Lévi helped circulate the equation of magical cup and Water; Papus and the Golden Dawn integrated it into a fourfold initiatory system.

## Jungian reading

The fish lives below the water’s surface and therefore readily represents autonomous unconscious content. Its emergence into the cup creates an encounter between ego and image.

The Page may be:

- the receptive ego encountering a dream;
- the inner child;
- a new feeling not yet understood;
- creative imagination becoming conscious;
- a surprising intuition that resists rational control.

**First appearance:** Not Jung himself writing about the Page of Cups. This is a mid-to-late twentieth-century application of Jungian vocabulary spread by tarot writers including Pollack, Sallie Nichols, Mary K. Greer, and their successors.

## Psychological shadow

Modern readers commonly identify:

- emotional immaturity;
- idealization;
- avoidant fantasy;
- suggestibility;
- performative innocence;
- passive seduction;
- messages that seem sincere but are unreliable.

These extend Waite’s reversed “seduction, deception, artifice” and Crowley’s ill-dignified passivity.

## Paintings and buildings

No securely documented historical painting or building was found that intentionally quotes the Page of Cups as a distinct card.

The reverse influence is better established: tarot courts draw on late-medieval and Renaissance court costume, manuscript illumination, heraldry, and woodcut conventions. Claims that a cathedral sculpture or Renaissance portrait is “the source” require a demonstrable visual chain, not resemblance alone.

## Controversies and disputes

## Egyptian Book of Thoth

**[Legend, first clearly promulgated 1781]** Court de Gébelin claimed tarot preserved Egyptian priestly wisdom in disguised form. He offered speculative Egyptian etymologies without hieroglyphic evidence; Egyptian script had not yet been deciphered.

Etteilla adopted and elaborated the narrative, dating tarot to a conclave of Egyptian sages and claiming it had been inscribed on gold plates.

**Evidence against:** Tarot appears in northern Italian records of the 1440s; its trumps reflect late-medieval European social, Christian, and civic imagery. No Egyptian tarot manuscript, deck, or transmission chain survives. Dummett summarizes the documentary problem in [“Origins of Tarot”](https://www.nybooks.com/articles/1981/05/14/origins-of-tarot/).

**Important nuance:** The Mamluk playing-card ancestry is genuinely Egyptian in the geographic sense of medieval Mamluk Egypt. It does not validate a pharaonic Book of Thoth.

## Romani or “Gypsy” transmission

**[Legend]** Court de Gébelin and later authors associated tarot with people then incorrectly believed to have come from Egypt. Nineteenth-century writers popularized “Tarot of the Bohemians.”

**Evidence:** The earliest Italian tarot documents do not connect the cards with Romani communities. Historians have found no evidence that Romani people brought tarot from Egypt or preserved an ancient priestly book.

**Distinction:** Romani fortune-tellers later used cards within European cartomantic culture. That later practice does not establish ancient invention or transmission.

## Cathar claims

**[Modern legend/disputed]** Some twentieth-century writers linked tarot to Cathar survival, southern French heresy, or hidden dualist doctrine.

**Evidence:** No Cathar manuscript mentions tarot; tarot appears more than a century after the Albigensian Crusade’s decisive phase and first in northern Italian court records. The Page of Cups contains no securely Cathar-specific sign.

## Templar claims

**[Modern legend]** Tarot has been described as encoded Templar teaching preserved after the order’s suppression in 1307.

**Evidence:** No Templar tarot, inventory, rule, or contemporary reference survives. The chronological gap and lack of transmission documents make the claim unsupported.

## Sufi and Persian esoteric claims

**[Speculative reconstruction]** Paul Huson and others have drawn connections among Mamluk suits, Persian polo symbolism, Sufi poetry, and fourfold spiritual schemes.

There is real historical relevance in the Islamic playing-card structure and polo-stick suit. The claim that a Sufi initiatory doctrine generated tarot’s later European allegories is not demonstrated by a documentary chain. Farley describes this as speculative in [*A Cultural History of Tarot*](https://e-isomero.com/pdf/book/1611846398.pdf).

## Was the Visconti Page a portrait?

**[Disputed]** Luxury courts may idealize or allude to patrons, relatives, and court costume.

**Evidence:** Heraldry securely associates the pack with the Visconti-Sforza world, but the Page lacks a name. Physiognomic resemblance is insufficient for identification.

## Was Smith’s fish a Grail or Christian symbol?

**[Open/disputed]** Fish and cup can support Christian interpretation; Waite was a Christian mystic and Grail scholar.

**Evidence for a narrower claim:** Waite himself explains the scene as mental images taking form. No located instruction by Waite says “draw the Christian fish” or identifies the Page as a Grail novice.

The safest conclusion is that Christian/Grail readings are plausible secondary interpretations, not established sources of the drawing.

## Did Sola Busca directly supply the Page?

**[Disputed]** Smith demonstrably borrowed or adapted several Sola Busca compositions in the minor arcana.

**Finding:** No close Page-of-Cups prototype in Sola Busca was located. General access does not prove card-specific dependence.

## “Conver 1760”

**[Bibliographic controversy]** Reproductions often call every Conver-derived card “1760.” Nicolas Conver’s pattern is traditionally dated 1760, but individual surviving sheets or BnF impressions can be later.

The date of design, block, impression, and modern restoration must be separated.

## Misprints and traditions

No Page-of-Cups misprint comparable to famous trump-title corruptions was identified.

Relevant broader examples include:

- **Maison Dieu:** a historical French title of the Tower, later reinterpreted as House of God, lightning-struck tower, or divine catastrophe.
- **Papess/Pope → Juno/Jupiter:** deliberate regional substitutions, not printing accidents.
- **Fool 0:** the historic Fool was commonly unnumbered; zero is an occult/modern organizing convention.
- **Strength VIII and Justice XI:** Waite followed Golden Dawn ordering; Marseille usually has Justice VIII and Force XI.
- **Princess = Page:** a deliberate Golden Dawn renaming, often misreported as if all tarot traditions used Princess.
- **Princess’s zodiac range:** later tables sometimes mistakenly assign her a thirty-degree span by analogy with other courts.

## Open questions

1. **Exact date of the Morgan Page:** The Morgan uses ca. 1450–1480 for the broader object record while art historians often date the principal deck near 1450. Technical study may narrow production phases.

2. **Artist:** Bonifacio Bembo remains the traditional attribution, but Zavattari-workshop arguments require continued material comparison.

3. **Portrait identity:** No named Visconti-Sforza sitter has been established for the Page of Cups.

4. **Earliest written individual name:** The physical card survives from the fifteenth century, but the earliest located document saying precisely *Fante di Coppe* remains to be established from game rules or inventories.

5. **Smith’s fish:** Waite explains its conceptual effect, but its exact pictorial source is untraced.

6. **Golden Dawn original artwork:** Surviving manuscripts, private copies, and later reconstructions do not always agree in detail. Wang and Cicero should not automatically be treated as identical to the earliest order sketches.

7. **Etteilla court mapping:** A rigorous answer requires collation of the 1783–1785 *Cahiers*, the 1789 deck, later Grand Etteilla types, and French card inscriptions. Modern English lists frequently merge them.

8. **Case’s specific wording:** Openly accessible BOTA sources did not yield a verifiable edition-and-page upright/reversed Page-of-Cups entry.

9. **Modern authors’ exact wording:** Pollack and Greer are well documented as major spreaders of psychological court-card reading, but reliable edition-specific excerpts were not openly available for verbatim quotation.

10. **Grail derivation:** Symbolically productive but not historically established for the early Page or Smith’s design.

## Conclusions

The Page of Cups has three recoverable historical layers.

First is the **playing-card layer**: a lowest Cup court structurally descended from the Mamluk court hierarchy, transformed in Europe into a human servant or youth, and securely represented by the Milanese Visconti-Sforza Page around 1450. Its historical job was to rank and score in a trick-taking game.

Second is the **Marseille-cartomantic layer**: the *Valet de Coupe*, a standing young retainer carrying an oversized cup. Etteilla, Mathers, and Waite attached the durable meanings of young person, service, study, reflection, message, attachment, seduction, and artifice.

Third is the **Hermetic-psychological layer**: the Golden Dawn changed Page to Princess and defined her as Earth of Water, final Heh, throne of the Ace, and ruler of a celestial quadrant. Waite preserved the male Page but added Smith’s fish—the “pictures of the mind taking form.” Crowley and Harris made the figure female and cosmic, replacing the fish with the tortoise of crystallized water. Pollack, Greer, Jodorowsky, and later readers turned these images toward imagination, emotional learning, inner-child work, creativity, dream, and unconscious emergence.

The Egyptian, Romani, Cathar, Templar, and ancient Grail histories belong to the mythology subsequently built around tarot. They remain culturally influential, but none is supported by the fifteenth-century evidence for this card.
