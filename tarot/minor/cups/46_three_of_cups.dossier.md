# Three of Cups: Research Dossier

## Method and evidentiary labels

This dossier distinguishes:

- **Documented fact** — supported by a surviving card, contemporary record, first-edition text, or institutional catalogue.
- **Scholarly reconstruction** — an inference made from incomplete objects or documents by historians.
- **Tradition** — a reading repeated within an identifiable cartomantic or occult lineage.
- **Esoteric attribution** — a deliberately constructed occult correspondence, not a claim demonstrated by historical evidence.
- **Disputed claim** — supported by some writers but questioned or contradicted by others.
- **Legend** — a historical-origin narrative unsupported by contemporary evidence.
- **Modern invention** — a recent interpretation or redesign without an older traceable pedigree.

A limitation should be made explicit: unlike a trump, the Three of Cups did not possess an individual name, character, Hebrew letter, or allegorical identity in Renaissance tarot. Before cartomancy it was simply the third numeral of a playing-card suit. Consequently, many famous disputes about trump names and orders do not attach individually to this card.

### Earliest surviving example

**Documented fact:** the earliest securely catalogued surviving Three of Cups located for this investigation is the Visconti–Sforza card now at the Morgan Library, MS M.630.25, Milan, conventionally dated about 1450–1480. It shows three gold cups surrounded by a foliate border. The Morgan attributes it to Bonifacio Bembo while recording later scholarly attribution questions. [Morgan object record](https://www.themorgan.org/collection/tarot-cards/the-three-of-cups), [Morgan manuscript catalogue](https://www.themorgan.org/manuscript/158525), [Morgan image record](https://ica.themorgan.org/manuscript/page/25/158525).

**Qualification:** the Brambilla and Cary–Yale/Visconti di Modrone packs may be slightly earlier as decks, but this inquiry did not locate an institutional record establishing a surviving Three of Cups from either pack earlier than M.630.25. “Earliest deck containing a cups suit” and “earliest surviving physical Three of Cups” must not be conflated.

### Golden Dawn attribution

**Documented esoteric attribution:** in the Golden Dawn’s *Book T* system:

- Title: **Lord of Abundance**
- Sephirah: **Binah**, the third sephirah
- Suit/world: Cups; Water; Briah
- Astrology: **Mercury in Cancer**
- Decan: **10°–20° Cancer**, not “Moon in Cancer”
- Image formula: three cups supplied by water from lotuses
- Traditional meaning: abundance, success, pleasure, hospitality and bounty

A commonly circulated *Book T* transcription gives: “Three of Cups. The Lord of Abundance. Mercury in Cancer.” The same scheme appears in the Golden Dawn papers printed in *The Equinox* and in later Cicero reconstructions. [Golden Dawn material in *The Equinox*, I:8](https://hermetic.com/_media/crowley/equinox/equinox-iviii.pdf), [Ciceros’ reconstruction, pp. 423–24 in the scanned edition](https://kobayashi-dxn.s3.eu-west-2.amazonaws.com/Golden%20Dawn/cicero-%20secrets%20of%20the%20golden%20dawn%20temple/Cicero_-_Self_Initiation_into_%282%29.pdf).

**Correction of a frequent error:** the Thoth Three of Cups is Mercury in Cancer. Some modern reference pages incorrectly call its decan ruler the Moon, probably by confusing Cancer’s zodiacal ruler with the Golden Dawn decan ruler.

### Cups, Heh and Water

**Esoteric attribution, not Renaissance history:** the Golden Dawn assigns the four suits to the letters of the Tetragrammaton:

- Wands — Yod — Fire — Atziluth
- Cups — first Heh — Water — Briah
- Swords — Vav — Air — Yetzirah
- Pentacles — final Heh — Earth — Assiah

The Three of Cups therefore belongs to **Heh/Briah/Water**, but it has **no individual Hebrew letter**. Individual Hebrew-letter assignments belong to the twenty-two trumps. Paul Foster Case explicitly describes Cups as “Briah, creative world; soul … water.” [Case, *Introduction to the Study of the Tarot*](https://www.mr-kaplan.com/Library/PFCase/Introduction-to-Tarot.html).

## Iconography deck by deck

## Fifteenth-century precursors and luxury decks

### Marziano da Tortona–Michelino da Besozzo pack, circa 1412–1425

**Documented fact:** Marziano’s text describes a game of sixteen classical gods painted for Filippo Maria Visconti by Michelino da Besozzo. The cards are lost.

**Relevance:** this was a courtly trump game but not a standard 78-card tarot. Its four suits were allegorical birds rather than Cups, Swords, Batons and Coins. It therefore contained no demonstrable Three of Cups.

**Scholarly reconstruction:** it shows that special courtly card packs with permanent trump hierarchies preceded or accompanied the development of tarot. It is not evidence that the Three of Cups had a classical or occult meaning.

### Cary–Yale / Visconti di Modrone, circa 1440s

**Documented fact:** this Milanese luxury pack has an unusual structure, including male and female versions of several court ranks and additional virtue trumps. The Met describes 69 surviving cards and notes the structural divergence from standardized tarot. [Metropolitan Museum history](https://www.metmuseum.org/fr/perspectives/tarot-2).

**Three of Cups:** a secure institutional image and catalogue entry for a surviving Three of Cups was not located in the sources consulted. Where modern reproductions supply one, they may reconstruct a missing card.

### Brambilla pack, before 1447

**Documented fact:** preserved in the Pinacoteca di Brera and associated with Filippo Maria Visconti. It is attributed to the Bembo workshop. [Met overview](https://www.metmuseum.org/fr/perspectives/tarot-2).

**Three of Cups:** not securely documented in the accessible catalogue material consulted. No specific iconographic claim should be made without the object entry.

### Visconti–Sforza, circa 1450–1480, Milan

**Documented fact:** M.630.25 has three elaborate covered or ciborium-like gold cups against a tooled gold ground, connected and enclosed by a symmetrical floreate border. There is no human scene and no written divinatory keyword. [Morgan image and description](https://ica.themorgan.org/manuscript/page/25/158525).

**Meaning in context:** the repetition marks number and suit. The costly gold ground, heraldic setting and ornamental symmetry belong to a luxury court commission, not to a separately encoded “celebration” narrative.

**Disputed interpretation:** the cup form resembles late-medieval ciboria, reliquaries and Grail vessels. That makes Christian visual comparison possible, but no contemporary document says the card represents the Grail, Eucharist, clergy, or Queen of Heaven. Such interpretations are analogical. A modern comparative proposal may be seen [here](https://voynichlunarium.wordpress.com/2018/11/03/the-six-containers/).

### Ferrarese and Este fragments

**Documented fact:** Ferrarese records prove production of cups, swords, coins, batons and “figures” in packs of *trionfi*. The February 10, 1442 ledger pays Jacopo Sagramoro for painting four packs:

> “le chope e le spade e li dinari e li bastoni”

—that is, “the cups, swords, coins and batons.” [Transcription and translation](https://www.oocities.org/autorbis/ferrara1442.html).

**Three of Cups:** surviving Este fragments do not provide a securely identified card-specific lineage comparable with the Morgan Three. The ledger proves that numbered Cups existed in those packs, but it does not describe their arrangement.

### Sola Busca, 1491; painted copy circa 1490s

**Documented fact:** the Sola Busca is the earliest surviving complete 78-card tarot and the earliest surviving complete tarot with fully illustrated number cards. The unique painted set is now at Brera. Its engraved plates are dated 1491.

**Actual Three of Cups:** it is a fully illustrated Renaissance object-picture rather than a Marseille floral pip, but it does **not** show Pamela Colman Smith’s three women dancing and toasting.

**Disputed deck-level influence:** photographs of the Sola Busca were supplied to the British Museum in 1907, two years before publication of the Waite–Smith deck. Several Waite–Smith cards show persuasive visual parallels—notably the pierced heart of the Three of Swords and burden-bearing compositions associated with the Tens. Robert M. Place discusses those parallels [here](https://robertmplacetarot.com/2015/01/18/pamela-colman-smiths-tarot/).

**Finding on the item-specific lead:** no good source was found for the claim that the **Sola Busca Three of Cups itself** was the source of Smith’s Three-of-Cups scene. The evidence supports access to and influence from the Sola Busca deck generally, not this specific derivation. The three-women scene is more plausibly related to the Three Graces, ritual dance, theatrical composition and Waite’s harvest/celebration brief.

### “Mantegna Tarocchi,” circa 1460s

**Documented fact:** these are engraved educational or emblematic series of fifty figures arranged in hierarchical groups.

**Correction:** despite the later name, they are not a tarot deck and contain no four suits, no pip cards and no Three of Cups. Their relevance is limited to the Renaissance culture of ordered emblematic series.

### Rosenwald and Budapest sheets, late fifteenth or early sixteenth century

**Documented fact:** these are early printed sheets or fragments important to the development of standardized trumps and popular card production.

**Three of Cups:** no securely identified Three of Cups from the accessible surviving material was located. They should not be used to fill a gap in the card’s iconographic genealogy.

## Tarot de Marseille lineage

### General Marseille form

**Documented fact:** Marseille numeral cards are non-scenic. The Three of Cups generally has:

- one cup above and two below, or a closely related triangular arrangement;
- bilateral Roman numerals, normally III;
- curving leaves, stems, flowers or fruit around the cups;
- no people and no explicitly narrated event.

The plants are ornamental and compositional, although later readers interpret them symbolically.

### Jean Noblet, Paris, circa 1650

**Documented fact:** Noblet is the earliest substantially surviving named French deck classed in the Marseille lineage.

**Three of Cups:** a triangular group of cups incorporated into compact floral arabesques. The treatment is rougher and denser than later standardized Marseille designs.

### Jean Dodal, Lyon, circa 1701–1715

The three cups are more clearly separated within a vertically balanced floral framework. Stems and leaves organize the empty spaces; III appears along the margins.

### Nicolas Conver, Marseille, 1760

Conver’s pattern became especially influential in later Marseille reproduction. The upper cup is raised above two lower cups; symmetrical flowers and leaves visually bind the three. The card remains a gaming pip with no printed meaning.

### Grimaud / Paul Marteau, twentieth century

**Documented fact:** Grimaud’s *Ancien Tarot de Marseille*, commonly associated with Paul Marteau, regularized the Conver-derived pattern and its modern color conventions.

**Tradition:** Marteau read the elevated third cup as a generative or reconciling force emerging from duality. That is a twentieth-century occult reading of the geometry, not an established eighteenth-century printer’s intention.

### Camoin–Jodorowsky, 1997

**Documented modern reconstruction:** Philippe Camoin and Alejandro Jodorowsky produced a restored Marseille deck by comparing historical exemplars and also making interpretive choices.

**Three of Cups:** two lower cups support or frame an upper cup; intertwined stems form heart-like curves and flowers suggest emotional generation. In *The Way of Tarot*, Jodorowsky and Marianne Costa interpret the card as ideal or first romantic love, something forming but not yet stabilized. [Jodorowsky and Costa, *The Way of Tarot*, searchable scan](https://irp-cdn.multiscreensite.com/24669b51/files/uploaded/The_Way_of_Tarot%20%281%29.pdf).

**Disputed terminology:** it is marketed as a “restoration,” but historians distinguish conservation of a single historical state from the creation of a synthetic modern deck assembled from multiple sources.

## Regional patterns

### Minchiate

**Documented fact:** Florentine Minchiate expanded the trump sequence to forty-one trumps while retaining Italian suits. The Three of Cups remained an ordinary numeral card rather than one of the added astrological, elemental or virtue trumps.

Its suit function and ranking followed the ordinary Cups sequence. Modern divinatory Minchiate decks that give it “celebration” or other Waite-derived meanings are modern additions.

### Bolognese Tarocchino

**Documented fact:** reduction of the pack removed low numeral cards. In the established 62-card Bolognese pack, the Two through Five of each suit are omitted.

**Result:** the Three of Cups is absent from ordinary Bolognese Tarocchino. A “Bolognese Three of Cups” in a modern expanded deck is therefore a reconstruction or invention.

### Sicilian Tarot

**Documented fact:** the traditional Sicilian pack is reduced to 63 cards. Cups, Swords and Batons begin at Five; Coins alone retains the Four. Therefore the Three of Cups is absent. [Rules and pack description](https://www.pagat.com/tarot/sicilian.html).

### Besançon pattern

**Documented fact:** the Besançon pattern’s celebrated substitutions concern trumps II and V—Juno and Jupiter for the Papess and Pope. Those regional renamings do not affect the Three of Cups, which remains *Trois de Coupes* with conventional pip ornament.

### Modern French and Central European gaming tarot

French Tarot Nouveau, Austrian Tarock and related packs normally use French suits. Thus there is generally no Cup suit; Hearts function as the corresponding red suit. Pictures on numeral cards are scenic or reversible genre illustrations, not occult scenes.

## Etteilla and nineteenth-century occult decks

### Grand Etteilla, circa 1788–1789

**Documented fact:** Etteilla rearranged tarot, placed divinatory language on the cards, Egyptianized its imagery and numbered the whole pack in his own sequence.

A Grand Etteilla-derived Three is normally no. 47 and shows three chalices in a compartmented design. One surviving printed form reads:

- upright: **RÉUSSITE** — success
- reversed: **EXPÉDITION D’AFFAIRES** — dispatch or rapid conduct of business

[Facsimile of *Le Grand Etteilla*](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/2/2f/Le_grand_Etteilla%2C_ou%2C_l%27art_de_tirer_les_cartes_..._Le_tout_recueilli_..._%28IA_b29321220%29.pdf).

Other Etteilla-family editions give “soulagement”/relief and “travail journalier”/daily work. These are edition or lineage variants, not reliable evidence of one immutable Etteilla keyword.

### Oswald Wirth, 1889

Wirth designed twenty-two trumps, not a complete illustrated 78-card pack. Therefore he supplied no original Three of Cups image. Later “Wirth” packs with minors are editorial completions.

### Papus, *Le Tarot des Bohémiens*, 1889

Papus synthesized Lévi, Etteilla, Christian and contemporary occult correspondences. For the minor cards he used number, suit, divine-name and decan structures rather than creating the later Waite–Smith social scene.

Papus’s “Bohemian” title helped spread the Romani-origin legend. It is not documentary evidence that Romani people created or transmitted tarot.

## Golden Dawn manuscripts and reconstructions

### Original *Book T* formula

The Golden Dawn image description has three overflowing cups and lotuses or water lilies, commonly with a white radiant hand issuing from cloud. The image is non-narrative and ceremonial.

**Meaning of the change:** the Marseille floral pip was transformed into a diagram of emanation:

- three = Binah;
- cups = Water/Briah;
- overflowing liquid = abundance;
- lotus = generated life;
- Mercury in Cancer = intelligence or communication operating through a receptive watery sign.

### Robert Wang, *Golden Dawn Tarot*, 1977

Wang’s deck is a later painted reconstruction made with Israel Regardie. Its Three of Cups follows the ritual manuscript: hand, lotuses, three cups and flowing water. It is not a surviving nineteenth-century Golden Dawn card.

### Chic and Sandra Tabatha Cicero, 1990s onward

The Ciceros’ reconstructions similarly visualize the written temple descriptions. Their account specifies three overflowing cups, lotus flowers, Mercury in Cancer, Binah and “Abundance.” These are valuable reconstructions, but variations of color, cup shape and composition belong to the modern artists.

## Waite–Smith, 1909

### Actual image

**Documented fact:** Pamela Colman Smith shows three women dancing in a circle. Each raises a cup. Their arms overlap, and the ground carries grapes, fruit, flowers and a pumpkin or gourd. The figures wear different colored garments and floral wreaths.

The design changes the card from an abstract pip into a legible social event: shared harvest, reunion, toast, female solidarity and collective joy.

### Waite’s description and meanings

In *The Pictorial Key to the Tarot* (1910), Waite calls the image:

> “Maidens in a garden-ground with cups uplifted”

and associates it with “the conclusion of any matter in plenty, perfection and merriment.” His divinatory entry gives:

- Upright: conclusion, perfection, merriment; happy issue, victory, fulfilment, solace and healing
- Reversed: expedition, dispatch, achievement, end; also excess in physical enjoyment and sensual pleasure

[Waite, *Pictorial Key*, Three of Cups chapter](https://sacred-texts.com/tarot/pkt/index.htm?chaptersPage=3).

### Sources

**Documented:** Smith worked under Waite’s direction and had access to Golden Dawn meanings.

**Strong scholarly inference:** the harvest and abundance scene converts the Golden Dawn title into a human tableau.

**Plausible art-historical source:** the circular female group evokes the Three Graces, whose intertwined pose was widespread in Renaissance and later art.

**Disputed:** direct dependence on a particular Three Graces painting has not been proved.

**Rejected item-specific lead:** the Sola Busca pack was almost certainly available to Smith, but its Three of Cups is not the visual prototype for Smith’s dancing women.

## Paul Foster Case and BOTA

**Documented fact:** Case retained Golden Dawn elemental, decan and sephirothic attributions but preferred a pip-only standard BOTA minor deck, designed under his direction with Jessie Burns Parke.

In Case’s tarot lessons:

- Cups correspond to Heh, Water, Briah and mental-emotional energy.
- Three corresponds to Understanding/Binah.
- Three of Cups: 10°–20° Cancer, Mercury ruler.
- Well dignified: success, abundance, pleasure, hospitality and good fortune through social contacts.
- Ill dignified: overindulgence, delay, superficiality or unreliable success.

A surviving lesson transcription describes, in the central vesica, “the letter H … surmounted by a flame,” linking Heh with Understanding and three. [Case lesson extract](https://www.scribd.com/doc/67617092/Tarot-Lessons-1-10-Paul-Foster-Case).

**Distinction:** Case’s H is a suit/Tetragrammaton emblem, not a unique Hebrew-letter assignment to this card.

## Crowley–Harris Thoth Tarot, painted 1938–1943; first full publication 1969

### Actual image

Lady Frieda Harris depicts three rounded, pomegranate-like cups arranged triangularly above a dark, calm sea. Golden lotuses feed them; the cups overflow and water circles or ripples beneath them. Mercury and Cancer glyphs appear on the card.

Crowley writes:

> “This card refers to Binah in the suit of Water.”

He identifies it with Demeter and Persephone, spiritual fertility, the “Will of Love,” and Mercury in Cancer. He also introduces a warning: the pomegranate binds Persephone to the underworld, so enjoyable things may contain a hidden obligation. [Crowley, *The Book of Thoth*, Three of Cups text](https://crowley-thoth.com/symbolism/cups-03/), [Hermetic Library transcription](https://hermetic.com/hermeneuticon/three-of-cups).

### Meaning of the design

Harris did not simply copy the Golden Dawn ceremonial card. She geometrized it, removed the cloud-hand and transformed the cups into mythic fruit. The result combines:

- Golden Dawn decan astrology;
- Binah as the “Great Sea”;
- fertility and maternal abundance;
- the Eleusinian Demeter–Persephone cycle;
- the ambiguity of pleasure that also binds.

### Tzaddi/Heh issue

**Not applicable to this pip:** Crowley’s “Tzaddi is not the Star” revision exchanges the Emperor and Star’s Hebrew-letter/zodiacal relations. It does not change the Three of Cups. The card’s Heh is the Tetragrammaton suit attribution, not the Emperor’s letter attribution.

## Notable modern redesigns

### Rachel Pollack and the modern Waite–Smith interpretive lineage

Pollack did not redesign the original card but decisively expanded its psychological and feminist reception. She interprets the intertwined women as equal participants sharing experience, not as a hierarchy.

### Jodorowsky–Camoin

Returns to a Marseille pip while treating its stems, heart-shapes, flowers and elevated vessel as an anatomical and psychological language. The meaning shifts from Waitean communal celebration to the emergence of idealized love.

### Wild Unknown and related minimalist decks

Modern decks often substitute three animals, birds, cups, moons or interlocking circles for Smith’s women. The continuity is relational triadic harmony, not historical iconography.

### Feminist, queer and culturally inclusive decks

**Modern invention/extension:** many recent decks deliberately portray chosen family, queer community, racial diversity, disability or non-binary celebrants. This develops the Waite–Smith community motif; it is not found in pre-1909 cards.

## The game

### Original function

**Documented fact:** the Three of Cups began as a suit card in a trick-taking game. It was not originally a “minor arcanum”; that terminology belongs to later occult tarot.

A standard early pack contained:

- 21 permanent trumps;
- the Fool;
- four suits of 14 cards;
- ten numeral cards and King, Queen, Knight, Knave in each suit.

[Metropolitan Museum overview](https://www.metmuseum.org/fr/perspectives/tarot-2).

### Rank

In most traditional tarot games with Italian or French suits:

- court cards rank highest;
- in the round or red suits—Cups and Coins, or Hearts and Diamonds—the numeral cards rank in reverse numerical order: Ace, Two, Three, Four … Ten;
- thus the Three of Cups beats Four through Ten but loses to Two, Ace and the courts.

Regional games vary, and some reduced packs omit the card entirely.

### Point value

The Three is ordinarily a low-value counting card:

- it has no special “oudler,” king, trump or court-card value;
- in common tarot counting systems it contributes the ordinary-card remainder—often effectively half a point when cards are counted in pairs, or one point before subtraction/group adjustment;
- its main importance is as a trick-taking card, discard, or suit-management card.

[Comparative tarot point-counting systems](https://www.pagat.com/tarot/counting.html).

### Minchiate

Present as an ordinary Cup numeral and not promoted by the additional trumps.

### Bolognese and Sicilian games

Absent because those packs delete the low Cup numerals. The Sicilian rules expressly begin Cups at Five. [Sicilian Tarocchi](https://www.pagat.com/tarot/sicilian.html).

### Trump-order disputes

These disputes concern the twenty-one trumps, not the Three of Cups:

- **Order A:** Bologna and much of northern Italy
- **Order B:** Ferrara and Venice
- **Order C:** Milan and the later Marseille lineage

Justice, Strength and the virtues occupy different relative places in these families. Waite numbered Strength VIII and Justice XI; Marseille commonly has Justice VIII and Strength XI. The Fool was usually unnumbered and served as an Excuse rather than an ordinary highest or lowest trump.

Regional religious substitutions—Juno for the Papess, Jupiter for the Pope, *Maison Dieu* for the Tower—likewise leave the Three of Cups unchanged.

| Jodorowsky–Costa | Three as active formation | Emotional/sexual energy | Not central | Numerological Marseille reading | First idealized love | Distinct from Waite’s friendship/harvest |
| Pollack | Appreciation and sharing | Grail, love and imagination | Secondary | Psychological rather than formal Qabalah | Joy, celebration, shared experience | Reversal stresses group failure or excess |
| Jungian schools | Third term generated from polarity | Feeling function, relationship, unconscious | Optional | Usually metaphorical | Creative social synthesis | Modern psychological overlay, not Jung’s own tarot doctrine |

### Binah

**Esoteric attribution:** Binah means Understanding in Hermetic Qabalah and is the third sephirah. It is associated with the Great Mother, form, limitation and the “Great Sea.” Hence three watery vessels can signify emotional abundance given form.

**Historical caution:** Jewish Kabbalah did not contain a Three-of-Cups doctrine. This is a Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn synthesis.

### Mercury in Cancer

In the Golden Dawn decan scheme, the minor cards Two through Ten correspond to the thirty-six decans. The Three of Cups occupies the second decan of Cancer, assigned to Mercury through the Chaldean planetary order.

This does not mean Mercury is “exalted” or domiciled in Cancer. It is a decan rulership internal to this occult table.

## Divinatory meanings by author

Short quotations are kept within copyright limits; longer entries are paraphrased.

### Etteilla, 1780s–1790s

- Upright: *réussite* — success; in other Etteilla-family lists, *soulagement* — relief.
- Reversed: *expédition d’affaires* — prompt dispatch of business; other editions give daily or ordinary work.

**Finding:** Etteilla-family packs are not textually uniform. “Relief/everyday work” and “success/business dispatch” should be cited to their respective editions rather than blended.

### S. L. MacGregor Mathers, 1888

In *The Tarot: Its Occult Signification, Use in Fortune-Telling, and Method of Play*:

- Upright: “Success, triumph, victory, favourable issue.”
- Reversed: “Expedition of business, quickness, celerity, vigilance.”

[Mathers scan](https://logoilibrary.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/07/Mathers.pdf).

Mathers is visibly close to Etteilla-derived vocabulary. His 1888 pamphlet should not be confused with the fuller Golden Dawn *Book T* attribution.

### Golden Dawn *Book T*

- Well dignified: abundance, success, pleasure, hospitality, bounty
- Ill dignified: sensuality, passivity, excess, or pleasure weakened by surrounding cards

The Golden Dawn originally relied primarily on dignity and context, not the modern assumption that reversal mechanically negates a card.

### Waite, 1910

- Upright: conclusion of a matter in plenty and merriment; victory, fulfilment, solace and healing
- Reversed: rapid completion or dispatch; alternatively excess in physical pleasure

Waite preserves Etteilla/Mathers “expedition” while making abundance visually intelligible through Smith’s harvest dance.

### Crowley, 1944

- Core: abundance, fertility and the fulfilment of love
- Qualification: pleasure is real but may bind; “good things … should be distrusted”
- Ill-dignified tendency: overindulgence or pleasure containing a concealed cost

Crowley does not supply a conventional upright/reversed pair because the Thoth method generally emphasizes elemental dignity and interaction.

### Paul Foster Case

- Well dignified: abundance, success, hospitality, pleasure, fortunate social or emotional developments
- Ill dignified: sensual excess, unreliable or fleeting success, indolence or dependence on approval

### Rachel Pollack

Pollack writes that the card signifies joy, celebration and “sharing the wonder of life.” She stresses that the women are so intertwined that their arms become difficult to distinguish.

- Upright: harvest after work or crisis; equality, support and shared experience
- Reversed: lost happiness, disappointed hopes, failed friendship, dissolution of a group, or sensual excess

[Pollack, *Seventy-Eight Degrees of Wisdom*, revised edition, pp. 218–19 in this scan](https://logoilibrary.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/07/Copia-de-Seventy-Eight-Degrees-Of-Eisdom.pdf).

### Mary K. Greer

Greer’s practice-centered work develops the card toward friendship, collaborative creativity, celebration and the support of women or peers.

Typical reversal approaches include:

- blocked or postponed celebration;
- exclusion from a group;
- over-socializing or overindulgence;
- gossip, rivalry or a friendship triangle;
- turning communal affirmation inward.

**Caution:** Greer advocates multiple ways of reading reversals; these are possibilities, not one fixed reversed definition.

### Recent popular meanings

- Upright: friendship, reunion, community, celebration, collaboration
- Reversed: gossip, cliques, exclusion, loneliness, excess
- “Third party” or infidelity: **modern popular reading**, derived from the literal number of participants rather than Waite, Mathers, Etteilla or Crowley. It requires context and is not the card’s historically primary meaning.

## Esoteric and symbolic readings

### Alchemical

**Modern Hermetic interpretation:** the three cups can represent Salt, Sulphur and Mercury, or a ternary stabilized in a receptive vessel.

No evidence was found that the Renaissance Three of Cups was designed as an alchemical diagram. Alchemical readings enter through nineteenth- and twentieth-century Hermetic synthesis and through modern interpretations of the Sola Busca.

### Kabbalistic

**Golden Dawn attribution:** three is Binah, form-giving Understanding; Cups occupy Briah. Thus emotional or creative force takes abundant form.

**Not historical Jewish teaching:** the exact mapping “Three of Cups = Binah in Briah” is a Golden Dawn construction.

### Astrological

**Golden Dawn:** Mercury in the second decan of Cancer. Mercury contributes communication, movement and exchange; Cancer supplies water, receptivity, family, memory and nurture.

**Modern interpretive extension:** “talking about feelings,” communal memory and emotionally intelligent friendship are deductions from this combination, not phrases found in the earliest manuscript.

### Hermetic

The card combines tetragrammatic suit theory, sephiroth, decan astrology, elemental color scales and ceremonial imagery. This synthesis first becomes securely visible in the late nineteenth-century Golden Dawn.

### Christian and Grail

**Documented visual possibility:** early Italian cups resemble liturgical vessels.

**Tradition:** Christian readers associate Cups with Eucharistic wine, grace, sacrifice and the Holy Grail.

**Modern spreader:** Pollack explicitly places the Grail “at the base of the suit” and reads the Three as sharing life’s wonder.

**Evidence limit:** no fifteenth-century text identifies the Three of Cups with the Grail, the Trinity or Eucharist. The parallel is symbolically coherent but historically unproved.

### Demeter and Persephone

**Crowleyan attribution:** Crowley explicitly calls the Thoth card that of Demeter or Persephone. Harris makes the cups pomegranates, turning abundance into a reminder of Persephone’s underworld bond.

This interpretation first appears as a specific Three-of-Cups doctrine in the Crowley–Harris work, not in Marseille or Waite.

### Three Graces

**Plausible source/tradition:** Smith’s three intertwined women resemble the Three Graces as a compositional type—three female bodies joined in dance or embrace.

**Disputed:** no letter or design note from Smith identifies them as Aglaea, Euphrosyne and Thalia, and no single painting has been demonstrated as the source.

### Jungian

Carl Jung did not create a canonical Three-of-Cups interpretation.

Later Jungian tarot authors use:

- the “third” as a new condition generated from opposing pairs;
- Cups as the feeling function or relational unconscious;
- the circle of women as ego supported by community;
- reversal as conformity, projection, exclusion or loss of individuality.

Karen Hamaker-Zondag’s *Tarot as a Way of Life* treats three as an active process and the Three of Cups as joyous harvested feeling. Pollack’s language of equal sharing also encouraged psychological readings. These are modern applications of Jungian concepts, not historical tarot origins.

## People and works

### Artists, printers and occultists directly attached to the card

- **Bonifacio Bembo / Bembo workshop:** conventional attribution for the Morgan Visconti–Sforza card; attribution remains debated.
- **Jean Noblet, Jean Dodal, Nicolas Conver:** major Marseille-lineage makers whose numeral-card patterns shaped later editions.
- **Etteilla / Jean-Baptiste Alliette:** first systematic published tarot cartomancer; supplied printed pip meanings.
- **S. L. MacGregor Mathers:** published an Etteilla-related meaning list and helped form the Golden Dawn system.
- **Arthur Edward Waite:** supplied the conceptual program and published meaning.
- **Pamela Colman Smith:** created the defining three-women scene.
- **Lady Frieda Harris:** created the Thoth pomegranate-lotus abstraction.
- **Aleister Crowley:** supplied the Demeter–Persephone and Mercury-in-Cancer commentary.
- **Paul Foster Case and Jessie Burns Parke:** BOTA doctrine and deck.
- **Robert Wang and Israel Regardie:** Golden Dawn reconstruction.
- **Chic and Sandra Tabatha Cicero:** later Golden Dawn reconstruction and explanatory texts.
- **Rachel Pollack:** major psychological and feminist interpreter.
- **Mary K. Greer:** influential practice- and reversal-oriented interpreter.
- **Alejandro Jodorowsky and Marianne Costa:** Marseille numerological reading.
- **Philippe Camoin:** co-creator of the Camoin–Jodorowsky deck.

### Literature, cinema and art

#### Italo Calvino, *The Castle of Crossed Destinies*

Calvino constructs narratives from tarot tableaux, especially using Visconti-style and Marseille cards. **No securely documented central Three-of-Cups episode was located** in the sources consulted. The work is highly relevant to tarot reception but should not automatically be attached to every card.

#### T. S. Eliot, *The Waste Land*

Madame Sosostris names the drowned Phoenician Sailor, Belladonna, the man with three staves, the Wheel, the one-eyed merchant and a blank card, and fails to find the Hanged Man.

**Correction:** “the man with three staves” is associated with the Waite–Smith Three of Wands, not the Three of Cups. Eliot supplies no identifiable Three-of-Cups allusion.

#### W. B. Yeats

Yeats belonged to the Golden Dawn and used its symbolic systems in poetry and ritual work. No specific, securely documented Three-of-Cups quotation was located.

#### Ingmar Bergman

Bergman’s *The Seventh Seal* uses chess, death and medieval religious imagery. A specific Three-of-Cups citation could not be verified. General resemblance to tarot imagery is insufficient evidence.

#### Jodorowsky’s films

Jodorowsky’s films use tarot, alchemical, Christian and initiatory symbolism, but this research found no securely documented shot directly quoting the Three of Cups. His strongest card-specific contribution is textual, in *The Way of Tarot*.

### Paintings and buildings

No painting or building was located that can be securely demonstrated to quote the historical Three of Cups specifically. Modern artworks frequently reproduce Smith’s three celebrants, but generalized groups of Three Graces cannot be assumed to be tarot quotations.

## Controversies and disputes

### Egyptian origin

**Legend:** Court de Gébelin claimed in 1781 that tarot preserved an Egyptian sacred book. He worked before Egyptian hieroglyphs had been deciphered and supplied speculative etymologies.

**Evidence:** northern Italian production records from 1440 onward, surviving Italian luxury cards, Italian suit structures and European iconography.

**Historical conclusion:** tarot as a 78-card trump game is a Renaissance Italian development. Earlier playing cards may ultimately have Near Eastern antecedents, including Mamluk suit structures, but that does not validate Gébelin’s Book of Thoth.

The Morgan catalogue explicitly calls the astrological Egyptian account a product of misinformation. [Morgan catalogue](https://www.themorgan.org/manuscript/158525). Gébelin’s original claim is available in [*Monde primitif*, vol. VIII](https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/Page%3ACourt_de_G%C3%A9belin_-_Monde_primitif_analys%C3%A9_et_compar%C3%A9_avec_le_monde_moderne%2C_8e_livraison.djvu/499).

### “The Lady” and Isis

An anonymous or appended eighteenth-century commentary associated a Three of Cups called *Madame* with the Queen of Heaven. Recent scholarship notes that some cards discussed in that passage appear to belong to the game of Aluette rather than tarot.

**Disputed:** this is not reliable evidence of an ancient Isiac Three of Cups. It is evidence of eighteenth-century Egyptianizing interpretation. [Monique Streiff Moretti discussion](https://traditionaltarot.wordpress.com/2020/12/25/the-isis-of-the-tarot-or-the-birth-of-a-myth/).

### Romani or “Gypsy” origin

**Legend:** occultists claimed Romani migrants preserved and transmitted the Egyptian tarot.

**History of spread:** the idea developed from the mistaken European belief that Roma came from Egypt and was amplified by nineteenth-century occult writers, especially Papus’s title *Le Tarot des Bohémiens*.

**Evidence:** no medieval or early Renaissance document links Roma with tarot’s invention. The earliest documentary chain instead concerns Italian courts, workshops and gaming.

### Cathar, Templar and heretical transmission

**Legend/disputed claim:** tarot is sometimes described as an encoded Cathar gospel, Templar secret book or survival of a persecuted mystery school.

**Evidence found:** none of the earliest ledgers, surviving cards, rules, sermons or literary notices identifies such a transmission. The subjects and suits fit ordinary late-medieval court, civic and Christian culture without requiring a clandestine organization.

### Sola Busca as the source of Waite–Smith

**Strongly supported in part:** Smith probably knew the 1907 British Museum photographs, and individual parallels exist.

**Overstatement:** “the Sola Busca minors are the source of all Waite–Smith minors.”

**Unsupported card-specific version:** “the Sola Busca Three of Cups supplied Smith’s Three of Cups.” The images do not match in the necessary way.

### Three Graces identification

**Plausible but unproved:** Smith’s figures resemble the Three Graces.

**Modern tradition:** they are often simply called maidens, friends, sisters, witches or women at harvest. None of these identities is named on the 1909 card.

### Pumpkin, grapes and harvest

**Documented:** fruit and vegetation are present.

**Interpretive:** assigning the scene to Samhain, a specific pagan festival, a wedding or a grape harvest is speculative. Waite says garden and merriment, not a named festival.

### “Third party” and infidelity

**Modern invention/popular reading:** the card is now frequently treated as a love triangle or affair card.

Earlier sources give success, abundance, victory, celebration, pleasure and sometimes excess; they do not make adultery its defining meaning. The interpretation arises through literalization of “three people,” often strengthened by surrounding cards such as the Devil, Seven of Swords or Three of Swords.

### Misprints and inherited errors

- Grand Etteilla card numbers and keywords vary by edition, so a later keyword may be falsely presented as Etteilla’s one original wording.
- OCR often corrupts degree ranges such as 10°–20° into “100–200.”
- Modern sites confuse Cancer’s sign ruler, the Moon, with this decan’s Golden Dawn ruler, Mercury.
- Some sources mistakenly call the Thoth card Venus in Cancer or Moon in Cancer.
- Claims that the Three of Cups itself bears Hebrew Heh confuse a suit attribution with the trump-letter series.
- Some reproductions call reconstructed missing cards authentic survivors.
- “Visconti di Modrone” and “Cary–Yale” are alternate names for the same pack, but are sometimes listed as separate decks.

### Historians’ corrections

Michael Dummett’s central correction was methodological: tarot must first be explained as a card game documented in Renaissance Italy. Ronald Decker and Thierry Depaulis traced how Gébelin, Etteilla and later occultists constructed the occult tarot. Stuart Kaplan catalogued decks and made early objects broadly accessible. Robert M. Place and Helen Farley combine art history with close attention to later esoteric reception.

These historians do not prove that occult meanings are spiritually “false”; they show that those meanings are later constructions rather than the original documented purpose of the cards.

## Open questions

1. Is the Morgan M.630.25 conclusively the earliest surviving physical Three of Cups, or does a securely catalogued Cary–Yale or Brambilla Three predate it?

2. Can a primary British Museum exhibition or reading-room record prove that Pamela Colman Smith personally examined the Sola Busca photographs, rather than merely establish that they were available?

3. Did Smith consciously intend the Three Graces, or did the resemblance arise from broader theatrical and decorative conventions?

4. Which wording belongs to Etteilla’s own earliest Three-of-Cups table, and which keywords first entered later Grand Etteilla editions?

5. When did “third party” or infidelity first appear in print as a Three-of-Cups meaning? It was not found in the principal Etteilla, Mathers, Golden Dawn, Waite or Crowley texts.

6. When was the Grail first attached specifically to the whole Cups suit, rather than cups being treated generally as water, love or religious vessels? Pollack spreads it widely, but she does not appear to be the first possible occurrence.

7. Are any modern claims of direct Three-of-Cups quotations in architecture or cinema based on documented artist statements, as opposed to visual resemblance?

8. Surviving Golden Dawn manuscripts have variant wording and later editorial layers. A full stemmatic comparison of *Book T*, the *Equinox* text, Regardie and later reconstructions remains desirable.
