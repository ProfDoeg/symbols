# Queen of Cups: Research Dossier

## Method and evidentiary labels

This dossier uses the following labels:

- **[Documented fact]** Surviving card, archival record, rulebook, or text directly establishes the claim.
- **[Scholarly reconstruction]** Historical conclusion inferred from objects and documents but not explicitly stated by contemporary witnesses.
- **[Tradition]** A persistent convention whose historical origin may be recoverable only in part.
- **[Esoteric attribution]** A correspondence deliberately assigned by an occult author or school.
- **[Disputed claim]** Proposed identification or relationship for which evidence is divided or insufficient.
- **[Legend]** Historically unsupported origin story transmitted as fact or sacred history.
- **[Modern invention]** Interpretation or visual feature first found in recent tarot culture.

The Queen of Cups is a court card, so most controversies concerning the sequence and numbering of the trumps apply only to the pack surrounding it. They are included where requested but should not be mistaken for properties of this card.

| French | **Reine de Coupe**, more usually **Reine de Coupes**; older rules also **Royne** |
| English | **Queen of Cups**; Golden Dawn title **Queen of the Thrones of the Waters** |

| Tetragrammaton formula | **Heh of Heh**: Queen = first Heh; Cups/Water = first Heh in the suit series |

| Decan cards governed | Ten of Swords, Two of Cups, Three of Cups |

| Earliest tarot context | Northern Italian *carte da trionfi*, documented by 1440–1442 |

The Morgan describes its Visconti-Sforza card simply and securely: a “Queen, wearing crown, seated, holds vessel in right hand.” It is by Bonifacio Bembo, Milan, approximately 1450–1480, 173 × 87 mm. The card bears neither title nor number. [Documented fact: [Morgan object record](https://www.themorgan.org/collection/tarot-cards/the-queen-of-cups); [Morgan manuscript record](https://ica.themorgan.org/manuscript/page/16/158525).]

### What “Heh of Heh” means

**[Esoteric attribution, nineteenth-century Hermetic tradition]** Éliphas Lévi connected the four court ranks with the four letters of the divine name Yod-Heh-Vav-Heh. Later Golden Dawn teaching systematized both ranks and suits:

| Series | Yod | Heh | Vav | Final Heh |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Court rank | King/Knight | Queen | Prince/Knight | Princess/Page |
| Suit | Wands/Fire | Cups/Water | Swords/Air | Pentacles/Earth |

Thus the Queen of Cups is **Heh of Heh**, or Water of Water. This is not written on any Renaissance Queen of Cups. It is an occult overlay, historically downstream from Lévi and fully articulated in Golden Dawn *Book T* material.

## Iconography, deck by deck

### Islamic and European antecedents

#### Mamluk playing cards, probably fifteenth-century Egypt, incorporating an older pattern

**[Documented fact]** The nearly complete Mamluk pack commonly associated with the Topkapı collection contains four suits—cups, coins, swords, and polo-sticks—and three court ranks: *malik* (king), *nāʾib malik* (deputy king), and *thānī nāʾib* (second deputy). The courts use ornamental suit-signs and calligraphy rather than human portraits.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** European Latin-suited cards derive substantially from this family of Islamic cards. The suit of Cups is therefore older than tarot. The European Queen is not a direct translation of a Mamluk Queen, because the Mamluk hierarchy contained no female rank. The substitution and expansion of human courts occurred in Europe. Dummett describes European cards as arriving from the Islamic world in the later fourteenth century with broadly the same four-suit structure but different court figures. [Dummett, [“The History of Card Games”](https://ideas.repec.org/a/cup/eurrev/v1y1993i02p125-135_00.html); L. A. Mayer, [*Mamluk Playing Cards*](https://www.persee.fr/doc/bifao_0255-0962_1939_num_38_1_1998).]

**[Disputed etymology]** Italian *naibi* and Spanish *naipes* have often been connected to Arabic *nāʾib*, “deputy.” This is plausible and widely repeated, but specialists have debated the exact path and whether the European plural derived directly from a court-rank word.

#### The Queen in European packs

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** The Queen was an especially visible innovation because ordinary Italian- and Spanish-suited packs generally came to use King, Cavalier and Fante/Sota rather than a Queen. Tarot retained four courts—King, Queen, Knight and Page—from its earliest surviving form.

This requires qualification:

- **[Documented fact]** Fifteenth-century tarot packs have Queens.
- **[Documented fact]** Standard modern Spanish packs normally use *rey, caballo, sota*, without a queen.
- **[Documented fact]** Many regional Italian packs likewise lack queens.
- **[Caution]** It is too broad to say that medieval Italy never had queens in ordinary cards. European court structures were locally variable, and French-suited packs made the queen standard.

### Marziano da Tortona and Michelino da Besozzo, Milan, approximately 1412–1425

Marziano’s description of a pack devised for Filippo Maria Visconti is the earliest known extended description of a special trump-like card pack. It assigned sixteen classical gods to four allegorical suits and was painted by Michelino da Besozzo.

**[Documented fact]** Marziano’s text survives indirectly through the copy sent by Jacopo Antonio Marcello in 1449.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** It is an ancestor or close precursor of tarot’s permanent-trump idea.

**[Not applicable]** It did not contain the canonical Queen of Cups. Calling any Marziano figure “the first Queen of Cups” would be anachronistic. The work belongs in tarot prehistory, not in the direct iconographic lineage of this card. [Andrea Vitali, [Italian origins of the *Ludus Triumphorum*](https://letarot.it/Le-origini-italiane-del-Ludus-Triumphorum_pag_pg439_ita.aspx).]

### Visconti di Modrone / Cary-Yale pack, Milan, approximately 1441–1447

This incomplete luxury pack now belongs to Yale’s Cary Collection. Its surviving structure is exceptional: alongside King, Queen, male Knight and male Page, it included a female Knight and female Page in each suit. The surviving pack has 67 cards.

**[Documented fact]** Queens formed part of this enlarged, gender-balanced court.

**[Caution]** Modern 86-card “Cary-Yale” editions restore missing cards. A reproduced Queen of Cups in such an edition must not automatically be treated as an original surviving card. The sources consulted clearly document the expanded courts but did not furnish a Yale object record securely identifying a surviving original Queen of Cups. [Cary-Yale pack description](https://www.learntarot.org/cydesc.htm); [Yale Cary Collection information](https://beinecke.library.yale.edu/collections/highlights/cary-playing-card-database).]

**Meaning of the change:** The paired male and female attendants have been interpreted as a dynastic or nuptial program, possibly connected with Visconti-Sforza marriage politics. That is a scholarly hypothesis, not an inscription on the pack.

### Brambilla-Brera pack, Milan, approximately 1440s

**[Documented fact]** The Brambilla group is a fragmentary luxury tarot associated with Filippo Maria Visconti and now at the Pinacoteca di Brera.

**[Negative finding]** The sources retrieved did not establish a surviving Brambilla Queen of Cups. General statements implying that every card of the canonical pack survives in each Visconti group are false. Fragmentary packs must be handled card by card.

### Visconti-Sforza / Pierpont Morgan–Bergamo pack, Milan, approximately 1450–1480

This is the earliest securely catalogued Queen of Cups located in this research.

**What is depicted:** A crowned, seated woman in an elaborate gold robe. She turns toward the large covered or deep vessel held upright in her right hand. The gold ground and punched decoration establish a luxury court object, not a cheap printed playing card. No water landscape is present; no fish, shell, angel-handles, lotus or crayfish appears. [Documented fact: [Morgan image and catalogue](https://www.themorgan.org/collection/tarot-cards/the-queen-of-cups).]

**What it probably meant originally:** The literal visual proposition is rank plus suit: queen + cup. The card’s primary function was to identify a high court card during play.

#### Was she a specific Visconti or Sforza woman?

**[Disputed claim]** Gertrude Moakley and Stuart R. Kaplan helped popularize attempts to read Visconti-Sforza figures as members of the ruling families, especially Bianca Maria Visconti, Francesco Sforza and their court.

The Queen of Cups is sometimes identified as Bianca Maria Visconti. Evidence cited consists of:

- the deck’s Visconti and Sforza emblems;
- its probable dynastic commission;
- resemblance to courtly dress and portrait types;
- the fact that Bianca Maria was the dynasty’s most important living woman.

Against a literal portrait identification:

- the card has no name or heraldic label identifying Bianca Maria;
- the four queens are differentiated by suit attributes rather than securely individualized portrait evidence;
- workshop convention can explain similar facial types;
- the Morgan does not identify the sitter.

**Conclusion:** “Bianca Maria” is a possible dynastic association, not a documented identification. Moakley’s wider reading of the pack through Petrarchan triumph and court pageantry remains historically influential but does not prove individual court-card portraits.

### Ferrarese and Este material, fifteenth century

Court ledgers record painted *trionfi* in Ferrara in 1442. On 10 February Jacopo Sagramoro was paid for painting cups, swords, coins, batons and the figures of four packs; on 28 July a merchant supplied a pair of *carte da trionfi* for Ercole and Sigismondo d’Este. [Documented fact: Farley’s transcription and discussion in [*A Cultural History of Tarot*](https://e-isomero.com/pdf/book/1611846398.pdf); Vitali, [Italian origins](https://letarot.it/Le-origini-italiane-del-Ludus-Triumphorum_pag_pg439_ita.aspx).]

**[Negative finding]** These ledgers establish suits and figures but do not name a Queen of Cups individually.

#### Charles VI / Estensi cards

The so-called Charles VI tarot is Italian, probably Florentine or Ferrarese, not a deck made for Charles VI of France. Its conventional name arose from an obsolete provenance theory.

**[Contested nomenclature]** “Charles VI tarot” is therefore a historical catalogue-name that can mislead. The fragments do not supply a securely relevant Queen of Cups.

### Mantegna “Tarocchi,” approximately 1460s

**[Documented fact]** The so-called Mantegna Tarocchi are fifty educational or emblematic engravings arranged into hierarchical series.

**[Scholarly consensus]** They are neither by Andrea Mantegna nor an ordinary tarot pack. They contain no four suits and no Queen of Cups. Their relevance is contextual: they show the fifteenth-century taste for ordered allegorical image series. Calling one of their female figures a Queen of Cups is modern free association.

### Sola Busca, engraved circa 1490; painted copy dated 1491

The Sola Busca is the earliest substantially complete surviving 78-card tarot and the earliest surviving pack in which the pip cards are fully illustrated with scenes.

**What is depicted:** Its Queen of Cups bears the inscription **POLISENA**, usually read as Polyxena, the Trojan princess. She is a fully figured historicizing or mythological personage rather than an anonymous suit queen. Her elaborate cup has been reported as containing or emitting a serpentine form.

**[Documented fact]** The card is named Polisena.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** Polyxena is the likely referent.

**[Disputed symbolic reading]** Alchemical readings of the serpent and vessel are common in modern Sola Busca scholarship, but no surviving fifteenth-century key states that the card represents an alchemical operation. The deck’s antique names, military figures, strange vessels and transformations invite such interpretation without proving a systematic alchemical code. [Sola Busca card list and history](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sola_Busca_tarot); comparative discussion of [Polisena](https://www.thequeenssword.com/sola-busca-tarot-by-mayer/).]

#### Did Sola Busca inspire Pamela Colman Smith?

**[Strong scholarly reconstruction]** Photographs of the Sola Busca were acquired by the British Museum in 1907, and Smith created her tarot in 1909. Several RWS minors have close compositional counterparts, especially Three of Swords and Ten of Wands/Ten of Swords. Smith probably saw the photographs or display.

**[Open card-specific question]** Resemblance between Polisena concentrating on an ornate vessel and Smith’s Queen is suggestive, but no letter or design notebook says Smith copied this particular card. It is safer to call Sola Busca a probable general source and a possible card-specific source.

### Rosenwald and Budapest sheets, approximately 1500

These printed sheets are crucial evidence for the transition from luxury hand-painted tarot to repeated popular patterns.

**[Documented fact]** They preserve rows of repeated court and trump designs. Their queens include recognizable profile or frontal court types.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** The Budapest Queen’s arm, hand and veil have been compared with an early French Queen of Cups, while the Rosenwald queen supplies an Italian profile comparison. Such comparisons help reconstruct transmission toward Lyon and the Marseille pattern.

**[Caution]** A sheet figure identified generically as a Queen does not always preserve enough suit-sign context to identify it securely as the Queen of Cups. [Discussion with Depaulis’s French criteria and sheet comparisons](https://www.tarotforum.net/threads/2-c-1500-bnf-cards-french.253696/).]

### Boiardo-Viti tarot, late fifteenth century

The poetic tarot designed around Matteo Maria Boiardo’s program assigned named ancient or biblical women to queens.

**[Tradition/documented literary program]** The Queen of Cups is often listed as **Judith**. This is a named literary recoding of the rank, not the origin of the anonymous Queen of Cups.

### Tarot de Marseille lineage

#### Jean Noblet, Paris, approximately 1659

The BnF calls Noblet’s pack the oldest known surviving example conforming to the graphic type conventionally called Tarot de Marseille, although it was made in Paris. It survives as 73 of 78 woodcut-and-stencil cards. [Documented fact: [BnF Noblet record](https://essentiels.bnf.fr/fr/image/4799f2d6-6f88-4f6f-a15c-40a1a943c95e-tarot-jean-noblet); [BnF authority record](https://catalogue.bnf.fr/ark:/12148/cb166981541).]

**Queen of Cups type:** A crowned queen, usually frontal or nearly frontal, displays a tall cup. The court is emblematic and hieratic. There is no seaside, no throne covered with marine carvings and no Golden Dawn apparatus.

#### Jean Dodal, Lyon, approximately 1701–1715

Dodal retains the conventional **REYNE DE COUPE** type: crowned seated woman, court dress, conspicuous chalice. Compared with Noblet, the outlines are heavier and the palette and details reflect Lyonnais production.

**Meaning:** Still principally a suit-and-rank image for play. Later readers may treat her gaze toward the cup as contemplation, but no Dodal divinatory manual establishes that meaning.

#### Nicolas Conver: the “1760” problem

Numerous modern reproductions date the Conver tarot to 1760. The BnF authority record identifies Nicolas Conver as born in 1784 and active in Marseille from 1809 to 1833; it explicitly says that the deck dated “1760” cannot be attributed to him, an ancestor or a namesake. [Documented bibliographical correction: [BnF Conver record](https://catalogue.bnf.fr/ark:/12148/cb16721159s).]

**[Misprint or inherited commercial tradition]** “Nicolas Conver, 1760” is therefore not a secure original publication date. It became conventional through later plates, reproductions and commercial histories.

The Conver-type Queen preserves the crowned woman and cup but regularizes printing and coloration within the Marseille II family.

#### Grimaud, nineteenth and twentieth centuries

Grimaud commercial editions standardized a Conver-derived Marseille pattern for mass circulation. The Queen remains a formal seated monarch holding the cup; line, palette and labels become mechanically consistent.

**[Tradition]** Many twentieth-century readers came to regard this industrially stabilized design as “the ancient Tarot de Marseille,” although it is one late branch of a longer family.

#### Camoin-Jodorowsky, published 1997

Philippe Camoin and Alejandro Jodorowsky presented their deck as a “restoration” of the Tarot de Marseille.

**[Documented modern redesign]** They reconstructed and redrew a complete pattern using historical Marseille materials, intensified colors and inserted or clarified minute symbolic details.

**[Disputed claim]** The stronger suggestion that the reconstruction recovers a single original, intentional sacred code is not established by a surviving prototype or historical key. It is simultaneously historical reconstruction and modern esoteric redaction.

For Jodorowsky and Marianne Costa, the Queen is receptive mastery, looking into or acting upon her cup; the cup can signify emotional and spiritual life. This is a twentieth-/twenty-first-century interpretive system, not a recovered medieval title.

### Besançon pattern, eighteenth and nineteenth centuries

The Besançon pattern chiefly differs from Marseille in replacing controversial trumps:

- Papess → **Juno**
- Pope → **Jupiter**

These substitutions developed in regions where explicitly papal figures were objectionable, especially in parts of Switzerland, Alsace and southern Germany during the eighteenth century.

**[Not card-specific]** The Queen of Cups generally remains a Queen of Cups. The Juno/Jupiter renaming is relevant to deck genealogy, not to her identity.

### Minchiate, Florence and Tuscany, sixteenth–nineteenth centuries

Minchiate expands the trump sequence to forty trumps but retains four Latin suits and their courts.

**Queen of Cups:** *Regina di Coppe*, below King, above Horse and Fante/Fantina. It remains an unnumbered suit court. The image is a crowned woman bearing the cup.

**[Documented game fact]** Cups and Coins use reverse numeral ranking: Ace high among numerals, then 2 downward to 10; court ranking remains King–Queen–Horse–Jack. [Minchiate rules](https://www.pagat.com/tarot/minchiate.html).]

The extra trumps do not turn the Queen into a numbered arcanum.

### Bolognese tarocchino

Bolognese tarot shortened the pack by removing low numeral cards but retained the court hierarchy.

**[Documented general pattern]** The Queen of Cups is a high suit honour below the King. Bolognese double-headed cards increasingly sacrificed narrative detail for easy play.

**[Negative finding]** No special Bolognese occult identity for the Queen of Cups was located. Bolognese divinatory traditions existed, but the later Golden Dawn reading should not be projected onto them.

### Sicilian tarot

The traditional 63-card Sicilian pack has:

- King (*Re*)
- Queen (*Regina*)
- Horse (*Cavallo*)
- Maid (*Donna*)

The Queen of Cups is therefore explicitly present and ranks immediately below the King.

The suit is *coppe*. The modern rules rank the cards K–Q–C–Donna–10–9–8–7–6–5. [Documented fact: [Sicilian Tarocchi rules](https://www.pagat.com/tarot/sicilian.html).]

### Grand Etteilla, Paris, first type circa 1788–1789

Etteilla did not merely interpret a Marseille pack; he created a reordered, retitled divinatory pack claiming to restore the Egyptian Book of Thoth.

The Queen of Cups survives as card **37**:

- **La Reine de Coupe**
- cross-referenced to **La Reine de Cœur**
- upright inscription: **FEMME BLONDE**
- reversed inscription: **FEMME DU HOMME EN PLACE**

The surviving printed manual/image shows a crowned seated woman with cup in an interior, with drapery and furniture rather than Waite’s sea. [Documented fact: [*Le Grand Etteilla* scan](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/2/2f/Le_grand_Etteilla%2C_ou%2C_l%27art_de_tirer_les_cartes_..._Le_tout_recueilli_..._%28IA_b29321220%29.pdf).]

**Important distinction:** Here the number 37 is a deck-wide occult order, not the card’s traditional game number.

### Oswald Wirth, 1889 and 1926

**[Documented fact]** Wirth’s 1889 *Les 22 Arcanes du Tarot Kabbalistique* comprised only the twenty-two trumps. It therefore contained no Queen of Cups. Modern 78-card “Wirth” decks append minors and courts designed by later publishers. [1889 Wirth archive](https://foolmade.art/archive/wirth-tarot); [Wirth deck history](https://www.learntarot.org/osdesc.htm).]

**[Common error]** Describing a modern Wirth-branded Queen of Cups as Wirth’s 1889 design is false.

### Golden Dawn manuscript designs, late 1880s–1890s

The Golden Dawn’s *Book T* supplies the decisive esoteric transformation:

**Title:** “Queen of the Thrones of the Waters,” also “Queen of the Nymphs and Undines.”

**Image:** A fair crowned queen seated upon a throne above flowing water and lotuses. She carries the Ace-of-Cups vessel; a serpent emerges from it and bends toward the water.

**Attribution:** Water of Water, Heh of Heh, 20° Gemini–20° Cancer.

**Meaning:** Receptivity, reflection, imagination, dream, adaptability; when ill-dignified, distortion, inconsistency and unreliability.

This image is unusually close in concept to Crowley’s eventual card. [Golden Dawn text in Regardie, [*Complete Golden Dawn System of Magic*](https://scriptaetveritas.com.br/wp-content/uploads/2016/04/ISRAEL_REGARDIE_COMPLETE_GOLDEN_DAWN_SYSTEM_OF_MAGIC.pdf).]

#### Wang and Cicero reconstructions

- Robert Wang’s *Golden Dawn Tarot* reconstructs the order’s designs from documentary descriptions.
- Sandra Tabatha Cicero’s *Golden Dawn Magical Tarot* offers a later reconstruction embedded in the Ciceros’ Golden Dawn curriculum.

**[Modern reconstructions]** Neither deck is an original nineteenth-century Golden Dawn pack. Their authority lies in following manuscript specifications, with artistic decisions added where the papers are incomplete or inconsistent.

### Waite-Smith, London, 1909

Pamela Colman Smith drew the card under Arthur Edward Waite’s direction.

**What is depicted:**

- a crowned queen seated at the edge of the sea;
- a massive stone throne carved with water-nymph or mer-child figures, fish and shell forms;
- pebbles beneath her feet;
- a large, ornate, covered cup with winged or angelic handles;
- her gaze directed intently toward the cup;
- her feet on land rather than in the water.

Waite’s description begins: “Beautiful, fair, dreamy—as one who sees visions in a cup.” He immediately qualifies passivity: “she sees, but she also acts.” [Documented primary text: Waite, [*Pictorial Key*, Queen of Cups](https://www.sacred-texts.com/tarot/pkt/pktcuqu.htm).]

#### Sources of Smith’s drawing

- **[Documented]** It belongs to the Golden Dawn milieu, whose Queen already sat above water holding a special cup.
- **[Strong reconstruction]** Smith likely knew the Marseille court type.
- **[Strong general reconstruction]** Sola Busca photographs were available at the British Museum from 1907 and influenced several RWS minors.
- **[Possible card-specific influence]** Polisena’s concentrated attention to an ornate cup may lie behind Smith’s design.
- **[Disputed]** The cup is variously called a ciborium, reliquary, Grail, Ark-like vessel or baptismal font. It resembles Christian liturgical metalwork, but Waite does not identify it as any one of these.
- **[Modern invention]** The claim that it is “the only closed cup in the entire deck” is overstated. It is the most conspicuously sealed and shrine-like vessel, but historical Marseille cups and ornamental vessels complicate absolute statements.

The decisive innovation is environmental: the queen has moved from an abstract court ground to the liminal shore. Water now supplies a visual basis for later psychological readings about emotion and the unconscious.

### Paul Foster Case and BOTA, twentieth century

The BOTA deck was designed by Paul Foster Case and drawn by Jessie Burns Parke. It closely follows Waite-Smith in the trumps but returns the numbered minors to suit emblems rather than narrative scenes.

**Queen of Cups:** Part of the full deck but not one of the major keys on which Case concentrated most of his published exposition.

**[Esoteric continuity]** Case retains the Golden Dawn elemental and Qabalistic framework.

**[Negative finding]** A reliable card-specific upright-and-reversed paragraph by Case was not located in the accessible primary material. *Highlights of Tarot* explicitly concentrates on the twenty-two Major Arcana. Assigning modern BOTA keywords to Case without an edition and page would be unsafe. [BOTA, [*Highlights of Tarot*](https://storebota.org/products/highlights-of-tarot); [BOTA history](https://www.bota.org/bota/history).]

### Thoth tarot, painted 1938–1943; first full publication 1969

Aleister Crowley designed the deck; Lady Frieda Harris painted it.

**What is depicted:**

- a queen almost dissolved into curvilinear veils of reflected light;
- enthronement on still water;
- a shell-like cup;
- a crayfish issuing from it;
- the Lotus of Isis;
- layered reflections that make figure, water and reflected image difficult to separate.

Crowley writes that she is the “watery part of Water,” expressing its “power of reception and reflection.” Her zodiacal realm extends from the twenty-first degree of Gemini through the twentieth degree of Cancer. [Primary text: Crowley, [*Book of Thoth*, Court Cards](https://hermetic.com/crowley/book-of-thoth/court-cards); [facsimile PDF](https://www.rahoorkhuit.net/library/crowley/equinox/volume3/vol_3_no_05.pdf).]

The crayfish alludes simultaneously to Cancer, lunar water and the creature emerging from the pool in the traditional Moon trump. Harris transforms the queen into a field of reflection rather than a Renaissance person holding a suit-sign.

### Notable modern redesigns

#### Motherpeace, Karen Vogel and Vicki Noble, 1981

**[Modern invention within feminist tarot]** Hierarchical European courts are reframed through goddess spirituality, matrifocality and cyclical ritual. The Queen-of-Cups function becomes a shamanic or maternal water figure rather than a feudal monarch.

#### Daughters of the Moon, Ffiona Morgan, 1984

The card participates in explicitly feminist, Goddess-centered tarot. Emotional and intuitive authority is decoupled from obedience to a king.

#### Thoth-derived decks

Many Thoth descendants preserve Water of Water, shell, lotus, Cancer and mirror imagery. Others emphasize sirens, ocean goddesses or psychic permeability.

#### Contemporary RWS descendants

The most common redesigns preserve the shore and closed cup while recoding the Queen as:

- empath;
- therapist or counselor;
- emotional-boundary keeper;
- intuitive artist;
- queer or nonbinary sovereign;
- self-care archetype.

These are useful living traditions but chiefly late twentieth- and twenty-first-century psychological developments.

## The game

### Rank

In traditional Italian-suited tarot, French tarot and most tarock descendants, the Queen ranks:

**King > Queen > Knight/Cavalier > Jack/Page**

The Queen of Cups is therefore the second-highest card of its suit.

In the oldest printed French rules associated with the Abbé de Marolles, approximately 1637, the courts are *Roy, Royne, Cheualier, Faon*. In Cups and Coins the numeral order is reversed, but the four courts retain their superior order. [Documented fact: [Marolles rules](https://www.pagat.com/tarot/marolles.html).]

### Card-point value

In the standard tarot counting system:

- King: 5 points
- Queen: 4 points
- Knight: 3 points
- Jack: 2 points
- ordinary cards: 1 point nominally, with batch deductions producing half-point accounting in French tarot

Thus the Queen of Cups contributes **4 card points** when paired with a low card under modern French counting. [French rules](https://www.pagat.com/tarot/frtarot.html); [comparative counting](https://www.pagat.com/tarot/counting.html).]

Historical regional methods vary in how cards are grouped and deductions made, but the Queen is consistently a valuable court honour.

### Suit ranking

In many early Italian and French tarot games:

- Swords and Batons: numerals descend 10–1.
- Cups and Coins: numerals descend 1–10.

The Queen stands above all numerals regardless of this reversal.

### Minchiate

The Queen of Cups ranks below King, above Horse and Jack. Minchiate’s expanded trump series does not alter the suit court. [Minchiate rules](https://www.pagat.com/tarot/minchiate.html).]

### Sicilian tarot

The Queen ranks below King and above Horse and Maid. The modern 63-card pattern gives the Queen the usual court value within its distinctive counting system. [Sicilian rules](https://www.pagat.com/tarot/sicilian.html).]

### Tarock

In central-European French-suited tarock packs the Italian Cup suit disappears visually, becoming Hearts. Functionally the comparable card is the Queen of Hearts. German terminology is *Herzdame* rather than *Königin der Kelche*. Occult German decks that preserve Latin suits use *Königin der Kelche*.

### Trump-order disputes do not affect this card

The Queen of Cups is never a trump in traditional tarot games. The regional order problem concerns the twenty-one permanent trumps.

Dummett grouped early orders into:

- **Order A:** associated broadly with Bologna, Florence and southern traditions.
- **Order B:** Ferrara and the eastern/northeastern sphere.
- **Order C:** Milan and regions leading toward the Marseille pattern.

Exact local orders vary. Justice, the virtues, Angel/Judgment and World move substantially; unnumbered early packs require reconstruction from lists and later rules. [Scholarly reconstruction: [overview of the three orders](https://pre-gebelin.blogspot.com/2007/11/three-regional-traditions.html).]

### Justice and Strength

Traditional Italian and Marseille numbering normally has:

- VIII Justice
- XI Strength

Waite exchanged them:

- VIII Strength
- XI Justice

**[Esoteric redesign]** The swap enabled Golden Dawn astrological sequencing: Leo for Strength at VIII and Libra for Justice at XI. Crowley retained that order. This has no bearing on the Queen’s rank.

### Fool

The Fool is normally unnumbered and is not an ordinary trump in traditional play. Its special role often allows it to be played without following suit and then recovered by its owner, subject to local rules. It is called *Matto*, *Le Mat*, *Excuse*, *Sküs*, *Fuggitivo*, and other regional names.

**[Common simplification]** Calling it “trump 0” describes occult and modern deck numbering, not its original game function.

## Chronology of attestation

### Before tarot

- **Late fourteenth century:** Playing cards appear in Europe. Their Latin suit system is related to the Mamluk suits of cups, coins, swords and polo-sticks. **[Documented fact plus scholarly reconstruction]**
- **Mamluk antecedent:** Three male or abstractly indicated court ranks; no Queen. **[Documented fact]**

### Approximately 1412–1425

Marziano da Tortona designs a mythological card pack for Filippo Maria Visconti; Michelino da Besozzo paints it. It contains neither a canonical Queen of Cups nor the later twenty-two-card trump sequence. **[Documented precursor]**

### 1440

A Florentine record discovered by Franco Pratesi records *naibi a trionfi* supplied to Sigismondo Pandolfo Malatesta. This now precedes the once-standard 1442 “first mention.” **[Documented fact]**

### 1441–1447

Probable period of the Cary-Yale and Brambilla luxury packs. Queens are present in the tarot court hierarchy. **[Object-based dating; exact year disputed]**

### 10 February 1442

Ferrara account: Jacopo Sagramoro is paid for coloring and painting cups, swords, coins, batons and figures for four packs of trumps. **[Documented fact]**

### 28 July 1442

Ferrara court pays Marchione Burdochi for a pair of *carte da trionfi* for Ercole and Sigismondo d’Este. **[Documented fact]**

### Approximately 1450–1480

Morgan MS M.630.16, the securely surviving Visconti-Sforza Queen of Cups, is produced in Milan. **[Documented object]**

### Approximately 1450–1470

The anonymous Dominican *Sermones de ludo cum aliis* condemns games and lists the trump subjects.

**[Documented fact]** It is the earliest surviving complete or near-complete textual trump list.

**[Important limitation]** It does not individually list the Queen of Cups, since she is an ordinary court card rather than a trump.

### 1490–1491

Sola Busca produced; Queen of Cups named **Polisena**. **[Documented object]**

### Approximately 1500

Rosenwald and Budapest sheets document printed tarot court types. **[Documented objects]**

### 1505

*Taraux* is recorded in French and *tarochi* in Ferrara documents. The word *tarocchi* gradually replaces *trionfi*. **[Documented nomenclature]**

### Early sixteenth century

Teofilo Folengo uses tarot cards and their names poetically. *Tarocchi appropriati* poems apply cards to named persons. **[Documented literary reception]** These works demonstrate social familiarity and metaphorical use; they are not proof of an ancient occult system.

### 1637

Marolles rules furnish the oldest known printed rules for tarot play, explicitly naming *la Royne* among the honours. **[Documented fact]**

### Approximately 1659

Noblet produces the earliest surviving Marseille-type pack. **[Documented object]**

### 1701–1715

Dodal produces Marseille-type packs at Lyon. **[Documented object]**

### Eighteenth century

Besançon patterns replace Papess/Pope with Juno/Jupiter. **[Documented regional adaptation]**

### 1770

Etteilla publishes on cartomancy with ordinary cards. Queen-of-Hearts traditions feed into his later Queen of Cups. **[Documented textual development]**

### 1781

Court de Gébelin and the Comte de Mellet publish tarot essays in volume VIII of *Monde primitif*.

- Gébelin announces an Egyptian origin.
- De Mellet offers a Hebrew-letter scheme for the trumps.
- Neither supplies the later Water-of-Water Queen doctrine.

### 1788–1789

Grand Etteilla appears as the first tarot pack deliberately designed for divination. Queen of Cups/Hearts is card 37, *Femme blonde*. **[Documented fact]**

### 1854–1856

Éliphas Lévi publishes *Dogme et Rituel de la Haute Magie*. Tarot is integrated with Kabbalah; court cards are explained as familial ranks.

### 1860

Lévi’s *Clefs majeures et clavicules de Salomon* more explicitly applies Tetragrammaton symbolism to court ranks. Queen = Heh. **[Esoteric attribution]**

### 1863 and 1870

Paul Christian develops pseudo-Egyptian initiatory tarot narratives in *L’Homme rouge des Tuileries* and *Histoire de la magie*. His “ancient initiation” is literary occult construction rather than a translation of an Egyptian temple text.

### 1888

S. L. MacGregor Mathers publishes *The Tarot: Its Occult Signification, Use in Fortune-Telling, and Method of Play*.

### 1889

Papus publishes *Le Tarot des Bohémiens*; Wirth publishes a 22-trump deck. Neither Wirth’s original deck nor Papus’s primary structural argument gives us the later pictorial RWS Queen.

### Late 1880s–1890s

Golden Dawn *Book T* develops the Queen of the Thrones of the Waters, Water of Water, 20° Gemini–20° Cancer.

### 1909–1911

Waite-Smith deck published in 1909; Waite’s illustrated *Pictorial Key* follows in 1910, frequently dated 1911 from the edition title page.

### 1938–1943 / 1969

Crowley and Harris create Thoth; complete deck first published in 1969.

### 1980–1983

Rachel Pollack publishes the two original volumes of *Seventy-Eight Degrees of Wisdom*, strongly contributing to psychological and experiential tarot.

### 1997

Camoin-Jodorowsky Marseille reconstruction published.

### Tree of Life caution

**[Common error]** Court cards do not have Hebrew-letter “paths” in the way the twenty-two trumps do in Golden Dawn Qabalah.

Queens as a class correspond especially to **Binah**, the Great Mother and the second Heh-associated receptive principle. But “Queen of Cups = a unique path from Sephirah X to Y” is not standard *Book T* doctrine.

### Decans

The Queen governs:

- **Ten of Swords:** 20°–30° Gemini; Golden Dawn title “Lord of Ruin”
- **Two of Cups:** 0°–10° Cancer; “Lord of Love”
- **Three of Cups:** 10°–20° Cancer; “Lord of Abundance”

These three cards form the Queen’s thirty-degree span. This does not mean she is simply “Cancer.” One third of her range lies in Gemini.

### Tzaddi/Heh controversy

Crowley’s statement “Tzaddi is not the Star” led him to exchange the Hebrew-letter correspondences of the Emperor and Star:

- traditional Golden Dawn: Emperor = Heh; Star = Tzaddi
- Crowley: Emperor = Tzaddi; Star = Heh

He retained their positions in the deck but modified their zodiacal logic.

**[Not card-specific]** This controversy concerns trumps IV and XVII. It does not alter the Queen’s Tetragrammaton Heh. Confusing the two uses of Heh—one letter assigned to a court rank, another assigned to a trump—is a category error.

## Divinatory meanings by author

Quotations are kept short; fuller entries are paraphrased to avoid silently combining editions.

### Etteilla / Grand Etteilla, late 1780s

**Upright, card 37:** “FEMME BLONDE”—fair-haired or blond woman.

**Reversed:** “FEMME DU HOMME EN PLACE”—literally, “woman/wife of the man in position/place.”

The card is also labeled *La Reine de Coupe* and *La Reine de Cœur*. [Primary scan](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/2/2f/Le_grand_Etteilla%2C_ou%2C_l%27art_de_tirer_les_cartes_..._Le_tout_recueilli_..._%28IA_b29321220%29.pdf).

**Interpretive note:** Later Etteilla manuals expand these phrases. Those expansions should not all be attributed verbatim to Etteilla himself.

### Mathers, 1888

Mathers’s lists vary by edition and transcription. His Queen of Cups belongs to the fair-haired-water character series and emphasizes a fair woman, imagination and poetic or visionary disposition; the reversed aspect turns toward dishonesty, vice or unreliability.

**[Textual caution]** Waite’s later wording is often copied onto websites and mislabeled “Mathers.” The consulted 1888 scan is [*The Tarot: Its Occult Signification*](https://www.howardnema.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/12/the-tarot-1888-by-macgregor-mathers-1.pdf). Because OCR around card 37 was incomplete, I cannot responsibly present a long “verbatim Mathers” quotation as if checked against clean type.

### Waite, *Pictorial Key to the Tarot*, 1910/1911

**Description:** “Beautiful, fair, dreamy—as one who sees visions in a cup.”

**Upright, paraphrased from Waite:** A good and devoted woman who assists the querent; loving intelligence, vision, success, happiness, wisdom, virtue, an excellent spouse and mother.

**Reversed, paraphrased:** Accounts differ; she may still be a good or distinguished woman, but can instead be untrustworthy, perverse, vicious or dishonored.

[Primary online text](https://www.sacred-texts.com/tarot/pkt/pktcuqu.htm).

Waite’s phrase “loving intelligence” is especially important. It joins feeling and understanding rather than treating the Queen as emotion without discernment.

### Crowley, *Book of Thoth*, 1944

Crowley does not organize the courts into simple upright/reversed lists. He uses dignified and ill-dignified manifestations.

**Dignified:** Reception, reflection, extreme subtlety, purity, beauty, tranquility; a perfect medium or mirror.

**Ill-dignified:** What passes through her is “refracted and distorted.” She becomes dreamy, illusory and unreliable through excessive receptivity.

[Primary text](https://hermetic.com/crowley/book-of-thoth/court-cards).

### Paul Foster Case

**[Negative finding]** No sufficiently secure card-specific Case entry with both upright and reversed wording was located in the accessible primary sources. Case’s publicly promoted *Highlights of Tarot* focuses on the twenty-two Major Arcana. He retained the Golden Dawn framework, but unattributed internet lists should not be presented as his words. [BOTA edition information](https://storebota.org/products/highlights-of-tarot).

### Rachel Pollack, *Seventy-Eight Degrees of Wisdom*, 1980/1983; combined edition 1997

Pollack interprets the Queen as a person whose imagination, love and intuition are deeply developed, but who can become passive, dreamy or absorbed in fantasy. Her mature aspect trusts feeling without surrendering judgment.

**Reversed:** Emotional confusion, dependency, fantasy, suppressed or manipulative feeling may emerge.

**[Publication fact]** Pollack dates the two original volumes to 1980 and 1983 and the combined revised edition to 1997. [Pollack’s bibliography](https://www.rachelpollack.com/tarot/tarot.html); [Morgan profile](https://www.themorgan.org/exhibitions/online/tarot/23).

**[Quotation limitation]** The copyrighted book was not available as a verified full-text primary scan, so these are attributed paraphrases, not invented quotations.

### Mary K. Greer

Greer’s reversal method does not equate “reversed” with simply “bad.” A reversed court can indicate:

- the trait is blocked or refused;
- it is private or unconscious;
- the person is not valued and “acts out”;
- the energy is excessive or misdirected;
- the card represents an inner rather than public self.

Applied to the Queen of Cups, this supports emotional inhibition, projection, flooding, martyrdom, unreliable intuition or a need to withdraw and restore inner resources.

[Greer, [“Using the Tarot Court Cards”](https://marykgreer.com/2016/03/23/using-the-court-cards/); [“Tarot Reversals”](https://www.llewellyn.com/journal/article/337).]

## Esoteric and symbolic readings

### Elemental: Water of Water

**First securely systematized appearance:** Golden Dawn *Book T*, late nineteenth century.

**Spreaders:** Mathers, the Golden Dawn, Israel Regardie, Crowley, Paul Foster Case, Robert Wang, Chic and Sandra Tabatha Cicero.

The Queen is not merely in the water suit; her rank itself is Water. Hence she is maximum receptivity, containment and reflection. In positive form this becomes intuition and empathy. In negative form it becomes lack of stable identity, emotional contagion or distortion.

### Kabbalistic: Heh of Heh and Binah

**Precursor:** Lévi’s fourfold court-family and Tetragrammaton model, especially 1860.

**Systematization:** Golden Dawn.

- Queen = Heh, receptive mother.
- Cups = Heh, Water.
- Queen of Cups = Heh of Heh.
- Queens collectively resonate with Binah, the Supernal Mother.

**Caution:** This is Hermetic Qabalah, not a traditional Jewish interpretation of playing cards.

### Astrological: Gemini–Cancer

**First explicit systematic appearance:** Golden Dawn court-card zodiac.

The Queen combines:

- Gemini’s final decan: mutable air, mental multiplicity, communicative reflection;
- Cancer’s first two decans: cardinal water, protection, memory, nurture and lunar receptivity.

Crowley emphasizes that she perfectly reflects the observer. Modern readers often omit Gemini and call her purely Cancerian; that is a simplification of *Book T*.

### Alchemical

Possible readings include:

- cup as vessel or *vas hermeticum*;
- queen as passive mercury, lunar matrix or dissolving water;
- serpent emerging from vessel as volatile spirit;
- still water as the reflecting medium of transformation.

**[Disputed origin]** These fit later Hermetic symbolism and the Sola Busca’s strange vessels, but no fifteenth-century text identifies the Visconti Queen as an alchemical queen. The coherent alchemical Queen of Cups is primarily a post-Lévi/Golden Dawn and modern construction.

### Christian

The cup has been interpreted as:

- chalice of the Eucharist;
- Grail;
- ciborium or covered sacramental vessel;
- baptismal font;
- Marian vessel.

**[Tradition]** Cups naturally permitted Christian associations in a Christian visual culture.

**[Disputed card-specific claim]** Smith’s ornate covered cup resembles liturgical metalwork. Waite never says it is literally the Holy Grail or Ark of the Covenant. “The Queen guards the Grail” is a later spiritual reading.

### Grail readings

The Grail association grows from three streams:

1. the cup suit itself;
2. nineteenth-century occult-Christian interest in the Grail;
3. Waite’s own extensive Grail scholarship and the RWS cup’s reliquary-like form.

This makes a Grail resonance plausible in Waite’s milieu.

**Evidence limitation:** No surviving instruction by Waite to Smith has been found saying, “Draw the Holy Grail as the Queen’s cup.” The Grail identification remains interpretive.

### Egyptian and Isiac

Etteilla’s entire deck claimed to restore Egyptian wisdom. Crowley’s Queen bears the Lotus of Isis and a crayfish.

**[Esoteric attribution]** In Thoth, Isis/Great Mother language is explicit.

**[Legend when applied historically]** The Visconti Queen is not evidence that Renaissance tarot preserved a secret Egyptian Queen-of-Water doctrine.

### Hermetic mirror

Crowley’s Queen is a mirror that assumes the qualities projected upon it. This develops the Golden Dawn watery throne and serpent into an ontology of passive transmission.

Psychologically, it produces both:

- extraordinary empathy;
- danger of losing identity in what is reflected.

### Jungian

Tarot’s Jungian interpretation became widespread chiefly in the twentieth century; Jung did not publish a systematic Queen-of-Cups commentary.

Modern Jungian readers treat her as:

- the feeling function;
- anima or soul-image;
- Great Mother;
- dream and imaginal psyche;
- container for unconscious material;
- boundary between conscious land and unconscious sea.

**[Modern application]** Calling this “Jung’s meaning of the Queen of Cups” is inaccurate unless a specific Jung text is produced. It is a post-Jungian tarot synthesis spread by authors such as Sallie Nichols, Pollack and later therapeutic readers.

### Modern psychological readings

“Empath,” “codependency,” “emotional boundaries,” “self-care” and “holding space” are conspicuously modern vocabulary.

They develop coherently from Waite’s loving intelligence and Crowley’s mirror, but they are not medieval meanings and are not Waite’s reversed list. [Example of the modern formulation](https://biddytarot.com/tarot-card-meanings/minor-arcana/suit-of-cups/queen-of-cups/).

## People and works

### Bonifacio Bembo

Attributed painter of the Morgan Visconti-Sforza Queen. The attribution links the card to a Milanese workshop producing elite dynastic imagery. [Morgan record](https://www.themorgan.org/collection/tarot-cards/the-queen-of-cups).

### Bianca Maria Visconti

Frequently proposed as the Queen’s sitter or dynastic referent.

**Status:** disputed identification, not museum fact.

### Gertrude Moakley

Her *The Tarot Cards Painted by Bonifacio Bembo for the Visconti-Sforza Family* (1966) joined detailed art-historical observation to a theory of the trumps as carnival/Petrarchan triumph imagery. Her family identifications influenced Kaplan and popular tarot history.

### Stuart R. Kaplan

His multi-volume *Encyclopedia of Tarot* reproduced numerous early packs and transmitted Moakley-style dynastic identifications to a wide audience. Kaplan’s facsimile publishing also made the Visconti-Sforza Queen globally familiar.

### Pamela Colman Smith

The artist responsible for the Queen’s most influential modern image. Her contribution is not adequately described as mere execution of Waite’s commands: composition, marine ornament, posture and atmosphere are pictorial thinking.

### Arthur Edward Waite

Supplied the verbal program and published interpretation. His Grail interests give context to—but do not prove—the specific Grail identity of the closed vessel.

### Aleister Crowley and Frieda Harris

Crowley supplied the correspondence and prose; Harris transformed the Queen into a nearly abstract field of water, veil and reflected light.

### W. B. and George Yeats

Golden Dawn-derived court-card practice reportedly treated the Queen of Cups as George Yeats’s significator, based on sex/age, complexion and possibly watery astrological factors. The surviving automatic-script context supports tarot’s place in the Yeatses’ symbolic experimentation, though it does not create a new public meaning for the card. [Discussion and manuscript-based reconstruction](https://yeatsvision.blogspot.com/2012/01/west-coast-tarot-reading.html).

### T. S. Eliot, *The Waste Land* (1922)

Madame Sosostris names “the drowned Phoenician Sailor,” Belladonna and “the man with three staves,” but **does not name the Queen of Cups**.

**[Negative finding]** Essays may retrospectively interpret *The Waste Land* through the Queen of Cups or Grail fertility, but claiming that Eliot explicitly quotes this card is unsupported. Eliot’s tarot passage is a deliberately altered occult-tarot collage, strongly mediated by Jessie Weston’s Grail study.

### Italo Calvino, *The Castle of Crossed Destinies*

First version 1969; expanded book 1973. Calvino used tarot cards to generate interlocking stories—Visconti cards for the castle sequence and Marseille cards for the tavern.

**[Documented literary use]** Tarot court cards participate in the combinatorial narratives.

**[Negative finding]** The searchable text and reliable summaries did not establish the Queen of Cups as a uniquely privileged character. The Queen of Clubs/Wands is more explicitly prominent in plot summaries. It would be misleading to attach the whole work specifically to this Queen. [Book record and preview](https://books.google.com/books/about/The_Castle_of_Crossed_Destinies.html?id=x5t6REL4ehgC&output=html_text).

### Alejandro Jodorowsky, *The Dance of Reality* (film, 2013)

Jodorowsky explicitly discusses a Queen of Cups figure appearing to the child protagonist. He calls her “the Queen of love” and a magical woman who tells the boy that killing one fish means killing all fish in the ocean. [Documented interview](https://tunicastudio.com/alejandro-jodorowsky/).

This is a genuine, card-specific cinematic use.

### Ingmar Bergman

Bergman’s *The Seventh Seal* uses chess, Death and medieval allegory, and his films are frequently compared with tarot imagery.

**[Negative finding]** No reliable source consulted established a deliberate Queen of Cups quotation in a Bergman film. General visual resemblance is not evidence of citation.

### Paintings and buildings

**[Negative finding]** No major historic painting or building was securely shown to quote the Queen of Cups as a card before modern tarot art.

There are contemporary paintings explicitly titled for the card, including Roger Williamson’s *Queen of Water / Queen of Cups: Sirens*, which uses the Thoth-derived idea of reflective passivity and dangerous attraction. [Artist’s page](https://rogerwilliamsonart.com/tarot-queen-water-queen-cups/).

Claims that a church façade, statue, chalice-bearing saint or enthroned Madonna “is the Queen of Cups” generally proceed by resemblance unless a patron or artist documents the tarot reference.

## Controversies and disputes

### Egyptian origin myth

Court de Gébelin claimed in 1781 that tarot preserved an Egyptian book carried into Europe. Etteilla embraced and operationalized this claim.

**Status: [Legend as history; documented as eighteenth-century belief].**

Evidence against:

- tarot is documented in fifteenth-century northern Italy;
- its suits descend from pre-tarot playing cards;
- its imagery belongs to late-medieval European visual culture;
- Gébelin wrote before Champollion’s decipherment of hieroglyphs;
- no Egyptian tarot pack, tarot text or transmission chain survives.

Dummett emphasized that no occult use is documented before the late eighteenth century and criticized efforts to treat Gébelin’s assertions as evidence. [Dummett, [“Origins of Tarot”](https://www.nybooks.com/articles/1981/05/14/origins-of-tarot/).]

The irony relevant to this card is that its **cup suit probably does have an Egyptian connection through Mamluk Egypt**, but this is a history of playing-card transmission, not Gébelin’s pharaonic Book of Thoth.

### “Gypsy” or Romani origin myth

Papus’s title *Tarot of the Bohemians* helped cement the claim that “Bohemians”—a period term for Romani people—carried tarot from Egypt.

**Status: [Legend].**

No medieval or early Renaissance document credits Romani communities with inventing or transmitting tarot. The claim combines European stereotypes about Romani divination with the false medieval belief that Romani people came from Egypt.

Romani practitioners later used cards in divination, but later use is not proof of origin.

### Cathar origin

Claims identify the Papess, Grail, dualism or a coded anti-Church program as Cathar survivals.

**Status: [Disputed modern theory, unsupported as a direct transmission].**

No Cathar manuscript describes tarot, no fifteenth-century deck identifies itself as Cathar and tarot appears long after the major Cathar centers were suppressed. The Queen’s cup supplies no independent Cathar evidence.

### Templar origin

The ornate cup is sometimes described as a Templar Grail guarded by a sacred queen.

**Status: [Modern legend].**

No Templar record mentions tarot; the order was dissolved more than a century before the first tarot documents. Similarity between a chalice and Grail lore is not a chain of provenance.

### Holy Grail as original suit meaning

**[Tradition/disputed historical claim]** Cups readily acquire Grail significance in Christian esotericism. But the suit’s demonstrable ancestry in Mamluk cups predates European Grail-tarot speculation. The Grail is a later interpretation, not the established origin of the suit.

### Queen as Bianca Maria Visconti

**Status: [Disputed].** Dynastic context supports association; absent label and conventionalized faces prevent secure identification.

### Smith’s cup as Ark of the Covenant

**Status: [Modern invention or speculation].** The vessel has a lid and wing-like handles, which encourages comparison to the Ark and cherubim. But it is cup-shaped, not chest-shaped, and neither Waite nor Smith is known to have identified it as the Ark.

### Smith’s cup as Eucharistic ciborium

**Status: [Plausible iconographic comparison].** Its covered form and finial resemble liturgical vessels. This is stronger than the Ark claim but remains an inference.

### Smith’s cup as a direct copy of Sola Busca

**Status: [Possible but unproved].** General Sola Busca influence is strong; direct copying of Polisena is not documented.

### Wirth’s 1889 Queen of Cups

**Status: [False attribution].** Wirth’s 1889 deck had twenty-two trumps only. Any Wirth Queen of Cups belongs to a later completion.

### “Conver 1760”

**Status: [Inherited erroneous attribution].** Nicolas Conver was born in 1784 and active after 1809. The BnF explicitly rejects attribution of a 1760 deck to him or a same-named predecessor. [BnF](https://catalogue.bnf.fr/ark:/12148/cb16721159s).

### “Rider deck, 1910”

The deck first appeared in 1909; the illustrated *Pictorial Key* is commonly cited as 1910 or 1911 depending on edition and title-page dating.

**Status: [Bibliographic ambiguity, not a substantive contradiction].**

### Thoth publication date

Painted 1938–1943; illustrations appeared in *The Book of Thoth* in 1944; the complete commercial deck appeared in 1969.

**Status: [Multiple legitimate dates referring to creation, book reproduction and deck publication].**

### Number 13

Calling the Queen the thirteenth Cup is a useful modern count but historically courts were not numbered. In some systems Queen and Knight positions differ conceptually, so “13” must not be treated as a universal ancient numerological identity.

### Court-card gender essentialism

Golden Dawn sources describe a fair woman and map Queens to feminine Water. Modern readers frequently interpret the Queen as a mode of receptive authority available to any gender.

**Status:** The former is historical occult doctrine; the latter is a modern interpretive development. Neither changes the printed rank.

## Dedicated contested-material findings

Searches were conducted for the Queen of Cups combined with “origin,” “myth,” “dispute,” “misattribution,” “Egyptian,” “Gypsy,” “Cathar” and “Templar.”

The results support these conclusions:

1. No Queen-specific Egyptian, Cathar or Templar provenance appeared in a pre-eighteenth-century primary source.
2. Queen-specific Egyptian imagery becomes explicit in the occult period, especially Etteilla’s deck ideology and Crowley’s Lotus of Isis.
3. Grail, Ark and secret-priestess readings cluster around the RWS covered vessel and Waite’s known Grail interests.
4. Modern websites frequently convert symbolic possibility into historical assertion without a cited design document.
5. The strongest real “Egyptian” thread is the Mamluk ancestry of the suit of Cups, which belongs to Islamic playing-card history and contradicts rather than confirms Gébelin’s pharaonic narrative.
6. No evidence was found that the Queen of Cups itself caused the Papess/Juno, Pope/Jupiter or Tower/Maison Dieu naming changes; those are trump-pattern histories.
7. No evidence was found for a Queen-of-Cups quotation in Bergman or an explicit appearance in Eliot’s tarot passage.
8. Jodorowsky’s *The Dance of Reality* does contain a deliberate and author-confirmed Queen of Cups figure.

---

## Open questions

1. **Exact dating of the Morgan card.** The catalogue gives approximately 1450–1480; scholars continue to debate phases of production and replacement cards within the Visconti-Sforza group.

2. **Identity of the sitter.** Bianca Maria Visconti remains possible as a dynastic association but unproved as a literal portrait.

3. **Cary-Yale survival.** The expanded female courts are secure, but a card-by-card museum catalogue is needed to determine whether an original Queen of Cups survives or only appears in reconstructed editions.

4. **Brambilla Queen.** The retrieved records did not verify a surviving Queen of Cups in the Brambilla fragment.

5. **Rosenwald/Budapest suit identification.** More direct high-resolution sheet examination is needed before identifying every queen by suit.

6. **Sola Busca’s program.** “Polisena” is secure; the intended significance of her cup and serpent remains debated. No contemporary explanatory key survives.

7. **Smith’s immediate source.** Sola Busca influence on her deck is highly plausible, but no surviving written instruction ties Polisena specifically to the RWS Queen.

8. **The closed cup.** Ciborium, reliquary and Grail readings are plausible; the Ark claim is substantially weaker. No definitive artist’s explanation survives.

9. **Early cartomancy transmission.** Etteilla’s *femme blonde* is documented, but the precise movement from oral/ordinary-card Queen-of-Hearts meanings to Grand Etteilla card 37 is still textually complex.

10. **Lévi-to-Golden-Dawn transmission.** Lévi clearly supplies the Tetragrammaton court principle; the exact manuscript stages by which it became the Golden Dawn decan system require comparison of surviving *Book T* recensions.

11. **Case’s card-specific wording.** A verified first-edition BOTA lesson or book page is needed before publishing an “in his own words” Queen-of-Cups entry.

12. **Pollack and Greer quotations.** Copyrighted editions should be consulted directly for page-perfect extended quotations; only securely available authorial summaries and short passages were used here.
