# Four of Cups: Research Dossier

## Basic identification

| Field | Identification |
|---|---|
| Standard English | **Four of Cups** |
| Italian | **Quattro di Coppe** |
| French | **Quatre de Coupes**; early rules also spell the suit *couppes* |
| Spanish | **Cuatro de Copas** |
| German | **Vier der Kelche**, **Vier Kelche**; in French-suited Tarock, its functional analogue is usually a low red-suit card rather than a card bearing cup signs |
| Latin-suit family | Cups: Italian *coppe*, French *coupes*, Spanish *copas* |
| Classification | Numeral or pip card; fourth rank of the suit of Cups; one of the 56 suit cards in a conventional 78-card tarot pack |
| Number | Four, ordinarily unnumbered in fifteenth-century painted cards; later marked **IIII**, **IV**, **4**, or by four repeated suit signs |
| Golden Dawn title | **The Lord of Blended Pleasure** |
| Golden Dawn astrology | **Moon in Cancer, 20°–30°**—the third decan of Cancer |
| Golden Dawn Qabalah | **Chesed of Heh**, conventionally understood as Chesed in the world/suit of Water, Briah |
| Thoth title | **Luxury** |
| Earliest surviving secure example | The Four of Cups in the **Visconti di Modrone/Cary–Yale group**, Milan, approximately 1440s–1450s, if its catalogued pip is accepted as original; otherwise the closely related mid-fifteenth-century Visconti-Sforza tradition. Yale holds 67 original Cary–Yale cards, but commercial “complete” versions add reconstructed cards, so any reproduction must be checked against the museum inventory. |
| Earliest surviving scenic example | **Sola Busca**, engraved about 1490 and inscribed/colored in 1491; it is the earliest substantially complete tarot whose numeral cards have independent scenes. |
| Earliest documented cartomantic meaning found | Etteilla’s late-eighteenth-century tradition: **Ennui** upright; **Nouvelles connaissances** reversed |
| Historical game status | An ordinary low-value suit card, not a trump or honor; usually worth no intrinsic card-points |

Two distinctions prevent several recurrent errors:

- **[Documented fact]** The Four of Cups was originally a card-game pip. Neither “Minor Arcana” nor a divinatory meaning is documented for it in fifteenth-century sources.
- **[Modern terminology]** “Minor Arcana” comes from the nineteenth-century occult re-description of the pack. Paul Christian helped popularize *arcane/lame* terminology in the 1860s; early players spoke of suit cards, *carticelle*, or simply cards.
- **[Not applicable]** Papess/Juno, Pope/Jupiter, Maison Dieu, Justice–Strength numbering, and the unnumbered Fool concern the trump sequence, not this card. The Four of Cups has no regional character-name to undergo such substitutions.
- **[Not applicable]** The Golden Dawn Tzaddi/Heh controversy concerns the Emperor and Star. It does not alter the Four of Cups. The **Heh** attached to this card is the elemental Tetragrammaton letter assigned to Cups, not the trump-path letter in that controversy.

## Iconography, deck by deck

### Before tarot: Islamic and European cup suits

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** Tarot’s four ordinary suits predate tarot. A surviving Mamluk pack at Topkapı uses cups, coins, swords, and polo-sticks; Europeans retained the first three and translated or replaced polo-sticks with batons. This is a plausible material ancestry for Italian *coppe*, but it is not proof that a specific Mamluk Four of Cups directly generated the Milanese design.

**[Documented fact]** Four-suit European playing cards are attested before the invention of tarot. Tarot arose when a permanent trump sequence and Fool were added to an already familiar Italian-suited pack. The cup was consequently a playing-card emblem long before it became Water, emotion, the Grail, or the letter Heh.

### Visconti di Modrone / Cary–Yale, Milan, approximately 1440s–1450s

**[Documented object; reconstruction cautioned]** The Yale group comprises 67 surviving originals, often called the Visconti di Modrone or Cary–Yale pack. Its unusual surviving female and male court figures have encouraged a reconstruction of an 86-card pack, but the full original composition remains debated. Modern facsimiles include nineteen replacements and must not be mistaken for an intact medieval deck. [Yale’s collection history](https://library.yale.edu/news/cards-library-partners-work-together-solve-mysteries-rare-tarot-deck?page=1)

The traditional Four of Cups design consists of four large gilt or silver-ground chalices arranged symmetrically, without a narrative actor. Repetitive stamped ornament fills the ground. It does not show apathy, meditation, a tree, or a supernatural offer.

**Meaning at the time:** no allegorical gloss survives. Its immediate function was numerical: four cup signs identify suit and quantity.

### Brambilla–Brera, Milan, approximately 1440s

**[Documented fact]** The Brambilla group is fragmentary; the Pinacoteca di Brera preserves 48 cards. Its surviving suit cards share the Milanese luxury-card vocabulary—metallic grounds, repeated emblems, courtly manufacture—but the evidence does not permit a complete Four-of-Cups-specific iconographic history. Brera itself distinguishes this fragmentary deck from the complete Sola Busca. [Pinacoteca di Brera](https://pinacotecabrera.org/news/mostra/il-segreto-dei-segreti-i-tarocchi-sola-busca/)

**[Negative finding]** I found no museum catalogue establishing a surviving Brambilla Four of Cups. Images in completed modern Brambilla editions may be reconstructions.

### Pierpont Morgan–Bergamo / Visconti-Sforza, approximately 1450–1460

**[Documented fact]** The Morgan, Accademia Carrara, and Colleoni holdings together constitute the best-known Visconti-Sforza group. The Morgan’s own inventory contains the Ace, Three, Five, and Nine of Cups, but not the Four. The cards are attributed probably to Bonifacio Bembo. [Morgan collection](https://www.themorgan.org/collection/tarot-cards)

The surviving pips place the required number of repeated suit emblems against decorated metallic grounds. The safe reconstruction of the Four is therefore four symmetrically placed cups, not a personified scene.

**[Correction]** Numerous contemporary websites reproduce a “Visconti-Sforza Four of Cups” without saying whether it is medieval or newly painted. The Morgan inventory does not support attributing such a replacement to Bembo.

### Ferrarese and Este material, approximately 1450–1500

The Beinecke preserves sixteen cards traditionally grouped as an Este deck, approximately 1450. Other Este/Ferrarese fragments survive in multiple collections.

**[Documented fact]** The evidence is too fragmentary to reconstruct a continuous Ferrarese Four of Cups type.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** Ferrarese workshops contributed to the transition from unique court commissions to engraved and printed tarot. Floral or ribbon ornament around repeated suit signs is consistent with later northern Italian practice, but no detailed meaning should be inferred from that alone.

### Mantegna “Tarocchi,” Ferrara or northeastern Italy, approximately 1460s

**[Documented correction]** The so-called Mantegna Tarocchi are fifty didactic engravings arranged into hierarchical series. They are not a tarot deck, are not by Mantegna, contain no Cups suit, and therefore have no Four of Cups.

They remain relevant only as evidence for the visual culture of ranked allegorical series around Ferrara.

### Sola Busca, northern Italy, about 1490–1491

**[Documented fact]** The Sola Busca is a 78-card engraved pack, subsequently painted with tempera and gold. A 1491 inscription supplies the conventional date. Acquired by the Italian state in 2009, the complete colored set is at Brera. It is the earliest surviving substantially complete tarot with scenic numeral cards. [Brera overview](https://pinacotecabrera.org/news/mostra/il-segreto-dei-segreti-i-tarocchi-sola-busca/), [Andrea Vitali and Michael Howard](https://www.letarot.it/Sola-Busca-Tarot_pag_pg824_eng.aspx?lng=ENG)

The Sola Busca Four of Cups is not the Waite–Smith youth beneath a tree receiving a cup from a cloud. Its imagery belongs to the pack’s distinctive pseudo-classical, martial, and emblematic world.

**[Verified correction to the item brief]** The broad statement “Sola Busca supplied Smith with models for scenic minors” is well supported by visual comparisons, particularly cards such as the Three of Swords and Ten of Wands. The narrower statement that **its Four of Cups is the source of Smith’s Four of Cups could not be verified and is visually implausible**. No primary statement by Smith or Waite identifies that particular borrowing.

**[Disputed interpretation]** Brera-associated scholarship has proposed an alchemical program for the Sola Busca. Andrea Vitali and Michael Howard warn that some supposedly alchemical identifications have ordinary late-medieval Christian iconographic parallels. Thus “the first alchemical tarot” is an interpretation, not an uncontested description of the object.

### Rosenwald and Budapest sheets, late fifteenth or early sixteenth century

**[Documented objects]** These printed sheets preserve early inexpensive tarot patterns rather than hand-painted ducal cards. Their chief historical value is evidence for serial production and evolving trump patterns.

**[Negative finding]** A securely isolated Four of Cups with a documented unique program could not be established from the available sheet descriptions. Where suit fragments occur, repeated cup signs rather than Waite-style narrative scenes are expected. It would be unsafe to give them a symbolic reading.

### Minchiate, Florence, sixteenth–nineteenth centuries

Minchiate expanded the trump sequence to forty while retaining Italian suits. Its numeral cards developed local ornamental peculiarities.

A nineteenth-century survey explicitly observes that the Minchiate Four of Cups bears a **monkey**: “*le quatre de coupes ne nous offre pas son singe*” when contrasting another fragment with the Minchiate pattern. [Romain Merlin, *Origines des cartes à jouer* (1869)](https://www.labirintoermetico.com/02Tarocchi/Merlin_R_Origine_des_cartes_a_jouer.pdf)

- **[Documented regional feature]** The monkey is ornamental/local iconography, not evidence that the card originally meant imitation, appetite, or vice.
- **[Modern esoteric extrapolation]** Reading the monkey psychologically is possible in contemporary practice, but no early rule or gloss located here assigns it such a meaning.

### Bolognese pattern

The reduced Bolognese pack removes ranks 2–5 from all suits. Consequently:

- **[Documented fact]** There is **no Four of Cups** in the 62-card Tarocco Bolognese used for Ottocento.
- The cup sequence runs King, Queen, Knight, Jack, Ace, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, with Ace high among numerals and 10 low. [Ottocento rules](https://www.pagat.com/tarot/ottocento.html)

This is a decisive example of why a universal card-by-card tarot history cannot presume every regional pack contained all 78 cards.

### Sicilian pattern

The traditional Sicilian pack is also reduced.

- **[Documented fact]** Modern Mineo-style Sicilian packs retain the Four only in Coins; Cups begins at Five. Thus no playable Four of Cups survives in the standard 63-card pattern. [Sicilian Tarocchi rules](https://www.pagat.com/tarot/sicilian.html)

### Tarot de Marseille lineage

#### Jean Noblet, Paris, approximately 1650

Four stylized cups are arranged symmetrically around a central vegetal axis. The foliage is not a scene: it functions as spacing, visual balance, and workshop ornament.

#### Jean Dodal, Lyon, approximately 1701–1715

Dodal retains the four-cup symmetry and central flower/stem. Line, cup shape, leaf terminals, and coloring differ, but the grammar is the same. The deck’s dating and Lyon origin are documented by surviving examples. [British Museum/Wikimedia record](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Tarot_de_Marseille_-_Jean_Dodal)

#### Nicolas Conver, Marseille, 1760

Conver regularizes the pattern that became a principal model for later “Ancien Tarot de Marseille” editions: paired upper and lower cups flank an elaborate upright flowering stem.

**[Documented fact]** The flower does not carry a printed eighteenth-century divinatory explanation.

**[Later tradition]** Paul Marteau and postwar Marseille readers interpreted the isolated cups and protected central growth as inner affective development, stability, or material structure.

#### Grimaud / Paul Marteau, 1930 onward

Grimaud’s *Ancien Tarot de Marseille* fixed a recolored Conver-derived pattern for mass circulation. Marteau’s interpretive writing made the ornament semantic: four became material organization; leaves and stem became forces protecting or elevating the inner life.

That meaning is **twentieth-century esoteric exegesis**, not evidence of Conver’s intention.

#### Camoin–Jodorowsky, 1997

Philippe Camoin and Alejandro Jodorowsky produced a digitally reconstructed/recomposed Marseille deck from multiple historical exemplars rather than a facsimile of one surviving pack.

The Four retains four cups and a central plant. Jodorowsky and Marianne Costa give it such phrases as “amour tranquille,” equilibrium, boredom, and new knowledge—combining Marseille numerology with echoes of Etteilla’s upright/reversed vocabulary. Their published summary lists: “*amour tranquille, accord par l’équilibre, ennui, connaissance nouvelle*.” [Camoin/Jodorowsky text](https://www.camoin.com/media/PDF/art_du_tarot_jodorowsky.pdf)

### Besançon pattern

The Besançon regional substitution of **Juno for the Papess** and **Jupiter for the Pope** developed in Franco-Swiss/German-border production, especially during the eighteenth century. It affects trumps II and V only.

The Four of Cups remains an ornamental four-cup pip. It is not renamed Juno, Jupiter, or anything else.

### Etteilla’s Grand Etteilla, Paris, approximately 1789

Etteilla rearranged, renumbered, and explicitly captioned an “Egyptian” divinatory tarot.

The Four of Cups becomes **card 46**, not the fourth card of the total pack. Surviving editions show four cup signs in a 2×2 arrangement and print the meanings on the border:

- upright: **ENNUI**
- reversed: **NOUVELLES CONNAISSANCES**

The facsimile page displays both captions and the card number. [*Le Grand Etteilla, ou l’art de tirer les cartes*, scan](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/2/2f/Le_grand_Etteilla%2C_ou%2C_l%27art_de_tirer_les_cartes_..._Le_tout_recueilli_..._%28IA_b29321220%29.pdf)

**[Documented fact]** This is the clearest historical ancestor of the card’s later boredom/discontent meaning.

**[Negative finding]** The sources checked did not establish a uniquely Four-of-Cups astrological figure in Etteilla comparable to the Golden Dawn’s Moon-in-Cancer decan. Claims that “Etteilla assigned Moon in Cancer” conflate his system with the Golden Dawn.

### Oswald Wirth, 1889

Wirth’s original 1889 tarot consisted principally of the 22 trumps drawn under Stanislas de Guaita’s direction.

- **[Documented correction]** It has no original Wirth Four of Cups.
- Later “Wirth” 78-card products extend his style posthumously and should be classified as modern extrapolations.

### Golden Dawn, approximately 1888–1900

The Golden Dawn manuscript convention known as **Book T** gives:

> “MOON IN CANCER, 20°–30°. THE LORD OF BLENDED PLEASURE.”

Its design calls for two upper cups overflowing into two lower cups that do not overflow; an angelic hand holds a lotus stem; a top flower issues water; two lateral leaves make a cross. Its meaning begins:

> “Success or pleasure approaching their end. A stationary period in happiness…”

It adds pleasure mixed with drawbacks and identifies the card as **Chesed of Heh**. [Regardie’s transcription of the Golden Dawn material](https://scriptaetveritas.com.br/wp-content/uploads/2016/04/ISRAEL_REGARDIE_COMPLETE_GOLDEN_DAWN_SYSTEM_OF_MAGIC.pdf)

Three distinctions matter:

- **[Esoteric attribution]** Moon in Cancer and Chesed of Heh are Golden Dawn constructions, not medieval meanings.
- **[Textual history]** “Book T, 1888” is often repeated too confidently. The Golden Dawn corpus developed through manuscripts and copies; the published Regardie text is later evidence for Order teaching, not an 1888 printed edition.
- **[Reconstruction]** Robert Wang’s *Golden Dawn Tarot* and the Ciceros’ decks visualize surviving descriptions. They are twentieth-century reconstructions, not rediscovered nineteenth-century printed cards.

The Ciceros summarize the reconstructed image and meaning as “mixed blessings, receiving pleasure but with some drawbacks, and anxieties.” [Cicero, *Self-Initiation into the Golden Dawn Tradition*, p. 424](https://kobayashi-dxn.s3.eu-west-2.amazonaws.com/Golden%20Dawn/cicero-%20secrets%20of%20the%20golden%20dawn%20temple/Cicero_-_Self_Initiation_into_%282%29.pdf)

### Waite–Smith, London, 1909

Pamela Colman Smith drew a young person seated cross-legged on grass beneath a tree. Three cups stand before the figure. A cloud-hand offers a fourth cup at face level. Folded arms, downturned head, and averted gaze create deliberate ambiguity: meditation, refusal, satiety, or failure to notice.

Waite’s description is:

> “A young man is seated under a tree and contemplates three cups set on the grass before him; an arm issuing from a cloud offers him another cup.”

He likens it to a fairy gift but interprets the figure as dissatisfied. His divinatory list reads:

> “Weariness, disgust, aversion, imaginary vexations, as if the wine of this world had caused satiety only…”

Reversed:

> “Novelty, presage, new instruction, new relations.”

[A. E. Waite, *The Pictorial Key to the Tarot*, 1910, Four of Cups](https://sacred-texts.com/tarot/pkt/index.htm?chaptersPage=3)

#### Sources of Smith’s composition

- **[Documented visual relation]** The cloud-hand offering an emblem resembles the Waite–Smith Aces and established emblematic conventions.
- **[Probable intellectual source]** The seated figure converts Etteilla’s **Ennui** and Book T’s pleasure approaching an end into a legible scene.
- **[Disputed source]** Sola Busca photographs were accessible in London and demonstrably resemble several Smith minors. But no matching Sola Busca Four-of-Cups tableau has been shown. The claim of a direct card-for-card source is unsubstantiated.
- **[Modern interpretation]** Calling the cloud-hand “divine grace” is now common, but Waite says “fairy gift,” not specifically Christian grace.

### Paul Foster Case / BOTA

Case transmitted Golden Dawn Qabalah and astrology through *An Introduction to the Study of the Tarot* (1920), *The Tarot* (1947), and BOTA teaching.

- **[Documented scope correction]** The familiar BOTA deck designed by Case and Jessie Burns Parke is a 22-trump coloring deck, not an original 78-card pack. It therefore contains no canonical BOTA Four of Cups.
- **[Esoteric attribution]** Case nevertheless accepts the suit/element and numerical framework: Cups–Water–Briah; Four–Chesed; and the Golden Dawn decanic pattern.
- **[Negative finding]** I did not locate an open primary Case passage giving a distinct upright/reversed Four-of-Cups list. Later web lists attributed to Case frequently reproduce Waite or Book T without edition or page.

### Thoth, painted 1938–1943; first full publication 1969

Aleister Crowley and Lady Frieda Harris titled it **LUXURY**. Four unstable-looking cups stand over a dark or ruffled sea, fed by a branching lotus. Harris transforms Book T’s diagram into a geometric, organic, almost hydraulic system.

Crowley writes:

> “This card refers to Chesed in the sphere of Water.”

He continues:

> “The card refers to the Moon in Cancer, which is her own house…”

But this strength is excessive; it introduces:

> “the seeds of decay into the fruit of pleasure.”

He interprets Four as consolidation that becomes restriction and a “dead stop.” [Crowley, *The Book of Thoth*, 1944, “Luxury—Four of Cups”](https://www.rahoorkhuit.net/library/crowley/equinox/volume3/vol_3_no_05.pdf)

**[Documented correction]** The cards were painted in 1938–1943, Crowley’s book appeared in 1944, a limited monochrome issue followed, and the first generally recognized full-color commercial deck appeared in 1969. “Published 1969” therefore applies to the deck’s broad color publication, not to the text or paintings.

### Notable modern redesigns

Most post-1970 occult decks choose one of three lineages:

1. **Marseille-derived:** four vessels and central vegetation.
2. **Waite–Smith-derived:** a withdrawn figure overlooks or rejects an offered vessel.
3. **Thoth-derived:** four vessels, water, lunar/Cancer symbols, and luxurious stagnation.

Examples include the Morgan–Greer, Aquarian, Robin Wood, Wild Unknown, Light Seer’s, and innumerable themed decks. Their changes—gender, species, ethnicity, landscape, spilled vessels, technology—are **modern inventions**. They frequently preserve Waite’s basic syntax even when the cloud-hand disappears.

## The game

### Rank

In traditional Latin-suited tarot, the suits divide into:

- **long suits:** Swords and Batons, ordinarily descending 10 to Ace;
- **round suits:** Cups and Coins, ordinarily descending Ace to 10.

In the round suits the sequence is normally:

**King, Queen, Knight, Jack, Ace, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10.**

Thus the Four of Cups:

- loses to Ace, Two, and Three of Cups;
- beats Five through Ten of Cups;
- cannot beat a trump;
- ordinarily cannot win against a higher court of Cups;
- has no intrinsic card-point value in standard tarot counting.

The earliest printed French rules associated with the Abbé de Marolles use a shortened 66-card pack. They explicitly rank *couppes* K-Q-C-J-A-2-3-4-5-6-7. The Four is therefore below the Three and above the Five. [Marolles rules](https://www.pagat.com/tarot/marolles.html)

### Card value

**[Documented general rule]** Low numeral cards usually contribute no card-points individually. Their value arises from:

- winning a trick;
- forming part of a counting packet;
- fulfilling discard or combination rules;
- denying an opponent a trick.

The Four is neither an *oudler* nor a *tarocco* honor.

### Regional survival

| Game/pattern | Four of Cups |
|---|---|
| Full 78-card Italian tarocchi | Present; low round-suit numeral |
| Early French tarot | Present unless removed for a shortened pack |
| Modern French Tarot Nouveau | Present functionally as the Four of Hearts, not as a cup-emblem card |
| Piedmontese Scarto | Present; Four is low in the round suit but above Five |
| Bolognese Tarocchino/Ottocento | Absent: ranks 2–5 removed |
| Sicilian Tarocchi | Absent from Cups; Four survives only in Coins |
| Minchiate | Present, with local ornament including a monkey |
| Central European Tarock | Usually no literal Cups suit; French-suited Hearts fills the red-suit role, often in shortened packs |

### Trump orders and the Fool

The disputes over the three historical Italian trump orders—commonly grouped by Dummett as A, B, and C—do not change the Four of Cups. They concern such matters as the relative placement of Justice, Fortitude, Temperance, virtues, celestial trumps, and the highest World/Angel.

Likewise:

- the Fool may excuse itself rather than take a trick;
- the Fool is often unnumbered;
- in some games it has substantial counting value.

None of this turns the Four of Cups into a trump.

**[Template correction]** Justice/Strength swaps, Papess/Juno, Pope/Jupiter, Maison Dieu, and the Fool’s number are indispensable to a general tarot history but do not supply alternate names or numbers for this pip.

## Chronology of attestation

### Approximately 1420: Marziano da Tortona

Marziano’s text describes a special card game made for Filippo Maria Visconti, with sixteen gods and four suit-like groups of birds.

- **[Documented fact]** It is an important precursor to tarot.
- **[Negative finding]** It has no suit of Cups and no Four of Cups.
- **[Scholarly reconstruction]** It demonstrates experimentation with permanent allegorical superior cards at the Milanese court.

### 1440s: northern Italian *carte da trionfi*

Court records in Ferrara and Milan document *carte da trionfi*.

### 1442 Ferrara ledger

A Ferrarese account records packs of *carte da trionfi*; another well-known notice concerns a Bolognese merchant supplying packs.

- **[Documented fact]** This establishes the game and commodity.
- **[Negative finding]** The account does not name the Four of Cups individually.

### Approximately 1440s–1450s: first surviving card

The Visconti/Cary–Yale material supplies the earliest plausible surviving Four of Cups. The card’s identity comes from its four cup emblems, not from a written title.

### Approximately 1470–1500: *Sermo perutilis de ludo cum aliis*

The anonymous “Steele Sermon” condemns gaming and provides an early complete trump list.

- **[Documented fact]** It is crucial for reconstructing the standard trump subjects.
- **[Negative finding]** It does not give individual pip-card meanings or name the Four of Cups.

### 1491: Sola Busca

The earliest secure scenic Four of Cups belongs to the complete illustrated pip program of the Sola Busca.

### Around 1500: poetic appropriations

Boiardo’s devised tarot poetry and later *tarocchi appropriati* assign persons, virtues, or verses to cards.

- **[Documented literary tradition]** These show tarot becoming a social and poetic language.
- **[Caution]** They do not establish a permanent Four-of-Cups divinatory meaning.

### 1527: Teofilo Folengo

Folengo’s *Chaos del Triperuno* contains a celebrated tarot-related literary episode.

- **[Documented fact]** Tarot images are used poetically and characterologically.
- **[Negative finding]** No stable Four-of-Cups doctrine comparable to Etteilla’s *Ennui* results from it.

### Seventeenth century: Marseille-pattern pips

Noblet and related printers stabilize four cups plus vegetal ornament.

### 1637/1650s onward: rules

Printed and manuscript rules clarify the reversed ranking of round suits. The Four functions as a low pip, not a symbol of apathy.

### 1781: Court de Gébelin and the Comte de Mellet

Court de Gébelin’s *Monde primitif*, volume VIII, declares tarot a surviving Egyptian book. The Comte de Mellet connects the 22 trumps to Hebrew letters.

- **[Documented publication]** This marks the decisive public occult reinvention of tarot.
- **[Negative finding]** Neither supplies a distinct Four-of-Cups correspondence. Mellet’s 22-letter scheme applies to trumps, not the 56 suit cards.
- **[Legend]** The Egyptian origin was asserted without documentary or linguistic evidence.

### 1783–1785: Etteilla’s instruction books

Etteilla publishes *Manière de se récréer avec le jeu de cartes nommées tarots*, making systematic tarot divination public.

### Approximately 1789: Grand Etteilla

The Four of Cups becomes card 46, **Ennui / Nouvelles connaissances**.

### 1854–1856: Éliphas Lévi, *Dogme et Rituel de la Haute Magie*

Lévi associates tarot with Kabbalah, ceremonial magic, and the four magical implements.

- **[Esoteric synthesis]** His cup participates in the elemental and ritual-vessel system later standardized as Water.
- **[Negative finding]** Lévi does not provide a Four-of-Cups-specific reading, decan, or scene. Requests for “Lévi’s words on the Four of Cups” cannot be met because no such passage was traced.

### 1863: Paul Christian

Christian elaborates Egyptian initiatory fiction and popularizes “arcana” language.

- **[Esoteric/fictionalized tradition]** His temple initiation narratives influenced Papus and later Egyptian decks.
- **[Negative finding]** No securely traced Four-of-Cups doctrine was found.

### 1888: S. L. MacGregor Mathers

Mathers publishes *The Tarot: Its Occult Signification, Use in Fortune-Telling, and Method of Play*. His Four of Cups reads:

> “Ennui, displeasure, discontent, dissatisfaction.”

Reversed:

> “New acquaintance, conjecture, sign, presentiment.”

[Mathers table with Etteilla notes](https://benebellwen.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/02/sightsee-the-tarot-macgregor-mathers-handout-meanings-of-the-cards.pdf)

This closely preserves Etteilla rather than originating a new meaning.

### 1888–1900: Golden Dawn Book T

The Four receives its lasting decan, Sephirah, world, and title.

### 1889: Papus, *Le Tarot des Bohémiens*

Papus builds an elaborate Tetragrammaton-based scheme, presents tarot as an initiatory book, and repeats the “Bohemian” or Romani-transmission mythology.

- **[Esoteric attribution]** Cups corresponds to the receptive/passive current and to a Tetragrammaton letter within suit cycles.
- **[Important conflict]** Papus’s letter arithmetic is not identical in all particulars to Golden Dawn Book T.
- **[Negative finding]** “Moon in Cancer, Four of Cups” is not securely attributable to Papus’s 1889 text; it belongs to the Golden Dawn decan system.

### 1909–1910: Waite–Smith and *Pictorial Key*

Smith creates the now-dominant scene; Waite fuses Etteilla’s ennui with a contemplative refusal of an offered cup.

### 1920s–1950s: Case and BOTA

Case spreads Golden Dawn correspondences in a more systematic American curriculum.

### 1938–1944 / 1969: Thoth

Harris paints the card; Crowley supplies the fullest metaphysical analysis; commercial publication makes **Luxury** internationally influential.

### 1978–1984: Pollack and Greer

Rachel Pollack’s *Seventy-Eight Degrees of Wisdom* and Mary K. Greer’s *Tarot for Your Self* help shift tarot from prediction toward reflection, choice, and psychological practice.

### 1997–present

Camoin–Jodorowsky and later Marseille revivals reassert number-and-suit interpretation alongside the dominant Waite–Smith narrative.

### Hebrew-letter problem

“Cups = Heh” needs precision:

- **[Golden Dawn attribution]** The suits follow Yod–Heh–Vav–Heh: Wands–Yod, Cups–first Heh, Swords–Vav, Pentacles–final Heh.
- The Four of Cups is therefore **Chesed of Heh**, or Chesed operating in the watery/creative world of Briah.
- **[Not a trump path]** It has no individual Hebrew letter and no Tree-of-Life path. Sephirah Four is a sphere attribution, not one of the 22 paths.
- Crowley’s Tzaddi/Heh exchange concerns Emperor IV and Star XVII, not this pip.

### Color scales

Golden Dawn color work varies by scale and by whether one colors the Sephirah, suit-world, planet, sign, or combined card. Typical ingredients are:

- Chesed: blue;
- Water/Briah: blue and deep watery tones;
- Moon: silver, pearl, pale blue;
- Cancer: amber or related zodiacal-scale color depending on the table employed.

**[Caution]** There is no single historically printed “official color” of the Four of Cups. Wang and Cicero reconstructions make editorial choices from layered manuscripts.

## Esoteric and symbolic readings

### Numerical: four and Chesed

**[Golden Dawn attribution]** Four is Chesed, Mercy: consolidation, benevolence, order, abundance, and stability. In Water this should create emotional security.

The card’s tension follows from too much stability:

- feeling becomes contained;
- contentment becomes satiety;
- protection becomes enclosure;
- repose becomes inertia.

Crowley makes this dialectic explicit: Four stabilizes the Dyad but also emphasizes separateness and limitation.

### Astrological: Moon in Cancer

**[Golden Dawn attribution]** The Moon is in its domicile in Cancer, so lunar, receptive, domestic, memory-laden Water is exceptionally strong.

Positive possibilities:

- emotional shelter;
- hospitality;
- receptivity;
- imagination;
- familial or domestic satisfaction.

Negative possibilities:

- retreat into habit;
- dependency on familiar feeling;
- moodiness;
- passive enjoyment;
- desire without movement.

**[Historical caution]** This decan attribution is not ancient tarot astrology. It was imposed through the Golden Dawn’s mapping of the 36 small cards from Two through Ten onto the 36 zodiacal decans.

### Hermetic and alchemical

**[General Hermetic tradition]** The cup is a receptive vessel: it contains, dissolves, mixes, and receives influence. Four vessels therefore make a stable matrix of reception.

**[Modern alchemical reading]** The stagnant cup may be compared with a sealed vessel whose contents cease transforming; the offered fourth cup can be imagined as a new reagent.

**[Negative finding]** No Renaissance alchemical text was found that names the tarot Four of Cups in this way. Applying *nigredo*, fermentation, or the four elements directly to Smith’s card is modern analogical interpretation.

### Kabbalistic

**[Golden Dawn attribution]** Chesed in Briah means the expansive fourth Sephirah working in the creative or watery world. The first Heh of the Divine Name is the Mother/receptive principle.

Crowley’s reading complicates this generosity: below the Abyss, form and abundance already contain limitation. The overflowing upper cups fail to make the lower cups overflow; transmission is incomplete.

### Christian

Several Christian readings circulate:

- the cloud-hand is divine grace;
- the fourth cup is Eucharistic;
- the three earthly cups are worldly satisfactions;
- the seated figure refuses a divine vocation;
- the Cups suit encodes the Holy Grail.

Their status differs:

- **[Documented background]** Chalices and divine hands are familiar Christian visual forms.
- **[Tradition]** Grail and Eucharistic readings are popular in twentieth-century esoteric tarot.
- **[Disputed intention]** Waite was a Christian mystic and wrote extensively on the Grail, making Christian resonance plausible.
- **[Negative finding]** Waite’s card entry calls the offer a “fairy gift”; it does not explicitly identify the vessel as Eucharist or Grail. No Smith statement confirms that intention.

### Jungian

**[Modern invention influenced by analytical psychology]** Jung did not publish a systematic interpretation of the Four of Cups. Later Jungian tarot writers use it to represent:

- withdrawal of libido from outer objects;
- introversion;
- inflation of a self-protective ego position;
- an unconscious content seeking recognition;
- projection of worthlessness onto the offered possibility;
- the neglected “fourth” needed to complete a psychological totality.

The last idea draws on Jung’s interest in quaternity, but applying it specifically to this card is a later tarot synthesis.

### Existential and therapeutic readings

The Waite–Smith figure is now often read as depression, dissociation, anhedonia, burnout, or avoidant attachment.

- **[Modern reading]** These can be useful metaphors.
- **[Clinical caution]** The card is not a diagnosis and historical “melancholy” or “ennui” should not be retroactively equated with a specific psychiatric disorder.

## People and works

### Directly attached to the card

- **Bonifacio Bembo and associated Milanese workshops:** probable artists of major Visconti groups; attribution of every individual pip remains uncertain.
- **The anonymous Sola Busca engraver and colorist:** created the earliest surviving complete scenic pip system.
- **Jean Noblet, Jean Dodal, Nicolas Conver:** printers/cardmakers who transmitted the ornamental four-cup pattern.
- **Jean-Baptiste Alliette/Etteilla:** supplied the first traceable enduring divinatory polarity, ennui versus new knowledge.
- **S. L. MacGregor Mathers and Golden Dawn compilers:** transmitted Etteilla and constructed the decan/Sephirah system.
- **Pamela Colman Smith:** invented the dominant modern narrative image.
- **A. E. Waite:** described and interpreted Smith’s image while suppressing much explicit Golden Dawn terminology.
- **Lady Frieda Harris:** transformed the Golden Dawn lotus-and-cups diagram into the Thoth painting.
- **Aleister Crowley:** named it *Luxury* and made limitation/decay its metaphysical center.
- **Paul Foster Case:** spread Golden Dawn-style tarot Qabalah in the United States.
- **Rachel Pollack and Mary K. Greer:** helped establish reflective and psychological reading practices.
- **Alejandro Jodorowsky and Marianne Costa:** renewed Marseille numerological interpretation.

### Literature, cinema, painting, and architecture

The requested examples require careful separation.

#### Italo Calvino

*The Castle of Crossed Destinies* uses tarot cards as a combinatorial narrative engine.

- **[Documented literary connection to tarot]**
- **[Negative finding]** No uniquely canonical Four-of-Cups episode was identified in the sources consulted. Assigning a particular silence, refusal, or character to this card depends on edition, layout, and interpreter.

#### T. S. Eliot

*The Waste Land* names “the drowned Phoenician Sailor,” “Belladonna,” and a missing Hanged Man in Madame Sosostris’s reading.

- **[Documented tarot allusion]**
- **[Negative finding]** It does not name or unmistakably describe the Four of Cups.

#### W. B. Yeats

Yeats belonged to the Golden Dawn and used its symbolic world.

- **[Documented institutional connection]**
- **[Negative finding]** No specific published Yeats identification with the Four of Cups was located.

#### Alejandro Jodorowsky

Jodorowsky’s films use tarot-like initiation, alchemy, symbolic mutilation, and sacred grotesque; he later co-created the Camoin deck and wrote with Marianne Costa.

- **[Documented broad influence]**
- **[Negative finding]** No film shot could be securely demonstrated as a direct quotation of the Four of Cups rather than a general tarot or chalice motif.

Bergman’s *The Seventh Seal* supplies medieval allegory, chess with Death, and a cultural atmosphere frequently associated with tarot.

- **[Modern association]**
- **[Negative finding]** It is not an adaptation of the Four of Cups and no direct quotation was traced.

#### Paintings and buildings

Cloud-hands, contemplative figures beneath trees, sacred cups, fountains, and quatrefoil planning all predate Smith.

- **[Iconographic parallel]** Such motifs may illuminate how the picture works.
- **[Negative finding]** I found no well-documented painting or building that demonstrably quotes the Four of Cups specifically.
- Lists claiming that a particular church, Grail chapel, or Renaissance painting “contains the Four of Cups” generally rely on resemblance, not provenance.

## Controversies and disputes

### Egyptian origin

**[Legend, first publicized in 1781]** Court de Gébelin claimed tarot was a surviving Egyptian sacred book and proposed unsupported Egyptian etymologies for *tarot*. The Comte de Mellet called it a Book of Thoth and connected its trumps with Hebrew letters.

**Evidence:** no Egyptian tarot pack, inscription, textual description, or transmission record. Gébelin wrote before Egyptian hieroglyphs were deciphered.

**Historical correction:** tarot is documented in fifteenth-century northern Italy as a card game; its ordinary suits have older playing-card ancestry. Dummett notes that occult writers offered supposed Egyptian derivations without hieroglyphic forms. [Dummett, “Origins of Tarot”](https://www.nybooks.com/articles/1981/05/14/origins-of-tarot/)

### Romani or “Gypsy” transmission

**[Legend]** The false identification of Roma as Egyptians facilitated the story that itinerant Roma carried the Book of Thoth into Europe. Nineteenth-century writers including Vaillant and Papus spread it.

**Evidence against:** European playing cards and tarot are documented before the proposed Romani transmission narrative; the card terminology and workshop history are Italian and French. The mythology is also entangled with an obsolete exonym and stereotyped marketing. [Historical overview](https://www.tarotassociation.net/tarot-history/)

### Cathar origin

**[Modern speculative claim]** Some twentieth-century writers align the Popess, Grail, dualism, or southern French geography with Cathar survival.

**Evidence:** no Cathar text names tarot; the earliest cards arise in northern Italian court culture after the Albigensian period. There is no Four-of-Cups-specific Cathar attestation.

### Templar origin

**[Legend/modern invention]** Tarot is sometimes described as encoded Templar doctrine preserved after 1307.

**Evidence:** no Templar tarot deck, rule, inventory, or textual key. The claim is chronologically unsupported and does not explain the documentary emergence of *trionfi* in fifteenth-century Italy.

### Grail origin of Cups

**[Tradition with plausible resonance]** Cups can evoke Eucharistic chalices and Grail literature, especially in Waite’s Christian esotericism.

**[Disputed historical claim]** That does not prove the Italian suit originated as the Grail. The suit has an earlier Islamic playing-card analogue, making a singular Grail origin unnecessary.

### Sola Busca as the direct Four-of-Cups source

**[Disputed claim]** Modern summaries frequently say the whole Waite–Smith minor arcana was copied from Sola Busca.

What the evidence supports:

- Sola Busca was known through London-held photographs and exhibition/catalogue culture;
- several Waite–Smith designs have strong specific visual analogies;
- it demonstrated that every pip could be scenic.

What it does not presently support:

- a one-to-one derivation of Smith’s seated Four of Cups from the Sola Busca Four;
- a written admission by Smith;
- the claim that every Waite–Smith minor came from that deck.

The best reconstruction is that Sola Busca provided precedent and selected motifs, while Etteilla and Book T provided the Four’s semantic structure.

### “Golden Dawn title: Luxury”

**[Common misattribution]** Golden Dawn Book T calls the card **Lord of Blended Pleasure**, not simply Luxury.

**[Documented correction]** **Luxury** is Crowley’s Thoth title. Crowley retains and intensifies Book T’s idea that stable pleasure contains the beginning of decline.

### “Binah of Heh”

One searchable transcription places “Binah of Heh” close to Four-of-Cups material because adjacent Three- and Four-of-Cups entries run together.

**[Textual correction]**

- Three of Cups = Binah of Heh.
- Four of Cups = **Chesed of Heh**.

This is a classic digital-excerpt/context error capable of becoming a tradition.

### Reversal as an ancient practice

**[Disputed generalization]** Etteilla’s cards explicitly carry upright and reversed captions; Mathers and Waite continue them.

**[Correction]** Medieval tarot rules do not document reversed divinatory meanings. Crowley and Golden Dawn dignity systems are not equivalent to mechanically reversing a card.

### “The fourth cup is definitely unseen”

Smith depicts an averted or lowered gaze. Waite presents dissatisfaction with what is offered.

- **[Traditional reading]** The figure fails to notice the cup.
- **[Equally possible visual reading]** The figure sees and refuses it.
- **[Disputed identification]** The person’s gender, age, motive, and spiritual state are not definitively fixed by the drawing.

### Misprints and editorial traditions

- Confusing **Binah** with **Chesed** results from adjacent entry extraction.
- Dating the Thoth deck simply “1969” erases its 1938–1943 painting and 1944 textual publication.
- Labeling modern completed Visconti packs as wholly fifteenth-century erases replacement artwork.
- Calling the BOTA deck a 78-card Case original confuses Case’s teachings with later or derivative extensions.
- Treating Camoin–Jodorowsky as a literal restoration of a single 1760 master obscures its composite editorial method.
- Calling all suit cards “arcana” projects nineteenth-century occult language backward onto Renaissance play.

## Open questions

1. **Which exact Cary–Yale Four of Cups is original?**  
   The group contains 67 original cards and modern editions contain nineteen reconstructions. A definitive answer requires the current Beinecke object-level inventory rather than a commercial deck list.

2. **What was intended by the Sola Busca Four of Cups?**  
   The card belongs to an organized scenic program, but the program’s political, alchemical, military, and antiquarian readings remain contested.

3. **Did Smith inspect the Sola Busca photographs personally?**  
   Access and visual dependence are probable; a diary, letter, or signed statement establishing the occasion has not surfaced in the sources consulted.

4. **What immediate visual source supplied Smith’s cloud-hand and seated figure?**  
   Etteilla and Book T explain the concept, while emblem books, Christian iconography, Smith’s theater work, and earlier illustration may explain its form. No single prototype is proven.

5. **How early was the precise Book T wording fixed?**  
   Surviving copies and later transcriptions establish Golden Dawn use, but a complete stemma of manuscript variants would be needed to date every phrase.

6. **What did Case specifically teach for this pip?**  
   Publicly accessible sources establish his wider Golden Dawn framework but did not yield an authoritative, paginated Four-of-Cups entry with reversals.

7. **Can a direct Calvino, Yeats, Eliot, Bergman, or architectural quotation be documented?**  
   Tarot connections exist at broader levels; a Four-of-Cups-specific allusion remains unproved.

8. **When did “missed opportunity” replace “ennui” as the most common keyword?**  
   Waite–Smith makes the reading visually available in 1909–1910, but a reception study of manuals and mass-market decks would be needed to date its dominance.
