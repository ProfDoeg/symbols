# Five of Cups: Research Dossier

## Method and evidence labels

This dossier uses the following labels:

- **[Documented fact]** directly supported by surviving cards, archival records, or the cited author’s text.
- **[Scholarly reconstruction]** an inference accepted or seriously entertained by specialist historians but not explicitly stated by contemporary evidence.
- **[Tradition]** a transmitted cartomantic or symbolic convention whose historical truth is not implied.
- **[Esoteric attribution]** an occult correspondence deliberately assigned by an author or order.
- **[Disputed claim]** proposed but contested or inadequately evidenced.
- **[Legend]** a historical-origin story contradicted or unsupported by the documentary record.
- **[Modern invention]** a comparatively recent interpretation presented as a new reading rather than recovered history.
- **[Not traced]** the requested association could not be established in the sources consulted.

Dates for handmade fifteenth-century cards are necessarily approximate. The Five of Cups is a pip, not a trump; several requested topics—trump order, the Papess/Juno substitution, the Fool’s number, and the Tzaddi/Heh controversy—belong to the pack surrounding it rather than to this card specifically. They are treated accordingly and not allowed to imply card-specific correspondences.

## Basic identification

| Field | Identification |
|---|---|
| Standard English | Five of Cups; Five of Chalices |
| Italian | *Cinque di Coppe*; in a tarot pack, *tarocchi*; originally part of a pack called *carte da trionfi* |
| French | *Cinq de Coupes* |
| Spanish | *Cinco de Copas* |
| German | *Fünf der Kelche* or *Kelch-Fünf* in divinatory decks; in traditional Tarock play the equivalent red-suit pip may be a *Herz Fünf*, if retained |
| Number | 5; commonly Roman **V** in occult decks, five unindexed cup emblems in early Italian and Marseille packs; Grand Etteilla pack number **45** |
| Class | Number card or pip in the suit of Cups; later called part of the “Minor Arcana” |
| Element | Water—an occult convention, not a Renaissance game rule |
| Tetragrammaton letter | Second **Heh** of Yod-Heh-Vav-Heh in the Golden Dawn/Case system—an occult attribution, not a Hebrew letter assigned uniquely to this card |
| Sephirah | Geburah, the fifth Sephirah, expressed in the World/suit of Cups |
| Golden Dawn title | **The Lord of Loss in Pleasure** |
| Golden Dawn astrology | Mars in the first decan of Scorpio, conventionally 0°–10° Scorpio |
| Thoth title | **Disappointment** |
| Earliest surviving securely identified example | The Visconti-Sforza Five of Cups, Milan, usually dated around 1450–1480; Morgan Library MS M.630, card 26 |
| Earliest surviving fully illustrated pip tradition | Sola Busca, engraved about 1490–1491 and painted about 1491; but the proposition that its particular Five of Cups directly supplied Smith’s Five of Cups scene is **not verified** |

**[Documented fact]** The Morgan Library catalogues an actual Five of Cups among the surviving Visconti-Sforza cards and identifies the group as Milanese, ca. 1450–1480. It is therefore earlier than the Sola Busca. The museum describes tarot’s original use as a card game and explicitly separates that use from its much later occult reputation. [Morgan Library collection record](https://www.themorgan.org/manuscript/158525)

**[Documented fact]** The name *Minor Arcana* is not fifteenth-century terminology. “Major” and “Minor Arcana” entered occult discourse through the nineteenth-century French tradition, particularly Paul Christian, and were subsequently naturalized in English-language tarot.

**[Important correction]** The Five of Cups has no individual Hebrew letter. Golden Dawn Hebrew-letter assignments belong to the twenty-two trumps. For this pip, **Heh means the suit/world of Cups**, while **five means Geburah**. Describing Heh as “the Hebrew letter of the Five of Cups” collapses two different levels of the system.

## Iconography deck by deck

### Fifteenth-century Milan

#### Visconti di Modrone / Cary-Yale, ca. 1440s–1450s, Milan

**[Documented fact]** This luxury hand-painted pack belongs to the earliest Milanese tarot family. It has unusual structural features, including extra female court cards, and is incomplete.

**[Not traced]** No surviving Five of Cups belonging to this group was located in the consulted institutional catalogues. Consequently, a deck-specific composition should not be reconstructed from modern facsimile replacements.

**Meaning of the absence:** nothing permits a historical divinatory reading. The surviving pack testifies to courtly card production, not to “loss,” Mars, Scorpio, or Geburah.

#### Brambilla-Brera, ca. 1440s, Milan

**[Documented fact]** Forty-eight cards from this late-Gothic Milanese group are held by the Pinacoteca di Brera. Brera identifies it as a precious deck made for the Duke of Milan.

**[Not traced]** A surviving Five of Cups was not established. Modern reconstructions may add one, but a newly painted completion is not evidence for the fifteenth-century card.

#### Visconti-Sforza / Pierpont Morgan–Bergamo, ca. 1450–1480, Milan

**[Documented fact]** The Five survives as Morgan MS M.630.26. Five ornate golden vessels are displayed as suit emblems on a tooled-gold ground, accompanied by painted floral or foliate decoration. There is no narrative figure, no spilled liquid, no bridge, and no visual grief.

The general structure is decorative and heraldic:

- five separate cup emblems;
- symmetrical or near-symmetrical arrangement;
- gold leaf and luxury manuscript technique;
- foliage occupying the spaces around the pips.

**What it meant:** in the evidence available, it identifies suit and rank. Any claim that its arrangement encoded Geburah, sorrow, the Grail, or Mars in Scorpio is a retrospective occult reading.

The Morgan attributes the cards probably to Bonifacio Bembo and notes that 35 Morgan cards, 26 Bergamo cards, and 13 Colleoni-family cards together make one of the most complete surviving fifteenth-century sets. [Morgan overview](https://www.themorgan.org/collection/tarot-cards), [Morgan manuscript record](https://www.themorgan.org/manuscript/158525), [Metropolitan Museum catalogue, *The World in Play*](https://resources.metmuseum.org/resources/metpublications/pdf/The_World_in_Play_Luxury_Cards_1430_1540.pdf)

### Fifteenth-century Ferrara and related material

#### Este cards, ca. 1470–1480, Ferrara

**[Documented fact]** Fragmentary hand-painted cards associated with the Este court preserve parts of distinctive Ferrarese packs.

**[Not traced]** The consulted sources did not establish a surviving Five of Cups. It is methodologically unsafe to describe the corresponding card from modern completed “Este” decks as an original.

#### Mantegna “Tarocchi,” ca. 1460s–1470s, probably northern Italy

**[Documented fact]** These are two series of fifty educational or emblematic engravings—social estates, liberal arts, virtues, cosmic spheres—not a normal tarot pack.

**Relevance:** none to the historical Five of Cups. They contain neither a Cups suit nor a Five of Cups. Their later name “Mantegna Tarocchi” is traditional but misleading; attribution to Andrea Mantegna is rejected by modern scholarship.

#### Sola Busca, engravings ca. 1490–1491; painted copy ca. 1491, northern Italy/Veneto-Ferrara cultural sphere

**[Documented fact]** This is the oldest surviving complete tarot pack. Brera holds the uniquely complete painted set. Unlike most earlier tarot pips, all fifty-six suit cards are pictorial: human figures act among oversized cups, swords, batons, or disks. [Pinacoteca di Brera](https://pinacotecabrera.org/en/news/mostra/il-segreto-dei-segreti-i-tarocchi-sola-busca/)

**[Documented fact]** In 1907 the British Museum received black-and-white photographs of the Sola Busca set. These made its imagery available in London shortly before Pamela Colman Smith designed the Waite-Smith deck.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** Smith probably consulted Sola Busca photographs or cards. Close visual correspondences are particularly persuasive for such cards as the Three of Swords and Ten of Wands, and the hypothesis is accepted in much modern scholarship.

**[Disputed claim—the item-specific lead]** The statement that “the Sola Busca Five of Cups was the source of Pamela Colman Smith’s scene” could not be verified. The broad Sola Busca-to-Smith influence is defensible, but no consulted museum or primary source demonstrates a direct compositional dependency for this particular card. Waite-Smith’s cloaked mourner, three fallen cups, two standing cups, river, bridge, and keep are not simply a copy of an identified Sola Busca Five. The card-specific claim should therefore be cited, if at all, as an extrapolation from the deck-wide influence rather than an established source relationship.

### Early printed sheets

#### Rosenwald sheet, probably late fifteenth or early sixteenth century, Italy

**[Documented fact]** The Rosenwald material consists of uncut printed tarot sheets, important evidence for the transfer from courtly hand-painted cards to cheaper production.

**[Not traced]** No securely isolated Five of Cups with a complete, independently interpretable design was established in the material consulted. As with ordinary early pips, its expected function would be to display five suit emblems.

#### Budapest sheets, late fifteenth or early sixteenth century, probably Ferrara

**[Documented fact]** These fragmentary sheets preserve an early Ferrarese-looking printed pattern.

**[Not traced]** The Five of Cups was not established as a surviving card. Claims about its detailed symbolism would be reconstruction.

### Minchiate, Bologna and Sicily

#### Minchiate, Florence/Tuscany, sixteenth century onward

**[Documented fact]** Minchiate expanded the trump series but retained Italian suits, including Cups. Its Five of Cups is a non-narrative pip whose primary information is five cup emblems plus decoration.

The expanded trump cycle—zodiac, elements, additional virtues—does not assign an individual zodiacal sign to the Five of Cups. Mars in Scorpio is a later Golden Dawn system, not a Minchiate rule.

#### Bolognese tarocchino

**[Documented fact]** Bolognese tarot packs were shortened by removing low pip cards. In the classic 62-card tarocchino, the Five of Cups is absent because only higher numerals were retained. Thus there is often no card to describe or play.

#### Sicilian Tarocchi

**[Documented fact]** The traditional Mineo pack has 63 cards. Cups (*coppe*) rank with the round suits; the Five is retained in the standard 63-card description, while the Four may be present only in some forms. It is an ordinary low-value suit card, not a trump. [Rules and historical notes](https://www.pagat.com/tarot/sicilian.html)

### Tarot de Marseille lineage

Across the historical Marseille family, the Five of Cups is a decorative pip, not a scene.

#### Jean Noblet, Paris, ca. 1650

**[Documented fact]** Five cups are arranged around an ornamental plant or floral axis. The growth threads among and around the vessels. There is no human mourner and no spillage.

#### Jean Dodal, Lyon, ca. 1701–1715

**[Documented fact]** The same basic five-cup architecture continues, with stylized floral stems and buds occupying the center and margins. The design is hand-stencilled and not perfectly symmetrical.

#### Nicolas Conver, Marseille, 1760/1761

**[Documented fact]** Conver’s *Cinq de Coupes* uses the familiar Marseille arrangement: four cups organized in upper and lower pairs, with a fifth centrally placed, enclosed or linked by elaborate foliage.

**What changed:** printers regularized inherited ornament, altered leaves, flowers, line blocks, and stencil colors. Nothing in the surviving cards proves that these alterations encoded a secret doctrine.

#### Grimaud/Paul Marteau, 1930 onward

**[Documented fact]** The Grimaud *Ancien Tarot de Marseille* standardized a Conver-derived image and a controlled modern color scheme.

**[Esoteric attribution]** Paul Marteau subsequently interpreted the central cup as a mediating element through which accumulated “animic” riches rise and flower. That is a twentieth-century metaphysical interpretation of the geometry, not documentation of the old cardmaker’s intention.

#### Camoin–Jodorowsky, 1997

**[Documented fact]** Philippe Camoin and Alejandro Jodorowsky produced a reconstructed/redrawn Marseille deck assembled from historical patterns and restored printing details.

**[Disputed claim]** Its promotion as a recovery of a unique primordial or “authentic” Marseille tarot goes beyond what the heterogeneous surviving decks can demonstrate. Its Five of Cups remains a floral five-pip card. Jodorowsky and Marianne Costa interpret the Cups as emotional/receptive life and read the number five as the appearance of a new ideal or temptation destabilizing the stability of four. This is their modern numerological synthesis.

### Besançon pattern

**[Documented fact]** Besançon packs replace the Papess and Pope trumps with Juno and Jupiter, largely in eighteenth-century eastern France, Switzerland, and adjoining German-speaking areas where confessional sensitivities made papal figures inconvenient. This change affects trumps II and V—not the Five of Cups. Its pip generally remains a five-cup ornamental design.

### Etteilla

#### Grand Etteilla, designed 1788–1789, Paris

**[Documented fact]** Etteilla reordered and redesigned the entire pack, printed meanings on the cards, and presented it as the restored Egyptian “Book of Thoth.” The Five of Cups became card **45**.

The surviving later Grand Etteilla form shows five cups, with one below a block of four. It bears:

- upright: **HÉRITAGE**—inheritance;
- reversed: **PARENTS**—relatives/parents;
- the suit identification *Cinq de Coupe* or *de Cœur* in some editions.

[Facsimile of *Le Grand Etteilla*](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/2/2f/Le_grand_Etteilla%2C_ou%2C_l%27art_de_tirer_les_cartes_..._Le_tout_recueilli_..._%28IA_b29321220%29.pdf)

**What changed:** a game pip became a purpose-built divinatory object, with orientation-dependent verbal meanings. Its imagery itself did not yet tell Waite’s story of bereavement.

### Oswald Wirth, 1889

**[Documented fact]** Wirth’s 1889 tarot, designed under Stanislas de Guaita’s direction, comprised the twenty-two trumps only.

**Therefore:** there is no historical Wirth Five of Cups from 1889. Any modern “Wirth” minor card is a later completion and must not be attributed to Wirth’s original deck.

### Golden Dawn manuscripts and reconstructions

#### Book T, late 1880s/1890s manuscript tradition

Book T describes:

> “A WHITE Radiating Angelic Hand, holding lotuses or water-lilies,” whose flowers fall aside; “no water flows” and the cups are empty.

Mars and Scorpio appear above and below. The cups resemble a Golden Dawn magical implement. The title is “The Lord of Loss in Pleasure.” [Book T, p. 33](https://jantetteroo.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/02/Mathers_and_felkin_golden_dawn_book_t_th.pdf)

**[Documented fact]** The manuscript describes a design; it does not preserve one universally authoritative painted Golden Dawn deck.

#### Robert Wang, *Golden Dawn Tarot*, 1977

**[Modern reconstruction]** Wang, working with Israel Regardie, visually realized Book T: hand, lotuses, empty cups, and astrological signs. It is a reconstruction of order documents, not a facsimile of a single original deck.

#### Chic and Sandra Tabatha Cicero, *Golden Dawn Magical Tarot*, 2000

**[Modern reconstruction]** The Ciceros likewise render Book T with clearer color scales and Golden Dawn ritual symbolism. Differences from Wang reflect interpretive execution.

### Waite-Smith, 1909

Pamela Colman Smith depicts:

- a dark-cloaked person, usually gendered male by commentators but not definitively identified in Waite’s prose;
- three fallen or “prone” cups in front;
- two upright cups behind;
- differently colored streams or pools issuing from the fallen cups;
- a river;
- a bridge leading to a small fortified dwelling or keep;
- barren or subdued ground and gray atmosphere.

Waite’s own description reads:

> “A dark, cloaked figure, looking sideways at three prone cups; two others stand upright behind him.”

His divinatory gloss begins:

> “It is a card of loss, but something remains over.”

[A. E. Waite, *The Pictorial Key to the Tarot* (1910), Five of Cups](https://www.free-ebooks.net/philosophy/A-Pictorial-Key-to-the-Tarot/html/61)

**What changed:** the abstract Golden Dawn idea “loss in pleasure” became a psychologically legible scene. The arithmetic—three lost, two remaining—creates the now-familiar tension between grief and overlooked resources.

**Sources:** Waite’s meaning preserves the older Etteilla/Mathers inheritance and marriage vocabulary, while Smith’s scene gives “loss” unprecedented visual priority.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** Sola Busca probably encouraged Smith’s general decision to narrativize every pip. A direct Sola Busca model for this precise scene remains unproven.

### BOTA / Paul Foster Case and Jessie Burns Parke

**[Documented fact]** The BOTA pip design returns to emblematic rather than scenic imagery. In Case’s *Oracle of the Tarot*, a serpent rises within a vesica above the five cups, with a flame of illumination above its head. The serpent signifies Scorpio and, for Case, latent illuminating power.

Case retains Mars in Scorpio and Golden Dawn meanings, calling the keyword **“Defeated desire.”** [Case, *Oracle of the Tarot*, pp. 22–24](https://media-01.imu.nl/storage/divona.be/17547/oracle-of-tarot-paul-foster-case.pdf)

### Thoth, painted 1938–1943; first full commercial publication 1969

Crowley’s title is **DISAPPOINTMENT**. Frieda Harris paints:

- five transparent or crystalline chalices;
- an inverted pentagram arrangement;
- lotuses whose petals are torn or withered;
- no nourishing water flowing into the cups;
- a stagnant, greenish “dead sea”;
- Mars and Scorpio glyphs.

Crowley explains:

> “This card is ruled by Geburah in the suit of Water.”

He calls the sea “arid and stagnant” and says the inverted pentagram signifies “the triumph of matter over spirit.” The damage is the beginning of destruction rather than completed decay. [Crowley, *The Book of Thoth*, Five of Cups](https://crowley-thoth.com/symbolism/cups-05/)

**What changed:** Harris translated Book T’s empty fountain into a geometric, elemental landscape. The Waite-Smith mourner disappears; the failure occurs within the cups, flowers, water, and pentagram themselves.

### Notable later redesigns

**[Tradition/modern invention]** Most post-1970 Waite-derived decks retain some combination of three spilled and two upright cups, often revising the figure’s gender, ethnicity, age, or setting. Many recent decks emphasize grief work, resilience, trauma recovery, or “turning toward what remains.”

**[Modern invention]** The widely repeated reversed meaning “acceptance, healing, moving on” is largely a late twentieth- and twenty-first-century psychological reading. It is not Waite’s reversed list, which concerns news, return, alliances, ancestry, and false projects.

## The game

### Original function

**[Documented fact]** The Five of Cups originated as a playing card. Tarot was a trick-taking game with a permanent trump suit added to four ordinary suits: cups, swords, batons, and coins. The fifteenth-century evidence does not give it a divinatory meaning.

### Rank

In the traditional Italian round suits—Cups and Coins—the pip ranking was normally reversed:

**King, Queen, Knight, Jack, Ace, Two, Three, Four, Five, Six, Seven, Eight, Nine, Ten.**

Thus the Five is:

- below the Four;
- above the Six;
- a middling-to-low trick card;
- beaten by every trump when legally trumped.

Modern French tarot instead uses French suits and ranks each suit conventionally from King down to Ace; its Five of Hearts is the closest functional analogue but is not iconographically a Five of Cups.

In many shortened Austrian and central-European Tarock packs, the Five of the red suits is removed. It therefore has no role in those variants.

### Point value

**[Documented fact]** In traditional card-point systems, a numeral card is an “empty card”:

- nominally 1 point in three-card counting systems;
- effectively ⅓ point under fractional reckoning;
- ½ point in modern French tarot;
- zero when Austrian games count low cards singly as valueless.

The Five has no special scoring privilege. Kings, queens, knights, jacks, and the principal trumps carry the value. [Pagat: counting tarot points](https://www.pagat.com/tarot/counting.html)

### Minchiate

The Five of Cups is an ordinary reversed-rank round-suit pip. It is not one of Minchiate’s scoring trumps and has no unique bonus.

### Bolognese

The Five is normally absent from shortened tarocchino packs.

### Sicilian

The Five of Cups is retained and ranks beneath the Six and above the optional Four. It is ordinarily a low, one-nominal-point card. [Sicilian Tarocchi](https://www.pagat.com/tarot/sicilian.html)

### Trump-order disputes

The three broad historical trump-order families—often called A, B, and C by historians—concern the sequence of the triumphs in places such as Bologna/Florence, Ferrara, and Milan.

**They do not affect the rank of the Five of Cups.**

Likewise:

- Justice and Strength/Fortitude exchange positions in various packs;
- the Fool is usually unnumbered and functions as an Excuse rather than an ordinary trump in many games;
- the Besançon Juno/Jupiter substitutions affect trumps II and V;
- these facts belong to the surrounding pack, not to this pip’s identity.

## Chronology of attestation

### ca. 1418–1425: Marziano da Tortona

**[Documented fact]** Marziano’s text describes a special card pack devised for Filippo Maria Visconti, with sixteen classical gods and four bird suits.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** Historians treat it as a significant precursor to tarot’s permanent trump suit.

**[Negative finding]** It has no Cups suit and no Five of Cups. It is not a direct attestation of this card.

### 1440: Giusto Giusti diary

**[Documented fact]** Giusti records sending Sigismondo Pandolfo Malatesta “a pair of hand-painted triumph cards” (*naibi a trionfi*). This is currently earlier than the long-cited Ferrara entries.

**[Negative finding]** The record does not enumerate a Five of Cups.

### 10 February and 28 July 1442: Ferrara accounts

The Este wardrobe account paid Jacopo Sagramoro to color and paint:

> “the cups, swords, coins and batons and all the figures”

of four triumph packs. A July entry records “uno paro de carte da trionfi” for Ercole and Sigismondo d’Este. [Italian documentary discussion](https://letarot.it/Le-origini-italiane-del-Ludus-Triumphorum_pag_pg439_ita.aspx), [Farley, *A Cultural History of Tarot*](https://e-isomero.com/pdf/book/1611846398.pdf)

**[Documented fact]** This is direct evidence that the suit of Cups existed in early *trionfi* packs.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** A normal ten-pip suit strongly implies a Five of Cups, but the account does not name individual ranks.

### ca. 1450–1480: Visconti-Sforza Five of Cups

**[Documented fact]** The earliest surviving card securely identified as a Five of Cups. Morgan MS M.630.26.

### ca. 1470–1500: *Sermo de ludo cum aliis*

**[Documented fact]** The anonymous sermon condemns dice, ordinary cards, and triumphs and gives an early trump list. Its order is associated with Ferrara/type B.

**[Negative finding]** It does not assign meanings to pip cards and does not name the Five of Cups individually. [Vanderbilt-Steele manuscript description](https://collections.library.vanderbilt.edu/repositories/2/archival_objects/261035)

### ca. 1490–1491: Sola Busca

**[Documented fact]** Earliest complete surviving tarot pack and earliest complete cycle of illustrated suit cards.

### Late fifteenth/early sixteenth century: Rosenwald and Budapest sheets

**[Documented fact]** Evidence for mass-produced tarot patterns, though the survival of individual cards is fragmentary.

### Early sixteenth century: *tarocchi appropriati* and poems

**[Documented fact]** Italian social poems assign tarot trumps—and in some compositions other cards—to named people or qualities.

**[Negative finding]** No early poem consulted yields the later canonical Five-of-Cups meaning “loss in pleasure.”

### Teofilo Folengo, 1527

**[Documented fact]** Folengo’s *Chaos del Triperuno* contains an early literary use of tarot in a divinatory or emblematic setting.

**[Negative finding]** It does not establish the Golden Dawn meaning of this card.

### Seventeenth–eighteenth centuries: Marseille pips

**[Documented fact]** Noblet, Dodal, and Conver establish the recognizable floral five-cup pattern.

### 1781: Court de Gébelin and Comte de Mellet

**[Documented fact]** Volume VIII of *Le Monde primitif* launches the printed claim that tarot is an Egyptian wisdom book. De Mellet maps Hebrew letters to the trumps and proposes a divinatory procedure.

**[Negative finding]** Neither supplies the later Golden Dawn Mars-in-Scorpio attribution to the Five of Cups. Their alphabetic work applies principally to the trumps.

### 1783–1789: Etteilla

**[Documented fact]** Etteilla publishes systematic tarot cartomancy and designs the first tarot pack expressly for occult/divinatory use. Five of Cups/card 45: *Héritage / Parents*.

### 1854–1856: Éliphas Lévi, *Dogme et rituel de la haute magie*

Lévi calls tarot:

> “une œuvre monumentale et singulière, simple et forte comme l’architecture des pyramides.”

**[Esoteric attribution]** He makes tarot a synthesis of Kabbalah, magic, and the Hebrew alphabet.

**[Negative finding]** Lévi does not give a distinct, developed Five-of-Cups doctrine comparable to Book T. Quoting his general tarot claims as this card’s meaning would be misleading.

### 1870: Paul Christian

**[Documented fact]** Christian elaborates an Egyptian initiation drama and popularizes “Arcana” terminology.

**[Negative finding]** His famous Egyptian temple narrative principally reorganizes the trumps; no distinct Mars-in-Scorpio Five of Cups was traced.

### 1888: S. L. MacGregor Mathers

Mathers gives:

- upright: “Union, Junction, Marriage, Inheritance”;
- reversed: “Arrival, Return, News, Surprise, False projects.”

[Mathers, *The Tarot: Its Occult Signification*, card 45](https://www.howardnema.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/12/the-tarot-1888-by-macgregor-mathers-1.pdf)

This is essentially the Etteilla-derived cartomantic strand, not yet Book T’s “Loss in Pleasure.”

### 1889: Papus and Wirth

**[Documented fact]** Papus’s *Le Tarot des Bohémiens* spreads the Kabbalistic/Egyptian/Romani occult synthesis. Wirth supplies twenty-two redesigned trumps.

**[Negative finding]** Wirth creates no Five of Cups. Papus transmits pip divination but does not originate the Golden Dawn title.

### ca. 1888–1890s: Golden Dawn Book T

**[Documented fact]** “The Lord of Loss in Pleasure”; Mars in Scorpio; Geburah of Heh; empty cups and failing lotuses.

### 1909–1910: Waite-Smith and *Pictorial Key*

**[Documented fact]** Smith creates the definitive mourning scene; Waite publishes its description and meanings in 1910.

### 1920s–1930s: Paul Foster Case/BOTA

**[Documented fact]** Case retains the Golden Dawn decan and calls the operative idea “Defeated desire.”

### 1938–1943 / 1944 / 1969: Thoth

- 1938–1943: Harris paints under Crowley’s direction.
- 1944: Crowley publishes *The Book of Thoth*.
- 1969: first full commercial deck publication.

### Late twentieth century onward

**[Tradition/modern invention]** Psychological readings increasingly emphasize grief processing, fixation on loss, remaining possibilities, acceptance, and recovery.

### Decan and calendar

Book T puts the Five at Mars in Scorpio. Modern tables usually express that as the first decan, 0°–10° Scorpio, approximately October 23–November 1/2; calendar dates vary with astrological convention and year.

### Sephirah and world

**[Esoteric attribution]**

- Five = Geburah, severity, force, restriction, Mars.
- Cups = Heh and Water, usually located in Briah.
- Thus: Geburah in Briah or “Geburah of Heh.”

The difficulty arises because fiery/martial Geburah acts inside receptive Water. Crowley calls this a “natural antipathy.”

### Grail

**[Tradition]** Cups are often connected to chalices, Eucharistic vessels, and the Holy Grail.

**[Negative finding]** No fifteenth-century source examined identifies the suit of Cups as the Grail suit, and Book T does not call the Five a Grail card. The Grail association is a later Christian-romantic and occult elaboration of vessel symbolism.

### Tzaddi/Heh swap

**[Documented fact but card-irrelevant]** Crowley’s “Tzaddi is not the Star” alteration exchanges the Hebrew-letter placements of the Emperor and Star in his Thelemic system.

It does **not** change the Five of Cups:

- the pip remains Geburah in the Water/Heh suit;
- it remains Mars in Scorpio;
- its Thoth title remains *Disappointment*.

Confusing the trump letter **Heh** in the Emperor/Star dispute with the suit-level Heh of Cups is a category error.

## Divinatory meanings by author

Quotations are kept short; editions and sources are identified.

### Etteilla, 1780s; Grand Etteilla card 45

- Upright: **“Héritage”**—inheritance.
- Reversed: **“Parents”**—parents, relatives, family lineage.

**[Documented tradition]** These words appear on the card. The stark upright/reversed pair explains the later succession, patrimony, ancestry, and returning-relative vocabulary.

### Mathers, 1888

- Upright: **“Union, Junction, Marriage, Inheritance.”**
- Reversed: **“Arrival, Return, News, Surprise, False projects.”**

**[Documented tradition]** Mathers’s public booklet preserves the Etteilla family of meanings. It must be distinguished from the later Golden Dawn Book T teaching.

### Golden Dawn Book T

Book T gives:

> “Death, or end of pleasure: disappointment, sorrow and loss in those things from which pleasure is expected.”

It adds sadness, treachery, deceit, ill-will, kindness poorly repaid, unforeseen anxiety, broken engagement, unkind friendship, and lost friendship.

Book T does not use Waite-style reversal. Meanings are modified by dignity—surrounding cards, elemental relations, or context.

### Waite, 1910

- Upright: loss, but not total loss; inheritance, patrimony, transmission falling short of expectation; marriage with bitterness or frustration.
- Reversed: news, alliances, affinity, blood relationship, ancestry, return, false projects.

Waite also prints a secondary, more favorable list elsewhere: happy marriage, patrimony, legacies, gifts, and success in enterprise. The tension reflects his compilation of divergent inherited lists.

### Crowley, 1944

Crowley’s title is **Disappointment**. His explanation centers on frustrated anticipated pleasure, empty cups, withering lotuses, stagnant water, and the first movement toward destruction.

Crowley generally rejects mechanical upside-down reversals; dignity and interaction with neighboring cards govern expression.

### Paul Foster Case

Case writes:

> “No meaning of this card is really good, but they are worse if ill-dignified.”

His list includes loss in pleasure, vain regret, sorrow concerning something greatly desired, treachery, deceit, unexpected trouble, broken engagement, and broken friendship. Keyword: **“Defeated desire.”**

### Rachel Pollack

**[Tradition/psychological interpretation]** In *Seventy-Eight Degrees of Wisdom*, Pollack reads the Waite-Smith image as genuine sorrow combined with narrowed perception: grief attends the three lost cups while emotional possibility remains in the two upright cups. The bridge suggests a route back toward ordinary life.

Pollack does not reduce the card to “think positive”; loss must be acknowledged before remaining life can be reclaimed.

**[Copyright note]** Only a brief paraphrase is supplied because the consulted online material did not provide an authoritative full-text edition of the relevant passage.

### Mary K. Greer

**[Tradition/modern practice]** Greer treats the card as grief, regret, bereavement, disappointment, and attention fixed on what is gone. Her reversal method is deliberately plural rather than a single dictionary antonym: a reversed card can be blocked, internalized, delayed, excessive, diminished, projected, or turned toward recovery.

Therefore “Five of Cups reversed = healing” is one Greer-compatible possibility, not her only permitted meaning.

**[Not traced]** A freely accessible primary page containing her full card entry was not located. Claims that she supplies one mandatory reversed keyword should be treated cautiously.

## Esoteric and symbolic readings

### Kabbalistic

**First securely traced card-specific appearance:** Golden Dawn Book T.

Five is Geburah—severity, judgment, strength, Mars—operating in Heh/Water. The receptive emotional matrix cannot preserve the stable satisfaction of the Four when martial force enters it. Hence broken pleasure.

**Spreaders:** Golden Dawn initiates; Mathers and Felkin manuscripts; Crowley; Regardie; Case; Wang; the Ciceros.

### Astrological

**First securely traced card-specific appearance:** Golden Dawn Book T.

Mars rules traditional Scorpio and is therefore dignified by sign, but Crowley argues that Scorpio’s lower watery aspect becomes putrefactive. Strong Mars prevents complete passive decay while initiating destructive disturbance.

This produces an important conflict:

- classical dignity suggests focused, forceful Mars;
- Book T/Thoth symbolism reads the combination pessimistically because it occurs in Water and Geburah;
- modern astrologers sometimes dispute the assumption that Mars in its own sign must signify emotional failure.

### Hermetic

The card expresses an incompatibility of active martial force and receptive Water. In Golden Dawn terms it belongs to a network of elemental weapons, color scales, decans, angelic names, and Qabalistic worlds.

Book T names the decan angels **Leuviah** and **Pahaliah** in common transliteration.

### Alchemical

**[Modern esoteric interpretation]** Crowley’s stagnant “dead sea,” torn petals, and Scorpio’s putrefactive capacity invite comparison with *putrefactio* or *nigredo*: desired form breaks down before transformation.

**[Negative finding]** Neither the Visconti card nor Marseille pip supplies evidence that Renaissance cardmakers intended an alchemical stage. Calling it “the alchemical card of putrefaction” is a twentieth-century Thoth-based interpretation, not an ancient key.

### Christian and Grail

**[Tradition]** The cup may evoke:

- Eucharistic chalice;
- Christ’s cup of suffering;
- the Grail;
- the vessel of grace;
- abundance withheld or spilled.

In a Christianized reading, three fallen cups can signify worldly attachments while two preserved cups suggest faith and charity, or grace remaining after loss.

**[Modern invention]** Those allocations are reader-made. Waite does not identify the two standing cups as specific theological virtues.

### Jungian

**[Modern invention grounded in analytical psychology]** The cloaked figure can be read as an ego overwhelmed by grief; the spill as lost libido or psychic investment; the unseen cups as unintegrated resources; river and bridge as a threshold between affective states; the distant keep as restored psychic containment.

**First appearance:** no single definitive “first Jungian Five of Cups” was established. Jung himself did not publish a canonical card-by-card tarot system.

**Spreaders:** postwar occult psychologists, humanistic tarot authors, Pollack, Sallie Nichols’s broader archetypal tarot work, Greer, and contemporary therapeutic readers.

Claims that Jung personally interpreted this card are unsupported.

## Controversies and disputes

### Egyptian origin

**[Legend]** Court de Gébelin claimed in 1781 that tarot was an Egyptian sacred book surviving beneath an Italian card game. De Mellet expanded the claim into a Book of Thoth and divinatory alphabet.

**Evidence:** no Egyptian tarot pack, text, image sequence, or transmission document predates the Italian cards. Gébelin wrote before Egyptian hieroglyphs were deciphered and offered fanciful Egyptian etymologies without hieroglyphic evidence.

**Card-specific result:** nothing connects the Five of Cups to pharaonic Egypt. Etteilla’s Egyptian styling is eighteenth-century redesign, not restoration.

### Romani or “Gypsy” transmission

**[Legend]** Nineteenth-century writers, especially Jean-Alexandre Vaillant and Papus, popularized the idea that Romani people carried tarot from Egypt or India into Europe.

**Evidence against:** tarot is documented in northern Italian court and municipal records before any evidence links Romani fortune-telling to tarot. Linguistic and historical evidence places Romani origins in India, not Egypt.

The word “Gypsy” reflects the old European misidentification of Romani people as Egyptians; it is not evidence of tarot transmission.

### Cathar origin

**[Disputed claim/modern legend]** Some twentieth-century writers interpret the trumps as concealed Cathar theology or a survival of southern French heresy.

**Card-specific problem:** the Five of Cups is an Italian suit card already present in Milanese tarot; no Cathar manuscript, ritual, or iconographic program names it. Marseille manufacture in France is later than Italian tarot.

### Templar origin

**[Legend]** Claims that the Knights Templar preserved tarot symbolism or encoded their doctrine in the cards lack a documentary chain.

**Card-specific result:** no Templar Five of Cups, cup diagram, or transmission record exists.

### Mamluk and Islamic playing-card ancestry

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** European suits of cups, swords, coins, and polo-sticks/batons plausibly derive from or share ancestry with Mamluk playing cards.

This is evidence about playing-card forms, not proof that the Five of Cups carried Islamic esoteric meaning. The Mamluk connection is materially and structurally plausible; it does not support Gébelin’s pharaonic Book of Thoth.

### Sola Busca as the source of Smith’s Five of Cups

**Verdict: partly supported at deck level, unverified card by card.**

- Smith’s access to Sola Busca photographs in London is plausible.
- Several Waite-Smith pips have strong compositional analogues.
- The exact Five-of-Cups mourning scene was not demonstrated to be copied from the Sola Busca Five.
- Treat categorical statements of direct derivation as **disputed**.

### Gender and identity of the Waite-Smith figure

**[Disputed]** Commentators call the figure a man, woman, monk, widower, exile, survivor, or bereaved traveler.

Waite says only “figure.” The heavy cloak hides most identifying features. No surviving statement by Smith settles the identity. Gendered certainty is interpretive.

### Contents of the fallen cups

**[Disputed]** Modern readers call the red fluid wine or blood and the green fluid poison, water, bile, or spoiled emotion.

The printing shows colored spills but neither Waite nor Smith identifies their substances. “Blood” and “poison” are interpretive additions.

### Three lost, two full

Waite says three cups have been “taken” and two are left. The image clearly shows three prone and two standing cups, but it does not prove the upright cups are full. “Two full cups” is customary shorthand rather than a precisely visible fact in every printing.

### “Do not cry over spilled milk”

**[Modern tradition]** This popular proverb summarizes the picture effectively but does not appear in Waite’s entry and is not an ancient tarot meaning.

### Reversed recovery

**[Modern invention/tradition]** “Acceptance, forgiveness, moving on, healing” is now nearly universal in popular guidebooks. Waite’s actual reversed list is news, alliances, affinity, ancestry, return, and false projects. The modern reading arose by visually imagining the figure turning toward the remaining cups.

### Misprints and reproduction traditions

The Thoth deck’s complicated publication history produced color and production differences among editions. No consulted source established a specific Five-of-Cups printing error that generated a major interpretive tradition.

Marseille stencil irregularities are sometimes treated as intentionally encoded color symbolism. Because historical packs vary substantially and stencil registration was imperfect, claims based on a single stray color patch require comparison across impressions.

### Historical correction

Michael Dummett, Thierry Depaulis, Ronald Decker, Stuart Kaplan, Robert M. Place, and Helen Farley differ in emphasis, but their documentary work supports a common core:

1. tarot arose in fifteenth-century northern Italy as a card game;
2. the ordinary suit cards precede tarot’s occult systems;
3. systematic occult tarot appears in late eighteenth-century France;
4. Egyptian, primordial Kabbalistic, Romani, Templar, and Cathar origin stories lack the required early evidence.

Dummett’s memorable characterization of occult tarot as an extraordinarily successful propaganda construction is polemical in tone but rests on the chronological gulf between fifteenth-century game records and eighteenth-century occult claims.

## Open questions

1. **Exact dating of the Visconti-Sforza Five:** museum dating remains a range, and workshop participation within the Bembo circle is not completely settled.

2. **Sola Busca authorship:** the designer, engraver, painter, patron, and intended historical-alchemical program remain subjects of debate.

3. **Smith’s immediate model:** Sola Busca influenced her pip project, but no diary, sketchbook, or surviving letter identifies the precise source of the Five-of-Cups mourner.

4. **Meaning of the colored spills:** neither Waite nor Smith explains them.

5. **Identity and gender of the figure:** unresolved by primary evidence.

6. **Source of Waite’s scenic details:** the bridge and keep may be Smith’s invention, a literary visualization of “something remains,” or derived from an unidentified visual source.

7. **Transition from Etteilla’s inheritance to Golden Dawn loss:** Book T combines inherited cartomantic terms with a decanic and Qabalistic system, but the exact editorial contributions of Mathers, Westcott, Felkin, and other Golden Dawn members are difficult to disentangle.

8. **Earliest Jungian use:** postwar psychological readings are evident, but no defensible single first author for a specifically Jungian Five of Cups was identified.

9. **Grail interpretation:** symbolically attractive, but no early tarot document was found that identifies Cups as the Grail suit.

10. **Card-specific cultural quotations:** claims involving Eliot, Yeats, Calvino, Bergman, Jodorowsky’s films, paintings, or buildings generally proved to be broad tarot associations rather than verifiable quotations of this pip.
