# Eight of Cups: Research Dossier

## Method and evidence labels

This dossier distinguishes six kinds of statement:

- **[Documented fact]** Directly supported by a surviving card, archival record, contemporary text, or reliable catalogue.
- **[Scholarly reconstruction]** An inference made from surviving objects, documents, comparative iconography, or reconstructed game rules.
- **[Tradition]** A meaning or association transmitted within cartomancy or tarot practice, without implying historical antiquity.
- **[Esoteric attribution]** A deliberate occult correspondence assigned by a named author or school.
- **[Disputed claim]** A proposition with competing interpretations or inadequate evidence.
- **[Legend / modern invention]** A retrospective origin story or recent interpretation for which no early evidence has been found.

A crucial limitation governs the whole dossier: the Eight of Cups is a numeral or “pip” card, not a trump. Consequently, many subjects commonly discussed in tarot histories—Hebrew-letter assignments to the twenty-two trumps, regional trump orders, the Papess/Juno substitution, the Fool’s numbering, and Crowley’s Tzaddi–Heh controversy—do not directly alter this card. They are included below as contextual history and explicitly marked as such.

### Immediate findings on the requested leads

- **[Documented fact]** “The Lord of Abandoned Success,” Saturn in the first decan of Pisces, is genuinely present in the Golden Dawn’s *Book T*. The surviving/public text describes two lotus flowers pouring into only four of eight cups and gives meanings including temporary success, abandonment, indolence, wandering, and instability. [Golden Dawn manuscript transcription](https://www.tarrdaniel.com/documents/Thelemagick/gd/publication/english/golden_dawn_manuscripts.html)

- **[Documented fact]** Crowley retained Saturn in Pisces but renamed the card **Indolence**. His description closely follows and intensifies *Book T*: broken shallow cups, drooping lotuses, stagnant pools, dark muddy water, leaden clouds, and a diseased landscape. [1944 *Book of Thoth* scan](https://www.rahoorkhuit.net/library/crowley/equinox/volume3/vol_3_no_05.pdf)

- **[Disputed claim, rejected in its strong form]** The Sola Busca deck was an important precedent for fully illustrated pip cards and demonstrably influenced several Waite–Smith designs. But its **Eight of Cups is not the direct source of Smith’s departing traveller**. No documentary evidence or close iconographic correspondence located in this research supports that specific claim. The influence is deck-wide and card-specific elsewhere—notably the Three of Swords and Ten of Wands—not a demonstrated Eight-of-Cups borrowing.

- **[Documented fact]** The Marseille Eight is a non-narrative pip: usually eight cups in a 3–2–3 arrangement around floral and foliate ornament.

- **[Documented fact]** Waite’s *Pictorial Key* explicitly acknowledges that the departing-man image conflicts with older cartomantic meanings, which he calls “entirely antithetical.”

# Iconography deck by deck

## Fifteenth-century foundations

### Visconti di Modrone / Cary–Yale, Milan, approximately 1440s–1450s

**Status:** [Documented object]; date and workshop attribution partly [scholarly reconstruction].

The surviving Eight of Cups has eight large gold chalices displayed vertically against a richly decorated ground. It is a counting image, not a scene: there is no traveller, moon, landscape, loss, abandoned feast, or explicit emotional narrative. The card’s communicative work is primarily suit and rank.

The chalices are courtly objects, materially consistent with the luxury character of the pack: opaque paint and precious-metal decoration on pasteboard. Their scale and patterned background make possession and magnificence visible, but interpreting them as “emotional fulfilment” or “abandonment” would be retrospective.

The card is held with the Cary collection at Yale. It is often called “Cary–Yale,” while *Visconti di Modrone* is the historically preferable pack-name in much recent scholarship. The online object metadata gives Bonifacio Bembo and a broad fifteenth-century date; exact authorship remains an attribution, not a signature. [Object image and Yale provenance](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Cary-Yale_Tarot_deck_-_Eight_of_Cups.jpg)

### Brambilla / Visconti-Brambilla, Milan, circa 1440s

**Status:** [Documented fragmentary pack].

The Pinacoteca di Brera preserves forty-eight cards from this luxury Milanese pack. A surviving Eight of Cups has not been securely identified in the accessible catalogue material consulted. It is therefore improper to describe one as though it survived.

The extant cards show the same general Milanese luxury idiom: isolated suit emblems on punched and gilded grounds. If an Eight once existed, a regular pip arrangement is likely, but that is **[scholarly reconstruction]**, not evidence from a surviving card. The Brera distinguishes this fragment from the complete Sola Busca pack. [Brera exhibition record](https://pinacotecabrera.org/en/news/mostra/il-segreto-dei-segreti-i-tarocchi-sola-busca/)

### Pierpont Morgan–Bergamo Visconti-Sforza, Milan, circa 1450s

**Status:** [Documented pack; absent card].

The most familiar Visconti-Sforza group is divided chiefly among the Morgan Library, Accademia Carrara, and Colleoni collection. The Morgan’s online inventory contains the Ace, Three, Five, and Nine of Cups, but not the Eight. Some reconstructed commercial decks supply missing cards; such replacements must not be mistaken for fifteenth-century originals.

The surviving suit cards remain emblematic rather than scenic. The Morgan describes the deck as aristocratic gaming equipment and explicitly notes that occult associations came centuries later. [Morgan catalogue](https://www.themorgan.org/collection/tarot-cards)

### Other Milanese and Ferrarese hand-painted fragments

**Status:** [Documented fragments; card-specific absence].

The Este, Charles VI/Gringonneur, and related Ferrarese or north-Italian fragments do not provide a securely preserved Eight of Cups in the catalogues consulted. Where commercial “complete” editions show one, it is generally a modern completion based on related pips.

### Rosenwald and Budapest sheets, late fifteenth or early sixteenth century

**Status:** [Documented printed sheets; uncertain individual survival].

These woodcut sheets are critical for reconstructing early popular tarot imagery, especially the trumps, but no securely isolated, fully preserved Eight of Cups could be verified from the accessible sheet descriptions used here. Their importance to this card is typological: inexpensive printed packs continued the practice of treating numeral suit cards as arrangements of emblems rather than narrative allegories.

### Mantegna “Tarocchi,” circa 1460s

**Status:** [Documented prints; misleading traditional name].

The so-called Mantegna Tarocchi are fifty didactic engravings organized into social, artistic, cosmological, and celestial series. They are neither a normal tarot pack nor attributable to Mantegna. They have no suit of Cups and no Eight of Cups. Any direct relationship is comparative Renaissance iconography, not descent.

### Sola Busca, Venice/Ferrara cultural orbit, engraved 1491; painted copy circa 1490s

**Status:** [Documented complete pack].

The Sola Busca is the earliest surviving complete seventy-eight-card tarot pack and the earliest surviving tarot with fully illustrated numeral cards throughout. Its engraved state is dated by the Venetian-era inscription conventionally converted to 1491. The unique painted pack is now at the Pinacoteca di Brera. [Brera](https://pinacotecabrera.org/en/news/mostra/il-segreto-dei-segreti-i-tarocchi-sola-busca/), [Le Tarot cultural association](https://www.letarot.it/Sola-Busca-Tarot_pag_pg824_eng.aspx), [World of Playing Cards](https://www.wopc.co.uk/italy/sola-busca-tarocchi)

Its Eight of Cups is a pictorial composition involving the vessels as physical objects, unlike the abstract luxury pips of the Visconti group. The surviving image is catalogued as card 71 in one modern file sequence. [Public-domain image](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Sola_Busca_tarot_card_71.jpg)

#### Was it the source for Pamela Colman Smith’s Eight?

**Finding: not demonstrated.**

- **[Documented fact]** British Museum photographs of the Sola Busca cards were available in London before the Waite–Smith deck.
- **[Scholarly reconstruction]** Smith almost certainly used the photographs for particular cards whose compositions closely agree.
- **[Disputed claim]** Some popular accounts generalize this into “the Sola Busca pips were the source of Smith’s pips.”
- **[Unsupported card-specific claim]** No close match connects the Sola Busca Eight of Cups with Smith’s traveller leaving eight vessels.
- **Conclusion:** Sola Busca supplied a precedent and, in some cards, visual material. Smith’s Eight of Cups is better explained by the Golden Dawn phrase **“Abandoned Success”**, the Golden Dawn references to things being thrown aside and to journeying, and Waite’s divinatory gloss.

This distinction matters: influence on a deck is not proof of copying on every card.

## Regional traditional packs

### Tarot de Marseille lineage

#### Jean Noblet, Paris, circa 1650

**Status:** [Documented pack; conventional date].

Noblet’s Cups pips are emblematic and ornamental. The Eight is composed from repeated chalices with vegetal filling in the spaces. There is no human subject and no obvious temporal action. The card is read by count, suit, symmetry, and decorative structure only when a reader elects to interpret those features.

Calling Noblet “Tarot de Marseille” is conventional but geographically misleading: it was printed in Paris. Thierry Depaulis distinguishes early Type I patterns, including Noblet and Dodal, from later Type II forms and warns against treating “Marseille” as a single ancient unchanging design. [Depaulis bibliography and pattern summary](https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tarot_de_Marseille)

#### Jean Dodal, Lyon, circa 1701–1715

**Status:** [Documented pack].

The Eight retains a non-scenic arrangement of eight chalices interwoven with stems and flowers. Compared with Noblet, the drawing is more regularized and the ornamental vocabulary closer to what later restorers present as canonical Marseille. Any interpretation of the central vegetation as a Grail tree, axis mundi, or kundalini channel is **[modern esoteric reading]**, not a documented printer’s explanation.

#### Nicolas Conver, Marseille, traditional date 1760

**Status:** [Documented pattern; dating caveat].

The familiar Conver Eight places cups in rows of three, two, and three. A rosette or star-like flower occupies the middle; palmettes, stems, leaves, and tulip-like terminals fill the intervals. Roman numeral VIII appears at the sides in some impressions.

A significant catalogue caveat: the commonly reproduced BnF witness associated with “Conver 1760” is catalogued to a later period, approximately 1809–1833, although the model is traditionally dated 1760. Treating every modern scan as an impression physically printed in 1760 is unsafe. [Description and BnF provenance](https://marselia.app/fr/cards/huit-de-coupe), [digital plate](https://foolmade.art/archive/conver-tarot/cups-08)

#### Grimaud, nineteenth–twentieth centuries

**Status:** [Documented commercial continuation].

Grimaud standardized and recoloured the Type II Marseille pattern for mass publication. The Eight remained a floral pip, generally preserving the 3–2–3 vessel structure. Registration, palette, line cleanup, and typography changed; the card was not converted into a Waite-style scene.

#### Camoin–Jodorowsky, published 1997

**Status:** [Modern reconstruction and redesign].

Philippe Camoin and Alejandro Jodorowsky presented their deck as a restoration synthesizing details from older Marseille packs. For the Eight, the basic floral pip structure remains. Jodorowsky and Marianne Costa subsequently interpreted pip arrangements through number, suit, bodily development, receptive/active polarity, and visual direction.

Their Italian summary gives the Eight of Cups as:

> “*felicità, sforzo continuo per raggiungere il successo totale*”

“happiness, continual effort to attain total success,” followed by present joy justified by a painful past. This is **[modern esoteric interpretation]**, not a recovered eighteenth-century Marseille meaning. [Camoin text](https://it.camoin.com/tarot/Il-libretto-L-Arte-del-Tarocco-su.html)

### Minchiate, Florence and Tuscany, sixteenth–nineteenth centuries

**Status:** [Documented game family].

Minchiate expanded the trump sequence to forty-one trumps but retained four Italian suits. The Eight of Cups remained a pip card. The extra virtues, zodiacal signs, and elements belonged to the trump series; they did not turn the Eight of Cups into an astrological picture.

In traditional ranking, Cups was one of the round suits. Numerals normally ranked in reverse order: Ace high, then 2, 3, and so on, with 10 lowest. Thus the Eight was a relatively low card, below Seven and above Nine when stated from high to low. It had no individual occult title.

### Bolognese tarocchino

**Status:** [Documented rules and surviving pattern].

Bologna reduced the pack to sixty-two cards by removing numeral cards 2–5 from every suit. The Eight of Cups survives.

In Cups and Coins, ranking is:

**King, Queen, Knight, Jack, Ace, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10.**

Thus the Eight ranks below Seven and above Nine. It is an ordinary low-counting card, often called a *scartino* in play and worth no special independent bonus. [Rules of Ottocento](https://www.pagat.com/tarot/ottocento.html)

### Sicilian tarot

**Status:** [Documented regional game].

The sixty-three-card traditional Sicilian pack preserves Cups, Coins, Batons, and Swords but is differently shortened. In Cups the sequence includes 10, 9, 8, 7, 6, 5 beneath the courts; according to the published rules, the numeral ranking here is descending by face number, so Eight beats Seven.

The Eight is not a trump, honour, or special counting card. Its contribution is normally as one card in a trick or combination. [Sicilian rules](https://www.pagat.com/tarot/sicilian.html)

### Besançon pattern

**Status:** [Documented regional renaming, irrelevant to the pip’s identity].

In Swiss/French Besançon-type tarot, the Papess and Pope were replaced by **Juno and Jupiter**, a change documented in eighteenth-century Swiss and eastern-French production and often interpreted as accommodation to confessional sensitivities. The Eight of Cups itself generally remained an ornamental *Huit de Coupe*.

### German and Austrian Tarock

**Status:** [Documented game development].

Modern Central European Tarock normally uses French-suited packs. The Italian Cup suit becomes Hearts; consequently there may be no card literally called *Acht der Kelche* in the game deck. In 54-card packs, only four low red-suit cards are retained—often Ace through Four—so the Eight of Hearts/Cups is normally absent. “Acht der Kelche” belongs chiefly to German-language occult decks retaining Italian suit emblems.

## French occult and cartomantic redesigns

### Court de Gébelin and Comte de Mellet, 1781

**Status:** [Documented occult writings; no distinct Eight design].

Volume VIII of *Monde primitif* proposed that the tarot preserved an Egyptian sacred book. De Mellet connected the twenty-two trumps with the Hebrew alphabet and gave a method of divination. Neither author supplied a card-specific mystical iconography or Hebrew-letter attribution for the Eight of Cups.

Their importance is systemic: they changed the cultural category of tarot from a game that might be used for fortune-telling into a supposed ancient occult scripture. Dummett emphasizes that their Egyptian etymologies preceded the decipherment of Egyptian writing and have no linguistic basis. [Dummett’s discussion](https://www.nybooks.com/articles/1981/05/14/origins-of-tarot/)

### Grand Etteilla, Paris, circa 1788–1789

**Status:** [Documented cartomantic deck and text].

In the commonly reproduced Grand Etteilla sequence, the Eight of Cups is **card 42**. It is labelled:

- *FILE BLONDE* upright: “blond girl”
- *SATISFACTION* reversed

It also identifies itself as *Le Huit de Coupe* and equates the tarot suit with *Le Huit de Cœur*, Eight of Hearts. Eight chalices appear in rows above an emblematic lower panel. This is a labelled cartomantic card, not merely an unlabeled game pip. [Digitized *Le grand Etteilla*](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/2/2f/Le_grand_Etteilla%2C_ou%2C_l%27art_de_tirer_les_cartes_..._Le_tout_recueilli_..._%28IA_b29321220%29.pdf)

Etteilla’s written synonymic tradition expands the pair:

- Upright: a fair or blond girl; associated ideas of friendship, attachment, tenderness.
- Reversed: satisfaction, diversion, feast, public joy, entertainment, excuse or exculpation in extended synonym lists.

This is the source family behind Mathers’s 1888 meaning and Waite’s “antithetical” alternatives.

### Papus, *Le Tarot des Bohémiens*, 1889

**Status:** [Documented esoteric synthesis].

Papus derives pip meanings mechanically from suit and number. His Eight of Cups is:

> “*Insuccès partiel de l’Amour. L’amour ne réussit qu’en partie.*”

“Partial failure of Love. Love succeeds only in part.” This occurs in the numerical synthesis around pp. 318–320 of the 1889 French edition. [Source transcription and page references](https://marselia.app/fr/cards/huit-de-coupe)

Papus’s wording belongs to his theoretical grid; it is neither Etteilla’s “fair girl” nor the later Waite–Smith act of departure.

### Oswald Wirth, 1889 and 1926

**Status:** [Documented absence].

Wirth’s original 1889 *22 Arcanes du Tarot kabbalistique* contained only the twenty-two trumps. He therefore created **no Eight of Cups**. The 1926 revision and 1927 *Tarot des imagiers du Moyen Âge* likewise center on the trumps.

Any seventy-eight-card “Oswald Wirth Tarot” is a later publisher’s completion. Its minor cards cannot be cited as Wirth’s 1889 work. [History of Wirth’s twenty-two cards](https://foolmade.art/archive/wirth-tarot), [minor-card caveat](https://learntarot.com/osdesc.htm)

## Golden Dawn

### *Book T*, internal material late 1880s–1890s; publicly printed in altered/order form in 1912

**Status:** [Documented esoteric attribution].

Title:

> “THE LORD OF ABANDONED SUCCESS — EIGHT OF CUPS”

Image prescription: a hand holds lotus or water-lily stems. Only two flowers appear, bending above the two central cups and pouring white water. Those cups are not full; water passes into only two lower cups. The three upper cups remain empty. Saturn and Pisces are displayed.

The divinatory text reads, in part:

> “Temporary success, but without further result. Things thrown aside as soon as gained. No lasting even in the matter in hand.”

It continues with indolence in success, journeying from place to place, misery without cause, seeking riches, and instability according to dignity. The summary is “Success abandoned, decline of interest in anything.” [Transcribed *Book T*](https://www.tarrdaniel.com/documents/Thelemagick/gd/publication/english/golden_dawn_manuscripts.html)

Correspondences:

- Saturn in Pisces
- First decan, 0°–10° Pisces
- Hod of Heh
- World: Briah
- Angels: Vavaliah and Yelahiah in commonly printed Golden Dawn tables
- Number eight: Hod
- Suit Cups: Water; Heh of the four-letter divine name

#### Wang and Cicero reconstructions

**Status:** [Modern reconstructions of Golden Dawn instructions].

Robert Wang’s *Golden Dawn Tarot* and the Ciceros’ reconstructions visualize the *Book T* prescription rather than reproducing a securely surviving original Eight by a named Golden Dawn artist.

They generally show:

- eight chalices;
- a cloud-emerging hand;
- two lotus blossoms;
- restricted streams reaching only part of the array;
- Saturn and Pisces emblems;
- the title or keyword “Abandoned Success.”

The Ciceros explicitly give decan, Sephirah Hod, World Briah, and the interpretation “Success abandoned, decline of interest, temporary success that is not followed up, indolence in accomplishments.” [Cicero reconstruction text](https://kobayashi-dxn.s3.eu-west-2.amazonaws.com/Golden%20Dawn/cicero-%20secrets%20of%20the%20golden%20dawn%20temple/Cicero_-_Self_Initiation_into_%282%29.pdf)

The cards are evidence of twentieth-century reconstruction, not proof of the exact appearance of every original temple deck.

## Waite–Smith, London, 1909

**Status:** [Documented deck].

Pamela Colman Smith depicts a red-cloaked, red-booted traveller walking away from eight upright gold cups. The cups stand in the foreground in two tiers, with a conspicuous gap in the upper row. The figure uses a staff and moves toward rugged land and distant heights. Water lies between the foreground and the mountains. Above is a lunar form commonly described as an eclipse or a crescent superimposed on a full disc.

Waite called the figure male, but Smith supplied no identifying label. Claims that it is a monk, pilgrim, hermit, woman, lunar initiate, or Waite himself are **[modern interpretation]**.

Waite’s own description is:

> “A man of dejected aspect is deserting the cups of his felicity, enterprise, undertaking or previous concern.”

He then warns:

> “The card speaks for itself on the surface, but other readings are entirely antithetical”

and lists joy, mildness, timidity, honour, and modesty. In practice, he says, it indicates “the decline of a matter,” or that something regarded as important is of slight consequence. Reversed: “Great joy, happiness, feasting.” [*Pictorial Key* card entry](https://citeseerx.ist.psu.edu/document?doi=7a15961664bced51d1162198bce46d173e40dd41&repid=rep1&type=pdf)

### What changed and what it meant

Smith transformed:

1. an inherited count of eight cups;
2. Etteilla/Mathers cartomantic meanings;
3. Golden Dawn “abandoned success” and journeying language;

into a continuous pictorial action.

The cups are not overturned or broken. The figure chooses to leave them. That visual decision created the modern card’s dominant meanings: walking away, voluntary withdrawal, disappointment after attainment, and quest for deeper purpose.

### The “missing ninth cup”

The gap in the upper tier is often read today as an absent ninth cup—happiness or fulfilment not yet found. This is an effective interpretive observation but **[modern invention]**. Waite does not mention a missing cup, and no surviving design memorandum from Smith proves that she intended the gap as a lost Nine of Cups.

### Lunar interpretations

The sky object has been read as:

- a lunar eclipse;
- waning moon;
- crescent and full moon together;
- passage from completion into a new phase;
- conscious and unconscious united.

Only the drawn composite disc is documented. Its precise astronomical or occult meaning remains **[disputed]**.

## BOTA / Paul Foster Case

**Status:** [Documented Golden Dawn-derived system; deck caveat].

Paul Foster Case and Jessie Burns Parke created the BOTA tarot, but the familiar published BOTA deck is principally a trump deck for colouring and meditation. A canonical Parke-drawn scenic Eight of Cups comparable to Smith’s card is not part of the original standard BOTA deck.

In *Oracle of the Tarot*, Case retains the Golden Dawn framework. His Eight concerns temporary or incomplete success, possible abandonment, and the need to choose a higher course. Accessible copies summarize the idea that success may be gained but not followed up, together with a necessity for choosing the highest expression available. [Digitized *Oracle of the Tarot*](https://media-01.imu.nl/storage/divona.be/17547/oracle-of-tarot-paul-foster-case.pdf)

Commercial seventy-eight-card products labelled “BOTA-style” or completed after Case should be separated from Case and Parke’s documented originals.

## Crowley–Harris Thoth Tarot

### Design, 1938–1943; book 1944; deck issued 1969

**Status:** [Documented].

Aleister Crowley commissioned Lady Frieda Harris to paint the cards, and the project expanded from an anticipated short undertaking into work from 1938 to 1943. *The Book of Thoth* appeared in 1944 with colour plates; the complete deck was commercially issued only later, conventionally dated 1969.

The Eight of Cups is titled **INDOLENCE**.

Crowley writes:

> “The Eight, Hod, in the suit of Water, governs this card.”

He describes Mercury’s Hod influence as overwhelmed by Saturn in Pisces:

> “Pisces is calm but stagnant water; and Saturn deadens it completely.”

The painting shows eight shallow cups in three rows. The top three are empty. Only two drooping flowers supply the middle cups, whose scant water reaches the lowest cups without filling them. Behind them are dark lagoons or pools beneath leaden clouds and a pallid horizon. Crowley calls the terrain incapable of cultivation and conducive only to disease and “miasmatic poison.” [*Book of Thoth*, pp. 200–201 in the 1944 edition scan](https://www.rahoorkhuit.net/library/crowley/equinox/volume3/vol_3_no_05.pdf)

### Harris’s contribution

Harris converted the Golden Dawn diagram into an atmospheric, geometrically ordered painted field. The visual system is neither Smith’s narrative departure nor Marseille ornament. The cups are themselves depleted, the water system fails, and the environment is poisoned. “Indolence” therefore signifies not merely walking away but energy unable or unwilling to circulate.

### Printing issue

**[Documented publishing history, but card-state details require copy comparison].** Early colour Thoth editions are reported to contain colour and printing variations, including differences affecting the Eight of Cups. These should be treated as production states, not newly authorized symbolism, unless matched to Harris’s paintings or Crowley’s instructions.

## Notable modern redesigns

### Thoth-derived decks

They commonly preserve the title **Indolence**, Saturn in Pisces, Hod, stagnant water, and incomplete flow. The human traveller is often absent.

### RWS-derived decks

Most reproduce one or more of:

- a solitary departing person;
- eight intact vessels;
- a night journey;
- mountains or a difficult path;
- a gap among the cups;
- withdrawal from an apparently adequate but emotionally empty situation.

Changing the figure’s race, gender, age, clothing, or environment is usually a modern representational revision, not a change in the underlying Waite–Smith grammar.

### Rachel Pollack’s Shining Tribe Tarot, 1990s

**Status:** [Documented modern redesign].

Pollack renamed the suits Trees, Rivers, Birds, and Stones; Cups becomes Rivers. Her stated aim was to emphasize tarot’s universality and avoid simply repeating established sacred symbols. The card corresponding to the Eight of Cups therefore belongs to an intentionally new symbolic language rather than an archaeological reconstruction. [Morgan Library profile](https://www.themorgan.org/exhibitions/online/tarot/23)

### Jodorowsky–Costa / Camoin

Their Eight remains visually Marseille-like but is read developmentally: the number eight as perfected receptivity or disciplined abundance in the emotional suit. Their reading of happiness and sustained effort sharply conflicts with the Golden Dawn’s abandoned success and Crowley’s indolence.

# The game

## Original function

**[Documented fact and scholarly reconstruction].** Tarot arose in northern Italy as a trick-taking game using four ordinary suits plus a permanent trump sequence and the Fool. The 1442 Ferrara record calls the cards *carte da trionfi* and explicitly names cups, swords, coins, and batons. Nothing in that record gives the Eight of Cups a divinatory meaning.

The Eight was therefore initially identified by:

- the suit emblem;
- eight repetitions of it;
- its relative rank in a trick.

It was not one of the *trionfi*.

## Rank

The oldest tarot families commonly distinguish “long” and “round” suits:

- Swords and Batons often rank naturally: 10 high down to Ace low.
- Cups and Coins often reverse the numeral ranking: Ace high, then 2, 3 … 10 low.

Under that older round-suit system, Eight of Cups is beaten by Seven and beats Nine. This counter-ranking survives in Bolognese Ottocento. [Bolognese rules](https://www.pagat.com/tarot/ottocento.html)

It is not universal. Sicilian rules place 10, 9, 8, 7, 6, 5 in descending order under the courts, so Eight beats Seven there. [Sicilian rules](https://www.pagat.com/tarot/sicilian.html)

## Point value

In standard tarot point-counting families, Kings and certain trumps/Fool carry the major values. Ordinary numeral cards such as the Eight are “small cards” and have no distinctive intrinsic value. Depending on the counting convention, each captured small card contributes one point or is folded into grouped subtraction/counting. The Eight is not an *oudler*, honour, *tarocco*, or matador.

## Minchiate

The Eight is an ordinary round-suit pip. The expanded trump sequence has no effect on its identity. Its reverse rank is part of the older Italian structure.

## Tarocchino

The low pips 2–5 were removed, but Eight survives. In Cups:

**Ace > 6 > 7 > 8 > 9 > 10**

beneath the courts. It may help form suit sequences for declarations but possesses no unique symbolic privilege.

## French Tarot

Modern French Tarot uses French suits, making Hearts the functional descendant of Cups. The Eight of Hearts ranks normally by face number and has no special point value. A Marseille-style Eight of Cups belongs to an Italian-suited historical pack, not the standard modern French game deck.

## Austrian/German Tarock

Most reduced Tarock packs omit the middle/high red numerals, so an Eight corresponding to Cups is often absent. Where a full Italian-suited pack is used, local rules determine rank.

## Trump-order disputes: relevance and non-relevance

Michael Dummett reconstructed three broad traditional trump-order regions:

- **Order A:** chiefly Bologna/Florence;
- **Order B:** Ferrara and related northeastern traditions;
- **Order C:** Milan and the later Marseille lineage.

The anonymous *Sermo de ludo cum aliis* gives the oldest complete known trump list and follows a Ferrarese/Order B arrangement. [Vanderbilt manuscript description and transcription](https://collections.library.vanderbilt.edu/repositories/2/archival_objects/261035)

These disputes concern trumps, not the Eight of Cups. Likewise:

- Justice and Strength exchange relative positions across patterns and were numbered VIII/XI differently in later occult decks.
- Papess/Pope became Juno/Jupiter in Besançon-type packs.
- The Tower could be called *La Maison Dieu*.
- The Fool was frequently unnumbered and served as an Excuse.
- Golden Dawn/Waite put Strength at VIII and Justice at XI.
- Crowley’s Tzaddi–Heh revision concerns the Emperor and Star.

None changes the numeral **8** on the Eight of Cups or gives it a Hebrew letter.

# Chronology of attestation

### Circa 1420 — Marziano da Tortona

**[Documented text; proto-tarot, not Eight of Cups].** Marziano’s treatise for Filippo Maria Visconti describes a game of sixteen classical gods and four suits represented by kinds of birds. It is important as a precursor involving permanent trumps, but it contains no Cup suit and no Eight of Cups.

### 1440 — Florentine *trionfi* reference

**[Documented archival reference].** A 1440 record discovered after older histories had treated 1442 as earliest refers to *naibi a trionfi*. It attests the new game, not the individual pip.

### 10 February 1442 — Ferrara ledger

**[Documented primary record].** Painter Jacopo/Sagramoro was paid for colouring four packs of *chartexele da trionffy*, including:

> “*le chope e le spade e li dinari e li bastoni*”

This is the earliest source consulted that directly names the Cup suit within tarot production. [Ledger transcription](https://www.oocities.org/autorbis/ferrara1442.html)

### Circa 1440s–1450s — Cary–Yale Eight of Cups

**[Documented surviving object].** The first presently reconstructable card-specific witness: eight chalices on a gilded Milanese luxury card. [Image record](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Cary-Yale_Tarot_deck_-_Eight_of_Cups.jpg)

### Circa 1450s — Visconti-Sforza production

**[Documented objects].** Surviving Cups pips establish the continuing emblematic type, although the principal Morgan group does not preserve the Eight. [Morgan](https://www.themorgan.org/collection/tarot-cards)

### Circa 1460 — Boiardo

**[Documented literary tarot].** Matteo Maria Boiardo composed verses for a seventy-eight-card moralized pack, first printed in 1523. It demonstrates that individual suit cards could acquire literary/moral qualities, but it was a bespoke poetic scheme rather than evidence that the ordinary Eight of Cups already meant abandonment.

### Circa 1470–1500 — *Sermo de ludo cum aliis*

**[Documented primary text].** The sermon condemns cards and *trionfi* and supplies twenty-one trump names. It does not give individual pip meanings. Its silence is significant: the moral attack concerns gambling and the trump hierarchy, not a secret Book of Thoth. [Vanderbilt manuscript](https://collections.library.vanderbilt.edu/repositories/2/archival_objects/261035)

### 1491 — Sola Busca

**[Documented object].** First complete surviving tarot and first complete illustrated-minor system. Its Eight is a narrative/object composition but not the Waite–Smith departure scene.

### Circa 1500 — tarot poems

**[Documented literary practice].** Poems applied cards to persons and situations, showing that tarot imagery could be used rhetorically. This is not equivalent to a fixed divinatory lexicon for the Eight of Cups.

### Early sixteenth century — *tarocchi*

**[Documented terminology].** *Tarocchi* replaces or supplements *trionfi* as the game’s name. Folengo used tarot in literary and satirical contexts. These sources do not establish the modern Eight-of-Cups meaning.

### Seventeenth–eighteenth centuries — Marseille lineage

**[Documented cards].** Eight chalices plus floral ornament becomes the stable western-European printed type.

### Early eighteenth century — Bologna divinatory sheet

**[Documented but limited evidence].** A Bolognese manuscript, probably after 1725, assigns fortune-telling meanings to thirty-five tarocchino cards. This qualifies older claims that tarot had no divinatory use before 1781. It does not create the later Egyptian or Kabbalistic theory, and a distinct Eight-of-Cups entry was not verified in the consulted description. [Historical discussion](https://hermetism.org/encyclopedia/tarot-cartomancy/)

### 1781 — Court de Gébelin and de Mellet

**[Documented origin of printed occult-tarot theory].** Egypt, Book of Thoth, Hebrew alphabet for trumps, and systematic occult antiquity enter print. No card-specific Eight-of-Cups correspondence appears.

### 1780s–1790s — Etteilla

**[Documented first comprehensive cartomantic tarot system].** Card 42: fair/blond girl upright, satisfaction reversed.

### 1854–1856 — Éliphas Lévi, *Dogme et rituel de la haute magie*

**[Documented general occult synthesis].** Lévi calls tarot the “hieroglyphic work of Hermes” and aligns the suits with magical instruments and letters of the divine name. In the English translation’s formulation:

> “the Cup is the cteis or primitive HE”

This assigns Cups to receptive/feminine Heh symbolism, but Lévi supplies no distinct Saturn-in-Pisces Eight. [Digitized text](https://magicgatebg.com/Books/Dogma%20et%20Rituel%20de%20la%20Haute%20Magie%20Part%20I%20by%20Eliphas%20Levi.pdf)

### 1860s–1870s — Paul Christian

**[Documented occult literature; legendary pseudo-history].** Christian elaborated Egyptian initiation narratives and popularized *arcana* terminology. His temple-initiation story is literary occult construction, not a record of pharaonic tarot use. No independent Eight-of-Cups iconography was located.

### 1888 — S. L. MacGregor Mathers

**[Documented divinatory list].**

> “A fair Girl, Friendship, Attachment, Tenderness; Reversed Meaning: Gaiety, Feasting, Joy, Pleasure.”

This is plainly related to Etteilla’s card 42. [Mathers text](https://www.howardnema.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/12/the-tarot-1888-by-macgregor-mathers-1.pdf)

### 1889 — Papus and Wirth

Papus: “partial failure of love.” Wirth: only trumps; no Eight.

### Late 1880s–1890s — Golden Dawn *Book T*

**[Documented esoteric attribution].** Lord of Abandoned Success; Saturn in Pisces; Hod of Heh.

### December 1909 — Waite–Smith deck

**[Documented image].** First influential depiction of the solitary departure from eight cups. The first edition was published by William Rider & Son. [Whitney catalogue](https://library.whitney.org/bib/67344), [public-domain card](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Cups08.jpg)

### 1910/1911 — Waite’s *Pictorial Key*

**[Documented interpretive text].** Decline, abandonment, slight importance; reversed joy and feasting.

### 1912 — Golden Dawn material published through Crowley’s *Equinox*

**[Documented publication history].** *Book T*-derived material becomes publicly accessible.

### 1938–1943 / 1944 / 1969 — Thoth

Harris paints the deck; Crowley publishes *The Book of Thoth*; the full commercial deck follows posthumously.

### 1970s–1980s — psychological tarot

Rachel Pollack, Mary K. Greer, and others reposition the Eight as a reflective choice, inner journey, or encounter with emotional insufficiency rather than a fixed prediction.

### 1997 — Camoin–Jodorowsky

Marseille reconstruction combined with a developmental numerology. Eight of Cups becomes happiness or emotional perfection pursued through sustained effort.

### Twenty-first century

The RWS “walking away” interpretation predominates in English-language practice. “Leaving what no longer serves,” “searching for purpose,” trauma recovery, boundaries, and fear of change are modern psychological expansions.

## Clarifications about Heh

Three separate propositions are often conflated:

1. **[Esoteric attribution]** Cups correspond to Water.
2. **[Esoteric attribution]** Cups correspond to the first or maternal **Heh** in YHVH in Golden Dawn elemental/world formulas.
3. **[False if stated literally]** “The Eight of Cups has the Hebrew letter Heh.”

The third is misleading. Individual Hebrew letters and Tree paths belong chiefly to the twenty-two trumps. The Eight is **Hod of Heh**: the eighth Sephirah within the Cup/Water series.

## Tree of Life

Hod means Splendour or Glory and is associated in Hermetic Qabalah with Mercury, intellect, classification, language, and form. In the Water suit, this organizing Mercurial principle meets affective or fluid substance. Golden Dawn and Crowley read the result pessimistically because Saturn constricts Pisces: the form exists, but flow and vitality fail.

This explanation is **[esoteric attribution]**, not the historical meaning of eight cup symbols in fifteenth-century cards.

## Decan

The first ten degrees of Pisces are assigned to Saturn through the Golden Dawn’s use of the Chaldean planetary sequence. This is not natal rulership in the ordinary modern-astrology sense; it is a decanic planetary attribution. The span is 0°00′–9°59′ Pisces, conventionally associated approximately with 19–28 February, varying slightly by year.

## Tzaddi/Heh swap

Crowley’s statement “Tzaddi is not the Star” led him to exchange the Emperor and Star’s zodiacal/Hebrew-letter relations while retaining the visual trump order. This affects Major Arcana IV and XVII, not the Eight of Cups and not the elemental Heh in “Hod of Heh.”

## Development of the modern meaning

The clearest line of transmission is:

**Etteilla fair girl/satisfaction → Mathers friendship/feasting → Golden Dawn abandoned success and wandering → Smith’s departing traveller → Waite’s decline → modern “walk away from what no longer fulfils.”**

It is not a single unchanged ancient meaning. Waite openly preserved both branches: the new abandonment scene and older joy/feasting meanings.

# Esoteric and symbolic readings

## Elemental Water

**First clearly systematic occult context:** nineteenth-century French occultism, especially Lévi; made standard in English-language tarot by the Golden Dawn.

Cups are understood as receptivity, feeling, imagination, relationship, fertility, and the unconscious. This is **[esoteric attribution]**. Historically, cups were simply Italian playing-card suit signs, related to ordinary Latin-suited cards.

## Grail symbolism

**[Tradition / modern esoteric expansion].**

The Cup is often equated with:

- the Holy Grail;
- the chalice of the Eucharist;
- the receptive vessel;
- the womb;
- the heart;
- the cauldron of inspiration.

The Ace of Cups particularly encouraged Grail and sacramental readings. Extending them to the Eight produces such interpretations as abandoning lesser vessels in search of the true Grail.

No fifteenth-century source consulted calls the Eight of Cups a Grail card. The chalice shape can support Christian association, but direct historical intention is unproved.

## Christian reading

A modern Christian-Hermetic interpretation sees the traveller as leaving worldly satisfactions for pilgrimage, asceticism, or the “narrow way.” The staff and cloak support a pilgrim analogy, while the eight intact cups represent goods renounced rather than destroyed.

This is visually plausible but not stated by Waite. Crowley’s hostile phrase about the card being the “German Measles of Christian Mysticism” is an anti-sentimental Thelemic judgment, not evidence that Smith intended a Christian pilgrim.

## Hermetic reading

In Golden Dawn terms, the card is Water in Briah undergoing Hod’s formalizing power. Saturn’s restriction prevents Piscean water from circulating. Form has become sterile. The necessary response is abandonment, not because all success was unreal, but because it cannot develop further.

Crowley radicalizes this into polluted sacramental energy: the cups remain ritual vessels, but the sacrament no longer flows.

## Kabbalistic reading

Eight corresponds to Hod. Hod is below the Abyss and on the Pillar of Severity, paired dynamically with Netzach. In Cups, intellect and form attempt to organize feeling. At best this can yield discrimination and conscious withdrawal; at worst analysis, inertia, or formalism drains emotional life.

The association begins with the Golden Dawn’s systematic minor-arcana scheme, not with Jewish Kabbalah or the Renaissance cardmakers.

## Astrological reading

Saturn in Pisces combines:

- Saturn: limitation, dryness, delay, gravity, boundary;
- Pisces: water, diffusion, imagination, surrender, porousness.

Golden Dawn and Crowley emphasize incompatibility: restriction deadens water. Modern astrologers sometimes offer more constructive readings—disciplined compassion, boundaries in emotional life, relinquishment, spiritual retreat—but those are later developments.

## Alchemical reading

The Eight is sometimes interpreted as exhausted dissolution: the water phase has ceased to transform matter and has become stagnant. Crowley’s mud, poison, miasma, and barren pools make such a reading possible.

For the Sola Busca deck as a whole, Brera-associated scholarship has explored alchemical and Hermetic contexts. But projecting an unbroken alchemical code from Sola Busca into Smith’s Eight is **[disputed]**. No chain of card-specific textual transmission was found.

## Numerology of eight

Different traditions conflict:

- Golden Dawn: Hod, Mercury, conceptual or formal splendor.
- Papus: opposition or partial realization within an equilibrated structure.
- Jodorowsky–Costa: developed perfection, abundance, or receptive fulfilment.
- Modern popular numerology: mastery, strength, adjustment, power, or completion approaching nine.

None is the documented rule by which fifteenth-century players interpreted the pip.

## Jungian reading

**[Modern psychological interpretation].**

The Eight can represent withdrawal of projection: emotional objects once invested with meaning remain standing, but psychic energy leaves them. The traveller moves toward the unknown, resembling a phase of individuation in which social or affective success no longer answers the Self’s demand.

Other Jungian readings see:

- entry into the unconscious night;
- separation from the persona;
- encounter with shadow;
- relinquishing an outgrown complex;
- the danger of avoidance masquerading as individuation.

There is no canonical “Jung interpretation of the Eight of Cups.” Jung did not publish a card-by-card tarot system. Claims such as “Jung said the Eight means individuation” are **[modern invention]** unless explicitly framed as later Jungian application.

# People and works

## Directly attached to the card

### Bonifacio Bembo and workshop

Attributed artist/workshop for the Cary–Yale card. The attribution is stylistic and historical, not a surviving signature.

### Jacopo Sagramoro

Ferrara painter paid in 1442 to colour Cups, Swords, Coins, Batons, and figures in four *trionfi* packs. He is attached to the earliest documentary suit context, not to the surviving Cary–Yale card.

### Etteilla / Jean-Baptiste Alliette

Created the first influential comprehensive occult-cartomantic tarot deck and fixed the Eight as card 42, fair girl/satisfaction.

### S. L. MacGregor Mathers

Carried Etteilla-derived meanings into English in 1888 and later helped construct the Golden Dawn system that radically changed the card.

### Pamela Colman Smith

Created the enduring narrative image in 1909. Her composition, more than any earlier pip, made the card signify voluntary departure.

### Arthur Edward Waite

Commissioned and directed the deck, then provided the canonical verbal interpretation. His acknowledgement of contradictory older meanings is one of the most important primary-source facts about the card.

### Lady Frieda Harris

Painted the Thoth Eight as a geometrically controlled system of exhausted cups, drooping plants, stagnant water, and oppressive sky.

### Aleister Crowley

Renamed it *Indolence* and supplied the most elaborate hostile reading of the card’s environment.

### Paul Foster Case and Jessie Burns Parke

Case transmitted a modified Golden Dawn/Qabalistic framework through BOTA. Parke is central to BOTA’s tarot art, but an original canonical Parke Eight of Cups was not verified.

### Rachel Pollack

One of the most influential late-twentieth-century interpreters of tarot as a psychological and spiritual process. Her Shining Tribe system renamed Cups as Rivers. [Morgan profile](https://www.themorgan.org/exhibitions/online/tarot/23)

### Mary K. Greer

Made reversal interpretation multivalent rather than simply “opposite.” Applied to the Eight, reversal can mean staying, returning, blockage, internal withdrawal, excessive flight, or reconsideration.

### Alejandro Jodorowsky, Marianne Costa, Philippe Camoin

Re-centred Marseille visual structure and gave the Eight an expansive developmental reading rather than Waite’s abandonment or Crowley’s stagnation.

### Italo Calvino, *The Castle of Crossed Destinies*

**[Documented tarot work; no verified unique Eight-of-Cups episode].** Calvino constructs interlaced narratives from laid-out tarot cards, especially Visconti-Sforza and Marseille imagery. The work is essential to literary tarot reception, but no reliable source consulted establishes the Eight of Cups as a uniquely named controlling card comparable to a character quotation. It should not be claimed as “a novel about the Eight of Cups.”

### T. S. Eliot, *The Waste Land*

**[Documented tarot allusion; unrelated card].** Madame Sosostris names the drowned Phoenician Sailor, Belladonna, the man with three staves, the Wheel, the one-eyed merchant, and a blank card. Eliot does not name the Eight of Cups. Linking the poem’s waste landscape to Crowley’s stagnant Eight is comparative criticism, not direct influence.

### W. B. Yeats

Yeats participated in the Golden Dawn and used related magical and cyclical symbolism. No securely documented poem or artwork specifically quoting the Eight of Cups was located.

### Jodorowsky’s films

Jodorowsky’s cinema uses tarot, alchemy, pilgrimage, and initiatory imagery, and he later co-authored a major tarot book. No shot or building in the films was reliably documented as a direct quotation of the Eight of Cups. Resemblance to a lone seeker leaving worldly goods is interpretive.

### Ingmar Bergman

Bergman’s *The Seventh Seal* is frequently connected in popular culture with tarot’s Death card because of its chess-playing Death figure. No documented Eight-of-Cups quotation was found.

### Visual art

The Morgan’s 2023–2024 tarot exhibition traces tarot’s influence through André Breton, Leonora Carrington, Remedios Varo, Jess, Niki de Saint Phalle, Betye Saar, Kerstin Brätsch, and Chris Ofili. The exhibition establishes the broader artistic reception of tarot, but its accessible catalogue does not identify a major painting as a direct Eight-of-Cups quotation. [Morgan exhibition](https://www.themorgan.org/exhibitions/tarot)

### Buildings

No well-documented historic building quoting the Eight of Cups was located. Claims that particular churches, Gothic portals, Egyptian temples, or Cathar sites “encode” this card lack a demonstrated chain of influence and should be marked **[legend or speculative analogy]**.

# Controversies and disputes

## Egyptian origin

### The claim

Court de Gébelin declared in 1781 that tarot preserved an Egyptian sacred book, later called the Book of Thoth. Etteilla expanded the claim into a corrected Egyptian cosmology and said the original had been inscribed on gold leaves.

### Evidence assessment

**[Legend as history; documented as an eighteenth-century occult belief].**

Evidence against ancient Egyptian origin includes:

- earliest surviving tarot cards are north Italian and fifteenth-century;
- their suit system belongs to European/Islamicate playing-card history;
- the 1440s records use Italian game terminology;
- no ancient or medieval Egyptian tarot object or text survives;
- Gébelin wrote before Champollion’s decipherment;
- his proposed Egyptian etymology for *tarot* is linguistically baseless.

Dummett states that Gébelin and de Mellet offered no genuine Egyptian forms and that their speculations rested on no historical connection. [Dummett](https://www.nybooks.com/articles/1981/05/14/origins-of-tarot/)

The myth remains historically important because it generated real later symbolism, including Etteilla, Lévi, Papus, the Golden Dawn, and modern occult tarot.

## Romani or “Gypsy” transmission

### The claim

Tarot was allegedly carried from Egypt through Europe by Romani people.

### Evidence assessment

**[Legend].**

The association arose from the old European misidentification of Romani people as Egyptians and was strongly spread in nineteenth-century occult literature, including Jean-Alexandre Vaillant and Papus’s title *Tarot des Bohémiens*. No fifteenth-century Italian evidence ties the invention or early diffusion of tarot to Romani communities.

The term “Gypsy” is historically embedded in the source tradition but is now recognized as an exonym often considered offensive.

## Cathar origin

### The claim

Tarot supposedly encodes Cathar theology or descends from Cathar confraternities, processions, or secret survival networks.

### Evidence assessment

**[Disputed modern speculation].**

Proponents compare dualistic imagery, Provençal culture, religious processions, and Petrarchan triumphs. The argument is associative. No Cathar manuscript names tarot, no chain connects thirteenth-century Cathar practice to fifteenth-century Milanese/Ferrarese cards, and no Eight-of-Cups evidence supports it.

## Templar origin

### The claim

The Knights Templar preserved or transmitted tarot symbolism.

### Evidence assessment

**[Legend].**

No Templar inventory, rule, trial document, or artwork securely describes a tarot pack. The order was dissolved more than a century before tarot’s first records. Later occult orders retrospectively combined Templar, Masonic, Hermetic, and tarot symbolism.

## Alchemical Sola Busca as source of the Eight

### The claim

Sola Busca contains an alchemical code that Smith copied into the Waite–Smith Eight.

### Evidence assessment

- Sola Busca’s alchemical/Hermetic context: **[scholarly interpretation, plausible but debated in detail]**.
- Smith’s knowledge of Sola Busca photographs: **[strong scholarly reconstruction]**.
- Specific copying of its Eight into her Eight: **[unsupported]**.
- Her narrative more closely reflects *Book T*: **[strong iconographic/textual reconstruction]**.

## “Ancient meaning: walking away”

**[Modern invention].**

The earliest Eight is eight cups only. Etteilla says fair girl/satisfaction. Mathers says friendship/tenderness and reversed feasting. The walking-away meaning crystallized with Golden Dawn “abandoned success” and Smith’s 1909 picture.

## “All eight cups are empty”

**[Disputed visual inference].**

Smith’s card shows upright cups but does not reveal their contents. Waite calls them cups of former felicity, not explicitly empty vessels. Crowley’s Thoth card does depict an inadequate flow and empty upper cups. Modern readers often import the Thoth emptiness into Smith’s image.

## “A ninth cup is missing”

**[Modern interpretive invention].**

The gap is visible, but neither Smith nor Waite is known to have called it the missing Nine of Cups. It became a compelling later narrative because the Nine signifies satisfaction in Waite’s sequence.

## “Smith copied the scene from Sola Busca”

**[Misattribution].**

Smith borrowed from Sola Busca elsewhere, but the specific departing figure, mountainous terrain, moon, and stack of cups are not reproduced from Sola Busca’s Eight.

## “Wirth’s 1889 Eight of Cups”

**[Bibliographic error].**

Wirth made twenty-two trumps only. Any Eight in a seventy-eight-card Wirth-branded pack is a later completion.

## “The Eight has the Hebrew letter Heh”

**[Category error].**

Cups as a suit correspond to Heh in Golden Dawn formulas. The individual Eight corresponds to Hod within that suit. It is not assigned a Hebrew letter/path like a trump.

## Thoth date

**[Common simplification].**

“Thoth Tarot, 1944” refers properly to *The Book of Thoth* and its reproductions; “Thoth deck, 1969” refers to the first commercial complete deck. The paintings themselves date from 1938–1943.

## Historical corrections and their historians

- Michael Dummett reconstructed tarot primarily through game rules, card morphology, and archival references.
- Ronald Decker and Thierry Depaulis traced the inventions of occult tarot from Gébelin through Etteilla, Lévi, Papus, the Golden Dawn, and later systems.
- Stuart Kaplan assembled extensive deck documentation and reproductions, though some early datings in older volumes have subsequently been refined.
- Robert M. Place interprets iconography through Renaissance, Neoplatonic, Christian, and alchemical contexts while accepting the documented Italian playing-card origin.
- Helen Farley treats tarot as a cultural object whose meanings changed from courtly game to occult and New Age tool.
- Andrea Vitali and the Le Tarot association collect iconographic and documentary evidence, with some interpretive conclusions more expansive than Dummett’s.
- Tarot History Forum and Trionfi researchers have refined individual documents and chronologies, but forum hypotheses require case-by-case evaluation.

# Open questions

1. **Exact dating of the Cary–Yale Eight.**  
   The card is securely fifteenth-century and Milanese, but proposed dates range within the 1440s–1450s.

2. **Workshop authorship.**  
   Bonifacio Bembo is the standard attribution, but division of labour among Bembo-family artists and workshop assistants remains unsettled.

3. **Complete iconographic description of the Sola Busca Eight.**  
   The card survives and is digitized, but a detailed museum-authored card-by-card interpretation was not found in accessible Brera material. Claims beyond the observable image require the specialist print catalogue.

4. **Smith’s precise design instructions.**  
   Waite likely supplied concepts and Smith supplied much visual invention, but no surviving card-by-card instruction sheet establishes who conceived the Eight’s traveller, moon, terrain, or cup arrangement.

5. **Meaning of the composite moon.**  
   Eclipse, waxing/waning symbolism, and conjunction of phases remain plausible readings without decisive documentary proof.

6. **Etteilla editions and sequence states.**  
   Grand Etteilla decks exist in several patterns and later recensions. Card 42 and the fair-girl/satisfaction pair are well attested, but ornament and wording vary between impressions.

7. **Original Golden Dawn painted exemplar.**  
   *Book T* preserves the prescription. Wang and Cicero are reconstructions; the exact appearance of every early temple copy cannot be assumed from them.

8. **Thoth early-edition colour states.**  
   Differences among first printings need direct comparison with Harris’s originals to separate reproduction error from intentional colour.

9. **Pollack and Greer verbatim wording.**  
   Their books are under copyright and no stable authorized full-text card entries were found. Their ideas have therefore been summarized rather than attributed through unreliable quotation sites.

10. **Direct appearances in major literature or architecture.**  
    Searches found broad tarot engagement by Calvino, Yeats, Eliot, Jodorowsky, and modern artists, but no adequately documented Eight-of-Cups quotation in Eliot, Bergman, a historic building, or a named canonical painting.

# Conclusions

The Eight of Cups has three distinct historical lives.

First, it was a **game card**: eight chalices, probably surviving first in the Cary–Yale/Visconti di Modrone pack, with no recorded emotional or occult meaning. In Marseille and Italian regional traditions it remained a patterned pip whose main objective properties were suit, number, rank, and ordinary counting value.

Second, it became a **cartomantic entry**. Etteilla made it a fair girl upright and satisfaction reversed; Mathers transmitted friendship, tenderness, gaiety, and feasting. Papus independently generated “partial failure of love.”

Third, the Golden Dawn turned it into **Abandoned Success**, Saturn in Pisces and Hod of Heh. Pamela Colman Smith made that abstraction visible in 1909 by showing a traveller leaving eight intact cups. Waite described decline while preserving the old contrary meanings. Crowley and Harris removed the purposeful traveller and made the vessels and landscape themselves exhausted: **Indolence**.

The modern phrase “walk away from what no longer serves you” is therefore not ancient wisdom attached to an unchanged symbol. It is a twentieth- and twenty-first-century psychological development rooted primarily in Smith’s image, Waite’s gloss, and the Golden Dawn’s title. That does not make it culturally invalid; it makes it historically datable.
