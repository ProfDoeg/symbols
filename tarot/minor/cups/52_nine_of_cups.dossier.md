# Nine of Cups: Research Dossier

## Method and evidentiary labels

This dossier distinguishes:

- **Documented fact** — supported by a surviving card, archival record, rulebook, or identifiable printed text.
- **Scholarly reconstruction** — an inference advanced by historians from surviving material.
- **Tradition** — a reading transmitted through cartomantic practice whose first author may be uncertain.
- **Esoteric attribution** — an explicitly occult correspondence, not a historical claim about the card’s original design.
- **Disputed claim** — proposed but contested, or inadequately demonstrated.
- **Legend** — a story contradicted by the documentary chronology or unsupported by evidence.
- **Modern invention** — a relatively recent interpretation, nickname, or redesign presented as such.

A central finding should be stated immediately:

> **The earliest surviving Nine of Cups is the Visconti–Sforza card now in the Morgan Library, probably Milan, circa 1450–1480—not the Sola Busca card.**  
> The Sola Busca is important because it is the earliest surviving complete tarot with illustrated narrative pips. However, its Nine of Cups does **not** furnish the seated banqueter composition used by Pamela Colman Smith. Claims that “the Sola Busca Nine of Cups” directly sourced Smith’s Nine are unsupported; demonstrable Sola Busca/Rider–Waite–Smith parallels concern other cards.

### Is it a “Minor Arcana”?

**Documented terminology, but late:** Renaissance cardmakers and players did not call the suit cards “Minor Arcana.” *Arcana* belongs to the occult reinterpretation of tarot. Jean-Baptiste Pitois, writing as Paul Christian, helped establish the terminology *arcanes majeurs* and *arcanes mineurs* in the nineteenth-century French occult tradition. Historically, this was simply the nine of a suit in a pack used for games.

### Names of tarot itself

- **Documented fact:** Fifteenth-century Italian sources initially call the special pack *carte da trionfi* or simply *trionfi*—“triumphs.”
- **Documented fact:** *Tarocchi* appears in Italy in the early sixteenth century, replacing *trionfi* as the usual name for the game and pack.
- **Documented fact:** French *tarot*, German *Tarock*, and related forms descend from the Italian name.
- **Disputed:** No accepted etymology explains *tarocco* conclusively.
- **Legend:** Court de Gébelin’s derivation from alleged Egyptian words *tar* and *ro*—“royal road”—has no linguistic foundation.

### Trump numbering and regional renamings

The requested examples—Papess/Juno, Pope/Jupiter, Maison Dieu, the Fool without a number, and the Justice/Fortitude dispute—belong to the trump series. They do not affect the identity or gaming rank of the Nine of Cups.

For orientation:

- The **Fool** was traditionally unnumbered and operated as the Excuse or a special card. Assigning it 0 became conventional in occult and later printed packs.
- In the **Besançon pattern**, the Papess and Pope were replaced by **Juno** and **Jupiter**, principally to avoid objectionable papal figures in regions where the original images were unacceptable.
- **La Maison Dieu** is the conventional Marseille name for trump XVI, commonly rendered “Tower” in English.
- Justice and Fortitude/Strength occupy different positions in different historical trump orders. Waite placed Strength VIII and Justice XI; Marseille normally has Justice VIII and Force XI.
- These variations must not be projected onto pip history: the Nine of Cups remained a suit card regardless of trump order.

# Iconography deck by deck

## Fifteenth-century Milan

### Visconti di Modrone / Cary–Yale deck, before 1447 or around 1441–1447

**Documented fact:** This Milanese luxury pack, probably made for Filippo Maria Visconti, survives in the Beinecke Library at Yale. Its unusual structure includes six court cards per suit and extra female virtues.

**Finding for this card:** A secure surviving Nine of Cups from this pack was not located in the consulted catalogue material. One therefore cannot responsibly supply a card-specific visual description.

**General pip convention:** Surviving Visconti-family number cards place repeated suit signs against richly decorated, usually gold grounds. Their function remains numerically legible rather than scenic.

### Brambilla deck, Milan, before 1447

**Documented fact:** The surviving cards are held by the Pinacoteca di Brera. The pack is associated with Filippo Maria Visconti and is commonly dated before his death in 1447.

**Finding:** No surviving Brambilla Nine of Cups could be confirmed from the consulted museum and scholarly catalogues. Assertions about its exact design would therefore be reconstruction.

### Visconti–Sforza / Pierpont Morgan–Bergamo deck, circa 1450–1480

**Documented fact:** Thirty-five cards are in the Morgan Library, twenty-six in the Accademia Carrara, and thirteen in the Colleoni collection. The Morgan catalogues **The Nine of Cups as MS M.630.27**. The pack was probably made for the Visconti–Sforza court after Francesco Sforza became duke in 1450. The Morgan attributes the work probably to Bonifacio Bembo or his milieu.

**What is depicted:** Nine ornate gold cups are arranged decoratively on a sumptuous patterned field. Vine, ribbon, flower, crown, and heraldic ornament integrate the separate cups into a courtly surface design. There is no person, banquet, predictive inscription, water symbolism, or narrative action.

**Meaning in its original context:** The ornament announces aristocratic luxury and serves the suit and number. No fifteenth-century source says it meant wishes, material happiness, Jupiter in Pisces, Yesod, or emotional fulfillment.

**Historical significance:** This is the earliest surviving Nine of Cups presently reconstructable from a named tarot deck. The Morgan explicitly stresses that these cards were created for an aristocratic game and acquired occult associations only centuries later.

## Fifteenth-century Ferrara and nearby production

### Este and so-called Charles VI cards

The surviving Este/Ferrarese fragments concentrate heavily on trumps and courts.

**Finding:** No card securely catalogued as their Nine of Cups was found. Their importance lies in establishing a Ferrarese tarot industry, not a card-specific iconographic genealogy.

### Rosenwald and Budapest sheets, late fifteenth or early sixteenth century

These printed sheets are crucial witnesses to inexpensive early tarot production.

**Finding:** The extant sheets do not provide a securely identifiable, complete Nine of Cups that permits a continuous iconographic line to Marseille or Waite–Smith. They demonstrate that tarot had moved beyond unique illuminated court objects into repeatable print production.

### Mantegna “Tarocchi,” circa 1460s

**Documented fact:** The so-called Mantegna Tarocchi are fifty didactic engravings arranged into classes—conditions of humanity, Apollo and the Muses, liberal arts, cosmic principles, and virtues.

**Not relevant as a deck ancestor:** They are not a tarot pack and possess no suits or Nine of Cups. Their conventional name is a later misnomer. They may illuminate Renaissance classificatory imagery but not this card’s descent.

## Sola Busca, northern Italy, circa 1490–1491

**Documented fact:** The Sola Busca is the earliest completely surviving seventy-eight-card tarot pack and the earliest surviving tarot in which every numbered suit card has an individual scenic illustration. The engravings are generally dated circa 1490; inscriptions on the painted pack support 1491. The complete pack is associated with the Sola-Busca family and is now in Brera. Uncoloured impressions entered other collections, including the British Museum.

**What the Nine of Cups depicts:** A human figure occupies the card among nine large cup forms. It belongs to the Sola Busca’s distinctive programme in which implements, furnaces, tools, vessels, labour, and human bodies replace the purely ornamental pip layouts of luxury Milanese cards.

**Important negative finding:** The Sola Busca Nine does **not** show Smith’s stout seated host before a curved counter of nine cups. Online statements that “the Sola Busca pip was the source of Pamela Colman Smith’s scene” overgeneralize a genuine deck-level relationship.

The evidence for Smith knowing the Sola Busca is circumstantial but strong:

1. The British Museum acquired photographs of the complete Sola Busca in 1908.
2. Smith was working in London and moved in circles with access to museum collections.
3. Several Waite–Smith cards possess striking compositional parallels to Sola Busca cards, particularly Three of Swords and Ten of Wands/related burden scenes.

But:

- **Scholarly reconstruction:** Smith probably consulted the Sola Busca or its photographs.
- **Disputed:** Which specific cards she borrowed.
- **Unsupported for this card:** A direct Nine-of-Cups-to-Nine-of-Cups derivation.
- **More plausible source:** Golden Dawn meaning and Mathers’s vocabulary of victory/success, converted by Smith into a material scene of achieved satisfaction.

The Brera’s modern exhibition literature proposes extensive alchemical and Hermetic readings of the Sola Busca.

- **Documented fact:** Alchemical implements and metallurgical operations appear throughout the deck.
- **Scholarly interpretation:** The programme may encode an alchemical process.
- **Not documented:** That its original maker assigned “Material Happiness” or Jupiter in Pisces to its Nine of Cups.

## Tarot de Marseille lineage

### General Marseille pip structure

The Marseille Nine of Cups normally uses nine goblet-shaped cup signs in a balanced vertical arrangement, interwoven with foliage and floral ornament. The vegetation occupies the spaces between cups and provides bilateral or near-bilateral rhythm.

**Documented visual fact:** There is ordinarily no human narrative.  
**Traditional modern reading:** Marseille readers infer growth, receptivity, accumulation, enclosure, fullness, or emotional organization from the number and layout.  
**Modern invention if asserted historically:** The flowers were not demonstrably designed to encode Yesod, Jupiter, the Grail, or wish fulfillment.

### Jean Noblet, Paris, second half of the seventeenth century

The BnF dates the surviving Noblet pack to the second half of the seventeenth century and notes that Jean Noblet was active between 1659 and 1681. “1650” is a useful conventional date, not a date printed on the cards.

**Documented fact:** Seventy-three of seventy-eight cards survive; the missing cards are Six through Ten of Swords, so the Nine of Cups survives.

**Iconography:** Nine cup signs with stylized floral connectors, printed from woodblocks and coloured through stencils. The design remains a playable pip, not a scene.

### Jean Dodal, Lyon, circa 1701–1715

**Documented fact:** Dodal’s pack belongs to the early Marseille Type I family.

**Iconography:** Nine repeated cups joined and separated by vegetal decoration. Changes from Noblet concern block cutting, cup contours, leaf shapes, and colour distribution, not a change into narrative symbolism.

### Nicolas Conver, Marseille, 1760/1761

**Documented fact:** Conver’s pack became a major ancestor of twentieth-century “Ancien Tarot de Marseille” editions.

**Iconography:** The cups are highly standardized, with strong vertical symmetry and foliate ornament. Modern reproductions sometimes alter the colour sequence or clean up broken lines.

### Grimaud / Paul Marteau, twentieth century

Grimaud’s *Ancien Tarot de Marseille*, especially the Paul Marteau edition of 1930, regularized Conver-derived forms and colours.

**Meaning of the change:** The pack was reframed increasingly as an occult or divinatory object, but the Nine itself retained the non-scenic Marseille pip.

### Camoin–Jodorowsky, 1997

**Documented fact:** Philippe Camoin and Alejandro Jodorowsky published a reconstructed/restored Marseille tarot based on comparison among older packs.

**Disputed claim:** Their rhetoric sometimes implies recovery of an original, intentional symbolic code. Historians generally treat the result as a modern synthetic restoration rather than a facsimile of one archetypal deck.

**Nine of Cups:** It retains the ornamental pip structure. Jodorowsky and Marianne Costa read colours, openings, directions, floral axes, and numerical stages psychologically and spiritually. Those readings are modern interpretive practice, not recovered instructions from seventeenth-century cardmakers.

## Besançon pattern

The Besançon family altered objectionable papal trumps, commonly substituting Juno and Jupiter.

**Nine of Cups:** It usually preserves the Marseille-derived suit pattern. There is no corresponding renaming: the card remains *Neuf de Coupes*. Jupiter as a Besançon trump should not be confused with the Golden Dawn’s astrological ruler Jupiter for this pip.

## Minchiate

Minchiate expanded tarot to ninety-seven cards by adding zodiac signs, four elements, virtues, and other trumps.

**Iconography:** The Nine of Cups remained a conventional Italian suit card composed of nine cups. It did not acquire a narrative scene merely because zodiacal subjects appeared among the additional trumps.

**Historical caution:** The presence of explicit Pisces and Jupiter imagery elsewhere in an expanded pack does not prove a pre-Golden-Dawn Jupiter-in-Pisces attribution to the Nine.

## Bolognese tarot

The Bolognese pack was shortened over time, with lower numeral cards removed.

**Nine of Cups:** Where present in older/full forms, it belongs to the round suit of cups; in the modern sixty-two-card pack many low cards are suppressed, but the Nine survives because only lower ranks were removed.

Bolognese cartomantic lists from the eighteenth century are among the earliest evidence that individual tarot cards acquired fortune-telling meanings, but the sources do not establish the Golden Dawn title or modern wish-card formula.

## Sicilian tarocchi

The traditional Sicilian pack contains sixty-three cards, now often sixty-four with a tax-stamp Ace of Coins.

**Documented fact:** In cups, the sequence includes 10, 9, 8, 7, 6, and 5 beneath four courts. The Nine of Cups is therefore present.

**Iconography:** Conventional suit emblems rather than a Waite-style scene.

## Etteilla’s Grand Etteilla, late 1780s

Jean-Baptiste Alliette, “Etteilla,” was the first professional occultist known to publish a comprehensive tarot divination method and the first to design a pack specifically around an occult theory.

A surviving printed Grand Etteilla version identifies:

- number **41**;
- *Le Neuf de Coupe* and its French-suited equivalent *Le Neuf de Cœur*;
- upright **VICTOIRE**;
- reversed **SINCÉRITÉ**.

**Iconography:** Nine cups in a grid occupy the upper part; a circular ornamental emblem appears below. The words and orientation make the divinatory binary part of the physical card.

**Change and meaning:** This is a decisive transition. The card no longer merely represents rank and suit: the printer supplies its interpretation. Etteilla did not yet give it the Golden Dawn’s Jupiter–Pisces scheme.

## Oswald Wirth, 1889

Wirth’s original twenty-two-card deck illustrated only the trumps. Consequently:

- there is no Wirth-designed Nine of Cups in the 1889 series;
- assigning Wirth a card-specific Nine of Cups image is anachronistic;
- later seventy-eight-card products marketed under his name are extensions by later publishers or artists.

## Golden Dawn manuscript design, late 1880s onward

The Golden Dawn’s tarot papers, conventionally called Book T, describe the card rather than preserving one universally authoritative painted pack.

The description reads substantially:

> “A White Radiant Angelic Hand, issuing from a cloud holding lotus or water-lilies, one flower of which overhangs each cup; from it a white water pours. Cups are arranged in three rows of 3.”

The manuscript adds Jupiter and Pisces signs and calls the card **The Lord of Material Happiness**.

**Meaning of the design:** Repeated pips are retained, but they become a ritual diagram. The cloud-hand represents an emanating power; lotuses distribute water; overflowing cups visibly encode fulfilled pleasure.

### Robert Wang, *The Golden Dawn Tarot*, 1977

**Modern reconstruction:** Wang’s deck, made with Israel Regardie’s cooperation, renders the manuscript descriptions in a complete painted pack. It is not an original nineteenth-century Golden Dawn deck.

Its Nine follows the hand, lotus, water, and three-by-three arrangement.

### Chic and Sandra Tabatha Cicero, *Golden Dawn Magical Tarot*, 2000

**Modern reconstruction:** The Ciceros likewise visualize the Order papers while applying Golden Dawn colour scales and modern editorial decisions. Their Nine emphasizes the overflowing cups, decan symbols, Yesod, and Briah.

Neither Wang nor Cicero can be treated as visual evidence for exactly how every original Golden Dawn adept painted the card; members were expected to construct or colour personal magical packs.

## Waite–Smith, 1909

**Documented fact:** The deck was designed under Arthur Edward Waite’s direction and drawn by Pamela Colman Smith. It was first issued by William Rider in December 1909.

**What Smith drew:** A stout or well-fed person sits frontally on a wooden bench. The figure wears a red cap, striped garment, white tunic, red stockings, and folded arms. Behind is a high, curved counter or display covered by blue cloth. Nine golden cups stand in a row or shallow arch. The setting is largely bare and yellow.

Waite’s description:

> “A goodly personage has feasted to his heart’s content, and abundant refreshment of wine is on the arched counter behind him, seeming to indicate that the future is also assured.”

He immediately cautions:

> “The picture offers the material side only, but there are other aspects.”

**Sources and transformations:**

- Etteilla and Mathers supplied an established vocabulary of victory and success.
- The Golden Dawn supplied “Material Happiness,” complete pleasure, and wishes fulfilled.
- Smith translated an abstract magical diagram into ordinary human comedy: the satisfied diner or host.
- The three-by-three Golden Dawn arrangement became nine cups displayed along an arched surface.
- The cloud-hand, lotuses, water, astrological glyphs, and explicitly magical apparatus disappeared.

**Direct Sola Busca source?** No card-specific visual derivation is demonstrable. Smith may have known the pack, but this scene corresponds more closely to the Golden Dawn title and meaning than to the Sola Busca Nine.

**Authorship caution:** Waite supplied programme and meanings, but Smith made consequential visual decisions. Waite’s book does not explain the folded arms, facial expression, clothes, or empty setting.

## Paul Foster Case and BOTA

Paul Foster Case’s BOTA deck was drawn by Jessie Burns Parke and intended to be coloured by students.

**Documented limitation:** Case’s widely available *The Tarot: A Key to the Wisdom of the Ages* concentrates on the twenty-two major keys. It is not a full illustrated commentary on all fifty-six pips. BOTA’s public deck is consequently best known for its majors.

BOTA teaching accepts Golden Dawn-derived suit, element, number, and astrological structures. Card-specific lesson material remained partly within correspondence-course publication. Claims that Case publicly coined a novel Nine of Cups image or reversed definition were not verified in the consulted open editions.

## Crowley–Harris Thoth Tarot, painted 1938–1943; published later

Aleister Crowley designed and Lady Frieda Harris painted the deck between 1938 and 1943. The full deck’s first widely recognized colour publication was in 1969; small earlier exhibitions and monochrome or partial reproductions complicate any single “publication” date.

The printed title is **Happiness**.

**What Harris painted:** Nine golden cups form a precise symmetrical matrix. Lotus-like flowers pour water into them; water overflows and connects the arrangement. The background uses luminous, watery, violet, rose, and golden tones. Jupiter and Pisces organize the design.

Crowley writes:

> “The Number Nine, Yesod, in the suit of Water, restores the stability lost by the excursions of Netzach and Hod from the Middle Pillar.”

And:

> “The Ruler is Jupiter in Pisces. This influence is more than sympathetic; it is a definite benediction.”

He describes nine cups “perfectly arranged in a square,” all “filled and overflowing with Water,” and calls the result:

> “the most complete and most beneficent aspect of the force of Water.”

**Change from Book T:** Crowley preserves nearly the entire Golden Dawn correspondence but compresses the title from “Lord of Material Happiness” to “Happiness.” Harris turns the manuscript’s botanical diagram into a radiant geometric field.

**Important textual discrepancy:** Some short Thoth divination summaries paraphrase Jupiter in Pisces as “good fortune, but only in the sense of complete satiety.” Crowley’s detailed Nine-of-Cups passage is strongly affirmative. The adjacent Ten, *Satiety*, carries his sharper warnings about stagnation. Web summaries sometimes conflate the two.

## Notable later redesigns

### Morgan–Greer Tarot, 1979

The close-cropped design intensifies facial expression and material warmth. It follows Waite–Smith narrative meanings rather than Marseille abstraction.

### Robin Wood Tarot, 1991

Recasts the card as celebratory emotional abundance within modern Pagan visual language. Such Pagan or Goddess framing is modern, not an original feature of the suit.

### Thoth-derived Hermetic Tarot, 1979

Godfrey Dowson foregrounds astrological glyphs, decan angels, and Qabalistic titles in black-and-white linework. It is a Golden Dawn synthesis, not an early historical form.

### Modern Witch Tarot, 2019

Lisa Sterle revoices the Waite–Smith composition through contemporary fashion, gender, and racial representation. The displayed cups and satisfied posture remain; the social identity of the figure changes.

### Light Seer’s Tarot, 2019

Chris-Anne shifts the emphasis from possessions displayed behind a sitter to embodied joy, gratitude, and manifestation. “Manifestation” is a modern New Age development of the wish-card tradition.

# The game

## Original function

**Documented fact:** Tarot was created as a trick-taking game. The fifteenth-century painted cards were not designed as a fortune-telling oracle. The Morgan’s curatorial statement is explicit on this point.

## Rank of the Nine of Cups

Tarot-family games ordinarily distinguish “round” and “long” suits.

In cups and coins—the round suits—the traditional Italian ranking is often:

> King, Queen, Knight, Jack, Ace, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10.

Thus the Ace is high among pips and the Ten low. On that traditional system, the Nine is the second-lowest pip.

There are regional exceptions:

- **Modern French tarot:** French-suited cards rank King high through Ace low, so Nine stands below Ten and above Eight.
- **Sicilian tarocchi:** With low cards removed, cups rank King, Queen, Horse, Maid, 10, 9, 8, 7, 6, 5. Nine is second among surviving pips beneath Ten.
- **Minchiate:** Cups and coins run Ace high toward Ten low; Nine is next to the bottom.

## Point value

In most tarot games, ordinary numeral cards are “empty” or low-value cards.

- A common nominal scheme gives them one point before packet-counting adjustments.
- In other descriptions they are treated as zero-value cards, with value arising only through the counting method.
- In Minchiate, only kings have individual value among suit cards; the Nine of Cups scores zero individually.
- The exact effective value therefore depends on whether rules quote raw card points or net packet-counting points.

It is never a trump and has no special power from its image.

## Following suit and trick-taking

If Cups is led, a player holding the Nine may be obliged to follow suit. Whether it wins depends upon the other Cups played. If a player cannot follow suit, most tarot variants require trumping if possible, although exact obligations vary.

No historical game rule assigns it a “wish,” emotional, astrological, or Qabalistic function.

## Fool and trump order

These matters affect the game but not the Nine’s rank:

- The Fool commonly acts as an Excuse: it may be played without following suit and is normally recovered by its owner, who compensates the trick winner with another card.
- In some Central European games the Fool became the highest trump.
- Historians distinguish three major early Italian trump-order families, usually associated with Milan, Ferrara/Venice, and Bologna/Florence.
- Justice and Fortitude vary in relative order.
- Because early cards often lacked printed numerals, order was maintained by convention.
- None of these disputes turns the Nine of Cups into trump IX. Trump IX and the Nine of Cups are entirely different cards.

# Chronology of attestation

### Circa 1420: Marziano da Tortona

**Documented fact:** Marziano wrote a description of a special card game for Filippo Maria Visconti, with sixteen classical gods divided into four groups and a hierarchy among them.

**Scholarly reconstruction:** This is a precursor to tarot because it combines suits with a permanent hierarchy of special cards.

**Finding:** It is not a standard tarot deck and contains no Nine of Cups. It should not be called the earliest attestation of this particular card.

### 1440–1442: Ferrara records

A 1440 record discovered in the twenty-first century may precede the formerly canonical 1442 reference.

**Documented fact:** In February 1442 the Este court accounts record packs of *carte da trionfi*. Another entry orders from Sagramoro painted cups, coins, swords, batons, and figures for several packs.

**What this establishes:** Four Italian suits plus triumph figures existed in Ferrara.

**What it does not establish:** The surviving text does not single out the Nine of Cups or give it a meaning.

### Before 1447: Cary–Yale and Brambilla packs

These establish tarot production in Milan during Filippo Maria Visconti’s lifetime, but no secure Nine of Cups from either was confirmed.

### Circa 1450–1480: Visconti–Sforza Nine of Cups

**First surviving physical card:** Morgan MS M.630.27.

### Circa 1470–1500: *Sermones de ludo cum aliis*

The anonymous anti-gambling sermon supplies the earliest complete surviving list of twenty-one triumphs plus the Fool.

**Relevance:** It proves the shape and moral reception of the trump sequence.

**Card-specific absence:** It does not name or interpret the Nine of Cups.

### 1490–1491: Sola Busca

First surviving complete seventy-eight-card tarot and first complete scenic pip programme.

### Around 1500: Ferrarese tarot poems

Poems associate individual cards with ladies or courtiers, using tarot as a literary and social game.

**Finding:** These sources are evidence for imaginative application of cards, but no securely traceable Nine-of-Cups interpretation equivalent to “happiness” was found.

### 1526: Teofilo Folengo

Folengo’s *Chaos del Triperuno* contains a literary tarot episode linking selected cards to character and fate.

**Significance:** It is early evidence for metaphorical or divinatory-like use.

**Caution:** It is not a systematic fortune-telling manual and does not supply the later Nine-of-Cups meanings.

### Seventeenth century: Marseille pattern

Noblet, Dodal, Payen, and later Conver stabilize a French woodblock lineage with ornamental suit cards.

### Eighteenth century: early cartomancy

Bolognese manuscript lists demonstrate divinatory meanings attached to tarot before Etteilla’s full published system.

### 1770 and 1773: Etteilla’s ordinary-card cartomancy

In Etteilla-related lists, the Nine of Hearts already bears meanings including *victoire*; reversed values vary between witnesses, including *ennui*.

### 1781: Court de Gébelin and Comte de Mellet

Tarot is publicly presented as Egyptian sacred wisdom. De Mellet aligns the twenty-two trumps with Hebrew letters. Neither gives a developed individual correspondence for the Nine of Cups.

### 1783–1785: Etteilla’s tarot manuals

Etteilla publishes systematic methods and card meanings. The Nine of Cups becomes *Victoire / Sincérité*.

### Late 1780s: Grand Etteilla pack

The meaning is printed onto card 41.

### 1854–1856: Éliphas Lévi, *Dogme et Rituel de la Haute Magie*

Lévi systematizes associations among tarot, Hebrew letters, ceremonial tools, and elements.

**Card-specific finding:** He does not provide the later Golden Dawn entry “Nine of Cups—Jupiter in Pisces—Material Happiness.” Quotations from Lévi purporting to define this individual card could not be traced.

### 1863/1870: Paul Christian

Paul Christian elaborates Egyptian initiatory tarot narratives and the language of arcana.

**Card-specific absence:** His best-known temple-initiation narrative chiefly concerns the major keys, not this pip.

### 1888: Mathers

Mathers prints:

> “Nine of Cups.—Victory, Advantage, Success, Triumph, Difficulties surmounted.”

Reversed:

> “Faults, Errors, Mistakes, Imperfections.”

This substantially transmits Etteilla-derived cartomancy into English.

### Circa 1888–1890s: Golden Dawn Book T

The card becomes the Lord of Material Happiness, Jupiter in Pisces, Yesod of Heh.

### 1889: Papus and Wirth

Papus publishes *Le Tarot des Bohémiens*; Wirth publishes a twenty-two-trump deck. Neither supplies a historically independent pip picture.

### 1909–1910: Waite–Smith and *Pictorial Key*

Smith supplies the now-dominant banquet/display image; Waite publishes its description and divinatory meanings.

### 1938–1943: Crowley and Harris

They design and paint *Happiness*.

### 1944: *The Book of Thoth*

Crowley publishes the card’s metaphysical rationale.

### 1969 onward

The Thoth deck receives broad colour circulation.

### 1970s–present

Waite–Smith and Thoth become the two dominant templates for scenic/esoteric redesigns. “Wish card,” manifestation, gratitude practice, emotional self-sufficiency, smugness, and hollow success proliferate.

## Suit: Cups, Water, Heh, Briah

The Golden Dawn uses the four letters of Tetragrammaton as a master scheme:

| Suit | Element | Letter | World |
|---|---|---|---|
| Wands | Fire | Yod | Atziluth |
| Cups | Water | first Heh | Briah |
| Swords | Air | Vau | Yetzirah |
| Pentacles | Earth | final Heh | Assiah |

Thus “Yesod of Heh” means the ninth Sephirah expressed through the Cups/Water/Briah quarter of the system. It does **not** mean that the individual card is itself the Hebrew letter Heh.

## Sephirah Nine: Yesod

**Esoteric attribution:** Yesod, “Foundation,” stands ninth on the Golden Dawn Tree of Life. It is associated with the Moon, reproductive and formative power, reflection, the astral field, and the mediation of higher forces into Malkuth.

For the Nine of Cups:

- the number supplies Yesod;
- Cups supplies Briah and Water;
- Jupiter in Pisces supplies the decan;
- abundance therefore becomes a stable, overflowing watery foundation.

Crowley explicitly says that Nine returns the sequence to the Middle Pillar after the instabilities of Netzach, Seven, and Hod, Eight.

## Jupiter in Pisces

**Golden Dawn attribution:** 10°–20° Pisces, the middle decan.

Why it was judged fortunate:

- Jupiter is traditionally a benefic.
- In traditional astrology Jupiter rules Pisces.
- Water is sympathetic to Cups.
- Jupiter also corresponds to Chesed, itself treated as watery and expansive in the Golden Dawn system.

Approximate tropical calendar dates are around March 1–10, but vary slightly by year and ephemeris. Book T defines zodiacal degrees, not immutable civil dates.

## Decan angels

Book T assigns Saliah and Aariel to the decan. Spellings vary across transliteration and typesetting. These are Shem ha-Mephorash angelic attributions, not figures in early card iconography.

## Colours

Golden Dawn reconstructions vary because colours may be taken from:

- the Sephirah in the world concerned;
- suit-element scales;
- planetary and zodiacal colours;
- complementary or flashing colours.

The Cicero and Wang decks are therefore reconstructions of instructions, not proof of a single canonical historical palette.

## Grail correspondence

- **Christian tradition:** Cups readily evoke chalices, Eucharistic vessels, and the Holy Grail.
- **Documented in Waite’s deck:** The Ace of Cups explicitly includes a dove, Host, and chalice-like vessel.
- **Modern extension:** Readers often treat the entire Cups suit as a Grail quest.
- **Card-specific caution:** No medieval or Renaissance source calls the Nine of Cups a Grail card. Applying Grail symbolism to it is a later Christian-esoteric reading.

## Tzaddi/Heh and Emperor/Star controversy

Crowley’s “Tzaddi is not the Star” revision swaps the conventional Golden Dawn Hebrew-letter relationship of the Emperor and Star.

**Finding:** It has no direct effect on the Nine of Cups. The card’s Heh is the suit’s Tetragrammaton letter, while the Emperor/Star dispute concerns Hebrew letters assigned to major trumps. Conflating the two different uses of Heh is an error.

# Divinatory meanings by author

Quotations are kept short and tied to identifiable editions.

## Etteilla

### 1783 tarot list / Grand Etteilla

- Upright: **“Victoire”** — victory.
- Reversed: **“Sincérité”** — sincerity.

Earlier ordinary-card lists give the Nine of Hearts *victoire* and sometimes a different reversed term, including *ennui*. This reveals revision rather than one immutable Etteilla doctrine.

**Interpretive development:** Victory is the oldest clearly traceable root of the later “success” cluster.

## S. L. MacGregor Mathers, 1888

From *The Tarot: Its Occult Signification, Use in Fortune-Telling, and Method of Play*:

- Upright: **“Victory, Advantage, Success, Triumph, Difficulties surmounted.”**
- Reversed: **“Faults, Errors, Mistakes, Imperfections.”**

Mathers’s list is closer to Etteilla than to the later psychological wish-card reading.

## Golden Dawn Book T

The manuscript gives:

> “Complete and perfect realization of pleasure and happiness, almost perfect.”

It continues with:

- self-praise;
- vanity and conceit;
- talking much about oneself;
- kindness and lovability;
- generosity and occasional foolishness;
- wishes fulfilled.

The card was never purely positive in Book T. Vanity and self-assumption were present before modern readers derived “smugness” from Smith’s folded arms.

## A. E. Waite, 1910

From *The Pictorial Key to the Tarot*, p. 55 in the consulted PDF:

### Upright

> “Concord, contentment, physical bien-être; also victory, success, advantage; satisfaction for the Querent.”

### Reversed

> “Truth, loyalty, liberty; but the readings vary and include mistakes, imperfections, etc.”

Waite also gives the supplementary meanings:

- upright: good augury for military men;
- reversed: good business.

### The “wish card” issue

Waite does not call it “the wish card” in *Pictorial Key*. The phrase is nevertheless grounded in Book T’s “wishes fulfilled.” Its later popular use is a compressed tradition, not a quotation from Waite.

## Aleister Crowley, 1944

Crowley titles the card **Happiness** and describes it as:

> “the culmination and perfection of the original force of Water.”

He calls Jupiter in Pisces:

> “a definite benediction.”

And the card:

> “the most complete and most beneficent aspect of the force of Water.”

Crowley ordinarily reads dignity through neighbouring cards and elemental relationships rather than printing a Waite-like upright/reversed binary. Possible adverse manifestations—complacency, vanity, excessive ease—follow from imbalance and from the Golden Dawn character description, not a formal reversed entry written by Crowley.

## Paul Foster Case

**Finding:** A reliable public passage in which Case gives distinct upright and reversed Nine-of-Cups meanings could not be verified in the consulted editions. *The Tarot: A Key to the Wisdom of the Ages* treats the major keys, and it should not be cited as though it contained a full fifty-six-pip dictionary.

Case’s broader BOTA system accepts:

- Cups/Water;
- the Qabalistic Tree;
- astrological tarot correspondences inherited from the Golden Dawn;
- tarot as a tool of psychological and spiritual transformation.

Any exact Case quotation for this pip requires citation to a specific BOTA lesson or another authenticated Case text.

## Rachel Pollack

In *Seventy-Eight Degrees of Wisdom* Pollack develops the Waite–Smith image as satisfaction and sensual enjoyment, while recognizing self-satisfaction’s potential shadow. Her method reads the picture sequentially and psychologically rather than as a fixed predictive cipher.

A short fair-use summary rather than an extended quotation:

- upright: satisfaction, pleasure, and a wish realized;
- shadow: complacency or isolation in one’s own good fortune;
- reversed: dissatisfaction, an unfulfilled desire, or difficulty appreciating what has been achieved.

Pollack’s first two volumes appeared in 1980 and 1983; the combined revised edition appeared in 1997.

## Mary K. Greer

Greer’s *Tarot for Your Self* places the card in a reflective, experiential practice and preserves the Jupiter-in-Pisces / Material Happiness correspondence.

Her later *Complete Book of Tarot Reversals* warns that reversal is not simply “bad.” A reversed card may be:

- blocked;
- excessive;
- internalized;
- projected;
- delayed;
- experienced as an opposite.

Applied to the Nine, this permits several readings: blocked satisfaction, private happiness, overindulgence, complacency, or the discovery that an achieved wish does not satisfy.

No single one of these should be represented as Greer’s exclusive definition.

## Jodorowsky and Costa

Their Marseille method does not depend on reversed cards or Golden Dawn astrology. Nine is a degree of developed completion immediately before the Ten’s transformation. In Cups it can suggest mature emotional fullness, an accumulated inner world, or emotional enclosure. These are modern Marseille readings, not meanings printed by Noblet or Conver.

# Esoteric and symbolic readings

## Alchemical

### Earliest plausible layer

The Sola Busca’s programme contains furnaces, vessels, fire, tools, metals, and transformative operations. Modern Brera scholarship and Giordano Berti’s circle interpret the pack in alchemical and Hermetic terms.

**Evidence:** The implements are visible.  
**Uncertainty:** The overall programme’s exact text or patronal intention is unknown.  
**Nine-specific limit:** No surviving fifteenth-century commentary identifies its nine cups with a particular alchemical operation.

### Later Nine-of-Cups alchemy

Modern Hermetic readers interpret overflowing cups as the stabilized elixir, multiplication, or completion of a watery work.

**Status:** Modern esoteric synthesis. It is consistent with Golden Dawn symbolism but not historically documented in the early card.

## Kabbalistic

### Lévi

Lévi’s great contribution was not a unique Nine-of-Cups meaning but the integration of tarot with Hebrew-letter and magical-instrument schemes. In *Dogme et Rituel* he famously presents tarot as a universal key and relates the four ritual implements to the elemental world.

**Absence finding:** No traceable Lévi passage calls this card “Material Happiness,” gives it Yesod of Heh, or assigns Jupiter in Pisces.

### Golden Dawn

The first securely reconstructable full card-specific Qabalistic formula is:

> Yesod in Briah / Yesod of Heh.

This formula was spread by Golden Dawn manuscripts, Crowley, Regardie, Paul Foster Case’s milieu, and later decks by Wang, Cicero, and Dowson.

## Astrological

Etteilla used astrological material throughout his reconstructed Egyptian tarot, but his Nine of Cups is not the Golden Dawn decan card.

The systematic attribution Jupiter in 10°–20° Pisces arises from the Golden Dawn’s division of the thirty-six decans among the numbered cards Two through Ten. Crowley retained it.

**Conflict:** Popular astrology sometimes treats “Jupiter in Pisces” as if it were visible in Marseille ornament or inherited from Renaissance cardmakers. There is no evidence of that.

## Hermetic

The Golden Dawn makes the card a miniature talisman:

- ninefold Yesodic structure;
- Cup/Water/Briah;
- Jupiter as benefic expansion;
- Pisces as receptive watery rulership;
- lotus flowers as channels of emanation;
- angelic names;
- colour scales.

Crowley adds the geomantic figure **Laetitia**, “Joy,” ruled in his account by Jupiter in Pisces. Harris makes the static table into a field of circulating force.

## Christian

### Historical visual basis

Cups resembled chalices before tarot existed, but resemblance alone does not prove theological intention for every pip.

### Waite’s Christian mysticism

Waite made the Ace of Cups overtly Eucharistic. The Nine is deliberately more worldly: food, wine, security, and material well-being. His statement that “the picture offers the material side only” leaves room for a higher, unstated counterpart.

### Grail reading

The Nine can be positioned near the culmination of a “Grail suit,” representing attainment before the Ten’s communal fulfillment.

**Status:** Later Christian-esoteric interpretation.  
**First appearance:** No single first text was located; it developed through twentieth-century Waitean and Grail-oriented tarot literature.

## Jungian and psychological

Tarot was increasingly read through Jungian concepts during the twentieth century. Jung himself did not publish a canonical card-by-card Nine-of-Cups commentary.

Common applications include:

- conscious recognition of desire;
- gratification of the ego;
- integration of pleasure rather than repression;
- the shadow of inflation, smugness, or narcissistic display;
- projection of happiness onto possessions;
- individuation through distinguishing authentic desire from social performance.

**Status:** Modern psychological interpretation.  
**Spreaders:** Sallie Nichols for Jungian tarot generally, followed by Pollack and numerous therapeutic or archetypal readers.  
**Caution:** Calling the Renaissance card a Jungian archetype confuses modern reception with origin.

## Numerological

Modern systems typically treat Nine as completion, culmination, gestation, or the last single digit.

The Golden Dawn’s reading is more specific: Nine equals Yesod. Jodorowsky’s decimal numerology treats Nine as highly developed realization immediately before the Ten opens a new cycle.

These systems can converge on “fullness” but arrive there through different doctrines.

Eliot’s Madame Sosostris mentions “a wicked pack of cards” and invented or altered tarot figures. The Nine of Cups is not individually named.

### W. B. Yeats

Yeats’s Golden Dawn membership places him within the same occult environment that produced Book T. No card-specific Yeats text establishing a Nine-of-Cups interpretation was found.

### Ingmar Bergman

Bergman’s *The Seventh Seal* uses medieval allegory, chess, Death, and apocalyptic imagery often compared with tarot. It is not a documented quotation of the Nine of Cups.

### Alejandro Jodorowsky

Tarot informs Jodorowsky’s creative practice and film symbolism, particularly *The Holy Mountain*. Identifying particular compositions as quotations of this card requires case-by-case visual evidence. No secure building or film shot directly reproducing the Nine of Cups was found.

## Paintings and buildings

No pre-1909 painting or building was identified as a secure direct source for Smith’s seated person and nine-cup counter. Generic banquet, tavern, guild, and still-life imagery offers analogues, but analogy is not derivation.

Likewise, many contemporary murals, album covers, and artworks reproduce Waite–Smith tarot imagery, but no single architectural quotation of this card has become historically canonical.

# Controversies and disputes

## Was the Nine of Cups created as an occult emblem?

**Legend/modern projection:** Yes, as part of an ancient initiatory book.  
**Documented chronology:** No. It began as a game card. Individual occult meanings appear centuries later.

The visible sequence is:

1. fifteenth-century game pip;
2. decorative printed pip;
3. eighteenth-century cartomantic label;
4. nineteenth-century esoteric correspondence;
5. twentieth-century narrative and talismanic picture.

## Egyptian origin

Court de Gébelin declared in 1781 that tarot preserved an Egyptian Book of Thoth. Comte de Mellet and Etteilla elaborated the idea; Mathers, Papus, and others transmitted it.

**Evidence against it:**

- tarot appears in northern Italian documentary records in the fifteenth century;
- its suits belong to European playing-card culture;
- its trumps use medieval and Renaissance Christian-social imagery;
- Gébelin could not read Egyptian hieroglyphs;
- no Egyptian tarot cards, Egyptian prototype text, or transmission chain survives.

**Classification:** Historically untenable legend, culturally influential esoteric tradition.

## Romani or “Gypsy” transmission

Nineteenth-century authors, particularly Jean-Alexandre Vaillant and popular occult writers, developed the idea that Romani people carried tarot from Egypt or India.

**Evidence:** None for Romani transmission of tarot into Italy.  
**Contrary chronology:** Tarot is recorded in Italian courts before any documented Romani tarot practice.  
**Modern context:** Linguistic and genetic research places Romani origins in the Indian subcontinent, not Egypt.

The word “Gypsy” itself arose from the mistaken belief that Roma were Egyptian. Modern scholarship treats the transmission story as legend and advises historically accurate language.

## Cathar claim

Some modern writers connect tarot imagery with Cathar survival, dualism, or secret heresy.

**Evidence:** No fifteenth-century document connects tarot’s invention, suit cards, or the Nine of Cups with Cathars. The Cathar institutions had been destroyed long before tarot’s documented appearance.

**Classification:** Modern speculative legend.

## Templar claim

Likewise, claims that the Knights Templar encoded doctrine in tarot lack a manuscript, deck, workshop record, or chain of transmission.

**Classification:** Legend or modern invention.

## Kabbalistic antiquity

The fact that tarot has twenty-two trumps and Hebrew has twenty-two letters encouraged occult correspondences.

- De Mellet published one scheme in 1781.
- Lévi reorganized the relationship.
- The Golden Dawn placed Aleph on the Fool and developed the best-known English system.
- Crowley revised the Emperor/Star letters.

**Documented fact:** These correspondences exist in modern occult texts.  
**Unsupported historical claim:** That fifteenth-century cardmakers deliberately encoded Hebrew letters.

For this pip, Yesod of Heh is still later: it depends on the Golden Dawn’s entire four-world and decan structure.

## Sola Busca as the source of Smith’s Nine

This requires unusually careful wording.

- **Documented:** Sola Busca has illustrated pips.
- **Documented:** British Museum photographs became available shortly before Smith’s work.
- **Scholarly reconstruction:** Smith probably saw or knew some Sola Busca imagery.
- **Documented visual parallels:** Several Smith cards resemble particular Sola Busca compositions.
- **Not verified:** A specific visual borrowing from Sola Busca Nine of Cups to Smith Nine of Cups.
- **Better explanation:** Smith dramatized the Book T title *Material Happiness* and earlier “victory/success” meanings.

Therefore the item-specific lead is **not confirmed**.

## “Wish card”

The nickname is often attributed to Waite.

**Correction:** Waite’s published Nine entry does not use the word *wish*. Book T does say “wishes fulfilled.” The popular nickname is therefore Golden Dawn-compatible but not a verbatim Waite title.

Its rise as a ubiquitous standalone nickname appears to be twentieth-century teaching tradition. A definitive first printed use of the exact phrase “the wish card” was not established from the consulted sources.

## Thoth title and “satiety”

The Thoth Nine is unequivocally titled **Happiness**. The Thoth Ten is **Satiety**.

Some secondary summaries attach “complete satiety” too strongly to the Nine or accidentally reproduce language belonging to the Ten. Crowley does speak of completion and overflowing fullness in the Nine, but his detailed paragraph calls it beneficent.

## Reversal as inherent tradition

Etteilla physically encoded upright and reversed values. Waite preserved reversals, sometimes with meanings that are not simple opposites.

Modern claims that reversal “always means blocked energy,” “always means the opposite,” or “always means the shadow” are modern methods, not universal historical rules.

## Misprints and variants

No famous historical misprint unique to the Nine of Cups was identified that demonstrably founded an enduring doctrine.

The main textual hazards are instead:

- confusing Nine/Happiness with Ten/Satiety;
- attributing “wish card” directly to Waite;
- treating modern Golden Dawn reconstructions as original Order paintings;
- treating “Noblet 1650” as an exact printed date;
- calling the Visconti–Sforza card later than Sola Busca without checking museum catalogues;
- claiming Sola Busca supplied Smith’s exact Nine composition;
- confusing the suit’s Heh with the Emperor/Star Hebrew-letter controversy.

# Open questions

1. **Exact dating of the Visconti–Sforza Nine.**  
   The Morgan gives circa 1450–1480. More precise workshop chronology remains contested.

2. **Authorship within the Bembo milieu.**  
   Bonifacio Bembo is the traditional attribution, but multiple hands and later replacements complicate the full pack.

3. **The complete intellectual programme of Sola Busca.**  
   Alchemical, Roman-historical, Venetian, Ferrarese, and political readings compete. No explanatory handbook survives.

4. **What Smith actually saw.**  
   Museum access and visual parallels make knowledge of Sola Busca plausible, but no diary or letter enumerates her borrowings.

5. **Immediate source of Smith’s banqueter.**  
   Golden Dawn meanings explain the concept. A specific print, stage costume, illustration, or model for the figure has not been securely identified.

6. **First exact use of “wish card.”**  
   “Wishes fulfilled” is in Book T, but the earliest printed sentence naming the Nine “the wish card” remains to be established.

7. **Etteilla textual stemma.**  
   His ordinary-card lists, tarot manuals, followers’ editions, and Grand Etteilla printings modify reversed meanings. “Sincerity” is securely printed on the Grand Etteilla card, but earlier witnesses differ.

8. **Case’s exact card-specific doctrine.**  
   Public Case books emphasize the majors; authenticated BOTA course lessons would be needed for a fully documented Case entry.

9. **Papus’s alleged “children” meaning.**  
   This appears in derivative tables, but a secure page in the original 1889 French edition was not located during this pass. It should not be quoted as settled without an edition and page.

10. **Marseille floral transmission.**  
    Woodblocks reveal obvious copying and workshop inheritance, but the degree to which individual leaf and flower changes were intentional symbols rather than decorative or technical variation remains disputed.
