# Six of Cups: Research Dossier

## Method and evidentiary labels

This dossier distinguishes six kinds of statement:

- **Documented fact** — supported by a surviving card, archival record, printed rulebook, manuscript, or author’s published text.
- **Scholarly reconstruction** — an inference argued from physical, documentary, stylistic, or game-historical evidence.
- **Tradition** — a conventional interpretation transmitted through tarot practice, without necessarily being historical in origin.
- **Esoteric attribution** — a correspondence deliberately assigned by an occult author or school.
- **Disputed claim** — proposed by identifiable writers but challenged or unsupported.
- **Legend / modern invention** — a narrative or symbolic elaboration not evidenced before modern occult or popular tarot.

The date of a surviving deck is not automatically the date of every modern facsimile image. Modern restorations often replace missing cards without marking them conspicuously.

### What the card was originally

**Documented fact:** In fifteenth-century Italian tarot, it was a game card bearing six cup symbols. It did not carry a printed title such as “Pleasure,” an astrological glyph, a Hebrew letter, a Grail interpretation, or a scene of childhood.

**Scholarly conclusion:** The earliest defensible meaning of the card is consequently ludic and ordinal: it is the six-rank member of the Cups suit. Per-card divinatory meanings appear only centuries later.

### Names and numbering

The card’s name is ordinarily generated rather than separately titled: numeral plus suit. “Six of Cups,” *Sei di Coppe*, and *Six de Coupes* are therefore descriptions of rank and suit, not allegorical names comparable to *La Maison Dieu* or *L’Amoureux*.

The Golden Dawn’s “Lord of Pleasure” and Crowley’s shorter “Pleasure” are nineteenth- and twentieth-century occult titles.

The following famous regional renamings do **not** affect this card:

- the Popess as Juno and Pope as Jupiter in the Besançon pattern;
- the Tower as *La Maison Dieu*;
- the unnumbered Fool, later numbered 0 or XXII by occultists;
- regional changes to Justice and Strength.

Those concern the trump sequence. They are relevant to the history of tarot packs but do not create an alternate number or identity for the Six of Cups.

“Minor Arcana” is also an occult-era classification rather than a fifteenth-century card-player’s term. In game history, “suit card,” “numeral card,” or “pip card” is less anachronistic.

# Iconography deck by deck

## Fifteenth-century Milan

### Visconti di Modrone / Cary–Yale, c. 1441–47, Milan

**Documented object:** The Beinecke Library’s Visconti di Modrone pack, often called Cary–Yale, includes a Six of Cups. The pack is normally dated around the marriage era of Bianca Maria Visconti and Francesco Sforza, although narrower dates vary by catalogue and scholar.

**Depiction:** Six gold cups are distributed symmetrically on a richly decorated field of foliage and flowers. There is no narrative action and no human figure.

**Meaning at the time:** No card-specific allegorical meaning is documented. The costly materials, heraldic setting, and courtly ornament locate it in elite Milanese gift and gaming culture.

**Caution:** Modern reproductions sometimes identify this deck simply as “Visconti,” causing it to be confused with the later Visconti-Sforza pack.

### Brera-Brambilla, mid-fifteenth century, Milan

The Brambilla pack survives only in part. Brera describes it as a group of forty-eight late-Gothic cards made for the Duke of Milan.

Where Cups pips survive, their method is the same general Milanese luxury vocabulary: repeated suit emblems, gold, floral ground, and dynastic ornament. The card is not a narrative scene.

**Open catalogue issue:** I did not find an accessible Brera object record conclusively itemizing a surviving Brambilla Six of Cups. It should not be silently reconstructed from a modern facsimile.

### Visconti-Sforza, c. 1450–58, Milan or Cremona

**Documented object:** The Six of Cups survives among the Accademia Carrara portion of the pack. Seventy-four of the original seventy-eight cards survive across Bergamo and New York.

**Depiction:** Six ornate goblets on a pale, densely flowered field, arranged symmetrically. Like the other numeral cards, it multiplies the suit sign rather than illustrating an event.

**Material context:** Tempera, gold, and silver on pasteboard; workshop attribution remains debated among Bonifacio Bembo, the Bembo circle, Zavattari, and related hands. The Morgan catalogue documents the larger pack and the history of the attribution.

**Documented versus interpretive:** The flowers are physical decoration. “Memory,” “childhood,” “Grail,” and “emotional reciprocity” are not documented fifteenth-century readings of them.

The Morgan’s portion contains the Ace, Three, Five, and Nine of Cups but not the Six; this is why searches limited to the Morgan catalogue can misleadingly suggest that the card is lost. [Morgan catalogue](https://www.themorgan.org/manuscript/158525)

## Fifteenth-century Ferrara and related cards

### Este cards

Several Ferrarese or Este-associated tarot fragments survive, but no coherent card-by-card genealogy of a particular Six of Cups can be established from the accessible fragments.

**Scholarly reconstruction:** Ferrara is unquestionably important to early tarot because its court ledgers document *carte da trionfi* in 1442. That does not mean every surviving anonymous fifteenth-century pip can safely be called “Este.”

### Mantegna “Tarocchi,” c. 1460s–70s

**Documented fact:** The so-called Mantegna Tarocchi are didactic engravings organized into five series, not a tarot game pack, and were not engraved by Mantegna.

They have no Cups suit and no Six of Cups. They are relevant only as evidence of Renaissance encyclopedic and hierarchical imagery later compared with tarot trumps.

---

## Sola Busca, engraving c. 1490; painted copy dated 1491

### The card itself

**Documented fact:** The Sola Busca is the earliest complete surviving tarot pack and the earliest surviving pack whose numeral cards are comprehensively illustrated or elaborated rather than being only repeated suit marks. Brera dates the engraved series around 1490 and the colored pack to 1491; the exact interpretation of the painted date inscription has received scholarly discussion. [Brera exhibition](https://pinacotecabrera.org/news/mostra/il-segreto-dei-segreti-i-tarocchi-sola-busca/)

The Six of Cups is organized around six vessels with elaborate plant or organic ornament. It is not the Waite-Smith scene of one child offering flowers to another. Different reproductions make the details easier or harder to distinguish, but no straightforward child-gift narrative is present.

### Was it Pamela Colman Smith’s source?

- **Documented fact:** In 1907 the Busca-Serbelloni owners supplied the British Museum with photographs of the complete pack.
- **Scholarly reconstruction:** Smith or Waite could therefore have seen the photographs before producing their deck in 1909.
- **Strong comparative evidence:** Several Waite-Smith pips—notably the Three of Swords and Ten of Wands—have striking compositional parallels to Sola Busca cards.
- **Disputed overextension:** It is often asserted that the Sola Busca Six of Cups directly supplied Smith’s Six. The visual evidence is not comparably strong. The RWS children, old courtyard, flower-filled vessels, and act of giving are not copied from the Sola Busca Six.

The best formulation is: **Sola Busca established an important precedent for scenic or symbolically elaborated pips and probably influenced Smith’s minor-arcana project, but a direct card-to-card derivation of the RWS Six of Cups remains unproved.**

## Budapest and Rosenwald sheets, late fifteenth or early sixteenth century

### Budapest sheets

The so-called Budapest sheets preserve portions of an early Italian woodcut tarot, usually reconstructed as a 78-card pack. Pip designs are formal and suit-emblematic, not Waite-like narrative scenes.

**Caution:** Commercial “Budapest Tarot” decks are modern reconstructions. Their completed Six of Cups may incorporate editorial drawing where the sheet is damaged or incomplete.

### Rosenwald sheets, c. 1500

The Rosenwald sheets are early woodcut evidence, some of it recovered from bookbinding waste. Their main importance is the early printed tarot pattern and trump sequence.

**Documented limitation:** The commonly reproduced Rosenwald material is especially associated with the trumps. Modern complete “Rosenwald” packs may supplement or reconstruct numeral cards. A reconstructed Six should not be cited as an intact c. 1500 original without an object-level provenance.

Some copied or retouched Rosenwald sheets contain erroneous Roman numerals; these are restoration or transmission problems, not evidence of a special Six of Cups tradition.

## Tarot de Marseille lineage

### General Marseille design

The Marseille Six de Coupes is a formal pip:

- three cups on the left;
- three on the right;
- a central vertical vegetal stem;
- leaves and floral terminals;
- frequently a conspicuous central flower;
- VI or split numerals at the margins, depending on edition.

It contains no children, gift, house, guard, or nostalgic episode.

**Documented physical fact:** Floral ornament organizes empty space around the suit emblems.

**Later symbolic tradition:** Authors such as Paul Marteau, Jodorowsky, and Costa read the central stem as organic growth, emotional circulation, gestation, union of ternaries, or a level of affective development. Those are twentieth- and twenty-first-century interpretations, not printer’s explanations from the seventeenth century.

### Jean Noblet, Paris, c. 1650

Noblet’s surviving Marseille-type cards are cruder and more compact than later standardized packs. The Six is a symmetrical pip field organized by vegetal ornament.

The exact appearance of individual cards in modern Noblet editions depends on restoration of damaged originals.

### Jean Dodal, Lyon, c. 1701–15

Dodal preserves six cups in two vertical columns with a strong central floral axis. Red, blue, yellow, and black linework create a dense, almost architectural balance.

No printed cartomantic meaning accompanies the historical card.

### Nicolas Conver, Marseille, traditionally “1760”

Conver’s name became shorthand for a highly influential Marseille pattern. Surviving BnF impressions catalogued to the nineteenth century complicate casual claims that every “Conver 1760” image is an impression printed in that year.

The Six shows two columns of three cups divided by a red central stem, with a star-like central flower and blue-red foliation. Modern card-by-card description is supported by the BnF scan tradition, but the dating of particular impressions requires care.

### Paul Marteau / Grimaud, 1930s

Marteau’s Grimaud Tarot de Marseille regularized line, color, spelling, and printing for the mass market.

**Esoteric attribution:** Marteau reads the six as equilibrium produced by two ternaries and the central plant as the physical, psychic, and spiritual forces needed for development. For Cups, this becomes balanced, protective feeling.

This is a modern numerological exegesis applied to inherited ornament.

### Camoin–Jodorowsky, 1997

Philippe Camoin and Alejandro Jodorowsky presented their deck as a restoration assembled through comparison of historical Marseille evidence.

**Documented fact:** It is a late-twentieth-century reconstruction and redesign.

**Disputed claim:** Promotional language sometimes implies recovery of a single original or hidden canonical tarot. Historians generally treat the Marseille family as a lineage of changing workshop patterns, not a lost pristine archetype recoverable in every detail.

In Jodorowsky and Marianne Costa’s system the Six of Cups belongs to the emotional series and can signify the pleasure of loving, reciprocity, or movement toward emotional maturity. This is an authorial reading, not an early Marseille gloss.

## Minchiate

Minchiate expanded the trump series to produce a 97-card pack but retained the four ordinary suits. Thus it includes a Six of Cups.

**Depiction:** In historical Florentine Minchiate patterns it remains a conventional pip: six cup emblems with ornamental fillers, not an allegorical scene.

**Game status:** It is a low-value numeral card. In the Cups and Coins suits, the pip ranking is normally reversed: Ace highest among pips and Ten lowest. Therefore the Six falls below Five and above Seven in trick-taking rank.

The added zodiacal and elemental trumps of Minchiate should not be confused with the Golden Dawn’s later assignment of Sun in Scorpio to this pip.

---

## Bolognese pattern

Tarocchino Bolognese normally removes the Two through Five from every suit, leaving Ace and Six through Ten. The Six of Cups therefore remains in the shortened 62-card pack.

Its image is a conventional six-cup pip arrangement. It acquires no special trump-like value.

The Bolognese tradition has unusual rules and four equally ranking *moretti* trumps, but those do not alter the identity of the Six of Cups.

---

## Sicilian pattern

The modern traditional Sicilian pack is reduced to sixty-three cards, plus a later tax-stamp Ace of Coins in sold packs. In Cups the surviving numerals run from Five through Ten, so the Six remains.

The current ranking is King, Queen, Horse, Maid, 10, 9, 8, 7, 6, 5. Thus the Six is the second-lowest Cups card.

Again, this is game rank, not a divinatory scale. [Sicilian rules](https://www.pagat.com/tarot/sicilian.html)

---

## Etteilla’s Grand Etteilla, Paris, c. 1788–89

Etteilla reorganized the tarot into an expressly occult and divinatory “Egyptian” book. Numeral cards were numbered within his reordered total sequence and commonly printed with upright and reversed keywords.

For the Six of Cups:

- upright: **Le Passé** — the past;
- reversed: **L’Avenir** — the future.

**Documented tradition:** These meanings are already present in the Etteilla stream and were repeated by Mathers and Waite.

**Important genealogy:** Modern “nostalgia” derives more securely from Etteilla’s “past” than from any medieval cup symbolism. Smith then gave that inherited temporal meaning a memorable visual scene.

The Grand Etteilla design history is complicated: later “Grand Etteilla I, II, and III” patterns do not all show the same scenes. An image from a nineteenth-century Lismon, Duserre, or Lemarchand issue should not automatically be dated 1789.

---

## Oswald Wirth, 1889

Wirth’s original deck contained twenty-two trumps only.

**Documented absence:** There is no original 1889 Wirth Six of Cups. Later commercial “Wirth” packs with fifty-six minors are expansions by publishers or subsequent designers.

Wirth’s numerology and occult philosophy may be applied to six and to Cups by followers, but no deck-specific 1889 pip image exists.

---

## Golden Dawn designs: Book T and reconstructions

### Book T, c. late 1880s–1890s

Book T gives the most important pre-Crowley occult design specification:

> “AN Angelic Hand … holds a group of stems of water-lilies or lotuses, from which six flowers bend, one over each cup. From these flowers a white glistening water flows into the cups as from a fountain, but they are not yet full. Above and below are Sun and Scorpio referring to the Decan.”

It then gives:

> “Commencement of steady increase, gain and pleasure; but commencement only.”

And the Qabalistic formula:

> “Tiphareth of H (Beginning of wish, happiness, success, or enjoyment).”

These are explicit in the manuscript tradition reproduced as Book T, pp. 33–34. [Book T scan](https://jantetteroo.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/02/Mathers_and_felkin_golden_dawn_book_t_th.pdf)

### Wang and Cicero reconstructions

Robert Wang’s *Golden Dawn Tarot* and the Ciceros’ later reconstructions visualize Book T’s verbal specification. Because no single universally authoritative, fully painted original Golden Dawn deck survives, these are modern realizations of manuscript instructions.

Typical components are:

- six chalices;
- an issuing angelic hand;
- lotus stems;
- one flower pouring into each cup;
- Sun and Scorpio glyphs;
- Golden Dawn color scales.

They should be called reconstructions, not reproductions of a securely preserved 1888 master card.

## Waite–Smith, London, 1909

### Pamela Colman Smith’s image

The card shows:

- a larger child or small youth bending toward a smaller child;
- the larger figure offering a cup filled with white flowers;
- five other flower-filled cups;
- an old, enclosed courtyard or garden;
- a large building or manor;
- an adult or guard-like figure walking away in the background;
- heraldic carving on the foreground pedestal.

Waite calls them “Children in an old garden.”

The flowers are often identified in modern sources as white, five-pointed blossoms; specific botanical identifications are speculative. The sex of the children is also not certain from Smith’s drawing. “Boy and girl” is traditional, not securely documented.

### Waite’s published explanation

In *The Pictorial Key to the Tarot*:

> “Children in an old garden, their cups filled with flowers.”

His divinatory text reads:

> “A card of the past and of memories, looking back, as—for example—on childhood; happiness, enjoyment, but coming rather from the past; things that have vanished.”

He also records an alternative forward-looking reading:

> “Another reading reverses this, giving new relations, new knowledge, new environment…”

Reversed:

> “The future, renewal, that which will come to pass presently.”

These are direct continuations and elaborations of the Etteilla–Mathers past/future polarity. [Waite text](https://sacred-texts.com/book/the-pictorial-key-to-the-tarot/read/six-of-cups)

### Sources of the picture

- **Documented:** Smith knew Waite and both were Golden Dawn members.
- **Documented:** Waite’s meaning stresses the past and childhood.
- **Probable:** The scene was designed to make the past/childhood meaning immediately readable.
- **Probable at deck level:** Sola Busca photographs were available at the British Museum and influenced the general experiment in illustrated pips.
- **Disputed:** The particular Sola Busca Six was the direct compositional source.
- **Untraced:** Claims that the scene directly copies a named painting, stage design, or building have not been demonstrated from a primary source.

The V&A’s statement that Smith had a completely “blank slate” on the minors should be qualified: her freedom was greater than on the trumps, but the images repeatedly encode meanings inherited from Etteilla, Mathers, and the Golden Dawn. [V&A history](https://www.vam.ac.uk/articles/tarot-cards)

## Paul Foster Case / BOTA, twentieth century

Paul Foster Case and Jessie Burns Parke’s BOTA deck deliberately returned the minor cards to non-scenic suit emblems. The Six of Cups is six cups rather than a copy of Smith’s children.

Case retained Golden Dawn-style astrology but revised the divinatory emphasis. In *The Oracle of the Tarot* he calls attention to the Qabalistic title of six/Beauty:

> “The King in the Vesica is a reminder that one of the titles of the number 6, or Beauty, in the Qabalah is Melek, the King.”

He assigns the second decan of Scorpio and gives the keyword **Betterment**. His planetary explanation uses later astrological rulership language involving Jupiter and Neptune rather than simply printing “Sun in Scorpio,” illustrating how Case’s published astrology does not always reproduce Book T verbatim. [Case text](https://media-01.imu.nl/storage/divona.be/17547/oracle-of-tarot-paul-foster-case.pdf)

## Crowley–Harris Thoth, painted 1938–43; book 1944; deck publication history

### The image

Frieda Harris constructed six rounded, orange-gold cups in a balanced geometric formation. Lotus blossoms and curling stems form a dancing network. Water pours from the flowers into the cups. Sun and Scorpio glyphs identify the decan. The background is blue-green and watery, with radiating or web-like geometry.

The card bears the title **PLEASURE**.

This is not an illustration of childhood or memory. It is a visual and metaphysical development of Book T’s lotus-fountain design.

### Crowley’s interpretation

Crowley writes:

> “This card shows the influence of the number Six, Tiphareth, in the suit of Water.”

He describes the central operation as:

> “the influence of the Sun on Water.”

Because the Sun is in Scorpio, he interprets its heat as a fertile putrefaction:

> “the basis of all fertility, all life.”

His definition of the title is crucial:

> “Pleasure … must be understood in its highest sense: it implies well-being, harmony of natural forces without effort or strain, ease, satisfaction.”

He explicitly excludes mere appetite:

> “Foreign to the idea of the card is the gratification of natural or artificial desires.”

He nevertheless associates it with the “fulfilment of the sexual Will” through the combined Sephirah, planet, element, and sign. [Book of Thoth scan](https://www.mr-kaplan.com/Library/Crowley/Book%20of%20Thoth.pdf)

### Publication date

The paintings were made from 1938 to 1943. *The Book of Thoth* appeared in 1944. Limited or incomplete reproductions preceded the widely distributed full-color deck; commercial publication dates are variously given as 1969 or 1970 depending on edition and definition. “Published 1969” is therefore defensible for an early commercial issue but should not be mistaken for the paintings’ date.

## Notable modern redesigns

Modern Six of Cups cards largely branch into three families:

1. **RWS descendants:** children, flowers, memory, reunion, generosity, inner child.
2. **Marseille revivals:** six emblems plus floral growth; meanings derived from number and suit.
3. **Thoth/Golden Dawn descendants:** six cups, lotus fountains, Sun in Scorpio, pleasure and fertility.

Common modern inventions include ancestral photographs, childhood toys, reunions with a former lover, therapy imagery, past lives, and explicit trauma or “inner-child healing.” These may be useful deck-specific symbols but are not antique meanings.

# The game

## Standard Italian tarot structure

A standard 78-card pack has:

- twenty-one trumps;
- the Fool;
- four fourteen-card suits;
- ten numeral cards plus Page/Knave, Knight, Queen, and King in each suit.

The Six of Cups is never a trump. Trump-order disputes do not change its rank within Cups.

## Trick-taking rank

In most early and traditional tarot games:

- Swords and Batons rank upward among pips: Ten high, Ace low.
- Cups and Coins rank in reverse: Ace high, Ten low.

Accordingly, among Cups numeral cards:

**Ace, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10**

is the descending order from high to low.

The Six therefore beats Seven through Ten but loses to Ace through Five and all Cups court cards.

Exceptions exist. Modern French Tarot uses French suits and natural ranking, while Sicilian Tarocchi has its own reduced-pack ordering.

## Card-point value

In the common tarot counting system:

- Kings and *oudlers* or special cards carry high values;
- Queens, Knights, and Jacks progressively less;
- ordinary pips such as the Six are “empty” or low cards.

In French Tarot a pip has a nominal value of one-half point when paired for counting. In several Central European Tarock games ordinary suit cards are worth no positive card points. The Six is strategically useful for following suit, forcing, discarding, or taking a low trick, but is not normally a counting card.

## Minchiate

The Six of Cups remains in the 97-card pack. It is a low pip and follows the reversed order of the round suits. It has no special *versicola* value.

## Bolognese Tarocchino

Because Two through Five are removed, the Six is the lowest surviving numbered Cups card above the Ace in the shortened pack’s peculiar sequence. It remains an ordinary suit card.

## Sicilian Tarocchi

In the 63-card traditional pack it ranks between Seven and Five and has no independent point value. It may contribute only through the game’s grouped card-counting formula.

## Fool and trump-order disputes

The Fool historically acts as an “excuse” in many tarot games: it can be played without following suit, is ordinarily retained by its owner, and does not win the trick, subject to local rules. In some Central European games the *Sküs* became the highest trump.

Historians classify early trump sequences into three broad regional orders, usually called A, B, and C:

- northern/northeastern Italian;
- Ferrara/Bologna;
- Milan/Marseille-associated orders.

Justice, Strength, Temperance, the virtues, and the ecclesiastical trumps shift among them. None changes the Six of Cups.

# Chronology of attestation

## c. 1420: Marziano da Tortona

Marziano described a bespoke pack made for Filippo Maria Visconti, painted by Michelino da Besozzo, with sixteen deity cards and suits of birds.

**Documented:** This is a precursor to tarot’s trump principle.

**Important absence:** It did not have a standard Cups suit and therefore contained no Six of Cups in the later sense.

## 1441–47: Visconti di Modrone/Cary–Yale

The first surviving candidate for the card itself: six cups on a decorated Milanese field.

## 28 July 1442: Ferrara ledger

The Este court paid Marchionne Burdochi for “uno paro de carte da trionfi” for Ercole and Sigismondo.

**Documented:** This is one of the earliest unambiguous archival attestations of *carte da trionfi*. [Le Tarot essay reproducing the ledger wording](https://letarot.it/Le-origini-italiane-del-Ludus-Triumphorum_pag_pg439_ita.aspx)

**Absence:** The ledger does not name the Six of Cups separately.

## c. 1450s: Visconti-Sforza

A complete Cups sequence survives, including the Six.

## c. 1470–1500: *Sermo perutilis de ludo cum aliis*

The anonymous sermon condemns games and supplies an early tarot trump list.

**Documented:** Valuable for trump names and order.

**Absence:** It does not supply individual meanings for numeral cards and is not an occult key to the Six.

## c. 1490–91: Sola Busca

Earliest complete surviving tarot, including an elaborately designed Six of Cups.

## c. 1500: appropriative poems

Early Italian poems assign persons, qualities, or verses to tarot cards. They are evidence of social and literary play with cards.

**Caution:** Such poems do not establish a stable occult divinatory meaning for the Six of Cups unless the manuscript explicitly identifies it. No secure c. 1500 “childhood/nostalgia” meaning was located.

## Early sixteenth century: *tarocchi*

The name *tarocchi* replaces or supplements *trionfi*. Folengo’s *tarocchi appropriati* is important evidence for literary card appropriation.

The etymology remains uncertain. Egyptian derivations proposed by Court de Gébelin are not linguistically supported.

## Seventeenth century: French rules and Marseille cards

French rules document trick-taking play and reversed ranking in Cups and Coins. Noblet, Dodal, and related makers establish the visible Marseille pip lineage.

## 1781: Court de Gébelin and Comte de Mellet

Gébelin declares tarot an Egyptian sacred book. De Mellet correlates the twenty-two trumps with the Hebrew alphabet and calls the pack a Book of Thoth.

**Absence:** Neither supplies the Golden Dawn formula for the Six of Cups. Their Hebrew-letter systems concern primarily the trumps, not an individual pip letter.

## 1780s–89: Etteilla

The Six acquires the explicit temporal polarity Past/Future. This is the first securely traceable ancestor of its later memory meaning.

## 1854–56: Éliphas Lévi

Lévi correlates the four magical instruments and suits with elemental and Qabalistic structures. In *Dogme et Rituel de la Haute Magie* he calls tarot:

> “une véritable machine philosophique”  
> “a veritable philosophical machine.”

He helped establish Cup/Water and tarot/Qabalah as an integrated occult language.

**Absence:** Lévi gives no identifiable card-specific Six of Cups paragraph comparable to Waite’s. Claims to quote “Lévi’s Six of Cups meaning” usually synthesize his general system later.

## 1870s: Paul Christian

Christian elaborates Egyptian initiation chambers and occult tarot traditions derived from earlier French speculation.

**Legend:** His initiatory narrative is literary occult construction, not an Egyptian archaeological report.

**Absence:** No historically influential, uniquely Christian Six of Cups meaning was found.

## 1888: Mathers

Mathers gives:

> “Shows either that what precedes it is past, has occurred already; or if R., what is going to happen.”

Elsewhere his expanded keywords are “The Past, passed by, Faded, Vanished, Disappeared”; reversed, “The Future … Shortly, Soon.” [Mathers text](https://hermetics.net/media-library/tarot/samuel-liddell-macgregor-mathers-the-tarot/)

## 1888–90s: Golden Dawn Book T

“Lord of Pleasure”; Sun in Scorpio; Tiphareth of Water; lotus fountains; steady increase and pleasure.

## 1889: Papus and Wirth

Papus’s *Tarot des Bohémiens* spreads the tarot-as-universal-occult-book model. Wirth supplies twenty-two occult trumps.

Papus-associated divinatory tables give the Six of Cups a severe reading—insuperable obstacles, widowhood, separation—which conflicts sharply with Golden Dawn “Pleasure.” The precise French table and edition must be cited when using it because Papus editions and derivative tables vary.

## 1909–10: Waite-Smith and *Pictorial Key*

Past becomes visibly childhood; memory becomes an old garden; reversed future becomes renewal.

## 1930s: Case and Marteau

Case gives “Betterment” in a Golden Dawn-derived astrological framework. Marteau gives numerological equilibrium and balanced feeling within Marseille ornament.

## 1938–44: Crowley and Harris

“Pleasure” becomes the solar fertilization of Water, including sexual and sacramental significance.

## 1970s–present

Humanistic, Jungian, feminist, queer, therapeutic, and trauma-aware readers transform “childhood” into inner-child work, family patterning, reunion, recovered memory, or nostalgia’s shadow.

## Heh

“Cups = Heh” requires precision.

In the Golden Dawn tetragrammaton formula:

- Wands = Yod;
- Cups = first Heh;
- Swords = Vav;
- Pentacles = final Heh.

Thus the Six of Cups is Tiphareth in the first-Heh suit or world, commonly associated with Water and Briah.

**It does not possess a unique Hebrew letter of its own.** The twenty-two Hebrew letters are assigned to trumps. Statements such as “the Hebrew letter of the Six of Cups is Heh” collapse a suit attribution into an individual card-letter correspondence.

## Tree of Life

Six corresponds to Tiphareth: Beauty, harmony, the Sun, the mediating center of the lower Tree, and the balance of surrounding Sephiroth.

In Cups/Water, the Golden Dawn reads this as emotion brought into solar harmony. Book T still qualifies it as a beginning: the cups receive water but are “not yet full.”

Crowley intensifies this into effortless natural harmony, fertility, sexuality, and sacramental putrefaction.

## Decan

The verified attribution is:

- 10°–20° Scorpio;
- Sun as decan ruler;
- “Lord of Pleasure.”

Calendar conversions vary by year and astrological convention, typically falling in early November. Exact civil dates are secondary to the zodiacal degrees.

## Colors

Golden Dawn reconstructions normally draw the card’s colors from:

- Tiphareth;
- Water/Briah;
- Scorpio;
- solar and complementary color scales.

No single modern printed reconstruction is automatically authoritative because Book T is more explicit about design than about a universally fixed commercial palette.

## The Emperor/Star Tzaddi–Heh question

Crowley’s famous “Tzaddi is not the Star” revision swaps the traditional Golden Dawn letter assignments of the Emperor and Star in his Thelemic system.

This has **no direct effect** on the Six of Cups. It concerns trump letters, not the first-Heh association of the Cups suit. Conflating these two uses of “Heh” is a frequent modern error.

# Divinatory meanings by author

## Etteilla, 1780s

- Upright: **The Past**.
- Reversed: **The Future**.

This is the earliest clearly traceable core from which Mathers and Waite develop the familiar meaning.

## Mathers, 1888

> “Shows either that what precedes it is past, has occurred already; or if R., what is going to happen.”

Expanded listings:

- Upright: “The Past, passed by, Faded, Vanished, Disappeared.”
- Reversed: “The Future, that which is to come, Shortly, Soon.”

## Golden Dawn Book T

Book T does not structure the meaning as a simple upright/reversed pair. It uses dignity:

- Well dignified: steady increase, gain, pleasure; beginning of wish, happiness, success, enjoyment; amiability and patience.
- Ill dignified: vanity, unwarranted self-assertion, thanklessness, presumption, contention, and strife.

## Waite, 1910

Upright:

> “A card of the past and of memories, looking back, as—for example—on childhood; happiness, enjoyment, but coming rather from the past; things that have vanished.”

Alternative upright reading:

> “new relations, new knowledge, new environment.”

Reversed:

> “The future, renewal, that which will come to pass presently.”

Additional Waite table:

- Upright: pleasant memories.
- Reversed: inheritance arriving quickly.

## Crowley, 1944

Crowley usually reads by dignity rather than mechanically reversing Thoth cards.

- Dignified: well-being, effortless harmony, ease, satisfaction, fertility, fulfillment of sexual Will.
- Ill dignified: the Book T shadow—vanity, presumption, contention, or unstable pleasure—may be used, but Crowley does not give a simple printed “reversed Six” formula.

## Paul Foster Case

Keyword: **Betterment**.

Well dignified:

- deep emotion;
- ardor and enthusiasm;
- generosity;
- gain through marriage, partner, or inheritance;
- beginning of steady gain or pleasure;
- unusual circumstances.

Ill dignified:

- deceit concerning a partner’s money;
- loss of inheritance through swindle;
- danger involving water, poison, or anesthetics;
- reversal of fortune.

Case’s list is far removed from RWS nostalgia, despite sharing Golden Dawn ancestry.

## Rachel Pollack

In *Seventy-Eight Degrees of Wisdom*, Pollack develops the RWS image as childhood, innocence, giving, reunion, nostalgia, and the return of something emotionally meaningful from the past. She also warns against sentimentalizing childhood or retreating from adult reality.

A verbatim card-length quotation could not be responsibly supplied from an openly licensed primary edition. Pollack’s own book, rather than internet summaries, should be consulted: revised edition, Thorsons/HarperCollins; later Weiser anniversary editions.

## Mary K. Greer

Greer treats the upright card through memory, childhood patterns, gifts, reunion, and emotional roots. Her reversal method resists one fixed antonym. A reversal may indicate:

- being stuck in the past;
- denying or revising childhood;
- blocked generosity;
- an internal or unconscious memory process;
- an excessive idealization of innocence;
- breaking free from family or past conditioning.

This is consistent with her broader reversal taxonomy: blocked, delayed, excessive, internalized, projected, refused, or overturned—not simply “bad.”

Again, an exact card-entry quotation was not verified from an authorized full-text edition. Her relevant primary books are *Tarot for Your Self* and *The Complete Book of Tarot Reversals* (Llewellyn, 2002). [Publisher record](https://www.llewellyn.com/product.php?ean=9781567182859)

# Esoteric and symbolic readings

## Alchemical

### Earliest traceable form

No alchemical meaning is documented on fifteenth-century Six of Cups cards.

Sola Busca scholarship explores possible Hermetic-alchemical programs at the pack level. That does not prove that every pip encodes a universally agreed laboratory operation.

### Crowley’s reading

Crowley supplies the clearest card-specific alchemical reading: solar heat acting on Water in Scorpio produces controlled putrefaction, which becomes the fertile basis of life.

Status: **esoteric attribution**, not historical explanation of the medieval card.

Spreaders: Crowley, Harris’s imagery, Thoth commentators, Thelemic tarot literature.

## Qabalistic

Six = Tiphareth or Beauty. Cups = Water, first Heh, and commonly Briah. Therefore:

**Tiphareth in Water = balanced, beautiful, solarized emotion.**

Book T is the first influential source to make this formula card-specific. Crowley and Case spread it.

## Astrological

Sun in Scorpio can appear paradoxical: solar clarity and vitality operate in a fixed-water sign associated with secrecy, sexuality, death, transformation, and regeneration.

Golden Dawn: commencement of pleasure and increase.

Crowley: fertile putrefaction and sexual fulfillment.

Modern astrologizing often adds warmth of memory or childhood illumination. That latter bridge is a modern synthesis of RWS imagery with Golden Dawn astrology, not Book T’s own explanation.

## Hermetic

The cup is the receptive vessel. Sixfold solar balance regulates the flow of Water. Lotus flowers mediate between source and vessel.

In Golden Dawn ritual symbolism, the form of the cups also refers to the magical implement of an Adeptus grade. This is Order-specific symbolism.

## Christian and Grail

Cups can naturally evoke chalices, Eucharistic vessels, baptism, the wedding at Cana, charity, and the Holy Grail.

- **Historical possibility:** European viewers could recognize a cup as a vessel with secular and sacred uses.
- **Tradition:** Cups came to represent clergy or ecclesiastical life in some suit-symbol theories.
- **Modern esoteric reading:** the suit is the Grail, and six vessels represent communion, grace, or recovered innocence.
- **Absence of evidence:** No fifteenth-century source was found identifying this particular card as the Grail, Eucharist, Cana, or six Christian sacraments.

Crowley’s “Eucharist” language comes through his I Ching and sacramental-sexual interpretation, not orthodox Christian exegesis.

## Jungian

Modern Jungian readings commonly treat the two children as:

- ego and inner child;
- adult consciousness meeting an earlier self;
- the return of a feeling-toned complex;
- integration rather than regression;
- nostalgia as projection;
- reconciliation with ancestry or family pattern.

Jung discussed tarot in seminars and referred to cards as psychologically suggestive images, but a fully developed Jung-authored doctrine of the Six of Cups has not been found. A recent study notes a seminar reference to *coupes 6*, but Jung’s surviving comment is brief and does not contain the now-standard “inner child” reading.

Thus “Jungian Six of Cups” is primarily a later application of analytical psychology, not a canonical interpretation by Jung.

- Arthur Edward Waite — supplied the interpretive program and published description.
- Jessie Burns Parke and Paul Foster Case — BOTA version.
- Frieda Harris and Aleister Crowley — Thoth “Pleasure.”
- Paul Marteau — Grimaud/Marseille numerological exegesis.
- Philippe Camoin, Alejandro Jodorowsky, Marianne Costa — reconstructed Marseille deck and modern decimal reading.
- Rachel Pollack and Mary K. Greer — major modern psychological and experiential interpreters.

## Literature and film

### Italo Calvino

*The Castle of Crossed Destinies* constructs narratives from tarot cards. Different editions use or evoke Visconti and Marseille imagery. Tarot operates as a combinatorial language.

**Caution:** No singular, universally stable Calvino episode can be assigned to the Six of Cups independently of edition, layout, and sequence. Claims that Calvino fixed its meaning as nostalgia require a page-specific edition citation.

### T. S. Eliot

*The Waste Land* contains Madame Sosostris and invented or adapted tarot references: the drowned Phoenician Sailor, Belladonna, the man with three staves, the Wheel, the one-eyed merchant, and the Hanged Man.

**Documented absence:** The Six of Cups is not named. Eliot is relevant to modern literary tarot reception, not specifically to this card.

### W. B. Yeats

Yeats was involved with the Golden Dawn and occult symbolism. He is relevant to the cultural milieu that produced Book T.

**Absence:** No verified Yeats work was located that makes the Six of Cups a named controlling image.

### Ingmar Bergman

Bergman’s films employ Death, chess, memory, childhood, and religious crisis.

**Absence:** No reliable evidence was found that a Bergman scene deliberately quotes the Six of Cups. Visual resemblance to childhood or an old courtyard is insufficient.

### Jodorowsky

Jodorowsky’s films are saturated with occult, alchemical, and tarot imagery, and he later became an influential Marseille reader.

**Documented:** His tarot books and Camoin collaboration are directly relevant.

**Unverified:** A particular film shot reproducing the Six of Cups was not securely traced.

## Paintings and buildings

No pre-1909 painting or building has been conclusively established as Smith’s direct model. The RWS architecture resembles a generalized late-medieval or Tudorized stage courtyard.

Many modern artworks, murals, tattoos, advertisements, and deck illustrations quote the RWS children, but no canonical building associated with the card emerged from the consulted museum or primary-text sources.

# Controversies and disputes

## Egyptian origin

Court de Gébelin wrote in 1781 that tarot preserved Egyptian wisdom. The accompanying Comte de Mellet essay developed the Book of Thoth and Hebrew-letter claims.

Status: **documented occult claim; historically unsupported**.

Evidence against it:

- surviving tarot cards and records emerge in fifteenth-century northern Italy;
- the early imagery belongs to European courtly, civic, and Christian visual culture;
- Gébelin wrote before hieroglyphic Egyptian had been deciphered;
- his proposed Egyptian etymologies do not cite or analyze hieroglyphic forms.

Dummett summarized the documentary correction by treating tarot first as a family of games. [Dummett, “Origins of Tarot”](https://www.nybooks.com/articles/1981/05/14/origins-of-tarot/)

## Romani or “Gypsy” transmission

Gébelin’s “Bohemians” and later nineteenth-century writers helped create the story that Romani travelers brought tarot from Egypt.

Status: **legend**.

Modern historical and linguistic scholarship locates Romani origins in South Asia, not Egypt. There is no documentary chain showing Romani introduction of tarot to Europe; the earliest tarot evidence is in Italian court society.

The historical term “Gypsy” is retained only when quoting or naming works such as Papus’s *Tarot des Bohémiens*.

## Cathar and Templar origins

Claims that the cards secretly preserve Cathar dualism, Templar doctrine, a Grail church, or heretical initiation are modern speculative traditions.

Problems include:

- no fifteenth-century document linking tarot manufacture with Cathars or Templars;
- chronological gaps after the suppression of those groups;
- ordinary court and civic sources adequately explain much of the early imagery;
- the Six of Cups begins as six cups, not an identifiable heretical tableau.

Status: **legend or disputed modern invention**, unless presented explicitly as symbolic meditation rather than history.

## Grail origins

The visual resemblance between a cup and the Grail makes later spiritual comparison understandable.

But resemblance is not genealogy. No early card title says “Grail,” and no maker’s explanation identifies the suit with Arthurian relics.

Status: **later Christian-esoteric tradition**.

## Sola Busca as the direct source of Smith’s Six

This is the item-specific controversy requiring the strongest correction.

What is supported:

- Smith’s deck postdates the arrival of Sola Busca photographs at the British Museum.
- Some RWS pips closely resemble particular Sola Busca compositions.
- Sola Busca is a precedent for fully elaborated numeral cards.

What is not established:

- a documentary statement by Smith naming Sola Busca;
- a close visual match between the two Sixes of Cups;
- evidence that the children and flower-gift were copied from Sola Busca.

Verdict: **deck-level influence plausible to probable; direct Six-to-Six derivation unproved and visually weak.**

## Were the RWS figures children, lovers, siblings, or the same person at two ages?

Waite says “Children,” plural. Smith provides no surviving caption explaining their relationship.

- boy and girl: tradition;
- siblings: modern inference;
- older child giving to younger child: visually plausible;
- adult and child: possible because of stylized scale;
- same self at two ages: Jungian modern invention;
- former lovers reunited: divinatory extrapolation.

The background person is often called a guard, servant, or adult leaving the children. None is verified by Waite.

## Does the flower contain a pentagram?

The white flowers are stylized and generally five-petaled. A pentagram symbolism is possible in an occult deck but not stated by Waite. Calling them literal pentagrams is interpretive.

## Nostalgia versus pleasure

Two historically distinct streams converge:

- Etteilla → Mathers → Waite: past, future, memory, childhood.
- Golden Dawn → Crowley: pleasure, Sun in Scorpio, Tiphareth of Water.

Modern RWS readers usually privilege nostalgia. Thoth readers privilege pleasure. Neither is the medieval “original meaning.”

## Misprints and restorations

- Rosenwald sheet numerals have suffered copying and retouching errors.
- “Conver 1760” is often applied to later impressions or modern redrawings.
- Golden Dawn decks by Wang and the Ciceros are reconstructions, not identical photographic witnesses.
- Thoth publication dates vary by whether one counts early monochrome, limited, or full commercial editions.
- Modern Visconti, Budapest, Rosenwald, and Wirth packs frequently fill missing cards; users may unknowingly describe a twentieth-century reconstruction as a fifteenth- or nineteenth-century original.

# Open questions

1. **Which surviving object is unambiguously the earliest Six of Cups?**  
   Cary–Yale/Visconti di Modrone is a strong candidate, but the exact dating and relative chronology of the earliest Milanese packs remain under refinement.

2. **What did the maker of the Sola Busca Six intend?**  
   Its elaborate pip program is real, but a card-specific contemporary commentary has not survived.

3. **Did Smith personally inspect the Sola Busca photographs?**  
   Access and opportunity are documented; a direct statement or visitor record identifying her study of them has not surfaced.

4. **What was Smith’s precise source for the children and courtyard?**  
   Waite’s Past/Childhood meaning explains the subject, but no source painting or building has been demonstrated.

5. **Are the children siblings, friends, allegorical selves, or giver and recipient?**  
   Waite identifies only children. Further specificity is interpretive.

6. **When exactly was Book T’s Six written in its surviving form?**  
   Its doctrine belongs to the early Golden Dawn period, but surviving manuscript copies and later publication layers complicate line-by-line dating.

7. **What is the precise source of Papus’s harsh “obstacles/widowhood/separation” reading?**  
   It appears in Papus-associated tables, but editions and later compilations need a dedicated bibliographical collation.

8. **Can Jung’s brief *coupes 6* comment be reconstructed from the original seminar record?**  
   The modern secondary report confirms a reference but not the developed inner-child doctrine often attributed to him.

9. **How much of modern nostalgia derives from picture rather than text?**  
   Textually the Past meaning begins with Etteilla. Smith’s image probably caused “childhood nostalgia” to eclipse Etteilla’s more neutral temporal boundary.

10. **Did early players attach informal associations to six cups?**  
    They may have, but undocumented private associations cannot be reconstructed as stable historical meanings.

# Conclusions

The Six of Cups began as a numeral game card: six ornate cups in a fifteenth-century Italian suit. Its earliest surviving forms are luxurious but non-narrative. Marseille printers retained this formal arrangement and filled its center with vegetal ornament.

The first recoverable individual divinatory identity is Etteilla’s eighteenth-century **Past**, reversed **Future**. Mathers transmitted it. Waite and Pamela Colman Smith made it visible as children in an old garden, turning abstract pastness into memory and childhood.

A second lineage arose inside the Golden Dawn. Book T called the card **The Lord of Pleasure**, assigned it Sun in Scorpio and Tiphareth of Water, and pictured six lotus fountains pouring into cups. Crowley and Harris preserved this structure in the Thoth **Pleasure**, transforming it into an account of solarized water, fertile putrefaction, natural harmony, and sexual-sacramental fulfillment.

Thus the card has no single continuous ancient meaning. It contains at least three historically distinct layers:

1. Renaissance game rank and ornamental pip;
2. Etteilla–Mathers–Waite past, memory, and childhood;
3. Golden Dawn–Crowley Tiphareth, Sun in Scorpio, and Pleasure.

Grail, alchemical, Christian, and Jungian readings can be meaningful symbolic applications. They become misleading only when represented as documented fifteenth-century origins.
