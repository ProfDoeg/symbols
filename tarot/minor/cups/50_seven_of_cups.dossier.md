# Seven of Cups: Research Dossier

## Method and evidentiary labels

This dossier distinguishes six kinds of statement:

- **Documented fact** — supported by a surviving card, archival record, contemporary text, or bibliographic record.
- **Scholarly reconstruction** — a conclusion drawn from surviving objects and documents but not stated by a contemporary witness.
- **Tradition** — a reading repeatedly transmitted within tarot practice.
- **Esoteric attribution** — an occult system’s deliberately assigned correspondence.
- **Disputed claim** — proposed but contested or weakly evidenced.
- **Legend / modern invention** — a retrospective story or symbolism for which no historical transmission has been demonstrated.

A central limitation should be stated at once: almost none of the famous medieval or Renaissance references to *trionfi* name an individual pip card. The Seven of Cups is documented primarily by surviving cards, not by fifteenth-century descriptions of its meaning.

### What does—and does not—apply from trump nomenclature

**Documented fact.** Names such as *Papess*, *Pope*, *Juno*, *Jupiter*, *Maison Dieu*, and the unnumbered Fool belong to the trump sequence, not to the Seven of Cups. Likewise, the three historical Italian trump orders and the Justice/Strength interchange do not change this card’s identity.

Their relevance is contextual:

- In the Besançon/Swiss lineage, the Papess and Pope were replaced by Juno and Jupiter because of regional religious sensitivities.
- *La Maison Dieu* is the conventional French title of trump XVI in the Marseille family.
- The Fool was historically unnumbered and had an excusing or special role in play.
- None of these produced a known regional renaming of the Seven of Cups.

The card remained, in substance, “the seven [cards/signs] of cups.”

# Iconography deck by deck

## Before tarot: the cup as a suit sign

### Mamluk and early Italian cards

**Scholarly reconstruction.** European Latin-suited cards belong to a family ultimately related to Islamic playing cards. Surviving Mamluk cards include a suit of cups, alongside coins, swords, and polo sticks. Italian cardmakers retained cups and swords, converted polo sticks into batons, and developed European court figures.

This history explains the suit emblem. It does not demonstrate that the Seven of Cups carried Eucharistic, Grail, elemental, or psychological meaning in medieval play.

---

## Marziano da Tortona’s pack, approximately 1414–1425

**Documented fact.** Marziano described a special game devised for Filippo Maria Visconti, with sixteen classical gods painted by Michelino da Besozzo and four suits represented by birds. Jacopo Antonio Marcello later called it a kind of *ludus triumphorum*.

**Finding:** it contained no documented suit of Cups and therefore no reconstructable Seven of Cups. It is a precursor to the tarot-trump principle, not an occurrence of this card.

---

## Cary-Yale / Visconti di Modrone, Milan, approximately 1440s

**Documented fact with disputed dating.** Sixty-seven cards survive at Yale. The pack had unusual additional female court ranks. Dating proposals range from the early 1440s to the 1460s; the earlier dating is widely argued from Visconti heraldry and coin imagery but is not universally accepted.

The Seven of Cups survives. It is a non-narrative pip:

- seven ornamental goblets;
- repeated suit emblems rather than seven different objects;
- tooled precious-metal ground;
- decorative plant or scroll filling;
- no human observer, cloud, treasure, snake, dragon, castle, wreath, or veiled figure.

**Meaning at the time:** no card-specific symbolic gloss survives. Claims that it already meant choice, fantasy, abundance, the seven deadly sins, or mystical vision are **modern projections**.

Because the dating of the Cary-Yale pack is contested, calling this unconditionally “the first Seven of Cups” would overstate the evidence.

---

## Brera-Brambilla, Milan, approximately 1442–1451

**Documented fact.** This deck, associated with Filippo Maria Visconti and attributed to Bonifacio Bembo or his workshop, preserves most of its numeral cards. Museum cataloguing places the group in the mid-fifteenth century.

Its cup pips use repeated, elaborately painted cup emblems on silvered grounds. The Seven follows the counting-card principle rather than presenting a narrative.

**Interpretation:** the sumptuous materials signify a courtly luxury object. A specific allegory of “seven cups” is not documented.

---

## Visconti-Sforza / Pierpont Morgan–Bergamo, Milan, approximately 1450–1480

**Documented fact.** Seventy-four of a presumed seventy-eight cards survive, divided between the Morgan Library, Accademia Carrara, and the Colleoni collection. The Seven of Cups is in the Bergamo group, not among the Morgan’s thirty-five cards.

It shows:

- seven stemmed chalices;
- a regular, balanced field;
- delicate vine or floral ornament;
- no continuous episode.

The Morgan’s own catalogue stresses that the deck was made for play and acquired occult associations only centuries later.

**Scholarly reconstruction.** The balanced emblem arrangement probably served legibility and decoration. Statements that it depicts Eucharistic plenty or courtly love are plausible contextual associations, but neither is documented as the card’s intended meaning.

---

## Ferrarese and Este cards, later fifteenth century

Scattered Este-associated cards survive, but no securely documented, complete Ferrarese sequence allows a continuous Seven-of-Cups genealogy.

**Open evidentiary point:** it is reasonable to infer that Ferrarese tarot packs contained a seven in the cup suit once the 78-card form stabilized, but surviving ledgers name suits and packs, not this individual card’s design or meaning.

---

## Mantegna “Tarocchi,” approximately 1460s

**Documented fact.** The so-called Mantegna Tarocchi are two series of fifty instructive engravings representing social estates, liberal arts, virtues, cosmic principles, and celestial spheres.

**Finding:** they are not an ordinary tarot pack and contain no suit of Cups or Seven of Cups. Their relevance is limited to Renaissance classificatory imagery and later occult attempts to place encyclopedic systems behind tarot.

---

## Sola Busca, 1491; later painted copy

**Documented fact.** The Sola Busca is the earliest substantially complete surviving tarot pack whose numeral cards are scenic or object-rich rather than simple repeated emblems. The complete painted deck is in the Pinacoteca di Brera; related uncoloured impressions survive elsewhere. The date 1491 is attached to the engraved series, although the surviving painted state is later.

The Seven of Cups is an elaborate Renaissance image involving seven vessels and active, enigmatic imagery rather than a Marseille-style floral count. Modern Brera exhibition material places it among cards discussed as alchemical.

### Was it the source of Pamela Colman Smith’s Seven of Cups?

**Disputed and unverified.**

There is good circumstantial evidence that Smith knew the Sola Busca:

- photographs of the complete deck reached the British Museum in 1907;
- Smith’s deck followed in 1909;
- several RWS cards have striking compositional parallels to Sola Busca cards, notably the Three of Swords and certain burden/weapon scenes.

But the requested specific claim—“the Sola Busca Seven of Cups was the source of Smith’s scene”—could not be traced to a comparative primary document, statement by Smith, or persuasive image-to-image demonstration.

The Sola Busca card does **not** present Smith’s silhouetted spectator confronting seven cups on a cloud, each containing a discrete apparition. The defensible conclusion is:

- **Scholarly reconstruction:** Sola Busca probably helped demonstrate that numeral cards could be pictorial.
- **Disputed claim:** particular Sola Busca compositions influenced particular RWS cards.
- **Untraced claim:** its Seven of Cups directly supplied Smith’s Seven of Cups.

---

## Rosenwald and Budapest sheets, late fifteenth or early sixteenth century

These woodcut sheets are important witnesses to early popular tarot production and trump iconography.

**Finding:** they do not provide a securely reconstructable, card-specific Seven-of-Cups symbolic program. Where numeral suit cards are absent or fragmentary, a Seven should not be silently reconstructed from later Marseille packs.

---

## Minchiate, Florence, sixteenth–nineteenth centuries

Minchiate expanded the trump series to ninety-seven cards but retained four Latin suits.

The Seven of Cups is a conventional pip: repeated cups organized decoratively, without a RWS-like scene.

**Meaning:** in surviving game practice it is a suit card. The expanded astrological and virtue trumps do not turn the cup pips into separately illustrated occult keys.

---

## Tarocchino Bolognese, sixteenth century onward

The Bolognese pack was reduced to sixty-two cards, but retained the 6–10 pips in each suit. The Seven of Cups therefore survived the reduction.

Modern Bolognese cards depict seven double-ended, stylized cup emblems. They can resemble wrapped sweets or “Christmas crackers” to modern eyes, but they are conventionalized cups.

No separate divinatory scene is traditional to the pattern.

---

## Sicilian Tarocchi, seventeenth century onward

The Sicilian pack retains the Seven of Cups among numeral cards 5–10. Modern examples bear an index such as **7.C**.

The pips rank in straightforward descending numerical order beneath the courts in Sicilian play, unlike the reversed rank historically found for round suits in many other tarot games.

---

## Jean Noblet, Paris, approximately 1650

**Documented fact.** Noblet is the earliest known surviving pack conforming substantially to what later scholarship conventionally calls Tarot de Marseille.

The Seven of Cups is non-scenic:

- three cups above;
- one central cup;
- three below;
- curling vegetal fillers joining or occupying the intervals.

It has no cloud and no different contents in the vessels.

---

## Jean Dodal, Lyon, approximately 1701–1715

Dodal preserves the same broad 3–1–3 arrangement but redraws the cup profiles and vegetation in Lyonnais woodcut style. The ornament organizes the otherwise empty field.

Any “growth of emotions” reading of these vines is a later divinatory interpretation, not a printer’s surviving explanation.

---

## Nicolas Conver, Marseille, 1760/1761

Conver’s Seven became particularly influential through later Marseille reproductions:

- seven repeated chalices;
- 3–1–3 hierarchy;
- long, symmetrical blue-red-yellow floral scrollwork;
- central cup isolated within the foliage;
- no figures.

The French heritage catalogue dates Conver’s blocks to 1760 and records continued production by his successors before Camoin inherited the business.

---

## Besançon pattern, eighteenth–nineteenth centuries

Besançon packs altered selected religious trumps—most famously substituting Juno and Jupiter—but retained conventional Latin-suited numeral cards.

**Finding:** no standard Besançon renaming or special illustration of the Seven of Cups has been found.

---

## Etteilla’s divinatory tarot, Paris, 1788–1791

### Card identity and numbering

In the principal Grand Etteilla sequence, the Seven of Cups is card **43**. Later Etteilla editions vary in numbering and spelling, so unqualified claims that it is always 43 are unsafe.

The card usually presents seven cups on a coloured field, with printed divinatory headings rather than a narrative illustration. In one documented edition:

- upright heading: **LA PENSÉE**;
- reversed heading: **PROJETS**;
- side identification: **Le Sept de Coupe** / Seven of Hearts correspondence.

A surviving printed manual states:

> “LA PENSÉE — Le Sept de Coupe … PROJETS — Renversé.”

**Documented significance:** Etteilla turned a traditional pip into an explicitly labelled divinatory object. This is earlier than the Golden Dawn’s “Illusionary Success” and earlier than Smith’s vision scene.

---

## Oswald Wirth, 1889

Wirth’s original twenty-two-card tarot was a trump-only work made with Stanislas de Guaita. It has no Seven of Cups.

Later decks sold under Wirth’s name sometimes add Marseille-derived minors. Those additions must not be attributed automatically to Wirth’s 1889 designs.

---

## Golden Dawn *Book T*, approximately 1890s

The Golden Dawn’s manuscript scheme describes a formal emblematic card:

- a hand;
- seven cups;
- lotus flowers above the cups;
- an arrangement governed by the number seven;
- Venus in Scorpio;
- title **Lord of Illusionary Success**.

The cups are commonly reconstructed as a geometric pattern with one larger or lower central vessel. The text’s divinatory vocabulary includes:

> “Lying, deceit, promises unfulfilled, illusion, deception. Error, slight success, but not enough energy to retain it.”

The Golden Dawn did not publish one authoritative commercial deck. Consequently:

- **Robert Wang’s Golden Dawn Tarot** is a twentieth-century reconstruction made with Israel Regardie.
- **Chic and Sandra Tabatha Cicero’s Magical Tarot of the Golden Dawn** is another reconstruction based upon order manuscripts.
- Differences in colour, vessel shape, hand placement, and lotuses belong to the reconstructors, not necessarily to a single lost “original Golden Dawn card.”

---

## Rider–Waite–Smith, London, 1909

Pamela Colman Smith’s image is the decisive transformation in the card’s modern visual history.

A dark, back-turned human figure confronts seven chalices floating upon a cloud. The vessels contain:

1. a human head;
2. a veiled, luminous figure;
3. a snake;
4. a castle or tower;
5. jewels or treasure;
6. a laurel wreath, with a dark skull-like form on the cup;
7. a dragon.

Waite described it in *The Pictorial Key to the Tarot*:

> “Strange chalices of vision, but the images are more especially those of the fantastic spirit.”

He continued:

> “Fairy favours, images of reflection, sentiment, imagination, things seen in the glass of contemplation; some attainment in these degrees, but nothing permanent or substantial is suggested.”

Reversed:

> “Desire, will, determination, project.”

### Sources of the scene

- **Documented fact:** Smith drew the card; Waite supplied or approved the interpretive program.
- **Strong reconstruction:** the Golden Dawn title and meanings explain the emphasis on illusion and unstable success.
- **Possible influence:** theatrical tableau, fairy imagery, crystal or cup scrying, emblematic catalogues of temptations.
- **Unproven modern tradition:** each object represents one of the seven deadly sins.
- **Unproven identification:** the head is specifically Medusa.
- **Unproven identification:** the veiled form is “the higher self.”
- **Unproven identification:** the seven objects encode a fixed hierarchy of choices.
- **Untraced:** direct borrowing from the Sola Busca Seven of Cups.

The laurel-and-skull reading is visually plausible, but Waite did not enumerate the seven objects in *Pictorial Key*.

---

## Paul Foster Case and BOTA, 1920s onward

Case’s BOTA deck, drawn by Jessie Burns Parke, deliberately returned the numeral cards to emblematic pip arrangements. The Seven of Cups is seven repeated cups rather than Smith’s cloud vision.

This separates two things Case considered distinct:

- the major trumps as concentrated occult “keys”;
- the minors as suit-and-number combinations.

BOTA’s black-and-white cards were intended to be coloured by students. Claims that Case supplied a universally published upright/reversed sentence for every BOTA pip require edition-specific citation; much of BOTA’s detailed teaching circulated through lessons rather than trade books.

---

## Thoth, painted 1938–1943; first issued in deck form after Crowley’s death

Lady Frieda Harris painted the deck under Aleister Crowley’s direction. The Seven is titled **DEBAUCH**.

Crowley’s description is unusually exact:

> “This card refers to the Seven, Netzach, in the suit of Water.”

> “The Lotuses have become poisonous, looking like tiger-lilies; and, instead of water, green slime issues from them and overflows, making the Sea a malarious morass.”

> “The cups are iridescent.”

> “They are arranged as two descending triangles interlaced above the lowest cup, which is very much larger than the rest.”

Harris’s image replaces Smith’s spectator and discrete visions with a diseased hydraulic system:

- seven cup forms;
- poisonous flowers;
- green, viscous discharge;
- interlaced triangular geometry;
- a swollen lower cup;
- murky aquatic ground;
- Venus/Scorpio colouring and symbolism.

Crowley calls it:

> “almost the ‘evil and averse’ image of the Six”

and warns that a sacrament can be “profaned and prostituted.”

**Meaning of the change:** Smith dramatizes fantasy offered to consciousness; Harris and Crowley portray desire’s internal corruption as a self-polluting system.

The paintings were completed in the early 1940s. A limited black-and-white presentation appeared in connection with *The Book of Thoth*; commercial colour deck publication followed posthumously, with 1969 commonly cited for an early deck issue and wider editions following in the 1970s. Edition histories vary, so “published in 1969” should not be confused with the date of painting.

---

## Grimaud/Paul Marteau, 1930 and later

Marteau’s *Ancien Tarot de Marseille*, published by Grimaud, regularized the Conver-derived pattern and colour palette. Its Seven remains a floral 3–1–3 pip.

Marteau’s numerological and colour readings are twentieth-century overlays on an older game-card design.

---

## Camoin–Jodorowsky, 1997

Alejandro Jodorowsky and Philippe Camoin presented their deck as a restoration of an original, coherent Marseille code assembled from historical fragments.

The Seven remains seven cups within organic ornament.

- **Documented fact:** it is a modern redrawing using historical models.
- **Esoteric claim:** colours, tiny details, and cross-card continuities recover a primordial symbolic structure.
- **Disputed claim:** one recoverable “original Tarot de Marseille” lies behind the surviving variants. Historians generally treat the Marseille pattern as an evolving family rather than a single concealed canonical deck.

Jodorowsky and Marianne Costa read Cups as the emotional/relational pole and seven as a high degree of receptive action. This is their modern psycho-spiritual system, not a documented Conver-era interpretation.

---

## Notable modern redesign tendencies

Modern decks usually choose one of four approaches:

1. **RWS repetition:** a person confronts seven tempting or confusing options.
2. **Psychological modernization:** screens, careers, lovers, consumer products, doors, or social identities replace Smith’s apparitions.
3. **Marseille revival:** seven plain vessels and plant growth, read through number and visual rhythm.
4. **Thoth-derived pathology:** intoxication, addiction, sensory excess, rot, poison, or emotional saturation.

“Too many choices,” “analysis paralysis,” and “wishful thinking” are primarily modern expansions of Smith and Waite. Addiction and corruption are especially strengthened by Crowley’s *Debauch*.

# The game

## Original function

**Documented fact.** Tarot originated as a family of trick-taking games. A standard early pack comprised four fourteen-card suits, twenty-one trumps, and the Fool.

The Seven of Cups:

- is not a trump;
- cannot beat a trump;
- follows the cup suit when cups are led, where the rules require following suit;
- wins only according to its rank within Cups;
- normally has no special bonus;
- is generally a low-value card or *scartino*.

## Historical rank

In the traditional Italian system, the “round” suits—Cups and Coins—reverse the order of their numeral cards:

**King, Queen, Knight, Jack, Ace, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10.**

Thus the Seven is below the Six and above the Eight. Numerically “larger” does not mean stronger.

Swords and Batons traditionally run the opposite way below the courts:

**King, Queen, Knight, Jack, 10, 9 … Ace.**

This asymmetric order is documented in historical tarot play and survives in games including Bolognese Ottocento.

### Variants

- **Bolognese Tarocchino:** Seven of Cups survives; Ace, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10 run downward in strength.
- **Sicilian Tarocchi:** courts followed by 10, 9, 8, 7, 6, 5; the Seven ranks conventionally between 8 and 6.
- **Modern French Tarot:** uses French suits; the corresponding red-suit seven is simply 7 and follows modern numerical ranking.
- **Central European Tarock:** many shortened packs omit lower red-suit cards. A Seven may be absent entirely.
- **Minchiate:** the Seven is an ordinary low counter in Cups.

## Point value

In many Italian scoring systems, a numeral card contributes only the residual value used in grouped card-counting—often effectively one nominal point before deductions, or one-half in an equivalent individual-card calculation.

It has none of the special status given to kings, high trumps, the Bagatto/Pagat, World, or Fool.

## Trump-order disputes

The northern Italian tarot families developed three principal regional trump orders conventionally called A, B, and C. Justice, Fortitude, Love, the virtues, and celestial cards could occupy different relative positions.

**Finding:** these disputes have no direct bearing on Seven of Cups rank. They are included only because the card participated in the same packs.

The Fool’s special role as an excuse, wild card, or protected card likewise does not alter the Seven’s suit status.

# Chronology of attestation

## Approximately 1414–1425 — Marziano da Tortona

**Documented precursor, not this card.** Marziano’s tract describes sixteen deity trumps and bird suits. No Seven of Cups is named.

## 1440 — Anghiari/Florentine record

A record associated with Giusto Giusti mentions *naibi a trionfi* made for or given to Sigismondo Pandolfo Malatesta.

**Finding:** no card list survives.

## 10 February 1442 — Ferrara ledger

Sagramoro was paid for painting:

> “le chope e le spade e li dinari e li bastoni e tute le fegure de 4 para de chartexele da trionffy”

That is, the cups, swords, coins, batons, and all the figures of four packs of triumph cards.

This is the earliest explicit documentary attestation found here of **cups inside named *trionfi* packs**. It does not enumerate the Seven.

## 28 July 1442 — Ferrara ledger

A cheap pack of *carte da trionfi* was supplied for young Ercole and Sigismondo d’Este.

Again, no individual cards are listed.

## 1440s–1450s — Milanese surviving cards

Cary-Yale, Brambilla, and Visconti-Sforza objects provide the first physical evidence for seven-cup pips.

## Approximately 1450–1500 — *Sermo perutilis de ludo cum aliis*

The Dominican sermon condemns games and lists the triumphs in moralizing terms.

**Finding:** it concerns the trump sequence, not a divinatory meaning for the Seven of Cups. It is evidence against claims that every individual card already carried a recorded occult gloss.

## 1491 — Sola Busca

Earliest surviving complete scenic treatment of the numeral suits, including Seven of Cups.

## Early sixteenth century — *tarocchi*

The word *tarocchi* replaces or supplements *trionfi*. The etymology remains uncertain.

## Folengo and the approximately 1500 tarot poems

Poetic and literary uses demonstrate that cards could be used as social, poetic, or characterizing emblems.

**Finding:** no securely traced early poem was found that assigns the modern illusion/choice meaning specifically to the Seven of Cups.

## Seventeenth century — Marseille lineage and early cartomantic evidence

Noblet fixes a durable French pip arrangement. Evidence from Bologna and other sources suggests pre-Gébelin card divination existed in limited forms, but no continuous Seven-of-Cups doctrine leading to Waite is demonstrable.

## 1781 — Court de Gébelin and Comte de Mellet

They propose Egyptian and Hebrew frameworks for tarot. Their discussions concentrate on the trumps and overall pack.

**Finding:** neither supplies the Golden Dawn’s Venus-in-Scorpio doctrine for the Seven of Cups.

## 1780s–1790s — Etteilla

The Seven becomes **La Pensée / Projets**, a documented printed divinatory card.

## 1888 — Mathers

Mathers publishes English meanings broadly related to Etteilla: thought, idea, design, resolution, and movement.

## 1890s — Golden Dawn

The decisive occult synthesis appears: Netzach, Water/Briah, Venus in Scorpio, “Illusionary Success.”

## 1909–1910 — Smith and Waite

The cloud-of-visions image and Waite’s “fantastic spirit” interpretation are published.

## 1938–1943 — Harris and Crowley

The card becomes *Debauch*, depicting poisonous desire and emotional putrefaction.

## Late twentieth century onward

Psychological, Jungian, choice-based, addiction, manifestation, and “shadow work” readings proliferate.

## Heh and the Cups suit

The brief requested “Heh.” This requires qualification.

In Golden Dawn tetragrammatic symbolism, the four suits and courts can be mapped to the letters of **YHVH**, but sources and contexts distinguish first Heh from final Heh. Cups are commonly assigned to **Heh**, the receptive/formative Water or Mother principle, while Pentacles correspond to final Heh.

**Esoteric attribution:** this does not make Heh the individual Hebrew letter of the Seven of Cups. Golden Dawn pip cards do not each receive one of the twenty-two Hebrew letters in the way the trumps do.

## Netzach and Sephirah 7

Netzach means “victory” or “endurance” and is placed seventh on the Hermetic Tree of Life. In Golden Dawn-derived tarot:

- all sevens correspond to Netzach;
- Cups place that Sephirah in Briah/Water;
- Venus, traditionally linked with Netzach, is intensified;
- Venus in Scorpio is treated as emotionally powerful but debilitated or corrupted.

This produces the paradox “victory” that proves illusory: desire appears to obtain its object but lacks balance or endurance.

This is a nineteenth-century Hermetic synthesis, not a fifteenth-century Jewish or tarot doctrine.

## Tzaddi/Heh swap

Crowley’s famous “Tzaddi is not the Star” revision exchanges the Hebrew-letter/zodiacal treatment of the Emperor and Star.

**Finding:** it concerns trumps IV and XVII. It does not alter the Seven of Cups, which remains Venus in Scorpio and Netzach of Water.

# Divinatory meanings by author

## Etteilla

Principal Grand Etteilla tradition:

> “LA PENSÉE”

Reversed:

> “PROJETS”

English gloss: thought, conception, reflection; reversed, projects or plans.

Some later manuals expand this into ideas about a woman, marriage plans, pleasant projects, or mental deliberation. Those expansions must be attributed to the later manual, not automatically to Etteilla himself.

## S. L. MacGregor Mathers, 1888

The transmitted wording is approximately:

- upright: **“Fair child; idea, design, resolve, movement.”**
- related tables and later summaries: thought, sentiment, reflection, project;
- reversed: plan, design, resolution, determination.

Edition typography and table alignment should be checked when quoting individual semicolon-separated terms: digitized reproductions can displace upright and reversed columns. The conceptual dependence on Etteilla is clear.

## Golden Dawn *Book T*

> “Lying, deceit, promises unfulfilled, illusion, deception. Error, slight success, but not enough energy to retain it.”

The formal title is **Lord of Illusionary Success**. Modern editions sometimes silently “correct” this to *Illusory Success*. *Illusionary* is the established order-title wording.

## Arthur Edward Waite, 1910

Upright:

> “Fairy favours, images of reflection, sentiment, imagination, things seen in the glass of contemplation; some attainment in these degrees, but nothing permanent or substantial is suggested.”

Reversed:

> “Desire, will, determination, project.”

Waite’s reversed reading preserves the Etteilla/Mathers shift from thought into resolved plan.

## Aleister Crowley, 1944

Crowley does not organize *The Book of Thoth* into ordinary upright/reversed fortune-telling definitions. His card title and prose give:

- debauchery;
- intoxication or emotional corruption;
- external splendour masking internal decay;
- profanation of a sacrament;
- loss of balance;
- poisoned pleasure.

Therefore a separate “Crowley reversed meaning” would be a later deck-reader’s invention, not Crowley’s own stated system.

## Paul Foster Case

Case’s accessible writings and deck preserve the suit-number approach rather than Smith’s seven apparitions. Publicly traceable BOTA material supports Cups/Water and the Golden Dawn-derived Qabalistic architecture.

**Absence finding:** I did not locate a reliable open primary text giving a concise, card-specific Case upright-and-reversed formula comparable to Waite’s. Statements online presenting one without lesson or page citation should be treated cautiously.

## Rachel Pollack

In *Seventy-Eight Degrees of Wisdom*, Pollack develops the RWS card as fantasy, possibility, emotional imagination, and difficulty converting visions into concrete choice. Her approach is psychological and narrative rather than strictly Golden Dawn astrological.

**Copyright limitation:** a reliable open edition was not found from which to reproduce a passage verbatim. Pollack should be consulted in the current Weiser/Thorsons editions in the Minor Arcana discussion of Cups.

## Mary K. Greer

Greer’s reversal method permits several operations rather than a single opposite meaning:

- blocked or resisted imagination;
- internalized visions;
- clearing confusion;
- committing to one option;
- intensified fantasy, depending upon context and reversal method.

This is a methodological reading, not an assertion that Waite secretly meant “clarity” whenever the card was reversed.

**Absence finding:** no authorized open text was located from which to quote Greer’s card entry verbatim. Relevant editions include *Tarot for Your Self* and *The Complete Book of Tarot Reversals*.

# Esoteric and symbolic readings

## Alchemical

The Sola Busca has been interpreted as an alchemical deck, and Brera exhibition material explicitly grouped its Cups and Coins with alchemical manuscripts.

For its Seven of Cups:

- **Esoteric/scholarly proposal:** vessels participate in operations of containment, heating, mixing, or transformation.
- **Disputed:** the entire card encodes one uniquely identifiable recipe or stage.
- **Unsupported leap:** Smith copied that exact alchemical operation into her card.

Crowley’s Thoth Seven is more explicitly alchemical in metaphor: pure water becomes slime; beauty becomes corruption; the receptive vessel cannot redeem an imbalanced influx.

## Kabbalistic

First securely systematic tarot application: nineteenth-century French occultism, elaborated decisively by the Golden Dawn.

The card is **Netzach in Briah**. Netzach/Venus governs desire, attraction, pleasure, imagination, and aesthetic force. In Water, and under Venus in Scorpio, the force becomes emotionally fertile but unstable.

This is Hermetic Qabalah, not an attested Jewish interpretation of a Renaissance card.

## Astrological

The Golden Dawn divides the zodiac into thirty-six ten-degree faces and distributes the 2–10 pips among them. The Seven of Cups receives:

- Scorpio, third decan;
- 20°–30° Scorpio;
- Venus as planetary ruler.

The exact civil-calendar dates vary with astrological convention and epoch; modern lists commonly approximate mid-to-late November. Book T’s attribution is zodiacal degree, not a permanently fixed Gregorian date.

Crowley retained it unchanged.

## Hermetic

The card’s Hermetic lesson is imbalance below the Middle Pillar. Desire has multiplied attractive forms but lost contact with unity or Kether. The many cups promise reception but cannot preserve what descends into them.

This reading appears explicitly in Crowley’s prose and implicitly in Golden Dawn architecture.

## Christian and Grail readings

The cup readily evokes:

- Eucharistic chalice;
- Christ’s cup at the Last Supper;
- the cup of blessing or suffering;
- medieval and modern Holy Grail literature.

But three levels must be separated:

1. **Historical context:** Renaissance viewers knew liturgical chalices.
2. **Possible association:** ornamental cup emblems could resonate with sacred vessels.
3. **Modern tradition:** the whole Cups suit is “the Grail suit,” and its Seven represents false grails among which the true Grail must be chosen.

The third formulation is primarily a twentieth-century Arthurian/Celtic tarot development. No fifteenth-century rulebook or maker’s statement calls the Seven of Cups a Grail card.

The Indiana Jones “choose the true Grail among false cups” parallel is a particularly clear **modern analogy**, not a historical source.

## Seven deadly sins

Smith’s seven objects are often assigned to pride, greed, lust, envy, gluttony, wrath, and sloth.

**Modern invention/disputed identification.** No such key appears in Waite’s published description, and commentators disagree over which object represents which sin. The count of seven invited the association after the fact.

## Jungian

A modern Jungian reader may treat the cups as autonomous images arising from the unconscious and the silhouetted figure as ego-consciousness confronting projections:

- treasure: material desire;
- castle: power or security fantasy;
- head: idealized person or persona;
- dragon/snake: instinct and shadow;
- wreath: achievement and inflation;
- veiled form: numinous Self-image.

This is a productive modern interpretation but not one written by Jung, Waite, or Smith. Claims that Smith intentionally illustrated Jungian archetypes are chronologically impossible in their mature post-Jungian tarot sense.

## Contemporary “manifestation” reading

Some modern readers interpret the card as an abundance of possible realities that can be manifested by focused intention.

This reverses Book T and Crowley’s warning: where they see promises that cannot be retained, manifestation literature sees a menu of futures. It is a late twentieth- or twenty-first-century New Age adaptation.

# Controversies and disputes

## Egyptian origin

Court de Gébelin asserted in 1781 that tarot preserved an ancient Egyptian book. He wrote before Egyptian hieroglyphs had been deciphered and furnished no chain of manuscripts or artifacts connecting the Italian game to pharaonic Egypt.

- **Legend as historical genealogy:** tarot is the Book of Thoth.
- **Documented historical fact:** Gébelin published the claim.
- **Historical correction:** fifteenth-century northern Italian documents and cards precede any evidence for an Egyptian occult tarot.

Waite himself eventually wrote:

> “There is no particle of evidence for the Egyptian origin of Tarot cards.”

The possible transmission of ordinary playing-card forms through Mamluk Egypt is not evidence that tarot symbolism encodes the Book of Thoth.

## Romani or “Gypsy” origin

Gébelin’s milieu associated Romani people with Egypt; nineteenth-century writers including Jean-Alexandre Vaillant helped popularize the story that Romani carriers transmitted tarot’s secret wisdom.

- **Documented fact:** Romani diviners later used cards.
- **Legend:** Romani people created tarot or carried it unchanged from ancient Egypt.
- **Evidence:** European tarot is documented in Italian courts before the claimed occult transmission, while linguistic and historical evidence places Romani origins in India, not ancient Egypt.

The older word “Gypsy” is retained here only when describing historical claims.

## Cathar claim

Modern esoteric writers sometimes interpret the trumps as concealed Cathar theology surviving persecution.

**Finding for this card:** no medieval Cathar text, inquisitorial record, cardmaker’s statement, or early Seven-of-Cups gloss connects this pip to Cathar doctrine. The theory is a modern retrospective system.

## Templar and Grail claims

The Cups suit has been linked to the Holy Grail and thence to Templars, secret bloodlines, or hidden relic traditions.

- **Tradition:** cup symbolism readily supports Grail meditation.
- **Legend:** the Seven of Cups encodes seven Grails or a Templar initiation test.
- **Evidence:** no documentary bridge from the Templars to fifteenth-century tarot manufacture has been found.

## Alchemical Sola Busca claims

Unlike the Egyptian and Templar myths, an alchemical reading of the Sola Busca has substantial iconographic context and is taken seriously in museum interpretation.

What remains disputed is specificity: identifying a vessel or operation as alchemical does not automatically prove a complete encrypted doctrine or a direct line to Waite and Smith.

## “Sola Busca directly inspired every RWS minor”

This is an overstatement.

The British Museum photographs establish opportunity; selected cards establish plausible borrowing. They do not prove that all fifty-six RWS minors, or the Seven of Cups in particular, derive compositionally from Sola Busca.

## “The seven objects are the seven deadly sins”

A popular modern decoding, but absent from Waite’s published entry. Competing lists cannot agree on the mapping. It should be labelled tradition or modern invention.

## “The veiled figure is the Holy Grail/higher self”

Visually suggestive, but Waite does not identify it. Smith left no known explanatory key. It is a later interpretation.

## “The face is Medusa”

Possible resemblance, no documentary confirmation. It may be a generic head, mask, beloved, glamour, or projected identity.

## “Illusionary” is a misprint

The established Golden Dawn title is **Lord of Illusionary Success**. Modern editors sometimes prefer grammatically smoother *Illusory Success*.

Without an earlier manuscript reading showing correction from one form to the other, it is safer to treat *Illusionary* as the received technical title, not a proven accidental misprint.

## Thoth “Debauch” as Crowley’s rejection of Waite

Crowley’s treatment is sharper than Waite’s, but both descend from Golden Dawn Venus-in-Scorpio doctrine. *Debauch* is not simply an arbitrary attack on Waite; it is Crowley’s intensified interpretation of the shared correspondence.

## Dummett and the historical correction

Michael Dummett’s central correction was methodological: before assigning ancient doctrine to the images, reconstruct what games used the cards, when designs appeared, and how patterns travelled.

Dummett, Ronald Decker, Thierry Depaulis, Stuart Kaplan, Robert M. Place, and Helen Farley differ in emphasis, but broadly agree that:

- tarot arose as a game in Renaissance Italy;
- occult tarot is a later historical development;
- Gébelin and successors created new systems rather than recovering a demonstrated Egyptian one;
- meaningful modern symbolism can exist without being ancient.

That conclusion does not disprove divination as a personal practice; it addresses historical provenance.

# Open questions

1. **Which surviving Seven of Cups is absolutely earliest?**  
   The relative chronology of Cary-Yale, Brambilla, and early Visconti-Sforza material remains debated.

2. **Who designed the Sola Busca program?**  
   Nicola di maestro Antonio is associated with its engraving, but authorship of its intellectual program remains under study.

3. **What exactly does the Sola Busca Seven depict?**  
   Alchemical readings have contextual support, but a uniquely agreed operation or text has not been demonstrated.

4. **Did Pamela Colman Smith see the British Museum photographs directly?**  
   Opportunity and parallels make this plausible, but no diary entry or letter saying “I used the Sola Busca” has surfaced.

5. **Who devised Smith’s seven individual apparitions?**  
   Waite may have provided instructions; Smith may have invented or synthesized them. No surviving design brief settles the division.

6. **Do the seven apparitions form a fixed code?**  
   Waite’s silence and later disagreement suggest not, or at least that the key has not survived.

7. **What is the exact textual stemma of Etteilla’s card 43?**  
   Deck types and manuals vary in numbering, spelling, and expansion of *La Pensée/Projets*. Each edition needs separate collation.

8. **Which Golden Dawn manuscript first used “Illusionary Success”?**  
   Later publications reproduce order papers, but securely dating individual manuscript witnesses remains specialist archival work.

9. **Case’s precise card meaning:**  
   A primary, publicly accessible BOTA lesson giving his exact Seven-of-Cups upright/reversed text was not located.

10. **Pollack and Greer quotations:**  
    Their interpretations are accessible in copyrighted editions, but reliable authorized open texts for verbatim quotation were not located.

11. **Card-specific literary influence:**  
    Calvino demonstrates tarot’s literary power, and Yeats belonged to its occult milieu, but a direct Seven-of-Cups source relationship remains to be shown.
