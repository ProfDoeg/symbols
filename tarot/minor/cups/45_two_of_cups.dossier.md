# Two of Cups: Research Dossier

## Method and evidentiary labels

This dossier distinguishes:

- **[Documented fact]** directly attested by a surviving card, archival document, contemporary rule, or primary printed text.
- **[Scholarly reconstruction]** an inference accepted or seriously entertained by card historians but not stated in a contemporary record.
- **[Tradition]** an established later convention whose historical origin may be known.
- **[Esoteric attribution]** a correspondence intentionally imposed by an occult system.
- **[Disputed claim]** a claim supported by some writers but rejected or left unresolved by others.
- **[Legend]** a story incompatible with the surviving chronology or unsupported by evidence.
- **[Modern invention]** a recent interpretation or redesign, not an early property of the card.

The item-specific brief controls here: the Two of Cups is a pip, not a trump. Consequently, trump-order controversies, the Tzaddi/Heh exchange, Papess/Juno substitutions, and the Fool’s numbering do not alter this card. They are addressed only where necessary to distinguish the history of the whole pack from the history of this pip.

### Essential correction

**[Documented fact]** *Trionfi* originally denoted the added trump sequence, not the Two of Cups by itself. *Tarocchi* later named the entire pack/game. French *tarot* and German *Tarock* are later linguistic descendants.

**[Documented fact]** The Two of Cups has no intrinsic Hebrew letter or Tree-of-Life path in Lévi’s or the Golden Dawn’s scheme. Hebrew letters and paths belong to the twenty-two trumps. In Golden Dawn number-card doctrine, the **2** instead refers to Sephirah 2, **Chokmah**; Cups refer collectively to **Briah, Water, and Heh**.

**[Finding]** The requested premise “the Sola Busca pip as the source of Pamela Colman Smith’s scene” could not be verified and is probably an overstatement. Smith demonstrably adapted several Sola Busca compositions, but the Sola Busca Two of Cups is not the clear compositional prototype for her courting couple, caduceus, and lion head. No primary statement by Smith or Waite identifying it as that card’s source was located.

## Iconography deck by deck

## Fifteenth-century Milan

### Cary–Yale / Visconti di Modrone, approximately 1440s

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** Usually dated approximately 1442–1447 and commissioned for Filippo Maria Visconti or the Visconti-Sforza milieu. Yale now cautions that Andrea Bembo may have participated where older literature attributed the cards broadly to Bonifacio Bembo. Sixty-seven cards survive; the original structure may have differed from the later 78-card standard.

The surviving numeral cards use repeated suit emblems enriched by flowers, vines, heraldry, gold, and punched-gesso decoration. They are not narrative scenes. Cups are courtly vessels rather than allegorical actors.

**[Open identification issue]** The online Yale summaries consulted do not supply an authoritative individual catalogue entry establishing that a particular surviving card is the Two of Cups. Therefore it should not automatically be called the earliest surviving example without checking the current Cary inventory object by object. Yale calls Cary–Yale and Brera–Brambilla the two earliest surviving groups, probably from the 1440s. [Yale Library](https://library.yale.edu/news/cards-library-partners-work-together-solve-mysteries-rare-tarot-deck?page=1)

### Brera–Brambilla, approximately 1442–1447

**[Documented object group; reconstructed date]** Preserved at the Pinacoteca di Brera. Only two trumps survive, but numerous suit cards do. Its coins reproduce Visconti money, supporting a date before Filippo Maria Visconti’s death in 1447.

As in Cary–Yale, the pips are luxurious arrangements of emblems rather than divinatory tableaux. If its Two of Cups survives, its content belongs to this emblematic convention; the accessible Brera summary did not individually catalogue it, so certainty about that single card is withheld. [Yale Library](https://library.yale.edu/news/cards-library-partners-work-together-solve-mysteries-rare-tarot-deck?page=1)

### Pierpont Morgan–Bergamo / Visconti-Sforza, approximately 1450–1470

**[Documented fact]** Seventy-four cards survive among the Morgan Library, Accademia Carrara, and private holdings. The Morgan catalogue’s holdings include the Ace, Three, Five, and Nine of Cups, but not the Two. Consequently the commonly sold “Visconti-Sforza Two of Cups” may be an authentic card held elsewhere or a modern reconstruction; it is not among the Morgan’s thirty-five cards. The pip grounds are cream-colored, with cup emblems incorporated into foliage and floral arabesques. [Morgan Library collection](https://www.themorgan.org/collection/tarot-cards)

**Meaning of the design:** **[Scholarly reconstruction]** decorative integration proclaimed expense, dynastic taste, and playability. No fifteenth-century text assigns romantic, Grail, alchemical, or Kabbalistic meaning to this pip.

### Ferrarese and Este fragments

The Charles VI/Gringonneur, Este, Catania, and other attributed Ferrarese groups are incomplete.

**[Finding]** No securely identified Two of Cups from the major Este fragments was located in the consulted institutional catalogues. It is therefore improper to describe a missing card as though its iconography survived.

### “Mantegna Tarocchi,” approximately 1460s

**[Documented fact]** These are didactic engraving series of fifty figures arranged in five decades, not a tarot pack and not a card-game ancestor containing suit pips. There is no Two of Cups.

### Rosenwald and Budapest sheets, late fifteenth or early sixteenth century

These woodcut sheets are important evidence for early inexpensive tarot patterns and for the emergence of regional trump iconography.

**[Finding]** They contribute little direct evidence to the Two of Cups because the best-known sheets principally preserve trumps and courts rather than an individually documented cup two. Their relevance is to the printing history of tarot, not to this card’s surviving picture.

## Sola Busca, 1491; colored around 1500

**[Documented fact]** The Sola Busca is the earliest surviving complete tarot pack and the earliest complete deck with extensively illustrated numeral cards. The engravings are conventionally dated 1491, probably Ferrara; the unique complete copy was colored in Venice around 1500. Brera acquired it in 2009. [Brera](https://pinacotecabrera.org/en/news/mostra/il-segreto-dei-segreti-i-tarocchi-sola-busca/), [Public Domain Review image sequence](https://publicdomainreview.org/collection/sola-busca)

The Two of Cups replaces a completely abstract alignment with an ornamental, emblematic composition involving the two vessels and elaborate supporting decoration. It does not depict Smith’s man and woman exchanging cups under a caduceus.

**[Documented reception fact]** Photographs of twenty-three Sola Busca cards were acquired by the British Museum and publicly exhibited from 1907. Smith’s 1909 designs for cards such as the Three of Swords and Ten of Wands show sufficiently close compositional relationships that direct study is widely accepted.

**[Disputed claim]** No equivalent visual demonstration establishes that the Sola Busca **Two of Cups specifically** supplied Smith’s scene. “Sola Busca influenced Smith’s minor arcana” is well supported; “its Two of Cups was her source” is not.

## Tarot de Marseille lineage

### Jean Noblet, Paris, approximately 1650

Two large cups occupy the upper field, while a dense heraldic or ornamental panel fills the lower portion. The surviving Noblet-pattern card is often described as bearing a crowned shield with three fleurs-de-lis and printer-related initials.

**[Documented iconography]** The plant and heraldic structure is decoration integrated with suit signs, not a surviving authorial statement about partnership. Later occult readers retroactively interpret two facing vessels and the mediating plant as union, generation, or reciprocity.

### Jean Dodal, Lyon, approximately 1701–1715

Dodal regularizes the woodcut pattern: two cups appear symmetrically, with a central floral stem and scrollwork. Differences of block cutting and color aside, it belongs to the same non-scenic tradition.

### Nicolas Conver, Marseille, 1760

Two golden-yellow cups face each other across a red-and-blue floral axis. A stem rises through a blossom and expands into elaborate upper foliage, sometimes perceived as paired fish or dolphin heads.

**[Documented fact]** Those forms are stylized vegetal ornaments.  
**[Esoteric attribution]** Later writers call them dolphins, alchemical currents, polar forces, or a caduceus. No Conver-era text proving those meanings was found.

### Grimaud / Paul Marteau, twentieth century

Grimaud’s “Ancien Tarot de Marseille” standardized a cleaned, strongly colored Conver-derived design. Paul Marteau’s *Le Tarot de Marseille* supplied an extensive esoteric exegesis to details that earlier game cards did not explain in writing.

**[Esoteric attribution]** Marteau read the two cups as receptivities or poles joined through ascending formative life; black divisions signify resistance or material limitation. This is twentieth-century commentary, not evidence for what Noblet, Dodal, or Conver intended.

### Camoin–Jodorowsky, 1997

The restoration enlarges and recolors the Marseille vocabulary and treats tiny marks as a coherent initiatory language.

**[Modern reconstruction / disputed claim]** Jodorowsky and Philippe Camoin presented the design as a restoration of an archetypal Marseille tarot. Critics answer that no single primordial master deck is documented and that the edition sometimes synthesizes features from different dates and makers.

In Jodorowsky and Marianne Costa’s reading, Cups correspond to emotional life, love, receptivity, and the heart. The Two is accumulation or gestation: attraction exists, but realization has not yet reached the creative activity of Three. The interpretation is systematic and psychologically useful, but modern.

## Minchiate, Bolognese, and Sicilian patterns

### Minchiate / Germini, Florence, sixteenth century onward

Minchiate expands the trump sequence to forty but retains the four Latin suits. Its Two of Cups is an emblematic pip. In Cups and Coins the numeral order is reversed relative to Swords and Batons: Ace is highest, then Two, down to Ten. Therefore the Two is the **second-highest numeral**, below the Ace and above Three. It has no individual point value; kings and selected trumps carry values. [Minchiate rules](https://www.pagat.com/tarot/minchiate.html)

### Tarocchino Bolognese

The shortened Bolognese pack removes numeral cards 2–5 in every suit. Thus the Two of Cups is absent from the standard 62-card tarocchino pack.

**[Documented game fact]** Its absence is a consequence of pack reduction, not censorship or esoteric suppression.

### Sicilian Tarocchi

The later surviving 63-card pattern also suppresses low numerals. Cups ordinarily retain 5–10, with the Four in some forms; Two is absent. Sicily’s Fool is *Il Fuggitivo / lu Fujutu*, but this regional name has no bearing on a removed Two of Cups. [Sicilian Tarocchi rules](https://www.pagat.com/tarot/sicilian.html)

## Etteilla

### Grand Etteilla, designed during the 1780s; printed forms from 1789 onward

Etteilla reorganized the entire tarot into an Egyptian creation narrative and printed upright and reversed keywords directly on cards.

For the Two of Cups:

- **Upright:** *Amour* — Love.
- **Reversed:** *Désir* — Desire.

Different Grand Etteilla editions and later Blocquel/Grimaud redactions use different overall sequence numbers; “card 48” appears in some arrangements. It is safer to identify it by suit and rank unless a specific edition is named.

The picture remains primarily a two-cup pip rather than a human relationship scene. Its revolutionary feature is semantic: the card now carries an explicit verbal oracle. [Grand Etteilla Cups comparison](https://etteilla.wordpress.com/etteilla-ii/etteila-grand-cups/)

**[Documented historical importance]** Etteilla’s practice is the earliest fully developed published system assigning systematic divinatory meanings to all seventy-eight cards.

## Oswald Wirth, 1889

Wirth’s 1889 deck illustrated only the twenty-two trumps. There is no Wirth Two of Cups.

**[Finding]** Descriptions of an “Oswald Wirth Two of Cups” ordinarily concern a later publisher’s completion or a Marseille pip paired with Wirth-style trumps. Wirth influenced occult tarot theory, but his original twenty-two-card series supplies no deck-specific image for this pip.

## Golden Dawn manuscript design, approximately 1888–1900

*Book T* gives the formal title **“The Lord of Love”** and describes:

> “A WHITE Radiant Hand … holds lotuses.”

The design has water below; a lotus rises, and its stems and flowers feed two cups. Venus and Cancer mark the decan. The text concludes:

> “Chokmah of H. (Marriage, love, pleasure).”

The same manuscript’s planetary table expressly lists “Venus … Cancer — Love — 2 of Cups.” [*Book T*, pp. 25–26, 45](https://jantetteroo.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/02/Mathers_and_felkin_golden_dawn_book_t_th.pdf)

The continuation omitted by some transcriptions gives the moral range according to dignity: pleasure, harmony, gaiety, and subtlety, but when ill dignified, folly, dissipation, waste, or silly actions.

### Wang and Cicero reconstructions

Robert Wang’s *Golden Dawn Tarot* and the Chic and Sandra Tabatha Cicero deck are twentieth-century visual reconstructions of manuscript instructions, not reproductions of one canonical historic Golden Dawn pack. Both restore the angelic hand, lotus, cups, water, and astrological signs, but differ in drawing, coloring, and small spatial decisions.

**Color tradition:** Cups operate in the Queen Scale/Briah framework; published reconstruction guides describe bluish-gray Chokmah water, darker Briatic water, and golden cups. Exact painted hues differ between reconstructors. [Golden Dawn coloring guide](https://www.daneel.franken.de/tarot/GoldenDawn/Coloring%20the%20Classic%20Golden%20Dawn%20Tarot.pdf)

## Waite–Smith, 1909

Pamela Colman Smith depicts a young man and woman facing one another, each holding a cup. He reaches toward her cup or hand. Above them rises a caduceus of two serpents, surmounted by a red winged lion’s head. A house or manor stands on a green hill behind them.

Waite’s description is:

> “A youth and maiden are pledging one another, and above their cups rises the Caduceus of Hermes, between the great wings of which there appears a lion’s head.”

His divinatory synthesis is unusually elevated:

> “Love, passion, friendship, affinity, union, concord, sympathy, the interrelation of the sexes, and—as a suggestion apart from all offices of divination—that desire which is not in Nature, but by which Nature is sanctified.”

Reversed:

> “False love, folly, misunderstanding.”

The wording is from *The Pictorial Key to the Tarot* (London: William Rider, 1910), Part III, Minor Arcana, Two of Cups. [Primary text](https://sacred-texts.com/tarot/pkt/index.htm)

### Sources of Smith’s design

- **[Documented]** Waite’s words and the Golden Dawn title make love, union, and marriage the intended semantic field.
- **[Likely reconstruction]** The two human figures concretize reciprocity already implied by Etteilla, Mathers, and *Book T*.
- **[Esoteric attribution]** The caduceus suggests balanced opposites, exchange, healing, mediation, or Hermetic union.
- **[Esoteric attribution]** The winged lion may join Leo/fire/desire to Mercury’s caduceus, or evoke a chimera-like guardian.
- **[Unresolved]** Waite did not explain the lion head in the entry.
- **[Not verified]** No evidence was found that the Sola Busca Two of Cups supplied this composition.

## BOTA: Paul Foster Case and Jessie Burns Parke

The BOTA deck deliberately returns most number cards to non-scenic emblem arrangements. Thus its Two of Cups is not a duplicate of Smith’s couple, although Case retained Golden Dawn-derived astrology and Qabalah in his lessons.

Case’s *Oracle of the Tarot* assigns the first decan of Cancer and gives the keynote “Response to environment.” Well dignified: reciprocity, reflection, benefits through parents, favors from the opposite sex, and changes of residence. Ill dignified: reverses through parents or the opposite sex, unfortunate residential changes, fluctuating mood, and unwise decisions.

**[Conflict]** Case’s practical list is less exclusively romantic than Waite’s and connects Cancer/Moon symbolism with home and parents. Modern summaries that quote Waite’s “desire which … sanctifies Nature” under Case are conflating sources. [BOTA comparison](https://moderatelyquicksilver.com/2024/07/05/tarot-encyclopedia-the-two-of-cups/)

## Thoth, painted 1938–1943; first full publication 1969

Frieda Harris creates a geometric aquatic emblem rather than a human couple. Two cups overflow into a calm green-blue sea. A lotus feeds the cups; above it twin dolphins entwine a central stem and support or channel the upper flower. Venus and Cancer are integral to the card.

Crowley writes:

> “The Two always represents the Word and the Will. It is the first manifestation.”

And:

> “Love … recovers unity from dividuality by mutual annihilation.”

He calls the dolphins alchemical and concludes that the card could be renamed:

> “the Lord of Love under Will.”

The design signifies:

> “perfect and placid harmony, radiating an intensity of joy and ecstasy.”

Crowley, *The Book of Thoth* (1944), p. 196. [Scanned edition](https://www.rahoorkhuit.net/library/crowley/equinox/volume3/vol_3_no_05.pdf)

**[Documented lineage]** Thoth preserves the Golden Dawn title, Venus/Cancer, lotuses, water, and two cups while eliminating the angelic hand and making the alchemical dolphins dominant.

**[Finding]** The famous Crowley Tzaddi/Heh exchange concerns the Emperor and Star trumps. It does not alter the Two of Cups, because this pip has no individual Hebrew-letter path.

## Notable modern redesigns

- **Thoth-derived decks:** emphasize dolphins, alchemical marriage, ecstatic polarity, and “Love under Will.”
- **RWS-derived decks:** generally retain two people exchanging cups, often broadening gender, sexuality, race, and relationship type.
- **Marseille revivals:** retain two vessels and vegetation, resisting scenic illustration.
- **Jungian and therapeutic decks:** make the encounter an image of ego–shadow, conscious–unconscious, inner masculine–feminine, attachment, or mutual recognition.
- **Queer and feminist redesigns:** **[Modern invention]** commonly remove Waite’s assumption that “the interrelation of the sexes” is necessarily heterosexual or binary.
- **Grail-themed decks:** replace ordinary cups with chalices and interpret the pair as communion, sacred marriage, or two persons sharing spiritual vocation. This is modern mythopoesis rather than a documented fifteenth-century meaning.

## The game

## Rank

In traditional Italian tarot games:

- The card belongs to a plain suit and cannot beat a trump.
- Players must normally follow suit.
- In the “round” suits, Cups and Coins, numeral cards traditionally rank in reverse numerical order: **Ace high, then Two, Three … Ten low**.
- Thus the Two of Cups is usually the second-highest numeral card, beneath the Ace but below all surviving court ranks in trick-taking hierarchy.

In modern French tarot the deck has French suits, not literal Cups. Its functional counterpart is the Two of Hearts or Diamonds only by color/suit analogy, not historical identity. All ordinary pips rank numerically upward under the courts and are worth one-half point apiece. [French Tarot rules](https://www.pagat.com/tarot/frtarot.html)

In Central European Tarock, shortened French-suited packs usually retain low red cards: Ace/One, Two, Three, Four. The red Two therefore survives as a low ordinary suit card. Literal cup symbols have disappeared.

## Value

The Two of Cups is ordinarily a low-value or blank counting card:

- In Minchiate: zero individual card points.
- In many Italian scoring systems: one of the ordinary *cartine*, contributing only through batch or trick counting.
- In French tarot: an ordinary card worth 0.5 point.
- It may nevertheless be strategically valuable as the second-highest numeral in the traditional round-suit ranking.

## Trump-order disputes

The northern, southern, and eastern regional orders; the exchanged order of Justice and Fortitude; Papess/Pope substitutions; and the Fool’s various functions concern trumps. They do not renumber or rename the Two of Cups.

The Fool was commonly an Excuse-like special card, not originally a trump of a numbered rank. This fact supplies no evidence that the Two of Cups was occult or divinatory.

### Exact Golden Dawn placement

**[Documented esoteric attribution]**

- Zodiacal span: approximately 0°–10° Cancer.
- Planetary ruler: Venus.
- Decan title: Lord of Love.
- Sephirah: Chokmah.
- World: Briah.
- Element: Water.
- Tetragrammaton: Heh.
- Decan angels in *Book T* transcription: Ayoel/Auwael and Chabooyah/Habuhiah, with transliteration varying.

The card is **not** “the path of Chokmah.” Number cards are Sephirotic combinations, whereas the twenty-two trump letters map paths.

## Divinatory meanings by author

## Etteilla, late eighteenth century

- Upright: **“Amour”** — Love.
- Reversed: **“Désir”** — Desire.

This polarity is not simply good versus bad: reversed desire can be passionate longing rather than love realized.

## S. L. MacGregor Mathers, 1888

From *The Tarot: Its Occult Signification, Use in Fortune-Telling, and Method of Play*:

- Upright: **“Love, Attachment, Friendship, Sincerity, Affection.”**
- Reversed: **“Crossed desires, Obstacles, Opposition, Hindrance.”**

Mathers closely anticipates the Golden Dawn emotional field, but the elaborate Venus/Cancer manuscript structure belongs to the Order’s technical papers.

## Golden Dawn *Book T*

- Well dignified: harmony, pleasure, gaiety, subtlety, love, marriage.
- Ill dignified: folly, dissipation, waste, silly action.
- Sephirotic gloss: **“Marriage, love, pleasure.”**

## A. E. Waite, 1910

- Upright: **“Love, passion, friendship, affinity, union, concord, sympathy, the interrelation of the sexes”** and sanctified desire.
- Reversed: **“False love, folly, misunderstanding.”**
- Additional or secondary table: favorable affairs of pleasure, business, love, wealth, and honor; reversed, passion.

The two Waite lists are not perfectly identical. Modern quotations often combine them without identifying the separate tables.

## Aleister Crowley, 1944

Crowley does not use a simple upright/reversed dictionary. His essential meaning is:

- “Love” as reunion of divided existence.
- Perfect male-female harmony in the broadest sense.
- Joy, ecstasy, and placid balance.
- Shadow by excess or corrupted dignity: sensual indulgence, dissipation, or the degradation of love into appetite.

## Paul Foster Case

- Well dignified: reciprocity, reflection, parental benefits, favors from the opposite sex, change of residence.
- Ill dignified: corresponding reverses, unfortunate moves, mood fluctuation, and poor decisions.
- Keyword: **“Response to environment.”**

## Rachel Pollack

In *Seventy-Eight Degrees of Wisdom*, Pollack treats the card as equal attraction and interchange, a beginning relationship whose force arises between the two participants. It may be erotic, emotional, spiritual, or reconciliatory. Its equality distinguishes it from domination or dependence.

**[Finding]** A reliable online scan supporting a verbatim Pollack quotation was not located, so no supposed “own words” are presented as exact quotation.

## Mary K. Greer

Greer’s reversal method resists one fixed antonym. For the reversed Two of Cups she explicitly allows inner polarity and the relationship between self and an inner masculine or feminine image. Depending on context, reversal can indicate blocked exchange, projection, imbalance, privacy, absence, excess, or an internalized union.

**[Documented quotation fragment]** Greer notes that the reversed card “can refer to inner polarity.”

This is a methodological departure from Waite’s fixed “false love.”

## Esoteric and symbolic readings

## Alchemical

**First securely developed on this card:** Golden Dawn manuscript imagery; dramatically expanded by Crowley.

Two vessels receive a common fluid; lotuses join above and below; Crowley’s dolphins embody the “Royal Art.” Later readers identify:

- two alchemical vessels;
- conjunction of sulfur and mercury;
- *coniunctio oppositorum*;
- dissolution of separateness;
- circulation and overflowing rather than static possession.

**[Caution]** Medieval and early modern alchemical images contain paired vessels and conjunction symbolism, but that does not prove that fifteenth-century tarot makers encoded alchemy into this pip.

## Kabbalistic

**First systematic form:** Golden Dawn.

Chokmah is undivided dynamic force; Briah is the creative world and is assigned Water. The Two of Cups is therefore Chokmah operating through Briah: primal force appearing as attraction, reflection, or relationship.

**[Esoteric attribution]** The suit’s Heh belongs to YHVH’s mother/water term. It is not the trump-letter Heh and does not participate in Crowley’s Tzaddi controversy.

## Astrological

**First securely attested assignment:** Golden Dawn, late nineteenth century.

Venus signifies attraction, pleasure, concord, and affection. Cancer signifies receptivity, protection, family, memory, home, and lunar fluidity. Their combination explains both the romantic Golden Dawn reading and Case’s emphasis on home and parents.

**[Historical caution]** No evidence places Venus in Cancer on the Renaissance card.

## Hermetic

The caduceus in Smith’s picture supplies the clearest Hermetic emblem: two serpentine forces balanced on one axis. Later interpreters add exchange, negotiation, healing, polarity, and the circulation of subtle force.

**[Unresolved]** Waite described but did not decode the caduceus and winged lion in the card entry. Claims that the lion definitively represents Kundalini, the alchemical red lion, Leo, sexual passion, or an Egyptian guardian exceed the primary text.

## Christian and Grail

Cups naturally resemble Eucharistic chalices; two cups can suggest shared communion, a wedding pledge, or Christ’s cup shared among believers.

**[Tradition]** Christian readers and Grail-centered decks develop these possibilities.

**[Finding]** No fifteenth-century source identifies the Two of Cups as the Holy Grail or Eucharist. The suit itself probably descends from the cups suit of Islamic/Mamluk playing cards, although European artists readily assimilated the form to familiar chalices.

## Jungian

Twentieth-century psychological tarot treats the card as:

- ego meeting other;
- conscious meeting unconscious;
- anima/animus encounter;
- recognition of projection;
- reconciliation of opposites;
- secure attachment and reciprocal witnessing.

Pollack, Greer, and later therapeutic tarot writers spread this approach. It is a modern interpretive language, not an archaeological account.

### Literature, painting, film, and architecture

Calvino’s *The Castle of Crossed Destinies* uses tarot cards structurally as a combinatorial narrative language. It does not give the Two of Cups a single, stable canonical role comparable to Waite’s entry.

T. S. Eliot’s *The Waste Land* invokes “Madame Sosostris,” the “wicked pack of cards,” the drowned Phoenician Sailor, Belladonna, the man with three staves, the Wheel, and the blank card. It does **not** name the Two of Cups.

W. B. Yeats participated in Golden Dawn culture, so he belongs to the milieu that generated the correspondence, but no securely traced Yeats text centered on this card was found.

Jodorowsky’s films repeatedly use initiatory, alchemical, and tarot-like structures; his direct, sustained Two-of-Cups doctrine is in the tarot work with Costa, not a single verified film quotation.

Bergman’s *The Seventh Seal* concerns chess, death, faith, and medieval allegory. A specific quotation of the Two of Cups could not be established.

**[Finding]** No historic building or canonical painting was securely verified as deliberately quoting the Two of Cups. Modern murals, tattoos, album art, and wedding designs commonly reproduce Smith’s composition, but resemblance alone cannot establish intentional quotation.

## Controversies and disputes

## Egyptian origin

Court de Gébelin argued in 1781 that tarot preserved an Egyptian book. Etteilla transformed that speculation into an Egyptianized deck and divinatory system; Papus, Falconnier, and later occultists spread it.

**[Legend as history; documented as reception]** No Egyptian tarot pack, Egyptian text naming tarot, archaeological sequence, or transmission record connects pharaonic Egypt to the fifteenth-century Italian game. Waite himself wrote that there was “no particle of evidence” for Egyptian origin. [Waite’s historical discussion](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Page:The_Illustrated_Key_to_the_Tarot.djvu/36)

The Two of Cups therefore is not demonstrably an Egyptian hieroglyph. Its later lotuses, zodiac symbols, and “Book of Thoth” setting are deliberate occult additions.

## Romani or “Gypsy” origin

Nineteenth-century writers associated tarot with Romani diviners and repeated the then-common false belief that the Roma came from Egypt.

**[Legend]** Tarot is documented in northern Italy before evidence of Romani tarot practice. Romani people later adopted cartomancy, as did other itinerant and settled diviners; adoption does not prove invention or transmission from India or Egypt.

The historical term is reproduced only when identifying the old claim. “Romani” is preferable for the people.

## Cathar, Templar, Gnostic, and heretical transmission

Various writers have claimed the tarot conceals Cathar dualism, Templar doctrine, a Gnostic gospel, Manichaean cosmology, or a survival of persecuted sectarian teaching.

**[Disputed claim / legend]** These proposals rely mainly on retrospective symbolic correspondences. No Cathar or Templar document describes the pack, and no transmission chain reaches the Milanese and Ferrarese workshops. Helen Farley documents how such origin stories entered modern tarot culture. [Farley, *A Cultural History of Tarot*](https://e-isomero.com/pdf/book/1611846398.pdf)

A modern defense of Cathar scenarios exists, but it concedes no new fifteenth-century object identifying the Two of Cups as Cathar doctrine. [Occult-tradition argument](https://ibnarabisociety.org/wp-content/uploads/PDFs/El-Moor_Occult-tradition-2.pdf)

## Sola Busca as Smith’s source

- **Well supported:** Smith knew or could access British Museum photographs; several cards show striking borrowings.
- **Unsupported generalization:** all RWS scenic pips derive from corresponding Sola Busca cards.
- **Not demonstrated:** the Sola Busca Two of Cups directly generated Smith’s couple, caduceus, and lion head.

This card is therefore an instructive case where a correct general statement became an incorrect card-by-card claim.

## Dolphins in Marseille

Later readers see two dolphin or fish heads in the upper foliage of Conver’s card.

- **Documented:** zoomorphic-looking scrolls exist.
- **Unresolved:** whether the engraver intended recognizable dolphins.
- **Esoteric attribution:** alchemical dolphins.
- **Potential conflation:** Crowley explicitly calls Harris’s animals dolphins; that certainty is sometimes projected backward onto Marseille.

## Misprints and editorial traditions

- Marseille block damage, recoloring, mirrored copies, and altered initials have sometimes been treated as primordial symbols.
- Grand Etteilla editions reorder and renumber cards; a number valid in one issue is falsely presented as universal.
- Waite’s primary meaning and his secondary divinatory table are frequently merged.
- “Rider-Waite” historically suppresses Smith’s authorship; “Waite-Smith” or “Rider-Waite-Smith” is more accurate.
- Thoth is often dated 1944, the year of the book, although Harris painted the deck chiefly in 1938–1943 and the complete commercial deck appeared in 1969.
- “Heh is the Hebrew letter of the Two of Cups” is an imprecise modern shorthand: Heh designates the Cups/Briah column, not a unique twenty-two-path correspondence.
- “Sephirah 2 means duality” oversimplifies Golden Dawn doctrine. Chokmah is dynamic unity or force; Crowley explicitly says Two is the first manifestation, not merely interpersonal duality.

## Open questions

1. Which mid-fifteenth-century Milanese collection contains the earliest securely catalogued surviving Two of Cups? The online museum summaries do not settle this object-level question.

2. What precise visual sources did Smith and Waite intend for the caduceus and winged lion? Waite names the figures but supplies no source note.

3. Did Smith independently conceive the human pledge, or did Waite give her a detailed unpublished instruction derived from *Book T*? Their division of labor is incompletely documented.

4. Did Conver and predecessors intend the upper vegetal scrolls as dolphins, or is that identification retrospective?

5. Which Etteilla printing first fixed *Amour / Désir* on the physical Two of Cups, and how did its overall sequence number change across the 1789, Blocquel, Dusserre, and Grimaud families?

6. Exactly how much of the received *Book T* wording is Mathers’s, how much is from other Golden Dawn compilers, and how much reflects later Felkin or editorial transmission?

7. Pollack and Greer are frequently paraphrased online. Exact page-controlled comparison requires consultation of their copyrighted print editions rather than derivative keyword sites.
