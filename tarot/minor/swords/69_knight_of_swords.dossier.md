# Knight of Swords: Research Dossier

## Editorial method and evidence labels

This dossier uses the following labels:

- **Documented fact** — supported directly by a surviving card, archival document, contemporary printed text, or museum record.
- **Scholarly reconstruction** — an inference advanced by historians from material, documentary, or comparative evidence.
- **Tradition** — a convention transmitted through card-making, game play, or cartomancy, without necessarily claiming ancient origin.
- **Esoteric attribution** — a correspondence deliberately assigned by an occult author or order.
- **Disputed claim** — an interpretation for which scholars or primary evidence disagree.
- **Legend** — a historical-origin narrative contradicted or unsupported by the surviving evidence.
- **Modern invention** — a substantially new interpretation or design first documented in modern tarot practice.

The Knight of Swords is a suit card, not a trump. Consequently, disputes over the three Italian trump orders, the numbering of Justice and Strength, the Papess/Juno substitution, and the Fool’s number do not change this card’s rank or identity. They are discussed only where they illuminate the deck containing it.

The Morgan Library describes the Visconti-Sforza pack as a 78-card structure containing King, Queen, Knight, and Knave in each suit. It dates the principal cards after Francesco Sforza’s entry into Milan as duke in 1450 and attributes the work to the workshop of Bonifacio Bembo. [Morgan Library collection record](https://www.themorgan.org/manuscript/158525); [Metropolitan Museum overview](https://www.metmuseum.org/exhibitions/listings/2016/world-in-play/exhibition-galleries).

### Is it “number 52”?

**Documented fact:** Traditional Italian and Marseille court cards do not bear sequence numbers.

**Tradition:** Mathers’s 1888 list numbers the Knight of Swords **52** because he places the suit after the trumps and other courts in a continuous explanatory sequence. Grand Etteilla editions likewise impose deck-wide numbers.

**Conclusion:** “52” is a cataloguing/cartomantic number, not the Knight’s historical tarot number. The most accurate identification is **unnumbered court card**.

# Iconography, deck by deck

## Mamluk antecedent: nāʾib and the suit of swords

### Fourteenth–fifteenth-century Mamluk cards, Egypt or Syria

**Documented fact:** The Topkapı group contains a 52-card structure with four suits conventionally translated as swords, polo-sticks, cups, and coins, each with ten numeral cards and three inscribed court ranks. The ranks are **malik** (“king”), **nāʾib malik** (“deputy king”), and **thānī nāʾib** (“second deputy”). The courts are represented by calligraphy and suit emblems, not mounted human figures.

**Scholarly reconstruction:** Dummett and Kamal Abu-Deeb argued that this Islamic card family is the most plausible immediate ancestor or close relative of the earliest European Latin-suited packs. The Italian **cavallo** is not a literal copy of a mounted Mamluk image; it is a European figural recasting of one of the subordinate court ranks, with a Queen added to create a four-member court.

**Disputed point:** L. A. Mayer reconstructed more ranks than Dummett accepted. Dummett concluded that some apparent additional courts were replacements or misidentified pip cards. See Dummett’s discussion of the three ranks and the curved sword emblems in the reproduced chapters of *The Game of Tarot*. [Dummett excerpt](https://forum.tarothistory.com/viewtopic.php?p=27685); [summary of the Mayer–Dummett controversy](https://tarot-heritage.com/2019/04/05/trzes-mamluk-deck-the-granddaddy-of-european-playing-cards/).

**Etymological caution:** Spanish **naipes**, Italian **naibi**, and related early European words have long been connected with Arabic **nāʾib**. This is a strong historical-linguistic hypothesis, not proof that a game called “Nāʾib” was mechanically identical to tarot.

## Fifteenth-century Milan

### Cary-Yale / Visconti di Modrone, approximately 1440–1445

These names refer to the same principal surviving group, not two independent decks.

**Documented iconography:** Its courts are richly painted Milanese aristocrats against tooled gold and silver grounds. The pack is exceptional in possessing additional female court ranks, commonly called a female knight and maid, alongside the conventional courts. A sword court appears mounted and bears the suit emblem as an actual weapon.

**Meaning in its original setting:** The figure expresses aristocratic and military rank. Nothing on the card securely documents Air, intellect, haste, divination, or occult initiation.

**Disputed claim:** Attempts to identify individual courts as Filippo Maria Visconti, Bianca Maria Visconti, Francesco Sforza, or other family members are attractive but generally rest on heraldry, costume, dynastic context, and physiognomic resemblance—not inscriptions naming the sitter.

### Brambilla deck, Milan, approximately 1440s

**Documented fact:** The surviving cards are associated with the Visconti court and are held principally by the Pinacoteca di Brera.

**Iconography:** Like the other Milanese luxury cards, its surviving suit figures inhabit a gold-ground courtly world. The sword courts are military figures, but the incomplete survival of the pack makes card-for-card comparison hazardous.

**Open issue:** Published summaries do not consistently identify every surviving court under uniform modern names. A claim that a particular Brambilla horseman is definitively “the earliest Knight of Swords” requires consultation of the Brera object-level catalogue, not merely reproduction decks.

### Visconti-Sforza / Pierpont Morgan–Bergamo group, approximately 1450–1455

**Documented fact:** Seventy-four of an expected seventy-eight cards survive across the Morgan Library, Accademia Carrara/Colleoni holdings, and related collections. The Knight of Swords survives; the missing mounted court is the Knight of Coins.

**Iconography:** The sword knight is a mounted, armored court figure. The horse proceeds ceremonially rather than charging. The long, straight European sword is carried prominently; the image is a heraldic or processional presentation of rank, not a narrative battle scene.

**Portrait identification:**

- **Moakley/Kaplan tradition:** Court cards have frequently been read as members of the Visconti-Sforza family or their circle.
- **Scholarly caution:** Heraldic devices securely connect the cards to the dynasty; they do not securely name the Knight of Swords as a particular person.
- **Finding:** I found no contemporary label or payment record identifying the sitter. Any personal name should therefore be marked **disputed reconstruction**, not fact.

The Morgan record notes intermingled Visconti and Sforza devices and cautiously says the cards were executed “by or for” a member of that family, probably Francesco Sforza. [Morgan catalogue](https://www.themorgan.org/manuscript/158525).

## Ferrara and related fifteenth-century cards

### Este/Ferrarese fragments

**Documented fact:** Ferrara is one of the earliest securely documented tarot centers. The 1442 Este ledger records the purchase of *carte da trionfi*.

**Iconography:** Surviving Este-associated cards and fragments display courtly costume, martial retainers, and elaborate painted or printed ornament. Their incompleteness prevents a continuous Knight-of-Swords lineage comparable to the Visconti material.

**Finding:** The 1442 ledger names the pack category, not an individual *cavallo di spade*. It is therefore an early attestation of tarot manufacture/use, not the first written naming of this card.

### Sola Busca, probably engraved in the late fifteenth century; painted exemplar dated to approximately 1491

**Documented iconography:** This is the earliest substantially complete surviving tarot with illustrated narrative pip cards. Its court cards carry names. The Knight of Swords is **AMONE**, a mounted armored figure associated in modern scholarship with Ammon/Zeus-Ammon.

**Scholarly reconstruction:** The Brera exhibition catalogue, especially the work of Sofia Di Vincenzo and Laura Paola Gnaccolini, interprets the Sola Busca as a coherent humanist and hermetic-alchemical program. Amone is connected with Zeus-Ammon, whom Alexander the Great consulted or claimed as divine father; the King of Swords, **ALECXANDRO M.**, is Alexander.

**Disputed scope:** That the deck uses classical, biblical, and pseudo-historical names is visible. Whether every detail encodes a precise alchemical operation remains interpretive.

**Importance for Waite-Smith:** Photographs of the Sola Busca were acquired by the British Museum before Smith drew the Waite deck. Several Waite-Smith pips show close compositional dependence on it. No equally exact one-to-one borrowing has been demonstrated for the Knight of Swords; Smith’s charging knight belongs more directly to the European mounted-court tradition and Golden Dawn temperament. [Sola Busca overview and bibliography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sola_Busca_tarot); [British Museum collection search/image record](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/image/603490001).

### “Mantegna Tarocchi,” approximately 1460s

**Documented fact:** These are instructional/allegorical engravings arranged in five series of ten, not a tarot pack and not a playable deck with suits.

**Conclusion:** There is no Knight of Swords in the tarot-card sense. Its relevance is contextual—Renaissance systems of social estates, virtues, liberal arts, cosmic spheres—not genealogical evidence for this court.

## Early printed sheets

### Budapest sheets, late fifteenth or early sixteenth century

**Documented fact:** The surviving sheets contain the trumps, courts, and sword and baton suits.

**Iconography:** The Knight of Swords is explicitly martial: an armored mounted warrior carrying a sword. The sword pips use curved and banded designs, but these ornamental curves should not be confused with the knight’s actual weapon.

The reconstruction literature notes that the Knight and Page of Swords are the most warlike courts in the sheet. [Budapest reconstruction discussion](https://tarot-heritage.com/2018/11/27/the-budapest-tarot-recreated-by-sullivan-hismans/).

### Rosenwald sheets, approximately 1500

**Documented fact:** Uncolored woodcut sheets now in the Rosenwald Collection preserve an early central-Italian tarot pattern. Some cards are missing and modern “complete decks” necessarily reconstruct absent material.

**Iconographic significance:** The sheets demonstrate the transfer of court-and-trump imagery from one-off luxury painting into repeatable print manufacture.

**Caution:** A modern restored Knight of Swords should not automatically be described as a wholly surviving original if its source sheet is damaged or absent. [Rosenwald reconstruction account](https://tarot-heritage.com/2017/10/31/the-rosenwald-deck/).

## Regional Italian patterns

### Bolognese tarot

**Documented tradition:** Bolognese packs retain four courts per suit, including the mounted sword court. In game play it ranks immediately below the Queen and above the Jack.

**Iconography:** Compact double-ended modern Bolognese cards have greatly simplified the older full-length horseman. This is a functional redesign for play, not an occult reinterpretation.

### Minchiate, Florence and later Tuscany

**Documented fact:** Minchiate expanded the trump series to forty trump cards while retaining the four Italian suits and their four courts.

**Knight of Swords:** Still an ordinary suit court. Its expanded pack context does not make it a trump or give it an esoteric number.

**Game value:** It normally retains the standard court hierarchy and scoring role. The extra trumps change the trump sequence, not the sword court’s identity.

### Sicilian tarot

**Documented tradition:** Sicilian tarot developed a reduced and regionally distinctive pack. Court costume and card proportions differ, but the mounted sword rank continues as a suit court.

**Caution:** Rules vary by date and locality; one should not transfer Piedmontese or French Tarot scoring mechanically to every historical Sicilian game.

## Tarot de Marseille lineage

### Jean Noblet, Paris, approximately 1650

The BnF calls Noblet the earliest known surviving pack conforming to what is conventionally called the Tarot de Marseille pattern. “Marseille” is retrospective here: Noblet worked in Paris. [BnF record](https://essentiels.bnf.fr/fr/image/4799f2d6-6f88-4f6f-a15c-40a1a943c95e-tarot-jean-noblet).

**Iconography:** A mounted **Cavalier d’Épée**, shown in profile, carries a large straight sword. The figure is heraldic and largely static, with patterned costume and a compressed horse.

**Survival caution:** Several Noblet sword cards are missing from the unique surviving pack. Any restoration should distinguish surviving linework from reconstructed material.

### Jean Dodal, Lyon, approximately 1701–1715

**Iconography:** A mounted, armored or helmeted cavalier carries the sword upright. Movement is modest. The name is cut in the border in the characteristic early Marseille manner.

**Change from Milan:** Flat woodcut color, strong outline, and emblematic pose replace the individualized aristocratic luxury portrait.

### Nicolas Conver, Marseille, 1760

**Documented fact:** Conver established his card-making business in Marseille in 1760. A museum photograph identifies the individual card as **“Cavalier d’épée.”** [Mucem record](https://mucem.org/notice/c/3118217/); [French museum record](https://pop.culture.gouv.fr/notice/joconde/M0277000850).

**Iconography:** The horseman, helmeted and richly patterned, holds a large sword upward. The horse moves laterally but does not charge toward an enemy. The card remains a social-rank emblem rather than a fully dramatized scene.

### The “curved scimitars” question

**Verified correction:** Marseille **pip-card** swords are commonly drawn as curved interlaced bands. These derive from a long Latin-suit ornamental tradition and have been compared with curved Mamluk sword emblems. The Knight normally holds a recognizable straight European sword.

Calling every Marseille sword a “scimitar” is therefore misleading:

- **Documented:** curved or S-shaped sword emblems occur in Mamluk and early Italian cards.
- **Scholarly reconstruction:** these helped generate the arched Marseille pip pattern.
- **Not documented:** that Marseille makers consciously meant each interlaced band as a specifically Ottoman scimitar.

### Grimaud, nineteenth–twentieth centuries

**Tradition:** Grimaud standardized a commercially influential Conver-derived Marseille pack. Cleaner registration and industrial color replaced hand-stenciled variation. The Cavalier remained a lateral heraldic horseman.

### Camoin–Jodorowsky, 1997

**Modern reconstruction/invention:** Philippe Camoin and Alejandro Jodorowsky presented their deck as a restoration assembled from old Marseille witnesses and Camoin printing inheritance.

The Knight remains a Marseille Cavalier, but colors and small details were regularized according to the creators’ symbolic system. Claims that this recovers a single pristine, secret original are **disputed**: historical Marseille cards exist in multiple local and chronological variants.

Jodorowsky and Marianne Costa interpret court figures developmentally and relationally, using direction of gaze, posture, color, and suit as a visual language. This is a modern hermeneutic, not a demonstrated seventeenth-century maker’s manual.

## Besançon pattern

**Documented regional fact:** Besançon-type packs famously replace the Papess and Pope with **Juno and Jupiter**, particularly in Protestant-sensitive or Swiss/German-border markets.

**Knight of Swords:** The substitution affects trumps II and V, not the Cavalier d’Épée. His name, rank, and general mounted form remain stable.

This illustrates why template-wide regional renamings must not be projected onto this particular suit card.

---

## Etteilla’s Grand Etteilla, first issued approximately 1788–1789

**Documented innovation:** Etteilla designed the first known tarot pack expressly organized for occult divination. He reordered and renamed the trumps, numbered the entire pack, and supplied upright/reversed meanings.

**Name and number:** **Le Chevalier d’Épée**, generally card **52** in later Grand Etteilla sequence tables.

**Iconography:** A playing-card-like mounted military man rather than a Renaissance allegorical scene.

**Meaning:** The principal personification is **Le Militaire**—a soldier or military man. Etteilla’s system treats the card as a divinatory sign with separate orientation-dependent keywords.

**Historical importance:** This is the decisive move from an unnumbered game court to a numbered occult-cartomantic card. [Scanned *Grand Etteilla* manual](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/2/2f/Le_grand_Etteilla%2C_ou%2C_l%27art_de_tirer_les_cartes_..._Le_tout_recueilli_..._%28IA_b29321220%29.pdf).

## Oswald Wirth, 1889

**Documented limitation:** Wirth’s original 1889 tarot consisted of the twenty-two trumps. It had no independently redesigned Knight of Swords.

Later “Wirth” complete decks add minors supplied by publishers or later artists. Those cards must not be retroactively credited to Wirth’s 1889 designs.

## Golden Dawn manuscripts, approximately 1888–1900

### Book T

**Esoteric attribution:**

- Title: **King of Swords**
- Descriptive title: **Lord of the Winds and Breezes**
- Element: **Fire of Air**
- Tetragrammaton: **Yod**
- Sephirah/rank principle: the fiery, initiating force traditionally connected with Chokmah
- Zodiacal rule: **20° Taurus–20° Gemini**
- Associated pip decans: last decan of Taurus and first two decans of Gemini:
  - 7 of Pentacles: 20°–30° Taurus, Saturn in Taurus
  - 8 of Swords: 0°–10° Gemini, Jupiter in Gemini
  - 9 of Swords: 10°–20° Gemini, Mars in Gemini

**Iconography in the manuscript description:** A winged warrior in active motion, mounted, holding a drawn sword; the scale/sigil appears on the weapon. Clouds, wind, and birds express the Air suit, while violent speed expresses Fire.

**Verified wording issue:** Some tables say “20° Taurus to 20° Gemini,” while prose says “from the 21st degree of Taurus to the 20th degree of Gemini.” These are two ways of expressing the same continuous forty-degree interval, not rival attributions.

[Book T scan](https://jantetteroo.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/02/Mathers_and_felkin_golden_dawn_book_t_th.pdf).

### Wang and Cicero reconstructions

**Modern reconstruction:** Robert Wang’s *Golden Dawn Tarot* and Chic and Sandra Tabatha Cicero’s deck translate manuscript descriptions into finished images. Neither should be mistaken for a mass-produced nineteenth-century Golden Dawn deck.

They preserve the mounted **King**, elemental colors, airy motion, sword, and zodiacal/decans symbolism.

### The Knight/Prince confusion

This is the most important nomenclature trap.

| Ordinary/RWS rank | Golden Dawn rank | Elemental letter |
|---|---|---|
| Mounted Knight | **King** | **Yod / Fire** |
| Queen | Queen | Heh / Water |
| Seated King | **Prince** | Vav / Air |
| Page | Princess | Final Heh / Earth |

Thus:

- RWS **Knight of Swords** is structurally the Golden Dawn **King of Swords**.
- Golden Dawn **Prince of Swords** is not Waite’s Knight merely because “Prince” sounds like a younger knight.
- Crowley resolves part of the confusion by calling the mounted Golden Dawn King a **Knight**, but calls the Golden Dawn Prince a **Prince**.

## Waite-Smith, 1909; *Pictorial Key*, 1910

### Pamela Colman Smith’s drawing

**Documented iconography:** An armored knight on a white horse charges at full speed through a wind-torn landscape. He leans forward with sword raised. Clouds stream across the sky; trees bend; birds are driven in the air. The horse’s harness bears decorative motifs, and the animal’s extended posture turns a formerly static court emblem into an event.

### What changed

Compared with Marseille:

- lateral procession became a headlong charge;
- a neutral court rank became a distinct temperament;
- the suit’s Air attribution became visible as wind and turbulent sky;
- the Golden Dawn King’s fiery activation of Air became pictorial speed and aggression.

Waite writes: **“He is riding in full course, as if scattering his enemies.”** He then compares the ideal aspect to Galahad, whose sword is swift and sure because of purity of heart. [Waite, *The Pictorial Key to the Tarot*, Knight of Swords section](https://www.mr-kaplan.com/Library/Waite/The%20Pictorial%20Key%20to%20the%20Tarot.pdf).

### Sources

**Documented:** Smith knew theatrical costume, contemporary illustration, Renaissance art, and the tarot models available to Golden Dawn members.

**Strong reconstruction:** Golden Dawn Book T supplied the active, windy Fire-of-Air temperament.

**Possible influence:** Sola Busca photographs were available in the British Museum, but no exact Sola-Busca-to-RWS compositional borrowing for this particular knight is as clear as the borrowing visible in several RWS pip cards.

**Modern invention:** The now-common interpretation of the Knight as reckless, argumentative, intellectually impulsive, or “charging before thinking” is driven at least as much by Smith’s picture as by Waite’s comparatively balanced printed list.

## Paul Foster Case and BOTA

**Documented lineage:** Case worked from Golden Dawn-derived correspondences while revising colors and explanatory doctrine. The BOTA tarot was designed principally as a coloring and meditation deck.

**Knight of Swords:** BOTA retains the ordinary mounted rank and the active sword figure. Case’s court-card teaching preserves the idea that knights signify motion—the coming or going of a person, force, or matter depending on direction.

**Caution:** Case’s best-known public tarot text concentrates heavily on the Major Arcana. Many detailed BOTA court correspondences circulated through lessons rather than a single universally paginated public manual. Claims of a unique Case meaning should cite the specific lesson or edition.

## Crowley–Harris Thoth, painted 1938–1943; first complete publication 1969

### Title and correspondence

**Knight of Swords — Fire of Air**, ruling 20° Taurus–20° Gemini.

### Frieda Harris’s image

The knight is driven forward through turbulent air on a racing horse. He holds a long sword and a smaller blade or poniard. Wings, streaming forms, and wheeling birds break the figure into aerodynamic motion. Unlike the compact RWS charge, Harris makes the entire card look centrifugally unstable.

### Crowley’s description

Crowley calls him the fiery part of Air: wind and storm, immensely active, subtle, clever, and difficult to hold to a purpose. In *The Book of Thoth* he emphasizes the contradiction between high intellectual capacity and unstable or purposeless movement. [Crowley, *The Book of Thoth*, Knight of Swords](https://www.exosomatic.org/ENTER/Beliefs/Tarot/THE%20BOOK%20OF%20THOTH%20-%20Aleister%20Crowley.pdf).

### Tzaddi/Heh does not alter this card

Crowley’s famous “Tzaddi is not the Star” revision exchanges the Emperor and Star’s Hebrew-letter placement in his system. It concerns trumps IV and XVII. It does **not** change the Knight of Swords’ Fire-of-Air identity, Yod status, or Taurus–Gemini span.

## Notable modern redesigns

### Aquarian, Morgan-Greer, Robin Wood and comparable RWS descendants

**Tradition:** These generally retain the RWS charge, wind, uplifted sword, and psychological reading of speed or confrontation.

### Crowley-derived decks

**Tradition:** They retain Fire of Air, the Taurus–Gemini span, birds or winds, and two-bladed or storm imagery.

### Gender-inclusive and nonhierarchical decks

**Modern invention:** “Knight” may become Seeker, Explorer, Son, Child, Warrior, or elemental Agent. Some decks remove gender and feudal rank while retaining the role of rapid mental action.

### Jodorowsky–Costa Marseille reading

**Modern interpretation:** The Cavalier becomes an energetic transmitter of the sword suit—thought, language, discernment, or intellectual action—read through posture and direction rather than an imposed narrative scene.

# The game

## Original function

**Documented fact:** Tarot was created as a trick-taking game. The Knight of Swords began as a court card used in play, not as an oracle.

### Rank

In the ordinary four-court Latin suit:

1. King
2. Queen
3. Knight
4. Jack/Knave/Page

The Knight therefore beats all sword pips and the Jack but loses to the Queen and King, unless a particular game introduces exceptional rules.

### Point value

A common tarot-game valuation is:

- King: 5 points
- Queen: 4
- Knight: 3
- Jack: 2
- Other cards: 1 or no intrinsic point, depending on scoring method

In packet-counting systems, one subtracts a point per card or applies equivalent grouped calculations, so published “raw values” and final counted values can look different while producing the same total.

### Suit ranking

In many historic Italian tarot games:

- Swords and Batons rank from King downward to Ace low.
- Cups and Coins often reverse the pip order, Ace high among pips and Ten low.
- Courts remain above the pips.

The Knight of Swords is unaffected by the reversed pip ranking because it remains a court.

### French tarot and Tarock

French tarot retains the Cavalier as the third-ranking court and a 3-point card before counting deductions. Many central-European Tarock packs omit most Latin-suited imagery or use French suits, but where a Cavall survives it is the equivalent mounted court.

### Minchiate

Minchiate retains the sword court below Queen and above Page. Its forty trumps do not alter the Knight’s suit rank.

---

## Trump-order disputes: relevance and irrelevance

Historians reconstruct three broad early Italian trump-order traditions, conventionally associated with northern/central regional families. Justice, Strength, the virtues, Popes, Emperor, Angel, and World vary in position.

**Finding:** No known trump-order dispute places the Knight of Swords among the trumps. Its hierarchy is internal to Swords.

### The Fool

The Fool is normally unnumbered in early packs and serves a special trick-taking function rather than operating as an ordinary highest or lowest trump. Again, this does not affect the Knight’s fixed suit-court identity.

# Chronology of attestation

## Before tarot

- **Late fourteenth century — documented fact:** Playing cards are reported in several European cities under forms such as *naibi*.
- **Mamluk evidence — documented material / scholarly reconstruction:** The surviving Islamic card family supplies close structural parallels for suits and subordinate court ranks.
- **No exact mounted ancestor — finding:** The Mamluk courts are nonfigurative. The European horseman is a transformation, not a copied picture.

## Approximately 1420: Marziano da Tortona

Marziano’s game for Filippo Maria Visconti describes sixteen classical gods organized into four allegorical suits.

**Documented fact:** It is an early custom trump-like pack.

**Finding:** It contains no ordinary Knight of Swords and is not the standard 78-card tarot structure. It belongs to the prehistory of commissioned Italian triumph packs, not the direct attestation of this card.

## 1442: Ferrara ledger

**Documented fact:** An Este account records payment for *carte da trionfi*.

**Finding:** It names the game/deck, not the Knight individually.

## 1440s–1450s: Milanese survivals

**Documented fact:** Mounted sword courts survive in the Visconti-associated luxury-card corpus. This is the earliest physical evidence for the card type.

## Approximately 1470: *Sermo perutilis de ludo cum aliis*

**Documented fact:** The Dominican sermon condemns the game and lists its triumph subjects.

**Finding:** Its famous list concerns the trumps, not a separate iconographic explanation of the Knight of Swords.

## Late fifteenth century: Sola Busca

The named sword knight **Amone** gives the first surviving individualized, inscribed program for this court known to this survey.

## Approximately 1500: poems and literary appropriations

Tarot poems assign social or moral characteristics to cards and persons.

**Caution:** General references to *cavalli* or swordlike temperaments are not automatically documentary meanings for the specific Knight of Swords unless the poem expressly names the card.

## Teofilo Folengo, sixteenth century

Folengo’s tarot sonnets demonstrate literary use of tarot imagery.

**Finding:** They are evidence for cultural familiarity with tarot, not proof of the Golden Dawn-style elemental meaning of this court.

## Seventeenth–eighteenth centuries

Noblet, Dodal, and Conver stabilize the French title **Cavalier d’Épée** and the emblematic mounted design.

## 1781

Court de Gébelin and the Comte de Mellet publish occult-origin theories. Neither provides the later Golden Dawn Fire-of-Air system for the Knight.

## 1783–1789

Etteilla develops systematic tarot cartomancy and publishes his divinatory pack. The Knight becomes a numbered oracle sign, **Le Militaire**.

## 1888

Mathers publishes *The Tarot: Its Occult Signification, Use in Fortune-Telling, and Method of Play*. Card 52 is the Knight of Swords.

## Approximately 1888–1900

Golden Dawn Book T creates the decisive Fire-of-Air, Yod, Taurus–Gemini formulation.

## 1909–1910

Smith pictures the knight charging; Waite publishes the accompanying description and meanings.

## 1938–1943 / 1969

Harris paints and Crowley interprets the Thoth Knight of Swords; the complete deck is commercially published in 1969.

## Late twentieth century to present

Psychological, Jungian, therapeutic, feminist, queer, and nonhierarchical decks reinterpret the knight as a mode of mind or behavior rather than necessarily a young man or soldier.

## Conflicts explained

### Yod versus Vav

- In **Papus-style ordinary court order**, King may be Yod, Queen Heh, Knight Vav, Knave final Heh.
- In the **Golden Dawn**, the mounted traditional Knight is renamed **King** and assigned Yod/Fire; the seated traditional King becomes **Prince**, assigned Vav/Air.
- Waite restored ordinary printed ranks without explaining the switch.
- Crowley calls the mounted fiery figure Knight and retains Prince for Vav.

Therefore “Knight = Vav” and “Knight = Yod” can both occur, but they refer to different court-naming systems.

### Swords = Air

**Esoteric attribution:** The Golden Dawn firmly made Swords the elemental suit of Air.

**Earlier traditions:** French occult authors did not always use the same suit-element table. Some modern writers present Swords–Air as primordial; the documentation instead points to a nineteenth-century occult synthesis that became dominant through the Golden Dawn, Waite, and Crowley.

### Taurus in an Air court

The first ten degrees of the court’s span lie in earthy Taurus because Golden Dawn court spans cross sign boundaries. The card is not “Earth of Air”: its elemental formula remains Fire of Air. Taurus supplies the first decan segment; Gemini dominates the following twenty degrees.

# Divinatory meanings by author

## Etteilla

**Documented tradition:** **Le Militaire**—a military man, soldier, disciplined man of action.

Etteillist editions and derivative manuals differ in their secondary upright/reversed vocabulary. The reliable common core is the personification of a soldier or martial man. I could not establish a single universally authoritative reversed word from the 1789 printing because surviving scans and later “Grand Etteilla” editions do not always preserve identical captions.

**Finding:** Modern websites that give Etteilla “recklessness, burnout, misinformation” are importing post-Waite meanings unless they cite an early Etteillist edition.

## S. L. MacGregor Mathers, 1888

Mathers’s entry reads:

> “A Soldier, a man whose profession is arms, Skillfulness, Capacity, Address, Promptitude.”

Reversed:

> “A conceited fool, Ingenuousness, Simplicity.”

This is card 52 in his list. [Mathers, 1888 scan](https://www.howardnema.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/12/the-tarot-1888-by-macgregor-mathers-1.pdf).

“Ingenuousness” is surprising beside “conceited fool,” but it is what the text prints; later compilers sometimes silently change it to “ingenuity” or “incapacity.” Such changes should be treated as editorial corruption unless supported by an edition.

## A. E. Waite, 1910

Upright:

> “Skill, bravery, capacity, defence, address, enmity, wrath, war, destruction, opposition, resistance, ruin.”

Waite adds that “there is therefore a sense in which the card signifies death, but it carries this meaning only in its proximity to other cards of fatality.”

Reversed:

> “Imprudence, incapacity, extravagance.”

[Waite scan](https://www.mr-kaplan.com/Library/Waite/The%20Pictorial%20Key%20to%20the%20Tarot.pdf).

**Source relation:** Waite closely follows and expands Mathers’s “skill/capacity/address” vocabulary while Smith’s image greatly intensifies motion and conflict.

## Aleister Crowley, 1944

Crowley does not use a simple upright/reversed list. He describes a spectrum.

Constructively, the figure is active, clever, subtle, delicate, courageous, and skillful. Destructively, his ideas lack sustained purpose; he may be incapable of decision, unreliable, domineering, or trapped in futile mental turbulence.

Crowley’s governing image is the fiery part of Air—“wind” and “storm”—passing from Taurus into Gemini. [*Book of Thoth* scan](https://www.exosomatic.org/ENTER/Beliefs/Tarot/THE%20BOOK%20OF%20THOTH%20-%20Aleister%20Crowley.pdf).

## Paul Foster Case

Case treats knights as forces in motion and as indications of coming or going according to the direction faced. For the sword knight, this naturally emphasizes intellectual, martial, argumentative, or decisive action.

**Source caution:** BOTA’s detailed court teachings exist in multiple lesson forms. I did not locate a freely accessible first-edition Case page giving a canonical upright/reversed Knight-of-Swords list comparable to Waite’s. It would be unsafe to fabricate one from modern BOTA summaries.

## Rachel Pollack

In *Seventy-Eight Degrees of Wisdom* and later writings, Pollack treats the RWS Knight as mental energy thrown into action: courageous, direct, committed to ideas, but liable to substitute certainty or combat for reflection. Reversal or shadow manifests scattered aggression, fanaticism, or an inability to direct thought constructively.

**Modern Jungian-developmental reading:** Pollack’s knight is not confined to a literal young man. He can be a psychic stance, relationship dynamic, or stage of development.

## Mary K. Greer

Greer reads courts through roles, qualities, behavior, and inner subpersonalities. For reversals she explicitly discourages a single automatic antonym. A reversed court may be:

- blocked,
- excessive,
- undeveloped,
- rejected,
- private rather than public,
- operating in an environment that does not value its natural qualities.

Applied to the Knight of Swords, this can yield blocked assertion, excessive argument, suppressed decisiveness, reckless speech, or an inner mental crusader. [Greer on court cards and reversals](https://marykgreer.com/2016/03/23/using-the-court-cards/).

# Esoteric and symbolic readings

## Martial and social

**First layer, fifteenth century — documented:** A mounted armed retainer signifies social rank, cavalry, service, nobility, and military ability.

**Spreaders:** Italian and French card-makers through repeated court patterns.

No evidence is required to infer “knight” or “soldier”; those are visually and lexically present. Abstract meanings such as ideology, trauma, or communication appear later.

## Christian

**Historical context:** The knight could naturally evoke Christian chivalry, just war, crusading, saints, or romance literature.

**Waite’s explicit Christianization:** His comparison to **Galahad** moralizes the sword’s speed: decisive action becomes legitimate when governed by purity of heart.

**Caution:** A specific identification with Galahad does not appear on the Visconti or Marseille card. It is Waite’s interpretive gloss.

## Hermetic and elemental

**Golden Dawn innovation:** Fire acts through Air. The card is not merely “thought,” but thought ignited into motion—judgment, argument, invention, strategy, or violence.

The formula also explains its instability: Fire accelerates Air, while Air spreads Fire.

## Kabbalistic

### Golden Dawn/Thoth

- Yod: initiating, seminal, fiery force
- Chokmah: dynamic outpouring of force
- Swords: the Yetziratic or airy mental field in common Hermetic-Qabalistic exposition

**Caution:** This is Hermetic Qabalah, not evidence that medieval Jewish kabbalists designed the card.

### Papus

Papus uses the court sequence as a Tetragrammaton cycle. In his naming scheme the ordinary Knight commonly occupies Vav, the mediating or transmitted product of King and Queen.

**Conflict:** Golden Dawn nomenclature transfers the mounted card to Yod.

## Astrological

The Book T span is:

- 20°–30° Taurus: Saturn in Taurus / Seven of Pentacles
- 0°–10° Gemini: Jupiter in Gemini / Eight of Swords
- 10°–20° Gemini: Mars in Gemini / Nine of Swords

This makes the court a synthesis of endurance or restriction, expansive reasoning, and conflict/anxiety. Crowley emphasizes the transition from earthy Taurus into strongly airy Gemini.

**Caution:** Exact calendar dates vary by year and ephemeris. Popular fixed dates such as May 11–June 10 are approximations, not the attribution itself.

## Alchemical

### Sola Busca

**Scholarly reconstruction:** Amone/Ammon belongs to an Alexander-centered hermetic and pseudo-historical sequence. The sword courts may participate in a transformative program involving rulership, intellect, ordeal, and initiation.

### Golden Dawn and Crowley

Fire of Air can be read alchemically as ignition, volatilization, expansion, combustion, or the rapid separation produced by a sharp intellect.

**Finding:** I found no medieval alchemical text naming a tarot Knight of Swords as a laboratory operation. Those readings are Renaissance-humanist reconstruction in the Sola Busca case and modern Hermetic analogy in the Golden Dawn/Thoth case.

## Jungian

**Modern interpretation:** The card represents an archetypal psychic pattern: the warrior intellect, Logos in motion, the argumentative ego, the crusader, or the mind cutting through confusion.

Constructive pole:

- discrimination,
- courage to speak,
- strategic intelligence,
- rapid decision.

Shadow pole:

- inflation,
- compulsive certainty,
- weaponized language,
- dissociation from feeling,
- projection of enemies,
- action faster than integration.

**First appearance problem:** Jung did not publish a canonical interpretation of the Knight of Swords. “Jungian tarot” is a later application of archetypal psychology by tarot writers, therapists, and symbolic practitioners. Claims that Jung personally assigned this card a meaning are unsupported.

Calvino uses tarot cards as a narrative-generating grammar. Court cards can serve as characters, actions, or social roles rather than fixed divinatory meanings.

**Finding:** The work is a major tarot literary appropriation, but no exclusive, controlling Knight-of-Swords episode can be established without specifying which deck layout and passage is meant.

### T. S. Eliot, *The Waste Land*

Eliot’s Madame Sosostris names “the man with three staves,” “the drowned Phoenician Sailor,” and other invented or adapted tarot figures.

**Finding:** Eliot does not name the Knight of Swords. Connecting the card to the poem is comparative criticism, not direct quotation.

### W. B. Yeats

Yeats belonged to the Golden Dawn and participated in its symbolic culture.

**Finding:** That association makes Book T relevant to his milieu, but it does not prove a specific published Yeats interpretation of the Knight of Swords.

### Ingmar Bergman

*The Seventh Seal* supplies an iconic medieval knight and Death imagery often associated with tarot in popular culture.

**Finding:** It is not a documented adaptation of the Knight of Swords card.

### Jodorowsky

Jodorowsky’s films repeatedly use initiatory, alchemical, Christian, and tarot-like imagery. His most direct contribution to this card is the Camoin-Jodorowsky deck and the interpretive method published with Marianne Costa.

### Buildings and paintings

**Finding:** I found no securely documented major building designed specifically as a quotation of the Knight of Swords. Generic equestrian monuments, St. George paintings, crusader imagery, and cavalry paintings are visual analogues, not demonstrated tarot quotations.

# Controversies and disputes

## Egyptian origin

**Legend, first printed systematically in 1781:** Court de Gébelin claimed tarot preserved an ancient Egyptian book.

**Evidence offered:** speculative visual comparisons and erroneous imagined etymologies made before Egyptian hieroglyphs had been deciphered.

**Evidence against:**

- no ancient Egyptian tarot cards;
- no Egyptian text describing the pack;
- no documentary chain from Egypt to fifteenth-century tarot;
- the earliest physical tarot imagery belongs to Renaissance northern Italy;
- trionfi records describe cards and play.

The Comte de Mellet extended the argument toward Hebrew letters and divination. Etteilla converted the idea into a working occult pack. Papus later popularized it internationally. [Court de Gébelin text resource](https://www.melmothia.net/3519/du-jeu-des-tarots-antoine-court-de-gebelin-pdf/); [Dummett’s historical criticism](https://www.nybooks.com/articles/1981/05/14/origins-of-tarot/).

## Romani or “Gypsy” transmission

**Legend:** Romani wanderers carried the Egyptian Book of Thoth into Europe.

**History of the claim:** It developed from early-modern European misidentification of Romani people as Egyptians and was spread strongly by nineteenth-century writers, especially Papus’s *Tarot of the Bohemians*.

**Evidence:** None links Roma to the invention of tarot in fifteenth-century Italy.

**Important distinction:** Romani fortune-tellers later used playing cards, as did many European populations. Later use is not evidence of original invention.

Papus’s title is preserved by the Library of Congress as *The Tarot of the Bohemians: The Most Ancient Book in the World*. [Library of Congress record](https://www.loc.gov/item/77355368/).

## Cathar origin

**Disputed modern claim:** Tarot concealed Cathar dualist doctrine after the Albigensian Crusade.

**Problems:**

- no Cathar tarot deck survives;
- no medieval Cathar text mentions tarot;
- the documentary and material record appears in fifteenth-century northern Italian courts, well after the principal Cathar suppression;
- ordinary courts such as the Knight of Swords require no Cathar explanation.

Possible thematic resemblances are interpretive parallels, not transmission evidence.

## Templar origin

**Legend/modern invention:** Knights Templar encoded secret teachings in tarot or transmitted them from the East.

The Knight of Swords’ chivalric appearance makes it especially easy to enlist in this story.

**Finding:** No Templar inventory, rule, trial record, or contemporary chronicle names tarot. The order was dissolved in the early fourteenth century, before the securely documented Italian triumph packs.

## Kabbalistic antiquity

**Esoteric attribution presented as history:** The 22 trumps correspond to 22 Hebrew letters; therefore Jewish adepts created tarot.

**Documented development:** The explicit tarot-Hebrew linkage appears in eighteenth- and nineteenth-century French occultism, then becomes systematic in Lévi, Papus, the Golden Dawn, Waite, Case, and Crowley.

**Knight-specific finding:** Hebrew-letter paths properly belong to the 22 trumps in these systems. The Knight’s Yod or Vav is a separate Tetragrammaton/elemental court attribution, not one of the 22 Tree-of-Life paths.

## Visconti portrait claims

**Disputed:** The Knight of Swords is sometimes assigned to a named Visconti or Sforza family member.

**Evidence for:** dynastic emblems, court commission, costume, family context.

**Evidence lacking:** identifying inscription, portrait contract, or close corroborating portrait with documented identity.

**Best conclusion:** A dynastic court figure, possibly idealized or portrait-like, but not securely named.

## “Scimitars prove Egyptian origin”

**Disputed inference:** Curved sword forms are taken as proof of direct Egyptian occult transmission.

**Correction:** Curved or S-shaped sword emblems support the broader historical relationship between Islamic and Latin playing-card suits. They do not support the Book-of-Thoth story. Material transmission of playing-card forms and an occult Egyptian priesthood are entirely different claims.

## Misprints and editorial mutations

### “Ingenuousness”

Mathers’s reversed meaning includes “Ingenuousness.” Later writers sometimes alter it because it seems inconsistent with “conceited fool.” The awkward original should be preserved unless a critical edition demonstrates a printer’s error.

### 20° versus 21st degree

“20° Taurus” and “the 21st degree of Taurus” are equivalent boundary conventions. Treating them as different zodiac assignments creates a false dispute.

### King/Knight/Prince

The most consequential inherited confusion arises from changing court names between ordinary tarot, Golden Dawn manuscripts, Waite, and Crowley. Modern tables frequently attach the Prince’s Vav/Air status to Waite’s mounted Knight. That is wrong within the Golden Dawn’s own structural system.

### Grimaud and restoration colors

Industrial redrawings sometimes regularize lines, facial features, suit emblems, or colors. Later interpreters may treat those printing contingencies as ancient encoded symbolism.

### Camoin-Jodorowsky “restoration”

Their deck is historically informed but necessarily synthetic. No single surviving archetype contains every reconstructed line and color choice.

# Open questions

1. **Which surviving mounted sword court is absolutely earliest?**  
   Cary-Yale/Visconti di Modrone is a leading candidate, but the relative chronology of early Visconti-associated fragments remains debated.

2. **Was the European Knight a direct translation of the Mamluk deputy?**  
   Structurally plausible, but the addition of the Queen and the allocation of the two subordinate male ranks were European transformations. No transitional card labels the change.

3. **Does the Visconti-Sforza Knight depict a particular person?**  
   No secure contemporary identification has been found.

4. **How much of the Golden Dawn Book T court system was Mathers’s own construction?**  
   Manuscript attribution and collaborative order development remain complicated. “Book T” represents Golden Dawn doctrine even where individual authorship is uncertain.

5. **How independently did Pamela Colman Smith design the card?**  
   Book T explains the elemental temperament, but surviving documentation does not allocate every visual choice between Waite and Smith.

6. **Was the Sola Busca Knight consciously alchemical?**  
   Its named humanist program is documented. The full alchemical reading is a substantial scholarly reconstruction, persuasive in parts but not accompanied by a surviving maker’s key.

7. **What was Etteilla’s exact first-edition reversed keyword?**  
   Later Grand Etteilla printings and manuals vary. Establishing the earliest form requires collation of specific surviving 1780s cards and manuals.

8. **When did “reckless intellectual” become the dominant popular meaning?**  
   It is implicit in Golden Dawn/Crowley polarity and strongly suggested by Smith’s picture, but the familiar modern keyword cluster developed cumulatively through twentieth-century guidebooks.

9. **Did Lévi give a card-specific Knight-of-Swords formula?**  
   I found no passage in *Dogme et Rituel* equivalent to Book T’s Fire-of-Air and Taurus–Gemini assignment. Claims that Lévi taught the complete Golden Dawn court table appear retrospective.

10. **Is there a documented Knight-of-Swords quotation in architecture or canonical painting?**  
    None was located. Numerous visual analogues exist, but analogy is not documented quotation.
