# Five of Swords: Research Dossier

## Method and evidence labels

This dossier distinguishes:

- **Documented fact** — supported by a dated object, archival record, rule book, or primary text.
- **Scholarly reconstruction** — an inference argued from surviving objects and documents.
- **Tradition** — a stable practice whose precise beginning is uncertain.
- **Esoteric attribution** — a deliberately constructed occult correspondence.
- **Disputed claim** — proposed but contested or weakly evidenced.
- **Legend** — a historical narrative contradicted or unsupported by documentary evidence.
- **Modern invention** — a recent interpretation or design retrospectively presented as ancient.

A crucial limitation applies: many fifteenth-century decks are fragmentary. Evidence that a *deck* once contained numbered Swords does not establish that its Five of Swords survives.

## Basic identification

| Field | Identification |
|---|---|
| Card | Five of Swords |
| Type | Number/pip card, not a trump |
| Conventional number | Five; V in many historic and modern decks; sometimes 5 in indexed gaming packs |
| Italian | **Cinque di Spade**; historically a card among the *spade* in *carte da trionfi*, later *tarocchi* |
| French | **Cinq d’Épées** in Italian-suited tarot; **cinq de pique** only when translated into the French suit system |
| Spanish | **Cinco de Espadas** |
| German | **Fünf der Schwerter**, *Schwert-Fünf*; in French-suited Tarock its functional analogue is the **Pik-Fünf** |
| English | Five of Swords |
| Etteilla | A reordered numbered card in the Grand Etteilla family; later editions vary in card numbering and captions |
| Golden Dawn | **Lord of Defeat** |
| Thoth | **Defeat** |
| Golden Dawn astrology | Venus in the first decan of Aquarius, 0°–10° Aquarius |
| Golden Dawn Qabalah | Geburah, the fifth sephirah, in Yetzirah, the formative world assigned to Swords |
| Suit element | Air in the Golden Dawn/Waite/Thoth system |
| Tetragrammaton letter | Vav/Vau in Lévi’s and Papus’s arrangement of the suits; Golden Dawn usage is more properly the whole Yetziratic suit-world rather than a Hebrew letter assigned individually to this card |
| Earliest securely identified surviving complete-deck example | The engraved and painted **Sola Busca Five of Swords**, from the deck engraved around 1490 and illuminated in Venice in 1491 |
| Earliest tarot context | Fifteenth-century northern Italian *carte da trionfi*; the suit of Swords is explicitly named in the 1442 Este accounts, although an individual Five is not named |

The Brera describes Sola Busca as a 78-card deck of twenty-two triumphs and fifty-six cards in Coins, Swords, Batons, and Cups, printed from engravings and illuminated in Venice in 1491. The British Museum calls its engraved series the earliest complete surviving true tarot pack and the earliest such pack in engraving. Those statements support Sola Busca as the earliest *securely catalogued surviving Five of Swords in a complete tarot*, not necessarily the first ever made. [Pinacoteca di Brera](https://pinacotecabrera.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/11/Il-Segreto-dei-tarocchi-eng.pdf), [British Museum](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/P_1845-0825-485)

### A nomenclature correction

**Documented fact:** *trionfi* originally denoted the added triumph/trump cards or the game containing them. *Tarocchi* appears later in the early sixteenth century as the ordinary Italian name for the pack/game. Neither term was the private name of the Five of Swords.

**Not applicable to this card:** Papess/Juno, Pope/Jupiter, Maison Dieu, Justice/Strength order, and the Fool’s numbering are trump questions. They matter to the history of tarot packs but never changed the identity or suit rank of the Five of Swords. Besançon’s Juno and Jupiter substitutions likewise affect only trumps II and V.

# Iconography deck by deck

## Fifteenth-century Milan

### Visconti di Modrone / Cary–Yale, approximately 1440s

**Documented fact:** Sixty-nine cards survive at Yale from a luxury hand-painted Milanese pack probably made before Filippo Maria Visconti’s death in 1447. The pack had unusual additional female court cards, making reconstruction of its original size controversial. The surviving cards establish an early Swords suit.

**Finding:** I could not verify from Yale’s accessible catalogue that an original Five of Swords survives. Modern “complete” Cary–Yale editions supply reconstructed cards and must not be cited as fifteenth-century originals.

**Likely original appearance — scholarly reconstruction:** numbered Swords arranged as gilded suit emblems, without a narrative scene. This follows the practice visible in comparable Milanese suited cards, but is not a description of a verified surviving Cary–Yale Five. [Metropolitan Museum overview](https://www.metmuseum.org/fr/perspectives/tarot-2)

### Brambilla deck, approximately 1440s, Milan

**Documented fact:** Forty-eight surviving cards are held by the Pinacoteca di Brera and are associated with Filippo Maria Visconti. They are lavishly painted luxury cards.

**Finding:** no securely catalogued Brambilla Five of Swords was located in the accessible Brera material. Later facsimiles commonly reconstruct absent cards. The Brera itself distinguishes the forty-eight-card Brambilla fragment from its complete Sola Busca deck. [Pinacoteca di Brera](https://pinacotecabrera.org/en/news/mostra/il-segreto-dei-segreti-i-tarocchi-sola-busca/)

### Visconti–Sforza / Pierpont Morgan–Bergamo group, approximately 1450–1480

**Documented fact:** Seventy-four cards survive across the Morgan Library, Accademia Carrara, and Colleoni holdings. The Morgan owns the Ace and Ten of Swords, but not a Five. Its catalogue expressly describes a 78-card pack with ten number cards and four courts in each suit. [Morgan Library collection](https://www.themorgan.org/collection/tarot-cards), [Morgan catalogue](https://www.themorgan.org/manuscript/158525)

**Iconographic reconstruction:** a Five, if made in the same workshop, would probably have shown five crossed or interlaced straight swords against a gold-ground luxury-card field. It would not have depicted Waite’s three human figures. This remains reconstruction, not a surviving object description.

**Misidentification warning:** modern Visconti–Sforza decks print newly painted replacements for missing cards. Their Five of Swords is a twentieth- or twenty-first-century completion.

## Fifteenth-century Ferrara and related sheets

### Este fragments

**Documented fact:** The Este court accounts explicitly paid for painted Cups, Swords, Coins, Batons, and “all the figures” in four packs of *carte da trionfi* in 1442. This demonstrates that a Five of Swords belonged structurally to those packs, assuming the standard ten-pip sequence, but the account does not name it individually. The recorded phrase includes “coppe, spade, denari e bastoni.” [1442 transcription and translation](https://www.oocities.org/autorbis/ferrara1442.html)

Surviving Este cards are fragments. I found no museum record securely identifying an original Este Five of Swords.

### Rosenwald and Budapest sheets, late fifteenth or early sixteenth century

These uncut or partly preserved printed sheets are important evidence for popular, repeatable tarot production after the hand-painted court decks.

**Documented fact:** their surviving imagery chiefly informs the history of early trump and court patterns.

**Finding:** accessible catalogue material did not yield a securely isolated, fully documented Five of Swords. It would be unsafe to describe a specific arrangement without consulting the sheets directly at object resolution.

### “Mantegna Tarocchi,” approximately 1460s

**Documented fact:** these are two engraved series of fifty didactic images—social estates, Apollo and the Muses, liberal arts, virtues, cosmological spheres—not playing-card tarot packs.

**Conclusion:** no Five of Swords exists in them. Their traditional name “Mantegna tarocchi” is a later misnomer, and their attribution to Andrea Mantegna is rejected. They may illuminate Renaissance emblematic culture but not the direct genealogy of this pip.

## Sola Busca, engraved approximately 1490; illuminated Venice, 1491

**Documented fact:** this is the earliest complete surviving tarot, and the first surviving pack to give every number card a substantial figural scene. The Brera attributes its illumination to Venice in 1491, probably for an owner connected with the Venier and Sanudo families; the engraving’s artist and precise production center remain debated. [Brera exhibition text](https://pinacotecabrera.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/11/Il-Segreto-dei-tarocchi-eng.pdf)

### What is depicted

The Sola Busca Five of Swords is not a Marseille lattice. Five large weapons dominate a scene involving a human figure and military or workshop-like action. Like other Sola Busca pips, the suit objects function as physical implements within an enigmatic narrative rather than as a purely abstract count.

Exact identifications of what the figure is doing vary among modern commentators. The deck’s larger program has been read through Roman history, biblical exempla, Hermeticism, and alchemy.

- **Documented:** figural, narrative pips; five physical swords; 1491 illumination.
- **Scholarly reconstruction:** the deck may encode a learned Hermetic-alchemical program.
- **Disputed:** identification of every pip as a precise alchemical operation.
- **Unsupported:** treating later divinatory keywords as the original explanation of the scene.

### Was it Pamela Colman Smith’s source?

**Partly verified, but the item-specific claim is too strong.**

The Sola Busca was exhibited at the British Museum in 1908, immediately before Smith drew the Waite deck, and several Waite–Smith minors show persuasive compositional affinities with Sola Busca cards—most famously the Three of Swords and Ten of Wands.

For the **Five of Swords specifically**, however, Smith’s three-figure battlefield scene is not a close copy of the Sola Busca Five. No letter, diary, design note, or statement by Smith or Waite identifies it as her source. Thus:

- **Documented:** Smith could have seen Sola Busca material in London; conspicuous parallels occur elsewhere in her minors.
- **Scholarly reconstruction:** Sola Busca helped demonstrate that narrative minors were possible and probably influenced the deck as a whole.
- **Disputed/overstated:** “the Sola Busca Five of Swords is the direct source of Smith’s Five.”
- **More immediate source:** Golden Dawn’s title and meaning, *Lord of Defeat*, explain Smith’s subject more securely than the Sola Busca composition.

## Minchiate, Florence, sixteenth century onward

Minchiate expanded tarot to ninety-seven cards by adding zodiacal, elemental, and virtue trumps. Its ordinary suits remained Coins, Cups, Batons, and Swords.

The Five of Swords retained a conventional pip structure: five sword signs, not an independent allegorical tableau. In play, Swords were a “long” suit: 10 high down to Ace low. [Minchiate rules](https://www.pagat.com/tarot/minchiate.html)

The number five in Minchiate carries no documented intrinsic occult meaning. Later numerological interpretations are imports from occult tarot.

---

## Bolognese tarot

The Bolognese pack was shortened for play by suppressing low numeral cards. In the traditional 62-card form, the 2–5 of each suit are absent. Consequently:

- **Historical full-pack inference:** an early Bolognese 78-card form would have included a Five of Swords.
- **Documented later game:** the standard reduced pack does not.
- **Iconography:** where present in reconstructed or full variants, it is a conventional five-sword pip, not a Waite-style scene.

---

## Sicilian tarot

The 63-card Sicilian pack removes A–4 from Swords, Batons, and Cups, making the Five the **lowest surviving numeral** in those suits. It is indexed “5 S” or an equivalent local form.

The suit is *spade*. Its rank is below 6 and above nothing in its suit. It is an ordinary low-value card, not a trump or special counting card. [Sicilian Tarocchi rules](https://www.pagat.com/tarot/sicilian.html)

## Tarot de Marseille lineage

### General construction

Marseille Swords are conventionally curved, scimitar-like bands. Strictly speaking, the curved forms on the pip cards are highly stylized scabbards, blades, or weapon units rather than naturalistic swords. They interlace to form a pointed oval or mandorla.

On odd Swords cards, one straight, fully realized central sword passes through the oval formed by four curved units. Floral sprays and leaves occupy openings and corners.

This is a counting design, not originally a picture of combat. [Tarot de Marseille overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tarot_of_Marseilles)

### Jean Noblet, Paris, approximately 1650–1659

The BnF identifies Noblet as the oldest known substantially surviving deck conforming to what is conventionally called the Tarot de Marseille pattern, even though it was made in Paris. [BnF](https://essentiels.bnf.fr/fr/image/4799f2d6-6f88-4f6f-a15c-40a1a943c95e-tarot-jean-noblet)

The Five survives. It shows:

- four curved interlaced sword units forming an almond;
- one straight sword on the vertical axis;
- restrained floral ornaments;
- no persons, landscape, defeat, or divinatory caption.

### Jean Dodal, Lyon, approximately 1701–1715

Dodal preserves the same basic Type-I grammar: four curved weapons around a central straight sword, colored floral terminals, and carved linework. Differences are stylistic—line, color, hilt shape, and vegetation—not a change of subject.

### Pierre Madenié, Dijon, 1709

Later Type-II Five-of-Swords designs develop paired leafy or laurel-like sprays behind or beside the central sword. Their appearance is an important warning: botanical details that modern writers assign fixed meanings were historically variable.

### Nicolas Conver, Marseille, 1760

Conver stabilizes the influential Marseille Type-II pattern:

- four curved blue/red sword bands;
- a central vertical sword with a distinct hilt;
- foliage in and around the central opening;
- stencil coloring.

A French museum record dates Conver’s woodblocks to 1760 and notes continued production by his successors and, after 1880, by Camoin. [French Ministry of Culture catalogue](https://pop.culture.gouv.fr/notice/joconde/M0277000850)

### Grimaud / Paul Marteau, 1930s

Grimaud’s *Ancien Tarot de Marseille*, associated with Paul Marteau, regularized line and color for mass printing. Marteau later interpreted the four curved swords as structured material limitation and the central fifth sword as active mental force escaping or penetrating it.

That reading is:

- **Documented twentieth-century interpretation;**
- **not documented as the intention of Noblet, Dodal, or Conver;**
- sometimes mistakenly presented as an ancient Marseille doctrine.

### Camoin–Jodorowsky, 1997

Philippe Camoin and Alejandro Jodorowsky produced a restored/reconstructed Marseille deck using historical exemplars and editorial choices.

The Five remains a central sword within four curved swords and vegetation. Jodorowsky and Marianne Costa read Marseille pips structurally through number, suit, symmetry, growth, and receptivity.

- **Tradition/modern system:** five as crisis, novelty, temptation, or passage beyond stable four.
- **Modern invention:** treating every restored color or tiny feature as the recovery of a single primordial tarot code.
- **Disputed:** the claim that the reconstruction restores an original, esoterically exact master deck.

## Besançon pattern

The Besançon pattern substituted Juno and Jupiter for the Papess and Pope, primarily in Swiss, Alsatian, and southern German markets from the eighteenth century.

The Five of Swords was not renamed. Its relevance is only that it continued the Italian-suited pip pattern in the same pack. No “Juno Five” or “Jupiter Five” exists.

---

## Grand Etteilla, 1789 and later states

Etteilla’s deck was designed for occult and cartomantic use. Its cards were reordered, numbered continuously, and furnished with upright and reversed keywords. Later “Grand Etteilla” editions do not always agree in numbering, captions, or imagery.

The card corresponding to the Five of Swords remains associated with mourning, grief, loss, or affliction. Some editions place emblematic figures or inscriptions around the five suit signs rather than presenting a Marseille lattice.

**Caution:** online tables frequently conflate:

1. Etteilla’s 1770 ordinary-playing-card meanings;
2. his 1783–1785 tarot writings;
3. the 1789 deck;
4. nineteenth-century “Etteilla II/III” revisions;
5. Mathers’s 1888 English digest.

An exact original 1789 plate/caption for this card could not be securely recovered from an authoritative digital facsimile during this investigation. “Mourning” is securely transmitted through Mathers, who says his additional observations are “chiefly taken from Etteilla,” but this is not the same as locating Etteilla’s autograph wording.

---

## Oswald Wirth, 1889

Wirth’s original twenty-two-card deck, drawn for Stanislas de Guaita, contains **trumps only**. It has no Five of Swords.

Modern “Wirth” 78-card editions supply minors devised by later publishers or artists. They must not be attributed to Wirth’s 1889 deck.

## Golden Dawn Book T, approximately 1888–1900; published 1912 and later

### Design specification

Book T calls the card **“The Lord of Defeat.”** It assigns:

- 0°–10° Aquarius;
- Venus;
- Geburah of Vav/Yetzirah, in the suit of Swords;
- five swords whose points meet;
- the signs of Venus and Aquarius above and below.

The manuscript description states that the sword-points “just touch one another,” with the central sword not completely dividing the others. The geometry supplies the visual equivalent of unstable, frustrated force. [Regardie, *The Complete Golden Dawn*, Book T](https://s3.us-west-1.wasabisys.com/luminist/EB/R/Regardie%20-%20The%20Complete%20Golden%20Dawn.pdf)

### Wang and Cicero reconstructions

- **Robert Wang, Golden Dawn Tarot, 1978:** reconstructs the Book T scheme in elemental colors, with five swords, astrological sigils, and Golden Dawn color-scale conventions.
- **Chic and Sandra Tabatha Cicero, Golden Dawn Magical Tarot, 2000:** another ritual-order reconstruction, more explicitly diagrammatic and colored according to published Golden Dawn instructions.

Neither is an original nineteenth-century Golden Dawn deck. Both are modern visual realizations of surviving written specifications.

## Waite–Smith, drawn 1909; book 1910

Pamela Colman Smith replaced the traditional pip lattice with a scene:

- a foreground man turns toward two retreating figures;
- he holds three swords;
- two swords lie abandoned on the ground;
- water and distant land occupy the background;
- torn, wind-driven clouds intensify the emotional discord;
- his expression may be read as disdainful, smug, watchful, or uneasy.

Waite’s own description is decisive:

> “A disdainful man looks after two retreating and dejected figures.”

He says the man is “master in possession of the field.” His meanings are:

> “Degradation, destruction, revocation, infamy, dishonour, loss…”

Reversed:

> “The same; burial and obsequies.”

These quotations occur in *The Pictorial Key to the Tarot* (London: William Rider, 1910), Part III, “Five of Swords.” [Digital text](https://citeseerx.ist.psu.edu/document?doi=7a15961664bced51d1162198bce46d173e40dd41&repid=rep1&type=pdf)

### Meaning of the redesign

Smith converted the Golden Dawn’s abstract “Defeat” into a morally ambiguous aftermath. The foreground figure appears victorious in possession of the weapons, but Waite’s keywords attach dishonor and loss to the card as a whole.

“Pyrrhic victory,” “winning the argument but losing the relationship,” “bullying,” and “hollow victory” are useful modern syntheses. They are **not Waite’s own phrases**.

## Paul Foster Case / BOTA

The BOTA Minor Arcana are non-scenic and intended to be colored by the student. The Five therefore moves back toward an emblematic arrangement rather than copying Smith’s narrative.

Case’s *Oracle of the Tarot* gives the keyword **Defeat** and treats the card as failure, adversity, poverty, anxiety, and possible slander. His imagery includes a scourge and ankh as signs of suffering and karmic instruction.

Case assigns the time to approximately January 20–29, but describes Aquarius under Saturn and Uranus rather than foregrounding the Golden Dawn’s Venus decan lord. His interpretive text nevertheless retains the Golden Dawn title. [Case, *Oracle of the Tarot*, p. 32](https://media-01.imu.nl/storage/divona.be/17547/oracle-of-tarot-paul-foster-case.pdf)

---

## Thoth, painted 1938–1943; first fully published 1969

Frieda Harris renders five swords as a disintegrating geometric structure:

- mismatched hilts form an inverted pentagram;
- blades bend, break, or droop;
- their points fail to create stable unity;
- the rose of the Four of Swords has broken apart;
- Venus and Aquarius sigils identify the decan;
- the title is **DEFEAT**.

Crowley writes:

> “Geburah, as always, produces disruption; but as Venus here rules Aquarius, weakness rather than excess of strength seems the cause of disaster.”

He continues:

> “The intellect has been enfeebled by sentiment. The defeat is due to pacifism. Treachery also may be implied.”

And of the design:

> “The hilts of the swords form the inverted pentagram…”

*The Book of Thoth* (London: O.T.O., 1944), p. 206. [Facsimile PDF](https://www.rahoorkhuit.net/library/crowley/equinox/volume3/vol_3_no_05.pdf)

Crowley’s subsequent historical-religious polemic on the page is his personal Thelemic interpretation and includes explicitly antisemitic and anti-Christian language. It is evidence for Crowley’s worldview, not evidence about the card’s Renaissance origin.

### Tzaddi/Heh issue

Crowley’s “Tzaddi is not the Star” revision exchanges the Emperor and Star’s Hebrew-letter relations. It affects trumps IV and XVII, not the Five of Swords. Venus in Aquarius and Geburah remain unchanged.

## Selected modern redesigns

### Aquarian Tarot, David Palladini, 1970

A stylized modernist adaptation of the Waite–Smith narrative. The emotional conflict is retained, with Art Deco/psychedelic simplification.

### Morgan–Greer Tarot, Bill Greer and Lloyd Morgan, 1979

A close-cropped Waite-derived scene intensifies facial emotion and confrontation. This helped establish the foreground victor as the psychological focus.

### DruidCraft, Robin Wood, Modern Witch, Light Seer’s, and similar decks

Common redesigns reinterpret the scene as:

- bullying or abuse;
- interpersonal rupture;
- shame after conflict;
- internal self-sabotage;
- withdrawal from an unwinnable struggle;
- social exclusion.

These are **modern developments of Smith’s scene**, not meanings recoverable from medieval pip arrangements.

# The game

## Original function

**Documented fact:** tarot originated as a trick-taking game. A standard early pack contained twenty-one permanent trumps, the Fool, and fifty-six suited cards. The Morgan explicitly notes that Renaissance cards were for play, not originally fortune-telling. [Morgan Library](https://www.themorgan.org/manuscript/158525)

The Five of Swords is never a trump. It must follow Swords when that suit is led, subject to the rules of the particular game.

## Rank

In the traditional Italian “long” suits, Swords and Batons rank:

**King, Queen, Knight, Jack, 10, 9, 8, 7, 6, 5, 4, 3, 2, Ace.**

Thus the Five:

- beats 4, 3, 2, and Ace;
- loses to 6–10 and every Sword court;
- is beaten by any trump when legally trumped.

Cups and Coins traditionally reverse the numeral direction. Dummett’s account of this long-suit/round-suit structure is reproduced in extracts from *The Game of Tarot*. [Dummett excerpt](https://forum.tarothistory.com/viewtopic.php?t=1175)

## Point value

The Five is ordinarily a low or “empty” card:

- often worth no independent card point in early Italian scoring;
- in modern French-style half-point counting, ordinary pips count effectively as 0.5 when cards are paired for scoring;
- not an *oudler*, king, queen, knight, or jack.

Its strategic value depends on holding length, voiding the suit, and drawing trumps—not on iconographic meaning.

## Regional examples

- **Minchiate:** long-suit rank, 10 high to Ace low; the Five is mid-low.
- **Sicilian Tarocchi:** A–4 are removed, so Five is the lowest Sword.
- **Bolognese Tarocchino:** Five is removed with other low pips.
- **French Tarot/Tarock:** the Italian Sword suit may be replaced by Spades. In black suits, Five normally ranks naturally between 4 and 6.
- **Austrian Tarock:** many shortened packs retain only high and/or low suit cards; a Five may be absent entirely.

## Trump-order disputes

Dummett classified early Italian trump orders into regional groups A, B, and C. Justice, Strength, the Angel/Judgment, and World vary; the Fool has an exceptional role and is generally outside the normal trump sequence.

None of these disputes changes the Five of Swords. Its plain-suit rank is governed by suit-order rules, not trump-order iconography.

### Main conflicts

1. **Swords as Air or Fire.**  
   Golden Dawn, Waite, Case, and Thoth use Swords = Air. Lévi’s sexualized Tetragrammaton symbolism does not map cleanly onto the later Golden Dawn elemental scheme, and French occultists sometimes assign Sword to Fire.

2. **Vav.**  
   Lévi explicitly writes that the Sword is represented by Vau. This is a **suit-level** assignment, not the individual Hebrew letter of the Five. Saying “the Five of Swords’ Hebrew letter is Vav” compresses two different systems.

3. **Geburah.**  
   Lévi groups all four Fives under the fifth sephirah, Geburah. The Golden Dawn develops this as Geburah operating in Yetzirah/Swords.

4. **Astrological decan.**  
   Venus in Aquarius is Golden Dawn, not medieval, Marseille, Etteilla, Lévi, or Papus tradition. It is verified in Book T and retained unchanged by Crowley.

5. **Court de Gébelin/Mellet letters.**  
   Their alphabetic schemes concern the twenty-two trumps. Applying one of those letters directly to the Five is a category error.

6. **Tzaddi/Heh.**  
   Crowley’s Emperor/Star revision has no effect on this pip.

### Lévi in his own words

In *Dogme et Rituel de la Haute Magie*, vol. II, chapter 10, Lévi writes:

> “the Sword is the conjunction of both, or the lingam, represented in Hebrew … by Vau”

He then groups the four Fives under:

> “5 GEBURAH”

and glosses them poetically as divine justice avenging sins. [English text](https://www.hermetics.org/pdf/Eliphas_Levi_Dogma%20et%20Rituel%20de%20la%20Haute%20Magie%201.pdf)

This is the first clearly traced printed source in this research for the paired claims **Sword–Vav** and **Five–Geburah**.

### Papus in his own words

Papus calls the Sword:

> “The Vau or Sword”

and integrates the suits into a fourfold Yod–He–Vau–He cycle. His *Tarot of the Bohemians* was first published in French in 1889; the cited English edition is London: George Redway, 1896. [Library of Congress facsimile](https://www.loc.gov/resource/gdcmassbookdig.tarotofbohemians00papu_0/)

Papus did not originate *Lord of Defeat* or Venus in Aquarius. Those belong to the Golden Dawn’s Book T.

# Divinatory meanings by author

## Etteilla, 1780s

**Best reconstructable attribution:** mourning (*deuil*), grief or sadness, affliction; reversed, loss and trouble, with little or no amelioration.

**Caution:** a primary 1789 plate with stable card number and wording was not recovered. These meanings are securely transmitted through Mathers’s explicitly Etteilla-dependent table but should not be placed in quotation marks as Etteilla’s verified exact wording.

## S. L. MacGregor Mathers, 1888

From *The Tarot: Its Occult Signification, Use in Fortune-Telling, and Method of Play*, p. 17 in the digitized edition:

> “Mourning, Sadness, Affliction.”

Reversed:

> “Losses, Trouble (same signification, whether reversed or not.)”

Mathers states that his additional cartomantic observations are “chiefly taken from Etteilla.” [Facsimile, lines 529–530](https://www.howardnema.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/12/the-tarot-1888-by-macgregor-mathers-1.pdf)

## Golden Dawn Book T

The transmitted meanings include contest, loss, defeat, anxiety, dishonor, and malicious or treacherous action, modified by dignity. Book T does not use Waite’s simple modern “upright/reversed” binary; elemental dignity determines whether the expression is relatively constructive or destructive.

## A. E. Waite, 1910

Upright:

> “Degradation, destruction, revocation, infamy, dishonour, loss, with the variants and analogues of these.”

Reversed:

> “The same; burial and obsequies.”

Waite’s wording is strikingly close to the older Etteilla/Mathers mourning-and-loss complex but stages it through Smith’s battlefield aftermath. [*Pictorial Key*](https://citeseerx.ist.psu.edu/document?doi=7a15961664bced51d1162198bce46d173e40dd41&repid=rep1&type=pdf)

## Aleister Crowley, 1944

Crowley does not emphasize reversed cards. His operative meanings are:

- weakness producing disaster;
- intellect weakened by sentiment;
- defeat associated with pacifism;
- possible treachery;
- collapse and disintegration.

His interpretation should be quoted with caution because the following historical analogy becomes sectarian and racist polemic. [*Book of Thoth*, p. 206](https://www.rahoorkhuit.net/library/crowley/equinox/volume3/vol_3_no_05.pdf)

## Paul Foster Case, 1933

Well dignified:

> “Decision adverse to Querant in any matter of material nature”

but an apparent spiritual defeat may eventually produce a favorable result. In all cases Case gives:

> “failure, defeat, trouble, poverty, and anxiety.”

Ill dignified, the trouble is intensified and more directly produced by the querent’s failings, especially avarice. Keyword: **Defeat**. [*Oracle of the Tarot*, p. 32](https://media-01.imu.nl/storage/divona.be/17547/oracle-of-tarot-paul-foster-case.pdf)

## Rachel Pollack

In *Seventy-Eight Degrees of Wisdom* (1980 combined edition), Pollack develops the Waite image as conflict in which victory itself is suspect: humiliation, separation, and the possibility that the apparent winner has damaged the human relationship or acted from hostility.

**Copyright limitation:** an accessible authorized text was not found from which to verify a short exact quotation. The above is a paraphrase, not Pollack’s wording.

## Mary K. Greer

Greer’s reversal method permits several operations: opposite, blocked, internalized, delayed, or excessive energy. Applied to the Five of Swords, modern Greer-influenced readings include internal conflict, resentment, unfinished hostility, making amends, or ending a destructive contest.

**Finding:** I did not locate an accessible authorized page establishing a single canonical Greer upright/reversed wording for this card, so no purported verbatim quotation is supplied.

# Esoteric and symbolic readings

## Kabbalistic

**First clearly traced form:** Lévi, 1854–1856.

Five is Geburah: severity, judgment, limitation, force, and cutting. Swords are the cutting or discriminating suit. The combined formula therefore makes “severity in the realm of intellect” a natural later reading.

The Golden Dawn sharpens this into **Geburah in Yetzirah**:

- Geburah supplies disruption and severity;
- Yetzirah is the formative world and the world allocated to Air/Swords;
- hence mental conflict, hostile reasoning, defeat, or disintegration.

This is an occult construction, not a meaning documented in Renaissance play.

## Astrological

**First securely traced card-specific attribution:** Golden Dawn Book T.

Venus in Aquarius appears paradoxical:

- Venus seeks attraction, agreement, and cohesion;
- Aquarius is fixed Air, abstraction, systems, collective ideals, and intellectual distance;
- in Geburah, cohesion is strained by severity.

The Golden Dawn labels the result Defeat. Crowley interprets the failure as weakness and sentiment compromising intellect.

Dates such as January 20–29 or 20–30 are modern calendar approximations. They vary with ephemerides and with whether an author uses inclusive civil dates.

## Hermetic and alchemical

Sola Busca’s larger program has been interpreted by Brera scholars through late-fifteenth-century Hermetic and alchemical culture. For the Five specifically, proposed operations must remain **scholarly reconstruction** unless tied to a contemporary explanatory text.

In later occult tarot:

- crossed swords can represent opposed forces;
- the center can act as a vessel of synthesis or breakdown;
- Crowley’s broken rose signifies the destruction of the Four’s temporary truce;
- the inverted pentagram signifies unbalanced or materialized force.

The broken rose and inverted pentagram are Crowley/Harris features, not ancient Sola Busca or Marseille meanings.

## Christian

Early tarot’s trump imagery is readily explicable within Renaissance Christian moral culture, but the Five of Swords is a suit-count card, not a Christian allegory.

Later Christianizing readings treat it as:

- the sin of pride;
- violence of speech;
- false victory;
- humiliation followed by repentance;
- “those who take the sword” imagery.

No medieval sermon or theological commentary was found applying those themes specifically to the Five. They are later homiletic readings.

## Jungian

Jung did not publish a systematic tarot interpretation, and no authentic Jungian “Five of Swords doctrine” exists.

Later Jungian tarot writers interpret Smith’s figures as aspects of one psyche:

- foreground victor as inflated ego or shadow aggression;
- retreating figures as disowned vulnerability;
- abandoned swords as exhausted defenses;
- the entire scene as projection, splitting, or conflict between persona and shadow.

This is a **modern application of Jungian concepts**, spread chiefly by post-1960 psychological tarot literature, especially Sallie Nichols and later writers. It should not be quoted as Carl Jung’s own tarot teaching.

## Modern ethical reading

“Hollow victory” or “winning at too high a cost” is probably the dominant current English-language reading.

Its genealogy is:

**Golden Dawn Defeat → Smith’s victor-and-defeated scene → Waite’s dishonor/loss → late twentieth-century relational and psychological interpretation.**

It does not derive from medieval gameplay or from the Marseille flowers.

## Literature and film: claims tested

### Italo Calvino, *The Castle of Crossed Destinies*

Calvino used tarot cards as a combinatorial narrative engine, initially with the Visconti tarot and later with Marseille imagery. This is directly relevant to tarot’s literary reception.

**Finding:** no uniquely canonical Five-of-Swords episode could be securely isolated from consulted sources. Saying the entire novel “is based on the Five of Swords” would be false.

### T. S. Eliot, *The Waste Land*

Madame Sosostris uses a “wicked pack of cards,” and Eliot names the drowned Phoenician Sailor, Belladonna, the man with three staves, the Wheel, and the Hanged Man.

**Finding:** the Five of Swords is not named. The work belongs to general tarot reception, not this card’s individual dossier.

### W. B. Yeats

Yeats belonged to the Golden Dawn and knew its symbolic systems. No poem or prose text was located that singles out the Five of Swords.

### Ingmar Bergman

Bergman’s *The Seventh Seal* employs chess, Death, medieval apocalypse, and danse-macabre imagery—not a documented quotation of the Five of Swords.

### Jodorowsky’s films

Tarot strongly informed Jodorowsky’s creative practice and appears explicitly or structurally in his work, but no secure shot-for-card quotation of the Five of Swords was located.

### Paintings and buildings

No pre-1909 painting or building was found that can responsibly be said to “quote the Five of Swords.” Similar scenes of disarmament, defeated soldiers, five weapons, mandorlas, or floral interlace are insufficient evidence of quotation.

Modern murals, tattoos, album art, and illustrations frequently reproduce Smith’s composition, but a comprehensive catalogue would require a separate reception-history project.

# Controversies and disputes

## Egyptian origin

**Legend:** Court de Gébelin asserted in 1781 that tarot preserved an Egyptian sacred book, despite lacking deciphered Egyptian texts. Comte de Mellet supplied related Hebrew and divinatory speculation.

**Evidence against:** fifteenth-century Italian documents, Italian suit structures, Renaissance imagery, and an absence of Egyptian tarot evidence. Dummett notes that both authors’ Egyptian etymologies lacked hieroglyphic forms and were produced before reliable decipherment. [Dummett](https://www.nybooks.com/articles/1981/05/14/origins-of-tarot/)

No Egyptian Five of Swords prototype is known.

## “Book of Thoth”

**Esoteric tradition:** Etteilla and later occultists developed the claim that tarot was the Book of Thoth.

**Historical finding:** no ancient Egyptian book or card series corresponding to the 78-card tarot has been found. Crowley retained *The Book of Thoth* as an initiatory title, not as a demonstrated historical provenance.

## Gypsy/Romani origin

**Legend:** tarot was brought from Egypt or India into Europe by “Gypsies.”

**Evidence:** tarot is documented in northern Italian courts before any evidence connects Romani communities with tarot divination. The word “Gypsy,” historically derived from a mistaken identification with Egyptians, helped the story propagate. Papus’s title *Tarot of the Bohemians* spread it internationally.

This correction should not erase genuine later Romani participation in fortune-telling economies; it distinguishes later practice from invention of the pack.

## Cathar and Templar origins

**Legend/disputed:** tarot supposedly encodes Cathar dualism, Templar secrets, Grail doctrine, or a persecuted heretical catechism.

**Finding:** no fifteenth-century Cathar or Templar document, card order, workshop record, or iconographic program connects either movement to the Five of Swords or the tarot pack. The Templars had been suppressed more than a century before tarot’s documentation.

These theories generally interpret selected trump images and offer almost nothing specific about the ordinary Five of Swords.

## Kabbalistic antiquity

**Esoteric attribution:** Lévi, Papus, Golden Dawn, and successors align tarot with Hebrew letters and the Tree of Life.

**Historical correction:** there is no evidence that fifteenth-century makers assigned the Five to Geburah in Yetzirah or Venus in Aquarius. Dummett observed that occultists’ disagreement over Hebrew-letter assignments itself weakens the claim of an obvious original correspondence. [Dummett](https://www.nybooks.com/articles/1981/05/14/origins-of-tarot/)

The system can be internally coherent without being historically ancient.

## Sola Busca as Smith’s direct source

**Documented:** Sola Busca material was visible in London before Smith’s work, and there are deck-wide parallels.

**Disputed:** no surviving statement establishes that Smith copied the Sola Busca Five to create her Five.

**Best reconstruction:** the Golden Dawn meaning supplied the conceptual program; exposure to Sola Busca may have encouraged scenic minor cards generally.

## Who won in the Waite–Smith scene?

Waite calls the foreground figure “master in possession of the field.” That establishes possession, not moral righteousness.

Competing readings include:

- victor humiliating defeated opponents;
- collector retrieving weapons after combat;
- bully or thief;
- survivor after a costly struggle;
- one psyche divided into aggression and grief.

Only the first factual layer—one figure possesses the field while two retreat—is supplied by Waite. “Pyrrhic victory” is modern interpretation.

## Marseille botanical symbolism

Claims that every flower, leaf, red stem, or white gap carries an invariant ancient doctrine are disputed by variation among Noblet, Dodal, Madenié, Payen, Conver, and later recolorings.

The floral ornament is documented. A single fixed esoteric glossary is not.

## “Scimitars”

The Marseille curved forms are frequently called scimitars. That is visually convenient but historically imprecise: printers transformed repeated sword/scabbard signs into a decorative interlace. Treating them as evidence of Islamic, Egyptian, Crusader, or Templar origin is unwarranted.

## Misprints and restorations

Important sources of accidental tradition include:

- reversal or inconsistent orientation of unindexed pips;
- recutting damaged woodblocks;
- stencil-color drift;
- replacement of missing cards in facsimiles;
- publishers labeling reconstructed Visconti cards as if original;
- later Etteilla decks changing numbering and captions;
- Grimaud/Marteau colors being read backward as medieval doctrine;
- modern “Wirth” minors attributed to a trumps-only 1889 deck.

No single celebrated printing error uniquely responsible for the Five’s meaning was securely documented.

# Open questions

1. Does an original Five of Swords survive among a less-accessibly catalogued Milanese fragment, predating Sola Busca? Accessible museum records did not establish one.

2. What exactly is happening in the Sola Busca Five? High-resolution object study and comparison with contemporary military, metallurgical, and alchemical imagery are needed.

3. Can Smith’s visit to or awareness of the 1908 British Museum display be documented by a letter, diary, exhibition register, or recollection? Visual comparison alone establishes probability, not direct testimony.

4. Which precise Etteilla edition first printed the Five’s *deuil* meaning, and under what card number? The tradition is recoverable, but an authoritative scan of the relevant 1780s plate remains needed.

5. How much of Book T was fixed in 1888, and how much assumed its surviving wording during later copying? “Golden Dawn attribution” is secure; exact manuscript priority is more difficult.

6. Did Smith intend the foreground man as victor, scavenger, aggressor, or simply “master of the field”? Waite’s book does not settle the moral question.

7. Can Paul Marteau’s symbolic interpretations be separated cleanly from the earlier Camoin/Conver color history? Modern editions often collapse them.

8. No reliable evidence was found for a Five-of-Swords-specific link to Calvino, Eliot, Yeats, Bergman, a historic painting, or a building. General tarot influence should not be mistaken for card-specific reception.
