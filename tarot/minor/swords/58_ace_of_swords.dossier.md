# Ace of Swords: Research Dossier

## Method and evidence labels

This dossier distinguishes six kinds of statement:

- **[Documented fact]**: supported by a surviving card, archival record, printed rulebook, or primary occult text.
- **[Scholarly reconstruction]**: an inference argued from objects, documents, manufacture, or game structure.
- **[Tradition]**: a convention demonstrably transmitted within cardmaking, cartomancy, or an occult school.
- **[Esoteric attribution]**: a deliberately assigned symbolic correspondence, not a claim about the card’s historical origin.
- **[Disputed claim]**: proposed but contested, weakly evidenced, or dependent on uncertain identification.
- **[Legend/modern invention]**: unsupported historical genealogy or a recent interpretive elaboration.

“Earliest” normally means earliest presently known or surviving, not proof that no earlier example existed.

## Basic identification

### Names, number, classification

| Context | Name |
|---|---|
| Fifteenth-century Italian | *Asso di Spade*; in inventories often simply a card of the suit *spade* |
| Modern Italian | *Asso di Spade* |
| French tarot | *As d’Épées*, historically also *as d’espées* |
| English | Ace of Swords |
| Spanish | *As de Espadas* |
| Portuguese | *Ás de Espadas* |
| German tarot literature | *As der Schwerter* or *Schwert-Ass* |
| French-suited equivalence | Ace of Spades, *As de Pique* |
| German-suited approximate equivalence | Ace of Leaves or Acorns only by functional analogy; neither is historically “the Sword” |
| Golden Dawn | **The Root of the Powers of Air** |
| Crowley–Harris Thoth | **Ace of Swords — The Root of the Powers of Air** |
| BOTA/Paul Foster Case | Ace of Swords; Golden Dawn-derived attribution retained |

**Number:** one, Ace, Italian *asso*, French *as*. It is a numbered or pip card, not a court and not an *atout/trionfo*. Old cards commonly carry no printed Arabic “1.”

**Suit:** Swords—Italian *spade*, French *épées*, Spanish *espadas*. French occultists and English writers frequently equated Swords with French-suited Spades/Piques.

**Earliest securely surviving tarot example:** **[Documented fact]** the Ace of Swords in the Pierpont Morgan–Bergamo Visconti-Sforza group, probably made in Milan after Francesco Sforza became duke in 1450. The Morgan catalogues it expressly as “The Ace of Swords,” MS M.630.34, and attributes the group probably to Bonifacio Bembo. It consists of a single upright sword with heraldic and vegetal decoration. The qualification matters: ordinary sword-suit cards existed earlier, and other early tarot packs may originally have contained an ace now lost. [Morgan collection overview](https://www.themorgan.org/collection/tarot-cards), [Morgan manuscript record](https://www.themorgan.org/manuscript/158525).

**Immediate ancestor of the suit:** **[Scholarly reconstruction, strongly supported]** the sword suit of Islamic playing cards, particularly the Mamluk system of swords, polo-sticks, cups, and coins. A substantially preserved Mamluk pack in Istanbul has ten numerals and three courts per suit. Dummett and Kamal Abu-Deeb corrected L. A. Mayer’s initial treatment of replacement cards as a fifth suit. The direct ancestor is the **suit structure**, not an occult “Mamluk tarot”: the Mamluk pack has no tarot trumps. [IPCS introduction](https://www.i-p-c-s.org/wp/an-introduction/), Mayer’s primary study at [Persée](https://www.persee.fr/doc/bifao_0255-0962_1939_num_38_1_1998), Martínez Sáez summarizing Dummett–Abu-Deeb, pp. 73ff., [PDF](https://reference-global.com/download/article/10.2478/bgs-2025-0004.pdf).

### Golden Dawn attribution

**[Esoteric attribution, documented in the Order’s Book T tradition]**

- Title: **“The Root of the Powers of Air.”**
- Sephirah: **Kether**, the first emanation or Crown.
- World: **Yetzirah**, the Formative World.
- Element: **Air**.
- Tetragrammaton letter: **Vav/Vau**, the third letter of YHVH.
- Zodiacal decan: **none**. Aces are elemental roots and are not assigned one of the thirty-six decans.
- Planet: **none as the card’s primary assignment**.
- Tree position: **Kether in Yetzirah**, not one of the twenty-two inter-sephiroth paths.
- Book T image: a white radiating angelic hand from clouds grips a sword supporting or penetrating a radiant crown; olive and palm branches hang from it.
- Book T’s moral qualification: the sword’s force may be used for good or evil and becomes justice, authority, force, or conflict according to dignity and context.

The primary published Book T descendant states: “The Ace of Swords is called the Root of the Powers of Air.” [Equinox I.8 text](https://hermetic.com/crowley/equinox/i/viii/eqi08016). A manuscript compilation is available as [Book T PDF](https://jantetteroo.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/02/Mathers_and_felkin_golden_dawn_book_t_th.pdf).

A recurrent online error calls it the “watery root of Air.” **[Modern error]** That confuses the Ace with a court-card sub-element. An Ace is the undivided root/Kether of its element; “Water of Air” belongs to a court attribution in systems using elemental subdivisions.

## Iconography deck by deck

## Precursors and fifteenth-century cards

### Mamluk sword suit, probably fifteenth century

**[Documented object; genealogy reconstructed]** The surviving cards use calligraphic and vegetal ornament, Arabic inscriptions, and curved sword forms without human court figures. The Ace/One is one sword, although the familiar European word “Ace” is not evidence for its original Arabic name.

The material connection is structural:

- four suits correspond closely to swords, batons/polo-sticks, cups, and coins;
- ten numeral cards occur;
- three dignitary cards complete each suit;
- sword and polo-stick signs provided plausible models for early Latin-suited cards.

**Meaning:** there is no evidence that the Mamluk one of swords meant truth, intellect, Air, divine justice, or a “breakthrough.” Its inscriptions may be benedictory or poetic, but later tarot meanings cannot be projected backward.

### Marziano–Michelino pack, Milan, c. 1418–1425

**[Documented by a surviving treatise; cards lost]** Marziano da Tortona designed a pack for Filippo Maria Visconti, painted by Michelino da Besozzo. It had four bird suits—eagles, phoenixes, turtledoves, and doves—and sixteen deified heroes who overruled the suit cards.

It is relevant as a **proto-trump structure**, not as an Ace of Swords. No sword suit is described, and no Ace of Swords survives. Marziano says the numerical hierarchy ran in opposite directions between paired suits, showing that ace rank was a game convention, not an occult valuation. [Marziano study](https://tarot-heritage.com/2019/08/26/the-deck-and-book-of-the-deification-of-sixteen-heroes/).

### Visconti di Modrone / Cary-Yale, Milan, c. 1440s

These names normally designate the same incomplete luxury pack in Yale’s Beinecke Library.

**[Documented survival]** Numerous suit and trump cards survive, but the Ace of Swords is not among the secure surviving cards. The deck is unusual for six court cards per suit—male and female figures in several ranks—and for heraldry connecting it to the Visconti household.

**Finding:** claims that a particular modern “Cary-Yale Ace of Swords” reproduces a surviving original should be checked against the Beinecke inventory; commercial completions supply missing cards conjecturally.

### Brambilla tarot, Milan, c. 1440s–1450s

**[Documented incomplete deck]** Associated with the Visconti court and often attributed to Bonifacio Bembo or his circle. Only a small portion survives. The surviving cards emphasize Visconti devices and gold-ground luxury production.

**Finding:** no securely preserved Brambilla Ace of Swords was located in the consulted museum and specialist records. Reproduction decks necessarily reconstruct absent minors.

### Pierpont Morgan–Bergamo Visconti-Sforza, Milan, after 1450

**[Documented fact]** This provides the earliest securely catalogued tarot Ace of Swords found in this survey. A single straight, double-edged sword occupies the vertical axis. Its hilt, guard, pommel, and ornament turn the mere suit sign into a courtly, heraldic object. Gold ground and family devices locate it within elite Milanese manufacture.

It is not a narrative scene. No hand issues from a cloud; no crown is pierced; no mountains or Hebrew signs appear. The object signifies the suit and rank. Political, psychological, or elemental readings are later overlays.

### Ferrarese and Este cards, later fifteenth century

The fragmentary Este-related groups preserve evidence for a Ferrarese tarot style, but attribution and grouping have repeatedly changed.

**[Documented fragments; disputed reconstruction]** Some suit cards survive, but an intact, securely assigned Ace of Swords sequence cannot be reconstructed for every named “Este” group. The heraldic and emblematic ornament supports court manufacture; it does not establish occult meanings.

The 1442 Ferrara account is evidence for production of *carte da trionfi*, not a description of this Ace’s picture.

### Sola Busca tarot, probably Ferrara/Venice cultural orbit, 1491

**[Documented fact]** The Sola Busca is the earliest complete surviving tarot whose pip cards are fully illustrated rather than merely repeating suit signs. Its seventy-eight engravings were later colored in at least one surviving set.

Its Swords pips depict antique or pseudo-antique weapons incorporated into scenes, trophies, figures, or emblematic constructions. The Ace is treated as an individualized weapon/emblem rather than the later Golden Dawn cloud-hand composition.

**Important correction:** the often-repeated assertion that Pamela Colman Smith simply copied a Sola Busca “hand from cloud” for the Ace is not established. Smith demonstrably borrowed compositional ideas from several Sola Busca pip cards—most famously the Three and Ten of Swords and Seven of Wands—but the crowned cloud-hand Ace belongs more directly to the Golden Dawn/Marseille heraldic line.

### Mantegna “Tarocchi,” Ferrara or northeastern Italy, c. 1460s

**[Documented object; misleading traditional name]** These are fifty educational engravings arranged in five decimal series—social estates, Apollo and Muses, liberal arts, virtues, and celestial spheres. They are not a tarot pack and contain no suits or Ace of Swords.

Their relevance is contextual: they show the same humanist appetite for ordered encyclopedic imagery that surrounded early tarot. Calling them a source for the Ace is unsupported.

### Rosenwald and Budapest sheets, late fifteenth or early sixteenth century

**[Documented printed sheets; dating and grouping debated]** These uncut or fragmentary woodcut sheets show the transition from hand-painted court packs to reproducible popular cards. Their chief importance lies in trump types and the economical repetition of suit signs.

Where sword pips are present, they remain functional suit cards. They provide no evidence for Air, Kether, an emerging cloud-hand, or divinatory texts.

## Regional game patterns

### Minchiate, Florence, sixteenth–nineteenth centuries

Minchiate expanded the trump sequence to forty trump cards plus the Fool while retaining four ordinary suits.

**[Documented game tradition]** The Ace of Swords is a plain suit card. Florentine packs may decorate aces and courts elaborately, but it is not one of the expanded astrological or elemental trumps. Confusing it with Minchiate’s zodiacal or elemental cards is a category error.

### Tarocchino Bolognese

The shortened Bolognese pack removes low pips 2–5, leaving Ace and 6–10 in every suit.

**[Documented game tradition]** The Swords order is King, Queen, Knight, Jack, 10, 9, 8, 7, 6, Ace. Thus the Ace of Swords is the **lowest** sword. [Bolognese rules](https://gambiter.com/cards/tarot/ottocen.html).

The Bolognese Ace is generally one sword, often decorated and oriented vertically. Its survival despite removal of 2–5 reflects pack economy and pattern identity, not occult prominence.

### Sicilian tarot

The traditional 63-card Sicilian pack does **not** contain an Ace of Swords. Swords run from King through 5. A nineteenth-century Ace of Coins was added as a fiscal/tax-stamp carrier.

**[Documented fact]** This sharply corrects the notion that every tarot pattern necessarily includes the Ace of Swords. [Sicilian rules and pack history](https://www.pagat.com/tarot/sicilian.html).

### Besançon tarot

Besançon-pattern decks replace the Papess and Pope with Juno and Jupiter, especially in Protestant or confessionally mixed regions of eastern France, Switzerland, and southern Germany.

**[Documented regional renaming]** This affects trumps II and V, not the Ace of Swords. The Sword Ace generally retains Marseille-derived pip structure. Juno/Jupiter substitutions are evidence of regional religious adaptation, not a change in the Ace’s identity.

## Tarot de Marseille lineage

### Jean Noblet, Paris, c. 1650

**[Documented, incomplete surviving deck]** Noblet’s Sword pips use interlaced curved bands. Odd cards contain a straight central sword; even cards consist chiefly of paired curved signs. The Ace is unnumbered and displays one elaborate upright sword.

Noblet’s surviving pack lacks Swords VI–X, but its Ace survives. The maker’s name appears elsewhere—not on the Sword Ace. A useful catalogue description notes the unnumbered Ace and curved Sword sequence. [Noblet overview](https://www.apprendre-tarotdemarseille.com/histoire/les-anciens-tarots-de-marseille/le-tarot-de-jean-noblet/), [card description](https://www.aeclectic.net/tarot/cards/jean-noblet/).

### Jean Dodal, Lyon, c. 1701–1715

**[Documented fact]** Dodal regularizes the Marseille Type I vocabulary. The Ace is a monumental upright sword grasped at the bottom by a hand or hand-like cuff, rising through a crown-like upper structure with foliate sprays. The surrounding ornament makes it simultaneously a suit sign, heraldic achievement, and printerly showpiece.

The crown/branch arrangement predates the Golden Dawn and Waite. The later occultists transformed an inherited cardmaking motif into an explicit celestial revelation.

### Pierre Madenié, Dijon, 1709, and Type II

**[Documented pattern history]** Madenié is the earliest securely dated Type II Marseille deck. Type II altered numerous trump details while retaining the basic Sword pip grammar: curved interlacing pips and an individualized Ace.

### Nicolas Conver, Marseille, 1760/1761

**[Documented fact]** Conver’s pack became an especially influential Marseille model. Its Sword Ace is upright, richly hilted, held from below, and surmounted by a crown with branching vegetation. The sword is a singular heraldic emblem rather than a literal landscape scene.

### Grimaud / Paul Marteau, Paris, 1930 edition

**[Documented modern standardization]** Grimaud’s *Ancien Tarot de Marseille*, supervised by Paul Marteau, normalized colors, linework, and titles for mass manufacture. Marteau’s blue-heavy palette changed the Ace’s visual emphasis while preserving the crowned sword.

Claims that Marteau reproduced an unchanged medieval archetype are **[Disputed]**: his deck is a twentieth-century redrawing based principally on later Marseille forms.

### Camoin–Jodorowsky, 1997

**[Documented modern reconstruction; disputed claims of restoration]** Philippe Camoin and Alejandro Jodorowsky produced a reconstructed Marseille deck using several historical exemplars and newly regularized details. Its Ace preserves the hand-held crowned sword but intensifies color-symbolic and sexual/alchemical readings.

The deck is historically informed, but its claim to recover a unique original or “authentic” Tarot matrix is not demonstrated by a surviving master deck. It is best classified as a modern synthetic reconstruction.

Jodorowsky and Marianne Costa interpret the Ace as concentrated intellectual energy, the capacity to distinguish and cut through illusion, and the active beginning of language or thought. Those are **modern symbolic readings**, not meanings recoverable from Noblet or Conver workshop documents.

## Occult and illustrated decks

### Etteilla’s Grand Etteilla, Paris, 1789

**[Documented occult redesign]** Jean-Baptiste Alliette reorganized and renamed the entire pack according to his supposed Egyptian cosmogony and cartomantic system. The traditional Marseille identities no longer map transparently one-to-one onto the numbered Grand Etteilla cards; editions and successor packs vary.

For the Sword Ace, Etteilla’s inherited cartomantic vocabulary emphasized **force, extremity, conflict, passion, and excess** in one orientation, with **pregnancy, conception, multiplication, or production** in the other. This strange reversal pair entered Mathers and then Waite.

Etteilla’s deck is the first major purpose-designed occult tarot. The astrology and “Book of Thoth” frame are **[Esoteric attribution/legend]**, not recovered pharaonic doctrine.

### Oswald Wirth, 1889

Wirth’s first tarot, made for Stanislas de Guaita, concentrated on the twenty-two trumps.

**[Documented fact]** There was therefore no original 1889 Wirth Ace of Swords comparable to his trump designs. Later seventy-eight-card “Wirth” decks extend his aesthetic posthumously or editorially. Treating such an Ace as Wirth’s 1889 drawing is a misattribution.

### Golden Dawn working designs, c. 1888 onward

**[Documented tradition]** Book T directs members to construct personal packs. The Ace of Swords is a white angelic hand issuing from cloud, grasping an upright sword supporting a radiant crown, with olive and palm branches.

Its innovation is interpretive explicitness:

- Marseille’s hand/crowned sword becomes angelic and celestial.
- The crown is Kether.
- The sword becomes elemental Air and the Magician’s weapon.
- Olive signifies peace; palm signifies suffering or victory through ordeal.
- The same force may establish justice or destructive domination.

Robert Wang’s *Golden Dawn Tarot* (1977) and Chic and Sandra Tabatha Cicero’s later reconstructions are modern visual realizations of the written Order corpus. They are evidence for twentieth-century reception, not facsimiles of a single canonical nineteenth-century deck.

### Waite–Smith, London, 1909

**[Documented fact]** Designed by Arthur Edward Waite, drawn by Pamela Colman Smith, and published by William Rider & Son.

A gray-white hand issues from a cloud at the left and grips an upright sword. The blade passes through a golden crown. An olive branch hangs on one side, a palm on the other. Six flame- or yod-like droplets surround the blade. Jagged mountains form a cold distant horizon.

Waite’s description is concise: “A hand issues from a cloud, grasping a sword, the point of which is encircled by a crown.” In *The Pictorial Key to the Tarot* he adds that the crown may bear a meaning beyond ordinary fortune-telling. [Primary text](https://www.sacred-texts.com/tarot/pkt/pktswac.htm); alternate [scan](https://www.mr-kaplan.com/Library/Waite/The%20Pictorial%20Key%20to%20the%20Tarot.pdf).

**Sources of the design:**

1. **[Documented tradition]** Golden Dawn Book T supplies the cloud-hand, sword, crown, olive, and palm.
2. **[Documented visual inheritance]** Marseille provides the single crowned sword held from below.
3. **[Probable reconstruction]** Smith converted the abstract heraldic emblem into a naturalistic visionary scene.
4. **[Disputed]** Sola Busca influenced her treatment of illustrated minors generally, but no evidence found makes its Sword Ace the immediate model for this card.
5. **[Modern interpretation]** the droplets are commonly called Hebrew *yods*. Waite does not identify them in his printed description.

The visual change moves the card from suit identifier to epiphany: intellect or power is offered from an unseen realm.

### Paul Foster Case / BOTA, 1920s onward

Case’s *The Tarot: A Key to the Wisdom of the Ages* and the BOTA deck retain Waite/Golden Dawn structure while expecting students to color the cards.

**[Esoteric attribution]** Ace = Kether; Swords = Air, Vav, Yetzirah; the sword is discriminative intelligence and directed mental force. Case generally rejects fixed fatalism and treats divination as secondary to occult training.

The BOTA Ace preserves the cloud-hand and crowned blade. Its significance is pedagogical: color and emblem train correspondential memory.

### Crowley–Harris Thoth, painted 1938–1943; first complete publication 1969

**[Documented fact]** Aleister Crowley directed; Frieda Harris painted. Publication history includes partial or limited appearances before the widely distributed 1969 deck.

The card presents a large, dynamically modeled sword or magical weapon against luminous, turbulent Air. Its point is crowned by a twenty-two-rayed diadem. Greek *Thelema* may be read upon the weapon in published versions.

Crowley writes:

> “The Ace of Swords is the primordial Energy of Air, the Essence of the Vau of Tetragrammaton, the integration of the Ruach.”

He calls it the Sword of the Magus crowned with a “twenty-two rayed diadem of pure Light.” [*Book of Thoth*, small cards](https://hermetic.com/crowley/book-of-thoth/small-cards).

The Thoth Ace is less a divine hand offering victory than an abstract eruption of intellect, word, wind, and division. Crowley stresses Air’s ambiguity: rational consciousness is accessible, but thought readily becomes unstable, deceptive, or cruel.

### Notable modern redesigns

**[Documented modern reception]**

- **Aquarian Tarot** (David Palladini, 1970): streamlines the crowned upright sword into Art Deco poster design.
- **Morgan-Greer Tarot** (Bill Greer/Lloyd Morgan, 1979): crops the Waite-Smith image closely and saturates it with color.
- **Robin Wood Tarot** (1991): intensifies pagan and elemental imagery while retaining the offered blade.
- **Wild Unknown Tarot** (Kim Krans, 2012): replaces the hand with a minimal sword and radiating light, converting “divine offer” into sudden illumination.
- **Modern Witch Tarot** (Lisa Sterle, 2019): retains the RWS grammar while recasting figures and hands through contemporary, inclusive visual culture.
- **Light Seer’s Tarot** (Chris-Anne, 2019): turns the card toward mental breakthrough and personal clarity rather than heraldic conquest.

These are not stages in a single continuous secret doctrine. They are adaptations of the dominant Waite-Smith template.

## The game

### Fundamental correction

The Ace of Swords is **not a trump**. It cannot be “high in tarocchi trumps.”

A tarot pack normally contains:

- twenty-one permanent trumps;
- the Fool;
- four ordinary suits, each originally with ten pips and four courts.

When Swords is led, the Ace competes only as a Sword unless trumped.

### Classical ranking

In the dominant historical tarot convention, suits divide into two directional pairs:

- **Swords and Batons, the long suits:** King, Queen, Knight, Jack, 10 down to Ace.
- **Cups and Coins, the round suits:** King, Queen, Knight, Jack, Ace up to 10.

Accordingly, the Ace of Swords is usually the **lowest Sword**. The Ace may still have a point value in scoring systems, but trick rank and scoring value are separate questions.

The old French game described by the Abbé de Marolles retains Swords as a long suit with descending pips. [Marolles rules](https://www.pagat.com/tarot/marolles.html). General Italian-suited tarot history is summarized at [Pagat](https://www.pagat.com/class/itarot.html).

### Regional variation

- **Bologna:** Ace is below 6 because 2–5 have been removed.
- **Sicily:** no Sword Ace in the traditional shortened pack.
- **Piedmont/related Italian traditions:** Sword Ace normally low.
- **Swiss Troccas:** inverse ranking survives in round suits; long suits descend to Ace.
- **French Tarot Nouveau:** French suits and modern rules replace the direct iconographic Sword Ace with the corresponding French-suited card system.
- **Non-tarot games:** Briscola makes an Ace extremely high and valuable, but that is not tarot evidence.

### Point value

In many tarot point-count systems, ordinary pips—including the Ace—are low-value “counters” or have no special card-point value beyond their contribution to grouped counting. Kings, honors, and extreme trumps carry the substantial values.

There is no universal “Ace = eleven points” tarot rule. That belongs to unrelated ace–ten games.

### Fool and trump-order dispute

The Fool is normally unnumbered and played under a special excuse rule rather than as an ordinary highest or lowest trump. Regional names include *il Matto*, *le Fou*, *l’Excuse*, and Sicilian *il Fuggitivo*.

Dummett identified three principal early Italian trump-order traditions:

- **Order A:** Bologna and Florence.
- **Order B:** Ferrara.
- **Order C:** Milan, ancestor of the Marseille ordering.

Justice and Fortitude/Strength shift relative positions among these orders. This has no effect on the Ace of Swords because it is outside the trump sequence.

Regional substitutions such as Papess → Juno and Pope → Jupiter, or names such as *Maison Dieu* for the Tower, likewise concern trumps and should not be imported into the Sword suit.

## Chronology of attestation

### Before tarot

- **c. fourteenth–fifteenth century:** **[Documented objects]** Islamic/Mamluk sword-suit cards.
- **1370s onward:** **[Documented European records]** playing cards appear in European sources; no tarot yet.
- **c. 1418–1425:** **[Documented treatise]** Marziano describes a trump-like pack with bird suits, not Swords.

### Emergence of trionfi/tarocchi

- **1440s:** **[Documented records and surviving objects]** tarot-like *trionfi* packs emerge around Milan, Ferrara, Bologna, and Florence.
- **10 February 1442, Ferrara:** **[Documented archival entry]** payment to Jacomo Sagramoro for painted *carte da trionfi*. This names the class of cards, not the Ace.
- **after 1450, Milan:** **[Documented surviving card]** Morgan Visconti-Sforza Ace of Swords.
- **c. 1460s:** “Mantegna Tarocchi” engravings, not tarot cards.
- **c. 1470–1500:** the *Sermo perutilis de ludo cum aliis* condemns card play and lists the tarot trumps. **Finding:** it gives no special Ace of Swords symbolism.
- **1491:** Sola Busca deck dated in its engraved program; illustrated pips.
- **c. 1490s–early 1500s:** Rosenwald/Budapest sheets.
- **early sixteenth century:** *tarocchi* increasingly replaces *trionfi* as the Italian game name.
- **1520s:** Teofilo Folengo’s tarot-related poetic material associates cards with literary characterization. It does not supply a securely isolated doctrine for the Sword Ace.
- **c. 1500 tarot poems:** cards are used as prompts for praise, satire, or characterization. These are precursors of associative reading, not proof of a secret divinatory system.

### Printed patterns and occultization

- **c. 1650:** Noblet, Paris.
- **c. 1701–1715:** Dodal, Lyon.
- **1709:** Madenié, Dijon, dated Type II.
- **1760/1761:** Conver, Marseille.
- **1781:** Court de Gébelin and Comte de Mellet publish the Egyptian/Book of Thoth hypothesis.
- **1783–1791:** Etteilla publishes systematic tarot divination and occult correspondence.
- **1789:** first Grand Etteilla deck.
- **1854–1856:** Éliphas Lévi’s *Dogme et Rituel de la haute magie* binds tarot to ceremonial magic and Kabbalah.
- **1863:** Paul Christian develops Egyptian initiatory tarot narratives.
- **1888:** Mathers publishes *The Tarot: Its Occult Signification, Use in Fortune-Telling, and Method of Play*.
- **1888 onward:** Golden Dawn’s Book T system.
- **1889:** Papus, *Le Tarot des Bohémiens*; Wirth’s twenty-two trumps.
- **1909:** Waite-Smith pack.
- **1910:** Waite’s *Pictorial Key* text; commonly dated 1911 in later issues.
- **1930:** Grimaud/Marteau standard Marseille.
- **1938–1943:** Harris paints Thoth under Crowley.
- **1944:** Crowley’s *Book of Thoth*.
- **1969:** broadly marketed complete Thoth deck.
- **1970s onward:** Jungian, therapeutic, feminist, queer, and secular narrative tarot proliferate.
- **1997:** Camoin–Jodorowsky reconstruction.

### Conflicts and clarifications

1. **Ace versus Vav:** Vav belongs to the **entire Sword suit**, not specifically to the numeral one. The Ace itself is Kether within that Vav/Yetzirah field.

2. **Path confusion:** the Ace has no unique Hebrew letter or Tree path in Golden Dawn doctrine. Only the twenty-two trumps occupy paths.

3. **Decan confusion:** Two through Ten receive decans in Book T. Aces do not.

4. **Court-card data do not apply:** zodiacal spans and “Air of Air,” “Water of Air,” and similar sub-elements belong to courts. They should not be attached to this Ace.

5. **Tzaddi/Heh controversy:** Crowley’s exchange concerns the Emperor and Star trump correspondences. It does not alter the Ace of Swords.

6. **Air assignment:** it is an occult convention, decisively systematized in nineteenth-century Hermetic tarot. It is not evidenced in fifteenth-century game manufacture.

## Divinatory meanings by author

Short quotations are used to identify each author’s language; surrounding explanations are paraphrased.

### Etteilla, 1780s

**[Documented cartomantic tradition, exact wording varies by edition]**

- Upright: extreme force, excess, violence, passionate energy, domination, or an emotionally cutting message.
- Reversed: conception, pregnancy, childbirth, multiplication, productiveness, or reward of what has been sown.

This is the source-family behind Waite’s otherwise surprising reversed “conception, childbirth, augmentation, multiplicity.”

An exact, stable English quotation cannot responsibly be assigned to “Etteilla 1789” without naming the particular Grand Etteilla edition: labels and orientations changed among Alliette’s publications and successor decks.

### S. L. MacGregor Mathers, 1888

In *The Tarot: Its Occult Signification* Mathers gives the Sword Ace a forceful cluster inherited substantially from French cartomancy:

- Upright: triumph, fertility/productiveness, intense force, love or hatred carried to an extreme.
- Reversed: closely related force with damaging consequences; conception or multiplication appears in variant lists.

[1888 scan](https://www.howardnema.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/12/the-tarot-1888-by-macgregor-mathers-1.pdf).

### A. E. Waite, 1910

Waite’s own words:

> “Triumph, the excessive degree in everything, conquest, triumph of force.”

He adds that it is “a card of great force” in both love and hatred.

Reversed:

> “The same, but the results are disastrous”; alternatively “conception, childbirth, augmentation, multiplicity.”

This is not the now-common simple opposition “clarity/confusion.” [Primary text](https://www.sacred-texts.com/tarot/pkt/pktswac.htm).

### Aleister Crowley, 1944

Crowley does not supply a neat upright/reversed dictionary. Thoth practice generally uses dignity and interaction rather than mechanical reversal.

- Well dignified: invocation, intelligence, word, wind, analysis, justice, tremendous mental force.
- Ill dignified: chaotic thought, argument, cruelty, futility, rationalization, force without a worthy object.

His core statement is “primordial Energy of Air.” [Primary text](https://hermetic.com/crowley/book-of-thoth/small-cards).

### Paul Foster Case

Case reads it as the root of intellectual power: discrimination, decision, command, justice, and mental initiative. In adverse expression it becomes severance, destructive criticism, coercion, or conflict.

Case’s system does not make reversal mechanically authoritative; polarity depends on context and the operator’s direction of force.

### Rachel Pollack

In *Seventy-Eight Degrees of Wisdom* Pollack emphasizes the intensity of clear perception. The gripping hand signifies concentration; the austere mountains signify objective reality; the cloud introduces a deeper spiritual source that prevents intellect from becoming merely cold.

- Upright: mental clarity, truth, a new idea, decisive perception, the ability to cut through confusion.
- Reversed/adverse: confused or misused intelligence, oppressive “truth,” blocked insight, or force turned destructively.

[Pollack’s significance and deck work at the Morgan](https://www.themorgan.org/exhibitions/online/tarot/23).

### Mary K. Greer

Greer’s *Complete Book of Tarot Reversals* (2002) treats reversals as modes rather than a second fixed vocabulary. Applied to this Ace, the force may be:

- blocked or resisted;
- delayed or unavailable;
- internal or unconscious;
- excessive;
- misdirected;
- being reconsidered or overturned.

This framework explains why contemporary readers variously call the reversed Ace confusion, suppressed truth, weaponized intellect, or inward clarity. [Overview of Greer’s reversal taxonomy](https://querent.life/reversals).

## Esoteric and symbolic readings

### Hermetic and magical

**First clear development:** Lévi in the 1850s; expanded by Papus, the Golden Dawn, Case, and Crowley.

The sword becomes the magician’s instrument of division: it separates, defines, names, and commands. As Air, it represents language and rational consciousness. Its double edge permits both liberation and destruction.

The Golden Dawn’s cloud-hand converts the medieval heraldic emblem into an emanation. The crown is Kether: undifferentiated intellectual potential before the numbered stages Two through Ten.

### Kabbalistic

**[Esoteric attribution, not historical origin]**

- Ace = Kether.
- Swords = Yetzirah.
- Suit letter = Vav.
- Therefore the card = Kether in Yetzirah.

This is a Hermetic-Qabalistic construction using Jewish symbols in a Christian and ceremonial-magical synthesis. No evidence connects it to the fifteenth-century Milanese maker.

### Astrological

The Ace is elemental Air but has no specific planet, sign, or decan in Book T. Some modern decks attach Mercury, Uranus, Gemini, Libra, or Aquarius by thematic analogy. Those are **[Modern inventions or deck-specific extensions]**, not Golden Dawn assignments.

The three Air signs govern the Sword court quadrants and the numbered Sword decans, but none uniquely governs the Ace.

### Alchemical

Marseille and Jodorowsky traditions sometimes read the upward blade as sublimation, separation, or the alchemical cutting faculty; the crown may signify the perfected volatile principle.

**Evidence:** this is a post hoc symbolic reading. No cardmaker’s document from Noblet, Dodal, or Conver identifies the Ace as an alchemical diagram.

### Christian

The cloud-hand resembles the medieval *manus Dei*, the Hand of God emerging from heaven. The sword can recall:

- the flaming sword guarding Eden;
- the sword of divine or temporal justice;
- Christ’s apocalyptic mouth-sword;
- the “sword of the Spirit” as divine word;
- the sword that pierces Mary’s soul.

**[Scholarly iconographic possibility]** Cloud-hands and divine hands were common Christian visual devices, making this a plausible image vocabulary for later designers. **[Disputed]** no evidence proves that a particular biblical passage was intended by Marseille cardmakers.

### Jungian and psychological

**First widespread tarot form:** chiefly twentieth century, after Jungian concepts were imported into occult and therapeutic tarot; neither Jung nor Freud devised a canonical Ace-of-Swords meaning.

Modern Jungian readers treat it as emergence of a discriminating idea from the unconscious: the hand from cloud makes a thought available to ego-consciousness. The sword separates self from undifferentiated psychic material. Its shadow is inflation, sterile intellectualism, or cutting oneself off from feeling.

Calling the card an archetype directly identified by Jung is **[Misattribution]**. It is a later Jungian application.

## People and works

### Artists, makers, occultists, and scholars

- **Bonifacio Bembo:** probable artist or workshop attribution for Visconti-Sforza cards, including the Morgan Ace.
- **Michelino da Besozzo and Marziano da Tortona:** creators of the lost proto-trump pack; not creators of a surviving Sword Ace.
- **Jean Noblet, Jean Dodal, Nicolas Conver:** major transmitters of the French crowned-sword pattern.
- **Jean-Baptiste Alliette/Etteilla:** first systematic professional occult tarot author and designer.
- **Éliphas Lévi:** made tarot a key of ceremonial magic and Kabbalah.
- **Papus/Gérard Encausse:** disseminated the “Bohemian,” Egyptian, numerical, and Tetragrammaton synthesis.
- **S. L. MacGregor Mathers:** transmitted French cartomancy and helped formulate Golden Dawn tarot.
- **Pamela Colman Smith:** created the dominant modern image.
- **A. E. Waite:** supplied its published divinatory text and Golden Dawn-informed program.
- **Frieda Harris and Aleister Crowley:** transformed the Ace into an abstract Thelemic emblem.
- **Paul Foster Case:** systematized it pedagogically in BOTA.
- **Michael Dummett:** reconstructed tarot primarily as a family of games.
- **Ronald Decker and Thierry Depaulis:** traced the documentary construction of occult tarot.
- **Stuart R. Kaplan:** documented decks and popularized historical collecting.
- **Gertrude Moakley:** connected Visconti-Sforza trumps with Renaissance triumphal culture.
- **Robert M. Place:** combines art-historical and esoteric interpretation while rejecting literal Egyptian-origin legends.
- **Helen Farley:** examines tarot’s transition from play to occultism within cultural history.
- **Andrea Vitali and Le Tarot:** publish documentary and iconographic research, though individual interpretations should be weighed separately.

### Literature, cinema, and visual culture

**Italo Calvino, *The Castle of Crossed Destinies*:** uses tarot cards, especially Visconti and Marseille imagery, as combinatorial narrative language. Particular cards acquire meaning through sequence; it is not a historical commentary on the Sword Ace alone.

**T. S. Eliot, *The Waste Land*:** Madame Sosostris names “the man with three staves,” the Wheel, the one-eyed merchant, and a blank card. She does not name the Ace of Swords. Claims that the Ace secretly governs the episode are interpretive speculation.

**W. B. Yeats:** deeply involved with the Golden Dawn and therefore with its tarot correspondences. No primary text located in this research makes the Ace of Swords a central Yeatsian emblem.

**Ingmar Bergman:** *The Seventh Seal* draws upon medieval Death imagery and chess, not a documented quotation of the Ace of Swords.

**Alejandro Jodorowsky:** tarot informs his teaching, writing, psychomagic, and filmic imagination, but identifying a sword or crowned blade in a film as a deliberate Ace quotation requires production evidence. General visual resemblance is insufficient.

**Paintings and buildings:** no securely documented major painting or building was found whose designer explicitly cited the Ace of Swords. Crowned swords, hands of God, and swords of justice are older and broader motifs; resemblance alone cannot establish tarot borrowing.

## Controversies and disputes

## Dedicated contested-material findings

Searches were run specifically for combinations of “Ace of Swords” with *origin*, *myth*, *dispute*, *misattribution*, *Egyptian*, *Gypsy/Romani*, *Cathar*, and *Templar*. The results overwhelmingly repeated general tarot-origin legends; no independent ancient or medieval source gave the Ace its modern occult meaning.

### Egyptian origin

Court de Gébelin declared in 1781 that tarot was an Egyptian book preserved unnoticed in European play. His opening asks readers to imagine an ancient Egyptian work that escaped burned libraries and survives in everyone’s hands. [French primary text](https://www.melmothia.net/3519/du-jeu-des-tarots-antoine-court-de-gebelin-pdf/).

**Label:** **[Documented eighteenth-century belief; legend as history]**.

Evidence against literal origin:

- tarot’s trump structure is first documented in fifteenth-century northern Italy;
- its ordinary suits derive from earlier playing-card traditions;
- no pharaonic tarot object, description, or transmission chain exists;
- Egyptian hieroglyphs were not yet deciphered when Gébelin proposed his etymologies.

The Mamluk connection does involve medieval Egypt, but it does not vindicate Gébelin: Mamluk Islamic playing cards are neither pharaonic nor a Book of Thoth, and they have no tarot trumps.

### Comte de Mellet and Hebrew letters

Mellet’s companion essay in Gébelin’s volume attached Hebrew letters to tarot and described divination.

**Label:** **[Documented esoteric innovation]**, not a recovered ancient correspondence. Later Lévi, Papus, Golden Dawn authors, and Crowley repeatedly revised the order, demonstrating that no historically transmitted consensus existed.

### “Gypsy” or Romani transmission

Papus’s *Le Tarot des Bohémiens* (1889) helped cement the notion that Romani people carried the ancient tarot westward.

**Label:** **[Legend]**.

Romani arrival in western Europe and Italian tarot’s documentary emergence do not furnish a transmission chain. The title depends on nineteenth-century stereotypes equating Romani people with Egypt, wandering occult wisdom, and fortune-telling. Modern scholarship treats these ideas as constructions projected onto Romani livelihoods and identity. [Library of Congress record for Papus](https://www.loc.gov/item/77355368/), [study of Romani magical stereotyping](https://doi.org/10.1163/15685276-01234012).

### Cathar and Templar claims

Some twentieth-century writers interpret tarot as a coded survival of Cathar dualism, Templar secrets, Gnosticism, or a clandestine Grail tradition.

**Label:** **[Disputed claim/legend]**.

Evidence offered usually consists of:

- geographic overlap between northern Italian tarot production and former heretical regions;
- symbolic resemblance;
- presumed need for secrecy;
- later occult meanings read backward.

Missing are a Cathar or Templar tarot deck, a contemporary text connecting either community to cards, a documentary transmission chain, or distinctively Cathar/Templar content that cannot be explained by ordinary late-medieval Christian allegory. A modern defense of a Cathar scenario illustrates the argument but supplies no such direct chain. [Example](https://ibnarabisociety.org/wp-content/uploads/PDFs/El-Moor_Occult-tradition-2.pdf).

### Air and intellect as “ancient” Sword meanings

**Label:** **[Esoteric attribution, later tradition]**.

The sword’s capacity to divide made the Air/intellect analogy persuasive to Hermetic occultists. But the earliest surviving Sword Aces are game objects without interpretive texts. Air, Vav, Yetzirah, and Kether are nineteenth-century systematic attributions.

### Marseille scimitars

The curved interlacing Sword pips are often said to be literal surviving Mamluk scimitars.

**Label:** **[Plausible scholarly reconstruction, simplified in popular accounts]**.

The Mamluk sword ancestry of the suit is strong. Yet Marseille’s nested arcs are highly conventional printer’s signs, not naturalistic depictions of several complete swords. On odd cards the central straight sword clarifies the suit; the curved bands may simultaneously be read as stylized blades and decorative framework.

### Crown as “Crown of Kether”

In Marseille cards, a crown or crowned finial precedes the Golden Dawn.

- As a historical Marseille object: **[Documented iconography]**.
- As Kether: **[Golden Dawn esoteric attribution]**.
- As proof that Marseille cardmakers secretly knew Kabbalah: **[Disputed/unsupported]**.

### Olive and palm

Book T explicitly identifies peace and suffering/victory branches. Waite-Smith depicts two different sprays.

**Uncertainty:** botanical identification in early woodcuts is rarely exact. Calling every left spray olive and every right spray palm in pre-Golden Dawn decks projects the Order’s gloss backward.

### Sola Busca as Smith’s source

Smith’s access to Sola Busca imagery and her borrowings on several minors are well supported. But the Ace’s precise cloud-hand/crown design is already present in Golden Dawn instructions.

**Finding:** Sola Busca is a contextual source for the illustrated-minor project, not the necessary immediate source for this Ace.

### The Ace as “Excalibur,” “sword of Solomon,” or Justice’s own sword

These are productive contemporary analogies.

**Label:** **[Modern invention unless a deck explicitly depicts or names one]**. Neither Waite nor Smith identifies the blade as Excalibur, Solomon’s sword, or the identical physical weapon held by Justice.

### Misprints and editorial drift

Several recurrent errors have become quasi-traditions:

- Waite’s printed “grasping as word” in some electronic transcriptions is an OCR error for “grasping a sword.”
- Conver is variously dated 1760 and 1761 because cards, privileges, cataloguing conventions, and later impressions are not always synchronized.
- The Waite-Smith deck is called 1909, 1910, or 1911 depending on whether one means first cards, book issue, or revised publication.
- “Golden Dawn description from 1888, eighty-three years before Waite” is arithmetically false: 1888 to 1909 is twenty-one years.
- Sicilian fiscal history is sometimes generalized into “the Ace carried the tax stamp.” In the Sicilian tarot case it was specifically an added Ace of Coins, not a Sword Ace.
- Crowley’s “Tzaddi is not the Star” correction is sometimes applied to the whole deck. It concerns Emperor/Star correspondences, not this Ace.

## Open questions

1. **Exact earliest tarot Sword Ace:** the Morgan card is the earliest securely catalogued surviving example found here, but incomplete Milanese packs and disputed groupings leave room for an earlier lost or reclassified example.

2. **Workshop genealogy of the crowned sword:** the transition from Italian single-sword heraldry to the French hand-held, crowned Ace deserves a card-by-card comparison of surviving sixteenth-century Lyonnais and Piedmontese sheets.

3. **Botanical identity:** early leaves called olive, palm, laurel, or oak need object-level examination rather than reliance on modern redrawings.

4. **Etteilla wording:** a definitive account must collate Alliette’s own manuals, the 1789 cards, and later “Grand Etteilla” editions. Modern tables often merge them.

5. **Golden Dawn manuscript dating:** “Book T, 1888” is convenient shorthand for a corpus compiled and transmitted over time. Individual folios and image directions need archival dating.

6. **Smith’s exact visual sources:** Sola Busca influence is certain at deck level, but no letter or notebook located here enumerates the sources of her Ace.

7. **Early divination:** fifteenth- and sixteenth-century poems show cards used in literary and characterizing games. Whether any individual Sword Ace acquired a stable oral fortune-telling meaning before printed cartomancy remains unproved.

8. **Maker’s marks and taxes:** the Ace of Coins, Two of Cups, Two of Swords, and other locations could bear maker, city, privilege, or fiscal information depending on pattern and law. There was no universal European rule placing such information on the Sword Ace.
