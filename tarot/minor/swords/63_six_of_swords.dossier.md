# Six of Swords: Research Dossier

## Method and evidentiary labels

This dossier distinguishes:

- **Documented fact** — supported by surviving cards, archival records, contemporary texts, or an author’s own publication.
- **Scholarly reconstruction** — a conclusion proposed from incomplete material evidence by historians or cataloguers.
- **Tradition** — a customary name, rule, or interpretation whose precise origin is uncertain.
- **Esoteric attribution** — an occult system’s intentionally assigned correspondence, not a historical property of the medieval card.
- **Disputed claim** — an attribution for which scholars or primary evidence disagree.
- **Legend** — a historical-origin story contradicted or unsupported by the surviving evidence.
- **Modern invention** — a demonstrably recent interpretation, title, or visual feature.

Because this is a numbered suit card rather than a trump, many requested trump-specific matters—Hebrew-letter paths, Justice/Strength order, the Papess/Juno substitution, and Crowley’s Tzaddi/Heh revision—do not attach individually to it. They are included only where they explain the pack containing the card.

# Iconography deck by deck

## Before tarot: the suit of Swords

**Documented fact.** Tarot inherited four Italian suit signs—Swords, Batons, Cups, and Coins—from the ordinary playing-card pack. The signs have close structural predecessors in Mamluk cards: swords, polo sticks, cups, and coins. This ancestry concerns the suit system, not Waite’s ferry scene and not any occult doctrine.

**Scholarly reconstruction.** European makers adapted the Islamic suit signs rather than transmitting an Egyptian hieroglyphic book. The sword sign was sometimes rendered as a straight European weapon and sometimes as a long curved blade. Repeated suit signs indicated numerical value. The Metropolitan Museum summarizes this basic early structure: “The pip cards were conventionally organized, with the repetition of the symbol indicating the value.” [Metropolitan Museum](https://www.metmuseum.org/perspectives/tarot-2)

## Marziano da Tortona and Michelino da Besozzo, c. 1412–1425, Milan

**Documented fact.** Marziano da Tortona described a special card pack devised for Filippo Maria Visconti and painted by Michelino da Besozzo. Its suits were birds and its special figures classical gods.

**Negative finding.** This was not a conventional 78-card tarot, had no suit of Swords, and therefore contained no Six of Swords. It is relevant as a precursor to the northern Italian fashion for enhanced, allegorical card packs—not as the first attestation of this card.

## Visconti di Modrone / Cary–Yale, c. 1440s, Milan

These names ordinarily refer to the same surviving group, now primarily in Yale’s Beinecke Library.

**Documented fact.** The deck has elaborate gold-ground pips and an unusual expanded court: male and female pages and knights. Its pip suit corresponding to Batons employs arrows.

**Reconstruction.** It may be the oldest surviving conventional tarot group, but its original pack structure remains disputed. The surviving cards do not establish a standard 78-card form as securely as later decks.

**Six of Swords.** Where reproduced or reconstructed, it is a non-scenic pip: six weapons arranged decoratively on a tooled ground. It has no boat, travellers, astrological signs, keyword, or pictorial narrative.

## Brera–Brambilla, c. 1440s, Milan

**Documented fact.** This is another fragmentary luxury pack associated with Filippo Maria Visconti and commonly attributed to the Bembo orbit. Its surviving pips are heraldic and ornamental.

**Negative finding.** I could not establish from an accessible museum catalogue that an original Six of Swords survives in this particular fragment. Modern “complete” Brambilla decks necessarily reconstruct missing cards.

## Visconti–Sforza / Pierpont Morgan–Bergamo, c. 1450s, Milan

**Documented fact.** The Morgan group forms part of a dispersed luxury deck associated with Francesco Sforza and Bianca Maria Visconti. The Morgan catalogue describes the complete conventional pack as 21 trumps, Fool, and 56 suit cards, while acknowledging that the surviving object is incomplete. Its cards employ opaque paint on pasteboard and tooled gold grounds. [Morgan Library catalogue](https://www.themorgan.org/manuscript/158525)

**Iconography.** The Swords pips consist of repeated straight swords, ornamented with family devices, ribbons, crowns, or foliage. They remain enumerative rather than scenic.

**Disputed detail.** Attributions to Bonifacio Bembo, his workshop, and later hands have changed. Six replacement trumps were evidently executed by another hand. None of that makes the pips occult diagrams.

## Ferrarese and Este cards, c. 1450–1500

**Documented fact.** Ferrara’s court records are among the earliest written evidence for *carte da trionfi*. Surviving Este-associated fragments show decorative Italian-suit pips.

**Negative finding.** No secure card-specific documentary description of a Ferrarese Six of Swords was located. Surviving loose pips can be difficult to assign to a named historical pack, patron, or workshop.

## The so-called “Mantegna Tarocchi,” c. 1465–1475, Ferrara or northeastern Italy

**Documented fact.** These are two series of fifty educational or encyclopedic engravings representing social estates, Apollo and the Muses, liberal arts, virtues, cosmological principles, and the First Cause.

**Negative finding.** They are not a tarot pack, have no ordinary suits, and contain no Six of Swords. “Tarocchi” is a later collector’s label.

## Sola Busca, engraved 1491; painted c. 1490s, northern Italy/Venice

**Documented fact.** The Sola Busca is the earliest surviving substantially complete tarot with fully illustrated or emblematic numbered suit cards. It was engraved in 1491; the famous painted set was illuminated afterward.

### Its Six of Swords

The card is not Waite’s ferry. It is a self-contained Renaissance emblematic composition organized around six weapons, with the deck’s characteristic antiquarian, military, and pseudo-classical vocabulary.

### Relationship to Pamela Colman Smith

- **Documented fact:** Photographs of the Sola Busca were displayed at the British Museum in 1908, before Smith made the Waite deck.
- **Scholarly reconstruction:** Smith probably consulted those photographs. Specific parallels are especially strong for the Waite–Smith Three of Swords and Ten of Wands; other cards share compositional ideas.
- **Disputed claim:** That the **Sola Busca Six of Swords itself** supplied Smith’s boat scene.
- **Finding:** I found no card-specific visual evidence for that claim. The Sola Busca six has neither ferry nor cloaked passengers. The correct formulation is that the deck influenced Smith’s pip project generally, not that its Six of Swords is the source of her Six.
- **Alternative documented context:** Smith had already published maritime imagery, including her headpiece for “The Rim of the Sea” in *The Green Sheaf* no. 6 (1903). That does not prove a direct source but establishes the motif within her independent repertoire. [Yellow Nineties archive](https://1890s.ca/gsv6-smith-sea-illustration/)

## Rosenwald and Budapest sheets, late fifteenth or early sixteenth century

These uncut or partly preserved woodcut sheets are crucial for reconstructing inexpensive popular tarot patterns.

**Documented fact.** Their pips are schematic repeated suit signs rather than Waite-like narratives.

**Negative finding.** Their survival and card identifications are incomplete. I found no contemporary explanatory text assigning a meaning to their Six of Swords. Claims that a floral pip encoded a secret doctrine are therefore retrospective.

## Minchiate, Florence, sixteenth–nineteenth centuries

**Documented fact.** Minchiate expanded the trump series to 40 while retaining four Italian suits. It was a separate game family, not simply a “97-card esoteric tarot.”

**Iconography.** Its Swords pips use relatively legible weapon forms and decorative arrangements; the Six remains a six-sign numeral card.

**Game implication.** In the long suits, numbered cards rank according to the suit’s orientation. The exact hierarchy must be taken from the particular Florentine or Genoese rules; no independent divinatory meaning was attached to the Six.

## Bolognese tarot / Tarocchino

**Documented fact.** Bolognese packs were shortened for play. The 2–5 numeral cards were removed from each suit.

**Consequent fact.** The Six of Swords becomes the lowest surviving numeral in that suit. This is a game-design fact, not a mystical elevation of six.

**Iconography.** Traditional Bolognese cards use repeated suit signs, often straighter and more weapon-like than Marseille arches. Some early Bolognese sheets are fragmentary, so neat lineages from one woodblock to Marseille remain reconstructions.

## Sicilian tarot

**Documented fact.** The surviving Sicilian game uses a distinctive shortened 63-card pack. Swords rank downward from King, Queen, Horse, Maid, 10, 9, 8, 7, 6, 5, and sometimes 4. Numeral cards, including Six of Swords, have no card-point value. [Rules of Sicilian Tarocchi](https://www.pagat.com/tarot/sicilian.html)

**Iconography.** Modern Sicilian game cards identify the numeral and suit; the Six remains a plain suit card.

## Tarot de Marseille lineage

### Jean Noblet, Paris, c. 1650–1670

**Documented fact.** Noblet is the earliest substantially preserved deck of the pattern now called Tarot de Marseille.

**Crucial lacuna.** Original Noblet cards Six through Ten of Swords are missing. Any “Noblet Six of Swords” sold today is a restorer’s reconstruction, not a surviving seventeenth-century card. [Noblet conservation note](https://tarology.fr/2020/09/16/jean-noblet/)

### Jacques Viéville, Paris, c. 1650

The surviving Swords pips already show the interlaced, bowed-blade convention: long curved forms cross to produce a pointed oval or mandorla.

### Jean Dodal, Lyon, c. 1701–1715

Six blue/red/black curved sword-forms interlace symmetrically, normally three from each side. Handles or terminal forms appear at the margins. A small flower or stem occupies the central opening.

### Pierre Madenié, Dijon, 1709

This is the earliest dated representative of the Marseille Type II pattern. The six continues the woven blade arrangement, regularized for stencil printing.

### Nicolas Conver, Marseille, 1760/1761

The Six presents three nested pairs of arched, interlaced blades. The central almond contains floral ornament. Red, blue, yellow, black, and unprinted ground articulate the pattern.

**Documented function:** enumeration and suit recognition.

**Traditional symbolic reading:** the central growth suggests life or thought protected within conflict.

**Evidence qualification:** no eighteenth-century Conver text has been found explaining it that way. Such plant-as-soul readings are later Marseille divination.

### Grimaud/Paul Marteau, 1930 onward

Marteau regularized a Conver-derived pattern for Grimaud’s *Ancien Tarot de Marseille*. Lines and stencil colours became cleaner and more repeatable. Marteau’s interpretive writing greatly spread symbolic colour-reading.

### Camoin–Jodorowsky, 1997

Philippe Camoin and Alejandro Jodorowsky produced a digital/composite “restoration” based on multiple historic decks.

- **Documented fact:** It is a modern reconstruction.
- **Deck-makers’ claim:** It restores lost details of an archetypal Marseille tarot.
- **Disputed claim:** That the reconstructed details reproduce one ancient original.
- **Six of Swords:** The central flower and intersecting blades are sharpened into a deliberately readable symbolic organism. Jodorowsky and Marianne Costa interpret number, colour, suit, and visual growth as a psychospiritual language.

## Besançon pattern, eighteenth–nineteenth centuries

**Documented fact.** Besançon makers replaced the controversial Papess and Pope with **Juno and Jupiter**, particularly in regions where Protestant or mixed-confessional markets resisted papal figures.

**Card-specific effect:** None. The Six of Swords remains a conventional pip. Juno/Jupiter substitutions concern trumps II and V.

## Etteilla’s Grand Etteilla, c. 1788–1789, Paris

**Documented fact.** Etteilla reorganized and renamed the tarot as an Egyptian “Book of Thoth,” numbered all cards through 78, printed upright and reversed meanings, and partly equated Italian suits with French ones.

The card commonly numbered **58** is labelled:

- **Le Six d’Épée**
- **Le Six de Pique**

One historical printing shows six compact sword/spade-like emblems in two rows, with an inserted male bust below. The ordinary and tarot suit names appear vertically. [Digitized *Grand Etteilla*](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/2/2f/Le_grand_Etteilla%2C_ou%2C_l%27art_de_tirer_les_cartes_..._Le_tout_recueilli_..._%28IA_b29321220%29.pdf)

This is a radical change: the card becomes an indexed divinatory token whose printed words control its interpretation.

## Oswald Wirth, 1889

**Negative finding.** Wirth’s 1889 *Les 22 Arcanes du Tarot kabbalistique* was a set of 22 trumps. It contained no Six of Swords. Later “Wirth” 78-card editions supplement his system and should not be silently attributed to the 1889 deck.

## Golden Dawn manuscripts / *Book T*, c. 1890s

The design prescribed in *Book T* has two hands, each holding two crossing swords, with additional swords arranged through the centre; a rose occupies the meeting point. The full entry assigns Mercury and Aquarius symbols, the title “Lord of Earned Success,” Tiphareth of Vav, and angelic rulers.

This is neither Marseille floralism nor Waite’s ferry: it is a magical diagram synthesizing number, element, decan, planet, divine name, and colour.

### Wang reconstruction, 1978

Robert Wang’s *Golden Dawn Tarot*, under Israel Regardie’s guidance, converts the manuscript specifications into clean ritual plates. Its Six displays the crossed-sword/rose design and astrological glyphs.

### Cicero reconstruction, 1991–2000s

Sandra Tabatha and Chic Cicero’s *Golden Dawn Magical Tarot* provides a richer, more painterly realization of the same instructions. It is a modern reconstruction of Golden Dawn documents, not an excavated Victorian deck.

## Waite–Smith, 1909; *Pictorial Key*, 1910

Pamela Colman Smith depicts:

- a standing boatman pushing a long pole;
- a seated, cloaked adult;
- a child;
- six upright swords embedded in or standing against the forward part of the boat;
- rippled water on one side and smoother water toward the farther shore;
- low land and trees in the distance.

Waite describes it as a ferryman carrying passengers and says the cargo is light. His divinatory list is: “journey by water, route, way, envoy, commissionary, expedient.” Reversed: “declaration, confession, publicity.” [Waite, *The Pictorial Key to the Tarot*](https://sacred-texts.com/tarot/pkt/pktsw06.htm)

### What changed

- The enumerative pip became a complete narrative.
- Mathers/Etteilla’s voyage and messenger vocabulary became literal transportation.
- The cloaked passenger introduced sorrow, exile, or bereavement without Waite definitively identifying the person.
- The swords became freight or a barrier within the craft.
- The contrasted water encouraged the now-common “moving from troubled to calmer waters” reading.

### Identity of the passengers

**Documented fact:** Waite calls them a man and woman or ferryman and passengers depending on edition/description, but supplies no biography.

**Modern traditions:** widow and child; refugees; a family fleeing war; souls ferried by Charon; Mary and Jesus fleeing to Egypt.

**Finding:** These are interpretive analogies, not identities documented by Smith or Waite.

## Paul Foster Case / BOTA, 1931 onward

Case’s BOTA tarot visually retains the Waite–Smith model for the minor cards where published or used in instructional contexts, while embedding it in Golden Dawn-style Qabalah and astrology.

**Documented dependence:** Case inherited the Tiphareth/Mercury-in-Aquarius framework from the Golden Dawn current.

**Negative finding:** I did not find a securely accessible first-edition Case passage giving distinct upright and reversed Six of Swords wording. Attributing contemporary “recovery/transition” lists to Case without page evidence would be unsafe.

## Thoth, painted 1938–1943; first full commercial publication 1969

Crowley titles the card **SCIENCE**. Frieda Harris paints six swords converging in an exact radial geometry. Their hilts and blades create a sixfold intellectual instrument or hexagram-like structure; a central rose-cross/solar centre receives the points. Mercury and Aquarius glyphs appear.

Crowley writes that Mercury in Aquarius represents intelligence and humanity and calls the design a balance of faculties “hardly won” and difficult to maintain. [Crowley, *The Book of Thoth*, Six of Swords](https://book-of-thoth.net/Six-of-Swords-Science)

### Meaning of the redesign

- **Golden Dawn “Earned Success”** becomes **Science**.
- The boat journey disappears.
- Success is intellectual coordination: separate analytical blades agree at a centre.
- Harris turns *Book T*’s diagram into a modernist image of method, measurement, geometry, and unstable equilibrium.
- The central Tiphareth point reconciles the otherwise destructive nature of Swords.

Crowley and Harris made the deck between 1938 and 1943. A limited monochrome presentation appeared in the 1944 *Book of Thoth*; the full deck was not commercially issued until 1969.

## Notable modern redesigns

- **Aquarian Tarot, David Palladini, 1970:** compresses Waite’s ferry into stylized Art Deco/Aquarian imagery.
- **Morgan–Greer, Bill Greer/Lloyd Morgan, 1979:** close-crops the Waite scene, intensifying psychological immediacy.
- **Motherpeace, Vicki Noble and Karen Vogel, 1981:** replaces patriarchal linear narrative with feminist, circular, communal imagery.
- **Haindl Tarot, Hermann Haindl, 1980s:** uses the title “Science” and abstract/elemental symbolism, demonstrating Thoth influence.
- **Wild Unknown, Kim Krans, 2012:** removes human travellers; six swords and horizontal colour movement preserve passage abstractly.
- **Light Seer’s Tarot, Chris-Anne, 2019:** recasts the passage as contemporary emotional transition.

These are **modern inventions** built primarily from either Waite’s journey or Crowley’s intellectual equilibrium.

# The game

## Original function

**Documented fact.** Tarot arose as a trick-taking game in northern Italy. The numbered suit cards were functional playing cards before any surviving evidence of tarot divination. The earliest references cluster in Milan, Ferrara, Florence, and Bologna during the 1440s–1450s. [Metropolitan Museum](https://www.metmuseum.org/perspectives/tarot-2)

## Rank

There is no single universal rank.

- In many Italian and French tarot games, Swords and Batons are **long suits**: King high, then Queen, Knight, Page, 10 downward to Ace. Under this rule the Six beats 5, 4, 3, 2, Ace and loses to 7 upward and courts.
- Cups and Coins often reverse the numeral direction, Ace high over 2, 3, and so forth, beneath the courts.
- In Bolognese Tarocchino the 2–5 are absent, making Six the lowest numeral of Swords.
- In Sicilian rules, Six lies between 7 and 5 and has no independent point value.
- In French Tarot the suit is French-suited, so there is a **Six of Spades**, not a Six of Swords; ordinary suit cards generally carry no card points.
- Austrian Tarock packs use French suits. Depending on the game, low cards are removed; a particular 54-, 42-, or 40-card pack may not contain the Six of Spades at all.

## Point value

In the major surviving tarot-game families, a plain numeral such as the Six normally has **no counting-point value**. It matters by taking tricks, following suit, and being captured with counters. Court cards and oudler/honour trumps carry the principal points.

## The Fool

- In classical Italian and French play, the Fool is an **Excuse**: it may be played instead of following suit and is ordinarily recovered by its owner in exchange for a low card.
- In many Central European Tarock games, the Fool became the highest trump, often numbered XXII and called **Sküs**.
- The Fool’s treatment does not alter the Six’s inherent rank but changes tactics and pack structure.

## Trump-order disputes

These concern the pack, not the Six directly.

Historians distinguish regional families conventionally called:

1. **Order A**, associated especially with Bologna/Ferrara;
2. **Order B**, associated with Milan;
3. **Order C**, associated with Florence and later Marseille-derived sequences.

Justice and Fortitude/Strength exchange positions in later occult decks:

- Marseille normally has Justice VIII and Strength XI.
- Waite places Strength VIII and Justice XI.
- Crowley retains VIII for Adjustment and XI for Lust within his own renaming scheme.

The Papess/Pope became Juno/Jupiter in Besançon; the Tower is *La Maison Dieu* in Marseille; Death often lacks a printed name; the Fool is traditionally unnumbered. None of these variations renames or renumbers Six of Swords as a trump.

## Air and Vav

**Esoteric attribution.** Golden Dawn systems map:

- Wands → Fire → Yod
- Cups → Water → Heh
- Swords → Air → Vav
- Pentacles → Earth → final Heh

Thus Six of Swords is Tiphareth expressed through **Vav/Air**, not “the Hebrew letter Vav” assigned to this card as a unique path. Vav belongs to the whole Swords suit in this formula.

## Sephira 6: Tiphareth

*Book T* describes the Sixes under Tiphareth as “Definite accomplishment. Thing carried out.” This explains **Earned Success**: the disruptive Five/Geburah resolves temporarily into the solar balance of Six/Tiphareth. [*Book T*, pp. 27–38](https://benebellwen.com/wp-content/uploads/2013/02/mathers-and-felkin-golden-dawn-book-t-the-tarot-1888.pdf)

## Decan

The Six governs the middle ten degrees of Aquarius, conventionally 10°–20° Aquarius, with Mercury as decan ruler under the Golden Dawn’s Chaldean planetary sequence.

This is an **esoteric attribution**, not a medieval astrological meaning. *Book T* explicitly places it among “the thirty-six Decans of the Zodiac.”

## Tzaddi/Heh

Crowley’s Tzaddi/Heh exchange concerns the Emperor and Star trumps:

- Golden Dawn: Emperor = Heh/Aries; Star = Tzaddi/Aquarius.
- Crowley: Emperor = Tzaddi/Aries; Star = Heh/Aquarius.

It does **not** change the Six of Swords, whose attribution remains Mercury in Aquarius. Confusing the Star’s Aquarius path with the Six’s Aquarius decan is a category error.

## Courts

The requested sub-element and zodiacal span for courts do not belong to this pip. In *Book T*, the Swords court family embodies Air in differing elemental modes; the Prince/King of Swords spans part of Capricorn and Aquarius, while Queen and Knight ranges follow the Golden Dawn’s separate 30-degree court scheme. None replaces the Six’s fixed second decan.

# Divinatory meanings by author

Short quotations are retained because wording differences are historically important.

## Etteilla, late eighteenth century

**Documented lineage, imperfect textual access.** Etteilla associates the card with travel or a journey and, reversed, with declaration or avowal. The exact vocabulary varies among Grand Etteilla I–III printings and later disciples.

The strongest accessible physical evidence is card 58 itself, labelled *Le Six d’Épée / Le Six de Pique*. Mathers says his additional material is “chiefly taken from Etteilla,” but that warning does not prove that every word in his list is verbatim Etteilla.

Therefore:

- Upright: journey, voyage, messenger/envoy.
- Reversed: declaration, proposal, revelation.
- **Status:** Etteilla tradition; exact first-edition phrasing remains an open bibliographical question.

## S. L. MacGregor Mathers, 1888

Mathers prints:

- Upright: “Envoy, Messenger, Voyage, Travel.”
- Reversed: “Declaration, Love proposed, Revelation, Surprise.”

[*The Tarot: Its Occult Signification*, p. 16 in the scanned edition](https://www.howardnema.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/12/the-tarot-1888-by-macgregor-mathers-1.pdf)

This is the clearest bridge between Etteilla’s French cartomancy and Waite.

## Golden Dawn *Book T*, c. 1890s

The card means labor, success after anxiety, journeying, and a sometimes presumptuous but intelligent nature. Its success is qualified: it is earned, not effortless, and may be temporary because Air remains mutable.

**Conflict:** Golden Dawn success is more affirmative than Etteilla/Mathers’s neutral travel.

## A. E. Waite, 1910

- Upright: “journey by water, route, way, envoy, commissionary, expedient.”
- Reversed: “declaration, confession, publicity.”

[*Pictorial Key to the Tarot*, “Six of Swords”](https://sacred-texts.com/tarot/pkt/pktsw06.htm)

Waite preserves Mathers’s envoy/journey and declaration pattern. The now-standard phrases “moving on” and “calmer waters” are inferences from Smith’s image, not Waite’s keyword list.

## Aleister Crowley, 1944

Crowley calls it **Science** and describes an equilibrium of intellectual and moral faculties achieved through work. Mercury operates beneficially in Aquarius, but the balance is difficult to maintain.

- Upright or essential meaning: intelligence, analysis, scientific method, exact discrimination, coordinated mental power.
- Shadow: over-intellectualization, instability of a hard-won balance.
- Reversed: Crowley does not provide a separate conventional reversed list; Thoth practice usually reads the card through dignity, context, or excess/defect rather than mechanical inversion.

[Crowley, *The Book of Thoth*](https://www.rahoorkhuit.net/library/crowley/equinox/volume3/vol_3_no_05.pdf)

## Paul Foster Case

**Negative finding.** Case accepts the Golden Dawn framework and reads Swords as formative mental force, but I did not verify a distinct, quotable Six-of-Swords upright/reversed entry in an accessible authorized edition. Modern lists attributed to “BOTA” should not be treated as Case quotations without a lesson number and page.

## Rachel Pollack

In *Seventy-Eight Degrees of Wisdom* (1980/1983), Pollack reads the Waite image as a passage away from difficulty while noting the emotional flatness or sorrow of the travellers. Later readers commonly summarize her approach as transition, recovery, and carrying past thought-patterns into a new situation.

**Reversals:** blocked or resisted transition, difficulty escaping the past, or a journey that cannot yet be completed.

**Evidence caution:** No legal open-text edition was located from which to quote her wording. These are concise paraphrases, not quotations.

## Mary K. Greer

Greer’s approach treats reversal dynamically rather than as one fixed opposite. A reversed Six may indicate:

- delay or refusal to move;
- returning;
- an internal rather than external passage;
- release of mental baggage;
- intensified or diminished journey imagery.

This follows the reversal methods developed in *The Complete Book of Tarot Reversals* (2002), not a single immutable dictionary gloss. Pollack explicitly credited Greer with developing many possible operations for reversals. [Pollack interview](https://rachelpollack.com/reviews/interviewwilkes.html)

# Esoteric and symbolic readings

## Alchemical

**Modern esoteric development.** The medieval card has no documented alchemical emblems.

Later readers treat:

- boat = vessel or *vas*;
- water = prima materia/unconscious;
- swords = discriminating operation;
- sixfold centre = reconciliation;
- farther bank = completed stage of transformation.

The likely spreaders are twentieth-century Hermetic and Jungian tarot writers, not Renaissance cardmakers. Crowley’s “Science” encourages operational/alchemical readings, although his primary explanation is astrological and Qabalistic.

## Kabbalistic

**First secure card-specific appearance:** Golden Dawn *Book T*.

Tiphareth is the sixth sephira, the solar centre mediating Chesed and Geburah. In the Air world/suit, it signifies mental forces brought into proportion. The six sword-points converge at the centre: multiplicity submits to a harmonizing principle.

This is Hermetic Qabalah, not a demonstrated Jewish interpretation of medieval playing cards.

## Astrological

**First secure complete attribution:** Golden Dawn.

Mercury signifies language, calculation, transit, exchange, and analysis. Aquarius contributes system, abstraction, collectivity, and fixed-air stability. Together they can support:

- scientific method;
- planned travel;
- communication across distance;
- rational solutions;
- mental detachment.

The last four are interpretations of the attribution, not independent ancient meanings.

## Hermetic

The Golden Dawn integrated number, suit, colour scale, planetary decan, divine names, angels, and geomantic/rule-based divination. Crowley retained the skeleton but changed the title from an outcome—success—to a method of knowing—science.

## Christian

**Historical caution.** A sixfold harmony can be related to the six days of Creation, and a woman and child crossing water can be compared to the Flight into Egypt. Neither identification appears in Waite’s account, and neither is evidenced in early Six of Swords cards.

“Tiphareth as the Christ-centre” belongs to Christian Cabala and occult-Christian interpretation. It is not a property documented in the fifteenth-century pip.

## Jungian

**Modern invention/tradition.** The boat becomes the ego-container crossing unconscious water; the boatman becomes psychopomp; the veiled adult represents grief, withdrawal, or a persona between identities; the child is the vulnerable future self. The far shore is individuation’s next stage rather than final completion.

This style grew from twentieth-century depth psychology and the post-1960s psychological tarot. Jung himself did not publish a card-by-card Six of Swords exegesis.

## Marseille numerology

Jodorowsky and Costa treat six as the first movement beyond the stable square/four and conflict/five toward beauty, receptivity, or pleasure. Swords signify mind or intellect; the central flower is therefore flowering consciousness.

This reading is internally coherent within their system but is a modern hermeneutic, not a recovered explanation by Dodal or Conver.

# People and works

## Directly attached

- **Bonifacio Bembo and workshop:** probable makers of major Visconti-family cards; connection is to the early suit tradition, not the ferry image.
- **Sola Busca engraver and illuminator:** anonymous; created the first surviving extensive emblematic pip cycle.
- **Jean Dodal, Nicolas Conver, Paul Marteau:** transmitted and standardized the Marseille curved-blade Six.
- **Jean-Baptiste Alliette/Etteilla:** made the pip an explicitly labelled divinatory card.
- **S. L. MacGregor Mathers:** carried Etteilla-derived meanings into English and helped form Golden Dawn tarot.
- **Pamela Colman Smith:** invented the canonical modern ferry scene.
- **A. E. Waite:** supplied its published interpretive frame.
- **Frieda Harris:** designed the geometric Thoth realization.
- **Aleister Crowley:** renamed it “Science” and explained its Mercury–Aquarius equilibrium.
- **Paul Foster Case:** transmitted Golden Dawn-style tarot in BOTA.
- **Alejandro Jodorowsky and Marianne Costa:** spread Marseille numerological and psychospiritual reading.
- **Rachel Pollack and Mary K. Greer:** major spreaders of modern psychological and reader-centred interpretation.

## Literary and artistic claims requiring restraint

### Italo Calvino, *The Castle of Crossed Destinies*

Calvino constructs stories through tarot images, using the Visconti and Marseille traditions. Tarot is structurally central to the work.

**Negative finding:** I found no reliable scholarship identifying the Six of Swords as a uniquely privileged card in the novel. It may occur in layouts or editions, but a card-specific claim requires a cited sequence and page.

### T. S. Eliot, *The Waste Land*

Madame Sosostris presents several invented or adapted tarot cards, including “the drowned Phoenician Sailor” and “the one-eyed merchant.”

**Negative finding:** Eliot does not name the Six of Swords. Identifying the drowned sailor or water imagery with it is a modern association.

Yeats belonged to the Golden Dawn and knew its tarot correspondences.

**Negative finding:** No verified poem or prose passage specifically centred on the Six of Swords was located.

### Alejandro Jodorowsky’s films

Tarot imagery and initiatory symbolism pervade *El Topo*, *The Holy Mountain*, and his later work.

**Negative finding:** No documented shot-for-shot quotation of the Six of Swords was established. General boat, passage, or initiation imagery is insufficient.

*The Seventh Seal* uses Death, chess, sea, and medieval allegory.

**Negative finding:** No reliable evidence links a Bergman composition specifically to the Six of Swords.

Claims that the card derives from Charon, the Flight into Egypt, cathedral geometry, or a specific medieval building remain speculative unless a demonstrable compositional source is produced. No secure architectural quotation was located.

# Controversies and disputes

## Egyptian origin

**Legend.** Court de Gébelin announced in 1781 that tarot was an Egyptian book surviving the destruction of ancient libraries. He offered speculative etymologies and symbolic resemblance, not archival evidence. His contemporary Comte de Mellet expanded the Egyptian and Hebrew framework.

The Morgan Library correctly treats this as the beginning of tarot’s occult reappraisal, not evidence of its medieval origin. [Morgan, “Occult Tarot”](https://www.themorgan.org/exhibitions/online/tarot/11)

## “Book of Thoth”

**Esoteric tradition.** The name descends from the 1781 Egyptian hypothesis and was adopted by Etteilla and Crowley. No pharaonic object or Egyptian text contains the 78-card tarot.

## Romani or “Gypsy” origin

**Legend.** Gébelin associated the pack’s diffusion with people then incorrectly believed to be Egyptian. Boiteau d’Ambly and Jean-Alexandre Vaillant spread the Romani-carrier story in the nineteenth century.

**Historical correction.** Tarot is documented at northern Italian courts before evidence supporting any Romani transmission. Modern scholarship places Romani linguistic origins in India and rejects an ancient tarot connection.

## Cathar origin

**Disputed modern claim/legend.** Some twentieth-century writers interpret Marseille trumps as encoded Cathar theology or a survival of southern French heresy.

**Card-specific finding:** No Cathar text, object, or trial record identifies a Six of Swords or a 78-card tarot. The early documentary concentration in northern Italy and the ordinary playing-card structure weigh against the claim.

## Templar origin

**Legend.** Claims that persecuted Templars encoded doctrine in tarot appeared centuries after the order’s suppression. No fourteenth-century Templar inventory or text documents tarot, much less this card.

## Ancient Hebrew origin

**Esoteric attribution, not historical genealogy.** The numerical coincidence of 22 trumps and 22 Hebrew letters was exploited in 1781 and systematized by Lévi and the Golden Dawn. The Six of Swords itself has no unique Hebrew letter. “Vav” is its suit/world attribution only in the Golden Dawn tetragrammaton model.

## Sola Busca as the source of Smith’s Six

**Disputed and unsupported card-specific claim.**

Evidence supports Smith’s access to Sola Busca reproductions and probable borrowing elsewhere. But the Sola Busca Six does not depict her boat. The claim should be rejected in its strong form and retained only as a general-deck influence hypothesis.

## Why do Marseille swords look like scimitars?

**Scholarly reconstruction.** The bowed forms may descend from curved weapon conventions ultimately related to Mamluk suit signs, then became increasingly abstract through copying and the need to interlace multiple signs.

**Open dispute.** On many cards both ends look hilt-like, and later makers copied the convention even where literal weapon anatomy was obscure. Whether that began as a cutter’s reversal, symmetrical stylization, or inheritance from a lost Italian prototype is unresolved.

## Were the Waite passengers refugees?

**Modern invention/tradition.** The image strongly permits exile and migration readings. Waite does not identify a war, nation, widowhood, or refugee status.

## Are the swords piercing the boat?

**Visual ambiguity.** The blades seem to stand vertically inside or against the hull; literal puncture would make the craft sink. Readings that the travellers “carry their problems” are modern symbolic responses to the visual paradox.

## “Moving from rough water to calm water”

**Modern traditional reading.** Smith visibly differentiates the water, making this inference persuasive. But the exact slogan is not Waite’s printed meaning and should not be backdated to Etteilla or Marseille.

## Reversal as exact opposite

**Methodological dispute.** Etteilla and Waite print separate reversed meanings. Crowley’s Thoth practice emphasizes dignity and imbalance. Greer offers many reversal operations. Accordingly, “reversed = unable to move on” is one modern method, not a universally inherited meaning.

## Misprints and transmission errors

- *Book T* copies contain transcription and typographical inconsistencies; one accessible copy accidentally prints “9 of Swords — Pisces — Material Happiness” where the table’s logic requires the **Nine of Cups**. This is a copying error, not a new correspondence.
- Court de Gébelin’s plates were sometimes laterally reversed in printing; his Temperance numbering also required correction in the text. [Tyson transcription and notes](https://www.hermetica.info/GameOfTarots.pdf)
- “Commissionary” in Waite is an unusual inherited English word and is frequently modernized to commission, agent, or messenger.
- Dates such as “Conver 1761” and “Conver 1760” coexist because scholars distinguish engraving, imprint, or conventional catalogue dating.
- “Cary–Yale” and “Visconti di Modrone” are not separate decks in standard modern usage; listing them separately can double-count one group.
- Some reproductions call the Waite pack “Rider Tarot,” obscuring Smith’s authorship. “Rider–Waite–Smith” is the more accurate modern name.
- Claims that the Thoth deck was “published in 1944” conflate the book’s limited card plates with the full colour deck’s 1969 commercial publication.

# Open questions

1. Which surviving mid-fifteenth-century Milanese group contains the earliest securely catalogued original Six of Swords? Museum databases often describe dispersed groups rather than indexing every pip.

2. What is the precise provenance and earliest printed wording of Etteilla’s Six d’Épée meanings? Later Grand Etteilla editions and disciples vary, while Mathers acknowledges dependence without itemizing it.

3. Did Smith base the ferry composition on a specific literary illustration, print, stage scene, or her own earlier maritime work? No decisive model has been demonstrated.

4. Are the figures intended as a family? The adult’s gender and relationship to the child remain visually and textually underdetermined.

5. What exact workshop sequence produced the abstract Marseille sword arches? Surviving popular cards are too late and too fragmentary to identify a single prototype.

6. How much of the *Book T* small-card system was Mathers’s own work, and how much came from earlier cipher manuscripts, astrological tables, or collaborators? The surviving documents show the system, not a complete authorship history.

7. Can a first-edition Case lesson be located that gives an independently worded Six of Swords divination? Until then, Case-specific keyword lists should be treated cautiously.

8. No primary evidence located ties this individual card to Cathars, Templars, Egyptian priests, Jewish Kabbalists before the modern occult revival, Calvino’s controlling card structure, Eliot’s drowned sailor, Yeats’s poetry, Bergman, or a particular historic building. Those absences are substantive findings.
