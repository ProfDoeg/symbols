# Queen of Swords: Research Dossier

## Method and evidence labels

This dossier distinguishes:

- **[Documented fact]** Directly attested by a surviving card, archival record, contemporary printed text, or institutional catalogue.
- **[Scholarly reconstruction]** An inference accepted or seriously entertained by specialist historians but not stated by contemporary evidence.
- **[Tradition]** A recurring interpretation transmitted through cartomancy or deck-making.
- **[Esoteric attribution]** A deliberate occult correspondence, not a historical claim about the fifteenth-century card.
- **[Disputed]** A claim for which specialists or material evidence disagree.
- **[Legend]** A historical-origin story unsupported by contemporary evidence.
- **[Modern invention]** A demonstrably recent reinterpretation or redesign.

A necessary limitation: many works requested in the general template—Marziano da Tortona, the *Sermones de ludo cum aliis*, Gébelin, Lévi, Paul Christian, and the Trump-order literature—concern the trumps, not individual suit courts. They are included where they illuminate the pack, but they contain no recoverable Queen-of-Swords doctrine. Silence is reported rather than filled with later symbolism.

The Morgan identifies its card simply and materially: a “Queen, wearing crown and armor, seated, holds sword in right hand.” The museum dates the assembled Visconti–Sforza group to Milan, about 1450–80, with most cards probably made for Francesco Sforza after 1450. ### Heh, Vav, and a frequent transcription error

**[Esoteric attribution]** In the Golden Dawn’s Tetragrammaton scheme:

- Knights/Kings: Yod, Fire
- Queens: first Heh, Water
- Princes/Kings: Vav, Air
- Princesses: final Heh, Earth
- Wands: Yod/Fire
- Cups: first Heh/Water
- Swords: Vav/Air
- Pentacles: final Heh/Earth

Thus the Queen of Swords is **first Heh of Vav**, not *final Heh of Vav*. The latter belongs to the Princess of Swords and appears in modern notes where “Queen” was accidentally left in a Princess paragraph. The original *Book T* formula “Water of Air” resolves the matter. [Book T transcription](https://www.mr-kaplan.com/Library/Mathers/Book-T.html).

## Iconography deck by deck

### Antecedent: Mamluk nāʾib cards, fourteenth–fifteenth centuries

**[Documented fact]** The nearly complete Mamluk pack discovered in Istanbul has four suits—polo sticks, cups, coins, and swords—and three male court ranks: *malik* (king), *nāʾib malik* (deputy king), and *thānī nāʾib* (second deputy). Human figures were replaced by inscriptions and ornamental suit emblems.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** European Latin-suited cards probably descend, directly or through intermediary trade networks, from this general card family. The shared Cups, Coins, Swords, and polo-stick/Baton structure is strong comparative evidence.

**What it does not establish:** there is no Mamluk Queen of Swords. The European queen was an adaptation of an all-male court system, not a translated *nāʾib* title. The exact route from Mamluk cards to northern Italian tarot remains incompletely documented. [L. A. Mayer’s foundational study](https://www.persee.fr/doc/bifao_0255-0962_1939_num_38_1_1998).

### Cary–Yale / Visconti di Modrone, Milan, about 1441–47

**[Documented fact]** This pack preserves a crowned Queen of Swords. She is enthroned, shown in profile, holds a long straight sword, and appears in sumptuous court dress on a worked precious-metal ground. The sword court is militarized more strongly than the cup or coin courts. [Surviving image from the Beinecke source](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Cary-Yale_Tarot_deck_-_Queen_of_Swords.jpg).

This unusual pack had, or was designed to have, six courts in each suit: king and queen, male and female mounted figures, and male and female attendants. That is not evidence for an ancient matriarchal tarot; it is a Milanese luxury-pack innovation.

**[Disputed]** Dating and occasion. The pack has been associated with Filippo Maria Visconti’s marriage to Maria of Savoy in 1428, with later Visconti-Sforza occasions, and with heraldic/dynastic programs. A mid-1440s date is common, but the precise commission is not documented.

**[Disputed]** Portrait identity. Gertrude Moakley and Stuart Kaplan participated in a wider tradition of identifying figures in Visconti cards with named Visconti or Sforza family members. No inscription names the Queen of Swords, and no securely documented portrait comparison establishes her as Maria of Savoy, Bianca Maria Visconti, or another individual. It is safer to call her a dynastic court type.

### Brambilla–Brera pack, Milan, about 1440s

**[Documented fact]** The Brambilla pack, attributed to Bonifacio Bembo’s milieu and now at the Pinacoteca di Brera, is fragmentary. Its sword pips use conspicuously curved blades.

**Finding:** a securely identified original Queen of Swords is not among the commonly catalogued surviving Brambilla cards. Descriptions of “the Brambilla Queen of Swords” frequently conflate the pack with the Cary–Yale or Visconti–Sforza queen.

### Visconti–Sforza / Pierpont Morgan–Bergamo, Milan, about 1450

**[Documented fact]** The Queen of Swords survives as Morgan MS M.630.23. She sits in right-facing profile on a high-backed seat, wearing a crown, white gown, and armored sleeves or gauntlets. She holds a straight sword vertically in her right hand; the left hand is lifted. The ground is punched gold with a blue border. [Morgan image and description](https://ica.themorgan.org/manuscript/page/23/158525).

The combined surviving pack consists of 35 Morgan cards, 26 at the Accademia Carrara, and 13 in the Colleoni collection. The Morgan dates the principal campaign after Francesco Sforza’s entry into Milan in 1450 and attributes it probably to Bonifacio Bembo. [Morgan catalogue](https://www.themorgan.org/manuscript/158525).

**Meaning in its own period:** crown, throne, sword, armor, and heraldic luxury establish rank and suit. Nothing on the card documents widowhood, Air, Libra, severed heads, Jungian discrimination, or divination.

**[Disputed] Portrait claim.** Moakley and Kaplan explored family portrait identifications throughout the deck. No consensus identifies this particular woman. The museum does not name a family member.

### Ferrarese and Este material, later fifteenth century

The surviving Este, Charles VI/Gringonneur, Alessandro Sforza, Rothschild, and related Ferrarese fragments are predominantly trumps.

**Finding:** no securely catalogued Queen of Swords survives in the best-known Este fragment groups. Therefore a deck-specific iconographic narrative cannot be reconstructed.

The 1442 Ferrara payment records “*uno paro de carte da trionfi*” made available for Ercole and Sigismondo d’Este. It proves the existence of triumph packs, not the appearance or meaning of an individual Queen of Swords. [Italian transcript](https://letarot.it/Le-origini-italiane-del-Ludus-Triumphorum_pag_pg439_ita.aspx).

### Mantegna “Tarocchi,” about 1460s

**[Documented fact]** The so-called Mantegna Tarocchi are didactic engravings arranged in five ten-image series: social estates, Apollo and the Muses, liberal arts, virtues/cosmic principles, and celestial spheres.

They are neither a standard tarot pack nor playing cards and contain no Queen of Swords. Their relevance is contextual—Renaissance classificatory imagery—not genealogical evidence for this court.

### Sola Busca, probably Venetian/Ferrarese cultural sphere, 1491

**[Documented fact]** This is the earliest surviving complete 78-card tarot and the earliest complete pack whose pip cards bear independent scenes. Its courts and trumps carry classical or pseudo-classical names.

The Queen of Swords is conventionally identified as **Olimpia/Olympias**, mother of Alexander the Great. She is shown as an individualized antique heroine rather than the anonymous dynastic queen of Milan.

**[Scholarly reconstruction/disputed]** The pack contains a coherent humanistic program involving Roman and Macedonian names, military history, and possibly Hermetic-alchemical imagery. The exact program and the relationship of every name to every scene remain disputed. Calling the entire deck an “alchemical initiation manual” goes beyond what its inscriptions alone prove. [Public Domain Review facsimile](https://publicdomainreview.org/collection/sola-busca), [scholarly/esoteric study](https://avalonlibrary.net/The_Sola_Busca_Tarrochi/The_Sola-Busca_Tarocchi_The_Alchemical_Symbolism_of_a_Renaissance_Masterwork.pdf).

**Influence on Pamela Colman Smith:** documented visual similarities exist between several Sola Busca scenes and Smith’s pip illustrations. For the Queen of Swords, however, the often-repeated claim of direct borrowing is weaker than for the RWS Three of Swords or Ten of Wands. One proposed source is instead the pose of the Visconti–Sforza Queen of Swords.

### Rosenwald and Budapest sheets, late fifteenth/early sixteenth century

These woodcut sheets are vital evidence for inexpensive printed tarot, but surviving sheets are incomplete and their card assignments and datings have been repeatedly revised.

**Finding:** no securely preserved Queen of Swords supplies a continuous bridge from the Milanese queen to the Marseille figure. Assertions that every Marseille court detail can be derived from these sheets are reconstructions, not documented transmission.

### Minchiate, Florence, sixteenth–nineteenth centuries

**[Documented fact]** Minchiate enlarged the trump series to forty trump subjects plus the Fool while retaining four Latin suits and four courts per suit. The Queen of Swords remained a non-trump court.

Iconographically, printed Minchiate queens are crowned or elaborately dressed seated women carrying the suit emblem. She is not given an occult name or number. In play, she ranks immediately below the King.

The extra Minchiate trumps, regional orders, and unnumbered lowest trumps do not alter her identity.

### Bolognese tarocchino

**[Documented fact]** The shortened Bolognese pack removes low pip cards but retains courts. A Queen of Swords therefore remains part of the playing pack.

Regional reductions and the special unnumbered group of low Bolognese trumps affect trump play, not the queen’s rank. Her value remains that of a queen in point-counting families.

### Sicilian tarot

**[Documented fact]** Modern Mineo Sicilian Tarocchi ranks each suit:

**King – Queen – Horse – Maid – 10 – 9 – 8 – 7 – 6 – 5 – sometimes 4.**

The female lowest court (*Donna*) is distinct from the Queen (*Regina*). [Rules and terminology](https://www.pagat.com/tarot/sicilian.html).

This is a warning against translating every female Sicilian court as “queen.”

### Early French and Tarot de Marseille lineage

#### Jean Noblet, Paris, about 1650

The Queen is seated or half-length, crowned, and turns sideways while holding a large upright sword. The card is titled in archaic French. Her function remains immediately legible through crown and weapon.

#### Jean Dodal, Lyon, about 1701–15

Dodal regularizes the Marseille court: bold black outlines, flat stencil color, crowned sideways queen, large sword rising through the upper field. Facial and costume details differ from Noblet, but there is no documentary evidence that they encode a different divinatory character.

#### Nicolas Conver, Marseille, 1760

The Conver queen faces toward the viewer’s left, holds a red-bladed sword upright, and wears a large crown and patterned robe. Later observers sometimes see a broom-like secondary shape beside or behind the weapon.

**[Disputed/modern projection]** The “hidden broom,” pregnancy, severed arm, or secret female-initiatory symbolism is not identified by Conver or contemporary rules. Some shapes can be explained as costume, scabbard, throne, or degraded woodblock detail.

#### Curved “scimitars”

**[Documented fact]** Curving, interlaced forms dominate Marseille sword **pip cards**. These are stylized blades and framing arabesques. The court’s principal sword is usually straighter, though blades vary among printers.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** Curved blades may preserve Italian scimitar-like sword conventions or may have evolved to fit symmetric pip layouts. There is no text proving that they represent lunar, vaginal, or “feminine” energy.

#### Besançon pattern, eighteenth–nineteenth centuries

Besançon packs famously replace the Papess and Pope with Juno and Jupiter. This affects trumps II and V, not the Queen of Swords, which remains the *Reine d’Épée*.

This illustrates why regional trump renamings—Juno/Jupiter, *Maison Dieu*, or an unnumbered Fool—must not be imported into court-card history.

#### Grimaud / Paul Marteau, 1930

The Grimaud “Ancien Tarot de Marseille,” redrawn and recolored under Paul Marteau, standardized the Conver-derived pattern for mass twentieth-century circulation. Its Queen of Swords is a crowned white-haired or light-haired seated woman with an upright red sword.

Marteau later supplied occult/numerological interpretations to the image. Those readings are twentieth-century interpretations of the pattern, not transmitted seventeenth-century explanations.

#### Camoin–Jodorowsky, 1997

**[Modern reconstruction]** Philippe Camoin and Alejandro Jodorowsky produced a restored Marseille deck by comparing surviving historical packs and introducing reconstructed details and a revised palette. Its Queen follows the Conver family.

Their claim to have recovered a single original or “authentic” Tarot de Marseille is disputed: surviving Marseille packs form a historical family, not copies of a known lost master deck.

### Grand Etteilla, Paris, 1789 onward

Etteilla reorganized and numbered the entire pack from 1 to 78. His card system does not map visually or numerically onto Marseille in a simple one-for-one fashion.

**[Documented tradition]** The sword/spade queen belongs to the semantic family of widowhood, privation, absence, sterility, and poverty. The immediate source predates his tarot: Etteilla’s 1770/1773 ordinary-card cartomancy gave the Queen of Spades a widow association.

**[Disputed]** Different Grand Etteilla printings and later “Julia Orsini” manuals shift numbering and keywords. A number assigned by one edition should not be presented as the universal number of the Queen of Swords.

### Oswald Wirth, 1889

Wirth’s original 1889 deck consisted of twenty-two trumps. It has **no Queen of Swords**.

Later packs marketed under Wirth’s name sometimes add minors, but these are later editorial completions. Wirth’s major-arcana symbolism cannot supply a deck-specific Queen iconography.

### Golden Dawn manuscripts, 1888–1900; first public printing 1912

*Book T* describes:

> “A graceful woman with curly waving hair,” crowned and enthroned above “grey cumulous clouds.”

She bears a winged child’s head as crest, a drawn sword, and a newly severed bearded male head. The clouds manifest Air; the queen’s flowing, receptive rank supplies Water; the severed head visualizes discriminating intellect and liberation from obsolete thought. [Book T](https://www.mr-kaplan.com/Library/Mathers/Book-T.html).

The Golden Dawn’s official title is **Queen of the Thrones of Air**. “Queen of the Sylphs and Sylphides” is the elemental title.

#### Wang and Cicero reconstructions

Robert Wang’s *Golden Dawn Tarot* and the Ciceros’ *Golden Dawn Magical Tarot* visualize the written temple specifications. They are not surviving nineteenth-century Golden Dawn deck originals. Variations of costume, colors, posture, and background belong to the modern reconstructing artists; cloud-throne, winged-child crest, sword, and severed head come from *Book T*.

### Waite–Smith, 1909; *Pictorial Key*, 1910/1911

Pamela Colman Smith depicts a crowned queen in profile, sitting on a carved stone throne above a bank of clouds. Her right hand holds the sword perfectly upright. Her left hand extends outward with an open palm. Butterflies decorate the crown and throne; clouds, wind-bent trees, and a distant bird establish the airy environment.

Waite describes her as one whose right hand “raises the weapon vertically” while the left “is extended.” He explicitly warns that, despite the hand gesture, “her countenance is severe.” [Public-domain *Pictorial Key*](https://sacred-texts.com/tarot/pkt/index.htm?chaptersPage=3).

**Probable sources:**

- Marseille’s crowned, sideways Queen holding an upright sword.
- Golden Dawn sword/Air symbolism and court divination.
- Possibly the Visconti–Sforza queen’s profile posture.
- Smith’s theatrical training and contemporary illustration vocabulary.
- Sola Busca is a documented general source for some RWS minors, but direct dependence for this queen is not demonstrable.

The butterflies and open summoning hand are Smith’s most consequential additions. They enabled later readings of mental transformation, invitation to truth, boundaries, and impartial judgment, none of which Waite states so positively.

### BOTA, Jessie Burns Parke and Paul Foster Case, twentieth century

The BOTA deck is a line-art “corrected” Waite-derived pack intended for students to color. The Queen retains the enthroned RWS type. Case’s instructions impose Qabalistic and astrological doctrine upon the image.

**Important conflict:** Case’s *Oracle of the Tarot* assigns the Queen of Swords roughly **10° Capricorn–20° Aquarius**—January 10 to February 8—not the Golden Dawn’s 20° Virgo–20° Libra. His text calls the card mental and creative activity on the astral plane, with the vesica/ankh referring to Heh. [Case, *Oracle of the Tarot*](https://media-01.imu.nl/storage/divona.be/17547/oracle-of-tarot-paul-foster-case.pdf).

### Crowley–Harris Thoth, painted 1938–43; book 1944; full deck 1969

Lady Frieda Harris paints the Queen enthroned on clouds, partially nude, wearing a gleaming belt and sarong. Her helmet carries a child’s head, with sharp rays radiating from it. She raises the sword in her right hand and holds a newly severed bearded head in the left.

Crowley calls her:

> “the watery part of Air, the elasticity of that element, and its power of transmission.”

She is “the clear, conscious perception of Idea, the Liberator of the Mind.” The figure is a direct elaboration of *Book T*, transformed by Harris’s geometric, luminous modernism. [Crowley, *Book of Thoth*, court-card chapter](https://hermetic.com/crowley/book-of-thoth/court-cards).

Crowley adds the I Ching attribution **hexagram 28, Ta Kwo/Great Exceeding**.

### Notable modern redesigns

- **Jodorowsky–Camoin:** restores the Marseille queen as a bodily, relational figure rather than a Golden Dawn elemental spirit.
- **Rachel Pollack and feminist/secular tarot:** recast the widow from passive deprivation into the experienced, independent truth-speaker.
- **Mary K. Greer:** loosens biological-gender correspondence; a queen may represent any person, role, psychological capacity, or mode of action.
- **Robert M. Place, Alchemical Tarot:** depicts a woman with one sword held and another discarded or planted, making conscious discrimination an alchemical operation.
- **Slow Tarot/Lacey Bryant:** a contemporary oil-painting interpretation consciously embedded in the artist’s life and community. [Gallery record](https://www.moderneden.com/products/queen-of-swords-1).
- **Tarot of Architectural Objects:** maps the Queen to the Rain Vortex at Jewel Changi Airport—an explicit twenty-first-century conceptual substitution, not inherited symbolism. [Deck index](https://queenofwans.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/05/tao-lwb-locations.pdf).

## The game

### Rank

**[Documented fact]** In standard tarot’s fourteen-card suits:

**King > Queen > Knight/Cavalier > Jack/Page > 10 … Ace**

The precise pip direction changes by suit and game, but the Queen remains below King and above Cavalier.

In French Tarot she is an *honour* but not an *oudler*. Only the 1 and 21 of trumps and the Excuse are oudlers.

### Value

Several notations describe substantially the same inherited counting practice:

| System | Queen’s expression |
|---|---|
| French three-card packets | Queen + two low cards = 4 points |
| Half-point method | Queen nominally 3½, low card ½ |
| Intrinsic-plus-counters explanation | Queen 3, with packet adjustment |
| Austrian Tarock simplified rules | Queen = 4 points |
| Sicilian packet counting | Queen’s realized value depends on what accompanies it |

The frequent statement “a Queen is worth four points” is operationally correct in many modern rules but conceals the traditional packet calculation. [Comparative counting systems](https://www.pagat.com/tarot/counting.html), [French rules](https://www.letarot.net/en/rules.html).

### Trick-taking role

The Queen is an ordinary suited card:

- she must follow suit when required;
- any trump beats her unless the rules permit or require some other action;
- she captures lower cards of her own suit;
- she is vulnerable to the King;
- losing her matters because she carries card points.

She has no special divinatory or character power in game rules.

### Tarocchi, tarot, tarock, and minchiate

- **Italian tarocchi:** court hierarchy as above, subject to regional suit-ranking rules.
- **French tarot:** Queen is worth four in completed counting groups.
- **Austrian/Hungarian tarock:** queens survive even in shortened French-suited packs and normally count four.
- **Minchiate:** Queen remains a suited court; the expanded trump sequence does not change her status.
- **Bolognese tarocchino:** survives reductions that remove low pips.
- **Sicilian:** *Regina* is distinct from *Donna*.

### Trump-order disputes are not Queen-of-Swords variants

Dummett reconstructed three broad early Italian trump-order families—often called A, B, and C—from game evidence. Disputes over Justice, Fortitude, Temperance, Popess, Pope, Emperor, World, and the Fool concern the trumps alone. The Queen of Swords never enters that sequence.

Similarly:

- Fool unnumbered: relevant to trump/excuse play, not this queen.
- Justice VIII or XI: no effect on Queen of Swords.
- Strength VIII or XI: no effect.
- Papess/Pope renamed Juno/Jupiter in Besançon: no effect.
- Tower called *Maison Dieu*: no effect.

## Chronology of attestation

### About 1420: Marziano da Tortona

Marziano’s text for Filippo Maria Visconti describes a special game with sixteen classical gods and four suits of allegorical birds. It is a precursor to the invention of trumps, not a standard tarot. It contains no Queen of Swords.

### 1440: Giusto Giusti diary, Florence

**[Documented fact]** Records a specially made pack of *naibi a trionfi* given to Sigismondo Pandolfo Malatesta. It does not enumerate its courts.

### 1442: Ferrara ledger

Records payment for “*uno paro de carte da trionfi*” for Ercole and Sigismondo d’Este. This is direct documentary evidence for triumph cards, but not a named Queen of Swords. [Transcript](https://letarot.it/Le-origini-italiane-del-Ludus-Triumphorum_pag_pg439_ita.aspx).

### About 1441–47: Cary–Yale Queen of Swords

Earliest surviving candidate: the identity is visible, although the card lacks a printed name.

### About 1450: Visconti–Sforza Queen of Swords

Secure surviving crowned and armored example. [Morgan](https://www.themorgan.org/collection/tarot-cards/the-queen-of-swords).

### 1457: Ferrara, seventy-card packs

A payment mentions two packs of large triumph cards, seventy cards per game. This implies a nonstandard regional composition; it does not name the queen.

### About 1470–1500: *Sermones de ludo cum aliis*

The Dominican sermon attacks *triumphi* and lists trump subjects. It does not list individual suit courts; therefore it is not the Queen’s first verbal attestation.

### Late fifteenth century: Sola Busca

The queen becomes a named classical figure, Olympias/Olimpia.

### 1505: “tarocchi” and “taraux”

The earliest known uses of *tarocchi* at Ferrara and *taraux* at Avignon belong to the pack/game as a whole. Earlier documents call the cards *trionfi*. [Dummett discussion](https://forum.tarothistory.com/viewtopic.php?t=1185).

### Early sixteenth century: Folengo and tarot poems

Teofilo Folengo’s writings and poetic tarot compositions show the cards being used for literary characterization. No stable occult Queen-of-Swords doctrine emerges from them.

### Seventeenth century: French printed courts

Noblet and successors supply the printed named **Reyne d’Épée**, making the verbal card title directly visible.

### 1770s–1780s: Etteilla

The Queen of Spades/Swords acquires an explicit cartomantic meaning: widowhood and deprivation.

### 1888–1912: Mathers and Golden Dawn

Mathers publishes older French-style fortune-telling meanings in 1888. The Golden Dawn then creates the Water-of-Air, Virgo–Libra, severed-head synthesis; *Book T* becomes public through Crowley’s *Equinox* material in 1912.

### 1909–10: Waite–Smith

Smith’s open hand, butterflies, clouds, and solitary throne become the dominant modern visual type.

### 1938–44/1969: Thoth

Harris and Crowley render the full Golden Dawn description and intensify its doctrine of liberating intellect.

### Late twentieth century to present

Psychological, Jungian, feminist, queer, secular, and therapeutic readers increasingly detach the queen from literal widowhood and from mandatory female gender.

### The three decans

Under *Book T*, the queen’s span contains:

1. **20°–30° Virgo:** Ten of Pentacles/Disks, “Lord of Wealth,” Mercury in Virgo.
2. **0°–10° Libra:** Two of Swords, “Lord of Peace Restored,” Moon in Libra.
3. **10°–20° Libra:** Three of Swords, “Lord of Sorrow,” Saturn in Libra.

This combination helps explain the modern synthesis: material or intellectual competence, peacemaking discrimination, and sorrow. It is an occult construction from the late nineteenth century, not an embedded fifteenth-century program.

### Hebrew letters and the Emperor–Star problem

The famous Tzaddi/Heh exchange concerns **the Emperor and Star trumps**, not court-card Tetragrammaton. Crowley’s reassignment does not turn the Queen of Swords into Tzaddi or alter her formula as Heh of Vav.

## Divinatory meanings by author

### Etteilla, 1770s–1780s

**Upright:** *veuvage*, widow/widowhood; privation, absence, want, sterility, poverty.

**Reversed:** later Etteilla-family lists give a malicious or bad-tempered woman and combinations of wealth with discord or pleasure with sorrow. Exact vocabulary varies across editions.

**Finding:** Waite’s meanings substantially preserve this semantic family. The historical source is cartomancy with the Queen of Spades, not the iconography of the medieval tarot queen.

### S. L. MacGregor Mathers, *The Tarot*, 1888

Mathers gives:

- **Upright:** widowhood, loss, deprivation, absence, separation.
- **Reversed:** a bad, ill-tempered, prejudiced woman; wealth with discord; abundance accompanied by anxiety; pleasure with sadness.

He adds that the card may indicate that a nearby person “has just lost, or is likely soon to lose, wife or husband.” [Mathers text](https://hermetics.net/media-library/tarot/samuel-liddell-macgregor-mathers-the-tarot/).

This is still French cartomancy, before the fully developed *Book T* elemental personality.

### Golden Dawn, *Book T*

*Book T* does not use upright/reversed so much as **well- or ill-dignified**:

- Well dignified: intensely perceptive, keenly observant, subtle, quick, confident, perseveringly accurate, graceful, fond of dancing and balancing.
- Ill dignified: cruel, sly, deceitful, unreliable, despite a good exterior.

The characterization is the primary source of Crowley’s later account.

### A. E. Waite, *Pictorial Key to the Tarot*, 1910

Waite’s printed list:

- **Upright:** “Widowhood, female sadness and embarrassment, absence, sterility, mourning, privation, separation.”
- **Reversed:** malice, bigotry, artifice, prudery, deceit.

He remarks that the queen’s uplifted hand does not make her a figure of mercy; her expression remains severe. [*Pictorial Key* scan](https://www.junkybooks.com/administrator/thebooks/68b0001928cee-learn-to-read-tarot-cards-the-pictorial-key-to-the-tarot-by-arthur-edward-waite-tarot-card-illustrations-by-pamela-coleman-smith-1909.pdf).

The now-common “independent, clear-minded truth-teller” is therefore not Waite’s explicit divinatory meaning, although Smith’s image facilitated it.

### Aleister Crowley, *Book of Thoth*, 1944

Crowley does not organize courts as upright/reversed:

- Well dignified: intensely perceptive; keen observer; subtle interpreter; individualistic; swift and accurate in recording ideas; confident, gracious, and just; graceful and skilled in balance or dancing.
- Ill dignified: cruel, sly, deceitful, unreliable, and dangerous because of outward attractiveness.

His decisive innovation in emphasis is the positive formulation: “clear, conscious perception of Idea” and liberation of the mind.

### Paul Foster Case, *Oracle of the Tarot*

- General: woman with authority, agreeable externally but ambitious and subtle; keen perception; confidence; accurate in superficial detail; graceful and fond of dancing.
- Well dignified: friendly.
- Ill dignified: deceitful, sly, cruel.

Case’s wording plainly descends from *Book T*. His Capricorn–Aquarius timing, however, departs from it. [*Oracle of the Tarot*, p. 38 in the linked scan](https://media-01.imu.nl/storage/divona.be/17547/oracle-of-tarot-paul-foster-case.pdf).

### Rachel Pollack

**[Modern interpretive development]** In *Seventy-Eight Degrees of Wisdom*, Pollack reads the card as sorrow transformed into wisdom and independence: someone who has faced pain and now sees clearly. The severity is no longer simply a prediction of widowhood; it becomes consciousness gained through loss.

Her reversed treatment emphasizes blocked communication, bitterness, isolation, misuse of intelligence, or emotional pain disguised as objectivity. This is a summary rather than a long quotation because the work remains copyrighted.

### Mary K. Greer

Greer emphasizes process and role over fixed gender. The Queen may indicate a person of any gender, an internal capacity, an adviser, critic, boundary-setter, or future guide. Reversal can be read through multiple methods—blockage, projection, excess, deficit, shadow, or inward expression—rather than as a single canonical “bad woman.”

Greer’s approach helps spread the modern reading of the Queen as autonomy and honest discernment. [Greer on court-card flexibility, as cited here](https://marykgreer.com/).

## Esoteric and symbolic readings

### Christian

**[Historical context]** A queen bearing a sword could evoke royal justice, martial virtue, or Christian rulership, but no fifteenth-century text identifies this court as a saint, biblical queen, widow, or Christian virtue.

**[Modern invention]** Equating her specifically with Judith, Salome, Mary as *Mater Dolorosa*, or the Virgin wielding divine truth is deck-dependent and should not be projected onto the Visconti card.

### Hermetic and elemental

The Golden Dawn made Swords = Air and Queen = Water. “Water of Air” is interpreted as:

- currents and transmissibility of air;
- reflection or reception of ideas;
- imagination carrying intellectual forms;
- language or thought made mobile;
- emotional intelligence operating within analysis.

This doctrine first becomes clearly traceable in Golden Dawn *Book T*, then spreads through Crowley, Regardie, Wang, the Ciceros, and modern correspondence manuals.

### Kabbalistic

The queen is first Heh, the archetypal Mother, associated with Binah in the court hierarchy. Swords are Vav/Air and the world of Yetzirah in many Golden Dawn-derived tables. Thus she is the receptive/formative power within the intellectual or formative world.

There is no historical evidence that Bonifacio Bembo designed the Visconti Queen according to Tetragrammaton.

### Astrological

The Golden Dawn span crosses Virgo into Libra:

- Virgo contributes discrimination, exact observation, sorting, and critique.
- Libra contributes balance, justice, social judgment, and mediation.
- The Saturn-in-Libra Three of Swords adds sorrow and severity.

These meanings are internal interpretations of the Golden Dawn system. They do not prove that the medieval Queen portrayed Libra or Virgo.

### Alchemical

The sword may be read as *separatio*: the alchemical operation of cutting, distinguishing, and purifying. The Queen’s Water of Air then becomes a volatile medium made perceptible or thought clarified through reflection.

**First secure spread:** nineteenth- and twentieth-century Hermetic synthesis; later particularly influential in alchemical tarot decks and Robert M. Place’s work.

**Disputed:** claims that the Sola Busca Queen encodes a fully specified alchemical stage. The pack has plausible alchemical/Hermetic material, but interpretations of individual cards are not supported by a surviving key.

### Severed head

In *Book T* and Thoth, the severed bearded head signifies ruthless discrimination: an idea cut free from the personality or obsolete authority that held it. It also draws on the “dangerous woman with a head” complex—Judith and Holofernes, Salome and John, Perseus/Medusa—without *Book T* explicitly identifying a single narrative.

Treating it as a literal prophecy of murder is neither required by the source nor historically continuous with Marseille.

### Jungian

**[Modern psychological reading]** Post-Jungian tarot interprets the Queen as an archetypal thinking function made inwardly receptive:

- conscious discrimination;
- capacity to name painful truths;
- individuation after bereavement;
- shadow forms of cynicism, projection, intellectual cruelty, or defensive isolation;
- a mature inner feminine, inner authority, or gender-independent personality function.

There is no single “Jungian meaning” authored by Jung. Jung did not publish a systematic Queen-of-Swords interpretation. Later tarot writers combined analytical psychology with RWS and Golden Dawn symbolism.

### Feminist and queer readings

The traditional widow is re-read as a woman outside patriarchal ownership: self-governing, sexually and intellectually autonomous, able to set terms and boundaries. The sword becomes speech, consent, and the power to refuse.

This is a significant modern interpretive turn, not a recovery of the card’s medieval meaning. It responds critically to Etteilla and Waite’s negative construction of an unattached older woman.

## People and works

### Artists and makers

- **Bonifacio Bembo and workshop:** probable makers of major Milanese tarot groups, including the Morgan Queen.
- **Unknown Sola Busca engraver / Nicola di Maestro Antonio attribution debates:** responsible for the individualized Olympias type.
- **Jean Noblet, Jean Dodal, Nicolas Conver:** printers in the Marseille lineage.
- **Etteilla/Jean-Baptiste Alliette:** made the widow meaning central to occult cartomancy.
- **S. L. MacGregor Mathers:** transmitted fortune-telling meanings and helped formulate *Book T*.
- **Pamela Colman Smith:** created the most influential modern image.
- **A. E. Waite:** commissioned the deck and supplied its published description and meanings.
- **Jessie Burns Parke and Paul Foster Case:** BOTA image/system.
- **Lady Frieda Harris and Aleister Crowley:** Thoth image and commentary.
- **Robert Wang; Chic and Sandra Tabatha Cicero:** modern visual reconstructions of Golden Dawn instructions.
- **Philippe Camoin and Alejandro Jodorowsky:** Marseille reconstruction.
- **Rachel Pollack and Mary K. Greer:** leading spreaders of psychological, experiential, and less gender-literal readings.

### Calvino

In Italo Calvino’s *Il castello dei destini incrociati*—first version 1969, expanded 1973—the tarot images become a combinatorial language for mute storytellers. In Roland’s story the Queen of Swords signifies **Angelica**, the woman whose pursuit leads Orlando/Roland into jealousy and madness. [Synopsis identifying the card](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/history/castle-crossed-destinies-italo-calvino).

This is a literary use of the card, not a proposed universal interpretation.

### T. S. Eliot

*The Waste Land* (1922) invokes Madame Sosostris and “a wicked pack of cards,” but never names the Queen of Swords. Attempts to identify the “Belladonna, the Lady of the Rocks” as this queen are critical speculation. Competing identifications include Queens of Cups, Wands, or Pentacles and non-court cards.

### Yeats

W. B. Yeats belonged to the Golden Dawn and used its symbolic universe, but no canonical Yeats text was traced in which the Queen of Swords is an individually decisive named figure. General statements that “Yeats wrote about the Queen of Swords” need a work and passage before acceptance.

### Bergman

Ingmar Bergman’s *The Seventh Seal* uses chess, Death, and medieval allegory, not a documented Queen-of-Swords quotation. Visual resemblance between sword-bearing women and tarot imagery is insufficient evidence of borrowing.

### Jodorowsky

Jodorowsky uses Marseille tarot throughout his psychomagical practice and writing. His films are saturated with tarot-like figures, but a specific shot securely documented by him as the Queen of Swords was not traced. Claims that any armed female character in his films “is” this queen are interpretive.

### Modern cultural afterlife

- The television series *Queen of Swords* (2000–01) explicitly derives its title from a tarot card; the heroine’s destiny is associated with the draw.
- Contemporary artists including Lacey Bryant and Sharon Stallings-Wilfong have produced paintings titled *Queen of Swords*.
- Numerous character-based decks cast modern heroines, goddesses, fictional figures, and historical women in the role. These are creative mappings.

No historical building was found that securely quotes the Queen of Swords as a card before modern tarot-themed design. Claims that cathedral sculptures “are the original Marseille courts” remain unproven.

## Controversies and disputes

### Egyptian origin

**[Legend]** Court de Gébelin asserted in 1781 that tarot preserved an Egyptian book of wisdom. Etteilla transformed that proposal into an operational “Book of Thoth” deck.

**Evidence against:** tarot is documented in northern Italy in the 1440s; its courts, suit structure, paper technology, heraldry, clothing, and game mechanics belong to late-medieval card culture. No pharaonic object or Egyptian text contains a tarot sequence.

The Mamluk connection is not confirmation of Gébelin. Mamluk cards are medieval Islamic playing cards, not a pharaonic initiatory book.

### Romani or “Gypsy” origin

**[Legend]** Nineteenth-century writers increasingly claimed that Romani people brought tarot from Egypt or India. Papus’s title *Tarot of the Bohemians* helped popularize the association.

**Evidence against:** Italian tarot records predate evidence of Romani tarot transmission. Romani origins are in South Asia, not Egypt, and the word “Gypsy” reflects an old European misidentification. The Romani did become important practitioners of European cartomancy, but practice is not proof of invention.

### Cathars and Templars

**[Modern legend/disputed]** Some twentieth- and twenty-first-century writers argue that Marseille imagery secretly preserves Cathar doctrine, Templar mysteries, or heretical cathedral teachings.

No medieval Cathar or Templar document mentions tarot, and the military orders were suppressed before tarot’s documented appearance. Proposed clues usually concern trumps, not the Queen of Swords. No evidence links her weapon, crown, or posture specifically to Cathar or Templar teaching.

### Mantegna ancestry

**[Disputed, generally rejected as direct genealogy]** Similar Renaissance subjects once encouraged theories that the Mantegna engravings generated tarot imagery. The engravings are probably later than tarot’s first documentation and are not playing cards. They illustrate a shared intellectual environment rather than a demonstrated parent pack.

### Named Visconti portrait

**[Disputed]** Identifying the Queen as Bianca Maria Visconti, Maria of Savoy, or another duchess can be attractive in a dynastic commission. Yet the card lacks inscription, distinctive portrait physiognomy, or an archival key. “Idealized court lady” remains the evidentially safer description.

### Widowhood as an ancient meaning

**[False retrospective tradition]** Nothing in the fifteenth-century queen establishes widowhood. The traceable line runs through early modern ordinary-card cartomancy, Etteilla, Mathers, and Waite. The solitary RWS queen may visually reinforce it, but did not originate it.

### “Sword equals Air from the beginning”

**[Esoteric attribution, not documented history]** Medieval suit symbolism did not carry a universally recorded four-element grid. Swords = Air became standardized through nineteenth-century occult synthesis. Other occultists have assigned Swords to Fire, showing the correspondence is conventional, not inevitable.

### The Queen as Libra or Virgo

**[Esoteric attribution]** The Golden Dawn span is 20° Virgo–20° Libra. Calling her simply “Libra” or simply “Virgo” abbreviates a thirty-degree span and loses one-third or two-thirds of the stated attribution. Case supplies a completely different Capricorn–Aquarius span.

### “Number 65”

**[Bibliographic misunderstanding]** Waite’s online table of contents calls the Queen chapter 65. The card itself is unnumbered. Grand Etteilla numbers every card, but editions vary and do not establish 65 as a universal identity.

### King/Queen/Knight title confusion

Golden Dawn, Waite, and Crowley do not use court names identically. Golden Dawn’s mounted fiery court is often called Knight; Crowley retains Knight, Queen, Prince, Princess; some modern Golden Dawn decks substitute King for Prince or shift King/Knight language.

The Queen is relatively stable, but careless tables can produce errors such as calling her Heh-final or transferring the Prince’s Vav to her rank.

### Misprints and copied errors

- “Heh final of Vav” for the Queen is a modern copying error from Princess material.
- *Book T* transcriptions give either 20° Virgo or “above 20°”; Crowley’s “21st degree” is inclusive notation, not a one-degree doctrinal change.
- References to Waite saying she is “fond of dancing” are mistaken; that trait is in *Book T*, Crowley, and Case, not Waite’s *Pictorial Key*.
- Claims that the RWS Queen directly copies the Sola Busca Queen are weaker than the demonstrable Sola Busca parallels among several pip cards.
- Confusing *Regina* and Sicilian *Donna* collapses two separate court ranks.

### Historiographical correction

Michael Dummett’s decisive contribution was to treat tarot first as a family of games whose geography, rules, pack structures, and documents can be reconstructed. Ronald Decker and Thierry Depaulis traced how eighteenth- and nineteenth-century occultists successively created the Egyptian, Hebrew, and Romani narratives. Helen Farley examined the cultural transition from aristocratic game to esoteric and psychological device.

Their correction does not make later symbolism culturally meaningless. It changes its date and category: Golden Dawn Water of Air is a documented nineteenth-century esoteric invention, not a fifteenth-century secret.

## Open questions

1. **Exact date and patron of the Cary–Yale pack:** the 1440s are plausible, but proposed marriage and dynastic occasions remain debated.
2. **Portrait status:** no surviving key establishes whether either Milanese Queen of Swords depicts a specific woman.
3. **Transmission into Marseille:** the broad Italian-to-French lineage is secure; the intermediate model for every court detail is not.
4. **Sola Busca program:** the Queen’s identification with Olympias is supportable, but the total intended philosophical or alchemical narrative remains contested.
5. **Pamela Colman Smith’s immediate model:** Marseille, Golden Dawn instructions, Visconti posture, and Smith’s own invention all contributed; no surviving design diary apportions them.
6. **Etteilla editions:** card numbers and reversed keywords require edition-by-edition collation. No single table should be treated as the immutable 1789 original.
7. **Case’s changed zodiac:** the surviving material demonstrates a different span, but the precise rationale for departing from *Book T* deserves further manuscript study.
8. **Modern positive meaning:** the route from “widowhood” to “independent truth-speaker” is clear in outline but needs a bibliographic study across Eden Gray, Pollack, Greer, feminist tarot, and internet-era deck manuals.
9. **Material census:** a complete census of every surviving fifteenth- and sixteenth-century Queen of Swords would require direct comparison of museum catalogues and unpublished/private fragments.
10. **No traced evidence:** no reliable Queen-specific doctrine was found in Marziano, the *Sermones de ludo*, Gébelin, de Mellet, Lévi, Paul Christian, the original Wirth deck, Eliot, Yeats, or Bergman.
