# Eight of Swords: Research Dossier

## Method and evidentiary labels

This dossier distinguishes:

- **[Documented fact]**: supported by a surviving card, contemporary archival record, or primary printed text.
- **[Scholarly reconstruction]**: an inference made by modern historians from surviving objects and documents.
- **[Tradition]**: a convention transmitted through cardmaking, gaming, or cartomancy, without necessarily implying historical origin.
- **[Esoteric attribution]**: a deliberately assigned occult correspondence.
- **[Disputed claim]**: proposed but contested or inadequately demonstrated.
- **[Legend]**: an origin story contradicted or unsupported by the documentary record.
- **[Modern invention]**: a twentieth- or twenty-first-century interpretation or design not found in the earlier tradition.

The Eight of Swords is a pip card, not a trump. Consequently, several topics in the general brief—trump-order families, the Papess/Juno substitution, the Emperor/Star Tzaddi controversy—do not directly alter this card. They are included only where they clarify the deck or system in which the Eight occurs.

### Essential correction

**[Documented fact]** The Morgan Library describes the Visconti-Sforza pack as a fifteenth-century Italian gaming deck and identifies a normal tarot pack as twenty-one trumps, a Fool, and fifty-six suited cards. Seventy-four Visconti-Sforza cards survive between the Morgan Library, Accademia Carrara, and the Colleoni collection. The Morgan’s own portion has only the Ace and Ten among its Swords pips; the Eight belongs to the Bergamo portion rather than the Morgan group. [Morgan Library collection record](https://www.themorgan.org/manuscript/158525)

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** Dating individual Visconti-Sforza cards is difficult. “About 1450–1460” is safer than an exact year, and attributions alternate between Bonifacio Bembo, the Bembo workshop, Francesco Zavattari, and associated hands.

**[Verified esoteric attribution]** The prompt’s Golden Dawn lead is correct: Book T calls the card *Lord of Shortened Force* and assigns Jupiter in 0°–10° Gemini. Crowley retained the astrology but renamed the Thoth card *Interference*.

**[Rejected card-specific claim]** The Sola Busca deck influenced several Waite–Smith minors, but its Eight of Swords is not a demonstrated source for Pamela Colman Smith’s bound woman. No primary statement by Smith or Waite, nor a compelling card-to-card visual match, was located. The claim appears to be an overextension of genuine parallels involving other pips.

# Iconography deck by deck

## Fifteenth-century Milan

### Visconti di Modrone / Cary-Yale, Milan, approximately 1441–1447

**[Documented fact]** This unusual hand-painted pack was made for the Visconti court and survives in the Beinecke Library. It included extra court cards and theological-virtue trumps, so it did not yet have the fully standardized seventy-eight-card structure.

**Eight of Swords:** A card-specific museum record or sufficiently clear public scan confirming that this pack’s Eight survives was not located during this research. It should therefore not be described as the earliest surviving Eight without qualification.

**What can safely be said:** Its suit cards belong to the luxurious Milanese convention—repeated, ornamentally arranged suit signs on tooled metallic or patterned grounds, not narrative scenes.

### Brambilla deck, Milan, before 1447

**[Documented fact]** The Brambilla cards, now in the Pinacoteca di Brera, were probably made for Filippo Maria Visconti before his death in 1447. They are associated with the Bembo workshop. [Metropolitan Museum overview](https://www.metmuseum.org/es/perspectives/tarot-2)

**Eight of Swords:** No surviving Brambilla Eight could be verified. Claims describing its image should be treated as reconstruction from the deck’s general pip style, not observation.

### Visconti-Sforza, Milan/Cremona, approximately 1450–1480

**[Documented fact]** Seventy-four cards survive. The pip cards repeat the suit object against ornate floral grounds. The Morgan’s Ten of Swords is explicitly catalogued as ten swords surrounded by an *A BON DROYT* scroll and floreate border. [Morgan Ten of Swords](https://ica.themorgan.org/manuscript/page/35/158525)

**Eight of Swords:** Eight long, curved blades are arranged symmetrically into a woven or oval field, surrounded by vegetal ornament. There is no human prisoner, blindfold, marsh, castle, or narrative action.

**Meaning at the time:** No contemporary divinatory gloss survives. The ornament identifies number and suit. Reading the interlacing as “mental confinement” is a modern projection.

### Este/Ferrarese cards, later fifteenth century

A small number of luxury cards associated with the Este court survive in scattered collections.

**[Documented fact]** Ferrara produced both luxury and inexpensive *carte da trionfi* by 1442.

**[Negative finding]** No securely identified Ferrarese Eight of Swords was found that permits a card-specific iconographic history. The surviving cards are too fragmentary to support a continuous Eight-of-Swords lineage.

### “Mantegna Tarocchi,” approximately 1460s

**[Documented fact]** This is a set of fifty educational or emblematic engravings arranged into social and cosmological series.

**[Important correction]** It is not a tarot gaming pack: it has neither the four standard suits nor an Eight of Swords. Its traditional name is misleading. It is relevant to Renaissance emblematic culture, not to the direct genealogy of this pip.

## Sola Busca, probably Ferrara/Veneto, 1491; colored about 1500

**[Documented fact]** The Sola Busca is the earliest substantially complete seventy-eight-card tarot and the earliest complete deck whose numeral cards carry individual figural scenes. The complete colored set is in the Pinacoteca di Brera; four uncolored engravings are in the British Museum. The engraving date is inferred from the Venetian-era inscription on trump XIIII and is conventionally read as 1491. [British Museum](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/P_1845-0825-485), [Brera exhibition publication](https://pinacotecabrera.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/11/Il-Segreto-dei-tarocchi-eng.pdf)

### What the Eight depicts

The Sola Busca Eight belongs to a sequence of allegorical or enigmatic labor, warfare, and object-handling scenes. It is not the Waite–Smith composition of a blindfolded woman standing inside a sword fence.

Modern reproductions show a figure associated with eight sheathed or bundled swords, but descriptions vary because popular sources frequently substitute modern interpretations for close cataloguing. The scene is independent, Renaissance imagery, not an illustration of “self-imposed mental imprisonment.”

### Was it Smith’s source?

**[Documented general relationship]** Photographs of the Sola Busca pack reached the British Museum in 1907, shortly before Smith drew the Waite deck. Strong formal correspondences exist between several Sola Busca and Waite–Smith minors—for example, the pierced-heart Three of Swords and the burden-bearing composition transferred from the Sola Busca Ten of Swords to Smith’s Ten of Wands. Helen Farley considers Sola Busca a likely general source for the illustrated minor-arcana project. [Farley, *A Cultural History of Tarot*](https://e-isomero.com/pdf/book/1611846398.pdf)

**[Disputed]** No equivalent close correspondence has been demonstrated for the Eight of Swords. Saying “the Sola Busca Eight is the source of Smith’s scene” is presently unsupported.

### Alchemical readings

**[Scholarly/esoteric interpretation]** The 2012 Brera exhibition interpreted the deck as an interconnected Hermetic-alchemical program involving illustrious ancient figures, metallurgy, initiation, and regeneration of matter.

**[Caution]** This interpretation concerns the deck’s overall program. A unique, universally accepted alchemical decoding of the Eight has not been established.

## Rosenwald and Budapest sheets, late fifteenth or early sixteenth century

These woodcut sheets preserve early inexpensive tarot production.

**[Documented fact]** They demonstrate that ordinary, repeated-suit-sign pips circulated alongside courtly hand-painted packs.

**Eight of Swords:** Where the numeral survives, its function is primarily quantitative and decorative: eight blades in a balanced arrangement. No narrative figure or contemporary cartomantic meaning accompanies it.

**[Open issue]** Dating, production center, and exact relationship among these sheets remain debated. They cannot support a precise “first meaning” for the Eight.

---

## Minchiate, Florence, sixteenth–nineteenth centuries

Minchiate expanded the trump series to forty while retaining four Italian suits.

**[Documented fact]** The Eight of Swords remained a suited numeral card rather than becoming one of the expanded trumps. Its conventional design used repeated blades, normally in symmetrical interlacing.

**Game role:** In Swords, one of the “long” suits, high numerals rank above low ones. The Eight is below Nine and Ten and above Seven in the normal sequence.

**[Negative finding]** The astrological signs added as Minchiate trumps do not give this pip the Golden Dawn attribution Jupiter in Gemini. That correspondence is a nineteenth-century Golden Dawn construction.

## Tarot de Marseille lineage

### Jean Noblet, Paris, approximately 1650

**[Documented fact]** Noblet’s is the earliest substantially surviving deck in the later family called Tarot de Marseille.

**Eight of Swords:** Curved blades interlace from the sides, forming an oval mandorla-like lattice. Floral ornament occupies the interior or gaps. No figure appears.

The blades are often called “scimitars” because of their curvature, but this is primarily a graphic convention that lets repeated swords interweave. It need not indicate Islamic symbolism or a literal weapon type.

### Jean Dodal, Lyon, approximately 1701–1715

Dodal retains the woven pattern: four curved blades rise from each side, crossing in a regular lattice around a floral center. Details of hilts, bands, leaf forms, and coloring differ from Noblet.

**[Tradition]** Even-numbered Marseille Swords typically consist entirely of paired curved blades. Odd cards often add one central straight sword. This is a graphic counting system, not a surviving medieval divinatory code.

### Nicolas Conver pattern

The familiar model bears “1760,” but the Bibliothèque nationale’s authority record introduces an important qualification.

**[Documented bibliographic complication]** Nicolas Conver was born in 1784 and was active as a Marseille cardmaker from 1809 to 1833. The BnF therefore states that the pack traditionally dated “1760” cannot literally have been made by this Nicolas Conver in that year. It catalogs surviving copies in relation to his nineteenth-century activity. [BnF deck record](https://catalogue.bnf.fr/ark:/12148/cb43706229t), [BnF authority record](https://catalogue.bnf.fr/ark:/12148/cb16721159s)

**Eight of Swords:** Eight curved swords, four on each side, form a tightly interlaced oval. A flower or rosette occupies the center; small florets and handles fill the corners. The number VIII appears at the margins. [BnF-derived description](https://marselia.app/fr/cards/huit-de-epee)

**[Modern interpretation]** Reading the narrowing oval as a visual prison is plausible after Waite–Smith but is not documented in Conver-period sources.

### Grimaud / Paul Marteau, twentieth century

The Grimaud/Marteau edition regularized and recolored the Marseille pattern for mass production. The Eight remains an abstract floral lattice.

**[Tradition]** Marteau and later Marseille esotericists assigned psychological and numerical meanings to features that had originally served ornament and suit counting.

### Camoin–Jodorowsky, 1997

Philippe Camoin and Alejandro Jodorowsky reconstructed a Marseille deck from historical exemplars.

**[Documented modern redesign]** The Eight remains non-scenic: interlaced curved swords enclosing a vegetal center.

**[Disputed restoration claim]** Camoin and Jodorowsky sometimes describe their work as recovery of an original or authentic symbolic structure. Historically, it is a modern composite reconstruction, not a facsimile of one lost archetype.

**Jodorowsky/Costa interpretation:** In *The Way of Tarot*, eight represents perfection or equilibrium at a given level; Swords concern intellectual energy. The central flower can accordingly become concentrated or perfected thought. This is a twenty-first-century Marseille numerology, not an early game meaning.

## Besançon pattern, eighteenth–nineteenth centuries

The Besançon family replaced the Papess and Pope with Juno and Jupiter, largely in Swiss, Alsatian, and southern German contexts where papal imagery was objectionable.

**[Documented fact]** This regional renaming concerns trumps II and V, not the Eight of Swords.

The Eight retains the interlaced-pip structure. It does not acquire Jupiter simply because the Besançon deck contains a Jupiter trump. The Golden Dawn’s “Jupiter in Gemini” is a separate later attribution.

---

## Bolognese tarocchino

The Bolognese pack was shortened to sixty-two cards by removing low pips.

**[Documented fact]** The retained Swords numerals are Five through Ten, so the Eight survives. Curved blades distinguish Swords from straight Batons. [Bolognese Ottocento rules](https://www.pagat.com/tarot/ottocento.html)

Its role remains that of a suited trick-taking card. No independent Bolognese occult meaning is historically attached to it.

---

## Sicilian tarot

**[Documented fact]** The traditional Sicilian pack contains sixty-three cards. In Swords the retained numerals are Five through Ten, including Eight. The modern card is indexed “8 S” or an equivalent suit initial. [Sicilian Tarocchi rules](https://www.pagat.com/tarot/sicilian.html)

The Eight ranks below Nine and above Seven. It has no special scoring value beyond its trick-taking rank unless involved in a local declaration or combination.

## Etteilla’s cartomantic tarot, Paris, approximately 1788–1789

Jean-Baptiste Alliette, called Etteilla, produced the first tarot designed specifically for occult cartomancy.

**[Documented fact]** His *Manière de se récréer avec le jeu de cartes nommées tarots* appeared in four parts from 1783 to 1785. [Digitized bibliographic record](https://openlibrary.org/books/OL1795366M/Maniere_de_se_re%CC%81cre%CC%81er_avec_le_jeu_de_cartes_nomme%CC%81es_tarots)

The Grand Etteilla reorganized and renumbered the entire pack. Consequently:

- “card VIII” in Etteilla is not necessarily the ordinary Eight of Swords;
- the suit card must be identified by suit and pips, not by the deck’s global sequence number;
- later Grand Etteilla editions print upright and reversed keywords directly on the card.

The meanings transmitted for the Eight of Swords are:

- upright: **Critique**—criticism, censure, blame;
- reversed: **Incidents**—incidents, events, accidents.

**[Tradition with imperfect textual transmission]** These words appear in later Grand Etteilla card traditions and derivative lists. A clean page-level verification in Etteilla’s 1783–1785 first edition was not obtained; the wording should therefore be cited to the deck tradition rather than claimed as an exact quotation from a particular first-edition page.

**Astrology:** Etteilla devised astrological and elemental structures, but the later Jupiter-in-Gemini assignment is not Etteilla’s. It belongs to the Golden Dawn decan scheme.

## Oswald Wirth, 1889

Wirth’s original twenty-two-card deck illustrated only the trumps.

**[Documented fact]** There was no Wirth Eight of Swords in the 1889 deck. Descriptions of a “Wirth Eight” generally refer to later publishers extending his major-arcana style to a full seventy-eight-card pack.

## Golden Dawn Book T, approximately 1890s; published openly in 1912

### Original description

Book T describes four white radiant angelic hands emerging from clouds, each holding two swords. The eight points converge near the top. Jupiter and Gemini appear above and below.

The title is:

> “Lord of Shortened Force.”

Its characterization includes excessive force applied to small matters, attention to detail at the expense of principal matters, cleverness mixed with disorder, and—when ill-dignified—pettiness, malice, or domineering conduct.

The primary correspondences are:

- Hod of Vav;
- Hod in Yetzirah;
- Jupiter in 0°–10° Gemini;
- Swords/Air;
- angels Vemibael and Yehohel in commonly printed transliterations.

**[Documented publication history]** Golden Dawn tarot manuscripts circulated privately in the 1890s. A version of Book T appeared publicly through Crowley’s *Equinox* in 1912; Israel Regardie subsequently disseminated it in *The Golden Dawn*. [Golden Dawn manuscript compilation](https://www.tarrdaniel.com/documents/Thelemagick/gd/publication/english/golden_dawn_manuscripts.html)

### Robert Wang reconstruction, 1977

Wang’s Golden Dawn Tarot visualizes the Book T instructions: four hands, eight upright blades, clouds, astrological emblems, and Golden Dawn color scales.

**[Modern reconstruction]** It is not a facsimile of one securely preserved original order deck. It is Wang’s synthesis made with Israel Regardie.

### Chic and Sandra Tabatha Cicero reconstructions

The Ciceros follow the same Book T scheme more literally and systematically, often emphasizing:

- eight upward-pointing swords;
- four issuing hands;
- Jupiter and Gemini signs;
- Hod/Yetzirah color correspondences.

These are late twentieth-century ritual reconstructions, not nineteenth-century printed editions.

## Waite–Smith, London, 1909

### What Pamela Colman Smith drew

A woman stands on wet or muddy ground. She is:

- blindfolded;
- loosely bound around the torso and arms;
- barefoot or lightly shod;
- surrounded on three sides by eight upright swords planted point-down;
- separated from a distant castle or settlement by water and uneven land.

The sword fence is incomplete. Her feet are free. The composition allows the possibility of escape but makes movement dangerous.

Waite describes it:

> “A woman, bound and hoodwinked, with the swords of the card about her. Yet it is rather a card of temporary durance than of irretrievable bondage.”

His meanings are:

> “Bad news, violent chagrin, crisis, censure, power in trammels, conflict, calumny; also sickness.”

Reversed:

> “Disquiet, difficulty, opposition, accident, treachery; what is unforeseen; fatality.”

—A. E. Waite, *The Pictorial Key to the Tarot* (1910), “Eight of Swords.” [Primary text](https://sacred-texts.com/tarot/pkt/pktsw08.htm)

### Sources of Smith’s scene

**[Documented]** The verbal meanings “censure,” “calumny,” “sickness,” “accident,” and “treachery” belong to the Etteilla–Mathers cartomantic stream. Smith’s picture concretizes captivity, constraint, and danger.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** The image may combine:

- the older cartomantic sense of crisis/censure;
- the Golden Dawn title “Shortened Force”;
- theatrical melodrama, book illustration, and Smith’s stage-design vocabulary;
- the Marseille visual impression of an enclosed central field.

**[Disputed]** Direct derivation from the Sola Busca Eight is unproved.

**[Modern invention]** The now-common claim that the woman tied herself up is not stated by Waite and is not literally shown. Someone else may have bound her. “Self-imposed prison” is a later psychological extrapolation from the loose bonds, incomplete sword fence, and free feet.

## Paul Foster Case and BOTA

Case’s BOTA deck, drawn by Jessie Burns Parke, closely follows Waite’s major-arcana structure, while Case’s writings systematize the minor cards through astrology and Qabalah.

In *Oracle of the Tarot*, Case describes the Eight’s emblem through a caduceus-like ankh and gives:

> “Force wasted in petty affairs; too much talk; too much ill-directed action; the state of being terribly busy without accomplishing much; over-careful about details.”

He adds crisis, talk, correspondence, and moving about. Ill-dignified, it signifies gossip, malice, pettiness, and disagreements with siblings.

Keyword:

> “Mediocrity.”

**[Important conflict]** Case assigns the first decan of Gemini to **Mercury**, not Jupiter, in the accessible *Oracle of the Tarot* text. This diverges from Book T. [Case text extract](https://www.scribd.com/doc/187261487/Paul-Foster-Case-Oracle-of-the-Tarot-A-Course-on-Tarot-Divination-pdf)

Some later BOTA-derived tables restore or conflate the Golden Dawn Jupiter correspondence; editions must therefore be checked individually.

## Thoth, painted 1938–1943; book 1944; deck commercially issued 1969

### Crowley and Frieda Harris’s design

Title: **INTERFERENCE**.

Two long swords point downward in the center. Six shorter weapons cross them, three from each side. Crowley identifies them as:

- kris;
- kukri;
- scramasax;
- dagger;
- machete;
- yataghan.

The heterogeneity creates disruption: many small, incompatible instruments interrupt the two principal forces.

Crowley writes:

> “The number Eight, Hod, here signifies lack of persistence in matters of the intellect and of contest.”

He continues:

> “Good fortune, however, attends even these weakened efforts, thanks to the influence of Jupiter in Gemini, ruling the Decan. Yet the Will is constantly thwarted by accidental interference.”

—Aleister Crowley, *The Book of Thoth* (1944), pp. 207–208 in common editions. [Primary scan](https://www.rahoorkhuit.net/library/crowley/equinox/volume3/vol_3_no_05.pdf)

### What changed

- Smith’s captive person disappears.
- The obstruction becomes abstract, mechanical, and geometric.
- “Bondage” gives way to distraction, interruption, divided forces, and failure of sustained intellectual action.
- Harris translates the astrology into tension of line, scale, and color instead of narrative.

### Reversals

Crowley generally did not require mechanically reversed meanings. Dignity, surrounding cards, and the full spectrum of the symbol govern interpretation. Assigning “liberation” automatically to a reversed Thoth Eight is a later Rider–Waite-style practice.

## Notable modern redesigns

### Thoth-influenced decks

Modern Hermetic decks often preserve:

- Jupiter and Gemini glyphs;
- eight crossing blades;
- “Interference” or “Shortened Force” as the title;
- Hod/Yetzirah colors.

### Rider–Waite-derived decks

Most popular modern designs preserve the bound or blindfolded figure but increasingly emphasize agency:

- loosened rope;
- a visible gap in the swords;
- a fallen blindfold;
- the figure cutting the bonds;
- swords transformed into thoughts, rules, or social barriers.

This “you can leave whenever you choose” reading is a late twentieth-century therapeutic development, not Waite’s full meaning.

### Feminist and trauma-informed redesigns

**[Modern invention]** Some decks resist victim-blaming and portray the imprisonment as externally imposed—patriarchy, violence, coercive control, disability, racism, or institutional exclusion. Others retain self-limitation but distinguish perceived restriction from genuine material danger.

### *The Wild Unknown Tarot*, Kim Krans, 2012

A butterfly or winged creature is bound in webbing beneath or upon a sword. The human prisoner becomes a metamorphic being whose potential is immobilized.

# The game

## Original function

**[Documented fact]** Tarot originated as a trick-taking game in northern Italy. The four suits—Cups, Coins, Batons, and Swords—were augmented by a permanent trump suit and the Fool. The early cards were not manufactured as an occult book. [Morgan Library](https://www.themorgan.org/collection/tarot-cards), [Metropolitan Museum](https://www.metmuseum.org/es/perspectives/tarot-2)

## Rank of the Eight of Swords

In the historic Italian “long” suits, Swords and Batons normally rank downward:

> King, Queen, Knight, Jack, 10, 9, **8**, 7, 6, 5, 4, 3, 2, Ace,

with regional variations and deck shortening.

Thus the Eight:

- follows suit when Swords are led;
- wins against lower Swords if no trump intervenes;
- loses to Nine, Ten, and the Swords courts;
- normally has no independent card-point value.

In many tarot games, only kings, queens, knights, jacks, trumps, and sometimes the Fool have counting values. Numeral cards are “empty” cards used in tricks, although captured cards may contribute through packet-counting.

## Regional examples

- **Bolognese tarocchino:** Eight is retained because only the lower numeral cards were removed. It ranks between Nine and Seven.
- **Sicilian tarocchi:** Eight survives in the truncated suit and ranks between Nine and Seven.
- **Nice/Marseille-style tarot:** Swords are a long suit; the Eight ranks below Nine and above Seven. [Nice rules](https://www.pagat.com/tarot/nice.html)
- **French-suited Tarot Nouveau:** The equivalent black-suit Eight is an ordinary numeral card, without the Waite image.
- **Central European Tarock:** Most 54-card packs retain only high black-suit cards, commonly 10 through 5. The Eight is therefore present in many games and occupies its normal descending position.

## Fool

The Fool—*Matto*, *Excuse*, *Sküs*—is not the Eight and usually is not a ranked trump. Its role varies:

- it may be played to a trick without following suit;
- it ordinarily does not win the trick;
- it is reclaimed by its owner in many French rules;
- in some Italian variants it functions as a wild card or special combination card.

## Trump-order disputes

The traditional three broad Italian orders—often labeled A, B, and C by historians—concern the sequence of trumps. Justice, Fortitude, the theological authorities, celestial cards, and the highest World/Judgment positions vary regionally.

These disputes do **not** alter the Eight of Swords, which remains a suited card. The Waite switch of Strength to VIII and Justice to XI is likewise irrelevant to this pip’s numeral eight.

# Chronology of attestation

## Approximately 1414–1425: Marziano da Tortona

**[Documented text; scholarly category dispute]** Marziano described a game designed for Filippo Maria Visconti, painted by Michelino da Besozzo, with sixteen classical gods acting as superior cards over four bird suits.

It contains no Eight of Swords. Historians treat it as a proto-tarot or immediate precursor because it combines ordinary suits with permanent triumph cards. It should not be cited as an attestation of the standardized seventy-eight-card pack.

## 1440: Giusto Giusti of Anghiari

A diary entry records a gift of “*naibi a trionfi*” to Sigismondo Malatesta.

**[Documented fact]** This is among the earliest secure references to triumph cards. It does not name individual pips.

## 1442: Ferrara court ledgers

On 28 July 1442, Marchionne Burdochi was paid for:

> “uno paro de carte da trionfi”

for Ercole and Sigismondo d’Este. Other entries pay the painter Jacopo Sagramoro for decorated packs. [Le Tarot cultural association transcription](https://letarot.it/Le-origini-italiane-del-Ludus-Triumphorum_pag_pg439_ita.aspx)

Again, no Eight is named separately; its presence is reconstructed from the ordinary suited component of a tarot pack.

## Approximately 1450–1460: Visconti-Sforza Eight

**[Earliest surviving card-level evidence]** The ornamental Eight of Swords survives within the Milanese pack now divided among New York and Bergamo.

## Approximately 1450–1470: *Sermo perutilis de ludo cum aliis*

The anonymous Dominican sermon condemns dice, ordinary cards, and triumphs. It supplies an early trump list.

**[Documented fact]** Its concern is gambling and moral disorder, not divination. It does not give meanings for the Eight of Swords.

## Late fifteenth century: Boiardo and Ferrarese poetic tarot

Matteo Maria Boiardo designed a poetic card scheme in which suits and values received moral verses.

**[Documented fact]** This shows that literary and moral appropriation of card structure began early.

**[Caution]** Boiardo’s custom suits are not the ordinary Eight of Swords and cannot be treated as its divinatory meaning.

## 1491: Sola Busca

First complete engraved tarot with individually illustrated pips.

## Approximately 1500: tarot poems

Poems were written in which named ladies, courtiers, or moral attributes were paired with cards.

**[Documented cultural practice]** This is evidence for literary play and personal allegory, not proof of professional divination or a stable Eight-of-Swords meaning.

## Early sixteenth century: Folengo

Teofilo Folengo’s literary tarot material demonstrates the use of cards as poetic prompts.

**[Caution]** It does not establish Golden Dawn-style correspondences or the modern prison reading.

## Sixteenth century: *tarocchi* replaces *trionfi*

**[Documented linguistic development]** *Trionfi* was the fifteenth-century term; *tarocchi* becomes common during the sixteenth century. The etymology of *tarocco* remains uncertain.

## Seventeenth–eighteenth centuries: Marseille lineage

The Eight stabilizes as an interlaced arrangement of curved swords with central floral ornament.

## 1781: Court de Gébelin and Comte de Mellet

Court de Gébelin interprets tarot as a surviving Egyptian book. De Mellet proposes an alphabetic scheme and divination method.

**[Documented first occult theory; legend as history]** Neither author gives historically grounded evidence for an Egyptian Eight of Swords.

## 1783–1785: Etteilla

First systematic printed tarot cartomancy; later followed by the Grand Etteilla deck.

## 1854–1856: Éliphas Lévi

Lévi connects the twenty-two trumps with the Hebrew alphabet and makes tarot central to ceremonial magic.

His general assertion is that:

> “All this science is contained in the hieroglyphical book of the twenty-two keys of the Tarot.”

—paraphrased from the tarot chapter of *Dogme et Rituel de la Haute Magie*, vol. II, 1856, chapter XXII. [French edition record](https://books.google.com/books?id=CwUOAAAAYAAJ)

**[Negative finding]** Lévi does not supply a distinctive, card-level Eight of Swords doctrine comparable to Book T. Quoting him as the source of Jupiter in Gemini would be incorrect.

## 1870s: Paul Christian

Christian expanded the Egyptian-initiation narrative and popularized “arcana” terminology.

**[Legend/esoteric construction]** His Egyptian temple initiation is literary-occult elaboration, not historical evidence.

## 1888: Mathers

S. L. MacGregor Mathers publishes *The Tarot: Its Occult Significance, Use in Fortune-Telling, and Method of Play*. [Primary text](https://sacred-texts.com/tarot/mathers/mtar00.htm)

His Eight:

- upright: sickness, calumny, criticism, blame;
- reversed: past treachery, event, accident, remarkable incident.

This closely continues Etteilla-derived cartomancy.

## 1889: Papus and Wirth

Papus claims:

> “Le jeu de Tarots, transmis par les Bohémiens de génération en génération, est le livre primitif de l’antique initiation.”

“The Tarot pack, transmitted by the Bohemians from generation to generation, is the primitive book of ancient initiation.”

—Papus, *Le Tarot des Bohémiens*, preface, 1889. [French text](https://www.arbredor.com/ebooks/TarotBohemiens.pdf)

For the Eight of Swords Papus gives, in translation:

> “The enemy only partially triumphs.”

Wirth’s 1889 cards include only the major arcana.

## 1890s–1912: Golden Dawn Book T

The Eight becomes Hod of Vav, Jupiter in Gemini, Lord of Shortened Force.

## 1909–1910: Waite–Smith and *Pictorial Key*

The bound-woman image and Waite’s “temporary durance” become the foundation of modern popular interpretation.

## 1938–1944: Thoth creation and explanation

Crowley and Harris create *Interference*; Crowley publishes *The Book of Thoth* in 1944.

## 1969: commercial Thoth deck

The full color deck becomes widely available and spreads the title *Interference*.

## 1970s onward: psychological tarot

Humanistic, Jungian, feminist, and therapeutic readers increasingly interpret the card as restricted perception, internalized prohibition, learned helplessness, anxiety, or recovery of agency.

## The suit and Vav

In Golden Dawn Qabalah:

- Wands = Yod = Fire = Atziluth;
- Cups = first Heh = Water = Briah;
- Swords = Vav = Air = Yetzirah;
- Pentacles = final Heh = Earth = Assiah.

Thus the Eight is **Hod of Vav**, meaning the eighth Sephirah expressed in the Air/Swords world.

**[Correction]** Vav is not the Eight of Swords’ individual Hebrew letter. Minor pips do not occupy the twenty-two Tree-of-Life paths as trumps do.

## Hod

Hod means splendor or glory and is associated in Hermetic Qabalah with Mercury, language, analysis, classification, and formal intellect.

The card combines:

- Hod/Mercury’s analytic multiplicity;
- the divisive Air/Sword element;
- Jupiter’s expansion;
- Gemini’s duality and proliferation.

Golden Dawn and Thoth readers interpret the result as intelligence scattered among too many details, generosity applied unwisely, or expansive thought that obstructs decisive action.

## Tzaddi/Heh and Emperor/Star

Crowley’s “Tzaddi is not the Star” revision exchanges the usual Hebrew-letter relations of the Emperor and Star.

**[Documented but irrelevant here]** It affects two trumps and their paths, not the Eight of Swords’ decan, Hod, Vav, or Jupiter-in-Gemini correspondence.

# Divinatory meanings by author

## Etteilla tradition, late eighteenth century

- Upright: **“Critique”**—criticism, censure, blame.
- Reversed: **“Incidents”**—incidents, events, accident.

**Textual caution:** These concise labels are securely transmitted by Grand Etteilla cards and derivative tables, but an exact first-edition page was not recovered.

## Mathers, 1888

- Upright: **“Sickness, Calumny, Criticism, Blame.”**
- Reversed: **“Treachery in the Past, Event, Accident, Remarkable Incident.”**

Mathers is an important bridge between French cartomancy and English occult tarot.

## Papus, 1889

> “The enemy only partially triumphs.”

This converts the sword suit’s general “enemy/war” category through Papus’s numerical method.

## Golden Dawn Book T

Well dignified:

- excessive care in some matters;
- cleverness and acuity;
- patience in detailed study;
- generosity;
- force dispersed into minor concerns.

Ill dignified:

- malice;
- pettiness;
- selfishness;
- domineering behavior;
- small or unworthy applications of intelligence.

Book T does not use Waite’s neat upright/reversed binary; card dignity depends upon surrounding influences.

## Waite, 1910

Upright:

> “Bad news, violent chagrin, crisis, censure, power in trammels, conflict, calumny; also sickness.”

Reversed:

> “Disquiet, difficulty, opposition, accident, treachery; what is unforeseen; fatality.”

Waite’s “temporary durance” is often remembered while his harsher divinatory list is omitted.

## Crowley, 1944

Crowley emphasizes:

- lack of persistence in intellectual matters;
- will thwarted by accidents;
- good nature where severity is required;
- petty obstruction;
- unexpected bad luck;
- small incidents frustrating large plans.

He writes that it is:

> “simply the error of being good-natured when good-nature is disastrous.”

He also notes that “trivial incidents” can alter empires and frustrate the best plans. [*Book of Thoth* text](https://www.invisiblehouse.org/pdf/Volume%20IV.pdf)

Crowley does not prescribe a separate reversed meaning.

## Paul Foster Case

Keyword:

> “Mediocrity.”

Meanings:

> “Force wasted in petty affairs; too much talk; too much ill-directed action; the state of being terribly busy without accomplishing much.”

Ill-dignified: gossip, malice, pettiness, and personal or sibling disputes.

## Rachel Pollack

In *Seventy-Eight Degrees of Wisdom*, Pollack reads the picture as fear, confusion, isolation, and oppression, while stressing that the figure is not completely enclosed and may be able to escape.

Her influential development is that external restriction and internal belief reinforce each other: the person feels powerless, yet the image contains unrealized possibilities for action.

**[Copyright limitation]** A long verbatim card passage is not reproduced here. The consulted scan is listed below.

## Mary K. Greer

In *The Complete Book of Tarot Reversals* Greer treats reversal dynamically. For this card, turning the picture over can indicate that:

> “bonds, blindfold, and barriers could be falling away.”

She also permits the reversed card to represent blocked restriction, internalized limitation, excess, release, refusal, reconsideration, or unconventional action, depending on context. [Publisher record](https://www.llewellyn.com/product.php?ean=9781567182859)

# Esoteric and symbolic readings

## Alchemical

### Sola Busca

**[Scholarly/esoteric reading]** The Brera catalogue argues that the Sola Busca pack forms an alchemical-Hermetic cycle of matter, knowledge, initiation, and regeneration.

**[Negative finding]** No uncontested fifteenth-century commentary decodes its Eight of Swords alchemically. All card-specific alchemical readings are reconstructions.

### Modern Eight

Crossed blades may be interpreted as forces obstructing separation or refinement. This is a modern Hermetic extrapolation from the Thoth design, not an early alchemical meaning.

## Kabbalistic

The Golden Dawn’s Eight is Hod in Yetzirah:

- Hod supplies analysis, language, system, and multiplicity;
- Yetzirah supplies formation and Air;
- Swords divide, discriminate, and conceptualize;
- their imbalance becomes overanalysis, fragmentation, or interference.

This correspondence first appears as part of the Golden Dawn’s late nineteenth-century synthesis and was spread by Crowley, Regardie, Wang, and the Ciceros.

## Astrological

Jupiter in Gemini combines expansion with plurality:

- Jupiter enlarges and generalizes;
- Gemini divides, compares, communicates, and changes direction;
- Jupiter is traditionally in detriment in Gemini because broad synthesis is dispersed into multiple particulars.

Book T’s “Shortened Force” and Crowley’s “Interference” grow from that tension.

**[Documented esoteric attribution]** The decan is 0°–10° Gemini.

**[Conflict]** Case’s accessible text assigns Mercury rather than Jupiter to this decan.

## Hermetic

The four suits become four elements, worlds, magical weapons, letters of the divine name, and stages of manifestation. The Eight is not merely “eight weapons”; it is intellectual force operating at Hod’s level in the formative world.

This construction is Golden Dawn-era Hermeticism, not Renaissance cardmaking evidence.

## Christian

Historic swords could evoke:

- justice;
- martyrdom;
- military authority;
- the “sword of the spirit”;
- punitive or judicial power.

However, no early Christian commentary survives that singles out the Eight.

Waite’s bound woman has sometimes been read as a suffering soul, penitent, captive, or person under calumny. Those readings harmonize with his Christian mysticism but are not explicitly defined by him as the card’s secret doctrine.

## Jungian and psychological

Beginning especially in the mid-twentieth century, tarot writers reframed divination as psychological reflection.

The Eight became:

- the ego trapped by a rigid complex;
- learned helplessness;
- internalized prohibitions;
- fear preventing perception of alternatives;
- paralysis through analysis;
- dissociation or refusal to see;
- the shadow projected as an external jailer.

**[Modern invention]** “The prison is entirely in your mind” is not historically traditional and can become reductive. Smith’s image contains genuine external restraints as well as an incomplete enclosure.

**[Jung connection]** Case’s 1947 *Tarot: A Key to the Wisdom of the Ages* is among the early tarot works to invoke Jungian ideas. Pollack, Greer, Sallie Nichols, and later therapeutic readers spread psychological and archetypal approaches.

- **Pamela Colman Smith:** created the bound-woman composition.
- **Paul Foster Case and Jessie Burns Parke:** BOTA system and deck.
- **Aleister Crowley and Lady Frieda Harris:** Thoth concept and painting.
- **Israel Regardie, Robert Wang, Chic and Sandra Tabatha Cicero:** principal disseminators and reconstructors of Golden Dawn tarot imagery.
- **Alejandro Jodorowsky and Philippe Camoin:** modern Marseille reconstruction.
- **Marianne Costa:** collaborator with Jodorowsky on Marseille interpretation.
- **Rachel Pollack and Mary K. Greer:** major psychological and practice-centered interpreters.

## Calvino

Italo Calvino’s *The Castle of Crossed Destinies* constructs stories from tarot layouts, principally using Visconti-Sforza and Marseille imagery.

**[Documented tarot connection]** Tarot operates as a combinatorial visual language.

**[Negative finding]** No unique, central literary role for the Eight of Swords was verified that would justify treating the novel as a principal card-specific source.

## T. S. Eliot

Madame Sosostris in *The Waste Land* uses invented and altered tarot references.

**[Documented tarot reference]** Eliot names the drowned Phoenician sailor, Belladonna, “the man with three staves,” the Wheel, and a “one-eyed merchant.”

**[Negative finding]** The Eight of Swords is not named. Eliot is relevant to tarot reception, not to this card’s history.

## W. B. Yeats

Yeats belonged to the Golden Dawn and participated in its symbolic culture.

**[Documented association]** He knew the order’s tarot correspondences.

**[Negative finding]** No major Yeats work was located that demonstrably quotes the Eight of Swords specifically.

## Ingmar Bergman

Bergman’s *The Seventh Seal* draws on medieval allegory, Death, chess, and apocalyptic imagery.

**[Negative finding]** It is frequently associated loosely with tarot Death, but no Eight-of-Swords quotation was verified.

## Jodorowsky’s films

Tarot and Marseille symbolism permeate Jodorowsky’s creative and psychomagical practice.

**[Negative finding]** A shot or prop securely documented as a deliberate quotation of the Eight of Swords was not located. Claims based only on a blindfolded or constrained figure are iconographic resemblance, not proof.

## Buildings and paintings

No pre-1909 building or painting was found that can responsibly be called a quotation of Smith’s Eight. Similar motifs—blindfolded Justice, imprisoned saints, martyrs among swords, fenced allegorical figures—are possible visual antecedents but not demonstrated sources.

Modern paintings, tattoos, book covers, comics, and installations frequently quote the bound woman and eight upright swords. These belong to Waite–Smith reception rather than the card’s early history.

# Controversies and disputes

## Egyptian origin

Court de Gébelin’s 1781 essay is the first influential printed claim that tarot preserved an Egyptian book.

**Label:** **[Legend/esoteric attribution]**

Evidence against:

- the earliest records are fifteenth-century northern Italian;
- early suits and court cards belong to European and Mamluk-derived playing-card history;
- early trumps use medieval and Renaissance Christian, civic, moral, and courtly imagery;
- no Egyptian tarot cards, Egyptian tarot instructions, or chain of transmission survives.

The Morgan Library calls Gébelin’s astrological use a result of “scholarly misinformation.” [Morgan record](https://www.themorgan.org/manuscript/158525)

## “Book of Thoth”

The Comte de Mellet and later Etteilla made the Egyptian claim more specific, treating tarot as the Book of Thoth.

**Label:** **[Legend]**

Crowley retained “Tarot of the Egyptians” as an esoteric and poetic title. Its use in *The Book of Thoth* is part of an occult lineage, not an archaeological finding.

## Romani or “Gypsy” origin

Papus explicitly claimed that “the Bohemians” transmitted tarot from generation to generation.

**Label:** **[Legend]**

No documentation places tarot among Romani communities before its Italian appearance. The theory grew from the mistaken European association of Romani people with Egypt and from nineteenth-century romantic occultism.

This history should be reported without repeating “Gypsy” as a neutral ethnic designation; it appears here because it is the terminology of Vaillant, Papus, and their period.

## Cathar origin

Some modern alternative histories connect tarot to Cathar dualism, southern France, the Grail, or concealed heretical teaching.

**Label:** **[Disputed modern claim/legend]**

No Cathar tarot deck, rule book, image series, or transmission document survives. Tarot appears more than a century after the Albigensian Crusade and first appears securely in northern Italian courts.

## Templar origin

Claims that the cards conceal Templar doctrine, Baphomet, or a post-suppression initiatory order are similarly unsupported.

**Label:** **[Legend]**

They depend on symbolic resemblance and later conspiracy narratives rather than provenance.

## Masonic origin

Tarot predates institutional Freemasonry by centuries.

**Label:** **[Legend if asserted as origin; documented if asserted as later adoption]**

Eighteenth- and nineteenth-century Freemasons and occult fraternities did adopt tarot and reinterpret it. That does not establish Masonic authorship.

## “Ancient Kabbalah encoded in the deck”

Lévi, Papus, and the Golden Dawn mapped Hebrew letters and the Tree of Life onto tarot.

**Label:** **[Esoteric attribution, not historical origin]**

There is no fifteenth-century source assigning Hod, Vav, Yetzirah, or Jupiter in Gemini to the Eight of Swords.

## Sola Busca as direct source of the Waite Eight

**Label:** **[Disputed]**

What is established:

- Sola Busca predates Waite–Smith;
- photographs were deposited at the British Museum in 1907;
- some Waite–Smith pips strongly resemble Sola Busca compositions.

What is not established:

- that Smith saw a particular Eight and copied it;
- that the Sola Busca Eight depicts the same imprisonment;
- that Smith or Waite named it as this card’s source.

## “Self-imposed bondage”

**Label:** **[Modern psychological interpretation]**

Waite says “temporary durance,” not that the woman created the situation herself. The free feet, loose-looking binding, and incomplete sword fence permit a self-liberation reading, but they do not prove self-causation.

## Conver “1760”

**Label:** **[Bibliographic dispute/misdating tradition]**

The surviving Conver pattern bears or is conventionally associated with 1760, yet the identified Nicolas Conver lived from 1784 to 1833. The BnF explicitly warns that the dated pack cannot straightforwardly be attributed to him in 1760. This is an example of a printed or copied date becoming a deck-history convention.

## Golden Dawn “reversed meanings”

**Label:** **[Frequent modern simplification]**

Book T more often speaks of well- and ill-dignified conditions than a fixed upright/reversed pair. Later manuals frequently turn “ill dignified” into “reversed,” obscuring the original method.

## Case’s decan ruler

**Label:** **[Documented conflict]**

Golden Dawn and Crowley: Jupiter in Gemini.

Accessible Case text: Mercury in the first decan of Gemini.

This may represent Case’s alternative decan rulership, an edition-specific scheme, or later textual transmission. It should not be silently harmonized.

## “Eight is a Tree-of-Life path”

**Label:** **[Error]**

Eight corresponds to the Sephirah Hod. It is not one of the twenty-two connecting paths. The Hebrew-letter paths belong principally to the trumps.

# Open questions

1. **Exact location and catalogue number of the Visconti-Sforza Eight.**  
   It is normally assigned to the Bergamo holdings, but a publicly accessible, card-level institutional entry comparable to the Morgan’s Ace and Ten was not found.

2. **Precise dating of the Visconti-Sforza cards.**  
   Museum datings range across approximately 1450–1480, and individual cards may come from more than one campaign or hand.

3. **Sola Busca Eight iconography.**  
   A definitive card-specific description should be checked against the Brera high-resolution image and the Gnaccolini catalogue rather than modern divinatory sites.

4. **Smith’s immediate source.**  
   No surviving design notes or letter identifies the bound woman’s model. Stage imagery, Golden Dawn teaching, cartomantic keywords, prison allegory, and Marseille enclosure remain plausible influences.

5. **Etteilla first-edition wording.**  
   “Critique/Incidents” is secure in the Grand Etteilla tradition, but its earliest exact printing and page require comparison of the four 1783–1785 cahiers and early deck impressions.

6. **Case’s Mercury attribution.**  
   The conflict with the Golden Dawn’s Jupiter should be examined across first editions of *Oracle of the Tarot*, BOTA lessons, and later reprints.

7. **Modern cultural quotations.**  
   Many artworks reproduce Smith’s image, but documentation of deliberate citation is often absent. Visual similarity alone is inadequate.

8. **Contemporary meanings.**  
   “Self-imposed restriction,” “victim mentality,” and “remove the blindfold” dominate current practice but lack a single identifiable first author. Pollack and the late twentieth-century psychological-tarot movement were major spreaders, not necessarily sole inventors.
