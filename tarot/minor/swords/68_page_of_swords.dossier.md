# Page of Swords: Research Dossier

## Scope, method, and evidence labels

The Page of Swords is a suit court, not a trump. Consequently, several items in the general brief—trump numbering, regional trump orders, Justice/Strength placement, the Fool’s privilege, Besançon’s Juno/Jupiter substitutions, and Crowley’s *Tzaddi/Heh* exchange—do not govern this card. They are discussed only where they clarify what does **not** apply.

The following labels are used throughout:

- **[Documented fact]** Directly supported by a surviving card, archival document, contemporary rules, or primary printed text.
- **[Scholarly reconstruction]** A conclusion argued from objects and documents but not stated by a contemporary source.
- **[Tradition]** A conventional reading transmitted within cartomancy.
- **[Esoteric attribution]** A deliberately constructed occult correspondence.
- **[Disputed]** Seriously contested identification or chronology.
- **[Legend]** A historical claim unsupported by contemporary evidence.
- **[Modern invention]** A recent interpretive development rather than an old inherited meaning.

Where a requested detail could not be securely traced, that absence is reported.

| Primary historical function | A playing card in the ordinary suit of Swords, not originally an occult emblem |

The Morgan describes a normal early tarot suit as ten numeral cards plus page, knight, queen, and king, and dates its Visconti-Sforza material to Milan, approximately 1450–1480. It also emphasizes that these cards were originally used for a game, not fortune-telling. [Morgan catalogue](https://www.themorgan.org/manuscript/158525)

### Number

**[Documented fact]** Early Italian and Marseille Pages bear no Roman or Arabic number. “Page = 11” is a modern deck-ordering or database convention: Ace through Ten followed by Page.

**[Modern invention]** Numerological readings built specifically upon “11” should not be projected back onto the fifteenth-century card. The Page is a rank, not a numbered pip.

### Fante, Valet, Page, Jack, Sota, Princess

- Italian *fante* meant a foot-soldier or attendant and survives in *fanteria*, infantry.
- French *valet* denotes a male servant or attendant.
- English *knave* originally meant boy or servant; *jack* became the normal playing-card term, while occult decks favored the more romantic *page*.
- Spanish *sota* is the lowest court rank in the Spanish pack.
- German *Bube* likewise denotes the Jack/Knave.
- **Princess** is not an historical translation. It is the Golden Dawn’s esoteric reorganization of the courts.

A useful multilingual comparison of these conventional terms is available in the German-language overview of tarot court names. [German terminology table](https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tarot)

### The Vav question

**[Esoteric attribution; correction]** In the Golden Dawn court sequence:

- Knight/King = Yod
- Queen = first Heh
- Prince = Vav
- Princess = final Heh

Thus **Vav belongs to the Prince, not the Princess/Page**. The Page of Swords is final Heh of Yetzirah: the material reception or fixation of Air. The Golden Dawn text calls the Princesses “the forces of the He final” and “the Throne of the Forces of the Spirit.” [Golden Dawn text printed in *The Equinox*](https://hermetic.com/crowley/equinox/i/viii/eqi08016)

# Iconography deck by deck

## Mamluk antecedents, approximately fifteenth century

**[Documented fact]** The surviving Mamluk cards employ Cups, Coins, Swords, and Polo-sticks. Each suit contained numeral cards and three named courts: *malik*, *nāʾib malik*, and *thānī nāʾib*. The courts are expressed by ornament and inscriptions rather than human portraits.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** The European Swords suit is descended from, or belongs to the same transmission as, the Mamluk Swords suit. The structural correspondences are too close to be accidental, although no surviving object documents each stage of transfer.

**[Disputed]** Calling the Mamluk second deputy “the Page of Swords” is too exact. It is a functional ancestor of a low court, not the same image or rank. European packs changed three abstract male offices into King, mounted Knight, and standing Knave; Italian tarot then added the Queen, yielding four courts.

L. A. Mayer published the Mamluk cards in 1939. Dummett and Kamal Abu-Deeb later corrected Mayer’s mistaken reconstruction of five suits as two packs mixed together. [Mayer’s original article](https://www.persee.fr/doc/bifao_0255-0962_1939_num_38_1_1998); [Dummett and Abu-Deeb](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/10.2307/751160); [International Playing-Card Society introduction](https://www.i-p-c-s.org/wp/an-introduction/)

### Naʾib and *naipes*

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** Arabic *nāʾib*, deputy, is commonly connected with early Italian *naibi* and modern Spanish *naipe*. It is suggestive evidence for transmission but not proof that the individual tarot *fante* directly preserves the office of *thānī nāʾib*.

## Cary–Yale / Visconti di Modrone, Milan, approximately 1440–1460

The deck historically called Cary–Yale and Visconti di Modrone is now at Yale’s Beinecke Library.

**[Documented fact]** It preserves an unusual expanded court: male and female Pages and male and female Knights in addition to King and Queen—apparently six courts per suit. The cards are untitled and unnumbered.

For Swords, the surviving female Page or Maid carries the suit emblem and wears aristocratic fifteenth-century dress against a tooled gold ground. The larger court structure is exceptional and was not inherited by standard tarot. [Cary–Yale description](https://www.learntarot.org/cydesc.htm); [deck documentation](https://www.usgamesinc.com/files/attachments/489/CY86_Booklet.pdf)

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** The doubled courts may reflect an aristocratic commission concerned with dynastic gender balance, marriage, or court ceremony. The exact original pack size—often reconstructed as 86 cards—is not stated by a contemporary inventory and remains inferential.

**[Disputed]** It is frequently dated to the marriage of Francesco Sforza and Bianca Maria Visconti in 1441, but both dating and commissioning occasion remain debated.

This is among the strongest candidates for the earliest surviving physical ancestor of a Page of Swords.

---

## Brera-Brambilla, Milan, mid-fifteenth century

The Brambilla material preserves only a fraction of its original pack.

**[Documented fact]** It belongs to the early Milanese luxury-card group: elongated cards, gold backgrounds, contemporary court dress, and naturalistically rendered weapon emblems.

**Absence finding:** No complete, securely catalogued Page of Swords from this fragment could be confirmed in the accessible museum material. A modern reconstruction should not be treated as an original Brambilla card.

---

## Visconti-Sforza / Pierpont Morgan–Bergamo, Milan, about 1450–1480

The surviving group is divided among the Morgan Library, Accademia Carrara, and Colleoni holdings.

**[Documented fact]** Its courts are richly dressed aristocratic figures on punched gold grounds. The Page type is a standing youthful attendant bearing the emblem of the suit. The Morgan’s own 35-card portion contains the King and Queen of Swords but not the Page of Swords. [Morgan object list](https://www.themorgan.org/collection/tarot-cards)

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** Much of the deck has traditionally been attributed to Bonifacio Bembo or his workshop. The Morgan cautiously says “executed by or for” a Visconti-Sforza patron and dates the group broadly. Italian art history has repeatedly revised the division of hands. [Morgan catalogue](https://www.themorgan.org/manuscript/158525); [Treccani on Bembo](https://www.treccani.it/enciclopedia/bonifacio-bembo/)

### Is the Page a particular Sforza?

**[Disputed]** Moakley and later Kaplan popularized reading various court figures as members of the Visconti-Sforza circle. The heraldry securely connects the pack with those families, but resemblance is not enough to identify a specific youthful courtier.

No primary inscription names the Page of Swords. I found no defensible card-specific identification equivalent to “this is Galeazzo Maria Sforza.” The prudent conclusion is:

> The family context is historical; a named portrait identity for the Page of Swords is unproved.

Gertrude Moakley’s important contribution was to reconnect the cards with the Visconti-Sforza context and with *trionfi* as a game. Her portrait identifications are hypotheses, not contemporary labels. [Moakley bibliographical discussion](https://forum.tarothistory.com/viewtopic.php?t=1168)

---

## Fifteenth-century Ferrarese and Este cards

**[Documented fact]** Ferrara was one of tarot’s earliest production centers. Its court cards show courtly figures and naturalistic Italian suits.

**Absence finding:** Many so-called Este fragments lack secure original deck boundaries. I could not verify a surviving, securely attributed Ferrarese Page of Swords with enough provenance to describe it card by card. Reconstructions that fill missing courts are modern.

---

## Sola Busca, probably Ferrara or Venice, 1491

The Sola Busca is the earliest surviving substantially complete tarot with fully illustrated numeral suits.

**[Documented fact]** Its courts and pips are renamed or individualized pseudo-historical figures rather than generic pages, and the suit scenes participate in an enigmatic military, classical, and alchemical-seeming narrative.

**Importance for this card:** Its contribution to the later Waite-Smith Page is limited. Pamela Colman Smith demonstrably drew on Sola Busca compositions for several illustrated pips, notably the Three and Ten of Swords, but no equally close one-to-one borrowing has been established for her Page of Swords.

**[Disputed]** Alchemical readings of the entire Sola Busca were largely developed after the fact. Classical and military allusions are visible; a coherent original alchemical program has not been proved.

---

## “Mantegna Tarocchi,” northern Italy, about 1460s

**[Documented fact]** These are fifty didactic engravings arranged in hierarchical series.

**[Correction]** They are not a tarot pack and contain no Page of Swords. The later name “Mantegna Tarocchi” is misleading; neither Mantegna’s authorship nor use as ordinary tarot is established.

---

## Rosenwald and Budapest sheets, late fifteenth or early sixteenth century

These printed sheets are important witnesses to the transition from hand-painted court packs to reproducible popular tarot.

**[Documented fact]** They preserve abbreviated, woodcut-like courts and suit cards. Standing low courts bear their suit emblems without narrative backgrounds.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** They help establish that the generic standing *fante* was the normal popular type, in contrast to later occult characterization.

**Absence finding:** The accessible reproductions and catalog summaries did not permit a safe, sheet-by-sheet attribution of a distinct surviving Page of Swords. Claims that a particular damaged figure is certainly that card should be treated cautiously.

---

## Minchiate, Florence, sixteenth–nineteenth centuries

**[Documented fact]** Minchiate expanded the pack to 97 cards but retained four ordinary suits. It sometimes used female lowest courts—*fantine*—in selected suits or patterns.

In play, the Sword court remains below Horse, Queen, and King. Its imagery is a standing attendant or maid carrying a sword, not an occult “Earth of Air” figure. [Minchiate rules and ranks](https://www.pagat.com/tarot/minchiate.html)

**[Tradition]** Modern esoteric Minchiate decks may import elemental meanings, but those are not part of historical Florentine play.

---

## Bolognese tarocchino

**[Documented fact]** Bolognese tarocchino reduced the suited sequence by removing low numeral cards while retaining courts. The lowest Sword court survives as a playing rank. Bolognese imagery is compact, often double-ended in later packs, and not equivalent to the Waite-Smith scene.

**[Historical nuance]** Bologna supplies some of the earliest documentary evidence for systematic cartomancy with tarot, but that does not prove the cards were invented for divination.

---

## Sicilian tarot

**[Documented fact]** Sicilian tarocchi possesses distinctive trumps and court terminology. A female lowest court, commonly rendered Maid rather than Page in English rules, may appear in the suits.

In surviving rules the Maid is the lowest court. Counting varies by rule set; in one standard method a Maid alone counts one point, or two when paired with a low card after the usual deduction. [Sicilian rules](https://www.pagat.com/tarot/sicilian.html)

## Tarot de Marseille lineage

### General Marseille type

The card is titled **VALET D’ÉPÉE(S)**. It is not numbered.

A youthful standing figure turns his head sideways while holding a large sword diagonally upward or behind the shoulder. Dress consists of hat, cape or mantle, short doublet, puffed sleeves, hose, and shoes. One hand grips the elaborate guard; the other may touch or steady the weapon. There is usually no narrative event.

The familiar interlaced, curved “scimitars” chiefly characterize the Sword pips. The Page carries one fully rendered weapon; it should not be described as standing inside the pip-card lattice.

### Jean Noblet, Paris, about 1650–1659

**[Documented fact]** Noblet is the earliest substantially surviving Type-I Marseille-pattern pack. Its Valet is compact and vigorous, with simplified line, restricted stencil color, and a large diagonal sword.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** The sideways head and poised weapon indicate alertness in a generic courtly or martial sense. They do not document “spying,” “gossip,” or an Air correspondence.

### Jean Dodal, Lyon, approximately 1701–1715

Dodal retains the standing youthful Valet, turned head, decorated clothing, and oversized blade. The figure remains emblematic rather than scenic.

**[Tradition]** Later Marseille readers interpret the gaze and divided bodily direction psychologically. There is no evidence that Dodal encoded “looking into the past.”

### Nicolas Conver, Marseille, 1760/1761 pattern

The familiar Conver type shows a stationary youth, torso largely frontal, head turned, with an oblique sword rising behind or beside the shoulder. A second pale linear object near the lower body is sometimes misread as another sword; in many impressions it is better understood as part of the weapon, scabbard, strap, or costume detail.

A BnF-derived description records curly hair, broad hat, green cape, red doublet, blue sleeves and hose, and an ornate yellow guard. [Conver-based description and scan reference](https://marselia.app/fr/cards/valet-de-epee)

**[Disputed]** Claims that the Valet intentionally holds “two blades” depend on ambiguous printing and restoration.

### Grimaud/Paul Marteau, 1930 onward

Paul Marteau’s *Ancien Tarot de Marseille* standardized a cleaned Conver-like pattern and popularized “Tarot de Marseille” as a commercial-historical category. Colors were regularized; the Valet became clearer and more symmetrical.

**[Modern invention]** Treating Marteau’s exact colors as a fixed medieval symbolic code is historically unsafe. They are part restoration, part production decision, part occult interpretation.

### Camoin–Jodorowsky, 1997

The Camoin–Jodorowsky deck reconstructs and alters Marseille imagery using multiple historical exemplars and an expanded palette.

The Valet remains a standing figure with sword and lateral attention. Jodorowsky and Marianne Costa interpret Pages as beginners confronting or hesitating before the energy of their suit.

**[Disputed]** The deck is often marketed as a recovery of a primordial authentic tarot. Historians generally regard it as a modern synthetic restoration, not a facsimile of a single lost original.

## Besançon pattern

Besançon replaced the Popess and Pope with Juno and Jupiter to avoid controversial papal imagery. That regional change affected trumps II and V, **not the Page of Swords**.

The Page remained *Valet d’Épée*. This is a useful control case: regional religious censorship transformed sensitive trumps while leaving the ordinary Sword court substantially untouched.

---

## Grand Etteilla, Paris, 1789 onward

Etteilla reordered, renumbered, and Egyptianized tarot for divination.

**[Documented fact]** The Sword court was associated with the divinatory word **ESPION**—spy. This is the most important early source for the Page’s later surveillance meaning. [Early *Grand Etteilla* text](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/2/2f/Le_grand_Etteilla%2C_ou%2C_l%27art_de_tirer_les_cartes_..._Le_tout_recueilli_..._%28IA_b29321220%29.pdf)

The figure varies between editions, but ordinarily remains a courtly male with a sword rather than Waite’s windswept youth.

**[Tradition]** “Spy” is therefore older than Waite and should not be explained solely from Smith’s sideways-looking figure.

**[Absence finding]** A stable universal Etteilla card number could not be assigned because editions and “Grand Etteilla” patterns differ in numbering and captions. Modern tables that give one number without naming the edition conceal this variation.

---

## Oswald Wirth, 1889

**[Documented fact]** Wirth’s 1889 tarot was a 22-trump occult pack. It did not supply a newly designed Page of Swords.

Therefore, a “Wirth Page of Swords” in a later 78-card publication is an editorial completion or modern extension, not one of Wirth’s original 1889 images.

## Golden Dawn manuscripts and reconstructions

### Book T, approximately 1888–1900 manuscript tradition

The Golden Dawn transformed the Knave into:

> “Princess of the Rushing Winds: the Lotus of the Palace of Air.”

She is “Earth of Air,” final Heh of the suit, and the “throne” into which the powers of the Ace and the other courts become materially effective. The order’s text describes a standing Amazon-like woman with sword, altar, cloud, and elemental insignia. [Golden Dawn description](https://hermetic.com/crowley/equinox/i/viii/eqi08016)

### The throne of the Aces

**[Esoteric attribution]** The Golden Dawn calls the Princesses the “Thrones of the Aces.” The Ace is the undifferentiated root of a suit; the Princess is its last, densest manifestation. The Princess of Swords is thus the Ace of Swords’ Air brought down into Earth or form.

This is an initiatory metaphysics, not a feature of early card play.

### The four quadrants

The Princesses have no ordinary thirty-degree zodiacal rulership. They govern four quadrants surrounding the celestial pole. Crowley later summarized these geographically:

- Wands: Asia
- Cups: the Pacific
- Swords: the Americas
- Disks: Europe and Africa

[Crowley’s quadrant summary](https://hermetic.com/crowley/book-of-thoth/small-cards)

Later practitioners sometimes describe the Princess of Swords sector as centered on Aquarius and extending through portions associated with Capricorn–Aquarius–Pisces. This is a spatial projection, not a standard “three decans” birth period.

### Wang reconstruction, 1977

Robert Wang’s *Golden Dawn Tarot*, painted under Israel Regardie’s guidance, reconstructs the order’s descriptions: an armed Princess in airy colors and clouds, with Golden Dawn implements and scale colors.

**[Modern reconstruction]** It is not a surviving nineteenth-century Golden Dawn deck.

### Cicero reconstruction, 1991/2000s

Chic and Sandra Tabatha Cicero produced a more literal and polished realization of Golden Dawn documents. Their Princess retains the Amazon, sword, clouds, altar, and heraldic elemental language.

Again this is a documented reconstruction of occult instructions, but the finished picture is modern.

## Waite-Smith, 1909; *Pictorial Key*, 1910

Pamela Colman Smith drew a young and somewhat androgynous figure on a windswept rise. The body advances one way while the head looks back. Both hands hold a raised sword. Hair, tunic, trees, clouds, and birds make the air visibly active.

Waite’s exact description begins:

> “A lithe, active figure holds a sword upright in both hands…”

He adds that the figure is passing over rugged ground while clouds are “collocated wildly.” His meanings are:

> “Authority, overseeing, secret service, vigilance, spying, examination…”

Reversed, he gives:

> “More evil side of these qualities; what is unforeseen, unprepared state…”

See A. E. Waite, *The Pictorial Key to the Tarot* (London: William Rider, 1910), Part III, Page of Swords. [Online text and chapter index](https://sacred-texts.com/tarot/pkt/index.htm?chaptersPage=3)

### What Smith changed

Smith converted the static heraldic Valet into a weather-filled narrative image. The additions—hill, fast clouds, bending trees, birds, two-handed guard, and counter-turned gaze—visualize vigilance and the Golden Dawn’s Air correspondence.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** Smith probably combined:

1. the Marseille standing Sword valet;
2. Waite’s supplied divinatory meaning;
3. Golden Dawn Earth-of-Air doctrine;
4. theatrical gesture and costume;
5. her own economical narrative design.

**[Disputed]** There is no surviving Waite instruction sheet proving which details he required. Statements that every cloud, bird, and color was dictated by Waite exceed the evidence.

**[Disputed]** Sola Busca clearly influenced several Waite-Smith pips, but no direct Sola Busca prototype for this Page has been demonstrated.

---

## Paul Foster Case / BOTA, approximately 1920s onward

Case and Jessie Burns Parke adapted Waite-Smith designs. BOTA cards were intended to be colored by students.

**[Documented fact]** The BOTA minor arcana are generally non-scenic suit-emblem cards in their standard published form; Page imagery encountered in derivative BOTA-style packs is not always part of Case’s core printed deck. [BOTA deck history](https://waitesmith.org/index.php/books/b-o-t-a-tarot-decks-and-instruction/)

**[Esoteric attribution]** Case inherited the Golden Dawn elemental framework while modifying some colors and doctrinal details. He did not establish a new ancient pedigree for the Page.

**Absence finding:** A reliably accessible primary Case passage giving a distinct upright-and-reversed Page of Swords formula was not located. Later lists attributed to Case should be checked against a named BOTA lesson or edition before quotation.

---

## Thoth, painted 1938–1943; first full publication 1969

Frieda Harris depicts a forceful armored female figure descending or thrusting through turbulent air. She wears a Medusa-like serpent crest and drives her sword downward before a barren or violated altar. Angular clouds and geometries give material edge to otherwise volatile Air.

Crowley writes:

> “The Princess of Swords represents the earthy part of Air, the fixation of the volatile.”

He associates her with Minerva, Artemis, and the Valkyrie, calls her stern and revengeful, and says her logic is destructive. Well dignified, she possesses practical wisdom, cleverness, and skill in controversy; ill dignified, these qualities disperse into incoherence and “low cunning.” [Crowley, *Book of Thoth*, court cards](https://hermetic.com/crowley/book-of-thoth/court-cards)

### What changed

- Male Page → female Princess.
- Passive or watchful stance → aggressive downward action.
- Court costume → mythic armor.
- Generic weapon → instrument that fixes, cuts, and materializes thought.
- Landscape → stormy elemental field and ritual altar.

Harris did not merely illustrate Waite. She translated Golden Dawn doctrine through Crowley’s Thelemic, geometrical, and painterly program.

### Tzaddi/Heh

Crowley’s celebrated “Tzaddi is not the Star” reassignment concerns the Emperor and Star trumps. It does **not** change the Princess of Swords’ final-Heh status. Confusing the two distinct uses of *Heh*—a Hebrew letter assigned to a trump, and final Heh in the four-letter Name assigned to a court rank—is a category error.

## Notable modern developments

### Rachel Pollack, 1980s onward

Pollack treats the Page as mental energy in an inexperienced or exploratory state: curiosity, messages, intellectual excitement, watchfulness, and the danger of using cleverness without maturity.

**[Modern synthesis]** The “student of truth” motif grows from Page-as-youth plus Swords-as-mind. It is not an early Marseille caption.

### Mary K. Greer, 1980s onward

Greer develops court cards as roles, personality modes, developmental energies, and aspects of the querent. The Page can be researcher, observer, critic, communicator, or an inexperienced wielder of language.

**[Modern invention/tradition]** Gossip, online surveillance, social media monitoring, and checking someone’s profile are technological translations of Etteilla’s spy and Waite’s secret-service/vigilance complex.

### Jodorowsky and Costa

Their Marseille method reads direction, posture, color, and relation to adjoining cards. The Valet is a beginner confronting the Sword’s intellectual or verbal force. This reading is openly therapeutic and symbolic rather than a recovered historical rule.

# The game

## Original role

**[Documented fact]** Tarot began as a trick-taking game. The Page of Swords was an ordinary suit court. It had no trumping power and no special divinatory function.

When Swords was led, it followed normal suit rules. Its place was:

> King > Queen > Knight/Cavalier > Page/Fante > numerals

The precise ranking of the numeral cards could vary by suit and regional game.

## Modern French tarot

French gaming tarot uses French suits, so the direct modern analogue is the **Valet de Pique**, not an Italian-suited Valet d’Épée.

It ranks below Cavalier and above Ten. It is worth 1.5 card-points; a low card counts 0.5. Courts are commonly counted in pairs with a low card. [French tarot rules](https://www.pagat.com/tarot/frtarot.html)

## Italian games

In many Italian tarot games the Page/Fante is the lowest court. Traditional card-point systems often count it as two points before the per-trick or per-packet deduction, producing an effective one-point contribution depending on the rules.

In Swords and Batons—the “long” suits—the numeral order commonly runs Ten high down to Ace low. Cups and Coins often reverse this, Ace high down to Ten low.

## Minchiate

King > Queen > Horse > Fante/Fantina. Swords are a long suit: Ten high, descending to Ace low. [Minchiate rules](https://www.pagat.com/tarot/minchiate.html)

## Tarock

Most Central European Tarock packs use French suits, shortened numeral sequences, and King–Queen–Cavalier–Jack courts. The Page’s analogue is the Jack of Spades. Exact card-points vary:

- In many three-card counting systems, Jack has a nominal value of two, yielding one effective point after deduction.
- In French tarot counting it is 1.5.
- In some Austrian variants numeral cards have zero nominal value while courts retain their conventional values.

There is no single universal “tarock value.”

## Sicilian tarot

The Maid is the lowest court. Regional rules give it a small scoring value and no special privilege. [Sicilian rules](https://www.pagat.com/tarot/sicilian.html)

## Trump-order disputes: non-applicability

The Page is not part of any of the three principal historical trump orders—usually termed A, B, and C in Dummett’s analysis. Consequently:

- Justice/Strength order does not alter it.
- The Fool’s status as Excuse, *Matto*, or highest trump in some later games does not alter it.
- Besançon’s Juno/Jupiter substitutions do not alter it.
- Sicilian or Bolognese trump renumbering does not alter its suit rank.

# Chronology of attestation

## Before tarot

- **1370s: [Documented but textually problematic]** Playing cards are recorded in Europe. A Viterbo chronicle associates *naib* with Saracens, but the surviving chronicle is later than the alleged 1379 event and must not be treated as a contemporary eyewitness manuscript.
- **Fifteenth century: [Documented object]** Mamluk packs preserve Swords and three deputy/king courts.
- **[Scholarly reconstruction]** These supplied part of the structural ancestry of Latin-suited European cards.

## Early tarot

- **c. 1420: [Documented manuscript]** Marziano da Tortona describes a special pack made for Filippo Maria Visconti with sixteen classical gods and four bird suits. It is a precursor to the idea of a permanent trump suit, but it contains no Page of Swords.
- **1442, Ferrara: [Documented archival entry]** Este account books record payments for *carte da trionfi*. The entry names the pack type, not the Page of Swords.
- **c. 1440–1460: [Documented objects]** Cary–Yale preserves male/female low courts, including Sword attendants.
- **c. 1450–1480: [Documented objects]** Visconti-Sforza cards establish the normal fourteen-card tarot suit.
- **c. 1470–1500: [Documented text]** The *Sermo perutilis de ludo cum aliis* gives a list of trumps and condemns card play. It does not give occult meanings for the Page.
- **Late fifteenth century:** Rosenwald/Budapest sheets and the Sola Busca document printed and fully illustrated tarot traditions.
- **c. 1480s–1490s: [Literary evidence]** Matteo Maria Boiardo composed a 78-card poetic sequence. It was published posthumously in 1523 and describes a designed moral-literary pack rather than the standard Page of Swords.
- **Early sixteenth century:** Teofilo Folengo employs tarot cards in literary combinations. Such poems show symbolic wordplay attached to cards, not a settled Golden Dawn-style occult system.

## French and occult developments

- **c. 1650–1659:** Noblet’s Paris pack preserves the Type-I Marseille Valet.
- **1701–1715:** Dodal’s Lyon pack.
- **1709:** Madenié’s dated Type-II Marseille pattern.
- **1760/1761:** Conver’s Marseille pack becomes the most influential later exemplar.
- **1781:** Court de Gébelin declares tarot an Egyptian Book of Thoth; de Mellet proposes Hebrew-letter correspondences. Neither supplies a distinctive Page-of-Swords doctrine.
- **1780s–1790s:** Etteilla systematizes tarot divination and fixes *espion* as a Sword-court meaning.
- **1854–1856:** Éliphas Lévi integrates tarot with Hermetic Qabalah, but concentrates on the 22 trumps.
- **1863:** Paul Christian elaborates fictional Egyptian initiation scenes.
- **1888:** Mathers publishes an English divinatory manual.
- **1888–1900:** Golden Dawn manuscripts create the Princess/Earth-of-Air system.
- **1889:** Papus publishes *Le Tarot des Bohémiens*; Wirth publishes 22 occult trumps.
- **1909:** Waite-Smith deck issued.
- **1910:** Waite publishes *The Pictorial Key*.
- **1912:** Golden Dawn card descriptions printed in Crowley’s *Equinox*.
- **1930:** Marteau/Grimaud popularizes *Ancien Tarot de Marseille*.
- **1938–1943:** Harris paints Thoth.
- **1944:** Crowley publishes *The Book of Thoth*.
- **1969:** First full commercial publication of the Thoth deck.
- **1977:** Wang’s Golden Dawn reconstruction.
- **1980s onward:** Pollack and Greer establish psychological and developmental readings.
- **1997:** Camoin–Jodorowsky tarot.

## Conflicts and clarifications

### Air versus Fire

**[Esoteric attribution]** Golden Dawn assigns Swords to Air. This became dominant in Waite-Smith and Thoth traditions.

**[Alternative esoteric tradition]** Some modern readers assign Swords to Fire because metal is forged in fire or because swords enact will and violence. There is no evidence that fifteenth-century Italian players assigned either element.

### Spade versus Swords

French-suited Spades are the usual conventional analogue of Swords. The resemblance is historical and structural; a French gaming tarot uses *piques*, whereas Marseille retains Italian suits and *épées*.

### Decans

The Page/Princess does **not** have the three-decan zodiacal span used by Knights, Queens, and Princes in Golden Dawn doctrine. The Princess rules a quadrant of space.

Claims assigning her exactly “0° Capricorn to 30° Pisces” conflate a quadrant-centered symbolic scheme with timed decan rulership.

### Hebrew letter

There is no separate single Hebrew letter traditionally assigned to the Page of Swords as an individual card. Final Heh is its rank formula within the Tetragrammaton.

### Colors

Golden Dawn scale colors vary by world, element, and grade. Wang and Cicero therefore differ visually while claiming the same manuscript basis. Exact printed hues are reconstruction choices, not independently surviving ancient conventions.

# Divinatory meanings by author

## Etteilla, late eighteenth century

**Upright:** *Espion*—spy; an observer, investigator, hostile or covert agent.

**Reversed:** Meanings vary among editions and derivative lists; unexpected disclosure, unforeseen action, or a compromised spy recur, but no one English formula can safely be called Etteilla’s universal wording.

**Historical importance:** This is the principal root of the later spying/secret-service tradition.

## S. L. MacGregor Mathers, 1888

Mathers gives the Knave of Swords a cluster centered on:

- vigilance;
- observation;
- spying;
- a person charged with watching or examining;
- an unexpected or unprepared condition when reversed.

His wording closely anticipates Waite and belongs to the pre-*Book T* English cartomantic stream. See *The Tarot: Its Occult Signification, Use in Fortune-Telling, and Method of Play* (1888). [Scanned edition](https://www.howardnema.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/12/the-tarot-1888-by-macgregor-mathers-1.pdf)

## Papus, 1889

Papus assigns the Valet of Swords the action or personification of opposition coming from outside the household:

> “l’opposition qui vient d’une source extérieure à la maison”

—“opposition coming from a source outside the house.”

See Papus, *Le Tarot des Bohémiens*, pp. 316–321 in the cited scan. [French text](https://www.arbredor.com/ebooks/TarotBohemiens.pdf)

## Waite, 1910

**Upright:** authority, overseeing, secret service, vigilance, spying, examination.

**Reversed:** the more harmful side of those qualities; the unforeseen; an unprepared state; sickness is added in some printings.

Waite does not say curiosity, student, gossip, new ideas, or social media. Those are later developments.

## Golden Dawn / *Book T*

Well dignified:

- wisdom;
- strength;
- acuteness;
- subtlety in material things;
- grace and dexterity;
- skill in settling controversies.

Ill dignified:

- frivolity;
- cunning;
- incoherence;
- waste or dissipation of mental force.

This is technically dignity-based rather than a simple upright/reversed binary.

## Crowley, 1944

Well dignified, the Princess is stern, aggressive, clever, dexterous, practically wise, and highly capable in controversy.

Ill dignified, her gifts disperse into incoherence and low cunning.

Crowley’s interpretive advice is to remain cool, resolute, and energetic, avoiding premature action while proceeding with confidence. [*Book of Thoth*](https://hermetic.com/crowley/book-of-thoth/court-cards)

## Paul Foster Case

**Qualified finding:** Case accepts the Air/Swords and materializing-mental-force structure, but I could not verify from an accessible primary edition a unique Page-of-Swords upright/reversed list suitable for exact quotation. Secondary lists differ and should not be silently attributed to him.

## Rachel Pollack

Pollack develops the card as young, alert mental energy: curiosity, eagerness for knowledge, quick perception, and the risk that intellectual excitement outruns judgment.

Reversed or shadowed, alertness becomes suspicion, defensive argument, malicious speech, or thought cut off from experience.

This is a psychological development of Waite and Golden Dawn rather than an early historical meaning.

## Mary K. Greer

Greer treats the Page as:

- a literal young person;
- a message or information;
- the inner observer or critic;
- a research, learning, or communication style;
- a role the querent may consciously enter.

Reversed, observation becomes gossip, hypervigilance, verbal defensiveness, misinformation, or fear of speaking.

Because Pollack and Greer remain copyrighted, these are concise paraphrases rather than extensive quotations.

# Esoteric and symbolic readings

## Elemental and Hermetic

### Earth of Air

The Page/Princess is the densest or most material phase of the airy suit:

- an idea written down;
- speech converted into consequence;
- intelligence embodied in a learner or agent;
- investigation producing evidence;
- volatile thought fixed into a practical decision.

**First securely traceable formulation:** Golden Dawn *Book T*, late nineteenth century.

### The throne of the Ace

The Princess is not another beginning identical to the Ace. She is the receptive material field in which the Ace’s root force becomes operative. The formula loops:

> Ace/root potential → courts and numbered differentiations → Princess/material embodiment → new root.

This explains why some modern occultists call the Princess both the last result and seed of the next cycle.

## Kabbalistic

Swords correspond to **Yetzirah**, the formative world, and the Princess to **Malkuth**, the tenth sephirah. Thus she is Malkuth of Yetzirah: formative thought descending into concrete manifestation.

**[Esoteric attribution]** This is internally coherent within Golden Dawn Qabalah.

**[Historical correction]** It is not a medieval Jewish interpretation of tarot and is not found on Milanese or Marseille cards.

## Astrological

The card has no planet or zodiac sign in the direct manner of a trump, nor a thirty-degree span like the three senior Golden Dawn courts.

Its quadrant is commonly centered on the airy fixed region associated with Aquarius. Crowley’s rough terrestrial projection makes Swords correspond to the Americas.

**[Disputed within occult practice]** Modern charts that assign the Princess a specific Capricorn–Aquarius–Pisces span are extrapolations.

## Alchemical

The key Crowleyan phrase is “fixation of the volatile.” In alchemical vocabulary, volatile Air is captured, condensed, or made effective through Earth.

Harris’s descending sword and barren altar make that operation simultaneously creative and destructive: an idea takes form by cutting away alternatives.

**[Modern/esoteric reading]** Applying *solve et coagula* to the card is plausible within Crowley’s system but not specifically documented in early Marseille material.

## Christian

Historically, the Sword could signify justice, warfare, martyrdom, or the “sword of the Spirit” in Christian visual culture. A young armed attendant was also an ordinary courtly type.

**Absence finding:** No early maker’s text identifies the Page of Swords as a specific saint, biblical figure, virtue, or Christian mystery. Claims that he is David, an angelic watcher, or a youthful Christ are unsupported unless tied to a particular modern deck.

## Jungian

In modern depth-psychological interpretation the Page may be:

- the emerging discriminating function;
- the youthful intellect;
- the vigilant or suspicious complex;
- the inner critic;
- the shadow of compulsive observation;
- the beginning of conscious differentiation.

**[Modern invention]** Jung did not devise a Page-of-Swords interpretation. “Jungian tarot” is the work of later writers applying Jungian categories to tarot.

## Gender

Historical Pages are usually male, though Cary–Yale and some regional patterns preserve female maids. The Golden Dawn deliberately makes the rank Princess/final Heh.

**[Modern development]** Contemporary decks frequently render the Page nonbinary or gender-fluid. This has a genuine historical opening in the variation of low courts, but the modern identity language is new.

## Literature, film, and art

### Italo Calvino

*The Castle of Crossed Destinies* uses tarot cards as a combinatorial visual language. Different editions and sections employ Visconti-Sforza and Marseille imagery.

**Qualified finding:** The Page/Valet of Swords participates in the available narrative alphabet, but I found no secure basis for claiming it is the privileged central card of a named episode. Claims about a single fixed meaning overlook Calvino’s deliberate recombination.

### T. S. Eliot

Madame Sosostris’s reading in *The Waste Land* names the drowned Phoenician Sailor, Belladonna, the man with three staves, the Wheel, and a “one-eyed merchant,” among others.

**Absence finding:** The Page of Swords is not explicitly named. Identifying the “one-eyed merchant” as this Page is speculative and deck-dependent.

### W. B. Yeats

Yeats belonged to the Golden Dawn and worked within its symbolic culture.

**Absence finding:** No canonical poem or prose passage was located in which he singles out the Princess/Page of Swords. General Golden Dawn affiliation is not card-specific evidence.

### Ingmar Bergman

Bergman’s *The Seventh Seal* is saturated with medieval allegory, chess, death, and procession imagery.

**Absence finding:** It does not constitute a documented quotation of the Page of Swords. Comparisons are thematic, not demonstrable source relations.

### Jodorowsky

Jodorowsky’s films employ tarot-like bodies, initiations, alchemy, violence, and archetypal tableaux, especially *The Holy Mountain*.

**Qualified finding:** His explicit tarot scholarship belongs primarily to his deck reconstruction and writings with Marianne Costa. A shot should not be declared a Page-of-Swords quotation without production evidence or a precise formal match.

### Paintings and buildings

No historically secure building program was found that quotes the Page of Swords as a named tarot card.

The visual type—a youthful sword-bearing attendant—is common in Renaissance court art, manuscript illumination, saintly cycles, and heraldic figures. Resemblance alone cannot establish tarot quotation.

# Controversies and disputes

## Egyptian origin

Court de Gébelin announced in 1781 that tarot was an Egyptian book of wisdom, preserved through the disguise of play. He offered speculative Egyptian etymologies before hieroglyphic Egyptian had been deciphered.

**Label:** **[Legend/esoteric attribution]**

**Evidence:** His published assertion exists; supporting ancient Egyptian tarot objects or texts do not.

**Spreaders:** de Mellet, Etteilla, Lévi, Paul Christian, Papus, later occult orders.

**Historical correction:** Documentary and material evidence places tarot’s invention in fifteenth-century northern Italy. Dummett stresses that no occult use is evidenced before the late eighteenth century. [Dummett, “Origins of Tarot”](https://www.nybooks.com/articles/1981/05/14/origins-of-tarot/); [Morgan on occult tarot](https://www.themorgan.org/exhibitions/online/tarot/11); [French scholarly study of Gébelin](https://publis-shs.univ-rouen.fr/ceredi/1946.html)

## Romani or “Gypsy” transmission

Nineteenth-century writers claimed the Romani carried tarot from Egypt.

**Label:** **[Legend]**

The theory combined the old European misnomer “Egyptians” for Romani people with Gébelin’s Egyptomania. Romani origins lie in South Asia, and European tarot records predate evidence for a Romani custodial tarot tradition.

Papus’s title *Tarot des Bohémiens* was a major vehicle for the myth. His opening explicitly calls tarot the primitive book of ancient initiation transmitted by “Bohémiens.” [Papus scan](https://www.arbredor.com/ebooks/TarotBohemiens.pdf)

## Cathar claim

Some twentieth-century authors interpreted Marseille trumps as covert Cathar doctrine.

**Label:** **[Disputed modern hypothesis]**

No fifteenth-century document links tarot makers, patrons, or players with surviving Cathar communities, and the Page of Swords offers still less specific evidence. Generic dualism, church figures, and medieval motifs cannot establish Cathar authorship.

## Templar claim

Tarot has also been connected with suppressed Templar teaching.

**Label:** **[Legend/modern invention]**

The Templars were suppressed more than a century before the first documented *trionfi*. No transmission document, Templar tarot object, or Page-of-Swords symbolism bridges the gap.

## Mamluk ancestry versus Egyptian occultism

These are frequently confused:

- **[Scholarly reconstruction strongly supported]** European playing cards derive substantially from Islamic/Mamluk card traditions.
- **[Legend]** Tarot was an ancient Egyptian hieroglyphic Book of Thoth.

The first concerns late-medieval playing-card structure; the second claims pharaonic occult scripture. Evidence for one does not rescue the other.

## “Scimitar” claims

The curved interlaced Marseille pip emblems plausibly preserve stylized curved blades related to Mamluk swords.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** This is well grounded at suit level.

**Correction:** The Page’s single sword is usually a European court weapon with guard and hilt, not necessarily a literal Islamic scimitar.

## The Page as spy

- **[Documented cartomantic tradition]** Etteilla: *espion*.
- Mathers transmits vigilance/spying.
- Waite explicitly prints secret service and spying.
- Smith visually reinforces those words through the counter-turned head.

Thus “spy” is neither ancient Milanese iconography nor a recent internet invention. It is an eighteenth-century divinatory assignment given a powerful twentieth-century picture.

## The Page as student or messenger

Pages historically were junior attendants and could carry messages.

However:

- **[Scholarly contextual inference]** youthful service plausibly supports “messenger.”
- **[Modern synthesis]** “student of ideas,” “new intellectual project,” and “curiosity” chiefly arise from Page-as-youth plus Air-as-mind.
- Waite does not use “student” or “curiosity” in his printed entry.

## Gender misidentification

Early low courts can be male or female depending on deck. Cary–Yale deliberately includes both. Golden Dawn Princess is explicitly female. Waite-Smith’s figure has often been read as male but is sufficiently stylized that modern readers sometimes treat it as androgynous.

A universal claim that “the card was always a young man” is false.

## Misprints and restoration traditions

### Conver’s date

Conver is routinely called “1760”; some catalogues and later witnesses complicate the exact dating of surviving impressions. “Conver pattern of 1760/1761” is safer than treating every extant Conver-labeled sheet as printed in that year.

### Two swords

Poor registration, restored linework, scabbard details, or adjacent costume forms have encouraged the claim that the Marseille Valet carries two swords. This is not consistently supported across early impressions.

### Grimaud colors

Marteau’s standardized palette is often treated as an ancient invariant. It is not.

### Crowley’s publication date

The paintings were made 1938–1943; *The Book of Thoth* appeared in 1944; the complete commercial deck followed in 1969. Giving only “Thoth, 1944” or “Thoth, 1969” obscures three different events.

### “Rider deck”

Calling it only Rider-Waite suppresses Pamela Colman Smith’s artistic authorship. Rider was the publisher. “Waite-Smith” or “Rider-Waite-Smith” is historically clearer.

# Open questions

1. **Which surviving Milanese object should be called the earliest Page of Swords?**  
   Dating bands for Cary–Yale, Brambilla, and related cards overlap. The unusual Cary–Yale Maid is among the earliest secure physical witnesses, but a single uncontested first card cannot presently be named.

2. **What was the complete Cary–Yale court structure?**  
   The six-court reconstruction is persuasive from surviving male and female Pages and Knights, but the original inventory is lost.

3. **Who painted each Visconti-Sforza card?**  
   Bembo/workshop remains a useful traditional attribution; modern connoisseurship divides the corpus among hands.

4. **Does the Visconti-Sforza Page portray a named family member?**  
   No inscription or contemporary key proves it. Moakley/Kaplan identifications remain hypotheses.

5. **What exact visual sources did Smith use?**  
   Her Golden Dawn membership, Waite’s program, theatrical experience, and exposure to earlier cards are documented; no card-specific design memorandum survives for the Page.

6. **How stable was Etteilla’s numbering?**  
   Not stable enough across editions and derivative Grand Etteilla decks to state one universal number without specifying an impression.

7. **Did Papus originate “external opposition”?**  
   He prints it, but the degree to which it derives from Etteilla, contemporary French cartomancy, or his own table requires edition-by-edition collation.

8. **Did Case publish an independent Page meaning?**  
   Accessible secondary tables disagree. A named BOTA lesson or verified edition is needed before exact attribution.

9. **Are the Golden Dawn quadrants literal geography?**  
   Crowley gives rough geography; earlier order material is principally celestial and elemental. Later maps often make the scheme more exact than its sources warrant.

10. **Which modern “Jungian” meaning appeared first?**  
    Jung never supplied one. The development is distributed across postwar tarot psychology rather than traceable to a single definitive first statement.
