# Seven of Swords: Research Dossier

## Method and evidentiary labels

This dossier distinguishes:

- **Documented fact** — supported by a surviving card, archival record, contemporary text, or first-edition publication.
- **Scholarly reconstruction** — an inference accepted or seriously entertained by historians but not stated directly in surviving contemporary evidence.
- **Tradition** — a historically transmitted interpretation whose origin may be traceable but whose underlying claim is not independently demonstrable.
- **Esoteric attribution** — a deliberately constructed occult correspondence.
- **Disputed claim** — a proposal on which relevant authorities disagree or for which evidence is incomplete.
- **Legend** — a historical-origin story contradicted or unsupported by the documentary record.
- **Modern invention** — a recent reading retrospectively projected onto older cards.

The distinction is crucial here. Before the late eighteenth century, the Seven of Swords was principally a playing card. No surviving Renaissance source assigns it the modern meanings “theft,” “deception,” or “getting away with something.”

### Names and terminology

**Documented fact:** Fifteenth-century Italian records first call tarot packs *carte da trionfi*, “cards of triumphs.” The word *tarocchi* appears later, around the opening of the sixteenth century. *Trionfi* originally referred especially to the added permanent-trump series, not to a mystical journey.

**Documented fact:** The suit-name *spade* is plural Italian for swords; Spanish has *espadas*. French Marseille cards use *épées*. German occult decks commonly use *Schwerter*; English uses *Swords*.

**Clarification:** “Spade” in Italian tarot means a sword, not originally the modern black French-suit ♠. Through later suit-equivalence, Swords correspond to French *piques* and English spades in many games and cartomantic tables, but the signs have different visual histories.

**No card-specific regional renaming:** Papess/Juno, Pope/Jupiter, Maison Dieu/Tower, and numbered or unnumbered Fool variations belong to the trump sequence. They did not rename the Seven of Swords. Besançon, Bolognese, Sicilian, and Minchiate packs alter trumps or pack composition but continue to identify this card by suit and number.

# Iconography deck by deck

## Before tarot: the sword suit

**Scholarly reconstruction:** European Latin-suited cards probably developed partly from Mamluk playing cards, whose suits included scimitars. Italian and Iberian makers retained recognizable sword signs. This is a history of playing-card transmission, not evidence that tarot itself originated in Egypt.

Early Italian sword pips may be straight or curved. The highly interlaced, crescent-shaped Marseille “swords” are ornamental descendants of recognizable blades, not necessarily literal scimitars observed from life.

---

## Marziano da Tortona and Michelino da Besozzo, Milan, circa 1412–25

**Documented fact:** Marziano described a special card game devised for Filippo Maria Visconti and illustrated by Michelino da Besozzo. It had sixteen classical gods divided among four thematic suits—virtues, riches, virginities, and pleasures—not the later standard four suits plus twenty-two trumps.

**Finding:** It contained no identifiable Seven of Swords. It is relevant as an early experiment in permanent trumps, not as a direct surviving ancestor of this pip.

---

## Visconti di Modrone / Cary–Yale, Milan, probably 1440s

**Documented fact:** This incomplete luxury pack survives at Yale’s Beinecke Library. It has an unusual expanded court structure and gilt or silver grounds. All fifty-six surviving suit cards have encouraged reconstructions of an original pack larger than the later standard seventy-eight cards.

**Iconography:** Its Sword pips use repeated, generally straight blades on precious grounds, arranged decoratively rather than as scenes.

**Caution:** “Cary–Yale” and “Visconti di Modrone” refer to the same principal group, not two independent decks. Dates from the late 1420s through the 1440s have been proposed, but circa 1445 is the safer catalogue-era estimate.

---

## Brambilla deck, Milan, before 1447

**Documented fact:** The incomplete Brambilla group is held by the Pinacoteca di Brera and was probably made for Filippo Maria Visconti.

**Iconography:** Its suit pips multiply emblems against silvered grounds. Its Swords are notably curved, unlike the straighter Cary–Yale examples.

**Finding:** No securely catalogued surviving Brambilla Seven of Swords was located in the online Brera selection consulted. Modern completed “Brambilla” decks necessarily reconstruct absent cards.

---

## Visconti–Sforza / Pierpont Morgan–Bergamo, Milan, after 1450

**Documented fact:** The most nearly complete fifteenth-century luxury tarot survives divided among the Morgan Library, Accademia Carrara, and Colleoni collection. The Morgan dates it after Francesco Sforza’s entry into Milan as duke on 25 March 1450 and attributes much of it probably to Bonifacio Bembo or his workshop. The Morgan describes tarot at this date explicitly as a courtly card game, not an occult book. [Morgan Library](https://www.themorgan.org/manuscript/158525)

### Seven of Swords

**Documented fact:** A Seven of Swords survives in the dispersed group.

**Iconography:** Seven large swords are disposed symmetrically on a decorated ground. They are suit-signs rather than actors in a narrative. The blades and hilts remain individually recognizable; floral or ribbon-like ornament fills the field. There is no thief, camp, concealment, Moon, Aquarius glyph, or written divinatory keyword.

**Historical meaning:** Its primary meaning was its playable identity—Sword suit, rank seven. Claims that the arrangement originally encoded betrayal, lunar instability, Netzach, or an alchemical operation are later interpretations.

---

## Ferrarese and Este cards, circa 1470–1500

The Este, Charles VI, Rothschild, and related Ferrarese groups preserve many magnificent trumps and court cards but are incomplete.

**Finding:** No individual surviving card in these groups can securely replace the Visconti–Sforza Seven as an earlier documented specimen. Where commercial restorations supply a Seven of Swords, the image is normally extrapolated from related pips.

**Documented context:** Este court accounts record purchases of *carte da trionfi*, including the payment of 28 July 1442 for “*uno paro de carte da trionfi*” for Ercole and Sigismondo d’Este. [Le Tarot—Italian origins](https://letarot.it/Le-origini-italiane-del-Ludus-Triumphorum_pag_pg439_ita.aspx)

---

## Mantegna “Tarocchi,” circa 1465–80

**Documented fact:** The so-called Mantegna Tarocchi are two series of fifty didactic engravings representing social ranks, Apollo and the Muses, liberal arts, virtues, and celestial spheres.

**Finding:** They are not a tarot pack and contain no suit of Swords or Seven of Swords. “Tarocchi” is a later collecting name. Their relevance is comparative Renaissance iconography only.

---

## Sola Busca, probably Ferrara or Venice, circa 1490–91

The Brera describes the Sola Busca as the oldest complete surviving Italian tarot pack. It was engraved around 1490–91; its unique complete copy was subsequently hand-colored. It entered the Brera after state acquisition in 2009. [Pinacoteca di Brera](https://pinacotecabrera.org/en/news/mostra/il-segreto-dei-segreti-i-tarocchi-sola-busca/)

### Seven of Swords

**Documented fact:** Unlike ordinary early pips, the Sola Busca numeral cards are fully illustrated. Its Seven of Swords shows a human figure handling or carrying a cluster of swords in an active setting rather than seven isolated emblems.

**Scholarly reconstruction:** This is the strongest visual ancestor proposed for Pamela Colman Smith’s 1909 composition. The correspondence lies in converting the pip count into a person bodily carrying multiple weapons.

**Provenance of the influence:**

1. In 1907 the Busca-Serbelloni family supplied the British Museum with black-and-white photographs of all seventy-eight cards.
2. The photographs were exhibited or made available in London shortly before the Waite–Smith deck was drawn.
3. Multiple Waite–Smith minors have conspicuous Sola Busca parallels.
4. Smith lived and worked in London and had access to the British Museum’s visual culture.

**Limit of the evidence:** No surviving letter or diary entry has Smith say, “I copied the Sola Busca Seven of Swords.” Therefore direct consultation is a strong reconstruction, not a documented confession. The frequently repeated statement that she certainly saw a particular 1908 exhibition overstates the evidence. [Public Domain Review](https://publicdomainreview.org/collection/sola-busca)

**Disputed interpretation:** Brera scholarship has explored Hermetic-alchemical programs in the Sola Busca. Other iconographic historians warn that individual implements can also derive from conventional devotional, military, or classical imagery. Even if the deck has an alchemical program, it does not follow that the Seven carried a divinatory alchemical meaning.

---

## Rosenwald and Budapest sheets, circa 1500

**Documented fact:** These uncut or fragmentary printed sheets are crucial evidence for inexpensive tarot production beyond aristocratic painted packs.

**Iconography:** Their pips are repeated suit-emblems arranged for quick recognition. They do not provide a Waite-like episode of stealth or theft.

**Finding:** Online reproductions and restorations differ in completeness. Any reconstructed Seven should be distinguished from an original surviving impression. These sheets establish the wider circulation of conventional pip layouts, not occult meanings.

---

## Minchiate / Germini, Florence, sixteenth century onward

**Documented fact:** Minchiate expanded the trump series to forty-one trumps and used a ninety-seven-card pack, while preserving fifty-six suit cards.

**Seven of Swords iconography:** Conventional repeated blades, normally organized ornamentally. No narrative is required.

**Game significance:** In the “long” suits, Swords and Batons, numeral cards rank normally from ten down to ace; therefore the Seven stands below eight and above six. In Cups and Coins the numeral ordering reverses. [Pagat—Minchiate](https://www.pagat.com/tarot/minchiate.html)

**Esoteric readings:** Later divinatory editions of Minchiate sometimes retrofit Etteilla, Marseille, or Golden Dawn meanings. These do not belong to the historical Florentine game.

---

## Bolognese tarocchino

**Documented fact:** Bolognese tarocchino reduced the full pack, normally removing low numeral cards. In the standard sixty-two-card form, cards two through five of each suit are absent; six through ten remain.

Thus the Seven of Swords survives as a low, non-honor numeral card.

**Iconography:** Repeated swords, not a narrative scene.

**Historical distinction:** Bologna preserves unusually early evidence for cartomantic lists, but surviving evidence does not establish that the Renaissance Seven of Swords meant theft.

---

## Sicilian Tarot

**Documented fact:** The traditional Sicilian pack has sixty-three cards. In Swords, Cups, and Batons, only five through ten survive; the Seven remains. Modern packs show indices such as “7 S” for *sette di spade*. The rank descends King, Queen, Horse, Maid, 10, 9, 8, 7, 6, 5. [Pagat—Sicilian Tarocchi](https://www.pagat.com/tarot/sicilian.html)

**Iconography:** Spanish/Portuguese-style straight swords are more recognizable as weapons than Marseille arcs.

---

## Tarot de Marseille lineage

### Jean Noblet, Paris, circa 1650

**Documented limitation:** In the principal surviving Noblet copy, Swords VI–X are missing. Therefore an “original Noblet Seven of Swords” sold today is a restoration extrapolated from lower surviving pips.

### Jean Dodal, Lyon, circa 1701–15

**Iconography:** Six curved swords form three interlaced pairs around a central, fully drawn seventh sword. Floral or leafy ornament occupies openings. The blades curve inward to form a mandorla or oval lattice.

**Documented fact:** There is no person and no enacted theft.

### Pierre Madenié, Dijon, 1709, and Type-II development

**Scholarly reconstruction:** The mature Type-II layout adds or standardizes leafy ornaments around the central weapon. Madenié is an important witness in the transition to the arrangement subsequently associated with Conver.

### Nicolas Conver, Marseille, 1760

The canonical Seven has:

- three curved blades on each side;
- a straight seventh sword up the center;
- interwoven top and bottom terminals;
- colored bands;
- small vegetal or floral ornaments;
- Roman numeral VII, depending on impression or restoration.

A high-resolution sequence of the Conver suit is available through the [Etteilla historical-deck archive](https://etteilla.org/en/deck/41/tarot-de-marseille-by-nicolas-conver).

**Interpretive caution:** Calling the outer forms “six scimitars” is understandable but imprecise. In the stylized pattern, blade, guard, binding, and ornament have fused into a decorative lattice. Older versions make their weapon origin clearer.

### Grimaud / Paul Marteau, 1930

**Documented fact:** Paul Marteau’s *Ancien Tarot de Marseille*, published by Grimaud, helped standardize both the name “Tarot de Marseille” and a restricted modern palette.

**Change:** Cleaner registration, strong primary colors, and regularized outlines made the Seven easier to reproduce. Marteau also assigned symbolic meanings to colors and numerical structures. Those meanings are twentieth-century esoteric interpretation, not evidence of a medieval code.

### Camoin–Jodorowsky, 1997

**Documented fact:** Philippe Camoin and Alejandro Jodorowsky published a reconstructed Marseille deck assembled from historical models but also regularized according to their theory of a recoverable symbolic “original.”

**Change and meaning:** Restored colors, tiny details, and symmetrical relations are treated as an integrated spiritual language. Jodorowsky and Marianne Costa interpret seven as action in the world and Swords as mental/intellectual energy.

**Disputed claim:** Calling the result a restoration of one pristine ancient Tarot goes beyond the evidence. Historians treat Marseille as a changing family of printed patterns rather than a damaged copy of one fixed esoteric prototype.

---

## Besançon pattern

**Documented fact:** Especially in Swiss, Alsatian, and southern German production, the Papess and Pope were replaced by Juno and Jupiter to avoid confessional objections. These changes became conspicuous during the eighteenth century.

**Seven of Swords:** It remains a pip of seven interlaced blades. The Juno/Jupiter substitution does not change its identity or rank.

---

## Etteilla’s tarot, Paris, 1788–89

**Documented fact:** Jean-Baptiste Alliette, called Etteilla, reorganized and renamed the tarot as an explicitly divinatory “Book of Thoth.” His pack substantially departs from Marseille order and imagery.

**Seven of Swords:** The inherited card is tied to the keyword family **Espérance/attente**—hope or expectation—and, reversed, **bon avis**—wise or good advice. Early and later Etteilla editions differ in wording and printed card numbering.

**Important correction:** Modern sources often call every nineteenth-century “Grand Etteilla” image the 1789 original. There are at least three materially different Grand Etteilla types. Images should not be assigned to the first edition without edition-level provenance.

---

## Oswald Wirth, 1889

**Documented fact:** Wirth’s original twenty-two-card deck, designed under Stanislas de Guaita’s influence, consisted only of trumps.

**Finding:** There is no original 1889 Wirth Seven of Swords. Later seventy-eight-card products marketed as “Wirth Tarot” add minors not drawn for the original deck. Any Wirth-style Seven is therefore later completion or modern invention.

---

## Golden Dawn Book T, circa 1888–1900

**Documented fact:** The Golden Dawn assigned the thirty-six small cards from twos through tens to zodiacal decans. Book T explicitly gives the Seven of Swords to the Moon and 20°–30° Aquarius. [Book T transcription](https://www.tarrdaniel.com/documents/Thelemagick/gd/publication/english/golden_dawn_manuscripts.html)

### Prescribed design

Book T describes six swords held by two groups of hands, with a seventh sword upright in the center. The planetary and zodiacal emblems accompany the composition.

### Title and meaning

**Esoteric attribution:** *Lord of Unstable Effort*.

Its operative meaning combines partial success, insufficient persistence, vacillation, deceptive or unreliable action, and intellectual cleverness whose results remain unstable.

### Qabalistic placement

- Seven = **Netzach**, Victory.
- Swords = **Yetzirah**, the formative world.
- Suit = **Air**.
- Tetragrammaton = **Vav**.
- Astrology = Moon in Aquarius, third decan.
- Approximate tropical interval = when the Sun traverses 20°–30° Aquarius, conventionally around 9–18 February, with annual boundary variation.

**Clarification:** Vav belongs to the Sword suit in the four-world formula; it is not the Hebrew letter assigned uniquely to the Seven of Swords. Likewise, the card occupies a sephirotic position, not one of the twenty-two Tree-of-Life paths.

### Wang and Cicero reconstructions

**Modern reconstruction:** Robert Wang’s *Golden Dawn Tarot* and Sandra Tabatha and Chic Cicero’s *Golden Dawn Magical Tarot* translate Book T’s verbal diagrams into finished cards. Both show a central seventh sword opposed or surrounded by six subsidiary swords, with Moon and Aquarius symbols.

They are valuable visualizations of the manuscript system, but neither is an original nineteenth-century Golden Dawn deck.

## Waite–Smith, London, 1909

### Pamela Colman Smith’s scene

A brightly dressed man tiptoes or hurries away from a tented encampment. He carries five swords awkwardly by their blades, looking back over his shoulder. Two swords remain planted upright. Small figures appear in the distance.

**Documented description:** Waite calls him “a man in the act of carrying away five swords rapidly”; the other two remain in the ground. [Waite, *Pictorial Key*](https://www.free-ebooks.net/philosophy/A-Pictorial-Key-to-the-Tarot/html/73)

### What changed

This is no longer an abstract pip. Smith made an ambiguous human action visible:

- carrying may become stealing;
- looking back suggests vigilance, anxiety, or concealment;
- the tents suggest a military camp, fair, tournament, or temporary settlement;
- carrying blades rather than hilts makes the act precarious;
- leaving two behind makes success incomplete.

### Sources

**Strong scholarly reconstruction:** The Sola Busca provided the precedent of a figure physically burdened by weapons.

**Esoteric source:** Golden Dawn’s “Unstable Effort” and its central-versus-six design supplied an interpretive architecture.

**Cartomantic source:** Etteilla and Mathers supplied hope, attempt, plan, counsel, and uncertain results.

### What Waite did not say

Waite never explicitly calls the figure a thief, spy, deserter, or traitor. Those identities are later readings of Smith’s picture.

“Thief stealing from an enemy camp,” “deserter removing weapons,” “circus confidence man,” and “Ganymede stolen by Zeus” are all interpretive proposals. None has decisive documentary support.

---

## BOTA / Paul Foster Case

**Documented fact:** Case’s BOTA tarot generally uses uncolored Waite-derived line art intended for the student to color. The Seven of Swords retains the man, five carried swords, two remaining, and camp.

**Esoteric attribution:** Case preserves Golden Dawn fundamentals—Air, Moon in Aquarius, Netzach in Yetzirah—but filters them through his own color scales and psychological-Qabalistic teaching.

**Divinatory tendency:** Unstable plans, partial success, cunning, and the need for discrimination or better counsel.

**Bibliographic caution:** Case’s 1947 *Tarot: A Key to the Wisdom of the Ages* concentrates heavily on the major keys. Detailed minor-card teachings also circulate through BOTA lessons and *Oracle of the Tarot*, whose editions and dates are less transparent than the public book.

---

## Thoth Tarot, painted 1938–43; first full publication 1969

### Crowley and Frieda Harris

**Documented fact:** Crowley designed the deck’s symbolic program and Lady Frieda Harris painted it over roughly five years. The complete deck was commercially published after both artists’ deaths.

### Image

Six short swords point inward toward or press against one dominant central sword. Their hilts or terminals bear planetary emblems. The Moon and Aquarius govern the field. Harris replaces Smith’s anecdotal thief-like figure with an abstract structure of competing intellects.

### Title

**FUTILITY**

Crowley states: “The Seven of Swords is called Futility.” He interprets the weak, watery Moon operating in fixed intellectual Aquarius as indecision and compromise. His practical maxim is that, in the realm of Swords, a half-hearted blow fails: “if one must hit at all, a knock-out blow is best.” [Crowley, *Book of Thoth*](https://hermetic.com/crowley/book-of-thoth/small-cards)

**Meaning of the redesign:** The central blade retains the possibility of decisive will, while six lesser forces sap it. The failure is intellectual dispersal, rationalization, or divided purpose rather than necessarily theft.

### Tzaddi/Heh issue

Crowley’s famous “Tzaddi is not the Star” revision exchanges the traditional Golden Dawn letter assignments of the Emperor and Star. It does **not** alter the Seven of Swords, whose Moon-in-Aquarius decan survives unchanged.

## Notable modern redesigns

### Aquarian Tarot, David Palladini, 1970

**Modern development:** Streamlined Art Deco imagery retains the stealthy solitary figure and thereby helps normalize “deception” as the card’s dominant English-language meaning.

### Motherpeace, Vicki Noble and Karen Vogel, 1981

**Modern reinterpretation:** Feminist and circular-deck readings shift emphasis toward strategy, alienation from community, and recovery of one’s mental agency.

### Robin Wood Tarot, 1991

**Modern development:** Strengthens narrative cues of stealth and theft, contributing to popular keyword readings.

### Wild Unknown, Kim Krans, 2012

**Modern invention:** Seven swords arranged with an animal or predatory suggestion abstract the card into secrecy, concealment, and instinct. This descends more from the modern RWS semantic field than from Marseille pip structure.

### Modern inclusive and decolonial decks

Many recent decks replace the racialized “dark man” and “dark girl” complexion significators inherited from eighteenth- and nineteenth-century cartomancy. Others recast the apparent thief as whistleblower, survivor, strategist, refugee, spy, or person escaping coercion.

These are consciously modern ethical revisions, not recovered Renaissance meanings.

# The game

## Basic role

**Documented fact:** The Seven of Swords is an ordinary suited numeral card. It is not a trump and ordinarily carries no special score by itself.

In most tarot games:

- players must follow the led suit;
- trumps defeat suit cards;
- the Seven wins only according to its rank within Swords;
- it usually contributes as a low card when cards are counted in groups.

Kings and selected trumps are the principal counters. The Seven’s value commonly disappears into the subtraction used when counting packets of cards.

## Rank variations

| Game family | Rank of Seven of Swords |
|---|---|
| Early Italian / Minchiate | In the long suit: 10 high through ace low; Seven below Eight, above Six |
| French Tarot | King, Queen, Knight, Jack, 10…1; Seven below Eight, above Six |
| Piedmontese and many Italian games | Long-suit order broadly preserved |
| Bolognese tarocchino | Seven survives after low pips 2–5 are removed |
| Sicilian Tarocchi | Below Eight and above Six; part of a shortened suit |
| Central European Tarock | Often absent because packs are shortened; where present, rank follows the black-suit descending sequence |

## Point value

**Documented fact:** In French Tarot the Seven is a *carte basse* worth one-half point before the pair-counting convention. It is not an *oudler* or honor.

In Italian counting systems, low cards are commonly grouped and offset against one another; the Seven has no independent honor value.

## Trump-order disputes

These disputes concern trumps, not this pip, but define the pack around it.

Historians classify three principal early Italian orders:

1. **Type A, Bologna/Ferrara:** characteristic eastern order.
2. **Type B, Ferrara/Venice:** related but distinct ordering.
3. **Type C, Milan:** basis of the order transmitted into the Marseille family.

Justice and Fortitude/Strength move relative to other moral and temporal powers. The Fool is normally unnumbered and functions as an excuse or special card rather than as the lowest ordinary trump. Marseille later stabilizes Justice VIII and Strength XI; Waite switches them to Strength VIII and Justice XI, largely to satisfy Golden Dawn zodiacal sequencing.

None of these changes affects the Seven of Swords’ rank inside its suit.

# Chronology of attestation

## Circa 1412–25 — Marziano da Tortona

**Documented fact:** Marziano’s description and Marcello’s later letter attest a bespoke trump game for Filippo Maria Visconti. No Seven of Swords is named.

## 1440 — Florence/Anghiari evidence

**Documented fact:** A record discovered by Thierry Depaulis moved the earliest secure documentary use of *trionfi* in a playing-card context earlier than the famous Este entries. Giusto Giusti recorded giving *naibi a trionfi* to Sigismondo Malatesta.

## 1442 — Ferrara ledger

**Documented fact:** The Este account registers a pack of *carte da trionfi* for the young Ercole and Sigismondo. It names the pack, not individual pips.

## Circa 1450 — Visconti–Sforza Seven of Swords

**Documented object:** Earliest surviving, securely identifiable Seven of Swords located for this dossier.

## Circa 1450–1500 — *Sermones de ludo cum aliis*

The so-called Steele or Vanderbilt manuscript contains a sermon condemning games and a list of tarot trumps. Depaulis has shown that part of it adapts a sermon by St James of the March, with added paragraphs on cards and triumphs. [Vanderbilt catalogue](https://collections.library.vanderbilt.edu/repositories/2/archival_objects/261035)

**Finding:** It inventories the pack and its trumps but supplies no divinatory meaning for the Seven of Swords.

## Late fifteenth century — Boiardo and Sola Busca

Boiardo devised a moralized poetic tarot with suit themes and verses. The Sola Busca supplied fully illustrated pips. Neither establishes the later meaning “deception.”

## Circa 1494–1527 — Folengo and tarot poetry

Teofilo Folengo and other writers used tarot cards as literary prompts, including sonnets associated with dealt cards. These texts demonstrate social and poetic play.

**Finding:** No secure early poem was located that fixes the Seven of Swords as a thief or deceiver. Claims of a universal Renaissance divinatory lexicon are therefore unsupported.

## Early sixteenth century — *tarocchi*

**Documented fact:** The term *tarocchi* supersedes *trionfi* as the name of the pack and game. The etymology remains uncertain.

## Seventeenth century — Marseille-family pips

The Seven becomes the familiar interlaced six-plus-one sword pattern.

## 1781 — Court de Gébelin and Comte de Mellet

**Documented fact:** Volume VIII of *Le Monde primitif* claims an Egyptian origin. De Mellet connects the twenty-two trumps to Hebrew letters and proposes divination.

**Finding:** Neither provides the Golden Dawn correspondence of the Seven of Swords. The Hebrew-letter scheme initially concerns trumps, not individual pips.

## 1783–89 — Etteilla

**Documented fact:** Etteilla systematizes tarot divination and publishes an altered divinatory pack. Hope/expectation and good advice become the earliest well-attested printed meaning-family for the Seven of Swords.

## 1854–56 — Éliphas Lévi, *Dogme et rituel de la haute magie*

**Documented fact:** Lévi identifies tarot with Kabbalah and the Hebrew alphabet and gives the occult-tarot synthesis enormous influence.

**Finding:** Lévi does not give a developed, card-specific Seven of Swords interpretation equivalent to Waite’s or Crowley’s. Attributing “Moon in Aquarius” or “Unstable Effort” to Lévi is anachronistic.

## 1870 — Paul Christian

**Tradition:** Christian dramatizes tarot trumps as stations of Egyptian initiation and popularizes “major” and “minor arcana” terminology.

**Finding:** His Egyptian temple drama does not supply historically ancient evidence about this pip.

## 1888 — Mathers

Mathers prints the Etteilla-derived meanings “Hope, Confidence, Desire, Attempt, Wish,” reversed “Wise Advice, Good Counsel, Wisdom, Prudence, Circumspection.” [Mathers, p. 16](https://www.howardnema.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/12/the-tarot-1888-by-macgregor-mathers-1.pdf)

## 1888–1900 — Golden Dawn Book T

“Lord of Unstable Effort”; Moon in the third decan of Aquarius; Netzach in Yetzirah.

## 1889 — Papus and Wirth

Papus systematizes the tetragrammaton: Swords correspond to Vau. Wirth publishes only trumps.

## 1909–10 — Waite and Smith

Smith creates the now-dominant camp scene. Waite retains the older hope/plan/advice meanings while acknowledging their inconsistency.

## 1938–43 / 1944 / 1969 — Thoth

Harris paints Crowley’s card; *The Book of Thoth* appears in 1944; the full commercial deck follows in 1969. “Futility” becomes the lasting Thoth title.

## Late twentieth century onward

The RWS picture progressively outweighs Waite’s printed meanings. Theft, deception, infidelity, espionage, avoidance, and “getting away with it” become common. Psychological readers add self-deception, impostor syndrome, strategic withdrawal, and private planning.

## Netzach and the number seven

**Esoteric attribution:** Golden Dawn Qabalah maps all sevens to Netzach, the seventh sephirah, commonly translated Victory. In the suit of Air and world of Yetzirah, emotional-desiring force interferes with or destabilizes formal thought.

This yields the paradoxical title: Netzach promises victory, yet in Swords its diffuse affective force makes effort unstable.

**Correction:** This is not an ancient Jewish interpretation of a card. It is a nineteenth-century Hermetic-Qabalistic construction.

## Air and Vav

Swords = Air is standard in the Golden Dawn-derived English occult tradition. Papus’s formula gives Swords to Vau/Vav, the third letter of YHVH.

Alternative modern decks occasionally exchange Air and Fire between Swords and Wands. That is a genuine conflict among esoteric systems, not a historical ambiguity in Italian game rules.

### The decisive semantic change

The early line is:

> hope → attempt → plan → unstable effort.

The modern popular line is:

> stealth → theft → deception → betrayal.

The bridge is Pamela Colman Smith’s picture. Waite’s own text does not make theft the principal meaning, but later readers understandably narrativized the image as such.

“Affair” or sexual infidelity is a late contextual specialization. It is not in Etteilla, Mathers, Waite, Book T, or Crowley.

# Esoteric and symbolic readings

## Alchemical

**Possible Renaissance layer:** The Sola Busca as a whole has been read as a Hermetic-alchemical program involving transformation, metallurgy, Roman history, and initiatory struggle.

**Disputed at card level:** There is no surviving fifteenth-century gloss saying its Seven of Swords represents a named alchemical stage. Specific claims about mercury, separation, calcination, or seven metals are modern iconographic or esoteric extrapolations.

**Modern Thoth reading:** Harris’s central blade under pressure can be read as a failed *solve et coagula*: analytic forces divide without achieving a new synthesis. This is a legitimate modern symbolic reading, not Crowley’s documented historical origin claim.

## Kabbalistic

- First systematic background: Lévi, 1850s, for tarot broadly.
- Systematization of suits through YHVH: Papus, 1889.
- Specific Seven placement: Golden Dawn, late nineteenth century.
- Seven = Netzach.
- Swords = Yetzirah and Vav.
- Result: desire, affect, and instinct destabilize analysis.

No evidence places this scheme in fifteenth-century Milan.

## Astrological

**Golden Dawn first attested card-specific form:** Moon in the third decan of Aquarius.

Symbolically:

- Aquarius fixes and systematizes Air, favoring theory, policy, abstraction, groups, and future-oriented schemes.
- The Moon fluctuates, reflects, remembers, and reacts.
- The result is unstable calculation: clever but inconsistent, collective in theory but privately motivated, ingenious yet unable to sustain direction.

Crowley stresses the incompatibility. Modern astrologers sometimes rehabilitate it as lateral thinking, intelligence gathering, strategic anonymity, or unconventional planning.

## Hermetic

The Hermetic reading fuses:

- seven/Netzach;
- Air/Swords;
- Moon/Aquarius;
- Vav/Yetzirah;
- planetary color scales;
- decanic spirits and geomantic or angelic names.

This system first becomes fully card-specific in Golden Dawn materials and is spread by Mathers’s successors, Crowley, Israel Regardie, Case, Wang, and the Ciceros.

## Christian

**Historical iconography:** Swords in Christian art can signify justice, martyrdom, sorrow, persecution, or spiritual warfare. Seven swords famously pierce the Virgin’s heart in imagery of the Seven Sorrows.

**Caution:** The presence of seven swords does not by itself make the tarot pip an image of the Seven Sorrows. The Visconti and Marseille arrangements do not show Mary’s heart.

**Modern moral reading:** Taking weapons from a camp can become theft, betrayal, false witness, or alternatively the peacemaking removal of weapons. These are theological applications to Smith’s ambiguous scene, not fixed original meanings.

## Jungian

Carl Jung did not publish a systematic interpretation of the Seven of Swords.

Later Jungian tarot writers read:

- the apparent thief as the **Trickster**;
- the camp as collective convention;
- the stolen swords as appropriated ideas or mental functions;
- the backward glance as fear of the observing ego;
- the two abandoned swords as dissociated material;
- reversal as projection returning to the self;
- “deception” as the shadow’s covert strategy.

This approach was spread by postwar archetypal psychology, New Age tarot, Rachel Pollack, Sallie Nichols’s tarot psychology, and later counseling-oriented readers. Calling it “Jung’s meaning” is inaccurate.

# People and works

## Directly attached to the card

- **Bonifacio Bembo or workshop** — probable artist of much of the Visconti–Sforza group, including its emblematic pip tradition.
- **Nicola di Maestro Antonio** — proposed engraver of the Sola Busca.
- **Pamela Colman Smith** — created the decisive modern narrative.
- **A. E. Waite** — supplied the published description and meanings.
- **S. L. MacGregor Mathers and Golden Dawn compilers** — “Unstable Effort,” Moon in Aquarius.
- **Lady Frieda Harris** — transformed the card into abstract geometric conflict.
- **Aleister Crowley** — renamed it “Futility.”
- **Paul Foster Case** — transmitted a Golden Dawn/RWS synthesis through BOTA.
- **Rachel Pollack and Mary K. Greer** — major spreaders of psychological and reversal-sensitive modern readings.
- **Alejandro Jodorowsky and Marianne Costa** — influential Marseille numerology.

## Literature, film, and visual culture

### Italo Calvino, *The Castle of Crossed Destinies*

Calvino constructs stories through sequences of tarot images, especially Visconti and Marseille cards.

**Finding:** The work is essential to tarot’s literary reception, but no universally agreed, isolated Seven-of-Swords episode can safely be treated as Calvino’s definitive meaning. The card gains meaning syntactically from neighboring cards.

### T. S. Eliot, *The Waste Land*

Madame Sosostris names the drowned Phoenician Sailor, Belladonna, the man with three staves, the Wheel, and a blank card, and says she does not find the Hanged Man.

**Finding:** Eliot does not name the Seven of Swords. Claims that it is secretly one of Sosostris’s cards are speculative.

### W. B. Yeats

Yeats participated in the Golden Dawn and knew its tarot correspondences.

**Finding:** No primary text was located in which he develops the Seven of Swords individually. General Golden Dawn affiliation should not be turned into a card-specific Yeats doctrine.

### Ingmar Bergman

Bergman’s *The Seventh Seal* uses chess, Death, medieval plague, and apocalyptic imagery.

**Finding:** Despite “seven,” Death, and medieval visual culture, it is not a documented adaptation of the Seven of Swords.

### Jodorowsky’s films

Jodorowsky’s films contain extensive tarot, alchemical, circus, initiatory, and Marseille-derived imagery.

**Finding:** No specific scene could be securely documented as a quotation of the Seven of Swords. Claims that a furtive weapon-bearing figure “must be” this card require production evidence.

### Paintings and buildings

No pre-1909 painting or building can securely be said to “quote” Smith’s Seven of Swords. Similar motifs—weapon bearers, soldiers stealing arms, the Seven Sorrows, interlaced blades—are iconographic analogues, not demonstrated quotations.

Modern murals, tattoos, album art, and deck illustrations frequently quote Smith’s backward-looking figure, but attribution must be handled object by object.

# Controversies and disputes

## Egyptian origin

**Legend:** Court de Gébelin claimed in 1781 that tarot preserved an Egyptian sacred book. Comte de Mellet expanded the thesis, linked trumps to Hebrew letters, and described an alleged Egyptian divination method.

**Evidence against:** Tarot is documented in fifteenth-century northern Italy as a game. Its suits belong to the history of playing cards; its trump subjects fit medieval and Renaissance European visual culture. Gébelin supplied no Egyptian manuscript, object, or deciphered inscription. Hieroglyphic Egyptian had not yet been deciphered.

Dummett, Decker, Depaulis, and Farley trace the Egyptian tarot as an eighteenth-century intellectual construction, not an ancient transmission. [Dummett, “Origins of Tarot”](https://www.nybooks.com/articles/1981/05/14/origins-of-tarot/) and [Decker–Depaulis–Dummett](https://books.google.com/books?id=nVyDAAAAMAAJ)

## “Gypsy” or Romani origin

**Legend:** Tarot was brought from Egypt to Europe by “Gypsies.”

**History of the claim:** Gébelin-era Egyptian speculation was joined during the nineteenth century to the mistaken European belief that Romani people came from Egypt. Jean-Alexandre Vaillant and Papus helped popularize the narrative.

**Evidence against:** Tarot records in northern Italy precede evidence linking Romani communities to tarot. Linguistic and historical evidence places Romani origins in northern India, not Egypt. “Tarot of the Bohemians” documents nineteenth-century occult ideology, not tarot’s medieval provenance.

## Cathar origin

**Disputed modern claim:** Tarot allegedly encoded Cathar dualism or preserved Cathar doctrine after the Albigensian Crusade.

**Evidence:** No Cathar manuscript mentions tarot; no continuous object sequence connects thirteenth-century Cathar communities to fifteenth-century Italian packs. General resemblances—Popess, duality, spiritual hierarchy—are insufficient.

**Classification:** Modern speculative history.

## Templar origin

**Legend/modern invention:** Suppressed Templars allegedly hid initiatory secrets in the cards.

**Evidence:** The Templars were dissolved in the early fourteenth century, roughly a century before documented tarot. No Templar rule, trial record, inventory, or artifact contains a tarot pack or its standard sequence.

## Ancient Kabbalistic origin

**Esoteric attribution presented as history:** The twenty-two trumps are said to have been created for the twenty-two Hebrew letters; the minors are said to encode the Tree of Life from the beginning.

**Evidence:** No fifteenth-century card, ledger, sermon, or game rule states this. The explicit correspondence appears in eighteenth- and nineteenth-century French occultism and is systematized by Lévi, Papus, and the Golden Dawn.

## Was the RWS figure definitely a thief?

**Disputed identification.**

Arguments for:

- he carries away weapons;
- he looks backward;
- his gait appears furtive;
- the camp occupants seem unaware;
- later readers consistently perceive theft.

Arguments against certainty:

- Waite calls him only “a man” carrying swords;
- no visible owner confronts him;
- the camp could be his own;
- removing weapons could be sabotage, reconnaissance, strategic retreat, or peacemaking;
- his bright costume is poor camouflage;
- the two remaining swords complicate a simple successful theft.

Best conclusion: “thief” is the dominant later reading, not a documented title.

## Did Smith copy Sola Busca?

**Strong reconstruction, not proven statement.**

The dating, museum access, and repeated visual parallels make influence highly probable. “Copy,” however, may imply literal tracing. Smith transformed compositions, changed suits in some parallels, and integrated Waite’s meanings and Golden Dawn structures.

## Did the Seven always mean deception?

**No.** Etteilla and Mathers foreground hope and advice; Waite foregrounds plan and attempt; Book T gives instability; Crowley gives futility. Deception becomes dominant after readers narrate Smith’s image.

## Is “Unstable Effort” a misquotation?

Book T’s formal title is **Lord of Unstable Effort**. “Unstable Effort” is a normal abbreviated label. “Transitory Effort,” “Unstable Endeavor,” and “Futility” are later variants or paraphrases; Crowley’s official Thoth title is *Futility*.

## Is the decan Moon in Aquarius?

**Verified.** Book T assigns:

> “7 of Swords 20°–30° of Aquarius, Moon.”

Crowley retains the same planet, sign, and decan. Sources assigning Venus, Mercury, or Uranus to this card are using another system or are erroneous.

## Is the suit Air?

**Verified within Golden Dawn-derived occultism.** It is not a rule of Renaissance tarocchi. Some rival modern esoteric systems give Swords Fire, demonstrating that elemental assignments are conventional.

## Is the suit letter Vav?

**Verified in the Papus/Golden Dawn fourfold formula:** Wands–Yod, Cups–Heh, Swords–Vav, Pentacles–final Heh.

**Common error:** Treating Vav as if it were a card-specific Hebrew-letter assignment equivalent to the trump-letter correspondences.

## Misprints and unstable traditions

Several textual instabilities deserve caution:

- Etteilla’s words were expanded by followers, translators, and derivative lists.
- Mathers borrowed chiefly from Etteilla but not always transparently.
- Waite juxtaposed mutually inconsistent meanings and admitted as much.
- Commercial RWS booklets abbreviate “a plan that may fail,” sometimes leaving only deception-oriented keywords inferred from the picture.
- Modern “Noblet” Seven of Swords images are reconstructions because the relevant original cards are missing.
- “Wirth 1889 Seven of Swords” is an anachronism because Wirth drew only twenty-two trumps.
- Different Grand Etteilla types are repeatedly conflated.
- Thoth publication is variously dated 1944, 1969, or 1970: 1944 is the book and limited card reproductions; 1969 is the generally cited first complete commercial deck, with distribution continuing around 1970.

# Open questions

1. **Exact ownership of the surviving Visconti–Sforza Seven:** The deck is dispersed, and online reproductions often omit current repository-level provenance.

2. **Smith’s precise encounter with Sola Busca:** Museum photographs and visual borrowing are well supported; a dated visitor record, letter, or sketchbook statement naming the Seven has not surfaced.

3. **What action Smith intended:** Theft, espionage, removal of arms, strategic withdrawal, and comic precariousness remain possible.

4. **Sola Busca’s complete program:** Classical, political, Hermetic, and alchemical readings overlap. No contemporary explanatory manual survives.

5. **Etteilla first-edition wording:** Later synonym-rich lists cannot automatically be projected verbatim into the earliest printed pack. A card-by-card census of surviving 1788–89 impressions would clarify exact numbering and reversal legends.

6. **Rosenwald and Budapest Seven provenance:** Restored decks should identify which lines survive and which were supplied by the restorer.

7. **Earliest dominance of “theft”:** Waite does not explicitly use the word. A bibliographic survey of English guidebooks between 1910 and 1970 could identify when “thief/deception” overtook “hope/plan.”

8. **Specific literary or architectural quotations:** Calvino is demonstrably tarot-based, but Eliot, Yeats, Bergman, and individual buildings lack securely documented Seven-of-Swords quotations.
