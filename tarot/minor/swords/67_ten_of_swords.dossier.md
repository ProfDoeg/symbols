# Ten of Swords: Research Dossier

> **Scope note.** The Ten of Swords is a numbered suit card, not a trump. Consequently, trump names, trump-order disputes, Hebrew-letter/path allocations to the twenty-two trumps, and regional substitutions such as Papess/Juno or Pope/Jupiter do not belong directly to this card. They are included only where they clarify the pack in which the Ten occurs.
>
> **Evidence labels**
>
> - **Documented fact** — supported by a surviving card, contemporary record, or primary printed text.
> - **Scholarly reconstruction** — an inference by historians from surviving material.
> - **Tradition** — a practice or interpretation transmitted within card play or cartomancy.
> - **Esoteric attribution** — an occult correspondence asserted within a named system.
> - **Disputed claim** — proposed but unsupported or contested.
> - **Legend** — a historical-origin narrative contradicted by the documentary record.
> - **Modern invention** — a recent interpretation or design not demonstrable in older sources.

The title “Minor Arcana” is not fifteenth-century terminology. **Documented fact:** Paul Christian helped popularize *Major* and *Minor Arcana* in the nineteenth-century occult framework; medieval and Renaissance records speak instead of cards, suits, figures and *trionfi*.

Likewise, “Tarot de Marseille” is retrospective. **Scholarly reconstruction/documented terminology:** Romain Merlin used the expression in 1856; later French occultists and Paul Marteau popularized it. Jean Noblet and Jean Dodal did not market their seventeenth-century cards under that modern collective title.

## Iconography, deck by deck

### Proto-tarot and the fifteenth-century court packs

#### Marziano da Tortona–Michelino da Besozzo pack, Milan, c. 1414–25

**Documented fact:** Marziano described a pack commissioned for Filippo Maria Visconti, with sixteen classical gods arranged in four groups and four bird suits. Jacopo Antonio Marcello called it a *triumphorum genus* in 1449. The cards themselves are lost.

**Finding:** It contained no demonstrable Ten of Swords. It is relevant only as an early experiment in permanent trump cards and as a possible precursor to tarot’s structure. Calling it the first Ten-of-Swords-bearing tarot is unjustified.

#### Cary-Yale / Visconti di Modrone, Milan, c. 1440s

The names Cary-Yale and Visconti di Modrone refer to the same surviving group, now at Yale.

**Documented fact:** Thirty-nine numbered suit cards survive, together with unusually numerous court cards and eleven trumps. Surviving suit cards place repeated emblems against lavish gold grounds.

**Iconographic character:** Swords are actual straight weapons, arranged decoratively rather than woven into the later Marseille oval. Visconti and ducal emblems fill intervening spaces.

**Open point:** Published inventories disagree over whether every relevant ten survives, and the original pack may have had an expanded court structure. The deck therefore cannot safely be treated as a standard seventy-eight-card prototype in every detail.

#### Brambilla deck, Milan, c. 1440s

**Documented fact:** Forty-eight Brambilla cards are in the Pinacoteca di Brera. The cards are conventionally associated with the Visconti court and attributed to the workshop of Bonifacio Bembo or a closely related hand.

**Iconography:** The numbered cards use repeated suit emblems on gilt grounds rather than narrative scenes. There is no occult title or illustrated catastrophe attached to ten swords.

#### Visconti-Sforza / Pierpont Morgan–Bergamo group, Milan, after 1450

**Documented fact:** The Morgan identifies its cards as commissioned in the Visconti-Sforza milieu, probably after Francesco Sforza’s entry into Milan as duke on 25 March 1450, and traditionally attributes much of the work to Bonifacio Bembo.

The Morgan explicitly catalogues **The Ten of Swords**. Its pip-card nature is crucial:

- Ten weapons, not ten abstract “thoughts,” constitute the subject.
- The swords are presented as repeated heraldic/decorative objects.
- The gold ground and family devices locate the card in courtly material culture.
- There is no wounded figure, sunrise, Gemini glyph, Sephirotic diagram or divinatory caption.

**Documented fact:** This is an early surviving Ten of Swords.  
**Modern projection:** reading its ten weapons as betrayal, nervous collapse or “rock bottom” imports meanings developed centuries later.

#### Este/Ferrarese fragments, c. 1450–80

Several hand-painted fragments and cards associated with Ferrara or the Este court survive in scattered collections.

**Documented fact:** Ferrarese ledgers record *trionfi* purchases in 1442 and later, but an account for a pack does not establish the imagery of its individual Ten of Swords.

**Iconographic tendency:** Surviving numbered cards generally arrange suit objects ornamentally. No Ferrarese Ten has been shown to contain the Waite-Smith deathlike scene.

#### Mantegna “Tarocchi,” c. 1460s

**Documented fact:** The so-called Mantegna Tarocchi are fifty instructional engravings organized into five decades of social ranks, liberal arts, cosmic principles and virtues.

**Finding:** They are not a tarot pack, contain no four suits and have no Ten of Swords. Their name is antiquarian and misleading. They can illuminate Renaissance emblematic culture but not this card’s direct genealogy.

### Sola Busca, c. 1490–91

The complete illuminated deck is now at the Pinacoteca di Brera. The Brera describes it as seventy-eight engraved cards—twenty-two triumphs and fifty-six cards in the traditional suits—subsequently illuminated in tempera and gold.

**Documented fact:** It is the earliest complete surviving tarot and the earliest complete surviving pack in which every numbered card has a distinct figural scene.

The Ten of Swords shows:

- A male figure bent sharply forward.
- Ten long weapons gathered behind, over or around him.
- A posture of carrying, depositing or struggling with the swords.
- A spatially integrated scene rather than ten independent pips.

The precise action is not self-explanatory. “Burdened by swords” is a reasonable visual description; elaborate narratives about execution, initiation or alchemical death are interpretations.

### Was it Smith’s source?

The answer requires a correction to a frequently repeated claim.

- **Documented fact:** photographs of the complete Sola Busca were deposited at the British Museum in 1907, shortly before Pamela Colman Smith made the Waite-Smith deck.
- **Strong scholarly reconstruction:** Smith saw or otherwise knew the photographs. Several correspondences are too close to dismiss easily, notably the Sola Busca Three of Swords and Smith’s heart pierced by three swords.
- **Strong visual inference:** the bent carrier in the Sola Busca **Ten of Swords** is the most conspicuous prototype for Smith’s **Ten of Wands**, not her Ten of Swords.
- **Disputed/overstated claim:** “The Sola Busca Ten of Swords is the direct source of Smith’s prone corpse pierced by ten swords.” The subjects and postures do not match. At most, the older card helped demonstrate that numbered pips could become narrative pictures.

Thus the item-specific lead is partly verified and partly corrected: the Sola Busca deck is a major source for Smith’s scenic-minor project, but its Ten of Swords is not a close compositional model for her Ten of Swords.

### Rosenwald and Budapest sheets, late fifteenth or early sixteenth century

These woodcut sheets preserve popular, less luxurious Italian cards.

**Documented fact:** They are important evidence that tarot existed outside illuminated court commissions and that repeated suit-sign arrangements were standard.

**Finding:** Their relevance to this card is structural and graphic. They do not supply the prone victim, Golden Dawn “Ruin,” or a recoverable independent divinatory meaning. Precise assignment of isolated sheet fragments to named regional patterns remains debated.

### Tarot de Marseille lineage

#### General sword-pip grammar

Marseille-pattern sword cards use curved blades interlaced into an almond, mandorla or double-oval lattice. What can look like ten scimitars is partly a graphic convention: curved blade-bands cross one another symmetrically, with hilts or terminals at the margins. Floral sprays occupy openings and corners.

**Documented fact:** The ornament is inherited from playing-card design.  
**Tradition/modern interpretation:** treating the enclosure as a “mental prison,” womb, auric field or sealed totality is a modern symbolic reading, not an attested intention of the cardmakers.

#### Jean Noblet, Paris, c. 1650–59

A vital negative finding: the sole substantially surviving Noblet pack lacks Swords VI–X. Therefore:

- No original Noblet Ten of Swords survives in the known pack.
- “Noblet Ten of Swords” images in commercial restorations are reconstructions.
- The reconstructed cards are valuable design hypotheses, not primary objects.

The surviving lower Swords establish the pack’s Type-I grammar, but they cannot prove the exact lost Ten.

#### Jacques Viéville, Paris, c. 1650

Viéville’s complete or nearly complete pack provides seventeenth-century evidence outside the strict later Marseille Type-II line.

Its Swords retain the curved, interlaced Italian-suit convention. Ornament and orientation differ from later standardized examples. There is no figural tragedy.

#### Jean Dodal, Lyon, c. 1701–15

**Documented fact:** Dodal’s Ten survives.

It has:

- Ten curved sword components organized symmetrically.
- Two central vertical blades or blade-like forms completing the even-number pattern.
- Floral growth and small ornaments filling negative space.
- No human figure and no printed divinatory keyword.

The card is frequently shown in different orientations in reproductions. Wear, mounting and restoration complicate assertions that one end is definitively “up.”

#### Pierre Madenié, Dijon, 1709

Madenié is the earliest dated surviving representative of the Type-II Marseille pattern.

The Ten stabilizes a denser, symmetrical sword lattice with more regular floral decoration. Type-II features subsequently dominate many southeastern French decks.

#### Nicolas Conver, Marseille, 1760

The Conver Ten presents ten curved swords in the established lattice, with vegetal corner and central ornament. Surviving Conver impressions and later reprints vary in color and registration.

**Caution:** Numerous cards marketed as “the 1760 Conver” are later impressions or restorations. A digitized BnF impression is catalogued to the workshop’s later operating period, not automatically to the first 1760 printing.

#### Grimaud–Marteau, 1930 and later

Paul Marteau’s *Ancien Tarot de Marseille*, published by Grimaud, regularized color, line and nomenclature and became the twentieth century’s most influential “Marseille” deck.

The Ten retains the lattice but makes color choices—especially blue, red, yellow and flesh tones—more categorical. Marteau then interpreted those colors esoterically. Those interpretations belong to Marteau’s twentieth-century system, not demonstrably to Dodal or Conver.

#### Camoin–Jodorowsky, 1997

Philippe Camoin and Alejandro Jodorowsky presented their deck as a restoration of an original sacred structure.

**Documented fact:** It combines consultation of historical Marseille cards with redrawing, recoloring and completion.

**Disputed claim:** that it recovers a single pristine, deliberately encoded ur-Tarot. Multiple historical workshops, regional families, copying errors and design changes make such singular recovery impossible to demonstrate.

Its Ten preserves the interwoven swords and vegetation. Jodorowsky and Marianne Costa read the tens structurally as completion and transition: intellectual energy reaches its limit and becomes available to a new cycle. That is a modern numerological reading, not a seventeenth-century caption.

### Minchiate

Minchiate expanded tarot to ninety-seven cards, adding signs of the zodiac, elements and virtues while retaining Italian suits.

**Documented fact:** The Ten of Swords remains a suit card, not an added trump. It is normally a conventional array of ten weapons.

The larger trump series does not give this pip a Hebrew letter or Egyptian identity. Any such assignment is a later occult overlay.

### Bolognese pattern

Tarocchino Bolognese reduced the pack, usually retaining courts, aces and higher pip cards while omitting lower ranks.

The exact retained numeral range varies by historical form and ruleset. When present, the Ten of Swords is a high pip in the long suit and is visually non-scenic.

**Documented fact:** Bolognese trump ordering belongs to Dummett’s Order A.  
**Irrelevant extrapolation:** Justice/Strength placement does not alter the identity or astrological attribution of the Ten, since the gaming pack has no such pip astrology.

### Sicilian pattern

The Sicilian tarot has distinctive shortened suits and a reduced trump sequence.

In the modern Mineo game, Swords rank King, Queen, Horse, Maid, 10, 9, 8, 7, 6, 5, and sometimes 4. The Ten is consequently the highest numeral below the courts.

The Fool is **Il Fuggitivo / lu Fujutu**, reflecting a regional gaming role and name. That fact does not turn the Ten into a trump or change its suit.

### Besançon pattern

The Besançon pattern substitutes **Juno** and **Jupiter** for the Papess and Pope, chiefly in Swiss, Alsatian and southern German contexts from the eighteenth century.

**Finding:** This affects trumps II and V, not the Ten of Swords. Its sword pips continue the ornamental Latin-suited family.

### Etteilla’s Grand Etteilla, 1788–89

Etteilla radically reordered and relabelled the pack. The Ten of Swords became **card 54**, with printed cartomantic captions:

- Upright: **Pleurs** — “Tears.”
- Reversed: **Avantage** — “Advantage.”

Later editions show ten relatively straight blue swords, points downward, filling a framed panel. Unlike Marseille cards, the design tells the user its reading in print.

**Documented fact:** Surviving nineteenth-century Grand Etteilla impressions carry these words.  
**Reconstruction:** individual captions derive from Etteilla’s late-eighteenth-century teaching, although precise iconographic details vary among Type I, II and III editions.

### Oswald Wirth, 1889

Wirth’s original deck consisted of twenty-two trumps.

**Finding:** It contains no Ten of Swords. Any “Wirth Ten” belongs to a later completion or derivative deck. Wirth’s writings about number, suit and universal symbolism can be applied to the card only secondarily.

### Golden Dawn manuscript design: *Book T*

The Golden Dawn assigned the card the title **Lord of Ruin**, Sun in 20°–30° Gemini.

The canonical design described in the order’s tarot papers belongs to the “flashing-colour,” angelic-hand tradition:

- Ten swords are arranged according to an ordered magical scheme.
- An angelic hand and rose or rose-tree motifs occur in Golden Dawn reconstructions.
- Solar and Gemini symbols identify the decan.
- Malkuth colors integrate citrine, olive, russet and black.
- The image is explicitly talismanic and correspondential, unlike Marseille ornament.

The historical manuscripts circulated privately and in variants; there was no single mass-produced nineteenth-century “official Golden Dawn deck.”

#### Wang reconstruction, 1978

Robert Wang’s *Golden Dawn Tarot*, prepared with Israel Regardie, visualized the order papers in a full deck. Its Ten uses clouds, a divine hand, ten blades and astrological symbols.

**Status:** modern reconstruction of nineteenth-century instructions, not a surviving Victorian deck.

#### Cicero reconstruction, 1990s onward

Chic and Sandra Tabatha Cicero’s *Golden Dawn Magical Tarot* likewise reconstructs the papers, with more explicit color-scale and ritual detail.

Different Wang and Cicero choices show that manuscript instructions underdetermine exact composition, anatomy, shading and color.

### Waite-Smith, 1909

Pamela Colman Smith’s image shows:

- A person lying face-down near water.
- Ten swords vertically or diagonally piercing the back.
- One hand making a gesture sometimes read as benediction.
- A black sky overhead.
- A yellow or golden horizon beneath the darkness.
- Distant hills or land across calm water.
- A red mantle covering the lower body.

Waite’s own description is stark: “A prostrate figure, pierced by all the swords belonging to the card.” He immediately cautions that it is “not especially a card of violent death.”

#### Sources and changes

1. **Documented source:** the inherited cartomantic meaning—tears, affliction and grief—appears in Etteilla and Mathers before Smith.
2. **Documented design context:** Smith knew theatrical staging, illustration and Catholic visual culture; the scene reads like a staged martyrdom or tragic tableau.
3. **Strong reconstruction:** Sola Busca helped authorize fully scenic pips.
4. **Disputed identification:** the hand gesture has been called the Christian sign of blessing, a Masonic gesture, a reference to Christ, or a sign that the figure remains spiritually conscious. Smith left no card-by-card explanation confirming these.
5. **Modern invention:** “The sunrise proves recovery” is now ubiquitous, but Waite did not state it. His reversed reading supplies temporary advantage rather than the modern upright slogan “the worst is over.”

The scene transforms an enumerated suit card into the climax of a narrative sequence. Ten no longer merely counts swords; it becomes visual excess—one sword would kill, ten make defeat categorical.

### Paul Foster Case and BOTA

Paul Foster Case and Jessie Burns Parke’s BOTA tarot is principally known for its line-drawn trumps intended for student coloring. Case’s pip teachings, however, extend Golden Dawn correspondences.

In *Oracle of the Tarot*, the Ten of Swords includes an ankh-like composition whose lower point pierces a serpent, compared with the seal of Cagliostro. Case reads this as ultimate victory over an adverse force.

Case changes the astrological rulership:

- Golden Dawn/Crowley: **Sun in Gemini**.
- Case: the third Gemini decan under **Saturn and Uranus**, approximately 9–20 June.

This is a genuine system conflict, not a printing variant.

### Thoth, painted 1938–43; first full publication 1969

Frieda Harris’s Ten is a geometric destruction rather than a corpse scene.

It contains:

- Ten swords whose hilts occupy Sephirotic positions.
- A central sword corresponding to the sixth Sephirah and the Sun.
- Nine other points converging upon and shattering that central blade.
- Gemini and solar signs.
- A fractured, crystalline field in Harris’s layered geometric style.
- The title **RUIN**.

Crowley explains that the ten expresses the exhaustive manifestation of intellectual energy. He calls it “the ruin of the Intellect,” but adds that solar influence prevents ruin from being absolutely final.

**Documented fact:** The Book of Thoth verifies **Sun in Gemini**, not a substitute planetary attribution. This continues *Book T*.

The picture’s logic differs from Waite-Smith:

- Smith dramatizes a human result.
- Harris diagrams the collapse of the rational/analytic instrument itself.
- The “victim” is the solar central sword, not a human body.

### Notable modern redesigns

Modern decks usually choose among four inherited grammars:

1. **Marseille continuation:** ten abstract curved swords and vegetation.
2. **Waite-Smith continuation:** betrayal, prone body, many wounds and dawn.
3. **Thoth continuation:** fracture, technological or intellectual collapse.
4. **Revisionist recovery:** the figure rises, removes swords or becomes compost for renewal.

The increasingly common reversed meaning “healing/recovery” is frequently painted directly into upright redesigns. That is a modern therapeutic development, not Etteilla’s exact “advantage” or Waite’s merely temporary success.

## The game

### Original function

**Documented fact:** Tarot originated as a family of trick-taking games. The earliest records concern production, purchase and play, not a secret initiatory scripture.

A standard Italian pack eventually comprised:

- 21 permanent trumps;
- the Fool;
- four suits of fourteen cards;
- in each suit, Ace–10 plus King, Queen, Knight and Page.

The Ten of Swords was an ordinary suit card. It could win only by following suit at the appropriate rank or when other players failed to beat it; a trump defeated it.

### Rank

In traditional Latin-suited tarot games, the two long or “black” suits—Swords and Batons—normally rank in natural ascending order:

**King > Queen > Knight > Page > 10 > 9 > … > Ace.**

Cups and Coins often reverse the numeral portion:

**King > Queen > Knight > Page > Ace > 2 > … > 10.**

Thus the Ten of Swords is normally the highest pip below the four courts. This inversion between long and round suits is a rule of play, not occult polarity.

### Value

In many tarot point-counting systems:

- Kings are highest-value court cards.
- Queens, Knights and Pages descend in value.
- Trumps XXI, I and the Fool form special counters in many games.
- Ordinary pips, including the Ten of Swords, are low-value cards or effectively fillers when counted singly, although their trick-taking rank can be high.

Exact values vary. In French Tarot, ordinary suit cards from 10 down are “small cards” worth one-half point each under the half-point convention. The Ten has no special occult score.

### Tarock

Central-European Tarock games often use French suits and shortened packs. Depending on the game, low black-suit cards are removed first; where the black Ten remains, it is a high-ranking suit card but not a special counter.

The German name **Zehn Pik** may consequently describe the functional descendant in French-suited Tarock, while **Zehn der Schwerter** describes the esoteric/Italian-suited tarot card.

### Minchiate

The Ten remains a high pip in Swords. Minchiate’s expanded trump structure does not grant it a separate bonus merely because it is ten.

### Trump-order disputes

Dummett classified historical Italian trump orders as:

- **A:** Bologna and Florence;
- **B:** Ferrara;
- **C:** Milan and the ancestry of the Marseille line.

These orders vary in the relative positions of virtues and other trumps. Justice and Fortitude/Strength move; the Fool remains outside the ordinary sequence and has a special excusing role.

**Important:** none of these disputes changes the Ten of Swords. A pip is ranked within its suit, not within the trump hierarchy.

### Malkuth and ten

In Golden Dawn Qabalah the numbered cards correspond to Sephiroth:

Ace/Kether through Ten/Malkuth.

Malkuth means “Kingdom,” manifestation or the terminal receptacle of the preceding emanations. In the Swords/Yetzirah combination, intellectual or formative energy has reached its densest end-state.

The standard Malkuth color complex is:

- citrine;
- olive;
- russet;
- black.

Golden Dawn sources describe Malkuth as the “Daughter” fallen toward or touching the shells, which supplies a metaphysical grammar for the tens’ instability: manifestation is complete but furthest from the undivided source.

**Esoteric attribution:** This is not evidence that fifteenth-century cardmakers designed ten swords as the tenth Sephirah.

### Air and Vav

The Golden Dawn aligns the four suits with the divine name:

| YHVH letter | Suit | Element | World |
|---|---|---|---|
| Yod | Wands | Fire | Atziluth |
| First Heh | Cups | Water | Briah |
| Vav | Swords | Air | Yetzirah |
| Final Heh | Pentacles | Earth | Assiah |

Vav belongs to the suit as a whole. The Ten itself is not “Hebrew letter Vav” in the way a trump receives one of twenty-two alphabet letters.

### Decan: Sun in Gemini

*Book T* assigns:

- Eight of Swords: Jupiter, 0°–10° Gemini.
- Nine of Swords: Mars, 10°–20° Gemini.
- Ten of Swords: Sun, 20°–30° Gemini.

The Golden Dawn used the traditional Chaldean planetary sequence applied to zodiacal decans. Crowley retained this attribution.

Symbolically, the Sun’s unity and centrality are dispersed through mutable, Mercurial Gemini. Crowley turns that tension into the multiplication of analysis until intellect destroys its own center.

### Tzaddi/Heh and Emperor/Star

Crowley’s famous “Tzaddi is not the Star” revision swaps the Golden Dawn Hebrew-letter associations of the Emperor and Star in his Thelemic system.

**Finding:** It has no direct effect on the Ten of Swords. Neither Heh nor Tzaddi is the card’s letter. The Ten remains Sun in Gemini, Malkuth of Air. Bringing the Emperor/Star controversy into its attribution is category confusion.

### Courts

The requested “sub-element and zodiacal span for the courts” does not apply to the Ten. Golden Dawn courts possess mixed elemental identities and approximately thirty-degree zodiacal ranges; numbered cards 2–10 receive ten-degree decans. The Ten is therefore a decan card, not “Air of Air” in the same technical sense as a court.

## Divinatory meanings by author

Quotations from twentieth-century copyrighted authors are kept brief; their positions are otherwise paraphrased.

### Etteilla, late 1780s

Grand Etteilla card 54:

- Upright: **“Pleurs”** — tears.
- Reversed: **“Avantage”** — advantage.

Later explanatory lists expand these toward affliction/grief and temporary benefit.

**Documented tradition:** Etteilla reversals are independent alternatives, not mechanically “the opposite” of upright meanings.

### Mathers, 1888

In *The Tarot: Its Occult Signification, Use in Fortune-Telling, and Method of Play*, no. 54:

- Upright: **“Tears, Affliction, Grief, Sorrow.”**
- Reversed: **“Passing Success, Momentary Advantage.”**

Mathers says his supplementary instructions are chiefly drawn from Etteilla. This is an explicit transmission chain, not an independent medieval tradition.

### Golden Dawn, *Book T*

Well dignified, the solar factor can imply partial recovery, disciplined reasoning or a limited benefit amid collapse. Ill dignified, the card signifies ruin, disruption, insolence, failure, undisciplined or overactive mental force.

The memorable title is **The Lord of Ruin**.

### Waite, 1910

Waite’s *Pictorial Key*, p. 85 in the consulted PDF:

- Upright: the design itself, then “pain, affliction, tears, sadness, desolation.”
- Qualification: “It is not especially a card of violent death.”
- Reversed: “Advantage, profit, success, favour,” none permanent; also power and authority.

Waite closely preserves the Etteilla–Mathers meaning chain while giving it a new narrative picture.

### Crowley, 1944

Crowley calls it “the ruin of the Intellect” and describes reason run riot: a mechanism of analysis that continues dividing after usefulness is exhausted. Yet the Sun makes complete destruction impossible; when conditions become bad enough, reaction and recovery begin.

Crowley does not use reversals as Waite does. Dignity, position and interacting cards modify the meaning.

### Paul Foster Case

Case distinguishes spiritual and material operation:

- Spiritually: ending delusion, defeating an adverse force, breaking restrictive conditions.
- Materially: sudden disruptive change, disappointment, interference by others, and loss through indiscreet writing or contracts.
- Ill dignified: complete failure, desolation and ruin.
- Keyword: **Destruction**.

Unlike Book T, Case gives the decan to Saturn and Uranus rather than the Sun.

### Rachel Pollack

In *Seventy-Eight Degrees of Wisdom*, Pollack reads the excessive ten swords as the absolute limit of defeat and reaction. The card can expose melodrama or a mentality that declares total disaster; because the image has reached an extreme, continuation becomes impossible.

The yellow horizon permits a developmental reading: consciousness may rise after old mental structures collapse. Pollack’s emphasis helped spread “bottom reached, therefore change can begin,” although that exact therapeutic formula is later popular shorthand.

Pollack generally does not offer a fixed reversed dictionary equivalent to Waite’s. Reversal can intensify resistance to ending or indicate emergence from the extreme.

### Mary K. Greer

In *The Complete Book of Tarot Reversals*, Greer treats reversal contextually rather than as a single antonym. Her general modes include blocked, delayed, internalized, projected, excessive, releasing and breaking-through expressions.

Applied to the Ten:

- Upright: painful ending, feeling stabbed in the back, exhaustive defeat or a story of total victimization.
- Reversed possibilities: survival, removing the swords, lingering trauma, refusal to accept finality, relapse, or pain becoming internal rather than publicly visible.

Greer’s system is influential but should not be retrojected into Etteilla, whose *Avantage* is a specific printed alternative.

## Esoteric and symbolic readings

### Alchemical

**Disputed scholarly/esoteric reading:** Sofia Di Vincenzo and other interpreters have read Sola Busca imagery through Renaissance alchemy. The deck certainly contains furnaces, vessels, metals and enigmatic operations.

For its Ten of Swords, burden, cutting, division or the transport of metal can be placed within *separatio* or *mortificatio*. No surviving fifteenth-century commentary says this. It remains iconographic interpretation, not documented divination.

Crowley’s card more securely permits an alchemical analogy: over-differentiation shatters the fixed intellectual structure, preparing dissolution and reconstitution.

### Kabbalistic

The earliest published tarot–Hebrew alphabet linkage is associated with the Comte de Mellet’s essay in Court de Gébelin’s 1781 volume. It concerned the twenty-two trumps.

Lévi made tarot central to a Qabalistic magical synthesis in *Dogme et Rituel*. Papus then diagrammed tarot through letter, number and tetragrammaton cycles.

The specific formula **Ten of Swords = Malkuth of Yetzirah** is Golden Dawn, late nineteenth century. It is not present in medieval Jewish Kabbalah or Renaissance game records.

### Astrological

Etteilla’s work is astrologically structured at pack level, but the presently consulted primary and museum sources did not substantiate a Sun-in-Gemini attribution to his Ten of Swords.

**Documented first secure source in this dossier:** Golden Dawn *Book T*: Sun in 20°–30° Gemini.

Crowley preserves it. Case changes it. Modern decks that print “Sun in Gemini” inherit Golden Dawn/Crowley rather than Marseille or Etteilla.

### Hermetic

Lévi’s synthesis and the Golden Dawn made tarot a portable table of Hermetic correspondences. In this framework:

- sword = analytic discrimination;
- Air = thought, language and division;
- Gemini = multiplicity and duality;
- Sun = center, identity and illumination;
- ten/Malkuth = complete manifestation.

“Ruin” results when discriminating mind becomes endlessly divisive and attacks its own organizing center.

### Christian

Smith’s fallen body, red covering, blessing-like hand and dawn invite Christian comparisons:

- martyrdom;
- the dead Christ;
- sacrificial suffering;
- darkness before resurrection.

These are plausible visual resonances. No surviving explanation by Smith identifies the figure as Christ or assigns ten wounds a biblical source. Calling the card a secret Crucifixion is disputed.

Waite was a Christian mystic, but his published text deliberately limits the card: it does not especially mean violent death.

### Jungian

Jung did not create a tarot-card correspondence system and did not publish an interpretation of the Ten of Swords.

Later Jungian tarot writers treat the image as:

- collapse of an overidentified ego position;
- confrontation with the shadow of victimhood or betrayal;
- death of an obsolete complex;
- psychic enantiodromia—an extreme turning into its opposite;
- individuation through relinquishing a false mental identity.

**Modern invention/tradition:** These readings can be psychologically fruitful but should be attributed to post-Jung tarot practice, not to Jung or Renaissance makers.

- **Bonifacio Bembo and workshop:** traditional attribution for major Visconti-Sforza groups, including the milieu of the early ornamental Ten.
- **Nicola di Maestro Antonio:** proposed by Brera scholarship as the Sola Busca painter; exact division between engraver, designer and illuminator remains debated.
- **Jean Noblet:** important Type-I maker, but his original Ten is lost.

- **Pierre Madenié:** early dated Type-II maker.
- **Nicolas Conver:** influential Marseille cardmaker.
- **Jean-Baptiste Alliette/Etteilla:** gave it the explicit cartomantic identity *Pleurs/Avantage*.
- **S. L. MacGregor Mathers:** transmitted Etteilla’s meanings and then helped construct the Golden Dawn system.
- **Pamela Colman Smith:** created the definitive modern narrative image.
- **A. E. Waite:** designed the deck’s program and published its meanings.
- **Frieda Harris:** converted “Ruin” into a modernist geometric painting.
- **Aleister Crowley:** supplied its Thelemic/Hermetic rationale.
- **Paul Foster Case and Jessie Burns Parke:** developed the BOTA version.
- **Paul Marteau:** standardized the influential Grimaud Marseille deck.
- **Robert Wang, Israel Regardie, Chic Cicero and Sandra Tabatha Cicero:** published competing Golden Dawn reconstructions.
- **Alejandro Jodorowsky, Philippe Camoin and Marianne Costa:** spread a modern structural Marseille interpretation.

### Literature, film and visual culture

The requested cultural names require careful negative findings.

#### Italo Calvino, *The Castle of Crossed Destinies*

**Documented fact:** Calvino constructs stories through tarot cards, drawing on the Visconti-Sforza and Marseille traditions in different sections.

**Finding:** No source consulted established the Ten of Swords as a uniquely named governing card comparable to the work’s better-known trump sequences. It is relevant to tarot’s literary afterlife, but a specific Ten claim requires passage-level verification.

#### T. S. Eliot, *The Waste Land*

Madame Sosostris names “the drowned Phoenician Sailor,” Belladonna, “the man with three staves,” the Wheel and “the one-eyed merchant,” and says “this card, / Which is blank, is something he carries on his back.”

**Finding:** Eliot does not name the Ten of Swords. Identifying the blank card, corpse imagery or “fear death by water” with it is speculative. Jessie Weston, Frazer and occult tarot shaped the poem more broadly, but the card-specific claim is unproved.

#### W. B. Yeats

Yeats belonged to the Golden Dawn and worked with its symbolic system.

**Finding:** This establishes proximity to *Book T*, not a demonstrable poem or artwork specifically about the Ten of Swords. Claims that a particular Yeats poem “is” this card require separate textual evidence.

#### Ingmar Bergman

*The Seventh Seal* uses chess, Death and medieval apocalypse.

**Finding:** It does not document a Ten of Swords quotation. Similarity between a prone or doomed figure and a tarot card is insufficient evidence.

#### Alejandro Jodorowsky’s films

Jodorowsky’s films use tarot-like initiatory and alchemical imagery; *The Holy Mountain* is particularly saturated with esoteric symbolism.

**Finding:** His direct, documented Ten-of-Swords interpretation belongs to his tarot teaching with Costa. A particular film shot copying the Ten was not traced in the consulted material.

### Paintings and buildings

No securely documented pre-1909 painting or building has been established here as a quotation of the Waite-Smith Ten; that would be chronologically impossible unless discussing later art.

Modern murals, tattoos, album art and installations frequently quote Smith’s prone body and ten blades. Without artist statements, these are iconographic reuses rather than evidence for the card’s historical origin.

## Controversies and disputes

### Egyptian origin

Court de Gébelin announced in 1781 that tarot was an Egyptian book surviving the destruction of ancient libraries. His opening explicitly declares: “Ce Livre Egyptien … est en un mot le JEU DES TAROTS”—“This Egyptian Book … is, in a word, the game of Tarot.”

**Legend:** He supplied no Egyptian object, inscription, papyrus or transmission chain. He wrote before Champollion’s decipherment of hieroglyphs.

**Historical correction:** surviving fifteenth-century cards, Italian court records, Christian-European trump subjects and the genealogy of European playing cards locate tarot in northern Italy. Dummett, Decker, Depaulis, Farley and Place differ in emphasis but agree that Gébelin’s Egyptian antiquity is unsupported.

For the Ten specifically, the early Italian examples are decorated weapons. The Egyptian “Book of Thoth” identity appears more than three centuries later.

### Romani or “Gypsy” origin

Gébelin referred to supposed Egyptian bands “mal nommés Bohêmiens.” Later writers fused “Egyptian,” “Gypsy,” wandering fortune-teller and tarot into a single story. Papus’s title *Le Tarot des Bohémiens* helped spread it.

**Legend:** Romani people did not invent the documented Italian trionfi pack. Their deeper origins are South Asian, not pharaonic Egyptian.

**Nuance:** Romani practitioners have real and diverse cartomantic traditions. Rejecting an origin myth does not deny later Romani participation in fortune-telling or cultural ownership of their own practices.

### Cathar claim

Modern authors sometimes identify tarot’s dualities, female religious figures or southern French survival with Cathar teaching.

**Disputed/unsupported:** no Cathar manuscript mentions tarot; the Cathar movement substantially predates the first trionfi records; the earliest cards are Milanese and Ferrarese, not an underground Languedoc scripture. No Ten-of-Swords Cathar meaning has been traced.

### Templar claim

A related legend treats the pack as coded Templar doctrine surviving suppression after 1307.

**Legend:** there is no fourteenth-century Templar tarot, textual chain or Ten of Swords bearing a demonstrable Templar cipher. The gap between Templar suppression and documented tarot is evidentially fatal to claims of direct transmission unless new evidence appears.

### Ancient Kabbalistic origin

**Esoteric tradition:** occultists treat the twenty-two trumps as the Hebrew alphabet and the four suits as the four worlds/divine name.

**Historical finding:** the first published traceable tarot-alphabet association occurs in the eighteenth century, not the fifteenth. The Ten’s Malkuth-of-Yetzirah identity is later still, in the Golden Dawn.

This does not adjudicate spiritual usefulness; it dates the attribution.

### “Sola Busca source” misstatement

The claim contains three separable propositions:

1. Sola Busca preceded Waite-Smith as a fully scenic-pip tarot — **documented**.
2. Smith probably used Sola Busca photographs — **strong reconstruction**, supported by close parallels.
3. Sola Busca Ten directly supplied Smith’s Ten — **misleading**. Its burdened pose is much closer to Smith’s Ten of Wands.

A broad statement became card-specifically inaccurate through repetition.

### “Corpse,” murder and betrayal

Smith’s figure appears dead, but Waite explicitly warns against treating the card chiefly as violent death.

- “Corpse” is a natural visual inference.
- “Murder” is not Waite’s specified meaning.
- “Backstabbing/betrayal” is a powerful modern inference from the blades entering the back.
- Waite’s listed upright nouns are pain, affliction, tears, sadness and desolation.
- His reversed meanings are temporary advantage and authority.

Modern keywords often overwrite Waite’s caution.

### Sunrise and recovery

The yellow horizon is visible in Smith’s card.

- **Documented image:** darkness is giving way to yellow light.
- **Plausible interpretation:** the scene contains a temporal contrast.
- **Modern tradition:** “The worst is over; now recovery must begin.”
- **Caution:** Waite does not make that the upright meaning. Temporary advantage belongs to his reversal.

### Marseille “scimitars”

The curved bands are commonly called scimitars.

**Iconographic caution:** the pattern evolved through the need to interlace repeated sword signs within a narrow card. Some terminals resemble hilts on both ends; woodblock copying progressively abstracted weapon anatomy. Treating every curve as evidence of Islamic scimitars or crusading contact exceeds the evidence.

### Orientation and “misprints”

Historical pip cards may appear inverted because:

- even-numbered symmetrical designs lack an obvious top;
- cards were trimmed and mounted;
- woodblocks were copied;
- registration and stencil colors shifted;
- surviving museum sets were reordered;
- modern restorers had to reconstruct missing cards.

A reversed scan or differently colored blade can become a supposed symbolic tradition. Noblet reconstructions are the clearest warning: the original VI–X Swords are missing.

### Camoin–Jodorowsky restoration claim

Their deck is an important modern synthesis. Its claim to reconstruct the original tarot’s exact code is disputed because no unique complete fifteenth-century Marseille prototype survives. “Restoration” necessarily includes selection and invention.

### Thoth publication date

The artwork was painted between 1938 and 1943; *The Book of Thoth* appeared in 1944; limited or partial presentations preceded the complete commercial deck, generally dated 1969.

Confusing painting, book and mass-publication dates produces several apparently conflicting dates that are all attached to different events.

### Malkuth colors and deck reconstructions

Golden Dawn color instructions exist in layered manuscripts and later edited publications. Wang and the Ciceros do not look identical.

**Finding:** a reconstruction can be faithful to a textual system without reproducing a single lost “original card.” Claims that one commercial reconstruction is exactly what Mathers saw are too strong.

### Case’s planetary ruler

The Case attribution of Saturn/Uranus is not a typo for the Golden Dawn’s Sun. It reflects Case’s revision of decan rulership. Modern tables that silently combine “BOTA” with “Sun in Gemini” erase this difference.

## Open questions

1. **Which surviving fifteenth-century Ten is absolutely earliest?**  
   Museum datings overlap and are frequently approximate. The Morgan card is securely early, but a finer ordering among Cary-Yale, Brambilla and Visconti-Sforza components depends on unresolved chronology.

2. **Who designed and engraved the Sola Busca?**  
   Brera scholarship advances Nicola di Maestro Antonio for the painted realization; older scholarship proposed Mattia Serrati da Cosandola and other hands. Designer, engraver, iconographic adviser and illuminator may not have been one person.

3. **What exactly is the Sola Busca figure doing?**  
   Carrying, depositing and being oppressed by the swords remain possible descriptions. No contemporary explanatory text survives.

4. **Did Waite specify Smith’s individual pip scenes?**  
   Their surviving statements do not provide a complete card-by-card division of responsibility. Waite supplied the program and meanings; Smith executed the designs, but exact authorship of the prone-body conception is not documented.

5. **What immediate visual source supplied Smith’s Ten?**  
   Etteilla and Mathers explain the emotional meaning; Sola Busca explains scenic precedent but not the exact corpse. A specific painting, theatrical photograph or martyrdom print has not been conclusively identified.

6. **What did Etteilla’s earliest issued card look like?**  
   Later Grand Etteilla Types I–III preserve his system, but printing chronology and plate revisions complicate claims that a nineteenth-century image reproduces every feature of 1788–89.

7. **Was the Golden Dawn title initially “Lord of Ruin” in every manuscript witness?**  
   Published order papers strongly establish it, but manuscript variants and editorial transmission deserve folio-by-folio comparison.

8. **Can the Noblet Ten be reconstructed?**  
   Only conjecturally from his lower Swords and related Type-I packs. No modern completion should be mistaken for the lost original.

9. **Are alchemical Sola Busca readings intentional?**  
   The deck’s workshop imagery makes alchemical context plausible, but the absence of an explanatory programme prevents certainty about individual pips.

10. **What is the earliest printed occurrence of the modern phrase “rock bottom”?**  
    It is absent from Etteilla, Mathers and Waite in the consulted editions. Pollack and later therapeutic tarot helped normalize the concept, but its first exact card-specific appearance remains untraced.

11. **Is the RWS figure’s hand deliberately blessing?**  
    The gesture is visible; its theological meaning is undocumented.

12. **Do Calvino, Yeats, Eliot, Bergman or a named historical building quote this card specifically?**  
    Tarot connections exist for some, but a defensible Ten-of-Swords-specific identification was not established by the consulted sources. Absence of a traced citation should be preferred to associative certainty.
