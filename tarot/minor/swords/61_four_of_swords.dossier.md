# Four of Swords: Research Dossier

## Method and evidentiary labels

This dossier distinguishes:

- **[Documented fact]** Surviving object, archival record, contemporary printed text, or explicit statement in a cited primary source.
- **[Scholarly reconstruction]** Conclusion inferred by modern historians from objects, records, rules, or textual transmission.
- **[Tradition]** Repeated practice or interpretation whose origin may not be demonstrable.
- **[Esoteric attribution]** A correspondence deliberately assigned within an occult system.
- **[Disputed]** A claim for which substantial competing identifications or chronologies exist.
- **[Legend]** A historical claim unsupported or contradicted by the surviving record.
- **[Modern invention]** A recent interpretive or artistic development, rather than an inherited Renaissance feature.
- **[Not traced]** No reliable card-specific evidence was located in the sources consulted.

“Four of Swords” is a pip card, not a trump. Consequently, many subjects requested in the general template—regional trump orders, the Papess/Juno substitutions, the Fool’s numbering, and the Tzaddi controversy—belong to the history of the deck around the card but do not alter this card’s identity or playing rank.

| Etteilla card words | Upright **Solitude**; reversed **Économie** |

The Italian government catalogue dates the Brambilla card to circa 1442–1451, attributes its execution to Bonifacio Bembo, and records tempera on card, 178 × 89 mm, in the Pinacoteca di Brera. This is the earliest securely catalogued surviving Four of Swords located in this research—not the 1491 Sola Busca card. [Italian General Catalogue](https://catalogo.beniculturali.it/detail/HistoricOrArtisticProperty/0300097697-16)

The Morgan describes a normal complete Renaissance pack as 21 trumps, the Fool, and 56 suit cards—four suits of ten numerals and four courts—and emphasizes that these cards were originally made for play, not occult divination. [Morgan Library collection record](https://www.themorgan.org/manuscript/158525)

### Names and suit-language

**[Documented fact]** *Spade* is the ordinary Italian plural of *spada*, sword. Spanish retains *espadas*. French tarot uses *épées*. German Italian-suited packs use *Schwerter*.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** English “spades” probably derives ultimately from Italian/Spanish *spada/espada*, even though the modern French-suited black emblem no longer depicts a literal sword. [Spades etymology overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spades_%28suit%29)

**[Tradition]** Etteilla and Mathers explicitly treated Swords as corresponding to French-suited Piques/English Spades. Etteilla’s later handbook calls No. 60 “*Quatre d’Épée ou de Pique*”—“Four of Swords or Spades.” [Grand Etteilla handbook, p. 129](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/2/2f/Le_grand_Etteilla%2C_ou%2C_l%27art_de_tirer_les_cartes_..._Le_tout_recueilli_..._%28IA_b29321220%29.pdf)

No traditional tarot pack gives the Four of Swords a trump number. “IV,” “IIII,” or “4” identifies its rank only. The card is likewise unaffected by whether the Fool is unnumbered, numbered 0, or placed after XXI.

# Iconography deck by deck

## Fifteenth-century Milanese cards

### Brera–Brambilla, Milan, circa 1442–1451

**[Documented fact]** This deck contains a surviving *Quattro di Spade*. The official record identifies the object, date range, artist attribution, medium, dimensions, and Brera location. [Catalogue record](https://catalogo.beniculturali.it/detail/HistoricOrArtisticProperty/0300097697-16)

The card is a luxury hand-painted pip: four sword emblems arranged ornamentally rather than a narrative scene. The weapons, metallic decoration, and costly tempera/gilding belong to the courtly Milanese vocabulary of the deck.

**What it does not show:** no tomb, knight, church, stained glass, astrological glyph, Hebrew letter, or divinatory caption.

**Meaning at the time:** **[Not traced]** No fifteenth-century text gives this individual pip a symbolic meaning. Its documented function is as a suited playing card. Any reading of the four swords as “mental rest,” “truce,” or Chesed is retrospective.

### Visconti di Modrone / Cary–Yale, Milan, circa 1440s–1460s

**[Documented fact]** A Four of Swords survives in the Beinecke/Cary–Yale group; a public-domain reproduction is catalogued as “Cary-Yale Tarot deck – Four of Spades.” [Wikimedia object page](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File%3ACary-Yale_Tarot_deck_-_Four_of_Spades.jpg)

Its design is non-scenic and emblematic: four swords on a richly ornamented field. The Cary–Yale pack is exceptional for its extra male and female court figures, but that does not alter the rank of this pip.

**[Disputed dating]** The Cary–Yale group has been dated at several points between the early 1440s and 1460s. It may therefore be nearly contemporary with Brambilla, but the official Brambilla catalogue supplies the more explicit early range.

### Pierpont Morgan–Bergamo Visconti-Sforza, Milan, after about 1450

**[Documented fact]** A Four of Swords survives in the Bergamo portion, while the Morgan’s own 35-card portion contains only the Ace and Ten among its Swords pips. The complete dispersed group preserves 74 of an original 78 cards. [Morgan overview](https://www.themorgan.org/manuscript/158525), [Morgan card list](https://www.themorgan.org/collection/tarot-cards)

The surviving Four is an ornamental pip: four curved or interlaced blades on a pale vegetal ground. It is not a figural narrative. Contemporary suit cards were composed by multiplying the emblem, with vines, flowers, ribbons, dynastic devices, and patterned backgrounds filling the space.

**[Disputed attribution]** The original cards have been attributed to Bonifacio Bembo, Francesco Zavattari, or a Bembo-associated workshop. The Morgan notes that replacement/additional cards have separately been assigned to Antonio Cicognara, Benedetto Bembo, or Franco dei Russi. These attribution disputes do not imply multiple Four-of-Swords designs.

**[Modern invention]** Facsimile decks often reconstruct missing cards or consolidate the three dispersed collections. Such editions should not be mistaken for intact fifteenth-century packs.

## Ferrarese and other late-fifteenth-century fragments

### Este / Charles VI / Catania-type cards

The surviving luxury Ferrarese or north-Italian groups generally preserve trumps and courts rather than a securely identified Four of Swords.

**[Not traced]** No surviving Four of Swords from the so-called Charles VI/Este group was located in the consulted museum and scholarly records. Modern “Este” decks may supply invented minor cards based on contemporary frescoes; those are reconstruction products, not surviving Este pips. One modern edition openly says that its minors use scenes adapted from a period fresco. [Tarot Heritage deck survey](https://tarot-heritage.com/cartomancy/choose-a-deck/)

### Rosenwald and Budapest sheets

These printed sheets are critical evidence for inexpensive early tarot production.

**[Documented fact / scholarly reconstruction]** Rosenwald-type sheets preserve early woodcut suit and trump designs, showing that tarot was not confined to unique gilded court objects.

**[Not traced]** A sufficiently clear, institutionally catalogued individual Four of Swords was not recovered from the online material consulted. Accordingly, no card-specific scene or meaning is asserted here.

### Mantegna “Tarocchi”

**[Documented fact]** The so-called Mantegna Tarocchi are fifty didactic engravings arranged in hierarchical series; they are not a normal tarot pack and contain neither four suits nor a Four of Swords.

**Relevance:** contextual only. Their Renaissance cosmological ladders later encouraged comparison with tarot, but they are not an ancestor-object for this pip.

## Sola Busca, probably Ferrara/Venice, engraving dated around 1491

**[Documented fact]** The Sola Busca is the earliest complete surviving 78-card tarot and the earliest complete surviving pack in which every numeral card is converted into an individual illustrated composition. It was engraved in the late fifteenth century and later hand-coloured; the precise place, artist, commissioner, and colouring date remain debated. The complete coloured set is now at the Pinacoteca di Brera. [Brera exhibition publication](https://pinacotecabrera.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/11/Il-Segreto-dei-tarocchi-eng.pdf), [British Museum Sola Busca record](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/P_1845-0825-485), [Public Domain Review](https://publicdomainreview.org/collection/sola-busca)

Its Four of Swords is a fully illustrated pip rather than a Marseille-style emblem array. However:

**Finding on the item-specific lead:** **[Disputed / probably false as stated]** The proposition that “the Sola Busca pip (1491)” was the direct source for Pamela Colman Smith’s *Four of Swords* scene could not be verified. The well-documented visual borrowings concern other cards—most conspicuously the Sola Busca Three of Swords’ pierced heart and imagery adapted across several Waite-Smith pips. The Four of Swords’ praying tomb effigy has no close Sola Busca Four counterpart.

Photographs of the Sola Busca were acquired by the British Museum in 1908, shortly before Smith drew her deck, making her access plausible. Similarity and opportunity establish probable influence at deck level, but no surviving statement by Smith identifies the source of this particular card. The stronger Four-specific visual source is the medieval funerary effigy tradition.

**[Disputed esoteric interpretation]** Sofia Di Vincenzo and later Peter Mark Adams have interpreted the deck alchemically or as an initiatory/grimoire-like program. The Brera publication confirms that alchemical readings form part of the modern interpretive history, but no fifteenth-century guide survives to prove an intended divinatory meaning for its Four.

## Tarot de Marseille lineage

### General Marseille pip form

The Four of Swords typically consists of four highly stylized curved blades. Their arcs interlace or enclose a central floral spray, bud, or blossom, producing a pointed oval or mandorla-like enclosure. Small leaves and blossoms occupy the central and marginal gaps.

**[Documented iconography]** The “scimitars” are conventionalized swords, although repeated copying made both ends resemble hilts or handles in some impressions.

**[Disputed reconstruction]** Their exact graphic ancestry—Italian interlaced blades, copying errors, decorative ribbons, or combinations of these—remains unsettled. Interpretations of the central oval as a vulva, mandorla, aura, protected mind, or sealed psychic space are later readings, not documented cardmakers’ explanations.

### Jean Noblet, Paris, circa 1650

Noblet is the earliest substantially surviving deck of the recognized Marseille design family. Its Four uses the interlaced curved-sword lattice with vegetal ornament rather than a human scene.

**[Documented fact]** “Tarot de Marseille” was not Noblet’s own historical product name. The collective label is later; historians commonly credit Romain Merlin with using it in 1856, with Papus, Wirth, and especially Paul Marteau helping to popularize it. [Historical overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tarot_of_Marseilles)

### Jean Dodal, Lyon, circa 1701–1715

Dodal retains the Type-I vocabulary: four arched blades, bilateral organization, and central floral growth. Differences lie in carving, colour, terminals, and foliage—not in a new narrative meaning.

### Pierre Madenié, Dijon, 1709, and Type II

Madenié supplies the earliest dated surviving Type-II pattern. Type II regularizes details subsequently familiar through Marseille production, including the organisation and colouring of suit ornaments.

### Nicolas Conver, Marseille, matrices dated 1760

Conver’s Four became especially influential through nineteenth- and twentieth-century reprints. Four curved swords form an enclosure around a flowering branch; red, blue, yellow, green, and unprinted ground articulate the pattern.

**[Documented fact]** Surviving packs bearing Conver’s name are not all impressions made in 1760. A BnF example catalogued to 1809–1833 lacks Swords III–X, so many modern “1760 Conver” decks are restorations or composites. [BnF-record discussion](https://www.marselia.app/blog/history-of-the-marseille-tarot)

### Lequart/Grimaud and Paul Marteau, circa 1890–1930

**[Documented fact]** Lequart/Grimaud editions derived their design from the Conver stream. Paul Marteau’s *Ancien Tarot de Marseille* regularized the palette and made this version the dominant twentieth-century French reading deck. [Tarot History](https://tarot-history.com/history-pages-history-page-2/)

**[Modern esoteric attribution]** Marteau read the four swords as constructive forces enclosing a complete branch: four orders and consolidates energy so development can occur securely. That interpretation belongs to his 1949 *Le Tarot de Marseille*, not to the seventeenth-century cardmakers.

### Camoin–Jodorowsky, 1997

This is a modern composite “restoration,” created by Philippe Camoin and Alejandro Jodorowsky by comparing Conver, Dodal, Tourcaty, Payen, and other packs. [Jodorowsky and Costa, *The Way of Tarot*](https://irp-cdn.multiscreensite.com/24669b51/files/uploaded/The_Way_of_Tarot%20%281%29.pdf)

The Four retains four curved swords and the central plant. Colour and small ornamental details were reconstructed or selected across multiple exemplars.

**[Disputed claim]** The authors present the work as restoring an original sacred Tarot. Historians object that no single recoverable prototype contains all the selected details; the result is therefore better classified as a learned modern synthesis.

Jodorowsky’s reading of the enclosed plant as concentrated mental energy is a modern numerological/psychological reading, not evidence of the original makers’ intention.

## Besançon pattern

Besançon decks primarily alter objectionable trumps—most famously replacing the Papess and Pope with Juno and Jupiter. Their suit pips, including the Four of Swords, broadly retain the Marseille emblem structure.

Thus:

- Papess → Juno and Pope → Jupiter are **regional trump substitutions**, chiefly associated with eighteenth-century eastern France, Switzerland, and German-speaking production.
- They do not rename or renumber the Four of Swords.
- “Maison Dieu” and the unnumbered Fool likewise concern trumps, not this card.

## Minchiate

Minchiate expanded the trump series to 40 and used a 97-card pack, while retaining four ordinary suits.

The Four of Swords is still a numeral suit card. It does not acquire a trump identity or occult title. In long suits such as Swords, higher numerals outrank lower numerals.

**[Not traced]** No unique, historically standard Minchiate Four-of-Swords symbolic scene or divinatory definition was found. Surviving examples are ornamental Italian pips.

## Bolognese tarot / tarocchino

Tarocchino packs were shortened by suppressing low numeral cards. In the standard 62-card Bolognese pack, Swords 2–5 are omitted; consequently, there is no Four of Swords in ordinary later Bolognese play. [Ottocento rules](https://gambiter.com/cards/tarot/ottocen.html)

Early printed sheets show fuller numeral sequences, so a Four existed before or alongside abbreviation. This is a useful warning: “the Four of Swords” is not present in every regional tarot pack.

## Sicilian tarot

The traditional 63-card Sicilian pack removes the Four from Swords, Cups, and Batons. Only the Four of Coins remains. Thus there is no playable Four of Swords in the standard Mineo/Sicilian game. [Sicilian Tarocchi rules](https://www.pagat.com/tarot/sicilian.html)

## Grand Etteilla, Paris, 1780s–1790s

The commonly transmitted Grand Etteilla orders the Four of Swords as **No. 60**, labelled:

> “Solitude. — Economie. Quatre d’Épée ou de Pique.”

The card shows four swords arranged as suit signs, with upright and reversed captions built into the card. Later Type I, Type II, and Grand Etteilla publishers vary colours and inscriptions. [Grand Etteilla handbook, p. 129](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/2/2f/Le_grand_Etteilla%2C_ou%2C_l%27art_de_tirer_les_cartes_..._Le_tout_recueilli_..._%28IA_b29321220%29.pdf), [1791 deck scan](https://etteilla.org/en/deck/10/original-etteilla-tarot-le-jeu-de-toth)

**What changed:** the pip ceased to be merely an uncaptioned game emblem. Orientation, sequence number, and printed meaning became integral to its design.

## Oswald Wirth

### 1889 deck

Wirth’s original 1889 pack, designed under Stanislas de Guaita’s influence, comprised the 22 trumps. It therefore has no Four of Swords.

### Later writings

Wirth discussed tarot numerology and the four suits, but the familiar “Wirth deck” is fundamentally a trump deck. Modern 78-card Wirth-labelled products contain publishers’ supplementary minors and should not be treated as Wirth’s 1889 drawings.

## Golden Dawn drafts and reconstructions

The Golden Dawn did not leave one mass-produced canonical deck. Its initiates were instructed to construct or colour personal packs from written descriptions and occult correspondences.

**Book T Four of Swords:**

- Title: **Lord of Rest from Strife**
- Decan: **Jupiter in Libra**
- Span: **20°–30° Libra**
- Number: 4 = Chesed
- Suit: Swords = Air, Vav, Yetzirah
- Decan angels in the Golden Dawn scheme: traditionally associated with the final ten degrees of Libra

Book T’s meaning includes rest after conflict, recovery, relief from anxiety, and change for the better, but also qualified or temporary success.

Robert Wang’s *Golden Dawn Tarot* and Chic and Sandra Tabatha Cicero’s reconstructions visually systematize these instructions. They usually show four swords in formal symmetry, elemental colours, astrological emblems, and Golden Dawn symbolic scenery rather than Smith’s naturalistic tomb.

**[Reconstruction]** Wang and the Ciceros are twentieth-century interpretations of order documents; neither is a photographic reproduction of a unique nineteenth-century official deck.

## Waite–Smith, London, 1909

Pamela Colman Smith depicts a golden or stone-coloured knightly effigy lying full-length on a tomb inside a chapel. His hands meet in prayer. Three swords hang point-down on the wall above him; a fourth lies horizontally along the tomb. A stained-glass window at left shows two figures, commonly read as a supplicant receiving blessing or aid.

Waite’s 1910 description is concise:

> “The effigy of a knight in the attitude of prayer, at full length upon his tomb.”

His meanings explain why Smith selected funerary repose:

> “Vigilance, retreat, solitude, hermit’s repose, exile, tomb and coffin.”

Reversed:

> “Wise administration, circumspection, economy, avarice, precaution, testament.”

[A. E. Waite, *The Pictorial Key to the Tarot*, Four of Swords](https://sacred-texts.com/tarot/pkt/index.htm?chaptersPage=3)

### Sources of Smith’s composition

- **[Documented visual tradition]** Recumbent knightly tomb effigies with hands in prayer are common in medieval churches.
- **[Documented textual inheritance]** Waite’s keywords descend substantially from Etteilla through Mathers.
- **[Scholarly reconstruction]** Golden Dawn “Rest from Strife” helps explain the state of suspension rather than death.
- **[Plausible but unproven]** Smith probably saw the British Museum’s Sola Busca photographs, acquired in 1908.
- **[Not verified]** The Sola Busca Four itself is not demonstrated as the source of this tomb composition.
- **[Modern interpretation]** Claims that the lone sword signifies a single future purpose, that the three wall swords equal past battles, or that the window shows a specific biblical episode are later readings. Waite does not say these things.

The scene transformed Etteilla’s abstract “solitude” into a memorable Christian-funerary tableau. It is an effigy, not unambiguously a live knight sleeping, a corpse, or a wounded soldier.

## Paul Foster Case and BOTA

Paul Foster Case retained Golden Dawn Qabalah but objected to what he considered Waite’s “blinds.” The BOTA deck, executed with Jessie Burns Parke, uses scene-less geometric suit pips rather than copying Smith’s illustrated stories. [BOTA deck history](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/B.O.T.A._tarot_deck)

Thus the BOTA Four of Swords is again an arrangement of four sword emblems. The occult meaning resides in number, suit, colour, and doctrine:

- Four/Chesed: order, beneficence, consolidation
- Swords/Vav/Air: formative or intellectual force
- Jupiter in Libra: expansive beneficence operating through balance
- Result: rest, settlement, or ordered cessation following conflict

**[Not traced]** A securely accessible primary Case text giving a distinct upright/reversed word-list for this card was not found. Claims that Case authored a novel independent Four-of-Swords meaning should therefore be treated cautiously.

## Crowley–Harris Thoth Tarot, painted 1938–1943; first full publication 1969

Frieda Harris’s Four of Swords is titled **TRUCE**. Four swords create a tense geometrical cross or pinwheel directed into a central rose. Astrological glyphs identify Jupiter and Libra. Greens and blues suggest Libra; violet/purple invokes Jupiter. The image is balanced but under strain.

Crowley explains:

> “The symbol represents the great Sword of the Magician, point uppermost; it cuts the junction of two short curved swords.”

He adds that Chesed refers to Jupiter ruling this decan of Libra. The condition is not lasting peace but stabilization.

[Crowley, *The Book of Thoth*, 1944 text](https://www.rahoorkhuit.net/library/crowley/equinox/volume3/vol_3_no_05.pdf)

**What changed:** Smith’s religious, human, sepulchral stillness became an abstract diagram of forces. “Rest” became “Truce”—a negotiated equilibrium containing unresolved antagonism.

**[Documented fact]** Harris painted the deck between 1938 and 1943; Crowley’s book appeared in 1944, while the complete commercial deck appeared posthumously in 1969.

## Notable modern redesign families

Modern Four-of-Swords designs commonly follow one of four lineages:

1. **RWS literalists:** a reclining person, bed, crypt, couch, or hospital setting.
2. **RWS psychological updates:** meditation, burnout recovery, therapy, digital disconnection, or sleep.
3. **Thoth-derived abstraction:** crossed blades, a central flower, and precarious geometric balance.
4. **Marseille revival:** four curved swords enclosing a living plant, without a scene.

Inclusive decks change the effigy’s gender, ethnicity, age, or bodily condition. Queer and feminist decks may reconceive the enclosed or sleeping body as protected withdrawal rather than defeat. Decks such as the Numinous Tarot rename the whole suit—there, Swords become Bells—demonstrating that “Four of Swords” is no longer universal even within modern 78-card structures. [Numinous Tarot review](https://littleredtarot.com/numinous-tarot-review/)

These are **modern inventions**, often valuable artistically, but they should not be projected backward onto Renaissance pips.

# The game

## Rank

In the traditional “long” suits, Swords and Batons rank:

**King > Queen > Knight > Jack > 10 > 9 > 8 > 7 > 6 > 5 > 4 > 3 > 2 > Ace**

Consequently, the Four is:

- above the Three, Two, and Ace;
- below the Five and every higher numeral;
- far below every court;
- unable to beat a trump.

In reduced packs that remove Ace–Three, the Four may be the lowest Sword. In the seventeenth-century 66-card French game described by the Abbé de Marolles, Swords retained 10 down to 4, making the Four the lowest Sword. [Marolles rules](https://www.pagat.com/tarot/marolles.html)

In complete Piedmontese-style play it remains a low numeral in a 14-card long suit. [Scarto rules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scarto)

## Trick-taking function

**[Documented tradition]**

- Players must generally follow the led suit.
- If no trump is played, the highest card of the led suit takes the trick.
- If one or more trumps are played, the highest applicable trump takes it.
- The Four of Swords has no special power, declaration, or divinatory action.
- It is ordinarily a low-value or blank counting card.

## Point value

In standard tarot counting systems, numeral cards such as the Four usually have no independent honour value. They contribute only through grouped card-counting or as part of a captured trick. Kings, high trumps, the Fool, and sometimes the lowest trump carry the large card points.

**[Important distinction]** “Four = Chesed” is an occult value, not a game score.

## Reduced regional packs

- **Bolognese tarocchino:** removes Swords 2–5; Four absent.
- **Sicilian tarot:** removes the Four from Swords; Four absent.
- **French 66-card Marolles pack:** retains Four and removes lower Swords; Four becomes lowest.
- **Central-European Tarock:** most packs use French suits and heavily shortened black suits. A literal Four of Spades is commonly absent; the surviving black numerals are typically 10–7.
- **Minchiate:** Four remains a low long-suit card in the full 97-card form.

## Trump-order disputes and why they do not change this card

Dummett classified early trump orders as:

- Type A / Southern: Bologna, Florence, later Rome and Sicily
- Type B / Eastern: Ferrara and probably Venice
- Type C / Western: Milan and France

The orders differ chiefly in the relative placement of virtues, papal/imperial figures, and celestial trumps. Justice and Fortitude/Strength shift position. The Fool remains outside the ordinary numbered hierarchy and is normally excused rather than functioning as an ordinary pip.

These disputes do not affect the Four’s place within Swords. They matter only because early decks containing the card belonged to different regional games. [Dummett order analysis](https://dummettsmondo.blogspot.com/2015/07/chapters-6-and-7-game-and-its-trump.html)

# Chronology of attestation

## Before tarot

### Fourteenth century

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** European suit cards were probably adapted from Islamic/Mamluk cards. Mamluk packs already used a sword suit, but no surviving object has been shown to be a tarot Four of Swords: tarot’s added trump sequence was a later Italian innovation.

## Circa 1420: Marziano da Tortona

Marziano’s text describes a special pack for Filippo Maria Visconti, with sixteen gods and four suits represented by birds.

**Finding:** it contains no Four of Swords. It is a precursor or parallel experiment in an added hierarchy, not a standard 78-card tarot pack.

## 1442: Ferrara accounts

**[Documented fact]** Este court records use *carte da trionfi* for packs acquired or produced at Ferrara. This is among the earliest secure documentary evidence for the tarot game family.

**Finding:** the entry does not name the Four of Swords individually. It attests packs whose normal suit structure likely included it.

## Circa 1442–1451: Brambilla Four of Swords

This is the earliest securely catalogued surviving physical card found in this investigation. [Official catalogue](https://catalogo.beniculturali.it/detail/HistoricOrArtisticProperty/0300097697-16)

## Circa 1450 onward: Visconti-Sforza and Cary–Yale examples

Surviving Milanese Fours establish that this rank was an ordinary part of luxury tarot packs.

## Circa 1470–1480: *Sermo de ludo cum aliis*

The anonymous sermon condemns games and lists the tarot trumps in order.

**Finding:** because it lists trumps, it does not name the Four of Swords. Its relevance is proof of an established tarot pack and the moral suspicion surrounding games—not a meaning for this pip.

## 1491: Sola Busca

The complete illustrated Sola Busca supplies the earliest surviving full pictorial minor-arcana program.

**Finding:** its existence proves that illustrated pips predate Waite-Smith by centuries. It does not prove a continuous illustrated-pip tradition leading to Smith.

## Around 1500–1520s: appropriated tarot poems

Boiardo’s tarot verses and the *tarocchi appropriati* genre attach literary qualities or courtly identities to cards. Folengo’s *Chaos del Triperuno* describes participants randomly dividing trumps and asking for sonnets about their lots.

**Finding:** the famous Folengo passage says *carte lusorie de trionfi* and concerns trumps. No securely traced Four-of-Swords definition emerges from it. [Folengo passage and discussion](https://forum.tarothistory.com/viewtopic.php?p=10921)

## 1637 rule description

A French rule cited by Andrea Vitali describes four suits—swords, batons, cups, and coins—each with fourteen cards: four honours and numerals from ten to ace. This expressly confirms the normal structural place of the Four. [Le Tarot association](https://www.letarot.it/Tarocchi-e-Neopitagorismo_pag_pg83_ita.aspx?lng=ITA)

## Seventeenth–eighteenth centuries: Marseille print tradition

The card is repeatedly printed as four curved swords with floral ornament. No divinatory captions appear.

## 1781: Court de Gébelin and Comte de Mellet

Gébelin declares tarot an Egyptian book; Mellet connects the 22 trumps with Hebrew letters and presents a divinatory method.

**Finding:** neither establishes the later Jupiter-in-Libra attribution to the Four of Swords. Their Hebrew-letter proposals concern the trumps. [Gébelin primary text](https://www.tarock.info/gebelin.htm)

## 1783–1785: Etteilla’s tarot manuals

Etteilla creates the first sustained printed system of tarot cartomancy and astrology. The Four receives “Solitude” and reversed “Economy.”

## 1789–1791: Grand Etteilla deck

A specifically divinatory deck prints meanings and reorganizes the sequence. The Four of Swords/Spades appears as No. 60 in the familiar transmission.

## 1854–1856: Éliphas Lévi, *Dogme et Rituel*

Lévi maps:

- suit signs to the letters of Tetragrammaton;
- numbered pips to Sephiroth;
- Swords to Vav;
- fours to Chesed.

The English translation states:

> “The Sword is the conjunction of both … represented … by Vau.”

and for the fours:

> “Four benefactions from his mercy come.”

[Éliphas Lévi, *Dogme et Rituel*, English translation, Doctrine, chapter X, pp. 49–50 of the displayed edition](https://www.magicgatebg.com/Books/Dogma%20et%20Rituel%20de%20la%20Haute%20Magie%20Part%20I%20by%20Eliphas%20Levi.pdf)

Lévi does not yet give the card Jupiter in Libra or “Rest from Strife.”

## 1860s–1870s: Paul Christian

Paul Christian spreads the language of “arcana” and constructs an Egyptian initiation narrative, falsely lending it ancient authority.

**Finding:** no distinctive, securely traced Four-of-Swords doctrine was found. His influence is primarily on the trump/initiatory superstructure.

## 1888: Mathers

Mathers prints a Four-of-Swords list largely continuous with Etteilla:

> “Solitude, Retreat, Abandonment, Solitary, Hermit”

Reversed:

> “Economy, Precaution, Regulation of Expenditure.”

[Mathers, *The Tarot*, 1888](https://hermetics.net/media-library/tarot/samuel-liddell-macgregor-mathers-the-tarot/)

## 1888–1912: Golden Dawn Book T

The order assigns the title “Lord of Rest from Strife” and Jupiter in Libra. The teachings circulated in manuscript before Crowley published much of the material in *The Equinox* in 1912.

## 1889: Papus and Wirth

Papus’s *Le Tarot des Bohémiens* spreads Lévi-derived Kabbalistic tarot and the Romani title-myth. Wirth publishes his 22-trump deck.

**Finding:** neither 1889 deck supplies a newly illustrated Four of Swords. Papus’s later compiled divinatory tables sometimes give “enemy defeated,” but this is not as firmly traceable as Etteilla’s and Mathers’s lists.

## 1909–1911: Waite-Smith and *Pictorial Key*

Smith creates the now-dominant tomb-effigy scene. Waite publishes its description and Etteilla-derived meanings.

## 1910s–1930s: Case

Case systematizes Golden Dawn-derived Qabalah in America and later establishes BOTA; Jessie Burns Parke renders the BOTA cards. The BOTA pip returns to emblematic geometry.

## 1938–1944/1969: Thoth

Harris paints the card; Crowley calls it “Truce.” The book is published in 1944 and the complete deck in 1969.

## 1949: Paul Marteau

Marteau supplies an influential French numerological-symbolic reading of the Marseille Four as stabilizing construction and gradual realization.

## 1969–1973: Calvino

Calvino uses the Visconti-Sforza pack in the first part of *Il castello dei destini incrociati* and a Marseille/Grimaud pack in the second, making tarot a combinatorial narrative machine. [Work history](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Castle_of_Crossed_Destinies)

## Late twentieth century onward

Rachel Pollack, Mary K. Greer, Jodorowsky, Jungian readers, therapeutic tarot writers, and thousands of RWS-derived decks turn the card into an image of retreat, recuperation, meditation, suspended action, burnout, or strategic disengagement.

## Principal conflicts

### Swords and the elements

- Lévi: Swords correspond to **Vav**, the conjunction of masculine and feminine principles.
- Golden Dawn: Swords = **Air**.
- Some modern alternative systems: Swords = **Fire**, because weapons cut, burn metaphorically, and signify conflict.
- Etteilla’s precise elemental distribution differs across his texts and deck traditions; claims that his system was identical to the later Golden Dawn table are false.

Therefore “Swords are Air” is not a Renaissance fact. It is a dominant nineteenth-century Hermetic attribution.

### Vav

Lévi explicitly assigns Sword to Vav. Golden Dawn inherits the suit–Tetragrammaton structure:

- Wands: Yod
- Cups: Heh
- Swords: Vav
- Pentacles/Disks: final Heh

This Vav belongs to the suit, not uniquely to the Four.

### Chesed

Lévi explicitly groups “the four Fours” under Chesed. Golden Dawn elaborates this through four worlds:

- Four of Wands: Chesed in Atziluth
- Four of Cups: Chesed in Briah
- Four of Swords: Chesed in Yetzirah
- Four of Pentacles: Chesed in Assiah

The Four of Swords is therefore mercy, order, or expansive stabilization in the formative/airy world.

### Jupiter in Libra

**Verified:** Book T and Crowley agree on Jupiter in the third decan of Libra. “Truce” is Crowley’s new title, not a correction of the decan.

The rationale is esoteric:

- Jupiter corresponds to Chesed and expansion/beneficence.
- Libra corresponds to equilibrium and negotiated relationship.
- In Swords, their balance temporarily settles conflict.

This is a nineteenth-century occult construction using the decan rulers inherited from traditional astrological schemes, not a property printed on Renaissance cards.

### The Tzaddi/Heh controversy

Crowley’s instruction “Tzaddi is not the Star” led him to exchange the Hebrew-letter positions of the Emperor and Star while retaining their zodiacal Aries/Aquarius identities in a characteristic reworking.

It does not affect the Four of Swords, which is a decanic pip rather than a Hebrew-letter trump. Any claim that the Tzaddi exchange changes Jupiter in Libra here is mistaken.

### Justice/Strength order

Waite exchanges the conventional Marseille numbers of Justice and Strength so astrological letters and trump order align with Golden Dawn doctrine. Crowley uses his own numbering and letter solution.

Again, no effect on this pip’s rank or decan.

# Divinatory meanings by author

Brief quotations are given where accessible; longer entries are paraphrased.

## Etteilla, 1780s; later Grand Etteilla handbook

**Upright printed word:** “Solitude.”

Associated ideas in later lists include retreat, hermitage, abandonment, remoteness, and the tomb.

**Reversed printed word:** “Économie.”

Associated ideas include savings, prudent management, foresight, discretion, and precautions.

The later handbook predicts possible entry into a religious community and warns that reversed success depends on sensible administration. [*Le grand Etteilla*, No. 60, p. 129](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/2/2f/Le_grand_Etteilla%2C_ou%2C_l%27art_de_tirer_les_cartes_..._Le_tout_recueilli_..._%28IA_b29321220%29.pdf)

**Transmission:** These meanings are the clear ancestors of Mathers and Waite. Waite’s “wise administration” is especially close to the handbook’s *sage administration*.

## Mathers, 1888

**Upright:**

> “Solitude, Retreat, Abandonment, Solitary, Hermit.”

**Reversed:**

> “Economy, Precaution, Regulation of Expenditure.”

[Mathers, *The Tarot: Its Occult Significance…*, 1888](https://sacred-texts.com/tarot/mathers/mtar00.htm)

This is primarily an English compression of the Etteilla tradition, not yet the full Book T decan treatment.

## Golden Dawn Book T

**Title:** “Lord of Rest from Strife.”

The entry combines:

- rest after war;
- relaxation from anxiety;
- recovery following illness;
- release after sorrow;
- change soon to come;
- qualified success;
- temporary cessation rather than final resolution.

Book T’s card is neither simply positive nor negative: Jupiter tempers the conflict of Swords through Libra, but Swords remain unstable.

## Waite, 1910/1911

**Image:**

> “The effigy of a knight in the attitude of prayer, at full length upon his tomb.”

**Upright:**

> “Vigilance, retreat, solitude, hermit’s repose, exile, tomb and coffin.”

**Reversed:**

> “Wise administration, circumspection, economy, avarice, precaution, testament.”

[Waite, *The Pictorial Key to the Tarot*](https://sacred-texts.com/tarot/pkt/index.htm?chaptersPage=3)

Waite admits that the last ideas—tomb and coffin—suggested the design. The image is thus not a mysterious medieval survival; it is a deliberate twentieth-century visualization of inherited cartomantic vocabulary.

## Crowley, 1944

**Title:** “Truce.”

Crowley says the Four restores order in the suit but only weakly. The swords meet in a central rose, holding forces in equilibrium. Jupiter’s dignity through Chesed moderates Libra’s indecision, yet the peace is contractual and temporary.

**Practical reading:** respite, settlement, negotiated balance, mental order, relief, or convalescence; negatively, compromise without solution, stagnation, or peace maintained only by continuing pressure.

Crowley did not normally build his card discussions around Waite-style upright and reversed pairs. Reversal meanings attributed to him on modern websites are generally later extrapolations. [*The Book of Thoth*](https://www.rahoorkhuit.net/library/crowley/equinox/volume3/vol_3_no_05.pdf)

## Paul Foster Case

Case accepts the Golden Dawn framework of Chesed, Air, and Jupiter in Libra. His BOTA imagery suppresses Waite’s tomb and makes the card a formal symbolic diagram.

**[Not traced]** A reliable publicly accessible primary passage giving separate Case upright/reversed wording for the Four was not located. The safest attribution is “rest or ordered cessation after strife,” inherited from Golden Dawn doctrine. More elaborate Case quotes circulating without page references should not be treated as verified.

## Rachel Pollack

In *Seventy-Eight Degrees of Wisdom*, Pollack treats the card as withdrawal from activity, peace after struggle, and the need to stop before re-entering life. The chapel/tomb setting can indicate turning inward, meditation, or emotional and mental recovery.

The potential shadow is remaining too long in protected suspension: repose can become avoidance, isolation, or lifelessness.

**Reversed in Pollack-derived practice:** renewed activity or emergence, but possibly premature return before recovery is complete.

**Source limitation:** A reliable openly accessible page image for the relevant edition was not found; therefore no verbatim Pollack sentence is presented as verified. Consult Rachel Pollack, *Seventy-Eight Degrees of Wisdom*, Part II, Swords section.

## Mary K. Greer

Greer’s reversal method resists a single antonym. A reversed Four of Swords may express:

- blocked rest;
- excessive withdrawal;
- private or internal repose;
- rejection of passivity;
- recovery and return;
- restlessness or hypervigilance;
- reconsideration of retreat.

This follows the reversal taxonomy she developed in *The Complete Book of Tarot Reversals* (2002): reversed energy may be blocked, resisted, projected, delayed, excessive, internalized, released, or reoriented. [Summary of Greer’s reversal framework](https://www.hermetica.info/Tarot.pdf)

**[Not traced]** A legally accessible primary page containing her complete card entry was not located. Exact word-lists reproduced by unsourced sites were therefore excluded.

# Esoteric and symbolic readings

## Christian

### Earliest secure layer

The Renaissance decks were made in Christian societies and their trumps openly contain Christian imagery. That does not make every pip a hidden Christian allegory.

### Waite-Smith development

Smith places the Four in a chapel with:

- prayer posture;
- tomb effigy;
- stained glass;
- medieval knight;
- suspended weapons.

This supports a Christian reading of vigil, retreat, remembrance of death, intercession, and rest in sacred space.

**[Modern interpretation]** The card is sometimes compared with monastic retreat, vigil before battle, Christ’s entombment, or the “peace that passes understanding.” Waite does not specify any of these.

## Kabbalistic

Lévi is the first major writer consulted here to state the exact scaffold:

- four Fours = Chesed;
- Swords = Vav;
- therefore Four of Swords = Chesed of Vav.

Golden Dawn refines that into Chesed in Yetzirah.

Symbolically:

- Chesed stabilizes and governs;
- Yetzirah is formation;
- Air is thought and relation;
- the result is ordered mental space or cessation imposed on conflict.

This is **esoteric attribution**, not Jewish traditional commentary on a tarot card. It belongs specifically to Western Hermetic Qabalah.

## Astrological

Jupiter in Libra joins:

- Jupiter: expansion, law, beneficence, magnanimity;
- Libra: balance, agreement, proportion, arbitration;
- Swords: division, analysis, speech, conflict.

Book T resolves the mixture as “rest from strife.” Crowley sharpens it to “truce,” stressing that Libra balances opposed blades without removing them.

## Hermetic

The central flower in Thoth and Marseille can be read as a reconciled centre. Four weapons define a stable cross; the rose is mind or spirit protected by disciplined force.

In Crowley, this stability is provisional because Swords are inherently divisive. The card is a constructed armistice, not spontaneous harmony.

## Alchemical

### Sola Busca

Modern researchers have proposed that the deck encodes operations of Renaissance alchemy. The Brera has exhibited and discussed this interpretive tradition.

**[Disputed]** No surviving fifteenth-century manual explains the Four of Swords alchemically. Claims that its four weapons represent particular substances or laboratory stages are reconstructions.

### Modern Thoth reading

The central rose surrounded or pierced by swords may be read as fixation, coagulation, or equilibrium between antagonistic forces. Crowley’s explicit account is primarily Qabalistic and astrological, so detailed alchemical stage-mapping is later elaboration unless separately cited.

## Numerological

Four is widely read as:

- structure;
- square, cross, and four directions;
- stability;
- boundary;
- material or psychic enclosure.

In Marseille, four arched blades visibly enclose the plant. In RWS, the three-plus-one distribution turns four into an ordered architecture: three weapons suspended above, one horizontal below. In Thoth, four blades geometrize a precarious centre.

These readings first become systematic in the nineteenth-century occult fusion of pips with Sephiroth, rather than in documented Renaissance commentary.

## Jungian

Tarot was not a central system developed by Jung himself. “Jungian tarot” is mainly a later synthesis created by tarot authors and depth-psychology interpreters.

The Four is read as:

- withdrawal of libido from outward action;
- psychic incubation;
- protected descent into the unconscious;
- reintegration after conflict;
- restoration of ego boundaries;
- confrontation with the image of death without literal death.

The tomb becomes a liminal container. The recumbent figure is neither acting nor annihilated: it inhabits a threshold between identity states.

**[Modern invention]** Statements that Jung personally assigned the Four of Swords a fixed archetype are unsupported.

# People and works

## Artists, printers, occultists, and historians

- **Bonifacio Bembo / associated Milanese workshops:** attribution for Brambilla, Cary–Yale, and Visconti-Sforza cards; exact hands disputed.
- **Unknown Sola Busca engraver:** sometimes connected to Nicola di Maestro Antonio; disputed.
- **Jean Noblet, Jean Dodal, Pierre Madenié, Nicolas Conver:** major makers in the printed French lineage.
- **Jean-Baptiste Alliette/Etteilla:** first sustained published tarot cartomantic system and first purpose-designed occult tarot.
- **Éliphas Lévi:** joined suit letters and pip numbers to Tetragrammaton and Sephiroth.
- **S. L. MacGregor Mathers:** translated Etteilla-derived meanings and helped formulate Golden Dawn Book T.
- **A. E. Waite:** provided the conceptual and divinatory program for the 1909 deck.
- **Pamela Colman Smith:** created the tomb-effigy image that became the modern standard.
- **Frieda Harris:** converted the Golden Dawn/Crowley doctrine into the geometrical Thoth painting.
- **Aleister Crowley:** renamed the card “Truce” and supplied its Thelemic-Hermetic analysis.
- **Paul Foster Case and Jessie Burns Parke:** BOTA synthesis and non-scenic pip.
- **Paul Marteau:** influential French Marseille numerology.
- **Alejandro Jodorowsky and Philippe Camoin:** modern Marseille reconstruction; with Marianne Costa, psychological/numerological reading.
- **Michael Dummett, Sylvia Mann, Ronald Decker, Thierry Depaulis, Stuart Kaplan, Robert M. Place, Helen Farley:** historians and synthesizers central to distinguishing archival history from occult origin stories.

## Calvino

The first part of Italo Calvino’s *Il castello dei destini incrociati* appeared in 1969 using the Visconti-Sforza deck; the expanded 1973 book adds a section using the Marseille tradition. Cards become a combinatorial writing device through which mute travellers tell intersecting stories. [Overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Castle_of_Crossed_Destinies)

**[Not traced]** The Four of Swords was not verified as the controlling card of a specific named episode. Claims that Calvino supplied a canonical meaning for it should not be made without a page-specific concordance.

## T. S. Eliot

*The Waste Land* names a “wicked pack of cards” and invents/repurposes tarot-like figures, including the “drowned Phoenician Sailor” and “the man with three staves.”

**Finding:** Eliot does not name the Four of Swords. The poem is important to modern literary tarot reception, not to this card’s individual history. [*The Waste Land* overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Waste_Land)

## W. B. Yeats

Yeats belonged to the Golden Dawn and knew its tarot correspondences.

**[Not traced]** No primary Yeats passage specifically interpreting the Four of Swords was located. General Golden Dawn membership is insufficient to attribute “Rest from Strife” to a poem or play without textual evidence.

## Bergman

Ingmar Bergman’s *The Seventh Seal* invokes a medieval knight, death, chess, chapel imagery, and spiritual suspension, making it visually congenial to the Waite-Smith card.

**[Not traced]** No evidence was found that Bergman quoted the Four of Swords or used it as a source. The resemblance is thematic comparison, not documented influence.

## Jodorowsky’s films

Jodorowsky uses tarot extensively in his artistic and spiritual practice, and the imagery of *The Holy Mountain* is openly occult and initiatory.

**[Not traced]** No reliable shot-to-card documentation establishes a deliberate Four-of-Swords quotation in a particular film. His card-specific importance comes from the Camoin deck and *The Way of Tarot*, not a verified cinematic quotation.

## Paintings and buildings

Smith’s strongest likely visual context is not one unique painting but the widespread medieval and Gothic Revival form of a recumbent funerary effigy:

- armoured body stretched on a chest tomb;
- hands joined in prayer;
- weapons or heraldry nearby;
- chapel or stained-glass setting.

**[Not traced]** No documentary statement identifies a particular church, tomb, manuscript, or painting as Smith’s model. Specific identifications circulating online remain conjectural.

# Controversies and disputes

## Was the Sola Busca Four the direct source of Smith’s Four?

**Verdict: disputed; evidence insufficient.**

What is documented:

- the Sola Busca supplied fully illustrated pips;
- the British Museum acquired photographs in 1908;
- Smith worked in 1909;
- several Smith cards share striking motifs with Sola Busca cards.

What is not documented:

- a statement by Smith that she copied the Sola Busca Four;
- close compositional identity between that Four and her tomb effigy;
- a continuous historical lineage from the Sola Busca Four to Waite’s meaning.

The more defensible reconstruction is that Sola Busca demonstrated a precedent for scenic pips and supplied several motifs elsewhere, while Waite’s Etteilla-derived “tomb” meaning and Smith’s knowledge of funerary sculpture account for the Four’s particular scene.

## Egyptian origin

### Claim

Court de Gébelin asserted in 1781 that tarot preserved an Egyptian Book of Thoth. He used speculative etymology and analogies between the pack, sacred numbers, and Egyptian wisdom. [Primary French text](https://www.tarock.info/gebelin.htm)

### Status

**[Legend]** No Egyptian tarot cards, Egyptian Book of Thoth in card form, hieroglyphic prototype, or transmission record has been found.

The claim predates Champollion’s decipherment of Egyptian hieroglyphs. Gébelin did not cite hieroglyphic spellings supporting his proposed etymology. Dummett emphasized this lack of linguistic evidence. [Dummett, “Origins of Tarot”](https://www.nybooks.com/articles/1981/05/14/origins-of-tarot/)

### Spreaders

- Comte de Mellet
- Etteilla
- Éliphas Lévi
- Paul Christian
- Papus
- Golden Dawn writers
- twentieth-century Egyptian-themed decks

### Card-specific effect

The myth does not explain the actual fifteenth-century Four of Swords. Its main effect was to recast an ordinary pip as a hieroglyph capable of universal occult correspondence.

## Romani/Gypsy transmission myth

### Claim

Romani people preserved or brought the Egyptian tarot to Europe.

### Status

**[Legend]** The idea grew in the nineteenth century, especially in works by Jean-Alexandre Vaillant and writers influenced by the old European misidentification of Romani people as Egyptians. Papus’s title *Tarot des Bohémiens* helped popularize it.

The earliest Italian tarot records do not connect the packs with Romani communities. Linguistic and historical evidence locates Romani origins in India, not ancient Egypt. [Historical summary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tarot_card_reading), [Farley/Dummett bibliography](https://hermetism.org/encyclopedia/tarot-cartomancy/)

The term “Gypsy” is retained here only when identifying the historical myth or titles.

## Cathar origin

### Claim

Tarot preserved Cathar dualist doctrine after the medieval crusade against the Cathars.

### Status

**[Legend / modern speculative tradition]** No fifteenth-century card, rule, ledger, or commentary connects the Four of Swords—or tarot generally—to Cathar transmission. The surviving objects appear in northern Italian courtly and commercial contexts long after the principal Cathar institutions were destroyed.

Possible thematic resemblances are not evidence of genealogy.

## Templar origin

### Claim

The Knights Templar transmitted sacred knowledge that became tarot.

### Status

**[Legend / modern occult construction]** No chain of manuscripts, card objects, workshops, or terminology connects the dissolved Templar order to fifteenth-century tarot. The knight on Waite’s Four is a twentieth-century funerary image, not evidence that the card originated among Templars.

## Alchemical Sola Busca

### Claim

The complete Sola Busca is a coherent alchemical grimoire.

### Status

**[Disputed scholarly/esoteric reconstruction]** Its strange scenes, classical personages, inscriptions, vessels, fire, and transformations make alchemical readings possible. Modern researchers have constructed elaborate programs.

Against certainty stands the absence of a contemporary key. Political, moral, antiquarian, satirical, and game-related readings may coexist. The Brera’s presentation acknowledges the alchemical research without turning every proposed identification into documented fact.

## Occult antiquity of “Rest from Strife”

### Claim

The title is an ancient or Renaissance secret name.

### Status

**[Esoteric attribution, late nineteenth century]** It is securely associated with Golden Dawn Book T. No Renaissance card or rule carries it.

Etteilla’s “Solitude” and Mathers’s “Retreat” supplied semantic ingredients; Golden Dawn astrology reorganized them into a decanic title.

## “Truce” versus “Rest from Strife”

“Truce” is not a different astrological correspondence. Crowley retains Jupiter in Libra and Chesed but changes emphasis:

- Golden Dawn: repose following struggle;
- Crowley: equilibrium maintained between continuing antagonists.

Both are documented within their respective systems.

## Is the Waite figure alive, sleeping, wounded, or dead?

Waite calls it an **effigy** on a tomb. An effigy is a sculpted representation, not the literal body of the knight.

Therefore:

- “sleeping knight” is a common modern simplification;
- “dead knight” confuses sculpture and represented subject;
- “wounded soldier convalescing” is possible as divinatory metaphor but not literal description;
- “vigil before returning to battle” is modern narrative elaboration.

## Is the single horizontal sword his weapon?

Smith places it on the tomb, visually available as “one set apart from three.”

**[Modern interpretation]** Readers call it a reserved weapon, single purpose, underlying thought, or latent readiness. Waite supplies none of those explanations.

## Marseille floral symbolism

The central plant is definitely part of the printed design. Claims that it specifically represents consciousness, semen, Christ, the Tree of Life, the spinal column, or a vulva are later interpretations unless tied to a named modern author.

Paul Marteau and Jodorowsky are important spreaders of organic/numerological readings, but their meanings should not be passed off as woodblock-makers’ explanations.

## Misprints that became traditions

Several general tarot printing accidents became iconographic traditions: inverted or ambiguous sword terminals, copied colouring errors, altered captions, and reconstructed missing cards.

For this Four:

- repeated copying made blade ends ambiguous;
- modern facsimiles normalize damaged lines and colours;
- “Conver 1760” editions often combine later impressions and reconstructed cards;
- Jodorowsky–Camoin introduces a synthetic detail-set presented as restoration.

**[Not traced]** No single famous Four-of-Swords typographical error was found that demonstrably generated its principal divinatory meaning. The tomb scene was intentional, not a misprint.

## Historical correction associated with Dummett and successors

Dummett, Mann, Decker, Depaulis, Kaplan, Place, and Farley collectively established or spread a documentary model:

1. Tarot arose as a fifteenth-century Italian card game.
2. Its suit cards belonged to the ordinary playing-card system.
3. Trump orders varied regionally.
4. No evidence supports an Egyptian, Templar, Cathar, or Romani origin.
5. Printed occult interpretation begins in late eighteenth-century France.
6. Nineteenth-century occultists added Kabbalah, astrology, elemental correspondences, and initiatory myths.
7. Golden Dawn, Waite-Smith, BOTA, and Thoth are creative modern syntheses, not recovered ancient books.

That correction does not make occult meanings culturally unimportant. It relocates them to the periods and authors that actually produced them.

# Open questions

1. **Exact priority among the earliest Milanese Fours.** Brambilla has the clearest official early date range, but shifting dates for Cary–Yale and workshop attributions could alter which surviving card was physically painted first.

2. **Exact makers.** “Bonifacio Bembo” remains a useful conventional attribution, but the distribution of labour among Bembo, Zavattari, related workshops, and later replacement artists remains disputed.

3. **Graphic ancestry of the arched Marseille swords.** The route from Milanese interlace to French standardized “scimitars” remains reconstructable only in stages.

4. **Smith’s working references.** Access to Sola Busca photographs is plausible, but her card-by-card decisions are incompletely documented. A sketchbook, letter, or design instruction naming the Four’s model has not surfaced in the consulted sources.

5. **Specific tomb model.** No church monument has been securely demonstrated as the direct source of Smith’s effigy.

6. **Etteilla’s earliest exact wording.** “Solitude/Économie” is secure in the transmitted system, but surviving editions and deck types should be collated to determine the first printing of every subsidiary synonym.

7. **Book T manuscript dating.** The system circulated internally before its open publication. Individual surviving manuscripts may differ slightly in wording and punctuation.

8. **Case’s card-specific teaching.** Much BOTA curriculum remains organizational or commercially restricted; a page-verifiable public Case entry for upright/reversed meanings was not located.

9. **Papus’s “enemy defeated.”** This appears in later attribution tables, but its precise first Papus edition and wording need checking against a paginated scan rather than derivative charts.

10. **Calvino concordance.** A card-by-card index would be required to establish precisely where the Four appears in the layouts and what local narrative function it performs.

11. **Sola Busca intention.** Alchemical, political, antiquarian, moral, and satirical interpretations remain debated because no contemporary explanatory key survives.
