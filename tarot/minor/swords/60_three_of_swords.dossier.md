# Three of Swords: Research Dossier

## Method and evidentiary labels

This dossier distinguishes six kinds of statement:

- **[Documented fact]**: supported directly by a surviving card, archival record, contemporary rule book, or primary occult text.
- **[Scholarly reconstruction]**: an inference accepted or seriously entertained by historians, but not stated in a contemporary document.
- **[Tradition]**: a historically transmitted convention whose first origin may be uncertain.
- **[Esoteric attribution]**: an occult correspondence intentionally imposed on the card.
- **[Disputed claim]**: a claim for which evidence or interpretation remains contested.
- **[Legend / modern invention]**: unsupported historical narrative or a demonstrably recent interpretive development.

A significant limitation must be stated at the outset: surviving fifteenth-century cards are unevenly catalogued online, and some relevant books by Dummett, Kaplan, Pollack, Greer, Decker, Depaulis, Place, and Farley are not legally available in full text. Where an exact passage could not be checked against a scan, I identify the secondary trail rather than pretending to have verified it.

### A correction about “earliest”

**[Documented fact]** The standard Visconti–Sforza pack divided among the Morgan Library, Accademia Carrara, and Colleoni collection lacks four cards: the Devil, Tower, Knight of Coins, and Three of Swords. Any Three of Swords supplied in a modern Visconti–Sforza reproduction is therefore a reconstruction.

**[Documented fact, with catalogue caveat]** The Visconti di Modrone or Cary–Yale pack retains thirty-nine numeral cards, and published card inventories indicate that its Three of Swords survives. Yale describes this as one of three luxury Visconti-family packs made between roughly 1440 and 1480. Current scholarship has questioned older blanket attribution of all these cards to Bonifacio Bembo and has proposed Andrea Bembo for at least part of the Cary–Yale group. [Yale Library](https://library.yale.edu/news/cards-library-partners-work-together-solve-mysteries-rare-tarot-deck?page=1)

**[Documented fact]** The Sola Busca is not the earliest surviving Three of Swords, but it is the earliest surviving fully illustrated, narrative or emblematic version of the card in a complete tarot pack.

## Names, vocabulary, and what does not apply

### The card itself

The fifteenth-century cards generally carry no written suit title. “Tre di Spade” is the normal modern Italian descriptive name, not necessarily a caption printed on early examples.

- Italian: *tre di spade*
- French: *trois d’épées*
- Spanish: *tres de espadas*
- German: *Drei der Schwerter*
- English: Three of Swords
- Golden Dawn: “Lord of Sorrow”
- Thoth: “Sorrow”
- BOTA: Three of Swords, with the keyword “Sorrow”

### *Trionfi*, *tarocchi*, and tarot

**[Documented fact]** The earliest records call the augmented packs *carte da trionfi*, “cards with triumphs/trumps.” The word *tarocchi* is first securely attested in Ferrarese accounts in 1505. Its singular is *tarocco*. Farley summarizes the linguistic transition and notes that Albert Lollio already said in 1550 that the word’s origin was unknown. [Farley, *A Cultural History of Tarot*](https://e-isomero.com/pdf/book/1611846398.pdf)

**[Scholarly consensus]** French *tarot* and German *Tarock* derive from the Italian word, not from Egyptian expressions such as *tar ro* (“royal road”). The Egyptian etymologies were created by eighteenth-century occultists without hieroglyphic evidence.

### Trump renamings in the user’s general template

Papess/Juno, Pope/Jupiter, Maison Dieu/Tower, Justice/Strength order, and the number of the Fool concern the trump sequence, not the Three of Swords. They do not rename or renumber this card.

For orientation:

- **[Documented fact]** In the Besançon pattern, produced principally in eastern France and Switzerland from the eighteenth century, the Papess and Pope were replaced by Juno and Jupiter, largely to avoid objectionable papal imagery.
- **[Documented fact]** *La Maison Dieu* is the Marseille title for trump XVI; “Tower” is an English descriptive replacement.
- **[Documented fact]** The Fool was commonly unnumbered in traditional packs.
- **[Documented fact]** The Marseille sequence normally places Justice VIII and Strength XI. Waite’s Golden Dawn-based deck exchanges them: Strength VIII, Justice XI.
- **[No card-specific effect]** None of these changes alters the rank, picture, or play of the Three of Swords.

# Iconography deck by deck

## Fifteenth-century Milan

### Visconti di Modrone / Cary–Yale, approximately 1440s

**[Documented fact]** This is a hand-painted Milanese court pack, now at Yale’s Beinecke Library. Its surviving structure is exceptional: it included additional female counterparts to the conventional male court ranks, probably yielding six courts per suit and an eighty-six-card pack if reconstructed symmetrically.

**Three of Swords:** three straight, double-edged swords arranged on a silver field, treated as sumptuous heraldic objects rather than as a narrative event. The exact orientation in reproductions should be checked against Yale’s object photography; modern facsimiles sometimes crop or recolor the originals.

**Meaning at the time:** **[No evidence]** No contemporary text assigns sorrow, heartbreak, Saturn, Air, or Binah to this card. Its function was identification of suit and rank.

### Brera–Brambilla, before 1447

**[Documented fact]** The surviving forty-eight-card group at the Pinacoteca di Brera was made for Filippo Maria Visconti. Unlike the Cary–Yale Swords, Brambilla’s swords are curved. The cards have silver grounds and Visconti emblems.

**Three of Swords:** published inventories indicate that many Brambilla numeral cards survive, but the online museum material consulted did not give an individually indexed, high-resolution record sufficient to certify every detail of this particular pip.

**[Open catalogue question]** Whether the surviving Brambilla group includes an uncontested Three of Swords needs confirmation from the Brera’s full printed catalogue, not modern reconstructed decks.

### Visconti–Sforza / Morgan–Bergamo, shortly after 1450

**[Documented fact]** The Three of Swords does not survive. The Metropolitan Museum’s catalogue identifies the missing cards as the Devil, Fire/Tower, Knight of Coins, and Three of Swords. [Metropolitan Museum, *The World in Play*](https://resources.metmuseum.org/resources/metpublications/pdf/The_World_in_Play_Luxury_Cards_1430_1540.pdf)

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** It probably resembled the surviving Swords pips: three actual swords symmetrically disposed on a gold ground, with Visconti–Sforza heraldic ornament and the motto *a bon droyt*. No heart, rain, human figure, astrology, or narrative is warranted.

**[Modern invention]** Painted “restorations” in commercial Visconti–Sforza decks are new compositions. They are not recovered Bembo images.

## Fifteenth-century Ferrara and related cards

### Este cards

Several small groups of late-fifteenth-century painted cards have been associated with the Este court or Ferrara, sometimes too confidently.

**[Documented fact]** Surviving Ferrarese-related cards establish regional variation in trump design and luxury production.

**[Absence of evidence]** I found no securely catalogued, surviving Este Three of Swords whose iconography can be described independently of a modern reconstruction. Claims that a particular detached three-sword pip belonged to a named Este tarot require provenance stronger than stylistic resemblance.

### Mantegna “Tarocchi,” approximately 1460s–1470s

**[Documented fact]** The so-called Mantegna Tarocchi are two series of fifty educational or encyclopedic engravings—social estates, Muses, liberal arts, virtues, celestial spheres—not a tarot pack and not by Andrea Mantegna.

**Relevance:** none to the Three of Swords as a playing card. They are relevant only as comparative evidence for Renaissance emblematic culture.

## Sola Busca, engraved about 1490; painted Venice, 1491

**[Documented fact]** The Sola Busca is the earliest complete surviving true tarot pack and the earliest surviving engraved tarot. One painted card bears a Venetian-era date equivalent to 1491. Its complete painted copy is at the Pinacoteca di Brera; unpainted impressions survive in the British Museum, Albertina, Hamburg, and Paris. [British Museum](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/P_1845-0825-485), [Brera exhibition release](https://pinacotecabrera.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/11/Il-Segreto-dei-tarocchi-CS.pdf)

### What the Three actually shows

A large red, anatomical-heraldic heart is pierced by three swords. The hilts and blades are individually articulated; vegetation or flame-like forms surround the heart in the colored version.

**[Interpretive tradition]** The heart has been described as a symbol of fire, with swords interpreted as metals in an alchemical operation. [Finestre sull’Arte](https://www.finestresullarte.info/en/works-and-artists/the-sola-busca-tarot-the-world-s-oldest-complete-deck?set_dark_mode=0)

**[Disputed]** The Sola Busca’s imagery has been read as:

- alchemical stages;
- Roman history mixed with pseudohistory;
- Renaissance Hermetism;
- a satire or moralized epic;
- imagery connected with Ludovico Lazzarelli;
- designs by Nicola di Maestro Antonio.

None of these proposed programs has produced a universally accepted card-by-card decipherment. “Heart = fire and swords = metals” is an interpretive proposal, not an inscription on the object.

### Was it Pamela Colman Smith’s source?

**[Strong scholarly reconstruction]** In 1907 the British Museum acquired photographs of all seventy-eight Sola Busca cards. Smith produced the Waite deck in 1909. Several of her minor-card compositions resemble Sola Busca designs closely, especially Three of Swords and Ten of Wands/Ten of Swords transformations.

**[Not directly documented]** No surviving statement by Smith says, “I copied the Sola Busca Three of Swords.” It is therefore safer to say that access and visual dependence are highly probable, not proved by a written confession. [Sola Busca history](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sola_Busca_tarot)

The key discovery is that Smith did not invent the pierced-heart emblem from nothing. She simplified and monumentalized an image already present in the Sola Busca.

## Rosenwald and Budapest sheets, approximately 1500

### Rosenwald sheet

**[Documented fact]** The Rosenwald sheets are early printed Italian tarot sheets, usually associated with Florence and dated around the end of the fifteenth or beginning of the sixteenth century. They are important for the development of inexpensive printed tarot.

**Three of Swords:** where reconstructed from the sheets, it belongs to the conventional non-scenic Italian pip tradition: three suit signs, not an emotional tableau.

### Budapest sheets

**[Documented fact]** Related early printed fragments are held in Budapest and other collections. The Fondazione Giorgio Cini dates one associated printed group approximately 1490–1500. [Fondazione Cini](https://archivi.cini.it/storiaarte/detail/32547/carta-gioco-32547.html)

**[Catalogue limitation]** I found no authoritative online object entry isolating a Budapest Three of Swords. It would be unsafe to attribute a pierced heart or other narrative scene to it. Its importance is typological: these sheets document cheap printed tarot alongside elite painted packs.

## Tarot de Marseille lineage

The term “Tarot de Marseille” is retrospective. Noblet was a Parisian and Dodal a Lyonnais. The label identifies a design family, not necessarily a place of manufacture.

### General Marseille Sword grammar

**[Documented fact]** Numbered Swords are formed from curved, interlaced blades or blade-like bands. On odd cards, a single straight or fully realized sword occupies the central axis inside the framework. Floral leaves and blossoms fill remaining spaces. [Tarot de Marseille overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tarot_of_Marseilles)

This is not a scene of combat. The curves are conventionalized swords; calling them “scimitars” is descriptively understandable but historically misleading if it implies that the pattern represents three literal Ottoman weapons.

### Jean Noblet, Paris, about 1650

**[Documented fact]** Noblet is the oldest substantially surviving tarot of the Marseille design family. His Swords VI–X are missing, but the Three survives.

**Image:** two curved swords or sword-bands form a pointed oval/mandorla; a straight central sword runs vertically through it; floral growth appears at the ends and margins. There is no heart and no human action.

### Jean Dodal, Lyon, about 1701–1715

**Image:** the same basic three-blade scheme, more regular and emphatic. The curved swords interlace into a narrow almond around the central sword. Floral ornament balances the field.

**Change:** stronger repetition and standardization of the pattern. The card communicates suit and number efficiently in play.

### Pierre Madenié, Dijon, 1709, and Type II

Madenié is the earliest securely dated Type-II Marseille pack. It helps establish that features often called “Conver” existed before Conver.

**Three:** a symmetric central blade enclosed by two curved forms, with increasingly standardized foliage.

### Nicolas Conver, Marseille, 1760/1761

**[Documented fact]** Conver’s design became the dominant source for later commercial Marseille packs. Surviving copies and later impressions must be distinguished: some online “1760 Conver” cards are nineteenth-century impressions from inherited or reworked blocks.

**Image:** a tall straight sword in the center, two curved interlaced sword forms around it, and red/blue/yellow floral terminals. The flower does not signify heartbreak in any contemporary rule or divination text.

### Lequart/Grimaud and Paul Marteau, late nineteenth century–1930

**[Documented fact]** Grimaud’s familiar *Ancien Tarot de Marseille*, especially the 1930 Paul Marteau coloring, adapted the Conver line. Color assignments were regularized and sometimes changed.

**Three:** remains an abstract pip. Marteau-era color symbolism is a twentieth-century esoteric overlay, not evidence for a medieval code.

### Camoin–Jodorowsky, 1997

**[Documented fact]** Philippe Camoin and Alejandro Jodorowsky produced a restored/reconstructed Marseille pack, using old cards and Camoin printing material.

**Image:** traditional three-sword construction, recolored with an expanded palette and carefully restored small details.

**[Disputed claim]** Publicity sometimes presented this as recovering a single secret, original tarot. Historians generally treat Marseille packs as a family of changing workshop patterns, not corrupt copies of one recoverable occult archetype.

**Jodorowsky–Costa reading:** the flower and emerging organic forms are read developmentally. Three is the first outward burst after accumulation in Two; Swords belong to mental/intellectual energy. Their reading does not reduce the card to romantic heartbreak.

## Minchiate

**[Documented fact]** Minchiate expanded the trump series to forty-one while retaining four Italian suits. The Three of Swords remains a numeral suit card.

**Image:** generally a conventional Italian pip arrangement, varying by Florentine workshop; not the Waite heart.

**Play:** in the long suits—Swords and Batons—cards ordinarily rank King, Queen, Knight, Page, Ten downward to Ace. Thus Three beats Two and Ace but loses to Four and above. Exact scoring depends on the Minchiate rule set; an ordinary Three is not one of the special high-counting cards.

---

## Bolognese tarocchino

**[Documented fact]** Bolognese tarot was shortened to sixty-two cards by removing the 2–5 from every suit. This practice is documented by the sixteenth century and retained in surviving Bolognese packs.

**Consequence:** there is no Three of Swords in the standard shortened Bolognese tarocchino.

A complete modern “Bolognese Three of Swords” therefore belongs either to a reconstructed full pack, a nonstandard regional pack, or an oracle adaptation.

---

## Sicilian tarot

**[Documented fact]** The Sicilian pattern is a shortened regional tarot with an altered trump series and reduced suits.

**Card-specific problem:** historic Sicilian packs typically omit low numeral cards, and rules/deck composition changed over time. A Three of Swords is not a stable member of the standard surviving Sicilian pattern.

---

## Besançon pattern

Besançon replaces Papess and Pope with Juno and Jupiter but otherwise preserves a Marseille-like suit system.

**Three of Swords:** conventional Marseille-style interlaced curved swords and foliage. The Juno/Jupiter substitutions have no card-specific consequence.

---

## Etteilla’s Grand Etteilla, approximately 1789

**[Documented fact]** Jean-Baptiste Alliette, “Etteilla,” issued the first tarot designed expressly for occult divination. His *Manière de se récréer avec le jeu de cartes nommées Tarots* appeared in parts in 1783–1785; his purpose-built deck followed around 1789.

Etteilla reorganized and renumbered the entire pack. Therefore “Three of Swords” can mean either:

1. the suit card bearing three sword signs;
2. its position in Etteilla’s continuous sequence, which differs among editions and derivative “Grand Etteilla” packs.

**Image:** early Etteilla numeral cards remain substantially pip-like, with three swords and inscriptions or keywords indicating upright and reversed meanings. They do not employ Smith’s rainy heart.

**[Documented occult attribution]** The card’s core meanings are *éloignement*—removal, distance, separation—and, reversed, *égarement*—wandering, aberration, mental error. Later English lists expand these into absence, rupture, dispersion, alienation, distraction, and confusion.

**Astrology:** Etteilla connected tarot generally with astrology and the elements, but the now-standard “Saturn in Libra” assignment belongs to the Golden Dawn decan system, not demonstrably to Etteilla’s Three of Swords.

## Oswald Wirth, 1889

**[Documented fact]** Wirth’s original *Les 22 Arcanes du Tarot Kabbalistique* contains only twenty-two trumps. There is no Wirth Three of Swords. [British Museum](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/P_1904-0511-48-1-22)

Modern seventy-eight-card “Wirth” decks add minors by later artists or publishers. Their Three of Swords cannot be attributed to Wirth. [Lo Scarabeo description](https://www.loscarabeo.com/en/products/golden-wirth-tarot-grandi-trionfi)

## Golden Dawn manuscript designs, late 1880s–1890s

### Book T

**[Documented esoteric attribution]** Book T calls the card “The Lord of Sorrow” and gives:

- Saturn in Libra;
- 10°–20° Libra;
- Binah of Vav;
- the world Yetzirah;
- two Shem angels, Hariel and Hakamiah/Hekamiah, spellings varying by transliteration.

The prescribed design has three white radiant angelic hands issuing from clouds and holding three swords upright. The center sword parts the two others and cuts a five-petaled rose; its petals fall. Saturn and Libra signs appear above and below. [Regardie, *Complete Golden Dawn System*](https://scriptaetveritas.com.br/wp-content/uploads/2016/04/ISRAEL_REGARDIE_COMPLETE_GOLDEN_DAWN_SYSTEM_OF_MAGIC.pdf)

This is an important correction: the Golden Dawn design is not Smith’s heart. Its destroyed rose is the emblem of disrupted harmony.

### Robert Wang, *The Golden Dawn Tarot*, 1977

**[Documented modern reconstruction]** Wang, working with Israel Regardie, rendered the Book T description in full color: cloud-borne hands, upright swords, broken rose, astrological glyphs, Golden Dawn color scales.

### Chic and Sandra Tabatha Cicero, 1990s onward

**[Documented modern reconstruction]** The Ciceros similarly reconstruct Golden Dawn teaching diagrams. Their Three emphasizes three hands, three upright swords, the severed five-petaled rose, Saturn and Libra, Binah, and Yetzirah. Their instructional text gives “Sorrow” as its keyword. [Cicero text](https://kobayashi-dxn.s3.eu-west-2.amazonaws.com/Golden%20Dawn/cicero-%20secrets%20of%20the%20golden%20dawn%20temple/Cicero_-_Self_Initiation_into_%282%29.pdf)

These decks reconstruct an initiatory manuscript tradition; they are not facsimiles of one canonical Victorian painted deck.

## Waite–Smith, 1909; *Pictorial Key*, 1910

### Pamela Colman Smith’s drawing

A large red heart floats against gray clouds. Three steel swords pierce it: one vertical from above and two diagonally crossing. Rain descends in parallel strokes. No person, landscape, blood, or healing element appears.

The composition combines:

- the Sola Busca precedent of three blades piercing a heart;
- the Golden Dawn title and affect of Sorrow;
- Waite’s inherited Etteilla/Mathers vocabulary of division and rupture;
- Smith’s theatrical simplification into a single instantly readable emblem.

**[Strong reconstruction]** Sola Busca is the visual source.

**[Documented]** Golden Dawn “Sorrow” predates Smith.

**[Disputed]** Whether Waite specified the composition or Smith independently chose the Sola Busca solution is unknown. Waite’s book describes the finished image but does not allocate authorship card by card.

### Waite’s own words

Waite writes:

> “Three swords piercing a heart; cloud and rain behind.”

His upright meanings are:

> “Removal, absence, delay, division, rupture, dispersion…”

He says the rest is what “the design signifies naturally.” Reversed:

> “Mental alienation, error, loss, distraction, disorder, confusion.”

These quotations are from Part III, “The Lesser Arcana,” p. 115 in the consulted scan. [Waite, *Pictorial Key to the Tarot*](https://www.ifate.com/arcanum/Pictorial-Key-to-the-Tarot-iFate.pdf?v=2)

Waite does not print “betrayal by a lover” or “third-party affair.” Those are later popular specializations of the image.

## Paul Foster Case and BOTA

**[Documented fact]** Paul Foster Case founded Builders of the Adytum; Jessie Burns Parke drew the BOTA deck. The deck follows Waite/Golden Dawn structure while simplifying line work and inviting the student to color the cards.

**Three:** essentially the Waite pierced-heart emblem in spare black outline, intended to be colored according to BOTA instructions.

**Correspondence:** Saturn in Libra, second decan; Binah in Yetzirah; keyword “Sorrow.” Case states more generally that cards 2–4 of each suit correspond to the three decans of the cardinal sign belonging to that suit’s element. [Case, *Oracle of the Tarot*](https://benebellwen.com/wp-content/uploads/2013/02/paul-foster-case-oracle-of-the-tarot-1933-source-credit-tarotworks.pdf)

BOTA’s suit doctrine is not identical in every detail to Waite’s published exoteric explanations, although both descend from Golden Dawn material.

## Crowley–Harris Thoth, painted 1938–1943; first complete publication 1969

### Title and picture

The card is titled **SORROW**.

Frieda Harris painted a geometric, nocturnal design:

- one long central sword stands point upward;
- two shorter curved swords intersect it;
- the central impact destroys a rose;
- petals fall;
- the background is black, stormy, organic, and tense;
- Saturn and Libra glyphs identify the decan.

Crowley describes it as a dark and heavy card: Binah, the Great Mother, operates in Air, but here as the darkness of the Great Sea. The desire to create becomes monstrous or perverse; the storm “broods under implacable night.” [Crowley, *Book of Thoth* excerpt](https://book-of-thoth.net/Three-of-Swords-Sorrow)

The design is a radical abstraction of Book T’s central sword cutting the rose. It is not derived primarily from Smith’s heart, though both images share a genealogy of piercing/destruction.

### Verification of the requested lead

- “Sorrow”: **verified**.
- Saturn in Libra: **verified**.
- Second decan, 10°–20° Libra: **verified**.
- Binah of Vav: **verified**.
- Air: **verified through the Sword suit**.
- Three hands: present in Book T’s verbal design; Harris suppresses literal cloud-hands in favor of geometric sword forms.

### Tzaddi/Heh issue

Crowley’s famous “Tzaddi is not the Star” revision exchanges the Hebrew-letter assignments of the Emperor and Star in his system. It affects trumps IV and XVII, not the Three of Swords. The pip remains Saturn in Libra, Binah of Vav, regardless of that controversy.

## Notable modern redesign families

### Marseille restorations

Decks by Jean-Claude Flornoy, Yves Reynaud, Yoav Ben-Dov, and others preserve the abstract three-sword pattern. Their interpretive books often treat the flower and blade geometry generatively rather than as heartbreak.

### RWS-derived decks

Most modern Anglo-American decks retain one or more of:

- three blades;
- a heart;
- rain or storm;
- separation imagery;
- a grieving person;
- a triangular relationship.

**[Modern tradition]** “Heartbreak card” is now the dominant popular shorthand.

### Revisionist and therapeutic decks

Late-twentieth- and twenty-first-century decks frequently show:

- swords being removed;
- a stitched or healing heart;
- a mourner processing grief;
- spoken words piercing another;
- trauma held in the body;
- a dove or flower wounded rather than a literal heart.

These are modern psychological elaborations, not recoveries of a medieval meaning.

# The game

## Original function

**[Scholarly consensus]** Tarot was created as a trick-taking game using an ordinary four-suit pack expanded by permanent trumps and a special Fool. The suit cards predate occult tarot by centuries.

A traditional seventy-eight-card pack contains:

- twenty-one trumps;
- the Fool;
- four suits of fourteen cards;
- ten numeral cards and four courts in each suit.

The Three of Swords is an ordinary numeral card. It has no trump power.

## Rank

In traditional Italian and many derivative games, Swords and Batons are “long” or black suits with natural order:

King > Queen > Knight > Page > 10 > 9 > 8 > 7 > 6 > 5 > 4 > 3 > 2 > Ace.

Thus the Three:

- beats the Two and Ace;
- loses to Four through Ten and all courts;
- loses to any trump when legally trumped.

Cups and Coins commonly reverse the pip order, with Ace high and Ten low. This asymmetry is a defining feature of many historic tarot games.

## Point value

**[Documented game tradition]** Low numeral cards ordinarily have no intrinsic counting value beyond their role in tricks or the fractional/group-counting system. Kings and selected trumps are the major counters. Depending on the rules, every captured card helps complete a counting group, so the Three can contribute indirectly without being a high-value counter.

Modern French tarot uses French suits rather than Swords. Its “3 of Spades” is not visually the tarot Three of Swords, though it occupies an analogous low-card position. Modern French scoring values ordinary suit cards at one-half point under the standard pairing method. [Pagat, French Tarot](https://www.pagat.com/tarot/frtarot.html), [Pagat, card counting](https://www.pagat.com/tarot/counting.html)

## Tarock

Central-European Tarock packs commonly omit low suit cards. In fifty-four-card packs:

- black suits retain 5–10;
- red suits retain Ace–4.

Consequently there is normally no Three of Spades/Swords analogue in the black suit. Its absence is a consequence of pack reduction, not occult suppression.

## Minchiate

The Three survives as a low card of the long Sword suit. It has no special esoteric or scoring identity.

## Bolognese and Sicilian reductions

The Three is absent from standard shortened Bolognese tarocchino and usually absent from reduced Sicilian configurations.

## Trump-order disputes

These concern the twenty-one trumps, not the Three of Swords, but they matter for reconstructing early tarot:

- Type A or Southern: Bologna and Florence;
- Type B or Eastern: Ferrara and Venice;
- Type C or Western: Milan, Lombardy, and the line ancestral to French tarot.

Dummett identified the clusters from surviving lists and game rules. Their existence shows that tarot spread early enough for regional hierarchies to crystallize independently. [Dummett translation and commentary](https://dummettsmondo.blogspot.com/2015/07/chapters-6-and-7-game-and-its-trump.html)

The Three’s rank is much less controversial than the order of virtues and papal trumps.

## The Fool

The Fool traditionally functions as an excuse: it may be played without following suit and usually returns to its player’s captured pile in exchange for another card. It is not originally trump 0 or trump 22 in the gaming sense.

Again, this does not change the Three of Swords.

# Chronology of attestation

## About 1420: Marziano da Tortona

**[Documented fact]** Marziano composed a description of a special card game for Filippo Maria Visconti, with sixteen classical gods arranged into four thematic suits and ordinary bird suits beneath them.

**[Scholarly significance]** It is a precursor to tarot because it has a permanent hierarchy of special cards, but it is not a standard tarot pack. It has no Three of Swords.

## 1440–1442: northern Italian records

**[Documented fact]** Records from Florence and Ferrara mention *carte da trionfi*. The February 1442 Ferrarese entry is one of the earliest securely dated uses of *trionfi* for cards. These records name packs, not individual numeral cards.

## 1440s: Milanese surviving cards

The Cary–Yale and Brambilla groups provide physical evidence for Swords pips, including a probable surviving Three in Cary–Yale.

## 1449: Marcello letter and Marziano manuscript

Jacopo Antonio Marcello sent Queen Isabella of Lorraine a pack made for Filippo Maria Visconti and a copy of Marziano’s explanatory text. This is a special classical-god pack, not necessarily the standard twenty-two-trump tarot.

## About 1450: Visconti–Sforza

The Three existed as part of the original pack but is now lost.

## 1460s–1490s: Ferrarese literary and artistic culture

Boiardo devised a poetic card scheme using passions, virtues, and historical or mythological figures. Such works demonstrate that cards could support literary invention, but they do not establish occult meanings for the standard Three of Swords.

## About 1470–1500: *Sermones de ludo cum aliis*

**[Documented fact]** The anonymous sermon condemns games and supplies an early complete list of tarot subjects. Its list is crucial for trump order and names.

**Card-specific limitation:** it lists trumps, not the individual meanings of pip cards. It does not call the Three “Sorrow.” [Manuscript image](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Sermones_de_Ludo_cum_Aliis.jpg)

## 1491: Sola Busca

Earliest complete surviving tarot and earliest surviving scenic Three of Swords: pierced heart.

## About 1500: Rosenwald/Budapest sheets

Physical evidence for printed, comparatively inexpensive tarot.

## 1505: *tarocchi*

The Ferrarese court accounts provide the earliest currently accepted occurrence of *tarocchi* as the pack’s name.

## Early sixteenth century: appropriated-tarot poems

In *tarocchi appropriati*, trump names are assigned to court personalities, often satirically or complimentarily. These compositions chiefly use trumps, not the Three of Swords.

## 1527: Teofilo Folengo, *Chaos del Triperuno*

**[Documented fact]** Folengo used tarot cards in literary divinatory episodes. This is evidence that symbolic or fortune-telling play with tarot existed before Etteilla.

**[Important limitation]** It is not a systematic cartomantic manual and does not establish a continuous Three-of-Swords meaning. [Folengo project](https://folengos.com/teofilo-folengo-1491-1544/)

## 1637: Marolles rules

The Abbé de Marolles produced one of the earliest printed French tarot rule descriptions. It treats the cards as a game, not an Egyptian oracle.

## About 1650–1760: Marseille lineage

The Three is consistently geometric and floral.

## About 1750: Bolognese cartomancy sheet

**[Documented fact]** A handwritten Bolognese cartomancy list predates Court de Gébelin and Etteilla. It demonstrates that popular divination with tarot was not invented wholly by Parisian occultists.

Card meanings in that tradition differ from the later Golden Dawn system.

## 1770: Etteilla and ordinary-card cartomancy

Etteilla publishes his first cartomancy book, originally for a standard pack.

## 1781: Court de Gébelin and Comte de Mellet

Gébelin declares tarot an Egyptian book; Mellet connects its trumps to Hebrew letters and gives a divination method. Neither supplies the later Golden Dawn pip decans.

## 1783–1785: Etteilla’s tarot manuals

First systematic published method of tarot cartomancy. Three of Swords: distance/removal and, reversed, aberration or wandering.

## About 1789: Grand Etteilla deck

First purpose-designed occult tarot deck.

## 1854–1856: Éliphas Lévi, *Dogme et rituel de la haute magie*

Lévi makes tarot central to ceremonial magic and Kabbalah. His attention concentrates on twenty-two keys; he does not create the later Saturn-in-Libra pip scheme.

## 1863: Paul Christian

Christian expands Egyptianizing occult history, including initiation narratives set in underground Egyptian temples. These are literary occult constructions, not documentary history.

## 1888: Mathers and Golden Dawn

Mathers’s public booklet gives traditional cartomantic meanings. Golden Dawn Book T develops the systematic decan, Sephirah, elemental, angelic, and color framework.

## 1889: Papus and Wirth

Papus publishes *Le Tarot des Bohémiens*; Wirth produces twenty-two Kabbalistic trumps. Neither original Wirth deck has a Three of Swords.

## 1909–1910: Waite–Smith

Smith turns the Sola Busca pierced heart and Golden Dawn sorrow into the best-known modern image.

## 1930s: Case/BOTA

The Golden Dawn framework is systematized in American occult instruction.

## 1938–1943 / 1969: Thoth

Harris paints the deck; full commercial publication follows in 1969.

## 1970s onward: psychological tarot

Humanistic, feminist, Jungian, and therapeutic readings recast sorrow as a process: acknowledging pain, integrating loss, speaking difficult truth, and recovery. Reversed meanings increasingly become “healing” rather than Waite’s confusion.

## Vav

The requested “Vav” lead is verified, but it needs precision.

- In Golden Dawn language, Swords correspond to **Vav** in the divine name Yod–Heh–Vav–Heh.
- The card is therefore “Binah of Vav”: the third Sephirah acting in the Sword/Air quarter.
- This does not mean that the Three of Swords itself is the Hebrew letter Vav in the same way a trump is assigned one of twenty-two letters.
- Spellings *Vav*, *Vau*, and *Waw* are transliteration variants.

## Sephirah 3: Binah

**[Esoteric attribution]** Three corresponds to Binah, Understanding, the third Sephirah.

Golden Dawn pip logic combines:

1. number 3 → Binah;
2. suit Swords → Air/Yetzirah/Vav;
3. zodiacal decan → Saturn in Libra.

In the Three of Swords, Binah’s formative, containing power operates in unstable or divisive Air. Crowley interprets this as the dark Great Mother or womb of chaos. This is a Thelemic-Qabalistic reading, not a historical explanation of fifteenth-century pips.

## Saturn in Libra

**[Documented Golden Dawn attribution]** The card governs 10°–20° Libra.

The apparent paradox is deliberate:

- Libra seeks balance and relation;
- Saturn restricts, separates, judges, and crystallizes;
- Saturn is traditionally exalted in Libra;
- Book T derives sorrow, separation, and severity from this combination.

Earlier astrological traditions supply images for Libra’s second face, but the identification of that face specifically with the Three of Swords is Golden Dawn synthesis.

## Color scales

Golden Dawn reconstructions use the color scales for Binah in Yetzirah together with the suit and decan coloring. Exact painted palettes vary because Book T survives as instructions and copies, not one universally authoritative colored production deck.

Wang and Cicero consequently differ in hue while agreeing on structure.

## Courts

The requested court “sub-element and zodiacal span” does not apply to the Three, but within the Golden Dawn system:

- Knights/Kings: Fire of the suit;
- Queens: Water of the suit;
- Princes: Air of the suit;
- Princesses: Earth of the suit.

Each non-Princess court spans thirty degrees, conventionally from 20° of one sign through 20° of the next. The Three is instead a ten-degree decan card.

# Divinatory meanings by author

Because copyright restrictions prevent extensive quotation from modern authors, only brief phrases are quoted; the remainder is close paraphrase.

## Etteilla, 1783–1789

**Upright:** *éloignement*—distance, removal, separation, absence.

**Reversed:** *égarement*—wandering, going astray, aberration, confusion.

**[Documented core; edition problem]** These keywords recur in Etteilla-derived lists, but surviving Grand Etteilla editions differ in numbering and expanded gloss. I could not verify a universally authoritative card number and page across the first-state deck and all four *Cahiers*. Etteilla’s primary tarot texts are described and partially translated in the Third Cahier project. [Etteilla Third Cahier](https://thirdcahier.blogspot.com/)

## S. L. MacGregor Mathers, 1888

Mathers’s entry belongs to the Etteilla-derived cartomantic stream.

**Upright:** separation, removal, delay, rupture, dispersal.

**Reversed:** mental alienation, error, loss, distraction, disorder, confusion.

The consulted 1888 scan places “Three of Swords” near the end of the divinatory list. [Mathers, 1888 scan](https://www.howardnema.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/12/the-tarot-1888-by-macgregor-mathers-1.pdf)

**Textual observation:** Waite’s later wording is extremely close. This is evidence of inheritance, not an independent divinatory revelation.

## Golden Dawn Book T

No simple upright/reversed pair is primary. Book T reads dignity—how surrounding elemental forces strengthen or weaken the card.

Core meanings include sorrow, tears, disruption, separation, quarreling, discord, and mischief; it also paradoxically allows certain pleasurable, honest, or generous tendencies “according to dignity.”

## A. E. Waite, 1910

**Upright, exact core:** “Removal, absence, delay, division, rupture, dispersion.”

**Reversed, exact core:** “Mental alienation, error, loss, distraction, disorder, confusion.”

Waite treats the picture as sufficiently obvious that it does not require detailed allegorical commentary. [*Pictorial Key*, p. 115](https://www.ifate.com/arcanum/Pictorial-Key-to-the-Tarot-iFate.pdf?v=2)

## Aleister Crowley, 1944

Crowley does not use Waite-style reversals. Cards are modified by dignity and context.

**Core:** sorrow, disruption, separation, quarrelling, discord, strife, tears, deceit, and intellectual or emotional darkness. At a higher extreme, the card may imply transcendence of ordinary nature through confronting the monstrous products of creation.

His brief title is “Sorrow.” [*Book of Thoth* text](https://book-of-thoth.net/Three-of-Swords-Sorrow)

## Paul Foster Case, 1930s

**Keyword:** “Sorrow.”

Case retains Saturn in Libra and the Golden Dawn decan arrangement. His approach treats adverse events as the fruition of causal or karmic patterns, particularly in relationships, agreements, judgment, and equilibrium.

Case generally uses dignity and combinations more than a mechanically opposed reversed meaning. [*Oracle of the Tarot*](https://benebellwen.com/wp-content/uploads/2013/02/paul-foster-case-oracle-of-the-tarot-1933-source-credit-tarotworks.pdf)

## Rachel Pollack, 1980 and later editions

Pollack treats the card as real sorrow that must be acknowledged rather than denied. The pierced heart can represent honest emotional truth: suffering becomes conscious and therefore potentially workable.

**Upright:** grief, painful truth, heartbreak, separation, the necessity of experiencing pain.

**Reversed:** blocked or prolonged sorrow, difficulty releasing grief, or the beginning of recovery depending on context.

**[Modern psychological reading]** This is not Waite’s literal reversed list, though it grows from his picture.

## Mary K. Greer

Greer’s reversal method does not impose one universal opposite. A reversed card may be blocked, delayed, internalized, excessive, deficient, private, or in recovery.

**Upright:** grief, cutting words, divided affections, painful realization.

**Reversed:** healing or release is one possibility; repressed pain, denial, recurring grief, or self-inflicted mental anguish are others.

**[Modern practice]** “The swords fall out of the heart when reversed” is a popular visual mnemonic. It is not Waite’s own reversed meaning and cannot presently be traced to Greer as its inventor.

# Esoteric and symbolic readings

## Alchemical

### Sola Busca

**[Disputed scholarly/esoteric reconstruction]** The burning or living heart has been read as sulfur or fire, while the three swords are metallic instruments or principles. The image might represent penetration, separation, mortification, or transformation.

Evidence:

- the deck contains furnaces, vessels, armor, heroes, and strange operations;
- Renaissance Hermetic and alchemical interests provide a plausible cultural setting;
- the imagery is unusually systematic.

Against:

- no surviving contemporary key explains the card;
- many figures also support classical, military, genealogical, or moral readings;
- “three swords = three metals” is not uniquely determined.

### Golden Dawn and Thoth

Here the alchemical register is explicit in a general Hermetic sense: the rose of harmony is broken by the central sword, and analysis becomes separation. Crowley’s “womb of Chaos” joins destruction and potential creation.

## Kabbalistic

### Gébelin and Mellet, 1781

Mellet first proposed a systematic relation between twenty-two tarot trumps and twenty-two Hebrew letters. This did not yet place the pips on the ten Sephiroth as Golden Dawn later did.

### Lévi, 1854–1856

Lévi made tarot a “key” to Kabbalah and ceremonial magic. In the consulted English scan he discusses “Clubs, Cups, Swords, and Circles or Pantacles” as a fourfold magical arrangement. [Lévi scan](https://www.hermetics.org/pdf/Eliphas_Levi_Dogma%20et%20Rituel%20de%20la%20Haute%20Magie%201.pdf)

A card-specific quotation for the Three of Swords could not be located because Lévi does not supply a modern seventy-eight-card dictionary. Attributing “Saturn in Libra” to Lévi would be anachronistic.

### Papus, 1889

Papus organizes the suits through the Tetragrammaton and cycles of active/passive transformation. Swords are assigned Vav in his scheme.

In English translation, Papus repeatedly describes tarot as “the oldest book in the world” and associates it with “Bohemians.” Those are occult-historical claims, not results of archival research. [Papus edition](https://silverowltarot.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/07/papus-the-tarot-of-the-bohemians-1892.pdf)

### Golden Dawn

This is the first fully articulated card-specific fusion:

> Three = Binah; Swords = Vav/Air/Yetzirah; second Libra decan = Saturn; result = Lord of Sorrow.

## Astrological

The Golden Dawn assigned the thirty-six cards 2–10 to the thirty-six zodiacal decans. Aces and courts follow different rules.

The Three of Swords is not simply “a Libra card.” It is Saturn operating in one ten-degree sector of Libra. Venus rules Libra, while Saturn is exalted there; their mixture yields restraint, judgment, alienation, or sorrow inside relationship and balance.

**[Modern elaboration]** “Painful but fair consequences,” “relationship karma,” and “the truth that restores balance” are twentieth- or twenty-first-century psychological expansions.

## Hermetic

The sword is the instrument of division and discrimination. Three introduces form or understanding; the mind’s first durable structure arises by separating one thing from another. Hence:

- distinction produces knowledge;
- distinction also wounds unity;
- speech and thought can divide relationships;
- sorrow becomes the cost of consciousness.

This formulation is a synthesis of Golden Dawn Qabalah and modern Hermetic commentary, not a documented medieval meaning.

## Christian

The pierced heart naturally evokes:

- the Sacred Heart;
- the Immaculate Heart of Mary pierced by a sword;
- Simeon’s prophecy to Mary in Luke 2:35;
- Christ’s wounds and instruments of the Passion.

**[Iconographic parallel]** These motifs existed before Sola Busca and provide a plausible cultural background.

**[Disputed direct-source claim]** No document proves that the Sola Busca engraver meant the Three as the Immaculate Heart, nor that Smith consciously referenced Marian imagery. The Immaculate Heart convention of multiple swords, especially seven swords for the Seven Sorrows, makes the resemblance real without proving direct borrowing.

## Jungian

Carl Jung did not publish a canonical meaning for every tarot card.

**[Modern invention/tradition]** Later Jungian tarot authors use the Three for:

- confrontation with the shadow of attachment;
- differentiation of thinking from feeling;
- conscious mourning;
- projection withdrawn after betrayal;
- the wound that breaks an inflated or idealized image;
- integration through admitting pain.

Calling these “Jung’s meaning” is inaccurate. They are applications of Jungian concepts by later tarot practitioners.

# People and works

## Artists, printers, and occultists directly attached

- **Andrea or Bonifacio Bembo and workshops:** attribution of the earliest Milanese luxury cards remains under revision.
- **Nicola di Maestro Antonio:** proposed, not universally accepted, as designer of the Sola Busca engravings.
- **Jean Noblet:** Parisian cardmaker, about 1650.
- **Jean Dodal:** Lyon cardmaker, approximately 1701–1715.
- **Nicolas Conver:** Marseille cardmaker, 1760/1761.
- **Jean-Baptiste Alliette/Etteilla:** first systematic published tarot cartomancer and creator of the first purpose-built occult deck.
- **Éliphas Lévi:** made tarot central to nineteenth-century occult Kabbalah.
- **Stanislas de Guaita and Oswald Wirth:** produced a twenty-two-trump occult deck, not a Three of Swords.
- **S. L. MacGregor Mathers:** transmitted both public cartomantic meanings and Golden Dawn esoteric structures.
- **Pamela Colman Smith:** created the canonical modern pierced-heart image.
- **Arthur Edward Waite:** supplied the deck’s program and published meaning.
- **Jessie Burns Parke and Paul Foster Case:** BOTA adaptation.
- **Frieda Harris and Aleister Crowley:** Thoth abstraction.
- **Robert Wang; Chic and Sandra Tabatha Cicero:** Golden Dawn reconstructions.
- **Philippe Camoin and Alejandro Jodorowsky:** Marseille restoration and modern numerological interpretation.

## T. S. Eliot, *The Waste Land* (1922)

Madame Sosostris names:

- “the drowned Phoenician Sailor”;
- Belladonna;
- “the man with three staves”;
- the Wheel;
- “the one-eyed merchant”;
- a blank card;
- the Hanged Man, which she does not find.

The Three of Swords does not appear. Eliot admitted limited knowledge of the actual pack. Therefore linking the poem specifically to the Three of Swords is unsupported. [Discussion of Eliot and tarot](https://www.nybooks.com/articles/1977/05/12/fortune-hunting/)

## Italo Calvino, *The Castle of Crossed Destinies*

**[Documented fact]** Calvino constructs narratives from sequences of tarot pictures, using Visconti cards in one section and Marseille cards in another.

**Card-specific caution:** the work uses many sword cards, but I found no defensible basis for calling the Three of Swords the governing card of the novel. Its importance is methodological: pip arrangements become rebuses inside a combinatorial narrative.

## W. B. Yeats

Yeats participated in the Golden Dawn and knew its symbolic systems. Tarot imagery belongs to the intellectual atmosphere of his occult practice.

**[Absence of evidence]** No primary Yeats passage located in this research gives a distinctive Three-of-Swords interpretation. General claims that a particular Yeats poem “is based on the Three of Swords” should be treated as modern criticism unless supported by notebooks or drafts.

## Jodorowsky

Jodorowsky uses tarot throughout his writing, performance, psychomagic, and cinema, and coauthored *The Way of Tarot* with Marianne Costa.

**[Documented]** His Marseille Three is not simply Waitean heartbreak; it participates in the developmental series of Threes and the intellectual Sword suit.

**[Disputed/interpretive]** Assertions that a particular film shot is a deliberate quotation of the Three require production documentation or a close compositional match. His films quote tarot broadly, but card-specific claims are often retrospective fan interpretation.

## Ingmar Bergman

*The Seventh Seal* centrally uses chess, Death, medieval plague imagery, and religious doubt.

**[Absence of evidence]** I found no primary or scholarly evidence that Bergman deliberately quoted the Three of Swords. The emotional resemblance of certain images to the card is not enough.

## Paintings and buildings

The pierced-heart emblem has extensive pre-tarot and extra-tarot parallels:

- Marian images of the Seven Sorrows;
- Sacred and Immaculate Heart devotional art;
- heraldic hearts pierced by weapons;
- funerary and memorial imagery;
- tattoo and popular graphic design derived explicitly from Waite–Smith.

**[Open question]** No building could be securely identified as intentionally quoting the tarot Three rather than the much older pierced-heart religious emblem. Attribution requires evidence from the architect, patron, title, or commission.

# Controversies and disputes

## Egyptian origin

### Claim

Gébelin announced in 1781 that tarot was an Egyptian book whose hieroglyphs survived unnoticed in European card play. He described it as ancient wisdom and interpreted its imagery accordingly. [Gébelin primary text](https://ldysinger.com/%40texts2/1776_Court_Masons_Tarot/02_Monde_Primitif.htm)

Etteilla transformed the claim into a deck and divinatory system. Paul Christian later supplied elaborate Egyptian initiatory narratives.

### Evidence

**[Legend]** No ancient Egyptian tarot cards, descriptions, or transmission documents exist.

**[Documented contradiction]**

- tarot appears in fifteenth-century northern Italian records;
- its trumps depict medieval and Renaissance European social and Christian subjects;
- Gébelin wrote before Champollion’s decipherment of Egyptian hieroglyphs;
- his alleged Egyptian etymologies have no linguistic basis.

Dummett aptly notes that the proposed Egyptian derivations cite no hieroglyphic forms. [Dummett, “Origins of Tarot”](https://www.nybooks.com/articles/1981/05/14/origins-of-tarot/)

### Important nuance

European playing cards probably reached Europe through the Islamic world, and Mamluk cards used Cups, Coins, Swords, and polo sticks. This is a genuine Egyptian geographical connection—but to medieval Mamluk Egypt and ordinary playing-card ancestry, not to pharaonic priests or a Book of Thoth.

## Romani or “Gypsy” origin

### Claim

Nineteenth-century writers such as Vaillant, Lévi, and Papus associated tarot preservation or transmission with “Bohemians” or “Gypsies.” Papus’s title *Tarot des Bohémiens* became especially influential.

### Evidence

**[Legend]** The earliest Italian tarot records predate the developed Romani-tarot narrative and do not identify Romani makers or transmitters. No chain connects fifteenth-century Milanese or Ferrarese workshops to Romani custodians of an Egyptian book.

**[Historical context]** Europeans once falsely believed Roma came from Egypt, contributing to the exonym “Gypsy.” Later linguistic and historical research traced Romani origins to the Indian subcontinent.

## Cathar origin

### Claim

Tarot supposedly encodes Cathar doctrine, survived persecution in Languedoc, or conceals dualist theology.

### Evidence

**[Disputed/unsupported]**

- no Cathar manuscript mentions tarot;
- no tarot survives from the Cathar period;
- earliest secure tarot evidence is fifteenth-century northern Italy, long after the Albigensian Crusade;
- Christian imagery in the trumps reflects widespread medieval motifs and does not uniquely indicate Cathar doctrine.

Tarot can be interpreted through dualist symbolism, but that is not evidence of Cathar authorship.

## Templar origin

### Claim

Knights Templar allegedly carried secret Egyptian, Grail, or Kabbalistic teachings into tarot.

### Evidence

**[Legend]**

- the Templar order was suppressed in the early fourteenth century;
- secure tarot evidence begins roughly a century later;
- no Templar inventory, rule, trial record, or esoteric manuscript describes tarot.

Similarities involving towers, popes, knights, or Grail-like cups are generic medieval imagery.

## Alchemical “code”

The Sola Busca almost certainly emerges from a learned, emblematic Renaissance environment; an alchemical reading is plausible.

What remains unproved is a single decipherable code mapping every card to a laboratory operation. A persuasive interpretation must explain inscriptions, historical figures, sequences, suit structure, and the whole deck—not only evocative objects.

## Smith and Sola Busca

### Strong case

- the Sola Busca Three already has a heart pierced by three swords;
- British Museum photographs became available in 1907;
- Smith worked on her deck in 1909;
- several other minor cards show comparable structural borrowing.

### Remaining gap

No known Smith letter or diary states that she used those photographs. “Sola Busca was the source” is a strong reconstruction, not a documented artist’s declaration.

## Was Waite’s image caused by the Golden Dawn title?

Probably in part, but the paths cannot be separated perfectly:

- Etteilla/Mathers supplied removal and rupture;
- Book T supplied “Lord of Sorrow” and destroyed flower;
- Sola Busca supplied pierced heart;
- Smith supplied the monumental storm image.

Calling any one of these the sole source oversimplifies the evidence.

## Is Swords always Air?

No.

- **[Golden Dawn tradition]** Swords = Air.
- Some French occult and modern alternative systems assign Swords to Fire or otherwise rearrange suit elements.
- Historic players assigned no classical element at all.
- The Mamluk Sword suit was a suit sign, not an occult elemental cipher.

Thus “Swords are Air” is standard in Golden Dawn-descended English-language tarot, not an ancient universal.

## Does Three mean creation, or sorrow?

Both occur in different systems.

- Golden Dawn: Three = Binah, formation/Understanding; in Air under Saturn, this becomes sorrow.
- Marseille numerology: Three may be outward growth, creativity, or first expression.
- Waitean image reading: heartache, division, rupture.
- Game: none of these; it is simply the Three.

The apparent contradiction comes from combining systems built for different purposes.

## Reversal as healing

**[Modern invention/tradition]** Many contemporary readers interpret the reversed card as recovery because inversion appears to let the swords fall out.

Waite’s printed reversed meaning is not healing; it is alienation, error, loss, distraction, disorder, and confusion. Healing is a later therapeutic reinterpretation.

## Love triangle and infidelity

**[Modern popular tradition]** Three blades are often treated as three people.

Neither Waite nor Book T makes a love triangle the mandatory meaning. Book T includes discord, deceit, and separation, which can support the reading contextually, but “third-party affair” is not the original fixed definition.

## “Scimitars” in Marseille cards

**[Terminological dispute]** The curved bands are conventionally understood as stylized swords, but some look more like enclosing ribbons with hilts. Calling them scimitars may imply a specific Islamic weapon that the cards do not clearly depict.

## Misprints that became traditions

No card-specific printing error comparable to a securely documented mistaken title was found for the Three of Swords.

Several broader textual instabilities matter:

- Vav/Vau/Waw spelling;
- Hakamiah/Hekamiah transliteration;
- 10°–20° written inclusively or as 11°–20° in popular tables;
- “Lord of Sorrow” shortened to “Sorrow” in Thoth;
- modern Conver reproductions labeled “1760” though based on later impressions;
- “Wirth Three of Swords” falsely implying Wirth created minors;
- reconstructed Visconti cards reproduced without prominent notice that originals are missing.

These are transmission problems, commercial shorthand, or modern additions rather than proved historic printer’s errors.

# Historians’ corrections

## Michael Dummett

Dummett’s central correction was methodological: tarot history must be reconstructed through cards, rule books, archival records, regional games, and dated design families.

His conclusions include:

- tarot originated as a game in northern Italy;
- occult explanations begin centuries later;
- regional trump orders preserve early lines of transmission;
- Tarot de Marseille belongs to a historical design family descended largely from northern Italian patterns and developed in France.

## Ronald Decker, Thierry Depaulis, and Michael Dummett

*A Wicked Pack of Cards* traces the occult tarot from Gébelin through Etteilla, Lévi, Papus, and later occultists. Its argument is not merely “the myths are false”; it reconstructs who invented each link and how later writers inherited it. [Google Books record](https://books.google.com/books?id=nVyDAAAAMAAJ)

## Stuart Kaplan

Kaplan’s encyclopedia assembled deck histories and images on an unprecedented scale. Some older dating and attribution proposals in early volumes have been revised by later scholarship, so “Kaplan says” should not automatically be treated as current consensus.

## Robert M. Place

Place emphasizes late-medieval and Renaissance visual sources, Christian allegory, Petrarchan triumphs, and the history of Western esotericism. His work is often corrective of literal Egyptian-origin stories while remaining sympathetic to symbolic use.

## Helen Farley

Farley treats occult tarot as a historically situated reinvention. She is particularly useful for distinguishing fifteenth-century elite play, literary tarot, eighteenth-century occultism, and modern New Age reception. [Farley, *A Cultural History of Tarot*](https://e-isomero.com/pdf/book/1611846398.pdf)

## Andrea Vitali and Le Tarot association

Vitali’s project documents iconographic antecedents in medieval and Renaissance art. Its strength is comparative imagery; as with any iconological method, resemblance must be distinguished from proof of direct copying. [Le Tarot](https://www.letarot.it/)

# Open questions

1. **Exact earliest surviving object.** A full museum-level comparison of the Cary–Yale and Brambilla numeral inventories is needed to determine which surviving Three was painted first under current dating.

2. **Cary–Yale authorship.** Andrea versus Bonifacio Bembo and workshop participation remain under art-historical review.

3. **Sola Busca program.** No comprehensive interpretation of its heart, heroes, inscriptions, alchemical objects, and sequence has achieved consensus.

4. **Smith’s access.** The British Museum photographs establish opportunity; a personal note by Smith confirming direct consultation has not surfaced.

5. **Division of labor.** It remains unclear how specifically Waite directed individual pip compositions and how much iconographic initiative Smith exercised.

6. **Etteilla first-state wording.** A critical collation of the 1783–1785 *Cahiers*, surviving 1789 cards, and later Grand Etteilla printings is needed to determine the exact earliest form and number of every Three-of-Swords keyword.

7. **Book T dating and authorship.** The system belongs to early Golden Dawn teaching, but individual contributions by Mathers, Westcott, and predecessors are difficult to disentangle.

8. **Decan sources.** Golden Dawn astrology combines traditional decan rulers, magical images, tarot numbers, and Kabbalah. The exact editorial path from Picatrix/Agrippa material to Book T deserves card-by-card textual study.

9. **Marseille floral meaning.** No early cardmaker’s explanation survives. Sexual, alchemical, anatomical, and initiatory readings by modern interpreters cannot presently be privileged as original intent.

10. **Modern cultural quotations.** Many tattoos, album covers, films, installations, and architectural ornaments reproduce a pierced heart, but distinguishing Waite–Smith quotations from Christian or heraldic pierced-heart traditions requires provenance case by case.

---

# Conclusions

The Three of Swords has three historically distinct lives.

First, it was a low numeral card in the Sword suit of an Italian trick-taking game. The earliest Milanese versions were arrangements of actual sword signs. Nothing surviving from the fifteenth century calls the card sorrowful, airy, Saturnine, Kabbalistic, or romantic.

Second, the Sola Busca pack of 1491 gave it an extraordinary emblem: a heart pierced by three swords. That image was exceptional rather than standard. Marseille packs returned to abstract interlaced blades and flowers, while Etteilla gave the card the cartomantic ideas of distance, removal, separation, and error.

Third, the Golden Dawn transformed it into the “Lord of Sorrow,” Saturn in Libra and Binah of Vav in Yetzirah. Pamela Colman Smith fused that title and inherited divinatory vocabulary with the Sola Busca heart, placing it under rain. Crowley and Harris instead developed Book T’s shattered rose into a dark geometric metaphysics of creation, division, and pain.

The familiar modern “heartbreak card” is therefore neither a timeless meaning nor a wholly modern invention. Its picture reaches back to 1491; its published meaning to Etteilla and Mathers; its title and astrology to the Golden Dawn; its canonical form to Smith in 1909; and its therapeutic readings—trauma, acknowledgment, release, and healing—to the later twentieth century.
