# Nine of Swords: Research Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary method

This is a dossier on the **Nine of Swords**, not on the ninth trump. References in the general brief to the Papess, Pope, Tower, Fool, trump-order controversies, Hebrew-letter paths, and the Tzaddi/Heh controversy concern the twenty-two trumps. They are included only where they clarify the history of the deck or prevent a category error.

Evidence labels used throughout:

- **Documented fact** — supported directly by a surviving card, archival record, contemporary text, museum catalogue, or author’s own publication.
- **Scholarly reconstruction** — an inference by modern historians from surviving objects and documents.
- **Tradition** — a convention transmitted within cartomancy or occult tarot but not necessarily historical in origin.
- **Esoteric attribution** — a deliberately constructed correspondence within an occult system.
- **Disputed claim** — asserted in published or popular sources but challenged by competing evidence.
- **Legend** — a historical-origin story unsupported by contemporary documentation.
- **Modern invention** — an interpretation or design first introduced in recent tarot practice.
- **Not traced** — no adequate source for the claim was located.

## Basic identification

| Field | Identification |
|---|---|
| Card | Nine of Swords |
| Italian | **Nove di Spade** |
| French | **Neuf d’Épées** |
| Spanish | **Nueve de Espadas** |
| German | **Neun der Schwerter**, more idiomatically **Neun Schwerter** or **Schwerter Neun** |
| English | **Nine of Swords** |
| Playing-card analogue | Italian/Spanish *Spade/Espadas*; French-suited **Piques/Spades** by conventional cartomantic equivalence |
| Number | 9; early Italian cards may use an Arabic 9 or no numeral; Marseille packs normally show Roman **VIIII**, sometimes IX in nonstandard or modern editions |
| Category | Numeral or “pip” card, one of ten numeral cards in the suit of Swords |
| Historical function | Ordinary suit card in a tarot-family trick-taking game |

| Earliest surviving fully scenic Nine | Sola Busca, engraved in the late fifteenth century and painted in 1491 or shortly thereafter |
| Golden Dawn title | **Lord of Despair and Cruelty** |

| Golden Dawn Qabalah | **Yesod in Yetzirah**: the ninth sephirah expressed in the world/suit of Air |
| Tetragrammaton | Swords = **Vav/Vau (ו)** in Yod-Heh-Vav-Heh |
| Thoth title | **Cruelty** |

The Morgan identifies a normal fifteenth-century tarot pack as twenty-one trumps, the Fool, and four fourteen-card suits, each containing ten numeral cards plus page, knight, queen, and king. It also emphasizes that these cards were made for play, not originally as occult objects. [Morgan Library, Visconti-Sforza catalogue](https://www.themorgan.org/manuscript/158525)

### Earliest-example qualification

**Documented fact:** The 1442 Ferrara account is earlier than any securely dated surviving Nine of Swords. It reads, in part:

> “cholorido e depento le chope e le spade e li dinari e li bastoni…”

That is, Sagramoro was paid for coloring and painting “the cups, the swords, the coins and the batons” and all the figures of four triumph packs. The ledger proves that Swords numeral cards existed in tarot packs by 1442, but it does not itemize the Nine. [Transcription and archival reference](https://www.oocities.org/autorbis/ferrara1442.html)

**Scholarly reconstruction:** The Visconti-Sforza Nine is therefore the earliest presently reconstructable surviving specimen of the card itself. Its exact dating and workshop attribution remain debated within a relatively narrow mid-fifteenth-century range.

# Iconography deck by deck

## Milanese and Ferrarese beginnings

### Marziano da Tortona/Michelino da Besozzo pack, before 1425

**Documented fact:** Marziano described a pack made for Filippo Maria Visconti containing sixteen deified heroes in four moral categories. Jacopo Antonio Marcello later called it a novel kind of *ludus triumphorum*.

**Not applicable:** This was not a standard seventy-eight-card tarot with the four later suits. No Nine of Swords is described or known. It is a precursor in the history of cards with trumps, not the earliest specimen of this card.

### Visconti di Modrone/Cary-Yale, about 1441–1447

**Documented fact:** This incomplete luxury pack has unusually expanded court cards and extra theological-virtue trumps. It is traditionally called the Cary-Yale or Visconti di Modrone tarot.

**Not traced:** No surviving Nine of Swords from this pack could be verified in the accessible Yale inventory. It should not be illustrated or described by silently substituting a reconstruction.

**Significance:** Its structure shows that the tarot pack had not yet become completely standardized. The absence of this particular card is an accident of survival, not evidence that the rank did not exist.

### Brera-Brambilla tarot, about 1442–1445

**Documented fact:** This Milanese pack survives only in part. Most surviving numeral cards are Coins, with only two surviving trumps.

**Not extant:** No Nine of Swords survives. Accordingly, claims about its precise arrangement are reconstruction.

### Visconti-Sforza tarot, about 1450–1460

**Documented fact:** The combined Morgan, Accademia Carrara, and Colleoni holdings contain seventy-four original cards from a probable seventy-eight-card pack. Only the Devil, Tower, Three of Swords, and Knight of Coins are missing. Consequently, the Nine of Swords is extant. The Morgan dates its portion to Milan, about 1450–1480, while newer exhibition work favors roughly 1456–58 for the principal production. [Morgan collection overview](https://www.themorgan.org/collection/tarot-cards), [Morgan manuscript record](https://www.themorgan.org/manuscript/158525)

**What is depicted:** Nine long, curved swords form an ornamental, interlaced field. As in other early Italian-suited packs, the weapons are suit emblems, not weapons acting in a narrative. Floral and heraldic decoration fills the ground. There is no bed, suffering figure, darkness, or explicit moral drama.

**Meaning at the time:** No contemporary divinatory meaning is documented. The imagery identifies suit and rank and displays aristocratic craftsmanship. The Morgan describes the pack as a courtly game and warns that occult readings came centuries later.

**Terminological caution:** Describing the weapons as “scimitars” is visually understandable but historically imprecise. They are highly curved conventionalized swords whose interlacing creates the characteristic Italian/French tarot pip pattern.

### Este/Ferrarese fragments, later fifteenth century

**Documented fact:** Ferrarese cards and fragments survive in several collections, often catalogued under Este or Charles VI labels. Attribution, grouping, and exact dating have repeatedly changed.

**Not traced:** No securely attributable Este Nine of Swords was located. General descriptions of the Ferrarese style cannot be transferred to a nonexistent card.

### Mantegna “Tarocchi,” about 1460s

**Documented fact:** The so-called Mantegna Tarocchi are fifty didactic engravings arranged in five alphabetical groups. They are not a tarot pack and contain no four suits.

**Not applicable:** There is no Nine of Swords. The traditional name “tarocchi” is a later bibliographic misnomer.

### Sola Busca, engraving late fifteenth century; painted pack dated 1491

**Documented fact:** The Sola Busca is the earliest substantially complete engraved tarot and the earliest complete surviving pack with individually illustrated numeral cards. The painted set is in the Pinacoteca di Brera. A painted inscription is generally read as yielding 1491, though an alternative calculation produces 1523. The British Museum calls 1491 the more likely date. [British Museum Sola Busca record](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/P_1845-0825-485)

**What is depicted:** Unlike the ornamental Visconti and Marseille pips, the Nine presents a human/alchemical-looking episode incorporating nine swords. Modern alchemical readings describe an older figure and a putto or youthful figure associated with a furnace or vessel.

**Disputed interpretation:** The Brera exhibition scholarship associates the pack broadly with Renaissance hermetic, metallurgical, and alchemical culture. Exact “operation-by-operation” assignments—such as identifying this card specifically as fermentation or *rubedo*—are modern reconstructions rather than labels written on the card.

### Did the Sola Busca Nine inspire Pamela Colman Smith’s Nine?

**Finding: unsubstantiated.**

The user’s item-specific lead—“the Sola Busca pip (1491) as the source of Pamela Colman Smith’s scene”—does not survive close checking in that card-specific form.

What can be established is:

1. **Documented fact:** In 1907 the Busca-Serbelloni family supplied the British Museum with photographs of the complete pack, shortly before Smith drew her deck.
2. **Scholarly reconstruction:** Smith probably saw or knew these photographs.
3. **Strong visual evidence:** Her Three of Swords resembles the Sola Busca Three; the burdened posture of her Ten of Wands closely resembles the figure on the Sola Busca Ten of Swords.
4. **Absence of evidence:** The Sola Busca Nine does not contain Smith’s recognizable bedroom, waking sleeper, nine horizontal wall-mounted swords, quilt, or carved bed.
5. **Conclusion:** Sola Busca was probably an important source for Smith’s decision to make the pips scenic, but no adequate evidence was located that its **Nine** supplied her **Nine’s scene**.

The distinction matters: a deck-level source is not automatically a card-by-card source.

## Early printed numeral patterns

### Rosenwald sheets, late fifteenth or early sixteenth century

**Documented fact:** These uncut Italian sheets preserve early printed tarot imagery.

**What the Swords pips show:** Repeated curved sword emblems arranged decoratively, not narrative scenes. Odd-numbered cards characteristically contain a central sword surrounded by paired arcs.

**Caution:** Attribution and dating of the sheets have varied. No occult meaning is documented.

### Budapest sheets, late fifteenth/early sixteenth century

**Documented fact:** These are further uncut early tarot sheets, grouped by historians according to woodblock style.

**What changed:** Numerals and suit objects became reproducible woodcut patterns. The Nine remained a pip configuration; it was not yet the “nightmare card.”

**Not traced:** No contemporary explanation of its ornament has survived.

## Regional playing-card traditions

### Tarot de Marseille lineage

The name *Tarot de Marseille* is retrospective as a pattern label; seventeenth-century makers did not necessarily market their cards under that fixed historical title.

#### Jean Noblet, Paris, circa 1659

The BnF dates the complete Noblet pack to about 1659 and identifies it as a woodcut, stencil-colored, seventy-eight-card Italian-suited tarot. [BnF catalogue](https://catalogue.bnf.fr/ark:/12148/cb43634904c)

**What is depicted:** Four pairs of long curved swords interlace to make a pointed oval or mandorla. A ninth, straighter sword occupies the center. Leaves, flowers, and small ornamental forms fill the openings. The rank is conveyed primarily by counting; later restorations may add or regularize numerals.

**Meaning:** No scene and no documented card-specific allegory. The floral material participates in the visual grammar of the suit and helps fill the negative space created by the blades.

#### Jean Dodal, Lyon, about 1701–1715

**What is depicted:** The same essential odd-card structure: eight curved blades in paired arcs and a central ninth blade, with floral infill. Line, color, flowers, and the exact crossing points differ from Noblet.

**Documented function:** A playing-card pip.

#### Nicolas Conver, Marseille, 1760

**What is depicted:** A highly regularized Marseille Type II composition. Eight curved swords create an interlaced enclosure; the ninth stands vertically through the middle. Red, blue, yellow, black, and uncolored paper articulate blades, flowers, and leaves.

**What changed:** The composition became cleaner and more symmetrical, supplying the model for numerous nineteenth- and twentieth-century Marseille editions. A complete searchable card sequence is available in the Conver reproduction catalogue. [Conver deck record](https://etteilla.org/en/deck/41/tarot-de-marseille-by-nicolas-conver)

#### Grimaud/Paul Marteau, 1930

**Documented fact:** Grimaud’s *Ancien Tarot de Marseille*, edited by Paul Marteau, popularized a standardized Conver-derived design and restrictive color palette.

**What changed:** Colors and linework were normalized for industrial printing. Marteau subsequently assigned symbolic meanings to colors and ornamental details. Those meanings are twentieth-century esoteric interpretation, not evidence of what Conver or Noblet intended.

#### Camoin–Jodorowsky, 1997

**Documented fact:** Philippe Camoin and Alejandro Jodorowsky issued a reconstruction advertised as recovering a primordial or authentic Marseille symbolism.

**What changed:** Details and colors were assembled from several historical exemplars and supplemented by editorial judgments.

**Disputed claim:** It is a modern synthetic reconstruction, not a facsimile of a single archetypal original. Jodorowsky’s numerological reading of the Nine as crisis, gestation, or approach to completion belongs to his and Marianne Costa’s interpretive system.

### Besançon pattern

**Documented fact:** Besançon packs commonly replace the Papess and Pope with **Juno and Jupiter**, a change found in eighteenth-century French, Swiss, and German-border production. That regional renaming concerns trumps II and V, not the Nine.

**Nine of Swords:** Ordinarily retains the interlaced French/Italian-suited pip construction. It was not renamed “Juno” or otherwise transformed.

### Minchiate

**Documented fact:** Minchiate expanded the trump series to forty trump-like cards plus the Fool while retaining four ordinary suits.

**Nine of Swords:** A numeral card in the sword suit, generally rendered as a decorative arrangement of sword emblems.

**Game significance:** It is a low, non-counting card. It is not the ninth trump, and the expanded Minchiate trump structure supplies no special occult identity to it.

### Bolognese tarocchino

**Documented fact:** The Bolognese pack was shortened by removing lower numeral cards. Traditional tarocchino retains the higher pips; the Nine of Swords survives.

**Iconography:** An ornamental Italian-suited Sword pip, not a Waite-style scene.

**Game:** Its trick rank depends on suit order and local rules; it normally carries no intrinsic counting value.

### Sicilian tarot

**Documented fact:** The Sicilian pattern developed its own shortened pack, trump subjects, and card values.

**Nine:** Where the Swords sequence includes it, it is a playing-card numeral. Sicilian tarocchi may award special values to certain trumps, but no evidence was found for a special point value attached to the Nine of Swords.

## Cartomantic and occult packs

### Etteilla’s Grand Etteilla, first issued in parts about 1788–1789

**Documented fact:** Jean-Baptiste Alliette, called Etteilla, produced the first tarot designed expressly as a complete occult/cartomantic instrument. Its ordering and imagery differ substantially from Marseille.

**Nine of Swords meanings:** A nineteenth-century *Grand Etteilla* manual states:

> “Cette carte représente le neuf d’épée; elle signifie, religion, conscience…”

“This card represents the nine of swords; it signifies religion, conscience.” It predicts attendance at a religious ceremony, modified by neighboring cards. The same entry gives the maxim *La défiance est la mère d’assurance*—“Mistrust is the mother of security.” [Scanned *Le Grand Etteilla* manual](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/2/2f/Le_grand_Etteilla%2C_ou%2C_l%27art_de_tirer_les_cartes_..._Le_tout_recueilli_..._%28IA_b29321220%29.pdf)

**Important qualification:** This accessible manual is later than Etteilla’s original 1780s publications. It documents the Etteillist tradition but does not prove every phrase appeared on the first 1789 printing.

**What changed:** The pip became an explicitly labeled oracle card with separate upright and reversed or alternate meanings. That is a major transition from anonymous playing-card ornament to printed cartomantic semantics.

### Oswald Wirth, 1889

**Documented fact:** Wirth’s 1889 deck comprised twenty-two trumps.

**Not applicable:** It has no Nine of Swords. Later commercial “Wirth” seventy-eight-card decks add minors not drawn in the original project.

### Golden Dawn manuscript designs, circa 1888–1900

**Documented fact:** *Book T* names the card **Lord of Despair and Cruelty**, assigns it to Mars in Gemini, and describes a talismanic arrangement:

- four hands hold eight swords;
- a fifth holds the ninth upright in the center;
- the blades separate or destroy the central rose;
- the symbols of Mars and Gemini appear above and below.

The surviving *Book T* table explicitly gives “Gemini — Despair and Cruelty — 9 of Swords.” [Book T scan](https://benebellwen.com/wp-content/uploads/2013/02/mathers-and-felkin-golden-dawn-book-t-the-tarot-1888.pdf)

**Meanings in Book T:** despair, cruelty, pitilessness, malice, suffering, want, loss, misery, burden, oppression, labor, subtlety, dishonesty, lying, and slander; but under good dignity, obedience, faithfulness, patience, and unselfishness.

**Reconstruction caveat:** Decks painted by Robert Wang and by Sandra Tabatha and Chic Cicero are later visual realizations of Golden Dawn manuscripts. They are not surviving nineteenth-century order-issued packs. Where they differ in hands, roses, colors, and lettering, they represent reconstruction decisions.

### Waite–Smith, drawn 1909; guide published 1910/1911

**Documented fact:** Arthur Edward Waite conceived the deck, Pamela Colman Smith drew it, and William Rider published it. Its fully scenic minor cards became the dominant English-language tarot model.

**What is depicted:**

- a person sits upright in a bed;
- the face is buried in both hands;
- nine swords run horizontally across a black wall;
- a carved bed panel shows one figure overcoming or attacking another;
- the coverlet contains roses and alternating astrological-looking or heraldic figures.

Waite’s description is:

> “One seated on her couch in lamentation, with the swords over her.”

His divinatory list is:

> “Death, failure, miscarriage, delay, deception, disappointment, despair.”

Reversed:

> “Imprisonment, suspicion, doubt, reasonable fear, shame.”

[Pictorial Key scan](https://www.integralpsychology.org/uploads/1/5/3/0/15300482/waite_tarot.pdf)

**Gender ambiguity:** Waite’s “her” is documentary evidence for his reading, but the printed figure has often been perceived as male or androgynous. Assertions that it depicts a particular real person are unsubstantiated.

**Sources of the design:**

- **Documented/esoteric source:** Golden Dawn title and meanings.
- **Probable source:** established visual vocabulary of lamentation, waking from nightmare, and devotional sorrow.
- **Probable deck-level source:** Sola Busca’s scenic pips.
- **Not established:** direct copying from the Sola Busca Nine.
- **Modern interpretation:** the swords as thoughts “hung on the wall” and therefore self-created anxiety. This is compatible with the image but is not stated in Waite’s short description.

### Paul Foster Case/BOTA

**Documented fact:** The physical BOTA deck designed by Paul Foster Case and Jessie Burns Parke is principally a twenty-two-trump teaching deck. It does not supply a canonical BOTA picture for the Nine comparable to Smith’s.

Case nevertheless interpreted the minor cards in *The Oracle of the Tarot*. For the Nine he gives:

- Yesod/Moon symbolism through a crescent and ankh;
- second decan of Gemini;
- the keyword **Worry**;
- well-dignified faithfulness, obedience, unselfishness, patience, eventual good fortune after uncertainty;
- ill-dignified despair, cruelty, unfaithfulness, want, loss, and misery.

The accessible edition surprisingly assigns the decan to **Venus**, not Mars. [Case, *Oracle of the Tarot*, p. 35](https://media-01.imu.nl/storage/divona.be/17547/oracle-of-tarot-paul-foster-case.pdf)

**Conflict:** Mars is the standard Golden Dawn/Thoth ruler. “Venus” may be Case’s intentional alteration, an editorial corruption, or a transmission error. It should not be silently normalized to Mars.

### Thoth, painted 1938–1943; first complete commercial publication 1969

**Documented fact:** Aleister Crowley designed the deck with Lady Frieda Harris, who painted it. Crowley titles the card **CRUELTY**.

**What is depicted:** Nine dark, unequal, jagged swords descend toward a common lower point. Their blades appear rusted and damaged; drops of poison and blood fall from them. The geometry replaces Smith’s bedroom with an impersonal mechanism of violence. Mars and Gemini glyphs identify the decan.

Crowley writes:

> “The symbol shows nine swords of varying lengths, all striking downwards to a point.”

He continues that they are “jagged and rusty” and that poison and blood drip from the blades. He describes Mars in Gemini as:

> “crude rage of hunger operating without restraint.”

Crowley, *The Book of Thoth*, Part IV, p. 208 in the linked scan. [Book of Thoth PDF](https://www.rahoorkhuit.net/library/crowley/equinox/volume3/vol_3_no_05.pdf)

**Meaning:** Crowley turns the Golden Dawn’s moral list into a psychological theory of intellect degenerating into “heartless passions,” fanaticism, and cruelty. Passive resistance, martyrdom, and revenge remain possible responses.

### Notable modern redesigns

**Documented trend:** Most modern English-language decks derive from either:

1. **Marseille:** ornamental nine blades;
2. **Waite–Smith:** a person suffering from insomnia, guilt, fear, or nightmare;
3. **Thoth:** geometric or abstract mental cruelty.

Common modern transformations include:

- explicit depictions of anxiety or depression;
- nightmares emerging physically around the sleeper;
- self-directed blades or accusatory words;
- release from anxiety when reversed;
- inclusive or ungendered sufferers;
- therapeutic imagery about intrusive thoughts.

These are modern developments. They should not be projected backward onto fifteenth-century playing cards.

# The game

## Rank

The Nine of Swords is a **plain suit card**, not a trump.

In most tarot-family games:

- the King is highest in every ordinary suit;
- Queen, Knight, and Jack follow;
- numeral ordering may run either upward or downward depending on suit and regional rule.

In many traditional Italian and French games, Swords and Batons rank:

**King, Queen, Knight, Jack, 10, 9, 8 … Ace**

while Cups and Coins reverse the numeral sequence:

**King, Queen, Knight, Jack, Ace, 2, 3 … 10**

Thus the Nine of Swords is usually the second-highest numeral in its suit, below the Ten and above the Eight. Rules vary, especially in shortened tarock packs and modern national games.

## Point value

**Documented general rule:** Numeral cards are normally “empty” or low cards. In the familiar grouped counting system, each is nominally one point but contributes no surplus value when cards are counted in groups. Pagat describes the common schedule:

- Kings and the three principal tarots: 5;
- Queens: 4;
- Knights: 3;
- Jacks: 2;
- other trumps and numeral cards: nominally 1.

[Pagat, tarot counting](https://www.pagat.com/tarot/counting.html)

Therefore the Nine of Swords is valuable mainly because it may win a suit trick, not because it is a counting card.

## Trump-order controversies do not determine this card’s identity

Historians group early Italian trump orders broadly into regional A, B, and C families—associated approximately with Bologna/Florence, Ferrara, and Milan. These disputes concern the sequence of allegorical trumps, including Justice, Fortitude, the virtues, Pope/Papess, and cosmic cards.

They do **not** renumber the Nine of Swords. Its “nine” is the number of suit emblems, not its position among trumps.

## The Fool

The Fool was normally unnumbered and had a special role, often excused from following suit and returned to its player in exchange for another captured card. Its exceptional operation has no special relationship to the Nine of Swords.

## Earliest rules

**Documented fact:** Early fifteenth-century records name and purchase tarot packs but do not preserve full rules. The earliest detailed printed rules known to historians are much later, in the seventeenth century. Accordingly, the exact trick rank of the Nine in the 1442 Ferrara packs cannot be proved directly.

# Chronology of attestation

### Before 1425 — Marziano da Tortona

Marziano’s sixteen-god pack is an early documented game with permanent trumps. It lacks a standard Nine of Swords.

### 1440–1442 — emergence of *trionfi*

Recent documentary discoveries place references to *trionfi* around 1440. The securely transcribed Ferrara payment of 10 February 1442 explicitly names Swords among the four suits.

### 1440s — Cary-Yale and Brambilla packs

Luxury Milanese packs establish the coexistence of suit cards and allegorical trumps. No relevant Nine survives in these fragments.

### About 1450–1460 — Visconti-Sforza Nine

The earliest surviving reconstructable Nine of Swords: nine curved swords arranged as an ornamental pip.

### About 1470–1500 — *Sermones de ludo cum aliis*

The anonymous sermon gives the earliest known complete trump list and condemns the game as diabolical gambling. It lists the twenty-one trumps but does not name individual pip cards. Vanderbilt’s manuscript catalogue describes its order as the Ferrarese or B order. [Vanderbilt manuscript description](https://collections.library.vanderbilt.edu/repositories/2/archival_objects/261035)

### 1491 — painted Sola Busca

The earliest surviving fully scenic treatment of the card. Its imagery later becomes relevant to occult and alchemical reconstruction, though no contemporary key survives.

### Early sixteenth century — literary uses

Poems and *tarocchi appropriati* assign cards to people or use tarot vocabulary socially and satirically. Teofilo Folengo’s literary tarot material demonstrates cultural familiarity with the cards.

**Absence finding:** No securely sourced pre-eighteenth-century poem giving the Nine of Swords its later meaning of nightmares or despair was found.

### Seventeenth century — Marseille-pattern stabilization

Noblet, Dodal, and related makers preserve the interlaced-sword pip. The card remains visually non-narrative.

### 1781 — Court de Gébelin and Comte de Mellet

Court de Gébelin identifies tarot as an Egyptian Book of Thoth. De Mellet develops letter correspondences and a divinatory reading method.

**Critical limitation:** Their systems center on the trumps. They do not establish the later Golden Dawn identity of the Nine as Mars in Gemini.

### 1780s–1790s — Etteilla

The Nine becomes an explicitly interpretable oracle: religion, conscience, ceremony, suspicion or mistrust. This is the earliest securely traceable systematic cartomantic layer for the individual card.

### 1854–1856 — Éliphas Lévi, *Dogme et rituel de la haute magie*

Lévi links twenty-two trumps with Hebrew letters and Kabbalistic doctrine.

**Absence finding:** Lévi does not supply the later Golden Dawn decan title for the Nine of Swords. Attributing “Despair and Cruelty” to Lévi is incorrect.

### 1870s — Paul Christian

Christian expands Egyptian initiatory mythology around the twenty-two arcana.

**Not applicable to the pip:** No historically influential individual Nine-of-Swords correspondence was traced to him.

### 1888 — Mathers and the Golden Dawn period

Mathers publishes a divinatory tarot booklet; the Golden Dawn develops *Book T*. The decisive modern change is the assignment:

**Nine of Swords = Yesod of Air/Yetzirah = Mars in Gemini = Lord of Despair and Cruelty.**

### 1889 — Papus and Wirth

Papus systematizes tarot through Tetragrammaton, number cycles, suits, and Hebrew-letter speculation. Wirth publishes twenty-two trumps only.

### 1909–1911 — Waite–Smith

Smith transforms the pip into the now-iconic scene of nocturnal lamentation.

### 1912 — public dissemination of Golden Dawn minor-card material

Crowley publishes tarot material derived from *Book T* in *The Equinox*. The Golden Dawn titles become accessible outside the order.

### 1930 — Grimaud/Marteau

The Marseille pip is standardized for mass circulation and receives modern color-symbolic interpretation.

### 1930s — Paul Foster Case

Case preserves much Golden Dawn structure but emphasizes worry and delayed favorable outcomes; the accessible text anomalously gives Venus rather than Mars.

### 1938–1943; 1969 onward — Thoth

Harris and Crowley turn “Despair and Cruelty” into the abstract, blood- and poison-dripping **Cruelty** design.

### 1970s–1980s — psychological tarot

Rachel Pollack and other writers develop the Waite image into a psychology of isolation, guilt, fear, and suffering intensified by the mind.

### 1997 — Camoin–Jodorowsky

The Marseille arrangement is redrawn within Jodorowsky’s numerological, therapeutic, and “restoration” program.

### 2002 — Mary K. Greer

Greer’s systematic treatment of reversals enables the inverted Nine to mean release, internalization, exaggeration, blockage, or the opposite of the upright condition rather than one fixed prediction.

| Pollack | 1980s | Mental anguish, guilt, isolation; possibility that suffering is generated or magnified internally | Jungian/psychological extension of Smith |
| Greer | 2002 | Reversal can be opposite, blocked, excessive, delayed, internalized, or emerging | Rejects one mechanically fixed reversed meaning |
| Popular modern RWS | late 20th–21st century | Anxiety, insomnia, nightmares, intrusive thoughts; reversed release/recovery | “Recovery” conflicts with Waite’s own darker reversal |

## Suit: Swords, Air, and Vav

**Historical fact:** Swords are one of the Italian suits. Their conventional equivalence to French **Piques/Spades** developed through cartomantic comparison, not because the visual suit signs are identical.

**Esoteric attribution:** Golden Dawn and related systems give:

- Wands — Fire — Yod — Atziluth
- Cups — Water — first Heh — Briah
- Swords — Air — Vav — Yetzirah
- Pentacles/Coins — Earth — final Heh — Assiah

The Golden Dawn tables explicitly associate Swords with Yetzirah. [Regardie compilation](https://www.labirintoermetico.com/06Numerologia_Cabala/I.Regardie_Complete_Golden_Dawn_%28II%20ed.deluxe%29.pdf)

**Conflict:** Some earlier and independent occultists exchanged the elemental assignments of Swords and Wands. Air/Swords is not an ancient Italian doctrine; it is an occult convention consolidated by the Golden Dawn.

## The number Nine and Yesod

**Esoteric attribution:** In the Golden Dawn’s four-world scheme, every Nine corresponds to the ninth sephirah, **Yesod**, “Foundation.” The Nine of Swords is therefore Yesod as expressed in Yetzirah/Air.

Traditional Golden Dawn color tables give Yesod:

- King scale: indigo;
- Queen scale: violet;
- Prince scale: very dark purple;
- Princess scale: citrine flecked azure.

[Golden Dawn color table](https://www.lelandra.com/tarotbook/TreeofLifeColors.htm)

**Interpretive development:** Later writers connect Yesod with the Moon, dreams, the astral realm, sexuality, imagination, and subconscious formation. Applied to Smith’s bed scene, this produces the familiar association with nightmare and psychic distress.

**Historical caution:** Neither the fifteenth-century Visconti artist nor Conver is known to have designed the Nine around Yesod.

## Mars in Gemini

**Documented esoteric attribution:** *Book T* assigns the second decan of Gemini to Mars and the Nine of Swords.

**Traditional date span:** approximately 1–10 June, though exact ingress dates vary annually and occult tables differ by whether 10° or 11° is treated as the beginning.

**Interpretation:**

- Mars — aggression, cutting, force, conflict;
- Gemini — speech, thought, multiplicity, nervous movement;
- synthesis — hostile thought, verbal cruelty, agitation, obsessive mental conflict.

This synthesis is an occult interpretation of the astrological table, not the historical origin of the card.

## Hebrew letters and the Tzaddi/Heh issue

**Category correction:** Minor pips do not individually receive one of the twenty-two Hebrew letters. The Nine receives Vav only through its **suit’s** place in the Tetragrammaton.

Crowley’s “Tzaddi is not the Star” exchange between the Emperor and Star concerns trumps IV and XVII. It does not alter the Nine of Swords, its decan, or its Yesod/Vav attribution.

### Important reversal conflict

Popular modern sources often say the reversed Nine means recovery, daylight, or the end of anxiety. That is **not Waite’s reversal**, which remains ominous: imprisonment, suspicion, doubt, fear, and shame.

The optimistic reversal is a later development produced by reading reversal as the opposite or release of the upright image. Greer’s system explains how that reading can be legitimate without pretending it is Waite’s wording.

# Esoteric and symbolic readings

## Alchemical

### Sola Busca

**Scholarly reconstruction/disputed:** Modern Brera-associated scholarship reads the Sola Busca’s furnaces, vessels, tools, bodies, and substances in relation to Renaissance metallurgy and alchemy. Some interpreters identify its Nine of Swords with fermentation or the transition toward *rubedo*.

**Evidence:** The visual apparatus and the pack’s broader learned culture support an alchemical context.

**Limit:** No surviving fifteenth-century key says “Nine of Swords = fermentation.” Precise stage assignments remain reconstruction.

### Thoth

**Esoteric attribution:** Rust, broken blades, blood, poison, and the destruction of the rose describe corrupted Air or intellect. This resembles an alchemical state of putrefaction, but Crowley’s own treatment is primarily Qabalistic and astrological, not a formal laboratory operation.

## Kabbalistic

**Golden Dawn:** Yesod in Yetzirah. Foundation receives the Sword/Air current. Ideally Yesod restores energy to the middle pillar; in this suit it becomes automatic, imaginal, and compulsive mental activity.

Crowley writes:

> “The number Nine, Yesod, brings back the Energy to the central pillar of the Tree of Life.”

The apparent contradiction—Yesod restoring order while the card remains cruel—is resolved by Crowley through the degeneration of the suit: mental form has become detached from humane reason.

## Astrological

Mars in Gemini was first firmly attached to this card within the Golden Dawn’s decan system. It was spread by *Book T*, Waite’s imagery, Crowley’s publications, later Golden Dawn reconstructions, and modern decan-based tarot.

It is not found in the Visconti, Marseille, or Etteilla imagery.

## Hermetic

The card becomes a Hermetic composite only in the nineteenth century:

- number 9/Yesod;
- world of Yetzirah;
- element Air;
- Vav;
- Mars;
- Gemini;
- a pair of ruling decan angels in Golden Dawn tables;
- geometric weapon symbolism.

Calling that synthesis “ancient Hermetic tarot” is a historical overstatement. It is a modern occult construction using older astrological and Kabbalistic materials.

## Christian

### Historical card

No explicitly Christian subject appears on the early Nine itself, although the decks were made in Christian societies and their trump series included virtues, ecclesiastics, Judgment, and other Christian imagery.

### Etteilla/Mathers

Religion, conscience, priesthood, ceremony, monastic life, and shame enter the card’s written divinatory tradition through Etteillist lists.

### Waite

Smith’s posture resembles lamentation, penitence, or anguished prayer. Roses on the quilt can support devotional interpretation.

**Caution:** Waite does not explicitly identify the figure as praying, Mary Magdalene, a penitent, or a biblical character. Those are later readings.

## Jungian and psychological

**Modern interpretive tradition:** The Waite-Smith figure is treated as an image of:

- anxiety and insomnia;
- guilt or shame;
- intrusive thoughts;
- catastrophizing;
- trauma replay;
- self-criticism;
- the “shadow” made conscious;
- a threshold at which psychic pain must be acknowledged.

Pollack and later writers helped move tarot reading from event prediction toward inner process. The scene’s enclosure, absence of an external attacker, and covered face make it especially adaptable to depth psychology.

**Historical caution:** Carl Jung did not write a canonical interpretation of the Nine of Swords. “Jungian Nine of Swords” readings are applications of Jungian concepts by later tarot authors.

# People and works

## Pamela Colman Smith

Smith is the single most consequential artist for the modern identity of this card. She converted an ornamental numeral into a recognizable human situation. Her authorship is now acknowledged in the preferred name **Rider–Waite–Smith** or **Waite–Smith**.

## Arthur Edward Waite

Waite supplied the occult and cartomantic framework, but his published description is very short. Claims that every quilt symbol and bed carving came from a detailed written instruction by Waite are unproved.

## Lady Frieda Harris and Aleister Crowley

Harris translated Crowley’s correspondences into an abstract, dynamic design. The Nine demonstrates her importance: the effect comes as much from geometry, descending rhythm, red-black color, and damaged texture as from Crowley’s prose.

## W. B. Yeats

**Documented association:** Yeats was an important Golden Dawn member and knew Smith.

**Absence finding:** No securely sourced Yeats poem or prose passage centered specifically on the Nine of Swords was found. General claims that it is “Yeats’s nightmare card” are unsupported.

## T. S. Eliot, *The Waste Land* (1922)

Madame Sosostris lays out an invented and altered tarot sequence, including “the drowned Phoenician Sailor,” Belladonna, the man with three staves, the Wheel, and the one-eyed merchant.

**Absence finding:** The Nine of Swords is not named. Eliot is important to modern literary tarot but not directly attached to this card.

## Italo Calvino, *The Castle of Crossed Destinies*

**Documented literary use:** Calvino constructs stories from laid-out tarot cards, using both a Visconti-style deck and a Marseille deck. In the Italian text, the **Nove di Spade** appears as part of the visual syntax of siege, encirclement, danger, and hoped-for military intervention.

This is one of the clearest literary appearances of the specific card. The passage is not a traditional divinatory definition; Calvino uses the swords composition as a narrative image.

## Alejandro Jodorowsky

Jodorowsky’s engagement is primarily with Marseille tarot, psychomagic, and his collaboration with Philippe Camoin. His and Marianne Costa’s *The Way of Tarot* reads numbers and suits developmentally.

**Absence finding:** No film by Jodorowsky was verified as directly quoting the Nine of Swords image. Broad similarities between nightmare sequences in his films and Smith’s card are interpretive.

## Ingmar Bergman

*The Seventh Seal* is frequently juxtaposed with tarot because of Death, gaming, medieval plague imagery, and existential symbolism.

**Absence finding:** Its chess game is not a quotation of the Nine of Swords, and no direct card-specific source was established.

## Visual art, buildings, and quotations

Tarot has influenced artists including Leonora Carrington, Niki de Saint Phalle, Betye Saar, Remedios Varo, and contemporary artists surveyed by the Morgan. [Morgan exhibition overview](https://www.themorgan.org/exhibitions/tarot)

**Absence finding:** No building or major pre-1909 painting could be securely identified as quoting the Nine of Swords specifically. Similar images of lamentation, sleeplessness, or rows of weapons are possible visual precedents, but resemblance alone does not demonstrate quotation.

# Controversies and disputes

## Egyptian origin

### Claim

Court de Gébelin announced in 1781 that tarot preserved an Egyptian Book of Thoth. Etteilla transformed that proposal into an operative occult deck.

### Evidence status

**Legend/modern eighteenth-century invention.** Court offered comparative symbolism and speculative etymology, not a transmission document. He wrote before Egyptian hieroglyphs had been deciphered.

### Historical correction

Surviving cards and records place tarot’s emergence in fifteenth-century northern Italy. The Morgan explicitly describes Court’s astrological/Egyptian account as scholarly misinformation. [Morgan catalogue](https://www.themorgan.org/manuscript/158525), [Morgan on occult tarot](https://www.themorgan.org/exhibitions/online/tarot/11)

Dummett argued that no occult use is documented before Court de Gébelin’s intervention in 1781. [Dummett, “Origins of Tarot”](https://www.nybooks.com/articles/1981/05/14/origins-of-tarot/)

### Nine-specific conclusion

There is no Egyptian Nine of Swords prototype and no ancient Egyptian evidence for Mars in Gemini, Yesod, Vav, or the Waite bedroom scene as a unified card.

## “Gypsy” or Romani transmission

### Claim

Tarot was carried from Egypt or the East into Europe by “Gypsies,” hence *Tarot des Bohémiens*.

### History

The theory grew during the nineteenth century and was spread by writers including Jean-Alexandre Vaillant and Papus. Farley traces its literary development and the way “Bohemian” identity became fused with occult tarot. [Farley, *A Cultural History of Tarot*](https://e-isomero.com/pdf/book/1611846398.pdf)

### Evidence status

**Legend.** Tarot is documented in northern Italian courts before evidence links Romani communities to its creation or transmission. The term is also historically burdened and should not be treated as an ethnic history of the cards.

## Cathar and Templar claims

Claims that tarot preserves Cathar doctrine, Templar secrets, a concealed Grail initiation, or suppressed heresy generally depend on interpreting later Marseille images as coded documents.

**Evidence status:** disputed claim or legend. No fifteenth-century source links the Nine of Swords—or the tarot pack as a whole—to Cathar or Templar institutional transmission.

The sword suit alone is not evidence of Templar origin.

## “Ancient Kabbalah”

The Golden Dawn mapping is internally coherent, but:

- no fifteenth-century card labels the Nine “Yesod”;
- no early rules mention Vav;
- no premodern Jewish source identifies the Nine of Swords as Mars in Gemini.

**Evidence status:** nineteenth-century esoteric attribution, not ancient provenance.

## The Sola Busca source claim

### Strong version

“The Sola Busca Nine of Swords is the source of Smith’s Nine.”

**Verdict:** unsubstantiated.

### Defensible version

“The Sola Busca demonstrated a precedent for fully illustrated pips and probably influenced Smith’s minor-arcana project.”

**Verdict:** scholarly reconstruction supported by chronology, museum access, and several strong visual parallels.

## Was Smith’s figure male or female?

Waite writes “her,” but viewers often see a man or an androgynous person.

**Verdict:** Waite’s intended grammatical gender is documented; the image remains visually ambiguous. Identifications with Smith, Waite, a widow, or a named sitter are unproved.

## Are the swords mounted on the wall?

Many modern readings assume the swords are literal wall decoration. The black field has little spatial modeling; they may be symbolic, visionary, or compositional rather than physically installed.

**Verdict:** disputed iconographic detail. Waite merely says “the swords over her.”

## Is the quilt astrological?

The alternating signs are frequently called zodiacal symbols.

**Verdict:** plausible but not conclusively glossed by Waite. At least some figures resemble planetary/zodiacal emblems; an exact complete program has not been demonstrated.

## “The nightmare card”

This is an excellent modern mnemonic, but Waite’s published list does not contain the word “nightmare.”

**Verdict:** modern interpretive shorthand, grounded in Smith’s imagery and the Golden Dawn title.

## Misprints and transmitted anomalies

### Case’s Venus

The accessible *Oracle of the Tarot* assigns the Nine’s Gemini decan to Venus. Golden Dawn and Thoth give Mars.

**Status:** unresolved textual anomaly. Without comparison to Case’s manuscript and multiple first printings, it cannot safely be called either a misprint or an intentional reform.

### “Lord of Cruelty”

Modern sources often call the Golden Dawn card “Lord of Cruelty.”

**Correction:** *Book T* gives **Lord of Despair and Cruelty**. Crowley shortens the displayed Thoth title to **Cruelty**.

### 1909 versus 1910/1911

The Waite-Smith cards were issued in 1909; *The Pictorial Key* appeared afterward, with first-edition title pages commonly dated 1911 although publication is often catalogued as 1910.

These are bibliographic distinctions, not competing card designs.

### Thoth publication date

The paintings were executed 1938–1943 and examples appeared in Crowley’s publications, but the complete commercial deck was not issued until 1969.

Saying simply “the 1944 Thoth deck” confuses the book with the full deck’s publication history.

## Historiographical positions

### Michael Dummett

Established through rules and archival records that tarot originated as a game and that occult interpretations were late.

### Ronald Decker and Thierry Depaulis

With Dummett, reconstructed the formation of occult tarot from Court de Gébelin through Etteilla and nineteenth-century occultists.

### Stuart R. Kaplan

Popularized reproductions and assembled an enormous documentary and pictorial record. Some dates and attributions in early volumes have subsequently been refined.

### Robert M. Place

Combines historical iconography with esoteric interpretation. His distinction between Renaissance allegory and later occult overlay is useful, though some symbolic genealogies remain interpretive.

### Helen Farley

Traces tarot as a cultural object: game, occult emblem, literary technology, and modern spiritual tool. Particularly useful for the social construction of Egyptian and Romani narratives.

### Andrea Vitali/Le Tarot association and tarot-history researchers

They have published transcriptions, iconographic comparisons, and archival findings. Individual hypotheses require evaluation separately; the field contains both rigorous documentation and speculative synthesis.

# Open questions

1. **Exact dating of the Visconti-Sforza Nine:** The pack is securely fifteenth-century, but workshop stages and dates remain debated.

2. **Exact maker of Sola Busca:** Nicola di Maestro Antonio has been proposed, but the British Museum says the attribution is not wholly convincing.

3. **Meaning of the Sola Busca Nine:** Its imagery supports learned, metallurgical, or alchemical interpretation, but the lack of a contemporary key prevents certainty.

4. **Smith’s immediate source for the bed scene:** No drawing, instruction sheet, diary entry, or identified single painting has been found that conclusively explains it.

5. **Waite versus Smith authorship:** Waite supplied meanings and overall design direction; the extent of Smith’s independent invention for each pip is not documented card by card.

6. **Case’s “Venus in Gemini”:** A source-critical comparison of first printings, typescripts, and BOTA teaching materials is needed.

7. **Early cartomantic wording:** Later Etteilla manuals preserve “religion/conscience” and mistrust, but determining which phrases occur in each 1780s printing requires collation of rare editions.

8. **Marseille flowers:** No contemporary Noblet, Dodal, or Conver text explains the floral ornament. Alchemical, sexual, Kabbalistic, or “energy flow” readings are later interpretations.

9. **Modern therapeutic vocabulary:** Anxiety, depression, intrusive thoughts, and catastrophizing fit Smith’s image but are twentieth- and twenty-first-century psychological categories, not recoverable Renaissance meanings.

10. **Card-specific cultural quotations:** Calvino is securely relevant; proposed links to Eliot, Yeats, Bergman, or Jodorowsky are generally tarot-wide rather than Nine-specific.
