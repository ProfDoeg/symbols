# King of Swords: Research Dossier

## Basic identification

### Identity at a glance

| Tradition or deck | Name | Number or structural place |
|---|---|---|
| Fifteenth-century Italian tarot | **Re de Spade / Re di Spade** | Unnumbered court card; highest court of Swords |
| Italian game terminology | **Re di Spade** | Normally four or five card-points depending on counting convention |
| French Tarot de Marseille | **ROY D’ÉPÉE / ROI D’ÉPÉE(S)** | Unnumbered court; above Dame, Cavalier, Valet |
| Besançon tarot | **Roi d’Épée** | Same suit rank; regional changes principally affect trumps |

### Earliest surviving example

**[Documented fact]** The earliest securely catalogued surviving tarot King of Swords is in the Visconti-Sforza or Pierpont Morgan–Bergamo group, Milan, approximately 1450–1480. The Morgan Library catalogues it as card 22 of MS M.630, painted by or attributed to Bonifacio Bembo, 173 × 87 mm. It shows a crowned, enthroned, armoured king with an upright sword and a shield. The shield bears a haloed lion holding a book—an image resembling the Venetian Lion of Saint Mark. The museum consequently records a possible connection with the Venetian Bon family, but does not treat this as established identification. [Morgan card record](https://www.themorgan.org/collection/tarot-cards/the-king-of-swords); [Morgan manuscript catalogue](https://www.themorgan.org/manuscript/158525).

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** The card was probably produced after Francesco Sforza’s triumphal entry into Milan as duke on 25 March 1450. The mingling of Visconti and Sforza emblems securely places the pack within their dynastic milieu, but the precise patron, date, original deck composition and ownership history remain debated.

**[Disputed claim]** Gertrude Moakley and Stuart Kaplan encouraged attempts to read individual Visconti-Sforza court figures as portraits or dynastic allusions. No contemporary inscription names the King of Swords as Francesco Sforza, a Venetian Bon, or another historical person. The shield is evidence for heraldic association, not proof that the king’s face is a portrait.

### Golden Dawn attribution

**[Esoteric attribution, approximately 1890s]**

- Title: **Prince of the Chariot of the Winds; King of Swords**
- Elemental nature: **Air of Air**
- Tetragrammaton: **Vav** in the suit governed by elemental Air
- Qabalistic world: conventionally **Yetzirah**, the formative world
- Sephirah: **Tiphareth** as the Prince/Vav station in the court-card schema
- Zodiacal span: **20° Capricorn to 20° Aquarius**
- Decans crossed: final decan of Capricorn, followed by the first two decans of Aquarius
- Image: a winged king in a chariot drawn by winged youths or “arch-fays,” bearing a sword and sickle
- Formula: “With the sword he rules, with the sickle he slays.”
- Character: prolific in ideas, careful, suspicious, observant, capable simultaneously of construction and destruction

The original wording survives through Golden Dawn manuscript traditions and later publication in the *Equinox* and Israel Regardie’s compilations. [Golden Dawn manuscript transcription](https://www.tarrdaniel.com/documents/Thelemagick/gd/publication/english/golden_dawn_manuscripts.html); [*Equinox* I.8 PDF](https://hermetic.com/_media/crowley/equinox/equinox-iviii.pdf).

A persistent source of confusion must be resolved at the outset:

**[Documented esoteric convention]** The Golden Dawn’s traditional **King of Swords** is its chariot-borne **Prince**, not its mounted Knight. Crowley renamed this figure simply **Prince of Swords**. The mounted Golden Dawn Knight became Crowley’s Knight. Therefore “Thoth Prince = old King,” while “Thoth Knight = Golden Dawn Knight,” not the Waite–Smith Knight.

## Iconography, deck by deck

### Mamluk antecedent: the *malik* of Swords

**[Documented artifact; genealogical relation is scholarly reconstruction]** The Topkapı Mamluk pack, usually dated around the fifteenth century, contains four suits—swords (*suyūf*), polo sticks, cups and coins—and three courts in each: *malik* (“king”), *nāʾib malik* (“deputy king”) and *thānī nāʾib* (“second deputy”). The cards avoid human portraiture and identify rank through calligraphy and ornamental design. The word *kanjifah* appears on the King of Swords.

Dummett and Kamal Abu-Deeb argued that the close structural correspondence—four suits, ten numerals and three courts—makes Mamluk cards the principal demonstrable predecessor of early Latin-suited European cards. This is not the same as saying that tarot’s twenty-two trumps came from Egypt: the imported structure was the ordinary suited pack to which northern Italians later added trumps and Queens. [International Playing-Card Society introduction](https://www.i-p-c-s.org/wp/an-introduction/); Dummett and Abu-Deeb, “Some Remarks on Mamluk Playing Cards,” *Journal of the Warburg and Courtauld Institutes* 36 (1973), 106–128, [DOI record](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/10.2307/751160); L. A. Mayer, *Mamluk Playing Cards*, [original study](https://www.persee.fr/doc/bifao_0255-0962_1939_num_38_1_1998).

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** European figurative Kings probably replace the inscribed Mamluk *malik*. Knight and Knave are more plausibly transformations of the two deputies. The European Queen is an addition.

**[Disputed linguistic tradition]** Spanish *naipes* is very plausibly related to Arabic *nāʾib*. The old Italian *naibi* has the same association. This does not establish that every European card term or design was borrowed directly from one surviving Mamluk pack.

### Visconti di Modrone / Cary-Yale

**[Documented fact]** The deck usually called Cary-Yale or Visconti di Modrone, approximately 1440s, is exceptional in having six courts per suit: male and female servants, male and female mounted figures, King and Queen. Its surviving composition shows that fifteenth-century tarot court structure was not yet completely standardized.

**[Caution]** “Visconti di Modrone” and “Cary-Yale” ordinarily refer to the same surviving group, not two independent decks.

Its King of Swords is an enthroned dynastic warrior holding the long suit-sign. The court costumes and heraldry belong to the Visconti milieu. No contemporary evidence identifies the sitter by name.

### Brambilla-Brera deck

**[Documented fact]** The Brambilla deck, now principally in the Pinacoteca di Brera, belongs to the early Milanese luxury-card tradition, normally dated around 1440–1450. Only a small portion survives, heavily weighted toward Coins. A securely preserved King of Swords is not available for detailed comparison.

**[Absence of evidence]** Modern composite “complete Brambilla tarot” products include reconstructed cards. Such reconstructions cannot be cited as surviving fifteenth-century iconography.

### Visconti-Sforza / Pierpont Morgan–Bergamo

The Morgan King is:

- crowned and seated frontally or slightly turned;
- dressed in plate armour beneath a rich mantle;
- holding a large sword nearly vertical;
- equipped with a heraldic shield;
- surrounded by tooled gold ground and courtly decoration.

**[Documented historical meaning]** Armour, crown, throne, sword and shield identify secular martial sovereignty. In a playing-card context, these attributes distinguish rank and suit.

**[Scholarly interpretation]** The shield’s haloed lion and book evoke Saint Mark. The Morgan catalogue cautiously suggests Venetian heraldic connections. The image may signal alliance, patronage or later ownership rather than portrait identity.

**[Disputed]** Calling the figure Francesco Sforza is plausible only at the level of dynastic evocation. Neither Moakley nor Kaplan supplied contemporary evidence naming him on this card.

### Ferrarese and Este-related fragments

The so-called Charles VI, Catania, Ercole I d’Este and related Ferrarese cards establish a vigorous fifteenth-century court-card tradition, but preservation is fragmentary and attribution has repeatedly shifted between Ferrara, Florence and northern Italy.

**[Absence of evidence]** A complete, securely localized sequence allowing a card-by-card King of Swords evolution from Ferrara to Marseille does not survive.

### Rosenwald and Budapest sheets

**[Documented fact]** The late-fifteenth- or early-sixteenth-century Rosenwald and Budapest uncut sheets preserve inexpensive printed tarot imagery, in contrast with aristocratic painted packs.

**[Scholarly importance]** Their simplified line work shows that popular printed tarots existed alongside luxury decks. They help reconstruct early numeral and trump types.

**[Absence of evidence]** Surviving damage and incomplete sheets prevent a detailed, secure King-of-Swords genealogy comparable to the Morgan card. Claims that a particular Marseille shoulder ornament or posture comes directly from these sheets exceed the evidence.

### Sola Busca, 1491

**[Documented fact]** The Sola Busca is the earliest surviving substantially complete tarot whose numbered suit cards receive independent figurative scenes. Its courts and trumps are named and classicized. The King of Swords is identified with **Alexander**, conventionally Alexander the Great.

Its historic importance to the King of Swords is twofold:

1. It transforms the generic king into a named ancient warrior.
2. It demonstrates that narrative, all’antica treatment of the minors existed centuries before Waite–Smith.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** Pamela Colman Smith demonstrably knew photographs of Sola Busca cards preserved at the British Museum; several Waite–Smith pip compositions, especially Three and Ten of Swords, show strong visual debts. There is no comparably exact proof that her King of Swords copied Sola Busca’s Alexander.

### “Mantegna Tarocchi”

**[Documented correction]** The so-called Mantegna Tarocchi, approximately 1460s, are fifty educational or emblematic engravings arranged in five series. They are neither by Andrea Mantegna nor a normal tarot pack. They contain no King of Swords. Their relevance is contextual: they demonstrate the Renaissance practice of arranging social estates, virtues, liberal arts and cosmic principles in numbered image series.

### Tarot de Marseille lineage

#### Jean Noblet, Paris, approximately 1650

The Noblet King is a seated, crowned ruler holding the sword upright. The throne is barely architectural; the body turns within the narrow card; dress, shoulder pieces and sword fill the surface. The title is in old spelling, normally **ROY D’ESPÉE** or its worn equivalent.

**[Documented design history]** Noblet is the earliest substantially surviving deck conventionally grouped with Tarot de Marseille Type I, although “Tarot de Marseille” itself is a later generic name.

**[Interpretive caution]** Features sometimes called faces, masks or solar emblems on the shoulders may be ornamental conventions or degraded carving. No seventeenth-century explanatory text decodes them.

#### Jean Dodal, Lyon, approximately 1701–1715

Dodal retains the seated king, upright sword, elaborate costume and spatial compression. The cutting is bolder and the title more legible. The figure’s sword is straight, while the pip cards of the suit employ interlaced, curved forms.

**[Documented fact]** The curved “swords” of Marseille pip cards are not scimitars held by the King. They are conventionalized interlaced blades or blade-shaped bands, usually paired with floral ornaments. The court King holds a recognizable straight European sword.

#### Pierre Madenié and Type II development

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** Madenié’s Dijon tarot of 1709 is an early secure representative of Marseille Type II. Later Chosson, Tourcaty and Conver packs transmit this pattern. Type I/II differences affect costume, faces, lettering and numerous trump details more strongly than the King’s essential enthroned-swordsman formula.

#### Nicolas Conver, Marseille, 1760/1761

Conver’s King wears a crown and elaborate tunic, sits or braces himself on a throne-like support, and holds the sword upright at an angle. His shoulder ornaments sometimes resemble small faces or masks.

**[Tradition]** Later occult commentators treated every colored dot and ambiguous woodcut curl as intentional symbolic writing. Contemporary cardmakers left no key confirming those readings.

#### Grimaud / Paul Marteau, 1930s

Paul Marteau’s Grimaud deck standardized a strong modern palette—blue, red, yellow, flesh and black—and helped turn “Tarot de Marseille” into a named commercial type.

**[Modern esoteric interpretation]** Marteau read the King’s posture, colors, dots and shoulder mask numerologically and spiritually. These interpretations belong to twentieth-century occult exegesis, not to documented eighteenth-century cardmaking intention.

#### Camoin–Jodorowsky, 1997

Philippe Camoin and Alejandro Jodorowsky created a synthetic “restoration” using several historical Marseille-family decks rather than reproducing one surviving original.

**[Documented modern invention]** Additional details and colors were selected or reconstructed according to the creators’ theory of an underlying original tarot. It should be described as a scholarly-esoteric reconstruction, not a facsimile. Jodorowsky and Marianne Costa interpret the King as disciplined intellectual authority whose sword separates truth from falsehood.

### Besançon pattern

Besançon tarots chiefly distinguish themselves by replacing the Papess and Pope with **Juno and Jupiter**, a change documented in eastern France and Switzerland during the eighteenth century and probably motivated by confessional sensitivity. The King of Swords remains a King of Swords.

This illustrates why trump renamings in the question must not be transferred to this court:

- Papess → Juno: regional trump substitution.
- Pope → Jupiter: regional trump substitution.
- Tower → *Maison Dieu*: French trump title.
- Fool unnumbered: trump convention.
- King of Swords: unaffected suit court.

### Minchiate

Florentine Minchiate expanded the trump series to forty-one but retained the four ordinary Italian suits and their courts. The King of Swords remains the top Swords court.

The design varies by workshop, normally presenting a crowned male with the suit weapon. There is no Minchiate-specific occult King-of-Swords doctrine in the early game sources.

### Bolognese *tarocchino*

The Bolognese pack removes numeral cards 2–5 but keeps courts and Swords. Its King is a high-ranking suit card. Later double-headed designs make him more compact and symmetrical.

**[Documented historical curiosity]** A Bolognese manuscript of the eighteenth century is among the earliest surviving records assigning divinatory phrases to a full tarot sequence. This does not show that fifteenth-century players used the cards occultly.

### Sicilian tarot

Tarot reached Sicily during the seventeenth century, probably through Rome. Sicilian packs developed radically altered trumps and shortened suits but retained the King of Swords. In the surviving Mineo game, the King is a counting card and the highest card of its suit. [Sicilian rules and card illustrations](https://www.pagat.com/tarot/sicilian.html).

### Grand Etteilla, design published from 1788–1789

Etteilla reorganized the tarot completely. The corresponding card is usually numbered **50** and titled **Homme de robe**, “man of the robe”—that is, a lawyer, magistrate or legal professional. Later examples show a grey-bearded armoured ruler in a red mantle, resting a drawn sword across his knees.

Printed meanings:

- upright: **Homme de robe**
- reversed: **Homme méchant**

**[Documented innovation]** Etteilla converts a game court into an explicitly cartomantic legal personality and prints upright/reversed concepts on the card. [Grand Etteilla archive, card 50](https://foolmade.art/archive/grand-etteilla/card-050).

### Oswald Wirth, 1889

Wirth’s original twenty-two-card tarot contains only trumps. Consequently:

**[Absence of evidence]** There is no 1889 Wirth King of Swords. Later “Wirth” seventy-eight-card decks extend his style through modern invention.

### Golden Dawn manuscript image

*Book T* describes:

> “A WINGED King with Winged Crown, seated in a chariot drawn by Arch Fays…”

He has long, whirling hair, scale armour formed from vortices, a sword in one hand and sickle in the other. Grey nimbus clouds lie beneath the chariot. The sword rules; the sickle kills.

This is the decisive break with Marseille iconography:

| Marseille | Golden Dawn |
|---|---|
| Seated earthly king | Mobile elemental prince |
| Throne | Chariot |
| One sword | Sword and sickle |
| Generic secular ruler | Personification of Air within Air |
| No zodiac printed or documented | 20° Capricorn–20° Aquarius |
| Game court | Ritual and divinatory formula |

Robert Wang’s *Golden Dawn Tarot* and Chic and Sandra Tabatha Cicero’s *Golden Dawn Magical Tarot* are modern visual reconstructions of manuscript specifications, not facsimiles of one authoritative Order deck.

### Waite–Smith, 1909

Pamela Colman Smith depicts a crowned king seated frontally on a stone throne. He holds a naked sword upright in his right hand, though the blade inclines slightly. His blue-grey tunic, purple cloak and red hood stand against a cloudy sky. The throne carries butterfly-like reliefs and small crescent or sylph-like figures; trees and birds occupy the distance.

Waite’s own description is:

> “He sits in judgment, holding the unsheathed sign of his suit. He recalls, of course, the conventional Symbol of justice in the Trumps Major, and he may represent this virtue, but he is rather the power of life and death, in virtue of his office.”

A. E. Waite, *The Pictorial Key to the Tarot* (London: Rider, 1910), Part III, King of Swords. [Primary text](https://sacred-texts.com/book/the-pictorial-key-to-the-tarot/read/king-of-swords).

**[Documented interpretation]** Waite connects the image to Justice but distinguishes judicial office from the abstract virtue.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** Smith synthesized Marseille court posture, Golden Dawn doctrine, theatrical costume and her own decorative vocabulary. Butterflies are visually present, but the widespread equation “butterfly = transformation of thought” is a modern reading; neither Smith nor Waite documented it for this card.

### BOTA: Paul Foster Case and Jessie Burns Parke

The BOTA deck reworks Waite–Smith in clean outlines intended for students to color. Jessie Burns Parke supplied the drawings under Case’s direction.

Case’s *Oracle of the Tarot* places **Yod**, not Vav, in the King’s ankh and describes him as will-force acting on the astral plane. His zodiacal period is given as approximately 13 September–2 October, spanning the last Virgo decan and first two Libra decans.

**[Documented conflict]** This is not the Golden Dawn Prince’s 20° Capricorn–20° Aquarius. Case reorganized the relation between traditional rank names and elemental court functions. [Case, *Oracle of the Tarot*, King of Swords](https://media-01.imu.nl/storage/divona.be/17547/oracle-of-tarot-paul-foster-case.pdf).

### Crowley–Harris Thoth, painted 1938–1943; book 1944; deck 1969

Frieda Harris depicts the **Prince of Swords** in an abstract chariot rushing through geometric air. Winged or childlike figures draw it. The Prince controls a sword and sickle; wheels, wings and intersecting planes evoke unstable thought, analysis and centrifugal motion.

Crowley writes:

> “This card represents the airy part of Air. With its particular interpretation, it is intellectual, it is a picture of the Mind as such.”

He calls the mind’s mechanism admirable but unstable: ideas crowd, conflict and may fail to reach practical purpose. This elaborates *Book T*’s simultaneous creation and destruction. [Crowley, *The Book of Thoth*, Court Cards](https://hermetic.com/crowley/book-of-thoth/court-cards); [theoretical table, “Prince of Swords—Air of Air”](https://hermetic.com/crowley/book-of-thoth/theory).

**[Documented chronology]** Harris painted the cards between 1938 and 1943. *The Book of Thoth* appeared in a limited edition in 1944. A full commercial deck was first issued in 1969.

### Notable modern redesigns

- **Aquarian Tarot, 1970:** David Palladini streamlines the king into Art Deco planes, retaining sword and royal profile.
- **Morgan-Greer, 1979:** Bill Greer presents a close, saturated portrait, intensifying personality at the expense of landscape.
- **Motherpeace, 1981:** Vicki Noble and Karen Vogel’s feminist round deck reconsiders hierarchical and gendered courts.
- **Robin Wood, 1991:** retains the rational sovereign but integrates neo-pagan costume and clearer elemental scenery.
- **DruidCraft, 2004:** recasts authority within Celtic revival imagery.
- **Light Seer’s Tarot, 2019:** Chris-Anne replaces medieval monarchy with contemporary psychological leadership.
- **Modern gender-neutral decks:** frequently rename courts Seeker, Knower, Sovereign, Mentor or Guardian. These are intentional modern inventions, not recovered ancient titles.

## The game

### Rank

**[Documented game structure]** In Italian-suited tarot, Swords is ordinarily a “long” suit. The usual trick-taking order is:

**King > Queen > Knight > Jack > 10 > 9 … > Ace**

The precise direction of numeral rank varies among games and between long and round suits, but the King remains the highest Swords card.

In French Tarot:

**Roi > Dame > Cavalier > Valet > 10 … > Ace.**

The King of Swords therefore wins a Swords trick unless a trump is played.

### Value

Several superficially different statements mean the same thing because tarot games employ different counting conventions:

- French Tarot: King = **4½ points** when counted with a low card.
- Nominal-value description: King = **4 points**, while each captured card contributes the remaining half-point.
- Many Italian and central-European three-card packet systems: King is conventionally described as **5 counting points**, but packet subtraction changes the effective total.
- Minchiate: King = **5 points**; other suit courts have no independent card value.
- Sicilian tarot: King is a counting card, with exact totals depending on local rules.

Thus “King = 4,” “4½,” or “5” is not necessarily a historical contradiction; one must specify the counting system. [Comparative counting systems](https://www.pagat.com/tarot/counting.html); [Minchiate rules](https://www.pagat.com/tarot/minchiate.html).

### The King and the trumps

The King of Swords has no place in the trump order. Any tarot trump ordinarily beats it. The famous regional trump-order disputes concern the allegorical trump suit:

- **Order A, southern:** Bologna and Florence.
- **Order B, eastern:** Ferrara and Venice.
- **Order C, western:** Milan, Piedmont and later France.

Justice, Fortitude, Temperance, the papal figures, World and Angel move relative to one another in these orders. None of that changes the King’s position inside Swords. [Dummett’s three orders summarized from *Il Mondo e l’Angelo*](https://dummettsmondo.blogspot.com/2015/07/chapters-6-and-7-game-and-its-trump.html).

### Fool

The Fool—*Matto*, *Excuse*, *Sküs*—is normally unnumbered or specially marked and has an exceptional role allowing it to be played without following suit, often being reclaimed by its player. This does not make it the “twenty-second trump” in early play.

### Earliest rules

The Abbé de Marolles manuscript, 1637, is the earliest known detailed printed-era description of French tarot play. It names the courts *Roy, Royne, Chevalier,* and *Faon/Fante*. [Marolles rules](https://www.pagat.com/tarot/marolles.html).

## Chronology of attestation

### Before tarot

- **1370s, Europe — documented:** ordinary playing cards appear in European records. References to *naib/naibi* support transmission through the Islamic Mediterranean.
- **Late medieval Mamluk world — documented artifact:** packs use Swords and a *malik*. This is the closest structural ancestor of the European King of Swords.
- **No evidence:** there is no ancient Egyptian tarot King of Swords, Hebrew tarot court or Romani tarot pack from this period.

### Approximately 1420: Marziano da Tortona

Marziano’s text for Filippo Maria Visconti describes a pack of sixteen classical gods arranged into four orders, supplementing four suits of birds. Marcello’s later account calls the associated object a *ludus triumphorum*.

**[Documented precursor]** It is an early Italian trumped card pack.

**[Correction]** It does not name the standard King of Swords and does not contain the later suit system in recognizable form.

### 1442: Ferrara ledger

The Este court accounts record payments for *carte da trionfi*, including “pare uno de carte da trionfi.”

**[Documented fact]** This is the earliest uncontested documentary use of the term for standard tarot’s immediate tradition. It does not inventory individual suit cards. [Farley, *A Cultural History of Tarot*, discussion and transcription](https://e-isomero.com/pdf/book/1611846398.pdf).

### Approximately 1450 onward: surviving Milanese cards

The Visconti-Sforza King of Swords supplies the earliest direct visual attestation.

### Approximately 1470–1500: *Sermo perutilis de ludo cum aliis*

The anonymous “sermon on the game” lists and condemns the tarot trumps.

**[Documented importance]** It provides an early trump list and proves familiarity with tarot as a game.

**[Correction]** It does not single out the King of Swords; ordinary courts were already conventional and were not the preacher’s main target.

### 1491: Sola Busca

Alexander as King of Swords supplies the first substantially complete, named, classicizing treatment of the figure.

### Early sixteenth century: Folengo and tarot poems

Teofilo Folengo and the *tarocchi appropriati* tradition use tarot names as literary emblems assigned to persons.

**[Documented cultural use]** These poems show social and poetic appropriation of card imagery.

**[Absence of evidence]** They do not establish Golden Dawn-style astrology, Kabbalah or Air-of-Air doctrine.

### Seventeenth century

- Noblet and related French packs establish the recognizable seated *Roi d’Épée*.
- French rules document the King as an honour and counting card.
- Bolognese material eventually records cartomantic phrases, but no universal occult system.

### 1781: Court de Gébelin and Comte de Mellet

Court de Gébelin asserts an Egyptian origin for tarot. De Mellet calls it a Book of Thoth, proposes Hebrew-letter relationships and describes a divinatory procedure.

**[Documented first-wave occultism]** These essays launch the published esoteric history of tarot.

**[Important limitation]** Their elaborate allegory concentrates on trumps. They do not provide the later Golden Dawn Air-of-Air and zodiacal doctrine for this King.

### 1788–1789: Etteilla

Etteilla issues a redesigned, explicitly divinatory pack. The King becomes *Homme de robe*. This is the first firmly traceable printed occult recoding specific to the card.

### 1854–1856: Éliphas Lévi

Lévi’s *Dogme et Rituel de la Haute Magie* links twenty-two trumps with Hebrew letters and the Kabbalah.

**[Correction]** Lévi’s celebrated alphabet primarily structures the trumps, not a developed King-of-Swords horoscope. Later writers retrojected Golden Dawn court correspondences into Lévi.

### 1870s: Paul Christian

Christian elaborates an Egyptian initiation narrative and uses the term “Arcana.” His staged temple imagery profoundly influenced later occultists.

**[Legend/esoteric invention]** His account is literary occult historiography, unsupported by Egyptian documents.

### 1888: Mathers

S. L. MacGregor Mathers publishes *The Tarot: Its Occult Signification, Use in Fortune-Telling, and Method of Play*. His King of Swords meanings reproduce the legal-authority stream descending from Etteilla.

### 1889: Papus and Wirth

Papus’s *Tarot des Bohémiens* develops a comprehensive Tetragrammaton scheme. Wirth issues twenty-two occult trumps. Neither yet supplies the later public Golden Dawn chariot design in a commercial seventy-eight-card deck.

### 1890s: Golden Dawn *Book T*

The King becomes Prince/Vav/Air of Air and receives its zodiacal territory.

### 1909–1910: Waite–Smith

Smith returns to the traditional seated King while Waite gives him the judicial “power of life and death.”

### 1920s–1940s: Case

Case revises rank-element-letter relations and publishes his own divinatory teaching.

### 1938–1969: Thoth

Crowley and Harris literalize the Golden Dawn chariot-prince as a modernist image of unstable intellect.

### 1970s to present

Psychological, Jungian, feminist, queer and therapeutic decks progressively detach the card from literal male monarchy. “Ethical intellectual authority,” “boundaries,” “clarity,” and “weaponized reason” become common modern themes.

### Conflicts

1. **Vav versus Yod:** Golden Dawn assigns the traditional King-equivalent to Prince/Vav; Case’s explicitly named King carries Yod.
2. **Air of Air versus Fire of Air:** In Golden Dawn/Thoth, Prince is Air of Air and Knight is Fire of Air. Systems that map modern “King” to the mounted Knight call the King Fire of Air.
3. **Zodiacal span:** Golden Dawn/Thoth gives 20° Capricorn–20° Aquarius; Case gives late Virgo through early Libra.
4. **Element of Swords:** Air is dominant in Golden Dawn-derived English tarot. Some older occult systems assigned Swords to Fire. Air is therefore an influential nineteenth-century attribution, not a property documented on medieval cards.
5. **Hebrew letter:** No ancient letter belongs intrinsically to the King. Vav is an Order attribution based on the Prince as son; Tzaddi/Heh disputes concern the Emperor and Star trumps and do not change this court’s Vav.

### Tzaddi/Heh and the Emperor/Star issue

Crowley’s “Tzaddi is not the Star” revision exchanges the Hebrew-letter relationships of Emperor and Star while retaining their zodiacal signs through a loop in the scheme. It does **not** alter the Prince of Swords’ Air-of-Air nature or 20° Capricorn–20° Aquarius span. Invoking the swap to reassign this King is a category error.

## Divinatory meanings by author

Short quotations are retained because the wording itself documents transmission.

### Etteilla, 1788–1789

- Upright: **“Homme de robe.”**
- Reversed: **“Homme méchant.”**

Meaning: legal professional or magistrate; reversed, wicked man. [Card and inscriptions](https://foolmade.art/archive/grand-etteilla/card-050).

### Mathers, 1888

> “A Lawyer, a Man of Law, Power, Command, Superiority, Authority.”

Reversed:

> “A Wicked Man, Chagrin, Worry, Grief, Fear, Disturbance.”

S. L. MacGregor Mathers, *The Tarot* (London, 1888), card 50, p. 16 in the linked scan. [Scan](https://www.howardnema.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/12/the-tarot-1888-by-macgregor-mathers-1.pdf).

**[Documented transmission]** This closely follows Etteilla’s legal/wicked polarity.

### Golden Dawn, *Book T*

Well dignified:

> “Full of ideas and thoughts and designs, distrustful, suspicious, firm in friendship and enmity; careful, observant, slow, over-cautious…”

Ill dignified:

> “harsh, malicious, plotting; obstinate, yet hesitating; unreliable.”

The Golden Dawn generally speaks of dignity rather than simple reversal.

### Waite, 1910

Upright:

> “judgment and all its connexions—power, command, authority, militant intelligence, law, offices of the crown…”

Reversed:

> “Cruelty, perversity, barbarity, perfidy, evil intention.”

[*Pictorial Key*, primary text](https://sacred-texts.com/book/the-pictorial-key-to-the-tarot/read/king-of-swords).

### Crowley, 1944

Crowley does not reduce the Prince to a compact upright/reversed list and generally rejects mechanical reversal. Dignified, the card denotes intensely intellectual, inventive, idealistic, flexible mental activity. Ill dignified, the intellect becomes purposeless, unstable, suspicious or deceitful—“a mass of fine ideals unrelated to practical effort.”

[*Book of Thoth*, Court Cards](https://hermetic.com/crowley/book-of-thoth/court-cards).

### Paul Foster Case

General:

> “A man of strong and powerful imagination; hard worker, and having authority; keen in understanding law…”

Well dignified: friendly and cooperative.

Ill dignified:

> “inimical, harsh, malicious and plotting; obstinate, and wholly unreliable.”

[*Oracle of the Tarot*, p. 37](https://media-01.imu.nl/storage/divona.be/17547/oracle-of-tarot-paul-foster-case.pdf).

### Rachel Pollack

Pollack emphasizes intellect placed at the service of authority and action:

> “The King’s intellect does not exist for itself alone, but rather for what he can do, as a tool of authority.”

She notes that the sword inclines toward the active side rather than standing in the abstract balance associated with Justice. Rachel Pollack, *Seventy-Eight Degrees of Wisdom*, revised edition, [searchable scan](https://logoilibrary.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/07/Seventy-Eight-Degrees-of-Wisdom-A-Book-of-Tarot.pdf).

### Mary K. Greer

Greer’s work treats courts as roles, people, personality modes and elemental combinations rather than fixed fortune-telling tokens. For the King of Swords she develops themes of intellectual leadership, analysis, judgment and verbal or institutional authority; shadow expressions include coldness, dogmatism and manipulative rhetoric.

**[Source limitation]** A freely accessible primary-text page permitting exact verification of Greer’s full upright/reversed wording was not located. It would be misleading to present modern web lists as verbatim Greer.

## Esoteric and symbolic readings

### Christian and political sovereignty

**[Historically proximate reading]** Crown, throne, armour and sword make the early king a recognizable image of secular rulership. The sword signifies the ruler’s coercive and judicial power. Waite’s “power of life and death” is an occult-era restatement of that older political symbolism, not proof of a secret fifteenth-century doctrine.

### Legal judgment

**[First securely traceable card-specific appearance]** Etteilla’s *Homme de robe*, 1788–1789, explicitly makes the King a lawyer or magistrate. Mathers and Waite spread this through English-language occult tarot. The modern professions “judge, lawyer, surgeon, military officer, executive” descend from this stream.

### Elemental Air

**[Esoteric attribution]** Swords = Air becomes systematic in Golden Dawn teaching. The weapon’s cutting and separating action is read as analysis, discrimination, language and reason.

**[Historical caution]** Medieval cardmakers did not label Swords “Air.” Earlier occultists sometimes gave the weapon suit to Fire. Treat elemental assignment as system-dependent.

### Vav and the Son

In the Golden Dawn family formula:

- Knight = Yod/fire/father
- Queen = Heh/water/mother
- Prince = Vav/air/son
- Princess = final Heh/earth/daughter

The Prince’s chariot signifies the outward operation of combined parental force. Hence the traditional King of Swords, converted to Prince, becomes **Vav of Air** and therefore Air of Air.

### Kabbalah

**[Golden Dawn attribution]** The Prince corresponds to Tiphareth and the formative world, Yetzirah. These placements arise from nineteenth-century Hermetic Kabbalah. They are not recoverable from the Visconti-Sforza image or Jewish medieval tarot documents.

### Astrology

The Prince rules:

- 20°–30° Capricorn;
- 0°–10° Aquarius;
- 10°–20° Aquarius.

This combines Saturn-ruled Capricorn and Aquarius in traditional astrology, reinforcing caution, structure, abstraction and severity. Modern Uranian readings of Aquarius are later overlays.

### Alchemy

**[Modern esoteric synthesis]** The sword may be read as *separatio*: discriminating or cutting apart mixed substance. The King/Prince becomes the intellect directing analysis. No fifteenth-century alchemical text has been found identifying the Re di Spade with a specific operation.

### Hermetic reading

The card represents mind acting upon mind: concepts multiplying, subdividing and recombining. Crowley’s sword and sickle enact both governance and destruction. The chariot suggests velocity without guaranteed direction.

### Jungian reading

**[Modern invention, twentieth century onward]** The King is frequently treated as the mature “thinking function”: capacity for definition, boundaries and impersonal judgment. His shadow is hypertrophied reason—authoritarianism, dissociation from feeling, verbal domination and rationalization.

Jung did not publish a canonical King-of-Swords interpretation. “Jungian tarot” is a later application of analytical psychology, spread through writers such as Sallie Nichols and subsequent therapeutic tarot authors.

### Gender and hierarchy

**[Modern critical reading]** Feminist and queer decks question why mastery must appear as a male monarch. Some retain “King” as an archetypal function open to any gender; others rename the rank. This is conscious revision, not evidence that historical Kings were originally gender-neutral.

## People and works

### Artists, printers and occultists directly connected

- **Bonifacio Bembo:** traditionally credited with much of the Visconti-Sforza group.
- **Jean Noblet, Jean Dodal, Nicolas Conver:** major transmitters of the French seated King.
- **Jean-Baptiste Alliette/Etteilla:** first major printed cartomantic recoder of the card.
- **S. L. MacGregor Mathers:** translated and systematized meanings; participated in Golden Dawn doctrine.
- **Oswald Wirth:** important to occult trump design but did not make a 78-card King in his 1889 deck.
- **Pamela Colman Smith:** created the most influential modern seated King.
- **A. E. Waite:** supplied its published judicial explanation.
- **Paul Foster Case and Jessie Burns Parke:** created the BOTA revision.
- **Aleister Crowley and Frieda Harris:** transformed the King-equivalent into the modernist chariot Prince.
- **Philippe Camoin, Alejandro Jodorowsky and Marianne Costa:** promoted a synthetic restored Marseille image and psychospiritual reading.
- **Gertrude Moakley, Michael Dummett, Thierry Depaulis, Ronald Decker and Stuart Kaplan:** central historians or cataloguers for separating artifact history from occult legend.

### Literature, film and art

The requested cultural names require careful separation between tarot generally and this individual card.

- **Italo Calvino, *The Castle of Crossed Destinies* (1969/1973):** characters construct narratives from tarot cards. The work is central to tarot literature, but no unique, stable King-of-Swords doctrine can be extracted independently of the layouts and editions.
- **T. S. Eliot, *The Waste Land* (1922):** Madame Sosostris names the drowned Phoenician Sailor, Belladonna, the man with three staves, Wheel and “one-eyed merchant.” The King of Swords is not named.
- **W. B. Yeats:** Golden Dawn member whose poetry and magical work share the Order’s symbolic environment. No major poem is securely a direct quotation of the King of Swords.
- **Ingmar Bergman:** *The Seventh Seal* uses chess, Death and medieval allegory, not a documented King-of-Swords quotation.
- **Jodorowsky’s films:** saturated with tarot and initiatory symbolism, but a particular armed or enthroned man should not automatically be identified as the King of Swords without production evidence.
- **Paintings and buildings:** no securely documented medieval building has been shown to quote this card. Resemblances between enthroned rulers in art and the card reflect a common iconographic vocabulary.
- **Sola Busca and Waite–Smith:** the demonstrable art-historical quotation concerns several illustrated pip cards. A direct Sola Busca-to-Waite King borrowing remains unproved.

## Controversies and disputes

### Egyptian origin

**[Legend, first published systematically in 1781]** Court de Gébelin claimed that tarot preserved an ancient Egyptian book carried through the ages. De Mellet associated it with Thoth.

**Evidence offered:** visual analogy, speculative etymology and presumed survival of priestly wisdom.

**Evidence missing:** Egyptian cards, Egyptian texts describing tarot, hieroglyphic originals, or a transmission chain to fifteenth-century Italy.

Dummett observed that Gébelin’s Egyptian derivations of “tarot” cite no hieroglyphic forms. [Dummett, “Origins of Tarot”](https://www.nybooks.com/articles/1981/05/14/origins-of-tarot/).

The actual Mamluk-Egyptian connection applies to ordinary playing-card structure and suit signs, not Gébelin’s Pharaonic Book of Thoth.

### Romani or “Gypsy” origin

**[Legend]** Nineteenth-century writers including Jean-Alexandre Vaillant and Papus popularized the claim that Romani carried tarot from Egypt or India into Europe.

**Documented correction:** European tarot records precede the relevant published Romani-tarot theory, and no early Romani pack or testimony establishes transmission. Linguistic and historical research locates Romani origins in India, not ancient Egypt. The title *Tarot des Bohémiens* is occult ideology, not an ethnographic provenance.

### Cathar origin

**[Disputed modern hypothesis]** Writers have proposed that tarot encodes Cathar dualism or survived from Languedoc heresy.

**Evidence offered:** broad thematic resemblances—dualism, spiritual ascent, institutional criticism—and geographic overlap.

**Counterevidence:** no Cathar text names tarot; the earliest secure tarot evidence is fifteenth-century northern Italian court culture, long after the Albigensian Crusade. Even sympathetic investigator Robert V. O’Neill concluded that direct ancient-Gnostic influence was unsupported and that Christian and Neoplatonic sources were nearer at hand. [O’Neill conclusion](https://www.tarot.com/tarot/robert-oneill/catharism-and-the-tarot-conclusions).

### Templar origin

**[Legend/modern invention]** The Templars are sometimes said to have preserved tarot wisdom, Baphomet imagery or a secret Book of Thoth.

No medieval Templar inventory, trial record or rule describes tarot. The Order was dissolved in 1312, roughly a century before the first standard tarot evidence. Similarity between swords, knights and military orders is generic, not genealogical evidence.

### Hidden portraits in Visconti-Sforza courts

**[Disputed]** Moakley and Kaplan’s dynastic readings encouraged identifications of court figures as specific Visconti or Sforza persons.

**What is secure:** family emblems, luxury manufacture, Milanese dynastic context.

**What is not secure:** the King’s individual identity. The Morgan’s Saint-Mark-like shield complicates any simple “this is Francesco Sforza” claim.

### Curved Marseille swords as Islamic scimitars

**[Disputed reconstruction]** The Mamluk sword suit is a genuine ancestor of European Swords, and curved Islamic weapons may be part of the remote visual history. But the Marseille pip lattice is a stylized repeated suit system, while the King holds a straight sword. Calling every curved band a literal scimitar overstates the surviving evidence.

### “Ancient Air correspondence”

**[Modern misattribution]** Swords = Air is often described as timeless. Its comprehensive use for this King is securely documented in Golden Dawn-derived occultism, not medieval tarot.

### Rank confusion

**[Documented source of error]** Some authors say “King of Swords = Fire of Air” because they map King to the Golden Dawn mounted Knight. Others say “King = Air of Air” because they follow *Book T*’s explicit “Prince … King of Swords.” Both may be internally coherent modern systems, but only the latter reproduces *Book T*’s naming.

### “Reversed” versus “ill-dignified”

Etteilla, Mathers and Waite print reversed meanings. Golden Dawn and Crowley often judge a card by surrounding elemental dignity rather than physical reversal. Publishing Crowley’s “reversed meaning” as though he supplied one is therefore misleading.

### Misprints and degraded carving

Repeated copying from woodblock to woodblock produced:

- variant spellings such as *Roy*, *Roi*, *Espée*, *Épée*;
- ambiguous shoulder “faces”;
- altered blade colors;
- lost or duplicated decorative marks;
- apparent anatomical anomalies.

**[Tradition]** Later occultists sometimes interpret every corruption as intentional sacred symbolism.

**[Historical alternative]** Many differences are normal workshop copying, coloring, wear or restoration. Without a cardmaker’s explanation, one cannot decide each case with certainty.

### Anachronistic “Major and Minor Arcana”

The fifteenth-century terms were ordinary suits and *trionfi*. “Major Arcana” and “Minor Arcana” belong to later occult classification. Describing the Morgan King as a “Minor Arcanum designed to encode Air of Air” projects nineteenth-century doctrine backward.

## Open questions

1. Was the Morgan King’s Saint-Mark shield original to the first commission, a patron-specific addition or evidence of another intended owner?
2. Is the face a generalized sovereign or a portrait? No naming document survives.
3. How directly did the Mamluk *malik al-suyūf* influence the first Italian figurative King, as opposed to influencing the deck structure transmitted through intermediate packs?
4. Which lost northern Italian printed pattern most directly mediated between fifteenth-century courts and French Marseille courts?
5. Do the shoulder “faces” in some Marseille Kings preserve meaningful workshop iconography, or are they decorative forms later overinterpreted?
6. Which precise photographs of early courts were available to Pamela Colman Smith, beyond the documented Sola Busca material?
7. Did Smith intend the throne butterflies as transformation, Air, soul, decoration or several things at once? No card-specific statement from her is known.
8. What is the exact date and manuscript genealogy of every component of *Book T*? Surviving copies and later publications do not amount to one authorial master manuscript.
9. Which details in Wang and Cicero are recoverable from Order papers, and which are artists’ necessary completion?
10. A verifiable verbatim King-of-Swords entry from Mary K. Greer was not located in an authorized open edition; exact wording should be checked in the relevant print edition.
11. No reliable evidence was found for a card-specific King of Swords quotation in Eliot, Yeats, Bergman or a historic building. Their genuine relevance is to tarot or allegorical culture generally.
12. No independent card-specific Egyptian, Romani, Cathar or Templar transmission evidence was found.
