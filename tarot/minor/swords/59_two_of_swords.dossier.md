# Two of Swords: Research Dossier

> **Scope and method.** This dossier concerns the pip card, not Trump II. Labels are used throughout:
>
> - **[Documented fact]** directly supported by a surviving object, archival record, or primary printed text.
> - **[Scholarly reconstruction]** a conclusion advanced from material or documentary evidence.
> - **[Tradition]** a received usage whose historical origin may be diffuse.
> - **[Esoteric attribution]** a deliberately constructed occult correspondence.
> - **[Disputed]** a claim on which identification, date, dependence, or interpretation remains contested.
> - **[Legend]** a historical-origin narrative unsupported by contemporary evidence.
> - **[Modern invention]** a comparatively recent interpretation or redesign, rather than an inherited medieval meaning.
>
> Quotations are necessarily short. Page numbers refer to the editions listed under Sources.

### Names, terminology, and a necessary correction

**[Documented fact]** The earliest fifteenth-century records call the new pack or game *carte da trionfi* or simply *trionfi*. *Tarocchi* appears only around the beginning of the sixteenth century. German **Tarock** and French **tarot** descend from Italian *tarocchi*; the deeper etymology remains uncertain.

**[Documented fact]** The suit is Italian *spade*, singular *spada*. French tarot translates this as *épées*; Spanish playing cards retain *espadas*. French-suited tarot substitutes *piques*, English *spades*. The latter is historically related to sword terminology, but “Spade = Sword = Air” combines two separate developments:

1. a historical conversion between Latin and French suit systems; and
2. a later occult elemental attribution.

**[Esoteric attribution]** In the Golden Dawn, Swords are the element **Air**, the world of Yetzirah, and the letter **Vav** of the Tetragrammaton. This does not appear in medieval game records.

**[Not applicable to this card]** Renamings such as Papess → Juno and Pope → Jupiter in the Besançon pattern, or *La Maison Dieu* for the Tower, concern trumps. They did not rename the Two of Swords. Likewise, whether the Fool is unnumbered, 0, or XXII does not alter this pip’s identity.

## Iconography, deck by deck

### Fifteenth-century Milan

#### Cary–Yale / Visconti di Modrone, probably Milan, c.1440–1450

**[Documented object; date reconstructed]** The surviving card conventionally identified as the Two of Swords shows two long, straight weapons placed decoratively against a punched and gilded ground. The weapons are suit signs, not props in a scene. No blindfold, moon, sea, person, or printed title appears.

The pack is exceptional for having extra female court figures and probably a larger original structure than the later standard 78-card pack. That irregularity affects reconstruction of the deck, not the identity of the surviving Two.

**Meaning at the time:** no contemporary symbolic gloss survives. The safest description is functional: two sword emblems declare rank and suit.

#### Brera-Brambilla, Milan, probably 1440s

**[Documented object; disputed dating]** The Brambilla pack preserves numerous suit cards. Its Swords are curved and interlaced in the Italian manner, whereas its Batons may resemble arrows on some cards. Published inventories are inconsistent about which low pip cards survive, so I could not establish a securely catalogued Brambilla Two from an accessible museum record.

**[Absence finding]** Modern reconstructed Brambilla packs should not be cited as proof that their Two is original unless the particular card’s provenance is supplied.

#### Pierpont Morgan–Bergamo Visconti-Sforza, Milan, c.1450–1460

**[Documented object]** The surviving Two of Swords consists of two straight, slender swords disposed symmetrically on a luxurious gold ground decorated with Visconti-Sforza devices. It has no narrative figure and no occult symbols. A public-domain photograph identifies the card as a work associated with Bonifacio Bembo.

The complete original pack is reconstructed as 78 cards, of which 74 survive among the Morgan Library, Accademia Carrara, and Colleoni holdings. The Morgan describes the normal structure as 21 trumps, Fool, and 56 suit cards. Attribution to Bembo or his workshop is conventional but individual hands remain debated.

**Change from Cary–Yale:** not an iconographic revolution; both treat “two swords” literally. Differences concern weapon shape, heraldry, workmanship, and deck structure.

### Ferrarese and related fifteenth-century material

#### Este and “Charles VI” cards

**[Documented objects]** The surviving Este/Ferrarese luxury fragments are dominated by trumps and court cards. No securely identified Two of Swords relevant to this investigation survives among the best-known fragments.

#### Rosenwald and Budapest sheets, late fifteenth or early sixteenth century

**[Documented objects; dating and localization debated]** These woodcut sheets are crucial for reconstructing inexpensive printed tarot, but their surviving arrangements emphasize trumps and selected suit cards. They demonstrate that repeating suit emblems—not full narrative scenes—remained normal for pips.

**[Absence finding]** I found no museum-quality online record allowing a confident card-by-card description of a Two of Swords from either sheet. Claims assigning detailed divinatory symbolism to their low sword pips are retrospective.

#### Sola Busca, engraving dated 1491; colored copy probably c.1500, northern Italy

**[Documented object]** The Sola Busca is the earliest surviving complete tarot with every pip represented pictorially. Its cards are engravings, later richly colored in the Brera copy. The deck’s imagery draws on Roman history, pseudo-history, biblical figures, and Hermetic-alchemical culture.

**Important correction:** the surviving catalogues and online image indexes do not consistently display the Two of Swords, and some inventories imply that particular digitized selections omit it. Consequently, I could not verify from a high-resolution Brera record the frequently repeated assertion that the **Sola Busca Two itself** supplied Smith’s seated, blindfolded scene.

What can be verified is narrower:

- **[Documented fact]** Photographs of the Sola Busca were deposited at the British Museum in 1907, before Smith drew her deck.
- **[Scholarly reconstruction]** Smith probably saw them.
- **[Strong visual dependence]** Several RWS minors closely echo particular Sola Busca cards: especially the Three of Swords, Seven of Swords, Ten of Wands, and some court or suit compositions.
- **[Disputed/misattribution]** Calling the Sola Busca **Two of Swords** the direct model for the RWS Two is unsupported by the imagery presently traceable. The RWS blindfolded sitter is not a copied Sola Busca composition.

The item-specific lead is therefore **not verified** and, in its strong form, probably false.

#### “Mantegna Tarocchi,” c.1460s

**[Documented fact]** These are fifty educational engravings arranged in five ranked decades—social estates, Apollo and the Muses, liberal arts, virtues, and cosmic principles. They are not a tarot pack and have no suits or Two of Swords.

**[Historical misnomer]** Their relevance is to Renaissance serial imagery and later occult comparison, not to this card’s genealogy.

### Minchiate

**[Documented fact]** Florentine *Minchiate* or *Germini* expands the trump series to forty but retains the four Latin suits. Its Two of Swords remains a non-narrative pip: two long sword signs, normally interlaced or symmetrically opposed depending on edition.

**[Game function]** In the long suits, Swords and Batons descend from 10 toward Ace. The Two is therefore next-to-lowest among the numeral cards and carries no intrinsic card points.

**[Later esoteric reading]** Assigning the Minchiate Two the Golden Dawn’s Moon-in-Libra correspondence is an import from a different deck system.

### Bolognese pattern

**[Documented fact]** The shortened Tarocco Bolognese eliminated the low numeral cards 2–5. Consequently, the Two of Swords is absent from the surviving 62-card pack used for Ottocento.

Earlier full packs probably possessed it before shortening, but:

- **[Scholarly reconstruction]** its exact early appearance must be inferred from related Bolognese and northern Italian patterns;
- **[Documented fact]** a modern Bolognese pack contains no Two of Swords.

### Sicilian pattern

**[Documented fact]** Modern Sicilian Tarocchi is also shortened. Swords ordinarily retain numerals 5–10, so the Two of Swords is absent. The lowest surviving Sword is the Five.

This is not an occult omission: it is the result of adapting the pack for play.

### Tarot de Marseille lineage

#### Jean Noblet, Paris, c.1650

**[Documented object]** The *Deux d’Épées* is a formal pip. Two strongly curved, scimitar-like blades arc around a central floral stem or blossom, joining at top and bottom to produce an almond or lens-shaped enclosure. Small flowers or leaves fill peripheral spaces. Roman **II** indicates rank.

The “swords” are highly conventionalized. Their colored outer bands can look like foliage, frames, or crescents; handles and tips show that the pattern represents weapons.

#### Jean Dodal, Lyon, c.1701–1715

**[Documented object]** Dodal preserves the paired curved blades and central plant. Line, flower shape, and stencil colors differ; the basic syntax does not. The pip is ornamental rather than scenic.

#### Nicolas Conver, Marseille, 1760

**[Documented object; surviving copies complicated]** Conver’s Two establishes the familiar Type II Marseille appearance: two large curved swords form an oval around a prominent central flower with stems and leaves; corner florets balance the composition. Some cards or later printings display **II** at both ends.

A cataloguing caution is necessary. The familiar phrase “Conver 1760” names the original pattern and dated edition, but particular BnF impressions can be later nineteenth-century pulls or reissues associated with Conver’s workshop. The pattern date and the physical specimen date must not be conflated.

#### Grimaud / Paul Marteau, 1930s

**[Documented fact]** Grimaud’s *Ancien Tarot de Marseille*, codified and interpreted by Paul Marteau, regularized color and line. The Two remains two curved weapons surrounding vegetation. Marteau supplied numerological and color symbolism that is not demonstrably part of Noblet’s or Conver’s intentions.

#### Camoin–Jodorowsky, 1997

**[Modern reconstruction/redesign]** Philippe Camoin and Alejandro Jodorowsky produced a restored Marseille tarot assembled from several historical witnesses rather than a literal facsimile of one deck. Its Two preserves the floral enclosure.

Jodorowsky and Marianne Costa read the even sword pips as receptive accumulation. The central flower becomes a generative mental potential contained within duality. This is a twentieth-/twenty-first-century interpretive system, not a recovered medieval key.

### Besançon pattern

**[Documented fact]** Eighteenth-century Swiss, Alsatian, and southern German decks of the Besançon family replace the Papess and Pope with **Juno** and **Jupiter**, avoiding Catholic figures in Protestant or confessionally mixed markets. The Two of Swords itself normally remains a Marseille-derived floral pip.

These regional trump substitutions are important to tarot history but supply no special Two-of-Swords renaming.

### Etteilla

#### Etteilla’s original Grand Etteilla / *Livre de Thot*, 1789

**[Documented fact]** Jean-Baptiste Alliette (“Etteilla”) produced the first tarot explicitly redesigned for divination. Card **62** corresponds to the Two of Swords. Surviving uncut sheets are held by the Bibliothèque nationale de France.

The deck prints divinatory wording in opposite orientations. Later Grand Etteilla I, II, and III editions are not iconographically identical to the 1789 sheets and should not be casually substituted for them.

For the Two:

- upright: **Tendresse**—tenderness;
- reversed: **Fausseté** or equivalent wording—falsehood/deceit.

**[Documented tradition]** This semantic pair underlies later French and English lists.

**[Absence finding]** I found no defensible card-specific astrological assignment for Etteilla’s Two equivalent to the later Golden Dawn Moon in Libra. Etteilla astrologized his total deck, but online summaries often project Golden Dawn decans backward onto him.

### Oswald Wirth, 1889

**[Documented fact]** Wirth’s original tarot consisted of twenty-two trumps. It contains no Two of Swords. Later publishers have issued 78-card “Wirth” expansions, but their minors are post-Wirth additions.

### Golden Dawn manuscript tarot, c.1888–1900

**[Documented esoteric attribution]** The Golden Dawn’s *Book T* calls the card:

> “The Lord of Peace Restored.”

Its design prescription is two crossed swords or Air daggers, held by radiant angelic hands, with a five-petalled rose at their crossing. Small daggers support the Moon and Libra glyphs. It is **Chokmah of Vav**, Moon in Libra.

The text reads the Four Twos as the powers of King and Queen “just uniting and initiating the Force,” hence initiation and fecundation. For this card it gives, in part:

> “Arrangement, peace restored; truce; truth and untruth; sorrow and sympathy.”

It concludes:

> “Quarrel made up, yet still some tension in relations.”

**[Documented manuscript tradition; editorial caveat]** *Book T* circulated within the Order before appearing through later compilations and Crowley’s *Liber 78*. Exact authorship is distributed among Golden Dawn adepts rather than securely assignable to one hand.

#### Robert Wang reconstruction, 1977

**[Modern reconstruction]** Wang’s *Golden Dawn Tarot*, produced with Israel Regardie, visualizes the Order’s descriptions. Its Two uses crossed swords, central rose, angelic hands, and astrological signs. It is an illustration of nineteenth-century instructions, not a facsimile of a known official deck.

#### Sandra Tabatha and Chic Cicero reconstruction, 1991

**[Modern reconstruction]** The Ciceros’ deck likewise follows Golden Dawn color scales and ritual symbolism. Differences from Wang chiefly concern execution, palette, hand positions, and the application of Order color correspondences.

### Waite–Smith, 1909; book 1910/1911

**[Documented object]** Pamela Colman Smith replaces the heraldic pip with a human drama:

- a seated woman in a white robe;
- a blindfold over her eyes;
- two long swords crossed over her chest or balanced on her shoulders;
- a stone bench;
- water, rocky islets, and a crescent moon behind her.

Waite describes it succinctly:

> “A hoodwinked female figure balances two swords upon her shoulders.”

The body is still; the weapons form an X; the sea and crescent introduce the Moon-in-Libra atmosphere without printing astrological glyphs.

**What changed:** the Golden Dawn’s abstract balance became a psychologically ambiguous posture. “Peace restored” now looks guarded, effortful, and possibly avoidant.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** Likely sources include:

- Golden Dawn title and Moon-in-Libra attribution;
- conventional allegories of blindfolded Justice;
- theatrical tableau and Smith’s experience as an illustrator and stage designer;
- general, not card-specific, knowledge of the Sola Busca.

**[Disputed]** No surviving instruction sheet proves precisely how much Waite prescribed. The card cannot safely be described as a direct copy of the Sola Busca Two.

**[Modern interpretive development]** “Indecision,” “stalemate,” “denial,” and “refusal to choose” became dominant through readings of Smith’s image. Waite’s printed list stresses equipoise, concord, courage, friendship, and qualified harmony, not the now-standard phrase “difficult decision.”

### Paul Foster Case / BOTA

**[Documented esoteric lineage]** Case retained the Golden Dawn correspondences: Swords–Air, Two–Chokmah, Moon in Libra, “Peace Restored.” His BOTA tarot, drawn by Jessie Burns Parke and intended to be hand-colored, fully illustrates the trumps but ordinarily leaves the minor pips closer to formal emblems than the RWS narratives.

**[Tradition]** Case interprets the Two as reconciliation, balanced force, and a temporary settlement. Exact wording differs among BOTA lesson editions, many of which remain proprietary; I could not verify an authorized public-domain page giving a distinct upright/reversed pair.

### Thoth, painted 1938–1943; first full publication 1969

**[Documented object]** Aleister Crowley and Frieda Harris eliminate the human figure. Two swords cross in exact symmetry through a blue five-petalled rose. The flower emits white geometrical rays. Small upper and lower weapons bear or support the Moon and Libra symbols. The sword hilts incorporate peaceful or devotional figures.

Crowley writes:

> “The Two of Swords was formerly called the Lord of Peace Restored; but this word ‘restored’ is incorrect, because there has been no disturbance.”

He therefore renames it **Peace**. In his card commentary:

> “The Moon is change, but Nature is peaceful; moreover, Libra represents balance.”

The rose represents the Mother’s harmonizing influence. Harris turns the Golden Dawn diagram into a crystalline field of equilibrium.

**Meaning of the change:** peace is primordial balance in Chokmah, not merely reconciliation after a quarrel. Yet Crowley insists that the active Sword makes such peace conceptually difficult and potentially unstable.

### Notable modern redesigns

**[Modern invention]** Most contemporary decks follow one of three families:

1. **RWS-derived:** blindfolded person, crossed swords, moon, sea; frequently reframed as decision paralysis or boundary-setting.
2. **Thoth-derived:** sacred geometry, crossed blades, rose, Moon and Libra; framed as equilibrium.
3. **Marseille-derived:** two curved blades enclosing a flower; read numerologically rather than as a scene.

Examples such as the *Modern Witch Tarot* diversify the sitter’s gender, race, clothing, and setting while retaining Smith’s posture. Robert M. Place’s alchemical decks typically recast suit imagery through emblematic alchemy. These are intentional contemporary interpretations, not evidence for fifteenth-century meaning.

## The game

### Original function

**[Documented fact]** The Two of Swords began as a playing card. There is no evidence that fifteenth-century owners treated it as an occult “minor arcanum.”

A tarot trick is won by the highest trump, or—if no trump is played—the highest card of the suit led. Players normally must follow suit where the regional rules require it.

### Rank

In the traditional Latin long suits, Swords and Batons rank downward:

**King, Queen, Knight, Page, 10, 9, 8, 7, 6, 5, 4, 3, 2, Ace.**

Thus the Two of Swords is:

- below the Three;
- above the Ace;
- the second-lowest card of its suit.

Cups and Coins traditionally reverse the numeral hierarchy, with Ace high among the numeral cards. Dummett suggested that this opposition of “long” and “round” suits was fundamental to early tarot play.

In modern French Tarot, all ordinary suits are usually ranked uniformly:

**King, Queen, Knight, Jack, 10 … 2, Ace.**

The Two of Spades is correspondingly low.

### Card points

**[Documented fact]** The Two normally has no special counting value. In point-count systems it functions as an empty or low card that completes a counting group. Kings, queens, knights, pages, high trumps, and the Fool receive the principal values.

In Minchiate, only the Kings score among the ordinary suits; the Two is worthless individually. In many classic tarot games, low cards matter tactically as suit-length, discard material, and counters in grouped scoring.

### Shortened packs

- **Tarocco Bolognese:** Twos through Fives removed; no Two of Swords.
- **Tarocco Siciliano:** Swords below Five removed; no Two.
- **54-card central European Tarock:** low black-suit cards retained differently from red suits; a Two of Spades may be absent depending on the pack.
- **78-card French Tarot:** Two of Spades retained.

### Fool

**[Documented fact]** The Fool is not the Two’s suit superior. In many games it is an Excuse: played to a trick without following suit and normally recovered by its owner. In others, especially central-European forms, the *Sküs* acts as the highest trump.

### Trump orders and why they do not rank this card

Dummett classified early Italian trump sequences as:

- **Order A:** Bologna and Florence;
- **Order B:** Ferrara;
- **Order C:** Milan.

Justice and Fortitude/Strength shift relative position between these orders. Papal and imperial trumps may be equalized in Bologna; the Fool may be unnumbered or treated specially. None of this makes the Two of Swords a trump. The disputes matter only to the overall pack in which it was played.

## Chronology of attestation

### c.1418–1425 — Marziano da Tortona

**[Documented text; proto-tarot status reconstructed]** Marziano described a pack commissioned for Filippo Maria Visconti and painted by Michelino da Besozzo. Sixteen gods served as superior cards over four bird suits.

It contains no Two of Swords: the ordinary suits were Eagles, Phoenixes, Turtledoves, and Doves. It is relevant as the earliest surviving description of a pack with permanent trump-like cards, not as an attestation of this pip.

### 1440 — Florence

**[Documented archival record]** Records mention *naibi a trionfi*. They attest triumph packs, but not individual pip names.

### 1441–1442 — Ferrara

**[Documented archival records]** Ferrarese ledgers pay painters and merchants for *carte da trionfi*. A July 1442 entry records packs supplied for Ercole and Sigismondo d’Este. Another record concerns painted suit signs and figures.

These demonstrate that Swords belonged to the ordinary component of triumph packs. They do not name the Two individually.

### c.1440s — Cary–Yale and Brambilla cards

**[Documented objects]** The Cary–Yale Two is the earliest surviving physical witness located for the card. It is a rank sign, not a named allegory.

### c.1450–1460 — Visconti-Sforza

**[Documented object]** The surviving Two confirms the standard fourteen-card Sword suit in an almost complete luxury tarot.

### c.1480–1500 — *Sermo de ludo*

**[Documented manuscript; date disputed]** The anonymous anti-gambling sermon lists the standard trumps, including the Fool. Robert Steele initially dated the manuscript c.1450–1470, then 1450–1480. Watermark study moved the volume toward the 1490s or c.1500, though the sermon’s composition may be earlier.

It is not an individual attestation of the Two of Swords. Its importance is that it distinguishes ordinary cards from *trionfi* and supplies the earliest full named trump list.

### 1491 — Sola Busca

**[Documented date on object]** The engraved deck is the earliest complete surviving tarot and earliest complete scenic pip series.

### c.1500 — *tarocchi*

**[Documented linguistic development]** The word *tarocchi* replaces *trionfi* as the ordinary name of the game and pack. The etymology remains unresolved.

### 1527 — Teofilo Folengo

**[Documented text]** Folengo’s *Caos del Triperuno* incorporates tarot trumps into sonnets describing four people’s fortunes. It is sometimes called early tarot divination, but it is a literary/social use of dealt trumps rather than proof of a stable 78-card occult lexicon. It does not give a Two-of-Swords meaning.

### 1530s–1550s — *tarocchi appropriati*

**[Documented literary game]** Poets assigned trump subjects to people in laudatory or satirical verse. Again, the practice concerns trump allegories, not pip-card divination.

### 1637 — Abbé de Marolles

**[Documented text]** Provides the earliest detailed rules for French tarot play. The suit cards are gaming instruments; no occult meaning is assigned to the Two.

### c.1650–1760 — Marseille lineage

**[Documented objects]** Noblet, Dodal, Madenié, Conver, and others establish the paired-curved-sword floral pattern.

### 1781 — Court de Gébelin and Comte de Mellet

**[Documented occult texts]** Court identifies tarot as an Egyptian sacred book; Mellet connects the 22 trumps with Hebrew letters and describes divination. Neither supplies the later Golden Dawn meaning or Moon-in-Libra attribution for the Two.

### 1783–1789 — Etteilla

**[Documented occult practice]** Etteilla publishes systematic upright and reversed meanings and then a divinatory tarot. The Two becomes card 62, Tenderness/Falsehood.

### 1854–1856 — Éliphas Lévi

**[Documented occult text]** *Dogme et rituel de la haute magie* systematizes the 22 trumps, Hebrew alphabet, and Kabbalah. It does not establish a card-specific doctrine for the Two of Swords.

### 1863–1870 — Paul Christian

**[Documented occult invention]** Christian develops Egyptian initiation narratives and popularizes *arcane/lame* terminology. His system is overwhelmingly trump-centered.

### 1888 — Mathers

**[Documented printed text]** *The Tarot: Its Occult Signification* prints the Two’s meanings: Friendship, Valor, Firmness, Courage; reversed False Friends, Treachery, Lies. Mathers acknowledges that much supplementary material comes from Etteilla.

### 1888–1900 — Golden Dawn

**[Documented esoteric construction]** The Two receives its decan, Sephirah, divine-name structure, angelic imagery, and title “Peace Restored.”

### 1889 — Papus and Wirth

**[Documented publications]** Papus’s *Tarot des Bohémiens* spreads the Hebrew-letter, Kabbalistic, and “Gypsy book” mythology. Wirth supplies twenty-two occult trumps. Neither originated the Golden Dawn Two.

### 1909–1911 — Waite–Smith and *Pictorial Key*

**[Documented object and text]** Smith supplies the canonical blindfolded woman; Waite supplies the qualified harmony/falsehood meanings.

### 1938–1943; 1969 — Thoth

**[Documented object/publication history]** Harris paints the card; Crowley retitles it “Peace.” The full deck appears commercially only after both creators’ principal work was complete.

### 1970s onward — psychological tarot

**[Modern development]** Writers such as Rachel Pollack and Mary K. Greer shift emphasis from fortune-telling events to consciousness, blocked choice, projection, shadow, relationship dynamics, and therapeutic reflection.

### Vav and Chokmah

**[Esoteric attribution]** In Golden Dawn notation, the four suits correspond to the letters of YHVH:

- Wands — Yod;
- Cups — Heh;
- Swords — **Vav**;
- Pentacles — final Heh.

The pip number maps to a Sephirah. Two corresponds to **Chokmah**, Wisdom, the second Sephirah. Thus “Chokmah of Vav” means the second Sephirah operating in the elemental/suit world of Air.

This is not the card’s “Hebrew letter” in the same sense as a trump-path attribution. Pips do not each receive one of the 22 alphabetic paths.

### Moon in Libra

**[Esoteric attribution]** The Golden Dawn distributes cards 2–10 over the 36 zodiacal decans. The Two of Swords governs the first decan of Libra. The decan ruler is the Moon under the Chaldean planetary sequence.

Symbolic synthesis:

- Moon: fluctuation, reflection, receptivity;
- Libra: balance, mediation, equality;
- Air/Swords: intellect, distinction, conflict;
- Chokmah/Two: first differentiated force.

The resulting “peace” is a balance of opposed motions, not inert passivity.

### Courts: not applicable

The requested sub-element and zodiacal span apply to court cards, not this pip. For orientation, Golden Dawn courts combine elements—Knight/King as Fire, Queen as Water, Prince as Air, Princess as Earth—with each senior court ruling roughly 30° of zodiac. The Two instead occupies one decan, approximately 0°–10° Libra.

### Tzaddi/Heh and Emperor/Star

**[Documented Crowleyan revision; irrelevant to this pip]** Crowley’s “Tzaddi is not the Star” exchange moves Hebrew-letter relationships among Trump IV, the Emperor, and Trump XVII, the Star. It does not change the Two of Swords, whose Moon-in-Libra decan remains intact.

## Divinatory meanings by author

### Etteilla, 1780s

**[Documented divinatory attribution]**

- Upright: **“Tendresse”** — tenderness.
- Reversed: **“Fausseté”** — falsity, deceit.

Later Grand Etteilla editions sometimes modernize or substitute near-synonyms. These should be identified by edition rather than silently attributed to 1789.

### S. L. MacGregor Mathers, 1888

From *The Tarot: Its Occult Signification*, p.17 in the consulted PDF:

- Upright: **“Friendship, Valor, Firmness, Courage.”**
- Reversed: **“False Friends, Treachery, Lies.”**

Mathers adds that, if confirmed by surrounding cards, the reversed card may merely mean that friends “are not of much use” in the matter.

### Golden Dawn, *Book T*

**[Documented esoteric divination]**

Well-dignified or general meanings include:

- arrangement;
- peace restored;
- truce;
- justice;
- unselfishness;
- aid to the weak;
- strength through suffering;
- pleasure after pain.

Ambivalent or ill-dignified expressions include:

- truth mixed with untruth;
- repeated affronts after pardon;
- injury while meaning well;
- tactlessness;
- excessive petitioning or talk.

The summary is “quarrel made up, yet still some tension.”

Golden Dawn practice usually reads dignity from neighboring cards rather than mechanically turning every card upside down.

### A. E. Waite, *Pictorial Key*, 1910/1911

Waite’s complete concise entry says:

> “Conformity and the equipoise which it suggests, courage, friendship, concord in a state of arms; another reading gives tenderness, affection, intimacy.”

He immediately qualifies this:

> “The suggestion of harmony … must be considered in a qualified manner, as Swords generally are not symbolical of beneficent forces.”

Reversed:

> “Imposture, falsehood, duplicity, disloyalty.”

**Genealogy:** Waite blends Mathers and Etteilla but filters them through Smith’s tense image and the Golden Dawn’s “Peace Restored.”

### Aleister Crowley, *Book of Thoth*, 1944

Crowley does not favor a simple upright/reversed dictionary. The operative meaning is **Peace**, the highest possible manifestation of the unstable Sword suit. His inherited practical list includes reconciliation with lingering tension.

- Constructive: peace, arrangement, truce, balance, reconciliation.
- Adverse/ill-dignified: unstable agreement, contradiction, renewed affront, tactlessness, mixed selfish and unselfish action.
- Mechanical reversed meaning: **not independently specified** in the *Book of Thoth*.

Claims that Crowley explicitly made the reversed Two “release of tension” or “the end of false peace” were not found in his text and appear to be modern extrapolations.

### Paul Foster Case

**[Golden Dawn-derived attribution]**

- Upright/general: peace restored, settlement, balancing opposed forces, reconciliation, a period of quiet after strain.
- Adverse: precarious truce, insincerity, unresolved opposition.

**[Source limitation]** I could not verify a stable, public primary Case text containing a canonical upright/reversed formula. Commercial BOTA lesson texts and later Case-derived manuals vary. Exact lists should not be put in quotation marks without a specific edition.

### Rachel Pollack, *Seventy-Eight Degrees of Wisdom*, 1980 combined edition

**[Modern psychological reading]** Pollack treats the RWS figure as shutting out involvement so that a fragile balance can be maintained. The crossed arms and blindfold embody emotional withdrawal and refusal to engage; the sea suggests feeling held at a distance.

Typical Pollack-derived summary:

- upright: blocked choice, emotional closure, tense balance, denial;
- reversed: the stalemate breaks, but feeling or confusion can flood back.

**[Quotation limitation]** I did not locate an authorized open text permitting exact verification of her card entry. These are paraphrases, not claimed verbatim quotations.

### Mary K. Greer, *The Complete Book of Tarot Reversals*, 2002

**[Modern psychological/practical reading]** Greer does not treat reversal as a single fixed opposite. Her system permits the upright quality to be blocked, delayed, internalized, excessive, projected, or released.

Applied to this card:

- upright: truce, indecision, maintaining boundaries, holding alternatives in suspension;
- reversed: breaking a stalemate, forced choice, information or emotion breaking through; alternatively greater confusion or refusal to decide.

A frequently repeated “release of blocked energy, often through tears or anger” is plausible as a Greer-style application but could not be verified as her exact sentence from an accessible primary scan. It should not be quoted as certain.

## Esoteric and symbolic readings

### Numerological

**[Golden Dawn attribution, late nineteenth century]** Two is Chokmah: the first outflow from unity and the beginning of polarity. In *Book T*, all Twos show King and Queen “just uniting,” before the forces represented by Prince and Princess are fully active.

**[Jodorowsky–Costa, modern]** Two is receptive accumulation—energy gathered but not yet released. In the Marseille Two of Swords, the central flower is thought incubating inside a closed oval.

No evidence traces these readings to fifteenth-century cardmakers.

### Kabbalistic

**[Esoteric attribution]** Chokmah in the world/suit of Vav–Air gives primordial Wisdom in the sphere of analytical mind. The two blades are differentiation held in equilibrium.

The five-petalled rose has several overlapping occult readings:

- harmonizing influence of the Mother;
- five as the reconciling pentagram or microcosm;
- organic life flowering from the crossing of severe forces.

These belong to Golden Dawn and Crowleyan synthesis.

### Astrological

**[Esoteric attribution]** Moon in Libra produces mutable feeling regulated by balance, courtesy, and relationship. The favorable reading is diplomacy; the shadow is appeasement, indecision, or peace maintained by avoiding unwelcome knowledge.

The popular modern interpretation “head versus heart” is an intelligible synthesis of Air/Swords and Moon/water imagery but is not a medieval doctrine.

### Hermetic

**[Golden Dawn/Crowleyan]** Crossed blades reconcile opposites around a living center. Crowley connects the card with silence, chastity, and the “negative form of the positive idea”: tremendous energy expressed as zero or stillness.

### Alchemical

**[Modern extension grounded in Sola Busca scholarship]** Two weapons may be read as contrary agents, and the flower as a conjunctive product. Yet no early alchemical gloss of the Visconti or Marseille Two survives.

The Brera exhibition interprets the Sola Busca deck broadly within Hermetic-alchemical culture. That does not prove that every pip, much less the later Marseille Two, encodes a specific laboratory operation.

### Christian

**[Possible traditional association, not documented card doctrine]** Crossed swords can evoke conflict restrained under charity; the central flower can be read as grace or peace. The Golden Dawn’s angelic hands are explicitly sacral.

There is no evidence that the medieval card was designed as a Christian allegory comparable to a trump such as Judgment or the Pope.

### Jungian

**[Modern invention/application]** Later psychological tarot treats the RWS woman as an image of:

- tension between conscious alternatives;
- repression of feeling;
- protective withdrawal;
- ego-maintained equilibrium;
- the “transcendent function,” in which opposites are held until a third possibility emerges.

Jung himself did not publish an interpretation of the Two of Swords. “Jungian tarot” is an application by later therapists, analysts, and tarot writers, not Jung’s tarot system.

#### Italo Calvino

**[Documented tarot reception]** *Il castello dei destini incrociati* (1969/1973) constructs narratives from tarot layouts, using the Visconti-Sforza and Marseille traditions. It demonstrates tarot’s literary combinatorics.

**[Absence finding]** No unique, sustained Calvino episode can securely be assigned to the Two of Swords alone without specifying the edition’s layout and card sequence.

#### T. S. Eliot

**[Documented literary use]** *The Waste Land* invokes “Madame Sosostris” and names several invented or transformed tarot images.

**[Absence finding]** The Two of Swords is not named. Eliot’s tarot is relevant to modern cultural reception, not this card’s specific history.

#### W. B. Yeats

**[Documented occult context]** Yeats belonged to the Golden Dawn and engaged with its symbolism.

**[Absence finding]** I found no primary Yeats text centered on the Two of Swords.

#### Alejandro Jodorowsky

**[Documented practice]** Tarot profoundly shaped Jodorowsky’s writing, teaching, “psychomagic,” and visual imagination. With Costa he gave the Marseille pips a systematic numerological reading.

**[Disputed overreach]** Identifying individual film shots as deliberate quotations of the Two requires production evidence. Formal resemblance to crossed swords or blindfolded figures is insufficient.

#### Ingmar Bergman

**[Absence finding]** Bergman’s *The Seventh Seal* concerns chess, Death, faith, and medieval imagery, not a documented quotation of this card. I found no reliable source tying Bergman specifically to the Two of Swords.

#### Paintings and buildings

**[Absence finding]** I found no securely documented pre-1909 painting or building designed to quote the Two of Swords. Blindfolded Justice, crossed-weapon trophies, and lunar seascapes are possible visual sources or analogues, not necessarily tarot quotations.

Modern tarot murals, tattoos, album artwork, and installations frequently reproduce the RWS pose, but a comprehensive catalogue would require a separate contemporary-art survey.

## Controversies and disputes

### 1. Was the Sola Busca Two Smith’s direct source?

**Classification: disputed/misattribution.**

Evidence supporting a general connection:

- Sola Busca photographs reached the British Museum in 1907.
- Smith worked in London and likely had access.
- Several RWS minors closely parallel Sola Busca compositions.

Evidence against the card-specific claim:

- The RWS Two’s blindfolded seated woman, crescent moon, sea, and symmetrical crossed swords are not established as a copy of the Sola Busca Two.
- Golden Dawn *Book T* already supplied crossed swords, equilibrium, Moon in Libra, and “Peace Restored.”
- Blindfolded Justice offered a readily available allegorical model.

**Finding:** Sola Busca is a probable deck-wide source, but the assertion “the Sola Busca Two is the source of Smith’s scene” could not be verified.

### 2. Egyptian origin

**Classification: legend/esoteric attribution.**

Court de Gébelin asserted in 1781 that tarot preserved an Egyptian Book of Thoth and proposed an imaginary Egyptian etymology. He wrote before Egyptian hieroglyphs were deciphered and cited no medieval chain of transmission.

Etteilla made the Egyptian framework into an operational divination system. Paul Christian, Papus, and later occultists amplified it.

Material and archival evidence instead places tarot’s formation in fifteenth-century northern Italy. Egyptian or Mamluk playing cards contributed to the earlier history of European suit systems, but that is different from saying the 78-card tarot was an Egyptian sacred book.

### 3. Romani or “Gypsy” transmission

**Classification: legend.**

Nineteenth-century authors, especially Jean-Alexandre Vaillant and Papus, popularized the idea that Roma preserved and transmitted tarot. “Egyptian” was then wrongly connected with the exonym “Gypsy.”

No documentary evidence links Romani communities to the invention of fifteenth-century Italian tarot. Roma have, however, participated in European fortune-telling traditions; denying the origin legend should not erase that later cultural history.

### 4. Cathar and Templar tarot

**Classification: modern legend/disputed speculation.**

Claims that tarot secretly preserved Cathar dualism or Templar teaching generally arise in twentieth-century esoteric literature. No fifteenth-century ledger, rule text, card inscription, or inquisitorial document connects the invention of tarot with either community.

The Two’s paired weapons can be made to illustrate dualism after the fact, but this is analogy, not provenance.

### 5. Ancient Kabbalistic origin

**Classification: esoteric attribution presented as history.**

The correspondence between the 22 trumps and 22 Hebrew letters first becomes visible in the 1781 Comte de Mellet essay and is systematized by Lévi, Papus, the Golden Dawn, Case, and Crowley.

No fifteenth-century Hebrew-letter labels survive on tarot cards. The Two’s “Chokmah of Vav” is a Golden Dawn construction, not a Renaissance title recovered from an Italian workshop.

### 6. Air as the “original” suit element

**Classification: esoteric attribution.**

Swords = Air is standard Golden Dawn, RWS-adjacent, and Thoth practice. Other occultists and modern decks have occasionally assigned Swords to Fire. Playing-card evidence establishes Swords as a suit sign, not its primordial elemental meaning.

### 7. Blindfold = indecision

**Classification: modern interpretive dominance.**

Waite calls the woman “hoodwinked” but gives courage, friendship, concord, and qualified equipoise. He does not print “indecision” as the headline meaning.

The now-standard “cannot choose” reading arose from later viewers treating Smith’s posture as psychological narrative. It is coherent and influential, but later than the image.

### 8. Justice’s blindfold as a direct source

**Classification: scholarly reconstruction.**

Blindfolded Justice was common in European art, but not universal, and tarot Justice itself was usually not blindfolded. Smith may have deliberately displaced the attribute from Justice to a pip about suspended judgment. No surviving statement from her proves this intention.

### 9. “Curved scimitars”

**Classification: descriptive convention.**

Marseille commentators call the curved forms scimitars. Historically, they are stylized sword suit signs evolved through copying and interlacing. Calling them literal representations of a particular Islamic weapon type goes beyond the evidence.

### 10. Conver 1760

**Classification: cataloguing problem.**

“Conver 1760” can mean:

- a deck actually printed in 1760;
- the pattern established by Conver;
- a later impression from inherited blocks;
- a modern reconstruction colored after another edition.

Iconographic discussion must distinguish these.

### 11. Wirth’s “Two of Swords”

**Classification: modern extension.**

Wirth’s 1889 tarot has only 22 cards. A 78-card Wirth deck necessarily supplies minors created or compiled by later editors.

### 12. Crowley’s reversals

**Classification: modern misattribution.**

Crowley generally works through dignity, elemental interaction, and the card’s inherent polarity. Modern websites that give a neat “Crowley reversed Two” often manufacture a reversal from his general discussion.

### 13. Misprints and editorial traditions

Several textual problems affect this card’s neighborhood:

- Golden Dawn manuscripts circulate with variant titles and transcription errors.
- Thoth booklets sometimes print a neighboring Sword title incorrectly—most famously calling the Four “Truth” where the card says “Truce.”
- “Peace Restored” is not a misprint: Crowley deliberately rejected “Restored.”
- Conver’s date is often incorrectly used as the date of any specimen bearing his design.
- Modern deck indexes sometimes omit or misidentify Sola Busca cards, producing false claims about what survives.

No consequential early misprint unique to the Two of Swords was securely traced.

## Open questions

1. **Which surviving Two is absolutely earliest?** Cary–Yale is the best candidate located, but dates for Cary–Yale and Brambilla remain ranges rather than documented commission dates.

2. **Does a securely catalogued original Brambilla Two survive?** Accessible summaries establish the pack’s Sword style but did not settle this card’s inventory status.

3. **What exactly is depicted on the Sola Busca Two?** A high-resolution, card-specific Brera catalogue entry is needed. General deck descriptions are insufficient.

4. **What instructions did Waite give Smith?** No known surviving card-by-card design brief resolves where prescription ended and Smith’s invention began.

5. **Did Smith consciously quote blindfolded Justice?** The resemblance is strong but documentary proof is absent.

6. **How early was the “indecision” interpretation?** Waite does not foreground it. A systematic survey of interwar and postwar English manuals could date its transition from visual inference to standard keyword.

7. **Which Etteilla issue first fixes the Two as *Tendresse/Fausseté*?** The attribution is stable in the tradition, but the precise evolution across Etteilla’s *cahiers*, 1789 sheets, and nineteenth-century Grand Etteilla editions warrants bibliographic collation.

8. **What were the original colors of individual Marseille specimens?** Stencil variation, later pulls, recoloring, and restorations complicate claims about symbolic color.

9. **Can a Renaissance meaning be reconstructed?** At present there is no contemporary evidence that the paired swords or flower carried a divinatory message. Any answer beyond rank, suit, ornament, and game function remains conjectural.
