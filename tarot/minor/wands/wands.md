# The Suit of Wands: Research Dossier

## Method and evidentiary labels

This dossier concerns the complete fourteen-card suit—Ace through Ten, Page, Knight, Queen, King—not a single card.

Labels used throughout:

- **Documented fact**: stated in a dated object, archival record, rules text, contemporary publication, or institutional catalogue.
- **Scholarly reconstruction**: a conclusion argued from surviving objects and documents but not directly stated by them.
- **Tradition**: a convention transmitted within card-making, cartomancy, or occult practice.
- **Esoteric attribution**: a deliberately constructed occult correspondence.
- **Disputed claim**: supported by competing interpretations or inadequate evidence.
- **Legend**: a historical claim contradicted by chronology or unsupported by surviving evidence.
- **Modern invention**: a demonstrably recent interpretation, image, or system presented as new—or sometimes retrospectively presented as ancient.

No surviving medieval source says that Batons/Wands represent Fire, will, creativity, the phallus, intuition, the Grail lance, Atziluth, or Yod. Those are later interpretive systems.

### Names and what they imply

**Documented fact:** Italian *bastoni*, French *bâtons*, and Spanish *bastos* designate sticks, staffs, cudgels, or batons—not necessarily a magician’s wand.

**Scholarly reconstruction:** these Latin-suit signs probably descend from the Mamluk polo-stick suit. The Mamluk cards have four suits—coins, cups, swords, and polo-sticks—with numeral cards and three inscribed court ranks. The structural and emblematic correspondence with early Latin cards is much closer than with any proposed Egyptian temple book. [International Playing-Card Society overview](https://www.i-p-c-s.org/wp/an-introduction/), [L. A. Mayer, “Mamluk Playing Cards”](https://www.persee.fr/doc/bifao_0255-0962_1939_num_38_1_1998)

**Tradition:** “Wands,” “Rods,” and “Sceptres” are English occult refinements. They encourage magical and royal readings that are not inherent in *bastone*. Waite alternates “Wands or Sceptres”; Mathers used Sceptres in some formulations; modern publishers frequently use Wands.

**Documented absence:** “Suit of Wands” has no number comparable to a trump number. The fourteen cards are individually ranked; the suit itself is neither an *arcano maggiore* nor an additional trump.

# Iconography, deck by deck

## 1. Mamluk playing cards: the polo-stick ancestry

### Topkapı Mamluk pack, probably fifteenth century

**Documented fact:** the surviving group contains cards bearing Arabic suit names and inscriptions. Its four emblems are cups, coins, swords, and polo-sticks. The courts are not portraits: they are ornate geometric cards named by inscriptions—King, Deputy King, and Second Deputy.

The polo sticks are long, often curved at one end, and arranged symmetrically. They are sporting implements, not botanical branches or magical rods.

**Scholarly reconstruction:** the European baton suit descends from this *jawkān* suit. The parallel comprises:

1. the same four-suit system;
2. ten numeral ranks;
3. several court ranks;
4. close correspondence between three suit signs;
5. transformation of polo sticks into more familiar European sticks or clubs.

The idea that Europeans “mistook” polo sticks for cudgels is plausible shorthand, not a documented account from a medieval card-maker. Cultural adaptation could have been deliberate.

**Disputed details:** L. A. Mayer’s reconstruction, the date of individual fragments, and whether a few cards belong to the same pack have been revised since 1939. The broad Mamluk-to-Latin relationship is much firmer than the exact genealogy of one particular surviving pack.

## 2. Marziano da Tortona and Michelino da Besozzo, Milan, approximately 1412–1425

Marziano’s *De deificatione sedecim heroum* describes a commissioned pack for Filippo Maria Visconti, painted by Michelino da Besozzo.

**Documented fact:** its four suits were birds—eagles, phoenixes, turtledoves, and doves—and its sixteen superior cards represented gods. No cards are known to survive.

**Scholarly reconstruction:** it is a “proto-tarot” because a separately ordered series beats the ordinary suit cards.

**Not evidence for Wands:** Marziano’s text neither names *bastoni* nor supports Wands = Fire. Phoenixes belong to one of his bird suits, but connecting them to the later fiery Wands is retrospective symbolism.

## 3. Visconti di Modrone / Cary-Yale group, Milan, approximately 1440s

The names “Visconti di Modrone” and “Cary-Yale” have often been applied to the same extraordinary partial pack.

**Documented fact:** it differs from the eventual standard tarot by having six court figures per suit—male and female equivalents—and by including unusual trumps. Its staves bear courtly heraldic ornament.

**Iconography:** the numeral cards display actual staff-like emblems on richly gilded grounds; the court cards turn the sign into an attribute held by an aristocratic figure. The object remains a baton or staff, not yet the leafing magical branch familiar from Waite-Smith.

**Meaning:** the imagery communicates dynasty, rank, wealth, and courtly display. An elemental Fire doctrine is not attested.

## 4. Brambilla-Brera tarot, Milan, approximately 1440s

**Documented fact:** the surviving Brambilla cards are preserved in the Pinacoteca di Brera. Pip cards from the baton suit arrange blue-and-gold staff forms against decorated grounds; courts carry the emblem.

**Scholarly reconstruction:** heraldic details associate the pack with Filippo Maria Visconti and the Milanese court.

**Iconographic importance:** these are luxury transformations of ordinary suit signs. The baton can look ceremonial, but “ritual wand” would be anachronistic.

## 5. Visconti-Sforza group, Milan, approximately 1450–1460, with later additions

The most substantial surviving group is divided among the Morgan Library, Accademia Carrara, and Colleoni family.

**Documented fact:** the Morgan owns the Ace, Four, and Nine of Staves and the King of Staves. The full reconstructed pack had fourteen cards in each suit. Attribution has moved among Bonifacio Bembo, the Bembo workshop, Francesco Zavattari, and other candidates; some stylistically different cards have been treated as replacements or additions. [Morgan object record](https://www.themorgan.org/collection/tarot-cards)

**Iconography:**

- The pips are elongated, ornamented staves laid on gold.
- Repetition and symmetry organize the numeral cards.
- Heraldic devices—Visconti crowns and Sforza interlaced rings—bind the suit to family identity.
- The King holds or is associated with the staff as a mark of suit and rank.

**Meaning:** these cards display noble possession and play. Nothing in the objects labels the staff as Fire, inspiration, sexuality, or spiritual Will.

## 6. Ferrarese and Este cards, later fifteenth century

Surviving Ferrarese cards are fragmentary and divided among collections. Attribution and grouping remain difficult because luxury cards could be copied, dispersed, or combined by later collectors.

**Iconography:** Ferrarese suit cards generally retain recognizable Latin emblems, though ornament may swallow the distinction between literal cudgel, sceptre, and decorative rod.

**Documented fact:** Este accounts record purchases of *carte da trionfi*, including the celebrated 1442 entry. Such ledgers prove the production or purchase of triumph packs, but usually do not describe each suit card.

**Disputed claim:** individual anonymous fragments are frequently assigned to “the Este tarot” more confidently in popular books than their provenance permits.

## 7. Mantegna “Tarocchi,” approximately 1460s–1470s

**Documented fact:** the so-called Mantegna Tarocchi are fifty didactic engravings arranged into five hierarchical series. They are neither by Mantegna nor a normal tarot pack.

**Relevance:** they demonstrate the Renaissance appetite for ordered visual encyclopaedias—social estates, Muses, liberal arts, virtues, and celestial spheres—but contain no fourteen-card baton suit.

**Finding:** they are relevant as comparative Renaissance imagery, not as an ancestor or witness of Wands.

## 8. Sola Busca, northern Italy, approximately 1491

**Documented fact:** the Sola Busca is the earliest surviving substantially complete tarot with illustrated narrative scenes across its numeral cards. It contains a Bastoni suit, but each card is more than a pip pattern.

**Iconography:** armour, strange historical or pseudo-historical figures, vessels, fires, tools, military actions, and enigmatic episodes replace the non-scenic convention. The staves are integrated into scenes and may become poles, clubs, implements, or compositional structures.

**Waite-Smith connection—scholarly reconstruction:** Pamela Colman Smith probably saw British Museum photographs of the Sola Busca in 1908. Several Waite-Smith compositions have unusually close analogues, especially the Three of Swords and Ten of Wands; the latter’s burdened figure is the clearest Wands-suit comparison. “Smith copied the whole minor arcana from Sola Busca” is too strong. She selectively transformed motifs into a new, coherent narrative programme.

## 9. Rosenwald and Budapest sheets, late fifteenth or early sixteenth century

These uncut or partially preserved woodcut sheets are central evidence for the passage from luxury hand-painted cards to printed popular cards.

**Documented fact:** they preserve economical line designs and repeated Latin suit signs.

**Iconography:** batons are crossed, layered, and interlaced. Floral or foliate fillers occupy spaces between them. This anticipates the later Marseille habit of making the numbered cards ornamental patterns rather than narrative tableaux.

**Caution:** exact dates, places, and relations among anonymous sheets remain scholarly reconstructions. “Prototype of the Tarot de Marseille” describes family resemblance, not a surviving printer’s model book.

## 10. Bolognese tarocchino

Bologna reduced the ordinary tarot pack by removing low pip cards; later play commonly uses a sixty-two-card pack.

**Iconography:** traditional double-ended Bolognese designs preserve Latin suits. Batons are compact clubs or ceremonial rods rather than living twigs.

**Game consequence:** because low cards were removed, a “complete fourteen-card Wands system” is not present in ordinary tarocchino play.

**Documented fact:** Bolognese tarot remained principally a game. Occult Wands meanings are later imports.

## 11. Minchiate, Florence, sixteenth century onward

Minchiate expands the trump series to forty-one numbered trumps plus the Fool, making ninety-seven cards with the ordinary suits.

**Iconography:** the baton suit follows the long, straight northern-Italian staff convention more than the knobbly Spanish club. In many packs the pages of Cups and Coins are female while those of Swords and Batons remain male—a gender distinction with game-pattern ancestry, not necessarily elemental symbolism.

**Game order:** Batons are a “long” suit, 10 high and Ace low. Minchiate scoring emphasizes combinations or *versicole* as well as tricks; ordinary suit cards are mostly low-value trick material compared with kings and selected honours. [Minchiate rules and historical summary](https://www.pagat.com/tarot/minchiate.html)

## 12. Sicilian tarot

Sicilian tarocchi developed a shortened, distinctive pack.

**Iconography:** the suit is called **Bastoni** and appears as thick clubs. Numerals are often indexed by number and suit initial.

**Game:** in the surviving game the suit ranks King, Queen, Horse, Maid, 10 downward; shortened packs may omit the lowest ranks. The exact pack varies locally. [Sicilian Tarocchi rules](https://www.pagat.com/tarot/sicilian.html)

## 13. Tarot de Marseille lineage

### Jean Noblet, Paris, approximately 1650

The surviving Noblet is incomplete, but it is the earliest substantial named example of what is now called Tarot de Marseille Type I.

**Iconography of Bâtons:**

- The numeral batons cross diagonally to form a lattice.
- Their cut ends, wrappings, and coloured shafts distinguish them from Swords.
- Flowers, leaves, and curling stems fill the central and marginal openings.
- The Ace is an oversized hand-held club or branch.
- Court figures hold a baton vertically or diagonally as their suit badge.

The plant ornament is not yet a narrative that “the suit is alive.” It is part of a broader pip-card vocabulary.

### Jean Dodal, Lyon, approximately 1701–1715

Dodal preserves the Type I family.

**Iconographic change:** line work becomes more expansive and idiosyncratic; captions and card-maker inscriptions are prominent. Batons remain a woven lattice with floral fillers.

### Pierre Madenié, Dijon, 1709; Jean-Pierre Payen, 1713

These help establish Type II. Small details of terminals, crossings, foliage, and court posture differ, but the suit’s grammar remains stable.

### Nicolas Conver, Marseille, 1760/1761

Conver’s pack became an influential reference for later commercial Marseille editions.

**Iconography:** thick crossed batons, alternating colours, central rosettes or blossoms, and curling vegetation. The Ace is a large rough club rising from a hand.

**Interpretive caution:** twentieth-century readers often discover faces, sexual organs, chakras, or coded alchemical emblems in the interstices. Some shapes are genuinely printed; claims that their occult meanings were intended by Conver require independent evidence, which has not been found.

### Grimaud / Paul Marteau, Paris, 1930

**Documented fact:** Marteau’s *Ancien Tarot de Marseille*, issued by Grimaud, regularized line, colour, and labelling and became the dominant twentieth-century French Marseille deck.

**Modern reinterpretation:** Marteau attached systematic colour symbolism and divinatory explanations to a pattern originally made for gaming.

### Camoin–Jodorowsky, 1997

Philippe Camoin and Alejandro Jodorowsky described their deck as a “restoration.”

**Documented fact:** it synthesizes details from several historical packs and introduces editorial reconstruction.

**Disputed claim:** it is not a facsimile of one lost, original, secretly encoded Marseille tarot. Its additional lines, eggs, droplets, colours, and restored details may support Jodorowsky’s interpretive system, but their status is modern reconstruction. The historical lineage of Noblet, Dodal, and Conver is surveyed by the French card-history site [Le Tarot](https://www.letarot.com/imagiers-du-tarot/index/).

## 14. Tarot de Besançon

Besançon-pattern decks replace the Papess and Pope with Juno and Jupiter, partly to avoid confessional objections. This regional renaming concerns the trumps, not the baton suit.

**Documented fact:** Bâtons remain Bâtons. The substitution of Juno and Jupiter is principally eighteenth-century and associated with eastern France, Switzerland, and German-speaking markets.

**Relevance:** it demonstrates that regional religious editing affected the trump sequence while leaving the ordinary suit system comparatively stable.

## 15. Etteilla’s Grand Etteilla, first issued 1788–1789

Jean-Baptiste Alliette, “Etteilla,” produced the first tarot designed expressly for occult divination.

**Names:** traditional Batons are transformed in many Etteilla-derived packs into more explicitly symbolic objects and reordered within an “Egyptian” system. Later editions vary, and twentieth-century Grimaud numbering should not automatically be projected onto the first printing.

**Iconography and meaning:**

- upright and reversed keywords are printed on cards;
- the suit participates in an astrological and elemental cosmology;
- ordinary ranks cease to be only game counters;
- courts become physiognomic and divinatory characters.

**Documented fact:** Etteilla claimed to restore a corrupted Egyptian book.

**Legend:** no Egyptian prototype, transmission record, or pre-eighteenth-century version of his meanings survives. His system is historically important precisely as an eighteenth-century invention.

## 16. Oswald Wirth, 1889

Wirth’s first deck, made under Stanislas de Guaita’s direction, consisted of twenty-two trumps.

**Finding:** there is no Wirth-designed fourteen-card Wands suit in the 1889 deck. Later “Wirth tarot” packs with minors are publisher or successor expansions.

Wirth matters to the suit through his occult writings: he accepted the suits as magical implements and social-symbolic categories, but one should not attribute modern illustrated Wands pips to his original deck.

## 17. Golden Dawn manuscripts, approximately 1888–1900

The Golden Dawn did not issue one canonical commercial pack. Members made or coloured personal cards from instructions.

**Documented/esoteric attribution:** *Book T* converts Batons into Wands and imposes a complete structure:

- Wands = Fire;
- Wands = Yod;
- Wands = Atziluth;
- Ace = Kether in Fire;
- 2–10 = Chokmah through Malkuth in Fire;
- those nine numbered cards receive the thirty-degree spans of Aries, Leo, and Sagittarius by decans;
- the four courts are Fire, Water, Air, and Earth within Fire.

The manuscript material became publicly accessible in Crowley’s 1912 *Equinox* presentation and later through Israel Regardie.

### Golden Dawn reconstruction decks

**Robert Wang, Golden Dawn Tarot, 1977:** a painted reconstruction based chiefly on Regardie’s published materials. The pips use ceremonial colour and Book T symbolism rather than Waite-Smith narratives.

**Chic and Sandra Tabatha Cicero, Golden Dawn Magical Tarot, 2000:** a later reconstruction with explicit ritual implements, colour scales, astrological signs, Hebrew lettering, and elemental emblems.

Neither is the surviving original “Golden Dawn deck”; both are twentieth-century interpretive realizations.

## 18. Waite–Smith, 1909; book 1910

Arthur Edward Waite devised the programme and Pamela Colman Smith drew it. Rider published the deck in late 1909; Waite’s *Pictorial Key* followed in 1910.

### The major visual change

Smith turned every numbered Wand card into a scene:

- **Ace:** a cloud-hand offers a stout living branch; detached leaves float around it.
- **Two:** a richly dressed figure holds a globe between one fixed and one held wand.
- **Three:** a figure watches ships from a height; three wands frame him.
- **Four:** a floral garland hangs between four upright poles; celebrants and a town appear behind.
- **Five:** five youths brandish or clash wands.
- **Six:** a mounted victor bears a laurelled wand amid followers.
- **Seven:** one figure on a height resists six lower wands.
- **Eight:** eight leafing wands fly through open air.
- **Nine:** a bandaged guard grips one wand before a palisade of eight.
- **Ten:** a stooping figure carries a bundle of ten toward a town.
- **Page:** a youth examines a sprouting wand in a desert.
- **Knight:** an armoured rider charges with a wand; salamander imagery marks Fire.
- **Queen:** a queen with sunflower and wand sits with lions and a black cat.
- **King:** a fiery king holds a flowering wand; lions and salamanders decorate his throne and robe.

Waite states of the Ace: “A hand issuing from a cloud grasps a stout wand or club.” [Waite, Ace of Wands](https://sacred-texts.com/tarot/pkt/pktwaac.htm)

**Interpretive effect:** the suit becomes visibly organic, energetic, mobile, competitive, and burdensome. The ever-leafing rods make the old baton into potential life. However, the assertion that every leaf has a fixed occult code is unsupported.

**Sources:** Book T supplied titles, decans, personalities, and many situations; Etteilla and Mathers contributed cartomantic vocabulary; Sola Busca supplied selected compositional precedents; theatrical illustration and Smith’s own imagination unified them.

## 19. Paul Foster Case and the B.O.T.A. Tarot

Paul Foster Case’s deck was drawn by Jessie Burns Parke and developed during the twentieth century.

**Iconography:** the trumps and courts resemble Waite-Smith line art, but numbered minors return to emblematic arrangements instead of narrative scenes. Students are traditionally asked to colour the cards.

**Meaning:** Case regarded Waite’s deck as containing omissions or “blinds.” His minors emphasize the combination of number, element, astrology, and Qabalah rather than Smith’s narrative.

**Documented limitation:** published B.O.T.A. materials and proprietary lessons are not all identical in date or availability; unattributed online “Case meanings” frequently mix his *Oracle of the Tarot* with Golden Dawn and modern keywords. [B.O.T.A. history](https://web.bota.org/bota/history), [Case, *Oracle of the Tarot* scan](https://benebellwen.com/wp-content/uploads/2013/02/paul-foster-case-oracle-of-the-tarot-1933-source-credit-tarotworks.pdf)

## 20. Crowley–Harris Thoth Tarot, painted 1938–1943; first full commercial publication 1969

Aleister Crowley designed the programme; Lady Frieda Harris painted the cards.

### Suit logic

Crowley retains Wands = Fire = Yod = Atziluth but makes the suit a sequence of changing ritual weapons and energetic geometries.

- **Ace, Root of the Powers of Fire:** a vast flaming club/torch; flames form Yods and a Tree-of-Life-like burst. It is primal energy before division.
- **Two, Dominion:** crossed Tibetan-style dorjes or fire-wands under Mars in Aries.
- **Three, Virtue:** three lotus-headed wands, Sun in Aries; solar establishment of the fiery current.
- **Four, Completion:** four ram-headed or solar/fire-associated wands form a stable enclosure, Venus in Aries.
- **Five, Strife:** a dominant wand of the Chief Adept confronts four Phoenix-headed wands; Saturn in Leo introduces restriction and collision.
- **Six, Victory:** balanced wands bearing solar and fiery insignia; Jupiter in Leo harmonizes force.
- **Seven, Valour:** one rough, oversized club opposes six refined weapons; Mars in Leo. Crowley calls it “a large crude uneven club, the first weapon to hand.”
- **Eight, Swiftness:** straight, arrow-like rays cross a prismatic field; Mercury in Sagittarius.
- **Nine, Strength:** lunar Sagittarius concentrates and repeatedly transmits force through balanced wands.
- **Ten, Oppression:** heavy crossed weapons and Saturnian geometry show Fire clogged by excessive structure.
- **Knight:** Fire of Fire, mounted amid flame, carrying a torch.
- **Queen:** Water of Fire, enthroned on flame, holding the Phoenix Wand and controlling a leopard.
- **Prince:** Air of Fire, in a flame-wheeled chariot drawn by a lion, bearing the Phoenix Wand.
- **Princess:** Earth of Fire, standing in flame with a solar wand; combustible seed embodied.

Crowley’s own explanations are available in [*The Book of Thoth*: small cards](https://hermetic.com/crowley/book-of-thoth/small-cards), [court cards](https://hermetic.com/crowley/book-of-thoth/court-cards), and [theory](https://hermetic.com/crowley/book-of-thoth/theory).

**Correction to a common summary:** not every Thoth card literally uses a Phoenix Wand or Lotus Wand. Harris varies the implements to express the card’s Sephirah, planetary ruler, sign, and qualitative title.

## 21. Notable modern redesigns

### Aquarian Tarot, David Palladini, 1970

Stylized Art Deco figures retain Waite-Smith’s narrative vocabulary but reduce scenery and foliage.

### Motherpeace, Vicki Noble and Karen Vogel, 1981

Round cards replace patriarchal court and heroic conventions with feminist, communal, and Goddess-centred scenes. Wands remain Fire but become embodied creative and political agency.

### Wild Unknown, Kim Krans, 2012

Animal and branch imagery removes human narrative. The suit’s earlier botanical suggestion becomes the entire visual language.

### Jodorowsky–Costa, *The Way of Tarot*, 2004

The suit is read through number, colour, growth, sexual polarity, and the geometry of Marseille pips. This is a productive modern hermeneutic, not proof of fifteenth-century intention.

# The game

## The suit before tarot

**Documented fact:** four-suited playing cards circulated in Europe before tarot. Tarot arose when a permanent trump sequence was added to an existing suit pack.

**Scholarly reconstruction:** the earliest European Latin suits adapted the Mamluk system. Tarot did not invent Batons.

## Rank

In the classical Italian and old French tarot family:

- Courts normally rank King, Queen, Knight, Page.
- Batons and Swords are usually the **long** or “masculine” suits: Ten high, descending to Ace low.
- Cups and Coins often reverse the numeral order: Ace high, descending to Ten low.

Thus the ordinary Baton order is usually:

**King > Queen > Knight > Page > 10 > 9 > 8 > 7 > 6 > 5 > 4 > 3 > 2 > Ace.**

The oldest surviving printed French rules, recorded by the abbé de Marolles in 1637, rank retained Batons King through Four in descending order; the lowest three had been stripped from the pack. [Marolles rules](https://www.pagat.com/tarot/marolles.html)

This ordering is a game convention. Occult writers later read “rising” numbers as a spiritual or numerological sequence, which is almost the reverse of the suit’s trick-taking strength.

## Card-point values

Exact scoring varies, but in many tarot games:

- King: 5 points;
- Queen: 4;
- Knight: 3;
- Page/Jack: 2;
- ordinary numeral card: 1, usually with card-pairing or trick-count adjustments.

The suit as such has no elemental advantage. A Wand does not beat a Cup because Fire dominates Water; one must follow suit, and trumps beat ordinary suits.

## Tarocchi, tarot, tarock

**Tarocchi:** Italian family name for the game and pack, attested in the early sixteenth century after the earlier name *trionfi*.

**Tarot:** French form.

**Tarock/Tarok:** German and central-European forms. Most later tarock packs replace Italian suit signs with French clubs, spades, hearts, and diamonds. Calling their clubs “Wands” is an occult translation, not normal gaming terminology.

## The Fool

The Fool is not a Wand and is usually unnumbered in early and Marseille packs.

In many historical tarot games it functions as the **Excuse**: it may be played instead of following suit, does not normally win the trick, and is recovered by its player subject to compensation. In other regional games its role differs; in some modern games it is the highest trump. “Zero” is an occult and modern printed convention, though it fits the Fool’s lack of place in the trump sequence.

## Trump-order disputes

Dummett grouped early Italian orders into three broad regional families:

- **A, Bologna/Ferrara:** characteristic eastern order;
- **B, Ferrara/Venice:** another eastern grouping in which virtues and papal/imperial figures occupy different relations;
- **C, Milan and western tradition:** ancestor of the Marseille sequence, with Justice VIII and Fortitude XI.

These disputes concern the trumps, not the internal rank of Batons. Waite’s exchange—Strength VIII and Justice XI—reflects the Golden Dawn’s astrological alphabet, not a discovery of an original game order.

## Minchiate and regional reductions

- **Minchiate:** Batons remain a long suit, Ten high and Ace low.
- **Bolognese tarocchino:** low pip cards are removed.
- **Sicilian tarot:** suit is shortened differently; local surviving rules rank the remaining numeral cards downward from Ten.
- **French Tarot today:** uses French suits and different-looking court and pip cards; occult Wands are not required for play.

| c. 1370s | European prohibitions and references to playing cards | **Documented fact:** suit cards existed before tarot; surviving notices seldom enumerate their emblems. |
| Before 1400–15th c. | Mamluk card tradition | **Documented objects / reconstructed dating:** polo-sticks provide the closest ancestor of Batons. |
| c. 1412–1425 | Marziano–Michelino pack | **Documented text:** superior cards added to bird suits; proto-tarot, but no Batons. |
| 1440 | Florentine record of *naibi a trionfi* discovered by Franco Pratesi | **Documented fact:** currently among the earliest clear tarot/triumph records. |
| 1442 | Ferrara Este ledger | **Documented fact:** payment for *carte da trionfi*. It does not describe Wands iconography. |
| 1440s | Cary-Yale and Brambilla groups | **Surviving objects:** early tarot Batons in Milan. |
| c. 1450–1460 | Visconti-Sforza | **Surviving objects:** decorated staves in a fourteen-card suit. |
| 1450s | Jacopo Antonio Marcello’s letter concerning Filippo Maria’s game | **Documented text:** preserves knowledge of the Marziano-Michelino commission. |
| c. 1470–1500 | Anonymous *Sermones de ludo cum aliis* | **Documented manuscript:** earliest complete named list of twenty-one trumps plus Fool; attacks games as morally dangerous. It confirms the trump series, not an esoteric suit doctrine. |

## Golden Dawn/Thoth court structure

| Court | Sub-element | Zodiacal governance in Book T/Thoth |
|---|---|---|
| Knight/King of Wands | Fire of Fire | 20° Scorpio–20° Sagittarius |
| Queen of Wands | Water of Fire | 20° Pisces–20° Aries |
| Prince/King in some older nomenclature | Air of Fire | 20° Cancer–20° Leo |
| Princess/Page | Earth of Fire | Not one thirty-degree zodiacal arc; assigned an elemental terrestrial quadrant and the region around the North Pole in Book T cosmology |

Court nomenclature is a genuine source of confusion. Golden Dawn manuscripts use King/Knight, Prince/King, and Knave/Princess inconsistently across summaries. Crowley’s Knight–Queen–Prince–Princess set is the safest terminology when describing Thoth.

## Seasons

- **Golden Dawn-derived tradition:** Wands/Fire = spring.
- **Modern variation:** some writers assign Fire to summer, reasoning from heat rather than the zodiacal year.
- **Finding:** no universal historical season exists. Aries begins the astrological fire sequence and northern-hemisphere spring, which explains the Golden Dawn-derived convention; it is not a medieval rule.

## The Emperor/Star and Tzaddi/Heh question

This controversy belongs to the trumps, but affects the larger system into which Wands were fitted.

The Golden Dawn gives Emperor = Heh/Aries and Star = Tzaddi/Aquarius. Crowley interpreted “Tzaddi is not the Star” in *The Book of the Law* as requiring an exchange: Emperor receives Tzaddi while the Star receives Heh, without simply exchanging the zodiacal signs in every table.

**Esoteric attribution/dispute:** this has no bearing on the historical origin of Bastoni. It is an internal Thelemic correction to a nineteenth-century occult schema.

# Divinatory meanings by author

Because this dossier treats the suit as a system, the table records each author’s governing vocabulary and the distinctive sequence through the fourteen cards. Individual-card dossiers should reproduce full entries and variant editions.

## Etteilla

**Documented fact:** Etteilla made upright and reversed orientations integral to printed tarot divination. His vocabulary includes domestic events, enterprise, money, delay, quarrel, news, social type, and moral character.

**Source problem:** there is no single stable English “Etteilla meanings” list. The first Grand Etteilla, later *Livre de Thot* manuals, Julia Orsini’s nineteenth-century handbook, and Grimaud cards diverge. Many internet lists falsely label Orsini’s 1850 text “Etteilla 1789.”

**Finding:** without specifying an edition and card-number mapping, a fourteen-entry quotation would manufacture false precision. What can safely be attributed is the method: printed paired meanings, one for each orientation, attached to a reordered Egyptian tarot.

## Mathers, 1888

Mathers’s *The Tarot: Its Occult Signification, Use in Fortune-Telling and Method of Play* gives concise paired meanings, drawing heavily on French cartomancy and occasionally marking “Etteilla’s” additions.

Across Batons/Sceptres his upright field includes:

- enterprise and beginnings;
- honour, gain, inheritance, and country life;
- journeys, messages, competition, victory, resistance, strength, burden;
- courts as dark or fiery persons, messengers, travellers, and people of command.

Reversals introduce:

- fall or failure;
- surprise and agitation;
- delay;
- quarrels and disputes;
- obstacles;
- treachery;
- rupture, severity, or unreliable news.

[Mathers, 1888 online text](https://hermetics.net/media-library/tarot/samuel-liddell-macgregor-mathers-the-tarot/)

## Crowley, 1944

Crowley advises reading the full dignity of the card rather than attaching a mechanical opposite to inversion. Thoth cards are not normally supplied with separate reversed meanings.

His progression is:

- **Ace:** the essence of Fire; natural force as yet unformed.
- **Two, Dominion:** Will in its most exalted form; harmonious rule.
- **Three, Virtue:** established primal energy.
- **Four, Completion:** manifestation and settlement, though the end of initial impetus.
- **Five, Strife:** restriction causing conflict.
- **Six, Victory:** balance and triumph.
- **Seven, Valour:** courage under bad odds; individual force against the multitude.
- **Eight, Swiftness:** rapid, direct energy and communication.
- **Nine, Strength:** great accumulated force, resilient and steady.
- **Ten, Oppression:** Fire crushed by materialized, Saturnian excess.
- **Courts:** four modes of fiery personality from explosive Knight to volatile embodied Princess.

**Reversed:** not given as a fixed parallel list. Ill-dignified cards express imbalance—violence, exhaustion, boastfulness, impulsiveness, oppression, or frustrated energy.

## Paul Foster Case

Case retains the Fire/Yod/Atziluth structure and reads Wands as spiritual, volitional, and vocational energy. His *Oracle of the Tarot* combines astrological decans with practical divination.

**Upright suit logic:** initiative, will, command, business, ambition, enterprise, and the progressive embodiment of spiritual force.

**Reversed/ill-dignified logic:** misdirected will, impatience, conflict, frustrated enterprise, domination, or exhaustion.

Case’s exact word lists should be taken from a specified edition; later B.O.T.A. teaching material expands them. [Case, *Oracle of the Tarot*, 1933 scan](https://benebellwen.com/wp-content/uploads/2013/02/paul-foster-case-oracle-of-the-tarot-1933-source-credit-tarotworks.pdf)

## Rachel Pollack

In *Seventy-Eight Degrees of Wisdom* (first volumes 1980 and 1983), Pollack treats Wands as action, movement, social identity, work, and the energy of Spirit entering life.

**Suit-wide reading:** Fire must act; its virtue is vitality and its danger is becoming trapped in contests, status, or relentless activity. The sequence moves from pure potential through planning, establishment, conflict, public success, defence, speed, endurance, and burden.

**Reversed:** blocked, weakened, distorted, private, or released energy depending on the card; Pollack does not reduce every reversal to the simple contrary.

## Mary K. Greer

Greer’s *Complete Book of Tarot Reversals* (2002) reframes reversal as a family of operations:

- blocked or resisted;
- delayed or unavailable;
- excessive or deficient;
- projected onto another;
- internal, unconscious, or private;
- misused or misdirected;
- overturning, refusing, or breaking through;
- opposite in the narrower traditional sense.

For Wands this means passion may become burnout, initiative may be blocked, conflict may become internal, victory may be hollow, or burden may finally be put down. Greer also associates Wands with Jungian Intuition while warning against rigidly gendered court assignments. [Mary K. Greer on court cards](https://marykgreer.com/2016/03/23/using-the-court-cards/)

# Esoteric and symbolic readings

## 1. Four estates

Court de Gébelin’s 1781 scheme is the first clearly traceable printed claim that the four tarot suits represent social estates:

- Sword: sovereign and military nobility;
- Cup: clergy or priesthood;
- Baton/Hercules’ club: agriculture;
- Coin: commerce.

He writes: “Le Bâton, ou Massue d’Hercule, l’Agriculture.” [Original French text](https://www.tarock.info/gebelin.htm)

**Esoteric attribution, not medieval fact:** Court says these are Egyptian estates. No Egyptian deck or medieval explanatory text confirms this.

**Later spread:** occult authors recast Baton as peasants, labourers, artisans, farmers, or productive workers. Those are related but not identical categories.

## 2. Four elements

**First securely influential formulation:** Lévi’s mid-nineteenth-century synthesis links the magician’s four implements with elemental and Qabalistic powers. Golden Dawn then standardizes:

- Wand—Fire;
- Cup—Water;
- Sword—Air;
- Pentacle—Earth.

**Conflict:** Papus and other French occultists sometimes interchange the Air/Earth placement of Sword and Pentacle. Modern alternative decks exchange Wands and Swords, assigning Wand to Air and Sword to Fire.

**Evidence:** symbolic analogy only. Wood burns, a wand transmits will, and a growing branch resembles flame; none proves historical intention.

## 3. Qabalah

Golden Dawn places:

- Wands in **Atziluth**;
- Yod as the seed-point of the divine name;
- Ace through Ten in the ten Sephiroth;
- courts as the four letters/elements within Fire.

Thus every card has the form:

**Sephirah or sub-element × Fire × astrological force × colour scale.**

This is a powerful closed system but a nineteenth-century construction.

## 4. Astrology

The numbered cards 2–10 divide the fire signs into their nine decans. The planetary rulers follow the traditional Chaldean sequence, not a free association based on card pictures.

The court family crosses sign boundaries:

- fiery father/Knight;
- watery mother/Queen;
- airy son/Prince;
- earthy daughter/Princess.

**Tradition:** later writers read this as an elemental family, developmental psychology, or alchemical genealogy.

## 5. Alchemy

The strongest alchemical readings develop after Lévi and the Golden Dawn:

- Ace: undifferentiated sulphurous Fire;
- 2–4: ignition, expansion, stabilization;
- 5–7: trial, calcination, struggle, purification;
- 8–9: transmission and concentration;
- 10: fixation so complete that living Fire becomes oppressive matter.

Crowley’s Phoenix wands explicitly suggest destruction, purgation, and resurrection from ash. [Crowley, small cards](https://hermetic.com/crowley/book-of-thoth/small-cards)

**Finding:** medieval Baton pips contain no securely identified alchemical sequence. Alchemical readings are later applications.

## 6. Hermetic ceremonial magic

The magical wand directs force. Its symbolism differs from the staff as support, the club as weapon, and the sceptre as sovereignty, but occult decks collapse all four into one polyvalent sign.

Lévi helped establish the wand as one of the operator’s four elemental instruments; Golden Dawn ritual developed highly specific implements, including:

- Lotus Wand;
- Phoenix Wand;
- Fire Wand;
- Chief Adept’s Wand;
- double-power forms;
- zodiacal and elemental colour bands.

Crowley and Harris imported these ritual objects into individual cards. Their presence is deliberate and documented, unlike supposed hidden ritual implements in fifteenth-century packs.

## 7. Christian readings

Possible Christian associations include:

- Aaron’s budding rod;
- Moses’ staff;
- the flowering rod of Joseph;
- episcopal crosier;
- royal sceptre;
- the rod “out of the stem of Jesse”;
- the spear/lance of the Passion.

**Tradition:** these analogies appear in Christian-Hermetic and devotional tarot interpretation.

**Finding:** the generic suit sign cannot be securely identified with any single biblical staff in the earliest decks.

## 8. Grail lance

Waite’s Grail scholarship encourages a striking reinterpretation. In *The Hidden Church of the Holy Graal*, he says the Wand’s proper symbolic alternative is “a spear or lance.” [Waite, *Hidden Church*, p. 603 in this scan](https://pubhtml5.com/ejtz/cpkk/basic/601-650)

This yields a four-Hallows scheme:

- Cup = Grail;
- Wand = Lance;
- Sword = Sword;
- Pentacle = Dish or stone-like talisman.

**Esoteric attribution:** early twentieth-century Grail occultism.

**Not historical origin:** no fifteenth-century tarot source calls Bastoni the Lance of Longinus, the bleeding spear, or the spear of Celtic romance. The suit’s Mamluk polo-stick ancestry makes a literal Grail origin very unlikely.

## 9. Sexual symbolism

The upright wand can be read as phallic; its sprouting branch can signify fertility, potency, birth, or libido. Waite himself includes “virility” under the Ace.

**Esoteric/psychological attribution:** strong in twentieth-century psychoanalytic and Jodorowskian interpretation.

**Caution:** an elongated object may support a sexual reading without proving that every historical card-maker intended one.

## 10. Jungian readings

The standard modern allocation is:

- Wands = Intuition;
- Cups = Feeling;
- Swords = Thinking;
- Pentacles = Sensation.

Greer notes that this has become common while remaining imperfect. Jung formulated four functions of consciousness, but he did not leave an authoritative tarot-suit table.

**Modern invention/application:** tarot authors mapped Jung’s functions onto an existing occult four-element system. Courts then become personas or sixteen psychological types. [Greer](https://marykgreer.com/2016/03/23/using-the-court-cards/)

# The suit as a fourteen-card system

## Number sequence

The Golden Dawn-derived logic is:

1. **Ace/Kether:** undivided fiery potential.
2. **Chokmah:** force asserts dominion.
3. **Binah:** force takes intelligible form.
4. **Chesed:** ordered, stable manifestation.
5. **Geburah:** conflict and severity.
6. **Tiphareth:** balanced victory.
7. **Netzach:** emotional and instinctive courage under pressure.
8. **Hod:** rapid, articulated transmission.
9. **Yesod:** concentrated reservoir of strength.
10. **Malkuth:** maximum embodiment; energy becomes weight and oppression.

This progression is internally elegant but reverses the literal gaming hierarchy, where the Ten of Batons is stronger than the Ace.

## Court as family

Golden Dawn and Thoth treat the court as a generative family:

- Knight/King: father, Fire;
- Queen: mother, Water;
- Prince: son, Air;
- Princess: daughter, Earth.

Within Wands, this produces Fire of Fire, Water of Fire, Air of Fire, and Earth of Fire.

**Esoteric attribution:** based on Tetragrammaton and elemental generation.

**Modern psychological use:** four ways fiery energy appears—impulse, sustaining charisma, intellect/strategy, and embodied potential.

**Historical caution:** medieval courts primarily express social ranks. The “elemental family” is not documented in the Visconti or Marseille periods.

# People and works

## Artists, printers, and occultists directly attached

- **Michelino da Besozzo:** painted Marziano’s lost proto-tarot.
- **Bonifacio Bembo and workshop; Francesco Zavattari and other proposed artists:** competing attributions for Milanese luxury cards.
- **Jean Noblet, Jean Dodal, Pierre Madenié, Nicolas Conver:** major named printers/card-makers in the Marseille lineage.
- **Jean-Baptiste Alliette/Etteilla:** first professional occult-tarot system builder.
- **Antoine Court de Gébelin:** introduced the Egyptian-book interpretation and four-estates theory in 1781.
- **Louis-Raphaël-Lucrèce de Fayolle, comte de Mellet:** probable author of the companion essay connecting the trumps with Hebrew letters.
- **Éliphas Lévi:** integrated tarot into ceremonial magic and Qabalah.
- **Paul Christian:** created influential Egyptian initiation narratives.
- **Papus/Gérard Encausse:** popularized Tetragrammaton structures and the “Tarot of the Bohemians.”
- **Oswald Wirth:** redesigned the trumps and spread symbolist tarot.
- **S. L. MacGregor Mathers:** published an English divinatory handbook and helped construct Golden Dawn tarot.
- **Arthur Edward Waite:** designed the Waite-Smith programme and linked the suit with Grail symbolism.
- **Pamela Colman Smith:** created the definitive living-branch and scenic Wands.
- **Paul Foster Case and Jessie Burns Parke:** produced the B.O.T.A. revision.
- **Aleister Crowley and Frieda Harris:** transformed the suit into Thelemic elemental abstraction.
- **Paul Marteau:** standardized a major twentieth-century Marseille edition.
- **Alejandro Jodorowsky and Philippe Camoin:** produced a modern synthetic Marseille reconstruction.
- **Rachel Pollack and Mary K. Greer:** major psychological and practice-oriented interpreters.

## Literature and film

### Italo Calvino, *Il castello dei destini incrociati*, 1969/1973

**Documented fact:** Calvino constructs interlocking narratives from tarot layouts, using both the Visconti-Sforza and Marseille visual vocabularies in different parts of the work. Baton cards operate as visual words—woods, combat, journeys, power, obstacles—depending on their neighbours.

**Literary invention:** Calvino does not claim to recover a fixed medieval meaning. His achievement demonstrates tarot’s combinatorial narrative capacity.

### T. S. Eliot, *The Waste Land*, 1922

Eliot names a “wicked pack of cards,” Madame Sosostris, the drowned Phoenician sailor, Belladonna, the man with three staves, the Wheel, and the one-eyed merchant.

**Disputed identification:** “the man with three staves” is often associated with the Three of Wands, especially Waite-Smith, but Eliot’s invented reading does not reproduce Waite’s published meaning. It is a literary tarot assemblage, not evidence about the historical suit.

### W. B. Yeats

Yeats belonged to the Golden Dawn and participated in its broader symbolic culture.

**Documented fact:** he knew the Order’s elemental and Qabalistic framework.

**Caution:** generalized claims that a particular poem “is the Suit of Wands” require textual demonstration. His importance is institutional and symbolic rather than the authorship of a distinct Wands doctrine.

### Alejandro Jodorowsky

Tarot imagery and initiation structures permeate Jodorowsky’s films and writings, particularly *The Holy Mountain*. He later made Marseille tarot central to psychomagic and collaborative interpretation.

**Disputed/modern claim:** his assertion that restored card details recover one authentic total language is not accepted as documentary card history.

### Ingmar Bergman

*The Seventh Seal* uses medieval allegory, chess, Death, procession, and apocalyptic imagery.

**Finding:** no firm evidence establishes a specific quotation of the Suit of Wands. Visual resemblance between staffs in medieval films and tarot Batons is insufficient. Bergman belongs to the broader afterlife of medieval allegory, not securely to this suit.

## Painting and architecture

- Renaissance sceptres, clubs of Hercules, rods of office, and processional staffs provide a broad European visual vocabulary.
- The four ceremonial implements appear repeatedly in nineteenth- and twentieth-century occult temple design.
- Jodorowsky’s and Harris’s imagery has been quoted in murals, album art, fashion, and installation work.

**Finding:** no building has been securely established as the source of the Marseille crossed-baton pattern. Claims about cathedral plans, Templar churches, or alchemical façades encoding the suit generally proceed by visual resemblance without workshop documentation.

# Controversies and disputes

## 1. Egyptian origin

Court de Gébelin declared tarot an Egyptian book in 1781. He proposed the etymology *Tar* = road and *Ro/Rog* = royal, “the royal road.”

**Legend:** Egyptian was not deciphered when he wrote; his etymology is linguistically untenable. No ancient Egyptian tarot deck, rule, image series, or transmission text survives.

**Historical importance:** false as origin history, foundational as occult reception. The full claim should not be discarded merely because it is false: it generated Etteilla’s deck, nineteenth-century Egyptian tarots, the name “Book of Thoth,” and much of modern tarot’s prestige.

## 2. Gypsy/Romani transmission

Papus’s title *Le Tarot des Bohémiens* and writers such as Jean-Alexandre Vaillant helped popularize the claim that Romani people carried tarot from Egypt or India.

**Legend:** European tarot records precede evidence connecting Romani communities with tarot divination. Linguistic and historical research places Romani origins in India, not Egypt.

**Documented social history:** Romani fortune-tellers did later use cards, but that does not make them the inventors or medieval transmitters of tarot.

## 3. Cathar origin

Claims connect the four suits or trumps with Cathar dualism, secret survival after the Albigensian Crusade, or hidden heretical doctrine.

**Disputed claim tending to legend:** no Cathar text, inquisitorial record, deck, or card-maker’s document identifies Batons as a Cathar emblem. Tarot appears in fifteenth-century northern Italy, long after the destruction of institutional Catharism in southern France.

## 4. Templar origin

The Wand is variously called a Templar lance, pilgrim’s staff, or surviving emblem of Temple mysteries.

**Legend:** there is no documented chain from the suppressed Templars to fifteenth-century card production. Similarities among rods, lances, crosses, and staffs are not provenance evidence.

## 5. Grail origin

The suit’s identification with the Grail lance is traceable to modern occult and literary synthesis, particularly Waite’s Grail studies.

**Esoteric attribution:** symbolically coherent.

**Historical finding:** no evidence that the Mamluk polo-stick sign or Italian *bastoni* was created to represent a Grail relic.

## 6. Four estates: peasants or merchants?

Court de Gébelin explicitly assigns Baton to agriculture and Coin to commerce. Later tables vary:

- Wands = peasants;
- Wands = artisans;
- Wands = merchants;
- Pentacles = merchants;
- Pentacles = peasants or landowners.

**Cause of conflict:** writers conflate occupational estates, elemental tools, French playing-card suits, and modern psychological categories. Court’s own 1781 allocation is unambiguous, but historically unsupported.

## 7. Fire or Air?

**Golden Dawn tradition:** Wands = Fire, Swords = Air.

**Alternative modern tradition:** Wands = Air because rods point, move through air, resemble breath or thought; Swords = Fire because they cut, spark, and embody conflict.

Neither arrangement is medieval. Golden Dawn’s became dominant because Waite-Smith and Thoth became dominant.

## 8. Smith and the Sola Busca

**Strong evidence:** Smith had access to photographs of the Sola Busca; several designs show meaningful compositional similarity.

**Dispute:** the extent of borrowing. The Ten of Wands is persuasive; claiming that every Waite-Smith minor has a Sola Busca prototype overstates the evidence.

## 9. Marseille “secret code”

Jodorowsky, Camoin, Marteau, and numerous modern readers find systematic colour, sexual, numerological, and initiatory codes in Marseille cards.

**Documented fact:** repeated pattern conventions and intentional decorative choices exist.

**Open/disputed:** whether particular tiny forms were deliberate esoteric signs, printer’s ornaments, copying errors, stencil conveniences, or accidental line residues. No surviving Conver manual supplies the claimed total code.

## 10. Misprints and copying errors

Re-cut woodblocks, worn matrices, stencil slippage, inverted cards, corrupted captions, and editorial redrawing repeatedly changed tarot images. Some alterations later acquired meanings.

For Wands:

- a crossing may become a knot;
- a cut branch may acquire or lose leaves;
- decorative foliage may be interpreted as male or female anatomy;
- an indistinct baton head may become a lotus, spear, or flame;
- court rank titles may shift when publishers rename King/Knight/Prince.

**Documented process:** card patterns were copied repeatedly.

**Disputed interpretation:** identifying any one irregularity as a deliberate initiate’s cipher.

## 11. Thoth publication date

The paintings were completed in the early 1940s and some cards appeared in *The Book of Thoth* in 1944. Small or incomplete printings preceded the familiar full commercial edition.

**Correct formulation:** designed and painted 1938–1943; book published 1944; full commercial deck commonly dated 1969. Saying simply “the Thoth deck was created in 1969” is misleading.

## 12. “Minor Arcana”

**Modern occult terminology:** *arcana* derives from nineteenth-century occult discourse and was amplified by Paul Christian and successors.

**Historical terminology:** fifteenth-century players had suit cards and triumphs, not “Major and Minor Arcana” in the modern initiatory sense.

## 13. Modern numerology mistaken for game history

The occult sequence Ace-to-Ten suggests an ascent from source to completion. In historical trick-taking, Batons usually rank Ten-to-Ace.

**Finding:** the esoteric numerical drama is not recoverable from game rank alone.

# Historiographical corrections

## Michael Dummett

Dummett’s central correction is that tarot was invented as a game in northern Italy and acquired occult explanations only in eighteenth-century France. His argument depends on rules, archival records, deck geography, and chronological priority—not on dismissing symbolism as psychologically useless.

## Ronald Decker and Thierry Depaulis

With Dummett, they traced the occult tradition author by author in *A Wicked Pack of Cards*, showing how Gébelin, de Mellet, Etteilla, Lévi, Christian, Papus, and others successively constructed what later appeared to be an ancient consensus. [Google Books record](https://books.google.com/books?id=nVyDAAAAMAAJ)

## Stuart R. Kaplan

Kaplan’s encyclopaedias made large numbers of historical decks accessible and documented modern production. Some early editions repeated origin theories more cautiously treated by later scholarship. His value as collector and bibliographer should be distinguished from the evidentiary strength of every historical inference.

## Robert M. Place

Place accepts the Renaissance origin while emphasizing contemporary emblematic, Neoplatonic, Hermetic, and alchemical contexts. His interpretations are better described as historical-symbolic reconstructions than proof of a single encoded doctrine.

## Helen Farley

Farley’s *A Cultural History of Tarot* traces how esoteric and popular meanings were layered onto the gaming pack. She notes Waite’s Grail-Hallows correspondences and the nineteenth-century dependence on Egyptian legend. [Farley, open scan](https://e-isomero.com/pdf/book/1611846398.pdf)

## Andrea Vitali and Le Tarot association

Vitali’s work is valuable for Renaissance visual parallels, sermons, moral imagery, and Italian literary sources. Iconographic analogy can establish cultural availability but not necessarily direct authorial intention.

## Thierry Depaulis and current documentary scholarship

More recent archival work has moved the earliest firm evidence earlier than the once-standard 1442 Ferrara date, notably to Florence in 1440. This illustrates why “first tarot” claims must distinguish:

- earliest written reference;
- earliest surviving cards;
- earliest nearly complete pack;
- earliest named trump list;
- earliest rules;
- earliest divination;
- earliest occult correspondence.

They are different events.

# Open questions

1. **Exact route of transmission:** Mamluk-to-European derivation is strong, but the precise ports, intermediaries, dates, and workshops that transformed polo sticks into Batons remain unknown.

2. **Earliest surviving European Baton card:** dating and grouping of isolated fourteenth- and fifteenth-century fragments remain contested. “Earliest tarot Baton” and “earliest ordinary playing-card Baton” are not the same question.

3. **Milan or Ferrara—or Florence?** Documentary discoveries complicate older single-city invention narratives. Tarot may have developed through courtly exchange among several northern Italian centres.

4. **Exact authorship of the Visconti cards:** Bembo, Zavattari, and related workshop attributions remain under revision.

5. **Sola Busca’s programme:** its imagery is intentional and learned, but no universally accepted decoding exists.

6. **Pamela Colman Smith’s working process:** access to Sola Busca photographs is persuasive, yet no complete design diary survives specifying each source.

7. **Earliest suit-element table:** Lévi made the four magical implements central, but the exact point at which the now-standard Wand/Fire, Cup/Water, Sword/Air, Pentacle/Earth table was first printed in its complete modern form deserves edition-by-edition study. Golden Dawn unquestionably standardized it.

8. **Etteilla textual variants:** a critical concordance of the first Grand Etteilla cards, early manuals, later “Book of Thoth” editions, and Grimaud printing would prevent recurrent false attributions.

9. **Golden Dawn manuscript variants:** court titles, colour instructions, and portions of Book T differ among manuscript and published witnesses.

10. **Marseille micro-iconography:** distinguishing meaningful workshop conventions from wear, stencil error, re-cutting, and modern restoration remains difficult.

11. **Seasonal attribution:** spring is conventional in Golden Dawn-derived systems; summer is widespread elsewhere. Neither can claim medieval authority.

12. **Jungian lineage:** Jung’s four functions are documented; the exact first author to print the now-common Wands–Intuition table remains less securely established than modern summaries imply.
