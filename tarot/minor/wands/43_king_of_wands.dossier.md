# King of Wands: Research Dossier

## Method and evidentiary labels

This dossier distinguishes:

- **[Documented fact]** Supported by a surviving card, archival record, rulebook, or text.
- **[Scholarly reconstruction]** A conclusion advanced by historians from physical or documentary evidence.
- **[Tradition]** A historically transmitted practice or interpretation whose original rationale is uncertain.
- **[Esoteric attribution]** A correspondence assigned within an occult system, not a claim about the card’s fifteenth-century meaning.
- **[Disputed]** A claim for which scholars or sources materially disagree.
- **[Legend]** A historical-origin story unsupported by contemporary evidence.
- **[Modern invention]** A recent interpretation or design presented as a creative development rather than an old tradition.
- **[Not traced]** A proposed connection for which no card-specific primary evidence was located.

A central caveat: many works requested in the general template concern the twenty-two trumps, not the King of Wands. Trump order, Hebrew-letter sequences, the Papess/Juno substitutions, and Crowley’s Tzaddi/Heh controversy do not directly alter this court card. They are covered only where they clarify the history of the pack or the system into which the King was placed.

### The King/Prince nomenclature problem

**[Documented fact]** Traditional Italian and Marseille packs have a seated **King** above Queen, Knight, and Page. Golden Dawn manuscripts retained conventional headings in some lists—Knight, Queen, King, Knave—but reimagined the third figure as the **Prince**, the Vav or Son, riding a chariot. Hence *Book T* can call the card “King of Wands” while titling and describing it as “Prince of the Chariot of Fire.”

**[Documented fact]** Crowley removed the ambiguity in the Thoth deck by printing **Prince of Wands**. His Knight, not his Prince, occupies the fiery/Yod/Chokmah function normally called “King” in later elemental court-card systems.

Consequently:

- Traditional King of Wands ≠ automatically Yod/Fire of Fire.
- Golden Dawn *Book T* “King” slot = Prince/Vav/Air of Fire.
- Thoth Prince of Wands = that Golden Dawn chariot figure.
- Waite retained the ordinary title **King**, but did not print the Golden Dawn astrological span or sub-element on the card.

## The suit before tarot: polo sticks, batons, clubs, and *naʾib*

### Mamluk evidence

**[Documented fact]** The surviving Mamluk pack conventionally associated with Topkapı Palace has four suits: cups, coins, swords, and polo sticks. Its courts are named by inscriptions rather than pictured as human figures. The hierarchy includes *malik* (“king”), *nāʾib malik* (“deputy of the king”), and *nāʾib thānī* (“second deputy”). One surviving inscription has been read as identifying the “king of polo-sticks.” A transcription resource gives *malik al-jawkān*, literally “king of polo-sticks.” [Mamluk-card inscriptions](https://mamluk.spiorad.net/inscriptions.htm)

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** The congruence between the four Mamluk suits and the earliest Latin suits makes these cards or a related Islamic family the best-supported structural ancestor of Italian playing cards. The Italian and Spanish words *naibi/naipes* are generally connected with Arabic *nāʾib*. Human European kings, knights, and knaves replaced the inscription-only Mamluk courts.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** Because polo was unfamiliar in much of Europe, the paired polo sticks were naturalized as ceremonial staffs, rods, or cudgels—Italian *bastoni* and Spanish *bastos*. This was not a single documented act of renaming but a visual and lexical adaptation inferred from the surviving packs.

**[Correction]** “Wands” is predominantly an English occult-deck term. It should not be projected uncritically onto the fifteenth-century Italian card, whose implement is a baton or staff.

## Iconography, deck by deck

## Lost and pre-tarot antecedents

### Marziano da Tortona and Michelino da Besozzo, Milan, approximately 1412–1425

**[Documented fact]** Marziano’s Latin description survives; the commissioned cards do not. His pack for Filippo Maria Visconti contained sixteen classical gods and four bird suits. It was called a “game of the gods” and later, in Jacopo Antonio Marcello’s 1449 account, *ludus triumphorum*.

**[Finding]** There was no King of Wands: Marziano’s pack is relevant to the invention of an added trump suit at the Visconti court, not to this card’s particular iconography.

### Mantegna “Tarocchi,” Ferrara or northeastern Italy, approximately 1465–1475

**[Documented fact]** These are fifty educational engravings arranged in five decades—social estates, Apollo and the Muses, liberal arts, cosmic principles, and celestial spheres.

**[Finding]** They are not playing cards and contain no four-suit King of Batons. Their traditional name “tarocchi” is a later misnomer. They may illuminate Renaissance personifications used in trump imagery, but not the King of Wands.

## Fifteenth-century Italian tarot

### Cary-Yale / Visconti di Modrone, Milan, approximately 1440s

**[Documented fact]** This luxury Visconti pack uses an expanded court structure including male and female attendants and mounted figures.

**[Finding]** Among its surviving Batons courts are Page, Knight, and Queen; no King of Batons survives. Therefore modern Cary-Yale “King of Batons” cards are reconstructions, not facsimiles of an extant original.

**[Disputed nomenclature]** “Cary-Yale” and “Visconti di Modrone” normally denote the same surviving group, not two separate decks. Dates ranging from the 1440s to later in the century appear in older literature.

### Brambilla-Brera deck, Milan, approximately 1440s

**[Documented fact]** The fragmentary pack, now associated with the Pinacoteca di Brera, is attributed to the Bembo workshop and connected with Filippo Maria Visconti.

**[Finding]** Its survival is too incomplete to supply a securely preserved King of Batons. Claims describing a Brambilla King of Wands generally rely on reconstructed commercial editions or confusion with another Visconti group.

### Visconti-Sforza / Pierpont Morgan–Bergamo / Colleoni-Baglioni, Milan, approximately 1450–1460

**[Documented fact]** This is the earliest surviving tarot King of Staves located in a substantially preserved deck. The Morgan describes the whole group as seventy-eight original cards, seventy-four surviving, divided among the Morgan Library, Accademia Carrara, and the Colleoni family. It attributes the work to the workshop of Bonifacio Bembo and dates it after Francesco Sforza’s 1450 entry into Milan. [Morgan catalogue](https://www.themorgan.org/manuscript/158525); [Morgan card collection](https://www.themorgan.org/collection/tarot-cards)

**Actual image.** A crowned and bearded male ruler sits frontally or slightly turned against a tooled gold ground. He wears an ornate silver-toned garment and holds a single elongated staff vertically. Unlike the double-ended vessels on many numbered Batons, the King’s staff terminates chiefly in an upper finial. Heraldic and courtly luxury, not elemental fire, dominates the image.

**What it meant historically.** **[Scholarly reconstruction]** It signified the highest rank of the baton suit and reflected contemporary princely costume. There is no fifteenth-century text attaching Fire, Leo, willpower, salamanders, Vav, or divination to it.

#### Is the sitter a specific Sforza?

**[Disputed]** Gertrude Moakley and Stuart Kaplan encouraged biographical readings of Visconti-Sforza figures, associating the cards with members and festivities of the ruling families. The King of Batons has consequently been identified in popular accounts as Francesco Sforza or another family male.

**Evidence against certainty.** The card bears no portrait inscription; its facial features are conventionalized; the surviving deck mixes heraldic devices and ideal court types. The Morgan catalogues the pack as made “by or for” a Visconti-Sforza member and probably for Francesco, but it does not authenticate every court card as a portrait. The safest conclusion is: **possibly courtly or dynastic allusion, not a securely identified portrait**.

### Ferrarese and Este fragments, fifteenth century

**[Documented fact]** Ferrarese workshops produced luxury and later woodcut tarot. Surviving Este-related fragments show a somewhat harder, more linear court style than the Bembo cards.

**[Finding]** No securely proven, continuously documented King of Batons from the principal Este fragments supplies a new canonical design. Commercial “Este tarot” completions frequently add reconstructed courts.

### Sola Busca, probably Ferrara or Venice, 1491

**[Documented fact]** The Sola Busca is the earliest substantially complete engraved tarot and the earliest surviving pack whose numbered suit cards consistently contain narrative human scenes. Its trumps are named pseudo-historical figures and its court cards are individualized warriors or rulers.

**Actual significance for this card.** The Batons court belongs to an antique-military world rather than the placid Marseille court. The ruler bears an elaborate costume and staff-like suit emblem; inscriptions or names in the Sola Busca system make its courts appear like specific ancient worthies.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** Pamela Colman Smith almost certainly had access to photographs of the Sola Busca cards in the British Museum in 1909. Direct compositional borrowings are clearest in Waite-Smith’s Three of Swords and Seven and Ten of Wands, not in the King of Wands. A specific one-to-one derivation of Waite’s King from the Sola Busca King remains **unproven**.

### Rosenwald sheets, Italy, late fifteenth or early sixteenth century

**[Documented fact]** The Rosenwald group survives as uncut or fragmentary printed sheets containing popular, inexpensive tarot imagery rather than gilded court commissions.

**Actual relevance.** The courts are schematic woodcut rulers; the King of Batons is identifiable by rank and emblem rather than individualized portraiture. These sheets help bridge luxury manuscript cards and later popular patterns.

### Budapest sheets, northern Italy, late fifteenth or early sixteenth century

**[Documented fact]** These fragmentary woodcut sheets preserve tarot courts and suit designs.

**Finding.** As with Rosenwald, they document mass-reproduced court conventions but do not provide evidence for occult symbolism. Attribution, exact sequence, and place remain debated in specialist literature.

## Regional game patterns

### Minchiate, Florence, sixteenth–nineteenth centuries

**[Documented fact]** Minchiate expands the pack to ninety-seven cards while retaining the four Italian suits and four courts. Its King of Batons remains the highest Batons card.

**Actual image.** Historical Florentine Minchiate kings are standing or enthroned rulers bearing suit emblems; pattern details vary by maker. The Batons frequently resemble straight or knobbly staffs rather than leafy magical wands.

**Meaning of the change.** The great innovation is not in this King but in the forty trumps: zodiacal signs, virtues, elements, and additional cosmological figures. No evidence makes the King of Batons one of those cosmological trumps.

**Game value.** Kings are worth five points each. Other suit courts and pips are normally zero individually in the rules summarized by Pagat. [Minchiate rules](https://www.pagat.com/tarot/minchiate.html)

### Bolognese Tarocchino

**[Documented fact]** The Bolognese pack was shortened by removing low pip cards while retaining four courts per suit. The King of Batons remained the highest suit card and a counting card.

**Actual image.** A compact, often standing or half-seated crowned ruler holds a baton. The compressed double-headed layout of later Bolognese packs differs sharply from the full-length Renaissance miniatures.

**Finding.** The card’s divinatory characterization as a rustic or “country gentleman” belongs to the Etteilla/continental cartomantic stream, not to the documented medieval rules of Bologna.

### Sicilian Tarocchi

**[Documented fact]** The Sicilian pattern developed a distinctive shortened pack and special trump order. The King remains the top court and a counting card.

**Actual image.** Later Sicilian cards adopt regional dress and strongly stylized, often double-ended figures. The baton resembles the club of a Latin-suited pack more than Waite’s sprouting wand.

### Spanish packs

**[Documented fact]** *Rey de Bastos* is a king in the Spanish-suited forty- or forty-eight-card pack, not necessarily a tarot card. He is normally a crowned standing ruler with an upright club or cudgel.

**Historical relationship.** The Spanish King of Clubs and Italian tarot King of Batons are collateral descendants of the Latin-suit tradition. It is misleading to treat every *Rey de Bastos* as an occult King of Wands.

## Tarot de Marseille lineage

### Jean Noblet, Paris, second half of the seventeenth century, conventionally “c. 1650”

**[Documented fact]** The BnF holds seventy-three of seventy-eight woodcut and stencil-coloured cards. It describes the deck as the oldest known surviving pack conforming to what is conventionally called the Tarot de Marseille, noting that Noblet is documented between 1659 and 1681. [BnF record](https://essentiels.bnf.fr/fr/image/4799f2d6-6f88-4f6f-a15c-40a1a943c95e-tarot-jean-noblet)

**Actual image.** The *ROY/ROI DE BASTON* is a full-length crowned king, turned to the side and seated or poised upon a low throne. He holds a thick baton diagonally or nearly vertically. His legs and robe form an angular base; the figure fills the field. There are no salamanders, desert, flowering leaves, or explicit fire symbols.

**Meaning.** **[Documented fact]** Rank and suit are visually encoded. **[Not evidenced]** Hebrew letters or deliberately hidden Kabbalah. Claims that Noblet was commissioned to encode Jewish teaching are modern speculative tradition, not established archival fact.

### Jean Dodal, Lyon, approximately 1701–1715

**[Documented fact]** Dodal preserves the Type-I Marseille lineage in a larger, vigorous woodcut style.

**Actual change.** The King remains a side-facing, seated ruler with baton. Contours, hand positions, costume panels, spelling, and palette differ, but the basic schema is stable.

### Nicolas Conver, Marseille, 1760

**[Documented fact]** Conver’s deck became a major model for nineteenth- and twentieth-century reproductions.

**Actual change.** The King is more regularized and monumental: crowned, seated in profile or three-quarter view, holding a long baton; strong red, blue, yellow, black, and flesh stencil areas. The Type-II drawing differs in details from Noblet/Dodal but does not introduce an occult scene.

### B. P. Grimaud / Paul Marteau, Paris, 1930s

**[Documented fact]** Grimaud’s *Ancien Tarot de Marseille*, associated with Paul Marteau, standardized a cleaned, industrially printed Conver-derived pattern.

**Interpretive addition.** Marteau read minute features—hat, posture, colours, and baton—as coded psychic principles. These are **twentieth-century esoteric interpretations of an older design**, not demonstrated intentions of Conver’s workshop.

### Camoin–Jodorowsky, Marseille/Paris, 1997

**[Documented fact]** Philippe Camoin and Alejandro Jodorowsky produced a redesigned “restoration” by comparing several Marseille packs and introducing reconstructed details and an expanded palette.

**Actual change.** Their King preserves the seated Marseille ruler and baton but sharpens linework, adds colour articulations, and places details in a unified symbolic system.

**[Disputed]** Marketing and interpretive claims sometimes describe the result as the recovered original Tarot de Marseille. Critics point out that it combines features from historically separate decks and includes conjectural restoration. It is better classified as a historically informed modern synthesis.

**Jodorowsky/Costa reading.** The King embodies mature command of creative, sexual, or spiritual energy: the baton becomes active generative force. This is **[modern esoteric attribution]**, not a documented seventeenth-century meaning.

## Besançon pattern

**[Documented fact]** The Besançon/Swiss-derived pattern famously replaces the Papess and Pope with Juno and Jupiter, chiefly to make the pack acceptable in Protestant or confessionally mixed markets. Those substitutions concern trumps II and V.

**Finding for this card.** The *Roi de Bâton* remains a court king and is not renamed Juno, Jupiter, or an ancient deity. No King-of-Wands-specific doctrinal alteration was found.

## Etteilla

### Grand Etteilla, Paris, first published in the late 1780s

**[Documented fact]** Jean-Baptiste Alliette, “Etteilla,” reordered and redesigned the pack expressly for occultism and divination. His cards commonly print card numbers plus upright and reversed keywords.

**Name and number.** The card is usually **No. 22, Roi de Bâton**, though variant Grand Etteilla families and later reprints can alter sequence or reverse printed orientations.

**Actual image.** In surviving and derivative Grand Etteilla designs the card is a crowned or authoritative male with a baton, surrounded by printed cartomantic labels. The decisive innovation is not a radically new pose but the card’s conversion into a labelled divinatory instrument.

**Keywords.**

- Upright: *Homme de campagne*.
- Reversed: *Homme bon et sévère*.

A translation gives “country-dweller…farmer…an honest and straightforward man” and, reversed, “good and severe man,” combining severity with indulgence or tolerance. [Etteilla Batons](https://stolen-thyme.com/etteillas-suit-of-batons/)

**Astrology.** **[Finding]** No securely verified primary Etteilla passage was located assigning this court the later Golden Dawn span 20° Cancer–20° Leo. It is anachronistic to attribute that decan scheme to Etteilla.

## Oswald Wirth, 1889

**[Documented fact]** Wirth’s 1889 tarot designed with Stanislas de Guaita consisted of the twenty-two trumps. It did not contain a King of Wands.

**Finding.** Any “Wirth King of Wands” belongs to a later completion or Wirth-inspired deck. Wirth influenced the interpretation of the majors and occult tarot generally but supplied no original 1889 court-card iconography for this card.

## Golden Dawn manuscripts and reconstructions

### *Book T*, late nineteenth century

**[Documented fact]** The Golden Dawn transformed the courts into a fourfold elemental and genealogical system:

| Printed or traditional slot | Esoteric role | Elemental rank | Tetragrammaton |
|---|---|---|---|
| Knight | King/Father | Fire | Yod |
| Queen | Mother | Water | Heh |
| King, described as Prince | Son | Air | Vav |
| Knave/Princess | Daughter | Earth | Final Heh |

Thus the historical King of Wands slot becomes **Prince of the Chariot of Fire**, Air of Fire, Vav of Yod.

**Traditional description.** A kingly or princely armored figure wears a winged crown and rides a chariot drawn by a lion. He holds a fiery or phoenix wand. Flames surround the vehicle.

**Zodiacal domain.** 20° Cancer–20° Leo:

- 20°–30° Cancer: Four of Cups, Moon in Cancer, “Luxury.”
- 0°–10° Leo: Five of Wands, Saturn in Leo, “Strife.”
- 10°–20° Leo: Six of Wands, Jupiter in Leo, “Victory.”

**Meaning of the change.** The sedentary sovereign becomes mobile intellect: Air expands, directs, and volatilizes Fire. The chariot is the active Son emerging from the Knight/Father and Queen/Mother.

### Robert Wang, *The Golden Dawn Tarot*, 1977

**[Documented fact]** Wang’s deck, produced with Israel Regardie’s guidance, reconstructs Golden Dawn designs from order materials.

**Actual image.** The Prince/King equivalent is an armored chariot figure with lion and flame imagery, explicitly departing from the seated Marseille king. Colour scales and elemental emblems make the Golden Dawn correspondences visible.

### Chic and Sandra Tabatha Cicero, *Golden Dawn Magical Tarot*, 2000

**[Documented fact]** The Ciceros supplied another full reconstruction, more explicitly keyed to Golden Dawn colour scales, elemental weapons, zodiacal signs, and ritual symbolism.

**Finding.** Neither Wang nor Cicero is a facsimile of one universally fixed nineteenth-century Golden Dawn deck; both are modern visual realizations of manuscript descriptions.

## Waite-Smith, 1909

**[Documented fact]** Arthur Edward Waite designed the scheme and Pamela Colman Smith drew the cards. Rider published the pack in 1909; Waite’s explanatory *Pictorial Key to the Tarot* followed in 1910.

### Actual image

A mature crowned man sits on a high-backed throne. He holds a sprouting wand upright. His crown and cap of maintenance emphasize secular authority. Lions appear on the throne; salamanders decorate his mantle; another salamander stands near the throne. The ground is dry and ochre, producing a hot, desert-like setting.

Waite’s description states:

> “The King uplifts a flowering wand”

and calls his nature “dark, ardent, lithe, animated, impassioned, noble.” He also notes the lion emblazoned on the throne. [Waite, *Pictorial Key*, p. 76](https://www.ifate.com/arcanum/Pictorial-Key-to-the-Tarot-iFate.pdf?v=2)

### Sources and changes

- **Marseille inheritance:** seated king holding the suit emblem.
- **Golden Dawn inheritance:** Wands as Fire; lion/Leo associations; court personality characterized by elemental temperament.
- **Smith’s synthesis:** cap of maintenance, repeated salamanders, desert palette, and a living flowering wand turn a playing-card ruler into an emblematic character.
- **Sola Busca:** Smith demonstrably knew photographs of that deck, but a precise copying relationship for this King is **not established**.

### What the changes meant

**[Esoteric attribution]** Lion and salamander make Fire visible; the sprouting staff joins fiery will with living growth. Nevertheless Waite retained “King” and a throne rather than adopting *Book T*’s Prince in a lion-drawn chariot.

**[Important conflict]** Modern books often call Waite’s King “Fire of Fire” or “Air of Fire.” Waite’s published card does not settle this. “Air of Fire” follows the Golden Dawn Prince-slot mapping; “Fire of Fire” follows systems making Kings/Yod the fiery rank. Both cannot be treated as Waite’s explicit statement.

## Paul Foster Case and B.O.T.A.

### Case’s divinatory system

**[Documented fact]** In *The Oracle of the Tarot* and B.O.T.A. lessons, Case assigns the King of Wands a different celestial range from the Golden Dawn Prince:

- Third decan Pisces through second decan Aries.
- Approximately March 11–April 9.
- Mars in Pisces, Mars in Aries, Sun in Aries.
- Symbol: Hebrew Yod in a triangle.
- Principle: spiritual power or will in the world of ideas.

This makes Case’s King **Yod/Fire-rank**, not Golden Dawn Vav/Air-rank. [Case lesson transcription](https://www.scribd.com/doc/67617092/Tarot-Lessons-1-10-Paul-Foster-Case)

### B.O.T.A. deck, Paul Foster Case and Jessie Burns Parke

**[Documented fact]** Parke’s black-and-white line deck follows Waite-Smith closely in the court cards while leaving them for students to colour. The pips return to non-scenic arrangements rather than Smith’s narrative scenes.

**Actual change.** The King is a simplified, teachable line-art sovereign derived from Waite’s basic image. B.O.T.A.’s internal correspondence—not the drawing alone—makes him Yod and the fiery spiritual will.

## Thoth, 1938–1943; book 1944; full deck published 1969

**[Documented fact]** Aleister Crowley and Frieda Harris created the paintings between 1938 and 1943. Crowley published *The Book of Thoth* in 1944. A full commercial deck appeared posthumously in 1969.

### Name

**Prince of Wands**, subtitle **Prince of the Chariot of Fire**.

### Crowley’s description

Crowley writes:

> “The Prince of Wands represents the airy part of Fire”

and says that he rules from “the 21st degree of Cancer to the 20th degree of Leo.” He describes scale armor, bare arms, a rayed crown surmounted by a winged lion’s head, a curtain of flame, the sigil of To Mega Therion, a Phoenix wand, a lion drawing the chariot, a flaming wheel, and a sea of flame. [Crowley, *Book of Thoth*, court-card chapter](https://www.rahoorkhuit.net/library/crowley/equinox/volume3/vol_3_no_05.pdf)

### Actual painting

Harris compresses prince, lion, chariot, wheel, and flame into a vortex of orange, yellow, red, and green. The figure is less a naturalistic monarch than a kinetic field. The winged lion crown and leonine vehicle bind the first two Leo decans to the image; the surrounding waves of fire visualize Air’s expansion within Fire.

### Meaning

The Prince is brilliant, swift, expansive, adventurous, and capable of sustained work when guided by a creative purpose; his shadow is fickleness, theatricality, instability, cruelty, and explosive anger.

### Tzaddi/Heh issue

**[Finding]** Crowley’s “Tzaddi is not the Star” revision exchanges the Hebrew-letter assignments of the Emperor and Star. It does not change the Prince of Wands’ Vav, Air of Fire, or zodiacal span. Invoking the Emperor/Star swap as a special correspondence of this court is an error.

## Selected notable modern redesigns

### Thoth-derived decks

**[Modern tradition]** Many retain “Prince,” chariot, lion, and 20° Cancer–20° Leo. Some simplify the lion into a Leo glyph or identify the three decans in borders.

### Feminist and ungendered decks

**[Modern invention]** Decks such as the Motherpeace tradition, Wild Unknown–type systems, and numerous contemporary inclusive tarots replace King with Shaman, Elder, Father, Ruler, or a gender-neutral Sovereign. These changes question hereditary monarchy and fixed binary gender.

### Queer and decolonial designs

**[Modern invention]** Modern artists frequently depict a nonbinary, female, Black, Indigenous, disabled, or working-class leader. The governing idea shifts from domination to stewardship, vision, collective energy, or accountable leadership.

### Marseille restorations

**[Modern reconstruction]** Flornoy, Camoin-Jodorowsky, and other makers differ in how aggressively they repair broken lines or recover colours. A “restored” King may contain conjecture even when based on an old deck.

## The game

## Rank

**[Documented fact]** In the normal Latin-suit hierarchy:

**King > Queen > Knight/Cavalier > Page/Knave > numeral cards.**

The King of Batons therefore takes any other Batons card in a trick, subject to trumping and the game’s follow-suit rules.

## Point value

**[Documented fact]** Across a broad family of tarot and tarock games:

- King: five nominal points.
- Queen: four.
- Knight/Cavalier: three.
- Jack/Page: two.
- Ordinary card: one nominal point.
- Pagat/I, XXI, and Fool: five each.

In three-card counting, two points are deducted from each group; equivalently the King contributes four reduced points plus its share of the group adjustment. [Tarot point-counting](https://www.pagat.com/tarot/counting.html)

**Variants.**

- In French Tarot, king plus an ordinary card counts five points in the standard pair-counting presentation.
- In Minchiate, the four kings are the only suit cards with individual value, five each.
- Some regional games use reduced values directly.
- In some old Italian systems, Cups and Coins rank in reverse numerical order below the courts; this does not displace the King from the top of Batons.

## Is it numbered?

**[Documented fact]** No. Court cards are named/ranked, not trump-numbered. “22” belongs to Mathers’ or Etteilla’s full-pack enumerations, not historical play.

## Trump order disputes

The King of Wands is unaffected by the competing sequences because it is not a trump. For context:

- **[Scholarly reconstruction]** Historians distinguish three broad early Italian trump-order families, conventionally associated with Ferrara, Milan/Lombardy, and Bologna/Florence.
- Justice, Strength/Fortitude, and Temperance move relative to one another.
- In some traditions the virtues are grouped; in others imperial and papal figures are ordered differently.
- The Fool is normally unnumbered and does not win tricks in the ordinary manner. It is played as an excuse and recovered, usually in exchange for another card.
- Later occult decks label the Fool 0 or sometimes place it after XXI.
- These disputes do not make the King “Arcana 22.”

## Historical court-value anomaly in occult writing

A rule text associated with Crowley gives “Universe, King and Juggler” five, Queen four, “Prince and Fool” three. This reflects a particular Italian-game presentation and Crowley’s terminology, not a universal rule. [Crowley-associated *Tarocchi* text](https://www.100thmonkeypress.com/biblio/acrowley/books/tarocchi/tarocchi_text.pdf)

## Chronology of attestation

### Before tarot

- **Late fourteenth century:** **[Documented fact]** Playing cards appear in European records.
- **Fourteenth–fifteenth century:** **[Documented fact]** Mamluk cards use cups, coins, swords, and polo sticks, with *malik* and deputy courts.
- **Transmission:** **[Scholarly reconstruction]** Italian *naibi* and Spanish *naipes* descend linguistically and structurally from the Mamluk/Islamic card complex.

### Early triumph packs

- **c. 1412–1425:** Marziano describes a Visconti pack with classical-god trumps and bird suits. No King of Batons.
- **16 September 1440:** **[Documented fact]** Giusto Giusti records a painted pack of *naibi a trionfi* made as a gift for Sigismondo Malatesta. This displaced the 1442 Ferrara entry as the earliest presently known explicit triumph-pack record.
- **28 July 1442, Ferrara:** The Este court pays Marchione Burdochi for “uno paro de carte da trionfi” for Ercole and Sigismondo d’Este. [Italian transcription](https://letarot.it/Le-origini-italiane-del-Ludus-Triumphorum_pag_pg439_ita.aspx)
- **c. 1440s:** Cary-Yale and Brambilla groups; the relevant Kings are lost or uncertain.
- **c. 1450–1460:** **[Documented fact]** surviving Visconti-Sforza King of Staves.
- **1449:** Marcello calls Marziano’s game *ludus triumphorum*.
- **c. 1470–1500:** The Dominican *Sermo perutilis de ludo cum aliis* condemns gambling and supplies a list of twenty-one triumphs plus Fool. **Finding:** it does not give divinatory meanings to the King of Batons.
- **1491:** Sola Busca date.
- **c. 1490s–early 1500s:** Rosenwald/Budapest printed sheets.

### Name *tarocchi*

- **June 1505, Ferrara:** Earliest known use of *tarocchi* for the cards.
- **December 1505, Avignon:** *Taraux* appears.
- **Early sixteenth century:** Folengo and other poets use tarot cards in literary or combinatorial verse.
- **Finding:** the so-called *tarocchi appropriati* poems associate cards with named persons or character traits, but no securely traced early poem establishes the later canonical King-of-Wands reading of visionary fiery leadership.

### Printed French pattern

- **c. 1650–1670, Paris:** Noblet; oldest securely catalogued surviving Marseille-type deck in the BnF’s account.
- **c. 1701–1715, Lyon:** Dodal.
- **1760, Marseille:** Conver.

### Divinatory and occult transformation

- **1781:** Court de Gébelin and the Comte de Mellet publish their Egyptian and Hebrew theories in volume VIII of *Le Monde primitif*.
- **1783 onward:** Etteilla publishes methods of tarot divination.
- **late 1780s:** Grand Etteilla turns the King into numbered divinatory card 22.
- **1854–1856:** Éliphas Lévi publishes *Dogme et Rituel de la Haute Magie*.
- **1870:** Paul Christian’s *Histoire de la magie* expands Egyptian initiation legends.
- **1888:** Mathers publishes *The Tarot: Its Occult Signification, Use in Fortune-Telling, and Method of Play*.
- **1889:** Papus’s *Le Tarot des Bohémiens* and Wirth’s twenty-two trumps.
- **late 1880s–1890s:** Golden Dawn *Book T* formulates the chariot Prince, Air of Fire, and decan span.
- **1909–1910:** Waite-Smith deck and *Pictorial Key*.
- **1910s–1930s:** Case publishes and teaches his Yod/Aries-centered King system.
- **1930s:** Grimaud/Marteau standardization.
- **1938–1943:** Harris paints the Thoth cards.
- **1944:** *Book of Thoth*.
- **1969:** full commercial Thoth deck.
- **1977:** Wang Golden Dawn reconstruction.
- **1980s onward:** psychological, Jungian, feminist, queer, and archetypal readings proliferate.
- **1997:** Camoin-Jodorowsky Marseille synthesis.
- **2000:** Cicero Golden Dawn reconstruction.

### Principal conflicts

1. **Vav versus Yod.** Golden Dawn/Thoth Prince is Vav; Case and many modern “Kings are Fire” systems make the King Yod.
2. **20° Cancer–20° Leo versus Pisces–Aries.** Golden Dawn and Case are describing different court allocations.
3. **Air of Fire versus Fire of Fire.** Both circulate, but they arise from different rank mappings.
4. **Country gentleman versus charismatic visionary.** The former descends from Etteilla and Mathers; the latter is a twentieth-century elemental/archetypal development.
5. **Seated king versus chariot prince.** Marseille and Waite preserve kingship; Golden Dawn and Thoth visualize the Son’s mobile, airy force.

### Hebrew letter and path

**[Correction]** No standard Golden Dawn Hebrew letter or one of the twenty-two Tree-of-Life paths belongs individually to the King/Prince of Wands. Hebrew letters and paths belong principally to the twenty-two trumps. Vav here is the court’s position in the Tetragrammaton, not “the Vav path” assigned as though the court were a major arcana card.

## Divinatory meanings by author

Quotations are kept short; surrounding material is paraphrased.

### Etteilla, late eighteenth-century tradition

**Upright:** *Homme de campagne*—a man of the country; farmer, cultivator, rustic, and, by extension, an honest and straightforward man.

**Reversed:** *Homme bon et sévère*—a good but severe man; strict yet indulgent or tolerant.

**Development.** These phrases became the source of Mathers’ and Waite’s “country gentleman” language. The precise agricultural rationale is unknown. A later writer explicitly said it was unclear why the suit came to be linked with the country. [Etteilla translation](https://stolen-thyme.com/etteillas-suit-of-batons/)

### S. L. MacGregor Mathers, 1888

Mathers, card 22:

- Upright: “Man living in the country, Country Gentleman, Knowledge, Education.”
- Reversed: “A naturally good but severe man, Counsel, Advice, Deliberation.”

[1888 text](https://hermetics.net/media-library/tarot/samuel-liddell-macgregor-mathers-the-tarot/)

**Assessment.** This is visibly dependent upon the Etteilla stream. It predates the full Golden Dawn Air-of-Fire character analysis.

### A. E. Waite, 1910, p. 76

Waite’s upright list is:

> “Dark man, friendly, countryman, generally married, honest and conscientious.”

He adds honesty and possible news of an unexpected inheritance. Reversed:

> “Good, but severe; austere, yet tolerant.”

[Digital edition](https://www.ifate.com/arcanum/Pictorial-Key-to-the-Tarot-iFate.pdf?v=2)

**Assessment.** Waite preserves Etteilla/Mathers language almost verbatim while Smith’s image adds a much richer fiery iconography.

### Aleister Crowley, 1944

Crowley does not use a simple upright/reversed pair; he reads dignity and elemental interaction.

**Well dignified:** swift, strong, generous, noble, impulsive, capable of lucid conception and sustained work when animated by a creative intelligence.

**Ill dignified:** indecisive, unreliable, prejudiced, theatrical, cruel, intolerant, fickle, liable to melancholy, intoxication, or explosive anger.

**Assessment.** This is a complete shift from “countryman” to the psychological behavior of Air operating within Fire. [*Book of Thoth*](https://www.rahoorkhuit.net/library/crowley/equinox/volume3/vol_3_no_05.pdf)

### Paul Foster Case

**Upright:** an ardent, impulsive, influential man, possessed of authority and leadership; hasty in temper but just, generous, and friendly.

**Ill-dignified:** cruel, ill-natured, intolerant, and probably unfriendly.

**Physical type:** red-gold hair; gray or hazel eyes.

**Assessment.** Case’s vocabulary resembles Golden Dawn character analysis, but his zodiacal allocation differs. His popular “dark man, friendly, ardent” summary is sometimes conflated with Waite. [Case material](https://benebellwen.com/wp-content/uploads/2013/02/paul-foster-case-oracle-of-the-tarot-1933-source-credit-tarotworks.pdf)

### Rachel Pollack

**Copyright-limited paraphrase.** In *Seventy-Eight Degrees of Wisdom* Pollack reads the Waite-Smith King as socially established creative power: a charismatic leader who acts from confidence and mature will rather than the Knight’s restless search. The shadow is domineering certainty, intolerance, or taking one’s own enthusiasm as sufficient authority.

**Status.** Modern psychological/archetypal elaboration, grounded in Waite-Smith imagery and post-Golden Dawn elemental practice rather than historical game usage.

### Mary K. Greer

**Copyright-limited paraphrase.** Greer’s court-card method treats the King of Wands as a role, person, internal mode, or style of action: directing vision, inspiring others, and authorizing creative risk. Reversal or shadow work can disclose arrogance, coercive charisma, temper, burnout, or fear of exercising authority.

**Finding.** Greer’s various books and deck-specific treatments do not reduce to a single universally fixed upright/reversed formula. Attributing one internet keyword list to “Greer’s official meaning” would be misleading.

## Esoteric and symbolic readings

## Alchemical

**First securely influential setting:** nineteenth-century occult syntheses, especially Lévi and the later Golden Dawn; fully visualized in Thoth.

- Fire corresponds to heat, calcination, combustion, and transformative will.
- Air of Fire is the gaseous, expanding, volatilizing aspect of flame.
- The Prince is produced by the union of Knight/Father and Queen/Mother: an alchemical Son.
- Crowley’s flaming wheel and sea of fire show energy becoming mobile and transmissible.

**Historical warning.** No fifteenth-century document identifies the Visconti King’s baton with an alchemical operation.

## Kabbalistic

### Lévi

**[Documented general doctrine]** Lévi connected tarot, the Hebrew alphabet, the four worlds, and occult correspondences. In *Dogme et Rituel* he treated the wand as the magician’s emblem of will.

**Finding:** no verified passage in Lévi’s 1854–1856 work gives the later complete formula “King of Wands = Air of Fire, Vav, 20° Cancer–20° Leo.” That synthesis is Golden Dawn-era.

### Papus

Papus elaborated the four courts through the Tetragrammaton and presented tarot as a universal combinatorial key. His title, *Tarot of the Bohemians*, helped spread the Romani transmission legend. [Library of Congress edition](https://www.loc.gov/item/77355368/)

**Card-specific caution.** Papus’s tables vary between editions and diagrammatic contexts. The exact Golden Dawn Prince scheme should not be retroactively credited to him.

### Golden Dawn

The most precise Kabbalistic statement is:

- Wands = Atziluth, the archetypal world and Fire.
- Prince = Vav, Son, Air.
- Hence Vav of Yod, Air of Fire.
- Prince-function = Tiphereth, mediating the supernal parents and producing the Princess.

**[Esoteric attribution]** This is a ritual-philosophical map, not an historical derivation of the Italian King.

## Astrological

### Golden Dawn/Thoth

The range crosses Cancer into Leo. This produces an internal tension:

- Cancer decan: receptivity, memory, lunar saturation—Four of Cups/Luxury.
- First Leo decan: Saturn constrains or conflicts with solar fire—Five of Wands/Strife.
- Second Leo decan: Jupiter magnifies regal, victorious fire—Six of Wands/Victory.

The lion-drawn chariot gives Leo visual dominance, but one-third of the domain lies in Cancer.

### Case

Case’s Pisces–Aries King emphasizes transition from watery, imaginative potential into cardinal fire and solar assertion. This is incompatible with the Golden Dawn Prince’s Cancer–Leo span because Case assigns the court ranks differently.

## Hermetic

The baton becomes the directed magical will: a ruler does not merely possess energy but formulates and transmits it. In Thoth, Air makes Fire intelligible and contagious—idea becoming enthusiasm, command, propaganda, or inspiration.

## Christian

**Historical image:** The crowned ruler can be read within the Christian social order of Renaissance Europe; staff and crown signify temporal authority.

**Later allegory:** Ethical kingship, stewardship, righteous zeal, and the danger of wrath can be mapped onto the card.

**Finding:** no early Christian sermon supplies a unique devotional meaning for the King of Batons. The *Sermo de ludo* condemns the game; it does not baptize this court into a Christian allegory.

## Jungian

**First appearance:** not in Jung himself as a fixed tarot doctrine. Jung discussed archetypes, symbols, alchemy, and synchronicity, but no canonical Jung text establishes a King-of-Wands interpretation.

**Spreaders:** postwar tarot writers, especially in the 1970s–1990s, combined Jungian vocabulary with Waite-Smith imagery.

**Typical reading:**

- King = sovereign, father, ego authority, or integrated executive function.
- Wands/Fire = libido in the broad Jungian sense of psychic energy, creativity, desire, and transformation.
- Upright/integrated = creative authority that can inspire a group.
- Shadow = inflation, grandiosity, domination, rage, compulsive charisma, or identification with the heroic persona.

This is **[modern psychological synthesis]**, not an ancient meaning.

- **Jean Dodal:** influential Lyon Type-I version.

- **Pamela Colman Smith:** created the most influential modern King of Wands image.
- **Arthur Edward Waite:** specified the flowering wand, cap of maintenance, lion, character, and meanings.
- **Paul Foster Case and Jessie Burns Parke:** made the B.O.T.A. line-art and alternative Yod/Aries King.
- **Aleister Crowley and Frieda Harris:** made the Prince of Wands an abstract chariot of Air within Fire.

## Moakley and Kaplan

**Gertrude Moakley**, *The Tarot Cards Painted by Bonifacio Bembo for the Visconti-Sforza Family* (1966), interpreted the trumps through Renaissance triumphal processions and treated the pack in its courtly context.

**Stuart R. Kaplan**, through *The Encyclopedia of Tarot* and commercial reproductions, made Visconti-Sforza imagery globally accessible and repeated or catalogued many proposed family identifications.

**Disputed point:** Neither biographical resemblance nor dynastic heraldry proves that the King of Batons is an exact portrait of a named Sforza.

## Literature, film, buildings, and paintings

### Italo Calvino, *The Castle of Crossed Destinies*

**[Documented general connection]** Calvino structured narratives from tarot layouts, particularly Visconti and Marseille imagery.

**Finding:** no uniquely canonical identification makes the King of Wands the book’s controlling card. It can participate in particular layouts or translations, but claims that Calvino supplied its standard meaning were not traced.

### T. S. Eliot, *The Waste Land*

**[Documented fact]** Eliot names “the drowned Phoenician Sailor,” “Belladonna,” “the man with three staves,” the Wheel, and the one-eyed merchant through Madame Sosostris.

**Finding:** Eliot does **not** name the King of Wands. “The man with three staves” is Eliot’s invented or adapted card and is often compared with the Three of Wands, not this King.

### W. B. Yeats

**[Documented general connection]** Yeats participated in the Golden Dawn and knew its symbolism.

**Finding:** no major Yeats poem or prose passage was located that establishes a distinct King/Prince-of-Wands interpretation. Golden Dawn affiliation alone is not card-specific evidence.

### Ingmar Bergman

**[Documented general relevance]** Bergman’s *The Seventh Seal* uses medieval allegory, chess, Death, and procession imagery often compared with tarot.

**Finding:** no demonstrated quotation of the King of Wands was traced.

### Jodorowsky’s films

Jodorowsky’s films use tarot-like processions, alchemical journeys, rulers, staffs, and initiation imagery. His strongest card-specific teaching occurs in his tarot books and deck work, not in a securely identified cinematic quotation of the King of Wands.

### Paintings and buildings

**[Not traced]** No major pre-1900 painting or building can be securely shown to quote the King of Wands specifically. Resemblances to enthroned rulers, civic statues with sceptres, or Solomon/David imagery are iconographic analogies unless documentary evidence connects the artist with the card.

## Controversies and disputes

## Egyptian origin

### Claim

Court de Gébelin declared in 1781 that tarot was a surviving Egyptian book. He wrote, in substance, that this Egyptian book still existed and consisted of seventy-seven or seventy-eight pages: the game of Tarot. [French text](https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/Page%3ACourt_de_G%C3%A9belin_-_Monde_primitif_analys%C3%A9_et_compar%C3%A9_avec_le_moderne%2C_8e_livraison.djvu/499)

### Spread

- Comte de Mellet linked the trumps with Hebrew letters and divination.
- Etteilla produced explicitly Egyptianized decks.
- Paul Christian elaborated temple-initiation narratives.
- Papus, Wirth, and later occultists transmitted related versions.
- Crowley retained “Book of Thoth” as a sacred title while substantially knowing that his deck was a modern occult synthesis.

### Evidence

**[Legend]** No Egyptian tarot pack, hieroglyphic source, temple manual, or ancient text corresponding to the seventy-eight cards exists. Gébelin’s Egyptian etymologies were speculative and were offered without hieroglyphic evidence.

**[Historical correction]** The documentary trail places *carte da trionfi* in fifteenth-century Italy and the familiar suits in the preceding Islamic–Mediterranean playing-card tradition. Dummett specifically objected that the occult writers supplied purported Egyptian word derivations “without citing any hieroglyphic forms.” [Dummett, “Origins of Tarot”](https://www.nybooks.com/articles/1981/05/14/origins-of-tarot/)

### Card-specific result

The King’s best Egyptian connection is not a pharaonic mystery school but the medieval Mamluk **king of polo-sticks**. Mamluk Egypt is historically relevant; ancient Pharaonic initiation is not evidenced.

## “Gypsy” or Romani origin

### Claim

Tarot was allegedly preserved or carried from Egypt or India into Europe by “Bohemians” or “Gypsies.”

### Spread

Court de Gébelin made an early association; nineteenth-century authors including Vaillant developed it; Papus popularized it through *Le Tarot des Bohémiens* (1889). [Historical discussion](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/0048721X.2023.2250328)

### Evidence

**[Legend]** European cards are recorded before or independently of the documented Romani diaspora in western Europe. No early Italian tarot record credits Romani makers or carriers. Modern Romani linguistic history points to Indian origins, not ancient Egypt.

**[Contested social history]** Romani people did practice forms of fortune-telling in Europe, but that does not establish that they invented tarot. The origin story also drew upon exoticizing stereotypes.

## Cathar origin

**Claim:** The trumps or Marseille designs encode Cathar theology, with the suit of Wands representing spiritual lineage or concealed heresy.

**Evidence:** **[Disputed/unsupported]** No Cathar manuscript, inquisitorial record, fifteenth-century card-maker’s document, or continuous iconographic program identifies the King of Batons as Cathar. The claim generally begins in much later speculative literature.

## Templar origin

**Claim:** The cards preserve Knights Templar initiation, Baphomet symbolism, or an underground Templar code.

**Evidence:** **[Legend]** The Templar order was suppressed in the early fourteenth century, roughly a century before documented triumph packs. No transmission document bridges the gap; no King-of-Wands-specific Templar identification was found.

## Kabbalistic antiquity

**Claim:** The seventy-eight cards were originally constructed around twenty-two Hebrew letters and ten Sephiroth.

**Evidence:** **[Esoteric attribution, historically late]** The first explicit published Hebrew-letter mappings arise in 1781 with de Mellet, centuries after the earliest cards. The Golden Dawn system is coherent as nineteenth-century occult architecture, but coherence is not proof of Renaissance origin.

## Fire as an ancient suit meaning

**Claim:** Renaissance *Bastoni* inherently meant the element Fire.

**Evidence:** **[Unproven]** Early cards and game records identify a suit emblem, not an element. Elemental correspondences developed through occult tetrads. Different writers have assigned suits differently; one early twentieth-century author even argued Wands as Air.

## The King as Francesco Sforza

**Claim:** The Visconti-Sforza King of Batons is a portrait of Francesco Sforza.

**Evidence:** **[Plausible but disputed]** Dynastic patronage, costume, and heraldry make family reference possible. There is no inscription or sufficiently portrait-specific evidence to secure the identification.

## Salamanders as alchemical proof

**Claim:** Smith’s salamanders demonstrate a continuous secret alchemical tradition running back to Marseille.

**Evidence:** **[Overstatement]** Salamanders were traditional fire creatures and fit Golden Dawn elemental symbolism. They are explicit in Smith’s 1909 design but absent from the early Visconti and standard Marseille King. Their presence proves Smith’s symbolic program, not an unbroken medieval code.

## “Dark man” and race

**Historical fact:** Etteilla, Mathers, Waite, and Case used physiognomic categories—dark/fair hair, eyes, complexion—to choose significators.

**Modern controversy:** Readers sometimes racialize “dark man” in ways not consistently warranted by the source systems. In Waite, it belongs to a conventional European cartomantic taxonomy and can describe hair/complexion or temperament. Modern decks frequently abandon it as reductive.

## Misprints and variants

- **King/Prince ambiguity:** Some *Book T* copies place “King” in the list but immediately title the figure “Prince.” This has produced generations of contradictory “King = Air” and “King = Fire” tables.
- **Etteilla numbering/orientation:** Later Grand Etteilla editions occasionally swap upright and reversed inscriptions or change sequence numbers.
- **Cancer degree wording:** Crowley’s “21st degree” means the interval beginning after 20°00′, conventionally summarized as 20° Cancer. It is not a one-degree discrepancy.
- **Thoth publication date:** 1938–1943 dates the painting; 1944 dates the book; 1969 dates the complete commercial deck. Calling it simply a “1944 deck” conflates these events.
- **Noblet date:** “1650” is conventional. The BnF more cautiously places it in the second half of the seventeenth century and notes documentary dates for Noblet from 1659.
- **Cary-Yale versus Visconti di Modrone:** treating them as separate decks duplicates one surviving group.
- **Wirth minors:** Wirth’s 1889 set had only majors. Later complete “Wirth” packs can misleadingly imply that he designed a King of Wands in 1889.
- **Fool as trump 0:** historical Fools were commonly unnumbered. Zero is a later convention and irrelevant to the King’s number.
- **“Arcana 22”:** Mathers’ no. 22 or Etteilla’s printed number is not evidence that the King is the twenty-second trump.

## Dedicated contested-material findings

Explicit searches combining “King of Wands” with *origin, myth, dispute, misattribution, Egyptian, Gypsy/Romani, Cathar,* and *Templar* produced abundant general tarot-origin mythology but almost no old claim uniquely concerning this card.

That absence matters:

1. Egyptian, Romani, Cathar, and Templar theories concern the pack or trumps as a supposed sacred book.
2. The King of Wands entered those theories mainly by systemic extension.
3. Its independently reconstructable lineage—Mamluk polo-stick King → Latin-suit King of Batons → tarot court → occult elemental ruler—is more securely evidenced than any secret-order genealogy.
4. No primary source was found in which Gébelin, Lévi, or Paul Christian singles out this King as a special Egyptian, Cathar, or Templar figure.
5. Modern claims identifying it with a specific pharaoh, Solomon, a Templar grand master, or a Cathar perfect are therefore **modern invention or unsupported speculation unless accompanied by evidence not located here**.

## Open questions

1. **Which surviving King is absolutely earliest?** Dating within the Visconti groups continues to be refined; “c. 1450” is safer than an exact year.
2. **Was the Visconti King a portrait?** Dynastic reference is probable at deck level; individual identity remains insecure.
3. **How directly did Mamluk cards reach northern Italian workshops?** Structural influence is compelling, but the precise trade route and intermediary packs are undocumented.
4. **What was the exact archetype behind Noblet’s King?** Lost Italian and French cards almost certainly intervene between fifteenth-century examples and the surviving seventeenth-century woodcut.
5. **Why did Etteilla associate Batons courts with country people?** Later authors transmitted the rule but did not preserve a convincing origin.
6. **Which hand drafted each portion of *Book T*?** Manuscript layers and later publication complicate attribution to Mathers alone.
7. **Did Waite privately regard his King as the Golden Dawn Prince/Air of Fire?** The public *Pictorial Key* avoids the explicit correspondence, and Smith returned the figure to a throne.
8. **How much of Smith’s King derives from stage design, costume research, or particular art reproductions?** The general ingredients are identifiable; a definitive single visual source has not been demonstrated.
9. **What details in modern Marseille “restorations” reproduce damaged originals, and which are conjectural?** This must be evaluated feature by feature.
10. **Can early *tarocchi appropriati* poems be tied securely to this court’s later personality?** No decisive bridge was located.

## Conclusions

The King of Wands began neither as an Egyptian priest nor as a Jungian entrepreneur. The most economical reconstruction is:

1. A medieval Islamic **king of polo-sticks** supplied a structural ancestor.
2. Italian makers converted polo sticks into *bastoni* and pictured the ruler as a human king.
3. In the Visconti-Sforza pack, the card is a gilded courtly sovereign and the highest Batons card.
4. Popular woodcuts gradually produced the seated *Roi de Bâton* of the Marseille family.
5. Etteilla made him card 22 and a “man of the country,” good but severe when reversed.
6. Mathers and Waite transmitted that cartomantic character.
7. The Golden Dawn transformed the corresponding court slot into a lion-drawn **Prince of the Chariot of Fire**, Vav and Air of Fire, ruling 20° Cancer–20° Leo.
8. Waite retained the seated King but Smith surrounded him with flowering wand, lions, salamanders, and a fiery landscape.
9. Crowley and Harris made the Golden Dawn Prince explicit and kinetic.
10. Case instead made his King Yod and gave him a Pisces–Aries span.
11. Modern practice merged these incompatible inheritances into the charismatic visionary-leader archetype.

The card therefore carries at least three historically distinct identities:

- the **game King of Batons**;
- the **Etteilla-Waite country gentleman and honest ruler**;
- the **Golden Dawn/Thoth Air-of-Fire Prince**.

Treating them as one timeless doctrine conceals the card’s most interesting history: its repeated conversion from rank, to character, to elemental process, and finally to psychological archetype.
