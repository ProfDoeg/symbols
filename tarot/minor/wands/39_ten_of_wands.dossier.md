# Ten of Wands: Research Dossier

## Method and evidentiary labels

This is a dossier on a numbered suit card, not a trump. Many requested systems—Court de Gébelin’s trump essay, de Mellet’s Hebrew alphabet, Wirth’s twenty-two-card deck, the Tzaddi/Heh controversy—do not assign anything specifically to the Ten of Wands. They are included only where they altered the suit, number, or interpretive framework.

Claims are marked:

- **[Documented fact]** Directly attested by a surviving card, archival record, rulebook, or cited primary text.
- **[Scholarly reconstruction]** A conclusion argued from surviving objects or documents but not stated in them.
- **[Tradition]** A historically transmitted reading whose empirical truth is not asserted.
- **[Esoteric attribution]** A deliberately constructed occult correspondence.
- **[Disputed claim]** A published claim rejected or questioned by other researchers.
- **[Legend]** A historical narrative unsupported by contemporaneous evidence.
- **[Modern invention]** A substantially twentieth- or twenty-first-century interpretation presented as a new synthesis.
- **[Not traced]** No adequate card-specific evidence was found in the sources consulted.

Quotations retain historical spelling where useful. Page numbers refer to the named editions; digitized copies sometimes count covers differently.

### What “Wands” means historically

**[Documented fact]** The old Italian suit is *bastoni*: batons, sticks, clubs, or staves. Its French equivalent is *bâtons* and its Spanish equivalent *bastos*. The Latin-suited signs—swords, cups, coins and batons—precede tarot and belong to the ordinary playing-card pack to which trumps were added.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** The European baton suit probably descends from the polo-stick suit in Mamluk cards. Because polo was unfamiliar in much of Europe, the implement became a club, cudgel, staff, or ceremonial baton. Italian packs commonly stylized it as a straight rod; Spanish packs emphasized knobbly clubs. See the comparative history of the [baton suit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Batons_%28suit%29).

**[Esoteric attribution]** “Wands” is the preferred English occult name, especially after the Golden Dawn and Waite. It turns a playing-card club into a magical implement: will, Fire, vitality, enterprise, and creative force.

## Iconography deck by deck

## Fifteenth-century Milan

### Visconti di Modrone / Cary–Yale, approximately 1440s

**[Documented fact]** The deck usually called Cary–Yale or Visconti di Modrone survives at Yale’s Beinecke Library. It is an anomalous luxury pack: its courts include additional female figures, and its original total may have exceeded the later standard seventy-eight.

**[Not traced]** A securely catalogued, surviving Ten of Batons from this pack could not be established from the accessible museum records consulted. Modern “complete” reproductions routinely supply missing cards; such additions cannot be treated as fifteenth-century evidence.

**Meaning of the absence:** nothing can responsibly be said about a Cary–Yale Ten of Batons beyond the likely expectation that, if present, it belonged to the conventional non-scenic pip family.

### Brambilla / Brera–Brambilla, before approximately 1450

**[Documented fact]** This fragmentary Milanese luxury pack preserves only a limited number of cards. It is associated with the Visconti court and named for the Brambilla collection.

**[Not traced]** No surviving Ten of Batons was confirmed. Reconstructed editions are modern completions.

### Pierpont Morgan–Bergamo Visconti–Sforza, approximately 1450–1460

**[Documented fact]** A Ten of Staves survives. Ten long, narrow, usually red-brown or greenish staffs are arranged as a formal pip display on a gold ground, with ribbons, flowers, or heraldic ornament. There is no human burden-bearer and no divinatory narrative. The card identifies number and suit.

The surviving Visconti–Sforza cards are divided principally between the Morgan Library, Accademia Carrara, and the Colleoni collection. The Morgan describes the family as Milanese, approximately 1450–1480, and stresses that these were game cards, not originally fortune-telling instruments. The attribution to Bonifacio Bembo has been traditional; Francesco Zavattari and later hands have also been proposed. See the [Morgan catalogue](https://www.themorgan.org/manuscript/158525) and a reproduction of the [Ten of Batons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Bembo-Visconti-tarot-staves-10.jpg).

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** This is the earliest presently identifiable surviving Ten of Wands/Batons. Because the related Visconti groups have disputed dates and incomplete provenances, “approximately mid-fifteenth-century Milan” is safer than an exact year.

**What changed later:** the Visconti card is enumerative and heraldic. Meaning resides in suit membership, number, patronal devices, and play—not in an illustrated episode.

## Ferrarese and north-Italian sheets

### Este fragments, late fifteenth century

**[Documented fact]** Several cards associated with Ferrara or the Este court survive, but “the Este tarot” names more than one fragmentary object or attributed group.

**[Not traced]** A securely identified Este Ten of Batons was not located. Claims that a modern restored Este pack shows the historical card often conflate surviving originals with twentieth-century completions.

### Rosenwald sheets, approximately 1500

**[Documented fact]** The Rosenwald sheets preserve early printed Italian tarot designs, including pips presented schematically rather than as narrative scenes. The exact place of production has been debated, with Florence and other central/northern Italian origins considered.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** Where a Ten of Batons is reconstructed from the sheets, it belongs to the intersecting, repeated-suit-sign tradition: straight clubs or rods arranged symmetrically, supplemented by foliage. It is a playing-card design, not an image of oppression.

### Budapest sheets, late fifteenth or early sixteenth century

**[Documented fact]** The Budapest material consists of fragmentary printed sheets conventionally associated with early Italian tarot manufacture.

**[Not traced]** A sufficiently clear, securely numbered Ten of Batons could not be confirmed from an authoritative online catalogue. Modern reconstructions should not be silently substituted for the fragments.

### Mantegna “Tarocchi,” approximately 1460s–1470s

**[Documented fact]** The so-called Mantegna Tarocchi are fifty educational or emblematic engravings arranged in five groups. They are neither a standard tarot pack nor a work securely attributable to Mantegna.

**Relevance:** none to the Ten of Wands. They contain no four-suited pip sequence and therefore no Ten of Batons.

## Sola Busca, engraved 1491; colored approximately 1500

**[Documented fact]** The Sola Busca is the earliest substantially complete surviving tarot pack and the earliest surviving pack to give its numbered suit cards extensive pictorial settings. Modern scholarship generally places its engraving in 1491, probably in the Ferrara/Venice cultural orbit; the surviving colored set is at the Pinacoteca di Brera. See the [Public Domain Review overview](https://publicdomainreview.org/collection/sola-busca) and [complete card gallery](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Sola-Busca_tarot_deck).

### Its actual Ten of Batons

**[Documented fact]** The Sola Busca does contain a Ten of Batons, but it is not the direct model normally meant when writers compare Sola Busca with the Waite–Smith Ten of Wands. It is a Renaissance scenic pip built around the physical batons and an enigmatic human activity. Its imagery belongs to the deck’s wider repertoire of labor, implements, classical names, military objects, and cryptic actions.

### The crucial correction: its Ten of Swords

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** Smith’s Ten of Wands is much closer compositionally to the **Sola Busca Ten of Swords**, whose man bends forward beneath or while carrying ten long blades. A comparative study explicitly places the Sola Busca Ten of Swords beside Smith’s Ten of Wands: [Nogales Vargas, p. 62](https://rest-dspace.ucuenca.edu.ec/server/api/core/bitstreams/f203eae1-e17e-467b-a07e-21229d604748/content).

Photographs of the Sola Busca cards were displayed at the British Museum from 1907, shortly before Smith made her deck. Smith demonstrably echoed other Sola Busca pips, so access and general influence are strongly plausible.

**Conclusion on the requested lead:** “The Sola Busca pip was Smith’s source” is supportable only when stated precisely:

> **Probable visual source: the Sola Busca Ten of Swords, not the Sola Busca Ten of Batons.**

No surviving letter from Smith or Waite says, “I copied this card.” Direct dependence is therefore a strong visual and circumstantial reconstruction, not a documented confession.

## Minchiate

**[Documented fact]** Minchiate expanded the tarot family to ninety-seven cards: ordinary Italian suits plus forty trumps and the Fool. Surviving printed packs generally give the Ten of Batons a conventional Italian pip arrangement—ten straight or interlaced rods with ornamental vegetation—not a scenic burden-bearer.

**Game meaning:** the Ten is especially strong because batons are a “long” suit. In the reconstructed and recorded rules, long-suit numeral cards rank 10 high down to Ace low. It usually carries no intrinsic card points, though it may win tricks. See [Minchiate rules](https://www.pagat.com/tarot/minchiate.html).

**[Modern invention]** Jodorowsky-style developmental readings—ten as completion, maximum expansion, or transition to a new cycle—are interpretive additions, not meanings printed on historic Minchiate cards.

---

## Bolognese tarocchino

**[Documented fact]** The Bolognese game reduced the standard pack by removing the low numeral cards. The retained suit cards preserve Italian signs, including Batons. The Ten remains the strongest numerical card in the long suit.

**[Documented fact]** Bolognese cards provide important evidence for early cartomancy: manuscript lists of card meanings have been dated to the eighteenth century and possibly preserve earlier practices. This does not establish occult use in the fifteenth century.

**Iconography:** formal, repeated clubs/batons rather than a person carrying a bundle.

---

## Sicilian tarot

**[Documented fact]** Tarot was introduced to Sicily in the seventeenth century. The later Sicilian pattern acquired distinctive trumps, court structures, and tax-related alterations. The Ten of Batons is a high numeral card in the long suit, above 9. Some forms omit low cards but retain 10 through 5 or 4. See [Sicilian Tarocchi rules](https://www.pagat.com/tarot/sicilian.html).

**Iconography:** repeated *bastoni*, conventionally thick or club-like, without a Waitean episode.

## Tarot de Marseille lineage

### General construction of the Marseille Ten of Batons

**[Documented fact]** The card normally displays eight batons interlaced diagonally into a lattice, crossed or bisected by two additional batons. Leaves and flowers occupy the open fields or emerge around the intersection. “Ten” may be signaled only by counting the suit signs; numeral captions vary by edition.

The floral growth is ornamental and structurally useful. Historic printers did not supply a prose meaning. Claims that a particular blossom “is certainly” a named alchemical plant are usually later interpretation.

### Jean Noblet, Paris, approximately 1650

**[Documented fact]** Noblet is among the earliest surviving decks in what is now called the Marseille family. Its pips use compact interlaced construction and vegetal fillers.

**Caution:** some cards in the surviving Noblet pack are damaged or missing. Restorations by Jean-Claude Flornoy and others necessarily reconstruct lost detail.

### Jean Dodal, Lyon, approximately 1701–1715

**[Documented fact]** The Dodal Ten of Batons retains the interwoven grid. The outer batons are stylized, the central pair clearer, and small plant ornaments fill the remaining spaces. It is decorative enumeration, not narrative illustration.

### Nicolas Conver, Marseille, 1760

**[Documented fact]** Conver regularizes the familiar Type II Marseille pattern. The Ten has eight diagonally interlaced batons, two stronger axial rods, and paired floral/leaf ornaments. Conver’s design became extraordinarily influential through later Marseille editions. Dates for Noblet, Dodal, and Conver can be compared at [Tarot History](https://tarot-history.com/) and [LeTarot](https://www.letarot.com/).

### Grimaud, nineteenth and twentieth centuries

**[Documented fact]** Grimaud industrialized a Conver-derived Marseille pattern. Lines and colors became more uniform; printing registration and typography replaced much hand-cut irregularity.

**Change in meaning:** none was inherent in the print. Its later sale as a divinatory deck placed older cartomantic lists beside what remained a non-scenic pip.

### Camoin–Jodorowsky, 1997

**[Documented fact]** Philippe Camoin and Alejandro Jodorowsky published a restoration/recomposition claimed to recover lost Marseille details from multiple historical decks and printing matrices.

**[Disputed claim]** Calling it the uniquely “restored original Tarot de Marseille” goes beyond the evidence. There was no single recoverable master original; Noblet, Dodal, Madenié, Conver and others preserve related but divergent stages.

**[Modern esoteric interpretation]** Jodorowsky and Marianne Costa read the suit numerologically and visually. Ten signifies fulfillment, completion, or the end of a cycle; Wands concern instinctive, sexual, creative, or vital energy. The flowers and crossing rods become evidence for maximum realized energy and transition. This is an interpretive method applied to the historic pattern, not a documented seventeenth-century meaning.

## Besançon-pattern tarot

**[Documented fact]** In the Besançon/Swiss-derived pattern, the controversial Popess and Pope trumps were renamed **Juno** and **Jupiter**, a change associated with production in Protestant or confessionally sensitive markets from the eighteenth century onward. The Fool remained normally unnumbered, and the Tower inherited names such as *La Maison Dieu* from French tarot traditions.

**Card-specific effect:** essentially none. The Ten of Batons remained a Marseille-like ornamental pip.

The Juno/Jupiter changes concern trumps II and V; they should not be projected onto the Ten.

---

## Etteilla and the Grand Etteilla, 1780s–1790s

**[Documented fact]** Jean-Baptiste Alliette, “Etteilla,” published the first systematic printed method devoted to tarot cartomancy in *Manière de se récréer avec le jeu de cartes nommées tarots* (1783–1785) and issued an occult/divinatory pack commonly dated 1788–1789. Grand Etteilla packs reorder all seventy-eight cards and print meanings directly on them.

### Card 26

A surviving later manual shows card 26 labelled:

- *Le Dix de Bâton*
- upright: **TRAHISON**
- reversed: **OBSTACLE**

The illustration combines ten baton marks with an emblem resembling a key or barrier. See the digitized [*Le grand Etteilla, ou l’art de tirer les cartes*](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/2/2f/Le_grand_Etteilla%2C_ou%2C_l%27art_de_tirer_les_cartes_..._Le_tout_recueilli_..._%28IA_b29321220%29.pdf).

**[Documented tradition]** Different Grand Etteilla families and later printers moved or altered keywords. Some lists give *Trahison* upright and *Obstacle* reversed; others expand the semantic field to betrayal, duplicity, conspiracy, hypocrisy, and impediment. The safest statement is that treachery/obstruction belongs to the Etteilla lineage, but exact orientation depends on edition.

**Important conflict:** this is not the later Golden Dawn “Oppression” doctrine, although obstruction supplied semantic material that Mathers and Waite inherited.

## Oswald Wirth, 1889

**[Documented fact]** Wirth’s *Les 22 Arcanes du Tarot kabbalistique*, made under the influence of Stanislas de Guaita and Éliphas Lévi, contained only twenty-two trumps.

**Result:** there is no Wirth Ten of Wands. Images sold today as a “complete Wirth tarot” contain later additions by other hands.

## Golden Dawn: Book T and reconstructed decks

### Book T, approximately 1888 onward

**[Documented fact]** The Golden Dawn manuscript gives:

> “THE LORD OF OPPRESSION  
> TEN OF WANDS  
> Four hands upholding 8 wands crossed as before. A fifth hand holding two wands upright, which traverse the junction of the others. Flames issuant. Saturn and Sagittarius.”

It continues:

> “Cruel and overbearing force and energy, but applied only to material and selfish ends. Sometimes shows failure in a matter, and the opposition too strong to be controlled…”

The text also gives malice, slander, envy, obstinacy, lying and deceit when ill-dignified, but generosity, disinterestedness and self-sacrifice when well-dignified. An online transcription appears in the [Golden Dawn manuscripts](https://www.tarrdaniel.com/documents/Thelemagick/gd/publication/english/golden_dawn_manuscripts.html).

### Qabalistic and astrological construction

**[Esoteric attribution]**

- 10 = Malkuth.
- Wands = Yod = Atziluth = Fire.
- Therefore: Malkuth of Yod, the materialized or terminal condition of Fire.
- 20°–30° Sagittarius = Saturn by the Golden Dawn’s decan sequence.
- Title = Lord of Oppression.
- Shem angels = Reyel/Reiyel and Omael, with spellings varying by transliteration.
- Color work depends on world/scale and reconstruction; there is no single flat “color of the card.”

### Robert Wang, 1977

**[Documented fact]** Wang’s *Golden Dawn Tarot* is a modern realization of Order descriptions, not an antique Golden Dawn deck. Its Ten of Wands follows the hand-held crossed-wand diagram and incorporates elemental, zodiacal and planetary emblems.

### Chic and Sandra Tabatha Cicero, 1990s onward

**[Documented fact]** The Ciceros’ Golden Dawn deck likewise reconstructs the manuscript system. It is more explicitly color-coded and symbolically annotated than surviving nineteenth-century sketches.

**Meaning:** both Wang and Cicero translate textual ritual specifications into publishable pictures. Differences between them are reconstruction choices, not rival surviving originals.

## Waite–Smith, 1909

### What Smith drew

**[Documented fact]** A man seen largely from behind or in profile bends forward while gripping ten cut, leafy staffs in a dense bundle. The staffs obstruct his face and line of sight. He advances toward a walled settlement or manor in the middle distance. The ground is cultivated and the sky clear.

This converts a schematic pip into a temporal episode: the load has been gathered; it must be carried somewhere; the destination is near but difficult to see.

### Waite’s text

In *The Pictorial Key to the Tarot*, Part III, “The Lesser Arcana,” Wands: Ten, Waite writes:

> “A man oppressed by the weight of the ten staves which he is carrying.”

He then says:

> “The chief meaning is oppression simply, but it is also fortune, gain, any kind of success, and then it is the oppression of these things.”

He adds false-seeming, disguise and perfidy, warns that the place approached “may suffer from the rods,” and gives reversed:

> “Contrarieties, difficulties, intrigues, and their analogies.”

See Waite’s complete [Ten of Wands entry](https://sacred-texts.com/book/the-pictorial-key-to-the-tarot/shell/ten-of-wands). In the Rider 1911 edition this is in the Wands pip section; scan pagination varies, commonly around pp. 199–200.

### Sources of the image

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** The strongest specific source is the Sola Busca **Ten of Swords**: a bent figure and an unwieldy mass of long objects. Smith transformed weapons into living staffs and added a destination.

**[Documented intellectual source]** Waite’s word “oppression” repeats the Golden Dawn title. His additional fortune/gain/perfidy material incorporates older cartomantic lists, including the Mathers–Etteilla stream.

**[Open question]** No surviving design brief partitions responsibility between Waite and Smith. Waite may have specified the divinatory idea; Smith almost certainly determined much of the scene’s theatrical composition.

## Paul Foster Case and BOTA

**[Documented fact]** Paul Foster Case founded Builders of the Adytum; Jessie Burns Parke drew the BOTA cards. The trumps and courts closely revise Waite–Smith, but the numbered minors return to non-scenic arrangements of suit signs. Students traditionally color the line drawings themselves. See the [BOTA deck overview](https://waitesmith.org/index.php/books/b-o-t-a-tarot-decks-and-instruction/).

**Ten of Wands:** ten repeated wand symbols, without Smith’s laborer.

**[Esoteric attribution]** Case retained the broad Golden Dawn architecture: Wands/Fire, ten/Malkuth, and astrological decans. Case’s public works concentrate more heavily on the twenty-two Keys than on giving an independent narrative divination for every pip.

**[Not traced]** A primary Case passage giving a distinctive upright-and-reversed Ten of Wands list comparable to Waite’s was not verified. Lists attributed to Case online often reproduce Golden Dawn material without edition or page.

## Crowley–Harris Thoth Tarot, 1938–1943; published 1969

### What Harris painted

**[Documented fact]** Eight relatively thin, blue-violet wands form a constrained lattice. Two heavy foreground rods—commonly described as elongated dorjes or crushing bars—stand vertically across them. Jagged flames escape around the edges. Saturn and Sagittarius glyphs identify the decan. The title is **OPPRESSION**.

The design is geometric rather than anecdotal. There is no person; pressure is expressed structurally, as force pinned beneath heavier force.

### Crowley’s explanation

In *The Book of Thoth*, “The Small Cards,” Ten of Wands, Crowley writes:

> “The number Ten refers to Malkuth, which depends from the other nine Sephiroth, but is not directly in communication with them.”

He continues:

> “It shows the Force detached from its spiritual sources. It is become a blind Force…”

And:

> “The card also refers to the influence of Saturn in Sagittarius. Here is the greatest antipathy.”

His conclusion is:

> “The whole picture suggests Oppression and repression. It is a stupid and obstinate cruelty from which there is no escape.”

He describes a will consumed by its own “lust of result.” See the [*Book of Thoth* scan](https://www.rahoorkhuit.net/library/crowley/equinox/volume3/vol_3_no_05.pdf) and a searchable [card transcription](https://book-of-thoth.net/Ten-of-Wands-Oppression). Depending on issue, the passage falls in the small-card discussion around pp. 188–190.

**[Esoteric attribution]** Crowley intensifies rather than invents Book T’s title and decan. Harris turns the manuscript’s held crossed rods into a diagram of imprisonment and compression.

### Tzaddi/Heh does not apply here

**[Documented fact]** Crowley’s “Tzaddi is not the Star” revision rearranges the Emperor/Star letter-zodiac problem among trumps. It does not change the Ten of Wands, which remains Saturn in Sagittarius and Malkuth of Fire.

## Notable modern redesigns

### Rider–Waite–Smith descendants

**[Modern tradition]** Most modern scenic decks preserve one or more of:

- a bent carrier;
- ten bundled poles;
- obscured vision;
- a visible destination;
- overwork or excessive responsibility.

Some redesigns change the laborer’s gender, distribute the bundle among several people, or show the figure putting rods down. These changes commonly recast “oppression” as burnout, boundary-setting, emotional labor, or failure to delegate.

### Thoth descendants

**[Modern tradition]** The geometric family emphasizes suppression of vitality, authoritarian force, or energy trapped in matter. Later Thelemic decks frequently retain the title *Oppression* even when replacing Harris’s dorjes with bars, chains, machinery, or a constricted flame.

### Marseille revivals

**[Modern invention/tradition]** Contemporary Marseille readers often decline fixed scenic meanings and read the two vertical rods, eight crossed rods, symmetry, and vegetation relationally. Ten may mean fullness, saturation, completion, or passage to a new level. This practice is legitimate as a modern method but cannot be attributed to Noblet or Conver without evidence.

## The game

## Status in the pack

**[Documented fact]** In the normal seventy-eight-card tarot pack, the Ten of Batons is one of forty pip cards:

- four suits;
- Ace through Ten in each;
- four court cards per suit;
- twenty-one permanent trumps;
- the Fool.

It is not itself a trump and possesses no occult privilege in game play.

## Rank

In the older Italian and French systems:

- **Long suits—Swords and Batons:** King, Queen, Knight, Jack, 10, 9, 8 … Ace.
- **Round suits—Cups and Coins:** King, Queen, Knight, Jack, Ace, 2, 3 … 10, with details varying by game.

Thus the Ten of Batons is normally the highest numeral card in its suit, immediately below the lowest court.

The seventeenth-century rules associated with the Abbé de Marolles give Batons and Swords in descending order K–Q–Cavalier–Jack–10–9 and so forth. See [Marolles tarot rules](https://www.pagat.com/tarot/marolles.html).

## Trick-taking value

**[Documented fact]** The card wins a trick led in Batons against lower Batons unless a higher court or a trump is played, subject to the game’s follow-suit and trumping rules.

**Point value:** in many historical games it is an “empty” or low-value card for counting, despite its high trick-taking rank. Tarot point systems usually privilege kings, other courts, and special trumps/Fool. Rules vary; there is no universal “Ten of Wands is worth ten points.”

## Minchiate

The Ten of Batons is top numeral in its long suit but ordinarily has no independent point value. Captured combinations and special trumps matter more.

## Bolognese tarocchino

The removal of low pips leaves the Ten among the retained long-suit numeral cards. It can be strategically powerful but is not one of the pack’s highest scoring cards merely because it bears ten signs.

## Sicilian tarot

The Ten remains above the lower retained Batons. The Sicilian court order and pack composition differ, but the card is still an ordinary suit card rather than a tarocco.

## French Tarot

Modern French Tarot uses French suits rather than Batons: the analogous card is the Ten of Clubs, not a pictorial Ten of Wands. Tens are worth one-half counting point under the common half-point method; the card has no special contract status.

## Central-European Tarock

Many Austrian and central-European Tarock packs are shortened. Black suits often retain 10 downward while red suits retain Ace upward. A Ten of Clubs or Spades may therefore survive as a high plain-suit card, but “Ten of Wands” is not its usual printed name.

## Trump-order disputes: relevant context, not card identity

**[Documented/scholarly reconstruction]** Dummett grouped early Italian trump orders into three broad regional families, commonly called A, B and C:

- A: Bologna and related regions;
- B: Ferrara/Venice;
- C: Milan and the lineage ultimately associated with Marseille.

The relative order of Justice, Fortitude/Strength, the virtues, papal/imperial figures and celestial trumps varies.

**The Fool:** usually unnumbered historically and functions as an Excuse rather than an ordinary high trump in many games. The later occult number 0 is a convention, not the universal early-game number.

**Justice/Strength:** Waite–Smith follows the Golden Dawn by numbering Strength VIII and Justice XI; Marseille normally has Justice VIII and Force XI.

**No consequence for the Ten:** none of these disagreements changes the Ten of Batons’ suit position.

## Chronology of attestation

### Before standard tarot

- **Late fourteenth century — [Documented fact]:** Latin-suited playing cards with swords, cups, coins and batons circulate in Europe.
- **Approximately 1418–1425 — [Documented fact with disputed relevance]:** Marziano da Tortona devises for Filippo Maria Visconti a special game with sixteen Greco-Roman gods. It is an ancestor or close relative of trump-taking games but does not contain a standard Ten of Wands identifiable with the later tarot card.
- **1440 — [Documented fact]:** Giusto Giusti records having a hand-painted *naibi a trionfi* pack made in Florence for Sigismondo Pandolfo Malatesta. This now predates the once-standard 1442 “first tarot” date.
- **28 July 1442 — [Documented fact]:** the Ferrara court pays Marchionne Burdochi for “uno paro de carte da trionfi” for Ercole and Sigismondo d’Este. The text is reproduced by [Le Tarot Associazione culturale](https://letarot.it/Le-origini-italiane-del-Ludus-Triumphorum_pag_pg439_ita.aspx).
- **Mid-fifteenth century — [Documented fact]:** Visconti-family luxury packs establish the later four-suit-plus-trumps structure. The surviving Visconti–Sforza Ten of Staves is the earliest identifiable object in this dossier.
- **Approximately 1450–1470 — [Documented fact]:** the anonymous *Sermones de ludo cum aliis* condemns the game and supplies the earliest full surviving standard-trump list. It does not name individual pips because “Ten of Batons” required no iconographic explanation.
- **Late fifteenth century — [Documented fact]:** Boiardo composes verses for a nonstandard moralized tarot; poetic meanings are assigned to his redesigned cards rather than proving a universal traditional meaning.
- **1491/c.1500 — [Documented fact]:** Sola Busca engraved and later colored; scenic numbered cards appear throughout a complete tarot.

### Principal conflicts

1. **Etteilla versus Mathers:** Etteilla-family packs put treachery prominently upright; Mathers moves confidence and good faith upright and treachery to the reversal.

2. **Mathers’s public book versus Book T:** Mathers’s 1888 public list is favorable upright, whereas the Order manuscript titles the card *Oppression*. Waite combines both streams.

3. **Marseille versus Golden Dawn:** historical Marseille cards have no documented Saturn glyph, element label, Sephirah, or title.

4. **Papus versus Golden Dawn:** both employ YHVH, but their card sequencing and court/suit mechanics are not identical.

5. **Modern “completion” versus old cartomancy:** completion is a numerological inference; treachery and obstacle are the earlier printed fortune-telling keywords.

6. **Thoth versus modern therapeutic reading:** Crowley emphasizes cruel blind force; modern readers commonly soften this to overwork and the need to delegate.

## Divinatory meanings by author

## Etteilla

**Editional caution:** there are several Grand Etteilla families. Card numbering and orientation can shift.

**Upright, documented on a card/manual:**

> “TRAHISON” — treachery.

Expanded Etteilla-family lists include betrayal, lying, concealment, hypocrisy, conspiracy and duplicity.

**Reversed:**

> “OBSTACLE.”

Some later decks or summaries interchange or supplement these fields. The direct card image is visible in [*Le grand Etteilla*](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/2/2f/Le_grand_Etteilla%2C_ou%2C_l%27art_de_tirer_les_cartes_..._Le_tout_recueilli_..._%28IA_b29321220%29.pdf).

## S. L. MacGregor Mathers, 1888

In *The Tarot: Its Occult Signification, Use in Fortune-Telling, and Method of Play*, p. 26 in one modern scan’s internal pagination:

> “Ten of Sceptres.—Confidence, Security, Honour, Good Faith.”

Reversed:

> “Treachery, Subterfuge, Duplicity, Bar.”

See the [1888 text](https://hermetics.net/media-library/tarot/samuel-liddell-macgregor-mathers-the-tarot/) or [scanned edition](https://www.howardnema.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/12/the-tarot-1888-by-macgregor-mathers-1.pdf).

**Historical importance:** Waite’s apparently incompatible meanings—oppression and success, honesty set aside but perfidy retained—make sense when read against Mathers and Etteilla.

## Golden Dawn Book T

**Well dignified:**

> “Generosity, disinterestedness and self-sacrifice.”

**Ill dignified:**

> “Cruel and overbearing force,” failure through overpowering opposition, ill will, lying, malice, slander, envy and obstinacy.

Book T does not use the later simplified upright/reversed opposition; dignity depends on neighboring cards and elemental relations.

## A. E. Waite, 1910/1911

**Upright:**

> “The chief meaning is oppression simply, but it is also fortune, gain, any kind of success, and then it is the oppression of these things.”

Also false seeming, disguise, perfidy, danger to the destination, and particular failure if combined with specified cards.

**Reversed:**

> “Contrarieties, difficulties, intrigues, and their analogies.”

Waite explicitly says that the inherited readings “cannot be harmonized.” That admission is evidence of compilation, not necessarily carelessness.

## Aleister Crowley, 1944

Crowley did not normally reduce Thoth cards to fixed upright/reversed lists.

**Core:**

> “Force detached from its spiritual sources.”

> “Fire in its most destructive aspect.”

> “Oppression and repression.”

The Book T list survives beneath the philosophical interpretation: cruelty, malice, revenge and injustice, with better manifestations possible under favorable dignity.

**Reversed:** no authoritative, separate reversed meaning. Many Thoth practitioners do not reverse cards. Published “reversed Crowley meaning” lists are later inventions.

## Paul Foster Case

**[Not traced]** No verified Case passage was found giving a separate, distinctive upright/reversed list in his own words. Case adopted the Golden Dawn’s Qabalistic architecture but his BOTA deck deliberately makes the pips schematic.

The frequently repeated attribution “oppression, Saturn in Sagittarius” is consistent with Case’s inherited system but should not be presented as a newly coined Case interpretation without a primary citation.

## Rachel Pollack, 1980/1983

In *Seventy-Eight Degrees of Wisdom*, Pollack interprets the card through Waite–Smith’s picture: achievement has produced responsibilities and burdens; the person may be carrying everything alone or refusing to relinquish control. The destination suggests that the work can be completed.

**Upright, close paraphrase:** burden of achievement; responsibilities accumulated through success; overextension; carrying the load alone.

**Reversed, close paraphrase:** release or breakdown; laying burdens down, or being overwhelmed by them.

**Quotation warning:** no sufficiently reliable open primary transcription of Pollack’s exact card paragraph was found. The above is paraphrase, not a purported verbatim quotation. A searchable scan consulted was [*Seventy-Eight Degrees of Wisdom*](https://legacy-eutopia.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/02/Seventy-Eight-Degrees-of-Wisdom.pdf).

## Mary K. Greer

Greer’s *Tarot for Your Self* and *The Complete Book of Tarot Reversals* turn the card toward self-assessment: identify burdens, determine which actually belong to the querent, and reconsider excessive responsibility.

Her reversal theory does not make “reversed” automatically good or bad. A reversal can indicate:

- blocked or internalized burden;
- an excessive pattern;
- putting the load down;
- refusing responsibility;
- collapse under an unsustainable load;
- projecting the work onto others.

Greer explains her general method in [“Tarot Reversals”](https://www.llewellyn.com/journal/article/337).

**Quotation warning:** a verified open text containing her exact Ten-of-Wands wording was not located, so these are identified as paraphrases.

## Esoteric and symbolic readings

## Qabalistic

### Lévi’s tenfold foundation

Lévi writes in *Dogme et Rituel* that if the four Aces, four Twos and so forth are grouped, one obtains:

> “ten packs of cards giving the hieroglyphic interpretation of the triangle of Divine Names on the scale of the denary…”

See [*Dogme et Rituel*, Part I](https://www.astrumargenteum.org/wp-content/uploads/2015/06/Rituel-et-Dogme-de-la-Haute-Magie-by-Eliphas-Levi-Part_I.pdf).

**[Esoteric attribution]** This is the essential bridge from ten pips in each suit to the ten Sephiroth. Lévi did not yet give the fully elaborated Golden Dawn Ten-of-Wands decan.

### Malkuth

In Golden Dawn and Thoth doctrine:

- Kether/1 is the root or source.
- Malkuth/10 is manifestation, density and completion.
- Wands/Fire is will, force and initiation.
- Therefore the Ten is Fire at maximum materialization.

**Book T reading:** materialized force becomes overbearing.

**Crowley’s development:** Malkuth is “not directly in communication” with the other Sephiroth, so the force becomes blind and detached from its spiritual source.

**Historical caution:** “Malkuth is oppressive” is not a general Jewish Kabbalistic doctrine. It is a Hermetic-Qabalistic interpretation applied to this card.

## Tetragrammaton

**[Esoteric attribution]** Golden Dawn-derived systems associate:

- Wands = Yod;
- Cups = first Heh;
- Swords = Vav;
- Pentacles = final Heh.

Yod is fiery, initiating and paternal. The Ten of Wands is consequently **Malkuth of Yod**.

Papus also maps Sceptres to Yod, but his cyclical analysis of numbers and courts is not simply interchangeable with the Golden Dawn’s four-world/sephirotic system. His statement that “the wands or sceptres” correspond to clubs and that the colors reproduce Yod-He-Vau-He appears in [*The Tarot of the Bohemians*](https://benebellwen.com/wp-content/uploads/2013/02/papus-the-tarot-of-the-bohemians-1892.pdf).

## Astrological

**[Esoteric attribution]** The Golden Dawn assigns the card to Saturn in the final ten degrees of Sagittarius.

Symbolic rationale, made explicit by Crowley:

- Sagittarius: swift, aspiring, spiritual, expansive, fiery.
- Saturn: slow, heavy, restrictive, cold, limiting.
- Their “antipathy” produces blocked fire, constraint and oppressive effort.

The decan is approximately the Sun’s annual passage through 20°–30° Sagittarius. Calendar dates vary slightly by year and zodiac convention; fixed popular dates such as December 12–21 are approximate.

**Historical caution:** this is not known from Renaissance tarot. It is a nineteenth-century occult synthesis using decanic astrology.

## Hermetic

**[Tradition/esoteric attribution]** The card shows will that has reached manifestation without maintaining relation to its source. In constructive form, disciplined will completes a difficult labor. In destructive form, will becomes compulsion, bureaucracy, domination, or “lust of result.”

Crowley and Harris made this reading visual: two crushing rods impose rigidity upon eight active rods while fire escapes laterally.

## Alchemical

**[Modern esoteric extension]** No early alchemical text was found assigning a Ten of Batons to a named operation.

Later alchemical readings interpret:

- the rods as combustible matter;
- the escaping flames as uncontrolled *calcinatio*;
- Saturn as lead, heaviness or fixation;
- Sagittarius as volatile fire;
- the card as excessive fixation smothering or consuming the volatile principle.

This is a defensible symbolic overlay on Crowley’s card, but it should not be called a fifteenth-century alchemical key.

## Christian

**[Traditional/modern interpretation]** Waite–Smith’s burden-bearer evokes Christian images of carrying one’s cross, bearing another’s burdens, laboring toward a city, and the moral ambiguity of self-sacrifice.

Possible biblical parallels include Matthew 11:28–30, Galatians 6:2, and the Passion.

**Evidence limitation:** neither Waite nor Smith identifies the man as Christ, Simon of Cyrene, or a biblical character. The scene lacks a cross. A direct Christian identification is interpretive, not documented.

## Jungian

**[Modern invention]** Jung did not publish a card-by-card tarot psychology. “Jungian Ten of Wands” is post-Jungian tarot.

Typical readings include:

- overidentification of ego with duty;
- a persona built around competence or martyrdom;
- repressed resentment in the shadow;
- libido bound into compulsive achievement;
- the burden of an unintegrated complex;
- individuation requiring the surrender of tasks or identities that no longer belong to the self.

These readings became common through twentieth-century psychological tarot writers, especially Pollack, Greer and their successors, rather than through Jung himself.

## People and works

## Artists, printers and occultists

- **Bonifacio Bembo / Francesco Zavattari / Milanese workshop:** competing attributions around the Visconti–Sforza group and its Ten of Staves.
- **Anonymous Sola Busca engraver and colorist:** created the earliest surviving scenic pip system; the Ten of Swords probably supplied Smith’s burden composition.
- **Jean Noblet, Jean Dodal, Nicolas Conver:** major transmitters of the French ornamental Ten of Batons.
- **Jean-Baptiste Alliette/Etteilla:** gave the card its first securely printed cartomantic identity: treachery/obstacle.
- **S. L. MacGregor Mathers:** published an inherited meaning list and participated in the Golden Dawn system that retitled it Lord of Oppression.
- **Arthur Edward Waite:** integrated Book T “oppression” with Etteilla/Mathers meanings.
- **Pamela Colman Smith:** invented the card’s dominant modern narrative image.
- **Paul Foster Case and Jessie Burns Parke:** returned the BOTA pip to emblematic arrangement while retaining occult correspondences.
- **Aleister Crowley and Frieda Harris:** transformed oppression from a labor scene into a geometric, cosmological mechanism.
- **Alejandro Jodorowsky, Philippe Camoin and Marianne Costa:** revived Marseille visual numerology.

## T. S. Eliot, *The Waste Land* (1922)

**[Documented fact]** Eliot uses a fictional tarot reading by Madame Sosostris, naming cards including the drowned Phoenician Sailor, Belladonna, the man with three staves, the Wheel, the one-eyed merchant, and the absent Hanged Man.

**[Not documented]:** the Ten of Wands is not explicitly named.

**[Scholarly/critical interpretation]** Some critics compare the poem’s burdened Fisher-King or urban pilgrim with Waite–Smith’s Ten of Wands. A 1971 article argues that the bearer may reach his goal or be frustrated by treachery and relates him to the “unreal city.” This is a critic’s interpretive parallel, not an Eliot citation of the card. See the [Northern Illinois University article](https://www.niu.edu/clas/english/_pdf/towers-docs/Issue35-1971-January.pdf).

## W. B. Yeats

**[Documented fact]** Yeats was active in the Golden Dawn and used its symbolic world.

**[Not traced]** No poem or prose passage was found that securely singles out the Ten of Wands. General references to Yeats and “tarot symbolism” should not be inflated into card-specific evidence.

## Italo Calvino, *The Castle of Crossed Destinies*

**[Documented fact]** Calvino constructs narratives from laid-out tarot cards. The book’s sections use reproductions associated with the Visconti and Marseille traditions.

**[Not traced]** A uniquely decisive narrative role for the Ten of Batons was not verified in the editions consulted. It may occur as part of a spread, but no card-specific authorial commentary comparable to Waite’s survives.

## Alejandro Jodorowsky

**[Documented fact]** Jodorowsky’s tarot work is extensive, including the Camoin reconstruction and *The Way of Tarot* with Marianne Costa.

**Films:** his cinema contains initiatory, alchemical, Christian, bodily and tarot-adjacent symbolism.

**[Not traced]** No film shot was verified as a direct quotation of the Ten of Wands burden-bearer. General resemblance between a burdened figure and the card is insufficient.

## Ingmar Bergman

**[Documented fact]** Bergman’s films use medieval allegory, Death imagery, games, religious doubt and processional composition.

**[Not traced]** No reliable source connects a Bergman film, painting or stage design specifically to the Ten of Wands. *The Seventh Seal*’s chess game and personified Death are not tarot evidence.

## Paintings and buildings

**[Not traced]** No pre-1909 painting or building was found that scholarship generally recognizes as the direct source for Smith’s Ten of Wands. The walled destination is a generic English/European settlement motif.

Later artworks, murals, tattoos and commercial illustrations quote Smith’s figure extensively, but no canonical architectural work emerged from the source pass. Assertions that a particular town is Rye, Glastonbury, Canterbury or a Jamaican location remain unproved.

## Controversies and disputes

## Was the Waite–Smith card copied from Sola Busca?

**Finding:** partly, but the often-repeated wording is imprecise.

- **[Documented fact]** Sola Busca photographs were available at the British Museum before 1909.
- **[Documented fact]** Smith’s deck contains multiple visual correspondences with Sola Busca pips.
- **[Scholarly reconstruction]** Her Ten of Wands most closely resembles the Sola Busca **Ten of Swords**.
- **[Unsupported simplification]** “Smith copied the Sola Busca Ten of Wands” misidentifies the source card.
- **[Open question]** No surviving statement from Smith confirms her exact intention.

## Did “Oppression” originate with Crowley?

No.

- **[Documented fact]** “The Lord of Oppression” appears in Golden Dawn Book T decades before the Thoth deck.
- **[Documented fact]** Waite publicly used “oppression” in 1910/1911.
- **[Documented fact]** Crowley retained and philosophically intensified it.
- **[Modern misattribution]** Calling *Oppression* Crowley’s original title erases Book T.

## Egyptian origin

Court de Gébelin declared in *Le Monde primitif*, vol. VIII (1781), that tarot was a surviving Egyptian book. He proposed fanciful Egyptian etymologies for *tarot* and treated the game’s survival as evidence that sacred wisdom had been hidden in play.

- **Classification:** **[Legend/esoteric attribution]**
- **Evidence offered:** visual analogy, speculative etymology and a universalist theory of ancient religion.
- **Evidence absent:** Egyptian cards, Egyptian texts describing tarot, a transmission chain, or readable hieroglyphic corroboration.
- **Historical problem:** Egyptian hieroglyphs had not yet been deciphered.
- **Modern correction:** documented tarot appears in fifteenth-century northern Italy and develops from European/Mediterranean playing-card practice.

The argument and its later reception are surveyed in Decker, Depaulis and Dummett’s *A Wicked Pack of Cards*; see also the [Le Tarot essay on Court de Gébelin](https://www.letarot.it/Antoine-Court-de-Gebelin_pag_pg192_ita.aspx?lng=ENG).

Nothing Egyptian is specifically attested for the Ten of Batons before Etteilla’s Egyptianizing program.

## “Book of Thoth”

**[Documented fact]** “Book of Thoth” is an occult title, not the name found in Renaissance card records.

**[Tradition]** De Mellet and Etteilla helped spread the claim; Crowley deliberately reused the name for his twentieth-century tarot treatise and deck.

The two uses must be separated:

1. alleged ancient Egyptian book embodied in tarot—legend;
2. Crowley’s modern book and deck title—documented publication.

## Romani or “Gypsy” origin

**[Legend]** Nineteenth-century writers increasingly claimed that Romani people brought tarot from Egypt or preserved its secret.

Court de Gébelin’s exact role in the Romani transmission story is sometimes overstated; later authors such as Jean-Alexandre Vaillant and Papus were major spreaders. Papus’s title *Tarot of the Bohemians* helped fix the association.

**Evidence against:** tarot is documented in Italy before any evidence of Romani custody or invention of the pack. Linguistic and historical research places Romani origins in India, not ancient Egypt.

**Card-specific conclusion:** there is no Romani Ten-of-Wands doctrine traceable before European occultists attributed one to “Gypsy tradition.”

## Cathar origin

**[Disputed claim]** Some twentieth-century writers interpret tarot’s dualities, ecclesiastical figures and northern-Italian geography as evidence of Cathar survival.

**Evidence offered:** thematic resemblance, Cathar presence in regions later associated with tarot, speculative readings of watermarks and symbolic concealment.

**Evidence lacking:** a Cathar tarot pack, Cathar text describing the cards, demonstrable secret succession, or card-specific explanation of the Ten of Batons.

The Ten is especially resistant to the claim: ten clubs are already explicable as an ordinary suit card. See an advocate’s extended argument in [*The Occult Tradition of the Tarot*](https://ibnarabisociety.org/wp-content/uploads/PDFs/El-Moor_Occult-tradition-2.pdf). Its existence documents the theory, not the theory’s truth.

## Templar origin

**[Legend/disputed claim]** Claims that imprisoned or fugitive Templars encoded doctrine in tarot arise long after the suppression of the Order in 1312.

No chain connects Templar workshops, documents or possessions to the earliest tarot packs. No distinctive Templar explanation of the Ten of Batons predates modern occult literature.

## Alchemical secret book

**[Disputed/esoteric claim]** Sola Busca’s furnaces, vessels, tools and enigmatic classical figures have encouraged alchemical readings. Some may illuminate individual motifs.

What is not established is that every card constitutes a fixed alchemical stage or that the Ten of Batons encoded a universally recognized operation. The deck’s iconographic program remains partly unresolved.

## “Minor Arcana” as an ancient term

**[Modern occult terminology]** Fifteenth-century players did not call the Ten a Minor Arcanum. The terminology *major/minor arcana* is associated with Jean-Baptiste Pitois, “Paul Christian,” in the nineteenth century. Earlier records speak of cards, suits and triumphs.

## Misprints and editorial changes

1. **Etteilla orientation changes:** later editions can reverse or replace upright/reversed captions. A keyword must be cited to a particular deck family.

2. **Wirth complete decks:** Wirth’s 1889 project had twenty-two trumps. Modern seventy-eight-card products under his name contain later extrapolations.

3. **Noblet restorations:** missing or damaged originals have been reconstructed. Modern floral detail is not automatically seventeenth-century evidence.

4. **Thoth publication date:** 1969 is the normal full-deck date, but earlier partial printings, exhibitions and book illustrations create inconsistent dates in secondary sources.

5. **Rider/Waite naming:** “Rider–Waite” credits the publisher and designer but omits the artist. “Waite–Smith” or “Rider–Waite–Smith” better reflects Pamela Colman Smith’s role.

6. **Etteilla-to-Waite drift:** treachery migrated between upright, reversed and secondary meanings, while Golden Dawn “oppression” became primary. Modern lists often flatten these distinct strata.

---

## Open questions

1. **Exact dating of the Visconti–Sforza Ten of Staves.** Workshop attribution and chronology remain contested.

2. **Smith’s working process.** No surviving original drawings or detailed card-by-card correspondence proves which details came from Waite and which from Smith.

3. **Sola Busca dependence.** Visual correspondence with its Ten of Swords is strong, but direct documentary confirmation is absent.

4. **Etteilla edition genealogy.** A full stemma comparing all surviving Grand Etteilla Ten-of-Batons cards, their numbers, images, and upright/reversed captions remains desirable.

5. **Early Italian cartomantic meanings.** Later Bolognese manuscripts may preserve older oral readings, but dating a meaning’s oral origin is difficult.

6. **Marseille floral species.** Historical printers used stylized vegetation. Confident botanical or alchemical identifications generally outrun the evidence.

7. **Case’s exact pip wording.** A primary, accessible BOTA/Case passage specifically defining both orientations of this card was not found.

8. **Pollack and Greer verbatim text.** Copyrighted editions were not quoted without a verified accessible passage; their meanings above are marked as paraphrase.

9. **Literary citations.** Eliot and Calvino used tarot, but the Ten of Wands’ alleged special importance is substantially interpretive. Card-specific claims about Yeats, Jodorowsky’s films and Bergman remain untraced.

10. **The scenic card’s ultimate mundane model.** Smith may have combined Sola Busca with familiar images of harvesting, fuel gathering, or carrying fascines. No single external print or painting has been securely identified.
