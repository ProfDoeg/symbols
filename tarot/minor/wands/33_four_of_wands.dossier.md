# Four of Wands: Research Dossier

## Method and evidentiary key

This dossier distinguishes six kinds of statement:

- **[Documented fact]** Supported by a surviving card, archival record, contemporary printed text, manuscript, or securely catalogued museum object.
- **[Scholarly reconstruction]** A conclusion argued from material evidence by historians such as Michael Dummett, Thierry Depaulis, Ronald Decker, Stuart Kaplan, Robert M. Place, or Helen Farley, but not stated by a contemporary witness.
- **[Tradition]** A durable interpretation transmitted by cardmakers, cartomancers, or occult schools.
- **[Esoteric attribution]** A symbolic correspondence deliberately imposed by an occult author or organization.
- **[Disputed claim]** A proposal for which evidence or scholarly agreement is insufficient.
- **[Legend/modern invention]** A retrospective origin story or interpretation for which no historical evidence has been located.

A central correction is necessary at the outset: the Four of Wands is a **pip or numeral card**, not a trump. Consequently, many matters specified in the general template—Papess/Juno, Pope/Jupiter, Maison Dieu, trump orders, the Tzaddi controversy—concern the pack surrounding it rather than this card individually. They are included where they clarify the card’s historical setting, but they are not intrinsic correspondences of the Four of Wands.

### What the name originally meant

**[Documented fact]** Early Italian cards do not call the suit “Wands” in the modern magical sense. The suit-sign is a stick, staff, cudgel, club, or baton: Italian *bastone/bastoni* and Spanish *basto/bastos*. *Bastoni* remains the normal Italian name.

**[Tradition]** French Latin-suit tarots use *Bâtons*. Older English writers variously translated this as “Batons,” “Staves,” “Sceptres,” or “Rods.” S. L. MacGregor Mathers used **Sceptres** in *The Tarot: Its Occult Signification, Use in Fortune-Telling, and Method of Play* (1888); the Golden Dawn manuscript tradition settled upon **Wands**.

**[Esoteric attribution]** In the Golden Dawn system the ordinary club or staff was transfigured into the magician’s Wand, the implement of Will and Fire. Crowley retained **Wands**. This is an occult reinterpretation, not evidence that fifteenth-century players regarded the suit as magical.

**[Documented fact]** In Spanish- and Italian-suited playing cards, the equivalent suit persists as *bastos/bastoni*. In German-language occult tarot it is generally *Stäbe*; German tarock made for play often abandoned Italian suit-signs altogether in favor of French suits.

# Iconography deck by deck

## Before tarot: Mamluk and European suit systems

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** European Latin suits—swords, polo-sticks or clubs, cups, and coins—are closely related to the four suits of surviving Mamluk cards. In Europe, the polo-stick was interpreted as a baton or cudgel because polo was not a familiar European game. This ancestry concerns the suit-sign, not a Four of Wands scene or meaning.

No securely identified Mamluk “Four of Polo-sticks” has been shown to be the direct graphic model for the Visconti Four. The relationship is typological.

## Marziano da Tortona and Michelino da Besozzo, circa 1412–1425

**[Documented fact]** Marziano’s *Tractatus de deificatione sedecim heroum* describes a special pack made for Filippo Maria Visconti, with four bird suits and sixteen gods or heroes. Michelino da Besozzo painted it. The cards themselves are lost.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** It is reasonably described as a precursor to tarot because its special subjects outranked the ordinary suit cards. It was not a standard tarot pack and contained no documented suit of Bastoni. It therefore supplies no surviving Four of Wands.

## Visconti di Modrone / Cary-Yale, circa 1442–1447, Milan

The names **Visconti di Modrone** and **Cary-Yale** normally designate the same surviving group at Yale’s Beinecke Library.

**[Documented fact]** This unusually large luxury pack included female counterparts to the page and knight, producing six court cards per suit. Its numeral cards use heraldic suit-signs on decorated grounds.

**Finding:** I could not verify an extant Four of Staves in the surviving Cary-Yale group. Accordingly, it cannot safely be called the earliest surviving Four of Wands. Reproduction decks that supply missing cards are modern reconstructions and must not be mistaken for originals.

## Brambilla-Brera deck, circa 1440s, Milan

**[Documented fact]** This is another incomplete Visconti-associated luxury pack. Its surviving cards use Visconti emblems, patterned gold grounds, and largely non-scenic pips.

**Finding:** No securely catalogued surviving Brambilla Four of Staves was located. Modern completed “Brambilla decks” interpolate absent cards.

## Visconti-Sforza / Pierpont Morgan–Bergamo group, circa 1450s

**[Documented fact]** The Morgan Library identifies an original **Four of Staves** among its fifteenth-century cards. Four long, relatively straight staves are disposed symmetrically against an ornate gold ground. The card bears the Visconti motto **“A bon droyt”**, approximately “to good/rightful purpose” or “with good right.” The same motto occurs on the Ace of Staves. The Morgan attributes the cards probably to Bonifacio Bembo for the Visconti-Sforza family.

The ornament is heraldic and courtly, not narrative. There are:

- no celebrating people;
- no castle or town;
- no wedding canopy;
- no astrological glyphs;
- no written divinatory meaning.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** Its function was that of a luxury playing card. The motto may lend dynastic resonance, but there is no contemporary evidence that it encoded the later meaning “perfected work.”

This is the earliest securely verifiable surviving Four of Wands found in the consulted museum documentation. [Morgan exhibition label](https://www.themorgan.org/sites/default/files/pdf/press/MedievalLabels.pdf)

## Ferrarese and Este cards, fifteenth century

**[Documented fact]** Este court ledgers record the manufacture and purchase of *carte da trionfi*, including a 1442 entry. Other fragments associated with Ferrara survive.

**Finding:** No individual surviving Este Four of Batons could be securely identified. Statements that a particular modern-looking Four belongs to an “Este Tarot” often refer to a reproduction completed from multiple fragmentary sources.

## Mantegna “Tarocchi,” circa 1460s–1470s

**[Documented fact]** The so-called Mantegna Tarocchi are two series of fifty educational engravings representing social estates, liberal arts, cosmic principles, and celestial spheres. They are not a standard tarot pack and have no four suits.

**Conclusion:** There is no Four of Wands. The word *tarocchi* was attached later, and Mantegna’s authorship is rejected.

## Rosenwald sheets, late fifteenth or early sixteenth century

**[Documented fact]** The Rosenwald material consists of uncut or fragmentary printed sheets containing trumps, courts, and numeral suit cards. The baton numerals demonstrate the movement from hand-painted court cards to economical woodcut production.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** Their arrangement helps historians reconstruct Florentine/Bolognese-type packs and regional trump order. A Four of Batons, where restored from the sheet layout, is a conventional, non-scenic disposition of four suit-signs.

No divinatory text accompanies it.

## Budapest sheets, late fifteenth or early sixteenth century

These fragmentary printed cards are likewise important for reconstructing early popular tarot patterns.

**Finding:** The surviving material does not furnish a securely individualized narrative Four of Batons or an occult meaning. Assertions that the Budapest sheets encode alchemy are interpretive rather than documentary.

## Sola Busca, conventionally 1491; painted copy in Venice, late fifteenth century

**[Documented fact]** The Sola Busca is the earliest surviving complete 78-card tarot and the earliest complete pack whose numeral cards are individually illustrated. The engravings are normally dated 1491; one surviving set was later elaborately colored. The complete painted set is in the Pinacoteca di Brera.

The Four of Batons is not the Waite–Smith celebration scene. It presents a laboring or martial human figure interacting with four heavy baton-like objects in a constructed outdoor setting. The entire Sola Busca suit turns simple suit-signs into implements handled, carried, erected, or struggled with by human beings.

**[Esoteric/disputed]** Brera’s exhibition material interprets the baton suit as containing alchemical and agricultural motifs, linking cultivation to the *opus alchemicum*. That is a curatorial iconographic argument, not a surviving fifteenth-century explanatory key. [Brera, *The Secret of the Tarot*](https://pinacotecabrera.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/11/Il-Segreto-dei-tarocchi-eng.pdf)

### Did the Sola Busca Four directly supply Smith’s Four of Wands?

**Finding: not demonstrated.**

**[Documented fact]** Photographs of the complete Sola Busca pack entered the British Museum in 1907, shortly before Smith drew her deck. Smith could plausibly have seen them.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** Several Waite–Smith cards have striking Sola Busca analogues, especially the Three of Swords and the burdened figure that Smith adapted for the Ten of Wands from the Sola Busca Ten of Swords. This makes general influence very plausible.

**[Disputed claim]** No comparably close formal match links the two **Fours of Wands**. The claim “the Sola Busca Four of Wands was the source of Smith’s scene” exceeds the evidence. Smith’s Four is closer in structure to:

1. the Golden Dawn meaning “rest after labour” and “mirth”;
2. Etteilla/Mathers meanings of society, union, concord, and prosperity;
3. the appearance of four upright supports as an arbor or festival gateway;
4. Waite’s explicit “harvest-home” description.

The Sola Busca may have authorized Smith’s general decision to illustrate every pip, but its Four is not a demonstrated scene-by-scene source.

## Minchiate, Florence, sixteenth–nineteenth centuries

**[Documented fact]** Minchiate expanded the trump series to forty but retained four Latin suits and ordinary numeral cards. The Four of Batons is a patterned pip: four batons, normally crossed or symmetrically arranged, with foliage or ribbons depending on edition.

In play it remained a low-value suit card. The expanded trump cosmology does not give the Four an individual astrological identity.

## Bolognese tarot

The shortened Bolognese pack used for *tarocchino* eventually removed lower numeral cards. The surviving 62-card form ordinarily retains cards from 6 upward in the plain suits.

**Conclusion:** The Four of Batons is absent from the reduced modern Bolognese pack. An older full pack may originally have contained it, but one must distinguish that reconstruction from the later game.

## Sicilian tarot

**[Documented fact]** The Sicilian pack was reduced to 63 cards. The Four survives only in some suits/configurations and functions as the lowest card where present. In modern descriptions of Sicilian Tarocchi, the suit order descends from courts and ten toward four. It has no exceptional point value. [Rules of Sicilian Tarocchi](https://www.pagat.com/tarot/sicilian.html)

## Tarot de Marseille lineage

### Jean Noblet, Paris, circa 1650–1680

**[Documented fact]** The BnF’s Noblet pack is the earliest substantially surviving pack conventionally classed as Tarot de Marseille. It has 73 of 78 cards; its missing cards are in Swords, so the Baton suit survives.

The Four of Batons shows two pairs of long, crossing batons, interlaced in a regular lattice. Leaves or small floral forms grow from the center and spaces between them. It is an ornamental pip, not a scene. [BnF Noblet catalogue](https://essentiels.bnf.fr/fr/image/4799f2d6-6f88-4f6f-a15c-40a1a943c95e-tarot-jean-noblet)

### Jean Dodal, Lyon, circa 1701–1715

Four interlaced batons create a stable symmetrical field. Floral ornament emerges about the central crossing. The image preserves the practical need to distinguish four suit-signs while turning the empty spaces into a decorative garden.

### Nicolas Conver, Marseille, 1760/1761

Conver regularizes the Type II Marseille pattern. Four batons cross diagonally in two opposed pairs. A central floral or foliate unit and lateral leaves soften the geometry.

**[Tradition]** Later Marseille readers see growth, circulation, or organization in this vegetation.

**[Documented fact]** No surviving statement by Conver assigns those meanings. The foliage was first a cardmaker’s ornament and suit differentiator.

### Grimaud/Paul Marteau, 1930s

Grimaud’s *Ancien Tarot de Marseille*, associated with Paul Marteau, standardized a strong limited palette and became enormously influential. Marteau gave occult-numerological explanations to colors, leaves, and crossings.

**[Esoteric attribution]** Marteau treated Four as material stabilization and the foliage as organized vital energy. These meanings belong to his twentieth-century synthesis, not demonstrably to Noblet, Dodal, or Conver.

### Camoin–Jodorowsky, 1997

Philippe Camoin and Alejandro Jodorowsky produced a “restored” Marseille tarot by comparing several historic packs and supplying details according to their theory of a hidden unified code.

**[Documented fact]** The resulting Four of Batons remains an ornamental crossed-baton pip with colored leaves and flowers.

**[Disputed claim]** Calling it the recovery of one pristine original deck is controversial. It is a modern editorial synthesis, not a facsimile of a single historical exemplar.

**[Esoteric attribution]** Jodorowsky and Marianne Costa read Four as security, order, and a danger of rigidity; in Wands it can mean the stable organization of desire or creative energy.

## Etteilla and Grand Etteilla, Paris, 1780s–1789

**[Documented fact]** Jean-Baptiste Alliette, “Etteilla,” developed the first systematic published tarot cartomancy in the 1780s and issued a purpose-built occult deck usually dated 1789. His pack reorganized and renumbered tarot under a supposed Egyptian creation narrative.

The suit-card corresponding to the Four of Batons retains suit-signs but acquires printed or keyed divinatory senses. It does not show the Waite–Smith festival.

**[Esoteric attribution]** The Four’s governing idea is social aggregation: *société, association, union, assemblée,* alliance, convention, or compact. Reversal moves toward prosperity or fortunate results in later Etteilla-derived lists.

Etteilla’s elaborate elemental and astrological scheme was not identical to the later Golden Dawn assignment of Venus in Aries. Claims that Etteilla originated that particular decan correspondence could not be substantiated.

## Oswald Wirth, 1889

Wirth’s original 1889 deck comprised twenty-two trumps designed under Stanislas de Guaita’s influence.

**Conclusion:** It has no Four of Wands. Later 78-card products sold as “Wirth tarot” contain modern publishers’ additions. Wirth’s writings helped popularize occult tarot, but he did not provide a canonical 1889 Four.

## Golden Dawn *Book T*, circa 1888–1900 manuscript tradition

The Golden Dawn design is fully specified in *Book T*:

> “TWO White Radiating Angelic Hands … clasped in the centre with the grip of the First Order, holding four wands or torches crossed. Flames issue from the point of junction.”

Small flaming wands bear Venus and Aries. The title is **“The Lord of Perfected Work.”** The attribution is **Chesed of Yod**. The text glosses this as “Settlement, arrangement, completion.” [*Book T*, pp. 41–42 of the linked transcription](https://benebellwen.com/wp-content/uploads/2013/02/mathers-and-felkin-golden-dawn-book-t-the-tarot-1888.pdf)

### Wang and Cicero reconstructions

**[Modern reconstruction]** Robert Wang’s *Golden Dawn Tarot* and the Ciceros’ *Magical Tarot of the Golden Dawn* translate the manuscript instructions into finished cards. They should not be described as antique Golden Dawn decks. Both present crossed, flaming wands, divine hands, and astrological/Qabalistic color symbolism; their stylistic details differ because the surviving source is verbal and diagrammatic.

## Waite–Smith, London, 1909

Pamela Colman Smith drew four tall green staves planted in the foreground. A heavy garland of fruit, flowers, and greenery hangs between them like a festive threshold. Beyond it, two white-clad figures lift bouquets. Other celebrants stand nearer a fortified manor or town reached by a bridge.

Waite describes:

> “From the four great staves planted in the foreground there is a great garland suspended; two female figures uplift nosegays…”

He calls the setting a “domestic harvest-home.” [Waite, *The Pictorial Key to the Tarot*](https://www.integralpsychology.org/uploads/1/5/3/0/15300482/waite_tarot.pdf)

### Sources and transformations

**[Documented fact]** Smith converted a non-scenic pip into an intelligible human event.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** The scene visualizes the Golden Dawn text’s “completion,” “rest after labour,” “beauty,” “mirth,” and “success,” combined with Mathers’s “society, union, association, concord, harmony.”

**[Disputed]** The four posts are often called a Jewish **chuppah**. They can certainly resemble a canopy or wedding gateway, and Smith had Jewish theatrical and artistic acquaintances. But Waite calls the event a harvest-home, not a wedding, and the garland is suspended across the foreground rather than clearly forming a cloth-covered four-post canopy. “It is a chuppah” is therefore a modern interpretive possibility, not an authorially documented identification.

**[Disputed]** The figures’ genders are not entirely clear from the drawing. Waite says “two female figures.” Describing them definitively as bride and groom contradicts his text.

## Paul Foster Case and BOTA, 1920s–1930s

Jessie Burns Parke’s BOTA minor cards largely preserve the Waite–Smith pictorial vocabulary while making Case’s occult instruction explicit through prescribed coloring.

Case retained:

- Wands = Fire/Yod;
- Four = Chesed;
- Venus in the last decan of Aries;
- keyword **Perfected Work**.

**[Esoteric attribution]** Case connected Four with measurement, order, square form, and the stabilizing or beneficent force of Chesed. His divinatory method normally used elemental dignity rather than mechanically reversing every meaning.

A secondary source assigning the card to **Jupiter** appears to conflate Chesed’s planetary correspondence, Jupiter, with the card’s actual decan ruler, Venus. In Case’s Golden Dawn-derived structure:

- the **sephirah** Chesed corresponds to Jupiter;
- the **decan** corresponds to Venus in Aries.

These are simultaneous layers, not alternatives.

## Thoth Tarot, painted 1938–1943; first complete commercial publication 1969

Crowley titled the card **COMPLETION**. Frieda Harris painted four double-ended wands arranged as a closed wheel or square-within-circle.

Each wand terminates in:

- a ram’s head for Aries;
- a dove for Venus.

The ends meet a circle. Eight balanced flames play within it. Crowley writes that the circle shows “the completion and limitation of the original work.” He adds that Venus in Aries means one cannot establish work “without tact and gentleness.” [Crowley, *The Book of Thoth*, pp. 190–191](https://www.rahoorkhuit.net/library/crowley/equinox/volume3/vol_3_no_05.pdf)

The change from Smith is fundamental:

- Smith shows human celebration after work.
- Harris shows an impersonal energetic system whose circuit has closed.
- Crowley stresses law, government, manifested power, completion, and the latent disorder created by limitation.

## Notable modern redesigns

### Aquarian Tarot, David Palladini, 1970

**[Modern invention/tradition]** Streamlines the Waite–Smith celebratory architecture through Art Deco graphics. It transmits the RWS meaning rather than recovering an older pip form.

### Morgan-Greer Tarot, Bill Greer/Lloyd Morgan, 1979

Brings the garland and celebrants close to the viewer, intensifying emotional warmth and communal welcome.

### Robin Wood Tarot, 1991

Expands the RWS harvest festival into pagan or neopagan seasonal celebration.

### Wild Unknown, Kim Krans, 2012

Reduces the scene to four crossed wands and blossoms. This reunites Marseille-like abstraction with modern meanings of stability and joy.

### Modern “wedding” decks

Many contemporary decks explicitly draw a bride, groom, wedding arch, rainbow family, or homecoming party.

**[Modern invention]** These make literal a possibility latent in “union” and “harvest-home,” but neither the historic Marseille card nor Waite’s description specifically says “wedding.”

# The game

## Original function

**[Documented fact]** The Four of Batons originated as a playing card. Tarot was a trick-taking game in which a permanent trump suit was added to four ordinary suits.

In most Italian-suited tarot games:

- the Four of Batons is not a trump;
- it must follow suit when rules require;
- it wins only within its suit unless trumped;
- it normally carries no special card-point value;
- its strength depends on whether the suit ranks upward or downward in that game.

## Rank

In many historic tarot games the two “long” or dark suits—Swords and Batons—ranked:

> King, Queen, Knight, Page, 10, 9, 8 … Ace

while Cups and Coins ranked oppositely below the courts:

> King, Queen, Knight, Page, Ace, 2, 3 … 10.

**[Tradition/game rule]** Under that common Italian convention, the Four of Batons outranks the Three, Two, and Ace but loses to Five and above.

This rule is not universal. Modern French tarot uses French-suited cards, so there is no Four of Wands: its structural counterpart is the Four of Clubs. Austrian tarock likewise normally uses French suits.

## Point value

In most point-trick tarot systems numeral suit cards are **counting blanks** or low-value cards. Depending on the counting method, several low cards are grouped and offsets are subtracted; the Four has no individual honor value comparable with kings, trumps, or *oudlers*.

## The Fool

**[Documented fact]** The Fool’s gaming role belongs to the whole pack, not to the Four:

- In many continental games it is the **Excuse**, allowing a player to avoid following suit and normally recover the card after the trick.
- In some Italian games it is a special unnumbered card.
- In modern Central European tarock, the highest trump is often numbered XXII and the special role differs.

## Trump-order disputes

Dummett classified early Italian trump orders into three broad families:

- **A:** Bologna and Florence;
- **B:** Ferrara;
- **C:** Milan and the tradition ancestral to the Marseille order.

Justice and Fortitude/Strength moved relative to other subjects. The equal-ranking “popes” of Bologna and regional substitutions likewise affected trumps.

**Important:** none of these changes altered the Four of Batons’ identity. It remained a suit card. Waite’s later interchange of Strength and Justice is irrelevant to its rank.

## Minchiate

Minchiate added zodiacal signs, elements, virtues, and other trumps, producing a 97-card pack. The Four of Batons nevertheless remained an ordinary low suit card.

# Chronology of attestation

## Circa 1412–1425: Marziano’s game

**[Documented fact]** Marziano describes a special trick-taking pack for Filippo Maria Visconti. It is a proto-tarot comparison point, not an attestation of the Four of Wands.

## 1440: earliest securely documented *trionfi* language

**[Documented fact]** Florentine records now supply early references to *naibi a trionfi*. Older literature often called the 1442 Ferrara record the earliest.

## 1442: Ferrara ledger

**[Documented fact]** The Este account books record payment for packs of *carte da trionfi*. The entry proves the game’s presence but does not name its Four of Batons.

## Circa 1450s: Visconti-Sforza Four of Staves

**[Documented fact]** The earliest surviving physical card securely verified here.

## Circa 1470–1480: *Sermones de ludo cum aliis*

The anonymous preacher enumerates twenty-one trumps plus the Fool and condemns the game.

**[Documented fact]** This is crucial for trump history.

**Negative finding:** it does not individually list or interpret the Four of Batons. Its silence is expected because ordinary suits were already familiar.

## Fifteenth-century Este production records

Numerous accounts pay painters and cardmakers for triumph packs. They show production but rarely inventory individual pips.

## 1491: Sola Busca

**[Documented fact]** Earliest surviving complete illustrated tarot, including an illustrated Four of Batons.

## Circa 1500: Ferrarese tarot poems

Poems use tarot subjects to characterize courtly persons. They primarily concern the trumps.

**Negative finding:** no securely traced poem supplies a meaning for the Four of Batons comparable with later cartomancy.

## Teofilo Folengo, 1520s

Folengo’s *Caos del Triperuno* contains a celebrated literary use of tarot in fortune-telling-like poetic combinations.

**[Documented fact]** It demonstrates imaginative and literary card interpretation.

**Caution:** it does not prove a stable, universal divinatory lexicon for the Four of Batons.

## Sixteenth century: the name *tarocchi*

The earlier phrase *carte da trionfi* gradually yielded to *tarocchi*. The etymology of *tarocco* remains uncertain; proposed Egyptian derivations are later inventions.

## Seventeenth century: French *tarot* and *bâtons*

The standard French form is *quatre de bâtons*. “Tarot de Marseille” itself is a later classificatory name, generally credited to Romain Merlin in 1856, not the seventeenth-century cardmakers’ own historical style-label. [History of the name and pattern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tarot_of_Marseilles)

## 1780s: Etteilla

**[Documented fact]** First sustained published cartomantic system for the tarot. The Four gains an explicit lexical meaning centered upon society and association.

## 1788: Mathers

Mathers translates or adapts the French cartomantic stream:

> “Society, Union, Association, Concord, Harmony.”

This precedes and helps explain the communal content of the Waite–Smith picture.

## Circa 1888–1900: Golden Dawn

The Four is assigned to Venus in Aries, Chesed of Yod, and titled “Lord of Perfected Work.”

## 1909–1911: Waite–Smith and *Pictorial Key*

Smith creates the now-dominant festival scene; Waite publishes “harvest-home,” harmony, prosperity, and perfected work.

## 1930s: Case/BOTA and Marteau

Two different syntheses spread:

- Case develops Golden Dawn Qabalah and astrology.
- Marteau overlays Marseille ornament with occult numerology and color symbolism.

## 1938–1943 / 1969: Thoth

Crowley and Harris reconceive the card as a closed energetic circuit titled Completion.

## Late twentieth century onward

The RWS scene generates the modern keywords:

- celebration;
- homecoming;
- wedding;
- community;
- foundation;
- belonging;
- milestone.

“Wedding” becomes much more categorical online than in Waite’s text.

## The number four and Chesed

**[Esoteric attribution]** The Golden Dawn maps cards 2–10 to the corresponding sephiroth. Therefore every Four belongs to **Chesed**, the fourth sephirah. Chesed is associated with mercy, beneficence, organization, kingship, and Jupiter.

This creates two planetary layers:

- **Jupiter** as the planet of Chesed;
- **Venus** as ruler of the last decan of Aries.

Treating the card simply as “Jupiter in Aries” is an error produced by collapsing those layers.

## Yod and Atziluth

The four suits are assigned to the four letters of the divine name:

- Wands — Yod — Fire — Atziluth;
- Cups — first Heh — Water — Briah;
- Swords — Vav — Air — Yetzirah;
- Pentacles — final Heh — Earth — Assiah.

Thus “Chesed of Yod” or “Chesed in Atziluth” means the organizing fourth power appearing in the most fiery, archetypal world.

**[Esoteric attribution]** This is Golden Dawn metaphysics, not a historical feature of Renaissance Bastoni.

## Venus in Aries

The Golden Dawn allocates the 36 decans to cards 2–10. The Aries sequence is:

- 2 of Wands — Mars in Aries — Dominion;
- 3 of Wands — Sun in Aries — Established Strength;
- 4 of Wands — Venus in Aries — Perfected Work.

The Four covers approximately 20°–30° Aries, often translated into about 10–19 April, though exact calendar dates vary with year and astrological convention.

Venus is traditionally in detriment in Aries. Golden Dawn and Crowley resolve the tension as beauty, tact, or concord moderating assertive fire.

## Hebrew letters and paths

**Correction:** No single Hebrew letter or Tree-of-Life path belongs specifically to the Four of Wands in the Golden Dawn system. Hebrew letters and paths are principally assigned to the 22 trumps. The Four belongs to the **sephirah Chesed**, not to a path.

## Tzaddi/Heh controversy

Crowley’s “Tzaddi is not the Star” exchange reassigns **Heh to the Star** and **Tzaddi to the Emperor**, reversing the Golden Dawn’s Emperor/Aries/Heh and Star/Aquarius/Tzaddi pair.

**Conclusion:** This does not change the Four of Wands. It remains Venus in Aries and Chesed of Fire in both Golden Dawn and Thoth.

# Divinatory meanings by author

Quotations are kept brief where the works remain under copyright.

## Etteilla school, 1780s

**Upright:** *Société; association; assemblée; union; alliance; convention; pacte*—society, association, assembly, union, alliance, convention, compact.

**Reversed:** prosperity, success, happiness, or advantage in derivative lists.

**Textual caution:** Etteilla’s meanings circulated through several editions, tables, and followers. The very long synonym lists frequently presented online are often nineteenth- or twentieth-century expansions rather than verbatim text from a single 1780s printing.

## S. L. MacGregor Mathers, 1888

Mathers, card 32:

> “Society, Union, Association, Concord, Harmony.”

Reversed:

> “Prosperity, Success, Happiness, Advantage.”

[*The Tarot*, p. 14 in the scanned edition](https://www.howardnema.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/12/the-tarot-1888-by-macgregor-mathers-1.pdf)

This is plainly closer to Etteilla’s social lexicon than to medieval game usage.

## Golden Dawn, *Book T*

The card means:

> “Perfection or completion of a thing built up with trouble and labour. Rest after labour…”

It continues with subtlety, cleverness, beauty, mirth, successful completion, reasoning, and—when poorly dignified—unreadiness, anxiety, hurry, unreliability, or insincerity.

The Golden Dawn ordinarily speaks of good or bad **dignity**, not simple upright/reversed polarity.

## A. E. Waite, 1910/1911

Upright:

> “country life, haven of refuge, a species of domestic harvest-home, repose, concord, harmony, prosperity, peace…”

He concludes with “the perfected work of these.”

Reversed:

> “The meaning remains unaltered; it is prosperity, increase, felicity, beauty, embellishment.”

This is one of Waite’s unusually benign reversals. [*Pictorial Key*](https://www.integralpsychology.org/uploads/1/5/3/0/15300482/waite_tarot.pdf)

## Aleister Crowley, 1944

Crowley does not give a conventional upright/reversed pair. He identifies:

- order;
- law;
- government;
- manifested active power;
- completion and limitation.

The condition of completion “bears in itself the seeds of disorder.” That final qualification prevents the card from meaning an eternally static happy ending.

## Paul Foster Case, 1933

Case’s keyword is **Perfected Work**.

Well dignified: completion of work, rest, order, beauty, success, and effective arrangement.

Ill dignified: loss or failure through haste, unpreparedness, anxiety, or unstable action.

**Caution:** Published paraphrases sometimes label the card “ruled by Jupiter.” The decan ruler is Venus; Jupiter is the planetary correspondence of Chesed.

## Rachel Pollack

In *Seventy-Eight Degrees of Wisdom* Pollack develops the RWS festival as communal celebration, domestic happiness, freedom, and a stable space within which energy can move. Her emphasis is not merely “finished work,” but life enjoyed in shared human form.

Reversed, the celebratory or home energy may be incomplete, private, restricted, or unstable.

**Copyright note:** No extended verbatim passage is reproduced. Pollack’s precise discussion should be consulted in the edition listed below.

## Mary K. Greer

Greer’s work treats the card through active reading and personal inquiry: celebration, public recognition, household or community stability, rites of passage, and the question of what foundation supports joy.

Reversal can internalize the celebration, reveal household tension, or ask whether the apparent structure is genuinely secure.

Greer also helped popularize experiential and Jungian-style engagement: the reader enters the picture, identifies the threshold, and asks what is being celebrated.

# Esoteric and symbolic readings

## Alchemical

**First securely relevant visual field:** Sola Busca, late fifteenth century, though no explanatory manual survives.

**[Disputed scholarly/esoteric interpretation]** Modern Brera scholarship connects the Sola Busca Batons with agriculture and alchemical labor: cultivation becomes an analogue of the *opus*. This may illuminate the deck’s repeated furnaces, vessels, tools, and laboring figures.

**Caution:** It does not prove that the Sola Busca Four meant the Golden Dawn’s “Perfected Work,” nor that Smith copied that particular card.

Crowley later makes completion explicitly alchemical in structure: force has become an ordered closed system, after which decomposition or a new phase must follow.

## Kabbalistic

**First systematic attachment to this card:** the Golden Dawn, late nineteenth century.

The card is Chesed of Yod. Four stabilizes the initial fiery force. The danger is that stability becomes enclosure. This produces the characteristic double sense:

- benevolent organization;
- limitation and incipient rigidity.

Lévi and Papus made tarot/Qabalah correspondences culturally available, but no evidence was found that Lévi himself formulated “Four of Wands = Chesed of Yod.”

## Astrological

**First securely traced specific attribution:** Golden Dawn *Book T*.

Venus softens Aries without extinguishing it. Hence:

- festive beauty built upon action;
- diplomacy after conquest;
- concord among forceful participants;
- completion made socially acceptable.

Crowley is explicit that “tact and gentleness” are necessary to establish the work.

## Hermetic

The four crossed wands reconcile multiple levels:

- elemental Fire;
- Venus and Aries;
- the fourth sephirah;
- the fourfold divine name;
- the magical Wand as directed Will.

This compact multilayered structure is Hermetic-Golden Dawn synthesis, not an ancient property of the card.

## Christian

Historic Marseille floral ornament can be read as a garden, cross, or life growing from ordered matter.

**[Modern/traditional interpretation]** Christian tarotists sometimes relate the four supports to:

- the four Evangelists;
- the four corners of the world;
- the heavenly city;
- the altar canopy;
- fellowship or church community.

No fifteenth- or seventeenth-century source located here identifies the Four of Batons that way.

## Jungian

**[Modern interpretive tradition]** Jung wrote about symbols, mandalas, alchemy, and archetypes, but did not establish a canonical Four-of-Wands meaning.

Post-Jungian tarot interpreters treat:

- the four posts as a protected psychic temenos;
- the garland as a mandala-like boundary;
- the castle as an achieved ego structure or secure Self-image;
- the celebrating figures as integration with community;
- reversal as persona-level happiness masking instability.

Calling these “Jung’s meaning” is inaccurate. They are later applications of Jungian concepts to Smith’s picture.

# People and works

## Directly attached to the card

- **Bonifacio Bembo or workshop:** probable painter of the Visconti-Sforza Four of Staves.
- **Anonymous Sola Busca engraver, sometimes called the Master of the Sola Busca Tarocchi:** created the earliest surviving fully illustrated Four.
- **Jean Noblet, Jean Dodal, Nicolas Conver:** transmitted the crossed-baton floral pattern.
- **Jean-Baptiste Alliette/Etteilla:** first supplied a systematic cartomantic meaning.
- **S. L. MacGregor Mathers and Golden Dawn compilers:** transmitted “society” and created “Lord of Perfected Work.”
- **Pamela Colman Smith:** invented the canonical modern harvest-festival picture.
- **Arthur Edward Waite:** supplied its published description and meanings.
- **Jessie Burns Parke and Paul Foster Case:** transmitted the BOTA version.
- **Lady Frieda Harris and Aleister Crowley:** created the Thoth Completion design.

## Calvino

Italo Calvino’s *Il castello dei destini incrociati* (*The Castle of Crossed Destinies*, 1969/1973) constructs stories through arrangements of tarot cards, first using a Visconti-style pack and later Marseille imagery.

**Finding:** No unique, historically influential interpretation of the Four of Wands could be securely isolated from the work. Calvino is important to tarot’s literary reception, not specifically to this card’s established meaning.

## T. S. Eliot

*The Waste Land* uses “Madame Sosostris,” the “wicked pack of cards,” the “drowned Phoenician Sailor,” the Belladonna, the Wheel, and the one-eyed merchant.

**Negative finding:** Eliot does not name the Four of Wands. Linking the poem’s unreal city or vegetation ritual specifically to this card is a modern analogy.

## W. B. Yeats

Yeats belonged to the Golden Dawn and worked within its ritual-symbolic world.

**Negative finding:** No published poem or prose passage securely assigning a special role to the Four of Wands was located. General statements that it is “Yeats’s card of perfected work” should not be repeated without a citation.

## Jodorowsky

Jodorowsky used tarot throughout his teaching, writing, and artistic persona and collaborated with Philippe Camoin on a restored Marseille deck. His films contain abundant initiatory and tarot-like motifs.

**Negative finding:** No film was found to quote the Four of Batons as unmistakably as a direct visual citation. Claims that any four-pillared ceremonial shot is deliberately this card require production evidence.

## Ingmar Bergman

Bergman’s *The Seventh Seal* concerns Death, chess, medieval performance, and plague.

**Negative finding:** No documented Four-of-Wands quotation was found. Its frequent inclusion in broad “tarot in cinema” lists appears associative rather than evidentiary.

## Paintings and buildings

The RWS image has been compared to:

- flowered triumphal or festival arches;
- harvest-home decorations;
- castle or manor approaches;
- Jewish wedding canopies;
- Christian ciboria or baldachins.

**Finding:** No single painting or building has been established as Smith’s direct model. The architecture is generic enough that confident identifications are speculative.

# Controversies and disputes

## Egyptian origin

Court de Gébelin asserted in *Le Monde primitif* VIII (1781) that tarot preserved an Egyptian sacred book. The Comte de Mellet’s accompanying essay called it a Book of Thoth, supplied divinatory procedures, and tried to align the trumps with Hebrew letters.

**[Documented fact about a legend]** These claims appear in the 1781 text.

**[Legend as history]** Neither author produced Egyptian cards, hieroglyphic texts, or an archival transmission connecting Egypt to fifteenth-century Italy. Their proposed Egyptian etymologies predate Champollion’s decipherment and are linguistically spurious. Dummett specifically notes that their pseudo-Egyptian derivations cite no actual hieroglyphic forms. [Dummett, “Origins of Tarot”](https://www.nybooks.com/articles/1981/05/14/origins-of-tarot/)

For the Four of Wands, Egyptian origin has no evidentiary support. Ram and dove imagery appears only after the Golden Dawn attribution and Harris’s Thoth design.

## Romani or “Gypsy” transmission

Nineteenth-century writers increasingly claimed that Romani people carried tarot from Egypt. Jean-Alexandre Vaillant helped popularize the story; Papus’s *Tarot des Bohémiens* made it famous.

**[Legend]** The theory relies partly on the erroneous European belief that Romani people came from Egypt.

**[Documented correction]** Tarot is recorded in northern Italy before evidence connecting Romani cartomancy to tarot. Modern linguistic and genetic research locates Romani origins in South Asia, not ancient Egypt.

This myth should be recorded because it shaped occult tarot, but it is not a viable history of the Four of Wands.

## Cathar and Templar claims

Modern esoteric literature sometimes presents the four suits as relics of:

- Cathar dualism;
- Templar secrets;
- a Grail brotherhood;
- suppressed medieval heresy.

**[Legend/modern invention]** No medieval Cathar or Templar document mentions tarot, a Four of Batons, or a 78-card initiatory book. The Templars were suppressed more than a century before tarot’s documented appearance.

Visual resemblance between crossed batons and crosses does not establish institutional descent.

## “Tarot de Marseille” as an ancient name

**[Documented correction]** Seventeenth-century makers did not use “Tarot de Marseille” as the name of an immemorial esoteric canon. Romain Merlin is generally credited with the expression in 1856. Many early examples were made in Paris, Lyon, Dijon, Switzerland, and elsewhere.

## Sola Busca as Smith’s direct Four-of-Wands source

General Sola Busca influence on Smith is plausible. Specific dependence of her Four on the Sola Busca Four is not demonstrated.

The strongest evidence points instead toward Smith pictorializing the already established cartomantic concepts:

- society and union;
- concord and harmony;
- perfected work;
- rest and mirth after labor.

## The “chuppah”

Calling the foreground a chuppah is meaningful for contemporary readers but not certain historically.

Against certainty:

- Waite calls the two figures women;
- he says “harvest-home”;
- no cloth canopy is shown;
- no explicit Jewish ritual object is named.

For possibility:

- four uprights and suspended foliage can evoke a ceremonial canopy;
- later wedding interpretation fits older meanings of union.

Verdict: **plausible modern interpretation, not documented identification**.

## Misprints and conflations

### “Perfected Work” versus “Completion”

These are not competing originals:

- **Perfected Work** is the Golden Dawn title.
- **Completion** is Crowley’s shortened Thoth title.

### Jupiter versus Venus

“Jupiter in Aries” is usually a conflation:

- Chesed corresponds to Jupiter;
- the decan is Venus in Aries.

### 10° versus 20° boundaries

Some sources write 21°–30° Aries; others 20°–30°. The difference is conventional counting of degree boundaries, not a different attribution.

### 1969 versus 1944 for Thoth

- paintings: primarily 1938–1943;
- *Book of Thoth*: 1944;
- complete commercial deck: 1969.

Calling it simply “the 1969 design” obscures its production history.

### “First illustrated minor arcana”

The Sola Busca is the earliest surviving **complete** tarot with systematically illustrated pips. It is unsafe to claim that no earlier illustrated numeral card ever existed.

### Reproduction completions

Modern Visconti, Este, Wirth, and fragment-sheet decks routinely add cards that do not survive. Those additions are useful but are not primary artifacts.

# Open questions

1. **Exact dating of the Visconti-Sforza Four.** The 1450s attribution is strong, but individual cards may have been produced or replaced at different moments.

2. **Exact authorship.** Bonifacio Bembo and his workshop remain the leading attribution, but workshop participation complicates single-artist claims.

3. **Meaning of “A bon droyt.”** Its heraldic and dynastic function is clear; its precise intended application to the Four rather than the suit or patron remains uncertain.

4. **Sola Busca program.** The deck unquestionably has unusual classical, agricultural, and technical imagery. Whether every pip participates in one coherent alchemical code remains debated.

5. **Smith’s working process.** No surviving card-by-card notebook explains her sources. We can demonstrate opportunity to see Sola Busca photographs and identify visual parallels, but not reconstruct every decision.

6. **Identity of Smith’s figures.** Waite calls both female; modern viewers frequently see a mixed-sex couple. The drawing alone does not settle the matter.

7. **Festival type.** Harvest-home is Waite’s phrase. Wedding, homecoming, May Day, civic fête, and sukkah/chuppah interpretations are possible overlays but lack direct documentary confirmation.

8. **Etteilla’s exact first wording.** His material exists in several editions and derivative synonym tables. Establishing the earliest exact French entry requires collating physical copies of the relevant *Cahiers* and early Grand Etteilla manuals.

9. **Pollack and Greer edition variance.** Both authors revised or expanded their discussions across editions. Exact quotations should be tied to a specified printing.

10. **Earlier popular printed Fours.** The fragmentary Rosenwald, Budapest, and regional sheets can shift datings as new fragments or watermarks are identified.
