# Nine of Wands: Research Dossier

## Method and evidentiary labels

This dossier distinguishes:

- **Documented fact** — supported by a surviving card, archival record, contemporary publication, or authorial text.
- **Scholarly reconstruction** — an inference accepted or seriously argued by card historians but not stated in a surviving contemporary record.
- **Tradition** — a repeated convention whose original reasoning may be unknown.
- **Esoteric attribution** — an occult correspondence proposed within a named system.
- **Disputed claim** — a claim for which specialists offer competing accounts.
- **Legend** — a historically unsupported origin story inherited from occult literature.
- **Modern invention** — a recent interpretive development, often psychological or deck-specific.

Dates for handmade decks are necessarily approximate. “Tarot de Marseille” is used as a modern pattern-name; surviving seventeenth-century makers did not consistently market their cards under that fixed label.

### Important scope correction

**Documented fact:** Unlike a trump, the Nine of Wands has no individual Hebrew letter, Tree-of-Life path, or place in a trump sequence. Golden Dawn correspondences attach:

1. **Yesod** to every Nine;
2. **Fire/Yod** to the suit of Wands;
3. **Moon in Sagittarius** to this particular pip.

Consequently the Papess/Juno, Pope/Jupiter, Tower/Maison Dieu, Strength/Justice-order, and Fool-numbering controversies concern the trump series, not this card. They are included below only where they establish the historical environment of the pack.

# Iconography, deck by deck

## The fifteenth-century Milanese decks

### Visconti–Sforza, Milan, c. 1450–80

**Documented fact:** The Morgan Library preserves a card catalogued as “The Nine of Staves,” attributed to Bonifacio Bembo, Milan, c. 1450–80, 173 × 87 mm, MS M.630 no. 30. It is therefore the earliest securely catalogued surviving Nine examined for this dossier. [Morgan Library record](https://www.themorgan.org/collection/tarot-cards/the-nine-of-staves)

The card is an unscenicized luxury pip:

- nine elongated staves;
- regular, heraldic arrangement;
- gold-ground courtly decoration;
- no wounded defender, landscape, wall, narrative action, astrological glyph, or divinatory caption.

**Scholarly reconstruction:** The essential visual purpose was quick suit-and-rank recognition, embellished to suit a ducal commission. Reading the arrangement as “defense,” “resilience,” or “spiritual initiation” projects later cartomancy backward onto a gaming card.

### Cary–Yale / Visconti di Modrone, Milan, c. 1440s

Names in the literature overlap: “Cary–Yale” and “Visconti di Modrone” ordinarily denote the same surviving group.

**Documented fact:** The surviving pack is incomplete and structurally unusual, with additional female court cards. It belongs to the early Milanese luxury-card milieu.

**Finding:** I did not locate an authoritative museum catalogue establishing a surviving Nine of Batons in this group. Accordingly it cannot securely replace the Morgan Visconti–Sforza Nine as the earliest surviving example.

### Brambilla–Brera deck, Milan, c. 1440s

**Documented fact:** The Pinacoteca di Brera holds the surviving Brambilla group, traditionally associated with Francesco Sforza and attributed to Bonifacio Bembo’s milieu. Brera describes it as a group of 48 late-Gothic cards. [Brera exhibition notice](https://www.arte.it/calendario-arte/milano/mostra-il-segreto-dei-segreti-i-tarocchi-sola-busca-2100)

**Finding:** No securely catalogued Brambilla Nine of Batons was found in the accessible Brera material. General statements that “the Brambilla Nine” supplies the Nine of Wands archetype should therefore be treated as unsupported unless tied to a specific inventory number and image.

### Other Visconti-associated fragments

**Documented fact:** Numerous cards now divided among the Morgan Library, Beinecke Library, Accademia Carrara, and private or historical collections have been reorganized into named “decks” by later cataloguers.

**Open attribution problem:** The precise original pack boundaries, painters, dates, and patrons remain partly reconstructed. A surviving card’s present deck-name is not necessarily a contemporary title.

## Ferrarese and related material

### Este cards, c. 1470s–1500

**Documented fact:** Several hand-painted cards are assigned stylistically to Ferrara or the Este court. The groups are fragmentary.

**Finding:** No surviving Ferrarese luxury Nine was found that supplies a continuous iconographic link between the Milanese geometric pip and the later Sola Busca scene.

### Sola Busca, engraved 1491; colored c. 1500

**Documented fact:** The Sola Busca is the earliest completely surviving 78-card tarot and the earliest completely surviving tarot whose numeral suit cards carry individual figural scenes. The scholarly consensus summarized by Brera-related material dates the engraving to 1491, probably Ferrara, and the coloring of the complete luxury copy to about a decade later, probably Venice. [Brera exhibition account](https://www.arte.it/calendario-arte/milano/mostra-il-segreto-dei-segreti-i-tarocchi-sola-busca-2100), [Public Domain Review survey](https://publicdomainreview.org/collection/sola-busca)

Its Nine of Batons is:

- a single human figure in a Renaissance setting;
- associated with nine heavy, naturalistic implements rather than a purely abstract Marseille lattice;
- part of a suit-wide sequence of enigmatic actions involving labor, burden, construction, destruction, fire, armor, and implements.

**Scholarly reconstruction:** The deck probably encodes a humanist, historical, military, Hermetic, or alchemical program, but the precise narrative has not been recovered. The 2012 Brera exhibition explicitly foregrounded “Hermetic-alchemical culture”; that is an interpretive framework, not a surviving fifteenth-century instruction manual.

### Was Sola Busca the source of Pamela Colman Smith’s Nine?

**Disputed claim; lead checked:** Smith almost certainly knew photographs of the Sola Busca. The Busca-Serbelloni family supplied black-and-white photographs to the British Museum, where they were displayed in 1908, immediately before Smith designed the Waite pack. Several Waite–Smith compositions are remarkably close to Sola Busca cards, most famously:

- Three of Swords;
- Ten of Wands;
- Seven of Swords;
- certain court-card and implement motifs.

However, I found **no primary statement by Smith or Waite** saying that the Sola Busca Nine of Batons was the source for Smith’s Nine of Wands, and the two compositions are not close enough to demonstrate direct copying. Smith’s Nine is a stationary, wounded sentry behind a palisade; the Sola Busca pip belongs to a different Renaissance action-scene.

**Conclusion:** “Sola Busca influenced Smith’s scenic minors” is strongly supported. “The Sola Busca Nine specifically supplied Smith’s Nine” remains an unproved attribution, not a documented fact.

### Mantegna “Tarocchi,” c. 1460s

**Documented fact:** The so-called Mantegna Tarocchi are educational/allegorical engravings arranged in five classes of ten. They are neither by Mantegna nor a standard tarot pack and contain no four-suit Nine of Batons.

**Relevance:** They document the same Renaissance taste for hierarchically ordered allegorical images but have no individual Nine of Wands.

## Early printed sheets

### Rosenwald sheets, late fifteenth or early sixteenth century

**Documented fact:** The Rosenwald sheets preserve early woodcut tarot designs, especially trumps and courts, associated with central or northern Italy.

**Finding:** No securely identified Rosenwald Nine of Batons was found. Assertions that its composition directly anticipates Waite–Smith should be withheld.

### Budapest sheet, early sixteenth century

The same evidentiary limitation applies. It is important for early printed tarot iconography, but no complete narrative Nine of Batons traceable through it was located.

---

## Minchiate

**Documented fact:** Minchiate expanded the tarot structure to 97 cards by adding trumps representing virtues, zodiacal signs, and elements. Its earliest secure name in records is *germini* in 1506; “minchiate” became the ordinary later term.

Its suit of *bastoni* retained ordinary pip organization. The Nine remained a gaming numeral, not an individually titled astrological card.

**Game/iconographic meaning:** Any zodiacal or elemental imagery belonged to the expanded trump series. It does not show that the Nine of Batons itself originally meant Moon in Sagittarius.

---

## Bolognese pattern

**Documented fact:** The reduced Tarocchino Bolognese pack has 62 cards. In batons it retains Ace and Six through Ten, so the Nine survives. The removed numeral ranks are Two through Five.

The Bolognese Nine is a conventional pip rather than a Waite-style scene. In present rules batons rank:

> King, Queen, Knight, Jack, 10, 9, 8, 7, 6, Ace.

The card is an ordinary one-point card before counting deductions. [Rules of Ottocento](https://www.pagat.com/tarot/ottocento.html)

---

## Sicilian pattern

**Documented fact:** The Sicilian Tarot retains the Nine of *bastoni* or *mazze*. In the modern Mineo form the suit runs King, Queen, Horse, Maid, 10, 9, 8, 7, 6, 5. [Sicilian Tarocchi rules](https://www.pagat.com/tarot/sicilian.html)

Again, it is a conventional game card rather than a personalized occult picture.

## Tarot de Marseille lineage

### General Marseille Nine

**Documented fact:** Marseille-pattern numeral batons interlace in a geometric lattice. In representative versions, eight diagonal batons form a woven lozenge or X-structure and a ninth passes vertically through the center. Numerals are commonly written VIIII.

Plant ornament varies by maker and rank. A surviving Conver-line Nine described from the BnF scan has a particularly spare central lattice, with no substantial floral spray. [Conver-deck catalogue and images](https://etteilla.org/en/deck/41/tarot-de-marseille-by-nicolas-conver)

**Correction to the item-specific lead:** It is unsafe to define every Marseille Nine as “the Marseille pip with its floral ornament.” Floral or foliate ornament is characteristic of Marseille pip design generally, but the Nine can be notably austere. Exact decoration must be stated maker by maker.

### Jean Noblet, Paris, c. 1650

**Documented fact:** The Noblet is an early surviving French tarot of the family later called Tarot de Marseille Type I.

For the numeral batons, the governing visual grammar is crossed implements, symmetry, and central structure—not individualized human drama. No divinatory caption appears.

### Jean Dodal, Lyon, c. 1701–15

**Documented fact:** Dodal preserves the interlaced baton pattern in a coarser, forceful woodcut style. The suit is French *Bâtons*. The card remains a game pip. The British Museum preserves Dodal material; public image collections date the deck to c. 1701–15. [Dodal image collection](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Tarot_de_Marseille_-_Jean_Dodal)

### Nicolas Conver, Marseille, 1760 pattern

**Documented fact:** Conver’s pattern has eight crossed red batons around a pale-blue central lattice and a ninth upright baton. A later BnF witness catalogued under Conver’s name may date from 1809–33, so “Conver 1760” can mean the design lineage rather than the date of every surviving impression.

### Grimaud / Paul Marteau, 1930

**Documented fact:** Paul Marteau’s Grimaud *Ancien Tarot de Marseille* standardized and commercialized a recolored Conver-descended pattern. This publication did much to make “Tarot de Marseille” seem like the name of a single immemorial deck.

**Modern tradition:** Numerological Marseille readers subsequently treated the central ninth baton as concentration, completion, gestation, or a force approaching the Ten. These are reading practices, not documented intentions of Conver.

### Camoin–Jodorowsky, 1997

**Documented fact:** Philippe Camoin and Alejandro Jodorowsky issued a reconstructed/redrawn Tarot de Marseille assembled from several historical exemplars.

**Disputed claim:** Their presentation sometimes describes the reconstruction as restoring a hidden or original symbolic unity. Historically, no single complete ur-deck matching every restored detail has been demonstrated.

For Jodorowsky and Marianne Costa, the Nine tends to mean an advanced, inward, near-complete state of the suit’s energy. In Wands it becomes powerful creative or sexual force approaching culmination. This is a **modern esoteric/numerological synthesis**, not a recovered fifteenth-century meaning.

## Etteilla

### Etteilla’s divinatory system, 1783–91

Jean-Baptiste Alliette, “Etteilla,” published tarot divination before the Golden Dawn and designed the first surviving tarot intended from its inception as an occult/divinatory instrument.

**Documented textual evidence:** In Etteilla’s 1783/1785 baton lists, the Nine is:

- upright: *retard* — “delay”;
- reversed: *traverses* — “obstructions, setbacks, crossed circumstances.”

A detailed historical transcription comparing Etteilla and later D’Odoucet forms records exactly this pair. [Etteilla text-history discussion](https://forum.tarothistory.com/viewtopic.php?start=10&t=1152)

### Grand Etteilla, c. 1789

**Documented fact:** The Grand Etteilla rearranged the deck, numbered all 78 cards serially, printed interpretive labels, and overlaid supposed Egyptian, elemental, and astrological doctrine. Surviving original sheets are in the BnF. [BnF original sheets](https://gallica.bnf.fr/ark:/12148/btv1b10545802x)

**Caution:** Later decks called “Grand Etteilla II” or “III,” including nineteenth-century Grimaud issues, alter images, numbering, and labels. Meanings printed on a late nineteenth-century card are not automatically Etteilla’s exact 1789 words.

---

## Oswald Wirth, 1889

**Documented fact:** Wirth’s original tarot consisted of 22 trumps, not a fully illustrated 78-card deck. Therefore there is no original 1889 Wirth Nine of Wands.

Later “Wirth” packs that provide 56 minors are posthumous or modern extensions. Their Nine is a modern invention built from Marseille, Golden Dawn, or Waite conventions.

## Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn

### Book T, late 1880s–1890s

**Documented within the Order:** The Golden Dawn manuscript tradition calls the card:

> “The Lord of Great Strength.”

Its prescribed emblem is:

> “Four hands … holding eight wands crossed four and four; but a fifth hand at the foot of the card holds another wand upright, which traverses the point of junction … flames leap herefrom.”

Above and below appear the glyphs of the Moon and Sagittarius. Its qualities include tremendous steady force, success attended by strife, victory after apprehension, health and recovery, generosity, obstinacy, and curiosity.

**Esoteric attribution:**

- second decan of Sagittarius, 10°–20°;
- Moon as decan ruler;
- Yesod of Atziluth / Yesod in the suit of Fire;
- suit letter Yod;
- Shem angels commonly transliterated Yerathel and Seheiah.

### Wang reconstruction, 1977

Robert Wang’s *Golden Dawn Tarot*, painted under Israel Regardie’s direction, visualizes the Book T prescriptions: crossed wands, issuing hands, flames, and astrological signs.

**Documented dependence:** This is a twentieth-century reconstruction of Order papers, not a surviving official nineteenth-century Golden Dawn printed deck.

### Cicero reconstruction, 1990s onward

Sandra Tabatha Cicero’s artwork for the *Magical Tarot of the Golden Dawn*, with Chic Cicero, likewise follows Book T while imposing a polished modern ritual palette.

Differences between Wang and Cicero are interpretive choices in reconstructing manuscripts; neither can be treated as the unique original Golden Dawn card-face.

## Waite–Smith, 1909

### What Pamela Colman Smith drew

**Documented fact:** Smith shows:

- a standing man in profile;
- a bandage around his head;
- one staff gripped in both hands;
- eight other staffs planted behind him like a palisade;
- uneven terrain and distant hills;
- a tense, sideways, expectant gaze.

Waite describes it:

> “The figure leans upon his staff and has an expectant look, as if awaiting an enemy. Behind are eight other staves—erect, in orderly disposition, like a palisade.”

He interprets it as:

> “strength in opposition.”

A. E. Waite, *The Pictorial Key to the Tarot*, first published 1910, Part III, “Nine of Wands.” [Validated text](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Pictorial_Key_to_the_Tarot)

### What changed

The transformation is profound:

- from counted emblems to a dramatic episode;
- from symmetrical arrangement to embodied vigilance;
- from “great strength” as an abstract decanic force to a wounded human defender;
- from nine equal implements to one actively held staff plus eight forming a boundary.

**Scholarly reconstruction:** Smith probably translated Waite’s phrase “strength in opposition,” and behind it the Golden Dawn’s apprehension-before-victory, into a readable stage scene. Her background in theater and book illustration helps explain the compressed dramatic tableau.

**Disputed:** The Sola Busca may have encouraged Smith to illustrate every pip, but its Nine is not demonstrably the direct composition source.

## Paul Foster Case and BOTA

### BOTA deck, Jessie Burns Parke and Paul Foster Case

**Documented fact:** The BOTA pip cards reject Smith’s scenic-minor model. They return to patterned suit emblems, supplemented by simple geometric symbols for Qabalistic teaching. Jessie Burns Parke drew the deck under Case’s direction.

For the Nine:

- nine wands;
- a pillar within a triangle, indicating support/foundation;
- correspondence to Yesod.

Case writes that the pillar “suggests support, and thus harmonizes with 9, called Foundation or Basis in Qabalah.”

### Case’s significant disagreement

Case gives the Nine of Wands:

- second decan of Sagittarius;
- **Mars**, not the Golden Dawn’s Moon;
- keyword “Preparedness.”

This is a genuine system conflict, not a typographical variant. His meanings include strength in reserve, recovery, success with strife, and—when ill-dignified—violence, travel dangers, obstinacy, and conflict with religious or legal authorities. [Case, *Oracle of the Tarot*, searchable scan](https://www.scribd.com/doc/187261487/Paul-Foster-Case-Oracle-of-the-Tarot-A-Course-on-Tarot-Divination-pdf)

## Thoth, painted 1938–43; first full publication 1969

### Crowley and Frieda Harris

**Documented fact:** Crowley calls the card simply “Strength.” Harris depicts:

- eight small background wands transformed into arrows;
- a dominant central “master arrow”;
- lunar crescents as arrowheads;
- a solar driving force at the opposite end;
- flames directed downward;
- Moon and Sagittarius glyphs;
- an abstract, dynamic, nearly scientific geometry rather than a human scene.

Crowley writes:

> “The Nine of Wands is called Strength.”

And:

> “Here the Moon, the weakest of the planets, is in Sagittarius, the most elusive of the Signs; yet it dares call itself Strength.”

His explanation is that Yesod restores balance and that stability exists through continuous change:

> “change is stability; … stability is guaranteed by change.”

A. Crowley, *The Book of Thoth*, 1944, pp. 185–86 in commonly paginated editions. [Public scan](https://www.rahoorkhuit.net/library/crowley/equinox/volume3/vol_3_no_05.pdf)

### Publication dates

**Documented fact:** Harris painted the deck between 1938 and 1943. Crowley published *The Book of Thoth* in 1944 with selected images. A full commercial deck appeared only in 1969, after both collaborators’ deaths.

## Notable modern redesigns

### Rider–Waite–Smith descendants

**Modern tradition:** Most mass-market scenic decks retain some combination of:

- wounded or exhausted figure;
- defensive wall;
- “one last test”;
- vigilance, boundaries, perseverance;
- trauma or hypervigilance.

The explicit language of trauma, PTSD, boundary work, and “survivor mode” is largely late twentieth- or twenty-first-century psychology applied to Smith’s visual cues.

### Thoth descendants

Modern Thelemic decks usually retain Moon in Sagittarius, Yesod, arrows, or “strength through change.”

### Marseille revival decks

Restorations by Flornoy, Ben-Dov, Camoin–Jodorowsky, and others keep the geometric lattice but disagree on line, color, plant details, and whether restoration should reproduce a single historical witness or synthesize several.

# The game

## Original function

**Documented fact:** Tarot originated as a family of trick-taking games. The earliest cards were called *carte da trionfi*: ordinary Italian suits plus a permanent trump sequence and the Fool.

The Nine of Batons was never a trump. It could take a trick only:

- by following suit and outranking another baton;
- when led after stronger cards were exhausted;
- or under local rules governing failure to follow suit.

A trump beats it.

## Rank

Batons are a “long” suit. In standard Italian-suited tarot ranking:

> King > Queen > Knight > Jack > 10 > 9 > 8 > … > Ace.

Thus the Nine is immediately below the Ten and above the Eight. Cups and Coins traditionally rank their numeral cards in the reverse direction.

This long/round distinction survives in Piedmontese tarocchi and related games. [Tarocco Piemontese summary](https://www.pagat.com/tarot/), [Scarto description](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scarto)

## Point value

**Documented fact:** In most tarot point-counting systems the Nine is a low or “empty” card:

- nominally 1 point in some Italian accounts;
- effectively a fraction after cards are counted in groups and deductions made;
- no special honor status.

In Bolognese Ottocento all noncourt, non-*tarocchi* cards are listed at 1 before pair deduction. In many French/Tarock counting systems it is simply one of the low cards needed to complete counting groups. [Comparative counting systems](https://www.pagat.com/tarot/counting.html)

## Tarock

In French-suited Tarock packs, Wands no longer appear as a suit: Clubs replace them. A Nine of Clubs may occupy the analogous game rank but is not iconographically or historically the occult Nine of Wands.

## Minchiate

The Nine of Batons remains an ordinary long-suit card in the 97-card pack. Added zodiac and elemental images are trumps, not pip correspondences.

## Trump-order disputes: contextual only

Michael Dummett grouped early trump orders into:

- **A / Southern:** Bologna and Florence; Angel highest, then World.
- **B / Eastern:** Ferrara; World highest, followed by Justice and Angel.
- **C / Western:** Milan and later Marseille; World highest, then Angel.

Justice and Strength shift within those sequences. The Fool is generally outside ordinary trump rank and is excused or exchanged according to the game. None of these disputes changes the rank of the Nine of Batons. [Dummett’s Bologna address](https://www.tarocchinobolognese.it/pages/sir_michael_dummett/il_saluto_allaccademia.php)

# Chronology of attestation

### c. 1418–25 — Marziano da Tortona

**Documented fact:** Marziano described a game commissioned by Filippo Maria Visconti and painted by Michelino da Besozzo. It used four bird suits and sixteen deified classical figures with trumping power.

**Scholarly reconstruction:** It is a precursor or collateral ancestor of tarot, not a standard 78-card tarot. It contained no documented Nine of Batons.

### 1440 — Florence

A record discovered after the long-cited Ferrara evidence reportedly moves secure *trionfi* documentation to 1440.

### 10 February 1442 — Ferrara ledger

**Documented fact:** Sagramoro was paid for painting the cups, swords, coins, batons, figures, and backs of four packs of *carte da trionfi*. The entry explicitly establishes the four standard suits, including batons. [Transcription and translation](https://www.oocities.org/autorbis/ferrara1442.html)

It does not name the Nine separately.

### c. 1450–80 — Visconti–Sforza Nine

Earliest securely catalogued surviving Nine of Staves identified in this research: Morgan MS M.630 no. 30.

### c. 1470 — *Sermones de ludo cum aliis*

**Documented fact:** An anonymous Dominican sermon attacks games and supplies an early trump list. It demonstrates that the tarot sequence was recognizable and morally controversial.

**Finding:** It lists trumps, not individual numeral suit cards. Therefore it is not the earliest text naming the Nine of Batons.

### 1491 / c. 1500 — Sola Busca

First completely surviving individualized Nine of Batons in a complete illustrated tarot.

### Late fifteenth–early sixteenth century — poems and literary lists

Folengo and the so-called tarocchi appropriati use tarot figures or cards poetically. They illuminate social and literary circulation but provide no securely traceable special meaning for the Nine of Batons.

### 1505–06 — *tarocchi*

**Documented fact:** The word *tarocchi* replaces or supplements *trionfi* in Italian records. The etymology remains unresolved.

### 1650–1760 — French printed line

Noblet, Dodal, and Conver transmit the geometric *Neuf de Bâtons*.

### 1783–85 — Etteilla

First located individually attested cartomantic meaning for the Nine of Batons: *retard / traverses*.

### c. 1789 — Grand Etteilla

Meanings and occult claims become embodied in a purpose-designed divinatory deck.

### 1888 — Mathers

S. L. MacGregor Mathers’s *The Tarot: Its Occult Signification, Use in Fortune-Telling, and Method of Play* renders the card “Nine of Sceptres.”

### 1889 — Papus and Wirth

Papus publishes *Le Tarot des Bohémiens*. Wirth supplies only trumps. Papus’s pip synthesis gives the Nine of Batons the sense:

> “Réalisation du succès. Le succès se maintient.”

“Realization of success. Success is maintained.” Papus, 1889, pp. 316, 320 in the cited French edition. [Gallica scan](https://gallica.bnf.fr/ark:/12148/bpt6k5477758p)

### Late 1880s–1890s — Golden Dawn Book T

“The Lord of Great Strength”; Moon in Sagittarius; Yesod of Fire.

### 1909–10 — Waite–Smith and *Pictorial Key*

Smith establishes the wounded-sentry image. Waite publicly defines it as strength in opposition, delay, suspension, and adjournment.

### 1930s — Case/BOTA

Patterned emblem, pillar/Foundation, “Preparedness”; Case substitutes Mars for Moon.

### 1938–44 / 1969 — Thoth

Harris paints Crowley’s arrow geometry; Crowley publishes the explanation; full deck follows in 1969.

### 1980 — Rachel Pollack

*Seventy-Eight Degrees of Wisdom* helps shift English-language tarot toward psychological and experiential reading.

### 1980s onward — Mary K. Greer and later psychological tarot

Reversal practice expands beyond fixed opposites into blocked, internalized, excessive, delayed, or projected expressions. The Nine increasingly becomes a card of personal boundaries, exhaustion, and accumulated experience.

### 1997 — Camoin–Jodorowsky

A synthetic Marseille restoration and numerological reading renew the geometric Nine for contemporary readers.

## Conflicts and cautions

1. **Moon versus Mars:** Golden Dawn and Crowley give Moon in Sagittarius. Case gives Mars. Calling Mars “the original Golden Dawn ruler” is incorrect.
2. **Yesod versus a Tree path:** Nine corresponds to the Sephirah Yesod; it does not receive one of the 22 inter-Sephiroth paths.
3. **Yod:** Yod belongs to the entire Fire/Wands suit through the Tetragrammaton formula. It is not the Nine’s personal Hebrew letter.
4. **Tzaddi/Heh:** Crowley’s Emperor/Star exchange concerns trumps IV and XVII. It does not alter the Nine of Wands.
5. **Court de Gébelin, Lévi, and Papus:** Their elaborate Hebrew/Egyptian discussions focus mainly on the 22 trumps. Modern charts that assign their trump letters directly to every pip often conflate later Golden Dawn doctrine with earlier authors.
6. **Element Fire:** Wands = Fire is an occult convention, fully systematized by nineteenth-century Hermetic orders. Historical Italian players did not need an elemental attribution to play *bastoni*.

# Divinatory meanings by author

Copyright limits prevent extended quotation from modern copyrighted works; their meanings are therefore carefully paraphrased.

## Etteilla, 1783/1785

- Upright: **“retard”** — delay.
- Reversed: **“traverses”** — obstacles, crossed circumstances, setbacks.

These are the earliest directly traced meanings found for this individual card.

## S. L. MacGregor Mathers, 1888

Mathers, *The Tarot*, “Nine of Sceptres”:

- Upright: **“Understanding, observation, good arrangement.”**
- Reversed: **“Obstacles, crosses, delay, displeasure.”**

[Public scan](https://logoilibrary.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/07/Mathers.pdf)

Mathers clearly preserves Etteilla’s delay/obstruction family for the reversal but introduces a more constructive upright sense.

## Golden Dawn Book T

- Tremendous, steady, difficult-to-shake force.
- Success through strife and energy.
- Victory preceded by fear or apprehension.
- Health and reliable recovery.
- Generosity, curiosity, obstinacy.
- When poorly dignified, the force becomes stubborn, combative, or misapplied.

Book T ordinarily uses elemental dignity rather than the later simple upright/reversed binary.

## A. E. Waite, 1910

Waite:

> “The card signifies strength in opposition.”

He adds that if attacked the person will meet the assault boldly and may prove a formidable opponent.

- Upright: strength in opposition; delay; suspension; adjournment.
- Reversed: **“Obstacles, adversity, calamity.”**

[Waite’s complete entry](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Pictorial_Key_to_the_Tarot/Part_III#Nine)

## Aleister Crowley, 1944

Crowley’s title is “Strength.”

- Power secured through dynamic equilibrium.
- The fullest practical development of fiery force at Yesod.
- Health and recovery.
- Success after opposition.
- Change as the condition of stability.
- The paradox of a weak/changeable Moon operating through elusive, fiery Sagittarius.

Crowley does not center a simple reversed-card list; orientation is less important than dignity and interaction.

## Paul Foster Case, 1930s

Keyword: **Preparedness**.

- Well-dignified: strength held in reserve, health after illness, success with some strife, originality, independence, daring.
- Ill-dignified: violence or danger during foreign travel, disputes involving a spouse’s relatives, conflict with prominent religious or legal persons, obstinacy.

## Rachel Pollack, 1980

Pollack reads Smith’s figure psychologically:

- strength already proved by previous struggles;
- readiness for one more challenge;
- defensiveness created by experience;
- possible withdrawal behind one’s own protective barrier;
- the paradox that walls provide security but restrict life.

A reversed or shadow expression may indicate weakness, inability to defend oneself, or the need to lower obsolete defenses. This is a paraphrase of *Seventy-Eight Degrees of Wisdom*, not a verbatim quotation.

## Mary K. Greer

Across *Tarot for Your Self* and *The Complete Book of Tarot Reversals*, Greer’s approach allows several reversal modes rather than a single dictionary antonym:

- blocked endurance;
- inwardly experienced defensiveness;
- hypervigilance;
- exhaustion;
- excessive barriers;
- persistence that has become rigidity;
- alternatively, release of one’s guard.

These are systematized modern reading techniques, not claims about Renaissance intent.

# Esoteric and symbolic readings

## Elemental and Hermetic

**First securely systematized appearance:** Golden Dawn, late nineteenth century.

Wands represent Fire:

- will;
- energy;
- expansion;
- initiation;
- creative and sexual force.

The Nine is Fire stabilized in Yesod. Because Yesod is lunar and formative, fiery impulse becomes a stored, patterned reserve rather than an uncontrolled blaze.

**Spreaders:** Mathers, Golden Dawn members, Waite indirectly, Crowley, Case, Regardie, Wang, Cicero, and modern correspondence manuals.

## Tetragrammaton

**Esoteric attribution:** The four suits are mapped to Yod–Heh–Vav–Heh:

- Wands = Yod = Fire = archetypal initiative;
- Cups = first Heh = Water;
- Swords = Vav = Air;
- Pentacles = final Heh = Earth.

Thus the card is Yesod of Yod, conventionally phrased Yesod in the world/suit of Fire.

**Caution:** This is not evidence that fifteenth-century cardmakers encoded the Tetragrammaton.

## Qabalah and the number nine

**Golden Dawn/Thelemic attribution:** Nine corresponds to Yesod, “Foundation.”

Symbolic consequences:

- force has descended through the higher Sephiroth and becomes a stable formative pattern;
- it is nearly materialized but has not yet reached Malkuth/Ten;
- lunar cyclicity produces stability through repeated change;
- the central ninth wand organizes or supports the other eight.

Dion Fortune later reinforced the link by citing the Nine of Wands as “Lord of Great Strength” when discussing Yesod’s divine name Shaddai and imagery of strength.

## Astrology

**Golden Dawn:** Moon in the second decan of Sagittarius.

The synthesis joins:

- Moon: change, reflection, rhythm, memory, bodily habit, receptivity;
- Sagittarius: mutable Fire, aiming, distance, aspiration, travel;
- Jupiter: sign ruler;
- Yesod: lunar Sephirah.

Crowley resolves the apparent weakness of Moon plus mutability through a physics-like maxim: a living system remains stable only by changing.

**Case’s alternative:** Mars in the same decan intensifies preparedness, danger, courage, travel, and conflict. This is a revision, not the Book T correspondence.

## Alchemical reading

**Modern extension with Renaissance prompts:** Sola Busca scholarship has read its suit scenes in relation to furnaces, labor, matter, and transformation. Applied to the Nine, the reading becomes material tested repeatedly before completion.

**Evidence level:** Plausible for the Sola Busca program in general; no recovered key proves a specific alchemical operation for its Nine of Batons.

## Christian reading

Historical Christian culture supplied:

- staff as support, pilgrimage, office, or chastisement;
- nine as angelic orders or spiritual completion;
- endurance under trial.

**Finding:** No early Christian commentary on this particular card was traced. Later devotional readings of the wounded man as Job, a penitent, Christ-like sufferer, or spiritual warrior are modern analogies.

## Jungian reading

**Modern invention:** Jung did not publish an interpretation of the Nine of Wands.

Post-Jungian readers treat Smith’s scene as:

- the ego guarding a wounded complex;
- repetition compulsion: expecting the old attack again;
- a boundary protecting psychic integrity;
- accumulated ordeal preparing individuation;
- the shadow possibility that defenses have become identity.

These readings descend from twentieth-century archetypal psychology and Smith’s picture, not from historical tarot rules.

*The Castle of Crossed Destinies* uses tarot cards as a combinatorial narrative language, in different editions drawing on Visconti imagery and later Marseille material.

**Finding:** No frame or production statement securely identifying the Nine of Wands as the source of a particular film composition was located.

# Controversies and disputes

## Egyptian origin

### Claim

Court de Gébelin, in *Le Monde primitif* VIII (1781), declared tarot a surviving Egyptian book. The Comte de Mellet added Hebrew-letter and cosmological interpretations. Etteilla then built a practical “Book of Thoth” system.

### Evidence

**Legend:** Neither writer could read Egyptian hieroglyphs; Champollion’s decipherment came decades later. No pharaonic tarot cards, 78-card Egyptian book, or transmission text has been found.

### Historical correction

The tarot pack is documented in fifteenth-century northern Italy. Playing-card suit ancestry may ultimately involve Mamluk Egypt and the Islamic world, but that is not Gébelin’s claim that the tarot’s trump allegories preserve an ancient Egyptian priestly book.

## “Gypsy” or Romani origin

### Claim

Tarot was brought from Egypt by “Gypsies,” imagined as wandering Egyptians and keepers of occult wisdom.

### Evidence

**Legend:** Tarot is documented in Italy before evidence that Romani communities used it as their special inherited divinatory scripture. Romani language and migration history point to South Asian origins, not descent from Egyptian priesthood.

### Spreaders

Gébelin’s milieu, Etteilla’s Egyptianism, nineteenth-century occultists, and especially titles such as Papus’s *Tarot des Bohémiens* helped fix the myth.

## Cathar and Templar claims

### Claim

Tarot secretly records Cathar dualism, Templar doctrine, Grail initiation, or suppressed medieval heresy.

### Evidence

**Disputed/unsupported:** No fifteenth-century rule, cardmaker’s statement, inquisitorial record, or continuous transmission document connects the Nine of Batons—or the tarot pack as a whole—to Cathars or Templars.

Individual visual parallels can be produced after the fact, but resemblance is not provenance.

## The “Book of Thoth”

“Book of Thoth” can mean three different things:

1. Gébelin/Etteilla’s imagined Egyptian book;
2. Crowley’s 1944 explanatory volume;
3. the commercial name of the Crowley–Harris deck.

Conflating them creates a false continuous Egyptian lineage.

## Was divination original?

**Historical correction associated with Dummett, Decker, and Depaulis:** The earliest evidence presents tarot as a game. Secure evidence for systematic tarot divination appears only in the eighteenth century.

This does not prove that no earlier person ever used a card divinatory; it means such a practice has not been documented early enough to establish original purpose.

## Sola Busca and Smith

- Smith’s exposure to Sola Busca photographs: strongly supported.
- General influence on scenic minors: strongly supported by close compositions.
- Direct copying of the **Nine**: unproved.
- Statement that Sola Busca was exhibited in the British Museum in 1908: repeated in museum and scholarly reception histories, but no statement by Smith saying “I used it” has surfaced.

## Moon or Mars in Sagittarius?

- Golden Dawn Book T: Moon.
- Crowley: Moon.
- Case: Mars.

A chart that gives Mars while citing Book T has merged Case with the Golden Dawn.

## “Great Strength” versus “Strength”

- Golden Dawn: “The Lord of Great Strength.”
- Crowley/Thoth card: “Strength.”
- Waite: “strength in opposition.”
- Mathers’s 1888 public list: understanding/observation/good arrangement.

These are related traditions, not identical quotations.

## Floral Marseille Nine

The claim that the Marseille Nine necessarily contains a flowering central ornament is too broad. Maker-specific cards vary, and some Conver-line examples are markedly bare. Symbolic interpretations based on a flower must identify the exact edition.

## Misprints and inherited errors

Common modern errors include:

- calling the Thoth correspondence “Sun in Sagittarius” because the card contains a solar emblem;
- assigning an individual Hebrew letter to the Nine;
- calling it the ninth Major Arcana because Thoth prints “Strength” on it;
- presenting Case’s Mars as Golden Dawn doctrine;
- dating every Conver-looking impression to 1760;
- treating VIIII as an error rather than an ordinary historical additive numeral;
- treating modern “Rider–Waite” recolorings as Smith’s original palette;
- claiming the Nine of Wands, rather than several other cards, is an exact Sola Busca copy.

# Open questions

1. **Precise Sola Busca program:** No consensus explanation accounts for every figure, tool, inscription, classical name, and suit action.
2. **Artist attribution:** The Sola Busca engraver has been variously attributed; the evidence remains debated.
3. **Smith’s working process:** Waite’s directions, Smith’s independent invention, stage imagery, Sola Busca photographs, and Golden Dawn manuscripts can be separated only partially.
4. **Earliest individual written name:** Fifteenth-century records name the suit *bastoni* and entire packs, but no contemporary list explicitly saying “nove di bastoni” was located. The surviving card is earlier than a located textual naming.
5. **Cary–Yale and Brambilla Nines:** Accessible catalogues did not verify surviving examples. A complete object-level census across museum databases and printed catalogues could refine the “earliest surviving” statement.
6. **Etteilla’s original card-face:** Original 1789 sheets, later colored copies, D’Odoucet’s redactions, and nineteenth-century Grimaud decks require impression-by-impression comparison. “Retard/traverses” is textually traceable, but not every surviving deck prints the same wording.
7. **Golden Dawn manuscript dating:** “Book T, 1888” is conventional shorthand. Surviving copies and later publication layers do not necessarily preserve a single fixed 1888 exemplar.
8. **Case’s Mars:** The fact of the substitution is clear; his exact technical rationale and its relationship to alternative decan rulership tables deserve a dedicated study.
9. **Modern Jungian genealogy:** Psychological readings often invoke Jung without citing a text. The precise first author to apply “wounded ego/hypervigilance” to this card remains untraced.
10. **Card-specific cultural quotation:** Calvino, Eliot, Yeats, Bergman, and Jodorowsky belong to tarot’s cultural history, but a documented Nine-of-Wands quotation by them was not found.
