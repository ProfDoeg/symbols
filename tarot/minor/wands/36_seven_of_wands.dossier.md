# Seven of Wands: Research Dossier

## Method and evidence labels

This dossier distinguishes six kinds of statements:

- **[Documented fact]** Directly supported by a surviving card, contemporary record, first edition, manuscript, or institutional catalogue.
- **[Scholarly reconstruction]** A conclusion argued from material, documentary, or comparative evidence by historians.
- **[Tradition]** A repeated interpretation whose first author or evidentiary basis is uncertain.
- **[Esoteric attribution]** A correspondence deliberately assigned by an occult author or school, not a claim about the card’s fifteenth-century meaning.
- **[Disputed claim]** A proposition supported by some writers but challenged by evidence or other specialists.
- **[Legend]** A historical-origin story unsupported by contemporary evidence.
- **[Modern invention]** A comparatively recent interpretive or artistic addition.

A central distinction governs the whole dossier: the Seven of Wands began as a numeral card used in a game. Its title *Valour*, Mars-in-Leo correspondence, fire symbolism, defensive scene, psychological meanings, and Kabbalistic identity are later layers.

### The suit name

**[Documented fact]** Fifteenth-century Italian records call the suit *bastoni*. The February 1442 Ferrara account explicitly lists “*le chope e le spade e li dinari e li bastoni*”—cups, swords, coins, and batons—in four packs of *chartexele da trionffy*. The staff suit was therefore present from the earliest documentary phase of standard tarot. [Ferrara account transcription](https://www.oocities.org/autorbis/ferrara1442.html)

**[Documented fact]** Court de Gébelin in 1781 called the sign *Bâton* and described its ace as “*une vraie massue*,” a real club. He associated it with agriculture and Hercules’ club. This was his speculative Egyptian social allegory, not a fifteenth-century explanation. [Court de Gébelin, *Du Jeu des Tarots*, pp. 365–410](https://www.tarock.info/gebelin.htm)

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** Latin-suited packs divide visually into Italian and Spanish families. Italian batons are generally straight ceremonial rods crossed into lattices; Spanish *bastos* are separate knobbly clubs. Tarot’s suit belongs historically to this broader Latin-suit family. [Pagat, Latin-suited cards](https://www.pagat.com/class/latin.html)

**[Esoteric attribution]** “Wands” became dominant in English occult tarot because the Golden Dawn understood the suit as the magical fire implement. “Wands” is therefore not a translation that perfectly preserves the historical playing-card object: *bastone/bâton/basto* can mean staff, stick, baton, or club.

## What does not apply to this card

The template’s Papess/Juno, Pope/Jupiter, Tower/*Maison Dieu*, Fool numbering, Justice/Strength positions, Tzaddi–Heh controversy, and three regional trump orders concern the trumps. They do not change the Seven of Wands’ identity, rank, or suit.

For completeness:

- The Besançon pattern replaces the Papess and Pope with Juno and Jupiter.
- *La Maison Dieu* is the conventional French name of trump XVI.
- The Fool was historically unnumbered because it normally did not take tricks as an ordinary ranked trump; modern occult decks commonly print 0.
- Justice and Strength change places between several historical and occult sequences.
- Crowley’s Tzaddi–Heh revision changes the Emperor and Star correspondences, not any Seven.

Including those facts in this card’s correspondence table would falsely imply that they control the Seven of Wands.

# Iconography deck by deck

## Early Milanese painted packs

### Marziano–Michelino pack, Milan, approximately 1418–1425

**[Documented fact]** Marziano da Tortona described a special pack commissioned for Filippo Maria Visconti and painted by Michelino da Besozzo. It had four bird suits and sixteen deified heroes acting as superior cards. No cards survive. A letter of Jacopo Antonio Marcello dated 11 November 1449 describes the earlier commission. [Study of the Marziano pack](https://naibi.net/A/70-MARZI-Z.pdf)

**Finding:** There was no historically identifiable Seven of Wands in this pack. It is relevant as a structural predecessor—a regular suited pack plus permanent superior cards—not as an iconographic ancestor of this particular card.

### Brera-Brambilla, Milan, approximately 1442–1451

**[Documented fact]** Forty-eight cards survive in the Pinacoteca di Brera. The group is attributed to the Bembo workshop and is commonly placed in the 1440s, though proposed dates vary. Almost the complete numeral-card series survives; institutional records date individual examples approximately 1442–1451. [Italian national catalogue example](https://catalogo.beniculturali.it/detail/HistoricOrArtisticProperty/0300097697-19)

**[Documented fact / visual evidence]** The numeral cards place the suit signs on silvered grounds, in contrast to the gold grounds of courts and trumps. The batons are slender heraldic or weapon-like rods rather than natural tree branches. Their repeated arrangement is decorative, not narrative.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** If the Brambilla Seven of Batons is accepted as contemporary with the group’s earliest production, it is the earliest surviving physical Seven of this suit now known. Dating the several Milanese groups relative to one another remains contested, so “earliest” should not be turned into a precise production year.

### Cary–Yale / Visconti di Modrone, Milan, mid-fifteenth century

**[Documented fact]** Sixty-seven cards survive at Yale’s Beinecke Library. The pack has an expanded court structure, including female knights and pages, and probably had more than the later canonical 78 cards. [Yale conservation account](https://library.yale.edu/news/cards-library-partners-work-together-solve-mysteries-rare-tarot-deck?page=1)

**[Documented fact / visual evidence]** Its baton pips resemble arrows or ceremonial shafts and are disposed as repeated emblems on costly illuminated grounds. They do not form a human scene.

**Caution:** Modern “complete Cary–Yale” editions reconstruct missing cards. A Seven seen in a commercial reproduction may therefore be partly or wholly modern unless matched against Yale’s surviving-card catalogue.

### Pierpont Morgan–Bergamo / Visconti-Sforza, approximately 1450s

**[Documented fact]** Seventy-four associated cards survive across the Morgan Library, Accademia Carrara, and Colleoni collection. The Morgan’s online group includes the Ace, Four, and Nine of Staves but not a Seven. [Morgan Library catalogue](https://www.themorgan.org/collection/tarot-cards)

**[Documented fact / visual evidence]** The staves are Visconti-Sforza heraldic implements repeated on decorated fields. As in the other luxury Milanese packs, the numeral card is primarily an enumeration and suit display, not an episode about courage.

**Finding:** Commercial Visconti-Sforza Seven cards must be checked against the surviving Carrara/Colleoni holdings and the publisher’s reconstruction policy. The Morgan record alone does not establish an extant Seven.

## Ferrarese and related cards

### Este cards

Several fragmentary fifteenth-century groups associated with Ferrara or the Este court survive, but their attribution and grouping have repeatedly changed.

**[Documented fact]** Ferrarese court accounts mention painted and inexpensive *trionfi* packs in 1442, but do not describe the individual Seven of Batons.

**Finding:** I found no museum record that securely establishes a surviving Este Seven of Batons with a card-specific description. It would be unsafe to reconstruct one from commercial “Este Tarot” editions.

### Mantegna “Tarocchi”

**[Documented fact]** The so-called Mantegna Tarocchi are two fifteenth-century series of fifty didactic engravings arranged into social, cosmic, liberal-art, virtue, and celestial hierarchies.

**Finding:** They are not tarot packs, contain no four ordinary suits, and therefore have no Seven of Wands. Their name is a later bibliographical convention. They can illuminate Renaissance allegorical culture but not this pip’s genealogy.

## Sola Busca, 1491; coloured later

**[Documented fact]** The Sola Busca is the earliest surviving complete 78-card tarot and the earliest complete tarot with narrative or figure imagery throughout the numeral suits. It consists of twenty-two triumphs and fifty-six cards in coins, swords, batons, and cups. Its copper engravings are dated 1491; the unique complete copy was subsequently illuminated with tempera and gold. [Pinacoteca di Brera exhibition catalogue](https://pinacotecabrera.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/11/Il-Segreto-dei-tarocchi-eng.pdf)

**[Documented fact]** In 1907 the Busca-Serbelloni family supplied the British Museum with black-and-white photographs of the complete pack. This made the designs available in London shortly before the Waite–Smith deck was drawn.

**[Visual fact]** The Sola Busca Seven of Batons is a figured Renaissance scene, not the Marseille lattice and not Pamela Colman Smith’s composition of one man warding off six invisible opponents from a hilltop.

### Was it the source of Smith’s Seven?

**[Disputed claim, not verified]** The item-specific lead that “the Sola Busca pip (1491)” was the source of Smith’s Seven-of-Wands scene could not be substantiated.

There is strong comparative evidence for Sola Busca influence elsewhere:

- Waite–Smith Three of Swords resembles Sola Busca Three of Swords.
- Waite–Smith Ten of Wands closely resembles the burdened figure in Sola Busca Ten of Swords.
- Other correspondences have been proposed with varying degrees of plausibility.

But general access to Sola Busca photographs does not prove that every scenic Waite–Smith pip derives from the correspondingly numbered Sola Busca card. No statement by Smith or Waite naming Sola Busca as the source of the Seven has been located. The Seven’s exact one-against-six composition is much more directly explained by Golden Dawn *Book T* and Waite’s supplied divinatory programme.

The appropriate verdict is therefore:

> Sola Busca influence on the Waite–Smith minors: **probable scholarly reconstruction**.  
> Sola Busca Seven as the particular source of Smith’s Seven: **unverified and probably a misattribution**.

## Rosenwald and Budapest sheets, approximately 1500

**[Documented fact]** These uncut or partially preserved woodcut sheets witness the transition from courtly hand-painted cards to printed popular tarot.

**[Visual evidence]** Their suit cards are conventional enumerated pips. Batons cross or repeat in symmetrical arrangements; floral or leaf-like infill helps occupy the spaces. No defender scene appears.

**Open issue:** Reconstruction of complete packs from sheets can be uncertain. A restored Seven may contain duplicated or inferred line work. These sources document a printed pip tradition, not a card-specific meaning of “valour.”

## Tarot de Marseille lineage

### General form

**[Documented fact / visual evidence]** The Marseille Seven of Batons normally has six diagonal batons interlaced as three crossing three, with a seventh vertical baton passing through the centre. Leaves, flowers, and small coloured ornaments fill the open spaces. It is a symmetrical or near-symmetrical pip, with no human subject.

The exact ornament and colour distribution vary by maker. Its geometry supplied later occultists with a tempting “one among six” structure, but there is no evidence that seventeenth-century cardmakers meant it as a lone combatant.

### Jean Noblet, Paris, approximately 1650

**[Documented fact]** Noblet is among the oldest substantially surviving Marseille-type packs. His numbered batons continue the Italian interlaced-stick convention.

**Visual change:** Compact, vigorous woodcutting; crossed batons and vegetation; no narrative.

### Jean Dodal, Lyon, approximately 1701–1715

**[Documented fact]** Dodal preserves the same basic trellis, with differences in flowers, tips, bands, line, and stencil colour.

**Meaning:** No contemporary guide assigns the visual scheme a divinatory meaning. Later symbolic readings are retrojections.

### Pierre Madenié, Dijon, 1709

Madenié is the earliest dated exemplar normally classed as Marseille Type II. Its relevance is genealogical: later Conver-like pip arrangements are already visible before Conver.

### Nicolas Conver, Marseille, blocks dated 1760

**[Documented fact]** Conver’s design became a principal model for later commercial Marseille editions. The Seven keeps the central vertical baton and interlaced six, with foliate ornament.

### Lequart/Grimaud and Paul Marteau, late nineteenth century–1930

**[Documented fact]** Grimaud commercialized a standardized “Ancien Tarot de Marseille”; Paul Marteau’s 1930 deck and later book made its colour symbolism highly influential.

**[Esoteric attribution]** Marteau treated seven as 6 + 1 and read the white central baton as a higher support or personal inspiration acting through a complex field. This is a twentieth-century symbolic exegesis of the geometry, not recovered seventeenth-century doctrine.

### Camoin–Jodorowsky, 1997

**[Documented fact]** Philippe Camoin and Alejandro Jodorowsky issued a “restored” Marseille deck derived from multiple historical sources, especially Conver.

**[Disputed claim]** Its promoters sometimes present the result as a recovery of a primordial or secret canonical tarot. Historians generally treat it as a modern composite restoration: historically informed, but containing editorial decisions, normalized details, and esoteric reconstruction.

The Seven retains a Marseille pip structure. Jodorowsky and Marianne Costa read suit cards through number, colour, orientation, and developmental sequences. That method is a modern hermeneutic, not documentary evidence about Conver’s intentions.

## Minchiate

**[Documented fact]** Minchiate expanded the trump sequence to forty special cards but retained the standard fifty-six suited cards, including the Seven of Batons.

**Visual evidence:** Historical Florentine Minchiate Sevens are enumerated pips, frequently with Spanish/Italian club-like batons disposed separately or decoratively. There is no Golden Dawn title or combat scene.

The expansion of the trumps does not change this card into trump VII. “Seven of Batons” and “trump 7” remain entirely different cards.

## Bolognese tarocchino

**[Documented fact]** The reduced 62-card Bolognese pack removes numeral cards 2–5 but retains 6–10. The Seven of Batons therefore remains in the pack.

Its design is a regional repeated-pip pattern. It has no historical printed title *Valour*.

## Sicilian tarot

**[Documented fact]** Sicilian tarot was introduced in the seventeenth century, probably through Rome. The surviving reduced pack retains the Seven. In current Mineo rules, suit order is King, Queen, Horse, Maid, 10, 9, 8, 7, 6, 5, and sometimes 4. [Rules of Sicilian Tarocchi](https://www.pagat.com/tarot/sicilian.html)

## Grand Etteilla

### Date and identity

**[Documented fact]** Etteilla began publishing tarot instruction in 1783 and produced an explicitly occult “Book of Thoth” pack in the later 1780s. What collectors call Grand Etteilla I, II, and III reflects several historical redactions, not one perfectly fixed “1789 deck.”

The Seven of Batons became card 29 in the standard Grand Etteilla ordering.

### Image

**[Documented fact / surviving decks]** The card retains a patterned arrangement of seven batons rather than a Waite–Smith human scene. Printed headings encode meanings, commonly *POURPARLER* upright and an instability/indecision term in the inverse orientation.

### Meaning

Etteilla’s card belongs to an older semantic stream centred on talking and negotiation—not primarily combat:

- Upright: *pourparler*, conference, conversation, discussion, negotiation, exchange.
- Reversed: irresolution, uncertainty, perplexity, inconstancy, hesitation.

These ideas survive in Waite’s “discussion,” “wordy strife,” “negotiations,” “barter,” and “competition.” Thus Waite combines Etteilla’s verbal/commercial current with the Golden Dawn’s *Valour*.

## Oswald Wirth, 1889

**Finding:** Wirth’s 1889 tarot consisted of twenty-two trumps drawn for Stanislas de Guaita. It has no Seven of Wands.

Later publishers have issued 78-card “Wirth” packs, but their minor cards are extensions by later artists or publishers. They should not be attributed to Wirth’s original 1889 iconographic programme.

## Golden Dawn *Book T*, approximately 1890s

**[Documented fact]** *Book T* calls the card:

> “LORD OF VALOUR — SEVEN OF WANDS”

It prescribes two hands holding six wands, three crossed with three, and a third hand holding an upright central wand passing between them. Flames leap from the junction; Mars and Leo symbols appear above and below. [Golden Dawn manuscript transcription](https://www.tarrdaniel.com/documents/Thelemagick/gd/publication/english/golden_dawn_manuscripts.html)

This is a decisive intermediate design:

Marseille interlaced six + central seventh baton → Golden Dawn magical hands, flames, and Mars/Leo → Waite–Smith human combat.

### Wang reconstruction, 1978

Robert Wang’s *Golden Dawn Tarot*, developed with Israel Regardie, visualizes the manuscript instructions. The Seven presents the crossed implements, cloud-borne hands, central wand, astrological emblems, and fiery junction.

### Cicero reconstruction, 1991 and later editions

Chic and Sandra Tabatha Cicero’s *New Golden Dawn Ritual Tarot*, later issued as the *Golden Dawn Magical Tarot*, is another reconstruction of the order’s papers. It follows the prescribed crossed-wand magical tableau rather than Smith’s landscape narrative.

**Caution:** Neither Wang nor the Ciceros supplied an untouched nineteenth-century “original Golden Dawn deck.” Both are twentieth-century visual reconstructions of manuscript specifications.

## Waite–Smith, 1909

### What Smith drew

**[Documented fact]** Pamela Colman Smith drew a young man on a craggy height. He holds one leafy staff crosswise and faces six other staffs thrust toward him from below. Their holders remain outside the frame. His position is superior but precarious. He wears a green tunic, yellow leggings, and apparently mismatched footwear. [Original illustration](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Pictorial_Key_to_the_Tarot_Wands_07.jpg)

### Waite’s description

Waite writes:

> “A young man on a craggy eminence brandishing a staff; six other staves are raised towards him from below.”

His meaning begins:

> “It is a card of valour, for, on the surface, six are attacking one…”

[Pictorial Key, Seven of Wands](https://sacred-texts.com/tarot/pkt/pktwa07.htm)

### Sources and synthesis

**[Documented textual relationship]** “Valour,” opposition, obstacles, courage, quarrelling, and small victory appear in *Book T*. The one-against-six composition literalizes those words.

**[Documented textual relationship]** Discussion and commercial negotiation preserve the Etteilla-derived current present in nineteenth-century meaning lists.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** Smith transformed the Golden Dawn ritual diagram into genre narrative: magical hands became anonymous opponents; crossed rods became an attack; the central wand became the defender’s weapon; positional superiority made “possible victory” visible.

### Mismatched footwear

**[Tradition / modern interpretation]** The shoes are widely read as evidence that the man was surprised, improperly prepared, or forced to improvise.

**Finding:** Waite does not mention them, and no surviving statement by Smith explains them. The interpretation is plausible visual exegesis, not an authorially documented symbol. Printing, colouring, or stylization may also contribute to the effect.

## Paul Foster Case and BOTA

Case’s BOTA tarot visually remains close to the Marseille/Waite lineage in the majors, while his teachings transmit Golden Dawn correspondences.

**[Esoteric attribution]** Case accepts the Seven as Mars in Leo, Netzach of Fire, associated with valour, opposition, courage, and potential victory. His divinatory material substantially belongs to the Golden Dawn–Waite family.

**Finding:** The publicly traceable material did not support a reliable card-specific verbatim quotation with an unambiguous original page. Accordingly, no invented Case quotation is supplied here.

## Thoth: Crowley and Frieda Harris, painted 1938–1943; book 1944; deck publication history

**[Documented fact]** Frieda Harris painted the Thoth cards under Aleister Crowley’s direction between 1938 and 1943. Seven colour plates appeared in *The Book of Thoth* in 1944; a complete deck was not commercially distributed until after both creators’ deaths. Small test editions appeared in the late 1960s, with broader publication following in the 1970s. “Published 1969” is defensible for an early complete issue, but should not imply ordinary wide release that year.

### Image

The printed title is **VALOUR**. Six elaborate ceremonial wands form a balanced but pressured background. A rough, asymmetric, club-like seventh staff stands forward. Flames are dispersed rather than harmoniously concentrated. The colour field is dark, hot, and violet-red.

Crowley explains:

> “The Adept’s Wands are relegated to the background; in front is a crude, uneven club…”

He adds:

> “Valour implies fear as well as courage.”

And characterizes the possible outcome as a “‘soldiers’ battle.’” [Crowley, *The Book of Thoth*, Seven of Wands](https://www.rahoorkhuit.net/library/thoth/fire/07_fire.html)

### Meaning of Harris’s changes

**[Esoteric attribution]** Seven is Netzach, below the balanced central sephiroth. In fire, its expansive instinct is no longer in ideal equilibrium. Mars in Leo supplies individual courage, but the card’s force is strained and personal rather than smoothly victorious.

The rude club is not merely stronger than the ceremonial wands. It signifies that when formed order breaks down, immediate animal or martial courage may be all that remains.

### Tzaddi/Heh

Crowley’s “Tzaddi is not the Star” doctrine swaps the Hebrew-letter assignments of the Emperor and Star. It has no direct effect on the Seven of Wands, whose attribution is generated through decan, suit, and sephira rather than a trump’s Hebrew path.

## Notable modern redesigns

Modern decks usually follow one of four strategies:

1. **Waite–Smith reiteration:** a lone figure holds high ground against many challengers. Examples include the Morgan–Greer and many mass-market decks.
2. **Identity revision:** the defender is recast as a woman, person of colour, queer figure, protester, worker, or boundary-setter. The *Modern Witch Tarot* is a prominent inclusive RWS-derived example.
3. **Psychological abstraction:** threats become lines, animals, flames, or energetic pressure; the literal combatants disappear.
4. **Thoth-derived abstraction:** a single force confronts six ordered forces under Mars/Leo symbolism.

**[Modern invention]** Readings such as “online criticism,” “impostor syndrome,” “activist resistance,” “maintaining boundaries,” or “protecting a creative platform” are contemporary applications. They may be useful, but they are not early meanings.

# The game

## Original function

**[Documented fact]** Tarot was created for trick-taking play. The Morgan describes its cards as a fifteenth-century Italian game for aristocratic use and notes that occult and fortune-telling associations arose centuries later. [Morgan Library](https://www.themorgan.org/collection/tarot-cards)

The Seven of Batons is an ordinary suited card. It is not one of the permanent trumps and has no special “valour” ability in historical play.

## Rank

In the traditional Italian reverse ranking:

- Swords and Batons: King, Queen, Cavalier, Jack, 10, 9, 8, **7**, 6, 5, 4, 3, 2, Ace.
- Cups and Coins reverse their numeral direction: Ace is immediately below the courts and 10 is lowest.

Thus the Seven of Batons:

- beats 6 through Ace of Batons;
- loses to 8, 9, 10 and all four Baton courts;
- cannot beat a trump;
- takes a trick only when it is the highest card of the led suit after trumping rules are applied.

Dummett’s account of the traditional order is reproduced in a chapter excerpt from *The Game of Tarot*. [Dummett excerpt](https://forum.tarothistory.com/viewtopic.php?t=1175)

Regional games differ. Modern French tarot uses French suits and ordinary black-suit ranking, so there is a Seven of Clubs rather than a Seven of Batons. Central European Tarock packs often omit low black-suit numerals; the surviving “seven” may be the lowest card of clubs depending on the pack and game.

## Counting value

**[Documented fact]** In most tarot scoring systems, numeral suit cards are low-value or “empty” cards. Their practical value lies chiefly in taking, shedding, or protecting tricks, not in individual scoring.

- **Minchiate:** only kings among the suits carry card points; ordinary pips, including the Seven of Batons, score zero individually. [Minchiate rules](https://www.pagat.com/tarot/minchiate.html)
- **Sicilian tarot:** the Seven participates in trick and grouped-card scoring but is not a high-value honour.
- **Bolognese tarocchino:** combinations and honours dominate scoring; the Seven is a retained ordinary pip.

## Fool and trump-order disputes

**[Documented fact]** The Fool normally functions as an excuse: it is played to a trick but does not win by rank and is usually recovered by its player subject to compensation rules. This is why historical packs could leave it unnumbered.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** Dummett classified early Italian trump orders into:

- Order A: principally Bologna and Florence;
- Order B: Ferrara;
- Order C: Milan and the ancestry of Marseille.

The location of Justice, Strength, and other trumps varies between orders. None changes the Seven of Batons because trump order and suit order are separate systems.

## The anonymous sermon

**[Documented fact]** The anti-gambling *De ludo* sermon lists standardized trump subjects and condemns the game. Modern scholarship dates the preaching between approximately 1480 and 1500 rather than securely to “c. 1470.” [Dummett translation and discussion](https://dummettsmondo.blogspot.com/2015/07/chapters-6-and-7-game-and-its-trump.html)

**Finding:** It names the trumps, not the Seven of Batons as a symbolic personality.

## Folengo and early fortune poetry

**[Documented fact]** Folengo’s *Caos del Triperuno* appeared in 1527 and contains sonnets generated around dealt tarot cards. This is important evidence for playful literary divination.

**Caution:** It does not establish the later fixed meaning *Valour*, Mars in Leo, or “stand your ground.” [Folengo tarot poetry](https://marykgreer.com/2008/05/28/tarot-poetry-the-sonnets-of-theofilo-folengo/)

## The number seven and Netzach

**[Documented within Golden Dawn doctrine]** Seven corresponds to Netzach because the minor cards 2–10 are assigned in order to Chokmah through Malkuth. All four Sevens are therefore Netzach cards.

**[Esoteric attribution]** Netzach means “victory” or “endurance” and is associated with Venus. This produces an apparent conflict: a Venusian sephira contains a card ruled by Mars.

Golden Dawn practice handles the layers hierarchically:

- 7 supplies the sephira Netzach;
- Wands supply Fire/Atziluth/Yod;
- the calendrical decan supplies Mars in Leo.

The result is not “Venus instead of Mars.” It is Mars in Leo operating in the Netzach level of the Fire suit.

## Yod and Tetragrammaton

**[Esoteric attribution]** Golden Dawn-derived tarot maps:

- Wands → Yod → Fire → Atziluth
- Cups → Heh → Water → Briah
- Swords → Vav → Air → Yetzirah
- Pentacles → final Heh → Earth → Assiah

Thus “Netzach of Yod” is shorthand for the seventh sephirotic mode in the fire/world-of-emanation column.

**Historical caveat:** No fifteenth-century card or rulebook connects *bastoni* to Yod. This is a nineteenth-century Hermetic synthesis.

## Decan

**[Documented Golden Dawn attribution]** The Seven of Wands governs the third decan of Leo, 20°–30°, ruled by Mars.

The deck’s practical seasonal span varies slightly by ephemeris and year but is usually placed approximately 12/13–22/23 August.

**Conflict:** Traditional astrological decan rulership schemes are not all identical. “Mars in the third face of Leo” here belongs specifically to the Chaldean planetary sequence adopted by the Golden Dawn.

## Hebrew letters and paths

There is no individual Hebrew letter or Tree-of-Life path assigned to the Seven of Wands in *Book T*. Letters and connecting paths belong primarily to the twenty-two trumps. The Seven instead corresponds to a sephira within a world.

Claims that the Seven of Wands is “the Hebrew letter X” ordinarily conflate trump and minor-arcana systems.

# Divinatory meanings by author

Short quotations are kept within copyright limits; longer entries are faithful summaries.

## Etteilla, 1780s–1790s

**Upright:** *Pourparler*—conversation, conference, discussion, negotiation, verbal exchange, commerce, correspondence.

**Reversed:** indecision, irresolution, uncertainty, perplexity, instability, vacillation.

**Status:** **[Documented tradition, exact edition-dependent wording].** The core pair is stable, but the long synonym lists come especially from the 1791 *Dictionnaire synonymique* and later Grand Etteilla editions, not necessarily from a single 1789 card.

[Grand Etteilla card 29 concordance](https://stolen-thyme.com/tarocchino-arlecchino/guidebook/29-le-sept-de-baton/)

## S. L. MacGregor Mathers, 1888

Mathers’s short pre-Golden-Dawn booklet gives the Batons Seven a semantic complex of:

- Upright: discussion, negotiation, conference, commerce, success, advantage.
- Reversed: perplexity, embarrassment, anxiety, and warning against indecision.

**Source relationship:** This is very close to the Etteilla stream and anticipates Waite’s public list. It is not yet the full *Book T* Mars-in-Leo doctrine. [Mathers, 1888 first-edition scan](https://www.howardnema.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/12/the-tarot-1888-by-macgregor-mathers-1.pdf)

## Golden Dawn *Book T*

> “Possible victory, depending upon the energy and courage exercised…”

Other terms include opposition, obstacles, difficulties, quarrelling, wrangling, threatening, and victory in small matters. Dignity determines which pole predominates.

The order used elemental dignities rather than a simple printed “reversed” list.

## A. E. Waite, 1910/1911

### Upright

> “It is a card of valour…”

Waite continues with discussion, “wordy strife,” negotiations, trade war, barter, competition, and success because the combatant occupies the top position.

### Reversed

> “Perplexity, embarrassments, anxiety.”

He also calls it a caution against indecision.

This is a synthesis of Etteilla/Mathers negotiation language and Golden Dawn valour/opposition.

## Aleister Crowley, 1944

Crowley does not use a simple upright/reversed system. His short divinatory register is:

- energy at its last gasp;
- struggle;
- possible victory;
- obstacles met by courage;
- victory in small matters;
- when ill-dignified, quarrelling.

His distinct contribution is to make *Valour* ambivalent:

> “Valour implies fear as well as courage.”

Courage matters because the larger energetic situation is already deteriorating.

## Paul Foster Case

**Attributable summary:** courage under opposition, possible victory through effort, and the Mars-in-Leo force expressed through Netzach of Fire.

**Source limitation:** I could not verify a freely accessible original Case page giving separate upright and reversed Seven-of-Wands wording. Later BOTA summaries often mix Case, Golden Dawn, and Waite. An exact quotation would therefore risk false attribution.

## Rachel Pollack, *Seventy-Eight Degrees of Wisdom*

**Attributable summary:** The image shows the need to defend an achieved position. The person has strength and advantage but cannot treat success as secure. The card can indicate courage, independence, and resistance to social pressure; negatively it can become chronic defensiveness.

Pollack’s importance lies in moving the card from prediction toward psychological and spiritual process. Her original editions do not establish a universal reversed formula comparable to Waite’s.

## Mary K. Greer, *Tarot for Your Self*

**Attributable summary:** Greer treats the card experientially—standing up for oneself, meeting challenge, defending beliefs, coping with competition, or maintaining a boundary. Reversal can indicate blocked courage, excessive defensiveness, exhaustion, avoidance, or conflict turned inward.

**Caution:** Different editions of *Tarot for Your Self* expanded reversal material. Online lists frequently paraphrase Greer without edition or page; they should not be quoted as though verbatim.

# Esoteric and symbolic readings

## Alchemical

### Etteilla

**[Esoteric attribution]** Etteilla embedded tarot in an Egyptian-Hermetic and alchemical cosmology during the 1780s. Yet no early source securely makes the Seven of Batons a particular chemical operation such as calcination.

### Modern alchemical reading

**[Modern invention]** Because Wands are Fire and seven represents destabilized development, modern authors sometimes call the card calcination, ordeal by fire, or the testing of a substance’s integrity.

Evidence: analogical fit only. No fifteenth-century alchemical programme has been demonstrated for the card.

## Kabbalistic

### First stages

- **1781, de Mellet:** maps the trumps to Hebrew letters and interprets the minors numerically.
- **1854–1856, Lévi:** makes tarot and Kabbalah mutually explanatory, chiefly through twenty-two trumps and letters.
- **1889, Papus:** systematizes the minors through number cycles and YHVH.
- **Golden Dawn:** fixes the surviving modern formula, Netzach of Yod.

### Card reading

**[Esoteric attribution]** Netzach is desire, instinct, affect, attraction, persistence, and victory. In Fire it becomes passionate will. Mars in Leo adds combative pride and personal courage.

The position is off the Tree’s central pillar. Crowley consequently sees imbalance: individual bravery compensating for the loss of harmonious force.

## Astrological

**[Golden Dawn attribution]** Mars in Leo:

- Mars: contest, assertion, heat, weapon, courage, quarrel.
- Leo: solar visibility, pride, rulership, dramatic self-expression, fixed fire.
- Third decan: Leo’s power late in its cycle, maintained under pressure.

This explains *Valour* more directly than historical baton iconography does.

**Conflict:** A dignified Mars can be courageous; an afflicted Mars can be belligerent. Hence *Book T* includes both bravery and wrangling.

## Hermetic

**[Esoteric attribution]** In the Hermetic Order’s design, seven is not self-sufficient perfection. Below Tiphareth, force disperses into more particular and unstable expressions. The central wand must command or withstand the six.

Waite externalizes this as one person against many; Harris internalizes it as coarse surviving energy pushing through collapsing order.

## Christian

**Historical layer:** The early game existed in a Christian society, and many trumps used Christian imagery. The Seven of Batons itself contains no stable Christian scene.

**[Traditional Christian symbolism]** Seven can signify creation completed by Sabbath, gifts of the Spirit, virtues, sacraments, or the opposing seven deadly sins. A staff can signify pastoral authority, pilgrimage, punishment, or the rod of Jesse.

**Finding:** No early source connects those particular Christian sevens to this pip. Christian readings of “defending faith” are later applications, especially to Smith’s martial scene.

## Jungian

**[Modern invention / psychological tradition]** Jung did not publish a card-by-card tarot commentary. “Jungian Seven of Wands” readings are work by later analysts and tarot writers.

Common readings include:

- ego defending a newly conscious value against collective pressure;
- individuation requiring separation from group expectations;
- projection, with the unseen attackers representing disowned aggression;
- shadow inflation, where every disagreement is experienced as attack;
- healthy boundary formation versus rigid defensive identity.

Pollack, Greer, Sallie Nichols, and later therapeutic-tarot writers helped normalize this approach, but it should not be attributed directly to Jung.

# Names, regional terminology, and numbering

## Italian

- *Sette di Bastoni*
- Suit: *Bastoni*
- Pack/game: *carte da trionfi* in the fifteenth century; *tarocchi* becomes usual in the early sixteenth century
- Singular *tarocco* may refer to a card or the game/deck depending on context

## French

- *Sept de Bâtons*
- *Baguettes* is a later occult translation choice
- *Tarot de Marseille* is a nineteenth-century classificatory label, not what Noblet printed as a lineage name in 1650. The expression is commonly credited to Romain Merlin in 1856 and was later popularized by occultists and Paul Marteau.

## Spanish

- *Siete de Bastos*
- Ordinary Spanish packs represent *bastos* as separate knobbly clubs.
- Spanish-language editions of RWS often preserve *Bastos* even though Smith’s objects are leafy staffs.

## German

- *Sieben der Stäbe*
- German-language occult decks may also use *Stäbe*, *Ruten*, or occasionally *Zepter*.
- In historical Tarock gaming, French-suited clubs rather than Latin batons are common; translation must therefore distinguish occult tarot from game packs.

## Number variants

The card is consistently seven within complete 56-card suit structures. There is no historically significant VIII/VII numbering dispute analogous to Justice and Strength. The main variation is whether the number is printed at all: many early and Marseille pips rely on counting the suit signs.

# People and works

## Directly connected

### Bonifacio Bembo and workshop

Associated with several Milanese luxury decks and with the transition from ordinary suit signs to courtly painted tarot. Whether every card assigned to “Bembo” is by Bonifacio’s own hand remains an attribution question.

### The anonymous Sola Busca designer and engraver

The pack’s authorship remains disputed. Nicola di Maestro Antonio and Ferrarese/Venetian humanist circles have figured in proposed attributions. The Seven participates in the deck’s unusual programme of antique military and historical figures.

### Jean Noblet, Jean Dodal, Nicolas Conver

Printers/cardmakers who transmit the interlaced-baton visual grammar on which later esotericists could build.

### Jean-Baptiste Alliette, “Etteilla”

First major professional tarot occultist and systematic cartomantic author. For this card he established the negotiation/indecision stream.

### S. L. MacGregor Mathers

Published the English Etteilla-derived list in 1888 and, through Golden Dawn materials, helped create the title *Lord of Valour* and Mars-in-Leo structure.

### Pamela Colman Smith

Created the most influential Seven-of-Wands image in 1909. Her authorship is material: Waite supplied programme and meanings, but Smith devised the finished composition, gesture, costume, spatial drama, and ambiguity.

### Arthur Edward Waite

Published the image’s first authoritative verbal description and combined several inherited meaning streams.

### Frieda Harris and Aleister Crowley

Harris converted Crowley’s layered instructions into the geometric *Valour*. The finished card must be treated as a collaboration, not merely an illustration of Crowley’s prose.

### Paul Foster Case

Carried Golden Dawn tarot into American occult education and the BOTA curriculum.

### Paul Marteau

Established a colour-and-number symbolic reading of Marseille pips independent of Smith’s scene.

### Rachel Pollack and Mary K. Greer

Major transmitters of psychological, experiential, feminist, and self-reflective tarot in the late twentieth century.

### Alejandro Jodorowsky and Marianne Costa

Popularized reading Marseille pips as stages in numerical and suit development.

## Literary and cinematic works named in the brief

### Italo Calvino, *The Castle of Crossed Destinies*

**[Documented fact]** Calvino constructed narratives from tarot layouts, using the Visconti-Sforza and Marseille traditions in different portions of the work.

**Finding:** The work is relevant to tarot as combinatorial literature, but I found no reliable source making the Seven of Wands a uniquely privileged card or assigning it a stable Calvino meaning.

### T. S. Eliot, *The Waste Land*

Madame Sosostris names “the drowned Phoenician Sailor,” Belladonna, “the man with three staves,” the Wheel, and the one-eyed merchant, and says the Hanged Man is absent.

**Finding:** “The man with three staves” is normally related to the Three of Wands, not the Seven. Eliot should not be attached specifically to this card.

### W. B. Yeats

Yeats was involved in the Golden Dawn and therefore inhabited the milieu in which *Lord of Valour* was created.

**Finding:** No Yeats text was located that singles out the Seven of Wands. Institutional proximity is not card-specific authorship.

### Ingmar Bergman

Bergman’s *The Seventh Seal* employs medieval Christian allegory, chess, death, and apocalypse.

**Finding:** No evidence was located that it quotes the Seven of Wands. The number seven and medieval appearance are insufficient.

### Jodorowsky’s films

Jodorowsky’s cinema contains Hermetic, Christian, alchemical, and tarot-like images, and he later became a major Marseille teacher.

**Finding:** I found no verified shot or production statement identifying a direct Seven-of-Wands quotation.

## Paintings and buildings

No pre-1909 painting or building could be securely demonstrated as the source of Smith’s one-against-six composition.

Modern murals, tattoos, posters, album illustrations, and deck art frequently quote her hilltop defender, but without a specified work it is not possible to distinguish deliberate quotation from generic combat imagery.

**Negative finding:** Claims that the scene reproduces a specific Renaissance battle painting, stage production, or architectural sculpture were not traceable to a primary statement by Smith or Waite.

# Controversies and disputes

## Egyptian origin

### The claim

**[Legend]** Court de Gébelin declared in 1781 that tarot was a surviving Egyptian book. He proposed an Egyptian derivation of the name and interpreted the suits as Egyptian estates: sword/nobility, cup/priesthood, baton/agriculture, coin/commerce.

His own opening frames the discovery as an Egyptian book that had escaped the destruction of the ancient libraries. [Court de Gébelin text](https://www.tarock.info/gebelin.htm)

### Evidence

None of the relevant hieroglyphic evidence was supplied; Egyptian hieroglyphs had not yet been deciphered. The documented card chronology leads instead to fifteenth-century northern Italy. Dummett observes that Gébelin and Mellet gave supposed Egyptian word derivations without hieroglyphic forms. [Dummett, “Origins of Tarot”](https://www.nybooks.com/articles/1981/05/14/origins-of-tarot/)

### Relevance to the Seven

The claim gives the baton an Egyptian agricultural identity but does not yield *Valour* or Mars in Leo. Those are Golden Dawn additions approximately a century later.

## Romani or “Gypsy” origin

### The claim

**[Legend]** Tarot was allegedly carried from Egypt by “Gypsies,” a term historically based on the false European belief that Romani people came from Egypt.

### Development

The association becomes especially prominent in nineteenth-century French writing, including Jean-Alexandre Vaillant and Papus’s *Tarot des Bohémiens*.

### Evidence

There is no contemporary documentation connecting Romani communities with tarot’s fifteenth-century invention. Linguistic and historical evidence places Romani origins in South Asia; tarot documentation begins in Italian courts and cities.

## Cathar claim

**[Legend / modern speculative tradition]** Some writers treat tarot as encoded Cathar doctrine, often reading the Popess, Pope, Devil, Tower, or Grail-like Cups as evidence.

### Evidence

No fifteenth-century document links tarot production with surviving Cathars or a Cathar transmission. The Seven of Batons supplies no distinctive Cathar iconography. Applying “defence against persecution” to Smith’s 1909 scene is a modern thematic reading, not evidence of origin.

## Templar claim

**[Legend / modern invention]** Tarot is sometimes said to preserve Knights Templar secrets transmitted after the order’s suppression.

### Evidence

There is a chronological gap of more than a century between the 1312 suppression and documented *trionfi* packs. No chain of manuscripts, card designs, patrons, or rules connects the Templars with the Seven of Batons or tarot’s invention.

## Kabbalistic origin

**[Disputed historical claim / valid later tradition]** Tarot unquestionably acquired a rich Kabbalistic system. The disputed point is whether Kabbalah caused the deck’s original structure.

The earliest explicit Hebrew-letter tarot alignment appears in de Mellet’s 1781 essay, more than three centuries after the earliest packs. Lévi, Papus, the Golden Dawn, Case, and Crowley expanded it.

Verdict:

- Kabbalistic Seven of Wands as an occult practice: documented.
- Kabbalah as its fifteenth-century design source: unsupported.

## Sola Busca as Smith’s Seven source

This dossier’s dedicated check found:

- British Museum access before Smith’s deck: documented.
- Sola Busca influence on some RWS minors: strong reconstruction.
- Exact Seven-to-Seven dependence: not demonstrated.
- Golden Dawn *Book T* as the immediate conceptual source: strongly supported by wording and structure.

The claim should therefore be marked **misattribution unless new visual or archival evidence appears**.

## Misprints and details that became traditions

### Mismatched shoes

A visual detail became a widely repeated doctrine of surprise or unreadiness. No authorial explanation survives.

### “Seven enemies”

Only six staffs are visible. Their owners are not. They may represent six persons, a crowd, competing claims, or merely emblematic opposition. “Six enemies” is an inference, not a depicted fact.

### Mars in “the first” or “second” decan of Leo

These are usually copying errors. *Book T* specifies 20°–30° Leo: the third ten-degree division.

### “Netzach equals Mars”

Incorrect compression. Netzach is conventionally Venusian; Mars comes from the decan. The card combines layers.

### Conver 1760 versus later impressions

Some surviving Conver-labelled packs were printed from blocks bearing 1760 but produced later. A block date is not automatically the physical impression date.

### Thoth published “in 1944”

The book and limited plates appeared in 1944; the complete commercial deck’s publication was posthumous. Conversely, saying simply “published 1969” omits the edition’s limited character and later mass distribution.

# Historiographical corrections

## Michael Dummett

Dummett’s central correction was methodological: tarot history must begin with rules, inventories, surviving packs, regional card orders, and terminology. His reconstruction places tarot’s origin in fifteenth-century northern Italian game culture and rejects an occult origin for lack of early evidence.

## Ronald Decker and Thierry Depaulis

With Dummett, Decker and Depaulis traced occult tarot author by author in *A Wicked Pack of Cards*. Their history shows that the Egyptian and Kabbalistic traditions developed through identifiable modern texts rather than an unbroken secret lineage.

## Stuart R. Kaplan

Kaplan’s *Encyclopedia of Tarot* reproduced and catalogued large numbers of historical decks, making comparative iconography widely available. Some dates and attributions in earlier volumes require revision as new archival evidence has appeared.

## Robert M. Place

Place situates tarot imagery in Renaissance art, Christianity, Neoplatonism, and emblematic culture while rejecting literal Egyptian-origin claims. His work is more receptive than Dummett’s to symbolic and initiatory interpretation, but not to unsupported ancient transmission.

## Helen Farley

Farley studies tarot’s transition from Renaissance court game to modern occult and New Age object. Her work is especially useful for distinguishing historical production from later religious identity.

## Andrea Vitali and Le Tarot association

Vitali’s scholarship gathers Italian archival, theatrical, literary, and iconographic sources. It is valuable for early documents and Renaissance cultural context, while individual iconographic proposals should still be distinguished from documentary proof.

# Open questions

1. **Exact priority among the earliest Milanese packs.** Brambilla and Cary–Yale dating remains debated, affecting which physical Seven of Batons is earliest.

2. **Authorship of the Sola Busca.** Its engraver, intellectual designer, and patronage network have not been conclusively identified.

3. **The full programme of the Sola Busca Seven.** Its individual figure and objects invite historical identification, but competing Roman, biblical, alchemical, and Ferrarese political readings have not produced consensus.

4. **Smith’s working process.** No surviving card-by-card design notes explain whether Waite dictated the Seven’s exact scene or Smith independently dramatized *Book T*.

5. **Mismatched footwear.** Deliberate iconographic signal, ordinary costume variation, drawing shorthand, or colour-production effect? No decisive evidence.

6. **Exact first circulation of *Book T*.** The teachings developed in manuscript and ritual use before authorized publication. Dating a particular wording depends on manuscript recension.

7. **Case’s card-specific wording.** A verified scan of the relevant original BOTA lesson or *Oracle of the Tarot* edition is needed before quoting his Seven verbatim.

8. **Pollack and Greer edition variants.** Their books exist in revised editions; card summaries and reversals should be cited to a specified printing rather than amalgamated from web lists.

9. **Thoth complete-deck publication.** Bibliographies distinguish prototypes, test printings, small 1969 issues, and later commercial editions differently.

10. **Modern cultural quotations.** Many visual works resemble Smith’s defender, but resemblance alone cannot demonstrate conscious quotation.

# Findings in compact form

- **Earliest identity:** an ordinary Seven of *bastoni* in a northern Italian trick-taking pack.
- **Earliest surviving candidate:** Brera-Brambilla, approximately 1440s.
- **Earliest complete scenic context:** Sola Busca, engraved 1491.
- **Marseille image:** six interlaced diagonal batons and one vertical central baton with floral ornament.
- **Etteilla meaning:** conversation/negotiation; reversed indecision.
- **Golden Dawn transformation:** *Lord of Valour*, Mars in Leo, Netzach of Yod; possible victory through courage.
- **Waite–Smith transformation:** a lone defender on higher ground attacked by six raised staffs.
- **Sola Busca claim:** general influence probable, card-specific derivation of the Seven unverified.
- **Thoth transformation:** a crude central club before six ceremonial wands; courage amid energetic degeneration.
- **Historical game value:** ordinary non-trump pip, normally low or zero individual points.
- **Modern dominant meaning:** stand one’s ground, defend an achievement or boundary, resist pressure.
- **Historical-origin verdict:** Egyptian, Romani, Cathar, Templar, and primordial Kabbalistic origins are later legends or disputed constructions; the documented history begins in fifteenth-century Italy.
