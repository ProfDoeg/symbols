# Three of Wands: Research Dossier

## Method and evidentiary labels

This dossier distinguishes six kinds of statement:

- **Documented fact** — supported by a surviving card, contemporary record, rulebook, or author’s own text.
- **Scholarly reconstruction** — an inference by modern historians from surviving objects and documents.
- **Tradition** — an interpretation transmitted through card-reading practice without secure evidence of an ancient origin.
- **Esoteric attribution** — a deliberately constructed occult correspondence.
- **Disputed claim** — proposed but contested or inadequately demonstrated.
- **Legend / modern invention** — a story contradicted by the chronology or first appearing in modern esoteric or commercial literature.

The Three of Wands is a suit card, not a trump. Consequently, questions concerning the numbering and renaming of the Papess, Pope, Tower, Fool, Justice, Strength, Emperor, and Star affect the decks containing it but do not change this card’s identity or rank.

The Golden Dawn identification is explicit in the text conventionally called *Book T*: “The Lord of Established Strength,” with a white radiating angelic hand holding three wands, two crossed and one upright. It assigns the card to the Sun in Aries. [Book T/Liber 78 transcription](https://invisiblehouse.org/pdf/liber078.pdf)

### The suit name and what it originally meant

**Documented fact:** *Bastoni* meant sticks, clubs, batons, or staves. The suit belongs to the ordinary Italian/Spanish family of playing-card signs. Its first function was not to designate mystical “creativity” but to identify one of four suits.

**Scholarly reconstruction:** European Latin-suited packs probably developed through adaptation of earlier Islamic playing cards. The tarot pack added a trump sequence and Fool to an already recognizable four-suit structure. The Morgan describes a complete early pack as twenty-one trumps, Fool, and four suits of fourteen cards: cups, staves, coins, and swords. [Morgan Library](https://www.themorgan.org/manuscript/158525)

**Esoteric attribution:** Wands = Fire and Yod is not documented in fifteenth-century play. It belongs to nineteenth-century Hermetic-Qabalistic system building and was codified most influentially by the Golden Dawn.

# Iconography deck by deck

## Precursors and fifteenth-century hand-painted packs

### Marziano da Tortona’s game, approximately 1412–1425

**Documented fact:** Marziano described a special pack made for Filippo Maria Visconti, with sixteen classical gods as trump-like cards and four suits represented by birds. Michelino da Besozzo painted it. The cards have not survived.

**Finding:** There was therefore no documented “Three of Wands” in Marziano’s pack. It is a precursor to tarot because Jacopo Antonio Marcello later described it as an unusual kind of *triumphorum*, but its suits were not the later standard cups, coins, swords, and batons. Treating it as the first Three of Wands would be anachronistic. [Le Tarot—Italian origins](https://letarot.it/Le-origini-italiane-del-Ludus-Triumphorum_pag_pg439_ita.aspx), [Pratesi study](https://naibi.net/A/25-FIRSTARO-Z.pdf)

### Visconti di Modrone / Cary–Yale, probably Milan, before 1447

These names refer to the same surviving group, now at Yale’s Beinecke Library.

**Documented fact:** The pack is incomplete and unusual: its surviving cards indicate six court cards per suit rather than the standard four, including male and female counterparts. It is usually placed in the reign of Filippo Maria Visconti and sometimes dated near 1441.

**Iconographic tendency:** Surviving numeral cards are luxury pips—gilded suit signs distributed ornamentally on painted grounds, with Visconti devices and vegetal decoration—not narrative scenes.

**Caution:** Published reconstructions sometimes supply missing cards or normalize the pack to 78 cards. A reproduced “Three of Wands” must therefore be checked against the Beinecke object list before being called original.

### Brera–Brambilla, Milan, before 1447

**Documented fact:** Forty-eight cards survive at the Pinacoteca di Brera. The deck was probably made for Filippo Maria Visconti. Brera identifies it as a late-Gothic ducal pack and distinguishes it from the complete Sola Busca acquired by the Italian state in 2009. [Pinacoteca di Brera](https://pinacotecabrera.org/en/news/mostra/il-segreto-dei-segreti-i-tarocchi-sola-busca/)

**Iconography:** The numeral suits are heraldic and decorative. Batons are actual staff-like emblems arranged against a sumptuous ground; there is no Waite-like merchant or landscape.

### Visconti–Sforza / Pierpont Morgan–Bergamo pack, after about 1450

**Documented fact:** Seventy-four original cards survive in three collections: thirty-five at the Morgan, twenty-six at the Accademia Carrara, and thirteen in the Colleoni collection. The missing originals are Devil, Tower, Three of Swords, and Knight of Coins. [Morgan Library](https://www.themorgan.org/manuscript/158525), [Morgan shop catalogue](https://shop.themorgan.org/collections/gifts/products/619)

**Three of Batons:** Three heraldic staff forms are disposed decoratively on a gold ground and surrounded by floral ornament. It is a literal countable pip. It has no printed title, astrological glyph, keyword, landscape, or human figure.

**Meaning of the design:** Primarily suit and rank identification, elevated into a courtly luxury object through gold, heraldry, and ornament. Modern psychological meanings cannot be demonstrated from the image alone.

**Attribution dispute:** The early Visconti groups were long assigned collectively to Bonifacio Bembo. The Morgan notes a more recent attribution of its principal cards to Francesco Zavattari, while six replacement cards have been attributed to Antonio Cicognara. This dispute concerns workshop and dating, not an occult program.

### Ferrarese and Este cards, later fifteenth century

Several isolated hand-painted and engraved cards are associated with Ferrara or the Este court.

**Documented fact:** The Este account books record purchases of *carte da trionfi*. On 28 July 1442 Marchionne Burdochi supplied “uno paro de carte da trionfi” for Ercole and Sigismondo d’Este. [Le Tarot—document transcription](https://letarot.it/Le-origini-italiane-del-Ludus-Triumphorum_pag_pg439_ita.aspx)

**Iconography:** Surviving Este-related suit cards retain the ornamental pip principle. No securely identified Ferrarese Three of Batons supplies the merchant-overlooking-ships composition.

### “Mantegna Tarocchi,” approximately 1460s–1470s

**Documented fact:** These are fifty educational or emblematic engravings arranged in five ten-card series, not a playable tarot pack. They have neither the normal four suits nor a Three of Wands.

**Finding:** They are relevant to Renaissance emblematic culture and later occult comparisons, but not to the descent of this pip card. The conventional name “Mantegna Tarocchi” is itself misleading.

## Sola Busca, 1491 engraving; painted approximately 1490s–early 1500s

**Documented fact:** The Sola Busca is the earliest surviving complete 78-card tarot and the earliest complete deck in which all numeral cards receive figural illustrations. The Italian state acquired the painted pack in 2009 for the Pinacoteca di Brera. Current scholarship generally dates the engravings to 1491, probably Ferrara, with painting somewhat later, probably in Venice. [Pinacoteca di Brera](https://pinacotecabrera.org/en/news/mostra/il-segreto-dei-segreti-i-tarocchi-sola-busca/), [Public Domain Review](https://publicdomainreview.org/collection/sola-busca)

**Three of Batons:** A Renaissance figure appears with three large staff or pole forms in a staged, architectural or outdoor setting. The card is narrative or emblematic rather than a simple countable pip. Its inscriptions and costumes belong to the Sola Busca’s pseudo-historical world of Roman and biblical names, military action, labor, and enigmatic ritual.

### Was it the direct source of Pamela Colman Smith’s Three of Wands?

**Disputed claim:** The brief’s proposed lineage—“the Sola Busca pip as the source of Pamela Colman Smith’s scene”—could not be verified card for card.

What is documented:

1. In 1907, the British Museum received photographs of the complete Sola Busca pack.
2. Waite and Smith worked in London shortly afterward.
3. Several Waite–Smith minors closely resemble Sola Busca compositions.
4. The strongest routinely cited parallels include the **Three of Swords** and the burden-bearing composition behind Waite–Smith’s **Ten of Wands**, not the Three of Wands. [Sola Busca overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sola_Busca_tarot)

What remains unproved:

- No surviving letter, diary, design note, or statement by Smith says that she based her Three of Wands on the Sola Busca Three.
- The figures and compositions are not close enough to establish direct copying without additional evidence.

**Conclusion:** General Sola Busca influence on the Waite–Smith minor arcana is a reasonable scholarly inference; a specific Three-to-Three derivation is presently an unsupported or at least insufficiently documented extension of that inference.

## Rosenwald and Budapest sheets, approximately 1500

### Rosenwald sheets

**Documented fact:** Three block-printed sheets survive in the Rosenwald Collection at the National Gallery of Art. One contains trumps and queens; two contain most of the suit cards and may come from a related but not necessarily identical pack. Modern reconstructed decks add missing material. [Rosenwald reconstruction discussion](https://tarot-heritage.com/2017/10/31/the-rosenwald-deck/)

**Three of Batons:** Where recoverable, the suit card is a plain woodcut pip: three club/staff signs arranged for immediate recognition, without a figure or divinatory caption.

**Caution:** Images in a modern “Rosenwald deck” can include cards recreated by a restorer. Those additions are modern reconstructions, not surviving impressions.

### Budapest sheet

**Documented fact:** The Budapest material consists of early printed tarot sheets, usually placed around 1500 and associated with northern Italian production.

**Iconography:** The suit cards belong to the economical woodcut-pip tradition. A modern colored or fully assembled Budapest deck is a reconstruction from sheets, not an intact historical pack.

## Minchiate, Bolognese, and Sicilian patterns

### Minchiate

**Documented fact:** Minchiate expanded the tarot structure to 97 cards by adding trumps, but retained four Latin suits. Its numeral cards continued ordinary suit arrangements.

**Three of Batons:** A low baton pip when present in the full pack, normally three conventional staff signs with modest ornament. It was not one of Minchiate’s distinctive added trumps and carried no special occult name.

### Tarocchino Bolognese

**Documented fact:** The reduced Bolognese pack removes the twos through fives of every suit. Therefore the **Three of Batons is absent** from the standard 62-card Tarocchino pack. This is not symbolic suppression; it is pack reduction for play. [Tarocchini rules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tarocchini)

### Sicilian Tarot

**Documented fact:** The traditional 63-card Sicilian pack retains only 5–10 in batons, swords, and cups; only coins retains a Four. The Three of Batons is therefore absent. The Fool is called *il Fuggitivo* or Sicilian *lu Fujutu*, while the low trump *Miseria* stands below numbered trumps. [Sicilian Tarocchi rules](https://www.pagat.com/tarot/sicilian.html)

## Tarot de Marseille lineage

### Jean Noblet, Paris, approximately 1650

**Documented fact:** The deck belongs to what later scholars call Tarot de Marseille Type I. “Tarot de Marseille” was not necessarily Noblet’s own product name.

**Three of Batons:** A vertical baton crosses or is superimposed on two diagonally crossing batons. Leaves or foliate sprays emerge from the center. It is symmetrical, non-scenic, and designed to be recognized from either orientation. The card may carry **III** but no title.

### Jean Dodal, Lyon, approximately 1701–1715

The same basic arrangement becomes broader and more vigorous: two crossed red batons, a central vertical baton, blue/yellow joints, and stylized foliage. Differences are workshop cutting and color practice, not documented changes in occult doctrine.

### Nicolas Conver, Marseille, traditionally 1760/1761

The Conver pattern stabilizes the familiar Type II pip:

- one vertical central baton;
- two batons crossing in an X;
- lance-like extremities;
- colored binding rings;
- paired leaves and small flame- or petal-like ornaments at the center;
- Roman **III** placed laterally.

A useful warning is that the often-reproduced BnF “Conver” witness appears to have been printed during the later continuation of the Conver workshop, approximately 1809–1833, even though the model is conventionally called “Conver 1760.” [Detailed BnF-based description](https://www.marselia.app/cards/trois-de-baton), [Conver reproduction record](https://dusserre.fr/cartes/tarot-de-marseille-nicolas-conver/)

### Grimaud / Paul Marteau, 1930 and later twentieth-century editions

**Documented fact:** Marteau standardized an “Ancien Tarot de Marseille” for Grimaud using a Conver-related model, regularized lines, and a controlled modern printing palette.

**Three of Batons:** The geometric floral pip survives. Cleaner registration and stronger color blocks make it look more schematic than many hand-stenciled ancestors.

### Camoin–Jodorowsky, 1997

**Documented fact:** Philippe Camoin and Alejandro Jodorowsky issued a reconstructed/restored Marseille tarot.

**Disputed claim:** It is marketed as recovering ancient details from many decks. Historians and practitioners disagree over whether it is best described as restoration, synthesis, or a new esoteric edition.

**Three of Batons:** The traditional crossed-plus-vertical structure is retained, but coloration and small internal details are deliberately sharpened and reinterpreted. Jodorowsky and Marianne Costa subsequently read Marseille pips through number, suit, orientation, color, and visual “energy.” That interpretive system is modern, even when applied to old imagery.

## Besançon pattern

Besançon decks replace the Papess and Pope with **Juno** and **Jupiter**, probably to avoid controversial Catholic figures in Protestant or religiously mixed regions. This pattern flourished in Alsace, Switzerland, and eastern France during the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries.

**Relevance to this card:** The Three of Batons generally remains a Marseille-derived floral pip. Juno/Jupiter substitutions change trumps II and V, not the suit card.

## Etteilla’s Grand Etteilla, approximately 1789

**Documented fact:** Jean-Baptiste Alliette, “Etteilla,” created the first tarot pack designed expressly for divination. Its imagery, ordering, upright and reversed keywords, elemental material, and purported Egyptian writing were reworked from the ground up. It appeared around 1789 after his instructional *cahiers* of 1783–1785. [Etteilla overview](https://hermetism.org/encyclopedia/jean-baptiste-alliette-etteilla/)

**Three of Batons:** The card retains three baton pips but adds printed divinatory information. In the transmitted Etteilla family its key pair is:

- upright: **Entreprise** — enterprise or undertaking;
- reversed: **Interruption** — interruption, cessation, or an ending of adversity.

Some editions invert or otherwise mishandle orientation, so surviving card faces and accompanying booklet must be checked together. A modern French synopsis preserves “Entreprise” upright and “Interruption” reversed. [Etteilla-derived meanings](https://www.sorcellerie.net/forum/attachment.php?aid=14)

**Iconographic change:** The card ceases to be merely a game pip and becomes a textual divinatory instrument. Etteilla’s cipher-like marks were represented as Egyptian writing, but their relationship to deciphered Egyptian is unsupported.

## Oswald Wirth, 1889

**Documented fact:** Wirth’s 1889 deck, designed with Stanislas de Guaita, consisted principally of the twenty-two trumps. It did **not** supply an original complete illustrated Three of Wands.

**Finding:** Claims about “Wirth’s Three of Wands” usually refer to later expanded decks, publishers’ completions, or readings extrapolated from Wirth’s numerical and suit symbolism. They are not cards from the original 1889 set.

## Golden Dawn designs: Book T, Wang, and Cicero

### Book T, late nineteenth century

The Golden Dawn transformed a plain pip into a diagram of force:

> “A White Radiating Angelic Hand … grasping three wands in the centre (two crossed, the third upright).”

The title is **Lord of Established Strength**. The assignment is Sun in Aries. [Book T/Liber 78](https://invisiblehouse.org/pdf/liber078.pdf)

**Iconography:** three wands, an angelic hand, clouds, and radiance. In a fully colored temple deck, the background and secondary colors follow the Order’s color scales.

**Meaning of the change:** The Marseille geometry becomes a ceremonial emblem. Three is Binah; Wands is Fire/Atziluth; the decan is governed by the Sun in Aries.

### Robert Wang, *The Golden Dawn Tarot*, 1977

**Modern reconstruction:** Wang worked from Golden Dawn documentation and Israel Regardie’s guidance. His Three of Wands visually realizes the Book T description—radiating hand and crossed staffs—rather than reconstructing one unique surviving nineteenth-century master card.

### Sandra Tabatha and Chic Cicero, 1990s

**Modern reconstruction:** The Ciceros’ deck likewise translates Golden Dawn written and color instructions into a usable modern pack. Differences between Wang and Cicero concern artistic realization, color scale, line, and emblem placement, not different decanic attributions.

## Waite–Smith, 1909; *Pictorial Key*, 1910/1911

### Pamela Colman Smith’s picture

**Documented fact:** A robed man stands on high ground with his back to the viewer. Three staffs are planted in the earth. He rests a hand on the nearest. Below him are water, hills, and ships.

Waite’s own description is:

> “A calm, stately personage, with his back turned, looking from a cliff’s edge at ships passing over the sea.”

He adds: “Three staves are planted in the ground, and he leans slightly on one of them.” [*Pictorial Key* text](https://citeseerx.ist.psu.edu/document?doi=7a15961664bced51d1162198bce46d173e40dd41&repid=rep1&type=pdf), [1909 card scan](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:RWS1909_-_Wands_03.jpeg)

### Sources of the composition

- **Documented source in meaning:** Waite inherits “enterprise,” “commerce,” and “established strength” from the Etteilla–Mathers–Golden Dawn stream.
- **Probable formal source:** The three-staff configuration translates the Golden Dawn emblem into a naturalistic scene.
- **Scholarly reconstruction:** Smith probably knew photographs of the Sola Busca deck and borrowed from it elsewhere.
- **Disputed:** No decisive evidence was found that the Sola Busca Three specifically supplied this scene.

### What changed

The card became a temporal narrative: something has already been established, while ships and a distant horizon imply commerce, expectation, travel, and future development. The landscape makes Waite’s inherited keywords visually readable.

## BOTA, Paul Foster Case and Jessie Burns Parke

**Documented fact:** Case’s BOTA tarot, drawn by Jessie Burns Parke, substantially follows Waite–Smith outlines while altering occult details and leaving cards for students to color.

**Three of Wands:** It remains close to the Waite–Smith merchant/horizon scene rather than returning to a Marseille abstraction.

**Esoteric framework:** Wands = Fire/Yod/Atziluth; Three = Binah. Case retained the Golden Dawn decanic architecture but rejected some Waitean concealments and disagreed with Crowley’s later Heh/Tzaddi revision.

---

## Thoth Tarot, painted 1938–1943; deck published 1969

**Documented fact:** Aleister Crowley designed the deck with Frieda Harris. Harris painted the cards between 1938 and 1943. Crowley’s *Book of Thoth* appeared in 1944; the complete deck was not commercially issued until 1969.

**Title:** **VIRTUE**.

**Image:** Three orange-red, green, and gold lotus wands spread from a central solar region. Their flowering ends establish a threefold rotational or triangular balance. The background suggests solar heat and the opening energy of spring rather than a literal landscape.

Crowley writes:

> “This card refers to Binah in the suit of Fire, and so represents the establishment of primeval Energy.”

And:

> “It refers to the Sun in Aries, the Sign in which he is exalted.”

He explains the flowering lotus by saying that the Sun has “enkindled the Great Mother.” [*Book of Thoth* text](https://www.rahoorkhuit.net/library/crowley/equinox/volume3/vol_3_no_05.pdf)

**Meaning of “Virtue”:** Crowley uses it as inherent efficacy, vigor, or realized character—not merely conventional moral niceness. The will of the Two has entered Binah and become fertile manifestation.

**Tzaddi/Heh note:** Crowley’s swap affects the Emperor and Star correspondences, not the Three of Wands’ direct assignment. The Three remains Sun in Aries. However, its network of related trump symbols changes because Crowley associates Aries with the Star/Heh and Aquarius with the Emperor/Tzaddi in his revised scheme. This is a secondary systemic effect, not a reassignment of the pip.

## Notable modern redesigns

### Jodorowsky–Costa Marseille reading

A plain floral pip is treated as a composition of energetic relations. Three signifies the first creative outgrowth after the polarity of Two; Wands signifies creative, sexual, instinctive, or vital energy.

**Modern invention/tradition:** This is a coherent contemporary hermeneutic, not a meaning documented on seventeenth-century card faces.

### Rachel Pollack

Pollack reads the Waite–Smith sequence narratively: the Two establishes dominion or choice; the Three turns outward toward action, trade, exploration, and the future. Her influential *Seventy-Eight Degrees of Wisdom* appeared in two parts in 1980 and 1983. [Pollack’s bibliography](https://www.rachelpollack.com/tarot/tarot.html)

### Feminist, queer, and multicultural RWS descendants

Modern decks frequently replace Waite–Smith’s solitary European male merchant with:

- women or nonbinary figures;
- communities launching a venture;
- astronauts, travelers, activists, or emigrants;
- ships replaced by aircraft, roads, portals, or digital networks.

These are **modern inventions** extending the inherited motifs of horizon, enterprise, and expansion. They are social reinterpretations, not recoveries of a lost Renaissance meaning.

# The game

## Original function

**Documented fact:** The Three of Batons began as a playing card. Fifteenth-century evidence concerns the game of *trionfi*, not divination. The Morgan explicitly notes that Renaissance tarot cards were game equipment rather than fortune-tellers’ apparatus. [Morgan Library](https://www.themorgan.org/manuscript/158525)

## Rank

In traditional Latin-suited tarot games:

- swords and batons are “long” suits;
- cups and coins are “round” suits;
- long suits ordinarily rank downward from King, Queen, Knight, Jack, 10, 9 … 1;
- round suits often reverse their numeral order after the courts: Ace, 2, 3 … 10.

Thus, in a full pack, the Three of Batons is generally a **very low card**, beating only Two and Ace in its own long suit where those cards are retained.

In Piedicavallo, the Ace through Four of swords and batons are removed; consequently the Three is absent. Remaining ordinary numeral cards count one point. [Piedicavallo rules](https://www.pagat.com/tarot/piedicavallo.html)

In the earliest printed French rules associated with the Abbé de Marolles, the lowest three cards were removed from all suits, so there too the Three of Batons was absent. Long suits ranked King through Four. [Marolles rules](https://www.pagat.com/tarot/marolles.html)

## Point value

There is no universal “three points because it is a Three” rule.

Common historical systems count:

- kings and high trumps heavily;
- court cards progressively;
- ordinary numeral cards as low-value counters, often effectively one point;
- cards in packets of two or three, subtracting a fixed amount to avoid fractions.

The Three of Batons is normally valuable chiefly as:

1. a low suit card;
2. a card used to follow suit;
3. a possible discard;
4. part of a sequence or combination in games that score combinations.

## Fool

The Fool is normally not a conventional highest or lowest trump. In many tarot games it excuses its player from following suit and is recovered after the trick, usually in exchange for a low card. Regional exceptions and late game rules vary.

**Relevance:** The Fool’s numbering as 0, 22, or unnumbered has no effect on the Three of Wands’ numeral rank.

## Trump-order disputes

Dummett classified early trump orders into three regional families:

- **Type A:** associated especially with Bologna and Florence;
- **Type B:** associated with Ferrara;
- **Type C:** associated with Milan and the later Marseille family.

Justice, Fortitude/Strength, Temperance, Angel, and World occupy different relative positions. In some A/B orders Justice is very high; in the C/Marseille line it appears as VIII and Strength as XI. Waite famously exchanges their printed numbers—Strength VIII, Justice XI—while preserving Golden Dawn correspondences. Crowley changes the name Strength to Lust and introduces the Heh/Tzaddi revision.

**Finding:** These disputes concern trumps. They do not change the Three of Batons, which remains an ordinary suit card. [Dummett order discussion](https://pre-gebelin.blogspot.com/2007/11/three-regional-traditions.html), [Dummett commentary](https://dummettsmondo.blogspot.com/2015/07/chapter-17-surface-meanings.html)

## Conflicts

1. **Etteilla versus Golden Dawn:** Etteilla’s “enterprise/interruption” is operational fortune-telling. Golden Dawn’s Sun in Aries/Binah is a ceremonial cosmology. They overlap semantically but arise from different constructions.

2. **Papus versus Golden Dawn:** Papus derives the Three by combinatorial number-and-suit logic. Golden Dawn locates it in a specific decan and Sephirah.

3. **Marseille versus RWS:** The Marseille card has no inherent horizon, merchant, or ships. Those narrative details enter with Smith and Waite.

4. **Crowley versus Waite:** Both inherit “Established Strength,” but Crowley removes the merchant scene and emphasizes metaphysical fecundity: energy becomes character or “Virtue.”

5. **Tzaddi/Heh:** Crowley’s swap does not change Sun in Aries for this pip; it changes which major trump participates in the Aries correspondence network.

6. **Sephirah 3:** “Three equals Binah” is a Golden Dawn/Crowley/Case reading of numbered pips. It is not demonstrated by the three batons on a fifteenth-century card.

# Divinatory meanings by author

Copyright limits prevent extensive verbatim reproduction of modern books. Short historical quotations and close paraphrases are supplied.

## Etteilla, 1780s tradition

- **Upright:** **“Entreprise”** — undertaking, attempt, business enterprise.
- **Reversed:** **“Interruption”** — interruption, cessation, repose, consolation, the ending of a painful or adverse condition.

**Source problem:** Different Grand Etteilla recensions, later “Julia Orsini” manuals, and printers sometimes shift numbering or orientation. The keywords should therefore be attributed to the Etteilla tradition unless verified against a particular first-edition card. [Translation history of Etteilla’s Third Cahier](https://thirdcahier.blogspot.com/2012/10/blank_7674.html), [later French text](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/2/2f/Le_grand_Etteilla%2C_ou%2C_l%27art_de_tirer_les_cartes_..._Le_tout_recueilli_..._%28IA_b29321220%29.pdf)

## S. L. MacGregor Mathers, 1888

Mathers calls it the **Three of Sceptres**.

- **Upright:** “Enterprise, Undertaking, Commerce, Trade, Negotiation.”
- **Reversed:** “A Man of good position, but shifty” in character or dealings.

The second formula differs sharply from Waite’s later benevolent reversal. [Mathers, *The Tarot*, 1888 scan](https://www.howardnema.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/12/the-tarot-1888-by-macgregor-mathers-1.pdf)

## Golden Dawn, Book T

- **Positive expression:** established force, realization of hope, success after effort, pride, nobility, wealth or power.
- **Ill-dignified expression:** arrogance, presumption, insolence, or strength used without sufficient balance.

The Order reads dignity largely through neighboring cards rather than simply turning every card upside down.

## A. E. Waite, 1910/1911

Waite says the man symbolizes:

> “established strength, enterprise, effort, trade, commerce, discovery.”

He adds that the ships carry his merchandise and that the card can mean able cooperation from a successful business figure.

- **Reversed:** “The end of troubles, suspension or cessation of adversity, toil and disappointment.”

This reversed meaning follows the Etteilla-derived “interruption” complex: interruption can mean interruption of trouble, hence relief. [*Pictorial Key*](https://citeseerx.ist.psu.edu/document?doi=7a15961664bced51d1162198bce46d173e40dd41&repid=rep1&type=pdf)

## Aleister Crowley, 1944

Crowley’s title is **Virtue**.

- **Core meaning:** the will has become established as character; primeval fire has entered Binah and become capable of manifestation.
- **Astrological quality:** harmonious because the Sun is exalted in Aries and spring has begun.
- **Negative possibility:** Crowley gives less of a conventional reversed list; imbalance would turn strength of character into self-importance, rigidity, or scorched-earth will.

Crowley’s own concise formulation is that the card represents “the establishment of primeval Energy.” [*Book of Thoth*](https://www.rahoorkhuit.net/library/crowley/equinox/volume3/vol_3_no_05.pdf)

## Paul Foster Case

Case retains the Golden Dawn backbone:

- Binah in the world of Fire;
- Sun in Aries;
- enterprise that has moved beyond conception into an established basis;
- constructive foresight and commercial or organizational ability.

**Reversed/ill-dignified:** plans insufficiently grounded, overconfidence, pride, or delayed realization.

Case’s *Oracle of the Tarot* is available in scan form, but the accessible copy did not yield a sufficiently reliable page-level Three-of-Wands quotation for verbatim reproduction. [*Oracle of the Tarot* scan](https://benebellwen.com/wp-content/uploads/2013/02/paul-foster-case-oracle-of-the-tarot-1933-source-credit-tarotworks.pdf)

## Rachel Pollack, 1980/1983

Pollack treats the card as an outward-looking stage following the more enclosed dominion of the Two:

- plans put into action;
- enterprise, exploration, and opportunity;
- a stable beginning whose results are not yet complete;
- the ability to look beyond immediate surroundings.

**Shadow/reversal:** delays, blocked expansion, fear of risk, or waiting for results without taking the necessary next action.

These are paraphrases because a legitimately accessible authorial text containing the complete card entry was not located. Pollack herself dates the two original volumes to 1980 and 1983. [Rachel Pollack](https://www.rachelpollack.com/tarot/tarot.html)

## Mary K. Greer

Greer resists reducing reversal to a single antonym. Her framework permits a reversed Three of Wands to mean:

- enterprise blocked or delayed;
- expansion turned inward;
- overextension;
- reconsideration of a plan;
- withdrawal from an external venture;
- an unconventional or private version of foresight.

Her reversal taxonomy includes blocked, resisted, projected, delayed, excessive, misused, internalized, reconsidered, and other modes. [Summary citing *The Complete Book of Tarot Reversals*](https://www.hermetica.info/Tarot.pdf)

**Finding:** A lawful open source reproducing Greer’s complete card entry was not found, so invented “own words” have not been supplied.

# Esoteric and symbolic readings

## Alchemical

**Esoteric attribution:** Wands supply sulfuric, fiery activity; Binah gives it a vessel or womb. The Three can therefore be read as fiery seed entering a receptive matrix and beginning fixation or manifestation.

**First secure form:** This particular formulation belongs most clearly to Golden Dawn and Crowley-era Hermetic synthesis, not Renaissance cardmaking.

Crowley intensifies it: the Father’s energy enters the Great Mother and flowers. Harris’s lotus ends visualize this fecundation.

## Kabbalistic

**Golden Dawn attribution:** Three = **Binah**, Understanding, the supernal Mother. Wands = **Atziluth**, the archetypal world associated with Fire. The card is therefore Binah of Atziluth.

This creates a productive tension: Binah is associated with form and receptive understanding, while Fire is active, eruptive will. “Established Strength” means energy taking intelligible, durable form.

**Historical caution:** Neither Jewish Kabbalistic texts nor fifteenth-century tarot documents identify a Three of Batons as Binah. This is a Hermetic-Qabalistic construction.

## Astrological

**Golden Dawn attribution:** Sun in the second decan of Aries, 10°–20°.

Symbolic logic:

- Sun: vitality, center, identity, authority;
- Aries: initiation, cardinal fire, spring;
- solar exaltation in Aries: strength in a favorable dignity;
- Binah: the energy becomes organized and fruitful.

**Calendar variation:** Modern sources sometimes attach approximate dates around 31 March–10 April. Exact civil dates vary slightly by ephemeris and convention; Book T’s essential assignment is the zodiacal span, not a fixed modern birthday table.

## Hermetic

The three wands integrate:

- elemental Fire;
- the planetary Sun;
- zodiacal Aries;
- Sephirah Binah;
- Atziluth;
- Yod of the divine name;
- Golden Dawn color scales;
- the image of a hand emerging from clouds.

This is a deliberately synthetic ritual diagram, not a single ancient tradition.

## Christian

### Historical layer

Batons can evoke rods of authority, pilgrims’ staffs, military clubs, or the staff that buds, but no surviving early source identifies the Three specifically with the Trinity, three crosses, or Aaron’s rod.

### Later symbolic reading

**Tradition/modern invention:** Some Christian readers see the three staffs as Trinity, faith–hope–charity, or three crosses. These meanings are possible devotional appropriations but lack an identified Renaissance source.

## Jungian and psychological

**Modern interpretation:** The solitary person looking toward a horizon becomes an image of ego orientation toward an unrealized future. The ships may represent projects launched into the unconscious or world; the elevated viewpoint represents perspective; the planted wands represent stable commitments.

Potential shadow:

- inflation and grandiosity;
- identifying with future success rather than present work;
- avoidance of intimacy through perpetual exploration;
- projection of fulfillment onto distant places.

These readings spread through twentieth-century psychological tarot, especially writers such as Pollack and Greer. They should not be retrojected into the Visconti or Marseille cards.

## Numerological

Three is commonly treated as:

- synthesis after the polarity of Two;
- first visible product of one-plus-two;
- growth, generation, and creative issue;
- in Papus, realization of a beginning;
- in Golden Dawn, Binah, which receives and gives form.

The idea that “Three always means growth” is a useful modern rule of thumb, not a historically universal law.

# People and works

## Card makers and artists

- **Bonifacio Bembo / Francesco Zavattari:** competing attributions surrounding early Visconti–Sforza cards.
- **Michelino da Besozzo:** painter of Marziano’s lost precursor pack.
- **Jean Noblet:** Parisian maker of the approximately 1650 Marseille-family deck.
- **Jean Dodal:** Lyon maker, approximately 1701–1715.
- **Nicolas Conver:** Marseille maker whose 1760/1761 model became canonical.
- **Jean-Baptiste Alliette (Etteilla):** first designer of a tarot explicitly made for cartomancy.
- **Oswald Wirth:** designed twenty-two occult trumps, not a historical complete Three of Wands.
- **Pamela Colman Smith:** created the definitive scenic Three in 1909.
- **A. E. Waite:** specified its public description and meanings.
- **Jessie Burns Parke / Paul Foster Case:** BOTA adaptation.
- **Frieda Harris / Aleister Crowley:** geometric and Thelemic reconstitution as Virtue.
- **Robert Wang; Chic and Sandra Tabatha Cicero:** modern Golden Dawn reconstructions.
- **Philippe Camoin / Alejandro Jodorowsky:** 1997 Marseille reconstruction.
- **Rachel Pollack and Mary K. Greer:** major spreaders of psychological and experiential readings.

## T. S. Eliot, *The Waste Land* (1922)

Madame Sosostris produces:

> “the man with three staves”

Eliot’s note says he associates this figure “quite arbitrarily” with the Fisher King.

**Documented literary allusion:** The phrase plainly recalls the Waite–Smith Three of Wands or Waite’s “personage” among three staves.

**Caution:** Eliot did not claim that the historical card originally was the Fisher King. His own note declares the identification arbitrary.

## Italo Calvino, *The Castle of Crossed Destinies*

Calvino constructed narratives from tarot layouts, first using a Visconti-style pack and later a Marseille deck.

**Finding:** Tarot is structurally central to the work, but no secure source located in this survey establishes the Three of Wands as a uniquely privileged card. Claims that the novel supplies a canonical meaning for this card should be treated cautiously.

## W. B. Yeats

Yeats participated in the Golden Dawn and absorbed its symbolism. He is therefore historically connected to the system that named the card Lord of Established Strength.

**Finding:** No Yeats text was located that gives an individual interpretation of the Three of Wands. General Golden Dawn affiliation is not evidence for a card-specific literary allusion.

## Alejandro Jodorowsky

Jodorowsky made tarot central to his public teaching and collaborated on the Camoin deck. His films contain tarot-like archetypal and alchemical imagery.

**Finding:** No securely documented film shot was established as a quotation of the Three of Wands specifically. Similar-looking figures, staffs, or horizons are insufficient evidence.

## Ingmar Bergman

Bergman’s films use medieval religious imagery, death, games, and allegorical tableaux.

**Finding:** No reliable source was found connecting a particular Bergman image to the Three of Wands. Association by atmosphere is not a documented quotation.

## Paintings and buildings

No painting or building could be securely shown, from artist or architect testimony or a strong scholarly source, to quote the Three of Wands specifically.

Modern visual culture repeatedly uses a back-turned figure overlooking ships or a distant landscape, but the motif predates tarot and is too general to prove dependence on Smith’s card without documentary evidence.

# Controversies and disputes

## Egyptian origin

**Legend:** Court de Gébelin announced in 1781 that tarot preserved an ancient Egyptian book. He derived *Tarot* from supposed Egyptian words without presenting hieroglyphic evidence and treated the images as corrupted Egyptian theology.

**Historical correction:** Tarot is documented in northern Italy in the fifteenth century, centuries before the occult-Egyptian theory. Gébelin wrote before Champollion’s decipherment of Egyptian hieroglyphs. Dummett notes that Gébelin and Mellet offered invented Egyptian etymologies without hieroglyphic forms. [Dummett, “Origins of Tarot”](https://www.nybooks.com/articles/1981/05/14/origins-of-tarot/)

**Card-specific consequence:** Sun in Aries, Binah, and “Established Strength” cannot be evidence that the Renaissance Three of Batons was Egyptian; those correspondences first appear much later.

## “Book of Thoth”

**Tradition:** Etteilla transformed Gébelin’s theory into a functioning divinatory pack. Crowley later used *Book of Thoth* as the title of his tarot treatise.

**Fact:** The phrases refer to modern occult interpretations. No ancient Egyptian papyrus or temple archive containing the 78 tarot cards is known.

## Romani or “Gypsy” origin

**Legend:** Tarot was brought from Egypt by “Gypsies” and preserved by wandering Romani fortune-tellers.

**Development:** The link grew from early-modern European beliefs that Romani people came from Egypt. Authors including Vaillant helped popularize it in the nineteenth century; Papus’s title *Tarot des Bohémiens* gave it enormous reach.

**Historical correction:** Linguistic and historical evidence places Romani origins in South Asia. Tarot is documented in Italian court culture before the later literary consolidation of the Romani origin story. The story concerns imagined transmission, not evidence from any Three of Wands. [History of the claim](https://www.nybooks.com/articles/1981/05/14/origins-of-tarot/), [occult-tarot history overview](https://www.letarot.it/page.aspx?id=350)

## Cathars

**Legend/modern invention:** Tarot images supposedly encode Cathar doctrine hidden from the Church.

**Evidence:** No fifteenth-century rulebook, ledger, card inscription, inquisitorial record, or Cathar text links tarot to Cathar survival. The earliest packs occur in elite courts embedded in Catholic culture. No card-specific Cathar reading of the Three of Batons has an early source.

## Knights Templar

**Legend/modern invention:** The Templars transmitted Egyptian, Grail, or Temple secrets through tarot.

**Evidence:** The order was suppressed in the early fourteenth century; tarot appears in documentation roughly a century later. No transmission document bridges that gap. Three staffs are not evidence of three Templar pillars or initiates.

## Alchemical Sola Busca

**Disputed but serious hypothesis:** The Sola Busca contains imagery that may draw on alchemical, metallurgical, military, humanist, or initiatory sources. Its images are intentionally enigmatic.

**Limit:** Even if the pack contains alchemical programs, it does not follow that every later card with the same numeral and suit preserves that meaning. A general Sola Busca–Waite influence must be tested card by card.

## The Sola Busca as Smith’s direct Three-of-Wands source

As noted above:

- general influence: **plausible scholarly reconstruction**;
- specific Three-to-Three copying: **not demonstrated**;
- claiming it as certain: **misattribution or overstatement**.

The British Museum photograph chronology creates opportunity, not proof.

## “Marseille sacred geometry”

**Tradition/disputed claim:** Modern teachers sometimes claim that every Marseille pip was deliberately constructed from a hidden golden ratio, cathedral plan, or initiatory geometry.

**Evidence:** Symmetry, compass work, repeated woodblock conventions, and workshop geometry are visible. A universal secret canon has not been established by surviving cardmakers’ instructions. Practical print design should not automatically be equated with esoteric architecture.

## Reversals

**Historical complication:** Turning a symmetrical Marseille pip upside down may produce no visually obvious change. Etteilla solved this with printed headings and orientation. Waite supplied separate reversed meanings even where the picture remained intelligible upright.

**Misprint issue:** Grand Etteilla recensions sometimes swap keywords, orientation, or numbering. A printing accident can subsequently be copied as tradition. No single universal “original Etteilla Three” should be asserted without naming the edition.

## Thoth publication date

A frequent simplification says “Thoth Tarot, 1944.” More precisely:

- designs painted: 1938–1943;
- Crowley’s explanatory book: 1944;
- complete commercial deck: 1969.

## “Virtue” as morality

**Disputed interpretation:** Reading Crowley’s title merely as moral goodness flattens his usage. The image and text emphasize effective strength, character, generative power, and energy properly established. Ethical integrity may be inferred, but the title is not reducible to conventional respectability.

## Trump renamings mistakenly attached to the pip

Papess → Juno, Pope → Jupiter, Tower → *Maison Dieu*, unnumbered Fool, or the Waite Strength/Justice exchange are genuine deck-history questions, but none is a regional renaming of the Three of Wands. Importing them into this card’s identity would confuse pack-level history with card-level history.

# Dedicated contested-material pass

Explicit searches were conducted around “Three of Wands” combined with origin, Egyptian, Gypsy/Romani, Cathar, Templar, dispute, misattribution, and Sola Busca.

The results divide into three groups:

1. **General tarot-origin myths** repeated without Three-specific evidence.
2. **Modern spiritual interpretations** presenting the card as ancient solar, Egyptian, Atlantean, Templar, or initiatory wisdom.
3. **The Sola Busca influence claim**, which has a real historical basis at deck level but is frequently overstated at the individual-card level.

No pre-eighteenth-century text was found that calls this card:

- Sun in Aries;
- Binah in Atziluth;
- Lord of Established Strength;
- Virtue;
- a symbol of Egyptian merchants;
- a Cathar emblem;
- a Templar degree;
- a Jungian archetype of expansion.

The first four belong respectively to the Golden Dawn/Crowley lineage; the last three are modern projections or general interpretive analogies.

# Open questions

1. **Exact ownership and access to the British Museum’s Sola Busca photographs:** Opportunity for Smith to see them is strong, but a documentary record of her consultation would materially improve the case.

2. **Specific source of Smith’s merchant and ships:** Waite’s inherited words explain the scene, but an exact pictorial prototype has not been identified.

3. **Early Visconti Three of Batons object-level description:** A complete modern catalogue correlating every dispersed card, accession number, workshop hand, restoration, and pigment analysis would refine the claim about the earliest surviving example.

4. **Sola Busca Three’s intended narrative:** Its imagery remains open to military, classical, alchemical, satirical, and initiatory readings. No universally accepted contemporary key survives.

5. **Etteilla first-edition orientation:** “Entreprise/Interruption” is well established in the tradition, but the exact typography and upright orientation should always be tied to a named impression rather than normalized across Grand Etteilla I, II, and III.

6. **Golden Dawn manuscript precedence:** “Book T” survives in several copies and publications. Establishing the earliest exact wording for every pip requires manuscript comparison, not reliance on one later transcription.

7. **Modern literary and architectural quotations:** Eliot is secure; Calvino is tarot-wide; Yeats is systemically connected; Bergman, Jodorowsky’s particular films, paintings, and buildings require stronger card-specific documentation.
