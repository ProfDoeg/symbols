# Queen of Wands: Research Dossier

## Method and evidentiary labels

This dossier distinguishes:

- **Documented fact** — supported by a surviving card, archival record, contemporary text, or museum catalogue.
- **Scholarly reconstruction** — a conclusion argued from surviving objects and records but not directly stated by a contemporary witness.
- **Tradition** — a repeated convention whose historical origin may be uncertain.
- **Esoteric attribution** — a deliberately constructed occult correspondence.
- **Disputed claim** — a proposal on which authorities or evidence conflict.
- **Legend** — an origin story contradicted by, or unsupported by, the documentary chronology.
- **Modern invention** — an interpretation or image demonstrably introduced in recent tarot practice.

Quotations are kept short; page numbers refer to the editions listed under Sources. “Queen of Wands” is a court card, not a trump, so many requested trump-only systems—Hebrew-letter paths, trump numbers, and the Tzaddi/Heh controversy—do not attach to it directly.

The Accademia Carrara Queen is therefore the earliest surviving material attestation presently traceable for this particular card. The National Gallery of Australia’s exhibition record calls it “Queen of staves [Regina di bastoni],” dates it to the 1440s, and locates it in the Accademia Carrara. The Morgan dates the dispersed Visconti–Sforza group more cautiously to Milan, circa 1450–1480. Both datings are catalogical judgments rather than dates written on the card. [NGA exhibition record](https://digital.nga.gov.au/archive/exhibition/renaissance/default.cfm%3Firn%3D202512%26bioartistirn%3D37018%26mnuid%3D3%26viewid%3D2.html); [Morgan catalogue](https://www.themorgan.org/manuscript/158525).

### The queen and the ancestry of the court

**Documented fact:** The surviving Mamluk *kanjifa/nāʾib* pack has four suits closely corresponding to cups, coins, swords, and polo sticks, with ten numeral cards and three titled courts: *malik* (king), *nāʾib malik* (deputy king), and *thānī nāʾib* (second deputy). The courts are represented by ornament and inscriptions, not queens. [International Playing-Card Society overview](https://www.cs.man.ac.uk/~daf/i-p-c-s.org/faq/basics.php); [Mamluk-card survey](https://www.wopc.co.uk/egypt/mamluk-cards-and-the-making-of-the-european-deck).

**Scholarly reconstruction:** European suit systems probably descend, directly or through Mediterranean intermediaries, from this family. Polo sticks became batons or clubs where polo was unfamiliar. Human king, mounted knight, and knave figures replaced inscribed offices.

**Documented fact:** Fourteenth- and fifteenth-century European packs did not follow a single court system. Queens appear in some early European packs, but the ordinary Italian and Spanish regional patterns stabilized around King–Knight–Knave without a queen. Tarot, by contrast, normally retained four courts: King–Queen–Knight–Knave. The Morgan describes the complete fifteenth-century tarot structure as four fourteen-card suits, each with ten numerals and four picture cards. [Morgan catalogue](https://www.themorgan.org/manuscript/158525); [Metropolitan Museum essay](https://www.metmuseum.org/perspectives/tarot-2).

Thus “the queen was added to the Mamluk deck” is a useful shorthand, but not a documented single event. The chain contains adaptation, lost intermediate packs, and regional experimentation.

## Iconography deck by deck

## Fifteenth-century Milan

### Visconti di Modrone / Cary–Yale

**Date and place:** Milan, usually circa 1440–1460; exact dating remains disputed.

**Documented image and structure:** This exceptional luxury pack had six courts per suit: male and female counterparts at three levels—page/damsel, mounted knight/lady, and king/queen. Its female hierarchy is therefore richer than the later standard tarot. The surviving court cards wear Visconti heraldic dress and stand or sit against gold grounds.

**Queen of Staves:** A crowned court woman belongs to the baton suit, with the suit emblem identifying her rank and suit. The card is not labeled by a printed title.

**Meaning at the time:** No contemporary divinatory commentary survives. The safest reading is dynastic and ludic: a female sovereign rank within an expanded court.

**Scholarly reconstruction:** The six courts may reflect marriage, dynastic balance, or a deliberate male/female organization of court society. It should not be retroactively described as “Water of Fire”; that formula is nineteenth-century Golden Dawn doctrine.

### Brera–Brambilla

**Date and place:** Milan, probably mid-fifteenth century; associated with Filippo Maria Visconti’s environment.

The incomplete Brambilla group preserves many coins and court cards, but the Queen of Batons is not securely available among the surviving cards. Therefore no card-specific iconographic development can be asserted. Reproductions that supply a Queen of Batons are modern completions.

### Visconti–Sforza / Bergamo–New York group

**Date and place:** Milan, approximately 1450–1480; parts now at the Morgan Library, Accademia Carrara, and in the Colleoni holding.

**Documented image:** The surviving Queen of Staves is a crowned, richly dressed woman presented against a punched and gilded ground. She carries the long staff that identifies her suit. There is no landscape, sunflower, cat, leopard, elemental sign, zodiacal glyph, or printed card name. The luxurious gold ground and costume place her in the visual world of the Sforza court. [NGA record](https://digital.nga.gov.au/archive/exhibition/renaissance/default.cfm%3Firn%3D202512%26bioartistirn%3D37018%26mnuid%3D3%26viewid%3D2.html).

**Authorship:** Usually attributed to Bonifacio Bembo or his workshop; divisions of labor and later replacement cards make attribution of the entire surviving group more complicated.

**Who is she?**

- **Documented fact:** The card carries no name identifying a historical sitter.
- **Scholarly reconstruction:** Gertrude Moakley and Stuart Kaplan looked for Visconti–Sforza persons, emblems, clothing, and marriage references throughout the deck.
- **Disputed claim:** Identification of the Queen of Staves as a particular Visconti or Sforza woman is not securely established. Bianca Maria Visconti is a plausible dynastic association for the pack as a whole, but resemblance and family context do not prove portraiture.
- **Finding:** No contemporary inventory or inscription was located that identifies this Queen as Bianca Maria, another named family member, or a specific allegorical person.

### Other Milanese fragments

Numerous single cards and fragmentary groups have been associated with the Visconti and Sforza courts. Because few contain a securely attributed Queen of Staves, they illuminate production and court style more than the card’s individual development.

## Fifteenth-century Ferrara and related north-Italian cards

### Este court cards and fragments

**Documented fact:** Ferrara’s court accounts record *carte da trionfi* in 1442 and later payments for painted packs. These prove the game and its patronage but do not enumerate the Queen of Batons.

Surviving Este-related cards use elaborately dressed figures, ornamental borders, heraldic devices, and Italian suit signs. Where a female baton court appears, it is a rank-and-suit figure rather than an occult allegory.

### Sola Busca, 1491

The Sola Busca is crucial to the history of illustrated **number cards**, because its pip cards contain narrative military and classical-looking scenes. It is not the source of the Waite–Smith Queen of Wands in the same direct sense that several Waite–Smith pip compositions appear to borrow from Sola Busca photographs.

Its baton court belongs to a pseudo-antique military world, not to the sunflower-and-cat scheme. The British Museum possessed photographs of Sola Busca cards before 1909, and Pamela Colman Smith probably consulted them for some pip designs. No equally strong visual borrowing has been demonstrated for the Queen of Wands.

### Mantegna “Tarocchi”

**Not a tarot deck.** These are fifteenth-century educational engravings in five ordered series—social estates, Apollo and the Muses, liberal arts and sciences, virtues, and celestial spheres. They have neither the four tarot suits nor a Queen of Batons. Their later designation as “tarocchi” produced confusion, but they are irrelevant to the direct genealogy of this card.

### Rosenwald and Budapest sheets

These late-fifteenth- or early-sixteenth-century printed sheets are major witnesses for cheap, mass-produced Italian tarot.

- **Documented fact:** They preserve suit and trump designs in uncut-sheet form.
- Their court figures are simplified woodcuts compared with Milanese luxury cards.
- Where the baton queen survives or can be reconstructed, she is a conventional crowned female court holding the suit emblem.
- They supply no evidence for divinatory symbolism, elements, astrology, a cat, or personal portraiture.

Because surviving sheets are incomplete and sometimes damaged, claims that a particular fragment is the Queen rather than another court must follow sheet position and rank attributes, not later occult expectations.

## Minchiate

**Place and date:** Florence; expanded tarot form documented in the sixteenth century, with roots often placed earlier.

**Pack:** Ninety-seven cards: the ordinary four suits plus forty-one trumps and the Fool.

**Queen of Batons:** A conventional Italian-suited court. It remains unnumbered and is not one of Minchiate’s added zodiacal or elemental trumps.

**Important distinction:** Minchiate contains separate trump images of the four elements and zodiac signs. Their presence does not make the Queen of Batons “Water of Fire.” That is a later Golden Dawn court-card formula.

**Game value:** In known Minchiate scoring, only the king among ordinary suit courts normally carries a counting value; the queen ranks below him for taking tricks but is not independently worth points. An IPCS study notes that Minchiate’s selection of counting cards is unusually concentrated in its trump suit. [IPCS, *The Playing-Card* 49:2](https://www.i-p-c-s.org/journal/TPC-49-2-web.pdf).

## Bolognese tarot

**Pattern:** Tarocco Bolognese/Tarocchino, eventually reduced to sixty-two cards by removing numeral cards 2–5.

**Queen of Batons:** Crowned female court; in later double-headed patterns she became a compact reversible playing-card figure. Some eighteenth-century examples bear the Fibbia arms on the Queen of Batons.

**Fibbia claim:**

- **Documented fact:** Some eighteenth-century Bolognese packs do show the Fibbia arms on this queen and Bentivoglio arms on the Queen of Coins.
- **Legend:** The seventeenth-century Fibbia inscription says Francesco Antelminelli Castracani Fibbia invented tarot and obtained the right to place those arms on the queens.
- **Historical difficulty:** Tarot is documented before the period assigned to Fibbia’s alleged invention. The heraldry demonstrates family appropriation or commemoration, not invention. [Tarocchino Bolognese historical discussion](https://www.tarocchinobolognese.it/pages/storia/il_falo_di_san_bernardino.php).

**Rank:** In batons, King > Queen > Knight > Knave > 10 down to 6 > Ace. [Tarocco Bolognese rules](https://www.pagat.com/class/itarot.html).

## Sicilian tarot

The Sicilian pattern is a reduced regional descendant influenced by Bolognese tarot.

**Court:** King, Queen, Horse, Maid. In modern terminology the baton suit is *bastoni* or *mazze*. The Queen is above the Horse and Maid but below the King.

**Iconography:** Regional costumes and compact woodcut-derived figures replace the monumental Marseille manner. The card is primarily a playing piece, not a symbolic tableau.

**Rank:** King > Queen > Horse > Maid > 10 downward. [Pagat rules](https://www.pagat.com/tarot/sicilian.html).

## Tarot de Marseille lineage

### Jean Noblet, Paris, circa 1659

The BnF describes Noblet’s as the oldest surviving deck conforming to the family conventionally called Tarot de Marseille, though it was made in Paris. It preserves seventy-three of seventy-eight cards. [BnF catalogue](https://catalogue.bnf.fr/ark:/12148/cb43634904c); [BnF presentation](https://essentiels.bnf.fr/fr/image/4799f2d6-6f88-4f6f-a15c-40a1a943c95e-tarot-jean-noblet).

**Title:** *ROYNE DE BASTONS*.

**Image:** A crowned seated woman holds a massive baton. Her body is turned rather than strictly frontal, her elaborate dress fills the card, and the staff resembles both a natural branch and a ceremonial club. The color is stencil-applied. There is no cat, sunflower, or scenic background.

### Jean Dodal, Lyon, circa 1701–1715

**Title:** Generally *REYNE DE BATON* or comparable period spelling.

**Image:** A seated crowned queen holds a thick baton diagonally or close to the shoulder. The figure becomes more standardized, with strong black woodcut contours and a restricted stencil palette. Small alterations in hand, sleeve, baton head, and seat reflect recutting rather than a new esoteric program.

### Nicolas Conver, Marseille, model dated 1760

**Title:** *REYNE DE BASTON* in the surviving pattern.

**Image:** Crowned, seated, oriented toward her right, holding a heavy club-shaped baton against the shoulder. A lobed shield- or seat-like form appears beside her. Her long light hair and layered garments produce a closed, self-contained silhouette.

**Dating caution:** “Conver 1760” refers to the dated model. The particular BnF witness digitized today is catalogued later, from the continuing workshop. Treating every modern “1760 Conver” as an untouched impression from 1760 is inaccurate.

### B. P. Grimaud / Paul Marteau

Grimaud industrialized the Conver-derived pattern. Paul Marteau’s *Le Tarot de Marseille* (first issued in 1930) gave the old cards a systematic symbolic commentary.

For the Queen of Batons, Marteau read her wrapped, contained posture as an inward gathering of energy, her gaze as vigilance, and the large baton as mastery of material force. This is **twentieth-century esoteric interpretation**, not evidence of the woodcutter’s intention.

### Camoin–Jodorowsky, 1997

Philippe Camoin and Alejandro Jodorowsky reconstructed a brightly colored Marseille deck from old patterns.

- **Documented modern intervention:** Lines, colors, facial details, and small symbols were normalized or restored through comparison with multiple decks.
- **Claim of recovery:** Camoin and Jodorowsky present the result as restoration of a coherent symbolic original.
- **Disputed:** Historians generally do not accept that surviving variants permit recovery of one pristine archetype in every detail.
- The Queen remains a crowned court holding a baton; Jodorowsky and Marianne Costa interpret her through gesture, receptivity, sexuality, creativity, and the stage of mastery represented by queens.

## Besançon pattern

Besançon tarot chiefly changes two **trumps**: Juno replaces the Papess and Jupiter replaces the Pope, a convention documented in eastern France and adjoining Swiss/German regions from the eighteenth century.

The Queen of Batons itself remains a normal suit court. Calling her “Juno” is an error: Juno belongs to trump II, not to the baton court.

Likewise:

- *La Maison Dieu* is the conventional French title of trump XVI.
- An unnumbered Fool concerns *Le Mat*.
- Justice/Strength numbering disputes concern trumps.
- None changes the Queen’s number, because traditional courts are unnumbered.

## Etteilla’s Grand Etteilla

### First system and deck

Jean-Baptiste Alliette, “Etteilla,” published his tarot method in installments beginning in 1783. His purpose-designed “Egyptian” tarot appeared in the late 1780s, commonly dated 1789.

**Card:** No. 23, Dame/Reine de Bâton.

**Image:** A woman associated with country life rather than a medieval enthroned queen. Etteilla reordered all seventy-eight cards and printed divinatory words for upright and reversed readings. Later Type I, II, and III designs altered images, spellings, and sometimes orientation of keywords.

**Meaning:** The card belongs to the cluster of person-cards inherited from French cartomancy: a countrywoman, economical or virtuous woman, rural excursion, harvest, or service and devotion, depending on edition and orientation.

A nineteenth-century Etteilla manual says of No. 23: “une bonne femme, a beaucoup d’esprit, économe, vertueuse”—a good woman, intelligent, economical, virtuous—and connects the upright card with abundant harvests or a country outing. This wording comes from a later school manual, not necessarily verbatim from Etteilla himself. [Digitized *Grand Etteilla* manual](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/2/2f/Le_grand_Etteilla%2C_ou%2C_l%27art_de_tirer_les_cartes_..._Le_tout_recueilli_..._%28IA_b29321220%29.pdf).

**Astrology:** Etteilla supplied astrological and elemental ideas to the deck as a whole, but the Golden Dawn’s exact Water-of-Fire and 21° Pisces–20° Aries formula is not Etteilla’s.

## Oswald Wirth, 1889

Wirth’s original 1889 tarot, designed under Stanislas de Guaita’s direction, consisted of the twenty-two trumps. It therefore has **no Queen of Wands**.

Later seventy-eight-card “Wirth” editions add minors by later artists or publishers. Their Queen of Batons must not be attributed automatically to Wirth’s 1889 drawings.

## Golden Dawn manuscripts and reconstructions

### Book T

The Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn created the first traceable full sub-elemental and zodiacal scheme for this card.

**Title:** “Queen of the Thrones of Flame.”

**Image prescribed:** A crowned queen with long red-gold hair sits above steady flames. She wears scale-mail, with winged leopard heads on armor and crown. A couchant leopard rests beside her, and she carries a long wand with a heavy conical head. Her face is “beautiful and resolute.”

**Attribution:**

- Queen = Water, Heh, receptive formative force.
- Wands = Fire, Yod, Atziluth.
- Combined = Water of Fire.
- Zodiac = last decan Pisces plus first two decans Aries.
- Title = Queen of the Thrones of Flame.
- Character = adaptability, steady force, authority, attraction; when ill-dignified, obstinacy, revenge, domination, tyranny.

The best-known published form appeared through Crowley’s 1912 “A Description of the Cards of the Tarot” and later Israel Regardie’s Golden Dawn compilations. [Crowley/Book T text](https://www.100thmonkeypress.com/biblio/acrowley/downloads/equinox1_8/tarot/tarot.pdf).

### Wang and Cicero reconstructions

Robert Wang’s *Golden Dawn Tarot* and Sandra Tabatha and Chic Cicero’s *Golden Dawn Magical Tarot* are twentieth-century visual realizations of manuscript descriptions.

- **Documented modern reconstruction:** Both show the red-haired armored queen, flame throne, wand, and leopard.
- **Not facsimiles:** Color decisions, composition, line, and ornamental details are the modern artists’ solutions.
- **Meaning:** They make visible the Golden Dawn formula more literally than Waite–Smith does.

## Waite–Smith, 1909

**Creators:** Arthur Edward Waite designed the program; Pamela Colman Smith executed the art. Published by William Rider & Son in 1909.

**Image:** A crowned queen sits frontally on a high throne in a yellow desert. Her right hand holds a living, leafing wand; her left holds a sunflower. Lions and sunflowers decorate the throne. Three pyramids rise behind her, and a black cat sits at her feet.

Waite writes:

> “The Wands throughout this suit are always in leaf, as it is a suit of life and animation.”

He says the Queen resembles the King emotionally but is “more magnetic.” In the original edition this discussion occurs around pp. 75–76. [Digitized *Pictorial Key*](https://benebellwen.com/wp-content/uploads/2013/02/a-e-waite-pictorial-key-to-the-tarot-1911-source-credit-sacredtexts.pdf).

### Sources and innovations

- The basic enthroned queen holding the suit emblem continues the Marseille and Italian court type.
- The leafy wand and lion decoration tie her to the fire/lion language of the Wands court.
- The sunflower, desert, pyramids, frontal symmetry, and black cat are Smith/Waite-era additions, not inherited features of the Marseille card.
- The cat is not explained by Waite.
- **Disputed reconstruction:** Marcus Katz and Tali Goodwin propose that the cat was modeled on “Snuffles,” the cat of Edith Craig at Smallhythe. This is possible biographical reconstruction, not established by Smith’s written testimony. [Llewellyn article](https://www.llewellyn.com/journal/article/2501).
- **Modern tradition:** The cat is variously interpreted as witchcraft, intuition, independence, sexuality, the unconscious, Bastet, or a familiar. These meanings are absent from Waite’s text and cannot be treated as Smith’s documented intention.
- **Modern tradition:** The sunflower is usually read as solar vitality and confidence. Its literal solar association is obvious, but the detailed psychological meanings are later commentary.

## Paul Foster Case and BOTA

Case’s BOTA deck adapts Waite–Smith while supplying explicit coloring instructions and Qabalistic doctrine.

- The Queen remains enthroned with wand, sunflower, lion imagery, and cat.
- BOTA practitioners color the cards themselves, making color part of contemplative training.
- Case inherited the Golden Dawn’s element and zodiacal range through his occult lineage.
- **Bibliographical caution:** *The Tarot: A Key to the Wisdom of the Ages* concentrates primarily on the twenty-two Keys. Exact Queen-of-Wands meanings often quoted online derive instead from Case’s *Oracle of the Tarot*, BOTA lessons, or later summaries.
- No reliable primary-page support was found for the frequently repeated claim that Case originated this Queen’s 20° Pisces–20° Aries attribution. He transmitted it; *Book T* predates him.

## Thoth Tarot

**Creation:** Designed by Aleister Crowley and painted by Frieda Harris, approximately 1938–1943. Crowley’s *Book of Thoth* appeared in 1944; the complete deck was first commercially published in 1969.

**Title:** Queen of Wands; esoteric title Queen of the Thrones of Flame.

**Image:** The red-gold-haired queen wears scale armor and a crown bearing a winged globe radiating flame. She sits on a geometrically ordered throne of fire. Beneath it, formerly surging fire has become steady. She holds a wand topped by a pinecone-like form, which Crowley associates with Bacchic mystery. A crouching leopard attends her, and she places a hand upon its head.

Crowley describes her as:

> “the watery part of Fire, its fluidity and colour.”

He gives the same zodiacal span, 21° Pisces–20° Aries, and emphasizes “adaptability, persistent energy, calm authority.” Her adverse possibilities include vanity, brooding, tyranny, revenge, and destructive fury. He also relates her to hexagram 17, *Sui*, “Following.” [*Book of Thoth*, court-card chapter](https://hermetic.com/crowley/book-of-thoth/court-cards).

**Development from Golden Dawn:** Harris did not invent the leopard, mail, fire throne, or conical wand; these occur in *Book T*. Her innovations lie in abstraction, geometry, color, bodily dynamism, and the fusion of woman, flame, throne, and animal.

**Publication-date correction:** “The Thoth deck was published in 1944” is imprecise. Card images accompanied the 1944 book, but the complete commercial pack appeared in 1969.

## Notable modern redesigns

Modern decks usually follow one of three genealogies:

1. **Marseille-derived:** A static crowned Queen holding one oversized baton.
2. **Waite–Smith-derived:** Throne, sunflower, lions, leafy wand, black cat.
3. **Thoth/Golden Dawn-derived:** Flame throne, red-gold hair, scale armor, leopard, Water of Fire.

Common late-twentieth- and twenty-first-century changes include queens of varied ethnicity, age, body type, and gender presentation; standing or dancing figures; witches and priestesses; entrepreneurs, performers, activists, and artists; lions or cats enlarged into companions; and titles such as Mother of Wands, Seer of Wands, or Guardian of Fire.

These are **modern inventions**, often valuable as cultural interpretation, but they should not be projected backward as the card’s Renaissance identity.

## The game

## Rank and trick-taking function

In traditional tarot games, the Queen of Batons is an ordinary suit court, not a trump.

The general Italian-suited ranking is:

**King > Queen > Knight > Page/Knave > numeral cards**

The numeral order depends on suit and game:

- In “long” suits, normally swords and batons, numerals descend 10 to Ace.
- In “round” suits, cups and coins, numeral order may be reversed.
- Bolognese batons: King > Queen > Knight > Knave > 10 … 6 > Ace.
- Sicilian batons: King > Queen > Horse > Maid > 10 downward.
- French tarot: King > Queen > Knight > Jack > 10 downward.

The Queen follows suit, may win a trick against lower baton cards, loses to the King of Batons, and loses to any legally played trump.

## Point value

Point-counting varies by game.

- In modern French tarot, a Queen is worth **4 card points**; the King is 5, Knight 3, Jack 2, ordinary cards ½ when counted in pairs.
- In many Italian tarot games, queens are counting courts but the exact accounting convention varies.
- In Minchiate, the Queen ranks for taking but ordinarily has no separate counting value; kings retain suit-court points.
- In some tarock games played with French-suited shortened packs, queens remain below kings and above cavaliers; exact values depend on the national game.

There is no single timeless “tarot value” for the Queen.

## The Fool

The Fool is usually a special card:

- In many Italian and French games it excuses the player from following suit and is recovered after the trick, subject to compensation.
- In Central European tarock it often functions as the highest trump under names such as *Sküs*.
- It is historically unnumbered in many Italian and Marseille packs.
- Its rules do not affect the Queen’s identity except when the Fool is used to avoid losing her in a trick.

## Regional trump orders

Dummett classified early orders into three families:

- **Type A:** Bologna and much of northeastern Italy; Angel/Judgment above the World, with virtues grouped.
- **Type B:** Ferrara; World and Justice unusually high, with virtues dispersed.
- **Type C:** Milan and later Marseille lineage; World at or near the top, with the sequence closest to the familiar Marseille order.

Justice and Strength change positions within those histories. Waite’s exchange of Strength VIII and Justice XI follows Golden Dawn astrological requirements.

None of these alters the Queen of Batons, because she is outside the trump sequence. [Summary of Dummett’s classification](https://pre-gebelin.blogspot.com/2007/11/three-regional-traditions.html).

## Chronology of attestation

### Circa 1420 — Marziano da Tortona

Marziano composed a description of a special pack for Filippo Maria Visconti with sixteen classical gods arranged in four suits of birds.

- **Documented fact:** It is an early intellectually organized trump-like card game.
- **Not documented:** It does not contain the standard tarot suits or a Queen of Batons.
- **Scholarly relevance:** It demonstrates experimentation with permanent trumps at the Milanese court shortly before standard *trionfi* appears.

### 1442 — Ferrara ledger

On 28 July 1442 the Este court paid for “uno paro de carte da trionfi” for Ercole and Sigismondo d’Este. Other 1442 records mention Sagramoro painting packs.

- **Documented:** *Trionfi* packs existed.
- **Not documented:** Individual card names or a Queen of Batons.
- [Le Tarot, Italian documentary survey](https://letarot.it/Le-origini-italiane-del-Ludus-Triumphorum_pag_pg439_ita.aspx).

### 1440s–1470s — surviving Milanese cards

The Visconti di Modrone, Brambilla, and Visconti–Sforza groups establish the material existence of queens within tarot suits. The Queen of Staves survives in the Visconti–Sforza group.

### Circa 1450–1500 — *Sermo de ludo cum aliis*

The anonymous Dominican sermon condemns games and lists tarot trumps.

- **Documented:** A nearly complete early trump list and moral glosses.
- **Not documented:** It does not separately name the Queen of Batons.
- It helps establish the 78-card structure indirectly, not this court’s divinatory meaning.

### Circa 1500 — tarocchi appropriati

Poems assign tarot cards to members of courts or social circles. These are literary party exercises based on recognizable cards.

- **Documented:** Tarot images could serve as social and poetic metaphors.
- **Caution:** Assignments vary; they are not a stable divinatory code.
- No securely early Queen-of-Batons poem was located that establishes the later “magnetic, fiery woman” meaning.

### 1527 — Teofilo Folengo

Folengo’s *Chaos del Triperuno* includes a literary divination episode using tarot-associated sonnets.

- **Documented:** Tarot was used imaginatively in literary fortune construction.
- **Not proof:** It does not establish a continuous professional divination tradition from the fifteenth century.

### Circa 1659 — Noblet

First surviving securely catalogued Marseille-family *Royne de Bastons*.

### Early eighteenth century — Dodal

The standardized Lyonese Queen appears.

### 1760 — Conver model

The best-known Marseille Queen design is fixed in the Conver lineage.

### 1781 — Court de Gébelin and de Mellet

Tarot is publicly recast as Egyptian sacred knowledge. Their Hebrew-alphabet discussions concern the twenty-two trumps, not the Queen of Batons.

### 1783–1789 — Etteilla

First comprehensive published tarot cartomancy and first purpose-built occult tarot. Queen of Batons becomes numbered No. 23 and receives printed upright/reversed meanings.

### 1854–1856 — Éliphas Lévi

*Dogme et rituel de la haute magie* connects the twenty-two trumps with Hebrew letters and ceremonial magic.

- Lévi does not give the Queen the later Golden Dawn decans.
- His wand/cup/sword/pentacle schema helps transform the suits into magical implements.

### 1870 — Paul Christian

*Histoire de la magie* develops Egyptian initiation narratives around twenty-two arcana.

- No specific Queen-of-Wands doctrine comparable to *Book T*.
- His importance is spreading pseudo-Egyptian temple initiation mythology.

### 1888 — Mathers

Mathers’s *The Tarot* supplies English divinatory meanings for every card, calling this one Queen of Sceptres.

### 1889 — Papus and Wirth

Papus systematizes tarot through YHVH and cyclic numerology. Wirth publishes twenty-two trumps only.

### Circa 1890s–1912 — Golden Dawn / Book T

The Queen becomes Water of Fire, Heh of Yod, Queen of the Thrones of Flame, 21° Pisces–20° Aries.

### 1909–1911 — Waite–Smith

Smith introduces sunflower, lion throne, desert, and black cat; Waite publishes the “life and animation” and “more magnetic” descriptions.

### 1938–1944 / 1969 — Thoth

Harris paints the Golden Dawn Queen as an ecstatic armored fire sovereign. Crowley publishes the commentary in 1944; the full deck appears commercially in 1969.

### Mid-twentieth century onward

Marteau, Case/BOTA, Eden Gray, Rachel Pollack, Mary K. Greer, Jodorowsky and Costa, and numerous later authors shift the Queen from a literal woman—often classified by complexion and marital status—toward psychological agency, charisma, creativity, embodied confidence, and shadow qualities such as jealousy or domination.

### Yod and Heh clarified

The item-specific brief mentions both “Water (Heh) of the suit” and “Yod of the Tetragrammaton.” In Golden Dawn teaching these refer to different axes:

- Wands/Fire = **Yod**.
- Queen/Water = first **Heh**.
- Queen of Wands = **Heh operating within Yod**, hence Water of Fire.

Calling the Queen herself “Yod of Wands” collapses rank and suit. The fiery/Yod court rank is the Golden Dawn King or Crowleyan Knight, not the Queen.

### Zodiacal decans

The Queen’s thirty-degree sector comprises:

- 21°–30° Pisces: usually associated with the Ten of Cups in Golden Dawn minor-card decans.
- 1°–10° Aries: Two of Wands, “Dominion.”
- 11°–20° Aries: Three of Wands, “Established Strength” or “Virtue,” depending on deck vocabulary.

She therefore spans one water-sign decan and two fire-sign decans. This boundary-crossing helps explain later language of fluid fire, transformation, and unstable but powerful combination. It is an occult construction, not an astronomical discovery encoded in fifteenth-century art.

### Hebrew-letter and Tree-of-Life conflicts

No individual Hebrew letter or Tree-of-Life **path** belongs to the Queen of Wands in the Golden Dawn system. The twenty-two paths correspond to trumps. Courts are placed through sephiroth, worlds, elements, and zodiacal sectors.

The famous Crowley switch—“Tzaddi is not the Star”—concerns the Emperor and Star, Heh and Tzaddi. It does not change this Queen’s Heh-as-court-rank attribution. Confusing those two uses of *Heh* creates a false “Queen/Emperor/Star controversy.”

## Divinatory meanings by author

## Etteilla and the Etteilla school

### Upright

Countrywoman; sensible, economical, virtuous or intelligent woman; harvest, rural pleasure, or a country excursion.

### Reversed

Meanings vary sharply by edition. Service, devotion, goodness, or assistance may appear on the reversed end; some later decks exchange upright and reverse keywords.

**Textual caution:** The most easily accessible wording comes from nineteenth-century followers such as Julia Orsini/Z. Lismon, not from a clean 1780s authorial card list. The divergence is itself historical evidence: Etteilla was a school and publishing lineage, not one immutable deck.

## S. L. MacGregor Mathers, 1888

Mathers calls the card **Queen of Sceptres**.

### Upright

> “Woman living in the country, Lady of the Manor, Love of Money, Avarice, Usury.”

### Reversed

The accessible transcription is truncated after “R.” in some web editions; variant reprints supply favorable traits derived from the Etteilla tradition. A definitive reversed quotation should be checked against a clean 1888 scan rather than completed from modern lists. [Mathers transcription](https://hermetics.net/media-library/tarot/samuel-liddell-macgregor-mathers-the-tarot/).

Mathers also recommends the Queen of Wands/Sceptres as significator for a “very fair” female querent. This complexion convention conflicts with Waite, who calls her dark.

## Papus, 1889

Papus gives:

> “Femme brune, amie.”

He further describes a serious woman of good counsel, often a mother of a family. His general framework makes Batons signify “les Entreprises et la Gloire”—enterprises and glory. [Papus PDF](https://www.cercle-papus.com/ouvrages/papus-le-tarot-des-bohemiens.pdf), pp. 316–319.

This is still principally a literal person-card, not a gender-neutral psychological energy.

## Waite, 1910

### Upright

> “A dark woman, countrywoman, friendly, chaste, loving, honourable.”

Waite adds favorable disposition toward a nearby man, interest in the querent when beside a woman, love of money, or success in business.

### Reversed

> “Good, economical, obliging, serviceable.”

In certain combinations, he adds opposition, jealousy, deceit, and infidelity. [*Pictorial Key* text](https://pdfcoffee.com/a-pictorial-key-to-the-tarot--pdf-free.html), pp. 75–76.

Waite’s reversal is not simply the opposite of upright. It preserves Etteilla-style alternate keywords.

## Golden Dawn / Book T

The Golden Dawn ordinarily speaks of a card as well- or ill-dignified rather than relying solely on physical reversal.

### Well dignified

Adaptability, steady application, stable rule, attraction, command, popularity, kindness, generosity.

### Ill dignified

Obstinacy, revenge, domination, tyranny, and turning against another without cause.

## Crowley, 1944

Crowley did not favor the simple upright/reversed binary; dignity and surrounding cards determine expression.

### Balanced or favorable

Adaptability, persistent energy, calm authority, attractiveness, generosity, friendship, love, and steady work.

### Unbalanced or adverse

Vanity, snobbery, brooding, wrong conclusions, savagery, gullibility, stupidity, obstinacy, tyranny, resentment, fickleness, or disloyalty.

His memorable image is that when she “misses her bite, she breaks her jaw.” [*Book of Thoth*](https://hermetic.com/crowley/book-of-thoth/court-cards).

## Paul Foster Case

Case’s accessible *Oracle of the Tarot* material broadly preserves an active, magnetic, practical woman and Golden Dawn-derived elemental/astrological correspondences.

**Finding:** A stable, verified primary-text pair of Case’s exact upright and reversed Queen-of-Wands words was not located in the consulted digital copy. Modern websites often blend Case, Waite, and Golden Dawn without attribution. It would be misleading to manufacture a quotation.

## Rachel Pollack

In *Seventy-Eight Degrees of Wisdom* (1980), Pollack treats the Queen as self-confident, warm, sexually and creatively alive, and able to enjoy power without denying femininity. The black cat introduces mystery and instinct beneath the overtly solar image.

Her adverse expression includes self-absorption, domineering behavior, jealousy, or fire turned inward. Pollack helped popularize the Queen as an archetypal mode available to anyone rather than only a literal dark-haired woman.

## Mary K. Greer

Greer’s *Tarot for Yourself* (1984) encourages readers to experience every court as a role or part of the self. The Queen becomes confidence, social magnetism, creative leadership, warmth, and the ability to energize others.

In reversal work, Greer does not reduce a card to one antonym. Its energy may be blocked, delayed, private, excessive, projected onto another person, or emerging unconsciously. This method is a major source of contemporary “shadow Queen” readings.

## Esoteric and symbolic readings

## Elemental and Hermetic

**First securely traced complete appearance:** Golden Dawn *Book T*, late nineteenth century.

Water of Fire can signify flame made fluid: color, radiance, heat distributed through a medium, emotional enthusiasm, and the capacity to shape rather than merely discharge will. The steady flame beneath her throne expresses energy stabilized by the Queen’s formative function.

Crowley spread this interpretation through *The Equinox*, *777*, and *The Book of Thoth*. Regardie, Wang, and the Ciceros made it widely accessible.

## Qabalistic

The queen corresponds to Heh, the receptive/formative stage of the divine name YHVH. In Wands, she is Heh of Yod.

Later Golden Dawn teaching places Queens in Binah, Understanding, and Wands in Atziluth, the archetypal world of Fire. Thus “Binah in Atziluth.”

This is **Hermetic Qabalah**, not a teaching found in Jewish sources about playing cards.

## Astrological

The Queen rules 21° Pisces–20° Aries: the zodiacal transition from mutable water to cardinal fire.

Later interpreters read this as:

- dream or feeling becoming initiative;
- empathy becoming will;
- gestation becoming action;
- spring equinox imagery;
- fire that can adapt because it contains water.

These are interpretations of the Golden Dawn allocation, not reasons independently documented for its creation.

## Alchemical

The Water-of-Fire formula naturally invited alchemical analogies: volatile tincture, fluid flame, dissolution within calcination, and the contained heat of a vessel.

**Finding:** No fifteenth-century alchemical commentary on the Queen of Batons was found. Card-specific alchemical readings appear to be twentieth-century elaborations of Golden Dawn elemental doctrine. Claims that the Visconti Queen secretly illustrates an alchemical operation are unsupported unless tied to a specific historical text.

## Christian

The Renaissance image can be understood within Christian court culture, but it has no explicit saintly attribute. A crowned woman with a staff is not enough to identify a biblical queen, the Virgin, or a virtue.

Waite’s “chaste,” “loving,” and “honourable” vocabulary reflects Edwardian moral characterization more than an established Christian iconographic identity.

Modern associations with Mary Magdalene, the Queen of Sheba, Sophia, or a “sacred feminine” are deck- or reader-specific inventions.

## Jungian

Later Jungian tarot writers treat the Queen as an archetype of mature sovereign femininity and Wands as libido in Jung’s broad sense of psychic energy.

Positive pole: creative self-possession, courage, warmth, erotic vitality, leadership.

Shadow pole: inflation, possessiveness, devouring or controlling fire, jealousy, theatricality, burnout.

**Historical qualification:** Jung wrote about alchemy, archetypes, and synchronicity, but did not publish a Queen-of-Wands interpretation. “Jungian Queen of Wands” is a post-Jungian tarot synthesis.

## Feminist, queer, and psychological readings

From the late twentieth century onward, the card increasingly ceased to mean only a biological woman. It may represent:

- a leadership style;
- confidence in one’s body or creative identity;
- charisma without institutional rank;
- reclaimed anger or sexuality;
- performance and public presence;
- the capacity to nurture another’s agency;
- shadow forms such as attention-seeking or coercive charm.

These readings are modern and should be identified as such, not described as meanings preserved unchanged since Renaissance Italy.

## Literature and film

### Italo Calvino, *The Castle of Crossed Destinies*

Calvino structures narratives by laying out tarot cards, using both Visconti-style and Marseille imagery in different sections.

**Relevant but not exclusive:** Court cards become characters in combinatorial stories, but there is no single canonical identification making the entire work specifically about the Queen of Wands.

### T. S. Eliot, *The Waste Land*

Madame Sosostris names “the drowned Phoenician Sailor,” Belladonna, the “one-eyed merchant,” and other tarot-like figures.

**Finding:** The Queen of Wands is not named. Identifying Belladonna as this Queen is speculative.

### W. B. Yeats

Yeats belonged to the Golden Dawn and knew its elemental and tarot systems.

**Documented connection:** He participated in the occult environment that produced *Book T*.

**Not established:** No specific poem or surviving statement securely centers on the Queen of Wands.

### Ingmar Bergman

*The Seventh Seal* strongly features chess, death, medieval imagery, and existential allegory.

**Finding:** No securely documented quotation of the Queen of Wands was found. Broad resemblance to tarot’s Death imagery does not establish a card-specific source.

### Alejandro Jodorowsky’s films

Jodorowsky’s cinematic imagery shares initiatory, alchemical, circus, sexual, and sacred-symbolic concerns with his tarot work.

**Finding:** No individual building or film tableau could be securely documented as a direct quotation of the Queen of Wands rather than a general overlap of symbolic vocabulary.

## Paintings and buildings

No pre-1909 painting or building was found that is securely documented as quoting this particular card.

Modern murals, paintings, tattoos, fashion photographs, book covers, and installations frequently quote Smith’s frontal queen, sunflower, and black cat. Unless the artist states the source, visual similarity alone should not be promoted into documented influence.

## Controversies and disputes

## Egyptian origin

### Claim

Court de Gébelin claimed in 1781 that tarot preserved ancient Egyptian wisdom. Etteilla, Paul Christian, Papus, and later occultists expanded the story.

### Evidence

No tarot cards or descriptions of tarot exist in ancient Egypt. The earliest secure standard tarot evidence is fifteenth-century northern Italian.

### Card-specific result

The Visconti Queen is a European court figure with an Italian baton suit. Her eventual “Egyptian” meanings originate in late-eighteenth- and nineteenth-century French occultism.

The Mamluk ancestry of European playing cards does provide a real Egyptian connection—medieval Islamic Egypt, not pharaonic hieroglyphic wisdom. Conflating these is a common historical error.

## “Gypsy” or Romani origin

### Claim

Papus’s title *Le Tarot des Bohémiens* helped popularize the idea that Romani people carried tarot from Egypt or India.

### Evidence

Tarot is documented in Italian courts before evidence of a distinct Romani tarot tradition. The ethnonym “Gypsy” itself arose from the mistaken European belief that Romani people came from Egypt.

### Conclusion

As an origin history it is **legend**. Romani individuals have certainly practiced cartomancy, but that does not establish invention or ancient transmission of tarot.

## Cathar origin

### Claim

Tarot images supposedly encode Cathar dualism or preserve persecuted southern-French heresy.

### Evidence

No medieval Cathar text mentions tarot, and the Cathar institutions had been destroyed well before the first tarot documentation. The earliest evidence lies in northern Italian court culture.

### Queen-specific finding

No feature of the early Queen of Batons requires a Cathar reading. “Feminine spiritual authority” is too general to function as evidence.

## Templar origin

### Claim

The twenty-two trumps preserve Templar secrets carried after the suppression of the order.

### Evidence

No Templar record, card, or intermediate document links the order to tarot. The Templars were suppressed in the early fourteenth century; tarot appears in the fifteenth.

The Queen of Wands, being a suit court rather than a trump, is even less relevant to the supposed twenty-two-part code.

## Kabbalah before the occultists

### Claim

Hebrew letters, YHVH, and the Tree of Life were hidden in tarot from its creation.

### Evidence

The earliest surviving cards contain no Hebrew letters, and the first explicit published tarot–Hebrew associations appear with de Mellet and later Lévi. Golden Dawn court attributions appear later still.

### Conclusion

Heh of Yod is a documented Golden Dawn attribution, not a decipherment of a Renaissance inscription.

## Identity of the Visconti Queen

Moakley and Kaplan correctly emphasized family heraldry and court context. Their work made named-person readings tempting.

Yet:

- no label names the Queen;
- luxury court figures can be types rather than portraits;
- workshop stylization limits physiognomic comparison;
- family emblems may identify patronage, not sitter.

“Bianca Maria Visconti” should therefore be labeled **possible association**, not fact.

## The black cat

Proposed meanings include:

- Smith’s acquaintance’s pet;
- witch’s familiar;
- intuition or occult knowledge;
- feminine independence;
- absorbed lunar darkness beneath solar imagery;
- Bastet;
- the Queen’s animal or shadow nature.

Only the cat’s presence is documented. Waite does not explain it. All symbolic identifications are later interpretations; the pet hypothesis is a modern biographical reconstruction.

## Hair color

Mathers recommends this Queen for a “very fair” woman. Waite calls her “a dark woman.” Golden Dawn imagery gives red-gold hair. Modern decks depict every complexion.

This is not a copyist error with one correct solution. Different cartomantic systems deliberately classified court cards differently.

## Number XIII

Calling the Queen “the thirteenth card of Wands” is mathematically reasonable in an Ace-to-King list but historically noncanonical. It can also cause confusion with Death, trump XIII.

Grand Etteilla No. 23 is a genuine numbered variant, but it belongs only to Etteilla’s reordered pack.

## Wands versus clubs

The Mamluk suit is polo sticks, not European cudgels. Italian *bastoni* and Spanish *bastos* became club/baton imagery. English occult decks preferred “Wands,” stressing magical and living rods.

This evolution is both linguistic and iconographic. It is not proof that the suit was always a ceremonial magic wand.

## Misprints and copying traditions

- Historic cards vary between *Royne*, *Reyne*, *Reine*, *Baston*, *Bastons*, and *Bâton(s)*. These are orthographic and workshop variants, not separate esoteric cards.
- Grand Etteilla editions sometimes reverse or replace keywords. Later manuals then preserve the variant as authoritative.
- Conver reproductions often silently redraw damaged lines and advertise the result as “1760.”
- Wirth-branded seventy-eight-card decks may encourage the false belief that Wirth drew minors in 1889.
- “Thoth published 1944” confuses the book and preliminary images with the complete 1969 commercial deck.
- Modern tables sometimes call the Queen simply “Aries,” suppressing her final Pisces decan.
- Some lists assign her 20° Pisces–20° Aries; *Book T* says from above the last decan of Pisces to above 20° Aries, normally rendered 21° Pisces–20° Aries. The one-degree discrepancy is mostly inclusive/exclusive notation.

## Historians’ correction

Michael Dummett’s core correction was methodological: tarot must first be treated as a documented family of card games invented in Renaissance Italy. Ronald Decker, Thierry Depaulis, Stuart Kaplan, Robert M. Place, and Helen Farley differ in emphases, but accept the Italian documentary chronology and reject an ancient Egyptian tarot deck.

- **Dummett:** game history, regional rules, trump orders.
- **Depaulis:** archives, French cardmakers, nomenclature, and cartomancy.
- **Decker:** occult tarot’s intellectual genealogy.
- **Kaplan:** deck documentation, images, and collecting history.
- **Place:** iconographic history combined with symbolic interpretation.
- **Farley:** cultural history and the changing social uses of tarot.

Their correction does not make occult meanings “invalid” as religious or artistic creations. It dates them properly.

## Open questions

1. **Exact dating of the Visconti–Sforza Queen:** Catalogues range from the 1440s to the later fifteenth century. Technical study of paper, pigments, punching, and workshop hands may narrow it.

2. **Specific sitter:** Bianca Maria Visconti remains possible as an association, but no inscription or inventory proves it.

3. **Immediate model for the European queen rank:** The developmental path from Mamluk deputies and varied European courts to tarot’s standard four courts remains partly reconstructive.

4. **Golden Dawn authorship:** Mathers is the leading architect of *Book T*, but the surviving corpus was collaborative and transmitted in copies. The exact author of every court-card sentence is not always demonstrable.

5. **Why 21° Pisces–20° Aries?** The allocation follows the Golden Dawn’s systematic distribution of thirty-six decans among thirty-six small cards and thirty-degree sectors among courts. No manuscript located explains a unique historical reason for assigning this particular boundary to this Queen beyond the system’s sequence.

6. **Smith’s cat:** The Snuffles hypothesis is appealing but requires firmer documentary confirmation from letters, diaries, or an artist’s statement.

7. **Smith’s specific visual sources:** Marseille courts, stage design, contemporary illustration, Golden Dawn knowledge, and Smith’s surroundings all matter, but their individual contribution to this card is not fully documented.

8. **Etteilla’s original wording:** Later Grand Etteilla manuals and deck families differ. A card-by-card collation of the 1783–1785 texts, circa-1789 cards, and nineteenth-century Type I–III editions is needed to distinguish Etteilla’s own terms from those of his school.

9. **Early literary references:** Fifteenth- and sixteenth-century poems may contain still-unindexed or ambiguously titled female baton figures. No early text located gives the modern fiery-charismatic meaning.

10. **Marseille reconstruction:** No evidence presently permits recovery of one exact, symbolically pristine prototype behind Noblet, Dodal, Conver, and related patterns.
