# Five of Wands: Research Dossier

## Method and evidentiary labels

This dossier uses the following labels throughout:

- **[Documented fact]** Directly supported by a surviving card, archival record, contemporary publication, or the author’s own text.
- **[Scholarly reconstruction]** A conclusion inferred by modern historians from surviving objects and documents.
- **[Tradition]** A received practice or interpretation whose origin is partly recoverable but which is not an original feature of the card.
- **[Esoteric attribution]** A correspondence intentionally imposed by an occult author or school.
- **[Disputed claim]** A proposition for which significant counter-evidence or disagreement exists.
- **[Legend]** A historical-origin story unsupported by contemporary evidence.
- **[Modern invention]** A recent interpretive or artistic development not found in the historical card tradition.

“Tarot de Marseille,” “Minor Arcana,” “Fire,” “Geburah,” and “Saturn in Leo” belong to different historical layers. They should not be projected backward onto every fifteenth-century Five of Batons.

**[Documented fact]** A normal tarot pack contains four fourteen-card suits—ten number cards plus Page, Knight, Queen, and King—and a separate series of trumps. The Five of Batons is one of the ordinary suit cards. The Morgan catalogue emphasizes that Renaissance tarot was a card game rather than the apparatus of professional fortune-tellers. [Morgan Library catalogue](https://www.themorgan.org/manuscript/158525).

**[Documented fact]** The labels “Major Arcana” and “Minor Arcana” are not fifteenth-century game terminology. They belong to nineteenth-century occult discourse and are usually attributed to Jean-Baptiste Pitois, writing as Paul Christian.

**[Important scope correction]** The Papess/Juno, Pope/Jupiter, Maison Dieu/Tower, Justice/Strength, Fool numbering, and Emperor/Star Hebrew-letter disputes concern trumps. They illuminate the changing structure of tarot packs but do not rename, renumber, or change the game identity of the Five of Wands.

## The names

### Fifteenth- and sixteenth-century Italy

**[Documented fact]** The earliest packs were called *carte da trionfi*, *ludus triumphorum*, or simply *trionfi*. The word referred to the game or its special trump cards, not to the Five as an individual allegory.

**[Documented fact]** The suit was *bastoni*: staffs, batons, cudgels, or clubs. The card itself needed no literary title beyond its suit and count.

**[Documented fact]** The terms *tarocchi* and French *taraux* are first documented around 1505, at Ferrara and Avignon respectively. Earlier cards now called tarot were generally described as *trionfi*. The Five therefore began historically as something like “five batons in a pack of triumphs,” not as an “Arcana Minor card called Strife.”

### France

- *Cinq de Bâtons*: the normal historical and Marseille name.
- *Cinq de Baguettes*: a later translation or modernization used by some occult decks.
- Etteilla’s sequence numbered the entire pack continuously. In later Grand Etteilla arrangements the Five of Batons is commonly card **31**.

### England

Mathers listed the equivalents as:

> “Bastoni … Bâtons … Wands, Sceptres, or Clubs.”

His table also associated this suit with ordinary playing-card **diamonds**, a mapping that conflicts with the now-common Wands–clubs equation. S. L. MacGregor Mathers, *The Tarot: Its Occult Signification, Use in Fortune-Telling, and Method of Play* (London, 1888), suit table; [online transcription](https://hermetics.net/media-library/tarot/samuel-liddell-macgregor-mathers-the-tarot/).

“Wands” became standard in English occult tarot through the Golden Dawn, Waite, Case, and Crowley.

### Spain

*Cinco de Bastos* also names the corresponding card in ordinary Spanish-suited packs. That resemblance is historically meaningful: Italian and Spanish packs preserve the Latin suit of clubs/batons. It does not mean an ordinary Spanish Five of Clubs carries the Golden Dawn’s astrological or divinatory meanings.

### Germany and Central Europe

*Fünf der Stäbe* is chiefly a divinatory-deck translation. Most historical German-language Tarock packs adopted French suits; in such packs no literal Five of Batons exists. The corresponding low suit card may be the five of hearts, diamonds, clubs, or spades according to the regional pack and rules.

### Golden Dawn and Thoth

- Golden Dawn: **Lord of Strife**
- Thoth: **STRIFE**
- Card number: 5 within Wands; it is not Trump V.
- Astrology: Saturn in the first ten degrees of Leo.
- Qabalah: Geburah of Yod/Atziluth.

## Iconography, deck by deck

## Early Milanese painted packs, approximately 1440–1460

### Cary-Yale / Visconti di Modrone

**[Documented object; dating partly reconstructed]** This luxury Milanese pack is usually dated around the 1440s. It contains unusual additional court cards, including female counterparts to Page and Knight. Its numbered suit cards are heraldic and ornamental.

The Five of Batons is not a narrative scene. Five elongated batons are arranged as countable suit signs against a rich painted ground. The purpose is immediate game recognition, decorated with courtly materials and dynastic emblems.

**Meaning at the time:** no surviving accompanying text assigns conflict, competition, Fire, or Geburah. Those are later overlays.

### Brera-Brambilla

**[Documented object; attribution reconstructed]** Usually associated with the Visconti court and Bonifacio Bembo’s workshop or circle. The surviving cards are held in the Pinacoteca di Brera.

As with related Milanese cards, the numbered Batons are repeated emblems rather than enacted stories. Gold, punchwork, heraldic color, and symmetrical distribution turn a functional pip into a luxury object.

### Pierpont Morgan-Bergamo / Visconti-Sforza

**[Documented object]** Approximately 1450s, divided among the Morgan Library, Accademia Carrara, and the Colleoni collection. The Morgan describes the surviving group as one of the most complete fifteenth-century tarot ensembles and attributes it probably to Bonifacio Bembo. [Morgan overview](https://www.themorgan.org/collection/tarot-cards).

The Five is an ornamental arrangement of five straight batons. The suit is connected visually with Sforza heraldry; surviving Batons court figures carry staffs with elaborate terminals.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** Whether the Cary-Yale, Brambilla, or Morgan-Bergamo Five is literally the earliest surviving Five cannot be settled merely from the broad catalogue dates. The deck chronologies overlap and individual cards have been dispersed.

### Other Milanese fragments

Numerous isolated fifteenth-century fragments exist, but many packs are too incomplete to provide a Five of Batons. A modern reproduction that fills missing cards is not evidence that the reconstructed card survived.

## Ferrarese and related fifteenth-century material

### Este cards

**[Documented objects]** Small groups of richly painted cards associated with Ferrara and the Este court survive. Many are isolated trumps or courts. No complete Ferrarese Five of Batons sequence survives from which a distinct local design history can be reconstructed.

**[Finding from absence]** The 1442 Ferrara accounts prove that *trionfi* packs with Cups, Coins, Swords, and Batons were made or purchased. They do not describe the artwork or occult meaning of the Five.

### Mantegna “Tarocchi”

**[Documented fact]** The so-called Mantegna Tarocchi are two series of fifty didactic engravings, probably produced in northern Italy around the 1460s. They are not a tarot pack, contain no four ordinary suits, and have no Five of Batons. Their relevance is comparative—Renaissance hierarchical imagery—not genealogical evidence for this card.

### Sola Busca, 1491

**[Documented fact]** The Sola Busca is the earliest complete surviving tarot whose pip cards are extensively figured rather than merely repeating suit signs. The engraved deck is dated 1491; the uniquely complete hand-colored copy is now at Brera.

Its Five of Batons is marked **V** and **CATVLO/Catulo**. It shows a single classically dressed male figure identified by the inscription as Catulus. He holds or is surrounded by the suit’s heavy implements and an emblematic object; the composition is not five boys fighting.

The identity is usually related to a Roman Catulus—most plausibly one of the historical Lutatii Catuli—but precise identification and narrative significance are disputed.

### Did Sola Busca supply Smith’s Five of Wands?

Two claims must be separated:

1. **[Strong scholarly reconstruction]** Sola Busca photographs influenced the Waite-Smith pip program generally. Photographs of the full deck entered the British Museum before Smith drew her deck. Some correspondences—notably the pierced-heart Three of Swords and burdened figures in the Ten cards—are visually persuasive.
2. **[Unsubstantiated stronger claim]** The Sola Busca **Five of Batons itself** was the direct source for Smith’s five-youth mock battle.

The second claim could not be verified. The Sola Busca Five has one named figure and does not furnish Smith’s group of five combatants. Smith may have drawn on the earlier deck’s broader precedent for narrativizing pips, but the scene-specific derivation is not demonstrated by the cards. The item-specific lead is therefore **partially verified, but overstated when phrased card-to-card**.

[Brera/Sola Busca image collection via Public Domain Review](https://publicdomainreview.org/collection/sola-busca).

## Rosenwald and Budapest sheets

**[Documented objects]** These late-fifteenth- or early-sixteenth-century woodcut sheets are important witnesses to inexpensive printed Italian tarot patterns, in contrast with the gilded court decks.

Where suit cards occur, the design principle remains repetition and ornamental interlacing, not individualized narrative illustration. Their significance for the Five lies in showing how baton pips could be made legible in mass-produced woodcut form.

**[Open issue]** Sheet reconstruction is difficult because cards are incomplete, uncut, or preserved as recycled printed matter. Attribution, chronology, and the original complete order remain matters of specialist reconstruction.

---

## Minchiate

**[Documented fact]** Minchiate expanded the tarot structure to 97 cards: 56 ordinary suit cards, 40 trumps, and the Fool. The Five of Batons remained an ordinary suit card.

Its iconography is conventional Italian pip geometry. Minchiate’s added zodiac, element, and virtue trumps did not turn its Five of Batons into “Saturn in Leo.” That attribution belongs to the Golden Dawn centuries later.

In play the Five is a low-ranking card in the long suit of Batons and normally has no intrinsic point value beyond its contribution to grouped card counting.

---

## Bolognese pattern

**[Documented fact]** Tarocchino Bolognese reduces the pack to 62 cards by removing the 2–5 from each ordinary suit. Consequently, the Five of Batons is **absent** from the standard historical Bolognese tarocchino pack. [Accademia del Tarocchino Bolognese](https://www.tarocchinobolognese.it/).

That absence is crucial: no continuous Bolognese Five iconography exists in the reduced game pack. Modern 78-card “Bolognese-style” restorations may supply one, but that is reconstruction.

---

## Sicilian pattern

**[Documented fact]** The traditional Sicilian pack is reduced to 63 playing cards. In Batons, Swords, and Cups, the Five is the lowest remaining number card; only Coins retains a Four. The Sicilian suit uses Spanish-style knobbly clubs rather than the smooth intersecting rods of northern Italian or Marseille patterns.

The Five is indexed “5 B” in modern packs. It is a low card, not a titled allegory. [Rules and pack description](https://www.pagat.com/tarot/sicilian.html).

## Tarot de Marseille lineage

### Jean Noblet, Paris, approximately 1650

The Five is a non-scenic pip: four diagonally interlaced batons surrounding or crossed by a fifth central vertical baton, with floral foliage occupying open spaces. Roman numerals or repeated counts identify the rank.

### Jean Dodal, Lyon, approximately 1701–1715

Dodal preserves the interwoven lattice. Woodblock cutting, stencil colors, leaf forms, terminals, and the exact over-under sequence differ, but the governing structure remains five countable batons plus vegetation.

### Nicolas Conver, Marseille, 1760/1761

Conver’s familiar type shows four crossing rods in paired diagonals and a fifth vertical member through the center, surrounded by curling leaves and a colored central lozenge. A surviving BnF witness traditionally called “Conver 1760” is catalogued as a later printing from the inherited blocks or pattern, so object date and design date must not be conflated.

A detailed visual description of the BnF witness notes red shafts, pale-blue interweaving, yellow leaves, and the numeral V. [BnF-based description](https://marselia.app/fr/cards/cinq-de-baton).

### Besançon pattern

The Besançon pattern is defined principally by trump substitutions—Juno for the Papess and Jupiter for the Pope—made in Protestant or confessionally sensitive regions such as Alsace, Switzerland, and eastern France. The Five of Batons itself stays a conventional French pip. It was not renamed Juno or Jupiter.

### Grimaud / Paul Marteau

The twentieth-century Grimaud “Ancien Tarot de Marseille,” associated with Paul Marteau’s 1930 publication, regularized outlines and imposed a standardized color symbolism. The Five remains a geometric interlacement.

**[Disputed commercial genealogy]** Philippe Camoin argues that Marteau’s drawing follows a late Besançon/Lequart-Arnoult-derived model more closely than a literal Conver facsimile. [Camoin’s comparison](https://en.camoin.com/tarot/Grimaud-Arnoult-Marseilles-Tarot.html).

### Camoin-Jodorowsky, 1997

The Five remains non-figurative but is redrawn and recolored from multiple historical models. Camoin expressly says the result is not simply a Conver restoration and acknowledges borrowing from Marseille Types I and II and Besançon material. [Camoin interview](https://fr.camoin.com/tarot/_-10-Extraits-d-Interviews.html).

Jodorowsky and Marianne Costa read the five as a movement from stable four toward a new center or level. Their published schema says that the fives “enrich” and rise from material to spiritual. This is **[modern esoteric numerology]**, not documented Renaissance intent. [Jodorowsky-Costa text](https://www.camoin.com/media/PDF/art_du_tarot_jodorowsky.pdf).

## Etteilla’s Grand Etteilla, approximately 1788–1789 and later forms

**[Documented fact]** Etteilla was the first known person to design and publish a tarot specifically organized for occult divination. His patent application for a deck was filed in January 1789.

The Grand Etteilla did not simply reproduce the Marseille order. All 78 cards were consecutively numbered and carried upright/reversed keywords. The ordinary pips remained countable suit cards but were incorporated into a continuous “Book of Thoth.”

The Five of Batons is usually numbered **31**. In later Etteilla witnesses it remains recognizably a five-baton pip with inscriptions rather than becoming a five-person tableau.

Meanings transmitted in the Etteilla line:

- upright: *or*, fortune, opulence, wealth, success or gain;
- reversed: lawsuit, lost or confused legal affair, tribunal or judgment.

**[Source qualification]** Those meanings are securely present in the Etteilla-derived tradition and in Mathers’s explicitly Etteilla-derived list. Exact wording varies among Etteilla’s own *cahiers*, the 1791 *Dictionnaire synonymique*, D’Odoucet, Julia Orsini, and later Grand Etteilla manuals. It is unsafe to attribute every later expanded phrase verbatim to Etteilla personally.

## Oswald Wirth, 1889

Wirth’s original *Les 22 Arcanes du Tarot kabbalistique* depicted only the 22 trumps. It therefore contains **no Five of Wands**.

Any “Wirth Five” belongs to a later publisher’s completion or adaptation, not to Wirth’s 1889 deck. This is a frequent cataloguing error.

## Golden Dawn designs

### Book T

The Golden Dawn replaced traditional untitled pips with internally titled, astrologically determined compositions.

The surviving/public Book T form describes:

> “Two White Radiant Angelic Hands … hold … five wands or torches … One wand is upright in the middle; the others cross each other. Flames leap from the point of junction. Above the middle wand is the sign Saturn, and below is that of Leo.”

It names the card:

> “THE LORD OF STRIFE.”

And gives:

> “Violent strife and boldness, rashness, cruelty, violence, lust, desire, prodigality and generosity; depending on whether the card is well or ill dignified.”

*The Equinox*, vol. I, no. 8 (1912), pp. 176–77 in the original pagination; [scan/transcription](https://hermetic.com/crowley/equinox/i/viii/eqi08016).

### Attribution

- Five → Geburah, Severity/Strength
- Wands → Fire; Yod; Atziluth
- Decan → first decan of Leo
- Planetary ruler → Saturn
- Approximate tropical span → July 23–August 1, with small variations depending on ephemeris convention
- Angels → Vahaviah and Yelayel in the Golden Dawn’s Shem ha-Mephorash allocation

**[Esoteric attribution]** This system is late nineteenth century. There is no evidence that fifteenth-century cardmakers assigned Saturn in Leo to this card.

### Wang and Cicero reconstructions

Robert Wang’s *Golden Dawn Tarot* and the later Cicero Golden Dawn deck are twentieth-century visual reconstructions based on Order papers. They show the prescribed crossed ritual wands, cloud-hands, flames, and astrological glyphs rather than five fighting youths.

Differences between them arise from color-scale choices, manuscript variants, and the artists’ reconstruction of ritual implements. Neither is the unique surviving “original Golden Dawn deck.”

## Waite-Smith, 1909

Pamela Colman Smith depicts five youths in differently colored clothing, each holding a leafy staff. Their rods cross in a confused overhead mesh. No one has fallen, bloodied another, or clearly won. The open landscape and theatrical poses allow either combat or play.

Waite’s own description is exact:

> “A posse of youths, who are brandishing staves, as if in sport or strife. It is mimic warfare.”

His meanings are:

> “Imitation, as, for example, sham fight, but also the strenuous competition and struggle of the search after riches and fortune. In this sense it connects with the battle of life.”

Reversed:

> “Litigation, disputes, trickery, contradiction.”

A. E. Waite, *The Pictorial Key to the Tarot* (London: William Rider & Son, 1910), Part III, “Five of Wands,” approximately pp. 83–84 depending on issue; [public-domain text](https://citeseerx.ist.psu.edu/document?doi=7a15961664bced51d1162198bce46d173e40dd41&repid=rep1&type=pdf).

### What changed

- Marseille: five botanical-ornamental suit signs.
- Golden Dawn: astral ritual diagram of Saturn in Leo.
- Smith: an intelligible human scene staging “strife.”
- Waite: softened Book T’s cruelty and violence into mimic conflict and worldly competition, while retaining Etteilla/Mathers’s riches and litigation residues.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** Smith synthesized Waite’s assigned meanings, theatrical visual language, Golden Dawn teaching, and the precedent of illustrated Sola Busca pips.

**[Not verified]** No primary document has been found in which Waite tells Smith to copy the Sola Busca Five specifically.

## Paul Foster Case and BOTA

Jessie Burns Parke drew the BOTA deck under Case’s direction. It was issued as black-line art intended for students to color. Its trumps substantially revise Waite-Smith; its pip arrangements follow Case’s Hermetic curriculum.

Case retains:

- Five of Wands;
- Saturn in Leo;
- first decan of Leo;
- Geburah and Fire;
- “Strife” or competition as the central keyword;
- good expressions such as boldness, command, and generosity;
- ill-dignified cruelty, violence, lust, prodigality, and rashness.

His design places the Tablets of the Law in a triangle, linking five with Justice/Severity and the sphere of Mars. This is BOTA symbolism rather than a historical Marseille feature.

**[Publication caution]** Case’s *Oracle of the Tarot* circulated as course material in several forms; online scans often lack stable edition data. Its wording should not be assigned a single publication year without identifying the specific BOTA issue.

## Crowley-Harris Thoth Tarot, painted 1938–1943; published 1969

The finished Thoth card is titled **STRIFE**. Frieda Harris replaces both Marseille vegetation and Smith’s human quarrel with ritual geometry.

Crowley explains:

> “This card is referred to Geburah in the suit of Fire. Geburah itself being fiery, it is a purely active force. It is ruled also by Saturn and Leo.”

The central staff is the Wand of the Chief Adept. Two Phoenix-headed wands signify destruction or purgation by fire and renewed energy; another pair represents subordinate adepts and the mitigating feminine influence.

Crowley writes:

> “There is no limit to the scope of this volcanic energy.”

And elsewhere:

> “The Five of Wands is therefore a personality; the nature of this is summed up in the Tarot by calling it ‘Strife’.”

Aleister Crowley, *The Book of Thoth* (London: O.T.O., 1944), “The Small Cards,” Five of Wands; [online text](https://hermetic.com/crowley/book-of-thoth/small-cards).

### Harris’s contribution

The card is not merely a diagram copied from Book T. Harris turns the prescribed wands into a tense, near-architectural field of intersecting ritual forces. The heavy central Chief Adept wand visibly restrains or commands four opposing energies.

### Tzaddi/Heh

Crowley’s “Tzaddi is not the Star” revision exchanges the traditional Golden Dawn Hebrew-letter assignments of the Emperor and Star. It has **no direct effect** on the Five of Wands, whose decan, Sephirah, and suit attribution remain Saturn in Leo, Geburah of Fire.

## Notable modern redesigns

Most modern decks belong to one of four families:

1. **Marseille-derived:** retain five abstract or botanical rods.
2. **Waite-Smith-derived:** depict a quarrel, game, sports contest, debate, audition, or clashing team.
3. **Thoth-derived:** emphasize pressure, explosive force, frustration, or crossed magical weapons.
4. **Revisionist/Jungian:** transform the figures into competing inner drives, creative multiplicity, group process, or necessary conflict.

These are **[modern inventions]** when presented as ancient meanings. They are legitimate redesign strategies but not evidence about the Renaissance card.

# The game

## Original status

The Five of Batons is a suited card. It is not one of the 21 permanent trumps and cannot normally beat a card of another suit merely by being a Five.

In classical tarot games:

- players must follow suit when able;
- a trump must often be played when unable to follow suit, subject to regional rules;
- the trick is won by the highest trump, or otherwise the highest card of the suit led;
- the Fool normally has a special excuse function rather than acting as an ordinary high trump.

## Rank

Batons and Swords are traditionally the “long” or pointed suits. In many Italian games their numeral cards rank in natural order:

King, Queen, Knight, Page, 10, 9, 8, 7, 6, **5**, 4, 3, 2, Ace.

Cups and Coins often reverse their numeral order, making the Ace high among pips.

Consequently, where a complete suit survives, the Five of Batons is a middle-low card. In reduced Sicilian tarot it is the lowest Baton. In Bolognese tarocchino it is removed.

French tarot and Central European Tarock developed different suit reductions and ranking conventions. In French-suited Tarock packs, the historical Italian Baton identity disappears with the suit sign.

## Point value

The Five is ordinarily a low “empty” card:

- it carries no special individual point premium;
- it helps form counting groups or packets;
- kings and certain trumps are valuable honors;
- the Five’s practical value depends more on trick position, voiding a suit, and endgame control than on intrinsic points.

Italian point-counting often values Kings and key trumps at five, Queens at four, Knights at three, Pages at two, and counts the remaining cards collectively. Treccani summarizes this conventional hierarchy. [Treccani](https://www.treccani.it/enciclopedia/tarocchi/).

## Sicilian play

The Five is the bottom card in Batons, Cups, and Swords. It can still matter tactically as a safe lead, discard, or component of captured-card counting, but it is not an honor.

## Minchiate

The Five remains one of the ordinary low Batons. Minchiate’s enlarged trump sequence does not affect its suit identity.

## Trump-order disputes

Historians distinguish three broad early Italian trump-order families:

- **Order A:** associated especially with Bologna and Florence;
- **Order B:** Ferrara and Venice;
- **Order C:** Milan and the lineage that helped generate the Marseille order.

These systems differ over such matters as the placement of virtues, Emperor/Pope groupings, Angel/World order, and the relative positions later numbered Justice VIII/XI and Strength XI/VIII.

They do **not** change the Five of Batons, because the Five is outside the trump sequence.

### The Fool

The Fool was historically unnumbered and normally served as an “excuse”: it could be played without following suit or losing itself, although rules differed concerning compensation and the final trick. It is not an original “Trump 0” in the modern divinatory sense.

# Chronology of attestation

### Before approximately 1425: Marziano da Tortona

**[Documented manuscript; relationship reconstructed]** Marziano described a pack commissioned by Filippo Maria Visconti, with sixteen deified classical figures and four suits of birds. Michelino da Besozzo painted it. This is the earliest known detailed description of a trump-taking pack connected with the milieu from which tarot emerged.

It has no Five of Batons. It is a precursor or collateral experiment, not a standard tarot deck.

### 1440

**[Documented fact]** A Florentine notarial record discovered by Franco Pratesi uses *naibi a trionfi*, pushing documentary evidence slightly earlier than the once-standard Ferrara date.

### 1 January 1441, Ferrara

Fourteen painted figures were ordered from Jacopo Sagramoro for festivities surrounding Bianca Maria Visconti’s visit. The entry does not itself say *trionfi*, so its tarot status is reconstructed, not certain.

### February and July 1442, Ferrara

**[Documented fact]** Este accounts record packs of *carte da trionfi*. One entry describes painted Cups, Coins, Swords, Batons, and figures for several packs; another records a pair of trionfi packs for Ercole and Sigismondo d’Este. [Le Tarot historical study](https://letarot.it/Le-origini-italiane-del-Ludus-Triumphorum_pag_pg439_ita.aspx).

This is evidence that a Five of Batons probably existed as part of the suit, but the ledger does not name that card individually.

### Approximately 1440s–1450s, Milan

The Cary-Yale, Brambilla, and Visconti-Sforza cards supply the earliest surviving physical examples of traditional suited tarot pips.

### Approximately 1450–1470: *Sermones de ludo cum aliis*

The anonymous Dominican preacher lists the trumps while condemning games of chance. It is the earliest surviving complete named trump list. It does not list ordinary cards one by one and supplies no occult interpretation. [Manuscript page](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File%3ASermones_de_Ludo_cum_Aliis.jpg).

### 1491: Sola Busca

The first securely dated, complete, extensively figured pip program. The Five is “Catulo.”

### Approximately 1500

Anonymous poems and later literary references enumerate or use the trumps as social and moral metaphors. Again, ordinary Fives are rarely named because poets were interested in allegorical trumps.

### 1526–1527: Teofilo Folengo

Folengo’s *Chaos del Triperuno* contains one of the earliest literary uses of tarot cards in a divinatory or characterizing arrangement. This is not evidence of an established ancient occult system, and the Five of Batons is not foundational to the episode.

### Sixteenth–seventeenth centuries

Tarot spreads through France, Switzerland, and German-speaking territories as a game. The Marseille-style Baton pip stabilizes as an interlaced, floral geometry.

### 1781: Court de Gébelin and Comte de Mellet

Tarot is declared an Egyptian book. Mellet associates the 22 trumps with Hebrew letters and publishes a divinatory method. Neither author gives a distinctive Saturn-in-Leo doctrine for the Five.

### 1783–1789: Etteilla

Etteilla publishes systematic tarot cartomancy and an occult deck with upright and reversed meanings. The Five acquires “gold/opulence” and legal meanings.

### 1856: Éliphas Lévi

*Dogme et Rituel de la Haute Magie* makes tarot central to ceremonial Kabbalah:

> “An imprisoned person, with no other book than the Tarot, if he knew how to use it, could in a few years acquire universal knowledge.”

This frequently translated sentence concerns tarot as a whole. Lévi does not provide a deck-by-deck divinatory entry for the Five of Wands or the later Saturn-in-Leo attribution. Any claim that he did is anachronistic.

### 1888: Mathers

The Five of Sceptres is assigned gold and wealth upright, legal proceedings reversed. This derives chiefly from Etteilla rather than the still-secret Golden Dawn decan system.

### 1889: Papus and Wirth

Papus integrates tarot, the Tetragrammaton, numbers, and occult cycles. Wirth publishes 22 trumps only. Neither supplies the later Thoth picture.

### Late 1880s–1890s: Golden Dawn Book T

The card becomes Lord of Strife, Saturn in Leo, Geburah in Atziluth.

### 1909–1910: Waite-Smith and *Pictorial Key*

Five youths enact mimic warfare; Waite publishes the scene and its meanings.

### 1912

The Golden Dawn card descriptions are printed in *The Equinox* I.8, making the Strife/Saturn-in-Leo system publicly accessible.

### 1938–1943 / 1944 / 1969

Harris paints the Thoth deck; Crowley publishes *The Book of Thoth* in 1944; the deck itself is first commercially published in 1969.

### Later twentieth century

Case/BOTA, Wang, Cicero, Marseille revivals, Pollack, Greer, Jodorowsky, and innumerable modern decks reinterpret the card psychologically or experientially.

## Conflicts

- Etteilla/Mathers: material gain and lawsuits.
- Golden Dawn/Crowley: volcanic conflict created by Saturn restricting Leo.
- Waite: theatrical or “mimic” conflict, while retaining wealth and litigation.
- Marseille numerology: the fifth principle entering a stable four.
- Modern reversal practice: conflict avoided or resolved—the opposite of Waite’s explicit litigation.
- Papus and Golden Dawn both use Tetragrammaton structures, but their detailed card allocations should not be treated as identical.
- “Five = Geburah” is specifically Hermetic Qabalah. It is not a universal Jewish Kabbalistic interpretation of a playing card.

# Divinatory meanings by author

Because older editions are public domain but Pollack and Greer remain copyrighted, the latter are summarized rather than extensively quoted.

## Etteilla tradition, 1780s–early 1800s

**Upright:** gold, money, fortune, prosperity, inheritance, gain, opulence.

**Reversed:** lawsuit, legal judgment, tribunal, lawyer, confused or lost legal business.

**Attribution warning:** These exact English clusters usually descend through later Etteilla dictionaries and Mathers. Surviving Etteilla-related sources vary in wording and card numbering.

## Mathers, 1888

> “Gold, Opulence, Gain, Heritage, Riches, Fortune, Money.”

Reversed:

> “Legal proceedings, Judgment, Law, Lawyer, Tribunal.”

Mathers, *The Tarot*, card 31, “Five of Sceptres”; [scan/transcription](https://logoilibrary.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/07/Mathers.pdf).

## Golden Dawn Book T

Well or ill dignified:

> “Violent strife and boldness, rashness, cruelty, violence, lust, desire, prodigality and generosity.”

Qabalistic summary:

> “Geburah of Yod (Quarrelling and fighting).”

The Golden Dawn normally used dignity rather than simple physical reversal.

## Waite, 1910

**Upright:** imitation, sham fight, strenuous competition, struggle for riches and fortune, the battle of life; possibly gold, gain, and opulence.

**Reversed:**

> “Litigation, disputes, trickery, contradiction.”

Waite is consciously reconciling two inherited streams: Etteilla/Mathers wealth-law meanings and Book T strife.

## Crowley, 1944

Crowley discourages a rigid upright/reversed binary; dignity and context matter.

**Core:** volcanic energy, struggle, contention, irrational disturbance, fighting; actively, the proper course may be to contend.

His concise practical formulation is:

> “There is going to be a fight.”

When used actively:

> “the proper course of conduct is to contend.”

Crowley, *Book of Thoth*, “Theory of the Tarot”; [online text](https://hermetic.com/crowley/book-of-thoth/theory).

## Paul Foster Case

**Well dignified:** boldness, command, generosity, vigorous competition, willingness to contend.

**Ill dignified:** cruelty, violence, lust, rashness, prodigality, destructive rivalry.

Case generally preserves the Golden Dawn doctrine but moralizes it through law, severity, and controlled will.

## Rachel Pollack

In *Seventy-Eight Degrees of Wisdom* (first published in two parts, 1980 and 1983), Pollack interprets the image as competition, conflict, scattered energy, and the friction of people pursuing incompatible purposes. Unlike the Five of Swords, the conflict need not be cruel or produce a humiliated loser.

A reversal may indicate suppressed conflict, release from it, or energy failing to find a constructive focus. This is a contextual psychological development rather than Waite’s fixed “litigation.”

## Mary K. Greer

Greer’s reversal method permits several operations: blocked, delayed, internalized, excessive, deficient, projected, or opposite expression.

Applied to the Five of Wands, this can mean internal conflict, avoidance of necessary confrontation, covert hostility, exhaustion after competition, or cooperation emerging after disagreement. It should not be reduced to the modern keyword “conflict resolved.”

# Esoteric and symbolic readings

## Alchemical

**First securely traceable layer:** Etteilla’s late-eighteenth-century “Book of Thoth” deck incorporated alchemical and elemental imagery into an occultized tarot.

For the Five specifically, strong claims that the Marseille floral crossing encodes an ancient alchemical operation are not supported by contemporary cardmakers’ texts.

Crowley’s Phoenix wands make an alchemical reading explicit: fire destroys or purges, then energy rises renewed from ashes. This is twentieth-century Hermetic alchemy, not a decipherment of the Visconti pip.

## Kabbalistic

### Lévi

Lévi made tarot a key to the Hebrew alphabet and ceremonial Kabbalah, but his principal one-to-one system concerns the 22 trumps. No reliable passage was located assigning the Five of Batons to Geburah in the later Golden Dawn manner.

### Golden Dawn

Five corresponds to the fifth Sephirah, Geburah: severity, strength, restraint, destruction, judgment, and Mars-like force.

Wands correspond to Atziluth, the archetypal world, and to Yod, the first letter of YHVH.

Thus the card is Geburah in Atziluth: fiery force acting in the world of Fire. This generates Book T’s “quarrelling and fighting.”

### Historical caution

Geburah is a real Kabbalistic Sephirah. “Five of Wands = Geburah in Atziluth” is a Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn synthesis, not an attested medieval Jewish teaching.

## Astrology

The Golden Dawn assigns the 36 cards from Two through Ten to the 36 decans. The Five of Wands receives the first decan of Leo and Saturn as its Chaldean planetary ruler.

Symbolic logic:

- Leo: radiant, sovereign, creative Fire.
- Saturn: contraction, weight, frustration, boundary, age, obstruction.
- Combined: power forced against resistance; authority contested; energy made harsh or disciplined.

Crowley calls Saturn’s action a weighing down and embittering of Leo’s force.

**[Esoteric attribution]** This correspondence is verified in Book T and Thoth. It is not found in Marseille, Sola Busca, Etteilla, or Waite’s printed card entry.

## Hermetic

The card embodies force meeting limit. Its five wands are ritual grades, not arbitrary sticks. The Chief Adept wand prevents chaotic energy from becoming wholly disastrous; Phoenix imagery makes destruction purgative.

## Christian

Historical baton ornament sometimes resembles crosses, budding rods, or liturgical staffs, and Mathers read the Two of Sceptres through the Rose of Sharon and Lily of the Valley.

For the Five, however, no early Christian commentary survives. Modern associations with the Five Wounds, Mosaic law, or righteous struggle are analogical readings. Case’s Tablets of the Law are an explicit twentieth-century Christian-Hermetic intervention.

## Jungian

The five youths may be read as semi-autonomous complexes struggling for psychic energy. The absence of a victor suggests an ego unable to coordinate competing aims. Constructive conflict can differentiate the personality; chronic combat disperses libido.

This approach spread through late-twentieth-century psychological tarot, especially writers influenced by Jungian archetypal language. It is **[modern psychological interpretation]**, not a recovered ancient doctrine.

## Directly connected

- **Bonifacio Bembo and workshop:** probably responsible for significant Visconti-Sforza material; the attribution of every individual card remains a matter of workshop scholarship.
- **Filippo Maria Visconti, Francesco Sforza, Bianca Maria Visconti:** patrons and dynastic context of early Milanese decks.
- **Jacopo Sagramoro:** documented Ferrara painter of playing cards and festive figures.
- **Jean Noblet, Jean Dodal, Nicolas Conver:** principal cardmakers in the Marseille lineage.
- **Jean-Baptiste Alliette/Etteilla:** established systematic professional tarot cartomancy.
- **Éliphas Lévi:** made tarot central to nineteenth-century ceremonial Kabbalah.
- **Gérard Encausse/Papus:** systematized Tetragrammaton and tarot cycles.
- **Oswald Wirth:** created a major occult trump deck but no original Five of Wands.
- **S. L. MacGregor Mathers:** published Etteilla-derived meanings and helped formulate Golden Dawn tarot.
- **A. E. Waite:** designed the interpretive program of the Waite-Smith deck.
- **Pamela Colman Smith:** created the five-youth image that now dominates popular understanding.
- **Aleister Crowley and Frieda Harris:** transformed Strife into a geometric ritual composition.
- **Paul Foster Case and Jessie Burns Parke:** adapted the Golden Dawn-Waite system for BOTA.
- **Robert Wang and Sandra Tabatha Cicero/Chic Cicero:** produced Golden Dawn reconstruction decks.
- **Philippe Camoin, Alejandro Jodorowsky, Marianne Costa:** redrew and reinterpreted the Marseille pattern.
- **Rachel Pollack and Mary K. Greer:** major spreaders of modern psychological and contextual readings.

## Literary and cinematic associations

### Italo Calvino

*Il castello dei destini incrociati* / *The Castle of Crossed Destinies* uses tarot cards as a combinatorial narrative language. Calvino worked chiefly from Visconti-Sforza and Marseille images. The work is relevant to tarot reception, but no defensible claim was found that the Five of Wands has a uniquely privileged role.

### T. S. Eliot

*The Waste Land* mentions “Madame Sosostris,” “the drowned Phoenician Sailor,” “Belladonna,” “the man with three staves,” and a “one-eyed merchant.” Eliot’s invented or altered tarot does not name the Five of Wands. Identifying “the man with three staves” as the Five is unsupported; it more naturally evokes a Three of Wands-like card while deliberately departing from standard packs.

### W. B. Yeats

Yeats’s Golden Dawn membership connects him to the milieu that generated Book T. No specific published Yeats interpretation of the Five of Wands was located.

### Jodorowsky

Jodorowsky’s tarot practice informs his writings and cinematic symbolism, but assertions that a particular film scene secretly quotes the Five require case-specific visual evidence. No securely documented quotation was located in the sources consulted.

### Ingmar Bergman

Bergman’s *The Seventh Seal* uses chess, medieval death imagery, and religious allegory. Claims that it quotes this card are modern associative readings, not documented production history.

### Paintings and buildings

No painting or building could be securely documented as intentionally quoting the Five of Wands specifically. Crossed staffs, cudgels, tournament scenes, and five-part ornamental lattices are too generic to establish dependence without artist testimony or a close, distinctive visual correspondence.

# Controversies and disputes

## Egyptian origin

### Claim

Court de Gébelin declared in 1781 that tarot was an Egyptian book surviving in disguised form. He proposed fanciful Egyptian etymologies for *tarot*. The accompanying Comte de Mellet essay called it the Book of Thoth and linked the trumps with Hebrew letters.

### Evidence

No Egyptian tarot cards, Egyptian text describing such a pack, or transmission chain to fifteenth-century Italy exists. Egyptian hieroglyphs had not yet been deciphered when Gébelin wrote.

### Assessment

- Egyptian origin: **[Legend]**
- Gébelin’s authorship of the theory: **[Documented fact]**
- Later historical importance of the theory: **[Documented fact]**

The Morgan summarizes Gébelin and Mellet’s role in establishing occult tarot’s theoretical foundation. [Morgan, “Occult Tarot”](https://www.themorgan.org/exhibitions/online/tarot/11).

## Romani or “Gypsy” origin

### Claim

Tarot was carried from Egypt or India by “Bohemians/Gypsies,” who preserved it as a secret divinatory book.

### History of the claim

Gébelin associated the cards with “Bohemians” in an Egyptianizing context. Nineteenth-century writers including Vaillant and Papus spread the story; Papus’s title *Le Tarot des Bohémiens* made it especially durable.

### Evidence

Tarot is documented in northern Italian courts before the earliest evidence for Romani tarot cartomancy. The Romani language and population history point to South Asian ancestry, not ancient Egyptian priesthood. There is no fifteenth-century Romani transmission document.

### Assessment

**[Legend]**, historically important but unsupported. It should also be separated from the genuine later participation of Romani fortune-tellers in European cartomancy.

## Cathar origin

The claim that tarot secretly preserved Cathar doctrine lacks medieval documentary support. It arose from modern attempts to align dualistic or ecclesiastical trump imagery with Cathar history.

For the Five of Batons, there is no Cathar-specific iconography or textual interpretation. **[Modern legend/disputed claim]**.

## Templar origin

No trial record, Templar inventory, medieval rule, or surviving Templar object describes tarot. The order was suppressed more than a century before the first secure tarot records.

Connecting crossed wands to Templar crosses is formal resemblance, not provenance. **[Modern legend]**.

## Ancient Kabbalistic origin

The four suits, ten pips, and 22 trumps can be made to correspond elegantly to later Hermetic Kabbalah. That structural elegance is not evidence the fifteenth-century designers intended the correspondence.

The first printed Hebrew-letter connection belongs to the Comte de Mellet in 1781; Lévi, Papus, the Golden Dawn, Waite, Case, and Crowley elaborated it.

**Assessment:** the correspondence is a documented occult tradition but the claim of ancient authorship is unsupported.

## Was tarot used exclusively for games before the eighteenth century?

Dummett, Decker, and Depaulis stress the overwhelmingly game-based evidence and the absence of a continuous ancient occult tradition. Their formulation that tarot was used “exclusively” for games for nearly four centuries has occasionally been qualified because:

- ordinary cards were used for fortune-telling before Etteilla;
- Folengo and some early literary episodes permit divinatory or characterological use;
- isolated non-game experimentation may leave little evidence.

The defensible conclusion is that tarot was invented as a game and that no systematic occult tarot doctrine is documented before eighteenth-century France. That does not prove no individual ever improvised divination earlier.

[A Wicked Pack of Cards](https://books.google.com/books/about/A_Wicked_Pack_of_Cards.html?id=MG6DAAAAMAAJ).

## “Sola Busca directly inspired this card”

This is often repeated too strongly.

- General access and influence: plausible to strong.
- Specific visual copying of the Sola Busca Five: unsupported by the very different compositions.
- Waite’s five youths: more directly explainable through his written “sport or strife,” Book T’s “quarrelling and fighting,” and Smith’s theatrical imagination.

## Thoth publication date

The paintings were made between 1938 and 1943. *The Book of Thoth* appeared in 1944. The deck was not commercially issued until 1969. Calling it simply “the 1944 Thoth deck” confuses book publication with deck publication.

## Conver “1760” objects

Nicolas Conver’s pattern originates in 1760/1761, but many surviving packs and scans are later printings or descendants. A catalogue date attached to a physical witness does not necessarily date the original design.

## Grimaud as a pure Conver facsimile

Grimaud/Marteau regularized design and color. Camoin has argued it also inherits Besançon/Lequart-Arnoult material. “Ancient Tarot of Marseille” is a commercial-historical label, not proof of an untouched 1760 facsimile.

## Reversals as ancient

Etteilla institutionalized systematic upright/reversed meanings. Historical game cards did not have divinatory reversals. Golden Dawn practice emphasized elemental and positional dignity rather than mechanically reversing every meaning. Modern “reversed = conflict resolved” is later still.

## Misprints becoming tradition

No single Five-of-Wands printing error was found that demonstrably created a major interpretive tradition comparable to famous trump-title corruptions. More relevant are:

- unstable translations: batons, clubs, sceptres, staffs, rods, wands;
- Mathers’s Wands-to-diamonds playing-card mapping, now often silently “corrected” to clubs;
- deck dates repeated from design dates rather than surviving-object dates;
- calling Wirth’s later completed deck an original 1889 seventy-eight-card production;
- treating the Golden Dawn manuscript text as if it were publicly available from the Order’s founding year.

# Open questions

1. Which surviving Milanese Five of Batons is physically earliest? The answer depends on unresolved deck dating and card-group attribution.

2. Who designed and engraved the Sola Busca, and which historical Catulus does its Five represent? Several classical identifications remain possible.

3. What precise documents did Smith consult at the British Museum? Access to Sola Busca photographs is plausible, but no card-by-card instruction list survives.

4. How much of the Waite-Smith Five came from Waite and how much from Smith? Waite supplied a meaning compatible with the image, but surviving evidence does not allocate each pictorial decision.

5. Which Etteilla wording is earliest for card 31? Later dictionaries conflate Etteilla, his followers, D’Odoucet, and Julia Orsini. A definitive answer requires collation of each French edition and surviving deck state.

6. When exactly was the Golden Dawn title “Lord of Strife” first written? Its public appearance in 1912 is secure; the internal manuscript’s exact composition date and individual authorship are less so.

7. Can the Rosenwald and Budapest fragments yield a secure regional Five-of-Batons genealogy? Reconstruction remains limited by fragmentary sheets and uncertain printing centers.

8. Did early players attach informal proverbial meanings to pip cards? Such oral associations may have existed, but no reliable fifteenth-century Five-specific testimony survives.
