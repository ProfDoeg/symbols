# Six of Wands: Research Dossier

## Method and evidentiary labels

This dossier distinguishes:

- **Documented fact** — supported by a surviving card, archival record, rule book, or primary printed text.
- **Scholarly reconstruction** — an inference argued from material or documentary evidence.
- **Tradition** — a convention transmitted in cardmaking, cartomancy, or occult practice.
- **Esoteric attribution** — a deliberately assigned symbolic correspondence, not a claim about the card’s historical origin.
- **Disputed claim** — proposed but not established, or contradicted by stronger evidence.
- **Legend** — a historical-origin narrative unsupported by contemporary evidence.
- **Modern invention** — a demonstrably recent interpretation or redesign.

Dates for handmade Renaissance decks remain approximate unless an inscription or archival entry supplies a firmer terminus.

## Basic identification

| Card | Six of Wands |
| Kind | Numbered suit card, later called a “Minor Arcana” card |
| Rank | Six |
| Suit | Italian *bastoni*; French *bâtons*; Spanish *bastos*; German occult tarot *Stäbe* or *Keulen*; English *batons*, *staves*, *rods*, or—especially after the nineteenth century—*wands* |
| Golden Dawn title | **The Lord of Victory** |
| Golden Dawn decan | **Jupiter in Leo**, conventionally 10°–20° Leo |

| Game function | An ordinary suit card, not a trump; normally an “empty” or low-value card in point-trick tarot games |

### Names

**Documented fact.** In historical Italian packs the card is not normally furnished with an esoteric title: it is simply the six of the *bastoni* suit—modern Italian *Sei di Bastoni*. French: *Six de Bâtons*. Spanish: *Seis de Bastos*. German: *Sechs der Stäbe* or *Stäbe Sechs*. English occult usage settled on *Six of Wands*, although Mathers also used “Sceptres” and “Clubs” when comparing terminology.

The Latin-suit names are:

| Language | Historical/ordinary name |
|---|---|
| Italian | *bastoni*, sometimes *mazze* |
| French tarot | *bâtons* |
| Spanish | *bastos* |
| English | batons, clubs, staves, sceptres, rods; occult convention: wands |
| German occult tarot | *Stäbe*, sometimes *Keulen* |
| Portuguese | *paus* |

Italian baton signs are conventionally slender, straight and intersecting; Spanish *bastos* are usually separately drawn cudgels. German-suited playing cards have acorns, leaves, hearts and bells, so *Stäbe* in German tarot is a translated occult-tarot term rather than a native German suit sign. [Comparative suit vocabulary](https://www.math.bas.bg/ml/iad/tyalie/damapik.html); [historical description of batons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Batons_%28suit%29).

### What does not apply to this card

The Papess/Juno, Pope/Jupiter, Maison Dieu/Tower, and numbered or unnumbered Fool are **trump-pattern variations**. They never renamed the Six of Wands. Likewise, the Justice/Strength order, the Tzaddi/Heh controversy, and the three historical trump orders do not alter this pip’s identity or rank. They matter only to the history of the pack surrounding it.

## Iconography deck by deck

## Fifteenth-century Milanese and Ferrarese cards

### Marziano–Michelino pack, Milan, approximately 1418–1425

**Documented fact with reconstruction.** Marziano da Tortona’s surviving description concerns a special pack painted by Michelino da Besozzo for Filippo Maria Visconti. Its sixteen elevated figures were classical gods, and its four suits were birds rather than Cups, Coins, Swords and Batons.

Consequently there was **no Six of Wands in the later technical sense**. The pack is important as a precursor to the idea of permanent superior cards, not as the first surviving standard tarot deck. Calling it a Six-of-Wands ancestor would be speculation.

### Visconti di Modrone/Cary–Yale, Milan, approximately 1440s

**Documented fact.** The surviving cards use richly illuminated, heraldic suit signs. The pack originally had unusual court-card complements, apparently including male and female attendants and mounted figures. Its surviving cards do not establish a separately narrated Six of Batons comparable to Waite–Smith.

**Scholarly reconstruction.** A Six of Batons, if present in the original pack, would have been an ornamental count card: six repeated Visconti-style staves, not a victory scene.

“Visconti di Modrone” and “Cary–Yale” usually refer to the same surviving group, now at Yale’s Beinecke Library. They should not be counted as two independent deck witnesses.

### Brambilla, Milan, approximately 1440s

**Documented fact.** The Brambilla fragments preserve Visconti heraldry and conventional suit cards. No securely catalogued surviving Six of Batons was located in the consulted digital sources. It is therefore unsafe to describe a detailed Brambilla Six.

### Pierpont Morgan–Bergamo Visconti-Sforza, Milan, approximately 1450–1480

**Documented fact.** The surviving deck is divided principally between the Morgan Library, Accademia Carrara in Bergamo, and the Colleoni family. Its pip cards use repeated suit emblems against elaborate punched-gold grounds. They do not supply the scenic, cartomantic Six of Wands later familiar from Waite–Smith.

Modern “complete Visconti-Sforza” editions often add replacement cards. **Modern invention:** an image in such an edition is not automatically a surviving fifteenth-century original and must be checked against the institutional inventories.

### Este/Ferrarese cards, approximately 1450s–1470s

**Documented fact.** Surviving Ferrarese cards and fragments exhibit courtly ornament, heraldic devices and repeated suit signs. No narrative “victor” Six has been established. Ferrara is nevertheless essential because its court accounts provide the earliest secure use of *carte da trionfi* in February 1442.

### Mantegna “Tarocchi,” northern Italy, approximately 1460s

**Documented fact.** These fifty educational engravings—Estates of Man, Apollo and the Muses, Liberal Arts, Virtues, celestial spheres and first causes—are neither a tarot pack nor playing cards. They have no four suits and therefore no Six of Wands.

The customary name “Mantegna Tarocchi” is an old misnomer: attribution to Andrea Mantegna is not accepted, and their relation to tarot is analogical rather than genealogical.

## Sola Busca, northern Italy/Veneto, approximately 1490–1491

**Documented fact.** This is the earliest surviving complete 78-card tarot and the earliest complete tarot in which every numbered suit card carries a substantial figurative scene. The engraved series is generally dated about 1490. The painted pack now in the Pinacoteca di Brera bears evidence interpreted as 1491. [Le Tarot/Brera-oriented account](https://www.letarot.it/Sola-Busca-Tarot_pag_pg824_eng.aspx).

The **Six of Batons** shows a standing, partly undressed male figure in an awkward or comic predicament among six massive staves. Accounts of the image note slipping clothing or exposed undergarments. It is not a mounted commander, does not wear a triumphal laurel, and does not reproduce the Waite–Smith procession.

### Was it the direct source of Pamela Colman Smith’s Six?

**Disputed claim.** Smith and Waite very probably knew reproductions of Sola Busca: the Busca-Serbelloni family supplied a complete photographic set to the British Museum in 1907, two years before Smith drew her deck. Several Waite–Smith pips have persuasive individual parallels with Sola Busca—especially the Three of Swords and Ten of Wands/Ten of Swords family of compositions.

But the specific statement “the Sola Busca Six of Batons was the source of Smith’s Six of Wands” is not supported by the two images. A closer documented visual precursor is Smith’s own mounted-knight illustration for Lady Alix Egerton’s “The Knight Errant,” published in Smith’s *The Green Sheaf* in 1903. The mounted profile, standard and procession-like bearing anticipate the 1909 card. [Discussion and reproduction history](https://www.tarotassociation.net/6-of-wands/).

The defensible conclusion is:

- **Documented:** Smith’s Six resembles her 1903 knight design.
- **Scholarly reconstruction:** familiarity with Sola Busca encouraged the larger project of illustrating the pips.
- **Disputed:** Sola Busca’s particular Six supplied the scene.

### Rosenwald and Budapest sheets, late fifteenth/early sixteenth century

**Documented fact.** These woodcut sheets witness inexpensive serial tarot production and regional pip arrangements. The batons are repeated, crossed suit signs, not narrative tableaux. Surviving sheets, impressions and modern reconstructions differ in completeness; a specific “Six” should not be inferred from a reconstructed sheet without identifying the surviving impression.

Their importance for this card is formal: they represent the ordinary game-card model from which Marseille-style abstract pips descend.

## Minchiate, Florence/Tuscany, sixteenth–nineteenth centuries

**Documented fact.** Minchiate expanded the trump sequence to forty trump-like cards but retained the four Italian suits. Its Six of Batons is an ordinary pip, normally a symmetrical arrangement of six staves, not “Victory.”

Minchiate’s expanded zodiacal and virtue imagery occurs among its additional trumps. It does **not** furnish an early astrological Jupiter-in-Leo identity for the Six of Batons.

## Bolognese tarocchino

**Documented fact.** The reduced Bolognese pack removes the 2–5 of every suit. The Six of Batons survives and is the lowest numbered baton card in the standard 62-card form. It remains a game pip without a personal title.

## Sicilian tarot

**Documented fact.** The traditional 63-card Sicilian pack retains 5–10 in Batons; the Six therefore survives as a low pip. In the documented Mineo rules the suit ranks King, Queen, Horse, Maid, 10, 9, 8, 7, 6, 5. [Rules and pack description](https://www.pagat.com/tarot/sicilian.html).

## Tarot de Marseille lineage

### Jean Noblet, Paris, approximately 1650

**Documented fact.** Six straight batons cross in a regular lattice, accompanied by colored leaves or floral filling. There is no human figure, horse, wreath or written divinatory title.

### Jean Dodal, Lyon, approximately 1701–1715

**Documented fact.** Dodal maintains the crossed-lattice baton pattern. Small foliate and floral elements fill the spaces and ends. Differences from Noblet are those of carving, coloring, terminal shapes and ornament—not narrative meaning.

### Nicolas Conver, Marseille, 1760

**Documented fact.** Six batons form an open symmetrical grid, with ornamental foliage/flowers around the intersections and margins. The card counts its suit signs visually. Conver became a particularly influential Type II Marseille model through continued Marseille production.

### Grimaud, nineteenth–twentieth centuries

**Documented fact.** Grimaud regularized and recolored the Conver-derived pattern. The pip remained abstract. Any meanings printed in accompanying manuals belong to modern cartomancy, not to the original image.

### Camoin–Jodorowsky, 1997

**Documented fact/modern reconstruction.** Philippe Camoin and Alejandro Jodorowsky presented their deck as a restoration assembled from old printing evidence. Its Six of Batons remains a Marseille lattice with carefully recolored floral details.

**Disputed claim.** Assertions that restored tiny marks encode a single ancient initiatory doctrine exceed the documentary evidence. The result is historically informed but is also a modern synthetic edition, not a facsimile of one recovered archetype.

### Meaning of the Marseille floral ornament

**Documented:** leaves and flowers are conventional suit-card fillers and balance the composition.

**Tradition/esoteric reading:** modern Marseille readers may interpret them as growth, generation, opening or the organic life of the suit.

**Absence of evidence:** no seventeenth- or eighteenth-century cardmaker’s explanation assigning “Victory,” Jupiter, Leo, Tiphareth or Jungian individuation to the Six’s foliage was located.

## Besançon pattern

**Documented fact.** Besançon tarot chiefly differs in the controversial trumps: Juno replaces the Papess and Jupiter replaces the Pope, avoiding papal subjects in particular Swiss/French contexts from the eighteenth century onward. Its Six of Batons remains a conventional pip.

The Juno/Jupiter substitutions are therefore relevant to the deck but not a renaming of this card.

## Etteilla

### Early Etteilla cartomancy, 1770–1780s

Etteilla first published meanings for an ordinary 32-card pack in 1770. His full tarot system followed Court de Gébelin, beginning in 1783.

### Grand Etteilla, Paris, 1788–1789 onward

**Documented fact.** The Grand Etteilla reorganizes and renumbers the entire pack. In common Grand Etteilla ordering the Six of Batons is card **30**, although later Etteilla I, II and III patterns differ in illustrations and wording.

The card may bear keywords rather than merely six uncaptioned suit signs. Surviving editions are not identical, making undifferentiated claims that “Etteilla says…” hazardous.

**Traditional Etteilla meaning:** *domestique*—a servant, domestic, subordinate or person in service—is the meaning most consistently transmitted for the upright Six of Batons; the reversed meaning is commonly *attente*—waiting or expectation. These meanings belong to French cartomantic word-association, not to the Golden Dawn’s “Victory.”

**Caution:** Waite’s own list gives “servant” for the card and attributes certain alternate meanings to older authorities. Exact wording must be tied to a specified Etteilla edition, not retroactively to every Grand Etteilla deck.

## Oswald Wirth, 1889

**Documented fact.** Wirth’s original twenty-two-card tarot consisted only of trumps. It has no Six of Wands. Later seventy-eight-card “Wirth” packs are expansions by later artists or publishers.

Any Wirth-specific Six is therefore a **modern invention**, even when designed in sympathy with his symbolism.

## Golden Dawn manuscript designs, approximately 1888–1900

**Documented esoteric attribution.** In *Book T*, the card is:

> “LORD OF VICTORY … Two hands in grip … holding six Wands crossed, 3 and 3.”

The manuscript continues with flames, the symbols of Jupiter and Leo, and the attribution:

> “Tiphareth of Yod.”

Its meanings include victory after strife, pleasure gained by labor, carefulness and sociability, together with the shadow possibilities of insolence and pride; the result depends upon elemental dignity. [Regardie’s transcription of the Golden Dawn material](https://www.labirintoermetico.com/06Numerologia_Cabala/I.Regardie_Complete_Golden_Dawn_%28II%20ed.deluxe%29.pdf).

The title is more exactly **“Lord of Victory,”** not merely “Victory.” “Victory” is the shortened Thoth card-face title.

### Robert Wang, *The Golden Dawn Tarot*, 1977

**Modern reconstruction.** Wang’s deck translates Regardie’s published descriptions into a complete painted pack. Its Six follows the crossed-wands, hands, flames and astrological-emblem scheme rather than Smith’s procession.

### Chic and Sandra Tabatha Cicero, *Golden Dawn Magical Tarot*, 2001

**Modern reconstruction.** The Ciceros likewise render the manuscript correspondences, with a more systematically colored ritual design. Neither reconstruction is an original nineteenth-century Golden Dawn deck.

## Waite–Smith, London, 1909

**Documented fact.** Pamela Colman Smith draws a laurel-crowned horseman riding a white horse. He raises a living wand surmounted by a second laurel wreath. Five attendants or companions carry the other staves. Architecture and pale sky occupy the distance.

Waite’s 1910 description is:

> “A laurelled horseman bears one staff adorned with a laurel crown; footmen with staves are at his side.”

He deliberately makes the card polyvalent:

> “On the surface, it is a victor triumphing, but it is also great news … the crown of hope.”

[Pictorial Key, Six of Wands](https://sacred-texts.com/tarot/pkt/pktwa06.htm).

### What changed

Marseille’s counted lattice became a public human drama. The Golden Dawn’s technical “Victory” was translated into triumphal procession, social recognition and successful news. Smith supplied emotional ambiguity absent from the abstract design: the crowd might acclaim the rider, accompany him, or merely march behind him; the horse’s costume can look theatrical.

### Likely sources

- **Documented visual precursor:** Smith’s own 1903 mounted-knight composition.
- **Probable conceptual source:** Golden Dawn “Lord of Victory.”
- **Broad probable influence:** Sola Busca’s precedent for narrated pips.
- **Disputed:** the particular Sola Busca Six as a direct compositional source.
- **Possible traditional source:** Roman and Renaissance triumphal-entry imagery, heraldic pageants, messengers in state, and laurel as a classical victory emblem.
- **Unproved:** identification of the rider as a named historical person, Christ, Julius Caesar or a specific tarot trump.

## Paul Foster Case/BOTA

**Documented esoteric tradition.** BOTA’s deck retains Waite–Smith-derived structure while Case’s teaching makes the Golden Dawn correspondences explicit. Case’s *Oracle of the Tarot* assigns:

- second decan of Leo;
- August 2–11 in his convention;
- Jupiter;
- keyword **Victory**;
- success, happiness and gain;
- pleasure in labor and gain after competition;
- ill-dignified loss or waste through pleasure, pride or insolence.

Case describes the six wands as balanced triangles and relates six to Beauty/Tiphareth and the solar sphere. [Searchable edition of *Oracle of the Tarot*](https://www.scribd.com/doc/187261487/Paul-Foster-Case-Oracle-of-the-Tarot-A-Course-on-Tarot-Divination-pdf).

## Thoth, paintings 1938–1943; deck published 1969

**Documented fact.** Frieda Harris replaces Smith’s procession with a radiant geometric structure. Six crossed wands are organized as three paired ritual weapons associated with three grades or adepts. Nine steady flames appear; the Jupiter and Leo glyphs state the decan. The card is titled **VICTORY**.

Crowley writes:

> “This card represents Tiphareth of the suit of Fire. This shows Energy in completely balanced manifestation.”

He connects the Five’s disruption with a new equilibrium:

> “The result is the Son, and the Sun.”

He explains Jupiter in Leo as:

> “a benediction on the harmony and beauty of this arrangement.”

And concludes:

> “It is self-supporting, like the Sun.”

[Transcription of *The Book of Thoth*, Six of Wands](https://book-of-thoth.net/Six-of-Wands-Victory).

A web transcription stating that the card “derives from Netzach (Victory)” is almost certainly a transcriptional or editorial error: six corresponds to **Tiphareth**, while Netzach is seven. Crowley’s substantive paragraph and *Book T* both say Tiphareth. This is a useful example of a digital misprint capable of becoming a false tradition.

## Notable modern redesigns

### Rachel Pollack and later psychological tarot

Pollack generally reads the Waite–Smith rider as actual or hoped-for triumph, emphasizing confidence and the danger of defining success entirely through public acclaim. In modern psychological practice, the crowd becomes the social mirror and the mounted figure the conscious ego.

### Mary K. Greer

Greer’s reversal method does not impose one fixed contrary meaning. A reversed Six of Wands may be blocked, delayed, internalized, excessive or projected victory—hence failure to receive recognition, private achievement, impostor feelings, or arrogant overidentification with applause.

### Jodorowsky–Costa

In *The Way of Tarot*, sixes are associated with the beginning or pleasure of beauty and the Wands suit with creative/sexual energy. Their reading grows from number-plus-suit and Marseille geometry rather than from Waite’s horseman. It is a modern synthetic numerological system.

## The game

## Original function

**Documented fact.** Tarot arose as a trick-taking game. The Six of Batons was created as a playing card, not as an occult emblem. Dummett emphasized that the documentary record supports game use centuries before the first explicit occult-tarot system. [Dummett, “Origins of Tarot”](https://www.nybooks.com/articles/1981/05/14/origins-of-tarot/).

### Rank

In traditional Italian ordering:

- Courts: King, Queen, Cavalier, Jack.
- In the “long” or severe suits, Swords and Batons, pip cards descend 10, 9, 8, 7, **6**, 5, 4, 3, 2, Ace.
- In Cups and Coins, many early games reverse the numeral direction.

Thus the Six of Batons lies below 7 and above 5. [Dummett extract](https://forum.tarothistory.com/viewtopic.php?t=1175).

French tarot eventually ranks every suit uniformly: King, Queen, Cavalier, Jack, 10 down to Ace. Again, the Six lies between 7 and 5.

In Bolognese tarocchino, removal of 2–5 makes Six the lowest remaining Batons pip.

### Point value

The Six is ordinarily an **empty card**:

- nominal traditional value: one point before packet deductions;
- reduced value: zero;
- in three-card counting, it principally completes groups from which points are subtracted;
- in many modern Central European games, numeral suit cards count zero individually.

[Comparative tarot counting](https://www.pagat.com/tarot/counting.html).

It is not an *oudler*, honor, *bout*, king or counting trump. Its practical strength depends on rank, whether Batons are trumped, and whether it becomes the last surviving card of the led suit.

### Tarock packs

In French-suited Austrian and Central European Tarock packs, traditional Italian suit signs are replaced by Hearts, Diamonds, Clubs and Spades. Many games reduce the black suits from the bottom and red suits from the top. Consequently there may be **no literal Six of Wands** in a shortened 54-, 42- or 40-card Tarock pack. A black Six, if retained, is a game analogue, not an occult Six of Wands.

### Minchiate

Minchiate retains the Six as a low ordinary suit card. Its additional trumps and special combinations affect scoring, but do not give the Six a special “victory” bonus.

## Trump orders and Fool

These do not govern the Six, but clarify the surrounding historical pack.

**Scholarly reconstruction.** Historians group early Italian trump rankings into three broad regional orders:

- **A:** Bologna/Ferrara-related order;
- **B:** Milan;
- **C:** Florence and related regions.

Justice, Fortitude/Strength and Temperance change relative position, and the theological virtues or Emperor/Empress can behave differently in special packs.

The Fool/*Matto* generally cannot win a trick as a numbered trump. In many games it is excused and recovered by its player in exchange for another card; in some late games it has different roles. Its being unnumbered is not evidence that it was always “zero” in an esoteric sequence.

None of these disputes changes the Six’s suit rank.

### Court de Gébelin and Mellet

The often-repeated statement that Court de Gébelin assigned all Hebrew letters to tarot needs qualification. Gébelin proposed an Egyptian sacred book and calendrical/cosmological interpretations; the adjacent essay by the Comte de Mellet made the explicit Hebrew-alphabet arrangement. Their schemes concern the trump series, not a Jupiter-in-Leo Six.

### Lévi

Lévi’s own words most pertinent to tarot history are directed at the 22 keys and their relation to the Hebrew alphabet. A verified passage in *Dogme et rituel* naming the Six of Batons, “Victory,” or Jupiter in Leo was not found. Attributing the Golden Dawn pip table directly to Lévi is therefore historically misleading.

### Papus

Papus popularized a Tetragrammaton-based organization of tarot and reproduced Wirth-like trump material. His fourfold logic helped prepare the environment in which Wands could be called Fire/Yod, but the exact Book T card title and decan should be credited to Golden Dawn documents, not retroactively to Papus.

### Tzaddi/Heh and Emperor/Star

Crowley’s “Tzaddi is not the Star” revision exchanges the conventional Hebrew-letter assignments of the Emperor and Star while generally leaving their zodiacal identities Aries and Aquarius in a deliberately rearranged scheme. It affects trumps IV and XVII, not the Six of Wands. Jupiter in Leo and Tiphareth of Fire remain unchanged.

## Esoteric and symbolic readings

### Fire and Yod

**Esoteric attribution, Golden Dawn, late nineteenth century.** Wands are Fire, the archetypal world Atziluth and Yod, the first letter of YHVH. This is not a meaning documented on Renaissance *bastoni*. It comes from Hermetic-Qabalistic system building.

Yod is treated as the seminal spark: initiative, will and emanation. The Six consequently becomes stabilized or harmonized fire.

### Tiphareth and six

**Esoteric attribution.** Tiphareth, the sixth sephirah, means Beauty and occupies the balancing center of the standard Hermetic Tree of Life. It is associated with the Sun, the sacrificial/redeeming son, harmony and integration.

Accordingly:

- Four contains fire.
- Five disrupts it through conflict.
- Six restores centered, solar equilibrium.
- Seven risks renewed excess or instability.

Crowley explicitly articulates this dialectic in *The Book of Thoth*.

A terminological trap should be avoided: **Netzach literally means Victory, but it is sephirah seven.** The Six is called Victory because of its full Golden Dawn synthesis, not because it belongs to Netzach.

### Jupiter in Leo

**Documented Golden Dawn attribution.** The second decan of Leo is assigned to Jupiter by the Golden Dawn’s use of the Chaldean planetary order.

Symbolically:

- Leo supplies solar visibility, command, nobility, pride and performance.
- Jupiter supplies expansion, generosity, honors and success.
- Their favorable combination yields public victory.
- Their excess yields theatrical self-importance, overconfidence or pride.

The association is not ancient tarot astrology. It is a late nineteenth-century assignment imposed on an older game card.

### Alchemy

No Renaissance alchemical gloss on the Six of Batons has been found. Claims that the Sola Busca deck as a whole encodes alchemical, metallurgical or Hermetic operations are modern scholarly/esoteric hypotheses prompted by furnaces, vessels, weapons and unusual classical figures.

For this card specifically:

- **Modern esoteric reading:** six crossed fiery rods form a stable coagulation after conflict.
- **Crowleyan analogy:** opposing forces marry to produce the Sun/Son.
- **Unproved historical claim:** the fifteenth-century cardmaker intended the Six as a coded alchemical stage.

### Christian symbolism

The Waite–Smith rider invites comparison with Palm Sunday, Christ’s entry into Jerusalem, apocalyptic riders, royal entries and the Christian victor’s crown.

- Laurel is a documented classical and Renaissance victory symbol.
- Triumphal entry was a widely available Christian and civic image type.
- No statement by Waite or Smith identifies the rider as Christ.
- Therefore a Passion/Palm Sunday identification is a **possible modern symbolic reading**, not documented iconographic fact.

### Hermetic reading

The card reconciles expansion with solar form: Jupiter magnifies, Leo dramatizes, Tiphareth centers and Fire acts. The productive expression is confidence joined to order; the distorted expression is an ego that mistakes acclaim for intrinsic worth.

### Jungian reading

**Modern invention/tradition, principally twentieth century onward.** The rider can represent the ego or heroic persona; the crowd represents collective recognition; the horse represents instinctual energy carrying consciousness.

Constructive reading: previously conflicting psychic energies are organized around a viable center.

Shadow reading: inflation, dependence on applause, projection of inferiority onto followers, or a brittle persona vulnerable to reversal. This approach derives from analytical psychology and later tarot counseling, not directly from Jung’s own writings. Jung did not publish a card-by-card Six of Wands interpretation.

## People and works

### Pamela Colman Smith

Smith is the person most directly responsible for the card’s modern identity. Her 1909 drawing converts a counted pip into the iconic laurel-crowned rider. Her 1903 *Green Sheaf* knight is closer to this particular scene than Sola Busca’s Six.

### Arthur Edward Waite

Waite supplied the conceptual program and published the first authoritative explanation. His “victor,” “great news,” “King’s courier” and “crown of hope” show that the picture was intended to embrace both triumph and an awaited announcement.

### Golden Dawn figures

Samuel Liddell MacGregor Mathers and the Golden Dawn manuscript tradition supplied “Lord of Victory,” Jupiter in Leo and Tiphareth of Yod. Israel Regardie later made the Order’s material widely accessible. Robert Wang and the Ciceros translated the written descriptions into modern decks.

### Aleister Crowley and Frieda Harris

Crowley reconceived the Six as a Thelemic-Hermetic structure; Harris made it visual through precise crossing weapons, nine flames and astrological glyphs. Her achievement is not merely decorative: it removes the triumphant personality and depicts victory as equilibrium of energy.

### Paul Foster Case

Case transmitted Golden Dawn correspondences through American occult education and BOTA. His version stresses balanced geometry, solar Beauty and the keyword Victory.

### Etteilla, Lévi, Christian, Papus and Wirth

Their importance is genealogical:

- Etteilla established systematic tarot divination and the first purpose-made occult deck.
- Lévi joined tarot trumps to Hebrew-Qabalistic symbolism.
- Paul Christian elaborated the Egyptian-initiation romance and popularized “arcana.”
- Papus systematized and popularized French occult tarot.
- Wirth supplied influential occult trump designs.

Only Etteilla has a direct independent pip meaning here. The others did not leave a comparably secure, card-specific Six doctrine.

### Calvino, Eliot, Yeats, Bergman and Jodorowsky

- **Italo Calvino, *The Castle of Crossed Destinies* (1969/1973):** constructs narratives from tarot sequences, especially Visconti and Marseille imagery. Tarot is a combinatorial language; no uniquely canonical Six-of-Wands episode was verified.
- **T. S. Eliot, *The Waste Land* (1922):** invents/uses Madame Sosostris’s tarot-like cards, including the “drowned Phoenician Sailor” and “one-eyed merchant.” The Six of Wands is not named.
- **W. B. Yeats:** participated in the Golden Dawn and used related magical symbolism, but no securely traced poem assigning special meaning to this card was located.
- **Ingmar Bergman:** *The Seventh Seal* uses medieval allegory, chess and Death, not a documented Six-of-Wands quotation.
- **Alejandro Jodorowsky:** tarot shaped his books, workshops, visual practice and cinema, but claims that a particular film scene directly quotes the Six require shot-level evidence. None was verified in the consulted sources.

These names belong to tarot’s wider cultural reception. Including them as if each were specifically “attached” to the Six would exaggerate the evidence.

### Paintings and buildings

No securely documented historic painting or building intentionally quoting the Six of Wands was located. The direction of influence is more plausibly the reverse: Smith drew on the broad Western vocabulary of equestrian portraits, civic triumphs, heraldic standards and laurel-crowned victors.

Modern murals, tattoos and illustrations frequently quote Smith’s card, but no authoritative census exists.

## Controversies and disputes

## Egyptian origin

**Legend, first influentially printed in 1781.** Court de Gébelin asserted that tarot preserved an Egyptian sacred book. He worked before Champollion’s 1822 decipherment and offered no chain of cards, texts or artifacts connecting pharaonic Egypt to fifteenth-century Italy.

The Comte de Mellet’s adjoining essay expanded the Egyptian and Hebrew reading. Etteilla then turned “Book of Thoth” tarot into a working divinatory system.

**Historical assessment:** tarot’s vocabulary, suit structure, Christian-social trumps and documentary trail place its creation in northern Italy in the fifteenth century. Dummett notes that no evidence of occult meaning precedes the eighteenth-century intervention of Gébelin. [Dummett’s statement](https://www.nybooks.com/articles/1981/05/14/origins-of-tarot/).

The myth remains culturally important because it created the intellectual setting from which occult tarot arose.

## Romani or “Gypsy” transmission

**Legend.** Tarot was subsequently said to have been carried from Egypt by Romani people, then widely called “Egyptians” in Europe. This claim became especially prominent through nineteenth-century authors such as Boiteau d’Ambly and Jean-Alexandre Vaillant and through the title of Papus’s *Tarot of the Bohemians*.

Evidence does not connect Romani migration with tarot’s invention or its Italian court records. The claim also depends on an obsolete European misidentification of Romani origins; linguistic and historical evidence points to South Asian origins.

## Cathars and Templars

**Modern legends/disputed claims.** Proposals that tarot secretly preserved Cathar dualism, Templar doctrine, Grail mysteries or an underground medieval church generally interpret later Marseille details as deliberate codes.

Problems include:

- no fifteenth-century document identifying such a program;
- no chain of custody from Cathar or Templar communities to cardmakers;
- ordinary Christian and civic sources sufficiently explain most trump subjects;
- the Six of Batons is a repeated suit card before occult interpretation.

These theories can be studied as modern mythmaking but are not historical explanations supported by contemporary evidence.

## Sola Busca as the source of every Waite–Smith pip

**Overstatement.** The deck supplied a precedent and several strong particular parallels. That does not demonstrate one-to-one borrowing for all thirty-six illustrated small cards.

For the Six:

- the Sola Busca composition is not the Waite–Smith procession;
- Smith’s 1903 knight is a closer source;
- the Golden Dawn title explains the victory theme;
- Sola Busca remains a probable general catalyst.

## “First fully illustrated minors”

Sola Busca is the earliest surviving complete tarot whose numbered suits are all scenically illustrated. It does not follow that no earlier illustrated pip ever existed; survival is not invention. The precise formulation should be “earliest surviving complete example.”

## Rider identification and the disguised horse

The rider has been called a returning general, royal courier, actor, fool, false victor or Christ. Some viewers see a cloth-covered hobby horse or notice the animal’s unusual head and costume.

Waite’s primary text calls him a horseman and victor/courier. No named identity is supplied. Interpretations of falsity, theatricality or imminent fall are legitimate readings of ambiguity, but not proven facts about Smith’s intention.

## Victory versus pride

Golden Dawn and Crowley sources already include insolence and pride of riches as possible ill-dignified expressions. The modern “ego inflation” reading is therefore not wholly new, although its Jungian vocabulary is twentieth-century.

## Netzach/Tiphareth error

Because *Netzach* translates as Victory, secondary sites occasionally assign the card to Netzach. In the Golden Dawn numerical structure:

- Six = Tiphareth.
- Seven = Netzach.
- Six of Wands = Tiphareth of Yod.

Calling it “Netzach of Fire” is a mistake unless an author explicitly announces a different system.

## Dating the Thoth deck

“1938–43, published 1969” is essentially correct but needs distinction:

- Harris’s paintings were produced primarily from 1938/39 to 1943.
- Crowley’s book appeared in 1944.
- A limited test edition of the cards appeared in the 1960s.
- The first broadly distributed complete commercial edition followed in 1969.

## Marseille restoration claims

Camoin–Jodorowsky’s deck incorporates historical research and inherited workshop knowledge, but the language of recovering the one “original Tarot” is disputed. No single complete medieval Marseille prototype survives against which every restored line and color can be checked.

## Misprints that became traditions

Identified or recurrent examples include:

- “Netzach” instead of Tiphareth in a Six-of-Wands transcription.
- Treating Wirth’s later 78-card adaptations as his original 1889 deck.
- Treating Cary–Yale and Visconti di Modrone as separate deck witnesses.
- Giving Sola Busca’s painted date as the certain engraving date.
- Repeating “Sola Busca Six inspired Smith’s Six” without comparing the cards.
- Conflating Golden Dawn “Lord of Victory” with the shorter printed Thoth title “Victory.”
- Assigning universal exact Gregorian dates to decans despite differing ephemerides and conventions.

---

## Open questions

1. **Exact design genealogy of Smith’s Six.** Her 1903 knight is a demonstrable precursor, and Book T supplies the meaning. Whether another painting, stage design, procession print or Sola Busca detail mediated the final composition remains open.

2. **Smith’s access to Sola Busca photographs.** The British Museum received them in 1907, and Smith could have seen them. Direct documentary proof—diary entry, letter or explicit statement naming the cards she consulted—has not been found.

3. **Sola Busca Six’s original subject.** The comic, partially undressed figure has generated conjectures, but no universally accepted identification or contemporary explanatory text survives.

4. **Etteilla edition control.** Grand Etteilla I, II and III families vary. A definitive stemma of the Six’s printed captions requires examination of specific surviving copies rather than reliance on consolidated modern lists.

5. **Earliest surviving abstract Six of Batons.** Fifteenth-century fragments may antedate Sola Busca, but the accessible catalogues consulted did not permit a securely verified earlier individual card.

6. **Golden Dawn manuscript date and authorship.** The contents are securely Golden Dawn, but exact strata, authors and copying dates within *Book T* remain subjects of manuscript scholarship.

7. **Modern psychological firsts.** Confidence, recognition and pride are already latent in Waite and Book T. Pinning down the first explicitly Jungian “persona/inflation” reading would require a comprehensive survey of mid-twentieth-century tarot manuals.

8. **Card-specific cultural quotations.** Calvino, Yeats, Eliot, Bergman and Jodorowsky have real tarot associations, but most published claims collapse general tarot influence into attachment to this particular pip.
