# Knight of Wands: Research Dossier

## Method and evidentiary labels

This dossier uses the following labels throughout:

- **[Documented fact]** — supported by a surviving card, archival record, dated printed book, manuscript, or rules text.
- **[Scholarly reconstruction]** — an inference accepted or seriously entertained by modern historians but not stated by an original document.
- **[Tradition]** — a recurring interpretation transmitted within cartomancy or occult practice.
- **[Esoteric attribution]** — a deliberately assigned occult correspondence, not a claim about the card’s original fifteenth-century function.
- **[Disputed claim]** — proposed by identifiable writers but challenged or unsupported.
- **[Legend]** — an origin story or attribution lacking historical evidence.
- **[Modern invention]** — a feature first introduced by a modern artist, occult school, restorer, or interpreter.

A recurrent problem is that “Knight of Wands” can denote two different things:

1. the historical tarot court card below the Queen and King; or
2. the Golden Dawn/Thoth mounted sovereign, called a **King** in Golden Dawn manuscripts and a **Knight** by Crowley.

Those are not the same rank in every occult deck.

### Names, numbering, and regional renaming

**[Documented fact]** Early hand-painted Italian cards generally have neither printed titles nor numbers. “Cavallo di Bastoni” is therefore a modern descriptive Italian catalogue name based on rank and suit, not necessarily an inscription once printed on the card.

**[Documented fact]** French Marseille-family packs label it *Cavalier de Bâton* or a spelling variant. Jean Dodal’s export pack of Lyon, 1701–15, carries export inscriptions on the four Cavaliers. The Bibliothèque nationale de France catalogues Dodal’s complete pack as a 78-card woodcut-and-stencil game.

**[Documented fact]** Spanish Latin-suited cards retain *caballo* as the mounted court between the *sota* and *rey*. In Spanish occult tarot, *Caballero de Bastos* is also common, but *Caballo de Bastos* is the normal playing-card name.

**[Documented fact]** German terminology is less uniform because traditional German/Austrian tarock normally uses French suits. In translations of occult tarot, *Ritter der Stäbe* is standard; *Stabritter* is a compact alternative.

**[Important limitation]** Papess/Juno, Pope/Jupiter, *Maison Dieu*, Fool 0/XXII, and the three regional trump orders concern the trump sequence. They do not rename or renumber the Knight of Wands. Their relevance is contextual only. The Knight is a suited court and never belongs to the numbered trump series.

# Iconography deck by deck

## Mamluk predecessors, probably fifteenth century or earlier

**[Documented fact]** The surviving Mamluk pack in Istanbul has four suits: cups, coins, swords, and polo sticks (*jawkan*), with three inscribed but non-figural court ranks: king, deputy of the king, and second deputy. The polo court captions include *nāʾib malik al-jawkān*, “deputy of the king of polo sticks,” and a second deputy.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** Historians regard Mamluk cards as the closest surviving ancestor of the Latin-suited European pack. Polo sticks became European batons or clubs because polo was unfamiliar in much of Europe; the deputies were recast as recognizable human ranks, particularly mounted knight and foot-servant.

This establishes an ancestry for the suit and the intermediate court rank. It does not establish a continuous iconographic prototype for the individual Knight of Wands: the Mamluk courts carried calligraphy and ornament, not a painted knight.

## Marziano da Tortona and Michelino da Besozzo, c. 1414–25

**[Documented fact]** Marziano’s description of a special pack for Filippo Maria Visconti is the earliest detailed description of a European trump-like pack. Its four suits were birds and its special figures were classical gods.

**Finding:** It contains no Knight of Wands, no Wands suit, and no standard tarot court. It is relevant to the invention of an added permanent-trump suit at the Milanese court, not to this card’s iconography.

## Cary–Yale / Visconti di Modrone, Milan, approximately 1442–47

**[Documented fact]** Sixty-seven cards survive at Yale. The pack has extraordinary courts: surviving cards indicate male and female pages and mounted figures in addition to King and Queen. Its “Staves” are often long shafts or arrows rather than the rustic clubs of later Marseille designs.

The male Knight of Staves is represented as a mounted nobleman or soldier amid the gilt courtly splendor characteristic of the pack. The horse, armor/costume, lance-like suit emblem, and heraldic luxury identify rank and household. It is not a narrative of impulsiveness, emigration, or Fire.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** A complete pack may have contained six courts per suit—King, Queen, male and female knights, male and female pages—and perhaps 86 cards. The surviving distribution makes this plausible, but the complete original inventory does not survive.

**[Disputed claim]** Proposed dates range from the early 1440s into the 1460s. The coin imagery has been used to support 1442–47; other catalogues have preferred later dates.

**[Modern invention]** Luigi Scapini’s modern completion of the Cary–Yale pack includes reconstructed missing courts. Such cards must not be mistaken for surviving fifteenth-century originals.

## Brera–Brambilla Visconti pack, Milan, c. 1440s

**[Documented fact]** This fragment is normally associated with Filippo Maria Visconti and survives principally in the Pinacoteca di Brera. It preserves only two trumps and many suit cards.

**Finding:** A securely identifiable surviving Knight of Batons is not established in the sources consulted. It should not be illustrated or described as though the card were present merely because modern completed editions supply it.

Its chief relevance is the development of Milanese suit emblems and Visconti coinage immediately before or alongside Cary–Yale.

## Visconti–Sforza, Milan/Cremona, c. 1450–60

**[Documented fact]** Seventy-four original cards survive across the Morgan Library, Accademia Carrara, and Colleoni holdings. Four cards are lost: Devil, Tower, Three of Swords, and Knight of Coins. The Knight of Staves therefore survives, but not in the Morgan’s 35-card portion.

The Knight is a mounted man in fifteenth-century dress, carrying a staff or baton. The horse proceeds with relative calm against a decorated gold ground. There is no desert, pyramid, salamander cloak, exploding flame, or rearing black horse.

**[Scholarly interpretation]** The figure represents a social rank—mounted officer, courtier, or noble retainer—more readily than an individual personality. Movement follows naturally from the mounted rank, but “restlessness” is not inscribed in the image.

### Was he a Sforza family member?

**[Disputed claim]** Gertrude Moakley and Stuart Kaplan discussed portrait-like identifications in the Visconti–Sforza pack and the relationship between court figures, heraldry, and the ducal family. Popular sources subsequently turned many courts into named portraits.

For this card, no secure contemporary inscription identifies Francesco Sforza, a son, or another family member. Costume and household emblems establish patronage, not individual likeness.

**Finding:** The specific claim “Knight of Wands = Francesco Sforza” could not be traced to a contemporary source and should be treated as modern romantic attribution. Moakley’s festival/procession theory and Kaplan’s portrait discussions do not convert the identification into documented fact.

## Ferrarese and Este material, later fifteenth century

**[Documented fact]** Ferrara’s court records document painted *carte da trionfi* and payments to artists. Surviving Ferrarese or Este fragments show stylistically distinct trumps and suit figures.

**Finding:** The incomplete fragments do not provide an unbroken, securely catalogued Knight-of-Batons sequence comparable to Cary–Yale and Visconti–Sforza. Modern “Este Tarot” completions often combine originals with reconstructed cards.

## Sola Busca, Ferrara/Venice milieu, 1491

**[Documented fact]** The Sola Busca is the earliest substantially complete tarot whose numeral suit cards carry extensive figurative scenes. Its trumps and suits are populated by named classical or pseudo-classical warriors and elaborate narratives.

For the Knight of Batons, the card remains a mounted or martial court figure tied to the suit’s weapon/staff emblem. The pack’s extraordinary historical importance concerns the illustrated pips—especially its influence on Pamela Colman Smith—not a direct copying of its Knight of Batons.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** Smith likely saw the Sola Busca cards exhibited at the British Museum in 1908. Several Waite–Smith pip compositions have close Sola Busca analogues. No comparably exact derivation for her Knight of Wands has been demonstrated.

## “Mantegna Tarocchi,” c. 1460s

**[Documented fact]** These are fifty educational engravings organized into social stations, liberal arts, virtues, cosmic principles, and spheres. They are not a tarot pack and contain no suits or Knight of Wands.

**Finding:** Not relevant to the card except as evidence of the fifteenth-century visual culture sometimes incorrectly absorbed into “tarot.”

## Rosenwald and Budapest sheets, late fifteenth/early sixteenth century

**[Documented fact]** These printed sheets preserve early popular tarot designs and help bridge luxury Milanese cards and later woodcut patterns.

**Finding:** Their fragmentary state and sheet organization do not support a detailed, securely individualized Knight-of-Batons genealogy. They demonstrate that mounted suit courts were moving into reproducible popular cardmaking, but claims about the exact coloring or divinatory symbolism of this card would be reconstruction.

## Minchiate, Florence, sixteenth–nineteenth centuries

**[Documented fact]** Minchiate expanded the trump suit to forty-one cards and retained Latin suits and four courts. The Cavalli are famously hybrid or fantastic: horses or monstrous animals with humanlike heads occur in standard patterns.

The Batons Knight is therefore less a realistic chivalric horseman than a conventional rider on a grotesque mount. The suit emblem remains a baton/club.

**Game meaning:** It is still a suited court, not an astrological trump, despite Minchiate’s zodiac and element trumps.

## Bolognese tarocchino

**[Documented fact]** Bolognese tarocchino reduced the pack to sixty-two cards by removing low numeral cards but retained the courts. Its Batons Knight is a mounted rank in a compressed, often double-ended later design.

No Golden Dawn-style Fire-of-Fire symbolism belongs to the historical Bolognese game.

## Sicilian tarot

**[Documented fact]** The Sicilian pattern altered both trumps and court arrangements. Its court terminology and costume are regional, and female or maid-like figures survive in ways recalling the broader Italian experimentation with court gender.

**Finding:** No stable Sicilian card titled in English “Knight of Wands” should be assumed without naming a specific edition; regional packs differ, and the card remains primarily a playing-card *cavallo*.

## Jean Noblet, Paris, c. 1650

Noblet’s pack is the earliest substantially surviving example normally placed in the Tarot de Marseille Type I family.

The Cavalier of Batons is a single-headed mounted figure holding a club/staff. The horse and rider are stylized through woodcut outlines and limited stencil color. The image gives rank and suit; it does not provide a desert story.

The familiar phrase “Tarot de Marseille” is retrospective. Romain Merlin used the designation in 1856; it was later popularized by occultists and twentieth-century publishers.

## Jean Dodal, Lyon, 1701–15

The BnF’s complete Dodal pack has seventy-eight woodcut and stencil-colored cards. Its Batons Cavalier is a mounted figure with the organic club characteristic of Type I Marseille designs. Inscriptions on the Cavaliers include *F. P. Le Trenge/Trange*, interpreted as *fait pour l’étranger*, made for export.

Compared with Milanese luxury cards:

- the gold ground disappears;
- heraldic specificity yields to a reproducible popular pattern;
- the baton becomes a conspicuous rustic branch or club;
- the rider is a type, not a proposed portrait.

## Nicolas Conver model, Marseille, 1760

The extant BnF witness commonly called “Conver 1760” was printed later, in approximately 1809–33, from a model traditionally dated 1760.

The card shows a rider moving left, the horse’s head lowered, and a large green baton held upright and behind the rider. The cartouche reads *CAVALIER DE BATON*.

The gait is controlled rather than headlong. Much modern talk of a rearing, impulsive horse belongs to Waite–Smith, not Conver.

## Grimaud / Paul Marteau, twentieth century

Paul Marteau’s Grimaud *Ancien Tarot de Marseille* normalized the Conver-derived pattern with strong industrial color and clear black outlines. The Cavalier retains the green baton, side-on horse, and traditional inscription.

**[Tradition]** Marteau’s interpretive system treats colors, gestures, and numerical relationships symbolically. Those meanings are twentieth-century exegesis of an older game pattern, not evidence of the seventeenth-century cardmakers’ intent.

## Camoin–Jodorowsky, 1997

Philippe Camoin and Alejandro Jodorowsky advertised their deck as a “restoration” of an authentic Marseille structure.

The Cavalier’s staff was intentionally made green and directed upward. Jodorowsky interprets it as returning to purity and rising toward heaven, a sign of evolution. He ranks the Cavalier as the culmination of each court sequence rather than merely below Queen and King.

**[Modern invention/tradition]** This is a modern interpretive reconstruction. The claim that the restored colors encode an ancient single “code” is disputed because historical Marseille packs vary and no master archetype survives.

Jodorowsky’s published meaning includes a person who changes life abruptly under an illuminated impulse, providential aid, protection, support, and force capable of challenging destiny.

## Etteilla’s Grand Etteilla, 1789 onward

Etteilla reorganized, renumbered, renamed, and Egyptianized tarot. The card corresponding to the Knight of Batons appears as no. 24 in important Grand Etteilla traditions and is captioned:

> “Départ — Désunion.”

The card shows a rider poised to leave. The text explains that his position indicates impending departure and that the card signifies change. Reversed, especially in a reading for a young woman, it may announce a marriage nearly broken off.

The double identification *Chevalier de Bâton ou de Carreau* reflects Etteilla’s correspondence between tarot Batons and ordinary French Diamonds.

## Oswald Wirth, 1889 and later

Wirth’s celebrated 1889 deck, drawn for Stanislas de Guaita, consisted of twenty-two trumps. It therefore has no Knight of Wands.

Later full-pack “Wirth” decks are expansions by later publishers and artists. Their Knight cannot be attributed directly to Wirth’s original 1889 images.

## Golden Dawn manuscript designs, c. 1888–1900

In the Golden Dawn system the mounted Knight is the highest and most active court rank. The manuscripts also call him the King.

His full title is:

> “Lord of the Flame and Lightning; King of the Spirits of Fire.”

The design places an armored warrior on a black horse amid fire. He carries a torch or fire-wand; winged or flame-associated insignia express violent, initiating energy.

The Golden Dawn’s Prince of Wands is a different card: a crowned warrior in a chariot, the Air of Fire.

### Wang and Cicero reconstructions

- Robert Wang’s *Golden Dawn Tarot* reconstructs the order’s manuscript descriptions in a formal, emblematic style.
- Sandra Tabatha and Chic Cicero’s *Golden Dawn Magical Tarot* similarly visualizes the color scales, implements, astrological fields, and elemental rank structure.

Both are twentieth-century reconstructions of manuscript instructions, not surviving cards painted for the original order.

## Waite–Smith, 1909

Pamela Colman Smith’s Knight wears armor and a yellow cloak patterned with salamanders. His plumed helmet and the horse’s red-orange tack amplify the Wands palette. He holds a short sprouting wand. The horse rears or prances in a sandy landscape before three low mounds or pyramidal formations.

Waite describes him as traveling, armed but not on a warlike errand:

> “The motion of the horse is a key to the character of its rider.”

The horse supplies the “precipitate mood.” The mounds create distance and unfamiliar territory. The salamanders derive from Renaissance and occult associations between salamanders and Fire.

### Sources of Smith’s composition

- **[Documented context]** Smith was a Golden Dawn initiate and worked from Waite’s program.
- **[Strong scholarly reconstruction]** The mounted court corresponds to the Golden Dawn Fire-rank Knight/King rather than its chariot Prince.
- **[Documented context]** Smith had access to historic playing-card and costume sources and probably saw the Sola Busca exhibition.
- **[Unproved claim]** No exact old-master or Sola Busca prototype for this particular pose has been demonstrated.
- **[Modern invention]** The desert, salamander garment, strong rearing motion, and coherent Fire palette are distinctive features of Smith’s design.

### The rank confusion

Waite retained ordinary tarot names—King, Queen, Knight, Page—but much of the underlying Golden Dawn scheme had **Knight/King, Queen, Prince/King, Princess**.

Accordingly:

| Waite–Smith visible title | Closest Golden Dawn elemental office |
|---|---|
| King of Wands | Prince/King, Air of Fire |
| Queen of Wands | Queen, Water of Fire |
| Knight of Wands | Mounted King/Knight, Fire of Fire |
| Page of Wands | Princess/Knave, Earth of Fire |

Calling the Waite Knight “the Golden Dawn Prince” is therefore a misattribution. The Prince is chariot-borne and corresponds more closely to Waite’s King.

## Paul Foster Case / BOTA

Case and Jessie Burns Parke produced a deck based heavily on Waite–Smith but intended for student coloring. The courts are line drawings; the pips revert to suit-symbol arrangements rather than Smith’s full scenes.

Case preserved the Knight of Wands as an active, generous, impetuous, quick-moving man younger than the King in his public divinatory material. In later BOTA instruction, the Knight covers the last decan of Scorpio and first two of Sagittarius.

**Tension:** Case’s public rank language follows King–Queen–Knight–Page, while his occult schooling derives from Golden Dawn correspondences. This reproduces some of the Waite/GD ambiguity.

## Thoth, painted 1938–43; first full publication 1969

Crowley and Frieda Harris made the Golden Dawn’s mounted elemental King explicitly the **Knight of Wands**.

He is an armored warrior on a leaping black horse. His helmet bears a black-horse crest. He holds a flaming torch; his mantle flames, and he rides on fire. Geometric, radiant color turns the scene into a diagram of explosive energy rather than a naturalistic journey.

Crowley states:

> “The Knight of Wands represents the fiery part of Fire.”

His moral qualities include activity, generosity, fierceness, impetuosity, pride, impulsiveness, and unpredictable speed. Badly energized, the same force becomes cruel, bigoted, brutal, and unable to adapt after an initial failure.

Crowley also links Fire of Fire with the I Ching hexagram 51, Shock/Thunder, emphasizing sudden and revolutionary action.

## Notable modern redesigns

### Thoth-derived decks

These usually retain the mounted highest court, black or fiery horse, torch, and Fire-of-Fire attribution. When titled King rather than Knight, the underlying office may still be the Golden Dawn King.

### Feminist and queer decks

**[Modern invention]** Decks such as Motherpeace and many later inclusive decks replace feudal male hierarchy with daughters, sons, shamans, or gender-neutral figures. A “Son of Wands,” “Explorer of Fire,” or “Knight of Inspiration” may occupy roughly this card’s narrative function without preserving either the historical *cavallo* or Golden Dawn office.

### Modern Witch and other RWS descendants

These retain Smith’s rearing horse, wand, desert, and forward motion while changing gender, ethnicity, costume, or setting. Their visual ancestry is Waite–Smith rather than Marseille.

# The game

## Rank

In standard Latin-suited tarot, the court order is:

**King > Queen > Knight/Cavalier > Page/Valet > numeral cards.**

The Knight is therefore the third-highest suited card and second-lowest court.

In earlier three-court Latin packs without Queens, the order was King > Knight > Knave. Tarot’s innovation was the addition of a Queen, not necessarily the addition of a Knight.

## Card value

Values depend on the regional game and counting method.

### French tarot

In modern French tarot:

- King: 4½ points
- Queen: 3½
- Knight/Cavalier: 2½
- Jack/Valet: 1½
- other cards: ½

Thus the Cavalier of the Wands-equivalent suit in an archaic Latin-suited pack would count 2½, but modern French tournament tarot uses French suits—clubs, hearts, spades, diamonds—not Batons.

### Italian and central European games

Many tarocchi/tarock systems count in grouped tricks, deducting a fixed amount from card totals. Common relative values remain King 5, Queen 4, Cavalier 3, Jack 2 before the game’s deduction formula. Exact net points vary.

### Minchiate

The Batons Knight is an honor/court card and outranks all numeral Batons below it, but scoring conventions vary by ruleset. It has no special occult or elemental status in play.

## Suit-card ranking

In many traditional tarot games:

- Swords and Batons rank from King downward through courts, 10, 9 … Ace.
- Cups and Coins reverse the numeral sequence below the courts: King, Queen, Knight, Page, Ace, 2 … 10.

This “long suits/round suits” asymmetry is a game convention. It is unrelated to modern divinatory notions of ascending spiritual energy.

## Trump-order disputes

Tarot historians distinguish regional trump orders, often grouped broadly as:

- **Type A:** associated especially with Bologna/Ferrara;
- **Type B:** associated with Venice and some northeastern traditions;
- **Type C:** associated with Milan and the line ancestral to Marseille.

Justice, Fortitude, and Temperance shift positions; Popess/Pope and Emperor/Empress can be reordered, equalized, or replaced; the Angel and World exchange precedence in some games.

These disputes do not affect the Knight of Wands. A suited Knight always loses to a trump when a trump is legally played.

## The Fool

The Fool historically acts as the Excuse in many tarot games: it may be played without following suit and normally does not win the trick, after which it is recovered subject to compensation rules. In some late games it becomes the highest trump.

Again, this does not number the Knight. Modern lists assigning the Knight a number such as 12 are editorial suit positions, not game identities.

## Decans in Book T

The Knight’s thirty-degree domain comprises:

- 21°–30° Scorpio: the Golden Dawn Five of Cups, “Loss in Pleasure,” Mars in Scorpio;
- 1°–10° Sagittarius: Eight of Wands, “Swiftness,” Mercury in Sagittarius;
- 11°–20° Sagittarius: Nine of Wands, “Great Strength,” Moon in Sagittarius.

A common secondary source instead calls the Scorpio portion “Moon in Scorpio”; that results from mixing decan tables or modern astrological schemes. In the standard Golden Dawn pip attribution, the final Scorpio decan is Mars in Scorpio.

Crowley rounds the field verbally to “the 21st degree of Scorpio to the 20th degree of Sagittarius.” Modern books sometimes write “20° Scorpio to 20° Sagittarius,” meaning the boundary at 20°/21°, not forty degrees.

## Hebrew letter and Tree of Life

**Finding:** The Knight of Wands has no individual Hebrew letter and no individual path among the twenty-two paths. Hebrew-letter assignments belong to the trumps.

His Kabbalistic identity is structural:

- Wands = Atziluth, the archetypal world, and Fire;
- Knight/King = Yod;
- therefore Knight of Wands = Yod of Yod, Fire within Fire.

Claims assigning the card Heh, Vau, or a numbered Tree path usually arise from:

- Papus’s different court ordering, where Knight = Vau;
- confusion between Waite’s printed Knight and the Golden Dawn Prince;
- importing trump correspondences into courts.

## Tzaddi/Heh and Emperor/Star

Crowley’s “Tzaddi is not the Star” revision exchanges the Hebrew-letter attributions of the Emperor and Star in his Thelemic system.

It has no direct effect on the Knight of Wands. The Knight has no trump letter to swap. References connecting its zodiacal span to the Tzaddi controversy are modern conflations.

## 777

Crowley’s *777* tabulates the four court elements, worlds, color scales, zodiacal triplicities, and decans underlying the card. It supports Fire of Fire/Yod of Yod but does not transform the court into a twenty-second path.

# Divinatory meanings by author

Quotations are kept short. Page numbers refer to the editions identified; online transcriptions often paginate differently.

## Etteilla school

*Le Grand Etteilla*, card no. 24, p. 88 in the scanned edition consulted:

> “Départ — Désunion.”

The accompanying text says the pictured cavalier is about to leave and “signifie changement”—signifies change. Reversal can indicate a nearly failed marriage.

- Upright: departure, journey when combined with the appropriate neighboring card, change.
- Reversed: disunion; threatened or failed marriage in specified contexts.
- Method: meanings depend heavily on adjacent numbered cards.

## S. L. MacGregor Mathers, 1888

*The Tarot: Its Occult Signification, Use in Fortune-Telling, and Method of Play*, court-card table:

- Upright: departure, absence, flight, emigration; a dark young man and friend; change of residence.
- Reversed: rupture, division, interruption, discord.

Mathers’s printed booklet largely transmits French cartomantic meanings. It predates the surviving/publicly known Golden Dawn *Book T* system and should not be assumed to contain its full elemental court doctrine.

## Papus, 1889

*Le Tarot des Bohémiens*, pp. 316–19:

- Sceptres: enterprises and glory.
- Knight: young man.
- Knight of Sceptres: “Jeune homme brun, ami”—a dark-haired young man, friend.

Papus’s abstract scheme makes the Knight Vau, the neuter/adolescent term. This conflicts with Golden Dawn Yod/Fire.

## Waite, 1910/1911

*The Pictorial Key to the Tarot*:

- Upright: “Departure, absence, flight, emigration”; a friendly dark young man; change of residence.
- Reversed: rupture, division, interruption, discord.

Waite’s list is almost identical to Mathers’s, but Smith’s image greatly intensifies the ideas of speed, heat, and impulsiveness.

## Golden Dawn / Liber LXXVIII

The Golden Dawn personality description gives a brown-haired, fair-complexioned figure with blue or gray eyes, active, generous, fierce, hasty, impetuous, and unpredictable. Ill-dignified, he becomes evil-minded, cruel, bigoted, and brutal.

The system generally uses dignity—relations among cards—rather than simply turning every card upside down. “Reversed” is therefore an imperfect translation of “ill-dignified.”

## Crowley

*The Book of Thoth*, “Court Cards”:

- Well dignified: activity, generosity, fierceness, pride, impulsiveness, rapid unpredictable action.
- Badly dignified: cruelty, bigotry, brutality, inability to modify the first burst of action.
- Core limitation: immense initiating force but poor staying power.

Crowley did not advocate a simple upright/reversed dictionary. Orientation alone does not mechanically determine the “wrongly energized” state.

## Paul Foster Case

*Oracle of the Tarot* (1933):

> “An active, generous, impetuous, quick-moving man.”

Case adds that he is younger than the King of Wands. Later BOTA material ties the card to Scorpio–Sagittarius and the three relevant decan influences.

Publicly accessible Case material consulted did not establish a separate, fixed reversed phrase as securely as Mathers or Waite. It is safer to describe adverse expression as rashness, violence, or unstable energy than to fabricate an exact Case reversal.

## Rachel Pollack

In *Seventy-Eight Degrees of Wisdom*, Pollack treats the Knight as Wands energy in movement: adventurous, charismatic, confident, sexually and creatively charged, but prone to impatience, self-dramatization, and failure to sustain effort.

Pollack does not merely copy Waite’s “departure.” She helps shift the court from external messenger/person toward a psychological or behavioral energy.

For reversal or adverse manifestation: frustration, recklessness, quarrelsomeness, boasting, scattered action, or energy that cannot find a productive direction.

Because Pollack’s book remains under copyright, this is a close summary rather than extended quotation.

## Mary K. Greer

Greer’s *Complete Book of Tarot Reversals* does not reduce reversal to one antonym. Her twelve approaches include blocked, delayed, internalized, projected, excessive, deficient, misdirected, or unconventional expression.

Applied to the Knight of Wands:

- upright: energetic adventurer, passionate initiative, travel, bold action;
- reversed possibilities: rashness; aborted travel; anger turned inward; boasting without action; excessive risk; restless energy delayed or resisted.

This plurality is Greer’s distinctive contribution. A single “reversed = bad man” formula misrepresents her method.

## Jodorowsky and Marianne Costa

The Cavalier of Batons represents a sudden life redirection produced by illuminated spirit; providential help, support, protection, and strong positive energy. Its shadow may be dogmatic or overconfident intervention.

Their court order culminates in the Cavalier, who carries the suit’s energy outward and toward another cycle. This is not the historical rank order of tarot games.

# Esoteric and symbolic readings

## Elemental and Hermetic

### First secure appearance

The systematic **Fire of Fire** attribution is traceable to the Golden Dawn’s late nineteenth-century *Book T* tradition, later published in Crowley’s Liber LXXVIII and developed in *The Book of Thoth*.

The formula is:

- Wands = Fire;
- mounted King/Knight = Fire;
- Knight of Wands = Fire of Fire.

This means initiating ignition, lightning, combustion, attack, inspiration, and rapid expenditure. It is not a meaning recoverable from fifteenth-century card-game documents.

### Spreaders

Mathers and Golden Dawn teaching; Crowley; Israel Regardie; Robert Wang; the Ciceros; Case/BOTA; modern ceremonial-magical tarot writers.

## Kabbalistic

The card is Yod of Yod and belongs to Atziluth. Yod is the seminal point of the divine Name, so the card becomes the initiating spark within the archetypal world.

**Conflict:** Papus makes the Knight Vau because his order is King/Yod, Queen/Heh, Knight/Vau, Knave/final Heh. Golden Dawn makes its mounted Knight/King Yod and its Prince Vau. Both systems are deliberate occult arrangements; neither is an ancient card meaning.

## Astrological

The Book T span crosses Scorpio into Sagittarius:

- concentrated, potentially destructive fixed water at the Scorpio edge;
- expansive, mobile, fiery Sagittarius through two decans.

Modern readers interpret this as a passage from stored intensity to outward quest.

Strictly, the Knight is not simply “Sagittarius.” About one-third of his field is Scorpio. “Knight of Wands = Sagittarius” is a convenient modern simplification.

## Alchemical

Salamanders and fire in Waite–Smith and Thoth encourage an alchemical reading of calcination: heat burns away fixed matter and releases active spirit.

**[Modern/esoteric attribution]** No fifteenth-century text connects the historical Cavallo di Bastoni with calcination. The connection becomes plausible only after Fire is assigned to Wands and fiery emblems are added.

Crowley’s black horse within flame can be read as dark prima materia energized or consumed by spiritual fire, but this is interpretive extension rather than Crowley’s exclusive documented definition.

## Christian

The original mounted court fits Christian-European social hierarchy: King, Queen, mounted warrior, servant. The budding staff may later be compared with Aaron’s rod, pilgrimage staffs, or the virile/creative rod.

**Finding:** No contemporary Christian commentary on the Knight of Batons was located. Allegories involving Aaron, Moses, or divine mission are later esoteric readings.

Jodorowsky’s staff raised toward heaven explicitly spiritualizes the Cavalier as a providential, illuminated agent.

## Jungian and psychological

Twentieth-century humanistic tarot transformed courts from literal people into parts of the psyche.

The Knight of Wands becomes:

- activated libido or creative drive;
- the puer/eternal youth who seeks novelty;
- heroic inflation;
- extraverted intuition;
- a willingness to risk identity through travel or adventure;
- shadow forms of rage, seduction, grandiosity, and failure to finish.

**[Modern invention]** Jung did not create a Knight-of-Wands interpretation. “Jungian tarot” is the work of later writers applying archetype, shadow, projection, individuation, and enantiodromia to the cards.

Pollack, Greer, Sallie Nichols, Mary-Louise von Franz-influenced writers, and contemporary therapists or readers spread this approach. Nichols wrote primarily about the trumps, so citing her as the origin of this court’s specific psychology would be inaccurate.

# People and works

## Artists, patrons, printers, and occultists directly connected

- **Filippo Maria Visconti:** patron of Marziano’s early pack and probably of Visconti cardmaking; no secure portrait identification with this Knight.
- **Bianca Maria Visconti and Francesco Sforza:** patrons or dynastic context for the Visconti–Sforza pack.
- **Bonifacio Bembo/workshop:** traditional attribution for Cary–Yale and Visconti–Sforza cards; some individual attributions remain disputed.
- **Francesco Zavattari, Benedetto Bembo, Franco dei Russi, Antonio Cicognara:** names proposed for portions or replacement groups of early Milanese cards; not all apply directly to the Knight.
- **Jean Noblet:** Paris cardmaker, c. 1650.
- **Jean Dodal:** Lyon cardmaker, 1701–15.
- **Nicolas Conver:** Marseille model traditionally dated 1760.
- **Jean-Baptiste Alliette/Etteilla:** first professional systematizer to give the full pack printed divinatory identities.
- **S. L. MacGregor Mathers:** published the English cartomantic meaning and constructed/transmitted Golden Dawn material.
- **Pamela Colman Smith:** created the best-known modern image.
- **A. E. Waite:** commissioned and described Smith’s deck.
- **Paul Foster Case and Jessie Burns Parke:** adapted Waite/Golden Dawn symbolism for BOTA.
- **Aleister Crowley and Frieda Harris:** made Fire of Fire the card’s visible governing principle.
- **Alejandro Jodorowsky and Marianne Costa:** developed the evolutionary Marseille court theory.
- **Philippe Camoin:** co-produced the 1997 reconstructed Marseille deck.
- **Rachel Pollack:** major humanistic and psychological interpreter.
- **Mary K. Greer:** expanded reversal and court-personality methods.

## Literature and film

### Italo Calvino, *The Castle of Crossed Destinies*

Calvino builds narratives from tarot layouts, using both Marseille and Visconti imagery. The work demonstrates tarot’s capacity to generate combinatorial fiction. No single universally agreed passage establishes the Knight of Wands as a privileged protagonist; claims that a particular unnamed rider “is” this card depend on the edition’s plates and the reader’s reconstruction.

### T. S. Eliot, *The Waste Land*

Eliot invokes “Madame Sosostris,” the drowned Phoenician Sailor, Belladonna, the man with three staves, the Wheel, and the one-eyed merchant. He does not name the Knight of Wands. Identifying one of Eliot’s figures with this court is speculative.

### W. B. Yeats

Yeats belonged to the Golden Dawn and worked within its symbolic world. Tarot informed the order’s initiatory atmosphere, but no canonical Yeats poem was located that unmistakably quotes the Knight of Wands image.

### Ingmar Bergman

*The Seventh Seal* supplies an iconic medieval knight and personified Death, frequently juxtaposed with tarot in later visual culture. It is not a documented quotation of the Knight of Wands. Conflating its knight with the card is modern association.

### Jodorowsky

Tarot informs Jodorowsky’s films, comics, psychomagic, and public persona. His Knight/Cavalier of Batons doctrine is explicit in his tarot work, but identifying individual film characters as fixed embodiments of the card requires interpretation rather than direct attribution.

## Paintings and buildings

No premodern painting or building was located that can be securely demonstrated to quote the Knight of Batons as a tarot card.

Mounted courtiers, equestrian saints, condottieri, and triumphal riders supplied the visual environment from which cardmakers worked. Similarity is not proof of quotation.

The desert “pyramids” behind Smith’s Knight are sometimes connected to Golden Dawn Egyptomania. Waite merely calls them “mounds or pyramids.” No documented architectural source for them has been established.

# Controversies and disputes

## Egyptian origin

Court de Gébelin’s 1781 *Monde primitif* declared tarot an Egyptian book surviving in disguised form. He offered speculative Egyptian etymologies for *tarot* before hieroglyphic Egyptian had been deciphered.

- **Status:** legend/esoteric attribution.
- **Evidence for:** resemblance as perceived by Gébelin; no archival chain.
- **Evidence against:** tarot appears in fifteenth-century northern Italian records and imagery; no Egyptian tarot text, deck, or transmission document survives.
- **Spreaders:** Comte de Mellet, Etteilla, Lévi, Christian, Papus, Mathers, later occult publishers.
- **Historians correcting it:** Dummett; Decker, Depaulis, and Dummett; Kaplan; Farley; Place.

There is an important nuance: European playing cards probably do descend in part from cards transmitted through the Mamluk world, including Egypt. That does not validate Gébelin’s claim that the tarot trumps encode an ancient Egyptian Book of Thoth.

## Romani or “Gypsy” transmission

Nineteenth-century authors claimed Romani travelers preserved or brought tarot from Egypt or India.

- **Status:** legend.
- **First major spread:** Gébelin/Mellet’s Egyptian framework, followed more explicitly by Vaillant and Papus.
- **Evidence:** no early document connects Romani communities with the invention of tarot.
- **Contrary evidence:** Italian court and civic documents precede the later literary myth; the expensive early hand-painted packs were elite court objects.
- **Cultural caution:** “Gypsy” is an exonym historically tied to the mistaken belief that Roma came from Egypt.

Romani practitioners later participated in European fortune-telling cultures. That later practice is not evidence that Roma invented tarot.

## Cathar and Templar claims

Modern books sometimes treat the trumps as concealed Cathar doctrine, a Templar survival, a Grail initiation, or heretical Christianity hidden from the Church.

- **Status:** disputed claim/modern legend.
- **Evidence offered:** symbolic resemblance, southern French geography, persecution narratives, numerical correspondences.
- **Missing evidence:** no Cathar tarot, Templar rule, initiation manuscript, trial record, or continuous transmission identifies the pack.
- **Specific to this card:** nothing in the historical Knight of Batons indicates a Templar, Grail knight, or Cathar perfect. The staff is the suit sign, not documented heretical insignia.

## Kabbalistic origin

The twenty-two trumps were correlated with the twenty-two Hebrew letters in the late eighteenth and nineteenth centuries.

- **Status:** esoteric attribution when used symbolically; disputed when presented as historical authorship.
- **Evidence for ancient origin:** none discovered.
- **Historical development:** Mellet and later Lévi supplied competing letter orders; the Golden Dawn systematized one especially influential order.
- **Specific card:** the Knight is not one of the twenty-two trumps and therefore has no individual Hebrew letter.

## “Fire was always the meaning of Batons”

No fifteenth- or seventeenth-century playing-card document consulted assigns Batons to Fire.

- **Status:** late esoteric attribution.
- **First systematic secure form:** nineteenth-century occult syntheses, especially Golden Dawn.
- **Historical origin of the suit:** Mamluk polo sticks and European clubs/staves.
- **Conclusion:** Fire is foundational to modern occult tarot but not demonstrated as the original game meaning.

## Waite’s Knight versus the Golden Dawn Prince

This is one of the most persistent technical confusions.

- Golden Dawn mounted Knight/King = Yod, Fire.
- Golden Dawn chariot Prince/King = Vau, Air.
- Waite’s pictured Knight is mounted and fiery.
- Waite’s King is seated and corresponds more closely to the stabilized Prince/King office.

Thus, “Waite Knight = Golden Dawn Prince” is generally wrong. It arises from matching the printed words *Knight* and *Prince* by ordinary feudal age rather than matching the elemental office and vehicle.

## “Knight of Wands is Aries”

Crowley discusses Aries as the fiery/cardinal expression of Fire in a general triplicity analysis, but the Knight’s actual zodiacal sector is Scorpio–Sagittarius.

- **Status:** oversimplification or confusion.
- **Possible source:** conflating Fire of Fire with Aries, or confusing the court’s elemental type with its thirty-degree celestial rulership.
- **Verified span:** 21° Scorpio through 20° Sagittarius.

## Decan boundary misprints

Modern tables alternate between:

- “20° Scorpio–20° Sagittarius,” and
- “21st degree Scorpio–20th degree Sagittarius.”

These usually describe the same boundary system: after 20° Scorpio, through 20° Sagittarius. The first wording can misleadingly suggest a full forty-degree span.

A second error assigns the card the final Leo decan through Virgo. That span belongs to the Knight of Disks in the Thoth/Golden Dawn court scheme.

## Alleged family portraits

The Visconti–Sforza courts have often been sold as a family portrait gallery.

- Heraldic patronage is documented.
- Individual likenesses are sometimes plausible.
- Exact identifications are usually uninscribed and disputed.
- The Knight of Staves cannot securely be named as Francesco Sforza or a particular son.

## Camoin–Jodorowsky restoration claims

Camoin and Jodorowsky argue that comparison of surviving cards permits recovery of a coherent original code.

- **Support:** comparative reconstruction can restore missing lines and reveal recurrent workshop conventions.
- **Problem:** historical packs vary in drawing, color, orientation, spelling, and chronology; no single original master sheet survives.
- **Conclusion:** the deck is an influential modern synthesis, not a neutral facsimile.

## Misprints and traditions

- *Baston/Batons/Bâton/Bâtons* varies across printers and translations.
- Dodal’s irregular inscriptions reflect early printing and export, not secret codes by default.
- “Rider Tarot” obscures Smith’s authorship; “Waite–Smith” or “Rider–Waite–Smith” is more informative.
- Thoth is sometimes dated 1944 because of the book; the paintings were created 1938–43 and the complete commercial deck appeared in 1969.
- Wirth 1889 is sometimes cited as a full 78-card source. It originally comprised twenty-two trumps.
- “Cary–Yale has 86 surviving cards” is false: 67 survive; 86 is a reconstruction of the possible original pack.
- “Conver 1760 BnF deck” can be misleading: the design/model bears the 1760 tradition, while the digitized physical impression is catalogued later.

# Open questions

1. **Exact dating of Cary–Yale.** Coin evidence supports the 1440s, but catalogues and scholars have proposed later dates.

2. **Original Cary–Yale pack size.** Six courts per suit are strongly suggested, but the complete trump complement and total number remain reconstructed.

3. **Exact workshop hands.** Bonifacio Bembo remains central, but individual cards and later replacements have been assigned to several artists.

4. **Precise collection record for the Visconti–Sforza Knight of Staves.** It belongs to the Bergamo-distributed portion; public summaries do not always distinguish Accademia Carrara from the private Colleoni holding card by card.

5. **Portrait identity.** No documentary evidence securely names the rider.

6. **Waite–Smith visual source.** Golden Dawn structure is clear, and Smith’s access to historic cards is documented, but no single prototype for the rearing horse, desert, or clothing has been proven.

7. **Etteilla’s first exact printing.** The no. 24 Departure/Disunion text is present in the scanned Grand Etteilla tradition, but editions were repeatedly revised and undated/reprinted. Establishing the absolute first impression requires bibliographical comparison beyond the accessible scan.

8. **Lévi’s card-specific teaching.** Lévi is crucial to tarot–Kabbalah, but no separate Knight-of-Wands paragraph comparable to Waite or Crowley was located in *Dogme et rituel*. Quoting a modern Fire-of-Fire formula as Lévi’s would be anachronistic.

9. **Case reversal wording.** Accessible public texts establish Case’s upright/personality formula and zodiacal field, but not a uniquely authoritative verbatim reversed entry.

10. **Pollack and Greer page variation.** Their books exist in several editions. Their readings are summarized here; exact page citations should be checked against the reader’s edition before quotation.

11. **Contested-origin search result.** No Knight-specific Egyptian, Romani, Cathar, or Templar evidence was located. Those claims attach to tarot as a whole and are subsequently projected onto the rider.
