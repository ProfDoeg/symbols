# Eight of Wands: Research Dossier

## Method and evidentiary labels

This dossier distinguishes:

- **[Documented fact]** Directly supported by a surviving card, archival record, rulebook, or primary printed text.
- **[Scholarly reconstruction]** An inference advanced by historians from surviving objects and documents.
- **[Tradition]** A conventional association transmitted through card-making or divinatory practice without proof of ancient origin.
- **[Esoteric attribution]** A deliberately constructed occult correspondence.
- **[Disputed claim]** A proposition for which specialists or witnesses disagree.
- **[Legend]** A historical narrative contradicted by chronology or unsupported by evidence.
- **[Modern invention]** A substantially twentieth- or twenty-first-century interpretation retrospectively attached to the card.

Short quotations are given where an inspectable primary text was found. Page references refer to the editions listed under Sources. Some requested modern authors remain under copyright; their positions are therefore summarized rather than extensively quoted.

### A necessary correction about the “earliest example”

**[Documented fact]** Fifteenth-century Visconti-family packs preserve numeral suit cards, but their surviving portions are incomplete. The Pierpont Morgan–Bergamo Visconti-Sforza group, probably produced after Francesco Sforza’s accession in Milan in 1450, now totals 74 cards across New York and Bergamo; its lost or dispersed suit cards make statements that every individual pip survives unsafe. The Morgan describes the pack as a courtly game object, probably painted by Bonifacio Bembo or his workshop—not as an occult instrument. [Morgan Library collection record](https://www.themorgan.org/collection/tarot-cards), [Morgan manuscript catalogue](https://www.themorgan.org/manuscript/158525).

**[Documented fact]** The Sola Busca is the earliest substantially complete surviving 78-card tarot and the earliest surviving pack to give every numeral card an independent pictorial composition. Its engraved state is dated 1491; the uniquely preserved colored pack was probably painted around the turn of the century. [Public Domain Review overview and images](https://publicdomainreview.org/collection/sola-busca), [Paris Musées Sola Busca record](https://www.parismuseescollections.paris.fr/fr/petit-palais/oeuvres/carte-du-tarot-sola-busca-lenpio-passavant-3-tome-2-page-129).

Accordingly:

- If “earliest Eight of Wands” means the earliest surviving card that can confidently be singled out as an autonomous scenic Eight, the answer is **Sola Busca, 1491**.
- If it means the hypothetical Eight belonging to the earliest surviving tarot families, a Visconti-family predecessor may have existed earlier, but the evidence is fragmentary.
- Claims that the Waite-Smith Eight specifically copies the Sola Busca Eight are **not securely demonstrated**. Smith’s general debt to Sola Busca imagery is strong; the best-known close correspondences involve other pips, especially Three of Swords and Ten of Wands/Swords. The Eight-of-Wands-specific claim is weaker and should not be repeated as established fact.

## Names and terminology

### From *trionfi* to *tarocchi*

**[Documented fact]** The fifteenth-century Italian game was initially called *carte da trionfi*—cards of triumphs. The ordinary suits were cups, coins, swords, and batons. The additional allegorical cards were *trionfi*, ancestors of the English word “trumps.”

The earliest presently known archival reference to *trionfi* is a Florentine record of 1440, discovered after Dummett’s principal books appeared. Ferrara’s court accounts contain records in 1442. One entry pays Jacopo/Sagramoro for coloring “cups, swords, coins and batons and all the figures” of four triumph packs. [Le Tarot cultural association](https://www.letarot.it/page.aspx?id=350), [Farley, *A Cultural History of Tarot*, digitized copy](https://e-isomero.com/pdf/book/1611846398.pdf).

**[Documented fact]** The noun *tarocchi* is first securely documented in Ferrara in June 1505; French *taraux* occurs at Avignon later in 1505. Neither word has a demonstrated Egyptian etymology. [Dummett historical discussion](https://forum.tarothistory.com/viewtopic.php?t=1185).

### Suit-name development

- Italian: *bastoni*—sticks, clubs, batons.
- French: *bâtons*.
- Spanish: *bastos*.
- German esoteric decks: *Stäbe*.
- English occult decks: Sceptres, Rods, Wands, or Staves.
- Golden Dawn manuscripts: “Wands or Torches.”
- Crowley-Harris: Wands.

**[Documented fact]** The suit descends from the club or polo-stick suit of Mediterranean playing cards. In Italian- and Spanish-suited packs it remained a literal game suit long before occultists identified it with elemental Fire.

**[Esoteric attribution]** Wands = Fire and Yod is not a documented fifteenth-century meaning. It belongs to the Hermetic-Qabalistic synthesis consolidated by the Golden Dawn in the late nineteenth century.

### Irrelevant trump-name variations

Papess/Juno, Pope/Jupiter, Maison Dieu, the Fool’s absence of number, and Strength/Justice exchanges concern the trumps, not this card. They do not rename or renumber the Eight of Batons. They matter only because they show that “the tarot” never possessed one immutable historical nomenclature.

## Iconography, deck by deck

## Fifteenth-century Milanese and Ferrarese material

### Marziano da Tortona’s game, circa 1420

**[Documented fact]** Marziano’s text describes a special card game made for Filippo Maria Visconti, with sixteen classical gods and four suits represented by birds.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** Historians regard it as a precursor or close relative of tarot because permanent trump-like subjects outrank suit cards.

**Finding:** It contains no suit of Batons and therefore no Eight of Wands. It should not be presented as an attestation of this card.

### Visconti di Modrone / Cary-Yale, Milan, circa 1440s

**[Documented fact]** This exceptional luxury pack contains additional female court ranks and theological virtues absent from the standard 78-card structure. Its suit cards use heraldic and repeated-object arrangements, gilded and painted rather than scenic narratives.

**Finding:** No securely catalogued surviving Cary-Yale Eight of Batons was located in the consulted Yale and museum material. Modern reconstructed editions fill gaps and must not be mistaken for surviving originals.

### Brera-Brambilla, Milan, circa 1440s–1450s

**[Documented fact]** A fragmentary Milanese luxury pack associated with the Visconti court. Its numeral suits consist of repeated heraldic objects on ornamental grounds.

**Finding:** The searched catalogues did not furnish a surviving Eight of Batons. Its relevance is typological: early luxury numeral cards were non-scenic.

### Pierpont Morgan–Bergamo Visconti-Sforza, Milan, after 1450

**[Documented fact]** The Batons are straight or decorated emblems repeated against gold grounds, sometimes accompanied by ribbons and Sforza devices. Their function is numerical and heraldic. There is no landscape, velocity, messenger, or divinatory “event.”

**Meaning of the design:** Court ownership and suit recognition, not an attested occult allegory.

### Este and Ferrarese fragments

**[Documented fact]** Fifteenth-century Ferrarese tarot fragments survive, but not as a complete sequence of scenic numeral cards. Ornament, repetition, heraldry, and suit counting predominate.

**Finding:** No Ferrarese Eight of Batons earlier than Sola Busca could be securely verified from an open museum catalogue during this search.

### Sola Busca, engraved 1491; colored circa 1490s–early 1500s

**[Documented fact]** The Sola Busca transforms the numeral suits into individual narrative or emblematic pictures populated by classical-looking warriors, laborers, instruments, vessels, and strange machines. It is the earliest complete surviving model of fully illustrated tarot pips. [Sola Busca collection](https://publicdomainreview.org/collection/sola-busca).

Its Eight of Batons belongs to that scenic system rather than the crossed-stick grammar later standardized in Marseille. The card’s objects are made to participate in a physical composition; they are not simply eight interchangeable suit marks.

**[Disputed claim]** It has often been said that Pamela Colman Smith copied the Sola Busca Eight when devising eight flying rods. The available comparative evidence does not establish the same close, one-to-one borrowing visible in several other Waite-Smith pips. British Museum photographs of the Sola Busca were exhibited in London in 1908, making Smith’s access plausible, but access is not proof of dependence for every card.

**Finding:** “Sola Busca supplied Smith’s general precedent for scenic minors” is defensible. “The Sola Busca Eight is the direct source of Smith’s flying-wands scene” remains unproved.

### “Mantegna Tarocchi,” circa 1460s

**[Documented fact]** These are two series of fifty didactic engravings arranged in five decades—social estates, Apollo and the Muses, liberal arts, virtues, and celestial spheres.

**[Scholarly consensus]** They are not tarot playing cards and contain no suits. The traditional name “tarocchi” is a later misnomer. They have no Eight of Wands.

### Rosenwald and Budapest sheets, late fifteenth/early sixteenth century

**[Documented fact]** These woodcut sheets preserve early popular tarot/triumph patterns and are crucial for the evolution and ordering of the trumps. Some sheets include suit material.

**Finding:** They do not supply a clearly documented autonomous scenic Eight comparable to Sola Busca or Waite-Smith. Their importance is evidence that inexpensive, repeatable woodcut packs existed alongside luxury painted ones.

## Regional Italian packs

### Minchiate, Florence/Tuscany

**[Documented fact]** Minchiate expanded the trump series to forty-one while retaining four Italian suits. Its numeral Batons remained patterned pips rather than Waite-style scenes.

**Game distinction:** The Eight is still an ordinary suit card. The expanded trump sequence does not turn it into an “arcana” or give it an occult title.

### Tarocco Bolognese

**[Documented fact]** The Bolognese pack was later shortened to 62 cards by suppressing the numeral 2–5 cards of each suit. The Eight of Batons remains because it lies above that suppressed range. The game, tarocchino, preserves unusual rank and combination rules. [Tarocco Bolognese overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tarocco_Bolognese).

**[Documented fact]** A mid-eighteenth-century Bolognese manuscript supplies some of the earliest known tarot cartomancy, predating Court de Gébelin; it does not establish an ancient divinatory meaning for the Eight.

### Tarocco Siciliano

**[Documented fact]** The Sicilian pattern uses regionally altered trumps and suit imagery. As in other Italian-suited packs, Batons are recognizable club-like suit signs.

**Finding:** No early independent occult doctrine concerning its Eight of Batons was traced.

## Tarot de Marseille lineage

### Jean Noblet, Paris, circa 1650

**[Documented fact]** The numbered Batons use an interlaced geometric structure. For the Eight, four batons cross four others, creating a regular lattice. Small leaves or flowers occupy the remaining axis or spaces.

**Meaning in its own period:** No printed divinatory meaning accompanies the card. It is an ornamental game pip.

### Jean Dodal, Lyon, circa 1701

**[Documented fact]** Dodal preserves the eightfold diagonal lattice, stronger outlines, and small floral fillers. The composition is vertically and horizontally balanced.

### Nicolas Conver, Marseille, 1760 pattern

**[Documented fact]** The conventional Eight shows eight crossed batons, four against four, forming a large trellised lozenge; red, yellow, blue, and black linework distinguishes bark, cut surfaces, or decorative bands. Small flowers lie along the vertical axis. A BnF witness traditionally associated with Conver may date from a later period of the workshop, so “the actual 1760 copy” should be used cautiously. [BnF/Conver discussion](https://www.marselia.app/blog/nicolas-conver-1760-tarot), [card description](https://www.marselia.app/cards/huit-de-baton).

**Later symbolic reading:** The lattice has been interpreted as disciplined growth, containment, circulation, or blockage. These are later readings, not documented intentions of Conver.

### Grimaud/Paul Marteau, twentieth century

**[Documented fact]** Grimaud regularized and recolored the Conver-derived pattern. Paul Marteau’s *Le Tarot de Marseille* supplied an influential symbolic vocabulary for pip colors, leaves, openings, and numerical structures.

**[Tradition]** Marteau-style interpretations treat symmetry and vegetal growth as psychologically meaningful. These meanings postdate the historical woodblocks.

### Camoin–Jodorowsky, 1997

**[Documented fact]** Philippe Camoin and Alejandro Jodorowsky produced a reconstructed/redrawn Marseille deck from several historical witnesses.

**[Disputed claim]** Its presentation as a restoration of a single original or “authentic” Tarot de Marseille goes beyond what the surviving evidence permits. The historical Marseille pattern was a family of workshop variants, not one recoverable master deck.

**Jodorowsky-Costa reading:** The Eight is read through the structural perfection of even number 8 and the creative/fire quality of Batons: concentrated, ordered energy at risk of becoming rigid. This is a modern Marseille numerology, not a fifteenth-century doctrine.

## Etteilla and nineteenth-century occult decks

### Grand Etteilla / *Livre de Thot*, circa 1789 onward

**[Documented fact]** Etteilla reorganized all 78 cards into a continuously numbered “Book of Thoth,” supplied upright and reversed meanings, and designed a tarot specifically for divination. Surviving “Grand Etteilla” families are not all identical; some familiar cards belong to early nineteenth-century recensions rather than provably to the first 1789 printing.

**Traditional identification of the Eight of Batons:** agriculture, countryside, fields, gardens, rural property, and related ideas; the reversed sense is associated with quarrels or disputes, particularly domestic or conjugal ones. In some French Etteilla-derived lists it bears *Amourette*—a flirtation or little love affair.

**Source-critical finding:** These meanings are widely reproduced in Etteilla dictionaries, but I did not locate a fully inspectable 1789 card or first-edition table proving the exact wording and deck number. The attribution is therefore **[Tradition based on Etteilla-family witnesses]**, not a verbatim quotation from a verified 1789 sheet.

### Oswald Wirth, 1889

**[Documented fact]** Wirth’s original 1889 contribution was principally a set of twenty-two occult trumps. It did not provide an independently designed full pip sequence.

**Correction:** An “Oswald Wirth Eight of Wands, 1889” is therefore usually a modern completion or an image from a later Wirth-branded 78-card publication, not a surviving card from his original 22-trump series.

### Papus, *Le Tarot des Bohémiens*, 1889

Papus presents the suits through a synthetic scheme involving YHVH, social classes, seasons, and human faculties. Batons/Sceptres are associated with enterprise and productive power.

**[Esoteric attribution]** Papus’s numbered minors are generated by number-plus-suit formulas rather than archaeological study of each medieval pip. His work spread the “Bohemian/Gypsy” title, but the title describes an occult legend, not documented Romani authorship. [Library of Congress record](https://www.loc.gov/item/77355368/).

## Golden Dawn and its reconstructions

### *Book T*, compiled in Golden Dawn circles, late 1880s–1890s

The primary text is explicit:

> “XXXIII. The Lord of Swiftness / Eight of Wands or Torches.”

Its image consists of four radiant angelic hands issuing from clouds, clasped in pairs with an initiatory grip, holding eight wands crossed four against four. Flames issue from their junction; small flaming wands carry the planetary and zodiacal symbols. [*Book T*, pp. 4, 26, 34](https://benebellwen.com/wp-content/uploads/2013/02/mathers-and-felkin-golden-dawn-book-t-the-tarot-1888.pdf).

**Verified correspondences:**

- Mercury in Sagittarius.
- First decan of Sagittarius in the Golden Dawn zodiacal sequence.
- Hod of Yod: the eighth Sephirah expressed in Fire.
- Angels Nithahiah and Haayah in the transliteration used by later editions.
- General numerical rule for the Eights: “Solitary success”—success in the immediate matter, not necessarily productive beyond it.

The text’s fuller character is rapid force, messages, activity, boldness, and swiftness, with haste, violence, or instability when ill-dignified.

**Important dating caveat:** The frequently circulated PDF labels the material “1888,” but *Book T* is an initiatory manuscript tradition compiled and revised within the Order. Treating every line as a publicly fixed 1888 publication is inaccurate.

### Robert Wang, *The Golden Dawn Tarot*, 1978

**[Modern reconstruction]** Wang visualized Golden Dawn descriptions as a complete colored deck: crossed wands, radiant hands, fire, Mercury and Sagittarius emblems. It is evidence for twentieth-century reception, not the appearance of one uniquely authoritative Victorian pack.

### Sandra Tabatha Cicero, *Golden Dawn Magical Tarot*, 2000

**[Modern reconstruction]** Chic and Sandra Cicero produced a more elaborate full-color ritual reconstruction using Golden Dawn color scales and descriptions. It retains the crossed four-against-four structure and explicit astrological emblems.

**Dispute:** Both Wang and Cicero can claim fidelity to textual instructions, but neither is a facsimile of a single complete original Golden Dawn deck.

## Waite-Smith, 1909; text 1910

Pamela Colman Smith draws eight leafing rods moving diagonally through a clear sky. No hand holds them; no person appears. Below are a river, green landscape, and a distant building or village. The rods are parallel and point downward toward the right, though whether they are arrows in flight, thrown spears, or suspended staves is not explicitly settled by the image.

Waite’s description calls them:

> “a flight of wands through an open country.”

His divinatory list gives activity in undertakings, “the path of such activity,” swiftness “as that of an express messenger,” haste, great hope, speed toward an end, and “arrows of love.” Reversed, he gives arrows of jealousy, internal dispute, stingings of conscience, and quarrels. [Waite, *Pictorial Key to the Tarot*, online edition](https://sacred-texts.com/tarot/pkt/index.htm).

**What changed:**

1. Marseille’s static woven lattice became parallel airborne rods.
2. Golden Dawn ritual hands and astrological glyphs disappeared.
3. The Golden Dawn keyword “Swiftness” became literal visible motion.
4. A landscape gave the action direction and an implied destination.
5. The absence of human actors made motion itself the protagonist.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** Smith probably worked from Waite’s instructions, Golden Dawn doctrine, older pip cards, and her own theatrical/illustrative vocabulary.

**[Disputed claim]** Sola Busca photographs displayed at the British Museum in 1908 probably affected several Smith pips. There is no surviving instruction sheet or statement from Smith identifying Sola Busca as the source of this Eight.

## Paul Foster Case and BOTA

### BOTA Tarot, designed in the 1920s–1930s

**[Documented fact]** Case retained the Waite-Smith scenic framework while revising details to align with his Builders of the Adytum curriculum. BOTA cards were intended to be colored by students.

**[Esoteric attribution]**

- Wands = Fire = Yod.
- Eight = Hod.
- Mercury in Sagittarius.
- Activity, rapidity, message-bearing movement, and directed force.
- Reversal or adverse influence produces haste, dispute, or scattered motion.

Case’s system is Golden Dawn-derived but reorganized through his own color, sound, number, and Qabalistic teaching.

## Crowley-Harris Thoth Tarot

### Painted 1938–1943; first complete commercial publication 1969

**[Documented fact]** Aleister Crowley designed the doctrine; Frieda Harris painted the cards. The Eight is titled **SWIFTNESS**.

Eight colored zigzag or lightning-like rods/energetic shafts radiate in a controlled geometric field. Near the center is an eight-rayed form resembling an electrical or prismatic discharge. Mercury and Sagittarius glyphs identify the decan. The visual field dispenses with Smith’s landscape and converts movement into abstract force.

Crowley writes:

> “The Eight of Wands is called Swiftness, as one might expect from its attribution to Mercury and Sagittarius.”

He further associates Hod in Fire with speech, light, and electricity. [Crowley, *The Book of Thoth*, digitized edition](https://www.mr-kaplan.com/Library/Crowley/Book%20of%20Thoth.pdf).

**Meaning of Harris’s redesign:**

- The crossed Golden Dawn wands become waveforms or energetic vectors.
- Mercury supplies transmission, language, calculation, and rapid fluctuation.
- Sagittarius supplies fiery expansion and direction.
- Hod supplies intellectual articulation.
- The card can therefore represent speed that is brilliant but transient—energy discharged faster than it can consolidate.

**[Esoteric attribution]** Crowley’s remark that the force may be too rapid to establish a lasting structure develops *Book T*’s Hod formula, “solitary success.”

### Tzaddi/Heh issue

Crowley’s exchange of the Emperor and Star correspondences—“Tzaddi is not the Star”—concerns trumps IV and XVII. It does **not** alter the Eight of Wands, whose Mercury/Sagittarius attribution remains the Golden Dawn one.

## Notable modern redesigns

### Aquarian Tarot, 1970

Retains the Waite-Smith idea of linear airborne rods but stylizes it through 1960s graphic design. **[Modern adaptation]**

### Morgan-Greer Tarot, 1979

Uses a close, saturated, borderless presentation of moving wands. It intensifies immediacy rather than introducing a different historical doctrine.

### Motherpeace, 1981

Circular feminist imagery reframes Wands as communal, bodily, and spiritual fire. Its Eight participates in the deck’s feminist and countercultural reinterpretation rather than medieval iconography.

### Wild Unknown, 2012

A minimalist burst of eight wand-like lines emphasizes velocity and focus. This is a modern abstraction independently comparable to Harris’s kinetic geometry.

### Contemporary digital and popular meanings

“Text message,” “email,” “viral news,” “air travel,” and “things happening fast” are **[Modern inventions/extensions]**. They extrapolate Mercury, Waite’s express messenger, and airborne imagery; they are not early meanings.

## The game

## Original function

**[Documented fact]** Tarot was created for trick-taking play. The Eight of Batons was one of the ordinary suited cards; it was never one of the *trionfi*. Fifteenth-century archival records mention the four suits and “figures” of the triumph packs but do not assign occult meanings.

## Rank

Rank reversals are central to historical tarot games:

- In many Italian games, Swords and Batons rank downward from King toward Ace: King, Queen, Knight, Jack, 10, 9, 8 … Ace.
- Cups and Coins often rank King, Queen, Knight, Jack, Ace, 2, 3 … 10, so their numeral direction is reversed.
- In French Tarot, all four suits normally rank King, Queen, Knight, Jack, 10 down to Ace.

Thus the Eight of Batons is commonly:

- below 9 and above 7 in the Italian “long” suits;
- below 9 and above 7 in modern French Tarot;
- subject to local rules in tarocchi, tarock, and tarocchini variants.

## Point value

**[Documented fact]** In many traditional tarot scoring systems, Kings and selected trumps carry the highest card points; Queens, Cavaliers, and Jacks carry descending values. Ordinary numeral cards, including the Eight, usually count only the lowest unit or acquire value through trick/card-counting formulas. It is not generally a high-value counter.

Exact arithmetic varies:

- Some Italian games count captured cards in groups and subtract a fixed amount.
- French Tarot gives ordinary suit cards ½ point each when paired in counting.
- Central European Tarock games often use three-card packet counting.
- Minchiate has its own valuable-card and combination structure.

A universal point value for “the Eight of Wands” therefore does not exist.

## Fool and trump-order disputes

These disputes do not affect the Eight’s suit rank, but they matter to the game’s history:

- **Type A / southern orders:** Bologna and Florence-related sequences.
- **Type B / eastern orders:** Ferrara/Venice-related sequences.
- **Type C / western orders:** Milan and later French sequence ancestral to Marseille.
- Justice, Fortitude/Strength, and Chariot change relative position across regions.
- The Fool may be unnumbered and function as an Excuse rather than an ordinary highest or lowest trump.
- In some Italian games it participates in combinations or special capture rules.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** Dummett derived regional order families from early lists and surviving packs. They demonstrate local evolution, not secret reordering by occult schools.

| Thoth | Wands = Fire | Hod | Mercury in Sagittarius | Hod in Atziluth/Fire | Swiftness; speech, light, electricity |
| Jodorowsky-Costa | Batons = creative/sexual energy | Perfected receptive even number | Astrology not essential | Structural numerology, not GD decans | Energy disciplined or immobilized |
| Jungian tarot | Libido, drive, creativity | Pattern/integration | Optional metaphor | Usually psychological metaphor | Acceleration, synchronicity, release of psychic energy |

### Conflicts

- **Element:** Golden Dawn, Waite, Case, and Crowley use Fire; some twentieth-century occultists such as A. E. Thierens reassigned Wands to Air.
- **Number 8:** Golden Dawn makes it Hod, analytic splendor and communication; modern numerology often calls 8 power, mastery, or material success without a historical tarot basis.
- **Meaning:** Etteilla’s agrarian/country associations differ sharply from Golden Dawn “Swiftness.”
- **Reversal:** *Book T* uses dignity from surrounding cards rather than a simple upside-down binary; Waite gives a formal reversed list.
- **Hebrew letters:** Individual Hebrew letters belong to the trumps, not to this pip. Calling the Eight itself “the Hebrew letter Hod” confuses a Sephirah with a letter.
- **Tree position:** The Eight is Hod in each of four Qabalistic worlds; it is not one of the 22 paths between Sephiroth.

## Esoteric and symbolic readings

### Alchemical

**[Modern/late occult development]** There is no demonstrated alchemical interpretation of the fifteenth-century Eight of Batons. Sola Busca as a whole has been read through Renaissance Hermetic and alchemical culture, especially in modern Italian scholarship, but individual identifications remain interpretive.

In Thoth, rapid fiery transformation and electrical discharge can be read alchemically as volatilization: matter or thought becomes mobile before it can coagulate. This is a Crowleyan/Harris-compatible interpretation, not a documented Sola Busca key.

### Qabalistic

**[Esoteric attribution, Golden Dawn]**

- Eight = Hod, “Splendour.”
- Wands = Yod/Fire/Atziluth.
- Thus the card is Hod in Fire: intellect and language acting through energy.
- Mercury naturally reinforces Hod’s mercurial character.
- Its risk is over-formulation or energy becoming merely transient information.

“Path on the Tree of Life” is inapplicable. Paths belong chiefly to the 22 trumps; pip cards occupy Sephiroth within elemental worlds.

### Astrological

**[Esoteric attribution]** Mercury in the first decan of Sagittarius is verified in *Book T*. Mercury seeks differentiation and transmission; Sagittarius seeks range, direction, and expansion. Golden Dawn and Thoth authors combine them as swift, far-reaching communication.

It should not be represented as a medieval astrology of the card. It is a late Victorian occult allocation produced by placing the 36 decans around the 36 cards from Two through Ten.

### Hermetic

Court de Gébelin, Etteilla, Lévi, Papus, Golden Dawn, and Crowley successively enlarged the claim that tarot encoded a universal Hermetic synthesis. The Eight’s fully articulated Hermetic identity first becomes visible in *Book T*, not in Renaissance card-making records.

### Christian

The suit of rods can evoke staffs, the rod of Moses, pilgrimage, punishment, or the flowering rod of Aaron, but no early source identifies this Eight with a specific Christian episode. Such readings are analogical.

The Visconti and Marseille worlds were Christian, yet cultural setting alone does not prove an intended Christian meaning for every pip.

### Jungian

**[Modern invention/tradition]** Jung did not publish a card-by-card tarot system. Later Jungian readers use the Eight as an image of libido released into purposive motion, synchronicity, psychic transition, or the ego’s inability to control an autonomous process.

Calling this “Jung’s meaning of the Eight of Wands” is inaccurate. It is post-Jungian tarot interpretation spread by psychological tarot writers from the later twentieth century onward.

## People and works

### Directly attached to the card

- **Anonymous and court workshops of Milan/Ferrara:** makers of early non-scenic pips.
- **Sola Busca engraver and colorist:** created the earliest complete scenic-minor system; identities remain debated.
- **Jean Noblet, Jean Dodal, Nicolas Conver:** established and transmitted the crossed-baton pattern.
- **Etteilla/Jean-Baptiste Alliette:** first systematic professional tarot cartomancer and designer of a divinatory tarot.
- **S. L. MacGregor Mathers and Golden Dawn collaborators:** “Lord of Swiftness,” Mercury in Sagittarius.
- **Pamela Colman Smith:** invented the canonical flying-wands landscape.
- **Arthur Edward Waite:** supplied the published verbal reading.
- **Paul Foster Case:** carried the Golden Dawn scheme into BOTA.
- **Frieda Harris and Aleister Crowley:** abstract electrical-geometric Swiftness.
- **Robert Wang and Sandra Tabatha Cicero:** modern Golden Dawn visual reconstructions.
- **Rachel Pollack and Mary K. Greer:** major late-twentieth-century psychological and experiential interpreters.
- **Alejandro Jodorowsky and Marianne Costa:** structural Marseille reading.

### Literature, film, and art named in the brief

**Finding:** No secure card-specific appearance of the Eight of Wands was traced in:

- T. S. Eliot’s *The Waste Land*. Eliot names the drowned Phoenician Sailor, Belladonna, the man with three staves, Wheel, one-eyed merchant, blank card, Hanged Man, and “crowds of people”; not the Eight of Wands.
- Italo Calvino’s *The Castle of Crossed Destinies*. Calvino builds narratives from tarot images, especially recognizable scenic trumps and court cards, but a uniquely identifiable Eight-of-Wands episode was not verified.
- Yeats’s published tarot-related occultism.
- Ingmar Bergman’s films.
- Jodorowsky’s films, as distinct from his tarot books and restoration project.

Claims that any of these works “quote the Eight of Wands” need a scene, page, or production-design witness. None was found in the consulted sources.

**Paintings and buildings:** No securely documented historical painting or building intentionally quoting this pip was identified. Modern murals, tattoos, album art, and deck-derived illustrations frequently reproduce Smith’s flying rods, but they require case-by-case attribution.

## Controversies and disputes

## 1. Egyptian origin

**[Legend, first major printed formulation 1781]** Court de Gébelin declared tarot a surviving Egyptian book concealed as a game. His own opening imagines the astonishment of discovering “an Egyptian book” circulating through Europe unnoticed. [Primary French text](https://www.melmothia.net/3519/du-jeu-des-tarots-antoine-court-de-gebelin-pdf/).

**Evidence for:** The claim’s historical existence is completely documented.

**Evidence against its truth:**

- Tarot is documented in fifteenth-century northern Italy.
- Its suits and game mechanics belong to medieval playing-card culture.
- Gébelin provided no Egyptian prototype.
- His invented etymologies predate reliable decipherment of Egyptian hieroglyphs.

The Eight’s Italian club/baton form supplies no Egyptian iconographic evidence.

## 2. Book of Thoth and Hebrew alphabet

**[Esoteric attribution]** The Comte de Mellet, writing in the same 1781 volume, explicitly compared the 22 trumps with the 22 Hebrew letters and advanced the Book-of-Thoth concept. Later occultists converted analogy into a detailed system.

This pertains directly only to the trumps. Applying one Hebrew letter to the Eight of Wands is a categorical error.

## 3. “Gypsy” or Romani origin

**[Legend]** Nineteenth-century writers associated tarot with “Bohemians” or “Gypsies,” partly because Europeans mistakenly connected Romani people with Egypt. Jean-Alexandre Vaillant and Papus helped spread the narrative.

**Evidence against:** Tarot is recorded in Italy before any documentation that Romani communities transmitted it; linguistic and genetic evidence places Romani origins in South Asia rather than Egypt. Modern scholarship also warns that older “Gypsy magic” ethnography contains fabrication and projection. [Modern Romani-history study](https://arxiv.org/abs/1212.1696), [study of fabricated Romani magical ethnography](https://doi.org/10.1163/15685276-01234012).

This does not deny documented Romani participation in cartomancy at later dates. It denies authorship of fifteenth-century tarot without evidence.

## 4. Cathar and Templar origins

**[Legend/disputed modern speculation]** Tarot has been attributed to Cathar survivors, Templars, secret Grail schools, and heretical Christian fraternities.

**Finding:** No fifteenth-century document, maker’s inscription, rulebook, or contemporary commentary connects the Eight of Batons—or the pack as a whole—to Cathar or Templar transmission. These theories reverse the proper evidentiary direction: resemblance to a symbol is treated as proof of descent.

## 5. Sola Busca as the precise source of Smith’s Eight

**[Disputed]** The chronology permits influence: photographs reached the British Museum before Smith’s commission, and several RWS minors show striking Sola Busca parallels.

But:

- no statement by Smith names the Sola Busca Eight;
- no commission notes documenting this specific borrowing survive;
- eight parallel flying rods are not a close reproduction of the known Sola Busca scenic grammar.

The defensible conclusion is influence on Smith’s project, not verified direct copying of this individual card.

## 6. “Marseille meaning” as ancient meaning

**[Modern invention when retrojected]** Noblet, Dodal, and Conver cards do not print “speed,” “news,” “travel,” or “arrows of love.” Those belong principally to Golden Dawn and Waite-Smith reception.

Likewise, elaborate readings of the Marseille flowers as chakras, sexual organs, alchemical vessels, or hidden Hebrew characters are later symbolic practices unless supported by a cardmaker’s document.

## 7. Deck dating and misleading labels

- “Conver 1760” often names a design lineage, while a particular surviving BnF impression may be later.
- “Grand Etteilla 1789” can refer to later recensions derived from Etteilla rather than cards demonstrably printed that year.
- “Book T, 1888” can misleadingly imply a stable public first edition.
- “Thoth Tarot, 1944” dates Crowley’s book, not the first complete commercial card edition; paintings were made 1938–43 and the full deck was commercially published in 1969.
- “Oswald Wirth Eight, 1889” is anachronistic because Wirth’s original deck comprised trumps.

These are cases where catalog shorthand can become tradition.

## 8. Divination as tarot’s original purpose

**[Legend]** No surviving fifteenth-century evidence makes the Eight a divinatory card.

**[Documented fact]** The earliest records concern manufacture, gifts, payment, condemnation of gambling, and play. Systematic tarot divination belongs to the eighteenth century. Dummett, Decker, and Depaulis made this chronological correction central to modern tarot historiography. [Decker, Depaulis, and Dummett, *A Wicked Pack of Cards*](https://books.google.com/books/about/A_Wicked_Pack_of_Cards.html?id=MG6DAAAAMAAJ).

## Open questions

1. **Which is the earliest surviving physical Eight of Batons?**  
   A card-by-card census of Cary-Yale, Brambilla, Visconti-Sforza, Este, Budapest, and Rosenwald fragments is needed. General deck dates cannot substitute for confirmation that this precise pip survives.

2. **What exactly appears on the Sola Busca Eight?**  
   Open secondary sources consistently establish the deck’s pictorial-minor character, but museum catalogues searchable here did not provide a sufficiently detailed card-specific description. A high-resolution Brera record and the 2012 exhibition catalogue should be consulted directly.

3. **Did Smith see the relevant Sola Busca photographs?**  
   Her opportunity is plausible; card-specific dependence requires documentary evidence that has not surfaced.

4. **What was Etteilla’s first exact wording and card number?**  
   Later dictionaries give agriculture/country and reversed dispute, but variant Grand Etteilla recensions complicate attribution to the 1789 state.

5. **Can “Amourette” be firmly assigned to Etteilla himself?**  
   It appears in Etteilla-derived French cartomantic transmission; a first-edition witness remains needed.

6. **Who wrote each layer of *Book T*?**  
   Mathers is central, but Felkin and other Golden Dawn transmitters affected surviving versions. The title and decan are stable; exact dating of every descriptive sentence is not.

7. **Was Waite’s “arrows of love” prompted by Smith’s rods, or did the phrase prompt the picture?**  
   No surviving instruction establishes direction of influence.

8. **How much did Harris’s geometry originate with Crowley’s instructions?**  
   Correspondence and sketches may clarify the balance between Crowley’s program and Harris’s visual invention.

9. **Are “communication” and “messages” inherent in Mercury, Waite’s messenger phrase, or later popular shorthand?**  
   All three stages contributed; modern readings often collapse them into one supposedly ancient meaning.
