# Two of Wands: Research Dossier

## Method and evidentiary labels

This dossier distinguishes six kinds of statement:

- **Documented fact** — supported by a surviving card, archival record, contemporary printed text, manuscript, or museum catalogue.
- **Scholarly reconstruction** — an inference advanced by historians from material, documentary, or game evidence.
- **Tradition** — an interpretation transmitted within a recognized cartomantic or occult lineage.
- **Esoteric attribution** — a deliberately constructed symbolic correspondence, not a historical claim about the card’s origin.
- **Disputed claim** — proposed but contested, weakly documented, or dependent on ambiguous evidence.
- **Legend / modern invention** — a story or interpretation arising substantially later than the card and unsupported as an account of its historical origin.

Quotations are kept short; longer passages are summarized and linked to the editions consulted.

### The earliest surviving card

**Documented fact.** Italy’s national cultural-property catalogue identifies a *Due di Bastoni* in the Brambilla tarot, dated approximately 1442–1451, attributed to Bonifacio Bembo and preserved in the Pinacoteca di Brera. It consists of two heraldically arranged batons on an illuminated ground rather than a narrative scene. This is presently the earliest securely catalogued surviving Two of Batons found in this investigation. [Italian national catalogue](https://catalogo.beniculturali.it/detail/HistoricOrArtisticProperty/0300097697-38)

**Qualification.** Dates and workshop attributions for individual Visconti-family packs remain subjects of refinement. The catalogue’s range is therefore stronger evidence than an exact commissioning year.

### Golden Dawn attribution

**Documented esoteric attribution.** The published Golden Dawn tarot material calls it:

> “THE LORD OF DOMINION”

and assigns it to **Mars in Aries**, the first decan of Aries, and to **Chokmah of Fire**. Its divinatory complex includes strength, domination, boldness, courage, ambition, turbulence, authority, and power. [Golden Dawn/Book T text](https://www.100thmonkeypress.com/biblio/acrowley/downloads/equinox1_8/tarot/tarot.pdf)

**Correction of a common simplification.** “Dominion” is not a medieval name for the card. It belongs to the Golden Dawn’s late-nineteenth-century magical synthesis and was transmitted publicly in the early twentieth century.

# Iconography deck by deck

## Before tarot: the baton or club suit

### Mamluk and early European playing cards

**Scholarly reconstruction.** Tarot’s four ordinary suits were inherited from the already existing structure of playing cards. Italian and Spanish packs preserve the Latin suit-system of swords, cups, coins, and batons/clubs. The suit-sign was originally an object, not an elemental glyph.

Batons could be represented as:

- rough clubs;
- straight ceremonial staves;
- knobby cudgels;
- interlaced rods forming a lattice;
- later, a stylized occult wand.

**Documented fact.** Nothing in the earliest gaming evidence establishes that *bastoni* meant Fire, will, spiritual energy, Yod, or phallic creative force. Those are later occult interpretations.

## Milanese hand-painted packs, approximately 1440s–1470s

### Visconti di Modrone / Cary-Yale

**Documented fact.** The Visconti di Modrone pack, now primarily in Yale’s Beinecke collection, is an incomplete Milanese luxury pack. Its surviving structure is unusual: it included additional female court figures, so it may once have contained more than the later standard seventy-eight cards.

**Card-specific result.** A Two of Batons survives in the Visconti di Modrone/Cary-Yale material according to comparative surviving-card tables. Its imagery is a luxury pip design: two baton signs disposed symmetrically on an ornamented or gilded field. It does not depict a human scene.

**Meaning of the design.** In evidentiary terms, the batons count the card and participate in courtly decoration. Reading their crossing as alchemical union or conflict is a later interpretation.

### Brambilla tarot

**Documented fact.** The Brambilla *Due di Bastoni*, approximately 1442–1451, is a large illuminated card, about 178 by 89 mm, associated with Bonifacio Bembo and preserved at Brera. [Official catalogue record](https://catalogo.beniculturali.it/detail/HistoricOrArtisticProperty/0300097697-38)

**What is depicted.** Two decorated batons are arranged on an elaborate painted ground. As with other Milanese pips, the visual language is heraldic and ornamental, not scenic.

**Importance.** This is a strong candidate for the earliest surviving physical Two of Wands. It demonstrates that the card began as a countable suit card in a luxury gaming pack, not as a picture of a lord holding a globe.

### Pierpont Morgan–Bergamo / Visconti-Sforza

**Documented fact.** The divided Visconti-Sforza group comprises thirty-five cards at the Morgan Library, twenty-six at the Accademia Carrara, and thirteen in the Colleoni collection. The Morgan dates its portion broadly to Milan, approximately 1450–1480. The whole surviving group includes decorated pips. [Morgan catalogue](https://www.themorgan.org/manuscript/158525)

The Morgan explains the standard structure as twenty-one trumps, the Fool, and fifty-six suit cards divided among cups, staves, coins, and swords. It also explicitly states that these cards were used for gaming, not originally as fortune-telling paraphernalia.

**Two of Batons.** The Two survives in the divided group, reportedly in the Colleoni portion. It follows the same ornamental suit-sign principle: two batons against a richly decorated field.

**Artists.** Original cards have variously been attributed to Bonifacio Bembo, the Bembo workshop, or Francesco Zavattari; several later cards have been attributed to Antonio Cicognara, Benedetto Bembo, Franco dei Russi, or another Ferrarese/Milanese hand. These disagreements affect exact workshop history but do not change the Two’s status as a non-scenic pip.

## Ferrarese and related fifteenth-century cards

### Este cards

**Documented fact.** Various surviving fifteenth-century cards associated with Ferrara or the Este court are fragments, not a complete demonstrable deck. Attribution among the so-called Charles VI, Ercole d’Este, and other groups has changed repeatedly.

**Card-specific result.** No securely catalogued Este Two of Batons was located in the sources consulted. It is unsafe to describe a particular Este image as this card without an institutional identification.

### Sola Busca, 1491

**Documented fact.** The Sola Busca is the earliest substantially complete surviving tarot and the earliest surviving pack in which all numeral cards are individually illustrated with figures and actions. Its engravings carry a date conventionally read as 1491; the hand-coloring is usually placed around a decade later. The complete colored pack is now at the Pinacoteca di Brera. [Brera exhibition record](https://pinacotecabrera.org/en/news/mostra/il-segreto-dei-segreti-i-tarocchi-sola-busca/) [Le Tarot account](https://www.letarot.it/Sola-Busca-Tarot_pag_pg824_eng.aspx)

**What the Two depicts.** The card retains two long batons crossing diagonally. Behind or among them stands a solitary male figure in a landscape, looking outward rather than performing the dramatic burdens or weapon actions found on several other Sola Busca pips. Modern observers have variously described him as forlorn, contemplative, obstructed, or surveying what lies beyond.

**Documented visual fact:** a human figure, landscape, and crossed batons.

**Disputed interpretation:** that the crossed batons deliberately “bar the way,” represent exile, or encode a specific philosophical allegory. No surviving fifteenth-century explanatory text establishes this.

### Was the Sola Busca Two the source of Pamela Colman Smith’s Two?

**Partly documented, partly disputed.**

What is documented:

1. Photographs of the Sola Busca were available at the British Museum by 1907.
2. Smith’s 1909 minors contain unmistakable adaptations of particular Sola Busca compositions, especially cards such as the Three of Swords and Ten of Wands.
3. Both the Sola Busca Two and Smith’s Two place a solitary person in relation to two batons and an outward view.

What is not documented:

- no surviving instruction from Waite says “copy the Sola Busca Two”;
- no letter by Smith identifying that source was located;
- the compositions are substantially different;
- the globe, battlement, sea, fixed ring, rose-cross-lily device, and Alexander all belong to Smith/Waite’s new conception, not to the Sola Busca card.

**Finding.** The Sola Busca is a verified source reservoir for Smith’s minors, but calling its Two a direct source for the Waite-Smith Two overstates the surviving evidence. At most, it is a plausible compositional precedent.

### Mantegna “Tarocchi”

**Documented correction.** The so-called Mantegna Tarocchi are fifteenth-century educational or emblematic engravings arranged in five series of ten. They are not a tarot pack, were not made by Andrea Mantegna, and contain no suit of batons or Two of Wands. They are therefore relevant only to the broader Renaissance emblematic environment.

## Early printed tarot sheets

### Rosenwald sheets, late fifteenth or early sixteenth century

**Documented fact.** The Rosenwald material consists of early printed Italian tarot sheets rather than a complete hand-painted luxury pack.

**Card-specific significance.** Its pip cards represent suit objects schematically. The Two of Batons belongs to the crossed or symmetrically arranged baton tradition, not the scenic Sola Busca tradition.

### Budapest sheets

**Documented fact.** The Budapest sheets are another fragmentary early printed tarot witness. Their precise workshop and dating have been debated.

**Card-specific result.** They support the ordinary gaming-pip tradition: suit signs and decoration, without an independently titled allegorical “Dominion” scene. Modern restored decks made from the sheets necessarily reconstruct missing portions and color.

## Regional game patterns

### Tarocco Bolognese

**Documented fact.** The Bolognese pack is reduced to sixty-two cards by removing numeral cards 2–5 from each ordinary suit.

**Consequent fact.** The Two of Batons does not exist in the standard reduced Tarocco Bolognese game pack. It may appear in modern full artistic adaptations, but not as an active card in the traditional sixty-two-card game.

### Minchiate / Germini

**Documented fact.** Minchiate expanded the trump series to forty while retaining fourteen cards in each suit. Thus the Two of Batons remained present.

**Iconography.** Florentine Minchiate pips normally use an interlaced trellis of straight rods, frequently with floral ornament. The Two is a non-scenic arrangement of two batons.

### Sicilian tarot

**Documented fact.** The Sicilian pattern was altered and shortened in the course of its history. Low suit cards were among those subject to omission in regional forms.

**Card-specific caution.** Depending on the precise period and pack reduction, the Two of Batons may be absent. No stable esoteric iconography attaches to it in traditional Sicilian play.

## Tarot de Marseille lineage

### General form

**Documented fact.** In the Marseille tradition, the Two of Batons is an abstract pip. Two straight batons cross diagonally to make an X, commonly surrounded by leaves, flowers, or small floral terminals. The plant ornament fills and balances the field rather than illustrating a story.

The label *Tarot de Marseille* is retrospective. Card historian Romain Merlin used the expression in 1856; occult and commercial writers later popularized it. The pattern itself is much older and was manufactured outside Marseille as well. [History and terminology](https://www.nybooks.com/articles/1981/05/14/origins-of-tarot/) [Marseille history summary](https://www.letarot.com/imagiers-du-tarot/index/)

### Philippe Vachier, Marseille, 1639

A newly recognized Vachier fragment has been presented as the earliest securely dated Marseille-made witness of the pattern. This complicates older statements that Noblet was necessarily the first surviving example.

### Jean Noblet, Paris, approximately 1650s

**Documented fact.** Noblet worked in Paris; archival evidence places his activity in the period 1659–1664. His is the oldest nearly complete familiar Type-I Marseille-pattern pack.

**Two of Batons.** Two rods cross centrally. Small floral forms occupy the surrounding spaces. There is no figure, globe, castle, title, astrology, or elemental label.

### Jean Dodal, Lyon, approximately 1701–1715

Dodal preserves essentially the same crossed-baton formula. Differences from Noblet concern cutting, curvature, color, and the shapes of leaves and flowers rather than narrative content.

### Pierre Madenié and Type II, Dijon, 1709

Madenié supplies an early dated Type-II witness. Type-II packs regularize details later familiar through Conver.

### Nicolas Conver, Marseille, 1760

**Documented fact.** Conver’s 1760 pack became the most influential source for later commercial “Marseille” reconstructions.

**Two of Batons.** Two red/yellow/blue or otherwise stencil-colored batons cross in an X. Floral sprigs or blossoms rise and fall in the open spaces. The image remains a countable, reversible ornamental pip.

**Later esoteric reading:** the crossing may be read as polarity, union, resistance, or balanced force. Nothing on the original card selects one interpretation.

### Paul Marteau / Grimaud, 1930s onward

Marteau’s *Ancien Tarot de Marseille*, manufactured by Grimaud, systematized a limited color palette and promoted symbolic readings of those colors. The Two remains crossed batons with floral ornament.

**Tradition.** Later readers often treated the vegetation as growth and the crossing as the meeting of two creative forces. This is interpretation, not a documented seventeenth-century program.

### Camoin–Jodorowsky, 1997

**Documented fact.** Philippe Camoin and Alejandro Jodorowsky produced a reconstructed Marseille deck after comparing multiple historical editions. Jodorowsky and Marianne Costa later made it central to *The Way of Tarot*.

**Two of Batons.** It preserves the crossed trellis and restores or intensifies small floral and color details. In Jodorowsky’s system, the Twos are accumulation, preparation, or potential not yet released; Batons concern creative and sexual energy.

**Disputed claim.** Promotional language sometimes suggests recovery of an exact primordial tarot. Since the reconstruction selects and combines details from different surviving editions, it is better described as a modern critical-esoteric synthesis.

## Tarot de Besançon

**Documented fact.** The Besançon pattern replaced the Papess and Pope with Juno and Jupiter, probably emerging around Strasbourg in the early eighteenth century and spreading through eastern France and Switzerland. Those replacements concern trumps II and V, not the Two of Batons.

The Two of Batons ordinarily retained the same abstract crossed-baton structure.

---

## Etteilla’s Grand Etteilla, design of 1788–1789

**Documented fact.** Etteilla reorganized the entire pack into a numbered divinatory sequence. The Two of Batons became **card 34**, paired typologically with the ordinary Two of Diamonds.

The surviving printed card reads:

- upright: **CHAGRIN** — sorrow;
- reversed: **SURPRISE**.

Its lateral titles identify *Le Deux de Baton* and *Le Deux de Carreau*. [Scanned Grand Etteilla card](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/2/2f/Le_grand_Etteilla%2C_ou%2C_l%27art_de_tirer_les_cartes_..._Le_tout_recueilli_..._%28IA_b29321220%29.pdf)

**What is depicted.** Two crossed batons appear over a mountainous landscape. A serpent lies in the foreground in at least one well-preserved edition. A second vignette includes a vehicle or carriage appropriate to reversal or combination readings.

**Importance.** This is the first securely traced printed transformation of the Two from ornamental pip into explicitly labeled divinatory emblem.

**Astrology.** No reliable primary evidence was found assigning this card Mars in Aries in Etteilla’s own original system. That attribution belongs to the Golden Dawn, not Etteilla. Statements that “Etteilla gave the Two of Wands Mars in Aries” appear to be backward projections.

---

## Oswald Wirth, 1889

**Documented correction.** Wirth’s 1889 *Les 22 Arcanes du Tarot Kabbalistique*, made under Stanislas de Guaita’s direction, consisted only of the twenty-two trumps. Wirth did not design an 1889 Two of Batons. [Wirth archive](https://foolmade.art/archive/wirth-tarot)

Modern commercial “Oswald Wirth Tarot” packs with seventy-eight cards contain later-created minors. They should not be cited as Wirth’s 1889 iconography.

## Golden Dawn manuscript designs and reconstructions

### Book T / Liber T design

**Documented esoteric design.** The Golden Dawn description calls for:

- a white radiating angelic hand issuing from clouds;
- the hand grasping two crossed wands;
- flames issuing from the crossing;
- small wands or tablets bearing decanic symbols;
- Aries and Leo signs marking the decan’s bounds or related zodiacal symbolism;
- the angels Hevaiah and Daniel.

The card is Chokmah of Fire and Mars in Aries. [Published Book T text](https://www.100thmonkeypress.com/biblio/acrowley/downloads/equinox1_8/tarot/tarot.pdf)

### Wang’s Golden Dawn Tarot

**Modern reconstruction.** Robert Wang’s deck, illustrated by Israel Regardie’s project collaborator and published in 1977, translates manuscript instructions into colored cards. The Two shows a radiating hand and crossed magical wands, flames, and astrological signs.

It is not a facsimile of a single original Golden Dawn master deck.

### Cicero Golden Dawn Magical Tarot

**Modern reconstruction.** Chic and Sandra Tabatha Cicero produced another systematic visual realization of Golden Dawn documents. Its Two likewise emphasizes crossed magical weapons, flame, Chokmah, and Mars in Aries.

**Interpretive difference.** Wang and Cicero inevitably resolve ambiguities of manuscript color, weapon design, and layout differently. Both are reconstructions of instructions, not the historical source itself.

## Waite–Smith, 1909

### Production

**Documented fact.** Arthur Edward Waite conceived the deck and Pamela Colman Smith drew and painted all seventy-eight cards. William Rider & Son issued it in London late in 1909; *The Pictorial Key to the Tarot* followed in 1910.

### What Smith depicts

A tall, richly dressed figure stands on a battlement or fortified terrace:

- seen mostly from behind or in profile;
- holding a small globe in the right hand;
- grasping one tall wand with the left;
- with a second wand fixed to the masonry by a metal ring;
- overlooking sea, shore, buildings, and distant mountains;
- with a rose, cross, and lily device on the wall;
- wearing a red cap and patterned garment.

Waite describes the figure as a lord surveying “his dominion” and compares his melancholy with Alexander the Great amid worldly greatness. [Waite’s card text](https://sacred-texts.com/tarot/pktwa02.htm)

### What changed

The card moved from:

- two countable crossed batons;
- through Etteilla’s labeled sorrow-landscape;
- and the Golden Dawn’s crossed magical weapons;

to a psychologically suggestive human situation involving possession, command, remoteness, and dissatisfaction.

### Sources and synthesis

**Documented sources:**

- Golden Dawn title and meaning: Dominion, authority, ambition.
- Etteilla/Mathers–Waite divinatory tradition: riches alongside sorrow and physical trouble.
- Earlier pip convention: two staffs remain visibly present.

**Plausible source:** Sola Busca’s use of a solitary figure with two crossed batons and landscape.

**Unproven direct source:** the specific Sola Busca Two. The Waite-Smith composition is not a close copy.

### The “Alexander” question

**Documented authorial gloss.** Waite explicitly invokes Alexander.

**Disputed identification.** The figure is not labeled Alexander on the card, and Smith supplied no known portrait key. Calling the person literally Alexander the Great is therefore stronger than the evidence permits. The comparison explains mood, not necessarily identity.

### Rose, cross, and lily

**Documented visual fact.** The device appears on the wall.

**Esoteric interpretation.** It is generally read as a Rosicrucian or Golden Dawn reference: rose and cross signify the union of opposites or sacrificial spiritual unfolding; lily signifies purification.

**Caution.** Waite merely tells readers to notice it. He does not provide a complete card-specific exegesis.

## BOTA / Paul Foster Case

**Documented fact.** Paul Foster Case’s BOTA tarot, drawn with Jessie Burns Parke, deliberately returned most numeral cards to emblematic, repeated suit-sign designs rather than Smith-style scenes.

The Two contains crossed wands and an esoteric emblem: Case describes a coiled serpent in a triangle as Wisdom and the crossed wands as balanced force.

Case gives:

- first decan of Aries, approximately March 21–30;
- ruler Mars;
- keyword **Dominion**;
- well-dignified: force, enterprise, boldness, resolution, originality;
- ill-dignified: fierceness, shamelessness, ambition, restlessness, turbulence, obstinacy, revenge.

[Case, *The Oracle of the Tarot*](https://benebellwen.com/wp-content/uploads/2013/02/paul-foster-case-oracle-of-the-tarot-1933-source-credit-tarotworks.pdf)

## Thoth, painted 1938–1943; book 1944; full deck 1969

### Production

**Documented fact.** Aleister Crowley devised the Thoth Tarot and Frieda Harris painted it in a project lasting approximately 1938–1943. Crowley’s *Book of Thoth* appeared in 1944. The complete card deck was not commercially issued until 1969, after both collaborators’ deaths. [Publication history](https://thelemapedia.org/index.php/Thoth_Tarot)

### What Harris depicts

The card is titled **DOMINION**. Its central form consists of two Tibetan *dorjes* or thunderbolt-scepters crossed diagonally. Six flames radiate from the center. The background is a fiery geometrical field in reds and oranges, with Martian and Aries symbolism integrated into the composition.

Crowley explains:

- Chokmah in Fire;
- Will in an exalted, object-independent form;
- Mars in Aries as initiating energy;
- the crossed dorjes as celestial power, more destructive than creative in their first action;
- the six rays as the Sun exalted in Aries;
- the geomantic figure Puer.

[Crowley’s Two of Wands commentary](https://crowley-thoth.com/symbolism/wands-02/)

### What changed

Harris discarded both Marseille botany and Smith’s human narrative. The card becomes an almost abstract energy-diagram:

- no castle;
- no globe;
- no landscape;
- no melancholy ruler;
- two ritual thunderbolts instead of wooden rods;
- force shown as symmetrical geometric eruption.

**Meaning.** Dominion is cosmic and magical rather than merely territorial. Creation begins as rupture: Crowley uses the breaking of an ovum before fertilization as his analogy.

## Notable modern redesigns

### Marseille restorations

Modern Noblet, Dodal, Conver, and Rosenwald restorations generally preserve the non-scenic Two. Differences of line and color should not automatically be treated as new symbolism; many are restorers’ solutions to damaged originals.

### RWS-derived decks

The overwhelming modern convention retains:

- a figure at a height;
- two upright staffs;
- a globe, map, or planning device;
- an outward horizon.

Common modern additions — crossroads, passport, map, ships, a literal choice between home and travel — are **modern inventions derived from the RWS picture**, not Waite’s complete published meaning.

### Feminist, queer, and culturally localized decks

Modern Witch, This Might Hurt, Light Seer’s, and numerous independently published decks recast the ruler as a woman, nonbinary figure, traveler, business planner, or creator.

Their dominant keywords — planning, choosing a path, leaving one’s comfort zone — represent a twentieth- and twenty-first-century interpretive narrowing of Waite’s much more contradictory riches/sorrow complex.

# The game

## Original function

**Documented fact.** The Two of Batons began as a gaming card. The standard historical tarot pack had:

- four fourteen-card suits;
- ten numeral cards in each;
- four courts;
- twenty-one trumps;
- the Fool.

The Morgan’s museum record explicitly describes this structure and warns against projecting fortune-telling into the Renaissance deck. [Morgan](https://www.themorgan.org/manuscript/158525)

## Rank

In the “long” suits — swords and batons — ordinary numeral cards generally rank in ascending order:

**King, Queen, Knight, Jack, 10, 9, 8, 7, 6, 5, 4, 3, 2, Ace**

Thus the Two of Batons is normally:

- above the Ace;
- below the Three;
- among the lowest suit cards.

In cups and coins, the numeral order is commonly reversed, with Ace high among the numeral cards. This long-suit/round-suit asymmetry is documented in Minchiate and early French/Italian tarot rules. [Minchiate rules](https://www.pagat.com/tarot/minchiate.html)

## Point value

**Documented game tradition.** The Two is normally a low or empty card, not a counting honor. Its exact accounting value depends on the game:

- in many additive descriptions, it contributes one raw point;
- in traditional packet-counting methods, low cards may effectively cancel deductions and have no independent premium;
- it is never comparable in value to a king, the Fool, or a major counting trump.

It may nevertheless matter tactically as:

- a low card used to avoid taking an unwanted trick;
- a card used to exhaust a suit;
- a potential last-card or sequence component under local rules.

## Tarocchi and Minchiate

- **Full Italian tarocchi:** present, low-ranking baton card.
- **Minchiate:** present; batons rank 10 downward to Ace, making the Two next-to-lowest.
- **Tarocco Bolognese:** absent because 2–5 are removed.
- **Reduced Piedmontese forms:** often absent if the lowest four swords and batons are removed.
- **Sicilian forms:** status varies with reduction.

## French tarot and Central European Tarock

Modern French tarot and most Central European Tarock games use French-suited packs — clubs, hearts, spades, diamonds — rather than Italian batons. Consequently there is no card literally called the Two of Wands.

Depending on the reduced pack:

- a Two of Clubs may exist in a full French-suited tarot pack;
- it is absent from many shortened 54-, 42-, 40-, or 36-card Tarock packs;
- it does not carry the occult title Dominion in game play.

## The Fool

The Fool’s role is germane to tarot games but has no direct effect on the Two’s identity. Historically the Fool is commonly an “excuse” card:

- it may be played instead of following suit;
- it normally does not win the trick;
- it is recovered by its player under prescribed compensation rules.

Treating the Fool as ordinary trump 0 or as the highest trump is a later deck-numbering or game-specific convention, not universal historical practice.

## Trump-order disputes

These concern the twenty-one triumphs, not the Two of Batons. Historians distinguish three broad early Italian orders:

1. **Order A:** associated chiefly with Bologna and Florence.
2. **Order B:** associated chiefly with Ferrara.
3. **Order C:** associated chiefly with Milan and the later Marseille lineage.

Justice, Fortitude/Strength, Temperance, the theological virtues, Emperor/Empress, Pope/Papess, World, and Angel shift relative positions between these orders.

The Waite-Smith switch placing Strength VIII and Justice XI is an occult-system decision; Marseille generally has Justice VIII and Force XI. None of this changes the suit rank of the Two.

# Chronology of attestation

## Approximately 1420: Marziano da Tortona

**Documented fact.** Marziano described a special card pack made for Filippo Maria Visconti, organized around sixteen gods and four suit-like groups of birds.

**Correction.** It is a precursor in the invention of permanent-trump games, not a standard tarot. It does not attest a Two of Batons.

## 1442: Ferrara ledger

**Documented fact.** The Este court account books record payment for *carte da trionfi*. This is one of the earliest explicit documentary attestations of triumph cards.

**Card-specific limitation.** The ledger does not name the Two of Batons.

## Approximately 1442–1451: Brambilla Two

**Documented material evidence.** The surviving Brambilla *Due di Bastoni* supplies the earliest card-specific witness found here.

## Approximately 1450–1480: Visconti-Sforza groups

Multiple luxury pip cards survive, confirming that the standard suit contained ten numeral cards and that the Two was an ornamental gaming pip.

## Approximately 1470–1500: *Sermo de ludo cum aliis*

The sermon survives in the Vanderbilt/Steele manuscript, probably copied around 1490–1500 from an earlier text. It condemns gambling and supplies the oldest known ordered list of the twenty-one tarot trumps. Its order is Ferrarese or Type B. [Vanderbilt manuscript description and text](https://collections.library.vanderbilt.edu/repositories/2/archival_objects/261035)

**Card-specific limitation.** It enumerates the triumphs, not the Two of Batons.

## 1491: Sola Busca

The dated engravings provide the earliest surviving fully illustrated Two of Batons.

## Early sixteenth century: *tarocchi*

The name *tarocchi* replaced or supplemented *trionfi*. The etymology remains uncertain.

## Teofilo Folengo, 1520s

Folengo used tarot trumps and suit material poetically, evidence that the cards were culturally legible beyond the gaming table.

**Card-specific limitation.** No securely isolated Folengo passage naming the Two of Batons was located.

## “Tarocchi appropriati” poems, approximately 1500–1550

Italian poets assigned tarot subjects to persons, especially court women. These poems demonstrate literary appropriation of the pack.

**Correction.** They focus primarily on trumps or broad card associations and do not constitute a systematic divinatory meaning for the Two.

## 1637: Abbé de Marolles rules

The earliest detailed surviving French rules describe a reduced sixty-six-card game. In long suits, low numerals including the Two had already been removed, illustrating how game practice could eliminate this card. [Marolles rules](https://www.pagat.com/tarot/marolles.html)

## Seventeenth–eighteenth centuries: Marseille pips

Noblet, Dodal, Madenié, Conver, and related packs stabilize the crossed-baton-and-flower form.

## 1781: Court de Gébelin and comte de Mellet

Court de Gébelin asserted an ancient Egyptian origin and a Book-of-Thoth identity. Mellet proposed a Hebrew-letter sequence and an alleged Egyptian method of divination.

For the suits, Mellet associated:

- swords with sovereign or military power;
- cups with priesthood;
- coins with commerce;
- batons with agriculture and the countryside.

He treated batons as broadly neutral rather than fiery. [Court de Gébelin’s French text](https://www.tarock.info/gebelin.htm)

## 1783–1789: Etteilla

Etteilla published practical tarot cartomancy and designed a reordered deck. The Two of Batons became no. 34, Chagrin / Surprise.

## 1854–1856: Éliphas Lévi

Lévi integrated the suit and number into Kabbalah:

- batons = Yod;
- the four Twos = Chokmah;
- the suits = the four letters of the Tetragrammaton.

He did not assign the Two of Batons Mars in Aries. [Lévi, *Dogme et Rituel*](https://www.astrumargenteum.org/wp-content/uploads/2015/06/Rituel-et-Dogme-de-la-Haute-Magie-by-Eliphas-Levi-Part_I.pdf)

## 1888: Mathers

Mathers published English divinatory meanings derived substantially from Continental cartomancy. His work precedes the Golden Dawn’s mature Book T system.

## 1889: Papus and Wirth

Papus published *Le Tarot des Bohémiens*, systematically developing Tetragrammaton, numbers, astrology, and cycles. Wirth supplied twenty-two trumps only; there was no Wirth Two.

## Late 1880s–1890s: Golden Dawn Book T

The card becomes the Lord of Dominion, Mars in Aries, Chokmah of Fire.

## 1909–1910: Waite–Smith

The abstract pip becomes the battlement scene. Waite’s published meanings preserve both riches and misery rather than a simple “planning” keyword.

## 1933 onward: Paul Foster Case

Case preserves Dominion, Mars in Aries, Chokmah/Wisdom, crossed wands, and balanced force.

## 1938–1944 / 1969: Thoth

Harris paints the crossed-dorje Dominion; Crowley publishes its interpretation in 1944; the full deck follows in 1969.

## Late twentieth century

Pollack, Greer, Jodorowsky, and others reinterpret the card psychologically. “Planning,” “future vision,” “personal power,” and “leaving the comfort zone” become dominant RWS-derived meanings.

## Sephirah 2: Chokmah

Lévi explicitly groups the four Twos under Chokmah. He writes of the four Twos as the expression of divine Wisdom flowing outward. The Golden Dawn then particularizes this:

- Two = Chokmah;
- Wands = Fire/Atziluth;
- therefore Two of Wands = Chokmah in Fire.

**Esoteric meaning.** Chokmah is undifferentiated force, the Father, Wisdom, or dynamic outpouring. In Wands, it becomes primal fiery Will.

**Historical caution.** The Milanese card was not designed with a Tree-of-Life label. The correspondence first becomes explicit in nineteenth-century occult tarot.

## Yod of the Tetragrammaton

Lévi calls the club/baton the hieroglyph of **Jod/Yod**. Golden Dawn writers identify Wands with Fire and Yod, the first letter of YHWH.

Consequently the card can be written symbolically as:

**Chokmah of Yod**  
or  
**the Two in the world of Fire/Atziluth**.

## Mars in Aries

This is a **Golden Dawn attribution**, not an ancient or Marseille one.

The system assigns the thirty-six decans to the 2–10 cards:

- first decan of Aries — Two of Wands — Mars;
- second decan — Three of Wands — Sun;
- third decan — Four of Wands — Venus.

Mars traditionally rules Aries, making this a planet in its own sign. Hence the unusually forceful title Dominion.

## Hebrew letter and Tree-of-Life path

A common categorical error is to ask for “the Hebrew letter and Tree-of-Life path” of the Two of Wands as if it were a trump.

- Golden Dawn trumps receive individual Hebrew letters and paths.
- Pip cards receive Sephiroth, elemental worlds, and decanic astrology.
- Therefore the Two has **no unique Hebrew letter or inter-sephirotic path** in standard Book T.
- It has Chokmah as its Sephirah and Yod/Fire through its suit.

## Court-card sub-element and zodiacal span

This template item does not apply directly because the Two is not a court card.

For context, Golden Dawn Wands courts are elemental subdivisions of Fire and span zodiacal sectors. Those court attributions cannot be transferred to the Two.

## Tzaddi/Heh and Emperor/Star dispute

Crowley famously proposed “Tzaddi is not the Star,” exchanging the Hebrew-letter treatment of the Emperor and Star within his revised scheme.

**Card-specific result.** This does not change the Two of Wands. Mars in Aries remains its decanic attribution, and Dominion remains its title.

# Divinatory meanings by author

## Etteilla

### Printed card

> “CHAGRIN”  
> “SURPRISE”

Upright is sorrow; reversed is surprise.

A later *Grand Etteilla* manual expands this into:

- a relative obliged to go away;
- danger of a fall during travel;
- shipwreck if traveling by sea, with survival possible near favorable cards;
- small sorrows concerning a fiancé for a young woman;
- jealousy in particular combinations;
- fortunate or adverse change depending on neighboring cards.

[Etteilla-derived manual text](https://www.lepetitsalondanthenea.com/post/grand-etteilla-complet-z-lismon)

**Source-critical note.** The one-word card captions are stronger evidence for Etteilla’s designed system than every detail in later manuals issued under names such as Julia Orsini or Z. Lismon.

## S. L. MacGregor Mathers, 1888

Mathers gives the Two of Sceptres/Wands essentially the paired tradition later repeated by Waite:

- riches;
- fortune;
- magnificence;
- physical suffering;
- disease;
- chagrin;
- sadness or mortification;
- reversed: surprise, astonishment, emotion, trouble, fear.

[Mathers, *The Tarot*](https://sacred-texts.com/tarot/mathers/index.htm)

**Historical importance.** This is not yet the Golden Dawn’s fully developed Dominion formulation. Mathers’s 1888 booklet and Book T must not be collapsed into one moment.

## Golden Dawn, Book T

Book T’s card is powerful but morally and psychologically mixed:

- strength;
- domination;
- harmonious rule and justice;
- boldness and courage;
- fierceness and shamelessness;
- revenge;
- resolution;
- pride and ambition;
- refinement and sagacity;
- restlessness, turbulence, unforgiving obstinacy;
- influence, authority, power, dominion.

The outcome is modified by elemental dignity rather than by a fixed upright/reversed binary.

## A. E. Waite, 1910

Waite’s text is unusually explicit that the alternatives cannot be reconciled simply:

> “riches, fortune, magnificence”

versus physical suffering, disease, chagrin, sadness, and mortification.

For reversal:

> “Surprise, wonder, enchantment, emotion, trouble, fear.”

He reads the picture as a lord overlooking his dominion but suffering Alexander-like dissatisfaction amid greatness. [*Pictorial Key*](https://sacred-texts.com/tarot/pktwa02.htm)

### Modern distortion

The now-common meaning “planning your future” is not Waite’s stated keyword. It is a later visual extrapolation from the globe and distant horizon.

## Aleister Crowley, 1944

Crowley identifies it with pure or exalted Will. Mars in Aries initiates a current of force; the dorje represents celestial, initially destructive power. His Book T inheritance supplies:

- energy;
- authority;
- strength;
- audacity;
- ambition;
- restlessness;
- possible turbulence or destructiveness.

Crowley does not use Waite’s literal upright/reversed lists as his main method. Dignity, surrounding cards, and energetic balance determine constructive or destructive expression.

## Paul Foster Case, 1933

### Well dignified

> “force, enterprise, boldness, resolution, originality”

with some combativeness.

### Ill dignified

Fierceness, shamelessness, ambition, restlessness, turbulence, obstinacy, revenge, enthusiasm, and strong self-interest.

Keyword: **Dominion**. [Case text](https://benebellwen.com/wp-content/uploads/2013/02/paul-foster-case-oracle-of-the-tarot-1933-source-credit-tarotworks.pdf)

## Rachel Pollack

Pollack’s *Seventy-Eight Degrees of Wisdom* appeared in two parts in 1980 and 1983 and helped establish psychological RWS reading. [Pollack bibliography](https://www.rachelpollack.com/tarot/tarot.html)

Her Two of Wands is usually summarized through:

- command of a situation;
- worldly or social power;
- success accompanied by dissatisfaction;
- a person whose achievements have separated them from spontaneous life;
- the world held as possession rather than directly experienced.

The reversed card relaxes or destabilizes that control and may restore wonder, surprise, or direct feeling.

**Source limitation.** A legally accessible primary page containing Pollack’s complete card entry was not located. Exact extended wording circulating online could not be authenticated against a specified edition, so no purported verbatim paragraph is reproduced here.

## Mary K. Greer

Greer’s reversal method rejects a single universal negative meaning. A reversed card may indicate that its energy is:

- blocked or resisted;
- delayed;
- internalized;
- projected;
- excessive;
- deficient;
- misused;
- reconsidered;
- released or overturned.

[Greer reversal-method summary](https://querent.life/reversals)

Applied to the Two of Wands, this may yield:

- authority turned inward;
- blocked initiative;
- domination or overcontrol;
- fear of leaving known territory;
- plans unable to become action;
- surrender of control;
- surprise breaking a rigid worldview.

**Source limitation.** An authenticated open page containing Greer’s exact Two-of-Wands entry was not located. These are a documented application of her reversal taxonomy, not a claimed verbatim quotation.

# Esoteric and symbolic readings

## Alchemical

### First secure appearance

No fifteenth-century source was found describing the Two of Batons alchemically. Alchemical readings enter tarot through nineteenth-century occult synthesis and later Golden Dawn descendants.

### Readings

- crossed wands = interaction of active forces;
- two = polarity required for manifestation;
- Fire doubled = ignition or destructive purification;
- Thoth’s crossed dorjes = shattering preliminary to creation;
- red field = calcination, heat, sulfuric will.

**Status:** esoteric attribution.

**Spreaders:** Lévi’s occult synthesis, Golden Dawn teaching, Crowley, Harris, Case, later Hermetic tarot writers.

## Kabbalistic

### Lévi

The first explicit, influential card-structural formula located is Lévi’s:

- batons = Yod;
- Twos = Chokmah.

### Golden Dawn

The system becomes:

- Chokmah in Atziluth;
- Wisdom expressed as pure fiery force;
- Dominion as primal authority.

### Case

Case makes Wisdom visible through the serpent-in-triangle emblem and balanced crossed wands.

**Status:** nineteenth- and twentieth-century esoteric construction, not medieval origin.

## Astrological

### Golden Dawn first decan system

Mars in Aries produces:

- initiative;
- conquest;
- command;
- courage;
- impatience;
- aggression;
- inability to tolerate opposition.

### Thoth elaboration

Crowley adds:

- Puer, the Mars-like geomantic figure;
- the exalted Sun’s sixfold flame;
- destructive power as the initial creative act.

**Conflict.** Etteilla’s Chagrin/Surprise system is not based on this decanic structure. Modern texts that blend Etteilla’s sorrow with Mars in Aries combine different historical systems.

## Hermetic

Hermetic readings treat the card as Will descending from a supernal source into duality:

- one force becomes knower and known;
- power requires an object or realm;
- “dominion” is the ordering of a field by directed Will;
- imbalance becomes tyranny or compulsive conquest.

The globe in RWS is frequently interpreted as the microcosm held by the magician or ruler. Waite himself speaks more cautiously of worldly greatness and melancholy.

## Christian and Rosicrucian

The RWS wall contains a rose-cross-lily emblem.

Possible readings:

- rose and cross — spiritual life flowering through sacrifice;
- lily — purity;
- two staffs — temporal and spiritual authority;
- globe — worldly sovereignty;
- melancholy — the insufficiency of worldly possession.

**Caution.** Only the visual emblem and Waite’s instruction to notice it are documented. A complete doctrinal reading is reconstructed from Waite’s Christian mysticism and Golden Dawn symbolism.

## Jungian

### Historical emergence

Jung did not publish a card-by-card tarot system assigning an archetype to the Two of Wands. “Jungian Two of Wands” readings are post-Jungian applications developed by tarot writers and therapists.

### Common modern readings

- the battlement = defended ego position;
- the globe = ego fantasy of controlling the whole;
- sea and distant land = unconscious and unrealized life;
- fixed wand = established identity;
- held wand = available agency;
- outward gaze = projection of unlived potential;
- leaving the castle = an individuation threshold;
- reversed = inflation, fear, arrested development, or withdrawal of projection.

**Status:** modern invention informed by Jungian vocabulary, not a teaching of Jung.

## Marseille numerology

In Jodorowsky and Costa’s system:

- 1 is total potential;
- 2 is accumulation, gestation, or preparation;
- Batons carry creative, instinctive, work, and sexual energy.

The crossed Two therefore contains energy without yet discharging it.

**Conflict with Golden Dawn.** Golden Dawn’s Two is already supreme initiating Will. Jodorowsky’s Two is often latent. Both are coherent within their systems but cannot be treated as identical historical meanings.

# People and works

## Artists, printers, and occultists

### Bonifacio Bembo and the Milanese workshops

Attached through attribution to the Brambilla Two and other Visconti-family cards. The association is art-historical, not divinatory.

### Sola Busca engraver and colorist

The engraver remains unidentified with certainty. Nicola di Maestro Antonio has been associated with the coloring or artistic milieu, but precise responsibility remains debated.

### Jean Noblet, Jean Dodal, Pierre Madenié, Nicolas Conver

These cardmakers transmit the crossed-baton floral form. They left no known card-specific interpretive manuals explaining its ornament.

### Etteilla / Jean-Baptiste Alliette

First major professional systematizer of tarot cartomancy; transformed the Two into card 34, Chagrin/Surprise.

### Éliphas Lévi

First highly influential occultist to connect suit and number structurally to Yod and Chokmah.

### S. L. MacGregor Mathers

Published the early English divinatory list in 1888 and helped create the Golden Dawn system that renamed the card Lord of Dominion.

### Pamela Colman Smith

Created the canonical modern scene. Her visual contribution should not be reduced to passive execution: no surviving set of Waite instructions accounts for every compositional choice.

### Arthur Edward Waite

Supplied the interpretive framework and published description. His Alexander analogy is responsible for much later “melancholy ruler” interpretation.

### Lady Frieda Harris

Reconceived the card as projective geometry, using crossed dorjes and radiating force.

### Aleister Crowley

Retained and intensified Book T’s Chokmah/Fire and Mars/Aries formula.

### Paul Foster Case and Jessie Burns Parke

Returned the pip to emblematic abstraction while keeping Golden Dawn correspondences.

### Alejandro Jodorowsky and Marianne Costa

Popularized a Marseille numerological and psychogenealogical reading; Jodorowsky also collaborated with Philippe Camoin on a reconstructed deck.

## Literature, poetry, film, and visual culture

### Italo Calvino, *The Castle of Crossed Destinies*

**Documented fact.** Calvino constructs narratives from tarot cards, using Visconti imagery in one part and Marseille imagery in another.

**Card-specific finding.** No stable, universally agreed identification of a crucial Two-of-Wands episode was verified. Because Calvino’s characters communicate through sequences, individual cards function grammatically and contextually rather than through one fixed dictionary meaning.

### T. S. Eliot, *The Waste Land*

Madame Sosostris names the drowned Phoenician Sailor, Belladonna, the man with three staves, the Wheel, the one-eyed merchant, and a blank card associated with the Hanged Man.

**Correction.** Eliot does not name the Two of Wands. “The man with three staves” points to the Three of Wands. Attempts to attach *The Waste Land* directly to the Two are misidentifications.

### W. B. Yeats

Yeats participated in the Golden Dawn and used occult symbolism extensively.

**Card-specific finding.** No poem or prose passage securely centered on the Two of Wands was located. His institutional connection establishes access to the system, not a specific literary quotation of this card.

### Ingmar Bergman

*The Seventh Seal* uses chess, death, plague, confession, and apocalyptic imagery.

**Correction.** No verified quotation of the Two of Wands was found. Associating the film with the card appears to be later thematic analogy.

### Alejandro Jodorowsky’s films

Jodorowsky’s films use alchemical, Christian, initiatory, and tarot-adjacent imagery. He later became an influential Marseille reader.

**Card-specific finding.** No film shot was verified as a deliberate quotation of the Two of Batons. Similarities involving towers, rulers, globes, or crossed staffs are insufficient evidence.

### Paintings and buildings

The RWS battlement, globe, and ruler resemble longstanding Western motifs of:

- the world-ruler holding an orb;
- the melancholy monarch;
- fortified prospects;
- maps and maritime expansion;
- Alexander surveying conquered territory.

**Open finding.** No single painting or building has been documented by Smith or Waite as the direct architectural source. Claims naming a particular castle or portrait should therefore be treated as speculative unless supported by a newly discovered sketch, letter, or publication.

# Controversies and disputes

## Egyptian origin

### Claim

Court de Gébelin argued in 1781 that tarot preserved an Egyptian sacred book. Etteilla elaborated the story and reorganized his deck as an Egyptian cosmology.

### Evidence for the tradition

- surviving printed occult texts;
- tremendous influence on later tarot;
- internal symbolic ingenuity.

### Evidence against it as history

- no Egyptian tarot cards;
- no Egyptian text describing a tarot pack;
- tarot’s suit and trump structures are documented in Renaissance Italy;
- Court wrote before Egyptian hieroglyphs were deciphered;
- his Egyptian etymologies provide no hieroglyphic evidence.

Dummett emphasizes that Gébelin and Mellet offered supposed Egyptian word derivations without hieroglyphic forms and that no occult tarot evidence precedes their intervention. [Dummett](https://www.nybooks.com/articles/1981/05/14/origins-of-tarot/)

**Verdict:** legend as origin history; documented and enormously consequential as an occult tradition.

## Romani or “Gypsy” origin

### Claim

Tarot was allegedly carried from Egypt or India into Europe by Romani travelers and preserved by “Gypsy” fortune-tellers.

### History of the claim

Gébelin’s Egyptian narrative encouraged the association. Nineteenth-century writers including Vaillant and Papus spread the Romani carrier story. The older English exonym “Gypsy” itself arose from the mistaken belief that Romani people came from Egypt.

### Counterevidence

- Italian tarot records precede the established literary Romani-tarot story;
- early tarot records concern court commissions and games;
- no early Romani source documenting custody or transmission of tarot was located.

**Verdict:** legend and nineteenth-century ethnic stereotype, though Romani diviners later genuinely used cards within European cartomantic culture.

## Cathar origin

### Claim

The trump sequence allegedly hides Cathar theology or survived persecution as a secret heretical catechism.

### Evidence offered

Proponents point to:

- Papess imagery;
- dualistic symbolism;
- the Tower;
- ecclesiastical satire;
- geographical proximity to southern France.

### Problems

- tarot first appears in northern Italian court culture well after the Albigensian Crusade;
- no Cathar manuscript mentions tarot;
- pip cards, including the Two of Batons, supply no specific Cathar testimony;
- the claim usually interprets later Marseille images retrospectively.

**Verdict:** disputed modern legend without direct documentary evidence.

## Templar origin

### Claim

Templars allegedly transmitted Egyptian, Jewish, Islamic, or Grail wisdom through tarot after the order’s suppression.

### Problems

- the Templars were suppressed in the early fourteenth century;
- the first tarot evidence is fifteenth-century;
- no Templar inventory or trial record mentions a tarot-like pack;
- no demonstrable chain links Templar symbolism to the Two of Batons.

**Verdict:** modern esoteric legend.

## Sola Busca as the single source of all RWS minors

**Claim.** Smith copied or directly derived the entire scenic minor arcana from the Sola Busca.

**Evidence for limited influence.**

- British Museum photographs were available;
- several cards show close compositional correspondences;
- Sola Busca offered the only known old model of a fully illustrated pip series.

**Evidence against the maximal claim.**

- many RWS scenes have no close Sola Busca parallel;
- Smith used theatrical, literary, landscape, religious, and contemporary visual sources;
- no surviving document states that Sola Busca supplied the whole project;
- the respective Two cards are only loosely related.

**Verdict:** documented deck-level influence; disputed and overstated as a direct card-by-card source.

## Was Waite or Smith the “real creator”?

Waite commissioned and conceptually directed the deck; Smith made the images. Waite’s book supplies explicit descriptions and meanings, but his surviving text does not explain all the artistic details.

**Best reconstruction:** collaborative authorship with unequal historical credit. Smith deserves authorship of the visual realization; Waite supplied the occult and editorial framework.

## Dominion versus planning

- **Book T:** authority, domination, courage, ambition, turbulence.
- **Waite:** riches and magnificence opposed to sorrow, illness, and mortification.
- **Modern RWS:** planning, decision, travel, future vision.

“Planning” is therefore a modern visual reading, not the oldest or even Waite’s principal word.

## Reversal versus elemental dignity

Etteilla and Waite print explicit reversed meanings. Golden Dawn practice often evaluates a pip as well or ill dignified according to neighboring elemental cards.

Treating “ill-dignified” as exactly synonymous with “upside down” is a modern simplification.

## The number 2

Competing systems produce opposite emphases:

- Lévi/Golden Dawn: Chokmah, dynamic outpouring;
- Case: balanced force;
- Marseille numerology: gestation or accumulation;
- Jungian reading: polarity;
- popular RWS: decision between two paths.

No historical evidence shows that the original Milanese maker selected two batons to encode one of these later theories; it was first of all the second numeral card.

## Misprints and publication variants

### Thoth deck

Early Thoth printings vary in color, cropping, titles, and production quality. Some editions corrected printing errors in earlier versions.

**Card-specific result.** No misprint was found that demonstrably created a major Two-of-Wands doctrine. “Dominion” is intentional, not a printer’s error.

### Wirth minors

A more consequential bibliographic error is the marketing of seventy-eight-card “Wirth” decks as though Wirth designed all the minors in 1889. He did not.

### Marseille dates

Noblet is often casually dated “1650,” Dodal “1701,” and the Marseille pattern said to have begun in Marseille. These are useful shorthand but obscure documentary ranges, non-Marseille production, and the recently identified Vachier fragment.

# Open questions

1. **Exact commissioning date of the Brambilla Two.** The catalogue gives approximately 1442–1451; the precise occasion and original pack structure remain debated.

2. **Workshop attribution.** Bembo-family and related Milanese/Ferrarese workshop assignments continue to shift.

3. **Complete provenance of the Sola Busca imagery.** Its figures, names, classical references, and narrative program remain disputed, and no contemporary explanatory manual survives.

4. **Direct influence of the Sola Busca Two on Smith.** General Sola Busca influence is secure; card-specific dependence is not.

5. **Smith’s architectural source.** No identified battlement, castle, painting, or stage design has been proved to be her model.

6. **The identity of the RWS figure.** Waite compares the scene to Alexander but does not unambiguously label the figure as Alexander.

7. **The rose-cross-lily device.** Its Rosicrucian character is persuasive within Waite’s milieu, but Waite did not publish a full card-specific explanation.

8. **Etteilla’s earliest exact card text.** The card captions are clear, but longer manuals transmitted under later pseudonyms may expand or alter Etteilla’s own formulations.

9. **Pollack and Greer verbatim entries.** Complete legally accessible primary pages for their individual Two-of-Wands discussions were not found in this web investigation; summaries should be checked against specific print editions.

10. **Pre-eighteenth-century divination.** Early Bologna cartomantic material suggests some card divination before Gébelin, but no securely dated medieval or Renaissance source supplies a divinatory meaning for the Two of Batons.

11. **Modern source drift.** “Planning,” “comfort zone,” “choose a path,” and “international travel” are now widespread, but their precise first popularizer remains to be established through systematic comparison of twentieth-century manuals.
