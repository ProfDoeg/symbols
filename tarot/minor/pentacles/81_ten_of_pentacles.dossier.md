# Ten of Pentacles: Research Dossier

## Method and evidentiary labels

This dossier uses the following labels throughout:

- **Documented fact** — supported directly by a surviving card, archival record, museum catalogue, or primary printed text.
- **Scholarly reconstruction** — an inference advanced by historians from surviving objects and documents.
- **Tradition** — a durable interpretation or practice whose historical transmission is demonstrable, but whose propositions are not independently verifiable.
- **Esoteric attribution** — a deliberately constructed occult correspondence.
- **Disputed claim** — a proposition for which specialists or sources disagree.
- **Legend** — a historical narrative unsupported or contradicted by the surviving evidence.
- **Modern invention** — an interpretation or redesign first documented in recent tarot literature or practice.

“Pentacles” is used here when discussing the Golden Dawn, Waite–Smith, BOTA, or derivative decks. For early Italian and French cards, “Coins,” *Denari*, or *Deniers* is historically preferable.

### The earliest example

**Documented fact.** Italy’s national cultural catalogue dates the Brambilla tarot to approximately 1442–1451 and inventories its *Dieci di Denari*. The card has ten stamped or painted gold coins disposed in two vertical columns on an ornamented silver-toned field. The deck is attributed to Bonifacio Bembo and preserved at the Pinacoteca di Brera. The same catalogue says that forty-eight cards survive, including the numerical series of Coins.

**Caution.** “Earliest” cannot be reduced to an uncontested year. The Brambilla and Cary–Yale/Visconti di Modrone groups overlap in proposed dating, and the dates of individual surviving luxury packs remain subjects of attribution and heraldic analysis. The Morgan dates its Visconti–Sforza group more broadly to approximately 1450–1480. It is therefore safest to say that the earliest surviving Ten of Coins belongs to the Milanese luxury-card production of the 1440s or early 1450s, with the Brambilla card a leading candidate.

The Morgan describes its own card succinctly: “Ten coins are surrounded by floreate border.” Its online record identifies Bonifacio Bembo, Milan, and MS M.630.33. [Morgan Library object record](https://www.themorgan.org/collection/tarot-cards/the-ten-of-coins); [Morgan image catalogue](https://ica.themorgan.org/manuscript/page/33/158525); [Italian national catalogue, Brambilla Ten](https://catalogo.beniculturali.it/detail/HistoricOrArtisticProperty/0300097697-34).

## Names and terminology

### The suit

**Documented fact.** The original Italian suit is *denari*: literally money, coins, or denarii. Its emblem is a round coin, often bearing heraldic or floral decoration.

- Italian: *denari*, singular *denaro*.
- French: *deniers*, singular *denier*.
- Spanish: *oros* (“golds”) in Spanish-suited packs; *monedas* is a descriptive modern translation.
- English historical translation: Coins.
- English occult term: Pentacles.
- German: *Münzen* (“coins”); *Scheiben* (“disks”) in Crowley-derived nomenclature.
- Crowley: Disks.

**Esoteric attribution.** “Pentacles” is not the name found on fifteenth-century Italian cards. In ceremonial magic a pentacle is a talismanic disk or seal, not necessarily a pentagram. Golden Dawn usage joined the coin-shaped suit object to ritual pentacles; Waite institutionalized “Pentacles” in the English-language deck tradition. Paul Foster Case explicitly defined the object as a circular wax or metal plate engraved with magical names or sigils in *The Oracle of the Tarot*.

**Important correction.** It is imprecise to say the Golden Dawn “introduced the pentagram” into every coin. A *pentacle* need not contain a five-pointed star. Pamela Colman Smith nevertheless placed a visible pentagram in each golden disk, making the popular visual equation “pentacle = coin with pentagram” exceptionally durable.

### Number

The numeral is ten. Historical cards may have no index, use ten repeated emblems, or bear Roman X/Arabic 10. The number does not make it “Trump X.” The Wheel of Fortune is the tenth numbered trump in many later packs, but has no historical identity with the Ten of Coins.

### Irrelevant trump renamings

The requested examples—Papess/Juno, Pope/Jupiter, Tower/*Maison Dieu*, unnumbered Fool, Justice/Strength exchanges—belong to the trump sequence. They never rename or renumber the Ten of Coins.

For context:

- The Besançon pattern replaced the Papess and Pope with Juno and Jupiter, especially in eighteenth-century eastern France, Switzerland, and German-speaking production.
- *La Maison Dieu* is the conventional Marseille title of the Tower.
- The Fool was historically unnumbered in Italian and Marseille packs; zero is a later indexing convention.
- Waite exchanged the printed positions of Justice and Strength, placing Strength at VIII and Justice at XI.
- Crowley retained the corresponding order but renamed Strength “Lust” and Justice “Adjustment.”

These trump changes did not alter the Ten’s numerical place within Coins.

## Iconography, deck by deck

## Fifteenth-century Milan

### Visconti di Modrone / Cary–Yale, approximately 1440s

**Documented fact.** A Ten of Coins survives in the Cary–Yale group at Yale’s Beinecke Library. It consists of ten gilt coin emblems on an elaborately worked luxury ground. There is no narrative scene and no printed divinatory title.

**Scholarly reconstruction.** The group was probably made for Filippo Maria Visconti or his immediate dynastic circle. Proposed dates and precise commissioning circumstances vary. “Cary–Yale” and “Visconti di Modrone” are modern collection/provenance names, not fifteenth-century deck titles.

**Meaning in its original setting.** The coins were suit and rank signs in a courtly playing pack. Heraldry and actual Milanese coin types may also have advertised patronage and status. There is no contemporary evidence that ten coins represented Malkuth, Mercury, Virgo, family inheritance, or an occult “completion.”

### Brambilla tarot, Milan, approximately 1442–1451

**Documented fact.** Ten gold coin devices form two orderly vertical columns. The dark or silvered ornamental ground and red border give it the character of a costly illuminated object. The Italian catalogue attributes the deck to Bonifacio Bembo and preserves it at Brera.

This may be the earliest surviving Ten of Coins. Its significance is heraldic and ludic before it is divinatory.

### Visconti–Sforza / Pierpont Morgan–Bergamo group, approximately 1450–1480

**Documented fact.** Ten round, gilded coin emblems are set in a symmetrical field of delicate curling foliage. The coins themselves display dynastic monetary imagery; the borders are floral and manuscript-like. The card is not a scene of people using money.

The Morgan identifies a complete original pack as four fourteen-card suits plus twenty-one trumps and the Fool, although only seventy-four cards of this particular group survive. [Morgan manuscript catalogue](https://www.themorgan.org/manuscript/158525).

### Other Milanese fragments

**Documented fact.** Several related late-fifteenth-century fragments include Ten-of-Coins patterns with coin emblems and floral fillers. Attribution to particular workshops and separation into distinct packs are scholarly catalogue problems.

**Absence finding.** No surviving Milanese Ten of Coins carries an original written divinatory gloss. Interpretations such as “legacy,” “family estate,” and “Malkuth” are later.

## Fifteenth-century Ferrara and the Este cards

Luxury cards assigned to Ferrara or the Este cultural sphere survive incompletely.

**Absence finding.** No sufficiently documented surviving Este Ten of Coins was located in the museum catalogues consulted. It would be unsafe to reconstruct its picture from modern “Este tarot” completions. Reproduction decks routinely manufacture missing cards in a historically sympathetic style; those additions are modern inventions, not recovered originals.

### Mantegna “Tarocchi”

**Documented fact.** The so-called Mantegna Tarocchi are two series of fifty educational or emblematic engravings, not a tarot pack and not by Mantegna. They have no four-suit Ten of Coins.

**Relevance.** Their hierarchies of social ranks, virtues, liberal arts, cosmic principles, and celestial spheres are relevant to Renaissance emblem culture and occasionally to interpretations of tarot trumps. They provide no direct iconographic prototype for the Ten of Pentacles.

---

## Rosenwald and Budapest sheets

The Rosenwald and Budapest sheets preserve early printed tarot imagery, primarily important for the trump sequence and regional style.

**Absence finding.** They do not provide a securely identifiable, complete, card-specific Ten of Coins comparable to the Morgan or Brambilla cards in the accessible reproductions consulted. Claims that their damaged ornaments prove a particular occult meaning would exceed the evidence.

Their real importance is to the transition from luxury hand-painted cards to repeatable woodcut production and to the reconstruction of regional trump patterns.

## Sola Busca, engraved about 1490–1491; colored later

### What it depicts

**Documented fact.** Unlike the usual non-scenic Italian numeral cards, all Sola Busca pips are illustrated. The Ten of Coins shows a human figure with a candelabrum or candle-bearing structure and a palm; another conspicuous detail in reproductions is a dog. Ten coin emblems participate in the composition rather than merely filling a geometric field.

The Brera describes the Sola Busca as the oldest surviving complete tarot pack and acquired it for the state collection in 2009. Modern scholarship generally places the engravings around 1490/1491, with hand coloring later, possibly in Venice. [Brera exhibition record](https://pinacotecabrera.org/en/news/mostra/il-segreto-dei-segreti-i-tarocchi-sola-busca/).

### Alchemical interpretation

**Disputed claim.** Modern interpreters have read the Coins sequence as stages of an alchemical *opus* and identified the turbaned Ten figure with Hermes Trismegistus. The palm can be construed as triumph, martyrdom, transformation, or immortality; the candle as fire, illumination, or laboratory process.

**Evidence.** The deck demonstrably uses classical, historical, and pseudo-historical personages and unusually dense emblematic objects. That makes learned allegory likely.

**Limit.** No surviving maker’s commentary identifies the figure as Hermes or supplies an alchemical sequence. Even sympathetic iconographic writers acknowledge that the candle and palm remain difficult to explain. “Hermes Trismegistus” should therefore be labeled a modern scholarly/esoteric proposal, not the card’s documented name.

### Was it the source of Pamela Colman Smith’s Ten?

**Documented fact.** Photographs of the Sola Busca were available at the British Museum by 1908, immediately before Smith drew the Waite deck. Several Waite–Smith pips have striking Sola Busca analogues.

**Scholarly reconstruction.** Smith probably saw or knew the photographs. The case is particularly strong for such cards as the Three of Swords and Ten of Wands/Ten of Swords comparison.

**Finding for this card.** The Waite–Smith Ten of Pentacles is not a close copy of the Sola Busca Ten of Coins. The former is an architectural, intergenerational household scene; the latter is an enigmatic emblematic figure with candle/candelabrum, palm, coins, and dog. The Sola Busca established the important precedent of fully illustrated pips and may have licensed Smith’s general method, but calling its Ten the direct source of her Ten overstates the resemblance.

## Tarot de Marseille lineage

### Jean Noblet, Paris, approximately 1650–1659

**Documented fact.** The *Dix de Deniers* consists of ten rosette-like coins arranged around an axial floral ornament. Colored stems, leaves, or ribbons connect and balance the field. The card is ornamental, not narrative.

The numeral and denomination are communicated primarily through repetition. Some surviving/restored versions print X.

### Jean Dodal, Lyon, approximately 1701–1715

Dodal retains the ten circular deniers and interlaced vegetation. Minor variations occur in the rosettes, colors, leaf shapes, and spatial rhythm.

**Scholarly observation.** Such floral fillers are normal design solutions for open spaces between suit emblems. Reading every leaf as a secret alchemical glyph is a later interpretive practice.

### Nicolas Conver, Marseille, 1760

The Conver Ten places four paired levels of deniers around two axial deniers, with a central cross-shaped flower, toothed leaves, diagonal red petals, and lateral tulip-like terminals. It is one of the clearest canonical Marseille arrangements. A digitized description based on the BnF scan enumerates the pattern in detail. [Conver Ten description and scan reference](https://marselia.app/fr/cards/dix-de-denier).

### Grimaud / Paul Marteau, twentieth century

Grimaud’s *Ancien Tarot de Marseille*, particularly the Paul Marteau edition associated with 1930, regularized line, registration, typography, and palette. The Ten remains a symmetrical floral pip. Modern industrial printing makes its geometry cleaner and more uniform than the irregular hand-stenciled ancestors.

### Camoin–Jodorowsky, 1997

**Modern reconstruction.** Philippe Camoin and Alejandro Jodorowsky assembled details from several historical Marseille witnesses, presenting the result as restoration of a deep symbolic language. The Ten preserves the traditional denier-and-vegetation structure with cleaned colors and deliberate detail.

**Disputed claim.** Their deck is not a facsimile of one recoverable “original Tarot de Marseille.” It is a synthetic modern reconstruction. Jodorowsky and Marianne Costa read the suit as materiality, body, money, work, and concrete realization, and ten as completion that opens into a new cycle.

## Minchiate

**Documented fact.** Minchiate expanded the tarot structure to ninety-seven cards, adding zodiac signs, virtues, elements, and other trumps. Its earliest documentary name was *germini*; evidence appears in the early sixteenth century.

The ordinary suits still contain numeral cards, including the Ten of Coins. Its image is normally a conventional coin pip rather than an individualized occult tableau.

In play, it is a low-value numeral card. The special scoring combinations and valuable trumps, not the Ten of Coins by itself, are central.

---

## Bolognese pattern

Tarocco Bolognese was reduced to sixty-two cards by removing the Twos through Fives from every suit. The Ten of Coins remains.

**Documented fact.** In round suits such as Coins and Cups, traditional tarot ranking is often reversed: King, Queen, Knight, Jack, Ace, Two, and so forth down to Ten. In that arrangement the Ten is the lowest card of its suit.

It is an “empty” or low-value card for counting purposes, though it can contribute to combinations in particular Bolognese rules.

---

## Sicilian pattern

**Documented fact.** The traditional sixty-three-card Sicilian pack includes the Ten through Four of Coins, with an Ace of Coins later added chiefly to bear the tax stamp. The order runs King, Queen, Horse, Maid, 10, 9, 8, 7, 6, 5, 4. Thus the Ten is the highest numeral immediately below the lowest court card.

It carries the ordinary numeral-card value—nominally one point before batch subtraction—not ten points. [Rules and historical pack structure](https://www.pagat.com/tarot/sicilian.html).

## Etteilla’s Grand Etteilla, 1788–1789 and later redactions

### Card identity

In the standard later Grand Etteilla sequence the Ten of Deniers is card 68 and is commonly titled:

- Upright: **La Maison** — The House.
- Reversed: **Jeu de hasard** — Game of Chance.

**Documented tradition.** A nineteenth-century Etteilla-line commentary says:

> “Cette carte représente la maison…”

That is, “This card represents the house.” It adds that the card has little determinate value alone and must be read from neighboring cards. In combinations it may mean treasure in a house, payment of a debt, money received, or winnings at a social gathering.

**Caution.** The surviving Etteilla family contains several editions and recensions. Card numbering, caption placement, and secondary glosses vary. The fully elaborated combination text quoted online is from later Etteilla-line compilations, especially the “Julia Orsini”/Lismon tradition, not demonstrably every word of Etteilla’s 1780s first issue. [Etteilla’s 1785 BnF record](https://catalogue.bnf.fr/ark:/12148/cb39316184x); [digitized Etteilla compilation](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/2/2f/Le_grand_Etteilla%2C_ou%2C_l%27art_de_tirer_les_cartes_..._Le_tout_recueilli_..._%28IA_b29321220%29.pdf).

### Astrology

**Esoteric attribution.** Etteilla’s deck integrates astrology, the four elements, creation mythology, and “Book of Thoth” theory.

**Absence finding.** The exact Golden Dawn assignment Mercury in 20°–30° Virgo does not originate in Etteilla’s surviving Ten-of-Deniers gloss. It belongs to the later Golden Dawn decanic synthesis.

## Oswald Wirth, 1889

Wirth’s original twenty-two-card tarot contains only trumps. There is no Wirth Ten of Pentacles.

**Absence finding.** Any “Wirth Ten of Pentacles” belongs to a later publisher’s completion or to a deck inspired by Wirth. It is not one of Wirth’s 1889 cards.

Wirth’s indirect contribution lies in popularizing a Lévi-derived, French occult interpretation of the trumps and their initiatory symbolism.

## Golden Dawn manuscripts / Book T, late 1880s–1890s

### Draft design

**Documented esoteric attribution.** Book T calls the card **Lord of Wealth**. Its conventional design prescription has:

- a hand issuing from clouds;
- a flowering or rose-bearing branch;
- ten pentacles;
- no unopened buds;
- a symmetrical emblematic layout rather than a household scene.

The lack of buds marks completion: the suit’s material process has reached its terminal numerical state.

### Correspondences

- Malkuth of Earth.
- Mercury in Virgo.
- Third decan of Virgo, 20°–30°.
- Earth suit in Assiah.
- Final Heh.
- Decan angels commonly given as Lauviah and Hahaiah in Golden Dawn derivative tables.
- Principal meaning: material completion, wealth, prosperity, gain.
- Adverse or ill-dignified tendency: sloth, dullness, or degradation attending material satiety.

### Wang and Cicero reconstructions

**Modern reconstructions.**

- Robert Wang’s *The Golden Dawn Tarot* translates manuscript instructions into a complete painted deck. The Ten keeps the hand, rose branch, pentacles, and color-scale logic.
- Sandra Tabatha Cicero’s *Golden Dawn Magical Tarot* likewise renders the prescribed emblem using a more polished late-twentieth-century magical-deck style.

Neither is an extant nineteenth-century Golden Dawn deck. Both are later realizations of manuscript descriptions.

## Waite–Smith, 1909

### Pamela Colman Smith’s picture

**Documented fact.** Under a masonry arch, an elderly white-haired man in a patterned robe sits or rests with two dogs. Beyond him stand a man and woman; a child reaches toward a dog. Buildings, a tower or estate, heraldic devices, and a domestic urban domain fill the background. Ten pentacles are superimposed across the scene.

The pentacles’ placement is commonly seen as approximating the ten Sephiroth of the Tree of Life.

**Esoteric attribution / plausible design reading.** The ten-disk arrangement strongly supports a Tree-of-Life reading, especially given Waite and Smith’s Golden Dawn background. But Waite’s published description does not explicitly say, “these ten pentacles are the Tree of Life.” It should not be presented as an artist’s documented statement.

### Waite’s text

In *The Pictorial Key to the Tarot* (1910), Waite describes:

> “A man and woman beneath an archway which gives entrance to a house and domain.”

He continues with the child, dogs, and “ancient personage.”

His divinatory meanings are:

> “Gain, riches; family matters, archives, extraction, the abode of a family.”

Reversed:

> “Chance, fatality, loss, robbery, games of hazard; sometimes gift, dowry, pension.”

The word “extraction” means descent, ancestry, or lineage—not mining. “Archives” joins wealth to family records and inherited identity. [Waite, *Pictorial Key*, Ten of Pentacles](https://www.sacred-texts.com/tarot/pktpe10.htm); [scan](https://www.integralpsychology.org/uploads/1/5/3/0/15300482/waite_tarot.pdf).

### Sources and changes

- From Marseille: ten visible suit emblems.
- From the Golden Dawn: Pentacles, Earth, completion, “wealth,” and probably the Tree-of-Life arrangement.
- From Etteilla/cartomancy: the house, gaming/chance, and domestic-property meanings have clear antecedents.
- From contemporary illustration: the fully staged intergenerational genre scene.
- From Sola Busca: probably the general precedent for pictorial pips, but not a direct copy of its Ten.

The decisive innovation is that material abundance becomes social continuity: estate, household, child, elder, animals, architecture, genealogy, and memory.

## Paul Foster Case and BOTA

### Deck

The BOTA deck, designed by Paul Foster Case with drawings by Jessie Burns Parke, is principally famous for its trumps and for being supplied in black outline for student coloring. BOTA’s pip treatment follows Case’s Golden Dawn-derived correspondence system.

### Doctrine

**Esoteric attribution.**

- Ten = Malkuth, the Kingdom.
- Pentacles = the material plane and body.
- Assiah = World of Action.
- Final Heh = physical manifestation.
- Mercury in Virgo = organized, intelligent, discriminating management of material conditions.
- “Wealth” includes accumulated skill and practical organization, not money alone.

Case’s ritual definition is especially important: the suit emblem is a magical pentacle, a disk engraved with names and sigils. His design also associates the pentagram with governance of elemental forces and radiant energy.

## Thoth: Crowley and Frieda Harris, 1938–1943; book 1944; deck publication history

The paintings were produced between 1938 and 1943. Crowley’s *Book of Thoth* appeared in 1944. Partial or trial printings preceded the first broadly distributed commercial deck; 1969 is often cited for an early published edition, while the major commercial edition followed in the 1970s. Statements that the deck was simply “published in 1969” require that qualification.

### Title and design

**Documented fact.** The card reads:

**10 — WEALTH — DISKS**

Harris arranges ten gold/yellow disks in the pattern of the Tree of Life over a dark violet, olive, black, and earthy geometric field. The disks bear Mercurial signs, abstract glyphs, and monetary or magical seals. The Malkuth disk is enlarged. Crowley states that the disks have become coins and emphasizes both wealth and the dead end of accumulated matter.

A concise passage from *The Book of Thoth* says:

> “The disks are arranged on the Tree of Life.”

Crowley describes Mercury in Virgo as intelligence applied to material or agricultural affairs. Yet completed wealth is inert unless put to use; the lowest point of material descent becomes the signal for renewal.

### Meaning of the redesign

The Waite–Smith family scene is replaced by a diagram of forces. The wealth is not represented by property-owning people but by the entire material current reaching Malkuth. The enlarged bottom disk makes completion visually heavy. Wealth can stabilize or clog the cycle.

### Tzaddi/Heh question

Crowley’s “Tzaddi is not the Star” exchange concerns the Emperor and Star among the trumps:

- Emperor: reassigned to Tzaddi.
- Star: reassigned to Heh.

It does **not** change the Ten of Disks’ final-Heh status in the elemental Tetragrammaton. These are separate uses of Hebrew letters: one maps letters to paths/trumps; the other maps Yod–Heh–Vav–Heh to the four elements/court hierarchy.

## Notable modern redesigns

### Jodorowsky–Camoin

Retains the Marseille floral pip. The meaning comes from number-and-suit structure rather than a literal scene: ten as maximum realization; Coins as body, finance, work, and material life.

### Thoth derivatives

Decks such as Tabula Mundi, Hermetic Tarot, and other Golden Dawn/Thelemic decks retain “Wealth,” Mercury in Virgo, and the Tree-of-Life geometry. Their novelty lies in the symbols substituted for Harris’s disks.

### RWS derivatives

Most modern scenic decks preserve one or more of:

- multigenerational family;
- ancestral house;
- inheritance;
- dogs;
- arch or gateway;
- ten pentagrams in Tree arrangement.

**Modern invention.** Contemporary readings increasingly expand the card to “chosen family,” community wealth, cultural inheritance, intergenerational trauma, retirement, estate planning, or institutional legacy. These can be useful applications but are not meanings documented in the fifteenth-century card.

## The game

## General function

**Documented fact.** Tarot was created as a trick-taking game. The Ten of Coins originally belonged to the ordinary four-suit pack onto which a permanent trump suit and Fool were added.

It is not a trump. It cannot beat a trump merely by being a Ten.

### Rank

In many traditional tarot games:

- Swords and Batons rank naturally downward from King through Ace.
- Cups and Coins rank in reverse numerical order after the courts: Ace high among numerals, then 2, 3 … 10 low.

Thus the Ten of Coins is frequently the lowest card in its suit.

Exceptions arise in shortened regional packs and modern French-suited games. In Sicilian Tarocchi, for example, the surviving sequence makes the Ten the highest numeral below the Maid.

### Point value

The Ten is normally an “empty” numeral card:

- nominal one point in classic batch-counting descriptions;
- effectively zero after the standard subtraction used in counting groups;
- 0.5 in some modern fractional systems;
- not a five-point honor.

Its practical value lies chiefly in following suit, taking or losing tricks, and sometimes contributing to sequences or combinations.

**Common error.** It is not worth ten card points merely because it bears ten coins.

[John McLeod, tarot counting systems](https://www.pagat.com/tarot/counting.html).

## Trump-order disputes and the Fool

These affect the game surrounding the Ten but not the card’s identity.

### Three principal orders

Michael Dummett reconstructed three broad regional families:

| Type | Region | Salient feature |
|---|---|---|
| A / Southern | Bologna, Florence; later Rome and Sicily | Angel above World; virtues grouped or closely related |
| B / Eastern | Ferrara, probably Venice | World highest; Justice and Angel unusually high |
| C / Western | Milan, later France and Marseille | World above Angel; order close to the Marseille sequence |

**Scholarly reconstruction.** Early trumps lacked printed numbers; players had to learn a local order. Divergence could therefore arise rapidly as the game spread. [Dummett translation and commentary](https://dummettsmondo.blogspot.com/2015/07/chapters-6-and-7-game-and-its-trump.html).

### Justice and Strength

The historical disputes concern the positions of virtues among the trumps. Waite’s printed VIII Strength/XI Justice is an occult redesign, not evidence that early game players ranked those cards that way universally.

### Fool

The Fool normally excuses its player from following suit and is retrieved after the trick, with a compensating card supplied in many games. It is not originally “trump zero.” Its special status and scoring vary regionally.

None of these issues gives the Ten of Coins an occult or trump role in play.

## Chronology of attestation

### Before tarot

- **Fourteenth century — documented context:** Italian-suited playing cards with Swords, Batons, Cups, and Coins circulate in Europe. Coins are therefore older as a suit than tarot.
- **Scholarly reconstruction:** European cards probably entered through Islamic/Mamluk channels, but a direct surviving chain from one particular Mamluk card to this Ten has not been demonstrated.

### Approximately 1420: Marziano da Tortona

Marziano wrote a description for a game devised for Filippo Maria Visconti, with sixteen classical gods and four groups of birds.

**Documented fact.** This is a precursor or close relative in the development of special permanent trump packs.

**Important limit.** It is not the standard seventy-eight-card tarot and does not attest a Ten of Coins in the later form.

### 1442: Ferrara ledger

The Este accounts record purchases of *carte da trionfi*, including a “pair” or pack of triumph cards.

**Documented fact.** This is among the earliest secure archival attestations of the standard tarot family.

**Absence finding.** The ledger names the pack, not the Ten of Coins individually. Since ordinary suits were structurally familiar, archival writers rarely enumerated every pip.

### Approximately 1442–1451: Brambilla Ten

Earliest surviving candidate: ten gilt *denari* in a Milanese court pack.

### 1440s: Cary–Yale Ten

A closely contemporary luxury Ten of Coins survives.

### Approximately 1450–1480: Visconti–Sforza Ten

The Morgan’s MS M.630.33 supplies a securely catalogued and viewable Ten with floral ornament.

### Approximately 1470: *Sermo de ludo cum aliis*

The anonymous sermon condemns the game and gives an early trump list.

**Documented fact.** It is indispensable for reconstructing the subjects and moral reception of the trumps.

**Card-specific limit.** It does not confer a symbolic doctrine on the Ten of Coins.

### Late fifteenth century: Rosenwald/Budapest sheets

Early print culture replaces unique painted cards with repeatable woodcuts.

### Approximately 1490–1491: Sola Busca

First surviving complete tarot and first surviving pack in which every numeral card carries a pictorial scene. Its Ten of Coins is an emblematic figure composition, not a family estate.

### Around 1500 and early sixteenth century: appropriated tarocchi and poems

Poets assigned trumps to courtly persons, ladies, or moral subjects. Boiardo devised a poetic tarot system; later *tarocchi appropriati* linked cards to named individuals.

**Documented fact.** These texts prove an early literary afterlife.

**Card-specific limit.** The surviving famous compositions focus heavily on trumps or construct new suit meanings; they do not establish the modern Ten-of-Pentacles family meaning.

### Teofilo Folengo, early sixteenth century

Folengo’s literary use of tarot imagery is evidence that cards and their names were culturally legible.

**Limit.** It is not a source for Mercury in Virgo or Malkuth.

### Seventeenth–eighteenth centuries

- Noblet, Dodal, and Conver stabilize the floral *Dix de Deniers*.
- The Ten remains a game card.
- No evidence located for an occult title before eighteenth-century cartomancy.

### 1781: Court de Gébelin and Comte de Mellet

Gébelin declares tarot an Egyptian book. Mellet connects twenty-two trumps with Hebrew letters and presents a divinatory theory.

**Card-specific limit.** Their Hebrew-letter mapping is for the trumps, not a letter assignment unique to the Ten of Coins.

### 1783–1785: Etteilla’s writings

Etteilla publishes a systematic tarot cartomancy. The Ten of Deniers becomes the House/Game of Chance in the later stabilized Grand Etteilla tradition.

### 1788–1789: Grand Etteilla deck

First tarot designed and printed expressly around an occult/divinatory system.

### 1854–1856: Éliphas Lévi, *Dogme et rituel de la haute magie*

Lévi consolidates the tarot–Kabbalah–magic synthesis.

He calls tarot:

> “a truly philosophical machine”

in later English translation.

**Card-specific limit.** Lévi concentrates on the twenty-two keys and the universal structure. He does not supply the later Book T title “Lord of Wealth” or Mercury-in-Virgo attribution for this pip.

### 1870s: Paul Christian

Jean-Baptiste Pitois, writing as Paul Christian, elaborates Egyptian initiatory tableaux and helps popularize “arcana” terminology.

Again, this is principally a trump-centered mythos.

### 1888: Mathers

Mathers publishes *The Tarot: Its Occult Signification, Use in Fortune-Telling, and Method of Play*. For the Ten of Pentacles he gives:

- Upright: **The house and household, economy, gain, riches, family matters.**
- Reversed: **Building, foundation, some account or reckoning.**

Wording differs slightly among transcriptions and editions. Mathers’s booklet also repeats an Egyptian-origin position that later historical research rejects. [1888 scan](https://www.howardnema.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/12/the-tarot-1888-by-macgregor-mathers-1.pdf).

### 1888–1890s: Golden Dawn Book T

The title Lord of Wealth and Mercury in Virgo are systematized here.

### 1889: Papus and Wirth

- Papus publishes *Le Tarot des Bohémiens*, spreading Egyptian, Hebrew, numerical, and Tetragrammaton theories.
- Wirth publishes twenty-two redesigned trumps; no Ten of Coins.

Papus’s premise is explicit:

> “this game, as possessed by the Gypsies, is of Egyptian origin”

—a tradition claim, not documented history. [Papus, chapter XX](https://archive.sacred-texts.com/tarot/tob/tob58.htm).

### 1909–1910: Waite–Smith and *Pictorial Key*

The Ten becomes an intergenerational family-domain scene. Waite’s meanings crystallize gain, archives, extraction, household, and inheritance-related themes.

### 1910s–1930s: Paul Foster Case

Case transmits Golden Dawn correspondences through American occult teaching and BOTA.

### 1938–1944: Crowley–Harris

The card is abstracted into ten disks on the Tree of Life and explicitly captioned Wealth.

### 1969 onward: Thoth publication

The design enters mass tarot practice.

### 1970s onward: psychological tarot

Humanistic and Jungian authors reinterpret material inheritance as psychic inheritance, family systems, ancestral pattern, and achieved integration.

### 1997: Camoin–Jodorowsky

A reconstructed Marseille Ten renews number-and-suit reading without adopting Smith’s family scene.

### Number ten and Malkuth

**Esoteric attribution.** In Hermetic Qabalah, ten corresponds to Malkuth, “Kingdom,” the terminal Sephirah. It represents manifestation, concreteness, and the reception of the other nine Sephiroth.

**Tradition.** Because Pentacles represent Earth, the Ten is often called “Malkuth in Malkuth” or Earth at its fullest manifestation. Strict Golden Dawn phrasing is Malkuth in the suit of Earth/Assiah, not literally a second independent Sephirah named Malkuth.

### Final Heh

The four suits are correlated with Yod–Heh–Vav–Heh:

- Yod: Fire/Wands.
- First Heh: Water/Cups.
- Vav: Air/Swords.
- Final Heh: Earth/Pentacles.

Thus the Ten belongs to final Heh at the suit level. It has no unique Hebrew letter in the way a trump is assigned a letter/path.

### Color scales

Golden Dawn-derived renderings combine:

- Malkuth colors: citrine, olive, russet, black; or black rayed yellow in the relevant scale.
- Mercury: yellow, violet, gray, indigo/violet variations.
- Virgo: yellow-green, slate gray, green-gray, plum.

Exact shades and scale labels vary among manuscript transcriptions and reconstruction decks.

## Divinatory meanings by author

## Etteilla tradition

### Upright

**La Maison** — the house, residence, household, or material base.

A later Etteilla compilation explains that the card has little isolated value and should be interpreted by adjacent cards. Combinations can produce treasure in the house, a gift, money received, or settlement of a debt.

### Reversed

**Jeu de hasard** — game of chance, gambling, uncertain gain.

**Transmission note.** The compact paired captions belong to the Grand Etteilla deck tradition. The expanded combination instructions are best attested in nineteenth-century redactions rather than securely as verbatim 1789 Etteilla.

---

## S. L. MacGregor Mathers, 1888

### Upright

House and household; economy; gain; riches; family matters.

### Reversed

Building or foundation; an account or reckoning.

**Historical relation.** Mathers’s household and riches meanings overlap the Etteilla stream and anticipate Waite.

---

## Golden Dawn / Book T

### Well dignified

Completion of material gain and fortune; riches; wealth; successful material accumulation.

### Ill dignified

Material success becoming dull, heavy, slothful, or morally degraded; prosperity without vitality.

Book T generally uses elemental dignity rather than the simpler upright/reversed binary.

---

## A. E. Waite, 1910

### Upright

> “Gain, riches; family matters, archives, extraction, the abode of a family.”

### Reversed

> “Chance, fatality, loss, robbery, games of hazard; sometimes gift, dowry, pension.”

Waite’s reversed “games of hazard” is a particularly visible survival of the Etteilla line.

---

## Aleister Crowley, 1944

Crowley does not organize the Thoth meanings simply as upright/reversed. His core meaning is wealth, riches, material completion, intelligence applied to practical affairs, and the need for accumulated wealth to enter a new cycle.

### Favorable manifestation

Wealth, commercial intelligence, sound administration, completion of material work.

### Adverse manifestation

Inert accumulation; wealth without purpose; fixation at the material limit; eventual dissipation if resources are not used creatively.

---

## Paul Foster Case

### Upright/core

Material prosperity, successful organization, established position, the culmination of physical processes, and the intelligent direction of resources.

### Adverse/reversed

Mismanagement, excessive concern with status or money, unstable foundations, or the obstruction produced by material inertia.

**Caution.** Case’s different instructional works distribute meanings across correspondence tables and exercises; compact internet lists often silently combine them.

---

## Rachel Pollack

In *Seventy-Eight Degrees of Wisdom*, Pollack treats the Waite–Smith scene as established social order, inheritance, family continuity, convention, and permanent-seeming material structure. The old man embodies accumulated history; the arch and estate embody a civilization or family world.

The shadow is conformity, emotional distance, or a structure so settled that genuine contact becomes difficult.

**Modern psychological interpretation.** Pollack’s emphasis is not a fifteenth-century meaning; it is a late-twentieth-century synthesis of Waite’s picture, occult symbolism, and psychological reading.

No long verbatim quotation is reproduced here because the work remains copyrighted.

---

## Mary K. Greer

Greer’s work treats reversals as a family of possible operations rather than fixed opposites: blockage, delay, internalization, excess, absence, or an alternative viewpoint.

Applied to the Ten:

- Upright: family or collective resources, enduring prosperity, inheritance, social structures.
- Reversed: disputes over property or inheritance, family burdens, instability, rejection of family expectations, or an inner reassessment of what “wealth” means.

This is a modern reading method rather than a single canonical sentence assigned by Etteilla or Book T.

## Esoteric and symbolic readings

## Alchemical

### First relevant appearance

The Sola Busca is now frequently interpreted as containing alchemical imagery, especially in its Coins sequence.

**Disputed claim.** The Ten’s palm, candle/candelabrum, unusual clothing, and coins have been read as illumination after material transformation or Hermes presiding over the completed work.

**Evidence for:** learned emblematic imagery; metal/coin production motifs elsewhere in the suit; classical and pseudo-historical subjects.

**Evidence against certainty:** no contemporary key; objects such as palms and candles have many Christian, civic, and triumphal meanings.

### Later spreaders

Twentieth- and twenty-first-century Sola Busca studies, occult publishers, and deck commentators expanded this interpretation. It should not be retroactively called the card’s original “meaning.”

---

## Kabbalistic

### First appearance

The explicit mapping of twenty-two tarot trumps to Hebrew letters begins with Mellet in 1781. Lévi made tarot–Kabbalah structurally central. The Golden Dawn extended a comprehensive system to all seventy-eight cards.

### Ten-specific structure

- Ten = Malkuth.
- Pentacles/Earth = Assiah.
- Ten disks may be laid out as the ten Sephiroth.
- Material wealth is the terminal condensation of higher forces.

Waite–Smith implies this through disk placement; Thoth makes it explicit.

---

## Astrological

### First secure Ten-specific form

**Golden Dawn, late nineteenth century:** Mercury in the third decan of Virgo.

Mercury is both ruler of Virgo in traditional astrology and associated with calculation, commerce, records, language, and skilled administration. In the card this becomes organized wealth, archives, accounts, inheritance documents, cultivation, and practical intelligence.

**Caution.** Claims that Renaissance makers knowingly designed this card as Mercury in Virgo have no documentary support.

---

## Hermetic

The suit’s magical pentacles, final Heh, Assiah, Mercury, and Malkuth combine into the maxim that mind becomes material form. Crowley intensifies the cyclical element: matter at its maximum must be reanimated by spirit.

This is Golden Dawn/Thelemic Hermeticism, not an interpretation recorded in the Ferrara ledger or early game rules.

---

## Christian

### Historical imagery

- Coin imagery naturally evokes wealth, tribute, charity, avarice, and stewardship in Christian Europe.
- The palm may mean victory or martyrdom.
- Family, elder, child, dogs, and walled estate can invite readings of household, lineage, or covenant.

### Evidence boundary

No early maker’s commentary identifies the Ten of Coins with a particular biblical verse. Modern links to the talents, “treasure in heaven,” Abrahamic lineage, or the heavenly Jerusalem are devotional analogies.

---

## Jungian and family-systems readings

### First spread

Jung did not author a canonical Ten-of-Pentacles interpretation. “Jungian tarot” developed through later writers who treated tarot pictures as archetypal projections and tools for individuation.

### Common modern readings

- ancestral inheritance, both beneficial and burdensome;
- family complexes;
- the social persona and inherited role;
- integration of spiritual value into embodied life;
- the “Self” expressed through a durable community;
- tension between belonging and individuation.

These readings spread especially through late-twentieth-century psychological tarot writers, including Pollack and Greer, and through therapeutic or family-systems practice.

## People and works

### Bonifacio Bembo

Traditionally attributed artist or workshop head for the Brambilla and major Visconti groups. His Ten is a luxury game object, not an occult illustration.

### Nicola di maestro Antonio and the Sola Busca workshop

Brera’s research associates the Sola Busca imagery with Nicola di maestro Antonio and a learned humanist program. Attribution and division between engraver, designer, and colorist remain active specialist questions.

### Jean Noblet, Jean Dodal, Nicolas Conver

Cardmakers whose decks preserve the French floral Ten-of-Deniers tradition.

### Jean-Baptiste Alliette / Etteilla

Made the Ten a named cartomantic card—House/Game of Chance—and placed it inside an Egyptian “Book of Thoth” system.

### Éliphas Lévi

Did not originate the Ten’s Mercury-in-Virgo title, but supplied the influential synthesis of tarot, ceremonial magic, and Kabbalah from which later systems grew.

### Papus

Popularized tarot as an Egyptian/Romani book and used number, suit, and Tetragrammaton structures. His 1889 *Tarot des Bohémiens* was a major international transmitter.

### S. L. MacGregor Mathers

Published household/riches meanings in 1888 and helped construct Book T’s comprehensive correspondence system.

### Pamela Colman Smith

Created the definitive modern family-and-estate scene in 1909. Her contribution is substantive authorship, not mere execution of Waite’s instructions.

### Arthur Edward Waite

Published the scene description and the famous gain/family/archives meanings.

### Paul Foster Case and Jessie Burns Parke

Transmitted a purified Golden Dawn-Qabalistic model through BOTA.

### Aleister Crowley and Frieda Harris

Turned the card into an abstract, geometric diagram of material completion. Harris’s disks and color fields are not decorative afterthoughts; they carry the argument.

### Robert Wang and Sandra Tabatha Cicero

Produced influential modern reconstructions of the Golden Dawn’s unpublished or incompletely visualized tarot instructions.

### Alejandro Jodorowsky and Marianne Costa

Read the Marseille Ten through numerical completion, material realization, and generative renewal.

## Literature, film, painting, and architecture

### Italo Calvino, *The Castle of Crossed Destinies*

Calvino used tarot cards to generate intersecting narratives, chiefly through arrangements of recognizable historical deck images.

**Card-specific finding.** No uniquely canonical episode centered on the Ten of Coins was verified in the sources consulted. It is safer to say the work demonstrates tarot’s combinatorial literary power than to assign one of its stories exclusively to this card.

### T. S. Eliot, *The Waste Land*

Eliot names “the man with three staves,” “the Wheel,” “the one-eyed merchant,” and “the drowned Phoenician Sailor,” among others.

**Absence finding.** The Ten of Pentacles is not named. Connecting the “one-eyed merchant” to it is an interpretive speculation, not textual identification.

### W. B. Yeats

Yeats belonged to the Golden Dawn milieu and engaged ceremonial symbolism.

**Absence finding.** No securely sourced poem or prose passage uniquely centered on the Ten of Pentacles was located. His relevance is institutional and symbolic, not card-specific.

### Ingmar Bergman

Bergman’s *The Seventh Seal* uses Death and chess, not a documented Ten-of-Pentacles quotation. Claims that its medieval household imagery quotes this card require evidence not located here.

### Jodorowsky’s films

Jodorowsky’s cinema uses tarot-like initiatory, alchemical, dynastic, and bodily imagery.

**Absence finding.** No shot conclusively documented by Jodorowsky as a quotation of the Ten of Deniers was found. His explicit, traceable treatment is in his tarot deck and writing with Costa.

### Paintings and buildings

The Waite–Smith arch, estate, and heraldic old man have generated many later artistic quotations. But no earlier Renaissance painting or building was securely identified as the direct model for Smith’s complete composition.

Architectural comparisons—to manor gates, medieval town arches, or family compounds—are visual analogies unless supported by Smith’s sketches or correspondence.

## Controversies and disputes

## Egyptian origin

**Legend.** Court de Gébelin announced in 1781 that tarot was a surviving ancient Egyptian book. His opening rhetorical claim imagines “an ancient Egyptian work” saved from the destruction of Egyptian libraries. He supplied speculative etymologies before Egyptian hieroglyphs had been deciphered.

**Spreaders:** Mellet, Etteilla, Lévi, Paul Christian, Papus, Mathers, and numerous twentieth-century occult publishers.

**Evidence:** none connecting the fifteenth-century Italian tarot pack to an ancient Egyptian book.

**Historical correction:** the surviving material and archival record places tarot’s formation in fifteenth-century northern Italy. The Morgan’s exhibition explicitly contrasts the original game with later occult use. [Court de Gébelin’s 1781 text](https://www.tarock.info/gebelin.htm); [Morgan occult-tarot exhibit](https://www.themorgan.org/exhibitions/online/tarot/11).

## “Book of Thoth”

**Tradition/legend.** Mellet and Etteilla transformed the Egyptian claim into a lost sacred book associated with Thoth or Hermes Trismegistus.

Crowley deliberately reused *The Book of Thoth* as the name of his modern tarot treatise. That reuse is a Thelemic literary and esoteric act, not evidence of an excavated Egyptian original.

## Romani or “Gypsy” origin

**Legend.** Papus’s title *Tarot of the Bohemians* helped popularize the belief that Romani people carried tarot from Egypt or India into Europe.

**Evidence against:** documentary tarot evidence in northern Italy predates evidence for a specifically Romani tarot transmission; “Gypsy” itself reflects Europeans’ mistaken association of Romani people with Egypt.

**Nuance:** Romani fortune-tellers later used cards, and their real role in European cartomancy should not be erased. That is different from inventing or importing the tarot pack.

## Cathar origin

**Modern disputed claim/legend.** Some twentieth-century writers interpret Marseille trumps as coded Cathar theology, often locating the secret in southern France.

**Card-specific problem:** the Ten of Coins predates the Marseille pattern in Milanese packs and possesses no distinctly Cathar iconography. Ten coins with floral ornament provide no direct evidence of Cathar doctrine.

## Templar origin

**Modern legend.** Claims that Knights Templar encoded secrets in tarot confront a chronological gap: the order was suppressed in the early fourteenth century, while tarot’s documentary record begins in the fifteenth.

No Templar document, excavated deck, or transmission chain names the Ten of Coins.

## Kabbalistic origin

**Disputed claim.** Renaissance Italy certainly contained Jewish, Christian Kabbalistic, Neoplatonic, and Hermetic currents. Similarities between twenty-two trumps and twenty-two Hebrew letters invited later synthesis.

**Evidence boundary:** the first explicit surviving tarot/Hebrew-letter construction is Mellet’s 1781 essay, not a fifteenth-century cardmaker’s document. The Golden Dawn mapping is historically real as nineteenth-century occultism, not proven as the deck’s medieval blueprint.

## The Sola Busca–Smith claim

**Supported in general, overstated for this card.**

- Smith probably had access to Sola Busca reproductions.
- Several RWS pips closely echo it.
- The Ten of Pentacles does not closely reproduce the Sola Busca Ten of Coins.
- The defensible influence is the pictorial-pip concept, not a direct scene copy.

## “Pentacles always contained pentagrams”

**Modern misconception.** Early Italian *denari* display coins, coats of arms, stamped monetary devices, or floral rosettes. They are not systematically five-pointed stars.

The English occult tradition converted the suit into magical pentacles; Smith made the pentagram conspicuous.

## Tree of Life hidden in the Waite–Smith Ten

**Plausible esoteric attribution, not explicit artist testimony.** The arrangement resembles the Sephiroth and fits the creators’ Golden Dawn background. Crowley’s later Ten explicitly uses the Tree. Nevertheless, Waite’s published paragraph does not identify the layout.

## “Ancient family/inheritance meaning”

**Incorrect as stated.** Household meanings are traceable through Etteilla and Mathers and become pictorially decisive with Smith. There is no located fifteenth-century gloss equating the Ten with ancestry.

## Misprints becoming tradition

Several transmission problems matter:

- “Pentacles” is sometimes wrongly explained as simply “pentagrams.”
- “Extraction” in Waite is sometimes modernized into mining or resource extraction rather than ancestry.
- Thoth publication is often given as a single uncomplicated date, usually 1969; trial, limited, and commercial publication histories are more complex.
- Etteilla deck numbers and captions vary across editions; later combination books are frequently quoted as if they were Etteilla’s untouched 1789 wording.
- Modern reproductions silently complete missing early cards, causing newly painted pips to be mistaken for originals.

No single famous printing error unique to the historical Ten of Coins was securely traced. The larger problem is editorial conflation.

---

## Historians’ corrections

### Michael Dummett

Dummett’s central correction was methodological: tarot must first be studied as a family of games through cards, rules, documents, and regional patterns. He reconstructed the three trump-order traditions and rejected ancient Egyptian origins.

### Ronald Decker, Thierry Depaulis, and Dummett

*A Wicked Pack of Cards* traces occult tarot to eighteenth-century France rather than projecting it backward into the Renaissance. Its thesis is not that modern occult meanings are worthless; it is that they have a documentable modern history.

### Stuart R. Kaplan

Kaplan’s encyclopedic work made large numbers of historical decks available for comparative study. Some datings and attributions in early volumes have been refined by later scholarship, but the documentary contribution remains foundational.

### Robert M. Place

Place distinguishes historical development from occult mythology while also examining how Renaissance Hermetic and emblematic culture can illuminate the images. His work represents a more symbolically sympathetic historical approach without accepting a literal Egyptian transmission.

### Helen Farley

Farley’s cultural history emphasizes tarot’s movement from entertainment to esotericism and the social conditions under which occult claims acquired authority.

### Thierry Depaulis and Le Tarot association

Depaulis and associated French/Italian card historians have refined printer histories, regional patterns, terminology, and archival dating, often showing that “Tarot de Marseille” is a later category covering several lines rather than one pristine archetype.

## Open questions

1. **Which surviving Ten is strictly earliest?**  
   Brambilla and Cary–Yale datings overlap. More precise technical analysis of paper, pigment, heraldry, and coin types may adjust their order.

2. **What exactly does the Sola Busca Ten depict?**  
   Candle/candelabrum, palm, dog, costume, and coins are visible; their integrated program remains disputed. No contemporary explanatory key survives.

3. **Did Smith personally view the Sola Busca photographs?**  
   Access and chronology make it probable, and particular cards support it, but a direct statement from Smith naming the Sola Busca has not been located.

4. **Did Smith consciously arrange the Ten as the Tree of Life?**  
   Highly plausible, but not explicitly stated in Waite’s published description or known Smith commentary.

5. **How much of Waite’s household scene was his instruction and how much Smith’s invention?**  
   Surviving public documentation does not allocate every detail. Her acknowledged freedom on the minor cards argues against treating her as a passive illustrator.

6. **Which Etteilla Ten meanings are genuinely first-edition wording?**  
   House/Game of Chance is stable in the deck tradition; elaborate combination paragraphs often come from later redactions.

7. **What is the precise first manuscript date of the Book T wording?**  
   The system belongs to the Golden Dawn’s late-nineteenth-century corpus, but surviving copies and later publications complicate exact priority among drafts.

8. **Was alchemy intentionally encoded in the Sola Busca Coins sequence?**  
   The hypothesis has substantial iconographic interest but no surviving maker’s declaration.

9. **Are Waite’s ten pentacles a literal Sephirotic diagram or a looser allusion?**  
   The irregularity of the layout permits both readings.

10. **Does the card represent healthy continuity or suffocating convention?**  
    The first is Waite’s overt divinatory direction; the second is a productive modern psychological inference from the same scene.

## Conclusions

The Ten of Pentacles began as the Ten of *Denari*: ten coin signs in a fifteenth-century Italian playing pack. Its earliest surviving examples are luxury Milanese cards—probably Brambilla or Cary–Yale—whose meaning was primarily rank, suit, heraldry, and play.

Its major transformations were chronological and traceable:

1. Floral and heraldic Ten of Coins in fifteenth-century Milan.
2. Fully illustrated but enigmatic Sola Busca Ten around 1490–1491.
3. Symmetrical floral *Dix de Deniers* in the Marseille tradition.
4. Etteilla’s House/Game of Chance in the late eighteenth century.
5. Golden Dawn **Lord of Wealth**, Mercury in Virgo, Malkuth of Earth.
6. Smith’s 1909 multigenerational family and estate.
7. Crowley and Harris’s geometric **Wealth**, with ten disks explicitly arranged on the Tree of Life.
8. Modern readings of inheritance, social structure, ancestry, chosen family, and intergenerational psychology.

The verified answer to the item-specific leads is therefore:

- **Book T title “Wealth”: verified**, more exactly **Lord of Wealth**.
- **Mercury in Virgo: verified**, specifically the third decan, 20°–30° Virgo.
- **Sola Busca as the direct source of Smith’s Ten: not verified and visually unlikely**; general pictorial-pip influence is probable.
- **Marseille floral ornament: verified.**
- **Waite household scene and quoted meanings: verified.**
- **Crowley–Harris geometric Tree-of-Life design: verified.**
- **Etteilla House/Game of Chance: verified in the stabilized tradition**, with caution about later explanatory redactions.
- **Denari/Deniers/Coins: historically verified suit names.**
- **Earth, final Heh, Malkuth, Assiah: Golden Dawn esoteric attributions, not Renaissance meanings.**
- **“Pentacles” and the pentagram: late occult developments, not original Italian terminology.**
- **Game card status: verified; ordinarily a low-value numeral, not a trump or ten-point honor.**
