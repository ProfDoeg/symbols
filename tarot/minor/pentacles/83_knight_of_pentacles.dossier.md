# Knight of Pentacles: Research Dossier

## Method and evidentiary labels

This dossier uses the following labels consistently:

- **[Documented fact]** Directly supported by a surviving card, archival record, printed rulebook, or primary occult text.
- **[Scholarly reconstruction]** A conclusion argued from surviving objects or documents by modern historians.
- **[Tradition]** A convention transmitted in cardmaking, cartomancy, or occult practice without necessarily claiming ancient origin.
- **[Esoteric attribution]** A deliberately constructed symbolic correspondence.
- **[Disputed]** A claim for which scholars or sources disagree.
- **[Legend]** A historical-origin story unsupported by evidence.
- **[Modern invention]** A demonstrably modern redesign or interpretation.
- **[Absence of evidence]** A requested connection that could not be traced to a pertinent primary source.

Two cautions govern the report:

1. “Knight of Pentacles” is principally an English occult-era name. The historical card is a mounted court of **Coins**: Italian *Cavallo/Cavaliere di Denari*, French *Chevalier/Cavalier de Deniers*.
2. General trump questions—Justice versus Strength, Papess/Juno, Pope/Jupiter, Hebrew-letter assignments, Tzaddi/Heh—do not directly determine this unnumbered suit card. They are included only where they clarify the systems in which the Knight was interpreted.

## Basic identification

| Field | Identification |
|---|---|
| Card | Knight of Pentacles |
| Historical type | Mounted court card of the suit of Coins |
| Tarot division | One of the sixteen court cards in the 56-card suited series, later called the Minor Arcana |
| Number | Historically unnumbered. Sometimes treated as the twelfth card of its suit when the order is Ace–10, Page, Knight, Queen, King; this is a cataloguing convention, not a number printed on traditional cards |

| English | Knight of Coins; Knight of Pentacles |
| Spanish | *Caballo de Oros* or *Caballero de Oros*. *Caballo* is the normal Spanish-playing-card rank; *caballero* is frequent in translated occult decks |
| German | *Ritter der Münzen*, *Ritter der Scheiben*; in Tarock play, usually simply *Cavall* or *Reiter*, depending on the regional pack |
| Crowley–Harris | Knight of Disks |
| Golden Dawn manuscript title | “Lord of the Wide and Fertile Land; King of the Spirits of Earth” |
| Golden Dawn rank-name | **King of Pentacles**, with the explanatory or cross-reference name **Knight of Pentacles** |
| Golden Dawn element | Fire of Earth |

| Zodiacal span | 20° Leo through 20° Virgo: last decan of Leo and first two decans of Virgo |
| Earliest surviving example | The male Knight of Coins in the Cary–Yale/Visconti di Modrone pack, Milan, generally dated in the 1440s or mid-fifteenth century |

The Morgan describes the principal Visconti–Sforza group as a Milanese, fifteenth-century pack with four fourteen-card suits, each containing page, knight, queen, and king. It also confirms that the surviving 74-card group is divided among the Morgan, Accademia Carrara, and Colleoni collections. The Knight of Coins is not among those 74 cards; modern editions reconstruct it. [Morgan collection record](https://www.themorgan.org/manuscript/158525), [Morgan facsimile description](https://shop.themorgan.org/collections/gifts/products/619).

### What “Pentacles” means here

**[Documented fact]** Medieval and early-modern Italian and French cards display coins or ornamental roundels, not necessarily five-pointed stars.

**[Tradition]** French occultists increasingly called the suit *deniers*, *monnaies*, or *pentacles*. Éliphas Lévi helped establish *pentacles* as an English occult vocabulary through Waite’s translations, although Lévi did not design a complete illustrated minor-arcana Knight comparable to Waite’s.

**[Esoteric attribution]** Golden Dawn manuscripts prescribe a magical pentacle rather than an ordinary monetary coin. Waite retained the word and generally placed a five-pointed star in the disk. Crowley changed the suit-name to **Disks**, emphasizing rotating, living matter instead of currency.

The ordinary Italian *denaro* and French *denier* mean a coin or unit of money. The occult “pentacle” is therefore a reinterpretation, not the medieval name or necessarily the medieval image.

# Iconography deck by deck

## Mamluk antecedents, fourteenth–fifteenth centuries

**[Documented fact]** The surviving Mamluk pack associated with the Topkapı collection uses four suits: cups, coins, swords, and polo sticks. Each suit had ten pip cards and three text-designated court ranks, conventionally translated as king, deputy king, and second deputy. The cards do not portray mounted European knights. [Mamluk-card survey](https://www.wopc.co.uk/egypt/mamluk-playing-cards).

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** Card historians regard the Mamluk system as a major ancestor of early Latin-suited European cards. The shared suits and three masculine ranks make transmission more economical than independent invention.

**[Disputed]** It cannot be demonstrated that the European knight corresponds one-to-one with a particular Mamluk deputy. European makers transformed a non-figural title into a visible social hierarchy—king, mounted man, foot servant—and subsequently inserted queens into tarot.

**[Absence of evidence]** There is no surviving Mamluk “Knight of Pentacles” in the occult sense, no pentagram, and no Fire-of-Earth attribution.

## Cary–Yale / Visconti di Modrone, Milan, c. 1440s–mid-fifteenth century

**[Documented fact]** This is the earliest securely surviving Knight of Coins presently identifiable. The card is untitled and unnumbered. It shows a richly dressed or armored male horseman carrying or displaying the suit emblem. The painted ground is gilded and decorated in the luxurious manner of a ducal commission. The image is preserved at Yale’s Beinecke Library; a reproduction identifying it as *Cavaliere di monete* is available through [Wikimedia’s Beinecke-derived record](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Cary-Yale_Tarot_deck_-_Knight_of_Coins.jpg).

The pack is exceptional because its suited series included:

- King and queen
- Male mounted court and female mounted court
- Male page and female maid/page

Thus Coins once had both a male and a female rider.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** Heraldry associates the pack with the Visconti–Sforza milieu, but its precise commission and date remain debated. Dates near the marriage of Filippo Maria Visconti’s daughter Bianca Maria to Francesco Sforza have been proposed; other scholars place it later.

**[Disputed identification]** Older catalogues and popular books sometimes assign individual court figures to named Visconti or Sforza family members. Gertrude Moakley and Stuart Kaplan explored dynastic associations, dress, heraldry, and possible portraiture. The Knight of Coins has no universally accepted personal identification. Heraldic belonging is much better supported than literal portrait identity.

**What changed later:** the female parallel courts vanished from the standard 78-card form, leaving four ranks per suit.

## Brambilla–Brera pack, Milan, c. 1440s

**[Documented fact]** The Brambilla pack survives only in part. Its painted cards employ Visconti emblems and gilt grounds and belong to the same broad Milanese luxury-card culture.

**[Absence of evidence]** No securely identified surviving Knight of Coins from this pack was located in the consulted Brera and catalogue materials. It should not be illustrated by substituting the Cary–Yale card.

## Pierpont Morgan–Bergamo Visconti–Sforza, Milan, c. 1450s

**[Documented fact]** Seventy-four cards survive across the Morgan, Accademia Carrara, and Colleoni holdings. The original Knight of Coins is missing. The Morgan explicitly lists the Knight of Coins among the four reconstructed cards in its commercial facsimile. [Morgan](https://shop.themorgan.org/collections/gifts/products/619).

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** A complete pack almost certainly contained it because the other suits and courts establish a regular fourteen-card structure.

**[Modern invention]** Modern facsimile versions supply a horseman holding a coin in a Bembo-like style. That image must not be described as an original fifteenth-century Visconti–Sforza card.

**[Disputed identification]** Moakley associated suit emblems and figures with Visconti–Sforza dynastic themes and carnival pageantry. Neither Moakley nor Kaplan can identify the missing Knight from direct visual evidence.

## Ferrarese and Este cards, later fifteenth century

**[Documented fact]** Ferrara was an early center of *trionfi* production; court accounts record painted packs. Surviving Este-related cards show a distinct, vigorous Ferrarese style.

**[Absence of evidence]** A continuous, securely attributed Este Knight of Coins lineage comparable to the Cary–Yale card could not be reconstructed from the consulted museum records. Loose attributions in dealer and popular literature should be treated cautiously.

## Rosenwald sheets, late fifteenth or early sixteenth century

The printed Rosenwald sheets preserve an early popular rather than ducal form.

**[Documented fact]** Their mounted suit figures are represented unusually as centaur-like creatures. This motif later persisted selectively in minchiate. The evidence is important because it shows that *cavallo* could be interpreted literally and fantastically rather than as a fixed naturalistic portrait.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** Dating and regional attribution have varied, commonly Florence or central Italy around the end of the fifteenth century.

## Budapest sheet

**[Documented fact]** The so-called Budapest sheet is an early woodcut survival associated with the broader north-Italian tarot tradition.

**[Absence of evidence]** The fragmentary sheet does not provide a safe, complete Knight-of-Coins sequence from which a detailed iconographic genealogy can be drawn. Claims that it proves a specific Waite or Marseille symbol are speculative.

## Sola Busca, Ferrara/Venice, 1491

**[Documented fact]** The Sola Busca is the earliest substantially complete tarot whose pip cards are fully figured scenes. Its cards are usually titled with classical or pseudo-classical personal names rather than the later generic Marseille court titles.

**[Important limitation]** It influenced Pamela Colman Smith most visibly in several numbered cards—especially the Three of Swords and Ten of Wands—but a direct copying relationship between a Sola Busca court and Waite’s Knight of Pentacles has not been demonstrated.

**[Absence of evidence]** Calling a Sola Busca figure “the Knight of Pentacles” without identifying its original inscribed name collapses two different naming systems.

## “Mantegna Tarocchi,” c. 1460s

**[Documented fact]** These are fifty didactic engravings arranged into social, artistic, cosmic, and theological series.

**[Scholarly consensus]** They are not a tarot pack and contain neither four suits nor a Knight of Coins. “Tarocchi” is a later conventional misnomer.

## Minchiate, Florence, sixteenth–nineteenth centuries

**[Documented fact]** Minchiate expanded the trump series while retaining four Latin suits. Coins and Cups commonly replaced male foot pages with female *fantine*; mounted courts remained.

A striking Coins rider evolved in some patterns into a hybrid creature: the Knight of Coins could have the body of a winged dragon or other beast rather than an ordinary horse. Earlier Rosenwald centaurs are a plausible part of this experimental lineage. [Italian minchiate overview](https://it.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minchiate).

**Meaning of the change:** no contemporary document says that the dragon signified occult Earth, alchemy, or demonic money. Those are later readings. In the historical pack it is first an inherited pattern feature.

## Bolognese tarocchino

**[Documented fact]** The shortened Bolognese pack removes low pips but retains the courts: *Re, Regina, Cavallo, Fante*. The Coins knight remains below Queen and above Page in trick-taking rank.

**[Absence of evidence]** It did not acquire a Golden-Dawn-style pentagram, element, or personality meaning in traditional play.

## Sicilian tarot

**[Documented fact]** In the surviving Sicilian game the court hierarchy is King, Queen, Horse, Maid: *Re, Regina, Cavallo, Donna*. Coins is one of the suits. [Sicilian rules](https://www.pagat.com/tarot/sicilian.html).

The survival of *Cavallo*—literally “horse”—shows that a card’s regional name need not correspond exactly to English noble titles.

## Tarot de Marseille lineage

### Jean Noblet, Paris, c. 1650

The card is a mounted Coins court titled in the Type-I form **CHEVALIER DE DENIERS** or its abbreviated historical spelling. The rider and horse are shown in profile. A large coin is carried or appears immediately before the rider; the image is heraldic and emblematic, not a narrative scene.

### Jean Dodal, Lyon, c. 1701–1715

Dodal’s mounted court belongs to the same family but uses the later *Cavallier/Cavalier* terminology. The horse advances laterally; the rider holds or follows the prominent denier. Line, costume, saddle, and floral ground differ from Noblet, but the compositional type is stable.

### Nicolas Conver, Marseille, 1760/1761

Conver regularized what became the most reproduced Type-II design. The rider, horse, and hovering or displayed coin form a compact profile composition. The card remains unnumbered.

### Grimaud/Paul Marteau, 1930

**[Modern standardization]** Marteau’s *Ancien Tarot de Marseille*, published by Grimaud, redrew and recolored the Conver family for industrial printing. It became for many twentieth-century users “the” Marseille tarot, although it was not a photographic restoration.

### Camoin–Jodorowsky, 1997

**[Modern reconstruction and disputed claim]** Philippe Camoin and Alejandro Jodorowsky combined evidence from several old packs while also redrawing lines and adding or emphasizing details. Their Knight retains the Marseille horseman and coin.

They presented the result as a restoration of an underlying authentic system. Historians generally treat it as a modern synthetic reconstruction, not a recovered single original block set.

Jodorowsky’s later reading sees the Knight of Pentacles as completing the courts’ cycle. In *The Way of Tarot*, the card speaks of the Page’s and King’s two pentacles becoming “a single star floating in space.” This is a twenty-first-century interpretive voice, not a surviving Marseille caption. [Text scan](https://irp-cdn.multiscreensite.com/24669b51/files/uploaded/The_Way_of_Tarot%20%281%29.pdf).

## Besançon pattern

Besançon decks are chiefly distinguished from Marseille in certain trumps: the Papess and Pope become Juno and Jupiter, partly to avoid confessional offense.

**[Documented fact]** This renaming did not transform the Knight of Coins; it remained a mounted Coins court.

**[Relevance warning]** Juno/Jupiter, Maison Dieu, and the unnumbered Fool belong to the trump history and supply no Knight-specific correspondence.

## Etteilla’s Grand Etteilla, Paris, 1788–1789

**[Documented fact]** Etteilla produced the first tarot specifically designed as an occult and cartomantic instrument rather than a gaming pack. Its cards carried numbers and upright/reversed keywords, and the entire order and imagery were rearranged as a supposed Egyptian *Book of Thoth*. [Historical overview](https://hermetism.org/encyclopedia/tarot-cartomancy/).

The ordinary Coins court becomes part of Etteilla’s systematically numbered divinatory series and is usually called *Cavalier de Deniers* in later editions.

**[Textual difficulty]** The Grand Etteilla exists in multiple states, re-engravings, and keyword traditions. Online lists frequently mix the 1780s publications, later Etteilla schools, and nineteenth-century decks. I could not verify a stable 1789 card-number and exact paired keywords for the Knight against a fully photographed first-state pack. They should therefore not be asserted from modern charts alone.

## Oswald Wirth, 1889

**[Documented fact]** Wirth’s original deck comprised twenty-two trumps. It has no Knight of Pentacles.

**[Frequent error]** Tables assigning a Wirth Knight of Pentacles usually import later Golden Dawn or Marseille court material into Wirth’s name.

## Golden Dawn *Book T*, c. 1888–1900; publication 1912 and later

The Golden Dawn manuscript supplies the decisive occult transformation:

> “The Lord of the Wide and Fertile Land; the King of the Spirits of Earth.”

It calls the court **King of Pentacles**, then cross-labels or equates it with the **Knight of Pentacles**. It depicts:

- A dark, winged warrior
- Winged and crowned helmet
- A light-brown horse
- Stag or antelope crest
- Fertile ground and ripened corn
- A hexagram-topped sceptre
- A magical pentacle

It assigns:

- Fire of Earth
- King of Gnomes
- 20° Leo to 20° Virgo

[Book T transcription/PDF](https://jantetteroo.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/02/Mathers_and_felkin_golden_dawn_book_t_th.pdf).

### Wang reconstruction

Robert Wang’s *Golden Dawn Tarot* reconstructs Golden Dawn temple manuscripts under Israel Regardie’s supervision. It shows the winged mounted warrior, agricultural Earth landscape, sceptre, and pentacle. It is a reconstruction of manuscript instructions, not a facsimile of one canonical Golden Dawn pack.

### Cicero reconstruction

Chic and Sandra Tabatha Cicero’s *Golden Dawn Magical Tarot* similarly realizes the written color scales and magical implements. Differences from Wang are artistic decisions within the same instructional framework.

## Waite–Smith, London, 1909

Pamela Colman Smith’s card shows:

- A knight in dark armor
- A black or very dark, heavy horse
- Horse and rider completely stationary
- A ploughed or furrowed agricultural landscape
- The knight holding a golden pentacle level before him
- Yellow sky and restrained composition

Waite’s own description is:

> “He rides a slow, enduring, heavy horse.”

He adds that the knight displays his symbol but does not look into it. [*Pictorial Key*, card text](https://www.mr-kaplan.com/Library/Waite/The%20Pictorial%20Key%20to%20the%20Tarot.pdf), p. 69 in the linked PDF.

### Sources and changes

**[Documented dependence]** The stationary agricultural figure strongly echoes the Golden Dawn description of fertile land, patience, material labor, and a mounted Earth court.

**[Tradition]** The rider also continues the Marseille *Cavalier de Deniers*.

**[Modern invention]** Smith makes the card psychologically legible through arrested motion: the other Waite Knights move, but this one pauses with the weight of horse, armor, soil, and coin.

**[Disputed]** Specific visual borrowing from a named painting or theatrical costume has not been established. Smith’s experience as a theatrical designer and illustrator is relevant context but not proof of a particular model.

### The rank-confusion problem

This is one of the card’s most persistent technical confusions.

The Golden Dawn hierarchy is:

| Golden Dawn manuscript | Crowley | Elemental rank |
|---|---|---|
| King, mounted | Knight | Fire |
| Queen, enthroned | Queen | Water |
| Prince, chariot | Prince | Air |
| Princess, standing | Princess | Earth |

In ordinary Waite–Smith naming:

| Waite rank | Image |
|---|---|
| King | Enthroned male |
| Queen | Enthroned female |
| Knight | Mounted male |
| Page | Standing youth |

Therefore:

- **Waite’s Knight of Pentacles is iconographically and elementally closest to the Golden Dawn mounted King / Crowley’s Knight: Fire of Earth.**
- **Waite’s King of Pentacles is structurally closer to the Golden Dawn Prince: Air of Earth.**

Calling Waite’s Knight the Golden Dawn **Prince** reverses this relationship. The confusion arises because readers align the ordinary rank-names—King with King, Knight with Prince—instead of the images and elemental hierarchy.

## Paul Foster Case and BOTA

**[Documented fact]** Case preserved Pentacles and much Golden Dawn correspondence while rejecting or altering some of Waite’s “blinds.” The BOTA deck was designed for students to color according to prescribed scales.

**[Deck variation]** Published BOTA editions differ in how fully the minors are pictured. BOTA’s own current description includes all 78 cards, while reference descriptions often characterize the minors as suit-symbol rather than Waite-style narrative scenes. [BOTA deck](https://storebota.org/products/major-and-minor-tarot-keys).

**[Esoteric attribution]** In Case’s system the fiery Earth court retains the Golden Dawn zodiacal territory. Case’s *Oracle of the Tarot* treats Coins/Pentacles as corresponding to material manifestation and practical affairs. [1933 text](https://benebellwen.com/wp-content/uploads/2013/02/paul-foster-case-oracle-of-the-tarot-1933-source-credit-tarotworks.pdf).

**[Textual caution]** Case’s publications and BOTA lessons do not always use the same rank-conversion terminology. Any quotation must identify whether “Knight” means the ordinary Waite rank or the Golden Dawn mounted King.

## Crowley–Harris Thoth, painted 1938–1943; first full publication 1969

The card is renamed **Knight of Disks**.

Frieda Harris shows a heavily armored rider on a sturdy horse in a mature agricultural landscape. The figure bears:

- A short flail or agricultural weapon
- A large, extremely solid disk used almost like a shield
- Grain or cultivated vegetation
- Dark, weighty armor
- A grounded rather than charging horse

Crowley writes:

> “The Knight of Disks represents the fiery part of Earth.”

He connects it to mountains, earthquakes, gravitation, agriculture, and the life-producing activity of Earth. Its fire is not a wildfire but the smoldering heat of germination and growth. The disk signifies nutrition. [1944 *Book of Thoth* scan](https://www.rahoorkhuit.net/library/crowley/equinox/volume3/vol_3_no_05.pdf); [transcription](https://book-of-thoth.net/Knight-of-Disks).

Crowley gives the span as the twenty-first degree of Leo through the twentieth degree of Virgo—equivalent to 20°00′ Leo through 20°00′ Virgo in modern notation.

### Rank equivalence

Crowley’s Knight is not a renaming of Waite’s ordinary King. It is Crowley’s name for the Golden Dawn’s mounted fiery court, which Golden Dawn manuscripts called the King. Thus it corresponds most directly to Waite’s mounted Knight.

## Notable modern redesigns

### Rachel Pollack’s Shining Tribe Tarot, 1990s

Pollack replaces Pentacles with **Stones**, using deliberately archaizing rock-art forms. This shifts materiality away from commerce toward body, land, ancestry, and embodiment. The Morgan notes that Pollack renamed the suits Trees, Rivers, Birds, and Stones. [Morgan](https://www.themorgan.org/exhibitions/online/tarot/23).

### Robert M. Place, Alchemical Tarot

Place depicts an armored Knight of Coins in a fertile landscape with a coin-shield bearing a protective pentagram. This is explicitly alchemical and modern: the knight mediates matter, cultivation, protection, and the physical stage of the work. [Place’s deck text](https://robertmplacetarot.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/Alchemical-Tarot-6th-Ed.pdf).

### Feminist, queer, and decolonial decks

**[Modern invention]** Many recent decks replace Knight with Seeker, Explorer, Son, Daughter, Warrior, or an ungendered court. Others portray women, nonbinary people, farmers, laborers, animals, or machines.

These changes respond to the historically aristocratic, masculine rank and to the difficulty of reading courts solely as white European personality types. They are reinterpretations, not recoveries of a secret original—although the Cary–Yale female mounted courts demonstrate that gender variation itself existed in fifteenth-century tarot.

# The game

## Rank

In traditional 78-card tarot games the Knight is an ordinary suit card, not a trump. The usual high-to-low order is:

**King – Queen – Knight – Jack/Page – pip cards.**

In many Italian traditions:

- Swords and Batons descend 10 to Ace.
- Cups and Coins reverse the pip order, with Ace above 2 and higher numerals progressively lower.
- The courts remain above all pips.

Modern French tarot uses King, Queen, Knight, Jack, 10 down to Ace in every suit. [French tarot rules](https://www.pagat.com/tarot/frtarot.html).

## Point value

Under the standard modern French paired-counting convention:

- King: 4½ nominal points
- Queen: 3½
- Knight: 2½
- Jack: 1½
- Ordinary card: ½

When counted in pairs and subtracting one point per pair, a Knight plus a low card yields **2 points**. Some rule summaries call the Knight “3 points” by using a different gross or rounded convention. These are accounting systems, not contradictory card identities. [Counting systems](https://www.pagat.com/tarot/counting.html).

Tarock and regional Italian games vary. In many point-counting systems the Knight/Cavall ranks below Queen and above Jack; its adjusted value may be two points. In some combinations or meld systems, court sets earn additional bonuses.

## Minchiate

Minchiate retains King, Queen, Knight, and page/maid. The Coins Knight has the same suit-taking function as other mounted courts; its fantastic body does not make it a trump.

## Bolognese and Sicilian play

- Bolognese: *Cavallo* remains a figure card, though low pips are stripped from the compact pack.
- Sicilian: *Cavallo* ranks below *Regina* and above *Donna*.

## The Fool and trump orders

These issues affect the game around the Knight, not the Knight’s intrinsic identity.

**[Documented fact]** The Fool is normally outside the numbered trump sequence. In many games it excuses its player from following suit and is recovered after the trick, subject to compensation rules.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** Early Italian tarot developed at least three principal trump-order families—commonly labeled A, B, and C—associated broadly with Bologna/Ferrara, Venice, and Milan. Local orders differed before numbers were printed on trumps.

Justice and Strength consequently appear in different positions. These changes do not move the Knight, which remains inside Coins.

# Chronology of attestation

## c. 1420: Marziano da Tortona

Marziano’s text for Filippo Maria Visconti describes a special pack of sixteen classical gods divided among four thematic suits.

**[Documented fact]** It is an important precursor to *trionfi*.

**[Absence of evidence]** It is not the standard 78-card tarot and does not name a Knight of Coins.

## 1440–1442: Florence and Ferrara records

**[Documented fact]** The Florentine record involving Giusto Giusti and the 1442 Este court ledgers use *naibi a trionfi* or *carte da trionfi* for special packs.

**[Absence of evidence]** The 1442 ledger does not list individual courts. It proves production of triumph packs, not the written name *Cavallo di Denari*.

## c. 1440s: Cary–Yale Knight of Coins

**[Documented fact]** Earliest surviving physical image securely identifiable as this card.

## c. 1450s: Visconti–Sforza pack

**[Documented fact]** A four-court Coins suit is reconstructable, but the specific Knight is lost.

## c. 1470–1500: Sermon and literary evidence

The anonymous *Sermo perutilis de ludo cum aliis* or “Steele Sermon” condemns gaming and gives an early ordered list of twenty-one trumps plus the Fool.

**[Relevance limitation]** Its famous list concerns trumps, not the individual Coins court.

Poems associated with Matteo Maria Boiardo and later *tarocchi appropriati* assign people or moral themes to cards. Teofilo Folengo’s *Chaos del Triperuno* demonstrates literary tarot use in the early sixteenth century.

**[Absence of evidence]** None establishes the modern patient-workman meaning of the Knight of Pentacles.

## Fifteenth–sixteenth centuries: from *trionfi* to *tarocchi*

**[Documented fact]** *Trionfi* was the earlier name for the special trumped game. *Tarocchi* appears around the beginning of the sixteenth century, perhaps because *trionfi* was becoming a general term for other trump games.

The etymology of *tarocco* remains unresolved.

## Seventeenth century: French tarot manufacture

Noblet and related French packs title the card *Chevalier de Deniers*. Later Type-II packs prefer *Cavallier/Cavalier*.

## 1781: Court de Gébelin and Comte de Mellet

**[Documented fact]** Court de Gébelin declared tarot an Egyptian book surviving in card form. De Mellet related the twenty-two trumps to Hebrew letters.

**[Absence of Knight-specific scheme]** Their principal Hebrew-letter constructions concern the trumps, not the Knight of Coins. Neither supplied Fire of Earth or 20° Leo–20° Virgo.

## 1783–1789: Etteilla

Etteilla published the first systematic tarot-divination manuals and then the Grand Etteilla pack.

**[Documented fact]** Courts now bore stable upright/reversed meanings as part of a complete occult deck.

## 1854–1856: Éliphas Lévi

Lévi’s *Dogme et Rituel de la haute magie* made tarot the key to Kabbalah and ceremonial magic.

**[Documented fact]** Lévi discusses the court sequence as master, mistress, knight, and squire and uses the language of pentacles.

**[Absence of evidence]** He did not give the Knight of Pentacles the later Golden Dawn zodiacal span. Quoting Lévi’s trump-centered statements as though they were a card-specific meaning is misleading. [French edition](https://www.cercle-papus.com/ouvrages/eliphas-levi-dogme-et-rituel-tome-1.pdf).

## 1863–1870: Paul Christian

Paul Christian elaborated a fictional Egyptian initiation through twenty-two arcana.

**[Legend]** His temple galleries and initiation rituals are literary occult constructions, not records from ancient Egypt.

**[Absence of Knight-specific evidence]** The narrative is centered on the major arcana.

## 1888: Mathers

Mathers published *The Tarot: Its Occult Signification, Use in Fortune-Telling and Method of Play*. His Knight of Pentacles meaning is discussed below. [Primary text](https://hermetics.net/media-library/tarot/samuel-liddell-macgregor-mathers-the-tarot/).

## 1889: Papus and Wirth

Papus’s *Tarot des Bohémiens* places:

- Kings under Yod
- Queens under Heh
- Knights under Vau
- Knaves under final Heh

It also identifies Pentacles as the final-Heh suit. Therefore, in Papus’s nested construction, the Knight of Pentacles is **Vau of final Heh**, not Yod of final Heh. [1892 English translation, p. 42 in the linked scan](https://benebellwen.com/wp-content/uploads/2013/02/papus-the-tarot-of-the-bohemians-1892.pdf).

This conflicts with the Golden Dawn, which assigns its mounted Knight/King rank to Fire/Yod.

Wirth’s 1889 deck has only trumps.

## c. 1888–1900: Golden Dawn *Book T*

The first traceable appearance of the full complex—Fire of Earth, 20° Leo–20° Virgo, fertile land, winged rider, magical pentacle—is in the Golden Dawn manuscript tradition.

## 1909–1911: Waite–Smith and *Pictorial Key*

Smith turns the Golden Dawn’s fertile Earth rider into the stationary, heavy-horse Knight familiar today. Waite publishes the accompanying divinatory text.

## 1930s: Case and BOTA

Case transmits and modifies the Golden Dawn–Waite synthesis.

## 1938–1944 / 1969: Thoth

Harris paints the deck between 1938 and 1943. Crowley’s *Book of Thoth* appears in 1944. The complete deck receives its first general publication in 1969.

## Golden Dawn decans

The 30° span covers three ten-degree faces:

| Span | Golden Dawn pip | Planetary attribution | Book T title |
|---|---|---|---|
| 20°–30° Leo | Seven of Wands | Mars in Leo | Lord of Valour |
| 0°–10° Virgo | Eight of Pentacles | Sun in Virgo | Lord of Prudence |
| 10°–20° Virgo | Nine of Pentacles | Venus in Virgo | Lord of Material Gain |

Thus the court combines:

- Martial endurance or resistance at the Leo boundary
- Solar craftsmanship and method
- Venusian cultivation, property, and harvest

The court’s span does **not** include the Ten of Pentacles/Sun? Correction: the Ten of Pentacles is Mercury in the final decan of Virgo, 20°–30°, which lies immediately after the Knight’s jurisdiction.

## Tree of Life

**[Important correction]** Courts do not have a single Hebrew letter or a single Tree-of-Life path in the manner of the twenty-two trumps.

The Golden Dawn places them through a nested elemental and sephirothic scheme:

- Knight/King: Chokmah and Yod/Fire
- Queen: Binah and Heh/Water
- Prince: Tiphareth and Vau/Air
- Princess: Malkuth and final Heh/Earth

The Knight of Pentacles is therefore the Chokmah/Yod or fiery force acting within Assiah/Earth, not “the path of Yod” on the Tree.

## Tzaddi/Heh

Crowley’s famous “Tzaddi is not the Star” change affects the Emperor and Star:

- Golden Dawn: Emperor = Heh; Star = Tzaddi
- Crowley: Emperor = Tzaddi; Star = Heh

**[Documented fact]** This does not change the Knight of Disks’ Fire-of-Earth attribution or zodiacal span. Invoking the Emperor/Star controversy as a reason to reassign this court is a category error.

# Divinatory meanings by author

## Etteilla

**[Documented general attribution]** Etteilla established paired upright and reversed meanings for all cards and characteristically read courts as people, social roles, or agents.

**[Textual problem]** A single exact “1789 Knight of Coins” wording could not be safely established from the digitized sources consulted. Later Etteilla-derived lists disagree over numbering and sometimes conflate *Cavalier*, *Homme brun*, business, service, utility, and profit.

**Finding:** Etteilla should be credited with the divinatory court tradition, but a verbatim first-edition quotation is withheld rather than silently copied from an undated modern keyword list.

## S. L. MacGregor Mathers, 1888

Mathers gives:

> “A useful Man, Trustworthy, Wisdom, Economy, Order, Regulation.”

His reversed continuation denotes the bad use or negation of those traits, including idleness or careless management depending on the edition/transcription. [Mathers, *The Tarot*, 1888](https://hermetics.net/media-library/tarot/samuel-liddell-macgregor-mathers-the-tarot/).

This is still primarily a person-card: a reliable practical man.

## Golden Dawn *Book T*

Well dignified:

- Laborious
- Clever and patient in material matters
- Dependable when properly supported

Ill dignified:

> “avaricious, grasping, dull, jealous”

The manuscript adds that he is not naturally courageous unless aided by other cards. [*Book T*, court-card section](https://jantetteroo.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/02/Mathers_and_felkin_golden_dawn_book_t_th.pdf), PDF p. 19.

“Dignified” is not identical to physically reversed. It depends on elemental and positional relationships in a spread.

## A. E. Waite, 1910

Upright:

> “Utility, serviceableness, interest, responsibility, rectitude”

Waite confines these qualities to the “normal and external plane.”

Reversed:

- Inertia
- Idleness
- Repose
- Stagnation
- Placidity
- Discouragement
- Carelessness

[*Pictorial Key to the Tarot*, Pentacles: Knight](https://www.mr-kaplan.com/Library/Waite/The%20Pictorial%20Key%20to%20the%20Tarot.pdf), p. 69 in the linked PDF.

This is the source of the modern “slow but reliable” versus “stuck or lazy” polarity.

## Aleister Crowley, 1944

Crowley treats the card more as a natural force and character type than as a simple event.

Positive qualities include:

- Industriousness
- Practical competence
- Patience
- Agriculture and material production
- The deep internal heat by which life germinates

Negative qualities include stolidity, lack of initiative, dullness, and preoccupation with material processes. Crowley memorably characterizes its fire as:

> “the smouldering fire of the process of growth.”

[*The Book of Thoth*, Knight of Disks](https://www.rahoorkhuit.net/library/crowley/equinox/volume3/vol_3_no_05.pdf).

Crowley generally uses elemental dignity rather than a mechanically separate reversed definition.

## Paul Foster Case

Case preserves the practical, persistent, earthy person and the Golden Dawn idea of fiery activity within matter. In his cartomantic writings, Coins/Pentacles concern business, possessions, work, and the embodied result of intention.

**[Textual limitation]** The publicly accessible *Oracle of the Tarot* scan does not provide a confidently separable sentence for this card that can be quoted without resolving Case’s King/Knight conversion. His system should be cited through the complete court table rather than modern paraphrases. [Case, *Oracle of the Tarot*, 1933](https://benebellwen.com/wp-content/uploads/2013/02/paul-foster-case-oracle-of-the-tarot-1933-source-credit-tarotworks.pdf).

## Rachel Pollack

In *Seventy-Eight Degrees of Wisdom*, Pollack develops the card as the most settled and materially responsible Knight: dedicated to work and reality, yet at risk of losing imagination, adventure, or emotional spontaneity.

Her influential move is psychological. The card need not be a literal young man; it can be an attitude of steady application.

Reversed or shadow manifestations include becoming mechanical, stubborn, unimaginative, or trapped in routine.

**[Copyright note]** The consulted sources did not supply a legally reliable open full-text edition from which to quote a card-specific sentence. This section therefore paraphrases Pollack rather than fabricating pagination.

## Mary K. Greer

Greer treats courts as:

- Actual people
- Aspects of oneself
- Personas projected upon other people
- Modes of action valued or frustrated by the situation

Her Knight of Pentacles wants physical, outdoor, hands-on activity; in an unsuitable environment he can become stubborn and miserable. [Greer’s own court-card essay](https://marykgreer.com/2016/03/23/using-the-court-cards/).

Greer does not reduce reversals to simple opposites. A reversal can indicate:

- Blocked expression
- Excess
- Deficiency
- Rejection of the role
- Private rather than public expression

For this card that can mean inability to work concretely, workaholism, refusal of responsibility, or Earth energy forced into an alien setting.

# Esoteric and symbolic readings

## Material and mercantile

**First basis:** the literal coin suit of medieval cards.

Coins signify money only because they visibly depict money-like disks. The broader associations—property, labor, body, soil, food, craft, and practical management—developed through cartomancy and occult elemental theory.

## Elemental Earth

**[Esoteric attribution]** Nineteenth-century occultists stabilized Coins/Pentacles as Earth. The Golden Dawn made the assignment systematic.

Earth contributes:

- Weight
- Patience
- Form and embodiment
- Agriculture
- Property and resources
- Slowness or resistance to change

## Fire of Earth

The Golden Dawn’s novel contribution was to combine rank and suit:

- Mounted King/Knight = Fire
- Pentacles = Earth

This produces the apparent paradox of a slow Knight. His fire is internal:

- Volcanic or seismic energy
- Digestion and metabolism
- Seed heat and germination
- Sustained labor
- The will required to shape matter

Crowley spread this natural-philosophical reading most forcefully.

## Kabbalistic

The card is **Yod of final Heh**, or the initiating seed within material manifestation.

Possible readings include:

- Spirit entering matter
- The seminal force that makes Earth fertile
- Chokmah acting through Assiah
- Dynamic intention embodied in work

These are Golden Dawn/Hermetic Kabbalah readings, not medieval Jewish interpretations of the card.

Papus differs: his ordinary Knight rank is Vau, so his Knight of Pentacles is Vau of second Heh. The discrepancy results from different court hierarchies rather than a scribal error.

## Astrological

The Golden Dawn span 20° Leo–20° Virgo places the card across a sign boundary.

Leo supplies:

- Pride
- Persistence
- Sovereign will
- Heat

Virgo supplies:

- Cultivation
- Technique
- Discrimination
- Service
- Harvest and accounting

The resulting figure is less “Virgo alone” than a transition from fixed solar will into mutable earthly craft.

## Alchemical

**[Modern esoteric development]** No fifteenth-century source calls the Coins Knight an alchemical card.

Later alchemical tarot reads him as:

- The worker who tends the vessel
- Fixed matter undergoing slow internal transformation
- Sulfur/fire working within salt/body
- The agricultural metaphor for maturation of the Stone

Crowley’s mountains, earthquakes, seed heat, and nutrition encouraged this reading; Robert Place made it pictorially explicit.

## Christian

**[Historical iconographic possibility]** A mounted knight in medieval Europe naturally carried Christian associations of chivalry, service, estate, and just stewardship.

**[Absence of evidence]** The Cary–Yale card has no known contemporaneous commentary identifying it with a saint, crusader, parable, or Christian virtue.

Modern Christian interpretations—faithful stewardship of talents, patient vocation, honest labor—are analogical applications.

## Hermetic

The card represents force descending into dense form. It reconciles opposites:

- Fire and Earth
- Motion and stillness
- Seed and field
- Warrior and farmer
- Will and patience

The hexagram sceptre in *Book T* introduces macrocosmic equilibrium; the pentacle is the microcosmic magical instrument of Earth.

## Jungian

**[Modern invention]** Carl Jung did not publish a developed tarot-card system assigning this Knight a fixed archetype.

Post-Jungian readers variously see:

- The ego building a stable relation to body and work
- The craftsman or steward archetype
- The puer beginning to accept responsibility
- The shadow of compulsive routine
- Libido becoming embodied in practical action

Calling this “Jung’s meaning” is inaccurate. It is later Jungian or archetypal tarot practice, spread especially by twentieth-century psychological writers such as Pollack and Greer.

### Italo Calvino, *The Castle of Crossed Destinies*

Calvino constructs narratives from tarot cards, principally Visconti–Sforza and Marseille imagery.

**[Documented relevance]** The work demonstrates tarot’s capacity for combinatorial storytelling.

### T. S. Eliot, *The Waste Land*

Eliot mentions Madame Sosostris and several tarot-like cards, including invented or altered ones.

### W. B. Yeats

Yeats participated in the Golden Dawn and knew its symbolic world.

Bergman’s *The Seventh Seal* contains a medieval knight and a game with Death.

# Controversies and disputes

## Egyptian origin

### The claim

Court de Gébelin asserted in 1781 that tarot preserved an Egyptian book of wisdom. Etteilla elaborated it into a reconstructed *Book of Thoth*. Later occultists repeated and expanded the story.

### Evidence

**[Legend]**

- No Egyptian tarot cards survive.
- The earliest surviving tarot packs are fifteenth-century northern Italian objects.
- Gébelin wrote before Champollion deciphered Egyptian hieroglyphs.
- His proposed Egyptian etymologies were not supported by Egyptian writing.

Dummett points out that Gébelin and Mellet supplied purported Egyptian derivations without hieroglyphic forms. [Dummett, “Origins of Tarot”](https://www.nybooks.com/articles/1981/05/14/origins-of-tarot/).

### Knight-specific consequence

The Knight of Pentacles is not an Egyptian cavalry figure recovered from a temple. Its visible genealogy runs through Islamic and European playing-card courts; its “Earth-fire” identity begins in the Golden Dawn.

## “Gypsy” or Romani origin

### The claim

Nineteenth-century writers claimed Roma carried tarot from Egypt or India into Europe. Papus’s title *Tarot of the Bohemians* helped spread the association.

### Evidence

**[Legend]** There is no documentary chain connecting the first Italian *trionfi* commissions with Roma. The word “Bohemians” reflects a historical European label for Roma, not evidence of authorship.

**[Documented history of the myth]** Jean-Alexandre Vaillant and other nineteenth-century writers helped popularize the Romani-transmission theory. It became entangled with the older mistaken belief that Roma were Egyptian.

## Cathar origin

**[Disputed modern legend]** Some twentieth-century writers interpret Marseille symbols as Cathar theology or a suppressed southern-French gospel.

Problems:

- Tarot’s first secure documents and surviving packs are Italian, not Cathar-era Languedoc.
- The historical Cathar institutions had been destroyed well before the first known *trionfi*.
- No Cathar manuscript describes a tarot sequence or Knight of Coins.

Symbolic parallels may be used devotionally, but they do not establish transmission.

## Templar origin

**[Legend]** Claims that Knights Templar encoded secrets into tarot often point to knights, temples, Baphomet, or fourfold suits.

There is no known Templar rule, inventory, trial record, or manuscript mentioning tarot. The order was suppressed in 1312, before the first secure European playing-card and tarot evidence. Lévi’s nineteenth-century Baphomet drawing is sometimes retrojected into medieval tarot history.

## Alchemical origin

**[Disputed]** Renaissance card imagery can be interpreted using alchemical symbolism, and later occult decks deliberately do so. But no fifteenth-century commentary identifies the Knight of Coins as sulfur acting in salt or as an alchemical adept.

The responsible distinction is:

- Renaissance society possessed alchemy.
- Some images are compatible with alchemical readings.
- Compatibility is not proof of design intention.

## The “pentacle was always there” claim

**[Modern retrojection]** Medieval coins and deniers often contain floral, heraldic, or radial decorations. Some resemble stars. That does not make every historical coin a Golden Dawn pentacle.

The explicit magical pentacle is documented in Golden Dawn descriptions and Waite’s deck. Earlier star-like ornament must be interpreted case by case.

## Visconti family portraits

Moakley and Kaplan investigated whether painted figures portray specific family members.

**[Disputed]** Heraldic ownership and dynastic commemoration are well supported. Literal portrait identifications are much less secure, especially when the relevant Visconti–Sforza Knight of Coins is missing.

The surviving Cary–Yale male rider may embody aristocratic rank or family identity without being a recognizable portrait of one named person.

## Waite’s Knight versus the Golden Dawn Prince

**[Frequent modern error]** Some tables equate them because both occupy a “third court” position in differently named sequences.

The manuscript iconography resolves the issue:

- Golden Dawn mounted King = Fire = Thoth Knight = closest equivalent of Waite Knight.
- Golden Dawn chariot Prince = Air = closest structural equivalent of Waite’s seated King.

Waite did not publicly print the full Golden Dawn court key in *Pictorial Key*, helping the confusion persist.

## Final Heh confusion

There are two independent questions:

1. Which Tetragrammaton letter belongs to the **rank**?
2. Which belongs to the **suit**?

Golden Dawn:

- Mounted King/Knight = Yod
- Pentacles = final Heh
- Therefore Yod of final Heh

Papus:

- Knight = Vau
- Pentacles = second/final Heh
- Therefore Vau of final Heh

Saying merely “final Heh” identifies Earth/Pentacles but not the sub-element.

## Misprints and edition drift

Several recurrent problems were found:

- 1760 versus 1761 for Conver: the Two of Coins bears the production date in forms catalogued differently by modern publishers.
- “Chevalier,” “Cavallier,” and “Cavalier” are often silently modernized.
- “20° Leo to 20° Virgo” is sometimes printed as “21st degree Leo to 20th Virgo”; these are compatible boundary conventions.
- “King of Pentacles” and “Knight of Pentacles” are sometimes separated as though *Book T* described two cards.
- Modern Visconti–Sforza facsimiles often fail to emphasize that their Knight of Coins is reconstructed.
- A Golden Dawn “reversed” meaning is often substituted for “ill dignified,” although those techniques are not identical.
- Modern charts assign a Hebrew letter or Tree path to the court as if it were a trump.
- Wirth is credited with 78 card designs although his original 1889 pack contained twenty-two trumps.

# Open questions

1. **Exact dating of the Cary–Yale pack.** Its place in the 1440s–mid-century Milanese sequence remains debated.

2. **Authorship within the Bembo workshop.** “Bonifacio Bembo” is convenient but may conceal collaboration or later additions.

3. **Identity of the Cary–Yale rider.** No named Visconti or Sforza individual has achieved scholarly consensus.

4. **The lost Visconti–Sforza image.** Its existence is highly probable; its pose, costume, horse color, and precise suit-emblem placement are unknowable.

5. **Rosenwald dating and origin.** The relationship between centaur courts and later minchiate creatures deserves closer comparative work.

6. **Etteilla’s first-state card.** A complete comparison of surviving 1788–1789 impressions is needed to stabilize its card number, caption, and reversed keyword without relying on later Etteilla schools.

7. **Smith’s precise visual sources.** Golden Dawn dependence is strong, but no documentary record specifies the model for the dark horse, armor, or agricultural horizon.

8. **Waite’s private intended court mapping.** Iconography supports the mounted-King equation, but Waite’s public text deliberately suppresses much Golden Dawn doctrine.

9. **BOTA edition history.** Published minors and instructional versions should be catalogued separately; current commercial descriptions do not fully resolve early production states.

10. **Modern cultural quotations.** The Waite image is ubiquitous in illustration, fashion, tattooing, and online media, but there is no authoritative card-specific catalogue.

# Conclusions

The Knight of Pentacles is historically a **Cavallo di Denari**, a mounted Coins court inherited from the social structure of early Mediterranean playing cards. Its earliest surviving image is the male Knight of Coins in the Cary–Yale/Visconti di Modrone pack. The corresponding Visconti–Sforza card is lost, despite appearing in modern reconstructed facsimiles.

For roughly four centuries its documented function was principally ludic: a suit honor ranking below Queen and above Page. Marseille makers stabilized the profile horseman confronting or carrying a denier. Minchiate demonstrated that even this apparently conventional image could mutate into centaurs and dragon-bodied mounts.

The decisive occult reinterpretation occurred in the Golden Dawn. Its mounted **King of Pentacles**, also identified as the Knight, became Fire of Earth, Yod of final Heh, ruler of 20° Leo through 20° Virgo and lord of fertile land. Pamela Colman Smith distilled that system into a stationary armored rider on a heavy horse above cultivated fields. Crowley and Harris made its suppressed doctrine explicit as the Knight of Disks: geology, gravity, agriculture, nutrition, and the slow inner fire of growth.

Its now-familiar meanings—reliability, duty, patience, work, and their shadows of inertia and dullness—are therefore layered:

1. Literal medieval coin and mounted rank  
2. Early cartomantic “useful man”  
3. Golden Dawn Fire-of-Earth theory  
4. Waite’s practical, motionless worker  
5. Crowley’s agricultural and geological force  
6. Modern psychological archetype of disciplined embodiment  

Egyptian, Romani, Cathar, and Templar origin stories belong to the reception history of tarot, not the documented genealogy of this card.
