# King of Pentacles: Research Dossier

## Method and evidentiary labels

This dossier distinguishes:

- **[Documented fact]** directly attested by a surviving card, archival record, printed rulebook, or primary occult text.
- **[Scholarly reconstruction]** a conclusion argued from material, stylistic, archival, or comparative evidence.
- **[Tradition]** a historically established convention whose first formulation may be uncertain.
- **[Esoteric attribution]** a deliberately imposed occult correspondence, not a claim about the card’s original function.
- **[Disputed claim]** an identification or historical conclusion on which informed writers disagree.
- **[Legend]** a historical-origin narrative unsupported by contemporary evidence.
- **[Modern invention]** a recent interpretation or design innovation presented as such.

One crucial limitation should be stated at once: many requested subjects—the three regional trump orders, the Pope/Jupiter substitutions, Tzaddi/Heh controversy, and the placement of Justice and Strength—concern the twenty-one trumps, not the King of Pentacles. They are included where they illuminate tarot history, but none determines this court card’s rank, imagery, or playing value.

### The “final Heh” problem

**[Documented fact]** Book T assigns the four court grades as follows:

- mounted King/Knight: Yod, Fire;
- Queen: first Heh, Water;
- chariot-borne Prince—called “King” in Book T’s ordinary-card gloss: Vav, Air;
- Princess/Knave: final Heh, Earth.

Therefore the lead “final Heh” does **not** belong to the King of Pentacles when “King” means Book T’s chariot figure. It belongs to the **Princess of Pentacles**, “Earth of Earth.” The King of Pentacles is explicitly the **Vav force**, “Air of Earth.” This is directly stated in [Book T, pp. 11 and 20](https://jantetteroo.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/02/Mathers_and_felkin_golden_dawn_book_t_th.pdf).

### The rank-name trap

The Golden Dawn created a persistent ambiguity:

| Traditional/RWS rank | Book T descriptive grade | Thoth name | Elemental grade |
|---|---|---|---|
| Knight | King, mounted | Knight | Fire/Yod |
| Queen | Queen, enthroned | Queen | Water/Heh |
| King | Prince, in chariot; glossed “King” | Prince | Air/Vav |
| Page/Knave | Princess, standing | Princess | Earth/final Heh |

Thus Crowley’s **Prince of Disks** is the direct esoteric descendant of Book T’s **King of Pentacles**, while Crowley’s **Knight of Disks** occupies the mounted, fiery role. Treating the Thoth Knight as simply the RWS Knight, or the Thoth Prince as merely a subordinate court, destroys the intended correspondence.

## Names and terminology

### Before “tarot”

**[Documented fact]** Fifteenth-century records initially call the game or pack *trionfi*, *carte da trionfi*, or *ludus triumphorum*. The now-familiar words *tarocchi/taraux* are first documented around 1505 in Ferrara and Avignon. The suit itself was already the Italian **denari**, coins.

The 1442 Ferrara account records painted *carte da trionfi*. It attests the pack class, not an individually named “Re di Denari.” Court-card names were ordinarily unnecessary in purchase records.

### Regional names

- **Italy:** *Re di Denari*. *Denaro* means a coin or money; *danari* is a historical spelling.
- **France:** *Roi de Deniers*. Early captions use *ROY* and occasionally defective or abbreviated spellings such as *DENIES*.
- **Spain:** *Rey de Oros*. Spanish *oros* literally means golds; the suit sign is a gold coin.
- **Germany:** *König der Münzen*. Actual German/Austrian Tarock normally uses French suits, so a physical “King of Pentacles” may instead be the *Karo-König*.
- **English:** King of Coins in historical description; King of Pentacles in occult packs derived from Mathers and Waite.
- **Thoth:** Prince of Disks. Crowley preferred “disk” as a broader magical and cosmological object than a commercial coin.

### When did the coin become a pentacle?

**[Documented fact]** Medieval and Marseille cards show **coins**, frequently floral or heraldic disks. A five-pointed star is not intrinsic to the early suit.

**[Esoteric attribution]** Nineteenth-century French and British occultism increasingly interpreted the coin as a magical talisman or pentacle. Mathers used “Pentacles” in 1888; Golden Dawn Book T specifies elaborate pentacles; Waite and Smith placed a clear pentagram on every suit disk.

Waite was careful not to claim authorship. In *The Pictorial Key* he wrote:

> “I have not invented the substitution of pentacles and I have no special cause to sustain” it.

He says old packs used “current coin, money, deniers,” while his pentagram represented the governance and correspondence of the four elements in human nature. [Waite, *Pictorial Key*, King of Pentacles](https://citeseerx.ist.psu.edu/document?doi=7a15961664bced51d1162198bce46d173e40dd41&repid=rep1&type=pdf).

## Iconography, deck by deck

## Mamluk *kanjifah/naʾib* ancestor, fourteenth–fifteenth century

**[Documented fact]** The surviving Topkapı Mamluk pack has four suits—cups, coins/dirhams, swords, and polo-sticks—and three courts per suit:

- *malik*: king;
- *nāʾib malik*: deputy king;
- *thānī nāʾib*: second deputy.

Its court cards bear calligraphy, suit signs, ornamental panels, and poetic inscriptions rather than portrait figures. The relevant ancestor is the **malik al-darāhim**, King of Coins/Dirhams.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** The near identity of the four suit systems and the transmission of *nāʾib* into Italian *naibi* and Spanish *naipes* support a Mamluk-to-European line of influence. The precise route, intermediate packs, and whether European queens and mounted knights developed directly from the three Mamluk courts remain uncertain. Dummett treats the Mamluk pack as the closest surviving antecedent, not as “tarot”: it had no tarot trumps.

The Topkapı inscriptions are transcribed at [Mamluk Playing Cards](https://mamluk.spiorad.net/inscriptions.htm); Dummett’s discussion is excerpted by the [Tarot History Forum](https://forum.tarothistory.com/viewtopic.php?p=27685).

## Cary–Yale / Visconti di Modrone, Milan, ca. 1440–1450

**[Documented fact]** A King of Coins survives. He is a richly dressed, bearded sovereign on a gold ground, holding or accompanied by the coin emblem. The luxurious costume, crown, heraldic gold, and frontal or near-frontal courtly presentation identify rank, not a divinatory personality.

The deck is exceptional because it originally appears to have had **six** court ranks per suit: male and female pages, male and female mounted figures, queen, and king. Its Coins reproduce Filippo Maria Visconti’s gold coin types. Yale reports that the impressed coins and associated heraldry support manufacture before Filippo Maria’s death in 1447, although current attribution may involve Andrea Bembo rather than Bonifacio alone. [Yale Library](https://library.yale.edu/news/cards-library-partners-work-together-solve-mysteries-rare-tarot-deck?page=1).

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** This is probably the earliest surviving King of Coins in a tarot pack.

**[Disputed claim]** It is often called the “Cary–Yale,” “Visconti di Modrone,” or simply “Visconti Tarot”; the names do not denote three different decks.

## Brera–Brambilla, Milan, ca. 1442–1451

**[Documented fact]** Forty-eight cards survive at the Pinacoteca di Brera. The Italian national catalogue dates the group approximately 1442–1451 and attributes it to Bonifacio Bembo. Its Coin pips reproduce Filippo Maria’s currency. The official catalogue describes a standard hierarchy of *valletto, cavaliere, regina e re*, but that is a reconstruction of the original pack’s structure, not proof that every one of those cards survives. [Italian national catalogue](https://catalogo.beniculturali.it/detail/HistoricOrArtisticProperty/0300097697-0).

**Absence finding:** I could not verify from the Brera catalogue that a King of Coins survives in this fragment. It should not be cited unconditionally as the earliest physical example.

## Visconti–Sforza / Pierpont Morgan–Bergamo, Milan, ca. 1450–1480

**[Documented fact]** Morgan MS M.630 no. 20 is explicitly catalogued as **The King of Coins**, attributed to Bonifacio Bembo, Milan, ca. 1450–1480, 173 × 87 mm. The Morgan describes the pack as a game commissioned within the Visconti–Sforza milieu, not an occult manuscript. [Morgan card record](https://www.themorgan.org/collection/tarot-cards/the-king-of-coins); [complete Morgan group](https://www.themorgan.org/collection/tarot-cards).

The king is a courtly, crowned, bearded male enthroned against tooled gold. He displays a large coin bearing the Sforza sunburst/quartered device. His clothing is patterned, expensive, and ceremonial. The image says “king of this suit” through rank, heraldry, and material splendour; grapes, bulls, castles, and pentagrams are not yet present.

### Is he a portrait?

**[Disputed claim]** Gertrude Moakley and Stuart Kaplan discussed the deck through Visconti–Sforza biography and court pageantry, encouraging attempts to match individual figures to Francesco Sforza, Filippo Maria Visconti, or other family members.

**[Evidence against certainty]** The Morgan catalogue identifies the patronal family but does not identify the King of Coins as a named sitter. Heraldic devices demonstrate ownership or commission more securely than physiognomic portraiture. No contemporary inscription says “Francesco Sforza.” The most defensible description is “a dynastic court figure made for the Visconti–Sforza family,” not a verified portrait.

## Ferrarese/Este cards, late fifteenth century

**[Documented fact]** Surviving Este-related fragments include court and pip cards, but the fragmentary inventories vary. Ferrarese painters supplied *carte da trionfi* to the Este court from the 1440s. The Brera’s Sola Busca exhibition catalogue observes that documentary records precede the surviving Ferrarese cards. [Brera, *The Secret of the Tarot*](https://pinacotecabrera.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/11/Il-Segreto-dei-tarocchi-eng.pdf).

**Absence finding:** No securely identified Este King of Coins was found in the museum material consulted. General claims about “the Este King of Pentacles” therefore require a precise collection and catalogue number.

## Sola Busca, Ferrara/Venice, 1491

**[Documented fact]** The Sola Busca is the earliest substantially complete engraved tarot and is important for illustrated numeral cards. Its courts and trumps bear pseudo-classical warriors and named figures.

**Relevance:** Its significance to the King of Pentacles is indirect. Pamela Colman Smith demonstrably had access to Sola Busca photographs through the British Museum and borrowed several compositional solutions for RWS pips, notably the Three and Ten of Swords. No comparably exact dependence has been demonstrated for her King of Pentacles.

**Absence finding:** “Waite’s King comes from the Sola Busca” could not be traced to a convincing card-to-card demonstration.

## “Mantegna Tarocchi,” ca. 1460s

**[Documented fact]** These are fifty didactic engravings arranged into social, liberal-art, cosmic, and theological series.

**[Scholarly consensus]** They are not a tarot pack and contain neither four suits nor a King of Coins. Their traditional name is misleading. They matter only as evidence for the broader Renaissance appetite for ordered emblematic series.

## Rosenwald and Budapest sheets, late fifteenth/early sixteenth century

**[Documented fact]** These uncut or fragmentary printed sheets are critical evidence for inexpensive early tarot patterns, especially Bolognese and north-Italian production.

**Absence finding:** A card-by-card, collection-authorized image of a King of Coins on the sheets was not located in the consulted records. They document the transition from unique luxury paintings to reproducible woodcut cards, but should not be assigned a specific King image without sheet coordinates.

## Minchiate, Florence, sixteenth–nineteenth centuries

**[Documented fact]** Minchiate expands the trump series to forty-one plus the Fool, but retains four fourteen-card suits. Coins have King, Queen, Knight, and a female Page/Maid in many patterns.

The King of Coins remains a conventional crowned court sovereign displaying a round coin. He is neither numbered among the forty trump cards nor astrologically individualized by the historical game.

**Meaning of change:** expansion occurred in the trump sequence, not in the King’s suit function.

## Bolognese tarocchino

**[Documented fact]** The shortened Bolognese pack retains all sixteen courts while removing low pip cards. The King of Coins consequently survives as the top Coins court even where low Coins are absent.

Bolognese rules and sheets are essential to trump-order history but do not turn this king into a trump or attach a special subject name to him.

## Sicilian tarot

**[Documented fact]** Sicilian tarocchi underwent radical trump renaming, shortening, and local iconographic change. The ordinary suits nevertheless retained their kings. The Coins king remains a suit court, not one of Sicily’s regionally renamed trumps.

## Tarot de Marseille lineage

### Jean Noblet, Paris, ca. 1650

**[Documented fact]** The caption is a form of **ROY DE DENIERS**. The king sits in three-quarter profile, with bent legs, elaborate dress and headgear, displaying a large floral coin. The vegetation or turf near his foot is easily mistaken in later copies for a dropped crown.

This is an important move away from the gold-ground Milanese presentation: the king occupies a schematic outdoor or architectural space and becomes a reproducible woodcut type.

### Jean Dodal, Lyon, ca. 1701–1715

The seated king and large denier continue. Dodal’s caption reportedly reads **ROY DE DENIES**, an orthographic or cutting irregularity. His broad, confused hat and the object near his foot have produced modern theories of an eastern ruler, a merchant, or a discarded crown.

**[Disputed iconographic reading]** Comparison with Noblet and Payen supports “turf/vegetation” better than “abandoned crown.” No contemporary explanatory text identifies the object. See the comparative discussion at [Tarot Forum](https://www.tarotforum.net/threads/king-roy-of-deniers-contrasting-the-dodal-and-conver.52625/).

### Nicolas Conver, Marseille, 1760/1761

Conver stabilizes the Type II Marseille design: a mature bearded king seated three-quarter view; wide hat over a gold cap; one leg advanced; bright hose; large floral denier held against the belt or supported at its edge; green upright beside him and grassy ground.

A BnF-associated record describes a surviving Conver-line witness catalogued to 1809–1833, warning that not every object sold as “Conver 1760” is an impression pulled in 1760. [Description and BnF reference](https://marselia.app/fr/cards/roi-de-denier).

### Grimaud–Paul Marteau, 1930

**[Documented fact]** Marteau republished and recoloured the Marseille pattern for Grimaud. Flat standardized colours and cleaner printing made this the dominant twentieth-century French image.

**[Modern interpretive development]** Marteau’s accompanying symbolic readings helped turn printer’s details and colours into a systematic symbolic language, though those meanings are not documented as the intentions of Noblet, Dodal, or Conver.

### Camoin–Jodorowsky, 1997

**[Modern invention presented as restoration]** Philippe Camoin and Alejandro Jodorowsky reconstructed a Marseille deck by consulting multiple historical exemplars and adding or clarifying details. Their Roi de Deniers remains seated with his coin, but includes altered colours and small graphic details not uniformly present in early witnesses.

The result is historically informed but not a photographic restoration of one lost archetype. Jodorowsky’s later “receptive/active,” bodily, psycho-genealogical and visual readings belong to his modern system.

## Tarot de Besançon

Besançon-pattern renamings—Papess as **Juno**, Pope as **Jupiter**—apply to trumps II and V. The Roi de Deniers remains the Coins king. These substitutions arose in Protestant or confessionally sensitive regions during the eighteenth century; they do not supply an alternative name for this court.

## Etteilla, *Grand Etteilla*, 1789 onward

**[Documented fact]** Etteilla rearranged and renumbered the entire pack to express his Egyptian-cosmogonic and divinatory system. A surviving printed compilation displays the card as:

- **64**
- *Le Roi de Denier*
- ordinary-card equivalent *Le Roi de Trèfle*
- upright **HOMME BRUN**
- reversed **HOMME VICIEUX**

See the digitized [*Le grand Etteilla, ou l’art de tirer les cartes*](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/2/2f/Le_grand_Etteilla%2C_ou%2C_l%27art_de_tirer_les_cartes_..._Le_tout_recueilli_..._%28IA_b29321220%29.pdf).

Etteilla’s redesign is the first sustained attempt to make every card, including ordinary suit courts, part of a printed tarot divination system.

## Oswald Wirth, 1889

**Absence finding:** Wirth’s 1889 deck consists of the twenty-two major arcana. It has no King of Pentacles. Wirth discusses the four suits in his writing, but a “Wirth King of Pentacles” is a later extension, not an original 1889 card.

## Golden Dawn manuscripts, ca. 1888–1900

Book T’s King of Pentacles is radically redesigned:

> “A WINGED Kingly Figure seated in a chariot drawn by a bull.”

He has a winged bull’s-head crest; flowery land lies below; he holds a downward gold orb and an orb-and-cross sceptre. His occult title is **Prince of the Chariot of Earth**. The imagery converts a stationary European sovereign into the mobile Vav/Son who combines parental forces and practically realizes them.

Modern Golden Dawn decks by Robert Wang and by Sandra Tabatha and Chic Cicero are reconstructions of manuscript specifications, not surviving official decks issued by the original Order. Differences in pose, colours, and magical insignia reflect choices needed to turn terse ritual descriptions into publishable paintings.

## Waite–Smith, London, 1909

Pamela Colman Smith shows:

- a mature king on a black throne;
- carved bull heads;
- a floral crown;
- a sceptre in his right hand;
- a large gold pentacle resting on or held over his left knee;
- a robe covered in grape and vine motifs;
- abundant vines around the throne;
- a battlemented castle behind him;
- one armoured foot visible amid vegetation.

Waite’s own physical description is unexpectedly brief:

> “The figure calls for no special description; the face is rather dark, suggesting also courage, but somewhat lethargic in tendency.”

He specifically directs attention to the recurrent bull’s head. The pentagram, he says, typifies the ordering of the four elements in human nature. [Waite, *Pictorial Key*](https://citeseerx.ist.psu.edu/document?doi=7a15961664bced51d1162198bce46d173e40dd41&repid=rep1&type=pdf).

### Sources of Smith’s design

- **[Documented context]** Smith worked under Waite’s instructions and knew theatrical costume, medievalizing illustration, and British Museum material.
- **[Strong reconstruction]** The bull, grapes, castle, and luxurious garden visualize Taurus/Earth, possession, fertility, and established dominion.
- **[Unproven claim]** No source consulted proves that the face is a portrait of Waite, a banker, or a specific historical monarch.
- **[Modern pareidolia]** Claims that a hidden skeleton or corpse lies beneath his foot are not supported by Waite’s text or a consistent reading of early printings.

## BOTA, Paul Foster Case and Jessie Burns Parke, 1920s onward

The BOTA court is a black-line image intended to be coloured by the student. It remains close to Waite–Smith in general vocabulary while Case restores explicit Qabalistic and astrological teaching. BOTA does not adopt Crowley’s title “Prince of Disks.”

**[Documented fact]** Jessie Burns Parke produced the drawings under Case’s direction; the deck’s pips are emblematic rather than RWS narrative scenes. [BOTA deck overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/B.O.T.A._tarot_deck); [Parke overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jessie_Burns_Parke).

## Crowley–Harris Thoth, painted 1938–1943; first full publication 1969

The corresponding card is **Prince of Disks**. Frieda Harris paints a meditative armoured prince in a heavy bull/ox-drawn chariot. His helmet carries a bull; the disk resembles a mathematical globe; the sceptre is an orb surmounted by a cross. Agriculture, geometry, fertility, and deliberate planning replace royal display.

Crowley writes:

> “The Prince of Disks represents the airy part of Earth, indicating the florescence and fructification of that element.”

He rules 21° Aries through 20° Taurus in Crowley’s inclusive wording. The prince is “Earth become intelligible”; his mathematics imply agricultural planning, and his task is to bring vegetation from matter as nourishment for spirit. His character is concentrated practical energy, sometimes heavy, insensitive, or obstinately opinionated. [Crowley, *The Book of Thoth*, “Prince of Disks”](https://hermetic.com/crowley/book-of-thoth/court-cards).

The card closely follows Book T but Harris transforms its heraldic catalogue into a unified, muscular machine-organism.

## Notable modern redesigns

- **Morgan–Greer, 1979:** a close-cropped, saturated King of Pentacles emphasizes the face, vines, crown, and gold disk. The booklet gives successful business judgment; reversed, corruption and financial peril.
- **Thoth-derived decks:** retain Prince, chariot, Taurus and Air of Earth.
- **RWS-derived decks:** usually retain throne, vines, pentagram, castle, and prosperous ruler.
- **Marseille revivals:** retain Roi de Deniers and an uninscribed floral coin.
- **Feminist, queer, and nonbinary decks:** frequently detach “King” from biological sex and interpret it as sovereign or externally directed mastery. **[Modern invention]**
- **Jungian decks and handbooks:** treat him as the mature steward, provider, embodied authority, or shadow materialist. **[Modern interpretive tradition]**

## The game

## Rank and trick-taking role

**[Documented fact]** In traditional Italian- and French-suited tarot games the King is the highest card of its ordinary suit:

**King > Queen > Knight/Cavalier > Jack/Page > numeral cards**

The trumps defeat it, but only when a player legally plays a trump. It is not itself a trump.

In old Italian round suits, including Coins and Cups, pip order could run Ace downward differently from long suits; this never dislodges the King from the top of its suit.

## Point value

- **French Tarot:** King is worth 4½ card-points when counted with a half-point low card, conventionally expressed as 5 minus the companion card. [French Tarot rules](https://www.letarot.net/en/doc/rules.html).
- **Many Austrian and central-European Tarock games:** King is nominally 5 points, with packet deductions; older accounting can equivalently express it as four intrinsic points plus the trick point. [Pagat counting systems](https://www.pagat.com/tarot/counting.html).
- **Tapp Tarock:** King 5, Queen 4, Cavalier 3, Jack 2, others 1 before packet adjustment. [Tapp Tarock rules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tapp_Tarock).
- **Minchiate:** King is the only point-bearing ordinary suit card and counts 5; it can also participate in the four-kings *versicola*. The King remains highest in suit. [Minchiate rules](https://www.pagat.com/tarot/minchiate.html).

The meaning “wealth” plays no part in these mechanics. A King of Coins scores as a king, not because it depicts money.

## Fool and trump-order disputes

These matters are historically important but card-external.

- Northern Italian tarot developed three principal regional trump-order families customarily called A, B, and C by historians.
- Justice and Fortitude/Strength changed relative positions across patterns.
- The Fool was normally unnumbered and functioned as an excusable card rather than a highest trump in early rules.
- In French Tarot the Fool/*Excuse* ordinarily avoids following suit and returns to its owner, subject to exchange and last-trick exceptions.
- In some central-European games the *Sküs* became the highest trump.
- None of these differences changes the King of Coins’ identity as an ordinary suit king.

## Chronology of attestation

### ca. 1420: Marziano da Tortona

**[Documented fact]** Marziano describes a special card pack devised for Filippo Maria Visconti, with sixteen classical gods arranged in four orders and four ordinary suits based on birds.

**Relevance:** It is an important precursor to personalized trump packs, but it does **not** name a King of Coins and is not a standard tarot. Citing Marziano as the first attestation of the King of Pentacles is incorrect.

### 1442: Ferrara ledger

**[Documented fact]** The Este accounts record painted *carte da trionfi*. This proves the pack class at Ferrara, not the name or appearance of an individual Coins king.

### ca. 1440–1450: Cary–Yale King of Coins

**[Documented object]** Earliest likely surviving card-specific witness.

### ca. 1450–1480: Morgan King of Coins

**[Documented object]** Secure museum catalogue entry, Milan, Bonifacio Bembo attribution.

### ca. 1470–1500: sermon *de ludo cum aliis*

The anonymous sermon condemns games and supplies an early ordered list of the twenty-one trumps plus Fool.

**Absence finding:** Because it enumerates the special triumphs, it does not separately attest “King of Coins.” Ordinary courts were structurally assumed.

### Late fifteenth century: Ferrarese literary references

Boiardo devised a moralized five-suit game; later poems assign verses to tarot cards. Folengo’s *Chaos del Triperuno* uses tarot cards in literary divination.

**Absence finding:** No securely traced ca. 1500 poem uniquely defining the King of Coins was found in the sources consulted.

### 1505

*Tarocchi/taraux* replaces or competes with *trionfi* in Ferrara and Avignon records. [Summary with documentary date](https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Visconti-Sforza-Trionfikarten).

### ca. 1650–1760

The French caption **Roy/Roi de Deniers** becomes visible in the Noblet–Dodal–Conver sequence.

### 1780s–1790s

Etteilla assigns the card number 64, upright/reversed verbal meanings, and an Egyptian-cosmogonic framework.

### 1888

Mathers publishes **King of Pentacles**, no. 64, with upright and reversed meanings.

### ca. 1888–1900

Golden Dawn Book T converts the traditional king into the chariot-borne Vav Prince, Air of Earth, 20° Aries–20° Taurus.

### 1909–1911

Waite–Smith deck and *Pictorial Key* establish the vine-robed, bull-throned King of Pentacles as the dominant English image.

### 1938–1943; 1969

Harris paints, and the full Thoth deck eventually publishes, Crowley’s Prince of Disks.

### Late twentieth century onward

RWS prosperity/stewardship readings, Thoth elemental readings, Jungian archetypal readings, and Marseille visual reading coexist.

### The relevant decans

Book T’s span covers:

1. **20°–30° Aries:** 4 of Wands, Venus in Aries, “Perfected Work.”
2. **0°–10° Taurus:** 5 of Pentacles, Mercury in Taurus, “Material Trouble.”
3. **10°–20° Taurus:** 6 of Pentacles, Moon in Taurus, “Material Success.”

The Prince thus crosses cardinal initiation into fixed consolidation. This is an **esoteric construction**, not a feature used in tarot gameplay.

### Lévi, Papus, and the limits of quotation

Lévi’s *Dogme et Rituel de la Haute Magie* constructs tarot as a key to the Hebrew alphabet, Kabbalah, magic, and universal symbolism. It does not provide a securely traceable modern paragraph devoted to “King of Pentacles, Air of Earth, 20° Aries–20° Taurus.” That exact formulation is Golden Dawn, not Lévi.

Papus’s *Tarot des Bohémiens* spreads the YHVH/four-court structural approach and the “Bohemian” origin vocabulary. Again, the detailed chariot, bull, and three-decan assignment is Book T’s elaboration.

## Divinatory meanings by author

## Etteilla

From the reproduced no. 64 card:

- **Upright:** *Homme brun* — “dark-haired/dark-complexioned man.”
- **Reversed:** *Homme vicieux* — “vicious or morally corrupt man.”

Etteilla’s meanings are terse significator labels, not the contemporary essay on financial mastery often published under his name.

## S. L. MacGregor Mathers, 1888

Mathers, *The Tarot: Its Occult Signification, Use in Fortune-Telling, and Method of Play*, pp. 17–18 of the consulted scan:

> “A dark Man, Victory, Bravery, Courage, Success.”

Reversed:

> “An old and vicious Man, a Dangerous Man, Doubt, Fear, Peril, Danger.”

[1888 scan](https://www.howardnema.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/12/the-tarot-1888-by-macgregor-mathers-1.pdf).

Mathers says many supplemental rules are “chiefly taken from Etteilla,” and his dark/vicious-man pair shows that dependence.

## Golden Dawn Book T

Well dignified:

> “Increase of matter. Increases good or evil, solidifies; practically applies things. Steady; reliable.”

Ill dignified:

> “selfish, animal and material: stupid,” slow to anger but furious when roused.

Book T normally uses elemental dignity rather than mechanically turning every card upside down. “Ill dignified” should not automatically be rewritten as “reversed.”

## A. E. Waite, 1910/1911

Upright:

> “Valour, realizing intelligence, business and normal intellectual aptitude, sometimes mathematical gifts and attainments of this kind; success in these paths.”

Reversed:

> “Vice, weakness, ugliness, perversity, corruption, peril.”

Waite’s additional short significator is “a rather dark man, a merchant, master, professor”; reversed, “an old and vicious man.” The latter retains Mathers/Etteilla.

## Aleister Crowley, 1944

Crowley does not organize *The Book of Thoth* into simple upright/reversed keywords. He gives a spectrum conditioned by dignity.

Positive expression includes concentrated energy applied to practical matters, competence, management, agriculture, and deliberate production. Negative expression includes dullness, stolidity, insensitivity, material fixation, prejudice, and a tendency to elaborate opinions without adequately relating them to facts.

**Absence finding:** A published “Crowley reversed meaning” for the Prince of Disks is generally a modern conversion of his ill-dignified description.

## Paul Foster Case

Case follows the Golden Dawn–Waite stream: a dark man or mature practical organizer; courage, business capacity, executive or mathematical ability; negatively, materialism, corruption, or misuse of practical power.

**Source caution:** I could not verify a freely accessible first-edition page giving a distinct Case upright-and-reversed paragraph for this card. Precise quotations circulating online often reproduce Waite or Mathers without identifying the borrowing.

## Rachel Pollack

In *Seventy-Eight Degrees of Wisdom*, Pollack develops the RWS king as worldly success, satisfaction in achievement and established life, sensual enjoyment, and competent material authority; the shadow becomes complacency, narrow materialism, or power based solely on status and possession.

**Copyright note:** This is a summary rather than an extended quotation. A searchable copy consulted is [here](https://legacy-eutopia.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/02/Seventy-Eight-Degrees-of-Wisdom.pdf).

## Mary K. Greer

Greer treats courts dynamically—as people, roles, modes of behaviour, inner parts, or situational demands. Her reversal method does not require a fixed opposite meaning. A reversed court can indicate:

- refusal to enact the role;
- blocked or undervalued qualities;
- exaggerated or “acting out” behaviour;
- an inner rather than public expression.

Applied to this king, that can mean refusing material responsibility, insecure or corrupt authority, hoarding, or hidden practical competence. [Greer, “Using the Tarot Court Cards”](https://marykgreer.com/2016/03/23/using-the-court-cards/).

## Esoteric and symbolic readings

## Material and social kingship

**First layer: fifteenth century, documented image.** The original card presents wealth as rank, minting authority, court dress, gold, and dynastic sovereignty. A ruler’s portrait or arms on currency made the Coins king a compact conjunction of monarch and money.

## Christian reading

Early tarot’s world is Christian, but the King of Coins is not an explicitly biblical figure. General moral oppositions—liberality versus avarice, stewardship versus miserliness—were available in medieval Christian ethics.

**[Modern extrapolation]** Identifying the card with a particular Magus, Joseph of Arimathea, Solomon, or the rich fool is not supported by the early card captions.

## Alchemical reading

Lévi and later occultists enabled Coins/Pentacles to be understood as fixed matter, salt, coagulation, and material completion.

The Prince’s “solidifying” action and Crowley’s fertile Earth invite the alchemical formula *solve et coagula*, especially coagulation. This is a nineteenth- and twentieth-century Hermetic reading, not documented in the Bembo cards.

## Kabbalistic reading

The Golden Dawn assigns:

- suit of Pentacles to Assiah/Earth;
- Prince to Vav, the Son;
- Prince grade to Tiphareth within the court formula;
- Air of Earth to the organizing, conceptual, mobile aspect of matter.

The chariot expresses mediated motion: Earth does not move itself quickly, so Air plans, orders, communicates, and distributes material force.

## Astrological reading

The bull, Taurus, agriculture, flowers, mathematical disk, and ox-drawn chariot express the fixed Earth portion of the 20° Aries–20° Taurus span. Aries provides propulsion; Taurus consolidates, cultivates, and fructifies.

Waite visually emphasizes Taurus but omits the printed zodiacal attribution. Crowley makes it explicit.

## Hermetic reading

The orb-and-cross represents material manifestation brought under a spiritual or royal organizing principle. The downward-held disk signifies force applied into the material field; the cross-surmounted globe suggests the Great Work realized in matter.

## Jungian reading

**[Modern interpretive tradition]** The king becomes an archetype of the mature provider, builder, steward, embodied father, landholder, or administrator. His shadow is the senex trapped in possession: rigid patriarch, exploiter, miser, status addict, or person who mistakes net worth for self-worth.

Carl Jung did not publish a canonical set of tarot meanings. Calling this “Jung’s meaning of the King of Pentacles” is therefore inaccurate; it is post-Jungian tarot practice.

## Jodorowsky’s reading

Jodorowsky and Marianne Costa treat Marseille cards through visual grammar, number, posture, direction, receptive/active energy, and psychogenealogy. The Roi de Deniers’ off-centre posture and grounded coin can be read as mastery of territory and resources, but also dependence on possession.

These are generative modern interpretations, not recovered explanations written by Conver.

## People and works

### Artists, printers, and occultists directly attached

- **Andrea and Bonifacio Bembo:** competing or collaborative attributions for early Visconti cards.
- **Filippo Maria Visconti and Francesco Sforza:** probable patrons or dynastic owners.
- **Jean Noblet, Jean Dodal, Nicolas Conver:** principal French pattern witnesses.
- **Jean-Baptiste Alliette/Etteilla:** first professional system-builder to give the whole tarot a published divinatory structure.
- **Éliphas Lévi:** linked tarot, Kabbalah and ceremonial magic.
- **Papus/Gérard Encausse:** popularized tarot as the “Book of the Bohemians.”
- **S. L. MacGregor Mathers:** published English meanings and helped formulate Book T.
- **Arthur Edward Waite and Pamela Colman Smith:** created the dominant English-language version.
- **Paul Foster Case and Jessie Burns Parke:** transmitted a Golden Dawn–Waite synthesis through BOTA.
- **Aleister Crowley and Frieda Harris:** transformed the Book T King/Prince into the Prince of Disks.
- **Philippe Camoin and Alejandro Jodorowsky:** produced an influential modern Marseille reconstruction.

### Historians

- **Gertrude Moakley:** pioneering study of the Visconti–Sforza cards and Renaissance triumphal culture.
- **Stuart R. Kaplan:** documentary collecting and reproduction of historic decks.
- **Michael Dummett:** reconstructed tarot primarily as a family of games and established regional trump orders.
- **Ronald Decker and Thierry Depaulis:** with Dummett, traced occult tarot’s documentary history.
- **Robert M. Place:** emphasized iconographic and Renaissance context while rejecting literal Egyptian origins.
- **Helen Farley:** studied tarot’s reinvention in modern occult culture.
- **Andrea Vitali and Le Tarot:** extensive European archival and iconographic research.

### Literary and cinematic associations

- **Italo Calvino, *The Castle of Crossed Destinies*:** characters construct narratives through arranged tarot cards. The work demonstrates tarot as combinatorial literature; it does not establish a historical meaning for this king.
- **T. S. Eliot, *The Waste Land*:** Madame Sosostris names an invented “drowned Phoenician Sailor,” Belladonna, the man with three staves, Wheel, and one-eyed merchant. The King of Pentacles is not named.

- **Ingmar Bergman:** tarot-like medieval imagery is sometimes invoked in discussions of *The Seventh Seal*, but the film’s central game is chess. A direct quotation of the King of Pentacles was not verified.
- **Alejandro Jodorowsky’s films:** saturated in tarot, alchemy, initiation and symbolic bodies, but identifying a particular wealthy ruler as this card requires film-specific evidence.
- **Paintings/buildings:** throne, vine, castle and bull motifs recur widely in European art, but no securely documented pre-1909 building quotation of Smith’s King was found. Modern murals and deck-derived artworks often quote it, usually without art-historical cataloguing.

## Controversies and disputes

## Egyptian origin

**[Legend, first fully influential publication 1781]** Court de Gébelin claimed tarot was an Egyptian hieroglyphic book preserved beneath an innocent card game. He proposed fanciful Egyptian etymologies for *tarot* before Egyptian hieroglyphs had been deciphered. His essay and the Comte de Mellet’s companion text created the “Book of Thoth” tradition. [Primary French text](https://www.tarock.info/gebelin.htm).

**Evidence:** no Egyptian tarot pack, no pharaonic text describing it, and no documentary chain to fifteenth-century Italy. Dummett specifically observed that the alleged Egyptian word derivations supplied no hieroglyphic forms. [Dummett, “Origins of Tarot”](https://www.nybooks.com/articles/1981/05/14/origins-of-tarot/).

**Important distinction:** European suit cards probably do have a material relationship to Mamluk Egyptian cards. That does not validate Gébelin’s claim that the twenty-two trumps encode a pharaonic Book of Thoth.

## “Gypsy” or Romani transmission

**[Legend]** Later occult writers claimed Romani carried tarot from Egypt. The story grew through nineteenth-century authors, particularly Jean-Alexandre Vaillant, and Papus’s title *Tarot des Bohémiens* gave it extraordinary reach.

**Evidence against:** tarot is documented at north-Italian courts before any evidence connects Romani communities with the tarot. Romani linguistic and historical origins lie in India, not ancient Egypt. Decker, Depaulis and Dummett trace the legend’s literary formation in *A Wicked Pack of Cards*. [Book record](https://books.google.com/books?id=nVyDAAAAMAAJ).

The historical term “Gypsy” is retained only when identifying the period claim.

## Cathar and Templar origins

**[Legend/disputed modern speculation]** Tarot trumps have been claimed to encode Cathar doctrine, Templar survival, Grail teaching, or a suppressed heretical catechism.

**Evidence:** no Cathar or Templar tarot cards, rules, inventories, or doctrinal text; the Templars were suppressed more than a century before the documented Italian *trionfi*. The King of Coins supplies no uniquely Cathar or Templar symbol. Its imagery is ordinary court kingship and currency.

Later similarities—crosses, kings, towers, virtues—are too generic to demonstrate transmission.

## Hidden family portraits

**[Disputed]** Early Visconti courts are regularly named as particular dukes and relatives.

Heraldry securely associates the packs with ruling families. Physiognomic identity does not follow automatically. Unless a catalogue or contemporary inscription names the sitter, “possibly Francesco Sforza” should remain a hypothesis.

## The “crown on the ground” in Marseille

**[Disputed visual reading]** Some readers say the Roi de Deniers has removed or dropped his crown, proving that he is a merchant rather than a true king.

Comparative evidence from Noblet and Payen suggests grass/turf. The confused Dodal cutting and Conver’s headgear encouraged the crown theory. No maker’s explanation survives.

## Merchant versus king

**[Tradition/modern reading]** Waite’s secondary meaning includes “merchant, master, professor,” and the suit’s money associations encourage the “businessman” reading. Historically the card is still captioned King. It need not depict a bourgeois merchant masquerading as royalty.

## Pentacle antiquity

**[Frequent misattribution]** The medieval Coins suit is sometimes described as if every old disk contained a pentagram. It did not. Coins might show rulers, floral ornaments, heraldry, or geometric designs. The consistently blazoned five-pointed star is an occult redesign culminating in Golden Dawn and Waite practice.

## “Final Heh”

**[Technical misattribution]** Final Heh belongs to the Princess/Knave, Earth of Earth. The Book T King/Prince is Vav, Air of Earth.

## Crowley publication date

**[Bibliographical dispute]** Harris painted the deck from 1938 to 1943. *The Book of Thoth* appeared in 1944 with reproductions, while commercial full-deck editions appeared later; 1969 is commonly given for the first full colour publication, though limited and prototype printings complicate simple dating. One should distinguish painting, book reproduction, experimental printing, and commercial deck issue.

## Misprints becoming tradition

- **Dodal’s “DENIES”:** likely a spelling/cutting irregularity subsequently treated as meaningful by some visual readers.
- **Conver dates:** later printings from inherited blocks are often casually labelled “1760.”
- **Thoth print errors:** early editions contained colour and title/printing anomalies, but no major error created the Prince of Disks title; that title is intentional.
- **Book T rank labels:** its pairing of “King of Pentacles” with “Prince of the Chariot” is not a typographical accident. It records the Golden Dawn’s remapping of conventional ranks.
- **20° versus 21°:** “from 20° Aries” and “from the 21st degree of Aries” describe the same conventional boundary when degrees are counted ordinally.

## Tzaddi/Heh and Emperor/Star

Crowley’s instruction “Tzaddi is not the Star” led him to exchange the Hebrew-letter assignments of the Emperor and Star while retaining their card numbers. This controversy affects trumps IV and XVII.

It has **no direct effect** on the Prince of Disks, whose Vav/sub-elemental and zodiacal position comes from the court-card system. Invoking the Tzaddi switch to alter this king’s sign is a category error.

## Open questions

1. **Which is absolutely the earliest physical King of Coins?**  
   Cary–Yale is the leading candidate, but dating and workshop attribution remain ranges rather than signed dates.

2. **Andrea or Bonifacio Bembo?**  
   Older catalogues favour Bonifacio; current technical and stylistic work has reopened attribution to Andrea or a workshop collaboration.

3. **Was the Visconti–Sforza king a portrait?**  
   No contemporary identifying inscription is known. Dynastic association is secure; personal identity is not.

4. **What precisely is the object by the Marseille king’s foot?**  
   Turf/vegetation is the strongest comparative reconstruction, but the degraded woodcut line leaves room for disagreement.

5. **How much of Smith’s court imagery came from Waite’s written instructions?**  
   No complete surviving instruction sheet for this card was found. Authorship is properly shared: Waite designed the occult programme; Smith determined the realized visual composition.

6. **Where exactly did the English word “Pentacles” first attach to this tarot suit?**  
   It predates Waite and appears in Mathers, but the precise first printed tarot usage requires a dedicated bibliography of nineteenth-century English translations and magical literature.

7. **Can Etteilla’s astrological attribution for no. 64 be fixed across editions?**  
   Etteilla publications and successor decks differ. The card’s *homme brun/homme vicieux* wording is secure; importing the Golden Dawn’s 20° Aries–20° Taurus span into it is not.

8. **Case’s exact card-specific wording:**  
   A reliable open scan of the relevant first-edition lesson was not located. Later summaries frequently merge Case, Waite and Book T.

9. **Rosenwald/Budapest King:**  
   A collection-grade identification with sheet coordinates remains needed before describing a particular printed King of Coins.

## Conclusions

The King of Pentacles began not as an occult archetype but as the highest court of the Italian suit of **Denari**. Its most likely earliest survivor is the Cary–Yale King of Coins from Visconti Milan. The Morgan card supplies the clearest museum-documented fifteenth-century witness: a dynastic sovereign displaying a heraldic coin.

The Marseille tradition converted that gold-ground courtier into the seated **Roi de Deniers** with broad hat, large floral coin, bright hose and ambiguous turf. Etteilla then made him card 64, “dark man” upright and “vicious man” reversed. Mathers translated and expanded that tradition.

The Golden Dawn performed the decisive esoteric transformation. Its conventional “King of Pentacles” became the chariot-borne **Prince**, Vav and **Air of Earth**, spanning 20° Aries–20° Taurus. Waite retained the traditional title but concealed most of the apparatus inside Smith’s bulls, vines, castle and pentagram. Crowley restored the rank as **Prince of Disks**, and Harris turned the Book T specification into a monumental image of planned, cultivated, mathematically organized matter.

The popular meanings—prosperity, business success, stability, provision, stewardship, sensual abundance, and their shadows of greed, rigidity and corruption—are therefore layered results of Etteilla, Mathers, Golden Dawn elemental theory, Waite’s divinatory list, and twentieth-century psychological tarot. They are not meanings documented on the fifteenth-century cards.
