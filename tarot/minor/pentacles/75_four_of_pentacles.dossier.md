# Four of Pentacles: Research Dossier

## Method and evidence labels

This dossier uses the following labels throughout:

- **[Documented fact]** directly supported by a surviving card, archival record, contemporary printed text, or museum catalogue.
- **[Scholarly reconstruction]** an inference advanced by historians from material, stylistic, documentary, or comparative evidence.
- **[Tradition]** a historically transmitted interpretation whose original proof may be unavailable.
- **[Esoteric attribution]** a deliberately constructed occult correspondence, not a claim about the card’s original game function.
- **[Disputed claim]** a proposition accepted by some writers but challenged or unproved.
- **[Legend]** a historical-origin story unsupported by the surviving evidence.
- **[Modern invention]** a recent symbolic or therapeutic reading not found in the older sources.

Exact quotations are given only where the consulted edition could be checked. Page numbers vary in digitized editions; where stable pagination could not be verified, the chapter or card entry is identified.

### Names across languages and systems

- **Italian:** *quattro di denari*, *quattro di danari*, or *quattro di ori* in regional packs. *Trionfi* originally designated the added trump series or the game, not this particular pip. *Tarocchi* became the usual Italian name for the pack/game during the early sixteenth century.
- **French:** *quatre de deniers*. Marseille-pattern cards normally bear no written card title on the numeral itself.
- **Spanish:** *cuatro de oros*; modern Spanish-language occult decks may say *cuatro de pentáculos*.
- **German:** *Vier der Münzen*; occult translations also use *Vier der Pentakel* or, for Crowley, *Vier der Scheiben*.
- **English:** Four of Coins; Four of Pentacles in Golden Dawn, Waite-Smith, and most English occult decks; Four of Disks in Crowley.
- **Etteilla:** *quatre de deniers*, situated within Etteilla’s reordered Grand Etteilla system.
- **Golden Dawn:** *The Lord of Earthly Power*.
- **Thoth:** *Four of Disks—Power*.

**[Documented fact]** “Pentacles” is not the original historical suit-name. Fifteenth-century cards show coins. In older magical literature a *pentacle* meant a consecrated talisman and did not necessarily contain a five-pointed star. Its systematic replacement of “coins” in English occult tarot belongs principally to the Golden Dawn tradition and its descendants. Waite and Smith then placed an actual pentagram inside every disk, causing *pentacle* to be widely understood as “a coin bearing a five-pointed star.” [Metropolitan Museum overview](https://www.metmuseum.org/de/perspectives/tarot-2), [V&A history of tarot](https://www.vam.ac.uk/articles/tarot-cards), [Waite’s card entry](https://sacred-texts.com/tarot/pkt/pktpe04.htm).

The requested examples “Papess as Juno,” “Pope as Jupiter,” *Maison Dieu*, the Fool’s numbering, and the Justice–Strength exchange concern **trumps**, not the Four of Pentacles. They are discussed below only where necessary to distinguish pack systems; they never changed this pip’s number.

## Iconography deck by deck

### Fifteenth-century Milan

#### Brera–Brambilla, probably before 1447

**[Documented fact]** The Brera–Brambilla pack was made for Filippo Maria Visconti and is now in the Pinacoteca di Brera. Its pip cards use repeated suit emblems on precious grounds.

**Important negative finding:** the **Four of Coins is missing** from the surviving Brambilla group. Claims describing its exact composition are therefore reconstructions or descriptions of modern completion cards, not observations of an original. The deck preserves nearly all its pips but specifically lacks the Four of Denari. [Brera Sola Busca/early tarot guide](https://pinacotecabrera.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/11/Il-Segreto-dei-tarocchi-eng.pdf), [Visconti-Sforza survey](https://www.metmuseum.org/de/perspectives/tarot-2).

#### Cary–Yale / Visconti di Modrone, Milan, probably 1440s

These are two names for the same surviving group, not two independent decks.

**[Documented fact]** Its Coins carry ducal or numismatic devices on gilded or silvered grounds. The numeral cards are essentially heraldic arrangements rather than narrative scenes.

**[Disputed dating]** Dates from about 1441–47 to the 1460s have been proposed. The earlier date partly depends on identification of the coin image with a particular Visconti issue. That numismatic argument is useful but not universally conclusive. Yale describes the early Visconti-family groups collectively as products of approximately 1440–80. [Yale Library](https://library.yale.edu/news/cards-library-partners-work-together-solve-mysteries-rare-tarot-deck?page=1).

The surviving Cary–Yale numeral sequence is missing the Three of Coins, not the Four. The Four therefore provides a plausible candidate for the earliest surviving Four of Coins, subject to the dating dispute.

#### Pierpont Morgan–Bergamo / Visconti-Sforza, about 1450–60

**[Documented fact]** The Four of Coins is a non-scenic pip: four repeated gold coin or heraldic devices disposed symmetrically on an ornamented field. The Morgan assigns the cards probably to Bonifacio Bembo and the Visconti-Sforza court. [Morgan Library collection](https://www.themorgan.org/collection/tarot-cards).

**Meaning in context:** the coin images advertise lineage, authority, magnificence, and patronage, but no contemporary text says the Four itself signified avarice, security, Chesed, or Capricorn. Those are later readings.

### Ferrarese and Este materials

The surviving Este-associated fragments and Ferrarese cards use repeated suit signs and courtly ornament rather than continuous narrative scenes.

**[Documented fact]** Ferrara produced and purchased *carte da trionfi* in the 1440s.  
**[Negative finding]** No securely identified surviving Este Four of Coins supplies a demonstrable iconographic ancestor for Waite’s seated possessor.

### Mantegna “Tarocchi,” about 1465–75

**[Documented fact]** The so-called Mantegna Tarocchi are fifty educational or emblematic engravings arranged in five decades.

**[Scholarly correction]** They are neither a tarot pack nor playing cards and contain no Four of Coins. Their relevance is environmental: they testify to the same Renaissance taste for ranked emblematic series. They are irrelevant to the direct genealogy of this pip.

### Sola Busca, 1491

**[Documented fact]** The Sola Busca is the earliest surviving complete 78-card tarot and the earliest complete tarot in which every numeral card has a pictorial scene. The engraved deck is dated 1491; the famous hand-colored copy is later. It is now associated particularly with the Pinacoteca di Brera. [Brera guide](https://pinacotecabrera.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/11/Il-Segreto-dei-tarocchi-eng.pdf).

The Four of Coins shows a **nude or nearly nude female figure**, outdoors, physically engaged with a chest, container, or large object, while the four coin devices are incorporated around the scene. The composition is emphatically bodily and narrative, unlike the abstract Visconti and Marseille fours.

#### Was it the source of Waite-Smith?

**Finding: only partly, and not securely card-for-card.**

- **[Documented fact]** Photographs of the Sola Busca cards were exhibited at the British Museum in 1908, shortly before Pamela Colman Smith designed the Waite-Smith deck.
- **[Strong scholarly reconstruction]** Several Waite-Smith pip compositions—most famously the Three of Swords and Ten of Wands—show close formal debts to Sola Busca.
- **[Disputed claim]** The Sola Busca Four specifically was the direct source for Smith’s Four of Pentacles. The two cards share the broad idea of a person materially enclosed by four coins, but they differ in sex, dress, posture, setting, and action. The Waite figure more directly literalizes Waite’s divinatory phrase “cleaving to that which one has.” The resemblance is suggestive, not demonstrative. [Sola Busca/Waite comparison](https://waitesmith.org/index.php/decks/sola-busca-vs-waite-smith/), [card comparison](https://wayfindertarot.com/four-of-pentacles-sola-busca/).

Accordingly, the item-specific lead “the Sola Busca pip as the source” should be rewritten: **the deck is a documented available source for Smith’s project, but direct derivation of this exact composition remains an inference.**

### Rosenwald and Budapest sheets, late fifteenth or early sixteenth century

These woodcut sheets preserve inexpensive popular cards, crucially different from luxury Visconti painting.

**[Documented fact]** Their numeral cards use repeated Italian suit signs with functional ornament.  
**[Negative finding]** No published evidence consulted establishes a narrative Four-of-Coins scene comparable to Sola Busca or Waite. Their importance is transmission of standard game-card structure, not the “miser” image.

### Minchiate

Minchiate expanded the trump series to forty, producing a 97-card pack, but retained the ordinary Italian suits.

**[Documented fact]** The Four of Coins remains a repeated-emblem pip. It is not one of the additional zodiac, element, or virtue trumps. Its game rank in the round suits is discussed below. [Minchiate rules and bibliography](https://www.pagat.com/tarot/minchiate.html).

### Bolognese tarot

The Bolognese pack underwent reductions for play. In reduced forms many low numeral cards were removed.

**[Documented fact]** Where present, the Four of Coins is a conventional numeral, not an occult “minor arcanum.” In some shortened Bolognese games it is absent with other low pips.

### Sicilian tarot

**[Documented fact]** The Sicilian pack retains the Four of Coins/Oro as the lowest surviving numeral of that suit in the common reduced pattern: King, Queen, Horse, Maid, 10 down to 4. [Sicilian Tarocchi rules](https://www.pagat.com/tarot/sicilian.html).

### Tarot de Marseille lineage

#### Jean Noblet, Paris, about 1650

The Four of Deniers consists of four large circular coin emblems arranged two above and two below, linked or separated by plant ornament.

#### Jean Dodal, Lyon, about 1701–15

Dodal retains the fourfold coin layout and increases the rhythmic floral scrolling. The plant is ornamental and compositional; there is no contemporary evidence that it encoded a specific alchemical doctrine.

#### Nicolas Conver model, Marseille, dies dated 1760

The familiar Type II Marseille Four has four coins at the corners of a central floral rectangle or lozenge. Stems, leaves, blossoms, and buds create a self-contained, balanced field.

**[Later esoteric reading]** Twentieth-century Marseille writers interpret this enclosure as material stability, a treasury, a house, or matter organized into boundaries. These readings arise from formal analysis; they are not printed on the historical card.

#### Grimaud/Paul Marteau, twentieth century

Grimaud’s *Ancien Tarot de Marseille*, particularly the influential Marteau edition, regularized Conver-like drawing, color, and nomenclature. It helped turn one historical printing family into the apparent “canonical Marseille Tarot.”

#### Camoin–Jodorowsky, 1997

**[Documented fact]** Philippe Camoin and Alejandro Jodorowsky produced a reconstruction/redesign based on several historical packs, principally Conver-line material.

**[Disputed claim]** Its makers sometimes describe restored details as recovery of an ancient sacred code. Historians object that the product necessarily combines sources, editorial choices, and modern redrawing. Its Four of Deniers intensifies the central vegetative symmetry and supports Jodorowsky and Costa’s interpretation of the Four as stabilization of material energy.

### Besançon pattern

The Besançon pattern mainly alters controversial trumps: the Papess becomes **Juno** and the Pope becomes **Jupiter**, particularly in eighteenth- and nineteenth-century Swiss, Alsatian, and southern German production. The pip remains *quatre de deniers*. Thus the regional renaming has no card-specific effect here.

### Etteilla, Grand Etteilla, Paris, 1789 onward

**[Documented fact]** Etteilla reordered the entire tarot, printed upright and reversed meanings, and presented it as the Egyptian Book of Thoth. His suit remains *deniers* rather than Golden Dawn “pentacles.”

Etteilla editions are bibliographically difficult: plates, numbers, and keywords vary between early states, later “Grand Etteilla” editions, and nineteenth-century school manuals.

**[Negative finding]** I could not verify from a dependable facsimile a single stable pair of original keywords for the exact Four of Deniers. Online lists often silently reproduce later Julia Orsini, Lismon, or modern Etteilla editions. They should not be quoted as Etteilla’s own 1780s wording without an edition and plate. An accessible deck overview confirms the card and the printing tradition but not a reliable original semantic transcription. [Original Etteilla deck survey](https://fr.etteilla.org/fr/jeu/10/tarot-original-d-etteilla-le-jeu-de-toth).

### Oswald Wirth, 1889

Wirth designed twenty-two trumps, not a complete illustrated 78-card deck. Therefore:

**[Documented fact]** there is no original 1889 Wirth Four of Pentacles. Later “Wirth” full packs or books that assign it imagery are expansions by other hands.

### Golden Dawn manuscripts and reconstructions

#### Book T, late 1880s–1890s

Book T gives the Four of Pentacles:

> “The Lord of Earthly Power.”

It assigns the card Sun in Capricorn and describes a hand grasping a branch with four pentacles. The full divinatory formula includes:

> “Assured material gain: success, rank, dominion; earthly power completed but leading to nothing beyond.”

Wording varies slightly among manuscript and published recensions. [Book T facsimile/transcription](https://jantetteroo.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/02/Mathers_and_felkin_golden_dawn_book_t_th.pdf).

#### Golden Dawn reconstructions

- **Robert Wang, Golden Dawn Tarot, 1977:** reconstructs the elemental hand, branch, four disks, astrological signs, and Golden Dawn colors.
- **Chic and Sandra Tabatha Cicero, Golden Dawn Magical Tarot, 2000:** another reconstruction, more explicitly keyed to color scales and ritual papers.

**[Scholarly caution]** Neither is an original Golden Dawn-issued mass-market deck. Both are twentieth-century visualizations of manuscript instructions.

### Waite–Smith, London, 1909

Pamela Colman Smith depicts a crowned urban figure seated outside a city:

- one pentacle balanced over the crown;
- one clasped tightly against chest and abdomen;
- two pinned beneath the feet;
- the distant city excluded behind him.

Waite describes it exactly:

> “A crowned figure, having a pentacle over his crown, clasps another with hands and arms; two pentacles are under his feet. He holds to that which he has.”

The design converts a verbal divinatory meaning into a psychologically legible scene. Mind, heart, arms, and locomotion are all occupied by possessions. The city implies wealth and society, yet the figure is isolated from both. [Waite, *The Pictorial Key to the Tarot*, Four of Pentacles](https://sacred-texts.com/tarot/pkt/pktpe04.htm).

**[Modern invention]** Chakra readings—coin blocking crown, heart, and root chakras—are late twentieth- or twenty-first-century superimpositions. Waite never mentions chakras in the card entry.

### BOTA, Paul Foster Case and Jessie Burns Parke

BOTA’s deck returns the numeral cards to geometric, repeated suit emblems rather than Smith’s narrative scenes. The Four therefore displays four pentacles in an ordered pattern.

**[Esoteric attribution]** Case retained the Golden Dawn’s number-element logic while revising some wider tarot doctrines. The BOTA Four emphasizes number, pattern, and meditation rather than a literal miser. [BOTA deck history](https://benebellwen.com/2021/07/16/botatarot/).

### Thoth, designed 1938–43; book 1944; deck published 1969

Frieda Harris, under Aleister Crowley’s direction, replaced the human figure with an architectural mandala: a square fortress viewed from above, with four bastion-like disks at its corners, standing at a crossroads. Solar and Capricornian emblems specify the decan.

Crowley writes:

> “This card refers to Chesed in the suit of Earth. The Sun is in Capricorn, the sign in which he is reborn.”

He continues with the result:

> “The card represents Law and Order, maintained by constant vigilance.”

Its force is defensive and consolidated. Crowley’s further divinatory summary is “assured material gain, success, rank, dominion,” but the design warns that the fortress securing power can also imprison it. [Crowley, *The Book of Thoth*, “The Small Cards”](https://hermetic.com/crowley/book-of-thoth/small-cards).

“Power” therefore does not simply mean domination. It is organized, fortified material power—effective precisely because it has assumed boundaries.

### Notable modern redesigns

- **Marseille revival decks:** restore or reinterpret floral geometry rather than Smith’s character.
- **Aquarian and Morgan-Greer decks:** preserve the clutching possessor and emphasize facial psychology.
- **Thoth derivatives:** preserve fortress, square, solar, or Capricorn motifs.
- **Feminist and therapeutic decks:** often recast the figure as establishing boundaries or conserving limited resources.
- **Anti-capitalist decks:** turn the card into hoarding, enclosure, landlordism, or concentrated wealth.
- **Modern “release” reversals:** depict opening hands, circulating coins, mutual aid, or divestment.

All of these are **modern inventions**, even when they are legitimate reinterpretations of inherited imagery.

## The game

### Original function

**[Documented fact]** Tarot originated as a northern Italian trick-taking family. A conventional full pack contained:

- 21 permanent trumps;
- the Fool;
- four suits of fourteen cards: King, Queen, Knight, Page, and ten numerals.

The Four of Coins was a plain suited card. It had no permanent trumping power, occult title, astrological dignity, or special scoring value.

### Rank

In many historical Italian tarot games the two “round” suits, Cups and Coins, ranked in reverse numerical order:

**King, Queen, Knight, Page, Ace, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10.**

Thus the Four of Coins beats the Five through Ten but loses to Ace through Three and the courts. In Swords and Batons the sequence commonly runs King down through Ten to Ace. [Historical-play summary](https://www.knauer.org/mike/sca/classes/Class_-_Game_of_Tarot.pdf).

In Minchiate, Coins likewise rank Ace high, then 2, 3, 4, continuing downward toward 10. [Minchiate rules](https://www.pagat.com/tarot/minchiate.html).

In modern French Tarot, German Tarock, and Austrian games, packs often use French suits or are stripped so severely that no Four of Coins exists. Where an Italian-suited Four remains, its rank depends on the local rules.

### Value

**[Documented fact]** It is normally a low-value counter or blank card, not an honor. Exact counting varies by game, but the valuable cards are typically kings, selected trumps, and the Fool. The Four’s strategic value comes from taking a trick, voiding a suit, or being discarded—not from a fixed esoteric valuation.

### Fool and trump-order disputes

These do not alter the Four’s identity but locate it historically:

- The Fool commonly functions as an excuse: it may be played without following suit and is usually recovered by its player, subject to compensation rules.
- The Fool was originally unnumbered. Etteilla’s occult pack helped popularize zero; later French occultists placed it at different positions.
- Historians distinguish regional trump orders, conventionally called types A, B, and C, associated broadly with Bologna/Ferrara, Milan, and later southern/French traditions.
- Justice, Fortitude, and other trumps varied in relative order before occult decks fixed printed numbers.
- Waite exchanged the Marseille positions of Justice and Strength: Strength VIII, Justice XI.
- Crowley retained that exchange.
- None of this applies to a numbered pip: the Four remains four.

## Chronology of attestation

### About 1420: Marziano da Tortona

Marziano’s text for Filippo Maria Visconti describes a special card game with sixteen classical gods and four bird suits.

**[Documented fact]** It is an ancestor or close relative of the idea of an added trump series.  
**[Scholarly correction]** It is not the standard 78-card tarot and contains no Four of Coins.

### 1440: Florence

A record mentions *naibi a trionfi* supplied to Sigismondo Pandolfo Malatesta. This is currently earlier than the long-famous Ferrara entries.

### 1442: Ferrara accounts

Este court ledgers record the purchase or production of hand-painted *carte da trionfi*.

**[Documented fact]** These are among the earliest certain tarot records.  
**[Negative finding]** They name packs, not individual pip cards.

### 1440s–1460s: surviving Visconti-family cards

The first material evidence for the Four itself appears in the Milanese painted packs, although the exact sequence of the Cary–Yale and Pierpont Morgan groups remains disputed.

### About 1470–1500: *Sermo perutilis de ludo cum aliis*

The anonymous sermon condemns games and lists the tarot trumps in order.

**[Documented fact]** It is essential for reconstructing a fifteenth-century trump series.  
**Card-specific limitation:** it does not separately name the Four of Coins.

### 1491: Sola Busca

The first surviving fully pictorial Four of Coins.

### About 1500: Ferrarese tarot poems

Poems assign ladies or personalities to particular cards, providing evidence of playful or allegorical card association.

**[Documented fact]** Such literary uses predate formal tarot cartomancy.  
**[Caution]** They do not establish the later Four-of-Pentacles meanings.

### 1520s: Teofilo Folengo

Folengo’s *tarocchi appropriati* associates tarot cards with people in literary play.

Again, this is evidence for cultural and poetic use, not a Golden Dawn-like divinatory system.

### Seventeenth century: Marseille lineage

The abstract four-coin floral arrangement stabilizes in printed French decks.

### 1781: Court de Gébelin and Comte de Mellet

Gébelin declares tarot an Egyptian book; de Mellet proposes a Hebrew-letter scheme and a method of divination.

**Card-specific limitation:** their principal attention is the trumps. They do not provide the mature Sun-in-Capricorn/Chesed doctrine of the Four.

### 1783–91: Etteilla

Etteilla creates the first systematic published occult tarot program and the first purpose-designed divinatory tarot deck.

### 1854–56: Éliphas Lévi

*Dogme et rituel de la haute magie* makes tarot a key to ceremonial magic and Kabbalah.

Lévi’s often-quoted claim is:

> “An imprisoned person, with no other book than the Tarot, if he knew how to use it, could in a few years acquire universal knowledge…”

**[Esoteric attribution]** Lévi’s detailed Hebrew-letter architecture concerns the twenty-two trumps. He does not attest Sun in Capricorn for the Four of Coins.

### 1863: Paul Christian

Christian develops an elaborate Egyptian initiation narrative and helps introduce “Major” and “Minor Arcana.”

### 1888: Mathers

Mathers publishes an English meaning for every card. For the Four of Pentacles:

> “Pleasure, Gaiety, Enjoyment, Satisfaction; Reversed Meaning: Obstacles, Hindrances.”

This is materially different from Waite’s later possession/inheritance reading. [Mathers, *The Tarot: Its Occult Signification*](https://hermetics.net/media-library/tarot/samuel-liddell-macgregor-mathers-the-tarot/).

### 1888 onward: Golden Dawn

Book T supplies “Lord of Earthly Power,” Sun in Capricorn, Chesed in Earth, and the interpretive line from assured gain to sterile consolidation.

### 1889: Papus and Wirth

Papus’s *Le Tarot des Bohémiens* synthesizes tarot, Hebrew letters, number, and Tetragrammaton. It disseminates the supposed “Bohemian” or Romani connection. Wirth supplies the trump designs, not an illustrated Four.

### 1909–10: Waite-Smith and *Pictorial Key*

The human possessor becomes the dominant English-language image.

### 1938–44/1969: Thoth

Crowley and Harris turn possession into fortified geometric power.

### Late twentieth century onward

Pollack, Greer, Jodorowsky/Costa, Jungian writers, and therapeutic readers reinterpret the enclosure as ego structure, boundaries, trauma-defense, fear of scarcity, or necessary conservation.

### Sephirah 4

**[Esoteric attribution]** Four corresponds to **Chesed**, Mercy, the fourth Sephirah. In Golden Dawn minor-card doctrine, every Four expresses Chesed through one elemental suit. In Pentacles this becomes **Chesed of Assiah**, or expansive ordering power fixed in the material world.

This produces a real tension:

- Chesed is expansion, generosity, authority, and beneficence.
- Capricorn and Earth consolidate, limit, and define.
- The Sun supplies centered power and visible authority.
- The result is wealth or dominion successfully secured—but liable to stagnate.

### Sun in Capricorn

The Four of Pentacles is the third decan of Capricorn, conventionally **20°–30° Capricorn**. This attribution is documented in Golden Dawn Book T and retained by Crowley. It is not medieval astrology encoded secretly in Marseille pips.

### Final Heh

The Golden Dawn assigns the four elemental suits to the four letters of YHVH. Pentacles/Earth corresponds to final **Heh**, the daughter/princess or material completion of the formula.

**Clarification:** final Heh belongs to the suit and elemental world. It is not a separate Hebrew letter assigned uniquely to this card.

### Tzaddi/Heh controversy

Crowley’s “Tzaddi is not the Star” revision concerns the trumps Emperor and Star and their Hebrew letters. It does not change the Four of Disks, whose relevant Heh is the final Heh of YHVH, a different application. Conflating these two Heh questions is an error.

## Divinatory meanings by author

### Etteilla

**[Unresolved primary wording]** Later Grand Etteilla manuals commonly associate the Four of Deniers with material possession, order, or a bounded material condition and print a separate reversed sense.

However, an exact pair “in Etteilla’s own words” could not be securely established from the accessible early facsimiles consulted. Variant decks and later manuals have been repeatedly conflated. No quotation is offered as Etteilla’s without a verified edition.

### S. L. MacGregor Mathers, 1888

> “Pleasure, Gaiety, Enjoyment, Satisfaction.”

Reversed:

> “Obstacles, Hindrances.”

This comes from *The Tarot: Its Occult Signification, Use in Fortune-Telling, and Method of Play*, entry 74 in the consulted scan. It demonstrates that the “miser” meaning was not yet inevitable.

### Golden Dawn Book T

Well dignified:

> “Assured material gain: success, rank, dominion; earthly power completed but leading to nothing beyond.”

Ill dignified, the power becomes suspicious, covetous, prejudiced, or constrained. Manuscript recensions differ in punctuation and some adjectives.

### A. E. Waite, 1910

> “The surety of possessions, cleaving to that which one has, gift, legacy, inheritance.”

Reversed:

> “Suspense, delay, opposition.”

Waite does **not** use “greed” or “miser” in the formal card entry, although Smith’s picture readily generated those later keywords. [*Pictorial Key*, Four of Pentacles](https://sacred-texts.com/tarot/pkt/pktpe04.htm).

### Aleister Crowley, 1944

Crowley treats the card as:

> “Law and Order, maintained by constant vigilance.”

and as:

> “assured material gain, success, rank, dominion.”

Crowley generally interprets elemental dignity rather than printing separate reversed meanings. A badly situated Four of Disks becomes fortress mentality, stagnation, restriction, or power defended for its own sake. [*Book of Thoth*, “Small Cards”](https://hermetic.com/crowley/book-of-thoth/small-cards).

### Paul Foster Case

Case’s accessible public works concentrate more heavily on the major keys; BOTA’s full pip instruction circulated substantially through lessons.

**[Reconstruction from the BOTA system]** The Four combines orderly formation with Earth/Pentacles: material order, conservation, established possession, and the danger of excessive fixation. No securely verified Case sentence unique to this card was found in the sources consulted, so none is placed in quotation marks.

### Rachel Pollack

In *Seventy-Eight Degrees of Wisdom*, Pollack reads the Waite-Smith figure as the mind imposing structure on the material universe. Structure makes human life possible but becomes sterile when treated as absolute. The man’s coins close him off from ground, feeling, action, and community.

**Reversed:** release, loosening fixed structures, or—in a less favorable context—the collapse of necessary security.

This is an influential **modern psychological/Jungian development**, not Waite’s own wording. [Accessible edition](https://legacy-eutopia.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/02/Seventy-Eight-Degrees-of-Wisdom.pdf).

### Mary K. Greer

Greer’s *Complete Book of Tarot Reversals* does not reduce reversal to a single antonym. A reversed card may be blocked, delayed, excessive, internalized, projected, resisted, misused, or breaking through.

Applied to the Four of Pentacles:

- upright: holding, possession, conservation, control, boundaries;
- reversed: opening or relinquishing; resources leaking away; financial insecurity; rigidity becoming excessive; or the need to create an internal boundary.

This flexible method dates particularly to the 2002 book and helped spread therapeutic readings of reversal. [Publisher’s edition record](https://www.llewellyn.com/product.php?ean=9781567182859).

## Esoteric and symbolic readings

### Alchemical

**[Later esoteric attribution]** The card can be read as coagulation: matter fixed into stable form. The four disks are a quaternity of manifested elements; the square fortress is the alchemical vessel or protected work.

No evidence places this meaning on the fifteenth-century Four of Coins. It grows from nineteenth-century occult synthesis and is made visually persuasive by Harris’s Thoth design.

### Kabbalistic

The card is Chesed in Assiah: the expansive ordering principle of Mercy operating in material manifestation. Its paradox is generosity converted into possession. Authority can steward resources or monopolize them.

First securely systematized for this card in Golden Dawn materials; disseminated by Crowley, Case, Wang, and Cicero.

### Astrological

Sun in the third decan of Capricorn:

- Sun: center, sovereignty, vitality, rank;
- Capricorn: structure, limit, administration, duration;
- Earth: material actuality.

The result is sustainable authority, institutional wealth, and defended territory. Under adverse dignity it becomes pride in position, inflexibility, and fear of dispossession.

### Hermetic

Four establishes a stable field. The disks define the corners of manifested space. Power becomes operative by limitation: a wall protects because it excludes.

Crowley’s fortress is the clearest visual statement of this doctrine.

### Christian

**[Modern or weakly traditional reading]** The clutching figure can embody avarice, one of the capital sins; inheritance and stewardship offer the contrary Christian themes. “Lay not up for yourselves treasures upon earth” is frequently invoked by modern readers.

There is no evidence that Waite identified the figure explicitly as a Christian personification of Avarice, nor that the medieval pip was designed as such.

### Jungian and psychological

**[Modern invention]**

- the coins form an ego-shell;
- the city represents collective life from which possessiveness alienates the subject;
- the crowned coin suggests identification of self with status;
- the chest coin armors feeling;
- the foot coins prevent grounded movement despite appearing to provide security;
- reversal may signify either healthy release or loss of psychological containment.

Pollack was a major spreader of this interpretive direction. Later trauma-informed readers often rehabilitate the card: holding tightly may be an adaptive response to deprivation before it becomes pathology.

### Socioeconomic

A modern political-economic reading sees:

- enclosure of common resources;
- capital made sterile by hoarding;
- property as both protection and separation;
- the contradiction between private security and social circulation.

This is not present in Waite or Book T as an explicit doctrine, though their language of dominion and possession makes it possible.

## People and works

### Directly attached

- **Filippo Maria Visconti, Francesco Sforza, and the Visconti-Sforza family:** patrons of the earliest surviving tarot groups.
- **Bonifacio Bembo:** probable artist or workshop attribution for major parts of the Visconti-family cards; attribution remains partly stylistic.
- **Anonymous Sola Busca engraver and later colorist:** produced the first fully scenographic Four.
- **Jean Noblet, Jean Dodal, Nicolas Conver:** major makers in the Marseille lineage.
- **Jean-Baptiste Alliette/Etteilla:** first major systematic tarot cartomancer and creator of a purpose-designed occult deck.
- **S. L. MacGregor Mathers:** published the 1888 English meaning and participated in Book T.
- **A. E. Waite:** supplied the occult/cartomantic framework and published description.
- **Pamela Colman Smith:** created the defining modern scene.
- **Paul Foster Case and Jessie Burns Parke:** BOTA system and deck.
- **Aleister Crowley and Frieda Harris:** Thoth conception and painting.
- **Robert Wang; Chic and Sandra Tabatha Cicero:** later Golden Dawn reconstructions.
- **Rachel Pollack and Mary K. Greer:** psychological and reader-centered reinterpretations.
- **Alejandro Jodorowsky and Marianne Costa:** Marseille numerological/body-energy reading.
- **Philippe Camoin:** Jodorowsky’s collaborator in the 1997 Marseille reconstruction.

### Literature and film

The requested names—T. S. Eliot, W. B. Yeats, Italo Calvino, Ingmar Bergman, and Jodorowsky—are important to tarot reception, but direct Four-of-Pentacles links require restraint.

- **Calvino, *The Castle of Crossed Destinies*:** characters construct narratives from tarot layouts. **No uniquely canonical Four-of-Coins episode was securely traced in the consulted material.**
- **Eliot, *The Waste Land*:** Madame Sosostris names several invented or altered tarot cards. The Four of Pentacles is not among them.
- **Yeats:** Golden Dawn member and tarot user; no securely documented work centered on this card was found.
- **Bergman:** tarot-like medieval and allegorical imagery appears in reception discussions of his films, especially *The Seventh Seal*, but no documented quotation of the Four was traced.
- **Jodorowsky:** directly relevant through the Camoin deck and *The Way of Tarot*; tarot also permeates his films and comics, but treating any miserly or square composition as a quotation of this card would be speculative.

### Paintings and buildings

**Negative finding:** no historically documented pre-1909 painting or building can presently be identified as quoting the Waite-Smith Four, for the obvious chronological reason, and no well-attested famous building was traced as an intentional quotation of Harris’s fortress. Modern artworks frequently reproduce the card, but claims of architectural quotation need artist or architect testimony.

## Controversies and disputes

### Egyptian origin

Court de Gébelin claimed in 1781 that tarot preserved an Egyptian Book of Thoth and offered fanciful Egyptian etymologies for *tarot*.

**Label:** **[Legend]**.

Evidence against:

- standard tarot appears in fifteenth-century northern Italian records;
- its suits belong to the history of European and Mamluk-derived playing cards;
- no Egyptian tarot pack, text, or transmission chain survives;
- Gébelin wrote before Egyptian hieroglyphs were deciphered;
- he supplied no hieroglyphic originals for his proposed etymology.

Dummett emphasized that the documented occult history begins with Gébelin rather than antiquity. [Dummett, “Origins of Tarot”](https://www.nybooks.com/articles/1981/05/14/origins-of-tarot/).

### Romani or “Gypsy” transmission

Papus’s title *Le Tarot des Bohémiens* and nineteenth-century writers helped spread the claim that Romani people carried tarot from Egypt or India.

**Label:** **[Legend/disputed claim]**.

Romani migration history and tarot history do not supply a documented transmission chain. The association was strengthened by the mistaken European belief that Romani people came from Egypt and by their later cultural association with fortune-telling. It is also historically misleading to treat “Gypsy” as a neutral ethnic description.

### Cathar and Templar origins

Claims that the trumps encode Cathar theology, Templar secrets, Baphomet, or a persecuted underground church are **[modern legends or disputed symbolic readings]**.

No fifteenth-century ledger, rule, sermon, or surviving Four of Coins identifies such a program. Moreover, the Four is an ordinary suit card inherited from playing-card structure, making a unique Templar origin particularly implausible.

A modern interpreter may construct a Templar or Cathar reading, but that is reception history, not provenance.

### Ancient Kabbalah

**[Esoteric attribution, not documented origin]** Hebrew letters and Tree-of-Life paths were mapped onto tarot by eighteenth- and nineteenth-century occultists. The Four’s identity with Chesed in Assiah belongs to the Golden Dawn synthesis. There is no evidence that the Milanese painters encoded this scheme.

### “Sola Busca directly copied”

The overall influence on Smith is strong; the exact Four-to-Four derivation is weaker.

- Sola Busca was available to her historical environment.
- Some cards show close visual parallels.
- The Four cards share enclosure by coins.
- But the compositions are not duplicates.

The responsible label is **[plausible scholarly reconstruction]**, not documented fact.

### “Waite invented the miser”

Waite’s printed terms are “surety of possessions” and “cleaving.” Smith supplies the bodily drama. Neither calls him “the Miser” in the official entry.

**Finding:** the popular “miser/greed” keyword is a later interpretive condensation, although it is a natural reading of the 1909 image.

### Marseille plant symbolism

Some readers find a purse, enclosure, vulva, garden, heraldic device, or alchemical vessel in the central ornament.

**Label:** normally **[modern interpretation]** unless supported by a maker’s text. Repeated carving and copying can also turn ordinary foliage into apparently intentional hidden figures.

### Conver “1760”

The date 1760 appears in the Conver model or dies, but some surviving impressions catalogued as “Conver” were printed later by successor workshops.

**Finding:** “Conver 1760” often names a pattern/date on the blocks rather than proving that every surviving physical deck was pulled in 1760.

### Misprints that became tradition

No single demonstrable misprint was found that uniquely transformed the meaning of this card. More general transmission problems include:

- **IIII** being “corrected” to IV;
- later Etteilla captions being quoted as if they came from the first edition;
- “Coins” being retroactively renamed Pentacles in medieval decks;
- Golden Dawn titles being printed without “Lord of”;
- “Sun in Capricorn” being presented as an ancient Marseille secret;
- modern completion cards being mistaken for missing Visconti originals.

## Open questions

1. Which surviving Four of Coins is materially earliest: Cary–Yale or another Visconti-family specimen? The answer depends on unresolved deck dating.

2. Can the exact early Etteilla upright and reversed keywords for the Four of Deniers be established from a collated set of first-state plates rather than later manuals?

3. How much instruction did Waite give Smith for individual pips? Waite’s published entry explains the concept, but the surviving evidence does not provide a complete card-by-card art-direction archive.

4. Did Smith consciously adapt the Sola Busca Four, or did she independently picture “cleaving to possessions” after seeing the deck more generally?

5. What did the Sola Busca Four’s original figure and object signify? Readings involving alchemy, satire, classical history, or initiation remain contested.

6. Were particular floral structures on early French Fours heraldic or symbolic, or chiefly workshop ornament? Maker testimony is absent.

7. Which Book T wording is chronologically earliest? Surviving manuscripts and later printed recensions contain small verbal differences.

8. Exactly how much of BOTA’s minor-card teaching was Case’s own revision rather than a pedagogical restatement of Golden Dawn material? Public and members-only sources need careful separation.

---

## Conclusions

The Four began as an ordinary game card: four representations of money in a suit of Coins. Its earliest surviving Milanese forms are courtly, heraldic, and non-narrative. The Sola Busca transformed it into a scene, but the direct connection between that exact scene and Pamela Colman Smith’s card remains plausible rather than proved. Marseille printers instead developed an abstract floral enclosure.

The decisive esoteric change came from the Golden Dawn. “Lord of Earthly Power,” Chesed in Assiah, final Heh/Earth, and Sun in Capricorn are late nineteenth-century constructions, not recovered medieval meanings. Waite and Smith humanized the formula as a possessor immobilized by what he owns. Crowley and Harris abstracted it again into a fortified square: material power secured through law, order, boundary, and vigilance.

Its modern ambivalence follows directly from those designs. The Four can mean stability, stewardship, conservation, inheritance, and boundaries—or greed, scarcity-fear, enclosure, stagnation, and isolation. Historically these are layers, not a single timeless meaning.
