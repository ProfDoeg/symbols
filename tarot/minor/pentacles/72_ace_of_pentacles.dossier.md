# Ace of Pentacles: Research Dossier

## Method and evidentiary labels

This dossier uses the following labels:

- **Documented fact** — supported by a surviving card, archival record, or primary printed text.
- **Scholarly reconstruction** — an inference made from surviving objects, rules, or records; plausible but not directly stated by a contemporary witness.
- **Tradition** — a historically transmitted practice whose initial formulation may be uncertain.
- **Esoteric attribution** — a deliberate occult correspondence, not a claim established by card-game history.
- **Disputed claim** — asserted by identifiable writers but contested by historians or competing evidence.
- **Legend** — a narrative lacking contemporary supporting evidence.
- **Modern invention** — a comparatively recent interpretation or design presented without an older documented pedigree.

Translations are mine unless an edition is identified.

## Basic identification

| Field | Identification |

| English | Ace of Coins; Ace of Pentacles; in Crowley, Ace of Disks |

Two terminological corrections are essential:

1. **Documented fact:** Renaissance and Marseille cards show a coin, medal, shield, or ornamental roundel. They were *denari/deniers*, not five-pointed stars.
2. **Esoteric attribution:** “Pentacles,” Earth, Kether of Assiah, and “Root of the Powers of Earth” belong to the nineteenth-century Hermetic/Golden Dawn transformation of the suit. Waite and Smith popularized that transformation in English-speaking tarot.

The Ace is therefore not “the first Minor Arcana card” in any universal historical sequence. Decks were organized and counted in different ways. In Etteilla’s reordered pack it became card 77; in most historic gaming packs it had no overall serial number.

## The names and their development

### Italian

**Documented fact:** Fifteenth- and sixteenth-century sources call the added trump series *trionfi* and later *tarocchi*. The four ordinary suits remained swords, cups, batons, and coins: *spade, coppe, bastoni,* and *denari*. The present card is consequently the *asso di denari*.

The names *arcani maggiori* and *arcani minori* are occult-era classifications, not the normal vocabulary of the fifteenth-century players. “Arcana” became particularly influential through Paul Christian and later French occultism.

### French

**Documented fact:** The Marseille family calls the suit *Deniers*. The card is the *As de Deniers*. *Denier* is an ordinary word for a coin or monetary unit.

“Tarot de Marseille” itself is a retrospective pattern-name. It should not be read as evidence that Marseille invented tarot. Surviving seventeenth-century French packs were made in Paris, Lyon, Marseille, and elsewhere.

### English

“Coins” is the direct historical translation. “Pentacles” became standard in the English occult tradition through Golden Dawn usage and the Waite-Smith deck. Smith placed an actual pentagram on the gold disk, making the linguistic change visually explicit.

### Spanish

Latin-suited Spanish cards call the suit *oros*, conventionally translated “golds.” The Ace is *As de oros*. Modern Spanish tarot literature also uses *pentáculos* and *monedas*, especially for Waite-Smith-derived decks.

### German

Gaming terminology depends on the pack: *Münzen* for Coins, while French-suited Tarock uses diamonds rather than the Italian coin suit. Occult translations employ *Pentakel*; Thoth editions use *Scheiben* for Disks.

### Etteilla and Crowley

Etteilla retained *deniers*. His Ace is card **77**, labeled *Parfait Contentement* upright and *Bourse d’Argent* reversed.

Crowley deliberately rejected “Coins”:

> “Nor are the Disks any more to be considered as Coins; the Disk is a whirling emblem.”

His card is therefore the **Ace of Disks**, while retaining the Golden Dawn subtitle “The Root of the Powers of the Earth.” [Crowley, *The Book of Thoth*, “The Small Cards”](https://hermetic.com/crowley/book-of-thoth/small-cards)

## Iconography, deck by deck

## Mamluk precursor, fifteenth century or earlier

**Documented fact:** The near-complete Mamluk pack discovered in the Topkapı Palace contains four suits: cups, coins, swords, and polo-sticks. Each has ten numeral cards and three court ranks. The cards employ repeated suit-signs, calligraphy, and geometric ornament rather than human court figures.

The coin suit is labeled through forms of *darāhim*, “dirhams/coins.” L. A. Mayer published the pack in 1939. Michael Dummett and Kamal Abu-Deeb subsequently analyzed its peculiar suit ranking.

**Scholarly reconstruction:** European Latin suits are closely related to this suit system. Coins required less visual reinterpretation than polo-sticks, which became batons. The evidence supports transmission or common ancestry, but it does not identify a surviving Mamluk card as the direct workshop model of the Milanese Ace. [Mayer, “Mamluk Playing Cards”](https://www.persee.fr/doc/bifao_0255-0962_1939_num_38_1_1998); [Dummett and Abu-Deeb](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/10.2307/751160)

## Visconti di Modrone / Cary-Yale, Milan, circa 1440s–1450s

“Visconti di Modrone” and “Cary-Yale” are names for the same surviving group, not two independent decks.

**Documented fact:** This exceptionally luxurious Milanese pack used gold and silver and had an expanded court structure, apparently including male and female attendants and mounted figures. It demonstrates that the suit of Coins was present in the earliest surviving tarot milieu.

**Open question:** Catalogues and reproductions do not give an entirely secure reconstruction of every missing pip. Claims that a particular isolated Ace is definitively the earliest Ace of Coins should therefore specify its present collection and attribution rather than treating the reconstructed deck as complete.

## Brambilla-Brera deck, Milan, mid-fifteenth century

**Documented fact:** This is another fragmentary Visconti-associated luxury pack, traditionally connected with Bonifacio Bembo or his circle. Many suit cards survive, but the deck is incomplete.

**Finding:** No distinctive, securely traceable Ace-of-Coins innovation comparable to the later Golden Dawn hand or Smith garden can be assigned to it. Its evidentiary importance is the Milanese luxury-pip tradition—gold ground, heraldic signs, and court patronage—not an occult pentacle.

## Visconti-Sforza, Milan, approximately 1450–1480

The surviving cards are divided between the Morgan Library, the Accademia Carrara, and the Colleoni family.

**Documented iconography:** The Ace of Coins is a large, ornate golden circular medallion with a red shield at its center, surrounded by delicate gold work and floral ornament. It contains one suit-sign, monumentalized to fill the card. It has neither a pentagram nor a cloud-hand nor a landscape.

The Morgan identifies the group as probably produced for the Visconti-Sforza family and associated with Bonifacio Bembo. It also emphasizes that the pack was created for play, not originally as occult paraphernalia. [Morgan catalogue](https://www.themorgan.org/manuscript/158525); [public-domain image](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Bembo-Visconti-tarot-coins-01-ace.jpg)

**Meaning in context:** The object can support readings about wealth, dynastic display, patronage, heraldry, or precious materials. Those are contextual art-historical observations. They are not proof that fifteenth-century users divined “prosperity” from the card.

## Ferrarese and Este cards, late fifteenth century

**Documented fact:** Ferrarese courts commissioned and purchased *carte da trionfi*. Surviving Este-associated fragments show different artistic vocabularies from Milanese packs.

**Finding:** Because the relevant packs are fragmentary and their attribution has repeatedly shifted, a continuous, deck-by-deck evolution of the Ace cannot be reconstructed. It is safer to report absence than to fill the gaps using modern facsimile cards.

## Mantegna “Tarocchi,” circa 1460s–1470s

**Documented fact:** The so-called Mantegna Tarocchi are two engraved educational or emblematic series of fifty images. They are neither by Mantegna nor a standard tarot pack.

**Not relevant to the Ace:** They contain no ordinary four-suit sequence and no Ace of Coins. Their graded cosmos later interested occultists, but they are not an ancestor attested for this card.

## Sola Busca, engraved 1491; painted copy probably later

**Documented fact:** The Sola Busca is the earliest substantially complete 78-card tarot to survive and the earliest surviving complete tarot in which all numeral cards receive pictorial treatment. The Italian state acquired the celebrated colored pack in 2009 and assigned it to the Pinacoteca di Brera. [Le Tarot/Brera summary](https://www.letarot.it/Sola-Busca-Tarot_pag_pg824_eng.aspx?lng=ENG)

The suit is still Coins, not occult Pentacles. Its numeral scenes feature tools, vessels, bodies, and emblematic actions in relation to the suit objects.

**Caution:** Internet descriptions frequently confuse individual Sola Busca coin cards. The much-reproduced chest of disks with a small figure belongs to a numbered coin card, not automatically to the Ace. I found no sufficiently authoritative, card-specific Brera catalogue entry allowing a confident prose description of the Ace beyond its single monumental coin-sign. Consequently, claims that Smith copied her Ace of Pentacles from the Sola Busca are unsupported.

**Documented influence elsewhere:** Smith demonstrably borrowed compositions from Sola Busca for several other Waite-Smith pip cards, particularly the Three of Swords and Ten of Wands. That does not establish it as the source of every illustrated Smith pip.

## Rosenwald sheets, probably late fifteenth or early sixteenth century

**Documented fact:** The Rosenwald material consists of uncut woodcut sheets, not a complete bound deck. The Ace of Coins shows a central coin or shield-like disk with distinctive animal or heraldic ornament.

**Disputed:** Its place and date have been variously argued as Florentine, Bolognese, or central Italian. Comparisons with later Bolognese *tarocchini*, whose Aces of Coins are very similar, support a Bolognese relationship, but do not prove the exact production site. [Comparative discussion and images](https://www.tarotforum.net/threads/bologna-tarot-questions.251970/page-4)

## Budapest sheets, late fifteenth or early sixteenth century

**Documented fact:** These are fragmentary early printed tarot sheets, important for the transition from luxury painted cards to reproducible woodcut packs.

**Finding:** Published online records do not supply a securely identified surviving Ace of Coins with enough resolution and catalogue documentation to support a separate iconographic genealogy. It should not be silently reconstructed from modern completed editions.

## Minchiate, Florence, sixteenth century onward

Minchiate expanded the trump series to make a 97-card pack but retained the ordinary Italian suits.

**Documented iconography:** The Ace of Coins remains a large decorative single coin/roundel. It is a suit card, not one of the additional astrological or elemental trumps.

**Important distinction:** Because Minchiate adds an Earth trump among its expanded subjects, it is tempting to interpret its Ace of Coins as inherently “elemental Earth.” That identification is an occult inference, not a rule or title printed on the Ace.

## Bolognese tarocchino

The Bolognese pack reduced the low numeral cards in play, conventionally omitting Twos through Fives, while retaining Aces and higher pips.

**Documented iconography:** Surviving seventeenth- and eighteenth-century examples monumentalize the single coin with decorative animals, foliage, heraldic devices, or workshop marks. The Rosenwald Ace is closely comparable.

**Game consequence:** The Ace survived the reduction and functions as the lowest retained numeral in the coin suit, though its trick-taking rank can be high within the reversed-ranking round suits.

## Sicilian tarot

**Documented fact:** Sicilian tarot developed a distinctive reduced pack and trump system. Its Latin suits include Coins; surviving regional Italian cards often enlarge the Ace into a heraldic or tax-bearing display card.

**Finding:** The suit card is not the source of the Golden Dawn pentacle. Regional substitutions such as Atlas or altered trump subjects concern the trumps, not the Ace.

## Tarot de Marseille lineage

### Jean Noblet, Paris, circa 1650

The pack is incomplete but is the earliest substantially surviving example of what is now called the Type I Marseille pattern.

The Ace of Coins, where supplied in restorations, is a large central ornamental disk. Care is necessary: restoration editions replace missing cards, so a modern Noblet Ace may be a reasoned reconstruction rather than a surviving Noblet impression.

### Jean Dodal, Lyon, circa 1701–1715

The *As de Deniers* is a large flower-like coin with concentric borders and surrounding vegetal flourishes. It is neither a five-pointed star nor a narrative scene.

### Nicolas Conver, Marseille, 1760

The Ace is a single elaborate yellow roundel with colored petals or foliage arranged around it. It operates visually as both coin and mandala-like workshop ornament.

### Maker’s card and tax stamp

**Documented fact:** French and Italian cardmakers commonly used low or otherwise conspicuous suit cards to carry maker, place, fiscal, or workshop information. The Two of Coins is particularly famous in Marseille tarot because its crossed ribbons conveniently accommodate the maker’s name and date. Tax regulations and practice varied by jurisdiction and period.

**Correction to the prompt:** “The Ace of Coins as the maker’s card” is not a universal Marseille rule. Some regional packs or later tax regimes placed identifying or fiscal material on an Ace, but in classic Marseille tarot the **Two of Coins**, not consistently the Ace, is the conspicuous signature/date card. England’s tax-stamped Ace of Spades is a separate national tradition and should not be retrojected onto French tarot.

### Grimaud / Paul Marteau, 1930

Marteau’s *Ancien Tarot de Marseille* regularized the inherited design, colors, and printing for twentieth-century occult and cartomantic use. The Ace remains an ornate central coin.

Marteau’s commentary treats the thin outer line and concentric structure as cosmic radiance and condensed activity. This is a **twentieth-century esoteric reading**, not evidence for the intention of Conver’s woodblock.

### Camoin-Jodorowsky, 1997

Philippe Camoin and Alejandro Jodorowsky presented their deck as a “restoration” synthesized from several old packs.

**Documented modern design:** Their Ace preserves the Marseille floral coin but regularizes colors and small details.

**Disputed claim:** Camoin and Jodorowsky sometimes describe recovered details as parts of a primordial symbolic code. Because the deck combines evidence from different printers and centuries and supplies editorial reconstruction, historians generally treat it as a modern synthesis rather than a facsimile of a single lost original.

## Grand Etteilla, design issued 1788–1791

Etteilla reordered and redesigned tarot into an avowedly divinatory “Book of Thoth.”

**Documented iconography and numbering:** The Ace of Coins is card **77**, near the end of the pack. Different Type I, II, and III editions alter the picture. One early tradition shows a celestial or youthful figure supported by an extended hand under a golden sun, with a coin-like roundel below.

Its printed keywords became the card’s most stable feature:

- Upright: *Parfait Contentement* — perfect contentment
- Reversed: *Bourse d’Argent* — purse of money

A later manual says that, if Egyptians drew it first, they stopped because it was the greatest sign of happiness. That is an **Etteilla-school Egyptian legend**, not an Egyptian document. [Surviving 1791 Type I pack](https://fr.etteilla.org/fr/jeu/10/tarot-original-d-etteilla-le-jeu-de-toth); [nineteenth-century manual, card 77](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/2/2f/Le_grand_Etteilla%2C_ou%2C_l%27art_de_tirer_les_cartes_..._Le_tout_recueilli_..._%28IA_b29321220%29.pdf)

## Oswald Wirth, 1889

Wirth’s original set, designed under Stanislas de Guaita’s direction, consisted of the 22 trumps. It did **not** contain an Ace of Pentacles.

Later “Wirth” 78-card editions necessarily add suit cards from other sources. Their Ace should not be attributed to Wirth’s 1889 artwork without edition-specific evidence.

## Golden Dawn manuscripts, late 1880s–1890s

Book T gives the decisive occult redesign:

> “A WHITE Radiant Angelic Hand, holding a branch of a Rose Tree, whereon is a large Pentacle, formed of Five concentric circles.”

The inner circle is white with a red Greek cross; twelve white rays extend outward; above are a small circle, a Maltese cross, and wings; four crosses and two buds appear. The hand issues from clouds.

Book T then says:

> “It represents materiality in all senses, good and evil: and is, therefore, in a sense, illusionary: it shows material gain, labour, power, wealth, etc.”

[Book T, p. 10 in this edition](https://jantetteroo.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/02/Mathers_and_felkin_golden_dawn_book_t_th.pdf)

**Documented development:** This is the clear textual bridge from coin to ceremonial pentacle and from an abstract pip to the cloud-hand composition.

### Robert Wang and Cicero reconstructions

Robert Wang’s *Golden Dawn Tarot* and Chic and Sandra Tabatha Cicero’s Golden Dawn decks visualize Book T’s manuscript instructions. They therefore show the rose branch, concentric pentacle, crosses, wings, rays, and cloud-hand more fully than Smith did.

They are **modern reconstructions of a nineteenth-century specification**, not facsimiles of one canonical secret Golden Dawn deck. Differences in colors, cross forms, and geometry reflect editors’ choices and inconsistencies among transmitted diagrams.

## Waite-Smith, 1909; book 1910

Pamela Colman Smith depicts a white hand emerging from a gray cloud and offering a gold disk marked with a pentagram. Below lies a flowering garden; a path passes through a rose-covered arch toward distant mountains.

Waite’s description is deliberately terse:

> “A hand—issuing, as usual, from a cloud—holds up a pentacle.”

His meanings are:

> “Perfect contentment, felicity, ecstasy; also speedy intelligence; gold.”

Reversed:

> “The evil side of wealth, bad intelligence; also great riches.”

He concludes that in either direction it shows prosperity and comfortable material conditions; whether these benefit the possessor depends on orientation. [Waite, *Pictorial Key to the Tarot*, 1910, Ace of Pentacles](https://sacred-texts.com/tarot/pkt/pktpeac.htm)

**Source analysis:**

- The cloud-hand and proffered magical object derive directly from Golden Dawn Ace descriptions.
- Smith removes Book T’s elaborate rose branch, nested circles, crosses, wings, and astrological construction.
- The pentagram makes “Pentacle” unmistakable.
- The garden, gate, road, and mountain create a narrative invitation: material potential is offered, but the path must be entered.
- No primary evidence has been located showing that Smith identified a particular painting, building, or Sola Busca card as the direct model for this Ace.

## BOTA, Paul Foster Case and Jessie Burns Parke

Case’s BOTA deck closely follows Waite-Smith for the trumps and courts but generally returns the numeral cards to non-scenic suit arrangements. The BOTA Ace of Pentacles is therefore closer to an emblematic occult pentacle than to Smith’s garden narrative.

**Esoteric attribution:** Case retained the Golden Dawn framework: Pentacles/Earth, final Heh, Assiah, and the Ace as the root or seed of material manifestation. Case’s black-and-white cards were intended to be colored by students according to prescribed correspondences.

**Finding:** A stable, independently authored Case upright/reversed keyword pair for this Ace could not be verified in the accessible primary material. Attributing Waite’s wording to Case would therefore be unsafe.

## Thoth, painted 1938–1943; book 1944; commercial deck 1969

Frieda Harris painted a complex winged disk rather than a coin held by a hand. The center contains Crowley’s personal solar and magical formulae, including “666” and the Greek *To Mega Therion* (“the Great Beast”). Six wings surround the disk; rotating, layered geometry suggests matter as energy rather than inert substance.

Crowley writes:

> “The Ace of Disks pictures the entry of that type of Energy which is called Earth.”

He also insists that the disk is “a whirling emblem” and treats the card as an affirmation of the identity of sun and earth, spirit and flesh. [Crowley, *Book of Thoth*, “The Root of the Powers of the Earth”](https://www.rahoorkhuit.net/library/thoth/earth/01_earth.html)

**Change in meaning:** Smith offers a gift entering a cultivated world. Harris and Crowley depict material existence itself as rotating solar-magical energy. The card becomes explicitly Thelemic and autobiographical.

**Printing issue:** Early commercial Thoth editions contain color and production variants, and bibliographies report printing errors involving the Ace of Disks. These should be distinguished from Harris’s original painting; not every odd color or inscription in a reproduction is an intended esoteric “secret.”

## Notable modern redesigns

- **Paul Marteau/Grimaud:** historic-looking Marseille emblem plus twentieth-century numerological cosmology.
- **Camoin-Jodorowsky:** synthetic “restoration,” emphasizing color-coded bodily and cosmic symbolism.
- **Rachel Pollack’s Shining Tribe:** replaces Pentacles with Stones. This is an openly modern attempt to loosen the suit from European ceremonial-magic vocabulary.
- **Contemporary Waite-Smith clones:** commonly turn the card into “a new job,” “investment,” “manifestation,” or “financial opportunity.” These are practical extensions of Waite’s prosperity meanings, not Renaissance definitions.
- **Eco-spiritual decks:** frequently make the Ace a seed, crystal, planet, or living Earth. This develops the Golden Dawn Earth attribution but is modern iconography.

## The game

## Rank

The Ace of Coins is **not a trump**. It cannot outrank trumps merely because it is an Ace.

In many traditional tarot games, suit ranks are divided:

- In the “long” or straight-ranking suits, Swords and Batons often run King high, then Queen, Knight, Jack, Ten downward to Ace.
- In the “round” or reverse-ranking suits, Cups and Coins often run King, Queen, Knight, Jack, then Ace, Two, Three upward to Ten low.

Thus the Ace of Coins can be the highest numeral card of its suit while remaining below every court card. Exact order depends on regional rules.

This reversed ranking has possible Mamluk antecedents: Dummett and Abu-Deeb identified inverse numeral ranking in coins and polo-sticks in the surviving Mamluk material. That supports continuity in trick-taking structure, though the exact Mamluk game rules are lost.

## Point value

In many tarot games the Ace is a low-value ordinary suit card, commonly worth one card point or only contributing through packet-counting rules. It is not one of the *oudlers*, *honours*, or high-value trumps of French tarot.

Rules vary among tarocchi, tarock, tarocchino, Minchiate, and modern regional games; no universal Ace value should be quoted without naming the game.

## Bolognese and Minchiate play

Bolognese tarocchino removes low numeral cards but retains the Aces. In Minchiate, the Ace remains a normal suit card despite the expanded trump series.

## Fool

The Fool’s special role—excuse, sküs, matto, or highest trump in some later games—does not change the status of the Ace of Coins. The Ace never becomes the “first trump.”

## Trump-order disputes

Historians reconstruct three broad early Italian trump-order families, often called A, B, and C, associated approximately with Bologna, Ferrara, and Milan. Justice, Fortitude, and several papal or imperial cards move between them.

This has virtually no direct effect on the Ace because suit-card rank is a separate question. References to the unnumbered Fool, Papess/Juno, Pope/Jupiter, or Maison Dieu belong to trump-pattern history and must not be treated as names or number variants of this pip.

## Chronology of attestation

- **Before tarot, fourteenth–fifteenth centuries — documented/scholarly reconstruction:** Mamluk cards supply the closest surviving analogue for the Latin suits, including coins.

- **Circa 1412–1425 — documented text:** Marziano da Tortona describes a special card game devised for Filippo Maria Visconti with sixteen classical gods and four groups of birds. It is a precursor to trump games, not a standard 78-card tarot, and it does not name an Ace of Coins.

- **1440, Florence — documented:** A record concerning *naibi a trionfi* is widely treated as the earliest presently known unambiguous reference to triumph cards.

- **1442, Ferrara — documented:** Este accounts record purchases of *carte da trionfi*. The entries name packs, not individual Aces.

- **Mid-fifteenth century, Milan — documented objects:** Visconti-family packs establish Coins as one of tarot’s four suits. The Visconti-Sforza Ace survives.

- **Circa 1470–1500 — documented:** The sermon *De ludo* or Steele Sermon condemns games and gives the earliest surviving full list of 21 tarot trumps. It confirms the game structure but does not list ordinary suit cards one by one. [Vanderbilt manuscript description and transcription](https://collections.library.vanderbilt.edu/repositories/2/archival_objects/261035)

- **Late fifteenth century — documented:** Rosenwald and related sheets show mass-produced pip iconography, including an ornate Ace of Coins.

- **1491 — documented object:** Sola Busca engravings demonstrate a complete illustrated-minor program.

- **Early sixteenth century — documented literature:** Teofilo Folengo and other poets use tarot cards in literary and mnemonic games. These sources show social familiarity with tarot, but no secure occult doctrine of the Ace.

- **Seventeenth century — documented:** French Type I patterns and Bolognese tarocchino stabilize regional forms of the Ace as an ornamental single coin.

- **1701–1715 — documented:** Dodal’s Lyon pack.

- **1760 — documented:** Nicolas Conver’s Marseille pack.

- **1781 — documented occult reception:** Court de Gébelin publishes the Egyptian-origin theory; the Comte de Mellet links the 22 trumps to Hebrew letters. Neither gives the Ace a Golden Dawn-style Hebrew path.

- **1788–1791 — documented:** Etteilla produces the first tarot designed and printed specifically for occult/cartomantic use. Ace of Coins becomes card 77.

- **1854–1856 — documented:** Éliphas Lévi’s *Dogme et rituel de la haute magie* identifies the four suits with the letters of the Tetragrammaton and calls the coin-sign the circle or pantacle, “image of the world.”

- **1888 — documented:** S. L. MacGregor Mathers publishes *The Tarot: Its Occult Signification, Use in Fortune-Telling, and Method of Play*.

- **1888 onward — documented institutional tradition:** Golden Dawn manuscripts codify Pentacles as Earth and the Ace as the Root of the Powers of Earth.

- **1889 — documented:** Papus publishes *Le Tarot des Bohémiens*; Wirth creates a 22-trump deck. Neither original project supplies a new illustrated Ace comparable to Smith’s.

- **1909–1910 — documented:** Waite-Smith deck and Waite’s *Pictorial Key* popularize the cloud-hand, pentagram disk, garden, gate, and mountain.

- **1938–1944 — documented:** Harris paints the Thoth card; Crowley writes *The Book of Thoth*.

- **1969 — documented publication history:** Thoth deck becomes commercially available after Crowley’s and Harris’s deaths.

- **Late twentieth century onward — documented reception:** Psychological, Jungian, feminist, ecological, and entrepreneurial readings make the card a general emblem of embodiment, opportunity, resources, health, and “manifestation.”

### Conflicts

- **Coin versus pentacle:** historical suit-sign versus occult magical tool.
- **Static matter versus living energy:** Book T calls materiality partly illusory; Crowley insists Earth is dynamic.
- **Gift versus achievement:** Smith’s offered disk suggests grace or opportunity; modern work-oriented readings often make it a reward for effort.
- **Upright versus reversed wealth:** Etteilla and Mathers make both directions favorable, but differently so. Many modern manuals turn reversal into loss or missed opportunity.
- **Astrology:** no canonical Golden Dawn planet or zodiac sign belongs to the Ace. Assignments such as Taurus or “all Earth signs” are modern simplifications.
- **Hebrew letters:** Hebrew-letter assignments belong to the 22 trumps. The Ace has no individual letter. Final Heh belongs to the entire Pentacles suit.
- **Tzaddi/Heh controversy:** Crowley’s “Tzaddi is not the Star” revision concerns Emperor and Star among the trumps. It does not change the Ace of Disks or the final-Heh suit attribution.

### Transmission of the familiar “new financial opportunity”

This phrase is not found verbatim in the Renaissance packs. Its genealogy is approximately:

1. Etteilla and Mathers: happiness, money, gain, riches.
2. Book T: material gain, labor, power, wealth.
3. Waite: prosperity and comfortable material conditions.
4. Smith: a proffered object and an open path.
5. Twentieth- and twenty-first-century manuals: job offer, investment, business, financial beginning, or “manifestation.”

The last stage is a **modern practical elaboration**, though it is reasonably derived from the earlier cluster.

## Esoteric and symbolic readings

### Kabbalistic

**First systematic foundation:** Lévi connected tarot, Hebrew letters, the Tetragrammaton, and the four suits. He wrote of:

> “Clubs, Cups, Swords, and Circles or Pantacles, commonly called Deniers—all these are hieroglyphics of the Tetragram.”

The pantacle is the circle or image of the world, associated with the final Heh. [Lévi, English scan of *Dogma and Ritual of High Magic*](https://www.hermetics.org/pdf/Eliphas_Levi_Dogma%20et%20Rituel%20de%20la%20Haute%20Magie%201.pdf)

**Golden Dawn elaboration:** The Ace is Kether, the first sephirah, operating in Assiah, the lowest or action-world. Hence it is not “Malkuth” simpliciter: it is **Kether of Assiah**, the root of Earth before differentiation through Two to Ten.

### Hermetic and elemental

The Golden Dawn made the suit Earth and described a magical pentacle suspended from a rose branch. Earth here encompasses wealth, labor, power, and the danger of illusion or material fixation.

Crowley revised inert “matter” into energy. The disk whirls; the solar number six and Thelemic insignia collapse the opposition between spirit and flesh.

### Alchemical

**Tradition/esoteric attribution:** Etteilla’s pack explicitly embedded Creation, elements, and alchemical-medical ideas. Card 77’s vocabulary includes “perfect medicine” and “solar medicine” in later transmitted lists.

**Modern extension:** Contemporary readers associate the Ace with prima materia, coagulation, salt, or the beginning of material fixation. No evidence places those interpretations on the Renaissance Ace itself. They are retrospective applications of alchemical language.

### Christian

The early coin emblem exists inside a Christian European material culture and can evoke stewardship, charity, tribute, or worldly riches. Nevertheless, no contemporary source identifies this particular Ace with Judas’s silver, the tribute penny, Eucharist, or divine providence.

Waite’s cloud-hand resembles the conventional “Hand of God,” and the garden may invite Edenic readings. These are iconographically plausible interpretations, but Waite’s own description does not explicitly call the hand God’s.

The ornate Ace of Cups, often resembling a fountain, church, reliquary, or Eucharistic vessel, should not be confused with the Ace of Coins. Waite himself discussed the Cup’s ecclesiastical appearance; it does not prove a parallel church origin for the Coin.

### Astrological

The Ace has no decan. In Golden Dawn theory it contains the root of the whole Earth quadrant rather than one 10-degree span.

Modern “Taurus Ace,” “Capricorn Ace,” or seasonal timing systems are later conventions. Crowley’s solar symbolism is card-specific Thelemic iconography, not an assignment of the Ace to the planet Sun in the Book T decan table.

### Jungian

Jung did not leave a canonical card-by-card tarot dictionary assigning a meaning to the Ace of Pentacles.

**Modern Jungian construction:** Post-Jungian tarot writers interpret the hand and disk as an archetypal gift emerging from the unconscious; the garden as cultivated psyche; the gate as transition; the mountain as individuation; and the pentacle as a mandala of embodied wholeness.

These interpretations were spread by late twentieth-century writers such as Sallie Nichols and the wider humanistic tarot movement. They are legitimate reception history, but calling them “Jung’s meaning” is a misattribution.

- Pamela Colman Smith — gave the Ace its most influential modern scene.
- Arthur Edward Waite — supplied its English occult framework and published meanings.
- Jessie Burns Parke and Paul Foster Case — BOTA adaptation.

### Literature, film, and visual arts

The requested cultural figures require careful separation between tarot generally and this exact card:

- **T. S. Eliot, *The Waste Land*:** Madame Sosostris names several invented or altered tarot cards, including the “one-eyed merchant” and “drowned Phoenician Sailor.” The Ace of Pentacles is not named.
- **Italo Calvino, *The Castle of Crossed Destinies*:** tarot cards generate intersecting stories. Different editions reproduce different decks, notably Visconti and Marseille imagery. The book is highly relevant to tarot reception, but there is no single fixed Ace-of-Pentacles episode that can be isolated across the work without specifying the layout and edition.
- **W. B. Yeats:** deeply involved with the Golden Dawn and its elemental tools. No securely documented poem centered specifically on the Ace of Pentacles was located.
- **Ingmar Bergman:** *The Seventh Seal* uses chess, Death, and medieval allegory, not a documented quotation of this Ace.
- **Jodorowsky:** tarot informs his writings, workshops, and symbolic cinema, but claims that a particular film shot is a direct quotation of the Ace require shot-specific evidence.
- **Paintings and buildings:** no securely documented pre-1909 painting or building has been traced as Smith’s direct source for the card’s garden arch. Similar cloud-hands, walled gardens, triumphal arches, and distant mountains are widespread motifs; resemblance alone does not establish borrowing.

Absence of a card-specific connection is important. General tarot association should not be inflated into attachment to this Ace.

## Controversies and disputes

## Egyptian origin

**Claim:** Court de Gébelin asserted in 1781 that tarot preserved an ancient Egyptian sacred book. Etteilla converted the claim into a designed “Book of Thoth” deck.

**Evidence for the belief:** Their published texts survive. The claim is therefore a documented eighteenth-century belief.

**Evidence for ancient origin:** None identified. There is no ancient Egyptian tarot deck, matching textual description, archaeological sequence, or continuous transmission into fifteenth-century Italy. Egyptian hieroglyphs had not yet been deciphered when Gébelin wrote.

**Historical assessment:** Legend as an origin story; foundational fact in occult tarot’s reception. Court’s own essay can be read in facsimile through [Wikisource](https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/Page:Court_de_G%C3%A9belin_-_Monde_primitif_analys%C3%A9_et_compar%C3%A9_avec_le_monde_moderne%2C_8e_livraison.djvu/499).

## Romani or “Gypsy” transmission

**Claim:** Tarot was carried from Egypt or India by “Bohémiens,” preserving an ancient initiatory book.

**Spreaders:** Nineteenth-century writers including J.-A. Vaillant and Papus. Papus’s 1889 preface states:

> “Le jeu de Tarots, transmis par les Bohémiens de génération en génération, est le livre primitif de l’antique initiation.”

“The tarot game, transmitted by the Gypsies from generation to generation, is the primitive book of ancient initiation.” [Papus, *Le Tarot des Bohémiens*, 1889, p. 4](https://www.arbredor.com/ebooks/TarotBohemiens.pdf)

**Evidence:** Romani fortune-tellers later used ordinary and tarot cards, but tarot is documented in northern Italian courts before evidence that Romani communities transmitted this particular pack or doctrine. Waite himself concluded that evidence for Romani tarot use appeared too late to support the origin story. [Waite, historical discussion](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Page:The_Illustrated_Key_to_the_Tarot.djvu/36)

**Assessment:** Legend regarding origin; documented later practice and cultural association. Modern scholarship also notes the ethnic stereotyping embedded in the myth.

## Cathar, Albigensian, and Templar origins

**Claim:** Tarot pictures allegedly conceal Cathar dualism, Templar teaching, a suppressed pope, or an underground heretical church.

**Evidence:** No Cathar or Templar tarot deck or medieval text describing such a system has been found. The earliest cards belong to Catholic Renaissance court culture and ordinary gaming practice.

Waite explicitly mocked the attempt to turn every suggestive detail into evidence for a secret Albigensian patriarch. [Waite, “Introductory and General”](https://sacred-texts.com/tarot/pkt/pkt0101.htm)

**Assessment:** Modern or nineteenth-/twentieth-century legend unless a claimant can supply a dated intermediate source. Symbolic compatibility is not proof of descent.

## “Pentacles are ancient coins bearing pentagrams”

**Claim:** The suit has always been Pentacles and its disks originally bore five-pointed stars.

**Finding:** False as a general historical statement. Mamluk and Renaissance examples are coins or ornamental roundels; Marseille terminology is *deniers*. The pentagram-bearing disk is characteristic of Golden Dawn-derived decks and Waite-Smith.

Earlier magical texts certainly use *pentacles*, often meaning inscribed talismans rather than necessarily five-pointed stars. The innovation is the systematic identification of the tarot coin suit with the elemental magical pentacle.

## Sola Busca as the source of Smith’s Ace

**Claim:** Because Smith consulted Sola Busca imagery, every illustrated Waite-Smith pip derives from it.

**Finding:** Overstatement. Specific borrowings elsewhere are visually convincing. The Ace of Pentacles’ decisive source is Book T’s cloud-hand holding a pentacle. No matching Sola Busca composition is necessary to explain it.

## Maker’s card and tax stamp

**Claim:** The Marseille Ace of Coins universally carried the printer’s name and tax stamp.

**Finding:** Too broad. The Two of Coins often carries the maker and date in Marseille decks. Aces served fiscal or maker functions in several national patterns, but placement varied. The British tax-stamped Ace of Spades is not evidence for a universal tarot rule.

## “Ace is high among the trumps”

**Finding:** Category error. It is not a trump. It may be high among the numeral cards of Coins because Cups and Coins traditionally reverse the numeral order, but courts and all played trumps remain above it in ordinary trick-taking hierarchy.

## Misprints becoming traditions

- Restored editions may silently replace missing Noblet or early-sheet cards.
- Early commercial Thoth editions differ in color and detail from Harris’s original paintings.
- The Chosson deck’s famous “1672” date is disputed; archival and stylistic arguments favor 1736.
- “Rider Tarot” often erases Pamela Colman Smith’s authorship.
- “Pentacle” is frequently defined as necessarily a pentagram. Historically a pentacle can be a broader category of inscribed magical disk or seal.
- Modern books regularly assign an Earth zodiac sign to the Ace despite Book T’s lack of a decan assignment.

## Historical correction and its limits

Dummett, Decker, Depaulis, Kaplan, Farley, Place, and other historians place tarot’s demonstrable origin in fifteenth-century northern Italy as a trick-taking game with an added permanent trump suit.

The strongest evidence consists of:

- Italian account books and legal records;
- surviving fifteenth-century cards;
- early sermons and literary references;
- continuity into documented regional games;
- absence of occult interpretations from the early evidence;
- traceable emergence of occult theories in eighteenth-century France.

This correction does not make later symbolism culturally meaningless. It distinguishes **when a belief appears** from **the ancient date claimed for it**. [Dummett, “Origins of Tarot”](https://www.nybooks.com/articles/1981/05/14/origins-of-tarot/); [Decker, Depaulis, and Dummett, *A Wicked Pack of Cards*](https://books.google.com/books/about/A_Wicked_Pack_of_Cards.html?id=MG6DAAAAMAAJ)

## Open questions

1. Which Visconti-group Ace of Coins should be called the earliest depends on unsettled dating and attribution among related Milanese packs.
2. The precise route by which Mamluk suit systems entered Europe remains a historical reconstruction rather than a documented shipment from a named workshop.
3. Rosenwald’s date, locality, and relationship to Bologna versus Florence remain disputed.
4. Fragmentary Budapest and Este material prevents a continuous genealogy of the Ace.
5. Etteilla Type I, II, and III images need to be distinguished; later manuals preserve his keywords but sometimes redraw or rename the image.
6. The exact date and internal authorship layers of Book T manuscripts require archival comparison; the public text is a transmission of Golden Dawn material, not one uncontested autograph.
7. No card-specific proof was found that Smith used a particular garden, painting, architectural arch, or Sola Busca Ace.
8. A primary BOTA text giving a unique Case upright-and-reversed pair for the Ace was not located.
9. Pollack’s and Greer’s exact wording varies by edition and remains under copyright; their meanings above are consequently summarized rather than presented as invented quotations.
10. No reliable evidence attaches Eliot, Bergman, Yeats, or a particular historic building directly to this card, as distinct from tarot or occultism generally.
