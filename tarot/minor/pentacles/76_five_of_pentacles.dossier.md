# Five of Pentacles: Research Dossier

> **Method and labels.**  
> **[Documented fact]** means supported by a surviving card, contemporary record, or primary printed text.  
> **[Scholarly reconstruction]** means an inference accepted or seriously argued by specialist historians but not stated in a contemporary source.  
> **[Tradition]** means a historically transmitted practice whose point of origin is uncertain.  
> **[Esoteric attribution]** means an occult system’s deliberate symbolic assignment, not a claim about fifteenth-century intentions.  
> **[Disputed]** identifies a live attribution or identification problem.  
> **[Legend]** identifies a historical claim unsupported by the documentary record.  
> **[Modern invention]** means a demonstrably recent reading or redesign.

The Morgan describes the Visconti–Sforza pack as a 78-card structure of twenty-one trumps, Fool, and fifty-six suit cards, and explicitly distinguishes its Renaissance gaming use from the astrology and divination imposed centuries later. Its institutional catalogue dates the principal cards around 1450, while acknowledging disputed artist attributions and several later replacement cards. [Morgan Library catalogue](https://www.themorgan.org/manuscript/158525)

### What “pentacle” means here

**[Documented fact]** The original suit sign is a coin: Italian *denaro*, French *denier*, Spanish *oro*. Fifteenth- through eighteenth-century cards do not constitute a suit of five-pointed stars.

**[Esoteric attribution]** Éliphas Lévi used *pantacle* for a consecrated magical emblem and connected tarot’s material suit with talismanic symbolism. The English Golden Dawn normalized **Pentacles** as the suit name. Waite retained it; Crowley replaced it with **Disks**.

**[Correction]** “Pentacle” is not historically derived simply from Greek *pente*, “five,” and it need not contain a pentagram. The frequent modern equation *pentacle = pentagram = five elements* is a later simplification. Ironically, the Thoth Five of Disks really does arrange five disks as an inverted pentagram.

## Iconography deck by deck

## Fifteenth-century Milan and Ferrara

### Marziano da Tortona’s game, approximately 1412–25

**[Documented fact]** Marziano’s text describes a special pack devised for Filippo Maria Visconti, with sixteen classical gods and four bird suits. It is an important precursor to *trionfi* but did not contain the ordinary suit of Coins and therefore had no Five of Coins.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** Historians treat it as evidence that the Visconti court experimented with permanent trump suits before standard tarot emerged. It is not evidence for the later 78-card pack or for occult meanings.

### Visconti di Modrone / Cary-Yale, approximately 1440s

**[Documented fact]** This incomplete luxury deck, now at Yale’s Beinecke Library, contains unusual six-card courts and several distinctive theological or virtue trumps. Coin cards carry Visconti emblems and gold-stamped coin devices.

No securely catalogued surviving Five of Coins from this group could be verified in the accessible Yale record. Accordingly, claims that the Cary-Yale Five is the earliest surviving example should be treated as **[unverified]**.

### Brambilla deck, approximately 1440s

**[Documented fact]** Forty-eight cards are preserved at the Pinacoteca di Brera. Coins bear Visconti currency imagery; the pip cards are repeated suit signs on richly worked grounds, not narrative scenes. The Brera itself calls the group a late-Gothic deck made for the duke of Milan. [Brera Sola Busca exhibition record](https://pinacotecabrera.org/en/news/mostra/il-segreto-dei-segreti-i-tarocchi-sola-busca/)

The surviving Brambilla inventory does not securely supply a Five of Coins. Its importance is typological: it shows what a Milanese money-suit pip looked like—heraldic, luxurious, and functional—without poverty symbolism.

### Visconti–Sforza / Colleoni-Baglioni, approximately 1450–60

**[Documented fact]** The Five of Coins survives in the Bergamo/Accademia Carrara portion. Five circular gold coin emblems are placed symmetrically on a tooled or painted ground surrounded by vegetal ornament. There are no people, snow, church, injury, exclusion, or implied narrative.

The coins in this family of decks display dynastic and currency-like emblems. The cards themselves are not titled or numbered. The Morgan’s 35 cards, Carrara’s 26, and the Colleoni family’s 13 together make 74 surviving cards; four are missing from the reconstructed 78-card pack. [Morgan overview](https://www.themorgan.org/collection/tarot-cards) The exact workshop remains **[disputed]**: Bonifacio Bembo and his circle are traditional candidates; Francesco Zavattari, Benedetto Bembo, Franco dei Russi, and others have been proposed for portions or replacements.

**Meaning at the time:** gaming rank. No surviving fifteenth-century text says the five coins represented scarcity, lovers, Geburah, or Mercury.

### Este and Ferrarese cards

**[Documented fact]** Ferrara’s court accounts name painted *carte da trionfi* in 1442. A 10 February entry records Jacopo Sagramoro’s payment for painting Cups, Swords, Coins, Batons, figures, and backs in four packs; a 28 July entry records inexpensive packs bought for Ercole and Sigismondo d’Este. The translated ledger text is reproduced in Helen Farley’s historical study. [Farley, *A Cultural History of Tarot*](https://e-isomero.com/pdf/book/1611846398.pdf)

Individual Este-related cards now dispersed among collections are conventional luxury pips: repeated coin signs plus ornament. No surviving Ferrarese Five of Coins carries a stable narrative that anticipates Waite–Smith.

### Sola Busca, engraved about 1490–91; colored about 1500

**[Documented fact]** This is the oldest substantially complete surviving 78-card tarot and the earliest complete deck whose numbered suit cards carry individual scenes. The complete colored deck is now at the Pinacoteca di Brera; uncolored impressions and fragments survive elsewhere. Brera calls it the oldest complete Italian tarot. [Brera](https://pinacotecabrera.org/en/news/mostra/il-segreto-dei-segreti-i-tarocchi-sola-busca/)

The Five of Coins shows a single muscular or laboring male figure working beside or over a furnace, brazier, or earthen installation; five coin-emblems belong to the composition. It has been read by Sofia Di Vincenzo and later esoteric commentators as an alchemist heating or fecundating matter.

- **[Documented fact]** The figure, implements, five coins, and heat/work imagery are visible.
- **[Scholarly or esoteric interpretation, disputed]** The figure is specifically an alchemist and the scene encodes preparation of the philosophers’ stone.
- **Evidence:** the deck contains enigmatic classical names, workshop imagery, and imagery compatible with late-medieval metallurgy/alchemy. No contemporary key survives, so a continuous alchemical program remains debated.

#### Was it the direct source of Smith’s Five of Pentacles?

The item-specific lead does **not** survive card-by-card comparison.

- **[Documented fact]** In 1907 the Busca-Serbelloni family supplied the British Museum with photographs of all 78 cards. Smith produced her deck in 1909 and may have seen those photographs or their exhibition.
- **[Scholarly reconstruction]** Several Waite–Smith pips closely echo Sola Busca cards—especially the Three of Swords and Ten of Wands, and plausible correspondences elsewhere.
- **[Correction]** The Sola Busca Five of Coins does not show two mendicants, snow, a church, a crutch, or stained glass. Therefore it cannot be called the direct compositional source of Smith’s Five. At most, the Sola Busca established the precedent that a pip could be converted into an individualized human scene.

The recent study of Smith’s imagery lists likely Sola Busca correspondences but does not include the Five of Pentacles among its strongest matches. [“Framing Postcolonial Place,” note 69](https://doi.org/10.4000/15aa0)

### “Mantegna Tarocchi,” approximately 1460s

**[Documented fact]** These are fifty educational engravings arranged in five decades—social estates, Apollo and Muses, liberal arts, virtues, and celestial spheres. They are not a tarot deck and have no suits or Five of Coins.

**[Tradition/modern cataloguing error]** The word *tarocchi* became attached to them later. They may illuminate Renaissance emblematic culture but are irrelevant to the genealogy of this pip card.

### Rosenwald and Budapest sheets, late fifteenth or early sixteenth century

These woodcut sheets preserve popular, less luxurious tarot production associated with northern Italy.

**[Documented fact]** Where money pips occur, they follow the ordinary repeated-sign convention. They do not supply a mendicant narrative or an occult title.

**[Open attribution]** Dates, production centers, and relationships among the sheets remain debated. They are evidence for the transition from unique court commissions to reproducible packs, not for an early divinatory Five.

## Regional and popular patterns

### Tarot de Marseille family

The Marseille Five of Deniers is an abstract or semi-abstract pip:

- four coins occupy the corners or quadrants;
- a fifth coin is centered;
- curving stems, leaves, flowers, and calyxes bind or separate the disks;
- printer initials, heraldic details, or workshop peculiarities may appear inside the center.

This central-five layout is old and visually stable. It expresses rank efficiently and furnishes ornamental balance. Claims that it was designed as a Tree of Life, human body, pentagram, or map of five wounds are **[later esoteric readings]**, not established printer intent.

#### Jean Noblet, Paris, approximately 1650

**[Documented fact]** The surviving Noblet pack is incomplete, but its pips establish the early French type: bold round deniers with floral interlace, stenciled colors, and no narrative scene. The surviving evidence does not support poverty or “help nearby.”

#### Jean Dodal, Lyon, approximately 1701–15

The Cinq de Deniers presents five circular deniers in a quincunx-like vertical/floral structure. The line is vigorous; stems and leaf forms fill the interstices. It remains a game card.

#### Nicolas Conver, Marseille, 1760

Conver’s version became especially influential through later Marseille reproductions. Four deniers frame a central fifth; symmetrical vegetation grows through the spaces. The image is decorative, not scenic.

#### Grimaud, nineteenth and twentieth centuries

Grimaud regularized the Marseille image for industrial color printing. Line, palette, and labels became cleaner and more standardized. This version helped make “Tarot de Marseille” appear more uniform than the older workshop tradition actually was.

#### Camoin–Jodorowsky, 1997

**[Modern reconstruction]** Philippe Camoin and Alejandro Jodorowsky advertised their deck as a restoration based on old printing evidence. Its Five of Deniers preserves the five-denier floral structure with newly regularized colors and small reconstructed details.

**[Disputed claim]** Calling it the recovery of a single original “authentic Tarot” exceeds the evidence. Historians generally see Marseille as a family of evolving patterns, not one pristine encoded original.

Jodorowsky and Marianne Costa read the central coin and four surrounding coins developmentally: five introduces a new ideal, temptation, or bridge beyond the stability of four. This is a modern numerological-hermeneutic system, not a recovered seventeenth-century explanation.

### Besançon pattern

Besançon packs are mainly defined by replacement of the Papess and Pope trumps with Juno and Jupiter, especially in Swiss, Alsatian, and southern German markets from the eighteenth century. That regional renaming does not materially change the Five of Deniers, which remains a floral five-coin pip.

The examples requested in the general brief—Papess/Juno, Pope/Jupiter, Tower/Maison Dieu, unnumbered Fool—belong to trump nomenclature and should not be projected onto the Five. For this card, the important regional change is **deniers → coins/oros/Münzen → pentacles/disks**.

### Minchiate

**[Documented fact]** Florentine Minchiate expands the trump series to forty-one special cards but retains the four Italian suits. The Five of Coins is an ordinary pip, generally five coin devices with decorative vegetation.

In play, it belongs to a round suit and ranks on the reversed numeral scale characteristic of Cups and Coins. It carries no special point value. The expanded cosmological trumps do not turn it into an astrological pip; any later attempt to assign Minchiate’s zodiac trumps to individual suit cards is a reconstruction.

### Bolognese tarocchino

The standard Bolognese 62-card pack removed the 2–5 from every suit. Consequently:

**[Documented fact]** There is no Five of Coins in the normal surviving Bolognese tarocchino pack.

The pack retains Ace and 6–10 in Cups and Coins and Ace and 6–10 in Swords and Batons, with suit ranking reversed between round and long suits. [Pagat, Bolognese Ottocento](https://www.pagat.com/tarot/ottocento.html)

This is a useful negative finding: modern “Bolognese Five of Pentacles” cards are restorations or inventions, not part of the reduced gaming pattern.

### Sicilian tarot

The Sicilian pack was also reduced, but the Mineo form retains a Five of Coins among the low suit cards. In Cups and Coins, cards rank King, Queen, Horse, Maid, then 10 downward through 5, with local variation over the 4 and Ace of Coins. The Five itself has no special card-point value. [Pagat, Sicilian Tarocchi](https://www.pagat.com/tarot/sicilian.html)

### Spanish terminology

Traditional Spanish-suited packs call the suit **oros**, literally “golds,” and the card **cinco de oros**. Spanish tarot translations often retain *oros* even when reproducing Smith’s pentagrams. *Pentáculos* is primarily an occult/translation choice, not the historical Spanish suit name.

### German terminology

German-language gaming packs use French suits in most modern Tarock forms, so the literal Five of Coins may not occur. In Italian-suited historical packs or esoteric translations it is *Fünf der Münzen*. Crowley translations use *Fünf der Scheiben*. When present in a game, it is a low, usually valueless suit card rather than a trump or counting card.

## Etteilla and nineteenth-century occult decks

### Grand Etteilla, approximately 1788–89

**[Documented fact]** Etteilla reorganized the entire pack into a continuously numbered “Book of Thoth,” printed upright and reversed keywords on the cards, and supplied the first systematic published tarot-cartomancy corpus. His *Manière de se récréer avec le jeu de cartes nommées Tarots* appeared in four parts, 1783–85. [Wellcome Collection scan and catalogue](https://wellcomecollection.org/works/ux34pwxe)

The Five of Coins remains identifiable as the traditional pip, though Grand Etteilla editions differ in ornament, captions, numbering, and iconographic framing.

Its core meanings are:

- upright: **“Amant ou maîtresse”**—lover or mistress; love, sweetness, affection, legitimate or chaste attachment;
- reversed: misconduct, disordered or illicit love, debauchery/profligacy.

**[Documented textual transmission]** Mathers says that his additional meanings are “chiefly taken from Etteilla” and reproduces this pair almost verbatim. [Mathers 1888, pp. 17–20](https://www.howardnema.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/12/the-tarot-1888-by-macgregor-mathers-1.pdf)

This is the older meaning Waite found incompatible with the Golden Dawn’s “Material Trouble.”

### Oswald Wirth, 1889

Wirth’s original deck contained only the twenty-two trumps. Therefore:

**[Documented fact]** There is no Wirth-designed Five of Pentacles in the 1889 pack.

Later “Wirth” 78-card editions that add illustrated minors are editorial expansions and must not be treated as Wirth’s 1889 iconography.

### Papus, *Le Tarot des Bohémiens*, 1889

Papus did not create a historically independent scene for this card. His importance lies in systematic correspondences: the four suits form a cycle of Yod–Heh–Vau–final Heh, and the numbered cards repeat emanation, opposition, equilibrium, and transition in a tetragrammatic scheme.

Pentacles occupy **final Heh**, realization or material embodiment. The Five belongs to the second numerical ternary as a transitional/active term. Papus’s English edition is available through the Library of Congress. [Papus, 1896 English edition](https://www.loc.gov/item/77355368/)

The often-repeated Papus gloss “success coming that will balance loss” is traceable to later tables and paraphrases, but I could not securely locate that exact wording in the 1889 French first edition. It should be marked **[secondary attribution]**.

### Éliphas Lévi, 1854–56

Lévi’s *Dogme et rituel de la haute magie* discusses the tarot chiefly through the twenty-two keys, Hebrew alphabet, tetragrammaton, and magical pantacles. It does not give an individual upright/reversed Five of Coins entry.

Thus:

- **[Documented fact]** Lévi helped create the conceptual route from coins to magical pentacles.
- **[Negative finding]** No distinct Lévi Five-of-Pentacles divinatory text was found.
- **[Correction]** Quoting modern “Five of Pentacles according to Lévi” meanings as though they came from *Dogme et rituel* is unwarranted.

## Golden Dawn, Waite, Case, and Crowley

### Golden Dawn “Book T,” approximately 1888 onward

The Golden Dawn transformed each numbered pip into a conjunction of:

1. a sephirah represented by its number;
2. one of the four Qabalistic worlds represented by the suit;
3. a ten-degree zodiacal decan;
4. a planetary ruler;
5. a divine name, angelic names, colors, and a descriptive title.

For the Five of Pentacles:

- **Geburah** = five;
- **Assiah/Earth** = Pentacles;
- **0°–10° Taurus**;
- **Mercury** rules the decan by the Chaldean sequence;
- title: **Lord of Material Trouble**.

A published Golden Dawn table in *The Equinox* gives “5 Pentacles — Material Trouble — Mercury — Taurus.” [*The Equinox* I.8 table](https://hermetic.com/_media/93beast.fea.st/files/section1/equinox/equinox_i_08_.pdf)

The descriptive divination includes material trouble, loss of money, poverty, labor, cultivation, intelligence applied to practical matters, and—in better dignity—some gain after effort. “Dignity” means neighboring cards and elemental harmony could alter the expression; the Golden Dawn did not rely solely on upright/reversed orientation.

#### Colors and magical image

Golden Dawn manuscripts and later reconstructions vary in palette because they distinguish the four color scales. Geburah’s characteristic colors include scarlet/red and related martial tones; Earth adds citrine, olive, russet, and black. Reconstructions by Robert Wang and by Chic and Sandra Tabatha Cicero translate these instructions differently.

- **Wang’s Golden Dawn Tarot:** a ritualized, emblematic pip based on Golden Dawn tables.
- **Cicero reconstruction:** more explicitly systematized color scales and decanic symbols.
- **[Caution]** Neither should be mistaken for a unique surviving Victorian “official deck.” Original members produced personal manuscript decks under shared instructions.

### Waite–Smith, 1909

Pamela Colman Smith shows two impoverished figures moving through falling snow beside a glowing stained-glass window containing five golden pentacles. One figure uses crutches and has a bandaged foot; the other is barefoot or inadequately shod and wrapped in a tattered covering. Neither appears to enter the building.

Waite’s complete description is concise:

> “Two mendicants in a snow-storm pass a lighted casement.”

His upright meaning is “material trouble above all,” especially destitution. He then records the older love/lovers/concord tradition and says, “These alternatives cannot be harmonized.” Reversed: disorder, chaos, ruin, discord, profligacy. [Waite, *Pictorial Key*, Five of Pentacles](https://sacred-texts.com/tarot/pkt/pktpe05.htm)

#### What Smith changed

1. Five repeated coin signs became five pentagrams embedded in stained glass.
2. Abstract floral ornament became a human crisis.
3. Golden Dawn “material trouble” became literal poverty and disability.
4. The lighted window created a potential contrast between material want and spiritual/community wealth.
5. The figures’ companionship complicates total isolation: they suffer, but do not suffer alone.

#### What can and cannot be securely inferred

- **[Documented fact]** Waite calls them mendicants and the window a lighted casement.
- **[Strong visual inference]** The architecture resembles a church or chapel.
- **[Modern tradition]** “Help is available, but they refuse or fail to see it.” Waite never says this.
- **[Modern tradition]** The window is arranged exactly as the five lower sephiroth on the Tree of Life. It resembles a vertical five-pip grouping but is not a canonical ten-sephiroth Tree.
- **[Disputed identification]** The bell or physical marks prove one figure is a medieval leper. The imagery can suggest sickness and exclusion, but no annotation by Smith or Waite confirms leprosy.
- **[Unsupported card-specific claim]** The composition comes directly from Sola Busca’s Five of Coins.

The original drawings are lost. Smith completed roughly eighty designs in about six months in 1909. The Morgan’s biographical exhibition emphasizes her independent career and Golden Dawn context. [Morgan on Pamela Colman Smith](https://www.themorgan.org/exhibitions/online/tarot/13)

### Paul Foster Case and B.O.T.A.

Case accepted the Golden Dawn correspondence framework but rejected scenic pips for his teaching deck. The B.O.T.A. pack, drawn by Jessie Burns Parke, presents black-line suit emblems intended to be colored meditatively by students.

The Five is therefore five pentacles in a formal arrangement rather than Waite’s mendicants. Its doctrinal meaning derives from:

- five/Geburah: severity, limitation, cutting, testing;
- Pentacles/Earth/Assiah: body, property, work, concrete circumstances;
- Mercury in Taurus: mental or commercial activity under material resistance.

**[Documented fact]** Case’s publicly available *Introduction to the Tarot* treats tarot as a symbolic alphabet preserving secret doctrine but does not give a full individual Five-of-Pentacles entry. [Case, *Introduction to the Tarot*](https://www.mr-kaplan.com/Library/PFCase/Introduction-to-Tarot.html)

The often-circulated Case meaning “loss of profession; monetary anxiety; sometimes recovery through orderly work” appears in B.O.T.A.-derived lesson material and later tables, but exact wording varies by edition. It should not be presented as a quotation from *The Book of Tokens*, which concerns the twenty-two major keys only.

### Crowley–Harris Thoth, painted 1938–43; book 1944; deck published 1969

Title: **WORRY — FIVE OF DISKS**.

Frieda Harris paints five complex disks at the points of a downward or inverted pentagram. Their emblems evoke the five tattvas or elemental principles. The background is fractured, angry red and ochre, like cracked earth, strained machinery, or seismic stress.

Crowley explains:

> “The Number Five, Geburah, in the suit of Earth, shows the disruption of the Elements.”

He calls Mercury and Taurus opposed types, says the natural meaning is “Intelligence applied to Labour,” and describes the inverted pentagram as instability in matter’s foundations. The five tattvas nevertheless hold the organism together. [Crowley text and plate commentary](https://crowley-thoth.com/symbolism/disks-05/) The complete period scan is available in [*The Book of Thoth*](https://www.rahoorkhuit.net/library/crowley/equinox/volume3/vol_3_no_05.pdf).

#### What changed

- Smith’s social narrative disappears.
- Five material disks become a single unstable geometric organism.
- Poverty becomes **anxiety, structural strain, and inertia**.
- The disks are not simple coins: each is a magical-technological seal.
- The red, fractured field visualizes Geburah disturbing Earth.

Crowley’s Tzaddi/Heh revision concerns the Emperor and Star, not this pip. It does not change Mercury in Taurus, Geburah, or the Five of Disks. Invoking “Tzaddi is not the Star” to alter this card is a category error.

## Notable modern redesigns

### Marseille revivals

Modern Marseille decks generally retain five floral coins. Readers increasingly generate meaning through number-plus-suit rather than an inherited picture:

- five = disturbance, crisis, expansion, or transition;
- coins = body, money, craft, territory;
- central coin = seed, new value, or destabilizing fifth element.

These are **[modern interpretive methods]**, even when presented as “traditional Marseille numerology.”

### Jodorowsky–Costa

In *The Way of Tarot*, five marks emergence of a new ideal or temptation after four’s stability. Applied to Deniers, it can mean new investment, altered material consciousness, generosity, avarice, bodily change, or a value demanding embodiment. Reversal is not fundamental to their method.

### Jungian and therapeutic decks

Modern psychological readings commonly convert the scene into:

- scarcity consciousness;
- social exclusion or shame;
- bodily vulnerability;
- the wounded ego outside institutional belonging;
- unnoticed resources;
- solidarity between marginalized people;
- recovery through asking for help.

These are **[modern inventions or adaptations]**, not ancient archetypal meanings. “Jungian” here describes an interpretive style. Jung did not publish a card-by-card tarot system or a Five of Pentacles interpretation.

### Social-justice redesigns

Recent decks often replace medieval beggars with unhoused people, disabled people confronting inaccessible architecture, refugees, medical debt, eviction, unemployment, or mutual-aid networks. These designs make structural poverty explicit and sometimes reject the moralizing “they simply failed to notice help” reading.

## The game

## Rank

In tarot games, suit-card ranking typically differs between the “long” suits and “round” suits:

- Swords and Batons: high numerals outrank low ones.
- Cups and Coins: low numerals outrank high ones beneath the court cards, so the Ace is usually highest among the pips and Ten lowest.

Accordingly, in a complete Italian 78-card pack, the Five of Coins is normally:

- below Ace, Two, Three, and Four;
- above Six through Ten;
- below all four court cards.

Regional reductions alter or remove it. Piedicavallo likewise describes Cups and Coins as *rossi* and uses the reversed round-suit order. [Pagat, Piedicavallo](https://www.pagat.com/tarot/piedicavallo.html)

In modern French Tarot, where French-suited cards replace Italian Coins with Diamonds, ordinary suit cards rank naturally from Ace low to Ten high. There is therefore no literal Five of Pentacles in the standard gaming deck; its functional counterpart is Five of Diamonds.

## Value

In most tarot point-counting systems:

- Kings and selected trumps/Fool carry high values;
- Queens, Cavaliers, and Jacks carry descending values;
- numeral suit cards, including the Five of Coins, are “small cards” or fillers and have no independent premium.

They contribute only through grouping/counting, capture, sequences, or local bonuses. Tarot-game point counting is commonly performed in packets rather than by assigning “five points” to a Five. [Pagat, tarot point counting](https://www.pagat.com/tarot/counting.html)

**[Correction]** The printed numeral is rank, not card-point value.

## Trick-taking role

The Five must follow Coins if possible. If unable to follow, a player may have to trump, depending on the regional rules. It has no occult privilege, cannot substitute for a trump, and does not ordinarily score five points.

## Tarocchino and Sicilian exceptions

- Bologna: the Five is removed.
- Sicily: the Five survives as a low Coins card.
- Minchiate: it survives as an ordinary Coins pip.
- German/Austrian Tarock: Italian suits were largely replaced by French suits; Five of Diamonds occupies the corresponding structural position where low numeral cards are retained.

## Trump orders and the Fool

These matters do not change this card but clarify its environment.

Historians reconstruct three broad Italian trump-order families:

1. **Type A, associated with Bologna/Ferrara:** virtues and papal/imperial figures arranged in an order differing from later Marseille.
2. **Type B, associated especially with Venice and northeastern traditions.**
3. **Type C, associated with Milan and the line that ultimately informs Marseille.**

Early cards were often unnumbered, so local players learned their ranking. Justice and Fortitude/Strength move between systems; Waite later deliberately placed Strength at VIII and Justice at XI. The Fool generally has no fixed rank and functions as an excuse that can be played without following suit, subject to surrender or compensation rules.

The Five of Coins is unaffected: it remains a suit card and cannot enter trump-order disputes.

### The “1500 poems”

The phrase covers several bodies of material rather than one canonical text: *tarocchi appropriati*, in which named cards are assigned to people or qualities; Boiardo’s moralized card project; and later macaronic or satirical poetry.

**Finding:** they document tarot’s social and literary presence. I found no securely transcribed early poem that assigns poverty, snow, a church, Mercury in Taurus, or “Material Trouble” specifically to the Five of Coins.

### Conflicts

1. **Etteilla versus Golden Dawn/Waite:** love and affinity versus material trouble.
2. **Waite versus modern hopeful reading:** Waite names destitution but does not say the figures overlook available sanctuary.
3. **Papus versus Golden Dawn:** both use Qabalistic structures, but Papus’s tetragrammatic cycling is not identical to Book T’s Geburah-in-Assiah/decan system.
4. **Marseille numerology versus Golden Dawn astrology:** Marseille five-as-development can be constructive; Golden Dawn’s Geburah disrupting Earth is predominantly difficult.
5. **Thoth versus Waite–Smith:** same decan, very different image—abstract structural anxiety rather than social poverty.
6. **Reversals:** Etteilla, Mathers, and Waite use them; Golden Dawn elemental dignity, Crowley, and Jodorowsky need not.

### Final Heh

Papus and related systems map the suits to the letters of YHVH:

- Wands = Yod;
- Cups = first Heh;
- Swords = Vau;
- Pentacles = final Heh.

Final Heh signifies manifestation, completion, the daughter, or material receptacle. This is **[esoteric attribution]**, not evidence that Renaissance coin pips encoded the divine name.

### Sephirah Five

In the Golden Dawn/Crowley system, Five is Geburah: strength, severity, cutting, martial force, correction, and disruption. When Geburah operates in Earth/Assiah, fixed material structures are pressured or broken. This yields the title “Material Trouble.”

A frequent modern gloss—“Five always means change”—is too generic. Book T’s logic is specifically Geburic severity; Crowley calls it disruption and strain.

### Mercury in Taurus

Mercury is mobile, analytical, commercial, and communicative; Taurus is fixed Earth, material endurance, possession, and inertia. Golden Dawn and Crowley interpret their combination as friction:

- anxious thought fixed on security;
- bookkeeping or intelligence forced into labor;
- movement constrained by material resistance;
- ingenuity under economic pressure.

The attribution is to the **first face/decan of Taurus**, not to a natal placement or the entire sign.

## Divinatory meanings by author

Quotation is kept brief; wording beyond those excerpts is paraphrased.

### Etteilla, 1783–89 tradition

- Upright: lover or mistress; husband/wife; affection, sweetness, attachment, concord.
- Reversed: misconduct, illicit or disgraceful love, disorder, dissipation, debauchery.

**Source status:** The vocabulary is transmitted through Grand Etteilla cards and Mathers’s explicitly Etteilla-derived table. Exact captions vary by Grand Etteilla state and later printer.

### S. L. MacGregor Mathers, 1888

Mathers prints:

> “Lover or Mistress, Love, Sweetness, Affection, Pure and Chaste Love.”

Reversed: disgraceful love, imprudence, license, profligacy. He adds that it “shows simply that there is someone whom the person loves.” [Mathers, pp. 17, 19–20](https://www.howardnema.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/12/the-tarot-1888-by-macgregor-mathers-1.pdf)

This is not yet the Golden Dawn “Material Trouble” meaning, despite Mathers’s later central role in that order.

### Golden Dawn, Book T

- Core: material trouble, loss, poverty, anxiety over resources.
- Better expression: labor, building, cultivation, practical intelligence, gain following effort.
- Worse expression: financial loss, unemployment, destitution, stagnation.
- No simple reversed meaning is necessary; neighboring elemental dignity modifies it.

### A. E. Waite, 1910–11

- Upright: “material trouble above all”; destitution; alternatively the inherited lovers/affinity meanings, which Waite declares irreconcilable.
- Reversed: disorder, chaos, ruin, discord, profligacy.

Waite does not explicitly list spiritual poverty, ignored help, recovery, or “scarcity mindset” in the card entry.

### Aleister Crowley, 1944

Crowley’s title is **Worry**. His prose emphasizes:

- Geburah disrupting Earth;
- Mercury unsettling Taurus;
- intelligence applied to labor;
- instability in matter’s foundations;
- great strain combined with long inaction.

Crowley does not employ a separate reversed meaning. The card contains a residual cohesive force: the five tattvas prevent total disintegration.

### Paul Foster Case / B.O.T.A.

Case retains Mercury in Taurus and the Golden Dawn title complex. The usual B.O.T.A. reading is material limitation, worry about work or money, the necessity of careful practical thought, and constructive adaptation through disciplined labor.

**Textual caution:** *The Book of Tokens* supplies no pip-by-pip meanings. Attributions to that title are erroneous.

### Rachel Pollack

In *Seventy-Eight Degrees of Wisdom*, Pollack reads the Waite–Smith card as hardship, ill health, unemployment, poverty, and exclusion, but also stresses companionship and the possibility that spiritual meaning survives material deprivation. Reversal can indicate improvement, acceptance of assistance, or continuing disorder depending on context.

**[Modern interpretive development]** Pollack is among the major spreaders of psychological and narrative reading of Smith’s pips. Her wording should be consulted in the edition cited below; no authorized full online text was located.

### Mary K. Greer

Greer’s practical and therapeutic approach emphasizes material or bodily loss, rejection, insecurity, illness, and the experience of being “left out in the cold.” Reversal may externalize, release, internalize, or begin recovery from the upright condition rather than merely negate it.

**[Modern interpretive development]** The common contemporary formula “recovery from financial loss” is compatible with Greer-style reversal work but is not a historically ancient reversed meaning. Exact editions vary, and no openly licensed primary text was located.

## Esoteric and symbolic readings

## Alchemical

### Sola Busca

The furnace-like Five of Coins has been interpreted as heat acting on matter, the male operator penetrating or cultivating Earth, and a stage in production of the philosophers’ stone.

- First visible basis: approximately 1490–91 card.
- Explicit alchemical exposition: principally twentieth-century scholarship, especially Sofia Di Vincenzo in the 1990s.
- Spreaders: modern Sola Busca editions, museum commentary, occult tarot writers.
- Evidence: compatible images and deck-wide metallurgy.
- Limitation: no Renaissance explanatory key.

### Thoth

Crowley’s five tattva disks create an organism held together at the edge of material breakdown. This is Hermetic-elemental rather than laboratory alchemy strictly construed.

## Kabbalistic

Lévi joined tarot, Hebrew letters, tetragrammaton, and magical doctrine, but the fully articulated Five of Pentacles as **Geburah in Assiah** belongs to the Golden Dawn.

The card can therefore be read as:

- Severity in the World of Action;
- force cutting into resources;
- judgment embodied as economic or physical limit;
- the corrective power that exposes unsound foundations.

No evidence links this reading to Renaissance cardmakers.

## Astrological

Mercury in Taurus first becomes securely attached to the card in Golden Dawn material. It was spread through *Book T*, Crowley’s publications, Waite-adjacent occult teaching, Case, and modern decan tarot.

A productive reading emphasizes planning, craft, and practical intelligence. A difficult reading emphasizes worry, obsessive accounting, immobilized thought, and labor without immediate reward.

## Hermetic

The Five combines:

- number/sephirah;
- element/world;
- planet;
- zodiacal face;
- divine and angelic names;
- color scale;
- geometric talisman.

This synthetic construction is characteristic of the late-nineteenth-century Hermetic revival, not of medieval tarot.

## Christian

Smith’s apparent church window encouraged Christian readings:

- bodily poverty outside spiritual wealth;
- the Church’s duty of charity;
- the “least of these” in Matthew 25;
- exile, pilgrimage, mercy, and the five wounds of Christ.

Only the lighted casement and mendicants are documented in Waite’s text. Calling the five pentacles the Five Wounds is **[modern Christian interpretation]**; no statement by Smith or Waite verifies it.

An ethically opposite Christian reading sees institutional failure: sanctuary glows, yet the suffering remain outside. This is particularly prominent in contemporary social-justice readings.

## Jungian

Typical modern Jungian interpretations include:

- the wounded ego confronting loss of persona or status;
- shadow material around dependency, shame, money, illness, and exclusion;
- winter as psychic withdrawal;
- the window as an image of the Self or containing community;
- the pair as split aspects of a single psyche;
- asking for help as reintegration.

**Historical caution:** these are post-Jung tarot applications. Jung did not identify tarot’s Five of Pentacles with an archetype in a published systematic commentary.

## People and works

### Artists and makers directly attached to the card

- **Bonifacio Bembo and workshop/circle:** traditional attribution for Visconti–Sforza cards; disputed at card and subgroup level.
- **Jacopo Sagramoro:** documented Ferrara painter of trionfi packs in 1442, although no surviving Five is securely tied to his hand.
- **Sola Busca engraver:** anonymous; Mattia Serrati da Cosandola and Nicola di Maestro Antonio have appeared in attribution debates.
- **Jean Noblet, Jean Dodal, Nicolas Conver:** printers/cardmakers in the Marseille lineage.
- **Jean-Baptiste Alliette/Etteilla:** first great systematizer of tarot cartomancy.
- **Pamela Colman Smith:** creator of the decisive scenic image.
- **A. E. Waite:** designer/author who supplied “material trouble” and the published caption.
- **Jessie Burns Parke and Paul Foster Case:** B.O.T.A.’s non-scenic Hermetic redesign.
- **Frieda Harris and Aleister Crowley:** Thoth’s abstract “Worry.”
- **Robert Wang; Chic and Sandra Tabatha Cicero:** modern Golden Dawn reconstructions.
- **Philippe Camoin and Alejandro Jodorowsky:** Marseille restoration/redesign and numerical reading.

### Occultists and historians

Court de Gébelin, Comte de Mellet, Etteilla, Lévi, Paul Christian, Papus, Wirth, Mathers, Waite, Crowley, Case, and Israel Regardie successively constructed or transmitted occult systems.

Michael Dummett, Thierry Depaulis, Ronald Decker, Stuart Kaplan, Robert Place, Helen Farley, and Italian historians including Franco Pratesi and the Le Tarot association reconstructed the gaming and documentary history. Their conclusions differ on details, but they broadly agree that tarot was created in fifteenth-century northern Italy for play and acquired systematic occult interpretation in eighteenth-century France.

### Literature, poetry, film, and art

#### Italo Calvino

*The Castle of Crossed Destinies* constructs narratives from tarot layouts, particularly Visconti and Marseille imagery. Tarot functions as a combinatorial language.

**Finding:** no securely established, uniquely identifiable literary episode centered on the Five of Pentacles was located. Claims that a particular Calvino character “is” this card require edition/layout-specific demonstration.

#### T. S. Eliot, *The Waste Land*

Eliot names “Madame Sosostris,” “the drowned Phoenician Sailor,” “Belladonna,” “the man with three staves,” “the Wheel,” and “the one-eyed merchant,” while inventing or altering the deck.

**Negative finding:** the Five of Pentacles is not named. Identifying Eliot’s crowd, winter, or poverty imagery with it is modern comparison, not documented allusion.

#### W. B. Yeats

Yeats belonged to the Golden Dawn and knew its tarot symbolism, but no card-specific poem or prose passage securely centered on the Five of Pentacles was located.

#### Ingmar Bergman

Tarot imagery and the personification of Death are often invoked when discussing *The Seventh Seal*. The direct visual comparator is the Death trump, not the Five of Pentacles.

#### Alejandro Jodorowsky

Jodorowsky’s films use initiatory, alchemical, Christian, and tarot-like imagery, and he later wrote extensively about Marseille tarot. No single film shot could be securely documented as a quotation of the Five of Deniers. Broad thematic resemblance is not proof of quotation.

### Paintings and buildings

No pre-1909 painting or building was found that can be documented as Smith’s direct model for this card. Gothic stained glass, medieval mendicant imagery, winter charity scenes, and illustrations of poverty form plausible visual contexts, but a specific source remains open.

Modern stained-glass windows and murals sometimes reproduce Smith’s pentacle-window design, but these are quotations *from* the card, not its historical sources. A reliable catalogue raisonné of such installations was not located.

## Controversies and disputes

## Egyptian origin

Court de Gébelin’s 1781 essay claimed tarot was the surviving Egyptian Book of Thoth. Comte de Mellet added Hebrew-letter and divinatory constructions; Etteilla converted the claim into an Egyptianized divination system.

- **Status:** **[Legend/esoteric attribution]**
- **Evidence offered by proponents:** speculative etymology, visual analogy, a belief that Egyptian priests encoded wisdom in images.
- **Counter-evidence:** Italian documentary records, Italian suits and social imagery, fifteenth-century manufacture, and the absence of any Egyptian tarot object or transmission record.
- **Historical importance:** enormous. The theory generated occult tarot even though it does not explain tarot’s actual invention.

The Morgan catalogue bluntly identifies tarot’s astrological use as deriving from misinformation published by Gébelin. [Morgan catalogue](https://www.themorgan.org/manuscript/158525)

## “Gypsy” or Romani origin

Nineteenth-century writers, most influentially Papus through the title *Tarot des Bohémiens*, promoted the idea that Romani people carried ancient Egyptian or Indian tarot wisdom into Europe.

- **Status:** **[Legend]**
- **Evidence:** none linking Romani migration to the invention or early transmission of tarot.
- **Mechanism of the myth:** the obsolete English word “Gypsy” itself arose from the mistaken belief that Roma came from Egypt; this meshed conveniently with the Book-of-Thoth theory.
- **Correction:** tarot is documented at Italian courts before any evidence of a specifically Romani tarot tradition.

Roma did later practice many forms of fortune-telling, as did non-Roma Europeans. That historical practice does not prove invention.

## Cathar and Templar claims

Claims connect tarot to:

- Cathar dualism;
- a Grail tradition at Montségur;
- Templar secrets or Baphomet;
- underground Gnostic watermarks;
- coded survival of persecuted initiates.

- **Status:** **[Disputed fringe reconstruction/legend]**
- **Evidence cited:** analogies among trumps, Grail literature, medieval watermarks, and supposed heretical symbols.
- **Problems:** no Cathar or Templar document describes tarot; the earliest secure tarot evidence is later and concentrated in northern Italian court and urban play; the Five of Coins supplies no specifically Cathar or Templar content.
- **Modern spreaders:** occult and alternative-history writers influenced by nineteenth-century Grail speculation, Harold Bayley’s watermark theories, and later esoteric authors.

A defense of the Cathar scenario exists, but it explicitly contests the documentary school rather than adding a contemporary tarot source. [“Occult Tradition of the Tarot”](https://ibnarabisociety.org/wp-content/uploads/PDFs/El-Moor_Occult-tradition-2.pdf)

## Was tarot originally occult?

Dummett’s central argument was methodological: the rules and structure establish a trick-taking game; contemporary documents call it a game; early moralists condemn gambling; no contemporary occult key survives.

- **Documented:** gaming origin and centuries of game use.
- **Possible but unproven:** card imagery could support moral, mnemonic, festive, dynastic, or devotional associations.
- **Unsupported:** a single secret initiatory code explaining all 78 cards from inception.

Robert Place and other symbol-oriented historians allow more Renaissance Hermetic or Neoplatonic context than Dummett did. That is a dispute over cultural meaning, not evidence for Golden Dawn correspondences in the fifteenth century.

## The Sola Busca source claim

“Smith copied the Sola Busca minors” is too broad.

- Clearly related compositions exist for several cards.
- Smith plausibly had access to photographs.
- The Five of Coins compositions are not alike.
- Therefore the Sola Busca is a **deck-level precedent and partial source**, but not the demonstrated source of the Five of Pentacles scene.

## Who are Smith’s figures?

Proposed identifications include beggars, pilgrims, lepers, an injured man and impoverished woman, social outcasts, or spiritually blind seekers.

Only “two mendicants” is Waite’s wording. Leprosy, marriage, moral fault, and deliberate refusal of help remain interpretations.

## Is it definitely a church?

The Gothic tracery and stained glass strongly suggest one. Waite says only “lighted casement.” Calling it a church is a reasonable visual inference, not a confirmed title supplied by the creators.

## Does the card promise available help?

This is one of the most pervasive modern readings. The visible light makes it psychologically persuasive, but:

- no door is shown;
- the building’s institutional identity is unconfirmed;
- Waite says nothing about help;
- the figures may be passing an inaccessible sanctuary.

Thus “help is nearby” is **[modern tradition]**, while “warmth exists on the other side of a barrier” is a defensible visual observation.

## Misprints and traditions

No single famous printing error specific to the Five comparable to major-trump numbering disputes was verified.

What can be documented is cumulative editorial drift:

- *Coins* becomes *Pentacles* and is then mistaken for an ancient suit name.
- Etteilla’s lover meaning is sometimes described as “traditional Marseille,” although the Marseille cards carried no printed divinatory key.
- Grand Etteilla continuous numbering differs among editions, leading to inconsistent card numbers.
- “Thoth published 1944” often conflates publication of Crowley’s book/plates with the full commercial deck, which appeared in 1969.
- “Sola Busca 1491” is sometimes treated as an exact printing date; the engraving, inscription, coloring, and later ownership history do not all fall in one securely documented moment.
- Waite’s Strength/Justice exchange is sometimes attributed to Smith; it reflects the Golden Dawn/Waite scheme.
- Modern captions turn the visible pentagrams into a complete Tree of Life, although only five emblems are present.

## Open questions

1. **The direct visual source of Smith’s mendicants:** no specific print, painting, stage design, or photograph has been demonstrated.
2. **The identity and condition of the figures:** Waite’s “mendicants” is secure; leprosy and family relationship are not.
3. **Smith versus Waite authorship:** no surviving design memorandum assigns individual details of this pip to either creator.
4. **The Sola Busca program:** metallurgical and alchemical interpretations are plausible but lack a contemporary key.
5. **Exact early dating:** Visconti-family decks are dated by heraldry, style, coin devices, and family events, not colophons.
6. **Earliest extant Five of Coins:** the Visconti–Sforza/Carrara card is the earliest securely identified in the sources consulted, but unpublished fragments or revised cataloguing could alter priority.
7. **Etteilla state and numbering:** Grand Etteilla impressions vary. “No. 73” should always be tied to a named edition rather than treated as universal.
8. **Case’s precise pip vocabulary:** much resides in graded B.O.T.A. lessons rather than stable public first editions.
9. **Pollack and Greer wording:** copyright-limited searchable sources do not support extensive verbatim quotation. Their books should be consulted in the cited editions.
10. **Marseille printer intention:** floral designs may contain workshop emblems and visual jokes, but comprehensive documentation of intended meanings does not survive.
11. **Modern “spiritual poverty”:** it is thematically compatible with the window but not in Waite’s entry; its earliest exact printed occurrence remains to be established.
12. **Architectural quotations:** no authoritative inventory of real stained glass or buildings modeled on Smith’s card was found.

## Conclusions

The Five of Pentacles has three substantially different histories superimposed upon it.

The first is the **game card**: five money signs, probably present from tarot’s formation in fifteenth-century northern Italy, ranked within the reversed “round” suit and normally worth no special points. Its oldest securely identified survivor is an ornamented Visconti–Sforza Five of Coins. Marseille preserved this non-narrative type for centuries.

The second is the **cartomantic card**: Etteilla and Mathers made it a card of lovers, affection, and—when reversed—misconduct. That meaning is older in print than poverty.

The third is the **Hermetic card**: the Golden Dawn assigned it Geburah in Earth, Mercury in Taurus, and the title “Lord of Material Trouble.” Waite and Smith literalized that title as two mendicants in snow; Crowley and Harris abstracted it into an inverted pentagram of strained disks titled “Worry.”

The most familiar modern lessons—unnoticed help, spiritual warmth, scarcity mentality, institutional exclusion, mutual aid, and recovery—are fruitful readings of Smith’s image, but they are twentieth- and twenty-first-century developments. They are not meanings recoverable from fifteenth-century play.
