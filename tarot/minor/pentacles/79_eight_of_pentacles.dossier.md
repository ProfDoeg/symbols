# Eight of Pentacles: Research Dossier

## Method and evidentiary labels

This dossier uses the following labels throughout:

- **Documented fact** — supported by a surviving card, archival document, contemporary printed text, or museum catalogue.
- **Scholarly reconstruction** — a conclusion argued from surviving objects, documents, stylistic evidence, or game history but not stated in a contemporary source.
- **Tradition** — a convention transmitted by cardmakers, players, or readers without a demonstrable ancient origin.
- **Esoteric attribution** — an intentionally constructed occult correspondence.
- **Disputed claim** — a proposal for which competent authorities or evidence disagree.
- **Legend** — a historical-origin narrative unsupported by contemporary evidence.
- **Modern invention** — an interpretation or design first demonstrable in modern tarot culture.

Short quotations are taken from the cited editions; spelling and capitalization are retained where useful.

The Morgan Library identifies its surviving card M.630.32 explicitly as “The Eight of Coins” and describes it as “Eight coins … surrounded by floreate border.” The pack was probably made in Milan after Francesco Sforza’s entry as duke on 25 March 1450. This is the earliest securely catalogued surviving Eight of Coins located in the present research. [Morgan collection overview](https://www.themorgan.org/collection/tarot-cards), [object image and description](https://ica.themorgan.org/manuscript/page/32/158525), [manuscript catalogue](https://www.themorgan.org/manuscript/158525).

### What is not applicable to this card

The Papess/Juno, Pope/Jupiter, Maison Dieu/Tower, Fool numbering, Justice–Strength interchange, and the three regional trump orders concern the trump sequence. They never renamed or renumbered the Eight of Coins. They matter to the history of the pack but not to this pip’s identity.

Likewise:

- The Gébelin, Mellet, Lévi, Papus, and Golden Dawn assignments of individual Hebrew letters are principally assignments to the **twenty-two trumps**.
- Crowley’s Tzaddi/Heh revision concerns the Emperor and Star. It does not alter the Eight of Disks.
- Court-card sub-elements and zodiacal spans do not apply to a numeral card.
- The Eight has a **decan** attribution, not a single Hebrew letter or a Tree-of-Life path.

# Iconography deck by deck

## 1. Marziano da Tortona and Michelino da Besozzo, Milan, approximately 1415–1420

**Documented fact:** Marziano described a specially designed pack for Filippo Maria Visconti, with sixteen classical gods divided among four suits of birds. The accompanying paintings by Michelino are lost.

**Finding:** It was not the standard seventy-eight-card tarot, and no Eight of Coins belonging to it is known. Calling it the first tarot is a **scholarly classification or disputed precursor claim**, not something Marziano wrote. It supplies evidence for experimentation with permanent trump-like cards at the Milanese court, not an ancestral image for this card. Le Tarot’s documentary discussion dates the commission approximately 1415–1420. [Le Tarot, “Le origini italiane del Ludus Triumphorum”](https://letarot.it/Le-origini-italiane-del-Ludus-Triumphorum_pag_pg439_ita.aspx).

## 2. Visconti di Modrone / Cary-Yale, Milan, approximately 1440s

**Documented fact:** The deck uses coins bearing Visconti monetary and heraldic imagery and differs structurally from the standard later tarot, notably through extra court figures and theological-virtue trumps.

**Finding:** I could not verify a surviving Eight of Coins in the accessible institutional catalogues. Reproduction decks often fill lacunae; those restorations must not be mistaken for surviving fifteenth-century cards.

“Cary-Yale” and “Visconti di Modrone” normally designate the same surviving group, not two independent complete decks.

## 3. Brambilla deck, Milan, approximately mid-fifteenth century

**Documented fact:** The Brambilla cards are preserved at the Pinacoteca di Brera and attributed to the Milanese/Visconti environment, conventionally to Bonifacio Bembo’s workshop. Brera describes itself as holding 48 cards from the late-Gothic pack made for the duke of Milan. [Pinacoteca di Brera](https://pinacotecabrera.org/news/mostra/il-segreto-dei-segreti-i-tarocchi-sola-busca/).

**Finding:** No securely catalogued Brambilla Eight of Coins was located. Its coin suit uses repeated monetary devices rather than narrative scenes.

## 4. Visconti-Sforza / Pierpont Morgan–Bergamo pack, Milan, after 1450

**Documented fact:** The Eight survives. It consists of eight circular gold coin devices set symmetrically on a tooled-gold field, surrounded and connected visually by foliage. There is no human figure, workshop, apprentice, or divinatory inscription.

The coin faces display dynastic/monetary imagery. Its primary function is immediate recognition of suit and rank; the foliage is ornamental and courtly.

**Meaning at the time:** There is no contemporary evidence that the floral pattern encoded “prudence,” work, apprenticeship, Hod, Virgo, or Earth. Those are later readings.

## 5. Ferrarese and Este cards, later fifteenth century

The surviving Este/Ferrarese fragments establish regional tarot production but do not provide a continuous, securely attributable iconographic history of this particular pip.

**Finding:** No surviving Este Eight of Coins was verified in the institutional material consulted. Repeated suit emblems were normal. Any claim that an isolated floral or heraldic motif carried the modern meaning “craftsmanship” is retrospective.

## 6. Rosenwald and Budapest sheets, late fifteenth or early sixteenth century

These woodcut sheets are important for reconstructing inexpensive printed tarot production, especially the trumps and court types.

**Finding:** I did not locate an institutionally catalogued Eight of Coins on the accessible reproductions sufficient to describe its exact ornament confidently. Their importance to this card is structural: printed popular tarot retained non-scenic numeral pips.

## 7. Sola Busca, engraving dated 1491; painted copy probably 1490s

**Documented fact:** The Sola Busca is the earliest surviving complete seventy-eight-card tarot and the earliest surviving deck in which all numeral cards receive elaborate pictorial treatment. The copper engravings carry a 1491 date; the surviving colored pack was painted subsequently. It entered the Brera in 2009. [Brera](https://pinacotecabrera.org/news/mostra/il-segreto-dei-segreti-i-tarocchi-sola-busca/), [complete public-domain image set](https://publicdomainreview.org/collection/sola-busca).

The Eight of Coins is not a simple Marseille-style arrangement. Its shields/coins participate in the Sola Busca’s dense emblematic, vegetal, and material world.

### Was it the source of Pamela Colman Smith’s Eight?

**Lead checked: not established.**

Smith almost certainly had an opportunity to know the Sola Busca photographs exhibited by the British Museum in 1908, one year before the Waite-Smith deck. Several Waite-Smith cards have striking Sola Busca analogues, especially the Three of Swords and Ten of Wands/Ten of Swords configuration.

But no close compositional prototype for Smith’s seated craftsman has been demonstrated in the Sola Busca Eight of Coins. The claim “the Sola Busca pip was the source of Smith’s scene” is therefore too strong.

The defensible statement is:

- **Scholarly reconstruction:** Sola Busca helped demonstrate a historical precedent for illustrated pip cards and probably influenced selected Waite-Smith compositions.
- **Unproved attribution:** Smith’s Eight of Pentacles specifically derives from the Sola Busca Eight.
- **Likely modern development:** the craftsman-at-bench scene is Smith’s own pictorial realization of the Golden Dawn/Waite meanings of skill, industry, money in small sums, and painstaking work.

A comparative museum page documents several genuine visual parallels but does not establish an Eight-to-Eight copying relationship. [House of White comparative survey](https://waitesmith.org/index.php/decks/sola-busca-vs-waite-smith/).

## 8. “Mantegna Tarocchi,” approximately 1460s

**Documented fact:** These engravings are arranged in pedagogical series—social estates, Apollo and the Muses, liberal arts, virtues, cosmological spheres—not as a playable tarot deck.

**Conclusion:** They are irrelevant to the Eight of Coins except as part of the Renaissance visual and mnemonic environment. The label “tarocchi” is later and misleading.

## 9. Minchiate, Florence and Tuscany, sixteenth century onward

Minchiate expanded the trump series to 40 trumps while retaining four Latin suits.

The Eight of Coins remained a repeated-emblem suit card. It did not acquire an independent workshop scene. Its exact rank depended on the game’s suit-ranking convention; it carried no occult title.

**Negative finding:** Minchiate’s added virtues, zodiacal signs, and elements are trumps. They do not prove that the ordinary Eight of Coins originally meant Virgo, Earth, or Prudence.

## 10. Bolognese tarocchino

The shortened Bolognese pack removed lower numeral cards but normally retained the higher cards including the Eight. It was an ordinary suited card.

**Documented game distinction:** Bolognese trump peculiarities—the four equal-ranked *Moretti*, special status of Angel, World, Bagatto, and Fool—do not change the Eight of Coins. [Pagat summary of Bolognese traditions](https://www.pagat.com/tarot/).

## 11. Sicilian tarot

In the surviving Sicilian game, the suits include *oro* or coins. The cards descend King, Queen, Horse, Maid, 10, 9, 8, 7, 6, 5 and sometimes 4. Thus the Eight is a surviving ordinary numeral rank below 9 and above 7. [Pagat, Sicilian Tarocchi](https://www.pagat.com/tarot/sicilian.html).

Its imagery is patterned rather than RWS-style narrative art.

## 12. Jean Noblet, Paris, approximately 1650

**Documented fact:** Noblet is the earliest known substantially surviving deck conforming to what scholars conventionally call the Tarot de Marseille graphic type. “Tarot de Marseille” is a later category; Noblet worked in Paris. [BnF](https://essentiels.bnf.fr/fr/image/4799f2d6-6f88-4f6f-a15c-40a1a943c95e-tarot-jean-noblet).

The Huit de Deniers displays eight circular deniers in a balanced ornamental field. Curving stems, leaves, and flowers occupy the intervals. There is no figure and ordinarily no Arabic numeral: the viewer counts the suit signs.

## 13. Jean Dodal, Lyon, approximately 1701–1715

Dodal maintains the same fundamental structure: eight deniers integrated with floral scrollwork. Differences are those of block cutting, coloring, flower shape, and spacing—not a change into narrative symbolism.

**Interpretive caution:** Statements that the flowers “mean growth of craftsmanship” are modern readings unless tied to a named later author.

## 14. Nicolas Conver, Marseille, 1760/1761 and later impressions

Conver’s Eight is a particularly influential form: four horizontal rows of two deniers divided by a large central floral cross, with lateral floral ornaments and ribbons/stems.

A BnF witness often reproduced as “Conver 1760” is a later impression associated with Conver’s workshop/successors; catalogue dating of individual sheets must not automatically be equated with the first cutting of the blocks. The accessible BnF-based description records eight coins, a central eight-part flower or floral cross, side tulips, and no printed rank numeral. [BnF-based card description](https://www.marselia.app/cards/huit-de-denier).

### Marseille ornament and meaning

- **Documented fact:** the ornament organizes the suit signs and fills negative space.
- **Tradition:** later Marseille readers treat vegetation as living energy circulating through material form.
- **Modern invention:** assigning each leaf, petal, or color a fixed ancient alchemical code without a historical source.

## 15. Besançon pattern

The Besançon pattern’s famous changes—Juno for the Papess and Jupiter for the Pope—were responses to confessional or regional sensitivities affecting trumps. The Eight of Coins remained a conventional pip. It was not renamed Juno, Jupiter, or otherwise.

## 16. Etteilla’s Grand Etteilla, first published approximately 1788–1789

Etteilla reorganized, renumbered, and retitled the tarot as an alleged restored Egyptian *Book of Thoth*. His cards bear upright and reversed keywords.

In later Grand Etteilla editions, card 70 combines:

- eight floral coin emblems in a 3–2–3 arrangement;
- a seated young woman writing;
- upright **FILLE BRUNE** (“dark-haired girl”);
- reversed **USURE** (“usury”);
- side labels identifying *Le Huit de Denier* and its ordinary-card equivalent, *Le Huit de Trèfle*.

This is visible in the digitized historical manual/deck plate. [Le grand Etteilla, digitized edition](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/2/2f/Le_grand_Etteilla%2C_ou%2C_l%27art_de_tirer_les_cartes_..._Le_tout_recueilli_..._%28IA_b29321220%29.pdf).

**Important distinction:** This card does not originally mean “Prudence.” Etteilla’s printed axis is a person-card upright and usury reversed. Later English occultists combined or replaced this system with Golden Dawn meanings.

## 17. Court de Gébelin, Mellet, Lévi, Paul Christian, Papus, and Wirth

None supplied a historically important new Eight-of-Coins picture comparable to Smith or Harris.

- Gébelin and Mellet concentrated on the trumps and Egyptian theory.
- Lévi’s Hebrew-letter architecture concerned the twenty-two keys.
- Paul Christian elaborated imaginary Egyptian initiations and popularized *arcana* terminology.
- Papus systematized tarot numerology and suit correspondences.
- Wirth’s 1889 deck comprised twenty-two trumps, so there is no Wirth Eight of Coins.

Claims about “Wirth’s Eight of Pentacles” ordinarily refer to a later publisher’s completion or a deck inspired by Wirth, not Wirth’s original 1889 twenty-two-card work.

## 18. Golden Dawn manuscripts / Book T, approximately 1888 onward

Book T prescribes an emblematic, non-narrative card:

- eight pentacles;
- green plant stems joining or surrounding them;
- Sun and Virgo glyphs;
- Golden Dawn color scales;
- title “Lord of Prudence.”

Its text gives:

> “Over-careful in small things at the expense of great: ‘Penny wise and pound foolish’: gain of ready money in small sums; mean; avaricious; industrious; cultivation of land; hoarding, lacking in enterprise.”

It then locates the card in:

> “Hod of Heh. (Skill: prudence: cunning).”

The digitized Book T scan confirms Sun and Virgo and these meanings. [Book T PDF](https://benebellwen.com/wp-content/uploads/2013/02/mathers-and-felkin-golden-dawn-book-t-the-tarot-1888.pdf).

“Hod of Heh” means the eighth sephirah expressed through the final-Heh/Earth field. It is not a claim that the card bears the Hebrew letter Heh in the way a trump bears one of twenty-two letters.

### Wang and Cicero reconstructions

Robert Wang’s *Golden Dawn Tarot* and Chic and Sandra Tabatha Cicero’s later reconstruction translate manuscript instructions into publishable decks. They are **modern reconstructions**, not surviving official Victorian Golden Dawn cards. Their foliage, colors, and pentagram-inscribed disks make Book T’s abstract force-field visible.

## 19. Waite-Smith, London, 1909

Pamela Colman Smith depicts a young craftsman seated sideways on a bench outside a town. He holds one disk against a support and works it with a hammer/chisel. Six completed pentacles hang vertically; another lies near or below the bench. The distant town suggests separation from ordinary civic life while the work is done.

Waite calls him:

> “An artist in stone at his work, which he exhibits in the form of trophies.”

The displayed disks carry pentagrams. This is one of Smith’s decisive transformations of a numeral pip into a human scene.

### What changed

- Coins became **pentacles**—coin-like disks explicitly bearing pentagrams.
- Floral symmetry became sequential production.
- The card’s meaning became visible as labor, repetition, apprenticeship, and technique.
- Book T’s ambivalence remains latent: careful work can become narrowness, cunning, avarice, or “penny wise” over-attention.

### Who introduced the pentacle?

Pentagrams long predate tarot, and nineteenth-century French occultists made extensive magical use of “pentacles.” But the standard historical French and Italian suit was coins/deniers, not pentagrams.

The Golden Dawn institutionalized **Pentacles** as the Earth suit and placed pentagrams on the disks; Waite and Smith made that terminology globally familiar. Thus:

- “Pentacle” as a magical object: much older.
- Pentacles as the historical fifteenth-century tarot suit name: false.
- Pentacles as the dominant English esoteric tarot suit: Golden Dawn and Waite-Smith tradition.

## 20. Paul Foster Case and BOTA, twentieth century

Case adopted the Golden Dawn correspondences but did not retain Smith’s illustrated minor-suit scenes. The BOTA Minor Arcana are emblematic pips intended for study and coloring.

The Eight therefore returns to geometric suit signs rather than a worker at a bench. BOTA teaches the first decan of Virgo and the Golden Dawn-style polarity of skill/industry versus avarice and shortsightedness.

The deck is credited to Case and artist Jessie Burns Parke. [BOTA deck overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/B.O.T.A._tarot_deck), [BOTA materials](https://storebota.org/products/major-and-minor-tarot-keys).

## 21. Crowley–Harris Thoth Tarot, painted 1938–1943; published 1969

The card is titled **PRUDENCE** and the suit **DISKS**.

Frieda Harris paints a stylized plant or tree rooted in earth. Its eight disks appear as flowers or fruits arranged in a calculated organic-geometric scheme. The card is neither Marseille floral filler nor Smithian genre illustration: it is a diagram of growth through intelligent material organization.

Crowley writes:

> “The Eight of Disks is called Prudence.”

and:

> “The number Eight, Hod, is very helpful in this card, because it represents Mercury in his most spiritual aspect, and he both rules and is exalted in the sign of Virgo, which belongs to the Decan, and is governed by the Sun.”

He defines it as:

> “intelligence lovingly applied to material matters, especially those of the agriculturalist, the artificer and the engineer.”

He further connects the disks to flowers or fruit, fertile land, birth, and the geomantic figure *Populus*. [Crowley, *The Book of Thoth*, “The Small Cards”](https://hermetic.com/crowley/book-of-thoth/small-cards).

### Textual anomaly

The same online chapter appears to preserve displaced text about the Eight of Disks as “Rubeus,” “Blight,” and Saturnian bad money. That description properly belongs with a different disks card in the surrounding sequence and conflicts with the printed Prudence discussion and actual Harris card.

**Classification:** transmission/OCR or HTML-assembly error, not an alternative doctrine of the Eight. The card itself and Crowley’s dedicated “PRUDENCE / EIGHT OF DISKS” section give Sun in Virgo.

## 22. Grimaud / Paul Marteau, 1930 and later

The Grimaud *Ancien Tarot de Marseille* regularized a Marseille/Conver-derived design and coloring. The Eight remains eight deniers around a central floral cross.

Marteau’s twentieth-century commentary interprets the balanced four-and-four arrangement as material equilibrium and reciprocal organization. That is a **modern symbolic exegesis of traditional ornament**, not evidence for the intentions of seventeenth-century block cutters.

## 23. Camoin–Jodorowsky, 1997

Philippe Camoin and Alejandro Jodorowsky produced a reconstructed/restored Marseille deck from surviving models and printing traditions. It preserves the eight-denier floral configuration while adjusting line, color, and details.

Claims that this is the single “original” tarot recovered intact are **disputed**. It is a modern synthesis based on historical materials, not a facsimile of one complete primordial deck.

Jodorowsky and Marianne Costa read the card through number, suit, symmetry, and circulation of material energy. Their approach is psycho-spiritual and morphological rather than archival.

## 24. Notable modern redesigns

Modern decks generally follow one of four families:

1. **RWS narrative:** apprentice, artisan, engraver, potter, coder, student, or repetitive skilled labor.
2. **Marseille restoration:** eight coins and vegetation without a human scene.
3. **Thoth abstraction:** organized growth, plant geometry, disks as fruit or cells.
4. **Cultural relocation:** Smith’s workshop is translated into a specific craft tradition.

These are intentional reinterpretations. The frequent modern keywords “practice,” “mastery,” “upskilling,” “career development,” “quality control,” and “perfectionism” descend chiefly from Waite-Smith plus Golden Dawn “skill/prudence,” not from medieval game use.

# The game

## Original function

**Documented fact:** Tarot originated as a trick-taking game. The standard pack contains 21 permanent trumps, the Fool, and four fourteen-card suits, each with ten numeral cards and four courts. The Eight of Coins is one of those ordinary numeral cards. [Metropolitan Museum overview](https://www.metmuseum.org/exhibitions/listings/2016/world-in-play/exhibition-galleries).

## Rank

Historical tarot games used two principal numeral rankings:

- In many early Italian traditions, Swords and Batons ranked 10 high downward, while Cups and Coins ranked Ace high in reverse numerical order.
- In France and in some later or simplified systems, all four suits rank 10 high down to Ace.

Consequently, an Eight of Coins could be:

- below 7 and above 9 in an inverse Coins ranking; or
- below 9 and above 7 in the ordinary 10-high ranking.

Early French rules of 1637 retained reverse ranking in Coins and Cups; by the 1659 rules all suits were reported 10 high. This history is summarized from Thierry Depaulis’s rule discoveries and Dummett–McLeod. [Historical-rules discussion and references](https://forum.tarothistory.com/viewtopic.php?p=7782).

In 54-card Central European Tarock packs, lower red-suit cards were often removed while the 1–4 survived; black suits retained 7–10. That explains why an Eight may be absent depending on how the Latin Coins suit was converted into red or black French suit signs.

## Point value

The Eight is normally a **low or empty card**:

- it takes tricks by suit rank;
- it ordinarily contributes no special card-point premium;
- kings, honors, high trumps, the Fool, and sometimes other named cards carry the scoring weight.

Exact counting systems vary and often count cards in packets of three, subtracting a fixed amount per packet. [Pagat, tarot card-point counting](https://www.pagat.com/tarot/counting.html).

## Fool and trump-order issues

The Fool is generally an excuse-like card or special honor rather than a numbered trump in traditional play. The three principal Italian trump orders reconstructed by Dummett, and disputes over Justice, Fortitude, and the theological cards, have no bearing on the rank of the Eight of Coins. The card can never beat a trump merely because it has the number eight.

## Conflicts and corrections

### Sun or Mercury?

Book T assigns the **Sun** to the first decan of Virgo. Mercury is relevant because:

- Mercury rules Virgo;
- in traditional astrology Mercury is exalted in Virgo;
- Hod is the Mercurial sephirah.

Thus “Sun in Virgo” and “Mercurial Hod” are different layers, not necessarily contradictory.

Some later summaries mistakenly call the card “Mercury in Virgo,” apparently collapsing sign ruler, sephirah, and decan ruler. Crowley explicitly says the decan is governed by the Sun.

### Sephirah 8

The numeral Eight corresponds to **Hod**, the eighth sephirah. In Golden Dawn small-card doctrine, each Eight manifests Hod in one of the four suit/world fields.

For Pentacles this is conventionally Hod in Assiah/Earth, expressed as **Hod of final Heh**. It does not place the card on one of the twenty-two paths.

### Final Heh

In fourfold Hermetic mappings:

- Wands = Yod / Fire
- Cups = first Heh / Water
- Swords = Vav / Air
- Pentacles = final Heh / Earth

This is an **esoteric attribution**, not a historical explanation of Italian *denari*.

### Tzaddi/Heh swap

Crowley’s reassignment of Tzaddi and Heh concerns the Emperor and Star:

> “All these old letters of my Book are aright; but צ is not the Star.”

It does not modify the Eight of Disks’ Sun–Virgo–Hod attribution.

# Divinatory meanings by author

## Etteilla

### Grand Etteilla card tradition

- Upright: **“Fille brune”** — dark-haired girl.
- Reversed: **“Usure”** — usury.

These are printed on surviving later Grand Etteilla plates. They differ sharply from the now-familiar apprentice interpretation. [Digitized *Grand Etteilla*](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/2/2f/Le_grand_Etteilla%2C_ou%2C_l%27art_de_tirer_les_cartes_..._Le_tout_recueilli_..._%28IA_b29321220%29.pdf).

Attribution to “Etteilla” must be edition-specific because decks issued by his followers altered numbering, pictures, equivalents, and keywords. The exact 1789 first-state wording could not be independently verified from a clean first-edition scan; the cited plate witnesses the established Grand Etteilla lineage.

## S. L. MacGregor Mathers, 1888

Mathers’s printed handbook gives the inherited cartomantic cluster:

- Upright: a dark girl, generally presented as honest.
- Reversed: usury, voided ambitions, vanity, cupidity, avarice.

His advice was to write upright meanings at one end of a card and reversed meanings at the other. [Mathers, *The Tarot*, 1888](https://hermetics.net/media-library/tarot/samuel-liddell-macgregor-mathers-the-tarot/).

This is not yet the full Golden Dawn Book T “Lord of Prudence” doctrine. Conflating Mathers’s public 1888 booklet with the order’s later/internal Book T recension obscures that distinction.

## Golden Dawn Book T

Book T does not use a simple upright/reversed binary; it speaks in terms of dignity and mixed potential:

> “Over-careful in small things at the expense of great: ‘Penny wise and pound foolish’”

alongside:

> “gain of ready money in small sums,” “industrious,” and “cultivation of land.”

The card combines:

- skill;
- prudence;
- industry;
- cultivation;
- small material gain;
- cunning;
- avarice;
- hoarding;
- lack of enterprise.

This ambivalence directly anticipates both Waite’s craftsmanship and his reversed cunning/usury.

## A. E. Waite, *Pictorial Key to the Tarot*, 1910/1911

Waite’s complete entry reads:

> “An artist in stone at his work, which he exhibits in the form of trophies.”

Upright:

> “Work, employment, commission, craftsmanship, skill in craft and business, perhaps in the preparatory stage.”

Reversed:

> “Voided ambition, vanity, cupidity, exaction, usury. It may also signify the possession of skill, in the sense of the ingenious mind turned to cunning and intrigue.”

[Waite, “Eight of Pentacles”](https://sacred-texts.com/tarot/pkt/pktpe08.htm).

“Apprentice” is a reasonable reading of the preparatory stage and of Waite’s comparison with the Three of Pentacles, but the Eight’s own caption says “artist,” not explicitly “apprentice.”

## Aleister Crowley, *The Book of Thoth*, 1944

Crowley did not normally recommend reversals as a separate dictionary. Dignity, context, and surrounding forces modify a card.

Core wording:

> “It signifies intelligence lovingly applied to material matters, especially those of the agriculturalist, the artificer and the engineer.”

Its positive range includes prudence, careful cultivation, technical intelligence, preparation, and the turn toward renewed fertility. Shadow implications follow from excessive caution, withdrawal, or overly calculated action, but Crowley does not supply a formal “reversed Eight of Disks” paragraph. [Crowley](https://hermetic.com/crowley/book-of-thoth/small-cards).

## Paul Foster Case / BOTA

The BOTA teaching transmitted by Case and Ann Davies gives approximately:

- Well dignified: skill managing material affairs; industry; gain in subordinate, clerical, writing, or travel-related work; ready money in small amounts.
- Ill dignified: avarice, hoarding, “penny wise and pound foolish,” meanness, problems with superiors or inferiors, loss through trying to overreach others.

This is recognizably a Golden Dawn recension rather than an independent medieval tradition. [Comparative BOTA summary](https://moderatelyquicksilver.com/tag/builders-of-the-adytum/).

## Rachel Pollack

In *Seventy-Eight Degrees of Wisdom* (first published in two parts, 1980 and 1983), Pollack develops the RWS image as disciplined work, education, craftsmanship, and commitment to doing a task well. The card’s limitation is absorption in technique or work at the expense of broader life.

**Source caution:** A reliable searchable page containing Pollack’s complete card paragraph was not located. I therefore do not present wording as a verbatim quotation. Consult Pollack, *Seventy-Eight Degrees of Wisdom*, revised edition, the Pentacles section. The available digitized volume is indexed here: [digitized Pollack volume](https://legacy-eutopia.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/02/Seventy-Eight-Degrees-of-Wisdom.pdf).

## Mary K. Greer

Greer treats the card through work, productive skill, practice, attention to detail, and development of competence. Her broader reversal method refuses to equate reversal automatically with “bad.” A reversed card may be:

- blocked or resisted;
- internal or private;
- delayed;
- excessive or underdeveloped;
- misused or redirected;
- undergoing reconsideration.

Thus a reversed Eight may indicate blocked practice, compulsive perfectionism, unrewarding repetition, underdeveloped competence, or skill turned toward an unworthy end.

Again, a copyright-compliant searchable source did not expose a definitive card-specific sentence suitable for verbatim quotation. The method is documented in Greer’s *Tarot for Your Self* and *The Complete Book of Tarot Reversals*. [Digitized *Tarot for Your Self*](https://legacy-eutopia.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/02/Tarot-for-Your-Self-Workbook.pdf), [Google Books edition](https://books.google.com/books/about/Tarot_for_Your_Self.html?id=TJSMDwAAQBAJ).

# Esoteric and symbolic readings

## Alchemical

### Historical finding

No fifteenth-century source identifies the Eight of Coins as an alchemical emblem.

### Later readings

- Etteilla’s entire restored “Book of Thoth” was embedded in Hermetic and alchemical speculation.
- The coin may be read as fixed matter; repeated working as purification; eight completed forms as stages of material perfection.
- In Thoth, rooted vegetation, fruit/disks, and solar influence in an earth sign support a reading of carefully ripened matter.

These are **esoteric or modern analogies**, not the original cardmakers’ documented program.

## Kabbalistic

The Golden Dawn places the card at Hod in the Earth/final-Heh field.

Symbolic logic:

- Hod: intellect, analysis, classification, language, technique, Mercury.
- Earth/Pentacles: material embodiment and results.
- Combined: intelligence applied to making, organizing, cultivating, or engineering matter.

Shadow:

- analysis narrowed into pedantry;
- cleverness into cunning;
- conservation into hoarding;
- attention to detail into failure to see the whole.

This appears in Book T and is spread internationally through Waite, Crowley, Case, Regardie, Wang, and Cicero.

## Astrological

The card is the first decan of Virgo, conventionally 0°–10° Virgo, ruled by the Sun in the Golden Dawn’s use of the Chaldean planetary sequence.

Its standard modern calendar dates are approximately 23 August–2 September, but exact ingress dates vary by year and ephemeris. The astrology is symbolic rather than evidence that medieval cardmakers encoded a birth period.

Crowley combines:

- solar vitality and gestation;
- Virgo’s agriculture, craft, discrimination, and preparation;
- Mercury’s rulership and exaltation in Virgo;
- Hod’s intellectual skill.

## Hermetic

Etteilla’s Hermeticism emphasizes an imaginary Egyptian restoration. Golden Dawn Hermeticism instead integrates:

- four elements;
- four worlds;
- Tetragrammaton;
- ten sephiroth;
- 36 decans;
- seven classical planets;
- color scales and divine names.

The Eight’s Hermetic identity is therefore a composite constructed in the late nineteenth century, not recovered from the Visconti coin design.

## Christian

Coins naturally invited Christian moral readings concerning labor, stewardship, avarice, honest exchange, usury, talents, and the dignity of craft.

But no medieval sermon or theological commentary specifically identifying the tarot Eight of Coins with these themes was found. Applying the parable of the talents, Benedictine labor, or the sin of avarice to this card is a later analogical reading.

Etteilla/Waite’s reversed “usury” gives the clearest documented bridge to a Christian moral category.

## Jungian

Jung did not publish a card-by-card interpretation of the Eight of Pentacles.

Modern Jungian tarot commonly treats Smith’s worker as:

- ego discipline serving individuation;
- repeated conscious practice that gives form to unconscious potential;
- the artisan archetype;
- embodiment rather than fantasy;
- creation of a stable psychic container.

Its shadow becomes:

- compulsive productivity;
- perfectionism;
- identification with work;
- mechanical repetition;
- failure to integrate craft into a larger Self.

This is **modern Jungian application**, developed by post-Jungian tarot writers, especially from the later twentieth century. It must not be attributed directly to Jung.

- **Pamela Colman Smith:** devised the canonical modern craftsman image.
- **Arthur Edward Waite:** supplied the conceptual and divinatory program.
- **Jessie Burns Parke and Paul Foster Case:** BOTA’s emblematic redesign.
- **Frieda Harris and Aleister Crowley:** botanical-geometric Eight of Disks, “Prudence.”
- **Paul Marteau:** influential twentieth-century Marseille symbolism.
- **Philippe Camoin and Alejandro Jodorowsky:** 1997 Marseille reconstruction.
- **Marianne Costa:** co-developed Jodorowsky’s modern structural and psycho-spiritual readings.
- **Rachel Pollack and Mary K. Greer:** major modern psychological and reader-centered interpreters.

## Literature, film, and art

### Italo Calvino

*The Castle of Crossed Destinies* uses tarot cards as a combinatorial language for stories. Different editions and the paired texts use Visconti and Marseille imagery.

**Finding:** No canonical episode centered uniquely and securely on the Eight of Coins was located. Calvino is important to tarot reception generally, not demonstrably to this card’s iconographic history.

### T. S. Eliot

*The Waste Land* names “the man with three staves,” “the one-eyed merchant,” the Wheel, the drowned Phoenician Sailor, and “a blank card” in Madame Sosostris’s reading.

**Negative finding:** The Eight of Pentacles is not named. Identifying the “one-eyed merchant” with this card is speculative and unsupported by the poem.

### W. B. Yeats

Yeats was deeply involved with the Golden Dawn and occult symbolism.

**Negative finding:** I found no work in which he demonstrably identifies or interprets the Eight of Pentacles specifically.

### Bergman

Ingmar Bergman’s *The Seventh Seal* uses medieval Christian allegory, Death, chess, plague, and judgment imagery often compared with tarot trumps.

**Negative finding:** It does not provide a demonstrable quotation of the Eight of Pentacles.

### Jodorowsky’s films

Jodorowsky repeatedly employs tarot, alchemy, initiation, mutilation/reconstitution, and Marseille-derived symbolism. His books and reconstructed deck provide direct tarot doctrine.

**Finding:** No single shot or building in his films could be securely identified as a deliberate quotation of the Eight of Deniers rather than a broad artisanal, coin, or mandala motif.

### Paintings and buildings

Smith’s image belongs generally to the long European iconography of artisans at work: engravers, goldsmiths, stonemasons, and apprentices in workshop scenes.

**Open finding:** No specific painting or building was documented by Smith or Waite as its source. Claims linking it to one named guild image or cathedral mason should be treated as unproved until supported by correspondence, sketchbooks, or a close visual prototype.

# Controversies and disputes

## 1. Egyptian origin

### The claim

Court de Gébelin declared in volume VIII of *Le Monde primitif* (1781) that tarot preserved Egyptian priestly wisdom and invented Egyptian-sounding etymologies for *tarot*. Mellet’s accompanying essay linked tarot with the Book of Thoth, divination, and Hebrew letters.

### Evidence

No Egyptian tarot pack, Egyptian text describing tarot, or transmission chain exists. Gébelin wrote before Champollion’s 1822 decipherment of Egyptian hieroglyphs.

### Classification

- As history: **legend/disproved origin claim**.
- As the origin of Western occult tarot: **documented intellectual history**.

Dummett emphasized that documentary evidence points to fifteenth-century Italian play and that no occult use is demonstrable before the eighteenth-century intervention. [Dummett, “Origins of Tarot”](https://www.nybooks.com/articles/1981/05/14/origins-of-tarot/). The detailed history appears in Decker, Depaulis, and Dummett, *A Wicked Pack of Cards*. [Google Books record](https://books.google.com/books?id=nVyDAAAAMAAJ).

Nothing on the medieval Eight of Coins resembles a hieroglyphic text; Etteilla’s Egyptian card 70 is an eighteenth-century redesign.

## 2. Romani or “Gypsy” transmission

The Romani were incorrectly regarded in Western Europe as Egyptians; nineteenth-century writers, especially Jean-Alexandre Vaillant and popular occultists, connected them with tarot’s alleged Egyptian migration.

There is no evidence that Romani brought tarot into Europe. Tarot is documented in northern Italian elite and urban settings before that proposed role is recorded.

- Historical origin claim: **legend**.
- Romani participation in later European fortune-telling cultures: historically possible and in many contexts documented, but it cannot retroactively prove invention or transmission of tarot.

## 3. Cathar origin

Modern writers sometimes claim the trumps encode Cathar dualism or a secret survival of Cathar doctrine.

No fifteenth-century document, deck inscription, or transmission chain connects tarot with Cathars. More importantly for this card, repeated deniers are ordinary Italian playing-card suit signs and supply no specifically Cathar content.

Classification: **modern speculative legend**.

## 4. Templar origin

Claims that surviving Templars encoded their doctrine in tarot face a chronological problem: the order was suppressed in the early fourteenth century, while identifiable tarot appears in the fifteenth.

No Templar Eight of Coins, rule book, workshop record, or initiatory text survives.

Classification: **legend/modern invention**.

## 5. Sola Busca as direct source for Smith’s Eight

The broader Sola Busca influence on Waite-Smith is plausible and visually demonstrable for selected cards. The specific Eight-to-Eight derivation is not.

Classification:

- Smith saw or could have seen British Museum Sola Busca material: **scholarly reconstruction with strong circumstantial support**.
- Her Eight copies the Sola Busca Eight: **unsupported/disputed**.
- Her scene turns Golden Dawn “skill/industry” into visual narrative: **strong iconographic reconstruction**.

## 6. “First fully illustrated minors”

The Sola Busca, not Waite-Smith, is the earliest surviving complete tarot with pictorial treatments throughout the numeral suits.

Calling Waite-Smith “the first illustrated Minor Arcana” is therefore a common error. A more accurate statement is that Waite-Smith established the dominant modern narrative-pip system and made such scenes widely available.

## 7. “Pentacles” as the ancient suit

The fifteenth-century suit is *denari*—coins. French cards call it *deniers*. Spanish cards use *oros*. Pentacles are an occult renaming.

Classification:

- Coins as historical suit: **documented fact**.
- Earth as original medieval meaning: **unproved**.
- Pentacles/Earth/final Heh: **Golden Dawn esoteric attribution**.
- Calling a Visconti card “Eight of Pentacles”: acceptable modern cataloguing shorthand, but historically anachronistic.

## 8. Etteilla’s “Prudence”

Etteilla is sometimes credited online with all later conventional meanings.

For this card, the visible Grand Etteilla evidence gives “dark-haired girl” and “usury,” not “Lord of Prudence” or Sun in Virgo. “Prudence” belongs to the Golden Dawn decan system and was retained by Crowley.

## 9. Crowley’s geometry and geomancy

Crowley’s final discussion invokes *Populus*, but digitized copies sometimes place contradictory “Rubeus,” Saturn, and blight material adjacent to the Eight. The actual Harris image, card title, Sun/Virgo glyphs, and dedicated Prudence paragraph support *Populus* and cultivated growth.

Classification: likely **textual displacement or digitization error**, not a valid competing attribution.

## 10. Hod means “splendor,” not simply craftsmanship

Hod is conventionally translated “splendor,” “majesty,” or “glory.” Equating Hod directly with manual work is an interpretive simplification.

The craft meaning arises from the combination:

- Eight → Hod/Mercury/intellect;
- Pentacles → Earth/material form;
- Sun in Virgo → ordered, discriminating cultivation;
- Book T meanings → industry and skill.

## 11. Numerological eight as universally ancient

Eight can symbolize balance, doubling of four, cubic or spatial order, regeneration, infinity, or perfection in different systems. No evidence shows that all these were intended in early tarot pips.

The familiar sideways-eight infinity sign is especially suspect when projected backward: the card bears an upright rank and early pips do not assert that symbolism.

## 12. Misprints becoming tradition

The Grand Etteilla family contains reversed inscriptions, variant numberings, differently oriented keywords, and copied engraving errors. Later readers sometimes treated printer variations as intentional occult mysteries.

Similarly, modern Thoth booklets have circulated a misprint identifying the Eight of Disks as Mercury in Virgo; the painted card and Crowley’s full text give **Sun in Virgo**. The booklet/searchable text itself notes that the actual symbolism reads Sun in Virgo.

# Open questions

1. **Exact date of the Visconti-Sforza Eight:** “about 1450” or “after March 1450” is more defensible than a single year. Its workshop attribution and production sequence remain subjects of stylistic study.

2. **Exact relation between Smith and Sola Busca:** Circumstantial access and several visual correspondences are strong, but no diary or letter has been found in which Smith identifies the Sola Busca as her source.

3. **Smith’s immediate model for the craftsman:** The scene may draw on contemporary workshop illustration, guild imagery, medievalizing Arts-and-Crafts taste, theatrical design, or Smith’s own synthesis. No unique prototype is established.

4. **First exact printed occurrence of “Lord of Prudence”:** The title is secure in the Golden Dawn Book T tradition, conventionally dated to the order’s late-1880s material. Surviving copies are later transcripts and publications; manuscript stemmatics complicate the phrase “first appearance.”

5. **Etteilla first-state wording:** Later Grand Etteilla plates securely preserve “Fille brune / Usure.” A clean comparison across the 1788–1789 first issue and subsequent Etteilla I, II, and III families remains necessary before assigning every visual detail to Etteilla personally.

6. **Rosenwald/Budapest Eight:** Accessible scans did not permit a sufficiently reliable card-level identification. It should remain undescribed rather than reconstructed from modern facsimiles.

7. **Pollack and Greer verbatim entries:** Their copyrighted books were identifiable, but a trustworthy, edition-paginated public transcription of the complete Eight entry was not found. Paraphrase has therefore been used.

8. **Jodorowsky–Costa card-level wording:** Different English, French, and Spanish editions organize pip discussions differently. The general structural reading is clear, but card-specific quotation requires checking a named edition directly.

9. **Historical use in divination before Etteilla:** There is evidence for earlier cartomancy and for Bolognese card-derived lot practices, but no secure fifteenth- or sixteenth-century divinatory definition of the Eight of Coins located here.

---

# Conclusions

The reconstructable history divides into four distinct lives:

1. **Game card, fifteenth century onward:** eight repeated coins, without occult meaning demonstrably attached.
2. **Ornamental pip:** Visconti and Marseille cardmakers organize the coins through heraldry, foliage, symmetry, and printing craft.
3. **Cartomantic card, eighteenth–nineteenth centuries:** Etteilla and Mathers give person and usury meanings.
4. **Hermetic and pictorial card, late nineteenth century onward:** Book T makes it Hod in Earth, Sun in Virgo, “Lord of Prudence”; Smith converts skill and industry into an artisan scene; Harris converts them into cultivated organic geometry.

The most important verified corrections are:

- The earliest surviving example located is the Visconti-Sforza Eight of Coins, not the Sola Busca.
- Sola Busca is an important precedent for illustrated pips, but its Eight is not a demonstrated direct source for Smith’s craftsman.
- “Prudence” and Sun in Virgo are Golden Dawn attributions, explicitly retained by Crowley.
- The historical suit is Coins/Deniers/Denari/Oros; “Pentacles,” Earth, final Heh, and Hod are later occult constructions.
- Medieval game evidence supplies no documented divinatory meaning for the card.
- Egyptian, Romani-transmission, Cathar, and Templar origin narratives belong to the history of tarot mythology, not the documented origin of the card.
