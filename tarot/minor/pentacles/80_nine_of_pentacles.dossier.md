# Nine of Pentacles: Research Dossier

## Method and evidentiary labels

This dossier distinguishes six kinds of statement:

- **Documented fact** — supported by a surviving card, archival record, contemporary text, or authoritative catalogue.
- **Scholarly reconstruction** — a conclusion argued from surviving objects or documents but not explicitly recorded by contemporaries.
- **Tradition** — a meaning or practice transmitted within cartomancy or occult tarot.
- **Esoteric attribution** — a deliberately imposed occult correspondence, not a claim about the card’s original gaming function.
- **Disputed claim** — supported by some writers but challenged by evidence or other scholarship.
- **Legend/modern invention** — a late claim lacking evidence from the card’s historical period.

The Nine of Pentacles is a pip card, not a trump. Consequently, much of the requested material concerning trump names, Hebrew-letter sequences, Tzaddi/Heh, regional trump orders, and renamed Popess/Pope cards does not attach individually to it. Those matters are included only where they explain the historical pack containing the card.

### The suit-name problem

**Documented fact.** Medieval and early-modern Italian decks use *denari/danari*: literally money or coins. French packs use *deniers*, the name of a historical coin. Spanish-suited terminology uses *oros*, “golds.” The suit sign in early decks is therefore a coin or circular monetary emblem—not an occult pentagram.

**Esoteric attribution.** The English term **Pentacles** became conventional through nineteenth-century ceremonial magic and especially Golden Dawn-derived tarot. A magical pentacle is a consecrated disk or talisman; it need not bear a pentagram. Golden Dawn *Book T* nevertheless depicts its suit objects as pentacles, and Pamela Colman Smith placed five-pointed stars inside her disks.

**Modern convention.** Crowley renamed the suit **Disks**, arguing that the symbol should express rotating or material energy rather than simply money. His late pip cards increasingly resemble coins again.

## Iconography, deck by deck

### Fifteenth-century Milan

#### Brera–Brambilla tarot, Milan, approximately 1442–1447

**Documented fact.** This incomplete luxury deck was made for Filippo Maria Visconti and is now in the Pinacoteca di Brera. Its surviving Coins cards reproduce large gold coin-like emblems bearing ducal devices. Dummett noted that the impressions are larger than real coins; they imitate monetary iconography rather than being literal stamped currency. The Brera describes the group as forty-eight late-Gothic cards associated with the Milanese duke.

**Nine of Coins.** Where catalogued among the surviving numeral sequence, its composition is non-scenic: nine repeated ducal coin emblems on an illuminated ground. There is no vineyard, woman, falcon, pentagram, astrology, or divinatory inscription.

**Meaning in context.** The coin emblems advertise dynasty, wealth, legitimacy, and patronage, but interpreting the individual Nine as “material gain” would be anachronistic. That title first belongs to the Golden Dawn.

#### Cary–Yale / Visconti di Modrone tarot, Milan, approximately 1440s

**Documented fact.** Sixty-seven cards survive at Yale’s Beinecke Library. The pack unusually includes female Pages and Knights. Yale reports that recent attribution has shifted some attention from Bonifacio Bembo to his older brother Andrea Bembo. The Coins bear Visconti monetary/heraldic imagery.

**Nine of Coins.** Again, it is a heraldically decorated pip arrangement, not a narrative scene. The date has been variously placed around the 1441 Visconti–Sforza marriage, 1442–1447, or later. Consequently, calling it unconditionally “the first Nine of Pentacles” goes beyond the evidence. [Yale Library](https://library.yale.edu/news/cards-library-partners-work-together-solve-mysteries-rare-tarot-deck?page=1)

#### Pierpont Morgan–Bergamo / Visconti–Sforza tarot, Milan, approximately 1450s

**Documented fact.** The surviving cards are divided among the Morgan Library, Accademia Carrara, and Colleoni collection. Its pip cards consist primarily of repeated suit emblems placed decoratively against tooled gold grounds.

The Nine of Coins has nine circular emblems laid out ornamentally. The historical image supplies no separate individual narrative. Any modern association with self-sufficient womanhood comes from the Waite–Smith image, not this Milanese card.

#### Other Milanese and Ferrarese fragments

**Documented fact.** The Este, Charles VI, Catania, and other fifteenth-century fragments preserve parts of early tarot packs, but the survival of a card or group does not establish a complete individual genealogy for every pip. Several “Este” attributions and dates have moved as connoisseurship has developed.

**Absence finding.** No surviving contemporary text assigns a divinatory interpretation to a fifteenth-century Nine of Coins.

### Mantegna “Tarocchi,” approximately 1460s

**Documented fact.** The so-called Mantegna Tarocchi is a series of fifty educational engravings divided into social and cosmological orders. It is neither a tarot pack nor a playable 78-card deck and has no Nine of Coins.

**Relevance.** It belongs to the same Renaissance culture of ordered emblematic series and was later drawn into tarot comparisons. It is not an ancestor of this individual card.

### Rosenwald and Budapest sheets, late fifteenth or early sixteenth century

**Documented fact.** These uncut printed sheets document inexpensive tarot production, as opposed to unique courtly illuminated packs. They are important for the transition toward repeatable regional patterns.

For a pip such as the Nine of Coins, the relevant development is mechanical arrangement: repeated suit signs and restrained vegetal filling. Attribution, exact chronology, and intended regional pattern remain partly debated. No independent divinatory text accompanies these sheets.

### Sola Busca, engraving probably northern Italy; illuminated Venice, 1491

**Documented fact.** The Sola Busca is the earliest complete surviving 78-card tarot. It consists of copper engravings printed on paper and, in its unique complete copy, colored and gilded. Inscriptions support illumination in Venice in 1491. Brera scholarship associates its production with Nicola di Maestro Antonio and possibly the Venetian humanist Marin Sanudo; the proposed role of Ludovico Lazzarelli in designing its Hermetic-alchemical program remains a reconstruction. [Brera catalogue](https://pinacotecabrera.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/11/Il-Segreto-dei-tarocchi-eng.pdf)

#### What its Nine of Coins depicts

The card is not a garden lady. It shows a human figure lying or falling face-down beneath a structure in which circular coin-disks are gathered above heat or fire. Its imagery has been interpreted as metallurgical, alchemical, punitive, initiatory, or satirical.

- **Documented fact:** prone figure, fire/apparatus, and nine disks are visible.
- **Scholarly reconstruction:** the surrounding deck employs Hermetic-alchemical imagery.
- **Disputed claim:** the exact process or identity represented by the Nine is not securely decoded.

#### Was it the source of Pamela Colman Smith’s Nine?

The item-specific lead is only partly correct.

- **Documented fact:** photographic facsimiles of twenty-three Sola Busca cards entered the British Museum in 1907, shortly before Smith drew the Waite pack. Several Waite–Smith minors closely echo Sola Busca compositions.
- **Disputed inference:** Smith’s Nine of Pentacles was directly copied from the Sola Busca Nine of Coins.
- **Contrary visual evidence:** the two Nine cards depict fundamentally different scenes.
- **More plausible reconstruction:** Smith’s falconer may draw upon the Sola Busca **Page of Coins/Shields**, which includes falconry imagery, while her general decision to narrativize the pips may have been encouraged by Sola Busca. [Comparative card archive](https://waitesmith.org/index.php/decks/sola-busca-vs-waite-smith/)

Thus “the Sola Busca pip was the source” should not be repeated without qualification.

### Minchiate, Florence, sixteenth–nineteenth centuries

**Documented fact.** Minchiate expanded the trump series to forty but retained four ordinary Italian suits. The Nine of Coins remains a repeated-emblem numeral card. It acquired no special trump-like iconography.

In play, it belonged to the reversed-ranking round suits: high court cards and Ace, then lowward through the numeral sequence, subject to the particular rules and pack reduction in use.

### Bolognese tarocchino

**Documented fact.** The reduced 62-card Bolognese pack removes numeral cards 2–5 but retains 6–10 and Ace. Hence the Nine of Coins survives. Its imagery is a patterned pip, not a scene.

Its individual point value is negligible; it matters through trick-taking, sequences, and combinations. The Bolognese *Matto* has no rank, while four high trumps are equal in trick-taking order. These trump peculiarities do not alter the Nine’s identity. [Pagat: Ottocento](https://www.pagat.com/tarot/ottocento.html)

### Sicilian tarot

The traditional Sicilian pack is heavily shortened and locally distinctive. Depending on the form described, the surviving low cards of Coins do not necessarily include the Nine. It is therefore unsafe to describe a universal Sicilian Nine of Coins. In Mineo play, special low Coins cards and an inverted Ace complicate comparison with the full 78-card pack. [Pagat: Sicilian Tarocchi](https://www.pagat.com/tarot/sicilian.html)

### Jean Noblet, Paris, approximately 1650–1680

**Documented fact.** Noblet’s surviving seventy-three-card pack is the earliest substantially preserved example conventionally classified as Tarot de Marseille. The BnF dates it to the second half of the seventeenth century and identifies it as stencil-colored woodcut. [BnF](https://essentiels.bnf.fr/fr/image/4799f2d6-6f88-4f6f-a15c-40a1a943c95e-tarot-jean-noblet)

The **Neuf de Deniers** consists of nine coin-rosettes integrated with symmetrical foliage. It bears no scene and no printed meaning. “Tarot de Marseille” itself is a retrospective label; Romain Merlin used it in 1856, and Paul Marteau popularized it in the twentieth century.

### Jean Dodal, Lyon, approximately 1701–1715

Nine circular deniers appear as a balanced ornament embedded in flowering stems. The deck’s vocabulary and spelling vary—*Deniers/Dinies*—as was normal in vernacular card printing. Dodal’s is a Type-I Marseille-line pattern, made partly for export. [BnF-linked archive](https://foolmade.art/archive/dodal-tarot)

### Nicolas Conver, Marseille, traditional date 1760

The familiar composition is four pairs of deniers—two pairs above and two below—with a ninth in the center, enclosed by mirrored ribbon-like foliage and axial palmettes.

**Dating caution.** “Conver 1760” names the historical pattern or workshop foundation. The BnF witness often reproduced online is catalogued to 1809–1833; it should not automatically be described as a physical impression from 1760. [Card and catalogue discussion](https://www.marselia.app/cards/neuf-de-denier)

### Grimaud–Marteau, Paris, 1930

Paul Marteau’s **Ancien Tarot de Marseille**, issued by Grimaud, standardized a recolored Conver-derived pattern for twentieth-century occult and commercial use. The Nine preserves the central coin and four paired coins with floral arabesque. Its colors were treated symbolically by Marteau, but they are not proof of medieval color doctrine.

### Camoin–Jodorowsky, Marseille/Paris, 1997

Philippe Camoin and Alejandro Jodorowsky reconstructed a Marseille-style deck from multiple historical witnesses. They called the result a restoration of the authentic tarot.

- **Documented fact:** it is a late twentieth-century composite redesign.
- **Esoteric/traditional claim:** its small details and colors restore a primordial symbolic language.
- **Disputed claim:** no single historical printing corresponds exactly to the composite.

The Nine remains an abstract floral pip. Jodorowsky and Marianne Costa read Marseille pips relationally through number, suit, visual direction, and growth rather than through Waite’s garden narrative.

### Etteilla’s Grand Etteilla / *Livre de Thot*, 1780s–1790s

Etteilla reordered and redesigned tarot as an explicitly occult and divinatory book. The Nine of Coins appears in the numbered Coins sequence, usually as card 69 in later Grand Etteilla arrangements. Upright and reversed keywords are printed on the card.

Its central words are:

- Upright: **Effet** — effect, result, accomplishment.
- Reversed: **Duperie** — deception, trickery, fraud.

Later editions expanded these into lists of consequences, execution, achievement and personal effects versus swindle, error, infidelity and sleight of hand. [Etteilla-derived transcription](https://stolen-thyme.com/etteillas-suit-of-coins/)

**Dating caution.** The phrase “Grand Etteilla 1789” often compresses a complicated publication history. Etteilla issued his tarot teaching in installments from 1783 onward; surviving deck states are difficult to date with absolute precision, and later Types II and III contain alterations and printing errors.

### Oswald Wirth, 1889

Wirth’s famous twenty-two-card deck illustrated only the trumps. It therefore contains no Nine of Pentacles.

Papus’s *Tarot des Bohémiens*, which reproduced Wirth trumps, nevertheless supplied a numerological method for the minor cards. Papus calls the Nine of Coins:

> “Équilibre de l’Équilibre. Fortune durable.”

“Balance of balance; lasting fortune.” The phrase appears in the 1889 French text around pp. 316 and 322. [Papus source index](https://sacred-texts.com/tarot/tob/index.htm)

### Golden Dawn *Book T*, composed in the 1890s; first published in *The Equinox*, 1912

The manuscript description reads:

> “A WHITE Radiating Angelic Hand, holding a rose branch with nine white roses, each of which touches a Pentacle.”

The pentacles are arranged in a structured ninefold pattern, with Venus and Virgo shown above and below. The title is **Lord of Material Gain**. Its meaning includes:

> “Complete realization of material gain, good, riches; inheritance; covetous; treasuring of goods; and sometimes theft and knavery.”

The whole changes “according to dignity”—Golden Dawn practice did not depend solely on turning cards upside down.

The card is **Yesod of Heh**, more precisely Yesod in the elemental world represented by final Heh/Earth. It is assigned to the second decan of Virgo and to the angels Hazayel and Aldiah in published transliterations. [Book T scan, p. 30](https://benebellwen.com/wp-content/uploads/2013/02/mathers-and-felkin-golden-dawn-book-t-the-tarot-1888.pdf)

#### Wang and Cicero reconstructions

Robert Wang’s *Golden Dawn Tarot* and the Ciceros’ *Magical Tarot of the Golden Dawn* are twentieth-century visual realizations of Order documents, not surviving nineteenth-century Golden Dawn decks. Their Nine follows the white hand, rose branch, nine pentacles, and Venus/Virgo formula with different color execution.

### Waite–Smith, London, 1909

Pamela Colman Smith depicts a solitary richly dressed woman in a vineyard or manorial garden. Nine pentacles grow or stand among the vines. A hooded falcon rests on her gloved hand; a small snail crosses the foreground. A distant house or manor marks the enclosure as cultivated property.

Waite describes it:

> “A woman, with a bird upon her wrist, stands amidst a great abundance of grapevines in the garden of a manorial house.”

He adds that the wide domain suggests abundance and may be her own possession. His meanings are:

> “Prudence, safety, success, accomplishment, certitude, discernment.”

Reversed:

> “Roguery, deception, voided project, bad faith.”

[*Pictorial Key to the Tarot*, 1910/1911](https://www.integralpsychology.org/uploads/1/5/3/0/15300482/waite_tarot.pdf)

#### Sources and changes

- **Documented:** Smith knew theatrical costume, book illustration, and Golden Dawn tarot.
- **Strong reconstruction:** the image translates *Material Gain* and Waite’s “prudence/safety” into landed abundance.
- **Plausible source:** Renaissance falconry imagery, perhaps including Sola Busca’s Page of Coins.
- **Disputed:** that the woman is a widow, courtesan, specific historical person, or “kept woman.” Waite does not say so.
- **Modern interpretation:** financial independence and self-made feminine autonomy. This is now dominant but is not Waite’s explicit language.
- **Modern shadow reading:** enclosure becomes isolation or a gilded cage. The hooded bird becomes disciplined desire or restricted freedom.
- **Unverified popular claim:** the snail has a fixed occult meaning supplied by Waite. No such explanation has been traced in *Pictorial Key*.

### Paul Foster Case and BOTA

Case retained the Golden Dawn framework: Pentacles/Earth, Virgo decans, and the Tree of Life. The BOTA deck itself is chiefly a “corrected” major-arcana deck intended for hand coloring; published BOTA materials do not provide a canonical scenic Nine comparable to Smith’s or Harris’s.

**Absence finding.** A stable primary Case quotation giving a unique upright and reversed Nine of Pentacles meaning could not be verified in the accessible primary materials consulted. Many online lists silently combine Case, Golden Dawn, and later BOTA teaching. They should not be quoted as Case without an edition and page.

### Thoth tarot, painted 1938–1943; book 1944; public deck 1969

Lady Frieda Harris replaces the garden with an abstract geometrical construction. Three small interlinked central disks form an upward triangle; six larger disks form a surrounding hexagon. Planetary images or emblems appear on the outer coins. Rays express multiplication and productive circulation.

Crowley writes:

> “The number Nine, Yesod, inevitably brings back the balance of Force in fulfilment.”

And:

> “The card is ruled by Venus in Virgo. It shows good luck attending material affairs, favour and popularity.”

He explains the geometry as the multiplication of the established word through:

> “good luck and good management”.

The outer disks become actual coins because descent into matter progressively exhausts the original whirling energy. [*Book of Thoth*, Nine of Disks](https://www.bibliotecapleyades.net/crowley/libro_thoth08.htm)

The card’s printed title is **GAIN**. Crowley does not give a separate reversed meaning; elemental dignity and context replace mechanically inverted definitions.

### Notable modern redesigns

- **Morgan–Greer Tarot** (Bill Greer and Lloyd Morgan, 1979): compresses the Waite–Smith vocabulary into close-up, saturated imagery.
- **Motherpeace** (Karen Vogel and Vicki Noble, late 1970s): recontextualizes the suit within feminist, communal, and goddess-centered symbolism.
- **Mary Hanson-Roberts Tarot** (1984): softens and romanticizes Smith’s independent garden woman.
- **DruidCraft** (Will Worthington, 2004): presents abundance through explicitly pagan, naturalistic imagery.
- **Modern Marseille restorations:** preserve nine repeated coins and foliage rather than importing a falconer.
- **Modern diversity decks:** frequently reinterpret the figure as an entrepreneur, artist, gardener, queer or nonbinary person, or person enjoying bodily and financial autonomy. These are intentional modern inventions, not recoveries of fifteenth-century meaning.

## The game

### Original function

**Documented fact.** Tarot originated as a family of trick-taking games. A standard early pack added twenty-one permanent trumps and the Fool to four fourteen-card Italian suits. The Nine of Coins was an ordinary numeral card.

It has no intrinsic occult power in play. It can:

1. follow a Coins lead;
2. win a trick when higher cards have not been played, according to local rank;
3. be discarded or captured;
4. contribute as a low card during point counting.

### Rank

In most older tarot traditions the “round” suits—Cups and Coins—rank in reverse numerical order beneath their courts:

**King, Queen, Knight, Page, Ace, Two, Three … Nine, Ten**

Thus the Nine of Coins normally beats only the Ten of Coins within its suit and loses to Eight and above. The long suits, Swords and Batons, commonly rank in the opposite direction. This inversion is already present in early French rules and survives in many historical games. [Marolles rules](https://www.pagat.com/tarot/marolles.html)

Modern French tarot uses French suits and ranks the Diamonds Nine conventionally below Ten and above Eight. Central European shortened Tarock packs often omit the Nine altogether.

### Point value

In traditional tarot counting, numeral cards ordinarily carry the minimum nominal value—one raw card point, or effectively zero after the conventional grouping subtraction. Courts and selected trumps are the valuable counters. The Nine is therefore tactically useful but not a high-value counting card. [Counting tarot points](https://www.pagat.com/tarot/counting.html)

### Minchiate

The Nine of Coins remains a low round-suit numeral. The expanded forty-trump sequence does not promote or allegorize it.

### Trump-order disputes and the Fool

These concern the pack but not the Nine itself.

Scholars reconstruct three broad early Italian trump-order families:

- **A order:** associated principally with Bologna/Ferrara.
- **B order:** associated with Venice and some northeastern forms.
- **C order:** associated with Milan and ancestral to the Marseille line.

Justice, Fortitude, Temperance, the theological figures, and the celestial cards change relative position between regions. The Fool is generally outside the ordinary sequence and was historically unnumbered; it functions as an “excuse” or special card rather than as trump 0. Etteilla’s occult pack helped normalize 0, while Waite–Smith printed 0. These disputes do not change the Nine of Coins’ rank.

Regional renamings such as Popess → Juno and Pope → Jupiter in Besançon packs, or Tower → *Maison Dieu*, affect trumps only. They have no analogue in the Coins Nine.

## Chronology of attestation

### Approximately 1420: Marziano da Tortona

Marziano’s text for Filippo Maria Visconti describes a special card game with sixteen classical gods and four bird suits. It is a precursor or close relative in the history of permanent trumps, but not a standard tarot. It contains no Nine of Coins.

### 1442: Ferrara ledger

The Este account books record payment for *carte da trionfi*. This is among the earliest secure documentary notices of tarot/triumph cards. The ledger does not enumerate the Nine of Coins or give rules or meanings.

### 1440s: Milanese surviving cards

Brambilla and Cary–Yale packs provide the earliest physical evidence for the Coins pip sequence. Their exact dating and precedence remain debated.

### Approximately 1450s: Visconti–Sforza

The decorative nine-coin pip becomes materially attested within the best-known Milanese luxury tarot family.

### Approximately 1470–1500: *Sermo perutilis de ludo cum aliis*

The anonymous Dominican “sermon on games” lists and moralizes twenty-one trumps and the Fool. It proves that the standard allegorical trump sequence was established. It does not individually name the pip cards beyond their ordinary suit/rank structure.

### 1491: Sola Busca

The earliest complete surviving deck supplies the first fully narrative Nine of Coins.

### Late fifteenth to early sixteenth century: poems and literary evidence

Poems assigning tarot cards to court personalities and *tarocchi appropriati* demonstrate social and literary uses of tarot. They focus chiefly on trumps and persons. No secure individualized Nine-of-Coins doctrine emerges.

### 1527: Teofilo Folengo

Folengo’s *Chaos del Triperuno* contains a famous tarot-based sonnet sequence. It establishes literary divination-like play but does not yield the later Golden Dawn identity of the Nine.

### Seventeenth century: Noblet, Dodal, Marseille-pattern pips

The Nine stabilizes visually as nine deniers with vegetal ornament.

### 1637: Abbé de Marolles

The earliest detailed printed tarot rules document the round-suit rank reversal and special Fool conventions. They treat the Nine as a gaming card.

### 1781: Court de Gébelin and Comte de Mellet

They reinterpret tarot as Egyptian wisdom and link the trumps to an alphabetic/esoteric scheme. They do not supply the later Venus-in-Virgo attribution to the Nine.

### 1783 onward: Etteilla

Etteilla assigns systematic upright and reversed meanings to every card and redesigns the pack. For the Nine of Coins: **Effect / Dupery**.

### 1856: Éliphas Lévi, *Dogme et rituel de la haute magie*

Lévi makes tarot central to occult Kabbalah and associates the twenty-two trumps with Hebrew letters. He does not give a card-specific Venus-in-Virgo doctrine for the Nine of Coins.

His maxim that tarot is a “monumental and singular work” capable of summarizing the sciences concerns the whole pack. Later writers frequently make Lévi sound like the author of Golden Dawn minor-card correspondences; that is incorrect.

### 1870: Paul Christian

Christian popularizes “Major and Minor Arcana” and an Egyptian temple-initiation narrative. No evidence connects that narrative specifically to the Nine’s medieval origins.

### 1888: Mathers, *The Tarot*

Mathers gives:

> “Discretion, Circumspection, Prudence, Discernment”

with the reversed continuation commonly printed as:

> “Roguery, Deception, Void Project, Bad Faith.”

This is the immediate textual ancestor of Waite’s list. [Mathers, 1888](https://hermetics.net/media-library/tarot/samuel-liddell-macgregor-mathers-the-tarot/)

### 1889: Papus and Wirth

Papus supplies “Balance of balance; lasting fortune.” Wirth supplies only trumps.

### 1890s–1912: Golden Dawn *Book T*

The decisive synthesis appears: **Lord of Material Gain; Venus in Virgo; Yesod of Earth/final Heh**.

### 1909–1911: Waite–Smith and *Pictorial Key*

Smith creates the now-dominant garden and falcon scene; Waite publishes its description and meanings.

### 1938–1944/1969: Thoth

Crowley abbreviates the title to **Gain**; Harris turns the card into planetary geometry.

### 1970s onward

Humanistic, feminist, Jungian, therapeutic, and prosperity-oriented readings shift emphasis toward self-sufficiency, earned comfort, autonomy, boundaries, and the shadow of privileged isolation.

### Final Heh

The Golden Dawn maps the four suits/worlds onto the Tetragrammaton:

- Wands — Yod — Fire
- Cups — first Heh — Water
- Swords — Vav — Air
- Pentacles — final Heh — Earth

Therefore the Nine is **Yesod of final Heh**, conventionally Yesod in Assiah. “Final Heh” is not the card’s Hebrew letter in the manner of a trump; it is the suit’s place in a fourfold formula.

### Sephirah 9: Yesod

**Esoteric attribution.** Yesod means “Foundation.” It receives and organizes the preceding sephirotic forces before manifestation in Malkuth. Consequently, occultists read a Nine of Earth as material force consolidated immediately before final embodiment in the Ten.

This yields both sides of the card:

- stable accumulation, fertility, inheritance, and practical gain;
- attachment, hoarding, artificial enclosure, or dependence upon appearances.

### Venus in Virgo

The Golden Dawn divides each zodiac sign into three ten-degree decans and distributes planets through a Chaldean sequence. The Earth cards 8–10 receive Virgo’s three decans:

- Eight: Sun in Virgo — Prudence
- Nine: Venus in Virgo — Material Gain
- Ten: Mercury in Virgo — Wealth

Venus signifies attraction, pleasure, fertility, value, and cohesion; Virgo supplies cultivation, exactness, service, and management. The synthesis “good luck and good management” is Crowley’s characteristic formulation.

### Tzaddi/Heh and Emperor/Star

Crowley’s “Tzaddi is not the Star” revision exchanges the Hebrew-letter treatment of the Emperor and Star within his trump system. It does **not** modify the Nine of Disks, whose final Heh belongs to the elemental fourfold formula, not the Emperor’s contested trump letter. Confusing those two uses of *Heh* produces a false correspondence.

### Colors

Published Golden Dawn reconstructions commonly render Yesod through violet or purple registers, Earth through citrine/olive/russet/black or related Queen-scale colors, Venus through emerald green and Virgo through yellow-green or gray-green. Exact card palettes differ because *Book T*, the broader color scales, Wang, and the Ciceros are not identical visual documents.

The requested “in their own words” treatment encounters copyright and source-access limits:

## Esoteric and symbolic readings

### Alchemical

The strongest historically situated alchemical case belongs to Sola Busca. Fire, vessels, metals, bodies, and repeated disks recur throughout its minors.

- **Documented:** such images are present.
- **Scholarly reconstruction:** the deck carries a coherent Hermetic-alchemical program.
- **Disputed:** exact operation on the Nine—calcination, ordeal, monetary corruption, or another process.
- **Modern synthesis:** the Waite vineyard becomes the “harvest” of transformed matter. This is evocative but not documented as Smith’s intention.

### Kabbalistic

The Nine is Yesod, Foundation, in the material world. Yesod gathers subtle patterns; Pentacles manifests them. The card is therefore interpreted as accumulated conditions becoming materially reliable.

This is a Golden Dawn-era attribution, not a Renaissance one. Neither the Milanese coin pips nor Marseille foliage contains explicit evidence of the Tree of Life.

### Astrological

Venus in Virgo joins pleasure and valuation to discrimination and cultivation. Favorable readings emphasize refined craftsmanship, productive land, careful finance, and enjoyment disciplined by good management. Shadow readings emphasize perfectionism, status anxiety, sterility, overcontrol, or pleasure kept behind walls.

The assignment first becomes clearly traceable in Golden Dawn material and is retained by Crowley. Etteilla’s “astrology” belongs to a different system and should not be conflated with it.

### Hermetic

Hermetic readings treat the nine disks as multiplied expressions of one generative pattern. Crowley says the original “Word” is multiplied but also degraded as energy descends into matter. Gain is real, yet material proliferation complicates and exhausts the originating force.

### Christian

Early Coins could signify legitimate wealth, charity, avarice, tribute, or worldly estate within a Christian social order, but no medieval sermon located gives the individual Nine a Christian allegory.

Modern Christian tarot readings sometimes contrast stewardship with Mammon, or the enclosed fruitful garden with Eden. These are later analogies, not historically attested meanings of the fifteenth-century card.

### Jungian

From the late twentieth century onward, the solitary garden woman is interpreted as a developed ego capable of maintaining boundaries and enjoying its work. The cultivated garden becomes an ordered psyche; the wall is both healthy containment and defensive isolation; the hooded falcon is instinct educated but potentially overcontrolled.

This approach owes more to modern archetypal psychology and the Waite–Smith image than to Jung himself. No evidence was found that Jung published an interpretation of the Nine of Pentacles.

### Numerological

Papus’s “balance of balance” reads nine as 3 × 3, a completed ternary. Golden Dawn Qabalah instead emphasizes Yesod as the penultimate stage before Malkuth. Modern Marseille writers often describe nine as maximum development within a decimal cycle, with the Ten opening or completing the next condition.

These systems converge on maturity but do so for different reasons.

## Controversies and disputes

### Egyptian origin

**Legend/esoteric attribution.** Court de Gébelin asserted in 1781 that tarot preserved an Egyptian book and proposed an invented Egyptian etymology for “Tarot.” Etteilla developed the claim into a *Livre de Thot*.

**Evidence against.** No pharaonic cards, Egyptian tarot manuscripts, or transmission documents exist. Hieroglyphic Egyptian had not yet been deciphered when Gébelin wrote. Surviving tarot objects and account records place the tarot pack in fifteenth-century northern Italy. The Morgan exhibition identifies Gébelin’s and Mellet’s 1781 essays as the starting point of occult tarot. [Morgan Library](https://www.themorgan.org/exhibitions/online/tarot/11)

**Important distinction.** European suit systems may ultimately have been influenced by Islamic/Mamluk playing cards transmitted through the Mediterranean. That is a history of playing-card forms, not proof that tarot was a pharaonic book.

### Romani or “Gypsy” origin

**Legend.** Gébelin connected transmission with people then incorrectly identified as Egyptian; nineteenth-century writers including Vaillant and Papus elaborated the “Bohemian” custodians of ancient wisdom.

**Evidence against.** Tarot is documented in northern Italy before evidence of any Romani role in its invention or transmission. Modern scholarship traces Romani origins to South Asia, not ancient Egypt. Papus’s title *Tarot des Bohémiens* is part of the myth’s propagation, not supporting evidence.

### Cathar, Templar, and heretical survival claims

**Modern/disputed legends.** Some twentieth-century writers read the Popess, Grail-like cups, dualism, or initiation sequences as encrypted Cathar or Templar doctrine.

For this individual pip, no fifteenth-century document ties the Nine of Coins to Cathars or Templars. The ordinary Coins suit predates tarot’s trumps within European playing cards. Similarity between a symbol and a later esoteric narrative is not a chain of transmission.

### The “Sola Busca source” claim

The broad claim that Sola Busca influenced Smith has substantial circumstantial and visual support. The narrow statement “Smith copied the Sola Busca Nine of Coins for her Nine of Pentacles” is contradicted by the actual images.

The strongest card-specific route is:

**Sola Busca Page of Coins/falconry → possible memory or adaptation by Smith → Waite–Smith Nine’s falconer.**

That remains reconstruction, not a surviving statement by Smith.

### Was Smith’s woman independent, widowed, or kept?

Waite says only that the estate may be hers and testifies to material well-being.

- Independent landowner: plausible modern reading.
- Widow: possible narrative invention, not documented.
- Courtesan or kept woman: modern shadow reading, unsupported as original identification.
- Falcon as aristocracy: historically plausible.
- Hood as restricted vision: symbolically plausible but not stated by Waite.
- Snail as leisure, fertility, or contamination: modern interpretive options; no canonical explanation traced.

### Did the Golden Dawn invent the pentacle suit?

The Order did not invent magical pentacles. Pentacles existed for centuries in ritual magic. It did, however, decisively recast the tarot’s coin suit as the elemental suit of pentacles within its integrated magical system. Waite’s deck made the starred disk visually standard in English-language tarot.

### Misprints and unstable editions

Etteilla packs survive in multiple types with inverted labels, altered numbering, misplaced astrological symbols, and later editorial regularization. Some apparent “traditions” are inherited production variants.

Conver dating is another frequent problem: a later impression from inherited blocks or a workshop line is often captioned simply “1760.” Grimaud’s 1930 recoloring is regularly mistaken for an unchanged medieval Marseille palette.

### Historical correction

Michael Dummett, Ronald Decker, Thierry Depaulis, and later historians argue from games, cards, and archival documents that tarot was created for play in northern Italy and only became systematically occult in late eighteenth-century France. Dummett’s intentionally sharp summary calls occult tarot one of history’s most successful propaganda constructions. [Dummett review](https://www.nybooks.com/articles/1981/05/14/origins-of-tarot/)

Kaplan’s encyclopedic work documented surviving decks and modern publishing; Farley examined the cultural power of occult tarot without treating the invented history as literal fact; Robert M. Place combines iconographic analysis with historical skepticism.

Historical correction does not make the later systems culturally unreal. Etteilla, Golden Dawn, Waite, Crowley, Pollack, Greer, and Jodorowsky created demonstrably influential interpretive traditions. It means only that their meanings cannot be projected backward onto the fifteenth-century gaming card without a label.

## Open questions

1. Which surviving Milanese Nine of Coins should be called earliest depends upon unresolved dating within the Brambilla and Cary–Yale groups.

2. The Sola Busca Nine’s exact subject remains uncertain. An alchemical environment is credible; naming the precise operation or allegorical victim is not yet secure.

3. Direct evidence for Smith’s working process is incomplete. The Sola Busca photographs establish opportunity, and several cards establish likely influence, but no instruction sheet says how she devised the Nine.

4. It remains unclear how much detailed control Waite exercised over Smith’s pip compositions. His published prose sometimes fits the image closely and sometimes appears to preserve older keyword lists.

5. The first exact manuscript date for every layer of *Book T* is difficult to determine. “1888” is sometimes attached to Mathers’s independent booklet or retrospectively to Golden Dawn material; the Order text is more safely described as 1890s material publicly printed in 1912.

6. Case’s precise card-specific teachings remain partly locked in lesson material and inconsistently reproduced. A primary-edition quotation for his Nine should be required before attributing a distinctive wording to him.

7. Pollack’s and Greer’s meanings vary by edition and context. Their work is interpretive rather than a single official keyword list.

8. No documented historical bridge connects Marseille floral ornament with Venus in Virgo. Their later combination is an occult synthesis.

9. No secure source was found for a deliberate quotation of this specific card in Calvino, Eliot, Yeats, Bergman, or a canonical building.
