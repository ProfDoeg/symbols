# Two of Pentacles: Research Dossier

## Method and evidentiary labels

This dossier distinguishes:

- **[Documented fact]**: supported by a surviving card, archival record, contemporary rulebook, or primary printed text.
- **[Scholarly reconstruction]**: a conclusion argued from objects and documents but not stated directly in them.
- **[Tradition]**: a recurring convention whose historical origin may be only partly recoverable.
- **[Esoteric attribution]**: a correspondence assigned within an occult system rather than an historical property of the playing card.
- **[Disputed]**: a claim for which specialists or evidence materially disagree.
- **[Legend]**: an origin story contradicted or unsupported by surviving evidence.
- **[Modern invention]**: a recent interpretation or redesign presented without an old documentary pedigree.

“Pentacles” is used below when discussing occult decks. For pre-occult cards, “Coins,” *Denari*, or *Deniers* is historically preferable.

The Cary-Yale object is catalogued as *Due di monete* and attributed to Bonifacio Bembo or his workshop. Yale describes it as one of the three earliest surviving Milanese tarot groups; the precise dating and division of labor within the Bembo workshop remain reconstructed rather than documented by a commission record. ### A necessary scope correction

Papess/Juno, Pope/Jupiter, Maison Dieu, Fool numbering, the Justice–Strength interchange, the three Italian trump orders, and Crowley’s Tzaddi/Heh exchange concern **trumps**, not the Two of Coins. They are included where they clarify deck history, but none changes this card’s rank or identity.

# Iconography deck by deck

## Fifteenth-century Milan

### Cary-Yale / Visconti di Modrone, Milan, approximately 1440–45

**[Documented fact]** This is the earliest surviving Two of Coins presently identifiable as a tarot card. Two large gold coin devices are placed vertically against an elaborately decorated field. The card is a sumptuous hand-painted pip, not a genre scene and not an occult diagram.

The coins reproduce ducal monetary imagery rather than five-pointed stars. They signify the suit by actual coin types and dynastic authority. The gold ground and stamped ornament belong to the luxury-manuscript world of Visconti patronage. [Yale’s object-history summary](https://library.yale.edu/news/cards-library-partners-work-together-solve-mysteries-rare-tarot-deck?page=1), [Two of Coins image](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Cary-Yale_Tarot_deck_-_Two_of_Coins.jpg).

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** Coins resembling issues associated with Filippo Maria Visconti have helped date the Cary-Yale and Brambilla groups before his death in 1447. This is evidence for date and patronage, not proof that the card originally symbolized “wealth,” “balance,” or Chokmah.

### Brera-Brambilla, Milan, approximately 1440s

**[Documented fact]** The Brambilla group retains many suit cards, with *denari* represented as stamped gold coin devices. Only two of its trumps survive. It therefore provides strong evidence that repeated heraldic coins were an early Milanese suit convention.

**[Open finding]** I could not verify a publicly catalogued surviving Brambilla Two of Coins itself. The Italian national catalogue clearly exposes such records as the Brambilla Two of Cups, but no equally secure public record for its Two of Coins appeared in the sources consulted. It should not be silently reconstructed from a modern facsimile.

### Pierpont Morgan–Bergamo / Visconti-Sforza, Milan, approximately 1450s

**[Documented fact]** This pack is distributed among the Morgan Library, Accademia Carrara, and a private Bergamo collection. Its Coins pips are heraldic, gilded, and non-scenic. The Morgan’s own holdings list the Seven, Eight, and Ten of Coins, but not the Two; nevertheless, published inventories of the combined seventy-four surviving cards include a Two of Coins. The only conventionally reconstructed missing cards are the Devil, Tower, Three of Swords, and Knight of Coins. [Morgan catalogue](https://www.themorgan.org/collection/tarot-cards), [Morgan manuscript record](https://www.themorgan.org/manuscript/158525), [Morgan facsimile description](https://shop.themorgan.org/collections/gifts/products/619).

The surviving Two continues the Milanese practice: two gold coins on a highly worked field, without a person, ribbon, infinity sign, ship, astrological glyph, or divinatory label.

### Other Milanese fragments

**[Documented fact]** Numerous isolated cards are grouped loosely under “Visconti-Sforza.” Attribution and deck membership are unsettled; Kaplan’s older tendency to divide them into neat deck groups has been questioned by later cataloguers and researchers.

**[Open finding]** No additional independently datable fifteenth-century Milanese Two could be securely assigned from the online records consulted.

## Fifteenth-century Ferrara and neighboring production

### Este-related cards, approximately 1470s

The so-called Charles VI, Alessandro Sforza, and Este fragments are important for Ferrarese trump iconography.

**[Documented fact]** No Two of Coins belonging securely to those luxury fragments survives or is publicly identified. Consequently, nothing card-specific can be said about an “Este Two” without inventing it.

### Sola Busca, engraving dated 1491; probably Venetian painting of Ferrarese-oriented imagery

The Sola Busca is the earliest surviving complete or virtually complete tarot whose numeral suits were conceived as animated, pictorial scenes rather than merely arranged suit signs.

Its numbered suit cards show muscular figures, tools, weapons, vessels, and enigmatic actions. Several have demonstrable or striking compositional counterparts in Pamela Colman Smith’s 1909 cards—most famously the Three of Swords and Ten of Wands.

#### Was the Sola Busca Two of Coins Smith’s source?

**Finding: the broad claim is overstated.**

- **[Documented fact]** Photographs of the Sola Busca were acquired by the British Museum in 1908 and portions were exhibited in London before Smith drew the Waite deck.
- **[Scholarly reconstruction]** Smith probably saw the photographs or exhibition; particular Waite-Smith cards closely reproduce Sola Busca compositions.
- **[Disputed/card-specific]** The Waite-Smith Two of Pentacles is not among the clearest Sola Busca borrowings.
- **[Documented iconographic continuity]** Its most distinctive structure—two coins bound in a looping ribbon—already existed in ordinary Marseille-pattern Twos of Coins, where the ribbon carried the cardmaker’s name and date.
- **[Esoteric adaptation]** Book T explicitly turns that loop into a green-and-gold ouroboros shaped like an eight. Smith’s “endless cord” is therefore better explained by Marseille plus Golden Dawn imagery.
- **[Possible but unproved]** The Sola Busca may have encouraged Smith’s general decision to animate all pips. That is not the same as proving that its Two supplied her specific dancing juggler.

The online Public Domain Review/Brera selection reproduces selected Sola Busca cards but omits the Two of Coins while showing the Ace, Three, Four, Eight, Nine, and Ten. Absence from that web selection is not proof that the card is absent from the physical deck. [Sola Busca gallery](https://publicdomainreview.org/collection/sola-busca), [Metropolitan Museum catalogue essay](https://resources.metmuseum.org/resources/metpublications/pdf/The_World_in_Play_Luxury_Cards_1430_1540.pdf).

### “Mantegna Tarocchi,” approximately 1460s

**[Documented fact]** These are two series of fifty didactic engravings depicting social estates, Apollo and the Muses, liberal arts, virtues, cosmological spheres, and causes.

**[Historical correction]** They are not a tarot pack: they contain neither four suits nor a Two of Coins. Their later name is a bibliographical misnomer, and they are irrelevant to the direct genealogy of this pip.

## Early printed sheets

### Rosenwald sheets, late fifteenth or early sixteenth century

**[Documented fact]** The Rosenwald sheets preserve uncut Italian tarot designs, including numeral suits, and are fundamental evidence for inexpensive printed cards outside the luxury court packs.

**[Open finding]** The sources consulted did not yield a sufficiently detailed, card-level museum image and catalogue description to identify the Two’s exact ornaments responsibly. It should be described only as a repeated-suit-symbol pip unless the sheet itself is examined.

### Budapest sheet, approximately 1500

The same caution applies. It documents early woodcut tarot production and stylistic connections among northern Italian patterns, but no reliable card-specific description of the Two of Coins was recovered here.

---

## Minchiate

### Florentine Minchiate / Germini, sixteenth–nineteenth centuries

**[Documented fact]** The expanded Minchiate pack has ninety-seven cards: forty-one special cards including the Fool and fifty-six ordinary suit cards. Coins remain *denari*.

The Two of Coins generally remains a conventional pip with two monetary emblems and decorative foliage. It is not one of Minchiate’s added zodiacal, elemental, or virtue trumps.

**[Game fact]** Coins and Cups rank in reverse order: Ace high, then Two, Three … Ten low. Thus the Two is the second-highest numeral in its suit. [Rules and historical survey](https://www.pagat.com/tarot/minchiate.html), [eighteenth-century German rules](https://www.tarock.info/Minchiatta.htm).

---

## Bolognese pattern

**[Documented fact]** The historical Tarocchino Bolognese was reduced from seventy-eight to sixty-two cards by removing ranks Two through Five from every suit.

Therefore:

> There is no Two of Coins in the standard surviving sixty-two-card Bolognese pack.

This is a better historical answer than reconstructing a nonexistent card. [Bolognese pack and rules](https://www.pagat.com/tarot/ottocento.html).

Early full-pack Bolognese tarot may once have contained it before the reduction, but no continuous modern Two iconography belongs to the Bolognese pattern.

---

## Sicilian pattern

**[Documented fact]** The modern Sicilian tarot is also shortened. Coins retain ranks Four through Ten and, for tax purposes, a later Ace; the other suits begin at Five. There is no Two of Coins.

The Marchese di Villabianca reported that both a ninety-seven-card *Gallerini* and a seventy-eight-card tarot reached Sicily in the seventeenth century. A Two presumably belonged to those full packs, but no surviving Sicilian seventy-eight- or ninety-seven-card pack confirms its design. [Sicilian rules and history](https://www.pagat.com/tarot/sicilian.html).

## Tarot de Marseille lineage

### General formula

**[Documented fact]** The Marseille-pattern Two of Coins places two circular coins one above the other. A long foliate ribbon winds around or between them, frequently making an elongated S or figure-eight. Flowers and leaves fill the intervening space.

The ribbon was a practical cartouche: makers placed names, cities, workshop formulas, and sometimes dates on it. It is consequently one of the most useful cards for identifying a pack. [International Playing-Card Society pattern sheet](https://i-p-c-s.org/pattern/PS002.pdf).

The expression *Tarot de Marseille* is retrospective. Romain Merlin used it in 1856; Paul Marteau and Grimaud popularized it commercially in the twentieth century. Marseille was an important manufacturing center, but the pattern also flourished in Paris, Lyon, Dijon, Avignon, Switzerland, and northern Italy.

### Jean Noblet, Paris, approximately 1650

**[Documented fact]** Two vertically placed deniers are surrounded and connected by a broad scrolling band with the maker’s inscription and vegetal terminals. The treatment is bold, shallow, and asymmetrical compared with later Type II decks.

No person is juggling them. The “change” meaning is not printed on the card.

### Jean Dodal, Lyon, approximately 1701–15

**[Documented fact]** Dodal preserves the paired deniers, floral outgrowth, and inscribed ribbon. The composition is more regular but still belongs to what Thierry Depaulis calls Marseille Type I.

### Pierre Madenié, Dijon, 1709, and Type II

Madenié supplies the earliest securely dated Type II Marseille pack. The Two retains the maker-ribbon convention while its foliage becomes more standardized.

### Nicolas Conver, Marseille, 1760

**[Documented fact]** Two red-yellow-blue coin medallions sit within an elongated vegetal/ribbon enclosure; the cartouche identifies Conver and the place/date. This is the principal ancestor of later Grimaud imagery.

### Grimaud / Paul Marteau, twentieth century

Marteau’s *Ancien Tarot de Marseille* regularized Conver-derived linework and industrial color. His *Le Tarot de Marseille* appeared in 1949.

**[Modern esoteric reading]** Marteau treats the numeral pips through geometry, number, and color, thereby turning what had been printer ornament and suit arrangement into a metaphysical design. Such readings are not documented meanings of Noblet or Conver.

### Camoin–Jodorowsky, 1997

**[Modern reconstruction/invention]** Philippe Camoin and Alejandro Jodorowsky presented their edition as a restoration assembled from old decks and printing matrices. It intensifies color, regularizes details, and inserts or emphasizes small symbols.

The Two retains two coins in a large blue-red-yellow vegetal lemniscate. Jodorowsky and Marianne Costa read the empty center and circular movement as gestation, accumulation, and unrealized potential. This is a late-twentieth-century psychological/esoteric interpretation, not a deciphered Renaissance key.

## Besançon and regional renaming

The Besançon pattern is principally distinguished by replacing the Papess and Pope with Juno and Jupiter. Belgian/Rouen packs sometimes used related substitutions.

**[Documented fact]** Those changes affect trumps II and V, not the Two of Coins. The Two remains *Deux de deniers*, with paired coins and printer’s ribbon.

Likewise:

- *La Maison Dieu* is the French title of trump XVI, not a name of this card.
- Justice/Strength ordering concerns trumps VIII and XI.
- The Fool may be unnumbered or later numbered 0; none of this renumbers the Two.

---

## Etteilla’s Grand Etteilla, Paris, first editions approximately 1788–91

**[Documented fact]** Etteilla created the first tarot designed expressly for divination. His cards were reordered and supplied with upright and reversed inscriptions.

The card corresponding to the Two of Coins shows two suit emblems and printed verbal meanings rather than a narrative juggler. Surviving editions and later Duserre/Grimaud recensions vary typographically.

The stable keywords transmitted for this card are:

- upright: *Embarras*—embarrassment, obstruction, difficulty;
- reversed: *Lettre*—letter, missive, written communication.

A surviving edition’s misoriented words or signs can be a printing error, not a secret variant. [Grand Etteilla overview](https://fr.etteilla.org/fr/jeu/10/tarot-original-d-etteilla-le-jeu-de-toth), [card-by-card Etteilla compilation](https://etteilla.wordpress.com/etteilla-ii/etteilla-grand-pentacles/).

## Oswald Wirth, 1889

**[Documented fact]** Wirth’s original twenty-two-card *Tarot des imagiers du Moyen Âge* project was a trump-only deck. It has no Two of Pentacles.

Later seventy-eight-card products marketed under Wirth’s name add minor cards editorially. They are not evidence for an 1889 Wirth Two.

---

## Golden Dawn, approximately 1888–1900

Book T gives the card its first thoroughly integrated astrological-Qabalistic image:

> “TWO wheels, disks or pentacles … united by a green-and-gold serpent … like a figure of 8.”

A radiant angelic hand holds the center; Jupiter and Capricorn appear above and below; it is expressly “a revolving symbol.” Book T adds that “No roses enter into this card.” In the manuscript hierarchy it is Chokmah of Heh/Assiah. [Book T, p. 36](https://jantetteroo.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/02/Mathers_and_felkin_golden_dawn_book_t_th.pdf).

### Robert Wang, *Golden Dawn Tarot*, 1978

**[Modern reconstruction]** Wang and Israel Regardie visualized Book T’s specifications: hand from cloud, two pentacles, ouroboric figure-eight, planetary and zodiacal signs, and Golden Dawn color scale. It is a reconstruction of order papers, not a surviving official Victorian deck.

### Chic and Sandra Tabatha Cicero, 1991

**[Modern reconstruction]** The Ciceros likewise implement the Book T design but use their own drafting, colors, linework, and ritual synthesis. Differences between Wang and Cicero show that Book T is a specification, not a single recoverable master painting.

## Waite–Smith, London, 1909; guide 1910/1911

### What Smith drew

A young man dances or steps while holding a pentacle in each hand. A green endless cord loops around the disks as a horizontal eight. Behind him, two ships rise and fall on exaggerated waves.

Waite describes:

> “A young man, in the act of dancing, has a pentacle in either hand.”

He calls the joining band an “endless cord” like a reversed eight. [*Pictorial Key*, Two of Pentacles PDF](https://www.labirintoermetico.com/02tarocchi/Waite_Pictorial_Key_to_the_Tarot.pdf).

### Sources of the design

1. **[Documented continuity] Marseille:** paired coins plus looping maker’s ribbon.
2. **[Documented Golden Dawn source] Book T:** two disks enclosed by a revolving figure-eight serpent.
3. **[Likely artistic translation] Smith:** the abstract revolution becomes bodily juggling or dance; fluctuating gain/loss becomes waves and ships.
4. **[General influence, card-specific link unproved] Sola Busca:** Smith probably learned from its precedent of scenic pips, but the Two is not a demonstrated direct copy.
5. **[Modern invention] “Infinity symbol means infinite money/multitasking”:** common now, but not Waite’s stated interpretation.

The five-pointed star inside each gold disk identifies it as an occult pentacle. Medieval Italian and Marseille suit signs were coins, not pentagrams.

## BOTA / Paul Foster Case and Jessie Burns Parke, twentieth century

Case retained the Golden Dawn’s Qabalistic and astrological architecture but rejected Waite’s illustrated pip scenes for the minor cards.

**[Documented deck feature]** The BOTA Two is a non-scenic arrangement of two pentacles, intended to be colored by the student. This returns visually toward Marseille while retaining “Pentacles” as an occult suit. [BOTA deck summary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/B.O.T.A._tarot_deck), [BOTA history](https://web.bota.org/bota/history).

**[Important qualification]** Case’s best-known public book, *The Tarot: A Key to the Wisdom of the Ages* (1947), chiefly explains the twenty-two major keys. Detailed minor-card doctrine circulated largely in lessons. A definitive, public, card-specific Case quotation for this Two could not be verified in the consulted primary editions.

---

## Thoth, painted 1938–43; first complete commercial publication 1969

Frieda Harris painted two disks connected by a crowned green serpent in a figure-eight. Each disk contains a Chinese-style yin-yang motif, implying rotation and reciprocal transformation. Jupiter and Capricorn glyphs appear in the design. The card title is **CHANGE**.

Crowley writes:

> “Change is the support of stability.”

He identifies Jupiter and Capricorn as “most inharmonious,” limiting Jupiter’s good fortune in practical affairs, while treating cyclical alteration as the condition of material persistence. [*Book of Thoth*, “Two of Disks”](https://hermetic.com/crowley/book-of-thoth/small-cards).

The image is a direct transformation of Book T:

- the Golden Dawn hand disappears;
- the snake becomes architecturally dominant;
- the pentacles become dynamic disks;
- yin-yang signs add an East Asian emblem of interdependence;
- “Harmonious Change” becomes the terser “Change.”

Crowley’s Tzaddi/Heh switch concerns the Emperor and Star trumps. It does **not** alter the Two’s final-Heh suit attribution, Chokmah placement, or Jupiter-in-Capricorn decan.

## Notable modern redesigns

### Crowley-Harris descendants

Many Thoth-derived decks show two rotating wheels, coins, spirals, ouroboroi, yin-yang emblems, or seasonal cycles. These descend from Book T and Harris rather than medieval cardmaking.

### RWS descendants

Modern decks commonly show:

- juggling work, money, childcare, or devices;
- multiple jobs;
- balancing a budget;
- instability at sea;
- “work-life balance.”

These are interpretive descendants of Smith’s dancer and ships. “Multitasking” is especially a late-twentieth-/twenty-first-century keyword.

### Marseille revival

Restorations by Jean-Claude Flornoy, Yoav Ben-Dov, Yves Reynaud and others retain the Two as a non-scenic pip and preserve the printer-ribbon architecture. Their historical fidelity varies with whether they reproduce, restore, or normalize damaged originals.

# The game

## The Two in early Italian tarocchi

**[Documented general rule; card-specific early rules reconstructed]** Tarot began as a trick-taking game formed by adding a permanent trump suit and the Fool to an Italian-suited pack. The Two of Coins was an ordinary suit card, not a triumph and not a special scoring card.

In the “round” suits, Cups and Coins, early and many surviving Italian tarot games rank pips in reverse:

> Ace high, Two next, then Three … Ten low.

In Swords and Batons, Ten is high and Ace low. Thus the Two of Coins can be a relatively strong numeral even though it usually has little or no independent point value.

## Scoring

In classic point-trick tarot systems, high trumps and courts are valuable; ordinary numeral cards normally count only through grouped counting or as low residual fractions. The Two has no occult bonus and generally no special capture power.

Exact systems differ:

- **Italian tarocchi/minchiate:** ordinarily a non-counting pip, but high in trick rank among Coins.
- **Tarocchino Bolognese:** absent after removal of Twos through Fives.
- **Sicilian Tarocchi:** absent from the shortened pack.
- **French Tarot:** modern packs use French suits. The Two of Diamonds, not a Latin-suited Two of Coins, ranks low and counts 0.5 point under half-point accounting.
- **Austrian/Central European Tarock:** French-suited packs likewise use a low Two of Diamonds where that rank is present; many shortened packs omit low red-suit cards.
- **Minchiate:** Two of Coins is second-highest pip in Coins.

## The Fool

The Fool is not the ancestor of the modern joker in any simple documented line. In many tarot games it is an “excuse” that may be played without following suit and normally returned to its player’s trick pile after compensation. Italian regional practice varies.

This has no special interaction with the Two of Coins.

## Trump-order disputes

Dummett grouped Italian trump sequences into three broad regional orders:

- **Order A:** Bologna, Florence and related central Italian traditions;
- **Order B:** Ferrara and Venice;
- **Order C:** Milan and the lineage leading toward Marseille.

Justice and Strength exchanged relative positions in different regional and occult systems. Waite numbered Strength VIII and Justice XI; Marseille commonly does the reverse. These disputes concern trumps only.

### What the earliest documents do not say

Neither the 1442 Ferrara account nor the *Sermo de ludo* calls a card “Two of Pentacles,” assigns it Earth, or gives it a divinatory meaning. They attest packs and trumps. The first physical Two supplies the missing card-level evidence.

## Conflicts

### “Coins,” “Pentacles,” or “Disks”

- **Coins/Deniers** are historical suit signs.
- **Pentacles** recasts the coin as a magical disk, especially in nineteenth-century occultism and the Golden Dawn.
- **Disks** is Crowley’s preferred neutral-cosmic term.

A pentacle is not necessarily a pentagram. Historically it meant a magical talisman or inscribed disk. Waite and Smith nonetheless draw literal five-pointed stars.

### Jupiter in Capricorn

This is not medieval. It comes from the Golden Dawn’s division of the zodiacal decans among the thirty-six small cards from Two through Ten.

Jupiter traditionally signifies expansion and fortune; Capricorn, Saturn’s cardinal Earth sign, signifies limitation, structure, time, labor, and material constraint. Book T resolves the tension as alternating gain and loss managed prudently. Crowley stresses the rulers’ incompatibility.

### Chokmah and the number Two

The Golden Dawn maps all Twos to Chokmah, Wisdom, the second Sephirah. In Earth/Assiah this becomes primordial force beginning to move within material form.

This differs from generic numerology in which Two means passive receptivity, opposition, partnership, or indecision. Those are adjacent modern traditions, not exact equivalents.

### Final Heh

The suit corresponds to final Heh because the four suits are mapped to Yod–Heh–Vav–Heh:

- Wands = Yod/fire;
- Cups = first Heh/water;
- Swords = Vav/air;
- Pentacles = final Heh/earth.

This is a suit-wide Golden Dawn doctrine. It should not be confused with Crowley’s “Tzaddi is not the Star” adjustment, which exchanges letters assigned to two trumps.

### Hebrew letter

The Two of Pentacles has **no individual Hebrew letter** in the Golden Dawn. The twenty-two letters belong to the twenty-two paths and trumps. Its relevant Hebrew notation is instead the final Heh of the suit and Chokmah as Sephirah.

# Divinatory meanings by author

Quotations are kept short; surrounding entries are paraphrased from the specified editions.

## Etteilla, 1780s

**Upright:** *Embarras*—embarrassment, hindrance, difficulty, trouble, agitation.

**Reversed:** *Lettre*—letter, missive, epistle, message; writing or dispatch.

These are not logical opposites. Etteilla’s reversals often operate as a second independent lexicon. Later authors expanded each keyword into synonym lists. [Etteilla card compilation](https://etteilla.wordpress.com/etteilla-ii/etteilla-grand-pentacles/).

## S. L. MacGregor Mathers, *The Tarot*, 1888

Mathers gives:

> “Embarrassment, Worry, Difficulties”

and reversed:

> “Letter, Missive, Epistle, Message.”

This is plainly continuous with Etteilla rather than with the later Golden Dawn “Harmonious Change.” [Mathers, 1888 text](https://hermetics.net/media-library/tarot/samuel-liddell-macgregor-mathers-the-tarot/).

## Golden Dawn Book T

Book T does not employ a simple upright/reversed opposition. It reads cards according to dignity—whether neighboring forces support or corrupt them.

Dignified: harmonious alternation, prudent management, useful travel, adaptability.

Ill-dignified: unreliability, foolish management, suspiciousness, instability, argument, mood swings.

Its summary includes “alternation of gain and loss” and “everchanging occupation.” [Book T, p. 36](https://jantetteroo.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/02/Mathers_and_felkin_golden_dawn_book_t_th.pdf).

## A. E. Waite, *The Pictorial Key to the Tarot*, 1910/1911

**Upright:** gaiety, recreation, news and messages in writing; obstacles, agitation and trouble are also admitted.

**Reversed:** “enforced gaiety,” simulated enjoyment, handwriting, composition, exchange of letters.

Waite’s entry preserves incompatible Etteilla strands instead of reducing the card to modern “balance.” The picture makes fluctuation visually persuasive, but “work-life balance” is not Waite’s own phrase.

## Aleister Crowley, *The Book of Thoth*, 1944

Crowley does not prescribe reversed meanings. The card is “Change”: material stability exists only through constant transformation.

Favorable manifestation: adaptability, cyclic renewal, movement, flexible management.

Unfavorable manifestation: Jupiter’s abundance restricted by Capricorn, producing constrained fortune, instability, or gain that cannot freely expand.

Crowley’s key formulation is that change supports stability. [Crowley, “Two of Disks”](https://hermetic.com/crowley/book-of-thoth/small-cards).

## Paul Foster Case

**[Open finding]** A reliable primary, publicly accessible Case passage giving distinct upright and reversed meanings for the Two was not located. BOTA’s minor arcana broadly retains Golden Dawn attributions while using non-scenic pips. Later internet lists often attribute “harmonious change” to Case without naming an edition or lesson number; those should not be treated as verbatim Case.

## Rachel Pollack, *Seventy-Eight Degrees of Wisdom*, 1980 combined edition

Pollack interprets the RWS figure as enjoying or coping with continual movement rather than attaining static security.

Upright, in paraphrase: flexibility, play, the ability to keep several practical demands in motion, and the need to adapt to changing fortune.

Reversed, in paraphrase: demands becoming excessive, clumsy or joyless juggling, and practical disorganization.

**[Modern Jungian/experiential reading]** Pollack’s emphasis lies in how the figure relates inwardly to instability, not in Etteilla’s “letter.”

## Mary K. Greer, *The Complete Book of Tarot Reversals*, 2002

Greer treats reversal contextually rather than as one fixed antonym.

Upright, in paraphrase: juggling resources, flexibility, handling fluctuations, entertaining alternatives.

Reversed possibilities include imbalance, overextension, dropping obligations, resisting change, or moving from precarious juggling toward simplification.

Her general reversal taxonomy includes blocked, excessive, deficient, internalized, projected, delayed, or contrary expressions. [Summary of Greer’s reversal modes](https://querent.life/reversals).

# Esoteric and symbolic readings

## Alchemical

**[Modern esoteric extension with Golden Dawn/Thoth roots]** The two disks become opposed phases of material transformation: solve/coagula, expansion/contraction, generation/decay. Harris’s rotating yin-yang disks and ouroboros make this explicit visually.

No alchemical meaning is documented for the Cary-Yale or Marseille Two. The earliest defensible starting point is Book T’s revolving serpent; Crowley and later Thoth interpreters spread the alchemical reading.

## Kabbalistic

**[Esoteric attribution, late nineteenth century]**

- Two = Chokmah.
- Coins/Pentacles = Assiah and final Heh.
- Formula: Chokmah of Assiah, dynamic Wisdom or primal force acting in the world of material manifestation.

Court de Gébelin and Mellet did not supply this pip formula. Lévi established tarot–Hebrew/Kabbalistic interpretation at a system level; the Golden Dawn completed the minor-card grid.

## Astrological

**[Esoteric attribution]** Jupiter in the first decan of Capricorn.

The apparent contradiction is the point:

- Jupiter expands, circulates, confers opportunity.
- Capricorn structures, restricts, economizes and endures.
- Their negotiated result is managed fluctuation—growth within constraints.

The attribution first appears in the Golden Dawn corpus and was popularized through Crowley, Regardie, Waite-derived teaching, and reconstructed Golden Dawn decks.

## Hermetic

**[Esoteric attribution]** Unity manifests only by differentiating into two; opposites then circulate rather than cancel each other. The figure-eight or ouroboros makes the material world self-renewing.

This reading is firmly present in Book T and Crowley, but retrospective claims that the Marseille printer’s ribbon was intentionally Hermetic are unproved.

## Christian

Historical Coins sometimes reproduce rulers’ monetary types, but no consistent Christian program is documented on the Two.

Later Christianizing readings include:

- prudent stewardship;
- the moral danger of serving Mammon;
- the instability of worldly fortune;
- faithful management of material obligations.

**[Tradition/modern homiletic use]** These are compatible with Christian moral literature but were not found as original card-specific fifteenth-century meanings.

## Jungian

**[Modern invention/synthesis]** The dancer becomes an ego negotiating demands from conscious life while the sea represents affect or the unconscious. The lemniscate figures psychic energy circulating between opposed poles.

The healthy state is dynamic equilibrium, not rigid control; reversal can mean inflation, fragmentation, or loss of contact with embodied limits. Pollack and later therapeutic tarot writers spread this approach. Jung himself did not publish a Two-of-Pentacles interpretation.

## Numerological

Common modern readings of Two include duality, choice, relationship, reflection, polarity, receptivity, and oscillation.

These should be separated from:

- Chokmah, which is a specifically Qabalistic assignment;
- Etteilla’s “difficulty/letter” pair;
- the card-game fact that Two is simply a pip rank.

# People and works

## Artists and makers directly attached to the card

- **Bonifacio Bembo and workshop:** attributed painters of the earliest Milanese cards, including the Cary-Yale group.
- **Jean Noblet:** established the earliest surviving Marseille-style Two.
- **Jean Dodal:** continued the Type I form.
- **Pierre Madenié:** supplied the earliest dated Type II witness.
- **Nicolas Conver:** created the 1760 model later commercialized by Camoin and Grimaud.
- **Jean-Baptiste Alliette/Etteilla:** gave the card its first traceable fixed upright and reverse meanings.
- **S. L. MacGregor Mathers and Golden Dawn collaborators:** supplied its decan, Sephirah, title, and ouroboric design.
- **Pamela Colman Smith:** turned the abstract pip into a human performance.
- **A. E. Waite:** supplied its published description and divinatory list.
- **Jessie Burns Parke and Paul Foster Case:** restored non-scenic BOTA minors while retaining occult pentacles.
- **Lady Frieda Harris and Aleister Crowley:** converted it into an abstract engine of cyclical change.
- **Philippe Camoin and Alejandro Jodorowsky:** produced the 1997 Marseille reinterpretation.
- **Marianne Costa:** co-developed Jodorowsky’s modern interpretive presentation.

## Literature

### Italo Calvino, *The Castle of Crossed Destinies* (1969/1973)

Calvino used reproductions of Visconti-Sforza cards as a combinatorial narrative machine. The work is important to the modern literary reception of the deck.

**[Open finding]** No securely isolated episode depending uniquely upon the Two of Coins was established in the sources consulted. It is safer to say that the card belongs to Calvino’s available visual lexicon than to assign it a particular speech or character without checking the Italian layouts page by page.

### T. S. Eliot, *The Waste Land* (1922)

Madame Sosostris names the drowned Phoenician Sailor, Belladonna, the man with three staves, the Wheel, and a blank card. She does not name the Two of Pentacles.

**[Historical correction]** Linking Eliot specifically to this card is a modern associative reading, not textual fact.

### W. B. Yeats

Yeats belonged to the Golden Dawn and used its symbolic systems in his thought and poetry.

**[Open finding]** No published Yeats passage specifically treating the Two of Pentacles was traced. General Golden Dawn membership is insufficient evidence for a card-specific literary allusion.

## Film

### Alejandro Jodorowsky

Jodorowsky’s films use tarot-like, alchemical and Marseille imagery, and he later co-created the Camoin deck and *The Way of Tarot*.

**[Open finding]** No film shot was securely documented as a quotation of the Two of Coins. General doubling, circulation, coins, or juggling cannot establish a direct quotation.

### Ingmar Bergman

*The Seventh Seal* uses chess, Death, religious procession, plague and medieval allegory.

**[Historical correction]** It is often discussed beside tarot because Death is personified, but no direct Two-of-Pentacles quotation was found.

## Paintings and buildings

No historically documented painting or building specifically quoting the Two of Coins was identified. Modern murals, tattoos, prints, and installations reproduce the RWS card, but no single example emerged as sufficiently canonical or source-documented to include.

The Polish Pavilion at the 2022 Venice Biennale explicitly referenced the Visconti-Sforza Wheel of Fortune, not the Two of Coins. [Biennale catalogue](https://labiennale.art.pl/wp-content/uploads/2022/04/RE-ENCHANTING-THE-WORLD_catalogue.pdf).

# Controversies and disputes

## The Egyptian origin myth

Court de Gébelin’s 1781 essay declared tarot the remains of an Egyptian book. The Comte de Mellet added a Hebrew-letter structure and an alternative theory of transmission.

**[Legend, historically documented as a belief]** Their essays are real and enormously influential.

**[Historical correction]** They supplied no Egyptian cards, inscription, transmission record, or readable hieroglyphic source. Champollion’s decipherment came decades later. The surviving evidence instead places tarot’s creation in fifteenth-century northern Italy.

Michael Dummett emphasized that the historical record shows ordinary game use centuries before occult interpretation. [Dummett, “Origins of Tarot”](https://www.nybooks.com/articles/1981/05/14/origins-of-tarot/).

A genuine nuance must be preserved: European playing cards probably descend partly from Islamic/Mamluk cards, some surviving examples of which come from Egypt. That does **not** vindicate Gébelin’s claim that the twenty-two tarot trumps encoded a pharaonic Book of Thoth.

## Romani or “Gypsy” origin

**[Legend]** Nineteenth-century writers increasingly claimed that Romani people carried tarot from Egypt or India. Papus’s title *Le Tarot des Bohémiens* helped perpetuate the association.

**[Evidence]** Romani linguistic history points to South Asian origins, while tarot is documented in Italian courts before any evidence connects Romani communities to its invention. Later Romani fortune-telling traditions are genuine cultural history, but they do not establish authorship of the pack. [Italian historical discussion](https://www.letarot.it/page.aspx?id=210).

## Cathar and Templar encodings

**[Modern disputed claim]** Some twentieth- and twenty-first-century writers propose that tarot concealed Cathar, Templar, heretical, or initiatory teachings.

No fifteenth-century rule, commission, sermon, poem, or card identifies the Two of Coins—or the pack—as a Cathar or Templar cipher. The ordinary Latin suits and courtly trump subjects are explicable within contemporary games, civic processions, Christian moral imagery, and Renaissance culture.

Possible symbolic resonance is not equivalent to documentary descent.

## Sola Busca as the direct source of every RWS pip

**[Overgeneralization]** The Sola Busca unquestionably matters to the history of scenic pips, and several Smith designs show striking debts.

But the Two of Pentacles specifically:

- follows the Marseille paired-coins-and-ribbon formula;
- follows Book T’s ouroboric figure-eight;
- introduces Smith’s own dancer and seascape.

Calling it a “copy of the Sola Busca Two” is unsupported by the evidence recovered.

## The infinity sign

**[Modern tradition]** The loop is now routinely called an infinity symbol signifying endless resources or perpetual motion.

Historically the genealogy is layered:

1. printer’s ribbon in Marseille packs;
2. ouroboros explicitly prescribed by Book T;
3. Waite’s “endless cord” like a reversed eight;
4. modern mathematical/inspirational “infinity” readings.

It is therefore neither purely a medieval secret sign nor purely Smith’s invention.

## When did coins become pentacles?

**[Historical correction]** Medieval cards show monetary disks and heraldic coins. The occult “pentacle” emerges when nineteenth-century writers reinterpret the suit as magical Earth talismans.

Book T uses “Disks or Pentacles.” Waite depicts five-pointed stars, making the verbal pun visually literal. Crowley retreats to “Disks” while retaining the magical cosmology.

## Is “balance” the ancient meaning?

No.

- Etteilla: difficulty / letter.
- Mathers 1888: embarrassment and worry / written message.
- Book T: alternating gain and loss, instability managed through prudence.
- Waite: gaiety, recreation, written news, obstacles.
- Modern RWS culture: balance, multitasking, work-life management.

“Balance” is a compelling visual inference from Smith’s juggling figure, but it is not the earliest attested divinatory keyword.

## Misprints becoming traditions

Etteilla decks sometimes contain reversed or inconsistently placed labels and astrological marks. Later reprints copied some of them. A typographical error may therefore acquire traditional authority.

**[Open finding]** No particular Two-of-Coins misprint was securely documented as the source of a major later doctrine. The general warning is justified; a card-specific claim is not.

## Dummett and the historians’ correction

Dummett, Sylvia Mann, Thierry Depaulis, Ronald Decker, Stuart Kaplan, Helen Farley, and Robert M. Place differ in emphasis, but the documentary consensus is:

- tarot was created for play in fifteenth-century northern Italy;
- occult tarot begins as a late-eighteenth-century reinterpretation;
- the Egyptian, continuous-Romani, Cathar and Templar genealogies lack supporting transmission evidence;
- meaningful medieval imagery need not imply a secret occult manual.

Kaplan’s encyclopedias are invaluable object catalogues but sometimes preserve older dates or deck groupings now debated. Place and Farley are more receptive than Dummett to symbolic and cultural readings, while still rejecting literal Egyptian origins.

# Open questions

1. **Exact dating of the Cary-Yale Two.** Approximately 1440–45 is widely used, but catalogues sometimes broaden the date, and technical study may refine it.

2. **Workshop authorship.** “Bonifacio Bembo” can mean attribution to a workshop rather than proof of his individual hand.

3. **The earliest cheap printed Two.** Rosenwald and Budapest sheets may preserve a more socially representative design than courtly Milanese cards, but a card-level examination is needed.

4. **Sola Busca access.** A complete high-resolution sequence should be checked directly through Brera’s current catalogue before making any stronger claim about its Two.

5. **Etteilla edition priority.** The Grand Etteilla exists in closely related early states with variant dates, engravers, spelling and reversed inscriptions. The stable *Embarras/Lettre* pairing is clear; exact first-impression typography requires comparison of BnF exemplars.

6. **Golden Dawn manuscript history.** Book T circulated in manuscript and was revised by multiple members. “Mathers” is conventional authorship, not necessarily proof that every line was written by him alone.

7. **Smith’s precise visual access.** British Museum acquisition and exhibition make access plausible; no surviving statement by Smith says, card by card, which Sola Busca photographs she used.

8. **Case’s minor-card wording.** BOTA lessons are not all publicly accessible or consistently paginated. A verbatim Case upright/reversed entry remains unverified.

9. **Calvino’s specific use.** A complete concordance of every card in both narrative sequences would be needed to state exactly where the Two appears.

10. **Modern iconographic quotations.** Tattoos, public murals and contemporary artworks are abundant but poorly catalogued; attribution to a particular historical Two often rests only on visual resemblance.
