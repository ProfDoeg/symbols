# The Suit of Pentacles: Research Dossier

## Method and evidentiary labels

This dossier concerns the whole fourteen-card suit, not a single card. The following labels distinguish unlike kinds of evidence:

- **[Documented fact]** Surviving object, archival record, dated printed text, or explicit manuscript statement.
- **[Scholarly reconstruction]** Historical inference supported by comparative evidence but not directly stated in a contemporary record.
- **[Tradition]** A stable convention of card-making, play, or interpretation whose precise origin may be uncertain.
- **[Esoteric attribution]** A correspondence deliberately assigned by an occult author or organization.
- **[Disputed]** A proposition supported by some writers but rejected or materially qualified by others.
- **[Legend]** A historical-origin story unsupported by contemporary evidence.
- **[Modern invention]** A twentieth- or twenty-first-century reinterpretation presented as new symbolism rather than recovered history.

A limitation should be stated at the outset: museums and libraries frequently catalogue individual surviving cards rather than entire suits, and many early packs survive only in fragments. “Earliest surviving suit” and “earliest surviving individual Coin card” are therefore not always identical questions.

## Basic identification

### Names

| Context | Name |
|---|---|
| Medieval/Renaissance Italian | **denari**, occasionally *danari*; “money,” “pennies,” or coins |
| Modern Italian tarot | **Denari** |
| French tarot | **Deniers** |
| Spanish Latin-suited cards | **Oros** (“golds”); *monedas* is descriptive rather than the standard suit-name |
| Portuguese | **Ouros** |
| German historical writing | **Münzen**, *Geld*, or *Denare* when describing Italian cards |
| German occult tarot | **Münzen**, increasingly **Pentakel** |
| English historical usage | **Coins** |
| English occult usage | **Pentacles** |
| Golden Dawn | **Pentacles**, “the Powers of Earth” |
| Waite–Smith | **Pentacles** |
| Crowley–Harris Thoth | **Disks** |
| French-suited analogue | Usually **Diamonds / Carreaux** |
| Italian regional game terminology | Also *ori* in Sicily and some southern traditions |

**[Documented fact]** An orthodox tarot pack has fourteen cards in each ordinary suit: Ace, Two through Ten, Page/Valet, Knight/Cavalier, Queen, and King. The Morgan Library describes the fifteenth-century Visconti–Sforza structure precisely this way. [Morgan Library catalogue](https://www.themorgan.org/manuscript/158525)

**[Documented fact]** The suit is unnumbered as a whole. Its cards comprise ten numeral ranks and four courts. Unlike the trumps, it has no place in competing trump sequences.

**[Documented fact]** Coins belong to the ordinary four-suit pack that existed before tarot. Tarot was produced by adding a special trump sequence and Fool to such a pack; Coins were not devised as part of a twenty-two-key occult book. Michael Dummett’s historical distinction between the game and later occult tarot is summarized in his own discussion of his project. [Dummett, “Origins of Tarot”](https://www.nybooks.com/articles/1981/05/14/origins-of-tarot/)

### Earliest antecedent and earliest tarot examples

**[Documented fact]** The nearest preserved precursor is the fifteenth-century Mamluk pack in Istanbul, whose four suit-signs are cups, swords, polo-sticks, and coins. The coin suit is called *darāhim*, plural of *dirham*.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** Dummett and Kamal Abu-Deeb regarded the close structural correspondence between Mamluk and early European suits as evidence for transmission from the Islamic world. Dummett further discussed Heinrich Rosenfeld’s suggestion that Italian *denari* may reflect Arabic *danānīr*, gold dinars, subsequently replaced on the surviving Mamluk cards by *darāhim*. This is an etymological-historical hypothesis, not a statement preserved in a medieval card-maker’s record. [Dummett excerpt and discussion](https://forum.tarothistory.com/viewtopic.php?p=27685)

**[Documented fact]** Among the earliest surviving tarot cards are the hand-painted Milanese packs commissioned for the Visconti family:

- the **Visconti di Modrone**, conventionally dated about 1441–47;
- the **Brambilla**, about 1442–45;
- the **Visconti–Sforza**, broadly about 1450–80.

These contain gold coin devices carrying Visconti or Sforza heraldry. The Morgan’s portion of the Visconti–Sforza includes the Seven, Eight, and Ten of Coins, plus King and Queen of Coins. [Morgan object record](https://www.themorgan.org/collection/tarot-cards)

The precise relative priority of the Modrone and Brambilla cards remains dependent on art-historical dating. It is therefore safer to call them the earliest surviving group than to nominate one individual Coin card as unconditionally first.

### Golden Dawn attribution

**[Esoteric attribution, circa 1888–1900]**

- Suit: **Pentacles**
- Element: **Earth**
- Divine name/world: final **Heh** of **YHVH**, corresponding to **Assiah**, the material or active world
- Elemental direction: North
- Elemental weapon: Pentacle
- Primary four-colour Earth scale: citrine, olive, russet, and black
- Ace: Root of the Powers of Earth
- Two through Ten: the thirty-degree spans of Capricorn, Taurus, and Virgo
- Courts: elemental subdivisions of Earth
- French-suit counterpart: Diamonds

The Golden Dawn’s “pentacle” was a magical disk representing the universe or Earth. The familiar automatic equation “pentacle = pentagram inside a coin” is later and visually owes much to Waite–Smith derivatives. Book T actually describes the Ace as a pentacle made of **five concentric circles**, not simply a five-pointed star. [Book T transcription](https://www.studocu.vn/vn/document/truong-dai-hoc-khoa-hoc-xa-hoi-va-nhan-van/lich-su-van-minh-tg/mathers-and-felkins-book-t-the-tarot-1888/68241631)

# Iconography deck by deck

## Mamluk cards, fifteenth century

**[Documented fact]** The Mamluk coin cards use circular medallion-like coin signs, ornament and calligraphy, but no human court figures. Court rank is expressed by inscriptions: king, first deputy, and second deputy.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** Their suit system is the principal material ancestor of the Latin suits. This does not make Mamluk cards “tarot”: they lack the later Italian permanent trump sequence.

**[Disputed terminology]** “Darāhim” correctly describes the wording on the surviving pack; treating it as a direct etymon of Italian *denari* is less secure than the broader historical relationship between the suits.

## Visconti di Modrone / Cary–Yale, Milan, circa 1441–47

**[Documented fact]** This unusually elaborate hand-painted pack has six court figures per suit rather than the later standard four: male and female attendants and mounted figures, plus king and queen. The coins are gold disks marked with Visconti heraldic devices.

**Meaning of the design:** wealth, dynasty, and political identity are expressed literally through heraldic “money.” There is no accompanying text assigning Earth, commerce, or divination.

**[Disputed nomenclature]** “Cary–Yale” and “Visconti di Modrone” refer to the same surviving group; it should not be treated as a second deck.

## Brambilla tarot, Milan, circa 1442–45

**[Documented fact]** Forty-eight cards are held by the Pinacoteca di Brera. The surviving suit cards use repeated gold coins with Visconti emblems on richly tooled backgrounds. Brera describes it as a late-Gothic deck made for the Duke of Milan. [Brera](https://pinacotecabrera.org/en/news/mostra/il-segreto-dei-segreti-i-tarocchi-sola-busca/)

The layout remains emblematic and heraldic, not scenic.

## Visconti–Sforza, Milan, circa 1450–80

**[Documented fact]** The surviving cards are divided among the Morgan Library, Accademia Carrara, and Colleoni family. Coins are repeated gold roundels containing the Sforza sunburst or related heraldry; plants or ribbons organize some arrangements. Courts are aristocratic full-length figures with sumptuous clothing.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** Bonifacio Bembo and his workshop are the usual attribution, though the whole surviving aggregate may include more than one campaign or hand.

**Interpretive caution:** the luxurious gilding naturally suggests wealth, but no fifteenth-century source calls the suit “Earth,” “merchants,” or “material manifestation.”

## Ferrarese and Este cards, later fifteenth century

The fragmentary Charles VI/Estensi group and related Ferrarese cards preserve the same Latin suit structure, generally with ornamental coin pips.

**[Documented fact]** Ferrara’s court accounts record *carte da trionfi* in 1442, but the ledger does not give occult meanings for the suits.

**[Disputed]** “Charles VI tarot” is an obsolete attribution: the cards are Italian and later than the French king’s supposed 1392 pack.

## Mantegna “Tarocchi,” circa 1460s

**[Documented fact]** These are fifty instructional engravings arranged in five alphabetic decades—social estates, Apollo and the Muses, liberal arts, virtues, and cosmic spheres.

**Relevance:** essentially none to the history of Coins. They are not a playable tarot pack and have no four suits. The conventional name “Mantegna Tarocchi” is misleading.

## Sola Busca, northern Italy, circa 1490–91

**[Documented fact]** This is the earliest complete surviving 78-card tarot and the earliest surviving tarot with a narrative figure on every pip. Brera acquired it in 2009 and dates associated cards about 1490. [Brera exhibition record](https://pinacotecabrera.org/en/news/mostra/il-segreto-dei-segreti-i-tarocchi-sola-busca/)

In its Coin suit, human figures handle, weigh, inspect, strike, display, or surround disk-like objects. The cards can evoke minting, metallurgical work, treasure, and physical burden. A skull appears among the coins on the Nine.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** Scholars have connected this imagery with humanist, metallurgical, and Hermetic-alchemical culture. Proposed figures behind the program include Nicola di Maestro Antonio and Ludovico Lazzarelli.

**[Disputed]** Exact one-to-one alchemical readings—Four as “mother of metals,” Five as impregnation of Earth, Nine as *nigredo*—are modern interpretive reconstructions, principally associated with Sofia Di Vincenzo and later Brera-related scholarship, not surviving instructions for reading the deck.

**[Documented influence]** Photographs were exhibited at the British Museum in 1907. Pamela Colman Smith’s Three of Pentacles and Ten of Wands closely resemble Sola Busca compositions, and her Nine of Pentacles may recall a Sola Busca court figure with a bird. Direct copying is highly plausible for some cards, but not equally demonstrable for every proposed pair. [Comparative archive](https://waitesmith.org/index.php/decks/sola-busca-vs-waite-smith/)

## Rosenwald and Budapest sheets, circa 1500

**[Documented fact]** The Rosenwald sheets preserve early Florentine-style cards; the Budapest–Metropolitan sheets preserve Ferrarese material. Their Coin cards use essentially geometric arrangements of round pips rather than Waite-style scenes.

Their importance is structural: they document inexpensive printed tarot outside the princely hand-painted tradition. They do not document occult Earth symbolism.

## Tarot de Marseille lineage

### Jean Noblet, Paris, circa 1650

The Deniers are floral circular devices. Pip arrangements are bilateral or radial, interwoven with foliage. Courts hold or display oversized coins.

The Two of Deniers carries the characteristic intertwined ribbon or cartouche that later printers often used for their name, city, and date.

### Jean Dodal, Lyon, 1701–15

Dodal retains the floral Deniers and cartouche-bearing Two. Spellings and stencil details differ, while the basic syntax remains stable.

### Nicolas Conver, Marseille, 1760

Conver’s Deniers became a principal basis for later commercial Marseille editions. The pips remain unillustrated in the narrative sense: meaning arises from number, symmetry, vegetal ornament, and suit-sign rather than depicted events.

The dated exemplars and their locations are surveyed through the French historical-deck project covering Noblet, Viéville, Dodal, and Conver. [LeTarot](https://www.letarot.com/)

### Grimaud / Paul Marteau, twentieth century

Grimaud’s *Ancien Tarot de Marseille*, systematized and interpreted by Paul Marteau, regularized line, colour, and printing. Marteau’s 1949 book gave the traditional pip designs an extensive numerological and colour-symbolic reading.

**[Tradition]** The pack itself remained a Marseille-style game pattern.

**[Modern esoteric attribution]** Marteau’s highly detailed symbolic explanations are interpretations of the printed forms, not documentary evidence about what seventeenth-century card-makers intended.

### Camoin–Jodorowsky, 1997

Jodorowsky and Philippe Camoin reconstructed or redesigned details from several historical exemplars while describing the result as restoration.

**[Documented fact]** It is a modern composite edition, not a facsimile of one old deck.

**[Disputed]** Claims that tiny recovered marks disclose a single primordial esoteric code exceed what the mixed-source reconstruction can historically prove. Jodorowsky’s method nonetheless constitutes an influential modern Marseille tradition.

## Besançon pattern, eighteenth–nineteenth centuries

The Besançon tarot is principally distinguished from Marseille by trump substitutions—Juno for the Papess and Jupiter for the Pope—not by a radically different Coin suit. Deniers remain ornamental pips and coin-bearing courts.

These substitutions developed in regions where confessional sensitivities made papal figures undesirable, especially eastern France, Switzerland, and neighboring German-speaking markets.

## Minchiate, Florence and Tuscany

**[Documented fact]** Minchiate expanded the trump sequence to forty trump cards while retaining four ordinary suits. Coins remained one of the suits.

The suit contained king, queen, knight, page, and numeral cards, although shortened regional packs and surviving rules vary.

**Game significance:** Minchiate’s iconographic expansion belongs to the trumps—zodiac, elements, virtues—not to a transformation of Coins into “Earth.” It is therefore anachronistic to infer the later Golden Dawn correspondence merely because Minchiate includes an Earth trump.

## Bolognese tarot

The Bolognese pack was progressively shortened. In its later 62-card form, low numeral cards were suppressed. Coins remained a Latin suit, often with a special large Ace or heraldic stamp card.

**[Documented fact]** Bolognese practice is one source of the complications surrounding Ace/signature/tax-card claims: different ranks were retained or adapted for fiscal and manufacturing purposes.

## Sicilian tarot

**[Documented fact]** The Sicilian pack was shortened and uses **ori**, “golds,” for Coins. The Two and Three of Coins and several low cards in other suits were removed in a historical 64-card form; the Ace of Coins was at one stage absent from play but retained or restored as a tax-stamp carrier. [Overview of the shortened pack](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tarot)

This is decisive evidence against a universal claim that “the Ace of Coins always served as the maker’s signature.” Such roles depended on local regulation and pattern.

## Etteilla’s Grand Etteilla, Paris, 1788–89 onward

Etteilla reorganized the whole pack into a putatively Egyptian “Book of Thoth.” His numeral cards are not simply Marseille pips: captions, upright/reversed keywords, and a reordered sequence make each card a named oracle.

**[Documented fact]** Etteilla was the first publisher to offer an explicitly occult tarot deck and a systematic method covering the complete pack.

**[Esoteric attribution]** Coins became part of his astrological, elemental, and social divinatory system.

**Caution:** multiple “Grand Etteilla” editions differ in numbering and wording. A meaning cited only by modern card number without identifying the edition is unreliable.

## Oswald Wirth, 1889

Wirth’s original deck illustrated only the twenty-two trumps. It therefore has **no historical Wirth-designed suit of Coins**.

Later publishers created complete “Wirth” decks or supplied Marseille minors, but those are extensions rather than Wirth’s 1889 work. This is an important negative finding.

## Golden Dawn manuscripts / Book T, circa 1888–1900

Book T turns a Coin into a magical **Pentacle**. The Ace shows a radiant hand holding a rose branch bearing a large disk of five concentric circles, with wings and a Maltese cross. The numbered cards combine pentacles with planetary and zodiacal emblems. Courts carry pentacles and receive elemental-zodiacal identities.

**Meaning of the change:** money becomes the visible surface of a metaphysical category—Earth, embodiment, result, and Assiah.

Robert Wang’s *Golden Dawn Tarot* (1978) and Chic and Sandra Tabatha Cicero’s *Golden Dawn Magical Tarot* are twentieth-century reconstructions from published and surviving Order materials. Neither is an untouched facsimile of a single official nineteenth-century deck.

## Waite–Smith, 1909; book 1910

Pamela Colman Smith supplied full scenes for all numbered cards. Every disk bears a pentagram.

The suit becomes a sequence of embodied social and economic situations:

- Ace: gift-like pentacle over a garden
- Two: juggler balancing coins within an infinity loop
- Three: artisan at work in a church
- Four: possessive holder
- Five: impoverished figures outside a lit window
- Six: almsgiving and scales
- Seven: cultivation and waiting
- Eight: repetitive craftsmanship
- Nine: cultivated estate and falcon
- Ten: household, inheritance, and lineage
- Page: study
- Knight: patient stewardship
- Queen: cultivated fertility
- King: established material dominion

**[Documented fact]** Waite titled the suit Pentacles and described each scene in *The Pictorial Key to the Tarot*. [Waite’s text](https://sacred-texts.com/tarot/pkt/index.htm)

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** Smith probably consulted Sola Busca photographs for certain compositions.

**[Modern tradition]** “The suit of work and money” is a fair summary of the resulting imagery, but incomplete: the scenes also concern skill, charity, illness, land, embodiment, inheritance, and the moral use of resources.

## Paul Foster Case / BOTA

Case retained Golden Dawn correspondences but revised colour, wording, and some doctrinal emphases. BOTA’s published deck leaves the pip cards relatively formal and expects students to colour the deck.

**[Esoteric attribution]** Pentacles are Earth and final Heh, associated with manifestation and the completion of the Tetragrammaton formula.

Case discouraged dependence on reversed cards; consequently a tidy “Case upright/reversed list” is not native to his method.

## Crowley–Harris Thoth, painted 1938–43; first complete publication 1969

Crowley renamed Pentacles **Disks** to emphasize rotating, dynamic matter. Frieda Harris rendered the suit through geometric, botanical, architectural, planetary, and quasi-scientific abstractions.

The pip titles are:

- Ace: Root of the Powers of Earth
- Two: Change
- Three: Works
- Four: Power
- Five: Worry
- Six: Success
- Seven: Failure
- Eight: Prudence
- Nine: Gain
- Ten: Wealth

The Ace incorporates Crowley’s personal seal, 666, solar symbolism, interlaced pentagons, a decagon, and six wings. Crowley calls it his personal hieroglyph and “a glyph of Earth as understood in this New Aeon of Horus.” [Crowley, *Book of Thoth*, “Small Cards”](https://hermetic.com/crowley/book-of-thoth/small-cards)

Its joking colophon reads: “The Ace of Disks—the Author’s name.” This deliberately revives the old practice of putting a maker’s identity on a prominent suit card, but it does not prove that all historical Aces of Coins served that function.

## Notable modern redesigns

- **Aquarian Tarot, David Palladini, 1970:** streamlined Art Deco pentacles; retains Waite’s scenic logic.
- **Motherpeace, Vicki Noble and Karen Vogel, 1981:** round cards; Discs foreground bodies, women’s work, ecology, and communal material life.
- **DruidCraft, Will Worthington, 2004:** Pentacles combine Waite scenes with Celtic-revival landscape and seasonal imagery.
- **Wild Unknown, Kim Krans, 2012:** minimal animal and natural-form imagery; Pentacles become an ecological growth system.
- **Modern Witch Tarot, Lisa Sterle, 2019:** reproduces Waite’s compositions through contemporary, diverse figures.
- **Jodorowsky–Costa:** retains Coins rather than Waite pentagrams and interprets them through receptive/material numerology.

These are **[modern inventions]** within historically inherited structures.

# The game

## Rank and value

No single tariff applies to all tarot games.

### Traditional Italian and early French logic

**[Tradition]** Courts normally rank:

**King > Queen > Knight/Cavalier > Page/Valet**

In many old tarot games, the numeral cards of the “round” suits—Cups and Coins—rank in ascending order:

**Ace, Two, Three … Ten**

where Ten is the highest numeral below the courts.

Swords and Batons may reverse this order, with lower numeral cards beating higher ones. This alternating order is found widely in early tarot-family games but must not be projected onto every locality.

### Modern French Tarot

All four suits use the familiar descending order:

**King, Queen, Knight, Jack, 10 … Ace**

Card-point values are attached principally to courts and specific trumps; ordinary numeral cards count collectively in tricks rather than receiving divinatory values.

### Tarock traditions

Central-European Tarock packs usually employ French suits, making Diamonds the visual descendant or functional analogue of Coins. Rank direction can differ between the red and black suits, and packs are frequently shortened.

### Minchiate

Coins remain an ordinary suit. The numerous additional Minchiate trumps outrank suit cards; they do not turn Coins into a fifth esoteric category.

### Fool

The Fool is outside the Coin suit. In historical games it commonly functions as the Excuse: it may be played without following suit and is usually retrieved by its owner subject to compensation. In some variants it is a highest trump or has other special status.

### Trump-order disputes

They do not alter the Coin suit. The three major historical families of Italian trump ordering—commonly called orders A, B, and C—concern the relative positions of such figures as Justice, Fortitude, Temperance, virtues, celestial cards, and religious figures.

The familiar Waite exchange of Strength and Justice is a Golden Dawn-era occult rearrangement, not a change in the rank of Coins.

# Chronology of attestation

- **Before/around 1400:** Mamluk four-suit cards provide the closest surviving Islamic comparanda. Coins are one of the four suits.
- **1370s:** European records begin mentioning playing cards, but usually not individual suit signs.
- **Circa 1420:** Marziano da Tortona describes a special pack devised for Filippo Maria Visconti with gods and bird suits. It is a precursor to courtly trump packs, not evidence for tarot Coins.
- **1442, Ferrara:** account-book entry for *carte da trionfi*. It proves the name and manufacture of triumph cards, not occult meanings.
- **1440s:** Visconti di Modrone and Brambilla Coin cards survive.
- **Circa 1450–80:** Visconti–Sforza Coin cards.
- **Circa 1470–1500:** the anonymous sermon *De ludo cum aliis* lists the tarot trumps and condemns the game. It confirms the trump structure but supplies no suit symbolism.
- **Circa 1490–91:** Sola Busca provides the earliest complete and fully scenic Coin suit.
- **Circa 1500:** Rosenwald, Budapest, and related printed sheets document broader production.
- **Early sixteenth century:** Teofilo Folengo and the *tarocchi appropriati* poems use tarot cards poetically. These are literary appropriations, not proof of a continuous occult school.
- **Seventeenth century:** Noblet and related French packs establish surviving Marseille-style Deniers.
- **1701–15:** Dodal.
- **1760:** Conver.
- **1770s:** documented fiscal enforcement against untaxed tarot packs shows the practical importance of stamps and maker identification. [International Playing-Card Society article](https://www.i-p-c-s.org/journal/TPC-49-2-web.pdf)
- **1781:** Court de Gébelin and the Comte de Mellet publish Egyptian and alphabetic theories.
- **1780s:** Etteilla publishes systematic cartomancy and then an occultized deck.
- **1854–56:** Éliphas Lévi integrates tarot with ceremonial magic and Kabbalah.
- **1888:** Mathers publishes English divinatory meanings; the Golden Dawn begins elaborating Book T.
- **1889:** Papus and Wirth publish influential occult works/decks.
- **1909:** Waite–Smith deck.
- **1910:** Waite’s *Pictorial Key*.
- **1938–43:** Harris paints Thoth.
- **1944:** Crowley’s *Book of Thoth*.
- **1949:** Marteau’s *Le Tarot de Marseille*.
- **1969:** first complete commercial publication of Thoth.
- **1978 onward:** Wang and later Cicero reconstructions popularize Golden Dawn minors.
- **1980:** Dummett’s *The Game of Tarot* establishes the foundational modern historical synthesis.
- **1997:** Camoin–Jodorowsky deck.
- **Present:** Pentacles dominate Anglophone divinatory decks; Coins remain standard in historical and Marseille traditions; Disks identify chiefly Thelemic and Thoth-derived designs.

## Colour and season

**[Golden Dawn attribution]** Earth’s fourfold flashing colour field is citrine, olive, russet, and black.

**[Later tradition]** Green, brown, ochre, and black are common Pentacle colours.

**[Disputed tradition]** Season varies:

- Winter is common because Earth is cold, dense, and dormant.
- Autumn is also common because Pentacles signify harvest.
- Some systems assign spring through Taurus or vegetation.

No medieval source assigns a season to Denari, and there is no universal occult consensus.

## Tzaddi/Heh and the Emperor/Star issue

This dispute concerns the trumps, not final Heh as the suit’s tetragrammatic position.

Golden Dawn:

- Emperor = Heh
- Star = Tzaddi

Crowley interpreted *Liber AL* I:57, “Tzaddi is not the Star,” by exchanging their zodiacal-letter relations:

- Emperor associated with Tzaddi/Aries
- Star associated with Heh/Aquarius

This does **not** change Pentacles from final Heh of YHVH. Conflating the two Heh questions produces a common modern error.

# Divinatory meanings by author

## Etteilla

**[Documented text, edition-dependent]** Etteilla gives each card distinct upright and reversed propositions, commonly involving money, business, documents, expenditure, gifts, inheritance, delay, and loss. His reversed meanings are not always negative; reversal may change the grammatical or temporal relation.

Because Grand Etteilla printings reorder and renumber cards, no supposedly universal modern list should be attributed to “Etteilla” without naming the edition. The fullest secure statement is that Etteilla made Deniers a detailed economic and transactional oracle, not simply “Earth.”

## Mathers, 1888

Mathers’s terminology is **Pentacles**. His suit-level personification says Pentacles may represent people “not so dark” as those represented by Swords—a period-specific physiognomic convention that later readers generally abandon.

His card vocabulary centers on:

- Ace: perfect contentment, felicity, money
- Two: gaiety, recreation; reversed, correspondence or obstacles
- Three: nobility, rank, skill; reversed, weakness or mediocrity
- Four: pleasure, receiving possessions; reversed, delay or hindrance
- Five: material difficulty, lovers or affinity in some contexts; reversed, disorder
- Six: gifts and presents; reversed, ambition or desire
- Seven: money, business, employment; reversed, anxiety
- Eight: learning, work, craftsmanship; reversed, vanity or usury
- Nine: gain, inheritance, wealth; reversed, deception or disappointment
- Ten: house, family, wealth; reversed, chance, loss, or family misfortune
- Courts: practical persons differentiated by age, sex, complexion, station, and ethical character

These compact meanings should be read in Mathers’s full text rather than harmonized with Waite. [Mathers, *The Tarot* (1888)](https://hermetics.net/media-library/tarot/samuel-liddell-macgregor-mathers-the-tarot/)

## Waite, 1910

Waite’s principal upright/reversed vocabulary is:

| Card | Upright | Reversed |
|---|---|---|
| Ace | perfect contentment; felicity; gold | evil side of wealth; defective prosperity |
| Two | gaiety, recreation; written news | enforced gaiety; simulated enjoyment |
| Three | trade, skilled labour, nobility | mediocrity; pettiness; weakness |
| Four | possessions, gifts, inheritance | suspense, delay, opposition |
| Five | material trouble; destitution; lovers | disorder, discord, ruin |
| Six | presents, gifts, gratification | desire, cupidity, jealousy |
| Seven | money, business, barter | anxiety about money |
| Eight | work, employment, commission, craft | vanity, ambition, usury |
| Nine | prudence, safety, accomplishment | deception, bad faith, thwarted project |
| Ten | gain, riches, family matters, dwelling | chance, fatality, loss, robbery |
| Page | application, study, scholarship, news | prodigality; unfavorable news |
| Knight | utility, service, responsibility | inertia, idleness, discouragement |
| Queen | opulence, generosity, security | suspicion, fear, mistrust |
| King | enterprise, aptitude, intelligence, business | vice, weakness, corruption |

Waite borrowed extensively from prior cartomancy and Golden Dawn materials while Smith’s pictures made the meanings memorable. The primary text is [*The Pictorial Key to the Tarot*](https://sacred-texts.com/tarot/pkt/index.htm).

## Crowley, 1944

Crowley discourages simplistic moral binaries and normally reads dignity, combinations, and elemental balance rather than mechanically turning every reversed card into an antonym.

His essential Disk meanings are:

- Ace: material energy, wealth, labor, power
- Two/Change: alternating gain and loss; harmonious change
- Three/Works: construction, engineering, organized labor
- Four/Power: establishment, law, material authority; danger of fixation
- Five/Worry: material strain, constriction, preoccupation
- Six/Success: balanced material attainment
- Seven/Failure: blight, inertia, diminishing returns
- Eight/Prudence: cultivation, intelligence applied to material affairs
- Nine/Gain: inheritance, increase, good fortune
- Ten/Wealth: established material abundance that risks petrification
- Knight: patient, enduring, laborious, sometimes heavy or dull
- Queen: fertility, nourishment, sensuous and practical capability
- Prince: competent material administration, imperturbability, possible insensibility
- Princess: pregnancy, growth, renewal, Earth’s latent fertility

Crowley’s own account of the court imagery is available in [*Book of Thoth*, “Court Cards”](https://hermetic.com/crowley/book-of-thoth/court-cards).

## Paul Foster Case

Case follows Golden Dawn astrology and the Earth/final-Heh structure while emphasizing mental formation becoming physical result. His BOTA method does not require systematic reversals; shadow expressions are inferred from imbalance rather than orientation alone.

Therefore lists advertising “Case’s official reversed Pentacle meanings” should be treated cautiously unless tied to a specific Case publication.

## Rachel Pollack

**[Modern interpretive synthesis]** In *Seventy-Eight Degrees of Wisdom* (1980), Pollack treats Pentacles as nature, work, money, health, and the reality that spiritual life must become embodied. She reads the Waite images developmentally rather than as a vocabulary list.

A central Pollackian tension is not “spiritual versus material” but unconscious or distorted material attachment versus a material life fully inhabited.

## Mary K. Greer

**[Modern practice]** Greer treats Pentacles through body, resources, work, values, sensory experience, and the querent’s actual environment. Her methods encourage reversals as blockage, delay, internalization, excess, absence, or alternative perspective rather than a single opposite meaning.

Because Pollack and Greer remain copyrighted authors, their entries here are paraphrases rather than extended quotation.

# Esoteric and symbolic readings

## Alchemical

**First securely traceable applications:** nineteenth-century occult synthesis; retrospectively applied to Sola Busca in twentieth-century scholarship.

Coins readily became metals; the sequence could therefore be interpreted as mining, purification, assay, coagulation, and material transmutation.

**[Disputed]** Sola Busca contains genuine metallurgical-looking imagery and belongs to a culture familiar with alchemy. Whether each Coin card encodes a fixed stage of the Great Work remains unproved. Brera describes the deck’s complex Hermetic-alchemical cultural setting but not a surviving contemporary manual. [Brera](https://pinacotecabrera.org/en/news/mostra/il-segreto-dei-segreti-i-tarocchi-sola-busca/)

## Kabbalistic

**First major spreaders:** Lévi, Papus, Golden Dawn.

The ten pips are mapped to the ten Sephiroth; the four suits to four worlds and four letters of YHVH. Pentacles are final Heh and Assiah.

This scheme is elegant, influential, and historically late. No fifteenth-century maker’s document maps Denari to Assiah.

## Astrological

Etteilla introduced extensive astrological cartomancy, but the coherent system now most familiar—earth-sign decans for Two through Ten—comes from the Golden Dawn.

Capricorn gives Two–Four; Taurus Five–Seven; Virgo Eight–Ten. Planetary rulers follow the Chaldean sequence.

## Hermetic

Lévi transformed a coin/talisman into the magical pentacle: a visible condensation of will, cosmos, and elemental balance. The Golden Dawn formalized this as the elemental weapon of Earth; Waite made it the repeated suit-sign; Crowley made it dynamic “Disks.”

## Christian

Early tarot Coins appear within Christian Europe and may bear crosses, heraldic suns, foliage, civic devices, and quasi-Eucharistic round forms.

**[Disputed]** Claims that Deniers secretly represent communion hosts, Christ’s wounds, or the thirty pieces of silver can produce meaningful readings of particular designs but are not attested as a universal original suit doctrine.

Waite’s Three places craft inside a church and his Five outside a lit ecclesiastical window, explicitly bringing economic life into Christian architectural space.

## Social estates: “the merchants”

**[Tradition/modern shorthand]** A widespread four-estates analogy assigns:

- Swords: nobility or warriors
- Cups: clergy
- Batons: peasants
- Coins: merchants

This fits late-medieval occupational symbolism and is conceptually plausible.

**[Absence of evidence]** No known founding document says the four tarot suits were deliberately created as a complete four-estates diagram. It should be reported as later interpretation, not established origin.

## Jungian

Jung did not produce a canonical fourteen-card Pentacles system.

Later Jungian writers map the suit to:

- sensation as a psychological function;
- embodiment and grounded consciousness;
- the body and somatic shadow;
- projection onto money or status;
- integration through work, craft, and relationship with nature.

**[Modern invention]** This is a twentieth-century psychological appropriation. “Archetypal usefulness” is not evidence of medieval authorship.

# People and works

## Artists, printers, occultists, and scholars directly attached to the suit

- **Bonifacio Bembo and Milanese workshops:** probable makers of early Visconti-related Coin cards.
- **Nicola di Maestro Antonio:** proposed engraver of Sola Busca.
- **Jean Noblet, Jean Dodal, Nicolas Conver:** printers whose Deniers document the Marseille lineage.
- **Etteilla:** first systematic occult-cartomantic recoding of the whole pack.
- **Éliphas Lévi:** connected tarot, Kabbalah, and the magical pentacle.
- **S. L. MacGregor Mathers:** published English meanings and helped formulate Golden Dawn tarot.
- **Pamela Colman Smith:** created the most influential modern narrative Pentacle cycle.
- **A. E. Waite:** supplied its program and interpretive text.
- **Paul Foster Case:** transmitted a revised Golden Dawn/BOTA system.
- **Aleister Crowley and Frieda Harris:** transformed static Pentacles into dynamic Disks.
- **Paul Marteau:** popularized symbolic readings of Marseille pip design.
- **Robert Wang; Chic and Sandra Tabatha Cicero:** reconstructed Golden Dawn decks.
- **Alejandro Jodorowsky and Philippe Camoin:** created an influential composite Marseille restoration.
- **Michael Dummett, Ronald Decker, Thierry Depaulis, Stuart Kaplan, Robert M. Place, Helen Farley:** major modern historians or historical synthesists correcting occult-origin narratives.

## Literature and film

### Italo Calvino

*The Castle of Crossed Destinies* uses tarot cards as a combinatorial narrative language. Different cards acquire meanings through adjacency and story. Coins therefore function as narrative tokens of wealth, exchange, property, and worldly circumstance rather than one fixed occult doctrine.

### T. S. Eliot

Madame Sosostris’s reading in *The Waste Land* includes “the one-eyed merchant” and a blank card carried on his back, but not an explicitly named Pentacle card.

**[Disputed literary association]** Connecting the merchant directly to a particular Pentacle court is interpretive; Eliot’s poem does not make that identification.

### W. B. Yeats

Yeats’s Golden Dawn involvement connects him to elemental pentacle ritual and tarot culture. A specific canonical poem centered on the fourteen Pentacles, however, could not be traced.

### Ingmar Bergman

*The Seventh Seal* is frequently juxtaposed with tarot because of Death, chess, and medieval allegory, but it does not quote the Suit of Pentacles as a defined sequence.

### Jodorowsky

Tarot informs Jodorowsky’s films, visual compositions, psychomagic, and his collaboration with Marianne Costa. Specific cinematic images may resemble tarot tableaux, but claims that a scene quotes a particular Coin card require film-by-film evidence.

## Paintings and buildings

Waite–Smith’s Three of Pentacles depicts an artisan, cleric, and patron within Gothic architecture. It is an idealized composite, not a securely identified single building.

The Five uses a stained-glass window containing five pentacles. No firm evidence identifies it as a portrait of one particular church.

Modern murals, installations, and paintings quote Waite–Smith Pentacles extensively, but no single pre-1909 building has been established as the exclusive source for the suit.

# Controversies and disputes

## Egyptian origin

**[Legend, first explicit major publication 1781]** Court de Gébelin claimed tarot preserved an ancient Egyptian book of wisdom. The Morgan Library describes his immediate conviction that the cards were remnants of an Egyptian magical book. [Morgan, “Occult Tarot”](https://www.themorgan.org/exhibitions/online/tarot/11)

Mathers repeated the claim in 1888, saying Court de Gébelin, Lévi, and Etteilla assigned tarot a “Qabalistico-Egyptian origin,” which Mathers believed his research confirmed. [Mathers](https://hermetics.net/media-library/tarot/samuel-liddell-macgregor-mathers-the-tarot/)

**Evidence:** none connects the fifteenth-century Italian trump pack to an Egyptian Book of Thoth. Egyptian hieroglyphs were not deciphered when Gébelin wrote. The historical Egyptian contribution belongs instead to the probable route of ordinary playing-card suits through Mamluk Egypt—a different and much narrower claim.

## Romani or “Gypsy” origin

**[Legend]** Later authors claimed Romani travelers preserved or transmitted Egyptian tarot wisdom. Jean-Alexandre Vaillant and other nineteenth-century writers helped popularize this narrative.

**Evidence against:** tarot is documented in Italian court society before any evidence links Romani communities with its manufacture or introduction. Romani origins are South Asian, not pharaonic Egyptian.

The word “Gypsy” is retained only when identifying the historical title or claim; “Romani” is the appropriate name for the people.

## Cathar origin

**[Modern disputed claim]** Some twentieth-century writers interpreted the trumps as Cathar theology or a clandestine anti-clerical initiation sequence.

**Evidence:** no Cathar manuscript, inquisitorial deposition, card-maker record, or early deck provenance links the cards to Cathar communities. The ordinary Coin suit has even less specific connection.

## Templar origin

**[Legend/modern invention]** Claims that the Knights Templar encoded secrets in tarot depend on symbolic resemblance and later occult genealogy.

**Evidence:** no tarot cards are documented before the Templars’ suppression in 1312; the earliest secure tarot evidence is fifteenth-century northern Italian. No transmission document bridges the gap.

## Pentacles as ancient suit-sign

**[Modern misconception]** Pentacles are sometimes described as the original medieval suit.

**Correction:** the historic suit-sign is Coins/Deniers. Magical pentacles existed in grimoires, and Lévi linked pentacles with ceremonial magic, but the systematic tarot replacement belongs to late nineteenth-century occultism and was visually normalized by Waite–Smith in 1909.

## Pentacle versus pentagram

A pentagram is a five-pointed star. A pentacle historically means a magical figure or talisman and need not contain a pentagram.

Waite’s cards made a pentagram-on-a-disk the standard popular image. Book T’s Ace, however, specifies concentric circles. Treating the two words as timeless synonyms is inaccurate. [Historical overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pentacle)

## Diamonds as direct descendants

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** French Diamonds are the conventional modern counterpart to Coins in occult and game comparisons.

**Qualification:** French suit-signs emerged through a separate late-medieval simplification process. “Coins turned directly into Diamonds on a known date” is too literal. The functional equation Diamonds = Pentacles is a stable later convention.

## Ace as signature and tax card

**[Partly true, overgeneralized]**

- Marseille makers often put name, city, or date on the **Two of Deniers**, whose ribbon naturally accommodates text.
- Some patterns use a large Ace for maker or fiscal information.
- The Sicilian Ace could be retained specifically to bear the tax stamp.
- Different states required stamps on different cards.

Accordingly, “the Ace of Coins was the universal card-maker’s signature card” is false. Crowley’s signing of his Ace is a witty appropriation of a broader card-making convention.

## Sola Busca as Waite’s single source

**[Disputed overstatement]** Sola Busca clearly supplied or reinforced several compositional ideas. It does not explain the entire Waite–Smith suit. Smith also drew on stage design, contemporary illustration, Christian art, Golden Dawn instructions, Waite’s meanings, and her own invention.

## Misprints becoming tradition

No single famous Pentacle misprint has historical standing comparable to some trump-title and numbering controversies.

More common are:

- colour-registration variations treated as intentional symbols;
- publisher-added titles mistaken for an original author’s wording;
- Knight/King/Prince nomenclature mismatches;
- altered Golden Dawn colour scales;
- different Grand Etteilla numberings collapsed into one system;
- details introduced in restored Marseille decks presented as primordial.

These are transmission problems rather than one demonstrable founding misprint.

# Open questions

1. Which surviving individual Coin card is absolutely earliest? Current dating leaves the first Visconti groups close enough that a categorical answer remains unsafe.

2. What exact workshop relationships connect the Visconti di Modrone, Brambilla, and Visconti–Sforza campaigns?

3. How directly were Mamluk cards transmitted into northern Italy, and through which ports or trading communities?

4. Does *denari* preserve a phonetic memory of *danānīr*, or is it simply the natural Italian name for coin signs?

5. How much of Sola Busca’s Coin imagery is technical minting, how much alchemy, and how much humanist moral allegory?

6. Which Sola Busca photographs did Smith see, and which Waite–Smith compositions can be proven rather than merely plausibly inferred from them?

7. Which Golden Dawn member first standardized “Pentacles” as the suit-name? Lévi and earlier magical literature supply the talismanic vocabulary, but manuscript chronology remains complicated.

8. Can Etteilla’s Coin meanings be stemmatically reconstructed across first-state and later Grand Etteilla editions without silently harmonizing their numbering?

9. When did the “merchants” estate attribution first appear in an explicit printed tarot text? It is widespread but often repeated without a primary citation.

10. No early source was found assigning Denari a season. Autumn and winter remain competing modern correspondences.
