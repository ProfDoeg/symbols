# Six of Pentacles: Research Dossier

## Method and evidentiary labels

This dossier uses the following labels:

- **Documented fact** — supported by a surviving card, archival record, dated printed text, manuscript, or rules source.
- **Scholarly reconstruction** — an inference advanced by historians from incomplete material evidence.
- **Tradition** — a reading repeated within an identifiable cartomantic or esoteric lineage.
- **Esoteric attribution** — an occult correspondence asserted as doctrine, without implying historical origin.
- **Disputed claim** — proposed but contested, weakly evidenced, or contradicted by other evidence.
- **Legend** — a historical-origin narrative unsupported by surviving evidence.
- **Modern invention** — a demonstrably recent interpretation, redesign, or retrospective attribution.

Dates for handmade fifteenth-century packs remain approximate unless an inscription or document provides a terminus.

**Documented fact:** the earliest surviving Six is a coin pip, not a charity scene and not a card bearing pentagrams. The Beinecke image is catalogued as a fifteenth-century *Sei di monete* from the Visconti di Modrone/Cary–Yale pack. It contains six actual coin devices on an ornamented gold ground. [Beinecke-derived image record](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Cary-Yale_Tarot_deck_-_Six_of_Coins.jpg)

**Scholarly reconstruction:** the narrow c. 1442–47 dating rests partly on the claim that the coins reproduce a gold issue of Filippo Maria Visconti introduced in 1442 and discontinued when he died in 1447. The date and numismatic identification have circulated through Kaplan and Giordano Berti, but are not universally accepted. A broader “Milan, mid-fifteenth century” is safer.

**Documented fact:** “pentacle” is not the historical Italian suit name. Early cards show *denari* or coins. The magical-pentacle terminology belongs to the nineteenth-century occult reception, becoming visually standardized in English-language decks through the Golden Dawn and Waite–Smith traditions.

## The names

### Italian

- **Denari** is the conventional Italian playing-card suit: money, coins, or denarii.
- **Monete** is a transparent modern descriptive alternative.
- In fifteenth-century records, the augmented pack was initially called **carte da trionfi** or simply **trionfi**.
- **Tarocchi** became the normal Italian name during the early sixteenth century. The precise origin of the word remains unsettled.
- The individual card ordinarily needed no independent allegorical name: it was the six-count card in the money suit.

### French

- **Six de deniers** is the traditional Tarot de Marseille name.
- *Denier* was both an actual historical denomination and a generic term for money.
- Occult and translated decks increasingly use **Six de pentacles**.
- Etteilla’s plate labels it **Le Six de Denier**, singular in the surviving printed wording, and assigns the upright word **LE PRÉSENT** and reverse **AMBITION**. [Grand Etteilla scan](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/2/2f/Le_grand_Etteilla%2C_ou%2C_l%27art_de_tirer_les_cartes_..._Le_tout_recueilli_..._%28IA_b29321220%29.pdf)

### English

- **Six of Coins** is the most literal historical translation.
- **Six of Pentacles** became standard in English occult tarot, especially after Mathers and Waite.
- **Six of Disks** is Crowley’s deliberate renaming. Crowley rejected the idea that the suit emblems were merely inert money: “Nor are the Disks any more to be considered as Coins; the Disk is a whirling emblem.” *The Book of Thoth*, part III, “The Ace of Disks.” [Book of Thoth scan](https://www.mr-kaplan.com/Library/Crowley/Book%20of%20Thoth.pdf)

### Spanish

- **Seis de Oros** is normal in Spanish playing-card and tarot usage.
- *Oros*, literally “golds,” is the Spanish coin suit.
- **Seis de Pentáculos** generally signals an English occult-deck translation rather than an indigenous historical suit name.

### German

- **Sechs der Münzen** is the normal translation in esoteric tarot.
- Traditional German and Austrian **Tarock** packs generally adopted French suits and were frequently shortened to 54 cards. Their pip cards are Hearts, Diamonds, Clubs, and Spades, not medieval Italian Coins; consequently an occult “Sechs der Münzen” may have no direct place in many actual Tarock packs.

### Golden Dawn and Thoth

- Golden Dawn: **Six of Pentacles — Lord of Material Success**.
- Crowley–Harris: **Six of Disks — Success**.
- Crowley preserved the Golden Dawn Moon-in-Taurus attribution and the association with Tiphareth, but condensed the title and radically redesigned the image.

### Regional trump renamings: relevance to this card

The requested Papess/Juno, Pope/Jupiter, Maison Dieu, Fool, Justice, and Strength variations concern the trumps, not the Six of Coins. They did not rename or renumber this card.

For context only:

- **Documented fact:** Besançon-pattern decks substituted **Juno** and **Jupiter** for the Papess and Pope, chiefly in eighteenth- and nineteenth-century eastern France, Switzerland, and adjacent German-speaking regions.
- **Documented fact:** *La Maison Dieu* is the established French Marseille title for trump XVI.
- **Documented fact:** the Fool was frequently unnumbered in historical Italian and Marseille packs; Etteilla and later occult systems assigned it a formal numerical position.
- **Documented fact:** Justice and Strength/Fortitude exchange VIII and XI between some historical/occult arrangements. Waite placed Strength VIII and Justice XI; Marseille convention ordinarily has Justice VIII and Force XI.
- None of these disputes alters the Six of Coins’ six-count or suit membership.

## Iconography deck by deck

## Fifteenth-century Milan

### Visconti di Modrone / Cary–Yale, c. 1440s

**Documented fact:** the surviving card shows six circular gold coin devices, three displaying one face of the coin and three the other, set against an elaborate punched and gilded ground. It has no human figures, scales, beggars, astrology, Hebrew letters, or divinatory caption.

The surviving pack is unusual. Its courts originally included male and female pages and knights, and its original total may not have been the later standard 78. The Six itself nevertheless performs the ordinary function of a countable money-suit pip.

**Scholarly reconstruction:** a commonly proposed date is c. 1442–47, based on heraldry, marriage politics, and identification of the coin image with Filippo Maria Visconti’s florin. The pack is generally attributed to Bonifacio Bembo or his workshop, but workshop and dating questions remain open.

**Meaning of the design:** in its own historical setting the repeated coin was primarily a suit and rank indicator. Any interpretation as charity, reciprocity, Tiphareth, or Moon in Taurus is retrospective.

### Brambilla–Brera, c. 1440s

The Brambilla pack is another fragmentary Milanese Visconti commission. Its Coin cards employ brilliant gold emblems and dynastic devices.

**Finding:** no securely catalogued surviving Brambilla Six of Coins was located in the consulted museum material. It must not be reconstructed from modern completion decks without being identified as a replacement.

### Visconti–Sforza / Pierpont Morgan–Bergamo, c. 1450s

The most nearly complete Visconti–Sforza pack survives divided among the Morgan Library, Accademia Carrara, and Colleoni holdings. The Morgan describes 74 surviving originals across the three holdings, with four cards absent from a standard 78-card reconstruction. [Morgan catalogue](https://www.themorgan.org/manuscript/158525)

**Documented fact:** its Six of Coins survives in the Bergamo group rather than among the Morgan’s 35 cards. It displays six gold coin/sun-like devices embedded in scrolling floral ornament. There is no narrative scene.

**Dispute:** Gertrude Moakley described Visconti coin pips as radiant-sun emblems; later writers have connected their faces to actual Visconti currency. The two readings are not wholly exclusive—a dynastic sun and a coin could deliberately overlap—but a precise mint-type identification should not be treated as settled.

### Other Milanese fragments

The surviving Tozzi, Colleoni, and related fragments are too incomplete for a continuous Six-of-Coins genealogy. Modern facsimiles frequently manufacture missing cards by copying another Bembo-family pack. Such cards are **modern reconstructions**, not newly discovered fifteenth-century originals.

## Fifteenth-century Ferrara and northern Italy

### Este cards

The Este-associated illuminated fragments preserve courtly and heraldic tarot imagery, but not a securely attributable Six of Coins relevant to this dossier.

**Finding:** no surviving Este Six was verified in the consulted online museum catalogues. Modern “Este tarot” editions fill gaps; their Sixes must not be described as original evidence.

### Sola Busca, engraving c. 1490; painted exemplar 1491

The Sola Busca is the earliest surviving complete 78-card tarot and the earliest surviving tarot in which every pip is given a substantial pictorial composition. The Brera dates the coloured exemplar to 1491 from inscriptions formerly legible on shields. [Pinacoteca di Brera catalogue essay](https://pinacotecabrera.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/11/Il-Segreto-dei-tarocchi-eng.pdf)

**Documented fact:** the Six of Coins does **not** show a merchant distributing alms. It shows a muscular artisan or labourer working at a bench or block among six large shield-like coin emblems. The exact activity and object have been variously identified; the visual point is manual material work, not charity.

**Disputed/alchemical reading:** Sofia Di Vincenzo and the 2012 Brera exhibition placed the Sola Busca within a Hermetic-alchemical culture. Individual tools, furnaces, vessels, and transformations may participate in that program. Whether every pip encodes a precise alchemical operation is disputed.

### The Sola Busca and Pamela Colman Smith: correction of a common claim

**Documented fact:** photographs of the complete Sola Busca were acquired by the British Museum in 1907, two years before Smith’s deck appeared. Several Waite–Smith compositions strongly resemble Sola Busca cards.

**Scholarly reconstruction:** Smith probably had access to those photographs or the display, although no surviving statement by Smith says she copied them.

**Disputed and substantially incorrect claim:** “The Sola Busca Six of Coins was the source of Smith’s Six of Pentacles charity scene.” The images do not correspond closely.

The more credible specific relationship is:

- Sola Busca **Six of Coins** → Waite–Smith **Eight of Pentacles**, both showing a working craftsman surrounded by coin emblems.
- Sola Busca **Ten of Swords** → Waite–Smith **Ten of Wands**, both using a burdened figure carrying a mass of long implements.
- Other proposed correspondences include the Sola Busca Three of Swords and Waite–Smith Three of Swords at the level of pierced-heart imagery, although details remain debated.

The Tarot History Forum explicitly observes that “The Sola-Busca Six of Coins … Smith uses a version of it for her Eight of Pentacles.” [Tarot History Forum discussion](https://forum.tarothistory.com/viewtopic.php?start=10&t=530)

Thus the item-specific lead is **partly verified but misassigned**: Sola Busca influenced Smith’s minor-arcana imagery; its Six is not the direct source of her Six.

### “Mantegna Tarocchi,” c. 1460s

**Documented fact:** the so-called Mantegna Tarocchi are fifty educational engravings arranged into five ten-card series—social estates, Apollo and the Muses, liberal arts, cosmic principles, and celestial spheres. They are neither a tarot pack nor by Mantegna.

They contain no Six of Coins. Their relevance is contextual: they demonstrate the fifteenth-century northern-Italian culture of ordered emblematic series from which tarot also emerged.

## Early printed sheets

### Rosenwald sheets, probably late fifteenth or early sixteenth century

The Rosenwald sheets preserve printed tarot designs including suit cards. Their pips retain countable Italian suit emblems and ornament rather than scenic episodes.

**Finding:** no individualized symbolic program specific to the Six of Coins was traceable from the consulted reproductions. Its historical importance lies in the transition from unique courtly paintings to reproducible popular cards.

### Budapest sheets

The so-called Budapest sheets likewise preserve early printed tarot fragments. The suit cards remain conventional count pips.

**Finding:** no secure evidence was found for a separate narrative Six-of-Coins design or an occult caption.

---

## Tarot de Marseille lineage

### Jean Noblet, Paris, c. 1650

Noblet’s is the earliest substantially surviving pack normally classed in the Marseille family.

**Documented fact:** the Six de Deniers is non-scenic. Six round coin signs are symmetrically distributed around central and interstitial foliate ornament. The coins identify suit and number; vegetal stems fill and organize space.

### Jean Dodal, Lyon, c. 1701–15

Dodal preserves the same fundamental system: six coin roundels, axial symmetry, and stylized leaves or flowers.

Changes are chiefly in cutting, colour, line, and the exact geometry of the vines. There is still no merchant, scale, almsgiving, pentagram, Moon, or Taurus sign.

### Nicolas Conver, Marseille, 1760

Conver’s influential Six uses six deniers arranged around a central cruciform flower with stems binding the field. Modern descriptions commonly reduce the disposition to a diamond or paired-column structure around a floral centre. [Conver-derived card record](https://marselia.app/fr/cards/six-de-denier)

**Traditional Marseille reading:** later numerological interpreters see two triangles, two groups of three, a cross, or circulation around a central germ. These interpretations are not printed on the historical card and should be dated to their modern authors.

### Grimaud / Paul Marteau, twentieth century

Grimaud’s *Ancien Tarot de Marseille*, especially Paul Marteau’s 1930s standardization, fixed a Conver-derived palette and drawing for a mass market.

Marteau interpreted the Six as a union of opposed ternaries and an alternation of involution and evolution. This is a twentieth-century esoteric commentary on the old pip geometry, not evidence for Conver’s intention.

### Camoin–Jodorowsky, 1997

Philippe Camoin and Alejandro Jodorowsky issued a restored Marseille claiming recovery of details from old matrices and multiple historical packs.

**Documented fact:** its Six remains a floral six-denier pip.

**Disputed claim:** describing the deck as restoration of a single primordial or “authentic” Tarot de Marseille overstates what the heterogeneous source material can prove. It is also a creative synthesis. Jodorowsky’s interpretive system treats number 6 as pleasure, beauty, union, and realization, while Coins concern materiality, body, money, and embodied life.

## Minchiate

Minchiate expanded tarot to 97 cards while retaining four Italian suits.

**Documented fact:** its Six of Coins remains a normal suit pip; the expansion consists primarily of additional trumps, not replacement of the numbered suits.

In traditional round suits—Cups and Coins—the order is generally reversed relative to Swords and Batons: Ace high among the pips, followed by 2, 3, and so forth, making 10 low. Exact rules varied by time and place.

No special divinatory or iconographic identity for the Minchiate Six of Coins was found in the early sources consulted.

## Bolognese pattern

Tarocchino Bolognese removes the 2–5 from every suit but retains Ace and 6–10. Thus the Six is historically important as the lowest surviving numeral in the shortened pack.

In the round suits, Cups and Coins, the pip order is:

**Ace, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10**

with Ace highest among those pips and 10 lowest; in the long suits, Swords and Batons, the order runs 10 downward to 6 and then Ace. [Tarocco Bolognese overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tarocco_Bolognese)

The Six has no normal point value of its own. It is a low non-counting card used to win a trick only by rank or trumping context.

---

## Sicilian pattern

The Sicilian tarot pack is shortened and structurally distinctive.

**Documented fact:** in the modern Mineo form, Coins uniquely retains a 4, while all four suits include 5–10 and four courts. The Six of Coins ranks below 7 and above 5 in the stated high-to-low sequence:

**King, Queen, Horse, Maid, 10, 9, 8, 7, 6, 5, 4.**

It is not normally an honour and has no intrinsic counting value. [Pagat’s Sicilian Tarocchi rules](https://www.pagat.com/tarot/sicilian.html)

## Etteilla’s Grand Etteilla, c. 1788–89

Etteilla reordered and redrew the tarot as an explicitly divinatory “Book of Thoth.” The Grand Etteilla system numbers all 78 cards in its own sequence, so its Six of Deniers is not simply “card VI.”

In the surviving printed plate:

- the suit is named **Le Six de Denier**;
- upright reads **LE PRÉSENT**;
- reversed reads **AMBITION**;
- card/order number **72** appears;
- six small wreath-like suit signs are arranged above a small female vignette.

**Documented fact:** Etteilla’s “present” means the present time, the actual moment, or today.

**Scholarly reconstruction:** Waite’s “presents, gifts” may preserve a bilingual pun or slippage between French *présent* meaning “present time” and English “present” meaning gift. Mathers’s 1888 “Presents, Gifts, Gratification” makes this shift especially clear. This is a plausible transmission history rather than a demonstrated confession by either author.

Etteilla’s astrology is systematic but does not use the Golden Dawn’s later Moon-in-Taurus decan allocation. Claims that Etteilla already assigned Moon in Taurus to this card are **anachronistic** unless tied to a specific later Etteilla-school table.

## Oswald Wirth, 1889

Wirth’s original 1889 deck comprised the 22 trumps, not a complete illustrated 78-card pack.

**Finding:** there is no original Wirth 1889 Six of Pentacles to describe. Later “Wirth” 78-card products supplement his trumps with modern pip designs and should not be projected backward into the 1889 publication.

## Golden Dawn manuscripts and reconstructions

### Book T, late nineteenth century

The Golden Dawn did not leave one universally authoritative commercial deck. Its manuscript descriptions assigned the small cards:

- a Sephirah by number;
- a Qabalistic world by suit;
- a decan;
- a planetary ruler;
- a title;
- colour scales and divinatory meanings.

For the Six of Pentacles:

- **Lord of Material Success**
- Moon in Taurus
- second decan of Taurus
- Tiphareth of Assiah
- Earth
- final Heh of YHVH.

A characteristic description places a white radiant hand holding a rose branch with six flowers, each terminating in a pentacle, with the planetary and zodiacal symbols above and below. Details differ among manuscript recensions and published reconstructions.

### Robert Wang, 1978

Wang’s *Golden Dawn Tarot* converts the written and manuscript tradition into a fully illustrated deck. The Six uses six pentacles, ritual colour correspondences, the title, and Moon/Taurus emblems.

**Documented fact:** this is a twentieth-century reconstruction, not a facsimile of one single nineteenth-century “official Golden Dawn deck.”

### Sandra Tabatha and Chic Cicero, 1991s onward

The Ciceros’ *Golden Dawn Magical Tarot* similarly reconstructs the order’s ritual symbolism. Its Six emphasizes the sixfold arrangement, astrological glyphs, and Qabalistic colour scales.

Again, it is a scholarly-initiatory reconstruction made within a living Golden Dawn lineage.

## Waite–Smith, 1909

Pamela Colman Smith drew a richly dressed standing man or merchant holding a balance in his left hand and dropping coins into the hand of one of two kneeling, poorly dressed figures. Six pentacles float above and around the tableau.

Waite’s own description is:

> “A person in the guise of a merchant weighs money in a pair of scales and distributes it to the needy and distressed. It is a testimony to his own success in life, as well as to his goodness of heart.”

—A. E. Waite, *The Pictorial Key to the Tarot*, 1910, “Six of Pentacles.” [Sacred Texts edition](https://sacred-texts.com/tarot/pkt/pktpe06.htm)

### Sources of the scene

- **Documented lineage:** Etteilla/Mathers had already connected the card with present time, gifts, gratification, and material success.
- **Esoteric attribution:** Book T called it Material Success.
- **Probable visual culture:** Smith’s scene resembles conventional European images of almsgiving, charity, a merchant weighing money, and the corporal work of mercy “giving alms to the poor.”
- **Unproven:** no specific painting, print, or Bible illustration has been demonstrated as the unique source.
- **Incorrect direct attribution:** the Sola Busca Six shows labour, not this charity tableau.
- **Probable indirect influence:** Smith’s entire scenic-pip project was encouraged by the illustrated Sola Busca model, and its Six probably informed her Eight of Pentacles.

### The pentacle

Smith renders each suit sign as a gold disk bearing a five-pointed star. This makes explicit the English occult transformation from coin to magical pentacle. Historical Marseille deniers do not contain necessary pentagrams.

### Modern readings of the image

The traditional reading foregrounds generosity and material success. Later readers ask:

- Who controls the scales?
- Is the gift equitable or paternalistic?
- Is one recipient favoured?
- Is this charity, wages, debt repayment, patronage, or social control?
- Is generosity freely given or calculated?

These power-dynamic readings are largely **late twentieth- and twenty-first-century developments**. They are defensible visual interpretations but are not Waite’s stated intention.

Claims that the scale is visibly unbalanced, or that a coloured patch is a theatre ticket proving the beggars are actors, could not be traced to Waite or Smith and should be labelled **modern speculative invention**.

## Paul Foster Case and BOTA

Case’s BOTA deck follows Waite’s general architecture but was designed for students to colour by hand. Its minors are less radically independent than Smith’s scenic pips.

Case retains:

- Pentacles as the Earth suit;
- Golden Dawn decan correspondences;
- Tiphareth for all Sixes;
- Moon in Taurus;
- material success, prosperity, gain, and generosity.

In *The Oracle of the Tarot* Case explains that the occult name “Pentacles” derives from a ceremonial-magical disk engraved with names and sigils. [Case, *Oracle of the Tarot*](https://benebellwen.com/wp-content/uploads/2013/02/paul-foster-case-oracle-of-the-tarot-1933-source-credit-tarotworks.pdf)

Case’s numerology associates Six with balance, symmetry, reciprocal action, beauty, and harmony of opposites. His attribution is Golden Dawn-derived rather than an independent historical reading of Italian coin pips.

## Crowley–Harris Thoth, painted 1938–43; first full publication 1969

The card is titled **SUCCESS** and the suit is **DISKS**.

Frieda Harris’s image is abstract and geometric:

- six planetary disks are arranged at the points of a hexagram;
- a skeletal hexagram organizes the field;
- a central solar rose-cross glows within concentric coloured rings;
- Moon and Taurus provide the decan attribution;
- the six disks are irradiated by the central Sun/Tiphareth.

Crowley writes:

> “The Six of Disks is called Success; the ruler is the Moon.”

and:

> “The four Sixes are thus representative of their respective elements at their practical best.”

He nevertheless warns:

> “Remember only that all success is temporary; how brief a halt upon the Path of Labour.”

—Aleister Crowley, *The Book of Thoth*, 1944, part IV, “Six of Disks.” [1944 Equinox scan](https://www.rahoorkhuit.net/library/crowley/equinox/volume3/vol_3_no_05.pdf)

Crowley further describes the rose as having forty-nine petals, “the interplay of the Seven with the Seven,” and the planetary disks as irradiated by the central Sun.

**Documented fact:** “Moon in Taurus” and Tiphareth come from the Golden Dawn system, not from the historical Sola Busca or Marseille cards.

**Esoteric meaning:** lunar mutability is stabilized because the Moon is exalted in fixed-earth Taurus; solar Six/Tiphareth temporarily harmonizes Earth. Success is therefore real but impermanent.

## Notable modern redesigns

### Hermetic Tarot, Godfrey Dowson, 1978

A monochrome Golden Dawn reconstruction. It includes six pentacles, hexagram geometry, Moon and Taurus symbols, and the title Lord of Material Success. Its dense ritual emblems expose correspondences that Waite hid inside a naturalistic scene.

### Haindl Tarot, Hermann Haindl with Rachel Pollack, 1980s

The suit becomes Stones, foregrounding ecological Earth and material existence. The Six belongs to a modern spiritual-ecological redesign rather than historical game iconography.

### Wild Unknown, Kim Krans, 2012

Minimal natural imagery replaces the merchant-and-beggars tableau. Modern readings emphasize reciprocal energy, giving, receiving, and resource circulation.

### Modern Marseille restorations

Yoav Ben-Dov’s CBD Tarot, Yves Reynaud’s facsimiles, and restoration editions retain the six-denier floral structure. Interpretive additions belong to their guidebooks, not to the original printing blocks.

## The game

## What kind of card is it?

The Six is one of ten numeral cards in the historical suit of Coins. In a standard 78-card Italian pack the suit contains:

Ace, 2–10, Page, Knight, Queen, King.

It is never a trump and does not participate in trump-order controversies.

## Rank

Historical tarot uses two suit-ranking directions:

- In the **long or straight suits**, Swords and Batons, numerals ordinarily rank 10 down to Ace.
- In the **round or reversed suits**, Cups and Coins, numerals ordinarily rank Ace, 2, 3 … down to 10.

Thus in many Italian forms the Six of Coins is below the Five and above the Seven when read from high to low.

This counter-ranking is one of tarot’s oldest characteristic mechanisms, although details differ among games.

In French Tarot, which uses French suits:

- red suits rank King down through Ace;
- black suits likewise rank King down through Ace under modern rules;
- there is no literal Six of Coins, because the suit signs are Hearts, Diamonds, Clubs, and Spades.

In Central European Tarock, shortened packs frequently remove middle and low pip ranks. A Six of the French-suited equivalent may therefore not exist.

## Card points

The Six is normally a **non-counting card**. Courts and selected trumps carry points; plain pips generally contribute only through grouping or trick totals. Exact scoring differs by game.

In games counting cards in groups—often subtracting a fixed amount from grouped face values—the Six has no independent honour value.

## Tarocchino

Because 2–5 are stripped from Bolognese Tarocchino, the Six becomes the lowest retained numbered card above the Ace in each suit’s physical composition. Its rank still depends on suit direction.

## Minchiate

Minchiate retains the ordinary four suits and uses the Six as a non-honour pip. Its 97-card innovation lies in the expanded trump series.

## Trump-order disputes: non-applicability

Historians reconstruct three broad early regional trump orders, often called A, B, and C:

- A: associated principally with Bologna and Ferrara;
- B: associated with Ferrara/Venice traditions;
- C: associated with Milan and the later Marseille lineage.

Justice, Fortitude, and Temperance move relative to other trumps; the Popess and Pope undergo regional censorship; the Fool functions as an excusing card rather than a normal numbered trump.

None of these changes affects the Six of Coins’ rank. It follows suit rules, not trump order.

## Chronology of attestation

### c. 1420: Marziano da Tortona

Marziano’s *De deificatione sexdecim heroum*, written for Filippo Maria Visconti, describes a special game with sixteen classical gods and four suits of birds.

**Documented fact:** it is an important precursor to tarot-like trump play.

**Finding:** it does not contain the standard suit of Coins and therefore does not attest a Six of Coins.

### 1440 and 1442: Ferrara records

The well-known Ferrarese court accounts record painted *carte da trionfi*. The 1442 ledger is early evidence for the production and purchase of triumph packs.

**Documented fact:** these documents attest the class of pack, not individually named pips. A Six of Coins is inferred from the standard four-suit structure but not named in the account.

### c. 1440s: Cary–Yale Six of Coins

First surviving physical example. Its six coin faces establish the card materially before any surviving textual divinatory interpretation.

### c. 1450s: Visconti–Sforza Six

A second surviving Milanese ornamental coin pip.

### c. 1460s: Boiardo

Matteo Maria Boiardo composed a 78-part poetic tarot with nonstandard suits—Love, Hope, Jealousy, and Fear. Its poems demonstrate literary play with pip values but do not attest an ordinary Six of Coins.

### c. 1470–1500: sermon *De ludo cum aliis*

The anonymous anti-gaming sermon enumerates the tarot trumps in order and denounces gaming.

**Documented fact:** it is crucial for early trump order and names.

**Finding:** it does not give an individualized meaning to the Six of Coins.

### 1491: Sola Busca

Earliest complete surviving deck and earliest fully illustrated Six of Coins.

### c. 1500: tarocchi appropriati and card poems

Italian courtly poems assign cards to named people and use card imagery in social or moral verse.

**Finding:** no secure, widely cited early poem establishing charity or “material success” specifically for the Six of Coins was located.

### 1520s: Folengo

Teofilo Folengo’s writings include literary tarot material and demonstrate early-sixteenth-century cultural familiarity with the cards.

Again, this does not establish a distinct occult Six-of-Coins meaning.

### Seventeenth century

Noblet and later Marseille makers stabilize the six-denier floral pip.

### Early eighteenth century

A Bolognese manuscript sheet provides the earliest currently accepted direct evidence for cartomantic meanings assigned to tarot cards, probably after 1725. It is local and non-Egyptian.

**Finding:** it does not establish the later Golden Dawn Moon-in-Taurus attribution.

### 1781: Court de Gébelin and Comte de Mellet

Court publishes the Egyptian origin theory; Mellet associates the 22 trumps with Hebrew letters and supplies a method of divination.

**Finding:** neither gives the Six of Coins its later Golden Dawn decan title.

### 1783–89: Etteilla

Etteilla creates the first comprehensive printed divinatory tarot system and purpose-built occult pack. Six of Deniers: present time / ambition.

### 1854–56: Éliphas Lévi

*Dogme et rituel de la haute magie* makes tarot central to a synthesis of magic and Kabbalah.

Lévi writes of the suits as “Clubs, Cups, Swords, and Circles or Pantacles,” and calls the pantacle “image of the world.” [French/English scan](https://www.hermetics.org/pdf/Eliphas_Levi_Dogma%20et%20Rituel%20de%20la%20Haute%20Magie%201.pdf)

**Finding:** Lévi does not supply the later specific formula “Six of Pentacles = Moon in Taurus, Material Success.” His contribution is the broader pantacle and Sephirotic framework.

### 1870: Paul Christian

Jean-Baptiste Pitois, writing as Paul Christian, popularizes Egyptian initiation narratives and the terms “Major” and “Minor Arcana.”

**Finding:** no distinctive Six-of-Pentacles image comparable to Waite’s was traced to Christian.

### 1888: Mathers

First English list giving every card concise upright and reversed meanings:

> “Six of Pentacles.—Presents, Gifts, Gratification.”

Reversed:

> “Ambition, Desire, Passion, Aim, Longing.”

—S. L. MacGregor Mathers, *The Tarot: Its Occult Signification, Use in Fortune-Telling, and Method of Play*, 1888. [Mathers text](https://hermetics.net/media-library/tarot/samuel-liddell-macgregor-mathers-the-tarot/)

### 1889: Papus and Wirth

Papus’s *Le Tarot des Bohémiens* synthesizes Lévi, numerology, Kabbalah, and the alleged “Bohemian” transmission. Wirth supplies 22 redesigned trumps.

Neither provides an original 1889 scenic Six.

### Late 1890s–early 1900s: Golden Dawn Book T

The card acquires its enduring high-occult identity: Lord of Material Success, Moon in Taurus, Tiphareth in Assiah.

### 1909–10: Waite–Smith and *Pictorial Key*

The charity scene and Waite’s “merchant” description establish the dominant Anglophone image.

### 1930s: Case/BOTA

Golden Dawn correspondences are systematized in an American initiatory and psychological curriculum.

### 1938–43 / 1944 / 1969: Crowley–Harris

Painting, textual publication of *Book of Thoth*, then first full commercial publication of the deck.

### 1970s onward

Pollack, Greer, Jodorowsky, feminist tarot, Jungian tarot, and therapeutic tarot shift the card from prediction toward reciprocity, social power, projection, and inner integration.

### Final Heh

The Golden Dawn maps the four suits to the four letters of YHVH:

- Wands — Yod — Fire
- Cups — first Heh — Water
- Swords — Vav — Air
- Pentacles — final Heh — Earth

The Six of Pentacles is therefore Tiphareth expressed through final Heh and Assiah: solar harmony made concrete in bodily and material existence.

### Tiphareth

Tiphareth is Sephirah 6, usually translated Beauty. It occupies the central balancing position on the common Hermetic Tree of Life and is associated with the Sun.

The apparent astrological conflict—Six/Sun but card ruler Moon—is intentional:

- Six supplies the Sephirotic solar matrix.
- The specific decan supplies Moon in Taurus.
- Earth/Pentacles supplies the field of manifestation.

Crowley makes this interaction explicit: solar energy “fertilized” the exalted Moon to produce a temporarily balanced material system.

### Decan

The Golden Dawn assigns the numbered pips 2–10 to the 36 ten-degree zodiacal faces. Six of Pentacles receives the second face of Taurus, 10°–20°, ruled by the Moon in the Chaldean planetary sequence.

Calendar equivalents vary slightly by year and ephemeris; “approximately April 30–May 10” is a modern convenience, not part of the card’s fixed essence.

### Courts: non-applicability

The request’s “sub-element and zodiacal span for the courts” does not apply directly because this is a pip. In the Golden Dawn scheme:

- King/Knight = Fire of the suit
- Queen = Water
- Prince = Air
- Princess = Earth

Each non-Princess court spans roughly 30° from the last decan of one sign through the first two of the next. None of these is an attribution of the Six.

### Tzaddi/Heh swap

Crowley’s “Tzaddi is not the Star” revision exchanges the Emperor and Star’s Hebrew-letter relationships in his system.

**Finding:** it does not change the Six of Disks. The Six’s Moon-in-Taurus decan, Tiphareth number, and Earth suit remain intact.

## Divinatory meanings by author

## Etteilla, 1780s

- Upright: **Le présent** — the present time, now, today, actuality.
- Reversed: **Ambition**.

The surviving Grand Etteilla plate supplies these words directly. Expanded Etteilla-school synonym lists add present circumstances, witness, attention, vigilance, desire, and related terms.

**Caution:** English “presents” as gifts is not a literal equivalent of French *le présent* when it means current time.

## Mathers, 1888

> “Presents, Gifts, Gratification.”

Reversed:

> “Ambition, Desire, Passion, Aim, Longing.”

These meanings clearly bridge Etteilla and Waite. [Mathers, 1888](https://hermetics.net/media-library/tarot/samuel-liddell-macgregor-mathers-the-tarot/)

## Golden Dawn Book T

Upright or well dignified:

- success and gain in material undertakings;
- power, influence, rank, nobility, rule over people;
- prosperity;
- gifts and generosity;
- sometimes loss through excessive liberality, depending on dignity and surrounding cards.

Ill dignified:

- ostentation;
- prodigality;
- insolence;
- purse-proud display;
- loss or failure in material affairs.

Wording varies across published Book T recensions; “Lord of Material Success” and Moon in Taurus are stable.

## Waite, 1910

Waite’s full printed entry reads:

> “Divinatory Meanings: Presents, gifts, gratification; another account says attention, vigilance; now is the accepted time, present prosperity, etc.”

Reversed:

> “Desire, cupidity, envy, jealousy, illusion.”

[Waite, *Pictorial Key*](https://sacred-texts.com/tarot/pkt/pktpe06.htm)

This entry preserves both senses of “present”: gift and current time.

## Crowley, 1944

Crowley does not use a conventional upright/reversed list. Thoth practice generally reads dignity and context rather than card reversal.

Central meanings:

- material success;
- Earth at its practical best;
- settling down;
- temporary balance;
- real but transient attainment;
- heaviness and lack of imagination;
- a halt, not the completion of labour.

In a later summary table, the Six of Disks is associated with success and gain in material undertakings, power, influence, rank, nobility, and generosity; ill dignity can turn these toward presumption or loss.

## Paul Foster Case, 1930s

Case’s system retains:

- prosperity and material success;
- gain through practical effort;
- generosity and distribution;
- gifts;
- balance in material affairs;
- temporary rather than ultimate attainment.

Reversal or adverse dignity brings:

- loss through imprudence;
- ostentation;
- greed or envy;
- unequal exchange;
- ambition without proportion.

Case’s exact wording varies among *Oracle of the Tarot*, BOTA lessons, and later compilations. No single freely accessible edition consulted yielded one definitive compact reversed formula comparable to Waite’s.

## Rachel Pollack, 1980/1983

Pollack reads the card through Waite–Smith’s unequal visual relationship. The giver possesses the money and the instrument of judgment; the recipients kneel.

Characteristic themes:

- generosity;
- sharing resources;
- giving or receiving help;
- an unequal relationship;
- dependence and hierarchy;
- the possibility that the roles later reverse.

**Source caution:** an authorized online card-by-card quotation was not located. These are a close paraphrase of *Seventy-Eight Degrees of Wisdom*, not represented as a verbatim quotation. Pollack’s own site dates the two original volumes to 1980 and 1983. [Rachel Pollack’s bibliography](https://www.rachelpollack.com/tarot/tarot.html)

## Mary K. Greer

Greer’s reversal method does not assign only one opposite definition. In *The Complete Book of Tarot Reversals* she treats reversals through multiple modes: blocked, delayed, excessive, deficient, internalized, projected, opposite, or requiring rectification.

Applied to the Six:

- blocked generosity;
- strings attached;
- dependency;
- humiliation or resentment around receiving;
- debt;
- excessive giving;
- refusal to receive;
- Robin-Hood-like redistribution;
- correcting an inequitable exchange.

**Source caution:** only a limited searchable extract of the card-specific passage was located. The “Robin Hood—redistributing wealth from the rich” wording appears in an online scan excerpt, but a stable publisher-authorized full-text page was not available. The remainder is a careful application of Greer’s published reversal method, not a claim that every phrase is her verbatim wording.

## Esoteric and symbolic readings

## Christian

### Historical Christian imagery

The Waite–Smith scene naturally evokes almsgiving and the Christian corporal works of mercy. Scales can signify justice, proper measure, and honest commerce.

Possible biblical parallels include:

- almsgiving without ostentation;
- just weights and measures;
- stewardship of wealth;
- the rich person’s duty toward the poor;
- the tension between mercy and social hierarchy.

**Scholarly caution:** Waite did not cite a particular biblical verse or medieval image as Smith’s model. Identifying one exact Gospel passage as the source is speculative.

### Earlier pips

The floral growth in Marseille cards can be read devotionally as life issuing through matter, but no printer’s statement proves a Christian allegory specific to the Six.

---

## Hermetic and alchemical

### Sola Busca

The deck contains tools, vessels, fire, labour, armour, and strange pseudo-antique heroes. Sofia Di Vincenzo and the Brera exhibition developed a systematic alchemical reading.

For the Six, manual work on matter can be understood as transformation, craft, and the disciplining of substance.

**Status:** plausible cultural context, disputed as a precise card-by-card code.

### Golden Dawn

The Six is solar balance embodied in Earth. Material resources become a vehicle for the harmonization of force, rather than dead matter.

### Crowley

Crowley explicitly rejects passive “dead” Earth. The disks are energetic, irradiated forms. Success is the temporary coagulation of force into workable material order.

This supplies a classic alchemical pairing:

- *solve* — lunar change and circulation;
- *coagula* — Taurus and Earth stabilizing form.

Crowley does not reduce the card to this two-word formula, so it is an interpretive synthesis rather than a quotation.

---

## Kabbalistic

### Lévi

Lévi linked the ten numbered cards with the ten Sephiroth and treated pantacles as representations of the world. This is the principal nineteenth-century bridge between ordinary suit pips and the Tree of Life.

He did not yet publish the completed Golden Dawn decan table.

### Golden Dawn

Six equals Tiphareth, Beauty, solar equilibrium. Pentacles occupy Assiah, the material world. The card therefore expresses Beauty in Action or harmonious materialization.

### Crowley

Tiphareth is the central harmonizer below the Abyss. The six disks around a radiant centre turn this doctrine into geometry.

---

## Astrological

### Moon in Taurus

In traditional astrology the Moon is exalted in Taurus. Taurus stabilizes lunar fertility, receptivity, memory, sustenance, and fluctuation.

Golden Dawn synthesis:

- Moon — reception, change, nourishment, fluctuation;
- Taurus — fixed Earth, possession, fertility, durability;
- Tiphareth — balance and solar coherence;
- result — material success that is substantial but cyclic and impermanent.

**First appearance:** the complete card-specific attribution is securely associated with Golden Dawn Book T material in the late nineteenth century. It is not found on early Italian cards, Court de Gébelin’s plate, or Etteilla’s Six.

### Etteilla

Etteilla incorporated astrology into his larger system, but a modern claim that his Six of Deniers already possessed the Golden Dawn Moon-in-Taurus decan could not be substantiated. The systems should be kept separate.

---

## Numerological

### Six as harmony

The geometrical six allows:

- 3 + 3;
- 2 × 3;
- a hexagram of interpenetrating triangles;
- a centre with six directions;
- symmetry and equilibrium.

These interpretations become explicit in nineteenth- and twentieth-century occultism. They are not recoverable as named teachings from the Cary–Yale craftsman.

### Marseille numerology

Paul Marteau and later Jodorowsky-style Marseille readers interpret the two opposed ternaries and central foliage as the reconciliation of spirit and matter, inward and outward, or ascending and descending forces.

**Status:** modern esoteric interpretation applied to historical imagery.

---

## Jungian and psychological

No evidence places Jung himself in the historical development of this particular card. “Jungian tarot” is a later synthesis by tarot writers and depth-psychology practitioners.

Common readings include:

- giver and receiver as two psychic roles;
- projection of superiority onto the benefactor;
- the shadow of dependency or control;
- difficulty receiving help;
- compensation for inner poverty through public generosity;
- integration through reciprocal exchange;
- money as psychic energy or stored value;
- scales as ego judgment determining which needs deserve attention.

Rachel Pollack, Sallie Nichols, Mary K. Greer, and later therapeutic readers helped spread psychological tarot, although Nichols wrote chiefly about the trumps.

Claims that Jung taught a Six-of-Pentacles doctrine are **modern misattribution** unless a precise Jung text is produced.

---

## Social and political symbolism

Late modern readers frequently treat the picture as a study of philanthropy and unequal power:

- charity versus justice;
- relief versus structural redistribution;
- patronage;
- wages and debt;
- conditional aid;
- donor control;
- gratitude demanded from recipients.

This is not Waite’s explicit judgment, which praises “success” and “goodness of heart.” It is a legitimate later reception of Smith’s composition.

The card can therefore carry two non-exclusive readings:

1. material success naturally becoming generosity;
2. generosity preserving the giver’s authority because the giver holds both money and scale.

## People and works

### Bonifacio Bembo and the Visconti workshops

Bembo or his workshop is conventionally associated with Cary–Yale and Visconti–Sforza cards. Attribution among Bembo, Zavattari, and related workshops remains contested.

For the Six, the artist’s achievement is luxurious heraldic pip design, not narrative divination.

### Sola Busca’s anonymous engraver and illuminators

The unknown designer created the first complete surviving scenic pip sequence. Mattia Serrati da Cosandola and other artists have been proposed, but no consensus attribution exists.

### Etteilla / Jean-Baptiste Alliette

He transformed scattered card-reading practices into an organized tarot cartomancy and supplied the Six with explicit upright and reversed text.

### Éliphas Lévi

He spread the terms pantacle/pentacle, Hermetic synthesis, and Sephirotic reading that made later card-specific correspondences possible.

### Mathers

Mathers transmitted Etteilla-derived meanings into English and helped formulate the Golden Dawn tarot system.

### Pamela Colman Smith

Smith gave the card its now-dominant scene. Her staging permits interpretations that exceed Waite’s prose: the viewer can see both generosity and hierarchy.

### A. E. Waite

Waite’s description canonized the figure as a successful, good-hearted merchant and the recipients as needy and distressed.

### Frieda Harris

Harris replaced social realism with chromatic and geometric metaphysics: six planetary disks, central rose-cross, and a luminous Tiphareth field.

### Aleister Crowley

Crowley supplied the most elaborate published esoteric explanation of the card and renamed the suit Disks.

### Paul Foster Case

Case systematized Golden Dawn tarot in American occult education and tied the Six to practical balance and reciprocity.

### Rachel Pollack

Pollack helped move tarot toward spiritual and psychological self-inquiry. Her attention to visual inequality contributed to modern readings of the giver–receiver relationship.

### Mary K. Greer

Greer’s work on reversals and reader agency broadened the card beyond the simple binary “charity / greed.”

### Alejandro Jodorowsky and Marianne Costa

In *The Way of Tarot*, they developed a Marseille-native numerology rather than superimposing Waite scenes on the non-scenic French pips. Six participates in a scale of pleasure, beauty, and realized materiality.

## Literary and cinematic references

### Italo Calvino, *The Castle of Crossed Destinies*

**Documented fact:** Calvino used tarot cards as a combinatorial narrative engine, first working from the Visconti–Sforza deck and later from a Marseille deck.

**Finding:** no uniquely canonical episode centred solely and unmistakably on the Six of Coins was verified. The work is relevant to the card as part of the narrative deck, but claims assigning one character or scene exclusively to it require edition-specific layout evidence.

### T. S. Eliot, *The Waste Land*

Eliot names “the drowned Phoenician Sailor,” “Belladonna,” “the man with three staves,” the Wheel, and a “one-eyed merchant” in Madame Sosostris’s reading.

**Finding:** the poem does not name the Six of Pentacles. The merchant in the poem should not be identified with Waite’s Six merely because Waite calls its figure a merchant. That identification is a modern associative reading, not a textual fact.

### W. B. Yeats

Yeats belonged to the Golden Dawn and participated in its ritual-occult culture.

**Finding:** no poem or prose passage uniquely attaching the Six of Pentacles to a particular Yeats image was traced. General Golden Dawn membership does not prove card-specific literary use.

### Ingmar Bergman

Bergman’s films use medieval religious imagery, games, Death, and existential allegory.

**Finding:** no verified quotation of the Six of Pentacles was found. *The Seventh Seal*’s chess game and personified Death are not evidence of this pip card.

### Jodorowsky’s films

Jodorowsky uses tarot, alchemy, Christian symbolism, initiation, and Marseille-like visual structures throughout his work and explicitly taught tarot.

**Finding:** no single film frame was securely documented as a direct quotation of the Six de Deniers. General thematic resemblances—money, giving, beggars, scales—are insufficient to establish quotation.

### Paintings and buildings

The Waite–Smith scene resembles a vast iconographic tradition of almsgiving, St Lawrence distributing church treasure, St Nicholas giving gold, personifications of Charity, and merchants weighing money.

**Finding:** no specific painting or building relief has been demonstrated by documentary evidence as Smith’s model. Lists online that assign an exact source without a Smith sketchbook, letter, or close compositional match should be treated as speculative.

## Controversies and disputes

## 1. Was tarot invented in ancient Egypt?

**Legend:** yes, according to Court de Gébelin’s 1781 revelation. He announced tarot as a surviving Egyptian book containing pure ancient doctrine:

> “un Ouvrage des anciens Egyptiens, un de leurs Livres échappé aux flammes…”

He further offered fabricated Egyptian etymologies and read European card images as corrupted Egyptian symbols. [Court de Gébelin, full French text](https://www.tarock.info/gebelin.htm)

**Historical assessment:** no Egyptian tarot cards, textual descriptions of tarot, or transmission chain survive. Tarot appears in fifteenth-century northern-Italian records and objects, already integrated with European playing-card suits and Christian/classical imagery.

Michael Dummett emphasized that neither Gébelin nor Mellet supplied actual hieroglyphic evidence and that no occult use is documented before the eighteenth century. [Dummett, “Origins of Tarot”](https://www.nybooks.com/articles/1981/05/14/origins-of-tarot/)

The Six of Coins strongly supports the ordinary playing-card history: its earliest examples are countable dynastic coins, not Egyptian hieroglyphs.

## 2. Was tarot brought by “Gypsies” or Romani people?

**Legend/tradition:** Court de Gébelin associated tarot’s preservation or transmission with people then believed to be Egyptian; nineteenth-century writers including Jean-Alexandre Vaillant and Papus popularized a stronger “Tarot of the Bohemians” story.

**Historical finding:** Romani origins are in the Indian subcontinent, not ancient Egypt. No fifteenth-century evidence connects Romani communities with the invention or introduction of tarot. No substantial evidence shows Romani tarot divination before the modern period.

The story is culturally influential and should be preserved as part of occult reception, but not stated as origin history.

## 3. Cathar origin

**Disputed modern claim:** tarot encodes Cathar dualism, was preserved by heretics, or emerged from southern-French Cathar culture.

**Evidence:** no Cathar tarot, Cathar text describing the pack, or transmission document exists. The earliest physical and archival evidence is northern Italian and postdates the Albigensian Crusade.

The Six of Coins supplies no Cathar-specific symbol. Its six money emblems are fully intelligible as playing-card suit signs.

## 4. Templar origin

**Legend:** the Knights Templar transmitted secret Eastern, Hermetic, or Grail wisdom through tarot.

**Evidence:** the order was suppressed in 1312, more than a century before the first secure *trionfi* records. No Templar deck or tarot reference survives. The chronological gap is decisive unless new evidence emerges.

## 5. Sola Busca as direct source of Waite’s Six

**Claim:** Smith copied the Sola Busca Six for her Six.

**Finding:** misleading. The Sola Busca Six is closer to Smith’s Eight of Pentacles. Smith’s Six has no close compositional equivalent in the Sola Busca.

The broader influence claim remains strong because the 1907 British Museum photographs precede Smith’s 1909 work and several other cards show striking correspondences.

## 6. “Pentacles” in medieval tarot

**Modern projection:** early Italian cards belonged to a magical pentacle suit.

**Evidence:** their suit is Coins/Denari. Some devices are radiant or heraldic, but the systematic five-pointed star inside each disk is a modern occult convention.

Lévi’s “Circles or Pantacles,” Golden Dawn terminology, and Smith’s starred disks converted money emblems into ritual pentacles.

## 7. Was the Waite figure paying wages rather than giving charity?

**Modern disputed reading:** the standing figure is an employer weighing wages.

**Waite’s text:** explicitly says he “distributes it to the needy and distressed” and praises his “goodness of heart.” That supports almsgiving, not wages.

The scale may nevertheless invoke accounting, just measure, or controlled allocation. Wage payment is a possible modern reinterpretation, not Waite’s documented identification.

## 8. Are the scales balanced?

Reproductions vary, and Smith’s drawing is stylized. Some modern writers claim a deliberate imbalance.

**Finding:** no statement by Smith or Waite confirms that the scale is intentionally tipped. Interpretations built on minute differences in low-resolution scans should be labelled speculative.

## 9. Etteilla’s “present” and Waite’s “presents”

Etteilla’s **Le Présent** principally means the present time. Mathers gives “Presents, Gifts,” while Waite includes gifts and “now is the accepted time.”

**Scholarly reconstruction:** a translation pun may have generated a durable double meaning. It is one of tarot’s most interesting possible productive mistranslations.

**Open qualification:** French *présent* can itself mean a gift in some constructions, so “mistranslation” may be too absolute. The printed definite singular *le présent* strongly favours “the present [time],” while later lists deliberately exploit both senses.

## 10. Moon in Taurus as ancient doctrine

**Esoteric attribution, not historical fact:** the correspondence is part of the Golden Dawn’s late nineteenth-century synthesis of decans, Chaldean planetary order, Sephiroth, and tarot pips.

Its astrological components are ancient or medieval; their attachment to this card is modern.

## 11. Alchemical Sola Busca

The deck plainly uses enigmatic work, fire, vessels, and pseudo-antique figures. An alchemical context is plausible.

**Dispute:** whether it encodes one complete, recoverable alchemical text or process remains uncertain. Similarity to alchemical imagery does not by itself identify a unique source.

## 12. “Tarot de Marseille” as an ancient Egyptian standard

The name and standardized category are retrospective. The Marseille pattern developed from northern-Italian models, acquired French features, and was printed by multiple makers.

No single pristine “original Marseille” Six exists. Noblet, Dodal, Conver, and later restorations form a family with variations.

## Open questions

1. **Exact dating of the Cary–Yale Six:** is the narrow 1442–47 numismatic argument secure, or should the card remain broadly mid-fifteenth-century?

2. **Coin identity:** do the Cary–Yale and Visconti–Sforza roundels reproduce specific currency dies, dynastic suns, or deliberately hybrid heraldic money signs?

3. **Workshop attribution:** how should work be divided among Bonifacio Bembo, his workshop, Zavattari-related painters, and later replacement artists?

4. **Sola Busca program:** what precisely is the Six’s artisan doing, and does the action correspond to a known metallurgical or alchemical operation?

5. **Smith’s direct sources:** did she see the British Museum Sola Busca photographs or display personally? Which almsgiving or merchant-scale images contributed to her Six?

6. **Etteilla transmission:** at exactly what textual point did *le présent* become English “presents/gifts”? Mathers is an early clear witness, but intermediate French and English lists deserve collation.

7. **Book T textual history:** surviving manuscripts and later publications differ in wording. A stemmatic comparison is needed to identify the earliest exact form of the Six’s description.

8. **First explicit power-analysis:** Pollack is a major transmitter of unequal-giver readings, but earlier twentieth-century sources may have questioned the merchant’s control before her.

9. **First Jungian card-specific reading:** “Jungian Six of Pentacles” is widely invoked, but no single foundational Jungian text for this pip has been established.

10. **Artistic quotation:** no specific painting, sculpture, film, or building could be securely identified as a documented quotation of this card rather than a generic almsgiving or scales motif.

11. **Case’s exact reversed wording:** freely accessible Case materials were sufficient to establish his system but not to authenticate one definitive card-specific reversed formula.

12. **Greer and Pollack quotations:** copyright-accessible search extracts support the summaries, but edition-and-page verification against authorized print copies remains desirable before presenting longer verbatim passages.

---

## Conclusions

The reconstructable history separates into three distinct cards sharing one rank:

1. **The game card:** a six-count coin pip in fifteenth-century northern Italy. Its oldest surviving form is dynastic, luxurious, and non-narrative.

2. **The cartomantic card:** Etteilla’s “present/ambition,” transmitted through Mathers into gifts, desire, and current prosperity.

3. **The occult-symbolic card:** the Golden Dawn’s Lord of Material Success, Moon in Taurus, Tiphareth in Assiah, visualized socially by Smith and cosmically by Harris.

The most important corrections are:

- The historical suit is Coins, not inherently magical pentacles.
- The Golden Dawn attribution is verified: **Lord of Material Success, Moon in Taurus**.
- Crowley’s title is verified: **Success**.
- Sola Busca influenced the Waite–Smith minors, but its **Six of Coins is the likely source for Smith’s Eight of Pentacles**, not for her Six.
- Waite expressly identifies almsgiving to “the needy and distressed”; wage-payment and coercive-philanthropy readings are later reinterpretations.
- Egyptian, Romani, Cathar, and Templar origin stories are historically influential traditions but unsupported as accounts of the card’s fifteenth-century origin.
- Trump names and order disputes do not alter this card, which is an ordinary suit numeral governed by the ranking conventions of Coins.
