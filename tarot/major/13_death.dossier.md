# Death: Research Dossier

## Method and evidentiary labels

This dossier distinguishes:

- **[Documented fact]** — supported by a surviving card, archival document, contemporary printed text, or securely catalogued object.
- **[Scholarly reconstruction]** — a conclusion argued from material, stylistic, documentary, or game-historical evidence.
- **[Tradition]** — a customary name, interpretation, or practice whose exact beginning is uncertain.
- **[Esoteric attribution]** — a correspondence deliberately assigned by an occult author or organization.
- **[Disputed claim]** — a proposition for which scholars or sources disagree.
- **[Legend]** — a transmitted story lacking adequate historical evidence.
- **[Modern invention]** — a demonstrably recent interpretation presented as a new reconstruction, creative reading, or retrospective tradition.

Dates are dates of surviving objects or publication, not necessarily the first moment an image or interpretation existed. Tarot nomenclature is particularly hazardous: early Italian cards usually had neither printed titles nor numerals, while modern cataloguers must give them identifying names.

### Number and name

**[Documented fact]** Death is XIII in the dominant Milanese–French or “Type C” ordering inherited by the Tarot de Marseille. Medieval hand-painted Italian cards, including the Visconti-Sforza examples, are generally unnumbered and untitled. The numeral is therefore not visible on those objects even when historians can identify their position from later orders.

**[Documented fact]** The familiar Tarot de Marseille card often bears only `XIII`. It is nevertheless plainly Death: a skeleton or flayed cadaver cuts through a field containing heads and limbs.

**[Correction]** It is unsafe to say that *every* early Marseille Death was nameless. The surviving Jean Noblet pack, Paris, approximately 1650, appears to carry the compressed caption **LAMORT**. Later Marseille-pattern cards, including Dodal and Conver, omit it. Thus “the only unnamed trump” describes an influential strand of the pattern, not an inviolable original rule. The International Playing-Card Society’s pattern history treats Noblet as an early Type I Marseille representative; historical surveys likewise note the exceptional title.

**[Modern interpretive tradition]** “L’Arcane sans nom” is not a medieval card title. Oswald Wirth made the silence doctrinal in *Le Tarot des imagiers du Moyen Âge* (1927): “Seul l’arcane XIII reste intentionnellement muet”—“Arcanum XIII alone remains intentionally mute.” Alejandro Jodorowsky and Marianne Costa later popularized **L’Arcane sans Nom / The Nameless Arcanum** as the proper interpretive designation.

**[Disputed explanation]** Modern sources often claim printers omitted the title because naming Death was taboo or would summon it. No early printer’s statement proving this has been located. Because Noblet actually printed *LAMORT*, superstition cannot serve as a universal explanation. A missing label may have been conventional, accidental, economical, or meaningful; the evidence does not decide.

### The card in the larger vocabulary

- *Trionfi* (“triumphs”) was the fifteenth-century Italian name for the added trump sequence and its game.
- *Tarocchi* emerged around the opening of the sixteenth century, apparently to distinguish the card game from other things called *trionfi*.
- French *tarot*, German *Tarock*, central-European *tarok/tarokk*, and related forms name descendants of the game.
- **[Documented fact]** “Major Arcana” and “Minor Arcana” are occult-era classifications, generally credited to Jean-Baptiste Pitois, “Paul Christian,” in the nineteenth century; they are not medieval gaming terms.

Regional substitutions such as Besançon’s **Juno** and **Jupiter** for Popess and Pope, or Minchiate’s expanded trump cycle, concern the pack surrounding Death. They do not demonstrate that Death itself was originally a secret pagan figure.

# Iconography deck by deck

## Before tarot: sources of the image

### Medieval personifications and the danse macabre

**[Documented visual context]** Tarot did not invent the skeletal Death. Late-medieval Europe supplied:

- cadaver tombs and transi;
- plague imagery;
- the Three Living and the Three Dead;
- Triumphs of Death;
- Petrarch’s *Trionfi*;
- moralized pictures in which Death takes pope, emperor, king, merchant, woman, and child;
- the *danse macabre*, where representatives of every estate are led away.

The scythe converts Death into a harvester. Its biblical background includes harvest and judgment imagery; its everyday force is simple—the blade levels a field without regard to rank. The human remains beneath the tarot scythe similarly express mortality’s indiscrimination.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** The Death trump is best understood within this common late-medieval visual language. A direct derivation from one specific fresco, poem, or woodcut has not been established.

**[Correction]** The corpse or skeleton with a scythe is not the standard Greco-Roman image of Thanatos, who could appear as a winged youth with an extinguished torch. The skeletal reaper is a medieval development. This weakens claims that the tarot picture itself is an unchanged survival from pharaonic or classical antiquity.

### Petrarch

**[Documented context]** Petrarch’s *Trionfi* includes the Triumph of Death, which overcomes Love and earthly fame. Illustrated manuscripts and cassoni made “triumph” processions a major fifteenth-century genre.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** Gertrude Moakley argued that tarot’s trumps formed a Petrarchan carnival procession. The connection is suggestive but not a proven design program: neither an early commission nor a contemporary rule book says that the tarot sequence illustrates Petrarch.

## Marziano da Tortona and Michelino da Besozzo, approximately 1412–25

**[Documented fact]** Marziano composed a description of a special card game for Filippo Maria Visconti. Sixteen gods were divided among four allegorical suit categories; Michelino da Besozzo painted the cards. The deck itself is lost.

**Death status:** No tarot Death card is named. The scheme is a precursor to permanent allegorical trumps, not a surviving standard 22-trump tarot.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** Historians treat the Marziano pack as evidence that the Visconti court experimented with trump-like allegorical decks before standard tarot crystallized.

**[Correction]** It cannot be cited as the earliest picture of Death in tarot.

---

## Visconti di Modrone / Cary-Yale, Milan, approximately 1441–47

Names vary because “Cary-Yale” refers to the collection and “Visconti di Modrone” to the family provenance.

**[Documented object]** The fragmentary luxury pack is hand-painted, gilded, and associated with the Visconti court. Its original structure differed from the later standard pack, notably in additional court cards and virtue trumps.

**Death:** A cadaverous or skeletal figure traditionally identified as Death is shown **mounted on a horse**, carrying a scythe.

**Meaning of the change:** Mounted Death makes the figure resemble a triumphal rider or an apocalyptic horseman and gives Death forward momentum. It also belongs comfortably to the wider medieval Triumph of Death tradition.

**[Dating caution]** Published dates range across the 1440s; claims of an exact 1441–42 commission remain reconstructions from heraldic and dynastic evidence.

---

## Brambilla Tarot, Milan, approximately 1440s

**[Documented object]** A fragmentary hand-painted Visconti pack, named for a former owner and now largely associated with the Pinacoteca di Brera.

**Death status:** No securely identified surviving Death trump is ordinarily included in the extant Brambilla group.

**Finding from absence:** It is therefore wrong to describe “the Brambilla Death” as though the card survived. The deck helps reconstruct early Milanese production, but not this trump’s particular iconography.

---

## Visconti-Sforza, Milan or Cremona, approximately 1456–58, with disputed later additions

**[Documented object]** Seventy-four cards survive divided among the Morgan Library, Accademia Carrara, and the Colleoni family. The Morgan catalogues its Death as M.630.12.

**Depiction:** An unnumbered and untitled skeleton or desiccated cadaver stands frontally against a gold ground. A cloth is tied around the head. It holds two implements or sticks, sometimes described as a bow and arrows rather than a scythe. The Morgan catalogue cautiously says it “holds sticks in both hands.”

**Important correction:** Modern summaries frequently state that the Visconti-Sforza Death wields a scythe. The Morgan object does not show the familiar long Marseille reaping scythe. “Bow” is plausible from comparison and older catalogue tradition, but the physical object is damaged or ambiguous enough that “implements/sticks” is safer.

**Meaning:** This is Death as an emblematic personification, isolated from narrative scenery. No severed king, child, bishop, field, or sunrise appears.

**Artist:** Traditionally Bonifacio Bembo or his workshop; alternative attributions include Francesco Zavattari. Several other cards in the aggregate pack are stylistically distinct and have been attributed to Antonio Cicognara, Benedetto Bembo, Franco dei Russi, or another hand. The Morgan identifies Death with the original mid-century group, not the later replacements.

---

## Charles VI / Gringonneur group, northern Italy, late fifteenth century

Despite the old name, these cards were neither made for Charles VI in 1392 nor securely painted by Jacquemin Gringonneur.

**[Documented object; corrected provenance]** The surviving luxury cards are Italian and fifteenth-century.

**Death:** A standing personification uses a scythe; the image is closer to the reaper family than the Visconti-Sforza bow-bearing figure.

**[Disputed identification]** Exact place and date have moved in scholarship. “Charles VI Tarot” remains a collection-name convenience, not evidence of a fourteenth-century French tarot.

---

## Este and Ferrarese cards, Ferrara, later fifteenth century

**[Documented context]** Ferrarese court ledgers repeatedly mention *carte da trionfi*. Surviving hand-painted Este-associated fragments establish local luxury production.

**Death status:** Different “Este” groups are easily conflated, and not every fragment contains Death. Where a skeletal reaper appears, the Ferrarese sequence and style differ from Milan’s.

**[Caution]** Attribution of anonymous fragments to a particular Este patron is often heraldic or stylistic reconstruction. “The Este Tarot” is not one securely intact pack.

---

## Budapest–Metropolitan sheets, Ferrara or Venice, approximately 1500

**[Documented objects]** Fragmentary printed sheets survive in the Metropolitan Museum and Budapest. They preserve a popular, rather than luxury, tarot.

**Death:** Identifiable as **XIII** on the reconstructed sheet, near Wheel, Devil, and Tower. It is a skeletal Death with a scythe. The local order differs from the Marseille order around some virtues and celestial trumps.

**Significance:** These sheets prove that by about 1500 the reaping skeleton belonged to cheap printed tarot, not only to aristocratic illumination.

---

## Rosenwald sheets, Florence, late fifteenth or early sixteenth century

**[Documented object]** Uncut woodblock sheets in the National Gallery of Art’s Rosenwald Collection preserve an early Florentine trump series.

**Death:** A skeletal reaper with scythe.

**Significance:** The Florentine tradition leads toward Minchiate, but the sheets precede the fully expanded 97-card form. They should not be called a Marseille deck.

---

## Rothschild–Beaux-Arts sheets, Bologna, approximately 1500

**[Documented object; reconstructed provenance]** These woodcut fragments are treated as very early Bolognese tarot.

**Death:** A scythe-bearing skeletal figure.

**Significance:** Together with the Rosenwald and Budapest–Met sheets, they establish the scythe across several regional lineages.

---

## Cary sheet and Novati cards, Milan, approximately 1500–1600

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** These popular Milanese woodcuts share compositional features with the ancestry of the Tarot de Marseille.

**Death:** The surviving related material helps bridge the gold-ground Visconti world and later French block-printed cards. Not every relevant sheet is complete enough for every detail to be certain.

---

## Sola Busca, northern Italy, 1491

**[Documented object]** The Sola Busca is the earliest substantially complete engraved 78-card tarot. It has named classical or pseudo-classical trump figures rather than the standard medieval allegories. Its illustrated pip scenes later provided models for Pamela Colman Smith, especially the Three of Swords and Ten of Wands/Swords families.

**Death relevance:** There is no conventional card titled Death corresponding iconographically to Marseille XIII. Trump 13 is a named heroic figure in the deck’s idiosyncratic program.

**[Correction]** The Sola Busca is crucial for Waite-Smith’s illustrated **minor cards**, but no evidence shows that Smith copied her mounted Death composition from it.

---

## “Mantegna Tarocchi,” approximately 1460s–80s

**[Documented object; corrected name]** These fifty instructional engravings arrange social estates, liberal arts, cosmic principles, and celestial spheres. They are not a tarot pack and probably were not ordinary playing cards.

**Death relevance:** No standard Death trump. The series matters only as comparative evidence that ordered allegorical image cycles were common in Renaissance Italy.

---

## Minchiate, Florence and Tuscany, sixteenth–nineteenth centuries

**[Documented tradition]** Minchiate expands the tarot structure to 97 cards: 40 numbered trumps, the Fool, and 56 suit cards. It adds the cardinal virtues, elements, zodiac signs, and other cosmological subjects.

**Death:** Generally **trump XIII**, a skeletal reaper with scythe. In the traditional scoring system, XIII is a five-point counting card.

**Meaning:** In play, the picture is principally an identifier. The cosmological expansion can make Death appear as one stage within a larger natural and moral universe, but early rules do not assign it an occult meditation.

---

## Tarocchino Bolognese

**[Documented tradition]** Bologna retained an old tarot family while reducing the suit cards. Several low trumps were unnumbered or equal-ranked in later practice.

**Death:** A skeletal reaper, conventionally identifiable despite changes in numeral display. In the Bolognese Type A order, the exact ordinal implied by modern Marseille numbering should not be projected backward mechanically.

**Regional censorship:** From the eighteenth century, the four “popes” were replaced by **Moors** in Bolognese packs after controversy over papal and imperial figures. Death itself remained.

---

## Tarocco Siciliano, Sicily, seventeenth century onward

**[Documented tradition]** The Sicilian pack has 21 trumps including an unnumbered lowest **Miseria**, plus the Fool or *Fuggitivo*. Modern packs number the other trumps 1–20.

**Death:** A mounted skeleton with a scythe, commonly numbered XIII in modern Sicilian packs.

**Significance:** Horseback Death is not a Waite innovation. It has early Italian antecedents and survives regionally in Sicily.

**[Caution]** The current Sicilian sequence is not simply Marseille with one added card. Subjects and order have undergone local adaptation.

---

## Catelin Geofroy, Lyon, 1557

**[Documented object]** This is among the earliest securely dated surviving French tarot packs.

**Death:** A skeleton equipped with reaping or digging implements, commonly described as scythe and shovel.

**Significance:** It precedes the standardized “Tarot de Marseille” label and shows that French makers inherited and varied northern Italian imagery.

---

## Jean Noblet, Paris, approximately 1650

**[Documented object]** Earliest substantially surviving Type I Tarot de Marseille.

**Death:** A skeleton or flayed figure cuts through human remains and vegetation. The caption is usually read as **LAMORT**, unusually naming the card.

**Change:** The image has become a social field rather than a solitary emblem: heads and limbs lie among shoots while the blade sweeps across them.

**Meaning:** Visual equality before death is explicit. Crowned and uncrowned remains are treated alike.

---

## Jean Dodal, Lyon, 1701–15

**[Documented object]** Type I Marseille pack.

**Death:** `XIII` without a printed name. The skeletal reaper faces toward the viewer’s right and cuts among colored shoots, hands, feet, and heads. One head appears crowned.

**Change from Noblet:** The omission of the name becomes part of the influential Marseille convention.

---

## Besançon pattern, eighteenth–nineteenth centuries

**[Documented regional pattern]** Swiss and eastern-French makers replaced Popess and Pope with **Juno** and **Jupiter**, avoiding Catholic hierarchs in Protestant or confessionally mixed markets. The pattern is documented by the eighteenth century.

**Death:** It remains essentially the Marseille skeletal reaper, usually XIII. The Juno/Jupiter substitutions therefore show commercial and confessional adaptation of particular cards, not wholesale pagan origins for the sequence.

---

## Nicolas Conver, Marseille, 1760

**[Documented object]** Conver’s pack became a central model for later “Ancien Tarot de Marseille” reproductions.

**Death:** Numeral XIII only. A flesh-colored or bone-colored figure with an exposed spine swings a scythe. The black ground contains heads, hands, feet, and blue, red, and yellow plant shoots.

**Meaning in later readings:** The live-looking shoots and expressive remains encouraged interpretations of renewal from decomposition. Whether Conver intended that doctrine is unknown.

---

## Court de Gébelin’s engraved tarot, Paris, 1781

**[Documented publication]** Volume VIII of *Le Monde primitif* reproduces and interprets the trumps.

Court writes:

> “Le no. XIII représente la Mort: elle fauche les Humains, les Rois & les Reines, les Grands & les Petits.”

“Number XIII represents Death: it mows down human beings, kings and queens, great and small.”

He adds that XIII was always considered unlucky and proposes an Egyptian banquet skeleton called Maneros.

**Classification:** The description of the visible card is documentary; its Egyptian genealogy is **esoteric attribution/legend**, not archaeological history.

---

## Grand Etteilla / *Livre de Thot*, Paris, approximately 1788–89

**[Documented occult deck]** Jean-Baptiste Alliette, Etteilla, reordered the entire pack and printed upright and reversed meanings on the cards.

**Death equivalent:** Usually card **17**, labeled **MORT / DÉCÈS** in different states, paired with reversed **NÉANT** or **INCAPACITÉ** depending on edition or recension.

**Depiction:** Grand Etteilla editions vary. The Death subject retains a skeleton and cutting/reaping action but belongs to a reorganized Egyptian-Hermetic cosmogony rather than Marseille’s sequence.

**Change:** Death ceases to be merely trump XIII and becomes a specifically indexed divinatory proposition. Upright and reversed language is physically built into the design.

**Important caution:** “Grand Etteilla” is not one immutable 1789 image. Surviving uncut sheets, nineteenth-century editions, and Grimaud redactions differ in numbering, labels, coloration, and sometimes imagery.

---

## Oswald Wirth, first 22-card series, 1889; expanded exposition, 1927

**[Documented occult deck]** Wirth drew twenty-two trumps under the direction of Stanislas de Guaita.

**Death:** XIII, a skeleton mower derived from Marseille but regularized in a self-consciously occult graphic language. The card may remain without a conventional title in Wirth-derived presentations.

**Meaning:** Wirth interprets XIII as necessary dissolution, putrefaction, and the clearing away of exhausted forms. By 1927 he explicitly declares its silence intentional.

**Influence:** Wirth strongly spread the idea that the missing name is itself an initiatory secret.

---

## Golden Dawn manuscript designs, approximately 1888–1900

**[Documented organizational tradition; surviving evidence incomplete]** Golden Dawn initiates were expected to construct or color personal tarot packs from ritual descriptions and color scales. No single mass-produced “original Golden Dawn deck” existed.

**Death:** Nun–Scorpio; “Child of the Great Transformers; Lord of the Gates of Death.” Reconstructions commonly show a skeletal reaper, Scorpio emblems, and transformative water imagery.

**Colors:** Golden Dawn color attributions derive from Scorpio and the four color scales. Reconstructed decks disagree over their exact application because surviving manuscripts, temple practice, and later editorial systems are not identical.

### Robert Wang and Israel Regardie, 1978

**[Modern reconstruction]** *The Golden Dawn Tarot* translates Regardie's source material into a complete commercial deck. Its Death is a ritual-esoteric reaper rather than a historical facsimile.

### Chic and Sandra Tabatha Cicero, 1991; later editions

**[Modern reconstruction]** *The New Golden Dawn Ritual Tarot*, later *Golden Dawn Magical Tarot*, offers another realization, with more systematic Golden Dawn color symbolism.

**Meaning of the differences:** Wang and the Ciceros are witnesses to twentieth-century reconstruction, not interchangeable photographic records of Mathers’s original deck.

---

## Waite–Smith / Rider–Waite, London, 1909

**[Documented object]** Designed under Arthur Edward Waite and drawn by Pamela Colman Smith; first published by William Rider & Son.

**Depiction:**

- an armored skeleton rides a white horse;
- a black banner carries a white five-petalled rose;
- a king lies dead beneath the horse;
- a child, young woman, and bishop confront or receive the rider;
- a river and boat appear behind;
- between two towers a sun rises or sets.

Waite calls it an “apocalyptic vision.” The mounted figure strongly evokes the fourth horseman of Revelation 6, while the social ranks recall the danse macabre and Triumph of Death. The two towers and sun repeat a visual structure elsewhere in the pack.

Waite’s own description:

> “The mysterious horseman moves slowly, bearing a black banner emblazoned with the Mystic Rose, which signifies life.”

**Sources and agency:** The basic mounted Death has medieval and early Italian precedents. The apocalyptic knight, banner, white rose, bishop, child, and sunrise constitute Smith and Waite’s particular synthesis. Exact allocation of every detail between Waite’s instruction and Smith’s invention is not documented.

**Sola Busca question:** Smith clearly consulted the Sola Busca for several pip compositions. No equally direct Sola Busca prototype for her Death has been demonstrated.

---

## Paul Foster Case / B.O.T.A., twentieth century

**[Documented occult deck]** Case’s B.O.T.A. tarot returns from Waite’s mounted horseman to a standing skeleton with a scythe, broadly Marseille-derived but redrawn and designed to be colored by students.

**Depiction:** A skeleton harvests in a dark field; water and a rising sun signal continuity and renewal. The design emphasizes visible seeds or body parts as life’s durable principles.

**Attribution:** Nun, Scorpio, path 24. Case treats Scorpio’s rule of death and inheritance as a clue: the force that produces physical death is also humanity’s hidden inheritance of life and regeneration.

**Change:** Case makes the Golden Dawn correspondence pedagogically explicit while rejecting Waite’s narrative horseman.

---

## Aleister Crowley and Frieda Harris, Thoth Tarot, painted 1938–43; limited images 1940s; full deck 1969

**[Documented object]** Crowley devised the symbolic program; Lady Frieda Harris painted it. The deck was completed during the Second World War but published as a complete commercial pack only after both creators’ deaths.

**Depiction:**

- a black skeletal figure dances;
- it wields a scythe;
- the scythe’s sweep creates spirals or bubbles containing embryonic new forms;
- the skeleton wears an Osirian crown;
- fish, serpent, scorpion, and eagle imagery articulate Scorpio’s levels;
- the image is fluid, sexual, cellular, and alchemical rather than a literal field of corpses.

Crowley writes:

> “The card itself represents the dance of death; the figure is a skeleton bearing a scythe.”

And:

> “With the sweep of his scythe he creates bubbles in which are beginning to take shape the new forms.”

**Meaning:** Death is putrefaction, fermentation, regeneration, and continuous transformation. Crowley treats scorpion, serpent, and eagle as lower, middle, and higher modalities of Scorpio; fish comes from Nun’s letter-name.

**Important distinction:** The scythe and skeleton are called Saturnian by Crowley, even though the formal card attribution is Scorpio ruled by Mars. He explains Saturn as the enduring structural skeleton of things, not as the card’s primary astrological ruler.

---

## Grimaud *Ancien Tarot de Marseille*, twentieth century

**[Documented commercial lineage]** Grimaud’s Conver-derived Marseille became one of the most internationally familiar historical-looking packs.

**Death:** XIII, unnamed, skeletal reaper among limbs and shoots.

**Change:** Color blocks and lines were standardized for industrial printing. Some features treated by later readers as ancient precision may instead reflect modern redrawing or registration.

---

## Camoin–Jodorowsky, Marseille, 1997

**[Documented modern reconstruction]** Philippe Camoin and Alejandro Jodorowsky produced a redrawn Marseille deck, advertised as a restoration based on old exemplars.

**Death:** Kept unnamed; Jodorowsky calls it the **Nameless Arcanum**. The black ground, colored limbs, shoots, and active scythe are read as deep cleansing and transformation.

**[Disputed claim]** The deck’s claim to recover an original, hidden, canonical Marseille code exceeds what the heterogeneous surviving packs can securely prove. It is best classified as a modern synthetic reconstruction.

Jodorowsky and Costa call the superficial naming of XIII “Death” an error and interpret it as a revolutionary cleansing at the center of the series. This is a powerful modern reading but conflicts with Noblet’s historical *LAMORT* and Court de Gébelin’s unequivocal identification.

## Notable modern redesign tendencies

**[Modern invention/tradition]**

1. **Gentled Death:** butterflies, autumn leaves, phoenixes, doorways, or shedding snakes replace corpses to reduce fear.
2. **Literal horror:** Gothic and cinematic decks intensify skulls, plague, graves, or the Grim Reaper.
3. **Ecological Death:** compost, fungi, carrion, and seasonal cycles render transformation materially.
4. **Queer/feminist revision:** Death becomes transition, identity shedding, bodily autonomy, or escape from imposed social roles.
5. **Animal decks:** moths, vultures, snakes, and molting creatures replace the human skeleton.
6. **Abstract decks:** XIII is a rupture, void, black field, broken geometry, or cellular division.
7. **Pop-culture tarot:** a character who dies, transforms, or governs death occupies XIII.

These are legitimate artistic reinterpretations, not evidence for the fifteenth-century card’s original intention.

# The game

## Basic function

**[Documented game structure]** Tarot was invented as a trick-taking card game. The 21 special cards form a permanent trump suit; the Fool has a special excusing or highest-trump role depending on game family.

Death’s image does not ordinarily grant it a special power. It wins against lower trumps and loses to higher ones according to the local order.

In a modern Marseille-numbered 78-card pack:

- XII Hanged Man < **XIII Death** < XIV Temperance.
- In French Tarot, XIII is one of the ordinary trumps.
- It is not an *oudler*; only trump 1, trump 21, and the Excuse have that status.
- Like ordinary trumps 2–20, it contributes a low counting value—effectively 1½ points when paired under French counting rules.
- It has no rule of “killing” another card.

## Italian regional orders

Michael Dummett reconstructed three broad orders:

| Order | Core region | Characteristic |
|---|---|---|
| Type A | Bologna and Florence | Virtues clustered; Angel above World |
| Type B | Ferrara and neighboring areas | Distinct interleaving of virtues and temporal/celestial trumps |
| Type C | Milan; later France and Marseille | Basis of the familiar numbered Marseille sequence; World above Judgment/Angel |

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** These orders are inferred from surviving cards, poems, lists, and later rules. They need not have been fixed at tarot’s first invention.

Death normally remains in the central moral-mortality group, but its absolute ordinal can differ when numbering begins at the bottom and when virtues move. A surviving or reconstructed Type A list may effectively make Death the fourteenth trump rather than Marseille XIII.

## Justice and Strength

**[Documented variation]** The positions of Justice, Fortitude/Strength, and Temperance varied across early Italian orders.

- Marseille: Justice VIII, Strength XI.
- Golden Dawn/Waite-Smith: Strength VIII, Justice XI, made to align Leo with Strength and Libra with Justice.
- Crowley retains VIII as Adjustment and XI as Lust, although he renames them.
- This switch does not change Death’s XIII.

## The Fool

**[Documented variation]**

- Early packs usually leave the Fool unnumbered.
- In many Italian and French games it excuses its player from following suit and is retrieved after the trick, subject to compensation.
- In many central-European Tarock games the Sküs/Fool became the highest trump.
- Occult decks often number it 0 or place it between XX and XXI for letter-correspondence purposes.

Death never historically “kills the Fool” as a special game rule.

## Tarock

Central-European Tarock packs often abandon medieval allegories for animals, genre figures, military scenes, or double-ended designs. Consequently trump XIII may simply be a numbered genre card, not Death.

**[Documented game fact]** It remains an ordinary middle trump and usually a low-value card. High special values concentrate on Sküs, XXI/Mond, I/Pagat, kings, and sometimes bonuses.

## Minchiate

Death XIII is a permanent trump and a five-point counting card. Its importance is arithmetical and combinatorial, not divinatory.

## Sicilian Tarot

In the modern Sicilian sequence, Death is a middle trump above the Hanged Man and below the following local subject. The unnumbered **Miseria** lies below trump 1; the Fool is *Fuggitivo*. Death has no special “death” capture rule.

# Chronology of attestation

## Approximately 1412–25 — Marziano da Tortona

**[Documented text]** Marziano describes a Visconti game of sixteen classical gods. It proves courtly experimentation with allegorical trumps but does not name a Death card.

## 1440 — Florence

**[Documented archival evidence]** A Florentine record mentions two packs of *naibi a trionfi* supplied in connection with Sigismondo Pandolfo Malatesta. This is among the earliest secure standard-tarot references.

## 1442 — Ferrara ledger

**[Documented archival evidence]** An Este court account records payment for packs or production of *carte da trionfi*. The ledger does not provide a complete trump list and does not individually describe Death.

## 1440s–50s — Milan and Ferrara

**[Documented objects and accounts]** Visconti and Este cards establish the standard family of allegorical trumps. Death appears visually before surviving texts individually define its rules.

## Approximately 1450–58 — surviving Milanese Deaths

The mounted Cary-Yale/Visconti di Modrone card and standing Visconti-Sforza skeleton constitute the earliest surviving visual evidence, subject to dating disputes.

## Approximately 1470–1500 — *Sermo perutilis de ludo cum aliis*

**[Documented manuscript]** A Dominican sermon condemns dice, ordinary cards, and triumphs. It lists the trumps, including **Mors**.

Its sequence is a crucial witness to an early order. It also treats tarot as morally dangerous gaming imagery, not as a repository of Egyptian initiation.

**Dating caution:** “About 1470” and “about 1500” both appear in scholarship because the sermon’s composition and surviving manuscript compilation are not securely identical.

## Late fifteenth century — printed sheets

Rosenwald, Budapest–Met, and Rothschild–Beaux-Arts material supplies regional visual and ordering evidence. All show that a scythe-bearing Death was established.

## Approximately 1500 — Florentine songs and poems

**[Documented literary evidence]** A poem or song enumerates triumphs in an order related to Florence and early Minchiate. It helps identify Death’s regional place.

## Early sixteenth century — *tarocchi* replaces *trionfi*

**[Documented linguistic transition]** The game increasingly becomes *tarocchi*. The etymology of *tarocco* remains uncertain.

## Teofilo Folengo, 1520s

**[Documented literary use]** Folengo employs tarot cards in literary and divinatory-poetic exercises. This is evidence for symbolic and poetic appropriation, but not proof of a continuous Egyptian occult school.

## 1637 — Abbé de Marolles

**[Documented rules]** One of the earliest detailed printed descriptions of a tarot game appears in France. Death functions as an ordinary numbered trump.

## 1650 — Noblet

The early French card apparently says *LAMORT*.

## 1701–15 — Dodal

The influential unnamed XIII appears.

## 1760 — Conver

The familiar unnamed Marseille reaper receives its canonical commercial form.

## 1781 — Court de Gébelin and Comte de Mellet

**[Documented occult turning point]** *Le Monde primitif*, volume VIII, interprets tarot as Egyptian wisdom. De Mellet maps Hebrew letters to trumps.

**[Important correspondence conflict]** De Mellet’s precise allocation differs from the Golden Dawn. Systems that begin Aleph with World or arrange the trumps in reverse can assign Death to **Mem**, whereas the later Golden Dawn assigns it **Nun**.

## 1780s–90s — Etteilla

Tarot becomes a fully redesigned professional divinatory system. Death is reordered as card 17 and receives printed upright/reversed meanings.

## 1854–56 — Éliphas Lévi, *Dogme et rituel de la haute magie*

Lévi integrates tarot, Hebrew letters, Kabbalah, magic, and astrology. His chapter structure corresponds symbolically to the trumps but does not yet equal the later Golden Dawn table.

In Waite’s English translation of Lévi’s chapter XIII:

> “Death is neither the end of life nor the beginning of immortality: it is the continuation and transformation of life.”

This is a philosophical proposition in Lévi’s magical system, not a medieval gaming definition.

## 1863–77 — Paul Christian

**[Documented occult elaboration]** Christian develops an initiatory Egyptian temple narrative and popularizes *arcana* terminology. Death becomes a station in a supposed ancient initiation.

## 1888 — MacGregor Mathers

Mathers publishes *The Tarot: Its Occult Signification, Use in Fortune-Telling, and Method of Play*.

Death:

- upright: “Death, change, transformation, alteration for the worse.”
- reversed: “Death just escaped, partial change, alteration for the better.”

## 1888–1900 — Golden Dawn

The Order fixes Death as Nun, Scorpio, path 24, with the esoteric title “Child of the Great Transformers; Lord of the Gates of Death.”

## 1889 — Papus and Wirth

Papus’s *Tarot des Bohémiens* associates Death with **Mem**, not Golden Dawn Nun. Wirth produces an occult Marseille-derived image.

Papus writes:

> “Creation necessitates equal destruction in a contrary sense.”

And identifies the card as “Death, or the Skeleton Mower.”

## 1909–10 — Waite and Smith

The mounted black knight enters mass circulation; Waite supplies both mystical description and comparatively grim divinatory meanings.

## 1910s–30s — Paul Foster Case

Case publicizes Golden Dawn letter and astrological assignments through his own teaching system and B.O.T.A. designs.

## 1938–43 / 1969 — Thoth

Harris paints Crowley’s dancing alchemical Death; the full deck reaches the public in 1969.

## 1960s–80s — psychological tarot

Eden Gray, Sallie Nichols, Rachel Pollack, and others spread developmental, archetypal, and psychological readings. Literal death becomes less favored; transition and rebirth dominate.

## 1978 — Wang/Regardie Golden Dawn reconstruction

A commercial audience receives a purported Golden Dawn deck.

## 1991 onward — Cicero reconstruction

A second major ritual reconstruction enters circulation.

## 1997–2004 — Camoin/Jodorowsky and Jodorowsky/Costa

The “Nameless Arcanum” becomes a force of cleansing rather than Death properly so called.

### Nun and Scorpio

**[Esoteric attribution]** Golden Dawn Death is assigned **Nun**, Hebrew letter 14 by ordinary alphabetic counting, though the card is XIII because the Fool occupies a nonstandard position in the tarot-letter sequence. Nun means “fish.” Its path links solar Tiphareth to desire/emotion-centered Netzach.

**[Esoteric attribution]** Scorpio supplies death, sexuality, generation, poison, hidden processes, and regeneration. The familiar evolutionary ladder—scorpion, serpent, eagle—was used in Golden Dawn ritual and elaborated by Crowley.

**[Correction]** Scorpio is not visibly encoded in the fifteenth-century cards and is not attested as their original astrological meaning. The assignment is a late-nineteenth-century occult construction.

### Colors

Golden Dawn color scales assign several hues to each path depending on world or scale. Scorpio/Nun commonly receives combinations of green-blue, blue-green, very dark green or brownish tones, with complementary colors in other scales. Commercial reconstructions differ. “Death is black” is visually intuitive in Waite and Crowley but is not an adequate statement of the Golden Dawn’s complete color doctrine.

### Pips and courts

The requested decan and court data do not directly apply to Death because it is a trump:

- Golden Dawn pip cards 2–10 correspond to the 36 zodiacal decans and have titles such as “Lord of…”;
- court cards represent sub-elements and rule zodiacal spans;
- Death has neither a pip decan title nor a court-card sub-element;
- its applicable Book T data are its trump title, Nun, Scorpio, and path.

### The Tzaddi/Heh question

**[Documented esoteric controversy]** Crowley interpreted “Tzaddi is not the Star” in *Liber AL* as requiring an exchange between the Emperor and Star correspondences: Emperor receives Tzaddi/Aquarius and Star receives Heh/Aries in his revised scheme.

**Death is unaffected:** it remains Nun–Scorpio. The controversy matters because it proves that even within Golden Dawn-derived occultism, correspondences were editable theological claims, not historically recovered medieval facts.

## Etteilla: textual complications

**[Documented variation]** Card 17 appears as Death or decease in Grand Etteilla lineages, but reversed wording varies among surviving states and later editions. *Néant* means nothingness/nonbeing; *incapacité* means incapacity. These should not be silently harmonized.

**Finding:** A single accessible 1789 printed instruction securely proving every modern keyword and its orientation was not located in the consulted material. Later Grimaud and school editions are not perfect facsimiles of Etteilla’s first state.

## Waite: image versus divination list

Waite’s mystical exposition stresses passage, renewal, and higher consciousness, but his formal fortune-telling list includes literal “mortality,” destruction, corruption, and social misfortune. Modern readers often quote only the exposition and thereby make Waite sound less fatal than his own list.

## Crowley and reversals

Crowley’s Thoth method emphasizes elemental dignity and the card’s interaction with its neighbors. Publishing “upright: transformation; reversed: resistance” as though it came verbatim from Crowley is inaccurate. It is a later RWS-style accommodation.

## Pollack and Greer

Both authors helped normalize psychological and developmental reading. Neither erased mortality entirely, but both placed greater stress on surrender, release, and psychic transformation.

**Source limitation:** Search-accessible previews did not expose a complete, edition-stable Death entry from Pollack or Greer. Their summaries above are therefore paraphrase, not fabricated “own words.” Greer’s own 2002 article does document her broader principle: reversed cards need not be simple negatives and can expose constrained energy.

# Esoteric and symbolic readings

## Christian

### Medieval moral reading

**[Scholarly reconstruction grounded in visual culture]** The early image is compatible with Christian *memento mori*: all estates die; worldly dignity cannot resist Death; judgment follows. Death’s place below the celestial and Last Judgment trumps reinforces this moral cosmos.

**Caution:** Compatibility is not proof of a commissioned theological program. No surviving Visconti instruction says, “this card means Christian death and resurrection.”

### Apocalypse in Waite-Smith

**[Documented artistic synthesis]** The mounted skeleton echoes Revelation’s horsemen, especially Death on the pale horse. Bishop, king, maiden, and child recreate universal mortality. The sunrise gives the scene resurrection or immortality.

**Disputed detail:** Smith’s horse is white, not the biblical “pale” greenish horse in the Greek text. The image is therefore a transformation, not a literal illustration.

## Hermetic

**[Esoteric attribution]** Lévi turns death into transformation of the astral and vital principle. Papus makes it destruction required for formation. Golden Dawn authors place it on a path of occult initiation. None of these systems is documented in fifteenth-century tarot production.

## Kabbalistic

### Mem tradition

Court de Gébelin’s collaborator de Mellet and later French occultists generated letter mappings in which Death can be Mem, the maternal waters and transformation.

Papus states that Mem signifies “all transformations, and consequently death, regarded as the passage from one world to the other.”

### Nun tradition

Golden Dawn reorders the correspondence and assigns Nun. Nun’s fish is life within water; Scorpio makes the waters reproductive, poisonous, and regenerative.

**Conflict:** Both cannot be the uniquely recovered ancient correspondence. They are rival constructed systems.

## Astrological

**[Esoteric attribution]** Scorpio first becomes Death’s dominant astrology in the Golden Dawn milieu, not in Renaissance game documents.

Meanings derived from Scorpio include:

- fixed, intense water;
- decay and fermentation;
- sexuality and reproductive organs;
- secrecy;
- poison and healing;
- death and inheritance through the eighth-house analogy;
- scorpion, serpent, eagle, and sometimes phoenix.

**Historical caution:** The modern slogan “Scorpio rules the eighth house, therefore Death” combines sign-house analogies that later astrology systematized. It is not evidence for the card’s fifteenth-century design.

## Alchemical

**[Nineteenth-/twentieth-century esoteric attribution]** Death is *putrefactio* or *nigredo*: matter decomposes so another form can emerge.

Crowley is explicit:

> “In alchemy, this card explains the idea of putrefaction.”

The bubbles and embryos in Harris’s image make decomposition generative.

**Earlier visual compatibility:** A corpse nourishing plants can readily support alchemical analogy, but no fifteenth-century tarot document labels XIII *putrefactio*. Claims of an original alchemical code remain disputed.

## Danse macabre

**[Documented cultural source; reconstructed card relationship]** The skeletal figure and fallen social ranks belong to the same visual ecology as the Dance of Death. Waite-Smith intensifies the relationship by presenting king, cleric, woman, and child.

**No single-source proof:** No surviving artist’s contract identifies a particular dance cycle as the model.

## Jungian

**[Modern psychological invention/tradition]** Carl Jung did not publish a systematic interpretation of the tarot trumps. Later Jungian tarot writers—most notably Sallie Nichols and Robert Wang—read Death as:

- termination of an ego-structure;
- descent into the unconscious;
- shadow confrontation;
- disidentification from a persona;
- archetypal transition;
- a necessary stage of individuation.

Calling Death “a Jungian archetype” is defensible as later application of Jungian theory, not as a teaching authored by Jung about card XIII.

## Jodorowsky and Costa

They reject literal naming and emphasize vigorous cleansing. The severed parts are not inert: colors and shoots show life continuing. XIII is paired structurally with the Fool—one has number but no name, the other name but no number.

**Classification:** Modern Marseille numerology, influential but historically unproven.

## Literature

### Italo Calvino, *Il castello dei destini incrociati*

**[Documented use]** The first version appeared with Visconti cards in 1969; the expanded book followed in 1973 and juxtaposed Visconti and Marseille imagery. Characters deprived of speech narrate by laying cards.

Death functions combinatorially: its meaning changes with sequence and neighboring pictures. Calvino’s work demonstrates tarot as a narrative machine, not a fixed dictionary.

### T. S. Eliot, *The Waste Land*, 1922

**[Documented tarot use, but not direct Death-card appearance]** Madame Sosostris displays a “wicked pack of cards,” but Eliot mixes genuine and invented figures. The drowned Phoenician Sailor and blank/forbidden card have sometimes been identified with Death.

**[Disputed literary inference]** Eliot does not explicitly name tarot Death. Identifying it with the drowned sailor or unseen card is interpretation, not textual fact.

### W. B. Yeats

Yeats’s Golden Dawn membership links him to the Order’s tarot correspondences. Tarot-shaped initiatory symbolism is often sought in his poetry and *A Vision*.

**Finding:** No securely verified major work in which Yeats explicitly develops card XIII as Death was located in the consulted sources. Broad claims that a particular poem “is the Death card” require case-by-case proof.

## Film

### Ingmar Bergman, *The Seventh Seal*, 1957

**[Documented iconographic relation, not proven tarot quotation]** Bergman’s black-clad Death plays chess with the knight and leads a final dance. Bergman’s own visual sources include medieval church art, Dance of Death imagery, and earlier writings.

The film’s Death resembles the cultural ancestor of tarot XIII more than a direct copy of Marseille or Waite-Smith. Calling it a tarot quotation without production evidence is too strong.

### Alejandro Jodorowsky

Jodorowsky filled films such as *The Holy Mountain* (1973) with initiatory and tarot symbolism and later made tarot central to his teaching and psychomagic.

**[Documented]** *The Holy Mountain* explicitly identifies its thief with Fool-like symbolism and displays tarot imagery.

**[Caution]** A particular cinematic death/rebirth scene should not automatically be identified as card XIII unless the shot or production record establishes it.

- Clusone’s *Trionfo della Morte* and dance;
- European transi tombs;
- triumphal cassoni and Petrarch illustrations.

These works are relevant comparanda, not necessarily direct tarot sources.

No securely dated fifteenth-century document links Rosslyn’s program to tarot Death. The convergence is speculative and often depends on identifying generic medieval motifs as secret tarot symbols.

# Controversies and disputes

## 1. Egyptian origin

### Claim

Court de Gébelin declared tarot an Egyptian book preserved under the disguise of a game. He interpreted Death through supposed Egyptian customs and explained the pack as the Book of Thoth.

### Evidence offered

- perceived “Egyptian” appearance of selected Marseille images;
- speculative etymologies;
- analogies with Egyptian gods and customs;
- assumption that profound imagery must preserve priestly wisdom.

### Historical evidence

**[Documented contradiction]**

- tarot documents begin in fifteenth-century northern Italy;
- early cards exhibit Italian Christian, civic, courtly, and moral imagery;
- no pharaonic deck or transmission document exists;
- the standard trump sequence appears in European contexts;
- Court’s Egyptian readings do not match modern Egyptology.

Dummett argued that no evidence shows tarot carrying occult significance before Court’s intervention in 1781.

### Verdict

**Legend/esoteric attribution**, historically unsupported. It remains culturally important because it generated Etteilla’s and later occult tarot.

## 2. The Book of Thoth

An ancient Egyptian literary work now called the *Book of Thoth* does exist, as do Egyptian stories about magical books of Thoth.

**Correction:** Neither is a 78-card tarot pack. The shared name does not establish descent.

## 3. Romani or “Gypsy” origin

### Claim

Romani people allegedly brought tarot from Egypt, India, or the East and preserved its divinatory meaning.

### Spreaders

Nineteenth-century writers, notably Jean-Alexandre Vaillant and Papus’s “Bohemian” framing, spread versions of the claim. Popular fortune-telling culture reinforced it.

### Evidence

Romani practitioners have used cards for divination, but documented use after tarot’s invention is not evidence of invention. The early Italian court records predate any demonstrated Romani custody of a tarot tradition.

### Verdict

**Legend/disputed cultural claim.** Tarot is not proven to be an ancient or proprietary Romani religious artifact. Romani participation in European cartomancy is a separate historical subject and should not be erased.

## 4. Cathar/Albigensian origin

### Claim

The trumps allegedly encode Cathar theology and survived Catholic persecution in disguised form.

### Evidence

Usually thematic parallels: dualism, an evil material world, spiritual ascent, hidden heresy.

### Problems

- no Cathar tarot deck, rule book, or transmission document;
- tarot’s first patrons include orthodox Christian courts;
- Death, Devil, Pope, Judgment, and virtues fit ordinary late-medieval Catholic visual culture;
- thematic resemblance is non-exclusive.

### Verdict

**Disputed modern speculation**, unsupported as origin history.

## 5. Templar and Masonic origin

### Claim

Templars carried secret Egyptian or Jerusalem wisdom that became tarot; later Masons or Rosicrucians preserved it.

### Problems

The Templars were suppressed in 1312, over a century before secure tarot documentation. Freemasonry and Rosicrucian manifestos are later still. No transmission chain connects them to early Italian card makers.

### Verdict

**Legend.** Later Masons and occult fraternities unquestionably adopted tarot; later adoption is not medieval authorship.

## 6. Kabbalistic origin

### Claim

Twenty-two trumps were designed for the twenty-two Hebrew letters.

### Evidence

The number 22 and later symbolic correspondences.

### Problems

- the Fool plus 21 trumps may be counted in multiple ways;
- early orders vary;
- de Mellet, Lévi, Papus, Golden Dawn, and Crowley disagree about letters;
- no fifteenth-century card document gives Hebrew attributions.

### Verdict

**Eighteenth-/nineteenth-century esoteric attribution**, not proven origin.

## 7. Alchemical origin

### Claim

The sequence is a concealed alchemical operation; Death is nigredo or putrefaction.

### Evidence

Death, Temperance, Sun, Moon, World, and other images admit alchemical readings.

### Problems

Those symbols also belong to common religion, morality, astrology, and civic art. No early deck labels XIII as an alchemical stage.

### Verdict

**Plausible later symbolic application; disputed as original program.**

## 8. “The card was unnamed because people feared saying Death”

This is frequently repeated.

### Counterevidence

Noblet appears to print *LAMORT*. Court de Gébelin names it without hesitation. Early Italian cards routinely omit **all** titles, not just Death.

### Verdict

**Unverified tradition or modern explanatory legend.** Fear of the card is real in later reception, but the printing history does not prove it caused caption omission.

## 9. “Death never means physical death”

### Historical record

Etteilla, Mathers, and Waite all include literal death or mortality. Court de Gébelin describes Death mowing humanity. Popular fortune-telling associated it strongly with death.

### Later practice

Twentieth-century readers increasingly caution that it usually signifies ending or transformation, partly to avoid harmful prediction and partly because surrounding cards determine literalization.

### Verdict

“Never” is **modern invention and professional ethic**, not the historical meaning. “Usually not literal in contemporary practice” accurately describes a widespread convention.

## 10. The number thirteen caused the card

Court de Gébelin links Death to thirteen’s bad reputation.

**Problem:** Early cards lack printed numbers; regional orders could give Death a different ordinal; the history of triskaidekaphobia is itself complex.

**Verdict:** An influential 1781 speculation, not proof that medieval designers chose Death because thirteen was unlucky.

## 11. Misprints and redrawing as tradition

Tarot images were repeatedly copied from worn blocks or earlier cards. Printers repaired ambiguous lines and standardized colors. Later interpreters could treat a copying artifact as an ancient symbol.

Examples of the general process include:

- inconsistent labels and numeral forms;
- ambiguous plant shoots becoming body fragments or vice versa;
- reversed printing orientations;
- loss or restoration of small symbolic lines;
- industrial color changes;
- modern “restorations” choosing among conflicting exemplars.

**Death-specific finding:** No single securely documented Death-card printing error was found that can be shown to have created a major occult doctrine. Claims that every Marseille detail preserves a precise code should therefore be treated cautiously.

## 12. Dummett and historical correction

Michael Dummett’s core corrections were:

1. tarot was invented for playing games;
2. it arose in Renaissance Italy;
3. documentary history must precede occult speculation;
4. no evidence demonstrates an occult tarot tradition before the late eighteenth century;
5. Marseille is one historical pattern, not the primordial deck.

Ronald Decker and Thierry Depaulis reconstructed the rise of occult tarot in detail. Kaplan catalogued decks and objects; Farley studied the cultural shift from play to esotericism; Place connected iconography to Renaissance art while rejecting unsupported ancient-origin claims.

These historians do not prove that individuals before 1781 never privately contemplated cards. They show that an ancient organized Egyptian-Kabbalistic tarot tradition lacks evidence.

# Superstition and reception

## Fear of drawing Death

**[Documented modern tradition]** Popular media often treats drawing Death as an imminent fatal sentence. Horror films use it as economical foreshadowing.

**Historical nuance:** Older cartomancers did allow literal mortality, but responsible inference depended on combinations. Waite himself lists mortality while also calling the image change and renewal.

## Avoiding the word

**[Tradition]** Readers may call it XIII, Transformation, Transition, or the Nameless Card. Some remove it from decks used with anxious clients.

**[Ethical modern practice]** Many professional readers explicitly refuse to predict death. This reflects uncertainty, potential harm, and the lack of reliable predictive validation—not a discovery that the historical card “really” meant only rebirth.

## Thirteen

The card reinforces Western associations of thirteen with misfortune, and those associations reinforce fear of the card. Establishing which caused which in early tarot is impossible because early Milanese cards were unnumbered.

# Open questions

1. **Which surviving Death is earliest?**  
   The dating of Cary-Yale/Visconti di Modrone and related Milanese cards is not precise enough to establish an uncontested first object.

2. **What exactly does the Visconti-Sforza skeleton hold?**  
   “Bow and arrows” is traditional; the Morgan catalogue conservatively says sticks. Material examination may clarify the damaged implements.

3. **Why did many Marseille makers omit the caption?**  
   No printer’s explanation survives. Noblet’s *LAMORT* prevents a universal taboo theory.

4. **How fixed was Death’s rank in the 1440s?**  
   Regional orders are reconstructable later, but the exact initial sequence remains unknown.

5. **Did one particular Dance of Death or Triumph of Death supply the model?**  
   The relationship is iconographically strong but no direct model has been proved.

6. **How much of Waite-Smith Death came from Waite and how much from Smith?**  
   Waite’s published description matches the finished image, but no complete instruction sheet assigns each detail’s authorship.

7. **What is the exact first Grand Etteilla Death state?**  
   Surviving sheets and later editions differ. Card 17’s reversed term needs object-by-object citation.

8. **When did “Arcane sans nom” become common speech?**  
   Wirth documents intentional muteness in 1927; Jodorowsky greatly popularized the title. Earlier isolated uses require further bibliographic tracing.

9. **Which modern source first formulated “Death almost never means literal death”?**  
   The tendency develops across twentieth-century popular manuals. A single originator was not securely identified.

10. **Was Death ever independently associated with Scorpio before the Golden Dawn?**  
    No firm pre-Golden Dawn attribution was found. French systems typically used different letter logic.

11. **Are particular colored shoots in Conver deliberate resurrection symbols?**  
    They visibly resemble living growth, but no contemporary interpretive key survives.

12. **Can Jung himself be cited on tarot XIII?**  
    No. Jungian Death is the work of later tarot authors applying his psychology.
