# The Emperor: Research Dossier

> **Evidence labels**
>
> - **[DF] Documented fact** — supported by a dated object, archival record, rulebook, or primary text.
> - **[SR] Scholarly reconstruction** — an inference advanced by modern historians from incomplete evidence.
> - **[TR] Tradition** — a historically transmitted convention whose origin is uncertain.
> - **[EA] Esoteric attribution** — a deliberate occult correspondence, not a claim about the card’s medieval origin.
> - **[DC] Disputed claim** — materially contested or dependent on uncertain identification.
> - **[LG] Legend** — a historical origin story unsupported by contemporaneous evidence.
> - **[MI] Modern invention** — a recent redesign, interpretation, or retrospective claim.

### Names and numbering in context

**[DF]** Fifteenth-century Italian records call the new cards *carte da trionfi*, *trionfi*, or *ludus triumphorum*. The word *tarocchi* appears only around the beginning of the sixteenth century; records from Ferrara and Avignon in 1505 are among the earliest known uses. The etymology is unresolved.

**[DF]** The early painted cards carried neither titles nor numerals. “Imperator” is therefore a textual name applied by players, not an inscription on the earliest cards.

**[DF]** The Marseille family normally prints **IIII L’EMPEREUR**. IIII is not a tarot cipher: additive four was common on clocks, manuscripts, and playing cards. Modern decks frequently normalize it to IV.

**[DF]** The Fool was historically outside the numbered hierarchy and acted as the *matto*, *excuse*, or *sküs*, depending on the game. Later occultists variously made it 0, 22, or left it unnumbered. That variability does not alter the Emperor’s conventional number four.

**[DF] Regional substitutions.**

- Strasbourg/southern-German and Swiss decks ancestral to the **Besançon pattern** replaced the Papess and Pope with **Juno and Jupiter**, attested by the eighteenth century; the Emperor remained the Emperor. Production later became associated with Besançon, hence the misleadingly narrow modern name. [IPCS pattern history](https://i-p-c-s.org/pattern/ps-6.html)
- In **Bologna**, the Pope, Papess, Emperor, and Empress—the four low “Papi,” equal in play—were ordered replaced by four Moors in 1725 after the Montieri affair. Cardinal Tommaso Ruffo’s decree of 12 December 1725 condemned the offending geographical cards. [Accademia del Tarocchino account](https://www.tarocchinobolognese.it/pages/storia/i_papi_diventan_mori.php)
- Revolutionary French packs sometimes replaced Emperor and Empress with **Grand-père** and **Grand-mère**. This was political de-monarchization, not an ancient alternative identity.
- **La Maison Dieu** is the longstanding French name of trump XVI; “The Tower” is a later English normalization. This renaming does not concern the Emperor directly but demonstrates that translated trump lists are not perfectly equivalent.
- In the Sicilian pack, the Emperor survives at a locally different rank, normally **trump 3**, while *Miseria* lies below numbered trump 1.

## Iconography deck by deck

### Precursors: Marziano da Tortona, c. 1418–1425

**[DF]** Marziano’s lost deck for Filippo Maria Visconti had sixteen deified heroes or gods and bird suits. It was described retrospectively in Jacopo Antonio Marcello’s letter of 1449. It included Jupiter, but not the standard tarot Emperor.

**[SR]** It is a precursor because it is a permanent-trump card game made for the same Milanese court, not because Jupiter “became” the Emperor. No continuous iconographic chain can be demonstrated.

### Visconti di Modrone / Cary–Yale, Milan, c. 1441–1444

**[DF]** The surviving Emperor is a richly dressed, bearded sovereign beneath a gold ground. He wears an imperial crown or distinctive headgear, holds the globus cruciger, and is attended in a scene suggestive of investiture or homage. The pack belonged to the cultural and dynastic circle of Filippo Maria Visconti.

**[DC] Sigismund identification.** The forked beard resembles later portraits of Holy Roman Emperor Sigismund, and Sigismund confirmed Visconti claims to ducal legitimacy. Accordingly, some writers identify the card with him. The identification is plausible political iconography, not a documented portrait commission. No surviving ledger or caption names Sigismund. Moreover, proposed dates and interpretations of the kneeling or attending figure vary. The Italian historical survey that proposes the identification also acknowledges contradictions surrounding Sigismund’s 1433 Italian coronation and Filippo Maria’s relations with him. [Storia di Milano discussion](https://www.storiadimilano.it/arte/carte_gioco.htm)

**[SR]** If Sigismund is intended, the card makes “Emperor” legible by topical portraiture; it does not establish that every later Emperor represents Sigismund.

### Brambilla deck, Milan, c. 1440s

**[DF]** Only two trumps survive, including an Emperor. He is frontal or nearly frontal, richly dressed, holding an orb surmounted by a cross. The cards employ Visconti devices and coin imagery associated with Filippo Maria.

**[SR]** Bonifacio Bembo or his circle is the traditional attribution; workshop and dating assignments remain debated. The Emperor’s insignia expresses universal Christian-temporal sovereignty rather than a divinatory abstraction.

### Pierpont Morgan–Bergamo Visconti-Sforza, Milan/Cremona, c. 1450–1460

**[DF]** The Emperor survives outside the Morgan subset of thirty-five cards, in the dispersed Bergamo/Colleoni group. He is enthroned against a punched gold ground, crowned, and bears the orb and imperial eagle insignia. The pack combines Visconti and Sforza emblems.

The Morgan dates its portion broadly to Milan, c. 1450–1480, and notes competing attributions to Bonifacio Bembo, Francesco Zavattari, Benedetto Bembo, Franco dei Russi, and Antonio Cicognara for different groups. It emphasizes that no complete fifteenth-century deck survives. [Morgan catalogue](https://www.themorgan.org/manuscript/158525)

**[SR]** The Emperor and Pope represent terrestrial government within an ascending Christian-social hierarchy. The Morgan’s curator describes them as reflections of Renaissance structures of power, not encoded occult secrets. [Morgan trump commentary](https://www.themorgan.org/exhibitions/online/tarot/4)

### Ferrarese and Este cards, later fifteenth century

**[DF]** Ferrarese cards differ sharply from the Milanese luxury style: attenuated figures, classicizing costume, and decorative architectural or landscape settings. Surviving Este-related fragments do not preserve a single securely complete Emperor lineage.

**[SR]** Their sequence belongs to Dummett’s eastern or B order, in which Emperor follows Empress, while Papess follows Emperor. The order is clearer from texts than from any intact Ferrarese pack.

### Sola Busca, 1491

**[DF]** The Sola Busca is the earliest substantially complete engraved 78-card tarot. Its trumps bear named ancient or pseudo-ancient heroes rather than the standard Emperor/Empress/Pope series. It is crucial to tarot art and especially to Pamela Colman Smith’s pip compositions, but it has no card literally titled Emperor.

**[SR]** Attempts to map its individual heroes one-for-one onto Marseille trumps are retrospective. Its direct relevance to the Emperor is limited.

### “Mantegna Tarocchi,” c. 1460s

**[DF]** These fifty engravings arrange social estates, Apollo and the Muses, liberal arts, virtues, cosmic spheres, and First Cause. They are neither a tarot pack nor securely by Mantegna.

**[MI/TR]** The name “Mantegna Tarocchi” is a later bibliographical convention. The series illuminates Renaissance hierarchical imagery, including emperor as a social estate, but is not evidence for the playing-card Emperor’s original occult meaning.

### Rosenwald sheets, Florence, late fifteenth/early sixteenth century

**[DF]** The Rosenwald woodcut sheets include a numbered Emperor, **4**, holding imperial regalia. Titles are absent. Their sequence places Bagatella, Papess, Empress, Emperor, and Pope in the familiar low group.

**[SR]** They may preserve an early Florentine stage leading toward Minchiate. Sheet arrangement is evidence, but whether printing order exactly equals playing order has been debated. [National Gallery Rosenwald records](https://www.nga.gov/collection/art-object-page.39620.html)

### Budapest–Metropolitan sheets, Ferrara, c. 1500

**[DF]** The Emperor is recognizable through crown, orb/sceptre, and the conspicuous double-headed imperial eagle. The images preserve the Ferrarese rather than Marseille pattern.

**[SR]** The double eagle intensifies imperial universality; describing it specifically as separate “temporal and spiritual powers” is later interpretation unless tied to a contemporary heraldic source.

### Minchiate, Florence, sixteenth–nineteenth centuries

**[DF]** Minchiate expanded tarot to 97 cards: 40 trumps, the Fool, and 56 suit cards. Early low trumps became a group of five *Papi*, often represented as eastern and western emperors or rulers rather than the standard Emperor–Empress–Pope–Papess sequence. They were mutually unranked or ranked contextually in some rules.

**[DF]** The expansion added theological virtues, elements, zodiacal signs, and other cosmic subjects. Thus astrology appears pictorially in a gaming pack, but this does not prove that the ordinary Emperor had possessed an Aries correspondence.

### Bolognese tarocchino

**[DF]** The Bolognese pack was reduced to 62 cards by omitting numeral cards 2–5. Its four lowest power figures, collectively the *Papi*, were equal in rank: a player followed by another Papi did not lose merely because the second bore a “higher” subject.

**[DF]** In 1725 they became four Moors. The replacement preserved gaming function while suppressing contentious papal and imperial identities. The British Museum holds an eighteenth-century “Al Mondo” pack with the Moors. [British Museum](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/P_1896-0501-16)

### Sicilian tarot

**[DF]** The regional 63/64-card pattern uses Spanish-style suits and a distinctive trump series. Its Emperor is generally trump 3. It may show an enthroned or standing ruler with orb/sceptre, depending on period. Miseria is an unnumbered or specially indexed lowest trump.

**[SR]** Sicily’s pattern shares southern-order features but cannot simply be treated as unchanged medieval Bologna.

### Jean Noblet, Paris, c. 1650

**[DF]** Noblet’s **IIII LEMPEREUR** is the earliest surviving substantially complete Type I Marseille-pattern pack. The Emperor is shown in profile or three-quarter view, legs crossed, holding a sceptre; an eagle shield stands near him.

**[DF]** Orientation and small attributes differ from later “standard” Marseille decks. The crossed legs already form a conspicuous angular figure; the popular claim that they deliberately draw the numeral 4 is an attractive visual reading, not documented printer intent.

### Jean Dodal, Lyon, c. 1701–1715

**[DF]** Dodal regularizes the familiar pattern: bearded profile ruler, crown/helmet, crossed legs, sceptre topped by orb and cross, and eagle shield on the ground. The title reads **IIII L’EMPEREUR** in period spelling.

**[TR]** The eagle can signify imperial authority, heraldry, elevation, or the Holy Roman Empire. No surviving instruction sheet fixes one exclusive symbolic meaning.

### Nicolas Conver, Marseille, 1760

**[DF]** Conver’s version became the principal model for later commercial Marseille restorations. The Emperor leans or half-sits beside a throne, turns in profile, crosses his legs, and raises the sceptre. The shield’s single-headed eagle faces into the composition.

**[TR]** Modern readers interpret the posture as “ready to rise,” a stable four, or power grounded in matter. These are readings of the image, not eighteenth-century documented meanings.

### Grimaud, nineteenth and twentieth centuries

**[DF]** Grimaud’s *Ancien Tarot de Marseille*, standardized by Paul Marteau in the twentieth century, redraws and recolors the Conver family with cleaner industrial printing and more uniform captions. It did much to make the Conver-like Emperor internationally canonical.

### Camoin–Jodorowsky, 1997

**[MI]** Philippe Camoin and Alejandro Jodorowsky reconstructed a Marseille deck using multiple historic exemplars and Camoin family printing material. They call it a restoration, but many line details and colors necessarily involve editorial selection or invention.

Their Emperor retains the crossed legs, sceptre, eagle, belt, profile, and IIII. Jodorowsky and Marianne Costa interpret him as material stability, paternal power, government, and capacity to act. Their claim that minute restored details recover a uniquely original, initiatory code is not accepted as historical proof.

### Etteilla’s *Grand Etteilla*, 1788–1789

**[DF]** Etteilla reorganized all 78 cards into a purported Egyptian cosmogony. Consequently, Marseille trump IV is not simply retained as card 4 “The Emperor.” Images, numbers, and meanings were redistributed.

**[EA]** Etteilla’s system is the first purpose-built occult/cartomantic tarot. Later tables that report “Etteilla’s meaning of the Emperor” often silently translate between his reordered pack and Marseille IV. Such equivalences must be treated cautiously.

### Oswald Wirth, 1889

**[DF]** Under Stanislas de Guaita’s direction, Wirth produced twenty-two hand-colored *Arcanes du Tarot kabbalistique*. The Emperor is a deliberately occultized Marseille ruler on a cubic or throne-like base, furnished with imperial eagle, sceptre, and Hebrew letter.

**[EA]** Wirth followed Éliphas Lévi’s alphabet running Aleph at the Magician; therefore Emperor IV bears **Daleth**, not Golden Dawn Heh. [BnF/Wirth archive description](https://foolmade.art/archive/wirth-tarot)

### Golden Dawn manuscripts and reconstructions

**[DF]** *Book T* supplied titles, colors, Hebrew letters, astrology, and images or image instructions. Its Emperor is Heh/Aries and “Son [or Sun] of the Morning, Chief among the Mighty.”

**[DC] Textual variant.** Early printed *Book T/Liber 78* and Crowley’s *777* reportedly read **“Sun of the Morning”**; later Golden Dawn editions often print **“Son.”** This looks like a transmission or editorial variant, not two developed doctrines.

**[MI]** Robert Wang’s *Golden Dawn Tarot* and Sandra Tabatha and Chic Cicero’s reconstruction are twentieth-century visual realizations of manuscript instructions. They are evidence for modern reception, not surviving nineteenth-century “official decks.”

### Waite–Smith, 1909

**[DF]** Pamela Colman Smith drew a crowned, white-bearded ruler seated frontally on a stone throne. Four ram heads decorate throne and shoulders. He wears armor beneath red robes, holds an ankh-like sceptre and orb, and sits before barren red mountains.

**[EA]** The rams explicitly disclose the Golden Dawn Aries attribution. Armor supplies Mars-like martial force; the barren mountains and red-orange field emphasize fiery severity. Waite calls the sceptre a “form of the Crux ansata.”

**[SR] Sources.** The basic sovereign, sceptre, orb, and throne belong to the historic Emperor tradition. The overt rams derive from Golden Dawn Aries doctrine. Smith demonstrably borrowed several minor-arcana compositions from the Sola Busca, but no comparably exact Sola Busca model for her Emperor is established.

### Paul Foster Case and BOTA

**[DF]** Case retained Smith’s frontal armored Emperor and the Golden Dawn Heh/Aries attribution but redrew it with Jessie Burns Parke for the BOTA deck, designed to be colored by students.

Case explains: “The fundamental meaning of the 4th Key is Reason.” The Emperor oversees and controls as reason supervises activity; rams signify Aries, armor Mars, and the globe dominion. [BOTA text](https://www.botaineurope.org/the-tarot/4-the-emperor)

### Thoth, painted 1938–1943; book 1944; deck issued 1969

**[DF]** Frieda Harris’s Emperor is a geometric, dynamic red figure rather than a naturalistic monarch. He bears crossed or radiating forms, heraldic double-headed eagle imagery, rams, the Lamb and Flag, and bees. Crowley identifies him with Aries and Tzaddi.

**[EA]** The Lamb and Flag is both Christian Agnus Dei imagery and Aries on a lower plane. The wild ram represents aggressive Aries; the lamb its domesticated counterpart. The bees evoke ordered imperial society and, more specifically, Napoleonic imperial emblematic language. Bees were a prominent device of Napoleon I, chosen partly to rival or predate the Bourbon fleur-de-lis.

**[DC]** Claims that every bee is a direct Napoleon quotation are interpretive; Crowley discusses governmental, paternal, martial, and alchemical symbolism rather than documenting Harris’s source for each motif.

### Notable modern redesigns

**[MI]** Modern decks have made the Emperor female, nonbinary, disabled, Black, Indigenous, corporate, anti-authoritarian, ecological, or explicitly tyrannical. Common transformations include:

- ruler into architect or community elder;
- throne into office, machine, fortress, or city;
- Aries rams into horns, flames, or a Mars glyph;
- fatherhood into boundary-setting and institutional care;
- imperial domination into a warning about patriarchy.

These are contemporary interventions in the card’s reception, not recoveries of a lost medieval archetype.

## The game

### Function

**[DF]** In tarot-family trick-taking games, the Emperor is a permanent trump. It beats every ordinary suit card and any lower trump; it loses to higher trumps. Unlike the Fool, lowest trump, and highest trump in many games, it normally carries no exceptional bonus or point value.

In modern French tarot, trump 4 is simply one of the twenty-one numbered *atouts*. It is worth the ordinary half-point as a low trump, not one of the three *oudlers*—1, 21, and the Excuse.

In Austrian and central-European Tarock packs, traditional subjects were replaced by genre scenes or animals. Card IV still functions as trump IV, although no Emperor may be pictured.

### Three regional orders

Dummett’s A/B/C classification is structural and historical, not an occult sequence:

| Order | Principal region | Low power sequence | Highest end | Emperor |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A, southern | Bologna–Florence; later southern descendants | Bagatella, Papess, Empress, Emperor, Pope; local equalities occur | Angel above World | Low trump; in Bologna part of equal *Papi* |
| B, eastern | Ferrara/Veneto | Bagatella, Empress, Emperor, Papess, Pope | World above Justice and Angel | Third named trump in the Steele sermon |
| C, western | Milan, Piedmont, France/Marseille | Magician, Papess, Empress, Emperor, Pope | World above Angel | Number IIII |
| Possible fourth/Franco-Italian | Savoy/Belgian-related evidence | Variable | Variable | Incompletely reconstructable |

**[DF]** Justice, Strength, and Temperance move substantially between orders. In the B sequence Justice stands near the summit. In C/Marseille, Justice is VIII and Strength XI. The Golden Dawn/Waite system exchanged their printed numbers—Strength VIII, Justice XI—to align Leo and Libra with zodiacal order.

**[DF]** The Fool has no ordinary rank. It is played as an excuse in many games and normally cannot win a trick. In some Italian games it contributes to combinations or scoring; in later Austrian usage *Sküs* becomes one of the highest-value honors while remaining iconographically the Fool.

### Earliest documentary witnesses

- **c. 1418–1425:** Marziano’s god-card game; a precursor, not yet the standard Emperor sequence.
- **1440, Florence:** record of two *trionfi* packs sent to Sigismondo Pandolfo Malatesta; this presently precedes the once-canonical 1442 Ferrara evidence.
- **1442, Ferrara:** Este accounts pay for *carte da trionfi*. No individual cards are named.
- **1449:** Marcello calls Marziano’s earlier game a *ludus triumphorum*.
- **c. 1450–1480:** *Sermo perutilis de ludo cum aliis* gives the first full named list: “Imperatrix. Imperator. La papessa. El papa.”
- **Late fifteenth century:** literary lists and poems confirm local orders.
- **1505:** *tarocchi/taraux* appears in Ferrara and Avignon.
- **Teofilo Folengo, 1527:** *Chaos del Triperuno* uses tarot cards in literary-poetic combinations, important early evidence for emblematic or characterological use, but not a full modern divination system.

### The Tzaddi/Heh question

**[DF] Golden Dawn baseline.** Emperor = Heh = Aries; Star = Tzaddi = Aquarius. This preserves zodiacal order around the trumps once Strength and Justice are placed at VIII and XI.

**[DF] Revelation text.** *Liber AL vel Legis* I:57 says: “All these old letters of my Book are aright; but צ is not the Star.” The statement does not itself say “Tzaddi is the Emperor.”

**[EA] Crowley’s solution.** Crowley eventually exchanged the Hebrew letters of Emperor and Star: Emperor became Tzaddi, Star became Heh. He ordinarily retained Emperor–Aries and Star–Aquarius, producing a loop or double-loop in the zodiacal sequence rather than exchanging the zodiac signs.

In *The Book of Thoth*, Crowley writes that the Emperor “is attributed to the letter Tzaddi” and “refers to the sign of Aries.” He adduces phonetic wordplay—Tsar/Czar/Caesar and related sovereign titles—and the fishhook-like sense of Tzaddi.

**[DC] Diagrams.** Some reproduced schemas appear to exchange signs, paths, letters, or card positions inconsistently. Scholars and practitioners disagree whether these are typographical errors, different levels of attribution, or unresolved features of Crowley’s system. The safest formulation is:

- Golden Dawn: Emperor–Heh–Aries; Star–Tzaddi–Aquarius.
- Crowley’s stated prose: Emperor–Tzaddi–Aries; Star–Heh–Aquarius.
- Particular diagrams must be checked individually and should not override his explicit prose without explanation.

### Pips, courts, decans

The brief requests Book T pip and court data “where applicable.” It is **not applicable to the Emperor itself**:

- Minor pips 2–10 receive decans and titles.
- Courts receive sub-elements and zodiacal spans.
- The Emperor is a major trump and receives one zodiac sign, Aries, not a 10-degree decan or court span.

### Waite in his own words

Waite describes the figure as “commanding, stately” and calls him “executive and realization, the power of this world.” He distinguishes mundane royalty from “the higher kingship” and “lordship of thought.” [*Pictorial Key*, Emperor chapter](https://sacred-texts.com/tarot/pkt/pktar04.htm)

## Esoteric and symbolic readings

### Christian

**[DF/TR]** The original globe-and-cross is the *globus cruciger*: the world under Christ’s cross and the Christian sovereign’s divinely sanctioned office. Medieval and Renaissance viewers did not need an occult code to recognize it.

**[TR]** Emperor and Pope can be read as paired temporal and spiritual powers. Their relative order differed regionally, so claims about one universally “trumping” the other require caution.

**[EA]** Crowley’s Lamb and Flag deliberately imports Agnus Dei and resurrection/conquest imagery into an Aries system.

### Hermetic and alchemical

**[EA]** Nineteenth-century French occultists recast the Emperor as manifested, stabilized creative force: spirit condensed into form, the quaternary, cube, or foundation.

**[EA]** In Crowley, the Emperor expresses fiery sulphur, male creative energy, martial Aries, and government. Ram/lamb polarity is raw versus domesticated force. This is twentieth-century Thelemic alchemy, not fifteenth-century evidence.

### Kabbalistic

**[EA]** De Mellet first joined the 22 trumps to 22 Hebrew letters in 1781. Lévi made the sequence programmatic; Papus and Wirth spread it.

Two incompatible lineages followed:

- Lévi–Papus–Wirth: Magician = Aleph, hence Emperor = Daleth.
- Golden Dawn–Waite–Case: Fool = Aleph, Magician = Beth, hence Emperor = Heh.
- Crowley: Emperor = Tzaddi following *Liber AL* I:57.

No medieval Jewish manuscript connects the Emperor card with Daleth, Heh, Tzaddi, Aries, or the Tree of Life.

### Astrological

**[EA]** Explicit Emperor–Aries attribution belongs to the Golden Dawn current, not historic Marseille gaming practice. Waite’s rams made it visible; Case explicated it; Crowley retained Aries while changing the Hebrew letter.

Associated astrological ideas include cardinal initiative, fire, rulership, head, Mars as domicile ruler, and Sun as exalted in Aries.

### Egyptian

**[LG]** Court de Gébelin transformed Emperor into a “King” within his alleged Egyptian book. Etteilla elaborated Egyptian cosmogony; Paul Christian dramatized initiation through underground Egyptian temples. None had access to evidence linking Italian trumps to ancient Egypt.

### Numerological

**[EA/MI]** Four becomes square, cube, foundation, four elements, four directions, stability, law, or material realization. Wirth, Papus, Case, and Jodorowsky spread different versions.

The crossed legs “making a 4” are visually suggestive, but no early printer’s explanation survives.

### Jungian

**[MI]** Twentieth-century writers treat the Emperor as father archetype, lawgiving Logos, conscious order, animus, authority complex, or rigid shadow. Sallie Nichols, Pollack, Greer, and later therapeutic readers popularized this approach.

Jung himself did not publish a tarot system or a card-by-card interpretation of the Emperor. “Jung’s meaning of the Emperor” is therefore a modern application of Jungian concepts.

### Literature, cinema, and visual culture

**Italo Calvino, *The Castle of Crossed Destinies* (1969/1973).**  
**[DF]** Calvino constructs intersecting narratives from tarot spreads, using Visconti and Marseille imagery in different parts. The Emperor participates in tarot’s vocabulary of rulers and social order, but the work is not a historical commentary on Atu IV.

**T. S. Eliot, *The Waste Land* (1922).**  
**[DF]** Madame Sosostris names “the drowned Phoenician Sailor,” “Belladonna,” “the man with three staves,” “the Wheel,” and “the one-eyed merchant”; the Emperor is not named. Associating the poem specifically with the Emperor is therefore an extrapolation.

**W. B. Yeats.**  
**[DF]** Yeats was involved in the Golden Dawn and used astrological, magical, and gyre symbolism. No canonical Yeats poem or essay giving a distinct Emperor-card exegesis was located. His relevance is institutional, not card-specific.

**Jodorowsky’s films.**  
**[DF/MI]** *The Holy Mountain* (1973) is saturated with tarot, planetary, alchemical, and initiatory imagery. Jodorowsky’s later tarot teaching informs readings of patriarchal and institutional figures, but one-to-one claims that a particular film character “is the Emperor” require scene-specific evidence.

**Ingmar Bergman.**  
**Absence finding.** No reliable primary or scholarly source was located showing that Bergman directly quoted the Emperor card. *The Seventh Seal* invites comparison with medieval allegory and games, but its game is chess, not tarot.

**Paintings and buildings.**  
**Absence finding.** The profile Emperor resembles a broad repertory of enthroned rulers, imperial seals, *globus cruciger* portraits, and virtues-and-estates cycles. No single painting or building has been demonstrated as the unique model for the Marseille card. Assertions involving Castel del Monte, Chartres, Rosslyn Chapel, Templar buildings, or Egyptian temples are analogical unless supported by transmission evidence.

## Controversies and disputes

### Egyptian origin

**[LG]** Court de Gébelin claimed in 1781 that tarot was a surviving Egyptian book and derived “tarot” from supposed Egyptian words. Egyptian hieroglyphs were not deciphered until after Champollion’s 1822 breakthrough, so Gébelin could not test his linguistic claims.

**Evidence against:** northern Italian records and surviving objects appear centuries after pharaonic Egypt and use recognizably late-medieval European social, Christian, and allegorical imagery. No Egyptian tarot object, intermediary manuscript, or transmission record exists.

### Romani or “Gypsy” origin

**[LG]** De Mellet and especially nineteenth-century writers associated tarot with people then inaccurately believed to be Egyptian. Jean-Alexandre Vaillant, Papus’s title *Le Tarot des Bohémiens*, and popular fortune-telling culture spread the story.

**Evidence against:** European playing cards and Italian *trionfi* are documented before evidence that Romani communities carried tarot as a distinctive tradition. Linguistic and historical research places Romani origins in South Asia, not Egypt. “Gypsy” is retained here only when quoting historic titles or claims.

### Cathar, Bogomil, and Templar origins

**[LG/DC]** Twentieth-century occult and alternative-historical writers have read the Popess, Grail-like Cups, Tower, and dualistic imagery as secret Cathar or Templar doctrine.

**Evidence:** thematic parallels only. No Cathar or Templar text names tarot, lays out its trumps, or shows transmission to fifteenth-century Milan, Ferrara, Bologna, or Florence. Waite speculated about Christian mysteries and Grail traditions but did not establish documentary descent.

### Emperor as Sigismund

**[DC]** The Visconti di Modrone Emperor’s beard and political context support a possible reference to Sigismund. Against certainty:

- no caption names him;
- imperial attributes are conventional;
- dates of the pack remain contested;
- later Emperor cards are generic;
- proposed narratives about homage or coronation conflict.

The careful conclusion is “possible topical portrait,” not “the card is Sigismund.”

### The crossed legs

**[TR/MI]** Claimed meanings include the number four, alchemical sulphur, a Masonic sign, sovereignty, sexual closure, and readiness to act.

**Evidence:** crossed-leg authority poses occur in medieval art, and the shape resembles 4. No Noblet, Dodal, or Conver manual explains it. Specific occult readings are later overlays.

### Eagle identity

**[TR/DC]** It may be the imperial eagle, Holy Roman eagle, heraldic authority, solar bird, or spirit over matter. The double-headed eagle on the Budapest sheets is more specifically imperial; the single eagle of Marseille is less determinate. Claims that it is definitely Zeus’s eagle or an alchemical fixed-sulphur emblem depend on later systems.

### Bees and Napoleon in the Thoth Emperor

**[DF/EA]** Napoleon used bees as imperial emblems. Crowley and Harris’s card contains bees amid a consciously imperial composition.

**[SR]** Reading them as an allusion to Napoleon is strong contextual interpretation. Reading every bee as proof that the figure literally depicts Napoleon exceeds the evidence.

### “Misprints that became traditions”

- **IIII:** not demonstrably a misprint; additive Roman notation is historically ordinary.
- **Son/Sun of the Morning:** likely a textual transmission variant among *Book T*, *777*, and later Golden Dawn editions.
- **Crowley schemas:** some tables appear inconsistent with his prose on Tzaddi/Heh and Aries/Aquarius; edition-specific errors are likely, but not every discrepancy has been conclusively resolved.
- **Conver-derived details:** broken woodblocks, repainting, stencil drift, and commercial redrawings were sometimes spiritualized by twentieth-century restorers.
- **Waite–Smith publication date:** the deck appeared in 1909; *The Pictorial Key* is commonly dated 1910 but copies bear 1911. Treating all components as “the 1910 deck” blurs separate publication events.
- **Thoth publication date:** paintings date 1938–1943, *Book of Thoth* appeared in 1944, and the complete commercial deck in 1969. “The 1944 Thoth deck” conflates book and cards.

### Historical correction

**[SR supported by documents]** Dummett’s central correction was methodological: tarot began as a northern Italian card game; occult and divinatory systems are later historical developments. Decker and Dummett traced the occult tarot from Gébelin through Etteilla and later schools. Depaulis refined French cartomantic and manufacturing history. Kaplan documented a very large corpus, though some early volumes repeat older datings now superseded. Place and Farley emphasize Renaissance cultural imagery while sometimes offering more symbolic synthesis than strict game historians.

The correction does not make occult traditions “meaningless”; it dates them accurately. Aries, Heh, Tzaddi, the Tree paths, Egyptian initiation, Jungian fatherhood, and psychomagical readings belong to reception history rather than the demonstrable fifteenth-century design brief.

## Open questions

1. **Who designed the standardized twenty-one-trump sequence?** No author, commission contract, or prototype survives.
2. **Which city first created standard tarot?** Florence presently has the earliest documentary notice, while Milan, Ferrara, and Bologna each preserve early independent evidence.
3. **How precisely should the Visconti di Modrone deck be dated?** Heraldry, marriage context, workshop attribution, and coin evidence yield competing ranges.
4. **Does its Emperor portray Sigismund?** Plausible but unproven.
5. **What was the exact earliest trump order?** All surviving lists are later than the presumed invention, and three regional families were already established.
6. **Did sheet layout always encode playing order?** Often likely, never automatic.
7. **What was the immediate visual source of the Marseille crossed-leg pose?** General sovereign iconography is clear; a unique prototype is not.
8. **Was “Sun” or “Son of the Morning” the original Golden Dawn title?** Early witnesses favor “Sun,” while “Son” became standard in influential later editions.
9. **How should Crowley’s inconsistent-looking tables be reconciled with his prose?** Edition, printing history, path geometry, and separate letter/sign layers require further manuscript comparison.
10. **What precisely did Harris intend by the bees?** Napoleonic imperial resonance is strong, but a card-specific working note would be decisive.
11. **Can Etteilla’s original Emperor meaning be stated without retroactive remapping?** Only after identifying the precise Grand Etteilla edition and card-number equivalence; popular tables frequently conflate systems.
12. **Did any fifteenth-century players assign divinatory meanings to individual trumps?** Literary and emblematic use is documented; systematic cartomancy is not.
