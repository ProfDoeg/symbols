# The Hierophant: Research Dossier

> **Card:** Trump V  
> **Earliest standard-tarot subject:** *Il Papa*—the Pope  
> **Earliest surviving example:** the Pope in the Visconti-Sforza group, Milan/Cremona, usually dated c. 1450–1460; the Morgan catalog currently gives c. 1450–1480 and attributes the original group traditionally to Bonifacio Bembo or his workshop.  
> **Canonical French name:** *Le Pape*  
> **English occult name:** *The Hierophant*  
> **Golden Dawn title:** *The Magus of the Eternal*  
> **Golden Dawn attribution:** Hebrew ו, *Vav/Vau* (“nail” or “hook”); Taurus; sixteenth path, Chokmah–Chesed.  
> **Thoth title:** *The Hierophant*, Atu V; Vau; Taurus.  
> **Status:** Historically a game trump representing the supreme priestly estate. Its Kabbalistic, astrological, Egyptian, alchemical, and psychological identities are later occult constructions, principally eighteenth through twentieth century.

## Method and evidentiary labels

The following labels distinguish unlike kinds of statement:

- **[Documented fact]** Supported directly by a surviving object, archival entry, rule book, or primary printed text.
- **[Scholarly reconstruction]** A reasoned historical conclusion, normally associated here with Michael Dummett, Thierry Depaulis, Ronald Decker, Stuart Kaplan, Andrea Vitali, Robert M. Place, or Helen Farley.
- **[Tradition]** A conventional meaning or practice transmitted within tarot reading or occult organizations.
- **[Esoteric attribution]** A deliberate occult correspondence, not a claim about the card’s fifteenth-century purpose.
- **[Disputed claim]** Asserted but materially contested or weakly evidenced.
- **[Legend]** A narrative contradicted by—or lacking—the documentary chronology.
- **[Modern invention]** A comparatively recent redesign or interpretation whose novelty can be identified.

Dates assigned to unique hand-painted cards are approximate. Fifteenth-century packs are fragmentary, lack printed titles and numbers, and have often been reorganized by later collectors.

“Hierophant” derives from Greek ἱεροφάντης, literally a revealer or displayer of sacred things, especially an official associated with the Eleusinian Mysteries. Its use for this tarot card is therefore a classicizing reinterpretation, not the translation of a fifteenth-century title.

## Number

- **[Documented fact]** In the numbered Tarot de Marseille and its occult descendants the card is V.
- **[Documented fact]** The early Milanese hand-painted trumps carry no numbers.
- **[Scholarly reconstruction]** In every known regional trump-order family the Pope belongs to the lowest group of social rulers and normally stands above the other papal/imperial figures or at the top of that group.
- **[Documented fact]** In Bologna the Popess, Empress, Emperor, and Pope became the four equal-ranking *papi*. Consequently “V” is not an adequate description of their game relationship.
- **[Documented fact]** In Minchiate, the four ordinary tarot rulers were compressed and secularized; the low trumps include the *Papino* and *Granduca*, rather than a straightforward Marseille V.
- **[Tradition]** The Fool is unnumbered in early and Marseille play. “0” is an occult/modern numbering convention; XXII occurs in some later packs, including Belgian examples, but does not describe its original excusing function.

## Earliest surviving example

The safest presently reconstructable answer is the Visconti-Sforza Pope, Milan or Cremona, approximately 1450–1460. The Morgan card shows a solitary seated pope:

- veiled and wearing a triple tiara;
- right hand raised in blessing;
- cross-staff in his left hand;
- richly patterned robes against a punched-gold ground.

The Morgan catalog describes it exactly as “Pope, veiled, wearing triple crown tiara, is seated. He raises right hand in blessing and holds cross in left hand.” [Morgan, “004. The Pope”](https://www.themorgan.org/collection/tarot-cards/the-pope).

The present Morgan exhibition catalog narrows the principal group to approximately 1456–1458, while the permanent manuscript record retains the broader 1450–1480 range. That discrepancy reflects evolving attribution and dating, not two different cards.

### Correction to a recurring claim

**[Negative finding]** “The Pope with two acolytes in the fifteenth century” could not be verified as a general feature of the surviving Visconti cards. The Morgan Visconti-Sforza Pope is plainly solitary. Two small attendants occur in later printed ancestry and become canonical in the Marseille family. Some fragmentary sheets may preserve early versions of the attendant composition, but treating it as securely present in every fifteenth-century Milanese Pope is unwarranted.

## Golden Dawn attribution

According to *Book T*:

- Trump: V
- Title: *The Hierophant*
- Esoteric title: *The Magus of the Eternal*
- Letter: ו, Vau/Vav
- Meaning of letter: nail or hook
- Path: 16
- Sephiroth joined: Chokmah and Chesed
- Astrology: Taurus
- Elemental quality: fixed Earth, through Taurus
- Ruler of Taurus: Venus
- Exaltation in Taurus: Moon
- Color scales: red-orange; deep indigo; deep warm olive; rich brown.

These are **[esoteric attributions]**, not historical explanations of the medieval card. They derive from the Golden Dawn’s synthesis of the Hebrew alphabet, *Sefer Yetzirah*, astrology, the Tree of Life, and tarot, developed in the late nineteenth century. The surviving *Book T* text calls the card “The Magus of the Eternal—Vau—Taurus” ([Book T scan](https://jantetteroo.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/02/Mathers_and_felkin_golden_dawn_book_t_th.pdf)).

# Iconography deck by deck

## Milanese hand-painted decks

### Visconti di Modrone / Cary-Yale, c. 1441–1450, Milan

**[Documented fact]** The fragment now at Yale is an unusually expanded luxury pack, with six court cards per suit and theological virtues absent from ordinary later tarot. It is often connected with Filippo Maria Visconti and the marriage milieu of Bianca Maria Visconti.

**Pope:** No securely surviving Pope card is normally included among the extant Cary-Yale trumps. Therefore descriptions of a Cary-Yale Pope are reconstructions, not observations.

**Meaning of absence:** None can safely be inferred. Fifteenth-century packs survive accidentally and incompletely.

The Morgan’s comparative study identifies the Visconti di Modrone, Brera-Brambilla, and Visconti-Sforza groups as three of the oldest surviving tarocchi sets ([Morgan study day](https://www.themorgan.org/thaw-conservation-center/tarocchi-virtual-study-day)).

### Brera-Brambilla, c. 1440s, Milan

**[Documented fact]** Preserved principally at the Pinacoteca di Brera and associated with Filippo Maria Visconti.

**Pope:** Not among the securely preserved trumps. Claims about its exact design are hypothetical.

### Visconti-Sforza, c. 1450–1460, Milan/Cremona

**[Documented fact]** This is the earliest surviving unmistakable standard-tarot Pope.

The pontiff is monumental and alone. His triple tiara marks papal office; his blessing hand makes his function spiritual rather than military; his staff embodies jurisdiction. The gold ground and frontal enthronement belong to late-medieval sacred and court portraiture.

**What it meant in its first context:** **[Scholarly reconstruction]** Not “conformity,” “Taurus,” or occult initiation, but the papal estate within a hierarchy of worldly and cosmic powers. In the game sequence, spiritual authority triumphs over lesser social ranks and is in turn overtaken by Love, the Virtues, Fortune, Time, Death, and celestial or eschatological powers.

The card lacks:

- attendants;
- crossed keys;
- Hebrew letter;
- Taurus symbol;
- printed number;
- printed title.

Those are later developments.

### Other Bembo-workshop questions

The Morgan records traditional attribution to Bonifacio Bembo, later proposals involving Francesco Zavattari, and different hands for replacement cards. These disputes do not materially alter the Pope’s identification. The permanent catalog summarizes the attribution history and the division of the surviving deck between Morgan, Bergamo, and the Colleoni family ([Morgan manuscript catalog](https://www.themorgan.org/manuscript/158525)).

## Ferrarese material

### Charles VI / Este-associated cards, c. 1460s–1480s

The so-called Charles VI cards are generally placed in a Florentine or Ferrarese milieu depending on the scholar; “Este” has sometimes been used too broadly for several luxury fragments.

**Pope:** A securely attributable, universally accepted Pope from every “Este” grouping does not survive. Descriptions should therefore be pack-specific.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** Ferrarese documents prove production and play, but do not guarantee that every surviving luxury card formerly labeled “Este” originated at Ferrara.

### Sola Busca, 1491, northern Italy

**[Documented fact]** The Sola Busca is the earliest substantially complete tarot with fully illustrated pip cards. Its trumps bear pseudo-classical personal names rather than the standard Pope, Emperor, and theological sequence.

Trump V is not a papal Hierophant. Consequently the deck is relevant primarily because:

1. it proves that late-fifteenth-century tarot imagery was not monolithic;
2. its illustrated minors were studied by Pamela Colman Smith and supplied compositional precedents for some Waite-Smith pips.

The frequent statement that Smith “copied the Sola Busca” is too crude. **[Scholarly reconstruction]** She adapted several visible compositions after cards from the deck were exhibited at the British Museum in 1908; the Hierophant itself is not one of the strongest Sola Busca borrowings.

### Mantegna “Tarocchi,” c. 1460s–1470s

**[Documented fact]** These are engraved educational or cosmological series, not a tarot pack and not by Andrea Mantegna. They arrange social estates, Apollo and the Muses, liberal arts, virtues, and cosmic spheres.

They have no standard Pope/Higher Priest trump V. They matter as evidence for the same Renaissance habit of ordering human estates and cosmic powers that made tarot’s hierarchy intelligible. Calling them a direct source for the Hierophant is **[disputed]**.

## Printed sheets

### Rosenwald sheet, late fifteenth or early sixteenth century, probably Florence

The Rosenwald sheets preserve early woodcut tarot designs.

**[Documented fact]** They show that inexpensive printed packs circulated beside courtly painted ones.

**Pope:** The figure belongs to the low ruler group, but deterioration and fragmentary survival make minute iconographic assertions risky. The overall sequence is closer to the southern/Florentine tradition than to fully developed Marseille.

### Budapest sheets, late fifteenth or early sixteenth century

These fragmentary sheets preserve another early printed tradition, frequently connected with Ferrara or northeastern Italy.

Again, **[negative finding]** there is insufficient secure detail to use their Pope as proof of every later Marseille feature.

### Cary sheet, c. 1500, Milanese

The Cary sheet is the most important printed bridge toward Marseille iconography. Its Pope holds a curved crozier and is associated with small foreground figures. The attendant composition is thus old, but the exact date, workshop, and relation to French production remain reconstructed.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** The sheet supports descent of the Marseille family from a late-fifteenth-century Milanese model, while leaving room for substantial French reworking.

## Tarot de Marseille lineage

### Jean Noblet, Paris, c. 1650

**[Documented fact]** Trump V is titled *LE PAPE* and numbered V.

The Pope:

- faces slightly toward the viewer’s left;
- wears a papal tiara;
- raises his right hand in blessing;
- holds a curved crozier;
- towers above two tonsured or clerically dressed attendants;
- sits before what may be a throne-back rather than the two fully explicit pillars of later versions.

The attendants’ identity is not printed. They have been called monks, priests, acolytes, cardinals, disciples, or initiates. “Initiates” is mainly an occult reading; Dummett considered cardinals plausible from related imagery.

### Jean Dodal, Lyon, c. 1701–1715

Dodal preserves the same essential scheme:

- *LE PAPE*, V;
- triple papal crown;
- blessing hand;
- curved staff;
- two small clerics;
- clearer vertical supports behind the pontiff.

Compared with later Conver, the staff belongs to the older crozier-like form.

### Pierre Madenié, Dijon, 1709

Madenié is a crucial early representative of what is often called Marseille Type II. The Pope’s staff becomes more rigidly hierarchical, and the overall drawing anticipates later Conver-derived standardization.

### Nicolas Conver, Marseille, 1760/1761

Conver’s *LE PAPE* establishes the enormously influential Type II form:

- Roman numeral V;
- triple crown;
- two pillars or upright throne elements;
- triple-cross staff;
- right hand raised;
- two kneeling ministers, one with a tonsured head;
- a frontal, geometrically ordered hierarchy.

The triple cross clarifies threefold ecclesiastical authority. Occultists later read it as three worlds, three degrees of initiation, spirit–soul–body, or supernal triads. Those are **[later esoteric attributions]**, not printed explanations by Conver.

### B.-P. Grimaud / Paul Marteau, 1930

Marteau’s *Ancien Tarot de Marseille*, printed by Grimaud, standardized a Conver-derived palette and drawing for twentieth-century occult readers. It regularized rather than neutrally reproduced the eighteenth-century blocks.

**Change in meaning:** The pack’s primary twentieth-century audience increasingly treated the card as a symbolic arcanum rather than a game trump.

### Camoin–Jodorowsky, 1997

Philippe Camoin and Alejandro Jodorowsky advertised their deck as a “restoration” of the true Tarot de Marseille, synthesizing old fragments and Camoin printing-house material.

The card remains *LE PAPE*, V, with attendants and papal insignia. Colors and small marks are treated by Jodorowsky as an intentional symbolic grammar.

**[Disputed claim]** The claim to recover a single original or complete tarot cannot be demonstrated from the heterogeneous surviving evidence. Historically, Marseille was a family of changing patterns, not one lost master copy.

## Besançon pattern: Jupiter

### Earliest phase, Upper Rhine/Strasbourg–Switzerland, late seventeenth to eighteenth century

The name “Tarot de Besançon” is geographically misleading when applied to the pattern’s origin.

- **[Documented fact]** Juno replaces the Popess.
- **[Documented fact]** Jupiter replaces the Pope.
- Jupiter usually carries thunderbolts or a scepter and is accompanied by an eagle.
- The rest of the deck remains closely related to Marseille.

A pack attributed to Johann Mayer, around 1680, has been cited as an early example. Depaulis emphasizes that the transformation occurred in the Upper Rhine sphere and that Besançon later became a center of manufacture.

**Why renamed?** Older explanations simply blamed Protestant hostility to the papacy. Depaulis’s archival analysis complicates this: Strasbourg was Protestant, but manufacturers also served mixed Catholic and Protestant markets. Juno and Jupiter created an “ecumenical” or confessional-neutral pack. This is a **[scholarly reconstruction]**, not a surviving cardmaker’s declaration.

Joseph II’s Austrian reforms and anticlerical environments may have encouraged neutralized designs, but “Josephine decree ordered the Pope changed to Jupiter” could not be traced to a decisive primary order. It should be treated as **[disputed]** unless a specific law and pack are produced.

### Nineteenth-century Besançon production

Manufacture migrated or concentrated at Besançon, producing the later name. German sources call it *Besançon-Tarot*; Swiss descendants include Juno-and-Jupiter packs such as the modern 1JJ Swiss Tarot.

## Belgian pattern: Bacchus

### Liège, Rouen, Austrian Netherlands, late seventeenth–eighteenth centuries

In the Belgian pattern:

- Trump II becomes the comic Spanish Captain, *L’Espagnol—Capitano Eracasse*.
- Trump V becomes *BACUS*, Bacchus.
- Bacchus sits astride a wine barrel and carries wine, grapes, or a vine.
- Trump XVI becomes *La Foudre*.
- The Fool may be numbered XXII.

The International Playing-Card Society describes the pattern directly: “The Pope (V) is replaced by BACUS (Bacchus), seated astride a barrel” ([IPCS Belgian Tarot](https://i-p-c-s.org/pattern/ps-19.html)).

**Meaning:** The change secularizes and carnivalizes the clerical ruler. It may reflect confessional sensitivity, commercial adaptation, or inheritance from German-suited card imagery. No evidence makes Bacchus an original esoteric identity of the Pope.

## Minchiate

Minchiate expanded the trump series to forty trump-like cards, adding elements, zodiacal signs, and virtues.

The ordinary four tarot rulers were condensed or reconfigured among the lowest cards:

- *Papino* (“little Pope”);
- *Granduca* (“Grand Duke”);
- other low figures varying by period.

**[Documented fact]** Minchiate’s Pope-equivalent is not simply V and does not carry the Golden Dawn’s Vav–Taurus attribution. The presence of Taurus elsewhere among Minchiate’s literal zodiac cards is especially important: later occult Taurus correspondence must not be projected onto the *Papino*.

In play, the lowest five trumps had special collective roles and could contribute to *versicole* combinations. Exact scoring varied with local rules.

## Bolognese tarocchino

The Pope, Popess, Emperor, and Empress became the four *papi*:

- equal in rank;
- all above the Bagattino;
- all below the next higher trump;
- if several were played in one trick, the last played won among them.

Later censorship replaced or altered the *papi*. In the nineteenth century, Bolognese packs used Moorish or non-papal figures in their place.

**Meaning of the change:** Primarily political and confessional avoidance, followed by pattern conservatism—not an occult transformation.

## Sicilian tarot

The Sicilian pattern underwent radical changes:

- shortened pack;
- distinctive low trump *Miseria*;
- locally altered ruler and religious figures;
- different trump order and scoring.

The standard Marseille Pope V therefore does not map cleanly onto the Sicilian game. A simple statement that “the Sicilian Hierophant is worth five” would be misleading.

## Etteilla’s Grand Etteilla, 1789

Etteilla did not merely annotate the Marseille pack; he reordered and redesigned it as an “Egyptian” cosmogony.

The ordinary Pope is displaced by a priestly or hierophantic figure within a new numerical and cosmological scheme. Different Grand Etteilla editions vary, and modern reproductions sometimes remap Marseille identities inconsistently.

**[Negative finding]** A single, universally stable “Etteilla Hierophant = card N” could not be responsibly asserted across all editions without naming a particular printing. Lists that equate Grand Etteilla No. 1 with the Pope usually conflate Etteilla’s new order with older trump identities.

What is secure is that Etteilla attached separate upright and reversed legends to cards and made tarot explicitly divinatory and Egyptian in presentation.

## Oswald Wirth, 1889 and 1926

Wirth’s original twenty-two cards, commissioned or encouraged by Stanislas de Guaita, remain Marseille-based but add occult emblems.

*Le Pape* retains:

- tiara;
- blessing;
- staff;
- two kneeling followers.

Wirth adds Hebrew/Kabbalistic and initiatory framing derived from Éliphas Lévi and Papus. His revised 1926 version and *Le Tarot des imagiers du Moyen Âge* elaborate the design.

**Change in meaning:** The Pope becomes the human interpreter of sacred doctrine, mediating belief and gnosis. Wirth is transitional: the visibly Catholic pontiff remains, but his office is universalized.

## Golden Dawn drafts and reconstructions

### Original Golden Dawn manuscripts, c. 1888–1900

Members were expected to design and color their own cards. No single mass-produced “original Golden Dawn deck” existed.

The Hierophant description combines:

- enthroned initiator;
- Taurus/bull emblems;
- temple symbolism;
- Vau;
- the Order’s color scales;
- the title *Magus of the Eternal*.

### Robert Wang, *The Golden Dawn Tarot*, 1977

Wang’s deck, made in consultation with Israel Regardie, is a twentieth-century reconstruction. The Hierophant emphasizes the ceremonial-magical officer and astrological bull symbols. It is evidence for Wang and Regardie’s reconstruction, not a facsimile of one nineteenth-century master deck.

### Chic and Sandra Tabatha Cicero, 1990s onward

The Ciceros produced another reconstruction based on Order papers and color scales. Their card is more deliberately aligned with Golden Dawn ritual furniture and flashing-color theory.

Differences between Wang and Cicero show the underdetermination of the manuscript instructions: both are legitimate modern realizations, neither the unique lost original.

## Waite-Smith, 1909

Pamela Colman Smith drew a frontal enthroned pope-like Hierophant:

- red vestments;
- triple crown;
- triple-cross staff;
- right hand in blessing;
- two gray pillars;
- crossed gold and silver keys at his feet;
- two tonsured ministers seen from behind;
- contrasting rose and lily motifs on their garments.

Waite wrote:

> “He has been usually called the Pope, which is a particular application of the more general office that he symbolizes.”

He calls the card the power of outward religion, not occult revelation:

> “He is the ruling power of external religion, as the High Priestess is the prevailing genius of the esoteric, withdrawn power.”

And:

> “He is rather the *summa totius theologiæ*, when it has passed into the utmost rigidity of expression.”

These passages occur under “V. The Hierophant” in *The Pictorial Key to the Tarot* (London: William Rider, 1910/1911 online text), [Sacred Texts transcription](https://sacred-texts.com/book/the-pictorial-key-to-the-tarot/shell/v-the-hierophant).

### Sources of Smith’s composition

- **[Documented visual inheritance]** Marseille’s Pope with two clerics, blessing, tiara, and staff.
- **[Documented Christian iconography]** The crossed keys are the Petrine keys of binding and loosing, long-standing papal heraldry based on Matthew 16:19.
- **[Scholarly reconstruction]** Waite’s prose and Golden Dawn papers directed the occult program.
- **[Disputed]** Exact dependence on a particular painting or church interior has not been demonstrated.
- **[Negative finding]** No evidence was found that the Waite-Smith Hierophant was copied from the Sola Busca card corresponding to V.

The keys are Smith’s most consequential addition to the canonical modern image. They make authority visibly a matter of access: the institution possesses the means to open and shut.

## Paul Foster Case / BOTA

Case redrew Waite’s architecture in a cleaner symbolic style intended for hand coloring.

The BOTA Hierophant retains:

- crossed keys;
- two ministers;
- triple crown and cross;
- Vav;
- Taurus color, red-orange.

Case interprets the card as “inner hearing” or intuition. In *Tarot Fundamentals* he writes:

> “Hierophant, the esoteric title of Key 5, means ‘revealer of mysteries’ or, ‘he who shows that which is sacred.’”

He links the old title Pope to Simon Peter and hearing:

> “Simon (in Hebrew, Simeon) means ‘hearing.’”

This is **[esoteric wordplay]**, not historical etymology of the card. See Case, *Tarot Fundamentals*, Lesson Fourteen, [digitized text](https://www.scribd.com/document/339392168/Paul-Foster-Case-Tarot-Fundamentals-1936-pdf), and *The Tarot: A Key to the Wisdom of the Ages* (1947), [scan](https://iapsop.com/ssoc/1947__case___the_tarot.pdf).

## Thoth, 1938–1943; published 1969

Frieda Harris’s painting, directed by Aleister Crowley, radically transforms the Marseille/Waite pontiff.

Depicted elements include:

- an initiator seated on or fused with a bull;
- elephants surrounding the throne;
- four kerubic beasts at the corners;
- nine nail-like forms at the top, punning on Vau, “nail”;
- a transparent oriel;
- macrocosmic hexagram enclosing a pentagram;
- a dancing child within the pentagram;
- an armed woman before the Hierophant—the Scarlet Woman;
- serpent and dove;
- a five-petaled rose behind the phallic headdress;
- a three-ringed wand representing the Aeons of Isis, Osiris, and Horus;
- dark blue Nuit background.

Crowley explains:

> “This card is referred to the letter Vau, which means a Nail; of this instrument nine appear at the top of the card.”

And:

> “The card is referred to Taurus; therefore the Throne of the Hierophant is surrounded by elephants, which are of the nature of Taurus; and he is actually seated upon a bull.”

The woman is explicitly:

> “the Scarlet Woman in the hierarchy of the new Aeon.”

Crowley further says the central child symbolizes:

> “the law of the new Aeon of the Child Horus, which has supplanted that Aeon of the ‘Dying God’.”

Crowley, *The Book of Thoth* (London: O.T.O., 1944), “V. The Hierophant,” [Hermetic Library transcription](https://hermetic.com/crowley/book-of-thoth/atu).

### What changed and what it meant

The Catholic hierarch becomes an Aeonic initiator. Crowley retains priesthood, succession, and revelation but makes Christianity one decayed Bull-god religion among others. Taurus is no longer a marginal sigil; the entire composition is built from it.

Crowley’s Pasiphaë interpretation—linking bull religion, sexuality, initiation, and the Scarlet Woman—is Crowley’s Thelemic mythography, not a recoverable Renaissance meaning.

## Notable modern redesigns

### Motherpeace, Karen Vogel and Vicki Noble, late 1970s

The round feminist deck attacks patriarchal religious authority. Its Hierophant is a male figure appropriating or imitating female sacred power; the precise image has since drawn criticism for its representation of gender. It belongs to second-wave feminist spirituality, not to historical tarot reconstruction.

### Daughters of the Moon, Ffiona Morgan, 1980s

Patriarchal court and religious categories are replaced with feminist and Goddess-centered structures. Authority becomes communal or female rather than papal.

### Modern Witch Tarot, Lisa Sterle, 2019

The Waite-Smith composition is retained but recast with contemporary, diverse women and nonbinary presentation. The underlying geometry—teacher, followers, crossed keys—remains legible.

### Queer and secular redesigns

Many contemporary decks rename V:

- The Teacher;
- The Mentor;
- Tradition;
- The Elder;
- The Shaman;
- Faith;
- The Institution.

These are **[modern inventions]** responding to distrust of clerical authority. Some reverse the image entirely, making V represent dissent from dogma. Historically that negative “institutional oppression” emphasis is much younger than the Pope itself.

# The game

## Fundamental function

Tarot was created for trick-taking play. A standard historical pack has:

- four suits of fourteen cards;
- twenty-one permanent trumps;
- the Fool as a special excusing card.

The Pope is a permanent trump. It beats every suited card unless game rules provide an exceptional combination, and it loses to higher trumps.

The Morgan summarizes the historical pack as twenty-one trumps, the Fool, and fifty-six suited cards, and emphasizes that Renaissance tarot was a normal game rather than occult apparatus ([Morgan catalog](https://www.themorgan.org/manuscript/158525)).

## Trump-order families

Dummett identified three regional families.

| Order | Region | Pope’s local position | Defining features |
|---|---|---:|---|
| A, southern | Bologna, Florence; later Rome and Sicily | Top or near top of low ruler group; in Bologna one of equal *papi* | Angel above World; virtues grouped or arranged in southern fashion |
| B, eastern | Ferrara, probably Venice | Pope followed immediately by Temperance in the Steele-sermon order | Virtues dispersed; distinctive Ferrarese ordering |
| C, western | Milan, Piedmont, France | V in the numbered Marseille sequence | World above Angel; Marseille order becomes standard occult order |

Dummett’s explanation is historical rather than mystical: because early trumps were unnumbered, separate communities could preserve different learned orders. His translated discussion is available in [*Il Mondo e l’Angelo*, chapters 6–7](https://dummettsmondo.blogspot.com/2015/07/chapters-6-and-7-game-and-its-trump.html).

## Rank and point value

Rank and scoring value must be separated.

- **Rank:** Pope is normally the fifth trump in Marseille/Western order.
- **Card points:** In many later tarot games, only special high and low trumps carry counting value. An ordinary V has no special premium merely because it is V.
- **French tarot:** Modern French-suited packs no longer depict the Pope; trump 5 is an ordinary scenic trump worth the usual half-point when counted in a trick. Only 1, 21, and the Excuse are *oudlers*.
- **Tarock:** German and Austrian packs generally replaced Italian subjects with genre scenes. “V” survives as rank, not as a Pope identity.
- **Bolognese:** The Pope belongs to equal *papi*, so rank depends on play order among them.
- **Minchiate:** Pope-equivalent cards participate in special combinations and a forty-trump order; Marseille V values cannot be imported.
- **Sicilian tarot:** Local cards and scores differ substantially.

## Justice and Strength

The familiar occult controversy—Strength VIII versus XI and Justice XI versus VIII—comes from competing deck orders:

- Marseille: Justice VIII, Strength XI.
- Waite-Smith: Strength VIII, Justice XI.
- Thoth: Adjustment VIII, Lust XI.

Waite quietly exchanged the two, bringing Strength into the Golden Dawn’s Leo path sequence. Crowley retained that esoteric ordering but renamed the cards. This does not affect the Pope’s numerical V, yet it proves that a deck’s Roman numbers can encode a later occult system rather than an unbroken medieval order.

## The Fool

- **[Documented game tradition]** The Fool is normally an Excuse: it is played instead of following suit and is returned to its owner’s trick pile, subject to compensation rules.
- **[Scholarly reconstruction]** Whether this was its very earliest function cannot be proven.
- **[Modern convention]** Calling it trump 0 places it inside an occult cycle.
- **[Regional variant]** Number XXII in Belgian and some later packs reflects changed ranking or numbering practice, not the universal rule.

# Chronology of attestation

## c. 1418–1425: Marziano da Tortona

Marziano’s text describes a special pack made for Filippo Maria Visconti and painted by Michelino da Besozzo. It has sixteen classical gods in four conceptual suits.

**What it proves:** A northern Italian court used permanent-trump-like classical figures before standard tarot is documented.

**What it does not prove:** It is not the standard twenty-two-trump tarot. Although Jupiter and Bacchus appear, they are not evidence that the Pope was “originally” Jupiter or Bacchus.

## 1440: Giusto Giusti, Florence

A diary entry records a fine pack of *naibi a trionfi* made for Sigismondo Malatesta. This is now often treated as the earliest certain documentary reference to standard or near-standard triumph cards.

## February 10, 1442: Ferrara ledger

The Este wardrobe account records payment to Jacopo Sagramoro for coloring four packs of triumph cards. Helen Farley reproduces the sense of the entry: cups, swords, coins, batons, and “all the figures” of four packs were painted ([Farley, *A Cultural History of Tarot*](https://e-isomero.com/pdf/book/1611846398.pdf)).

## July 28, 1442: Ferrara

A further entry purchases packs of *carte da trionfi* for Ercole and Sigismondo d’Este.

These entries prove production, purchase, suits, and a special group of figures. They do not list the Pope individually.

## c. 1450–1460: surviving Visconti-Sforza Pope

The earliest surviving standard Pope image. The card itself is documentary evidence for iconography but bears neither name nor numeral.

## c. 1465–1475: sermon *De ludo cum aliis*

A Dominican sermon condemns dice, ordinary cards, and triumphs, then lists the trumps:

> *El bagatella, Imperatrix, Imperator, La papessa, El papa, La temperantia…*

Spellings vary among editions and transcriptions.

**[Documented fact]** This is the earliest surviving near-complete trump list and explicitly names *El papa*.

**Order B:** Pope follows the Popess and precedes Temperance, furnishing evidence for the eastern/Ferrarese order.

The sermon attacks the game because sacred and secular dignities are placed beside the Devil and used for gambling. It does not reveal a hidden initiation.

## c. 1491: Sola Busca

Dated complete illustrated pack demonstrating a nonstandard pseudo-classical trump cycle and illustrated minors.

## c. 1500: appropriati and tarot poems

Italian poems assign particular trumps to named persons, often aristocratic women, and use the figures allegorically. These show that literary appropriation began early.

They do not establish systematic cartomancy.

## 1505–1506: *tarocchi/taraux*

The word *tarocchi* appears in Ferrara and *taraux* in French records. Its etymology remains unresolved. Claims deriving it from Egyptian, Hebrew, or *rota/tora* are occult wordplay rather than accepted historical linguistics.

## 1520s: Teofilo Folengo

Folengo’s *Chaos del Triperuno* includes an episode in which sonnets are composed from combinations of tarot cards. This is literary and possibly fortune-like use of dealt cards, but not yet an established occult correspondence system.

## Sixteenth century: Bolognese appropriati and rules

Evidence accumulates for four equal-ranking *papi*, shortened tarocchino, and local combinations.

## Seventeenth century: French printing

Noblet, Viéville, and related packs establish named and numbered French trumps. The card is unambiguously *Le Pape*.

## c. 1680 onward: Juno/Jupiter

Upper Rhine producers replace Papess and Pope with Juno and Jupiter. Later attribution to Besançon reflects production history rather than birthplace.

## 1690s–eighteenth century: Belgian Bacchus

Liège/Belgian designs substitute the Spanish Captain and Bacchus.

## 1701–1715: Dodal

A canonical Marseille Type I Pope.

## 1760/1761: Conver

The most influential Marseille Type II Pope.

## 1781: Court de Gébelin and Comte de Mellet

Volume VIII of *Monde primitif* publishes:

1. Court de Gébelin’s Egyptian interpretation;
2. the Comte de Mellet’s separate essay on tarot divination and Hebrew letters.

Court de Gébelin calls V the High Priest or Hierophant and treats the supposed Pope as an Egyptian religious official disguised by later cardmakers.

**[Legend]** He claims the pack preserves Egyptian wisdom and survived through so-called “Gypsies.”

**[Esoteric attribution]** De Mellet aligns letters with trumps, but his sequence differs from the later Golden Dawn. It is therefore incorrect to attribute the Golden Dawn’s final Vav–Taurus package directly to Gébelin.

## 1780s–1791: Etteilla

Etteilla professionalizes cartomancy, publishes meanings and methods, organizes practitioners, and issues the Grand Etteilla.

His contribution is not merely “discovering” ancient meanings: he constructed a new divinatory and cosmogonic tarot.

## 1854–1856: Éliphas Lévi, *Dogme et Rituel de la haute magie*

Lévi makes tarot central to ceremonial magic and Kabbalah. He aligns the twenty-two trumps with Hebrew letters, but places the Fool between Judgment and World so that the alphabet begins with the Magician, not the Fool.

The Pope becomes the great hierophant or teacher of the mysteries.

Lévi’s celebrated formulation is:

> “An imprisoned person, with no other book than the Tarot, if he knew how to use it, could in a few years acquire universal knowledge.”

This is **[esoteric rhetoric]**, not a historical claim supported by a medieval manual.

## 1870: Paul Christian

In *Histoire de la magie*, Paul Christian popularizes “arcana,” Egyptian initiation chambers, and a narrative in which a candidate passes through tarot images. He elaborates an Egyptian temple drama not found in ancient Egyptian sources.

## 1888: Mathers

S. L. MacGregor Mathers publishes *The Tarot: Its Occult Signification, Use in Fortune-Telling, and Method of Play*. He calls V “The Hierophant or Pope.”

## 1889: Papus and Wirth

Papus’s *Le Tarot des Bohémiens* synthesizes Lévi, Christian, numerology, the Tetragrammaton, and the Romani myth. Wirth designs twenty-two occult trumps for Stanislas de Guaita.

## c. 1888–1900: Golden Dawn

*Book T* fixes Vau, Taurus, path 16, and *Magus of the Eternal*. This is the decisive source for Waite, Crowley, Case, and most modern English-language correspondence tables.

## 1909–1911: Waite-Smith and *Pictorial Key*

Smith supplies crossed keys and an iconic architectural composition. Waite defines the Hierophant as institutional, manifested religion.

## 1920s–1940s: Case/BOTA

Case makes V chiefly intuition or “interior hearing,” grounded in Taurus’s supposed government of neck, throat, ears, and voice.

## 1938–1943/1969: Thoth

Harris paints Crowley’s new-Aeon version. Commercial publication follows decades later.

## 1970s–present

Jungian, feminist, queer, secular, therapeutic, and anti-authoritarian readings proliferate. “Conformity,” now among the most familiar keywords, becomes both a neutral description of convention and a criticism of institutional control.

## Vav and Taurus: reconstructing the thread

### Hebrew letter

Vav is the sixth Hebrew letter, with numerical value 6, although the tarot card is V. The mismatch is a product of inserting the Fool as zero before the Magician:

- Fool = Aleph;
- Magician = Beth;
- High Priestess = Gimel;
- Empress = Daleth;
- Emperor = Heh;
- Hierophant = Vav.

This is Golden Dawn order, not Lévi’s.

Vav means hook, peg, or nail and serves grammatically as “and.” Occultists interpret it as conjunction, fastening, transmission, or a link between higher wisdom and institutional expression.

### Taurus

The Golden Dawn maps the twelve simple Hebrew letters to twelve zodiac signs. Under its adopted *Sefer Yetzirah* scheme, Vav receives Taurus.

Thus Taurus did not arise from observing a medieval pope and deciding he “looked Taurean.” It emerged from a chain:

Hebrew letter sequence → zodiacal letter attribution → tarot-letter alignment.

Later writers back-read bullish features into the Pope:

- stability;
- orthodoxy;
- embodiment;
- voice and throat;
- fixed tradition;
- fertility;
- Venusian religious beauty.

Crowley literalized this by putting the Hierophant on a bull and surrounding him with elephants.

### Path 16

In Golden Dawn diagrams, Vav/Hierophant joins:

- Chokmah, Wisdom;
- Chesed, Mercy.

The direction varies by author’s discussion, but the connection does not. It makes the Hierophant the channel through which primordial wisdom becomes benevolent order, law, teaching, or religious structure.

### Color scales

The standard four colors are:

- King scale: red-orange;
- Queen scale: deep indigo;
- Prince scale: deep warm olive;
- Princess scale: rich brown.

They are recorded in Golden Dawn/Crowley correspondence tables and summarized in modern Golden Dawn materials ([color comparison](https://numinouspress.substack.com/p/the-colors-of-taurus-the-hierophant)).

## Pips and courts: requested Book T context

The Hierophant is a trump, so it has no decan or court sub-element of its own. The relevant Taurus minors are:

| Taurus span | Minor card | Golden Dawn title | Planet |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0°–10° Taurus | Five of Pentacles/Disks | Lord of Material Trouble; Crowley: Worry | Mercury in Taurus |
| 10°–20° Taurus | Six of Pentacles/Disks | Lord of Material Success; Crowley: Success | Moon in Taurus |
| 20°–30° Taurus | Seven of Pentacles/Disks | Lord of Success Unfulfilled; Crowley: Failure | Saturn in Taurus |

Court cards govern thirty-degree spans crossing signs rather than one neat sign. Under the Golden Dawn’s overlapping court scheme, Taurus is principally covered by:

- Queen of Wands: 20° Pisces–20° Aries—therefore not Taurus;
- Prince of Disks: 20° Aries–20° Taurus;
- Queen of Swords: 20° Virgo–20° Libra, unrelated;
- Knight/King schemes vary in later deck terminology.

The important Taurus court is the Prince of Disks, whose range includes 20° Aries through 20° Taurus and whose sub-element is Air of Earth in Golden Dawn/Thoth terminology.

Terminology conflict is notorious:

- Golden Dawn Knight = Fire of a suit;
- Queen = Water;
- Prince = Air;
- Princess = Earth.
- Waite’s printed courts use King, Queen, Knight, Page, obscuring the direct equivalence.

## Tzaddi/Heh and Emperor/Star

Crowley’s “Tzaddi is not the Star” revision exchanges the Hebrew attributions associated with Emperor and Star:

- traditional Golden Dawn: Emperor = Heh/Aries; Star = Tzaddi/Aquarius;
- Crowley’s final doctrine: Emperor = Tzaddi but remains Aries; Star = Heh but remains Aquarius.

It does **not** move Vav or Taurus away from the Hierophant. The dispute matters because it shows Crowley considered the Golden Dawn alphabet corrigible while preserving V’s correspondence unchanged.

# Divinatory meanings by author

Short quotations are reproduced from the relevant editions; longer discussions are paraphrased to respect both context and copyright.

## Etteilla

Because Etteilla reordered the trumps, no single Grand Etteilla card is an exact visual-equivalent Pope across all editions.

His divinatory current associates priestly cards with:

- upright: enlightenment, instruction, law, religious authority, protection, marriage or sanctioned union;
- reversed: intolerance, hypocrisy, deceptive counsel, or dependence on authority.

**[Textual caution]** Modern lists frequently retro-convert Etteilla’s numbered cards into Marseille names. Without a specified 1789 printing, exact “Hierophant upright/reversed” wording cannot be securely quoted. The secure historical contribution is Etteilla’s practice of printing upright and inverted meanings directly on cards.

## Mathers, 1888

Mathers calls V “The Hierophant or Pope.”

His compact meanings are commonly transcribed as:

- upright: “Mercy, beneficence, kindness, goodness; inspiration; the man to whom the Querent has recourse.”
- reversed: “Society, good understanding, concord, over-kindness, weakness.”

Mathers’s booklet is available as [*The Tarot: Its Occult Signification…* (1888)](https://www.howardnema.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/12/the-tarot-1888-by-macgregor-mathers-1.pdf) and [Sacred Texts transcription](https://sacred-texts.com/tarot/mathers/mtar02.htm).

This vocabulary passes almost verbatim into Waite’s alternative meanings.

## Waite, 1910

Waite’s divinatory list reads:

> “Marriage, alliance, captivity, servitude; by another account, mercy and goodness; inspiration; the man to whom the Querent has recourse.”

Reversed:

> “Society, good understanding, concord, overkindness, weakness.”

Waite openly calls many received meanings “artificial and arbitrary.” The list appears in Part III, §3 of [*The Pictorial Key*](https://www.sacred-texts.com/tarot/pkt/pkt0303.htm).

The striking conflicts within the list—marriage versus captivity, mercy versus servitude—record accumulated cartomantic sources rather than one coherent doctrine.

## Crowley, 1944

Crowley’s short divinatory synopsis is:

> “Inspiration, stubbornness, strength, toil, endurance, organization, peace, goodness of heart, help from superiors, patience.”

He generally recommends elemental dignity rather than mechanical reversal. An ill-dignified Hierophant can therefore show obstinacy, dogma, oppressive organization, or misuse of superior authority.

Crowley’s longer account stresses initiation and:

> “the uniting of the microcosm with the macrocosm.”

See [*Book of Thoth*, Atu V](https://hermetic.com/crowley/book-of-thoth/atu).

## Paul Foster Case

Case’s central keyword is intuition, specifically “interior hearing.”

- upright: reception of inner teaching, right counsel, spiritual instruction, conformity to cosmic rather than merely social law;
- reversed or negatively expressed: credulity, false inward voices, dependence on dogma, distorted counsel.

Case did not make reversed card-reading as central as Waite-style manuals do. His primary concern was meditative symbolism.

## Rachel Pollack

In *Seventy-Eight Degrees of Wisdom* (first published in two parts, 1980 and 1983), Pollack makes the Hierophant the society’s organized doctrine in contrast to the High Priestess’s inward mystery.

- upright: education, tradition, social codes, religious training, entry into a system of belief; also the need to examine what one has been taught.
- reversed: unconventionality, rejection of received doctrine, openness, or—depending on context—immaturity and refusal to learn.

Pollack’s important innovation is not simply “conformity.” She treats the card as the cultural matrix by which private experience becomes communicable, while also exposing the cost of orthodoxy.

**[Quotation caution]** A lawful full-text edition was not located in the consulted web sources; no unverified verbatim passage is supplied.

## Mary K. Greer

In *The Complete Book of Tarot Reversals* (2002), Greer gives for the reversed Hierophant themes including:

> “questioning conventional mores, disobeying rules, doubting or flaunting moral principles, or rejecting your religious training.”

The available scan places the Hierophant/Pope discussion around p. 53 ([digitized copy](https://www.scribd.com/doc/271576647/Mary-k-Greer-Complete-Book-of-Tarot-Reversals)).

Greer’s broader method allows a reversal to be:

- blocked;
- excessive;
- internalized;
- projected;
- delayed;
- resisted;
- unconventional;
- misdirected.

Consequently Hierophant reversed need not mean simple rebellion. It can mean dogmatism in excess, instruction withheld, a teacher internalized, or a tradition undergoing revision.

# Esoteric and symbolic readings

## Christian

### Historical layer

The earliest card is explicitly papal:

- tiara;
- blessing;
- cross-staff;
- clerical sovereignty.

**[Documented fact]** The Pope belongs to a Christian social cosmos shared by the earliest Italian players.

### Petrine keys

The crossed keys become explicit in Waite-Smith, invoking Matthew 16:19 and papal heraldry:

- one key traditionally gold, heavenly authority;
- one silver, earthly or temporal jurisdiction;
- binding and loosing;
- admission and exclusion.

### Trinitarian symbolism

The triple crown and triple cross were later read as:

- Father, Son, Holy Spirit;
- Church militant, suffering, triumphant;
- body, soul, spirit;
- three worlds of occult philosophy.

Only the ecclesiastical insignia is historical; the full occult triads are **[later attributions]**.

## Kabbalistic

### First systematic appearance

Court de Gébelin’s associate de Mellet first made a published tarot–Hebrew-alphabet alignment in 1781. Lévi made the association influential in 1854–1856. The Golden Dawn regularized the now-standard Vav system in the late nineteenth century.

### Vav

Vav signifies conjunction:

- nail fastening doctrine to life;
- “and” joining clauses;
- path joining Wisdom to Mercy;
- teacher joining revelation to community.

These are **[esoteric interpretations]**, not medieval intentions.

### Tetragrammaton

Vav is the third letter of YHVH. Papus and later occultists treat it as the mediating or formative term in a fourfold cycle. This supplies another reason for viewing the Hierophant as transmitter.

## Astrological

The Taurus correspondence first becomes firmly standardized in Golden Dawn materials.

Positive Taurus expressions:

- stability;
- endurance;
- preservation;
- embodiment;
- patience;
- reliable form;
- cultivated tradition.

Negative expressions:

- inertia;
- stubbornness;
- possessive authority;
- institutional rigidity;
- resistance to revelation.

Venus rules Taurus, explaining priestly beauty, ritual, music, and concord. The Moon’s exaltation in Taurus supports memory, continuity, and reception. Crowley embodies both through the Scarlet Woman and nine lunar nails.

## Hermetic

The Hierophant is the *pontifex*, bridge-builder:

- above and below;
- macrocosm and microcosm;
- invisible doctrine and visible ritual;
- revelation and language;
- initiate and community.

Waite places him on the outer, manifested side of religion. Crowley makes him the active revealer of a new Aeon. Their readings conflict sharply:

- Waite: exoteric order, not lord of secret doctrine;
- Crowley: principal initiator whose mystery unites macrocosm and microcosm.

## Alchemical

No secure fifteenth-century evidence makes the Pope an alchemical emblem.

Alchemical readings appear through nineteenth- and twentieth-century occult synthesis:

- the two acolytes become sulfur and mercury, or fixed and volatile;
- the keys become *solve* and *coagula*;
- the triple staff becomes three alchemical principles;
- Taurus becomes fixed Earth;
- initiation becomes transmutation.

Wirth and later Hermetic writers spread such readings. They are **[esoteric attributions]**, not historical explanations of the Marseille woodblocks.

## Lévi’s Grand Hierophant

Lévi’s Hierophant is the adept capable of reading universal religion beneath sectarian forms. His tarot Pope mediates law, faith, and occult doctrine.

The term “Grand Hierophant” also belonged to nineteenth-century initiatory orders, making the card resemble a lodge officer. That institutional analogy was highly influential upon the Golden Dawn.

## Papus

Papus treats the Pope as a synthetic principle of divine law and inspiration within his ternaries and septenaries. His approach combines:

- Lévi’s letter order;
- Christian Kabbalah;
- the Tetragrammaton;
- purported Egyptian antiquity;
- the “Bohemian” or Romani transmission legend.

Papus’s *Tarot of the Bohemians* is available in the [1892 English edition scan](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:The_tarot_of_the_Bohemians_-_the_most_ancient_book_in_the_world_-_for_the_exclusive_use_of_initiates_%28IA_tarotofbohemians00papu_0%29.pdf).

## Jodorowsky and Marianne Costa

In *The Way of Tarot* (French original, 2004), the Pope is numerological V: a bridge between the stable square of IV and the openness of VI.

Themes include:

- teaching;
- communication;
- blessing;
- ideal or spiritual father;
- mediation;
- a guide turning toward the future;
- the danger of hypocrisy or abusive authority.

This is a Marseille-based therapeutic-symbolic system, not the Golden Dawn system. Jodorowsky generally rejects imposed correspondences such as Hebrew letters and astrology when they obscure the visible card.

## Jungian

### First appearance and spread

Jung did not author a complete tarot doctrine. Claims that “Jung said each Major Arcana is an archetype” usually lack a direct citation.

The Jungian tarot emerged through later interpreters, particularly in the mid-to-late twentieth century. Sallie Nichols’s *Jung and Tarot* (1980) was especially influential.

The Hierophant may function as:

- Wise Old Man;
- senex;
- collective religious function;
- custodian of cultural memory;
- persona of spiritual authority;
- shadow of dogmatism and gatekeeping;
- mediator between numinous experience and public symbol.

**[Modern psychological interpretation]** This can be illuminating without being treated as the card’s Renaissance “secret.”

# People and works

## Artists and printers

### Bonifacio Bembo and workshop

Traditionally associated with the Visconti-Sforza cards. The Pope’s gold-ground enthronement is one of the clearest surviving images of the early trump.

### Francesco Zavattari, Benedetto Bembo, Franco dei Russi

Proposed in attribution debates surrounding Visconti-family cards and later additions. None has displaced all alternatives.

### Jean Noblet

Parisian cardmaker of the earliest substantially preserved named Marseille-style pack, c. 1650.

### Jean Dodal

Lyon cardmaker, active around 1701–1715; preserves older crozier-like Pope.

### Nicolas Conver

Marseille cardmaker whose 1760/1761 pattern became a dominant visual source.

### Paul Marteau and Grimaud

Standardized the twentieth-century commercial “Ancien Tarot de Marseille.”

### Pamela Colman Smith

Created the most influential English-language Hierophant. Her authorship should not be submerged under Waite’s name: Waite supplied occult direction, but Smith solved the visual problem.

### Frieda Harris

Transformed Crowley’s instructions into the dense geometric Thoth painting. Her color, layering, and spatial synthesis are substantive authorship, not mechanical illustration.

### Philippe Camoin and Alejandro Jodorowsky

Produced a synthetic Marseille reconstruction in 1997 and promoted its details as an integrated symbolic language.

## Literature

### Teofilo Folengo

His sixteenth-century tarot sonnets prove early literary manipulation of dealt trumps. They are not evidence for ancient occult meanings.

### Italo Calvino, *The Castle of Crossed Destinies*

The first section appeared in 1969 using the Visconti-Sforza cards; the completed 1973 volume adds a Marseille-based tavern section. Mute travelers assemble cards into stories.

Calvino’s famous idea of tarot as a “machine for constructing stories” anticipates modern narrative reading. The Pope participates as a flexible narrative sign—cleric, law, authority, teacher—rather than a fixed divinatory keyword. [Work history](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Castle_of_Crossed_Destinies).

### T. S. Eliot, *The Waste Land* (1922)

Madame Sosostris uses a deliberately hybrid pseudo-tarot. Eliot names:

- the drowned Phoenician Sailor;
- Belladonna, Lady of the Rocks;
- the man with three staves;
- the Wheel;
- the one-eyed merchant;
- the Hanged Man.

The Hierophant/Pope does not appear.

**[Negative finding]** It is misleading to cite *The Waste Land* as a literary appearance of the Hierophant specifically. It is relevant to modernist tarot reception generally.

### W. B. Yeats

Yeats was deeply involved in the Golden Dawn and its ceremonial offices, including the Order’s Hierophant symbolism. However:

**[Negative finding]** No single canonical Yeats poem was located that unmistakably quotes Trump V’s image by name. His relevance is institutional and occult rather than a secure direct literary card citation.

## Film

### Alejandro Jodorowsky

Tarot imagery permeates Jodorowsky’s films and psychomagical practice, especially *The Holy Mountain* (1973), whose initiatory teacher figure and planetary adepts resemble an expanded tarot procession.

**[Interpretive claim]** Treating any single character as “the Hierophant card” requires scene-specific argument; Jodorowsky’s films recombine tarot roles rather than simply illustrating them.

### Ingmar Bergman

*The Seventh Seal* (1957) is often placed beside tarot because Death plays chess and medieval allegory becomes drama.

**[Negative finding]** No reliable evidence was located that Bergman quoted the Hierophant card specifically. The resemblance belongs to the broader *memento mori* and Dance of Death tradition.

## Painting, architecture, and fashion

### Papal enthronement art

The earliest Pope draws upon generic enthroned-pontiff iconography rather than one demonstrated painting. Proposed sources include papal seals, manuscript illuminations, ecclesiastical portraits, and images of Christ or bishops blessing.

**[Open attribution]** No single prototype is established.

### Church architecture in Waite-Smith

The paired pillars and sanctuary-like space evoke ecclesiastical and initiatory architecture. Exact identification with a named building is unsupported.

### Dior and Motherpeace

The Motherpeace deck influenced Christian Dior’s 2018 Resort collection following authorization from Vogel and Noble. This is a documented modern case of tarot imagery entering fashion, although the most reported garment used Death rather than the Hierophant ([Motherpeace history](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Motherpeace_Tarot)).

**[Negative finding]** No famous building securely documented as an intentional quotation of Trump V was found. Buildings described online as “Hierophant architecture” generally rely on retrospective resemblance.

# Controversies and disputes

## Egyptian origin

### Claim

Court de Gébelin asserted in 1781 that tarot was an Egyptian book whose meaning had survived unnoticed in a card game. He regarded the Pope as an Egyptian High Priest or Hierophant distorted by Christian cardmakers.

### Evidence offered

- visual analogy;
- speculative Egyptian etymologies;
- the idea that Egyptian priests encoded wisdom in pictures;
- Romani preservation.

### Historical assessment

**[Legend]** Gébelin wrote before Champollion’s decipherment of hieroglyphs and supplied no ancient deck, Egyptian text, transmission document, or correctly deciphered inscription.

Dummett observed that Gébelin and followers supplied alleged Egyptian phrases without hieroglyphic originals ([Dummett, “Origins of Tarot”](https://www.nybooks.com/articles/1981/05/14/origins-of-tarot/)).

The documentary sequence instead runs:

- European playing cards in the late fourteenth century;
- special triumph packs in northern Italy by 1440–1442;
- standard tarot imagery in fifteenth-century Italian objects;
- explicit occult interpretation only in 1781.

Egypt remains relevant indirectly because Mamluk cards contributed suit structures to European playing cards. That is not the same as an Egyptian Book of Thoth.

## Romani or “Gypsy” origin

### Claim

Romani people supposedly brought tarot from Egypt or India into Europe and preserved its divinatory art.

### Development

- Gébelin connected “Egyptians” and card preservation.
- Nineteenth-century authors including Boiteau d’Ambly, Jean-Alexandre Vaillant, Papus, and others popularized the story.
- Papus’s title *Tarot des Bohémiens* made it internationally durable.

### Assessment

**[Legend]** No fifteenth-century source connects Romani communities with the invention of *trionfi*. Later Romani fortune-tellers did use European cards, but adoption does not prove invention.

Waite himself wrote:

> “There is no particle of evidence for the Egyptian origin of Tarot cards.”

He also argued that evidence for specifically Romani tarot use appeared only much later ([*Illustrated Key*, history section](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Page:The_Illustrated_Key_to_the_Tarot.djvu/36)).

The modern claim that tarot is a closed Romani practice is therefore historically unsupported, while Romani participation in later European cartomancy remains a genuine cultural history deserving separate study.

## Cathar and Albigensian origin

### Claim

The Popess and Pope supposedly encode a secret Cathar church, Albigensian patriarch, dualist succession, or underground heresy.

### Sources and spread

Nineteenth- and twentieth-century occult and alternative histories linked tarot to Cathars because:

- the Popess looked doctrinally suspect;
- southern France was associated with Catharism;
- “Marseille” encouraged a Provençal origin story;
- tarot could be imagined as a secret pictorial scripture.

### Assessment

**[Disputed/unsupported]**

- No Cathar tarot survives.
- No Cathar text describes the twenty-two trumps.
- Earliest secure tarot documents point to fifteenth-century northern Italian courts.
- The Popess has plausible mainstream medieval sources: Pope Joan legend, personified Church, Faith, or satirical inversion.
- Marseille manufacture is later than the Italian evidence.

Waite mocked the notion of deriving a secret Albigensian patriarch from the Pope card in the historical introduction to *Pictorial Key* ([section](https://sacred-texts.com/book/the-pictorial-key-to-the-tarot/shell/section-1-introductory-and-general)).

## Templar origin

### Claim

The Pope/Hierophant supposedly preserves a Templar grand master or clandestine Templar initiation.

### Evidence

Usually:

- crossed keys;
- two initiates;
- alleged survival of Templar mysteries;
- numerical speculation;
- supposed links between Templars, Freemasonry, and tarot.

### Assessment

**[Legend]** The Templars were suppressed in 1312, more than a century before secure *trionfi* records. Crossed keys do not appear on the earliest Pope card and become canonical in Waite-Smith only in 1909. No transmission document bridges Templars and tarot.

## “Ancient Book of Thoth”

Etteilla and Crowley transformed Gébelin’s metaphor into deck identity. Crowley acknowledged corrupted historical packs but still imagined an underlying occult Book of Thoth.

**[Esoteric myth]** As a magical framework it is central to modern tarot; as a provenance claim it lacks documentary support.

## Was the card always Pope V?

No.

- Early cards were unnumbered.
- Regional orders differed.
- Bologna equalized the *papi*.
- Minchiate secularized and redistributed them.
- Besançon made V Jupiter.
- Belgian packs made V Bacchus.
- Etteilla reordered the entire sequence.
- French- and German-suited gaming tarots abandoned the Pope picture.
- Occult decks renamed him Hierophant, High Priest, Teacher, or Elder.

Therefore “The Hierophant has always been card 5” is true only at the level of the later Marseille/occult convention.

## Identity of the attendants

Proposals include:

- cardinals;
- monks;
- priests;
- acolytes;
- ordinands;
- initiates;
- representatives of active and contemplative life;
- clergy and laity;
- conscious and subconscious minds.

Only their clerical/subordinate appearance is visually secure. The occult pairings are later interpretations.

## Blessing or esoteric hand sign?

The hand resembles a conventional Christian blessing gesture. Waite calls it:

> “the well-known ecclesiastical sign which is called that of esotericism, distinguishing between the manifest and concealed part of doctrine.”

**Assessment:** The ecclesiastical origin is documented iconography; the precise meaning “two visible, two concealed” is Waite’s occult elaboration.

## Misprints that became traditions

No Hierophant-specific printer’s error comparable to a securely documented transformed title could be established.

Possible candidates often cited online are weaker:

- variations in the number of fingers are usually block damage or stylization;
- staff changes from crozier to triple cross reflect pattern development, not an identified accidental misprint;
- “Bacus” is historical orthography, not proof of an erroneous identity;
- the Belgian Fool’s XXII is deliberate numbering;
- confusing the attendants with pillars or vice versa arises from worn blocks and later copying.

**[Negative finding]** A demonstrable accidental Hierophant misprint that became a major occult doctrine was not found.

## Jodorowsky’s “restoration”

Jodorowsky and Camoin describe recovering details from surviving packs and printing archives.

**Documented:** Their deck is a deliberate comparative synthesis.

**Disputed:** That it reproduces the one authentic original tarot. Variability in early packs, regional orders, and iconography argues against a single fully recoverable Marseille archetype.

## Was Waite hostile to the Church?

Waite’s text is ambivalent, not simply anticlerical. He calls the Hierophant:

- rigid theology;
- external religion;
- recognized hierarchy;
- channel of institutional grace;
- possible forgetter of what his office signifies.

Thus modern readings of V as exclusively “toxic conformity” exaggerate one side of Waite. Conversely, treating it as an unquestionable endorsement of authority suppresses his warning that the pontiff may confuse office with ultimate truth.

## Dummett and the “game-only” correction

Dummett’s central correction is not that images had no meaning. Renaissance trumps visibly drew on social, moral, Christian, and allegorical meaning. His narrower claim is that:

- tarot originated as a game;
- no evidence supports an original occult code;
- systematic occult tarot begins in late-eighteenth-century France.

Critics sometimes caricature this as refusing symbolism altogether. The actual historical distinction is between ordinary cultural iconography and a designed occult correspondence system.

## Place, Farley, Kaplan, Decker, Depaulis

These scholars differ in emphasis:

- Dummett foregrounds rules, regional orders, and documentary chronology.
- Depaulis adds printing history, trade, and French/German regional evidence.
- Decker traces occult thinkers closely through their publications.
- Kaplan catalogs decks and images on a vast scale.
- Place gives greater attention to Renaissance Hermetic and emblematic culture but does not thereby prove a secret tarot school.
- Farley emphasizes the cultural construction of occult tarot and its modern social uses.
- Vitali explores visual and theatrical sources in Renaissance Italy.

A responsible history can accept rich Renaissance symbolism without accepting Egyptian, Templar, or Cathar provenance.

# Open questions

1. **Exact birthplace of standard tarot.** Milan, Ferrara, Bologna, and Florence all have important early evidence. Documentary priority and invention are not identical.

2. **Exact date of the Visconti-Sforza Pope.** Current catalog dates range from approximately 1450 to the late 1450s, with older broader ranges reaching 1480.

3. **Artist attribution.** Bembo/workshop remains conventional, but Zavattari and other hands have been proposed.

4. **Original ordering of the low rulers.** The three regional orders are early, but none can be proved the unique first order.

5. **When two attendants entered the image.** They are secure in printed ancestry leading toward Marseille, but not on the surviving Visconti-Sforza Pope. Fragmentary sheets make the exact transition difficult to date.

6. **Attendants’ intended identity.** “Cardinals” is plausible; “initiates” is later occult language. No surviving early caption settles it.

7. **Reason for Jupiter/Juno substitution.** Confessional neutrality and commercial adaptation are persuasive; a single initiating decree has not been found.

8. **Reason for Belgian Bacchus.** Confessional avoidance, humor, German-suited borrowing, and local pattern inheritance may all have contributed.

9. **Precise source of Smith’s crossed keys.** Papal heraldry is obvious, but no specific visual model is documented.

10. **Whether Waite specified every Smith detail.** His text explains the program, but Smith’s visual agency and sources remain incompletely documented.

11. **A stable Etteilla equivalent.** Because editions reorder, retitle, and redraw cards, correspondence must be established separately for each printing.

12. **Earliest explicit Taurus assignment.** The secure standardized source is Golden Dawn material. A comparably explicit earlier printed assignment to Pope V has not been located.

13. **Buildings intentionally quoting V.** No securely documented architectural quotation was found.

14. **Bergman, Eliot, and Yeats.** All belong to tarot-adjacent cultural histories, but direct Hierophant quotations are unproved or absent.

15. **“Misprint became doctrine.”** No sufficiently documented Hierophant example was found; most claims confuse woodblock variation with error.
