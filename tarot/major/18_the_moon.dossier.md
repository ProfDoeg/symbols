# The Moon: Research Dossier

## Method and evidentiary labels

This dossier distinguishes:

- **[Documented fact]** directly attested by a surviving card, archival record, printed rule, or named text.
- **[Scholarly reconstruction]** an inference advanced by historians from incomplete objects or records.
- **[Tradition]** a customary identification or interpretation whose historical origin may be uncertain.
- **[Esoteric attribution]** a deliberately constructed occult correspondence.
- **[Disputed]** a claim for which serious alternatives exist.
- **[Legend]** a historical narrative unsupported or contradicted by the surviving evidence.
- **[Modern invention]** an interpretation or design first demonstrable in modern tarot culture.

Quoted wording follows the editions linked in the Sources. Page numbers can differ among scans and reprints; PDF page and printed-page references are distinguished where possible.

**[Documented fact]** The card’s Renaissance subject was simply the Moon, one of the celestial trumps near the top of the sequence. It was not originally assigned to Pisces, Qoph, dreams, the unconscious, “shadow work,” or divination.

**[Esoteric attribution]** Qoph–Pisces–Path 29 belongs to the Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn’s late nineteenth-century synthesis. It was inherited by Waite, Crowley and Paul Foster Case.

### Names, numbering, and regional terminology

The Moon itself underwent fewer renamings than controversial religious cards:

- Italian gaming sources use **Luna**.
- French packs use **La Lune**.
- Spanish occult and commercial editions use **La Luna**.
- German-language occult decks use **Der Mond**; in Central European **Tarock**, however, pictorial trumps were often replaced by genre scenes and Roman numerals, so trump XVIII need not portray the Moon.
- English occultists standardized **The Moon**.
- Paul Christian called Arcanum XVIII **Le Crépuscule/Twilight**, retaining “Moon” as its ordinary identity.
- Etteilla’s rearranged system does not preserve “Marseille XVIII = Moon” as a simple equivalence. His specially designed pack reallocates subjects, numbers, elements and divinatory labels.

Related regional substitutions—important to the pack’s history but not renamings of the Moon—include:

- **[Documented fact]** In Besançon-pattern tarots, approximately the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, the Papess and Pope were replaced by **Juno** and **Jupiter**, probably to avoid objectionable papal imagery.
- **[Documented fact]** Bolognese packs suppressed or equalized the four imperial/papal trumps after political controversy; they became four **Mori/Moors**.
- **[Documented fact]** French **La Maison Dieu** is the longstanding title of trump XVI; “The Tower” is the usual English occult title.
- **[Documented fact]** The Fool was traditionally unnumbered because its special gaming role was outside the ordinary trump hierarchy. Zero is an occult/modern classificatory convention, although some gaming packs label it XXII.
- **[Documented fact]** Sicilian tarot renumbered the celestial sequence: Star XVI, Moon XVII, Sun XVIII, World XIX.
- **[Documented fact]** Minchiate expanded the trump series to forty numbered trumps. Its lunar subject is part of the five *Arie*, but does not occupy Marseille XVIII.

# Iconography deck by deck

## Fifteenth-century beginnings

### Marziano da Tortona and Michelino da Besozzo, Milan, approximately 1412–1425

**[Documented fact]** Marziano’s surviving Latin description, written for Filippo Maria Visconti, describes sixteen classical gods arranged as superior cards. The physical pack painted by Michelino is lost.

**[Important negative finding]** This is not the standard twenty-one-trump tarot, and it contains no standard card called La Luna. Diana appears among Marziano’s deities, but equating Marziano’s Diana directly with the later Moon trump is a **[scholarly speculation]**, not an attested genealogy.

### Visconti di Modrone / Cary–Yale pack, Milan, approximately 1440s

These names designate the same surviving luxury group, now principally at Yale.

**[Documented fact]** The surviving pack includes unusual male and female court figures and theological virtues. Its surviving trumps do not include a Moon card.

**[Open reconstruction]** It cannot be established whether its original structure contained a Moon that was lost, or whether its expanded sequence differed more radically from the later standard.

### Brambilla pack, Milan, before 1447

**[Documented fact]** This pack, now in the Pinacoteca di Brera, was probably made for Filippo Maria Visconti and is associated with Bonifacio Bembo’s workshop. Only two trumps survive.

**[Negative finding]** No Moon survives; descriptions of a “Brambilla Moon” are therefore reconstructions or confusion with the later Visconti-Sforza card.

### Pierpont Morgan–Bergamo Visconti-Sforza, approximately 1450–1460

**[Documented fact]** The surviving Moon is a hand-painted, gold-ground card conventionally attributed to Bonifacio Bembo’s workshop. It shows a richly dressed young woman standing in a landscape and raising a crescent Moon in her hand. A cord or ribbon descends from her other hand.

This is profoundly different from Marseille:

- no towers;
- no dogs;
- no pool;
- no crustacean;
- no road;
- no falling drops;
- no printed name or numeral.

The maiden parallels the female figure carrying a star in the same pack. Andrea Vitali describes this as a standard way of personifying or presenting the celestial body.

#### Is she Diana?

- **[Tradition]** The woman is frequently called Diana because Diana/Artemis is a lunar goddess and is commonly shown with a crescent.
- **[Disputed]** The card lacks Diana’s secure identifying attributes: bow, quiver, hunting dress or deer. Some modern accounts claim she has an “unstrung bow”; close inspection and museum descriptions do not establish that conclusively.
- **[Scholarly caution]** “A personification of Luna” is safer than “Diana.” The Diana identification is plausible iconographic interpretation, not a surviving title.

### Este/Ferrarese fragments, later fifteenth century

The Charles VI, Catania, Rothschild and related Este-style cards preserve Ferrarese imagery but do not supply an uncontested Moon equivalent in every group.

**[Documented fact]** Several Ferrarese traditions transformed the celestial cards into human activities conducted under celestial bodies. Surviving early printed examples include moonlit scenes rather than the Visconti court maiden.

**[Disputed reconstruction]** Individual fragments have been variously grouped and dated. “Este tarot” is not one securely intact deck.

### Rosenwald sheet, probably Florence, late fifteenth or early sixteenth century

**[Documented fact]** The uncut woodcut sheets preserve an early popular, non-luxury tarot pattern. Their celestial imagery is much closer to later printed traditions than Bembo’s gilded court cards.

**[Scholarly importance]** Such sheets demonstrate that the hand-painted Visconti cards are not a complete linear prototype for every subsequent form. Cheap printed cards, overwhelmingly less likely to survive, may have carried alternative designs from an early date.

### Budapest sheets, approximately 1500

**[Documented fact]** These fragmentary woodcut sheets preserve northern Italian tarot subjects and are central evidence for the transition from court painting to repeated print patterns.

**[Caution]** Their reconstruction into complete decks, and identification of damaged celestial cards, remain partly conjectural. Claims that they already contain the fully developed Marseille Moon should not be treated as settled without reference to the particular fragment.

### “Mantegna Tarocchi,” approximately 1465–1475

**[Documented fact]** These fifty educational engravings are neither by Mantegna nor a playable tarot pack. They arrange social estates, Apollo and the Muses, liberal arts, cosmic principles and celestial spheres.

The series includes **Luna** as a cosmic sphere. Its lunar iconography provides Renaissance context but is not direct evidence for the gaming trump’s original design.

### Sola Busca, Venice/Ferrara milieu, 1491

**[Documented fact]** The Sola Busca is the earliest substantially complete tarot whose pip cards are fully illustrated with narrative scenes.

**[Relevance limit]** Its trumps are named pseudo-classical warriors rather than the standard Moon, Star and Sun subjects. It is highly relevant to the eventual possibility of illustrated minors and probably influenced Pamela Colman Smith’s pips, but it is not a direct visual source for her Moon trump.

## Early printed French and Italian patterns

### Tarot de Paris, early seventeenth century

**[Documented fact]** Its Moon belongs to a non-Marseille French lineage and presents a human episode under the lunar body rather than the settled pool–dogs–crayfish composition.

### Jacques Viéville, Paris, approximately 1650

**[Documented fact]** Viéville’s pack is contemporary with Noblet but iconographically independent. Its celestial subjects retain alternative human and astrological scenes.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** Dummett associated Viéville-type cards with an older Savoy–Piedmont–Lombardy family. The exact route from northern Italy to Paris is not documented.

### Jean Noblet, Paris, approximately 1650–1659

This is the earliest surviving pack generally classified as Tarot de Marseille Type I.

**[Documented fact]** La Lune XVIII shows:

- a frontal lunar face surrounded by rays;
- colored droplets between Moon and earth;
- two crenellated towers;
- two canid animals facing or baying upward;
- water in the foreground;
- a crustacean in the water.

The card establishes essentially all the famous Marseille furniture. It does not yet turn these elements into Waite’s continuous initiatory road.

### Jean Dodal, Lyon, approximately 1701–1715

**[Documented fact]** Dodal preserves the Type I frontal Moon, towers, animals, drops and crustacean. Lettering and carving are rougher and more animated than later Conver-style designs.

### Pierre Madenié, Dijon, 1709

**[Documented fact]** This is the earliest dated surviving Tarot de Marseille Type II pack. In Type II, the Moon is characteristically shown in profile rather than frontally.

### Nicolas Conver, Marseille, 1760

**[Documented fact]** Conver’s Type II La Lune XVIII became the most influential historic model:

- a profile face occupies the crescent or lunar disk;
- alternating rays surround it;
- colored tear-, flame-, or Yod-like drops fill the air;
- two differently colored animals turn upward;
- twin towers stand behind them;
- a blue pool fills the foreground;
- a red or flesh-colored crayfish rises in it.

#### Do the drops fall or rise?

- **[Tradition]** Occultists from Gébelin onward called them rain, dew, tears, blood or descending influence.
- **[Disputed visual reading]** On several historic cards the pointed ends face upward, making them look as though moisture is being drawn toward the Moon.
- **[Conclusion]** Direction and substance are not textually identified by the makers. “Fertilizing dew” is Waite’s interpretation; “tears of Isis” is Gébelin’s Egyptian interpretation; “blood” belongs to Lévi/Papus’s occult recoding.

### Grimaud / Paul Marteau, Paris, 1930

**[Documented fact]** Marteau created the *Ancien Tarot de Marseille* for Grimaud from the later Conver manufacturing stream, regularizing lines and imposing a consistent modern color system.

**[Misleading commercial tradition]** “Ancien” does not mean that the printed deck itself is ancient. Its extraordinary circulation helped make the Marseille pattern appear more fixed and timeless than it was.

### Camoin–Jodorowsky, 1997

**[Documented fact]** Philippe Camoin and Alejandro Jodorowsky produced a reconstructed Marseille deck assembled from historic exemplars and Camoin manufacturing material.

**[Disputed claim]** Its promotion sometimes describes the result as restoration of a lost authentic code or exact original colors. Because no unique archetypal master deck survives, this is an interpretive reconstruction, not a recoverable facsimile.

For the Moon, they retain the Conver-family structure while sharpening details and colors. Jodorowsky reads the crustacean as deep receptive consciousness and the towers/animals as polarized forces around maternal, psychic and imaginal life.

## Regional game patterns

### Minchiate, Florence, sixteenth–nineteenth centuries

**[Documented fact]** Minchiate expands the pack to ninety-seven cards: forty trumps, fifty-six suit cards and the Fool.

Its Moon belongs to the five highest celestial *Arie*. Instead of Marseille’s baying dogs and crayfish, traditional Minchiate commonly depicts an astronomer or astrologer observing the Moon. The design emphasizes celestial observation, not a nocturnal bestiary.

### Tarocchino Bolognese

**[Documented fact]** The celestial sequence near the top is Angel, World, Sun, Moon. In modern Ottocento ordering, the Moon is therefore the fourth-highest ranked trump.

Traditional Bolognese cards are often double-headed and heavily conventionalized. The Moon remains identifiable by its celestial emblem rather than through Marseille’s full landscape.

### Sicilian Tarocchi

**[Documented fact]** The reduced Sicilian series ranks Star XVI, Moon XVII, Sun XVIII, World XIX, followed by the unnumbered/unnatural Fool role; a special lowest trump, *Miseria*, also exists.

The Sicilian Moon is thus XVII, not Marseille XVIII. Its appearance derives from an Italian regional game tradition rather than Golden Dawn symbolism.

## Occult and modern decks

### Etteilla’s *Grand Etteilla*, Paris, approximately 1788–1789

**[Documented fact]** Jean-Baptiste Alliette—Etteilla—created the first published tarot designed expressly for occult divination. It radically reordered the cards and printed upright and reversed meanings directly on them.

**[Critical distinction]** Grand Etteilla’s card numbers do not correspond one-for-one with Marseille trump numbers. References to “Etteilla’s Moon XVIII” often mistakenly impose Marseille numbering on a different architecture.

Etteilla treated the traditional celestial imagery as fragments of an Egyptian creation book. His pack distributes creation, celestial bodies, elements and moral conditions across a new sequence. Consequently, comparison should be made by subject and keyword, not merely by numeral.

### Oswald Wirth, Paris, 1889

Created under the direction of Stanislas de Guaita, Wirth’s first tarot consisted of twenty-two trumps.

**[Documented fact]** Wirth preserves the Marseille Moon—lunar face, droplets, towers, canids and crustacean—but adds occultly purposeful coloring and Hebrew-letter apparatus.

**[Esoteric attribution]** Wirth initially followed the French occult sequence derived from Lévi/Papus, in which the Fool’s placement causes the Moon to receive **Tsade/Tzaddi** and Aquarius, rather than the Golden Dawn’s Qoph/Pisces.

This is a major conflict often erased by modern correspondence tables.

### Golden Dawn manuscripts, approximately 1888–1900

**[Documented fact]** *Book T* assigns The Moon:

- Qoph;
- Pisces;
- Path 29, Netzach–Malkuth;
- “Ruler of Flux and Reflux; Child of the Sons of the Mighty” in the extended esoteric title tradition;
- a design derived from Marseille but interpreted as an initiatory passage through reflected light, instinct and illusion.

The original order’s designs survive in manuscripts and descriptions, not in one publicly issued canonical deck.

#### Reconstructions

- **Robert Wang, *The Golden Dawn Tarot* (1978):** a modern reconstruction based on Israel Regardie’s published materials and Golden Dawn symbolism.
- **Chic and Sandra Tabatha Cicero, *Golden Dawn Magical Tarot* (2000):** another reconstruction, with more systematic use of color scales and ritual symbolism.

Both are **[modern reconstructions]**, not facsimiles of a single 1890 “Golden Dawn deck.”

### Rider–Waite–Smith, London, 1909

Designed by A. E. Waite and drawn by Pamela Colman Smith.

**[Documented fact]** Smith retains the Marseille ensemble but turns it into a continuous landscape:

- a combined full disk and crescent-faced Moon;
- sixteen major and sixteen minor rays;
- fifteen visible Yod-like drops;
- a domestic dog and wild wolf;
- a crayfish emerging from a pool;
- two tall towers;
- a long yellow path passing between the animals and towers into distant hills.

The crucial compositional innovation is the road. Marseille provides the objects; Smith makes them stages along a journey from water and animal instinct toward an unknown horizon.

**[Likely source synthesis]**

- Marseille/Conver tradition: overall furniture.
- Golden Dawn teaching: Qoph, Pisces, reflected light, fear and threshold.
- Éliphas Lévi and Papus: the route between towers and the language of blood/reflection.
- Sola Busca: important source for Smith’s illustrated minors, but no demonstrated direct source for this trump.

Waite’s accompanying explanation says the card signifies “life of the imagination apart from life of the spirit.” The dog and wolf are “the fears of the natural mind,” while reflected lunar light cannot disclose the final mystery.

### Paul Foster Case / BOTA, United States, 1920s–1930s

Designed by Case and drawn in its familiar form by Jessie Burns Parke.

**[Documented fact]** BOTA follows the RWS/Golden Dawn structure but supplies uncolored line art for the student to color according to occult instructions. It regularizes:

- eighteen Yod-like drops;
- Qoph and Pisces;
- violet-red;
- the pool, crustacean, dog, wolf and path;
- cultivated land and distant heights.

Case calls the associated mode **Corporeal Intelligence** or body consciousness. The crustacean represents primitive instinct; dog and wolf are modified and wild animal life; the path is evolutionary return and balanced organization.

### Crowley–Harris Thoth Tarot, painted 1938–1943; first complete publication 1969

Designed by Aleister Crowley and painted by Lady Frieda Harris.

**[Documented fact]** The card is titled **The Moon**, Atu XVIII, Qoph/Pisces. Its imagery transforms Marseille/RWS into an explicitly Thelemic-Egyptian nightmare:

- a waning or composite Moon dominates the top;
- paired black towers or pylons flank the way;
- twin jackal-headed forms are identified by Crowley as double Anubis;
- the path or stream is tinged like blood or serum;
- nine Yod-shaped drops of “impure blood” descend;
- beneath the water, the sacred beetle **Khephra/Khepri** carries the solar disk;
- the landscape is barren and threshold-like.

Crowley writes that Pisces is winter’s last stage and a “Gateway of Resurrection.” The card is midnight, witchcraft, poisoned darkness and the crisis immediately before rebirth. Khephra does not literally carry the Moon: he bears the hidden **Sun** through night.

#### “Anubis twins”

**[Verified esoteric attribution]** Crowley explicitly identifies the doubled jackal guardian as Anubis “in double form between the Ways.” They transform the Marseille dogs into Egyptian threshold deities.

#### “Darkest hour”

**[Documented modern occult wording]** Crowley’s divinatory summary includes crisis and “the darkest hour before the dawn.” This is the clearest textual source for the now-common “dark night” Moon reading. It is not a medieval meaning.

### Notable modern redesigns

- **Thoth-derived occult decks:** emphasize nightmare, astral danger, initiation, Pisces and rebirth.
- **Jodorowsky–Camoin:** returns to Marseille while interpreting its anatomy psychologically and spiritually.
- **Motherpeace Tarot** (Karen Vogel and Vicki Noble, late 1970s): reframes lunar imagery through feminist spirituality and cyclical embodied consciousness.
- **DruidCraft and pagan decks:** frequently identify the Moon with goddess religion, seasonal cycles and intuition. These are modern religious adaptations.
- **Wild Unknown Tarot** (Kim Krans, 2012): reduces the scene to animal and lunar polarity, participating in minimalist psychological tarot.
- **Queer, decolonial and culturally specific decks:** often replace towers and canids with liminal experiences relevant to identity, uncertainty and hidden knowledge.

No single modern redesign can be called the culmination of the historic image; most descend either from Marseille, RWS or Thoth.

# The game

## Function

**[Documented fact]** Tarot originated as a trick-taking game. The Moon was a permanent trump capable of beating ordinary suit cards, subject to the rules of the local game.

In most games:

- players must follow suit when able;
- otherwise they may have to trump;
- higher trumps beat lower trumps;
- the Fool has a special excusing or highest-trump function depending on the game;
- scoring value and trick-taking rank are separate.

## Rank and card value

| Tradition | Rank of Moon | Counting value |
|---|---:|---:|
| Marseille-order/French tarot pack | XVIII, below XIX–XXI | In modern French tarot, an ordinary trump: normally ½ point, unlike I, XXI and Excuse |
| Bolognese Ottocento | Fourth-highest: below Sun, World, Angel | Important in combinations; local scoring differs from French tarot |
| Sicilian Tarocchi | XVII | Regional scoring; not one of French tarot’s three *oudlers* |
| Minchiate | One of five top *Arie*; conventional number differs from Marseille | High celestial trumps score and form *versicole* |
| Austrian/Hungarian Tarock | Card XVIII by numeral; usually no Moon image | Ordinarily an intermediate tarock; specific bonuses depend on game |
| Modern French Tarot Nouveau | XVIII by numeral, genre scene rather than Moon | ½ point as an ordinary trump |

**[Misprint-derived tradition]** In German-language Tarock, trump XXI is called **Mond** in some traditions. This arose from confusion of Italian *Mondo* or French *Monde* (“World”) with German *Mond* (“Moon”). It does not mean historic trump XXI was the Moon.

## Three regional trump orders

Dummett classified early orders as A, B and C.

- **Order A:** Bologna/Florence. Angel above World; celestial order differs from northern traditions.
- **Order B:** Ferrara. Characteristic placement of Justice and the celestial trumps.
- **Order C:** Milan and the line ancestral to French tarot; World above Judgment/Angel, followed by Sun, Moon and Star in descending play rank.

The labels describe relative orders recovered from poems, rules and packs, not three original printed numbering systems. Early cards were often unnumbered.

### Justice and Strength

**[Documented regional variation]** Justice and Fortitude/Strength changed relative positions among Italian orders. Marseille eventually prints Justice VIII and Strength XI.

**[Esoteric redesign]** Waite swaps them: Strength VIII, Justice XI, principally to preserve Golden Dawn zodiacal sequencing—Leo before Libra—while retaining the Hebrew-letter series.

### The Fool

- In many Italian and French games, the Fool is an **Excuse**: it does not win the trick but can ordinarily be reclaimed by its player in exchange for another card.
- In Central European Tarock, the Fool/Sküs often functions as the highest trump, effectively XXII.
- In Bolognese play, the Matto has no ordinary rank but is valuable in combinations.
- Calling it “trump 0” describes occult deck organization, not its original game rank.

# Chronology of attestation

### Approximately 1412–1425 — Marziano da Tortona

**[Documented fact]** Marziano describes a commissioned game of sixteen classical gods for Filippo Maria Visconti. Diana is present, but no standard Moon trump is named.

### 1440 — Florence

**[Documented fact]** A notarial record discovered by Thierry Depaulis records two packs of *naibi a trionfi* supplied by Giusto Giusti to Sigismondo Pandolfo Malatesta. This currently precedes the famous Ferrara records.

### 1442 — Ferrara

**[Documented fact]** Este accounts pay Jacopo Sagramoro for painting four packs of *carte da trionfi* and record another purchased pack for Ercole and Sigismondo d’Este.

**[Negative finding]** These entries do not list individual trumps; they do not name the Moon.

### 1440s–1460s — Milan

**[Documented fact]** Luxury Visconti-family cards survive. The Pierpont Morgan–Bergamo group contains the earliest surviving Moon.

### Approximately 1470–1480 — *Sermones de ludo cum aliis*

**[Documented fact]** An anonymous Dominican sermon denounces the game and gives the earliest complete surviving list of the standard trumps. Its high sequence includes *stella, luna, sol, angelus, mundus* in the sermon’s order.

This is the earliest secure textual naming of **Luna as a standard tarot trump**.

### Approximately 1490–1510 — printed sheets

Rosenwald and Budapest-type sheets attest the popular printed tarot, though their dating and grouping remain debated.

### Early sixteenth century — poems and literary lists

Tarot poems assign women or moral qualities to individual trumps. These prove that the images invited metaphorical social play but do not establish occult divination.

### 1526–1527 — Teofilo Folengo

**[Documented fact]** Folengo’s *Chaos del Triperuno* contains a literary tarot episode in which cards help structure sonnets and character descriptions.

**[Caution]** This is often called early divination, but historians distinguish poetic or character-association games from a formal fortune-telling system.

### 1540s–1560s — Lollio and Imperiali

Ferrarese verse debates list tarot subjects, including Luna, and complain about or defend the game.

### Approximately 1750 — Bolognese cartomantic manuscript

**[Documented fact]** An anonymous manuscript supplies simple divinatory meanings for Bolognese tarot cards. It is the earliest presently known systematic evidence of tarot cartomancy, preceding Gébelin.

### 1781 — Court de Gébelin and Comte de Mellet

Volume VIII of *Le Monde primitif* turns tarot into a supposed Egyptian book.

Gébelin interprets the Moon’s drops as the tears of Isis fertilizing the Nile. Mellet treats the trump as the formation of the Moon and terrestrial animals, represented by wolf and dog.

### 1783–1789 — Etteilla

Etteilla publishes practical tarot cartomancy and a redesigned Egyptianizing deck. He creates systematic upright/reversed meanings.

### 1854–1856 — Éliphas Lévi

*Dogme et rituel de la haute magie* integrates tarot with Hebrew letters, Kabbalah and ceremonial magic. His order differs from the later Golden Dawn.

### 1863 — Paul Christian

*L’Homme rouge des Tuileries*, followed by *Histoire de la magie* (1870), presents fictional Egyptian temple initiations as historical revelation and calls XVIII **Twilight**.

### 1888–1889 — Mathers, Golden Dawn, Papus and Wirth

- Mathers publishes English divinatory meanings in 1888.
- The Golden Dawn forms and develops Qoph/Pisces correspondences.
- Papus publishes *Le Tarot des Bohémiens* in 1889.
- Wirth publishes twenty-two redesigned trumps in 1889.

### 1909–1911 — Rider–Waite–Smith

The deck appears in 1909; Waite’s *Pictorial Key* follows in 1910, commonly dated 1911 on the title page.

### 1920s–1930s — BOTA

Case and Jessie Burns Parke reformulate the Golden Dawn image as a colorable occult textbook.

### 1938–1943; 1969 — Thoth

Harris paints the Thoth cards; the complete deck receives its first full commercial publication in 1969.

### 1970s onward — psychological tarot

Jungian terminology—shadow, unconscious, projection, archetype, individuation—becomes increasingly common through writers such as Sallie Nichols, Rachel Pollack and Mary K. Greer.

### The Pisces attribution

**[Verified esoteric attribution]** Pisces is not inferred from the Renaissance Moon image and is not the astrological Moon’s domicile, Cancer. It arises because the Golden Dawn assigns the Hebrew letters sequentially to the trumps and attributes Qoph to Pisces.

### Qoph versus Tzaddi

French occultism and the Golden Dawn use different alignments because they place the Fool differently relative to the Hebrew alphabet.

- Papus: Moon–Tzaddi–Aquarius.
- Golden Dawn: Moon–Qoph–Pisces.

### Crowley’s Tzaddi/Heh controversy

Crowley’s *Book of the Law* says, “Tzaddi is not the Star.” He eventually exchanged the Hebrew-letter/astrological attributions of the Emperor and Star:

- Emperor: Tzaddi/Aquarius;
- Star: Heh/Aries.

**[Important finding]** This alteration does **not** change The Moon. It remains Qoph/Pisces. The Emperor–Star controversy is relevant to the integrity of Crowley’s whole sequence, but not a Qoph swap.

### Pips and courts

The request asks for pip decans and court spans. These belong to the deck-wide *Book T* system, not specifically to The Moon:

- pip cards 2–10 receive zodiacal decans and titles;
- courts receive elemental sub-elements and zodiacal spans.

The Moon is a major trump, so it has neither a pip decan title nor a court sub-element/span. Attaching one to it is a category error.

# Divinatory meanings by author

Copyright permits only brief excerpts from modern authors; longer entries are close paraphrases.

## Etteilla

**[Documented system; exact Moon equivalence disputed]** Etteilla’s reordered pack labels cards directly with upright and reversed concepts. Later synoptic lists often transfer Etteilla-derived meanings back onto Marseille XVIII without preserving his numbering.

The reliably traceable Etteilla-family semantic field for the traditional Moon subject includes night, uncertainty, concealed action and changing conditions. Exact “upright Moon XVIII / reversed Moon XVIII” lists found online frequently conflate Grand Etteilla editions.

**Finding:** I could not establish a single edition-independent Etteilla Moon pair that can responsibly be quoted as if it referred to Marseille trump XVIII. The *Dictionnaire synonimique du Livre de Thot* must be consulted by Etteilla card number and edition.

## S. L. MacGregor Mathers, 1888

Mathers gives:

- Upright: “**Twilight, deception, error**.”
- Reversed: “**Instability, inconstancy, silence**,” with lesser degrees of deception and error in expanded derivative lists.

Mathers stands between French occult cartomancy and Waite; Waite substantially enlarges his cluster.

## A. E. Waite, *Pictorial Key*, 1910/1911

Upright:

> “Hidden enemies, danger, calumny, darkness, terror, deception, occult forces, error.”

Reversed:

> “Instability, inconstancy, silence, lesser degrees of deception and error.”

Waite’s higher-symbolism chapter is less simply negative: the card represents imagination under reflected rather than direct spiritual light.

## Aleister Crowley, *Book of Thoth*, 1944

Crowley did not make reversals central to Thoth reading.

His concise field is:

> “Illusion, deception, bewilderment, hysteria, even madness, dreaminess, falsehood, error, crisis.”

He adds “the darkest hour before the dawn” and “the brink of important change.”

**Reversed:** No canonical separate reversed meaning in Crowley’s own Thoth method; elemental and positional dignity modifies the card.

## Paul Foster Case

Case avoids a simple fortune-telling binary. His constructive meanings include body consciousness, sleep, organization, subconscious reproduction and the Path of Return. Negative expression involves domination by instinct, deceptive reflected impressions or failure to balance wild nature and artificial conditioning.

**Reversed:** No universally authoritative Case list comparable to Waite’s. BOTA teaching emphasizes polarity and stage of development more than mechanically reversed keywords.

## Rachel Pollack

In *Seventy-Eight Degrees of Wisdom*, Pollack reads the Moon as entry into unconscious experience after the Star’s openness: fear, fantasy, dream, animal nature and psychic descent coexist with genuine intuition.

- Upright: journey through the unconscious; images are powerful but not necessarily literal; fear and wonder accompany loss of rational control.
- Reversed: resistance to the descent, distorted emotion, or emergence from confusion, depending on context.

These are paraphrases because Pollack’s book remains copyrighted.

## Mary K. Greer

In *The Complete Book of Tarot Reversals* Greer associates the Moon with synchronicity, intuition, dreams, mystery, symbolism, displaced meaning, submerged fear and survival responses.

For reversal she offers a range rather than one opposite:

- denial or blocking of intuition;
- projection and irrational fear;
- concealed or private psychic material;
- emergence from illusion;
- excessive fantasy;
- unconventional or shamanic perception.

Her general reversal framework includes opposite, blocked, delayed, internalized, excessive and released forms.

# Esoteric and symbolic readings

## Christian cosmology

**First layer:** fifteenth-century trump sequence.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** The Star, Moon, Sun, Judgment/Angel and World form an ascent beyond terrestrial instability toward celestial and divine realities. The Moon’s original meaning may be primarily cosmological rather than moral.

The sequence is compatible with medieval Christian hierarchies but no surviving fifteenth-century manual supplies a secret Christian exegesis of the individual card.

## Classical lunar personification

The Visconti maiden participates in the Renaissance visual language of Luna and possibly Diana.

- crescent: lunar identity;
- feminine personification: conventional gendering of Luna;
- rich dress: courtly allegory.

Calling her Hecate, Isis or a lunar priestess is later interpretation unless supported by a particular redesigned deck.

## Astrological reading

Two systems conflict:

1. **Traditional astrology:** the Moon is a planetary luminary, ruling Cancer.
2. **Golden Dawn tarot:** trump XVIII corresponds to Pisces; the planetary Moon corresponds to the High Priestess/Gimel.

The crayfish may evoke Cancer, but no cardmaker’s text proves that this is why it was inserted.

## Kabbalah

- Lévi made the twenty-two trumps parallel the Hebrew alphabet.
- Papus assigned XVIII to Tzaddi.
- The Golden Dawn reassigned it to Qoph and Path 29.
- Crowley and Case preserved Qoph.

**[Esoteric attribution]** These are nineteenth- and twentieth-century constructions. No Hebrew letters appear on the fifteenth-century cards, and no Renaissance Italian source connects Luna to Kabbalah.

## Hermetic and astral

Lévi and Papus made lunar light a symbol of reflection: spirit weakened, reversed or materialized in the astral world. The road between towers becomes passage through unstable appearances.

This is the main intellectual ancestor of Waite’s contrast between imagination and spirit.

## Egyptian

- Gébelin: Moon as Isis; drops as Isis’s tears swelling the Nile.
- Mellet: cosmic creation.
- Paul Christian: Egyptian temple trial.
- Crowley: Anubis, Khephra and solar resurrection.

These are not one continuous ancient Egyptian tradition. They are successive European occult constructions using different Egyptian materials.

## Alchemical

Modern esoteric readings commonly associate the Moon with:

- silver;
- moisture;
- dissolution;
- *albedo*, the whitening phase;
- conjunction of conscious and unconscious;
- the lunar vessel or mercurial imagination.

**[Caution]** Direct identification of Marseille XVIII with *albedo* is not documented in its makers’ period. It becomes widespread through nineteenth-century Hermetic synthesis and twentieth-century occult/Jungian tarot.

Crowley’s version is closer to *nigredo* passing toward rebirth than to a serene completed *albedo*.

## Jungian

The Moon becomes:

- the collective or personal unconscious;
- dream production;
- projection under incomplete light;
- shadow figures;
- instinct represented by crustacean, wolf and dog;
- individuation as the path between opposites.

**[Modern invention with intellectual pedigree]** Jung did not author a canonical tarot interpretation of The Moon. Jungian tarot was developed by later writers, notably Sallie Nichols, Pollack and Greer. Claims that “Jung said The Moon card means…” require particular caution.

## The “dark night” reading

Three distinguishable layers exist:

- Night as literal iconography: early modern.
- Spiritual/astral darkness: Lévi, Christian and Papus.
- “Darkest hour before dawn”: explicitly Crowley.
- “Dark night of the soul”: modern synthesis, often borrowing Christian mystical language from John of the Cross without evidence that the card historically encoded his doctrine.

# People and works

## Makers, artists and occultists

- **Bonifacio Bembo and workshop:** attributed makers of the Visconti-Sforza Moon.
- **Jean Noblet, Jean Dodal, Pierre Madenié, Nicolas Conver:** printers who stabilize Type I and Type II Marseille imagery.
- **Court de Gébelin:** invents the Egyptian interpretation in 1781.
- **Comte de Mellet:** adds Hebrew-letter and creation readings.
- **Etteilla:** makes tarot an explicit divinatory product.
- **Éliphas Lévi:** joins tarot, Kabbalah and ceremonial magic.
- **Paul Christian:** fictionalizes an Egyptian initiation that later writers treated as history.
- **Stanislas de Guaita and Oswald Wirth:** create a purpose-built occult trump series.
- **MacGregor Mathers and Golden Dawn members:** establish Qoph/Pisces.
- **A. E. Waite and Pamela Colman Smith:** create the dominant English-language image.
- **Paul Foster Case and Jessie Burns Parke:** systematize it as BOTA instruction.
- **Aleister Crowley and Frieda Harris:** produce the radical Egyptian-Thelemic reinterpretation.
- **Alejandro Jodorowsky and Philippe Camoin:** reconstruct and psychologize Marseille.

## Literature

### T. S. Eliot, *The Waste Land* (1922)

Madame Sosostris produces “a wicked pack of cards,” but Eliot names the drowned Phoenician Sailor, Belladonna, the man with three staves, Wheel, one-eyed merchant and Hanged Man—not The Moon.

**[Negative finding]** The poem is relevant to modern tarot reception but does not securely quote trump XVIII.

### W. B. Yeats

Yeats participated in the Golden Dawn and worked deeply with lunar cycles, especially in *A Vision*. His system of twenty-eight lunar phases and masks resonates with tarot occultism.

**[Caution]** A direct, central literary treatment of The Moon card itself could not be securely traced. Conflating Yeats’s lunar philosophy with trump XVIII is interpretive.

### Italo Calvino, *The Castle of Crossed Destinies* (1969/1973)

Calvino constructs stories from arranged tarot cards, using both Visconti and Marseille imagery in different parts of the work.

**[Documented literary method]** The cards operate as a combinatorial visual language. Individual Moon appearances depend on the layout and edition; the work should not be cited as offering one fixed divinatory meaning for XVIII.

## Film and visual culture

### Ingmar Bergman

Bergman’s cinema—especially *The Seventh Seal*, *Hour of the Wolf*, *Persona* and *Cries and Whispers*—shares tarot-like medieval allegory, dream and nocturnal psychology.

**[Negative finding]** I found no primary evidence that Bergman deliberately quoted the Moon card’s complete iconography. Claims of a direct citation are interpretive.

### Alejandro Jodorowsky

Jodorowsky’s films repeatedly use tarot, alchemy, initiation and planetary imagery. His most direct contribution to The Moon remains his Marseille reconstruction and *The Way of Tarot*, written with Marianne Costa.

### Paintings and buildings

Twin towers, lunar faces, baying dogs and crustaceans individually occur widely in emblem books, astrology and architectural symbolism. However:

**[Open finding]** No pre-seventeenth-century painting or building has been demonstrated beyond reasonable doubt as the single complete source of the Marseille Moon. Proposed comparisons are iconographic analogies, not proven quotations.

Modern murals, illustrations and installations frequently quote the RWS composition, but a comprehensive census would be effectively unlimited.

# Controversies and disputes

## Egyptian origin

### Claim

Tarot is the lost Book of Thoth, encoded by Egyptian priests and preserved through playing cards.

### Source and spread

- Gébelin, 1781;
- Mellet, 1781;
- Etteilla, 1780s;
- Lévi, Christian, Papus and later occultists;
- popular tarot publishing.

### Evidence

**[Legend]** No Egyptian tarot pack, text or transmission chain exists. Gébelin wrote before hieroglyphic Egyptian had been deciphered and supplied no hieroglyphic evidence for his proposed *Tar* + *Ro* etymology.

The Moon-specific claim that its drops are Isis’s tears is an ingenious eighteenth-century interpretation, not an attested ancient explanation.

## Romani or “Gypsy” origin

### Claim

Roma carried tarot from Egypt or India to Europe.

### Source and spread

Gébelin associated tarot with people then called “Bohemians”; nineteenth-century writers including Vaillant and Papus amplified the story.

### Evidence

**[Legend]** Tarot is documented in northern Italian court and civic records in the fifteenth century. No comparable early Romani transmission evidence exists. Roma later practiced European cartomancy, but adoption is not invention.

“Gypsy” is retained only when quoting historical titles or claims; **Roma/Romani** is preferred in current usage.

## Cathar and Templar claims

### Claims

- Cathars encoded dualist doctrine in tarot.
- Templars preserved secret Egyptian or Johannine wisdom through the trumps.
- Troubadours transmitted the code after Montségur.

### Evidence

**[Legend/disputed modern speculation]** No medieval Cathar or Templar document identifies tarot cards, and the orders’ principal historical periods predate the documented tarot pack. Similarities between dualism, towers, celestial imagery or initiation narratives are retrospective.

## The crayfish

Proposed identities include:

- crayfish;
- lobster;
- crab/Cancer;
- Nile creature;
- primitive life emerging from water;
- devouring lunar monster;
- alchemical matter;
- Khephra precursor.

Only the first descriptive observation is secure.

Dummett noted that the crustacean appears in a Milanese prototype while the dogs do not, arguing that ordinary dogs baying at the Moon require no Egyptian explanation. The precise “Milanese prototype” and transmission route remain subjects for iconographic research.

## The dogs

- two dogs;
- dog and wolf;
- Anubis twins;
- tame and wild instinct;
- Canis Major and Minor;
- guardians of the horizon.

The first two are visual descriptions. Anubis is Crowley’s explicit redesign. The other readings are interpretive.

## The towers

Proposals include city gates, limits of civilization, solstitial markers, Hercules’ pillars, pylons of an Egyptian temple and the two pillars of Kabbalah.

No maker’s explanation survives. Egyptian pylons belong securely to Crowley and Egyptianizing decks, not demonstrably to Noblet or Conver.

## The droplets

Competing readings:

- rain;
- dew;
- lunar moisture drawn upward;
- tears of Isis;
- blood;
- Hebrew Yods;
- astral influence.

“Drops” is fact; direction and meaning are disputed. Gébelin supplies tears, Papus blood, Waite fertilizing dew/Yod-like forms and Crowley impure blood.

## The path

**[Documented development]** A path becomes explicit in Lévi/Papus-era occult imagery and central in RWS. It is not clearly a long initiatory route in the surviving early Marseille cards.

The popular assertion that Pamela Colman Smith simply copied an ancient path is therefore misleading: she synthesized older components into a much more emphatic journey.

## The lunar phase

Historic cards combine crescent, disk, face and solar-looking rays. Modern readers variously identify full, waxing, waning or eclipsed Moon.

**[Disputed]** These may reflect graphic convention rather than astronomical precision. Crowley explicitly makes the Moon waning and malignant; that specification should not be back-projected onto Conver.

## “Diana” in Visconti-Sforza

Plausible, traditional, but not proven. “Personification of Luna” remains the more conservative label.

## Secret occult Renaissance tarot

**[Legend]** No fifteenth- or sixteenth-century source supplies Qoph, Pisces, Tree paths, Egyptian initiation or fortune-telling meanings for the Moon. Early evidence consistently places tarot in gaming, courtly display, moral metaphor and literary play.

Dummett, Decker and Depaulis emphasize this documentary break. Kaplan catalogued the physical packs; Place and Farley explain how occult reinterpretation accumulated while acknowledging the cards’ rich Renaissance symbolism.

## Misprints and accidents that became traditions

- German **Mond** for trump XXI grew from *Mondo/Monde* confusion.
- Dates for Gébelin have repeatedly been misprinted; *Le Monde primitif* appeared in volumes from 1773 to 1782, with tarot in volume VIII, 1781.
- Conver is variously dated 1760 or 1761 depending on exemplar/cataloguing.
- The RWS book is described as 1910 or 1911 because publication and title-page dates differ.
- The Thoth deck is frequently dated 1944, the year of *Book of Thoth*, though the paintings were made 1938–1943 and the complete commercial deck appeared in 1969.
- “Cary–Yale” and “Visconti di Modrone” are sometimes treated as separate decks.
- The “Mantegna Tarocchi” name falsely implies both Mantegna’s authorship and tarot-game function.
- Grimaud’s *Ancien Tarot* is often mistaken for a direct ancient facsimile.
- Etteilla numbers are frequently mapped incorrectly onto Marseille numbers.

# Open questions

1. What exact printed or painted northern Italian intermediary connects the Visconti personification to the Marseille dogs–towers–crustacean landscape?

2. Is the crustacean primarily an emblem of Cancer, a natural-history inhabitant of lunar marshland, or a surviving element from a lost Milanese pattern?

3. Were Marseille droplets originally conceived as descending rain or ascending lunar attraction? Surviving cuts support both visual impressions, and no maker’s gloss settles it.

4. Does the Visconti figure deliberately represent Diana, generic Luna, or an unidentified courtly bearer of the celestial emblem?

5. How much of Pamela Colman Smith’s exact landscape originated in Golden Dawn manuscript designs rather than Waite’s instructions or her study of historic packs?

6. Which Wirth states and recolorings are being reproduced under the generic label “1889 Wirth”? Later editions sometimes silently modify the original.

7. What exact Grand Etteilla card should be treated as the functional equivalent of Marseille XVIII? Number, subject and divinatory equivalence diverge, and modern lists commonly conflate editions.

8. Can a pre-Noblet source for the complete two towers–two canids–crustacean ensemble be securely identified?

9. No secure direct Moon-card quotation by Eliot, Yeats or Bergman was found. Their inclusion in tarot discussions often reflects thematic affinity rather than documented borrowing.
