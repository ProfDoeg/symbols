# The Devil: Research Dossier

## Method and evidentiary labels

This dossier uses the following labels throughout:

- **[Documented fact]** Surviving card, archival entry, printed rule, authorial text, or museum record.
- **[Scholarly reconstruction]** Conclusion inferred by historians from surviving objects and documents.
- **[Tradition]** Interpretation repeatedly transmitted within a historical tarot school, though not inherent in the original playing card.
- **[Esoteric attribution]** Deliberately imposed occult correspondence.
- **[Disputed claim]** Proposed identification or history for which specialists disagree or the evidence is inadequate.
- **[Legend]** Story contradicted by, or unsupported in, the documentary record.
- **[Modern invention]** Recent reconstruction, redesign, or interpretation sometimes mistaken for an old tradition.

Dates for early printed sheets remain approximate. “Tarot de Marseille” is used as a modern pattern-name; seventeenth-century makers did not necessarily call their cards that.

### Immediate findings on the item-specific leads

- **“No fifteenth-century Devil survives.” — Requires qualification.**  
  **[Documented fact]** No Devil survives in any of the painted Visconti-family luxury groups normally meant by this assertion. In the Pierpont Morgan–Bergamo Visconti-Sforza group, the Devil and Tower are among the missing cards. Modern facsimiles supply newly invented replacements. [Morgan Library shop description](https://shop.themorgan.org/products/619)

  **[Documented fact / disputed dating]** Devil figures do survive on Italian woodcut sheets variously dated to the late fifteenth century or around 1500. Whether any sheet can securely be placed before 1500—and which is earliest—remains disputed.

- **Marseille Devil on a pedestal with two chained imps. — Verified.**  
  **[Documented fact]** This composition is visible in the early French lineage and became the defining Marseille form: a large horned, winged, often sexually ambiguous demon stands on a pedestal; two smaller horned beings are tethered to it.

- **Lévi’s Baphomet adopted by Waite. — Verified, with qualifications.**  
  **[Documented influence]** Waite explicitly calls his figure “the Horned Goat of Mendes.” The goat head, torch, breasts/sexual ambiguity, raised and lowered hands, and relation to occult polarity recall Lévi. Waite and Pamela Colman Smith nevertheless transformed rather than copied Lévi: bat wings replace Lévi’s feathered wings; the caduceus and “solve/coagula” are removed; the torch is placed in the hand; a reversed pentagram is put on the forehead; and the Marseille chained pair becomes an Adam-and-Eve-like human couple.

- **Crowley’s goat, third eye, testicles/root, Pan. — Verified.**  
  **[Documented fact]** Crowley describes a Himalayan goat with an eye in its forehead, identifying it with Pan. Frieda Harris placed it against transparent phallic/testicular forms and a root system containing proliferating sperm-like life. The card celebrates generative energy rather than presenting a conventional Christian Satan.

- **Templar Baphomet. — Historical accusation, later occult reinterpretation.**  
  **[Documented fact]** “Baphomet” occurs in medieval sources and Templar proceedings contain accusations concerning an idol or head.  
  **[Disputed claim]** The testimony is inconsistent and was gathered during a politically driven prosecution, frequently under coercion. No object resembling Lévi’s goat has been authenticated as a Templar idol.  
  **[Modern occult construction]** The now-standard goat-headed Baphomet is Lévi’s nineteenth-century synthesis, not a recovered medieval Templar image.

# Iconography, deck by deck

## Precursors and fifteenth-century Italy

### Marziano da Tortona and Michelino da Besozzo, Milan, c. 1414–1425

**[Documented fact]** Marziano’s surviving Latin treatise describes sixteen deified heroes or classical gods, arranged in four orders and used as superior cards alongside bird suits. Michelino da Besozzo painted the lost pack for Filippo Maria Visconti.

There is no Devil. The figures include Jupiter, Apollo, Mercury, Hercules, Aeolus, Daphne, Cupid and other classical beings.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** This is an important precursor because the special cards formed a ranked permanent trump series, but it is not a standard 22-trump tarot and cannot attest the Devil’s presence. A 1449 letter from Jacopo Antonio Marcello accompanied the treatise and deck when they were sent to Isabella of Lorraine. [Overview of the Marziano pack](https://www.usgamesinc.com/files/attachments/489/CY86_Booklet.pdf)

### Visconti di Modrone / Cary-Yale, Milan, c. 1440s

The names “Visconti di Modrone” and “Cary-Yale” refer to the same fragmentary luxury pack now at Yale.

**[Documented fact]** It has an unusual structure: six court ranks and additional theological virtues. No Devil survives.

**[Disputed claim]** No surviving inventory identifies its original trump total or proves that a Devil was painted. Supplying a Devil in a reproduction is therefore reconstruction, not restoration.

### Brera-Brambilla, Milan, c. 1440s

**[Documented fact]** Fragmentary hand-painted Visconti pack, named after Giovanni Brambilla and now principally associated with the Brera. Only two trumps survive, the Emperor and Wheel of Fortune. No Devil survives.

**[Open inference]** It probably belonged to the developing Milanese tarot family, but its missing cards cannot establish the Devil’s design.

### Pierpont Morgan–Bergamo Visconti-Sforza, Milan, c. 1450s

**[Documented fact]** Seventy-four cards survive in three collections. Four expected cards are absent: Devil, Tower, Three of Swords, and Knight of Coins. The cards originally bore neither printed titles nor trump numbers. [Morgan Library record and facsimile explanation](https://shop.themorgan.org/products/619)

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** The deck’s general structure and comparison with later Milanese sequences make an original Devil highly probable, but its image is unrecoverable.

**[Legend]** Suggestions that the Devil and Tower were deliberately destroyed because they were unlucky, heretical, or used magically have no known contemporary evidence. Ordinary attrition is sufficient explanation.

### Other Visconti and related fragments

No Devil is preserved in the smaller Tozzi, Victoria and Albert, Colleoni, or related painted fragments.

**Conclusion:** “The Visconti Devil” offered by modern publishers is always a new composition, usually synthesized from Marseille, Rosenwald, or Renaissance demon imagery.

### Charles VI / Gringonneur cards, northern Italy, c. late fifteenth century

**[Documented fact]** Despite the traditional name, these cards were not made for Charles VI by Jacquemin Gringonneur in 1392. The surviving Italian Renaissance group contains no Devil.

**[Corrected misattribution]** The royal French attribution originated in antiquarian association, not object evidence.

### Este / Ferrarese cards, late fifteenth century

The surviving Este-associated cards include figures such as Temperance, the Star, Sun, World, and allegorical subjects, but no securely attributable painted Este Devil survives.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** Ferrara was a major early tarot center, and Ferrarese lists include a Devil. The absence in the surviving fragments is not evidence that Ferrarese packs lacked it.

### Mantegna “Tarocchi,” Ferrara or northeastern Italy, c. 1460s–1470s

**[Documented fact]** The so-called Mantegna Tarocchi are fifty didactic engravings arranged into five ten-card series: social estates, Apollo and the Muses, liberal arts/sciences, virtues, and celestial spheres.

They are neither by Mantegna nor a tarot pack, and they contain no Devil trump.

**Relevance:** Their ordered ascent through social, intellectual, moral, and cosmic levels illuminates the Renaissance taste for hierarchically arranged images, but they cannot document the tarot Devil.

### Sola Busca, northern Italy, 1491; illuminated c. 1490s

**[Documented fact]** The Sola Busca is the earliest surviving complete 78-card tarot and the earliest surviving deck with fully illustrated suit cards. Its trump 15 is **Metelo**, not a conventional Devil: an armored ancient or pseudo-Roman hero carries or confronts an animal/dragon-like creature.

**Meaning of the change:** The entire trump sequence substitutes obscure classical or pseudo-historical characters for the usual medieval allegories. Metelo is only structurally equivalent to the Devil by position; identifying the figure himself as Satan or Baphomet is unjustified.

**Influence:** Pamela Colman Smith almost certainly knew the Sola Busca cards exhibited at the British Museum in 1908. Several Waite-Smith minor scenes closely resemble them. That does not make Sola Busca’s Metelo a source for her Devil.

### Budapest / Metropolitan sheets, Italy, late fifteenth century or c.1500

**[Documented object; disputed date and localization]** These uncut woodcut sheets preserve a nearly complete early printed tarot. They have often been called Ferrarese, though localization is not completely secure.

The Devil:

- is horned;
- has clawed feet;
- has large colored wings;
- appears to carry a belly-face or mask;
- holds a scourge/flail-like implement;
- looks closer to a costumed medieval stage devil than to Lévi’s goat.

There are no two chained captives in the familiar Marseille arrangement.

**[Disputed identification]** The apparent abdominal face may be anatomy, a strapped mask, or damage/coloring interpreted as a face.

### Rosenwald sheets, probably Florence, late fifteenth century–early sixteenth century

**[Documented object; disputed date]** The National Gallery of Art’s Rosenwald woodcut sheets preserve most of a tarot. The Devil is horned, claw-footed, furry or leaf-covered, and relatively animated rather than enthroned.

Some cards are numbered irregularly; the Devil itself may be unnumbered on the sheet. The placement in the sheet’s sequence corresponds approximately to the conventional Devil position.

**[Disputed chronology]** Dates proposed range from the later fifteenth century to the first years of the sixteenth. Damage and later retouching complicate close iconographic analysis. Dummett discusses the sheet as evidence for an early popular printed pattern. [Dummett excerpt and catalogue references](https://forum.tarothistory.com/viewtopic.php?t=1175)

### Rothschild / École des Beaux-Arts sheet, late fifteenth or early sixteenth century

A surviving sheet includes Chariot, Death, Devil, Tower, Star and Moon.

The Devil is part of a compact late-medieval infernal sequence. Its exact priority over the Rosenwald and Budapest examples cannot be demonstrated securely.

### Cary sheet, c.1500

The Yale/Cary sheet preserves fragmentary trumps including a Devil. It is important for the ancestry of Marseille-type imagery, although the image is damaged and cannot supply a complete iconographic description.

## Regional Italian patterns

### Minchiate, Florence, sixteenth–nineteenth centuries

Minchiate expanded the trump series to forty numbered trumps plus the Fool.

**[Documented fact]** The Devil is trump **XIV**, not XV. Death follows as XIII and the Devil comes before the House of the Devil/Fire card. The conventional fifteen-card rank is therefore shifted.

Typical Florentine Minchiate Devils retain the horned, winged demon and subordinate figures. The card belongs to a low-to-middle group rather than the zodiacal and cosmological high trumps added above it.

**Game meaning:** No occult Capricorn attribution belonged to historical Minchiate play. The deck itself separately included all twelve zodiac signs among its additional trumps.

### Tarocchino Bolognese

The reduced Bolognese pack removes low numeral suit cards but retains the trump series.

**[Documented fact]** Early Bolognese trumps were traditionally unnumbered, and the four highest *moretti* had shifting equal rank before play conventions resolved them. The Devil is conventionally the fourteenth trump in modern listings, between Temperance and the Tower/Lightning.

In Giuseppe Maria Mitelli’s engraved Bolognese tarot, c.1660s, the Devil is a vigorously theatrical demon. Later packs reduced and sometimes crudely standardized the image.

**[Tradition]** Players often used nicknames rather than formal printed titles. Its practical identity depended on the picture and memorized order.

### Tarocco Siciliano

Tarot reached Sicily in the seventeenth century, probably through Rome. The local pattern underwent severe compression and renaming.

The Devil is commonly called **Il Diavolo** and placed in the shortened trump sequence, but its exact printed numeral varies with historical stage. The Fool is **Il Fuggitivo / lu Fujutu**, “the Fugitive.” [Sicilian rules and terminology](https://www.pagat.com/tarot/sicilian.html)

Sicilian religious substitutions affected several cards more drastically than the Devil: Jupiter could replace a papal or high authority figure; the Miseria became the lowest trump. The cards were reordered for a local game, not to express a Golden Dawn-like occult ladder.

## Tarot de Marseille lineage

### Jean Noblet, Paris, c.1650

**[Documented fact]** Noblet’s is the earliest substantially surviving French pack now classified as Tarot de Marseille Type I.

Its Devil is labeled approximately **LE DIABLE**, XV. It is:

- frontal and apparently hermaphroditic;
- antlered or horned;
- bat-winged;
- naked, with conspicuous sexual ambiguity;
- standing on a pedestal;
- holding a staff or torch;
- accompanied by two smaller bound demons.

The smaller figures have horns, tails and animalized features. Their tether runs to the central support.

**Developmental importance:** Noblet provides the earliest relatively complete surviving form of the “demon above, two bound beings below” composition that would dominate the French lineage.

### Jacques Viéville, Paris, c.1650

Viéville represents a parallel French tradition rather than the canonical Marseille layout. His trump order, orientations and titles differ in several places.

The Devil remains a horned infernal figure but does not duplicate Noblet line for line. The related card XVI is called **La Foudre**, illustrating that “Maison Dieu” was never the only early French Tower title.

### Jean Dodal, Lyon, c.1701–1715

Dodal’s **XV LE DIABLE** regularizes the Type I form:

- blue-winged, horned central being;
- breasts and male genitalia or an ambiguous groin;
- face-like markings on the abdomen;
- eyes or facial marks at knees;
- clawed feet;
- two smaller beings joined by a cord.

The central being stands on a yellow plinth. The small figures are not ordinary nude humans; they are imps sharing the Devil’s horns and tails.

### Pierre Madenié, Dijon, 1709; Jean-Pierre Payen, Avignon, 1713; François Héri, Solothurn, 1718

These early eighteenth-century packs transmit related French patterns. Their Devils vary in line, genital detail, wing coloring, and the form of the pedestal and tether, but preserve the tripartite group.

### Nicolas Conver, Marseille, 1760

Conver’s version became a major source for twentieth-century Marseille restorations.

The Devil:

- is a frontal blue-and-flesh-colored hybrid;
- has branching horns or antlers and bat wings;
- raises one arm and lowers the other;
- holds a blade, torch, or staff-like object;
- stands upon a red/yellow pedestal;
- has two horned, tailed captives attached below.

**Interpretive caution:** The genital and facial details are stylized woodcut marks. Claims that every line was a deliberately encoded chakra, Hebrew letter, or alchemical formula are later readings unless supported independently.

### Tarot de Besançon, Constance/Alsace/Switzerland, c.1680 onward

The pattern generally replaces II Papess with **Juno/Junon** and V Pope with **Jupiter**, probably in confessionally mixed regions where papal cards were awkward. Early examples occur at Constance c.1680, Strasbourg by 1746, and Mannheim c.1750; substantial production at Besançon itself is mainly around and after 1800. [International Playing-Card Society pattern sheet](https://i-p-c-s.org/pattern/ps-6.html)

The Devil remains XV. A diagnostic feature is its furry torso and trousers. Thus the celebrated Juno/Jupiter substitution did not remove the Devil.

### Belgian tarot

Some Belgian packs replaced the Papess with **Captain Fracasse** and the Pope with **Bacchus**. The Devil remained recognizable but could differ in caption and drawing. These are regional adjustments to a playing-card pattern, not evidence of an ancient pagan original.

### Grimaud / Paul Marteau, 1930

**[Documented fact]** Paul Marteau published Grimaud’s **Ancien Tarot de Marseille**, doing much to popularize that retrospective name and a fixed color system.

The Devil closely follows Conver but is cleaned, recolored, and made mechanically reproducible. Marteau interpreted colors and details esoterically, so this edition stands between historical playing-card reproduction and occult manual.

### Camoin–Jodorowsky, 1997

**[Documented modern reconstruction]** Philippe Camoin and Alejandro Jodorowsky presented their deck as a restoration assembled from many old examples. It is not a facsimile of one historical pack.

Its Devil restores or emphasizes:

- multiple faces or eyes in the body;
- sexual ambiguity;
- a blue pedestal or pool-like ground;
- three-pointed or branching structures;
- the cord uniting the two subordinate figures;
- a more energetic, less morally condemnatory expression.

Jodorowsky and Marianne Costa interpret XV as immense creative, sexual and financial energy, attachment, subterranean treasure, temptation and liberation from moral convention. That is a modern psychospiritual reading, not a documented seventeenth-century meaning. [Jodorowsky/Costa Spanish text](https://www.tarotjunguiano.com/wp-content/uploads/2017/06/la-via-del-tarot-alejandro-jodorowsky.pdf)

## Occult and modern decks

### Court de Gébelin’s reconstructed tarot, Paris, 1781

Court renamed XV **Typhon**, treating the Marseille Devil as the Egyptian destructive principle.

He wrote:

> “Le no. XV représente un célèbre personnage Egyptien, Typhon … le mauvais Principe, le grand Démon d’Enfer.”

Gloss: “No. XV represents a celebrated Egyptian personage, Typhon … the evil Principle, the great Demon of Hell.”

He describes bat wings, harpy hands and feet, deer horns, and two little devils bound to the pedestal. [Court de Gébelin, *Monde primitif*, vol. VIII, 1781](https://www.tarock.info/gebelin.htm)

**[Esoteric attribution / unsupported historical claim]** This is interpretation of a French card through an imagined Egyptian key, not evidence that its makers intended Typhon.

### Grand Etteilla, Paris, 1789 and early nineteenth-century states

Etteilla reordered and redesigned the full pack as an Egyptian cosmogony.

The Devil-equivalent is normally no. **14**, associated with:

- **Force majeure** upright;
- **Force mineure** reversed;
- violence, irresistible motion, power and physical force.

Different historical states variously emphasize the occult operator, naked ascetic, demonic being, or hieroglyphic “greater force.” Consequently, reproductions labeled simply “Etteilla Devil” may conflate states.

A nineteenth-century catalogue confirms no. 14 as *Force Majeure* and numbers the entire pack consecutively from 1 to 78. [British Museum catalogue transcription](https://iapsop.com/ssoc/1876__willshire___descriptive_catalogue_of_playing_and_other_cards.pdf)

### Éliphas Lévi, 1854–1856

Lévi did not publish a complete tarot deck. His decisive image is the **Goat of the Sabbath / Baphomet of Mendes** in *Dogme et Rituel de la Haute Magie*.

It has:

- goat head and horns;
- a torch between the horns;
- upright pentagram;
- female breasts;
- feathered wings;
- caduceus replacing the genitals;
- one arm marked **SOLVE**, the other **COAGULA**;
- one hand pointing upward and the other downward;
- crescent moons, one white and one black;
- cloven hooves.

Lévi explains it as a compound sign of equilibrium rather than a simple portrait of Satan. In the English *Transcendental Magic*, the relevant passage calls it:

> “the Baphomet of the Templars, the bearded idol of the alchemist, the obscene deity of Mendes, the goat of the Sabbath.”

This is in the Ritual, chapter XV, “The Sabbath of the Sorcerers,” corresponding to vol. II of the French work. [French edition scan](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Dogme_et_rituel_de_la_haute_magie_(IA_b24884340_0001).pdf) and [English translation](https://www.rahoorkhuit.net/library/ceremonial/classics/pdf/transcendental/dogma_et_rituel_part_2.pdf)

**Important distinction:** Lévi’s pictured pentagram points upward. His discussion elsewhere associates a reversed pentagram with the goat of lust. Later images often merge those two passages and place a reversed pentagram directly on Baphomet’s forehead.

### Paul Christian, 1863 and 1870

Jean-Baptiste Pitois, writing as Paul Christian, popularized **arcane/lame** terminology and an elaborate Egyptian initiation narrative. His Devil/“Typhon” belongs to the astral, fatal and magical forces confronting an initiate.

**[Modern-in-its-period invention]** Christian presents theatrical Egyptian temple initiations as ancient fact, but no Egyptian source contains his tarot ritual.

### Papus, 1889

Papus’s *Le Tarot des Bohémiens* gives XV the Hebrew letter **Samech**, not Ayin, and Sagittarius, not Capricorn.

He calls the Devil:

> “the mysterious astral force”

and later:

> “the universal destroying force.”

The torch is black magic and destruction; the two subordinate beings repeat, in fallen form, the polarity of the Lovers. The cube over a sphere means domination of spirit by matter. [Papus, English ed., pp. 164–166](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/9/93/The_tarot_of_the_Bohemians_-_the_most_ancient_book_in_the_world_-_for_the_exclusive_use_of_initiates_(IA_tarotofbohemians00papu_0).pdf)

### Oswald Wirth, 1889; revised 1926

Commissioned by Stanislas de Guaita, Wirth’s first deck consisted only of 22 trumps.

The Devil has:

- goatlike head;
- torch or flame between the horns;
- breasts;
- bat wings;
- caduceus/genital symbolism;
- one arm raised and one lowered;
- two chained or cord-bound lesser beings;
- occult geometric and Hebrew additions.

Wirth combines Marseille composition with Lévi’s Baphomet. His later discussion treats the figure as **Hyle**, chaotic or unorganized matter, animated by selfish fire. [Wirth, *Le Tarot des imagiers du Moyen Âge* / *Tarot of the Magicians*](https://www.societyofguardians.org/resources/Tarot_of_the_Magicians_by_Oswald_Wirth.pdf)

### Golden Dawn manuscripts / “Book T,” c.1888–1900

The Golden Dawn’s canonical form is not one surviving printed deck but a corpus of manuscript descriptions and correspondences.

For XV:

- Hebrew: **Ayin**
- Astrology: **Capricorn**
- Path: **26**
- Sephiroth: **Tiphareth–Hod**
- Title: **Lord of the Gates of Matter; Child of the Forces of Time**
- Divination: materiality, material force, temptation, obsession
- Image: a compound animal Devil, horn, torch, altar, and two chained demons.

**Wang reconstruction:** Robert Wang’s *Golden Dawn Tarot* visualizes the instructions in a late-twentieth-century painterly form. It is evidence for Wang’s reading of the manuscripts, not an original Golden Dawn card.

**Cicero reconstruction:** Chic and Sandra Tabatha Cicero’s *Magical Tarot of the Golden Dawn* similarly expands the instructions with explicit Tree-of-Life and color-scale details. Their manual gives the keyword “Materiality.” [Cicero correspondence summary, p.416](https://kobayashi-dxn.s3.eu-west-2.amazonaws.com/Golden%20Dawn/cicero-%20secrets%20of%20the%20golden%20dawn%20temple/Cicero_-_Self_Initiation_into_%282%29.pdf)

### Waite–Smith, London, 1909

Pamela Colman Smith drew the cards under A. E. Waite’s direction. Rider published the deck.

The Devil:

- squats on a black half-cube;
- has a goatlike face, ram horns, bat wings and bird/harpy feet;
- displays a **reversed pentagram** on the forehead;
- raises the right hand in an inverted blessing;
- lowers a flaming torch in the left;
- presides over a nude man and woman chained to the pedestal;
- gives each human horns and a tail—the man’s tail is fiery, the woman’s grape-like.

Waite explicitly describes the couple as analogous to Adam and Eve after the Fall. Their chains are wide enough to suggest that bondage may be partly unconscious or voluntary, although Waite himself does not explicitly say “they can simply remove them.”

His description:

> “Hereof is the chain and fatality of the material life.”

and:

> “he who is exalted above them is not to be their master for ever.”

[*The Pictorial Key to the Tarot*, 1910, Part II, XV](https://sacred-texts.com/tarot/pkt/pktar15.htm)

**Sources and transformations:**

- Marseille: central demon, pedestal, paired captives.
- Lévi: Goat of Mendes, occult hand polarity, generative/sexual synthesis.
- Christian biblical art: fallen Adam and Eve.
- Golden Dawn: Ayin–Capricorn and matter.
- Smith’s own design: narrative humanization and visual psychology.

### Paul Foster Case / BOTA, c.1920s onward

Case’s BOTA card follows Waite’s broad architecture but changes details to make Golden Dawn correspondences explicit:

- black background for ignorance and Saturn;
- goat composite;
- Mercury sign at the navel;
- inverted pentagram;
- torch and Magician-like gesture;
- chained human pair;
- explicit Ayin and Capricorn framework.

Case writes:

> “The significance of the fifteenth Tarot Key has little, if anything to do with that creature of the nursery tale…”

His central doctrine is that form necessarily limits and that the frightening figure is humanity’s composite misconception of natural forces. He connects XV numerically with V, the Hierophant, and VI, the Lovers. [Case, *An Introduction to the Study of the Tarot*](https://studylib.net/doc/27694699/an-introduction-to-the-study-of-the-taro)

### Crowley–Harris Thoth, painted 1938–1943; published 1969

Frieda Harris’s card departs radically from the seated Baphomet.

It shows:

- a white Himalayan goat;
- enormous spiral horns;
- a third eye centered on its forehead;
- a wand rising through the composition;
- two translucent globes resembling testicles;
- roots, spermatozoa or generative seeds within them;
- a phallic/tree structure;
- interpenetrating rings and cosmic forms.

Crowley writes:

> “before it stands the Himalayan goat, with an eye in the centre of his forehead, representing the god Pan upon the highest and most secret mountains of the earth.”

He treats the transparent roots as showing “the innumerable leapings of the sap.” The Devil is creative energy at its most material, ecstatic and indiscriminate—not merely bondage or moral evil. [Crowley, *The Book of Thoth*, Devil chapter](https://www.mr-kaplan.com/Library/Crowley/Book%20of%20Thoth.pdf)

### Notable later redesigns

- **Aquarian Tarot, David Palladini, 1970:** stylized modernist horned figure; suppresses much explicit sexuality.
- **Morgan-Greer, Bill Greer/Lloyd Morgan, 1979:** close, saturated RWS adaptation; psychological immediacy replaces scenic distance.
- **Thoth-derived occult decks:** tend to emphasize Pan, kundalini, sexual vitality and magical power.
- **Feminist decks:** frequently reinterpret the card as imposed shame, patriarchy, coercive desire, or reclaimed sexuality.
- **Queer decks:** often recover the old Marseille figure’s sexual ambiguity and reject the strictly male Satan of many RWS copies.
- **Satanic and Left-Hand-Path decks:** may treat XV positively as autonomy, carnality or adversarial consciousness.
- **Therapeutic/Jungian decks:** commonly replace metaphysical evil with shadow, compulsion, addiction and projection.
- **Decks avoiding Christian language:** rename it **The Shadow**, **Materialism**, **Bondage**, **Temptation**, **Pan**, or **Cernunnos**. These are modern inventions, not early titles.

# The game

## Original function

**[Documented fact]** Tarot was created as a trick-taking game. The special figured cards were permanent trumps: they beat ordinary suit cards, subject to the rules of following suit and local exceptions.

A player did not ordinarily interpret the Devil symbolically while playing it. Its primary practical information was its rank.

## Rank

In the standard Marseille/French order, Devil XV defeats I–XIV and loses to XVI–XXI. The Fool is a special card and ordinarily is not part of the ordinary rank ladder.

In different Italian orders the Devil’s absolute position could shift:

| Historical order | Principal region | Devil’s approximate place |
|---|---|---|
| Type A / southern | Bologna, Florence and related games | Usually XIV because Temperance and virtue order differ; in Minchiate XIV |
| Type B / eastern | Ferrara, Venice | Devil lies between Death and Tower/Fire, but virtues and highest cards differ from Marseille |
| Type C / western | Milan, Piedmont, France | Standard later position XV: Temperance XIV, Devil XV, Tower XVI |

These “A/B/C” classifications are modern scholarly tools associated especially with Dummett; historical players did not use those labels.

## Card value

The Devil is generally an ordinary trump rather than one of the highest-value counting cards.

- In many French tarot games, only the **21**, **1**, and **Excuse** are *oudlers/bouts* with special counting importance. Devil XV has ordinary card value.
- In older Italian games, particular high and low trumps formed counting combinations, but Devil was not normally a supreme *honour*.
- In Bolognese play, combinations and sequences matter extensively; its contribution arises from membership in a trump run rather than unique “Devil points.”
- In Minchiate, many designated trumps count five points, but the principal privileged groups are the *arie* and other counting sequences. Rule sets differ by century and locality. [Historical discussion of counting structures](https://www.i-p-c-s.org/journal/TPC-49-2-web.pdf)
- In modern Austrian and central-European Tarock, packs often use French-suited scenic trumps labeled by Roman numerals. The fifteenth trump is mechanically Tarock XV and may have no Devil image at all.

## The Fool

The Fool was historically:

- often unnumbered;
- called *Il Matto*, *Le Mat*, *Le Fou*, *L’Excuse*, *Sküs*, or in Sicily *Il Fuggitivo*;
- commonly played as an excuse rather than as a trump that wins by rank;
- in some later games transformed into the highest trump.

Calling it “0” is mainly an occult/cartomantic standardization, not the universal early practice.

## Justice and Strength

The Waite-Smith exchange of Justice and Strength does not change the Devil’s XV but changes the numerical web around it:

- Marseille: Justice VIII, Strength XI.
- Waite-Smith: Strength VIII, Justice XI.
- Thoth: Adjustment VIII, Lust XI.
- Some early Italian orders placed the virtues in still other positions.

**[Scholarly conclusion]** No single surviving fifteenth-century order can be called the universally original order.

# Chronology of attestation

### c.1414–1425 — Marziano’s game

**[Documented fact]** A permanent-trump precursor is described, but no Devil appears. It is not evidence for XV.

### 1440 — Giusto Giusti diary, Florence

A recorded gift of *naibi a trionfi* to Sigismondo Pandolfo Malatesta is now generally cited as the earliest secure written notice of triumph cards.

### February and July 1442 — Ferrara ledgers

The Este court paid Jacopo Sagramoro for painting cups, swords, coins, batons, and “all the figures” of four *carticelle da trionfi*. A later entry records a comparatively inexpensive pack supplied for Ercole and Sigismondo d’Este. [Italian transcription and discussion](https://www.letarot.it/page.aspx?id=1046)

Neither entry names individual trumps. It therefore does not specifically attest the Devil.

### 1449 — Marcello letter and Marziano manuscript

The letter documents the earlier Michelino/Marziano game, not necessarily the standard 22-trump series.

### c.1450s — Milanese luxury cards

The painted Visconti-Sforza cards document a mature trump tradition. No Devil survives.

### c.1470–1500 — *Sermo perutilis de ludo cum aliis*

The anonymous Dominican sermon condemns games and supplies the earliest surviving near-complete standard trump list:

> *El bagatella … la papessa … el papa … la temperantia … la morte … el diavolo … la sagitta … la stella …*

The Devil is present between Death and the Tower-like *la sagitta* (“the Arrow/Thunderbolt”).

**[Documented fact]** This establishes that the Devil belonged to the standard sequence by the sermon’s date.

### Late fifteenth century / c.1500 — printed sheets

Budapest, Rosenwald, Rothschild and Cary fragments preserve the earliest known actual Devil images. Their dates overlap too broadly to identify one with certainty as first.

### 1491 — Sola Busca

Metelo occupies trump 15 in a complete alternative iconographic program. This proves structural variability, not the absence of a conventional Devil elsewhere.

### c.1500 — poems on the trumps

Anonymous Ferrarese and other poetic commentaries treat the trumps as moral or character emblems. The Devil can represent infernal power, temptation, fraud or a particular person’s vice.

### 1526 — Teofilo Folengo

Folengo’s *Chaos del Triperuno* includes a sonnet-driven tarot episode. It is important evidence for literary and possibly divinatory use of trump imagery, although it does not supply a modern fixed Devil meaning.

### Sixteenth century — *tarocchi*

The name *tarocchi* replaces or supplements *trionfi*. Its etymology remains uncertain. It is not securely derived from Egyptian *tar-ro*, Hebrew, or Romani vocabulary.

### 1637 — Abbé de Marolles

Publishes an early detailed French tarot-game account. This is evidence for play, not occultism.

### c.1650 — Noblet and Viéville

Earliest substantially surviving French packs; the chained-pair Devil becomes visible in a well-preserved lineage.

### 1701–1715 — Dodal

Classic Type I Marseille Devil.

### 1760 — Conver

Classic Type II source for later revivals.

### 1781 — Court de Gébelin and Comte de Mellet

Court publishes the Egyptian theory and renames XV Typhon. De Mellet proposes Hebrew-letter correspondences and a divinatory method.

### 1783–1789 — Etteilla

Etteilla publishes tarot cartomancy and issues the first deck deliberately redesigned for occult/divinatory use. The Devil-function becomes no.14 Force majeure.

### 1854–1856 — Lévi

Baphomet of Mendes published; supplies the decisive modern goat synthesis.

### 1863/1870 — Paul Christian

Popularizes Egyptian initiatory “arcana.”

### 1888 — Mathers

Publishes English tarot divinatory meanings. The Golden Dawn begins systematizing Ayin–Capricorn.

### 1889 — Papus and Wirth

Papus publishes Samech–Sagittarius system; Wirth produces a Lévi-derived occult trump deck.

### 1909–1910 — Waite–Smith and *Pictorial Key*

Smith fuses Marseille, Lévi, Golden Dawn and biblical motifs into the modern canonical Devil.

### 1920s–1930s — Case/BOTA and Marteau/Grimaud

Case psychologizes and Qabalizes XV; Marteau standardizes a commercially dominant Marseille coloration.

### 1938–1943 / 1969 — Thoth

Harris paints Crowley’s Pan-goat; deck is commercially published in 1969.

### 1970s–present — psychological and revisionist Devils

Shadow, addiction, consumerism, sexuality, structural oppression and reclaimed desire become increasingly common themes.

### Ayin and Capricorn

**[Esoteric attribution]** The Devil–Ayin–Capricorn triad is Golden Dawn, not medieval tarot.

Ayin means “eye.” In Golden Dawn usage it is one of the twelve simple Hebrew letters and is mapped to Capricorn. Path 26 connects Tiphareth, Beauty, with Hod, Splendour or intellect.

### Golden Dawn colors

The principal Golden Dawn color scales for Capricorn/Ayin are generally given as:

- King Scale: indigo;
- Queen Scale: black;
- Prince Scale: blue-black;
- Princess Scale: cold dark grey or related near-black tones.

Exact English color names vary among manuscript editions and later tables.

### The Tzaddi/Heh question

Crowley’s famous “Tzaddi is not the Star” revision affects the Emperor and Star, not the Devil:

- Golden Dawn: Emperor–Heh–Aries; Star–Tzaddi–Aquarius.
- Crowley: Emperor–Tzaddi–Aries; Star–Heh–Aquarius.
- Devil remains Ayin–Capricorn.

Thus citing the Tzaddi/Heh switch as a reason for changing the Devil’s letter is an error.

### Pips and courts requested in the template

The Devil is a trump, so it has:

- no minor-arcana decan;
- no “Book T” pip title;
- no court sub-element;
- no court zodiacal span.

Those categories apply to the 36 numbered small cards and sixteen court cards, not to XV.

# Divinatory meanings by author

Long verbatim passages from later copyrighted authors are not reproduced; concise quotations are given where legally and textually appropriate.

## Etteilla

Grand Etteilla no.14:

- Upright: **“Force majeure”**—greater or irresistible force, powerful movement, great effort, violence, physical power.
- Reversed: **“Force mineure”**—lesser force, weakness, reduced capacity.

An expanded French formulation says:

> “force majeure dans tout ce qui regarde les choses de la vie humaine et force mineure … la vie future ou éternelle.”

Gloss: greater force in matters of human life; lesser force in matters of the future or eternal life. [Historical meaning transcription](https://www.lepetitsalondanthenea.com/post/grand-etteilla-complet-z-lismon)

## S. L. MacGregor Mathers, 1888

Mathers gives essentially the vocabulary later reproduced by Waite:

- Upright: **fatality, evil destiny, violence, force, vehemence, extraordinary effort**.
- Reversed: **evil fatality, weakness, pettiness, blindness**.

Edition: S. L. MacGregor Mathers, *The Tarot: Its Occult Signification, Use in Fortune-Telling, and Method of Play* (London: George Redway, 1888). [1888 scan](https://www.howardnema.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/12/the-tarot-1888-by-macgregor-mathers-1.pdf)

## A. E. Waite, 1910

Waite’s exact list is:

> “Ravage, violence, vehemence, extraordinary efforts, force, fatality; that which is predestined but is not for this reason evil.”

Reversed:

> “Evil fatality, weakness, pettiness, blindness.”

[*Pictorial Key*, Part III](https://sacred-texts.com/tarot/pkt/pktar15.htm)

The often-seen modern reversed meaning “breaking addiction/freeing yourself” is not Waite’s printed list.

## Aleister Crowley, 1944

Crowley’s divinatory list includes:

> “Blind impulse, irresistibly strong and unscrupulous, ambition, temptation, obsession…”

It continues with secret plans, hard work, obstinacy, rigidity, dissatisfaction and endurance.

Crowley normally uses dignity and surrounding cards rather than a simple upright/reversed binary. [*Book of Thoth*](https://www.mr-kaplan.com/Library/Crowley/Book%20of%20Thoth.pdf)

## Paul Foster Case

Case stresses:

- bondage;
- materialism;
- false appearance;
- limitation;
- temptation;
- slavery to sense experience;
- release through correct understanding.

Case’s card is not fundamentally a supernatural evil being. It shows error produced when consciousness mistakes appearances for reality.

## Rachel Pollack

In *Seventy-Eight Degrees of Wisdom*:

- Upright: bondage to material or psychic patterns, shame, illusion of helplessness, suppressed or distorted desire, yet also vitality and humor.
- Reversed: release can begin, but the reversal can also mean that denied material is becoming more inward, secret or difficult to recognize.

Pollack helped spread the reading that the loose chains indicate a bondage people participate in and can potentially leave.

## Mary K. Greer

Across *Tarot for Your Self* and *The Complete Book of Tarot Reversals*:

- Upright: attachment, compulsion, sexual or creative power, projection, taboo, material fixation.
- Reversed: liberation, recognition of projection, withdrawal from addiction, or alternatively intensified/private bondage depending on context.

Greer explicitly treats reversals by multiple methods—opposite, blocked, delayed, internalized, excessive—not as one invariant dictionary antonym.

# Esoteric and symbolic readings

## Christian

### Earliest layer

The historical image belongs to late-medieval Christian visual culture:

- devils in Last Judgments and hellmouths;
- the Temptation of Christ;
- demons in mystery plays;
- monstrous composite bodies signifying disorder;
- bound sinners or subordinate demons.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** The tarot Devil likely functioned as an immediately recognizable moral and eschatological figure, probably situated near Death, Tower and Last Judgment because the sequence moved through mortality, catastrophe and final things.

There is no evidence that fifteenth-century players identified it with Capricorn, kundalini, or Jung’s shadow.

### Waite’s Christian reading

Waite makes the chained pair Adam and Eve “after the Fall.” The Devil’s gesture reverses the Hierophant’s blessing. Material bondage is real but not eternal.

## Hermetic and magical

Lévi transformed the figure into equilibrium:

- light and darkness;
- male and female;
- solve and coagula;
- spirit and matter;
- mercy and severity;
- generation and dissolution.

His Baphomet is therefore not simply “the evil one.” It is a deliberately shocking totality-image.

Papus made the Devil the astral fluid turned destructive. Golden Dawn authors made it the gate of matter and natural generative power. Crowley radicalized this into Pan and ecstatic creation.

## Alchemical

The most defensible alchemical connection begins with Lévi’s explicit **SOLVE / COAGULA**, caduceus and polarity.

Later readings identify:

- blackness with *nigredo*;
- the chained pair with fixed opposites;
- sexual fire with the furnace;
- Capricorn/Saturn with lead;
- liberation as dissolution followed by a higher coagulation.

**[Caution]** Claims that the original Marseille artisans intentionally encoded a complete alchemical operation remain speculative.

## Kabbalistic

### French occult line

Lévi and Papus mapped XV to Samech under a simple numerical progression. Papus read Samech as circular destiny or an enclosing limit.

### Golden Dawn line

The Golden Dawn shifted the card to Ayin by placing the Fool and elemental trumps differently in the alphabetic series. Ayin/“eye” encouraged interpretations of:

- deceptive outward appearance;
- sensual perception;
- the “evil eye” by pun or association;
- insight capable of seeing through matter.

**[Historical distinction]** Jewish Kabbalah did not contain a medieval doctrine that “the Devil tarot card is Path 26.” That compound attribution belongs to Western Hermetic Qabalah.

## Astrological

Capricorn supplies:

- goat imagery;
- cardinal earth;
- winter solstice;
- Saturnine limitation, time, structure and endurance;
- ambition and ascent.

Crowley emphasizes the goat’s mountain vitality and generative power, balancing the pessimistic “bondage” reading.

## Sexual and generative

Sexual ambiguity is visible in Marseille Devils well before modern occultism. Lévi made androgyny programmatic; Waite masculinized the central figure but made the lower couple explicitly heterosexual; Crowley turned the entire composition into an image of insemination and universal generation.

Interpretations range from lust as bondage to sexuality as sacred vitality. These readings belong to different authors and should not be collapsed into one “traditional meaning.”

## Jungian and psychological

Jung did not leave a systematic tarot commentary assigning a meaning to XV.

**[Modern Jungian development]** Later writers associated the card with:

- the Shadow;
- projection of disowned desire;
- compulsive repetition;
- repression;
- inflation and possession by an archetypal complex;
- libido in its creative and destructive forms;
- individuation through conscious confrontation.

Calling this “Jung’s meaning of the Devil card” is inaccurate; it is a post-Jungian tarot synthesis.

## Addiction and consumerism

These are predominantly twentieth- and twenty-first-century expansions:

- addiction develops from “chains,” habit and compulsion;
- consumerism from materiality and bondage;
- abusive relationships from the chained pair;
- systemic oppression from the pedestal hierarchy.

They are powerful modern applications, not meanings documented in the fifteenth-century game.

# People and works

## Cardmakers and artists

- Anonymous Milanese court painters: Visconti groups; no surviving Devil.
- Anonymous Budapest, Rosenwald, Rothschild and Cary woodcutters: earliest surviving Devil candidates.
- Jean Noblet: early preserved chained-pair Devil.
- Jacques Viéville: parallel French pattern.
- Jean Dodal: classic Type I.
- Nicolas Conver: principal Type II source.
- Paul Marteau/Grimaud: twentieth-century standardization.
- Philippe Camoin and Alejandro Jodorowsky: modern reconstruction.
- Éliphas Lévi: creator of modern Baphomet image.
- Oswald Wirth: first influential Lévi-derived occult tarot Devil.
- Pamela Colman Smith and A. E. Waite: creator/director of the canonical English design.
- Frieda Harris and Aleister Crowley: Thoth’s Pan-goat.
- Paul Foster Case/Jessie Burns Parke: BOTA design tradition.
- Robert Wang; Chic and Sandra Cicero: Golden Dawn reconstructions.

## Literature

### Italo Calvino, *The Castle of Crossed Destinies*

Calvino uses tarot cards as a combinatorial narrative machine. The Devil can function within stories of desire, temptation, violence and entrapment, but there is no single authorial essay establishing “Calvino’s definitive Devil meaning.”

**[Documented literary use]** Calvino worked from reproduced historical tarot imagery, notably Visconti and Marseille traditions.

### T. S. Eliot, *The Waste Land*

Madame Sosostris names “the drowned Phoenician Sailor,” Belladonna, “the man with three staves,” the Wheel, and “the one-eyed merchant”; she says the Hanged Man is absent.

**Finding:** The Devil is not explicitly named. Attempts to identify the one-eyed merchant with Ayin/the Devil are modern speculative overlays and are not supported by Eliot’s notes.

### W. B. Yeats

Yeats belonged to the Golden Dawn and knew its tarot correspondences. His poetry and occult papers use interlocking magical systems.

**Finding:** No securely traced major Yeats poem is a direct ekphrasis of the Devil card. General claims that a horned or bestial Yeats image “is XV” need work-specific evidence.

## Film

### Alejandro Jodorowsky

Tarot is central to Jodorowsky’s practice and appears throughout his films, interviews and psychomagic work. *The Holy Mountain* employs tarot-like initiatory staging, but individual scenes should not automatically be called quotations of XV without compositional evidence.

### Ingmar Bergman

*The Seventh Seal* contains Death playing chess, not a tarot game, and does not quote the Devil card.

**Finding:** Associating Bergman’s medieval Devil, Death or witch imagery with tarot XV is thematic comparison, not documented borrowing.

## Visual art and architecture

- **Niki de Saint Phalle’s Tarot Garden**, Garavicchio, Tuscany, realizes monumental tarot figures. It is the strongest architectural/sculptural example of a modern environment based explicitly on the cards.
- **Lévi’s Baphomet** has been quoted in occult illustration, album art, cinema and political satire far more often than the Marseille Devil itself.
- **The Devil’s architectural pedestal** has encouraged modern altar, stage and throne reconstructions, but no medieval building has been established as the single source for the card.

Claims that Rosslyn Chapel, Chartres Cathedral, or Templar buildings encode the complete tarot sequence are unsupported by contemporaneous tarot evidence.

# Controversies and disputes

## Egyptian origin

### Claim

Court de Gébelin asserted in 1781 that tarot preserved an Egyptian Book of Thoth. XV was Typhon.

### Evidence

- No Egyptian tarot pack survives.
- No ancient Egyptian text describes the 22 tarot trumps.
- Egyptian hieroglyphs were not deciphered when Court wrote.
- Tarot’s Pope, Papess, Emperor, Wheel, Christian Devil and Last Judgment belong naturally to late-medieval European imagery.
- Documents place *trionfi* in fifteenth-century northern Italy.

### Verdict

**[Legend as history; documented esoteric myth]** Historically unsupported, but foundational for occult tarot. Court’s own text is the source, not an older Egyptian manuscript. [Court’s 1781 essay](https://www.tarock.info/gebelin.htm)

## Romani or “Gypsy” origin

### Claim

Roma preserved tarot from Egypt or India and carried it into Europe.

### History of claim

Court’s Egyptomania and the European misconception that Roma came from Egypt provided the framework. Nineteenth-century writers such as Jean-Alexandre Vaillant and Papus spread it. Papus called tarot the “Bible of the Bohemians.”

### Evidence

Roma reached western Europe around the period tarot emerged, but no contemporary fifteenth-century source credits them with inventing or introducing *trionfi*. The earliest tarot documents are court, civic and merchant records from northern Italy.

### Verdict

**[Legend]** The narrative conflates Romani fortune-telling traditions, the exonym “Gypsy,” and the later occult tarot. It also helped stereotype Roma as hereditary occult outsiders.

## Cathar origin

### Claim

The trumps secretly preserve Cathar or dualist teachings, with the Devil representing the evil creator of matter.

### Evidence offered

- tarot arose in regions once affected by medieval heresy;
- an apparently dualistic series of Pope/Devil, virtues/vices, earthly/celestial images;
- speculative readings of watermarks and artisan brotherhoods.

### Counterevidence

- the geographical overlap is broad and temporally separated;
- no Cathar text mentions tarot;
- no fifteenth-century tarot commission identifies a Cathar program;
- Christian imperial, papal and Last Judgment imagery is not distinctly Cathar;
- the cards emerged in elite Catholic courts and ordinary workshops.

### Verdict

**[Disputed modern theory]** Possible thematic comparison, but no documentary chain of transmission.

## Templar origin and Baphomet

### Medieval layer

“Baphomet” appears as a medieval form related to the name Mahomet/Muhammad in some contexts. During the suppression of the Templars beginning in 1307, witnesses gave inconsistent accounts of a head, idol or figure.

### Lévi’s layer

Lévi combined:

- the Templar accusation;
- Goat of Mendes;
- Sabbath goat;
- alchemical androgyne;
- Hermetic polarity.

### Later confusion

Popular accounts reverse the evidentiary direction: they see Lévi’s 1856 picture, call it an authentic Templar Baphomet, then treat Waite’s 1909 Devil as proof that tarot had always carried a Templar secret.

### Verdict

**[Documented accusation → nineteenth-century occult synthesis → modern legend]** No medieval object matching Lévi’s goat is authenticated, and no evidence connects Templars to fifteenth-century tarot.

## “The Devil was absent from the earliest tarot”

There are three separate propositions:

1. No Devil survives in Visconti luxury decks — **true**.
2. Those decks originally had no Devil — **unproved**.
3. No fifteenth-century tarot had a Devil — **false or at least too strong**, because the sermon names it and printed-sheet dating may reach the fifteenth century.

The likely explanation is preservation bias: luxury packs survive fragmentarily; cheap printed sheets survive accidentally as binder’s waste.

## Baphomet’s pentagram

- Lévi’s illustrated Baphomet has an upright pentagram.
- Lévi textually contrasts upright and inverted pentagrams.
- Waite-Smith puts a reversed pentagram on the Devil’s forehead.
- Later copies often claim Lévi pictured it there first.

**Verdict:** The forehead placement and inversion are Waite-Smith’s synthesis, not a literal copy of Lévi’s plate.

## Loose chains

Modern readers commonly say the couple can remove their chains.

**[Visual inference]** Smith drew the neck loops unusually wide.

**[Textual caution]** Waite emphasizes “chain and fatality,” while also saying the Devil will not be their master forever. He does not plainly state that the pair can remove the chains at will. That explicit lesson was amplified by later writers.

## Marseille anatomy

Claims include breasts, penis, vulva, face on belly, eyes on knees and additional faces.

- Some of these marks are visibly present in particular impressions.
- Block wear, stencil displacement and copying can alter them.
- Decks differ.
- A detail on Dodal cannot automatically be attributed to Noblet or Conver.

**Verdict:** Describe each impression; do not posit one invariant anatomical code.

## “Maison Dieu” means House of God

Literally it can mean House of God and historically could denote a charitable hospital. In tarot it became the title of XVI, but early parallel names include *La Foudre*, *Saetta*, *Fuoco*, Tower, and House of the Devil.

Claims that XVI is specifically Solomon’s Temple, Babel, a hospital, an alchemical furnace or a lightning-struck initiation tower are interpretations, not universally documented identities.

## Misprints and copying traditions

Woodblock tarot is especially vulnerable to:

- reversed images caused by copying printed cards rather than blocks;
- damaged numerals;
- omitted strokes;
- misspelled captions such as corrupted *Diable*;
- stencil color wandering outside lines;
- restoration of ambiguous marks as eyes or genitalia.

Modern “restorations” sometimes regularize these accidents and then explain the regularized detail as ancient symbolism.

# Open questions

1. Which surviving woodcut contains the earliest Devil: Budapest, Rothschild, Rosenwald, or another fragment? Present datings do not settle the question.

2. Did every early standard pack contain Devil and Tower? The sermon supports their early inclusion, but the missing Milanese cards prevent direct confirmation for each commissioned deck.

3. What exactly is the Budapest Devil’s abdominal feature: a second face, theatrical mask, costume fastening, or damage?

4. How much of the Marseille chained-pair composition was inherited from an unknown Italian model? The Cary and other fragments suggest transmission, but no complete intermediate deck survives.

5. Were the subordinate beings originally demons, damned souls, parody attendants, or personifications of corrupted humanity? Decks make them increasingly human over time; Waite is the first major author to identify them expressly with fallen Adam and Eve.

6. How much freedom did Pamela Colman Smith have over XV? Waite describes the finished design, but no surviving instruction sheet fully divides his iconographic decisions from hers.

7. Did Waite intend the loose chains to announce self-liberation? His wording permits that inference but does not state the familiar modern maxim.

8. Which Grand Etteilla state should be treated as the first definitive no.14 image? Surviving states, re-engravings and later “Egyptian” editions differ.

9. What exact readings belonged to ordinary fifteenth-century viewers? Christian demonology supplies context, but no cardmaker’s explanation survives.

10. Can specific lines in Calvino or Yeats be tied to XV rather than to tarot generally? This requires manuscript or authorial evidence beyond thematic resemblance.

11. No trace was found for a securely documented medieval building deliberately quoting the tarot Devil. Modern tarot environments such as Saint Phalle’s are explicit; alleged Templar or cathedral precedents are not.
