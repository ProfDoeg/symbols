# Judgement: Research Dossier

> **Evidence labels**
>
> - **[FACT]** directly documented by a surviving card, archival record, early printed text, manuscript, or author’s own publication.
> - **[RECONSTRUCTION]** inference accepted or seriously proposed by card historians.
> - **[TRADITION]** inherited convention whose exact origin may be uncertain.
> - **[ESOTERIC]** correspondence asserted within an occult system, not a claim about the card’s historical origin.
> - **[DISPUTED]** attribution or interpretation for which significant alternatives exist.
> - **[LEGEND]** historical-origin story unsupported by surviving evidence.
> - **[MODERN]** twentieth- or twenty-first-century redesign or interpretation.

### A necessary distinction

**[FACT]** Judgement belongs to the standard 22-card trump series, but Marziano da Tortona’s earlier game for Filippo Maria Visconti—described around 1418–1425—had sixteen classical gods, not Judgement. It is a precursor to tarot’s trump principle, not evidence for this particular card.

**[FACT]** The standard historical subject was the Christian General Resurrection/Last Judgement. The name **L’Angelo** describes its most conspicuous game-card figure. “Judgement,” “awakening,” “rebirth,” and “the Aeon” are successive theological, occult, psychological, and Thelemic framings—not interchangeable original titles.

**[FACT]** “The Spirit of the Primal Fire” does not mean that Golden Dawn sources ambiguously assigned the card to both Fire and a separate element called Spirit. Its elemental attribution is **Fire**; “Spirit” occurs in its formal grade-title.

## Names, numbering, and regional terminology

### Italy

**[FACT]** Fifteenth-century documents call the new packs *carte da trionfi* or simply *trionfi*. The term *tarocchi* appears around the opening of the sixteenth century and gradually displaced *trionfi* as the game’s special name.

**[FACT]** Early painted Italian trumps generally have neither printed numbers nor captions. “XX” is therefore an anachronistic label when applied physically to the Cary–Yale or Visconti–Sforza cards, though convenient for identifying their later place in the Marseille order.

**[FACT]** The *Sermo de ludo* calls the card **Angelo**, placing it twentieth in its written list, after the Sun and before Justice and the World. That list reflects a Ferrarese or “eastern” order in which Justice was raised above the Angel.

**[TRADITION]** Piedmontese and several Italian game packs continued to prefer **L’Angelo** over *Il Giudizio*. In Bolognese play the Angel was the highest surviving trump, even after the numbered sequence and imagery were reduced or transformed.

### France

**[FACT]** Marseille-pattern cards print **XX LE JUGEMENT**. The so-called Tarot de Marseille pattern existed before that collective name: card historian Romain Merlin used *Tarot de Marseille* in 1856, and later occultists and Paul Marteau helped establish it as the conventional label. It does not mean that tarot itself originated in Marseille.

**[FACT]** Besançon-pattern packs replace the Papess and Pope with **Juno and Jupiter**, principally to avoid Catholic figures in Protestant or mixed-confessional markets. Judgement normally remains Judgement.

**[FACT]** The Tower’s title *La Maison Dieu*, the unnamed XIII, and the Fool’s lack of a sequence number illustrate how Marseille labels cannot simply be translated as a uniform set of literal scene descriptions.

### English, Spanish, and German

**[FACT]** English occult decks usually use *Judgement* or *The Last Judgment*. Waite printed “The Last Judgement” in his discussion, while surviving Rider/Waite/Smith cards commonly read **JUDGEMENT**.

**[FACT]** Spanish editions conventionally use *El Juicio*; German occult decks use *Das Gericht* or *Das Jüngste Gericht*. Historical German Tarock packs often replaced Italian allegories with animals, genre scenes, or national subjects, so a trump numbered XX need not depict Judgement at all.

**[FACT]** In modern French Tarot Nouveau used for play, trump 20 is a nineteenth-century genre scene, not the resurrection. Number and game function survived after the medieval subject disappeared.

## Iconography, deck by deck

### Christian source-image

**[FACT]** The card draws on the medieval and Renaissance iconography of Matthew 24:31, 1 Corinthians 15:52, 1 Thessalonians 4:16, and Revelation 20:11–15: a supernatural trumpet announces the resurrection of the dead before final judgment.

Common visual precedents include:

- Christ enthroned or half-length above;
- angels blowing long trumpets;
- a cross-bearing resurrection banner;
- naked bodies emerging from graves;
- Christ’s sword and lily, or sword and globe;
- the blessed and damned separated in larger church compositions.

**[RECONSTRUCTION]** The card compresses a vast church-wall or manuscript scene into a playing-card emblem. It is unnecessary to posit a secret initiatory source: Last Judgement frescoes, portals, panel paintings, and Books of Hours were ubiquitous.

**[DISPUTED]** The trumpeter is often called Gabriel. Western art and later popular tradition assign the Last Trump to Gabriel, but the relevant biblical passages do not name him. Early cards themselves are unlabeled. “Gabriel” is therefore traditional identification, not a securely documented portrait caption.

### Marziano–Michelino pack, about 1418–1425

**[FACT]** Marziano da Tortona described sixteen deified heroes or classical gods arranged in four groups; Michelino da Besozzo painted the cards. No cards survive.

**Finding:** Judgement was not among them. Its relevance is structural: the gods functioned as permanently superior cards. Calling it the earliest “Judgement deck” or a normal 78-card tarot is incorrect.

### Cary–Yale / Visconti di Modrone, Milan, about 1441–1445

**[FACT]** Judgement survives among eleven trumps. Above the resurrecting dead, two angels sound trumpets; a radiant dove or descending spiritual emblem appears in the heavenly zone. Figures below rise from earth or tombs, and heraldic/personal details distinguish the image from later anonymous popular cards.

**[DISPUTED]** The pack’s commission is frequently tied to the 1441 marriage of Bianca Maria Visconti and Francesco Sforza. Heraldry supports Visconti court patronage, but the precise occasion is reconstructed.

**[DISPUTED]** Attempts to identify individual resurrected figures as Visconti or Sforza family members are ingenious but unproven. No contemporary key names them.

### Brera–Brambilla, Milan, approximately 1440s

**[FACT]** The surviving fragment is mostly suit and court cards. No securely surviving Judgement card is known.

**Finding:** It supplies evidence for luxurious early Milanese tarot manufacture, not deck-specific evidence for Judgement’s appearance.

### Visconti–Sforza / Colleoni–Baglioni, Milan or Cremona, about 1450–1460

**[FACT]** Morgan M.630.14 shows a very different celestial authority from Marseille: a crowned, half-length **Christ** holds a sword and a cross-surmounted tripartite globe. Two flanking angels blow bannered trumpets. Below, resurrected figures emerge from graves; two raise joined hands. The Morgan catalog dates the main group approximately 1450–1480, while recent exhibition cataloguing favors about 1456–1458 for much of it. [Morgan object record](https://ica.themorgan.org/manuscript/page/14/158525)

**Meaning of the change:** later popular patterns remove Christ as visible judge and allow the trumpet angel to dominate. What becomes “the Angel” was originally part of a fuller Christological Judgement.

**[DISPUTED]** Bonifacio Bembo is the traditional artist. Francesco Zavattari and other workshop attributions have been proposed. The Morgan distinguishes original cards from stylistically different additions but acknowledges continuing disagreement. [Morgan collection catalogue](https://www.themorgan.org/manuscript/158525)

### Este/Ferrarese cards, late fifteenth century

**[FACT]** Ferrarese fragments and related luxury cards establish a vigorous local tarot culture, but the surviving groups do not yield a single uncontested Judgement card comparable in completeness to the Milanese examples.

**[RECONSTRUCTION]** The *Sermo* order—Angel, Justice, World—probably reflects the Ferrarese/eastern family. Here Judgement’s theological neighbor, Justice, becomes the penultimate trump, making divine judgment an implied two-card sequence.

### Charles VI / Gringonneur group, probably Ferrara, late fifteenth century

**[FACT]** The surviving Judgement presents a crowded, animated resurrection: multiple souls emerge from tombs while trumpeting angels occupy the upper register. At least one trumpet carries a cross banner.

**[DISPUTED]** The old attribution to Jacquemin Gringonneur and Charles VI of France is rejected by modern scholarship; style and costume point to northern Italy, usually Ferrara. “Charles VI Tarot” remains a collection-name, not reliable provenance.

### Sola Busca, Venice/Ferrara cultural orbit, 1491

**[FACT]** The Sola Busca is the earliest complete surviving 78-card tarot and the first surviving deck to illustrate all numbered suit cards with narrative scenes. Its trumps are Romanizing warriors and named historical or pseudo-historical figures rather than the standard ecclesiastical sequence.

**Finding:** it has no direct standard Judgement image. Its importance to Judgement is indirect: Pamela Colman Smith demonstrably borrowed several minor-card compositions from the Sola Busca, but no comparable borrowing is needed to explain her Judgement.

### Mantegna “Tarocchi,” about 1465–1475

**[FACT]** These are two sets of fifty educational engravings—social estates, Apollo and Muses, liberal arts, virtues, cosmic spheres—not a playable tarot. They include theological and cosmic ascent imagery but no standard Judgement trump.

**Finding:** relevant to the intellectual environment of allegorical series, not an ancestor-deck containing card XX.

### Rosenwald sheets, Florence, about 1500

**[FACT]** The Rosenwald group consists of uncut woodcut sheets from a popular Florentine tarot. The crude, economical technique contrasts with courtly painted packs and documents mass reproduction.

**[RECONSTRUCTION]** Its high trumps belong to the southern/type-A tradition, in which the Angel ranks above the World. The scene reduces the Last Judgement to immediately recognizable elements: trumpet, celestial messenger, rising bodies.

### Budapest sheets, northern Italy, late fifteenth or early sixteenth century

**[FACT]** Fragmentary woodcut sheets preserve early popular-card designs. Judgement, where reconstructed from the fragments and related patterns, belongs to the angel-and-resurrection family.

**Caution:** modern reconstructed decks sometimes complete missing lines or cards. Those additions are editorial, not surviving Budapest evidence.

### Minchiate, Florence, sixteenth–nineteenth centuries

**[FACT]** Minchiate expands the pack to 97 cards and forty numbered trumps, adding the remaining virtues, elements, zodiac signs, and other subjects.

**[FACT]** Judgement’s iconographic descendant is commonly called **Le Trombe** (“The Trumpets”) and stands near the summit. In the standard Florentine order, the highest five cards or *arie* are the Star, Moon, Sun, World, and Trumpets; the Trumpets is trump 40 and the highest.

**Meaning:** the title emphasizes the sounding instruments and its game rank rather than a doctrinal verdict. Multiple angels and resurrecting figures preserve the Last Judgement theme.

### Bolognese Tarocchino

**[FACT]** The Bolognese pack reduces the ordinary suits but retains 22 trumps. Its highest trump is the **Angel**, ranking above the World. Several low trumps are equal-ranking *papi*.

**[FACT]** Giuseppe Maria Mitelli’s seventeenth-century Bolognese designs freely transform traditional scenes while retaining game identities. The Angel remains the eschatological climax.

**Meaning:** Judgement is not “XX” in the Marseille sense during actual Bolognese play; it is the unnumbered or differently marked highest trump.

### Sicilian Tarot

**[FACT]** The distinctive Sicilian pack and game reorganize and rename several trumps, notably inserting *Miseria* at the bottom and altering conventional figures. The Angel/Last Judgement remains among the high trumps but belongs to a local order derived partly from southern Italian practice.

**Caution:** surviving Sicilian packs vary, and equivalence with Marseille XX should be given by subject, not assumed solely from printed numbering.

### Jean Noblet, Paris, about 1650

**[FACT]** Noblet’s **XX LE IUGEMENT** establishes the familiar French compression: a single winged angel emerges from clouds and sounds a long trumpet with a cross-bearing banner. Below, three principal human figures rise from rectangular graves—a central figure seen chiefly from behind between an adult man and woman.

**Meaning:** Christ the Judge has disappeared; the divine summons is concentrated in the angel and trumpet. The three-person group made familial and triadic occult interpretations possible.

### Jean Dodal, Lyon, about 1701–1715

**[FACT]** Dodal retains the angel, cross banner, cloud, and three resurrected figures. His vigorous cutting gives the figures conspicuous gestures and facial expressions. The title is **LE IUGEMENT**.

**[RECONSTRUCTION]** Noblet and Dodal are conventionally grouped as Marseille Type I, though they are not copies of one another.

### Nicolas Conver, Marseille, 1760/1761

**[FACT]** Conver’s Type-II formulation stabilizes the form best known through later Marseille decks: angel above, trumpet descending diagonally, cross flag, blue cloud zone, and three flesh-colored resurrected people below.

**[FACT]** The central figure’s back remains turned; the flanking figures face inward/upward. Claims that their asymmetric shapes encode hidden sexual anatomy, alchemical vessels, or additional faces are modern visual readings without documentary confirmation.

### Besançon pattern, eighteenth–nineteenth centuries

**[FACT]** The Judgement design remains substantially Marseille-derived. The conspicuous confessional substitutions occur at II and V—Juno and Jupiter in place of Papess and Pope—not at XX.

### Grand Etteilla, Paris, 1789 onward

**[FACT]** Etteilla reorganized the full pack as a deliberately divinatory “Book of Thoth.” The cards carry upright and reversed keywords and astrological or cosmological signs.

**Crucial identification:** ordinary Marseille Judgement does **not** remain card 20 in the same functional place. In Etteilla’s reordered sequence, recognizable Marseille trumps receive new numbers and meanings. Later Grand Etteilla editions vary and should not be conflated.

**[FACT]** Etteilla card 20 in early series can be a human significator—often a countryman or “good and severe man”—rather than the traditional Last Judgement. Conversely, the traditional Judgement image occurs elsewhere in the Etteilla numbering, usually associated with a legal or evaluative field.

**Meaning:** Etteilla breaks the presumed identity “number XX = Judgement.” His system is a total re-authoring, not merely a Marseille deck with annotations.

### Oswald Wirth, 1889

**[FACT]** Wirth designed twenty-two occult trumps under the direction of Stanislas de Guaita. Judgement retains the angel, trumpet, cross, and human triad but is geometrically regularized and supplied with occult-Hebrew framing.

**[ESOTERIC]** Wirth treated resurrection as regeneration and spiritual awakening. His late *Le Tarot des imagiers du Moyen Âge* spread this initiatory reading, even though the image remained Christian in form.

### Golden Dawn manuscript designs, approximately 1888–1900

**[FACT]** Golden Dawn teaching assigns Judgement to **Shin, Fire, and the 31st path from Hod to Malkuth**, with the title **The Spirit of the Primal Fire**.

**[FACT]** Surviving members’ cards and instructions were not one uniformly printed official deck. Later “Golden Dawn decks” are reconstructions:

- Robert Wang’s *Golden Dawn Tarot* (1977) visually codifies Israel Regardie’s published material.
- Sandra Tabatha and Chic Cicero’s *Golden Dawn Magical Tarot* (2000) is another informed reconstruction.
- Differences between them do not prove different nineteenth-century canonical originals.

**[ESOTERIC]** The traditional three figures may be arranged to suggest the three prongs of Shin. Crowley later explicitly read their bent arms as forming Shin; whether the medieval designers intended this is unsupported.

### Waite–Smith, London, 1909

**[FACT]** Pamela Colman Smith draws a blond, red-winged angel emerging from a cloud, blowing a golden trumpet. A white rectangular flag bears a red equal-armed cross. Naked gray-blue adults and children rise from coffins floating amid dark water; distant icy or mountainous forms close the horizon.

**What changed:**

- Marseille’s three figures become a larger resurrected humanity.
- Coffins stand in water rather than simply in earth.
- The central family is repeated in the distance.
- The cross banner is unusually clean and emphatic.
- The scene becomes panoramic and universal rather than compact.

Waite wrote that the added figures illustrate “the insufficiency of current explanations” and that the card records completion of inner transformation. His own description says the summons is “heard and answered from within.” [Waite, *Pictorial Key*, 1910](https://staging.sacred-texts.com/book/the-pictorial-key-to-the-tarot/reader/xx-the-last-judgement)

**[RECONSTRUCTION]** Smith’s immediate structural source was the Marseille/Golden Dawn Last Judgement. Her exposure to stage design, Symbolism, Catholic imagery, and the British Museum’s Sola Busca informed the deck generally. No specific Sola Busca card supplies the Judgement composition.

**[DISPUTED]** The water is read variously as universal resurrection, the sea giving up its dead (Revelation 20:13), subconscious depth, or simply compositional separation. Waite does not conclusively assign it one exclusive meaning.

### Paul Foster Case / BOTA, 1920s onward

**[FACT]** Case and artist Jessie Burns Parke retain the Waite–Smith family, coffins, angel, flag, sea, and Shin. BOTA cards were issued in black-and-white for students to color according to Case’s instructions.

**[ESOTERIC]** Case calls the angel Gabriel but distinguishes the key from literal end-time dogma. It signifies completion of the Great Work, realization of immortality, and consciousness of a “fourth dimension.” He reads the man, woman, and child through the magical IAO formula—Isis, Apophis, Osiris.

### Grimaud / Paul Marteau, 1930 edition

**[FACT]** Marteau’s *Ancien Tarot de Marseille*, published by Grimaud, regularized a Conver-derived pattern using strong flat colors. It became one of the twentieth century’s most widely reproduced Marseille decks.

**Meaning:** its apparent “antiquity” is partly a modern editorial stabilization. It is not a photographic facsimile of one pristine medieval original.

### Thoth Tarot, painted 1938–1943; first complete commercial publication 1969

**[FACT]** Crowley and Frieda Harris replace Judgement with **XX THE AEON**. Around the upper edge curves the starry body of Nuit. Hadit appears as a winged globe or point of fire. Ra-Hoor-Khuit/Horus appears in radiant, transparent, enthroned or standing form. At the bottom, a stylized **Shin** contains three human figures. A faint Libra glyph anticipates a future aeon.

Crowley says the traditional card had to be abandoned “in order to carry on that tradition.” He reads the old cross flag as the Osirian aeon and the new card as an adaptation of the Stele of Revealing. [Crowley, *The Book of Thoth*, “XX. The Aeon”](https://hermetic.com/crowley/book-of-thoth/atu)

**[ESOTERIC]** Crowley held that the Aeon of Horus began with his reception of *Liber AL vel Legis* in Cairo in April 1904. Nuit is unlimited possibility, Hadit the infinitely contracted point/energy, and their issue Heru-ra-ha, whose active form is Ra-Hoor-Khuit and passive form Hoor-paar-kraat.

**Meaning of the redesign:** external divine trial and bodily resurrection become a cosmic-historical change of magical formula. The old dead are not merely acquitted; humanity enters another aeon.

### Camoin–Jodorowsky, 1997

**[MODERN]** Philippe Camoin and Alejandro Jodorowsky presented their deck as a restoration of an authentic Marseille tradition, reconstructing lines and colors by comparing old packs. Judgement keeps the three figures and trumpet angel but uses newly standardized details and a richer palette.

**[DISPUTED]** Its claim to recover a single original esoteric code is not accepted as historical demonstration. It is an influential interpretive restoration, not a facsimile.

**[ESOTERIC/MODERN]** In *The Way of Tarot*, Jodorowsky and Marianne Costa treat XX as an irresistible call, vocation, healing of ancestry, emergence into a new consciousness, and a triad that can represent family relationships. These readings strongly shaped contemporary Marseille practice.

### Notable modern redesigns

- **Aquarian Tarot, David Palladini (1970):** Art Deco angel and simplified rising figures; emphasizes visionary awakening.
- **Motherpeace, Vicki Noble and Karen Vogel (1981):** renamed or reframed patriarchal judgment through feminist, circular, communal transformation.
- **Haindl Tarot, Hermann Haindl (1980s):** cosmic, ecological renewal rather than juridical condemnation.
- **Wild Unknown, Kim Krans (2012):** a bird rising or responding to light replaces the Christian graveyard; “awakening” is retained while doctrine is removed.
- **Modern queer, decolonial, and non-Christian decks:** frequently replace Gabriel, graves, and resurrection with ancestral recall, political reckoning, transition, community witness, or ecological regeneration.

These are **[MODERN]** transformations, not recoveries of an otherwise lost fifteenth-century meaning.

## The game

### Fundamental function

**[FACT]** Judgement was created as a permanent trump in a trick-taking game. It was not originally a fortune-telling card.

A trump beats cards of the four ordinary suits, subject to following-suit rules. Within the trump suit, higher-ranked trumps beat lower ones. This mechanical fact precedes every documented occult correspondence.

### Rank by regional order

Dummett grouped early orders into three broad families:

| Order | Principal region | Highest sequence | Judgement/Angel |
|---|---|---|---|
| Type A, “southern” | Bologna, Florence; later Rome and Sicily | … Sun, World, **Angel** | Highest trump |
| Type B, “eastern” | Ferrara; probably Venice | … Sun, **Angel, Justice, World** | Third from top |
| Type C, “western” | Milan; later France/Marseille | … Sun, **Angel, World** | Second from top, later XX |
| Mixed A/C | Piedmont and border traditions | virtues arranged more like C, but Angel may outrank World | Variable |

Dummett’s concise formulation is that Death through Sun remain strikingly stable, while “the positions of World and Angel are sometimes reversed.” [Translation and discussion of Dummett, *Il Mondo e l’Angelo*](https://dummettsmondo.blogspot.com/2015/07/chapters-6-and-7-game-and-its-trump.html)

**[RECONSTRUCTION]** The lack of numbers on early cards allowed local players to preserve different memorized orders. The three-order classification is strongly evidenced by rules and lists; the explanation of exactly how divergence arose remains reconstruction.

### Justice and Strength

**[FACT]** Waite’s exchange of Justice and Strength—Strength VIII and Justice XI—belongs to Golden Dawn-influenced occult ordering, not the historical game’s universal order.

Regional game orders already placed the virtues differently:

- Type A grouped Temperance, Fortitude, and Justice.
- Type B separated them and promoted Justice to just above the Angel.
- Type C placed Temperance just above Death while retaining Angel immediately below World.

Thus there was never one universally original numbered position for Justice or Fortitude recoverable from all fifteenth-century play.

### The Fool

**[FACT]** The Fool was ordinarily outside the numbered trump sequence. In classic tarot play it functions as the **Excuse**: it may be played instead of following suit and is generally reclaimed by its owner, who compensates the trick’s winner with another card.

**[RECONSTRUCTION]** Dummett considered this likely an early function, but the rules of the first fifteenth-century games do not survive. Statements that the Fool was certainly always zero, always the lowest trump, or always a mystical traveler through 21 stages exceed the evidence.

### Point value

In many traditional games Judgement/Angel is a high trump but not automatically one of the three universal *oudlers* or “honours” of modern French tarot—that trio is trump 1, trump 21, and the Excuse.

Its scoring value therefore depends on the game:

- In French tarot, trump XX is an ordinary trump for card-point purposes, worth **½ point** when paired in scoring, although tactically very powerful.
- In Bolognese Tarocchino the Angel belongs to the highest scoring combination and is the supreme trick-taking trump.
- In Minchiate the Trumpets is the highest trump and one of the valuable *arie*.
- In Tarock games using French-suited packs, trump XX normally has its printed rank but may carry no Judgement image.
- Local declaration, sequence, and combination bonuses matter more than an imagined universal “Judgement value.”

## Chronology of attestation

### About 1418–1425: Marziano’s game

**[FACT]** Marziano da Tortona describes a commissioned pack painted by Michelino da Besozzo for Filippo Maria Visconti. Sixteen classical gods act as superior cards. The description survives through Jacopo Antonio Marcello’s 1449 transmission.

**Finding:** neither Judgement nor the standard 22-trump sequence is named.

### February–July 1442: Ferrara ledgers

**[FACT]** Este court accounts pay the painter Sagramoro for four packs of *carte da trionfi* and record other packs bought for members of the ruling family. This is the earliest secure use of *trionfi* for the tarot-like court game. [Fondazione Estense account](https://rivista.fondazioneestense.it/1996/4/item/407-la-carta-del-principe.html)

**Finding:** the ledgers name the pack, not its individual Angel/Judgement card.

### 1440s–1450s: surviving cards

**[FACT]** The Cary–Yale and Visconti–Sforza Judgements establish the angel, trumpet, resurrected dead, and—in the latter—Christ as judge.

### Late fifteenth century: popular sheets

**[FACT]** Rosenwald, Budapest, and related woodcuts show the migration from unique court objects to reproducible popular cards.

### Approximately 1490–1500: *Sermo de ludo cum aliis*

**[FACT]** The anonymous anti-gambling sermon gives the earliest nearly complete named list:

*bagatella, imperatrix, imperator, papessa, papa, temperantia, amore, caro triumphalis, fortitudo, rota, gobbo, impiccato, morte, diavolo, sagitta, stella, luna, sol, angelus, justicia, mundus.*

Its commentary condemns the use of sacred figures in a game. [Surviving manuscript-page reproduction](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Sermones_de_Ludo_cum_Aliis.jpg)

Judgement is **Angelus**, not *Iudicium*.

### Early sixteenth century: *tarocchi*

**[FACT]** The word *tarocchi* becomes established. Literary *tarocchi appropriati* assign trump names to people, especially women at court.

**[FACT]** Teofilo Folengo uses tarot imagery in *Chaos del Tri per uno* and macaronic writing. Such texts demonstrate familiarity with the conventional trumps; they do not prove an ancient occult initiation system.

### 1520s–1540s: appropriated-tarot poems

**[FACT]** Poems and social games attach named people or character traits to the cards. The Angel can suggest blessedness, proclamation, or an elevated conclusion.

**Caution:** “the 1500 poems” is not one single canonical document, and dating individual anonymous compositions can be uncertain.

### 1565: Francesco Piscina

**[FACT]** Piscina’s *Discorso sopra l’ordine delle figure de’ tarocchi* provides an early printed moral interpretation and a western/Piedmontese order. Its importance is evidence of reflective Christian moralization during the playing-card era—not proof of occult Kabbalah.

### 1637: Abbé de Marolles

**[FACT]** Marolles records a French tarot order with the Angel near the top, assisting reconstruction of pre-numbered French play.

### About 1650–1760: Marseille stabilization

Noblet, Dodal, Madenié, Chosson, Payen, Conver and others transmit **Le Jugement XX** with an angel and three principal resurrected figures.

### 1781: Court de Gébelin and Comte de Mellet

**[FACT]** Volume VIII of *Le Monde primitif* launches the Egyptian-tarot theory. Gébelin sees tarot as the remains of an Egyptian sacred book; de Mellet supplies a different essay, divinatory claims, and Hebrew-letter arrangements.

**[FACT]** Their correspondences conflict. In arrangements beginning the alphabet with the World or Magician and moving through the cards in different directions, Judgement does not consistently receive the later Golden Dawn Shin attribution.

### 1783–1791: Etteilla

**[FACT]** Etteilla develops the first sustained published system of tarot divination and releases a purpose-built divinatory pack around 1789. His reordering severs Marseille number XX from a stable Judgement identity.

### 1854–1856: Éliphas Lévi

**[FACT]** *Dogme et rituel de la haute magie* makes the 22 trumps a Hebrew-Kabbalistic alphabet. Because Lévi begins the alphabet with the Magician rather than an initial zero-Fool sequence, XX Judgement corresponds to **Resh**, not Shin.

Lévi’s later *Sanctum Regnum* gloss gives the card “the generative force of the earth, vegetable life, and eternal life.” [1896 English edition, p. 77](https://biglevi.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/03/Magical-Ritual-Of-The-Sanctum-Regnum-E-Levi-1896.pdf)

### 1863–1870: Paul Christian

**[FACT]** Jean-Baptiste Pitois, writing as Paul Christian, elaborates the Egyptian initiation narrative and popularizes “Major” and “Minor Arcana.” Judgement becomes a station of resurrection in an alleged temple initiation.

**[LEGEND]** His detailed Egyptian chambers and initiation route are literary-occult constructions, not translations of Egyptian documents.

### 1888–1889: Mathers, Golden Dawn, Papus, Wirth

- Mathers publishes an English tarot divination manual in 1888.
- The Golden Dawn develops systematic letter, path, color, astrology, and elemental assignments.
- Papus’s *Le Tarot des Bohémiens* (1889) uses **Resh/Saturn**, following a Lévi-style ordering rather than the Golden Dawn’s Shin/Fire.
- Wirth publishes his 22 occult trumps in 1889.

### 1909–1910: Waite–Smith

**[FACT]** Deck published in 1909; Waite’s explanatory *Pictorial Key* follows in 1910.

### 1904 and 1938–1943: Crowley’s Aeon

**[ESOTERIC]** Crowley dates the Aeon of Horus to the 1904 reception of *Liber AL*.

**[FACT]** Harris painted the Thoth series between 1938 and 1943. *The Book of Thoth* appeared in 1944; the complete deck was first commercially published in 1969.

### Conflicts

1. **Resh versus Shin:** Lévi, Papus, and Wirth commonly give Judgement **Resh**; Golden Dawn, Waite’s initiatory background, Case, and Crowley give it **Shin**.
2. **Saturn versus Fire:** Papus pairs Resh with Saturn; Golden Dawn pairs Shin with elemental Fire.
3. **Fire versus Pluto:** Pluto is a modern astrological overlay, not Golden Dawn doctrine.
4. **Literal Last Judgement versus inner transformation:** medieval imagery concerns Christian resurrection; Waite inwardizes it; Crowley replaces its theology.
5. **The Tzaddi/Heh issue:** Crowley’s “Tzaddi is not the Star” exchange affects **The Emperor and The Star**, not Judgement. XX remains Shin/Fire in both Golden Dawn and Crowley.
6. **Pips and courts:** the requested decan and court-sub-element information is inapplicable to Judgement. Those Golden Dawn assignments concern minor cards: numbered pips receive decans and titles; courts receive sub-elements and zodiacal spans. Trump XX receives a path and elemental attribution instead.

### Textual cautions

**[FACT]** Waite gives two kinds of meaning: a symbolic exposition and short fortune-telling keywords. Treating the latter as his entire interpretation misses his explicit statement that the trumpet sounds within.

**[FACT]** Crowley’s practice does not support attaching an automatic negative “reversed Aeon” meaning. Many Thoth readers do not use reversed cards at all.

**[FACT]** Etteilla’s numeration must be checked edition by edition. A list silently mapping his number 20 to Marseille XX is unreliable.

## Esoteric and symbolic readings

### Christian

**[FACT]** This is the earliest recoverable semantic layer: bodily resurrection and divine judgment at the Last Trump. The dead rise because Christ has conquered death. The image is simultaneously terrifying and hopeful; salvation and condemnation belong to the full church scene even where the small card omits the damned.

Spreaders: cardmakers, Christian visual culture, anti-gambling sermons, and moralizing writers of the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries.

### Hermetic and Egyptian

**[ESOTERIC]** Gébelin reframed the whole pack as Egyptian sacred wisdom in 1781. Etteilla converted that hypothesis into an operational divinatory and cosmological system.

For Judgement, resurrection becomes restoration of hidden knowledge, initiation, and nature’s renewal.

**Evidence:** published occult texts beginning in 1781.  
**Evidence for ancient Egyptian origin:** none.

### Kabbalistic

**[ESOTERIC]** Lévi’s major innovation was to correlate 22 trumps with 22 Hebrew letters. His order made Judgement Resh. Golden Dawn restructured the alphabet so the Fool receives Aleph and Judgement receives Shin.

The Golden Dawn 31st path descends between Hod and Malkuth. Fire is the transforming divine force entering manifestation. The trumpet becomes the magical Word awakening matter.

**Historical finding:** no fifteenth-century card, rule, sermon, or ledger mentions Shin, Resh, the Tree of Life, or 22 Hebrew paths.

### Astrological

- **Papus/Wirth lineage:** Resh–Saturn, emphasizing cyclical renewal, time, and determinate motion.
- **Golden Dawn:** elemental Fire, not a planet or zodiac sign.
- **Modern popular astrology:** Pluto, because of death/rebirth symbolism.

**[MODERN INVENTION]** Pluto cannot be an early or Golden Dawn attribution; it was discovered in 1930.

### Alchemical

**[ESOTERIC]** Nineteenth- and twentieth-century authors read the rising dead as matter regenerated after putrefaction, often paralleling *rubedo*, resurrection of the philosophical body, or completion of the Great Work.

Lévi’s generative earth and vegetable life provide an early occult bridge. Waite explicitly calls the card accomplishment of “the great work of transformation.” Case and later authors make the alchemical implication more systematic.

**Finding:** no known fifteenth-century source calls Judgement an alchemical operation.

### Thelemic

**[ESOTERIC]** Crowley rejects the Osirian resurrection formula as obsolete. The Aeon announces Horus:

- Nuit: infinite possibility or circumference;
- Hadit: the infinitesimal omnipresent point and energy;
- Heru-ra-ha: their issue;
- Ra-Hoor-Khuit: active Crowned and Conquering Child;
- Hoor-paar-kraat: silent/passive form;
- Shin: primal Fire;
- three human figures: humanity arising within the new formula;
- Libra: foreshadowing a later aeon.

The claim that 1904 objectively inaugurated a cosmic age is religious doctrine within Thelema, not independently demonstrable history.

### Jungian and psychological

**[MODERN]** Jung did not publish a canonical card-by-card tarot system. “Jungian Judgement” was developed by later tarot writers and Jung-oriented therapists.

Common readings include:

- the ego answering the Self;
- emergence of repressed material;
- integration of ancestral or family complexes;
- release from shame;
- vocation or calling;
- reviewing the life narrative without condemnation;
- individuation after the solar illumination of XIX.

**Caution:** calling this “Jung’s meaning” is a misattribution unless tied to a later interpreter. Sallie Nichols, Rachel Pollack, Mary K. Greer, and numerous post-1970 writers spread such readings.

### Literature

#### T. S. Eliot, *The Waste Land* (1922)

**[FACT]** Madame Sosostris names “the drowned Phoenician Sailor,” Belladonna, “the man with three staves,” the Wheel, and “the one-eyed merchant,” while stating that the Hanged Man is absent. She does **not** explicitly draw Judgement.

**Finding:** attempts to assign every passage of the poem to all 22 trumps are later interpretive schemes. Judgement is not a named textual card.

#### W. B. Yeats

**[FACT]** Yeats was immersed in Golden Dawn symbolism and knew its tarot correspondences. Tarot imagery belongs to the intellectual environment of his occult work.

**Finding:** no primary passage located in this research establishes Judgement XX as the exclusive source of a particular famous Yeats poem. General claims should not be promoted to direct quotation without manuscript evidence.

#### Italo Calvino, *The Castle of Crossed Destinies* (1969/1973)

**[FACT]** Calvino constructs narratives by arranging tarot cards, first using a Visconti-type pack and later a Marseille pack. Cards change semantic function according to their neighbors.

**[TRADITION/LITERARY INVENTION]** Judgement can act as proclamation, return, resurrection, revelation, or collective reckoning, but Calvino’s combinatorial method deliberately refuses one dictionary meaning.

### Film

#### Ingmar Bergman

**[FACT]** *The Seventh Seal* (1957) employs medieval death, judgment, plague, confession, apocalypse, and chess imagery.

**Finding:** it is often placed beside tarot’s Death and Judgement in modern criticism, but the film does not establish a direct quotation of card XX. The title comes from Revelation; visual resemblance is thematic unless a production source proves otherwise.

#### Alejandro Jodorowsky

**[FACT]** Tarot is central to Jodorowsky’s creative and spiritual practice. *The Holy Mountain* (1973) uses overt tarot, alchemical, planetary, Christian, and initiatory imagery; his later work with Camoin and Costa made Judgement a familial and vocational awakening.

**Caution:** not every trumpet, resurrection, or rebirth scene in his films is a one-to-one citation of XX.

### Paintings and buildings

**[FACT]** The historical dependency normally runs from monumental Last Judgements to tarot, not from tarot to the buildings:

- Giotto’s Last Judgement in the Arena Chapel;
- Orcagna/Nardo di Cione’s Florentine judgment imagery;
- Last Judgement portals of Gothic cathedrals;
- Fra Angelico’s Last Judgement panels;
- Michelangelo’s Sistine Chapel Last Judgement, later than the earliest tarot but influential on subsequent visual culture.

**[RECONSTRUCTION]** Early cardmakers drew upon the common iconographic vocabulary represented by such works, not necessarily one identifiable painting.

**Finding:** widely repeated claims that a specific cathedral façade “contains the tarot” generally rely on retrospective matching, not documents showing that its program encoded a 22-card sequence.

## Controversies and disputes

### Egyptian origin

**[LEGEND]** Court de Gébelin asserted in 1781 that tarot preserved an Egyptian sacred book. De Mellet and Etteilla elaborated the claim.

Evidence against it:

- standard tarot is documented in fifteenth-century northern Italy;
- its named imagery is Christian and European;
- Gébelin supplied no Egyptian manuscript or archaeological example;
- Egyptian hieroglyphs had not yet been deciphered;
- his proposed Egyptian etymologies were not based on hieroglyphic forms.

Dummett, Decker, and Depaulis traced the occult tradition textually in *A Wicked Pack of Cards*. Dummett noted that the invented etymologies cite no hieroglyphic evidence. [Dummett, “Origins of Tarot”](https://www.nybooks.com/articles/1981/05/14/origins-of-tarot/)

**Nuance:** European suit cards have important Mamluk/Islamic antecedents. That is not evidence that the tarot trumps were an ancient pharaonic Book of Thoth.

### Romani or “Gypsy” transmission

**[LEGEND]** Nineteenth-century writers—especially Jean-Alexandre Vaillant and Papus’s *Tarot des Bohémiens*—popularized the idea that Romani travelers carried tarot from Egypt.

Evidence against it:

- tarot’s early production is documented through Italian court accounts and urban cardmaking;
- the standard trumps emerge within Italian Christian civic culture;
- Romani linguistic and historical origins point to India, not ancient Egypt;
- no early Italian tarot document credits Romani transmission.

The old term “Gypsy” itself reflects the mistaken belief that Romani people came from Egypt.

### Cathar origin

**[LEGEND/DISPUTED]** Modern authors sometimes interpret the Papess, Devil, Tower, and resurrection sequence as covert Cathar doctrine or survival after the Albigensian Crusade.

Evidence:

- thematic parallels can be constructed between dualist religion and almost any salvation sequence.
- No Cathar tarot, Cathar rules, commission, workshop document, or continuous transmission exists.
- The earliest cards arose more than a century after the destruction of organized Catharism and use orthodox Last Judgement imagery.

Conclusion: a modern speculative interpretation, not reconstructable provenance.

### Templar origin

**[LEGEND]** Claims that Templars encoded their secrets in tarot after 1307 or that 22 trumps represent Templar degrees lack documentary support.

Chronological problem: the Templars were suppressed in 1312; standard tarot is first documented in the 1440s. No intermediate deck, rule, inventory, or Templar text closes that gap.

### Ancient Kabbalistic design

**[LEGEND as origin; ESOTERIC as later system]** The numerical match of 22 trumps and 22 Hebrew letters made correspondence attractive. The first documented sustained association is eighteenth/nineteenth-century French occultism.

Evidence against original design:

- early trump orders differ;
- cards were unnumbered;
- regional packs added or removed trumps;
- no fifteenth- or sixteenth-century source mentions Hebrew letters;
- Lévi/Papus and Golden Dawn assign different letters to Judgement.

The correspondence can be internally coherent as occult practice without being historically original.

### Is the angel Gabriel?

**[TRADITION/DISPUTED]** Gabriel is the customary trumpeting angel in later Western culture and is named by Case and many modern writers.

**FACT:** scripture’s Last Trump passages do not identify Gabriel, and early cards carry no angel-name. “An angel” is secure; “Gabriel” is traditional.

### Are the three Marseille figures father, mother, and child?

**[RECONSTRUCTION/TRADITION]** Their ages, bodies, and positions invite that reading. Waite explicitly calls them woman, man, and child; Papus likewise uses the triad.

**Caution:** the figures may simply represent resurrected humanity. Early Judgement scenes often show numerous unrelated dead. No Noblet or Dodal commentary identifies a biological family.

### Are they alchemical Salt, Sulphur, and Mercury—or Isis, Apophis, Osiris?

**[ESOTERIC]** Such triads belong to later occult interpretation. Case explicitly uses IAO. No evidence attributes them to the historical cardmakers.

### Does the central figure form Shin?

**[ESOTERIC]** Crowley says the raised arms of the traditional central figure form Shin and thus reveal Fire.

**Historical problem:** Shin was not associated with Judgement until Golden Dawn ordering. The resemblance can support Golden Dawn meditation but cannot demonstrate fifteenth-century intent.

### Judgement above the World

**[FACT]** This is not an error. In type-A southern orders, the Angel is the highest trump and World lies below it. In type-C Milanese/Marseille order, World is highest and Judgement becomes XX immediately below XXI.

**[RECONSTRUCTION]** The reversal may reflect different theological narratives: cosmic totality above judgment, or final resurrection after the passing of the world. Rules document the difference; motives are inferred.

### Misprints and editorial “restorations”

Several practices can harden accidents into tradition:

- worn blocks turn lines into symbolic “hidden” objects;
- reversed copying changes gesture direction;
- stenciling moves color outside outlines;
- publishers silently redraw damaged cards;
- modern restorers combine details from several historical decks;
- Conver-derived twentieth-century decks are marketed as if they reproduced an unchanged medieval original.

**[DISPUTED]** Claims that a particular stray line is a coded letter, bodily organ, phoenix, or alchemical instrument require comparison across independent impressions. A detail first appearing in one damaged block is weak evidence for original doctrine.

### Dummett and historical correction

Dummett’s central correction was methodological:

1. begin with dated cards, account books, rules, and trump lists;
2. reconstruct how the game works;
3. distinguish the fifteenth-century playing-card object from eighteenth-century occult literature;
4. trace each occult claim from its actual author.

Ronald Decker and Thierry Depaulis extended this work through art history and French documentary research. Stuart Kaplan assembled enormous visual and bibliographic corpora, though his encyclopedic inclusiveness sometimes reproduces claims that require separate evaluation. Robert M. Place and Helen Farley combine art-historical context with accessible cultural history; Farley is especially useful on the construction of Egyptianism.

The strongest historical conclusion is not that later meanings are “bad,” but that they are later.

## Open questions

1. **Which surviving Judgement is earliest?** Cary–Yale is commonly preferred, but early Visconti dates and workshop groupings remain debated.
2. **Who designed the standard 22-trump sequence?** No commission or rules manuscript names an inventor.
3. **Where exactly did the standard form arise?** Milan, Ferrara, and Bologna all have early claims; documentary priority and iconographic development do not point perfectly to one city.
4. **Why did World and Angel exchange supremacy?** Regional rules are documented; the original rationale is not.
5. **When did “Judgement” replace “Angel” as the dominant card-name?** Printed French captions document the later state, but the intermediate linguistic path is incompletely preserved.
6. **Was the trumpeter conceived as Gabriel by the earliest makers?** Plausible within Western tradition, but unproven.
7. **What exact source did Smith use for the water and expanded company of dead?** Revelation 20:13 is a plausible textual source; no surviving statement from Smith settles it.
8. **How much of Golden Dawn card design was uniform?** Manuscript instructions survive, but later Wang and Cicero packs are reconstructions rather than identical reproductions of a single official deck.
9. **Etteilla variants:** numbering, captions, and images vary across impressions called “Grand Etteilla.” Claims must identify the edition.
10. **No trace found:** no primary evidence was located for a Cathar, Templar, ancient Egyptian, Romani, or pre-Renaissance Kabbalistic origin of Judgement.
