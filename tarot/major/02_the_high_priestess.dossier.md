# The High Priestess: Research Dossier

## Method and evidentiary labels

This dossier distinguishes six kinds of claim:

- **Documented fact** — supported by a surviving card, archival record, or text.
- **Scholarly reconstruction** — an inference argued from surviving evidence.
- **Tradition** — a durable interpretation or practice whose historical origin can be identified but whose metaphysical truth is not empirically testable.
- **Esoteric attribution** — a deliberately constructed occult correspondence.
- **Disputed claim** — supported and challenged by named interpreters.
- **Legend / modern invention** — a narrative or visual addition unsupported by evidence from tarot’s formative period.

Dates for handmade fifteenth-century cards are approximate. “Tarot de Marseille” is a retrospective pattern-name, not the original title of every early French pack.

The Morgan catalogues its Popess as a veiled woman wearing a triple crown and holding a cross and a book. It dates the deck broadly to 1450–1480, with recent material study favoring approximately 1456–58 for its original cards. [Morgan collection record](https://www.themorgan.org/manuscript/158525), [Morgan Popess image record](https://ica.themorgan.org/manuscript/page/3/158525).

The familiar equation **II = Gimel = Moon** is not medieval. It is a nineteenth-century **esoteric attribution**, systematized in the Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn. Earlier French occultists used competing letter sequences.

## The names, number and regional substitutions

### Trionfi and tarocchi

**Documented fact.** Fifteenth-century records normally call the new packs *carte da trionfi* or *chartexelle da trionfi*. The earliest presently known documentary occurrence is Florence in 1440; Ferrara court records explicitly record painted *trionfi* in February and July 1442. The February entry pays Jacopo or Iacomo Sagramoro for painting cups, swords, coins, batons, and “all the figures” of four packs. [Ferrara transcription](https://www.oocities.org/autorbis/ferrara1442.html), [Le Tarot/Vitali documentary survey](https://letarot.it/Le-origini-italiane-del-Ludus-Triumphorum_pag_pg439_ita.aspx).

*Tarocchi* became current around the opening of the sixteenth century. Its etymology remains uncertain. Spanish *tarot/tarocchi*, French *tarot*, and German *Tarock* are later borrowings or adaptations.

### Number II

**Documented fact with qualification.** Number II is the standard Marseille and modern occult number. The earliest luxury cards were generally unnumbered. Their relative order was learned in play.

In Milanese or “C-order” lists, the Papess usually occupies the second position above the Bagatto. In Ferrarese “B-order” sources, the placement of the four lowest dignitaries can differ. In Bologna, the four *papi*—Papess, Empress, Emperor and Pope—were traditionally equal in trick-taking rank, so “II” did not originally imply an operative superiority over the other three.

### Besançon: Juno

**Documented fact.** Packs now called Tarot de Besançon replace:

- II La Papesse with **Junon/Juno**, normally accompanied by a peacock;
- V Le Pape with **Jupiter**, normally accompanied by an eagle.

The old explanation placed this innovation in Switzerland. Thierry Depaulis’s reconstruction instead locates its formation in **Strasbourg or Alsace around 1715**, probably as an “ecumenical” avoidance of images offensive either to Catholics or Protestants. It later became a speciality of Besançon, especially through Jacob Jerger after about 1800. [Depaulis summary](https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tarot_de_Besan%C3%A7on), [IPCS discussion of German transmission](https://weltexperiment.com/pagat/depaulis_2010_when-and-how-did-tarot-reach-germany_tpc-39-2_tarock.pdf).

A 1748 date survives on blocks associated with the Renoult workshop, although particular impressions from those blocks can be nineteenth-century. [Renoult/Besançon record](https://foolmade.art/archive/besancon-tarot).

**Meaning of the change.** Historically it was a substitution of classical gods for contentious Christian offices, not originally a declaration that the Papess had “always” been Juno. The peacock establishes Juno iconographically.

### Other suppressions of the Papess

- **Bologna, 1725 — documented fact.** Papal legate Cardinal Tommaso Ruffo ordered the four *papi* replaced by four Moors following the Montieri controversy. The order specifically demanded “*ai 4 Papi 4 Mori*.” Cardmakers altered existing blocks economically, supporting the conclusion that the four old cards already shared equal rank. [Accademia del Tarocchino Bolognese](https://www.tarocchinobolognese.it/pages/storia/i_papi_diventan_mori.php).
- **Belgian pattern, late seventeenth century — documented fact.** The Papess could become *L’Espagnol—Capitano Eracasse*, a commedia-derived Spanish captain; the Pope could become Bacchus. Gérard Bodet’s Liège pack is dated 1693. [Marseille variants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tarot_of_Marseilles).
- **Minchiate — documented fact.** The conventional Pope and Papess are absent; its lowest dignitary group instead contains an Empress and two Emperors among an expanded series of forty trumps. [Minchiate rules and pack](https://www.pagat.com/tarot/minchiate.html).
- **Swiss 1JJ — documented fact.** Derived from Besançon; Juno and Jupiter remain. The Rauch factory version dates between 1831 and 1838; A. G. Müller’s “1JJ” reissue appeared in 1965. [Swiss Tarot history](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swiss_Tarot).
- **Sicily — documented fact.** The reduced Sicilian pattern does not preserve a conventional Papess. Its distinctive lowest trump is *Miseria*, a beggar. [Sicilian rules](https://www.pagat.com/tarot/sicilian.html).

The Fool was normally unnumbered. Occult decks later printed it as 0 or, occasionally, placed it differently in letter sequences. *Maison Dieu* is the traditional Marseille name of trump XVI; translating it literally as “House of God” and treating it as a church is hazardous because the picture and early Italian titles include Fire, Lightning, Hell, House and House of the Devil.

# Iconography deck by deck

## Proto-tarot: Marziano da Tortona and Michelino da Besozzo, approximately 1412–1425

**Documented fact.** Marziano described for Filippo Maria Visconti a trick-taking pack with sixteen classical gods as permanent trumps and four bird suits. Michelino da Besozzo painted it; the cards are lost, but Marziano’s text survives through a copy made for Jacopo Antonio Marcello.

This was not the later twenty-two-subject tarot. It contained **Juno**, but Juno was not a renamed Papess: she belonged to the category of riches and was an independent classical deity. Calling her “the first High Priestess” is a **modern conflation**. The pack matters because it demonstrates a courtly game with a permanently ranked illustrated trump suit before the canonical tarot. [Marziano pack overview](https://www.usgamesinc.com/files/attachments/489/CY86_Booklet.pdf).

## Visconti di Modrone / Cary-Yale, Milan, approximately 1440–47

**Documented fact.** Yale describes this as the oldest of the three surviving Visconti-family groups, approximately 1440–45. It originally used six court cards per suit and included the theological virtues Faith, Hope and Charity. [Yale Library](https://library.yale.edu/news/yale-library-displays-tarot-treasures-new-morgan-library-exhibition-through-oct-4?page=56).

**Important negative finding.** No Papess survives in this pack. Its eleven surviving trumps are Empress, Emperor, Love, Chariot, Death, Faith, Hope, Charity, Fortitude, Judgment and World. Consequently, descriptions of a “Cary-Yale Papess” are reconstructions or confusion with the later Visconti-Sforza Popess. [Surviving-card inventory](https://it.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tarocchi_Visconti_di_Modrone).

The names **Visconti di Modrone** and **Cary-Yale** identify the same group, not two distinct decks.

## Brera-Brambilla, Milan, approximately 1440s

**Documented fact.** This incomplete Visconti group preserves mainly suit cards and only two trumps, the Emperor and Wheel of Fortune.

**Negative finding.** No Papess survives. Any modern Brambilla “High Priestess” is a completion supplied by a reproducer, not an original card.

## Visconti-Sforza / Pierpont Morgan–Bergamo group, Milan, approximately 1450–60

**Documented visual evidence.** The surviving Popess is seated frontally on a simple bench. She wears:

- a white veil;
- a triple papal tiara;
- a brown, taupe or earthen mantle over a white garment;
- a cross-staff;
- a closed book resting across her lap.

The figure is monumental but austere. There are no pillars, lunar crown, veil of pomegranates, Torah inscription, keys, Isis symbols or camel. [Morgan image](https://ica.themorgan.org/manuscript/page/3/158525).

**Artist attribution.** The group was traditionally attributed to Bonifacio Bembo. The Morgan now records attribution debates involving Francesco Zavattari and identifies six later replacement cards with Antonio Cicognara; the Popess is not one of the six replacements. [Morgan catalogue](https://www.themorgan.org/manuscript/158525).

### Is she Sister Manfreda?

**Disputed scholarly reconstruction.** Gertrude Moakley proposed in *The Tarot Cards Painted by Bonifacio Bembo* (1966), pp. 72–73, that the figure represents Sister Maifreda/Manfreda da Pirovano, a Visconti relative associated with the Guglielmites. The sect expected a female ecclesiastical order; Manfreda was designated pope by adherents and was executed for heresy in 1300.

Moakley’s visual argument was that the card’s plain habit resembled an Umiliata’s clothing. Michael Dummett treated the identification sympathetically in *The Visconti-Sforza Tarot Cards*, pp. 105–06. Mary K. Greer and Robert M. Place helped disseminate it in modern tarot literature. [Moakley discussion](https://marykgreer.com/2009/11/07/papess-maifreda-visconti-of-the-guglielmites%E2%80%94new-evidence/).

**Evidence for it:**

1. Manfreda and the Visconti family were historically connected.
2. The commissioners belonged to the same dynastic milieu.
3. The costume is plausibly monastic.
4. A female pope would have carried unusually specific resonance in Milan.

**Evidence against certainty:**

1. The card has no inscription identifying her.
2. The costume’s exact order is disputed; it may resemble other mendicant habits.
3. Female personifications of Ecclesia, Faith, Wisdom, sibyls and the Church were familiar.
4. Papess imagery circulated beyond Milan, where the Guglielmite reference would be obscure.
5. The hypothesis was first published more than five centuries after the card.

Robert O’Neill therefore treats Manfreda, Pope Joan, Mary, Isis and Mother Church as competing iconological candidates and emphasizes that an allegorical **Ecclesia/Mother Church** may explain the transferable type better than a portrait. [O’Neill, “Early Papess Card Iconology”](https://www.tarot.com/tarot/robert-oneill/papess-cards).

**Finding:** Manfreda is historically real; her identification with this painted figure remains plausible but unproved.

## Ferrarese and Este cards, approximately 1460–90

The surviving Ferrarese luxury groups are fragmentary and were long confused with the so-called “Charles VI” tarot.

**Documented fact.** No securely identified original Papess survives in the best-known Este fragment of approximately 1473. Modern “Golden Tarot of the Renaissance” completions add newly designed cards inspired by Schifanoia frescoes and other Renaissance material. These are **modern inventions**, not recovered originals. [Este fragment](https://foolmade.art/archive/este-tarot), [discussion of reconstructed cards](https://www.lelandra.com/joan/tarotclassic.htm).

The older attribution of some cards to Jacquemin Gringonneur and Charles VI’s 1392 commission is a historiographic error. Style and provenance place the relevant cards in late-fifteenth-century northern Italy.

## Sola Busca, 1491, probably Venice/Ferrara cultural sphere

**Documented fact.** The Sola Busca is the earliest substantially complete engraved tarot and the earliest surviving deck with narrative illustrations on every pip. Its trumps are idiosyncratic ancient or pseudo-ancient warriors and rulers.

**Negative finding.** It has no conventional Papess. Its second trump is not a female pope or priestess. Its relevance to the High Priestess is indirect: Pamela Colman Smith apparently consulted Sola Busca compositions for several Waite-Smith **minor** cards, but no close Sola Busca source has been established for her High Priestess.

## “Mantegna Tarocchi,” approximately 1465–75

**Documented correction.** The so-called Mantegna tarocchi are fifty educational engravings arranged in five decads—social stations, Apollo and the Muses, liberal arts, virtues, and cosmic principles. They are not a tarot pack and were not engraved by Andrea Mantegna.

They contain female personifications and sacerdotal/intellectual allegories relevant to the period’s visual vocabulary, but no card demonstrably ancestral to the Papess. Their use as proof of an ancient esoteric tarot curriculum is a **later analogical construction**.

## Rosenwald sheet, Florence or central Italy, late fifteenth or early sixteenth century

**Documented visual evidence.** The uncut Rosenwald woodcut sheet contains an untitled Papess. She is a seated crowned religious woman in a simplified popular-print idiom. Surviving early Papess variants collectively show triple tiaras, books, cross-staffs, croziers, keys and, in some examples, clerical attendants. [Rosenwald image](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File%3ALa_Papessa.jpg), [comparative early iconology](https://www.tarot.com/tarot/robert-oneill/papess-cards).

The Rosenwald image shows that the subject was not confined to one Milanese presentation deck.

## Budapest sheets, late fifteenth or early sixteenth century

**Documented but incomplete evidence.** These are uncut woodblock sheets representing an early Italian family of popular tarot. Their subjects and ordering differ in places from both Marseille and surviving luxury decks.

A female pontifical figure belongs to the broader early-sheet evidence, but the damaged, dispersed and variably reconstructed sheets make confident claims about exact colour, title or number unsafe. Claims that the Budapest Papess possessed a particular occult emblem generally derive from modern restoration.

## Minchiate, Florence, sixteenth century onward

The expanded ninety-seven-card pack has forty-one special cards if the Fool is counted. It absorbs several tarot subjects but replaces the Pope/Papess/Emperor/Empress quartet with an Empress, two Emperors and a Grand Duke or comparable secular dignitary, depending on pattern and terminology.

**Meaning:** this is evidence that early players could alter the low temporal-spiritual hierarchy without treating it as an inviolable occult alphabet.

## Tarocco Bolognese

Before 1725, its low group comprised four equal *papi*. Individual titles were often absent because game play, not iconographic exegesis, governed their identity.

After Ruffo’s decree, cardmakers converted them into four Moors or *Moretti*. Their equality remained. The transformation was political-religious censorship followed by standardization, not an esoteric metamorphosis. [Bolognese history](https://www.tarocchinobolognese.it/pages/storia/i_papi_diventan_mori.php).

## Sicilian pattern

Tarot reached Sicily in the seventeenth century, probably from Rome. Its heavily reorganized pack lacks the conventional Papess. *Miseria* is its lowest trump; several other subjects and ranks differ markedly from Marseille. [Pagat](https://www.pagat.com/tarot/sicilian.html).

## Jean Noblet, Paris, approximately 1650

**Documented fact.** Noblet preserves the Type I French/Marseille lineage. La Papesse is seated, crowned with the papal tiara and reading or holding an open book. A veil or hanging textile occupies the rear field. The image remains ecclesiastical rather than Egyptian.

The surviving deck is incomplete and its cards have been trimmed. “1650” is an approximate conventional date, not a date printed clearly on the Papess.

## Jean Dodal, Lyon, approximately 1701–15

Dodal’s **LA PAPESSE** is a more standardized frontal seated pontiff:

- large triple crown;
- mantle and veil;
- open book;
- partly concealed hands;
- bold stencil colour and black contour.

The card maintains the ambiguity between a literal female pope and allegorical sacred authority.

## Nicolas Conver, Marseille, 1760

Conver’s Type II Papess became the most influential Marseille exemplar. She is seated in profile-three-quarter view, crowned and veiled, with an open book in her lap. The textile behind her creates a shallow sacred interior.

The date **1760** comes from the pack’s identifying inscription, though later impressions from Conver-derived blocks must not automatically be dated 1760.

## Grimaud / Paul Marteau, Paris, 1930

**Documented fact.** Paul Marteau’s *Ancien Tarot de Marseille*, issued by Grimaud in 1930, redrew and recoloured the Conver family. It helped make “Tarot de Marseille” a commercial and cultural standard. [Spanish exhibition discussion](https://elpais.com/cultura/2024-11-07/del-loco-al-mundo-el-reverso-cultural-del-tarot.html).

Its Papess retains crown, veil and book but regularizes outlines and imposes Marteau’s restricted symbolic palette. Later writers frequently mistake this twentieth-century colour system for an unchanged medieval code.

## Camoin–Jodorowsky, 1997

Philippe Camoin and Alejandro Jodorowsky presented their deck as a “restoration” derived from surviving historical exemplars. It is better classified as a **modern synthetic reconstruction**: historical forms are combined with interpretive additions.

Their Papess includes a conspicuous **egg**, a feature not established in Noblet, Dodal or Conver. Jodorowsky and Marianne Costa interpret it as gestation, potential, incubation and enclosed creation. Critics note that the egg is an authorial addition rather than recovered evidence. [Discussion of the egg](https://www.reddit.com/r/TarotDeMarseille/comments/oo4uu3).

## Etteilla’s Grand Etteilla, 1789

Etteilla reorganized the entire pack into a purported Egyptian cosmogony. Traditional trump identity and number are therefore unreliable guides.

Card **8**, *La Questionnante*, is a female significator: the woman asking the question. In common Grand Etteilla traditions its opposed inscriptions are **Etteilla:** *Tenacity* and, reversed, *Progress*. It is not visually a papal woman and is not the historical Papess under a new costume. [Grand Etteilla card 8](https://www.queenoftarot.com/tarot_cards/437).

Any table saying “Etteilla High Priestess = no. 8” should consequently read **functional comparison only**.

## Éliphas Lévi’s conceptual Papess, 1854–56

Lévi did not publish a complete painted deck in *Dogme et rituel de la haute magie*. He supplied a program for reconstructing the tarot as a Kabbalistic and Hermetic book.

In Waite’s 1896 translation, Lévi’s hieroglyph of the Female Pope is:

> “a woman crowned with a tiara, wearing the horns of the Moon and Isis”

and her head is enveloped in a mantle. The image fuses the traditional Papess with Isis and lunar symbolism. [French scan](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Dogme_et_rituel_de_la_haute_magie_(IA_b24884340_0001).pdf), [English text scan](https://magicgatebg.com/Books/Dogma%20et%20Rituel%20de%20la%20Haute%20Magie%20Part%20II%20by%20Eliphas%20Levi.pdf).

**Esoteric attribution.** Lévi’s alphabet normally begins with the Magician as Aleph, making the Papess **Beth**, not the Golden Dawn’s Gimel. His influential relocation of the unnumbered Fool affects the whole sequence.

## Oswald Wirth, 1889; revised 1926–27

Under Stanislas de Guaita’s direction, Wirth redrew the twenty-two trumps as a corrected “Kabbalistic” tarot. His 1889 *La Papesse* retains the tiara, veil and book but cleans the Marseille line into monumental occult emblem. Hebrew characters appear within his system.

The first version lacks the yin-yang later printed on her book. Later 1926–27 versions alter colour and add or modify symbols. [British Museum/Wirth card](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:02_La_Papesse,_Oswald_Wirth_Tarot_Deck_1889.jpg), [comparison of versions](https://shs.cairn.info/article/CDU_091_0043/pdf?lang=fr).

Wirth’s deck contains only the twenty-two trumps. “Wirth minors” are later supplementary creations.

## Golden Dawn manuscript design, approximately 1888–1900

The Golden Dawn’s initiatory papers renamed the card **The High Priestess of the Silver Star** and assigned:

- Gimel, “camel”;
- Moon;
- the thirteenth path;
- Kether–Tiphareth;
- blue and lunar colour scales.

The order’s members were expected to draw personal decks from written descriptions. There was no single mass-produced “original Golden Dawn deck.”

### Wang reconstruction

Robert Wang’s *Golden Dawn Tarot* (1977), designed in consultation with Israel Regardie, visualizes the papers in a clean ceremonial style. It is a **twentieth-century reconstruction**, not an original Victorian artifact.

### Cicero reconstruction

Chic and Sandra Tabatha Cicero’s *Golden Dawn Magical Tarot* similarly reconstructs Golden Dawn descriptions with more saturated colour-scale discipline and ritual detail. It represents their published recension of the tradition.

Both are valuable witnesses to Golden Dawn teaching but should not be cited as evidence for what a unique 1890 master-card looked like.

## Waite-Smith, 1909

Pamela Colman Smith’s picture is one of the decisive redesigns in tarot history. The card shows:

- a seated woman in blue;
- a lunar/Hathoric crown;
- a white cross on her breast;
- a crescent Moon beneath her feet;
- black and white pillars lettered **B** and **J**;
- a curtain or veil patterned with pomegranates and palms;
- water visible beyond;
- a partly concealed scroll inscribed **TORA**.

Waite explains that Tora signifies “the Greater Law, the Secret Law and the second sense of the Word.” He identifies the pillars as those of Solomon’s temple, **Boaz and Jachin**, and calls her the “Secret Church,” the Shekinah and the “Spiritual Bride.” [Waite’s card description](https://sacred-texts.com/book/the-pictorial-key-to-the-tarot/shell/ii-the-high-priestess).

### Sources and meanings of Smith’s imagery

- **Boaz and Jachin — esoteric/Masonic attribution.** From 1 Kings 7:21, elaborated in Masonic temple symbolism and inherited through Golden Dawn teaching.
- **TORA — Waite’s own stated esoteric emblem.** It signals written and secret law. The popular TAROT/TORA/ROTA wordplay is a later elaboration unless separately documented.
- **Pomegranates — traditional and esoteric.** They evoke fertility, the Temple decoration of 1 Kings, Persephone, and the many-seeded hidden life.
- **Palm — Judeo-Christian emblem.** Victory, righteousness and sacred decoration.
- **Moon crown and crescent — Golden Dawn/occult synthesis.** The Moon correspondence is explicit; the crown also recalls Hathor/Isis.
- **Veil — mystical tradition.** It distinguishes visible doctrine from concealed mystery.
- **Water — lunar-subconscious metaphor.** Later psychological tarot made this especially prominent.

No evidence shows that Smith merely copied a single earlier Priestess. The design synthesizes Golden Dawn specifications, Waite’s theology, biblical-Masonic architecture and her own staging.

## Paul Foster Case and the BOTA Tarot, approximately 1920s–1930s

Case retained the Waite-Smith architecture while making its Golden Dawn correspondences more explicit and systematic. Jessie Burns Parke drew the standard BOTA line art, intended to be hand-coloured by students.

The Priestess sits on a cube between pillars, holds a scroll identified with memory and cosmic law, wears a lunar crown, and is clothed in blue flowing into water. Case interpreted her as:

- **Prakriti**, primordial substance;
- the subconscious or “subjective” mind;
- memory;
- reflection and reproduction;
- the “Virgin” Isis, Artemis/Diana, and Mary in comparative symbolism.

Case writes that the scroll records the “Memory of Nature.” [Case, *The Tarot: A Key to the Wisdom of the Ages*, Key 2, pp. 51–59](https://iapsop.com/ssoc/1947__case___the_tarot.pdf).

This is a deliberate occult-psychological synthesis, not a historical account of the fifteenth-century card.

## Thoth Tarot, painted 1938–43; book 1944; deck published 1969

Frieda Harris’s **The Priestess** radically dissolves the seated pontiff into a luminous veiled goddess. Features include:

- a nude or diaphanously veiled central female;
- a lattice or web of light;
- a bow associated with Artemis;
- lunar curves and crystals;
- flowers and fruit;
- a camel at the lower field;
- flowing forms identified with the descent of light and life.

Crowley treats the card as the path crossing the Abyss between Kether and Tiphareth. The camel is the literal meaning of **Gimel** and an emblem of traversing the desert/Abyss. The veil is light itself: revelation both manifests and hides transcendent spirit.

Crowley writes:

> “the High Priestess is the first card which connects the Supernal Triad with the Hexad”

and warns that light is also “the veil which hides that Spirit.” [Crowley, *The Book of Thoth*, 1944, Priestess section](https://www.mr-kaplan.com/Library/Crowley/Book%20of%20Thoth.pdf).

Although Isis remains important, Harris’s figure also combines Nuit, Artemis and the “Virgin of the World.” Calling her simply “Crowley’s Isis” is incomplete.

### Tzaddi/Heh issue

Crowley reassigned **Tzaddi** from the Star to the Emperor and **Heh** from the Emperor to the Star in response to *The Book of the Law* I:57, “Tzaddi is not the Star.”

**Important finding:** this swap does **not** change the Priestess. She remains Gimel–Moon–path 13. It matters only as evidence that Crowley considered Golden Dawn correspondences revisable.

## Notable modern redesigns

- **Thoth-derived decks:** emphasize a cosmic, nude, lunar goddess and the veil of light.
- **RWS-derived decks:** usually retain pillars, moon, book/scroll and pomegranates; many replace explicit Jewish and Christian symbols with generalized “intuition.”
- **Feminist and goddess decks:** often identify her with Isis, Sophia, Persephone, the Virgin, Hecate or a non-hierarchical priestess. These are **modern spiritual reinterpretations**.
- **Jungian decks:** emphasize anima, unconscious knowledge, dreams and liminality.
- **Jodorowsky–Camoin:** returns to *La Papesse* but adds the egg and psychogenealogical meaning.
- **Niki de Saint Phalle:** translated the Popess into inhabitable monumental sculpture at the Tarot Garden; discussed below.

# The game

## What the image does in play

**Documented fact.** Tarot began as a trick-taking family of games. The symbolic scene normally has no rule-bearing effect beyond identifying the trump.

In a Marseille-numbered pack, II:

- beats trump I and every suit card;
- loses to trumps III–XXI;
- ordinarily carries no special honour status;
- ordinarily counts as an “empty card,” nominally one point before group-counting adjustments.

The usual five-point tarot honours are trump I, trump XXI, the Fool and the kings—not II. [Tarot counting systems](https://www.pagat.com/tarot/counting.html).

In some contracts and regional games II may acquire tactical or announcement significance, but this comes from the particular game, not from “High Priestess” symbolism.

## The three regional orders

Michael Dummett reconstructed three principal early families:

| Order | Region | Low dignitaries and characteristic features |
|---|---|---|
| **A** | Bologna and Florence | The four *papi* form a low group; in Bologna they are equal. Justice is very high in characteristic A sequences. |
| **B** | Ferrara and Venice-related sources | Empress generally leads the dignity sequence; virtues and celestial cards occupy positions unlike Marseille. |
| **C** | Milan; later western France | Papess near the bottom, then Empress, Emperor, Pope; ancestor of the Marseille sequence, though early C lists are not identical to modern Marseille. |

This is a **scholarly reconstruction** from rule evidence, poems and trump lists—not three printed rulebooks surviving from 1450. [Dummett translation and commentary](https://dummettsmondo.blogspot.com/2015/07/chapters-6-and-7-game-and-its-trump.html).

### Justice and Strength

The modern occult sequence is not universal:

- Marseille: Justice VIII, Strength XI.
- Waite-Smith: Strength VIII, Justice XI.
- Crowley: Adjustment VIII, Lust XI.
- Older Italian orders vary more widely; in some A orders Justice stands immediately below World and above Angel.
- Golden Dawn’s exchange was designed to align Leo with Strength and Libra with Justice.

The Waite sequence is therefore an occult reordering, not recovery of a universally original order.

## The Fool

**Documented fact with historical uncertainty.** The Fool is traditionally unnumbered and usually functions as an excuse: it may be played without following suit and is normally recovered by its owner in exchange for a low card. Some later Central European games transform it into the highest trump, the **Sküs**. [Tarot games](https://www.pagat.com/tarot/counting.html).

Whether the excuse function was present in the very first tarot rules is reconstructed, not directly documented by a surviving fifteenth-century rulebook.

## Minchiate

Minchiate expands the trump series and eliminates the conventional Papess. Its valued trumps and combinations differ substantially. There is no meaningful game-value answer for “the High Priestess in Minchiate” because that card is not present as such. [Minchiate rules](https://www.pagat.com/tarot/minchiate.html).

# Chronology of attestation

### Approximately 1412–25 — Marziano da Tortona

**Documented fact.** Marziano describes sixteen deified heroes for Filippo Maria Visconti. Juno appears, but no Papess. This is a precursor in game structure, not the canonical tarot.

### 1440 — Florence

**Documented fact.** A Florentine record mentions two packs of *trionfi* sent to Sigismondo Pandolfo Malatesta. It does not name individual trumps.

### 10 February and 28 July 1442 — Ferrara

**Documented fact.** Este accounts pay for packs of *chartexelle da trionfi*. The February record names four ordinary suits plus “all the figures.” Neither entry names the Papess individually. [Ferrara record](https://www.oocities.org/autorbis/ferrara1442.html).

### Approximately 1450–60 — Visconti-Sforza Popess

**Documented surviving object.** Earliest surviving conventional Papess presently known.

### Approximately 1470–1500 — *Sermones de ludo cum aliis*

**Documented text.** The anonymous preacher’s list includes:

> *El bagatella … Imperatrix … Imperator … La papessa … El papa…*

The manuscript’s dating has ranged from about 1450–70 to around 1500. It is safer to call it late fifteenth century than to present “1470” as exact. The sermon condemns the game morally; it does not identify occult correspondences. [Steele-sermon bibliography and discussion](https://letarot.it/Le-origini-italiane-del-Ludus-Triumphorum_pag_pg439_ita.aspx).

### Approximately 1490–1520 — Rosenwald and Budapest sheets

**Documented objects.** Popular woodcut evidence confirms circulation of the female pontiff outside one aristocratic commission.

### Around 1495–1500 — Boiardo poems

Matteo Maria Boiardo created a distinct poetic tarot scheme with passions, virtues and vices. It should not be used as a straightforward list of the standard Papess.

### 1522 onward — *Tarocchi appropriati*

**Documented literary practice.** Trump names were assigned to people in satirical or celebratory poems. This confirms titles in cultural circulation but is not a divinatory system.

### 1527 — Teofilo Folengo

Folengo’s *Chaos del Triperuno* uses tarot cards in a literary-divinatory scene and supplies sonnets built from card names. It is evidence for symbolic play and fortune-telling rhetoric, not proof of an ancient occult order.

### 1543–85 — Aretino, Lollio, Susio, Garzoni

Poems and invectives provide regional names such as *Traditore*, *Fuoco*, *Inferno* and *Casa del Diavolo*. Their variation is critical evidence against a single immutable primordial title list. [Early-title compilation](https://groups.google.com/g/opentarotnexus/c/VY68ec_o418).

### Approximately 1650 — Noblet

First surviving French pattern now classed as Tarot de Marseille Type I; titled *La Papesse*.

### 1693–approximately 1715 — Belgian and Alsatian substitutions

Papess becomes the Spanish Captain in one Belgian family and Juno in the emerging Besançon family.

### 1725 — Bologna

The four *papi* are officially suppressed and converted into Moors.

### Approximately 1750 — Bologna

An anonymous manuscript gives cartomantic meanings for the Bolognese pack. This is the earliest currently known systematic evidence for tarot divination and predates Gébelin.

### 1781 — Court de Gébelin and Comte de Mellet

Gébelin identifies the tarot as an Egyptian book and reads the Papess as an Isiac high priestess. Mellet links the twenty-two trumps to Hebrew letters and publishes an early tarot divination method. [Gébelin overview and text history](https://tarot-revelatio.com/fr/blog/court-de-gebelin-tarot-esoterique), [Mellet spread](https://marykgreer.com/2008/04/03/the-oldest-spread-by-le-comte-de-m/).

### 1789 — Etteilla

Grand Etteilla appears as the first tarot designed specifically around a published occult-divinatory cosmology. Its no. 8 female significator replaces rather than preserves the Papess.

### 1854–56 — Éliphas Lévi

Lévi integrates tarot with Kabbalah, ceremonial magic and the astral light; the Female Pope becomes lunar Isis.

### 1863 and 1870 — Paul Christian

Jean-Baptiste Pitois, “Paul Christian,” develops Egyptian initiation narratives and popularizes *arcana* terminology. His descriptions are **literary occult invention**, though enormously influential.

### 1888 — Mathers

*The Tarot: Its Occult Signification, Use in Fortune-Telling, and Method of Play* gives English readers a systematic occult/divinatory tarot vocabulary.

### 1889 — Papus and Wirth

Papus publishes *Le Tarot des Bohémiens*; Wirth publishes his twenty-two Kabbalistic trumps.

### Approximately 1888–1900 — Golden Dawn

The order fixes the now-standard occult formula High Priestess–Gimel–Moon–path 13.

### 1909–10 — Waite and Smith

The deck appears in 1909; *The Pictorial Key to the Tarot* follows in 1910, with some copies dated 1911.

### 1920s–40s — Case

Case develops the BOTA tarot and an explicitly psychological “subconscious” interpretation.

### 1938–44 / 1969 — Crowley and Harris

Thoth paintings completed; *Book of Thoth* published in 1944; commercial deck issued in 1969.

### 1970s–present

Pollack, Greer, Jungian writers, feminist spirituality and mass-market decks make intuition, unconscious knowledge and feminine spiritual autonomy the card’s dominant popular meanings.

### Golden Dawn colour scales

The principal attributions are blue and lunar:

- King scale: blue;
- Queen scale: silver;
- Emperor scale: cold pale blue;
- Empress scale: silver rayed sky-blue.

Published tables vary slightly in wording and reproduction. Deck printers also alter the colours.

### Pips and courts

The requested **decan, Book T title, sub-element and zodiacal span** fields are **not applicable to a trump**.

In Golden Dawn *Book T*:

- numbered minor cards 2–10 receive decans and titles;
- court cards receive elemental sub-elements and zodiacal spans;
- trumps receive a letter plus planet, zodiac sign or element.

The High Priestess consequently has no decan, pip title, court sub-element or court zodiacal span. Assigning one is a category error.

### Waite’s symbolic description

Waite calls the Priestess:

> “the Secret Tradition and the higher sense of the instituted Mysteries.”

He rejects reducing her to the Pope’s wife or to the historical Pope Joan. Mystically she is the Shekinah and “Spiritual Bride.” This is Waite’s **Christian-Kabbalistic esotericism**, not historical evidence about the Milanese image.

### Crowley’s method and reversal issue

Crowley did not use Waite-style mechanically reversed meanings. He preferred elemental dignity and the card’s relation to surrounding forces. Modern publishers who print a neat “Thoth Priestess reversed” list are supplying a post-Crowley convenience.

# Esoteric and symbolic readings

## Christian

### Mother Church / Ecclesia

**Scholarly reconstruction based on medieval iconography.** A crowned woman holding book, cross, key or chalice could personify Ecclesia. This provides a culturally ordinary explanation for a female religious sovereign. It does not prove that every Papess is Ecclesia.

### Faith

The Papess has sometimes been interpreted as Faith or one of the theological virtues. The Cary-Yale deck, however, contains a separate Faith trump, weakening the claim that “Papess always equals Faith.”

### Pope Joan

**Legend; possible iconographic source.** The story of a learned woman who entered the male clergy, became pope and was exposed through childbirth circulated from the thirteenth century onward. There is no accepted evidence that such a pope reigned.

Because the card shows precisely a female pope, the legend is an obvious possible referent. But no fifteenth-century card or rule calls her Joan. Waite later explicitly separates the “High Priestess” from Pope Joan. The legend is therefore relevant context, not a settled identification.

## Guglielmite / Manfreda

**Disputed historical-person identification.** Manfreda existed and was treated as a future pope by Guglielmite adherents. Moakley’s identification is the strongest specific-person hypothesis for the Visconti-Sforza card but remains inferential.

It cannot explain by itself why a generic Papess entered packs in Ferrara, Florence and France unless the Milanese image either spread widely or drew on a broader pre-existing female-pontiff allegory.

## Hermetic and Egyptian

### Gébelin’s Isis

**First clear occult appearance: 1781.** Gébelin argued that Christian cardmakers had misunderstood an Egyptian priestess. The horns in the crown supposedly preserved Isis beneath the Papess disguise.

This interpretation spread through Etteilla, Lévi, Papus, Wirth, the Golden Dawn and modern decks. Its influence is documented; its history of origin is unsupported.

### Lévi’s Isis

Lévi fused:

- papal tiara;
- horns of the Moon;
- Isis;
- the veil;
- Kabbalistic binary symbolism.

His “Isis” is an occult synthesis mediated through Renaissance Hermetism, the Isiac Tablet/Mensa Isiaca and nineteenth-century ceremonial magic—not a reconstruction based on Egyptian temple cards.

### Veiled Isis

The statement “I am all that has been, is, and shall be…” became associated with the veiled goddess of Sais through classical and later Romantic sources. Occultists used it to define the Priestess as Nature whose veil conceals ultimate truth.

## Kabbalistic

### Beth versus Gimel

- **Lévi/Papus/Wirth tradition:** Papess follows Magician and is commonly assigned **Beth**.
- **Golden Dawn tradition:** Fool receives Aleph, Magician Beth, Priestess **Gimel**.

The conflict results from where the Fool is placed in the Hebrew sequence. Neither correspondence is present in medieval tarot.

### Gimel and the camel

In the Golden Dawn, Gimel means camel. Crowley turns that lexical association into a major visual symbol: the camel crosses the desert as the Priestess’s path crosses the Abyss.

### Shekinah

Waite identifies the Priestess with the feminine indwelling divine presence. This is a Christian occultist’s Kabbalistic appropriation and synthesis. It should not be represented as a universal Jewish identification of tarot II.

## Astrological

The Moon attribution appears in the nineteenth-century occult synthesis and becomes definitive in the Golden Dawn. Earlier Papess cards lack an explicit Moon.

The resulting symbolic complex includes:

- reflection rather than emission;
- periodicity;
- water and tides;
- dream, memory and imagination;
- virgin goddesses;
- unconscious or hidden processes.

## Alchemical

The historical Papess is not demonstrably an alchemical card. Wirth, Case and later occultists nevertheless interpret her as:

- receptive matrix;
- *prima materia*;
- salt or crystalline form;
- the watery, cold and moist principle;
- concealed Nature;
- passive potential before manifestation.

These are **esoteric attributions**, first systematized in nineteenth- and early-twentieth-century occult commentary.

## Psychological and Jungian

Carl Jung did not publish a canonical card-by-card tarot system. Modern Jungian tarot emerged through writers who applied his concepts—archetype, anima/animus, shadow, individuation, collective unconscious and synchronicity—to existing cards.

The Priestess became:

- threshold guardian of the unconscious;
- anima or inner feminine;
- dream and imaginal knowledge;
- the capacity to tolerate ambiguity;
- an unintegrated secret or dissociated intuition when adverse.

Rachel Pollack was among the most influential popularizers of this psychological-spiritual reading. Mary K. Greer developed reflective and experiential methods, including contextual reversals.

The common statement “the High Priestess is a Jungian archetype” is therefore shorthand for a **post-Jung synthesis**, not a phrase or tarot attribution established by Jung himself.

# People and works

## Artists, printers and occultists

- **Michelino da Besozzo and Marziano da Tortona:** creators of the classical-god precursor; relevant through Juno and permanent trumps.
- **Bonifacio Bembo / Francesco Zavattari:** competing attributions for the Visconti-Sforza group.
- **Jean Noblet, Jean Dodal, Nicolas Conver:** principal makers in the French lineage that fixed *La Papesse*.
- **Paul Marteau:** popularized the Grimaud Marseille recension from 1930.
- **Antoine Court de Gébelin:** changed the Papess into an Egyptian high priestess in theory.
- **Comte de Mellet:** connected tarot with Hebrew letters and published an early divination scheme.
- **Etteilla:** built the first purpose-designed occult tarot, but displaced the Papess.
- **Éliphas Lévi:** fused her with lunar Isis and Kabbalah.
- **Stanislas de Guaita and Oswald Wirth:** produced an influential French Kabbalistic redesign.
- **S. L. MacGregor Mathers:** transmitted divinatory and Golden Dawn structures into English.
- **Pamela Colman Smith:** created the definitive modern image.
- **A. E. Waite:** supplied its Christian-Kabbalistic program.
- **Paul Foster Case and Jessie Burns Parke:** converted the card into a colourable lesson in subconsciousness.
- **Aleister Crowley and Frieda Harris:** transformed it into an abstract Thelemic goddess and path across the Abyss.
- **Alejandro Jodorowsky and Philippe Camoin:** created a modern synthetic Marseille recension.
- **Rachel Pollack and Mary K. Greer:** major modern psychological and experiential interpreters.

## T. S. Eliot, *The Waste Land* (1922)

**Documented tarot reference; negative card-specific finding.** Madame Sosostris displays “the drowned Phoenician Sailor,” Belladonna “the Lady of the Rocks,” the man with three staves, the Wheel and the “one-eyed merchant,” among others.

Eliot does **not** name the High Priestess. Identifying Belladonna/Lady of the Rocks as High Priestess is a critical speculation, complicated by Eliot’s note that he was not using the tarot precisely. The poem is important to modern literary tarot, but it is not secure High Priestess evidence.

## W. B. Yeats

Yeats’s Golden Dawn membership and occult writing connect him to the order’s tarot environment. No primary text located in this search establishes a major Yeats poem or artwork as a direct quotation of the High Priestess card. Claims that a particular “veiled woman” in Yeats is necessarily tarot II require separate textual demonstration.

## Italo Calvino, *The Castle of Crossed Destinies* (1969/1973)

Calvino constructs narratives by arranging tarot cards. One part uses the Visconti-Sforza deck; another uses a Marseille-type deck. Cards act as a combinatorial visual language rather than fixed fortune-telling definitions.

The Papess participates in the available lexicon, but claims that one character is unambiguously “the High Priestess” depend on the particular layout and edition. Calvino is evidence for tarot as narrative machine, not for the card’s historical origin.

## Alejandro Jodorowsky

Tarot permeates Jodorowsky’s psychomagic, writings, lectures and visual imagination. His explicit card-specific contribution is the 1997 Camoin deck and the Papess-as-incubation reading in *The Way of Tarot* with Marianne Costa.

Claims that a specific figure in *El Topo*, *The Holy Mountain* or *Santa Sangre* is a literal cinematic reproduction of trump II are generally interpretive unless supported by his commentary.

## Ingmar Bergman

Bergman’s *The Seventh Seal* uses chess, Death and medieval allegory, not a demonstrable High Priestess quotation. His films contain silent, doubled and enigmatic women, but resemblance to the Priestess is thematic.

**Finding:** no reliable primary source was located showing Bergman intentionally quoting tarot II.

## Niki de Saint Phalle and the Tarot Garden

**Documented artistic adaptation.** At the Tarot Garden in Garavicchio, Tuscany, Niki de Saint Phalle transformed major arcana into monumental sculptures and buildings. **The High Priestess/Papessa** forms part of the Empress complex: a monumental blue female head/body associated with water and access to the garden’s symbolic architecture.

This is an architectural inhabitation of modern tarot, indebted to Marseille and occult readings rather than a reconstruction of the fifteenth-century card.

## Leonora Carrington, Salvador Dalí and later artists

Carrington and Dalí both engaged tarot imagery; Dalí produced a complete deck. Their Priestesses belong to twentieth-century Surrealist and personal mythologies.

Modern exhibitions also connect tarot with Betye Saar, Dorothy Iannone, Agnès Varda, Andy Warhol and contemporary artists, but not every tarot-related work contains a distinct quotation of II. [Overview of modern tarot in art](https://elpais.com/cultura/2024-11-07/del-loco-al-mundo-el-reverso-cultural-del-tarot.html).

# Controversies and disputes

## 1. Did tarot originate in ancient Egypt?

**Legend/esoteric attribution, first clearly published in 1781.** Gébelin claimed tarot was an Egyptian sacred book preserved under harmless gaming imagery. Mellet and Etteilla expanded the idea; nineteenth-century Egyptomania made it culturally powerful.

**Evidence offered by proponents:**

- purported Egyptian etymologies of *tarot*;
- Isis-like horns and veils;
- twenty-two trumps as sacred alphabet;
- the supposed Book of Thoth;
- analogy with Egyptian initiation.

**Historical correction:**

- tarot is documented in fifteenth-century northern Italy;
- its recognizable trumps belong to late-medieval Christian and civic imagery;
- no Egyptian tarot cards, text or transmission record survives;
- Gébelin wrote before Champollion’s decipherment of hieroglyphs;
- the word *tarot* is not established by Gébelin’s proposed Egyptian roots.

Even Waite, while retaining Egyptian symbolism, wrote that there was “no particle of evidence” for Egyptian origin. [Waite’s historical critique](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Page:The_Illustrated_Key_to_the_Tarot.djvu/36), [Morgan historical note](https://www.themorgan.org/manuscript/158525).

## 2. Did Romani people bring tarot to Europe?

**Legend with racialized nineteenth-century transmission.** Gébelin connected the cards with “Egyptians/Bohemians”; Boiteau d’Ambly, Jean-Alexandre Vaillant and Papus helped popularize the story.

Romani cartomancers did use playing cards and helped shape popular fortune-telling culture. That documented practice is different from inventing or transporting tarot into fifteenth-century Italy.

The chronology and linguistic evidence do not support Romani origin. Waite already observed that Romani use of tarot was not evidenced before the nineteenth century and could be explained by adopting cards already present in Europe. [Farley’s history and transmission survey](https://e-isomero.com/pdf/book/1611846398.pdf).

## 3. Cathar origin

**Modern speculative tradition.** Some writers connect tarot’s dualities, Grail imagery or “heretical” Papess to Cathars, sometimes proposing clandestine Cathar papermakers after Montségur.

No fifteenth-century document, card inscription, Cathar text or transmission chain supports this. The theory often combines genuine medieval religious conflict with twentieth-century occult reconstruction. Its evidentiary status is **unsupported/disputed**, not documented history.

## 4. Templar origin

**Legend/modern invention.** Claims that Knights Templar encoded their doctrine in tarot face the same problems:

- the order was suppressed in 1312;
- canonical tarot appears more than a century later;
- no Templar deck, rule, inventory or explanatory text survives;
- the imagery is intelligible within ordinary late-medieval civic and Christian culture.

Possible thematic parallels—Temple pillars, initiation, Baphomet—entered tarot mainly through modern occultism, especially Lévi, Freemasonry and the Golden Dawn.

## 5. Pope Joan versus Manfreda versus Ecclesia

| Candidate | Status | Evidence | Principal weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pope Joan | Legend / possible traditional source | Famous medieval female-pope narrative | No card says “Joan”; Joan herself is not historical |
| Manfreda | Disputed scholarly reconstruction | Real Guglielmite “pope,” Visconti relation, Milanese context | No inscription; cannot readily explain wider pattern |
| Ecclesia/Mother Church | Scholarly iconographic reconstruction | Familiar female Church personification | Often crowned differently; no explicit label |
| Faith | Scholarly reconstruction | Book, cross and female virtue type | Cary-Yale has a separate Faith |
| Virgin Mary | Iconographic analogy | Veil, book, sacred maternity | Triple papal tiara is atypical |
| Isis | Esoteric attribution from 1781 | Horn-like crown, book and veil useful to occult synthesis | No evidence of fifteenth-century Egyptian intention |

The evidence permits contextually informed possibilities but not a final identification.

## 6. Was the Papess a protest against the Church?

The image can be satirical, legendary, allegorical or celebratory depending on context. Early packs were commissioned by elite Christian families and played openly. The card’s existence alone does not prove anticlerical protest.

Conversely, later censorship in Bologna and the Besançon substitutions shows that viewers could indeed regard a female pope as provocative or blasphemous.

## 7. Did the Golden Dawn recover ancient correspondences?

**Esoteric attribution presented as tradition.** The Golden Dawn system is coherent and historically influential. No evidence places Gimel–Moon–Kether/Tiphareth on fifteenth- or seventeenth-century cards.

The disagreements among Mellet, Lévi, Papus and the Golden Dawn demonstrate construction and revision rather than unbroken recovery.

## 8. Is TORA a printer’s error?

**No evidence found.** Waite explicitly explains the scroll as **Tora**, the Greater or Secret Law. It is therefore intentional in the Waite-Smith card.

Later readers’ anagram chains—TARO, TORA, ROTA, ORAT, ATOR—are occult wordplay. They should not be mistaken for proof that the printed word began as an error.

## 9. Is the Jodorowsky egg “restored”?

**Disputed modern addition.** The egg supports Jodorowsky’s interpretation brilliantly but lacks a demonstrated prototype in the principal Noblet, Dodal and Conver cards. Calling it a restoration overstates the evidence.

## 10. Did Pamela Colman Smith derive the Priestess from Sola Busca?

**Unsupported in this specific case.** Strong comparisons exist between Sola Busca compositions and several Waite-Smith minor cards. No comparably close Sola Busca model exists for the High Priestess, because Sola Busca lacks a conventional Papess.

## 11. Misprints that became traditions

No securely established Papess printing mistake comparable to the Central European confusion of *Mondo* with German *Mond* was found.

Relevant transformations are mostly intentional or interpretive:

- **TORA** is intentional.
- **Juno/Jupiter** are intentional substitutions.
- **Four Moors** are mandated redesigns.
- Wirth’s later yin-yang is a revision.
- Jodorowsky’s egg is a modern addition.
- “Cary-Yale Papess” is frequently a cataloguing or reproduction error because no such original survives.
- Dating the “Charles VI tarot” to 1392 is an antiquarian misattribution, not a cardmaker’s misprint.

# The historians’ correction

Michael Dummett’s central contribution was methodological: tarot’s history must begin with dated cards, records and games, not with an occult narrative projected backward. His work established the northern Italian game context and the regional trump-order families.

Thierry Depaulis has refined dates, regional manufacture and documentary transmission, including the Alsatian origin of the so-called Besançon pattern.

Ronald Decker and Dummett traced occult tarot as a historically later transformation rather than an ancient secret doctrine. Ronald Decker, Thierry Depaulis and Michael Dummett’s *A Wicked Pack of Cards* remains fundamental for Gébelin, Etteilla and the French occultists.

Stuart R. Kaplan assembled extensive deck documentation and made rare cards accessible, though individual volumes reflect the scholarship available at their publication dates.

Robert M. Place and Gertrude Moakley developed iconographic readings, including Manfreda. Their claims are most useful when kept distinct from direct documentation.

Helen Farley framed tarot as cultural history and carefully separated entertainment, esotericism and modern identity.

Andrea Vitali and Le Tarot have published valuable Italian and French primary materials and iconographic essays. Community sites such as Trionfi.com and Tarot History Forum preserve important transcriptions and debates, but arguments there should be checked against the underlying archival source.

# Open questions

1. **Who exactly is the Visconti-Sforza Popess?** Manfreda remains possible, but no identifying inscription has surfaced.
2. **Was the canonical Papess invented in Milan, Ferrara, Bologna or a lost common source?** Current documents establish several early centres but not the first designer.
3. **What was the precise original trump order?** The A/B/C families are strong reconstructions; no complete fifteenth-century rulebook settles a single archetype.
4. **When exactly was the *Sermones de ludo* written?** Published estimates range across the later fifteenth century and sometimes near 1500.
5. **How should the damaged Rosenwald and Budapest sheets be reconstructed?** Modern restorations inevitably decide ambiguous lines, order and colour.
6. **What was the precise motive for the first Juno/Jupiter substitution?** Confessional sensitivity is persuasive but remains partly reconstructed.
7. **How much design authority belonged to Waite and how much to Smith?** Waite supplied detailed doctrine, but surviving evidence does not document every pictorial decision.
8. **Which exact visual sources did Smith consult?** Golden Dawn manuscripts and biblical-Masonic symbolism are clear; claims about individual paintings require stronger documentation.
9. **What was the first use of “High Priestess” as the regular English card-name?** Mathers used “Female Pope” and “High Priestess” vocabularies before Waite, while Golden Dawn manuscript titles fixed the phrase. Exact priority depends on dating private manuscripts.
10. **Is there an intentional High Priestess quotation in Yeats or Bergman?** No decisive primary statement was located.
11. **Which modern architectural works quote the card directly?** Niki de Saint Phalle is secure; many other claimed “Priestess buildings” are resemblance-based.
12. **How much did actual Romani cartomancy influence later tarot reading methods?** Adoption and interaction are documented, but origin and transmission claims remain unsupported.
