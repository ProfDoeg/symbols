# The Lovers: Research Dossier

## Method and evidentiary labels

This dossier distinguishes:

- **Documented fact** — supported by a surviving card, archival record, dated publication, museum catalogue, or contemporary text.
- **Scholarly reconstruction** — an inference argued from surviving objects, rules, documentary sequences, heraldry, or comparative iconography.
- **Tradition** — a durable interpretation whose historical origin may be identifiable but whose truth is not empirically testable.
- **Esoteric attribution** — an occult correspondence deliberately assigned by a named author or school.
- **Disputed claim** — proposed by identifiable writers but rejected, qualified, or unresolved in scholarship.
- **Legend** — a narrative lacking contemporary evidence and usually contradicted by the documentary chronology.
- **Modern invention** — an interpretation or design introduced in modern tarot practice rather than transmitted from Renaissance tarot.

Dates for handmade fifteenth-century cards remain ranges unless documentary or heraldic evidence permits greater precision. Early Italian cards generally lack printed titles and numbers; names and ranks often come from later lists.

### Names, numbers, and adjacent regional renamings

**Documented fact:** Fifteenth-century luxury cards normally carried neither Roman numerals nor printed titles. “Trump VI” is therefore a later standardization, principally associated with the western or Milan–France sequence. Love’s position can be reconstructed from regional trump lists, but a painted Milanese Love card does not itself say “VI.”

**Documented fact:** In the Tarot de Marseille tradition the woodblock title is normally *L’Amoureux*, singular. English publishers commonly converted this into the plural *The Lovers*, particularly after Waite and Pamela Colman Smith made two lovers, rather than one hesitant lover, the visual center.

**Documented fact:** The term *trionfi* occurs in fifteenth-century records before *tarocchi*. A Florentine record of 1440 now precedes the long-famous Ferrara entries of 1442. *Tarocchi* becomes established around the turn of the sixteenth century; French *tarot* and German *Tarock* are later adaptations.

**Contextual regional renamings:** These do not directly rename The Lovers, but demonstrate that trump titles were historically mutable.

- In Protestant Besançon and Swiss patterns, the Papess and Pope were changed to **Juno** and **Jupiter**, avoiding Catholic pontiffs. This pattern is securely visible in eighteenth-century Franco-Swiss production.
- The Marseille Tower appears as **La Maison Dieu**, a title attested on French cards rather than the simple English “Tower.”
- The Fool may be **Le Mat**, **Le Fou**, *Il Matto*, or *der Sküs* and usually remains unnumbered in traditional game packs.
- In Bologna, the four lowest “papi” eventually became visually and hierarchically equal, making ordinary Marseille numbering unsuitable.
- Sicilian tarot renamed and redrew several trumps and drastically lowered the Fool’s rank in play.
- Etteilla dismantled the Marseille sequence and numbered a redesigned pack from 1 to 78; his Lovers-equivalent became **Mariage**, no. 13.

These mutations warn against treating the Marseille titles and numbers as primordial.

# Iconography deck by deck

## Marziano da Tortona and Michelino da Besozzo, c. 1418–1425

**Documented text; lost deck:** Marziano described a special card game made for Filippo Maria Visconti, with four bird suits and sixteen deified heroes painted by Michelino da Besozzo. Cupid ranked last among the divine heroes and governed pleasure.

**Scholarly reconstruction:** This is often called a proto-tarot because its special ranked picture cards overtrumped ordinary suits. It did not possess the canonical twenty-one trumps plus Fool, and Cupid is not demonstrably the direct model for the later Amore card.

**Importance:** It establishes that personified or mythological Love could function as a permanent trump in the Milanese court before the first standard tarot survivors. The accompanying text survives because Jacopo Antonio Marcello copied and sent it with a deck to Isabella of Lorraine in 1449.

## Visconti di Modrone / Cary–Yale, Milan, c. 1441–1445

**Documented object:** The card survives in the Beinecke Library’s Cary collection. A richly dressed man and woman join hands beneath a canopy bearing the word **AMOR**. Visconti and Sforza heraldic references appear in the scene, and a small white dog stands near the pair.

**Scholarly reconstruction:** It has repeatedly been identified as Bianca Maria Visconti and Francesco Sforza and connected with their marriage of 25 October 1441. The heraldry makes a dynastic-marriage connection plausible, but no surviving commission states that this card is their wedding portrait. The more cautious formulation is: it was made for the Visconti court and makes dynastic marriage its model of Love.

**Traditional iconographic reading:** The dog signifies fidelity; joined hands signify marital consent; the canopy supplies aristocratic ceremony and family protection.

**Disputed detail:** Some popular accounts call this unequivocally “the first Lovers card” and date it to the wedding day. It is probably the earliest surviving Love, but the exact date and occasion are reconstructed. [Le Tarot/Vitali overview](https://letarot.it/Visconti-Modrone-Tarot_pag_pg825_eng.aspx?lng=ENG), [Yale catalogue identifier cited in scholarship](https://collections.library.yale.edu/catalog/2002899).

## Brera-Brambilla, Milan, c. 1440s

**Documented fact:** The surviving Brera-Brambilla group is one of the three great Visconti-family painted remnants, together with Cary–Yale and Visconti-Sforza.

**Absence finding:** No securely identified Love trump survives in the Brera-Brambilla fragment. Modern reconstructions that supply one are inventions based on another Milanese pack, not facsimiles of a surviving Brambilla card.

## Visconti-Sforza / Colleoni-Baglioni / Pierpont Morgan–Bergamo, c. 1450–1460

**Documented object:** A couple joins right hands. Above them a blindfolded, winged nude Cupid stands on a pedestal, holding arrows. The Morgan records the card as “Love,” MS M.630.7. The scene is less explicitly ceremonial than Cary–Yale but still resembles betrothal or marriage imagery.

**Change:** The canopy and dog have disappeared; Cupid has become dominant. Love is presented both as social union and as a force that strikes blindly.

**Scholarly reconstruction:** The mingled Visconti-Sforza devices connect the pack to Francesco Sforza’s ducal circle after 1450. Attributions range from Bonifacio Bembo and his circle to Francesco Zavattari; individual replacement cards in the pack have separate attribution disputes. The Love card itself belongs to the principal original group.

**Wedding question:** Calling it “a wedding card” is a reasonable iconographic description, but saying it records a particular wedding goes beyond the evidence.

## Ferrarese and “Este” cards, later fifteenth century

Several dispersed cards have been called Charles VI, Gringonneur, Este, Catania, or Ferrarese cards, although older national and collector names are often misleading.

**Documented objects:** Ferrarese-style cards emphasize courtly life, elaborate costume, triumphal processions, and classical-humanist subjects. Some Love images show an embracing or kissing couple, or courtly groups affected by Cupid.

**Scholarly reconstruction:** The imagery is regularly compared with Francesco del Cossa’s April fresco in the Palazzo Schifanoia, where Venus presides over lovers. Similarity supports a shared Ferrarese courtly vocabulary, not direct copying.

**Absence finding:** “The Este Lovers” is not one single, universally agreed object with an uncontested date and pack membership. Catalogues differ over which dispersed cards belong together.

## Rosenwald sheets, probably Florence, late fifteenth or early sixteenth century

**Documented object:** The Rosenwald block-printed sheets preserve an early popular, non-luxury tarot pattern. The Love image is a couple rather than the fully developed Marseille man-between-two-women composition.

**Importance:** It weakens the claim that the three-person moral dilemma was tarot’s original Love picture. Early printed Italian evidence remains closer to couple/Cupid imagery.

## Budapest sheets, northern Italy, c. 1500

**Documented object:** The fragmentary sheets likewise show a two-person Love scene.

**Scholarly significance:** Along with Rosenwald and the Milanese cards, they support a developmental sequence from **Love/couple under Cupid** to the French **lover amid two women**, although the intermediate steps remain incompletely documented.

## Sola Busca, Venice/Ferrara orbit, 1491

**Documented fact:** The Sola Busca is the earliest surviving substantially complete tarot with fully illustrated numeral cards. Its trumps bear classical or pseudo-classical warriors rather than the standard allegorical subjects.

**Relevance limitation:** It has no straightforward Lovers trump corresponding iconographically to Marseille VI. Its importance for this dossier is indirect: Pamela Colman Smith demonstrably drew on Sola Busca compositions for several Waite-Smith pip cards after examples were exhibited in London in 1908. No comparably clear, card-specific borrowing has been established for her Lovers.

## “Mantegna Tarocchi,” c. 1460s–1470s

**Documented fact:** These are engraved educational or cosmological series of fifty figures arranged in five decades. They are not a tarot pack and are not by Mantegna.

**Relevance:** Their hierarchy of social estates, liberal arts, virtues, cosmic spheres, and first causes demonstrates the same Renaissance appetite for ordered allegorical images.

**Disputed/obsolete claim:** Treating them as an early tarot deck or as the source of the twenty-two trumps is no longer accepted. There is no direct Lovers equivalent.

## Tarot de Marseille formation, sixteenth–seventeenth centuries

### Catelin Geoffroy, Lyon, 1557, and Jacques Viéville, Paris, c. 1650

**Documented survivors:** Early French packs reveal that French tarot iconography was not initially uniform. Viéville’s trumps depart from what later became the Marseille standard.

**Scholarly reconstruction:** The three-figure Love composition emerged within the transmission of the Italian game into France, but no surviving document names its inventor or explains its figures.

### Jean Noblet, Paris, c. 1650

**Documented object:** Noblet’s *L’Amoureux* presents a central young man flanked by two women, with a winged Cupid above aiming an arrow. The central figure’s body and gaze do not resolve into a simple, unambiguous choice.

**Change:** The old couple has become a triangle. The title is singular: the subject is the person undergoing love, desire, or decision.

**Later interpretation:** One woman becomes mother or respectable bride, the other beloved or seductress. Neither identity is printed on the card.

### Jean Dodal, Lyon, c. 1701–1715

**Documented object:** The basic trio continues. Dodal’s Cupid appears blindfolded or visually obscured and flies diagonally downward.

**Interpretive effect:** Blind Cupid emphasizes contingency: the youth may be chosen by Love rather than freely choosing between women.

### Nicolas Conver, Marseille, 1760

**Documented object:** Conver regularized the composition familiar from modern Marseille reproductions: young man, two women, a radiant disk or sky-opening above, and Cupid aiming downward. Details of hands, gaze, flowers, crowns, and hair remain graphically ambiguous.

**Choice of Hercules claim:** The resemblance to Hercules at the crossroads between Virtue and Vice is strong enough to have attracted occultists and historians. It is nevertheless an iconographic hypothesis, not a title or explanation transmitted by Conver.

**Mother-and-bride claim:** Likewise plausible as social genre imagery but unproven. The figures might be two potential partners, kin arranging a marriage, or moral personifications.

### B.-P. Grimaud, nineteenth–twentieth centuries

**Documented object:** Grimaud’s *Ancien Tarot de Marseille* standardized Conver-derived forms and colors for mass circulation. A V&A example of *L’Amoureux* is dated 1891. [V&A tarot history](https://www.vam.ac.uk/articles/tarot-cards).

**Change:** Industrial lithography made one particular Marseille reading appear timeless and canonical.

### Camoin–Jodorowsky, published 1997

**Documented modern reconstruction:** Philippe Camoin and Alejandro Jodorowsky combined features selected from multiple historic Marseille exemplars, recolored them, and presented the result as a “restored” tarot.

**Modern interpretive emphasis:** Jodorowsky and Marianne Costa read the figures as a network of emotional, familial, erotic, and spiritual relationships rather than requiring a binary “good woman/bad woman” choice.

**Controversy:** It is not a facsimile of a lost single original. “Restoration” expresses their synthetic and esoteric theory; historians treat it as a modern redaction.

## Minchiate, Florence, sixteenth–eighteenth centuries

**Documented structure:** Minchiate expands the trump series to forty, creating a 97-card pack. The original tarot subjects survive selectively among zodiacal signs, elements, virtues, and additional figures.

**Love card:** Trump VI remains a Love/Lovers scene in surviving eighteenth-century examples; the V&A holds a Florentine card dated 1712–1721.

**Game consequence:** Because the pack adds nineteen trumps above and among the inherited subjects, correspondence based on a simple 22-card Major Arcana does not apply. Gemini also exists as its own separate zodiac trump—usually numbered 35—so identifying Love VI with Gemini would be redundant within historical Minchiate. [V&A](https://www.vam.ac.uk/articles/tarot-cards), [Minchiate overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minchiate).

## Bolognese tarocchino

**Documented pattern:** Bologna reduced the pack to 62 cards by removing numeral cards 2–5 from each ordinary suit. Its trumps were traditionally unnumbered; the four *papi* had equal rank.

**Love:** The card remains in the low earthly sequence and normally depicts a couple under Cupid rather than making the French triangle indispensable.

**Game meaning:** It is a low trump, valuable chiefly as a trump and, in some scoring combinations, as part of sequences rather than as an individually high-point card.

## Sicilian tarot

**Documented pattern:** Sicilian tarot developed a 63-card form with distinctive names, altered images, and an unusually low-ranking Fool. It belongs broadly to the southern family but cannot simply be equated with Bologna or Minchiate.

**Love:** A Love/Lovers subject survives in the sequence; iconography is closer to southern Italian pair/Cupid traditions than to Waite’s Eden.

**Caution:** Modern “Sicilian tarot” reproductions frequently normalize names and numbers. Historical packs and rules must be consulted separately.

## Besançon / Swiss 1JJ

**Documented fact:** These packs retain the Marseille Lovers composition. Their famous changes concern trumps II and V—Juno and Jupiter—rather than VI.

**Importance:** This demonstrates selective religious renaming: Love was stable enough to remain while the papal figures were replaced.

## Etteilla, Grand Etteilla, Paris, 1788–1789

**Documented redesign:** Jean-Baptiste Alliette, “Etteilla,” created the first tarot pack expressly designed for occult divination. He reordered all 78 cards and printed upright and reversed keywords.

**Equivalent card:** No. 13, **Mariage**. A high cleric joins or blesses a man and woman. The image fuses aspects of Marseille Pope and Love.

**Change:** Ambiguous desire becomes a formal, sacerdotal bond. The card’s upright heading is *Mariage*; the inverse term is *Union* in early lists, altered to *Liaison* in some later Grimaud forms.

**Meaning:** Etteilla’s operative vocabulary includes marriage, union, meeting, alliance, vow, intimacy, coupling, chain, servitude, and constraint. The apparently contradictory sequence treats marriage both as conjunction and as a binding tie. [Etteilla trump study](https://etteillastrumps.blogspot.com/2012/05/cards-13-17.html).

**Astrology caveat:** Etteilla distributed astrological and elemental material through his reconstructed Egyptian cosmogony, but a simple “Etteilla Lovers = Gemini/Zayin” is false. Those are Golden Dawn assignments.

## Oswald Wirth, Paris, 1889; revised 1926–1927

**Documented object:** Wirth produced twenty-two occult trumps under Stanislas de Guaita’s direction. His *L’Amoureux* intensifies the moral contrast: a modest, dignified woman and a younger, sensuous, vine- or flower-crowned woman flank the youth; Cupid hovers above.

**Meaning:** The picture visually commits to the Vice/Virtue reading that earlier Marseille cards merely permit. Wirth’s later *Tarot des imagiers du Moyen Âge* associates it with liberty, choice, temptation, doubt, and ordeal.

**Historical irony:** Wirth described the cards as medieval initiatory images, while his own diagrammatic refinements are late nineteenth-century occult inventions. [BnF-derived Wirth image](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:06_L%27Amoureux,_Oswald_Wirth_Tarot_Deck_1889_BnF.jpg), [study of Wirth’s two tarots](https://shs.cairn.info/article/CDU_091_0043/pdf?lang=fr).

## Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn, c. 1888–1900

**Documented manuscripts/tradition:** Members copied and painted personal decks according to order instructions rather than using one universally issued commercial pack.

**Design:** The Lovers was to embody Perseus rescuing Andromeda from the sea monster, or a closely related mythic deliverance scene, in some Golden Dawn descriptions. The card received Zayin, Gemini, and the title “Children of the Voice; Oracle of the Mighty Gods.”

**Reconstructions:**

- Robert Wang’s *Golden Dawn Tarot* (1977) is a modern painted reconstruction based on Israel Regardie’s publication and Golden Dawn material.
- Sandra Tabatha Cicero’s *Golden Dawn Magical Tarot* (2000) is another modern reconstruction associated with Chic and Sandra Cicero.
- Neither should be mistaken for a surviving official 1890s printed Golden Dawn deck.

**Conflict:** Waite, Case, Crowley, Wang, and the Ciceros inherit the correspondences but not a single immutable picture.

## Waite–Smith, London, 1909–1910

**Documented object:** Designed by Arthur Edward Waite, drawn by Pamela Colman Smith, first issued in 1909; Waite’s explanatory *Pictorial Key* followed in 1910. The V&A catalogues Smith as designer and Rider as publisher/printer. [V&A](https://www.vam.ac.uk/articles/tarot-cards).

**Image:** Naked Adam and Eve occupy a paradisal landscape. Behind Eve is the Tree of Knowledge with serpent and fruit; behind Adam is a tree bearing twelve flames. A mountain rises between them. Above, a great winged figure emerges from cloud and solar radiance.

Waite wrote:

> “The figures suggest youth, virginity, innocence and love before it is contaminated by gross material desire.”

He calls the scene “human love” restored to first principles and says it replaces both an old marriage card and the later “man between vice and virtue.” [Waite, *Pictorial Key*](https://sacred-texts.com/tarot/pkt/index.htm).

**Raphael question:** Waite’s published chapter calls the being a “great winged figure”; it does not explicitly name Raphael there. Identification as Raphael is a strong Golden Dawn-derived tradition, fitting Raphael’s Air/Mercury/healing associations, but should not be reported as Smith’s printed caption.

**Sources:** Genesis is explicit. Golden Dawn Zayin/Gemini symbolism explains the doubled humanity and airy angelic oversight. Sola Busca influenced several Smith pips, but no documented compositional source proves that its imagery generated this card.

**Change in meaning:** The decision between women becomes the archetypal polarity of consciousness, sex, innocence, knowledge, and Fall. Yet Eve faces the angel while Adam looks to Eve, implying a chain from conscious man through subconscious woman toward superconsciousness—an interpretation developed explicitly by Case.

## Paul Foster Case and BOTA, 1920s–1930s onward

**Documented design:** Case and Jessie Burns Parke adapted Waite-Smith. The deck was issued in black outline for students to color using prescribed correspondences.

**Image:** Adam, Eve, trees, serpent, mountain, sun, and angel remain. Zayin is printed on the card. Case corrected or exposed what he considered Waite’s deliberate “blinds.”

**Meaning:** “Discrimination is the keynote of the 6th Key.” The sword divides; Gemini presents complementary opposites; the angel is superconsciousness, man self-consciousness, and woman subconsciousness. BOTA assigns orange, musical note D, Gemini, and airy quality. [BOTA’s Case text](https://botaineurope.org/the-tarot/6-the-lovers), [BOTA publication scan](https://www.bota.org/images/resources/lantern/Lantern8-3P.pdf).

## Thoth Tarot, painted 1938–1943; book 1944; deck 1969

**Documented object:** Aleister Crowley designed the deck and Lady Frieda Harris painted it. The cards were completed largely during 1938–1943, reproduced in *The Book of Thoth* in 1944, and first commercially issued as a complete deck in 1969.

**Image:** A black-robed Hermit or Magus officiates an alchemical wedding. The royal pair are opposites—black king/white queen—whose union generates transformed children and symbols. Cain and Abel appear; weapons and cups mirror separation and conjunction. Lions, eagles, serpents, and an Orphic egg integrate alchemical emblems. Cupid/Eros appears in a transformed initiatory context.

Crowley says the card:

> “is the symbol of creation of the world.”

He treats it as the first alchemical solve, followed by Art/Temperance as coagula. He also writes that it “ought not to be called The Lovers, but The Brothers,” linking its sword-letter to division and its Gemini twins to Cain and Abel. [Crowley, *Book of Thoth* scan](https://www.rahoorkhuit.net/library/crowley/equinox/volume3/vol_3_no_05.pdf).

**Hermit:** The hooded initiator is the concealed Mercury who conducts the marriage of opposites. This makes the card simultaneously erotic union, fraternal conflict, analysis, and synthesis.

**Orphic egg:** The winged or serpent-entwined egg contains the result of the conjunction—the latent androgynous life born from opposition.

**Cain and Abel:** Crowley’s assertion that the Hebrew biblical account was stolen from a superior initiatory fable is an esoteric polemic, not historical scholarship.

## Jodorowsky and Costa, 1997–2004

**Documented modern system:** In the Camoin-Jodorowsky deck and *The Way of Tarot*, the Marseille image is interpreted through gesture, color, body orientation, family systems, sexuality, and vocation.

**Meaning:** The central figure may be forming an adult erotic bond while negotiating maternal attachment. But Jodorowsky and Costa deliberately proliferate possibilities: the card can be relational choice, pleasure, vocation, social group, or learning to love without possession.

**Status:** Modern therapeutic-esoteric reading, not recovered medieval doctrine.

## Notable recent redesign tendencies

**Modern invention:**

- LGBTQ+ and nonbinary decks replace Adam/Eve with varied bodies and relationships.
- Feminist decks reject the “virtuous woman versus temptress” binary.
- Animal and abstract decks render the card as attraction, mirroring, chemical affinity, or intersecting paths.
- Some decks restore the fifteenth-century wedding; others privilege Marseille choice or Thoth alchemy.
- Rachel Pollack’s *Shining Tribe* entwines angelic and human forms so sex and gender become deliberately indeterminate.
- Contemporary decks increasingly use **values alignment and consent** rather than marriage as the central theme.

These are legitimate artistic reinterpretations but should not be projected backward as the card’s original meaning.

# The game

## What kind of card is it?

**Documented fact:** The Lovers is a permanent trump. In trick-taking play it beats ordinary suit cards but loses to higher trumps. It is not a suit card and therefore has no suit-following identity when played as a trump.

## Rank

In the standard French/Marseille numbered order it is Trump VI:

1. Bateleur  
2. Papesse  
3. Impératrice  
4. Empereur  
5. Pape  
6. Amoureux  
7. Chariot

In many modern French tarot games, VI is an ordinary low trump. It carries no special *oudler* status; the three oudlers are 1, 21, and the Excuse.

## Point value

**French tarot:** Trump VI normally counts like an ordinary card—½ point when paired in standard counting. It has no intrinsic premium.

**Tarocchi/Tarocchino:** Scoring varies greatly. Love may contribute to a *versicola*, sequence, or combination even if it has little isolated capture value.

**Minchiate:** Individual trumps can score and combine in *versicole*. Love VI belongs to the inherited low sequence; precise scoring depends on rule set and period.

**Tarock:** Central European games normally retain numbered trumps but abandon the allegorical name. Trump VI is simply Tarock VI and generally has no special point value beyond card-counting conventions. Pagat/I, XXI, and Sküs/Fool hold the privileged roles.

## The three regional orders

Michael Dummett classified early Italian orders:

- **Type A, southern:** Bologna and Florence; later Rome and Sicily. The Angel is normally highest; the virtues cluster differently from Marseille.
- **Type B, eastern:** Ferrara and probably Venice. Justice stands unusually high, near Judgment/Angel.
- **Type C, western:** Milan, Piedmont, and France. This produced the familiar Marseille sequence and numbering.

**Scholarly reconstruction:** Since early cards lacked numbers, local players had to memorize rank. Divergent orders could therefore become established without changing the pictures.

**Love’s relative stability:** Love is consistently a low social or “conditions of life” trump, generally above papal/imperial figures and below the Chariot, although exact neighboring ranks vary.

## Justice and Strength

The familiar Marseille order has Justice VIII and Strength XI. Waite exchanged them—Strength VIII, Justice XI—to preserve Golden Dawn astrological ordering while retaining Hebrew letters in sequence. Crowley kept that exchange.

This dispute barely changes The Lovers’ number but reveals that “canonical order” depends on whether one means historical French play or Golden Dawn occult architecture.

## The Fool

- In most Italian and French tarot games, the Fool is an Excuse: it normally cannot win a trick but can be reclaimed by its player subject to compensation.
- In Central European Tarock, the Sküs became the highest trump.
- In Sicilian tarot, the Fool is a very low numbered trump.
- Occult decks commonly label it 0, while traditional game packs frequently leave it unnumbered.

The Lovers therefore does not interact with one universal Fool rule.

# Chronology of attestation

## c. 1418–1425 — Marziano da Tortona

**Documented text:** Marziano describes sixteen divine trumps painted for Filippo Maria Visconti. Cupid is the lowest.

**Qualification:** This is not the canonical twenty-two-trump tarot and does not name an Amore card in the later sense.

## 1440 — Florence

**Documented record:** Giusto Giusti records *naibi a trionfi*. This is currently the earliest widely accepted documentary use of triumph cards, earlier than the once-standard 1442 date.

## 10 February and 28 July 1442 — Ferrara

**Documented records:** The Este accounts record payments connected with *carte da trionfi*, including work by Jacopo Sagramoro.

**Important correction:** The ledger establishes packs called trionfi, not the individual name or image of Love.

## c. 1441–1445 — Cary–Yale Love

**Documented survivor:** The earliest extant Love subject in a tarot-like Visconti pack.

## c. 1450–1460 — Visconti-Sforza Love

**Documented survivor:** Couple joining hands beneath blind Cupid.

## 1450s–1470s — Italian diffusion

Records appear in Milan, Ferrara, Florence, Bologna, and other cities. Luxury painted packs coexist with cheaper printed cards.

## c. 1470–1500 — *Sermones de ludo cum aliis*

**Documented manuscript:** A Dominican or clerical sermon denounces games and gives one of the earliest complete trump lists. It includes **Amor** among the worldly powers.

**Dating caution:** “c. 1470” is traditional; paleographical dating and the manuscript’s compilation history produce broader late-fifteenth-century ranges.

**Importance:** It demonstrates a recognized named sequence, not merely isolated pictures.

## Late fifteenth century — Boiardo and tarot poems

Matteo Maria Boiardo devised a poetic tarot-like pack organized around virtues, passions, and literary figures. Other poems attach verses to individual cards.

**Qualification:** These works show that cards invited moral and literary interpretation during the Renaissance. They do not establish secret divination or Kabbalah.

## c. 1494–1509 — transition from *trionfi* to *tarocchi*

**Documented linguistic development:** Forms of *tarocco/tarocchi* appear around 1500. The etymology remains uncertain. It is not demonstrably Egyptian.

## 1520s — Teofilo Folengo

Folengo’s macaronic writing uses tarot cards and named trumps in literary or satirical play.

**Importance:** Tarot was culturally legible as a game and metaphor. Nothing in Folengo proves occult initiation.

## Sixteenth century — France

The Italian game enters France. French cardmakers establish patterns ancestral to Marseille.

## c. 1650 — Noblet and Viéville

The three-person French *L’Amoureux* becomes visible in surviving packs.

## 1701–1715 — Dodal

The Marseille triangle and Cupid are stabilized.

## 1760 — Conver

Conver’s model becomes one of the most reproduced Marseille standards.

## 1781 — Court de Gébelin and Comte de Mellet

Court de Gébelin publishes his Egyptian theory in volume VIII of *Monde primitif*. De Mellet assigns the twenty-two Hebrew letters to the trumps.

**Documented intellectual event; unsupported history:** This is the decisive public birth of occult tarot history, not evidence of ancient Egyptian tarot.

## 1783–1785 — Etteilla’s manuals

Etteilla publishes systematic tarot cartomancy and claims older Egyptian authority.

## 1788–1789 — Grand Etteilla

The first purpose-designed occult tarot enters circulation. Marriage becomes card 13.

## 1854–1856 — Éliphas Lévi

*Dogme et rituel de la haute magie* makes tarot a universal Kabbalistic alphabet. Lévi places Hebrew letters in natural sequence beginning with Magician/Aleph; Lovers therefore receives **Vav**, not Golden Dawn Zayin.

## 1863–1870 — Paul Christian

Jean-Baptiste Pitois, “Paul Christian,” develops Egyptian initiation narratives and popularizes the language of numbered “arcana.”

## 1888 — Mathers

S. L. MacGregor Mathers publishes *The Tarot: Its Occult Signification, Use in Fortune-Telling, and Method of Play*, giving practical meanings.

## 1888 onward — Golden Dawn

The order fixes Lovers = Zayin = Gemini = path 17 and supplies the esoteric title.

## 1889 — Papus and Wirth

Papus publishes *Le Tarot des Bohémiens*; Wirth produces his 22-card occult deck. Both build on Lévi, although Papus retains Vav/Taurus in his published natural-order scheme while Golden Dawn uses Zayin/Gemini.

## 1909–1910 — Waite–Smith

Smith replaces the Marseille triangle with Adam, Eve, and the angelic figure.

## 1931 onward — Case/BOTA

Case systematizes Lovers as discrimination, polarity, and superconscious mediation.

## 1938–1944 / 1969 — Thoth

Harris paints Crowley’s alchemical Lovers; the book appears in 1944 and the complete deck in 1969.

## 1969–1973 — Calvino

Calvino first publishes the Visconti-based section of *The Castle of Crossed Destinies* in 1969; the expanded book appears in 1973 and includes a Marseille-based second section.

## 1997–2004 — Camoin, Jodorowsky, Costa

A synthetic “restored” Marseille deck appears, followed by a comprehensive interpretive system.

## Golden Dawn technical correspondences

- **Key scale:** 17.
- **Letter:** Zayin, ז.
- **Literal mnemonic:** sword or weapon.
- **Zodiac:** Gemini.
- **Elemental quality:** mutable Air.
- **Planetary ruler:** Mercury.
- **Tree:** Binah to Tiphareth in the conventional Hermetic diagram.
- **Title:** “The Children of the Voice; the Oracle of the Mighty Gods.”
- **King scale color:** orange.
- Additional color scales in *777*: pale mauve and related yellow-grey/mauve tones, with editions differing in wording and printing.

**Important distinction:** The correspondence of Zayin to Gemini derives from Hermetic readings of *Sefer Yetzirah*; the overlay of that letter/sign on The Lovers is a Golden Dawn occult construction, not Jewish Kabbalah.

## Pips and courts requested in the template

The Lovers is a trump, so it has **no decan title** of its own and no court-card sub-element. For comparison:

- Golden Dawn pip cards 2–10 correspond to zodiacal decans and receive Book T titles such as “Lord of …”
- Courts correspond to elemental sub-elements and zodiacal spans.
- Those systems do not make Lovers VI a decan card or a court.

## Tzaddi/Heh and Emperor/Star

Crowley’s “Tzaddi is not the Star” revelation exchanges the Hebrew connections of Emperor and Star in his Thelemic system.

**Documented non-effect:** It does **not** change Lovers = Zayin = Gemini. It matters here only because correspondence tables that describe a universal, never-changing sequence are misleading: Crowley himself revised part of it.

# Divinatory meanings by author

Quotations are kept brief; editions and fuller texts are listed below.

## Etteilla, 1783–1789 tradition

### Upright

> “Mariage.”

Expanded vocabulary: union, meeting, joining, alliance, vow, oath, intimacy, coupling, chain, slavery, captivity, servitude.

### Reversed

> “Union.”

Later Etteilla-derived Grimaud packs often print **Liaison**, giving the reversed position a more illicit or unstable sexual implication.

**Interpretation:** Marriage may be desirable conjunction or constraining bond. The long word list is Etteilla’s actual combinatory cartomantic practice, not a modern set of “positive/negative keywords.”

## S. L. MacGregor Mathers, 1888

### Upright

> “Union, Junction, Marriage, Inheritance.”

### Reversed

> “Failure, Foolish Designs.”

Some editions and derivative tables add unwise plans or failure under trial. [Mathers scan](https://www.howardnema.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/12/the-tarot-1888-by-macgregor-mathers-1.pdf).

## A. E. Waite, 1910

### Upright

> “Attraction, love, beauty, trials overcome.”

### Reversed

> “Failure, foolish designs.”

Waite adds that another account gives frustrated marriage and contrarieties. [*Pictorial Key*](https://sacred-texts.com/tarot/pkt/index.htm).

## Aleister Crowley, 1944

Crowley’s appended divinatory summary emphasizes:

### Upright

Openness to inspiration, intuition, intelligence, childishness, attraction, beauty, love; sometimes union or marriage.

### Ill-dignified/reversed

Self-contradiction, instability, indecision, shallow combination, or separation. Crowley’s system relies more on dignity and surrounding cards than mechanically turning every card upside down.

**Interpretive center:** Analysis and synthesis: the sword divides, but alchemical marriage reunites.

## Paul Foster Case, 1930s–1940s

### Upright

Choice guided by discrimination; the right relation of conscious, subconscious, and superconscious mind; harmonizing complementary opposites; love as an attractive and synthesizing power.

### Reversed

Case did not make crude fortune-telling reversals central to BOTA teaching. The contrary condition is failed discrimination, emotional imbalance, or subconscious response uncontrolled by higher guidance.

## Rachel Pollack, 1980

In *Seventy-Eight Degrees of Wisdom*, Pollack treats the card as love, relationship, emotional and sexual maturity, and the choice to leave parental structures for adult partnership. She also connects the RWS image to innocence, communication, and the loss or transformation of Eden.

### Upright

Love, openness, relationship, meaningful choice, reconciliation of inner and outer life.

### Reversed

Blocked relationship, immature fantasies, difficulty making a choice, or failure to communicate the self honestly.

**Status:** Influential modern psychological interpretation, not a transcription of Golden Dawn doctrine.

## Mary K. Greer, 1980s onward

Greer’s interactive approach asks the reader to inhabit the image and identify projections among its figures.

### Upright

Relationship, attraction, values-based choice, communication, integrating opposites, or recognizing what another person mirrors.

### Reversed

Projection, split desire, incompatible values, relationship with oneself neglected, or a choice disowned.

**Caution:** Greer frequently resists universal fixed meanings; these are themes across her exercises, not a single immutable keyword list.

# Esoteric and symbolic readings

## Christian

### First historical layer

**Documented Renaissance context:** Marriage, Cupid, fidelity, and social alliance are legible in Christian Italy, even though Cupid is pagan. Tarot artists routinely mixed classical personifications with Christian hierarchy.

### Waite’s Eden

**Esoteric attribution, 1909–1910:** Adam, Eve, serpent, trees, and angel transform Love into prelapsarian unity and the conditions of the Fall. Waite insists the card is not merely fallen sexuality.

### Raphael

**Tradition:** The angel is commonly called Raphael, healer and guide of marriage in the Book of Tobit. Waite does not explicitly name him in the relevant printed description, so the attribution should be called Golden Dawn-derived tradition, not a documented Smith label.

## Classical and moral

### Cupid

**Documented from the fifteenth century:** Cupid makes love an external, irrational power. His blindfold occurs in early Italian and French images.

### Choice of Hercules

**Scholarly hypothesis / occult tradition:** Hercules at the crossroads chooses between Virtue and Vice, personified as two women. The literary theme is ancient and was widely represented in Renaissance and early-modern art.

**Evidence for connection:** The Marseille trio structurally resembles the subject; Lévi, Papus, and Wirth explicitly moralize the women.

**Evidence against certainty:** The cards do not label Hercules, Virtue, or Vice; the earliest surviving Love cards show a couple rather than a moral crossroads. Thus it is best understood as a later interpretive overlay on a changed French image.

## Kabbalistic

### Lévi’s Vav

**Esoteric attribution, 1850s:** Lévi mapped Aleph to Trump I and proceeded sequentially, giving Lovers VI Vav. Vav means hook or connector, suiting union.

### Golden Dawn Zayin

**Esoteric attribution, c. 1888:** By placing the Fool at Aleph/0 and shifting the following sequence, the Golden Dawn gave VI Zayin. Zayin’s sword supplies discrimination and division; Gemini supplies twins and duality.

**Conflict:** Both cannot be the original Hebrew identity of the card. They are competing nineteenth-century mappings.

## Astrological

- **Papus:** Vav, Taurus, April.
- **Golden Dawn/Waite/Case/Crowley:** Zayin, Gemini.
- **Popular modern tarot:** Gemini, communication, duality, choice.
- **Historical cards:** No fifteenth-century inscription supports either Taurus or Gemini.
- **Minchiate complication:** Gemini has its own separate trump, demonstrating that historical game imagery does not require Golden Dawn identity.

## Hermetic and magical

**Golden Dawn:** The card expresses an oracle or inspired voice descending from Understanding to Beauty.

**Case:** Higher consciousness influences conscious and subconscious mind impartially.

**Crowley:** Mercury officiates the conjunction of opposites; analysis and synthesis are phases of one magical act.

## Alchemical

**First explicit tarot elaboration:** Nineteenth-century occultists proposed alchemical readings generally, but Crowley’s Thoth card supplies the fullest card-specific alchemical program.

- Black king and white queen: opposed tinctures or principles.
- Marriage: *coniunctio*.
- Sword: separation and analysis.
- Hermit/Mercury: officiating transformative intelligence.
- Children/twins: divided products.
- Orphic egg: new life or synthesized substance.
- Art/Temperance XIV: continuation of Lovers VI, *solve* followed by *coagula*.

**Disputed historical projection:** Renaissance alchemical marriage imagery existed before Crowley, but no contemporary evidence says the fifteenth-century Love trump secretly encoded the Great Work.

## Jungian

### Emergence

Modern Jungian tarot arose mainly after Jung’s archetypal psychology had become popular in mid-century Anglophone esotericism. Jung commented on symbolic and synchronistic systems but did not publish a canonical tarot exegesis.

### Readings

- Lover and beloved as conscious identity and projected anima/animus.
- Two women as socially approved persona versus shadow desire.
- Adam and Eve as differentiated consciousness emerging from unconscious unity.
- Angel as Self or transpersonal regulating center.
- Choice as withdrawal of projection and responsible relation.
- Alchemical wedding as *coniunctio oppositorum*, integration of psychic opposites.

**Status:** Modern Jungian application. Claims that Jung personally assigned Lovers these meanings are usually untraceable.

# People and works

## Artists and printers

- **Michelino da Besozzo:** painter of Marziano’s lost divine-trump deck.
- **Bonifacio Bembo and workshop:** traditional attribution for Visconti cards.
- **Francesco Zavattari, Benedetto Bembo, Franco dei Russi:** alternative or card-specific attributions debated in museum scholarship.
- **Jacopo Sagramoro:** Ferrarese painter named in the 1442 accounts.
- **Jean Noblet, Jean Dodal, Nicolas Conver:** principal French pattern makers.
- **B.-P. Grimaud:** industrial transmitter of Conver-derived Marseille imagery.
- **Oswald Wirth:** occult designer of the 1889 trumps.
- **Pamela Colman Smith:** artist who made Eden the dominant English-language Lovers image.
- **Lady Frieda Harris:** painter who translated Crowley’s crowded alchemical program into the Thoth composition.
- **Jessie Burns Parke:** artist of Case’s BOTA keys.
- **Philippe Camoin and Alejandro Jodorowsky:** creators of a modern synthetic Marseille reconstruction.

## Occultists

Court de Gébelin, Comte de Mellet, Etteilla, Éliphas Lévi, Paul Christian, Papus, Stanislas de Guaita, Wirth, Mathers, Waite, Crowley, Case, Israel Regardie, Robert Wang, and Chic and Sandra Cicero successively redefined the card.

## Historians

- **Michael Dummett:** reconstructed tarot primarily through game history and regional trump orders.
- **Ronald Decker and Thierry Depaulis:** with Dummett, traced occult tarot to eighteenth-century France.
- **Stuart R. Kaplan:** assembled extensive deck and image documentation.
- **Gertrude Moakley:** connected Renaissance trumps with triumphal pageantry; influential, though not all conclusions prevailed.
- **Robert M. Place:** contextualizes images in Renaissance art and later occultism, while accepting the Italian game origin.
- **Helen Farley:** examines tarot’s cultural movement from entertainment to esotericism.
- **Andrea Vitali and Le Tarot association:** collect Italian documentary and iconographic materials.
- **Ross Caldwell, Franco Pratesi, and tarot-history researchers:** refined early documentary chronology, including the 1440 Florentine evidence.

## Literature

### Italo Calvino, *The Castle of Crossed Destinies*

**Documented:** The first section uses the Visconti-Sforza images; the later “Tavern” uses Marseille cards. Mute travelers arrange cards to narrate intersecting lives.

**Lovers function:** Depending on position and adjacent cards, it can signify erotic involvement, rival partners, seduction, alliance, or narrative intersection. Calvino’s method deliberately demonstrates that a card is not exhausted by a dictionary meaning. [Franco Maria Ricci](https://www.francomariaricci.com/en/books/tarots), [academic discussion](https://escholarship.org/content/qt4dq8w2g9/qt4dq8w2g9.pdf).

### T. S. Eliot, *The Waste Land* (1922)

**Documented:** Madame Sosostris handles a tarot-like pack, naming the drowned Phoenician Sailor, Belladonna, the man with three staves, the Wheel, the one-eyed merchant, and a blank card.

**Absence finding:** The Lovers is not named. Attempts to make it a hidden structural card are critical interpretations, not textual fact.

### W. B. Yeats

Yeats was deeply involved with the Golden Dawn and tarot symbolism was part of his occult milieu.

**Absence finding:** No single canonical Yeats work can securely be described as a direct literary treatment of The Lovers VI without further argument. General Golden Dawn affiliation is not card-specific evidence.

## Film

### Alejandro Jodorowsky

Tarot permeates Jodorowsky’s visual and spiritual practice and appears explicitly in his writings and later public tarot work.

**Caution:** Claims that a particular scene in *El Topo*, *The Holy Mountain*, or *Santa Sangre* is a literal quotation of Lovers VI generally depend on interpretive resemblance, not production documentation.

### Ingmar Bergman

Bergman’s films repeatedly treat couples, choice, temptation, faith, and death.

**Absence finding:** No reliable production or scholarly source located establishes a specific quotation of The Lovers card. Associating Bergman’s couples with this trump is thematic comparison, not documented influence.

### Other screen quotation

Disney’s *The Haunted Mansion* opening has been discussed as using Lovers, Death, and Three of Swords to summarize love and loss. This is an identifiable modern tarot motif, unlike broad thematic parallels. [Tarot-motif discussion](https://buffystudies.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/02/pie_watcherjunior_9.1.pdf).

## Paintings and buildings

**Comparative sources, not tarot quotations:**

- Palazzo Schifanoia’s April/Venus fresco is a useful parallel for Ferrarese Love imagery.
- Renaissance “Choice of Hercules” paintings offer a model for the Marseille moral reading.
- Marriage portraits with joined hands and fidelity dogs illuminate Cary–Yale.

**Absence finding:** Frequently repeated claims that a particular church portal or cathedral encodes the entire tarot sequence have not yielded documentary evidence connecting a building specifically to Lovers VI. Similarity of allegorical imagery is expected in a shared Christian-classical culture.

# Controversies and disputes

## Was the earliest card a wedding portrait?

**Evidence:** Cary–Yale has dynastic heraldry, joined hands, canopy, *AMOR*, and fidelity dog.

**Scholarly reconstruction:** A Visconti-Sforza marriage reference is persuasive.

**Unproved extension:** That it was commissioned specifically for Bianca Maria and Francesco’s 1441 wedding, or that the figures are individualized portraits.

**Verdict:** Probable dynastic-marriage imagery; exact event and identities remain disputed.

## Who are the Marseille figures?

Possibilities include:

1. A man choosing between Virtue and Vice.
2. Hercules at the crossroads.
3. A young man between mother and bride.
4. A man between wife and mistress.
5. A family conducting a marriage.
6. A generalized lover caught in erotic and social claims.

**Evidence:** Gesture, dress, age, wreaths, and Cupid.

**Problem:** Woodcut details differ by maker, and no early maker supplied an explanatory text.

**Verdict:** Deliberately or accidentally ambiguous. Lévi, Papus, and Wirth made Vice/Virtue explicit; that does not prove Noblet or Conver intended it.

## Is Cupid blindfolded?

**Documented variation:** The Milanese Eros is clearly blindfolded. Dodal’s Cupid appears blind or banded; Conver’s may appear to see his target.

**Interpretive tradition:** “Love is blind.”

**Caution:** Damage, recutting, and crude printing can convert a facial line into a blindfold. Each impression must be examined independently.

## Is Waite’s angel Raphael?

**Tradition:** Yes, overwhelmingly in Golden Dawn-derived teaching.

**Primary-text problem:** Waite’s *Pictorial Key* calls it a “great winged figure,” not Raphael.

**Verdict:** Plausible and tradition-backed, but not explicitly documented in Waite’s public description.

## Egyptian origin

### Claim

Court de Gébelin argued in 1781 that tarot preserved an Egyptian Book of Thoth. He supplied speculative Egyptian etymologies for *tarot*.

### Spreaders

Comte de Mellet, Etteilla, Paul Christian, Papus, nineteenth-century occult publishers, and Crowley’s deliberately Egyptianizing “Thoth” terminology.

### Evidence

No Egyptian tarot cards, no ancient text describing them, no transmission chain, and no valid Egyptian etymology. Gébelin wrote before Egyptian hieroglyphs were deciphered.

### Historical correction

Dummett, Decker, and Depaulis document an Italian fifteenth-century game followed by eighteenth-century occult reinterpretation. Dummett notes that occult etymologies cite no genuine hieroglyphic forms. [Dummett](https://www.nybooks.com/articles/1981/05/14/origins-of-tarot/), [*A Wicked Pack of Cards*](https://books.google.com/books/about/A_Wicked_Pack_of_Cards.html?id=MG6DAAAAMAAJ).

**Classification:** Legend as history; documented and highly influential esoteric myth.

## Romani or “Gypsy” origin

### Claim

Romani travelers carried tarot from Egypt, India, or an ancient initiatory center into Europe.

### Development

Court’s “Egyptian” story encouraged the association; nineteenth-century writers including Jean-Alexandre Vaillant helped popularize an explicitly Romani transmission.

### Evidence against

Italian tarot records and cards predate evidence of Romani tarot practice. Romani linguistic and genetic origins point to the Indian subcontinent rather than ancient Egypt. European cartomancers later associated fortune-telling with racialized images of “Gypsies.”

**Classification:** Legend and nineteenth-century cultural construction. It should not be treated as Romani oral proof of tarot’s invention.

## Cathar origin

### Claim

The trumps encode Cathar dualism or preserve a persecuted southern French heresy.

### Evidence

No Cathar manuscript names tarot; the first tarot evidence is northern Italian and postdates the destruction of organized Cathar communities. Marseille production is later still.

**Possible source of appeal:** Lovers’ apparent moral dualism and southern French cardmaking.

**Classification:** Modern speculative legend.

## Templar origin

### Claim

Templars transmitted Egyptian, Eastern, Grail, or Kabbalistic secrets through tarot after suppression in 1312.

### Evidence

No Templar deck, rule, inventory, or text. There is a documentary gap of more than a century between the suppression and Italian trionfi.

**Classification:** Modern occult legend.

## Secret Kabbalistic encoding

### Claim

Twenty-two trumps were designed for the twenty-two Hebrew letters.

### Evidence against original design

No fifteenth-century card or rule makes the correspondence. De Mellet and Lévi assign different letters from the Golden Dawn. Etteilla rearranges the whole pack.

**Classification:** Historically late esoteric attribution, not a demonstrated Renaissance intention.

## Alchemical origin

### Claim

The trumps were an alchemical book whose sixth image always meant conjunction.

### Evidence

Alchemical marriage imagery and tarot share cultural motifs, but no early tarot document defines Love as an alchemical operation.

### First major spreaders

Lévi’s universal occult synthesis, later French occultists, Golden Dawn adepts, and above all Crowley.

**Classification:** Productive esoteric reading; unsupported as origin history.

## “The Lovers originally meant choice”

**Problem:** The earliest cards show union or marriage under Cupid. The clear two-path moral choice becomes prominent in French occult explanations.

**Verdict:** Choice is old relative to modern tarot and deeply rooted in Marseille interpretation, but it is not demonstrably the card’s earliest meaning.

## “The Lovers originally meant romantic soulmates”

The earliest imagery concerns aristocratic union, social contract, dynastic alliance, and Cupid—not the modern therapeutic notion of perfectly aligned soulmates.

**Classification:** Modern simplification.

## Misprints that became traditions

No single typographical error can be shown to have created the Lovers archetype. More defensible cases are cumulative:

- Singular French *L’Amoureux* became plural English *The Lovers*, moving attention from the chooser to the pair.
- Later recolorings turn ambiguous hair, wreaths, hands, and eye-lines into fixed moral attributes.
- Modern Grand Etteilla editions label no. 13 “High Priest,” although *Mariage* is its operative Etteilla identity; this likely reflects conflation with the officiating cleric and the Pope.
- Reproductions sometimes make Cupid blind or sighted through line loss.
- “Raphael” is repeated as though printed by Waite, although his public text does not name the angel.

These are transmission effects rather than one traceable archetypal misprint.

# Open questions

1. Can the Cary–Yale Love be tied by archival commission to Bianca Maria Visconti and Francesco Sforza, rather than by heraldry alone?
2. What exact intermediary produced the shift from Italian couple to French trio?
3. Did the Marseille makers consciously quote the Choice of Hercules, or did occultists recognize a resemblance after the fact?
4. Are the flanking figures differentiated primarily by age, marital status, erotic availability, moral quality, or merely regional dress?
5. How stable was Love’s exact rank before printed numbers? The sermon lists and regional orders answer locally but not for every fifteenth-century pack.
6. Which Golden Dawn manuscript recension first gave the card’s complete esoteric title and mythic design instructions?
7. Did Waite privately specify Raphael to Smith, or did the identification arise from shared Golden Dawn assumptions?
8. Which visual sources besides Genesis and Golden Dawn diagrams did Smith use for the composition? A direct model has not been documented.
9. How much of Crowley’s iconographic program originated in his instructions and how much in Harris’s independent research and design judgment?
10. Can individual modern Jungian meanings be traced to a first author? Much of the vocabulary diffused through Pollack, Greer, Case, Sallie Nichols, and counseling-oriented tarot without clean priority.
11. No satisfactory card-specific evidence was found for direct Lovers quotations in Eliot, Yeats, or Bergman; future archival or production research could alter that finding.
12. “Cathar,” “Templar,” and ancient Egyptian origin theories lack contemporary evidence, but a full reception history of each legend remains larger than a single-card dossier.
