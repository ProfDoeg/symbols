# The Chariot: Research Dossier

> **Evidence labels**
>
> - **[FACT]** directly documented by a surviving object, archival record, or text.
> - **[RECONSTRUCTION]** inference accepted or seriously entertained by historians.
> - **[TRADITION]** an inherited interpretation or conventional attribution, without proof of ancient origin.
> - **[ESOTERIC]** a correspondence deliberately assigned within an occult system.
> - **[DISPUTED]** a claim for which specialists or sources disagree.
> - **[LEGEND]** a historical origin story unsupported by contemporary evidence.
> - **[MODERN]** a twentieth- or twenty-first-century redesign or interpretive development.

### The essential historical distinction

**[FACT]** The card began as a game trump in fifteenth-century northern Italy. No fifteenth-century document assigns it Cheth, Cancer, Kabbalah, alchemy, initiation, or divinatory meanings.

**[FACT]** The Morgan catalogue describes its Chariot as a crowned woman carrying a cross-surmounted globe beneath a Gothic arch, conveyed by two winged horses. The museum dates the object broadly to about 1450–80, while recent exhibition material favors approximately 1456–58. [Morgan object record](https://www.themorgan.org/collection/tarot-cards/the-chariot), [Morgan manuscript description](https://ica.themorgan.org/manuscript/page/6/158525).

**[ESOTERIC]** Cheth and Cancer belong to the Golden Dawn synthesis of the late nineteenth century, not to Renaissance tarocchi. That attribution was inherited by Waite, Crowley, Paul Foster Case, and many modern English-language decks.

## Names, numbering, and regional terminology

### From *trionfi* to *tarocchi*

**[FACT]** Fifteenth-century Italian records usually call the new cards *carte da trionfi*, “cards of triumphs.” The singular card could accordingly be a *trionfo*, although surviving early documents more often identify the pack than individual trumps.

**[FACT]** *Tarocchi* became common in Italy during the early sixteenth century. The etymology remains unsettled. Attempts to derive it from Egyptian phrases such as *tar-ro*, “royal road,” are products of eighteenth-century occult speculation, not historical linguistics.

**[FACT]** French *tarot*, German *Tarock*, and related forms descend from the Italian term. In gaming language, *tarot* can mean the pack, the family of games, or an individual trump.

### Number VII

**[FACT]** Early Italian painted trumps were normally unnumbered. “VII” is therefore not original to the surviving Visconti cards.

**[RECONSTRUCTION]** The Chariot nevertheless occupies a comparatively stable position in the low-middle sequence: above Love/Lovers and below a changing group containing Justice, Fortitude, Wheel, and the Hermit/Time.

**[FACT]** French Marseille-pattern cards print **VII**. Oswald Wirth, Waite-Smith, BOTA, and Thoth retain VII.

### Regional substitutions elsewhere in the sequence

These renamings concern the surrounding tarot tradition rather than Chariot itself, but they explain why historical lists cannot always be aligned mechanically:

- **[FACT]** In the Belgian/Besançon pattern, the objectionable Catholic figures Papess and Pope were replaced by **Juno** and **Jupiter**. This pattern was in circulation by the eighteenth century, especially in eastern France, Switzerland, and German-speaking markets.
- **[FACT]** Some French traditions call the Tower **La Maison Dieu**. This title is printed in the Marseille family and predates occult explanations of it as a temple, lightning-struck tower, or “house of God.”
- **[FACT]** The Fool/*Mat* was generally unnumbered in Marseille packs. Gébelin explicitly interpreted it as a cipher or zero; later occult decks commonly print 0.
- **[FACT]** Waite placed Strength at VIII and Justice at XI, following a sequence compatible with Golden Dawn astrological order. Marseille decks normally place Justice VIII and Strength XI.
- **[FACT]** In Bologna, the four highest-ranking “popes” became visually neutralized or equal-ranking *papi* after ecclesiastical and political pressure; numbering practices varied.
- **[FACT]** Sicilian tarot substantially renamed and reordered its trump series. Its distinctive *Miseria* is the lowest trump, while the Fool has a special gaming role.

## Iconography deck by deck

## Precursors and cultural sources

### Petrarch’s *Trionfi*

**[FACT]** Francesco Petrarca’s *Trionfi*, composed and revised during the fourteenth century, describes allegorical triumphal processions: Love, Chastity, Death, Fame, Time, and Eternity.

**[RECONSTRUCTION]** The poem and the broader culture of triumphal entries almost certainly helped make *trionfi* a natural name and visual framework for the card series. Gertrude Moakley made Petrarch’s poem central to her reconstruction of tarot as a courtly triumph procession.

**[DISPUTED]** No surviving commission, rule book, or artist’s note says that the tarot sequence was designed as a direct pictorial edition of Petrarch. Several tarot subjects do not map neatly onto his six Triumphs. Petrarch is therefore a plausible cultural source, not a proved blueprint.

### Marziano da Tortona and Michelino da Besozzo, about 1418–25

**[FACT]** Marziano designed for Filippo Maria Visconti a card game with sixteen classical gods and four bird suits. A 1449 letter by Jacopo Antonio Marcello calls it a *triumphorum genus*. The cards themselves are lost; Marziano’s explanatory treatise survives.

**[FACT]** There is no Chariot among Marziano’s named gods.

**[RECONSTRUCTION]** Historians regard the pack as a close precursor because its special allegorical cards outrank the ordinary suits. It is not, however, the standard twenty-one-trump tarot.

### Mantegna “Tarocchi,” about 1460s

**[FACT]** These are fifty educational engravings organized into five decades—social estates, Apollo and the Muses, liberal arts, virtues, and cosmic spheres.

**[FACT]** They are not a tarot pack and contain no standard Chariot trump.

**[TRADITION]** Occult and art-historical books long discussed them as “Mantegna Tarocchi,” encouraging the impression that they were an alternative tarot. Their relevance is comparative: they reveal the allegorical vocabulary available in Renaissance Italy.

## Fifteenth-century Milan

### Cary-Yale / Visconti di Modrone

**[FACT]** The deck was made for the Visconti court before Filippo Maria Visconti’s death in 1447, although dates between about 1441 and 1447 are proposed. It survives at Yale’s Beinecke Library.

**[FACT]** The Chariot depicts a richly dressed crowned woman carried in a ceremonial vehicle. The female ruler, court dress, heraldic luxury, and non-military presentation connect the image to dynastic triumph rather than ordinary transport.

**[DISPUTED]** “Cary-Yale” and “Visconti di Modrone” are often treated as names of the same surviving group, though cataloguing histories and reconstructions differ.

**[RECONSTRUCTION]** The woman has been identified variously as an allegory of Fame, Chastity, Victory, a Visconti bride, or a courtly ruler. No identifying inscription survives.

### Brambilla deck

**[FACT]** The Brambilla cards are attributed to the Bembo workshop and preserved principally at the Pinacoteca di Brera.

**[FACT]** The surviving set is fragmentary. A complete Chariot cannot safely be used to reconstruct the card’s iconography.

**Finding:** Claims that describe a fully preserved Brambilla Chariot usually conflate it with Cary-Yale or Visconti-Sforza material.

### Visconti-Sforza / Pierpont Morgan–Bergamo deck, about 1450s

**[FACT]** The Morgan card shows:

- a crowned woman seated or standing high in a chariot;
- a cross-surmounted orb in her left hand;
- a Gothic architectural canopy;
- two gold or pale winged horses;
- a gold ground and elaborate court dress.

The museum catalogues it as Milanese, about 1450–80, and attributes the original group traditionally to Bonifacio Bembo or his workshop, while recording alternative attributions. [Morgan catalogue](https://www.themorgan.org/manuscript/158525).

**[RECONSTRUCTION]** The winged horses evoke supernatural or triumphal transport rather than a working war chariot. The orb connotes dominion. The woman can be read as Victory, Fame, Chastity, or a ducal personification.

**[DISPUTED]** Her identity is not documented. Describing her categorically as Bianca Maria Visconti exceeds the evidence.

### Este/Ferrarese cards and the “Issy Chariot”

**[FACT]** A hand-painted Chariot associated stylistically with Ferrarese production survives at the Musée Français de la Carte à Jouer, Issy-les-Moulineaux. It belongs to a small group sometimes linked with cards now in Warsaw.

**[FACT]** The figure is a standing male charioteer with a staff, baton, sword, or sceptre—the object is not wholly unambiguous—and differs from the enthroned Milanese woman.

**[RECONSTRUCTION]** This may represent a regional move from feminine courtly triumph toward a victorious prince or commander.

**[DISPUTED]** Exact workshop, date, and relationship to the so-called Charles VI/Este cards remain contested.

### Sola Busca, 1491; colored copy about 1490s

**[FACT]** The Sola Busca is the earliest substantially complete engraved tarot and the earliest surviving pack with narrative scenes on all the pip cards.

**[FACT]** Its trumps bear classical or pseudo-classical warriors rather than the standard Chariot sequence. It is most relevant to Chariot history through its general militarized antiquarian language and its later influence on Pamela Colman Smith’s minor cards.

**[RECONSTRUCTION]** Several Waite-Smith pip compositions appear indebted to the Sola Busca, probably through photographs displayed at the British Museum in 1908. That does not establish a direct Sola Busca source for Smith’s Chariot.

## Early printed sheets

### Rosenwald sheets, Florence, around 1500

**[FACT]** The National Gallery of Art holds three uncut sheets generally assigned to Florence around 1500.

**[FACT]** The Chariot is a woodcut image of a standing crowned male in a wagon, holding an orb and a sword or staff. It has no large Marseille canopy. The draftsmanship of the wheels and vehicle is spatially awkward.

**[FACT]** Surviving numerals/order evidence differs from Marseille. One reconstruction places Chariot around X rather than VII.

**[RECONSTRUCTION]** The sheet supplies strong evidence that a male victor and a box-like vehicle were already established in popular printed tarot before the surviving French Marseille decks.

### Budapest/Metropolitan sheets

**[FACT]** Fragmentary uncut sheets in Budapest and New York preserve another early printed Italian pattern.

**[DISPUTED]** Their date, city, and precise regional family remain debated. Proposed descriptions of the Chariot’s implement alternate among sword, sceptre, and baton because the impression is damaged or stylized.

**Finding:** These sheets cannot securely prove a single linear evolution from Milan to Marseille. They demonstrate parallel early variants.

## Florence and Minchiate

**[FACT]** The expanded Florentine game, first called *germini* and later *minchiate*, contains forty trumps plus the Fool.

**[FACT]** Chariot survives among the inherited tarot subjects but is embedded in an enlarged series incorporating virtues, elements, and zodiac signs.

**[FACT]** In established minchiate order, the low sequence includes Fortitude, Justice, Wheel, and Chariot; Chariot therefore does not occupy Marseille VII.

**[FACT]** Minchiate Chariot imagery commonly presents a triumphal vehicle with a female or allegorical victor, varying by maker.

**[RECONSTRUCTION]** The female figure may preserve a Florentine conception of triumph/Fame rather than the princely Marseille hero.

---

## Bologna

**[FACT]** Tarocchino Bolognese reduced the pack to sixty-two cards by removing numeral cards 2–5 from each suit.

**[FACT]** Chariot is conventionally called **Carro** and belongs among the low trumps. Later printed packs may number it 6, but early practice did not consistently print all trump numerals.

**[FACT]** The Bolognese image can resemble a winged or Mercury-like traveler in a small vehicle.

**[DISPUTED]** Identifying the figure specifically as Mercury is an iconographic proposal, not a surviving historical title.

**[FACT]** In scoring, Chariot is generally an ordinary trump rather than a high-value counting card. Its importance lies in trick-taking rank and possible combinations, not an intrinsic five-point status like the highest trumps.

---

## Sicily

**[FACT]** Tarocco Siciliano is a shortened regional pack with distinctive trump names and order.

**[FACT]** Its Wheel and Chariot are adjacent in surviving order. The Chariot is an ordinary trump, not one of the pack’s supreme counting cards.

**[RECONSTRUCTION]** Sicilian features show contact with Bologna, minchiate, and other Italian traditions, but the direction and date of every borrowing cannot be proved.

## Tarot de Marseille lineage

### Jean Noblet, Paris, about 1650s

**[FACT]** Noblet’s is the earliest substantially surviving deck of what historians conventionally call the Tarot de Marseille type. The BnF dates the maker’s known activity to 1659–81 and the cards to the second half of the seventeenth century. [BnF Noblet record](https://essentiels.bnf.fr/fr/image/4799f2d6-6f88-4f6f-a15c-40a1a943c95e-tarot-jean-noblet).

**[FACT]** Chariot VII shows a crowned prince or warrior rising from a square vehicle under a canopy. Two horses face somewhat divergent directions. The driver has no visible reins.

**[FACT]** Faces or masks appear on the shoulders. Later readers call them lunar faces, Urim and Thummim, masks, epaulettes, or the letters “SM.”

**[DISPUTED]** No early key explains these shoulder ornaments. “SM” may be lettering, stylized faces, a workshop survival, or a reader’s visual extraction. Claims that it definitely means *Soufre et Mercure*, *Sa Majesté*, or a secret initiatic formula are later inventions.

### Jean Dodal, Lyon, about 1701–15

**[FACT]** Dodal standardizes the familiar frontal prince, four-post canopy, two horses, sceptre, wheels, and colored shoulder faces.

**[FACT]** The vehicle does not behave realistically: horses are not clearly harnessed, wheels are set oddly, and the prince seems fused to the car.

**[ESOTERIC]** Later Marseille readers interpret these discontinuities as evidence that will, mind, or invisible spiritual force—not reins—directs the horses. That meaning is not printed on the historical card.

### Nicolas Conver

**[FACT]** Modern reproductions frequently say **1760**.

**[FACT/DISPUTED DATE]** The BnF’s biographical authority record states that Nicolas Conver was born in 1784 and active as a Marseille card maker from 1809 to 1833. It explicitly says the deck conventionally dated 1760 cannot be attributed to him, an ancestor, or a namesake. [BnF Conver authority record](https://catalogue.bnf.fr/ark:/12148/cb16721159s).

Thus “Conver 1760” is an entrenched catalogue/reproduction tradition, not a secure manufacture date for Nicolas Conver’s pack.

### Grimaud, twentieth century

**[FACT]** Grimaud’s *Ancien Tarot de Marseille* standardized a cleaned, mass-market version of the Conver-associated pattern. Paul Marteau’s 1930s editions greatly shaped twentieth-century perceptions of “the” Marseille deck.

**[MODERN]** Color was regularized and linework rationalized. This made the deck easier to reproduce but concealed historical variation among Noblet, Dodal, Conver, and other makers.

### Camoin–Jodorowsky, 1990s

**[FACT]** Philippe Camoin and Alejandro Jodorowsky produced a reconstructed Marseille deck from comparison of surviving examples.

**[MODERN/DISPUTED]** They call it a “restoration,” but no single original printing contains every selected line and color. It is a synthetic reconstruction involving interpretive decisions.

**[MODERN]** Jodorowsky and Marianne Costa read VII as “action in the world.” They emphasize the prince’s composite or possibly androgynous appearance, the differently directed horses, the driver’s lower body hidden by the vehicle, and conscious direction without visible reins.

## Etteilla

### Grand Etteilla, 1789

**[FACT]** Jean-Baptiste Alliette, “Etteilla,” produced the first tarot specifically redesigned for occult divination. Original uncolored sheets survive in the BnF. [BnF/Gallica Grand Etteilla sheets](https://gallica.bnf.fr/ark:/12148/btv1b10545802x).

**[FACT]** Etteilla reordered and renamed the entire deck. Consequently, “card 7” in Grand Etteilla is not necessarily the Marseille Chariot.

**[ESOTERIC]** Marseille Chariot material was assimilated to Egyptian-cosmogonic and astrological concepts and to the meanings **Support/Protection** in later Grand Etteilla traditions.

**[CAUTION]** The widely reproduced “Grand Etteilla III” or *Grand Jeu de l’Oracle des Dames* belongs to a later nineteenth-century redaction, not unchanged 1789 artwork. Its titles must not automatically be quoted as Etteilla’s own 1789 wording.

## Éliphas Lévi and Oswald Wirth

### Lévi, 1854–56

**[ESOTERIC]** In *Dogme et Rituel de la Haute Magie*, Lévi describes the seventh key as a conqueror in a cubic chariot drawn by a white and a black sphinx. The opposing sphinxes embody forces mastered by intelligence and will.

**[FACT]** Lévi’s synthesis mixes Kabbalah, Hermeticism, Christian ceremonial magic, and Egyptianizing imagery.

**[FACT]** His French sequence aligns Chariot with **Zain**, not Golden Dawn Cheth, because he began the Hebrew alphabet differently in relation to the trumps.

### Oswald Wirth, 1889

**[FACT]** Wirth designed twenty-two trumps under the direction of Stanislas de Guaita; a later expanded version accompanied *Le Tarot des imagiers du Moyen Âge*.

**[FACT]** His Chariot closely visualizes Lévi: cubic vehicle, crowned initiate, sceptre, canopy, opposing black and white sphinxes, crescents, and occult emblems.

**[ESOTERIC]** Wirth reads the car as the astral vehicle and the sphinxes as contrary impulses mastered by intelligence. In the French occult allocation, Chariot is associated with **Zain** and commonly with **Gemini**, conflicting with the Golden Dawn’s Cheth–Cancer.

## Golden Dawn

### Book T and manuscript designs, approximately 1888–1900

**[FACT]** The Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn assigned:

- Chariot = **Cheth**;
- Cheth = **Cancer**;
- Path = **18**, Binah to Geburah;
- esoteric title = **Child of the Powers of the Waters; Lord of the Triumph of Light**.

**[ESOTERIC]** The four color scales for Cheth are generally given as amber; maroon; rich bright russet; and dark greenish brown.

**[FACT]** Members were expected to construct or color personal tarot designs. There was no single commercial “original Golden Dawn deck.”

### Wang and Cicero reconstructions

**[MODERN]** Robert Wang’s *Golden Dawn Tarot* and Chic and Sandra Tabatha Cicero’s *Golden Dawn Magical Tarot* reconstruct lodge designs from documentary descriptions and later Golden Dawn materials.

**[CAUTION]** Their images are reconstructions, not facsimiles of one definitive nineteenth-century master pack.

**[FACT]** The Chariot’s Cheth–Cancer attribution is consistent across these reconstructions, while artistic particulars differ.

## Waite-Smith, 1909

**[FACT]** Arthur Edward Waite directed the deck; Pamela Colman Smith drew it. It was first issued by Rider in 1909, with Waite’s illustrated guide appearing in 1910/1911.

**[FACT]** Smith depicts:

- a crowned, armored charioteer;
- a starry canopy;
- a city and river behind;
- black and white sphinxes instead of horses;
- no visible reins;
- lunar shoulder ornaments;
- a winged disk and lingam-yoni-like shield emblem;
- a wand rather than an operational driving implement.

**[FACT]** Waite openly acknowledges Lévi as the source of the sphinx substitution:

> “I have accepted the variation of Éliphas Lévi; two sphinxes thus draw his chariot.”

He calls the figure “conquest on all planes” but limits this conquest to mind, science, progress, and outward initiation, not the higher “world of Grace.” [Waite, *Pictorial Key*, Chariot chapter](https://www.sacred-texts.com/tarot/pkt/pktar07.htm).

**[RECONSTRUCTION]** Smith’s star canopy probably synthesizes Marseille’s baldachin with Golden Dawn celestial symbolism. There is no documentary proof that a particular Renaissance painting supplied the entire composition.

**[DISPUTED]** The emblem on the car is regularly called Masonic. It can be compared with a mallet, winged globe, and sexual/alchemical union signs, but Waite’s own published text does not decode every line.

## Paul Foster Case and BOTA

**[FACT]** Paul Foster Case commissioned Jessie Burns Parke to draw the BOTA deck. It closely follows Waite-Smith while exposing Golden Dawn correspondences more explicitly.

**[FACT]** The cards were issued in black and white for students to color according to prescribed scales.

**[ESOTERIC]** Case reads:

- charioteer = Inner Self;
- chariot = body or personality;
- sphinxes = senses;
- invisible reins = mind;
- wall, armor, car, crab, and Cheth = enclosure and protection;
- victory = “Conquest of Illusion.”

The official BOTA summary gives Cheth, Cancer, D-sharp, and “watery” alchemy. [BOTA, Key 7](https://botaineurope.org/the-tarot/7-the-chariot).

---

## Thoth, painted 1938–43; published 1969

**[FACT]** Aleister Crowley designed and Lady Frieda Harris painted the Thoth Tarot between 1938 and 1943. The full commercial deck appeared in 1969.

**[FACT]** Harris depicts:

- an armored charioteer in amber;
- four sphinxes rather than two, compounded from the four kerubic creatures—bull, lion, eagle, and human;
- a revolving or geometrically complex vehicle;
- a canopy and wheels integrated into an energetic field;
- the Holy Grail held before the charioteer.

Crowley writes:

> “The Charioteer is clothed in the amber-coloured armour appropriate to the sign.”

and states that the four sphinxes combine the four Kerubs. [Crowley, *Book of Thoth*, PDF](https://www.rahoorkhuit.net/library/crowley/equinox/volume3/vol_3_no_05.pdf).

**[ESOTERIC]** The card represents Cancer as the first onrush of cardinal Water and the descent of influence from Binah through the watery/bloody veil toward Geburah.

**[ESOTERIC]** Crowley identifies the Grail with the “blood of the Saints,” Babalon, and a receptive vehicle of transformed life-force.

**Important correction:** Crowley’s famous “Tzaddi is not the Star” revision affects the **Emperor and Star**, exchanging Heh and Tzaddi in his final scheme. It does **not** alter Chariot = Cheth = Cancer.

## Notable modern redesigns

**[MODERN]** Common twentieth- and twenty-first-century transformations include:

- replacing the male prince with a woman, nonbinary figure, animal, vehicle, or abstract mandala;
- turning the vehicle into a car, motorcycle, spacecraft, wheelchair, or psychological “container”;
- presenting the two animals as divided drives, racialized opposites, shadow/light, instinct/reason, or political factions;
- emphasizing travel and ambition rather than triumph;
- recasting conquest as emotional regulation or trauma survival.

**[MODERN]** Decks influenced by Jung increasingly treat the opposed creatures as autonomous complexes requiring integration rather than enemies to be subdued.

## The game

## Rank and trick-taking function

**[FACT]** In tarot games, a trump normally beats any card of the four ordinary suits. A higher trump beats a lower trump, subject to local rules.

**[FACT]** Chariot is not ordinarily a special high-value trump. Its value comes chiefly from its place in the trump hierarchy.

**[FACT]** In many French tarot and central-European Tarock games, traditional pictorial titles disappear: trump VII is simply Trump 7. Its historical Chariot identity survives only in Italian-suited or esoteric decks.

### French Tarot

**[FACT]** In the modern 78-card French game, trump VII is a low trump worth the ordinary card value—half a point under the standard paired-counting method. It is not an *oudler*. The three oudlers are Trump 1, Trump 21, and the Excuse.

### Tarock

**[FACT]** In Austrian and central-European Tarock packs, Chariot iconography is usually absent and Roman-numeral trumps are replaced by genre scenes. Trump VII ranks above VI and below VIII unless a game has special local rules. It normally carries one card point.

### Italian tarocchi and tarocchini

**[FACT]** Chariot is an ordinary trump. Bologna’s reduced pack and Sicilian tarot have distinctive ranking and scoring combinations, but Chariot is not one of the supreme counting cards.

### Minchiate

**[FACT]** Chariot belongs to the inherited lower series. The five highest trumps and the Fool are more important for counting. Chariot can matter in sequences or *verzicole*, depending on the ruleset.

## The three regional orders

Michael Dummett and Thierry Depaulis grouped early Italian sequences into three broad order families:

- **Order A:** Bologna and Florence;
- **Order B:** Ferrara and Venice;
- **Order C:** Milan and much of the later Marseille ancestry.

**[FACT]** Chariot is consistently near Love and the cardinal virtues, but Justice, Fortitude, Wheel, and Time/Hermit migrate around it.

**[FACT]** The sequence was originally learned by memory because early trumps lacked printed numerals.

**[DISPUTED]** No surviving fifteenth-century rulebook establishes one universal original order. Calling Marseille order the original order projects a later French standard backward.

## Justice and Strength

**[FACT]** Marseille has Justice VIII and Strength XI.

**[FACT]** Waite-Smith has Strength VIII and Justice XI.

**[FACT]** The switch was not Smith’s arbitrary mistake. It corresponds to Golden Dawn astrological ordering—Leo at VIII, Libra at XI—and has precedents in regional Italian variations.

## The Fool

**[FACT]** The Fool was historically a special card, not simply the lowest trump. It excused its player from following suit or surrendering a valuable card, subject to local compensation rules.

**[FACT]** In French play it remains the *Excuse* and is an oudler.

**[TRADITION]** Numbering it 0 is an occult and later publishing convention, though Gébelin explicitly compared its null value to zero.

## Chronology of attestation

### About 1418–25: Marziano

**[FACT]** Marziano’s mythological pack supplies the earliest surviving description of a game with a separate hierarchy of special figures. It does not name Chariot.

### 1440–42: early *trionfi* records

**[FACT]** Records from Florence and Ferrara establish *trionfi* as a card-game term.

**[FACT]** On 28 July 1442 the Ferrara court paid the Bolognese merchant Marchionne Burdochi for “uno paro de carte da trionfi” for the young Ercole and Sigismondo d’Este. [Le Tarot transcription](https://letarot.it/Le-origini-italiane-del-Ludus-Triumphorum_pag_pg439_ita.aspx).

### Approximately 1441–47: Cary-Yale Chariot

**[FACT]** Earliest surviving recognizable Chariot, subject to dating debate.

### Approximately 1450s: Visconti-Sforza

**[FACT]** The female sovereign, winged horses, canopy, and orb are materially documented.

### Late fifteenth/early sixteenth century: *Sermo de ludo cum aliis*

**[FACT]** The anonymous sermon condemns the game and supplies the earliest surviving complete or near-complete named trump list. It calls the card *lo carro triumphale vel mundus parvus*—“the triumphal chariot, or little world.”

**[RECONSTRUCTION]** “Little world” may describe the chariot as a mobile microcosm or merely gloss its appearance. Later occultists sometimes treat it as proof of Hermetic microcosm doctrine, but the sermon itself is polemical, not an occult manual.

### Around 1500: Florentine poems and printed sheets

**[FACT]** A Florentine *tarocchi* song and the Rosenwald sheets witness alternative ordering. Their lists help historians reconstruct Order A.

### 1527: Teofilo Folengo

**[FACT]** Folengo’s *Chaos del Triperuno* contains *tarocchi appropriati* sonnets distributing trump names poetically. One line reads:

> “vide MORTE su’l CARRO orrenda e bruna”

“[Love] saw dreadful, dark Death upon the Chariot.” [Italian text and discussion](https://forum.tarothistory.com/viewtopic.php?t=225).

**[FACT]** This is literary play, not proof of formal divination.

### 1534: Troilo Pomeran

**[FACT]** *Triomphi … sopra li Terrocchi* assigns tarot trumps to Venetian noblewomen. “Carro Triomphale” is associated with Maria Loredan. [Bibliographical description](https://ilab.org/assets/catalogues/catalogs_files_1689_16013_20pomeran.pdf).

### About 1650s–1700s: French pattern

**[FACT]** Noblet and Dodal establish the named and numbered **VII LE CHARIOT**.

### About 1750: Bologna cartomancy manuscript

**[FACT]** An anonymous Bolognese manuscript is the earliest securely documented set of tarot divinatory meanings currently known. It predates Gébelin.

**[CAUTION]** It does not demonstrate Kabbalistic or Egyptian tarot; its meanings are rudimentary fortune-telling glosses.

### 1781: Gébelin and de Mellet

**[FACT]** Gébelin’s *Monde primitif* reinterprets Chariot as **Osiris triumphant** and proposes an Egyptian origin.

**[FACT]** The Comte de Mellet’s accompanying essay connects the twenty-two special cards with Hebrew letters and describes a divinatory method.

### 1783–91: Etteilla

**[FACT]** Etteilla publishes operational tarot cartomancy and then a redesigned occult pack.

### 1854–56: Lévi

**[FACT]** Lévi transforms the horses into opposed sphinxes and integrates tarot with Kabbalah and ceremonial magic.

### 1889: Papus and Wirth

**[FACT]** Papus publishes *Le Tarot des Bohémiens*; Wirth issues twenty-two occult trumps. Both spread French Kabbalistic-Egyptian tarot internationally.

### 1888–1900: Golden Dawn

**[FACT]** The Order fixes Chariot = Cheth = Cancer = Path 18.

### 1909–11: Waite-Smith

**[FACT]** Smith popularizes the black and white sphinxes, star canopy, city, river, invisible control, and esoteric armor.

### 1930s: BOTA and Grimaud

**[FACT]** Case publishes his explicitly Qabalistic version; Grimaud/Marteau standardizes Marseille imagery for a mass audience.

### 1938–43/1969: Thoth

**[FACT]** Harris paints Crowley’s four-sphinx, Grail-bearing Chariot; the full deck is published posthumously in 1969.

### 1980 onward: psychological tarot

**[FACT]** Rachel Pollack, Mary K. Greer, and many successors shift emphasis toward self-direction, integration, disciplined will, and internal conflict.

### Golden Dawn colors and Path 18

**[ESOTERIC]** Cheth’s color scales are commonly transmitted as:

- King scale: amber;
- Queen scale: maroon;
- Emperor scale: rich bright russet;
- Empress scale: dark greenish brown.

**[ESOTERIC]** The path runs from Binah, Understanding/Great Mother, to Geburah, Severity or Power. This makes Crowley’s armored receptive vehicle a carrier of force across the watery veil.

### Pips and courts

The requested decan and court data apply to the whole Golden Dawn system, but **not specifically to Chariot**, which is a major trump:

- pips 2–10 correspond to 36 zodiacal decans and bear Book T titles;
- court Knights/Kings, Queens, and Princes occupy zodiacal spans of roughly thirty degrees, usually combining portions of three decans;
- Princesses correspond to terrestrial quadrants rather than ordinary thirty-degree spans;
- courts combine rank element and suit element, such as Prince of Cups = Air of Water.

Assigning one pip decan or court sub-element to Chariot would be a category error.

## Esoteric and symbolic readings

## Alchemical

**[ESOTERIC]** Lévi and Wirth made the paired black and white creatures emblems of opposites—fixed/volatile, active/passive, sulfur/mercury, or contrary currents—mastered by the operator.

**[ESOTERIC]** Case reads the winged solar globe and sexual union emblem as conjunction of opposing forces. His attribution “watery” follows Cancer.

**[MODERN]** Some contemporary writers call the Chariot *coagulatio* because spirit is enclosed in a vehicle; others call it *circulatio* because of wheels and cyclic movement. There is no single classical alchemical attribution universally attached to VII.

## Kabbalistic

**[ESOTERIC]** Lévi’s French ordering gives the card Zain. The Golden Dawn gives it Cheth. This is not a translation variant but a genuine systematic conflict.

**[ESOTERIC]** Golden Dawn Cheth means fence/enclosure. The body, armor, canopy, city wall, and crab shell become nested containers for consciousness.

**[ESOTERIC]** Path 18 links Binah and Geburah: understanding/maternal form flows into severity or directed power.

## Astrological

**[ESOTERIC]** Golden Dawn: Cancer, cardinal Water, ruled by the Moon.

**[ESOTERIC]** The apparent contradiction between watery Cancer and a fast war vehicle is answered by occultists in several ways:

- Cancer supplies protective armor and enclosure;
- cardinal Water is forceful initiation of feeling;
- the crab travels indirectly while retaining its inner center;
- lunar consciousness controls the vehicle invisibly.

**[FACT]** None of these explanations is attested in Renaissance card-game sources.

## Hermetic and Platonic

**[TRADITION/ESOTERIC]** Plato’s *Phaedrus* compares the soul to a charioteer managing two horses. Wirth and later authors explicitly use this analogy.

**[DISPUTED SOURCE]** The visual resemblance makes Plato attractive as an antecedent, but no fifteenth-century tarot source says the card illustrates the *Phaedrus*. It becomes a clearly documented interpretive source in modern occult commentary.

**[ESOTERIC]** The sermon’s “little world” was later read as the human microcosm carried through the macrocosm. This is a plausible Hermetic expansion, not necessarily the sermon writer’s intention.

## Christian

**[FACT]** The Visconti-Sforza orb is surmounted by a cross, a Christian and sovereign emblem.

**[TRADITION]** Waite uses the Pauline phrase “led captivity captive,” turning military triumph into a qualified initiatory victory.

**[ESOTERIC]** Crowley places a Grail at the center but interprets it through Thelema and Babalon rather than orthodox Christian sacrament.

**[DISPUTED]** Attempts to identify the Chariot as Ezekiel’s merkabah, Elijah’s fiery chariot, Christ’s triumphal entry, or a Templar vehicle are typological comparisons. No early card is labeled with those subjects.

## Jungian

**[MODERN]** Jung did not assign tarot trumps to archetypes in a formal deck system.

**[MODERN]** Jungian tarot authors nevertheless interpret Chariot as:

- the heroic ego separating from parental structures;
- a persona or psychic vehicle;
- tension between conscious purpose and autonomous complexes;
- integration of shadow and social identity;
- inflation when the victorious ego mistakes control for wholeness.

Pollack helped popularize the developmental reading in which Chariot is a successful but incomplete stage: mastery of outer life precedes deeper confrontation with unconscious forces.

- Jessie Burns Parke: artist of Case’s BOTA tarot.
- Frieda Harris: painter of the Thoth Chariot.
- Philippe Camoin and Alejandro Jodorowsky: creators of a synthetic Marseille “restoration.”

### Occultists and historians

- Court de Gébelin: Egyptian theory and “Osiris triumphant.”
- Comte de Mellet: Hebrew-letter and divinatory theory.
- Etteilla: first professional occult tarot system and purpose-built divinatory deck.

### Literature and cinema

#### Calvino

**[FACT]** Italo Calvino’s *Il castello dei destini incrociati*/*The Castle of Crossed Destinies* constructs stories by arranging tarot cards. The first section uses the Visconti-Sforza imagery; a second employs Marseille cards.

**[FACT]** Chariot can function syntactically as travel, triumph, a vehicle, social power, or a character’s movement. Calvino does not reduce it to one occult keyword.

#### T. S. Eliot

**[FACT]** *The Waste Land* names “the man with three staves,” the Wheel, and the drowned Phoenician Sailor in Madame Sosostris’s reading.

**Finding:** It does **not** explicitly name the Chariot. Claims that Chariot is one of Eliot’s stated cards are misremembered.

#### W. B. Yeats

**[FACT]** Yeats participated deeply in Golden Dawn symbolism, making the Order’s tarot correspondences relevant to his intellectual milieu.

**Finding:** No canonical Yeats poem could be securely identified as a direct quotation of Chariot VII merely from the searches consulted. General claims that “Yeats used the Chariot card” require work-specific evidence.

#### Jodorowsky

**[FACT]** Tarot permeates Jodorowsky’s psychomagic, writings, performances, and cinematic symbolism.

**[DISPUTED]** Vehicles, initiatory journeys, and triumphal figures in films such as *The Holy Mountain* can be compared to Chariot imagery, but not every such scene is a documented quotation of VII.

#### Bergman

**Finding:** Ingmar Bergman’s *The Seventh Seal* is frequently associated with Death, chess, and medieval allegory. A specific, documented quotation of the Chariot card could not be traced. Treat claims of direct Chariot influence as unverified.

### Paintings and buildings

**[RECONSTRUCTION]** Renaissance triumphal-entry art, Petrarchan *Trionfi* manuscripts, Roman triumph imagery, triumphal arches, and ceremonial parade cars supply the nearest cultural environment.

**[DISPUTED]** No single painting or building is demonstrably the unique prototype of the card.

**[TRADITION]** Later occultists compare the four-column canopy with a Masonic lodge, Temple of Solomon, cosmic cube, or mobile sanctuary. Those are retrospective symbolic readings.

## Controversies and disputes

## Egyptian origin

**[LEGEND]** Gébelin claimed tarot was an Egyptian wisdom book preserved under the guise of a game. He identified Chariot as Osiris triumphant.

**Evidence offered:** visual resemblance, speculative etymology, numerical analogy, and the assumption that Egypt was the fountainhead of ancient wisdom.

**Evidence absent:** Egyptian cards, a transmission chain, hieroglyphic texts, medieval references to Egyptian tarot, or recognizable pharaonic prototypes for Pope, Emperor, and other trumps.

**[FACT]** Gébelin wrote before Champollion’s decipherment of Egyptian hieroglyphs.

**[SCHOLARLY CORRECTION]** Dummett, Decker, and Depaulis trace tarot through fifteenth-century Italian documents and the occult theory to 1781. Dummett stressed that alleged Egyptian word derivations were presented without hieroglyphic originals. [Dummett, “Origins of Tarot”](https://www.nybooks.com/articles/1981/05/14/origins-of-tarot/).

## Romani or “Gypsy” origin

**[LEGEND]** Nineteenth-century writers increasingly claimed Romani travelers carried tarot from Egypt or India.

**[FACT]** Papus’s title *Tarot des Bohémiens* helped spread the association.

**[FACT]** Romani origins are now traced to the Indian subcontinent, not Egypt.

**[SCHOLARLY CORRECTION]** There is no evidence that Romani people invented tarot or introduced the original game into Italy. Romani fortune-tellers participated in European cartomancy, but that later practice cannot establish fifteenth-century authorship.

## Cathars, Templars, and secret heresy

**[LEGEND]** Modern esoteric histories sometimes read the Popess, Pope, Tower, Devil, and Chariot as encrypted Cathar, Templar, Gnostic, or anti-papal doctrine.

**Evidence offered:** symbolic resemblance, southern French geography, alleged survival through guilds or initiatory fraternities.

**Evidence absent:** a Cathar or Templar tarot deck, a contemporary rule or commentary, a transmission document, or an institutional record linking either movement to *carte da trionfi*.

**[DISPUTED]** Individual images may admit heterodox readings, but the origin claim is unsupported.

## Chastity, Fame, Victory, or a duchess?

**[DISPUTED]** The early female Chariot resembles personifications in Petrarchan triumph art. Chastity is often conveyed by unicorns; Fame and Victory may carry globes, crowns, or trumpets.

**Finding:** The Visconti-Sforza card’s winged horses and orb do not uniquely identify any one allegory. “Bianca Maria,” “Fame,” “Chastity,” and “Victory” should remain hypotheses.

## The Marseille shoulder masks and “SM”

**[FACT]** Shoulder ornaments are visible.

**[TRADITION]** Waite calls analogous forms Urim and Thummim.

**[MODERN/DISPUTED]** Readings such as Sun/Moon, sulfur/mercury, severe/merciful faces, theatrical masks, or initials are interpretive. No printer’s explanation survives.

## Sphinxes as ancient tarot

**[FACT]** Waite says he adopted Lévi’s sphinx variation.

**[FACT]** Standard Renaissance and Marseille Chariots use horses or horse-like creatures, not clearly Egyptian sphinxes.

**[DISPUTED]** Viéville’s unusual humanoid-faced animals may have encouraged later comparisons, but they do not prove an Egyptian prototype.

## “Conver 1760”

**[TRADITION/MISATTRIBUTION]** This date became canonical through reproductions and twentieth-century literature.

**[FACT]** BnF research places Nicolas Conver’s activity in 1809–33 and rejects attribution of a 1760 pack to him. This is a clear example of a repeated catalogue statement becoming tradition.

## Camoin–Jodorowsky as the “original” Marseille

**[MODERN/DISPUTED]** Their deck is a learned reconstruction from multiple witnesses. Calling it the one original tarot implies an archetype that has not survived and whose exact details cannot be demonstrated.

## The Fool’s zero

**[FACT]** Early Fool cards were normally unnumbered.

**[ESOTERIC]** Gébelin’s zero interpretation and later occult sequence made 0 meaningful as nothingness, potential, or the beginning/end of the path.

**[MODERN]** The “Fool’s Journey”—every trump as a stage in one protagonist’s life—is a valuable twentieth-century teaching structure, not a documented Renaissance design program.

## Tzaddi/Heh

**[FACT]** Crowley’s revelation “Tzaddi is not the Star” caused him to reassign Hebrew letters between Emperor and Star.

**Finding:** It does not involve the Chariot. Claims that Crowley moved Chariot away from Cheth are mistaken.

## Open questions

1. **Who is the early female charioteer?**  
   No surviving inscription settles whether she is a patron, bride, Victory, Fame, Chastity, or a composite courtly allegory.

2. **Which surviving card is absolutely earliest?**  
   Cary-Yale is normally given precedence, but dates for individual Visconti groups remain ranges rather than documented production days.

3. **What exactly did “little world” mean in the *Sermo de ludo*?**  
   It may describe the car’s enclosed miniature realm, invoke a microcosm, or preserve a regional alternative name.

4. **How direct was Petrarch’s influence?**  
   The cultural relationship is persuasive, but a commission explicitly telling artists to illustrate *Trionfi* has not been found.

5. **How did the female Milanese figure become the Marseille prince?**  
   The Rosenwald and Ferrarese material shows male variants early, suggesting parallel regional solutions rather than one documented transformation.

6. **What are the Marseille shoulder ornaments?**  
   No contemporary printer’s key survives.

7. **Which implement appears on damaged early printed Chariots?**  
   Sword, baton, and sceptre identifications depend on impression quality.

8. **Can Etteilla’s own Chariot-equivalent meanings be separated completely from posthumous Grand Etteilla redactions?**  
   This requires collation of the original sheets and successive manuals; common modern decks mix several stages.

9. **What was Pamela Colman Smith’s exact visual source beyond Lévi and Marseille?**  
   Waite documents Lévi’s sphinxes, but Smith left no known card-by-card source notebook.

10. **Which modern artworks deliberately quote VII?**  
    Many works resemble the archetype; documentary artist statements are needed to distinguish quotation from generic triumphal or vehicular imagery.
