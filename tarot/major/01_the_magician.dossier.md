# The Magician: Research Dossier

## Method and evidentiary labels

This dossier distinguishes six kinds of statement:

- **[FACT]** directly documented by a surviving card, archival record, printed rule, or primary text.
- **[RECONSTRUCTION]** an inference accepted or seriously proposed by historians from surviving objects and documents.
- **[TRADITION]** a historically transmitted interpretation whose factual premise may be unprovable.
- **[ESOTERIC ATTRIBUTION]** a correspondence deliberately assigned by an occult author or organization.
- **[DISPUTED]** a claim for which scholars or surviving evidence materially disagree.
- **[LEGEND/MODERN INVENTION]** an origin story or interpretation unsupported by evidence earlier than its identified modern source.

Dates for handmade decks are necessarily approximate. “Tarot de Marseille” is used as a modern pattern-name; the term was not the fifteenth-century name of the cards.

| French | **Le Bateleur**; Court de Gébelin also describes him as *Joueur de Gobelets* |
| English | Juggler, Mountebank, Magician, Magus |
| Spanish | **El Mago**; in historical descriptions also *el prestidigitador/titiritero* |
| German | **Der Magier**; in game dialects **Pagat**, *Bagatt/Bagat* |
| Golden Dawn title | **The Magus of Power** |

| Playing-card status | Lowest ordinary numbered trump in most 78-card Italian/French traditions |

| Earliest surviving textual name | **El Bagatella** in the anonymous *Sermo perutilis de ludo* or *Sermones de ludo cum aliis*, late fifteenth century |
| Earliest known tarot documents | Ferrara account entries of 1442; these say *carte da trionfi* but do not name the Bagatella individually |
| Earliest ancestor game | Marziano da Tortona’s sixteen-god pack, devised for Filippo Maria Visconti, about 1418–25; it is not a standard tarot and contains no Bagatto |

**[FACT]** The Morgan Visconti-Sforza card shows a seated man at a table bearing a cup, knife, and another hat- or bowl-like object. The museum calls it “The Juggler (Bagatto)” and dates it broadly to 1450–80; its current exhibition material gives the more focused attribution Milan/Cremona, about 1456–58. [Morgan object record](https://www.themorgan.org/collection/tarot-cards/the-juggler), [Morgan manuscript catalog](https://ica.themorgan.org/manuscript/158525).

**[FACT]** The earliest card is not called “Magician” on the object. Fifteenth-century painted trumps usually carry neither names nor numbers.

**[RECONSTRUCTION]** “Magician” is therefore a retrospective English title. Historically the figure began closer to a low-status entertainer, cup-and-ball operator, mountebank, or petty practitioner of tricks than to the commanding ritual adept of Waite and Crowley.

**[ESOTERIC ATTRIBUTION]** The equation **Magician = Beth = Mercury** belongs to the Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn’s late-nineteenth-century system. It is not attested in Renaissance tarot.

## The names, number, and regional vocabulary

### Italy

**[FACT]** In the fifteenth century the special trumped pack and its game were called *trionfi*—“triumphs.” Documents use phrases such as *carte da trionfi*. During the early sixteenth century *tarocchi/tarocco* displaced *trionfi* as the ordinary name.

**[FACT]** The late-fifteenth-century sermon calls the first trump:

> “El Bagatella … est omnium inferior”—“The Bagatella … is the lowest of all.”

This means lowest in game rank and simultaneously evokes a person or thing of little worth. Andrea Vitali collects later variants including *Bagatella, Bagattella, Bagatello, Bagatino, Gabbatèlla, Bagato*, and eventually *Bagatto*. [Vitali, “The Bagatto Magician”](https://letarot.it/page.aspx?id=112&lng=ENG), [Vitali, “El Bagatella”](https://letarot.it/page.aspx?id=386).

**[DISPUTED]** Its etymology is unresolved. Proposed connections include:

- *bagatella*, a trifle or worthless thing;
- *bagattino*, a low-value Venetian coin;
- low Latin *baga*, baggage or small property;
- terminology for a trick, deception, or petty entertainer.

The semantic cluster—small value, tricks, trifles, mountebanks—is better documented than any single derivation.

### France

**[FACT]** *Bateleur* meant a public entertainer, tumbler, juggler, or sleight-of-hand performer. Court de Gébelin in 1781 explicitly called the figure both *Joueur de Gobelets* and *Bâteleur*. He recognized him by the dice, cups, knives, balls, and the ball about to be palmed. [Court de Gébelin’s text](https://www.tarock.info/gebelin.htm).

**[DISPUTED]** Gébelin derived *bateleur* from *baste/bâton*. Modern etymology does not establish this as the card-name’s origin.

### German-speaking play

**[FACT]** In Austrian and central-European Tarock terminology, the lowest trump is commonly the **Pagat**, a borrowing ultimately related to Italian *Bagatto*. In many modern Tarock packs it is simply Roman numeral I and carries a genre scene rather than a magician.

**[FACT]** *Pagat ultimo* is the undertaking or bonus for winning the final trick with trump I. That gaming meaning is historically independent of the English occult “Magician.”

### Spanish usage

**[FACT]** Modern Spanish occult decks normally call the card **El Mago**. Spanish-language gaming traditions did not preserve a major indigenous 78-card tarot pattern comparable to Bologna or Marseille; Spanish names in modern decks are principally translations of French occult or Waite-Smith titles.

### Number

**[FACT]** The first trump was originally recognizable by image and order rather than printed numeral. In later Marseille packs it is **I**.

**[FACT]** The Fool normally remains outside the numbered sequence—*Matto*, *Le Mat*, *Le Fou*, *Excuse*—although occult decks frequently print **0**, and some nineteenth-century systems place it at 21 or 22 for Hebrew-letter schemes.

**[FACT]** Justice and Strength/Fortitude vary:

- Northern Italian/Milanese and Marseille-derived order: Justice VIII, Strength XI.
- Golden Dawn and Waite-Smith: Strength VIII, Justice XI.
- Crowley retains the Golden Dawn positions but renames them Lust VIII and Adjustment XI.
- Many later Marseille restorations retain Justice VIII and Strength XI.

The Magician itself remains I in virtually all standard tarot sequences, even where the Fool or middle trumps move.

### Regional renamings relevant to the wider sequence

These changes do not alter the Magician but locate it within regional patterns:

- **Besançon pattern, early eighteenth century onward, eastern France/Switzerland/Germany:** Papess II and Pope V replaced by **Juno** and **Jupiter**, probably to avoid controversial Catholic figures. [Besançon overview](https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tarot_de_Besan%C3%A7on).
- **Swiss 1JJ, nineteenth century:** retains Juno/Jupiter.
- **Bolognese Tarocchino:** four “Papi” became unnumbered/equal-ranking in play; later politically sensitive names and designs were altered.
- **Sicilian Tarot, seventeenth century onward:** trump XX is Jupiter; the lowest trump is **Miseria**, beneath trump I, while trump I is *i Picciotti* (“the lads”), also occasionally *i Bagotti*. [IPCS discussion](https://www.pagat.com/ipcs/journal/33-3.pdf).
- **Maison Dieu:** the Marseille title for trump XVI is not a Magician title but exemplifies how French card-names depart from English occult nomenclature.
- **The Fool:** unnumbered in historical play; *Excuse* in modern French game packs, *Sküs* in Austrian traditions, *Fuggitivo/Fujutu* in Sicily.

## Iconography deck by deck

## Fifteenth-century Italy

### Marziano da Tortona’s gods, Milan, about 1418–25

**[FACT]** Marziano described a pack of sixteen classical gods in four orders for Duke Filippo Maria Visconti. No cards survive, though the explanatory text does.

**[RECONSTRUCTION]** This is a precursor to the idea of permanent allegorical trumps, not an early edition of the standard twenty-two-card sequence. It has no identifiable Bagatto and supplies no evidence for Beth, Mercury, Egypt, or cups-and-balls.

### Visconti di Modrone / Cary-Yale, Milan, about 1441–45

**[FACT]** This exceptionally large luxury pack preserves only eleven trumps. No securely identified Magician survives.

**[DISPUTED]** “Visconti di Modrone” and “Cary-Yale” sometimes appear as if they were different decks, but they are alternative names for the same surviving group. Claims that its missing Bagatto had a known design are reconstruction, not observation.

### Brambilla-Brera, Milan, about 1440s

**[FACT]** The Brambilla group, now principally in the Pinacoteca di Brera, preserves two trumps and numerous suit cards. No Bagatto survives.

**[RECONSTRUCTION]** Its relation to the Visconti court establishes the same luxury-card context but tells us almost nothing directly about the first trump.

### Visconti-Sforza, Milan/Cremona, about 1456–58

**[FACT]** The man sits behind a narrow table or bench. He wears an elaborate red garment and hat. The tabletop includes a cup, a knife, and ambiguous small objects. The gold-ground treatment places a low-status performer inside aristocratic luxury.

**[DISPUTED: conjurer or craftsman?]**

- **Conjurer reading:** the cup and small objects resemble a cups-and-balls or thimblerig setup; the later French figure indisputably becomes a *bateleur*.
- **Craftsman/vendor reading:** the objects can look like tools or wares, and the seated posture lacks overt magical action.
- **Court-servant reading:** his rich clothing may reflect the visual decorum of a ducal commission rather than actual social rank.
- **Finding:** no surviving inscription says what occupation the artist intended. “Cups-and-balls player” is plausible but not demonstrable from this card alone.

The Morgan catalog’s concise description—“Man, wearing hat, sits on bench at table, upon which is hat form, cup, and knife”—appropriately avoids resolving the ambiguity. [Morgan image catalog](https://ica.themorgan.org/manuscript/158525).

### Ferrarese “Charles VI” / Este cards, late fifteenth century

**[FACT]** Several sumptuous isolated Ferrarese trumps survive in Paris and elsewhere, conventionally called the Charles VI Tarot. No secure Bagatto belongs to the surviving group.

**[DISPUTED]** Their attribution has moved among Florence, Ferrara, Bologna, and mixed workshop influence. Dress analysis has strengthened a Ferrarese/Bolognese context, but “Charles VI” is an obsolete provenance nickname, not a patronage fact.

### Sola Busca, Ferrara/Venice region, 1491

**[FACT]** The Sola Busca is the earliest substantially complete tarot with fully illustrated pip cards. Its trumps are named pseudo-Roman heroes rather than Marseille allegories.

**[FACT]** Trump I is **Panfilio**, a standing armored or classically costumed figure, not a table conjurer. Therefore it is structurally the first trump but not iconographically “The Magician.”

**[RECONSTRUCTION]** Pamela Colman Smith demonstrably borrowed compositional ideas from Sola Busca pip cards, especially Three and Ten of Swords, but no comparably direct Sola Busca source for her Magician has been established.

### Mantegna “Tarocchi,” Ferrara or northern Italy, about 1460s

**[FACT]** These are educational engravings arranged in five groups of ten, not tarot playing cards. The lowest social series begins Misero and includes **Artixan/Artigiano**, the artisan.

**[RECONSTRUCTION]** Artixan’s low social rank offers a useful comparison with Bagatella as a base or humble figure.

**[DISPUTED]** Calling Artixan “the Mantegna Tarot’s Magician” overstates the relationship. The series lacks tarot suits, tarot play, and a one-to-one trump sequence; Mantegna did not design it.

### Rosenwald sheets, Italy, late fifteenth or early sixteenth century

**[FACT]** Uncut woodblock sheets preserve a popular, non-courtly trump tradition. The low trump shows an entertainer in a conspicuous cap, closer to a juggler than the Visconti seated court figure.

**[RECONSTRUCTION]** The Rosenwald image helps bridge luxury Milanese cards and later printed popular patterns. It is evidence that the first trump was legible as a low entertainer before French occultism.

### Budapest sheets, northern Italy, late fifteenth/early sixteenth century

**[FACT]** Fragmentary printed sheets preserve another early popular pattern. Identification and original ordering of individual fragments remain partly uncertain.

**[RECONSTRUCTION]** Rosenwald and Budapest together show how standardized woodcut production simplified and circulated imagery formerly known through unique hand-painted packs.

## Regional Italian patterns

### Minchiate, Florence, sixteenth–nineteenth centuries

**[FACT]** Minchiate expands the pack to 97 cards: Fool, 56 suited cards, and 40 numbered trumps. Trump I retains a Bagatto-type performer and is the lowest numbered trump.

**[FACT]** Five highest trumps form the *Arie* and possess special scoring significance. Trump I is ordinarily low and not itself a high-value counting card, though it can participate in combinations.

**[RECONSTRUCTION]** The persistence of a low occupational figure at the bottom confirms that the early card’s place was social and ludic before it became metaphysical “creative Will.” [Minchiate rules](https://www.pagat.com/tarot/minchiate.html).

### Bologna / Tarocchino

**[FACT]** The Bolognese deck was shortened by removing numeral cards 2–5 from each suit. The Bagattino remains a low trump; the Fool remains special. Four courtly/religious trumps became equal-ranking “Papi.”

**[FACT]** In traditional scoring, Bagattino is among the cards important in combinations rather than powerful through trick rank.

**[DISPUTED]** Andrea Vitali proposes a moral “mystical staircase” in which the sinful or trivial Bagatella begins humanity’s ascent. This is an iconological argument, not a surviving fifteenth-century rulebook statement.

### Sicilian Tarot

**[FACT]** The Sicilian pattern inserts unnumbered **Miseria** beneath trump I. Trump I depicts *i Picciotti*, corresponding in play and ancestry to Bagatto. The Fool is *Fuggitivo*. [Sicilian rules](https://www.pagat.com/tarot/sicilian.html).

**[RECONSTRUCTION]** Miseria externalizes the “lowest social estate” theme found in Misero and Bagatella while allowing the inherited number I to rise one place.

## French printed lineage

### Jacques Viéville, Paris, about 1650

**[FACT]** Viéville’s pack belongs to a related Parisian family but is not identical to canonical Marseille. His low trump is a table performer.

**[RECONSTRUCTION]** Viéville and Noblet show that a recognizable *bateleur* pattern existed in Paris by the mid-seventeenth century.

### Jean Noblet, Paris, about 1650

**[FACT]** The card is labeled approximately **LE BATELEUR** and numbered I. A youthful performer stands behind a table with small cups, balls or coins, dice-like objects, knives, a purse, and a wand or implement. The tabletop and missing fourth leg create the famous three-legged appearance.

**[DISPUTED]** A damaged or crudely cut hand has been interpreted as an obscene gesture aimed at tax inspectors. No contemporary document establishes that meaning; it is modern speculation.

**[MODERN INVENTION]** Treating every ambiguous mark as an encoded sexual, alchemical, or initiatory secret is characteristic of modern close-reading, not established cardmakers’ doctrine.

### Jean Dodal, Lyon, about 1701–15

**[FACT]** Dodal stabilizes the jaunty Marseille Bateleur: broad hat, multicolored clothing, wand, table, cup-and-ball apparatus, knife, purse or counters.

**[RECONSTRUCTION]** The body twists between the wand hand and tabletop hand, making him visibly dexterous and equivocal—a seller of marvels who may also cheat.

### Pierre Madenié, Dijon, 1709

**[FACT]** The earliest dated surviving Type II Marseille pack retains Bateleur I and regularizes the figure’s face, costume, and table apparatus.

### Nicolas Conver, Marseille, 1760/1761

**[FACT]** Conver’s card is titled **LE BATELEUR**, numbered I. The youthful figure holds a short wand while manipulating objects spread across the table.

**The lemniscate hat**

- **[FACT]** The brim is broad, curved, and visually resembles a sideways figure eight in many impressions.
- **[FACT]** No early card labels it an infinity sign.
- **[ESOTERIC ATTRIBUTION]** Papus explicitly read it as “the divine sign of Universal Life ∞.”
- **[MODERN TRADITION]** It is now routinely said that the Marseille card “wears a lemniscate.”
- **Finding:** resemblance is real; an intended mathematical infinity symbol in seventeenth- or eighteenth-century Marseille manufacture is unproved.

### Grimaud / Paul Marteau, Paris, 1930

**[FACT]** Grimaud’s *Ancien Tarot de Marseille*, edited by Paul Marteau, standardized a recolored Conver-derived pattern for the twentieth century.

**[ESOTERIC ATTRIBUTION]** Marteau interpreted each color and object: the hat as adaptation and mental force, the table as living matter, and the Bateleur as humanity manipulating nature’s currents.

**[RECONSTRUCTION]** Many supposedly immemorial “Marseille meanings” descend from Marteau’s 1930s symbolic commentary rather than early cardmakers.

### Camoin–Jodorowsky, 1997

**[FACT]** Philippe Camoin and Alejandro Jodorowsky produced a redrawn, recolored Marseille deck drawing on Conver and other early packs.

**[DISPUTED]** They call it a restoration of original sacred structure. Critics describe it more accurately as an editorial synthesis containing newly selected details and colors. It is neither a literal Conver facsimile nor a neutral archaeological reconstruction.

**[ESOTERIC ATTRIBUTION]** Jodorowsky and Marianne Costa read Bateleur I as beginning, potential, youth, concentration, and the creative handling of possibilities. Sexual and numerological details receive deliberate emphasis.

## Occult redesigns

### Court de Gébelin, Paris, 1781

**[FACT]** Gébelin retained the cup-and-ball performer but Egyptianized the surrounding theory. He wrote:

> “Le premier de tous les Atous … est un Joueur de Gobelet; on le reconnoît à sa table couverte de dés, de gobelets, de couteaux, de bales…”

“The first of all the trumps … is a cups player; he is recognized by his table covered with dice, cups, knives, balls…” [*Monde primitif*, VIII, 1781](https://www.tarock.info/gebelin.htm).

**[ESOTERIC ATTRIBUTION]** The Jacob’s staff or “rod of the Magi” turns the street performer toward sacred operator, but Gébelin still describes the literal cups-and-balls trick.

### Etteilla / Grand Etteilla, Paris, 1788–89

**[FACT]** Etteilla did not simply rename Marseille I. He reordered and redesigned the entire pack as an Egyptian cosmogony. Card 1 is **Chaos**, often paired with “Etteilla / questioner” functions depending on edition; the traditional Bateleur is displaced to a different number, commonly card 15 in Grand Etteilla tables.

**[ESOTERIC ATTRIBUTION]** His system combines creation, the four elements, astrology, upright/reversed wording, and cartomantic keywords printed on cards.

**Finding:** Statements such as “Etteilla’s Magician is card I” confuse Marseille numbering with Etteilla’s independent sequence.

### Éliphas Lévi, 1854–56 and later *Sanctum Regnum*

**[FACT]** Lévi’s *Dogme et rituel de la haute magie* organizes two books into twenty-two chapters and identifies tarot with Hebrew letters and magical doctrine. He promoted the wand-bearing operator as a model of directed will.

**[ESOTERIC ATTRIBUTION]** Lévi generally begins the trumps with **Aleph** and the Magician, unlike the later Golden Dawn’s Fool–Aleph and Magician–Beth.

**[FACT]** Lévi’s best-known Magus illustration places a standing adept at an altar/table, holding a wand, with ritual instruments and correspondential symbols. It is a decisive bridge from mountebank to ceremonial magician.

**Quotation caution:** The frequently attributed maxim “as above, so below” is Hermetic in ancestry but is not a caption printed on the fifteenth-century card. Lévi’s doctrine makes the up/down gesture relevant; Waite makes it explicit pictorially.

### Paul Christian, 1870

**[FACT]** Jean-Baptiste Pitois (“Paul Christian”) converted Lévi’s correspondences into an Egyptian initiatory narrative in *Histoire de la magie*.

He defines Arcanum I:

> “in the intellectual world, Unity, the principle and synthesis of numbers; the Will, principle of action.”

[Paul Christian text](https://green-door.narod.ru/pctarot.html).

**[LEGEND]** His temple initiation and numbered Egyptian gallery are literary occult constructions, not translated Egyptian documents.

### Oswald Wirth, 1889

**[FACT]** Wirth drew twenty-two trumps under the direction of Stanislas de Guaita. His Bateleur/Mage stands behind a cubic altar with wand and elemental implements. Hebrew letter and occult symbols are made visually explicit.

**[ESOTERIC ATTRIBUTION]** Wirth followed the French Aleph–Magician scheme rather than the Golden Dawn’s Beth–Magician scheme.

**Meaning of change:** the humble operator is rehabilitated as conscious intelligence, initiator, and master of the four elemental tools.

### Papus, 1889; English translation 1892

Papus calls the card **The Juggler** and assigns **Aleph**, not Beth. He writes:

> “The top of the figure is occupied by the divine sign of Universal Life ∞ placed upon the head of the Juggler.”

and:

> “The Juggler holds the wand of the Mage in the left hand, which he raises, and the four great symbols of the Tarot are placed before him.”

[*Tarot of the Bohemians*, English ed., 1892, pp. 96–98](https://benebellwen.com/wp-content/uploads/2013/02/papus-the-tarot-of-the-bohemians-1892.pdf).

**[ESOTERIC ATTRIBUTION]** Papus reads the raised and lowered hands as liberty/necessity, good/evil, heaven/earth, and Adam Kadmon as universal mediator. He reads the table’s suit-signs as the Tetragrammaton.

### Golden Dawn drafts / *Book T*, about 1888–1900

**[ESOTERIC ATTRIBUTION]**

- Title: **The Magus of Power**
- Hebrew: **Beth**
- Planet: **Mercury**
- Tree path: 12, between Kether and Binah
- Magical image: robed adept before altar, wand raised, other hand lowered, four elemental weapons displayed
- Color scales: Mercury colors vary by world/scale, commonly yellow, purple, grey, and indigo-violet correspondences in Golden Dawn tables.

**[FACT]** This is where the now-dominant English correspondence Beth–Mercury becomes systematic.

**Wang and Cicero reconstructions**

- **[FACT]** Robert Wang’s *Golden Dawn Tarot* and Chic and Sandra Tabatha Cicero’s *Golden Dawn Magical Tarot* are twentieth-century reconstructions based on order papers and later published Golden Dawn material.
- **[RECONSTRUCTION]** They are evidence for how modern initiates interpret Golden Dawn instructions, not surviving original official decks.
- Wang emphasizes formal ritual posture and temple colors.
- The Ciceros supply dense elemental, Hebrew, astrological, and color-scale detail.

### Waite–Smith, London, 1909

**[FACT]** Pamela Colman Smith drew a youthful ceremonial adept before an altar. Above his head floats an explicit infinity sign. His right hand raises a double-ended wand; his left points downward. On the table lie a wand, cup, sword, and pentacle. Roses and lilies signify desire/passion and purified thought or aspiration; an ouroboros-like serpent forms his belt.

Waite writes:

> “With the Magus’s wand uplifted in his right hand, while the left is pointing to the earth, that familiar figure of the divine is reproduced…”

He gives the formula:

> “the suggestion throughout is therefore the possession and communication of the Powers and Gifts of the Spirit.”

And identifies the infinity sign as a “mysterious sign of the Holy Spirit.” [Waite, *Pictorial Key*, first published 1910](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Pictorial_Key_to_the_Tarot).

**Sources of Smith’s drawing**

- **[FACT]** Golden Dawn ritual designs: altar, four weapons, Beth/Mercury framework.
- **[FACT]** Lévi/Wirth/Papus tradition: wand-bearing conscious magician and up/down mediation.
- **[RECONSTRUCTION]** Marseille: table performer, broad infinity-shaped hat transformed into a literal floating lemniscate.
- **[RECONSTRUCTION]** Renaissance costume and theatrical sensibility reflect Smith’s illustration practice.
- **[DISPUTED]** Waite described the symbols, but surviving evidence does not justify reducing Smith to a passive copyist. Robert Place and later scholars emphasize her substantial design agency.
- **Finding:** no single older image is the complete visual source.

**Meaning of change:** deception and dexterity are sublimated into consecrated will. The scattered trickster’s objects become a complete elemental toolkit. “As above, so below” becomes anatomy.

### Paul Foster Case / BOTA, 1920s onward

**[FACT]** Case’s black-line BOTA tarot was designed for students to color. His Magician closely follows Waite/Golden Dawn: wand, altar, four tools, infinity sign, garden.

**[ESOTERIC ATTRIBUTION]** Beth, Mercury, path 12; attention, concentration, self-consciousness, and the transmission of superconscious power through the personality.

**Difference from Waite:** Case systematizes the card pedagogically in Qabalistic psychology and color meditation.

### Crowley–Harris Thoth, painted 1938–43; first full publication 1969

**[FACT]** Aleister Crowley and Frieda Harris replace the static altar adept with a dynamic Mercurial figure suspended in a field of curves, glyphs, and objects. A caduceus structures the image. A stylus, scroll, coin, cup, wand, winged egg and other emblems orbit the figure; an ape evokes Thoth’s simian form and Mercury’s mimicry.

Crowley writes:

> “The principal characteristic of Tahuti or Thoth, the Egyptian Mercury, is, firstly, that he has the head of the ibis.”

He warns that no static image can capture Mercury because:

> “any stasis contradicts the idea of the card.”

[*Book of Thoth*, “The Magus”](https://www.mr-kaplan.com/Library/Crowley/Book%20of%20Thoth.pdf).

**The three Magi paintings**

**[FACT]** Harris painted three principal versions or conceptions of the Magus. Crowley rejected or set aside alternatives before the published design was selected. One alternative is commonly reproduced as “The Magus I” or “unused Magus.”

**[DISPUTED]** Catalogs do not always count sketches, variants, and finished paintings consistently; therefore “three Magi” should mean three major surviving conceptions, not necessarily three printed cards.

**Meaning of change:** Mercury is no longer a human adept controlling stable tools. He is language, velocity, illusion, exchange, division, recombination, and the Logos in continuous motion.

### Notable modern redesigns

- **Paul Marteau/Grimaud (1930):** standardizes Conver-derived Bateleur.
- **BOTA (1920s–30s):** makes Waite’s image a student-colored initiatory diagram.
- **Wang (1978):** Golden Dawn reconstruction.
- **Jodorowsky–Camoin (1997):** Marseille synthesis emphasizing sexual polarity and psychic potential.
- **Robert M. Place, *Alchemical Tarot* and later historical decks:** returns the figure to Renaissance/alchemical visual languages while acknowledging that explicit occult correspondences are later additions.
- **Rachel Pollack-era feminist and psychological decks:** shift “power over matter” toward agency, integration, and ethical use of skill.
- **Contemporary decks:** frequently change gender, race, body type, tools, and setting; some depict artists, coders, shamans, scientists, or performers. These are modern applications of creative agency, not recovered fifteenth-century meanings.

## The game

## Rank and trick-taking function

**[FACT]** In tarot games, a trump beats any ordinary suited card. Among trumps, higher trumps ordinarily beat lower ones. Thus Magician/Bagatto/Pagat I is normally the lowest numbered trump.

**[FACT]** Low rank does not mean no strategic value. In different games trump I:

- can protect or complete a scoring combination;
- can win the last trick for a bonus or contract;
- can be deliberately captured;
- may carry a point value distinct from its trick rank.

### Italian Tarocchi

**[FACT]** The Bagatto is the bottom ordinary trump. The Fool usually does not beat or lose to it; the Fool is played under a special exemption rule and recovered by its owner, often in exchange for another captured card.

### French Tarot

**[FACT]** Modern French play uses a modern-suited pack. Trump 1 is the **Petit**, one of three *oudlers* with trump 21 and the Excuse. It is not pictorially a Magician in standard game packs.

**[FACT]** *Petit au bout* rewards taking the last trick with the Petit.

### Austrian and central-European Tarock

**[FACT]** Trump I is **Pagat**. *Pagat ultimo* requires it to take the last trick. In many contracts it must be declared.

### Minchiate

**[FACT]** Trump I is low. The highest five *Arie* dominate scoring. The Fool has a special role; combinations or *versicole* reward designated sequences and sets.

### Bologna

**[FACT]** Bagattino is low in trick rank but important in scoring combinations. Matto is special and the equal-Papi rule distinguishes Bolognese order from ordinary numerical hierarchies.

### Sicily

**[FACT]** *Miseria* ranks below trump I. Trump I (*i Picciotti*) is therefore not absolutely lowest. The Fool/Fuggitivo remains exceptional.

## The three regional trump orders

Michael Dummett classified early Italian orders as:

- **Order A:** Bologna and Florence.
- **Order B:** Ferrara/Venice.
- **Order C:** Milan and later Marseille.

**[RECONSTRUCTION]** These orders are reconstructed from poems, sermons, appropriated-tarot lists, and later rules because early hand-painted cards lack printed numbers.

**[FACT]** Bagatella remains at or near the bottom in all three. Disagreement centers especially on:

- Emperor/Empress/Pope/Popess order;
- Love versus Chariot;
- Justice, Fortitude, and Temperance;
- Hermit, Wheel, Hanged Man, and Death;
- position of the virtues;
- placement and status of the Fool.

**[FACT]** The *Sermo* order places Justice extremely high, just below World. Marseille places Justice VIII. Golden Dawn and Waite exchange Justice and Strength for their zodiacal scheme.

## The Fool’s role

**[FACT]** In historical play the Fool is normally an “excuse”: it can be played without following suit and reclaimed, but does not itself win the trick.

**[DISPUTED]** Calling it a “wild card” is a convenient analogy, but it can mislead: its powers are defined by a particular game, not arbitrary substitution as with a modern joker.

## Chronology of attestation

### About 1418–25 — Marziano da Tortona

**[FACT]** Marziano’s text describes a sixteen-god trick-taking pack for Filippo Maria Visconti.

**Finding:** no Bagatto, no 22-trump sequence, no Hebrew correspondence.

### 1442 — Ferrara ledger

**[FACT]** Este court accounts record payments for *carte da trionfi*. These are among the earliest unambiguous references to tarot-type cards.

**Finding:** the ledger does not name the first trump.

### 1440s–1450s — Milanese cards

**[FACT]** Visconti di Modrone and Brambilla cards survive; the Visconti-Sforza Bagatto provides the earliest surviving first-trump image.

### Late fifteenth century — *Sermo perutilis de ludo*

**[FACT]** First surviving full trump list beginning *El Bagatella* and ending *El Mondo*, followed by *El Matto*. The preacher condemns the game and glosses Bagatella as lowest.

### About 1460s — Mantegna educational prints

**[FACT]** Misero and Artixan furnish comparable social-rank imagery but are not tarot.

### 1491 — Sola Busca

**[FACT]** Dated complete illustrated tarot; first trump Panfilio, not Bagatto.

### About 1500 — *tarocchi appropriati* and poems

**[FACT]** Poems assign trumps to named persons, confirming recognizable trump characters and varying regional orders.

### Early sixteenth century — *tarocchi* replaces *trionfi*

**[FACT]** The new word appears in gaming and literary documents. Its etymology remains contested.

### 1527 — Teofilo Folengo

**[FACT]** Folengo’s *Chaos del Triperuno* contains celebrated sonnets structured through tarot cards and is evidence for literary/divinatory play with card associations.

**Caution:** poetic appropriation is not yet the eighteenth-century occult correspondence system.

### About 1650 — Noblet and Viéville

**[FACT]** Earliest surviving French packs with a clearly labeled *Bateleur* tradition.

### 1701–15 — Dodal; 1709 — Madenié

**[FACT]** Marseille types become stable and geographically widespread.

### Early eighteenth century — Besançon substitutions

**[FACT]** Juno and Jupiter replace Papess and Pope in eastern French/Swiss/German packs.

### 1760/61 — Conver

**[FACT]** The most influential historical Marseille model.

### 1781 — Gébelin and de Mellet

**[FACT]** First published large-scale occult theory of tarot. Gébelin claims Egyptian origin; de Mellet supplies divination and a Hebrew-alphabet scheme.

**[FACT]** De Mellet works backward through the trump sequence; therefore his letter placement is not the later Golden Dawn table.

### 1783–89 — Etteilla

**[FACT]** First professional, sustained published tarot-cartomancy system and first purpose-built occult tarot.

### 1854–56 — Lévi

**[FACT]** Tarot becomes central to ceremonial magic, Kabbalah, will, and the magical alphabet.

### 1870 — Paul Christian

**[FACT]** Egyptian initiation drama and Arcanum I as absolute Will.

### 1888 — Mathers

**[FACT]** First major English-language practical occult-tarot manual.

### 1888–1900 — Golden Dawn

**[FACT]** Beth–Mercury–path 12 correspondence standardized.

### 1889 — Wirth and Papus

**[FACT]** Wirth publishes occult trumps; Papus publishes *Le Tarot des Bohémiens*. Both remain in the French Aleph–Magician lineage.

### 1909–10 — Waite and Smith

**[FACT]** Deck issued in 1909; *Pictorial Key* first published in 1910, though copies and reprints bear 1911/1922 dates.

### 1920s–30s — Case/BOTA; 1930 — Marteau

**[FACT]** Two lasting streams: Golden Dawn-derived esoteric pedagogy and standardized Marseille revival.

### 1938–43 / 1969 — Thoth

**[FACT]** Harris paints the deck 1938–43; complete commercial publication follows in 1969.

### 1960s onward — Jungian and therapeutic tarot

**[TRADITION/MODERN INVENTION]** Magician becomes ego-function, conscious agency, archetypal trickster, individuation’s initiating consciousness, or capacity to translate unconscious material into action.

### 1997 — Camoin–Jodorowsky

**[FACT]** Influential synthetic Marseille restoration.

### The Tzaddi/Heh question

**[FACT]** Crowley’s famous “Tzaddi is not the Star” revision exchanges the Golden Dawn letter assignments of the Emperor and Star: Emperor receives Tzaddi and Star receives Heh.

**Finding:** it does **not** change Magus = Beth = Mercury. It matters to the integrity of Crowley’s larger table but not directly to trump I.

### Pips and courts

The user’s template requests decans and court spans, but these are not attributes of the Magician.

**[FACT]**

- Numbered minor cards 2–10 receive zodiacal decans and *Book T* titles.
- Aces are elemental roots.
- Golden Dawn courts combine sub-elements—e.g., Knight of Wands as Fire of Fire—and cover zodiacal sectors.
- The Magician, being a major trump, instead receives a single planetary attribution: Mercury.

Applying a decan or court zodiacal span to the Magician would be a category error.

## Esoteric and symbolic readings

## Christian and moral

**[RECONSTRUCTION]** Renaissance trump imagery can be explained through recognizable social and Christian-moral hierarchies without an occult code. The lowest operator may embody low estate, vanity, fraud, idle amusement, or humanity at the beginning of ascent.

**[DISPUTED]** Vitali’s stronger thesis makes Bagatella a specific emblem of “bagatella sin”—treating grave sins as trifles. The sermon’s low-rank gloss and later moral vocabulary support the association, but no fifteenth-century caption explicitly states the full theology.

## Hermetic

**[ESOTERIC ATTRIBUTION, nineteenth century]** The Magician mediates heaven and earth, mind and matter, above and below. Lévi, Papus, Golden Dawn, Waite, Case, and Crowley spread this reading.

**Finding:** the Hermetic maxim did not begin with Waite, but Waite-Smith supplied its most influential tarot image.

## Kabbalistic

Two incompatible major traditions exist:

- **French occult school:** Magician = **Aleph**. Lévi, Papus, Wirth.
- **Golden Dawn school:** Fool = Aleph; Magician = **Beth**.

**[FACT]** Beth means “house” in conventional Hebrew letter-name tradition.

**[ESOTERIC ATTRIBUTION]** Golden Dawn places it on the twelfth path between Kether and Binah, making Mercury the channel by which primal force takes intelligible form.

**Finding:** no Renaissance Jewish source associates Beth with the Bagatto.

## Astrological and Mercurial

**[ESOTERIC ATTRIBUTION]** Golden Dawn’s Mercury identification fits retrospectively because Mercury governs speech, writing, trade, calculation, skill, mediation, theft, and trickery.

**[RECONSTRUCTION]** This is symbolically persuasive but historically late. A fifteenth-century “Children of the Moon” tradition sometimes includes jugglers; thus the original performer was not unambiguously Mercurial.

**Important correction:** “Magician always meant Mercury” is false. Mercury is a nineteenth-century occult fixation imposed on an older performer.

## Alchemical

**[ESOTERIC ATTRIBUTION]** The table becomes a laboratory/altar; four suit-tools become elements; the operator becomes artificer who separates and recombines.

- Lévi supplies ritual-magical synthesis.
- Golden Dawn integrates elemental weapons.
- Case makes the process psychological.
- Crowley literalizes Mercurial volatility.
- Robert Place reconnects modern tarot to documented early-modern alchemical imagery.

**[DISPUTED]** Direct alchemical intent in the fifteenth-century Bagatto has not been demonstrated.

## The wand

- **[FACT]** Marseille Bateleurs hold a short baton or wand-like implement.
- **[ESOTERIC ATTRIBUTION]** Gébelin calls it Jacob’s staff or the rod of Magi.
- **[FACT]** Lévi transforms it into the ceremonial magician’s wand.
- **[FACT]** Waite-Smith raises a double-ended wand vertically.
- **[FACT]** Crowley makes wand/caduceus part of Mercury’s moving symbolic field.

The wand’s semantic path is unusually clear: entertainer’s implement → Magi’s rod → ritual will → divine channel.

## The four suit signs

**[DISPUTED]** Marseille tables contain cups, knives, coins/balls, rods, dice, and miscellaneous apparatus, but they do not always display one clean example of each tarot suit.

**[ESOTERIC ATTRIBUTION]** Papus declares the four great suit-symbols present and aligns them with Yod-Heh-Vav-Heh.

**[FACT]** Waite-Smith deliberately makes the four tools unmistakable.

Thus “the four suits were always on the Magician’s table” is an occult regularization of ambiguous earlier objects.

## Jungian

**[MODERN INVENTION/TRADITION]** Jung did not publish a canonical tarot system. Later Jungian writers identify the Magician with:

- conscious ego and intentionality;
- archetypal Trickster;
- psychopomp/Mercury;
- mediation between conscious and unconscious;
- symbolic transformation;
- the beginning of individuation.

**[DISPUTED]** Calling these “Jung’s meaning of the Magician” is inaccurate unless tied to a specific post-Jungian author.

## Literature

### Teofilo Folengo

**[FACT]** Folengo’s sixteenth-century tarot sonnets are early literary appropriations of card sequences.

### T. S. Eliot, *The Waste Land* (1922)

**[FACT]** Madame Sosostris reads a hybrid tarot including “the drowned Phoenician Sailor,” Belladonna, “the man with three staves,” Wheel, and “one-eyed merchant.” The Magician is not named.

**Finding:** claims that Eliot explicitly quotes the Magician are unsupported. The “one-eyed merchant” may invite Mercurial comparison, but that is interpretation.

### W. B. Yeats

**[FACT]** Yeats belonged to the Golden Dawn and knew its symbolism. Tarot-like elemental and initiatory structures inform his occult milieu.

**Finding:** no single canonical Yeats poem establishes an explicit portrait of tarot trump I. Broad claims should be tied to a named text, diary, or Golden Dawn document.

### Italo Calvino, *The Castle of Crossed Destinies*

**[FACT]** Characters unable to speak arrange tarot cards into narratives. Calvino used reproductions of the Visconti-Sforza deck in one part and a Marseille deck in another.

**Interpretive relevance:** the Magician becomes a combinatorial narrator—an apt Mercurial role—but the novel does not supply a fixed doctrinal meaning.

## Film

### Alejandro Jodorowsky

**[FACT]** Jodorowsky’s films repeatedly stage magicians, alchemists, tricksters, initiation, and sacramental theater. *The Holy Mountain* opens with an alchemist-like master arranging objects and transforming the protagonist.

**[INTERPRETATION]** Identifying this figure as “the Magician card” is compelling but should be stated as visual/thematic comparison unless supported by Jodorowsky’s own commentary.

**[FACT]** The film’s traveling mesmerist and theatrical troupe explore fraud, spectacle, belief, and genuine mystery.

## Controversies and disputes

## Egyptian origin

**[LEGEND, first systematic publication 1781]** Gébelin asserted that tarot was an Egyptian Book of Thoth preserved in disguised images. He supplied speculative “Egyptian” etymologies for *tarot*.

**Evidence against:**

- tarot documents occur in fifteenth-century northern Italy;
- surviving imagery belongs to European social, Christian, triumphal, and courtly vocabularies;
- no Egyptian tarot cards or Egyptian text describing tarot exists;
- Egyptian hieroglyphic was not deciphered when Gébelin wrote;
- his word derivations cite no hieroglyphic spellings.

Dummett concluded that Gébelin’s argument rested “not on a mistaken basis, but on none at all.” [Dummett, “Origins of Tarot”](https://www.nybooks.com/articles/1981/05/14/origins-of-tarot/).

**Historical importance:** false origin does not mean trivial influence. Gébelin’s claim made possible Etteilla, Lévi, Papus, Golden Dawn tarot, Waite, and Crowley.

## Romani or “Gypsy” transmission

**[LEGEND]** Tarot was brought from Egypt by “Gypsies.”

**History of the claim:**

- Gébelin helped associate Egyptian survival and itinerant transmitters.
- Jean-Alexandre Vaillant and other nineteenth-century writers popularized a specifically Romani transmission theory.
- Papus’s title *Tarot des Bohémiens* spread it internationally.

**Evidence against:**

- Romani origins lie in South Asia, not ancient Egypt.
- European tarot documentation precedes evidence of Romani tarot transmission.
- No fifteenth-century Italian source credits Romani makers or teachers.

**Ethical note:** “Gypsy” is retained only when naming historical claims or titles.

## Cathar origins

**[LEGEND/MODERN INVENTION]** The trumps encode Cathar doctrine and survived persecution as a secret catechism.

**Evidence:** visual parallels can be selected retrospectively, but there is no Cathar tarot, Cathar rulebook, inquisitorial mention of tarot doctrine, or continuous provenance from medieval Cathars to fifteenth-century Italian cards.

## Templar origins

**[LEGEND/MODERN INVENTION]** Knights Templar concealed teachings in tarot after the order’s suppression.

**Evidence:** no Templar-era tarot objects, text, or chain of transmission exists. Standard tarot appears more than a century after the 1312 suppression.

## Masonic origin

**[LEGEND when projected backward]** Tarot was designed by ancient or medieval Freemasons.

**[FACT]** Gébelin, de Mellet, Lévi, Papus, Wirth, and Golden Dawn members worked in Masonic or para-Masonic symbolic environments. Masonic columns, temple layouts, and initiatory grades influenced later interpretations.

**Conclusion:** Masonry influenced occult tarot reception, not the documented creation of fifteenth-century tarot.

## The Magician as craftsman versus cheat

**[DISPUTED]**

- The Visconti figure may be a craftsman/vendor because the objects are tool-like.
- The French *bateleur* clearly denotes entertainment and sleight of hand.
- Cups, balls, dice, and palming support a trickster-gambler interpretation.
- Rich clothing complicates a simple “lowest pauper” reading.

**Best reconstruction:** the original type deliberately occupies an unstable boundary—skilled operator and socially suspect trickster. That ambiguity enabled its later promotion into divine artisan.

## Cups-and-balls

**[FACT]** Gébelin explicitly identifies a *Joueur de Gobelets* and lists cups and balls.

**[RECONSTRUCTION]** Earlier Italian objects are compatible with the game, but individual painted forms are too ambiguous to prove the exact trick.

**Finding:** “The fifteenth-century Bagatto certainly plays cups-and-balls” is too strong; “the tradition develops into a documented cups-and-balls performer” is secure.

## The lemniscate

**[FACT]** Marseille hats often look like a sideways eight.

**[ESOTERIC ATTRIBUTION]** Papus explicitly calls it ∞; Waite detaches and floats it above the head.

**[DISPUTED]** No early cardmaker’s text says the hat was designed as the mathematical infinity sign. The historical direction may be resemblance first, occult interpretation second, graphic literalization third.

## “As above, so below”

**[FACT]** Waite-Smith’s arms enact vertical mediation.

**[TRADITION]** The phrase belongs to the Emerald Tablet’s Hermetic reception.

**[DISPUTED]** It is routinely quoted as if printed in Waite’s card description. Waite describes the gesture and divine transmission, but popular deck manuals sharpen it into the slogan.

## Beth versus Aleph

**[FACT]** Both are genuine historical occult systems:

- Lévi/Papus/Wirth: Magician–Aleph.
- Golden Dawn/Waite/Case/Crowley: Fool–Aleph, Magician–Beth.

**Conclusion:** Saying “the Magician’s Hebrew letter is Beth” is correct only within Golden Dawn-derived systems.

## Etteilla numbering

**[FACT]** Grand Etteilla reordered tarot.

**Common error:** reproductions map “card 1” directly to the Magician. In Etteilla, card 1 is commonly Chaos; the old Bateleur image/function appears elsewhere.

## Waite versus Smith authorship

**[DISPUTED]** Early marketing and scholarship foregrounded Waite and the Rider company. Modern writers increasingly use **Waite-Smith** or **Rider-Waite-Smith** to acknowledge Pamela Colman Smith.

**Evidence:** Waite specified symbolic program and supplied a text; Smith made the actual drawings and appears to have exercised compositional invention, especially in the minors. Exact division of decision-making cannot be recovered card by card.

## Crowley’s “three Magi”

**[FACT]** Harris produced multiple developed versions.

**[DISPUTED]** Modern lists sometimes confuse preliminary studies, rejected paintings, and separately titled reproductions. The published Magus must not be treated as the only image Harris made, but “three” needs catalog-specific definition.

## Misprints and accidents that became traditions

- **Marseille carving degradation:** unclear tools and table legs become symbolic secrets in later readings.
- **Noblet hand damage:** interpreted as an obscene gesture without contemporary support.
- **Conver color-registration and stencil variation:** later “restorers” treat accidental color boundaries as doctrinal.
- **Waite publication dates:** 1909 deck, 1910 first *Pictorial Key*, title pages dated 1911, and later 1922 impressions generate contradictory citations.
- **Strength/Justice exchange:** deliberate Golden Dawn reassignment, sometimes incorrectly described as a printing mistake.
- **Crowley Tzaddi/Heh:** deliberate doctrinal revision, not a typesetter’s error.
- **Etteilla card numbering:** independent system frequently “corrected” into Marseille order by later writers.

## Dummett and the historical correction

**[FACT]** Dummett did not merely argue “tarot was only a game.” He reconstructed the game’s regional history and argued that no evidence shows occult use before the late eighteenth century.

He expressly allowed that original imagery might contain Hermetic or magical elements, but judged standard Renaissance imagery sufficient to explain it. [Dummett](https://www.nybooks.com/articles/1981/05/14/origins-of-tarot/).

**[SCHOLARLY DEBATE]**

- Dummett, Decker, and Depaulis emphasize documentary chronology and the invention of occult tarot after 1781.
- Place and Vitali accept Renaissance origins while arguing for richer philosophical, Hermetic, alchemical, or moral iconology.
- Farley treats tarot as a cultural object whose invented histories are historically consequential even when factually false.
- Kaplan’s importance lies especially in cataloging, publishing, and making deck evidence available.

The real dispute is not “meaning versus no meaning.” It is whether Renaissance cultural meaning amounts to an encoded occult system. No surviving fifteenth-century source demonstrates the later Hebrew, planetary, or Egyptian tables.

## Open questions

1. **What exactly are the objects on the Visconti-Sforza table?** Cup, knife, and hat/bowl are visible; their precise use remains uncertain.

2. **Was the first trump originally a conjurer, craftsman, vendor, or deliberately composite low entertainer?** Later French evidence favors conjurer, but the earliest card is less definite.

3. **Where was the standard tarot invented?** Milan, Ferrara, and Bologna all have serious claims; surviving documentation does not identify one inventor.

4. **How early did the exact Bagatella image enter the sequence?** The 1442 ledger proves trionfi, not its individual iconography.

5. **Was the Marseille hat deliberately an infinity sign?** No early explanatory source has been found.

6. **How much did Smith independently determine the Waite-Smith Magician?** No complete instruction sheet or card-by-card design correspondence survives.

7. **What is the full catalog history of Harris’s alternative Magus paintings?** Reproductions often omit object history and inconsistently count variants.

8. **Did any pre-Gébelin readers use tarot systematically for divination?** Earlier Italian cartomantic documents and appropriated poems demonstrate associative use, but not the complete Egyptian-Kabbalistic system that appears after 1781.

9. **How much Renaissance Hermeticism belongs to original design rather than later recovery?** Visual compatibility exists; direct programmatic documentation does not.

10. **Can every modern “restored” Marseille detail be traced to a historical impression?** Often not. Restoration, synthesis, and creative redesign overlap.
