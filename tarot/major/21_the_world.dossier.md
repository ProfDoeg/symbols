# The World: Research Dossier

## Method and evidence labels

This dossier distinguishes:

- **Documented fact** — supported by a surviving card, archival record, contemporary text, or reliable catalogue.
- **Scholarly reconstruction** — an inference argued by card historians from surviving objects, documents, workshop practice, or comparative iconography.
- **Tradition** — a historically transmitted reading or name whose factual origin may be uncertain.
- **Esoteric attribution** — a deliberate occult correspondence, not a claim demonstrated by the fifteenth-century evidence.
- **Disputed claim** — proposed by identifiable writers but challenged or unsupported.
- **Legend** — a narrative presented as ancient history without contemporary evidence.
- **Modern invention** — a twentieth- or twenty-first-century reinterpretation, reconstruction, or redesign.

“Tarot de Marseille” is used here as a historical pattern-family designation. It was not the name under which the first Italian *trionfi* were made, and it did not become a standard commercial label until much later.

### The earliest-surviving-example problem

**Documented fact:** No complete fifteenth-century tarot pack survives. The cards called the Visconti-Sforza pack are now divided principally among the Morgan Library, the Accademia Carrara, and the Colleoni family. The Morgan dates the group broadly to Milan, approximately 1450–1480. It describes six cards—including the World—as stylistically distinct from the principal group. [Morgan catalogue](https://www.themorgan.org/manuscript/158525)

**Scholarly dispute:** Those six have been called later replacements and variously attributed to Antonio Cicognara, Benedetto Bembo, Franco dei Russi, or another Ferrarese/Lombard artist. Michael Dummett questioned both the Cicognara attribution and the assumption that they were replacements. Thus:

- The Visconti-Sforza is the earliest surviving deck-family with a World card generally reproduced today.
- The surviving World itself may date closer to the 1470s–1490s than the principal cards of approximately 1450–1460.
- A Ferrarese “Este” World and early printed World cards may be nearly contemporary. Exact priority cannot presently be proved.

### Names and numbering across traditions

- **Fifteenth-century Italian:** surviving luxury cards are normally untitled and unnumbered. Names are supplied from contemporary lists, later regional vocabulary, and iconography.
- **Anonymous sermon:** *El mondo (cioè Dio Padre)*, “The World (that is, God the Father).”
- **Italian game vocabulary:** *Il Mondo* or *Mondo*.
- **Marseille:** *LE MONDE*, usually XXI.
- **Etteilla:** the corresponding image becomes card **5**, **Voyage** upright and **Terre** reversed.
- **Mathers, 1888:** “The World.”
- **Wirth:** *Le Monde*, XXI.
- **Golden Dawn:** “The Universe,” XXI; “The Great One of the Night of Time.”
- **Waite-Smith:** “The World,” XXI.
- **BOTA:** “The World,” Key 21.
- **Thoth:** “The Universe,” Atu XXI.
- **Spanish:** *El Mundo*.
- **German:** *Die Welt*; in play, *XXI* is often more important than the picture-name.
- **French game tarot:** *le vingt-et-un* is the usual play-name.

### Regional renamings elsewhere in the trump series

These do not rename the World, but they matter because they show that titles and images were historically variable.

- **Besançon pattern, probably late seventeenth century onward in eastern France, Switzerland, and southern Germany:** Popess and Pope became **Juno** and **Jupiter**, probably to make the pack acceptable in mixed Catholic-Protestant markets. The World remained the World. [Besançon overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tarot_de_Besan%C3%A7on)
- **Bolognese tarocchino:** the four imperial/papal trumps became four **Moors** in the eighteenth century; the World remained a high trump.
- **Sicilian tarot:** the upper sequence was extensively renamed. *La Palla* or *Atlante*—Atlas supporting the celestial globe—occupies a place functionally and visually related to the World, but the highest trump is Jupiter.
- **Marseille Tower:** *La Maison Dieu*, not literally “The Tower”; Italian lists commonly say *la saetta* or *il fuoco*.
- **Fool:** normally unnumbered in historical play packs; modern occult decks often print 0. Etteilla numbered his Folly 78.
- **Papess/Pope substitutions:** they do not constitute evidence that the entire pack was secretly pagan; they are documented regional revisions to saleable playing cards.

# Iconography deck by deck

## Precursors and fifteenth-century material

### Marziano da Tortona and Michelino da Besozzo, Milan, approximately 1412–1425

**Documented fact:** Marziano described a special card game for Filippo Maria Visconti with sixteen classical gods in four conceptual suits. Michelino da Besozzo painted the cards; they are lost, but Marziano’s text survives.

**Relevance:** This is a precursor to tarot rather than a standard tarot pack. It had no card named the World and no twenty-one-trump sequence.

**Finding:** It should not be cited as the first attestation of The World. Its importance is that it documents a Milanese court commissioning an allegorical trick-taking pack before the surviving tarot decks.

### Visconti di Modrone / Cary-Yale, Milan, approximately 1441–1447

**Documented fact:** The surviving cards include unusual additional court cards and theological virtues. They are unnumbered and untitled. The Beinecke and Morgan treat the Visconti di Modrone, Brera-Brambilla, and Visconti-Sforza groups as three of the oldest surviving luxury *tarocchi*. [Morgan study day](https://www.themorgan.org/thaw-conservation-center/tarocchi-virtual-study-day)

**World:** No securely surviving World card belongs to the extant Cary-Yale group.

**Implication:** Statements describing “the Cary-Yale World” usually confuse reconstructions with an original surviving card.

### Brera-Brambilla, Milan, mid-fifteenth century

**Documented fact:** An incomplete luxury pack associated with Visconti heraldry survives in the Pinacoteca di Brera.

**World:** No securely surviving World card is known from the extant group.

### Visconti-Sforza, Milan/Lombardy, approximately 1450–1480

**What is depicted:** Two nude or lightly draped winged putti hold up a large circular or oval globe. Within it stands a compact walled city or settlement in a green landscape. Above is a starry or celestial zone. The effect is less a modern globe of Earth than a framed totality: inhabited world, ideal city, or earthly realm set within the heavens.

**Documented fact:** The World is one of the six Visconti-Sforza cards painted in a distinct hand/style from the principal Bembo group. [British Museum reproduction record](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/P_1976-0131-25-1-78)

**New Jerusalem hypothesis — disputed:** The enclosed city has often been interpreted as the Heavenly or New Jerusalem of Revelation 21. This is iconographically plausible because medieval depictions of the New Jerusalem show a walled celestial city, and because the sequence may culminate in cosmic or divine order. But:

- no surviving fifteenth-century title calls the card “New Jerusalem”;
- the sermon calls it *Mondo*;
- the city lacks decisive attributes identifying the twelve-gated apocalyptic Jerusalem;
- other scholars take it simply as the inhabited world, ideal city, Milan, or a generic cosmic city.

The strongest safe conclusion is: **the image uses a medieval-Christian visual vocabulary in which an ideal walled city could suggest both the ordered world and heavenly beatitude; a specific identification as New Jerusalem is a reconstruction, not documented fact.**

### Ferrarese “Este” cards, late fifteenth century

Several dispersed cards are associated with Ferrara and the Este court.

**World type:** A winged child or angel holds orb and sceptre above a circular terrestrial image containing hills, towers, or a city.

**Change from Visconti-Sforza:** The supporting pair of putti can become a single cosmic ruler or angel. The orb-and-sceptre vocabulary makes divine government more explicit.

**Meaning in contemporary context — scholarly reconstruction:** The card represents the ordered cosmos under divine sovereignty. This fits the sermon’s gloss “God the Father” better than later ideas of personal self-realization.

### Charles VI / Gringonneur group, northern Italy, late fifteenth century

**World:** The surviving group is incomplete. Attributions to Jacquemin Gringonneur and Charles VI are nineteenth-century cataloguing traditions, not secure commissioning history.

**Caution:** It should not be treated as a securely dated Parisian tarot of 1392. Historians identify the cards as northern Italian and later.

### Sola Busca, Ferrara/Venice, 1491

**Documented fact:** The first substantially complete illustrated tarot whose numeral cards carry narrative scenes.

**World:** Its trump cycle replaces the standard allegories with named ancient or pseudo-ancient heroes. It therefore supplies no direct Marseille-style World.

**Relevance:** Crucial to the history of illustrated minors and to Pamela Colman Smith’s sources, but not a deck-by-deck witness for the standard World composition. The British Museum exhibition of Sola Busca cards in 1908 is a plausible source environment for Smith’s 1909 pip designs.

### “Mantegna Tarocchi,” Ferrara or northeastern Italy, approximately 1460s–1480s

**Documented fact:** These are two series of fifty engravings arranged into estates of man, Muses, liberal arts, cosmic principles, and celestial spheres. They are neither by Mantegna nor a normal tarot pack. The British Museum explicitly calls the traditional title misleading. [British Museum catalogue](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/P_1895-0915-1)

**Relevant images:** *Cosmico*, the elements, planets, *Primo Mobile*, and *Prima Causa* provide a parallel ascending cosmology.

**Assessment:** Relevant to Renaissance visual culture and possible comparative sources, but not an early version of Trump XXI.

### Rosenwald sheets, northern Italy, late fifteenth or early sixteenth century

**Documented fact:** Printed woodcut sheets preserve an early numbered sequence close to the southern/order-A family.

**World:** A high cosmic figure or globe-bearing composition, placed immediately below the Angel in the Florentine/Bolognese order.

**Importance:** These sheets demonstrate that The World was not universally the highest trump. They also belong to the printed, popular rather than courtly-luxury phase.

### Budapest/Metropolitan sheets, northern Italy, approximately 1500

**Documented fact:** Early woodcut sheets preserve numbered trumps. Dating and exact geographical assignment remain debated.

**World:** A globe or cosmic enclosure beneath or before an angelic figure, continuing the world-as-total-cosmos type rather than the later nude dancer.

**Importance:** Evidence for early numbering and regional sequence, but the surviving impressions are fragmentary and should not be forced into a single “original order.”

## Regional Italian patterns

### Minchiate, Florence, sixteenth–nineteenth centuries

Minchiate expands the trump suit to forty-one special cards, adding zodiac signs, elements, virtues, and other subjects.

**World correspondence:** The standard tarot World is not simply retained as XXI. The culmination is distributed among the high trumps, conventionally including the theological virtues, the elements/zodiac, the Star, Moon, Sun, World, and Judgment/Fame.

**Iconography:** The high World-type card commonly shows a globe/city or cosmic realm; the Angel/Judgment remains above it.

**Game value:** The five highest trumps form a privileged group and acquire substantial counting value. In many minchiate rules the top five are worth five points each, with additional bonuses for sequences.

**Meaning:** This is game structure, not proof of occult initiation. The expanded cosmology made the trump suit more visibly encyclopedic.

### Bolognese tarocchino

**Iconography:** Older Bolognese World cards tend toward a globe, city, or cosmic scene. Some later narrow cards reduce the traditional image to the upper portion while filling the lower field with geographical or ornamental material.

**Rank:** Angel highest, World second highest.

**Counting:** Angel and World are among the four privileged *tarocchi* together with the Bagatto and Fool. In a common scoring convention Angel and World count five points, Bagatto and Fool five, while ordinary trumps are lower-valued; exact rules vary by period and game.

**Documented regional peculiarity:** The four papal and imperial cards once formed an equal-ranking group; later they became the four Moors. [Tarocchino rules overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tarocchini)

### Sicilian tarot

**Iconography and name:** Trump 19, *La Palla* (“the Ball”), shows Atlas supporting the celestial globe and is sometimes called *Atlante*. Trump 20 is Jupiter.

**Rank:** Jupiter is highest, then *La Palla*.

**Interpretive caution:** *La Palla* is historically related to the World/globe subject, but the Sicilian sequence has been reorganized enough that it should not be described simply as “The World XXI.”

**Documented chronology:** The older Sicilian standard was replaced around 1850 by a revised form from which the modern Modiano pattern descends. [IPCS study](https://www.i-p-c-s.org/journal/33-3.pdf)

## French and Swiss lineages

### Catelin Geoffroy, Lyon, 1557

**Documented fact:** This is the earliest substantially surviving French tarot, though incomplete and non-standard in its suit signs.

**World:** The remnants establish French reception of the trump sequence, but the surviving material does not provide a secure, complete Marseille-line World comparable to Noblet.

### Jacques Viéville, Paris, approximately 1650

**World:** A naked or lightly draped female figure stands above or upon a globe divided into terrestrial and celestial zones. The composition differs from the fully stabilized Marseille wreath-and-tetramorph design.

**Importance:** Viéville preserves a separate French/Italian-derived pattern. It warns against imagining that the Noblet form was already the only French standard.

### Jean Noblet, Paris, approximately 1650

**What is depicted:** A nude central figure, usually read as female or androgynous, dances or stands inside an oval wreath or mandorla. At the four corners are the human/angel, eagle, lion, and ox/bull.

**Change:** This is the decisive transition from “world as globe/city” to “world as an animated central figure within cosmic totality.”

**Mandorla:** In Christian art an almond-shaped glory surrounds Christ, the Virgin, or a transfigured sacred figure. Its use here is documented visually; its exact intended theology is not stated by the card.

**Tetramorph:** The four living creatures derive ultimately from Ezekiel 1 and Revelation 4 and were identified in Christian tradition with the four Evangelists:

- human/angel — Matthew;
- lion — Mark;
- ox — Luke;
- eagle — John.

This Christian source is much earlier and firmer than the later occult equation with zodiac signs and elements.

### Jean Dodal, Lyon, approximately 1701–1715

**What is depicted:** The central figure dances inside a thick oval garland, with a small wand or flask-like object in each hand. The four creatures occupy cloud-like corner compartments.

**Change from Noblet:** Greater standardization of pose and frame; the wreath becomes an unmistakable enclosing garland. The figure’s drapery emphasizes movement while leaving the body largely exposed.

### Nicolas Conver, Marseille, 1760

**What is depicted:** A nude dancing figure with crossed legs, a long scarf concealing the pelvis, and a short baton in either hand; an oval blue-red-yellow-green wreath; tetramorph in the corners.

**Importance:** Conver is the principal eighteenth-century exemplar behind many later restorations. The card is titled *LE MONDE* and numbered XXI.

**Symbolic reading:** The image visibly unites a central human body, vegetal enclosure, four creatures, and four corners. Completion, totality, and harmonious containment are reasonable visual readings; Saturn, Tav, and Jungian individuation are later overlays.

### Tarot de Besançon, late seventeenth–nineteenth centuries

**World:** The central figure often stands in contrapposto rather than dancing vigorously.

**Regional changes elsewhere:** Juno and Jupiter replace Popess and Pope. The pattern circulated in eastern France, Switzerland, and southern Germany. The name “Besançon” reflects an important later manufacturing centre, not necessarily the pattern’s place of invention. [German overview](https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Besan%C3%A7on-Tarot)

### Grimaud / Ancien Tarot de Marseille, Paris, 1930

**Modern commercial standardization:** Paul Marteau’s Grimaud version regularized Conver-derived linework and restricted the palette. It strongly shaped the twentieth-century idea of “the” Tarot de Marseille.

**World:** The dancer, wreath, and tetramorph remain, but line and colour are made mechanically consistent.

### Camoin-Jodorowsky, 1997

**Modern reconstruction:** Philippe Camoin and Alejandro Jodorowsky produced a reconstructed Marseille deck by comparing surviving historical packs and Camoin printing materials.

**World:** More detailed colours and small restored marks are presented as traces of a deeper symbolic grammar.

**Controversy:** Calling it the restoration of an original, unique tarot overstates the evidence. Historical Marseille packs differ, and no archetypal master copy survives. It is best described as a learned modern synthesis.

**Jodorowsky/Costa reading:** In *The Way of Tarot*, The World can represent fulfilment, realized totality, the soul of the world, and the union of receptive and active principles. The authors explicitly allow the central figure to be the *anima mundi*. [Accessible edition](https://irp-cdn.multiscreensite.com/24669b51/files/uploaded/The_Way_of_Tarot%20%281%29.pdf)

## Occult and modern redesigns

### Court de Gébelin’s engraved tarot, 1781

**Image:** The World is redrawn as an explicitly Egyptianized or classical female figure, identified with Isis or the Universe.

**Change:** Gébelin did not merely decode a historical image; he altered and interpreted it through eighteenth-century Egyptomania.

### Grand Etteilla / *Livre de Thot*, design approximately 1788, published 1789

**Title and number:** Card 5, *Voyage* upright, *Terre* reversed.

**What is depicted:** A woman bearing an olive branch stands in a circular wreath between pyramids; angel/human, eagle, bull, and lion remain around it.

**Change:** The Marseille World was re-engineered into a cartomantic card. Its orientation printed two different meanings directly into the design.

**Significance:** Etteilla preserved the tetramorph but made Egypt, elemental Earth, and travel explicit. [Grand Etteilla card archive](https://foolmade.art/archive/grand-etteilla/card-005)

### Oswald Wirth, 1889; revised 1926

**What is depicted:** A nude female or androgynous figure dances within a wreath, surrounded by the four living creatures. Wirth added occult letters, colours, and carefully articulated elemental symbolism.

**Change:** Wirth intentionally “restored” the Marseille images according to Lévi and Stanislas de Guaita’s occult synthesis.

**Wirth’s own correction:** He observed that the twenty-first Hebrew letter is Shin, but argued that **Tav**, the twenty-second, better suits XXI because it signifies completed totality. That is an esoteric structural choice, not historical philology. [Wirth text](https://le-miroir-alchimique.blogspot.com/2016/09/oswald-wirth-tarot-des-imagiers-du.html)

### Golden Dawn manuscript design, approximately 1888–1900

**Prescribed image:** A female figure dances within an oval formed by symbolic forces, with the four kerubic creatures at the corners. Golden Dawn colouring assigns Saturnine indigo and Earth colours.

**Title:** “The Universe” / “The Great One of the Night of Time.”

**Reconstruction problem:** The Order did not leave one mass-produced canonical deck. Members constructed or coloured personal packs from written and oral instructions. Surviving descriptions are brief.

#### Robert Wang / Israel Regardie, 1978

**Modern reconstruction:** Wang produced a full-colour Golden Dawn deck with Regardie’s guidance. The Universe follows the Golden Dawn ritual correspondence rather than documenting a single nineteenth-century master painting.

#### Chic and Sandra Tabatha Cicero, 1991; later editions

**Modern reconstruction:** The Ciceros developed a more elaborate ritual deck from Golden Dawn papers, colour scales, and temple symbolism.

**Assessment:** Wang and Cicero are valuable visualizations of the system, but neither can be treated as a facsimile of a lost original Golden Dawn card.

### Waite-Smith, 1909; *Pictorial Key*, 1910

**Artist:** Pamela Colman Smith, under Arthur Edward Waite’s direction.

**What is depicted:** A nude, seemingly female but deliberately ambiguous dancer floats within a green oval wreath tied at top and bottom by red infinity-shaped ribbons. A purple scarf spirals around the body. The legs cross. Each hand holds a short white wand. The tetramorph appears in four cloud banks.

**Changes from Marseille:**

- the figure is more airborne and dynamically dancing;
- the two implements become clearly paired wands;
- the garland becomes laurel-like and tied with red lemniscate forms;
- the creatures become naturalistic heads in clouds;
- colour and bodily gesture emphasize circulation and living completion.

**Sources:** The primary source is the Marseille World filtered through Golden Dawn teaching. Renaissance mandorlas, the tetramorph, and victory wreaths are background sources. Unlike Smith’s illustrated pip cards, there is no strong reason to derive this major directly from Sola Busca.

**Waite’s own description:** He calls it “the perfection and end of the Cosmos” and “the rapture of the universe when it understands itself in God.” In commonly available 1911 Rider pagination this appears in Part II under Key XXI; page numbers vary widely among reprints. [Scanned *Pictorial Key*](https://magicgatebg.com/Books/Waite_Tarot.pdf)

### Paul Foster Case / BOTA, design developed 1920s–1930s

**Artist:** Jessie Burns Parke, working with Case.

**What is depicted:** A Waite-derived dancer and wreath, but the figure is more explicitly androgynous. The objects in the hands are opposing spirals representing integration and disintegration. The scarf is shaped to recall Kaph, linking World XXI with Wheel X.

**Attributions printed or taught:** Tav, Saturn, blue-violet, note A, lead, Administrative Intelligence.

**Distinctive meaning:** Limitation is not merely obstruction; it is the form-building power that makes exact manifestation possible. BOTA describes the card as “the uses of limitation” and a stage of cosmic consciousness. [BOTA’s official exposition](https://www.botaineurope.org/the-tarot/21-the-world)

### Crowley-Harris Thoth, painted 1938–1943; first full publication 1969

**Title:** Atu XXI, **The Universe**.

**Artist and designer:** Frieda Harris painted it under Aleister Crowley’s direction.

**What is depicted:**

- a great naked female figure in spiral motion;
- a serpent coiled around or intertwined with her;
- a wheel, web, or lattice of cosmic forces;
- the four kerubim in the corners;
- an ellipse composed of seventy-two circles, referring to zodiacal quinaries and the Shem ha-Mephorash;
- the Eye of Shiva;
- at the bottom, a geometric “skeleton plan” or ground plan of the house of matter.

Crowley writes: “In the corners of the card are the four Kerubim showing the established Universe.” He then identifies the lower diagram as the “skeleton plan” of “the house of Matter.” *The Book of Thoth*, 1944, pp. 118–120 in the original edition; reprint pagination varies. [Online text](https://www.bibliotecapleyades.net/crowley/libro_thoth05.htm)

**Meaning of changes:**

- serpent and dancer express continuous creation rather than static arrival;
- Saturn is boundary, crystallization, fertility, death, and renewal;
- the Eye of Shiva is annihilating perception, but annihilation discloses an indestructible universe;
- the house-plan embodies cosmic energy in material structure;
- the old World becomes a relativistic, Thelemic, alchemical universe in ceaseless transformation.

**Earth/Saturn:** Crowley retains Tav-Saturn while also treating the card as the elemental completion of Earth. These are related but not identical correspondences.

**Tzaddi/Heh issue:** Crowley’s exchange of the Emperor and Star attributions concerns Heh and Tzaddi, Aries and Aquarius. It does **not** change the Universe’s Tav/Saturn attribution.

### Notable later redesigns

- **Salvador Dalí, 1984:** The World becomes a surrealist collage, translating totality into the artist’s personal iconography.
- **Niki de Saint Phalle, Tarot Garden, Garavicchio, begun 1979 and opened 1998:** Trump XXI becomes architectural environment. Her monumental tarot sculptures transform cards into habitable bodies and buildings.
- **Leonora Carrington Tarot, painted approximately 1955:** reimagines occult figures in Carrington’s private Surrealist mythology; publication came much later.
- **Motherpeace, 1981:** circular cards and feminist imagery replace the solitary canonical dancer with a more communal, embodied wholeness.
- **Thoth-derived, queer, and nonbinary decks:** often make explicit what Waite, Case, and Crowley left symbolically androgynous.
- **Jodorowsky-Camoin:** historicizing reconstruction rather than free redesign, though its claim to restore lost detail remains interpretive.
- **Contemporary “World” cards:** frequently replace the tetramorph with ecosystems, planetary Earth, digital networks, or diverse communities. These are modern responses to “world” as ecological and global totality.

# The game

## Original function

**Documented fact:** Tarot originated as a trick-taking game with a permanent trump suit. The Morgan emphasizes that the Renaissance cards were made for aristocratic play, not originally as occult paraphernalia. [Morgan collection](https://www.themorgan.org/collection/tarot-cards)

Each standard Italian pack came to contain:

- twenty-one ranked trumps;
- the Fool, with a special role;
- four suits of fourteen cards.

## Rank

### Milanese/Marseille order

The World is the highest numbered trump, XXI, above Judgment/Angel XX.

This is the order inherited by the Tarot de Marseille, Waite-Smith, and most English occult decks.

### Ferrarese order

The late-fifteenth-century sermon gives the upper sequence:

1. Angel  
2. Justice  
3. World

when read upward in rank, making World highest.

### Florentine/Bolognese order

Angel is highest; World is immediately below it.

This order survives in Bolognese tarocchino and the logic of minchiate.

## Dummett’s three regional orders

Card historians conventionally distinguish:

- **Order A — Bologna/Florence:** Angel above World; virtues grouped relatively early.
- **Order B — Ferrara:** World highest; Justice extraordinarily high, immediately below it; Angel below Justice.
- **Order C — Milan/Lombardy:** World above Angel; this family underlies the Marseille order.

**Scholarly reconstruction:** These categories organize surviving evidence; they are not three printed fifteenth-century rulebooks. Local variation and undated specimens prevent reconstruction of one absolutely original order.

## Justice and Strength

- Marseille/Waite traditionally place Justice VIII and Strength XI.
- Golden Dawn and Waite exchanged them so that Strength became VIII and Justice XI, aligning Leo with Strength and Libra with Justice in zodiacal order.
- Crowley retained VIII as Adjustment and XI as Lust.
- Marseille restorations normally keep Justice VIII and Strength XI.

This exchange does not affect The World’s number, but it shows that “XXI” belongs to a sequence repeatedly rationalized after the fact.

## The Fool’s role

In historical Italian games the Fool is not an ordinary highest or lowest trump.

- It normally cannot take a trick.
- It is played as an excuse and then recovered by its owner, who substitutes another captured card.
- In some regional rules the Fool participates in scoring combinations.
- The sermon says: *El Matto sie nulla (nisi velint)*—approximately, “the Fool is nothing, unless they wish [otherwise].”

Numbering it 0 is an occult and cartomantic convention. Numbering it XXII or 22 occurs in some later systems. Etteilla made Folly 78.

## Card value

### Traditional Italian systems

The World is commonly a high-value honour:

- Bolognese: one of the privileged *tarocchi*.
- Minchiate: among the high counting trumps.
- Piedmontese and related tarot games: XXI is a major counting card.
- Exact point schedules vary, so no single medieval value should be projected backward.

### Modern French tarot

Trump 21 is one of the three *bouts* with:

- the *petit* (trump 1);
- the *excuse* (Fool).

Each bout counts 4½ card-points. The number of bouts captured lowers the contract-maker’s target:

- no bouts: 56 points;
- one: 51;
- two: 41;
- three: 36.

### Central European tarock

In many tarock games XXI is a high honour worth five points. However, in games using the *Sküs* as the highest trump, XXI is second to the Sküs. The three top honours are frequently the Sküs, XXI, and Pagat/I.

### Sicilian play

Jupiter is highest; Atlas/*La Palla* is second. The Sicilian Fool and Miseria have locally distinctive rules.

# Chronology of attestation

## Approximately 1412–1425 — Marziano’s game

Marziano da Tortona describes a sixteen-deity game for Filippo Maria Visconti. No World card; relevant as a structural precursor.

## 1440 — Florence

**Documented fact:** A Florentine record refers to *carte da trionfi*. This is among the earliest secure documentary uses of *trionfi* for the new card type.

## 1442 — Ferrara ledger

**Documented fact:** Este court accounts record painted *carte da trionfi*. The ledger establishes production and purchase but does not name The World individually.

## 1450s–1480s — surviving Milanese decks

Luxury painted packs demonstrate the developed trump family. The Visconti-Sforza World survives, although its exact date and artist are debated.

## Approximately 1460–1500 — *Sermo de ludo cum aliis*

The anonymous preacher lists the trumps and condemns their treatment of sacred and social subjects. Its final entries are:

- 19 — *Lo angelo*
- 20 — *La iusticia*
- 21 — *El mondo (cioè Dio Padre)*
- Fool — *El Matto sie nulla (nisi velint)*

**Documented fact:** This is the earliest surviving text generally accepted as naming the complete standard sequence and specifically naming *El Mondo*.

**Disputed reading:** An online suggestion reads *modo* rather than *mondo*. This has not displaced the published scholarly reading and makes less sense beside the established card-name and surviving iconography. It remains a palaeographic proposal, not a recognized correction.

## Late fifteenth century — Ferrarese poetry and riddles

Poems associated with the Este court apply trump names allegorically to persons and situations. They show that the images were available for literary play, but they do not demonstrate systematic occult correspondences.

## Approximately 1500 — Roman and northern Italian verses

Several poems enumerate or pun on trumps. Their orders vary, contributing to the reconstruction of regional sequences.

## 1520s — Teofilo Folengo

Folengo uses tarot cards in literary and macaronic contexts. His work documents cultural familiarity with the trumps, not a hidden Kabbalistic curriculum.

## Sixteenth century — *tarocchi* replaces *trionfi*

The term *tarocchi* becomes normal. Its etymology remains uncertain. French *tarot*, German *Tarock*, and related forms derive from the Italian term.

## 1557 — Catelin Geoffroy

Earliest substantially surviving French tarot.

## Approximately 1650 — Noblet and Viéville

The French World types become visible: Noblet’s dancer in mandorla with tetramorph and Viéville’s globe-based variant.

## 1701–1715 — Dodal

The standard Marseille composition is firmly established.

## 1760 — Conver

The canonical Marseille World most influential on later restorations.

## 1781 — Court de Gébelin and Comte de Mellet

Tarot is declared Egyptian and linked to the Book of Thoth. De Mellet links the trumps to Hebrew letters in reverse order.

## 1783–1785 — Etteilla’s instructional works

First extensive printed cartomantic tarot system.

## 1788–1789 — Grand Etteilla

The World becomes card 5, Voyage/Earth, within an explicitly Egyptianizing divinatory pack.

## 1854–1856 — Éliphas Lévi, *Dogme et rituel de la haute magie*

Lévi makes the twenty-two trumps a Kabbalistic magical alphabet.

## 1863 — Paul Christian

Jean-Baptiste Pitois, writing as Paul Christian, systematizes Egyptian initiatory tableaux and popularizes *arcana* terminology. Much of his supposed temple ritual is literary invention.

## 1888 — Mathers

Publishes the first substantial English occult-tarot manual and maps The World to Tav.

## 1889 — Papus and Wirth

Papus publishes *Le Tarot des Bohémiens*; Wirth produces twenty-two occult trumps under Stanislas de Guaita’s influence.

## 1888–1900s — Golden Dawn

The Order integrates tarot with Tree of Life paths, astrology, alchemy, colour scales, ritual, and divination.

## 1909–1910 — Waite-Smith and *Pictorial Key*

Smith redraws the complete deck; Waite publishes an exoteric explanation while withholding much Order teaching.

## 1920s–1930s — BOTA

Case and Jessie Burns Parke revise Waite’s visual vocabulary and emphasize Tav/Saturn, limitation, and cosmic consciousness.

## 1938–1943; 1969 — Thoth

Harris paints Crowley’s deck; a full colour edition appears only after both creators’ deaths.

## 1969–1973 — Calvino

The Visconti-Sforza and Marseille cards become a combinatorial narrative machine in *The Castle of Crossed Destinies*.

## 1978 and 1991 — Golden Dawn reconstructions

Robert Wang/Regardie and the Ciceros visualize surviving Golden Dawn instructions.

## 1997 — Camoin-Jodorowsky

A modern synthetic Marseille reconstruction.

## Twenty-first century

The World increasingly signifies ecological interdependence, globalization, queer embodiment, network consciousness, and planetary crisis. These are modern developments, not recovered medieval meanings.

## Principal conflicts

### Tav versus Shin

There are twenty-two Hebrew letters but twenty-one numbered trumps plus the Fool. Different starting assumptions produce different mappings.

- De Mellet reversed the trump sequence: World first/Aleph, Fool last/Tav.
- Lévi and Papus often aligned numbered trump I with Aleph, making XXI correspond to Shin and leaving Tav for the Fool or terminal principle.
- Golden Dawn starts the Fool with Aleph, Magician with Beth, and therefore World with Tav.
- Wirth explicitly acknowledged the numerical mismatch and chose Tav symbolically.

No fifteenth-century evidence connects The World with any Hebrew letter.

### Saturn versus Earth

Golden Dawn doctrine gives Tav the planet Saturn. Yet the card visibly concerns the manifested world, and Golden Dawn supplementary correspondences assign Tav an Earth aspect.

- Saturn = boundary, time, crystallization, form.
- Earth = manifested material totality.
- Crowley and later occultists treat them as complementary.
- They are not interchangeable astronomical categories.

### Tetramorph correspondences

The earliest secure identification is Christian:

- Matthew, Mark, Luke, John.

Occultists later map the same figures to:

- Aquarius, Leo, Taurus, Scorpio;
- Air, Fire, Earth, Water;
- human, lion, bull, eagle;
- four suits;
- four quarters;
- four fixed signs;
- four letters of the divine name.

These correspondences conflict in details, especially whether the eagle represents Water/Scorpio and how the human figure is gendered.

### Tzaddi/Heh swap

Crowley’s “Tzaddi is not the Star” revision exchanges Emperor and Star letter/astrological correspondences. It does not alter Tav, Saturn, or The Universe. Describing the Universe as affected by the swap is a modern misunderstanding.

### Pip decans and court spans

The request’s “Book T title for the pips” and “sub-element and zodiacal span for the courts” do not apply directly to The World because it is a trump.

For comparison:

- Golden Dawn pips 2–10 receive thirty-six decans and titles such as “Lord of…”;
- courts receive elemental sub-elements and zodiacal spans;
- trumps instead receive letters plus planet/sign/element.

Therefore The World has no pip decan and no court-card zodiacal span.

# Divinatory meanings by author

Because several modern authors remain under copyright, their language is quoted only briefly and otherwise closely paraphrased.

## Etteilla and followers, 1780s onward

**Upright, card 5 — Voyage:**

“Journey—Route, Walk… Change of Place, Pilgrimage.”

Extended meanings include travel, movement, emigration, flight, circulation, interruption, and redirection.

**Reversed — Terre:**

“Earth. Matter, Mud… Prima Materia”; also territory, property, permanence, fixity, stagnation, inertia, animals, tomb, dust, fields, mountains, and countryside.

**Historical significance:** Waite’s later upright “voyage, route, emigration, flight, change of place” and reversed “inertia, fixity, stagnation, permanence” are unmistakably within this Etteilla-derived stream. [Etteilla comparison](https://etteillastrumps.blogspot.com/2012/05/cards-5-8.html)

## S. L. MacGregor Mathers, 1888

**Upright:** Worldly completion, success, recompense, voyage, change of place, and questions involving the physical world.

**Reversed:** Inertia, fixity, stagnation, and permanence.

**Source note:** Mathers’s list shows dependence on the French cartomantic tradition, especially Etteilla, while supplying his own Kabbalistic framework. [1888 scan](https://www.howardnema.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/12/the-tarot-1888-by-macgregor-mathers-1.pdf)

## A. E. Waite, 1910

Waite gives:

> “Assured success, recompense, voyage, route, emigration, flight, change of place.”

Reversed:

> “Inertia, fixity, stagnation, permanence.”

This is almost a compressed English translation of Etteilla’s Voyage/Earth polarity. Waite himself admits that many traditional trump meanings are “artificial and arbitrary.” [*Pictorial Key* scan](https://magicgatebg.com/Books/Waite_Tarot.pdf)

## Aleister Crowley, 1944

Crowley’s practical meanings emphasize:

- the matter itself;
- synthesis;
- the end of a process;
- delay, opposition, or inertia depending on surrounding cards;
- completion that immediately becomes the basis of another cycle.

He does not treat reversal as mechanically as Etteilla or Waite; Thoth readers commonly do not use reversals.

**Important distinction:** Crowley’s Universe is not simple “happily ever after.” Saturn means that manifestation is achieved through limit, structure, and resistance.

## Paul Foster Case

Case’s public BOTA material does not foreground ordinary upright/reversed fortune-telling. The card represents:

- completion of a cycle;
- cosmic consciousness;
- exact manifestation;
- successful use of restriction and concentration;
- the form-building power of Saturn.

Its negative or obstructed expression is being governed by limitation rather than consciously using it.

## Rachel Pollack, 1980

In *Seventy-Eight Degrees of Wisdom*, Pollack treats the World as:

- full participation in life;
- liberation within rather than escape from existence;
- unity of inner and outer experience;
- dance, freedom, and integration;
- completion that opens into another cycle.

Reversed, this energy is blocked, delayed, unrealized, or resisted: the person may be near completion but unable to embody it. Pollack later became less committed to fixed reversal dictionaries and encouraged attending to the reversed image itself. [Pollack interview](https://www.anntheato.com/blog/78-degrees-of-wisdom-explored)

## Mary K. Greer

Greer generally reads The World through synthesis, successful integration, recognition, embodiment, and the completion of a developmental cycle. Reversed possibilities include:

- unfinished business;
- constriction;
- delayed closure;
- perfectionism;
- being “world-bound” or overextended;
- a completion occurring inwardly rather than publicly.

Her method is deliberately interactive: meanings arise through journaling, personal associations, elemental dignities, and context rather than a single immutable definition.

# Esoteric and symbolic readings

## Christian

### Tetramorph

**Documented source tradition:** Ezekiel’s four living creatures and Revelation’s four beings were absorbed into Christian iconography and assigned to the Evangelists.

**Application to tarot:** Secure as an iconographic source for the Marseille corners. It does not prove that cardmakers intended the entire Apocalypse as a coded trump sequence.

### Mandorla

The dancer occupies a form traditionally used to frame glorified sacred bodies. This can imply transfiguration, heavenly manifestation, or sacred totality.

### God the Father

The sermon explicitly glosses The World as “God the Father.” That does not mean the surviving dancer is God the Father; it shows that one fifteenth-century preacher interpreted or caricatured the card as representing the supreme divine level.

### New Jerusalem

**Scholarly reconstruction/dispute:** The Visconti city may evoke the heavenly city. Evidence is circumstantial; no surviving caption establishes it.

## Platonic and *anima mundi*

The *anima mundi*, or World Soul, originates in ancient philosophy, especially Plato’s *Timaeus*, and was revived by Renaissance Neoplatonism.

**Historical caution:** No fifteenth-century tarot document calls the World figure *anima mundi*.

**Transmission:** Nineteenth- and twentieth-century occultists increasingly identified the central female figure with cosmic soul, universal life, Isis, Sophia, or Nature. Jodorowsky and Costa explicitly repeat the *anima mundi* possibility.

Thus *anima mundi* is a plausible learned comparison but an unproved original title.

## Kabbalistic

### Tav

In Golden Dawn-derived Kabbalah, Tav:

- is the final Hebrew letter;
- means a mark, sign, or cross;
- seals the sequence;
- connects Yesod, the formative/astral foundation, to Malkuth, the material kingdom.

**First appearance in this exact configuration:** Late nineteenth-century Golden Dawn synthesis, preceded by de Mellet, Lévi, Papus, and Wirth’s incompatible letter schemes.

**Evidence:** An occult construction based on structural analogy, not a recovered Renaissance tradition.

## Astrological

### Saturn

Golden Dawn Saturn signifies:

- limitation;
- time;
- form;
- crystallization;
- death and renewal;
- the boundary of the traditional visible planetary cosmos.

Case turns limitation into constructive concentration. Crowley emphasizes Saturn’s paradoxical fertility and maternity as well as age and restriction.

**First secure systematic tarot attribution:** Late nineteenth-century English occultism. Neither Gébelin’s 1781 essay nor the fifteenth-century cards establishes it.

### Earth

Etteilla’s reversed title *Terre* is an important eighteenth-century precursor. Golden Dawn later assigns Earth as a secondary elemental mode of Tav. Crowley makes the material-world dimension visually explicit.

## Hermetic

The card becomes the completed relation of microcosm and macrocosm: the human figure stands or dances at the centre of the fourfold cosmos.

The Hermetic maxim “as above, so below” is often applied to it, but no such phrase appears on the early card.

## Alchemical

Common correspondences include:

- completed Great Work;
- philosopher’s stone;
- conjunction of opposites;
- fixed matter animated by spirit;
- *prima materia* transformed into perfected body;
- Saturn/lead as starting heaviness and disciplined form.

**Chronology:** Etteilla explicitly associates reversed Earth with *prima materia*, sulfur, mercury, and philosophers’ salt. Wirth, Golden Dawn, Case, and Crowley expand the alchemical framework.

**Caution:** Medieval cardmakers left no alchemical commentary on The World.

## Androgyny

The central figure is frequently read as combining male and female traits.

- Marseille cutting and drapery make the body ambiguous.
- Waite calls attention to a cosmic figure rather than an ordinary woman.
- Case explicitly interprets the dancer as androgynous.
- Later Jungian and queer readings treat this as integration beyond binary division.

This is visually arguable, but the historical sex/gender identification remains disputed.

## Jungian

### Individuation and the Self

Post-Jungian tarot writers identify The World with:

- the Self as psychic totality;
- integration of conscious and unconscious;
- reconciliation of opposites;
- completion of individuation;
- return to life after encounter with the unconscious.

**Historical status:** Modern psychological interpretation. Jung did not produce a canonical tarot-by-tarot commentary assigning The World to the Self.

### Quaternity

The four corner creatures surrounding a centre readily fit Jung’s discussions of quaternity and mandala structure.

**Strength of comparison:** High as a psychological analogy; absent as fifteenth-century evidence.

### “Fool’s Journey”

Treating the major trumps as a single linear hero’s journey culminating in The World is a twentieth-century teaching device. It is not attested in Renaissance rules or trump lists.

## Italo Calvino

*Il castello dei destini incrociati* first appeared in a 1969 Visconti-tarot volume; the expanded book appeared in 1973. Its first portion uses the Visconti-Sforza cards and its “Tavern” portion uses a Marseille deck. Characters who cannot speak arrange cards into intersecting stories.

The World functions within Calvino’s combinatorial field as a maximum or encompassing card, but the novel does not establish a single historical meaning. [Franco Maria Ricci edition history](https://www.francomariaricci.com/en/books/tarots)

## T. S. Eliot

*The Waste Land* (1922) mentions a “wicked pack of cards,” the Wheel, the Hanged Man, the man with three staves, and several invented or transformed figures.

**Finding:** The World does **not** appear in Madame Sosostris’s named cards. It is therefore inaccurate to claim a direct World-card quotation. Eliot’s tarot passage belongs in general tarot reception, not this card’s specific history. [Study of Eliot’s tarot](https://athenaeumazostos.com/2022/12/20/a-wicked-pack-of-cards-100-years-of-madame-sosostris-mystical-work-in-t-s-eliots-the-waste-land/)

## W. B. Yeats

Yeats was a prominent Golden Dawn member and knew its tarot correspondences.

**Finding:** No securely traceable major poem was found that unambiguously names or describes Trump XXI. General claims that a particular Yeats image “is The World” should be treated as literary interpretation unless supported by manuscripts or correspondence.

## Alejandro Jodorowsky

Jodorowsky uses tarot throughout his writing, psychomagic practice, performance, and cinema. His work with Camoin and Marianne Costa is directly relevant.

**Film caution:** *The Holy Mountain* is saturated with tarot-like initiation and ends by breaking cinematic illusion, but identifying the ending specifically as Trump XXI is interpretive rather than documented by an on-screen card.

## Ingmar Bergman

**Finding:** No reliable primary or scholarly source was located connecting Bergman or a specific Bergman film directly to The World card. Bergman’s medieval allegory, Death figure, and chess imagery in *The Seventh Seal* are frequently conflated online with tarot. The chess-playing Death is not evidence of a World-card quotation.

## Niki de Saint Phalle’s Tarot Garden

The Tarot Garden at Garavicchio is the clearest architectural transformation of tarot imagery. Its monumental sculptures make tarot physically inhabitable.

**Qualification:** Individual structures quote particular trumps, but claims about a separate building exactly reproducing The World should be checked against the garden’s official inventory rather than inferred from general photographs.

## Paintings and visual quotations

- Christian mandorlas and tetramorphs predate tarot and are sources, not quotations from it.
- Renaissance Last Judgment imagery supplied the broader cosmic hierarchy.
- Surrealist and modern artists including Dalí, Carrington, Saint Phalle, Betye Saar, and others have redesigned tarot.
- A nude figure inside an oval with four corner creatures is a strong World quotation; a mandorla alone is not.

# Controversies and disputes

## Egyptian origin

### Claim

Court de Gébelin asserted in 1781 that tarot preserved an ancient Egyptian Book of Thoth. He treated the central World figure as Isis or the Universe.

### Evidence

None from ancient Egypt:

- no Egyptian tarot cards;
- no continuous textual transmission;
- no Egyptian source describing twenty-two tarot images;
- no tarot before late-medieval Europe;
- the suits and game mechanics belong to the history of playing cards and northern Italian trick-taking games.

### Historical importance

Although false as origin history, Gébelin’s theory is a documented and enormously influential eighteenth-century invention. Etteilla, Lévi, Papus, Golden Dawn writers, and modern “Egyptian tarots” transmitted or transformed it. [Morgan occult-tarot account](https://www.themorgan.org/exhibitions/online/tarot/11)

## Romani or “Gypsy” origin

### Claim

The Romani supposedly carried tarot from Egypt or India into Europe and preserved its divinatory secrets.

### Development

The association grew after Gébelin and was amplified by nineteenth-century occultists, especially Papus’s title *Tarot of the Bohemians*.

### Evidence

No fifteenth-century document links Romani people to tarot’s invention. Tarot is documented in northern Italian court and urban settings. Modern Romani scholarship also warns that nineteenth-century “Gypsy magic” literature contains romantic fabrication and ethnic stereotyping. [Recent scholarly study](https://doi.org/10.1163/15685276-01234012)

### Classification

**Legend**, with an important afterlife in popular cartomancy and literature.

## Cathar origin

### Claim

The trumps encode Cathar theology or preserve a persecuted dualist gospel.

### Evidence

No Cathar manuscript describes tarot, and the first documented cards postdate the Albigensian Crusade by roughly two centuries. The World’s Christian tetramorph and divine hierarchy fit mainstream medieval and Renaissance Christianity at least as well as any heterodox system.

### Classification

**Modern speculative construction.** Similarities between dualistic symbolism and selected cards do not demonstrate transmission.

## Templar origin

### Claim

The Knights Templar transmitted secret Eastern, Egyptian, or Grail wisdom through tarot.

### Evidence

The order was suppressed in 1312, more than a century before *trionfi* are documented. No Templar inventory, rule, confession, image cycle, or contemporary chronicle records tarot.

### Classification

**Legend/modern invention.**

## Kabbalistic origin

### Claim

The twenty-two special cards were originally designed for the twenty-two Hebrew letters.

### Evidence

The numerical coincidence is real. The historical correspondence is not documented until de Mellet in 1781. Early regional trump orders disagree, and the treatment of the Fool creates incompatible mappings.

### Classification

**Esoteric attribution**, not established origin history.

## The World as New Jerusalem

### Supporting considerations

- enclosed ideal city;
- celestial setting;
- Christian final-things imagery;
- sequence near Angel/Judgment;
- sermon’s God-the-Father gloss.

### Counter-evidence

- card is called World, not Jerusalem;
- city may be generic or Milanese;
- specific apocalyptic markers are absent;
- World and Angel reverse order regionally, so a single Resurrection–Judgment–Jerusalem narrative cannot explain every early sequence.

### Classification

**Plausible but disputed iconographic reconstruction.**

## The dancer as Christ, Sophia, Isis, Hermaphroditus, or *anima mundi*

Each identification has advocates:

- **Christ:** mandorla and tetramorph resemble a Maiestas Domini.
- **Sophia/Wisdom:** feminine cosmic centre.
- **Isis:** Gébelin and Egyptianizing occultists.
- **Hermaphroditus:** visual androgyny and union of opposites.
- **Anima mundi:** universal life within the fourfold cosmos.
- **Fortuna/Fama/Nature:** alternative allegorical identifications.

**Finding:** No surviving Noblet, Dodal, or Conver commentary identifies the dancer. “Female or androgynous cosmic figure” is the most defensible description; all named identities are interpretive.

## “Misprints that became traditions”

Several kinds of copying error affect tarot, but no single famous World-card misprint has been securely demonstrated as the origin of its core symbolism.

Documented mechanisms include:

- mirror reversal when blocks were copied;
- degraded details through repeated recutting;
- inconsistent Roman numerals and spelling;
- accidental colour shifts from stencil work;
- later “restorers” treating damage as secret signs.

**Absence-of-evidence finding:** Claims that a particular line on the Marseille World was deliberately hidden, or that the dancer’s anatomy derives from a printer’s mistake, could not be traced to contemporary cardmakers.

## Camoin-Jodorowsky “restoration”

The collaborators’ comparison of historical packs is documented. The stronger claim that their deck recovers the single original sacred tarot is not demonstrable because:

- no original master survives;
- Noblet, Viéville, Dodal, Conver, and regional packs differ;
- printing damage and workshop variation cannot always be distinguished from intended symbolism.

## Dummett and the historical correction

Michael Dummett’s central correction was methodological:

1. begin with dated cards, rules, and documents;
2. recognize tarot as a family of games;
3. separate the fifteenth-century invention from eighteenth-century occultism;
4. treat divinatory and esoteric systems as later historical developments worthy of study in their own right.

Ronald Decker and Thierry Depaulis extended this documentary history; Stuart Kaplan assembled a huge visual record; Robert Place and Helen Farley synthesized iconographic and cultural approaches; Andrea Vitali and the Le Tarot association published important Italian texts and iconographic comparisons.

The correction does not make occult tarot “unreal.” It changes the question from “Which ancient secret did the cards preserve?” to “When, how, and by whom was each secret meaning attached?”

# Open questions

1. **Exact date and artist of the Visconti-Sforza World:** workshop attribution remains unsettled.
2. **Original identity of the city:** Milan, generic ideal city, earthly world, or New Jerusalem cannot be conclusively chosen.
3. **Transition to the dancer:** no known text explains when or why the globe/city became a nude figure in a wreath.
4. **Gender of the Marseille figure:** female, male, deliberately androgynous, or simply crudely cut remains unresolved.
5. **Specific prototype for the wreath-and-tetramorph arrangement:** Christian mandorlas are evident, but a single copied artwork has not been identified.
6. **Earliest exact date of the *Sermo de ludo*:** late-fifteenth-century dating remains approximate.
7. **Primitive trump order:** the A/B/C reconstruction explains regional evidence but cannot identify one certain original sequence.
8. **Meaning of “God the Father” in the sermon:** it may report the card’s common identity, the preacher’s interpretation, or a polemical gloss.
9. **Lévi’s exact card-by-card World doctrine:** *Dogme et rituel* supplies the Kabbalistic program but not a Waite-style discrete commentary on every image. Many modern tables synthesize Lévi with later Papus or Golden Dawn material.
10. **Direct influence on Smith:** Marseille and Golden Dawn sources are clear; precise individual artworks beyond those traditions remain conjectural.
11. **Wang versus Cicero colours:** both derive from Golden Dawn materials, but no single surviving Order master deck resolves every difference.
12. **Bergman and Yeats:** broad tarot/occult connections are traceable, but a direct, explicit World-card quotation was not verified.
13. **A famous World “misprint”:** none could be documented as the origin of a major established tradition.
14. **Anima mundi as original title:** philosophically apt and later well established, but absent from surviving fifteenth-century names.
