# The Hermit: Research Dossier

## Method and evidentiary labels

This dossier distinguishes six kinds of statement:

- **[Documented fact]** — directly supported by a surviving card, archival record, printed text, or securely catalogued object.
- **[Scholarly reconstruction]** — an inference argued from dated objects, texts, iconographic parallels, or rules.
- **[Tradition]** — a historically transmitted interpretation whose original evidentiary basis may be uncertain.
- **[Esoteric attribution]** — a deliberate occult correspondence, not a claim about the card’s fifteenth-century function unless its author says otherwise.
- **[Disputed claim]** — proposed by identifiable writers but contested or unsupported.
- **[Legend/modern invention]** — a late origin story or symbolic elaboration without evidence from early tarot.

“Tarot de Marseille” is used as a modern pattern-name. **[Documented fact]** Romain Merlin used *tarots de Marseille* in 1856; Papus and later writers circulated the expression, and Paul Marteau’s Grimaud *Ancien Tarot de Marseille* made it commercially canonical in 1930. The seventeenth-century cardmakers discussed below did not market their cards under that collective label.

**[Documented fact]** The Hermit is a trump, not a pip or court card. Therefore Book T’s decan titles for the numbered minors and sub-element/zodiacal spans for the courts do **not** apply to it.

### Names and the change of identity

**[Documented fact]** Fourteenth- and fifteenth-century sources do not securely call the conventional figure a hermit. The early names emphasize age and bodily decline:

- *El Gobbo* — the hunchback.
- *Il Vecchio* — the old man.
- *Il Tempo* — Time.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** Michael Dummett, Gertrude Moakley, Helen Farley, Thierry Depaulis and later iconographic writers identify this early figure principally as old age or personified Time. The hourglass is decisive evidence; the bent posture, staff or crutches reinforce the interpretation. The association with Saturn/Chronos is plausible Renaissance iconography, but no surviving fifteenth-century label says “Saturn.”

**[Documented fact]** By the early seventeenth century the Paris-pattern card could be titled *L’Ermite*. Jean Noblet’s Paris tarot, c. 1650, has *LERMITE*. The figure now carries a lantern and staff and wears clothing readily read as eremitic or monastic.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** The history is not demonstrably a single accidental “misreading” of an hourglass as a lantern. Printed Italian evidence—including the Budapest sheets—already shows a lantern-like object before the named French Hermit. The safer reconstruction is a gradual visual and semantic convergence:

> decrepit old man / Time → travelling old man with lamp → religious solitary / Hermit.

## Iconography deck by deck

## Antecedents rather than conventional Hermits

### Marziano da Tortona and Michelino da Besozzo, Milan, c. 1412–1425

**[Documented fact]** Marziano’s Latin tract describes a commissioned game for Filippo Maria Visconti with sixteen classical gods arranged in four groups. The cards themselves are lost. It was called a *ludus triumphorum* retrospectively by Jacopo Antonio Marcello in 1449.

**[Important negative finding]** There is no conventional Hermit, Gobbo or Time trump in Marziano’s sixteen-god scheme. Saturn is not one of its described gods. The game is relevant as an antecedent for an added suit of privileged allegorical cards, not as the earliest attestation of The Hermit.

### “Mantegna Tarocchi,” Ferrara or northeastern Italy, c. 1460s–1470s

**[Documented fact]** These are two series of fifty educational engravings—social estates, Muses, liberal arts, virtues, celestial spheres—not a tarot pack and probably not playing cards.

**[Relevance]** *Cronico*, the aged chronicler, and Saturnine or hierarchical imagery offer cultural parallels for the learned old man, but none is a direct Hermit-card prototype. Calling the series “tarocchi” is a later misnomer.

## Fifteenth-century Milanese and Ferrarese cards

### Visconti di Modrone / Cary-Yale, Milan, c. 1440s

**[Documented fact]** This luxury deck survives only in fragments. It originally contained unusual extra court figures and theological virtues.

**[Important negative finding]** No secure Hermit/Old Man card survives in the extant Cary-Yale group. Modern reconstructed editions add a Hermit by analogy with other Visconti packs; such cards are **modern reconstructions**, not surviving originals.

### Brera-Brambilla, Milan, c. 1440s

**[Documented fact]** Another fragmentary luxury pack associated with the Visconti court.

**[Important negative finding]** No Hermit/Old Man survives. Any illustrated “Brambilla Hermit” in a modern complete deck is a replacement or reconstruction.

### Visconti-Sforza, Milan/Lombardy, c. 1450–1460

**[Documented fact]** The surviving card shows a richly dressed, white-bearded, stooping old man. He leans on a long staff and holds up an **hourglass**, not a lantern. Blue garments and gold ornament make him an allegory rather than a destitute wandering monk. The Morgan Library dates its group to mid-fifteenth-century Milan and notes competing attributions to Bonifacio Bembo and Francesco Zavattari. Some later replacement cards in the dispersed ensemble are in a different hand.

The Morgan describes the figure’s beard and stooped posture as signs of age and the hourglass as an allusion to passing time. This is the earliest surviving conventional ancestor presently identifiable with confidence. [Morgan Library collection record](https://www.themorgan.org/manuscript/158525) and [Morgan “It’s About Time” interpretation](https://www.themorgan.org/morganmobile/its-about-time/m630).

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** The figure is *Tempo* or *Vecchio*. The link to Petrarch’s *Trionfo del Tempo* is plausible in the culture of courtly triumphs, although Petrarch’s poem does not prescribe this precise costume.

**[Disputed]** “Bonifacio Bembo invented the Hermit” is too strong. Attribution of the cards remains debated, and no design document survives.

### So-called Charles VI / Gringonneur cards, probably Ferrara, late fifteenth century

**[Documented fact]** Despite their old attribution to Jacquemin Gringonneur and Charles VI of France in 1392, the surviving cards are Italian and later, conventionally dated to the late fifteenth century. The relevant old man bears an hourglass.

**[Corrective finding]** They cannot serve as evidence for a French Hermit in 1392. “Charles VI Tarot” is an inherited collection-name; identifying the extant cards with Gringonneur’s lost commission is rejected by modern card historians.

### Catania fragment, fifteenth century

**[Documented fact]** Dummett records an old man with hourglass among the surviving hand-painted Italian versions.

**[Limit]** Catalogue access was insufficient to resolve its exact workshop and date more narrowly. It supports the hourglass type but not a specific Saturn identification.

### Ferrarese “Este” cards, late fifteenth century

**[Documented fact]** The surviving Este-family fragments and related Ferrarese cards are incomplete and do not preserve a securely identified conventional Hermit in every group.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** Ferrarese written lists put the old man in the eastern, type-B sequence, but reconstructing the image in every incomplete Este pack from the lists would exceed the evidence.

### Sola Busca, Ferrara/Venice region, 1491; painted copy c. 1490s

**[Documented fact]** The Sola Busca is the earliest complete surviving 78-card tarot and the earliest surviving deck with fully illustrated suit numerals. Its twenty-one trumps use antique or pseudo-antique warriors and obscure historical names rather than the conventional triumph sequence.

**[Important negative finding]** It has no conventional Hermit IX. Trump XVII, **Ipeo**, is an elderly, Saturnine figure with time imagery and has sometimes been compared with Father Time; this is an iconographic analogy, not identity with the Marseille Hermit.

Its relevance to Pamela Colman Smith lies chiefly in compositions of several illustrated minors. No compelling evidence shows that her Hermit was copied from a Sola Busca trump.

## Printed transitional evidence

### Rosenwald/Rothschild sheet, Bologna, c. 1500

**[Documented fact]** The Bolognese old man walks on two crutches and has wings. This is a visual pun or emblem: old age moves slowly, but time flies.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** The figure combines the decrepitude of old age with the wings common in Triumph-of-Time imagery. The lack of a lantern makes it closer to Time than to the later hermit.

**[Disputed]** Dummett thought the wings might descend from an early copying error; other historians treat them as intentional “time flies” iconography. Both remain possible because no designer’s explanation survives.

### Budapest sheets, probably northern Italy, late fifteenth or early sixteenth century

**[Documented fact]** The surviving printed figure holds an object that reads as a **lantern**, making it the earliest known tarot evidence for the lamp-bearing old man.

**[Significance]** This falsifies the overly simple claim that a French seventeenth-century engraver first mistook the hourglass for a lantern. It does not tell us whether the Budapest figure was already called *Eremita*.

### National Library of Milan fragment and Catelin Geoffroy, Lyon, 1557

**[Documented fact]** Dummett identifies lamp-bearing old men in these early printed traditions. Geoffroy’s surviving cards belong to a nonstandard Lyonnais pack; the old man therefore helps document the transition but is not itself the mature Marseille pattern.

## Regional Italian patterns

### Tarocchino Bolognese

**[Documented fact]** The traditional Bolognese figure is a winged old man moving on crutches. The standard pack was reduced to 62 cards by removing low pip cards, but retained the trump sequence. Four equal-ranking papal trumps were replaced by four Moors after a 1725 dispute with the papal authorities.

**[Meaning]** The imagery preserves Time/Old Age more clearly than the French Hermit.

### Minchiate, Florence, attested sixteenth century; 97-card form

**[Documented fact]** The corresponding trump is traditionally numbered **5**, not IX. It shows an old, bent man on crutches; older examples include an hourglass suspended above or behind him, sometimes pierced by an arrow, and a deer or stag. The pack expands the triumphs to forty.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** Wings, arrow, hourglass and swift deer belong to the visual vocabulary of fugitive Time. Calling Minchiate V “The Hermit” is a modern cross-deck convenience; “Old Man” or “Time” is historically better.

### Tarocco Siciliano, Sicily, introduced c. mid-seventeenth century

**[Documented fact]** In the old Sicilian pattern the card is trump **12**, *Il Vecchio*. It holds an hourglass rather than a lamp. A redesigned pattern appeared around 1850; the modern Modiano descendant converted the object to an oil lamp. The Sicilian order runs Wheel 10, Hanged Man 11, Old Man 12, Death 13.

**[Meaning]** Sicily preserves unusually late evidence that the figure was still understood as an old man/Time, not necessarily a religious hermit. [Dummett’s discussion](https://dummettsmondo.blogspot.com/2015/07/chapter-17-surface-meanings.html) and [Sicilian rules](https://www.pagat.com/tarot/sicilian.html).

## Tarot de Marseille lineage

### Anonymous Tarot de Paris, early seventeenth century

**[Documented fact]** A lamp-bearing old man emerges or walks near an architectural opening. The surviving pack labels him *L’Ermite*.

**[Significance]** This is among the earliest explicit unions of name and lantern.

### Jean Noblet, Paris, c. 1650

**[Documented fact]** Trump **VIIII**, titled **LERMITE**, shows the hooded old man moving with staff and lantern. The mantle partly interacts with or obscures the light, a feature later occultists interpreted as guarded wisdom.

### Jean Dodal, Lyon, 1701–1715

**[Documented fact]** **VIIII L’HERMITE**: bearded figure in a hood or cap, holding a lantern forward beneath a fold of cloak and leaning on a staff. The face is shown in profile, looking toward the past in the usual left-to-right display.

**[Tradition]** Modern Marseille readers interpret the backward glance as retrospection and the lantern as limited, step-by-step knowledge. Such psychological language is not documented in Dodal’s workshop.

### Nicolas Conver, Marseille, 1760

**[Documented fact]** Conver stabilizes the familiar type-II Marseille Hermit: red-and-blue mantle, hunched body, staff, partially screened yellow lantern, **VIIII** numbering and *L’HERMITE* title.

### Grimaud / Paul Marteau, Paris, 1930

**[Documented fact]** Marteau’s *Ancien Tarot de Marseille* became the mass-market twentieth-century norm. Research on its manufacture shows that it was not a facsimile of a single Conver deck: it recombined a Grimaud/Lequart Besançon-derived block tradition with colours resembling late Camoin/Conver usage, restoring Pope and Popess in place of Juno and Jupiter. [University of Lausanne study](https://api.unil.ch/iris/server/api/core/bitstreams/4838fc13-8ed2-41c9-b807-8fa7265cac44/content).

### Camoin–Jodorowsky, Marseille/Paris, 1997

**[Documented fact]** Philippe Camoin and Alejandro Jodorowsky published a reconstructed Marseille deck assembled from numerous historic examples and surviving printing material.

**[Disputed claim]** Marketing and authorial language sometimes describe it as restoration of the authentic or complete tarot. It is more accurately a modern synthetic reconstruction: choices of line, colour and small detail necessarily involved interpretation.

**[Jodorowsky–Costa reading]** The Hermit walks backward while moving toward the future, relinquishing the old world between Justice VIII and the Wheel X. Their *Way of Tarot* says he “actively terminates his relationship with the old world and becomes receptive toward an unknown future.” This is a modern therapeutic-symbolic reading, not a recovered fifteenth-century meaning. [Text consulted](https://irp-cdn.multiscreensite.com/24669b51/files/uploaded/The_Way_of_Tarot%20%281%29.pdf).

## Besançon and related French/German-language patterns

**[Documented fact]** The pattern now called Tarot de Besançon probably developed in Strasbourg/Alsace around the early eighteenth century and spread through German-speaking lands, Switzerland, and finally Besançon. It replaces II Papess and V Pope with **Juno and Jupiter**. Some examples rename the Hermit **Le Capucin**. [Thierry Depaulis’s pattern history summarized here](https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tarot_de_Besan%C3%A7on) and in his study of tarot’s movement into Germany, [PDF](https://weltexperiment.com/pagat/depaulis_2010_when-and-how-did-tarot-reach-germany_tpc-39-2_tarock.pdf).

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** The substitutions probably reduced confessional offence: Papess offended Catholics, Pope could offend Protestants. This is probable context, not an extant cardmaker’s declaration.

**[Regional comparison]**

- Italian: *Il Gobbo, Il Vecchio, Il Tempo, L’Eremita*.
- French: *L’Ermite/L’Hermite*, occasionally *Le Capucin*.
- Spanish: *El Ermitaño*.
- German: *Der Eremit/Der Einsiedler*.
- English: The Hermit; older occult paraphrases include Sage and Prudence.

The Papess/Juno, Pope/Jupiter and Tower/*Maison Dieu* changes concern the surrounding regional vocabulary, not transformations of Hermit IX itself. The Fool remained unnumbered in many game packs; occult decks later printed 0 or placed it elsewhere.

## Occult and divinatory redesigns

### Court de Gébelin, Paris, 1781

Court called IX *“l’Hermite ou le Sage, la lanterne à la main, cherchant la Justice sur la Terre”*—“the Hermit or Sage, lantern in hand, seeking Justice on Earth.” **[Esoteric attribution]** He arranged the trumps as Egyptian hieroglyphs recounting primordial history. [Original French text](https://www.tarock.info/gebelin.htm).

The adjoining essay by the Comte de Mellet reversed the trump order to fit the Hebrew alphabet. In that scheme IX is not Yod: reversed assignment makes the correspondence depend upon the chosen ordering. The exact table varies in later summaries, which is one reason nineteenth-century systems conflict.

### Grand Etteilla, Paris, first issued 1789

**[Documented fact]** Etteilla radically reordered and redesigned the deck. The Marseille Hermit’s visual analogue becomes card **18, Le Traître**; reversed, **Faux Dévot**. It shows a Capuchin/hermit figure but assigns the morally negative identity “traitor.”

**[Tradition/esoteric rationalization]** Later Etteilla-school texts claimed an Egyptian word *capuce* denoted an initiate who degenerated into hypocrisy. No credible Egyptian etymology supports this. The transmitted meanings include treason, disguise, hypocrisy, corruption and seduction; reversed, hermit, anchorite, solitary, concealed, clever or politic. [Etteilla-school texts and translations](https://etteillastrumps.blogspot.com/2012/05/cards-18-21-78.html).

**[Correspondence caution]** Etteilla’s twelve zodiacal cards are chiefly cards 1–12; Virgo belongs to card 6 in one standard tabulation, not to his Hermit analogue 18. Therefore “Etteilla assigned Virgo to the Hermit” is false.

### Éliphas Lévi, *Dogme et rituel de la haute magie*, 1854–1856

Lévi aligns the trumps sequentially with Hebrew letters beginning with Aleph at I. Thus IX receives **Teth**, not Golden Dawn Yod.

In Waite’s English translation, Lévi describes:

> “a sage leaning on his staff, holding a lamp in front of him and enveloped completely in his cloak.”

He adds that its “true name” is **Prudence**, completing the four cardinal virtues. The associated terms are “Good, horror of evil, morality, wisdom.” [French work/English translation scan](https://www.rahoorkhuit.net/library/ceremonial/classics/pdf/transcendental/dogma_et_rituel_part_2.pdf), table at approximately PDF p. 10 in the consulted file.

**[Esoteric attribution]** The lamp becomes reason illuminated by knowledge; the mantle, discretion or self-possession; the staff, aid from enduring natural forces.

### Paul Christian, 1863–1870

**[Esoteric attribution]** Jean-Baptiste Pitois, writing as Paul Christian, elaborated a fictional Egyptian initiation conducted through subterranean galleries containing twenty-two monumental images. His ninth arcanum belongs to his synthetic occult narrative.

**[Legend presented as history]** Christian’s temple initiation has no Egyptian archaeological or textual documentation. It profoundly influenced Papus and later “Egyptian Tarot” makers.

### Oswald Wirth, Paris, 1889; expanded commentary 1926–1927

**[Documented fact]** Under Stanislas de Guaita’s direction, Wirth made twenty-two trumps. The Hermit retains lantern, cloak and staff but adds a serpent. His later text calls the staff a bamboo cane with seven knots; the sage avoids crushing the serpent and lets it twine around the staff like that of Aesculapius.

Wirth writes:

> “The Hermit does not tap upon the earth blindly, for a discreet glow throws light upon his firm and untiring path.”

The partially veiled lantern restricts illumination to what is useful; the closed cloak becomes an alchemical vessel and the serpent the vital current disciplined rather than killed. [Wirth, *The Tarot of the Magicians*, English ed., Hermit chapter](https://www.societyofguardians.org/resources/Tarot_of_the_Magicians_by_Oswald_Wirth.pdf).

### Golden Dawn, London, c. 1888–1900

**[Documented fact about the order’s doctrine]** Golden Dawn manuscripts assign IX to **Yod and Virgo**, the twentieth path joining Chesed to Tiphareth. The title is generally printed **“The Magus of the Voice of Light, the Prophet of the Gods.”** Some later sources substitute “Power” or “Eternal”; those are transmission variants, not separate early cards.

The design instructions circulated in manuscript rather than as a single commercial deck. Members were expected to draw or colour their own packs. Israel Regardie later published Golden Dawn material; Robert Wang’s *Golden Dawn Tarot* and Sandra Tabatha Cicero/Chic Cicero’s decks are twentieth-century reconstructions, not facsimiles of one authoritative Victorian original.

Typical reconstruction:

- hooded ancient;
- lamp and staff;
- Yod;
- Virgo symbolism;
- serpent, grain or wheat;
- path symbolism;
- colour scales.

**[Correspondence]** “Jupiter acting through Virgo upon Sol” is the path formula: Chesed/Jupiter connects through Virgo to Tiphareth/Sun. [Golden Dawn notes](https://www.tarrdaniel.com/documents/Thelemagick/gd/publication/english/Tarot_Notes.html).

### Waite–Smith, London, 1909

**[Documented fact]** Arthur Edward Waite designed the symbolic program; Pamela Colman Smith drew it. IX shows a solitary old man on a snowy mountain height, grey-cloaked, holding a tall staff and raising an uncovered lantern. Inside is a six-pointed star.

Waite explains:

> “It is a star which shines in the lantern.”

and:

> “His beacon intimates that ‘where I am, you also may be.’”

He explicitly rejects Gébelin’s man merely seeking justice and Lévi’s occultist protecting his personal magnetism. For Waite this is attained illumination made available as a beacon. [Waite, *Pictorial Key*, 1911 web edition](https://sacred-texts.com/book/the-pictorial-key-to-the-tarot/read/ix-the-hermit); the equivalent page is p. 55 in a commonly circulated facsimile.

**[Iconographic sources]**

- **Documented visual lineage:** Marseille lantern, staff, beard and mantle.
- **Esoteric source:** Golden Dawn Yod/Virgo framework, though Waite concealed explicit correspondences in the published book.
- **Probable artistic analogy:** “Ancient of Days” plus Christ as “Light of the World,” exactly the terms Waite uses.
- **Disputed:** Direct copying from Holman Hunt’s *The Light of the World*. The lamp-bearing Christ offers a clear cultural parallel, but no surviving Smith statement identifies it as her model.
- **Disputed:** The star is specifically a “Star of David.” It is visibly a hexagram; Waite calls it simply “a star.” Golden Dawn readers commonly interpret it as the macrocosmic hexagram or Seal of Solomon.

### Paul Foster Case and Jessie Burns Parke, BOTA, 1920s–1930s

**[Documented fact]** Case retained Waite’s mountaintop figure, Yod, Virgo and six-rayed lantern, but published a line deck intended for students to colour. Jessie Burns Parke executed the drawings.

BOTA summarizes the figure as the **ONE IDENTITY/I AM**: the illuminated consciousness stands above as a way-shower. Grey neutralizes opposites; the staff is Will; the six-rayed light signifies cosmic law. BOTA gives yellow-green, note F, Virgo and “earthy” alchemy. [BOTA Europe, Key 9](https://botaineurope.org/the-tarot/9-the-hermit).

### Thoth Tarot, Frieda Harris and Aleister Crowley, 1938–1943; first full publication 1969

**[Documented fact]** Crowley commissioned Lady Frieda Harris to paint the deck. The paintings were completed principally between 1938 and 1943. *The Book of Thoth* appeared in 1944; the complete commercial deck followed in 1969.

Atu IX retains **Yod and Virgo**, but radically transforms the image:

- the hooded Hermit occupies a field resembling wheat, roots, spermatozoa or energetic filaments;
- his lamp contains or evokes the Sun;
- the **serpent of Mercury** winds around the **Orphic egg**;
- three-headed **Cerberus** attends him;
- the foreground evokes generation, fertilization and descent into matter.

Crowley calls Yod “the secret seed of all life” and treats the Hermit as concealed creative force rather than merely celibate withdrawal. The egg and serpent signify cosmic generation; Cerberus is the guardian of the underworld and the tamed threefold animal nature. Crowley directs comparison with the Ten of Disks: the culmination of descent into matter initiates reintegration by spirit.

His practical oracle includes:

> “Illumination from within, secret impulse from within; practical plans derived accordingly. Retirement from participation in current events.”

and the verse:

> “Wander alone; bearing the Light and thy Staff.”

[The *Book of Thoth* scan consulted](https://www.rahoorkhuit.net/library/crowley/equinox/volume3/vol_3_no_05.pdf); see the Hermit discussion and divinatory appendix, commonly pp. 88–89 and 257 depending on edition.

### The Tzaddi/Heh controversy

**[Documented fact]** Crowley’s famous “Tzaddi is not the Star” revision exchanges the Hebrew-letter positions of the Emperor and Star: Emperor receives Tzaddi and Star Heh in his final system.

**[Important negative finding]** It does **not** alter Hermit/Yod/Virgo. Discussions that make the Tzaddi controversy a dispute over The Hermit conflate separate cards.

## The game

## General function

**[Documented fact]** Tarot was created as a family of trick-taking games. The trumps outrank ordinary suit cards, normally in numbered order. The Hermit has no fixed universal point value independent of the rules being played.

In modern French Tarot, IX is an ordinary trump:

- it beats trumps I–VIII;
- loses to X–XXI;
- counts as an ordinary card in trick-point calculation;
- is not one of the three *oudlers* or “honours” (I, XXI and the Excuse).

In many three-card counting systems, ordinary trumps effectively contribute one point before packet adjustments. In Austrian and central-European tarock games, the original allegorical Hermit generally disappears because animal, genre or *Industrie und Glück* scenes replaced the Italian subjects; nevertheless the numeral IX retains its rank as tarock IX.

## Italian regional orders

Dummett identified three main historic families:

| Order | Centre | Position of the Old Man/Hermit | Relevant surrounding dispute |
|---|---|---:|---|
| A, southern | Bologna; related Florentine/Roman traditions | Usually later/higher relative to Wheel than in Marseille; exact numbering absent in early packs | Virtues grouped; Angel above World; four *papi* could share rank |
| B, eastern | Ferrara/Venice | Old Man appears in the eastern list at a different ordinal place than Marseille | Justice exceptionally high in some lists; World/Angel order differs |
| C, western | Milan, Piedmont, France | The ancestor of Marseille’s familiar IX position | Justice VIII, Hermit IX, Wheel X; Strength XI in Marseille |

The orders were inferred from rules, numbered cards and surviving lists; unnumbered luxury cards alone cannot determine exact hierarchy. [Dummett’s translated chapters on orders](https://dummettsmondo.blogspot.com/2015/07/chapters-6-and-7-game-and-its-trump.html).

### Justice and Strength

**[Documented fact]** Marseille commonly has Justice VIII and Strength XI. Waite–Smith exchanges them: Strength VIII and Justice XI. This was an occult/astrological decision, allowing Leo to occupy VIII and Libra XI in the Golden Dawn sequence.

**[Corrective finding]** The swap does not renumber the Hermit; it remains IX.

### Fool

**[Documented fact]** The Fool/*Matto*, French *Excuse*, ordinarily does not behave as the lowest trump in traditional tarot games. It excuses its player from following suit and is normally recovered subject to replacement rules. It was frequently unnumbered.

**[Esoteric innovation]** Occult decks placed it at 0, before I, or occasionally between XX and XXI. This does not document the original game order.

### Minchiate

The Minchiate Old Man is trump 5 within a forty-trump series. It is not one of the highest special counting cards. Scoring is dominated by *versicole*—valuable sequences and combinations—rather than by a standalone “Hermit meaning.” [Minchiate rules](https://www.pagat.com/tarot/minchiate.html).

### Bologna and Sicily

In Bolognese Ottocento the old man is an ordinary trump; major value lies in combinations involving Angel, World, Bégato and Matto. [Ottocento rules](https://www.pagat.com/tarot/ottocento.html).

In Sicilian tarocchi the Old Man is XII, ranking above the Hanged Man and below Death. It is not one of the very highest ten-point trumps.

## Chronology of attestation

- **c. 1412–1425 — [Documented antecedent]:** Marziano da Tortona describes a sixteen-god trump game made for Filippo Maria Visconti. No Hermit.
- **1440 — [Documented]:** A Florentine record, identified by Thierry Depaulis, mentions *naibi a trionfi*. It predates the formerly standard Ferrara “first.”
- **February and July 1442 — [Documented]:** Este accounts record *carte da trionfi*. On 28 July Marchionne Burdochi supplied a pack for Ercole and Sigismondo d’Este. [Italian archival transcription](https://letarot.it/Le-origini-italiane-del-Ludus-Triumphorum_pag_pg439_ita.aspx).
- **c. 1440s — [Documented objects]:** Cary-Yale and Brambilla fragments; neither preserves a Hermit.
- **c. 1450–1460 — [Documented object]:** Visconti-Sforza old man with hourglass.
- **c. 1450–1500, commonly c. 1470 — [Documented text]:** *Sermo perutilis de ludo cum aliis* condemns the game and lists the trump as **El Gobbo**. Its precise date remains debated.

- **c. 1490s–1500 — [Documented]:** Bolognese printed winged old man; Budapest lantern-bearing old man.
- **c. 1500 — [Documented literary evidence]:** anonymous Ferrarese and northern Italian tarot poems associate individual trumps with persons or moral situations. These demonstrate cultural play with trump names but do not create a stable divinatory Hermit.
- **1505 — [Documented word history]:** *tarocchi* is recorded at Ferrara in June; French *taraux* at Avignon in December. Earlier packs were generally *trionfi*.
- **1527 — [Documented literature]:** Teofilo Folengo’s *Chaos del Triperuno* uses tarot cards in literary divination. It shows early allegorical use, not a secret ancient tarot system.
- **1557 — [Documented object]:** Catelin Geoffroy printed lamp-bearing figure, Lyon.

- **c. 1650 — [Documented]:** Noblet’s *LERMITE*.
- **1701–1715 — [Documented]:** Dodal’s *L’HERMITE*.
- **1709 onward — [Documented]:** type-II Marseille represented by Pierre Madenié and successors.
- **c. 1715–1750 — [Scholarly reconstruction]:** emergence of Besançon pattern in Strasbourg/Alsace; Hermit sometimes *Capucin*.
- **1760 — [Documented]:** Conver tarot.
- **1781 — [Documented text]:** Gébelin’s Egyptian theory and Sage seeking Justice; Mellet’s Hebrew-letter and divination proposals.

- **1888 — [Documented text]:** Mathers publishes *The Tarot: Its Occult Signification*.
- **1889 — [Documented]:** Wirth’s twenty-two occult trumps; Papus’s *Tarot des Bohémiens*.
- **c. 1888–1900 — [Documented manuscript tradition]:** Golden Dawn Yod/Virgo correspondence.
- **1909 — [Documented deck]:** Waite–Smith.

### Papus clarification

Papus’s *Tarot des Bohémiens* synthesized Lévi, Christian, numerology, Tetragrammaton and astrology. It did not originate Yod/Virgo. English editions sometimes flatten his changing diagrams into a single table. [Library of Congress scan](https://www.loc.gov/resource/gdcmassbookdig.tarotofbohemians00papu_0/?st=gallery).

### Pips and courts

Because The Hermit is a trump:

- there is no Book T decan or “Lord of…” pip title;
- there is no court sub-element or zodiacal span;
- Virgo is the trump’s zodiacal attribution, not a pip decan.

The Virgo decans instead belong to Eight, Nine and Ten of Pentacles in the Golden Dawn minor-arcana scheme.

## Divinatory meanings by author

Quotations are kept short to distinguish primary wording without reproducing whole copyrighted discussions.

### Etteilla school, 1789 onward

**Upright, card 18, *Le Traître*:**

> “Treason, Disguise, Dissimulation, hypocrite, Hypocrisy, Deceiver… Corrupter, Seducer.”

**Reversed, *Faux Dévot*:**

> “Hermit, Anchorite, Solitary, Hidden, Concealed, Disguised, Clever, Cunning.”

**Analysis:** This is the chief source of the apparently anomalous treason/corruption meanings later transmitted through Mathers and Waite. It reflects Etteilla’s reassignment of the Italian *Traditore* complex: the Hanged Man’s historic identity as a traitor becomes entangled with the Capuchin/Hermit image. [Etteilla translations](https://etteillastrumps.blogspot.com/2012/05/cards-18-21-78.html).

### S. L. MacGregor Mathers, 1888

**Upright:**

> “Prudence, Caution, Deliberation.”

**Reversed:**

> “Over-prudence, Timorousness, Fear.”

Mathers also describes the figure as carrying “the lantern of occult science” and a “magic wand half hidden beneath his cloak.” [Mathers text and scan](https://hermetics.net/media-library/tarot/samuel-liddell-macgregor-mathers-the-tarot/) and [facsimile PDF](https://www.howardnema.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/12/the-tarot-1888-by-macgregor-mathers-1.pdf), approximately p. 10.

### Waite, 1910/1911

**Upright:**

> “Prudence, circumspection; also and especially treason, dissimulation, roguery, corruption.”

**Reversed:**

> “Concealment, disguise, policy, fear, unreasoned caution.”

**Conflict:** Waite’s divinatory list retains Etteilla-derived negative material even though his symbolic essay rejects a merely secretive or selfishly isolated Hermit. The inconsistency reflects his use of inherited cartomantic lists alongside a different mystical doctrine.

### Crowley, 1944

Crowley did not emphasize mechanical reversal; Thoth practice ordinarily uses dignity and context.

**Core meanings:**

> “Illumination from within, secret impulse from within; practical plans derived accordingly.”

> “Retirement from participation in current events.”

Ill-dignified readings include isolation, obsession with detail, sterility or withdrawal, inferred from Virgo’s constricted aspect and the card’s placement rather than from a printed reversed list.

### Paul Foster Case, 1920s–1930s

Case’s published and BOTA teaching emphasizes:

- One Identity/I AM;
- illumination;
- will;
- completion;
- guidance from a higher vantage;
- discrimination and assimilation associated with Virgo.

**Reversal:** BOTA is less committed to a fixed inverted keyword list; negative manifestation is separateness, isolation, excessive criticism or failure to share light. This is a summary rather than a verbatim reversed entry because a securely paginated public-domain Case list was not located.

### Rachel Pollack, *Seventy-Eight Degrees of Wisdom*, 1980/1983

Pollack reads the upright Hermit as withdrawal from external rules to seek truth within, followed by a capacity to return as teacher or guide. The lantern lights only a limited area: genuine knowledge proceeds incrementally.

Reversed, depending on context:

- refusing necessary solitude;
- pathological isolation;
- fear of other people;
- rigid withdrawal;
- or emerging again into the world.

**Source limitation:** No authorized full online text was located, so these are close summaries, not purported verbatim quotations.

### Mary K. Greer, *The Complete Book of Tarot Reversals*, 2002

Greer’s upright/traditional cluster includes:

> “Prudence, caution, deliberation, discretion, circumspection.”

Her reversed discussion includes fear of old age alone, avoidance of relationships, desertion, a false teacher, perfectionism, claimed expertise and refusal to examine what is present. It can also mean withdrawing projections and “taking back your light.” [Searchable extract](https://www.scribd.com/document/792341916/558713195-Mary-K-Greer-The-Complete-Book-of-Tarot-Reversals-compressed-52-82).

## Esoteric and symbolic readings

### Time, age and mortality

**First secure appearance:** fifteenth-century cards with hourglasses and early names *Vecchio/Tempo/Gobbo*.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** This is the historical base layer. The old man marks the last age of human life; the hourglass makes time measurable and finite. Staffs and crutches signify weakened age. Wings or deer add the paradox that time flies though age moves slowly.

**Spreaders:** Moakley, Dummett, Kaplan, Farley, Place and specialist tarot-history sites.

### Saturn and Chronos

**[Scholarly reconstruction/tradition]** Renaissance Saturn is old, cold, dry, slow, melancholic and time-governing. Those qualities fit the old-man card.

**Evidence:** broad iconographic resemblance and period allegorical vocabulary.

**Absence:** no surviving early card is captioned Saturn, and the original figure normally lacks Saturn’s sickle. Greek Chronos and Kronos/Saturn were themselves conflated in late antique and Renaissance tradition. Therefore “the Hermit was originally the god Saturn” is possible interpretation, not documented identity.

### Petrarch’s Triumph of Time

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** Tarot’s name *trionfi*, courtly triumph culture, and comparable winged/crutch-bearing Time images make Petrarch relevant.

**Caution:** Petrarch does not list tarot trumps or describe a lantern-bearing hermit. Direct derivation of the entire tarot sequence from *I Trionfi* is Moakley’s influential thesis, not consensus fact.

### Christian hermit and monastic figure

**First secure appearance:** the explicit seventeenth-century name *L’Ermite*, after earlier lamp-bearing transitional cards.

The cowl, staff and solitary progress make the figure legible as an anchorite, pilgrim or Capuchin. Christian analogues include:

- St Anthony;
- St Jerome;
- desert fathers;
- the pilgrim;
- the “Ancient of Days”;
- Christ as Light of the World.

**[Tradition]** No single saint is securely intended by Noblet, Dodal or Conver.

### Diogenes

Court de Gébelin’s wording—lantern in hand seeking Justice—echoes the famous story of Diogenes searching by daylight for an honest human being.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** This is probably an eighteenth-century interpretive association.

**[Disputed as origin]** There is no fifteenth-century title “Diogenes,” no dog in the early conventional card, and the Visconti hourglass contradicts a straightforward Diogenes origin. Diogenes is therefore a later classical gloss, not the best explanation of the first image.

### Prudence

**First explicit major occult formulation:** Lévi, 1854–1856, although writers before him sought the missing fourth cardinal virtue.

Lévi called IX’s “true name” Prudence. The lamp represents foresight; the staff cautious progress; the cloak discretion. Wirth added the serpent, recalling Prudence’s snake and Matthew 10:16, “wise as serpents.”

**[Disputed historically]** Other early tarot evidence can place Prudence elsewhere or omit it. Calling the original hourglass figure secretly “Prudence” is an occult reconstruction.

### Kabbalah

- **Mellet, 1781:** first printed systematic Hebrew-letter linkage, in reversed trump order.
- **Lévi, 1850s:** IX=Teth because I=Aleph.
- **Golden Dawn, c. 1888 onward:** Fool=Aleph, Magician=Beth, making IX=Yod.
- **Case and Crowley:** retain Yod.

**[Esoteric attribution]** None of these correspondences is evidenced on fifteenth-century cards. The conflicting letters show successive authored systems, not recovery of a single ancient key.

### Astrology: Virgo

**First secure systematic appearance for IX:** Golden Dawn materials, late nineteenth century.

Virgo contributes:

- discrimination and analysis;
- harvesting or wheat;
- assimilation/digestion;
- service;
- solitary meticulous work;
- Mercury as ruler.

**[Esoteric attribution]** Virgo is not an original Renaissance label. Saturn better fits the early Time image iconographically, while Virgo fits the later Golden Dawn initiatory Hermit. The two layers should not be collapsed.

### Alchemy

Lévi and especially Wirth interpret the cloak as a sealed vessel: transformation occurs in seclusion. Wirth’s serpent becomes controlled vital force; Crowley’s egg and serpent depict generation and reintegration.

**[Esoteric attribution]** These readings first become explicit in nineteenth- and twentieth-century occultism. There are no alchemical inscriptions on the Visconti old man.

### Hermetic initiation

Lévi’s lamp of Hermes Trismegistus, cloak of Apollonius and patriarchal staff were expanded by Ouspensky:

> “The lantern of Hermes Trismegistus… is higher knowledge.”

The three objects become initiation’s illumination, silence and authority. [Ouspensky, *The Symbolism of the Tarot*, p. 54](https://sacred-texts.com/book/the-symbolism-of-the-tarot/shell/card-ix-the-hermit).

### Crowley’s sexual and generative Hermit

Crowley’s Hermit is simultaneously solitary and reproductive. Yod is the seminal point; Virgo is the maiden; spermatozoal forms, wheat, Orphic egg and mercurial serpent make withdrawal the concealment of generative force.

Cerberus indicates mastery of the infernal or animal world and guardianship of initiation. This is a distinct Thelemic redesign, not hidden Marseille iconography.

### Jungian readings

**First appearance as tarot synthesis:** chiefly twentieth-century writers after Jung’s archetypal psychology became popular; Jung himself did not leave a systematic card-by-card tarot doctrine.

The Hermit becomes:

- Wise Old Man;
- senex;
- psychopomp or inner guide;
- introversion required for individuation;
- encounter with the Self;
- shadow form: frozen isolation, authoritarian guru, sterile intellectualism.

**[Modern invention/application]** “The Hermit is Jung’s Wise Old Man archetype” is a useful modern comparison, not evidence that Jung designed or historically decoded the card.

## People and works

### Artists, printers and occultists

- **Bonifacio Bembo / Francesco Zavattari:** competing attributions for parts of the Visconti-Sforza ensemble.
- **Jean Noblet:** c. 1650 Paris printer of the earliest substantially surviving titled Marseille-type Hermit.
- **Jean Dodal:** early eighteenth-century Lyon cardmaker.
- **Nicolas Conver:** Marseille, 1760.
- **Court de Gébelin:** made IX the Sage seeking Justice and embedded tarot in the Egyptian myth.
- **Etteilla:** converted the figure into card 18, Traitor/Faux Dévot.
- **Éliphas Lévi:** Prudence, Teth, lamp of reason.
- **Stanislas de Guaita and Oswald Wirth:** serpent-bearing initiatory Hermit.
- **S. L. MacGregor Mathers and the Golden Dawn:** Yod/Virgo/Path 20.
- **Pamela Colman Smith and A. E. Waite:** mountaintop beacon and six-pointed star.
- **Jessie Burns Parke and Paul Foster Case:** colourable BOTA synthesis.
- **Frieda Harris and Aleister Crowley:** generative cosmic Hermit.
- **Philippe Camoin and Alejandro Jodorowsky:** late-twentieth-century Marseille reconstruction.

### Calvino

**[Documented fact]** Italo Calvino’s *Il castello dei destini incrociati* first appeared in a Visconti-tarot setting in 1969; the expanded 1973 book adds a Marseille section. Mute travellers arrange cards to tell intersecting stories. The same image changes meaning by sequence and narrator.

Calvino explicitly imagines Saint Jerome in place of the Hermit when translating cards into painting. This is literary semiotics, not a historical identification of the original card. [Work history](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Castle_of_Crossed_Destinies).

### T. S. Eliot

**[Documented fact]** *The Waste Land* invokes a “wicked pack of cards,” the Wheel and Hanged Man, but **does not explicitly name the Hermit**. Eliot admitted departing from the pack “to suit my own convenience.” [Text and Eliot’s note](https://wasteland.windingway.org/46/a-wicked-pack-of-cards).

**[Critical interpretation]** Some scholars identify the later hooded figure or hermit-thrush complex with The Hermit. This is a literary reading, not an explicit Eliot attribution.

### W. B. Yeats

Yeats joined the Golden Dawn in 1890 and knew its tarot system. Critics have proposed Michael Robartes as a Hermit-like initiate: venerable traveller, bearer of concealed wisdom and guide.

**[Disputed literary identification]** Yeats did not simply label Robartes “Tarot Hermit” in the relevant texts. The connection is scholarly interpretation. [Kathleen Raine discussion reproduced in the Societas Magica material](https://societasmagica.org/userfiles/files/Newsletters/docs/SMN_Spring_2006_Issue_15.pdf).

### Led Zeppelin IV

**[Documented fact]** Barrington Colby’s interior illustration for *Led Zeppelin IV* (1971) shows a mountaintop Hermit holding a lamp over a town. Jimmy Page acknowledged the tarot association.

**[Probable influence]** The composition is strongly RWS-derived. Page also associated it with Holman Hunt’s *The Light of the World*. This made the RWS Hermit an important popular-rock emblem.

### Bergman

**[Important negative finding]** Ingmar Bergman’s films repeatedly feature isolation, faith, silence, medieval allegory and encounters with Death—notably *The Seventh Seal*—but no securely documented direct quotation of The Hermit card was found. General resemblance between a lantern-bearing solitary and Bergmanesque imagery is interpretive.

### Jodorowsky’s films

Jodorowsky’s cinema uses hermits, alchemists, initiatory masters and tarot structures, especially in *The Holy Mountain*. He later made tarot central to psychomagic and co-created the Camoin–Jodorowsky deck.

**[Caution]** The alchemist of *The Holy Mountain* can be compared with the Hermit/Magician archetype, but it is not simply a filmed reproduction of trump IX.

### Paintings and buildings

Strong visual comparanda include:

- illustrated *Triumphs of Time* with winged old men, hourglasses, crutches and deer;
- Holman Hunt, *The Light of the World* (1851–1853), Christ bearing a lantern;
- representations of St Jerome and desert hermits;
- the “Ancient of Days” tradition.

**[Open/negative finding]** No historically verified Renaissance building program was located that demonstrably quotes the tarot Hermit as a card. Claims that cathedral sculpture encodes the entire Major Arcana are modern esoteric pattern-matching unless supported by building records.

## Controversies and disputes

### Egyptian origin

**Claim:** Tarot is the Egyptian Book of Thoth, preserved as hieroglyphs.

- **First major printed promoter:** Court de Gébelin, 1781.
- **Spreaders:** Mellet, Etteilla, Paul Christian, Papus and innumerable occult decks.
- **Evidence offered:** perceived Egyptian-looking imagery, speculative etymologies and the number 22.
- **Historical evidence:** none before Gébelin; no Egyptian tarot, text, archaeological sequence or valid Egyptian derivation of *tarot* exists.
- **Classification:** **[Legend/esoteric attribution]** as ancient history; **[documented fact]** as an influential eighteenth-century belief.

Waite himself concluded that there was “no particle of evidence” for Egyptian origin. [Waite historical chapter](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Page:The_Illustrated_Key_to_the_Tarot.djvu/36).

### Romani or “Gypsy” transmission

**Claim:** Romani travellers brought tarot from Egypt or India.

- **Early associations:** Gébelin/Mellet-era Egyptomania.
- **Major nineteenth-century spreaders:** Boiteau d’Ambly, Jean-Alexandre Vaillant and later Papus.
- **Evidence:** Romani fortune-tellers later used cards.
- **Counter-evidence:** tarot is documented in northern Italian courts in the fifteenth century; documentation of Romani card-reading does not show invention or transmission.
- **Classification:** **[Legend/disputed claim]**.

The historic exonym “Gypsy” arose from the mistaken belief that Roma came from Egypt. Modern scholarship traces Romani origins to South Asia.

### Cathar/Albigensian origin

**Claim:** Tarot covertly preserved Cathar dualism or a persecuted Gnostic gospel.

- **Spreaders:** twentieth-century esoteric and alternative-history writers.
- **Evidence offered:** geography, dual images, Popess, Devil and alleged doctrinal sequences.
- **Problem:** no Cathar document mentions tarot; the first cards postdate the suppression of organized Catharism; the images are intelligible in ordinary Christian and civic Renaissance culture.
- **Classification:** **[Disputed modern invention]**.

### Templar origin

**Claim:** Knights Templar transmitted a secret initiatory book later encoded in tarot.

- **Evidence:** symbolic resemblance, speculative lineage through Freemasonry.
- **Problem:** no Templar tarot, inventory, ritual text or transmission chain.
- **Classification:** **[Legend]**.

### Hermit as Diogenes

- **Evidence for:** lantern, wandering sage, Gébelin’s “seeking Justice.”
- **Evidence against:** earliest object is an hourglass; early names say Old Man/Time/Hunchback; no dog or Diogenes caption.
- **Conclusion:** **[Later interpretive tradition]**, not established origin.

### Hermit as Saturn/Chronos

- **Evidence for:** aged body, Time-name, hourglass, melancholy/slowness, Renaissance conflation of Saturn with Time.
- **Evidence against:** no early caption “Saturn”; sickle often absent.
- **Conclusion:** **[Strong scholarly reconstruction]**, but not exclusive proof of identity.

### Hourglass “misprinted” as lantern

A popular story says a careless engraver mistook the hourglass for a lantern.

- **Evidence for:** simplified woodcut copying could transform shapes; later packs clearly use a lantern.
- **Against:** Budapest and other transitional images deliberately resemble lamp-bearing walkers, and semantic change could accompany intentional redesign.
- **Conclusion:** **[Plausible but unproved reconstruction]**. It should not be repeated as documented fact.

### Missing Prudence

Lévi claimed Hermit’s true name was Prudence, completing four cardinal virtues.

- **Evidence:** cautious gait, lamp and serpent; tarot otherwise plainly contains Justice, Fortitude and Temperance.
- **Against:** early names identify Time/Old Man, and other cards—including the Hanged Man and World in various theories—have been proposed as Prudence.
- **Conclusion:** **[Occult attribution/disputed historical identification]**.

### Number disputes

- Early luxury trumps were often unnumbered.
- The *Sermo*’s **El Gobbo** is eleventh in its rhetorical list, not proof that every deck printed XI on the card.
- Marseille establishes VIIII/IX.
- Minchiate uses V; Sicilian uses XII; Etteilla’s visual analogue is XVIII.
- Golden Dawn, RWS, BOTA and Thoth retain IX.

Thus “The Hermit has always been number nine” is false.

### The “Gringonneur 1392 Hermit”

The surviving “Charles VI” cards were once joined to the 1392 payment to Jacquemin Gringonneur.

- Modern stylistic scholarship places them in late-fifteenth-century Italy.
- The name remains bibliographic shorthand.
- Using them to date the Hermit to 1392 is an antiquarian error that became tradition.

### Mantegna tarot

The engravings are not a tarot deck. Using their fifty images as proof that tarot began as a humanistic initiation system confuses a related educational image-cycle with playing cards.

### Sola Busca as occult cipher

Its imagery is undeniably unusual and may contain learned, alchemical or political references. But it is not the conventional Major Arcana and its Ipeo cannot simply be relabelled Hermit IX.

### Camoin–Jodorowsky “restoration”

The deck is valuable as an informed artistic synthesis. It cannot be treated as a photographic recovery of a lost ur-tarot because no complete fifteenth-century Marseille-pattern original survives against which its small reconstructed details could be checked.

### Jung’s tarot

Jungian interpretation has been enormously productive, but claims that Jung personally established a full 22-card archetypal system are overstated. Later writers applied Jungian categories to tarot.

## Historiographical correction

Michael Dummett’s central correction was methodological: surviving rules and documents show that tarot began as a card game; occult meanings appear centuries later. Ronald Decker and Thierry Depaulis traced the people and publications through which the occult history was constructed. Helen Farley situated tarot within Renaissance court culture and later esotericism. Stuart Kaplan catalogued early decks and helped expose their physical diversity. Robert M. Place popularized the Time/hourglass genealogy. Gertrude Moakley connected the cards to Renaissance triumphal imagery, though her Petrarch/procession thesis remains partly reconstructive.

Their work does not prove that occult readings are aesthetically or spiritually worthless. It proves that an attribution made in 1781, 1856 or 1888 must be dated to that period unless earlier evidence exists.

## Open questions

1. **Exact invention site:** Milan, Ferrara, Bologna and Florence all have early evidence. No surviving document says who invented the standard 22-trump structure.

2. **Exact date of the conventional Old Man:** the Visconti-Sforza example is mid-fifteenth century, but the prototype may be earlier.

3. **First lantern:** the Budapest sheets currently provide strong early evidence, but their precise date and locality are debated.

4. **Mechanism of change:** whether hourglass became lantern through copying error, deliberate iconographic reinterpretation, or multiple parallel models cannot be settled.

5. **Original primary identity:** Time is better supported than Hermit, but “old age,” “the last age of man,” Saturn and Petrarchan Time may have coexisted rather than being mutually exclusive.

6. **Winged Bolognese figure:** intentional “time flies” allegory and copying accident remain competing explanations.

7. **Workshop attribution:** Bembo versus Zavattari and workshop collaborators remains unsettled for parts of the Visconti-Sforza corpus.

8. **Exact *Sermo* date:** commonly c. 1470 but paleographical ranges extend from about 1450 to 1500.

9. **Smith’s immediate models:** Waite’s directions are known, but no complete working notebook by Pamela Colman Smith securely enumerates the visual sources of her Hermit.

10. **Holman Hunt influence:** attractive and plausible, but still inferential.

11. **Direct literary allusions:** Yeats and Eliot invite tarot readings, but claims about a particular unnamed figure must remain interpretations unless the author explicitly identifies the card.

12. **Buildings quoting IX:** no securely documented premodern architectural quotation was found.
