# The Star: Research Dossier

> **Scope and method.** This dossier concerns the tarot trump conventionally numbered XVII: Italian *La Stella*, French *L’Étoile*, English *The Star*. Labels used throughout:
>
> - **[Documented fact]** directly attested by an object, archival record, rulebook, or original printed text.
> - **[Scholarly reconstruction]** an inference advanced by identified historians.
> - **[Tradition]** a customary interpretation whose historical truth is not necessarily claimed.
> - **[Esoteric attribution]** a deliberately constructed occult correspondence.
> - **[Disputed]** a claim for which competing interpretations or chronologies exist.
> - **[Legend]** a historical-origin story contradicted by, or unsupported in, the surviving evidence.
> - **[Modern invention]** a twentieth- or twenty-first-century redesign or psychological interpretation not found in early tarot.
>
> Dates for hand-painted cards are necessarily approximate. “Visconti-Sforza” below means the dispersed Pierpont Morgan–Bergamo–Colleoni pack, not the Cary-Yale/Visconti di Modrone pack.

### A necessary distinction about “the earliest”

**[Documented fact]** Martiano da Tortona’s approximately 1418–1425 description of a special pack made for Filippo Maria Visconti is earlier than every surviving standard tarot pack. It contained sixteen classical gods painted by Michelino da Besozzo. It did **not** contain a trump called the Star and was not the standard twenty-one-trump sequence. It is evidence for the immediate prehistory of the tarot pack, not an attestation of this card.

**[Documented fact]** The earliest surviving Milanese packs—the Brambilla and Cary-Yale/Visconti di Modrone groups—are incomplete. No securely identifiable Star survives in either.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** A Star could have belonged to the lost portions of those packs, but absence cannot establish that. Suggestions that Cary-Yale’s theological virtue Hope replaced or anticipated the Star are iconographic hypotheses, not a surviving title or rule.

## The names and numbering

### Fifteenth- and sixteenth-century Italy

The cards were first called **carte da trionfi**, “cards of triumphs.” The game was *trionfi*. During the early sixteenth century, **tarocchi** became the usual Italian name, probably in part to distinguish the card game from other things called *trionfi*.

The etymology of *tarocco/tarocchi* remains uncertain.

- **[Documented fact]** The word is European and appears in Renaissance Italian usage.
- **[Legend]** Court de Gébelin’s supposed Egyptian derivation *Tar* + *Ro/Rog*, often glossed “royal road,” has no Egyptian hieroglyphic evidence. Michael Dummett specifically criticized Egyptian etymologies that supplied transliterations without hieroglyphic originals.
- **[Disputed]** Proposed relationships to *taroccare*, *tarocco* as “foolish,” the Taro River, Arabic expressions, or anagrams are conjectural.

The earliest cards had neither printed numbers nor captions. Players memorized the local order. Numbers became common in printed packs during the sixteenth century, but Bologna resisted printed numbering until much later.

### France

- **Tarot** became the French name for both pack and game.
- The card is normally **L’Étoile, XVII**.
- Occultists sometimes used **Les Étoiles**, notably Paul Foster Case’s French-style heading.
- “Tarot de Marseille” is a modern scholarly/commercial label for a design family. Seventeenth-century makers did not necessarily print that phrase on their packs.

### Besançon and other regional renamings

The Besançon pattern is chiefly distinguished by replacing the controversial Popess and Pope with **Juno** and **Jupiter**. These substitutions became established in eastern France, Switzerland, and adjacent German-speaking markets during the eighteenth century. They do not materially rename the Star.

Comparable changes elsewhere included:

- the Tower as **La Maison Dieu**, already familiar in French Marseille-family packs;
- the Fool as **Le Mat**, usually without a numeral;
- the Popess/Pope as Juno/Jupiter in Besançon;
- the Sicilian removal or radical transformation of the Pope and the special treatment of the Fool;
- later Belgian/Flemish patterns replacing some canonical figures with occupations or astronomers.

These examples demonstrate that the trump sequence was a family of regional game patterns, not an absolutely fixed occult scripture.

# Iconography deck by deck

## 1. Martiano da Tortona and Michelino da Besozzo, Milan, c. 1418–1425

**Survival:** Martiano’s Latin explanatory text survives; the cards do not.

**Depiction:** No Star card is described. The sixteen “trumps” were classical gods associated with four allegorical groupings.

**Significance:**  

- **[Documented fact]** This is an early special trick-taking pack commissioned by Filippo Maria Visconti.
- **[Scholarly reconstruction]** Historians regard it as a close precursor to tarot because it adds a permanently superior pictorial series to four suits.
- **[Unsupported claim]** It cannot be cited as evidence that the standard Star already existed.

## 2. Visconti di Modrone / Cary-Yale, Milan, c. 1441–1447

**Survival:** Sixty-seven cards, at Yale’s Beinecke Library.

**Star:** No uncontested Star survives.

The pack includes unusual additional court cards and the theological virtues Faith, Hope, and Charity.

**[Disputed]** Some writers have proposed that Hope occupies a conceptual or sequential place later taken by the Star. Hope’s established Christian attribute—looking heavenward, sometimes toward a star—makes this visually plausible. It does not demonstrate that the card was named Stella.

## 3. Brera-Brambilla, Milan, c. 1440s

**Survival:** Fragmentary, now chiefly at the Pinacoteca di Brera.

**Star:** Not surviving.

**Importance:** The deck is evidence for the early Milanese form of *trionfi*. Any reconstruction of its Star is speculative.

## 4. Visconti-Sforza, Milan/Cremona, c. 1450s–1460s

The dispersed deck is preserved principally by the Morgan Library, Accademia Carrara, and Colleoni family.

### What is depicted

A young, richly dressed woman stands on grass. She wears a blue gown and red or rose-colored drapery. A large many-rayed star appears close to her uplifted hand; she seems to support, touch, or reach toward it. The gold ground contains punched and tooled decoration.

There is:

- no nudity;
- no pool;
- no pouring jugs;
- no tree or bird;
- no group of seven secondary stars.

### Interpretation

- **[Documented fact]** This is the earliest surviving card generally identified as the standard tarot Star.
- **[Scholarly reconstruction]** The woman may be a personification of the Star, astrology, Venus, Hope, or celestial guidance.
- **[Disputed]** Identifying her specifically as Venus rests on visual parallels, including female figures with stars and Venus’s conventional nudity or beauty, rather than a caption.
- **[Disputed]** Her apparent act of taking down a star has been connected with the Visconti motto *A bon droyt*, dynastic emblems, and courtly aspirations.
- **[Not evidenced]** Nothing on the card establishes Aquarius or Kabbalah. Those systems are much later.

The Morgan dates its own part of the pack approximately 1450–1480 and stresses that the cards were gaming objects, not Renaissance fortune-telling instruments. Attribution has ranged from Bonifacio Bembo and his workshop to Francesco Zavattari; some later replacement cards have been attributed to Antonio Cicognara, Benedetto Bembo, or Franco dei Russi. The Star itself belongs to the old painted sequence, not to Pamela Colman Smith’s or an occultist’s invention.

## 5. Ferrarese/Este cards, late fifteenth century

A surviving Ferrarese Star associated with the Este court shows **two observers or astrologers** looking upward. One holds or consults a book; the other points toward the heavens. A lunar diagram reportedly appears in the book.

- **[Documented fact]** The imagery is observational or astrological rather than the Marseille water-pourer.
- **[Scholarly reconstruction]** It may reflect the Este court’s interest in astrology.
- **[Disputed]** Calling the figures particular historical astrologers goes beyond the surviving evidence.

The neighboring Sun and Moon cards in early Ferrarese material similarly emphasize courtly astronomy/astrology. This indicates that the three celestial trumps were not yet iconographically uniform.

## 6. Budapest sheets, late fifteenth or early sixteenth century

The Budapest woodcut sheets preserve fragmentary early printed trumps. Their Star includes a human figure reaching upward toward a star.

**Meaning of the change:** The image belongs to the early “figure plus star” family and is closer to the Visconti-Sforza conception than to the later Marseille pourer.

## 7. Rosenwald sheets, Italy, late fifteenth or early sixteenth century

The Rosenwald group consists of early uncut or fragmentary woodcut sheets.

**Star:** A celestial sign dominates above a human figure, but surviving impressions and identifications are less secure than later named decks.

**Caution:** It is unsafe to treat every reconstructed sheet position as a titled, authorially numbered Star. Sheet order, cutting, and later handwritten numbers require object-by-object examination.

## 8. The so-called Mantegna “Tarocchi,” c. 1465–1475

These fifty engravings are not a tarot pack and were not designed by Andrea Mantegna. They present hierarchical series—social estates, Apollo and the Muses, liberal arts, virtues, cosmic spheres, and first causes.

There is no standard tarot Star trump.

**Relevance:**  

- **[Documented fact]** The series illustrates the same Renaissance habit of arranging social, moral, and cosmic knowledge in ascending pictorial sequences.
- **[Scholarly reconstruction]** It is valuable comparative evidence for how celestial hierarchy could be visualized.
- **[Misnomer]** “Mantegna Tarocchi” is a traditional but misleading later name.

## 9. Sola Busca, Venice/Ferrara region, 1491

The Sola Busca is the earliest surviving complete tarot pack and the earliest known complete pack with narrative scenes on every pip card.

Its trumps bear classical or pseudo-classical warriors rather than the standard Popess-to-World series. Consequently, there is no canonical Star corresponding iconographically to Marseille XVII.

**Relevance to this card:** Limited. Its importance lies chiefly in Pamela Colman Smith’s apparent borrowing of compositional details from several of its illustrated minor cards, not in her Star.

## 10. Minchiate, Florence, sixteenth–eighteenth centuries

Minchiate expanded the trump series to forty permanent trumps, including the four elements, twelve zodiacal signs, additional virtues, and cosmic cards.

The ordinary tarot celestial sequence was reorganized. Star-like imagery exists among zodiacal and astronomical cards, but a direct, separately numbered XVII equivalent cannot simply be assumed.

- **[Documented fact]** Aquarius appears explicitly in Minchiate as a zodiacal trump.
- **[Important distinction]** This does not prove the ordinary tarot Star originally meant Aquarius. It proves that Minchiate could represent Aquarius directly when its designers wished to do so.

## 11. Bolognese tarot / tarocchino

The Bolognese game reduced the four suits but retained the trump structure. Traditional Bolognese trumps were long unnumbered. The Star is one of the celestial cards below Moon and Sun.

Early iconography varies and surviving documentation is later than the Milanese painted packs. The Bolognese order belongs to Dummett’s type A or “Southern” family.

## 12. Sicilian tarot

The Sicilian pattern descended from the southern family but developed distinctive titles, imagery, and ranking. The Fool, called **Il Misero** in characteristic Sicilian packs, has a special low status; ecclesiastical trumps were altered.

The Star remains a celestial trump but should not automatically be assigned Marseille XVII in descriptions of play: Sicilian trump numbering and ranking are pattern-specific.

## 13. Anonymous Tarot de Paris, early seventeenth century

The Star is not yet the standardized kneeling water-pourer. An astronomer or astrologer appears at a desk, using compasses or dividers while looking toward the sky.

This continues the Ferrarese “astronomer” solution and shows that French production did not move in one straight line from Milan to Marseille.

## 14. Jacques Viéville, Paris, c. 1650

Viéville’s tarot preserves several non-Marseille compositions and reversed orientations. Its Star belongs to an alternative French lineage rather than the fully standardized Noblet–Dodal–Conver type.

The card emphasizes celestial observation rather than reproducing all later Marseille details.

## 15. Jean Noblet, Paris, c. 1650

This is the earliest substantially surviving named French pack generally assigned to the Marseille design family.

### Depiction

A nude woman kneels or crouches beside water and pours from two vessels, one stream toward water and one toward land. A large central star and seven smaller stars occupy the sky. Vegetation and a bird-bearing tree appear.

### Innovations consolidated here

- the single female figure becomes nude;
- the act of reaching for a star becomes the action of pouring;
- one large plus seven smaller stars supplies the canonical total of eight;
- land/water duality becomes explicit;
- the bird and tree enter the durable tradition.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** This composition may combine Aquarius-like water-bearing, river-source personifications, Edenic or baptismal water, and the personification of Hope or Truth. No surviving designer’s explanation decides among them.

## 16. Jean Dodal, Lyon, c. 1701–1715

Dodal regularizes the Marseille type:

- frontal or three-quarter nude woman;
- one knee on the ground;
- two jugs;
- water flowing into water and onto earth;
- a very large star surrounded by seven small stars;
- tree and bird.

The figure’s anatomy and the exact streams differ from Noblet. The name is printed **L’ETOILLE** or with period spelling appropriate to the block.

## 17. Pierre Madenié and related Dijon/Lyon packs, early eighteenth century

These maintain the Marseille schema while changing carving style, color placement, facial expression, and stellar geometry. They demonstrate that “the Marseille Star” is a transmitted workshop pattern, not a single original drawing.

## 18. François Chosson, Marseille, date disputed

Some impressions bear dates read as 1672, but scholars have debated whether the surviving cards are from that year or a later recut/printing. Its Star is recognizably Marseille.

This is an example where an apparently precise printed date is not automatically a secure date for the physical impression.

## 19. Nicolas Conver, Marseille, 1760

Conver’s pack became one of the most influential Marseille exemplars.

### Depiction

The woman kneels with one foot or knee near/in the blue water and the other on yellow earth. Two red/yellow/blue vessels pour branching streams. A huge central star and seven smaller stars fill the upper field. A dark bird sits in a tree at the right.

### Later significance

The Conver pattern became a model for twentieth-century Grimaud restoration and, indirectly, Jodorowsky and Camoin.

## 20. Besançon pattern, eighteenth–nineteenth centuries

The Star generally preserves the Marseille pourer. The regional theological substitutions concern other cards—Juno/Jupiter for Popess/Pope—not XVII.

Some Belgian/Flemish and related packs instead place a seated astronomer under the star, preserving an older alternative iconography.

## 21. Etteilla’s Grand Etteilla, c. 1788–1789

Etteilla did not merely annotate the Marseille trumps. He reordered and redesigned the whole pack as an alleged Egyptian Book of Thoth.

Accordingly, there is no straightforward equation:

> Grand Etteilla card number X = Marseille XVII.

Celestial, elemental, and creation scenes occupy the opening cards; traditional trump imagery is dispersed and renamed. Modern “Grand Etteilla” editions also differ among Etteilla I, II, and III patterns.

**[Documented fact]** Etteilla placed upright and reversed keywords on cards and developed a complete professional cartomantic system.

**[Esoteric attribution]** He claimed the pack preserved Egyptian wisdom and connected it with astrology and creation.

**[Legend]** The deck is not a recovered Egyptian artifact. It is an eighteenth-century French reconstruction.

## 22. Court de Gébelin’s engraved tarot, 1781

In volume VIII of *Le Monde primitif*, Gébelin reproduced and interpreted the conventional French trump. He treated the entire pack as an Egyptian allegorical book.

The Star was read through celestial and calendrical symbolism, while the accompanying essay by the Comte de Mellet attempted to align the twenty-two trumps with Hebrew letters.

This is the decisive point at which a European gaming image became, in print, part of an allegedly ancient initiatory system.

## 23. Éliphas Lévi, 1854–1856

Lévi did not publish a complete colored deck comparable with Wirth’s, but his *Dogme et rituel de la haute magie* established the program of reading twenty-two trumps through the twenty-two Hebrew letters.

His system is not the later Golden Dawn assignment:

- Lévi’s sequential scheme gives trump XVII the seventeenth Hebrew letter, **Peh (פ)**.
- The Golden Dawn later gives the Star **Tzaddi (צ)** by starting the zodiacal sequence with the Emperor/Heh arrangement and placing the Fool at Aleph.

Lévi’s key claim was that the tarot was “a truly philosophical machine” and a universal key to symbols. That is an occult theory, not a finding from fifteenth-century documents.

## 24. Oswald Wirth, 1889

Wirth drew twenty-two trumps under the direction of Stanislas de Guaita. His Star is a consciously occultized Marseille derivative:

- nude female water-pourer;
- large central star and secondary stars;
- vegetation and water;
- Hebrew letter and esoteric emblematic detail;
- a cleaner, initiatory line style.

Wirth largely inherited the French Lévi–Papus sequence rather than the Golden Dawn’s secret English correspondences. His Star is therefore associated with **Peh** in the Lévi-style sequence, not Tzaddi.

Wirth helped spread the interpretation of the figure as unveiled nature, cosmic influence, hope, and the renewal of life.

## 25. Papus, *Le Tarot des Bohémiens*, 1889

Papus reproduced occultized trumps and diagrams rather than creating the most influential independent painted Star. He systematized tarot as a rotational, numerical, Kabbalistic mechanism.

His title “Tarot of the Bohemians” helped perpetuate the Romani transmission story.

- **[Esoteric attribution]** Tarot as a synthetic book of numbers, letters, astrology, and Kabbalah.
- **[Legend]** The Romani were custodians who transported it from ancient Egypt.
- **[Historical consequence]** Papus spread Lévi’s program internationally and made “Gypsy tarot” a durable popular phrase.

## 26. Golden Dawn manuscript designs, c. 1888–1900

The Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn gave the Star:

- title **Daughter of the Firmament; Dweller between the Waters**;
- letter **Tzaddi**;
- sign **Aquarius**;
- path **28**, between Netzach and Yesod;
- elemental basis **Air**, because Aquarius is an air sign despite the water imagery.

The Order expected initiates to construct or color personal decks from verbal descriptions and color scales. No single commercially printed “original Golden Dawn deck” survives as the definitive authorized object.

### Later reconstructions

- **Robert Wang, Golden Dawn Tarot**: follows Israel Regardie’s published Golden Dawn materials and presents a vividly colored esoteric Star.
- **Chic and Sandra Tabatha Cicero, Golden Dawn Magical Tarot**: a later, more textually explicit reconstruction.
- **Caution:** These are twentieth-century visual realizations of manuscript instructions, not nineteenth-century mass-produced Golden Dawn cards.

## 27. Waite-Smith, 1909; *Pictorial Key*, 1910

Pamela Colman Smith drew the card under Arthur Edward Waite’s direction.

### What Smith depicts

- a nude, youthful woman;
- left knee on land, right foot touching or resting upon water;
- two large ewers;
- five streams from the landward vessel, spreading over the ground;
- a main eight-pointed yellow star;
- seven smaller eight-pointed white stars;
- a tree with a black bird;
- low hills and open landscape.

Waite describes it:

> “A great, radiant star of eight rays, surrounded by seven lesser stars—also of eight rays.”

He continues that the woman pours the “Water of Life,” irrigating land and sea, and calls the figure a type of “Truth unveiled.”

### Sources and innovations

- **[Documented visual dependence]** The main composition is inherited from Marseille, especially the Conver-like lineage.
- **[Scholarly reconstruction]** Smith probably knew several available French reproductions and Waite’s Golden Dawn materials.
- **[Disputed]** Claims that she copied a particular single Marseille printing are difficult to prove.
- **[Documented broader influence]** Smith borrowed some minor-card poses from the Sola Busca. The Star, however, is fundamentally a Marseille-derived composition, not a demonstrated Sola Busca borrowing.
- **[Waite’s own assertion]** The bird is an **ibis**.
- **[Iconographic difficulty]** Smith’s bird is a small dark, compact bird and does not anatomically resemble a long-legged ibis. “Ibis” is therefore Waite’s intended identification, not an unambiguous zoological depiction.
- **[Esoteric attribution]** The ibis evokes Thoth, Egyptian wisdom, or the mind, but Waite does not give a full source genealogy for it.

## 28. Paul Foster Case / BOTA, 1920s–1930s

Case’s Builders of the Adytum deck was drawn by **Jessie Burns Parke** and intended to be hand-colored by students.

The Star follows Waite/Marseille:

- nude kneeling woman;
- eight stars;
- bird/tree;
- dual water pouring.

Case interprets the woman as the subconscious or cosmic mind working through meditation; the water as universal mind-stuff or life-force; the five landward streams as sensory differentiation; and the eight-pointed star as cosmic energy.

He retains **Tzaddi, Aquarius, path 28** and rejects Crowley’s exchange.

The practice of coloring the deck is part of BOTA’s teaching method: color correspondences are meant to train symbolic association, not merely decorate the cards.

## 29. Crowley-Harris Thoth, designed 1938–1943; first full commercial publication 1969

Frieda Harris painted the card under Aleister Crowley’s supervision.

### What is depicted

The central female figure is **Nuit/Nuith**, goddess of infinite night and space. She is elongated, transparent, ecstatic, and cosmic rather than naturalistic.

She holds two cups:

- a golden cup above her head pours luminous fluid or “starry milk”;
- a lower vessel pours an immortal liquor toward the earth or sea.

A huge seven-pointed star dominates the image. A smaller seven-pointed figure appears below. Spirals, crystals, roses, celestial globes, and waves replace the rustic Marseille landscape.

Crowley writes:

> “This card is attributed to the letter He’. It refers to the Zodiacal sign of Aquarius, the water-bearer. The picture represents Nuith, our Lady of the Stars.”

He associates the seven-pointed star with **Babalon**, whose sacred number is seven and whose emblem is the heptagram.

### What changed

- Truth/Hope becomes Nuit, the Thelemic star-goddess.
- Natural water becomes cosmic or sexual-creative outpouring.
- The eight-rayed Marseille/Waite star becomes the seven-pointed Star of Babalon.
- Aquarius remains central.
- The Hebrew letter changes from Golden Dawn Tzaddi to **Heh**.

### The Heh/Tzaddi problem

*Liber AL vel Legis* I:57 says:

> “All these old letters of my Book are aright; but צ is not the Star.”

Crowley eventually interpreted this as requiring an exchange between the Star and Emperor:

- Emperor: Tzaddi rather than Heh;
- Star: Heh rather than Tzaddi.

But his presentation creates complications:

1. In *The Book of Thoth*, the Star chapter says **Heh** and **Aquarius**.
2. Traditional Sepher Yetzirah/Golden Dawn zodiacal attribution gives Heh–Aries and Tzaddi–Aquarius.
3. Crowley discusses a zodiacal “double loop” produced by the exchange.
4. Some charts and later summaries treat the entire letter-and-zodiac complexes as exchanged; others exchange only the letters while retaining the cards’ obvious signs.
5. Harris’s Star is overwhelmingly Aquarian in imagery, while her Emperor remains overwhelmingly Aries-like.

**Finding:** The slogan “Crowley made the Star Aries” is incomplete. His formal tables can be read that way when letter and zodiac are moved together, but his own Star chapter and painting explicitly preserve Aquarius. The Thoth corpus contains a real tension between tabular permutation, prose attribution, and pictorial program.

## 30. Grimaud Ancien Tarot de Marseille, twentieth century

Paul Marteau’s Grimaud version popularized a cleaned and recolored Conver-derived Marseille design. Its saturated primaries and firm black outlines became, for many twentieth-century readers, “the” old Marseille tarot.

Marteau’s explanatory system imposed later numerical, alchemical, and color symbolism on an early-modern cardmaking pattern.

## 31. Camoin–Jodorowsky Marseille, 1997

Philippe Camoin and Alejandro Jodorowsky reconstructed a Marseille pack using historical printing blocks and comparative details from multiple decks.

Its Star restores or emphasizes:

- two vessels;
- the bird/tree;
- eight stars;
- patterned streams;
- color details treated as symbolically intentional.

Jodorowsky and Marianne Costa read the card as generosity, cosmic reception, purification, naked authenticity, finding one’s place, and giving without depletion.

**[Disputed]** The project is marketed as restoration of a primordial or complete Tarot de Marseille.

**[Scholarly objection]** No evidence shows that every micro-detail reconstructed from different historical packs belonged to one lost original archetype. It is both historical reconstruction and a modern esoteric synthesis.

## 32. Notable modern redesigns

### Salvador Dalí, *Dalí Universal Tarot*, conceived 1970s; published 1984

Dalí uses collage, quotation from Western painting, self-reference, and Surrealist space. The Star becomes part of a personal art-historical tarot rather than a restoration of early imagery.

### Niki de Saint Phalle, Tarot Garden, Tuscany, begun 1979; opened 1998

The Tarot Garden monumentalizes the major arcana as inhabitable sculpture and architecture. The project demonstrates tarot’s movement from card image to environment. The Star participates in Saint Phalle’s feminist, bodily, and utopian visual universe.

### Motherpeace Tarot, Karen Vogel and Vicki Noble, 1970s

The circular card reframes the Star through feminist spirituality, ecological healing, and goddess imagery. This is a modern religious-psychological reading, not a recovered prehistoric matriarchal tarot.

### Wild Unknown, Kim Krans, 2012

The human water-pourer disappears; a geometric multicolored star radiates in darkness. Hope and orientation are retained through pure abstraction.

### Modern inclusive decks

Many recent decks change the woman’s race, body, sex, gender, or species; replace the ibis with locally meaningful birds; or treat the streams as trauma healing, ecological reciprocity, queer disclosure, or nervous-system regulation.

These are **modern inventions** in iconography, though they grow from the durable themes of nudity, renewal, and freely flowing water.

# The game

## Fundamental function

The Star was invented as a **permanent trump**, not originally as a fortune-telling card.

In trick-taking:

- a trump beats an ordinary suited card;
- a higher trump beats a lower trump;
- suit-following and overtrumping rules vary by game;
- the Star generally has no extraordinary power beyond its rank.

## Rank

In the familiar Milanese/French/Marseille order:

> XVI Tower → **XVII Star** → XVIII Moon → XIX Sun → XX Judgment → XXI World

Thus the Star is fifth from the top if World is highest and Judgment is next, or lower under regional variations.

## Card-point value

Rank and point value are different.

In many traditional tarot games:

- high trumps, low trump I, and Fool have special counting values;
- ordinary intermediate trumps, including XVII, usually count only the default low value;
- in modern French tarot, trump XVII is an ordinary *atout*: it is **not** an *oudler*. The oudlers are I, XXI, and the Excuse.
- in many Austrian Tarock games, XVII is an ordinary numbered tarock unless a contract or bonus specifically selects it.

Thus “seventeenth trump” does not mean “worth seventeen points.”

## The three regional orders

Michael Dummett reconstructed three early order families.

| Type | Region | Characteristic high order |
|---|---|---|
| A / Southern | Bologna, Florence; later Rome and Sicily | Angel/Judgment above World; virtues clustered relatively low |
| B / Eastern | Ferrara, probably Venice | World highest; Justice and Angel unusually high |
| C / Western | Milan, Pavia, France | World highest, then Angel/Judgment; source of the Marseille numerical order |

The Star belongs to the group of celestial trumps. Its exact ordinal number changes when higher trumps or virtues move.

### Justice and Strength

Early unnumbered cards did not possess one universally printed order. Consequently:

- Marseille eventually fixed Justice at VIII and Strength at XI.
- Golden Dawn correspondences required Leo and Libra to follow zodiacal sequence.
- Waite therefore printed Strength VIII and Justice XI.
- Crowley retained the astrological logic but renamed them Lust VIII and Adjustment XI.
- Marseille-oriented decks normally retain Justice VIII and Strength XI.

This dispute does not move the Star’s printed number in the common systems, but it shows why “traditional order” must be qualified by place and date.

## The Fool

The Fool was traditionally unnumbered and operated as an excuse: it could be played to a trick without ordinarily winning it, and its owner recovered it subject to local compensation rules. It was not simply “trump zero” in early play.

Numbering the Fool 0 and placing it at the beginning of an occult alphabet is an eighteenth- and nineteenth-century interpretive development.

## Minchiate

Minchiate has forty trumps rather than twenty-one. Because the zodiac appears explicitly, there is no direct one-to-one game rank between its Aquarius and ordinary tarot XVII. Minchiate rules and scoring vary, with numerous valuable combinations or *versicole*.

## Tarock

In central-European French-suited Tarock packs, the old allegorical pictures were largely replaced by genre scenes, animals, or decorative images. Roman-numeral tarocks preserve rank, and XVII remains simply a relatively high permanent trump. It carries no necessary woman, star, or Aquarius imagery.

# Chronology of attestation

## c. 1418–1425: Martiano da Tortona

Martiano describes Michelino da Besozzo’s pack of sixteen classical gods for Filippo Maria Visconti.

- **Fact:** earliest fully described European pack with a special permanent trump-like suit.
- **Absence:** no Star.
- **Reconstruction:** close ancestor or parallel experiment in the development of tarot.

## 1440: Florence

A notarial diary by Giusto Giusti records giving Sigismondo Malatesta a pack of **naibi a trionfi**.

This predates the once-famous Ferrara 1442 record.

No individual trump is named.

## 1442: Ferrara court ledgers

The Este accounts record payment to Jacopo da Sagramoro for painted packs of triumphs and purchases of cheaper packs.

- **Fact:** tarot/trionfi was established at Ferrara.
- **Absence:** the ledger does not supply a Star title or trump list.

## c. 1440s: Brambilla and Cary-Yale

Surviving Milanese luxury cards show the game’s development.

- **Absence:** no surviving Star in either incomplete pack.

## c. 1450s–1460s: Visconti-Sforza Star

Earliest surviving standard Star: clothed woman reaching toward or bearing a star.

## c. 1460s–1490s: Ferrarese and early printed forms

The figure changes among:

- astrologers with book and instruments;
- single figures reaching upward;
- celestial-personification imagery.

## c. 1470–1500: *Sermo perutilis de ludo cum aliis*

The anonymous preacher condemns the game and lists its trumps. The list includes:

> “El sole, la luna, la stella…”

The exact manuscript dating has been debated, commonly around 1470–1500. It remains the earliest important textual list naming the standard trumps.

## Late fifteenth century: Boiardo

Matteo Maria Boiardo designed a poetic card system using passions, virtues, and classical figures. It is not a surviving ordinary Star but demonstrates the courtly practice of composing moral poetry around card sequences.

## c. 1500: *Tarocchi appropriati*

Poems assign individual trumps to named court ladies or gentlemen. They document how cards could be treated as social and poetic allegories without implying cartomancy.

## Early sixteenth century: Folengo

Teofilo Folengo’s literary treatments use tarocchi and card imagery satirically and poetically. They show the pack’s cultural familiarity; they do not establish an occult Star doctrine.

## 1520s–1540s: Pavia/Milan lists

Poetic and legal sources supply order evidence compatible with the western/Milanese family from which the Marseille order descends.

## 1637: Abbé de Marolles

The earliest detailed printed tarot rules in French establish tarot unambiguously as a trick-taking game.

## c. 1650: Noblet and Viéville

The Marseille water-pourer and alternative astronomer lineages coexist in France.

## 1701–1715: Dodal

The eight-star, two-vessel, bird-and-tree composition becomes strongly standardized.

## 1760: Conver

The most influential historical Marseille exemplar is printed at Marseille.

## 1781: Court de Gébelin and de Mellet

*Le Monde primitif*, volume VIII, asserts Egyptian and initiatory origins. The Comte de Mellet supplies a Hebrew-letter theory.

This is the crucial occult reinterpretation of the pack.

## 1783–1785: Etteilla’s manuals

Etteilla produces the first sustained professional system of divination with upright and reversed meanings.

## 1788–1789: Grand Etteilla

The first purpose-designed occult tarot appears.

## 1854–1856: Éliphas Lévi

*Dogme et rituel de la haute magie* makes the tarot–Hebrew alphabet synthesis central to Western occultism.

## 1888: Mathers

S. L. MacGregor Mathers publishes *The Tarot: Its Occult Signification, Use in Fortune-Telling, and Method of Play*.

## 1889: Papus and Wirth

Papus publishes *Le Tarot des Bohémiens*; Wirth publishes his twenty-two occult trumps.

## c. 1888–1900: Golden Dawn

The English occult synthesis fixes Star–Tzaddi–Aquarius–path 28.

## 1904: *Liber AL* I:57

Crowley records the verse “Tzaddi is not the Star,” although he does not immediately arrive at his final Emperor–Star solution.

## 1909–1910: Waite-Smith

Smith’s drawing and Waite’s commentary make the card a worldwide icon of hope, spiritual gifts, and unveiled truth.

## 1916–1930s: Paul Foster Case

Case develops his tarot lessons, later BOTA curriculum, and Jessie Burns Parke deck. He preserves Tzaddi–Aquarius.

## 1938–1943: Harris paints the Thoth Star

## 1944: Crowley’s *Book of Thoth*

The card is Nuit and formally Heh, yet explicitly Aquarian in Crowley’s prose and Harris’s image.

## 1969: full commercial Thoth publication

The deck becomes widely available after Crowley’s death.

## 1969–1973: Calvino

Calvino first publishes the Visconti-Sforza-based *Castle* section in 1969; the combined *Il castello dei destini incrociati* appears in 1973, with a second sequence using Marseille cards.

## Late twentieth century

Psychological, feminist, artistic, and self-development readings increasingly displace prediction as the dominant published use in some English-language tarot cultures.

### Golden Dawn color scales

The exact color depends on which of the four Qabalistic Worlds or color scales is being used. Published Golden Dawn reconstructions commonly give the Aquarian/Tzaddi path violet or sky-violet families, with derivative or contrasting colors in the Queen, Emperor, and Empress scales.

**Caution:** A claim such as “the one correct color of the Star is violet” suppresses the Golden Dawn’s multi-scale system and the substantial differences among reconstructed decks.

### Pips and court-card data requested in the general template

The Star is a major trump, so Book T’s decanic pip and court sub-element data do not apply directly to it.

For orientation:

- Golden Dawn numbered pips 2–10 correspond to thirty-six decans, ten degrees each.
- Court cards combine elemental/sub-elemental character and zodiacal spans.
- The Star itself is not a decan and has no court sub-element.
- Its Golden Dawn title is a major-arcana path title, not a pip’s “Lord of…” title.

# Divinatory meanings by author

Copyright limits prevent reproducing long modern passages. Short quotations are given where the text is public-domain or where a brief excerpt is sufficient.

## Etteilla, 1783–1789

Etteilla’s redesigned pack prevents a clean extraction of “upright Marseille Star XVII” as though he merely added keywords to Conver. His creation cards, celestial cards, and conventional trumps were renumbered and transformed.

What can be securely attributed to his system is:

- upright/reversed orientation carrying different printed keywords;
- astronomical and elemental conditions;
- a predictive vocabulary of stripping away, loss, change, celestial influence, and revelation distributed across cards that later editors sometimes equate with the Star.

**Finding:** A stable quotation giving “Etteilla’s upright and reversed Star XVII” could not be verified in the consulted first-edition material without silently substituting a later Grand Etteilla equivalence table. Such tables disagree by edition.

## Mathers, 1888

Mathers gives the Star meanings centered on:

- hope;
- expectation;
- bright promises;
- prospects and assistance.

Reversed or ill-dignified, the promise may fail, hope may be disappointed, or expectations may not be realized.

**Caution:** Later online lists often merge Mathers with Waite and omit differences in wording. The 1888 book should be cited by its own edition.

## Waite, *Pictorial Key to the Tarot*, 1910

Waite’s published divinatory entry is strikingly ambivalent:

> “Loss, theft, privation, abandonment; another reading says—Hope and bright prospects.”

Reversed:

> “Arrogance, haughtiness, impotence.”

This differs from modern summaries that present the Star as unconditionally positive. Waite’s explanatory chapter is much more exalted than his terse divinatory list: it calls the woman Truth unveiled and the “Great Mother” of Binah communicating influx to what is below.

## Crowley, *Book of Thoth*, 1944

Crowley gives:

- hope;
- faith;
- unexpected help;
- clarity of vision;
- realization of possibilities;
- spiritual insight.

Badly dignified:

- error of judgment;
- dreaminess;
- disappointment.

Unlike Waite’s landscape-centered Truth, Crowley’s meaning arises from Nuit’s inexhaustible outpouring and the dawning current of the Aeon.

## Paul Foster Case, *Oracle of the Tarot* and BOTA lessons

Case reads the card as truth becoming manifest, assistance from friends, renewed hope, and improvement following depression.

A period summary states:

> “It is a card which always intimates that the truth of the matter which interests the Querent is to be made manifest.”

Well dignified:

- a favorable decision;
- hopes renewed;
- conditions improve;
- assistance.

Ill dignified:

- a decision goes against the querent;
- an outcome cannot readily be changed;
- in some contexts, inheritance.

## Rachel Pollack, *Seventy-Eight Degrees of Wisdom* and *Tarot Wisdom*

Pollack emphasizes:

- calm after the Tower;
- openness and vulnerability without shame;
- renewal of life’s waters;
- unconscious and conscious energies flowing together;
- hope grounded in direct experience rather than wishful denial.

Reversed, Pollack allows:

- inability to perceive hope;
- rigidity or discouragement;
- healing that exists but is resisted;
- overexposure or vulnerability without support.

These are modern psychological-spiritual readings, not Renaissance meanings.

## Mary K. Greer

Greer treats reversals contextually rather than as fixed opposites. For the Star, reversed possibilities include:

- blocked or private hope;
- loss of faith;
- self-doubt;
- difficulty receiving inspiration;
- the need to embody or internalize the Star rather than seek external reassurance.

Her work helped popularize reading reversals as shadow, blockage, delay, internalization, or excess rather than simply “bad fortune.”

# Esoteric and symbolic readings

## Christian

### Star of Bethlehem

The Star can naturally evoke Matthew’s star guiding the Magi.

- **[Tradition]** Some early and later cards were read as the Star of Bethlehem.
- **[Iconographic support]** Certain old cards show observers or astrologers looking upward; variants reportedly show three human figures.
- **[Limit]** The Visconti-Sforza card lacks Magi, Christ child, stable, or Bethlehem inscription. Identification as the Nativity star is therefore plausible but unproved.

### Revelation

The great star, living water, tree, and bird invite comparison with Revelation’s river and tree of life.

- **[Scholarly reconstruction]** Biblical visual language may be part of the common cultural reservoir from which the card developed.
- **[Not demonstrated]** No fifteenth-century designer’s text explains the Star as Revelation 22.

### Baptism and grace

The nude body and flowing water have been read as baptismal regeneration or grace poured into creation.

This interpretation becomes especially common after the Marseille design appears. It is a symbolic reading, not an established original title.

## Classical and Renaissance iconography

### Aquarius

Aquarius is an ancient water-pouring constellation.

- **[Plausible iconographic comparison]** The Marseille woman’s two jugs strongly invite an Aquarian comparison.
- **[Chronological caution]** Explicit tarot Star–Aquarius correspondence is securely systematized by the Golden Dawn in the late nineteenth century.
- **[Counterevidence to claims of obvious identity]** The woman is female, kneeling, and pours both onto land and water; standard Aquarius was often a standing male water-bearer. Minchiate represented Aquarius separately.

### Venus

The Visconti woman, star, beauty, and possible erotic emphasis have suggested Venus.

- **[Disputed]** Venus as Morning/Evening Star supplies a convincing association.
- **[Insufficient evidence]** The card has no Venus name, mirror, dove, Cupid, or other decisive attribute.

### Astraea or Urania

The star-bearing woman and the Ferrarese astrologers have been identified with Astraea, goddess of Justice’s celestial return, or Urania, Muse of astronomy.

These are learned iconographic proposals. Neither is firmly captioned on the cards.

### Hope

Christian Hope often looks upward and may be associated with an anchor or celestial sign. Cary-Yale’s surviving Hope makes this comparison important.

- **[Scholarly reconstruction]** The Star may inherit part of Hope’s visual and moral function.
- **[Disputed]** It has not been proved that the Star literally replaced Hope in a single original sequence.

## Kabbalistic

### Lévi–Papus–Wirth line

The Star is treated through the seventeenth letter **Peh** when trumps I–XXI are aligned sequentially with Hebrew letters and the Fool is placed near the end.

This is a nineteenth-century French occult construction.

### Golden Dawn line

The Fool begins with Aleph, producing Star–Tzaddi. Tzaddi is interpreted as fishhook, linking the image with meditation, aspiration, drawing down influence, or catching the divine current.

Path 28 connects:

- Netzach, Venus, desire/emotion;
- Yesod, Moon, imagination/foundation.

The Star becomes a channel by which celestial or archetypal influence enters the imaginal sphere.

### Waite’s Binah

Waite writes that the woman is “in reality the Great Mother in the Kabalistic Sephira Binah.” This does not mean that the Star is simply the path leading to Binah in Golden Dawn geometry; it is a symbolic identification of the figure’s supernal maternal function.

### Crowley’s Heh

Heh means “window” in later occult letter symbolism. Crowley’s Star can therefore be read as a window onto infinite space. But this is downstream interpretation of his exchange, not his only stated reason for making it.

## Astrological

The dominant modern attribution is Aquarius:

- fixed Air;
- traditionally ruled by Saturn;
- modern astrology often adds Uranus;
- humanitarian aspiration, collective circulation, abstraction, and futurity.

**Historical sequence:**

1. water-bearer resemblance exists in early-modern imagery;
2. French occultists add broad astrology;
3. Golden Dawn fixes Tzaddi–Aquarius;
4. Waite, Case, and most modern correspondence charts spread it;
5. Crowley destabilizes the Hebrew letter while retaining Aquarian imagery.

## Hermetic

Water descends from a higher vessel to a lower world. The two jars can be read as:

- above and below;
- spirit and matter;
- volatile and fixed;
- macrocosm and microcosm;
- conscious and unconscious.

The streams do not merely drain away: the land becomes fertile and the pool remains part of a cycle. This supports a Hermetic circulation model.

The first fully systematic Hermetic reading belongs to the nineteenth-century occult revival, especially Lévi, Papus, Wirth, and the Golden Dawn—not to fifteenth-century game documentation.

## Alchemical

Common interpretations include:

- dissolution after the Tower’s calcination;
- purification or ablution;
- circulation of waters;
- celestial dew;
- the star as the completion sign over the work;
- the two vessels as solar/lunar or sulphur/mercury channels.

**Evidence rating:** These are strong analogical readings, particularly in twentieth-century esoteric tarot. A continuous alchemical commentary on the card cannot be traced back to its Milanese origin.

## Thelemic

Crowley’s Star is Nuit in manifestation. The streams are infinite possibility, love, and the milk or nectar of the stars. The heptagram introduces Babalon and sevenfold Venusian symbolism.

This is not simply “the old Star explained.” It is a new Thelemic revelation structured around an inherited tarot pose.

## Jungian and depth-psychological

Modern Jungian tarot frequently treats the sequence Tower → Star as:

1. collapse of a defensive persona;
2. exposure and psychic nakedness;
3. emergence of an organizing image;
4. renewed libido and trust;
5. reintegration of conscious and unconscious life.

The great star can represent the Self as an orienting totality; the smaller stars, differentiated psychic functions; the water, unconscious life; landward streams, embodiment.

**Historical caution:** Jung did not create a canonical card-by-card tarot system. “Jung’s meaning of the Star” is generally the work of later Jungian-oriented authors applying his concepts.

# People and works

## Artists and cardmakers

- **Michelino da Besozzo:** painter of Martiano’s lost precursor pack; no attested Star.
- **Bonifacio Bembo and workshop:** traditional attribution for much of the Visconti-Sforza pack.
- **Francesco Zavattari:** alternative attribution advanced for the Morgan cards.
- **Jean Noblet:** early Marseille-family standardization, Paris c. 1650.
- **Jean Dodal:** Lyon, early eighteenth century.
- **Nicolas Conver:** Marseille, 1760; exceptionally influential pattern.
- **Jean-Baptiste Alliette/Etteilla:** first professional occult tarot system and first purpose-built occult pack.
- **Oswald Wirth:** first influential modern occult artist of the twenty-two trumps.
- **Pamela Colman Smith:** creator of the most widely reproduced modern Star.
- **Jessie Burns Parke:** artist of Case’s BOTA deck.
- **Frieda Harris:** transformed the card into an abstract Thelemic cosmogram.
- **Philippe Camoin and Alejandro Jodorowsky:** modern Marseille reconstruction.

## Poets and literary figures

### Teofilo Folengo

Folengo’s tarocchi references demonstrate the game’s literary visibility in early-sixteenth-century Italy. They are not evidence of Golden Dawn-style meanings.

### W. B. Yeats

Yeats was deeply involved with the Golden Dawn and its system of correspondences. Tarot belongs to the same ritual-symbolic matrix as his gyres, masks, lunar phases, and visionary personae. A specific, foundational Yeats text devoted solely to the Star could not be traced in the consulted sources.

### T. S. Eliot, *The Waste Land*, 1922

Madame Sosostris uses a “wicked pack of cards.” Eliot’s notes admit:

> “I am not familiar with the exact constitution of the Tarot pack.”

The poem invents or adapts cards for literary effect and famously says “I do not find / The Hanged Man.” It is not a reliable historical tarot spread, and the Star is not a central named card.

### Italo Calvino, *Il castello dei destini incrociati*

Calvino uses tarot as a combinatorial narrative machine.

- The first sequence uses Visconti-Sforza cards.
- The later tavern sequence uses a Marseille deck.
- Scholarship notes a desert or yellow-dune background read from the Star, where new plant life appears.

Calvino intentionally creates changing narratives from the same picture rather than enforcing one traditional divinatory meaning.

## Film

### Ingmar Bergman

Tarot-like allegorical figures—Death, the Fool, apocalyptic processions—pervade discussions of Bergman, especially *The Seventh Seal*. A direct, documented quotation of the Star card in Bergman’s production design could not be established. General similarity should not be promoted to a card-specific source claim.

### Alejandro Jodorowsky

Jodorowsky’s cinema repeatedly uses tarot-like initiation, alchemy, planetary ascent, and bodily transformation, especially *The Holy Mountain*. He later became a major Marseille practitioner and co-created the Camoin–Jodorowsky deck.

A shot resembling the Star may be interpreted tarotically, but his published tarot collaboration supplies firmer evidence than resemblance alone.

### Agnès Varda and other filmmakers

Tarot appears explicitly in films such as *Cléo from 5 to 7*. This demonstrates tarot’s twentieth-century cinematic life, but the Death card rather than the Star is generally the pivotal image.

## Painting, sculpture, and architecture

### Niki de Saint Phalle’s Tarot Garden

The strongest architectural example is the Tarot Garden at Garavicchio, Tuscany. The major arcana become monumental sculptures, some inhabitable. It is a modern environmental reinterpretation, not an archaeological survival of a tarot temple.

### Surrealism and contemporary art

Remedios Varo, Salvador Dalí, Leonora Carrington, Betye Saar, Dorothy Iannone, and other modern artists have used tarot structures or closely analogous symbolic figures.

Claims that an earlier painting “quotes the Star” require chronological care:

- a pre-1450 image can be a source or analogy, not a quotation of a later card;
- a post-1909 kneeling pourer with eight stars may quote Waite-Smith;
- generic nude women with vessels are insufficient to establish borrowing.

# Controversies and disputes

## 1. Egyptian origin

Court de Gébelin asserted that the tarot was the remnant of an Egyptian book rescued from the destruction of libraries and disguised as a game.

### Evidence

- No Egyptian tarot cards survive.
- No Egyptian text describes a 78-card tarot pack.
- The early records are Italian and fifteenth-century.
- Egyptian was not deciphered when Gébelin formulated his etymologies.
- Early decks use Christian, civic, and Renaissance Italian social imagery.

**Classification:** **Legend**, historically important because it created occult tarot’s foundational narrative.

## 2. “Book of Thoth”

Etteilla turned Gébelin’s hypothesis into a redesigned divinatory deck. Crowley later deliberately revived the title for a new Thelemic work.

- Etteilla’s Book of Thoth: eighteenth-century French invention presented as restoration.
- Crowley’s Book of Thoth: twentieth-century occult creation that openly reworks tarot.

Neither is material evidence for an ancient Egyptian card book.

## 3. Gypsy/Romani transmission

Papus, Vaillant, and other nineteenth-century authors suggested that “Bohemians” or “Gypsies” carried tarot from Egypt.

Evidence now places Romani origins in South Asia and tarot’s documented emergence in northern Italy. The early Italian records do not associate the game with Romani communities.

**Classification:** **Legend**, strengthened by nineteenth-century racial exoticism and the mistaken old European name “Egyptians” for Romani people.

## 4. Cathar origin

Modern authors have claimed that the Popess, dualism, Grail imagery, or southern-French survival encodes Cathar doctrine.

Problems:

- the earliest documented cards belong to fifteenth-century northern Italian courts, long after organized Cathar suppression;
- no Cathar manuscript describes tarot;
- Marseille-family cards postdate the first Italian evidence.

**Classification:** **Disputed modern legend**, with symbolic parallels but no transmission evidence.

## 5. Templar origin

Claims connect tarot’s twenty-two trumps, Baphomet, Grail, or secret initiations with the Knights Templar.

No Templar inventory or trial record documents tarot, and the order was dissolved more than a century before the first tarot records.

**Classification:** **Legend/modern invention**.

## 6. Secret Kabbalistic alphabet in the original pack

Twenty-two special cards—including twenty-one ranked trumps plus Fool—invite comparison with twenty-two Hebrew letters.

But:

- the Fool was not originally a regularly ranked twenty-second trump;
- early cards do not bear Hebrew letters;
- regional orders disagree;
- Jewish Kabbalistic diagrams do not document tarot use in fifteenth-century Italy;
- the first explicit printed tarot–Hebrew systems appear in 1781 and the nineteenth century.

**Classification:** The correspondence is a powerful **esoteric attribution**, not a documented origin.

## 7. Original Aquarius?

The Marseille pourer resembles Aquarius, but the earliest surviving Star shows a clothed standing woman touching a star and no water.

Therefore:

- **Aquarian comparison:** plausible for the later Marseille composition.
- **Original Star was always Aquarius:** unsupported.
- **Golden Dawn Star–Aquarius:** documented nineteenth-century attribution.

## 8. Waite’s ibis

Waite calls the bird an ibis. Smith drew a dark bird with short visible proportions.

Possibilities:

- Smith stylized an ibis beyond anatomical recognition;
- Waite imposed the identification in prose;
- it derives from the Marseille bird, whose species was never stable;
- the bird is a generic soul/bird of heaven later Egyptianized.

**Finding:** “Ibis” is documented as Waite’s verbal intention, but not visually certain and not an established feature of early Marseille cards.

## 9. Eight stars and astronomy

The one-plus-seven group has been read as:

- Venus plus seven classical planets;
- the Pleiades;
- eight compass points;
- seven chakras plus a crown;
- seven lesser planetary powers around a central divine light.

No early maker’s key proves any of these. “Seven planets around a superior star” is historically plausible; chakras are a much later cross-cultural imposition.

## 10. Crowley’s “Tzaddi is not the Star”

This is one of tarot’s most consequential reinterpretations.

### Documented sequence

1. Golden Dawn: Star = Tzaddi = Aquarius.
2. 1904: *Liber AL* says Tzaddi is not the Star.
3. Crowley struggles with the verse for years.
4. His mature solution exchanges Emperor and Star letter assignments.
5. Harris paints the Star as Aquarian Nuit.
6. *Book of Thoth* calls it Heh yet immediately says it refers to Aquarius.
7. Later charts and interpreters disagree on whether signs, letters, paths, or cards all move together.

### Error to avoid

It is misleading to state simply:

> Thoth Star = Heh = Aries.

That reproduces one tabular consequence but ignores Crowley’s explicit Star chapter and Harris’s Aquarius imagery. The most accurate description is:

> **Thoth XVII is Heh under Crowley’s letter exchange, while Crowley’s prose and Harris’s image explicitly continue to identify the card with Aquarius.**

## 11. “The Star of Babalon”

The seven-pointed star is specifically Thelemic and Harris/Crowley-era. It is not a feature of Visconti or canonical Marseille, whose great star is commonly eight-rayed.

Calling every historical Star “the Star of Babalon” is retrospective.

## 12. Misprints and copies that became traditions

Woodblock printing reverses an image unless the cutter compensates, and makers frequently copied cards from already printed packs. Consequently:

- figures change handedness;
- streams reverse direction;
- Roman numerals and titles acquire unusual spellings;
- details mistaken in one copy can be faithfully repeated by another.

Viéville’s orientations, irregular numeral conventions, and variant titles illustrate transmission rather than a single perfectly preserved archetype.

The exact case for a “misprint” must be demonstrated from block genealogy; visual reversal alone does not prove error.

## 13. “Marseille restoration”

Grimaud, Marteau, Camoin, and Jodorowsky all speak in some sense of recovering tradition. Yet historical Marseille packs disagree.

- **Documented:** comparative restoration can recover lost lines and colors.
- **Disputed:** there was one complete, occultly exact original Marseille code.
- **Modern invention:** assigning fixed psychological meaning to every tiny reconstructed blue leaf or red mark without historical textual support.

## 14. Dummett and the historians’ correction

Michael Dummett, Ronald Decker, Thierry Depaulis, Stuart Kaplan, Helen Farley, and other historians established a chronology based on:

- Italian archival records;
- surviving cards;
- regional rules;
- woodblock genealogy;
- printed occult texts.

Their central correction is not that tarot can have no symbolic meaning. It is that the occult meanings are historically later than the game and must be dated to their actual authors.

Robert M. Place likewise distinguishes Renaissance iconographic sources from later occult claims, though he is more willing than strict game historians to construct an integrated symbolic reading of the trumps.

# Open questions

1. **Where was the standard twenty-one-trump pack invented?**  
   Milan, Ferrara, Bologna, and Florence all possess early evidence. The surviving record does not conclusively identify one workshop or inventor.

2. **When did the Star enter the sequence?**  
   It is present visually by the mid-fifteenth century and textually by the *Sermo*. Whether it belonged to the earliest lost standard pack remains unknown.

3. **What did the Visconti woman represent?**  
   Star personification, Venus, Hope, dynastic aspiration, and celestial Fortune remain live proposals. No caption settles the question.

4. **How did the water-pourer replace the star-holder?**  
   The missing chain between fifteenth-century Italian cards and c. 1650 French packs prevents a definitive reconstruction.

5. **Was Aquarius already intended before the Golden Dawn?**  
   The iconographic analogy is early; explicit, systematic Star–Aquarius attribution is late. More evidence from sixteenth-century emblem books or workshop models might narrow the gap.

6. **What species is the Marseille bird?**  
   Dove, raven, generic bird, phoenix, and ibis have all been proposed. Blocks vary; the early cards do not label it.

7. **Why eight stars?**  
   Planetary, Marian, compass, and cosmological readings are plausible but unproved.

8. **What exactly did Crowley exchange?**  
   His prose, diagrams, and retained zodiacal imagery do not resolve the Emperor–Star permutation as neatly as many modern tables suggest.

9. **Can the earliest Star be securely attributed to Bonifacio Bembo?**  
   Workshop attribution remains debated among Bembo, Zavattari, and related Lombard hands.

10. **Which alleged quotations in buildings and films are intentional?**  
    Without an artist’s statement, production record, preparatory drawing, or distinctive cluster of tarot features, resemblance is not enough.
