### Evidentiary vocabulary used below

- **[Documented fact]**: directly supported by a surviving object, archival entry, contemporary text, or rules.
- **[Scholarly reconstruction]**: an inference made from surviving objects and documents.
- **[Tradition]**: a historically transmitted convention whose point of origin is not completely documented.
- **[Esoteric attribution]**: a deliberately assigned occult correspondence.
- **[Disputed claim]**: proposed but contested identification or historical theory.
- **[Legend]**: a narrative contradicted by, or lacking support in, the documentary record.
- **[Modern invention]**: a recent symbolic construction sometimes presented as ancient.

## The names, numbers, and families

### Italian terminology

**[Documented fact]** The added allegorical cards were originally called *trionfi*, “triumphs.” The game and its packs were called *carte da trionfi* or simply *trionfi* in fifteenth-century records.

**[Documented fact]** The name *tarocchi* appears around the opening years of the sixteenth century and gradually replaces *trionfi* as the ordinary Italian name. Its etymology remains unresolved. No demonstrated Egyptian, Hebrew, Arabic, or Romani derivation exists.

Justice is *Giustizia* or *La Giustizia*. Latinized lists use *Iustitia*. In some Bolognese usage the card is called *Giusta*.

### French terminology

The normal French name is *La Justice*. In Tarot de Marseille packs it is ordinarily VIII.

“Tarot de Marseille” is not a fifteenth-century name. **[Documented fact]** It is a later classificatory and commercial label for a family of designs descended substantially from northern Italian—especially Milanese—models and standardized by French and Swiss cardmakers. Michael Dummett described Marseille tarot as descended from a late-fifteenth-century Milanese design, with features acquired in France.

The Besançon family replaced the Papess and Pope with Juno and Jupiter, respectively, because explicitly papal imagery was objectionable in some Protestant or mixed-confessional markets. Justice itself remained *La Justice* or *Die Gerechtigkeit*. A Jean Jerger pack from about 1800 and Renault packs made at Besançon around 1820–1830 exemplify the pattern. The name “Besançon Tarot” became attached after manufacture shifted to that city; related packs had also been made in Switzerland and southern Germany.

### English, Spanish, and German

- English: Justice.
- Spanish: *La Justicia*.
- German: *Die Gerechtigkeit*; in translated Thoth packs, *Ausgleichung* or a comparable translation of Adjustment may be used.
- Crowley: Adjustment.
- Some modern decks: Balance, Karma, Equity, Accountability, or Right Measure. These are modern interpretive renamings rather than historical titles.

### Numbering

**[Documented fact]** Early hand-painted trumps were normally unnumbered. Their order had to be memorized.

**[Tradition]** In the Milanese/Marseille or “Type C” order, Justice became VIII.

**[Documented fact]** In the Golden Dawn cipher-material scheme, Strength was placed at VIII and Justice at XI so the trumps could follow the zodiacal sequence: Leo followed Cancer, and Libra followed Virgo. Waite used this order in 1909. The Waite–Smith Justice is therefore XI.

**[Documented fact]** Crowley restored Justice/Adjustment to VIII while retaining Libra and Lamed. Thus Thoth rejects the Golden Dawn/Waite numerical rearrangement without rejecting the correspondence.

# Iconography deck by deck

## Pre-tarot visual ancestry

### Classical and medieval Justice

**[Documented fact]** Justice was one of the four cardinal virtues—Prudence, Justice, Fortitude, and Temperance—transmitted through Plato, Cicero, patristic Christianity, and medieval moral theology.

Medieval and Renaissance personifications commonly give Justice:

- scales, for weighing claims;
- a sword, for judgment and enforcement;
- a crown or throne, for legitimate authority;
- an uncovered face and open eyes.

**[Important correction]** Early tarot Justice is not blindfolded. Blindfolded Justice became prominent only in early-modern imagery and initially could signify the abuse or corruption of justice, not impartiality. Claims that the medieval tarot figure was “blind Justice” read later legal iconography backward.

Three of the four cardinal virtues became stable tarot trumps: Justice, Fortitude, and Temperance. Prudence is absent as an unambiguous standard trump.

**[Disputed claims]** Prudence has variously been sought in the Hermit, Hanged Man, World, or a lost card. No surviving standard pack securely labels any of these Prudence.

---

## Marziano da Tortona’s game, circa 1418–1425

Marziano’s *Tractatus de deificatione sedecim heroum*, written for Filippo Maria Visconti, describes a pack with sixteen classical gods arranged under four categories.

**[Documented fact]** Marziano’s pack is a precursor or close relative of tarot’s invention, but it does not contain the standard twenty-one tarot trumps and does not name a Justice card.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** Dummett and other historians treat it as evidence that special permanent-trump packs existed at the Milanese court shortly before ordinary tarot appears. It is not evidence that the standard Justice trump already existed around 1420.

The prompt’s proposed “earliest document that names it—Marziano” therefore requires correction: Marziano does not name the tarot Justice.

---

## Visconti di Modrone / Cary–Yale, Milan, circa 1440s

“Visconti di Modrone” and “Cary–Yale” generally name the same surviving group, now at Yale’s Beinecke Library.

**[Documented fact]** It is an unusual expanded pack with six court ranks in every suit, including female counterparts. Its surviving trumps include theological and cardinal-virtue imagery.

**[Absence of evidence]** No Justice card survives in the extant Cary–Yale group. One cannot securely describe its pose or determine its precise trump rank.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** Because the surviving pack contains Faith, Hope, Charity, Temperance, Fortitude, and related moral imagery, several scholars reconstruct an expanded virtue program that probably included Justice. This remains reconstruction, not a surviving card.

---

## Brambilla Tarot, Milan, circa 1440s

The Brambilla cards are associated with Filippo Maria Visconti and are preserved chiefly at the Pinacoteca di Brera.

**[Documented fact]** Only a small selection survives, mostly suit cards and two trumps.

**[Absence of evidence]** No Justice card survives. Assertions about a Brambilla Justice are therefore reconstructions or confusion with the later Visconti–Sforza card.

---

## Visconti–Sforza / Pierpont Morgan–Bergamo, Milan, circa 1450–1465

This is the earliest surviving tarot Justice securely identifiable.

**[Documented iconography]**

- A crowned woman sits frontally.
- She holds scales in her left hand.
- She raises a sword in her right.
- Above her is an armed knight riding a white horse and carrying a sword.
- Gold ground and courtly dress integrate the figure into the luxury culture of Visconti–Sforza Milan.
- The figure is not blindfolded.

The cards are dispersed principally among the Morgan Library, Accademia Carrara, and private collections. The Morgan attributes its portion probably to Bonifacio Bembo and describes the deck as a fifteenth-century aristocratic game, not an occult book.

### What is the knight?

- **[Documented fact]** A mounted swordsman is visibly present.
- **[Scholarly reconstruction]** He may represent justice put into executive action: judgment below, enforcement above.
- **[Disputed claim]** He has been identified as Francesco Sforza, symbolizing the duke as defender of Milan.
- **[Disputed claim]** He may be the emperor Trajan, whose legendary act of justice was a frequent medieval subject.
- **[Disputed claim]** He has been compared with Saint Michael or an anticipation of the Last Judgment.
- **Evidence:** no caption names him, and the horse’s caparison lacks an unequivocal identifying emblem. Specific identifications must remain tentative.

The inversion sometimes found in modern descriptions—placing the rider “beneath” Justice—results from reproductions, cropping, or mistaken verbal memory. In the surviving card the mounted figure occupies the upper register.

---

## Ferrarese and Este cards, later fifteenth century

Several surviving fragments have been attributed to Ferrara and the Este court, among them the Charles VI, Ercole d’Este, and related groups, though attributions and group boundaries have changed in catalogues.

**[Documented fact]** Ferrarese tarot possessed a distinctive trump order. Justice stood extraordinarily high: immediately below the World and above the Angel in the order reconstructed from Ferrarese lists.

**[Absence of evidence]** Not every named Este group preserves a Justice card. It is therefore unsafe to give one composite “Este Justice” as if a complete Ferrarese archetype survived.

Where Justice survives or can be compared through later northeastern patterns, it uses the conventional sword-and-scales virtue. What is distinctively Ferrarese is less the basic personification than the card’s exalted rank.

---

## Mantegna “Tarocchi,” circa 1460s–1470s

**[Documented fact]** The so-called Mantegna Tarocchi are fifty educational engravings arranged in five decades—social stations, Apollo and the Muses, liberal arts, virtues, and cosmic principles. They are not tarot cards and were not engraved by Andrea Mantegna.

A Justice engraving appears among the moral/cosmological sequence and belongs to the wider visual vocabulary from which tarot drew.

**[Relevance]** It demonstrates that personified virtues circulated in northern Italian prints close to the date and region of early tarot.

**[Disputed claim]** Direct copying in one direction or the other cannot be assumed merely from thematic resemblance.

---

## Rosenwald sheets, probably Florence, circa 1500

The Rosenwald sheets preserve woodcut tarot designs, often dated around the turn of the sixteenth century.

**[Documented fact]** They provide early evidence for a popular printed, rather than court-painted, tarot.

Justice is presented as the recognizable female virtue with sword and scales. The image is simplified for woodcut production. The card belongs to the southern or Florentine/Bolognese family rather than the later Marseille sequence.

**[Dating caution]** “1501” is often repeated as a precise date, but “circa 1500” is safer unless tied to a particular watermark or catalogue determination.

---

## Budapest sheet, late fifteenth or early sixteenth century

The Budapest sheet is another uncut early Italian woodcut sheet.

**[Documented fact]** It confirms that inexpensive repeated-print patterns coexisted with luxury court decks.

Justice follows the conventional allegory. Confusion in popular accounts sometimes substitutes the sheet’s Strength figure—often a man or woman engaging a lion—for Justice. The two should not be conflated.

---

## Sola Busca, 1491 and later painted version

**[Documented fact]** The Sola Busca is the earliest substantially complete surviving tarot and the earliest known pack with fully illustrated narrative pip cards. Its trumps are eccentric classical or pseudo-classical warriors and rulers.

It is highly relevant to Pamela Colman Smith’s pip designs: the British Museum displayed the Sola Busca cards in 1908, and Smith’s Three of Swords and Ten of Wands, among others, show compelling compositional relationships.

**[Absence of direct relevance]** Its Justice-equivalent is not a conventional enthroned Justitia and cannot be treated as the direct source for Smith’s Justice. The demonstrated influence concerns primarily the illustrated minor arcana.

---

## Bolognese tarot / tarocchino

The Bolognese pack was eventually reduced to sixty-two cards. Its trump VIII is commonly called *Giusta* or Justice.

**[Documented iconography]** A woman carries scales and an exceptionally conspicuous sword. Modern rules warn players not to mistake the figure for a sword-suit court card.

The four papal and imperial trumps were converted into four Moors in 1725 after controversy over papal imagery. Justice was not renamed.

In the Bolognese order the virtues occupy a consecutive low or middle group. Justice is not the near-highest card as in Ferrara.

---

## Minchiate, Florence and Tuscany, sixteenth–nineteenth centuries

Minchiate expands the tarot structure to ninety-seven cards, adding the four elements, zodiac signs, and additional virtues.

**[Documented fact]** The game is first documented as *germini* in the early sixteenth century; the term *minchiate* became standard later.

Justice remains in the low numbered sequence, conventionally VII in many Minchiate lists rather than Marseille VIII. She carries sword and scales.

**[Game distinction]** Because Minchiate added many trumps, the original tarot virtues no longer occupy equivalent numerical positions. Comparing “card VIII” across Minchiate and Marseille is therefore misleading.

---

## Sicilian tarot

Tarot entered Sicily probably in the seventeenth century, apparently through Rome. The surviving Sicilian pack is shortened and has distinctive names and designs.

Justice is normally the virtue with sword and scales, but regional numbering and the reduction of the deck distinguish it from Marseille.

The Fool is *Il Fuggitivo* or Sicilian *lu Fujutu*, “the Fugitive,” a regional renaming particularly important to the game. This is unrelated to Justice but illustrates how titles could change while functions persisted.

---

## Jean Noblet, Paris, circa 1650

The Noblet deck is the earliest surviving pack commonly placed in the Tarot de Marseille family, although it was made in Paris.

Justice is VIII:

- seated and frontal;
- sword upright;
- scales held outward;
- crown or headdress;
- throne-back or mantle forms that can look wing-like;
- no blindfold.

Noblet’s compact engraving makes the throne and clothing unusually ambiguous. Claims that Justice literally has angel wings are interpretive; the shapes can be read as stylized throne or drapery.

---

## Jean Dodal, Lyon, circa 1701–1715

Dodal preserves the Type I Marseille pattern:

- *LA JUSTICE*;
- VIII;
- frontal crowned woman;
- raised sword;
- somewhat uneven scales;
- elaborate throne or wing-like back.

Minor asymmetries are products of hand carving, stencil coloring, registration, and copying. Later occultists often turned such workshop irregularities into intentional secret signs.

---

## Nicolas Conver, Marseille, 1760/1761

Conver’s deck became one of the dominant models for later Marseille revivals.

Justice remains VIII and sits rigidly front-facing. She carries a blue or flesh-toned sword and a small balance; her large collar, crown, sleeves, and throne create a nearly bilateral composition.

Compared with Noblet and Dodal, Conver tends toward the later Type II Marseille treatment. The central meaning remains civil and moral authority, weighing followed by execution.

---

## Grimaud and Paul Marteau, Paris, 1930

Paul Marteau issued Grimaud’s *Ancien Tarot de Marseille* in 1930, based substantially on the Conver tradition but standardized and recolored.

**[Documented fact]** This edition did much to define the twentieth-century public idea of “the” Tarot de Marseille.

**[Tradition presented as recovery]** Marteau treated color, number, and small graphic details as an integrated symbolic code. His explanations are historically influential, but they do not demonstrate that every color patch was deliberately encoded by seventeenth-century cardmakers.

Justice remains VIII. Marteau interprets eight as material equilibrium and the frontal pose as full, direct action.

---

## Camoin–Jodorowsky, 1997

Philippe Camoin and Alejandro Jodorowsky digitally compared numerous Marseille-family packs and issued a “restored” Tarot de Marseille.

**[Documented fact]** Their reconstruction used Conver, Dodal, Payen, Tourcaty, Besançon, and other material.

**[Disputed methodological claim]** Marketing and interpretive literature sometimes call the result the recovery of an original, complete, universal tarot code. No single surviving archetype establishes all their restored details.

Justice remains VIII. Jodorowsky reads her asymmetrical scales, knee, throne, and colors as parts of a psychospiritual language of perfection, maternal authority, judgment, and self-evaluation.

---

## Etteilla’s Grand Etteilla, 1788–1789

Jean-Baptiste Alliette, “Etteilla,” produced the first tarot designed expressly for occult divination.

**[Documented fact]** His pack radically reordered, renamed, and Egyptianized the tarot. It is not simply a Marseille deck with meanings added.

The conventional Justice figure appears in the Grand Etteilla sequence as card 9 in influential editions and bears keywords involving Justice or legal/moral right; different Grand Etteilla states and successors complicate exact identification.

**[Caution]** Online catalogues frequently map Etteilla’s cards back onto Marseille titles inconsistently. The correct unit of analysis is the particular edition or state, not “Etteilla” as one fixed image.

Etteilla printed upright and reversed meanings on the cards. This made orientation a built-in textual component of the deck rather than an improvised inversion.

---

## Oswald Wirth, 1889

Wirth designed twenty-two occult trumps for Stanislas de Guaita; Papus reproduced or promoted them in *Le Tarot des Bohémiens*.

Justice is VIII and retains the Marseille woman, sword, scales, throne, and pillars while regularizing them into a deliberate initiatory diagram. Wirth’s later colored editions intensified Masonic, alchemical, astrological, and Hebrew symbolism.

**[Esoteric attribution]** Wirth followed the French occultist sequence associating VIII Justice with Heth rather than the Golden Dawn Lamed. This is a major conflict between French and English occult tarot.

---

## Golden Dawn tarot drafts, 1888 onward

The Golden Dawn’s cipher manuscripts and instructional papers assigned:

- VIII to Strength/Leo/Teth;
- XI to Justice/Libra/Lamed;
- path 22 to Lamed, connecting Geburah and Tiphareth.

**[Documented fact]** The reassignment concerns the cards’ positions, not an exchange of the figures’ moral identities. Justice remains Justice but moves from VIII to XI.

### “Book T”

The title *Book T* is associated chiefly with Golden Dawn tarot papers transmitted in later compilations. It gives elemental, zodiacal, decanic, court, and pip correspondences.

Justice itself is not a pip or court card. Therefore:

- it has no decan-specific pip title;
- it has no court-card sub-element;
- it represents the whole zodiacal sign Libra, not one ten-degree decan.

Libra’s three Golden Dawn decans are represented separately by:

- Two of Swords: Moon in Libra, “Peace Restored”;
- Three of Swords: Saturn in Libra, “Sorrow”;
- Four of Swords: Jupiter in Libra, “Rest from Strife.”

### Wang and Cicero reconstructions

Robert Wang’s *Golden Dawn Tarot* and Sandra Tabatha and Chic Cicero’s *Magical Tarot of the Golden Dawn* are twentieth-century reconstructions based on surviving order papers.

**[Scholarly/initiatory reconstruction]** They are valuable witnesses to published Golden Dawn teaching, but they are not photographic facsimiles of one authoritative original deck. Golden Dawn members were expected to color or construct personal packs.

## Waite–Smith, London, 1909

Arthur Edward Waite conceived the program; Pamela Colman Smith drew the cards; William Rider published them.

Justice is XI:

- a crowned, stern-faced woman;
- seated between two gray pillars;
- a purple veil behind her;
- red robe and green mantle;
- upright double-edged sword in her right hand;
- balanced scales in her left;
- one foot visible below the robe;
- square ornaments on crown and clasp;
- no blindfold.

Smith transforms the busier Marseille throne into an austere, shallow, almost theatrical tribunal. The pillars consciously echo the High Priestess, but Waite says they open “into one world” while the High Priestess’s open into another.

Waite’s own description states:

> “the moral principle which deals unto every man according to his works”

and distinguishes it from the “spiritual justice” of providential election. In the 1911 Rider edition of *The Pictorial Key to the Tarot*, this is on p. 112; pagination differs in later printings.

**Sources for Smith’s image**

- **[Documented]** Marseille and occult French Justice types provide the basic pose and attributes.
- **[Documented/inference]** Golden Dawn doctrine accounts for XI and Libra.
- **[Likely artistic context]** Smith’s theatrical experience helps explain the stage-like pillars and curtain.
- **[Unsupported claim]** No primary document proves that the Justice figure portraits a particular woman.
- **[Overstatement]** The Sola Busca influenced Smith’s illustrated minors, but there is no comparable demonstration that its imagery directly generated her Justice.

---

## Paul Foster Case and BOTA, 1920s–1930s onward

Paul Foster Case retained Justice XI, Lamed, and Libra. Jessie Burns Parke drew the BOTA deck as black outlines intended for students to color according to Case’s system.

Justice closely follows Waite–Smith:

- enthroned woman;
- sword and scales;
- paired curtains;
- crown with circle and square;
- explicitly color-coded green for Libra.

Case calls the law “Undeviating Justice” and treats the key as the center or balance point of the trumps. BOTA’s published summary says:

> “It manifests itself in the form of a Law of Undeviating Justice.”

Case interprets the sword as discrimination, the scales as equilibrium, and the whole as karma/action-reaction. This is a twentieth-century occult synthesis, not an attested medieval tarot doctrine.

---

## Crowley–Harris Thoth Tarot, 1938–1943; published 1969

Crowley commissioned Frieda Harris to paint the deck. The project expanded from a short redesign into years of work. The paintings were made primarily between 1938 and 1943; the full commercial deck appeared posthumously in 1969.

Justice becomes **VIII Adjustment**.

**[Documented iconography]**

- a masked or abstracted female dancer balanced on toe;
- a great downward-pointing sword held with both hands;
- a crystalline, diamond-shaped composition;
- chains or lines of cause suspending the balances;
- Maat’s paired ostrich feathers;
- uraeus serpent;
- Alpha and Omega poised in the scales;
- green-blue Libra colors;
- an equilibrium that is visibly dynamic, not restful.

Crowley writes:

> “She is the goddess Maat; she bears upon her nemyss the ostrich feathers of the Twofold Truth.”

And:

> “Alpha, the first, is poised in perfect equilibrium with Omega, the last.”

In *The Book of Thoth* the card means continuous compensation: action produces reaction, and every disturbance demands a new adjustment.

The sword, balances, dancer, and geometry make the card less a judge imposing a verdict than the universe correcting itself from instant to instant. Crowley preferred “Adjustment” because “Justice” sounded too human, moralistic, or legal for this cosmic process.

Correspondence between Harris and Crowley shows that the design was negotiated. Harris questioned or revised elements including Maat’s plumes and sought to express Venus–Saturn tensions and the proposition “Existence is pure joy.”

### The Tzaddi/Heh issue

Crowley’s famous declaration “Tzaddi is not the Star” exchanges the Hebrew-letter attributions of the Emperor and Star: Emperor receives Tzaddi and Star receives Heh in his final system.

**[Important distinction]** This does not alter Justice/Adjustment. Adjustment remains Lamed/Libra. The controversy belongs to the integrity of Crowley’s tarot sequence generally, not to an Emperor/Justice exchange.

## Notable modern redesigns

### Rachel Pollack and the Shining Tribe Tarot

Pollack shifts Justice toward relational, psychological, and communal truth. Her broader contribution was to frame tarot as an experiential symbolic language rather than either a fixed fortune-telling dictionary or a supposed ancient Egyptian scripture.

### Mary K. Greer

Greer emphasizes accountability, agreements, mediation, consequences, and the reader’s need to examine bias. Her reversal method allows Justice reversed to be opposite, blocked, excessive, delayed, internalized, projected, or consciously resisted.

### Robert M. Place

Place’s *Alchemical Tarot* and historical writing reconnect tarot with Renaissance emblem, virtue, Hermetic, and alchemical traditions while rejecting literal ancient-Egyptian ancestry. His alchemical readings are interpretive reconstructions, not proof that the original designers encoded a single alchemical manual.

### Jodorowsky–Camoin

Their Justice is an intensified Marseille reconstruction. Jodorowsky reads bodily asymmetry and color as psychological information, especially self-judgment, maternal law, rectitude, and perfectionism.

### Feminist, queer, and decolonial redesigns

Many recent decks replace the enthroned European woman with:

- nonbinary adjudicators;
- community councils;
- restorative-justice circles;
- ecological balance;
- reciprocal or indigenous legal concepts;
- protest and institutional critique.

These are **[modern inventions/reinterpretations]**, often purposeful corrections to the assumption that state law and justice are identical.

# The game

## Original function

**[Documented fact]** Tarot was created as a card game in northern Italy. For roughly three centuries, its principal documented use was play. The Morgan explicitly describes the Visconti–Sforza cards as a fifteenth-century aristocratic game and notes that occult and divinatory associations came centuries later.

Justice is a permanent trump. It beats ordinary suit cards, subject to following-suit and trumping rules of the particular game, and loses to higher trumps.

Its number in a printed pack is not necessarily its ordinal rank in every historical order.

## The three principal regional orders

Dummett classified early orders into three families.

| Order | Region | Characteristic place of Justice |
|---|---|---|
| A, Southern | Bologna, Florence; later Rome, Naples, Sicily | The three virtues are grouped relatively low; Justice is not a top trump |
| B, Eastern | Ferrara, Venice | World highest, Justice immediately below it, Angel below Justice |
| C, Western | Milan, Piedmont, France, Switzerland | Justice is VIII in the Marseille lineage; World and Angel occupy the summit |

These orders matter because an unnumbered trump game is impossible unless players share a memorized hierarchy.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** The existence of three stable orders near the earliest centers suggests that regional conventions became fixed very early. It does not reveal which city invented tarot.

## Justice and Strength

The medieval/regional differences are not the same event as Waite’s “swap.”

- In Marseille: Justice VIII, Strength XI.
- In Golden Dawn/Waite: Strength VIII, Justice XI.
- In Thoth: Adjustment VIII, Lust XI, while retaining Libra for Adjustment and Leo for Lust.
- In Ferrarese order: Justice was near the very top.
- In Bolognese/Florentine orders: the virtues formed a grouped block.

## Card value

In most tarot games Justice is simply a trump whose trick-taking power is determined by its rank. It is not generally one of the special high-value counters.

Tarot games commonly give extra counting value to kings, the Fool, and certain extreme trumps—especially I and XXI—rather than to Justice. Exact values vary by game.

In Bolognese Ottocento, Justice is trump 8. The pack contains twenty-one trumps plus the Matto. Justice’s prominent sword can cause visual confusion with a sword court card, but its role is that of a trump.

In Minchiate, Justice is one of forty trumps and is outranked by the numerous added virtues, elements, zodiac signs, and high cosmological cards.

## The Fool

The Fool is historically special:

- ordinarily unnumbered;
- not a normal ranked trump in Italian and French games;
- often played as an “excuse,” allowing its holder to avoid following suit while retaining it, subject to compensation;
- in some Central European tarock games it becomes the highest trump, usually called *Sküs*.

Justice never shares the Fool’s exemption.

# Chronology of attestation

### Circa 1418–1425: Marziano da Tortona

**[Documented fact]** Marziano describes a sixteen-god trump pack for Filippo Maria Visconti.

**Finding:** no Justice trump and no standard tarot sequence occur. Its relevance is developmental, not a direct attestation.

### 1440: Florence

A record of *naibi a trionfi* is commonly cited as the earliest secure documentary reference to tarot/triumph cards.

### 1442: Ferrara ledger

**[Documented fact]** Este court accounts record payments for packs or cards “da trionfi.” This proves that triumph packs were being produced at Ferrara.

**Finding:** the ledger does not provide a complete named trump list and therefore does not individually attest Justice by name.

### 1450s–1460s: Visconti–Sforza Justice

The surviving painted card supplies the earliest secure object-level evidence: enthroned Justice, sword, scales, and mounted knight.

### Circa 1450–1470: anonymous *Sermo de ludo*

The Dominican sermon condemns dice, ordinary cards, and triumphs and supplies a list of twenty-one trumps plus the Fool. *Iusticia* is named.

The list belongs to the Eastern/Ferrarese order and places Justice near the summit.

The dating “circa 1470” is conventional. Scholars have proposed a wider late-fifteenth-century range. The manuscript is therefore an early named attestation, but not securely year-dated.

### 1491: Sola Busca

A dated complete tarot structure with narrative pips, though its trump iconography is not the standard allegorical series.

### Circa 1500: Rosenwald and related sheets

Printed popular tarot confirms dissemination beyond unique court commissions.

### Circa 1500–1520: appropriati and poetry

*Tarocchi appropriati* assign trumps to court figures or subjects in poems. They document familiarity with trump names and personalities, but are literary games, not proof of ancient divination.

### Teofilo Folengo, 1527

Folengo’s *Caos del Triperuno* includes a sonnet sequence constructed from tarot-card names. It is evidence for literary and poetic use and possibly playful prognostication, not a complete occult tarot doctrine.

### Sixteenth century: France and Switzerland

The Italian game crosses the Alps. The Milanese order becomes ancestral to French Marseille-type patterns.

### Circa 1650: Noblet

Earliest surviving standard Marseille-family Justice.

### 1701–1715: Dodal

Stable *La Justice VIII* in the Lyon Type I pattern.

### 1760/1761: Conver

The influential Marseille Type II model.

### Before 1781: cartomancy

Ordinary playing-card divination was practiced before occult tarot theory. Evidence for isolated tarot divination exists in eighteenth-century Italy, including the Bolognese cartomancy sheet, but no fifteenth-century tarot divinatory system has been documented.

### 1781: Court de Gébelin and Comte de Mellet

Court publishes the Egyptian Book-of-Thoth hypothesis in volume VIII of *Le Monde primitif*. Mellet supplies an individual Hebrew-letter scheme and a tarot divination method.

### 1783–1789: Etteilla

Etteilla publishes systematic tarot cartomancy and issues the first occult/divinatory tarot pack.

### 1854–1856: Éliphas Lévi

*Dogme et rituel de la haute magie* makes tarot a central key to ceremonial magic and Christianized Kabbalah.

### 1863–1870: Paul Christian

Jean-Baptiste Pitois, writing as Paul Christian, elaborates Egyptian temple initiation and popularizes the language of “arcana.”

### 1888: Mathers

S. L. MacGregor Mathers publishes *The Tarot: Its Occult Signification, Use in Fortune-Telling, and Method of Play*.

### 1888–1889: Golden Dawn and Wirth/Papus

The Golden Dawn system assigns Justice XI/Lamed/Libra. Wirth and Papus retain French Justice VIII/Heth/Cancer in a different cabalistic system.

### 1909–1911: Waite–Smith

Deck published in 1909; illustrated *Pictorial Key* dated 1911 after the earlier 1909 *Key to the Tarot*.

### 1920s–1930s: Case/BOTA

Justice XI becomes a diagram of karma, action-reaction, and “undeviating justice.”

### 1938–1943; 1969: Thoth

Harris paints Adjustment; full deck published in 1969.

### 1970s onward

Pollack and other writers spread psychological, Jungian, feminist, and self-development readings.

### 1997

Camoin and Jodorowsky issue their reconstructed Marseille tarot.

## Primary conflicts

### Heth/Cancer versus Lamed/Libra

Papus says:

> “Hence the idea of balancing power, and consequently of Justice attributed to this letter.”

But the letter in his scheme is Heth, and he explicitly assigns it Cancer. This occurs in *The Tarot of the Bohemians*, French 1889; A. P. Morton translation, pp. 138–140 in commonly scanned editions.

The Golden Dawn instead gives Justice Lamed and Libra. Neither correspondence is medieval. Both are nineteenth-century constructions.

### VIII versus XI

- Marseille, Papus, Wirth, Crowley: VIII.
- Golden Dawn, Waite, Case: XI.

Crowley’s VIII demonstrates that Lamed/Libra does not logically require XI; the number depends on whether one preserves Marseille positions or the cipher-manuscript zodiacal sequence.

### Gébelin and Mellet

Court de Gébelin did not supply the same detailed card-by-card Hebrew system later standardized by the Golden Dawn. Mellet’s letter order is different. Later summaries often merge the two essays into one doctrine.

### Decans and courts

Justice itself has neither a decan title nor a court-card sub-element in Book T. Those belong to minor cards:

- Libra decans: Two, Three, and Four of Swords.
- Court cards span zodiacal thirty-degree arcs and combine two elements; none is “Justice’s court equivalent.”

# Divinatory meanings by author

Copyright restrictions prevent reproducing long passages from Pollack and Greer, but their positions can be reported with short quotations or close paraphrase.

## Etteilla

**Date:** 1780s; exact keywords vary among original and derivative Grand Etteilla states.

For the card mapped as Justice in widely circulated Grand Etteilla editions:

- Upright: justice, equity, probity, right, rectitude, reason, execution.
- Reversed: law, legal business, judgment, or complication, depending on edition.

**Caution:** Many online keyword lists silently combine Etteilla, Julia Orsini, Papus’s *Tarot Divinatoire*, and later publishers. A single definitive pair cannot safely be attributed to every 1789 state.

## Mathers, 1888

In *The Tarot: Its Occult Signification*:

- Upright: equity, rightness, probity, executive action, and success of the deserving side in law.
- Reversed: law in its departments, legal complications, bigotry, bias, or excessive severity.

This wording passed almost intact into Waite.

## Waite, 1910/1911

Waite’s divinatory list reads:

> “Equity, rightness, probity, executive; triumph of the deserving side in law.”

Reversed:

> “Law in all its departments, legal complications, bigotry, bias, excessive severity.”

*Pictorial Key*, Part III; p. 285 in the first Rider pagination reproduced by Wikisource, though pagination varies.

Waite’s symbolic discussion is subtler: ordinary moral recompense is not identical with unknowable providential election.

## Crowley, 1944

Crowley does not encourage a simple moral good/bad opposition. Adjustment means balance, suspension of action pending decision, lawsuits, agreements, and exact compensation.

His central formulation is that all acts disturb equilibrium and thereby compel adjustment. Ill-dignified, the same quality becomes overly legalistic, delayed, rigid, or entangled in adjudication.

Crowley generally used elemental dignity rather than mechanically reversing cards. A “reversed Crowley meaning” is therefore usually a later publishing convention, not his preferred method.

## Paul Foster Case

Case interprets Justice as:

- equilibrium;
- the law of cause and effect;
- karma;
- discrimination;
- accurate weighing of evidence;
- practical skill in directing life.

Case and BOTA generally emphasize meditation and correspondence more than fixed reversed meanings. A simple “BOTA reversed Justice” list is not securely primary.

## Rachel Pollack

In *Seventy-Eight Degrees of Wisdom*:

- Upright: taking responsibility for the past, recognizing that present conditions arise partly from previous choices, clear decision, truth, and balance.
- Shadow/reversal: dishonesty with oneself, evasion of responsibility, unfair judgment, or inability to understand the causes shaping the present.

Pollack’s important shift is psychological agency: Justice is not merely an external court handing down fate.

## Mary K. Greer

In *The Complete Book of Tarot Reversals*, pp. 67–69:

- Upright: being called to account; decisions, contracts, negotiations, settlement, mediation, litigation, or demanding/facilitating justice.
- Reversed: “not fair,” legal loss or complication, resistance to consequences, shame, excessive criticism, rationalization, indecision, bias, or poetic justice.

Greer also allows the reversal to indicate accepting mistakes with humility or challenging obsolete rules. The reversal is contextual rather than automatically negative.

# Esoteric and symbolic readings

## Christian moral reading

**First secure layer:** fifteenth-century image and trump lists.

Justice belongs to the cardinal virtues required for righteous government and personal conduct. Court de Gébelin still preserves this political-moral meaning when he writes that states require “Justice envers tous”—justice toward everyone.

The sword may enact Romans 13’s image of lawful authority bearing the sword, while the scales evoke judgment and equitable measure. Such biblical associations are culturally plausible but are not written on the early card.

## Civic and political reading

The Visconti–Sforza rider suggests the movement from judgment to enforcement.

- **[Plausible reconstruction]** scales deliberate; sword and knight execute.
- **[Disputed]** the rider glorifies Francesco Sforza specifically.
- **[Disputed]** the rider is Trajan’s legendary justice.

Justice’s exceptionally high Ferrarese rank may reflect the virtue’s sovereign importance, but no contemporary text explains why the Este order placed it below the World.

## Hermetic and Kabbalistic reading

This begins securely in the late eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, not in surviving Renaissance explanations.

- Mellet: trumps and Hebrew letters.
- Lévi: tarot as a universal alphabet of occult doctrine.
- Papus: VIII/Heth/Cancer and equilibrium.
- Golden Dawn: XI/Lamed/Libra/path 22.
- Case: karma, education, ox-goad, action-reaction.
- Crowley: Adjustment as the compensation inherent in existence.

The “ox-goad” is the conventional literal gloss of Lamed. Case connects it to teaching and disciplined direction; Crowley makes adjustment the goad that continually corrects movement.

## Astrological reading

Libra’s scales made the Justice–Libra identification visually compelling. Yet:

- **[Documented absence]** no fifteenth- or seventeenth-century card labels Justice Libra.
- **[Esoteric attribution]** the Golden Dawn system made the identification canonical in English-language occult tarot.
- Venus, ruler of Libra, supplies harmony and aesthetic proportion.
- Saturn, exalted in Libra, supplies law, constraint, exact judgment, and consequence.

Crowley’s image intentionally holds Venusian grace and Saturnian rigor together.

## Egyptian and Maat reading

The Maat connection is visually and conceptually apt: Maat represents truth, justice, order, and the weighing of the heart.

- **[No historical genealogy]** the early Italian Justice card has not been shown to descend from Egyptian psychostasia.
- **[Eighteenth-century mythic framework]** Gébelin declared tarot Egyptian without evidence.
- **[Twentieth-century explicit attribution]** Crowley and Harris consciously made Adjustment Maat, including ostrich feathers, uraeus, and cosmic scales.

Thus “Justice is Maat” is true of Thoth by design, not of the fifteenth-century card by demonstrated descent.

## Alchemical reading

Justice has been read as:

- balance of opposites;
- exact proportion in the work;
- separation by the sword;
- weighing and purification of substances;
- equilibrium between sulfur and mercury or fixed and volatile.

These are later Hermetic/alchemical applications. No surviving fifteenth-century workshop record identifies the card as an alchemical operation.

Wirth, Case, Crowley, and modern authors such as Place spread alchemical readings, but not always the same one.

## Jungian reading

Modern Jungian tarot treats Justice as:

- the ego’s ethical function;
- conscious discrimination;
- acknowledgment of shadow projections;
- integration of opposites;
- accepting consequences;
- differentiation between internal conscience and external law.

**[Modern intellectual genealogy]** Jung wrote about archetypes, synchronicity, alchemy, and symbolic images but did not create a canonical card-by-card tarot system. “Jung’s meaning of Justice” is normally the work of later writers applying Jungian concepts.

The popular “Fool’s Journey” is likewise a twentieth-century pedagogical narrative, not an attested medieval sequence.

## Literature

### Folengo

Folengo’s 1527 tarot sonnets document literary play with card names. They do not provide a sustained esoteric exegesis of Justice.

### Italo Calvino, *The Castle of Crossed Destinies*

Calvino uses tarot images as a combinatorial language through which mute travelers narrate stories. Different editions and sections use Visconti and Marseille imagery.

Justice can function grammatically—as judge, judgment, balance, punishment, or legal authority—rather than carry one fixed meaning. The book is a major literary afterlife of tarot, not evidence for its origins.

### T. S. Eliot, *The Waste Land*

Madame Sosostris names the drowned Phoenician Sailor, Belladonna, the man with three staves, the Wheel, and “the one-eyed merchant,” while noting a blank card.

**Finding:** Justice is not named. Claims that Eliot supplies a literary interpretation of Justice cannot be traced to the poem.

### W. B. Yeats

Yeats participated in Golden Dawn occult culture and knew its symbolic systems. Tarot imagery is part of his intellectual environment.

**Finding:** No canonical major poem gives Justice an isolated, explicit treatment comparable to Crowley’s *Book of Thoth*. Broad claims that Yeats “used the Justice card” should be tied to a specific manuscript or passage.

## Film

### Ingmar Bergman, *The Seventh Seal*

The chess game with Death resembles medieval *memento mori* and tarot Death imagery.

**Finding:** the film does not provide a documented quotation of the Justice card. Its inclusion in generalized “tarot in cinema” lists is associative.

### Alejandro Jodorowsky

Tarot permeates Jodorowsky’s films, comics, psychomagic, and public teaching. His cinema uses initiation, bodily symbolism, sacrifice, and archetypal figures, while his explicit Justice doctrine is most accessible in *The Way of Tarot*, written with Marianne Costa.

A claim that one particular film scene directly reproduces Justice requires shot-level evidence; resemblance to scales, judges, or enthroned women is insufficient.

## Paintings and buildings

Justice with sword and scales appears extensively in civic architecture, courtrooms, town halls, manuscripts, and Renaissance painting. Important antecedent contexts include:

- Giotto’s Justice in the Arena Chapel;
- civic virtue cycles in Italian communes;
- Ambrogio Lorenzetti’s *Allegory of Good Government*;
- later courthouse statues of Justitia.

These establish a common iconographic environment. They do not prove that a tarot artist copied a particular fresco.

A building containing a woman with scales is therefore better described as sharing the allegorical tradition than “quoting the tarot,” unless documentary evidence or exact compositional borrowing exists.

# Controversies and disputes

## Egyptian origin

Court de Gébelin claimed in 1781 that tarot preserved an Egyptian book of wisdom and proposed fanciful Egyptian etymologies for *tarot*.

**Classification:** **[Legend/esoteric attribution]**.

**Evidence against:**

- standard tarot appears in fifteenth-century northern Italian documents;
- its institutions—Pope, Emperor, cardinal virtues, Christian Last Judgment—belong directly to medieval European culture;
- no ancient Egyptian tarot pack, textual description, or transmission chain survives;
- Gébelin wrote before Champollion’s decipherment;
- his purported Egyptian word derivations do not cite hieroglyphic forms and are linguistically spurious.

Dummett’s correction is not merely “fact-checking”: it rests on dated Este accounts, surviving Italian cards, rules, names, tax records, and the absence of any earlier Egyptian witness.

## Romani or “Gypsy” transmission

Nineteenth-century authors increasingly claimed that Romani people carried tarot from Egypt into Europe. Jean-Alexandre Vaillant was especially influential, and Papus’s title *Tarot of the Bohemians* helped perpetuate it.

**Classification:** **[Legend]**.

Romani migration history points to South Asian origins, not descent from Egyptian priesthood. Moreover, European tarot is documented in Italian elite and urban cardmaking contexts before any evidence connects Romani fortune-tellers with tarot.

Romani practitioners later did use cards and participate in European divinatory culture. That later practice does not establish invention or ancient transmission.

## Cathar origin

Modern writers have proposed that the trumps encode Cathar dualism, persecution, or a secret initiatory gospel.

**Classification:** **[Disputed modern theory]**.

No Cathar tarot, Cathar textual explanation of the trumps, continuous chain of custody, or fifteenth-century accusation identifying tarot as Cathar has been produced. The theory typically works by matching ambiguous images retrospectively.

The chronological gap is also substantial: organized Catharism had been suppressed well before the first documented tarot packs.

## Knights Templar

Claims that the Templars preserved tarot symbols from Jerusalem or transmitted secret Egyptian wisdom are variations of later occult genealogy.

**Classification:** **[Legend]**.

No Templar inventory, rule, trial record, manuscript, or artifact describes tarot. The order was dissolved in the early fourteenth century, roughly a century before documented *trionfi* packs.

## Kabbalistic origin

The numerical coincidence of twenty-two trumps including the Fool and twenty-two Hebrew letters encouraged correspondence.

**Classification:** medieval origin claim **[disputed/unsupported]**; later correspondence **[documented esoteric attribution]**.

The first secure printed Hebrew-letter schemes are eighteenth-century French occult constructions. Their mutual contradictions—Mellet, Lévi/Papus, Golden Dawn, Crowley—are evidence of creative assignment rather than recovery of one continuously transmitted original table.

## “The Golden Dawn invented the swap”

Mostly true but needs precision.

The VIII/XI rearrangement appears in the cipher manuscripts underlying Golden Dawn teaching. Waite made it public through the deck while saying only that the alteration was made “for reasons which satisfy myself.”

**[Documented fact]** It was designed to preserve a zodiacal succession with Strength/Leo and Justice/Libra.

**[Caution]** “Swap” can falsely imply that all earlier European orders were identical. Early Italy already had several virtue orders. What the Golden Dawn reversed was the familiar Marseille VIII Justice/XI Strength arrangement.

## Crowley “restored” the original number

Crowley made Adjustment VIII and Lust XI.

- **[Documented]** This matches Marseille numbering.
- **[Not a historical restoration]** His titles, Maat imagery, Thelemic interpretation, and continued Lamed/Libra correspondence are modern occult synthesis.
- Crowley restored a number, not a lost fifteenth-century esoteric doctrine.

## Blindfold

Many modern descriptions say Justice is “usually blindfolded.”

**[Incorrect for the principal tarot lineage]** Visconti–Sforza, Marseille, Waite–Smith, BOTA, and Thoth Justice are not conventionally blindfolded in the familiar legal-statue sense.

Modern artists sometimes add a blindfold to express impartiality. That is a redesign.

## Uneven scales

Marseille scales are often visibly uneven.

Possible explanations:

- limitations or quirks of the woodblock;
- damage or imperfect copying;
- intentional depiction of active weighing;
- symbolic imbalance requiring correction.

Only the first two require no hidden program. Claims that a particular tilt encodes a secret doctrine need supporting comparative or textual evidence.

## Rider in the Visconti–Sforza card

The rider has been called:

- Francesco Sforza;
- Trajan;
- Saint Michael;
- an executioner;
- abstract executive force.

No inscription decides the issue. “Justice enforced” is the least specific and best-supported functional reading; proper-name identifications remain disputed.

## Misprints becoming traditions

Tarot copying frequently fossilized workshop accidents:

- reversed woodblocks;
- malformed letters;
- dropped lines;
- asymmetric scales;
- unusual hand positions;
- corrupted titles.

Occult commentators then interpreted these as intentional ciphers. Each alleged secret detail must be checked across earlier exemplars. Repetition proves transmission of a form, not necessarily intentional symbolic authorship.

The best-known tarot-wide example is *La Maison Dieu*, later interpreted literally as “House of God,” “House of the Devil,” hospital, temple, or lightning-struck Tower. The title’s evolution is relevant as a warning but does not establish a similar corruption in *La Justice*.

## Camoin–Jodorowsky “restoration”

Their edition is a serious comparative creative reconstruction.

**Documented:** they consulted multiple historic decks.

**Disputed:** the proposition that the resulting colors and micro-details recover one primordial, complete, mathematically encoded tarot.

Because no original master woodblock or complete archetype survives, restoration necessarily includes editorial judgment.

# Open questions

1. **Where was standard tarot invented?**  
   Milan, Ferrara, and Bologna all have early evidence. Dummett at different times regarded Ferrara or Milan as plausible; the record does not conclusively decide.

2. **What did the Visconti–Sforza rider mean?**  
   Justice-in-action is plausible; Trajan, Saint Michael, and Francesco Sforza remain unproved.

3. **What was Justice’s exact rank in the earliest Milanese pack?**  
   The cards are unnumbered. Its familiar VIII is reconstructed from later Type C orders, not printed on the surviving card.

4. **Did the Cary–Yale pack contain Justice?**  
   Probably, if its virtue series was systematic, but the card is lost.

5. **Why was Justice nearly supreme in Ferrara?**  
   No contemporary explanation survives.

6. **Was there fifteenth-century symbolic play beyond the obvious cardinal virtue?**  
   Almost certainly viewers brought Christian, civic, legal, and literary associations to the image. No surviving manual proves a detailed initiatory code.

7. **How early was tarot used in divination?**  
   Eighteenth-century evidence is secure; earlier isolated use is possible. No complete fifteenth- or sixteenth-century tarot divination system has been found.

8. **Which Grand Etteilla state should be treated as authoritative?**  
   Surviving editions and successors differ. A full answer requires card-by-card collation of dated impressions.

9. **Did Pamela Colman Smith leave an explanation of her Justice?**  
   No detailed artist’s commentary comparable to Waite’s text has been traced.

10. **How much of “Book T” represents original Golden Dawn papers?**  
    Published forms derive from manuscripts, copies, and later editorial arrangements. Wang and Cicero are reconstructions, not identical primary artifacts.

11. **Can Jungian Justice be attributed to Jung?**  
    Not without a specific text. It is chiefly a post-Jungian application.

12. **Where are the alleged Justice quotations in Eliot, Yeats, Bergman, and specific buildings?**  
    Eliot’s named tarot passage omits Justice; no securely traceable direct Justice quotation was found in Bergman. Yeats requires manuscript-specific investigation. Shared Justitia imagery alone does not demonstrate tarot quotation.
