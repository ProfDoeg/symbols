# The Fool: Research Dossier

## Method and evidence labels

This dossier distinguishes six kinds of statement:

- **[Documented fact]** directly supported by a surviving card, archival document, contemporary printed text, manuscript, or institutional catalogue.
- **[Scholarly reconstruction]** an inference accepted or seriously argued by modern card historians but not stated by a contemporary source.
- **[Tradition]** a conventional practice or interpretation transmitted within gaming, cartomantic, or occult communities.
- **[Esoteric attribution]** a deliberately constructed occult correspondence, not a claim established by historical evidence.
- **[Disputed claim]** an interpretation for which competent scholars or primary evidence point in different directions.
- **[Legend / modern invention]** a retrospective origin story or symbolism for which no appropriate early evidence has been found.

Dates normally refer to production or first publication. Dates printed on later editions are identified where possible.

## Basic identification

| Field | Identification |
|---|---|
| Standard English name | **The Fool** |
| Italian | **Il Matto**, occasionally *Matto*; the pack’s special cards were first called *carte da trionfi*, later *tarocchi* |
| French | **Le Mat**, also **Le Fou**; in the game, **l’Excuse** |
| Spanish | **El Loco**, sometimes **El Tonto** in explanatory literature |
| German | **der Narr**; in Central-European play **Sküs/Skys**, historically also *Stieß/Gstieß* |
| Number | Originally **unnumbered**; later **0**, **XXII/22**, or—in Etteilla—**78** |
| Marseille status | Unnumbered special card, conventionally printed beside the twenty-one numbered trumps but technically without trump rank |
| Golden Dawn title | **The Spirit of Aether**—normally expanded as “The Spirit of Aether” or “The Spirit of Air” in derivative decks |
| Golden Dawn attribution | **Aleph (א); Air; Path 11, joining Kether to Chokmah** |
| Thoth title | **The Fool**, Atu 0 |
| Earliest securely surviving example | The painted Fool in the Visconti-Sforza group, Milan or Cremona, approximately **1450s**; the Morgan currently dates the principal group about **1456–58**, while its collection record retains the wider range ca. 1450–1480 |
| Earlier possible deck | The Visconti di Modrone/Cary-Yale pack is probably 1441–44, but **its Fool does not survive** |
| Earliest named documentary ancestor | Marziano da Tortona’s pack for Filippo Maria Visconti, usually dated ca. 1412–25, describes sixteen gods and four suits of birds; it is an important precursor but **does not contain a card called the Fool** |
| Earliest secure documentary term for the new game | Ferrarese court accounts of **1442**, recording *carte da trionfi* |
| Earliest surviving full trump list | Anonymous Dominican *Sermones de ludo cum aliis*, generally dated ca. **1470–80**, ending with “El matto” outside the numbered moral hierarchy |

The Morgan describes its Fool as a male figure “with feathers in his hair, wearing rags,” holding a stick. The surviving Visconti-Sforza cards have neither printed numbers nor titles. The museum accordingly treats the Fool as a special or “wild” card rather than the twenty-second ranked trump. [Morgan Library collection record](https://www.themorgan.org/manuscript/158525), [Morgan Fool image record](https://ica.themorgan.org/manuscript/page/15/158525).

A terminology caution is essential:

- **[Documented fact]** *Trionfi* is the earliest name for the game and its added picture cards.
- **[Documented fact]** *Tarocchi* appears only around the opening of the sixteenth century.
- **[Scholarly reconstruction]** The unexplained change probably distinguished the card game from other games and spectacles called *trionfi*.
- **[Legend]** Derivations from Egyptian *tar* + *ro*, Hebrew *Torah*, the Taro River, or an anagrammatic mystical formula are not supported by the early documents.

# Iconography deck by deck

## Precursors and fifteenth-century Italy

### Marziano da Tortona / Michelino da Besozzo, Milan, ca. 1412–25

**[Documented fact]** Marziano’s surviving explanatory text describes a bespoke pack devised for Duke Filippo Maria Visconti: sixteen classical gods arranged under four principles and four suits of birds. The cards themselves are lost.

**Finding:** there is no Fool, Matto, or equivalent wandering outcast in Marziano’s description. Calling this “the first tarot” is therefore a **scholarly shorthand or disputed classification**, not evidence for an early Fool. It demonstrates the prehistory of adding permanent superior picture cards to a suited pack, not the complete standard tarot sequence.

### Visconti di Modrone / Cary-Yale, Milan, probably 1441–44

**[Documented fact]** This surviving luxury pack has an unusual structure, including six court cards per suit and extra virtue cards. Yale and the Morgan’s 2026 exhibition identify it with early-1440s Milanese production.

**Finding:** no Fool survives. Any reconstructed Cary-Yale Fool is a **modern replacement**, usually extrapolated from the later Visconti-Sforza figure. It cannot be cited as evidence for what the lost original showed.

“Cary-Yale” and “Visconti di Modrone” generally denote the same surviving group, not two independent decks.

### Brambilla-Brera pack, Milan, before 1447

**[Documented fact]** The Brambilla cards are associated with Filippo Maria Visconti and preserved in the Pinacoteca di Brera.

**Finding:** its Fool is not among the surviving cards. Claims that it depicted the familiar feathered beggar are **reconstruction from the Visconti-Sforza**, not direct observation.

### Visconti-Sforza, Milan/Cremona, ca. 1450s

**What survives:** a barefoot, ragged male stands against a gold ground. His hose or trousers are torn; feathers project from his hair. He carries a long club or staff over his shoulder. His expression is vacant or socially exposed rather than merrily comic. There is no dog, cliff, white rose, numbered zero, bag on a stick, or recognizable court-jester costume.

**[Documented fact]** The Morgan’s 35 cards, 26 cards in Bergamo’s Accademia Carrara, and 13 in the Colleoni collection make 74 surviving cards from a presumed 78-card pack. The Morgan Fool is MS M.630.15. The museum attributes most originals to the Bembo workshop, while recording competing attributions to Francesco Zavattari and debates surrounding the six stylistically different cards. [Morgan catalogue](https://www.themorgan.org/manuscript/158525).

**Interpretive context:**

- **[Scholarly reconstruction]** The feathers may evoke the disorderly dress, mental vacancy, or marginal status assigned to fools, beggars, madmen, and vagrants in late-medieval imagery.
- **[Disputed]** They are sometimes identified as literal fool’s feathers, a parody halo, or an emblem of mental lightness. No caption on the card decides among these.
- **[Modern invention]** Reading the feathers as chakras, kundalini, or a Golden Dawn-style crown of spirit projects much later systems onto the card.

The Morgan’s exhibition interprets the figure conservatively as representing “marginality and unpredictability,” within familiar Renaissance social and moral imagery, not a hidden initiatory code. [Morgan trump-card essay](https://www.themorgan.org/exhibitions/online/tarot/4).

### Ferrarese and Este cards, later fifteenth century

The surviving Este-related fragments include distinctive aristocratic, classical, and heraldic imagery, but no securely identified complete Fool comparable to the Morgan card.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** Ferrara was one of tarot’s formative centres: its court ledgers contain the 1442 *trionfi* purchases, and Ferrarese packs helped establish what Dummett classified as a separate “B” ordering of the trumps.

**Caution:** “Charles VI,” “Gringonneur,” “Este,” and “Ferrarese” have often been used loosely in older catalogues. The so-called Charles VI tarot is Italian and fifteenth-century, not a deck painted for Charles VI by Jacquemin Gringonneur in 1392. That identification is an antiquarian **misattribution**.

### Mantegna “Tarocchi,” Ferrara or northern Italy, ca. 1460s–70s

These are fifty didactic engravings in five groups of ten, not a playable tarot pack. The lowest Condition of Man, **Misero**—a ragged beggar leaning on a staff—is iconographically close to the Visconti Fool.

**[Documented fact]** The British Museum explicitly compares Misero with the Visconti-Sforza *Matto*. [British Museum record](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/P_1895-0915-1).

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** Both images draw upon the broader late-medieval visual category of the destitute outsider. This does not prove that one was copied directly from the other.

**[Documented correction]** The series was neither designed by Andrea Mantegna nor ordinarily used to play tarot. Its conventional title is historically misleading.

### Sola Busca, probably Ferrara/Venice, 1491

The Sola Busca is the earliest substantially complete engraved tarot and famously gives narrative scenes to the suit cards, making it important to the later Waite-Smith minors.

**Finding specific to the Fool:** the deck does not supply the primary visual source for Smith’s cliff-walker in the way its illustrated pip cards supplied models for several Waite-Smith minors. Claims that Smith simply copied a Sola Busca Fool are **unsupported**.

Its Fool belongs to the deck’s pseudo-antique martial world rather than the standardized Marseille vagabond. The Sola Busca’s greatest demonstrable influence on Smith is in cards such as the Three of Swords and Ten of Wands, not the central conception of Trump 0.

### Rosenwald sheets, Italy, ca. 1500

The surviving uncut Rosenwald woodcut sheets are fundamental evidence for an early popular, non-luxury pattern.

**Finding:** the known trump sheet contains the numbered trumps but **not the Fool**. A modern “Rosenwald Fool” supplied in reconstructed packs is conjectural. [Rosenwald reconstruction notice](https://tarot-heritage.com/2017/10/31/the-rosenwald-deck/).

### Budapest sheets, late fifteenth or early sixteenth century

Fragmentary printed sheets preserve portions of another early Italian pattern.

**Finding:** identification of individual fragmentary figures and their relation to later Marseille designs remains partly disputed. Modern restored decks inevitably fill gaps. A brightly coloured, complete “Budapest Fool” should not automatically be treated as a surviving original.

## Regional Italian patterns

### Bolognese tarocchino

The Bolognese pack was reduced to sixty-two cards by dropping low pip cards. Its Fool is **Il Matto**, a special unnumbered card.

**In play:** Matto and the lowest trump, the Bégato, are *contatori*—cards that can help complete scoring combinations. The Fool excuses its holder from following suit but is also vulnerable under special circumstances. The surviving rules are later than the pack’s fifteenth-century origins.

Bologna suppressed distinct numbering or ranking among several low trumps: the four *papi* were traditionally equal. This is one reason a single universal early trump order cannot be assumed.

### Florentine Minchiate

The 97-card Minchiate pack contains forty numbered trumps, fifty-six suit cards, and an unnumbered **Matto**.

The seventeenth-century form shows a ragged or comic Fool related to central-Italian patterns. In some examples his genitals or buttocks are exposed, reinforcing social shamelessness and bodily disorder rather than courtly wit.

**In play:** Matto is worth five card points and participates in *versicole*, or scoring combinations. A British Museum pack made around 1675 includes *Il Matto*. [British Museum Minchiate record](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/P_1876-0510-1009-1073). Detailed reconstructed rules and card values are summarized by John McLeod from historical rulebooks. [Pagat Minchiate](https://www.pagat.com/tarot/minchiate.html).

### Sicilian tarot

The Sicilian pattern, established after tarot reached Sicily in the seventeenth century, radically renamed and redesigned several trumps. The Fool remained a special card, often called **Il Fuggitivo** (“the Fugitive”) rather than merely Matto.

**[Tradition]** Its fleeing or wandering character makes the name more literal than the Marseille *Mat*.

**In play:** it normally excuses the player from following suit. The rules contain unusual exceptions, especially around a soloist attempting to take every trick. [Pagat Sicilian Tarot](https://www.pagat.com/tarot/sicilian.html).

## Tarot de Marseille lineage

### General Marseille type

The standardized image shows a bearded or stubbled traveler moving to the viewer’s right. He carries a bundle on a staff or spoon-like pole over one shoulder. His clothes are torn; bells may hang from his hood or belt. An animal—dog, cat, or indeterminate beast—jumps at, claws, or bites the rear of his hose, exposing flesh.

The title is **LE MAT** and the card is unnumbered.

Possible meanings must be separated:

- **[Documented iconography]** wandering, damaged clothing, baggage, animal at the rear.
- **[Scholarly reconstruction]** the animal may be harrying a vagrant out of settled society.
- **[Tradition]** it warns that animal instinct pursues unconscious folly.
- **[Modern esoteric reading]** the beast is the lower self propelling the soul into initiation.
- **[Disputed]** whether the animal tears the trousers, exposes the buttocks, bites, caresses, or pushes differs by block and interpreter.

### Jean Noblet, Paris, ca. 1650

Noblet’s is the earliest substantially surviving named French tarot of the Marseille family.

Its **LE FOL**—rather than the later standard *Le Mat*—is a coarse, compact wanderer. The animal attacks his exposed rear; his costume is torn and belled; he carries a pack or implement over his shoulder. Noblet’s explicit buttock/genital detail was softened in many later editions.

### Jean Dodal, Lyon, ca. 1701–15

Dodal labels the card **LE MAT**. The Fool strides rightward with staff and bundle while an animal claws or bites his torn breeches. The drawing is more elongated and becomes a major witness to the Type I Marseille pattern.

### Nicolas Conver, Marseille, 1760

Conver’s *Le Mat* established one of the most reproduced Type II versions. The animal rises against the Fool’s left rear; the pack and staff become more clearly separated; the palette and woodcut contours shaped later Marseille restorations.

### Besançon pattern, eighteenth–nineteenth centuries

The Besançon family is principally marked by replacing the potentially offensive Papess and Pope with **Juno** and **Jupiter**. The Fool generally remains *Le Mat* and unnumbered.

Thus Juno/Jupiter are regional renamings of other trumps, not alternate Fool names. Likewise:

- **La Maison Dieu** is the traditional French name of the Tower.
- **La Papesse → Junon** and **Le Pape → Jupiter** occur especially in Swiss, Alsatian, and Besançon-influenced production.
- These substitutions reflect confessional and market adaptation, not evidence that the entire tarot originated as pagan mythology.

### Grimaud / Paul Marteau, twentieth century

Grimaud’s widely distributed *Ancien Tarot de Marseille*, especially Paul Marteau’s 1930s form and his 1949 book, normalized a recoloured Conver-derived design. *Le Mat* remains unnumbered. Marteau’s printed colour symbolism encouraged readers to treat every patch of red, blue, yellow, and flesh tone as intentional esoteric language.

**[Disputed]** Such readings may be coherent within Marteau’s system, but they do not establish the intentions of seventeenth-century block cutters.

### Camoin–Jodorowsky, 1997

Philippe Camoin and Alejandro Jodorowsky issued a “restored” Marseille tarot assembled from multiple historical packs and printing details. Their *Le Mat* retains the animal, bag, bells, and unnumbered status, while adding or sharpening small symbolic elements and using a deliberately restricted palette.

- **[Documented fact]** It is a late-twentieth-century synthetic reconstruction.
- **[Esoteric attribution]** Jodorowsky and Marianne Costa interpret Le Mat as unlimited energy, freedom, pilgrimage, and force without definition.
- **[Disputed marketing claim]** Calling it the recovery of one pristine, original secret tarot overstates what the heterogeneous surviving blocks can establish.

## Etteilla and nineteenth-century occult decks

### Grand Etteilla, Paris, 1788–89

Etteilla dismantled the inherited trump sequence and rebuilt it as an “Egyptian” cosmogony. The Fool became card **78**, **La Folie**, also glossed in later Etteilla traditions as **the Alchemist**.

The image remains a wandering, disordered man attacked from behind, but its new placement is crucial: he is not zero opening a spiritual journey; he is the last card of the complete divinatory pack.

**[Esoteric attribution]** Etteilla declared the tarot Egyptian and treated its orientation as independently meaningful.

**[Documented innovation]** This was the first commercial deck designed expressly for occult divination rather than adapted from a gaming pack. The BnF’s scholarship on late-Enlightenment tarot records the Fool’s renaming as *Folie*. [Stone, “Tarot in Late Enlightenment France”](https://journals.library.mun.ca/index.php/bookish/article/download/2829/2300).

### Éliphas Lévi, 1854–56 and later designs

Lévi did not issue a complete standard deck with *Dogme et rituel de la haute magie*. He described the Fool as a purposeless wanderer carrying a wallet of vices, with disordered clothing and a tiger biting him.

A contemporary English rendering reads:

> “A man in the garb of a fool, wandering without aim.”

This excerpt is from the description of the twenty-two keys in *Rituel*, chapter XXI. Lévi made the Fool the unnumbered or zero-like term associated with Hebrew **Shin**, placing it after Judgment and before the World when the cards were correlated in Marseille order to Hebrew letters. That is, his arrangement is effectively **XX, Fool, XXI**, not the later Golden Dawn **0, I … XXI**.

**[Esoteric attribution]** Lévi’s Fool signifies blind force, material or ungoverned life, and the dangerous condition of power without intelligence.

### Paul Christian, 1863–70s

Paul Christian—Jean-Baptiste Pitois—expanded the Egyptian-initiation story into an imagined subterranean temple whose twenty-two images the candidate encountered. His terminology helped popularize **Major Arcana** and **Minor Arcana**.

His “Crocodile” or Fool-like final arcanum belongs to this pseudo-Egyptian initiatory narrative.

**[Legend / literary construction]** No ancient Egyptian temple, ritual manual, or archaeological object documents Christian’s initiation route. His account is an occult romance presented as recovered history.

### Papus, *Le Tarot des Bohémiens*, 1889

Papus follows Lévi’s cyclic ordering. His diagrams explicitly put:

**19 – 20 – 0 – 21**

and explain the Fool as the transitional term immediately before the World. In the English translation’s running display it can appear typographically as “THE FOOL 21,” which has generated confusion: Papus is discussing the unnumbered/zero Fool between numbered XX and XXI, not simply declaring it trump XXI. [Papus text](https://www.labirintoermetico.com/02Tarocchi/Papus_The_Tarot_of_the_Bohemians.pdf), [chapter transcription](https://guru-ai.org/read/western_esoteric/tarot-of-the-bohemians/019).

Papus’s book title made the “Bohemian” or Romani origin theory enormously influential.

### Oswald Wirth, 1889

Wirth designed twenty-two trumps for Stanislas de Guaita. His Fool, usually called **Le Fou**, follows Lévi: a ragged wanderer, animal at his leg, sack over his shoulder, placed between Judgment and the World and associated with **Shin**.

The crocodile near or before him makes the image more explicitly “Egyptian” and suggests devouring matter, instinct, or fatality.

**[Documented fact]** Wirth’s 1889 set predates his later, better-known coloured *Tarot des imagiers du Moyen Âge*.

**[Esoteric attribution]** The figure is uncontrolled impulse and passive irresponsibility, not yet the optimistic beginner familiar from popular Waite-derived decks.

## Golden Dawn designs and reconstructions

### Order papers / “Book T,” late 1880s–1890s

The Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn reassigned the Fool from Lévi’s Shin position to the first Hebrew letter:

- Aleph (א)
- element Air
- Path 11
- Kether–Chokmah
- title “Spirit of Aether”
- zero, before the Magician

This resolved Hebrew alphabetical order from the Golden Dawn’s perspective: Aleph through Tav could now correspond to Fool through Universe.

The associated colour scales include bright pale yellow for Air/Aleph in the King Scale, with derivative scales adding sky-blue, emerald-flecked tones, and related combinations. Exact card colouring varies by manuscript and reconstruction.

**No decan applies to the Fool.** Decans and “Lord of…” Book T titles belong to the thirty-six small cards from Two through Ten. Likewise, **court-card sub-elements and zodiacal spans do not apply to the Fool**. Listing a Fool decan or court sub-element is a category error.

### Robert Wang / Israel Regardie, *Golden Dawn Tarot*, 1978

Wang’s reconstruction shows a nude or lightly draped divine child in an Aleph-like posture, surrounded by airy, dawn-like emblems. It is a reconstruction based on Regardie’s published Golden Dawn material, not a facsimile of one universally authoritative Victorian deck.

### Chic and Sandra Tabatha Cicero, 1991; later editions

The Ciceros’ *New Golden Dawn Ritual Tarot*, later issued as the *Golden Dawn Magical Tarot*, provides another full-colour reconstruction. Its Fool emphasizes the innocent child, dawn, roses, and the body configured to Aleph.

The differing Wang and Cicero cards demonstrate that “the Golden Dawn Fool” is a set of textual instructions filtered through later artists, not a single surviving canonical painting. [History of the reconstructions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tarot_card_reading#Hermetic_Order_of_the_Golden_Dawn).

## Waite-Smith, 1909

Pamela Colman Smith’s card depicts a richly dressed, androgynously youthful traveler at the brink of a mountain precipice. The head tilts upward toward the sky; a small white dog leaps at the heels. A wand over the shoulder carries a decorated wallet. The left hand holds a white rose. A white sun shines behind; distant peaks rise beneath a yellow sky. The card is marked **0**.

Waite wrote:

> “a prince of the other world on his travels through this one”

and called the atmosphere “the keen air.” In *The Pictorial Key to the Tarot*, Part II, “Zero: The Fool,” he says the precipice has no terror, as though angels might uphold the traveler. [1910/1911 text](https://www.integralpsychology.org/uploads/1/5/3/0/15300482/waite_tarot.pdf).

What changed:

1. The degraded adult vagrant became a beautiful youth.
2. The beast attacking exposed flesh became a companion dog.
3. Aimless horizontal wandering became a suspended step at a cliff.
4. The staff and bag became elegant spiritual equipment.
5. The rose introduced an explicit emblem of purity.
6. Zero and Golden Dawn Air transformed social folly into pre-manifest potential.

Possible visual sources:

- **[Scholarly reconstruction]** Marseille supplied the traveler, pack, staff, and animal.
- **[Documented context]** Smith had access to occult tarot traditions and saw the Sola Busca cards in the British Museum; their strongest effect is demonstrable in the scenic minors.
- **[Plausible but unproved]** Pre-Raphaelite illustration, theatrical costume, and the conventional “youth at a perilous edge” contributed to the style.
- **[Disputed]** The exact composition has been compared with illustrations in contemporary books, but no single prototype has been established conclusively.
- **[Modern invention]** Waite never calls the step a “leap of faith.” That phrase is a later popular summary.

## Paul Foster Case / BOTA, 1920s–1940s

Case and artist Jessie Burns Parke adapted the Waite-Smith composition into a black-and-white initiatory colouring key. Students were expected to colour their own cards.

The Fool retains the sun, precipice, dog, rose, staff, and wallet but carries additional occult emblems, including the Hebrew Aleph and symbols Case interpreted through Qabalah, sound, breath, and the Cube of Space.

Case’s principal correspondences are Aleph, Air, Life-Breath, path 11, and superconsciousness. His Fool stands first, not between XX and XXI. [BOTA deck overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/B.O.T.A._tarot_deck), [Case excerpt and discussion](https://www.psyche.com/psyche/tarot/trumps/fool.html).

## Crowley-Harris Thoth Tarot, painted 1938–43; published 1969

Frieda Harris’s **Atu 0, The Fool** is a spiralling, green-clad or green-bodied figure suspended in an oval vortex. He combines Dionysian youth, Harlequin, horned fertility god, Green Man, Parsifal, and divine madman. Around him appear a tiger, crocodile, dove, butterfly, grapes, coins or disks, spiral force, umbilical forms, and other generative symbols.

Crowley’s headings explicitly include:

> “The ‘Green Man’ of the Spring Festival. ‘April Fool.’ The Holy Ghost.”

He associates the card with Aleph, Air, zero, the ox, Harpocrates, Dionysus, Parsifal, and creative nothingness. [Crowley, *Book of Thoth*, “The Atu”](https://hermetic.com/crowley/book-of-thoth/atu).

Compared with Waite-Smith, Harris eliminates the literal cliff and turns the entire card into ecstatic germination. The tiger no longer merely warns a traveler; it participates in the whirling complex of lust, danger, and creative force. The crocodile recalls Egyptianized occult tarot and Harpocrates/Hoor-paar-kraat.

**Tzaddi/Heh caution:** Crowley’s famous “Tzaddi is not the Star” swap concerns the **Emperor and Star**, not the Fool. The Fool remains Aleph/Air. Importing that controversy into the Fool’s attribution is erroneous.

## Selected notable modern redesigns

### Aquarian Tarot, David Palladini, 1970

A stylized Art Deco/psychedelic Fool translates the Waite-Smith youth into countercultural visual language. The optimistic seeker remains dominant.

### Motherpeace, Vicki Noble and Karen Vogel, 1981

The round feminist deck reconceives the journey through matrifocal, embodied, and communal symbolism. The Fool’s openness becomes liberation from patriarchal linearity.

### Haindl Tarot, Hermann Haindl, 1980s

The Fool combines painterly abstraction with ecological and cross-cultural references. It continues Crowley’s cosmic rather than merely naïve Fool.

### Wild Unknown, Kim Krans, 2012

A small chick poised on a branch replaces the human traveler. It is a clear **modern invention** translating innocence and risk into animal minimalism.

### Jodorowsky-Camoin and Jodorowsky-Costa

Their reading returns to *Le Mat* rather than the Waite cliff: energy is moving but has no numbered station. The lack of number permits it to be placed anywhere. This is a modern therapeutic-esoteric interpretation of an authentic historical feature.

# The game

## Original structural role

A standard historical Italian pack contains:

- four suits of fourteen cards;
- twenty-one ranked permanent trumps;
- one unnumbered Fool.

**[Documented fact]** The Fool was not originally “Trump 0.” It had no place in the rank sequence and ordinarily could not beat another card.

Its defining early role was the **excuse**: the player could put it to a trick instead of following the led suit or playing a trump. It usually returned to the player’s captured pile, sometimes in exchange for another card given to the winner of the trick.

This structure explains the absence of a number more economically than occult ideas about primordial nothingness.

## French tarot

The Fool is **l’Excuse**, one of the three *oudlers* or “honours,” with trump 1 and trump 21. Each normally counts 4½ card points.

When played:

- it ordinarily does not win the trick;
- its player retrieves it and compensates the trick winner with a low card;
- special last-trick rules apply;
- in the exceptional announced *chelem* situation, its timing can change.

Official and historical rules have varied in minor details. [Pagat French Tarot](https://www.pagat.com/tarot/frtarot.html).

## Italian tarocchi and tarocchino

The Matto normally excuses. In Bologna it is also a *contatore*, helping form combinations. It may substitute within sequences but cannot simply replace every card without restriction.

## Minchiate

The Matto is unnumbered, worth five points, and useful in *versicole*. It excuses the player and can fill internal gaps in combinations under rulebook-specific restrictions. [Pagat Minchiate](https://www.pagat.com/tarot/minchiate.html).

## Central European Tarock

In many Austrian, Hungarian, Czech, Slovenian, and related games the Fool ceased to excuse and became the highest trump, functionally **Tarock XXII**. It is commonly called **Sküs**.

Together with XXI and I it forms the **Trull**. In point-counting systems these cards are usually worth five points each before deductions used in packet counting.

The famous **Emperor’s trick / Märchenstich** occurs in some games when Sküs, XXI, and I meet: contrary to ordinary ranking, I may win. This is a regional special rule, not the Fool’s universal ancient function.

## Three major historical trump orders

Dummett classified early orders broadly as:

| Type | Centre | General distinguishing feature |
|---|---|---|
| A | Bologna and Florence | Virtues and papal/imperial cards arranged in central-Italian fashion; equal-ranking low cards occur in Bologna |
| B | Ferrara | Different placement of virtues and several celestial trumps |
| C | Milan; later France | Ancestor of the Marseille sequence |

These are families, not three perfectly invariant lists. At least ten or eleven local orders can be distinguished in fuller evidence.

The main Justice/Strength issue:

- Marseille/Type C eventually has Justice VIII and Strength XI.
- Waite exchanged them: Strength VIII, Justice XI.
- The Golden Dawn made this swap to align Leo with Strength and Libra with Justice in zodiacal sequence.
- Crowley retained the structural exchange but renamed them Adjustment VIII and Lust XI.

The Fool does not solve trump-order disputes because in early play it stands outside rank.

## Earliest rules

No complete fifteenth-century rulebook survives. The Fool’s original excusing function is reconstructed from later but geographically widespread rules and from the otherwise anomalous unnumbered card.

This is a strong **scholarly reconstruction**, not a verbatim rule from the 1442 account.

For the broad history of the rule family, Dummett and McLeod’s *A History of Games Played with the Tarot Pack* distinguishes games retaining the excuse from those that converted the Fool into the highest trump. [Authors’ supplement and project site](https://www.tarotgame.org/).

# Chronology of attestation

### ca. 1412–25 — Marziano da Tortona

Marziano describes a commissioned pack for Filippo Maria Visconti. It has superior gods and suited birds, but no Fool and no standard twenty-one-trump sequence.

**Status:** documented precursor; calling it a complete tarot is disputed.

### 1440 — Florence

A discovered notarial record mentions *naibi a trionfi* or the game of triumphs before the long-standard 1442 citation.

**Status:** documented according to recent archival scholarship; exact priority depends on how the wording and date are read.

### February and July 1442 — Ferrara

Este account books record payment for packs of *carte da trionfi*.

**Status:** secure documented evidence for the game; the entries do not enumerate the Fool or the individual trumps. Andrea Vitali’s historical overview notes that the 1442 references were formerly considered the earliest certain ones until the 1440 discovery. [Le Tarot history](https://www.letarot.it/page.aspx?id=350).

### ca. 1441–44 — Visconti di Modrone

Early painted cards survive, but not the Fool.

### ca. 1456–58 — Visconti-Sforza Fool

The feather-haired ragged man in the Morgan is the earliest securely surviving Fool image presently identifiable.

### ca. 1460s–70s — Mantegna “Tarocchi”

*Misero* supplies a close visual parallel but is not a tarot Fool in a playable pack.

### ca. 1470–80 — *Sermones de ludo cum aliis*

An anonymous Dominican preacher lists the tarot subjects while condemning gambling. The list includes *El matto* after the twenty-one ranked triumphs and calls it “nothing” in the ordering logic.

**Status:** earliest surviving substantially complete standard trump list. Its moral invective documents how one preacher read the images, not necessarily their designers’ intentions.

### 1490s — Sola Busca

Dated engraved narrative pack; no direct source for Smith’s Fool composition has been demonstrated.

### ca. 1500 — Florentine poems and lists

A Florentine verse lists the trumps in an order close to the Rosenwald pattern. Literary works begin using tarot names metaphorically. These sources help reconstruct regional order but do not yet provide occult correspondences.

### early sixteenth century — *tarocchi*

The word replaces *trionfi* as the specific name of the game. French *tarot* and German *Tarock* derive from the Italian term, not vice versa.

### 1520s — Teofilo Folengo

Folengo’s *Chaos del Triperuno* includes literary divination using tarot-card names in sonnets. This is evidence for poetic/cartomantic manipulation, but not for a continuous ancient divinatory system.

### seventeenth century — France and regional Italy

Printed patterns establish *Le Mat*, the animal-at-the-heels wanderer, Minchiate’s Matto, and Bolognese conventions.

### 1650 — Noblet

Earliest substantially surviving named French Marseille-family pack: *Le Fol*.

### 1701–15 — Dodal

*Le Mat* in a recognizably canonical Marseille form.

### 1760 — Conver

Highly influential Marseille Type II *Le Mat*.

### 1781 — Court de Gébelin and Comte de Mellet

Volume VIII of *Monde primitif* asserts that tarot is the Egyptian Book of Thoth. De Mellet correlates the twenty-two special cards with Hebrew letters, but in reverse or non-Golden-Dawn ordering.

**Status:** first major published occult-Egyptian reinterpretation; no historical evidence supplied.

### 1783–89 — Etteilla

Etteilla publishes a comprehensive tarot divination method and an expressly occult deck. The Fool becomes 78, *La Folie*.

### 1854–56 — Éliphas Lévi

*Dogme et rituel de la haute magie* makes tarot central to ceremonial magic and Kabbalah. The Fool remains between XX and XXI and corresponds to Shin.

### 1860s–70s — Paul Christian

Pseudo-Egyptian temple initiation and “arcana” language become influential.

### 1888 — S. L. MacGregor Mathers

*The Tarot: Its Occult Signification, Use in Fortune-Telling, and Method of Play* summarizes inherited meanings immediately before the Golden Dawn system was elaborated.

### 1889 — Papus and Wirth

Papus popularizes the “Tarot of the Bohemians”; Wirth publishes Guaita’s occult trumps.

### late 1880s–1890s — Golden Dawn

The Fool becomes zero/Aleph/Air/path 11.

### 1909–11 — Waite-Smith

Smith creates the cliff-edge youth; Waite publishes *The Key to the Tarot* and then *Pictorial Key*.

### 1938–43 / 1969 — Thoth

Harris paints the deck under Crowley’s direction; the complete deck is commercially published in 1969.

### 1947 — Paul Foster Case

*The Tarot: A Key to the Wisdom of the Ages* codifies BOTA’s Aleph/Air symbolism.

### 1970 — Eden Gray

*A Complete Guide to the Tarot* appears to be the earliest traceable sustained presentation of the Major Arcana as the **“Fool’s Journey.”**

**Finding:** the idea is modern, not a named medieval or Renaissance doctrine. [Historical summary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tarot_card_reading).

### 1977–present — Jungian and therapeutic tarot

Writers such as Sallie Nichols, Rachel Pollack, Mary K. Greer, Jodorowsky and Costa treat the Fool as archetype, developmental state, or open psychic energy.

## Conflicts

### Aleph versus Shin

- Lévi, Papus, and Wirth preserve the Fool near the end and assign **Shin**.
- The Golden Dawn moves it to the beginning and assigns **Aleph**.
- Waite, Crowley, and Case inherit the Golden Dawn solution.

No fifteenth-century card or game rule supports either Hebrew assignment. Both are **esoteric constructions**.

### Air versus Fire

Aleph is assigned Air in *Sefer Yetzirah*-based Golden Dawn doctrine. Shin is Fire. Moving the Fool from Shin to Aleph necessarily changed its element.

### Zero versus unnumbered

- Unnumbered means “no rank” in the gaming structure.
- Zero is an occult and editorial formalization.
- XXII makes it the highest trump in some Central-European games or the twenty-second esoteric key.
- Etteilla’s 78 makes it the final card of his reordered full pack.

These numbers encode different systems and should not be harmonized.

### Uranus

Uranus is common in modern occult tables because its disruptive, sky-like character suits the Fool. It is not the original Golden Dawn attribution, which is elemental Air. Treat Uranus as a **later esoteric extension**, especially associated with Case-influenced or modern astrology.

### Tzaddi/Heh

Crowley’s “Tzaddi is not the Star” doctrine swaps the Hebrew associations of the Emperor and Star. It does **not** replace Aleph on the Fool.

### A crucial Waite discrepancy

Waite’s **image-description** is exalted, but his official **fortune-telling list** is predominantly adverse. Modern popular tarot fused his radiant image with later affirmative concepts—new beginnings, trust, innocence, adventure—and often relegated Waite’s older “folly/madness” list to reversal.

That positive upright consensus is therefore largely a **twentieth-century interpretive development**, not Waite’s own unambiguous divinatory definition.

# Esoteric and symbolic readings

## Christian moral imagery

**First securely applicable context:** fifteenth-century Italy.

The ragged Fool belongs to a Christian social and moral vocabulary encompassing folly, madness, sin, poverty, exclusion, and the Psalmist’s “fool” who denies God. The anonymous *Sermones de ludo* reads the tarot morally and places the Fool at the end as a condition of worthlessness.

This does not prove that the card was designed as a specific biblical verse. It establishes that contemporary Christian viewers could interpret it within that moral field.

## Carnival, holy folly, and the licensed fool

The fool can invert order, speak truths unavailable to ordinary subjects, and stand outside hierarchy.

- **[Historical context]** Court fools, carnival inversions, wild men, beggars, and professional entertainers were familiar.
- **[Disputed identification]** The Visconti figure lacks the standard motley and cap-and-bells of a court jester. “Beggar,” “madman,” or social outcast may fit better.
- **[Later symbolic tradition]** The fool becomes wise because he has abandoned conventional reason.

## Hermetic and Egyptian

Court de Gébelin first made tarot a surviving Egyptian sacred book in 1781; Etteilla operationalized the claim; Paul Christian dramatized it; Papus, Wirth, the Golden Dawn, and Crowley redistributed Egyptian gods and Hermetic concepts across the cards.

For the Fool, the Egyptian crocodile became especially durable: devouring matter, danger at the end of the path, Sobek, or the animal beneath Harpocrates.

**Evidence:** eighteenth- and nineteenth-century occult texts.

**No evidence:** ancient Egyptian cards, inscriptions, papyri, or references describing the medieval tarot sequence.

## Kabbalistic

De Mellet first published a systematic twenty-two trumps/twenty-two Hebrew letters equation in 1781. Lévi made it central to occult magic. The Golden Dawn corrected Lévi according to its own alphabetic and astrological requirements.

This is a documented history of an **esoteric attribution**, not documentation of a Renaissance Jewish origin.

## Alchemical

The Fool has been read as:

- prima materia;
- volatile Mercury;
- undifferentiated chaos;
- the alchemist himself;
- spirit before fixation;
- *solve* preceding *coagula*.

The explicit title “Folly or the Alchemist” belongs to the Etteilla transmission. More systematic alchemical readings spread through nineteenth- and twentieth-century occultism.

**Caution:** no fifteenth-century Fool carries an alchemical label or laboratory apparatus. Retrospective claims that the Visconti card secretly encodes a complete alchemical operation are speculative.

## Astrological

The oldest tarot has no documented astrological attribution for the Fool.

- Etteilla distributed astrological and elemental meanings through his reordered pack.
- Golden Dawn: Air, not a planet or zodiac sign.
- Case and later occultists: Uranus sometimes added.
- Modern astrologers may associate it with Aquarius, but this conflicts with the Golden Dawn’s separate Aquarius/Star correspondence.

## Thelemic

Crowley makes the Fool the zero-point from which positive and negative, being and non-being, issue. He connects it with:

- Aleph and the ox;
- Air;
- Harpocrates;
- Dionysus Zagreus;
- Parsifal, the “pure fool”;
- the Green Man and spring;
- April Fool customs;
- the Holy Ghost;
- sexual and creative energy.

These are deliberately syncretic correspondences. They document Crowley’s system, not the historical origin of the Milanese card.

## Jungian

Jung did not publish a canonical tarot interpretation of the Fool comparable to Golden Dawn tables. Later writers applied Jungian concepts:

- the Trickster;
- the *puer aeternus*;
- ego before differentiation;
- the Self calling the personality into individuation;
- liminality and the confrontation with shadow;
- the wise fool who joins opposites.

Sallie Nichols’s *Jung and Tarot* was especially influential. Pollack, Greer, and Jodorowsky spread related psychological models.

**Finding:** “Jung said the Fool means…” is often a misattribution. In most cases the statement comes from post-Jungian tarot authors, not Jung’s collected works.

## The Fool’s Journey

The twenty-two trumps as a single biographical sequence beginning with the Fool is now ubiquitous.

**Finding:** no such named doctrine has been traced in Renaissance game documents, Gébelin, Lévi, or Waite. Eden Gray’s 1970 *Complete Guide to the Tarot* is the earliest widely cited sustained use of the “Fool’s Journey” teaching model. It was then spread by Pollack and popular instructional literature.

# People and works

## Artists, printers, and designers

- **Bonifacio Bembo / Bembo workshop:** traditional principal attribution for Visconti-Sforza cards.
- **Francesco Zavattari:** competing attribution recorded by the Morgan.
- **Antonio Cicognara, Benedetto Bembo, or Franco dei Russi:** proposed makers of certain replacement or stylistically different Visconti-Sforza cards; not the surviving Fool specifically.
- **Jean Noblet:** Parisian cardmaker, ca. 1650.
- **Jean Dodal:** Lyon/Avignon-associated cardmaker, ca. 1701–15.
- **Nicolas Conver:** Marseille cardmaker, 1760.
- **Jean-Baptiste Alliette/Etteilla:** cartomancer and occult-deck designer.
- **Oswald Wirth:** artist and occultist working with Stanislas de Guaita.
- **Pamela Colman Smith:** artist of the 1909 Waite-Smith deck.
- **A. E. Waite:** designer-author who supplied the occult programme.
- **Frieda Harris:** painter and co-creator of the Thoth Tarot.
- **Aleister Crowley:** Thoth’s designer-author.
- **Jessie Burns Parke:** artist of Paul Foster Case’s BOTA tarot.
- **Robert Wang; Chic and Sandra Cicero:** later Golden Dawn reconstructors.
- **Philippe Camoin and Alejandro Jodorowsky:** creators of the 1997 synthetic Marseille restoration.

## T. S. Eliot, *The Waste Land* (1922)

Madame Sosostris displays “a wicked pack of cards,” but the named cards are the drowned Phoenician Sailor, Belladonna, the man with three staves, the Wheel, and the one-eyed merchant; the Hanged Man is said to be absent.

**Finding:** **the Fool is not named in the poem.** Eliot admitted in his note that he was “not familiar with the exact constitution of the Tarot pack” and adapted or invented cards for his poetic design. Therefore statements that the Fool explicitly appears in *The Waste Land* are incorrect.

Tarot is relevant to the poem; this particular card is not textually present. Michael Wood makes the broader comparison when reviewing Calvino, but that is not evidence of an Eliot Fool. [NYRB review](https://www.nybooks.com/articles/1977/05/12/fortune-hunting/).

## Italo Calvino, *The Castle of Crossed Destinies*

Calvino constructs stories by laying out tarot cards, using both a Visconti-style pack and a Marseille pack in different sections/editions. The Fool functions within the combinatorial visual grammar of narrators who cannot speak.

**Finding:** tarot is structurally central, but there is no single definitive Calvino exegesis declaring one exclusive meaning for the Fool. Its significance changes according to the sequence in which it is read.

## W. B. Yeats

Yeats was a Golden Dawn initiate and knew its tarot correspondences. Tarot imagery belongs to the broader symbolic environment of his occult work.

**Finding:** broad claims that a particular famous Yeats poem is “about the Fool card” require case-by-case textual demonstration. Scholarship documents tarot use in Yeats, but the direct Fool correspondences are much less secure than general Golden Dawn influence. [Lund University research record](https://portal.research.lu.se/en/publications/vad-korten-ber%C3%A4ttar-tarot-hos-yeats-meyrink-kafka-ts-eliot-och-si/).

## Alejandro Jodorowsky

Jodorowsky’s films repeatedly feature holy innocents, clowns, pilgrims, mutilated bodies, alchemical journeys, and tarot-like initiation. *The Holy Mountain* is especially compatible with the Fool’s initiatory role, while *El Topo* uses wandering and spiritual ordeal.

**Caution:** visual or thematic similarity is not always a literal quotation of *Le Mat*. Jodorowsky’s explicit tarot doctrine is most securely documented in the Camoin deck and *The Way of Tarot* with Marianne Costa.

## Ingmar Bergman

Bergman’s *The Seventh Seal* uses medieval allegory, a wandering troupe, Death, and a final dance, all motifs that invite tarot comparison.

**Finding:** no reliable production evidence was found showing that Bergman deliberately quoted the Fool card. Describing the film as “tarot-like” is critical analogy, not documented source dependence.

## Paintings and buildings

The Fool’s broad iconographic relatives include:

- Boschian fools, beggars, and wayfarers;
- the “Ship of Fools” tradition;
- court-jester portraits;
- images of the prodigal son and wandering vagabond;
- the *Misero* engraving in the Mantegna series.

Claims that a particular cathedral portal or Renaissance building encodes all twenty-two trumps generally belong to modern occult architecture theories. No securely documented fifteenth-century building programme has been shown to copy the complete tarot sequence.

Niki de Saint Phalle’s **Tarot Garden** in Tuscany is a genuine modern architectural/sculptural tarot environment. Its monumental structures translate tarot into inhabitable art, although its best-known buildings emphasize the Empress, High Priestess, Magician, and other arcana rather than providing a universally canonical Fool design.

# Controversies and disputes

## Egyptian origin

### Claim

Court de Gébelin wrote in 1781 that the tarot was the remains of an Egyptian Book of Thoth, disguised as a game and carried through the centuries.

### Evidence for transmission of the belief

Strong: his essay survives; Etteilla, Paul Christian, Papus, and many later occultists repeated and elaborated it.

### Evidence for the historical origin claim

None known. Gébelin wrote before Champollion’s decipherment of hieroglyphs and supplied no Egyptian cards, inscription, text, or transmission chain. His proposed Egyptian word derivations lack hieroglyphic forms. Dummett specifically notes this evidentiary failure. [Dummett, “Origins of Tarot”](https://www.nybooks.com/articles/1981/05/14/origins-of-tarot/).

### Verdict label

**[Legend as history; documented as eighteenth-century esoteric myth.]**

## Romani or “Gypsy” origin

### Claim

Romani people brought tarot from Egypt or India into Europe.

### Development

De Mellet linked tarot, Egypt, and itinerant transmission; nineteenth-century writers such as Vaillant and Papus made the “Bohemian” story famous.

### Evidence

Romani migration into Europe is real, and Romani fortune-telling became culturally associated with cards. But northern Italian tarot documents and surviving court packs precede any evidence that Romani communities carried or invented tarot. The ethnic label “Gypsy” itself arose from the mistaken European belief that the Romani came from Egypt.

### Verdict label

**[Legend; historically important to occult reception, unsupported as the origin of tarot.]**

## Cathar origin

### Claim

The trumps encode Cathar dualism or survived from an Albigensian secret book.

### Evidence

No Cathar manuscript, inquisitorial record, card, or transmission chain links Cathars to tarot. The Cathar communities were crushed well before the securely documented fifteenth-century invention of *trionfi*.

Some visual themes—dualism, ecclesiastical criticism, spiritual ascent—can be read compatibly with later ideas about Cathars, but compatibility is not descent.

### Verdict label

**[Modern speculative tradition.]**

## Templar origin

### Claim

The Knights Templar preserved Egyptian, Johannite, or Grail wisdom in twenty-two tarot images.

### Evidence

No Templar inventory, rule, trial record, artwork, or document describes tarot. The order’s suppression in 1312 predates documented tarot by more than a century.

### Verdict label

**[Legend / modern invention.]**

## Kabbalistic origin

The numerical coincidence of twenty-two special cards and twenty-two Hebrew letters is real. But the first documented published equation is de Mellet’s 1781 essay, more than three centuries after tarot’s appearance.

### Verdict label

**[Esoteric attribution, not demonstrated origin.]**

## The Fool as Joker ancestor

Both cards are exceptional and often unnumbered, but the American Joker arose in the nineteenth-century game of Euchre as the highest bower.

### Verdict label

Direct descent from the tarot Fool is **disputed and generally rejected**. Analogy and later visual borrowing are possible; independent game history is better documented.

## “Wild card”

Museums sometimes call the Fool a wild card for accessibility. Technically, in many historical tarot games it does not assume arbitrary identity as a poker wild card does; it excuses its holder from the normal obligation to follow suit.

### Verdict label

Useful shorthand, but mechanically imprecise.

## Was the early Fool mentally ill, sinful, poor, or festive?

The surviving image can support several contemporary categories. Torn clothing and exposed flesh suggest social marginality; feathers and vacant demeanor may suggest madness; the staff and wandering suggest vagrancy.

No caption on the Visconti card says “beggar,” “madman,” or “holy fool.” The name *Matto* supports foolishness or madness but does not fix a medical diagnosis.

### Verdict label

**[Open iconographic dispute.]**

## Did the Marseille animal warn or propel the Fool?

The animal physically contacts the rear of the figure. Different blocks make biting, clawing, and pushing more or less plausible.

- warning conscience: later moral reading;
- animal instinct: occult/psychological reading;
- society’s dogs driving out a vagrant: social-historical reconstruction;
- faithful companion: especially Waite-derived modern reading.

No early maker’s explanation survives.

## Waite’s “leap of faith”

Waite describes a pause at a precipice and imagines angelic protection. He does not use the now-standard phrase “leap of faith.”

### Verdict label

**[Modern popular paraphrase.]**

## The white rose

Waite mentions the rose but does not in the card description explicitly define it by the popular formula “purity and freedom from baser desire.” That gloss is consistent with Western rose symbolism and Waite’s exalted Fool, but later manuals hardened it into a fixed authorial statement.

### Verdict label

**[Plausible esoteric interpretation; exact stock wording often misattributed.]**

## “Fool’s Journey” as ancient doctrine

No early game document presents the trumps as stations in one hero’s biography.

### Verdict label

**[Twentieth-century teaching construction, probably first popularized in sustained form by Eden Gray and expanded by Pollack.]**

## “Tarot de Marseille” as medieval name

French cardmakers produced the pattern in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, but the classificatory name *Tarot de Marseille* became standard much later, particularly through nineteenth- and twentieth-century cataloguing and occult publishing.

### Verdict label

**[Modern pattern name applied retrospectively.]**

## Misprints and editorial accidents

Several unstable readings became traditions:

1. **“Distribution” in Waite’s reversed Fool list** likely reflects inherited wording or textual corruption near “distraction”; later editions variously preserve or correct it.
2. **Papus’s “THE FOOL 21” layouts** can be mistaken for numbering the Fool XXI when the diagram actually inserts 0 between XX and XXI.
3. **Zero on historical reproductions** is often added by modern publishers to cards whose originals were blank.
4. **Restored colours and lines** in modern Marseille editions are editorial decisions frequently presented as recovery.
5. **The “Charles VI tarot”** acquired a false connection to Gringonneur and the French king from antiquarian attribution.
6. **The “Mantegna Tarot”** is neither demonstrably Mantegna’s nor a tarot pack.
7. **Rider Tarot** obscures Pamela Colman Smith’s authorship; “Waite-Smith” or “Rider-Waite-Smith” is historically fuller.

# Open questions

1. **Where was standard tarot invented?** Milan, Ferrara, Bologna, and Florence all possess early evidence. No surviving patent, designer’s statement, or first complete pack decides the issue.

2. **Was the standard Fool present by 1440–42?** It was almost certainly part of the mature game by the time of the surviving Visconti-Sforza card and *Sermones* list, but the earliest ledger entries merely say *trionfi*.

3. **What precisely do the Visconti Fool’s feathers signify?** Madness, degraded festivity, mental lightness, or a local costume convention remain possibilities.

4. **What were the complete images of the lost Cary-Yale and Brambilla Fools?** Modern restorations cannot answer this.

5. **How directly did Italian printed patterns generate the Marseille Fool?** The lineage is strongly supported in general, but intermediate cards are incomplete.

6. **What is the Marseille animal?** Dog is conventional; some blocks are anatomically ambiguous.

7. **Did Smith have one specific non-tarot prototype for the cliff scene?** No conclusive source was located. Marseille, Golden Dawn doctrine, contemporary illustration, and Smith’s own synthesis remain the safest account.

8. **When was the Fool first printed as Arabic 0?** Occult manuscripts and decks around the Golden Dawn period normalized it, but a comprehensive census of all eighteenth- and nineteenth-century editions is needed to establish the first physical card beyond doubt.

9. **What did the earliest players call the excusing action?** Later Italian and French terminology is clear; a fifteenth-century rule text has not survived.

10. **Can Crowley’s dense symbols be assigned solely to him or Harris?** Their correspondence shows close collaboration, but individual visual decisions cannot always be separated.

11. **Calvino’s Fool:** the card can be followed in particular layouts and editions, but no single statement exhausts its changing narrative use.

12. **Eliot:** the research lead produced a negative result. Tarot appears in *The Waste Land*; the Fool does not.

13. **Bergman and architectural quotations:** suggestive analogies greatly outnumber documented acts of borrowing.
