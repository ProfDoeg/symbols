# The Tower: Research Dossier

## Method and evidentiary labels

This dossier uses the following labels throughout:

- **[Documented fact]** directly attested by a surviving card, archival record, early printed book, manuscript, or museum catalogue.
- **[Scholarly reconstruction]** a conclusion inferred from surviving objects, textual evidence, or comparison of regional patterns.
- **[Tradition]** a historically transmitted interpretation whose empirical origin is secondary or uncertain.
- **[Esoteric attribution]** a correspondence deliberately imposed by an occult author or school.
- **[Disputed claim]** a proposal argued by identifiable authors but not accepted as established.
- **[Legend]** a historical claim contradicted by the documentary chronology or unsupported by evidence.
- **[Modern invention]** a recent redesign or interpretation presented without an ancient documentary pedigree.

“Tarot de Marseille” is used as a pattern-name, not as a claim that tarot originated in Marseille. The name itself became common only in modern French occult and commercial usage.

## Names and their history

### Italian

**[Documented fact]** The earliest lists do not consistently say *La Torre*. The anonymous *Sermones de ludo* calls the subject **la Sagitta**, literally “the arrow,” but in context the projectile or fiery bolt from heaven. Renaissance writers and regional lists give:

- *La Sagitta* — the Arrow/Bolt.
- *La Saetta* — Lightning or Thunderbolt.
- *Il Fuoco* — the Fire; found in or associated with Folengo, Piscina, Garzoni, and other sixteenth-century testimony.
- *La Casa del Diavolo* — the Devil’s House, especially associated with Ferrarese terminology.
- *La Casa del Dannato* — House of the Damned.
- *Inferno* — Hell.
- *La Casa* — the House.
- *La Magione* or *Casa di Plutone* — the Mansion/House of Pluto.
- *La Torre* — now standard Italian, but not demonstrably the earliest or universal Renaissance name.

Andrea Vitali’s survey assembles these names from sermons, literary testimony, and regional lists. Its iconological conclusions are interpretations, but its collection of early names is grounded in textual witnesses. [Vitali, “The Tower”](https://www.letarot.it/The-Tower_pag_pg128_eng.aspx)

### French

**[Documented fact]** The Marseille lineage labels the card **LA MAISON DIEV** or **LA MAISON DIEU**. Seventeenth-century spelling does not permit Jodorowsky’s modern punctuation to be read as an original grammatical instruction.

The title can mean “the House of God.” Historically, *maison-Dieu* was also a normal French institutional term for a hospital, hospice, almshouse, or pilgrims’ shelter. That lexical fact does not establish that the card portrays a hospital.

**[Disputed claim]** The hospital meaning has been proposed as the source of the title, sometimes through an association between hospitals and death, disaster, or charitable rescue. No early cardmaker’s explanatory text has been found confirming it.

**[Modern esoteric interpretation]** Jodorowsky and Costa read the title as *La Maison Dieu*, “The House God” or “House/God,” not *La Maison de Dieu*. This produces an image of the human body or awakened structure opening toward heaven. It is a creative grammatical and visual reading, not an attested seventeenth-century explanation.

Other French titles include:

- **La Foudre / La Fouldre** — Lightning, especially in Belgian and non-Marseille French patterns.
- **Le Feu du Ciel** — Fire from Heaven, Oswald Wirth’s 1889 title.
- **La Tour foudroyée** — the Lightning-Struck Tower, a descriptive occult title.

### English

The normal modern title is **The Tower**. Nineteenth-century occult writers also used:

- **The Lightning-Struck Tower** — Mathers.
- **The Blasted Tower** — Golden Dawn usage.
- **The House of God** — literal Marseille translation.
- **The Tower Struck by Lightning** — explanatory variant.

### Spanish and German

- Spanish: **La Torre**; Marseille editions also translate *Maison Dieu* as **La Casa de Dios**.
- German: **Der Turm**; multilingual Marseille editions use **Das Haus Gottes**.
- In German-speaking gaming practice, however, the card is usually identified simply as trump XVI or by the local pack’s index. French-suited Tarock packs often abandon the medieval allegory altogether.

### Besançon and regional religious renaming

**[Documented fact]** The Tarot de Besançon, developed in eastern France, Switzerland, and adjacent German-speaking regions, replaced the Papess and Pope with **Juno and Jupiter**, principally to avoid objectionable Catholic figures. It normally retained **La Maison Dieu** for XVI. Thus the frequently mentioned Juno/Jupiter substitutions are important regional context but are not Tower renamings. [Overview of the Besançon pattern](https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tarot_de_Besan%C3%A7on)

The Fool was normally unnumbered in historic Italian and Marseille packs. “0” is a later occult and commercial convention. Strength and Justice changed positions in some occult decks; the Tower remained XVI in the Marseille, Golden Dawn, Waite-Smith, and Thoth lines.

# Iconography deck by deck

## Proto-tarot and fifteenth-century court decks

### Marziano da Tortona and Michelino da Besozzo, circa 1418–1425

**[Documented fact]** Marziano’s treatise describes a special card game made for Filippo Maria Visconti, with sixteen classical gods divided among four symbolic suits. Michelino da Besozzo painted the cards; they are lost.

**[Important limitation]** This is not a surviving 22-trump tarot sequence, and it contains no identifiable Tower card. It is relevant as evidence that intellectually designed trump games existed at the Milanese court before the first records of *carte da trionfi*, not as an attestation of Tower XVI.

### Visconti di Modrone / Cary-Yale

These are two names for the same surviving group, now primarily at Yale’s Beinecke Library.

**[Documented fact]** Eleven trumps survive, including theological virtues not present in the later standard sequence. No Tower survives.

**[Open question]** Whether the original pack had 78, 86, or some other number of cards remains disputed. It is therefore unsafe to say simply that its Tower “was lost”: the precise original sequence is not known.

### Brera-Brambilla

**[Documented fact]** This Milanese Visconti group survives very incompletely. No Tower survives.

### Pierpont Morgan–Bergamo Visconti-Sforza

**[Documented fact]** Seventy-four cards normally assigned to the pack survive across the Morgan Library, Accademia Carrara, and Colleoni holdings; four standard cards are missing: the Devil, Tower, Three of Swords, and Knight of Coins. Six surviving cards were made by a different, later fifteenth-century hand, probably as replacements. [Morgan catalogue](https://www.themorgan.org/manuscript/158525)

**Finding on the item-specific lead:** there is **no surviving original fifteenth-century Tower in this Visconti-Sforza pack**.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** Because the late-fifteenth-century sermon lists both Devil and Bolt/Arrow, and because early printed Italian packs have both subjects, most historians infer that the standard Tower subject existed when the Morgan-Bergamo deck was made or completed. But inference is not the same as a surviving card.

**[Disputed claim]** Helen Farley has proposed that a Visconti-Sforza Tower may have commemorated or alluded to the defeat of the della Torre, historic enemies of the Visconti. The family name makes the hypothesis attractive, but no lost card or commissioning document supplies the necessary evidence. [Farley’s chapter abstract](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/261027119_The_Tower_and_the_Devil_in_the_Visconti-Sforza_Deck)

### “Sforza Castle sheet/cards”

This phrase is a recurrent source of confusion.

**[Documented fact]** Fragments recovered from a well at the Castello Sforzesco, together with the Cary sheet, show that Marseille-like imagery circulated in or reached Milan.

**[Disputed dating]** Some popular accounts call the Castello material fifteenth-century. Specialist discussion often places at least some of it in the sixteenth century, possibly late. Its relationship to French cards is also debatable: it may be an Italian ancestor, a parallel survival, or evidence of French imagery carried into Milan.

**Conclusion:** the Castle fragments do not supply an indisputable Visconti court-deck Tower. The approximately 1500 **Cary sheet**, not an autograph Bembo card, is the firmer Milanese printed witness.

## Ferrarese and related late-fifteenth-century cards

### “Charles VI” or Gringonneur tarot

**[Documented fact]** Seventeen luxury cards survive in the Bibliothèque nationale de France. They are Italian, dated approximately 1475–1500, and have no demonstrated connection to Charles VI or his painter Jacquemin Gringonneur. The old royal attribution is obsolete. [BnF-linked catalogue record](https://mlxdemo.com/book.php?id=17541)

**[Disputed Tower identification]** Secondary accounts sometimes identify a damaged, burning fortress in this group as the earliest surviving Tower. Cataloguing and reproduction histories are inconsistent, and the surviving group has often been confused with other Florentine or Ferrarese luxury cards. The cautious finding is that this evidence may preserve an early Fire/Tower subject, but it should not be presented as securely “the Charles VI Tower” without reference to the exact BnF item and image.

### Este cards

Several Ferrarese groups associated with the Este court survive, including cards now at Yale and elsewhere.

**[Documented fact]** Their style and sequence differ from the Milanese luxury packs. No universally agreed complete Tower lineage can be reconstructed from them.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** The sermon’s Type-B order and Ferrarese names such as *Casa del Diavolo* or *Fuoco* suggest that a destructive Fire/House trump was established in the region even when an individual surviving Este group lacks it.

### Budapest–Metropolitan sheets

**[Documented fact]** These printed sheets, around 1500, preserve a Ferrarese-style sequence. They are important evidence for cheaper, serially printed tarot outside elite court commissions.

The Fire/Tower subject belongs to the late-medieval catastrophe family: a building or fortress destroyed by celestial fire, with the precise number of victims and architectural details varying.

### Rothschild sheets

**[Documented fact]** The Rothschild cards/sheets, now divided among collections including the Louvre/Beaux-Arts context, are early Bolognese evidence.

The Tower composition introduces or clearly preserves falling human beings around a damaged tower, an important bridge to the later Marseille formula.

### Rosenwald sheets

**[Documented fact]** The Rosenwald sheets are usually classified as early Florentine tarot, preceding or related to Minchiate.

The relevant subject shows a fortress or tower being consumed or attacked by fire from a celestial disk. Compared with Marseille:

- the emphasis is on fire and ruined architecture;
- the bolt is less cleanly diagrammatic;
- figures are absent or less central;
- the crown-like top and paired tumblers are not yet the defining formula.

### Cary sheet, circa 1500

**[Documented fact]** This uncut printer’s sheet in the Cary Collection at Yale is normally assigned to Milan around 1500. It contains Marseille-like Pope, Emperor, Temperance, Star, Moon, and Tower imagery. [Cary-sheet survey](https://foolmade.art/archive/cary-sheet)

Its Tower is the most important early precursor of the Marseille card:

- a high masonry structure;
- a celestial or fiery impact;
- damage at the summit;
- continuity with the Star–Moon imagery that follows it.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** The sheet supports a northern Italian, probably Milanese ancestry for much of the later Marseille design. It does not prove that every feature originated in Milan, nor that French makers merely copied a single Italian model. [Survey of the Milan–Marseille evidence](https://tarot-heritage.com/history-4/italian-tarot-in-the-15th-century/)

## Sola Busca, 1491 and later coloring

**[Documented fact]** The Sola Busca is the earliest surviving substantially complete engraved 78-card tarot. Its trumps portray named ancient or pseudo-ancient warriors and rulers rather than the familiar medieval allegories. Trump XVI is therefore not a conventional lightning-struck Tower.

Its importance for the Tower is indirect:

- Pamela Colman Smith demonstrably or very probably used Sola Busca compositions for several Waite-Smith pip cards after examples were displayed at the British Museum in 1908.
- No comparably specific Sola Busca prototype for her Tower has been demonstrated.
- It proves that fully illustrated pip cards existed long before the Waite-Smith deck, but not that Smith copied its Tower.

## Mantegna “tarocchi”

**[Documented fact]** The so-called Mantegna Tarocchi are not a tarot deck and were not made by Mantegna. They are fifteenth-century educational engravings in five groups of ten, representing social estates, Apollo and the Muses, liberal arts, virtues, and cosmological spheres.

No Tower trump exists in the series. They matter only as evidence for the wider Renaissance culture of serial allegorical images.

## Minchiate

**[Documented fact]** Minchiate, also called *Germini*, expanded the trump series to forty numbered trumps plus the Fool. It added the four elements, twelve zodiacal signs, and virtues.

The Tower-corresponding image is generally a **House of the Devil**, burning building, or hell-mouth subject:

- a nude or scantily clothed man and woman flee a flaming building;
- some versions emphasize an open doorway resembling the entrance to Hell;
- the celestial lightning of Marseille is less important than interior fire and escape.

This is not merely Marseille XVI moved unchanged. It preserves the Italian *Casa del Diavolo/Inferno* branch of the subject.

In play it is an ordinary numbered trump. Minchiate’s principal high bonuses belong to the *arie*, not to this middle-ranking image. [Minchiate rules and pack structure](https://www.pagat.com/tarot/minchiate.html)

## Bolognese tarot / Tarocchino

**[Documented fact]** In the reduced 62-card Bolognese pack used for Ottocento, the Tower is trump **15**, not XVI. The Star is 16. Below these come the other numbered trumps, four equal-ranking Moors, and the Bégato. [Ottocento rules](https://www.pagat.com/tarot/ottocento.html)

The image belongs to the lightning/fire tradition. Because the four papal figures became four equal-rank Moors and the series was compressed, visual correspondence and printed number no longer match the Marseille count exactly.

## Sicilian tarot

**[Documented fact]** The Sicilian pack has twenty numbered trumps plus the unnumbered Miseria and a Fool called *Fuggitivo/Fujutu*. Its sequence and imagery are highly regional. The high *arie* are Jupiter, World, Sun, Moon, and Star. [Sicilian rules](https://www.pagat.com/tarot/sicilian.html)

A simple one-to-one Marseille Tower is not structurally secure in the surviving Sicilian pattern. Claims that “Sicilian XVI is the Tower” are wrong: XVI is the Star in the modern traditional pack. Sicilian imagery is evidence for a southern tarot family, not a numbered copy of Marseille.

## Jacques Viéville, Paris, circa 1650

Viéville’s pack is contemporary with Noblet but does not use the canonical Marseille Tower:

- a tree is struck by lightning or exposed to a celestial discharge;
- a shepherd and flock appear below;
- the title is **La Fouldre**.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** This may preserve the “Lightning” identity independently of tower architecture. Later Belgian cards similarly use a lightning-struck tree.

## Jean Noblet, Paris, circa 1650

This is the earliest securely surviving card normally classed within the Tarot de Marseille tradition to bear the title **LA MAISON DIEV**.

Depiction:

- a flesh- or brick-colored tower;
- its crown-like summit is lifted or blown aside;
- a flame or bolt connects it to a solar/celestial source;
- two human figures are falling or tumbling;
- colored pellets, sparks, masonry, or droplets fill the air.

Noblet’s image is ambiguous about the direction of the fire. It can appear to rise from the tower toward the sky rather than descend as lightning. That ambiguity matters: “struck by lightning” is partly a later reading of a stylized shape.

## Jean Dodal, Lyon, circa 1701–1715

Dodal consolidates the Type-I Marseille form:

- **LA MAISON DIEV**;
- tower with crown-like top displaced;
- two figures pitched toward the ground;
- multicolored round or flame-shaped particles;
- a strong plume joining tower and sky.

The two people are not securely identifiable as king and queen, Nimrod and minister, Adam and Eve, or builders. Those are later readings.

## Pierre Madenié, Dijon, 1709

The earliest complete surviving Type-II Marseille pack helps establish a second graphic line. The Tower remains XVI and *Maison Dieu*, but contours, facial positions, debris, and flame direction differ.

## Nicolas Conver, Marseille, 1760/1761

Conver’s engraving became one of the principal ancestors of later commercial Marseille packs.

Changes:

- the descending interpretation of the flame becomes visually easier;
- the celestial disk is reduced or obscured;
- the crown-like cap is strongly detached;
- one figure is inverted; the other appears to fall or brace near the base;
- colored droplets/debris are regularized.

**[Tradition]** Modern viewers routinely call the flying top “the crown,” interpreting it as overthrown sovereignty or ego. Architecturally, it can simply be crenellated roofing or a turret cap; its crown significance is not documented by Conver.

## Grimaud / Paul Marteau, 1930

The Grimaud *Ancien Tarot de Marseille* regularized and recolored the Conver tradition for twentieth-century mass circulation.

Paul Marteau interpreted the tower as an erroneous human construction enclosing consciousness. The card’s flesh color encouraged a body-as-building reading. These meanings belong to twentieth-century occult commentary, not the seventeenth-century cardmakers.

## Camoin–Jodorowsky, 1997

Philippe Camoin and Alejandro Jodorowsky advertised their deck as a restoration of the Marseille tarot.

Changes include:

- highly systematized color symbolism;
- a conspicuous opening or door at the tower’s base;
- restoration or invention of minute marks from comparison among old decks;
- an optimistic reading in which the tower opens and releases creative energy rather than simply collapsing.

**[Disputed claim]** Calling this the recovery of a single original, complete Tarot de Marseille overstates the evidence. The historical packs contain incompatible lineages and printing variations.

**[Modern esoteric reading]** Jodorowsky and Marianne Costa call XVI *La Maison Dieu*, the “House/God,” and read the figures as acrobatic, celebratory, or working with the earth rather than necessarily falling to their deaths. In their words, the card brings “great spiritual comfort.” [Jodorowsky and Costa, *The Way of Tarot*](https://irp-cdn.multiscreensite.com/24669b51/files/uploaded/The_Way_of_Tarot%20%281%29.pdf)

## Etteilla’s Grand Etteilla, circa 1788–1789

Etteilla reorganized the whole deck as an Egyptianizing oracle. The traditional Tower subject became card **19**, not XVI.

Common titles or associations:

- **Misère** / Poverty or Misery;
- **Prison** in the reversed orientation;
- the Capitol, castle, House of God, or Tower of Babel in later Etteilla-family descriptions.

The card shows a tall building, tower, or classical structure under destructive force. Upright and reversed keywords were printed at opposite ends.

**[Documented fact]** Etteilla’s was the first tarot designed from the outset as an occult and cartomantic instrument, with meanings and correspondences built into the cards. [Public Domain Review facsimile](https://publicdomainreview.org/collection/etteilla-thot)

**[Caution]** “1789” is the conventional publication date; advertisements and surviving states point to a process spanning approximately 1788–1789.

## Court de Gébelin’s redrawn tarot, 1781

Gébelin calls XVI the **Château de Plutus**, Castle of Plutus, treating *Maison Dieu* as a corrupt survival of Egyptian doctrine.

**[Legend/esoteric attribution]** He asserted an Egyptian origin but supplied no Egyptian card, text, or archaeological chain of transmission.

## Éliphas Lévi

Lévi did not create a complete published deck in *Dogme et Rituel*. His scheme placed Hebrew letters against the trumps according to a French sequence that differs from the Golden Dawn’s later correspondences.

For XVI the French occult line uses **Ayin**, not Peh. Lévi and followers associate it with the Tower of Babel, Nimrod, punishment of pride, reversal, or subversion.

## Oswald Wirth, 1889

Wirth, working under Stanislas de Guaita, published 22 occult trumps as *Les 22 Arcanes du Tarot Kabbalistique*.

His XVI is **LE FEU DU CIEL**:

- a masonry tower;
- a crown-like battlement;
- a golden bolt descending from a cloud;
- two falling men;
- debris or yod-like flames;
- the Hebrew letter **Ayin**, following the French occult rather than Golden Dawn arrangement.

Wirth’s title transfers attention from the building to the destructive celestial force. His 1926 revision further systematized color and symbolism. [BnF-copy overview](https://foolmade.art/archive/wirth-tarot)

## Golden Dawn manuscript designs

The Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn did not publish one canonical commercial deck. Members were expected to copy or paint their own cards from descriptions.

The Tower was called:

- **The Blasted Tower**;
- **The Lightning-Struck Tower**;
- **Lord of the Hosts of the Mighty**.

Its essential Golden Dawn symbolism is:

- a tower struck by lightning;
- crown or summit cast down;
- falling figures;
- Mars;
- Peh;
- destruction that is also purgation and liberation.

Robert Wang’s *Golden Dawn Tarot* (1978, with Israel Regardie’s involvement) and Chic and Sandra Cicero’s later Golden Dawn decks are reconstructions, not photographic reproductions of one surviving original master pack. They differ in coloration and detail while following Order papers.

## Waite-Smith, 1909

Artist: **Pamela Colman Smith**.  
Conceptual direction and book: **Arthur Edward Waite**.  
First published by William Rider & Son in December 1909; Waite’s explanatory book followed in 1910.

Depiction:

- a gray tower on a steep, isolated rocky height;
- a black night sky;
- a jagged yellow lightning bolt strikes the crown;
- the crown flies away;
- flames burst from three windows;
- two figures fall, one crowned and one in a cloak;
- 22 small yellow flame-forms or yods appear in groups of ten and twelve.

The 22 marks are commonly understood as Hebrew yods and as the 22 letters/trumps or paths. Paul Foster Case explicitly made that interpretation; Waite’s published description does not plainly enumerate it.

**Sources and transformations**

- The basic tower, crown, bolt, and two falling figures descend through Marseille and Wirth.
- The mountainous isolation and fully nocturnal catastrophe intensify the scene.
- Smith makes the lightning unambiguously descend.
- The figures are airborne rather than ambiguously tumbling or touching the earth.
- The composition turns a damaged but standing Marseille building into an unmistakable disaster.

**[Disputed source claim]** Smith’s knowledge of Sola Busca helps explain several minor-arcana scenes, but there is no demonstrated Sola Busca Tower source. Wirth and Marseille are much closer precedents.

## Paul Foster Case / BOTA

Case’s design, executed in BOTA form by Jessie Burns Parke, closely revises Waite-Smith:

- Peh and Mars are explicit;
- the tower represents false personal knowledge;
- lightning is spiritual comprehension;
- the falling figures are self-consciousness and subconsciousness;
- the discarded crown is false personal will;
- 22 yods are the 22 modes of consciousness.

Case calls the developmental stage **Awakening**. His table gives Peh, Mars, red, the musical note C, iron, and gematria 80. [BOTA’s official presentation](https://botaineurope.org/the-tarot/16-the-tower)

## Crowley–Harris Thoth Tarot, painted 1938–1943; published 1969

Artist: **Frieda Harris**, under Aleister Crowley’s close direction.

Title: **XVI The Tower**, alternatively **War**.

Depiction and Crowley’s explanations:

- a central tower is blasted open by fire;
- people become geometric, disintegrating figures;
- a huge eye is the **Eye of Horus** and also the **Eye of Shiva**;
- the Tower is broken by flames, lightning, and engines of war;
- at lower right, the **jaws of Dis** or hell-mouth belch fire;
- a dove with olive branch and a lion-serpent or serpent appear in the radiance;
- the card’s spiral construction conveys eruptive force;
- Peh, “mouth,” and Mars govern the card.

Crowley writes that it represents “the manifestation of cosmic energy in its grossest form” and “the destruction of existing material by fire.” These phrases appear in *The Book of Thoth*, the section on Atu XVI. [Online text](https://hermetic.com/crowley/book-of-thoth/atu)

The dove and serpent unite apparently contrary forces. Crowley relates them to Liber AL II:26 and to annihilation as ecstatic union. The figures are not rescued occupants; they are the old aeon’s garrison reduced to geometry.

## Notable modern redesigns

### Salvador Dalí Universal Tarot, 1984

Dalí retained the Tower catastrophe inside a collage-based Surrealist and art-historical system. The deck belongs to twentieth-century fine-art tarot rather than a historical gaming pattern.

### Niki de Saint Phalle Tarot Garden, begun 1979; opened 1998

The Tarot Garden in Tuscany translates the trumps into monumental inhabitable sculptures. The Tower becomes architectural environment rather than printed emblem. Its ruptured form participates in the garden’s passage from psychic enclosure to transformation.

### Robert M. Place, *Alchemical Tarot*, 1995 onward

Place recasts the trump through early-modern alchemical visual language. This is a deliberate modern synthesis: historically informed but not evidence that the fifteenth-century Tower was originally an alchemical furnace.

### Osho Zen Tarot, 1994

Renames the card **Thunderbolt**, emphasizing sudden breakthrough and ego-shattering awakening. This is a modern Buddhist-inflected recontextualization.

### Jodorowsky–Camoin, 1997

As above, presents a celebratory, opening, energy-releasing Tower. It has been especially influential in Spanish- and French-language therapeutic tarot.

# The game

## What kind of card is it?

**[Documented fact]** The Tower began as a trump in a trick-taking card game. It was not originally a “Major Arcana” used for fortune-telling.

A traditional tarot pack contains:

- 21 ordinary trumps;
- the Fool;
- four 14-card Italian suits.

“Major Arcana” is occult terminology popularized much later, especially through Paul Christian and subsequent French writers.

## Rank

In a standard Marseille/Milanese-style numbered order, XVI:

- beats trumps I–XV;
- loses to XVII–XXI;
- beats every ordinary suit card when legally played as trump;
- has no intrinsic divinatory function in the game.

Regional exceptions matter:

- **Bologna:** the Tower is numbered 15 because of the reduced and reordered trump sequence.
- **Minchiate:** the cognate House-of-the-Devil image belongs to an expanded 40-trump order.
- **Sicily:** XVI is the Star, not the Tower.
- **Piedmont:** XVI is the Tower, but in some games trump XX outranks XXI.
- **Central European Tarock:** the old picture often disappears; XVI is simply an ordinary numbered tarock.

## Point value

In most tarot-family games, middle trumps such as XVI are “empty” or low-counting cards:

- they win according to rank;
- they generally count only the minimal card-point value;
- they do not have the special point status of the highest trump, lowest trump/Pagat, or Fool/Excuse.

In common three-card counting systems, a low trump contributes one nominal point before deductions or grouping. Exact arithmetic varies by game. [Overview of tarot counting systems](https://www.pagat.com/tarot/counting.html)

In Bolognese Ottocento, Tower 15 participates in sequences and combinations but is not one of the primary five-point *tarocchi*. The special high-value cards are Angel, World, Bégato, Matto, and the kings.

## Fool’s role

**[Documented fact]** The Fool was historically usually unnumbered and normally functioned as the **Excuse**:

- it could be played instead of following suit;
- it did not ordinarily win the trick;
- its owner recovered it after the trick, often surrendering another low card;
- special last-trick rules varied.

This is not equivalent to the modern Joker, although analogy encouraged later confusion.

## Three regional trump orders

Michael Dummett classified early Italian orders broadly as:

| Order | Region | Major traits |
|---|---|---|
| Type A | Bologna and much of central/southern tradition | Angel above World; papal trumps could become equal-ranking; virtues arranged in the Bolognese manner |
| Type B | Ferrara | Order reflected in *Sermones de ludo*; distinctive placement of virtues; Angel normally at the top |
| Type C | Milan and western tradition | World above Angel/Judgement in the line ancestral to Marseille; Justice VIII and Strength XI in the eventual Marseille sequence |

The final catastrophe/celestial block is comparatively stable: Death, Devil, Tower/Fire, Star, Moon, Sun, Judgement/Angel, World, though World and Angel may reverse and exact numbers shift.

A translated discussion of Dummett’s ordering identifies the final segment and the regional A/B/C division. [Dummett, *Il Mondo e l’Angelo*, translated discussion](https://dummettsmondo.blogspot.com/2015/07/chapters-6-and-7-game-and-its-trump.html)

## Justice and Strength

- Marseille/Type C: Justice VIII, Strength XI.
- Waite-Smith: Strength VIII, Justice XI.
- Crowley follows the exchanged numerical positions but renames them Adjustment VIII and Lust XI.

The swap was made to align the zodiacal series with Golden Dawn correspondences. It does not alter Tower XVI.

# Chronology of attestation

## Circa 1418–1425: Marziano da Tortona

**[Documented fact]** Marziano describes Filippo Maria Visconti’s classical-god card game. It demonstrates a precursor environment for trump games.

**Not attested:** no Tower, no standard 22-trump sequence, no Peh, Mars, divination, or Egyptian doctrine.

## 1440: Giusto Giusti record

**[Documented fact]** A Florentine diary records a specially made pack of *naibi a trionfi* for Sigismondo Pandolfo Malatesta. This is now among the earliest direct documentary uses of *trionfi* for such cards.

No Tower is named.

## 10 February 1442: Ferrara ledger

The Este wardrobe account pays Jacopo Sagramoro for coloring four packs of trump cards, including cups, swords, coins, batons, and “all the figures.”

**[Documented fact]** This is an early, explicit *trionfi* account.

**Not attested:** no individual trumps are named, so it does not prove what the Tower looked like. The account is quoted in Helen Farley’s historical study. [Farley, *A Cultural History of Tarot*](https://e-isomero.com/pdf/book/1611846398.pdf)

## 1440s–1460s: Milanese and Ferrarese painted packs

Visconti, Brambilla, Cary-Yale, Morgan-Bergamo, and Este fragments establish the courtly card tradition.

No securely surviving Tower belongs to the principal Visconti groups.

## Late fifteenth century, often circa 1470–1500: *Sermones de ludo cum aliis*

This anonymous anti-gambling sermon supplies the oldest surviving complete named list of the 21 trumps and condemns them as a ladder descending into Hell.

The card is called **la Sagitta**.

**[Documented fact]** This proves that a Bolt/Arrow trump existed in the standard sequence by the manuscript’s date.

**[Dating caution]** “Circa 1470” is conventional but not certain; modern archival cataloguing allows a broader circa 1450–1500 range. The manuscript’s ordering is Type B and points toward Ferrara. [Vanderbilt manuscript record and transcription](https://collections.library.vanderbilt.edu/repositories/2/archival_objects/261035)

## 1490s–early 1500s: printed sheets

Rosenwald, Budapest–Met, Rothschild, and Cary-sheet evidence shows the visual subject branching among:

- burning fortress;
- celestial fire;
- falling people;
- damaged summit;
- hell-house;
- lightning.

## 1491: Sola Busca

Complete engraved pack, but not a conventional Tower image.

## Circa 1500: tarot poems

**[Documented fact]** Renaissance *tarocchi appropriati* poems assign trumps to people, usually for literary or social play. They are evidence of card names and metaphorical use, not systematic divination.

Tower-related names emphasize fire, ruin, prison, punishment, or celestial violence according to region.

## 1520s: Teofilo Folengo

Folengo’s macaronic writing mentions or plays upon tarot figures and the term *fuoco*. It helps document the card’s Renaissance name but is not an occult manual.

## Sixteenth century: Piscina, Pomeran, Garzoni, Aretino

These authors attest tarot as game, moral allegory, satire, or cultural object. Their Tower names include Fire, House, Pluto’s house, and destructive dwelling.

## Circa 1650: Noblet and Viéville

- Noblet: *Maison Dieu*, tower, displaced crown/top, falling figures.
- Viéville: *Fouldre*, lightning-struck tree with shepherd/flock.

## 1701–1715: Dodal

The Marseille Tower formula stabilizes.

## 1760/1761: Conver

Conver’s design becomes the basis for major modern Marseille editions.

## 1781: Court de Gébelin and Comte de Mellet

Gébelin declares tarot Egyptian; de Mellet proposes a Hebrew-letter sequence and a method of divination.

For the Tower:

- Gébelin: Castle of Plutus.
- De Mellet: expulsion or fall from terrestrial paradise, with celestial fire/comet imagery.
- Hebrew-letter correspondence belongs to an early French occult order, not the later Golden Dawn arrangement.

## 1783–1785: Etteilla’s tarot books

Etteilla develops the first systematic published tarot cartomancy, astrology, upright/reversed meanings, and Egyptian chronology.

## Circa 1788–1789: Grand Etteilla

Tower becomes card 19, Misery/Prison.

## 1854–1856: Éliphas Lévi, *Dogme et Rituel de la Haute Magie*

Lévi synthesizes tarot, Hebrew alphabet, magic, Kabbalah, and occult initiation. His 22-chapter structure parallels the trumps.

Tower receives the French occult Ayin/Babel/subversion complex, not Peh/Mars.

## 1863 and 1870: Paul Christian

In *L’Homme rouge des Tuileries* and *Histoire de la magie*, Christian invents an elaborate Egyptian initiation through 22 arcana beneath the Sphinx.

**[Modern invention of its day]** No ancient Egyptian source describes this initiation. Christian helped popularize *arcane* as the name for a tarot trump.

## 1888: Mathers

Mathers publishes *The Tarot: Its Occult Signification, Use in Fortune-Telling, and Method of Play*.

His printed table retains the French-style **Ayin** for the Tower. His divinatory title is *The Lightning-Struck Tower*.

## 1888–1890s: Golden Dawn

Order papers reorganize the correspondences:

- Tower → Peh;
- Peh → Mars;
- Path 27;
- Lord of the Hosts of the Mighty.

## 1889: Papus and Wirth

Papus publishes *Le Tarot des Bohémiens*; Wirth’s 22-card occult deck appears. Both spread the French Kabbalistic Tower, Ayin, Capricorn, Fall-of-Adam system.

## 1909–1910: Waite-Smith

Smith makes the catastrophe visually unequivocal; Waite supplies Christian and occult commentary but suppresses much explicit Golden Dawn apparatus.

## 1930–1931: Marteau and Case

- Marteau standardizes a French commercial Marseille tradition.
- Case publishes his BOTA formulation: Peh, Mars, Awakening, spiritual lightning.

## 1938–1943; 1944; 1969: Thoth

- Harris paints the cards, 1938–1943.
- Crowley publishes *The Book of Thoth*, 1944.
- The full deck is commercially published in 1969.

## 1978 onward

Wang, Cicero, Pollack, Greer, Jodorowsky, Place, and numerous modern designers shift the Tower from external catastrophe toward psychic breakthrough, liberation, trauma, individuation, and the collapse of false structures.

### Pip decans and courts

The requested pip-decan and court-zodiac information does not apply directly to the Tower:

- A trump has a letter and planetary attribution.
- Numbered minors receive decans and *Book T* titles.

The Tower therefore has **no pip decan** and **no court-card sub-element or zodiacal span**. “Lord of the Hosts of the Mighty” is its trump title, not a decan title.

## Tzaddi/Heh swap

Crowley’s “Tzaddi is not the Star” revision affects:

- Emperor IV;
- Star XVII;
- Hebrew letters Heh and Tzaddi.

It does **not** change the Tower’s Peh/Mars attribution. Peh remains Tower XVI. Any table moving Tower because of the Emperor/Star controversy is erroneous.

# Divinatory meanings by author

Quotations are kept short because the relevant modern editions remain copyrighted.

## Etteilla

In the Grand Etteilla line the former Tower is card 19.

- Upright: **Misère**, poverty, misery, distress.
- Reversed: **Prison**, confinement, captivity.

Later Grand Etteilla printings sometimes reverse which end is treated as upright depending on deck orientation. The words printed on the card are firmer evidence than later generalized lists. [Grand Etteilla card 19](https://www.queenoftarot.com/tarot_cards/451)

## S. L. MacGregor Mathers, 1888

Mathers writes:

- Upright: “Ruin, Disruption, Over-throw, Loss, Bankruptcy.”
- Reversed: “These in a more or less partial degree.”

Edition: S. L. MacGregor Mathers, *The Tarot: Its Occult Signification, Use in Fortune-Telling, and Method of Play* (London: George Redway, 1888), meaning table, approximately pp. 13–14 in digitized pagination. [1888 text](https://www.howardnema.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/12/the-tarot-1888-by-macgregor-mathers-1.pdf)

## Golden Dawn, *Book T*

Commonly transmitted meanings:

- ambition;
- fighting;
- war;
- courage;
- destruction, danger, fall, and ruin when ill-dignified or in hostile combinations.

Golden Dawn manuscripts generally emphasized elemental dignity and surrounding cards rather than a simple modern upright/reversed binary.

## Arthur Edward Waite, 1910

Waite’s divinatory list:

- Upright: “Misery, distress, indigence, adversity, calamity, disgrace, deception, ruin.”
- Additional sense: unforeseen catastrophe.
- Reversed: according to one transmitted line, the same evils “in a lesser degree”; Waite also lists oppression, imprisonment, and tyranny in editions following his full table.

His symbolic essay interprets the image as destruction of a false “House of Doctrine.” He says the persons are the literal word made void and its false interpretation.

Edition: A. E. Waite, *The Pictorial Key to the Tarot* (London: William Rider & Son, 1910), Part II, XVI, and Part III divinatory list. [Waite’s Tower chapter](https://staging.sacred-texts.com/book/the-pictorial-key-to-the-tarot/reader/xvi-the-tower)

## Aleister Crowley, 1944

Crowley does not recommend ordinary reversed-card meanings for the Thoth deck. The card’s range is controlled by dignity and context.

Central meanings:

- quarrel;
- combat;
- danger;
- ruin;
- destruction;
- escape from prison;
- destruction of old structures;
- revolutionary or martial force;
- ecstatic annihilation.

His key statement is that the card shows “the destruction of existing material by fire.” Edition: Crowley, *The Book of Thoth* (London: O.T.O., 1944), Atu XVI discussion; pagination varies, commonly around pp. 107–109. [Full section](https://hermetic.com/crowley/book-of-thoth/atu)

## Paul Foster Case

Case shifts emphasis from prediction to initiation:

- sudden overthrow;
- collapse of false knowledge;
- awakening;
- illumination;
- destruction of the illusion of separate personality;
- Mars as the force that breaks fixed form.

Case’s *Oracle of the Tarot* retains practical associations with sudden change and the Babel story, while BOTA’s later teaching stresses Awakening. [Case, *Oracle of the Tarot*](https://media-01.imu.nl/storage/divona.be/17547/oracle-of-tarot-paul-foster-case.pdf)

## Rachel Pollack

In *Seventy-Eight Degrees of Wisdom* (1980), Pollack reads the Tower as:

- explosion of repressed energy;
- collapse of rigid ego structure;
- liberating destruction;
- revelation that may be painful because prior defenses were entrenched;
- sexual and Kundalini imagery, including the tower as phallic structure and lightning as release.

Pollack’s crucial historical role is not inventing “change,” but making a positive or emancipatory reading central to late-twentieth-century English-language tarot. She normally treats reversal contextually rather than merely negating the upright card: resistance to release, confinement within the old structure, or a less visibly external crisis.

## Mary K. Greer

In *The Complete Book of Tarot Reversals* (2002), Greer gathers both traditional and psychological possibilities.

Upright range:

- calamity;
- catastrophe;
- destruction;
- ruin;
- anger;
- breakdown;
- breakthrough;
- sudden release;
- overthrow of established structures.

Reversed range includes:

- traditional “lesser degree” of ruin;
- imprisonment, oppression, entrapment;
- internal turmoil;
- blocked or delayed breakthrough;
- projection of destructiveness onto others;
- bodily eruption, surgery, or crisis;
- Kundalini or shamanic breakthrough.

Greer explicitly records the traditional reversed formula: “To a lesser degree: ruin, disruption, overthrow, loss.” [Digitized extract](https://www.scribd.com/doc/271576647/Mary-k-Greer-Complete-Book-of-Tarot-Reversals)

# Esoteric and symbolic readings

## Christian: Babel and punishment of pride

### Evidence

The Tower of Babel was a ubiquitous Christian image long before tarot. Genesis 11 supplies:

- humanity constructing upward;
- divine intervention;
- collapse of unified speech and social project;
- pride or presumption in later exegesis.

### Tarot history

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** Babel is an obvious possible source for a ruined tower under celestial judgment. It need not have been the sole source.

**[Esoteric tradition]** Gébelin, Lévi, Christian, Wirth, Waite, and many later authors explicitly spread the Babel interpretation.

**[Unproved detail]** The two figures are sometimes called Nimrod and his minister. No early card labels them.

## Harrowing of Hell and Hell-mouth

Medieval drama and art represented Hell as:

- a fortress;
- a flaming building;
- a monstrous mouth;
- a prison broken by Christ.

The Minchiate House of the Devil and the Thoth jaws of Dis belong to, or consciously revive, this image family.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** The sequence Devil → Tower/Fire → celestial lights can be read as descent into Hell followed by ascent. This coheres with medieval salvation imagery but is not explained in a fifteenth-century rulebook.

## Apocalypse and fall of Babylon

Revelation supplies falling cities, fire from heaven, divine judgment, and ruined worldly power.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** Robert O’Neill and other iconologists compare the high-trump sequence with Apocalyptic imagery. The comparison is stronger as a field of available motifs than as proof of one exact manuscript source. [O’Neill, “Iconology of the Tower Cards”](https://www.tarot.com/tarot/robert-oneill/tower-cards)

## Alchemy

Possible alchemical readings include:

- the athanor or furnace;
- calcination;
- breaking the vessel;
- release of spirit from matter;
- lightning as secret fire;
- dissolution before recombination.

**[Chronology]** These readings flourish principally after Lévi, Wirth, nineteenth-century occult synthesis, and twentieth-century designers such as Robert Place.

**[Caution]** A burning building resembles a furnace, but resemblance is not documentary proof that the Renaissance card was designed as an alchemical emblem.

## Kabbalah

### French occult school

- Tower XVI → **Ayin**.
- Papus calls Ayin materialized Vau and connects it with sensory, curved, false, or perverse materiality.
- Astrology: Capricorn.
- Meaning: Adam’s fall into matter.

Papus writes that the card “represents the material fall of Adam.” Edition: Papus, *Le Tarot des Bohémiens* (Paris, 1889), XVI, approximately pp. 196–200 in the French edition. [French scan](https://www.cercle-papus.com/ouvrages/papus-le-tarot-des-bohemiens.pdf)

### Golden Dawn school

- Tower XVI → **Peh**.
- Peh = mouth.
- Mars.
- Path 27, Netzach–Hod.
- Speech, force, eruption, conflict, and breaking form.

These systems conflict because the French occultists generally marched Hebrew letters through the trump order while relocating the Fool near the end, whereas the Golden Dawn reorganized the sequence around *Sepher Yetzirah*’s elemental, planetary, and zodiacal classes.

## Astrology

### Early cards

No evidence assigns a planet to the Tower in fifteenth-century gaming tarot.

### Papus/Wirth

Capricorn, through Ayin in their French alphabetic scheme.

### Golden Dawn onward

Mars, through Peh as one of the seven double letters.

Mars became dominant in English-language esoteric tarot because Waite, Case, and Crowley inherited Golden Dawn structure.

## Hermetic reading

The Tower becomes:

- fixed form shattered by living force;
- solve preceding coagula;
- the Word or Mouth breaking false intellectual structures;
- lightning flash as sudden gnosis;
- macrocosmic energy invading a closed microcosm.

This synthesis is nineteenth- and twentieth-century Hermeticism, not an attested meaning of the earliest playing card.

## Jodorowsky’s “House/God”

Jodorowsky and Costa argue:

- the tower is not destroyed;
- its top opens like a crown;
- colored elements descend or ascend as spiritual gifts;
- the figures may dance, work, or touch the earth;
- treasure or golden energy emerges;
- the card can mean joyful release, construction, or a move into material reality.

**Status:** influential modern esoteric invention based on close visual play with Marseille printing, not a recovered medieval commentary.

## Jungian and psychological readings

Jung did not write a canonical card-by-card tarot manual. “Jungian tarot” was developed by later therapists, occultists, and writers.

Typical correspondences:

- tower = ego structure or persona;
- lightning = autonomous unconscious content;
- fall = collapse of inflation;
- two figures = conscious and unconscious attitudes;
- catastrophe = enantiodromia, the reversal produced when a one-sided position reaches an extreme;
- rebuilding = individuation after disintegration;
- “shadow” material breaks through an inadequate identity.

Important spreaders include Sallie Nichols, Rachel Pollack, Mary K. Greer, and numerous post-1960 therapeutic tarot writers.

**[Modern invention]** The popular phrase “the universe removes what no longer serves you” is a recent New Age formulation, not Waite, Lévi, or a medieval meaning.

## Italo Calvino

*Il castello dei destini incrociati* appeared first in a limited art-book form in 1969 and in expanded book form in 1973.

- “The Tavern of Crossed Destinies” uses a Marseille pack.

The Tower functions as a narrative machine for catastrophe, burning castles, violent reversals, and broken order. Calvino does not submit to one traditional divinatory definition; he exploits image combinations.

## T. S. Eliot, *The Waste Land*, 1922

Eliot explicitly invokes a “wicked pack of cards,” Madame Sosostris, the Hanged Man, Wheel, and Three of Staves.

**Important correction:** he does **not explicitly draw or name the Tower card**.

The poem later presents falling towers—Jerusalem, Athens, Alexandria, Vienna, and London—and critics have compared this with Tower symbolism. That is a plausible literary parallel, not a documented use of card XVI.

Eliot knew Waite’s writings, but invented or altered several cards for the poem. A scholarly discussion notes that only four of Sosostris’s cards belong to the conventional pack. [Knox, “Between Fire and Fire”](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/heyj.12240)

## W. B. Yeats

Yeats was a Golden Dawn initiate and used towers extensively in poetry and autobiography, particularly after acquiring Thoor Ballylee.

**[Documented fact]** Tarot and Golden Dawn symbolism formed part of his intellectual world.

**[Disputed literary inference]** Yeats’s tower is sometimes identified directly with Atu XVI. His tower also functions as refuge, aristocratic lineage, poetic self, winding stair, and spiritual axis—often almost the opposite of the blasted Tower. A Tower-card resonance is possible but should not replace Yeats’s documented architectural and poetic meanings.

## Jodorowsky’s films

Jodorowsky’s *The Holy Mountain* (1973), *El Topo* (1970), and later psychomagical work use initiatory, alchemical, and tarot-like imagery.

No single scene should automatically be called a direct quotation of Tower XVI without production evidence. The destruction of false masters, structures, and identities fits Jodorowsky’s later Tower reading, but much of that comparison is retrospective.

## Ingmar Bergman

Bergman’s *The Seventh Seal* (1957) quotes medieval chess, Dance of Death, plague, confession, and apocalyptic imagery.

**Finding:** no dependable source was found establishing that Bergman deliberately quoted the Tower tarot card. Visual similarity between medieval catastrophe and Tower XVI is insufficient evidence.

## Visual art and architecture

### Niki de Saint Phalle’s Tarot Garden

The strongest documented architectural transformation of tarot imagery. The artist made monumental sculptures corresponding to the trumps in Garavicchio, Tuscany. Visitors enter and move around a tarot environment.

### “La Torre Invertida,” Madrid, 2024

La Casa Encendida’s exhibition treated tarot as artistic form and countercultural symbol, including works by Agnès Varda, Andy Warhol, Betye Saar, Dorothy Iannone, Niki de Saint Phalle, and Suzanne Treister. Its title directly invokes the reversed Tower. [Exhibition discussion in *El País*](https://elpais.com/cultura/2024-11-07/del-loco-al-mundo-el-reverso-cultural-del-tarot-mucho-mas-que-videntes-y-adivinacion.html)

### Other modern art decks

Dalí, Leonora Carrington, H. R. Giger, Suzanne Treister, and Robert Place transform tarot imagery, but each belongs to modern reception. Their work cannot be used backward as evidence for fifteenth-century meaning.

# Controversies and disputes

## Egyptian origin

### Claim

Court de Gébelin asserted in 1781 that tarot was an Egyptian book preserved in pictures. He believed *tar* and *ro* could be explained through purported Egyptian roots and treated the pack as the Book of Thoth.

### Evidence

- No ancient Egyptian tarot cards survive.
- No Egyptian text describes the 22-trump sequence.
- The card suits, social authorities, Christian virtues, and documented terminology are medieval/Renaissance European.
- The first tarot records cluster in northern Italy in the fifteenth century.

### Classification

**[Legend]** as literal history.  
**[Documented fact]** as the founding myth of occult tarot.

Gébelin’s original essay appears in *Le Monde primitif*, volume VIII, 1781, pp. 365–410. [French text and English translation](https://ldysinger.com/%40texts2/1776_Court_Masons_Tarot/02_Monde_Primitif.htm)

## Romani or “Gypsy” origin

### Claim

Romani people carried tarot from Egypt or India into Europe.

### Development

- Early modern Europeans wrongly called Romani “Egyptians.”
- Nineteenth-century authors including Vaillant and occult popularizers joined that stereotype to Gébelin’s Egyptian tarot.
- Papus’s title *Tarot des Bohémiens* gave the idea enormous circulation.

### Evidence

Romani linguistic and migration history points to Indian origins, but no evidence connects their arrival to the invention of tarot. European playing cards predate the first secure Romani presence in many relevant regions, and the first tarot records concern northern Italian courts and workshops.

### Classification

**[Legend]** as origin history.  
**[Documented fact]** that Romani fortune-tellers later used European cards and became associated with cartomancy.

## Cathar origin

### Claim

The trumps covertly encode Cathar dualism or preserve a persecuted Cathar scripture.

### Evidence problems

- The earliest tarot records postdate the Albigensian Crusade by roughly two centuries.
- No Cathar text lists tarot images or describes a card transmission.
- Pope, Papess, Christian virtues, Last Judgement, and imperial figures fit orthodox late-medieval civic-religious culture at least as readily as heresy.
- Tower/Devil imagery was common in orthodox Christian art.

### Classification

**[Disputed claim/modern legend]**. Iconographic parallels can be proposed card by card, but no transmission chain has been demonstrated.

## Templar origin

Claims that the Tower represents destruction of the Templars, the Temple of Solomon, Friday the thirteenth, or a concealed Templar initiation are modern speculative constructions.

No fifteenth-century source connects tarot with the Templars. The order was suppressed beginning in 1307, long before the earliest secure tarot records.

**Classification:** **[Legend]**.

## Tower of Babel identification

Unlike Egyptian, Romani, Cathar, and Templar origins, Babel is not chronologically impossible. Babel imagery was culturally available.

But:

- no early card labels the structure “Babel”;
- early names emphasize bolt, fire, Hell, Devil’s House, or House of God;
- Minchiate resembles a burning hell-house more than Genesis 11;
- the Marseille top may be opened by fire rather than cleanly struck by lightning.

**Classification:** **[Plausible scholarly reconstruction and later tradition]**, not documented original title.

## “Maison Dieu means hospital”

The linguistic premise is factual: *maison-Dieu* was used for hospitals and hospices.

The iconographic conclusion is unproved:

- the card shows a fortified tower, not an unmistakable hospital;
- no Noblet, Dodal, or Conver commentary explains the title;
- “hospital as place of death” is a modern inference.

**Classification:** **[Disputed claim]**.

## Lightning direction

In Noblet and Dodal, the flame can look as if it rises from the tower toward a sun or disk. In Conver and Waite-Smith, a descending strike is easier to see.

Therefore:

- “lightning strikes the tower” is certain for Waite-Smith;
- it is likely but visually ambiguous in early Marseille;
- some modern Marseille readers interpret the tower as opening and releasing flame upward.

## The crown

Possible readings:

- architectural cap;
- royal crown;
- pride;
- sovereignty;
- personal will;
- crown chakra.

Only the first two arise immediately from the picture. Pride was spread by Babel exegesis and Wirth/Waite. Personal will is especially Case. Crown-chakra language is modern.

## The two figures

Proposed identities include:

- anonymous inhabitants or builders;
- king and commoner;
- king and queen;
- Nimrod and minister;
- Adam and Eve;
- false prophet and literalist;
- self-consciousness and subconsciousness;
- masculine and feminine psychic functions.

No early title or caption identifies them. All named identifications are later interpretations.

## The 22 yods

Waite-Smith clearly contains 22 flame-like marks if counted as 12 and 10.

- Their shape resembles the Hebrew yod.
- Twenty-two naturally suggests letters, trumps, or paths.
- Case explicitly explains them as 22 modes of consciousness.

**Open question:** Waite did not plainly publish Smith’s intended explanation. It is safest to call the yod reading probable Golden Dawn symbolism, made explicit by Case, rather than a documented statement by Smith.

## Misprints and corruptions

### *Maison Dieu* versus *Maison de Dieu*

The historical card normally says *Maison Dieu/DIEV*, not *Maison de Dieu*. Modern authors sometimes treat the missing *de* as a secret metaphysical signal.

But early-modern card titles routinely use compressed grammar, inconsistent spelling, and woodblock abbreviations. The title alone cannot prove “House/God.”

### *Feu* versus *Foudre*

Regional blocks and worn inscriptions allow Fire, Lightning, and Bolt traditions to blur. Sometimes modern catalogues normalize a hard-to-read title into the expected one.

### Charles VI attribution

The luxury Italian cards once attributed to Charles VI and Jacquemin Gringonneur are later Italian cards. The traditional name survives as a cataloguing convenience.

### Visconti reconstruction cards

Modern commercial “complete Visconti-Sforza” decks supply newly painted Devil and Tower cards. These are reconstructions, not rediscovered Bembo originals. Reproductions sometimes fail to label them clearly.

### Rider versus Waite-Smith

Calling the 1909 deck simply “Rider” suppresses Pamela Colman Smith’s authorship. “Waite-Smith” or “Rider-Waite-Smith” is historically more informative.

## Dummett and historical correction

Michael Dummett’s central correction was methodological:

- establish tarot first as a family of card games;
- reconstruct regional rules and trump orders;
- date occult doctrines only when they appear in texts;
- do not infer ancient doctrine merely from later symbolic resemblance.

Ronald Decker, Thierry Depaulis, and Dummett traced French occult tarot from Gébelin through Etteilla, Lévi, and successors in *A Wicked Pack of Cards*. [WorldCat edition record](https://search.worldcat.org/title/A-wicked-pack-of-cards-%3A-the-origins-of-the-occult-tarot/oclc/36210146)

Dummett’s reconstruction has been refined by later discoveries—especially earlier documentary records—but its fundamental distinction between fifteenth-century game and eighteenth-century occult tarot remains supported by the evidence.

## Kaplan, Place, and Farley

- **Stuart Kaplan** assembled an immense documentary and visual corpus, though his encyclopedias sometimes reproduce inherited datings that later research revised.
- **Robert M. Place** restores Renaissance Christian, civic, Neoplatonic, and allegorical contexts while also offering his own spiritual readings.
- **Helen Farley** provides a cultural history emphasizing movement from entertainment to esotericism. Her della Torre hypothesis is imaginative but disputed.
- **Andrea Vitali** develops detailed Christian and Renaissance iconological parallels. These are strongest when he cites named texts and images and weaker when iconographic resemblance is treated as proof of design intention.

# Open questions

1. **What exactly did the earliest Tower look like?**  
   The sermon proves a Bolt/Arrow subject, but no securely identified Tower survives from the main Visconti groups. Printed witnesses around 1500 already show different solutions.

2. **Was Tower present in every earliest tarot pack?**  
   Probably in the sequence underlying the late-fifteenth-century sermon, but the variable Cary-Yale structure prevents certainty for every Milanese luxury pack.

3. **Which surviving object is the first Tower?**  
   This depends on contested dates and identifications among the BnF “Charles VI,” Rosenwald, Rothschild, Budapest–Met, and Cary-sheet material. “Noblet, 1650” is too late if the question is first image, but defensible as the first securely surviving titled Marseille Tower.

4. **Did the Marseille flame originally rise or descend?**  
   Early blocks remain visually ambiguous.

5. **Why *Maison Dieu*?**  
   House of God, hospital/hospice, euphemism for Devil’s House, printer’s corruption, or a deliberate paradox all remain possible. No cardmaker’s explanation survives.

6. **Who are the two people?**  
   Unknown.

7. **Are the colored pellets sparks, stones, hail, drops, yods, coins, or celestial seed?**  
   Old cardmakers do not say. Their visual ancestry may include both debris and conventional radiating droplets.

8. **Did Pamela Colman Smith intend exactly 22 yods?**  
   The count is real; the intended meaning is not documented in a surviving statement by Smith.

9. **Was the Tower originally Babel, Hell, a lightning-struck civic tower, or a general image of catastrophe?**  
   All were available cultural models. The diversity of early names may indicate that the image was multivalent from the beginning.

10. **Can alchemy be traced to the fifteenth-century card?**  
    Not presently. It is securely traceable as a later interpretive tradition.

11. **Is the Mars attribution ancient?**  
    No. It belongs to the late-nineteenth-century Golden Dawn synthesis. French occultists previously gave Tower/Ayin a Capricorn correspondence.

12. **Can Eliot, Yeats, Bergman, or Jodorowsky film scenes be called direct Tower quotations?**  
    Only where documentary or explicit textual evidence exists. Eliot’s falling towers and Bergman’s catastrophe are parallels; Calvino’s use of actual cards and Niki de Saint Phalle’s Tarot Garden are direct.
