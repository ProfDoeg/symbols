# The Empress: Research Dossier

## Method and evidence labels

This dossier distinguishes six kinds of assertion:

- **[Documented fact]** Directly supported by a surviving card, archival record, contemporary rule book, or primary printed text.
- **[Scholarly reconstruction]** An inference argued from material, documentary, linguistic, or iconographic evidence.
- **[Tradition]** A customary interpretation or naming practice whose historical origin may be diffuse.
- **[Esoteric attribution]** A correspondence asserted within an occult system; historically real as a doctrine, but not evidence of the card’s fifteenth-century meaning.
- **[Disputed claim]** A proposal debated among historians or contradicted by substantial evidence.
- **[Legend]** A story repeated without adequate historical evidence.
- **[Modern invention]** A symbolism or practice demonstrably introduced in modern deck design or interpretation.

Short quotations are given from the occultists’ texts. Pagination varies among editions and scans; searchable chapter or card headings are therefore supplied as well as pages where the consulted edition made them stable.

**Central finding.** **[Documented fact]** The oldest surviving Empress is a crowned secular ruler displaying imperial insignia. Fertility, pregnancy, wheat, Venus, Daleth, the Tree of Life, the “Great Mother,” and alchemical Salt are later interpretive layers. They are historically important layers, but no surviving fifteenth-century source assigns them to the card.

# Iconography, deck by deck

## Proto-tarot and fifteenth-century Italy

### Marziano da Tortona’s deck for Filippo Maria Visconti, c. 1412–1425

**[Documented fact]** Marziano’s Latin commentary describes a special pack of sixteen classical gods in four groups. The surviving text, copied for Jacopo Antonio Marcello, names figures such as Jupiter, Juno, Pallas, Venus, Apollo, and Hercules.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** This is a precursor or close relative of *trionfi*, not a standard 22-trump tarot. No cards survive, and there is no card called “The Empress.” Juno and Venus cannot simply be identified with the later Empress.

**[Modern conflation]** Lists saying “Marziano’s Empress was Juno” collapse two different packs. The later Besançon substitution of Juno for the Popess is likewise unrelated.

### Visconti di Modrone / Cary–Yale, Milan, c. 1441–1447

These are two names for the **same surviving group**, now at Yale’s Beinecke Library.

**[Documented fact]** The deck contains an enthroned Empress. She wears an imperial crown and carries or is accompanied by imperial-eagle insignia. Its unusually expanded court structure includes male and female counterparts—king/queen, knight/dame, and male/female knaves—and it has extra virtue trumps.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** The heraldry and luxury manufacture associate it with the Visconti court, often with the marriage of Bianca Maria Visconti and Francesco Sforza in 1441. Exact patronage, artist, date, and whether its expanded structure preceded or followed the standard 78-card form remain disputed.

**Meaning of the image.** In its contemporary visual vocabulary, crown, throne, sceptre, and eagle indicate the highest female temporal estate. Nothing in the card itself demonstrates Venus, maternity, or divination.

### Brambilla tarot, Milan/Lombardy, c. 1440s

**[Documented fact]** Forty-eight cards survive, principally suit cards and a very small number of trumps.

**Absence finding.** No securely identified surviving Brambilla Empress was located in the consulted museum and specialist catalogues. Descriptions that discuss “the Brambilla Empress” may be importing imagery from another Visconti group.

### Visconti–Sforza / Pierpont Morgan–Bergamo, Milan, c. 1450–1480

**[Documented fact]** Morgan MS M.630.2 shows a veiled, crowned woman seated frontally. She holds a sceptre in her right hand and a shield bearing an eagle in her left. Her robe contains Sforza interlinked-diamond devices and the Visconti ducal crown with palm and laurel. The Morgan catalog dates the group c. 1450–1480; older cataloguing usually attributed it to Bonifacio Bembo, while other attributions include Francesco Zavattari and members of the Bembo workshop.

The eagle is the decisive continuity with later Marseille Empresses. It is heraldic and imperial before it becomes occult.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** The mixed Visconti–Sforza devices probably commemorate or legitimize the dynastic union. The woman need not be a portrait of Bianca Maria. “Empress” is an estate or office, not securely a named sitter.

### Ferrarese and Este-related cards, later fifteenth century

Several incomplete luxury cards—often called the Charles VI, Ercole d’Este, or Este groups—have received shifting Florentine, Ferrarese, and northern-Italian attributions.

**[Documented fact]** Some groups preserve crowned female sovereigns identifiable as the Empress through sequence, attributes, or later comparison.

**[Disputed claim]** Older catalogues often assigned richly painted anonymous trumps to Ferrara because of style or supposed Este emblems. Modern art-historical reattributions have moved some toward Florence. “Ferrarese tarot” is therefore not a single securely bounded deck.

The Empress normally remains a courtly ruler; the shield, eagle, throne, crown, and sceptre vary. Explicit Venusian or maternal imagery is absent.

### Sola Busca, northern Italy, 1491

**[Documented fact]** The Sola Busca is the earliest surviving substantially complete 78-card tarot and the earliest complete illustrated pip sequence. Its trumps are mostly named classical or pseudo-classical warriors rather than the standard Pope–Empress–Emperor allegories. Trump III is not a conventional Empress.

**Relevance.** Pamela Colman Smith drew compositional ideas for several Waite–Smith pip cards from photographs of the Sola Busca shown at the British Museum in 1908. No comparably direct borrowing has been demonstrated for her Empress.

### “Mantegna Tarocchi,” c. 1460s–1470s

**[Documented fact]** These are two related series of fifty educational engravings arranged into estates, Apollo and the Muses, liberal arts, cosmic principles, and celestial spheres.

**[Scholarly consensus]** They are not tarot cards and probably were not playing cards. Their figures of estates, virtues, planets, and cosmic hierarchy form relevant Renaissance visual context, but they contain no tarot Empress. Calling them *tarocchi* is a later conventional misnomer.

### Rosenwald sheets, probably northern Italy, late fifteenth or early sixteenth century

**[Documented fact]** Woodcut sheets in the Rosenwald Collection preserve a popular, non-luxury tarot pattern. The trumps are numbered, unlike the great unnumbered Visconti cards.

The Empress is an enthroned, crowned ruler in the developing printed tradition. Her ordinal position is III.

**[Scholarly significance]** Rosenwald helps bridge courtly painted decks and mass-produced Italian/French patterns. Exact date and printing center remain debated.

### Budapest/Metropolitan-type sheets, c. 1500

**[Documented fact]** Fragmentary woodcut sheets, now divided among collections including Budapest and New York, preserve numbered trumps. The Empress belongs to the same emerging conventional sequence.

**[Disputed]** Dating, place, and whether handwritten numbers preceded or followed printing have been debated. They should not be treated as a pristine “original order.”

## Regional Italian patterns

### Bologna and tarocchino

**[Documented fact]** Bologna developed a 62-card *tarocchino*: pips 2–5 were removed, but the trump family remained. The lowest courtly/religious trumps were traditionally called the *papi*.

In older Bolognese packs, Empress, Emperor, Popess, and Pope formed a group whose individual order could be suppressed or treated as equal for play. In 1725, after a political controversy involving papal authority, cardmakers replaced the four figures with four “Moors.”

**Consequence.** The Empress disappeared as an individually named image in the standard later Bolognese pattern, though one Moor occupied the functional place within the lowest group.

### Minchiate / germini, Florence, sixteenth–nineteenth centuries

**[Documented fact]** Minchiate expanded the pack to 97 cards by adding signs of the zodiac, elements, virtues, and other trumps. Its five lowest trumps were collectively *papi*. The structure replaces the standard Pope–Popess–Emperor–Empress complex with an Empress and multiple imperial/papal rulers whose identification varies by manufacture.

**Game effect.** These low trumps were not the powerful “major arcana” imagined by modern divination. Their importance came chiefly from combinations or *versicole*, not high trick-taking rank.

### Sicily

**[Documented fact]** Sicilian tarot developed a shortened, regionally distinctive pack and sequence. Religious and political cards were renamed or redesigned: the Popess could become *Miseria* (“Poverty”), while other low trumps were altered.

The Empress survives in some Sicilian sequences, but numbering and rank are not safely inferred from Marseille III.

**Caution.** Modern charts frequently combine different dates of the Sicilian pattern. The historic pack changed over time.

## French traditions

### Jacques Viéville, Paris, c. 1650

Viéville’s pack is contemporary with Noblet but belongs to a distinct ordering and design tradition. Its Empress remains a crowned sovereign with sceptre and imperial symbolism.

**[Documented fact]** Viéville is not simply an early “Tarot de Marseille” in the strict pattern-classification sense.

### Jean Noblet, Paris, c. 1650–1659

**[Documented fact]** Trump III is titled **LEMPERATRISE**. She is crowned and enthroned, holding a cross-topped orb-sceptre and a shield with the imperial eagle.

The image preserves the Visconti formula but converts court illumination into robust woodcut grammar: frontal or three-quarter body, simplified throne, shield close to the torso, strong red-blue-yellow patterning.

### Jean Dodal, Lyon, c. 1701–1715

**[Documented fact]** The title appears as a nonstandard spelling such as **L’IMPERATRIS**. The crowned figure holds sceptre and eagle shield; her body, wings or throne drapery, and breast ornament are stylized.

**[Disputed interpretation]** Later writers find wings, pregnancy, hidden faces, or anatomical forms in the carving. Some shapes may be deliberate; no contemporary key confirms those readings.

### Nicolas Conver, Marseille, 1760

**[Documented fact]** **L’IMPERATRICE**, III. Conver standardizes the familiar Marseille figure: crown, sceptre topped by orb and cross, eagle shield, red and blue robes, and throne or wing-like back.

**Meaning of the change.** The card is visually paired with the Emperor. The shield and sceptre embody legitimate temporal authority; later occultists transform the bird into soul, spirit, sublimation, or alchemical eagle.

### Besançon and Swiss Catholic patterns, eighteenth–nineteenth centuries

The most famous religious substitutions are:

- II Papess → **Juno**
- V Pope → **Jupiter**

**[Documented fact]** These substitutions characterize the Besançon-type pattern and avoided objectionable papal imagery in Protestant or mixed-confessional markets.

**Important correction.** Juno replaces the **Papess**, not the Empress. The Empress generally remains herself.

Other regional renamings commonly confused with this issue include:

- XVI **La Maison Dieu** in Marseille: a traditional French title for the Tower card, not necessarily “House of God” in a narrowly theological sense.
- The Fool often unnumbered; later occult decks assign 0 or sometimes XXII.
- Belgian/Swiss variants replaced sensitive religious figures without thereby creating new ancient archetypes.

### Grimaud / Paul Marteau, 1930

**[Documented fact]** Grimaud’s *Ancien Tarot de Marseille*, edited and recolored by Paul Marteau, made a Conver-derived pattern internationally standard. Marteau regularized line, palette, spelling, and printing.

**[Modern interpretive consequence]** Many twentieth-century writers treated this commercial reconstruction as the timeless “original” Marseille tarot.

### Camoin–Jodorowsky, 1997

**[Documented fact]** Philippe Camoin and Alejandro Jodorowsky issued a reconstructed Tarot de Marseille based principally on historic Marseille-family exemplars.

The Empress retains III, crown, sceptre, eagle, shield, and red-blue body. Details and colors were selectively “restored.”

**[Disputed claim]** Camoin and Jodorowsky sometimes present small details as components of an intentional, universal “code.” Historians accept the deck as a modern reconstruction, not as a mechanically recovered unique archetype. Their Empress’s eagle becomes mind, creative conception, and rising consciousness in *The Way of Tarot*.

## Etteilla and French occult redesign

### Grand Etteilla, 1788–1789

**[Documented fact]** Jean-Baptiste Alliette, “Etteilla,” created the first tarot pack designed expressly for occult divination. Surviving 1789 uncut sheets are held by the Bibliothèque nationale de France.

Etteilla radically reordered and renamed the trumps. Cards 1–8 describe Creation; familiar Marseille identities no longer correspond reliably to the same numerals. Card III is associated with primordial creation imagery—commonly “Plants” or a stage/day of Creation in reconstructed charts—not the Marseille Empress.

**Critical finding.** There is therefore no historically sound equation:

> Grand Etteilla card III = Marseille III The Empress.

Later Grand Etteilla types contain cards corresponding iconographically or functionally to a male or female consultant, but publishers altered titles and numbering. Nineteenth-century Grand Etteilla II and III packs are often mislabeled online as Etteilla’s unchanged 1789 deck.

**Astrology.** **[Esoteric attribution]** Etteilla distributed zodiacal, planetary, elemental, and creation correspondences across his reordered pack. The Golden Dawn formula “Empress = Daleth = Venus” is not Etteilla’s system.

## Nineteenth-century occult Empresses

### Éliphas Lévi, 1854–1856

Lévi did not publish a complete deck in *Dogme et rituel de la haute magie*, but he made the 22 trumps into a Kabbalistic alphabet.

**[Esoteric attribution]** His straightforward numbering makes III correspond to the third Hebrew letter, **Gimel**, not Golden Dawn Daleth. He treats the third arcanum as generative or productive intelligence, the sacred triad manifested.

**Important conflict.** Lévi’s Fool is placed between XX and XXI for alphabetic purposes, whereas the later Golden Dawn begins Fool–Aleph, Magician–Beth, Priestess–Gimel, Empress–Daleth.

### Paul Christian, 1863 and 1870

Jean-Baptiste Pitois, writing as Paul Christian, elaborated a fictional Egyptian initiation in which tarot-like images appear in an underground temple.

His third arcanum is **Isis-Urania**, a winged or celestial generative woman rather than a medieval empress.

**[Legend presented as history]** Christian claimed that Iamblichus described this initiation. No such tarot corridor occurs in Iamblichus. Robert M. Place and historians of occult tarot trace the scenario to Christian’s literary construction.

### Oswald Wirth, 1889; revised 1926–1927

**[Documented fact]** Under Stanislas de Guaita’s direction, Wirth drew 22 Kabbalistic trumps in 1889.

His Empress:

- is winged;
- has twelve stars around her head;
- places a foot on a crescent moon;
- carries sceptre and eagle shield;
- bears a Hebrew letter under Lévi’s ordering.

**[Esoteric attribution]** Wirth combines Marseille sovereignty, Lévi’s Kabbalah, Revelation 12’s “woman clothed with the sun,” celestial femininity, and Hermetic symbolism.

In *Le Tarot des imagiers du Moyen Âge*/*The Tarot of the Magicians*, he describes her shield as purple with a silver eagle and reads her as creative intelligence and ideal generation.

**[Modern invention]** The explicit wings, twelve-star crown, and lunar crescent are not recoveries of a single medieval original. They are Wirth’s synthetic occult design.

### Papus, *Le Tarot des Bohémiens*, 1889

Papus reproduces or follows a Wirth-type Empress and calls her **Venus-Urania**. The English translation states:

> “This woman is represented with wings, or in the centre of a radiating sun.”

It also calls the eagle “the symbol of the soul and of life (Holy Spirit).”

**[Esoteric attribution]** Papus assigns the **third Hebrew letter, Gimel**, through the Lévi sequence and reads the card through ternary metaphysics: God the Holy Spirit, humanity/Eve, and universal attraction or productive force.

**[Legend]** His title *Tarot of the Bohemians* popularized the belief that Romani people preserved the ancient book. It documents nineteenth-century occult ideology, not tarot’s origin.

## Golden Dawn family

### Golden Dawn manuscripts / “Book T,” c. 1888–1900

**[Documented fact about occult history]** Golden Dawn teaching assigns:

- Trump III
- Daleth
- Venus
- Path 14
- Chokmah ↔ Binah
- “The Daughter of the Mighty Ones”
- Emerald green in the King Scale

Its conceptual formula is the attraction and union of the Father and Mother principles.

A surviving Golden Dawn summary gives:

> “Empress = The Wisdom of Understanding, the Union of the powers of Origination and Production; the Sphere of the Zodiac acting through Venus upon Saturn.”

**[Esoteric attribution]** Daleth means “door,” enabling readings of the card as womb, gate, mediation, or passage from archetype into form.

**Iconographic instruction.** Later published Golden Dawn material describes a crowned woman, Venusian emblems, twelve stars, sceptre, shield or eagle, vegetation, and colors corresponding to the four worlds.

### Robert Wang, *The Golden Dawn Tarot*, 1977

**[Modern reconstruction]** Wang worked with Israel Regardie to visualize Golden Dawn papers. His Empress is not an extant nineteenth-century order card but a twentieth-century reconstruction of its instructions.

She is richly enthroned, winged or framed by wings, Venusian, crowned with stars, and surrounded by generative natural imagery.

### Chic and Sandra Tabatha Cicero, *Golden Dawn Magical Tarot*, 1991

**[Modern reconstruction]** The Ciceros created another full visualization from Golden Dawn descriptions and color scales. It differs from Wang because Golden Dawn members were expected to draw and color their own cards; there was no single canonical commercial Golden Dawn deck.

## Waite–Smith, 1909

### Pamela Colman Smith’s image

**[Documented fact]** Pamela Colman Smith drew a crowned woman seated outdoors in a luxuriant landscape:

- crown of twelve stars;
- sceptre held upright;
- loose patterned gown decorated with pomegranate-like forms;
- cushions and a red upholstered seat;
- a heart-shaped shield bearing the sign of Venus;
- ripe wheat in the foreground;
- trees and flowing water behind her.

The card says **THE EMPRESS** and bears III.

Waite calls her:

> “a stately figure, seated, having rich vestments and royal aspect”

and:

> “above all things universal fecundity and the outer sense of the Word.”

He describes her setting as an “inferior Garden of Eden,” an earthly paradise rather than the higher mystery guarded by the High Priestess.

### Sources and changes

**[Documented influence]** Smith and Waite inherited Golden Dawn correspondences: Daleth, Venus, fertility, the twelve-star celestial woman, and the union of supernal principles.

**[Probable visual sources]**

- Wirth’s twelve-star, celestial Empress;
- Revelation 12:1;
- Victorian and Pre-Raphaelite images of richly dressed women in gardens;
- conventional Venus attributes;
- Marseille sceptre and sovereign pose.

**[Not demonstrated]** No primary document establishes one exact painting as Smith’s model. The wheat field is best treated as Smith’s/ Waite’s visual concretization of fecundity, not an authenticated copy of a named artwork.

**Major transformation.** The imperial eagle disappears. The shield becomes heart-shaped and carries **♀**, converting dynastic authority into Venusian nature, erotic attraction, maternity, and abundance. The interior throne room becomes a living field. This is the decisive source of the modern “pregnant Earth Mother” Empress.

**Pregnancy question.** The gown can suggest pregnancy, and later readers overwhelmingly describe her as pregnant. Waite calls her a fruitful mother but does not unambiguously state that Smith drew a visibly pregnant woman. Literal pregnancy is therefore **[tradition / interpretive inference]**, not a captioned fact.

## Paul Foster Case and B.O.T.A.

Case’s B.O.T.A. card closely follows Waite–Smith but is designed for hand coloring.

**[Esoteric attribution]**

- Daleth, “door”
- Venus
- Path 14
- Creative imagination
- Union of Wisdom and Understanding
- Green as the principal Venusian color

Case treats the stream as consciousness and the wheat as multiplication or cultivation. The Empress is subconscious creative power made fertile by self-conscious attention.

**[Tradition]** B.O.T.A. generally discourages treating reversed orientation as a mechanically opposite oracle. Case’s meanings are better understood as constructive and destructive poles than as a fixed upside-down glossary.

## Thoth tarot, painted 1938–1943; first complete commercial publication 1969

### Frieda Harris’s image

**[Documented fact]** Lady Frieda Harris paints a highly abstract, armored, enthroned Empress. The composition includes:

- Daleth;
- Venus;
- a lotus sceptre;
- a crescent or lunar form;
- a blue or green Venusian field;
- curving, revolving lines of attraction;
- bees and vegetal or generative emblems;
- a pelican feeding its young;
- an alchemical white eagle;
- a two-headed or double-eagle imperial/alchemical shield.

Crowley writes:

> “This card is attributed to the letter Daleth, which means a door, and it refers to the planet Venus.”

He continues:

> “she is fitted to represent one of the three alchemical forms of energy, Salt.”

And on the heraldry:

> “on the one side, the Pelican of tradition feeding its young from the blood of its own heart; on the other, the White Eagle of the Alchemist.”

### Meaning of the redesign

**[Esoteric attribution]** The Empress is crystallization, receptivity, attraction, love, and the matrix in which force becomes form. Salt is the stable, materializing member of the alchemical triad, paired—though Crowley’s own cross-references are not perfectly consistent—with Mercury and Sulphur.

- The **pelican** is Christian-Hermetic self-sacrifice: the mother reviving or feeding her young with her blood.
- The **white eagle** is purified alchemical matter, commonly associated with white tincture, sublimation, or lunar/feminine transformation.
- The **double eagle** joins imperial heredity to Hermetic union of opposites.
- The **lotus** marks receptive, generative femininity.
- The flowing geometry turns the sedentary ruler into a field of energetic attraction.

### Tzaddi/Heh issue

Crowley’s “Tzaddi is not the Star” revision swaps the Hebrew letters assigned to the Emperor and Star: Emperor becomes Tzaddi and Star becomes Heh.

**Finding.** This does **not** move the Empress. She remains Daleth–Venus. The controversy affects her only systemically: it shows that Crowley was willing to alter Golden Dawn letter assignments while retaining the Empress’s position.

## Notable later redesigns

### Motherpeace Tarot, Karen Vogel and Vicki Noble, 1981

**[Modern invention]** A round, explicitly feminist and Goddess-oriented tarot. The Empress becomes embodied female creativity, birth, ecology, and matrifocal community, replacing imperial hierarchy with a women’s-spirituality model.

### Thoth-derived and neo-Hermetic decks

Decks such as the Hermetic Tarot, Liber T, and modern Golden Dawn packs preserve Daleth, Venus, twelve stars, pelican/eagle motifs, or the Salt attribution in varying combinations.

### Feminist, queer, and ecological Empresses

From the late twentieth century onward, artists increasingly depict the Empress as:

- a pregnant or nursing person;
- Gaia or Mother Earth;
- nonbinary generativity;
- bodily sovereignty;
- artistic rather than biological fertility;
- abundance complicated by labor, race, ecology, or colonial power.

These are **[modern reinterpretations]**, not recoveries of a fixed Renaissance meaning.

# The game

## Original function

**[Documented fact]** Tarot originated as a trick-taking game. Trumps beat cards of the four ordinary suits. The Empress was one of the permanent trumps.

In every traditional sequence, low numbered trumps are weaker than higher trumps. Therefore the Empress is normally a low trump, not one of the game’s most valuable cards.

## Trump-order families

Michael Dummett classified early Italian orders into three broad families:

| Order | Principal region | General feature relevant to III |
|---|---|---|
| A | Bologna and much of eastern Italy | Low imperial/papal cards can form a special or equalized group; Justice and virtues occupy positions different from Marseille |
| B | Ferrara/Venice-associated traditions | Distinct hierarchy among virtues and celestial trumps; reflected in early literary lists |
| C | Milan, Piedmont, France; source of Marseille | Papess II, Empress III, Emperor IIII, Pope V; Justice VIII and Strength XI in Marseille |

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** These are families inferred from surviving cards, poems, lists, and later rules—not three statutes issued at tarot’s invention.

### Justice and Strength

- Marseille: Justice VIII, Strength XI.
- Golden Dawn/Waite: Strength VIII, Justice XI, to align Leo and Libra with the Hebrew-zodiac sequence.
- Crowley: Adjustment VIII and Lust XI.

This switch leaves the Empress at III.

## Scoring

There was never a single universal value.

- In many Italian games, the highest trumps, lowest trump or *bagatto*, Fool, and kings carry special counting value; ordinary trumps such as III may count only as a low-value card.
- In modern French tarot, only XXI, I, and the Excuse are *oudlers*. Each is worth 4½ points; another trump, including III, is worth ½ point.
- In many central-European Tarock games, III is an ordinary low trump, although named combinations may grant bonuses.
- In Austrian Königrufen, trump III is called the **Uhu** (“eagle owl”) in special announcements or penalties. This is a game nickname, not an occult identity.
- In Minchiate, the Empress belongs among low *papi* but can contribute to valuable sequences or *versicole*.

## The Fool

**[Documented fact]** In classical Italian and French games the Fool is normally the Excuse: it is played without following suit and usually returned to its owner’s captured pile, with a low card surrendered in exchange. It does not ordinarily beat a trick.

In many central-European games it became trump XXII, the highest trump.

**Numbering history.**

- Early painted Fools: usually unnumbered.
- Marseille: unnumbered.
- Court de Gébelin: treated outside the 1–21 run.
- Lévi: inserted alphabetically near the end.
- Golden Dawn/Waite: 0.
- Some occult traditions: XXII.

# Chronology of attestation

### c. 1412–1425 — Marziano da Tortona

**[Documented fact]** Marziano describes a classical-god pack for Filippo Maria Visconti. It is not the standard tarot and contains no named Empress.

### September 1440 — Florence

**[Documented fact]** Giusto Giusti records obtaining *naibi a trionfi* for Sigismondo Pandolfo Malatesta. This is currently the earliest widely accepted documentary use of *trionfi* for such cards.

### 1441–1447 — Visconti di Modrone/Cary–Yale

**[Scholarly dating]** The earliest surviving Empress is probably produced at the Milanese court.

### February and July 1442 — Ferrara

**[Documented fact]** Este accounts pay Jacomo Sagramoro for painted *carte da trionfi* and record a pack bought for the young Ercole and Sigismondo d’Este. One entry reads in part *uno paro de carte da trionfi*.

These records name the pack, not its individual Empress.

### 1440s–1450s — Milan and northern Italy

Luxury Visconti groups and correspondence surrounding Jacopo Antonio Marcello document the game as *ludus triumphorum*.

### c. 1450–1480 — Visconti–Sforza

**[Documented artifact]** The Morgan Empress with sceptre and eagle shield.

### c. 1470–1500 — *Sermones de ludo cum aliis*

**[Documented text]** The anonymous Dominican condemns dice, ordinary cards, and the “twenty-one triumphs.” The list includes the Empress as *Imperatrix*.

The sermon condemns gaming and the cards’ social/moral imagery; it does not reveal an occult initiatory system.

### Late fifteenth century — *tarocchi appropriati*

**[Documented literary practice]** Courtly poems assign trump names to people, using the cards for witty characterization. These are not necessarily fortune-telling readings.

### 1490s–1520s — Boiardo and “1500 poems”

Matteo Maria Boiardo devised a moralized poetic pack with different trump subjects. Other poems list or appropriate conventional trumps. The Empress appears as a social or allegorical type, but manuscript dates and attributions vary.

### 1505 — Ferrara and Avignon

**[Documented fact]** *Tarocchi* appears in Ferrara; French *taraux* is recorded at Avignon the same year. This is the transition from *trionfi* to the word family that produced *tarot* and *Tarock*.

### 1527 — Teofilo Folengo

In *Chaos del Tri per uno*, Folengo uses tarot imagery and card names in literary wordplay.

**[Documented fact]** This is evidence of cultural familiarity, not secret doctrine.

### Sixteenth–seventeenth centuries

Tarot spreads through Italy, France, Switzerland, and German-speaking Europe as a game. Rules and sequences diversify.

### Seventeenth century — Bologna

A manuscript survives in which cards are associated with people or fortunes.

**[Disputed significance]** It is evidence of divinatory or sortilege use, but not proof of an ancient occult system.

### 1750s

Italian and French evidence shows tarot being used in cartomancy before Court de Gébelin, though not yet under his Egyptian theory.

### 1781 — Court de Gébelin and Comte de Mellet

Court publishes *Du jeu des Tarots* in volume VIII of *Monde primitif*. He calls III “La Reine” while identifying the conventional figure as the Empress.

De Mellet prints the first systematic tarot–Hebrew-alphabet proposal and a divinatory method.

### 1783–1785 — Etteilla

Etteilla publishes *Manière de se récréer avec le jeu de cartes nommées Tarots*, establishing a practical occult-cartomantic system.

### 1788–1789 — Grand Etteilla

First purpose-built occult tarot appears.

### 1854–1856 — Éliphas Lévi

Tarot becomes an organizing alphabet of ritual magic and Kabbalah.

### 1863/1870 — Paul Christian

The fictional Egyptian temple-initiation narrative becomes influential.

### 1888 — Mathers

S. L. MacGregor Mathers publishes an English divinatory manual.

### 1888 onward — Golden Dawn

The Order formulates Daleth–Venus–Path 14 and “Daughter of the Mighty Ones.”

### 1889 — Wirth and Papus

Wirth publishes occult trumps; Papus systematizes the Lévi/Wirth “Bohemian” tarot.

### 1909–1910 — Waite–Smith and *Pictorial Key*

Smith supplies the modern wheat-field Venus; Waite publishes its symbolic and divinatory explanation.

### 1920s–1930 — Case, Wirth revision, Marteau/Grimaud

Occult, educational, and standardized Marseille forms circulate internationally.

### 1938–1943; 1969 — Thoth

Harris paints Crowley’s deck; the complete deck receives its first commercial publication in 1969.

### 1970s onward

Jungian, feminist, therapeutic, ecological, artistic, and identity-centered Empresses proliferate.

# Divinatory meanings by author

## Etteilla

**Finding.** Etteilla’s reordered pack prevents a responsible quotation of “Etteilla’s upright and reversed meanings for Marseille III The Empress.” His card III belongs to his Creation sequence. Later Grand Etteilla packs also differ from one another.

**[Frequent misattribution]** Modern sites often take Waite’s words or a nineteenth-century Etteilla-derived list and label them “Etteilla’s Empress.” Without a deck type, card number, edition, and French heading, that attribution is unreliable.

## S. L. MacGregor Mathers, 1888

In *The Tarot: Its Occult Signification, Use in Fortune-Telling, and Method of Play*, Mathers gives:

- Upright: “Action, plan, undertaking, movement in a matter, initiative.”
- Reversed: “Inaction, lack of concentration, vacillation.”

Some editions expand the list with delay or indecision. Mathers’s compact entries fed directly into Waite’s later divinatory vocabulary.

## A. E. Waite, 1910

Waite’s printed list is:

> “Fruitfulness, action, initiative, length of days; the unknown, clandestine; also difficulty, doubt, ignorance.”

Reversed:

> “Light, truth, the unravelling of involved matters, public rejoicings; according to another reading, vacillation.”

This is notably less sentimental than many present-day glossaries. “Motherhood, self-care, luxury” are modern simplifications of Waite’s much broader fecundity symbolism.

## Aleister Crowley, 1944

Crowley’s divinatory summary includes:

> “Love, beauty, happiness, pleasure, success, completion, good fortune, graciousness, elegance.”

Ill dignified, his Venusian abundance can become dissipation, debauchery, idleness, sensuality, or sterility.

Crowley did not make reversal mechanically decisive; elemental dignity and context matter more in the Thoth system.

## Paul Foster Case

Case emphasizes:

- fruitful imagination;
- growth and multiplication;
- beauty;
- love;
- production;
- subconscious response to conscious seed-images.

Negative polarity includes luxury, sensual excess, waste, vanity, or uncontrolled imagination.

**Caution.** Exact “upright/reversed” lists differ among Case editions and later B.O.T.A. teaching materials. B.O.T.A.’s core method is meditative and constructive, not simply reversal-based.

## Rachel Pollack

In *Seventy-Eight Degrees of Wisdom*, Pollack interprets the Empress as:

- nature;
- passion;
- sexuality;
- motherhood;
- sensory experience;
- the fecund world;
- the power to give form and life.

Reversed, the current may appear as blocked passion, sterility, repression, domestic or maternal difficulty, or—in an alternative positive reading—liberation from restrictive maternal/social roles.

**[Modern Jungian-feminist interpretation]** Pollack moves the card beyond literal childbirth toward embodied creativity and relationship with nature.

## Mary K. Greer

Greer resists a single fixed reversed definition. In *The Complete Book of Tarot Reversals*, a reversal may be:

- blocked or resisted;
- excessive;
- projected;
- delayed;
- internal or unconscious;
- misused;
- breaking through;
- being reconsidered.

Applied to the Empress, this yields infertility or creative blockage, smothering nurture, self-neglect, dependence, bodily alienation, or a private creative process.

**Important distinction.** A website’s phrase “The Empress reversed means smothering” is an application of Greer’s method, not necessarily Greer’s exclusive verbatim definition.

# Esoteric and symbolic readings

## Imperial and political

**First attested layer:** fifteenth-century cards.

Crown, throne, eagle, and sceptre identify the feminine half of imperial sovereignty. The card participates in the medieval hierarchy:

Fool or artisan → rulers → virtues → Fortune/Time/Death → celestial and theological powers.

The Empress’s low rank is not contemptuous; it places temporal authority below moral, cosmic, and divine forces.

## Christian

### Medieval

The card can be understood within Christian Europe’s hierarchy of estates. It is not itself securely the Virgin Mary, the Church, or a female pope.

### Revelation 12

The woman crowned with twelve stars, clothed with the sun, and standing on the moon enters explicit Empress design through Lévi/Wirth-era occultism and becomes highly visible in Waite–Smith.

### Refugium peccatorum

Waite calls the Empress *refugium peccatorum*, “refuge of sinners,” a Marian title. Yet he insists she is not simply the Virgin:

> “She is not Regina coeli, but she is still refugium peccatorum.”

This is deliberately hybrid Christian-Hermetic symbolism.

### Pelican

Crowley’s pelican derives from Christian bestiary and Eucharistic symbolism: the bird wounds its breast to revive or feed its young. It makes maternal sacrifice an emblem of spiritual love.

## Kabbalistic

- De Mellet, 1781: first printed tarot–Hebrew-letter scheme.
- Lévi, 1850s: tarot becomes the visible alphabet of occult Kabbalah; Empress follows Gimel.
- Golden Dawn, 1880s–1890s: Fool begins the alphabet; Empress therefore becomes Daleth.
- Golden Dawn places her between Chokmah and Binah, the supernal Father and Mother.
- Daleth/door becomes womb, threshold, mediation, and emergence.

**Historical caution.** No fifteenth-century Jewish or Christian Kabbalistic manuscript mapping the Empress to Daleth has been found.

## Astrological

- Court de Gébelin Egyptianizes the card but does not provide the mature GD Venus table.
- Papus calls her Venus-Urania while still following the Gimel sequence.
- Golden Dawn fixes Empress = Venus.
- Waite pictures ♀ rather than naming the planet on the face.
- Crowley retains Venus explicitly.

Venus governs attraction, beauty, pleasure, fertility, combination, and the cohesion of opposites.

## Alchemical

### Eagle

The eagle begins as imperial heraldry. Occultists later read it as spirit, sublimation, volatile matter, or the purified white phase.

### Salt

Crowley’s *Book of Thoth* is the clear, influential source for Empress = Salt:

> “she is fitted to represent one of the three alchemical forms of energy, Salt.”

Salt is the stable, receptive, bodily matrix in which Sulphur and Mercury become manifest.

**Conflict.** Crowley’s descriptions of which trumps complete the Sulphur–Mercury–Salt triad are not perfectly uniform. Empress = Salt is stable; the identity of her two partners varies across passages.

## Hermetic

Hermetic writers turn imperial government into cosmogenesis. The Empress is:

- Nature as formative power;
- the matrix receiving seed;
- the “door” between archetype and embodiment;
- the law of attraction;
- the outer or manifested Word;
- the conjunction of opposites.

These are late eighteenth- through twentieth-century readings, not documented rules of Renaissance card play.

## Jungian

Jung did not publish a canonical tarot commentary assigning meanings card by card. “Jung’s meaning of the Empress” is therefore usually shorthand for later Jungian tarot writers.

Later authors apply:

- the Great Mother archetype;
- positive mother: nourishment, protection, fecundity;
- negative mother: engulfment, possessiveness, stagnation;
- anima and embodiment;
- individuation through reconciliation with body, nature, and creativity.

Sallie Nichols, Rachel Pollack, Mary Greer, and many therapists/readers spread these approaches.

**[Modern invention]** Claims that tarot’s trumps were deliberately designed from Jungian archetypes are anachronistic. Jungian theory is a twentieth-century interpretive lens.

## Artists, printers, and occultists

- **Marziano da Tortona** — author of the earliest surviving commentary on a related triumph pack.
- **Michelino da Besozzo** — named as painter of Marziano’s lost pack.
- **Bonifacio Bembo / Bembo workshop / Francesco Zavattari** — competing attributions for Visconti cards.
- **Jean Noblet, Jean Dodal, Nicolas Conver** — principal early French pattern makers.
- **Etteilla** — first professional system-builder of occult tarot and creator of the first dedicated divinatory tarot.
- **Éliphas Lévi** — joined tarot and occult Kabbalah.
- **Paul Christian** — created the Egyptian temple-initiation legend.
- **Stanislas de Guaita and Oswald Wirth** — created the first influential Kabbalistic visual redesign.
- **S. L. MacGregor Mathers** — Golden Dawn organizer and early English tarot author.
- **Pamela Colman Smith** — artist responsible for the Waite–Smith imagery.
- **A. E. Waite** — designer/author who supplied the published symbolic program.
- **Paul Foster Case** — transmitted Golden Dawn tarot through American occult education and B.O.T.A.
- **Aleister Crowley and Frieda Harris** — creators of the Thoth tarot.
- **Paul Marteau** — standardized the Grimaud Marseille pack and its color interpretations.
- **Philippe Camoin and Alejandro Jodorowsky** — modern Marseille reconstruction and “code” interpretation.
- **Rachel Pollack and Mary K. Greer** — major feminist, psychological, and practice-centered interpreters.

## Literature

### Italo Calvino, *Il castello dei destini incrociati*

The first version appeared in 1969 with the Visconti cards; the expanded book appeared in 1973, and its second part uses Marseille cards.

**[Documented fact]** Characters tell stories by laying cards in intersecting sequences. The same card changes meaning with narrative position. The Empress can function as sovereign woman, beloved, mother, or narrative agent rather than carrying one dictionary meaning.

Calvino’s oft-cited formulation—tarot as a “machine for constructing stories”—accurately describes his combinatorial method.

### T. S. Eliot, *The Waste Land* (1922)

Madame Sosostris displays a “wicked pack of cards.” Eliot names the drowned Phoenician Sailor, Belladonna, the man with three staves, the Wheel, the one-eyed merchant, a blank card, and the absent Hanged Man.

**Absence finding.** The Empress is not expressly named. Identifying Belladonna or another woman with the Empress is literary interpretation, not textual fact.

### W. B. Yeats

**[Documented fact]** Yeats was a senior Golden Dawn member and worked within the same ritual-symbolic world that assigned Daleth and Venus to the Empress.

**Absence finding.** No single famous Yeats poem can be securely labeled a direct, explicit Empress-card quotation merely because it contains a queen, rose, Venus, or Great Mother. Claims require poem-specific evidence.

## Film

### Alejandro Jodorowsky

Jodorowsky used tarot in interviews, writings, performances, psychomagic, and visual projects, and collaborated on the Camoin deck.

**[Documented influence]** Tarot informs his artistic method.

**Caution.** Broad claims that a particular woman in *The Holy Mountain*, *El Topo*, or another film “is the Empress” are interpretive unless Jodorowsky explicitly identifies her.

### Ingmar Bergman

Bergman’s *The Seventh Seal* uses chess, Death, medieval pageantry, and apocalyptic imagery.

**Absence finding.** No reliable source consulted establishes a direct quotation of the Empress card. Its medieval allegorical atmosphere is analogous to tarot, but analogy is not documentary influence.

## Paintings and buildings

Wirth and Waite’s celestial Empress draws on Revelation 12 iconography found in Marian paintings, manuscript illumination, church sculpture, and images of the *mulier amicta sole*.

**Caution.** A woman with twelve stars and a moon is evidence of common Christian iconography, not proof that a church image “quotes tarot.” Conversely, modern murals and installations displaying the exact numbered card can be direct quotations, but no canonical historic building was verified as intentionally quoting The Empress.

# Controversies and disputes

## Egyptian origin

### Claim

Court de Gébelin asserted in 1781 that tarot was an Egyptian book concealed in a game. De Mellet developed the Book-of-Thoth and Hebrew-letter theory; Etteilla redesigned tarot accordingly.

### Evidence status

**[Legend / documented eighteenth-century doctrine]**

There is:

- no ancient Egyptian tarot deck;
- no Egyptian text describing 22 tarot trumps;
- no continuous transmission;
- no plausible Egyptian etymology for *tarot* in Gébelin’s proposed forms;
- no deciphered hieroglyphic evidence available to Gébelin, who wrote before Champollion.

There **is** an indirect Egyptian connection for ordinary European playing cards through Mamluk cards. That does not validate the occult “Book of Thoth” claim.

## Romani or “Gypsy” origin

### Claim

Romani wanderers carried the Egyptian book into Europe.

### Transmission

Hints appear around the Gébelin/de Mellet environment; the narrative was spread powerfully by nineteenth-century authors including Vaillant, Boiteau d’Ambly, Paul Christian, and Papus.

### Evidence status

**[Legend]** Tarot is documented at Italian courts before evidence that Romani communities introduced it. Linguistic and historical research places Romani origins in northern India, not ancient Egypt.

The word “Gypsy” itself reflects Europeans’ mistaken association of Roma with Egypt.

## Cathar origin

### Claim

The trumps encode Cathar theology or preserve a persecuted dualist gospel.

### Evidence status

**[Modern invention / disputed claim]** No Cathar manuscript describes tarot; no chain connects thirteenth-century Cathar communities to fifteenth-century northern-Italian card production. Individual images can be reread dualistically, but iconographic compatibility is not provenance.

## Templar origin

### Claim

The Knights Templar transmitted secret Egyptian, Johannite, or Grail wisdom into tarot.

### Evidence status

**[Legend]** The Templars were suppressed in the early fourteenth century; tarot appears in the fifteenth. No intervening deck, rule, inventory, ritual, or textual transmission has been demonstrated.

## Kabbalistic origin

### Claim

The 22 trumps were created to correspond to the 22 Hebrew letters.

### Evidence status

**[Disputed claim, unsupported as origin]** The first surviving explicit correspondence is de Mellet’s 1781 essay. Early packs vary in trump order, while letter schemes depend on a fixed order. The strongest evidence shows retrofit rather than original design.

## The Empress as Venus from the beginning

### Claim

The medieval Empress always represented Venus, fertility, or the Great Mother.

### Evidence status

**[Esoteric attribution projected backward]** The early image has imperial insignia. Venus becomes explicit through nineteenth-century occult synthesis, then visually definitive in Waite–Smith.

The older eagle shield does not itself prove Venus; the imperial eagle is sufficient in its historical context.

## The Empress as a portrait

Candidates include Bianca Maria Visconti and other dynastic women.

**[Disputed]** Heraldry links a deck to a patronal family but does not identify the sitter. The face may be typological rather than portrait-like.

## Wings in Marseille

Some writers say the Empress has literal wings; others see throne drapery.

**[Disputed iconographic reading]** Wirth unquestionably gives her wings. In Noblet, Dodal, and Conver, the form can be read as a throne back, fabric, or vestigial wings. No contemporary caption resolves it.

## Pregnancy in Waite–Smith

**[Tradition / disputed visual reading]** Waite says fecund mother; Smith draws a voluminous gown. The card does not display an unmistakable pregnant abdomen. Later reproduction and interpretation made pregnancy nearly canonical.

## Misprints and copied errors

- *LEMPERATRISE*, *L’IMPERATRIS*, and similar spellings are normal early-modern orthography or carving, not occult ciphers.
- The Emperor’s Marseille numeral **IIII**, not IV, is conventional additive notation, not evidence of a secret “four.”
- Grand Etteilla types are regularly confused, causing wrong numbers and keywords to be credited to Etteilla’s 1789 pack.
- Modern reproductions sometimes “correct” lines, colors, hands, shield birds, or titles and then treat the correction as ancient.
- OCR of Lévi and Papus can replace Hebrew letters with Latin characters, generating spurious correspondence tables.
- The “Tzaddi is not the Star” issue is sometimes misreported as moving every neighboring card. It does not change Empress–Daleth.

## Historians’ correction

Dummett, Decker, Depaulis, Kaplan, Farley, and Place differ on particulars, but the evidence-based core is:

1. Tarot appears in northern Italy in the fifteenth century.
2. It was made for a game.
3. Its imagery uses contemporary courtly, Christian, civic, moral, and classical visual culture.
4. Divinatory use is documented later.
5. The systematic Egyptian, Hebrew, astrological, and Hermetic tarot begins in late-eighteenth-century France and develops thereafter.

This correction does not make occult tarot culturally unreal. It dates its creation.

# Open questions

1. **Birthplace.** Milan, Ferrara, Bologna, and Florence remain competing centers; documentation records diffusion more securely than invention.

2. **Exact date of the Cary–Yale Empress.** Heraldry and style narrow the range, but no dated commission survives.

3. **Artist attribution.** Bembo, his workshop, Zavattari, and other Lombard hands remain debated across the Visconti groups.

4. **Original trump order.** No fifteenth-century rule book gives one universal sequence. Surviving regional orders may descend from an earlier common order or from early divergence.

5. **Why the Empress and Emperor?** The pair fits medieval hierarchies of temporal authority, but the precise literary or civic model is unknown.

6. **Meaning of the eagle.** Its imperial/heraldic function is secure; whether particular eagles refer specifically to Visconti or Holy Roman imperial claims varies by deck.

7. **Marseille “wings.”** No contemporary maker’s explanation has been found.

8. **Smith’s exact visual sources.** Golden Dawn and Wirth influence is strong; an exact painting for the wheat-field composition remains untraced.

9. **Etteilla equivalence.** A definitive answer requires specifying Grand Etteilla I, II, or III and consulting its exact French card face. “Etteilla’s Empress III” is not a stable historical object.

10. **Earliest literal fertility interpretation.** Generative readings are visible in eighteenth- and nineteenth-century occult texts, but the exact first use of “pregnancy” as a predictive meaning remains to be established from dated cartomantic editions.

11. **Direct artistic quotations.** Calvino’s use is documented; claims for Eliot, Yeats, Bergman, particular paintings, or buildings often turn out to be analogy rather than demonstrable quotation.
