# The Sun: Research Dossier

## Method and evidence labels

This dossier distinguishes six kinds of statement:

- **[Documented fact]** Directly supported by a surviving card, archival record, rule book, or primary printed text.
- **[Scholarly reconstruction]** An inference argued by historians from surviving objects, documents, or game structures.
- **[Tradition]** A historically established interpretation or practice whose original evidentiary basis may be uncertain.
- **[Esoteric attribution]** A correspondence deliberately assigned by an occult author or school.
- **[Disputed claim]** A claim supported by competing interpretations or inadequate evidence.
- **[Legend/modern invention]** A retrospective origin story or recent reading without support in the earliest evidence.

Dates for handmade decks are approximate. “Tarot de Marseille” denotes a later historical classification, not necessarily the name used by seventeenth-century makers.

### The identification in brief

**[Documented fact]** The card belongs to the special permanent trump suit invented for the north-Italian game called *trionfi*. The term *tarocchi* appears only around the beginning of the sixteenth century; the French *tarot* follows shortly thereafter. It is therefore anachronistic to imagine a fifteenth-century Milanese patron calling the card a “Major Arcanum.” That expression belongs to the nineteenth-century occult reception.

**[Documented fact]** In the familiar Marseille sequence the Sun is XIX, conventionally printed **XVIIII** rather than the classically subtractive **XIX**. Additive forms such as IIII and XVIIII were normal on playing cards and clocks; they are not evidence of a secret numerical cipher.

**[Documented fact]** The Morgan’s catalogue identifies its Sun as Milanese, approximately 1480, 173 × 87 mm, accession MS M.630.13. The catalogue presently attributes it to Antonio Cicognara while recording competing proposals: Benedetto Bembo, Franco dei Russi, a Ferrarese artist, or another hand. The deck’s core is usually dated around 1450–58, but the Sun is stylistically distinct. [Morgan object record](https://www.themorgan.org/collection/tarot-cards/the-sun) and [full manuscript catalogue](https://www.themorgan.org/manuscript/158525).

**[Scholarly reconstruction/dispute]** It is often called a “replacement card,” made after the loss of the original. Michael Dummett questioned both the attribution to Cicognara and the assumption that it was necessarily a replacement, proposing that distinct hands might have participated in the original workshop production. The Morgan records both positions rather than declaring the matter settled.

### Names and numbering in regional context

- **Northern Italy, fifteenth century:** *carte da trionfi*; the individual figures were *trionfi*. Surviving early painted cards usually carry no printed title or number.
- **Italy, c. 1500 onward:** *tarocchi* becomes the standard plural name; singular *tarocco*. The card is *Il Sole*.
- **France, sixteenth century onward:** *tarot*; in the Marseille pattern, *Le Soleil*, normally XVIIII.
- **Spain:** *tarot* or historically borrowed *tarocchi* in antiquarian discussion; the card is *El Sol*. Tarot was not an indigenous Spanish national pattern comparable to Marseille or Bologna.
- **German-speaking lands:** *Tarock* names the game/deck; *Die Sonne* names the pictorial trump when Italian-suited or occult cards retain the traditional subjects. Many gaming Tarock packs instead replaced allegories with genre scenes and identified trumps chiefly by number.
- **Besançon pattern:** The Sun remains *Le Soleil*. The famous substitutions Juno for the Papess and Jupiter for the Pope affect II and V, not XIX. These substitutions developed in Swiss/eastern-French Catholic-sensitive production in the eighteenth century.
- **Belgian or Rouen/Brussels pattern:** Regional titles and pictures differ substantially, but the solar trump remains recognizable.
- **Sicilian tarot:** The sequence, numbering, and hierarchy differ; the Fool is *Il Misero*, and several standard trumps were transformed or omitted. The Sun nevertheless remains among the celestial high trumps.
- **Bolognese tarocchino:** Many low trumps are unnumbered and grouped; the Sun belongs to the upper, individually ranked sequence.
- **Minchiate:** The expanded Florentine pack has forty trumps plus the Fool. The celestial cards occur near the top; in commonly printed later packs the Sun is 38, below the World and Trumpets/Judgment.
- **Etteilla:** The traditional Marseille sequence is dismantled and reassigned within a supposedly Egyptian cosmology. Depending on edition, cards may carry two numbering systems. The Sun can appear as card 2 in the cosmological sequence and 77 as a secondary/reference number.
- **Golden Dawn:** XIX, *The Sun—Lord of the Fire of the World*.
- **Waite-Smith:** XIX, *The Sun*.
- **BOTA:** XIX, *The Sun*.
- **Thoth:** XIX, *The Sun*; Crowley did not rename it, unlike *Adjustment*, *Lust*, *Art*, and *The Aeon*.

The notorious renamings in the prompt—Papess/Juno, Pope/Jupiter, Tower/*Maison Dieu*, unnumbered Fool—are properties of tarot history generally, not variant names for The Sun. *La Maison Dieu* is the inherited French name of XVI; it was not created by occultists.

# Iconography deck by deck

## 1. Before the standard tarot Sun

### Marziano da Tortona and Michelino da Besozzo, c. 1412–1425

**[Documented fact]** Marziano da Tortona wrote a text describing a card pack made for Filippo Maria Visconti, with sixteen classical gods arranged in four orders. Michelino da Besozzo was reportedly its painter. The cards are lost; Marziano’s explanatory text survives.

**Finding:** This is an important precursor to tarot because divine figures acted as special superior cards, but it did **not** contain a trump called the Sun. Apollo may carry solar associations, but equating Apollo with the later nineteenth trump would be retrospective.

### Visconti di Modrone / Cary-Yale, c. 1441–1445

**[Documented fact]** This surviving Milanese group includes additional court cards and theological virtues. No securely surviving Sun card belongs to it.

**Finding:** Claims describing a Cary-Yale Sun generally reproduce an imagined reconstruction, not a surviving original.

### Brambilla-Brera deck, c. 1440s

**[Documented fact]** This fragmentary Milanese pack, associated with Francesco Sforza and now principally at the Pinacoteca di Brera, does not preserve a securely identifiable Sun trump.

### “Mantegna Tarocchi,” c. 1460s

**[Documented fact]** These are educational or emblematic engravings arranged into fifty figures, not a tarot pack and not playing cards. They include cosmic/intellectual hierarchies, including planetary imagery.

**[Scholarly relevance]** They demonstrate the same Renaissance habit of arranging estates, virtues, liberal arts, and cosmic powers into ordered series. They do not supply a direct prototype provably copied by the tarot Sun.

## 2. Visconti-Sforza Sun, Milan/Ferrara, c. 1480

**[Documented fact]** The surviving card shows an enormous radiant, anthropomorphic solar disk above a nude or lightly draped winged child/putto. The child stands or floats amid cloud-like forms and raises the Sun itself, or a head-like solar emblem, in both hands. The gold ground and punched decoration place it within luxury court production rather than popular woodcut printing.

The prompt’s “putto with the sun’s head on a cloud” is substantially correct, with one qualification: descriptions vary over whether the lower forms are clouds, stylized ground, or drapery, and whether the child “holds the sun” or supports a solar head.

**[Iconographic context]** A winged putto was ordinary Renaissance visual language: it could personify a spirit, genius, celestial force, season, or abstract quality. The anthropomorphic sun was likewise widespread in manuscripts, astrology, civic decoration, and heraldry.

**[Disputed claim]** Some writers read the putto as Apollo, the Christ child, a solar genius, or the soul. No surviving inscription identifies it. “Solar genius” is the safest descriptive label; Apollo and Christ are interpretations.

**[Dispute]** The card has traditionally been attributed to Antonio Cicognara and treated as a later replacement. Other attributions include Benedetto Bembo and Franco dei Russi. The Morgan dates the object c. 1480–1500 on its individual page while its general catalogue discusses c. 1480.

## 3. Ferrarese and north-Italian printed evidence

### Este-related fragments and the so-called Charles VI / Gringonneur cards, late fifteenth century

The labels historically attached to these groups are treacherous. The “Charles VI Tarot” was once connected with Jacquemin Gringonneur’s 1392 payment, but its surviving cards are now generally placed in late-fifteenth-century northern Italy, often Ferrara.

**[Documented fact]** Fragmentary Ferrarese-style cards preserve celestial subjects, but catalogues differ over which fragmentary figure should be identified as the Sun. No continuous titled and numbered pack survives.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** The Ferrarese order recorded in literary and sermon sources places the celestial series Star–Moon–Sun above the Devil and Tower/Arrow and below Angel, Justice, and World in one important ordering.

### Rosenwald sheets, late fifteenth or early sixteenth century

**[Documented fact]** The Rosenwald material consists of uncut printed sheets representing a cheap, reproducible tarot tradition very different from courtly illuminated cards. Its figures helped establish the existence of a broad popular printed tradition.

**Iconography:** The Sun belongs to the celestial sequence and uses the great radiant solar face over a lower human scene. Detail is difficult because impressions are incomplete and heavily stylized.

**Caution:** Online reconstructions sometimes color, retouch, or silently complete damaged lines. Those reconstructions must not be treated as the original impression.

### Budapest sheets, late fifteenth/early sixteenth century

**[Documented fact]** These are fragmentary north-Italian woodcut sheets. As with Rosenwald, their importance lies partly in showing that tarot was not confined to Visconti or Este luxury commissions.

**Finding:** The surviving evidence does not justify a highly detailed narrative identification of “twins,” Dioscuri, or Gemini at this date.

## 4. Sola Busca, Venice/Ferrara milieu, 1491

**[Documented fact]** The Sola Busca is the earliest substantially complete surviving seventy-eight-card tarot and the earliest surviving deck with fully illustrated pip cards.

Its trump series uses named ancient or pseudo-ancient warriors rather than the ordinary Pope–Emperor–Virtues–celestial sequence. Consequently it has no standard trump XIX called *Il Sole*.

**Relevance to The Sun:** Pamela Colman Smith demonstrably or very probably borrowed compositions from Sola Busca pip cards for several Waite-Smith minors after examples were exhibited at the British Museum in 1908. There is no equally strong case that her Sun composition was copied from a Sola Busca “Sun,” because no such conventional Sun trump exists there.

## 5. Tarot de Marseille lineage

### Jean Noblet, Paris, c. 1650

**[Documented fact]** Noblet’s deck is the earliest substantially preserved French pack assigned to the Marseille family, although its small format and details distinguish it from later standard forms.

**Iconography:** A large human-faced sun emits alternating straight and flame-like rays. Coloured droplets descend. Beneath it are two nearly nude youths interacting in an enclosed or bounded landscape. The wall or masonry is comparatively rudimentary.

**Interpretive history:** The pair has been called children, twins, brothers, lovers, male and female, soul and body, or regenerated humanity. The print itself does not name them.

### Jean Dodal, Lyon, c. 1701–1715

Dodal stabilizes the familiar image:

- anthropomorphic sun;
- alternating straight and wavy rays;
- falling coloured droplets;
- two nearly identical figures;
- one places a hand near the other’s shoulder or neck;
- a low brick wall behind them;
- bare or sparsely vegetated foreground.

**[Documented fact]** The title is *LE SOLEIL* and the number is generally XVIIII.

**[Tradition]** The droplets are frequently called solar “tears,” gold, dew, or spiritual influence. The card itself does not settle which.

### Nicolas Conver, Marseille, 1760

Conver’s influential pattern retains the paired children and wall but regularizes posture, colour blocks, facial details, and rays. It became a major source for later commercial Marseille decks.

**Possible visual meanings:**

- The wall creates a protected enclosure, garden, or civic boundary.
- The children’s touch suggests alliance, mutual recognition, or play.
- Their near-nudity suggests innocence or exposure.
- The face of the sun makes celestial agency visible.

These are iconographic observations. Claims that the wall specifically represents an alchemical vessel, Jerusalem, Eden, or the limits of the ego are later interpretations.

### Grimaud, nineteenth–twentieth centuries

Grimaud’s *Ancien Tarot de Marseille* standardizes line, typography, and industrial colour. It spreads the Conver-like Sun globally.

**[Documented fact]** The title remains *LE SOLEIL*, XVIIII.

**Change in meaning:** Commercial standardization made one local French image look timeless and universal. Many later occultists consequently mistook the eighteenth-century Marseille pattern for an unchanged ancient book.

### Camoin–Jodorowsky, 1997

Philippe Camoin and Alejandro Jodorowsky advertised their deck as a “restoration” of the Tarot de Marseille, synthesizing details from several historical packs.

**[Documented fact]** Their Sun has two figures, wall, solar face, alternating rays, and multicoloured descending forms.

**[Disputed claim]** It is not a facsimile of one historical deck. “Restored original tarot” is an esoteric/editorial claim, not an archaeologically demonstrated recovery of a single lost prototype.

**Jodorowsky–Costa reading:** The pair may represent reconciliation, brotherhood, collaboration, paternal blessing, or a newly constructed common reality. In *The Way of Tarot*, the card’s warmth can be divine love, success, or the discovery of an ideal father. This is a modern symbolic system, not a recovered medieval gloss.

## 6. The Gemini, twins, and Dioscuri question

This requested lead requires especially careful separation.

**[Documented fact]** Marseille-family cards show two similar youthful figures.

**[Tradition]** They are commonly called twins.

**[Esoteric attribution]** Some nineteenth- and twentieth-century writers connect them with Gemini because Gemini is conventionally represented by twins.

**[Disputed claim]** No surviving seventeenth-century card title or maker’s text identifies the figures as Gemini, Castor and Pollux, or the Dioscuri. Tarot XIX later received the **planet Sun**, not Gemini, in the Golden Dawn system.

**[Counter-evidence]** Gemini is astrologically ruled by Mercury, while the Sun rules Leo. Thus a Gemini reading can explain the paired figures but conflicts with the card’s later formal planetary attribution.

**[Possible historical source]** Paired children under the sun occur in emblematic and astrological art, while the Dioscuri were ancient solar/stellar twins. Similarity is not proof of descent.

**Conclusion:** “Twins” is a reasonable visual description; “Gemini” is a later interpretive hypothesis; “Castor and Pollux” is an even more specific attribution for which no early textual evidence was located.

## 7. Minchiate

**[Documented fact]** Florentine Minchiate expanded the trump suit to forty cards plus the Fool, adding the four elements, twelve zodiac signs, and additional virtues.

In later standard Minchiate packs the high celestial sequence includes:

- Star;
- Moon;
- Sun;
- World;
- Trumpets/Judgment.

The Sun is normally identified as trump 38.

**Iconography:** A radiant Sun dominates the upper field; human figures below vary by maker. Because zodiac cards are separate, the Sun is not merely the Gemini card.

**Game meaning:** It is one of the highest ordinary trumps, but not the supreme trump.

## 8. Bologna and Sicily

### Tarocchino Bolognese

The shortened Bolognese pack removes the 2–5 of each suit but retains the trump structure. The Sun belongs to the individually ranked high trumps. Its imagery is derived from north-Italian celestial iconography but can reduce or transform the Marseille pair.

### Tarocco Siciliano

The Sicilian pack developed a distinctive order and iconography. Several cards were renamed or radically altered; its Fool became *Il Misero*. The Sun remains a celestial trump, but its rank should not simply be inferred from Marseille numbering because Sicilian trump numbering and hierarchy are independent.

## 9. Etteilla’s Grand Etteilla, c. 1788–1789

Jean-Baptiste Alliette, “Etteilla,” produced the first tarot designed expressly around a comprehensive occult and divinatory theory.

**Iconographic change:** Rather than preserving the Marseille trump sequence, Etteilla reordered the cards as episodes of Creation and Egyptian wisdom. The solar card may show the creation or ordering of celestial light rather than two anonymous Marseille children.

**Number:** In early Grand Etteilla systems it is associated with **2**, while some cards/editions also carry **77** as a secondary or reversed-order number.

**[Esoteric attribution]** Etteilla’s cosmology draws on Genesis, the four elements, astrology, and his supposedly Egyptian *Book of Thoth*.

**[Legend]** The pack does not reconstruct an ancient Egyptian original. It is an eighteenth-century French creation.

## 10. Éliphas Lévi and Paul Christian

Lévi did not publish a full designed tarot deck in *Dogme et Rituel de la Haute Magie*, but his twenty-two-chapter construction powerfully recoded the trumps.

For XIX, “The Mastery of the Sun,” he writes:

> “We come now to that number which is attributed in the Tarot to the Sign of the Sun.”

He connects it with “the Great Work of the sages,” Hermes, the Philosophers’ Stone, projection, and sublimation. This is from chapter XIX of the *Rituel* volume; see A. E. Waite’s translation, *Transcendental Magic*, Part II, chapter XIX. [Scanned text](https://www.rahoorkhuit.net/library/ceremonial/classics/pdf/transcendental/dogma_et_rituel_part_2.pdf).

**[Esoteric attribution]** Lévi’s chapter is alchemical and magical rather than a historical explanation of the card’s invention.

Paul Christian—Jean-Baptiste Pitois—later presented Egyptian initiatory tableaux and helped popularize *arcana* as the cards’ occult name.

**[Modern legend-making]** Christian’s Egyptian temple-initiation account is literary occult construction, not an Egyptian archaeological source.

## 11. Oswald Wirth, 1889; revised plates 1926–27

Wirth produced twenty-two trumps under Stanislas de Guaita’s direction.

**Iconography:**

- Marseille-style anthropomorphic Sun;
- two figures, more clearly differentiated than in many Marseille cards;
- controlled symbolic colouring;
- Hebrew and occult apparatus in editions or accompanying explanation;
- a deliberate “correction” of popular card imagery according to Lévi’s system.

Wirth interprets the pair as “children of light,” reconciliation, and the marriage of feeling and reason. In later commentary they become agents of human regeneration, those who reconquer Paradise.

**[Esoteric attribution]** Wirth links the image to initiation, conscious illumination, and the union of complementary principles.

**Gemini:** Wirth discusses the Sun’s relation to Gemini imagery, but this remains symbolic analogy rather than proof that the Marseille figures originally represented Gemini.

The 1889 deck consisted only of the twenty-two trumps. Modern “complete Wirth” seventy-eight-card decks add minors devised by later publishers.

## 12. Golden Dawn manuscript tradition, c. 1888–1900

The Golden Dawn’s tarot instructions were transmitted in manuscripts later published or reconstructed as “Book T.”

**Title:** *The Sun, Lord of the Fire of the World*.

**Design instructions generally include:**

- a radiant solar disk;
- a wall or enclosure;
- two children;
- solar and zodiacal symbolism;
- colours derived from the Order’s colour scales.

**Attribution:** Resh; Sun; path between Hod and Yesod.

The Golden Dawn did not leave one universally authoritative commercial deck. Consequently:

- Robert Wang’s *Golden Dawn Tarot* is a twentieth-century reconstruction prepared with Israel Regardie.
- Sandra Tabatha and Chic Cicero’s *Golden Dawn Magical Tarot* is another reconstruction grounded in Order documents.
- Differences in pose, colour, and subsidiary emblems do not necessarily represent historical Golden Dawn doctrinal disputes; some are choices made by reconstructors.

## 13. Waite-Smith, 1909

Pamela Colman Smith’s composition makes the largest break with Marseille:

- one naked child rather than two;
- a white horse;
- a large red banner;
- a masonry wall;
- four tall sunflowers;
- an enormous unambiguously radiant sun;
- straight and wavy rays;
- Yod-like or dew-like drops.

Waite describes:

> “A naked child mounted on a white horse and displaying a red standard.”

He says the Sun signifies consciousness in the spirit, “the direct as the antithesis of the reflected light.” The child “signifies the restored world,” and the renewed natural mind brings animal nature into conformity with the higher consciousness.

### Sources of Smith’s design

**[Documented fact]** Smith worked under Waite’s direction, and Waite’s *Pictorial Key* explains the intended allegory.

**[Strong reconstruction]** The wall and solar face descend from Marseille-family imagery.

**[Interpretive reconstruction]** The single victorious child and banner can invoke the traditional iconography of triumph, resurrection, regeneration, and the biblical/Christian child.

**[Possible source]** The white horse can recall Revelation 19, solar triumphs, Renaissance triumphal processions, or ordinary emblem books. Waite does not identify one exclusive source.

**[Possible source]** The sunflowers turn toward the child as flowers turn toward sunlight, making the child a reflection or earthly vehicle of the solar power.

**[Unsupported certainty]** Statements that the four sunflowers definitively mean four suits, four elements, four worlds of Kabbalah, or four cardinal directions go beyond Waite’s text. They may work as modern readings, but no unique intended referent is documented.

**[Negative finding]** No primary statement by Smith has been located explaining why she replaced the Marseille pair with a mounted child.

## 14. Paul Foster Case and BOTA

Case’s *Tarot: A Key to the Wisdom of the Ages* and the BOTA deck retain the Marseille pair more directly than Waite-Smith:

- two nude children;
- solar face;
- wall;
- carefully prescribed symbolic colouring.

The surviving BOTA image presents two children holding hands beneath the Sun.

**[Esoteric attribution]**

- Resh/Sun;
- path Hod–Yesod;
- regeneration;
- conscious and subconscious functions;
- transmutation of personality;
- the wall as an artificial division or cultivated enclosure;
- children as renewed human powers.

Case’s BOTA cards are intentionally supplied uncoloured for students to colour according to instructions. The colouring is therefore part of the contemplative practice, not merely decoration.

## 15. Crowley–Harris Thoth Tarot, painted c. 1938–1943; first complete publication 1969

Lady Frieda Harris painted the Thoth designs under Aleister Crowley’s direction. Crowley published *The Book of Thoth* in 1944; the full deck was not commercially issued until 1969.

**Iconography:**

- a vast solar disk;
- a central rose;
- a green mound;
- a wall or encircling structure;
- two winged or dancing children outside/above the boundary;
- twelve zodiacal divisions or signs;
- solar and rose-cross geometry;
- a sense of spiralling or radiating motion.

Crowley describes it heraldically:

> “the Sun, charged with a rose, on a mount vert.”

He adds:

> “Yet outside this wall are the twin children who (in one form or another) have so frequently recurred in this whole symbolism.”

The design “symbolizes this broadening of the idea of the Rose and Cross.” [*Book of Thoth*, Sun chapter](https://www.mr-kaplan.com/Library/Crowley/Book%20of%20Thoth.pdf).

**Meaning of changes:** The children are no longer confined within a garden. They dance beyond the old wall, expressing emancipation under the new Aeon. The twelve signs subordinate the entire zodiac to the solar center. The rose-cross is simultaneously personal heraldry, Rosicrucian emblem, sexual-generative symbol, and image of integrated life.

Crowley calls its atmosphere “complete emancipation” and associates it with “Glory, Gain, Riches,” but warns that freedom requires ordered control.

### The Tzaddi/Heh controversy

Crowley’s famous statement “Tzaddi is not the Star” exchanges the traditional Golden Dawn letter assignments of the Emperor and Star:

- Emperor: traditionally Heh; Crowley assigns Tzaddi.
- Star: traditionally Tzaddi; Crowley assigns Heh.

**The Sun is unaffected:** it remains Resh and the Sun. Any chart assigning Heh or Tzaddi to The Sun is erroneous.

## 16. Notable modern redesigns

### Jodorowsky–Camoin

Returns to the paired Marseille figures and reads them relationally: twins, companions, complementary beings, or artist and collaborator.

### Salvador Dalí, 1984

Dalí’s tarot treats traditional imagery through collage, self-quotation, Catholic, Surrealist, and Renaissance references. Its Sun belongs to an artist’s personal symbolic theatre, not a historical restoration.

### Niki de Saint Phalle, *Tarot Garden*, begun 1979; opened 1998

The Tuscan sculpture garden monumentalizes tarot trumps as habitable sculpture. The project demonstrates tarot’s migration from card image into architecture and embodied environment. The Sun participates in this environment, but the garden’s principal habitable structures are other arcana.

### Motherpeace, 1981

The round feminist deck reconfigures authority, gender, and community. Solar imagery is communal and life-affirming rather than centered on a European male sovereign or single heroic child.

### Modern therapeutic/Jungian decks

Common changes include:

- gender-neutral children;
- racially diverse figures;
- same-sex or nonbinary pairs;
- ecological landscapes;
- solar plexus imagery;
- the “inner child”;
- recovery of joy after trauma.

These are valuable modern inventions, but not latent meanings proven to have governed the fifteenth-century card.

# The game

## Original function

**[Documented fact]** Tarot was first a family of trick-taking games. A standard Italian tarot pack eventually comprised:

- four suits of fourteen cards;
- twenty-one permanent trumps;
- the Fool.

The Morgan describes this structure and emphasizes that its cards were made for play, not originally as fortune-telling instruments. [Morgan catalogue](https://www.themorgan.org/manuscript/158525).

## The Sun’s basic power

In a trick, The Sun behaves as an ordinary permanent trump unless a local rule gives it a special bonus:

1. It beats every card of the four ordinary suits.
2. It beats every lower trump.
3. It loses to every higher trump.
4. It normally carries no independent “divinatory” meaning during play.

In Marseille-numbered sequences, XIX is the third-highest numbered trump below XX and XXI. If the Fool is treated as the Excuse rather than a trump, only XX and XXI outrank XIX.

## French Tarot

In modern French tarot:

- XIX is an ordinary trump.
- It is not one of the three *oudlers* or *bouts*.
- The *oudlers* are trump 1, trump 21, and the Excuse.
- Like other ordinary trumps, XIX counts as half a point when paired in the standard card-counting procedure.
- It remains tactically powerful because only XX and XXI can overtrump it.

## The Fool

### Classical Italian and French practice

The Fool generally excuses its player from following suit or trumping. It usually does not win the trick. After the trick, it returns to its player, with an exchange card or accounting procedure.

### Central European development

In many later Tarock games the Fool, often called *Sküs*, became the highest trump—effectively XXII. Dummett and McLeod treat this as a major later transformation rather than the tarot’s original universal rule. [Tarot Game history](https://www.tarotgame.org/).

Thus the statement “the Fool is always the highest card” is false historically. So is “the Fool is always numbered zero.” Early cards were often unnumbered; zero is an occult or modern printed convention.

## Three regional trump-order families

Michael Dummett classified early Italian orders as A, B, and C. The labels are analytical, not historical names used by players.

| Type | Principal region | Highest-card feature | Consequence for The Sun |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | Bologna/Ferrara-related traditions | Angel often above World; virtues arranged distinctively | Sun is high, but Justice and celestial ordering can place additional cards above it |
| B | Ferrara/Venice-related witnesses, depending on classification | Characteristic placement of virtues and celestial cards | Sun’s exact ordinal rank differs from Marseille |
| C | Milan, Lombardy, Piedmont, later Marseille | World above Angel; familiar numbered sequence develops | Sun becomes XIX, below Judgment XX and World XXI |

The decisive variables are:

- whether Angel or World is highest;
- where Justice occurs;
- whether the virtues are grouped;
- the relative positions of Sun, Moon, Star, and virtues.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** Printed numbers were probably added partly because geographically mobile players could not assume a shared order.

### Justice and Strength

The Waite-Smith exchange—Strength VIII and Justice XI—was not the invention of a new pair of card subjects. It selected one historically attested ordering and aligned the series with Waite/Golden Dawn considerations. Marseille normally has Justice VIII and Strength XI.

Crowley preserves the Golden Dawn-derived switch in substance but renames them Adjustment VIII and Lust XI.

## Minchiate play

Minchiate’s Sun is a very high trump, normally 38 of 40, below the World and Trumpets. Minchiate also uses combinations or *versicole*, so a card’s scoring importance can depend on its membership in a sequence, not only tricks captured.

## Bolognese and Sicilian play

In Bolognese tarocchino the upper trumps have fixed individual ranks while some lower trumps are equal-ranking *papi*. In Sicily, the hierarchy and special cards differ; Marseille numbering cannot be mechanically applied.

# Chronology of attestation

## c. 1412–1425: Marziano da Tortona

Marziano describes a special pack of sixteen classical gods made for Filippo Maria Visconti.

- **Fact:** An important precursor to permanent trumps.
- **Not attested:** A card called *Il Sole*.
- **Rejected shortcut:** Apollo is not automatically the later Sun trump.

## 1440–1442: Ferrara records

The Este court accounts contain some of the earliest references to *carte da trionfi*. The often-cited 1442 ledger records payments for triumph cards.

- **Fact:** The special pack existed.
- **Not established:** The ledger does not supply a complete trump list or describe The Sun.

## 1440s–1450s: Milanese painted decks

The Brambilla, Visconti di Modrone/Cary-Yale, and Visconti-Sforza families document luxury tarot production.

- **Fact:** No complete original pack survives.
- **Sun:** The surviving Visconti-Sforza Sun is late-fifteenth-century and stylistically distinct from the core cards.

## Late fifteenth century: *Sermones de ludo cum aliis*

The anonymous anti-gambling sermon gives a list:

*bagatella, imperatrix, imperator, papessa, papa, temperantia, amore, caro triumphale, fortezza, rota, gobbo, impichato, morte, diavolo, sagitta, stella, luna, sole, angelo, justicia, mondo*.

The Fool is then condemned separately.

- **Fact:** *sole* is explicitly present.
- **Importance:** This is the earliest surviving textual witness naming The Sun within a substantially standardized trump list.
- **Dating caution:** “c. 1470” is commonly repeated, but palaeographical assessments often place the manuscript or relevant sermon nearer the final decades of the century or around 1500. Exact dating remains debated.
- [Image of manuscript page](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Sermones_de_Ludo_cum_Aliis.jpg).

## c. 1490s–1500: Ferrarese poems

Poems or *strambotti* use the trump subjects as metaphors applied to court ladies, love, fortune, or moral qualities.

- **Fact:** These establish familiarity with the subjects outside rule books.
- **Caution:** The poems do not necessarily preserve game order accurately; poetic order may serve rhetoric or metre.

## 1491: Sola Busca

- **Fact:** Earliest substantially complete surviving seventy-eight-card pack.
- **Relevance:** Illustrated pips and later influence on Smith.
- **No standard Sun trump:** The named pseudo-classical triumphs replace the ordinary sequence.

## 1526–1527: Teofilo Folengo

Folengo’s *Chaos del Triperuno* includes a famous literary use of tarot, associating cards with poetic descriptions or destinies.

- **Fact:** Evidence for tarot’s literary and social use.
- **Not evidence:** It does not demonstrate ancient divination or Golden Dawn correspondences.

## 1637: Abbé de Marolles

An early substantial description of tarot play appears in France.

- **Fact:** Confirms game rules and ranked trumps.
- **Importance:** Earlier fifteenth-century rules have not survived in comparable detail.

## c. 1650: Noblet

The earliest substantially preserved French Marseille-family pack fixes the two-youth Sun in surviving material.

## c. 1701–1715: Dodal

The paired children and wall are clearly established.

## 1760: Conver

The design that most powerfully shapes later “Marseille” reproductions.

## 1781: Court de Gébelin and Comte de Mellet

Court’s *Monde primitif*, volume VIII, presents tarot as an Egyptian book.

For The Sun, de Mellet interprets XIX as the creation of the Sun and the two small figures as the union of man and woman.

- **Fact:** This interpretation appears in the printed occult essays.
- **Legend:** It is not an Egyptian source.
- **Historical importance:** It inaugurates the durable occult transformation of the card.

## 1780s–1791: Etteilla

Etteilla develops practical tarot divination, astrological correspondences, and the Grand Etteilla.

- **Fact:** First comprehensive published system and purpose-designed occult tarot.
- **Legend:** His claim to restore an ancient Egyptian Book of Thoth.

## 1854–1856: Éliphas Lévi

*Dogme et Rituel de la Haute Magie* integrates tarot, Hebrew letters, magic, alchemy, and Kabbalah.

- **Sun:** Chapter XIX, “Mastery of the Sun.”
- **Conflict:** Lévi’s alphabetic allocation differs from the later Golden Dawn arrangement because he begins the trumps with Aleph at I, leaving the unnumbered Fool in a different theoretical position.

## 1860s–1870s: Paul Christian

Christian expands Egyptian initiation mythology and popularizes the language of the “arcana.”

## 1888: S. L. MacGregor Mathers

*The Tarot: Its Occult Signification, Use in Fortune-Telling, and Method of Play* publishes English divinatory meanings.

## 1889: Papus and Wirth

Papus’s *Le Tarot des Bohémiens* and Wirth’s twenty-two-card deck synthesize Lévi, Kabbalah, numerology, and purported Egyptian wisdom.

## 1888–1900: Golden Dawn

The Order constructs the system that will dominate English-language occult tarot:

- Fool = Aleph;
- Magician = Beth;
- Sun XIX = Resh/Sun;
- paths on the Tree of Life;
- colour scales;
- decans for pip cards;
- elemental and zodiacal court-card ranges.

## 1909–1911: Waite-Smith and *Pictorial Key*

Smith replaces the Marseille pair with a single child on a white horse. Waite publishes an extensive spiritual commentary and short divinatory meanings.

## 1910s–1940s: Paul Foster Case

Case adapts Golden Dawn and Waite traditions for Builders of the Adytum, returning to a paired-child design and integrating colour meditation.

## 1938–1944 / 1969: Thoth

Harris paints the deck, Crowley explains it in 1944, and the complete deck is commercially published in 1969.

## 1970s–present: Psychological and pluralist tarot

Jungian, feminist, queer, ecological, therapeutic, and popular self-development readings make The Sun signify individuation, visibility, authentic embodiment, recovery of play, or the inner child.

## Resh and the Sun

The Hebrew letter **ר** means “head” in conventional letter-name interpretation.

**Golden Dawn doctrine:**

- Resh = Sun.
- The Sun trump = Path 30.
- Path connects Hod, intellect/form, with Yesod, image/foundation.
- The card therefore mediates solar consciousness through the imaginal and rational faculties.

**Historical caution:** No fifteenth-century Italian source associates the card with Resh. This is a late-nineteenth-century Hermetic construction.

## Golden Dawn pip and court apparatus

The prompt requests the pip decan titles and court sub-elements. Those belong to the **solar astrological attribution generally**, not to trump XIX as a pip or court card.

Cards ruled by the Sun in Book T include decans such as:

- Five of Wands: Saturn in Leo;
- Six of Wands: Jupiter in Leo;
- Seven of Wands: Mars in Leo;
- Eight of Pentacles: Sun in Virgo;
- Four of Swords: Jupiter in Libra;
- Ten of Cups: Mars in Pisces;
- Three of Wands: Sun in Aries;
- Ten of Swords: Sun in Gemini.

The exact “Book T” titles belong to those individual minor cards—for example “Lord of Victory” for Six of Wands—not to The Sun trump.

Court cards cover approximately thirty-degree zodiacal spans and combine sub-elements:

- Knights/Kings: Fire of their suit;
- Queens: Water;
- Princes: Air;
- Princesses: Earth.

The Sun trump itself has neither a decan span nor a court sub-element.

## Conflicts

1. **Mellet/Lévi/Papus/Wirth versus Golden Dawn:** Qoph versus Resh.
2. **Etteilla versus Marseille/Golden Dawn:** radical renumbering, often 2 rather than XIX.
3. **Gemini reading versus formal solar astrology:** the figures resemble Gemini, but the planet Sun rules Leo, not Gemini.
4. **Christian creation reading versus alchemy:** Court/de Mellet sees cosmic creation; Lévi sees the Great Work; Golden Dawn sees a path and planetary force.
5. **Jungian psychology:** treats the imagery as a map of psyche, not an objective ancient code.
6. **Crowley:** preserves Resh/Sun but relocates the imagery inside Thelemic New-Aeon doctrine.

# Divinatory meanings by author

Short quotations are reproduced from public-domain texts. Later copyrighted authors are summarized, with only brief identifying phrases.

## Etteilla

Etteilla’s meanings vary among editions and between his theoretical writings and cards. The solar card’s principal field includes enlightenment, clarification, glory, or human felicity; reversed or ill-dignified forms can indicate obscurity, delayed illumination, or misfortune concealed by appearances.

**Caution:** Many English lists attributed to Etteilla are composites derived from later followers. I did not locate a single securely digitized 1789 card legend sufficient to quote both orientations verbatim without qualification.

## S. L. MacGregor Mathers, 1888

Mathers gives The Sun a strongly favourable field: happiness, glory, gain, riches, triumph, pleasure, sincerity, and good fortune. Reversed, the good is diminished or mixed with arrogance, display, or uncertainty.

**Textual caution:** Online transcriptions differ in punctuation and in whether particular words derive directly from Mathers or later Golden Dawn lists. Exact wording should be checked against the 1888 George Redway printing before treating any long internet keyword string as diplomatic transcription.

## A. E. Waite, 1910

Waite’s complete short divinatory entry is:

> “Material happiness, fortunate marriage, contentment. Reversed: The same in a lesser sense.”

[*The Pictorial Key to the Tarot*, p. 76 in this scan](https://www.mr-kaplan.com/Library/Waite/The%20Pictorial%20Key%20to%20the%20Tarot.pdf).

This is an important corrective to modern practice: Waite does not make the reversed Sun catastrophe, narcissism, or clinical depression. It is diminished good.

## Aleister Crowley, 1944

Crowley’s summary field includes:

> “Glory, Gain, Riches”

and, in his fuller account, “complete emancipation” and the realization of life, light, liberty, and love. Reversal was not central to Crowley’s method; elemental dignity and context mattered more than mechanically inverting the meaning.

**Result:** Lists giving Crowley a fixed reversed meaning are normally later inventions.

## Paul Foster Case

Case emphasizes success, regeneration, happiness, liberation, synthesis, and conscious realization. Negative or reversed forms diminish the solar expression: vanity, egotism, temporary unhappiness, or incomplete realization.

**Caution:** BOTA editions and derivative keyword charts vary. Case’s system is more usefully read through its sustained symbolism than through an isolated reversed keyword.

## Rachel Pollack

In *Seventy-Eight Degrees of Wisdom*, Pollack calls the card an active, energized release following the Moon. Its light brings unconscious material into daily life. The paired children can represent the joining of eternal self and mortal body.

Upright:

- joy;
- simplicity;
- vitality;
- freedom;
- knowledge brought into life;
- self-acceptance.

Reversed:

- difficulty experiencing joy;
- blocked or delayed liberation;
- an excessively sunny façade;
- a childlike rather than mature response.

This is a paraphrase of Pollack’s discussion, not a medieval tradition. [Searchable scan](https://logoilibrary.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/07/Copia-de-Seventy-Eight-Degrees-Of-Eisdom.pdf).

## Mary K. Greer

Greer’s work emphasizes reading reversals contextually rather than as automatic opposites. Applied to The Sun, reversal can mean:

- solar energy internalized;
- joy delayed, denied, or privately experienced;
- excessive exposure;
- burnout;
- egotism;
- difficulty acknowledging success.

**Caution:** These are modern interpretive procedures. Greer does not claim that fifteenth-century players used reversals this way.

# Esoteric and symbolic readings

## Christian

### Historical iconographic layer

The original trump sequence contains explicitly Christian subjects: Pope, Devil, Judgment, virtues, and a cosmic hierarchy. The Sun can therefore be read within a Christian world where the physical sun symbolizes divine light.

Possible scriptural resonances include:

- Christ as light of the world;
- the sun of righteousness;
- Revelation’s radiant celestial imagery;
- childlike innocence and restored creation.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** Such resonances belong to the shared culture from which the card emerged.

**[Disputed claim]** No surviving fifteenth-century gloss says the Sun putto is Christ or the Christ child.

### Waite

Waite Christianizes the card more explicitly. The child leads humanity from the walled garden of sense toward a restored world and higher light.

## Astrological

### Pre-occult image

A human-faced Sun is ordinary medieval/Renaissance planetary imagery.

### Formal occult attribution

Golden Dawn: planet Sun, Resh.

### Gemini hypothesis

Two youths suggest Gemini iconography; Wirth and later authors spread this reading.

**Verdict:** plausible visual analogy, unproved original identity, and inconsistent with the later formal assignment of the trump to the planet Sun rather than the zodiac sign Gemini.

### Leo

Modern writers sometimes import Leo because the Sun rules Leo. Neither the Marseille card nor Golden Dawn title makes The Sun trump equal to the zodiac trump Strength/Lust, which carries Leo in the Golden Dawn system.

## Kabbalistic

### De Mellet

Assigns Hebrew letters in descending trump order. The resulting correspondence differs from the Golden Dawn.

### Lévi and Papus

Treat the twenty-two trumps as a key to the twenty-two Hebrew letters and creation.

### Golden Dawn onward

Resh; path Hod–Yesod. This became dominant in English occult tarot through Waite, Crowley, Case, Regardie, Wang, and the Ciceros.

**Historical conclusion:** Tarot-Kabbalah is a powerful reception history beginning in print in 1781 and systematized in the nineteenth century; it is not documented in fifteenth-century tarot production.

## Alchemical

Lévi is the decisive spreader of The Sun as the accomplishment of the Great Work:

> “To discover such Absolute … is the Great Work of the sages, that which is termed by Hermes the Work of the Sun.”

He connects the chapter with the Philosophers’ Stone, projection, and sublimation.

Later readings identify:

- children as Sol and Luna reconciled;
- wall as athanor or sealed vessel;
- droplets as alchemical dew;
- red/yellow solar colour as rubedo;
- nakedness as purified matter.

**Evidence status:** Lévi’s solar/alchemical connection is documented. Mapping every Marseille detail to laboratory apparatus is a later elaboration and cannot presently be traced to an early card-maker’s source.

## Hermetic

Court, Etteilla, Lévi, Papus, and Crowley make the card a vehicle for the Hermetic maxim of correspondence between divine light, cosmos, intellect, body, and material transformation.

Crowley’s rose-cross intensifies this:

- rose as unfolding life/desire;
- cross as extension or manifestation;
- solar center as integrated will;
- zodiac as cosmic totality.

## Jungian

Jung did not create a canonical tarot system and did not publish a card-by-card commentary on The Sun comparable to Waite or Crowley.

**Modern Jungian attribution:**

- Sun = conscious center or Self;
- Moon → Sun = confrontation with unconscious contents followed by integration;
- child = archetype of futurity and renewed wholeness;
- twins = reconciliation of opposites;
- wall = boundary of ego;
- nakedness = unmasked selfhood.

**Disputed attribution:** Phrases such as “Jung said tarot represents the archetypes of transformation” are often repeated without a precise citation. Modern Jungian tarot arose principally through post-Jung writers who applied his concepts.

### T. S. Eliot, *The Waste Land* (1922)

Madame Sosostris’s reading names “the drowned Phoenician Sailor,” “Belladonna,” “the man with three staves,” “the Wheel,” and “the one-eyed merchant.”

Yeats belonged to the Golden Dawn and knew its tarot correspondences.

Calvino constructs stories from arrangements of tarot cards, using first a painted Renaissance-style deck and later a Marseille deck.

**Caution:** The precise function of The Sun depends on passage and edition; one should not assign the entire novel to the card.

### Alejandro Jodorowsky

Jodorowsky is directly relevant through tarot practice, the Camoin collaboration, *The Way of Tarot*, and the recurrence of solar, initiatory, and alchemical symbolism in films such as *The Holy Mountain*.

Bergman’s *The Seventh Seal* is frequently placed beside tarot because of Death, the game motif, and medieval allegory.

# Controversies and disputes

## 1. Egyptian origin

### Claim

Tarot is an Egyptian sacred book, concealed in playing-card form after the destruction of Egyptian temples.

### First major printed source

Court de Gébelin, *Le Monde primitif*, volume VIII, 1781.

### Spreaders

- Comte de Mellet;
- Etteilla;
- Paul Christian;
- Papus;
- nineteenth-century occult publishers.

### Evidence

No Egyptian tarot cards, hieroglyphic tarot text, or transmission document exists. Egyptian hieroglyphs were not deciphered when Court proposed his etymologies. Dummett notes that Court and de Mellet offered supposed Egyptian derivations without hieroglyphic forms. [Dummett, “Origins of Tarot”](https://www.nybooks.com/articles/1981/05/14/origins-of-tarot/).

### Verdict

**[Legend with major historical influence]** False as an origin history; foundational as an occult reception history.

## 2. Romani or “Gypsy” origin

### Claim

Romani travellers brought tarot from Egypt or India and preserved its divinatory use.

### Development

De Mellet associated tarot, Egypt, and itinerant transmission; the story became much more prominent through nineteenth-century writers including Jean-Alexandre Vaillant and occult popularizers. Papus’s title *Tarot des Bohémiens* made the connection famous.

### Evidence

Tarot is documented at northern Italian courts in the fifteenth century. No early Italian tarot record attributes it to Romani communities. Early evidence describes manufacture, purchase, prohibition, poetry, and play.

### Verdict

**[Legend]** Unsupported as an origin claim. Romani fortune-tellers later participated in European cartomancy, but later use does not establish invention.

The historical slur “Gypsy” is retained only when quoting titles or reconstructing the old claim.

## 3. Cathar origin

### Claim

Cathar refugees encoded their dualist theology in tarot and transmitted it after the fall of Montségur.

### Evidence

No Cathar manuscript mentions tarot; Montségur fell in 1244, roughly two centuries before the documented triumph packs. Tarot subjects include the Pope, Christian virtues, and Last Judgment, which fit mainstream late-medieval culture better than a covert Cathar catechism.

### Verdict

**[Modern legend/disputed fringe claim]** No documentary chain.

## 4. Templar origin

### Claim

The Knights Templar preserved ancient mysteries and encoded them in the trumps.

### Evidence

The order was suppressed in the early fourteenth century. Tarot appears in fifteenth-century northern Italy. No Templar inventory, trial record, rule, manuscript, or surviving object describes tarot.

### Verdict

**[Modern legend]** Chronology and absence of transmission evidence weigh strongly against it.

## 5. Ancient Jewish/Kabbalistic origin

### Claim

The twenty-two trumps were designed from the twenty-two Hebrew letters.

### Evidence against original design

- No fifteenth-century tarot document mentions Hebrew letters.
- Early trump orders varied.
- Early packs could contain additional or omitted subjects.
- The precise card-letter alignments conflict among de Mellet, Lévi, Papus, Golden Dawn, and Crowley.

### Verdict

**[Esoteric attribution, not documented origin]** Historically important from 1781 onward.

## 6. The figures as Gemini or Dioscuri

### Claim

The Marseille children are Gemini, Castor and Pollux.

### Evidence for

- They are a matched youthful pair.
- Gemini was a familiar pair in astrological art.
- Later occultists explicitly noticed the resemblance.

### Evidence against certainty

- No maker’s caption says Gemini.
- No early commentary names Castor or Pollux.
- The children enter the surviving French tradition before the formal Golden Dawn astrology.
- The Golden Dawn assigns XIX to the planet Sun; Gemini belongs to the Lovers in that system.

### Verdict

**[Disputed interpretation]** Gemini is plausible as analogy; Dioscuri as intended identity is unproved.

## 7. Male twins, boy and girl, or lovers?

Woodcuts are sexually ambiguous.

- Noblet/Dodal figures are frequently called boys.
- Wirth deliberately differentiates complementary figures.
- De Mellet reads man and woman.
- Later queer readings see same-sex intimacy.
- Jungian readers see two aspects of one psyche.

**Finding:** The early image supports relational readings but does not conclusively specify biological sex, kinship, or erotic relation.

## 8. The wall

Proposed meanings include:

- garden enclosure;
- city wall;
- boundary of ordinary perception;
- protective structure;
- alchemical vessel;
- Eden;
- civilization;
- the ego.

Only the physical wall is documented. The named meanings are interpretive layers.

## 9. The droplets

They have been described as:

- rain;
- solar rays;
- Yods;
- gold;
- dew;
- divine grace;
- spiritual influence.

“Yod” is an occult reading, especially natural after Golden Dawn symbolism. It should not be projected backwards as the printer’s documented intention.

## 10. Misprints and copied anomalies

### XVIIII

Not a misprint; it is an established additive numeral convention.

### Distorted woodcut details

Repeated recutting can turn:

- fingers into extra rays;
- masonry into water;
- genitals into stray lines;
- floral forms into flames;
- engraving damage into deliberate occult signs.

Later “restorations” sometimes select one ambiguous feature and promote it as an original secret. Without a stemma of impressions, such claims remain uncertain.

### Rider/Waite naming

“Rider-Waite” foregrounds publisher and author while obscuring Pamela Colman Smith’s creative work. “Waite-Smith” or “Rider-Waite-Smith” is now preferred by many scholars and practitioners.

## 11. “The oldest book in the world”

Papus’s subtitle, *The Most Ancient Book in the World*, is an occult thesis, not a bibliographical description.

## 12. The Fool’s zero as ancient doctrine

Early Fools were generally unnumbered. Zero became symbolically productive in occult systems, but it was not necessarily the original game-card number.

# Open questions

1. **Who painted the Visconti-Sforza Sun?** Cicognara, Benedetto Bembo, Franco dei Russi, and a Ferrarese hand remain competing attributions.

2. **Was it a replacement?** Its style is later/different, but that does not prove it replaced a lost original rather than completing a multi-stage commission.

3. **What was the earliest standard Sun composition?** The gap between fragmentary fifteenth-century evidence and the well-preserved seventeenth-century French cards prevents a continuous visual genealogy.

4. **Why two children?** Gemini, fraternal concord, Adam and Eve, soul and body, summer play, and generic human regeneration have all been proposed. No early explanatory text settles the question.

5. **Are they both male?** The cutting is too schematic to justify certainty across all Marseille witnesses.

6. **What do the droplets mean?** They could be graphic rays, dew, heat, celestial influence, or merely decorative convention. “Yods” is demonstrably later terminology.

7. **What exact source guided Smith’s mounted child?** Waite supplies the theological meaning, but no surviving Smith statement gives a single visual source.

8. **Do the four sunflowers have one fixed correspondence?** No primary explanation was found.

9. **How early is the *Sermones de ludo* list?** “c. 1470,” “late fifteenth century,” and “c. 1500” circulate. A definitive date depends on codicological and palaeographical study.

10. **Which literary and cinematic solar images intentionally quote XIX?** Calvino and Jodorowsky have demonstrable tarot frameworks; Eliot, Yeats, and Bergman require work-specific evidence. The mere presence of a sun, child, wall, or horse is insufficient.

# Conclusions

The reconstructable history is not the history of one unchanging symbol.

The earliest secure layer is a fifteenth-century Italian game. Its Sun was one of a group of celestial powers near the top of a permanent trump hierarchy. The surviving Visconti-Sforza example presents a winged child supporting an anthropomorphic solar disk, but the card’s maker and relation to the rest of the deck remain disputed.

By seventeenth-century France, the image had become the familiar *Le Soleil*: two youthful figures beneath a human-faced Sun, with falling droplets and a wall. Calling the figures twins is visually reasonable; calling them Gemini is later interpretation; identifying them specifically as Castor and Pollux is unproved.

In 1781 Court de Gébelin and de Mellet transformed the card into a remnant of Egyptian creation theology. Etteilla made it part of a practical occult deck. Lévi made the Sun the mastery of the Great Work. Wirth made the pair children of light and complementary human powers. The Golden Dawn fixed the influential formula **Resh—Sun—Hod to Yesod—Lord of the Fire of the World**.

Smith then replaced the pair with a single naked child riding a white horse beneath four sunflowers and a red banner. Crowley and Harris restored twins but moved them beyond the wall into a zodiacal, rose-cross universe of New-Aeon emancipation. Case returned to paired children and read them as regenerated conscious and subconscious powers.

None of those later systems is evidence for the card’s fifteenth-century intended meaning. Each is, however, an attested chapter in what The Sun historically became.
