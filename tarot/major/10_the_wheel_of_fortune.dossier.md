# The Wheel of Fortune: Research Dossier

## Method and evidentiary labels

This dossier separates six kinds of statement:

- **[Documented fact]** directly supported by a surviving object, archival record, contemporary text, or the named author’s publication.
- **[Scholarly reconstruction]** an inference advanced by modern historians from surviving cards, documents, game rules, and comparative iconography.
- **[Tradition]** a historically transmitted convention whose first maker or precise origin may be unknown.
- **[Esoteric attribution]** a correspondence deliberately assigned by an occult author or organization; historically real as an attribution, but not evidence about fifteenth-century tarot.
- **[Disputed claim]** a proposal for which specialists disagree or the evidence is incomplete.
- **[Legend / modern invention]** a claim contradicted by the documentary chronology or first attested only in modern esoteric literature.

“Tarot” below can mean either the historic trick-taking game or, after the eighteenth century, a divinatory and esoteric system. Those histories overlap but are not identical.

## Names, terminology, and numbering

### *Trionfi* and *tarocchi*

- **[Documented fact]** In fifteenth-century Italian records, the new game and its special cards were initially called *carte da trionfi* and *trionfi*. A Ferrara account of 10 February 1442 pays Jacopo Sagramoro for coloring four packs, including “*tute le fegure de 4 para de chartexele da trionffy*.” A second entry of July 1442 records an inexpensive pack for Ercole and Sigismondo d’Este. The account names no individual Wheel. [Transcription and archival reference](https://www.oocities.org/autorbis/ferrara1442.html).
- **[Documented fact]** *Tarocchi* and cognates become the ordinary Italian game-name in the early sixteenth century. The special cards remain “trumps” in play.
- **[Scholarly reconstruction]** The linguistic passage from Italian *trionfo* to French *triomphe*, German *Trumpf*, and English “trump” concerns the gaming function. It does not demonstrate that Petrarch’s poem *I Trionfi* supplied a fixed card sequence.

### Regional names

- Italian cards and lists use **Ruota** or **Ruota di Fortuna**.
- French makers standardized **LA ROVE DE FORTVNE**, **LA ROUE DE FORTUNE**, or spelling variants.
- Spanish occult translations normally use **La Rueda de la Fortuna**.
- German occult decks use **Rad des Schicksals** (“Wheel of Fate”) or **Glücksrad** (“Wheel of Fortune”).
- Golden Dawn manuscript terminology includes **Wheel of Fortune** and the initiatory title **Lord of the Forces of Life**.
- Crowley deliberately compresses the title to **FORTUNE**, Atu X.

### Why other regional renamings matter

They demonstrate that trump titles were editable conventions, not an invariant ancient scripture:

- **[Documented fact]** In the Besançon/Swiss tradition the objectionable Papess and Pope were replaced by **Juno** and **Jupiter**, established by the eighteenth century.
- **[Documented fact]** Marseille XVI is traditionally **La Maison Dieu**, not originally “The Tower”; English occultists variously rendered it “The Tower,” “Blasted Tower,” or “House of God.”
- **[Documented fact]** The Fool is normally outside the numbered trump sequence in historic packs. Modern occult systems variously assign it 0, place it before I, after XXI, or between XX and XXI.
- **[Documented fact]** Death XIII is frequently untitled in old Marseille packs, although its identity was understood.
- **[Documented fact]** Justice and Strength exchange VIII/XI in Waite–Smith relative to Marseille and the Golden Dawn’s traditional numerical display. This does not alter Wheel X.
- **[Esoteric attribution]** Crowley’s Heh/Tzaddi revision concerns Emperor and Star, not Wheel: Fortune remains Kaph–Jupiter.

# Iconography deck by deck

## Before tarot: the Rota Fortunae

### Boethius and the medieval image

**[Documented fact]** Boethius’s *Consolation of Philosophy*, composed c. 524, makes Fortune defend her mutability in Book II: if she ceased turning her wheel, she would cease to be Fortune. The text supplies the philosophical principle—worldly station is unstable—but the familiar four-caption picture developed in medieval manuscript and monumental art rather than being specified as a tarot diagram by Boethius.

The four phases became:

1. **regnabo** — I shall reign;
2. **regno** — I reign;
3. **regnavi** — I have reigned;
4. **sum sine regno** — I am without a kingdom.

**[Tradition]** The crowned ruler at the summit, climber, falling ruler, and abject figure below circulated in manuscripts of Boethius, moralized chronicles, Boccaccio, church sculpture, rose windows, and pavement imagery. Fortune might be crowned, winged, double-faced, or blindfolded. The blindfold emphasizes indifference rather than moral judgment.

**[Documented fact]** Siena Cathedral had a Wheel of Fortune pavement panel recorded from 1372, although it was substantially restored or remade in 1864. It predates tarot and is useful contextual evidence, not evidence of a tarot quotation. [Siena pavement account](https://www.poderesantapia.com/engels/siena/sienaduomosaicfloor.htm).

The Wheel card’s closest reconstructable ancestry is therefore ordinary medieval moral iconography, not Egyptian hieroglyphics.

## Marziano da Tortona and Michelino da Besozzo, c. 1418–25

**[Documented fact]** Marziano da Tortona designed for Filippo Maria Visconti a game of sixteen classical gods in four orders, painted by Michelino da Besozzo. The pack itself is lost; Marziano’s explanatory text survives through a copy associated with Jacopo Antonio Marcello.

**[Important negative finding]** It contains no standard Wheel of Fortune trump. It is relevant as proof that an intellectually organized suit-and-trump game existed in Visconti Milan before the standard tarot, but it cannot be cited as the earliest Wheel.

## Visconti di Modrone / Cary-Yale, Milan, c. 1440s

**[Documented fact]** This unusually structured Visconti pack survives at Yale’s Beinecke Library and includes extra court cards and theological virtues.

**[Important negative finding]** No Wheel of Fortune survives in the Cary-Yale group. Any reconstruction of its Wheel from the Brambilla or Visconti-Sforza image is conjectural. “Visconti di Modrone” and “Cary-Yale” denote the same principal surviving group, not two independent Wheel cards.

## Brera-Brambilla, Milan, probably c. 1442–45

**[Documented fact]** Forty-eight cards survive, but only two trumps: Emperor and Wheel of Fortune. The pack is held by the Pinacoteca di Brera, Milan.

The Wheel shows the medieval Fortune mechanism rather than the later animal triad. Fortuna and human figures enact the rise, reign, fall, and dispossession of worldly power.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** Many catalogues associate the pack with Filippo Maria Visconti and Bonifacio Bembo’s workshop. The exact commission and dating remain debated. It is nonetheless one of the earliest surviving physical instances of the subject and may precede the better-known Morgan card.

## Visconti-Sforza / Pierpont Morgan–Bergamo, Milan, c. 1450–80

**[Documented fact]** The surviving Wheel is in the Morgan Library, MS M.630.9. Its observable features are:

- a large wheel;
- at its hub, a winged and blindfolded female Fortuna;
- an ass-eared man climbing, labeled **REGNABO**;
- an ass-eared crowned ruler at the summit, scepter in hand, labeled **REGNO**;
- a falling figure, with tail, labeled **REGNAVI**;
- a bearded man on hands and knees below, **SUM SINE REGNO**.

The asinine ears and tail turn the moral allegory into satire: those intoxicated by power become bestial or foolish. Blind Fortune does not reward virtue; her mechanism redistributes condition.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** Bonifacio Bembo is the Morgan’s attribution, but the surviving Visconti-Sforza cards were produced by more than one hand and at more than one date. The attribution should not be stated as absolute.

## Other fifteenth-century Milanese fragments

An uncut late-fifteenth- or early-sixteenth-century Milanese sheet preserves a Wheel among a series strongly ancestral to Marseille. The Fondazione Cini record identifies **Ruota della Fortuna**, Chariot, Love, Fortitude, Papess, Emperor, Empress, Pope, Sun, Moon, Star, Bagatto, Fool, Tower, Devil, and Temperance. It reports Dummett’s Milanese attribution based on design and trump order. [Fondazione Giorgio Cini catalogue](https://archivi.cini.it/storiaarte/detail/28002/carta-gioco-28002.html?lang=en).

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** These sheets are among the strongest links between late Milanese popular cards and the later French Marseille pattern. “Ancestor” means a typological line, not proof that a particular surviving sheet was copied in France.

## Ferrarese and Este cards

### Charles VI / Estensi-type painted cards

Several luxurious late-fifteenth-century cards associated stylistically with Ferrara survive in Paris and elsewhere.

**[Important negative finding]** The Wheel does not survive in the so-called Charles VI group. The old attribution to Charles VI of France is obsolete; the cards are Italian and later.

### Este cards

Scattered Este-associated trumps and suit cards survive, but no Wheel gives a comparably complete Ferrarese version. Ferrarese written orders nevertheless place the Wheel within the middle group of worldly vicissitudes.

## Sola Busca, Venice/Ferrara sphere, 1491

**[Documented fact]** The Sola Busca is the earliest substantially complete engraved tarot and the first surviving pack with fully illustrated pip scenes.

**[Important negative finding]** Its trumps are named pseudo-historical or classical warriors, not the standard allegorical Marseille figures; it therefore has no literal “Wheel of Fortune” card. Its relevance is chiefly to the later history of illustrated minor cards. Pamela Colman Smith demonstrably borrowed compositions from Sola Busca pips for several Waite–Smith minors, but no comparable direct Sola Busca source exists for her Wheel X.

## “Mantegna Tarocchi,” c. 1460s

**[Documented fact]** These are fifty didactic engravings ordered into five social and cosmological decades.

**[Important negative finding]** They are not a tarot pack and contain no playable Wheel of Fortune trump. Their cosmological imagery is relevant to Renaissance visual culture, but calling them “tarocchi” is a later misnomer.

## Rosenwald and Budapest sheets, late fifteenth/early sixteenth century

**[Documented fact]** These woodcut sheets are major witnesses to inexpensive popular tarot production, in contrast to courtly hand-painted packs.

- The **Rosenwald sheets**, generally associated with a Florentine or central-Italian/southern-order pattern, preserve a Wheel in simplified popular woodcut form.
- The **Budapest sheets** preserve another early popular pattern with incomplete and worn imagery.

The wheel, its attached human or hybrid figures, and the hierarchy of rising/falling are retained, but fine courtly details and inscriptions are reduced.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** Exact dates, workshops, and sequence relationships remain debated because the objects are fragmentary, uncut, and sometimes known through later impressions.

## Minchiate, Florence, sixteenth–nineteenth centuries

The expanded Minchiate pack has forty trumps plus the Fool. The Wheel is normally **IX**, not X, because the lower papal and imperial sequence differs. It preserves a large wheel and comic bestial/human climbers and fallers; some examples restore a prostrate person beneath.

Its meaning in play is rank, capture, and possible combination membership—not divination. The pack’s astrology appears in separate added zodiac and element trumps; that does not turn Wheel IX into a Jupiter card. [Minchiate rules and pack structure](https://www.pagat.com/tarot/minchiate.html).

## Bolognese pattern / Tarocchino

Early Bolognese cards were unnumbered, so players memorized the local order. The Wheel, called **Roda/Ruota**, belongs above the grouped virtues and below the higher cosmic/death sequence, according to the applicable form of the southern order.

The design tends toward a compact wheel with human or animalized participants. Later double-headed modernization reduced or rearranged old scenes.

**[Documented fact]** The shortened Tarocchino pack contains 62 cards, produced by removing low pip cards, not by deleting the Wheel. Dummett conjectured that shortening occurred around the mid-sixteenth century. [Dummett’s Bologna address](https://www.tarocchinobolognese.it/pages/sir_michael_dummett/il_saluto_allaccademia.php).

## Sicilian Tarot

Tarot reached Sicily probably from Rome during the seventeenth century. The surviving local pack is shortened to 63 cards. The Wheel remains part of the special trump sequence, with its own local drawing and ranking.

**[Documented fact]** Sicilian tarot employs game conventions unlike Marseille divination: shortened suits, local court arrangements, valuable combinations, and an Excuse-like Fool. [Sicilian rules and history](https://www.pagat.com/tarot/sicilian.html).

## Jean Noblet, Paris, c. 1650

Noblet’s **LA ROVE DE FORTVNE**, X, supplies the earliest surviving substantially complete Marseille-type rendering.

Observable changes from Visconti:

- Fortuna disappears;
- the four human conditions become three animalized or grotesque bodies;
- a crowned creature balances at the top;
- one creature rises and another descends;
- the wheel stands on a wooden support;
- a crank is attached to the axle;
- the surrounding ground or water is schematic.

The crank is paradoxical because nobody turns it. It implies an available but absent mover: chance, Providence, invisible agency, or simply inherited machinery.

Noblet’s spokes remain comparatively intelligible; later recutting progressively corrupts them. [Le Tarot iconographic comparison](https://www.letarot.it/The-Wheel-of-Fortune_pag_pg271_eng.aspx).

## Jean Dodal, Lyon, c. 1701–15

Dodal retains X, the three creatures, crown, wheel, axle, crank, and platform. Lines become more decorative and anatomies more hybrid. The top creature is frequently called a sphinx in modern writing, but it lacks the full Egyptian program of Lévi, Wirth, Waite, and Crowley.

**[Disputed identification]** Calling all three Marseille creatures “Sphinx, Hermanubis, and Typhon” projects nineteenth-century occult nomenclature backward. They are securely describable as grotesque or animalized Fortune figures; the Egyptian names are later interpretations.

## Nicolas Conver, Marseille model engraved 1760

Conver’s 1760 model became enormously influential through later Marseille manufacture. The wheel is more stylized, the spokes less mechanically coherent, and the three figures less anatomically distinct.

The title remains **LA ROVE DE FORTVNE** or normalized **LA ROUE DE FORTUNE** in later editions.

**[Important bibliographic caution]** “1760 Conver” names the engraved model. A surviving BnF or commercial impression may be nineteenth century; model date and physical impression date must not be conflated.

## Grimaud / Paul Marteau

Grimaud popularized industrial Marseille cards in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries. Paul Marteau’s **Ancien Tarot de Marseille**, 1930, regularized the palette, lines, typography, and interpretive presentation, making the Conver-derived Wheel a twentieth-century standard.

**[Modern invention presented as restoration]** Some color symbolism in Marteau’s book belongs to his own occult interpretation rather than being demonstrably encoded by seventeenth-century cardmakers.

## Camoin–Jodorowsky, 1997

Philippe Camoin and Alejandro Jodorowsky constructed a “restored” Marseille deck from old patterns, especially the Camoin/Conver inheritance, while correcting, completing, and recoloring details.

The Wheel retains the crank, three creatures, blue support, and red/yellow/blue palette, but lines and color spots are editorially reconstructed.

**[Disputed claim]** Jodorowsky sometimes speaks of recovering a primordial or authentic Tarot. The product is historically valuable as a late-twentieth-century synthesis, but no complete ur-deck survives against which its restorations can be verified. It is therefore a reconstruction, not a facsimile.

## Etteilla’s Grand Etteilla, Paris, 1788–89

Etteilla reordered the entire pack to conform to his purported Egyptian cosmogony. The conventional Wheel moved from X to **card 20**, **Fortune**. The card carries upright and reversed reading words and belongs to a deliberate divinatory program.

Depending on state/type, the image is a Fortune-wheel composition, but its identity is controlled as much by printed keywords as by medieval iconography.

**[Esoteric attribution]** Etteilla treated the pack as the Book of Thoth and correlated it with astrology and elemental/cosmogonic doctrine. These are first-generation occult redesigns, not survivals from Egypt.

## Court de Gébelin’s reconstructed trumps, 1781

Gébelin reproduced or “corrected” a Marseille-like Wheel as evidence for his Egyptian interpretation. He retained **La Roue de Fortune** as number 10.

**[Legend]** His belief that the image was Egyptian was an interpretive assertion made before hieroglyphic decipherment, unsupported by an Egyptian object or text.

## Éliphas Lévi, 1850s–60s

Lévi transformed the Wheel into what he called the cosmogonic wheel of Ezekiel:

- Hermanubis ascending;
- Typhon descending;
- a sphinx poised above with a sword;
- a wheel associated with astral light, universal motion, and equilibrium.

The clearest published diagram is associated with *La Clef des grands mystères* (1861); related doctrine grows out of *Dogme et rituel de la haute magie* (1854–56).

A later English account summarizes the design: “Hermanubis stands on one side, and Typhon on the other; above is the Sphynx holding a sword.” See *Magical Ritual of the Sanctum Regnum*, English ed., p. 41. [Digitized edition](https://biglevi.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/03/Magical-Ritual-Of-The-Sanctum-Regnum-E-Levi-1896.pdf).

**[Esoteric attribution]** This is the source of the named Egyptian triad later inherited by Wirth, Waite, and Crowley. It is not an identification documented on Noblet, Dodal, or Conver.

## Oswald Wirth, Paris, 1889

Drawn under Stanislas de Guaita’s direction, Wirth’s hand-colored **Les 22 Arcanes du Tarot Kabbalistique** shows:

- a six-spoked wheel on a mast rising from a crescent-shaped base;
- a caduceus-bearing Hermanubis climbing;
- a trident-bearing Typhon falling;
- a crowned, sword-bearing sphinx above.

Wirth therefore visually fixes Lévi’s verbal/diagrammatic occult “correction.” [BnF-copy description and deck](https://foolmade.art/archive/wirth-tarot), [individual Wheel](https://foolmade.art/archive/wirth-tarot/card-010).

## Golden Dawn manuscript designs, c. 1888–1900

The Golden Dawn did not prescribe one mass-produced deck. Members colored or constructed personal packs from initiatory descriptions.

The Wheel’s governing data were:

- **Kaph כ**;
- **Jupiter**;
- **Lord of the Forces of Life**;
- yellow-violet, blue-violet, deep blue, and associated scale colors depending on the grade/system;
- path between Chesed and Netzach in the later conventional Tree diagram.

The designs continue Lévi/Wirth’s sphinx–Hermanubis–Typhon scheme, enriched by zodiacal, elemental, and color-scale symbolism.

### Robert Wang reconstruction, 1978

Robert Wang’s *Golden Dawn Tarot*, made with Israel Regardie, is a modern visualization of Golden Dawn documents. Its Wheel uses the Egyptianized triad and formal color correspondences.

### Cicero reconstruction, 1991 and later

Chic and Sandra Tabatha Cicero’s *New Golden Dawn Ritual Tarot*, later *Golden Dawn Magical Tarot*, is another reconstruction. It is not evidence that a single identical nineteenth-century “official Golden Dawn deck” once circulated.

## Waite–Smith, 1909; book 1910/11

Pamela Colman Smith draws an eight-spoked wheel floating in clouds:

- **T A R O** appears around the rim and can be cyclically read as **ROTA**;
- the Hebrew letters **Yod–Heh–Vav–Heh** alternate with them;
- alchemical symbols appear on the inner wheel;
- a yellow serpent descends at left—Waite calls it Typhon in serpent form;
- a red jackal-headed figure rises at right, ordinarily identified as Anubis or Hermanubis;
- a blue sphinx with sword sits above;
- winged Man/Angel, Eagle, Lion, and Bull occupy the corners, each with a book.

Waite explicitly states:

> “In this symbol I have again followed the reconstruction of Éliphas Lévi.”

He adds that the four Living Creatures of Ezekiel occupy the corners and that the transliteration of Tarot as Rota is interwoven with the Divine Name. He interprets it as “the perpetual motion of a fluidic universe and … the flux of human life”; the sphinx supplies equilibrium. *The Pictorial Key to the Tarot*, Part II, X. [Primary text](https://sacred-texts.com/book/the-pictorial-key-to-the-tarot/shell/x-wheel-of-fortune).

### Smith’s sources

- **[Documented through Waite’s text]** Lévi’s Wheel was the programmatic source.
- **[Documented iconographic tradition]** Ezekiel/Revelation supplied the four living creatures.
- **[Strong scholarly inference]** Smith synthesized Waite’s instructions with contemporary occult-Egyptian imagery.
- **[Important correction]** Sola Busca influenced several Smith minor cards, but no evidence makes it the source of Wheel X.
- **[Disputed detail]** The ascending figure is often called Anubis, while Lévi’s fuller term is Hermanubis. Smith’s drawing does not function as an archaeologically exact Egyptian representation.

## Paul Foster Case and BOTA, 1920s onward

Case retained X, Kaph, Jupiter, the four living creatures, the divine name, ROTA/TARO permutations, sphinx, serpent, and ascending jackal figure. Jessie Burns Parke drew the BOTA deck under Case’s direction as an uncolored key intended for personal coloring.

Case gives the wheel eight spokes rather than a literal medieval four-position sequence and reads rotation as cosmic law, involution/evolution, and cyclic manifestation. He connected TARO/ROTA with TORA/Torah and developed extensive gematria.

**[Esoteric attribution]** These word permutations and numerical readings are productive twentieth-century occult constructions; they do not occur on fifteenth-century Wheels.

## Thoth: Crowley and Frieda Harris, painted 1938–43; published 1969

Crowley renames the card **FORTUNE**. Harris depicts an immense, vertically turbulent wheel in violet, blue, and lightning-like energy. Three beings occupy it:

- the **Sphinx** above;
- **Hermanubis**, the ape-like or cynocephalic intelligence;
- **Typhon**, the descending reptilian or composite force.

The wheel has **ten principal spokes**, aligned with the ten Sephiroth or “principal bodies” in Crowley’s cosmology. It turns with explosive velocity against a Jupiterian field.

Crowley writes:

> “Follow thy Fortune, careless where it lead thee. The axle moveth not: attain thou that.”

He glosses the practical meaning as “Change of fortune,” generally good because consultation implies dissatisfaction. [*Book of Thoth*, general characters](https://hermetic.com/crowley/book-of-thoth/general-characters).

**[Documented fact]** The paintings were completed in the 1938–43 period and published as a deck only in 1969, after both creators’ deaths.

**[Important correction]** Crowley’s “Tzaddi is not the Star” revision relocates Heh and Tzaddi between Emperor and Star. It does not change Fortune’s Kaph–Jupiter attribution.

## Notable modern redesigns

- **Aquarian Tarot, David Palladini, 1970:** Art Deco/psychedelic simplification of the RWS occult wheel.
- **Morgan-Greer, Bill Greer/Lloyd Morgan, 1979:** close-cropped RWS-derived imagery.
- **Motherpeace, Vicki Noble and Karen Vogel, 1981:** circular feminist deck; the card’s very format intensifies cyclicality.
- **Mythic Tarot, Juliet Sharman-Burke and Liz Greene, 1986:** associates the Wheel with the Moirai/Fates—an explicit modern mythographic substitution.
- **DruidCraft and seasonal decks:** recast the Wheel as the Wheel of the Year, a modern Pagan correspondence rather than an early tarot meaning.
- **Jungian and therapeutic decks:** often remove Fortune’s external mechanism and emphasize psychological pattern, recurrence, synchronicity, or life transition.
- **Afrofuturist, queer, decolonial, and culturally specific decks:** replace medieval hierarchy or Egyptianizing figures with culturally situated images of time, social change, ancestry, and contingency. These are intentional modern revisions, not corruptions of a single canonical original.

# The game

## Function

**[Documented fact]** The Wheel originated as a permanent trump in a trick-taking game. It beats ordinary suit cards but loses to higher trumps. Its picture did not by itself confer a special action.

In classic 78-card tarot:

- 21 trumps form a ranked suit;
- the Fool is separate and commonly functions as an **Excuse**, allowing its player to avoid following suit or losing it in the ordinary manner;
- local rules determine whether the Fool must later be compensated, can be captured at the last trick, or exceptionally acts as a trump.

## Rank

In the Marseille/Western order, Wheel is X:

`… Lovers VI – Chariot VII – Justice VIII – Hermit IX – Wheel X – Strength XI – Hanged Man XII …`

It is a middle trump, not normally a high-value counting card. In many games its only intrinsic value is the ordinary value assigned to a non-counting trump; its tactical importance comes from rank and timing.

### The three regional orders

Michael Dummett classified early Italian sequences as:

| Type | Region | Relevant order |
|---|---|---|
| A, Southern | Bologna, Florence; related later forms in Rome/Sicily | Virtues grouped; Angel commonly outranks World in Bologna; Wheel’s exact numeral differs from Marseille |
| B, Eastern | Ferrara, probably Venice | Temperance low; Justice very high, often immediately below World; Wheel remains in the central worldly group |
| C, Western | Milan, Piedmont, France/Marseille | The familiar Marseille sequence: Justice VIII, Wheel X, Strength XI; World above Judgement |

This threefold classification is a scholarly synthesis of surviving lists and rules, not a single fifteenth-century regulation. [Dummett translation and discussion](https://dummettsmondo.blogspot.com/2015/07/chapters-6-and-7-game-and-its-trump.html).

### Justice and Strength

- Marseille: Justice VIII, Strength XI.
- Waite–Smith: Strength VIII, Justice XI.
- Crowley: Adjustment VIII, Lust XI.
- Historic regional orders sometimes cluster virtues elsewhere entirely.

**[Esoteric claim]** Waite’s transposition is usually explained as bringing zodiacal Leo and Libra into Golden Dawn alphabetic order while concealing or revising the inherited numbering. It is not an old game rule.

### The Fool

- Historic tarot: usually unnumbered.
- In play: Excuse, not simply “trump 0.”
- In Gébelin/Mellet and later occult sequences: placed at different alphabetic points.
- In Waite–Smith: printed 0.
- In Crowley: 0, Aleph/Air.

## Point value

In French Tarot, the special high-value cards are the 1 of trumps, 21 of trumps, and the Excuse—the *oudlers*. Wheel X is an ordinary trump for counting purposes.

In many Italian games, point-scoring concentrates on the Fool, Magician/Bagatto, highest trumps, court cards, and combinations. Wheel is not generally one of the chief individual counters.

In Minchiate, scoring emphasizes the five unnumbered *arie*, other designated counting cards, and *versicole*. Wheel IX is not equivalent to French Tarot’s oudlers.

In Central European Tarock, subject pictures often disappeared or became genre scenes. “Trump X” may occupy the corresponding numerical rank without being called Wheel of Fortune. Only the lowest and/or highest trumps have special values in many games.

# Chronology of attestation

## c. 524 — Boethius

**[Documented fact]** *Consolation of Philosophy*, Book II, articulates Fortune’s turning wheel as the necessary expression of her instability. It is the principal literary transmitter, not the inventor of every wheel-of-fortune image.

## Twelfth–fourteenth centuries — medieval Rota Fortunae

**[Documented fact]** Illustrated Boethius manuscripts, cathedral sculpture, rose windows, manuscripts of moral and historical works, and Boccaccian imagery circulate the king’s four conditions. The formula supplies the immediate visual vocabulary later used by Milanese tarot.

## 1372 — Siena Cathedral

A Wheel of Fortune pavement is recorded. The present appearance includes the 1864 restoration and must not be treated as an untouched fourteenth-century card prototype.

## c. 1418–25 — Marziano da Tortona

Marziano’s text describes sixteen deity-trumps made for Filippo Maria Visconti and painted by Michelino da Besozzo.

**Finding:** no Wheel; a precursor to tarot’s invention, not a standard tarot list.

## 1440 — Florentine reference

A record concerning two packs of *trionfi* sent to Sigismondo Pandolfo Malatesta is often cited as the earliest secure use of the game-name.

## 1442 — Ferrara ledgers

Jacopo Sagramoro is paid for four packs; another pack goes to the young Este princes. No individual trumps are named.

## c. 1442–45 — Brambilla Wheel

One of the earliest surviving Wheel cards.

## c. 1450–80 — Visconti-Sforza Wheel

The fullest early surviving rendering of Fortuna plus the four Latin stages.

## c. 1470–1500 — *Sermo de ludo*

The anonymous preacher condemns the *ludus triumphorum* and lists its subjects. **Rota** appears among them.

**[Documented fact]** This establishes the named Wheel inside a standard trump series.

**[Dating caution]** “c. 1470” is common shorthand; catalogues and scholars also use c. 1480 or the broader 1450–1500 range.

## Late fifteenth century — appropriated-trump poetry

*Tarocchi appropriati* assign trump subjects to named persons, proving that audiences could treat card titles as metaphors. Surviving examples cluster around northern Italian courts and academies.

## 1491 — Sola Busca

Complete illustrated suit program, but no standard Wheel title among its pseudo-historical trumps.

## c. 1500 — Ferrarese poems and lists

Poetic trump lists confirm the eastern order. The Wheel remains a metaphor for reversal and political instability.

## 1527 — Folengo

Teofilo Folengo’s *Chaos del Triperuno* includes a literary tarot divination episode and sonnets associated with selected cards.

**[Documented fact]** It is early evidence of imaginative and divinatory-like literary use.

**[Caution]** It is not evidence for a comprehensive Golden Dawn-style system or for an ancient occult doctrine.

## Sixteenth century — *tarocchi* replaces *trionfi*

The new name becomes standard. Numbering spreads in Milanese/Ferrarese-derived regions, while Bologna long retains unnumbered trumps.

## c. 1650 — Noblet

Earliest surviving substantially complete Marseille-type Wheel.

## 1701–15 — Dodal

Lyon continuation of the Marseille I pattern.

## 1760 — Conver model

The most influential Marseille II Wheel.

## 1781 — Court de Gébelin and Comte de Mellet

Gébelin asserts Egyptian origin. Mellet makes the first published systematic Hebrew-letter comparison and publishes a divinatory method. [Original French transcription](https://www.tarock.info/gebelin.htm), [Morgan overview](https://www.themorgan.org/exhibitions/online/tarot/11).

## 1783–89 — Etteilla

Etteilla turns these speculations into a professional cartomantic curriculum and purpose-built deck. Wheel becomes Fortune 20.

## 1854–56 — Lévi, *Dogme et rituel*

Tarot is integrated with ceremonial magic, Kabbalah, astral light, and Hebrew letters.

## 1861 — Lévi, *La Clef des grands mystères*

The sphinx–Hermanubis–Typhon Wheel is graphically established.

## 1888 — Mathers

S. L. MacGregor Mathers publishes *The Tarot: Its Occult Signification, Use in Fortune-Telling, and Method of Play*.

## 1888–89 — Golden Dawn and Wirth

The Golden Dawn fixes Kaph/Jupiter; Wirth publishes the Lévi-derived occult image.

## 1909–11 — Waite–Smith and *Pictorial Key*

Smith produces the now-dominant English-language image; Waite explicitly documents its Lévi and Ezekiel sources.

## 1938–43 / 1969 — Thoth

Harris paints Crowley’s Fortune; the deck appears commercially in 1969.

## 1969–73 — Calvino

Calvino uses Visconti-Sforza cards in the first portion of *Il castello dei destini incrociati* and Marseille cards in the second.

## 1970s–present

RWS-derived, Jungian, feminist, Pagan, therapeutic, and culturally revisionist Wheels proliferate.

## The central conflict: Kaph versus Yod

Golden Dawn Kaph is not “the ancient Hebrew correspondence.”

- Mellet was first to correlate 22 cards and 22 letters in print, but used a different ordering logic.
- Papus explicitly presents Wheel X as **Yod**, “the forefinger,” and associates it with Virgo/August. His text says: “The wheel of fortune suspended upon its axis,” with Anubis ascending, Typhon descending, and the Sphinx ruling the ternary. [Papus primary text](https://guru-ai.org/read/western_esoteric/tarot-of-the-bohemians/051).
- Golden Dawn assigns the Fool to Aleph and proceeds through the trumps so Wheel X receives the eleventh letter, Kaph.
- Later English-language tarot often treats Kaph/Jupiter as universal because Waite, Crowley, and Case made the Golden Dawn system globally influential.

## Kaph

**[Esoteric attribution]** Kaph means palm or hollow of the hand and has numerical value 20. Golden Dawn authors linked grasp, capacity, reception, and the hand that turns or controls with Fortune’s power. The pun is interpretive; it is not printed on early Wheels.

## Jupiter

**[Esoteric attribution]** Jupiter supplies expansion, beneficence, authority, cyclic growth, and ordered abundance. It helps explain why Golden Dawn-derived divination tends to treat Wheel upright as favorable change rather than morally neutral instability.

This differs sharply from the medieval Rota, where kings rise and fall without a promise of improvement.

## Golden Dawn color scales

Common printed reconstructions give Kaph/Jupiter colors along these lines:

- King scale: violet;
- Queen scale: blue;
- Emperor scale: rich purple or blue-violet;
- Empress scale: bright blue rayed yellow, with variation among printed tables.

Exact pigment wording differs among manuscript lineages and editors. Wang and the Ciceros should be treated as modern reconstructions of this material.

## Pips and courts: requested system context

Wheel itself has no decan or court sub-element, but Book T’s surrounding system assigns:

- numbered minors 2–10 to zodiacal decans, each with a planetary ruler and title;
- aces to elemental roots;
- Knights/Kings, Queens, Princes, and Princesses to elemental combinations and zodiacal spans.

Examples involving Jupiter and therefore sometimes compared with Fortune include:

- Four of Wands: Venus in Aries, **Lord of Perfected Work**;
- Six of Wands: Jupiter in Leo, **Lord of Victory**;
- Nine of Cups: Jupiter in Pisces, **Lord of Material Happiness**;
- Two of Pentacles/Disks: Jupiter in Capricorn, **Lord of Harmonious Change**.

The Two of Disks is especially relevant in Crowley: its Thoth title **Change** echoes Fortune, and Crowley explicitly notes that Jupiter “being himself the Wheel” reinforces the card. [Crowley on the small cards](https://hermetic.com/crowley/book-of-thoth/small-cards).

Court zodiacal spans do not belong to Wheel X and should not be imported into it. They are part of the same Golden Dawn tabular architecture.

## Tzaddi/Heh and Emperor/Star

Crowley interpreted *The Book of the Law*’s “Tzaddi is not the Star” as requiring:

- Emperor: Tzaddi/Aquarius;
- Star: Heh/Aries,

while preserving their displayed IV/XVII positions. Wheel remains Kaph/Jupiter. Claims that the swap changes Wheel’s letter are erroneous.

### A textual problem in the Waite/Mathers list

“Reversed: increase, abundance, superfluity” looks counterintuitive. It is not necessarily a printer’s accidental inversion: it belongs to an Etteilla-influenced stream where reversal may modify, intensify, or redirect rather than negate. Later manuals often replace it with “bad luck,” “delay,” or “resistance,” producing a modern convention rather than preserving Waite’s words.

# Esoteric and symbolic readings

## Medieval Christian-moral reading

**First secure matrix:** medieval Fortune literature and art; decisively articulated through Boethius and widely spread in Christian Europe.

The Wheel contrasts mutable earthly goods with the stable highest good. Kingship, wealth, health, and status belong to Fortune’s rim; wisdom seeks the unmoving center.

The tarot image is not necessarily anti-Christian. Fortune could be subordinated to Providence: apparently random worldly change exists within divine order.

## Political and courtly reading

In Visconti Milan, the king’s four conditions had immediate relevance to dynastic precarity. Dukes, condottieri, captives, and exiles knew how quickly power changed hands.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** The ass-ears mock the ruler’s pride. This is visually plausible and consistent with medieval satire, but no surviving commission text explains the card.

## Hermetic and astral-light reading

Lévi recasts the wheel as the circulation of the “great magical agent” or astral light. The opposed creatures are polarized forces; the sphinx is initiatory knowledge that stays balanced above them.

**First major spreader:** Lévi, followed by Christian, Papus, Wirth, Golden Dawn, Waite, and Crowley.

## Kabbalistic reading

The central Golden Dawn reading gives Wheel to Kaph and the path between Chesed and Netzach:

- Chesed/Jupiter: expansive ordering power;
- Netzach: desire, nature, recurrence, and affective momentum;
- Kaph: receptive or grasping hand;
- Wheel: patterned circulation of force.

**Historical caution:** this is a nineteenth-century Hermetic-Qabalistic overlay. No Hebrew appears on the Milanese or early Marseille Wheels.

## Astrological reading

- Papus: Yod/Virgo/August in his particular table.
- Golden Dawn, Waite’s esoteric background, Crowley, and Case: Kaph/Jupiter.
- Etteilla: a separate astrological program not reducible to Golden Dawn Jupiter.
- Marseille readers such as Jodorowsky may interpret Wheel X without astrology.

The conflict is real; there is no single “traditional” astrological attribution before occultists created one.

## Ezekiel and the four living creatures

Lévi called the card a cosmogonic Wheel of Ezekiel. Waite places the four winged beings of Ezekiel/Revelation in the corners.

In biblical iconography they became the tetramorph:

- Man/Angel — Matthew;
- Lion — Mark;
- Ox/Bull — Luke;
- Eagle — John.

Golden Dawn and later occultists additionally map them to fixed zodiacal signs:

- Aquarius;
- Leo;
- Taurus;
- Scorpio.

**[Esoteric synthesis]** Waite’s corners deliberately fuse biblical, zodiacal, and occult meanings. They are absent from the Marseille card.

## Alchemical reading

The RWS inner wheel bears signs commonly read as mercury, sulfur, salt, and a water-like or dissolving sign. Modern interpreters see rotation as *solve et coagula*: differentiation, dissolution, recombination.

**[Caution]** Waite confirms the Lévi/Ezekiel program but does not provide a fully itemized alchemical exegesis for every mark. Later detailed identifications often exceed his text.

Crowley’s Fortune makes change itself the cosmic process: apparently stable form is high-speed patterned energy. Harris’s violet field and lightning make Jupiter dynamic rather than merely beneficent.

## Buddhist/Hindu wheel analogies

Crowley explicitly compares the Tarot wheel with Samsara, Dharma, heavens, destiny, and life:

> “All these Wheels be one; yet … the Wheel of the TARO alone avails thee consciously.”

[*Book of Lies*, “Wheel and—Whoa!”](https://hermetic.com/crowley/book-of-thoth/wheel-and-whoa).

**[Esoteric syncretism]** Similarity to dharmachakra or samsara is a comparative spiritual interpretation, not evidence that the medieval tarot Wheel derives from Buddhism.

## Jodorowsky and Marianne Costa

In *The Way of Tarot* the crank becomes crucial: the cycle may have reached a turning point, but a conscious agent must operate it. The three creatures can represent instinctual, emotional, and mental dimensions or stages of ascent/descent.

This shifts emphasis from passive fate to psychospiritual participation.

**[Modern invention]** The idea that a specific hidden “initiatory code” was deliberately preserved intact by generations of Marseille printers is not established by workshop documents.

## Jungian readings

Modern Jungian tarotists read:

- rim as ego’s changing circumstances;
- center/axle as the Self or regulating center;
- ascent/descent as inflation and deflation;
- repeated revolution as complexes or recurring life patterns;
- a fortuitous draw as synchronicity;
- sphinx as the riddle or transcendent function holding opposites.

**First appearance:** Jung himself did not write a systematic Wheel-of-Fortune tarot commentary. Jungian tarot develops among later interpreters, notably Sallie Nichols, *Jung and Tarot* (1980), and spreads through Pollack and therapeutic tarot literature.

Thus “Jung said the Wheel means…” is normally a misattribution. The defensible phrase is “Jungian tarot authors interpret…”

## Literature

### T. S. Eliot, *The Waste Land* (1922)

Madame Sosostris produces “the Wheel” in line 51. Eliot mixes genuine tarot cards with invented or renamed figures. The Wheel participates in the poem’s imagery of sterile repetition, historical recurrence, and possible spiritual turning.

**[Documented fact]** The poem names the Wheel.

**[Disputed interpretation]** Exact one-to-one identification of every Sosostris card is contested. Eliot’s notes acknowledge selective unfamiliarity and invention; the passage is poetry, not a genuine spread manual. [Poem-context discussion](https://wasteland.windingway.org/43/madame-sosostris-famous-clairvoyante), [scholarly discussion](https://dspace.uni.lodz.pl/bitstream/handle/11089/14432/6_Acta%20Universitatis%20Lodziensis.%20Folia%20Littereria%20Anglica%2008.%202009.pdf?sequence=1).

### Italo Calvino, *Il castello dei destini incrociati*

The first component appeared in 1969 with Visconti-Sforza cards; the combined work appeared in 1973, with a second Marseille-based section. Mute travelers arrange cards to tell intersecting stories.

The Wheel operates as narrative reversal, recurrence, and the combinatorial machine of story. [Publication summary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Castle_of_Crossed_Destinies).

### W. B. Yeats

Yeats belonged to the Golden Dawn and used cycles, gyres, lunar phases, and occult correspondences extensively.

**Important negative finding:** Although his rotating gyres invite comparison with Wheel X, a specific canonical Yeats work centered unmistakably on the Wheel of Fortune tarot card could not be securely traced in the consulted primary sources. General claims that “Yeats based the gyres on this card” should therefore be labeled speculative.

## Film

### Alejandro Jodorowsky

*The Holy Mountain* (1973) contains pervasive tarot, alchemical, planetary, and initiatory imagery. Jodorowsky later became an influential Marseille interpreter and used tarot in psychomagic.

**Caution:** Individual shots may echo wheel symbolism, but the whole film should not be reduced to a literal adaptation of trump X.

### Ingmar Bergman

Bergman’s *The Seventh Seal* (1957) uses a chess game with Death and medieval allegory.

**Important negative finding:** A specific Wheel-of-Fortune tarot quotation in the film could not be verified. Its medieval danse-macabre and Fortune-like reversals make it comparable, but calling it a direct Wheel citation overstates the evidence.

### Other cinema

Tarot readings and Wheel-like motifs occur across horror, noir, and art cinema, but identification must be based on an actual visible card or production record. A rotating fairground wheel, roulette wheel, or fate motif alone is not a tarot quotation.

## Paintings and buildings

- **Siena Cathedral pavement**, Wheel of Fortune panel, recorded 1372; restored 1864.
- Medieval cathedral rose windows and sculpted wheels frequently place rulers around Fortune; these are antecedents, not tarot copies.
- Manuscripts of Boethius, Boccaccio’s *De casibus virorum illustrium*, and the *Roman de la Rose* repeatedly show Fortune turning rulers.
- Later occult art after Lévi and Wirth imports the sphinx, Typhon, and Hermanubis into paintings, posters, and architectural decoration, but the motif must be distinguished from the older Rota.

# Controversies and disputes

## Egyptian origin

### The claim

**[Legend, first securely published 1781]** Court de Gébelin claimed the tarot preserved an Egyptian sacred book. He derived “Tarot” from imaginary Egyptian word-elements and treated Marseille imagery as corrupted hieroglyphics. The Comte de Mellet called it a Book of Thoth and connected it to divination and Hebrew letters. [Original French](https://www.tarock.info/gebelin.htm).

### Spreaders

Etteilla, Paul Christian, Lévi, Papus, nineteenth-century occult publishers, and later New Age literature.

### Evidence

None of the following has been found:

- an ancient Egyptian 22-image sequence;
- an Egyptian text describing tarot;
- a transmission chain from an Egyptian priesthood to fifteenth-century Italy;
- Egyptian names or hieroglyphs on early Milanese cards.

The Pope, Papess, Emperor, virtues, Hanged Man, Last Judgement, and Rota Fortunae belong visibly to medieval European culture.

**Verdict:** historically documented as an influential eighteenth-century belief; unsupported as an origin account. Waite himself wrote that there was “no particle of evidence” for an Egyptian origin, even while using Egyptian symbols. [Waite page](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Page%3AThe_Illustrated_Key_to_the_Tarot.djvu/36).

## Mamluk cards versus an Egyptian Book of Thoth

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** European suit cards probably owe important structural debts to Islamic/Mamluk cards, whose suit system included cups, coins, swords, and polo-sticks.

This makes “Egypt” relevant to the prehistory of European playing cards in a material trade sense. It does not validate Gébelin’s pharaonic priesthood, Book of Thoth, 22 trumps, or Egyptian Wheel.

## Romani or “Gypsy” origin

### The claim

Romani travelers supposedly brought tarot from Egypt or India and preserved its divinatory key.

### History

Gébelin and Mellet helped connect cards, Egypt, and people then inaccurately called “Egyptians.” Nineteenth-century writers such as Vaillant entrenched the story; Papus’s title *Tarot des Bohémiens* gave it enormous reach.

### Evidence

The earliest tarot records are Italian court and civic documents. They do not mention Romani creators or transmitters. Tarot existed as a game before the first published occult history.

Modern scholarship finds no evidence that Romani people invented tarot or carried it into Italy. The stereotype instead reflects European “gypsylorism,” exoticization, and the real but later participation of some Romani practitioners in fortune-telling economies. [Smithsonian discussion](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/arts-culture/colorful-history-tarot-mesmerizing-decks-themseles-180986811/), [scholarship on stereotyping](https://doi.org/10.1163/15685276-01234012).

## Cathar, Albigensian, Waldensian, Gnostic, and Templar claims

### The claims

The trumps supposedly encode:

- Cathar dualism;
- a secret Waldensian catechism;
- Templar doctrine concealed from the Church;
- a Gnostic initiation sequence;
- Grail teaching carried by persecuted sects.

### Spread

Different versions appear in nineteenth- and twentieth-century alternative histories. Sir Steven Runciman entertained a Cathar possibility; Roger Tilley proposed Waldensian connections; later popular books broadened the theory to Templars and “heretical undergrounds.” Farley surveys these claims. [Farley, *A Cultural History of Tarot*](https://e-isomero.com/pdf/book/1611846398.pdf).

### Evidence

No Cathar, Waldensian, or Templar document names the tarot sequence. The first tarot records postdate the suppression of the Templars by more than a century and arise in elite northern-Italian court culture. The imagery is compatible with ordinary late-medieval Christian allegory.

**Verdict:** possible modern symbolic rereadings; unsupported as genealogical history.

## Secret Kabbalistic origin

The 22 trumps and 22 Hebrew letters make an attractive numerical match.

**Counterevidence:** the first known printed connection is Mellet in 1781. Different occultists then produced incompatible allocations—Mellet’s ordering, Papus’s Yod/Virgo, and Golden Dawn Kaph/Jupiter.

If the correspondence were an unchanged ancient key, this early divergence is difficult to explain. The documentary evidence favors creative eighteenth- and nineteenth-century synthesis.

## Is the top Marseille creature a sphinx?

- **Secure description:** crowned hybrid or animalized figure.
- **Traditional modern name:** sphinx.
- **Problem:** the old figure does not consistently have canonical sphinx anatomy or Egyptian attributes.
- **Likely history:** Lévi transformed the ambiguous crowned ruler into an explicit sphinx; Wirth, Waite, and Crowley then made the identification unmistakable.

The top Visconti figure is not a sphinx but a satirized ruler labeled **REGNO**.

## Are the side creatures Anubis and Typhon?

Not in securely documented seventeenth-century maker terminology. Their posture continues the rising/falling figures of medieval Fortune.

“Hermanubis” and “Typhon” are Lévi’s occult reinterpretation. Applying these names to Marseille is a retrospective tradition.

## Anubis, Hermanubis, Typhon, or Set?

Occult authors use these terms inconsistently:

- Hermanubis is a Greco-Egyptian fusion of Hermes and Anubis.
- Typhon was used by Greek and later occult writers as an analogue for Seth/Set.
- Waite calls the descending serpent Typhon.
- Popular RWS guides often call the ascending figure simply Anubis.
- Crowley’s figures are intentionally composite and magical rather than Egyptologically exact.

Treating any one of these drawings as a precise archaeological portrait is a category error.

## “TARO/ROTA” as ancient proof

**[Documented fact]** Waite–Smith prints TARO so that cyclic reading yields ROTA and other permutations.

**[Esoteric attribution]** Lévi and Case develop ROTA/TARO/TORA/ATOR wordplay.

**[Important correction]** The letters do not occur on the fifteenth-century Wheel or classic Noblet/Dodal/Conver design. They cannot prove ancient authorship.

## The crank

Some modern readers regard the Marseille crank as evidence that a human must consciously turn the wheel.

- **Observable fact:** a crank is present.
- **Unknown:** why the original woodcut designer included it or left it unattended.
- **Modern interpretation:** conscious agency, therapeutic choice, or the need for an operator.
- **Alternative reconstruction:** it is simply a mechanical clarification after Fortuna herself disappeared.

No surviving maker’s explanation settles the matter.

## Misprints and copying errors

Repeated block-cutting produced:

- malformed spokes;
- ambiguous limbs and tails;
- corrupted labels such as **ROVE** for normalized *roue*;
- Roman numeral variants;
- inconsistent colors;
- creatures whose identities became increasingly obscure.

Later occultists sometimes interpreted degraded carving as deliberate cryptography. Occasionally a copying accident became canonical because a widely distributed edition, especially Conver-derived Grimaud, normalized it.

The Wheel is a particularly clear case: Noblet’s mechanically intelligible spokes become less coherent in later recuts, after which interpreters assign metaphysical significance to the incoherence.

## Was tarot always divinatory?

No comprehensive pre-eighteenth-century occult system is documented.

Evidence before Gébelin includes:

- game play;
- moral condemnation of gambling;
- poetic appropriation;
- occasional lot-like or literary divinatory uses;
- a mid-eighteenth-century Bolognese cartomantic list.

This is enough to reject the simplistic statement “nobody ever divined with tarot before 1781,” but not enough to support an ancient secret school. Etteilla’s significance is the construction and publication of a complete, reproducible professional method.

## Dummett and corrections by later historians

Dummett’s central correction remains robust: tarot is first documentable as a northern-Italian game with permanent trumps. Decker, Depaulis, Kaplan, Farley, Pratesi, and museum catalogues have greatly expanded the object and document record.

Points still debated include:

- precise city of invention;
- whether Milan, Ferrara, Bologna, or another network first standardized 22 trumps;
- dating and workshop division among Visconti groups;
- how directly late Milanese sheets lead to Marseille;
- how early informal divination occurred;
- the extent of intentional Neoplatonic, astrological, or court-program symbolism in the first packs.

Dummett’s game-first history does not mean early images were meaningless. It means that meaning must be reconstructed from contemporary culture rather than back-projected from Golden Dawn tables.

# Open questions

1. **Which physical Wheel is earliest?**  
   Brambilla is a leading candidate, but the dating of early Milanese packs remains approximate.

2. **Where was the standard tarot invented?**  
   Milan, Ferrara, and Bologna all have early claims; the surviving record is too uneven for certainty.

3. **When exactly did the four caption formula enter tarot?**  
   It appears on the Visconti-Sforza Wheel, but whether it was present on the earliest standard cards is unknown.

4. **How did human rulers become three animals?**  
   The transformation is visible between Milanese court cards and Marseille witnesses, but no workshop text explains it.

5. **Who or what turns the crank?**  
   No historical explanation survives.

6. **Was the top animal already intended as a sphinx?**  
   Possible in the broad sense of a hybrid guardian, but explicit Egyptian identity is not secure before Lévi.

7. **How much did Waite specify and how much did Smith invent?**  
   Waite documents the program and sources; Smith’s preparatory material is incomplete, leaving the balance of authorial agency open.

8. **Exact Etteilla states:**  
   Grand Etteilla I, II, and III differ. “Card 20, Fortune/Augmentation” is stable in the main lineage, but images and ancillary correspondences must be cited to a specific impression.

9. **Informal pre-1781 divination:**  
   Scattered evidence exists, especially in Bologna and literary contexts, but no uninterrupted ancient occult curriculum has been demonstrated.

10. **Modern psychological efficacy:**  
    Jungian or therapeutic use can be studied as modern cultural practice; it cannot be validated historically by attributing it to fifteenth-century makers.
