# The Hanged Man: Research Dossier

## Method and evidentiary key

This dossier separates historical evidence from later interpretation.

- **[Documented fact]** Surviving object, archival record, printed primary text, or securely attributable manuscript.
- **[Scholarly reconstruction]** Historical conclusion argued from objects, documents, language, game structure, or comparative iconography.
- **[Tradition]** Repeated interpretation whose exact origin may be uncertain.
- **[Esoteric attribution]** Deliberate occult correspondence, not a claim about the card’s original fifteenth-century meaning.
- **[Disputed claim]** A proposal with substantial objections or inadequate evidence.
- **[Legend]** Origin story contradicted or unsupported by the documentary chronology.
- **[Modern invention]** A demonstrably recent redesign or interpretation.

Dates for handmade fifteenth-century cards remain approximate and differ among cataloguers. “Tarot de Marseille” is a later generic label for a family of designs, not the known original name of a single medieval deck.

The Morgan Library attributes its card to Bonifacio Bembo or his family/workshop and dates the group c. 1450–1480. The catalogue describes simply “Man hangs by foot from gallows.” [Morgan Library object record](https://www.themorgan.org/collection/tarot-cards/the-hanged-man)

## Number and order

**[Documented fact]** Surviving handmade fifteenth-century Italian trumps normally have no printed numbers or titles. “XII” is the Marseille and later occult convention, not something visible on the Visconti-Sforza original.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** The Hanged Man occupies a remarkably stable position between the virtues/low worldly trumps and Death. Its absolute number changes when regional orders differ:

- In the later Marseille/type-C sequence it is XII.
- In early Italian lists it may be the twelfth named subject while its practical rank differs according to whether equivalent “papi” are tied or whether virtues are inserted elsewhere.
- In Minchiate, whose expanded series contains forty numbered trumps, **Il Traditore** is ordinarily trump 12 in iconographic lists, but its effective place belongs to a much larger order.
- The Sicilian pattern numbers the subject **XI**, reflecting that pattern’s altered order.

The unnumbered Fool does not normally enter the trump hierarchy. It functions as an “excuse” or special card in many games, although its detailed rules changed by region.

## The requested neighboring renamings

These do not rename the Hanged Man but help identify regional packs:

- **[Documented fact]** In the Tarot de Besançon/Swiss-Besançon family, produced from the eighteenth century in eastern France and Switzerland, the Papess and Pope were replaced by **Junon/Juno** and **Jupiter**, partly avoiding Catholic figures in Protestant or confessionally mixed markets.
- **[Documented fact]** Surviving Swiss packs used in Troggu retain *Le Pendu* XII while giving II as *Junon* and V as *Jupiter*. [Troggu card list](https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Troggu)
- **[Documented fact]** Marseille XVI is titled **La Maison Dieu**. “Tower” is the later descriptive English title; the historical phrase may mean House of God, a hospital, or an institutional building, and its etymology remains debated.
- **[Documented fact]** The Marseille Fool, **Le Mat**, is unnumbered. Some early French variants numbered or positioned it differently; occultists subsequently made it 0, 21, or 22 according to their alphabetic scheme.
- **[Documented fact]** Etteilla replaced XII with **La Prudence**, but did not merely rename the conventional image: he substituted a new allegorical figure.

# Iconography deck by deck

## Background image: the *pittura infamante*

**[Documented fact]** Italian communes commissioned defamatory public images of fugitives, debtors, fraudsters, rebels, and traitors. Subjects could be painted upside down by one foot with identifying inscriptions. Documentary commissions survive; nearly all exposed wall paintings have disappeared, although preparatory drawings and literary descriptions remain.

Botticelli painted Pazzi conspirators after the 1478 Florentine conspiracy. Andrea del Sarto prepared drawings for inverted defamatory figures after the siege of Florence in 1530. Municipal evidence is assembled by Gherardo Ortalli in *La pittura infamante*.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** Gertrude Moakley, Samuel Y. Edgerton, Michael Dummett, Ronald Decker, Thierry Depaulis, Robert O’Neill, Andrea Vitali, Helen Farley, and other historians connect the tarot figure to this civic visual language. The combination of one-foot inversion and the early name *Traditore* makes the connection strong, although no document saying “this tarot trump was copied from a pittura infamante” survives. An illustrated historical discussion is provided by [Le Tarot cultural association](https://www.letarot.it/L--Appeso_pag_pg124_ita.aspx?lng=ENG).

**Correction:** These were normally punishments in effigy, not evidence that Italian traitors were routinely executed by being hung upside down from one ankle. The card’s posture signifies infamy and reversal more readily than a literal execution technique.

## Marziano da Tortona and Michelino da Besozzo, c. 1420–25

**[Documented fact]** Marziano described for Filippo Maria Visconti a special game with sixteen classical gods painted by Michelino da Besozzo. The gods were organized into four conceptual groups and acted as permanent trumps.

**Finding:** It contained no Hanged Man. It is a precursor or close relative of tarot, not the earliest standard 22-trump deck. Calling Marziano an early witness to the Hanged Man is an error.

## Visconti di Modrone / Cary-Yale, Milan, probably 1440s

**[Documented fact]** Eleven trumps survive: Empress, Emperor, Love, Chariot, Death, Fortitude, Faith, Hope, Charity, Judgment, and World. **No Hanged Man survives.**

The pack is unusual for its six court ranks and its theological virtues. Dates from c. 1441–47 to later in the century have been proposed; the earlier range is supported by Visconti heraldry and coin designs but is not proved by an account entry.

A defeated figure beneath Hope has a rope around his neck and an inscription conventionally read **“Juda Traditor”**—Judas the traitor.

- **[Documented fact]** The Judas figure exists on the Hope card.
- **[Scholarly reconstruction]** It demonstrates that “traitor,” rope, punishment, and Christian moral allegory belonged to the visual environment of the Milanese packs.
- **[Disputed claim]** It does not prove that a lost Cary-Yale Hanged Man depicted Judas.

[Cary-Yale overview and surviving cards](https://foolmade.art/archive/cary-yale-visconti)

## Brera-Brambilla, Milan, probably 1440s

Only two trumps survive, the Emperor and Wheel of Fortune.

**Finding:** There is no surviving Hanged Man and therefore no responsible deck-specific description to give. Reconstructions that supply one borrow from the later Visconti-Sforza pattern.

## Visconti-Sforza / Morgan-Bergamo, Milan, c. 1450s–1460s

This is the earliest surviving standard Hanged Man presently identifiable with confidence.

The young man:

- hangs head downward by one ankle from a horizontal wooden beam;
- has his hands bound or held behind his back;
- bends the free leg;
- wears fitted court clothing and hose;
- has no halo;
- bears no printed name or number;
- appears against a punched gold ground.

**Money-bag correction:** The Morgan Visconti-Sforza figure does **not** visibly hold two spilling money bags. That feature belongs to the so-called Charles VI/Estensi card and related descriptions. Mathers and Lévi later described weights or silver bags at the armpits, probably from another iconographic stream.

**[Disputed claim]** Attempts to identify the man as Muzio Attendolo, Francesco Sforza, a particular Visconti enemy, or a named traitor are conjectural. The generic *pittura infamante* explanation requires no individual portrait.

[Morgan image and catalogue](https://www.themorgan.org/collection/tarot-cards/the-hanged-man)

## “Charles VI” / Estensi tarot, probably Ferrara, c. 1470s

The conventional name “Charles VI” is obsolete: the cards were not made for the French king Charles VI. They are usually placed in fifteenth-century Italy, often Ferrara.

The man hangs by one foot and holds, or has attached near his hands, two bags from which coins appear to fall.

- **[Scholarly reconstruction]** The money may mark bribery, fraud, betrayal for payment, or Judas’s silver.
- **[Disputed claim]** “It is Judas” is possible but not established by an identifying inscription.
- **[Documented comparison]** The image resembles defamatory portraits in which the condemned person’s offense was communicated by attributes or written labels.

[Le Tarot: comparison of Charles VI and Visconti-Sforza cards](https://www.letarot.it/L--Appeso_pag_pg124_ita.aspx?lng=ENG)

## Other Ferrarese and Este cards

Fragments associated with the Este court show that Ferrarese trump iconography was not completely uniform. The surviving record is too incomplete to assign every isolated inverted figure to a securely named deck.

The 1442 Este ledgers record payments for *carte da trionfi*, but do not enumerate or describe a Hanged Man. Thus:

- the ledger is an early attestation of *trionfi* decks;
- it is not an attestation of this trump by name;
- the Charles VI/Estensi figure is later material evidence for the subject in a Ferrarese-associated style.

## Sola Busca, 1491

**[Documented fact]** The Sola Busca is a complete 78-card Italian tarot dated 1491, with named or pseudo-historical trump figures rather than the standard allegories. It does not supply a normal Hanged Man trump.

Its relevance is indirect:

- it is the earliest complete surviving tarot;
- its illustrated pip cards offered precedents for narrative minor-arcana designs;
- Pamela Colman Smith probably knew photographs of it in the British Museum, and several Waite-Smith minors closely echo Sola Busca compositions.

**Finding:** It is relevant to Waite-Smith’s minors, not a direct visual source for Smith’s Hanged Man.

## “Mantegna Tarocchi,” c. 1460s–1470s

These are two series of fifty didactic engravings arranged into social, liberal-art, cosmic, and theological hierarchies.

**[Documented fact]** They are neither a tarot deck nor securely by Mantegna. They contain no equivalent of the standard Hanged Man.

Their relevance is comparative: they show the same Renaissance appetite for ordered emblematic series, not descent from or direct influence on XII.

## Rosenwald sheets, late fifteenth or early sixteenth century

The Rosenwald material comprises uncut or partially cut printed sheets, probably Italian, showing a transition from luxury hand-painted cards to reproducible woodcut patterns.

The relevant figure follows the one-foot suspension type, greatly simplified for printing. Details such as facial expression, bags, and landscape are reduced or absent. The sheets help demonstrate that the subject belonged to popular printed tarot, not only courtly Milanese painting.

**Caution:** Dating and place have varied among cataloguers, and damaged or incomplete impressions make claims about precise original coloring speculative.

## Budapest sheets, early sixteenth century

The Budapest woodcut fragments likewise preserve an early printed Italian trump series. The Hanged Man/Traitor type is recognizable through the inverted body and gallows.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** Rosenwald, Budapest, and related sheets help bridge fifteenth-century northern Italian models and later regional woodcut traditions. They do not establish one simple linear descent into Marseille.

## Bolognese tarot

Traditional Bolognese cards omit printed titles and suppress or alter some numbers. The subject was known in play as **Il Traditore**.

The figure hangs inverted by a foot, preserving the shame-image. In Bolognese order the virtues are grouped differently from Marseille, and four papal/imperial trumps historically shared rank.

**[Documented fact]** In surviving Bolognese play the pictures identify ranks, but their divinatory symbolism is irrelevant to trick-taking.

## Florentine Minchiate, sixteenth century onward

Minchiate expands the trump series to forty numbered trumps plus the Fool, adding theological virtues, elements, and zodiac signs. The Hanged Man remains **Il Traditore** and retains the inverted one-foot posture.

Its meaning within the game is rank, not occult sacrifice. It is an ordinary trump rather than one of the highest valuable *arie*. [Minchiate rules and pack structure](https://www.pagat.com/tarot/minchiate.html)

## Tarocco Siciliano, seventeenth–eighteenth centuries

This is the major iconographic exception.

The Sicilian subject is hanged upright by the neck, not inverted by one foot. Dummett proposed that the northern/central Italian convention of defamatory one-foot hanging was not understood in Sicily, so the maker rendered the ordinary meaning of *impiccato*.

- **[Documented fact]** The changed posture survives on Sicilian cards.
- **[Scholarly reconstruction]** Loss of familiarity with *pittura infamante* is plausible, not proven.
- **[Disputed detail]** Whether the card shows formal execution rather than lynching depends upon the particular impression and gallows form.

[Dummett, “The Sicilian Tarot,” *The Playing-Card* 33.3](https://www.i-p-c-s.org/journal/33-3.pdf)

## Tarot de Marseille lineage

### Jean Noblet, Paris, c. 1650

Noblet’s surviving pack is the earliest substantially preserved French pack generally placed in the direct Marseille family. XII is an inverted youth suspended between two pruned trunks. One leg is bent across the other; hands are behind the back; the ground and tree stumps are schematic. The title is damaged or abbreviated in surviving impressions but belongs to the *Pendu* tradition.

The crossed legs create a figure-four-like shape. There is no luminous halo. The card still reads visually as punishment, inversion, or helpless suspension.

### Jean Dodal, Lyon, 1701–1715

Dodal’s **LE PENDU** standardizes the familiar image:

- two pruned uprights;
- crossbeam;
- one ankle tied;
- other leg crossed behind;
- hands hidden;
- colored fitted clothes;
- calm or neutral expression.

The pruned trunks sometimes have six cut branches apiece. Later occultists converted that printable detail into the twelve zodiacal signs; no eighteenth-century game source says that was its original purpose.

### Nicolas Conver, Marseille, 1760

Conver’s card became especially influential through later reproductions. It retains the Dodal arrangement, printed XII and titled **LE PENDU**.

The head points toward a green or colored ground zone; the arms form a downward triangular outline; the crossed legs form an upper cross. These geometries made the card attractive to nineteenth-century alchemical and Kabbalistic interpretation.

### Grimaud, nineteenth–twentieth centuries

Baptiste-Paul Grimaud’s commercial *Ancien Tarot de Marseille* regularized line, typography, and industrial color. It transmitted the Conver-derived image internationally and was the Marseille pack used by many twentieth-century writers.

### Camoin–Jodorowsky, 1997

Philippe Camoin and Alejandro Jodorowsky advertised their deck as a restoration of the Tarot de Marseille, synthesizing details from surviving historical packs.

- **[Documented fact]** It is a modern reconstruction, not a facsimile of one historic deck.
- **[Modern invention/reconstruction]** Its sharpened colors and restored minute marks embody Jodorowsky’s symbolic reading.
- **[Disputed claim]** Statements that it recovers a uniquely original, secret, or canonical tarot go beyond what the heterogeneous historical record can establish.

## Besançon and Swiss IJJ

These patterns preserve XII as **Le Pendu** while substituting Juno and Jupiter for the Papess and Pope. The Swiss 1JJ card remains recognizable as the Marseille inverted figure. In Troccas, XII is *Der Gehängte / il pendiu* and simply outranks XI and loses to XIII. [Troccas trump table](https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Troccas)

## Court de Gébelin’s engraved tarot, 1781

Court decided the historical Hanged Man was a cardmaker’s misunderstanding. He proposed that the lost original was Prudence: a man walking cautiously with one foot suspended, *pede suspenso*.

His words are explicit:

> “Le titre de cette carte étoit donc l’homme au pied suspendu, *pede suspenso*: le Cartier ne sachant ce que cela vouloit dire, en a fait un homme pendu par les pieds.”

“The title was therefore the man with the suspended foot; the cardmaker, not understanding it, made a man hanged by the feet.”

- **[Esoteric attribution]** Prudence as the missing fourth cardinal virtue.
- **[Disputed claim]** A copying error transformed a walking man into an inverted criminal.
- **Evidence against it:** fifteenth-century Italian cards already show deliberate one-foot inversion, and early names include *Traitor* and *Hanged Man*.

Court de Gébelin, *Monde primitif*, vol. VIII (1781), essay “Du jeu des Tarots,” around pp. 372–73 in the original pagination. [French transcription](https://www.tarock.info/gebelin.htm)

## Grand Etteilla, 1788–1789

Etteilla accepted the Prudence theory and replaced the Hanged Man altogether.

Card 12, **La Prudence**, depicts a woman lifting or managing her skirt while watching a serpent; a caduceus appears near or behind her. Printed keywords give:

- upright: **La Prudence**;
- reversed: **Le Peuple**.

This is not merely “The Hanged Man with an Etteilla meaning.” It is a different card occupying number 12 in Etteilla’s reordered “Book of Thoth.” [Grand Etteilla card 12](https://foolmade.art/archive/grand-etteilla/card-012)

## Éliphas Lévi, 1854–1856

Lévi restored the inverted adept and rejected the Prudence/error explanation. In Waite’s 1896 translation of *Dogme et Rituel* he writes:

> “This hanged man is, consequently, the adept, bound by his engagements, and spiritualised, that is, having his feet turned towards heaven.”

He adds Prometheus and, “vulgarly,” Judas. He reads the body as triangle-plus-cross and therefore an alchemical emblem of the completed Great Work.

- **[Esoteric attribution]** Adept, Prometheus, Great Work, binding vow.
- **[Tradition adapted]** Judas/traitor.
- **[Historical error]** Lévi describes the gallows as the Hebrew *tau* while printing or discussing Hebrew correspondences inconsistently; his alphabetic placement gives XII the twelfth letter, Lamed, not the later Golden Dawn Mem.

Éliphas Lévi, *Dogme et rituel de la haute magie*, vol. II, chapter XII (Paris, 1856); A. E. Waite translation, *Transcendental Magic* (London, 1896). [Scanned translation](https://awakenedonez.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/02/1896__levi___transcendental_magic.pdf)

## Oswald Wirth, 1889; revised 1926–27

Wirth, working under Stanislas de Guaita, drew the twenty-two *Arcanes du Tarot Kabbalistique*. His **Le Pendu** retains the Marseille suspension but makes the occult geometry intentional. Hebrew **Lamed** accompanies it in the Lévi sequence.

The later version emphasizes:

- two trees as columns;
- twelve lopped branches as zodiacal totality;
- crossed legs and triangular arms;
- serene self-sacrifice;
- coins or disks falling from the pockets/hands in some editions;
- colors assigned symbolic roles.

Wirth turns the punished traitor into the wise person who has abandoned personal ambition for humanity. [1889 deck history](https://foolmade.art/archive/wirth-tarot); [Wirth, *Tarot of the Magicians*](https://www.societyofguardians.org/resources/Tarot_of_the_Magicians_by_Oswald_Wirth.pdf)

## Golden Dawn manuscripts, c. 1887–1900

The Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn placed the Fool first in the alphabetic series. This changes XII from Lévi’s Lamed to the thirteenth Hebrew letter, **Mem**.

The manuscript title is **The Spirit of the Mighty Waters**. The design instructions variously call for:

- a man suspended by one foot;
- crossed legs;
- a downward triangle formed by the arms;
- water imagery;
- elemental coloring.

Later reconstructions are not original Golden Dawn decks:

- **Robert Wang, *The Golden Dawn Tarot* (1977):** visual realization made with Israel Regardie’s assistance.
- **Chic and Sandra Tabatha Cicero, *Golden Dawn Magical Tarot* (2000):** a later reconstruction drawing on Order papers and the four color scales.

Differences between them reflect incomplete, variant, or verbal manuscript specifications. Neither should be mistaken for a surviving deck routinely used by the original Order.

## Waite–Smith, 1909

Pamela Colman Smith draws a young man hanging calmly by his right foot from living wood. The upper beam and upright trunk form a **T/Tau-shaped cross**. His left leg bends behind the right, producing the fylfot or figure-four form. His arms are behind his back. He wears red hose, a blue tunic, and yellow shoes. A brilliant halo surrounds his head.

Waite’s description is decisive:

> “The tree of sacrifice is living wood, with leaves thereon.”

> “The face expresses deep entrancement, not suffering.”

> The figure suggests “life in suspension, but life and not death.”

Waite rejected simple martyrdom, Prudence, duty, and Great Work as complete explanations, saying it expresses a relation “between the Divine and the Universe.”

- **[Documented fact]** Smith introduced the conspicuous radiating halo into the best-known mass-market design.
- **[Scholarly reconstruction]** The living wood, halo, and serene face deliberately transform penal shame into voluntary spiritual suspension.
- **[Disputed claim]** The image is sometimes called a portrait of Odin. Neither Waite nor Smith says so.
- **Sources:** Marseille/Wirth/Golden Dawn geometry are demonstrable conceptual predecessors. The Sola Busca influenced several Smith minors, but no evidence makes it the source of this trump.

A. E. Waite, *The Pictorial Key to the Tarot* (London: William Rider, 1910), Part II, “XII. The Hanged Man.” [Scanned edition](https://www.labirintoermetico.com/02tarocchi/Waite_Pictorial_Key_to_the_Tarot.pdf)

## Paul Foster Case and BOTA, 1920s–1930s

Case’s deck, drawn by Jessie Burns Parke, closely revises Waite-Smith while making Golden Dawn correspondences explicit. Students traditionally color the line drawings.

The BOTA figure has:

- white hair and halo;
- blue jacket and red legs;
- Tau-shaped gallows;
- twelve lopped branches;
- a body interpreted as a motionless pendulum.

Case called the primary lesson **Reversal** or the “Law of Reversal.” Mem means Water; “Suspended Mind” is identified with *samadhi*. BOTA additionally gives Neptune as an astrological resonance, although the classical Golden Dawn attribution is elemental Water, not the planet Neptune. [BOTA’s official Key 12 synopsis](https://www.botaineurope.org/the-tarot/12-hanged-man)

## Crowley–Harris Thoth tarot, painted 1938–1943; first full commercial publication 1969

Frieda Harris radically reconstructs the card:

- a nude male figure is suspended from an enormous green ankh;
- the limbs form Crowley’s prescribed cross-and-triangle geometry;
- green disks mark extremities;
- the background and lower field are watery;
- a serpent coils at the left foot/ankle and participates in the suspension;
- the head descends toward the waters;
- the image suggests crucifixion, drowning, baptism, and ritual ordeal simultaneously.

Crowley writes:

> “This card, attributed to the letter Mem, represents the element of Water.”

> “It is a baptism which is also a death.”

He calls the figure an Osirian “dying god” and says that in an earlier Isis formula it would be **“The Drowned Man.”** The serpent is “creator and destroyer, who operates all change.”

The card is not an endorsement of indiscriminate suffering. Crowley declares that “the whole idea of sacrifice is a misconception of nature” in the new Aeon, while retaining the image as the old Osirian formula and as a mystical initiation.

[Aleister Crowley, *The Book of Thoth*, “XII. The Hanged Man”](https://hermetic.com/crowley/book-of-thoth/atu)

## Jodorowsky–Costa, 2004

In *The Way of Tarot*, Alejandro Jodorowsky and Marianne Costa make XII the second degree of the second decimal series, analogical to the High Priestess. The figure stops identifying with worldly theater and offers the ego’s anxieties to inner work.

This is a modern psychospiritual reading built from Marseille details, not a recovered medieval doctrine. [English edition scan](https://irp-cdn.multiscreensite.com/24669b51/files/uploaded/The_Way_of_Tarot%20%281%29.pdf)

## Notable modern redesigns

A complete catalogue is impossible; important directions include:

- **Motherpeace Tarot**, Vicki Noble and Karen Vogel, 1970s: feminist, circular, goddess-centered reinterpretation; suspension becomes altered consciousness outside patriarchal hierarchy.
- **New Palladini**, David Palladini, 1960s–70s: elongated, theatrical, Art Nouveau-influenced sacrifice figure.
- **Thoth-derived decks:** emphasize watery initiation, ankh, serpent, or Osirian death.
- **Queer and nonbinary decks:** replace the male martyr with gender-fluid suspension, reframing surrender as release from imposed identity.
- **Anti-racist and decolonial decks:** sometimes avoid lynching imagery or replace literal hanging with floating, diving, cocooning, or acrobatics.
- **Urban decks:** substitute window cleaners, aerialists, divers, astronauts, or stalled machinery.
- **Modern Odin decks:** deliberately use Yggdrasil and runes. These are explicit modern mythographic redesigns, not evidence for tarot’s origin.

# The game

## Basic mechanics

**[Documented fact]** Tarot originated as a family of trick-taking games. The twenty-one special picture cards form a permanent trump suit. A trump defeats an ordinary suit card; among trumps, the locally higher trump wins.

The Hanged Man is normally an **ordinary middle trump**:

- it has no inherent special power;
- it wins against lower trumps and loses to higher ones;
- in modern French tarot it is atout 12 and is worth the ordinary 0.5 card-point;
- it is not one of the three *bouts/oudlers*—1, 21, and the Excuse—which are worth 4.5 points and affect the declarer’s target. [French tarot rules](https://www.pagat.com/tarot/frtarot.html)

In Swiss Troggu, XII likewise has ordinary value: higher-numbered trumps beat it and lower-numbered ones lose to it. In many Central European tarock games, traditional allegorical pictures disappeared altogether; XII may be a genre scene whose subject has nothing to do with hanging.

## Three regional trump orders

Dummett classified early Italian orders as A, B, and C.

| Order | Region | Diagnostic features |
|---|---|---|
| **A** | Bologna, Florence and Minchiate territory | Angel above World; virtues grouped in a characteristic middle block; Bolognese papi may be equal |
| **B** | Ferrara and northeastern Italy | World above or differently related to Angel; Justice placed high, close to the final celestial series |
| **C** | Milan, Lombardy, later Marseille/Piedmont influence | The order that develops into the familiar Marseille numbering; Justice VIII, Fortitude XI, Hanged Man XII |

The Hanged Man’s relative position is much more stable than those of Justice, Fortitude, and Temperance. Trump-order disputes concern:

- whether World or Angel/Judgment is highest;
- where the three cardinal virtues belong;
- whether Justice is near the celestial judgment;
- whether Fortitude is XI or VIII;
- whether the four rulers/papi have individual or equal rank.

Waite-Smith follows Golden Dawn practice by making Strength VIII and Justice XI. Marseille has Justice VIII and Strength XI. This swap does not change Hanged Man XII.

[Dummett order discussion and translated excerpts](https://dummettsmondo.blogspot.com/2015/07/chapters-6-and-7-game-and-its-trump.html)

## The Fool

The Fool was originally not simply “trump 0.”

- In many Italian and French rules it may be played instead of following suit and is normally recovered by its owner in exchange for a low card: hence *Excuse*.
- It ordinarily does not win the trick.
- Special last-trick and slam rules developed.
- Occult systems numbered it 0, placed it between XX and XXI, or treated it as XXII to make different Hebrew alphabets fit.

## Minchiate

Minchiate has forty numbered trumps plus the Fool. *Il Traditore* is not among the highest special *arie*. Its iconic identity has no effect on its play.

## Tarock

Central European tarock packs progressively replaced medieval allegories with animals, occupations, landscapes, military scenes, or comic figures. Where a Marseille or Swiss pattern survives, XII remains *Der Gehängte*. Otherwise “Hanged Man” ceases to be a relevant name even though the twelfth trump remains.

# Chronology of attestation

## Before tarot

- **Fourteenth–sixteenth centuries — [Documented fact]:** Italian defamatory paintings depict condemned fugitives and traitors inverted by one foot.
- **c. 1420–25 — [Documented fact]:** Marziano describes a sixteen-god trump game for Filippo Maria Visconti. No Hanged Man.
- **1440 — [Documented fact]:** Florentine record concerning two *trionfi* packs sent or transferred to Sigismondo Pandolfo Malatesta; now earlier than the once-standard 1442 “first tarot” date.
- **1442 — [Documented fact]:** Ferrarese Este accounts pay Jacomo Sagramoro for *carte da trionfi*. No card list.

## Surviving images and names

- **1440s — [Documented object]:** Cary-Yale exists, but no Hanged Man survives. Hope tramples a Judas/traitor figure.
- **c. 1450s–60s — [Documented object]:** Visconti-Sforza Hanged Man, unnamed and unnumbered.
- **c. 1470s — [Documented object]:** Charles VI/Estensi inverted man with money bags.
- **late fifteenth/early sixteenth century — [Documented text]:** *Sermones de ludo cum aliis* lists **“lo impichato.”** Its traditional c. 1470 dating is not secure.
- **c. 1500 — [Documented prints]:** Rosenwald/Budapest-type sheets disseminate the figure through woodcut production.
- **early sixteenth century — [Documented literary tradition]:** poems use tarot subjects as moral or satirical vocabulary.
- **1521 — [Documented text/image]:** Sigismondo Fanti’s *Triompho di Fortuna* warns the inhuman or treacherous person that he will end his hours “in the air.”
- **1527 — [Documented literary allusion]:** Teofilo Folengo, under the Merlin Cocai persona, uses *appicato* and tarot-trump wordplay in *Caos del Triperuno*.
- **c. 1550 — [Documented literary evidence]:** Lollio/Imperiali tarot poems name or evoke the **Traditore**.
- **1585 — [Documented text]:** Tommaso Garzoni uses *Impiccato* in his account of games.
- **seventeenth century — [Documented regional tradition]:** Bolognese players call the subject *Traditore*.
- **c. 1650 — [Documented object]:** Noblet, Paris.
- **1701–15 — [Documented object]:** Dodal, Lyon, *Le Pendu*.
- **1760 — [Documented object]:** Conver, Marseille, *Le Pendu* XII.

## Occult reinterpretation

- **1781 — [Documented publication]:** Court de Gébelin calls XII Prudence and invents the “cardmaker reversed it by mistake” theory.
- **1781 — [Documented publication]:** Comte de Mellet proposes a Hebrew/alphabetic and Egyptian reading in the same volume.
- **1783–85 — [Documented publication]:** Etteilla publishes systematic tarot cartomancy.
- **1788–89 — [Documented deck]:** Grand Etteilla substitutes *La Prudence* for the Hanged Man.
- **1854–56 — [Documented publication]:** Lévi makes the figure adept, Prometheus, Judas, and alchemical Great Work.
- **c. 1870 — [Documented esoteric development]:** Paul Christian presents the trumps as Egyptian initiation stations and popularizes “Major/Minor Arcana.”
- **1888 — [Documented publication]:** Mathers, *The Tarot*, defines the image as Sacrifice.
- **1889 — [Documented deck/publication]:** Wirth’s twenty-two trumps; Papus’s *Tarot des Bohémiens* disseminates Lévi/Wirth Kabbalistic tarot.
- **c. 1887–1900 — [Documented manuscripts]:** Golden Dawn assigns Mem, Water, and “Spirit of the Mighty Waters.”
- **1909–10 — [Documented deck/book]:** Waite-Smith halo, living Tau-tree, voluntary entrancement.

- **1922 — [Documented literary use]:** Eliot writes “I do not find / The Hanged Man.”

- **1969/1973 — [Documented literary use]:** Calvino deploys Visconti and Marseille decks in *The Castle of Crossed Destinies*.
- **1984 — [Earliest trace located for a direct Odin table]:** Edred Thorsson/Stephen Flowers, *Futhark*, appendix D, associates a rune and Hanged Man. Earlier direct tarot-Odin usage may exist but was not located in the consulted sources.
- **1997 — [Modern reconstruction]:** Camoin–Jodorowsky Marseille.
- **2004 — [Modern interpretation]:** Jodorowsky and Costa, *The Way of Tarot*.

## Golden Dawn color scales

For Mem/Water, commonly transmitted scales are:

- Atziluth/King scale: deep blue;
- Briah/Queen: sea green;
- Yetzirah/Prince: deep olive green;
- Assiah/Princess: white flecked purple.

The path is commonly numbered 23 and joins Geburah to Hod. [Golden Dawn correspondence summary](https://www.iok-kabbalah.org/trees/Mem.html)

## Tzaddi/Heh issue

Crowley’s “Tzaddi is not the Star” exchange moves **Emperor** and **Star** correspondences, usually Heh and Tzaddi. It does not move the Hanged Man: XII remains Mem/Water.

## Pip decans and court spans

No decan belongs to XII. In *Book T*:

- numbered minor cards 2–10 receive zodiacal decans and titles;
- courts receive sub-elements and zodiacal spans;
- the Hanged Man receives an elemental path correspondence instead.

Claims such as “Hanged Man is Moon in Aquarius” confuse it with a pip-card decan.

# Divinatory meanings by author

## Etteilla

Because Etteilla replaced the subject, his meaning belongs to **La Prudence 12**, not the traditional Hanged Man.

- **Upright:** “La Prudence”—prudence, caution, circumspection.
- **Reversed:** “Le Peuple”—the people, populace, or masses.

Later Grand Etteilla manuals amplify this into careful conduct, wise advice, and protection against prejudice.

## S. L. MacGregor Mathers, 1888

Mathers’s symbolic chapter says:

> “He symbolises Sacrifice.”

His divinatory list gives:

- **Upright:** “Self-sacrifice, Sacrifice, Devotion, Bound.”
- **Reversed:** “Selfishness, Unbound, Partial sacrifice.”

Mathers, *The Tarot: Its Occult Signification, Use in Fortune-Telling, and Method of Play* (London, 1888). [Sacred Texts edition](https://sacred-texts.com/tarot/mathers/index.htm)

## Papus, 1889

Papus condenses the practical meaning to:

> “Trials. Sacrifice.”

His larger theoretical system relates XII to necessity and the transition or sacrifice through which one ternary passes into another.

## Waite, 1910

Waite’s divinatory list is:

- **Upright:** “Wisdom, circumspection, discernment, trials, sacrifice, intuition, divination, prophecy.”
- **Reversed:** “Selfishness, the crowd, body politic.”

These are not identical to his higher symbolic discussion, in which he denies that “martyrdom,” “prudence,” or “duty” alone explains the card.

## Crowley, 1944

Crowley’s practical summary gives:

- “Enforced sacrifice, punishment, loss, fatal or voluntary, suffering, defeat, failure, death.”

Crowley does not supply a separate conventional reversed list. Thoth practice often treats orientation contextually rather than by mechanically reversing every card. His mnemonic is:

> “In Mother-Deeps of Ocean the God-Man Hangs, Lamp of the Abyss Aeonian.”

## Paul Foster Case

Case distinguishes spiritual from material operation:

- spiritually: wisdom, reversal of ordinary consciousness, suspended mind or *samadhi*;
- materially: reverses, delay, loss, suffering, or sacrifice;
- no single canonical reversed list is central because **Reversal** is already the card’s governing law.

In *The Oracle of the Tarot*, he says the letter is Mem, attributed to Water, and that in spiritual questions the card represents wisdom; in ordinary affairs it can signify reverses and losses. [Case, *Oracle of the Tarot*](https://media-01.imu.nl/storage/divona.be/17547/oracle-of-tarot-paul-foster-case.pdf)

## Rachel Pollack

In *Seventy-Eight Degrees of Wisdom* (1980), Pollack stresses voluntary suspension, breaking conventional habits, peace through surrender, and reversal of viewpoint. Her compact phrase **“sacrifice as a route to liberation”** summarizes the movement.

For reversal, Pollack discusses resistance to sacrifice, useless waiting, inability to release the ego, or—depending on context—the end of suspension. She is one of the major spreaders of the modern “new perspective” reading.

## Mary K. Greer

Greer treats reversal in multiple possible ways rather than as a fixed opposite. For XII reversed, possibilities include:

- resistance to surrender;
- martyr complex or sacrifice performed for recognition;
- stalling and indecision;
- emerging from liminality;
- internalization of the upright process.

The exact reading depends on which reversal method is being used: opposite, blocked, delayed, unconscious, projected, or excessive.

# Esoteric and symbolic readings

## Christian: Judas, punishment, and the inverted sinner

The earliest secure Christian nexus is not mystical self-sacrifice but betrayal and infamy.

- The Cary-Yale Hope card includes a Judas/traitor figure.
- Money bags naturally encouraged comparison with Judas’s silver.
- Lévi explicitly says “vulgarly, it is the traitor Judas.”

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** Judas contributed to the reception of the card but is not proven to be the sole or original identity of every early figure.

The inverted crucifixion of St Peter is another later comparison. It lacks the one-ankle suspension and civic-infamy context; no fifteenth-century tarot source names Peter.

## Civic and juridical: the traitor

This is the strongest historical reading.

The figure represents the social inversion of someone who betrayed lawful bonds. Upside-down display removes honor and places the offender outside the properly ordered city. Money bags specify venality in some versions.

This explains why the card can function in a triumphal hierarchy: legitimate order “triumphs” over the traitor.

## Alchemical

Lévi is the decisive spreader. He reads:

- arms/head as a downward triangle;
- crossed legs as a cross;
- triangle surmounted by cross as completion of the Great Work;
- inversion as spiritualization, with feet toward heaven.

Wirth, Papus, Golden Dawn authors, Waite, and Crowley elaborate this. There is no evidence that the fifteenth-century cardmakers intended the figure as a secret alchemical diagram.

## Kabbalistic

The Hanged Man has no demonstrable Hebrew-letter attribution before the eighteenth-century occultists.

- de Mellet: early speculative alphabetic linkage;
- Lévi/Papus/Wirth: Lamed under their sequence;
- Golden Dawn onward: Mem because the Fool begins the sequence.

Mem’s traditional lexical association with water made baptism, womb, sea, dissolution, and drowning available to later interpreters. “Path 23, Geburah to Hod” is Golden Dawn Hermetic Qabalah, not medieval Jewish Kabbalah.

## Astrological

No original astrological meaning is attested.

- Etteilla embeds XII in his reorganized astrological scheme.
- Golden Dawn makes XII one of the three elemental trumps, Water, rather than planet or zodiac.
- BOTA and modern authors add Neptune because it governs dissolution, altered states, and oceanic consciousness.
- Saturn is sometimes assigned through hanging, delay, ordeal, or early fortune-book configurations, but it is not the canonical Golden Dawn attribution.

## Hermetic and initiatory

Lévi’s “adept bound by his engagements” is the turning point. The old punished traitor becomes an initiate voluntarily accepting constraint.

Golden Dawn: Spirit of the Mighty Waters.

Waite: a seeming martyr who is alive, entranced, and illuminated.

Crowley: baptism that is death; an Osirian formula which the Aeon of Horus must transcend.

Case: suspended mind and reversal of mass consciousness.

## Sacrificial or “Hanged God”

James Frazer’s comparative “dying and reviving god” model influenced literary modernism. Eliot explicitly associated the tarot card with Frazer’s Hanged God.

Crowley makes the dying-god formula Osirian and includes Christ-like redemption, but also criticizes sacrifice as a formula of the former Aeon.

## Odin

The *Hávamál* describes Odin hanging nine nights on a wind-swept tree, wounded with a spear, “myself to myself,” to gain the runes.

- **[Documented myth]:** Odin’s self-hanging is ancient Norse literary material.
- **[Modern comparative interpretation]:** The tarot card was equated with Odin in twentieth-century rune-occult and tarot literature.
- **[Finding]:** No Italian, French, Lévi, Golden Dawn, Waite, or early Crowley evidence derives the card from Odin.
- **Earliest explicit tarot correspondence located in this research:** Edred Thorsson/Stephen Flowers, *Futhark: A Handbook of Rune Magic* (1984), appendix D. This is a terminus located, not proof that no earlier comparison exists.
- **Evidence against historical derivation:** different geography, iconographic details, social meaning, and the strong Italian *pittura infamante* context.

## Jungian

Jung did not establish a tarot doctrine. “Jungian tarot” is a later interpretive movement drawing on archetype, individuation, shadow, liminality, and ego transformation.

XII becomes:

- suspension between conscious and unconscious;
- voluntary relinquishment of ego control;
- reversal of the dominant viewpoint;
- descent into psychic water;
- the liminal interval before Death/transformation;
- negative pole: inflation through martyrdom, passivity, dissociation, or victim identity.

Pollack, Sallie Nichols, Mary Greer, and numerous therapeutic tarot writers spread this family of readings.

# People and works

## Artists, printers, occultists, and historians

- **Bonifacio Bembo or workshop/family:** associated with the Visconti-Sforza card.
- **Michelino da Besozzo:** painter of Marziano’s lost god-trump deck; relevant precursor, not maker of a known Hanged Man.
- **Jean Noblet, Jean Dodal, Nicolas Conver:** principal early French transmitters.
- **Baptiste-Paul Grimaud:** industrial transmitter of Conver-derived Marseille imagery.
- **Court de Gébelin:** Prudence and accidental-reversal theory; Egyptian origin myth.
- **Etteilla:** first systematic professional occult/cartomantic tarot and redesigned card 12.
- **Éliphas Lévi:** adept, Prometheus, Judas, alchemy.
- **Paul Christian:** Egyptian initiatory “Arcana.”
- **Papus:** disseminated Lévi/Wirth synthesis.
- **Stanislas de Guaita and Oswald Wirth:** first major purpose-built Kabbalistic redraw.
- **S. L. MacGregor Mathers:** sacrifice and Golden Dawn system.
- **Pamela Colman Smith and A. E. Waite:** living tree, halo, serene willing suspension.
- **Jessie Burns Parke and Paul Foster Case:** BOTA Law of Reversal.
- **Frieda Harris and Aleister Crowley:** ankh, Water, serpent, dying/drowned god.
- **Gertrude Moakley:** major twentieth-century iconographic historian; emphasized Renaissance social imagery and Petrarchan triumph culture.
- **Michael Dummett:** reconstructed tarot-game history and regional orders.
- **Ronald Decker and Thierry Depaulis:** documented occult tarot’s eighteenth- and nineteenth-century formation.
- **Stuart Kaplan:** reproduced and catalogued large numbers of historical decks.
- **Robert M. Place:** spread the *pittura infamante* account among modern readers.
- **Helen Farley:** cultural history from game to esotericism.
- **Andrea Vitali:** Italian iconographic and documentary research.
- **Philippe Camoin, Alejandro Jodorowsky, Marianne Costa:** modern Marseille reconstruction and psychospiritual interpretation.

## T. S. Eliot, *The Waste Land* (1922)

Madame Sosostris says:

> “I do not find / The Hanged Man. Fear death by water.”

Eliot’s own note explains that the card served him in two ways: he associated it with Frazer’s Hanged God and with the hooded figure on the road to Emmaus. His drowned Phoenician Sailor is not a standard tarot card.

This literary conjunction of absent Hanged Man and “death by water” strikingly anticipates or parallels Crowley’s Mem/Water and “Drowned Man,” but Eliot’s explicit authority is his own Frazerian association, not evidence of a hidden historical card meaning. [Eliot’s text and note](https://wasteland.windingway.org/46/a-wicked-pack-of-cards)

## Italo Calvino, *The Castle of Crossed Destinies*

The first section appeared with the Visconti pack in 1969; the combined book appeared in 1973, adding a Marseille-based “Tavern.”

The Hanged Man closes Orlando’s movement from madness toward inverted comprehension. In a commonly translated formulation, Orlando asks to remain upside down because the world becomes legible in reverse.

Calvino uses tarot as a combinatorial narrative machine, not as obedience to one divinatory dictionary. [Publication history](https://www.francomariaricci.com/en/books/tarots)

## W. B. Yeats

Yeats belonged to the Golden Dawn and knew its tarot correspondences. Tarot structures and Golden Dawn symbolism inform his esoteric milieu.

**Finding:** No comparably explicit, securely sourced major Yeats passage centered on the Hanged Man was located in the consulted sources. General claims that a particular Yeats poem “is the Hanged Man” should be treated as critical interpretation unless supported by manuscripts or correspondence.

## Alejandro Jodorowsky’s films

Jodorowsky’s cinema repeatedly uses initiatory inversion, hanging, crucifixion, sacrifice, alchemy, mutilated authority, and tarot-like processions—especially *El Topo* and *The Holy Mountain*.

**Qualification:** These visual correspondences are real at the level of Jodorowsky’s established tarot practice, but a shot should not be called a quotation of XII without production evidence or a close iconographic match. His clearest direct contribution is the Camoin–Jodorowsky deck and *The Way of Tarot*.

## Ingmar Bergman

Bergman used medieval allegory, Death, sacrifice, Christian silence, and carnival imagery, most famously in *The Seventh Seal*.

**Finding:** A secure direct Bergman quotation of the Hanged Man card was not located. Claims that a hanging or inverted figure in Bergman is “the tarot Hanged Man” remain interpretive unless tied to a screenplay, interview, or production design source.

## Paintings and visual art

- Paul Cézanne’s *The House of the Hanged Man* shares a title motif but is not a depiction of tarot XII.
- Toulouse-Lautrec works bearing “hanged man” terminology likewise require case-by-case distinction from tarot quotation.
- Surrealist and postwar artists made extensive use of tarot; André Breton, Leonora Carrington, Remedios Varo, Niki de Saint Phalle, Betye Saar, and others belong to the broader reception history. Not every suspended figure in their work is a direct XII quotation.
- The Morgan’s modern-tarot exhibition identifies tarot’s continuing impact on modern and contemporary art. [Morgan exhibition overview](https://www.themorgan.org/exhibitions/tarot)

## Buildings

**Finding:** No securely documented historic building was found whose architecture intentionally quotes the Hanged Man card. Gallows-shaped Tau structures, twelve branches, or inverted figures in churches should not be labeled tarot-derived without documentary evidence. Modern themed interiors and esoteric temples do use the image, but none emerged as a canonical architectural example comparable to the literary cases above.

# Controversies and disputes

## Egyptian origin

Court de Gébelin argued in 1781 that tarot preserved Egyptian wisdom. Etteilla developed it into the *Livre de Thot*. Paul Christian transformed the trumps into Egyptian initiation scenes. Papus and later occultists repeated the story.

- **[Legend as intellectual history]:** enormously influential in occult tarot.
- **Evidence offered by its founders:** visual analogy, speculative etymology, assumed universal symbolism.
- **Evidence against:** tarot is documented in fifteenth-century northern Italy; standard images reflect medieval and Renaissance European institutions; no ancient Egyptian deck, text, archaeological object, or transmission chain exists; Gébelin wrote before Egyptian hieroglyphs were deciphered.

Farley’s history traces how Egypt supplied cultural prestige to the new occult tarot. [Farley, *A Cultural History of Tarot*](https://e-isomero.com/pdf/book/1611846398.pdf)

## Romani or “Gypsy” origin

Nineteenth-century writers, especially Jean-Alexandre Vaillant and popularizers after him, connected tarot to Roma because Europeans incorrectly imagined Roma as Egyptians and treated itinerancy as a channel for ancient secrets.

- **[Legend]:** tarot was invented or carried into Europe by Roma.
- **Evidence:** none linking Roma to the invention of the fifteenth-century *trionfi* pack.
- **Historical correction:** Romani cartomancers later used available European cards, but adoption is not origin.
- The older term “Gypsy” is retained only when quoting historical titles or describing the myth.

## Cathar and Albigensian claims

Modern esoteric writers have proposed that the trumps secretly encode Cathar dualism, suppressed Gnosticism, Mary Magdalene traditions, or an Albigensian church.

- **[Disputed/legend]:** no Cathar tarot manuscript, deck, maker’s statement, or transmission chain survives.
- Similarities such as inversion, anti-worldliness, dualism, or a female sacred figure are broad motifs.
- The documented courts producing tarot were Catholic northern Italian courts, not identifiable Cathar communities.

## Templar claims

Claims that the cards preserve Templar initiation, Baphomet, or post-suppression secrets are largely nineteenth- or twentieth-century retrojections.

Lévi’s Baphomet strongly influenced the occult Devil card, but his nineteenth-century synthesis does not prove a medieval Templar origin for tarot or XII.

## The individual depicted

Proposals include:

- Judas;
- a generic traitor;
- Muzio Attendolo;
- Francesco Sforza;
- a Visconti enemy;
- a debtor, bribe-taker, or fraudulent official;
- an initiate;
- Christ, Peter, Prometheus, Odin, or Osiris.

Assessment:

- **Generic traitor/shame portrait:** strongest historical reconstruction.
- **Judas:** plausible secondary Christian specification, particularly when silver bags appear.
- **Named Italian political person:** unsupported unless an inscription identifies him.
- **Adept/Prometheus/Osiris:** nineteenth- or twentieth-century esoteric attribution.
- **Odin:** modern comparative interpretation.
- **Christ/Peter:** later typological reading, not established original identity.

## Money bags

Some writers transfer the bags from the Charles VI/Estensi image or Lévi’s description to the Visconti-Sforza card.

**Finding:** This is a conflation. The Morgan card’s hands are behind the back; it does not plainly show the pair of spilling silver bags.

## “Reversed-card printing error”

This thread has two forms.

### Court de Gébelin’s theory

A cardmaker supposedly misunderstood *pede suspenso* and inverted a walking Prudence.

**Status:** documented eighteenth-century claim, historically implausible. Early Italian images and names make deliberate inversion clear.

### Modern printing-error theory

A separate modern story says the Hanged Man was accidentally printed upside down and the error became tradition.

**Finding:** No early printing record, corrected state, or paired upright/inverted block supports it. Moreover, the hand-painted Visconti-Sforza card predates mass-printed Marseille decks and already shows intentional inversion. The story is a distorted descendant of Gébelin’s speculation.

## Is the Marseille leg a cross, fylfot, or numeral four?

Visually it can support all three comparisons.

- **[Documented form]:** one straight suspended leg and one bent/crossed leg.
- **[Esoteric attribution]:** Waite calls the formation a fylfot cross; Case relates it to four/material order; occultists combine it with the arm triangle.
- **Historical caution:** an unstable hanging body naturally requires the bent leg for a legible composition. No early cardmaker’s explanation survives.

## Twelve branches and zodiac

Dodal/Conver-type trees may show six cut projections on each side.

- **[Documented feature]:** branch stubs appear.
- **[Esoteric attribution]:** Wirth and Case identify twelve zodiac signs.
- **Open historical question:** whether the count was deliberately symbolic before occult reinterpretation. Woodcut simplification and copying can create or lose projections.

## Halo and willingness

The Waite-Smith halo and serene face established the now-dominant voluntary-sacrifice reading.

- **[Documented innovation]:** conspicuous radiating nimbus in the 1909 design.
- **[Esoteric interpretation]:** illumination and chosen suspension.
- **Historical correction:** it should not be projected backward onto every early card; the fifteenth-century civic model is punitive and dishonoring.

## Dummett and historians’ correction

Dummett’s central correction was methodological: tarot history must begin with surviving game records, decks, rules, and regional orders, not occultists’ claimed antiquity.

His work, continued with Decker and Depaulis, establishes:

1. tarot appears as a northern Italian game in the fifteenth century;
2. divinatory and occult tarot are documented much later;
3. the Egyptian theory begins with identifiable eighteenth-century authors;
4. Hebrew, astrological, Hermetic, and initiatory systems conflict because they were successively constructed;
5. later spiritual meaning remains culturally important even when it is not original history.

This does not make occult tarot “false” as a spiritual practice. It dates the attributions and changes what they can prove about origin.

# Open questions

1. **Exact date of the Visconti-Sforza card.** Museum and scholarly ranges vary; workshop attribution and division among hands remain debated.

2. **Exact origin center of the standard 22-subject pack.** Milan, Ferrara, Bologna, and Florence all have early evidence. Marziano proves a Milanese trump-game precursor but not the standard sequence.

3. **Dating of the *Sermones de ludo cum aliis*.** “c. 1470” remains widely repeated, but paleographic and contextual datings around 1500 or later complicate its use as the earliest named list.

4. **Who, if anyone, the earliest figure represents.** Generic *Traditore* is more economical than a named Sforza-era individual, but no maker’s explanation survives.

5. **Whether Judas was primary or secondary.** Bags of silver and the Cary-Yale Hope figure support the association, but not universal identification.

6. **Exact meanings of money bags or weights.** Bribery, betrayal, fraud, or a generalized burden are all possible.

7. **Transmission from Italian woodcuts to Marseille.** The family resemblance is clear; the exact lost intermediaries and workshop routes are incomplete.

8. **The oldest direct Odin–tarot comparison.** A 1984 runic-tarot table was located, but an earlier twentieth-century occurrence may exist. No evidence found places it in early occult tarot.

9. **Smith’s immediate drawing sources.** Golden Dawn/Wirth/Marseille concepts are clear, but no surviving instruction sheet fixes which details were Waite’s and which were Smith’s invention.

10. **The branch count.** Repetition may be intentional, mechanical copying, or both; zodiacal explanations are securely late.

11. **Reversed-card error theory.** No evidence was found beyond Gébelin’s eighteenth-century conjecture and its modern mutations.

12. **Bergman, Yeats, and architectural quotations.** Broad symbolic affinity is demonstrable, but direct card-specific documentation remains insufficient.
