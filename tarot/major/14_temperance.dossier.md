# Temperance: Research Dossier

> **Method and evidence labels**
>
> - **Documented fact** — supported by a dated object, archival record, printed text, or institutional catalogue.
> - **Scholarly reconstruction** — an inference argued by historians from surviving cards, documents, or game rules.
> - **Tradition** — a recurring interpretation or practice whose origin may be diffuse.
> - **Esoteric attribution** — a deliberate occult correspondence, not a claim about the card’s fifteenth-century meaning.
> - **Disputed claim** — proposed identification or genealogy for which evidence is contested.
> - **Legend** — a story contradicted by, or unsupported in, the documentary record.
> - **Modern invention** — a recent redesign or interpretation presented as new rather than historically recovered.

### The “earliest surviving example” problem

**Documented fact:** The Morgan Library’s Visconti-Sforza Temperance shows a woman holding two pitchers in a landscape. The institution dates it to circa 1480 and identifies it, with Fortitude and the Sun, as a replacement made by a later hand. The deck’s principal group is dated circa 1450. [Morgan object record](https://www.themorgan.org/collection/tarot-cards/temperance), [Morgan manuscript catalogue](https://www.themorgan.org/manuscript/158525).

**Documented fact:** Temperance is missing from the surviving Cary-Yale/Visconti di Modrone group. Modern reconstructed decks supply it, but those supplied images are not surviving fifteenth-century cards.

**Scholarly reconstruction:** Some undated or broadly dated fifteenth-century fragments may precede Morgan M.630.5. Because workshop, city, and date assignments remain under revision, “the earliest Temperance” should not be stated more precisely than the evidence permits.

**Finding:** The commonly repeated statement that the Visconti-Sforza Temperance is a circa-1450 Bonifacio Bembo original is inaccurate. The Morgan catalogue explicitly calls it a later replacement.

## Names, numbering, and regional terminology

### Trionfi, tarocchi, tarot

**Documented fact:** Fifteenth-century Italian records first call the new packs or their special cards *trionfi*, “triumphs.” *Tarocchi* appears later, around the opening of the sixteenth century, after which the older name declined.

**Scholarly reconstruction:** *Trionfi* probably named the trumping series or the game employing it. It does not prove that every subject was derived directly from Petrarch’s poem *I Trionfi*. Similarities to triumphal processions and Petrarchan moral ordering are historically relevant, but a one-to-one Petrarchan program is not documented.

French *tarot*, German *Tarock*, and related forms descend from Italian *tarocco/tarocchi*. English eventually borrowed *tarot* from French.

### Card-specific names

- *Sermones de ludo*: **La temperantia**.
- Italian packs and lists: **Temperanza**, later normally **La Temperanza**.
- Tarot de Marseille: **TEMPERANCE**, later **TEMPÉRANCE**.
- Minchiate: **Temperanza**, trump VI.
- Bolognese: commonly abbreviated **Tempra**, trump 7 in the later numbered pattern.
- Piedmontese: **La Temperanza/Temperenza**, normally 14.
- French occult decks: **La Tempérance**.
- Waite-Smith and BOTA: **Temperance**.
- Crowley-Harris: **Art**.

### Contextual regional renamings

These do not rename Temperance, but they identify the deck families surrounding it.

**Documented fact:** In the Besançon pattern, the Popess and Pope became **Juno/Junon** and **Jupiter**, respectively. The International Playing-Card Society locates this convention in eastern France, Alsace, German-speaking regions, and parts of Switzerland. An early secure example is Johann Mayer’s Swiss pattern, circa 1680; the Renoult blocks at Besançon are dated 1748. Temperance itself generally retains its Marseille identity. [IPCS pattern sheet](https://i-p-c-s.org/pattern/ps-6.html).

**Documented fact:** In Belgium and related packs, Bacchus or other secular figures could replace a controversial ecclesiastical trump.

**Documented fact:** In Bologna, the four imperial and papal cards were reduced to four equal-ranking “Moors” after papal intervention in 1725.

**Documented fact:** Marseille XVI is **La Maison Dieu**, not simply “The Tower”; XIII is usually unnamed; the Fool is **Le Fou/Le Mat**, normally unnumbered or later zero.

These changes reflect regional manufacture, censorship, religious sensitivity, and playing convention—not a hidden alteration of Temperance.

## Iconography deck by deck

### Pre-tarot visual source: the cardinal virtue

**Documented fact:** By the fifteenth century, Temperance was conventionally personified as a woman transferring or mixing liquid. The action represented *temperare*: moderating, proportioning, or diluting—especially mixing water with wine.

Piero del Pollaiuolo’s Florentine *Temperance*, commissioned in 1469–70 for the Mercanzia tribunal, shows a young woman mixing hot and cold water by pouring from an aquamanile into a basin. It provides an excellent non-tarot parallel. [Uffizi catalogue](https://www.uffizi.it/opere/temperanza-piero-pollaiolo).

**Scholarly reconstruction:** This widespread virtue iconography adequately explains the early tarot image. No Egyptian, kabbalistic, or alchemical source is necessary.

### Marziano da Tortona and Michelino da Besozzo, circa 1412–1425

**Documented fact:** Marziano described a special pack for Filippo Maria Visconti whose sixteen superior cards represented classical gods. Michelino da Besozzo painted it. The pack is lost, but Marziano’s text survives.

**Finding:** It contained no card called Temperance. It is relevant as an early independent experiment with permanent trump cards, not as an early Temperance image.

Calling it “the first tarot” is a **scholarly convention or disputed classification**. “Proto-tarot” is safer because its suits, gods, and sixteen-card trump series differ from the later standard pack.

### Cary-Yale / Visconti di Modrone, Milan, circa 1440s

**Documented fact:** The surviving deck includes unusual female court ranks and additional theological virtues. Its exact original structure is disputed.

**Finding:** No surviving Temperance is securely present. Commercial reconstructions that show one are modern completions.

### Brambilla tarot, Milan, circa 1440s

**Documented fact:** Only part of the deck survives in the Pinacoteca di Brera. It is conventionally associated with the workshop of Bonifacio Bembo.

**Finding:** Temperance does not survive. Any deck-by-deck description of a “Brambilla Temperance” is therefore reconstruction.

### Visconti-Sforza / Pierpont Morgan–Bergamo, Milan, circa 1450 and circa 1480

**Documented fact:** M.630.5 shows a standing woman in a long dress, holding one pitcher above another so that liquid passes between them. She stands in a sparse landscape against the deck’s rich gold-ground aesthetic.

She is not visibly winged in the manner of Marseille.

**Documented fact:** Morgan identifies the card as a circa-1480 replacement, along with Fortitude and the Sun. The replacement hand is often called the “second artist” or attributed in older scholarship to Antonio Cicognara/Pseudo-Cicognara; that attribution remains a matter of connoisseurship.

**Meaning within its visual culture:** The image is an orthodox personification of the cardinal virtue. The liquid is most economically understood as the measured mixture associated with *temperantia*. Claims that the two jugs already encode alchemical sulphur and mercury are retrospective.

### Ferrarese and Este material

Several fragmentary Ferrarese or Este-associated cards survive, but identifications are difficult.

A figure accompanied by a stag has sometimes been identified as Temperance.

- **Disputed claim:** Ronald Decker supported the Temperance identification.
- **Problem:** The familiar two-vessel attribute is absent or unclear.
- **Finding:** It should not be described as a securely identified Temperance.

### Charles VI / Gringonneur cards

**Documented fact:** These are Italian Renaissance cards, not a pack painted for Charles VI by Jacquemin Gringonneur in 1392.

**Legend:** The Gringonneur attribution was influential in eighteenth- and nineteenth-century occult histories.

A secure Temperance does not survive in this fragment.

### Mantegna “Tarocchi,” circa 1460s

**Documented fact:** These are two fifty-print educational or encyclopedic series, not tarot packs and probably not playing cards.

Temperance appears among the virtues, pouring between vessels. Its relevance is comparative: it shows how conventional the virtue’s imagery was.

**Disputed claim:** Older writers treated these engravings as ancestors or sources of tarot.

**Current scholarly position:** They belong to the same broader emblematic culture but are not evidence that tarot originated as an occult book.

### Rosenwald sheets, late fifteenth or early sixteenth century

**Documented fact:** The Rosenwald sheets preserve early printed Italian tarot imagery, commonly connected with Florence or Bologna. Temperance belongs to the group of conventional virtues and pours between vessels.

**Importance:** These sheets demonstrate that the subject was already standardized in inexpensive printed cards, not confined to luxury court decks.

The precise dates and relationship between surviving impressions remain **scholarly reconstructions**.

### Budapest sheets

**Documented fact:** These fragmentary printed sheets preserve another early popular tarot tradition.

**Finding:** Published reproductions and descriptions do not support a uniquely new Temperance program comparable to the later Marseille angel. Claims about tiny details should be checked against the specific impression because surviving sheets are incomplete and damaged.

### Sola Busca, 1491

**Documented fact:** The Sola Busca is a complete, dated, engraved Italian tarot with numbered, named ancient or pseudo-ancient trump figures and fully illustrated suit cards. It is especially important as a source Pamela Colman Smith could see in the British Museum.

**Finding:** It does not contain a conventional Temperance trump. Its numbered historical-warrior trumps form a different iconographic program.

Its relevance to Temperance is indirect: Smith borrowed several compositional ideas for Waite-Smith pip cards from Sola Busca minors, but no equally direct Temperance-to-Temperance borrowing is demonstrable.

### Bolognese pattern

**Documented fact:** Temperance became **Tempra**, ordinarily numbered 7 in surviving later Tarocco Bolognese packs. She pours between containers.

**Game meaning:** The abbreviated name and number are practical identifiers. No occult correspondence is required.

### Minchiate, Florence, sixteenth–nineteenth centuries

**Documented fact:** Minchiate expanded the trump series to forty, adding the other virtue, Prudence, the four elements, twelve zodiac signs, and other subjects. Temperance is **VI**, one of a consecutive group of virtues. A surviving eighteenth-century Florentine example is catalogued as “VI, Tempérance.” [Paris Musées](https://parismuseescollections.paris.fr/fr/petit-palais/oeuvres/minchiate-florentin-vi-temperance).

The figure normally pours between vessels. Wings occur in some variants but are not necessary to identify her.

**Significance:** Because Sagittarius exists separately among the Minchiate zodiac cards, Minchiate supplies no historical support for the later Golden Dawn equation Temperance = Sagittarius.

### Sicilian tarot

**Documented fact:** The Sicilian pack radically reordered and renamed several trumps; its lowest trump is the Miseria, and Jupiter occupies the top of the sequence.

Temperance survives as a virtue subject but does not occupy Marseille XIV in the historical Sicilian order.

**Finding:** The Sicilian pattern is evidence against the idea that XIV was an original universal metaphysical number.

### Tarot de Marseille lineage

#### Jean Noblet, Paris, circa 1650

A winged, feminine or androgynous figure transfers liquid between two handled vessels. The card is numbered XIIII rather than XIV, consistent with early additive Roman numerals. The title is **TEMPERANCE**.

**Change:** Wings have become explicit. The earlier standing virtue is now unmistakably angelic.

**Scholarly reconstruction:** Wings may heighten the virtue’s supernatural or soul-like character. They do not by themselves prove an alchemical doctrine.

#### Jean Dodal, Lyon, circa 1701–1715

The winged figure and paired vessels continue. The woodcut’s bilateral flow, strong color blocks, and frontal pose stabilize the Marseille model.

#### Pierre Madenié, Dijon, 1709

A principal early “Type II” Marseille deck. Temperance remains XIIII, winged, and pouring; facial, garment, and wing conventions shift with the new block design.

#### Nicolas Conver, Marseille, 1760

Conver’s image became foundational for later commercial Marseille packs. The winged woman pours from a blue-associated vessel toward a red-associated one in familiar printings.

**Caution:** Later esoteric writers assign spirit/matter or active/passive meanings to these colors. That symbolism is not documented in Conver’s workshop records.

#### Grimaud–Marteau, 1930 and later

Paul Marteau regularized a Conver-derived pattern and a restricted industrial palette. His accompanying interpretation turned the card into a reservoir and circulation of universal forces.

This is an **esoteric reinterpretation of historical graphics**, not a neutral restoration of Conver.

#### Camoin–Jodorowsky, 1997

The deck was presented as a restoration assembled from historical Marseille witnesses. Temperance receives intensified color and micro-details, including readings of the breast area as a hand or heart sign and serpentine bodily currents.

**Disputed claim:** That every restored detail recovers a single secret original tarot.

**Documented fact:** It is a modern synthetic reconstruction. Jodorowsky and Marianne Costa read the figure as a protective, healing angel and the two flows as circulating energies. [Jodorowsky and Costa text](https://irp-cdn.multiscreensite.com/24669b51/files/uploaded/The_Way_of_Tarot%20%281%29.pdf).

### Tarot de Besançon

Temperance remains substantially Marseille-like. The signature change occurs elsewhere: Juno and Jupiter replace the Popess and Pope.

Thus “Besançon Temperance” is not a distinct mythological renaming.

### Etteilla’s Grand Etteilla, 1780s–1789

**Documented fact:** Jean-Baptiste Alliette, “Etteilla,” reorganized all seventy-eight cards into an explicitly divinatory and purportedly Egyptian system.

The Grand Etteilla Temperance is normally card 10, not XIV, although publications and derivative editions vary. It presents a personified virtue and prints upright/reversed keywords directly on or around the image.

**Importance:** Etteilla transformed tarot from a gaming pack adapted for divination into a purpose-designed occult-divinatory deck.

**Caution:** Some later “Grand Etteilla” images and keyword lists belong to nineteenth-century revisions by pupils and publishers, not securely to the 1789 first state. [Digitized *Grand Etteilla*](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/2/2f/Le_grand_Etteilla%2C_ou%2C_l%27art_de_tirer_les_cartes_..._Le_tout_recueilli_..._%28IA_b29321220%29.pdf).

### Oswald Wirth, 1889

Designed under Stanislas de Guaita, Wirth’s twenty-two trumps retain the winged mixer but deliberately occultize the image.

The vessels become contrasting metallic or chromatic containers; the liquid becomes the vital fluid moving between opposed poles.

Wirth later wrote:

> “Key 14 of the Tarot shows us this vital fluid poured from a silver urn into a gold one by Temperance who becomes the angel of universal Life.”

This is an **esoteric attribution**, not evidence for the intentions of Noblet or Conver. Wirth’s first deck appeared in 1889; his mature exposition appeared in *Le Tarot des imagiers du Moyen Âge* (1927), English as *The Tarot of the Magicians*. [Digitized edition](https://www.societyofguardians.org/resources/Tarot_of_the_Magicians_by_Oswald_Wirth.pdf).

### Golden Dawn designs, circa 1888–1900

The Golden Dawn retained Temperance but placed it within a systematic web:

- Samekh;
- Sagittarius;
- Path 25;
- Tiphareth–Yesod;
- mutable fire;
- the “Daughter of the Reconcilers, Bringer-forth of Life.”

Its design instructions emphasize an angel combining fire and water, solar and lunar forces, with a volcanic or rainbow-like synthesis.

The text describes:

> “the equilibrium not of the balance of Libra but of the impetus of the Arrow, Sagittarius.”

**Esoteric attribution:** This explains why Sagittarius rather than Libra was selected: the Golden Dawn read the card as directed, dynamically equilibrated force.

Israel Regardie published Golden Dawn papers from the 1930s onward. Robert Wang’s *Golden Dawn Tarot* and Chic and Sandra Tabatha Cicero’s reconstruction are twentieth-century visual realizations of manuscript instructions, not surviving official Victorian decks.

### Waite-Smith, 1909

Pamela Colman Smith drew an androgynous winged angel:

- a solar sign or disk on the forehead;
- the divine name YHWH on the white robe;
- a triangle within a square on the breast;
- one foot on land and one in water;
- liquid passing impossibly between two cups;
- irises beside the pool;
- a path leading toward mountains and a radiant crown-like light.

Waite wrote:

> “A winged angel, with the sign of the sun upon his forehead and on his breast the square and triangle of the septenary.”

And:

> “I speak of him in the masculine sense, but the figure is neither male nor female.”

He identifies the stance as illustrating “the nature of the essences” and sees the distant light as the “Crown” reached by the path.

**Sources and changes:**

- The winged pourer continues Marseille.
- The one-foot-on-land, one-in-water posture makes mediation between states explicit.
- The path and crown convert moral restraint into spiritual ascent.
- The irises may pun on Iris, rainbow messenger of the gods, but Waite does not state this.
- Paul Huson’s identification with Iris or Ganymede is a later **disputed interpretation**.
- Direct dependence on Sola Busca for this particular trump has not been demonstrated.

[Waite, *Pictorial Key*](https://www.mr-kaplan.com/Library/Waite/The%20Pictorial%20Key%20to%20the%20Tarot.pdf).

### Paul Foster Case and BOTA

Case largely retains the Waite-Smith angel but modifies details to accord with his Qabalistic system. The figure is Michael, neither male nor female; the brow bears a solar symbol; YHWH appears on the garment; the vessels show controlled equilibration.

Case insists:

> “The title, Temperance, does not refer to the meaning in common use. It is to be understood in its ancient signification as ‘the act of tempering, or mixing.’”

His central word is **verification**: forces and doctrines must be tested experimentally. [Case, *The Tarot: A Key to the Wisdom of the Ages*](https://iapsop.com/ssoc/1947__case___the_tarot.pdf).

### Crowley-Harris Thoth Tarot, painted 1938–1943; published 1969

Crowley renamed Temperance **ART**. Frieda Harris depicts an alchemical androgyne formed from the Lovers’ opposing royal figures:

- black and white or dark and light halves combine;
- one face or body unites masculine and feminine;
- fire is poured into water and water into fire;
- the red lion has become white;
- the white eagle has become red;
- an arrow rises from the cauldron;
- the mixture becomes a rainbow;
- the inscription encodes **VITRIOL**.

Crowley explains VITRIOL from *Visita Interiora Terrae Rectificando Invenies Occultum Lapidem*: visit the interior of the earth; by rectifying, find the hidden stone.

He writes:

> “This card is the complement and the fulfilment of Atu VI, Gemini.”

And:

> “The Red Lion has become white, and the White Eagle, similarly expanded, has become red.”

The card represents the Great Work as the union of differentiated opposites, the continuation of the Lovers.

**Important correction:** Crowley’s famous Tzaddi/Heh revision changes the Emperor and Star, not Art. Art remains Samekh/Sagittarius. [Crowley, *Book of Thoth* text](https://crowley-thoth.com/symbolism/art-14/).

### Notable modern redesigns

- **Thoth-derived decks:** usually retain “Art,” alchemical marriage, rainbow, lion, and eagle.
- **Jodorowsky-Camoin:** reasserts the Marseille angel as healing, circulation, and protection.
- **Robert M. Place, Alchemical Tarot:** makes the alchemical sequence explicit and openly modern rather than claiming it was the sole medieval intention. [Place guide](https://robertmplacetarot.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/Alchemical-Tarot-6th-Ed.pdf).
- **Queer and feminist decks:** frequently emphasize androgyny, nonbinary integration, ecological balance, or mutual transformation.
- **Psychological decks:** replace literal vessels with divided selves, colors, animals, or interpersonal exchange.

## The game

### General function

Temperance is a permanent trump. In ordinary trick-taking play it beats all suit cards and lower trumps but loses to higher trumps. It ordinarily has no intrinsic divinatory meaning during play.

In many tarot games, scoring concentrates on the Fool, trump I, trump XXI, and kings. Temperance is normally not an *oudler*, *bout*, or special counter.

### The three principal Italian orders

Dummett grouped the evidence into three broad families:

1. **Type A: Bologna/Florence.** The virtues form a cluster. Angel generally outranks World.
2. **Type B: Ferrara.** Temperance lies comparatively low, immediately above Pope in the sermon-type list; World, Justice, and Angel occupy the summit in an order unlike Marseille.
3. **Type C: Milan.** Temperance lies after Death and before Devil; Angel and World are the highest pair. This becomes the Marseille sequence.

These are historical playing orders, not three rival initiatory doctrines. [Summary of Dummett’s reconstruction](https://www.nybooks.com/articles/1981/05/14/origins-of-tarot/).

### Justice and Strength

Justice, Strength/Fortitude, and Temperance moved relative to one another between regions. Their variable placement is strong evidence that the exact numbers were not originally fixed universal correspondences.

Waite later exchanged Strength and Justice compared with Marseille:

- Waite: Strength VIII, Justice XI.
- Marseille: Justice VIII, Strength XI.

Temperance remains XIV, so its Samekh assignment is unaffected.

### The Fool

**Documented fact:** The Fool originally stood outside the trump hierarchy. It could excuse the player from following suit but normally did not capture a trick. Its precise recovery and compensation rules varied.

In the sermon it is:

> “El Matto sie nulla (nisi velint)” — the Fool is nothing, unless they wish otherwise.

The modern zero is convenient and esoterically productive, but the early Fool was not necessarily “trump zero.”

## Chronology of attestation

- **Circa 1412–1425 — documented precursor:** Marziano’s god pack for Filippo Maria Visconti. No Temperance; relevant only to the emergence of permanent trumps.
- **1442 — documented:** Ferrara accounts record purchases of *carte da trionfi*, including packs brought by a Bolognese merchant. They do not name Temperance.
- **1440s–1450s — documented:** Luxury tarot production in Milan, Ferrara, Florence, and related courts.
- **Later fifteenth century — documented object:** standard virtue image appears in surviving tarot cards and sheets.
- **Circa 1470–1500 — documented text:** *Sermones de ludo cum aliis* lists “La temperantia” sixth.
- **Circa 1480 — documented object:** Morgan Visconti-Sforza replacement Temperance.
- **1491 — documented:** Sola Busca dated; no conventional Temperance subject.
- **Circa 1500 — documented literary culture:** Ferrarese *tarocchi appropriati* and related poems assign trump names metaphorically to court figures or women. These attest familiarity with the subject but are not manuals of occult meaning.
- **1526 — documented literary reference:** Teofilo Folengo’s *Chaos del Triperuno* uses tarot cards in literary and divinatory play. It is evidence for imaginative card interpretation, not an ancient occult system.
- **Sixteenth century — documented:** Minchiate fixes Temperance as VI among its expanded trumps.
- **Seventeenth century — documented:** French woodcut tradition stabilizes the winged figure and XIIII.
- **Circa 1650:** Noblet.
- **Circa 1680:** early Besançon Juno/Jupiter pattern; Temperance unchanged.
- **1701–1715:** Dodal.
- **1709:** Madenié.
- **1760:** Conver.
- **1781:** Court de Gébelin publishes the Egyptian-origin theory; Comte de Mellet proposes Hebrew-letter correspondences.
- **1780s–1789:** Etteilla develops and publishes an explicitly occult/divinatory tarot.
- **1854–1856:** Éliphas Lévi publishes *Dogme et rituel de la haute magie*.
- **1888:** Mathers publishes *The Tarot: Its Occult Signification*.
- **1889:** Wirth’s twenty-two trumps and Papus’s *Tarot des Bohémiens*.
- **Circa 1888–1900:** Golden Dawn formulates Samekh/Sagittarius/Path 25.
- **1909–1910:** Waite-Smith deck and *Pictorial Key*.
- **1927:** Wirth’s mature *Tarot des imagiers*.
- **1930s:** Case/BOTA system and colorable deck.
- **1938–1943:** Harris paints Thoth.
- **1944:** Crowley’s *Book of Thoth*.
- **1969:** first full commercial Thoth publication.
- **1970s–1980s:** Pollack and Jungian/feminist writers spread psychological integration readings.
- **1997:** Camoin-Jodorowsky restoration.
- **2002:** Greer systematizes reversal methods.
- **Present:** balance, healing, sobriety, synthesis, ecology, and nonbinary integration coexist as modern interpretive families.

### Pips and courts

The requested decan, pip title, sub-element, and zodiacal-span categories do not belong to Temperance itself; they apply to minor and court cards.

Temperance’s relevant Golden Dawn classification is a zodiacal trump:

- mutable fire;
- Sagittarius;
- ruled astrologically by Jupiter;
- no individual ten-degree decan;
- no court-card sub-element.

## Esoteric and symbolic readings

### Christian moral reading

**Documented visual context, fifteenth century:** Temperance is one of the four cardinal virtues. Its act regulates appetite and pleasure through reason.

The liquid can be ordinary water tempering wine. This is more historically probable than identifying the two streams as sulphur and mercury.

The winged Marseille figure may signify an angel or the elevated, soul-like form of the virtue. It does not require identification with a named archangel.

### Alchemical reading

**Historical sequence:**

1. Fifteenth-century card: ordinary virtue iconography.
2. Eighteenth-century occultization: tarot becomes an alleged repository of ancient wisdom.
3. Nineteenth century: Lévi, Wirth, Papus, and related occultists increasingly read the vessels as transmutation.
4. Golden Dawn: fire and water, solar and lunar currents become systematic.
5. Crowley: alchemical conjunction becomes the card’s explicit subject and title.

**Finding:** A few seventeenth-century alchemical emblems resemble a winged figure mixing vessels, but resemblance does not establish fifteenth-century influence. Robert O’Neill explicitly calls projection of the later alchemical meaning backward by two centuries speculative. [O’Neill iconographic survey](https://www.tarot.com/tarot/robert-oneill/temperance-cards).

### Kabbalistic reading

The first printed tarot/Hebrew speculation appears with de Mellet in 1781.

Lévi and Papus’s sequential schemes associate XIV with **Nun**, because they align numbered trumps directly with Hebrew letters under their chosen starting rule.

The Golden Dawn instead gives XIV **Samekh**. Samekh is the central path from solar Tiphareth to lunar Yesod, interpreted as support, probation, and the disciplined traversal of the astral realm.

These systems conflict because there is no medieval Hebrew-letter key to arbitrate them.

### Astrological reading

**Esoteric attribution, Golden Dawn:** Sagittarius.

The reason is not that Sagittarius is the ordinary sign of “moderation.” It is the arrow’s straight, sustained dynamic equilibrium:

> “the equilibrium not of the balance of Libra but of the impetus of the Arrow.”

Sagittarius is mutable fire, ruled by Jupiter. Temperance therefore becomes controlled energetic transmission rather than static balance.

### Hermetic reading

The paired vessels represent above and below, volatile and fixed, spirit and matter. The uninterrupted flow signifies circulation. The angel mediates without annihilating difference.

Crowley intensifies this into *solve et coagula*: dissolve, combine, rectify, and manifest a new substance.

### Iris, Michael, Ganymede

- **Iris:** plausible modern symbolic reading because Iris is a winged divine messenger, associated with the rainbow, and Waite-Smith includes irises. Waite does not identify the angel as Iris.
- **Michael:** explicit in Paul Foster Case. It is a BOTA attribution, not a securely medieval identity.
- **Ganymede:** proposed by Paul Huson because he is the divine cupbearer. This lacks supporting fifteenth-century labels and is disputed.
- **Generic angel/personified virtue:** best supported for Marseille.

### Jungian reading

**Modern invention/tradition, twentieth century:** Temperance symbolizes the integration of conscious and unconscious, masculine and feminine, body and spirit, instinct and reflection.

Crowley’s androgyne and the Waite-Smith figure’s declared sexual indeterminacy made the card especially compatible with Jungian concepts of conjunction and individuation.

The psychological reading is interpretively productive but does not establish that Renaissance cardmakers anticipated Jung.

## People and works

### Artists, printers, and occultists

- Marziano da Tortona and Michelino da Besozzo — precursor trump pack, but no Temperance.
- Bonifacio Bembo and the Visconti workshops — early Milanese tarot tradition.
- The later Visconti-Sforza replacement artist — Morgan Temperance, circa 1480.
- Jean Noblet, Jean Dodal, Pierre Madenié, Nicolas Conver — Marseille lineage.
- Court de Gébelin and Comte de Mellet — occult-origin and Hebrew-letter theories, 1781.
- Etteilla — first systematic professional occult tarot.
- Éliphas Lévi — central transmitter of tarot–Kabbalah–magic synthesis.
- Stanislas de Guaita and Oswald Wirth — occult redesign.
- S. L. MacGregor Mathers — Golden Dawn and English divinatory transmission.
- Pamela Colman Smith and A. E. Waite — most influential modern Temperance image.
- Paul Foster Case — BOTA and verification doctrine.
- Frieda Harris and Aleister Crowley — *Art*, the definitive explicit alchemical redesign.
- Alejandro Jodorowsky and Marianne Costa — Marseille restoration and psycho-spiritual reading.
- Rachel Pollack, Mary K. Greer, Sallie Nichols — psychological, feminist, and reader-centered interpretation.
- Michael Dummett, Ronald Decker, Thierry Depaulis — documentary correction of occult origin legends.
- Stuart R. Kaplan — cataloguing and reproduction of historical packs.
- Robert M. Place — historical-symbolic and openly alchemical modern decks.
- Helen Farley — social and cultural history of tarot.

### Literature, film, and visual culture

**T. S. Eliot, *The Waste Land*:** Madame Sosostris names several tarot-like cards, but Temperance is not among them. Claims that the poem quotes Temperance specifically could not be substantiated.

**W. B. Yeats:** Yeats’s Golden Dawn membership connects him to the order’s tarot symbolism. A specific canonical literary passage centered on Temperance/Samekh was not securely traced.

**Italo Calvino, *The Castle of Crossed Destinies*:** Tarot images generate narratives through their placement and recombination. Temperance may appear in layouts and editions, but the work does not supply one stable authorial definition of the card.

**Jodorowsky:** Tarot permeates his creative practice and films, but claims that a particular scene is a direct quotation of Temperance require scene-specific evidence. His explicit account is strongest in *The Way of Tarot*.

**Ingmar Bergman:** No reliable primary or scholarly source was found establishing a deliberate quotation of Temperance in Bergman’s films.

**Paintings and buildings:** Personifications of Temperance occur widely in civic and ecclesiastical decorative programs—Pollaiuolo’s Mercanzia painting is a particularly close parallel. Similarity alone is not proof that a building quotes the tarot card; usually both draw on the older virtue tradition.

## Controversies and disputes

### Egyptian origin

**Legend:** Court de Gébelin declared in 1781 that tarot was an Egyptian book of wisdom preserved under the harmless appearance of a game. He describes recognizing it as Egyptian almost instantly.

His own account states that the deck was “explained, declared Egyptian” in about a quarter-hour. [University of Rouen study with original passage](https://publis-shs.univ-rouen.fr/ceredi/1946.html).

**Evidence against:**

- Italian records document *trionfi* in the 1440s.
- Early cards use familiar Italian suits and Christian-European social and virtue imagery.
- No Egyptian prototype containing the twenty-two subjects survives.
- No continuous chain connects pharaonic Egypt to Renaissance Italy.

Dummett concluded that no occult significance is documented before de Gébelin’s intervention. [Dummett, “Origins of Tarot”](https://www.nybooks.com/articles/1981/05/14/origins-of-tarot/).

### Romani or “Gypsy” origin

**Legend:** Romani custodians carried the Book of Thoth from Egypt and transmitted tarot divination through Europe.

**History of the legend:** Nineteenth-century occultists, especially writers using “Bohemian” and “Gypsy” language, spread it. Papus’s *Tarot of the Bohemians* was highly influential.

**Evidence:** Romani people historically practiced paid fortune-telling in some settings, but this does not establish invention or transmission of tarot. European tarot documentation precedes the developed occult-Romani genealogy. Modern scholarship also emphasizes the ethnic stereotyping embedded in the story. [Study of the occult/Romani myth](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/pdf/10.1080/0048721X.2023.2250328).

### Cathar and Templar origins

**Disputed/legendary claims:** The Marseille images allegedly encode suppressed Cathar theology, Templar secrets, or a clandestine heretical gospel.

**Evidence problem:**

- No Cathar or Templar manuscript describes tarot.
- The named subjects fit ordinary fifteenth-century Christian civic imagery.
- The earliest tarot documentation postdates the suppression of the Templars by more than a century.
- Proposed codes normally depend on selecting details from much later seventeenth- or eighteenth-century woodcuts.

No source was found showing that Temperance’s vessels or wings carried a specifically Cathar or Templar meaning before modern authors proposed it.

### Alchemy as original design program

**Disputed claim:** Temperance was created as an alchemical emblem.

**For:** Pouring, mixing, color oppositions, and later winged figures are highly compatible with alchemy.

**Against:** The standard virtue image predates the esoteric reading; contemporary moral art supplies a sufficient source; explicit tarot-alchemy explanations appear much later.

**Verdict:** Alchemy is securely documented as a nineteenth- and twentieth-century reinterpretation, magnificently explicit in Wirth, Case, and Crowley. Its presence in the original fifteenth-century card remains unproved.

### Was the figure originally an angel?

**Documented:** Morgan’s Temperance is a woman without prominent Marseille-style wings. Later Marseille cards show wings.

**Scholarly reconstruction:** The subject began as the personified virtue and became angelic in the popular design tradition.

Calling every early Temperance “the angel Temperance” collapses this development.

### Iris and the Waite-Smith flowers

The flowers are visually consistent with irises. Iris is also a winged messenger associated with the rainbow.

**Disputed claim:** Smith and Waite intended the angel literally as Iris.

Neither Waite’s published description nor a located Smith statement confirms it. The connection remains an elegant later reading.

### The liquid’s direction

Woodcut copying, reversal of blocks, restoration, and orientation cause the apparent direction to vary. Some cards appear to violate gravity.

**Tradition:** This is interpreted as spirit flowing into matter, matter rising into spirit, or a miraculous circuit.

**Historical caution:** Direction may reflect workshop copying as much as doctrine.

### Misprints and graphic accidents

- **XIIII** is not necessarily an error; additive Roman numeral forms were normal in early cards.
- Small Marseille details interpreted as Hebrew letters, snakes, hands, genital forms, or secret inscriptions can arise from damaged blocks, stencil drift, overprinting, or restoration.
- Conver-derived commercial editions altered colors substantially.
- Grimaud and Camoin-Jodorowsky are influential editorial versions, not transparent facsimiles.
- The persistent misspelling “Pamela Coleman Smith” should be corrected to **Pamela Colman Smith**.
- The 1392 “Gringonneur tarot” claim is a historical misidentification that became an occult tradition.
- Claims that missing fifteenth-century Devils were routinely stolen for black magic are unsupported legend.

### Tzaddi/Heh and Art

**Common mistake:** Crowley’s “Tzaddi is not the Star” reform is sometimes extended to all later trumps.

**Documented:** It changes Emperor/Star correspondences. Art remains Samekh/Sagittarius and Path 25.

## Open questions

1. Which surviving fragment should be called the earliest physical Temperance depends on refinements in dating and attribution.
2. When and where wings first became a stable part of the tarot image remains less securely documented than the underlying vessel-pouring virtue.
3. The exact relationship among late-fifteenth-century Milanese designs, surviving sheets, and the later French Marseille pattern remains reconstructive.
4. Smith’s personal sources for the iris, solar forehead, land-and-water stance, and distant crown are not fully documented.
5. No primary evidence was found identifying the medieval figure as Iris, Michael, Ganymede, an alchemical androgyne, or a Cathar messenger.
6. Etteilla’s editions require bibliographical care: later Grand Etteilla packs often conflate his work with that of pupils and nineteenth-century editors.
7. A definitive account of Temperance in every surviving Sicilian and Bolognese state would require direct comparison of museum impressions not all available in legible online reproductions.
8. Specific Temperance quotations in Yeats, Bergman, Eliot, or named architecture could not be traced; general tarot or virtue parallels should not be promoted into direct influence without evidence.
