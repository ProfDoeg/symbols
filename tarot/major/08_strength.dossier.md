# Strength: Research Dossier

## Method and evidentiary labels

This dossier distinguishes six kinds of statement:

- **[Documented fact]** directly supported by a surviving card, archival record, or published primary text.
- **[Scholarly reconstruction]** an inference argued from objects, texts, styles, game rules, or provenance.
- **[Tradition]** a historically transmitted practice or interpretation whose first author may be uncertain.
- **[Esoteric attribution]** a correspondence asserted inside an occult system, without implying historical antiquity.
- **[Disputed claim]** a proposition for which specialists offer competing interpretations.
- **[Legend / modern invention]** a story contradicted by the documentary chronology, unsupported by evidence, or demonstrably created in modern occult literature.

Short quotations are retained where they are especially important. Page numbers refer to the editions listed under Sources; pagination sometimes differs among scans.

### The central historical point

**[Documented fact]** Strength began as **Fortitude**, one of the four cardinal virtues of classical and Christian moral philosophy. Tarot retained Justice, Fortitude, and Temperance; Prudence is absent from the standard 22-trump sequence, although some Renaissance commentators interpreted the Hermit or Hanged Man as Prudence.

**[Documented fact]** Neither VIII nor XI can be called the universal “original” number. Early hand-painted cards were normally unnumbered, and regional trump orders differed. XI became canonical in the Lombard/French lineage represented by the Tarot de Marseille. VIII became dominant in English-language occult tarot through the Golden Dawn and Waite–Smith.

**[Esoteric attribution, late nineteenth century]** Teth and Leo are not documented medieval attributes. They belong to the Golden Dawn’s systematic alignment of the 22 trumps with the 22 Hebrew letters and the paths of the Tree of Life.

# Iconography deck by deck

## Precursors: Fortitude before tarot

**[Documented fact]** Medieval and Renaissance artists normally personified *Fortitudo* as a woman, often enthroned or armored. Her attributes could include:

- a lion;
- a column or broken column;
- a club;
- armor, shield, or sword;
- Samson, David, or Hercules as subordinate exemplars.

The column expresses constancy or firmness but also evokes Samson’s destruction of the Philistine temple in Judges 16. The lion may evoke Samson killing the lion in Judges 14, David’s lion, Daniel’s lions, Hercules and the Nemean lion, or the conventional heraldic and moral symbolism of courage.

**[Disputed identification]** A lion by itself does not determine whether a particular figure is Hercules, Samson, or an allegory of Fortitude. Renaissance imagery deliberately allowed overlapping pagan, biblical, and moral exempla. The most defensible identification is usually “Fortitude represented through a lion-subduing figure”; the hero’s name requires additional attributes.

## Marziano da Tortona and Michelino da Besozzo, Milan, c. 1412–1425

**[Documented fact]** Marziano’s *Tractatus de deificatione sedecim heroum* describes a special pack of sixteen classical gods painted for Filippo Maria Visconti. It is an important precursor to trionfi packs, but not the standard tarot: its trump subjects and suits differ.

**[Negative finding]** It contains no card securely equivalent to tarot Fortitude. Hercules is not one of its sixteen deities. It therefore documents courtly allegorical card-making before the standard tarot, but not Strength itself.

## Visconti di Modrone / Cary–Yale, Milan, c. 1440–1450

**[Documented object; date reconstructed from patronage and style]** The surviving Fortitude shows a richly dressed woman interacting physically with a lion, commonly described as opening or controlling its jaws. The image is unusually close to the later French woman-and-lion formula.

Meaning at this historical level: **the virtue Fortitude**, understood as courage and constancy. It does not itself document Leo, Teth, alchemy, psychoanalysis, or divination.

**[Disputed detail]** “Cary–Yale” and “Visconti di Modrone” are two names for this group; “Cary–Yale” derives from the collection. Dates in catalogues range roughly from the 1440s to the 1460s.

## Brera–Brambilla, Milan, mid-fifteenth century

**[Documented fact]** Only two trumps survive in this fragmentary pack; no Strength card is known.

**[Negative finding]** Statements describing a Brambilla Strength image are reconstructions from other Visconti packs, not observations of a surviving card.

## Pierpont Morgan–Bergamo Visconti–Sforza, Milan, c. 1450 with replacement c. 1480

The Morgan catalogue dates the pack’s main campaign to about 1450, after Francesco Sforza’s entry into Milan, but identifies Fortitude, Temperance, and the Sun as later replacement cards, approximately 1480–1500. The museum attributes Fortitude to Antonio Cicognara. [Morgan object record](https://www.themorgan.org/collection/tarot-cards/fortitude) and [pack catalogue](https://www.themorgan.org/manuscript/158525).

**[Documented depiction]** A muscular young man—often called Hercules—wears a short tunic, raises or carries a club, and grapples with or prepares to strike a lion.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** The club and lion make Hercules and the Nemean lion a strong identification. Yet the card still functions as the abstract virtue Fortitude. The male hero replaces the normal female personification.

**[Correction]** This is not securely the earliest surviving Strength: the Cary–Yale woman-and-lion card is probably earlier. Nor is the Morgan card necessarily part of the original c. 1450 painting campaign.

## “Charles VI” / Gringonneur tarot, probably Florence, c. 1460–1480

**[Documented object; attribution disputed]** Despite its traditional name, the cards were not made for Charles VI of France. Art-historical scholarship places them in fifteenth-century Italy, generally Florence.

**[Depiction]** Fortitude is a woman holding or breaking a column. This belongs to the second major early iconographic family.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** The broken column can evoke Samson; as a female virtue, however, she is not literally Samson. The attribute translates his strength and steadfastness into allegory.

## Ferrarese and Este cards, c. 1450–1500

**[Documented fact]** The Este court bought and commissioned *carte da trionfi*. The February 10, 1442 account paid Jacopo Sagramoro to color four packs, including cups, swords, coins, batons, and “all the figures.” The record does not enumerate the trumps or describe Strength. A transcription and contextual discussion appear in Farley’s *Cultural History*. [Accessible scan](https://e-isomero.com/pdf/book/1611846398.pdf).

**[Negative finding]** No securely identified surviving Este Strength card gives a complete deck-by-deck iconographic sequence comparable to the Milanese material. “Ferrarese Strength” is often reconstructed from regional lists, later sheets, and cards of uncertain attribution.

## Mantegna “Tarocchi,” northern Italy, c. 1465–1475

**[Documented fact]** These fifty engravings are not a tarot pack and were probably not playing cards. Their conventional name is misleading.

**[Relevance]** The series includes moral and cosmic personifications useful for comparison with Renaissance allegory. It does not supply an ancestral tarot Strength card in a 22-trump sequence.

## Rosenwald sheets, probably central Italy, c. 1500

The two uncut sheets survive in the National Gallery of Art’s Rosenwald Collection. Binding evidence has associated them with Perugia around 1501, though the place of card manufacture remains uncertain. [Discussion of the sheets and provenance problem](https://tarot-heritage.com/2017/10/31/the-rosenwald-deck/).

**[Documented depiction]** A haloed female Fortitude holds a column.

**[Documented anomaly]** Fortitude and Justice appear to bear the same or confusingly similar numeral, commonly read as VIII. This may be a cutting, engraving, or numbering error rather than evidence for a doctrinal identification.

**[Disputed]** Whether the sheets preserve a Florentine, Perugian, or other central-Italian pattern remains unsettled.

## Budapest sheets, late fifteenth or early sixteenth century

**[Documented depiction]** A man subdues a lion, apparently at its jaws. The image can be compared with Hercules or Samson.

**[Disputed]** The identification as Hercules is plausible but not certain; without a club, skin, inscription, or narrative setting, Samson remains possible. The surviving sheet demonstrates that male lion-fighters existed outside the Visconti replacement card.

## Cary sheet, Milanese, c. 1500

**[Documented fragment]** Only part of the figure survives, but the lion is visible. It supplies another link between Lombard lion iconography and the later French pattern.

## Sola Busca, Venice/Ferrara sphere, 1491; colored copy c. 1490s

**[Documented fact]** The Sola Busca is the earliest surviving substantially complete tarot whose numeral cards carry full narrative scenes. Its trumps bear classical or pseudo-classical names rather than the standard allegories.

**[Negative finding]** It has no card entitled Fortitude or Strength directly equivalent to Marseille XI. Its importance to Strength is indirect: Pamela Colman Smith demonstrably or very probably borrowed several compositional ideas from the Sola Busca minors after viewing the British Museum’s photographed cards. No comparably specific Sola Busca source for her Strength has been established.

## Minchiate, Florence, sixteenth century onward

Minchiate expanded the trump series to forty permanent trumps plus the Fool.

**[Documented pattern]** Fortitude belongs to the virtues and is commonly shown as a woman with a column. Its ordinal place differs from standard Marseille tarot.

**[Game significance]** It is an ordinary permanent trump, not one of the highest special scoring *arie*. Therefore its trick-taking force is its position in the regional sequence, not a fixed symbolic point value comparable to the Fool, World, or highest arie.

## Bolognese tarocchino

**[Documented pattern]** The early Bolognese virtues were grouped. Later packs may identify trumps chiefly through their established sequence rather than printed titles. Column-bearing Fortitude occurs in Bolognese-related imagery.

In the modern 62-card Ottocento pack the four lowest Moors are unnumbered/equal, followed by numbered trumps 5–16 and the celestial trumps. Strength’s historical subject is embedded in this reordered sequence rather than functioning as Marseille XI. [Ottocento rules and rankings](https://www.pagat.com/tarot/ottocento.html).

## Sicilian tarot

**[Documented fact]** Tarot reached Sicily in the seventeenth century, probably through Rome. The shortened modern pack has 21 trumps, twenty numbered above the unnumbered *Miseria*, plus the Fool (*Fuggitivo*). [Rules and historical outline](https://www.pagat.com/tarot/sicilian.html).

**[Documented regional reassignment]** Fortitude is conventionally identified with trump 6, not XI or VIII. In play it is simply trump 6: it beats lower trumps and loses to higher ones. Players normally call middle trumps by number rather than allegorical title.

## Tarot de Paris and Jacques Viéville, Paris, c. 1650

These are early French tarots but not identical to the later canonical Marseille pattern.

**[Documented depiction]** Both use lion-subduing Strength imagery, with differences in orientation, costume, and bodily relation to the animal. Their survival confirms that the woman/man-and-lion type was established in France by the mid-seventeenth century.

## Jean Noblet, Paris, c. 1650

**[Documented depiction]** *LA FORCE*, XI: a woman leans over a lion and places both hands at its mouth. Her broad, scalloped hat develops the shape later read as a sideways eight.

**[Important correction]** The object over her head is historically a hat. Calling it a deliberate mathematical lemniscate in Noblet or Marseille is an esoteric interpretation, not a documented cardmaker’s explanation.

## Jean Dodal, Lyon, c. 1701–1715

**[Documented depiction]** *LA FORCE*, XI: the standing woman holds the lion’s jaws. The hat has a conspicuous looping brim; the animal rises at her lower body. Color and line differ from Noblet, but the same type persists.

## Pierre Madenié, Dijon, 1709, and related Type II Marseille decks

**[Documented evolution]** The woman-and-lion scheme becomes more standardized. Details such as the exposed foot, hand placement, direction of gaze, and hat vary between workshops.

## Besançon pattern, eighteenth–nineteenth centuries

**[Documented regional renaming]** In Swiss, Alsatian, and eastern French contexts sensitive to Protestant or local markets, the Papess and Pope were replaced by **Juno** and **Jupiter**. The substitution defines the Besançon pattern; Strength generally remains *La Force*.

**[Correction]** Juno/Jupiter are not alternative names for Strength. They are cited because they demonstrate that trump titles and religious figures could be regionally altered while the game structure survived.

## Jean Dodal to Nicolas Conver, Marseille, 1760

**[Documented depiction]** Conver’s *LA FORCE*, XI, fixes the familiar late Marseille type: a woman in an elaborate hat opens or contains the lion’s jaws with bare hands. The action is ambiguous—opening, closing, or merely holding the mouth—and no contemporary cardmaker’s gloss settles it.

## Grimaud, late nineteenth and twentieth centuries

**[Documented modern standardization]** Grimaud’s *Ancien Tarot de Marseille* regularized lines and reduced the stencil palette. Its enormous distribution made the Conver/Besançon-derived visual language seem more singular and ancient than the actual family of historical patterns.

## Etteilla’s Grand Etteilla, Paris, first series 1788–1789

**[Documented fact]** Etteilla did not simply publish a Marseille deck with divinatory captions. He reorganized, renamed, and redesigned the pack as an Egyptian cosmogonic “Book of Thoth.”

**[Negative/qualified finding]** There is no straightforward card in the original Grand Etteilla sequence that preserves Marseille XI as “Strength” with woman and lion. Later Grand Etteilla editions and derivative manuals sometimes cross-reference ordinary Marseille titles, producing misleading claims that “Grand Etteilla card 11” is Strength. The system must be read by Etteilla’s own numbered subjects and paired upright/reversed inscriptions.

## Oswald Wirth, Paris, 1889

**[Documented fact]** Wirth created 22 occult trumps for Stanislas de Guaita; it was not originally a full 78-card deck.

**[Depiction]** XI *La Force*: an elegant woman calmly controls a lion’s open jaws. Wirth retained the Marseille number and added occultizing details and cleaner emblematic geometry.

Wirth later wrote:

> “L’énergie suprême, à laquelle ne résiste aucune brutalité, se présente… sous l’aspect d’une reine blonde et gracieuse…”

“Supreme energy, against which no brutality can resist, appears … as a graceful blond queen.” He contrasts this feminine, subtle power with muscular Hercules. [French text](https://maestrosdelsaber.com/wp-content/uploads/ftp-files/Tarot/a%20Wirth%2C%20Oswald/Tarot%20Wirth%20Oswald%20-%20Tarot.pdf).

## Golden Dawn manuscript tarot, c. 1888–1900

**[Documented esoteric system]** The Golden Dawn assigned:

- VIII Strength;
- Teth, “serpent”;
- Leo;
- Path 19 between Chesed and Geburah;
- title **Daughter of the Flaming Sword**;
- yellow/greenish-yellow and related color scales varying by grade and source.

**[Why VIII? Scholarly reconstruction from the system]** The Hebrew letters run in order through the astrological sequence. Leo needs to precede Libra. In Marseille numbering Justice was VIII and Strength XI; leaving them there would give Leo to Justice and Libra to Strength. The order therefore exchanged their positions so Strength/Teth/Leo became VIII and Justice/Lamed/Libra XI.

**[Qualification]** Waite later acknowledged the exchange but refused to explain it publicly. The swap originated in the Golden Dawn milieu, not in Pamela Colman Smith’s drawing and not solely with Waite.

### Wang and Cicero reconstructions

**[Modern reconstruction]** Robert Wang’s *Golden Dawn Tarot* and Chic and Sandra Tabatha Cicero’s *Golden Dawn Magical Tarot* reconstruct the order’s written instructions; neither is an untouched Victorian deck. They should be treated as editorial realizations of manuscripts, with artistic decisions made decades later.

Both retain VIII/Teth/Leo and the woman-and-lion emblem, adding formal Golden Dawn color and Tree-of-Life symbolism.

## Waite–Smith, London, 1909

**Artist:** Pamela Colman Smith.  
**Designer/author:** Arthur Edward Waite.  
**Publisher:** William Rider & Son.

**[Documented depiction]** A woman in white bends over a tawny lion. One hand rests above its muzzle and another below; the lion licks or approaches her hand. A chain or girdle of red flowers connects them. A horizontal figure eight floats over her head. Mountains and a green landscape replace the flat Marseille ground.

Waite’s own description:

> “A woman, over whose head there broods the same symbol of life which we have seen in the card of the Magician, is closing the jaws of a lion.”

He says that her “beneficent fortitude has already subdued the lion” and that it is led “by a chain of flowers.” He interprets the chain as the “sweet yoke and the light burden of Divine Law.” *The Pictorial Key to the Tarot* (1910), Part II, VIII. [Scan/text](https://magicgatebg.com/Books/Waite_Tarot.pdf).

**[Documented change]** The woman no longer looks as though she is exerting muscular force. Smith converted the ambiguous Marseille struggle into intimacy and voluntary submission. Waite calls it “innocentia inviolata,” contemplative strength, and the higher nature governing passion.

**[Source analysis]**

- Marseille woman and lion: direct traditional source.
- Floating infinity sign: repeats Smith’s Magician and makes explicit what occultists had read into the Marseille hat.
- Floral leash: a new Waite–Smith emblem, though floral bonds and garlands have broader Christian and occult precedents.
- Sola Busca: no specific derivation for Strength has been demonstrated.
- **[Disputed]** Exact division of invention between Waite and Smith cannot be reconstructed from the finished deck alone.

Waite wrote:

> “For reasons which satisfy myself, this card has been interchanged with that of Justice, which is usually numbered eight.”

This conceals rather than denies the Golden Dawn astrological reason.

## Paul Foster Case and the BOTA tarot, 1920s–1930s

**[Documented fact]** Case retained VIII/Teth/Leo and worked with artist Jessie Burns Parke. BOTA issued black-and-white keys for students to color according to prescribed correspondences.

**Depiction:** close to Waite–Smith: woman, lion, floral chain, horizontal eight. Case gives the lion a red color and emphasizes the woman’s control through knowledge rather than repression.

**[Esoteric attribution]** Case relates Teth both to serpent-power and the lion: sensation and desire are not annihilated but directed. The woman represents conscious control; the lion is the fiery life-power. Modern BOTA summaries call the lion both “Adversary” and “Redeemer.” [BOTA-oriented discussion](https://benebellwen.com/2021/07/16/botatarot/).

## Crowley–Harris Thoth Tarot, painted 1938–1943; published as cards 1969

**[Documented fact]** Aleister Crowley designed the esoteric program; Lady Frieda Harris painted the deck. The paintings were completed in the early 1940s, exhibited before full publication, described in *The Book of Thoth* (1944), and first issued as a complete commercial deck in 1969.

**Title and number:** XI **Lust**. Crowley restored XI to the card while keeping Teth and Leo.

**Depiction:** a nude or near-nude ecstatic Scarlet Woman rides a many-headed leonine Beast. She holds the reins in her left hand and raises a cup or Grail in her right. The cup contains or signifies the sacrament of the Aeon. Saints or human forms appear beneath; serpentine rays and sexual, solar, and apocalyptic imagery fill the composition.

Crowley writes:

> “She rides astride the Beast; in her left hand she holds the reins, representing the passion which unites them. In her right she holds aloft the cup, the Holy Grail aflame with love and death.”

For Crowley, “Lust” is not the Christian vice opposed to chastity but wholehearted participation in existence: “the joy of strength exercised.” [*Book of Thoth* scan](https://www.rahoorkhuit.net/library/crowley/equinox/volume3/vol_3_no_05.pdf).

**[Documented change]** The calm controller of animal passion becomes Babalon joyfully united with the Beast. Domination becomes sacramental identification.

### The Tzaddi/Heh issue

**[Important correction]** Crowley’s “Tzaddi is not the Star” revelation exchanges the Hebrew-letter attributions of the Emperor and Star: Emperor receives Tzaddi and Star receives Heh in his mature account. It does **not** exchange Strength and Justice. Lust remains Teth/Leo.

Crowley’s return of Lust to XI creates a non-sequential numbering relative to the Golden Dawn’s reordered VIII/Leo scheme; his titles and Hebrew attributions matter more than an unbroken numerical-letter series.

## Camoin–Jodorowsky, Marseille, 1997

**[Documented modern redesign]** Philippe Camoin and Alejandro Jodorowsky issued a newly drawn synthesis, not a facsimile of Conver. Camoin explicitly says they combined details from different Type I, Type II, and Besançon decks, introduced additional color shades, and added elements absent from known historical Marseille packs. [Camoin’s explanation](https://fr.camoin.com/tarot/_-10-Extraits-d-Interviews.html).

Strength remains XI *La Force*, woman and lion.

**[Disputed claim]** Marketing language about restoring the tarot’s “original purity” is esoteric/editorial, not an archaeological reconstruction. Camoin himself distinguishes “restoring” the tradition’s vigor from physically restoring one historical object.

## Notable modern redesigns

- **Thoth-derived decks:** make Strength erotic, ecstatic, or explicitly Babalonian.
- **Feminist decks:** often treat the woman and lion as alliance rather than hierarchy.
- **Animal and nature decks:** may remove the human figure, making the lion itself Strength.
- **Psychological/Jungian decks:** depict relationship with shadow, instinct, anger, or libido.
- **Queer and decolonial decks:** frequently replace the white European female virtue with varied bodies and cultures.
- **Minimalist decks:** retain only lion, flower, jaw, or infinity sign.

These are **[modern inventions]**, meaningful within their creators’ systems but not restorations of a single lost original.

# The game

## What Strength does in play

**[Documented fact]** Tarot was created for trick-taking. In most historical games, a permanent trump beats any ordinary suit card and any lower trump. Strength’s practical power depends entirely on its ordinal position in the local pack.

It normally has no special point value merely because its picture means strength. High trumps, the lowest trump, Fool, kings, and regional combinations receive special scoring in many games; a middle trump such as Strength usually counts as an ordinary card unless a local rule or combination says otherwise.

## Regional trump orders

Dummett reconstructed three principal Italian families:

| Order | Region | Virtue arrangement | Strength/Fortitude |
|---|---|---|---|
| A, “southern” | Bologna, Florence; later Rome/Sicily-related forms | Justice, Fortitude, Temperance grouped around Love/Chariot, with local internal changes | Variable; not Marseille XI |
| B, “eastern” | Ferrara, Venice | Virtues dispersed; World highest, Justice placed unusually high | Fortitude separated from Justice and Temperance |
| C, “western” | Milan/Lombardy, later France and Switzerland | Closest ancestor of Marseille | Fortitude becomes XI in the standard French sequence |

This three-family model is a **[scholarly reconstruction]** based on lists, rules, poems, surviving packs, and later regional continuity; it is not a fifteenth-century written classification. A concise translation of Dummett’s account appears in the [Tarot History Forum material](https://forum.tarothistory.com/viewtopic.php?t=1185), while *A Wicked Pack of Cards* summarizes the order families.

## The Fool

**[Documented tradition]**

- Italian *Matto*, French *Mat*, German/Austrian *Sküs* or related names.
- Usually unnumbered in traditional Italian and Marseille packs.
- In French tarot, the *Excuse* normally cannot win the trick but may be reclaimed by its player, subject to the last-trick rule.
- In many central-European Tarock games it became the highest trump, effectively XXII.
- In Bologna it has no normal rank and is played under a special rule.
- Occult decks commonly print 0, a convention strengthened by Court de Gébelin, Etteilla, Lévi-derived systems, and Waite.

## Strength in principal games

- **Marseille-pattern historical play:** trump XI, beating I–X and losing to XII–XXI.
- **French tarot with modern French-suited packs:** historical allegories disappeared; the corresponding card is simply trump 11.
- **Bolognese tarocchino/Ottocento:** the traditional virtue is embedded in the Bolognese sequence; players identify cards by regional number and picture. It is not privileged like Angel, World, Bégato, or Matto.
- **Minchiate:** one of forty trumps, below the highest *arie*; its exact conventional number follows the minchiate order, not Marseille.
- **Sicilian tarocchi:** Fortitude corresponds to trump 6; ordinary rank/value.
- **Austrian and German Tarock:** surviving packs generally use Roman-numeral trumps and genre scenes, so “Strength” as a named picture no longer exists. Trump XI is simply Tarock XI.
- **Divinatory tarot:** rank has symbolic, not trick-taking, function.

# Chronology of attestation

### c. 1412–1425 — Marziano da Tortona

**[Documented fact]** Marziano describes a commissioned allegorical card pack for Filippo Maria Visconti. It establishes the courtly prehistory of trumps but does not name Fortitude.

### 1440–1450 — earliest surviving Strength

**[Scholarly dating]** The Cary–Yale/Visconti di Modrone woman and lion is the leading candidate.

### February 1442 — Ferrara ledger

**[Documented fact]** Jacopo Sagramoro was paid to color four *carte da trionfi* packs. The entry names suits but not individual trumps. Therefore it attests trionfi production, not the word *Fortezza*.

### c. 1450 and c. 1480 — Visconti–Sforza

Main pack produced for the Visconti–Sforza court; the surviving Hercules/lion Fortitude is probably a later replacement.

### c. 1460–1480 — Charles VI/Florentine cards

Column-bearing female Fortitude.

### c. 1470–1500 — *Sermo de ludo cum aliis*

**[Documented fact, uncertain date]** Anonymous moralizing sermon gives the first complete surviving list of standard trumps and condemns their hierarchy. It names *Forteza*. This is the earliest secure textual naming of the card presently traceable.

### Late fifteenth century — Budapest and Rosenwald material

Lion-fighter and column types coexist.

### 1491 — Sola Busca

Complete narrative minors, but no direct standard Strength trump.

### c. 1500 — appropriati and poems

**[Documented fact]** *Tarocchi appropriati* assign trump subjects to named people, and poetic works use the trumps metaphorically. These sources are crucial for regional order.

### 1505 onward — *taraux* in France

**[Documented fact]** French documentary attestations appear in Avignon and subsequently elsewhere. The standard term shifts from Italian *trionfi* to *tarocchi/taraux/tarot* during the late fifteenth and early sixteenth centuries.

### 1527 — Teofilo Folengo

Folengo’s *Chaos del Triperuno* uses tarot cards in literary and divinatory-like poetic constructions. It does not prove a complete modern fortune-telling system.

### c. 1565 — Piscina and the anonymous *Discorso*

**[Documented fact]** Early interpretive discourses moralize the sequence. They remain Renaissance ethical/allegorical readings, not Golden Dawn-style occult correspondences.

### c. 1650 — Noblet, Viéville, Tarot de Paris

Earliest major surviving French witnesses to lion-subduing *La Force*.

### 1701–1760 — Dodal, Madenié, Payen, Conver

The Marseille woman/lion/looped-hat design stabilizes.

### 1781 — Court de Gébelin and Comte de Mellet

The first influential printed occult-historical reinterpretations: Egyptian Book of Thoth and Hebrew alphabet.

### 1783–1791 — Etteilla

First professional, systematic tarot cartomancy publications and first purpose-built occult tarot.

### 1854–1856 — Éliphas Lévi

*Dogme et rituel de la haute magie* makes tarot central to ceremonial magic and Kabbalah.

### 1863–1870 — Paul Christian

Elaborates the imaginary Egyptian initiation gallery and “arcana” language.

### 1888 — Mathers

*The Tarot: Its Occult Signification, Use in Fortune-Telling, and Method of Play* gives English divinatory meanings; Strength remains XI.

### 1889 — Wirth and Papus

Wirth publishes 22 occult trumps; Papus publishes *Le Tarot des Bohémiens*. Both initially retain Strength XI, although their letter systems differ from the later Golden Dawn.

### c. 1888–1900 — Golden Dawn

Strength becomes VIII, Teth, Leo, Path 19.

### 1909–1910 — Waite–Smith and *Pictorial Key*

Smith draws the influential gentle lion-tamer; Waite publishes the Christian-mystical explanation.

### 1930s — Case/BOTA

Systematic American Hermetic-Qabalistic elaboration of VIII/Teth/Leo.

### 1938–1944/1969 — Thoth

Harris paints Lust; Crowley publishes its doctrine; deck commercially published later.

### 1960s–1980s — historical and psychological revolutions

Dummett re-centers tarot as a Renaissance game; Kaplan inventories surviving decks. Jungian and humanistic readers increasingly understand Strength as integration of instinct.

### 1969/1973 — Calvino

Tarot becomes a combinatorial literary machine in *Il castello dei destini incrociati*.

### 1978–1980 — Rachel Pollack

*Seventy-Eight Degrees of Wisdom* helps establish modern psychological and initiatory readings.

Feminist, queer, Jungian, therapeutic, secular, and culturally localized Strength cards proliferate.

### Lévi in his own framework

Lévi organizes the two volumes of *Dogme et rituel* into 22 chapters paralleling the trumps. In the received Marseille sequence, chapter XI concerns magical force. His famous Hermetic quotation—“It is the strong force of all force, for it will conquer every subtle thing and penetrate every solid thing”—appears in the doctrine of magical power. [French edition, relevant passage around p. 262](https://books.google.com/books/about/Dogme_et_rituel_de_la_haute_magie_v_1.html?id=eN4FAAAAIAAJ).

**[Esoteric attribution]** Lévi’s general letter-by-number structure points XI toward Kaph, not Teth. Claims that Lévi assigned Strength directly to Teth/Leo usually read the later Golden Dawn table backward into him.

### Papus in his own words

Papus labels Strength:

> “11th Hebrew letter (Kaph).”

He derives it from “the hand of man … in the act of grasping strongly,” gives Mars and Tuesday, and summarizes the principle as liberty, courage, and “to dare.” *The Tarot of the Bohemians*, trans. A. P. Morton, section on the eleventh arcanum. [Text](https://guru-ai.org/read/western_esoteric/tarot-of-the-bohemians/052).

### Golden Dawn colors

Published reconstructions differ in wording and pigment:

- King Scale: greenish yellow;
- Queen Scale: deep purple;
- Emperor Scale: gray;
- Empress Scale: reddish amber or related complementary mixtures.

**[Qualification]** Color tables vary between manuscript families and published editors. Wang and the Ciceros are reconstructions, not independent Renaissance evidence.

# Divinatory meanings by author

## Etteilla, 1780s–1790s

**[Negative finding]** A reliable verbatim upright/reversed entry for a card identical to ordinary Strength could not be established from the accessible first-series sources. Etteilla’s reordered Grand Etteilla should not be silently converted through Marseille numbers.

Later Etteilla-derived lists assign Strength-like concepts—force, power, courage, magnanimity—to cross-referenced cards, but attribution varies by Grand Etteilla I, II, and III editions. Reporting a single “Etteilla Strength meaning” without naming the edition would create false precision.

## S. L. MacGregor Mathers, 1888

Mathers retains XI:

> “Power, Might, Force, Strength, Fortitude.”

Reversed:

> “Abuse of Power, Overbearingness, Want of Fortitude.”

*The Tarot: Its Occult Signification, Use in Fortune-Telling, and Method of Play* (London, 1888), table of meanings. [Scan](https://www.howardnema.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/12/the-tarot-1888-by-macgregor-mathers-1.pdf).

## A. E. Waite, 1910

Upright:

> “Power, energy, action, courage, magnanimity; also complete success and honours.”

Reversed:

> “Despotism, abuse of power, weakness, discord, sometimes even disgrace.”

*The Pictorial Key to the Tarot*, Part III, VIII.

Waite’s higher, non-divinatory meaning is more specifically Christian-mystical: strength grounded in God, contemplation, innocent purity, and mastery of the passions.

## Aleister Crowley, 1944

Crowley generally does not use simple upright/reversed pairs. For Lust he gives:

> “Courage, strength, energy and action, une grande passion; resort to magick; the use of magical power.”

Ill-dignified tendencies include surrender to appetite or dissipation rather than integrated ecstasy. Crowley’s method relies on elemental and astrological dignity, not mechanically turning the card upside down.

## Paul Foster Case

Case distinguishes genuine divination from “vulgar fortune telling.” Strength indicates courage derived from knowledge, control of the life-force, action, and conscious direction of desire.

**[Qualification]** Case’s published system does not reduce neatly to one canonical reversed list. Later BOTA teaching treats the negative pole as failure to direct desire, repression, animality, anger, or misused power.

## Rachel Pollack, 1978/1980

Pollack’s Strength is the reconciliation of consciousness and animal energy. The lion is not destroyed; the woman accepts and guides it. Courage, passion, healing, and confidence flow from that integration.

Reversed, Pollack discusses weakness, blocked or misdirected energy, fear of passion, and a split between conscious intention and instinct.

**[Modern psychological reading]** Pollack helped popularize the idea that Strength represents cooperation with the “inner lion,” rather than moral suppression.

## Mary K. Greer

Greer emphasizes embodied courage, self-trust, passion, endurance, and the capacity to engage anger or desire constructively. In reversed-card practice, Strength may become insecurity, depletion, blocked libido, rage, coercion, or an inward/private form of the upright quality.

**[Qualification]** Greer explicitly offers multiple ways to read reversals—opposite, blocked, delayed, projected, internalized—so a single antonym is not faithful to her method.

# Esoteric and symbolic readings

## Christian moral theology

**First layer:** fifteenth-century tarot.

Fortitude is one of the cardinal virtues. In Thomistic and wider Christian teaching it is firmness in pursuit of the good, especially under danger, suffering, or fear. It is not mere physical power.

- Woman: personified virtue.
- Lion: danger, wrath, passion, persecution, or courage.
- Column: steadfastness; sometimes Samson’s self-sacrificial final act.
- Club/Hercules: physical might elevated into moral exemplum.

This is the strongest historically contextualized reading of the earliest cards.

## Classical and humanist

Hercules was a Renaissance model of strength, labor, virtue, and choice. The Nemean lion explains the lion and club particularly well in the Morgan replacement card.

**[Disputed]** The exact figure may blend Hercules and Samson. Humanist court culture often aligned pagan heroes with Christian virtues rather than treating them as mutually exclusive.

## Hermetic and alchemical

**[Esoteric attribution, principally nineteenth century onward]**

Strength becomes the volatile or animal force mastered by a subtler agent:

- lion = sulfur, solar fire, appetite, prima materia, or fixed fire;
- woman = Mercury, purified will, soul, or alchemical operator;
- opening the mouth = release/articulation of force;
- closing the mouth = fixation or containment;
- union rather than killing = conjunction.

No fifteenth-century text has been found explaining the card alchemically. Lévi, Wirth, Golden Dawn writers, Case, and Crowley spread these readings.

## Kabbalistic

### Lévi/Papus/Wirth family

Strength XI aligns by number with Kaph, “palm/hand,” producing ideas of grasping, force, and magical agency.

### Golden Dawn family

Strength VIII aligns with Teth, “serpent,” Leo, and Path 19.

**Conflict:** both cannot be the original medieval correspondence. They are separate nineteenth-century constructions.

### Crowley

Lust remains Teth/Leo while returning to XI. This privileges the correspondence over ordinary numerical order.

## Astrological

**[Esoteric attribution]** The lion made Leo an attractive identification, but no evidence places Leo on medieval Strength. Papus instead gave Mars. Golden Dawn standardized Leo. Modern decks often treat the lion as proof of an ancient Leo correspondence; historically it is evidence only of leonine iconography.

## Hermetic-Qabalistic psychology

The woman is conscious or superconscious will; the lion is desire, libido, nervous energy, or astral force. The flowers signify attraction and harmony rather than chains of iron. Case and Waite spread this in English-language occultism.

## Thelemic

Crowley rejects “strength” as a sufficiently complete title:

- Babalon = ecstatic, receptive, active feminine divinity;
- Beast = the total force of life;
- reins = passion uniting them;
- Grail = love and death, blood of the saints, sacrament of the Aeon;
- many heads = forms of the Beast and the totality of desire.

Lust is “the joy of strength exercised,” not ascetic restraint.

## Jodorowsky–Costa

Strength XI begins the second decimal series after Wheel X. It is creative force becoming conscious: sexuality, voice, animal energy, discipline, seduction, digestion of ideas, and entry into action.

Their method rejects reversed cards and externally imposed Kabbalistic/astrological grids. The woman may be opening the lion’s mouth, closing it, feeding it, or speaking through it; the ambiguity is productive. [Spanish text](https://www.tarotjunguiano.com/wp-content/uploads/2017/06/la-via-del-tarot-alejandro-jodorowsky.pdf).

## Jungian

**[Modern psychological analogy]** Jung did not create a canonical tarot system and did not publish a card-by-card reading of Strength comparable to Waite or Crowley.

Later Jungian writers treat:

- lion as instinct, libido, aggression, shadow, or autonomous psyche;
- woman as ego, anima, Self, or mediating consciousness;
- their contact as integration rather than repression;
- reversal as dissociation, inflation, helplessness, or compulsive control.

Pollack, Sallie Nichols, Mary Greer, and therapeutic tarot communities spread this approach. Calling it “Jung’s meaning of Strength” is inaccurate.

## Paintings and buildings

Fortitude appears in major civic and religious virtue cycles, among them:

- Giotto’s virtues and vices in the Arena Chapel;
- Florentine civic virtue imagery;
- cassone panels such as Pesellino’s virtues;
- sculptural programs on cathedrals, baptisteries, tombs, and public buildings.

These works are iconographic relatives and possible visual reservoirs, not necessarily quotations of tarot. Conversely, modern murals and installations often quote Waite–Smith Strength directly, but no single building could be verified as a historically decisive transmission point.

# Controversies and disputes

## Egyptian origin

Court de Gébelin declared tarot a surviving Egyptian Book of Thoth. He wrote before Champollion’s decipherment and offered no Egyptian cards, inscriptions, archaeological chain, or hieroglyphic etymology.

**Label:** **[Legend, first influential printed form 1781].**

His statement about Strength says:

> “Onzième, la force vient au secours de la Prudence, & terrasse le Lion…”

“Eleventh, Strength comes to the aid of Prudence and overthrows the Lion,” which he calls a symbol of uncultivated, savage earth. [Original French transcription](https://www.tarock.info/gebelin.htm).

Etteilla, Paul Christian, Papus, and later occultists elaborated the Egyptian story. The documented fifteenth-century Italian objects and records contradict an Egyptian manufacture or transmission chain.

## Romani or “Gypsy” origin

**[Legend]** Nineteenth-century authors connected tarot with Romani people partly because Europeans wrongly called Roma “Egyptians.” Jean-Alexandre Vaillant and occult popularizers helped spread the idea; Papus’s title *Tarot of the Bohemians* cemented it.

Historical and linguistic research locates Romani origins in northern India, and tarot is documented in Italian courts before any evidence connects Roma with its invention. The slur-laden historical terminology should be quoted only when discussing the myth.

## Cathar origin

**[Modern invention / disputed fringe theory]** Claims that the trumps encode Cathar theology generally infer doctrine from later Marseille pictures and the geography of southern France. No Cathar manuscript, inquisitorial record, card, or continuous transmission identifies tarot with Cathar teaching.

The chronology is unfavorable: standard tarot is first documented in fifteenth-century northern Italy, long after the principal Cathar communities were destroyed.

## Templar origin

**[Modern legend]** The claim that Templars preserved tarot symbolism after 1307 lacks cards, rules, manuscripts, or testimony linking the order to tarot. Similarities between generic medieval symbols—towers, crosses, virtues, kings—do not establish descent.

## “Ancient Kabbalistic tarot”

**[Esoteric attribution presented as history]** No medieval Jewish source maps the 22 tarot trumps onto Hebrew letters. The first influential printed proposal is Comte de Mellet’s essay in 1781. Lévi, Papus, the Golden Dawn, Waite, Case, and Crowley created mutually conflicting maps.

The disagreement itself is evidence against one universally transmitted ancient correspondence:

- Mellet reverses/counts the sequence in his own fashion.
- Lévi/Papus place XI with Kaph.
- Golden Dawn moves Strength to VIII and assigns Teth.
- Crowley restores XI but keeps Teth.

## Hercules versus Samson

**[Disputed identification]**

- Morgan card: Hercules is strongly supported by club and lion.
- Budapest: Hercules or Samson; evidence insufficient.
- Woman and column: Fortitude borrowing Samson’s attribute, not “Samson disguised as a woman.”
- Woman and lion: standard virtue iconography with possible Hercules/Samson resonance.

Farley and other writers correctly warn that the Nemean lion was not the exclusive or universal emblem of Fortitude. [Farley scan](https://e-isomero.com/pdf/book/1611846398.pdf).

## Opening or closing the lion’s jaws

**[Unresolved iconographic ambiguity]** Marseille images do not unequivocally show motion. Waite explicitly says “closing.” Wirth says she holds them apart. Modern readers alternate between opening voice/creative expression and restraining passion.

This is not merely a mistranslation; it is a genuine ambiguity in the static image.

## The “lemniscate hat”

**[Later esoteric reinterpretation]** Marseille Strength wears a broad looped hat. Waite–Smith turns the resemblance into a separate floating infinity sign. Claims that the original cardmaker intentionally encoded the mathematical symbol ∞ are unsupported.

## Why Strength and Justice changed places

**[Documented occult rearrangement; motive reconstructed with high confidence]** The Golden Dawn required Teth/Leo and Lamed/Libra to follow its Hebrew-astrological sequence. Hence Strength became VIII and Justice XI.

Waite’s statement that he changed them “for reasons which satisfy myself” conceals the initiatory correspondence. The change was not a correction derived from a newly discovered medieval pack.

## Rosenwald’s double VIII

**[Possible misprint]** If both Justice and Fortitude carry VIII, at least one numeral is erroneous or the engraving was never brought to a final cuttable state. Later occultists sometimes cite it as ancient support for Strength VIII, but an isolated production anomaly does not demonstrate the Golden Dawn scheme.

## “Tarot de Marseille” as a medieval name

**[Historical correction]** The label *Tarot de Marseille* is later than the medieval invention of tarot. The pattern developed from Lombard and French cardmaking; “Marseille” should not be projected onto all fifteenth-century cards.

## Camoin–Jodorowsky as restoration

**[Disputed modern claim]** The deck is a learned synthetic redesign. Camoin acknowledges mixing historical types, changing the palette, and adding symbols not found in surviving old Marseille decks. It is therefore valuable evidence for late-twentieth-century esoteric Marseille practice, not direct evidence of a pristine medieval original.

## Occult tarot versus game tarot

Dummett’s correction is not that tarot “can only be a game” or that later spiritual uses are illegitimate. His historical conclusion is narrower:

- the pack arose for games;
- occult interpretation appears centuries later;
- later occult systems are historically creative constructions.

In his response on tarot origins, Dummett notes that the Marseille pattern descends from a late-fifteenth-century Milanese type but acquired features in France, and that Egyptian etymologies were proposed without hieroglyphic evidence. [Dummett, “Origins of Tarot”](https://www.nybooks.com/articles/1981/05/14/origins-of-tarot/).

# Open questions

1. **Exact birthplace of the standard tarot:** Milan, Ferrara, and Bologna all have early evidence. No surviving document says “the game was invented here on this date.”

2. **Exact date of the Cary–Yale Fortitude:** stylistic and patronage dates overlap several decades.

3. **Identity of individual Visconti artists:** workshop attributions to Bembo and others remain contested; the Morgan replacement is materially later than the core pack.

4. **Hero of the Budapest card:** Hercules and Samson are both plausible.

5. **Immediate source of the French woman-and-lion pattern:** Lombard transmission is likely, but the missing chain of printed packs around 1500 prevents proof of direct copying.

6. **Whether the woman opens or closes the lion’s mouth:** early cards supply no text explaining the action.

7. **Whether the looped Marseille hat carried a pre-occult symbolic meaning:** possible, but no cardmaker’s explanation survives.

8. **Original Golden Dawn drawings:** surviving instructions and later reconstructions do not always preserve a single finished prototype.

9. **Etteilla equivalence:** Grand Etteilla systems require edition-specific mapping; a universal “Etteilla Strength” is not recoverable without collapsing distinct versions.

10. **Exact Smith/Waite division of authorship:** Waite supplied doctrine and constraints; Smith’s visual agency was substantial, but card-by-card instructions do not survive completely.

11. **Modern literary and architectural quotations:** many generic woman/lion images resemble Strength without proving dependence on a tarot card.
