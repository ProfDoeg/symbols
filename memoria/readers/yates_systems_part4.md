## 1. The "London places" of the *Cena de le ceneri* (the journey to the Ash Wednesday Supper)

- **Name.** Yates does not give it a formal name; she calls the journey "something in the nature of an occult memory system" and speaks of Bruno "using 'London places'". Alternative: the topographical journey of the *Cena*.
- **Author, date, source.** Giordano Bruno, *La cena de le ceneri*, London, 1584 (Dialoghi italiani, ed. Aquilecchia, pp. 26 ff., 55–6, 63). Yates cites Bruno's own memory-book remark "To the last of the Roman places you may add the first of the Parisian places" (her p. 247) as the licence for reading it this way.
- **Principle.** The places are real streets and waterways of Elizabethan London — the French embassy in Butcher Row, the Strand, the Thames, Charing Cross ("the pyramid near the mansion where three streets meet"), a house near Whitehall — traversed in order from the embassy to the Supper. On these places Bruno "remembers" the themes of the debate on the Copernican Sun; the interlocutors (the Nolan, Florio, Gwinne, Greville, the pedants Torquato and Nundinio) are the characters who populate the places. Order is the order of the journey itself, and the invocation to Mnemosyne "hidden beneath the thirty seals" at the start marks it as issuing from the occult memory of *Shadows* and *Seals*.
- **Where.** Chapter XIV, pp. 309–313.
- **Quotations.**
  - "The journey is something in the nature of an occult memory system through which Bruno remembers the themes of the debate at the 'Supper'." (pp. 310–11)
  - "In the Cena de le ceneri, he is using 'London places', the Strand, Charing Cross, the Thames, the French embassy, a house in Whitehall, on which to remember the themes of a debate about the Sun". (p. 311)
  - "We can thus see in the Cena how the art of memory could as it were develop into literature; how the streets of memory places could become populated with characters, could become the backcloth for a drama." (p. 312)
- **Figures/plates.** None for this entry.
- **Related.** Descends from Bruno's *Shadows* and *Seals* (the invocation to "Mnemosyne… hidden beneath the thirty seals"); compared to Fra Agostino del Riccio's Florentine places for the *Summa* (her pp. 245–6). Yates flags "the influence of the art of memory on literature" as an untouched subject; she also offers an allegorical reading (the leaking boat as the Church, the walled lane as the convent).

## 2. The love-emblems of the *De gli eroici furori* as memory images

- **Name.** Not named as a system; Yates treats the roughly fifty emblems as "traces of the memory methods in a literary work". Alternative: the emblematic conceits (her 1943 JWCI article title).
- **Author, date, source.** Giordano Bruno, *De gli eroici furori*, London, 1585, dedicated to Philip Sidney (Dialoghi italiani, p. 1091 for the "contractions" reference).
- **Principle.** The work is patterned as a succession of about fifty emblems — Petrarchan conceits of eyes, stars, Cupid's arrows, and impresa shields — each described in a poem and expounded in a commentary. Yates reads them, in light of the memory works' insistence that magic images be "charged with affects, and particularly with the affect of love", as memory images carrying the "intentions" of the soul; the series culminates in a vision of Circe and in a great figure of Amphitrite "like some great memory statue". She explicitly does not call it a memory system.
- **Where.** Chapter XIV, pp. 313–314.
- **Quotations.**
  - "we begin to see the love emblems of the Eroici furori in a new context, not, of course, as a memory system, but as traces of the memory methods in a literary work." (p. 314)
  - "Did the persistent tradition which associated Petrarch with memory include some view of the conceits as memory images?" (p. 314)
  - "there appears a vast vision of Amphitrite, embodying like some great memory statue, the enthusiast's imaginative grasp of the monas or the One." (p. 314)
- **Figures/plates.** None.
- **Related.** Connected by Yates to the "contractions" of the *Seal of Seals* and to the affect-charged images of *Shadows*/*Seals*; the Circe ending ties it to the *Cantus Circaeus*.

## 3. The forty-eight constellations of the *Spaccio della bestia trionfante* (Hyginus's order occultised into a Seal)

- **Name.** Yates: the "celestial memory system" underlying the *Spaccio*; a Romberch text-book system "'occultised' … into a 'Seal' of his own". Alternative: the "Scepsian" memory system of the *Spaccio*.
- **Author, date, source.** Giordano Bruno, *Spaccio della bestia trionfante*, London, 1585, dedicated to Sidney (Dialoghi italiani, pp. 561 ff., 623–4; trans. Imerti, *The Expulsion of the Triumphant Beast*, pp. 69 ff., 124–5). Source of the order: Hyginus, *Fabularum liber*, recommended as a memory order by Romberch, *Congestorium artificiose memorie*, p. 25 recto (her pp. 116–17).
- **Principle.** The places are the forty-eight constellations of the sky in Hyginus's order — northern constellations, zodiac, southern constellations — a fixed and easily memorised sequence. On each constellation a vice is expelled and a virtue mounts, so the whole is a sermon on virtues and vices memorised on the heavens. The images are the reforming gods (Jupiter, Juno, Saturn, Mars, Mercury, Minerva, Apollo with Circe, Medea, Aesculapius, Diana, Venus and Cupid, Ceres, Neptune, Thetis, Momus, Isis), "perceived inwardly in the soul" as statues or pictures, i.e. magically animated memory statues in the manner of *Statues* and *Images*; the ethical reform is tied to the good and bad sides of planetary influence.
- **Where.** Chapter XIV, pp. 314–319.
- **Quotations.**
  - "Bruno uses the order of the constellations as the ground plan of his sermon on virtues and vices." (p. 314)
  - "he has taken a system from the memory text-books, the use of Hyginus on the order of the constellations as a memory order, and has 'occultised' it into a 'Seal' of his own." (p. 317)
  - "These figures perceived inwardly in the soul are said to have the appearance of statues or pictures. We are in the realms of the occult memory systems based on magically animated 'statues' as memory images." (p. 316)
- **Figures/plates.** None in this chapter.
- **Related.** Descends from Romberch's Hyginus advice and from the mediaeval friars' virtue-and-vice preaching (Yates recalls Aquinas's use of "Tullius" on the virtues); its speakers are related to the twelve principles of *Images* (her G.B. and H.T., pp. 326 ff.); the "planetary" oratory is likened to Camillo's Theatre. Yates ties it to Metrodorus of Scepsis and the zodiac, and suggests Shakespeare may have met this use of the zodiac in Greville's circle (p. 319).

## 4. Fludd's introductory memory picture: five loci with one main image (the "eye of imagination")

- **Name.** The first page of Fludd's *Ars memoriae* ("the science of spiritual memorising which is vulgarly called Ars Memoriae"). No alternative name.
- **Author, date, source.** Robert Fludd, *Utriusque Cosmi Maioris scilicet et Minoris… Historia*, Tomus Secundus (De Microcosmi Historia), sectio 2 (Technica Microcosmi Historia), Oppenheim, De Bry, 1619, pp. 48 ff.
- **Principle.** A man with a large "eye of imagination" in the fore part of his head stands beside five memory loci, each holding an image: an obelisk (the main image), the Tower of Babel, Tobias and the Angel, a ship, and the Last Judgment with the damned entering Hell's mouth. It illustrates two of Fludd's constant principles: five as the number of a group of memory places, and one main image in each memory room. The five images are never explained in the text; Yates ventures and then withdraws an allegorical reading (obelisk = Egyptian "inner writing", Babel = confusion overcome, angel = religious safety).
- **Where.** Chapter XV, pp. 326–327 (context of the "technical history of the microcosm", where Ars Memoriae sits between Geomancy and Genethliology, p. 326).
- **Quotations.**
  - "Five is Fludd's favourite number for a group of memory places, as will appear later, and the diagram also illustrates his principle of having one main image in a memory room." (p. 326)
  - "the Last Judgment with the damned entering the mouth of Hell—an interesting relic in this very late Renaissance system of the mediaeval virtue of remembering Hell by the artificial memory." (p. 326)
  - "These five images are nowhere explained or referred to in the following text." (p. 326)
- **Figures/plates.** Pl. 15, "First page of the Ars memoriae in Robert Fludd's Utriusque Cosmi… Historia, Tomus Secundus, Oppenheim, 1619".
- **Related.** Adumbrates the five doors of the memory theatre (entry 9); Yates says the "theatre or stage with its five doors… is the leading motif of the whole system. We can see it adumbrated in the introductory illustration" (p. 335). The Hell image links back to the mediaeval artificial memory of Part I.

## 5. The *ars rotunda* (Fludd's "round art")

- **Name.** *Ars rotunda*, "round art". Alternative: the "natural" art (Fludd's own term for it).
- **Author, date, source.** Robert Fludd, *Utriusque Cosmi… Historia*, II, 2, pp. 50–51, 54 (the chapter distinguishing the two arts; the "common place" of the round art at p. 54).
- **Principle.** The fantasy operates "through ideas", meaning anything not composed of the four elements: spirits, shadows (*umbrae*), souls, angels, demons, the effigies of the stars, images of gods and goddesses "to whom celestial powers are attributed", and virtues and vices "made into shadows, which were also to be held as demons". Its common place is the ethereal world, "the celestial orbs numbered from the eighth sphere and ending in the sphere of the moon". It requires the assistance of daemonic powers or of the Holy Spirit, "the fantasy should concur in the metaphysical act"; it is "natural" because naturally adapted to the microcosm, and "infinitely the superior" of the two arts, though harder.
- **Where.** Chapter XV, pp. 327–328, 329–330.
- **Quotations.**
  - "The 'round art', then, uses magicised or talismanic images, effigies of the stars; 'statues' of gods and goddesses animated with celestial influences; images of virtues and vices, as in the old mediaeval art, but now thought of as containing 'demonic' or magical power." (p. 327)
  - "Fludd is working at a classification of images into potent and less potent such as was Bruno's constant preoccupation." (p. 327)
  - "For the round art is 'natural' using 'natural' places and is naturally adapted to the microcosm." (p. 328)
- **Figures/plates.** Pl. 16, "The Zodiac", the basic diagram for the *ars rotunda* (see entry 7). Yates also compares the engraved title-page of Tomus Primus, with Time pulling the rope round macrocosm and microcosm (p. 330 n. 24).
- **Related.** Yates: Bruno "had also laid down the distinction between what Fludd calls the 'round' and the 'square' arts in his Seals" (her p. 249), thirty-six years earlier. The "shadows" (*umbrae*) recall *De umbris idearum*. The magical art Fludd heard of at Toulouse, "with the 'ring of Solomon'", sounds to Yates like the *ars notoria*, possibly Jean Belot's (p. 328 n. 17; see appendix).

## 6. The *ars quadrata* (Fludd's "square art") and the polemic against fictitious places

- **Name.** *Ars quadrata*, "square art". Alternative: the "artificial" art (Fludd's term).
- **Author, date, source.** Robert Fludd, *Utriusque Cosmi… Historia*, II, 2, pp. 50–52 (the chapter against fictitious places, pp. 51–2, opening "Some who are versed in this art wish to place their square art in palaces fabricated or erected by invention of the imagination…").
- **Principle.** Uses images of corporeal things — men, animals, inanimate objects — the men and animals "active, engaged in actions of some kind", i.e. the *imagines agentes* of *Ad Herennium*; and "square" presumably because it uses buildings or rooms as places. Fludd insists these must be *real* places in real buildings, never the "fictitious" places which *Ad Herennium* had allowed; fictitious buildings "confuse memory and add to its task". These two arts, round and square, are for Fludd the only two possible; memory can be improved only by medicaments, by the round art, or by the square art.
- **Where.** Chapter XV, pp. 327–329.
- **Quotations.**
  - "The 'square art' sounds like the ordinary art of memory, using the active images of Ad Herennium and perhaps 'square' because using buildings or rooms as places." (p. 327)
  - "Fludd is very much against the use of 'fictitious' buildings in the square art. These confuse memory and add to its task. One must always use real places in real buildings." (p. 328)
  - "if true to these strongly held views against fictitious places, the buildings which Fludd will use in his memory system will be 'real' buildings." (p. 329)
- **Figures/plates.** None specific.
- **Related.** The classical real/fictitious distinction from *Ad Herennium*; the square art is the "architectural" side of the Theatre system (entry 7) and is what Fludd's "theatres" belong to. The insistence on real places is the hinge of Chapter XVI's argument that the theatre engraving shows the Globe (entry 16).

## 7. Fludd's Theatre memory system (theatres in the zodiac; the round and square arts combined)

- **Name.** Yates: "the Theatre memory system", "Fludd's memory system", "a memory 'Seal'"; Fludd never uses the word Seal. Alternative: "the last of the Seals of Memory", "the last great monument of Renaissance memory".
- **Author, date, source.** Robert Fludd, *Utriusque Cosmi… Historia*, Tomus Secundus, sectio 2, pp. 54 ff. (common place of the round art, p. 54; description of the theatres, p. 55; relation of planets to signs, "principle ideas", p. 62; doors and columns, p. 63; images, pp. 65, 67). Published Oppenheim, De Bry, 1619, the volume dedicated to God, following the 1617 volume dedicated to James I.
- **Principle.** The great "common place" is the heavens: the eighth sphere or zodiac marked with its signs, enclosing seven circles for the planetary spheres and a central circle for the elements — "a 'natural' order of memory places based on the zodiac, and also a temporal order through the movement of the spheres". Into this round common place are set "theatres" (stages, not whole playhouses), buildings containing memory loci after the manner of the square art, so that their images are "astrally activated by being organically related to the stars". On the diagram two tiny theatres flank Aries; Yates infers an eastern and a western theatre for each sign, twenty-four in all. Within each theatre, order is kept by five doors and five columns of distinct shapes and colours (entry 9); the celestial images are the "principal ideas" (entry 11) and the images in the theatres the "less principal images" (entry 12). The diagram of the heavens and the engraving of the theatre face one another so that, closed, "the heavens cover the theatre".
- **Where.** Chapter XV, pp. 329–335 (summary pp. 335, 341); resumed Chapter XVI, pp. 346–347, 366.
- **Quotations.**
  - "This is a combination of the round and the square. Based on the round heavens, the zodiac and the spheres of the planets, it uses in combination with these, buildings which are to be placed in the heavens". (p. 329)
  - "The heavens work together with the theatres, and the theatres are in the heavens. The 'round' and the 'square' art are united to form a memory 'Seal', or an occult memory system of extreme complexity." (p. 333)
  - "Is it pure madness to place twenty-four memory theatres in the zodiac? Or is it a madness potentially leading to method? Or is such a system the Seal or secret code of a Hermetic sect or society?" (p. 341)
- **Figures/plates.** Pl. 16, "The Zodiac" (the eighth sphere with signs, seven planetary circles, elements at centre, two small theatres beside Aries, "Oriens" at top, "Occidens" at bottom); Pl. 17, "The Theatre" (the *Theatrum Orbi* engraving); Pl. 18a, b (secondary theatres); Pl. 15 (introductory page).
- **Related.** Yates's closest comparison is Bruno's *Images* (atria, cubicles and "fields" of images affiliated to a round celestial system); Fludd uses Bruno's terms "cubicles" and "fields" but not Lullism nor "thirty". The two tiny theatres by Aries are "never illustrated again nor referred to in the text" — one of the "unexplained lacunae" of an occult system. Camillo's Theatre "opened our series of Renaissance memory systems; Fludd's Theatre will close it" (p. 321). Possible immediate source: Willis's *Mnemonica* (entry 14). Chapter XVI reads the theatre as the Globe (entry 16). Fludd's whole effort is described as "re-creating the macrocosm-microcosm relationship" in the memory of the microcosm (p. 339).

## 8. The eastern and western theatres (day and night theatres in the heavens)

- **Name.** "The description of the eastern and western theatres" (Fludd's chapter heading). Alternative: the day and night theatres.
- **Author, date, source.** Fludd, *Utriusque Cosmi… Historia*, II, 2, p. 55, the chapter containing the theatre engraving, introduced by "I call a theatre (a place in which) all actions of words, of sentences, of particulars of a speech or of subjects are shown, as in a public theatre in which comedies and tragedies are acted."
- **Principle.** There are two theatres, identical in plan but different in colour: the eastern theatre "light, bright and shining", holding actions belonging to the day; the western "dark, black and obscure", belonging to the night. Both are placed in the heavens and refer, Yates presumes, to the day and night "houses" of the planets. She thinks a pair is meant for each sign of the zodiac, as the two little stages flank Aries on the plan, giving twenty-four identical memory rooms; the day/night pairing "introduce[s] time into a system which is attached to the revolution of the heavens".
- **Where.** Chapter XV, pp. 330–331.
- **Quotations.**
  - "The eastern theatre is to be light, bright and shining, since it will hold actions belonging to the day. The western theatre will be dark, black and obscure, belonging to the night." (p. 331)
  - "If he intends that two such 'theatres' are to be placed with each sign, then the 'theatre' which he illustrates would be one of twenty-four identical memory rooms." (p. 331)
  - "But we are in the realms of occult memory and it is not easy to follow how these theatres in the heavens are supposed to work." (p. 331)
- **Figures/plates.** Pl. 16 (two stages beside Aries), Pl. 17.
- **Related.** Yates suggests "a humble origin" in Willis's rule that "things charged in Memory by day, are to be deposited at least before sleep; things charged by night… immediately after sleep" (p. 338; entry 14). The inscription THEATRUM ORBI, dative, is read by Yates (with diffidence) as "one of the theatres… to be placed with or in the world" (p. 332).

## 9. The five doors and five columns (the *cinque portae*, with their shapes and colours)

- **Name.** Yates: "the five doors and five columns", "the cinque portae"; Fludd speaks of "fields" (*prata*) opposite five "feigned" columns. No other name.
- **Author, date, source.** Fludd, *Utriusque Cosmi… Historia*, II, 2, pp. 55 ("Each of the theatres will have five doors distinct from one another and about equidistant") and 63 (the Latin on the columns, quoted in n. 28).
- **Principle.** Each theatre has five entrances, about equidistant, which serve as five memory loci; opposite them are to be "feigned" five columns, distinguished by shape and colour — circular at the two extremes, square for the two intermediate ones, hexagonal in the middle — whose bases are shown in the foreground of the engraving. The doors are remembered as of different colours: first white, second red, third green, fourth blue, fifth black, the columns matching the colours of the doors opposite. This set of ten places, doors and columns, in every theatre is where things and words are remembered. Yates notes the doors spaced for suitable places and the columns varied "so that they may not be too much alike", as in *Ad Herennium*, while the colour rule is a memory-treatise addition; the geometrical forms on the battlemented terrace may indicate the correspondences.
- **Where.** Chapter XV, pp. 332–333; used as evidence in Chapter XVI, pp. 346–348, 355.
- **Quotations.**
  - "The first door will be white, the second red, the third green, the fourth blue, the fifth black." (p. 332)
  - "With this set of ten places, five doors and five columns, in all the 'theatres', Fludd is proposing to remember things and words in his magical memory system." (p. 333)
  - "The notion of remembering memory loci as of different colours as an additional help for distinguishing between them is not in Ad Herennium but is often advised in the memory treatises." (p. 333)
- **Figures/plates.** Pl. 17 (five entrances on the frons scaenae — three below, two on the terrace level — and five column bases: round, square, hexagon, square, round).
- **Related.** *Ad Herennium* rules for places; the colour rule shared with Willis (entry 14). In Chapter XVI the five entrances resolve Chambers's problem of the five classical entrances on a multilevel stage, and the column shapes are read as the hexagon, circle and square of the Globe's plan (entry 16). The Medea/Circe image sets are assigned door by door (entry 12).

## 10. The two subsidiary theatres (the "figure of a true theatre")

- **Name.** "Secondary Theatre" (plate caption); Yates: "subsidiary theatres", "two other 'theatres'". Fludd labels one "Sequitur figura vera theatri".
- **Author, date, source.** Fludd, *Utriusque Cosmi… Historia*, II, 2, p. 64.
- **Principle.** Two further memory rooms, not multilevel stages but rooms with one wall left out so that the spectator looks in; one has three doors, the other five, and the five-door one has a matching system of columns indicated by their bases, working with the doors as in the main theatres. Their battlements match those on the main theatre's terrace, which is how they "connect with the main theatres and through them with the heavens". Their walls are of different materials — plain stone blocks in one, jointed timbers in the other, against the main theatre's "rustic work" — following the treatise advice that places are better remembered if of different materials (Romberch; Bruno's *Seals*).
- **Where.** Chapter XV, p. 333; Chapter XVI, pp. 353–354.
- **Quotations.**
  - "These are not multilevel stages like the main theatres but more like rooms with one wall left out so that the spectator looks into them." (p. 333)
  - "the memory treatises often advise that memory places are better remembered if they are remembered as made of different materials." (p. 353)
  - "The subsidiary theatres are therefore, like the main one, not only magic memory theatres but reflections of something 'real' or true seen at the Globe." (p. 353)
- **Figures/plates.** Pl. 18a and 18b, "Secondary Theatre", from Fludd's *Ars memoriae*.
- **Related.** In Chapter XVI Yates reads them as moveable scenic constructions or screens with entrances (the Capulet orchard, the Friar's cell, the rival camps of *Richard III*), and, in a footnote, as a survival of the mediaeval "mansions" (p. 362 n. 44). Compare Willis's one-level repositories (entry 14).

## 11. The images of the "principal ideas" (the planets through the signs of the zodiac)

- **Name.** Fludd's chapter "on the order of the principle [sic] ideas through the spheres of the planets"; Yates: "the celestial or magically operative images to be used in the 'round' part of the system". No alternative name.
- **Author, date, source.** Fludd, *Utriusque Cosmi… Historia*, II, 2, p. 62 (the chapter on the relation of the planets to the signs of the zodiac, immediately preceding the chapter on the doors and columns).
- **Principle.** The system "works through being hitched to the stars, or rather to the 'principle ideas'". Fludd analyses the progression of Saturn through the zodiac, "giving different images of Saturn in different signs, and says that the same may be done with other planets"; these are the talismanic images which control the round side of the system. On the plan of the heavens only the signs of the zodiac and the characters of the planets appear, not images of decans, planets, or houses.
- **Where.** Chapter XV, pp. 333–334.
- **Quotations.**
  - "For his basic or celestial images he used talismanic or magic images such as Bruno uses on the central wheel of Shadows." (p. 334)
  - "he analyses the progression of Saturn through the zodiac, giving different images of Saturn in different signs, and says that the same may be done with other planets." (p. 334)
  - "This chapter gives the celestial basis of the system; and it is immediately followed by the chapter on the five doors and five columns in the memory theatres." (p. 333)
- **Figures/plates.** Pl. 16 (signs and planetary characters only).
- **Related.** Bruno's central wheel in *Shadows* (Pl. 11, her chapter IX); the decan and planet images of the Hermetic tradition; Fludd's "principal ideas" are the equivalent of the "causes" whose impressed images organise subsidiary images, which Yates later connects to Descartes's proposal (entry 19).

## 12. The "less principal images" in the theatres (the Jason, Medea and Circe sets; the alphabetical lists)

- **Name.** Fludd's chapter on "less principal images"; Yates: "the images to be used in the 'square' part of the art". No alternative name.
- **Author, date, source.** Fludd, *Utriusque Cosmi… Historia*, II, 2, pp. 65 (sets of five images; Medea on the white and red doors), 67 (a second set of five Medea images; Circe images), 68 (Lull as a memory image representing alchemy).
- **Principle.** Images placed on the doors and columns of the theatres, formed by the *Ad Herennium* rules for striking images, which Fludd quotes, "but as it were magicised in this magical system". They come in sets of five to match the five doors: Jason holding the golden fleece, Medea, Paris, Daphne, Phoebus; another set puts Medea collecting magic herbs on the white door, Medea killing her brother on the red door, and Medea in other aspects on the other three; a further set of five Medea images and some Circe images. Fludd also gives alphabetical lists of mythological figures and of virtues and vices in the manner of Romberch and Rossellius.
- **Where.** Chapter XV, p. 334.
- **Quotations.**
  - "They are to be formed in accordance with the rules for striking images in Ad Herennium, from which Fludd quotes, but as it were magicised in this magical system." (p. 334)
  - "Another set is Medea collecting magic herbs, to be put on the white door; Medea killing her brother on the red door; and Medea in other aspects on the other three doors." (p. 334)
  - "The magic of these sorceresses must have been very helpful to the system." (p. 334)
- **Figures/plates.** None reproduced.
- **Related.** *Ad Herennium* imagines agentes; Romberch's and Rossellius's alphabetical lists; the Circe images point back to Bruno's *Cantus Circaeus*; the virtue and vice lists to mediaeval artificial memory. Lull appears as an image for alchemy, though Fludd does not use Lullism (p. 335 n. 37).

## 13. Fludd's visual alphabets and images for numbers (*De Arithmetica Memoriali*)

- **Name.** "Visual alphabets" and "visual images for numbers". Alternative: *De Arithmetica Memoriali* (the section in Tomus Primus).
- **Author, date, source.** Fludd, *Utriusque Cosmi… Historia*, II, 2 (illustrated specimens of visual alphabets among the memory chapters); and I, 2, pp. 153 ff., the section "De Arithmetica Memoriali", with examples of memory places bearing images for numbers.
- **Principle.** Objects or figures whose shapes recall letters serve as inscriptions or word-memory aids on the images, the "sign manual of the old memory treatises" (Boncompagno, Publicius, Romberch, Rossellius); sets of visual images stand for numbers in the same way. Yates uses their presence to show that Fludd, like Bruno, saw his occult Seal "as still in continuity with the old memory tradition".
- **Where.** Chapter XV, p. 335 and n. 34.
- **Quotations.**
  - "Fludd indeed makes very clear his attachment to the old memory treatise tradition by including illustrated specimens of 'visual alphabets'." (p. 335)
  - "Bruno though he never actually illustrates a visual alphabet, frequently refers to them or describes them in words." (p. 335)
  - "He also gives sets of visual images for numbers, again an old tradition." (p. 335 n. 34)
- **Figures/plates.** None reproduced by Yates.
- **Related.** Her pp. 118 ff. (visual alphabets in Publicius, Romberch, Rossellius) and pp. 250, 294–5 (Bruno's references).

## 14. John Willis's memory "theatres" or "repositories" (*Mnemonica*)

- **Name.** "Theatres", "repositories" (Willis's terms). Alternative: *Mnemonica; sive Ars reminiscendi*.
- **Author, date, source.** John Willis, *Mnemonica; sive Ars Reminiscendi: e puris artis naturaeque fontibus hausta*, London, 1618; partial English translation by the author, *The Art of Memory*, London, 1621 (pp. 58–60 quoted); full translation *Mnemonica: or The Art of Memory*, London, 1661 (pp. 28, 30 quoted). Long extracts in Feinaigle, *The New Art of Memory*, 1813.
- **Principle.** The places are sets of identical "theatres" or "repositories": one-level buildings with the front wall omitted, divided into two halves by a column near the back wall, giving two memory rooms each. Repositories are imagined as of different colours, and every image placed in one must carry something of its colour (a bushel with handles "of pure gold" in the "golden" theatre; mowers whetting scythes "the blades whereof are of gold"). Images are related to one another "in respect of situation" on the same stage. It is a straight mnemotechnic, an "inner shopping list" for when one is "destitute of the aid of Paper, Ink, or Table-Books"; things charged by day are deposited before sleep, things charged by night immediately after sleep.
- **Where.** Chapter XV, pp. 336–338; recalled p. 348.
- **Quotations.**
  - "a memory system formed sets of identical 'theatres' is described. Willis illustrates one of his 'theatres', or 'repositories' as he also calls them". (p. 336; extraction reads "formed sets", probably "formed of sets")
  - "This seems a perfectly rational use of the art as a straight mnemotechnic; it might work very well as an inner shopping list". (p. 338)
  - "Possibly this is what Fludd did to Willis's sets of what he calls 'theatres' as memory rooms; he occultised them into magical activity by affiliating them to the zodiac." (p. 338)
- **Figures/plates.** Fig. 10, "Memory Theatre or Repository. From J. Willis, Mnemonica, 1618".
- **Related.** Possible immediate source of Fludd's theatres, columns and colour-differentiated places (entries 7–9) and of the day/night theatres (entry 8); Yates leaves open whether, as Paepp "detected" an occult undercurrent in Schenkel, "there was more than meets the eye in Willis's Mnemonica". Fludd's enclosed memory room is "perhaps rather like one of Willis's memory theatres" (p. 348).

## 15. Fludd's diagram of the three worlds reflected in the memory of the microcosm (the *mundus imaginabilis*)

- **Name.** Not named; Yates: "one of Fludd's illustrations expresses in visual form the reflection of the three worlds within the mind and memory of the microcosm". This is Fludd's theory of memory rather than a technique, entered because Yates presents it as the psychological basis of the system.
- **Author, date, source.** Fludd, *Utriusque Cosmi… Historia*, II, pp. 205 ff. (discussion of the *mundus imaginabilis*).
- **Principle.** A man takes in sense impressions from the *mundus sensibilis* through the five senses; deals with them within as images or *umbrae* in a *mundus imaginabilis*, which includes reflections of the zodiac and stars (unifying memory's contents on the celestial level); passes to the *mens*, the intellectual world, receiving a vision of the nine celestial hierarchies and the Trinity; and finally to the seat of memory at the back of the head "which receives all three worlds into itself". Fludd thus retains a Christianised, Trinitarian apex and a partial faculty psychology which Bruno abolished.
- **Where.** Chapter XV, pp. 339–340.
- **Quotations.**
  - "Fludd's occult art of memory is an attempt to reproduce or re-create the macrocosm-microcosm relationship by establishing… in the memory of the microcosm the world which he contains, which is the image of the macrocosm, which is the image of God." (p. 339)
  - "Finally the diagram comes to the seat of memory, at the back of the head, which receives all three worlds into itself." (p. 340)
  - "For Bruno there is but one power and one faculty which ranges through all the inner world of apprehension, namely the imaginative power… which passes immediately through the gates of memory and is one with memory." (p. 340)
- **Figures/plates.** The illustration is described but not reproduced in this book (Yates refers to her G.B. and H.T. plates for Fludd's diagrams).
- **Related.** Camillo's Theatre (same Christianised three-worlds outlook, sephiroth and angels beyond the stars); Bruno's rejection of the "metaphysical apex" and of faculty psychology in *Seals* (her pp. 256–7); Campanella's parallel rejection; the tradition reaching Fludd is said to be Dee's rather than Bruno's (p. 340).

## 16. The Globe Theatre as reconstructed from Fludd's *Theatrum Orbi* (the "real place" behind the memory room)

- **Name.** Yates: "the stage of the Globe as revealed by Fludd"; the engraving is the *Theatrum Orbi*. Alternative: "Another Globe Theatre" (Bernheimer's 1958 article title).
- **Author, date, source.** Robert Fludd's engraving (Pl. 17) and text (II, 2, pp. 55, 63–64), read against Richard Bernheimer, "Another Globe Theatre", *Shakespeare Quarterly* IX (1958); the De Witt sketch of the Swan; Chambers, *Elizabethan Stage*; the Fortune contract; Hester Thrale's statement that the Globe was "hexagonal in form without… round within"; Vitruvius V and Palladio's Vitruvian plan in Barbaro's commentary (1556); Dee's Preface to Billingsley's Euclid (1570).
- **Principle.** Because Fludd insists on real, not fictitious, places, the theatre in the engraving is taken to be a real stage in a real "public theatre" — the second Globe, rebuilt 1614 with James I's money. The engraving shows the tiring-house wall from beneath the stage "heavens": three entrances on ground level (the central one with hinged doors opening on an inner room), two on the upper level giving on a battlemented terrace, and a projecting bay window or "chamber" in the centre, the terrace passing behind it. The side walls with their boxes and the five columns are "mnemonic distortions" that crush the stage into an enclosed memory room; removed, two "posts" rise to the painted heavens, which are the zodiac diagram facing the engraving (Pl. 16), so the round art's common place is the real theatre's "heavens". The five column bases (round, square, hexagon, square, round) are read as the geometrical forms of the building — hexagonal without, round within, square for stage and tiring house — and the five entrances as the classical *frons scaenae* transposed to a multilevel stage; a plan is drawn from four Vitruvian triangles inscribed in the circle, with Fortune-contract dimensions (43 ft stage, 86 ft diameter); "Oriens" at the top of the heavens gives a stage at the east end "like the altar in a church".
- **Where.** Chapter XVI, pp. 342–367 (Bernheimer pp. 345–6; the reading of the engraving pp. 346–349; entrances, chamber and terrace pp. 349–352; subsidiary theatres pp. 353–354; column bases and plan pp. 354–359; Dee and Vitruvius pp. 360–363; orientation and levels pp. 364–365; comparison with Camillo p. 367).
- **Quotations.**
  - "The engraving represents that part of the stage of the Globe which would be covered by the stage 'heavens'." (p. 347)
  - "My explanation of these features is that they are distortions of the real stage introduced for mnemonic purposes. Fludd wanted a 'memory room' within which to practise his mnemonics with the five doors and the five columns." (p. 348)
  - "Encouraged by Mrs. Thrale, I believe that Fludd is stating through the shapes of the five column bases the geometrical forms used in the construction of the Globe, namely the hexagon, the circle, and the square." (p. 355)
  - "Camillo distorts the Vitruvian theatre by transferring… the five entrances to its stage to the seven times seven imaginary gates which he erects in the auditorium. Fludd stands with his back to the auditorium and looking towards the stage". (p. 367)
- **Figures/plates.** Pl. 17 (The Theatre / *Theatrum Orbi*); Pl. 16 (The Zodiac, as the stage "heavens"); Pl. 18a, b (secondary theatres, read as moveable battlemented scenic screens with entrances); Pl. 19, the De Witt sketch of the Swan (Utrecht); Pl. 20, "Sketch of the stage of the Globe Theatre based on Fludd" (with columns copied from the "Temple of Music" in Tomus Primus); Fig. 11, "Suggested Plan of the Globe Theatre"; Pl. 9a (Palladio's Vitruvian theatre in Barbaro), referred back to.
- **Related.** Camillo's Theatre as the other distortion of a real (Vitruvian) theatre; the Vitruvian *fabrica mundi* and zodiacal *trigona* (her pp. 170–1); Dee's Preface as the channel of Vitruvius and Alberti to the carpenter James Burbage; the mediaeval multilevel religious stage and "mansions" as the other ancestor. Yates ends by asking whether the twenty-four theatres in the zodiac are "an elaborate casket deliberately contrived to conceal his allusion to the Globe theatre from all but the initiated, of whom we must suppose that James I was one" (p. 366), and answers that occult memory "is not to be entirely accounted for as a cipher".

## 17. Cornelius Gemma's "circular method" (*De arte cyclognomica*)

- **Name.** The "circular method", "the Cyclognomic Art". Alternative: *Ars cyclognomica*.
- **Author, date, source.** Cornelius Gemma, *De arte cyclognomica*, Antwerp, 1569; later surveyed at length in Izquierdo's *Pharus Scientiarum* (1659).
- **Principle.** Yates gives no working description; she characterises it as "a 'circular method' for knowing everything" which is "a compound of Lullism, Hermetism, Cabalism, and the art of memory", offered as an example of the pre-Cartesian use of the word "method" for such procedures. She suggests it may have influenced Bruno, who also calls his procedures a "method".
- **Where.** Chapter XVII, p. 369; p. 379 (via Izquierdo).
- **Quotations.**
  - "there is the 'circular method' for knowing everything described by Cornelius Gemma in his De arte cyclognomica which is a compound of Lullism, Hermetism, Cabalism, and the art of memory." (p. 369)
  - "if anyone ever tries to understand the Cyclognomic Art which may be historically important, Izquierdo might help". (p. 379)
- **Figures/plates.** None.
- **Related.** Bruno's "method" (her p. 294); Izquierdo (entry 24). Yates pairs it with the 1632 Bureau d'Adresse conference on "method" (Cabalist, Lullian, ordinary philosophy) as showing how unsurprising Descartes's title would have seemed (see appendix).

## 18. Francis Bacon's art of memory (prenotions and emblems; the Gorhambury windows)

- **Name.** Bacon's art of memory "built upon two intentions; the one prenotion, the other emblem". Alternative: the reformed or "useful" art of memory.
- **Author, date, source.** Francis Bacon, *Advancement of Learning*, II, xv, 2 (Spedding III, pp. 398–9), also II, x, 2 and II, xvii, 14; *Novum Organum*, II, xxvi (Spedding I, p. 275); *De augmentis scientiarum*, V, v (Spedding I, p. 649); *Partis Instaurationis Secundae Delineatio* (Spedding III, p. 552); *Sylva Sylvarum*, Century X, 956 (Spedding II, p. 659); Aubrey, *Brief Lives* (ed. Dick, p. 14) on Gorhambury.
- **Principle.** "Prenotion" is Bacon's version of the place: it "dischargeth the indefinite seeking" and directs the search "in a narrow compass… that hath congruity with our place of memory"; places may be "a door, a corner, a window", familiar persons, or anything arranged in order — animals, herbs, words, letters, characters, historical personages. "Emblem" is his version of the image: it "reduceth conceits intellectual to images sensible, which strike the memory more" (a sportsman hunting the hare for invention, an apothecary ranging boxes for disposition, and so on). The art is to be turned from "ostentation prodigious" to "business and occasions", above all to memorising particulars of natural history in order so the judgment can work on them — order turning "into something like classification". The Sylva Sylvarum passage ("men are best places"; a man reading Aristotle's Physics for "philosophy") ties the image's power to "force of imagination". Aubrey reports the Gorhambury gallery's painted glass panes of beast, bird and flower as possible "topiques for locall use", one of the few known buildings designed for local memory.
- **Where.** Chapter XVII, pp. 370–373 (and 374 on Lull, 378 on "real characters").
- **Quotations.**
  - "He defines the art as based on 'prenotions' and 'emblems', the Baconian version of places and images". (p. 371)
  - "Here the art of memory is being used for the investigation of natural science, and its principles of order and arrangement are turning into something like classification." (p. 372)
  - "Though he is exploring the subject scientifically, Bacon is profoundly imbued with the classical belief that the mnemonic image has power through stirring the imagination, and he connects this with 'force of imagination' tricks." (p. 373)
- **Figures/plates.** None.
- **Related.** Straight out of the mnemonic text-books and the Thomist view that intellectual things are remembered through sensible ones; shows Bacon "though influenced by Ramism, was not a Ramist". His attack on the microcosm doctrine "fantastically strained by Paracelsus" targets "Metrodorian" systems such as Fludd's (entry 7). His demand for "real characters" seeds the universal-language movement (entry 23) and his "inventive logic" is taken up by Leibniz (entry 26). Rossi (*Clavis universalis*) is Yates's guide throughout.

## 19. Descartes's reform of the art of memory (reduction of images to their causes; interdependent images), prompted by Schenkel

- **Name.** Descartes: "the true art of memory". Alternative: the note "on reading through Schenkel's profitable trifles".
- **Author, date, source.** René Descartes, *Cogitationes privatae* (1619–21), Œuvres, ed. Adam and Tannery, X, p. 230; fragments of the *Studium bonae mentis* (c. 1620) in Baillet's *Life* (Œuvres X, pp. 200–1); *Discours de la méthode*, part II (on Lull); letter to Beeckman, March 1619 (Œuvres X, pp. 156–7). The trigger is Lambert Schenkel's *De arte memoria* (Schenkel treated by Yates at her pp. 299–302, outside this stretch).
- **Principle.** Two proposals. First, "the reduction of things to their causes": since all can be reduced to one, one need not remember all the sciences; "when one understands the causes all vanished images can easily be found again in the brain through the impression of the cause", and images should be "formed in dependence on one another" — the "key to the whole mystery" which Schenkel omits, whose art fills the space "with too many things and not in the right order". Second, from unconnected images to compose "new images common to them all", or one image referring to all the others (the fifth to the first by a spear thrown on the ground, the middle by a ladder, the second by an arrow). Descartes otherwise neglected local memory as "corporeal memory" and "outside of us" against an "intellectual memory" incapable of increase or decrease.
- **Where.** Chapter XVII, pp. 373–375.
- **Quotations.**
  - "Curiously enough, Descartes's suggested reform of memory is nearer to 'occult' principles than Bacon's, for occult memory does reduce all things to their supposed causes whose images when impressed on memory are believed to organise the subsidiary images." (p. 374)
  - "his brilliant new idea of organising memory on causes sounds curiously like a rationalisation of occult memory. His other notions about forming connected images are far from new and can be found in some form in nearly every text-book." (p. 374)
  - "It seems unlikely that Descartes made much use of local memory which… he neglected to practise much in his retreat and which he regarded as 'corporeal memory' and 'outside of us'". (p. 374)
- **Figures/plates.** None.
- **Related.** Schenkel (her pp. 299–302) and Paepp's "detecting" of Schenkel (her p. 301): "Had Descartes consulted Paepp… he would have known of this." The causal images correspond to Fludd's "principal ideas" and Bruno's central wheel. Descartes's method is "not an ars brevis of Lull, but a new science… concerning quantity" (p. 375); Rossi suggests the memory principles of order influenced him as they did Bacon.

## 20. The Art of Ramon Lull as a seventeenth-century "method" (Bacon's and Descartes's rejection; what it shared with them)

- **Name.** The Lullian Art, "the method of Ramon Lull", "the art which bears his name"; the *ars brevis*.
- **Author, date, source.** Ramon Lull's Art as judged in Bacon, *Advancement*, II, xvii, 14 ("a method of imposture… to make a show of learning who have it not"); Descartes, *Discours de la méthode*, II ("to speak without judgment of those things of which one is ignorant"); the 1632 Bureau d'Adresse conference ("method of Ramon Lull, based on divine attributes").
- **Principle.** Yates restates what Lullism offered the century: a universal art or method "which, because based on reality, could be applied for the solution of all problems"; "a kind of geometrical logic, with its squares and triangles and its revolving combinatory wheels"; and "a notation of letters to express the concepts with which it was dealing". Its combinatory systems are based on the Divine Names or attributes. Both the founder of the inductive method and the founder of analytical geometry reject it, yet it shares their aims.
- **Where.** Chapter XVII, pp. 374–375; developed through Alsted, Izquierdo, Kircher and Leibniz, pp. 375–376, 379, 381–383.
- **Quotations.**
  - "Nevertheless the Art of Ramon Lull had this in common with the aims of Bacon and Descartes. It promised to provide a universal art or method which, because based on reality, could be applied for the solution of all problems." (p. 375)
  - "These two great mediaeval arts, which the Renaissance had tried to combine, turn into methods in the seventeenth century and play their part in the methodological revolution." (p. 370)
  - "The occultist tide was receding and in the changed atmosphere the search turns in the direction of rational method." (p. 375)
- **Figures/plates.** None in this chapter.
- **Related.** Her Chapter VIII on Lullism as an art of memory; the pseudo-Lullian *De auditu kabbalistico* (her pp. 189, 197, 209) which may have propagated the word "method"; Alsted (entry 21), Izquierdo and Kircher (entry 24), Leibniz's *De arte combinatoria* (entry 26).

## 21. Alsted's *Systema mnemonicum* and reformed Lullism (Lullian circles as memory places)

- **Name.** *Systema mnemonicum duplex*; *Clavis artis Lullianae*. Alternative: the Alstedian "reformed version" of Brunian procedures.
- **Author, date, source.** Johann-Heinrich Alsted (1588–1638), *Systema mnemonicum duplex… in quo artis memorativae praecepta plene et methodice traduntur*, Frankfort, 1610 (p. 5 on *De auditu kabbalistico*); *Clavis artis Lullianae*, Strasburg, 1633 (preface dated 1609, naming Agrippa and Bruno as corrupters); *Methodus admirandorum mathematicorum*, Herborn, 1623; he also published Bruno's *Artificium perorandi* (Frankfort, 1612).
- **Principle.** Alsted, "encyclopaedist, Lullist, Cabalist, Ramist", defines method as "the mnemonic instrument which proceeds from generals to specials" and "calls the Lullian circles places corresponding to the places of the art of memory", assimilating Lullism to Cabalism through the pseudo-Lullian *De auditu kabbalistico* and describing Lull as "a mathematician and Cabalist". He fuses "every kind of method in the search for a universal key" while wishing to free Lullism from "idle dreams and fancies" and return to Lavinheta's purer doctrine.
- **Where.** Chapter XVII, pp. 375–377; cited by Leibniz as a mnemonic authority on "rules for places", p. 381.
- **Quotations.**
  - "He defines method as the mnemonic instrument which proceeds from generals to specials (a definition of course, also influenced by Ramism) and he calls the Lullian circles places corresponding to the places of the art of memory." (p. 376)
  - "There seems to be a movement going on in the Alstedian circle, in which Bruno is remembered, towards a reformed version of those procedures which Bruno had so extravagantly stimulated on a wildly Hermetic plane." (p. 376)
  - "But it would require a whole book to investigate the vast output of Alsted." (p. 377)
- **Figures/plates.** None.
- **Related.** Bruno's German seeds "germinated but were bringing forth fruits more suited to the new age"; Comenius was Alsted's pupil (entry 22); Leibniz names Alsted among modern Lullists in the *De arte combinatoria* and with Frey as mnemonic authors (entries 25–26).

## 22. Comenius's *Orbis pictus* (the City of the Sun turned into a picture primer)

- **Name.** *Orbis sensualium pictus*, "the picture book of Comenius". Alternative: the "new visual education".
- **Author, date, source.** J. A. Comenius, *Orbis sensualium pictus*, Nuremberg, 1658 (first edition; distinct from his earlier *Janua linguarum*). Ultimate source, following Rossi (*Clavis*, p. 186): Campanella's *City of the Sun* (her p. 298); also J. V. Andreae, *Reipublicae Christianopolitanae Descriptio*, Strasburg, 1619 (Christianopolis, likewise picture-decorated for instructing youth).
- **Principle.** A primer teaching children Latin, German, Italian and French by pictures "arranged in the order of the world" — heavens, stars and celestial phenomena, animals, birds, stones, man and all his activities; looking at the picture of the sun, or of a theatre, the child learns the word in every language. Yates reads it as the Solarian pedagogic method of the *City of the Sun* — where children walked the concentric walls of images dependent on the central causal sun-temple images, the world used "as a book" and as "local memory" — "translated" into a rational and original language primer.
- **Where.** Chapter XVII, pp. 377–378.
- **Quotations.**
  - "Now there can be no doubt that the Orbis pictus came straight out of Campanella's City of the Sun". (p. 377)
  - "The pedagogic method of the highly occult Solarians, and the whole plan of their City and its images, was a form of local memory, with its places and images." (p. 378)
  - "Translated into the Orbis pictus, the Solarian magic memory system becomes a perfectly rational, and extremely original and valuable, language primer." (p. 378)
- **Figures/plates.** None reproduced (the theatre picture is in Nicoll, *Stuart Masques*, fig. 113).
- **Related.** Campanella's City of the Sun as memory system (her pp. 297–8); Alsted as Comenius's teacher; Leibniz's Leipzig upbringing on "the picture book of Comenius" (p. 377); Comenius also carries Bacon's "real characters" into the universal-language movement (entry 23).

## 23. The universal language on "real characters" (Comenius, Bisterfield, Dalgarno, Wilkins)

- **Name.** "Universal language", "real characters" (Bacon's phrase). No other name given.
- **Author, date, source.** Bacon, *Advancement of Learning*, II, xvi, 3 (Spedding III, pp. 399–400) for "real characters"; Comenius and, through his influence, Bisterfield, Dalgarno, Wilkins "and others"; Rossi, *Clavis universalis*, chapter VII, pp. 201 ff., is Yates's source for the movement.
- **Principle.** Characters or signs "which should be really in contact with the notions they expressed" are sought as the basis of a universal language. Yates, after Rossi, holds that these efforts "come straight out of the memory tradition with its search for signs and symbols to use as memory images": the universal languages are conceived as aids to memory, their authors draw on the memory treatises, and the "real character" descends from the occult side of the tradition, rationalising Bruno's magic images "directly in contact with reality".
- **Where.** Chapter XVII, p. 378; resumed for Leibniz, p. 383.
- **Quotations.**
  - "As Rossi has shown, these efforts come straight out of the memory tradition with its search for signs and symbols to use as memory images." (p. 378)
  - "The seventeenth-century universal language enthusiasts are translating into rational terms efforts such as those of Giordano Bruno to found universal memory systems on magic images which he thought of as directly in contact with reality." (p. 378)
- **Figures/plates.** None.
- **Related.** Bacon (entry 18); Bruno's magic images; Leibniz's characteristica, "more than a universal language… a 'calculus'" (entry 27).

## 24. Izquierdo's *Pharus Scientiarum* and the "mathematicising" of Lullism (with Kircher's *Ars magna sciendi*)

- **Name.** *Pharus Scientiarum*; "mathematicised" Lullism. Alternative: Kircher's *Ars magna sciendi*.
- **Author, date, source.** Sebastian Izquierdo, S.J., *Pharus Scientiarum ubi quidquid ad cognitionem humanam humanitatis acquisibilem pertinet*, Leyden, 1659; Athanasius Kircher, S.J., *Ars magna sciendi in XII libros digesta*, Amsterdam, 1669 (Rossi, *Clavis*, pp. 194–6).
- **Principle.** A survey of universal-art projects — Gemma's cyclognomic art, Bacon's *Novum Organum*, the art of Lull, the art of memory — insisting on a universal science applied to all sciences of the encyclopaedia, a logic that includes memory, and an exact metaphysics modelled on mathematics. Its distinctive move: "for the Lullian combinations of letters, combinations of numbers have been substituted", pages and pages of them; Kircher likewise urged mathematicising Lullism. Rossi reads this as "a presage of Leibniz's use of the principles of the combinatoria as a calculus".
- **Where.** Chapter XVII, p. 379.
- **Quotations.**
  - "He insists that Lullism must be 'mathematicised' and in fact he gives pages and pages in which, for the Lullian combinations of letters, combinations of numbers have been substituted." (p. 379)
  - "the emergence of seventeenth-century methods should be studied in the context of the continuing influence of the arts." (p. 379)
- **Figures/plates.** None.
- **Related.** Gemma (entry 17), Bacon (entry 18), Alsted (entry 21); directly anticipates Leibniz's *combinatoria* (entry 26), which names Kircher and Alsted among modern Lullists.

## 25. Leibniz's *Mnemonica* (the *nota*; the "little memory treatise"; the emblem figure of the *Disputatio de casibus*; series of personages)

- **Name.** *Mnemonica* (Leibniz's term), alongside *Methodologia* and *Logica*. Alternative: *Ars reminiscendi*.
- **Author, date, source.** G. W. Leibniz, *Nova methodus discendae docendaeque jurisprudentiae*, 1667 (Philosophische Schriften, ed. Ritter, I, pp. 277–9); title-page of the *Disputatio de casibus in jure*, 1666 (ibid., p. 367); a remark of 1678 (Couturat, *Opuscules*, p. 281); unpublished Hanover manuscripts Phil. VI.19 and Phil. VII.B.III.7 mentioning Schenkel and Adam Bruxius's *Simonides Redivivus* (Leipzig, 1610); Rossi, *Clavis*, pp. 250–3.
- **Principle.** "Mnemonica provides the matter of an argument; Methodologia gives it form; and Logica is the application of the matter to the form." Mnemonica is "the joining of the image of some sensible thing to the thing to be remembered", the image being a *nota*, which must be connected to the thing by likeness, unlikeness, or association (the three Aristotelian laws); words can be remembered so, though with difficulty, and things. Things seen are better remembered than things heard, hence *notae*; Egyptian and Chinese hieroglyphs are of the nature of memory images; the distribution of things "in cells or places" helps memory, for which Alsted and Frey are to be consulted. Yates adds the emblem-figure on the *Disputatio* title-page, which she takes for "a local memory system for remembering law suits", and the 1678 remark that the *Ars memoriae* attaches a series of ideas to a series of personages — patriarchs, apostles, emperors.
- **Where.** Chapter XVII, pp. 380–381.
- **Quotations.**
  - "Here the mind of the great Leibniz is moving on lines which take us straight back to Ad Herennium, on images for things, and the harder images for words". (pp. 380–1)
  - "This passage is a little memory treatise by Leibniz." (p. 381)
  - "Thus Leibniz knew the memory tradition extremely well; he had studied the memory treatises and had picked up, not only the main lines of the classical rules, but also complications which had grown up around these". (p. 381)
- **Figures/plates.** None reproduced.
- **Related.** *Ad Herennium*; the scholastic laws of association; Alsted (entry 21), Frey, Schenkel, Bruxius; the *nota* returns as the alphabet of the *combinatoria* (entry 26) and as the "character" of the characteristica (entry 27); the personage-series is "one of the most characteristic and time-honoured of the memory practices".

## 26. Leibniz's *Dissertatio de arte combinatoria* (mathematical Lullism with *notae* as alphabet)

- **Name.** *Dissertatio de arte combinatoria*; "this new mathematical-Lullist art". Alternative: Leibniz's "new Lullism", the "combinatoria".
- **Author, date, source.** G. W. Leibniz, *Dissertatio de arte combinatoria*, 1666 (Philosophische Schriften, ed. Ritter, I, pp. 166 opening diagram, 194 on Bruno, 302 on notae); Couturat, *La logique de Leibniz*, pp. 36 ff.; Rossi, *Clavis*, p. 242. Leibniz cites the preface to Bruno's *De specierum scrutinio*, Prague, 1588.
- **Principle.** Lullism grasped "as a natural logic" (the opening diagram joins the square of the four elements to the logical square of opposition) and interpreted "with arithmetic and with the 'inventive logic' which Francis Bacon wanted to improve". The *notae* serve "as an alphabet": as natural as possible, a universal writing, like geometrical figures or the "pictures" of the Egyptians and Chinese, but better than these "for memory". The prefatory pages list modern Lullists — Agrippa, Alsted, Kircher, "Jordanus Brunus" — noting that Bruno called the Lullian Art a "combinatoria", the word Leibniz now uses. Leibniz said the germ of his later thinking lay here.
- **Where.** Chapter XVII, pp. 381–382.
- **Quotations.**
  - "There is already here the idea of using the 'combinatoria' with mathematics which, as we have seen, had been developing in Alsted, Izquierdo, and Kircher." (p. 381)
  - "It is perfectly clear that Leibniz is emerging out of a Renaissance tradition—out of those unending efforts to combine Lullism with the classical art of memory." (p. 382)
  - "Leibniz himself said that the germ of his later thinking was in the Dissertatio de arte combinatoria." (p. 382)
- **Figures/plates.** None reproduced (the opening diagram is described).
- **Related.** Direct heir of Bruno's placing of memory images on the Lullian wheels, and of Alsted, Izquierdo, Kircher (entries 21, 24); the *nota* of the *Mnemonica* (entry 25); leads to the characteristica and the infinitesimal calculus (entry 27).

## 27. Leibniz's *characteristica universalis* (the universal calculus and the encyclopaedia of "characters")

- **Name.** The "characteristica", "universal characteristic", "universal calculus". Alternatives in Leibniz's own list: *Ars Characteristica* or *Symbolica*; also presented as an "innocent magia" or "true Cabala", a "Great Key" (*Clavis*).
- **Author, date, source.** G. W. Leibniz: the project described in Couturat, *Logique*, pp. 51 ff., 84–5, 98, 131–8; *Fundamenta calculi ratiocinatoris* (Opera philosophica, ed. Erdmann, pp. 92–3; cf. Gerhardt VII, pp. 204–5); *Introductio ad Encyclopaediam arcanam* (Couturat, *Opuscules*, pp. 511–12); essay on the characteristica (Gerhardt VII, p. 184); *Initia et specimina scientiae novae generalis* (ibid., p. 67); letters (Ritter, Series I, vol. II, pp. 167–9); Rossi, *Clavis*, pp. 201 ff., 255.
- **Principle.** Lists are drawn up of all the essential notions of thought, each assigned a symbol or "character"; the characters are then combined logically "to form a universal art or calculus for the solution of all problems". This depends on an encyclopaedia systematising all knowledge; once complete, disputants (even over the Council of Trent) would say "Let us calculate". Characters are "signs which are either written, or delineated, or sculptured", the more useful the closer to the thing signified; the signs of chemists and astronomers (Dee's *Monas hieroglyphica*), Chinese and Egyptian figures, and the lost Adamic language are rejected in favour of "the notae of the arithmeticians and algebraists". Yates reads the whole as the search for "images for things" since Simonides transferred to mathematical symbolism, of which the infinitesimal calculus was "but a fragment, or a specimen"; the encyclopaedia, the assignment of characters, and the calculus were never completed. Bound up with it are plans for church reunion and an "Order of Charity" whose rules quote the Rosicrucian *Fama*.
- **Where.** Chapter XVII, pp. 380, 382–388.
- **Quotations.**
  - "The mature Leibniz, the supreme mathematician and logician, is obviously still emerging straight out of Renaissance efforts for conflating the classical art of memory with Lullism by using the images of the classical art on the Lullian combinatory wheels." (p. 383)
  - "it was through his invention of new 'characters' that he was able to operate the infinitesimal calculus, which was but a fragment, or a specimen, of the never completed 'universal characteristic'." (p. 384)
  - "here will be found a general science, a new logic, a new method, an Ars reminiscendi or Mnemonica, an Ars Characteristica or Symbolica, an Ars Combinatoria or Lulliana, a Cabala of the Wise, a Magia Naturalis". (p. 385, Yates paraphrasing the *Introductio ad Encyclopaediam arcanam*)
  - "If we delete Magic, substitute genuine mathematics for Mathesis, understand Art as the calculus, and retain Love, the Leibnizian aspirations seem to approximate strikingly closely… to those of Bruno." (p. 387)
- **Figures/plates.** None; Yates sends the reader back to Pl. 11 (Bruno's *Shadows* wheel diagram, her chapter IX).
- **Related.** Yates makes Leibniz "the seventeenth-century heir" of Lull's Art, Camillo's Theatre and Bruno's images on the Lullian wheels; the *Introductio* list "might be… the lengthy title-page of Bruno's Seals" (her p. 201). Rosicrucian aura (Couturat's claim of a 1666 Nuremberg affiliation; the Fama-derived rules) and the hypothesis of a Hermetic society founded by Bruno in Germany "afterwards developing as Rosicrucianism" are raised and left open (pp. 387–8). The monad as "living mirror" of the universe is noted as bearing "the obvious marks of the Hermetic tradition" (p. 388). Yates ends the history here: "Our history which began with Simonides must not end before Leibniz" (p. 369).
