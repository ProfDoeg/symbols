## 1. The 'Democritus' type of memory treatise

- **Name.** The 'Democritus' type (Yates's own label); e.g. the *regulae memoriae artificialis* of Lodovico da Pirano.
- **Author, date, source.** Lodovico da Pirano, Franciscan teaching at Padua from c. 1422 (Marciana VI, 274; other MSS Marciana XIV, 292 and Vat. Lat. 5347). Also Luca Braga, Padua 1477 (BM Add. 10,438).
- **Principle.** Follows the *Ad Herennium* plan (rules for places, rules for images) but assigns the invention of the art to Democritus, not Simonides, ignores the striking human figures and Thomas Aquinas, and rests the image rules on Aristotelian laws of association. Some MSS carry diagrams of rows of towers for the 'multiplication of places'. Yates hypothesises a Byzantine channel.
- **Chapter / pages.** V, pp. 106–107 (and n. 3).
- **Quotations.** "One type of memory treatise may be called the 'Democritus' type from the peculiarity that such treatises assign the invention of the art of memory to Democritus and not to Simonides." (p. 106) — "In their rules for images, such treatises do not mention the striking human figures of Ad Herennium but concentrate on Aristotelian laws of association." (p. 106) — "the curious diagrams of the rows of towers to be used for 'multiplication of places'" (p. 106, n. 3).
- **Figures.** None reproduced.
- **Related.** Merges into "the general agglomeration of the memory tradition" (p. 107); Bruno's later use of Aristotelian association is not traced to it here.

## 2. Lists of memory objects as prefabricated images

- **Name.** Memory objects; the object lists of the 'Ars memorie artificialis, pater reuerende' treatises.
- **Author, date, source.** Anonymous 15th-c. MSS with the incipit 'pater reuerende' (many copies); Lodovico da Pirano's list; Boncompagno, *Rhetorica novissima* (13th c.) as older precedent.
- **Principle.** Long lists of familiar objects (a paternoster, an anvil, a helmet, a lantern, a tripod) are supplied ready-made; the practitioner memorises them in order on sets of places. Order is kept by the place-sequence, not by the images' strikingness. The image rules of these treatises stress images "like people we know".
- **Chapter / pages.** V, p. 107 (nn. 5–6).
- **Quotations.** "A feature of earlier treatises is long lists of objects, often beginning with a 'paternoster' and followed by familiar objects, such as an anvil, a helmet, a lantern, a tripod, and so on." (p. 107) — "They are, I believe, as it were prefabricated memory images to be memorised on sets of places. This is almost certainly an old mediaeval tradition" (p. 107).
- **Figures.** Seen "in action" in Romberch's cuts, Pl. 5a–b (see entry 3).
- **Related.** Entry 3 (Romberch's Abbey); the 'straight mnemotechnic' (entry 9); Bruno's second wheel of objects (entry 63) is not linked by Yates to these.

## 3. Romberch's Abbey memory system

- **Name.** Abbey Memory System (caption to Pl. 5a); memorising real places on real buildings.
- **Author, date, source.** Johannes Romberch OP, *Congestorium artificiose memorie*, Venice 1520 (Yates uses ed. of 1533, p. 17 and pp. 35 recto ff.).
- **Principle.** An abbey with its courtyard, library and chapel supplies a numbered sequence of loci; the memory objects of entry 2 are set in them. Every fifth place bears a hand, every tenth a cross, after *Ad Herennium*, and the places are ticked off on the fingers as memory walks through. Yates reads it as a classical, mechanical use probably employed for psalms and prayers.
- **Chapter / pages.** V, pp. 107–108, 117.
- **Quotations.** "Each fifth place is marked with a hand and each tenth place with a cross, in accordance with the instructions given in Ad Herennium for distinguishing the fifth and tenth places." (p. 108) — "As Memory moved along the places, these were ticked off on the fingers." (p. 108) — "What Romberch describes as being practised in the abbey is a fully classical and mnemotechnical, use of the art, though probably mainly used for religious purposes" (p. 108).
- **Figures.** Pl. 5a (Abbey Memory System) and Pl. 5b (images to be used in it), from Romberch ed. 1533, p. 17.
- **Related.** Romberch's third type of place system (entry 13); Peter of Ravenna's church (entry 8); Rossellius's 'real' places (entry 19).

## 4. The anonymous Dominican treatise: the whole order of the universe and the roads to Heaven and Hell

- **Name.** 'De memoria artificiali' (Marciana VI, 238). With it Yates groups the scholastic-tradition MSS of Jacopo Ragone (BM Add. 10,438, with a drawing of a palazzo for memory places) and Matthew of Verona OP (Marciana XIV, 292).
- **Author, date, source.** Anonymous, probably Dominican; the copy is 15th-c., the work possibly earlier.
- **Principle.** Uses artificial memory to hold the entire ordered universe and the ways to Heaven and Hell; only 'devout images' and 'sacred histories' are admitted, no 'vana phantasmata'; saints with their attributes serve as memory images set on loci. Parts are nearly identical with Romberch's printed text, showing the MS tradition behind the printed treatises.
- **Chapter / pages.** V, p. 108 (nn. 7–10).
- **Quotations.** "An anonymous treatise, probably also by a Dominican, gives a most solemn description of how to remember the whole order of the universe and the roads to Heaven and Hell by the artificial memory." (p. 108) — "he will use, he says, in his art only 'devout images' and 'sacred histories' not fables or 'vana phantasmata'" (p. 108, n. 9) — "Such printed treatises came out of a manuscript tradition leading back into the Middle Ages." (p. 108)
- **Figures.** None (Ragone's palazzo drawing is described, not reproduced).
- **Related.** Romberch's cosmic/Dantesque place system (entry 11); Rossellius's Hell and Paradise (entry 18).

## 5. The Vienna codex row of memory figures

- **Name.** Row of memory images in Vienna National Library Codex 5395 (mid-15th c.), reproduced by Volkmann (Pls. 115–124).
- **Author, date, source.** Anonymous, mid-15th c.; inscription on the last figure: 'Ex locis et imaginibus ars memorativa constat Tullius ait.'
- **Principle.** A series of human figures, headed by a crowned lady (Prudence) and probably representing virtues and vices, with Christ at the centre and the mouth of Hell beneath his feet; subsidiary images on and around the figures are 'memory for words'. The faces are to resemble known persons ('Margaretha, Dorothea…', one male figure labelled 'Brueder Ottell'). A rare depiction of human memory images.
- **Chapter / pages.** V, p. 109 (nn. 11–14).
- **Quotations.** "The series is headed by a lady who is almost certainly Prudence; the other figures also probably represent virtues and vices." (p. 109) — "unfortunately the artist has made them all remarkably hideous." (p. 109) — "both 'things' and 'words' may be remembered through these figures, which may represent a debased survival of mediaeval artificial memory through inscriptions on the figures." (p. 109)
- **Figures.** Not reproduced by Yates (Volkmann's plates cited).
- **Related.** Romberch's inscribed Grammar (entry 16); the mediaeval virtue/vice images of Ch. IV.

## 6. Memory rooms with five places

- **Name.** Memory rooms (plans of rooms marked with five places).
- **Author, date, source.** Same Vienna codex 5395; also "other manuscripts and … printed treatises".
- **Principle.** A room is drawn with five loci, four in the corners and one at the centre; images are set on them. The regular, symmetrical layout ignores the classical advice that places be unlike one another and irregular. Yates takes it as the normal mediaeval and later interpretation of 'places'.
- **Chapter / pages.** V, p. 109.
- **Quotations.** "This manuscript also shows plans of memory rooms, marked with five places, four in the corners and one in the centre, on which images are to be memorised." (p. 109) — "The regular arrangement of the places in such memory rooms … was, I believe, a normal interpretation of places, both in the Middle Ages and in later times." (p. 109)
- **Figures.** None reproduced.
- **Related.** Romberch's memory rooms for each science (entry 17); Dürer's "chamber must have more than four corners" (entry 21).

## 7. Publicius's *Ars memorativa*: the spheres of the universe as imaginary places

- **Name.** *Ars memorativa* appended to the *Oratoriae artis epitome*; 'ficta loca' as the spheres of the universe. Yates: "The Spheres of the Universe as a Memory System" (Fig. 1 caption).
- **Author, date, source.** Jacobus Publicius, Venice 1482 (2nd ed. 1485), the first printed memory treatise. Copied in England in 1460 by Thomas Swatwell, probably a monk of Durham (BM Add. 28,805), who added diagrams, one "probably magical".
- **Principle.** Places are the spheres of the elements, planets, fixed stars and higher spheres, topped by 'Paradisus'; images follow Thomas ("simple and spiritual intentions slip easily from the memory unless joined to a corporeal similitude") and the *Ad Herennian* strikingness, with Ovid's Envy as model. A Dantesque memory of Hell, Purgatory and Paradise on the cosmic order, not a humanist mnemotechnic. It is also the first printed treatise to illustrate a visual alphabet (p. 118).
- **Chapter / pages.** V, pp. 110–111, 118 (n. 36); Fig. 1.
- **Quotations.** "Publicius's interpretation of these includes the construction of 'ficta loca', or imaginary places, which are none other than the spheres of the universe" (p. 110) — "Unhappy Envy as described by Ovid with her livid complexion, black teeth, and snakey hair, is a good example of what a memory image should be." (p. 110) — "this first printed memory treatise is not a symptom of the revival of the classical art of memory as part of the Renaissance revival of rhetoric; it comes straight out of the mediaeval tradition." (pp. 110–111)
- **Figures.** Fig. 1, The Spheres of the Universe as a Memory System, from Publicius 1482.
- **Related.** Romberch's cosmic diagram, Fig. 2 (entry 11) is the direct descendant; Romberch's objects alphabet is based on Publicius's (entry 15).

## 8. Peter of Ravenna's *Phoenix*

- **Name.** *Phoenix, sive artificiosa memoria* (Copland's English: *The Art of Memory that is otherwise called the Phoenix*).
- **Author, date, source.** Peter of Ravenna (Petrus Tommai), jurist trained at Padua; Venice 1491, many editions, included in Reisch's *Margarita philosophica* (Lib. III, Tract. II, cap. XXIII).
- **Principle.** Loci are formed in an unfrequented church, walked round three or four times, the first near the door, the next five or six feet on; new places are added on journeys (Quintilian's influence) until he claims 100,000 and more. Images should resemble known people (Juniper of Pistoia); persons in alphabetical order stand for syllables (Eusebius before Thomas = ET, reversed = TE). A lay success-technique, though with unexplained mediaeval residues. He also gives the rule that a locus be no larger than a man can reach (entry 14).
- **Chapter / pages.** V, pp. 111–114, 117, 119.
- **Quotations.** "the best type of building to use is an unfrequented church." (p. 113) — "As a young man he started with one hundred thousand memorised places, but he has added many more since then." (p. 113) — "Peter laicised and popularised memory and emphasised the purely mnemotechnical side." (pp. 113–114)
- **Figures.** None reproduced.
- **Related.** Cited by Romberch as an authority (p. 114); the persons-alphabet variant of the visual alphabet (entry 15); English translation treated later (p. 260).

## 9. The 'straight mnemotechnic' and the magical infiltration (Ars notoria)

- **Name.** 'The straight mnemotechnic' (Yates's term); and the treatises showing "infiltration of mediaeval forms of magic memory, such as the Ars notoria".
- **Author, date, source.** Category label for the Quintilianist type; magical examples Jodocus Weczdorff, *Ars memorandi nova secretissima* (c. 1600) and Nicolas Simon aus Weida, *Ludus artificialis oblivionis* (Leipzig 1510), reproduced by Volkmann (Pls. 168–71).
- **Principle.** Yates sorts the printed treatises into four strands: the plain classical mnemotechnic made clearer by the recovery of Quintilian (1416/1470); those entangled with mediaeval uses; those with traces of magical memory (Ars notoria); those touched by the Renaissance Hermetic transformation. No system is described for the magical works beyond their "heavily magical" frontispieces and diagrams.
- **Chapter / pages.** V, pp. 111–112, 114 (n. 25); Thomas's condemnation of the Ars notoria recalled at IX, p. 204 (n. 16).
- **Quotations.** "Some treatises teach what I shall hereafter call 'the straight mnemotechnic', perhaps better understood after the recovery of Quintilian." (p. 114) — "In some there are traces of infiltration of mediaeval forms of magic memory, such as the Ars notoria into the art." (p. 114) — "it is Quintilian who gives the clearest account of the art as a mnemotechnic." (p. 111)
- **Figures.** None reproduced (Volkmann's cited).
- **Related.** Erasmus's Colloquy against the Ars notoria (p. 127, n. 57); Thomas Aquinas's condemnation, *Summa* II, II, q. 96 (p. 204).

## 10. Romberch's *Congestorium*: plan of the book and the three types of place system

- **Name.** *Congestorium artificiose memorie* ("a strange congestion of memory material").
- **Author, date, source.** Johannes Romberch OP (German), 1520; Yates uses Venice 1533; Italianised by Dolce (entry 32).
- **Principle.** Four parts: introduction; places; images; an encyclopaedic memory system. Knows all three classical sources, cites Petrarch and Peter of Ravenna, but rests on Thomas Aquinas's formulations quoted "on nearly every other page". Three types of place system are all called artificial memory: the cosmos (entry 11), the zodiac/constellations (entry 12), real buildings (entry 13).
- **Chapter / pages.** V, pp. 114–115.
- **Quotations.** "The book is in four parts; the first introductory, the second on places, the third on images; the fourth part outlines an encyclopaedic memory system." (p. 115) — "Romberch envisages three different types of place systems, as all belonging to artificial memory." (p. 115) — "By his frequent citation of the name of Petrarch, he absorbs the poet into the Dominican memory tradition" (p. 115).
- **Figures.** Figs. 2, 3; Pls. 5a–b, 6a–c (see following entries).
- **Related.** Rossellius's *Thesaurus* "on similar lines" (entry 18); Dolce's *Dialogo* (entry 32); Bruno's memory books, into which the Romberch–Rossellius "congestions" crowd (p. 199).

## 11. The cosmos as place system: the 'Dantesque' type

- **Name.** "The Spheres of the Universe as a Memory System" (Fig. 2); the Dantesque type of artificial memory (Yates's label).
- **Author, date, source.** Romberch, *Congestorium*, pp. 17 recto ff., 31 recto ff. (ed. 1533).
- **Principle.** The spheres of the elements, planets, fixed stars, celestial spheres and nine angelic orders form the loci; at the bottom the letters L.PA, L.P, PVR, IN mark Paradise, the Earthly Paradise, Purgatory and Hell. These 'ficta loca' are 'common places' subdivided into particular places carrying inscriptions; Hell's places hold images of sinners punished according to their sins.
- **Chapter / pages.** V, pp. 115–116; Fig. 2.
- **Quotations.** "For the invisible things of Paradise we are to form places in memory in which we put the choirs of angels, the seats of the blessed, Patriarchs, Prophets, Apostles, Martyrs." (p. 115) — "This type of artificial memory may be called the Dantesque type, not because the Dominican treatise is influenced by the Divine Comedy, but because Dante was influenced by such an interpretation of artificial memory" (p. 116).
- **Figures.** Fig. 2, from Romberch ed. 1533; reproduced again in Dolce's *Dialogo* (p. 163).
- **Related.** Descends from Publicius's Fig. 1 (entry 7) and the anonymous MS (entry 4); Rossellius's Hell and Paradise (entry 18); survives as vestiges in Camillo's Theatre (entry 31); Gesualdo (entry 34).

## 12. The zodiac and the constellations as place system (Metrodorus of Scepsis revived)

- **Name.** Zodiacal memory system of Metrodorus of Scepsis; extended with Hyginus's constellation images.
- **Author, date, source.** Romberch, *Congestorium*, pp. 25 recto ff. (zodiac, citing Metrodorus from Cicero's *De oratore* and Quintilian) and p. 33 verso (Hyginus). Rossellius repeats it (*Thesaurus*, p. 22 verso).
- **Principle.** The twelve signs give an easily memorised order of places; if more are needed, the images of all the constellations from Hyginus supply a longer star-order. Romberch does not say what is to be memorised on them; Yates guesses preachers' sermons on virtues and vices in Heaven and Hell.
- **Chapter / pages.** V, pp. 116–117, 122; recalled at IX, pp. 206, 217.
- **Quotations.** "As another type of place system, Romberch envisages using the signs of the zodiac as giving an easily memorised order of places. He gives the name of Metrodorus of Scepsis as the authority on this." (p. 116) — "if a more extended star-order for memory is needed, it is useful to turn to the images given by Hyginus of all the constellations of the sky." (p. 116)
- **Figures.** None.
- **Related.** Rossellius's constellation places (entry 19); Porta condemns Metrodorus (entry 53); "The Metrodorian system has turned into a magical system" in Bruno (entry 62); the Renaissance's "other authorities for a celestial basing of memory" (p. 196).

## 13. Real buildings with memory objects and alphabetical lists

- **Name.** Romberch's third type of place system: memorising real places on real buildings, with 'alphabetical orders'.
- **Author, date, source.** Romberch, *Congestorium*, pp. 35 recto ff.
- **Principle.** Real buildings (the abbey of Pl. 5a) supply places; memory objects (Pl. 5b) are the images; lists of animals, birds and names in alphabetical order are kept as auxiliary stock. This is the ground of the 'straight mnemotechnic' of the mechanical Quintilian type, with non-classical alphabetical elaborations.
- **Chapter / pages.** V, p. 117.
- **Quotations.** "His third type of place system is the more normally mnemotechnical method of memorising real places on real buildings" (p. 117) — "It helps to have lists of animals, birds, names, arranged in alphabetical order to use with this system." (p. 117)
- **Figures.** Pl. 5a, 5b.
- **Related.** Entry 3; visual alphabets (entry 15).

## 14. The locus proportioned to a human image

- **Name.** Rule that a memory locus be no larger than a man can reach; "Human Image on a Memory Locus" (Fig. 3).
- **Author, date, source.** Romberch, *Congestorium*, p. 28 verso; also given by Peter of Ravenna and "may go back much earlier".
- **Principle.** A locus that is to hold a human memory image must be sized to the figure's reach upwards and sideways, so that regularly placed images stand out from their backgrounds. Yates ties it to the classical feeling for space, lighting and distance in the place rules and to Giotto's painted loci. It applies to human images, not to memory objects.
- **Chapter / pages.** V, p. 117; Fig. 3.
- **Quotations.** "A memory locus which is to contain a memory image must not be larger than a man can reach" (p. 117) — "This rule grows out of the artistic feeling for space, lighting, distance, in memory in the classical place rules" (p. 117).
- **Figures.** Fig. 3, from Romberch ed. 1533.
- **Related.** Peter of Ravenna (entry 8).

## 15. Visual alphabets (objects, birds and animals, persons, numbers)

- **Name.** Visual alphabets; Boncompagno's 'alphabetum imaginarium'.
- **Author, date, source.** Romberch, *Congestorium* (Pl. 6b–c, pp. 119–20 of ed. 1533); first printed in Publicius (Volkmann Pl. 146); Rossellius, *Thesaurus*, p. 119 verso; Peter of Ravenna, *Phoenix*, sig. c i recto; number images in Romberch, Rossellius, Porta (Volkmann Pls. 183–5, 188, 194).
- **Principle.** Letters are represented by images: objects whose shapes resemble letters (compasses or a ladder for A, a hoe for N); animals or birds in alphabetical order of their names (A Anser, B Bubo); persons known to the practitioner arranged alphabetically (Eusebius before Thomas = ET); objects for numbers. Yates believes they were meant for making inscriptions in memory ('memory for words') on images. Rossellius: AER through Ass, Elephant, Rhinoceros.
- **Chapter / pages.** V, pp. 118–119 (nn. 35–39), 123; Pl. 6b, 6c.
- **Quotations.** "Visual alphabets are ways of representing letters of the alphabet by images." (p. 118) — "The notion as worked out in the visual alphabets is of infantile simplicity, like teaching a child to remember C through the picture of a Cat." (p. 119) — "The visual alphabets illustrated in the memory treatises, were I believe, intended to be used for making inscriptions in memory." (p. 119)
- **Figures.** Pl. 6b (objects alphabet) and Pl. 6c (birds alphabet), from Romberch ed. 1533, pp. 119–20.
- **Related.** Grammar inscribed with them (entry 16); Porta's "images for letters and numbers" (entry 53); reproduced in Dolce (entry 32); Yates suggests an influence on the inscriptions of the *Hypnerotomachia* (entry 20).

## 16. Grammar as a memory image inscribed with visual alphabets

- **Name.** "Grammar as a Memory Image" (Pl. 6a).
- **Author, date, source.** Romberch, *Congestorium*, pp. 82 verso–83 recto (ed. 1533, cut on p. 119).
- **Principle.** The personification of Grammatica (scalpel, ladder) is a memory image; on her are inscriptions in the objects and birds alphabets that memorise the answer to whether Grammar is a common or particular science: *predicatio* by the Pica she holds, *applicatio* by the Aquila on her arm, *continentia* by the C-O-N-T objects across her chest. Personifications recall 'things'; inscriptions on them are 'memory for words'.
- **Chapter / pages.** V, pp. 119–120 (nn. 40–41), 123; Pl. 6a.
- **Quotations.** "the image turns out to be the familiar figure of old Grammatica, the first of the liberal arts, with some of her familiar attributes, the scalpel and the ladder." (p. 119) — "The images themselves recall the memory of the 'things' and the inscriptions memorised on them are 'memory for words' about the 'things'. Or so I would suggest." (p. 120) — "If we did not know the mnemonic explanation of Romberch's Grammar, she might seem like some inscrutable emblem." (p. 123)
- **Figures.** Pl. 6a, from Romberch ed. 1533, p. 119; "exactly reproduced" in Dolce (p. 163).
- **Related.** Confirms the reading of Holcot's Penance and the cardinal-virtue inscriptions of Ch. IV; the encyclopaedic system (entry 17); emblem and impresa (entry 20).

## 17. Romberch's encyclopaedic memory system: the sciences as figures in memory rooms

- **Name.** The encyclopaedic memory system of the fourth part of the *Congestorium*.
- **Author, date, source.** Romberch, *Congestorium*, pp. 81 recto, 84 recto (ed. 1533).
- **Principle.** For every science a figure is imagined (for Theology "a perfect and excellent theologian" with *cognitio, amor, fruitio* on his head and *essentia divina, actus, forma…* on his members); the parts and subdivisions of Theology, Metaphysics, Law, Astronomy, Geometry, Arithmetic, Music, Logic, Rhetoric and Grammar are set out in columns and memorised through images, associated images and inscriptions; each subject occupies a memory room. Yates suspects a decayed echo of Thomas Aquinas's own system and links it to the fourteen figures of the Santa Maria Novella fresco.
- **Chapter / pages.** V, pp. 120–121 (nn. 42–43).
- **Quotations.** "For Theology, for example, we may imagine a perfect and excellent theologian; he will have on his head images of cognitio, amor, fruitio" (p. 121) — "Each subject is to be placed in a memory room." (p. 121) — "some distant echo of the memory system of Thomas Aquinas himself." (p. 121)
- **Figures.** Pl. 6a is the worked example.
- **Related.** Memory rooms (entry 6); Rossellius placing practitioners for the sciences (entry 33); Camillo's Prometheus grade and Bruno's inventors' wheel as encyclopaedic memories (entries 25, 65).

## 18. Rossellius's *Thesaurus*: Hell in eleven places and Paradise as artificial memory

- **Name.** "Hell as Artificial Memory" and "Paradise as Artificial Memory" (Pl. 7a–b).
- **Author, date, source.** Cosmas Rossellius OP (Florentine), *Thesaurus artificiosae memoriae*, Venice 1579, pp. 2 verso, 33 recto (diagram p. 122).
- **Principle.** Hell is divided into eleven places: a central well reached by steps carrying Heretics, Jewish Infidels, Idolaters, Hypocrites, and around it seven places for the seven deadly sins, the diverse punishments, situations and gestures of the damned giving the images. Paradise is a gem-walled enclosure with the Throne of Christ at centre and ranked below it the hierarchies, Apostles, Patriarchs, Prophets, Martyrs, Confessors, Virgins, Holy Hebrews, saints; the orders "as painters paint them". Formed "with art and exercise and vehement imagination".
- **Chapter / pages.** V, pp. 122–123; VII, p. 164; Pl. 7a, 7b.
- **Quotations.** "Rossellius divides Hell into eleven places, as illustrated in his diagram of Hell as a memory place system" (p. 122) — "'the variety of punishments, inflicted in accordance with the diverse nature of the sins, the different situations of the damned, their varying gestures, will much help memory and give many places.'" (p. 122) — "There is nothing at all unusual about Rosellius's Paradise, except that it is classed as 'artificial memory'." (p. 122)
- **Figures.** Pl. 7a (Hell), Pl. 7b (Paradise), from *Thesaurus* 1579, p. 122.
- **Related.** Dantesque type (entry 11); Nardo di Cione's Hell (Pl. 8a, referring to pp. 94–5); Garzoni hopes to combine Rossellius with Lull (entry 55); Romberch–Rossellius congestions reach Bruno (p. 199).

## 19. Rossellius: constellation places, the Inquisitor's mnemonic verses, human images as places

- **Name.** The rest of the *Thesaurus* apparatus: constellations as place systems (Metrodorus), 'carmina' for orders of places, 'real' places, human images as places, general image rules and a visual alphabet.
- **Author, date, source.** Rossellius, *Thesaurus*, p. 22 verso (zodiac) and passim; the verses by "a fellow Dominican who is also an Inquisitor".
- **Principle.** Orders of places (in Hell, or the signs of the zodiac) are fixed by mnemonic verses; real places are made in abbeys and churches; a human figure serves as a place on which subsidiary images are hung; a visual alphabet of Romberch's type is added.
- **Chapter / pages.** V, pp. 122–123.
- **Quotations.** "A feature of Rossellius's book are the mnemonic verses given to help memorise orders of places, whether orders of places in Hell, or the order of the signs of the zodiac." (p. 122) — "These 'carmina' by an Inquisitor give an impressive air of great orthodoxy to the artificial memory." (p. 122) — "And discusses human images as places on which subsidiary images are to be remembered." (p. 122)
- **Figures.** None beyond Pl. 7a–b.
- **Related.** Entries 12, 15, 33.

## 20. Emblem and impresa as memory (with the 'wild imaginative' memory: Boethius, the *Hypnerotomachia*, Bocchius, Dee)

- **Name.** The impresa (device) and emblem seen "from the point of view of memory".
- **Author, date, source.** Yates's suggestion, pp. 123–124; examples: memorising Boethius's *Consolation* (Vienna codex 5393); Francesco Colonna OP, *Hypnerotomachia Poliphili* (before 1500); Ruscelli's heliotrope device (*Imprese illustri*, 1572); Achilles Bocchius, *Symbolicarum quaestionum* (Bologna 1555), Mercurius with the seven-branched candlestick; Dee's *Monas hieroglyphica* (1564); Farra's *Settenario* (1571).
- **Principle.** An impresa remembers a spiritual intention through a similitude, exactly in Thomas's terms, so emblem and device are memory images turned outward; the Dominican-authored *Hypnerotomachia*, with its Hell divided into places with inscriptions, may be artificial memory "gone out of control". Not a system with rules, but Yates treats the genre as a memory phenomenon and returns to it beside the Theatre.
- **Chapter / pages.** V, pp. 123–124; VII, pp. 169–170 (n. 25) and Frontispiece.
- **Quotations.** "The impresa, in particular, is the attempt to remember a spiritual intention through a similitude; the words of Thomas Aquinas define it exactly." (p. 124) — "Perhaps an artificial memory gone out of control into wild imaginative indulgence might be one of the stimuli behind such a work as the Hypnerotomachia Polyphili" (p. 123) — "The impresa is related to the memory image, as already suggested, and in commentaries on imprese there is frequently to be found a blend of Hermetic-Cabalist mysticism" (pp. 169–170).

- **Related.** Camillo's images "very like imprese" (p. 169); Grammar as inscrutable emblem (entry 16).

## 21. The humanist alternative: study, order and care; learning by heart

- **Name.** Erasmus's three things (study, order, care); Melanchthon's learning by heart; Agrippa's 'vanity' of the art; Dürer's chambers of memory.
- **Author, date, source.** Erasmus, *De ratione studii* (1512); Melanchthon, *Rhetorica elementa* (1534); Agrippa, *De vanitate scientiarum* (1530), cap. X; Dürer, letter to Pirckheimer (Sept. 1506).
- **Principle.** Quintilianist coolness: places and images may help, but the best memory rests on study, order and care; Melanchthon forbids mnemonic devices and prescribes ordinary memorising as the sole art. Agrippa dismisses the modern treatises as "an unworthy catalogue by obscure men". Yates sets this as the rational pole against which Camillo and Bruno stand.
- **Chapter / pages.** V, pp. 124, 126–128; VI, p. 132.
- **Quotations.** "Though I do not deny that memory can be helped by places and images, yet the best memory is based on three most important things, namely study, order, and care." (p. 127) — "Melanchthon forbids students to use any mnemotechnical devices and enjoins learning by heart in the normal way as the sole art of memory." (p. 127) — "A chamber must have more than four corners which is to contain all the gods of memory." (p. 126)
- **Figures.** None.
- **Related.** Logifer/'Magister Psicoteus' in Bruno's *Shadows* (entry 52); Viglius–Erasmus against Camillo (entry 22); Agrippa's *De occulta philosophia* (entry 54).

## 22. Camillo's Memory Theatre: the Vitruvian theatre reversed

- **Name.** The Memory Theatre (*L'Idea del Theatro*); called by contemporaries an 'Amphitheatre'; Camillo's own names "a built or constructed mind and soul", "a windowed one" (p. 132).
- **Author, date, source.** Giulio Camillo Delminio (c. 1480–1544); wooden theatre shown in Venice to Viglius Zuichemus (1532), another built in Paris for Francis I (1534); *L'Idea del Theatro*, dictated to Muzio on seven mornings, published Florence and Venice 1550 (Yates's plan reconstructed from it; "see Folder").
- **Principle.** A Vitruvian theatre with seven gangways and seven rising grades, but reversed: the solitary spectator stands on the stage and looks at the auditorium, where on each of the seven gangways stand seven imaginary decorated gates. The 7 × 7 gates are the memory places, stocked with images; the gangways are the seven planets, the grades the stages of creation. The great things are lowest, as in ancient seating. Beneath the images, drawers of papers (entry 27).
- **Chapter / pages.** VI, pp. 129–159 (structure pp. 136–138); VII pp. 160–172; Folder plan.
- **Quotations.** "The Theatre rises in seven grades or steps, which are divided by seven gangways representing the seven planets." (p. 136) — "The solitary 'spectator' of the Theatre stands where the stage would be and looks towards the auditorium, gazing at the images on the seven times seven gates on the seven rising grades." (p. 137) — "The work is of wood, marked with many images, and full of little boxes; there are various orders and grades in it." (Viglius, p. 131)
- **Figures.** Plan of the Theatre (Folder, not in the extraction: pdf pages 167–168 are blank); Pl. 9a–b for the Vitruvian model.
- **Related.** Descends from the classical art with Ficino–Pico occult philosophy poured in (p. 145); Bruno's *Shadows* is "the successor to Camillo's Theatre" (p. 200); Yates compared its plan with Bruno, Campanella and Fludd (p. 130, n. 4); Paolini's *Hebdomades* (entry 35); Gohorry's 1550 description (entry 55); Citolini suspected of theft (p. 144).

## 23. The seven pillars: Sephiroth, planets and angels through the three worlds

- **Name.** The seven pillars of Solomon's House of Wisdom; the 'seven measures' (Seven Governors of the *Pimander*); 'eternal places'.
- **Author, date, source.** Camillo, *L'Idea del Theatro*, pp. 9–14 (Proverbs 9; *Pimander* quoted in Ficino's Latin; Zohar); F. Secret suggests Egidius of Viterbo as intermediary for the deformed Sephiroth names.
- **Principle.** The whole system rests on seven pillars = the seven Sephiroth of the supercelestial world = the seven measures running through the celestial and elemental worlds = the Platonic Ideas, so that memory is founded on first causes as 'eternal places' rather than the orators' 'frail places'. Correlations (table p. 148, columns scrambled in extraction but recoverable): Luna–Marcut–Gabriel; Mercury–Iesod–Michael; Venus–Hod and Nisach–Honiel; Sol–Tipheret–Raphael; Mars–Gabiarah–Camael; Jupiter–Chased–Zadchiel; Saturn–Bina–Zaphkiel. Kether and Hokmah omitted ("not going above Bina, to which Moses ascended").
- **Chapter / pages.** VI, pp. 137–138, 145–146, 148–149.
- **Quotations.** "By these columns, signifying most stable eternity, we are to understand the seven Sephiroth of the supercelestial world, which are the seven measures of the fabric of the celestial and inferior worlds" (Camillo, p. 137) — "Camillo is basing his memory system on first causes, on the Sephiroth, on the Ideas; these are to be the 'eternal places' of his memory." (p. 137) — "He has left out the two highest Sephiroth, Kether and Hokmah." (p. 149)
- **Figures.** Shown on the lowest gates of the Folder plan (characters, names, Sephiroth, angels).
- **Related.** Paolini quotes the same angel names (p. 167); Bruno replaces Cabala's ten with his Thirty (entry 56; p. 229); Lull's Dignities as B to K (entry 37, p. 197); Lavinheta/Salzinger's celestial seven (entry 50).

## 24. The first grade: the seven planets

- **Name.** The planetary images and characters of the first (lowest) grade; Sol represented by a pyramid, Apollo above.
- **Author, date, source.** *L'Idea del Theatro*, p. 11.
- **Principle.** Because the Sephiroth are remote, the seven planets, with their strongly differentiated images and characters, occupy the lowest grade; once "organically grasped" the mind moves up into the Sephiroth and angels or down into the elemental world ranged on the upper grades. The Sun series is inverted: a pyramid (the Trinity) on grade one, Apollo on grade two.
- **Chapter / pages.** VI, p. 138; 151.
- **Quotations.** "he places, not these, but the seven planets on the first grade of the Theatre, for the planets are nearer to us and their images are better grasped as memory images, being strikingly differentiated from one another." (p. 138) — "To bring out the importance of Sol, he varies the arrangement in this case by representing the Sun on the first grade by the image of a pyramid" (p. 138).
- **Figures.** Folder plan.
- **Related.** The Sun series (entry 28); the planetary gods as "basic images" with affective appeal (p. 144).

## 25. The six upper grades: Banquet, Cave, Gorgon Sisters, Pasiphe and the Bull, Sandals of Mercury, Prometheus

- **Name.** The seven grades of the Theatre (the general image repeated on each of a grade's seven gates).
- **Author, date, source.** *L'Idea del Theatro*, pp. 17, 29, 53, 62, 67–68, 76, 79, 81; Homer, Porphyry, Hesiod, Zohar, *Pimander*, *Asclepius*; *Lettera del rivolgimento dell'huomo a Dio* on the three souls.
- **Principle.** Grade 2, the Banquet of Ocean to the gods: the simple elements, first day of creation. Grade 3, the Homeric Cave of the Nymphs: mixtures, *elementata*. Grade 4, the three Gorgon Sisters with one eye: the interior man, the three souls Nessamah, Ruach, Nephes (the eye = *intellectus agens*, Golden Bough image). Grade 5, Pasiphe and the Bull: soul joined to body; a Bull alone on each gate for the body parts under the zodiac. Grade 6, the Sandals of Mercury: natural operations without art. Grade 7, Prometheus with a torch: all arts, sciences, religion and law. The universe expands from first causes through the days of creation.
- **Chapter / pages.** VI, pp. 139–141, 146–147, 149–150.
- **Quotations.** "'The second grade of the Theatre will have depicted on all its gates the same image, and this will be a banquet.'" (p. 139) — "'The seventh grade is assigned to all the arts, both noble and vile, and above each gate is Prometheus with a lighted torch.'" (p. 141) — "Thus Camillo's Theatre represents the universe expanding from First Causes through the stages of creation." (p. 141)
- **Figures.** Folder plan (grade image named at the top of each gate).
- **Related.** Compared with Lull's B to K changing meaning by level (p. 197) and with Bruno's wheels (p. 229); Pico's *Heptaplus* days of creation (p. 150).

## 26. The planetary series: the same image changing meaning by grade

- **Name.** The seven planetary series (Jupiter, Saturn, Luna, Mercury, Venus, Mars, Sol) read up the gangways.
- **Author, date, source.** *L'Idea del Theatro*, pp. 11–12 (forest and hill), and the series passim; Klibansky–Panofsky–Saxl on Saturn; Macrobius/Panofsky on the three-headed time sign.
- **Principle.** An image keeps its theme but changes meaning on each grade: in the Jupiter series Juno suspended = air (simple under the Banquet, mixed under the Cave, breathing under the Sandals, windmills under Prometheus); the Three Graces = useful things, beneficent nature, exercising benevolence; Stork and Caduceus = the Jovial mind. In the Saturn series Cybele = earth (up to geometry, agriculture), the Solitary Sparrow = Saturnine sadness, Hercules and Antaeus = struggle to contemplation, the wolf–lion–dog heads = time, Pandora = ill fortune, the Ass = porterage. Luna uses Neptune for water, Mars uses Vulcan for fire. A talismanic planetary power is supposed to run through each series (entry 29).
- **Chapter / pages.** VI, pp. 141–143.
- **Quotations.** "The changing meaning of an image on different grades, without losing its basic theme, is a carefully thought out characteristic of the imagery of the Theatre." (p. 142) — "The wood is our inferior world; the slope is the heavens; the hill is the supercelestial world." (Camillo, p. 143) — "Once the method is understood, it can be followed in all the other planetary series." (p. 143)
- **Figures.** Folder plan.
- **Related.** Ficino's fluctuating Three Graces possibly explained by grades (p. 161); Lull's B to K (p. 197); Dantesque vestiges (entry 31).

## 27. The drawers: Ciceronian speeches stored under the images

- **Name.** The 'little boxes', drawers, coffers or 'volumes' beneath the images; Yates: "a highly ornamental filing cabinet".
- **Author, date, source.** Viglius's letters to Erasmus (1532); *L'Idea del Theatro*, p. 68 ("volumes containing things and words"); Bording to Dolet (1534): "for the purpose of marking out divisions of memory".
- **Principle.** Under each image are receptacles holding papers with speeches based on Cicero relating to the subject the image recalls; Camillo drew them out and recited them. Yates reads this as a new interpretation of memory for 'things' and 'words': the image is the thing, the stored text the words. The written material was lost (Citolini suspected of stealing it). The Theatre is thus a Ciceronian orator's memory in the *volgare*.
- **Chapter / pages.** VI, pp. 131, 144–145; VII, p. 166.
- **Quotations.** "under the images there were drawers, or boxes, or coffers of some kind containing masses of papers, and on these papers were speeches, based on the works of Cicero" (p. 144) — "He had hit upon a new interpretation of memory for 'things' and 'words' by storing written speeches under the images" (p. 144) — "the drawers under the images contained Ciceronian speeches." (p. 166)
- **Figures.** None (not shown on plan).
- **Related.** Dantesque coffers "full of sermons" (entry 31); Paolini's magically activated speeches (entry 35).

## 28. The Sun series: Ficinian solar magic in memory

- **Name.** The great central series of Sol/Apollo; the 'light' series Sol, Lux, Lumen, Splendor, Color, Generatio.
- **Author, date, source.** *L'Idea del Theatro*, pp. 20–21, 38–39; Ficino, *De sole*, *De vita coelitus comparanda*; *Corpus Hermeticum* V and XII; Proclus on cock and lion.
- **Principle.** The Sun as *statua Dei* and image of the Trinity heads the series (pyramid, then Apollo with the descending light hierarchy); Argus on the Cave grade = the world vivified by the astral spirit; the Golden Chain under the Sandals = going to, taking in, stretching toward the sun; the Cock and Lion carry the lion story proving Camillo's 'solar virtue'. The spiritus from the Sun is identified with the spirit of Christ. Yates reads it as an inner movement toward heliocentricity.
- **Chapter / pages.** VI, pp. 151–154.
- **Quotations.** "Camillo's suns series shows a typically Ficinian combination of sun mysticism with magical solarianism." (p. 152) — "The Sun series of the Theatre shows within the mind and memory of a man of the Renaissance the Sun looming with a new importance, mystical, emotional, magical" (p. 153) — "'This can only mean that this animal recognised that there was much of Solar Virtue in him.'" (Camillo, p. 152)

- **Related.** Bruno's inner Sun at the centre of the wheel of ideal intentions (entry 67); Copernicus quoting the *Asclepius* (p. 153).

## 29. Inner talismans and the magic of proportion

- **Name.** Memory images as "inner talismans"; the 'secret' of the Theatre; magic of perfect proportion (from the *Discorso in materia del suo Theatro*).
- **Author, date, source.** Yates's interpretation, pp. 154–157, from Ficino's *De vita coelitus comparanda*, *Picatrix*, the *Asclepius* god-making passages; Camillo, *Discorso* (*Tutte le opere*, 1552, p. 33); Pietro Passi's accusation (1614).
- **Principle.** The basic planetary images are supposed to have talismanic virtue, drawing astral spiritus into memory, and that power runs through the subsidiary images of each series, unifying memory from its 'causes'. Camillo's own gloss on the Egyptian statues locates the magic in perfect proportion: a statue or a composition of words brought to perfect proportion becomes animated. So the magic of the memory image would consist in its proportions.
- **Chapter / pages.** VI, pp. 154–158; VII, p. 168.
- **Quotations.** "The secret of this is, I believe, that the memory images were regarded as, so to speak, inner talismans." (p. 154) — "the basic planetary images are supposed to be talismans, or to have talismanic virtue, and that the astral power from them is supposed to run through the subsidiary images" (p. 155) — "a perfectly proportioned statue becomes animated with a spirit, becomes a magic statue." (p. 156)
- **Figures.** None.
- **Related.** Bruno's *sigilli* and 150 magic images of much "deeper dye" (entries 56–58, 66); Paolini's magical oratory through proportion (entry 35); Vitruvian proportion (entry 36).

## 30. Ficino's precept: order, proportion, harmony; the 'image of the world'; Pico's theatre of the world

- **Name.** Ficino's memory precepts (letter); the *imago mundi* of *De vita coelitus comparanda*; the *mundana scaena* of Pico's *Oration*.
- **Author, date, source.** Ficino, *Opera*, p. 616 (Kristeller, *Suppl. Fic.* I, p. 39); *De vita coelitus comparanda*; Pico, *De hominis dignitate* opening (Abdullah the Saracen).
- **Principle.** Ficino adds proportion, harmony and connection to the classical demand for order, so that matters "digested into a series" recall one another by natural necessity; his projected 'image of the world' supplies the materials for an astral memory building; Pico's "theatre of the world" may have suggested the theatre form. Yates concludes Camillo developed, rather than invented, occult memory.
- **Chapter / pages.** VII, pp. 160–162.
- **Quotations.** "Aristotle and Simonides think it useful to observe a certain order in memorising. And indeed an order contains proportion, harmony and connection." (Ficino, p. 160) — "Proportion and harmony are, so far as I know, new and significant Ficinian additions to the memory tradition." (pp. 160–161) — "it is, I think, unlikely that the occult memory system was invented by Camillo." (p. 162)
- **Figures.** None.
- **Related.** Gesualdo opens with Ficino (entry 34); Camillo's Theatre (entry 22).

## 31. Dantesque vestiges in the Theatre: Hell, Purgatory, Earthly Paradise, Paradise on the Banquet grade

## 32. Dolce's *Dialogo*: Romberch in Ciceronian Italian, with Titian as memory image

## 33. Rossellius: notable practitioners placed as images of the arts, sciences and virtues

## 34. Gesualdo's *Plutosofia*: memory on three levels

## 35. Paolini's *Hebdomades*: planetary oratory

- **Name.** *Hebdomades* (Venice 1589), discourses of the Accademia degli Uranici (founded 1587); 'planetary oratory'.

- **Quotations.** "a kind of 'planetary oratory' was envisaged which should produce effects on its hearers, like the fabled effects of ancient music" (p. 168) — "the Theatre also magically activated the speeches which the orator remembered by it, infusing them with planetary virtue through which they would have magical effects on the hearers." (p. 168) — "The Hebdomades might take the place of the great work explaining the background of his Theatre which Camillo himself never wrote." (p. 168)

## 36. The Vitruvian theatre as an image of the zodiac (Barbaro and Palladio)

- **Name.** The classical theatre of Vitruvius, *De architectura* V, 6, with four triangles inscribed in the circle; "Palladio's Reconstruction of the Roman Theatre" (Pl. 9a); the Teatro Olimpico (Pl. 9b).
- **Author, date, source.** Daniele Barbaro's commentary on Vitruvius (Venice 1556; ed. 1567, p. 188), illustrations influenced by Palladio; Alberti's 'via regia'.
- **Principle.** Seven gangways and five stage doors are fixed by the apices of four equilateral triangles in a circle, the astrologers' trigona in the zodiac; so the theatre's form mirrors the twelve signs. Camillo distorted this plan by moving the decorated doors from the *frons scaenae* to the gangways, but knew the astrological theory, so the Theatre reflects divine proportions in its architecture as well as its imagery.
- **Chapter / pages.** VII, pp. 170–172; VI, p. 136 (n. 27); Pl. 9a, 9b.
- **Quotations.** "The circular form of the theatre thus reflects the zodiac, and the seven entrances to the auditorium and the five entrances to the stage correspond to positions of the twelve signs" (p. 171) — "He would think of his Memory Theatre of the World as magically reflecting the divine world proportions in its architecture as well as in its imagery." (p. 171) — "Camillo's Memory Theatre is however a distortion of the plan of the real Vitruvian theatre." (p. 136)
- **Figures.** Pl. 9a (Barbaro/Palladio plan), Pl. 9b (Teatro Olimpico, Vicenza).
- **Related.** Camillo's Theatre (entry 22); the memory theatre tradition Yates carries on to Fludd later in the book.

## 37. The Art of Ramon Lull

- **Name.** The Lullian Art (*Ars Magna* / *Ars generalis ultima*, 1305–8; *Ars brevis*; earlier versions from c. 1272); 'ars Raymundi'.
- **Author, date, source.** Ramon Lull (c. 1235–1316), after the illumination on Mount Randa; Yates's own articles 'The Art of R.L.' and 'R.L. and S.E.'; Pseudo-Dionysius, Scotus Erigena.
- **Principle.** Founded on the Dignitates Dei (Bonitas, Magnitudo, Eternitas, Potestas, Sapientia, Voluntas, Virtus, Veritas, Gloria = B C D E F G H I K in the nine-form) as primordial causes issuing into the four elements; used by all three powers of the soul, as intellectus (finding truth), voluntas (loving it) and memoria (remembering it). No images: concepts carried by letter notation on geometrical figures, some of which revolve to give combinations. A missionary Art meant to demonstrate the Trinity from premisses common to Jews, Muslims and Christians.
- **Chapter / pages.** VIII, pp. 173–179.
- **Quotations.** "As intellectus, it was an art of knowing or finding out truth; as voluntas it was an art of training the will towards loving truth; as memoria, it was an art of memory for remembering truth." (p. 174) — "Lull designates the concepts used in his art by a letter notation, which introduces an almost algebraic or scientifically abstract note into Lullism." (p. 176) — "Lull introduces movement into memory. The figures of his Art, on which its concepts are set out in the letter notation, are not static but revolving." (p. 176)
- **Figures.** Figs. 4–7, Pl. 10 (following entries).
- **Related.** Contrasted point by point with the classical art (pp. 175–176); associated with the Franciscans as the classical art with the Dominicans (p. 175); taken up by Cusanus, Ficino's circle, Pico (pp. 187–188); fused with occult classical memory by Bruno (entries 56–65); 'Raymundus on the nine elements' on Bruno's wheel (p. 221).

## 38. The ladder of being: *ars ascendendi et descendendi*, the nine subjects

- **Name.** The ladder (scala) of creation; "The Ladder of Ascent and Descent" (Fig. 4); "Ramon Lull with the Ladders of his Art" (Pl. 10).
- **Author, date, source.** Lull, *Liber de ascensu et descensu intellectus* (ed. Valencia 1512); alphabet of the *Ars brevis*; Karlsruhe miniature (Cod. St. Peter 92).
- **Principle.** The nine subjects Deus, Angelus, Coelum, Homo, Imaginativa, Sensitiva, Vegetativa, Elementativa, Instrumentativa are rungs; B (Bonitas) and the rest are abstracted on each level (bonitas as Dignitas Dei; of an angel; of Aries and the signs and Saturn and the planets; in man; in imagination; in a lion; in the pepper plant; in fire; in the virtues, arts and sciences). - **Quotations.** "Bearing the geometrical figures of the Art, inscribed with their letter notations, the 'artista' ascends and descends on the ladder of being, measuring out the same proportions on each level." (p. 181) — "Lull's peculiarity is the selection of a certain number of Dignitates Dei and to find these descending in a precisely calculable manner, almost like chemical ingredients, on the grades of creation." (p. 181) — "on reaching the summit with Deus, the Intellect enters the House of Wisdom." (p. 181)
- **Figures.** Fig. 4 (cut of 1512 with tree, lion, man, stars, angel on the steps); Pl. 10 (14th-c. miniature).
- **Related.** Camillo's images changing meaning by grade (p. 197); Bruno's 'ladder' from *De la causa* and the Thirty passing through the three worlds (pp. 228–229); the Liber ad memoriam's ascending and descending (entry 48).

## 39. The A figure

- **Name.** 'A' Figure (Fig. 5).
- **Author, date, source.** Lull, *Ars brevis* (Opera, Strasburg 1617).
- **Principle.** B to K set on a wheel and joined by triangulations; a mystical figure for meditating the relations of the Divine Names with one another in the Godhead, before extension into creation, as aspects of the Trinity. Static.
- **Chapter / pages.** VIII, p. 181; Fig. 5.
- **Quotations.** "The A figure (Fig. 5) shows B to K set out on a wheel and joined by complex triangulations." (p. 181) — "This is a mystical figure in which we meditate on the complex relations of the Names with one another as they are in the Godhead" (p. 181).
- **Figures.** Fig. 5.
- **Related.** The T figure (entry 40); combinatory figure (entry 41).

## 40. The T figure

- **Name.** 'T' Figure (the relata of the Art).
- **Author, date, source.** Lull, *Ars brevis*.
- **Principle.** The relata *differentia, concordia, contrarietas; principium, medium, finis; majoritas, equalitas, minoritas* set out as triangles within a circle; a revolving figure whose triangles "pick up related concepts" and hold the Trinitarian structure on every level.
- **Chapter / pages.** VIII, pp. 176, 181–182.
- **Quotations.** "The T figure shows the relata of the Art … set out as triangles within a circle. Through the triangulations of the relata the Trinitarian structure of the Art is maintained on every level." (pp. 181–182) — "In another revolving figure, triangles within a circle pick up related concepts." (p. 176)
- **Figures.** Not reproduced.
- **Related.** Entries 39, 41, 42.

## 41. The combinatory figure: the *ars combinatoria*

- **Name.** Combinatory Figure (Fig. 6); the revolving concentric wheels; "the renowned ars combinatoria in its simplest form".
- **Author, date, source.** Lull, *Ars brevis*; derived, as Thorndike showed, from cosmological 'rotae' (Bede's *De natura rerum* diagrams).
- **Principle.** A stationary outer circle inscribed B to K, with concentric inner circles similarly inscribed revolving within it; as they turn, combinations of the letters are read off. This is the device that "breaks up the static schemata into new combinations" and makes Lullism "more scientific" than the image-stocked buildings.
- **Chapter / pages.** VIII, pp. 176, 178, 182; Fig. 6.
- **Quotations.** "The outer circle, inscribed B to K, is stationary and within it revolve circles similarly inscribed and concentric with it. As the circles revolve, combinations of the letters B to K can be read off." (p. 182) — "think of Lullism, with its algebraic notations, breaking up the static schemata into new combinations on its revolving wheels." (p. 176) — "the derivation from cosmological 'rotae' of the wheels of the Art is obvious" (p. 178).
- **Figures.** Fig. 6.
- **Related.** Pico's Cabalist *ars combinandi* (entry 46); Pseudo-Lullian alchemical wheels (entry 47); Bruno's thirty-lettered wheels bearing images (entries 56–57); Dee's thirty concentric circles (p. 211).

## 42. Circle, square and triangle: the geometry of the Art (and the hieroglyphic letters)

- **Name.** The three geometrical figures of the Art; the allegory of Circle, Square and Triangle (*Arbor scientiae*); the elements moving 'quadrangulariter, circulariter, et triangulariter'; the 'Figure of Solomon' (*Nova geometria*).
- **Author, date, source.** Lull, *Arbor scientiae* (Obres essentials I, p. 829); *Ars demonstrativa* Elemental Figures (Yates, 'La teoria luliana de los elementos'); *Nova geometria*, ed. Millás Vallicrosa.
- **Principle.** Square = the elements, circle = the heavens (Aries and his brothers, Saturn and his brothers), triangle = the divinity/Trinity and soul; the logical square of opposition is identified with the square of the elements, giving a 'natural' logic. Yates locates whatever "abstract or geometrical magic" Lullism may hold in these figures and in the letters, which "would have a hieroglyphic as well as a purely notatory value".
- **Chapter / pages.** VIII, pp. 178, 182–183, 196–197 (nn. 41–42).
- **Quotations.** "The square is the elements; the circle, the heavens; and the triangle, the divinity." (p. 182) — "The logical square of opposition is identified in Lull's mind with the square of the elements, hence his belief that he has found a 'natural' logic" (p. 178) — "Where there might be, however, an element of abstract or geometrical magic in Lullism would be in the figures themselves" (p. 196).
- **Figures.** None.
- **Related.** Bruno's conjuring wheels (entry 56).

## 43. The Lullian Art as *memoria*: memorising the Art

- **Name.** Lullian artificial memory / *Ars memorativa* (Lull's own terms); the 'Tree of Memory'; the trilogy *De memoria, De intellectu, De voluntate*; the three damsels of the *Book of Contemplation*.
- **Author, date, source.** Lull, *Arbor scientiae* 'Tree of Man' (Obres essentials I, p. 619); *De memoria* (Paris BN Lat. 16116, unpublished); *Arbre de filosofia desiderat*; *Liber contemplationis* (Opera Mainz X, p. 530); Aristotle, *De memoria* on mathematical order.
- **Principle.** Since the three powers of the Augustinian soul are one, the Art as memory consists in memorising the whole Art, its principles, terminology and operations, as intellect and will; the diagrams are in some sense 'places'; memory thereby becomes a method of logical investigation asking 'questions' of every subject, quite unlike the classical art that "seeks only to memorise what is given". No striking images, no corporeal similitudes.
- **Chapter / pages.** VIII, pp. 183–186.
- **Quotations.** "the Lullian Art as memory consists in memorising the Art as a whole, in all its aspects and operations." (p. 184) — "'We have spoken of memory and given the doctrine for artificial memory that it may attain its objects artificially.'" (Lull, p. 184) — "memory in memorising such procedures is becoming a method of investigation, and a method of logical investigation." (p. 185)
- **Figures.** None.
- **Related.** *Liber ad memoriam confirmandam* (entry 48); Lavinheta (entry 49); the *voluntas* element in Bruno's thirty intentions (entry 67, p. 227).

## 44. Tree diagrams as place system: the *Arbor scientiae*

- **Name.** The Tree; "Tree Diagram" (Fig. 7); the forest of trees of the *Arbor scientiae*.
- **Author, date, source.** Lull, *Arbor scientiae* (ed. Lyons 1515).
- **Principle.** The whole encyclopaedia is schematised as trees whose roots are B to K and the relata; branches and leaves bear abstract formulae and classifications; there are trees of Heaven and Hell and of virtues and vices, worked "with the scientific precision of elemental compounds" (vices 'devicted' by virtues). The nearest Lull comes to classical visual places; no striking images.
- **Chapter / pages.** VIII, pp. 186–187; Fig. 7.
- **Quotations.** "The tree, as he uses it, is a kind of place system." (p. 187) — "Their branches and leaves are decorated only with abstract formulae and classifications." (p. 187) — "memorisation from diagrams, figures, and schematisations is a kind of visual memory." (p. 186)
- **Figures.** Fig. 7.
- **Related.** Pseudo-Lullian alchemical tree with wheels (entry 47); the Tree of Memory (entry 43).

## 45. Lullian astral science and astral medicine on the level *coelum*

- **Name.** The Art done on the level coelum; Lullian medicine; *Tractatus de astronomia*; Bruno's *Medicina Lulliana*.
- **Author, date, source.** Lull, *Tractatus de astronomia*, *Liber de regionibus sanitatis et infirmitatis*; Bruno, *Medicina Lulliana* (Op. lat. III) and *De lampade combinatoria* preface (Paracelsus accused of borrowing from Lull); Pirovanus, *Defensio astronomiae* (Milan 1507) quotes the *Tractatus*.
- **Principle.** The twelve signs and seven planets are manipulated in combination with B to K on the figures, yielding a benevolent astral science and astral medicine distinct from judicial astrology; wholly abstract, using no images of planets, signs or constellations.
- **Chapter / pages.** VIII, pp. 178, 187–188 (nn. 20–22), 196.
- **Quotations.** "When the Art is done on the level coelum, it becomes a manipulation of the twelve signs of the zodiac and the seven planets, in combination with B to K, to form a kind of benevolent astral science" (p. 187) — "He does his astral science in a completely abstract and imageless way, with geometrical figures and letter notations." (p. 196)
- **Figures.** None.
- **Related.** Salzinger's Secret (entry 50); Bruno's star images on wheels supply exactly the imagery Lull excluded (entry 51).

## 46. Cabalist letter-combination (Abulafia) and the Pseudo-Lullian *De auditu kabbalistico*

- **Name.** The Cabalist *ars combinandi* with revolving alphabets; *De auditu kabbalistico* (Venice 1518, 1533).
- **Author, date, source.** Spanish Cabala of Lull's time (Abraham Abulafia; Scholem); Pico, *Conclusions* and *Apology* (Opera, Bâle 1572, p. 180); Pseudo-Lull (real author now known, Kristeller/Batllori); Bruno lists it among Lull's works (p. 209).
- **Principle.** Combining and recombining the letters of the sacred Hebrew alphabet to form the Names of God; Yates suggests Lull adapted this to Gentile use with B to K. Pico declared the Cabalist combinatory art like the ars Raymundi; the *De auditu* then does the Lullian Art with the normal figures but calls it Cabala and identifies B to K with the Sephiroth and angel names.
- **Chapter / pages.** VIII, pp. 177 (n. 6), 188–189.
- **Quotations.** "A form of Cabalist meditation particularly developed in Spain at this time consisted in meditating on the letters of the Hebrew alphabet, combining them and recombining them to form the Names of God." (p. 177) — "Pico states that one type of Cabala is an ars combinandi, done with revolving alphabets, and he further states that this art is like 'that which is called amongst us the ars Raymundi'" (p. 189) — "Lullism is now called Cabalism and B to K are more or less identified with Cabalist Sephiroth and associated with Cabalist angel names." (p. 189)
- **Figures.** None.
- **Related.** Camillo may have known the Cabalist Lull (p. 197); Bruno's Thirty as Cabalised Dignities (entry 56); Dee's *Clavis angelicae* (p. 211).

## 47. Pseudo-Lullian alchemy figures (and the Pseudo-Lullian rhetoric)

- **Name.** Figures of the Pseudo-Lullian alchemical works; the *Testament* (quoted by Camillo); *In Rhetoricen Isagoge* (Paris 1515, really by Remigius Rufus).
- **Author, date, source.** 14th–15th-c. treatises under Lull's name; diagram illustrated in F. Sherwood Taylor, *The Alchemists*.
- **Principle.** Combinatory wheels marked with letters at the root of a Lullian tree, with wheels of the twelve signs and seven planets at its top; more letters than any genuine Art. Possibly developed from the 'Tree of the Elements' and 'Tree of Heaven' of the *Arbor scientiae*. The Renaissance accepted them, making Lull a Magus; the rhetoric ends with an oration "mystically covering the whole universe".
- **Chapter / pages.** VIII, pp. 189–190 (nn. 26–28), 197.
- **Quotations.** "we see what look like combinatory wheels marked with letters at the root of a Lullian type of tree diagram; at the top of the tree are wheels marked with the twelve signs and the seven planets." (p. 190) — "So we see the Renaissance Lull building up as a kind of Magus, versed in the Cabalist and Hermetic sciences cultivated in the occult tradition." (p. 190)
- **Figures.** Not reproduced.
- **Related.** Bruno's wheel "is closer to those to be seen in Pseudo-Lullian alchemical diagrams" (p. 209); Camillo "thought of Lull as an alchemist" (p. 197).

## 48. *Liber ad memoriam confirmandam*: Lull's memory treatise

- **Name.** *Liber ad memoriam confirmandam* (Pisa, c. 1308); the 'Book of the Seven Planets' it refers to.
- **Author, date, source.** Ramon Lull, written at San Donnino, Pisa, with the *Ars generalis ultima* and *Ars brevis*; five MSS (Munich, Vatican, Milan, Paris); ed. Rossi, *Clavis universalis*, pp. 261–70.
- **Principle.** Memory is natural (received at generation and according to the reigning planet) or artificial; artificial memory is of two kinds, medicines and plasters (not recommended) and frequent going-over "like an ox chewing the cud" (Aristotle's *De memoria*, Thomas's fourth rule). The 'Tullian' places and images are pointedly omitted. What is to be remembered is the Art itself (prayers to Bonitas etc. as *voluntas*; ascent and descent, *discretio* as *intellectus*). Three times the reader is sent to the 'Book of the Seven Planets' for the real key.
- **Chapter / pages.** VIII, pp. 191–195 (nn. 29–38).
- **Quotations.** "'Natural memory', he continues, 'is that which a man receives in creation or generation, and according to what influence he receives from the reigning planet'" (p. 192) — "The other kind consists in frequently going over in memory what one wishes to retain, like an ox chewing the cud." (p. 192) — "But he deliberately leaves out the 'Tullian' rules." (p. 192)
- **Figures.** None.
- **Related.** Quoted by Lavinheta (entry 49); Salzinger's identification (entry 50); Bartolomeo da San Concordio's Pisan Dominican memory as the contrast (p. 193).

## 49. Lavinheta: the classical art for *sensibilia*, the Lullian Art for *intelligibilia*

- **Name.** Lavinheta's appendix on memory (*Explanatio compendiosaque applicatio artis Raymundi Lulli*, Lyons 1523; Alsted's ed. 1612, pp. 653–6).
- **Author, date, source.** Bernardus de Lavinheta, first holder of the chair of Lullism at the Sorbonne.
- **Principle.** Things to be remembered are divided into sensibilia, for which the classical places and images are briefly taught, and intelligibilia, speculative matters remote from sense and imagination, for which the *Ars generalis* of the Doctor Illuminatus "collects all things in his places, comprehending much in little". A misuse of scholastic terms that makes the classical art the inferior discipline.
- **Chapter / pages.** VIII, p. 194 (n. 37).
- **Quotations.** "'speculative matters which are far remote not only from the senses but even from the imagination one must proceed by another method of remembering. And for this is necessary the Ars generalis of our Doctor Illuminatus'" (p. 194) — "Lavinheta makes the classical art an inferior discipline used only for remembering 'sensibilia', whilst the higher 'intelligibilia' are to be remembered by a different Art, that of Lullism." (p. 194)
- **Figures.** None.
- **Related.** Paris as centre of Lullism when Bruno published *Shadows* (p. 208); Remigius Rufus his disciple (entry 47).

## 50. Salzinger's 'Secret of the Art': memory based on the celestial seven

- **Name.** 'Revelatio Secretorum Artis' (Mainz ed. of Lull, 1721–42, I, p. 154); the Book of the Seven Planets identified with the *Tractatus de astronomia*; the *ars memorandi* of the seven instruments.
- **Author, date, source.** Ivo Salzinger, 18th c., quoting the *Tractatus de astronomia* and the *Liber ad memoriam confirmandam*.
- **Principle.** The seven planets are 'seven instruments' through which all secrets of the Art are retained; Salzinger reads the fifth subject 'B C D' as coelum and takes the astral-elemental theory of the *Tractatus* as the key to memory. Yates: if the 16th century read Lull so, it found in him the basing of memory on the celestial seven that marks Camillo's Theatre, though without star images.
- **Chapter / pages.** VIII, pp. 195–196 (nn. 39–40).
- **Quotations.** "An ars memorandi, 'through which you will retain all the secrets of this Art disclosed in these seven instruments (the seven planets)'." (p. 196) — "Salzinger unhesitatingly identifies this book as the Tractatus de Astronomia." (p. 196) — "it might therefore have found in Lullism the basing of memory on the celestial 'seven' which is the outstanding feature of Camillo's Theatre." (p. 196)
- **Figures.** None.
- **Related.** Camillo's seven pillars (entry 23); Paolini's sevens (entry 35); Metrodorus as the other celestial authority (entry 12).

## 51. The Renaissance programme: magic images of the stars on the Lullian wheels

- **Name.** The projected fusion of Lullism with occultised classical memory (Yates's framing at the close of Ch. VIII); traces of the Renaissance Lull in Camillo's Theatre.
- **Author, date, source.** Yates, pp. 197–198; *L'Idea del Theatro*, p. 18 (Lull's *Testament* quoted).
- **Principle.** Lullism excludes images; Camillo's imagery belongs to the rhetoric tradition, astralised; the "grand Renaissance aim" is to set magic star images on the revolving Lullian figures. The Theatre shows only anticipations (planets extending into Sephiroth; images changing meaning by grade like B to K), and remains "static in a building".
- **Chapter / pages.** VIII, pp. 196–198.
- **Quotations.** "it was to be a grand Renaissance aim to bring together Lullism and the classical art of memory by using magic images of the stars on the Lullian figures." (p. 197) — "Memory may be already dynamised by magic images in the Theatre; but it is still static in a building." (p. 198) — "We are about to meet the master mind who will place magic images of the stars on the revolving combinatory wheels of Lullism" (p. 198).
- **Figures.** None.
- **Related.** Garzoni's Rossellius-plus-Lull ambition (entry 55); Bruno's *Shadows* (entries 56–65).

## 52. Bruno's memory works and the *Ars memoriae* of *Shadows*: *subjectus* and *adjectus*

- **Name.** Yates's short titles: *Shadows* (*De umbris idearum … ad internam scripturam*, Paris 1582), *Circe* (*Cantus Circaeus*, Paris 1582), *Seals* (*Ars reminiscendi … Explicatio triginta sigillorum … Sigillus sigillorum*, England 1583), *Statues* (*Lampas triginta statuarum*, c. 1587), *Images* (*De imaginum, signorum et idearum compositione*, Frankfort 1591). The Ars memoriae inside *Shadows* renames locus 'subjectus' and image 'adjectus'.
- **Author, date, source.** Giordano Bruno (1548–1600), Dominican at Naples 1563–76; Op. lat. II (i), pp. 1–77 etc.; his testimony to the Venetian Inquisitors on Henri III and Mocenigo; the St Victor librarian's note (psalm Fundamenta recited in Hebrew before Pius V).
- **Principle.** *Shadows*, *Circe* and *Seals* each contain an 'art of memory' on the old plan of rules for places and rules for images, with all the classical precepts and their accrued elaborations, under new terminology and a philosophy of the magically animated imagination; the *Circe* treatise (introduced by the sorceress's incantations, using Porta's animal physiognomies) is reprinted in *Seals*. Presented by Hermes to Philotheus against Logifer the pedant as a rising Egyptian sun.
- **Chapter / pages.** IX, pp. 199–204, 208; the *sigilli* at p. 223 (entry 66).
- **Quotations.** "The treatise in Shadows alters the old terminology calling the locus, the subjectus, and the image, the adjectus, but the ancient division of the two aspects of memory training is perfectly perceptible beneath this new guise" (p. 202) — "This book is the successor to Camillo's Theatre and Bruno is another Italian bringing a memory 'secret' to another King of France." (p. 200) — "in Circe he introduces the art of memory with fiercely magical incantations uttered by the sorceress." (p. 208)
- **Figures.** Pl. 11, 12a–b, Fig. 8 (entries 56–58).
- **Related.** Romberch–Rossellius congestions (p. 199); Thomas's imageless caution versus Albertus's "huge ram in the night skies" and the possible Albertist development in the Naples convent (pp. 204–205); *Seals*, *Statues*, *Images* treated in later chapters.

## 53. Porta's *Ars reminiscendi*

- **Name.** *Ars reminiscendi* (Naples 1602), Latin version of *L'arte del ricordare* (Naples 1566).
- **Author, date, source.** Giovanni Battista Porta, founder of the Academia Secretorum Naturae (1560), author of *Magia naturalis* (1558); Clubb suggests mnemonics for actors.
- **Principle.** Imagination draws images as with a pencil in memory; natural and artificial memory (Simonides); Dido's painted rooms in Virgil read as her memory system; places are palaces or theatres, or mathematical and geometrical figures for their order (Aristotle); images are striking human figures, best taken from pictures by Michelangelo, Raphael, Titian; Egyptian hieroglyphs may serve; images for letters and numbers. No overt magic; Metrodorus condemned for using the stars.
- **Chapter / pages.** IX, pp. 205–206 (n. 24).
- **Quotations.** "Porta regards Virgil's description of the rooms painted with pictures which Dido showed to Aeneas as really Dido's memory system" (p. 206) — "It is useful to take pictures by good artists as memory images for these are more striking and move more than pictures by ordinary painters." (p. 206) — "We might be reading Romberch or Rossellius, except that there is nothing about remembering Hell and Heaven." (p. 206)
- **Figures.** None (Porta's number images in Volkmann Pl. 194).
- **Related.** Dolce's Titian (entry 32); visual alphabets (entry 15); Porta's physiognomies and ciphers used by Bruno (p. 205).

## 54. Agrippa: the 'vanity' of the art and the image-lists of *De occulta philosophia*

- **Name.** *De vanitate scientiarum* (1530), cap. X; *De occulta philosophia* (1533), Book II, chs. 37–46 (celestial magic).
- **Author, date, source.** Henry Cornelius Agrippa.
- **Principle.** The *De vanitate* chapter condemns the art (Simonides invented, Metrodorus perfected it; the modern treatises "an unworthy catalogue by obscure men"), read by Yates as a safety device. The *De occulta philosophia* second book supplies lists of the talismanic images of the 36 decans (after 'Teucer the Babylonian'), of the planets and of the 28 mansions of the moon, for operating on the middle world of the stars; Bruno copies these, with slight variations, as memory images.
- **Chapter / pages.** V, p. 124; IX, pp. 206–207, 214–215 (nn. 41–43).
- **Quotations.** "it was from Agrippa's manual of magic that Bruno took the magic images of the stars which he used in the memory system in Shadows." (p. 207) — "Bruno is transferring such operations within, applying them to memory by using the celestial images as memory images, as it were harnessing the inner world of the imagination to the stars" (p. 215) — "Most occult philosophers of the Renaissance were interested in the art of memory and it would be surprising if Agrippa were an exception." (p. 207)
- **Figures.** None.
- **Related.** Entries 58–60; Passi on Agrippa and the animated statues (p. 157).

## 55. The Paris and Venice context: Gohorry's 'images and seals'; Garzoni's universal memory of Rossellius plus Lull

- **Name.** Gohorry ('Leo Suavius'), *De usu et mysteriis notarum* (Paris 1550), describing Camillo's 'wooden amphitheatre'; Garzoni, *Piazza universale* (Venice 1578), chapter on 'Professori di memoria'.
- **Author, date, source.** Jacques Gohorry's medico-magical academy; Tommaso Garzoni.
- **Principle.** Gohorry held that Ficino and Pico had been too timid with 'images and seals', a direction in which Bruno's systems advance. Garzoni, a layman, announced the ambition to build a universal memory system combining Rossellius's Dominican text-book with Lull. Both mark the climate into which *Shadows* fell.
- **Chapter / pages.** IX, pp. 207–209 (nn. 26–27, 30).
- **Quotations.** "Gohorry gives a brief description of the 'wooden amphitheatre' which Camillo had constructed for Francis I." (p. 207) — "states that it is his ambition to produce a universal memory system combining Rossellius and Lull." (p. 209) — "The problem must have excited a good deal of general interest, comparable to the popular interest in the mind machines of today." (p. 208)
- **Figures.** None.
- **Related.** Entries 18, 37, 51, 56.

## 56. Bruno's thirty-lettered Lullian wheels: the Thirty as Cabalised Dignities; conjuring wheels

- **Name.** "Memory Wheels" (Fig. 8); the thirty letters A–Z plus Greek and Hebrew letters; the 'thirty divine attributes'; the lost *Clavis magna*; *De compendiosa architectura artis Lullii* (Paris 1582).
- **Author, date, source.** Bruno, *Shadows*; *De compendiosa architectura* (Op. lat. II (ii), p. 42); sources suggested: Trithemius's *Steganographia* (31 spirits, abstracted for Bruno as 30), Greek magical papyrus, gnostic aeons, Simon Magus; parallel in Dee's *Clavis angelicae* (Cracow 1584).
- **Principle.** Lullian concentric circles divided into thirty segments, more letters than any genuine Art and with Greek and Hebrew letters as in Pseudo-Lullian alchemical wheels. The Thirty are Lullian Dignities Cabalised as Sephiroth ("the Jewish Cabalists reduce to ten sephiroth and we to thirty"), with the Trinitarian use rejected in favour of the Tetragrammaton multiplied to the universe. Yates believes the wheels doubled as 'practical Cabala', conjuring the spirits of the air, the 'architecture' of Lullian figures replacing the memory building.
- **Chapter / pages.** IX, pp. 208–211 (nn. 30–39); Fig. 8.
- **Quotations.** "His Lullian circle has more letters on it than in any genuine Lullian art, and a few Greek and Hebrew letters, which are never used in genuine Lullism." (p. 209) — "'All these (i.e. the Lullian Dignities), the Jewish Cabalists reduce to ten sephiroth and we to thirty . . .'" (Bruno, p. 210) — "He put the images of the classical art on the Lullian combinatory wheels, but the images were magic images and the wheels were conjuring wheels." (p. 211)
- **Figures.** Fig. 8, Memory Wheels from *De umbris idearum* 1582.
- **Related.** Thirty seals in *Seals*, thirty statues in *Statues*, thirty 'links' in *De vinculis* (p. 210); the Thirty as substitute for Camillo's Cabalism, passing through the three worlds (p. 229); Dee's thirty concentric circles (p. 211).

## 57. The *Shadows* memory system: concentric wheels of 30 × 5 = 150

- **Name.** "Memory System from Giordano Bruno's De umbris idearum" (Pl. 11), Yates's reconstruction; 'the secret of Shadows'.
- **Author, date, source.** Bruno, *Shadows*, the four lists of 150 items each (Op. lat. II (i), pp. 107–8, 124–132) marked Aa, Ae, Ai, Ao, Au … through thirty letters; diagram at p. 123 showing the fives revolving independently.
- **Principle.** Each wheel is cut into thirty segments (the letters) and each segment into five (the vowels), giving 150 divisions; four lists of 150 items, all so marked, are set on concentric wheels revolving in the Lullian manner: the central wheel of star images (the 'power station'), then a wheel of natural objects and artefacts, a wheel of adjectives, and outer wheels of inventors and inventions. The five subdivisions revolve independently within the thirty compartments, so the images "form and reform in ever changing combinations". The images of the stars organise and magically animate the whole.
- **Chapter / pages.** IX, pp. 212–213, 217–218, 223–224 (n. 68); Pl. 11.
- **Quotations.** "Here are concentric wheels divided into thirty main segments, each of which is again subdivided into five, giving 150 divisions in all." (p. 212) — "No one has realised that the lists of images given in the book, each list consisting of 150 images in sets of thirty are intended to be set out on concentric wheels" (p. 212) — "The revolving wheels of the Lullian Art have become the places for the reception of the images." (p. 223)
- **Figures.** Pl. 11 (Yates's plan, "the ancient Egyptian looking object"); Fig. 8; Pl. 12a–b.
- **Related.** Direct fusion of entries 41 and the classical art; compared with Camillo's seven-fold system (pp. 229–230); Yates's "mind machine" analogy (pp. 224–225).

## 58. Central wheel (i): the thirty-six images of the decans

- **Name.** "The images of the faces of the signs from Teucer the Babylonian which can be used in the present art" (Bruno's heading); "Images of the Decans of Aries / of Taurus and Gemini" (Pl. 12a–b).
- **Author, date, source.** Bruno, *Shadows*, copied with slight variations from Agrippa, *De occulta philosophia* II, 37; the decan images descend from Egyptian sidereal gods of time via late-antique 'Hermetic' astral magic (Yates, *G.B. and H.T.*, pp. 45–8; Palazzo Schifanoja).
- **Principle.** Each sign's three 'faces' of ten degrees carry an image, 36 in all, marked Aa–Au, Ba–Bu and so on; e.g. Aries: a huge dark man with burning eyes dressed in white; a woman; a man holding a sphere and a staff; Taurus: a man ploughing; a man bearing a key; a man holding a serpent and a spear; Gemini: a servingman with a rod; a man digging and a flute-player; a man with a flute. They head the list of 150 astral images on the innermost wheel.
- **Chapter / pages.** IX, pp. 208, 212–214 (nn. 40–41); Pl. 12a, 12b.
- **Quotations.** "In Shadows he does not hesitate to use the (supposedly) very powerful images of the decans of the zodiac" (p. 208) — "This heavily inscribed central wheel is the astral power station, as it were, which works the whole system." (p. 213) — "the images of the decans go back to ancient Egyptian sidereal gods of time; the lists of them were preserved in the archives of Egyptian temples" (p. 213).
- **Figures.** Pl. 12a (Aries), Pl. 12b (Taurus and Gemini), from the Naples 1886 edition.
- **Related.** Metrodorus "probably" used decan images (p. 217); Camillo's planetary talismans as the milder precedent (entry 29).

## 59. Central wheel (ii): the forty-nine images of the planets

- **Name.** Planet images, seven for each planet, each group headed by a cut of the planet.
- **Author, date, source.** Bruno, *Shadows*, mostly from Agrippa, *De occulta philosophia* II, 37–44.
- **Principle.** Talisman-like figures expressing each planetary god and its influences: first image of Saturn, a man with a stag's head on a dragon, holding an owl eating a snake; third of Sol, a diademed youth with rays from his head, bow and quiver; first of Mercury, a beautiful youth with a sceptre entwined by two opposed serpents; first of Luna, a horned woman riding a dolphin with a chameleon and a lily.
- **Chapter / pages.** IX, p. 214 (n. 42).
- **Quotations.** "After the thirty-six images of the decans there follow, in the list of star-images in Shadows, forty-nine images of the planets, seven for each planet." (p. 214) — "such images express the planetary gods and their influences, after the manner of planetary talismans." (p. 214)
- **Figures.** Written out on the central wheel of Pl. 11.
- **Related.** Camillo's seven planetary gods (entry 24); Agrippa (entry 54).

## 60. Central wheel (iii): the Draco lunae and the twenty-eight mansions of the moon

- **Name.** Images of the Draco lunae and of the 28 mansions (stations) of the moon.
- **Author, date, source.** Bruno, *Shadows*, from Agrippa, *De occulta philosophia* II, 46.
- **Principle.** One image for the moon's dragon and one for each daily station of the moon through the month, expressing the moon's role in passing on zodiacal and planetary influences to the lower world.
- **Chapter / pages.** IX, p. 214 (n. 43).
- **Quotations.** "Next follow, in Bruno's list, the image of the Draco lunae together with images of the twenty-eight mansions of the moon, that is of the stations of the moon on each day of the month." (p. 214) — "These images express the role of the moon and her movements in passing on the zodiacal and planetary influences." (p. 214)
- **Figures.** On Pl. 11.
- **Related.** Entries 54, 57.

## 61. Central wheel (iv): the thirty-six images of the twelve houses of the horoscope

- **Name.** Images of the houses, three for each of the twelve houses, following a cut of the horoscope.
- **Author, date, source.** Bruno, *Shadows*; faintly related to traditional house images (Reymann's *Nativität-Kalender*, 1515), otherwise "probably largely of his own invention".
- **Principle.** Images for the aspects of life ruled by the houses: birth, wealth, brothers, parents, children, sickness, marriage, death, religion, reign, benefactions, imprisonment. Bruno here 'composes' magic images, the subject of his last book (*Images*). With the decans, planets and mansions these complete the 150 astral images of the central wheel.
- **Chapter / pages.** IX, p. 215 (n. 44).
- **Quotations.** "Bruno gives a list of thirty-six images, three for each of the twelve houses." (p. 215) — "Bruno has strangely varied and added to these to produce a very eccentric list of images which are probably largely of his own invention." (p. 215) — "The whole sky with all its complex astrological influences was on this wheel." (p. 215)
- **Figures.** On Pl. 11.
- **Related.** *De imaginum … compositione* (entry 52); entry 57.

## 62. The shadows of ideas: star-images as intermediaries; Metrodorus made magical

- **Name.** *Umbrae idearum*, 'shadows of ideas' (title from Cecco d'Ascoli's necromantic commentary on Sacrobosco); "the book on the shadows of ideas contracted for inner writing".
- **Author, date, source.** Bruno, *Shadows*, Op. lat. II (i), pp. 9, 46, 51–52, 77–78; Ficino's *De vita coelitus comparanda* as the parallel mind-set.
- **Principle.** Star-images stand between the Ideas of the supercelestial world and the elemental world; being nearer to reality than physical things, they are the 'shadows of ideas'. Imprinted on memory and set on revolving wheels, they arrange the lower things from above ("the lower things will arrange themselves in memory once we have arranged there the images of the higher things"); the chaos of elements and numbers in one's primordial nature is ordered by the zodiac images, giving powers to all the faculties of the soul. Yates: the Metrodorian zodiac system turned magical.
- **Chapter / pages.** IX, pp. 215–217 (nn. 45–50).
- **Quotations.** "the star-images are the 'shadows of ideas', shadows of reality which are nearer to reality than the physical shadows in the lower world." (p. 216) — "'We form them in us, as in the revolution of wheels. If you know any other way, try it.'" (Bruno, p. 216) — "The Metrodorian system has turned into a magical system." (p. 217)
- **Figures.** None specific.
- **Related.** Metrodorus (entry 12); Camillo's forest-and-hill view from above (entry 26); the thirty intentions and concepts (entry 67).

## 63. Second wheel: vegetable, animal, mineral and artefacts

- **Name.** The wheel of the inferior levels of creation (list beginning Aa Oliua, Ae Laurus, Ai Myrthus, Ao Rosmarinum, Au Cypressus).
- **Author, date, source.** Bruno, *Shadows*, Op. lat. II (i), p. 132.
- **Principle.** 150 items: plants, birds, animals, stones and metals, artefacts and even sacred objects (ara, septem candelabra), "strangely jumbled"; placed immediately outside the star wheel to represent the vegetable, animal and mineral worlds moving in dependence on the celestial wheel.
- **Chapter / pages.** IX, p. 218 (n. 51).
- **Quotations.** "As can be seen, these all belong to the vegetable world. There are also birds in this list; animals; stones and metals; artefacts and other objects, strangely jumbled and including even sacred objects" (p. 218) — "The idea is, I believe, to represent on this wheel the inferior levels of creation, vegetable, animal, mineral, moving in dependence on the celestial wheel." (p. 218)
- **Figures.** On Pl. 11.
- **Related.** Compared with Camillo's grades of being (p. 229); the memory objects of entry 2 are not linked by Yates.

## 64. Third wheel: the 150 adjectives

- **Name.** The wheel of adjectives (list beginning Aa nodosum, Ae mentitum, Ai inuolutum, Ao informe, Au famosum).
- **Author, date, source.** Bruno, *Shadows*, Op. lat. II (i), p. 129.
- **Principle.** 150 adjectives (knotty, counterfeited, involved, formless, famous …) in the accusative, on the third wheel from the centre; Yates can explain neither the case nor the selection.
- **Chapter / pages.** IX, p. 218 (n. 52).
- **Quotations.** "These are all adjectives (knotty, counterfeited, involved, formless, famous)." (p. 218) — "Why given in the accusative case I cannot explain, still less explain the extraordinary selection of the 150 adjectives in this list." (p. 218)
- **Figures.** On Pl. 11.
- **Related.** Entry 57.

## 65. Outer wheels: the 150 inventors and their inventions (Rhegima to Melicus)

- **Name.** The wheel of inventors (name on the outermost wheel, invention on the wheel adjoining); list beginning Aa Rhegima (bread from chestnuts), Ae Osiris (agriculture), Ai Ceres (yokes for oxen), Ao Triptolemus (sowing), Au Pitumnus (manuring). Extraction of the Rhegima, Ma–Nu and Ya–Zu groups is column-scrambled but Yates's translations are legible.
- **Author, date, source.** Bruno, *Shadows*, Op. lat. II (i), pp. 124–128, drawing on Polydore Vergil, *De inventoribus rerum* (1499).
- **Principle.** Human figures as memory images in the ancient 'placing' tradition, revolved through the history of civilisation: agriculture, primitive instruments (Erichtonius the chariot, Pyrodes fire from flint), viticulture (Noah), tools, crafts; then the M and N groups of magical arts (Chiron surgery, Circe fascination, Pharphacon necromancy, Aiguam circles, Hostanes linking with demons, Zoroaster magic, Suah chiromancy, Chaldaeus pyromancy, Attalus hydromancy, Prometheus sacrificing bulls); religious inventors (Abel, Abraham, John the Baptist, Orpheus, Belus, Chemis); visual and musical arts (Mirchanes wax figures, Giges pictures, Marsias the flute, Tubal the lyre, Amphion notes); military art; Theut writing; astronomy and philosophy (Hipparcus, Atlas, Archimedes, Xenophanes on innumerable worlds, Plato on the ideas, Raymundus on the nine elements); then 'Ior. in clauim & umbras' (Bruno himself); Euclid, Epicurus, Philolaus, Anaxagoras; last, Melicus (Simonides) 'in memoriam'. The first of each five links back, the last forward.
- **Chapter / pages.** IX, pp. 218–223 (nn. 53–65).
- **Quotations.** "It seems to be a principle of the groups of five that the first figure in them links with the preceding group, whilst the last figure links with subjects which are to follow." (p. 220) — "He puts the image of himself on the wheel, for has he not himself produced a very great invention?" (p. 221) — "Bruno's list of images of inventors to be used as memory images is in itself … absolutely within the orthodox tradition of the classical art." (p. 223)
- **Figures.** On Pl. 11 (outermost wheels; Rhegima at the middle of the lower half, Melicus to its left).
- **Related.** Rossellius's placed practitioners and the Santa Maria Novella fresco (entry 33); Camillo's Prometheus grade of all arts and sciences (p. 229); Circe and the 'linking with demons' foreshadow *Circe* and *De vinculis*; innumerable worlds first named here.

## 66. The *sigilli*: degrees of potency of memory images

- **Name.** 'Sigilli' (seals); "Signs, Notae, Characters, and Seals" as the images of highest potency.
- **Author, date, source.** Bruno, the Ars memoriae within *Shadows*, Op. lat. II (i), pp. 58, 62; referred to the missing *Clavis magna*.
- **Principle.** Within the 'Ad Herennian' discussion of places and images Bruno grades images by their nearness to reality; the least opaque he calls sigilli, and Yates reads this as the theory of the 150 astral images: seals transmitting the astral forces, the eternal ideas being received "as an influx through the medium of the stars".
- **Chapter / pages.** IX, p. 223 (nn. 66–67).
- **Quotations.** "Those with the highest degree of potency, which are least opaque to reality, he calls 'sigilli'." (p. 223) — "In such passages, he is, I believe, explaining his use of 150 such 'sigilli', or magic seals, or astral images, in the memory system." (p. 223)
- **Figures.** None.
- **Related.** Camillo's inner talismans (entry 29); the thirty seals of *Seals* (later chapter); Gohorry's 'images and seals' (entry 55).

## 67. The thirty intentions of the shadows and the thirty concepts of ideas

- **Name.** 'Thirty intentions of the shadows' and 'thirty concepts of ideas' (the preliminary thirties of *Shadows*); the wheel "type of the ideal intentions" with a sun at its centre.
- **Author, date, source.** Bruno, *Shadows*, Op. lat. II (i), pp. 20–29, 45–52; Canticle II, 3; Bruno's *De la causa*, *Spaccio*, *Eroici furori* as parallels.
- **Principle.** Two sets of thirty gnomic statements, each headed by the thirty wheel-letters and illustrated with thirty-lettered wheels, expounding the philosophy of the system: sitting under the shadow of the good; light descending from the supersubstantial unity into vestiges and images; the golden chain by which ascent is made from earth to heaven; a poem on the zodiac in which Aries acts on Taurus, Taurus on Gemini; the first intellect as the light of Amphitrite; conforming to the superior agents to unify the received species; forming the shadows of ideas in us "as in the revolution of wheels". Yates reads the intentions as the Lullian *voluntas* element and the whole as the road from multiplicity to the One.
- **Chapter / pages.** IX, pp. 225–228 (nn. 70–86).
- **Quotations.** "These two sets of thirty statements are headed by thirty letters, which are the same as the letters of the wheel, and they are illustrated in the text with wheels marked with the thirty letters." (p. 227) — "the wheel which is said to be the 'type of the ideal intentions' has a sun at the centre of it, emblem of Bruno's inner strivings to arrive at the One Light" (p. 227) — "The aim of the memory system is to establish within, in the psyche, the return of the intellect to unity through the organisation of significant images." (p. 228)
- **Figures.** The wheels in Bruno's text (Fig. 8 type); not separately reproduced.
- **Related.** Lullian memory as *voluntas* (entry 43); the ladder of *De la causa* (entry 38); Camillo's mountain-top unification (p. 230).

## 68. The thirty mythological images (Lycaon to Glaucus): a separate system

- **Name.** The list of thirty mythological images in *Shadows* (pp. 107–8 of Op. lat. II (i)), beginning with Lycaon and ending with Glaucus.
- **Author, date, source.** Bruno, *Shadows*.
- **Principle.** Thirty figures lettered with the thirty divisions of the wheels, to be revolved, but only thirty rather than 150; Yates therefore takes them as a distinct system, of the type of the Thirty Statues of *Statues*.
- **Chapter / pages.** IX, p. 222 (n. 63).
- **Quotations.** "There is yet another images list in Shadows, of thirty mythological images beginning with Lycaon and ending with Glaucus" (p. 222, n. 63) — "I therefore suppose that they constitute a separate system, resembling the Thirty Statues of Statues" (p. 222, n. 63).
- **Figures.** None.
- **Related.** *Lampas triginta statuarum* (later chapter, pp. 292–3); the Thirty (entry 56).
