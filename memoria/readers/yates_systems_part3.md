Scope: Chapters X–XIII (printed pp. 231–307; extraction lines 10168–13350 of
`yates_art_of_memory.txt`). Chapter X, Ramism as an Art of Memory; Chapter XI,
Giordano Bruno: the Secret of Seals; Chapter XII, Conflict between Brunian and

Conventions. Entries are in the order Yates introduces them, not by Seal
number (she takes Seal 12 before Seal 9, and Seal 26 before Seal 25). Page

Bruno's *Opera latine conscripta*; "G.B. and H.T." is her *Giordano Bruno and

## 1. The Ramist method: dialectical order as the art of memory

- **Name.** "Ramist method" / "dialectical order"; also "the Ramist epitome"
  (the printed, bracketed outline from which a subject is memorised). Yates
  calls it "Ramism as an art of memory" and "memory as Ramism".
- **Author, date, source.** Pierre de la Ramée (Peter Ramus), 1515–1572.
  Sources Yates cites: *Scholae in liberales artes*, Scholae rhetoricae lib. XIX
  (ed. Basle 1578, col. 309), where Ramus quotes Quintilian XI, ii, 36;
  *Dialecticae institutiones* (Paris 1543), pp. 57 verso–58 recto, containing
  the first printed epitome (the dialectical analysis of Penelope's complaint in
  Ovid); *Aristotelicae animadversiones* (Paris 1543); *De religione Christiana*
  (Frankfort 1577, pp. 114–15) for the attitude to images. Yates also makes
  "natural" and "prudential" memory Ramus's two classes.
- **Principle.** Memory is abolished as a part of rhetoric and, with it, the
  artificial memory of places and images. In their stead every subject is set
  out in "dialectical order", a schematic outline descending by dichotomies
  from "generals" to "specials", and is memorised in that order from the
  printed epitome. Order is kept by the logical analysis itself; there are no
  places in buildings and, above all, no images, the "natural" stimulus being
  the abstract order which Ramus holds natural to the mind. Yates notes that the
  visually schematised page carries an element of spatial visualisation (Ong),
  which she traces back to Quintilian's advice to visualise the written tablet
  and to bracketed Lullist manuscript lay-outs rather than to print as such.
  She also stresses the anti-image side: an "inner iconoclasm" matching the
  outer Protestant one, and, behind the apparent rationalism, a Neoplatonic
  ascent-and-descent mysticism (Prometheus to Socrates as a prisca theologia,
  the "golden chain").
- **Where described.** Chapter X, pp. 231–242 (the method pp. 232–236; the
  Grammatica example p. 234; the Penelope epitome p. 235; images and
  iconoclasm pp. 236–237; kinship with Lullism pp. 237–238; Camillo and
  Sturm pp. 238–239; the mystical background pp. 240–241; contrast with Bruno
  pp. 241–242).
- **Quotations.**
  - Ramus, quoted by Yates: "The art of memory (says Quintilian) consists
    entirely in division and composition... For the true art of memory is one
    and the same as dialectics." (p. 233)
  - "Under Ramism, we smash the inner image of old Grammatica, and teach
    little boys to do so, substituting for her the imageless Ramist epitome of
    Grammar memorised from the printed page." (p. 234)
  - "a transformation which keeps and intensifies the principle of order but
    does away with the 'artificial' side, the side which cultivated the
    imagination as the chief instrument of memory." (p. 236)
  - On the brackets: "The genesis of the Ramist epitome should probably be
    sought in Lullian manuscripts with their heavily bracketed schemata."
    (p. 238, n. 17)
- **Figures.** None reproduced by Yates. She refers to the epitome of Penelope's
  complaint and the bracketed epitome of logic as reproduced in Ong, *Ramus*,
  pp. 181 and 202, and to Le Myésier's Lullist compendium (Paris, Bibl. Nat.,
  Lat. 15450, f. 99 verso) for the bracketed lay-out. She points back to Pl. 6
  (Romberch's Grammatica) and Pl. I (the Santa Maria Novella fresco) by
  contrast.
- **Related.** Descends, as Yates argues, from Quintilian's preference for
  division and composition, from the scholastic insistence on order, from the
  "common places" of Romberch and Rossellius, and from Lullism (generals and
  specials in Lull's *Liber ad memoriam confirmandam*); possibly a rationalised
  reaction to Camillo's Theatre (entry 2). Carried forward by Perkins (entry
  33) as "logical disposition"; the opposite pole to Bruno's Seals (entries
  7–30) and to Dicson (entry 32). Amalgamations of Lullism with Ramism are
  mentioned (p. 241).

## 2. Citolini's Tipocosmia (imageless encyclopaedic order) — marginal

- **Name.** *Tipocosmia* (Venice 1561), said to have been "stolen" from the
  papers of Camillo's Theatre.
- **Author, date, source.** Alessandro Citolini, protégé of Johannes Sturm;
  Betussi (*Raverta*, ed. Zonta, p. 57) and Liruti for the theft accusation.
- **Principle.** An encyclopaedic setting-out in order of subjects and themes,
  like the Theatre's plan but with no images and no descriptions of images.
  Yates uses it as a hint that a rhetorical-methodological memory movement
  begun by Camillo on the occult level was continued by Sturm and Ramus
  "rationalised by omitting the images".
- **Where described.** Chapter X, pp. 238–239.
- **Quotations.**
  - "Citolini 'stole' only an encyclopaedic setting out in order of subjects
    and themes—for that is what the Tipocosmia is—but without the images."
    (p. 239)
  - "Camillo might have started on his transcendental or occult level a
    rhetorical-methodological-memory movement which people like Sturm and Ramus
    continued, but rationalised by omitting the images." (p. 239)
- **Figures.** None.
- **Related.** Derived (allegedly) from Camillo's Theatre; a bridge, in Yates's
  hypothesis, toward the Ramist epitome (entry 1). Yates labels the paragraph
  "undigested and controversial hints".

## 3. Agostino del Riccio, *Arte della memoria locale* (the seven pictures)

- **Name.** *Arte della memoria locale*, "for the use of studious young
  gentlemen"; the seven titled drawings: The King, The First Counsellor, The
  Second Counsellor, The First Captain or the Straight Line, The Second Captain
  or the Circular Line, The Third Captain or the Transverse Line, The Meal and
  the Servant.
- **Author, date, source.** Fra Agostino del Riccio, Dominican of Santa Maria
  Novella, Florence, 1595; unpublished manuscript, Biblioteca Nazionale,
  Florence, II, I, 13 (ff. 5, 6, 16, 33, 35, 40, 40 verso, 46, 47 cited).
- **Principle.** The principles and techniques of the classical art are each
  presented as a little symbolic picture with a title. The King striking his
  brow is "local memory" itself; the First Counsellor touching a globe of
  places gives the place rules, with a worked example round the church of Santa
  Maria Novella (Charity on the high altar, Hope on the Ciodi altar, Faith on
  the Gaddi altar, and so on round the chapels, stoup and tombs back to the
  start); the Second Counsellor, surrounded by objects and a bust, is "use
  images" (real, imaginary, or the statues in Niccolò Gaddi's gallery),
  followed by alphabetical lists (mechanical arts, saints, Florentine
  families); the Third Captain memorises twelve objects on places along the
  Via della Scala (a religious with his cross on the Tabernacle, a star on the
  first house door, a sun on Jacopo di Borgho's door) and in a Dominican cell
  divided into places (Job's seven miseries of man); the Meal and the Servant
  teaches memory in small doses. Order is the walk round the building or
  street.
- **Where described.** Chapter XI, pp. 244–246.
- **Quotations.**
  - "beginning from the high altar, you may place there Charity; then
    continuing round the church you will perhaps place on the altar of the
    Ciodi, Hope; on the altar of the Gaddi, Faith" (p. 244)
  - "Local memory is like eating and drinking. If we ate all our food at once
    we should have indigestion, so we divide it into separate meals." (p. 245)
  - "his idea of presenting the principles and various techniques of the art
    through little symbolic pictures, with titles, exactly corresponds to what
    Bruno does in Seals" (p. 246)
- **Figures.** Pl. 13a (The King), 13b (First Counsellor), 13c (Second
  Counsellor; the extraction also labels the Third Captain "PI. 13c", almost
  certainly a misread of 13e), 13d (Second Captain), 13f (The Meal and the
  Servant). Yates gives no plate for the First Captain.
- **Related.** The "good old fashioned" Dominican devotional art (virtues on
  church places); Panigarola's hundred thousand places mentioned. Yates
  introduces it solely as the model for the mode of presentation of Bruno's
  Thirty Seals (entry 7): principle-pictures with titles such as "The Joiner"
  (association) and "Zeuxis the Painter" (images).

## 4. Riccio, The First Captain or the Straight Line (zodiac on the body)

- **Name.** "The First Captain or the Straight Line".
- **Author, date, source.** Riccio, 1595, manuscript cited, f. 33.
- **Principle.** A man with a vertical line down his body; the twelve signs of
  the zodiac are placed on the parts of the body they rule, and used in that
  order as a memory system. Yates says the zodiac is "automatically mentioned
  in memory treatises as a possible system" and here is used rationally, not
  magically.
- **Where described.** Chapter XI, p. 245; comment p. 246.
- **Quotations.**
  - "On him are to be placed the twelve signs of the zodiac, in accordance with
    the parts of the body over which they rule, and they are to be remembered on
    these places as a memory system." (p. 245)
  - "No suspicion need be attached to his use of the zodiac ... there is no
    reason at all why the order of the signs should not be used in a rational
    way as a memory order." (p. 246)
- **Figures.** None given (no plate letter assigned).
- **Related.** The rational counterpart of the zodiacal memory of Metrodorus,
  of Bruno's Chain (entry 10) and of Dicson's "Scepsian" order (entry 32).

## 5. Riccio, The Second Captain or the Circular Line (elements and heavens on the body)

- **Name.** "The Second Captain or the Circular Line".
- **Author, date, source.** Riccio, 1595, manuscript cited, f. 35.
- **Principle.** A man in a circle with arms and legs extended; the four
  elements and the eleven heavens are remembered on the places of his body:
  earth, feet; water, knee; air, flank; fire, arm; Luna, right hand; Mercury,
  fore-arm; Venus, shoulder; Sol, head; Mars, left shoulder; Jupiter, left
  fore-arm; Saturn, left hand; fixed stars, left shoulder; crystalline sphere,
  waist; primum mobile, knees; Paradise, under left foot. The aim is to
  memorise the order of the spheres, "in a way which, though puerile, is not
  magical".
- **Where described.** Chapter XI, p. 245; comment p. 246.
- **Quotations.**
  - "On the places of this man's body we are to remember the four elements and
    the eleven heavens: earth, feet; water, knee; air, flank; fire, arm; Luna,
    right hand" (p. 245)
  - "He aims at memorising the order of the spheres, but in a way which, though
    puerile, is not magical." (p. 246)
- **Figures.** Pl. 13d.
- **Related.** A tame counterpart of the astral place systems of Bruno (Heaven,
  Potter's Wheel, entries 9 and 20) and of Camillo's planetary "generals".

## 6. Bruno, *Ars reminiscendi* (the art of memory of the *Circe*, reprinted in *Seals*)

- **Name.** *Ars reminiscendi*; the art of memory first printed with the
  *Cantus Circaeus* (Paris 1582), where it followed the incantations of Circe
  to the seven planets. Yates also calls the whole 1583 volume "Seals".
- **Author, date, source.** Giordano Bruno; Paris 1582, reprinted London (John
  Charlewood, no place or date on title-page) early 1583 as the first of four
  items (Ars reminiscendi; Triginta sigilli; Explanatio triginta sigillorum;
  Sigillus sigillorum). Op. lat., II (i), pp. 211–57 (rules pp. 221 ff.,
  places pp. 224, 229–31, images pp. 241–6, memory for words p. 251, the "Song
  of Circe" p. 251).
- **Principle.** The classical rules expanded in the manner of a normal memory
  treatise, with Bruno's terms "subjects" for places and "adjects" for images.
  Very large numbers of places are aimed at (one house after another in
  different parts of the city; the last Roman place joined to the first
  Parisian); images must be striking and associated, with a list of thirty
  ways of forming images for notions by association; a claimed better memory
  for words than "Tullius" (Ad Herennium, still attributed to Cicero); and,
  as place systems, "semi-mathematical" subjects, diagrammatic figures that are
  mathematical "in some other way", i.e. magical. The "new and better way" of
  using the old rules is tied to the Song of Circe, the planetary incantations
  omitted in the English reprint.
- **Where described.** Chapter XI, pp. 243–244 (the four items), 247.
- **Quotations.**
  - "Continuing to use his terminology of 'subjects' for memory places and
    'adjects' for memory images, Bruno gives in this art the classical rules,
    expanding them very much after the manner of a normal memory treatise."
    (p. 247)
  - "When you have finished the last of the Roman places, you can connect it
    with the first of the Parisian places." (p. 247)
  - "He recommends as place systems what he calls 'semi-mathematical'
    subjects, that is diagrammatical figures which are not mathematical in the
    normal way, but in some other way." (p. 247)
  - "There was therefore some Circaean mystification at the heart of this
    memory treatise" (p. 247)
- **Figures.** None.
- **Related.** Belongs to the Romberch/Rossellius treatise genre; compared with
  Peter of Ravenna collecting places on his travels. The "semi-mathematical"
  place figures recur as the diagrams of the Seals (entries 9, 19, 20), of the
  Figuratio (entry 37) and, as "mathesistical" place systems, in the German
  Seals (entry 43). The Circe incantations are discussed in G.B. and H.T.,
  pp. 199–202.

## 7. The Thirty Seals (*Triginta sigilli* with the *Explanatio triginta sigillorum*)

- **Name.** "The Thirty Seals" (Triginta sigilli), each with an "Explanation";
  Yates: "thirty statements of principles and techniques of magic memory".
- **Author, date, source.** Bruno, London, early 1583; Op. lat., II (ii),
  pp. 69–217 (Seals pp. 79–106; Explanations pp. 121–160). Dedicated to
  Mauvissière; with an address to the Vice-Chancellor and doctors of Oxford.
- **Principle.** Each Seal is a titled statement of one principle or technique
  of the art (the Field is the memory to be worked, the Joiner is association,
  Zeuxis is "use images", the Table is the visual alphabet), on Riccio's
  pattern of principle-pictures, but "magicised, complicated with Lullism and
  Cabbalism, blown up into inscrutable mysteries". The old tricks of the memory
  trade are recognisable Seal after Seal; several allude to the system of
  *Shadows* (astral images on Lullian wheels), but the Seals are "not confined
  to any one system": Bruno tries "every possible way", varying astrological
  arrangements, Lullist devices and Cabalist infiltrations in search of an
  operative organisation of the psyche. The two sets of ideas worked together
  are memory and astrology: striking, associated images linked to the
  astrological system, on "semi-mathematical" places, in the associative
  orders of astrology. Some Explanations carry "semi-mathematical" diagrams.
- **Where described.** Chapter XI, pp. 243, 246–251 (survey), 251–254
  (interpretation), 259–265 (the Elizabethan reception).
- **Quotations.**
  - "This is what the Seals are, statements of the principles and techniques
    of the art—but magicised, complicated with Lullism and Cabbalism, blown up
    into inscrutable mysteries." (p. 246)
  - "thirty statements of principles and techniques of magic memory, followed
    by thirty more or less inexplicable 'explanations', some of which are
    illustrated with more or less insoluble 'semi-mathematical' diagrams."
    (pp. 247–248)
  - "He is working with two sets of ideas, memory and astrology." (p. 251)
  - "Bruno states that he is trying every possible way; perhaps something for
    which he is not looking may emerge out of this, as alchemists who do not
    succeed in making gold sometimes hit on other important discoveries."
    (p. 249)
- **Figures.** Pl. 14a (The Heaven), Pl. 14b (The Potter's Wheel); Fig. 9
  (Romberch's faculty-psychology diagram) is reproduced in the Seal of Seals
  discussion.
- **Related.** Presentation mode from Riccio (entry 3); contents from the
  memory treatises, from *Shadows*, from Lull and Cabala; the notion of
  combining memory with astral principles from Camillo's Theatre. Yates
  enumerates only a selection ("I shall therefore not enumerate the whole
  Thirty"): entries 8–29 below. The Joiner (association) is named only as an
  example of the mode (p. 246). Repeated, with the same titles and more
  obscurely, as the third part of *Images* (entry 43). The Figuratio (entry
  37) tells the reader to choose a Seal, Painter or Sculptor. No "Theatre"
  seal and no seal called "the Wheel of Circe" is described in this stretch.

## 8. Seal 1: The Field

- **Name.** "The Field".
- **Source.** Op. lat., II (ii), pp. 79–80, 121–2.
- **Principle.** The field is the memory or the phantasy, "the ample folds of
  which are to be worked upon by the art of places and images"; brief obscure
  summaries of the rules, insisting that images must move by their striking and
  unusual character. It also refers to "Solyman the Thalmudist", who had a
  memory system in twelve divisions marked with the names of the patriarchs.
- **Where described.** Chapter XI, p. 248.
- **Quotations.**
  - "This field is the memory, or the phantasy, the ample folds of which are
    to be worked upon by the art of places and images." (p. 248)
  - "a reference to 'Solyman the Thalmudist' who had a memory system in twelve
    divisions marked with the names of the patriarchs." (p. 248)
- **Figures.** None.
- **Related.** Cultivated by The Husbandman (entry 28); the classical rules of
  the Ars reminiscendi (entry 6).

## 9. Seal 2: The Heaven (the twelve houses as memory rooms)

- **Name.** "The Heaven".
- **Source.** Op. lat., II (ii), pp. 80, 121–2.
- **Principle.** So that "the order and the series of the images of heaven may
  be engraved", a sphere divided in a certain manner gives places and sites;
  the diagram is based on the twelve houses of a horoscope, which serve as
  memory places or memory rooms in which the "images of heaven" (the astral
  images) are engraved.
- **Where described.** Chapter XI, p. 248.
- **Quotations.**
  - "So that 'the order and the series of the images of heaven may be engraved'
    a sphere divided in a certain manner will give places and sites." (p. 248)
  - "Bruno is using the houses of a horoscope as memory places, or memory
    rooms, in which the 'images of heaven' will be engraved." (p. 248)
- **Figures.** Pl. 14a.
- **Related.** The horoscope diagram recurs in Squaring the Circle (19), the
  Potter's Wheel (20), the Field and Garden of Circe (22) and the place system
  of the Figuratio (37); the "images of heaven" are those listed in *Shadows*.

## 10. Seal 3: The Chain (the zodiac as associative order)

- **Name.** "The Chain".
- **Source.** Op. lat., II (ii), pp. 81, 123–4; cf. *Shadows*, Op. lat., II
  (i), p. 28.
- **Principle.** Memory proceeds from the preceding to what follows as links of
  a chain are involved with their neighbours, which sounds like the
  Aristotelianised association of ideas; but the Explanation says the chain is
  really the zodiac, whose signs run on one into the other, quoting the same
  Latin poem on the order of the signs as in *Shadows*.
- **Where described.** Chapter XI, p. 248.
- **Quotations.**
  - "memory must proceed from the preceding to what follows as parts of a chain
    are involved with the preceding and following links." (p. 248)
  - "in the explanation of this Seal we are told that the chain is really the
    zodiac, the signs of which run on, the one into the other" (p. 248)
- **Figures.** None.
- **Related.** The point at which Yates begins to suspect the Seals are about
  the system of *Shadows*; the zodiacal order of Metrodorus, of Riccio's
  Straight Line (4) and of Dicson (32).

## 11. Seals The Tree and The Wood (Lull's Arbor scientiae)

- **Name.** "The Tree" and "The Wood"; the first two of "the next three
  Seals", which "are Lullist" (Yates gives no numbers; they follow Seal 3).
- **Source.** Op. lat., II (ii), pp. 81–2, 124–7.
- **Principle.** Connected with Lull's *Arbor scientiae*, named in the text:
  a wood all of whose trees, representing all knowledge, are rooted in basic
  principles common to all. Yates asks whether these Seals give the principle
  of using Lullian combinatory systems with the astrologised classical art, as
  in *Shadows*.
- **Where described.** Chapter XI, p. 248.
- **Quotations.**
  - "connected with Lull's Arbor scientiae, which is mentioned by name, as a
    wood all the trees of which, representing all knowledge, are rooted in
    basic principles common to all." (p. 248)
- **Figures.** None.
- **Related.** Lullism as memory (chapter VIII of the book); the Combiner (25)
  and the Lullian wheels of the Statues (38).

## 12. Seal The Ladder (the third figure of the Ars brevis)

- **Name.** "The Ladder"; the third of the Lullist Seals.
- **Source.** Op. lat., II (ii), pp. 82, 127–8.
- **Principle.** Gives what is in fact the third figure of Lull's *Ars brevis*,
  showing combinations of letters combined on the Lullian wheels; Yates reads
  it as the combinatory ladder applied within the magicised art.
- **Where described.** Chapter XI, pp. 248–249.
- **Quotations.**
  - "The 'Ladder' gives what is actually the third figure of Lull's Ars brevis
    showing combinations of letters combined on the Lullian wheels." (pp. 248–249)
  - "we wonder whether these Seals are giving the principle of using Lullian
    combinatory systems with the astrologised and magicised classical art of
    memory, as in Shadows." (p. 249)
- **Figures.** None.
- **Related.** The Ladder of Minerva in the Statues (38); the Combiner (25).

## 13. Seal 12: Zeuxis the Painter (the principle "use images"; the "double picture")

- **Name.** "Zeuxis the Painter"; "the Seal of the Painter".
- **Source.** Op. lat., II (ii), pp. 85, 133–4.
- **Principle.** Represents the principle of using images in the art of memory.
  Its text ties the Seals to *Shadows*: "the images of Teucer the Babylonian
  supply me with the indications of three hundred thousand propositions" (the
  decan images), and Bruno "knows a double picture" for memory: one forming
  images and notae "from strange descriptions" (the astral images listed in the
  art attached to *De umbris idearum*), the other "feigning as need requires
  edifices" with images of sensible things for non-sensible things (normal
  classical memory, which in Bruno is nonetheless "galvanised into magical
  activity" by affiliation to astral systems). In its discussion of painting
  and poetry (ut pictura poesis; "to understand is to speculate with images")
  Zeuxis becomes the painter of images in the fantasy, and painter, poet and
  philosopher are made one.
- **Where described.** Chapter XI, p. 249 (system), pp. 252–253 (theory of
  imagination), p. 257 (looking back from the Seal of Seals), p. 263 (Sidney).
- **Quotations.**
  - "'the images of Teucer the Babylonian supply me with the indications of
    three hundred thousand propositions'." (p. 249)
  - "we know a double picture; the one when we form from strange descriptions
    images and notae for retaining in memory ... the other by feigning as need
    requires edifices" (p. 249)
  - "Whence philosophers are in some ways painters and poets; poets are
    painters and philosophers; painters are philosophers and poets." (p. 253)
- **Figures.** None.
- **Related.** Directly the memory of *Shadows*; the Figuratio and Statues are
  "examples of how to use the Seals Zeuxis the Painter or Phidias the
  Sculptor" (p. 293); the "double picture" is, Yates suggests, carried out
  simultaneously by the round-and-square system of *Images* (entry 42).

## 14. Seal 9: The Table (the visual alphabet of persons)

- **Name.** "The Table".
- **Source.** Op. lat., II (ii), pp. 83–4, 130–1.
- **Principle.** The form of "visual alphabet" in which letters are remembered
  by images of people whose names begin with those letters (Peter of Ravenna's
  Eusebius and Thomas changing places for ET and TE); Bruno mentions Peter of
  Ravenna with admiration.
- **Where described.** Chapter XI, p. 250.
- **Quotations.**
  - "describes that interesting form of the 'visual alphabet' which consists of
    remembering letters by images of people whose names begin with those
    letters." (p. 250)
- **Figures.** None.
- **Related.** Peter of Ravenna, *Phoenix* (p. 119 of the book); the visual
  alphabet of objects reappears in *Images* (entry 39).

## 15. Seal 11: The Standard (standard-bearing images)

- **Name.** "The Standard".
- **Source.** Op. lat., II (ii), pp. 84, 132–3 (the anti-Aristotelian passage
  p. 133).
- **Principle.** Leading images act as standard-bearers for whole groups of
  things: Plato, Aristotle, Diogenes, a Pyrrhonian, an Epicurean indicate not
  only themselves but the many notions having affinity with them. Yates
  identifies this with the ancient tradition of images of notable practitioners
  as memory images, and reads Bruno's gloss (all things in nature follow their
  assigned leaders, which Anaxagoras knew and Aristotle could not) as a root
  of his anti-Aristotelianism: an astrally based memory sees nature in
  magical, associative groupings.
- **Where described.** Chapter XI, p. 250, p. 252.
- **Quotations.**
  - "stands for leading images as standard-bearers for whole groups of things;
    thus Plato, Aristotle, Diogenes, a Pyhrronian, an Epicurean, would serve to
    indicate not merely those individuals but many notions having affinity with
    them." (p. 250)
  - "All things of nature and in nature, like soldiers in an army, follow
    leaders assigned to them ... This Anaxagoras knew very well but Father
    Aristotle could not attain to it." (p. 252)
- **Figures.** None.
- **Related.** The practitioners of the Santa Maria Novella fresco and of

## 30. The Seal of Seals (*Sigillus sigillorum*)

- **Name.** "Seal of Seals" (Sigillus sigillorum); Bruno says it forms part of
  his *Clavis magna*.
- **Author, date, source.** Bruno, London 1583, the fourth item of the volume;
  Op. lat., II (ii), pp. 160–217 (inspiration p. 161; the ancient
  practitioners pp. 162–3; opifex of nature p. 165; the Ad Herennian rule
  alluded to p. 166; love pp. 167 ff.; grades of knowing pp. 172 ff.; mens
  agitat molem pp. 174 ff.; fifteen contractions pp. 180 ff.; Thomas,
  Zoroaster, Paul pp. 190–1; four guides pp. 195 ff.; Light pp. 199 ff.).
- **Principle.** Not a place-and-image system but the visionary apotheosis the
  Thirty Seals lead up to (the reverse of *Shadows*, which begins with the
  vision and descends to the system). Claiming divine inspiration, it names
  Carneades, Cineas, Metrodorus and above all Simonides, made a mystagogue who
  has unified memory on the celestial grade and now leads to the supercelestial;
  promises conformity with the opifex of nature through memory and intellect
  grasping the triple world (Agrippa's Hermetic ascent). Against the faculty
  psychology of the treatises (Romberch's diagram of the head), it makes
  cognition one imaginative process in four grades (sense, imagination,
  reason, intellect) with the doors open between them; vital images charged
  with affects, above all Love, unify memory and set up correspondences between
  inner and outer worlds. A fifth grade of knowing is divided into fifteen
  "contractions" (religious experiences, good and bad contemplation, "magical
  religion" after Agrippa); Thomas Aquinas is ranked with Zoroaster and Paul;
  solitude is prescribed (Moses, Jesus, Lull, Paracelsus). Four guides, Love,
  Art, Mathesis, Magic, lead to four objects, the first being Light, the primal
  light of the Pimander and the sun adored by Chaldaeans, Egyptians,
  Pythagoreans and Platonists.
- **Where described.** Chapter XI, pp. 255–259 (and the concealment question
  pp. 259–260; the Clavis magna p. 264).
- **Quotations.**
  - "Seals reverses this order, beginning with the memory systems and ending
    with the 'Seal of Seals'." (p. 255)
  - "Simonides has been transformed into a mystagogue, one who has taught us
    how to unify memory on the celestial grade and will now introduce us to the
    supercelestial world." (p. 255)
  - "Love by which souls are raised to the divine by a divine furor; Art by
    which one may become joined to the soul of the world; Mathesis which is a
    magical use of figures; Magic, understood as religious magic." (p. 258)
  - "The art of memory has become in Giordano Bruno's occult transformation of
    it, a magico-religious technique, a way of becoming joined to the soul of
    the world as part of a Hermetic mystery cult." (p. 259)
- **Figures.** Fig. 9, "Diagram of Faculty Psychology. Redrawn from a diagram in
  Romberch, Congestorium artificiose memorie" (p. 256), given as the kind of
  scheme Bruno argues against; Rossellius and Leporeus (Volkmann, Pl. 172)
  cited for similar diagrams.
- **Related.** Corresponds to the first visionary part of *Shadows*; follows
  Agrippa on the Magus's ascent and on magical religion; within range of the
  *Eroici furori*. Yates weighs whether the Thirty Seals are a barrier
  protecting it, and concludes the Art "by which we may become joined to the
  soul of the world" is a main technique of the religion, not a cloak. The
  German Seals of *Images* (43) have no Seal of Seals.

## 31. Dee's *Monas hieroglyphica* as a unifying sign — marginal analogue

- **Quotations.**
  - "his monas was perhaps, in his eyes, a unified arrangement of significant

  - "Though Dee does not use the places and images of the art of memory for
    this effort, the assumption underlying it may be not dissimilar" (p. 263)

- **Related.** Dee as the only English representative of the Renaissance
  revival of Lullism (Lullist manuscripts, Digby MS. 197); Camillo's Theatre;

## 32. Alexander Dicson, *De umbra rationis* (the "Scepsian" Brunian art of memory)

- **Name.** *De umbra rationis* ("on the shadow of reason"), echoing Bruno's
  *De umbris idearum*; reprinted as *Thamus* (Leiden, Thomas Basson, 1597);
  Watson calls it the "Umbra artificiosa of Dicson"; Perkins's name for it,
  the "Scepsian" artificial memory (from Metrodorus of Scepsis, who used the
  zodiac).
- **Author, date, source.** Alexander Dicson of Errol ("Dicsono Arelio"),
  Scot, probably a political agent, d. c. 1604; printed by Thomas Vautrollier,
  London, dated 1583 on the title-page, dedication to Leicester dated "kalends
  of January", so early 1584. Pages cited: dialogues pp. 5–28; the art pp. 37,
  38 ff., 54, 61, 62, 69 ff. Defended in *Defensio pro Alexandro Dicsono*
  (1584) under the pseudonym "Heius Scepsius".
- **Principle.** A close imitation of *Shadows*: loci are "subjects", images
  "adjuvants" or, most often, "umbrae", the image being a shadow of the divine
  light sought through its shadows, vestiges and seals; the Ad Herennian rules
  for places and images are repeated "muffled in an obscure mystique"; the
  memory is based on the order of the signs of the zodiac, repeated, though
  the decan images are not listed; traces of Bruno's inventors (Theutates for
  letters, Nereus for hydromancy, Chiron for medicine). The Egyptians, not
  Simonides, invented the art, and "if it is separated from Egypt it can effect
  nothing". The long introductory dialogues (Mercurius, Thamus, Theutates,
  Socrates), built on the Phaedrus story of Theuth and Thamus and on Corpus
  Hermeticum XIII (the duodenarius driven out by the denarius) and IV (the
  crater), present the "inner writing" of occult memory as Egyptian
  regenerative wisdom against a Socrates who is a satire on Ramus.
- **Where described.** Chapter XII, pp. 266–273 (system p. 268; dialogues
  pp. 268–272); reception pp. 281–286.
- **Quotations.**
  - "the loci are called 'subjects' and the images 'adjuvants' or more
    frequently 'umbra'. Clearly he is using Bruno's terminology." (p. 268)
  - "The memory is to be based on the order of the signs of the zodiac which
    are repeated, though Dicson does not repeat the list of the images of the
    decans." (p. 268)
  - "Dicson's art of memory is but a fragmentary impression of the systems and
    expositions of Shadows from which it is nevertheless unmistakably derived."
    (p. 268)
  - "In Perkins's terminology a 'Scepsian' is one who uses the zodiac in his
    impious artificial memory." (p. 267)
- **Figures.** None.
- **Related.** Derived from *Shadows* (dialogues and system); allied by Perkins
  with Metrodorus, Rossellius and "Nolanus"; Dicson appears as "Dicsono" in
  Bruno's *De la causa* (1584); the art as taught to Hugh Platt is entry 36;
  commended by Del Rio (1600); Yates links its "underground" Hermetic form to
  possible secret groups (Family of Love, incipient Rosicrucianism or
  Freemasonry, entry 47).

## 33. Perkins's Ramist "logical disposition" as the only art of memory

- **Name.** "Logical disposition" / "the method"; Ramist memory as defended
  against the "impious artificial memory of Dicson".
- **Author, date, source.** William Perkins, "G. P. Cantabrigiensis", Puritan
  divine and Cambridge Ramist. *Antidicsonus* (London, Henry Middleton, 1584;
  dedication to Thomas Moufet; pp. 17, 19, 20, 21, 29–30, 45 cited), bound
  with *Libellus in quo dilucide explicatur impia Dicsoni artificiosa memoria*;
  *Libellus de memoria verissimaque bene recordandi scientia* (London, Robert
  Waldegrave, 1584; dedication to John Verner pp. 3–4) with *Admonitiuncula ad
  A. Dicsonum* (unpaged; Sig. C 8 verso, Sig. E i); *Prophetica* (Cambridge
  1592, Sig. F viii recto); *A Warning against the Idolatrie of the Last Times*
  (Works, Cambridge 1603, pp. 811, 830, 833, 716, 841).
- **Principle.** There are two arts of memory, one with places and "umbrae",
  the other by logical disposition as Ramus teaches; only the latter is true.
  The method records in memory through a natural order, using "true places":
  generals in the highest place, subalterns in the middle, specials in the
  lowest; its "images" are clear and distinct divisions, not fugitive shadows.
  The *Libellus de memoria* gives many examples of logical analysis of poetry
  and prose for memorising; the *Prophetica* prescribes the method for
  memorising sermons. The classical art is rejected as morally impious (the
  "animation" of images arouses carnal affects; Peter of Ravenna's libidinous
  images), as a triple burden (places, images, thing), as astrologically false
  (the "astronomy" attacked in the Admonitions, though on the Ramist ground
  that astronomy is a "special" art and memory a "general" one), and as
  "barbarism and Dunsicality". The iconoclasm is inner as well as outer: "a
  thing faigned in the mind by imagination is an idol".
- **Where described.** Chapter XII, pp. 267, 273–278.
- **Quotations.**
  - "there are two kinds of arts of memory, one using places and 'umbra', the
    other by logical disposition as taught by Ramus. The former is utterly vain;
    the latter is the only true method." (p. 273)
  - "The method uses true places, putting generals in the highest place,
    subalterns in the middle place, specials in the lowest." (p. 276)
  - "It burdens the mind and memory because it imposes a triple task on memory
    instead of one; first (the remembering of) the places; then of the images;
    then of the thing to be spoken of." (p. 277)
  - "The dialectical method was emotionally aseptic." (p. 275)
- **Figures.** None.
- **Related.** Quotes Ramus's *Scholae dialecticae* lib. XX and *Scholae
  rhetoricae* libs. I and 3 verbatim (entry 1); confirms Ong's view that the
  method was primarily a memory method; Perkins turns the terminology of the
  classical art (places, images) against it. Contrasted with Dicson (32),
  Watson (34) and Sidney (35).

## 35. Sidney's adaptation: the verse as a room divided into places

- **Name.** Yates: "This interesting adaptation of local memory".
- **Author, date, source.** Sir Philip Sidney, *Defence of Poetrie* (An
  Apologie for Poetrie, ed. Shuckburgh, 1905, p. 36); written c. 1583–85.
- **Principle.** Verse is more easily remembered than prose because it is in
  effect what the teachers of the art of memory recommend, "a certain room
  divided into many places, well and throughly known": every word has its
  natural seat in the line, and the seat makes the word remembered. Yates
  takes it as proof Sidney did not memorise poetry by the Ramist method.
- **Where described.** Chapter XII, p. 284.
- **Quotations.**
  - "they that have taught the art of memory have showed nothing so apt for it
    as a certain room divided into many places, well and throughly known; now
    that hath the verse in effect perfectly" (p. 284)
  - "This interesting adaptation of local memory shows that Sidney did not
    memorise poetry by the Ramist method." (p. 284)
- **Figures.** None.
- **Related.** Set against Ramus's dialectical analysis of Ovid (1) and
  Perkins (33); with the Sonnet to Stella on astrology, Yates's evidence that
  Sidney was not a pure Ramist and could receive Bruno's dedications.

## 36. Dicson's taught practice: places in tens and "animated umbrae" (per Hugh Platt)

- **Name.** "The Art of Memorie which master Dickson the Scot did teach";
  Platt calls it "Maister Dickson's art".
- **Author, date, source.** Reported by Hugh Platt, *The Jewell House of Art and
  Nature* (London 1592), pp. 81–83, who took lessons from Dicson after Bruno
  left England in 1586.
- **Principle.** Places are memorised in sets of ten, with images on them made
  lively and active, which Dicson termed "to animate the umbras or ideas rerum
  memorandarum"; example, "Bellona staring with her fierie eies", portrayed as
  the poets describe her. Platt found it worked up to a point but fell short of
  the "great and swelling art" promised; Yates judges he was taught a simple
  form of the straight classical mnemotechnic without initiation into the
  Hermetic mysteries.
- **Where described.** Chapter XII, pp. 284–285.
- **Quotations.**
  - "learned to memorise places in sets of ten with images on them which were
    to be made lively and active, a process which 'Maister Dickson tearmed to
    animate the umbras (sic) or ideas rerum memorandarum'." (p. 285)
  - "'Bellona staring with her fierie eies and portraied in all points
    according to the usual description of the Poets'." (p. 285)
  - "He seems to have been taught a simple form of the straight mnemotechnic
    which he did not know was a classical art but thought was 'Maister
    Dickson's art'." (p. 285)
- **Figures.** None.
- **Related.** The exoteric layer of Dicson's *De umbra rationis* (32); the
  "animation" of images Perkins found impious (33).

## 37. Bruno, *Figuratio Aristotelici physici auditus* (memory images for Aristotle's physics)

- **Name.** *Figuratio Aristotelici physici auditus*; Yates: "the Figuration".
- **Author, date, source.** Bruno, Paris 1586, "ex Typographia Petri Cheuillot
  ... sub Rosa rubra", dedicated to Piero Del Bene, Abbot of Belleville; Op.
  lat., I (iv), pp. 129 ff. (images pp. 137 ff.; place system p. 139;
  reference to the Seals p. 136). Companion to the anti-Aristotelian
  *Camoeracensis Acrotismus* delivered by Jean Hennequin at the Collège de
  Cambrai.
- **Principle.** Teaches how to memorise the physics of Aristotle (the *De
  auditu physico*) through a series of mythological memory images placed on a
  "curious looking" horoscope-like place system of the kind seen in the Seals:
  the Arbor Olympica, Minerva, Thetis as matter, Apollo as form, the "superior
  Pan" as nature, Cupid as motion, Saturn as time, Jupiter as the prime mover.
  The reader is told at the outset to turn to the Thirty Seals and choose,
  perhaps the Painter, perhaps the Sculptor. Yates argues the images, animated
  with the magic of divine proportions and in contact with cosmic powers,
  contain Bruno's own living philosophy, so that the memory system "is in
  itself a contradiction of the physics"; the book is itself a Seal. Romberch's
  story of a young man who memorised the *De auditu physico* from "rather
  inane little figures" painted on walls shows the Dominican precedent.
- **Where described.** Chapter XIII, pp. 287–289; confirmed by Statues p. 292;
  summed up p. 293.
- **Quotations.**
  - "teaches how to memorise the physics of Aristotle through a series of
    mythological memory images which are to be placed on a curious looking
    place system." (p. 288)
  - "Mythological figures are to be used as the memory images, the Arbor
    Olympica, Minerva, Thetis as matter, Apollo as form, the 'superior Pan' as
    nature, Cupid as motion, Saturn as time, Jupiter as the prime mover"
    (p. 288)
  - "The memory system by which the physics is to be 'figured' is in itself a
    contradiction of the physics. The book is a Seal" (p. 289)
- **Figures.** Pl. 14c (the place system).
- **Related.** Uses the Seals of Zeuxis (13) and Phidias (27); its figures
  recur in the Statues (38); a possible anticipation of Bacon's mythological
  vehicle for anti-Aristotelian philosophy (Rossi); Romberch's *Congestorium*,
  pp. 7 verso–8 recto.

## 38. Bruno, *Lampas triginta statuarum* (the Thirty Statues)

- **Name.** *Lampas triginta statuarum*, "Torch of the Thirty Statues"; Yates:
  "Statues".
- **Author, date, source.** Bruno, written at Wittenberg c. 1588, unfinished,
  unpublished in his lifetime; copied by Jerome Besler at Padua 1591 (Noroff
  manuscript), first printed Op. lat., III, pp. 1 ff. (introduction pp. 8–9;
  Orcus pp. 16 ff.; Apollo pp. 63–8; Prometheus pp. 68–77; Sagittarius
  pp. 97–102; Coelius pp. 106–11; Minerva and her Ladder pp. 140–50; Venus and
  Cupid pp. 151 ff.). Related Wittenberg Lullian works: *De lampade
  combinatoria lulliana*, *De progressu et lampade venatoria logicorum*.
- **Principle.** Bruno does what the Figuratio advised, using the Seal of
  Phidias: towering mythological statues sculptured within, which are his
  philosophy. The series begins with "infigurable" concepts (ORCUS or the
  ABYSS, the thirst for the infinite) and passes to figured Statues: APOLLO
  naked in his chariot, the MONAD or ONE; SATURN with sickle, Beginning or
  Time; PROMETHEUS devoured by the vulture, Causa efficiens; SAGITTARIUS bending
  his bow, the intention directed to an object; COELIUS, natural goodness in
  the order of heaven; VESTA, moral goodness; VENUS and CUPID, the unifying
  force of love; MINERVA, the mens, memory and reminiscence, continuity of
  human with divine and demonic intelligences; by the LADDER OF MINERVA "we
  rise from the first to the last, collect the external species in the internal
  sense, order intellectual operations into a whole by art". Order: Yates
  believes the thirty Statues were to be revolved on Lullian combinatory
  wheels, images replacing letters. Their theory: the ancients declared truths
  through fables to make them easier to remember; sensibilia are retained more
  easily than intelligibilia (Thomist); the Statues contain "intentions"; and
  they are talismanic statues in the manner of the Asclepius, applied inwardly.
  Three-fold power: true ancient philosophy in mythological form; memory images
  carrying intentions of the will; artistically magic images drawing divine and
  demonic intelligences.
- **Where described.** Chapter XIII, pp. 289–293.
- **Quotations.**
  - "He is using the Seal of Phidias the Sculptor. These towering mythological
    Statues sculptured within by the Michelangelesque memory artist do not
    merely express or illustrate Bruno's philosophy. They are his philosophy"
    (p. 289)
  - "We easily retain a sensible, visible, imaginable statue, we commend easily
    to the work of memory fabulous fictions; therefore (through them) we shall
    be able without difficulty to consider and retain mysteries" (pp. 290–291)
  - "The Thirty Statues are, I believe, supposed to be revolved on Lullian
    combinatory wheels." (p. 292)
- **Figures.** None.
- **Related.** Germ in the Lycaon-to-Glaucus series of thirty mythological
  figures in *Shadows* (Op. lat., II (i), p. 107); shares figures with the
  Figuratio (37); draws on Natalis Comes's *Mythologia*; imagery connects with
  the Frankfort Latin poems and with *Images* (39–42); Lullism's principia and
  relata; the Asclepius statue-magic as read by Camillo.

## 39. *Images*, Part I: the art of memory as "method" (rules for images and places)

- **Name.** The first part of *De imaginum, signorum et idearum compositione*
  ("On the composition of images, signs, and ideas"); Yates: "Images". Bruno:
  "We institute a method, not about things but about the significance of
  things."
- **Author, date, source.** Bruno, Frankfort 1591, probably written in
  Switzerland (Hainzell's castle near Zurich), dedicated to Johann Heinrich
  Hainzell; Op. lat., II (iii), pp. 85 ff. (method p. 95; "to think is to
  speculate with images" p. 103; visual alphabet p. 113; doors of the
  storehouse p. 121; the verse rule for places p. 188).
- **Principle.** The Ad Herennian rules once more, "in a yet more mystifying
  form", called a method rather than an art. Rules for images come first
  (wrong order): ways of forming images, images for things and for words
  (a visual alphabet of objects: O a sphere, A a ladder or compasses, I a
  column, straight out of Romberch), images lively, active, striking and
  charged with affects "so that they may pass through the doors of the
  storehouse of memory"; then rules for places, partly in Latin verse (loci
  not too large nor too small, not higher nor wider than a man can reach,
  Romberch's rule). Egyptian and Chaldaean mysteries are hinted at, but the
  treatise structure, mainly Romberch's, is visible beneath.
- **Where described.** Chapter XIII, pp. 293–295, 298–299.
- **Quotations.**
  - "'We institute a method, not about things but about the significance of
    things.'" (p. 294)
  - "the letter O may be represented by a sphere; the letter A by a ladder or
    compasses; the letter I by a column, he is simply describing in words one
    of the visual alphabets illustrated by Romberch." (pp. 294–295)
  - "It is the rule that memory loci should not be too large nor too small,
    with the addition in the last two lines of Romberch's advice that a memory
    locus should not be higher nor wider than a man can reach" (p. 295)
- **Figures.** None new; Yates refers back to Fig. 3 (Romberch's rule of the
  reach of a man). The six-line Latin verse is quoted (the extraction is
  clean).
- **Related.** Parallel to the arts in *Shadows* and *Circe* (6); Romberch's
  *Congestorium*; frames the architectural system (40).

## 40. *Images*, Part I: the architectural system of twenty-four atria, fifteen fields and thirty cubicles

- **Name.** Yates: "an architectural memory system of terrible complexity";
  "an architectural 'Seal'".
- **Author, date, source.** Bruno, *Images*, Part I (1591); the atria are
  illustrated diagrammatically on pages of Bruno's text.
- **Principle.** Sequences of memory rooms with images in each: twenty-four
  "atria", each divided into nine memory places with images; fifteen "fields",
  each of nine places; and thirty "cubicles". Everything in the lower world is
  to be memorised in them, all plants, stones, metals, animals and birds
  (classified with the treatises' alphabetical lists), every art, science,
  invention and human activity, "all things which can be said, known, or
  imagined": the encyclopaedic contents that in *Shadows* filled the wheels
  round the central wheel are here redistributed into rooms. The distribution
  of rooms is involved with magical geometry and the system "is worked from
  above by celestial mechanics", the celestial part being Part II (41).
- **Where described.** Chapter XIII, pp. 295–296; pp. 305–306.
- **Quotations.**
  - "There are twenty-four 'atria' or rooms each divided into nine memory
    places with images on them." (p. 295)
  - "There are also fifteen 'fields' in the system, each divided into nine
    places; and thirty 'cubicles', which bring the system within range of the
    'thirty' obsession." (p. 295)
  - "This is an architectural 'Seal' full of correspondences, associative
    orders, which are both mnemonic and astral." (p. 296)
- **Figures.** Pl. 14d (the plan of the twenty-four atria within the circle,
  discussed as entry 42).
- **Related.** Descends from the encyclopaedic wheels of *Shadows*; the memory
  rooms of the Peregrinator (23) and the shops of the Doctor (21); the
  "edifices" of the double picture (13); compared with Campanella's City of
  the Sun (44); Paepp's lists of magic images recall it (46).

## 41. *Images*, Part II: the twelve celestial principles (astral statues with talismanic images)

- **Name.** The twelve "principles" or figures, "the causes of all things",
  under "the ineffable and infigurable Optimus Maximus"; Yates: "the celestial
  system of Images, inner statues magically assimilated to the influences of
  the stars".
- **Author, date, source.** Bruno, *Images*, Part II; Op. lat., II (iii),
  pp. 200 ff.; analysed in G.B. and H.T., pp. 326 ff.
- **Principle.** JUPITER (with Juno), SATURN, MARS, MERCURY, MINERVA, APOLLO,
  AESCULAPIUS (with Circe, Arion, Orpheus), SOL, LUNA, VENUS, CUPID, TELLUS
  (with Ocean, Neptune, Pluto): the great statues of the cosmic gods, each
  surrounded by large numbers of talismanic or magic images composed to draw
  their powers into the psyche. Yates reads it as Ficinian talismanic magic
  applied to memory images, aimed at strong Solar, Jovial and Venereal
  influences in the personality of the Magus; "ideas" in the title means such
  astral images. Here the mnemonic tradition (striking images) and the
  talismanic tradition (astral power in images) fuse.
- **Where described.** Chapter XIII, p. 296; pp. 298–299.
- **Quotations.**
  - "twelve tremendous figures or 'principles' which are said to be the causes
    of all things, under the 'ineffable and infigurable Optimus Maximus'."
    (p. 296)
  - "Bruno is here applying the Ficinian talismanic magic to memory images,
    probably with the idea of drawing particularly strong Solar, Jovial, and
    Venereal influences into the personality" (p. 296)
  - "he as it were both 'talismanises' mnemonic images and introduces mnemonic
    aspects into talismans as he 'composes' the latter to suit his purposes."
    (p. 299)
- **Figures.** None.
- **Related.** The Statues (38); the central images wheel of *Shadows*; the
  Asclepius statue-magic; animates the atria (40) through the combined Seal
  (42).

## 42. *Images*: the combined round-and-square Seal ("Alta Astra")

- **Name.** The diagram of the arrangement of the twenty-four atria; Yates:
  "the memory building of the system in Images as a whole", perhaps "the memory
  temple of an astral religion".
- **Author, date, source.** Bruno, *Images*; the diagram reproduced as Pl. 14d
  (the lettering on the central circle is unexplained in the text and
  inaccurately reproduced in the nineteenth-century edition).
- **Principle.** A square plan of the twenty-four memory rooms, each atrium and
  the whole plan related to the four points of the compass, enclosed in a
  circle that Yates takes to be the heavens on which the celestial figures and
  images of Part II are inscribed: "the round celestial system animating,
  organising, unifying" the detail of the lower world memorised in the rooms,
  so that the world is remembered from above. She suggests this realises the
  "double picture" of Seal 12 used simultaneously, and reads the letters on the
  central circle as possibly "Alta Astra".
- **Where described.** Chapter XIII, p. 297; compared p. 298.
- **Quotations.**
  - "a round building representing the heaven with a square lay-out inside it,
    a building reflecting the upper and the lower worlds in which the world as
    a whole is remembered from above" (p. 297)
  - "This system would be using the 'double picture' simultaneously, combining
    the round celestial system with the square system composed of the memory
    rooms." (p. 297)
  - "do the letters on that circle begin to read as 'Alta Astra'? Is this the
    memory temple of an astral religion?" (p. 297)
- **Figures.** Pl. 14d.
- **Related.** Joins entries 40 and 41; fulfils Zeuxis's double picture (13);
  compared with the round temple at the centre of Campanella's City of the Sun
  (44); the round-in-square motif of Squaring the Circle (19).

## 43. *Images*, Part III: the German Thirty Seals

- **Name.** "Thirty Seals" (the third part of *Images*); Yates: "these German
  Seals", "these latest Seals".
- **Author, date, source.** Bruno, Frankfort 1591; Op. lat., II (iii), third
  part; connected with the Frankfort Latin poems (*De immenso*, *De triplici
  minimo*, *De monade*).
- **Principle.** A second listing of types of occult memory system, many with
  the same titles as the English Seals but "if possible, even more obscure",
  some described in Latin verse; new developments in "pseudo-mathematical, or
  'mathesistical', place systems". They do not lead up to a Seal of Seals.
  Yates declines further discussion, as the book is directed to Bruno's
  influence in England.
- **Where described.** Chapter XIII, pp. 293–294.
- **Quotations.**
  - "Many of these are the same as in the English Seals with the same titles,
    but these latest Seals are, if possible, even more obscure than the earlier
    ones." (p. 293)
  - "There may be new developments in these latest Seals, particularly in the
    elaboration of pseudo-mathematical, or 'mathesistical', place systems."
    (p. 294)
  - "they do not lead up to a 'Seal of Seals' revealing the religion of Love,
    Art, Mathesis, and Magic as the English series did." (p. 294)
- **Figures.** None.
- **Related.** The English Thirty Seals (7); the "semi-mathematical" places
  (6); flagged as the point of departure for a study of Bruno's influence in
  Germany.

## 44. Campanella's City of the Sun used as "local memory"

- **Name.** *Città del Sole* (City of the Sun) as a memory system, "using the
  world as a book".
- **Author, date, source.** Tommaso Campanella, written c. 1602 in the
  Inquisition's prisons at Naples, first published in Latin 1623; his letters
  (ed. Spampanato, pp. 27, 28, 160, 194) and Firpo's list for the repeated
  statement that the City or a model of it could serve local memory.
- **Principle.** A round Utopian city with a round temple at its centre
  depicting all the stars with their relations to things below; concentric
  circular walls (giri) on which are painted all mathematical figures,
  animals, birds, fishes, metals, human inventions and activities, with statues
  of great men and founders of religions on the outermost wall: an
  encyclopaedic lay-out with a "celestial" organising basis, static in a City
  as Camillo's is in a Theatre, and so far simpler than Bruno's systems.
- **Where described.** Chapter XIII, pp. 297–298.
- **Quotations.**
  - "The city is round, with a round temple in its centre on which are said to
    be depicted all the stars of heaven with their relationships to things here
    below." (pp. 297–298)
  - "his City of the Sun, or perhaps some model of it, could be used for 'local
    memory', as a very quick way of knowing everything 'using the world as a
    book'." (p. 298)
  - "It is much simpler than Bruno's systems because static in a City (as
    Camillo's system is static in a Theatre) and does not attempt Bruno's awful
    complexities." (p. 298)
- **Figures.** None.
- **Related.** Compared with the "Alta Astra" altar of *Images* (42) and with
  *Shadows*; both Bruno and Campanella trained in the Dominican convent at
  Naples; Camillo's Theatre.

## 45. Lambert Schenkel, *De memoria* and *Gazophylacium*

- **Name.** *De memoria* (Douai 1593); *Gazophylacium* (Strasburg 1610; French
  as *Le Magazin des Sciences*, Paris 1623).
- **Author, date, source.** Lambert Schenkel (1547–c. 1603), of the Catholic Low
  Countries, Louvain-trained, public memory performer, later accused of magic;
  he charged fees and withheld the full secrets from his books.
- **Principle.** Basically the classical art in the Romberch/Rossellius
  text-book stream, with long sections on places (diagrams of rooms containing
  memory places) and on images, and constant quotation of Thomas Aquinas to
  attach it to the Dominican tradition; a long, well-referenced history of the
  art (Simonides, Metrodorus, Tullius, Petrarch, Pico). "It could be a rational
  mnemotechnic", but obscure and citing suspect authors such as Trithemius.
- **Where described.** Chapter XIII, pp. 299–301.
- **Quotations.**
  - "What Schenkel teaches appears to be in no way unusual; it is basically the
    classical art, with long sections on places, giving diagrams of rooms
    containing memory places, and long sections on images." (p. 301)
  - "the full secrets were not, so he said, revealed in his books." (p. 300)
  - "But he is very obscure and he mentions some rather suspect authors, such
    as Trithemius." (p. 301)
- **Figures.** None.
- **Related.** A post-Brunian memory teacher; its hidden side is "detected" by
  Paepp (46); the Louvain revival (Mameranus, 1560–61) noted.

## 46. Johannes Paepp, *Schenkelius detectus* (the occult memory revealed)

- **Name.** *Schenkelius detectus: seu memoria artificialis hactenus occultata*
  (Lyons 1617), continued in *Eisagoge, seu introductio facilis in praxim
  artificiosae memoriae* and *Crisis, iani phaosphori, in quo Schenkelius
  illustratur* (both Lyons 1619).
- **Author, date, source.** Johannes Paepp, Schenkel's disciple and imitator;
  Bruno named (noted by Rossi from Badaloni); *Eisagoge* pp. 36–113 and *Crisis*
  pp. 12–13 quote *Shadows*; *Schenkelius detectus* p. 21; *Crisis* pp. 26–7
  for the quotation sequence.
- **Principle.** Reveals the "arcane philosophical mysteries" hidden in
  Schenkel's memory as Brunian: long lists of magic images to be used as
  memory images, reminiscent of *Images*. The key passage runs from Thomas's
  Summa (II, II, 49) on order in memory, to the fifth treatise of the Pimander
  on the order of the universe as revelation of God, to the Timaeus, to Cicero
  *De oratore* on order, to Ad Herennium on places and images, and back to
  Aristotle and Thomas on frequent meditation: the thought-sequence by which
  the Tullian and Thomist places and images became a technique for imprinting
  the universal order on memory, i.e. how artificial memory turned into occult
  memory.
- **Where described.** Chapter XIII, pp. 301–302.
- **Quotations.**
  - "his long lists of magic images to be used as memory images are very
    reminiscent of those in Images." (p. 301)
  - "The passage shows a transition from the places and images of the
    artificial memory to the order of the universe ecstatically perceived as a
    religious experience by 'Trismegistus'." (p. 302)
  - "how the techniques of artificial memory turned into the magico-religious
    techniques of the occult memory." (p. 302)
- **Figures.** None.
- **Related.** From *Shadows* and *Images* via Bruno; the same Hermetic
  treatise (Pimander V) is quoted in Camillo's *L'Idea del Theatro* and alluded
  to by Dicson (32); with Schenkel (45), evidence that memory teaching could
  carry a Hermetic sect's message.

## 47. The Rosicrucian vault and Royal Arch symbolism as occult memory — hypothesis

- **Name.** The "vault" of the *Fama Fraternitatis* (Cassel 1614) with its
  rotae; the "speculative" architecture of Freemasonry, especially Royal Arch
  prints, banners and aprons.
- **Author, date, source.** Anonymous manifesto, 1614 (Waite's translation,
  pp. 75, 77); Royal Arch illustrations in B. E. Jones, *Freemasons' Book of
  the Royal Arch* (1957); Knoop and Jones, *The Genesis of Freemasonry* (1947);
  rumour of Bruno's "Giordanisti" sect in Germany.
- **Principle.** Yates's closing suggestion, explicitly outside the book's
  scope: a sacred vault whose walls, ceiling and floor are divided into
  compartments each with figures or sentences, and mysterious wheels, "could be
  something like an occult use of artificial memory"; the symbolic columns,
  arches and geometrical figures of speculative masonry might descend from the
  imaginary architecture of Renaissance occult memory (Camillo, Bruno) rather
  than from operative masons' guilds.
- **Where described.** Chapter XIII, pp. 303–305.
- **Quotations.**
  - "speaks of mysterious rotae or wheels, and of a sacred 'vault' the walls,
    ceiling and floor of which was divided into compartments each with their
    several figures or sentences." (p. 304)
  - "This could be something like an occult use of artificial memory." (p. 304)
  - "the imaginary or 'speculative' architecture of the art of memory as the
    vehicle of its teachings." (p. 304)
- **Figures.** None.
- **Related.** Descends, in the hypothesis, from Camillo's Theatre and Bruno's
  Seals and *Images*; linked to Dicson's underground English form (32). Yates
  contrasts Bruno's industrious system-building with "the lazy magic of the Ars
  notoria" (p. 306), a mention only.
