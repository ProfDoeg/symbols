## 1. The Simonides method: places and images (loci and imagines)

- **Name.** The "art of memory" invented by Simonides; the classical "mnemonic of places
  and images". Yates prefers "art of memory" to "mnemotechnics" for it.
- **Exponent, date, source.** Simonides of Ceos (c. 556–468 BC), legendary inventor; told by
  Cicero, *De oratore* II, lxxxvi, 351–4 (Antonius's speech), with variants in Quintilian
  XI, ii, 11–16, and attested by Pliny, Aelian, Ammianus, Suidas and the Parian Chronicle
  (marble of c. 264 BC, entry for 477 BC, "the inventor of the system of memory-aids").
- **Principle.** After the collapse of Scopas's banqueting hall Simonides identified the
  mangled corpses by remembering where each guest had sat; from this he inferred that order
  is the key to memory and that sight is the strongest sense. One selects places, forms
  mental images of the things to be remembered, and stores the images in the places, so that
  the order of the places preserves the order of the things and the images denote the things
  themselves. Places and images are used "as a wax writing-tablet and the letters written on
  it".
- **Where.** Ch. I, pp. 1–4 (story, general principles, primacy of sight); Ch. II, pp. 27–30
  (Simonides's life, ut pictura poesis, Parian Chronicle).
- **Quotations.**
  - "he realised that orderly arrangement is essential for good memory." (p. 2)
  - (Cicero) "we shall employ the places and images respectively as a wax writing-tablet
    and the letters written on it." (p. 2)
  - "The classical sources seem to be describing inner techniques which depend on visual
    impressions of almost incredible intensity." (p. 4)
  - "Simonides is the cult hero, the founder of our subject" (p. 28)
- **Figures/plates.** None in this stretch.
- **Related.** Every later system in the book: *Ad Herennium* (4–7), Cicero (9), Quintilian
  (2, 11), the medieval transformation (20–23), and, as Yates says on p. 6, even Bruno's
  *De umbris idearum* is "going through yet once again the old, old business of rules for
  places, rules for images, memory for things, memory for words". Camillo's Theatre (Ch. VI)
  uses images on places "in a perfectly correct way" (p. 37).

## 2. Quintilian's architectural mnemonic (the building walked through)

- **Name.** The "architectural type" of mnemonic place system; Quintilian's description of
  the method of places and images. Yates uses it first, on p. 3, as "the clearest
  description of the process" before turning to *Ad Herennium*.
- **Exponent, date, source.** Quintilian, *Institutio oratoria* XI, ii, 17–22 (first century
  AD, "more than a century after Cicero's *De oratore*").
- **Principle.** A spacious and varied building is memorised in order: forecourt, atrium,
  round the impluvium, bedrooms, parlours, statues and ornaments. Signs (an anchor for
  navigation, a weapon for warfare) are placed on the memorised places; to recite, one
  begins from the first place and runs through all, "demanding what has been entrusted to
  them", so that the points come out "linked one to another as in a chorus". Places may be
  real or imaginary: houses, public buildings, a long journey, a city, or pictures.
- **Where.** Ch. I, pp. 3–4 (paraphrase) and pp. 21–23 (full quotation and comment).
- **Quotations.**
  - "We have to think of the ancient orator as moving in imagination through his memory
    building whilst he is making his speech" (p. 3)
  - (Quintilian) "What I have spoken of as being done in a house can also be done in public
    buildings, or on a long journey, or in going through a city, or with pictures." (p. 22)
  - "The perplexed student of the art of memory is grateful to Quintilian." (p. 23)
  - "It is in fact what we should call mnemotechnics. There was then, in antiquity, a
    practice of which that word can be used in the sense in which we use it." (p. 23)
- **Figures/plates.** None.
- **Related.** Yates stresses that the medieval tradition was NOT founded on Quintilian (p. 26,
  p. 56): his text on the mnemonic was not generally accessible before Poggio's find of 1416.
  His anchor-and-weapon images are the "Quintilian type" Yates recognises again in Martianus
  Capella's sword (18). His critique and "simpler precepts" are entry 11.

## 3. The object-placing mnemonic: the professor's party feat and Quintilian's auctioneer

- **Name.** Yates gives no name; the "private mnemotechnic" of a modern professor, which she
  identifies with the auctioneer's method Quintilian attributes to Hortensius.
- **Exponent, date, source.** An anonymous professor (anecdote told to Yates); Quintilian
  XI, ii, 23–26 (Hortensius after an auction; the elder Seneca's and Simplicius's feats of
  reciting backwards are cited on p. 16 from Seneca, *Controversiae* I, praef. 2, and
  Augustine, *De anima* IV, vii).
- **Principle.** Objects named by the company are placed in imagination on the window sill,
  the desk, the wastepaper basket, and so on; the places are then revisited in turn and
  their "deposits" demanded. Quintilian's users likewise "place the things themselves in
  their memory places", a table in the forecourt, a platform in the atrium. Yates uses it to
  show the classical method "will work for anyone", and that the trained memory could run
  its places backwards as well as forwards.
- **Where.** Ch. I, pp. 3–4, 16, 24.
- **Quotations.**
  - "He performed his little memory feat by placing the objects, as they were named, on the
    window sill, on the desk, on the wastepaper basket, and so on." (p. 3)
  - "He had never heard of the classical mnemonic but had discovered his technique quite
    independently." (p. 3)
  - "The method of the auctioneer who places images of the actual objects he has sold on
    memory places is precisely the method used by the professor" (p. 24)
- **Figures/plates.** None.
- **Related.** Quintilian allows this simple form but not its extension to speeches (11).

## 4. The *Ad Herennium* artificial memory and its rules for places

- **Name.** "Artificial memory" (*memoria artificiosa*) as against natural memory; "rules for
  places" (the loci). In the Middle Ages, "the Second Rhetoric of Tullius" / "Rhetorica nova".
- **Exponent, date, source.** Anonymous Roman rhetoric teacher, c. 86–82 BC; *Rhetorica ad
  Herennium* III, xvi–xxiv (memory section; places at III, xvi–xix). Drawn from lost Greek
  treatises; the "only complete source" for the classical art. Attributed to Cicero
  ("Tullius") throughout the Middle Ages.
- **Principle.** "Constat igitur artificiosa memoria ex locis et imaginibus." A locus is a
  place easily grasped by memory: a house, an intercolumnar space, a corner, an arch. Places
  must form a numbered series that can be run in either direction; every fifth locus is
  marked (a golden hand at the fifth, a friend named Decimus at the tenth). They should be
  formed in deserted buildings, be unlike one another, of moderate size, neither too bright
  nor too dark, about thirty feet apart; the loci are permanent and reusable like wax tablets
  once the images are effaced. Thought may also construct "fictitious places" at will.
- **Where.** Ch. I, pp. 4–8 (the treatise and the place rules); pp. 16 (summary of its
  character); cf. Ch. III, pp. 54–56 on its medieval transmission, and p. 63 on Albertus's
  reading of the five place rules.
- **Quotations.**
  - "The artificial memory is established from places and images ... the stock definition to
    be forever repeated down the ages." (p. 6)
  - (Ad Herennium) "For the places are very much like wax tablets or papyrus, the images
    like the letters, the arrangement and disposition of the images like the script, and the
    delivery is like the reading." (p. 7)
  - "what strikes me most about them is the astonishing visual precision which they imply."
    (p. 8)
  - "Who is that man moving slowly in the lonely building, stopping at intervals with an
    intent face? He is a rhetoric student forming a set of memory loci." (p. 8)
- **Figures/plates.** None (Yates regrets that the treatise gives rules but never a whole
  set of images on places, p. 10–11).
- **Related.** The plan of every later *Ars memorativa* treatise (p. 6); Albertus's and
  Thomas's rules (21, 22); the "real"/"fictitious" places distinction reappears as the
  Rosaio's "natural"/"artificial" places (26) and Albertus's preference for real buildings.

## 5. *Ad Herennium* rules for images: the imagines agentes

- **Name.** "Rules for images"; the *imagines agentes*, "active images".
- **Exponent, date, source.** As entry 4; *Ad Herennium* III, xxii (psychological reasons
  for choice of images) and III, xxiii, 39 (the tutorial principle of teaching method only).
- **Principle.** Nature is stirred not by the ordinary but by the novel and striking, so
  images must be striking similitudes: active human figures of exceptional beauty or singular
  ugliness, crowned or in purple cloaks, bloodstained, muddied, smeared with red paint, or
  comic. The places must be run over rapidly and often to refresh the images. The instructor
  teaches the method and gives a few examples only; each student invents his own images.
- **Where.** Ch. I, pp. 9–11; character summarised pp. 16–17; their medieval fate Ch. III,
  pp. 66–67, 77.
- **Quotations.**
  - (Ad Herennium) "if we set up images that are not many or vague but active (imagines
    agentes); if we assign to them exceptional beauty or singular ugliness" (p. 10)
  - "Our author has clearly got hold of the idea of helping memory by arousing emotional
    affects through these striking and unusual images, beautiful or hideous, comic or
    obscene." (p. 10)
  - "They remind one more of figures in some Gothic cathedral than of classical art proper."
    (p. 16)
  - "They appear to be completely amoral, their function being solely to give an emotional
    impetus to memory by their personal idiosyncracy or their strangeness." (pp. 16–17)
- **Figures/plates.** None here; Yates later reads Giotto's virtues and vices (Pl. 3) and
  the Vienna manuscript virtues (Pl. 4a, b) as imagines agentes.
- **Related.** Quintilian omits them (11); the Dialexeis's gods and heroes are their
  "archaically simple form" (13); Albertus quotes them in full into a treatise on ethics
  (21); moralised into virtues and vices (23, 27, 28); Martianus's personified liberal arts
  conform to them (18).

## 6. *Ad Herennium* memory for things (memoria rerum): the lawsuit image

- **Name.** "Memory for things" (*memoria rerum*), images for *res*.
- **Exponent, date, source.** *Ad Herennium* III, xx, 33; the distinction res/verba from
  Cicero, *De inventione* I, vii, 9.
- **Principle.** One image on one locus recalls a whole "thing" (argument, notion, fact). The
  specimen: the poisoned man lying ill in bed (resembling someone known), the defendant at the
  bedside with a cup (poison) in his right hand, tablets (the will) in his left, and on the
  fourth finger a ram's testicles (*testes*, witnesses). The following loci hold the rest of
  the case. Yates reads it as a lawyer's "filing cabinet in memory".
- **Where.** Ch. I, pp. 8–9 (res/verba defined), 11–13 (the image and Cicero's confirmation).
- **Quotations.**
  - (Ad Herennium) "we shall place the defendant at the bedside, holding in his right hand a
    cup, in his left, tablets, and on the fourth finger, a ram's testicles." (p. 11)
  - "It is as though, as a lawyer, he is forming a filing cabinet in memory of his cases."
    (p. 12)
  - "Though everything appears to be explained, I yet find this image baffling." (p. 12)
- **Figures/plates.** None.
- **Related.** Ram's testicles possibly an allusion to Aries as first sign, i.e. to
  Metrodorus's zodiacal order (12, p. 41); Albertus, using a corrupt text, turns *medico*
  into a doctor holding the testicles (21, p. 65) and later a ram "with huge horns and
  testicles" charging in the dark (p. 68); composite image with grouped secondary figures for
  the "parts" of a virtue (p. 21), Martianus's liberal arts (18), Lorenzetti's Justice (27).

## 7. *Ad Herennium* memory for words (memoria verborum): Domitius/Reges and Aesopus/Cimber

- **Name.** "Memory for words" (*memoria verborum*), images for *verba*.
- **Exponent, date, source.** *Ad Herennium* III, xxi, 34 (verse "Iam domum itionem reges
  Atridae parant"); III, xxiv, 40 (exhortation to exercise).
- **Principle.** Far more places are needed than for things. The line is remembered by two
  imagines agentes: "Domitius raising his hands to heaven while he is lashed by the Marcii
  Reges" (sound resemblance Domitius-Reges → domum itionem reges) and "Aesopus and Cimber
  being dressed for the roles of Agamemnon and Menelaus in Iphigenaia" (→ Atridae parant).
  The verse must also be learned by heart in the ordinary way, the images acting as cues;
  memory for words is an exercise to strengthen memory for things.
- **Where.** Ch. I, pp. 13–16.
- **Quotations.**
  - "The vivid image immediately brought to mind 'Domitius-Reges' and this reminded by sound
    resemblance of 'domum itionem reges'." (p. 14)
  - "The classical art is systematising that process." (p. 14)
  - "Did one really learn the poem by heart but set up some places with 'cue' images on them
    at strategic intervals?" (p. 15)
- **Figures/plates.** None.
- **Related.** The Greek notae method (8) is the rejected alternative; Cicero's obscure
  "similar words changed and unchanged in case" (9); Albertus's struggle with a corrupt text
  (21, p. 65–66); Yates's suggestion that the Middle Ages understood memory for words as
  inscriptions on images (31).

## 8. The Greek method of ready-made images for words (shorthand notae)

- **Name.** The "Greek" memory for words; images "that correspond to a great many words";
  Yates's conjecture: shorthand *notae* memorised on places, "a kind of inner stenography".
- **Exponent, date, source.** Unnamed Greek writers on memory, reported and rejected in
  *Ad Herennium* III, xxiii, 38; Quintilian XI, ii, 23–26 ("like short-hand writers, have
  definite images for everything"); Cicero, *De oratore* II, lxxxviii, 360 on Charmadas and
  Metrodorus writing down in places by images. Tironian notes: Milne, *Greek Shorthand
  Manuals*.
- **Principle.** A fixed stock of symbols, one per word, learned by heart so that images are
  ready without searching; the symbols are "written" inwardly on the memory places. Rejected
  by *Ad Herennium* because a thousand symbols cannot cover all words; Quintilian thinks this
  is what Charmadas and Metrodorus did and that it impedes the flow of speech.
- **Where.** Ch. I, pp. 15, 24–25; Ch. II, pp. 41–42 (Metrodorus), 43 (Ars Notoria as
  possible descendant).
- **Quotations.**
  - "It is possible that these Greek images for words are shorthand symbols or notae the use
    of which was coming into fashion in the Latin world at this time." (p. 15)
  - "by a kind of inner stenography, the shorthand symbols were written down inwardly and
    memorised on the memory places." (p. 15)
  - "this was the Greek method which the author of Ad Herennium discarded but which
    Quintilian thinks that Cicero admired in Charmadas and Metrodorus of Scepsis." (p. 25)
- **Figures/plates.** None.
- **Related.** Metrodorus (12); the Ars Notoria (16) "perhaps a bastard descendant ... of
  that difficult branch of it which used the shorthand notae" (p. 43); Quintilian's
  tablet method (11) substitutes ordinary writing for notae.

## 9. Cicero's version of the art (De oratore)

- **Name.** Cicero's "potted version of the rules"; "a highly condensed little Ars
  memorativa treatise".
- **Exponent, date, source.** Cicero, *De oratore* (finished 55 BC) I, xxxiv, 157 (Crassus);
  II, lxxiv, 299–300 (Themistocles); II, lxxxvi–lxxxviii, 351–360 (Antonius: Simonides
  story, rules, memory for words, Charmadas and Metrodorus).
- **Principle.** Same technique as *Ad Herennium*: many places, well lighted, in order, at
  moderate intervals; images "active, sharply defined, unusual" which strike the mind
  quickly. Memory for things is the orator's special property, imprinted by "the several
  masks (singulis personis)" on places; memory for words uses more images, including images
  of one word to recall a whole sentence and images "of similar words changed and unchanged
  in case", "as a consummate painter". Defends the art against the "inert" who fear a weight
  of images; cites the "almost divine" memories of Charmadas and Metrodorus.
- **Where.** Ch. I, pp. 17–20; p. 26 (not the foundation of the medieval tradition); Ch. III,
  pp. 55–56 (De oratore known only incompletely in the Middle Ages).
- **Quotations.**
  - (Cicero) "one must employ a large number of places which must be well lighted, clearly set
    out in order, at moderate intervals apart" (p. 17)
  - "The use of the word persona of the memory-for-things image is interesting and curious."
    (p. 18)
  - "Cicero is a believer and a defender. He evidently had by nature a fantastically acute
    visual memory." (p. 19)
  - "Cicero's elegant and obscure formulations" (p. 26)
- **Figures/plates.** None.
- **Related.** Quintilian quotes Cicero's abbreviation of the rules (2, 11); Petrarch uses
  *De oratore* rather than *Ad Herennium* (34); Cicero's *Tusculans* transposes memoria and
  inventio into proofs of the soul's divinity (see 15, 17).

## 10. Memory as a part of Prudence (the Tullian ethical frame)

- **Name.** Memory (*memoria*) as one of the three parts of Prudence (*memoria,
  intelligentia, providentia*); not itself a technique but, in Yates's account, the doctrine
  that made the artificial memory a moral duty in the Middle Ages.
- **Exponent, date, source.** Cicero, *De inventione* II, liii, 160 (c. 86–82 BC); known in
  the Middle Ages as the "First Rhetoric" or "Old Rhetoric" of Tullius, paired with
  *Ad Herennium* as "Second"/"New Rhetoric" (Rhetorica nova et vetus, Venice 1470).
- **Principle.** Virtue has four parts, Prudence, Justice, Fortitude, Temperance; Prudence's
  parts are memory (recalls what has happened), intelligence (ascertains what is), foresight
  (sees what is to come). Since Tullius in his First Rhetoric makes memory a part of Prudence
  and in his Second gives an art for improving memory, practising the artificial memory is a
  part of the virtue of Prudence. Yates wonders whether a stoic, ethical use of the fantasy
  already lay behind this in antiquity.
- **Where.** Ch. I, pp. 20–21; Ch. III, pp. 54–55, 57, 61–62 (Albertus), 73–74 (Thomas);
  Ch. IV, pp. 95, 97, 102.
- **Quotations.**
  - (Cicero) "Prudence is the knowledge of what is good, what is bad and what is neither good
    nor bad. Its parts are memory, intelligence, foresight" (p. 20)
  - "Therefore the practice of the artificial memory is a part of the virtue of Prudence."
    (p. 21)
  - "The argument was beautifully symmetrical" (p. 21)
  - "it rides with Prudence in a chariot of which Tullius is the driver, whipping up his two
    horses of the First and Second Rhetorics." (p. 67)
- **Figures/plates.** Pl. 1 (Tullius seated before Rhetoric in the Santa Maria Novella
  fresco, pp. 79–80); Pl. 4a (Prudence with the eight Thomist parts, p. 100).
- **Related.** Alcuin knows the parts of Prudence but not the art (19); Boncompagno (20);
  Albertus (21); Thomas's eight parts of Prudence (22); Dante's three cantiche as memoria,
  intelligentia, providentia (30); Ridevall's Saturn/Juno/Neptune/Pluto (31); Petrarch's
  *Rerum memorandarum* (34).

## 11. Quintilian's simpler precepts: memorising from the written tablet or page

- **Name.** Quintilian's "precepts ... of a simpler kind"; Yates: "Quintilianist memorising
  of a prepared written page".
- **Exponent, date, source.** Quintilian, *Institutio oratoria* XI, ii, 23–26 (critique),
  32–33 (the tablet method); XI, ii, 4 (wax-seal theory of memory). Martianus Capella's
  variant, *De nuptiis*, ed. Dick pp. 268–70.
- **Principle.** Rather than notae on a vast place system, learn a passage by heart from the
  same tablets on which it was written, so that the mind's eye is fixed on the page and its
  individual lines and one "speaks as though reading aloud"; privately invented marks may be
  set against difficult passages. Quintilian rejects the imagines agentes, doubts images for
  "things" or for a connected speech, and "may keep their systems for themselves" he says of
  Charmadas and Metrodorus.
- **Where.** Ch. I, pp. 24–26; Ch. III, pp. 51–52 (Martianus recommends the same: write the
  matter down, divide it, set notae at points, murmur it by night), p. 53.
- **Quotations.**
  - (Quintilian) "namely to learn a passage by heart from the same tablets on which he has
    committed it to writing." (p. 25)
  - "instead of attempting to visualise shorthand notae on some vast place system it
    visualises ordinary writing as actually placed on the tablet or page." (p. 25)
  - "Evidently the imagines agentes, fantastically gesticulating from their places ... seemed
    to him as cumbrous and useless for practical mnemonic purposes as they do to us."
    (pp. 25–26)
- **Figures/plates.** None.
- **Related.** Martianus (18); Yates notes it "might still have been useful" in the
  monasteries when oratory ceased (p. 53); Holcot's images placed on the pages of a text
  (32) is a medieval combination of page and image.

## 12. Metrodorus of Scepsis's zodiacal memory (360 places in the twelve signs)

- **Name.** The memory system of Metrodorus of Scepsis "based on the twelve signs of the
  zodiac"; the first "celestial memory system".
- **Exponent, date, source.** Metrodorus of Scepsis, Greek rhetor at the court of
  Mithridates (fl. c. 100–70 BC, "still living" in Cicero's time). Sources: Quintilian XI,
  ii, 22 ("three hundred and sixty places in the twelve signs"); Cicero, *De oratore* II,
  lxxxviii, 360 ("almost divine" memory); Pliny, *Natural History* VII, 24 (art "perfected"
  by Metrodorus, who could repeat what he heard "in the very same words"); Strabo XIII, i,
  55; Plutarch, *Lucullus*. Reconstruction by L. A. Post, *Classical Weekly* XV (1932).
- **Principle.** (Post's conjecture, endorsed by Yates as "sensible") the zodiac divided into
  12 signs and 36 decans with their decan-figures; ten loci grouped under each decan yield a
  numbered series 1–360, any place findable by calculation, none missable. The astrological
  images serve as the order of places, as memorised buildings do in the ordinary method; if
  Quintilian is right, Metrodorus then "wrote" shorthand notae on these places.
- **Where.** Ch. I, pp. 19–20, 23–25; Ch. II, pp. 39–42; Ch. IV, p. 100 (compared with the
  Thomist orders of inscribed images).
- **Quotations.**
  - (Quintilian) "how Metrodorus can have found three hundred and sixty places in the twelve
    signs through which the sun moves." (p. 23)
  - "Every subsequent user of a celestial memory system will invoke Metrodorus of Scepsis as
    the classical authority for bringing the stars into memory." (p. 39)
  - (Post) "Metrodorus probably grouped ten artificial backgrounds (loci) under each decan
    figure. He would thus have a series of loci numbered 1 to 360" (p. 40)
  - "It had to do with memory for words, possibly through memorising the notae or symbols of
    shorthand, and was connected with the zodiac. That is all we really know." (p. 42)
- **Figures/plates.** None in this stretch. The descendants are illustrated later: Pl. 11–12
  (Bruno's *De umbris idearum* wheels and decan images), Fig. 8, Pl. 16 (Fludd's zodiac).
- **Related.** Possible explanation of the ram's testicles in the lawsuit image (6, p. 41);
  Albertus's nocturnal ram (21, p. 69: "we might wonder whether Albertus thought that the ram
  was Aries"); decan images "certainly believed to be magical images" (p. 42), the root of
  the Renaissance occult systems (Bruno Ch. IX, Fludd Ch. XV); Thomist inscribed orders (33).

## 13. The Dialexeis sophist mnemonic (Hippias of Elis)

- **Name.** The memory section of the *Dialexeis* (Dissoi Logoi); Yates: "this earliest Ars
  memorativa treatise", "the sophist memory treatise".
- **Exponent, date, source.** Anonymous sophist fragment, c. 400 BC (Diels, *Fragmente der
  Vorsokratiker* II, p. 345); possibly reflecting the "science of memory" of Hippias of Elis
  (Greater Hippias 285D–286A, Lesser Hippias 368D).
- **Principle.** Three rules: pay attention; repeat what you hear; "what you hear, place on
  what you know". For names, etymological dissection: Chrysippus placed on chrysos (gold)
  and hippos (horse), Pyrilampes on pyr and lampein. For things, place them on gods and
  heroes: courage on Mars and Achilles, metal-working on Vulcan, cowardice on Epeus. No
  rules for places are given, but the practice of placing the thing to be remembered on the
  image "will recur all through the history of the art".
- **Where.** Ch. II, pp. 29–31 (p. 31 is scrambled in the extraction; see header).
- **Quotations.**
  - (Dialexeis) "Thirdly, what you hear, place on what you know." (p. 30)
  - "Memory for things; memory for words (or names)! Here are the technical terms for the two
    kinds of artificial memory already in use in 400 B.C." (p. 30)
  - "Here we may perhaps see in an archaically simple form those human figures representing
    'things' which eventually developed into the imagines agentes." (p. 30)
- **Figures/plates.** None.
- **Related.** Ancestor of the *Ad Herennium* rules (Yates: Simonides "invented, or codified"
  rules of this kind, p. 30); Plato's satire on sophist etymologies may aim at it (15, p. 37);
  Hippias also the originator of the liberal-arts scheme handed on by Martianus (18, p. 52).

## 14. Aristotle's doctrine of memory and reminiscence (as used by the scholastics)

- **Name.** Aristotle on memory and reminiscence; for Albertus and Thomas "a kind of memory
  treatise, to be conflated with the rules of Tullius".
- **Exponent, date, source.** Aristotle, *De memoria et reminiscentia* 449b–452a (esp. 450b
  1–10 wax seal; 451b 18–20 association; 452a 8–16 places and the milk-white-air-damp-autumn
  chain; 452a 16–25 the ABCDEFGH series); *De anima* 427b, 431b, 432b ("the soul never
  thinks without a mental picture"); *Topica* 163b 24–30; *De insomniis* 458b 20–22. A lost
  book on mnemonics listed by Diogenes Laertius V, 26.
- **Principle.** Imagination mediates between sense and thought; memory is a store of
  mental pictures from sense impressions with a time element, stamped like a seal on wax.
  Reminiscence (recollection) is a deliberate hunt through memory's contents by association
  (similar, contrary, contiguous) and by order, from a starting point; "some use places for
  the purposes of recollecting"; one may start from the middle of a series and travel in
  either direction. Aristotle only illustrates from the mnemonic, but the scholastics took
  him to justify the places (order, starting-point) and images (no thought without a
  phantasm) of Tullius.
- **Where.** Ch. II, pp. 31–35; Ch. III, pp. 61, 63–64, 67–68, 70–72, 75 (scholastic use).
- **Quotations.**
  - "'the soul never thinks without a mental picture'" (p. 32)
  - "Aristotle's work thus became for them a kind of memory treatise, to be conflated with
    the rules of Tullius and which provided philosophical and psychological justifications
    for those rules." (p. 32)
  - (Aristotle) "For this reason some use places for the purposes of recollecting." (p. 34)
  - "It is however very doubtful whether this is what Aristotle meant. He appears to use his
    references to the mnemonic technique only as illustrations of his argument." (p. 35)
- **Figures/plates.** None here (Fig. 9, faculty psychology after Romberch, belongs to
  Ch. XII).
- **Related.** Topics/topoi as mnemonic loci (p. 31); Albertus's ars reminiscendi and
  inspired melancholy (21); Thomas's second and fourth precepts (22); Bartolomeo's "Aristotile
  in libro memoria" (25); "Aristotle is essential for the scholastic and mediaeval form of the
  art; Plato is essential for the art in the Renaissance" (p. 39).

## 15. Plato's memory: the wax block, anamnesis, and the Theuth story

- **Name.** "Memory in the Platonic sense"; "a Platonic memory ... organised ... in relation
  to the realities". A doctrine, not a technique; Yates treats it as the ground of the
  Renaissance occult memory systems.
- **Exponent, date, source.** Plato, *Theaetetus* 191 C–D (wax block, "the gift of Memory,
  the mother of the Muses"); *Phaedo* 75 B–D (Idea of Equality latent in memory); *Phaedrus*
  249 E–250 D (recollection of the Ideas; rhetoric as persuasion to truth) and 274 C–275 B
  (Theuth and Thamus on letters). Cicero's Platonism, *Tusculan Disputations* I, xxiv–xxv,
  59–65 (pp. 44–46).
- **Principle.** The soul carries a wax block on which perceptions are stamped; but true
  knowledge is recollection of the Ideas seen before birth, so a memory worthy of the name
  must be organised in relation to the realities, not by trivial mnemotechnics. Writing is an
  "elixir not of memory but of reminding". Cicero, in the Tusculans, turns memoria and
  inventio, the rhetorical parts, into proofs of the soul's divinity.
- **Where.** Ch. II, pp. 36–39, 44–46.
- **Quotations.**
  - "A Platonic memory would have to be organised, not in the trivial manner of such
    mnemotechnics, but in relation to the realities." (p. 37)
  - "The grandiose attempt to do just this, within the framework of the art of memory, was
    made by the Neoplatonists of the Renaissance." (p. 37)
  - (Plato) "You have invented an elixir not of memory but of reminding" (p. 38)
  - "Memory for things; memory for words! It is surely significant that the technical terms
    of the artificial memory come into the orator's mind when, as philosopher, he is proving
    the divinity of the soul." (p. 45)
- **Figures/plates.** None (the Camillo Theatre folder belongs to Ch. VI).
- **Related.** Camillo's Theatre "based (so he believes) on archetypes of reality" with
  "eternal places" (p. 37–38, Ch. VI); the Theuth passage used by a disciple of Bruno for the
  "Egyptian" inner writing (p. 39, ref. p. 268); Augustine (17).

## 16. Neopythagorean memory-training and the Ars Notoria

- **Name.** The *Ars Notoria*, "a magical art of memory attributed to Apollonius or sometimes
  to Solomon"; the religious memory-training of the Pythagorean revival.
- **Exponent, date, source.** Apollonius of Tyana as described by Philostratus, *Life of
  Apollonius* I, 14; III, 16, 41 (the Brahmin's seven planetary rings); Iamblichus, Porphyry,
  Diogenes Laertius on Pythagorean memory; Dionysius of Miletus accused of "Chaldaean arts"
  (Philostratus, *Lives of the Sophists*); the medieval Ars Notoria per Thorndike, *History
  of Magic* II, ch. 49; condemned by Thomas Aquinas (ref. p. 204).
- **Principle.** Apollonius practised silence, storing all in memory, and sang a hymn to
  memory. The Ars Notoria practitioner gazes at curiously marked figures called *notae*
  while reciting magical prayers, a different nota for each art or science, hoping to gain
  knowledge or memory of all the arts. Yates suggests it descends, transformed underground,
  from the shorthand-notae branch of the classical art.
- **Where.** Ch. II, pp. 42–43.
- **Quotations.**
  - "The practitioner of the Ars Notoria gazed at figures or diagrams curiously marked and
    called 'notae' whilst reciting magical prayers." (p. 43)
  - "The Ars Notoria is perhaps a bastard descendant of the classical art of memory, or of
    that difficult branch of it which used the shorthand notae." (p. 43)
  - "a memory based on the zodiac sounds rather awe-inspiring and might give rise to rumours
    of magical powers of memory." (p. 42)
- **Figures/plates.** None.
- **Related.** The Greek notae method (8); Metrodorus's magical decan images (12); Bruno's
  Hermetic memory (later chapters).

## 17. Augustine's memory: the fields and spacious palaces (Confessions X)

- **Name.** Augustine's meditation on memory; Yates: possibly "a Christianised artificial
  memory". A reflection, not a stated technique.
- **Exponent, date, source.** Augustine (d. 430), former teacher of rhetoric; *Confessions*
  X, 8–9, 17, 25–26; *De Trinitate* IX, 6, xi (the arch seen at Carthage); *Confessions*
  III, 4 (Cicero's *Hortensius*).
- **Principle.** Memory is entered as a series of buildings, "fields and spacious palaces",
  a "vast court", holding the thesauri of innumerable images from sense, but also the arts
  "by actual presence", the affections "by certain notions", and at last God, "in no place".
  Yates argues the extraordinary capacity and organisation, the refrain of images, and the
  problem of remembering notions with or without images betray a memory trained on the
  classical mnemonic in the buildings of the antique world.
- **Where.** Ch. II, pp. 46–49.
- **Quotations.**
  - (Augustine) "I come to the fields and spacious palaces of memory (campos et lata praetoria
    memoriae), where are the treasures (thesauri) of innumerable images" (p. 46)
  - "one gains, I think, quite strongly the impression that Augustine's was a trained memory,
    trained on the lines of the classical mnemonic." (p. 46)
  - "Would human images of 'things' such as Faith, Hope, and Charity ... have been 'placed'
    in such a memory, and might the places now have been memorised in churches?" (p. 48)
- **Figures/plates.** None.
- **Related.** Memory as one of the three powers of the soul (Memory, Understanding, Will,
  p. 49) is later joined to Tullius's Prudence (p. 79); Albertus's suggestion that a church is
  the best building for places (21, p. 63); the whole medieval transformation (23).

## 18. Martianus Capella's compressed art (De nuptiis Philologiae et Mercurii)

- **Name.** Martianus's "brief description of the artificial memory" under memoria as the
  fourth part of rhetoric; "incomprehensibly potted version of the rules in a setting of
  allegory".
- **Exponent, date, source.** Martianus Capella of Carthage, fifth century AD; *De nuptiis*,
  ed. Dick, pp. 268–70.
- **Principle.** Simonides story; order sustains the precepts; precepts pondered "in
  well-lighted places (in locis illustribus)" where "images of things (species rerum)" are
  placed (a veiled girl for a wedding, a sword for a murderer); places are wax, images
  letters. Then the Quintilianist advice: write the matter down, divide it into parts, set
  notae against points, meditate in a murmur, by night. Memory for things and for words,
  words not always to be memorised. Yates adds that the personified liberal arts of the
  allegory (Grammar with knife and file, Rhetoric armed and dressed with figures of speech)
  "conform remarkably well" to the classical rules for images.
- **Where.** Ch. III, pp. 50–53; p. 57.
- **Quotations.**
  - (Martianus) "(to remember) a wedding you may hold in mind a girl veiled with a
    wedding-veil; or a sword, or some other weapon, for a murderer" (p. 51)
  - "Rules for places are reduced to one only (well-lighted); rules for striking, imagines
    agentes are not given" (p. 52)
  - "The personified liberal arts conform remarkably well to the rules for images in the
    artificial memory" (p. 52)
  - "He thus handed on the art to the Middle Ages firmly lodged in its correct niche in the
    scheme of the liberal arts." (p. 50)
- **Figures/plates.** None (the seven liberal arts as figures appear in Pl. 1 and the Vienna
  MS, entries 23, 33).
- **Related.** Quintilian's tablet method (11); Albertus's nocturnal ram perhaps from "too
  much memory work in the night ... as advised by Martianus" (21, p. 69); the medieval
  student's only other source besides *Ad Herennium* (p. 57).

## 19. Alcuin's precepts: the art lost (Carolingian rhetoric)

- **Name.** Alcuin's "precepts" for memory; Yates's demonstration that the artificial memory
  had "disappeared" from the Carolingian rhetoric.
- **Exponent, date, source.** Alcuin, dialogue *De rhetorica et de virtutibus* for
  Charlemagne (c. 800), ed. Howell pp. 136–9, 146; sources *De inventione*, Julius Victor
  (who dismisses places and images, Halm, *Rhetores latini* p. 440), Cassiodorus, Isidore.
  Cassiodorus, Isidore and Bede do not mention the art.
- **Principle.** Memory is "the treasure-house of all things" (Tullius), but the only
  precepts are "exercise in memorising, practice in writing, application to study, and the
  avoidance of drunkenness". Charlemagne is however taught the four virtues and the three
  parts of Prudence: the First Rhetoric without the Second.
- **Where.** Ch. III, pp. 53–54.
- **Quotations.**
  - (Alcuin) "We have no other precepts about it, except exercise in memorising, practice in
    writing, application to study, and the avoidance of drunkenness" (p. 53)
  - "The artificial memory has disappeared! Its rules have gone, replaced by 'avoid
    drunkenness'!" (p. 53)
  - "he did not seem to know the second horse of the chariot, the Ad Herennium" (p. 54)
- **Figures/plates.** None.
- **Related.** Memory as part of Prudence (10); the twelfth–fourteenth-century vogue of
  *Ad Herennium* manuscripts that follows (p. 54); John of Salisbury, *Metalogicon*, a
  non-Ad-Herennian approach "closer to what was later to be Lull's view" (p. 56 n. 16).

## 20. Boncompagno da Signa's memory (Rhetorica Novissima)

- **Name.** The memory section of Boncompagno's "mystical rhetoric"; the memory of Paradise
  and Hell; virtues and vices as "memorial notes" (*signacula*).
- **Exponent, date, source.** Boncompagno da Signa, Bolognese school of *dictamen*;
  *Rhetorica Novissima*, Bologna 1235, ed. Gaudenzi, *Bibliotheca Iuridica Medii Aevi* II,
  pp. 255, 275–79.
- **Principle.** Memory belongs to all arts, not rhetoric only. Natural and artificial memory
  defined after *Ad Herennium*, but no place-and-image rules follow: instead the corruption
  of memory by the Fall, humours (sanguine and melancholic best), stellar influence, a long
  list of "memory signs" in Scripture (the cock-crow reminding Peter), then the memory of
  Paradise (the divine throne, angelic orders) and of Hell (Etna seen from the sea), with the
  injunction to remember assiduously the joys of Paradise and torments of Hell, and a list of
  virtues and vices as "memorial notes ... through which we may frequently direct ourselves in
  the paths of remembrance".
- **Where.** Ch. III, pp. 57–60; recalled pp. 77, 93–94, 96.
- **Quotations.**
  - "WE MUST ASSIDUOUSLY REMEMBER THE INVISIBLE JOYS OF PARADISE AND THE ETERNAL TORMENTS OF
    HELL." (p. 59, Yates's capitals)
  - (Boncompagno) "memorial notes which we may call directions or signacula, through which we
    may frequently direct ourselves in the paths of remembrance" (p. 59)
  - "the matter which Boncompagno treats under memory seems to have little connection with
    the artificial memory as expounded in Ad Herennium." (p. 58)
  - "Boncompagno also foreshadows other characteristics of the later memory tradition"
    (p. 60)
- **Figures/plates.** None here; the diagrams of Hell and Paradise as memory places come in
  Ch. V (Pl. 7, Rossellius; refs pp. 108–11, 115–16, 122).
- **Related.** Yates's proposed background for Albertus and Thomas (21, 22: "Albertus studied
  at the Dominican house in Bologna", p. 60); Hell and Paradise as places (29); Dante (30,
  "metaphor was invented in the Earthly Paradise", p. 96); Giotto's virtues and vices framing
  the Last Judgment (28); Giamboni's detachable memory section perhaps placed at the end of
  the rhetoric under his influence (25, p. 89).

## 21. Albertus Magnus's ars memorandi (De bono and the De memoria commentary)

- **Name.** "The ars memorandi which Tullius gives in the Second Rhetoric" examined and
  confirmed under memory as a part of Prudence; Albertus's "ars reminiscendi".
- **Exponent, date, source.** Albertus Magnus, Dominican (entered 1223); *De bono*, tract.
  IV (Prudence), q. 2, two articuli with twenty-one points, ed. Cologne XXVIII (1951),
  pp. 245–52; commentary on *De memoria et reminiscentia*, ed. Borgnet IX, pp. 97 ff.
  (esp. pp. 108, 117).
- **Principle.** Artificial memory belongs to reminiscence, in the rational soul, and is thus
  a part of Prudence; Albertus is "the first to make this conflation" of Aristotle with
  Tullius. Places: real buildings rather than imaginary "camp or city" (so, Yates infers, a
  church; "solemn and rare" places move most); a field is memorable by nature, a building by
  hand; the five place rules confirmed. Images: the memory image contains its *intentio*
  (an image of Justice contains the intention to seek it); metaphorica are to be preferred
  to propria because "the wonderful moves the memory more"; the imagines agentes quoted in
  full. In the commentary the rules for dark and solitary places become a mystical retirement
  in which a ram with huge horns and testicles charges out of the darkness, and reminiscence
  belongs to the hot, "inspired" melancholy of the *Problemata*.
- **Where.** Ch. III, pp. 61–70; recalled pp. 90, 97–99, 104.
- **Quotations.**
  - "So far as I know, Albertus was the first to make this conflation." (p. 63)
  - "the metaphorica 'move the soul more and therefore better help the memory'." (p. 65)
  - "they stroll mysteriously, like players, out of antiquity into the scholastic treatise on
    memory as a part of Prudence." (p. 66)
  - (Albertus) "we should imagine some ram, with huge horns and testicles, coming towards us
    in the darkness." (p. 68) — "This ram gives one rather a fright!" (p. 68)
- **Figures/plates.** None.
- **Related.** Thomas continues the conflation "more explicitly" but avoids the
  "superstitious awe" (22); the licence for pagan fables as memory images is used by the
  English friars (31) and, Yates guesses, Petrarch (34); the artificial memory pertains "to
  the moral man and to the speaker" (p. 67), the warrant for lay use (26).

## 22. Thomas Aquinas's four precepts for memory (Summa II, II, 49 and the commentary)

- **Name.** "Thomas Aquinas's four precepts for memory"; the "Thomist memory rules" /
  "Thomist artificial memory"; "corporeal similitudes" of "simple and spiritual intentions".
- **Exponent, date, source.** Thomas Aquinas, Dominican; *Summa Theologiae* II, II,
  q. 48 (parts of Prudence: eight, memoria first) and q. 49, art. 1 (Utrum memoria sit pars
  Prudentiae; reply to objection 2 gives the four precepts); commentary *In De memoria et
  reminiscentia*, ed. Spiazzi pp. 85 ff. (esp. pp. 93, 107).
- **Principle.** (1) Assume "convenient similitudes", not too familiar, because "simple and
  spiritual intentions slip easily from the soul unless ... linked to some corporeal
  similitudes"; (2) place what is to be remembered in a considered order, so that from one
  point one passes to the next (Aristotle on places); (3) dwell with solicitude and cleave
  with affection to the things (a misreading of Ad Herennium's *solitudo* as *sollicitudo*);
  (4) meditate frequently. Images first, then places; order, "really the order of the
  argument", replaces the classical irregular loci.
- **Where.** Ch. III, pp. 70–77; Ch. IV, pp. 82–84 (the later tradition citing him),
  85–87 (San Gimignano, Bartolomeo), 99 (order as the order of the argument).
- **Quotations.**
  - (Aquinas) "if we wish to remember intelligible notions more easily, we should link them
    with some kind of phantasms, as Tullius teaches in his Rhetoric." (p. 71)
  - "The places of the artificial memory are thus given a rational grounding in Aristotelian
    theory of reminiscence based on order and association." (p. 72)
  - "Aquinas quotes this as sollicitudo conservat integras simulacrorum figuras, turning
    'solitude' into 'solicitude'" (p. 76)
  - "particularly in Thomas, one gains the impression that the important thing is order."
    (p. 76)
- **Figures/plates.** Pl. 1, *The Wisdom of Thomas Aquinas*, Andrea da Firenze, Santa Maria
  Novella (pp. 79–80).
- **Related.** Cited by Ragone (1434), Publicius (1482), Romberch (1520), Garzoni, Gesualdo,
  Paepp, Schenkel, Gratarolo/Fulwood, Feinaigle (1813) (pp. 82–83); San Gimignano (24),
  Bartolomeo (25), Rosaio (26), Giotto's regular placing (28), the Vienna MS (33), Romberch's
  places of Hell (29). Yates: Thomas "became something like its patron saint" (p. 82).

## 23. The scholastic transformation: virtues and vices as corporeal similitudes ranged in a solemn building (the inner Gothic cathedral)

- **Name.** "The mediaeval transformation of the classical art of memory"; Yates's
  reconstruction of what a scholastic artificial memory looked like; "the vast inner memory
  cathedrals of the Middle Ages".
- **Exponent, date, source.** Yates's own synthesis from Boncompagno, Albertus and Thomas;
  Panofsky, *Gothic Architecture and Scholasticism* (the cathedral as summa of "homologous
  parts"); the fresco of the Chapter House of Santa Maria Novella (Andrea da Firenze,
  mid-fourteenth century).
- **Principle.** The "things" the Middle Ages chiefly wished to remember were salvation and
  damnation, the articles of the faith, the roads to Heaven by virtues and to Hell by vices.
  The imagines agentes are moralised into beautiful or hideous human figures as corporeal
  similitudes of spiritual intentions, and "memorised as ranged in order in some 'solemn'
  building". If Thomas memorised his Summa so, the abstract Summa would be corporealised
  into a Gothic cathedral of images on ordered places; the fourteen seated female figures of
  the fresco may be his "corporeal similitudes".
- **Where.** Ch. III, pp. 55, 60–61, 76–81; Ch. IV, pp. 82, 101.
- **Quotations.**
  - "The imagines agentes would have been moralised into beautiful or hideous human figures as
    'corporeal similitudes' of spiritual intentions of gaining Heaven or avoiding Hell, and
    memorised as ranged in order in some 'solemn' building." (p. 77)
  - "the abstract Summa might be corporealised in memory into something like a Gothic
    cathedral full of images on its ordered places." (p. 79)
  - "And the fourteen female figures sitting in order in their places, as in a church, do they
    symbolise not only the learning of Thomas but also his method of remembering it?" (p. 80)
  - "Christian didactic art ... may owe more than we know to classical rules which have never
    been thought of in this context" (p. 79)
- **Figures/plates.** Pl. 1 (Santa Maria Novella fresco, pp. 79–80).
- **Related.** Its concrete form is entry 33 (orders of inscribed figures); its art-historical
  reflections are 27, 28, 29; Dante (30) is "the supreme example".

## 24. Giovanni di San Gimignano's four rules and the preachers' summae of similitudes

- **Name.** The abbreviated Thomist rules in a "summa of similitudes for the use of
  preachers"; the earliest known quotation of Thomas's memory rules.
- **Exponent, date, source.** Giovanni di San Gimignano, O.P.; *Summa de exemplis ac
  similitudinibus rerum*, Lib. VI, cap. xlii (written 1298–1314).
- **Principle.** Four things help a man to remember well: dispose the things in order; adhere
  to them with affection; reduce them to unusual similitudes; repeat them with frequent
  meditation. Yates distinguishes the similitude spoken in a sermon from the invisible memory
  image, while noting that the whole book of similitudes "is based on the memory principle"
  and that the memory image "can become the hidden generator of externalised imagery".
- **Where.** Ch. IV, pp. 85–86; p. 95 n. 35 (San Gimignano's similitudes of Prudence as a
  guide to the imagery of the Divine Comedy).
- **Quotations.**
  - (San Gimignano) "There are four things which help a man to remember well. The first is
    that he should dispose those things which he wishes to remember in a certain order."
    (pp. 85–86)
  - "the memory image is invisible, and remains hidden within the memory of its user, where,
    however, it can become the hidden generator of externalised imagery." (p. 86)
  - "Surely it would have been for remembering sermons, the mediaeval transformation of
    oratory, that the mediaeval transformation of the artificial memory would have been
    chiefly used." (p. 85)
- **Figures/plates.** None.
- **Related.** Thomas (22); Dante (30, via the similitudes of Prudence); the English friars'
  sermon-pictures (31).

## 25. Bartolomeo da San Concordio's rules and the Trattato della memoria artificiale (Bono Giamboni)

- **Name.** The eight rules for memory in the *Ammaestramenti degli antichi*; the *Trattato
  della memoria artificiale* (Giamboni's Italian translation of the memory section of
  *Ad Herennium*, detached from the *Fiore di Rettorica*).
- **Exponent, date, source.** Bartolomeo da San Concordio, O.P. (1262–1347), Pisa;
  *Ammaestramenti degli antichi* IX, viii (before 1323; ed. Milan 1808, pp. 85–86, Trattato
  pp. 343–56). Bono Giamboni's *Fiore di Rettorica* (thirteenth century), memory section
  placed at the end; codices Pal. 54 and J.I. 47, Bibl. Naz. Florence; Rossi, *Clavis
  universalis*, pp. 16–17, 271–5.
- **Principle.** An ethical treatise in the vernacular, ordered scholastically, whose rules
  under memoria (a part of Prudence) are quoted from "Tommaso nella seconda della seconda",
  "Tommaso sopra il libro de memoria", "Aristotile in libro memoria" and "Tullio nel terzo
  della nuova Rettorica": order (Aristotle/Thomas), uncommon similitudes because "pure and
  spiritual intentions slip out of memory", images in places like letters on tablets. The
  detached Ad Herennium memory section that travelled with it is "an ancestor of the separate
  Ars memorativa treatise". Laymen were to memorise the virtues and vices of the book by the
  art.
- **Where.** Ch. IV, pp. 86–90; compared with Petrarch pp. 103.
- **Quotations.**
  - "His eight rules for memory are mainly based on Thomas" (p. 87)
  - "The detached memory section of Ad Herennium in Giamboni's translation, circulating by
    itself, is an ancestor of the separate Ars memorativa treatise." (p. 89)
  - "This suggests that the artificial memory was coming out into the world, was being
    recommended to laymen as a devotional exercise." (p. 89)
- **Figures/plates.** None.
- **Related.** Thomas (22); the Rosaio (26, "might almost be an abridgement" of it, p. 90);
  Brunetto Latini's "memore artificiel" in the *Tresor* and the Lyons 1568 *Ethica* with
  vices "put into images" (p. 89 n. 23, p. 91 n. 29); Petrarch's *Rerum memorandarum* has the
  same plan (34).

## 26. The Rosaio della vita and its Ars memorie artificialis (natural and artificial places)

- **Name.** The *Ars memorie artificialis* "provided for memorising the virtues and vices of
  the Rosaio"; the artificial memory as "a lay devotional discipline".
- **Exponent, date, source.** Matteo de' Corsini (probable author), *Rosaio della vita*,
  1373, ed. Polidori, Florence 1845; the anonymous memory rules beginning "Poi che hauiamo
  fornito il libro di leggere resta di potere tenere a mente", printed by Rossi, *Clavis*,
  pp. 272–5; same codices as entry 25.
- **Principle.** Lists of virtues and vices with definitions (Wisdom, Prudence, Credulity,
  Litigation, War, Peace, Pride, Vain Glory) to be held in memory by rules closely based on
  *Ad Herennium* with expansions: "natural places" memorised in the country (trees in fields),
  "artificial places" memorised in buildings (a study, a window, a coffer), on which corporeal
  similitudes of the virtues and vices are set.
- **Where.** Ch. IV, pp. 90–91.
- **Quotations.**
  - (Ars memorie) "Now that we have provided the book to be read it remains to hold it in
    memory." (p. 90)
  - "The writer calls 'natural places' those which are memorised in the country, as trees in
    fields; 'artificial places' are those memorised in buildings, as a study, a window, a
    coffer, and the like." (p. 90)
  - "The artificial memory begins to appear as a lay devotional discipline, fostered and
    recommended by the friars." (p. 91)
- **Figures/plates.** None.
- **Related.** Albertus's "nature/hand" places (21, p. 63) and Ad Herennium's real/fictitious

## 27. Lorenzetti's Good and Bad Government read as memory imagery

- **Name.** No name; Yates's suggestion of "yet another approach" to Lorenzetti's frescoes,

## 28. Giotto's virtues and vices (Arena Chapel) as imagines agentes on variegated loci

- **Name.** Giotto's virtues and vices looked at "with the eyes of memory".
- **Exponent, date, source.** Giotto, Arena (Scrovegni) Chapel, Padua, c. 1306: Charity,
  Inconstancy, Envy, Folly and the rest, on the side walls framing the Last Judgment.
- **Principle.** The variety and animation of the figures and their new illusion of depth
  may owe something to memory: Tullius says each must form his own images, so variety; the
  dramatic, beautiful (Charity) or grotesque (Envy, Folly) figures are imagines agentes; the
  care with which they stand out from backgrounds is the care of the place rules (not too
  dark, not too light); the backgrounds are all made different in obedience to the rule of
  varied loci, while the regular placing on the walls follows the Thomist emphasis on order.
  The virtues and vices framing the Last Judgment answer Boncompagno's injunction to remember
  Paradise and Hell.
- **Where.** Ch. IV, pp. 92–94.
- **Quotations.**
  - "the illusion of depth depends on the intense care with which the images have been placed
    on their backgrounds, or, speaking mnemonically, on their loci." (p. 93)
  - "It is true that Giotto's images are regularly placed on the walls, not irregularly as
    the classical directions advise. But the Thomist emphasis on regular order in memory had
    modified that rule." (p. 93)
  - "all these brilliant and original features could have been stimulated by the influences
    of scholastic artificial memory and its powerful recommendation as a part of Prudence."
    (p. 94)
- **Figures/plates.** Pl. 3a Charity, Pl. 3b Envy (Giotto, Arena Chapel).
- **Related.** Ad Herennium place and image rules (4, 5); Thomas on order (22); Boncompagno
  (20); Hell and Paradise as places (29).

## 29. Hell and Paradise as memory places (Romberch's places of Hell; Nardo di Cione)

- **Name.** "Remembering Paradise, Purgatory, and Hell" as belonging to artificial memory;
  "the places of Hell ... regarded as variegated memory loci".
- **Exponent, date, source.** Johannes Romberch, O.P., *Congestorium artificiose memorie*
  (1520; ed. Venice 1533, p. 18); Ludovico Dolce's Italian version, *Dialogo ... del modo di
  accrescere et conservar la memoria* (1562; ed. 1586, f. 15v); the fourteenth-century Hell
  fresco by Nardo di Cione, Santa Maria Novella; rooted in Boncompagno (20).
- **Principle.** Hell is divided into many places, remembered with inscriptions on them,
  each punishment fitting its sin (the Proud crucified, the Greedy, Avaricious, Angry,
  Slothful, Envious, Luxurious punished with sulphur, fire, pitch); these varied places are
  memory loci and the damned are the striking images on them. Nardo di Cione's Hell, divided
  into inscribed compartments, reflected in memory "as a prudent reminder", would be
  artificial memory in the medieval sense.
- **Where.** Ch. IV, pp. 94–95; anticipated pp. 59–60; further diagrams promised for Ch. V
  (pp. 108–11, 115–16, 122).
- **Quotations.**
  - "Hell, he says, is divided into many places which we remember with inscriptions on them."
    (p. 94)
  - "This introduces the novel idea that the places of Hell, varied in accordance with the
    nature of the sins punished in them, could be regarded as variegated memory loci." (p. 94)
  - "If we were to reflect this picture in memory, as a prudent reminder, should we be
    practising what the Middle Ages would call artificial memory? I believe so." (p. 95)
- **Figures/plates.** Pl. 8a, *The Places of Hell*, Nardo di Cione (detail); later Pl. 7a–b,
  Hell and Paradise as artificial memory from Rossellius (Ch. V).
- **Related.** Boncompagno (20); Dante (30) is Dolce's addition to this very passage; Giotto's
  Last Judgment (28).

## 30. Dante's Divine Comedy as a memory system

- **Name.** "Dante's Inferno ... regarded as a kind of memory system"; the poem as "a summa
  of similitudes and exempla, ranged in order and set out upon the universe".
- **Exponent, date, source.** Dante Alighieri, *Divina Commedia*; the reading suggested by
  Dolce's gloss on Romberch ("the ingenious invention of Virgil AND DANTE will help us much",
  1562) and by Yates herself, with San Gimignano's similitudes of Prudence as the key (p. 95
  n. 35).
- **Principle.** The poem rests on orders of places in Hell, Purgatory and Paradise, a
  cosmic order in which the spheres of Hell are the spheres of Heaven in reverse, with
  striking images (the damned, the penitent, the blessed) on those places. Prudence being a
  leading symbolic theme, the three parts are memoria (vices and their punishments in Hell),
  intelligentia (the present used for penitence and virtue), providentia (looking forward to
  Heaven). Memory is "the converting power, the bridge between the abstraction and the image";
  the images of Tullius become poetic metaphors for spiritual things in a "mystical art
  attached to a mystical rhetoric".
- **Where.** Ch. IV, pp. 95–96; Preface p. xii ("the divisions of Dante's Hell as memory
  places").
- **Quotations.**
  - "That Dante's Inferno could be regarded as a kind of memory system for memorising Hell and
    its punishments with striking images on orders of places, will come as a great shock, and
    I must leave it as a shock." (p. 95)
  - "a summa of similitudes and exempla, ranged in order and set out upon the universe."
    (p. 95)
  - "The Divine Comedy would thus become the supreme example of the conversion of an abstract
    summa into a summa of similitudes and examples, with Memory as the converting power"
    (p. 96)
- **Figures/plates.** None (Pl. 8a, Nardo di Cione's Hell, is the neighbouring image).
- **Related.** Hell and Paradise as places (29); Prudence's three parts (10); San Gimignano
  (24); Boncompagno's metaphor invented in the Earthly Paradise (20); Thomas's second reason
  for corporeal similitudes, the Scriptures' use of them (22, p. 78).

## 31. The English friars' invisible 'pictures': Ridevall and Holcot (inscribed memory images)

- **Name.** The "pictures" (descriptions of images not meant to be represented) of John
  Ridevall and Robert Holcot; Yates: "invisible memory images"; medieval "memory for words"
  as inscriptions on the image.
- **Exponent, date, source.** John Ridevall, O.F.M., *Fulgentius metaforalis* (ed.
  Liebeschütz, 1926), early fourteenth century; Robert Holcot, O.P., *Moralitates*; Beryl
  Smalley, *English Friars and Antiquity* (1960), pp. 114–15, 121–3, 165, 172–80; Saxl,
  *JWCI* V (1942).
- **Principle.** Ridevall's "picture of Idolatry according to the poets": a prostitute,
  blind, ears mutilated, proclaimed by trumpet, face deformed, diseased, each feature keyed
  to a point of a sermon on idolatry and fixed by a mnemonic verse ("Mulier notata, oculis
  orbata ..."). His pagan gods are likewise moralised memory images (Saturn = Prudence, Juno =
  memoria, Neptune = intelligentia, Pluto = providentia). Holcot's "sham antique" pictures
  (Penance, a naked man with a five-thonged scourge bearing five inscribed sentences;
  Friendship, a youth in green with inscriptions on and around him) memorise sentences as
  written on the image.
- **Where.** Ch. IV, pp. 96–99.
- **Quotations.**
  - "These invisible 'pictures' provide us with specimens of invisible memory images, held
    within the memory, not intended to be externalised, and being used for quite practical
    mnemonic purposes." (p. 96)
  - (Holcot) "Penance used to be painted in the form of a man, his whole body naked, who held
    a five-thonged scourge in his hand. Five verses or sentences were written on it." (p. 98)
  - "the memorising of words or sentences as placed or written on the memory images was
    perhaps what the Middle Ages understood by 'memory for words'." (p. 98)
  - "the artificial memory may be a hitherto unsuspected medium through which pagan imagery
    survived in the Middle Ages." (p. 99)
- **Figures/plates.** Pl. 4c, *Penance*, fifteenth-century German MS, Biblioteca
  Casanatense 1404 (a later illustration of Holcot's image, "we ought not to be seeing this
  image represented").
- **Related.** Albertus's licence for the fables of the poets (21); memory for words (7);
  the Vienna MS inscribed virtues (33); Holcot's images on the page (32).

## 32. Holcot's memory images placed on the pages of a text

- **Name.** "Another very curious use of memory images"; images placed "in imagination, on
  the pages of a Scriptural text".
- **Exponent, date, source.** Robert Holcot, commentary on Hosea (Smalley, pp. 172–4).
- **Principle.** To remember how he will comment on a passage, the friar imagines a figure
  on the page of the prophet at that place: Ridevall's Idolatry on Hosea's mention of that
  sin, and even Cupid with bow and arrows, moralised, as the memory image for his expansion
  of the text. The page serves as the place system, the moralised pagan image as the image.
- **Where.** Ch. IV, p. 99.
- **Quotations.**
  - "He places such images, in imagination, on the pages of a Scriptural text, to remind him
    of how he will comment on the text." (p. 99)
  - "He even places on the text of the prophet an image of Cupid, complete with bow and
    arrows!" (p. 99)
- **Figures/plates.** None.
- **Related.** Quintilian's and Martianus's page-visualising (11, 18), here combined with

## 33. Orders of inscribed figures: the Vienna manuscript virtues and "the basic Thomist method"

- **Name.** Yates's reconstruction of "the basic Thomist method": "orders of images with
  inscriptions on them memorised in the order of the carefully articulated argument"; "the
  vast inner memory cathedrals of the Middle Ages".
- **Exponent, date, source.** Early fourteenth-century Italian illustrated MS, Vienna
  National Library 2639, ff. 33 r–v (theological and cardinal virtues seated in a row over
  crouching vices; the seven liberal arts with representatives); Schlosser, *Jahrbuch* XVII
  (1896); related to the Chantilly mnemonic poem on the virtues and sciences (Dorez, 1894)
  and to the Santa Maria Novella fresco.
- **Principle.** Since Thomist order is the order of the argument, figures need not be on
  classically differentiated places but may stand on a regular order of places. Prudence
  holds a circle (time) inscribed with her eight Thomist parts; Temperance's parts are written
  on a tree beside her; Fortitude's on her castle; Justice's definitions in her book. Enormous
  crowns and rich dress make them imagines agentes; the inscriptions memorise the Summa's
  sections, as Holcot memorised sentences on the scourge. Yates likens the method to
  Metrodorus writing on the order of the zodiac images, and allows that differentiated
  building-places may have been used in combination.
- **Where.** Ch. IV, pp. 99–101 (cf. pp. 79–80, and refs to pp. 120–1 in Ch. V).
- **Quotations.**
  - "Provided the material has been placed in order, it is to be memorised in this order
    through orders of similitudes." (p. 99)
  - "Prudence holds a circle, symbol of time, within which are written the eight parts of
    this virtue as defined by Thomas Aquinas." (p. 100)
  - "the basic Thomist method may have been orders of images with inscriptions on them
    memorised in the order of the carefully articulated argument." (p. 101)
  - "So might the vast inner memory cathedrals of the Middle Ages have been built." (p. 101)
- **Figures/plates.** Pl. 4a Temperance and Prudence, Pl. 4b Justice and Fortitude (Vienna
  MS 2639); Pl. 1 for the related fresco.
- **Related.** Thomas (22) and the scholastic transformation (23); Metrodorus (12); Holcot
  (31); Romberch's "Grammar as a memory image" with visual alphabets (Pl. 6, Ch. V) is the
  printed continuation.

## 34. Petrarch's Rerum memorandarum libri and Petrarch as memory authority

- **Name.** Petrarch's "Things to be Remembered"; Petrarch as one of the "Professors of
  Memory" in the later treatises; Yates: "an ethical treatise designed for memorisation".
- **Exponent, date, source.** Francesco Petrarca, *Rerum memorandarum libri*, c. 1343–45
  (ed. Billanovich, 1943, pp. 44–45, 60); Romberch, *Congestorium*, ff. 19v–20, 27v–28
  (rules on places attributed to Petrarch); Gesualdo, Garzoni, Agrippa (*De vanitate* cap.
  X), Schenkel, Diderot's *Encyclopédie* (Lucca ed., X, p. 263).
- **Principle.** The plan follows *De inventione*: preludes to virtue (leisure, solitude,
  study, doctrine), then Prudence under memoria, intelligentia, providentia, then the other
  virtues, presumably then the vices; under memoria Petrarch cites the classical art
  ("Memory is of two kinds, one for things, one for words"), Seneca reciting backwards,
  Latro Portius's memory "good both by nature and by art", Themistocles refusing the art.
  Romberch credits him with place rules: no perturbation must disturb the order of the
  places; places of medium size; use many places. Yates supposes pagan images, perhaps the
  virtues in chariots with their exempla as in the *Trionfi*, as his corporeal similitudes.
- **Where.** Ch. IV, pp. 101–104.
- **Quotations.**
  - "Petrarch has warned that no perturbation must disturb the order of the places." (p. 101)
  - "the chief of the 'things' to be remembered is the virtue of Prudence under her three
    parts of memoria, intelligentia, providentia" (p. 102)
  - "Petrarch's book comes straight out of scholasticism with its pious use of artificial
    memory as a part of Prudence." (p. 103)
  - "One wonders whether the virtues rode through Petrarch's memory in chariots, with the
    famous 'examples' of them marching in their train as in the Trionfi." (p. 104)
- **Figures/plates.** None.
- **Related.** Same plan as Bartolomeo's *Ammaestramenti* (25); Cicero's *De oratore* rather
  than *Ad Herennium* as source (9); Albertus's licence for pagan images (21); Romberch
  (Ch. V) transmits the "Petrarchan" place rules to the sixteenth century.
