## The system in detail

### 1. Simonides’ founding scene

#### The narrative in Cicero

**[TEXT/TRADITION/LEGEND]** In *De oratore* 2.351 Antonius thanks Simonides “whom they say first brought forth the art of memory”:

> *gratiamque habeo Simonidi illi Cio, quem primum ferunt artem memoriae protulisse.*

“and I am grateful to that Simonides of Ceos, whom they report to have been the first to bring forth the art of memory.”

The verbs matter: *ferunt* and later *dicunt*, “they report/say,” mark received tradition rather than eyewitness history.

Cicero’s story runs as follows. Simonides dines at Crannon in Thessaly with the wealthy noble Scopas. He performs a commissioned poem in which he devotes substantial praise to Castor and Pollux. Scopas grudgingly offers only half the agreed payment and tells Simonides to seek the remainder from the Dioscuri. Two young men call Simonides outside; he finds nobody. During his absence the hall collapses, killing Scopas and his relatives and crushing their bodies beyond recognition.

Cicero’s central passage is *De oratore* 2.353–354:

> *quos cum humare vellent sui neque possent obtritos internoscere ullo modo, Simonides dicitur ex eo, quod meminisset quo eorum loco quisque cubuisset, demonstrator unius cuiusque sepeliendi fuisse; hac tum re admonitus invenisse fertur ordinem esse maxime, qui memoriae lumen adferret.*

“When their relatives wished to bury them but could not in any way distinguish the crushed bodies, Simonides is said, because he remembered in what place each person had reclined, to have indicated each one for burial; and, prompted by this event, he is reported to have discovered that order above all brings illumination to memory.”

Latin text: *De oratore* 2.353, in the Packard Humanities Institute transcription. The complete passage is available in [PHI Latin Texts](https://latin.packhum.org/cit/Cic/deOrat/2.354).

#### What the scene demonstrates

**[TEXT]** The original operation is not yet a prepared “palace.” It is episodic spatial recollection:

1. The dining room supplies an ordered spatial field.
2. Each diner occupies a distinct position.
3. The ruins preserve approximately the spatial relation of bodies to seats.
4. Simonides recalls the former occupant of each position.
5. Place order permits identification when faces and bodies no longer do.

**[RECONSTRUCTION]** Cicero then transforms this episode into a general technique. The narrative’s transition from incidental memory to deliberate art is literary and theoretical: an accidental seating plan becomes the paradigm for constructing reusable mnemonic places.

**[LEGEND]** No surviving writing by Simonides describes this invention. Nothing establishes that the historical Simonides taught a formal system of *loci* and *imagines*. The earliest full account is Cicero, approximately four centuries later.

### 2. Cicero’s abstract formulation

Immediately after the story Cicero gives the canonical definition:

> *Itaque eis, qui hanc partem ingeni exercerent, locos esse capiendos et ea, quae memoria tenere vellent effingenda animo atque in eis locis conlocanda; sic fore, ut ordinem rerum locorum ordo conservaret, res autem ipsas rerum effigies notaret atque ut locis pro cera, simulacris pro litteris uteremur.*

“Therefore those who wished to exercise this part of their natural capacity must select places, fashion in the mind the things they wished to retain, and set them in those places; thus the order of the places would preserve the order of the things, while likenesses of the things would mark the things themselves, and we should use the places as wax and the images as letters.” (*De oratore* 2.354)

This yields four structural elements.

#### Places: *loci*

**[TEXT]** Places are selected in advance: *locos esse capiendos*.

**[TEXT]** Cicero later specifies that there should be many places:

> *locis est utendum multis, inlustribus, explicatis, modicis intervallis.*

“One must use many places, well lighted, clearly set out, and at moderate intervals.” (*De oratore* 2.358)

The adjectives admit some interpretive variation:

- *inlustribus*: illuminated, conspicuous, or visually distinct;
- *explicatis*: unobstructed, plainly unfolded, clearly recognizable;
- *modicis intervallis*: separated by moderate—not cramped and not excessively large—intervals.

**[TEXT]** Cicero does not require that the places be rooms in a literal building. The familiar modern term “memory palace” is not his term. He requires intelligible spatial “seats” because:

> *sede opus est, etenim corpus intellegi sine loco non potest.*

“A position is needed, for a body cannot be conceived without a place.” (*De oratore* 2.358)

**[MODERN]** “Memory palace” is a useful later label but becomes misleading when it implies that classical practitioners always imagined palatial interiors. Quintilian permits buildings, a road through a city, paintings, and other ordered configurations.

#### Images: *imagines*, *simulacra*, *effigies*, *formae*, *figurae*

**[TEXT]** Cicero’s images must be:

> *agentibus, acribus, insignitis, quae occurrere celeriterque percutere animum possint.*

“active, keen/sharply defined, distinguished—such as can present themselves and strike the mind quickly.” (*De oratore* 2.358)

- *agentes* suggests images that act, move, perform, or exert force.
- *acres* suggests sharpness and mental penetration.
- *insignitae* suggests marked, exceptional, conspicuous images.
- The image must “strike” the mind quickly: *percutere animum*.

**[TEXT]** Cicero does not reproduce the *Ad Herennium*’s longer recipe for beautiful, shameful, bloody, ridiculous, or otherwise exceptional figures. Those fuller prescriptions belong to the adjacent classical system, not strictly to Cicero’s abbreviated presentation.

#### Encoding

**[TEXT]** A thing heard or conceived is reshaped into something quasi-visible:

> *ea maxime animis effingi nostris, quae essent a sensu tradita atque impressa; acerrimum autem ex omnibus nostris sensibus esse sensum videndi.*

“Those things are most strongly fashioned in our minds which are transmitted and impressed by sense; and of all our senses the sense of sight is keenest.” (*De oratore* 2.357)

Cicero continues:

> *ut res caecas et ab aspectus iudicio remotas conformatio quaedam et imago et figura ita notaret, ut ea, quae cogitando complecti vix possemus, intuendo quasi teneremus.*

“So that a certain configuration, image, and shape might mark things blind and removed from visual judgment, enabling us to hold as if by looking what we could scarcely embrace by thought.” (*De oratore* 2.357)

**[TEXT]** The process is consequently not simple mental photography. Abstract, auditory, or discursive material is *configured* into an image.

#### Retrieval

**[TEXT]** The system has two parallel orders:

- the place sequence preserves the sequence of subjects;
- the images signify the subjects occupying those positions.

Cicero compresses it into:

> *sententias imaginibus, ordinem locis comprehendamus.*

“Let us grasp the thoughts by images and their order by places.” (*De oratore* 2.359)

**[RECONSTRUCTION]** Retrieval therefore consists of mentally revisiting the ordered loci, recognizing each installed image, and converting it back into its assigned subject or verbal unit. Cicero does not give a separate step-by-step “walk,” but the ordered-locus mechanism entails it; Quintilian makes the traversal explicit.

### 3. Memory for things and memory for words

#### *Memoria rerum*

**[TEXT]** Cicero considers memory for things—the argument, topic, or sense—proper to the orator:

> *rerum memoria propria est oratoris.*

“Memory for things is the orator’s proper concern.” (*De oratore* 2.359)

A single striking figure can represent a topic, person, proposition, or whole sentence:

> *unius verbi imagine totius sententiae informatio.*

“the shaping of a whole thought by the image of a single word.” (*De oratore* 2.358)

The “single word” functions as a cue for a larger semantic unit, as a painter distinguishes areas through varied forms.

#### *Memoria verborum*

**[TEXT]** Verbatim memory requires more images:

> *Sed verborum memoria, quae minus est nobis necessaria, maiore imaginum varietate distinguitur.*

“But memory for words, which is less necessary for us, is distinguished by a greater variety of images.” (*De oratore* 2.359)

Function words resist likeness:

> *multa enim sunt verba, quae quasi articuli conectunt membra orationis, quae formari similitudine nulla possunt.*

“For there are many words which, like joints, connect the limbs of speech and cannot be represented by any likeness.”

For these the practitioner must create conventional images used consistently:

> *eorum fingendae sunt nobis imagines, quibus semper utamur.*

“We must fashion images for them which we shall always use.”

**[TEXT]** This implies two image classes:

- ad hoc semantic images for subject matter;
- a learned personal lexicon of stable images for words without evident pictorial equivalents.

**[YATES]** Yates correctly recognizes here the classical distinction later spelled out more fully in the *Ad Herennium*. Her “masks” is an interpretive rendering of *personae*, not a technical term used by Cicero throughout.

### 4. Natural and artificial memory

**[TEXT]** Cicero rejects the view that technique creates memory ex nihilo:

> *huius boni naturam esse principem.*

“Nature is the chief source of this good.” (*De oratore* 2.356)

Art does not manufacture a wholly absent faculty; it elicits and confirms what nature has supplied:

> *non ut totum aliquid, cuius in ingeniis nostris pars nulla sit, pariat et procreet, verum ut ea, quae sunt orta iam in nobis et procreata, educet atque confirmet.*

“It does not produce and beget something wholly absent from our capacities, but draws out and strengthens what has already arisen and been produced in us.”

At 2.360:

> *hac exercitatione non eruenda memoria est, si est nulla naturalis; sed certe, si latet, evocanda est.*

“Memory is not to be dug up by this exercise if no natural memory exists; but if it lies hidden, it can certainly be called forth.”

**[TEXT]** Cicero thus presents artificial memory as disciplined enhancement through practice (*exercitatio*) and habit (*consuetudo*), not supernatural acquisition.

### 5. The objection from overload

**[TEXT]** Cicero records critics who say that images burden memory and obscure what nature would retain:

> *Neque verum est, quod ab inertibus dicitur, opprimi memoriam imaginum pondere et obscurari etiam id, quod per se natura tenere potuisset.*

“Nor is it true, as the inert say, that memory is overwhelmed by the weight of images and that even what nature could have retained by itself is obscured.” (*De oratore* 2.360)

**[YATES]** Yates calls Cicero a “believer and a defender.” That is fair: Antonius answers an objection and cites practitioners. But the dialogue’s dramatic and rhetorical construction cautions against simply identifying Antonius’s every formulation with Cicero’s personal daily practice.

### 6. Practitioners cited by Cicero

**[TEXT]** Cicero names Charmadas of Athens and Metrodorus of Scepsis:

> *tamquam litteris in cera, sic se aiebat imaginibus in eis locis, quos haberet, quae meminisse vellet, perscribere.*

“Each said that, just as with letters on wax, so with images upon the places he possessed, he wrote down whatever he wished to remember.” (*De oratore* 2.360)

**[TEXT/TRADITION]** Metrodorus was credited with an exceptionally developed spatial system. Pliny later says that Simonides invented and Metrodorus perfected *memoria technica*, enabling exact repetition of something heard. Pliny’s claim is testimony to ancient reputation, not a surviving description of Metrodorus’s method. See [Pliny, *Natural History* 7.88–90](https://www.attalus.org/translate/pliny_hn7b.html).

## The *Dialexeis*/*Dissoi Logoi* mnemonic

### Textual identity

**[MS]** The work is untitled in the manuscript tradition and transmitted after works of Sextus Empiricus. “*Dialexeis*” was the usual early-modern editorial title. Ernst Weber proposed “*Dissoi Logoi*” in 1897 from its opening words, and the title became standard especially through Diels–Kranz’s fifth and sixth editions. David Wolfsdorf documents this editorial history and the surviving manuscript census in “On the Unity of the *Dissoi Logoi*,” pp. 293–324, especially pp. 294–295 and 321–323: [open-access article](https://sites.temple.edu/dwolf/files/2020/06/Dissoi-Logoi-EGE.pdf).

### The four instructions

The conventional ninth section is short and textually incomplete. Its mnemonic teaching has four stages.

#### 1. Esteem memory and attend

**[TEXT]** *Dissoi Logoi* 9.1 calls memory a great and beautiful discovery, useful for life, philosophy, and wisdom. The text does not say that its anonymous author invented memory.

A representative reconstructed text is:

> *Μέγιστον δὲ καὶ κάλλιστον εὕρημα εὕρηται μνάμα, χρήσιμον εἰς πάντα, εἴς τε σοφίαν καὶ βίον.*

“A very great and beautiful discovery has been made: memory, useful for everything, for wisdom and for life.”

Dialect forms and wording vary slightly by editor.

#### 2. Repeat what is heard

**[TEXT]** The pupil should hear and say the same thing repeatedly. Repetition fixes it in memory.

**[RECONSTRUCTION]** Some scholars connect this advice with memorizing the preceding collection of arguments; that is plausible but not expressly stated.

#### 3. Concentrate and comprehend material as a whole

**[TEXT]** The text advises mental application or attention so that thought can grasp together what has been learned.

**[RECENT INTERPRETATION]** Wolfsdorf argues that memory here assists not merely storage but the unification of discrete pieces of knowledge and therefore belongs to the work’s proposed “master skill.” This is a philosophical reconstruction, not an explicit ancient label.

#### 4. Attach the unfamiliar to the familiar

The decisive instruction is summarized as:

> “Whenever you hear something, connect/place it upon what you know.”

The key verb has been interpreted as placing, depositing, or connecting. Unlike Cicero, the text does not describe a series of architectural loci.

### Examples for names

**[TEXT]** To remember *Chrysippos*, associate its sounded components with *chrysos*, “gold,” and *hippos*, “horse”: effectively a “golden horse.”

For *Pyrilampes*, associate *pyr*, “fire,” and *lampein*, “to shine.”

**[YATES]** Yates treats this as “memory for words (or names)” and exclaims that the two later technical kinds were already present by 400 BCE.

**[CORRECTION]** The fragment certainly distinguishes mnemonic handling of names from handling of subject matter. It does **not** employ the Latin technical pair *memoria verborum/memoria rerum*, nor does it state that these were already formal, universally recognized categories. Yates’s sentence is an illuminating backward comparison, but it overstates terminological identity.

### Examples for things

The closing surviving lines read:

> *τὰ δὲ πράγματα οὕτως· περὶ ἀνδρείας ἐπὶ τὸν Ἄρη καὶ τὸν Ἀχιλλῆα· περὶ χαλκείας δὲ ἐπὶ τὸν Ἥφαιστον, περὶ δειλίας ἐπὶ τὸν Ἐπειόν…*

“Things [or subjects] in this way: for courage, place it upon Ares and Achilles; for bronze-working, upon Hephaestus; for cowardice, upon Epeius…”

The manuscript then notes that the remainder was not found. The Greek ending and scribal note are visible in the transcription of Marcianus gr. 262, c. 408r, reproduced by Wolfsdorf.

### Is this the method of loci?

**[DISPUTED/CORRECTION]** Not demonstrably.

- Yates assimilates the instruction “place what you hear upon what you know” to the later art of places and images.
- Richard Sorabji and other scholars caution that *Dissoi Logoi* 9 is an associative imagery system, not an attested ordered-locus system.
- Its examples provide familiar semantic supports—gold, horse, gods, heroes—not a numbered route through physical places.
- No fixed succession of loci, directional traversal, interval rule, or architectural setting appears.

The safest formulation is: **the fragment documents repetition, attention, verbal decomposition, imagery, and association; it may belong to the prehistory or wider family of artificial memory, but it does not independently attest Cicero’s complete place system.**

### Was its author Hippias?

**[TRADITION/RECONSTRUCTION/DISPUTED]** The association rests principally on Plato’s portraits of Hippias as a polymath with extraordinary mnemonic training:

- *Hippias Major* 285d–286a presents his command of genealogies, foundations, and antiquarian lists.
- *Hippias Minor* 368d has Hippias claim he could repeat fifty names after one hearing.

These parallels suggest a Sophistic educational environment and perhaps Hippian influence.

They do not prove authorship. Modern surveys emphasize that Protagorean antilogy, Gorgianic *kairos*, Socratic themes, Pythagorean speculation, and Hippian polymathy have all been proposed. No candidate has achieved consensus. The anonymous work should not be catalogued simply as “by Hippias.” A recent annotated Portuguese discussion explicitly warns that chapter 9’s interest in memory does not establish Hippian authorship: [“Discursos Duplos,” SciELO](https://www.scielo.br/j/trans/a/FhN7fcyJvGx6hTwxBmfvJWg/?lang=pt).

## Cicero’s ethical frame: memory as part of Prudence

### *De inventione* 2.53.160

Cicero defines Prudence:

> *Prudentia est rerum bonarum et malarum neutrarumque scientia. Partes eius: memoria, intellegentia, providentia.*

“Prudence is knowledge of good things, bad things, and things neither good nor bad. Its parts are memory, understanding, and foresight.”

He then defines the three temporal operations:

> *Memoria est, per quam animus repetit illa, quae fuerunt; intellegentia, per quam ea perspicit, quae sunt; providentia, per quam futurum aliquid videtur ante quam factum est.*

“Memory is that through which the mind recalls what has been; understanding, that through which it perceives what is; foresight, that through which something future is seen before it happens.”

Latin text: [Cicero, *De inventione* 2.160](https://ww1.thelatinlibrary.com/cicero/inventione2.shtml).

### What Cicero does and does not say

**[TEXT]** Memory is one of three parts of Prudence, correlated with past, present, and future.

**[TEXT]** This passage does not mention loci, images, artificial memory, or a duty to use a particular mnemonic technology.

**[MEDIEVAL RECEPTION]** Medieval authors treated *De inventione* as Cicero’s “First Rhetoric” and the *Ad Herennium* as his “Second” or “New Rhetoric.” Because the first placed memory under Prudence while the second supplied rules for artificial memory, scholastic readers could connect mnemonic practice with moral virtue.

**[YATES]** Yates describes this conjunction as the route by which artificial memory became ethically and religiously authorized, particularly in Albert the Great and Thomas Aquinas.

**[CORRECTION]** It is too strong to say that Cicero himself made artificial memory a “moral duty.” The moralization is a medieval synthesis of different textual passages. Carruthers’s work confirms the importance of *memoria* to prudential judgment but shifts emphasis from rote retention to composition, meditation, invention, and ethical deliberation.

## Worked examples and material apparatus

### What this system actually supplies

- **Simonides:** the seating positions and the dead diners.
- ***Dissoi Logoi*:** Chrysippus → gold + horse; Pyrilampes → fire + shining; courage → Ares and Achilles; metalworking → Hephaestus; cowardice → Epeius.
- **Cicero:** no extended worked encoding, but the wax-tablet/letters analogy and the permanent images needed for unpicturable connective words.

### What it does not supply

**[TEXTUAL ABSENCE]**

- no complete practice route;
- no recommended number of loci;
- no rule for every fifth or tenth locus;
- no prohibition against similar places;
- no lawsuit image;
- no zodiac;
- no combinatorial wheel;
- no theatre grades;
- no alphabetic diagram;
- no surviving image said to have been drawn by Simonides.

The “lawsuit image”—a sick man in bed, defendant, ram’s testicles, tablets, and cup—is from *Rhetorica ad Herennium* 3.33, not from Simonides, *Dissoi Logoi*, or Cicero’s *De oratore*. Camillo’s grades and Bruno’s wheels belong to separate Renaissance systems. Importing them here would obscure precisely what makes this founding layer fragmentary.

### Diagrams, wheels, tables, and plates

**[TEXTUAL ABSENCE]** None of the three primary passages transmits an authorial diagram.

**[MS]** Manuscripts and early editions may have initials, paragraphing, marginal signs, and scholastic glosses, but these are presentation and reception features—not surviving diagrams of the ancient method.

**[YATES]** Her Plate 1, “Tullius before Rhetoric,” from Andrea da Firenze’s Spanish Chapel frescoes in Santa Maria Novella, illustrates medieval personification and the supposed two “Tullian” rhetorics. It is not a diagram transmitted with Cicero’s ancient text. Yates’s plates therefore document later reception, not the apparatus of the original system.

### Quintilian’s critical variant

*Institutio oratoria* 11.2.11–16 supplies a remarkably early source-critical discussion.

Quintilian says:

> *artem autem memoriae primus ostendisse dicitur Simonides.*

“Simonides is said to have been the first to reveal an art of memory.”

He then reports disagreement:

- the ode’s subject was variously Glaucus of Carystus, Leocrates, Agatharchus, or Scopas;
- the house was at Pharsalus according to one group of authorities, at Crannon according to Apollas/Callimachus and Cicero;
- Scopas’s death was accepted, but other details varied;
- Quintilian himself calls the part concerning the Dioscuri fabulous because Simonides nowhere mentioned so glorious an occurrence.

Latin and English: [Quintilian, *Institutio oratoria* 11.2.11–16](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Quintilian/Institutio_Oratoria/11B%2A.html); [Perseus Latin text](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A2007.01.0068%3Abook%3D11%3Achapter%3D2).

This is decisive evidence that the story was already unstable in antiquity.

### Pliny

Pliny, *Naturalis historia* 7.88–90, calls Simonides inventor and Metrodorus perfecter of *memoria technica*. Pliny’s surrounding catalogue includes Cyrus, Scipio, Cineas, Mithridates, Charmadas, and people who lost isolated portions of memory. [Text and translation](https://www.attalus.org/translate/pliny_hn7b.html).

### Other attestations

**[TRADITION]** Later biographical and encyclopedic sources—Aelian, Ammianus Marcellinus, the *Suda*, and scholia—associate Simonides with extraordinary memory, inventions, or proverbial wisdom. These expand his cultural persona but do not independently document his teaching of the locus method.

### The Parian Marble

**[CORRECTION]** The catalogue’s “c. 477 BCE (Parian Chronicle)” must be qualified.

The Parian Chronicle entry conventionally numbered FGrHist 239 A 54 dates a victory by the aged Simonides, usually to 476/475 or 477/476 BCE depending chronological conversion. It does **not** narrate the banquet and does **not** date the invention of artificial memory. Yates uses the entry to establish that Simonides was still winning poetic contests in old age. The move from “Simonides was active in 477” to “he invented the art in 477” is a later conflation.

### Manuscript and editorial variants

**[MS]**

- Some witnesses contain four chapters, others five, others the material now divided into nine.
- Only the first four are unambiguously structured as opposing arguments.
- Marc. gr. 262 has red headings, but not a complete modern chapter scheme.
- Estienne divided the material into five portions.
- Teichmüller produced eight divisions in 1884.
- Diels divided it into nine in 1903.
- The familiar “chapter 9” is therefore an editorial division.
- Its ending is defective: Marc. gr. 262 reports that the remainder was not found.

**[DISPUTED]** The ninth section may be integral, an appendix, lecture material, or one component of a compilation. Its thematic relationship to sections 1–8 remains debated.

## Yates’s account and scholarship since

### Yates’s construction

Yates begins *The Art of Memory* with the ruined banquet. Its effect is programmatic: the reader first sees death, visual order, burial, and the conversion of catastrophe into technique.

Her main theses in these chapters are:

1. **[YATES]** Simonides realized that orderly arrangement is essential to memory.
2. **[YATES]** The classical art rests on *loci* and *imagines*, analogous to wax and letters.
3. **[YATES]** Sight is the strongest sense, so invisible or abstract things are translated into quasi-visible figures.
4. **[YATES]** *Dissoi Logoi* already contains the two basic kinds, things and words/names.
5. **[YATES]** Cicero’s account is brief because the art was familiar.
6. **[YATES]** Cicero was a believer and defender, probably aided by extraordinarily acute natural visual memory.
7. **[YATES]** *De inventione*’s classification of memory under Prudence supplied the ethical warrant for its medieval Christian reception.
8. **[YATES]** Medieval images of “Tullius” and Rhetoric represent the two works believed to be Ciceronian, the *Rhetorica vetus* and *nova*.

### What later work confirms

**[CONFIRMED]**

- The place/image distinction is accurately drawn from *De oratore*.
- The visualizing of nonvisual material is explicit in Cicero.
- Cicero genuinely defends the art against an overload objection.
- The *vetus/nova* pairing was institutionally important in medieval schools.
- The three temporal parts of Prudence became a powerful Christian ethical framework.
- Artificial memory had a history far beyond mere party tricks and belonged to rhetoric, ethics, preaching, meditation, and knowledge organization.

### What requires correction

#### Yates’s “two kinds” in *Dissoi Logoi*

**[CORRECTED]** The fragment distinguishes names and subjects, but it does not attest a complete locus system or the later Latin terminology. Sorabji’s warning is especially important: associative imagery is not automatically the method of loci.

#### “Cicero’s fantastically acute visual memory”

**[YATES/RECONSTRUCTION]** Cicero was famous for memory and describes its oratorical importance. The claim that he personally possessed a specifically visual faculty of extraordinary sharpness is biographical inference, not securely documented self-report.

#### Memory as rote storage

Mary Carruthers’s *The Book of Memory* and *The Craft of Thought* substantially revise the picture.

**[POST-YATES CORRECTION]** Medieval *memoria* was not merely a warehouse for faithfully replaying stored units. It was a compositional and inventive matrix: material was gathered, divided, recombined, meditated upon, and used in judgment.

Carruthers writes that memory’s conservation depended upon its identification with “creative thinking, learning (invention and recollection), and the ability to make judgments (prudence or wisdom).” See [“Memory and the ethics of reading,” *The Book of Memory*, pp. 195–233](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/book-of-memory/memory-and-the-ethics-of-reading/0B814725AD33C80333A442F20137D96A).

Her *Machina memorialis* explicitly says Yates was wrong to characterize medieval memory as essentially static and imprisoned. [Cambridge sample](https://assets.cambridge.org/97805217/95418/sample/9780521795418ws.pdf).

#### Chronology and causation

**[CORRECTED]** Yates sometimes writes a powerful linear history: classical rhetoric → scholastic moralization → Renaissance occult transformation → scientific reorganization. Later scholarship tends to emphasize coexistence, overlap, local institutional practices, and recurrent reinvention rather than a single evolutionary line.

### Rossi

Paolo Rossi’s Italian *Clavis universalis* first appeared in 1960, six years before Yates. Its expanded English translation, *Logic and the Art of Memory*, emphasizes the intersection of mnemonic images, encyclopedism, combinatorics, universal language, and scientific method. Thus Rossi is not merely “scholarship since Yates”; he is an independent predecessor and continuing corrective. [Publisher’s description and contents](https://www.bloomsbury.com/us/logic-and-the-art-of-memory-9780826488619/).

### Small

Jocelyn Penny Small’s *Wax Tablets of the Mind* studies ancient memory through cognitive practice and literacy rather than as a self-contained secret tradition. Her approach reinforces distinctions among:

- ordinary memory;
- trained oral recall;
- writing as external aid;
- metaphorical mental inscription;
- specialized locus-and-image systems.

This makes Cicero’s wax tablet metaphor culturally embedded, not proof that all ancient remembering followed a single technical scheme. See Rosalind Thomas’s review in *Classical Philology* 95 (2000), pp. 486–490: [DOI landing page](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/pdf/10.1086/449516).

### Hajdu

Helga Hajdu’s *Das mnemotechnische Schrifttum des Mittelalters* (1936) preceded Yates and catalogued medieval mnemonic writing. Yates drew heavily upon it but interpreted medieval developments more strongly through moral and religious transformation. Later German scholarship has questioned whether Albert’s reconciliation of Aristotle with the *Ad Herennium* should be described as a full fusion of psychology and mnemonic practice. [Folger catalogue record](https://catalog.folger.edu/record/5655).

### Bolzoni

Lina Bolzoni’s *The Gallery of Memory* and related studies deepen the analysis of images, preaching, literary composition, galleries, theatres, and material visual culture. Her work generally confirms that memory arts participated in broad image-making practices but resists reducing all organized Renaissance imagery to a concealed mnemonic system.

### Limited relevance of the named specialist literatures

The requested Lull, Bruno, Camillo, and Ramus scholars—Bonner, Fidora, Sturlese, Ciliberto, Clucas, Gatti, Bolzoni, Stagno, and Ong—are indispensable to later catalogue systems. For the present founding layer their relevance is genealogical:

- Lullian combination is not documented in Simonides or Cicero.
- Bruno’s wheels are not elaborations explicitly contained in *De oratore*.
- Camillo’s theatre converts ordered spatial memory into an encyclopedic and performative architecture.
- Ramism relocates pedagogical order toward dialectical division and method.
- Their scholarship warns against treating all later systems as transparent continuations of a single unchanged Simonidean device.

## History and context

### Archaic poetry and memorial authority

**[RECONSTRUCTION]** Simonides stood at a transition between orally performed poetry, written circulation, professional patronage, and Sophistic expertise. The legend binds together:

- paid poetic performance;
- praise and its proper object;
- divine retribution;
- funerary identification;
- the poet’s authority over names and commemoration;
- spatial order converted into technical knowledge.

Simonides’ association with epitaph and commemoration makes him an apt culture hero for memory, even if he did not invent the formal system.

### Sophistic education

**[TEXT/RECONSTRUCTION]** *Dissoi Logoi* belongs to a culture of teachable verbal skill, argument on opposed sides, civic competence, polymathy, names, and classification. Memory supports the speaker’s ability to command accumulated arguments.

Its probable date is inferred from historical allusions and intellectual context. A recent synthesis places composition around 403–395 BCE while acknowledging minority dates around 440 and even a rejected Byzantine hypothesis. [Cambridge, *Herodotus and the Presocratics*, discussion and bibliography](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/8107F88AEFF72D79CBC4AC39226EDBF0/9781009338547AR.pdf/Herodotus_and_the_Presocratics.pdf).

### Aristotle

**[TEXT]** Aristotle’s *De memoria et reminiscentia* distinguishes memory from recollection and describes recollection as a sequenced search through associated movements. He refers to mnemonic starting points or “places” and illustrates associative chains such as milk → white → mist → moist → autumn. [English text](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/memory.html).

**[CORRECTION]** Aristotle does not give Cicero’s full architectural place system. His “loci,” associative starting points, and midpoint technique must not automatically be made identical with the Roman rhetorical art.

### Roman rhetoric

Memory became one of the conventional five offices of the orator:

1. invention;
2. arrangement;
3. style;
4. memory;
5. delivery.

In Cicero, the mnemonic art is valuable because an advocate must retain:

- facts learned from a client;
- his own planned arguments;
- the order and wording of a speech;
- the opponent’s statements;
- what he has already answered;
- what remains to be said;
- precedents from other cases.

The system is therefore a tool of courts, deliberative assemblies, teaching, and public performance.

### Medieval schools

**[MEDIEVAL RECEPTION]** *De inventione* and the *Ad Herennium* entered the trivium and became the “Old” and “New” Rhetorics attributed to Tullius. Their combination enabled mnemonic precepts to be placed within Prudence.

Dominican authors, especially Albert the Great and Thomas Aquinas, gave the art a major ethical and pedagogical reception. Franciscan traditions also cultivated ordered meditation, preaching aids, diagrams, and affective images, although not every such practice derived directly from the technical *Ad Herennium*.

### Courts, universities, and preaching

The system’s institutional environments included:

- civic law courts and assemblies;
- rhetorical schools;
- cathedral and monastic schools;
- universities;
- mendicant studia;
- courts and academies;
- preaching and confession;
- humanist education.

**[CAUTION]** Survival in an institution does not prove continuous practical use of a full locus system. Texts could be copied, glossed, moralized, or taught as rhetorical doctrine without every student constructing a memory building.

## People

### Simonides

**[TRADITION]** Poet, professional praise singer, epigrammatist, memorial authority, and inventor of mnemonic art.

**[LEGEND]** Saved by the Dioscuri and able to identify the mutilated dead.

**[DOCUMENTED LIMIT]** No extant Simonidean poem mentions the event; Quintilian expressly finds that silence suspicious.

### Scopas

**[TRADITION]** Wealthy Thessalian patron who withheld half Simonides’ fee.

**[ANCIENT DISPUTE]** Quintilian says Scopas died in the banquet but reports disagreement about whom the victory ode celebrated.

### Castor and Pollux

**[LEGEND]** Pay Simonides’ outstanding fee by saving his life.

**[ANCIENT CRITICISM]** Quintilian rejects this part as fabulous.

### Hippias of Elis

**[TEXT]** Plato represents him as a prodigious polymath and memorizer.

**[RECONSTRUCTION]** A possible influence on *Dissoi Logoi* 8–9.

**[DISPUTED]** Neither author nor securely established teacher of its author.

### Marcus Antonius

**[TEXT/LITERARY PERSONA]** Cicero’s speaker in *De oratore*. He gives the Simonides story and compact rules.

### Charmadas

**[TEXT/TRADITION]** Academic philosopher cited by Cicero and Pliny for extraordinary recall.

### Metrodorus of Scepsis

**[TEXT/TRADITION]** Practitioner who mentally “wrote” images in places; Pliny calls him perfecter of the technical memory.

### Themistocles

**[TEXT/ANECDOTE]** Cicero recalls the saying that Themistocles would prefer an art of forgetting, because he remembered what he wished to forget and could not forget what he wished to remember. The story dramatizes both exceptional natural memory and the limits of technique.

### Anecdotes requested in the catalogue brief

- **The auctioneer:** belongs to reports of prodigious or professional memory elsewhere in the tradition, not to the three primary texts here.
- **The professor’s party:** no secure instance was found in the Simonides–*Dissoi Logoi*–Cicero source cluster.
- **The student who lost his memory:** Pliny supplies cases of selective memory loss after injury or illness, but not this precise modern formulation.
- **The accused magician:** belongs to later practitioners and suspicion of extraordinary mnemonic performance, not to documented accusations against Simonides or Cicero.

**[FINDING FROM ABSENCE]** These should not be silently attached to system 1 merely because they occur in a larger mnemonic catalogue.

## The magic question

### Was Simonides’ art magical?

**[TEXT]** Cicero explains its efficacy through:

- sensory impression;
- the special sharpness of sight;
- mental configuration;
- spatial order;
- practice;
- habit;
- natural aptitude.

That is a rhetorical-psychological account, not an occult one.

### Is the rescue supernatural?

**[LEGEND]** Yes: the Dioscuri apparently summon Simonides out and avenge Scopas’s meanness.

**[DISTINCTION]** A miraculous foundation legend does not make the resulting mnemonic technique magical. The gods cause the rescue; spatial recollection explains identification.

### *Dissoi Logoi*

**[TEXT]** Its associations with Ares, Achilles, Hephaestus, and Epeius are conventional cultural exemplars. Nothing says the images invoke, compel, or talismanically embody the gods.

### Heresy and Inquisition

**[TEXTUAL ABSENCE]** No evidence associates Simonides’ mnemonic art, *Dissoi Logoi* 9, or Cicero’s instructions with an ancient prosecution for magic or a medieval heresy charge.

### The *Ars notoria*

**[LATER TRADITION]** The *Ars notoria* promised rapid or divinely assisted acquisition of disciplines through prayers, ritual observances, and notae or figures. It was sometimes condemned or treated with suspicion.

**[CORRECTION]** It is not simply Cicero’s place-and-image system under a magical name. Similar desires for comprehensive memory and visual knowledge do not establish direct technical identity.

### Why later systems became suspect

**[RECONSTRUCTION]** Suspicion arose when mnemonic imagery was combined with:

- astral or planetary powers;
- talismanic images;
- angelic names;
- ritual prayer;
- Hermetic ascent;
- universal claims;
- secret alphabets;
- combinatorial cosmology.

Those developments belong chiefly to later medieval and Renaissance systems. Projecting them backward onto Simonides is a Yates-influenced genealogical temptation, not primary evidence.

## Afterlife

### Classical succession

The direct textual sequence is:

Simonides legend → Hellenistic mnemonic manuals, now mostly lost → *Ad Herennium* → Cicero → Quintilian → late-antique rhetorical abbreviations.

The anonymous *Dissoi Logoi* represents a partly independent Greek associative tradition rather than a securely documented intermediate stage.

### Medieval ethical memory

Cicero’s three parts of Prudence were absorbed into Latin moral theology. Memory became necessary for prudent judgment because action requires correctly gathered experience of the past.

**[POST-YATES VIEW]** In Carruthers’s reconstruction, the trained memory functions as an inventory from which thought and composition proceed—not merely as a recording device.

### Dante and monumental art

**[YATES/RECONSTRUCTION]** Yates connects Dante’s ordered moral universe, vivid punishments, and scholastic Prudence to the artificial-memory tradition.

**[DISPUTED]** Dante certainly wrote within a culture saturated by rhetoric, mnemonic division, exempla, and vivid moral imagery. Demonstrating that a particular canto or architectural sequence is a technical memory scheme requires evidence stronger than resemblance.

Similar caution applies to fresco cycles: ordered walls and vivid figures are mnemonic-friendly, but not every cycle is a coded memory palace.

### Emblem books and galleries

Renaissance emblems combine motto, image, and explanatory text in a way congenial to mnemonic association. Bolzoni shows how galleries, theatres, manuscripts, printed images, sermons, and literary composition formed an interconnected visual culture.

**[CAUTION]** Congeniality and functional overlap are better established than descent from one invariant Simonidean program.

### Camillo, Lull, Bruno, and Fludd

- Camillo monumentalizes ordered loci into an encyclopedic theatre.
- Lull combines letters and divine attributes through figures and rules; his art is primarily combinatorial and demonstrative, not originally a classical image-and-place mnemonic.
- Bruno fuses classical images, Lullian combinations, cosmological order, and philosophical or magical aims.
- Fludd publishes elaborate theatres and cosmological diagrams.

Yates’s achievement was to reveal these connections. Later scholarship has differentiated their methods and resisted treating “memory art” as a single homogeneous technology.

### Literature

#### Borges’s Funes

**[MODERN LITERARY ECHO]** Funes possesses exhaustive involuntary memory but lacks abstraction. He is nearly the inverse of Cicero’s trained selectivity: Cicero uses images to recover ordered subjects, while Funes cannot forget particulars sufficiently to think generally.

#### Umberto Eco

Eco’s interest in lists, encyclopedias, semiotics, and the search for perfect language belongs to the afterlife described by Rossi: artificial memory merges with projects for organizing all knowledge.

### Modern memory palaces and athletes

**[MODERN REVIVAL]** Competitive mnemonists use ordered routes and bizarre, active images. The structural resemblance to Cicero and the *Ad Herennium* is genuine.

**[MODERN DIFFERENCE]** Contemporary athletes often use standardized encoding systems for cards and numbers, timed event rules, prebuilt “journeys,” and cognitive-scientific training concepts absent from the ancient sources.

### Computing and combinatorics

**[LONG-RANGE RECONSTRUCTION]** A defensible intellectual lineage runs:

- spatial and imagistic externalization of ordered information;
- Lullian symbolic combination;
- early-modern universal-character and encyclopedic projects;
- Leibnizian combinatorial calculation;
- later formal logic and computation.

**[CORRECTION]** Simonides did not anticipate a computer in a technical sense. The link is historical and conceptual—representation, addressable positions, encoding, retrieval, and rule-governed combination—not direct technological descent.

## Controversies and disputes

### 1. Did Simonides invent the art?

**Status:** **[TRADITION/LEGEND/DISPUTED]**

**Evidence for:**

- Cicero says he was reported to have invented it.
- Quintilian repeats the attribution.
- Pliny names him inventor and Metrodorus perfecter.

**Evidence against certainty:**

- no contemporary testimony;
- no surviving Simonidean account;
- sources are centuries later;
- Cicero says “Simonides or whoever else invented it” at 2.357;
- Quintilian identifies conflicting versions and rejects the divine-rescue element.

**Conclusion:** Simonides is the ancient tradition’s culture hero of mnemonic order; historical invention remains unproved.

### 2. Did the banquet happen at Crannon?

**Status:** **[ANCIENTLY DISPUTED]**

Cicero says Crannon. Quintilian reports Pharsalus from Simonidean and learned authorities and Crannon from another line followed by Cicero.

### 3. Was Scopas the ode’s subject?

**Status:** **[ANCIENTLY DISPUTED]**

Quintilian lists Glaucus of Carystus, Leocrates, Agatharchus, and Scopas.

### 4. Were the two visitors Castor and Pollux?

**Status:** **[LEGEND]**

The tale does not have the young men identify themselves; their identity is inferred from the miraculous outcome. Quintilian calls the episode fabulous.

### 5. Does the Parian Chronicle date the invention to 477 BCE?

**Status:** **[FALSE CONFLATION]**

It dates Simonides’ poetic victory or activity, not the invention. No secure date for the banquet exists.

### 6. Is *Dissoi Logoi* the “earliest treatise on artificial memory”?

**Status:** **[QUALIFIED]**

It is the earliest surviving continuous Greek text giving explicit practical mnemonic instructions. It is not a treatise devoted entirely to artificial memory, and its final mnemonic section is short and defective.

### 7. Is *Dissoi Logoi* 9 part of the original work?

**Status:** **[DISPUTED]**

Modern nine-part division is editorial. Manuscripts differ in extent and organization. Arguments for unity connect memory with mastery of knowledge and discourse; arguments against emphasize formal difference from the opening antilogies and the heterogeneous transmission.

### 8. Did Hippias write it?

**Status:** **[DISPUTED, INSUFFICIENT EVIDENCE]**

The connection first rests on similarities to Plato’s mnemonic and polymathic Hippias. These support contextual affiliation at most. No manuscript names Hippias.

### 9. Is its mnemonic an ordered place system?

**Status:** **[DISPUTED; PROBABLY NOT IN THE STRICT CICERONIAN SENSE]**

The fragment supplies associations but no ordered architectural sequence. Yates’s assimilation is suggestive rather than demonstrated.

### 10. Did Cicero personally practice it?

**Status:** **[PROBABLE BUT UNDOCUMENTED IN DETAIL]**

His dialogue knows the technique intimately and defends it. Nevertheless, Cicero gives no autobiographical record of constructing a particular route and image set.

### 11. Was memory for words unimportant?

**Status:** **[TEXT, CONTEXT-DEPENDENT]**

Cicero says it is “less necessary for us,” meaning less central to the mature advocate than memory for arguments and subjects. He does not say verbatim memory is impossible or universally worthless.

### 12. Did *De inventione* make artificial memory a moral duty?

**Status:** **[LATER SYNTHESIS]**

It placed memory under Prudence. Medieval authors later connected that doctrine with artificial-memory rules found in another work attributed to Cicero.

### 13. Were classical memory images necessarily grotesque?

**Status:** **[OVERGENERALIZATION]**

Cicero asks for active, sharp, conspicuous images. The more graphic recipe comes from the *Ad Herennium*. Modern retellings frequently combine the two without attribution.

### 14. Was the system secret?

**Status:** **[UNSUPPORTED FOR THIS LAYER]**

Cicero calls it familiar and commonly known—*re nota et pervulgata*. Later traditions sometimes cultivated secrecy; Cicero’s account does not.

### 15. Was it magic?

**Status:** **[NO, IN THESE TECHNICAL TEXTS]**

The banquet has a miraculous frame, but the technique’s explanatory mechanism is sensory and habitual. Occultization is a later development.

## What Yates got right, what she overstated, and what was not hers

### Right or substantially confirmed

- Place order and image representation are the foundations of Cicero’s account.
- Vision receives theoretical priority.
- Active and distinctive images are recommended.
- Memory for things and words requires different encoding burdens.
- The *vetus/nova* pairing shaped medieval reception.
- Memory’s association with Prudence became historically consequential.
- Later mnemonic history cannot be understood solely as trivial “memory tricks.”

### Overstated or corrected

- The *Dissoi Logoi* does not clearly contain the complete classical locus system.
- Its “things and names” are not demonstrably the already formalized Latin technical categories.
- The Parian Chronicle cannot date the invention.
- Cicero’s own “fantastically acute visual memory” is conjecture.
- Medieval memory was not merely static storage.
- Similarity between ordered art and mnemonic technique does not by itself prove a technical memory program.
- Later magical systems should not be projected backward onto classical rhetoric.

### Not original to Yates

- Hajdu had already surveyed medieval mnemonic writings.
- Rossi’s *Clavis universalis* preceded *The Art of Memory* and independently investigated memory, combination, and universal language.
- Classical philology had long distinguished Cicero’s account from the fuller *Ad Herennium* rules and recorded Quintilian’s doubts about the legend.

## Open questions

1. What earlier Greek technical literature lies behind Cicero’s summary? The manuals he presupposes are lost.
2. Was Simonides attached to mnemonic invention because of an actual pedagogical innovation, his reputation for memory, his funerary poetry, or the symbolic fitness of the banquet story?
3. How old is the banquet narrative before Cicero?
4. Did Cicero draw it from a Hellenistic rhetorical handbook, biography, paradoxographical tradition, or several sources?
5. What exactly did Metrodorus add to the system?
6. How should *imagines agentes* be rendered—active, acting, effective, or emotionally forceful images?
7. Does *Dissoi Logoi* 9 belong organically to the preceding sections or to a later pedagogical appendix?
8. What is the precise force of its “placing upon what one knows”: spatial placement, semantic attachment, or a general metaphor for association?
9. Are its name examples fundamentally visual, phonetic, etymological, or deliberately mixed?
10. How much of the *Dissoi Logoi*’s language reflects an original Doric author and how much later scribal transmission?
11. Can manuscript glosses reveal actual medieval or early-modern use of these passages rather than textual possession alone?
12. To what extent did medieval students practice architectural memory from Cicero, as opposed to learning the doctrine through the *Ad Herennium*?
13. Which parts of modern competitive mnemonic practice descend continuously from early-modern manuals, and which were independently reinvented?
14. How far can informational analogies with computer memory be pressed before they cease to be historical claims?

# Simonides, the *Dialexeis* and Cicero’s version of the art: Research Dossier

## The system in detail

### 1. Simonides’ founding scene

#### The narrative in Cicero

**[TEXT/TRADITION/LEGEND]** In *De oratore* 2.351 Antonius thanks Simonides “whom they say first brought forth the art of memory”:

> *gratiamque habeo Simonidi illi Cio, quem primum ferunt artem memoriae protulisse.*

“and I am grateful to that Simonides of Ceos, whom they report to have been the first to bring forth the art of memory.”

The verbs matter: *ferunt* and later *dicunt*, “they report/say,” mark received tradition rather than eyewitness history.

Cicero’s story runs as follows. Simonides dines at Crannon in Thessaly with the wealthy noble Scopas. He performs a commissioned poem in which he devotes substantial praise to Castor and Pollux. Scopas grudgingly offers only half the agreed payment and tells Simonides to seek the remainder from the Dioscuri. Two young men call Simonides outside; he finds nobody. During his absence the hall collapses, killing Scopas and his relatives and crushing their bodies beyond recognition.

Cicero’s central passage is *De oratore* 2.353–354:

> *quos cum humare vellent sui neque possent obtritos internoscere ullo modo, Simonides dicitur ex eo, quod meminisset quo eorum loco quisque cubuisset, demonstrator unius cuiusque sepeliendi fuisse; hac tum re admonitus invenisse fertur ordinem esse maxime, qui memoriae lumen adferret.*

“When their relatives wished to bury them but could not in any way distinguish the crushed bodies, Simonides is said, because he remembered in what place each person had reclined, to have indicated each one for burial; and, prompted by this event, he is reported to have discovered that order above all brings illumination to memory.”

Latin text: *De oratore* 2.353, in the Packard Humanities Institute transcription. The complete passage is available in [PHI Latin Texts](https://latin.packhum.org/cit/Cic/deOrat/2.354).

#### What the scene demonstrates

**[TEXT]** The original operation is not yet a prepared “palace.” It is episodic spatial recollection:

1. The dining room supplies an ordered spatial field.
2. Each diner occupies a distinct position.
3. The ruins preserve approximately the spatial relation of bodies to seats.
4. Simonides recalls the former occupant of each position.
5. Place order permits identification when faces and bodies no longer do.

**[RECONSTRUCTION]** Cicero then transforms this episode into a general technique. The narrative’s transition from incidental memory to deliberate art is literary and theoretical: an accidental seating plan becomes the paradigm for constructing reusable mnemonic places.

**[LEGEND]** No surviving writing by Simonides describes this invention. Nothing establishes that the historical Simonides taught a formal system of *loci* and *imagines*. The earliest full account is Cicero, approximately four centuries later.

### 2. Cicero’s abstract formulation

Immediately after the story Cicero gives the canonical definition:

> *Itaque eis, qui hanc partem ingeni exercerent, locos esse capiendos et ea, quae memoria tenere vellent effingenda animo atque in eis locis conlocanda; sic fore, ut ordinem rerum locorum ordo conservaret, res autem ipsas rerum effigies notaret atque ut locis pro cera, simulacris pro litteris uteremur.*

“Therefore those who wished to exercise this part of their natural capacity must select places, fashion in the mind the things they wished to retain, and set them in those places; thus the order of the places would preserve the order of the things, while likenesses of the things would mark the things themselves, and we should use the places as wax and the images as letters.” (*De oratore* 2.354)

This yields four structural elements.

#### Places: *loci*

**[TEXT]** Places are selected in advance: *locos esse capiendos*.

**[TEXT]** Cicero later specifies that there should be many places:

> *locis est utendum multis, inlustribus, explicatis, modicis intervallis.*

“One must use many places, well lighted, clearly set out, and at moderate intervals.” (*De oratore* 2.358)

The adjectives admit some interpretive variation:

- *inlustribus*: illuminated, conspicuous, or visually distinct;
- *explicatis*: unobstructed, plainly unfolded, clearly recognizable;
- *modicis intervallis*: separated by moderate—not cramped and not excessively large—intervals.

**[TEXT]** Cicero does not require that the places be rooms in a literal building. The familiar modern term “memory palace” is not his term. He requires intelligible spatial “seats” because:

> *sede opus est, etenim corpus intellegi sine loco non potest.*

“A position is needed, for a body cannot be conceived without a place.” (*De oratore* 2.358)

**[MODERN]** “Memory palace” is a useful later label but becomes misleading when it implies that classical practitioners always imagined palatial interiors. Quintilian permits buildings, a road through a city, paintings, and other ordered configurations.

#### Images: *imagines*, *simulacra*, *effigies*, *formae*, *figurae*

**[TEXT]** Cicero’s images must be:

> *agentibus, acribus, insignitis, quae occurrere celeriterque percutere animum possint.*

“active, keen/sharply defined, distinguished—such as can present themselves and strike the mind quickly.” (*De oratore* 2.358)

- *agentes* suggests images that act, move, perform, or exert force.
- *acres* suggests sharpness and mental penetration.
- *insignitae* suggests marked, exceptional, conspicuous images.
- The image must “strike” the mind quickly: *percutere animum*.

**[TEXT]** Cicero does not reproduce the *Ad Herennium*’s longer recipe for beautiful, shameful, bloody, ridiculous, or otherwise exceptional figures. Those fuller prescriptions belong to the adjacent classical system, not strictly to Cicero’s abbreviated presentation.

#### Encoding

**[TEXT]** A thing heard or conceived is reshaped into something quasi-visible:

> *ea maxime animis effingi nostris, quae essent a sensu tradita atque impressa; acerrimum autem ex omnibus nostris sensibus esse sensum videndi.*

“Those things are most strongly fashioned in our minds which are transmitted and impressed by sense; and of all our senses the sense of sight is keenest.” (*De oratore* 2.357)

Cicero continues:

> *ut res caecas et ab aspectus iudicio remotas conformatio quaedam et imago et figura ita notaret, ut ea, quae cogitando complecti vix possemus, intuendo quasi teneremus.*

“So that a certain configuration, image, and shape might mark things blind and removed from visual judgment, enabling us to hold as if by looking what we could scarcely embrace by thought.” (*De oratore* 2.357)

**[TEXT]** The process is consequently not simple mental photography. Abstract, auditory, or discursive material is *configured* into an image.

#### Retrieval

**[TEXT]** The system has two parallel orders:

- the place sequence preserves the sequence of subjects;
- the images signify the subjects occupying those positions.

Cicero compresses it into:

> *sententias imaginibus, ordinem locis comprehendamus.*

“Let us grasp the thoughts by images and their order by places.” (*De oratore* 2.359)

**[RECONSTRUCTION]** Retrieval therefore consists of mentally revisiting the ordered loci, recognizing each installed image, and converting it back into its assigned subject or verbal unit. Cicero does not give a separate step-by-step “walk,” but the ordered-locus mechanism entails it; Quintilian makes the traversal explicit.

### 3. Memory for things and memory for words

#### *Memoria rerum*

**[TEXT]** Cicero considers memory for things—the argument, topic, or sense—proper to the orator:

> *rerum memoria propria est oratoris.*

“Memory for things is the orator’s proper concern.” (*De oratore* 2.359)

A single striking figure can represent a topic, person, proposition, or whole sentence:

> *unius verbi imagine totius sententiae informatio.*

“the shaping of a whole thought by the image of a single word.” (*De oratore* 2.358)

The “single word” functions as a cue for a larger semantic unit, as a painter distinguishes areas through varied forms.

#### *Memoria verborum*

**[TEXT]** Verbatim memory requires more images:

> *Sed verborum memoria, quae minus est nobis necessaria, maiore imaginum varietate distinguitur.*

“But memory for words, which is less necessary for us, is distinguished by a greater variety of images.” (*De oratore* 2.359)

Function words resist likeness:

> *multa enim sunt verba, quae quasi articuli conectunt membra orationis, quae formari similitudine nulla possunt.*

“For there are many words which, like joints, connect the limbs of speech and cannot be represented by any likeness.”

For these the practitioner must create conventional images used consistently:

> *eorum fingendae sunt nobis imagines, quibus semper utamur.*

“We must fashion images for them which we shall always use.”

**[TEXT]** This implies two image classes:

- ad hoc semantic images for subject matter;
- a learned personal lexicon of stable images for words without evident pictorial equivalents.

**[YATES]** Yates correctly recognizes here the classical distinction later spelled out more fully in the *Ad Herennium*. Her “masks” is an interpretive rendering of *personae*, not a technical term used by Cicero throughout.

### 4. Natural and artificial memory

**[TEXT]** Cicero rejects the view that technique creates memory ex nihilo:

> *huius boni naturam esse principem.*

“Nature is the chief source of this good.” (*De oratore* 2.356)

Art does not manufacture a wholly absent faculty; it elicits and confirms what nature has supplied:

> *non ut totum aliquid, cuius in ingeniis nostris pars nulla sit, pariat et procreet, verum ut ea, quae sunt orta iam in nobis et procreata, educet atque confirmet.*

“It does not produce and beget something wholly absent from our capacities, but draws out and strengthens what has already arisen and been produced in us.”

At 2.360:

> *hac exercitatione non eruenda memoria est, si est nulla naturalis; sed certe, si latet, evocanda est.*

“Memory is not to be dug up by this exercise if no natural memory exists; but if it lies hidden, it can certainly be called forth.”

**[TEXT]** Cicero thus presents artificial memory as disciplined enhancement through practice (*exercitatio*) and habit (*consuetudo*), not supernatural acquisition.

### 5. The objection from overload

**[TEXT]** Cicero records critics who say that images burden memory and obscure what nature would retain:

> *Neque verum est, quod ab inertibus dicitur, opprimi memoriam imaginum pondere et obscurari etiam id, quod per se natura tenere potuisset.*

“Nor is it true, as the inert say, that memory is overwhelmed by the weight of images and that even what nature could have retained by itself is obscured.” (*De oratore* 2.360)

**[YATES]** Yates calls Cicero a “believer and a defender.” That is fair: Antonius answers an objection and cites practitioners. But the dialogue’s dramatic and rhetorical construction cautions against simply identifying Antonius’s every formulation with Cicero’s personal daily practice.

### 6. Practitioners cited by Cicero

**[TEXT]** Cicero names Charmadas of Athens and Metrodorus of Scepsis:

> *tamquam litteris in cera, sic se aiebat imaginibus in eis locis, quos haberet, quae meminisse vellet, perscribere.*

“Each said that, just as with letters on wax, so with images upon the places he possessed, he wrote down whatever he wished to remember.” (*De oratore* 2.360)

**[TEXT/TRADITION]** Metrodorus was credited with an exceptionally developed spatial system. Pliny later says that Simonides invented and Metrodorus perfected *memoria technica*, enabling exact repetition of something heard. Pliny’s claim is testimony to ancient reputation, not a surviving description of Metrodorus’s method. See [Pliny, *Natural History* 7.88–90](https://www.attalus.org/translate/pliny_hn7b.html).

## The *Dialexeis*/*Dissoi Logoi* mnemonic

### Textual identity

**[MS]** The work is untitled in the manuscript tradition and transmitted after works of Sextus Empiricus. “*Dialexeis*” was the usual early-modern editorial title. Ernst Weber proposed “*Dissoi Logoi*” in 1897 from its opening words, and the title became standard especially through Diels–Kranz’s fifth and sixth editions. David Wolfsdorf documents this editorial history and the surviving manuscript census in “On the Unity of the *Dissoi Logoi*,” pp. 293–324, especially pp. 294–295 and 321–323: [open-access article](https://sites.temple.edu/dwolf/files/2020/06/Dissoi-Logoi-EGE.pdf).

### The four instructions

The conventional ninth section is short and textually incomplete. Its mnemonic teaching has four stages.

#### 1. Esteem memory and attend

**[TEXT]** *Dissoi Logoi* 9.1 calls memory a great and beautiful discovery, useful for life, philosophy, and wisdom. The text does not say that its anonymous author invented memory.

A representative reconstructed text is:

> *Μέγιστον δὲ καὶ κάλλιστον εὕρημα εὕρηται μνάμα, χρήσιμον εἰς πάντα, εἴς τε σοφίαν καὶ βίον.*

“A very great and beautiful discovery has been made: memory, useful for everything, for wisdom and for life.”

Dialect forms and wording vary slightly by editor.

#### 2. Repeat what is heard

**[TEXT]** The pupil should hear and say the same thing repeatedly. Repetition fixes it in memory.

**[RECONSTRUCTION]** Some scholars connect this advice with memorizing the preceding collection of arguments; that is plausible but not expressly stated.

#### 3. Concentrate and comprehend material as a whole

**[TEXT]** The text advises mental application or attention so that thought can grasp together what has been learned.

**[RECENT INTERPRETATION]** Wolfsdorf argues that memory here assists not merely storage but the unification of discrete pieces of knowledge and therefore belongs to the work’s proposed “master skill.” This is a philosophical reconstruction, not an explicit ancient label.

#### 4. Attach the unfamiliar to the familiar

The decisive instruction is summarized as:

> “Whenever you hear something, connect/place it upon what you know.”

The key verb has been interpreted as placing, depositing, or connecting. Unlike Cicero, the text does not describe a series of architectural loci.

### Examples for names

**[TEXT]** To remember *Chrysippos*, associate its sounded components with *chrysos*, “gold,” and *hippos*, “horse”: effectively a “golden horse.”

For *Pyrilampes*, associate *pyr*, “fire,” and *lampein*, “to shine.”

**[YATES]** Yates treats this as “memory for words (or names)” and exclaims that the two later technical kinds were already present by 400 BCE.

**[CORRECTION]** The fragment certainly distinguishes mnemonic handling of names from handling of subject matter. It does **not** employ the Latin technical pair *memoria verborum/memoria rerum*, nor does it state that these were already formal, universally recognized categories. Yates’s sentence is an illuminating backward comparison, but it overstates terminological identity.

### Examples for things

The closing surviving lines read:

> *τὰ δὲ πράγματα οὕτως· περὶ ἀνδρείας ἐπὶ τὸν Ἄρη καὶ τὸν Ἀχιλλῆα· περὶ χαλκείας δὲ ἐπὶ τὸν Ἥφαιστον, περὶ δειλίας ἐπὶ τὸν Ἐπειόν…*

“Things [or subjects] in this way: for courage, place it upon Ares and Achilles; for bronze-working, upon Hephaestus; for cowardice, upon Epeius…”

The manuscript then notes that the remainder was not found. The Greek ending and scribal note are visible in the transcription of Marcianus gr. 262, c. 408r, reproduced by Wolfsdorf.

### Is this the method of loci?

**[DISPUTED/CORRECTION]** Not demonstrably.

- Yates assimilates the instruction “place what you hear upon what you know” to the later art of places and images.
- Richard Sorabji and other scholars caution that *Dissoi Logoi* 9 is an associative imagery system, not an attested ordered-locus system.
- Its examples provide familiar semantic supports—gold, horse, gods, heroes—not a numbered route through physical places.
- No fixed succession of loci, directional traversal, interval rule, or architectural setting appears.

The safest formulation is: **the fragment documents repetition, attention, verbal decomposition, imagery, and association; it may belong to the prehistory or wider family of artificial memory, but it does not independently attest Cicero’s complete place system.**

### Was its author Hippias?

**[TRADITION/RECONSTRUCTION/DISPUTED]** The association rests principally on Plato’s portraits of Hippias as a polymath with extraordinary mnemonic training:

- *Hippias Major* 285d–286a presents his command of genealogies, foundations, and antiquarian lists.
- *Hippias Minor* 368d has Hippias claim he could repeat fifty names after one hearing.

These parallels suggest a Sophistic educational environment and perhaps Hippian influence.

They do not prove authorship. Modern surveys emphasize that Protagorean antilogy, Gorgianic *kairos*, Socratic themes, Pythagorean speculation, and Hippian polymathy have all been proposed. No candidate has achieved consensus. The anonymous work should not be catalogued simply as “by Hippias.” A recent annotated Portuguese discussion explicitly warns that chapter 9’s interest in memory does not establish Hippian authorship: [“Discursos Duplos,” SciELO](https://www.scielo.br/j/trans/a/FhN7fcyJvGx6hTwxBmfvJWg/?lang=pt).

## Cicero’s ethical frame: memory as part of Prudence

### *De inventione* 2.53.160

Cicero defines Prudence:

> *Prudentia est rerum bonarum et malarum neutrarumque scientia. Partes eius: memoria, intellegentia, providentia.*

“Prudence is knowledge of good things, bad things, and things neither good nor bad. Its parts are memory, understanding, and foresight.”

He then defines the three temporal operations:

> *Memoria est, per quam animus repetit illa, quae fuerunt; intellegentia, per quam ea perspicit, quae sunt; providentia, per quam futurum aliquid videtur ante quam factum est.*

“Memory is that through which the mind recalls what has been; understanding, that through which it perceives what is; foresight, that through which something future is seen before it happens.”

Latin text: [Cicero, *De inventione* 2.160](https://ww1.thelatinlibrary.com/cicero/inventione2.shtml).

### What Cicero does and does not say

**[TEXT]** Memory is one of three parts of Prudence, correlated with past, present, and future.

**[TEXT]** This passage does not mention loci, images, artificial memory, or a duty to use a particular mnemonic technology.

**[MEDIEVAL RECEPTION]** Medieval authors treated *De inventione* as Cicero’s “First Rhetoric” and the *Ad Herennium* as his “Second” or “New Rhetoric.” Because the first placed memory under Prudence while the second supplied rules for artificial memory, scholastic readers could connect mnemonic practice with moral virtue.

**[YATES]** Yates describes this conjunction as the route by which artificial memory became ethically and religiously authorized, particularly in Albert the Great and Thomas Aquinas.

**[CORRECTION]** It is too strong to say that Cicero himself made artificial memory a “moral duty.” The moralization is a medieval synthesis of different textual passages. Carruthers’s work confirms the importance of *memoria* to prudential judgment but shifts emphasis from rote retention to composition, meditation, invention, and ethical deliberation.

## Worked examples and material apparatus

### What this system actually supplies

- **Simonides:** the seating positions and the dead diners.
- ***Dissoi Logoi*:** Chrysippus → gold + horse; Pyrilampes → fire + shining; courage → Ares and Achilles; metalworking → Hephaestus; cowardice → Epeius.
- **Cicero:** no extended worked encoding, but the wax-tablet/letters analogy and the permanent images needed for unpicturable connective words.

### What it does not supply

**[TEXTUAL ABSENCE]**

- no complete practice route;
- no recommended number of loci;
- no rule for every fifth or tenth locus;
- no prohibition against similar places;
- no lawsuit image;
- no zodiac;
- no combinatorial wheel;
- no theatre grades;
- no alphabetic diagram;
- no surviving image said to have been drawn by Simonides.

The “lawsuit image”—a sick man in bed, defendant, ram’s testicles, tablets, and cup—is from *Rhetorica ad Herennium* 3.33, not from Simonides, *Dissoi Logoi*, or Cicero’s *De oratore*. Camillo’s grades and Bruno’s wheels belong to separate Renaissance systems. Importing them here would obscure precisely what makes this founding layer fragmentary.

### Diagrams, wheels, tables, and plates

**[TEXTUAL ABSENCE]** None of the three primary passages transmits an authorial diagram.

**[MS]** Manuscripts and early editions may have initials, paragraphing, marginal signs, and scholastic glosses, but these are presentation and reception features—not surviving diagrams of the ancient method.

**[YATES]** Her Plate 1, “Tullius before Rhetoric,” from Andrea da Firenze’s Spanish Chapel frescoes in Santa Maria Novella, illustrates medieval personification and the supposed two “Tullian” rhetorics. It is not a diagram transmitted with Cicero’s ancient text. Yates’s plates therefore document later reception, not the apparatus of the original system.

### Quintilian’s critical variant

*Institutio oratoria* 11.2.11–16 supplies a remarkably early source-critical discussion.

Quintilian says:

> *artem autem memoriae primus ostendisse dicitur Simonides.*

“Simonides is said to have been the first to reveal an art of memory.”

He then reports disagreement:

- the ode’s subject was variously Glaucus of Carystus, Leocrates, Agatharchus, or Scopas;
- the house was at Pharsalus according to one group of authorities, at Crannon according to Apollas/Callimachus and Cicero;
- Scopas’s death was accepted, but other details varied;
- Quintilian himself calls the part concerning the Dioscuri fabulous because Simonides nowhere mentioned so glorious an occurrence.

Latin and English: [Quintilian, *Institutio oratoria* 11.2.11–16](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Quintilian/Institutio_Oratoria/11B%2A.html); [Perseus Latin text](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A2007.01.0068%3Abook%3D11%3Achapter%3D2).

This is decisive evidence that the story was already unstable in antiquity.

### Pliny

Pliny, *Naturalis historia* 7.88–90, calls Simonides inventor and Metrodorus perfecter of *memoria technica*. Pliny’s surrounding catalogue includes Cyrus, Scipio, Cineas, Mithridates, Charmadas, and people who lost isolated portions of memory. [Text and translation](https://www.attalus.org/translate/pliny_hn7b.html).

### Other attestations

**[TRADITION]** Later biographical and encyclopedic sources—Aelian, Ammianus Marcellinus, the *Suda*, and scholia—associate Simonides with extraordinary memory, inventions, or proverbial wisdom. These expand his cultural persona but do not independently document his teaching of the locus method.

### The Parian Marble

**[CORRECTION]** The catalogue’s “c. 477 BCE (Parian Chronicle)” must be qualified.

The Parian Chronicle entry conventionally numbered FGrHist 239 A 54 dates a victory by the aged Simonides, usually to 476/475 or 477/476 BCE depending chronological conversion. It does **not** narrate the banquet and does **not** date the invention of artificial memory. Yates uses the entry to establish that Simonides was still winning poetic contests in old age. The move from “Simonides was active in 477” to “he invented the art in 477” is a later conflation.

## Yates’s account and scholarship since

### Yates’s construction

Yates begins *The Art of Memory* with the ruined banquet. Its effect is programmatic: the reader first sees death, visual order, burial, and the conversion of catastrophe into technique.

Her main theses in these chapters are:

1. **[YATES]** Simonides realized that orderly arrangement is essential to memory.
2. **[YATES]** The classical art rests on *loci* and *imagines*, analogous to wax and letters.
3. **[YATES]** Sight is the strongest sense, so invisible or abstract things are translated into quasi-visible figures.
4. **[YATES]** *Dissoi Logoi* already contains the two basic kinds, things and words/names.
5. **[YATES]** Cicero’s account is brief because the art was familiar.
6. **[YATES]** Cicero was a believer and defender, probably aided by extraordinarily acute natural visual memory.
7. **[YATES]** *De inventione*’s classification of memory under Prudence supplied the ethical warrant for its medieval Christian reception.
8. **[YATES]** Medieval images of “Tullius” and Rhetoric represent the two works believed to be Ciceronian, the *Rhetorica vetus* and *nova*.

### What later work confirms

**[CONFIRMED]**

- The place/image distinction is accurately drawn from *De oratore*.
- The visualizing of nonvisual material is explicit in Cicero.
- Cicero genuinely defends the art against an overload objection.
- The *vetus/nova* pairing was institutionally important in medieval schools.
- The three temporal parts of Prudence became a powerful Christian ethical framework.
- Artificial memory had a history far beyond mere party tricks and belonged to rhetoric, ethics, preaching, meditation, and knowledge organization.

### What requires correction

#### Yates’s “two kinds” in *Dissoi Logoi*

**[CORRECTED]** The fragment distinguishes names and subjects, but it does not attest a complete locus system or the later Latin terminology. Sorabji’s warning is especially important: associative imagery is not automatically the method of loci.

#### “Cicero’s fantastically acute visual memory”

**[YATES/RECONSTRUCTION]** Cicero was famous for memory and describes its oratorical importance. The claim that he personally possessed a specifically visual faculty of extraordinary sharpness is biographical inference, not securely documented self-report.

#### Memory as rote storage

Mary Carruthers’s *The Book of Memory* and *The Craft of Thought* substantially revise the picture.

**[POST-YATES CORRECTION]** Medieval *memoria* was not merely a warehouse for faithfully replaying stored units. It was a compositional and inventive matrix: material was gathered, divided, recombined, meditated upon, and used in judgment.

Carruthers writes that memory’s conservation depended upon its identification with “creative thinking, learning (invention and recollection), and the ability to make judgments (prudence or wisdom).” See [“Memory and the ethics of reading,” *The Book of Memory*, pp. 195–233](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/book-of-memory/memory-and-the-ethics-of-reading/0B814725AD33C80333A442F20137D96A).

Her *Machina memorialis* explicitly says Yates was wrong to characterize medieval memory as essentially static and imprisoned. [Cambridge sample](https://assets.cambridge.org/97805217/95418/sample/9780521795418ws.pdf).

#### Chronology and causation

**[CORRECTED]** Yates sometimes writes a powerful linear history: classical rhetoric → scholastic moralization → Renaissance occult transformation → scientific reorganization. Later scholarship tends to emphasize coexistence, overlap, local institutional practices, and recurrent reinvention rather than a single evolutionary line.

### Rossi

Paolo Rossi’s Italian *Clavis universalis* first appeared in 1960, six years before Yates. Its expanded English translation, *Logic and the Art of Memory*, emphasizes the intersection of mnemonic images, encyclopedism, combinatorics, universal language, and scientific method. Thus Rossi is not merely “scholarship since Yates”; he is an independent predecessor and continuing corrective. [Publisher’s description and contents](https://www.bloomsbury.com/us/logic-and-the-art-of-memory-9780826488619/).

### Small

Jocelyn Penny Small’s *Wax Tablets of the Mind* studies ancient memory through cognitive practice and literacy rather than as a self-contained secret tradition. Her approach reinforces distinctions among:

- ordinary memory;
- trained oral recall;
- writing as external aid;
- metaphorical mental inscription;
- specialized locus-and-image systems.

This makes Cicero’s wax tablet metaphor culturally embedded, not proof that all ancient remembering followed a single technical scheme. See Rosalind Thomas’s review in *Classical Philology* 95 (2000), pp. 486–490: [DOI landing page](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/pdf/10.1086/449516).

### Hajdu

Helga Hajdu’s *Das mnemotechnische Schrifttum des Mittelalters* (1936) preceded Yates and catalogued medieval mnemonic writing. Yates drew heavily upon it but interpreted medieval developments more strongly through moral and religious transformation. Later German scholarship has questioned whether Albert’s reconciliation of Aristotle with the *Ad Herennium* should be described as a full fusion of psychology and mnemonic practice. [Folger catalogue record](https://catalog.folger.edu/record/5655).

### Bolzoni

Lina Bolzoni’s *The Gallery of Memory* and related studies deepen the analysis of images, preaching, literary composition, galleries, theatres, and material visual culture. Her work generally confirms that memory arts participated in broad image-making practices but resists reducing all organized Renaissance imagery to a concealed mnemonic system.

### Limited relevance of the named specialist literatures

The requested Lull, Bruno, Camillo, and Ramus scholars—Bonner, Fidora, Sturlese, Ciliberto, Clucas, Gatti, Bolzoni, Stagno, and Ong—are indispensable to later catalogue systems. For the present founding layer their relevance is genealogical:

- Lullian combination is not documented in Simonides or Cicero.
- Bruno’s wheels are not elaborations explicitly contained in *De oratore*.
- Camillo’s theatre converts ordered spatial memory into an encyclopedic and performative architecture.
- Ramism relocates pedagogical order toward dialectical division and method.
- Their scholarship warns against treating all later systems as transparent continuations of a single unchanged Simonidean device.

## History and context

### Archaic poetry and memorial authority

**[RECONSTRUCTION]** Simonides stood at a transition between orally performed poetry, written circulation, professional patronage, and Sophistic expertise. The legend binds together:

- paid poetic performance;
- praise and its proper object;
- divine retribution;
- funerary identification;
- the poet’s authority over names and commemoration;
- spatial order converted into technical knowledge.

Simonides’ association with epitaph and commemoration makes him an apt culture hero for memory, even if he did not invent the formal system.

### Sophistic education

**[TEXT/RECONSTRUCTION]** *Dissoi Logoi* belongs to a culture of teachable verbal skill, argument on opposed sides, civic competence, polymathy, names, and classification. Memory supports the speaker’s ability to command accumulated arguments.

Its probable date is inferred from historical allusions and intellectual context. A recent synthesis places composition around 403–395 BCE while acknowledging minority dates around 440 and even a rejected Byzantine hypothesis. [Cambridge, *Herodotus and the Presocratics*, discussion and bibliography](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/8107F88AEFF72D79CBC4AC39226EDBF0/9781009338547AR.pdf/Herodotus_and_the_Presocratics.pdf).

### Aristotle

**[TEXT]** Aristotle’s *De memoria et reminiscentia* distinguishes memory from recollection and describes recollection as a sequenced search through associated movements. He refers to mnemonic starting points or “places” and illustrates associative chains such as milk → white → mist → moist → autumn. [English text](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/memory.html).

**[CORRECTION]** Aristotle does not give Cicero’s full architectural place system. His “loci,” associative starting points, and midpoint technique must not automatically be made identical with the Roman rhetorical art.

### Roman rhetoric

Memory became one of the conventional five offices of the orator:

1. invention;
2. arrangement;
3. style;
4. memory;
5. delivery.

In Cicero, the mnemonic art is valuable because an advocate must retain:

- facts learned from a client;
- his own planned arguments;
- the order and wording of a speech;
- the opponent’s statements;
- what he has already answered;
- what remains to be said;
- precedents from other cases.

The system is therefore a tool of courts, deliberative assemblies, teaching, and public performance.

### Medieval schools

**[MEDIEVAL RECEPTION]** *De inventione* and the *Ad Herennium* entered the trivium and became the “Old” and “New” Rhetorics attributed to Tullius. Their combination enabled mnemonic precepts to be placed within Prudence.

Dominican authors, especially Albert the Great and Thomas Aquinas, gave the art a major ethical and pedagogical reception. Franciscan traditions also cultivated ordered meditation, preaching aids, diagrams, and affective images, although not every such practice derived directly from the technical *Ad Herennium*.

### Courts, universities, and preaching

The system’s institutional environments included:

- civic law courts and assemblies;
- rhetorical schools;
- cathedral and monastic schools;
- universities;
- mendicant studia;
- courts and academies;
- preaching and confession;
- humanist education.

**[CAUTION]** Survival in an institution does not prove continuous practical use of a full locus system. Texts could be copied, glossed, moralized, or taught as rhetorical doctrine without every student constructing a memory building.

## People

### Simonides

**[TRADITION]** Poet, professional praise singer, epigrammatist, memorial authority, and inventor of mnemonic art.

**[LEGEND]** Saved by the Dioscuri and able to identify the mutilated dead.

**[DOCUMENTED LIMIT]** No extant Simonidean poem mentions the event; Quintilian expressly finds that silence suspicious.

### Scopas

**[TRADITION]** Wealthy Thessalian patron who withheld half Simonides’ fee.

**[ANCIENT DISPUTE]** Quintilian says Scopas died in the banquet but reports disagreement about whom the victory ode celebrated.

### Castor and Pollux

**[LEGEND]** Pay Simonides’ outstanding fee by saving his life.

**[ANCIENT CRITICISM]** Quintilian rejects this part as fabulous.

### Hippias of Elis

**[TEXT]** Plato represents him as a prodigious polymath and memorizer.

**[RECONSTRUCTION]** A possible influence on *Dissoi Logoi* 8–9.

**[DISPUTED]** Neither author nor securely established teacher of its author.

### Marcus Antonius

**[TEXT/LITERARY PERSONA]** Cicero’s speaker in *De oratore*. He gives the Simonides story and compact rules.

### Charmadas

**[TEXT/TRADITION]** Academic philosopher cited by Cicero and Pliny for extraordinary recall.

### Metrodorus of Scepsis

**[TEXT/TRADITION]** Practitioner who mentally “wrote” images in places; Pliny calls him perfecter of the technical memory.

### Themistocles

**[TEXT/ANECDOTE]** Cicero recalls the saying that Themistocles would prefer an art of forgetting, because he remembered what he wished to forget and could not forget what he wished to remember. The story dramatizes both exceptional natural memory and the limits of technique.

### Anecdotes requested in the catalogue brief

- **The auctioneer:** belongs to reports of prodigious or professional memory elsewhere in the tradition, not to the three primary texts here.
- **The professor’s party:** no secure instance was found in the Simonides–*Dissoi Logoi*–Cicero source cluster.
- **The student who lost his memory:** Pliny supplies cases of selective memory loss after injury or illness, but not this precise modern formulation.
- **The accused magician:** belongs to later practitioners and suspicion of extraordinary mnemonic performance, not to documented accusations against Simonides or Cicero.

**[FINDING FROM ABSENCE]** These should not be silently attached to system 1 merely because they occur in a larger mnemonic catalogue.

## The magic question

### Was Simonides’ art magical?

**[TEXT]** Cicero explains its efficacy through:

- sensory impression;
- the special sharpness of sight;
- mental configuration;
- spatial order;
- practice;
- habit;
- natural aptitude.

That is a rhetorical-psychological account, not an occult one.

### Is the rescue supernatural?

**[LEGEND]** Yes: the Dioscuri apparently summon Simonides out and avenge Scopas’s meanness.

**[DISTINCTION]** A miraculous foundation legend does not make the resulting mnemonic technique magical. The gods cause the rescue; spatial recollection explains identification.

### *Dissoi Logoi*

**[TEXT]** Its associations with Ares, Achilles, Hephaestus, and Epeius are conventional cultural exemplars. Nothing says the images invoke, compel, or talismanically embody the gods.

### Heresy and Inquisition

**[TEXTUAL ABSENCE]** No evidence associates Simonides’ mnemonic art, *Dissoi Logoi* 9, or Cicero’s instructions with an ancient prosecution for magic or a medieval heresy charge.

### The *Ars notoria*

**[LATER TRADITION]** The *Ars notoria* promised rapid or divinely assisted acquisition of disciplines through prayers, ritual observances, and notae or figures. It was sometimes condemned or treated with suspicion.

**[CORRECTION]** It is not simply Cicero’s place-and-image system under a magical name. Similar desires for comprehensive memory and visual knowledge do not establish direct technical identity.

### Why later systems became suspect

**[RECONSTRUCTION]** Suspicion arose when mnemonic imagery was combined with:

- astral or planetary powers;
- talismanic images;
- angelic names;
- ritual prayer;
- Hermetic ascent;
- universal claims;
- secret alphabets;
- combinatorial cosmology.

Those developments belong chiefly to later medieval and Renaissance systems. Projecting them backward onto Simonides is a Yates-influenced genealogical temptation, not primary evidence.

## Afterlife

### Classical succession

The direct textual sequence is:

Simonides legend → Hellenistic mnemonic manuals, now mostly lost → *Ad Herennium* → Cicero → Quintilian → late-antique rhetorical abbreviations.

The anonymous *Dissoi Logoi* represents a partly independent Greek associative tradition rather than a securely documented intermediate stage.

### Medieval ethical memory

Cicero’s three parts of Prudence were absorbed into Latin moral theology. Memory became necessary for prudent judgment because action requires correctly gathered experience of the past.

**[POST-YATES VIEW]** In Carruthers’s reconstruction, the trained memory functions as an inventory from which thought and composition proceed—not merely as a recording device.

### Dante and monumental art

**[YATES/RECONSTRUCTION]** Yates connects Dante’s ordered moral universe, vivid punishments, and scholastic Prudence to the artificial-memory tradition.

**[DISPUTED]** Dante certainly wrote within a culture saturated by rhetoric, mnemonic division, exempla, and vivid moral imagery. Demonstrating that a particular canto or architectural sequence is a technical memory scheme requires evidence stronger than resemblance.

Similar caution applies to fresco cycles: ordered walls and vivid figures are mnemonic-friendly, but not every cycle is a coded memory palace.

### Emblem books and galleries

Renaissance emblems combine motto, image, and explanatory text in a way congenial to mnemonic association. Bolzoni shows how galleries, theatres, manuscripts, printed images, sermons, and literary composition formed an interconnected visual culture.

**[CAUTION]** Congeniality and functional overlap are better established than descent from one invariant Simonidean program.

### Camillo, Lull, Bruno, and Fludd

- Camillo monumentalizes ordered loci into an encyclopedic theatre.
- Lull combines letters and divine attributes through figures and rules; his art is primarily combinatorial and demonstrative, not originally a classical image-and-place mnemonic.
- Bruno fuses classical images, Lullian combinations, cosmological order, and philosophical or magical aims.
- Fludd publishes elaborate theatres and cosmological diagrams.

Yates’s achievement was to reveal these connections. Later scholarship has differentiated their methods and resisted treating “memory art” as a single homogeneous technology.

### Literature

#### Borges’s Funes

**[MODERN LITERARY ECHO]** Funes possesses exhaustive involuntary memory but lacks abstraction. He is nearly the inverse of Cicero’s trained selectivity: Cicero uses images to recover ordered subjects, while Funes cannot forget particulars sufficiently to think generally.

#### Umberto Eco

Eco’s interest in lists, encyclopedias, semiotics, and the search for perfect language belongs to the afterlife described by Rossi: artificial memory merges with projects for organizing all knowledge.

### Modern memory palaces and athletes

**[MODERN REVIVAL]** Competitive mnemonists use ordered routes and bizarre, active images. The structural resemblance to Cicero and the *Ad Herennium* is genuine.

**[MODERN DIFFERENCE]** Contemporary athletes often use standardized encoding systems for cards and numbers, timed event rules, prebuilt “journeys,” and cognitive-scientific training concepts absent from the ancient sources.

### Computing and combinatorics

**[LONG-RANGE RECONSTRUCTION]** A defensible intellectual lineage runs:

- spatial and imagistic externalization of ordered information;
- Lullian symbolic combination;
- early-modern universal-character and encyclopedic projects;
- Leibnizian combinatorial calculation;
- later formal logic and computation.

**[CORRECTION]** Simonides did not anticipate a computer in a technical sense. The link is historical and conceptual—representation, addressable positions, encoding, retrieval, and rule-governed combination—not direct technological descent.

## Controversies and disputes

### 1. Did Simonides invent the art?

**Status:** **[TRADITION/LEGEND/DISPUTED]**

**Evidence for:**

- Cicero says he was reported to have invented it.
- Quintilian repeats the attribution.
- Pliny names him inventor and Metrodorus perfecter.

**Evidence against certainty:**

- no contemporary testimony;
- no surviving Simonidean account;
- sources are centuries later;
- Cicero says “Simonides or whoever else invented it” at 2.357;
- Quintilian identifies conflicting versions and rejects the divine-rescue element.

**Conclusion:** Simonides is the ancient tradition’s culture hero of mnemonic order; historical invention remains unproved.

### 2. Did the banquet happen at Crannon?

**Status:** **[ANCIENTLY DISPUTED]**

Cicero says Crannon. Quintilian reports Pharsalus from Simonidean and learned authorities and Crannon from another line followed by Cicero.

### 3. Was Scopas the ode’s subject?

**Status:** **[ANCIENTLY DISPUTED]**

Quintilian lists Glaucus of Carystus, Leocrates, Agatharchus, and Scopas.

### 4. Were the two visitors Castor and Pollux?

**Status:** **[LEGEND]**

The tale does not have the young men identify themselves; their identity is inferred from the miraculous outcome. Quintilian calls the episode fabulous.

### 5. Does the Parian Chronicle date the invention to 477 BCE?

**Status:** **[FALSE CONFLATION]**

It dates Simonides’ poetic victory or activity, not the invention. No secure date for the banquet exists.

### 6. Is *Dissoi Logoi* the “earliest treatise on artificial memory”?

**Status:** **[QUALIFIED]**

It is the earliest surviving continuous Greek text giving explicit practical mnemonic instructions. It is not a treatise devoted entirely to artificial memory, and its final mnemonic section is short and defective.

### 7. Is *Dissoi Logoi* 9 part of the original work?

**Status:** **[DISPUTED]**

Modern nine-part division is editorial. Manuscripts differ in extent and organization. Arguments for unity connect memory with mastery of knowledge and discourse; arguments against emphasize formal difference from the opening antilogies and the heterogeneous transmission.

### 8. Did Hippias write it?

**Status:** **[DISPUTED, INSUFFICIENT EVIDENCE]**

The connection first rests on similarities to Plato’s mnemonic and polymathic Hippias. These support contextual affiliation at most. No manuscript names Hippias.

### 9. Is its mnemonic an ordered place system?

**Status:** **[DISPUTED; PROBABLY NOT IN THE STRICT CICERONIAN SENSE]**

The fragment supplies associations but no ordered architectural sequence. Yates’s assimilation is suggestive rather than demonstrated.

### 10. Did Cicero personally practice it?

**Status:** **[PROBABLE BUT UNDOCUMENTED IN DETAIL]**

His dialogue knows the technique intimately and defends it. Nevertheless, Cicero gives no autobiographical record of constructing a particular route and image set.

### 11. Was memory for words unimportant?

**Status:** **[TEXT, CONTEXT-DEPENDENT]**

Cicero says it is “less necessary for us,” meaning less central to the mature advocate than memory for arguments and subjects. He does not say verbatim memory is impossible or universally worthless.

### 12. Did *De inventione* make artificial memory a moral duty?

**Status:** **[LATER SYNTHESIS]**

It placed memory under Prudence. Medieval authors later connected that doctrine with artificial-memory rules found in another work attributed to Cicero.

### 13. Were classical memory images necessarily grotesque?

**Status:** **[OVERGENERALIZATION]**

Cicero asks for active, sharp, conspicuous images. The more graphic recipe comes from the *Ad Herennium*. Modern retellings frequently combine the two without attribution.

### 14. Was the system secret?

**Status:** **[UNSUPPORTED FOR THIS LAYER]**

Cicero calls it familiar and commonly known—*re nota et pervulgata*. Later traditions sometimes cultivated secrecy; Cicero’s account does not.

### 15. Was it magic?

**Status:** **[NO, IN THESE TECHNICAL TEXTS]**

The banquet has a miraculous frame, but the technique’s explanatory mechanism is sensory and habitual. Occultization is a later development.

---

## What Yates got right, what she overstated, and what was not hers

### Right or substantially confirmed

- Place order and image representation are the foundations of Cicero’s account.
- Vision receives theoretical priority.
- Active and distinctive images are recommended.
- Memory for things and words requires different encoding burdens.
- The *vetus/nova* pairing shaped medieval reception.
- Memory’s association with Prudence became historically consequential.
- Later mnemonic history cannot be understood solely as trivial “memory tricks.”

### Overstated or corrected

- The *Dissoi Logoi* does not clearly contain the complete classical locus system.
- Its “things and names” are not demonstrably the already formalized Latin technical categories.
- The Parian Chronicle cannot date the invention.
- Cicero’s own “fantastically acute visual memory” is conjecture.
- Medieval memory was not merely static storage.
- Similarity between ordered art and mnemonic technique does not by itself prove a technical memory program.
- Later magical systems should not be projected backward onto classical rhetoric.

### Not original to Yates

- Hajdu had already surveyed medieval mnemonic writings.
- Rossi’s *Clavis universalis* preceded *The Art of Memory* and independently investigated memory, combination, and universal language.
- Classical philology had long distinguished Cicero’s account from the fuller *Ad Herennium* rules and recorded Quintilian’s doubts about the legend.

## Open questions

1. What earlier Greek technical literature lies behind Cicero’s summary? The manuals he presupposes are lost.
2. Was Simonides attached to mnemonic invention because of an actual pedagogical innovation, his reputation for memory, his funerary poetry, or the symbolic fitness of the banquet story?
3. How old is the banquet narrative before Cicero?
4. Did Cicero draw it from a Hellenistic rhetorical handbook, biography, paradoxographical tradition, or several sources?
5. What exactly did Metrodorus add to the system?
6. How should *imagines agentes* be rendered—active, acting, effective, or emotionally forceful images?
7. Does *Dissoi Logoi* 9 belong organically to the preceding sections or to a later pedagogical appendix?
8. What is the precise force of its “placing upon what one knows”: spatial placement, semantic attachment, or a general metaphor for association?
9. Are its name examples fundamentally visual, phonetic, etymological, or deliberately mixed?
10. How much of the *Dissoi Logoi*’s language reflects an original Doric author and how much later scribal transmission?
11. Can manuscript glosses reveal actual medieval or early-modern use of these passages rather than textual possession alone?
12. To what extent did medieval students practice architectural memory from Cicero, as opposed to learning the doctrine through the *Ad Herennium*?
13. Which parts of modern competitive mnemonic practice descend continuously from early-modern manuals, and which were independently reinvented?
14. How far can informational analogies with computer memory be pressed before they cease to be historical claims?
