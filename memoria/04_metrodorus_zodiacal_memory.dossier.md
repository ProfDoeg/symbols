# Metrodorus of Scepsis’s zodiacal memory: Research Dossier

> **Name:** Metrodorus of Scepsis’s zodiacal memory  
> **Exponent:** Metrodorus of Scepsis, Academic philosopher, rhetorician, courtier, judge, diplomat, and anti-Roman writer at the court of Mithridates VI Eupator  
> **Date:** Probably active c. 100–70 BCE; the often-repeated lifespan c. 145–70 BCE is a modern reconstruction  
> **Source text:** No work by Metrodorus on memory survives. The essential testimonia are Cicero, *De oratore* 2.360; Quintilian, *Institutio oratoria* 11.2.22; Pliny, *Naturalis historia* 7.24; supplemented biographically by Cicero, *De oratore* 3.75, Strabo 13.1.55, and Plutarch, *Lucullus* 22  
> **Principle in one sentence:** Metrodorus reportedly organized artificial memory into 360 ordered *loci* associated somehow with the twelve signs traversed by the sun, upon which mnemonic images could be mentally “written”; the familiar scheme of thirty-six decans with ten places each is L. A. Post’s twentieth-century reconstruction, not an ancient description.  
> **Renaissance revivals:** Johannes Romberch, *Congestorium artificiose memorie* (Venice, 1520; revised edition 1533), especially tract II, fols. 19v–20r in the 1533 foliation and the celestial extension around 33v; Cosimo Rosselli, *Thesaurus artificiosae memoriae* (Venice, 1579), around fol. 22v  
> **Yates:** *The Art of Memory* (1966), ch. I, pp. 19–20 and 23–25; ch. II, pp. 39–42; ch. IV, p. 100; ch. V, pp. 116–17 and 122; ch. IX, pp. 206 and 217; ch. XII, p. 267. The concentrated reconstruction is pp. 39–42.

## Evidentiary key

The following labels are used throughout:

- **[DT] Documented text:** explicitly stated in a surviving primary source.
- **[SR] Scholarly reconstruction:** an inference proposed by a modern scholar.
- **[TR] Tradition/reception:** asserted by later writers, without surviving direct evidence from Metrodorus.
- **[YI] Yates’s interpretation:** an argument or synthesis peculiar to, or strongly associated with, Frances Yates.
- **[DIS] Disputed:** attribution, date, interpretation, or identification contested or materially uncertain.
- **[LEG] Legend/anecdote:** transmitted story whose historicity cannot be established.
- **[MI] Modern invention:** a detail first supplied by modern reconstruction or popular exposition and sometimes mistakenly presented as ancient fact.
- **[NEG] Negative finding:** something the surviving evidence does not supply.

## Basic identification

### The man

**[DT]** Metrodorus came from Scepsis in the Troad/Mysia. Cicero met or heard him in Asia while serving as quaestor in 79 BCE and calls him a rhetorician connected with the Academy. Strabo says he moved from philosophy into public affairs, wrote mostly in a rhetorical manner, cultivated an arresting new style, married advantageously at Chalcedon, and became a favored associate of Mithridates VI. He served as a judge whose decisions were not appealable to the king. [Cicero, *De oratore* 3.75](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Cic.%20de%20Orat.%203.75); [Strabo 13.1.55](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Strabo/13A3%2A.html).

**[DT]** Plutarch calls him a man of pleasing speech and broad learning and says his intimacy with Mithridates was such that he was styled the king’s “father.” Sent to ask Tigranes II for assistance against Rome, he privately advised Tigranes against helping. Mithridates learned of that answer; Metrodorus died while being returned to him. Strabo preserves two explanations—execution on Mithridates’ orders or natural illness—while Plutarch’s narrative connects the death to royal anger. [Plutarch, *Lucullus* 22](https://lexundria.com/plut_luc/22/prr); [Strabo 13.1.55](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Aabo%3Atlg%2C0099%2C001%3A13%3A1).

**[SR/DIS]** A death around 70 BCE is probable from the setting in the Third Mithridatic War. A birth around 145 or 140 BCE is inferred from Cicero’s references, academic affiliation, and career length; no ancient source supplies it directly.

**[DIS]** The surviving testimonia attached to “Metrodorus of Scepsis” may not all concern one individual. Ancient literature contains numerous Metrodori. Identification of the memorist, Academic rhetorician, Mithridatic statesman, historian, geographer, and *Misoromaios* or “Rome-hater” is plausible and conventional, but not demonstrable in every fragment.

### The system’s documentary core

Only three propositions about Metrodorus’s memory are securely attested:

1. **[DT]** Cicero says that Metrodorus used images in places, analogous to letters inscribed on wax.
2. **[DT]** Quintilian says that he “found” 360 places in the twelve signs through which the sun travels.
3. **[DT]** Pliny says that Metrodorus brought the art of memory to completion and could reproduce what he heard in the same words.

Everything more exact—the thirty-six decans, ten subordinate places per decan, decan gods or figures, numerical calculation, shorthand symbols, magical activation—is inferential.

# The system in detail

## 1. Classical artificial memory into which Metrodorus fits

The ordinary ancient technique is clearest in the anonymous *Rhetorica ad Herennium* 3.16–24, Cicero’s *De oratore* 2.350–60, and Quintilian’s *Institutio oratoria* 11.2.17–26.

**[DT]** Its two elements are:

- *loci* or *loca*: stable, clearly ordered mnemonic backgrounds;
- *imagines* or *simulacra*: changeable representations assigned to those places.

The standard analogy is tablet and writing: places are wax; images are letters. At recall, one mentally traverses the places in their fixed order and reads off the images.

Quintilian summarizes:

> “*locis pro cera, simulacris pro litteris utamur*”  
> “Let us use places as wax and images as letters.”  
> —*Institutio oratoria* 11.2.21, ed. Michael Winterbottom; trans. Donald Russell

[Latin and Butler translation](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Quintilian/Institutio_Oratoria/11b%2A.html).

### Rules for places

**[DT]** In *Ad Herennium* and Quintilian, places should be:

- numerous enough for the intended material;
- arranged in a definite sequence;
- well lit;
- clear and unobstructed;
- neither too large nor too small;
- moderately spaced;
- visually distinct;
- familiar or thoroughly rehearsed;
- capable of being traversed both forward and backward;
- reusable after old images are removed.

A house is exemplary, but streets, public buildings, journeys, paintings, and entirely imaginary regions can serve.

### Rules for images

**[DT]** Images should be:

- active or doing something;
- sharply defined;
- unusual, striking, beautiful, ugly, comic, obscene, bloody, or otherwise emotionally forceful;
- adapted either to the substance of a thing (*memoria rerum*) or to words and syllables (*memoria verborum*);
- personally memorable rather than mechanically copied from a teacher’s stock list.

The *Ad Herennium* says ordinary things slip from memory, whereas the exceptionally base, dishonorable, unusual, great, unbelievable, or laughable remain.

### The lawsuit image

This example belongs to the *Ad Herennium*, not to Metrodorus. It illustrates the classical mechanism into which Cicero places him.

The case concerns a defendant accused of killing a man by poison for an inheritance, with witnesses and accomplices. The mnemonic image puts the sick or murdered man in bed; the defendant stands beside him holding a cup in one hand, tablets in the other, and a ram’s testicles on one finger. The objects cue poison, inheritance or will, witnesses, and testimony.

**[DT]** The ram’s testicles depend on a Latin sound-association: *testiculi* helps recall *testes*, “witnesses.” The example demonstrates that images are constructed mnemonic signs, not necessarily representations of actual courtroom events.

**[YI/SR]** Yates playfully wonders whether the ram might also suggest Aries and thus a celestial place. No ancient source makes that connection. It is not evidence for Metrodorus.

## 2. Cicero’s direct evidence

Cicero’s dialogue is dramatically set in 91 BCE. The historical Cicero says elsewhere that he encountered Metrodorus during his Asian travels.

At *De oratore* 2.360:

> “*tamquam litteris in cera … imaginibus in eis locis … perscribere*”  
> “as with letters on wax … to write with images upon those places.”  
> —ed. Kazimierz Kumaniecki; trans. E. W. Sutton/H. Rackham, revised gloss

The complete sentence names Charmadas at Athens and Metrodorus in Asia as men of nearly divine memory. Each said that he mentally wrote, by images in places, whatever he wished to remember. [Latin text](https://m.thelatinlibrary.com/cicero/oratore2.shtml); [English text](https://www.attalus.org/cicero/deoratore2E.html).

What this establishes:

- **[DT]** Metrodorus used multiple places.
- **[DT]** He deposited images upon them.
- **[DT]** The process was compared to writing.
- **[DT]** Cicero judged his trained memory extraordinary.
- **[NEG]** Cicero does not mention the zodiac, 360 places, decans, astrology, shorthand, or magic here.
- **[NEG]** He gives no example of a Metrodorian image.
- **[NEG]** He does not describe how Metrodorus subdivided or navigated the celestial places.

The wax-writing metaphor need not imply literal written characters. In the ancient art, “letters” and “writing” regularly describe the relation of images to ordered places.

## 3. Quintilian’s 360 places

Quintilian first describes orthodox places and images and then remarks:

> “*Metrodorus in XII signis, per quae sol meat, trecenos et sexagenos invenerit locos*.”  
> “Metrodorus found 360 places in the twelve signs through which the sun moves.”  
> —*Institutio oratoria* 11.2.22

[Latin text](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A2007.01.0068%3Abook%3D11%3Achapter%3D2); [Loeb/Butler text](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Quintilian/Institutio_Oratoria/11b%2A.html).

Quintilian immediately calls it:

> “*vanitas nimirum fuit atque iactatio*”  
> “surely vanity and boastfulness.”

His criticism is that Metrodorus gloried in a memory owed more to art than nature. The syntax may express amazement as much as technical objection, but the adverse judgment is unmistakable.

What Quintilian establishes:

- **[DT]** The total was 360.
- **[DT]** The framework was “in” the twelve signs.
- **[DT]** The twelve signs were those through which the sun moves: the zodiacal or ecliptic signs.
- **[DT]** Quintilian regarded the performance as ostentatious.
- **[NEG]** He does not say “one place per degree,” although 360 naturally invites that interpretation.
- **[NEG]** He does not mention decans.
- **[NEG]** He does not say that each sign supplied exactly thirty loci.
- **[NEG]** He supplies no diagram, wheel, table, or list.
- **[NEG]** He does not explain whether the signs themselves, their constituent degrees, imagined compartments, or pictures associated with degrees constituted the loci.

### Are the 360 loci simply zodiacal degrees?

**[SR]** The economical reconstruction is twelve signs × thirty degrees = 360 degree-places. Romberch explicitly adopts this explanation in the sixteenth century: the zodiac is customarily measured by astrologers in as many degrees as Metrodorus assigned loci.

**[DIS]** That does not prove Metrodorus himself used individual degree-images. A geometrical degree is a coordinate, not automatically a visually distinctive mnemonic background. Ancient astrology supplied other subdivisions—decans, bounds or terms, dodecatemoria, houses, fixed-star configurations—but no source identifies which, if any, Metrodorus employed.

## 4. Pliny’s verbatim-memory notice

Pliny writes:

> “*ars … Simonide inventa, Metrodoro Scepsio consummata*”  
> “the art, invented by Simonides, was perfected by Metrodorus of Scepsis.”

He adds that Metrodorus could repeat what he had heard *iisdem verbis*, “in the same words.” [Pliny, *Natural History* 7.24](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.02.0137%3Abook%3D7%3Achapter%3D24).

The notice occurs in a catalogue of memory prodigies: Cyrus and Scipio remembering names, Cineas identifying Roman senators, Mithridates speaking the languages of his subjects, and Charmadas reproducing books.

Interpretive limits:

- **[DT]** Pliny associates Metrodorus with exact verbal recall.
- **[DT]** He treats Metrodorus as a historical improver or consummator of an art attributed to Simonides.
- **[TR]** The Simonides-to-Metrodorus succession is Pliny’s compressed history, not an extant lineage of manuals.
- **[NEG]** Pliny does not connect the verbatim feat explicitly to the zodiacal 360 loci.
- **[NEG]** He gives no procedure or demonstration.
- **[DIS]** “Could repeat what he heard in the same words” may be rhetorical exemplum rather than controlled report.

## 5. The Post–Yates reconstruction

L. A. Post proposed the most influential reconstruction in “Ancient Memory Systems,” *Classical Weekly* 25.14 (1932), pp. 105–10, especially p. 109.

Post’s reasoning was:

1. Ancient astrologers divided the zodiac into twelve signs.
2. Each sign contains thirty degrees.
3. The zodiac was also divided into thirty-six decans of ten degrees.
4. Each decan could be represented by a distinctive figure.
5. Metrodorus could therefore group ten loci beneath each decan figure.
6. Thirty-six groups × ten loci = 360.
7. Arithmetic would allow rapid access to a numbered locus and reduce the risk of omission.

**[SR]** This is ingenious and operationally plausible.

**[MI]** It is frequently retold as if Quintilian said that Metrodorus used thirty-six decan figures with ten loci apiece. He did not.

**[DIS]** Difficulties include:

- Post produced no Metrodorian text, diagram, or ancient parallel explicitly joining decans to mnemonic loci.
- Distinctive decan figures are unevenly documented across ancient astrological traditions.
- A decan is itself a ten-degree section. Post’s extra ten loci under each figure require another level of visualization that no source describes.
- Calculation may locate a number, but mnemonic places ordinarily must also be sensorially distinct.
- Nothing proves that Metrodorus used Egyptian-derived decan iconography rather than zodiacal degrees, an astronomical diagram, a verbal enumeration, or some lost arrangement.

Yates quotes Post at length and cautiously frames the conclusion with “if Post is right.” She nevertheless makes it central to her genealogy of celestial memory.

## 6. What serves as places and what serves as images?

The safest reconstruction separates three layers.

### Layer A: directly attested

- **Places:** 360 loci “in” the twelve solar signs.
- **Images:** unspecified images mentally inscribed in those loci.
- **Order:** zodiacal succession supplies at least a twelve-part order.
- **Retrieval:** the ordinary ancient procedure would be to traverse the loci and recover the deposited images.

### Layer B: strong inference

- Thirty loci probably belonged to each sign.
- The 360 total probably corresponds to the 360 degrees of the idealized zodiac.
- The stable celestial framework functioned like the stable rooms of a building.

### Layer C: speculative reconstruction

- Thirty-six decan figures were principal indexing places.
- Each decan held ten subsidiary loci.
- Mnemonic shorthand signs were written on them.
- Numerical calculation enabled direct random access.
- The decan figures were talismanic or magically efficacious.

Only Layer A should be called “Metrodorus’s system” without qualification.

## 7. How order and retrieval may have worked

**[SR] Sequential model:** Begin with Aries and traverse Taurus, Gemini, Cancer, Leo, Virgo, Libra, Scorpio, Sagittarius, Capricorn, Aquarius, Pisces. Within each sign, move through thirty subdivisions. Images deposited in successive loci reproduce a speech in order.

**[SR] Indexed model:** Convert an ordinal number into sign and degree. For item \(n\), the sign is determined by blocks of thirty, and the degree by the remainder. This is the attractive “random access” implication of a 360-place grid, but no ancient testimony says Metrodorus retrieved by arithmetic.

**[SR] Decanal model:** Convert \(n\) into a decan block of ten and a position within that block. The decan figure serves as a landmark. This is Post’s special contribution.

**[NEG]** No source says whether Metrodorus could move backward, jump directly to a requested item, erase images after use, or maintain multiple speeches in the same celestial array.

## 8. Worked examples, diagrams, wheels, and tables

### Metrodorus himself

- **[NEG]** No worked example survives.
- **[NEG]** No Metrodorian diagram survives.
- **[NEG]** No wheel or table can be securely attributed to him.
- **[NEG]** No original-language treatise survives.
- **[NEG]** No manuscript or title of a mnemonic work is known.

A modern diagram showing twelve sectors, thirty degrees per sector, thirty-six decans, or ten boxes beneath each decan illustrates a reconstruction; it is not a facsimile or ancient plate.

### Related examples requested in the general template

The lawsuit image belongs to *Ad Herennium*, Camillo’s seven grades to the sixteenth-century Memory Theatre, and Bruno’s concentric wheels to *De umbris idearum* and related works. None is an example supplied by Metrodorus or an ancient witness to his practice.

They matter as comparanda:

- *Ad Herennium* shows how vivid images encode propositions and words.
- Camillo turns an ordered cosmic-theatrical structure into a classified repository.
- Bruno combines celestial image catalogues with mobile combinatory wheels.

But using those systems to fill gaps in Metrodorus would be circular.

# The sources

## A. Ancient primary testimonia

### Cicero, *De oratore* 2.360

**Date:** composed 55 BCE; dramatic date 91 BCE.  
**Importance:** closest surviving testimony and the only source explicitly joining Metrodorus to the places-and-images method.

**Standard editions:**

- Kazimierz Kumaniecki, ed., *M. Tulli Ciceronis De oratore*, Teubner, Leipzig, 1969.
- E. W. Sutton and H. Rackham, trans., *Cicero: De Oratore*, Loeb Classical Library 348–49, Harvard University Press, 1942.
- James M. May and Jakob Wisse, trans., *Cicero: On the Ideal Orator*, Oxford University Press, 2001.

**Textual point:** Manuscripts and editions vary in spelling and punctuation, but no consequential variant is known that removes Metrodorus, the images, or the loci. Some older English translations render *imaginibus* loosely as “symbols” or “ideas,” which can make the mechanism seem more abstract than the Latin.

### Cicero, *De oratore* 3.75

**[DT]** Cicero says he encountered in Asia “that Metrodorus” whose memory Antonius had mentioned and identifies him as a rhetorician from the Academy. This is important evidence for joining the memorist to the Academic rhetorician.

### Quintilian, *Institutio oratoria* 11.2.17–26, especially 22

**Date:** c. 95 CE.  
**Importance:** sole source for the 360 zodiacal loci.

**Standard editions:**

- Michael Winterbottom, ed., *M. Fabi Quintiliani Institutionis oratoriae libri duodecim*, 2 vols., Oxford Classical Texts, 1970.
- Donald A. Russell, ed. and trans., *Quintilian: The Orator’s Education*, Loeb Classical Library 124–27, 2001.
- H. E. Butler, trans., *Institutio Oratoria*, Loeb Classical Library, 1920–22.

**Textual point:** Older texts vary in numerals—*XII* versus *duodecim*, *trecenos et sexagenos* versus orthographic variants—but the substance, 360 loci in twelve signs, is stable.

**Translation problem:** *Invenerit* may mean “found,” “devised,” or “managed to discover.” “Found” should not be taken to mean that 360 visually marked locations were objectively visible in the sky.

### Pliny, *Naturalis historia* 7.24

**Date:** completed by 77 CE.  
**Importance:** supplies the claim of exact verbal recall and the Simonides–Metrodorus genealogy.

**Standard editions:**

- Karl Mayhoff, ed., *C. Plini Secundi Naturalis historiae libri XXXVII*, Teubner.
- H. Rackham et al., trans., *Pliny: Natural History*, Loeb Classical Library.
- John Bostock and H. T. Riley, trans., *The Natural History of Pliny*, 1855.

**Caution:** Chapter numbering can differ among editions. Search by book 7 and the memory catalogue if “24” does not align.

### Strabo, *Geography* 13.1.55

**Date:** early Roman imperial period.  
**Language:** Greek.  
**Importance:** fullest biographical notice; it says nothing about memory.

A short phrase describing his rhetoric is:

> “καινὴν δέ τινα λέξιν … ἐξεπλήττετο”  
> approximately, “with a novel style of expression he astonished people.”

**Standard edition and translation:**

- Horace Leonard Jones, ed. and trans., *The Geography of Strabo*, Loeb Classical Library, vol. 6.
- Stefan Radt, ed. and trans., *Strabons Geographika*, Vandenhoeck & Ruprecht, 2002–11.

### Plutarch, *Lucullus* 22

**Date:** early second century CE.  
**Language:** Greek.  
**Importance:** court career, embassy to Tigranes, death tradition; no mnemonic information.

**Standard editions:**

- Konrat Ziegler, ed., *Plutarchi Vitae parallelae*, Teubner.
- Bernadotte Perrin, trans., *Plutarch’s Lives*, Loeb Classical Library.

### Other possible testimonia

**[DIS]** Pliny 34.34 mentions a Metrodorus of Scepsis surnamed for hatred of the Roman name and associated with hostile writing about Rome. Ovid, *Ex Ponto* 4.16.15–16, refers to a Scepsian Metrodorus who attacked Roman ways. These are conventionally linked with Mithridates’ courtier, but do not concern memory.

**[DIS]** Fragment collections may classify him as a historian—e.g. *Fragmente der griechischen Historiker* 184—but the boundaries of the corpus remain uncertain.

## B. Medieval transmission

Metrodorus’s own work did not circulate in the Middle Ages insofar as surviving catalogues show.

His name was accessible through:

- Cicero’s *De oratore*, although its medieval circulation was incomplete;
- Pliny’s widely copied *Natural History*;
- Quintilian, whose complete text was recovered by Poggio Bracciolini at St Gall in 1416, though partial medieval manuscripts existed;
- later rhetorical compilations.

**[NEG]** No medieval manuscript contains a Metrodorian zodiacal diagram identified as such.

## C. Johannes Romberch

### Bibliography

Johannes Host von Romberch, *Congestorium artificiose memorie*, Venice: Georgius de Rusconibus, 1520; revised Venice: Melchior Sessa, July 1533.

The work is divided into four treatises:

1. preliminaries and defenses of artificial memory;
2. the nature and manufacture of places;
3. images and their formation;
4. applications to sciences, sermons, arguments, histories, and verse.

The 1533 text’s contents and transcription are available through [Latin Wikisource](https://la.wikisource.org/wiki/Congestorium_Artificiosae_Memoriae). The Library of Congress has a digitized early copy under [Johannes Romberch](https://www.loc.gov/item/2017692231/).

### The Metrodorus passage

In the 1533 foliation, tract II, around fol. 19v, Romberch writes:

> “*Metrodorus … in duodecim signis … trescentos et sexagenos constituit locos*”  
> “Metrodorus established 360 places in the twelve signs.”

He names the signs and explains the total:

> “*totidem gradibus iste Zodiacus circulus apud astrologos metiri soleat*”  
> “because among astrologers the zodiacal circle is customarily measured by that many degrees.”

The Latin is reproduced and collated in Santiago García’s modern critical study of the *Congestorium*. [Critical study and transcription](https://dehesa.unex.es/server/api/core/bitstreams/d42663d2-4ddb-40c0-be50-6720bbee3a8b/content).

### What Romberch changes

- **[TR]** He converts Quintilian’s compressed notice into an explicit one-locus-per-degree explanation.
- **[DT, Romberch]** The twelve named signs provide an easily retained macro-order.
- **[TR]** When a larger celestial system is wanted, Romberch recommends the constellation figures associated with Hyginus.
- **[NEG]** The known Metrodorus passage does not establish that Romberch possessed an independent ancient source.
- **[NEG]** It does not furnish Metrodorian decan figures.
- **[YI]** Yates infers that preachers could use this celestial place order for sermons on virtues, vices, heaven, and hell. Romberch does not specify the stored material in the quoted passage.

### Hyginus and the constellations

The *Astronomica* transmitted under Hyginus describes the classical constellations and circulated with anthropomorphic and zoomorphic figures. Renaissance printed editions, especially Ratdolt’s Venetian editions of 1482 and 1485, made these pictures readily usable as mnemonic landmarks.

**[TR]** These Hyginian figures are Romberch’s extension, not evidence that Metrodorus used the same 48-constellation programme.

A surviving early twelfth-century illustrated Hyginus manuscript is Munich, Bayerische Staatsbibliothek, **Clm 10270**. [Library of Congress digital presentation](https://www.loc.gov/resource/gdcwdl.wdl_14682/?sp=9). The Ratdolt *Poeticon astronomicon* is described by the [University of Oklahoma’s Galileo collection](https://galileo.ou.edu/exhibits/astronomical-poem.html).

### Rosselli’s treatment

**[TR]** Rosselli again invokes Metrodorus for zodiacal places and treats constellations as possible place-orders.

**[DT, Rosselli]** His distinctive pedagogical device is mnemonic verse: verses stabilize sequences including the zodiac, hell, and other cosmological or theological arrangements.

**[DT]** The prefatory or accompanying verses were supplied by a Dominican associated with inquisitorial office. Yates emphasizes this as evidence that an elaborate imagistic memory art could possess an emphatically orthodox Catholic setting.

**[NEG]** Rosselli’s diagrams and plates are diagrams of Rosselli’s synthetic system, not surviving copies of a Metrodorian archetype.

### Edition variants

Only one early printed edition of Rosselli’s *Thesaurus* is generally recorded, though individual copies vary through binding, missing leaves, supplied leaves, folding-plate placement, and cataloguing. The Folger gives 145 leaves “[i.e. 147]”; other catalogues compress the physical description. These are copy and collation issues, not known doctrinal variants in the Metrodorus passage.

# Yates’s account and the scholarship since

## 1. Chapter I: the classical art

At pp. 19–20 and 23–25, Yates uses Quintilian to clarify the rules of classical places and images. She contrasts Cicero’s admiration of Metrodorus’s “almost divine” memory with Quintilian’s accusation of vanity and ostentation.

**[YI]** Yates sees the contrast as an early division between reverence for extraordinary artificial memory and suspicion that it is boastful, irrational, or excessive.

This remains a fair reading of the different evaluative tones. It does not prove a contemporary controversy specifically about astrology.

## 2. Chapter II: the reconstruction

At pp. 39–42 Yates:

- identifies Metrodorus through Strabo;
- quotes Post’s decanal reconstruction;
- joins Cicero’s “writing” metaphor to Quintilian’s phrase *imagines notae sunt*;
- proposes that shorthand *notae* might have been placed upon zodiacal or decanal images;
- cites Pliny’s verbatim-memory claim;
- concludes that Metrodorus’s art concerned memory for words and the zodiac.

Her most responsible sentence is:

> “It had to do with memory for words, possibly through memorising the *notae* or symbols of shorthand, and was connected with the zodiac. That is all we really know.”

This needs one refinement: even “memory for words” is an inference produced by joining Pliny’s verbatim-memory story to Cicero and Quintilian. Pliny does not explicitly say that the 360 loci produced the feat.

## 3. The decans and magic

Yates argues that if Metrodorus used decan figures, those figures “were certainly believed to be magical images.” She links them to Egyptian astrological imagery, temple lore, Renaissance talismanic traditions, and the decans represented in the Palazzo Schifanoia frescoes.

**[YI]** This is a genealogical hypothesis:

Metrodorus → zodiacal/decanal memory → Renaissance celestial mnemonic systems → Bruno’s magical memory.

**[DIS]** Each arrow requires qualification:

- Metrodorus → decans: Post’s reconstruction only.
- Decans → talismanic magic: true for some late antique, Arabic, and Renaissance contexts, but not all uses of decanal imagery.
- Metrodorus → Bruno: Renaissance authors used Metrodorus as an authority, but direct technical descent is unproved.
- Schifanoia → memory art: iconographic and structural analogy does not establish mnemonic use.

Yates’s fuller decan argument appears in *Giordano Bruno and the Hermetic Tradition*, pp. 45–48. Aby Warburg’s analysis of Schifanoia predates her; Yates integrates Warburg’s art history into a memory-and-Hermeticism narrative.

## 4. Chapter V: Romberch and Rosselli

At pp. 116–17 Yates accurately reports that Romberch proposes zodiacal signs as an easily memorized order and names Metrodorus. She notes the extension through Hyginus’s constellation images.

At p. 122 she describes Rosselli’s celestial place systems and mnemonic verses.

Her formulation that “every subsequent user of a celestial memory system will invoke Metrodorus” is rhetorically effective but too universal. **[DIS]** Many important celestial, astrological, cosmological, or combinatorial schemes do not name him. A narrower claim is defensible: Renaissance writers seeking a classical precedent for zodiacal mnemonic places repeatedly invoked the Quintilian notice.

## 5. Chapter IX: Bruno

At pp. 206 and 217 Yates reads Bruno’s celestial wheels and image lists through the Metrodorian genealogy. Her summary is:

> “The Metrodorian system has turned into a magical system.”

This is a statement about Bruno, not documentary evidence about Metrodorus. Bruno’s system includes planetary, zodiacal, lunar-mansion, decanal, mythological, and human-character images deployed on rotating combinatory structures. The premise of celestial mediation belongs to Bruno’s Renaissance philosophical and magical setting.

The Warburg Institute notes that later Bruno scholarship has treated Yates’s account with considerable skepticism even while recognizing its foundational importance. [Warburg Institute: “Yates and Bruno’s Mnemonics”](https://warburg.sas.ac.uk/research-fellowships/research-projects/archived-research-projects/giordano-bruno/yates-brunos-mnemonics).

## 6. Later scholarship: what stands and what changes

### Mary Carruthers

Carruthers confirms the importance of:

- ordered places;
- visual and affective images;
- inscription, storage, and treasury metaphors;
- memory as an instrument of composition and invention.

She corrects the narrowness of a history centered on spectacular architectural images and Renaissance occultism. Medieval *memoria* was a pervasive craft of reading, meditation, composition, ethics, and textual organization. Page layout, punctuation, rubrication, division, and topical structures were mnemonic technologies too.

Her most consequential correction for the present dossier is methodological: Metrodorus’s 360 loci represent one exceptional rhetorical device, not the essence of premodern memory. [Carruthers, “The Art of Memory and the Art of Page Layout”](https://doi.org/10.1177/039219210204919603).

### Lina Bolzoni

Bolzoni retains Yates’s insight that places-and-images thinking shaped Renaissance visual and literary culture but grounds the subject more closely in specific texts, reading practices, galleries, theatres, preaching, and image programmes.

For Metrodorus, this strengthens the reception history of celestial structures as repositories but supplies no new ancient evidence for decans or magic. *The Gallery of Memory* includes Rosselli among the richly illustrated mnemonic treatises. [Publisher preview](https://api.pageplace.de/preview/DT0400.9781442681330_A30532622/preview-9781442681330_A30532622.pdf).

### Paolo Rossi

Rossi’s *Clavis universalis*—translated as *Logic and the Art of Memory*—predated Yates’s book in Italian. Rossi situates memory arts within logic, encyclopedism, universal method, artificial languages, and seventeenth-century science. He complicates any straight decline from images to rational method.

For Metrodorus, Rossi’s framework encourages treating the numbered zodiac as an ordering technology rather than assuming it was an occult object.

### Jocelyn Penny Small

Small’s *Wax Tablets of the Mind* emphasizes the material and cognitive history behind metaphors of inscription, literacy, visual organization, and recall. This cautions against taking Cicero’s wax analogy as evidence that Metrodorus literally memorized stenographic characters.

### Walter J. Ong

Ong’s work on Ramus shows that Ramist dichotomies, printed diagrams, pedagogical method, and spatial display form another branch of information ordering. Later studies demonstrate that Ramism, Aristotelianism, Lullism, and imagistic memory could coexist.

Recent work has specifically rejected Yates’s broad equation of Ramist or Calvinist aniconism with rejection of imagination. Perkins objected to indecent and passion-stirring mnemonic images, but Reformed authors could endorse sacred mnemonic systems. [“William Perkins, the imagination in Calvinist theology and ‘inner iconoclasm’ after Frances Yates”](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/17496977.2021.1981695).

### Bruno scholarship

Giordano Bruno scholarship associated with Rita Sturlese, Michele Ciliberto, Hilary Gatti, Stephen Clucas, and the modern critical editions has corrected several Yatesian tendencies:

- Bruno cannot be reduced to a single coherent “Hermetic tradition.”
- His mnemonics engage Aristotle, medieval logic, Lull, Neoplatonism, astrology, rhetoric, combinatorics, and pedagogy.
- His wheels are not merely static talismanic image arrays; they operate syntactically and combinatorially.
- Celestial images may have cognitive, metaphysical, rhetorical, and magical roles at once.
- The dating, audience, and technical relations among *De umbris idearum*, *Cantus Circaeus*, *Explicatio triginta sigillorum*, and *De imaginum compositione* require work-specific analysis.

None of this disproves that Bruno could interpret celestial images magically. It does disprove using “Hermetic magic” as an exhaustive explanation.

### Camillo scholarship

Bolzoni and Alessandro Stagno have placed Giulio Camillo’s Memory Theatre within Venetian courtly, rhetorical, literary, architectural, and patronage networks. The Theatre’s planetary grades are documented from Camillo’s own *L’Idea del Theatro* and contemporary reports; they are not a surviving development of Metrodorus’s 360 degrees.

### Lull scholarship

Anthony Bonner and Alexander Fidora emphasize that Ramon Llull’s *Ars* is a logical-demonstrative and missionary system grounded in divine dignities, relational principles, and combinatorial figures. It is not originally an *ars memoriae* in the Ciceronian sense.

Yates’s tendency to place Lull, celestial memory, and Bruno in one developing stream therefore needs strong qualification. Renaissance Lullism could be mnemonicized and occultized, but Llull’s own rotating figures were intended to support discovery and demonstration, not primarily storage and recall.

### Hajdu, Rossi, Bolzoni, Carruthers, Small: collective correction

The main post-Yates correction is not a newly discovered account of Metrodorus. It is a better disciplinary map:

- rhetoric and delivery;
- dialectical topics and invention;
- manuscript/page organization;
- scholastic psychology;
- meditation and ethical formation;
- preaching;
- courtly performance;
- combinatorial logic;
- astrology and magic;
- pedagogical diagram;
- printed method.

Yates illuminated one dramatic pathway but sometimes treated analogies among these practices as genealogical descent.

# History and context

## 1. Greek rhetoric and the Academic setting

**[DT]** Cicero calls Metrodorus an Academic rhetorician. His art belongs first to rhetorical *memoria*, traditionally the fourth or fifth part of rhetoric depending on the scheme: invention, arrangement, style, memory, and delivery.

The skill had obvious practical value in:

- courtroom pleading;
- political address;
- diplomatic negotiation;
- extemporaneous response;
- teaching and declamation;
- retaining texts before convenient consultation.

**[SR]** A courtier in a multilingual Hellenistic kingdom would have had powerful incentives to cultivate ordered recall. No source, however, says Metrodorus designed the zodiacal array specifically for diplomacy or administration.

## 2. Mithridates’ court

Pliny juxtaposes Metrodorus with Mithridates, who allegedly administered twenty-two peoples in their own languages. This makes the Pontic court a literary locus of prodigious memory.

**[LEG/TR]** The linguistic feat may reflect genuine multilingual kingship, royal propaganda, or exemplary exaggeration. Pliny does not state that Metrodorus taught Mithridates his technique.

Metrodorus’s roles—rhetorician, judge, intimate adviser, ambassador—place memory inside courtly power. Exact recall could enhance authority, but it also encouraged stories of superhuman ability.

## 3. Astronomy and astrology

The zodiac offered:

- a canonical twelve-part sequence;
- a closed circle;
- thirty-degree subdivisions;
- repeated daily and annual familiarity;
- visually memorable animal and human signs;
- a numerical total of 360.

**[SR]** These are sufficient practical reasons for choosing it without invoking magic.

**[DIS]** “The signs through which the sun travels” is astronomical language compatible with astrology but not proof of astrological divination. In antiquity, astronomy, calendrics, astrology, mathematical geography, and celestial mythology overlapped institutionally and textually.

## 4. Medieval schools

There is no continuous institutional “Metrodorian school.” The artificial-memory tradition passed through Latin rhetoric, particularly the *Ad Herennium*, which medieval readers attributed to Cicero.

Dominicans and Franciscans adapted memory to:

- sermon composition;
- moral classification;
- confession;
- scriptural study;
- scholastic disputation;
- meditation.

Albertus Magnus and Thomas Aquinas integrated artificial memory into Aristotelian psychology and the virtue of prudence. Their discussions concern orderly, striking, and affectively charged representations, not a documented recovery of Metrodorus’s zodiac.

## 5. Renaissance courts, academies, and print

Humanist recovery of Quintilian supplied direct access to the 360-loci notice. Illustrated astronomical books supplied reusable celestial pictures. Printed memory manuals served preachers, lawyers, physicians, merchants, messengers, notaries, teachers, and students—audiences named on Romberch’s title page.

Thus the “revival” is bibliographical and rhetorical:

- recovery and printing of classical texts;
- printed synthesis of medieval and humanist mnemonic rules;
- appropriation of Metrodorus as antique authority;
- integration with illustrated celestial books.

It is not the recovery of a lost Metrodorian manual.

# People

## Practitioners and transmitters

### Metrodorus

Documented as rhetorician and political actor; reported by Cicero, Pliny, and Quintilian as an exceptional memorist.

### Charmadas

Frequently paired with Metrodorus by Cicero as an Academic with prodigious memory. His presence suggests a living Greek rhetorical-philosophical practice, not a solitary invention.

### Cicero

An admiring witness and transmitter. His account is especially valuable because he had traveled in Asia and heard Metrodorus.

### Quintilian

A critical transmitter. He preserves the only technical numerical detail while rejecting Metrodorus’s self-display.

### Pliny

Collector of exemplary memory stories; responsible for the influential claim that Metrodorus “perfected” Simonides’ art.

### Johannes Romberch

A Dominican compiler who transforms Quintilian’s report into a usable Renaissance place system. He explains 360 through zodiacal degrees and extends celestial loci using Hyginus.

### Cosimo Rosselli and Damiano Rosselli

Cosimo, a Dominican, composed the *Thesaurus*; Damiano edited it posthumously. The work integrates classical memory, Christian cosmology, alphabets, images, gestures, and mnemonic verse.

### Giordano Bruno

Invokes and transforms the celestial-memory tradition, but the relationship is typological and reception-historical, not a demonstrable direct transmission from Metrodorus.

### Alexander Dicson

A Bruno-associated Scottish writer. “Heius Scepsius,” the pseudonym on *De umbra rationis et iudicii*, evokes Scepsis and thereby the Metrodorian authority. The polemic shows that “Scepsian” had become a recognizable label for contentious imagistic memory.

### William Perkins—or “G. P. Cantabrigiensis”

The authorship of attacks associated with the Bruno–Dicson controversy has been disputed. Yates identified the author with Perkins; later bibliography has debated details. Perkins’s own *Arte of Prophecying* condemns animation of mnemonic images when it requires absurd, monstrous, or lust-provoking fantasies.

## Patrons and political associates

- Mithridates VI Eupator: patron and eventual enemy.
- Tigranes II of Armenia: recipient of Metrodorus’s embassy and, in Plutarch’s narrative, remorseful contributor to his fate.
- Renaissance patrons of Bruno and Camillo belong to the later celestial-memory story but are not links in a documented Metrodorian succession.

## Anecdotes and absent anecdotes

The catalogue brief requests stories such as the auctioneer, the professor’s party, the student who lost his memory, and the accused magician. None can be securely attached to Metrodorus.

- **[NEG]** No auctioneer story is found in the ancient testimonia about him.
- **[NEG]** No professor’s-party demonstration belongs to him.
- **[NEG]** No student of Metrodorus is recorded as losing his memory.
- **[NEG]** No source says he was accused of magic or tried for mnemonic practice.
- **[LEG]** Pliny’s claim of exact verbatim repetition is the chief performance anecdote.
- **[DT/TR]** Quintilian’s charge of vanity is criticism, not a report of prosecution.

# The magic question

## Was Metrodorus’s system magical?

The answer must be divided chronologically.

### In surviving ancient evidence

**[NEG]** No.

Cicero calls the memory nearly divine, but *divina prope memoria* is praise for extraordinary ability, not a technical allegation of sorcery.

Quintilian calls it vanity and boasting, not magic.

Pliny places it among marvels of human memory, not ritual arts.

Strabo and Plutarch describe political danger, not heresy or occult prosecution.

No source mentions:

- invocation of planetary spirits;
- talismanic fabrication;
- astrological election of a time;
- ritual purity;
- prayer;
- demons;
- divination;
- theurgy;
- Chaldaean rites;
- ecclesiastical condemnation.

### Yates’s conditional argument

**[YI]** If Post’s decan reconstruction is correct, Yates argues, Metrodorus used images that later traditions regarded as magical.

The conditional is crucial. Three different claims are often collapsed:

1. Metrodorus used the zodiac. **Documented.**
2. Metrodorus used decan figures. **Speculative.**
3. He used them as efficacious talismans. **Unsupported.**

A picture can function as an astronomical label, mythographic figure, mnemonic marker, astrological significator, or talisman. One function does not prove another.

### Philostratus’s accused sophist

Yates notes that Dionysius of Miletus, a sophist of Hadrian’s era, was accused of teaching pupils through “Chaldaean arts.” Philostratus rejects the allegation.

**[TR]** This shows that extraordinary mnemonic or pedagogical performance could generate rumors of occult assistance.

**[NEG]** It does not show that Metrodorus was accused, or that zodiacal loci were inherently magical.

### Romberch and Rosselli

Both were Dominicans. Rosselli’s work appeared with Dominican and inquisitorial endorsements. Their celestial places therefore cannot be classified simply as clandestine or heretical.

**[DT]** They present celestial orders inside Christian pedagogical works.

**[NEG]** No known inquisitorial proceeding arose from their invocation of Metrodorus.

### Bruno

With Bruno the magic question changes.

**[DT]** Bruno explicitly works with astral images, cosmic correspondences, and powers of imagination in a philosophical world where “magic,” natural causation, cognition, and metaphysics overlap.

**[YI, partly confirmed]** Yates is right that Bruno can transform a classical ordering device into something more ontologically ambitious.

**[DIS]** Whether every Brunian wheel is a magical talisman, and whether the operator expected literal astral influx, must be decided work by work. Later Bruno scholarship rejects a single undifferentiated “Hermetic magic” explanation.

### The *Ars notoria*

The *Ars notoria* promises rapid or infused acquisition of knowledge through prayers, ritual observances, and contemplation of complex *notae*.

Yates contrasts this with Bruno’s laborious mnemonic magic.

**[NEG]** There is no historical evidence that Metrodorus practiced the *Ars notoria*, which is medieval and Christian in its surviving forms.

**[DIS]** Cicero’s writing metaphor and Quintilian’s word *notae* do not link Metrodorus to the medieval ritual art. The shared vocabulary of signs is insufficient.

# Afterlife

## 1. Renaissance memory manuals

The clearest afterlife is the name “Metrodorus” as classical authorization for nonarchitectural, celestial loci.

Romberch establishes the model:

- twelve signs as major places;
- 360 degrees as total loci;
- Hyginian constellations for expansion.

Rosselli repeats and Christianizes the broader celestial-place repertoire.

## 2. Camillo

Camillo’s Memory Theatre arranges universal knowledge in seven rising grades governed by planetary and mythological imagery.

**[SR]** Both systems replace an ordinary building with a cosmic structure.

**[NEG]** No surviving text says Camillo derived his seven-grade architecture from Metrodorus’s 360 loci. The lineage is thematic, not documented.

## 3. Bruno

Bruno is the most elaborate “descendant” in Yates’s narrative:

- zodiacal and planetary imagery;
- decans and lunar mansions;
- lists of mythological agents;
- concentric wheels;
- mobile combination;
- memory as reformation of psychic and cosmic order.

**[TR]** Renaissance polemic made “Scepsian” a label for this celestial-memory tendency.

**[DIS]** Bruno’s technical sources include Llull, astrology, medieval logic, classical rhetoric, Ficinian and Neoplatonic materials, and his own inventions. Calling the whole system “Metrodorian” exaggerates one classical precedent.

## 4. Riccio, Dicson, Perkins, Fludd, Bacon

### Lodovico Riccio

Yates associates bodily and zodiacal orders in Renaissance memory manuals with the rationalizing continuation of celestial place systems.

### Alexander Dicson

The pseudonym “Scepsius” makes the reception explicit: Metrodorus’s place-name becomes a badge within the Ramist–Brunian controversy.

### Perkins

Opposes emotionally inflaming mnemonic imagery, not specifically the ancient 360-place array.

### Robert Fludd

Fludd’s cosmic theatres and zodiacal structures belong to an encyclopedic, astrological, theatrical, and mnemonic synthesis. Their relation to Metrodorus is retrospective classification.

### Francis Bacon

Bacon criticizes ostentatious feats and sterile memory arts. “Metrodorian” can thus become shorthand for prodigious but intellectually unproductive retention.

## 5. Literature and visual culture

### Dante

Dante’s ordered realms, vivid punishments, and spatially sequenced encounters are highly compatible with medieval mnemonic practice.

**[SR/DIS]** Carruthers and others illuminate Dante through memorial culture, but no evidence makes the *Commedia* a Metrodorian zodiacal system.

### Frescoes

The Palazzo Schifanoia frescoes at Ferrara contain zodiacal signs, decans, planetary gods, and courtly scenes.

**[DT]** Their astrological iconography is well documented.

**[YI/DIS]** Yates uses them to visualize the decan images she supposes Metrodorus may have used. That makes Schifanoia a Renaissance analogue, not evidence for his apparatus.

### Emblem books

Emblematic combinations of motto, image, and explanatory text exemplify the early modern conviction that striking images can store and stimulate discourse. The relationship is cultural and functional, not direct descent.

### Borges’s Funes

Funes possesses overwhelming, indiscriminate memory and cannot generalize. He is almost the inverse of artificial memory, whose places impose selection and order.

**[MI]** Calling Funes “Metrodorian” is a modern analogy; Borges does not reconstruct the zodiacal system.

### Umberto Eco

Eco’s interest in encyclopedias, signs, universal languages, Llull, and combinatorics makes him part of the intellectual afterlife of ordered symbolic systems. Again, the relation to Metrodorus is conceptual, not genealogical.

## 6. Universal language, combinatorics, and computing

A frequently repeated grand genealogy runs:

Metrodorus → Llull → Bruno → Leibniz → symbolic logic → computing.

It needs disassembly.

- **Metrodorus to Llull:** **[NEG]** no documented transmission.
- **Llull to Bruno:** **[DT/TR]** extensive engagement, though Bruno transforms Llull.
- **Llull to Leibniz:** **[DT/TR]** Leibniz knew and evaluated Lullian combinatorial traditions.
- **Leibniz to symbolic logic/computation:** **[SR]** important conceptual ancestry through the *characteristica universalis* and *calculus ratiocinator*.
- **Metrodorus to computers:** **[MI]** a metaphorical lineage only.

The interesting computing analogy is structural: a 360-place array resembles an addressed memory space. Post’s calculation model suggests random access by sign, decan, and local offset. But there is no evidence Metrodorus conceived addresses, registers, algorithms, or mechanical calculation in the modern sense.

## 7. Modern memory palaces and memory athletes

Modern competitors commonly use:

- journeys and loci;
- person–action–object encodings;
- number-shape or phonetic systems;
- large prelearned image vocabularies;
- grids and multi-level addressing.

A 360-place zodiac can be reconstructed and used today.

**[MI]** Such a modern implementation would demonstrate feasibility, not historical accuracy. Modern “Sabian symbols” for individual zodiacal degrees are nineteenth- and twentieth-century occult creations and must not be retrojected onto Metrodorus. The LacusCurtius note properly warns against that confusion. [Quintilian commentary](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Quintilian/Institutio_Oratoria/11b%2A.html).

# Controversies and disputes

## 1. Did Metrodorus invent a zodiacal art?

**Evidence:** Quintilian says he found 360 loci in the twelve signs.

**Verdict:** He used or claimed a distinctive arrangement; “invented the zodiacal art” is stronger than the text warrants.

## 2. Were there thirty places per sign?

**Evidence:** 360 divided by twelve; ancient zodiacal signs conventionally contain thirty degrees.

**Verdict:** Strong reconstruction, not explicit testimony.

## 3. Were the places individual degrees?

**Evidence:** Romberch’s sixteenth-century explanation and the mathematical structure of the zodiac.

**Verdict:** Plausible but not proven for Metrodorus.

## 4. Did he use thirty-six decan figures?

**First influential formulation:** L. A. Post, 1932.

**Spread by:** Yates, 1966; subsequently repeated in encyclopedias, popular memory histories, occult manuals, and web summaries.

**Evidence:** Compatibility with the number 360 and ancient astrological subdivision.

**Counter-evidence:** Total absence from Cicero, Quintilian, Pliny, and surviving Metrodorian fragments.

**Verdict:** Speculative.

## 5. Did each decan contain ten loci?

**First influential formulation:** Post.

**Verdict:** A useful modern model, but a second-order hypothesis resting on the first-order decan hypothesis.

## 6. Did Metrodorus use shorthand *notae*?

Yates argues that Quintilian’s statement “images are *notae* by which we mark things” and Cicero’s wax-writing metaphor may imply inner shorthand.

But *nota* can mean mark, sign, token, or mnemonic indicator without denoting a standardized stenographic script.

**Verdict:** Possible but weakly evidenced. It should not be stated as fact.

## 7. Did he memorize words rather than things?

Pliny says he could repeat heard material in the same words. The *Ad Herennium* distinguishes memory for things and memory for words.

**Verdict:** Exact verbal memory is attested as a feat; the method used to accomplish it is not.

## 8. Was he an astrologer?

Quintilian’s zodiac implies familiarity with celestial divisions.

**NEG:** No ancient source calls him an astrologer.

**Verdict:** Possible, not documented.

## 9. Was the art magical?

No ancient accusation survives.

**Verdict:** The ancient system is not demonstrably magical. Magic enters securely only in some later celestial-memory appropriations.

## 10. Are the decan images “certainly” magical?

Some decan figures occur in astrological, talismanic, ritual, and magical texts. Others function as calendar, astronomical, iconographical, or prognostic markers.

**Verdict:** Their status depends on context. Even if Metrodorus used them, mnemonic employment would not automatically prove talismanic intent.

## 11. Did Romberch preserve an ancient tradition independent of Quintilian?

Romberch closely follows the classical notice and adds the standard 360-degree explanation.

**Verdict:** No evidence of an independent Metrodorian source.

## 12. Did Rosselli preserve independent material?

The surviving treatment belongs to Renaissance compilation and reception.

**Verdict:** No identified independent ancient source.

## 13. Is every later celestial memory “Metrodorian”?

This is Yates’s expansive rhetorical claim.

**Verdict:** Metrodorus becomes an often-cited classical precedent, but “every” is unsustainable, and formal resemblance does not prove descent.

## 14. Was Metrodorus the *Misoromaios*?

Pliny and Ovid attest an anti-Roman Scepsian Metrodorus; Strabo and Plutarch attest a leading Mithridatic intellectual and diplomat.

**Verdict:** Probably the same man, but the identification remains prosopographically uncertain.

## 15. Was he executed by Mithridates?

Strabo gives royal order and natural illness as rival accounts. Plutarch supplies a narrative of betrayal, disclosure, and remorse.

**Verdict:** Ancient dispute; murder is not certain.

## 16. Did Yates “get it wrong”?

A differentiated answer is necessary.

### Confirmed or durable

- Cicero and Quintilian place Metrodorus within artificial memory.
- The zodiac supplied a nonarchitectural order of places.
- Renaissance mnemonists explicitly invoked him.
- Bruno’s celestial mnemonic structures could have magical and transformative ambitions.
- Artificial memory mattered far beyond rote recall.

### Overextended

- Post’s decans sometimes become quasi-factual in the momentum of Yates’s narrative.
- The ancient zodiacal notice becomes the root of later occult celestial systems without sufficient transmission evidence.
- “Every subsequent user” is too absolute.
- Ramism, Protestantism, iconoclasm, and hostility to imagination are aligned too neatly.
- “Hermeticism” can become an overly unified explanatory agent.

### Not actually claimed without qualification

Yates herself repeatedly says “if Post is right” and ends with “that is all we really know.” Many popular summaries are more dogmatic than Yates.

# Open questions

1. Did Quintilian know Metrodorus through Cicero, another rhetorical handbook, oral school tradition, or a now-lost work?
2. Does *invenerit locos* describe an actual diagram, a memorized verbal sequence, or merely an alleged feat?
3. Were the 360 loci one per degree?
4. If degree-loci were used, how were adjacent degrees made perceptually distinct?
5. Were decans, bounds, dodecatemoria, fixed stars, or mythological figures used as intermediate landmarks?
6. Did Metrodorus’s system support sequential recall only, or direct access by number?
7. Was Pliny’s exact verbal repetition achieved through this same array?
8. Did Metrodorus write any *techne mnemonike*, rhetorical handbook, or astrological work?
9. Which surviving fragments assigned to the historian, geographer, Academic, and anti-Roman polemicist belong to the same man?
10. Does any unedited Renaissance mnemonic manuscript preserve a variant of the Romberch–Rosselli celestial scheme?
11. What precise verses does each surviving Rosselli copy give for the zodiac, and do copy-specific states or corrections exist?
12. Did Renaissance readers actually practice the 360-degree arrangement, or was it chiefly a learned citation?
13. Can marginal annotations in surviving Romberch and Rosselli copies document use?
14. When did Post first connect Metrodorus specifically with decan figures, and did he draw on an earlier German, French, or Italian conjecture?
15. Can reception study identify the exact point at which Post’s “probably” became an apparent fact in reference literature?
16. How should astronomical, astrological, mnemonic, and magical uses of the same image be distinguished in individual Renaissance works?

The chief finding is therefore an absence: the surviving ancient evidence gives a striking numerical framework but almost none of its implementation. The fullest honest reconstruction is necessarily layered. A twelve-by-thirty zodiacal place system is likely; a thirty-six-by-ten decanal hierarchy is possible; shorthand and magical activation remain conjectural.

# Metrodorus of Scepsis’s zodiacal memory: Research Dossier

> **Name:** Metrodorus of Scepsis’s zodiacal memory  
> **Exponent:** Metrodorus of Scepsis, Academic philosopher, rhetorician, courtier, judge, diplomat, and anti-Roman writer at the court of Mithridates VI Eupator  
> **Date:** Probably active c. 100–70 BCE; the often-repeated lifespan c. 145–70 BCE is a modern reconstruction  
> **Source text:** No work by Metrodorus on memory survives. The essential testimonia are Cicero, *De oratore* 2.360; Quintilian, *Institutio oratoria* 11.2.22; Pliny, *Naturalis historia* 7.24; supplemented biographically by Cicero, *De oratore* 3.75, Strabo 13.1.55, and Plutarch, *Lucullus* 22  
> **Principle in one sentence:** Metrodorus reportedly organized artificial memory into 360 ordered *loci* associated somehow with the twelve signs traversed by the sun, upon which mnemonic images could be mentally “written”; the familiar scheme of thirty-six decans with ten places each is L. A. Post’s twentieth-century reconstruction, not an ancient description.  
> **Renaissance revivals:** Johannes Romberch, *Congestorium artificiose memorie* (Venice, 1520; revised edition 1533), especially tract II, fols. 19v–20r in the 1533 foliation and the celestial extension around 33v; Cosimo Rosselli, *Thesaurus artificiosae memoriae* (Venice, 1579), around fol. 22v  
> **Yates:** *The Art of Memory* (1966), ch. I, pp. 19–20 and 23–25; ch. II, pp. 39–42; ch. IV, p. 100; ch. V, pp. 116–17 and 122; ch. IX, pp. 206 and 217; ch. XII, p. 267. The concentrated reconstruction is pp. 39–42.

## Evidentiary key

The following labels are used throughout:

- **[DT] Documented text:** explicitly stated in a surviving primary source.
- **[SR] Scholarly reconstruction:** an inference proposed by a modern scholar.
- **[TR] Tradition/reception:** asserted by later writers, without surviving direct evidence from Metrodorus.
- **[YI] Yates’s interpretation:** an argument or synthesis peculiar to, or strongly associated with, Frances Yates.
- **[DIS] Disputed:** attribution, date, interpretation, or identification contested or materially uncertain.
- **[LEG] Legend/anecdote:** transmitted story whose historicity cannot be established.
- **[MI] Modern invention:** a detail first supplied by modern reconstruction or popular exposition and sometimes mistakenly presented as ancient fact.
- **[NEG] Negative finding:** something the surviving evidence does not supply.

## Basic identification

### The man

**[DT]** Metrodorus came from Scepsis in the Troad/Mysia. Cicero met or heard him in Asia while serving as quaestor in 79 BCE and calls him a rhetorician connected with the Academy. Strabo says he moved from philosophy into public affairs, wrote mostly in a rhetorical manner, cultivated an arresting new style, married advantageously at Chalcedon, and became a favored associate of Mithridates VI. He served as a judge whose decisions were not appealable to the king. [Cicero, *De oratore* 3.75](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Cic.%20de%20Orat.%203.75); [Strabo 13.1.55](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Strabo/13A3%2A.html).

**[DT]** Plutarch calls him a man of pleasing speech and broad learning and says his intimacy with Mithridates was such that he was styled the king’s “father.” Sent to ask Tigranes II for assistance against Rome, he privately advised Tigranes against helping. Mithridates learned of that answer; Metrodorus died while being returned to him. Strabo preserves two explanations—execution on Mithridates’ orders or natural illness—while Plutarch’s narrative connects the death to royal anger. [Plutarch, *Lucullus* 22](https://lexundria.com/plut_luc/22/prr); [Strabo 13.1.55](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Aabo%3Atlg%2C0099%2C001%3A13%3A1).

**[SR/DIS]** A death around 70 BCE is probable from the setting in the Third Mithridatic War. A birth around 145 or 140 BCE is inferred from Cicero’s references, academic affiliation, and career length; no ancient source supplies it directly.

**[DIS]** The surviving testimonia attached to “Metrodorus of Scepsis” may not all concern one individual. Ancient literature contains numerous Metrodori. Identification of the memorist, Academic rhetorician, Mithridatic statesman, historian, geographer, and *Misoromaios* or “Rome-hater” is plausible and conventional, but not demonstrable in every fragment.

### The system’s documentary core

Only three propositions about Metrodorus’s memory are securely attested:

1. **[DT]** Cicero says that Metrodorus used images in places, analogous to letters inscribed on wax.
2. **[DT]** Quintilian says that he “found” 360 places in the twelve signs through which the sun travels.
3. **[DT]** Pliny says that Metrodorus brought the art of memory to completion and could reproduce what he heard in the same words.

Everything more exact—the thirty-six decans, ten subordinate places per decan, decan gods or figures, numerical calculation, shorthand symbols, magical activation—is inferential.

# The system in detail

## 1. Classical artificial memory into which Metrodorus fits

The ordinary ancient technique is clearest in the anonymous *Rhetorica ad Herennium* 3.16–24, Cicero’s *De oratore* 2.350–60, and Quintilian’s *Institutio oratoria* 11.2.17–26.

**[DT]** Its two elements are:

- *loci* or *loca*: stable, clearly ordered mnemonic backgrounds;
- *imagines* or *simulacra*: changeable representations assigned to those places.

The standard analogy is tablet and writing: places are wax; images are letters. At recall, one mentally traverses the places in their fixed order and reads off the images.

Quintilian summarizes:

> “*locis pro cera, simulacris pro litteris utamur*”  
> “Let us use places as wax and images as letters.”  
> —*Institutio oratoria* 11.2.21, ed. Michael Winterbottom; trans. Donald Russell

[Latin and Butler translation](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Quintilian/Institutio_Oratoria/11b%2A.html).

### Rules for places

**[DT]** In *Ad Herennium* and Quintilian, places should be:

- numerous enough for the intended material;
- arranged in a definite sequence;
- well lit;
- clear and unobstructed;
- neither too large nor too small;
- moderately spaced;
- visually distinct;
- familiar or thoroughly rehearsed;
- capable of being traversed both forward and backward;
- reusable after old images are removed.

A house is exemplary, but streets, public buildings, journeys, paintings, and entirely imaginary regions can serve.

### Rules for images

**[DT]** Images should be:

- active or doing something;
- sharply defined;
- unusual, striking, beautiful, ugly, comic, obscene, bloody, or otherwise emotionally forceful;
- adapted either to the substance of a thing (*memoria rerum*) or to words and syllables (*memoria verborum*);
- personally memorable rather than mechanically copied from a teacher’s stock list.

The *Ad Herennium* says ordinary things slip from memory, whereas the exceptionally base, dishonorable, unusual, great, unbelievable, or laughable remain.

### The lawsuit image

This example belongs to the *Ad Herennium*, not to Metrodorus. It illustrates the classical mechanism into which Cicero places him.

The case concerns a defendant accused of killing a man by poison for an inheritance, with witnesses and accomplices. The mnemonic image puts the sick or murdered man in bed; the defendant stands beside him holding a cup in one hand, tablets in the other, and a ram’s testicles on one finger. The objects cue poison, inheritance or will, witnesses, and testimony.

**[DT]** The ram’s testicles depend on a Latin sound-association: *testiculi* helps recall *testes*, “witnesses.” The example demonstrates that images are constructed mnemonic signs, not necessarily representations of actual courtroom events.

**[YI/SR]** Yates playfully wonders whether the ram might also suggest Aries and thus a celestial place. No ancient source makes that connection. It is not evidence for Metrodorus.

## 2. Cicero’s direct evidence

Cicero’s dialogue is dramatically set in 91 BCE. The historical Cicero says elsewhere that he encountered Metrodorus during his Asian travels.

At *De oratore* 2.360:

> “*tamquam litteris in cera … imaginibus in eis locis … perscribere*”  
> “as with letters on wax … to write with images upon those places.”  
> —ed. Kazimierz Kumaniecki; trans. E. W. Sutton/H. Rackham, revised gloss

The complete sentence names Charmadas at Athens and Metrodorus in Asia as men of nearly divine memory. Each said that he mentally wrote, by images in places, whatever he wished to remember. [Latin text](https://m.thelatinlibrary.com/cicero/oratore2.shtml); [English text](https://www.attalus.org/cicero/deoratore2E.html).

What this establishes:

- **[DT]** Metrodorus used multiple places.
- **[DT]** He deposited images upon them.
- **[DT]** The process was compared to writing.
- **[DT]** Cicero judged his trained memory extraordinary.
- **[NEG]** Cicero does not mention the zodiac, 360 places, decans, astrology, shorthand, or magic here.
- **[NEG]** He gives no example of a Metrodorian image.
- **[NEG]** He does not describe how Metrodorus subdivided or navigated the celestial places.

The wax-writing metaphor need not imply literal written characters. In the ancient art, “letters” and “writing” regularly describe the relation of images to ordered places.

## 3. Quintilian’s 360 places

Quintilian first describes orthodox places and images and then remarks:

> “*Metrodorus in XII signis, per quae sol meat, trecenos et sexagenos invenerit locos*.”  
> “Metrodorus found 360 places in the twelve signs through which the sun moves.”  
> —*Institutio oratoria* 11.2.22

[Latin text](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A2007.01.0068%3Abook%3D11%3Achapter%3D2); [Loeb/Butler text](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Quintilian/Institutio_Oratoria/11b%2A.html).

Quintilian immediately calls it:

> “*vanitas nimirum fuit atque iactatio*”  
> “surely vanity and boastfulness.”

His criticism is that Metrodorus gloried in a memory owed more to art than nature. The syntax may express amazement as much as technical objection, but the adverse judgment is unmistakable.

What Quintilian establishes:

- **[DT]** The total was 360.
- **[DT]** The framework was “in” the twelve signs.
- **[DT]** The twelve signs were those through which the sun moves: the zodiacal or ecliptic signs.
- **[DT]** Quintilian regarded the performance as ostentatious.
- **[NEG]** He does not say “one place per degree,” although 360 naturally invites that interpretation.
- **[NEG]** He does not mention decans.
- **[NEG]** He does not say that each sign supplied exactly thirty loci.
- **[NEG]** He supplies no diagram, wheel, table, or list.
- **[NEG]** He does not explain whether the signs themselves, their constituent degrees, imagined compartments, or pictures associated with degrees constituted the loci.

### Are the 360 loci simply zodiacal degrees?

**[SR]** The economical reconstruction is twelve signs × thirty degrees = 360 degree-places. Romberch explicitly adopts this explanation in the sixteenth century: the zodiac is customarily measured by astrologers in as many degrees as Metrodorus assigned loci.

**[DIS]** That does not prove Metrodorus himself used individual degree-images. A geometrical degree is a coordinate, not automatically a visually distinctive mnemonic background. Ancient astrology supplied other subdivisions—decans, bounds or terms, dodecatemoria, houses, fixed-star configurations—but no source identifies which, if any, Metrodorus employed.

## 4. Pliny’s verbatim-memory notice

Pliny writes:

> “*ars … Simonide inventa, Metrodoro Scepsio consummata*”  
> “the art, invented by Simonides, was perfected by Metrodorus of Scepsis.”

He adds that Metrodorus could repeat what he had heard *iisdem verbis*, “in the same words.” [Pliny, *Natural History* 7.24](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.02.0137%3Abook%3D7%3Achapter%3D24).

The notice occurs in a catalogue of memory prodigies: Cyrus and Scipio remembering names, Cineas identifying Roman senators, Mithridates speaking the languages of his subjects, and Charmadas reproducing books.

Interpretive limits:

- **[DT]** Pliny associates Metrodorus with exact verbal recall.
- **[DT]** He treats Metrodorus as a historical improver or consummator of an art attributed to Simonides.
- **[TR]** The Simonides-to-Metrodorus succession is Pliny’s compressed history, not an extant lineage of manuals.
- **[NEG]** Pliny does not connect the verbatim feat explicitly to the zodiacal 360 loci.
- **[NEG]** He gives no procedure or demonstration.
- **[DIS]** “Could repeat what he heard in the same words” may be rhetorical exemplum rather than controlled report.

## 5. The Post–Yates reconstruction

L. A. Post proposed the most influential reconstruction in “Ancient Memory Systems,” *Classical Weekly* 25.14 (1932), pp. 105–10, especially p. 109.

Post’s reasoning was:

1. Ancient astrologers divided the zodiac into twelve signs.
2. Each sign contains thirty degrees.
3. The zodiac was also divided into thirty-six decans of ten degrees.
4. Each decan could be represented by a distinctive figure.
5. Metrodorus could therefore group ten loci beneath each decan figure.
6. Thirty-six groups × ten loci = 360.
7. Arithmetic would allow rapid access to a numbered locus and reduce the risk of omission.

**[SR]** This is ingenious and operationally plausible.

**[MI]** It is frequently retold as if Quintilian said that Metrodorus used thirty-six decan figures with ten loci apiece. He did not.

**[DIS]** Difficulties include:

- Post produced no Metrodorian text, diagram, or ancient parallel explicitly joining decans to mnemonic loci.
- Distinctive decan figures are unevenly documented across ancient astrological traditions.
- A decan is itself a ten-degree section. Post’s extra ten loci under each figure require another level of visualization that no source describes.
- Calculation may locate a number, but mnemonic places ordinarily must also be sensorially distinct.
- Nothing proves that Metrodorus used Egyptian-derived decan iconography rather than zodiacal degrees, an astronomical diagram, a verbal enumeration, or some lost arrangement.

Yates quotes Post at length and cautiously frames the conclusion with “if Post is right.” She nevertheless makes it central to her genealogy of celestial memory.

## 6. What serves as places and what serves as images?

The safest reconstruction separates three layers.

### Layer A: directly attested

- **Places:** 360 loci “in” the twelve solar signs.
- **Images:** unspecified images mentally inscribed in those loci.
- **Order:** zodiacal succession supplies at least a twelve-part order.
- **Retrieval:** the ordinary ancient procedure would be to traverse the loci and recover the deposited images.

### Layer B: strong inference

- Thirty loci probably belonged to each sign.
- The 360 total probably corresponds to the 360 degrees of the idealized zodiac.
- The stable celestial framework functioned like the stable rooms of a building.

### Layer C: speculative reconstruction

- Thirty-six decan figures were principal indexing places.
- Each decan held ten subsidiary loci.
- Mnemonic shorthand signs were written on them.
- Numerical calculation enabled direct random access.
- The decan figures were talismanic or magically efficacious.

Only Layer A should be called “Metrodorus’s system” without qualification.

## 7. How order and retrieval may have worked

**[SR] Sequential model:** Begin with Aries and traverse Taurus, Gemini, Cancer, Leo, Virgo, Libra, Scorpio, Sagittarius, Capricorn, Aquarius, Pisces. Within each sign, move through thirty subdivisions. Images deposited in successive loci reproduce a speech in order.

**[SR] Indexed model:** Convert an ordinal number into sign and degree. For item \(n\), the sign is determined by blocks of thirty, and the degree by the remainder. This is the attractive “random access” implication of a 360-place grid, but no ancient testimony says Metrodorus retrieved by arithmetic.

**[SR] Decanal model:** Convert \(n\) into a decan block of ten and a position within that block. The decan figure serves as a landmark. This is Post’s special contribution.

**[NEG]** No source says whether Metrodorus could move backward, jump directly to a requested item, erase images after use, or maintain multiple speeches in the same celestial array.

## 8. Worked examples, diagrams, wheels, and tables

### Metrodorus himself

- **[NEG]** No worked example survives.
- **[NEG]** No Metrodorian diagram survives.
- **[NEG]** No wheel or table can be securely attributed to him.
- **[NEG]** No original-language treatise survives.
- **[NEG]** No manuscript or title of a mnemonic work is known.

A modern diagram showing twelve sectors, thirty degrees per sector, thirty-six decans, or ten boxes beneath each decan illustrates a reconstruction; it is not a facsimile or ancient plate.

### Related examples requested in the general template

The lawsuit image belongs to *Ad Herennium*, Camillo’s seven grades to the sixteenth-century Memory Theatre, and Bruno’s concentric wheels to *De umbris idearum* and related works. None is an example supplied by Metrodorus or an ancient witness to his practice.

They matter as comparanda:

- *Ad Herennium* shows how vivid images encode propositions and words.
- Camillo turns an ordered cosmic-theatrical structure into a classified repository.
- Bruno combines celestial image catalogues with mobile combinatory wheels.

But using those systems to fill gaps in Metrodorus would be circular.

# The sources

## A. Ancient primary testimonia

### Cicero, *De oratore* 2.360

**Date:** composed 55 BCE; dramatic date 91 BCE.  
**Importance:** closest surviving testimony and the only source explicitly joining Metrodorus to the places-and-images method.

**Standard editions:**

- Kazimierz Kumaniecki, ed., *M. Tulli Ciceronis De oratore*, Teubner, Leipzig, 1969.
- E. W. Sutton and H. Rackham, trans., *Cicero: De Oratore*, Loeb Classical Library 348–49, Harvard University Press, 1942.
- James M. May and Jakob Wisse, trans., *Cicero: On the Ideal Orator*, Oxford University Press, 2001.

**Textual point:** Manuscripts and editions vary in spelling and punctuation, but no consequential variant is known that removes Metrodorus, the images, or the loci. Some older English translations render *imaginibus* loosely as “symbols” or “ideas,” which can make the mechanism seem more abstract than the Latin.

### Cicero, *De oratore* 3.75

**[DT]** Cicero says he encountered in Asia “that Metrodorus” whose memory Antonius had mentioned and identifies him as a rhetorician from the Academy. This is important evidence for joining the memorist to the Academic rhetorician.

### Quintilian, *Institutio oratoria* 11.2.17–26, especially 22

**Date:** c. 95 CE.  
**Importance:** sole source for the 360 zodiacal loci.

**Standard editions:**

- Michael Winterbottom, ed., *M. Fabi Quintiliani Institutionis oratoriae libri duodecim*, 2 vols., Oxford Classical Texts, 1970.
- Donald A. Russell, ed. and trans., *Quintilian: The Orator’s Education*, Loeb Classical Library 124–27, 2001.
- H. E. Butler, trans., *Institutio Oratoria*, Loeb Classical Library, 1920–22.

**Textual point:** Older texts vary in numerals—*XII* versus *duodecim*, *trecenos et sexagenos* versus orthographic variants—but the substance, 360 loci in twelve signs, is stable.

**Translation problem:** *Invenerit* may mean “found,” “devised,” or “managed to discover.” “Found” should not be taken to mean that 360 visually marked locations were objectively visible in the sky.

### Pliny, *Naturalis historia* 7.24

**Date:** completed by 77 CE.  
**Importance:** supplies the claim of exact verbal recall and the Simonides–Metrodorus genealogy.

**Standard editions:**

- H. Rackham et al., trans., *Pliny: Natural History*, Loeb Classical Library.
- John Bostock and H. T. Riley, trans., *The Natural History of Pliny*, 1855.

### Other possible testimonia

**[DIS]** Pliny 34.34 mentions a Metrodorus of Scepsis surnamed for hatred of the Roman name and associated with hostile writing about Rome. Ovid, *Ex Ponto* 4.16.15–16, refers to a Scepsian Metrodorus who attacked Roman ways. These are conventionally linked with Mithridates’ courtier, but do not concern memory.

**[DIS]** Fragment collections may classify him as a historian—e.g. *Fragmente der griechischen Historiker* 184—but the boundaries of the corpus remain uncertain.

## B. Medieval transmission

Metrodorus’s own work did not circulate in the Middle Ages insofar as surviving catalogues show.

His name was accessible through:

- Cicero’s *De oratore*, although its medieval circulation was incomplete;
- Pliny’s widely copied *Natural History*;
- Quintilian, whose complete text was recovered by Poggio Bracciolini at St Gall in 1416, though partial medieval manuscripts existed;
- later rhetorical compilations.

**[NEG]** No medieval manuscript contains a Metrodorian zodiacal diagram identified as such.

## C. Johannes Romberch

### Bibliography

Johannes Host von Romberch, *Congestorium artificiose memorie*, Venice: Georgius de Rusconibus, 1520; revised Venice: Melchior Sessa, July 1533.

The work is divided into four treatises:

1. preliminaries and defenses of artificial memory;
2. the nature and manufacture of places;
3. images and their formation;
4. applications to sciences, sermons, arguments, histories, and verse.

The 1533 text’s contents and transcription are available through [Latin Wikisource](https://la.wikisource.org/wiki/Congestorium_Artificiosae_Memoriae). The Library of Congress has a digitized early copy under [Johannes Romberch](https://www.loc.gov/item/2017692231/).

### The Metrodorus passage

In the 1533 foliation, tract II, around fol. 19v, Romberch writes:

> “*Metrodorus … in duodecim signis … trescentos et sexagenos constituit locos*”  
> “Metrodorus established 360 places in the twelve signs.”

He names the signs and explains the total:

> “*totidem gradibus iste Zodiacus circulus apud astrologos metiri soleat*”  
> “because among astrologers the zodiacal circle is customarily measured by that many degrees.”

The Latin is reproduced and collated in Santiago García’s modern critical study of the *Congestorium*. [Critical study and transcription](https://dehesa.unex.es/server/api/core/bitstreams/d42663d2-4ddb-40c0-be50-6720bbee3a8b/content).

### What Romberch changes

- **[TR]** He converts Quintilian’s compressed notice into an explicit one-locus-per-degree explanation.
- **[DT, Romberch]** The twelve named signs provide an easily retained macro-order.
- **[TR]** When a larger celestial system is wanted, Romberch recommends the constellation figures associated with Hyginus.
- **[NEG]** The known Metrodorus passage does not establish that Romberch possessed an independent ancient source.
- **[NEG]** It does not furnish Metrodorian decan figures.
- **[YI]** Yates infers that preachers could use this celestial place order for sermons on virtues, vices, heaven, and hell. Romberch does not specify the stored material in the quoted passage.

### Hyginus and the constellations

The *Astronomica* transmitted under Hyginus describes the classical constellations and circulated with anthropomorphic and zoomorphic figures. Renaissance printed editions, especially Ratdolt’s Venetian editions of 1482 and 1485, made these pictures readily usable as mnemonic landmarks.

**[TR]** These Hyginian figures are Romberch’s extension, not evidence that Metrodorus used the same 48-constellation programme.

A surviving early twelfth-century illustrated Hyginus manuscript is Munich, Bayerische Staatsbibliothek, **Clm 10270**. [Library of Congress digital presentation](https://www.loc.gov/resource/gdcwdl.wdl_14682/?sp=9). The Ratdolt *Poeticon astronomicon* is described by the [University of Oklahoma’s Galileo collection](https://galileo.ou.edu/exhibits/astronomical-poem.html).

### Rosselli’s treatment

**[TR]** Rosselli again invokes Metrodorus for zodiacal places and treats constellations as possible place-orders.

**[DT, Rosselli]** His distinctive pedagogical device is mnemonic verse: verses stabilize sequences including the zodiac, hell, and other cosmological or theological arrangements.

**[DT]** The prefatory or accompanying verses were supplied by a Dominican associated with inquisitorial office. Yates emphasizes this as evidence that an elaborate imagistic memory art could possess an emphatically orthodox Catholic setting.

**[NEG]** Rosselli’s diagrams and plates are diagrams of Rosselli’s synthetic system, not surviving copies of a Metrodorian archetype.

### Edition variants

Only one early printed edition of Rosselli’s *Thesaurus* is generally recorded, though individual copies vary through binding, missing leaves, supplied leaves, folding-plate placement, and cataloguing. The Folger gives 145 leaves “[i.e. 147]”; other catalogues compress the physical description. These are copy and collation issues, not known doctrinal variants in the Metrodorus passage.

# Yates’s account and the scholarship since

## 1. Chapter I: the classical art

At pp. 19–20 and 23–25, Yates uses Quintilian to clarify the rules of classical places and images. She contrasts Cicero’s admiration of Metrodorus’s “almost divine” memory with Quintilian’s accusation of vanity and ostentation.

**[YI]** Yates sees the contrast as an early division between reverence for extraordinary artificial memory and suspicion that it is boastful, irrational, or excessive.

This remains a fair reading of the different evaluative tones. It does not prove a contemporary controversy specifically about astrology.

## 2. Chapter II: the reconstruction

At pp. 39–42 Yates:

- identifies Metrodorus through Strabo;
- quotes Post’s decanal reconstruction;
- joins Cicero’s “writing” metaphor to Quintilian’s phrase *imagines notae sunt*;
- proposes that shorthand *notae* might have been placed upon zodiacal or decanal images;
- cites Pliny’s verbatim-memory claim;
- concludes that Metrodorus’s art concerned memory for words and the zodiac.

Her most responsible sentence is:

> “It had to do with memory for words, possibly through memorising the *notae* or symbols of shorthand, and was connected with the zodiac. That is all we really know.”

This needs one refinement: even “memory for words” is an inference produced by joining Pliny’s verbatim-memory story to Cicero and Quintilian. Pliny does not explicitly say that the 360 loci produced the feat.

## 3. The decans and magic

Yates argues that if Metrodorus used decan figures, those figures “were certainly believed to be magical images.” She links them to Egyptian astrological imagery, temple lore, Renaissance talismanic traditions, and the decans represented in the Palazzo Schifanoia frescoes.

**[YI]** This is a genealogical hypothesis:

Metrodorus → zodiacal/decanal memory → Renaissance celestial mnemonic systems → Bruno’s magical memory.

**[DIS]** Each arrow requires qualification:

- Metrodorus → decans: Post’s reconstruction only.
- Decans → talismanic magic: true for some late antique, Arabic, and Renaissance contexts, but not all uses of decanal imagery.
- Metrodorus → Bruno: Renaissance authors used Metrodorus as an authority, but direct technical descent is unproved.
- Schifanoia → memory art: iconographic and structural analogy does not establish mnemonic use.

Yates’s fuller decan argument appears in *Giordano Bruno and the Hermetic Tradition*, pp. 45–48. Aby Warburg’s analysis of Schifanoia predates her; Yates integrates Warburg’s art history into a memory-and-Hermeticism narrative.

## 4. Chapter V: Romberch and Rosselli

At pp. 116–17 Yates accurately reports that Romberch proposes zodiacal signs as an easily memorized order and names Metrodorus. She notes the extension through Hyginus’s constellation images.

At p. 122 she describes Rosselli’s celestial place systems and mnemonic verses.

Her formulation that “every subsequent user of a celestial memory system will invoke Metrodorus” is rhetorically effective but too universal. **[DIS]** Many important celestial, astrological, cosmological, or combinatorial schemes do not name him. A narrower claim is defensible: Renaissance writers seeking a classical precedent for zodiacal mnemonic places repeatedly invoked the Quintilian notice.

## 5. Chapter IX: Bruno

At pp. 206 and 217 Yates reads Bruno’s celestial wheels and image lists through the Metrodorian genealogy. Her summary is:

> “The Metrodorian system has turned into a magical system.”

This is a statement about Bruno, not documentary evidence about Metrodorus. Bruno’s system includes planetary, zodiacal, lunar-mansion, decanal, mythological, and human-character images deployed on rotating combinatory structures. The premise of celestial mediation belongs to Bruno’s Renaissance philosophical and magical setting.

The Warburg Institute notes that later Bruno scholarship has treated Yates’s account with considerable skepticism even while recognizing its foundational importance. [Warburg Institute: “Yates and Bruno’s Mnemonics”](https://warburg.sas.ac.uk/research-fellowships/research-projects/archived-research-projects/giordano-bruno/yates-brunos-mnemonics).

## 6. Later scholarship: what stands and what changes

### Mary Carruthers

Carruthers confirms the importance of:

- ordered places;
- visual and affective images;
- inscription, storage, and treasury metaphors;
- memory as an instrument of composition and invention.

She corrects the narrowness of a history centered on spectacular architectural images and Renaissance occultism. Medieval *memoria* was a pervasive craft of reading, meditation, composition, ethics, and textual organization. Page layout, punctuation, rubrication, division, and topical structures were mnemonic technologies too.

Her most consequential correction for the present dossier is methodological: Metrodorus’s 360 loci represent one exceptional rhetorical device, not the essence of premodern memory. [Carruthers, “The Art of Memory and the Art of Page Layout”](https://doi.org/10.1177/039219210204919603).

### Lina Bolzoni

Bolzoni retains Yates’s insight that places-and-images thinking shaped Renaissance visual and literary culture but grounds the subject more closely in specific texts, reading practices, galleries, theatres, preaching, and image programmes.

For Metrodorus, this strengthens the reception history of celestial structures as repositories but supplies no new ancient evidence for decans or magic. *The Gallery of Memory* includes Rosselli among the richly illustrated mnemonic treatises. [Publisher preview](https://api.pageplace.de/preview/DT0400.9781442681330_A30532622/preview-9781442681330_A30532622.pdf).

### Paolo Rossi

Rossi’s *Clavis universalis*—translated as *Logic and the Art of Memory*—predated Yates’s book in Italian. Rossi situates memory arts within logic, encyclopedism, universal method, artificial languages, and seventeenth-century science. He complicates any straight decline from images to rational method.

For Metrodorus, Rossi’s framework encourages treating the numbered zodiac as an ordering technology rather than assuming it was an occult object.

### Jocelyn Penny Small

Small’s *Wax Tablets of the Mind* emphasizes the material and cognitive history behind metaphors of inscription, literacy, visual organization, and recall. This cautions against taking Cicero’s wax analogy as evidence that Metrodorus literally memorized stenographic characters.

### Walter J. Ong

Ong’s work on Ramus shows that Ramist dichotomies, printed diagrams, pedagogical method, and spatial display form another branch of information ordering. Later studies demonstrate that Ramism, Aristotelianism, Lullism, and imagistic memory could coexist.

Recent work has specifically rejected Yates’s broad equation of Ramist or Calvinist aniconism with rejection of imagination. Perkins objected to indecent and passion-stirring mnemonic images, but Reformed authors could endorse sacred mnemonic systems. [“William Perkins, the imagination in Calvinist theology and ‘inner iconoclasm’ after Frances Yates”](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/17496977.2021.1981695).

### Bruno scholarship

Giordano Bruno scholarship associated with Rita Sturlese, Michele Ciliberto, Hilary Gatti, Stephen Clucas, and the modern critical editions has corrected several Yatesian tendencies:

- Bruno cannot be reduced to a single coherent “Hermetic tradition.”
- His mnemonics engage Aristotle, medieval logic, Lull, Neoplatonism, astrology, rhetoric, combinatorics, and pedagogy.
- His wheels are not merely static talismanic image arrays; they operate syntactically and combinatorially.
- Celestial images may have cognitive, metaphysical, rhetorical, and magical roles at once.
- The dating, audience, and technical relations among *De umbris idearum*, *Cantus Circaeus*, *Explicatio triginta sigillorum*, and *De imaginum compositione* require work-specific analysis.

None of this disproves that Bruno could interpret celestial images magically. It does disprove using “Hermetic magic” as an exhaustive explanation.

### Camillo scholarship

Bolzoni and Alessandro Stagno have placed Giulio Camillo’s Memory Theatre within Venetian courtly, rhetorical, literary, architectural, and patronage networks. The Theatre’s planetary grades are documented from Camillo’s own *L’Idea del Theatro* and contemporary reports; they are not a surviving development of Metrodorus’s 360 degrees.

### Lull scholarship

Anthony Bonner and Alexander Fidora emphasize that Ramon Llull’s *Ars* is a logical-demonstrative and missionary system grounded in divine dignities, relational principles, and combinatorial figures. It is not originally an *ars memoriae* in the Ciceronian sense.

Yates’s tendency to place Lull, celestial memory, and Bruno in one developing stream therefore needs strong qualification. Renaissance Lullism could be mnemonicized and occultized, but Llull’s own rotating figures were intended to support discovery and demonstration, not primarily storage and recall.

### Hajdu, Rossi, Bolzoni, Carruthers, Small: collective correction

The main post-Yates correction is not a newly discovered account of Metrodorus. It is a better disciplinary map:

- rhetoric and delivery;
- dialectical topics and invention;
- manuscript/page organization;
- scholastic psychology;
- meditation and ethical formation;
- preaching;
- courtly performance;
- combinatorial logic;
- astrology and magic;
- pedagogical diagram;
- printed method.

Yates illuminated one dramatic pathway but sometimes treated analogies among these practices as genealogical descent.

# History and context

## 1. Greek rhetoric and the Academic setting

**[DT]** Cicero calls Metrodorus an Academic rhetorician. His art belongs first to rhetorical *memoria*, traditionally the fourth or fifth part of rhetoric depending on the scheme: invention, arrangement, style, memory, and delivery.

The skill had obvious practical value in:

- courtroom pleading;
- political address;
- diplomatic negotiation;
- extemporaneous response;
- teaching and declamation;
- retaining texts before convenient consultation.

**[SR]** A courtier in a multilingual Hellenistic kingdom would have had powerful incentives to cultivate ordered recall. No source, however, says Metrodorus designed the zodiacal array specifically for diplomacy or administration.

## 2. Mithridates’ court

Pliny juxtaposes Metrodorus with Mithridates, who allegedly administered twenty-two peoples in their own languages. This makes the Pontic court a literary locus of prodigious memory.

**[LEG/TR]** The linguistic feat may reflect genuine multilingual kingship, royal propaganda, or exemplary exaggeration. Pliny does not state that Metrodorus taught Mithridates his technique.

Metrodorus’s roles—rhetorician, judge, intimate adviser, ambassador—place memory inside courtly power. Exact recall could enhance authority, but it also encouraged stories of superhuman ability.

## 3. Astronomy and astrology

The zodiac offered:

- a canonical twelve-part sequence;
- a closed circle;
- thirty-degree subdivisions;
- repeated daily and annual familiarity;
- visually memorable animal and human signs;
- a numerical total of 360.

**[SR]** These are sufficient practical reasons for choosing it without invoking magic.

**[DIS]** “The signs through which the sun travels” is astronomical language compatible with astrology but not proof of astrological divination. In antiquity, astronomy, calendrics, astrology, mathematical geography, and celestial mythology overlapped institutionally and textually.

## 4. Medieval schools

There is no continuous institutional “Metrodorian school.” The artificial-memory tradition passed through Latin rhetoric, particularly the *Ad Herennium*, which medieval readers attributed to Cicero.

Dominicans and Franciscans adapted memory to:

- sermon composition;
- moral classification;
- confession;
- scriptural study;
- scholastic disputation;
- meditation.

Albertus Magnus and Thomas Aquinas integrated artificial memory into Aristotelian psychology and the virtue of prudence. Their discussions concern orderly, striking, and affectively charged representations, not a documented recovery of Metrodorus’s zodiac.

## 5. Renaissance courts, academies, and print

Humanist recovery of Quintilian supplied direct access to the 360-loci notice. Illustrated astronomical books supplied reusable celestial pictures. Printed memory manuals served preachers, lawyers, physicians, merchants, messengers, notaries, teachers, and students—audiences named on Romberch’s title page.

Thus the “revival” is bibliographical and rhetorical:

- recovery and printing of classical texts;
- printed synthesis of medieval and humanist mnemonic rules;
- appropriation of Metrodorus as antique authority;
- integration with illustrated celestial books.

It is not the recovery of a lost Metrodorian manual.

# People

## Practitioners and transmitters

### Metrodorus

Documented as rhetorician and political actor; reported by Cicero, Pliny, and Quintilian as an exceptional memorist.

### Charmadas

Frequently paired with Metrodorus by Cicero as an Academic with prodigious memory. His presence suggests a living Greek rhetorical-philosophical practice, not a solitary invention.

### Cicero

An admiring witness and transmitter. His account is especially valuable because he had traveled in Asia and heard Metrodorus.

### Quintilian

A critical transmitter. He preserves the only technical numerical detail while rejecting Metrodorus’s self-display.

### Pliny

Collector of exemplary memory stories; responsible for the influential claim that Metrodorus “perfected” Simonides’ art.

### Johannes Romberch

A Dominican compiler who transforms Quintilian’s report into a usable Renaissance place system. He explains 360 through zodiacal degrees and extends celestial loci using Hyginus.

### Cosimo Rosselli and Damiano Rosselli

Cosimo, a Dominican, composed the *Thesaurus*; Damiano edited it posthumously. The work integrates classical memory, Christian cosmology, alphabets, images, gestures, and mnemonic verse.

### Giordano Bruno

Invokes and transforms the celestial-memory tradition, but the relationship is typological and reception-historical, not a demonstrable direct transmission from Metrodorus.

### Alexander Dicson

A Bruno-associated Scottish writer. “Heius Scepsius,” the pseudonym on *De umbra rationis et iudicii*, evokes Scepsis and thereby the Metrodorian authority. The polemic shows that “Scepsian” had become a recognizable label for contentious imagistic memory.

### William Perkins—or “G. P. Cantabrigiensis”

The authorship of attacks associated with the Bruno–Dicson controversy has been disputed. Yates identified the author with Perkins; later bibliography has debated details. Perkins’s own *Arte of Prophecying* condemns animation of mnemonic images when it requires absurd, monstrous, or lust-provoking fantasies.

## Patrons and political associates

- Mithridates VI Eupator: patron and eventual enemy.
- Tigranes II of Armenia: recipient of Metrodorus’s embassy and, in Plutarch’s narrative, remorseful contributor to his fate.
- Renaissance patrons of Bruno and Camillo belong to the later celestial-memory story but are not links in a documented Metrodorian succession.

## Anecdotes and absent anecdotes

The catalogue brief requests stories such as the auctioneer, the professor’s party, the student who lost his memory, and the accused magician. None can be securely attached to Metrodorus.

- **[NEG]** No auctioneer story is found in the ancient testimonia about him.
- **[NEG]** No professor’s-party demonstration belongs to him.
- **[NEG]** No student of Metrodorus is recorded as losing his memory.
- **[NEG]** No source says he was accused of magic or tried for mnemonic practice.
- **[LEG]** Pliny’s claim of exact verbatim repetition is the chief performance anecdote.
- **[DT/TR]** Quintilian’s charge of vanity is criticism, not a report of prosecution.

# The magic question

## Was Metrodorus’s system magical?

The answer must be divided chronologically.

### In surviving ancient evidence

**[NEG]** No.

Cicero calls the memory nearly divine, but *divina prope memoria* is praise for extraordinary ability, not a technical allegation of sorcery.

Quintilian calls it vanity and boasting, not magic.

Pliny places it among marvels of human memory, not ritual arts.

Strabo and Plutarch describe political danger, not heresy or occult prosecution.

No source mentions:

- invocation of planetary spirits;
- talismanic fabrication;
- astrological election of a time;
- ritual purity;
- prayer;
- demons;
- divination;
- theurgy;
- Chaldaean rites;
- ecclesiastical condemnation.

### Yates’s conditional argument

**[YI]** If Post’s decan reconstruction is correct, Yates argues, Metrodorus used images that later traditions regarded as magical.

The conditional is crucial. Three different claims are often collapsed:

1. Metrodorus used the zodiac. **Documented.**
2. Metrodorus used decan figures. **Speculative.**
3. He used them as efficacious talismans. **Unsupported.**

A picture can function as an astronomical label, mythographic figure, mnemonic marker, astrological significator, or talisman. One function does not prove another.

### Philostratus’s accused sophist

Yates notes that Dionysius of Miletus, a sophist of Hadrian’s era, was accused of teaching pupils through “Chaldaean arts.” Philostratus rejects the allegation.

**[TR]** This shows that extraordinary mnemonic or pedagogical performance could generate rumors of occult assistance.

**[NEG]** It does not show that Metrodorus was accused, or that zodiacal loci were inherently magical.

### Romberch and Rosselli

Both were Dominicans. Rosselli’s work appeared with Dominican and inquisitorial endorsements. Their celestial places therefore cannot be classified simply as clandestine or heretical.

**[DT]** They present celestial orders inside Christian pedagogical works.

**[NEG]** No known inquisitorial proceeding arose from their invocation of Metrodorus.

### Bruno

With Bruno the magic question changes.

**[DT]** Bruno explicitly works with astral images, cosmic correspondences, and powers of imagination in a philosophical world where “magic,” natural causation, cognition, and metaphysics overlap.

**[YI, partly confirmed]** Yates is right that Bruno can transform a classical ordering device into something more ontologically ambitious.

**[DIS]** Whether every Brunian wheel is a magical talisman, and whether the operator expected literal astral influx, must be decided work by work. Later Bruno scholarship rejects a single undifferentiated “Hermetic magic” explanation.

### The *Ars notoria*

The *Ars notoria* promises rapid or infused acquisition of knowledge through prayers, ritual observances, and contemplation of complex *notae*.

Yates contrasts this with Bruno’s laborious mnemonic magic.

**[NEG]** There is no historical evidence that Metrodorus practiced the *Ars notoria*, which is medieval and Christian in its surviving forms.

**[DIS]** Cicero’s writing metaphor and Quintilian’s word *notae* do not link Metrodorus to the medieval ritual art. The shared vocabulary of signs is insufficient.

# Afterlife

## 1. Renaissance memory manuals

The clearest afterlife is the name “Metrodorus” as classical authorization for nonarchitectural, celestial loci.

Romberch establishes the model:

- twelve signs as major places;
- 360 degrees as total loci;
- Hyginian constellations for expansion.

Rosselli repeats and Christianizes the broader celestial-place repertoire.

## 2. Camillo

Camillo’s Memory Theatre arranges universal knowledge in seven rising grades governed by planetary and mythological imagery.

**[SR]** Both systems replace an ordinary building with a cosmic structure.

**[NEG]** No surviving text says Camillo derived his seven-grade architecture from Metrodorus’s 360 loci. The lineage is thematic, not documented.

## 3. Bruno

Bruno is the most elaborate “descendant” in Yates’s narrative:

- zodiacal and planetary imagery;
- decans and lunar mansions;
- lists of mythological agents;
- concentric wheels;
- mobile combination;
- memory as reformation of psychic and cosmic order.

**[TR]** Renaissance polemic made “Scepsian” a label for this celestial-memory tendency.

**[DIS]** Bruno’s technical sources include Llull, astrology, medieval logic, classical rhetoric, Ficinian and Neoplatonic materials, and his own inventions. Calling the whole system “Metrodorian” exaggerates one classical precedent.

## 4. Riccio, Dicson, Perkins, Fludd, Bacon

### Lodovico Riccio

Yates associates bodily and zodiacal orders in Renaissance memory manuals with the rationalizing continuation of celestial place systems.

### Alexander Dicson

The pseudonym “Scepsius” makes the reception explicit: Metrodorus’s place-name becomes a badge within the Ramist–Brunian controversy.

### Perkins

Opposes emotionally inflaming mnemonic imagery, not specifically the ancient 360-place array.

### Robert Fludd

Fludd’s cosmic theatres and zodiacal structures belong to an encyclopedic, astrological, theatrical, and mnemonic synthesis. Their relation to Metrodorus is retrospective classification.

### Francis Bacon

Bacon criticizes ostentatious feats and sterile memory arts. “Metrodorian” can thus become shorthand for prodigious but intellectually unproductive retention.

## 5. Literature and visual culture

### Dante

Dante’s ordered realms, vivid punishments, and spatially sequenced encounters are highly compatible with medieval mnemonic practice.

**[SR/DIS]** Carruthers and others illuminate Dante through memorial culture, but no evidence makes the *Commedia* a Metrodorian zodiacal system.

### Frescoes

The Palazzo Schifanoia frescoes at Ferrara contain zodiacal signs, decans, planetary gods, and courtly scenes.

**[DT]** Their astrological iconography is well documented.

**[YI/DIS]** Yates uses them to visualize the decan images she supposes Metrodorus may have used. That makes Schifanoia a Renaissance analogue, not evidence for his apparatus.

### Emblem books

Emblematic combinations of motto, image, and explanatory text exemplify the early modern conviction that striking images can store and stimulate discourse. The relationship is cultural and functional, not direct descent.

### Borges’s Funes

Funes possesses overwhelming, indiscriminate memory and cannot generalize. He is almost the inverse of artificial memory, whose places impose selection and order.

**[MI]** Calling Funes “Metrodorian” is a modern analogy; Borges does not reconstruct the zodiacal system.

### Umberto Eco

Eco’s interest in encyclopedias, signs, universal languages, Llull, and combinatorics makes him part of the intellectual afterlife of ordered symbolic systems. Again, the relation to Metrodorus is conceptual, not genealogical.

## 6. Universal language, combinatorics, and computing

A frequently repeated grand genealogy runs:

Metrodorus → Llull → Bruno → Leibniz → symbolic logic → computing.

It needs disassembly.

- **Metrodorus to Llull:** **[NEG]** no documented transmission.
- **Llull to Bruno:** **[DT/TR]** extensive engagement, though Bruno transforms Llull.
- **Llull to Leibniz:** **[DT/TR]** Leibniz knew and evaluated Lullian combinatorial traditions.
- **Leibniz to symbolic logic/computation:** **[SR]** important conceptual ancestry through the *characteristica universalis* and *calculus ratiocinator*.
- **Metrodorus to computers:** **[MI]** a metaphorical lineage only.

The interesting computing analogy is structural: a 360-place array resembles an addressed memory space. Post’s calculation model suggests random access by sign, decan, and local offset. But there is no evidence Metrodorus conceived addresses, registers, algorithms, or mechanical calculation in the modern sense.

## 7. Modern memory palaces and memory athletes

Modern competitors commonly use:

- journeys and loci;
- person–action–object encodings;
- number-shape or phonetic systems;
- large prelearned image vocabularies;
- grids and multi-level addressing.

A 360-place zodiac can be reconstructed and used today.

**[MI]** Such a modern implementation would demonstrate feasibility, not historical accuracy. Modern “Sabian symbols” for individual zodiacal degrees are nineteenth- and twentieth-century occult creations and must not be retrojected onto Metrodorus. The LacusCurtius note properly warns against that confusion. [Quintilian commentary](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Quintilian/Institutio_Oratoria/11b%2A.html).

# Controversies and disputes

## 1. Did Metrodorus invent a zodiacal art?

**Evidence:** Quintilian says he found 360 loci in the twelve signs.

**Verdict:** He used or claimed a distinctive arrangement; “invented the zodiacal art” is stronger than the text warrants.

## 2. Were there thirty places per sign?

**Evidence:** 360 divided by twelve; ancient zodiacal signs conventionally contain thirty degrees.

**Verdict:** Strong reconstruction, not explicit testimony.

## 3. Were the places individual degrees?

**Evidence:** Romberch’s sixteenth-century explanation and the mathematical structure of the zodiac.

**Verdict:** Plausible but not proven for Metrodorus.

## 4. Did he use thirty-six decan figures?

**First influential formulation:** L. A. Post, 1932.

**Spread by:** Yates, 1966; subsequently repeated in encyclopedias, popular memory histories, occult manuals, and web summaries.

**Evidence:** Compatibility with the number 360 and ancient astrological subdivision.

**Counter-evidence:** Total absence from Cicero, Quintilian, Pliny, and surviving Metrodorian fragments.

**Verdict:** Speculative.

## 5. Did each decan contain ten loci?

**First influential formulation:** Post.

**Verdict:** A useful modern model, but a second-order hypothesis resting on the first-order decan hypothesis.

## 6. Did Metrodorus use shorthand *notae*?

Yates argues that Quintilian’s statement “images are *notae* by which we mark things” and Cicero’s wax-writing metaphor may imply inner shorthand.

But *nota* can mean mark, sign, token, or mnemonic indicator without denoting a standardized stenographic script.

**Verdict:** Possible but weakly evidenced. It should not be stated as fact.

## 7. Did he memorize words rather than things?

Pliny says he could repeat heard material in the same words. The *Ad Herennium* distinguishes memory for things and memory for words.

**Verdict:** Exact verbal memory is attested as a feat; the method used to accomplish it is not.

## 8. Was he an astrologer?

Quintilian’s zodiac implies familiarity with celestial divisions.

**NEG:** No ancient source calls him an astrologer.

**Verdict:** Possible, not documented.

## 9. Was the art magical?

No ancient accusation survives.

**Verdict:** The ancient system is not demonstrably magical. Magic enters securely only in some later celestial-memory appropriations.

## 10. Are the decan images “certainly” magical?

Some decan figures occur in astrological, talismanic, ritual, and magical texts. Others function as calendar, astronomical, iconographical, or prognostic markers.

**Verdict:** Their status depends on context. Even if Metrodorus used them, mnemonic employment would not automatically prove talismanic intent.

## 11. Did Romberch preserve an ancient tradition independent of Quintilian?

Romberch closely follows the classical notice and adds the standard 360-degree explanation.

**Verdict:** No evidence of an independent Metrodorian source.

## 12. Did Rosselli preserve independent material?

The surviving treatment belongs to Renaissance compilation and reception.

**Verdict:** No identified independent ancient source.

## 13. Is every later celestial memory “Metrodorian”?

This is Yates’s expansive rhetorical claim.

**Verdict:** Metrodorus becomes an often-cited classical precedent, but “every” is unsustainable, and formal resemblance does not prove descent.

## 14. Was Metrodorus the *Misoromaios*?

Pliny and Ovid attest an anti-Roman Scepsian Metrodorus; Strabo and Plutarch attest a leading Mithridatic intellectual and diplomat.

**Verdict:** Probably the same man, but the identification remains prosopographically uncertain.

## 15. Was he executed by Mithridates?

Strabo gives royal order and natural illness as rival accounts. Plutarch supplies a narrative of betrayal, disclosure, and remorse.

**Verdict:** Ancient dispute; murder is not certain.

## 16. Did Yates “get it wrong”?

A differentiated answer is necessary.

### Confirmed or durable

- Cicero and Quintilian place Metrodorus within artificial memory.
- The zodiac supplied a nonarchitectural order of places.
- Renaissance mnemonists explicitly invoked him.
- Bruno’s celestial mnemonic structures could have magical and transformative ambitions.
- Artificial memory mattered far beyond rote recall.

### Overextended

- Post’s decans sometimes become quasi-factual in the momentum of Yates’s narrative.
- The ancient zodiacal notice becomes the root of later occult celestial systems without sufficient transmission evidence.
- “Every subsequent user” is too absolute.
- Ramism, Protestantism, iconoclasm, and hostility to imagination are aligned too neatly.
- “Hermeticism” can become an overly unified explanatory agent.

### Not actually claimed without qualification

Yates herself repeatedly says “if Post is right” and ends with “that is all we really know.” Many popular summaries are more dogmatic than Yates.

# Open questions

1. Did Quintilian know Metrodorus through Cicero, another rhetorical handbook, oral school tradition, or a now-lost work?
2. Does *invenerit locos* describe an actual diagram, a memorized verbal sequence, or merely an alleged feat?
3. Were the 360 loci one per degree?
4. If degree-loci were used, how were adjacent degrees made perceptually distinct?
5. Were decans, bounds, dodecatemoria, fixed stars, or mythological figures used as intermediate landmarks?
6. Did Metrodorus’s system support sequential recall only, or direct access by number?
7. Was Pliny’s exact verbal repetition achieved through this same array?
8. Did Metrodorus write any *techne mnemonike*, rhetorical handbook, or astrological work?
9. Which surviving fragments assigned to the historian, geographer, Academic, and anti-Roman polemicist belong to the same man?
10. Does any unedited Renaissance mnemonic manuscript preserve a variant of the Romberch–Rosselli celestial scheme?
11. What precise verses does each surviving Rosselli copy give for the zodiac, and do copy-specific states or corrections exist?
12. Did Renaissance readers actually practice the 360-degree arrangement, or was it chiefly a learned citation?
13. Can marginal annotations in surviving Romberch and Rosselli copies document use?
14. When did Post first connect Metrodorus specifically with decan figures, and did he draw on an earlier German, French, or Italian conjecture?
15. Can reception study identify the exact point at which Post’s “probably” became an apparent fact in reference literature?
16. How should astronomical, astrological, mnemonic, and magical uses of the same image be distinguished in individual Renaissance works?

The chief finding is therefore an absence: the surviving ancient evidence gives a striking numerical framework but almost none of its implementation. The fullest honest reconstruction is necessarily layered. A twelve-by-thirty zodiacal place system is likely; a thirty-six-by-ten decanal hierarchy is possible; shorthand and magical activation remain conjectural.
