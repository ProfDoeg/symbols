# Rossellius's *Thesaurus artificiosae memoriae*: Research Dossier

## Basic identification

**Name:** *Thesaurus artificiosae memoriae*  
**Exponent:** Fra Cosma Rosselli, O.P. (Latinized *Cosmas Rossellius*), a Florentine Dominican  
**Date:** Written before Rosselli’s death on 26 July 1575; published posthumously in Venice in 1579  
**Source text:** *Thesaurus artificiosae memoriae … Authore R.P.F. Cosma Rossellio Florentino, sacri Ordinis Praedicatorum*, Venice, apud Antonium Paduanium [Antonio Padovani], 1579  
**Principle in one sentence:** Rosselli makes the ordered Christian cosmos—from Hell through the elements and celestial spheres to the Empyrean—an enormous reusable hierarchy of mnemonic places, supplemented by actual buildings, workshops, human bodies, animals, trees, alphabets, gestures, exemplary persons, and vivid images attached and retrieved through an orderly mental itinerary.

**Yates:** *The Art of Memory*, ch. V, pp. 121–23 (especially 122–23); plate 7a–b; ch. VII, pp. 163–64; also pp. 199, 209, 223, 256, 267. In the available Warburg scan, the relevant printed pages correspond to PDF pp. 73 and 97. [Warburg Institute scan of Yates](https://resources.warburg.sas.ac.uk/mnemosyne/fq/yates_memory.pdf)

### Evidentiary key

The following labels are used throughout:

- **[T] Documented text:** stated or visibly represented in Rosselli’s 1579 book.
- **[D] Documentary record:** early archival or bibliographical evidence independent of the treatise.
- **[R] Scholarly reconstruction:** an argument made by a named modern scholar.
- **[Y] Yates’s interpretation:** Frances Yates’s historical or iconographic reading.
- **[Tr] Tradition:** a claim transmitted by ancient or early-modern authorities without independent verification.
- **[Disputed]:** evidence or attribution is contested.
- **[Legend]:** an anecdote or genealogy lacking contemporary corroboration.
- **[Modern]:** a later analogy, adaptation, or invention that should not be attributed to Rosselli.

A central finding of this investigation is negative: despite the richness of Rosselli’s printed text, little biographical documentation survives, no authorial manuscript of the *Thesaurus* has been securely identified, and no evidence found connects Rosselli personally with magical practice, prosecution, or the Inquisition except through the thoroughly orthodox Dominican inquisitor who supplied mnemonic verses.

## The system in detail

## 1. What kind of book it is

### Bibliographical form

**[T/D]** The 1579 edition is a quarto of sixteen unnumbered preliminary pages, 145 numbered leaves plus an errata leaf. The ETH record describes twenty-six woodcuts and three folding plates; another detailed collation counts twenty-five full-page woodcuts or diagrams, one half-page diagram, and three plates. The apparent discrepancy results from counting sheets, impressions, and repeated or inserted plates differently. The volume’s own foliation is also irregular. [ETH e-rara bibliographical record](https://www.e-rara.ch/zut/content/titleinfo/4198306); [Folger catalogue record](https://catalog.folger.edu/record/381992)

The Folger collation is especially important:

> `[16], 145 [i.e. 147], [1] leaves; a–d⁴, A–R⁴ (plus two leaves, the second signed R3, bound after R3), S–2M⁴, 2N⁶.`

Its copy lacks some gatherings or leaves supplied by photostats from a Yale copy. Thus references such as “p. 33 recto” in Yates should properly be read as **folio 33r**, not page 33.

### Macrostructure

**[T]** The work is divided into two principal treatises:

1. **First part: places** (*de locis*), fols. 1r–77v.
2. **Second part: figures/images** (*de figuris*), fols. 78r–145r.

The first part proceeds from the largest possible locations—the cosmic regions—to successively smaller places:

- *loca amplissima*: Hell, the elementary world, celestial spheres, Empyrean/Paradise;
- *loca ampliora*;
- *loca communia amplissima*;
- *loca communia mediocria*;
- *loca communia minora*: workshops and professions;
- *loca communia minima*: persons, animals, and trees;
- practical cautions and conditions governing places.

The second part classifies figures as:

- natural;
- terrestrial and inanimate;
- vegetal;
- animal;
- aquatic, aerial, and fiery;
- celestial and supercelestial;
- artificial;
- alphabetic, numerical, gestural, or otherwise conventional;
- figures applied through direct likeness, association, linguistic resemblance, initial letters, rebus, narrative, or exemplary persons.

The printed table of contents can be followed leaf by leaf in the [ETH structured digitization](https://www.e-rara.ch/zut/content/structure/4198306).

## 2. The fundamental mechanism

### Places and figures

**[T]** Rosselli defines and subdivides *loci* before treating *figurae*. His terminology for images is unusually expansive:

> “*Figurae … multiplici appellatione ab huius artis peritis denominantur. Dicuntur enim etiam imagines, simulacra et idola; signa quoque, similitudines ac species; notae etiam vocitantur et memorandarum umbrae.*”  
> —*Thesaurus*, II.1, fol. 78r.

Gloss: “Figures are designated by many names by experts in this art: they are called images, likenesses and idols; also signs, similitudes and species; they are likewise called marks and shadows of things to be remembered.”

This passage is discussed by Lina Bolzoni in *I segni della memoria*. Its importance is semiotic: Rosselli does not restrict the mnemonic image to a literal picture. It may be a likeness, conventional sign, species, notation, or “shadow.”

**[T]** He explicitly permits a thing to function first as a place and then as an image:

> “*Nec mireris, quod eaedem res, quae pro locis serviunt in uno et eodem discursu minime alterato, ac in eodem tempore, etiam pro figuris deserviant. In consimilibus enim discursibus easdem res et figuras esse et loca non dedecet.*”  
> —Ibid., fol. 78r.

Gloss: “Do not be surprised that the same things which serve as places in one unchanged course, even at the same time, also serve as figures; in such courses it is not improper for the same things to be both figures and places.”

**[R]** Koji Kuwakino identifies this as a distinctive “interpenetration” or interchangeability of *loci* and *imagines agentes*. A person, animal, tree, constellation, or infernal circle can be a stable receptacle at one level and an active sign at another. [Kuwakino, “The ars memorativa and ecfrasis,” pp. 156–57](https://gal-studies.museogalileo.it/index.php/galilaeana/article/download/57/41)

### Fixing order

**[T]** Order is imposed hierarchically and itinerantly:

1. choose a stable domain;
2. divide it into major regions;
3. subdivide each region into numbered or otherwise unmistakable positions;
4. assign figures to those positions;
5. rehearse the route repeatedly;
6. retrieve by mentally traversing the same route.

The book offers several independent ordering systems:

- theological descent and ascent: Hell → elements → planets and spheres → Empyrean;
- concentric and radial order;
- architectural order through churches, convents, houses, rooms, doors, corners, altars, and furnishings;
- bodily order from head through limbs and digits;
- alphabetical order;
- lists of professions, plants, animals, philosophers, physicians, poets, wealthy men, and heroes;
- zodiacal and planetary order;
- verse as an auxiliary serial index.

### Retrieval

**[T/R]** Rosselli says surprisingly little about the moment of retrieval compared with the enormous labor he devotes to constructing places. The implied operation is classical: begin at the established starting point, traverse the sequence, and “repossess” what was deposited in each place.

**[R]** Kuwakino stresses this imbalance. Rosselli gives minute directions for building the mnemonic world but few extended demonstrations of a complete act of encoding and recall. That is not evidence that the system lacked a retrieval procedure; it means the procedure was assumed from the familiar Ciceronian tradition.

### Appropriate matching

**[T]** Places are not semantically indifferent. At fol. 51v Rosselli cautions against putting incongruous material into a domain—for example, the names of angels should not be assigned to Hell. Congruence reinforces recall: infernal material belongs in infernal places, celestial material in celestial places, trades in workshops, and sciences with their celebrated practitioners.

## 3. Rules for places

Rosselli’s prescriptions should be distinguished from those inherited from the *Ad Herennium*, Cicero, Quintilian, Peter of Ravenna, and Romberch.

### Hierarchical scale

**[T]** Rosselli distinguishes places by capacity:

- “very extensive” or world-sized places;
- large places;
- common places of several sizes;
- minimal places.

A minimal place is not simply a tiny patch of architecture. It may be a person, animal, or tree whose parts are themselves separately usable.

### Visibility and differentiation

**[T/Tr]** In agreement with the classical tradition, places must be distinct, clearly bounded, neither confused nor monotonously alike, and mentally visible. Repetition or insufficient differentiation causes one location to be mistaken for another.

### Moderate spacing

**[T/Tr]** The places should be separated by usable intervals. This descends from the *Ad Herennium*’s requirement that backgrounds be neither excessively close nor excessively far apart.

### Stability

**[T]** The system works only when its order is fixed before transient content is deposited in it. Cosmic, architectural, anatomical, zodiacal, and alphabetical structures are valuable because their internal relations are already stable.

### Actual buildings

**[T]** Rosselli permits or recommends places drawn from monasteries, abbeys, churches, houses, and similar familiar buildings. These are not merely metaphors: the practitioner is to learn their sequence so thoroughly that the mind can travel through them without hesitation.

This aspect places him firmly in the practical Dominican tradition. A preacher could use a familiar church or convent as a reusable matrix for sermon divisions, authorities, examples, objections, and conclusions.

### Imaginary places

**[T]** Rosselli also accepts deliberately fabricated places. The cosmological system is an extreme example: Hell and Paradise are made visually precise enough to be used like a known building.

At fol. 6r the reader is told, in effect:

> “*te oportet confingere*”

Gloss: “you must fashion [it] in imagination.”

**[R]** Kuwakino reads Rosselli’s elaborate descriptive method as “technical ekphrasis”: the prose gives construction instructions for a mental architecture, not simply literary ornament.

### Emptying and reuse

**[T]** Rosselli discusses deliberate forgetting or clearing of places. Bolzoni quotes his striking instruction: imagine someone returning home, casting the images onto the floor, then expelling or throwing them from the window.

This is an *ars oblivionalis* ancillary to the art of memory: the permanent place remains while temporary occupants are violently removed. It is neither iconoclasm in a doctrinal sense nor evidence of mental illness; it is a vivid erasure operation.

## 4. Rules for images

### Familiarity

**[T]** In the preface to the second book Rosselli limits himself to useful, recognizable figures:

> “*Primo enim figuras, non omnes, sed notiores nobis et familiariores, quae nostrae memoriae prodesse possint, ponere volumus.*”  
> —fol. 77v/78r vicinity; cited in scholarship as fol. 77r in some descriptions.

Gloss: “First, we wish to set down figures—not all of them, but those better known and more familiar to us—which can assist our memory.”

Foliation should be checked against the individual copy because inserted plates and misnumbering produce slight citation differences.

### Sensory force

**[T]** Images should be visible to the “eyes of the psyche,” affectively forceful, and capable of exciting the memory. Rosselli’s preferred language includes art, practice, imagination, motion, delight, horror, and astonishment.

### Congruity and association

**[T]** A figure may encode a thing through:

- direct resemblance;
- a characteristic instrument;
- profession or habitual action;
- a famous practitioner;
- first letter;
- sound resemblance;
- semantic association;
- emblematic or mythological convention;
- narrative episode;
- bodily gesture;
- written label;
- an accumulation of several of these.

### Variety and action

**[T/Tr]** Variety of posture, punishment, expression, gesture, clothing, and action increases discriminability. This is the direct heir of the *Ad Herennium*’s *imagines agentes*: active, striking images designed to “hit” the mind.

### The printed picture is a prompt, not the completed mental image

**[T/R]** Rosselli repeatedly asks the reader to add what cannot be fully represented by a crude woodcut—sound, smell, smoke, motion, cold, burning, demons, wounds, gestures, and the intensity of punishment. The physical plate scaffolds a richer internal spectacle.

## 5. Hell as artificial memory

### Structural position

**[T]** Hell is the first of the *loca communia amplissima*, the enormous common places through which Rosselli’s mnemonic cosmos begins. Its treatment proceeds through five stages:

1. general structure, fols. 2v–6r;
2. explanation of the usefulness of printed pictures, fol. 7r–v;
3. theological and sensory elaboration, fols. 7v–11v;
4. the woodcut, fol. 12r;
5. mnemonic verses by Nicolaus Alexius, fols. 13r–15r.

This five-part structure was reconstructed in detail by Kuwakino. [Kuwakino 2024, pp. 156–63](https://gal-studies.museogalileo.it/index.php/galilaeana/article/download/57/41)

### Eleven regions

**[T]** The infernal plan consists of a central fiery well and eleven surrounding subdivisions:

- four concentric, stair-like rings nearest the well:
  1. heretics;
  2. Jewish “infidels”;
  3. idolaters;
  4. hypocrites;
- seven outer sectors corresponding to the seven capital sins.

The seven labels visible in the plate include forms corresponding to pride, sloth, lust, envy, gluttony, avarice, and vainglory. Orthography is compressed in the engraving.

**Correction to oversimplified catalogue accounts:** “Hell is divided into eleven places” is true at the level of its principal *gironi*, but the system is recursively subdivisible. The damned, demons, walls, stairs, punishments, gestures, boat, river, cave, and body parts can all yield subsidiary places. Eleven is not the total storage capacity.

### Exact architectural instructions

**[T]** Rosselli locates the well in the center and describes outward construction. The four inner stone circuits are each approximately three or four *braccia* wide and about two *braccia* high. Walls separating the seven outer areas are assigned widths and heights in *braccia* or *palmae*; the outer circumferential wall is approximately three *palmae* high.

The prose uses exhortative subjunctives and first-person plurals—“let there be,” “let them be imagined,” “let them be placed,” “we add”—as though author and reader were building the structure together.

### Inhabitants and actions

**[T]**

- Heretics tear or mutilate Scripture.
- Jews are represented with iron faces, veiled eyes, and bent necks.
- Idolaters appear with ruined idols.
- Hypocrites occupy the fourth step.
- Lucifer emerges in the fiery torrent from the well.
- Demons act as guards and tormentors.
- The seven outer areas contain sinners punished according to the capital vice assigned to each.

These representations are documents of sixteenth-century Catholic polemic and anti-Jewish iconography, not neutral ethnography. Reporting them does not validate their theology or prejudice.

### Sensory completion

**[T]** Hell is the *carcer apostatarum spirituum*, “prison of apostate spirits.” Rosselli directs the reader to amplify the woodcut mentally with:

- thick smoke;
- red-hot fire;
- gnawing worms;
- screams and hideous noises;
- putrid stench;
- intolerable cold;
- demonic violence;
- punishments fitted to particular sins.

He writes:

> “*Nam cogita si potes omnia atrocissima tormenta, asperrimasque poenas a mundo condito a diversis tyrannis hominibus inflictas … nec tamen parem ullam invenies, quae etiam minimae inferni poenae vel sensus, vel damni coaequari possit.*”  
> —fol. 8v.

Gloss: “Think, if you can, of all the most atrocious torments and harshest punishments inflicted on human beings by tyrants since the creation of the world … yet you will find none equal to even the least punishment of Hell, whether of sense or of loss.”

And:

> “*Idcirco qualibet hac in pictura data poena, damnatis quaedam singulis, sed longe maior erit excogitanda.*”  
> —fol. 8v.

Gloss: “Therefore, whatever punishment is assigned to each of the damned in this picture, one far greater must be imagined.”

Yates’s famous paraphrase derives from the opening description:

> “the variety of punishments, inflicted in accordance with the diverse nature of the sins, the different situations of the damned, their varying gestures, will much help memory and give many places.”

Her adverb “cheerfully” is interpretive rather than textual.

### Peripheral features

**[T]** A river or lake surrounds the infernal region. A boat driven by demons carries souls to their places of punishment. A small marked opening, *peccatores caverna*, signals a tunnel through which souls descend; Rosselli admits that the subterranean route cannot itself be fully painted:

> “*attamen expostulabat ratio, ut illorum infelicium animarum ad Infernum descensus, qui pingi non poterat, hoc signo inspectoribus declaretur.*”  
> —fols. 11r–v.

Gloss: “Reason nevertheless required that the descent of those unhappy souls into Hell, which could not be painted, should be indicated to viewers by this sign.”

### Moral use

**[T]** The section closes with an address to the living viewer:

> “*Quaeso vos omnes lectores inspectoresque viventes: ad inferni profunda, cogitatu vestro descendite, poenasque universas perpendite …*”  
> —fol. 11v.

Gloss: “I beg all you living readers and viewers: descend in your thought to the depths of Hell, and weigh all its punishments …”

Thus the map has a double function:

- mnemonic storage;
- devotional and penitential admonition.

### Plate 7a / fol. 12r

**[T]** The woodcut reproduced by Yates as plate 7a is a roughly circular, diagrammatic Hell:

- a dark central well with Lucifer and flames;
- four concentric ring-steps carrying the four doctrinal categories;
- an outer annulus divided radially into seven vice-sectors;
- a surrounding river;
- a demon-driven boat;
- a small lateral cave or tunnel marker;
- labels printed directly into the architecture.

**[R]** Kuwakino emphasizes that Lucifer is depicted only schematically; the prose orders the practitioner to transform that crude mark into a smoky, flaming, active prince of demons.

## 6. The elementary and celestial universe

Hell is not an isolated “memory palace.” It begins an ascent through the whole created universe.

### Elementary world

**[T]** The diagram following the infernal material arranges the sublunary world in curved, amphitheatrical bands. It moves through regions of earth, water, air, and fire, with distinctions of purity, mixture, and position. The lowest band lies immediately above or around Hell.

### Celestial spheres

**[T]** Rosselli then supplies the ordered celestial spheres and constellations as places. The zodiac provides twelve familiar divisions; individual constellations and their parts can support further loci.

### Metrodorus of Scepsis

**[T/Tr]** Rosselli cites Metrodorus in association with a zodiacal system.

**[Tr/Disputed]** The claim descends through ancient testimony—especially Quintilian, *Institutio oratoria* XI.2.22—that Metrodorus found 360 places in the twelve signs through which the sun travels. Quintilian himself calls the achievement vanity and ostentation. No work by Metrodorus explaining the system survives; neither Rosselli nor modern historians can reconstruct its exact operation from primary Greek evidence.

The relevant Quintilian passage reads:

> “*Quo magis miror, quomodo Metrodorus in XII signis per quae sol meat trecenos et sexagenos invenerit locos. Vanitas nimirum fuit atque iactatio …*”

Gloss: “I therefore wonder all the more how Metrodorus found three hundred and sixty places in the twelve signs through which the sun travels. It was surely vanity and boasting.” [Perseus, Quintilian XI.2](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A2007.01.0068%3Abook%3D11%3Achapter%3D2)

Rosselli’s constellation places are documented; their direct descent from an actual Metrodoran technique is traditional, not demonstrable.

## 7. Paradise as artificial memory

### Location and sequence

**[T]** Paradise is introduced across fols. 29v–37r; its first large plate occurs at fol. 37v. Additional explanatory material runs through approximately fol. 51r, where the scheme appears again.

### Architecture

**[T]** The Empyrean is imagined as a circular or amphitheatrical celestial enclosure surrounded by a wall glittering with precious stones. Its center is the throne of Christ. Below and around it are ranked:

- the highest angelic orders;
- other angelic hierarchies;
- Apostles;
- Patriarchs;
- Prophets;
- Martyrs;
- Confessors;
- Virgins;
- Holy Hebrews;
- Innocents and Virtues in inner ranks;
- the innumerable multitude of saints.

The precise order is distributed between the diagram and the accompanying prose; some compressed labels are not fully intelligible without the explanation.

### Constructing the inhabitants

**[T]** The practitioner must form the places through art, rehearsal, and intense imagination; Christ’s throne should be envisioned so as to move the senses and excite memory. The spirits may be pictured:

> “as painters paint them.”

This direction is crucial. Rosselli does not demand an esoterically revealed angelic form. Conventional Catholic visual culture supplies serviceable images.

### Plate 7b / fols. 37v and c. 51r

**[T]** Yates’s plate 7b shows:

- a gem-walled enclosing circle;
- Christ’s throne at the center or apex of the inward hierarchy;
- concentric or stepped ranks;
- angelic labels in the inner celestial region;
- classes of saints distributed in outer sectors;
- compressed inscriptions such as *Apostoli*, *Patriarchae*, *Prophetae*, *Martyres*, *Confessores*, and *Virgines*.

**[Y]** Yates remarks:

> “There is nothing at all unusual about Rosellius’s Paradise, except that it is classed as ‘artificial memory’.”

That sentence understates the technical novelty. The iconography is conventional, but its reclassification as a reusable, measured hierarchy of mnemonic locations is precisely what makes it unusual as a cognitive instrument.

## 8. The mnemonic verses

### Nicolaus Alexius of Perugia

**[T/D]** The verses are attributed to:

> “*R.P.M. Nicolai Alexii Perusini Ordinis Praedicatorum, commissarii S. Inquisitionis*.”

That is, Reverend Father Master Nicolaus Alexius of Perugia, Dominican, commissioner of the Holy Inquisition.

The poems begin at:

- fol. 13r: Hell;
- later preliminary leaves: elements;
- fol. 27v: the heavens.

They turn spatial order into metrical/verbal order. Verse and diagram provide parallel retrieval paths: if the image-sequence falters, rhythm and verbal sequence can restore it, and vice versa.

### Orthodoxy

**[Y]** Yates writes:

> “These ‘carmina’ by an Inquisitor give an impressive air of great orthodoxy to the artificial memory.”

This is fair as an observation about presentation but remains interpretation. Alexius’s office does not by itself constitute formal ecclesiastical approval, an imprimatur, or a judgment on every mnemonic technique in the book.

**[D]** The convent chronicle says that “Master Nicolaus Alegius” had rendered Rosselli’s work into “most beautiful verses.” This independently confirms his collaboration.

## 9. Actual places: churches, convents, and houses

**[T]** Rosselli recommends familiar religious and domestic structures. A practitioner may mentally walk:

- into a church;
- past successive altars and chapels;
- around corners;
- through choir, sacristy, cloister, refectory, dormitory, or cells;
- through a house from vestibule into rooms;
- along known roads or urban circuits.

**[R]** The religious examples are institutionally significant. Rosselli had been a subprior, novice master, and confessor to nuns; churches and convents were not abstract examples but the spaces of his professional life.

**Absence of evidence:** no surviving record has been identified showing which particular convent Rosselli used as his personal mnemonic route.

## 10. Humans, animals, and trees as places

### Human bodies

**[T]** Rosselli treats the human figure as a *locus minimus* whose parts support subsidiary images. A stable traversal can proceed through head, forehead, eyes, ears, mouth, neck, shoulders, arms, hands, fingers, chest, abdomen, thighs, knees, legs, and feet.

This permits recursive storage: “a man” occupies one location in a larger building, while the man’s body itself provides many smaller locations.

### Alphabetical persons

**[T]** Lists include:

- philosophers;
- learned men;
- physicians;
- poets;
- men renowned for bodily strength;
- wealthy persons.

They are organized alphabetically and can function as places or figures.

### Animals and trees

**[T]** Animals and trees are likewise alphabetized. Their visible parts, attributes, names, habitats, and actions permit them to operate either as repositories or signs.

**[R]** This apparatus is encyclopedic but not yet a “universal language” in the seventeenth-century sense. It supplies a conventional image lexicon; it does not provide a logically exhaustive taxonomy comparable to Dalgarno or Wilkins.

---

## 11. Workshops and professions

**[T]** Rosselli gives alphabetized workshops or trades as intermediate-scale places. A workshop is useful because it already contains distinctive tools, actions, products, and practitioners. A smithy can hold images by forge, anvil, hammer, fire, bellows, and smith; other crafts are treated analogously.

The book even allows vernacular names for such workshops. That admission shows that effective mnemonic association takes precedence over an exclusively Latin scholarly vocabulary.

---

## 12. Visual, object, and gestural alphabets

### Shaped-letter alphabet

**[T]** Rosselli prints objects whose contours resemble letters: crescents, tools, croziers, limbs, and other familiar forms. This material derives substantially from the visual-alphabet tradition represented by Romberch.

### Initial-letter alphabet

**[T]** A word may be decomposed into letters represented by objects or animals beginning with the same letter. Rosselli’s famous example is:

> “*si pro A ponam Asinum, pro E Elephantem, pro R Rhinocerontem*”  
> —fol. 119v.

Gloss: “if for A I put an ass, for E an elephant, and for R a rhinoceros.”

Thus *AER* is memorized by an ass, elephant, and rhinoceros.

### Manual alphabet

**[T]** Several woodcuts show hands and fingers disposed to signify letters. Bolzoni reproduces it as “the silent alphabet of sign language.”

**[Disputed wording]** Booksellers and popular accounts often call this “the earliest known representation of a digital sign language.” That is too strong unless carefully qualified:

- the figures are genuinely early printed manual-letter forms;
- “digital” means finger-based, not electronic;
- they are not proof of a fully developed natural sign language;
- the claim to absolute priority depends on what counts as fingerspelling, monastic signs, chironomy, or a manual alphabet.

It is safer to call Rosselli’s plates **among the earliest printed systematic manual alphabets used for mnemonic or cryptographic purposes**.

### Cryptography

**[T]** Rosselli’s alphabets can support concealed or silent communication as well as private mnemonic substitution. Yet the work is not principally a cipher manual. Its encoding procedures remain subordinate to memory.

## 13. Famous persons as figures for arts and sciences

At fol. 113r Rosselli recommends an art’s most celebrated practitioner as its image:

> “For Grammar, Lorenzo Valla or Priscian; for Rhetoric, Marcus Tullius; for Dialectic Aristotle, and also for Philosophy; for Theology Plato; for Painting Phidias or Zeuxis; for Astrology Atlas, Zoroaster, or Ptolemy; for Geometry Archimedes; for Music Apollo or Orpheus …”

The Latin varies in case and abbreviation across the list, but this is the substance of Rosselli’s catalogue.

### How it works

**[T]** The relationship is metonymic rather than pictorial:

- Cicero signifies rhetoric because he is its paradigmatic practitioner.
- Archimedes signifies geometry through historical fame.
- Orpheus signifies music through mythic authority.
- Atlas signifies astrology or astronomy through iconographic convention.

The person can then be furnished with tools, inscriptions, gestures, companions, or objects encoding subdivisions of the discipline.

### “Magia”

**[T]** Pythagoras and Zoroaster appear in the list associated with *Magia*.

**[Y]** Yates writes that *Magia* “has moved up into the virtues” and takes this as evidence that the Dominican tradition is moving toward Renaissance occultism.

**Correction:** The text documents the label and the exemplary figures; it does not by that fact endorse demonic magic, talismanic operations, astral invocation, or Hermetic religion. Early-modern *magia* could mean ancient wisdom, natural philosophy, knowledge of occult properties, astrology, illicit conjuration, or several of these depending on context. Yates’s phrase compresses distinctions Rosselli does not explicitly resolve.

---

## 14. Worked examples and what is not Rosselli’s

### Rosselli’s own compact examples

The most securely identifiable examples include:

- *AER* encoded by ass–elephant–rhinoceros, fol. 119v;
- disciplines encoded by famous practitioners, fol. 113r;
- human, animal, and arboreal bodies subdivided into loci;
- detailed infernal and paradisal places;
- applications in the final chapter, fols. 132v onward.

Rosselli’s book contains examples of applying figures to words and things, but unlike the *Ad Herennium*, it does not organize its exposition around one canonical courtroom tableau.

### The lawsuit image

**[T-source ancestor, not Rosselli’s invention]** The famous lawsuit image belongs to the anonymous *Rhetorica ad Herennium* III.20.33. A prosecutor wishes to remember that a defendant killed a man by poison for an inheritance and that witnesses and accessories were involved. He imagines the sick victim in bed; the accused stands nearby holding a cup in the right hand, tablets in the left, and a ram’s testicles on the fourth finger. The cup signifies poison, tablets the will or inheritance, testicles the witnesses through a Latin sound association.

The Latin begins:

> “*Reum ad lectum eius adstituemus, dextra poculum, sinistra tabulas, medico testiculos arietinos tenentem …*”

Because manuscript texts and modern punctuation vary, the passage should be cited by section rather than isolated wording. [Latin and Caplan translation, *Ad Herennium* III.16–24](https://www.laits.utexas.edu/memoria/Ad_Herennium_Passages.html); [Latin, Teubner 1923 text](https://la.wikisource.org/wiki/Rhetorica_ad_Herennium/Liber_III)

Rosselli inherits the same operational principles—compression, action, objects, bodily placement, and phonetic association—but the lawsuit tableau is not evidence of a worked Rossellian demonstration unless he explicitly reproduces it in a passage not yet critically collated.

### Camillo’s grades and Bruno’s wheels

These are comparisons, not components of Rosselli’s own system:

- Camillo’s seven planetary grades structure a theatre of universal knowledge.
- Bruno’s rotating combinatorial wheels multiply lettered or imaged terms.
- Rosselli prints diagrams and circular cosmographies, but no evidence shows that his principal diagrams were physically rotating volvelles.

Calling Rosselli’s diagrams “wheels” without qualification risks importing Bruno or Lull into a largely static hierarchy.

## 2. Evidence for authorship and publication

A chronicle of the Dominican convent of San Marco supplies the strongest biographical documentation:

> “*Frater Cosmas Rossellius, filius huius conventus, vir fuit devotissimus et maximo ingenio praeditus, nec non et singulari prudentia, sed valetudinarius … Composuit autem opus … insigne de memoria artificiali seu locali, sed morte preventus in lucem edere non potuit. Huic autem defectui supplevit … germanus eius, frater Damianus Rossellus.*”

Gloss: “Brother Cosma Rosselli, a son of this convent, was a very devout man, endowed with great ability and singular prudence, but sickly … He composed a distinguished work on artificial or local memory but, prevented by death, could not bring it to light. His brother, Fra Damiano Rosselli, supplied this deficiency.”

The chronicle adds that Rosselli died at Santa Maria ad Gradus in Viterbo on **26 July 1575**, aged forty, after receiving the sacraments. [San Marco chronicle transcription](https://www.e-theca.net/emiliopanella/cronica3/mrc417.htm)

This corrects several catalogue errors:

- **[D]** Rosselli was born about 1535, not 1439.
- **[D]** He died in 1575, not 1578.
- **[Disputed/error]** Records giving 1439–1507 conflate him with the Florentine painter Cosimo Rosselli.
- **[D]** Fra Damiano Rosselli was his biological brother and posthumous editor, not co-author in the ordinary sense.
- **[D]** Nicolaus Alexius supplied verses.
- **[D]** The work’s publication was posthumous.

The title-page dedication is to Cardinal Vincenzo Giustiniani, a former master general of the Dominican Order. It situates the publication inside Dominican institutional patronage.

## 3. Classical sources

### Anonymous *Rhetorica ad Herennium*

Standard citation: III.16.28–24.40.

Principal modern edition:

- Friedrich Marx, ed., *Rhetorica ad Herennium*, Leipzig: Teubner, 1923.
- Harry Caplan, ed. and trans., *Rhetorica ad Herennium*, Loeb Classical Library 403, Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press, 1954.

Key precepts:

> “*Artificiosa memoria constat ex locis et imaginibus.*”

“Artificial memory consists of places and images.”

> “*Locos appellamus eos qui breviter, perfecte, insignite aut natura aut manu sunt absoluti … Imagines sunt formae quaedam et notae et simulacra eius rei quam meminisse volumus.*”

“We call places those backgrounds completed briefly, distinctly, and conspicuously by nature or art … Images are forms, marks, and representations of the thing we wish to remember.”

The work’s medieval attribution to Cicero is false. Rosselli nevertheless treats the *Ad Herennium* as Ciceronian, as Romberch did.

### Cicero, *De oratore*

Standard citation: II.86.351–88.360.

Standard Loeb:

- E. W. Sutton and H. Rackham, eds. and trans., *De oratore*, 2 vols., Loeb Classical Library 348–49.

Cicero supplies the Simonides story and the wax-tablet analogy:

> places are like wax tablets; images are like letters written upon them.

### Quintilian, *Institutio oratoria*

Standard citation: XI.2.1–51.

Standard modern edition:

- Donald A. Russell, ed. and trans., *The Orator’s Education*, 5 vols., Loeb Classical Library 124–27, 494, 2001.

Quintilian’s practical summary states:

> “*Opus est ergo locis, quae vel finguntur vel sumuntur, et imaginibus vel simulacris, quae utique fingenda sunt.*”

“We therefore need places, either invented or taken from reality, and images or likenesses, which must be fashioned.”

He describes assigning the first topic to the vestibule, the second to the atrium, then traversing impluvia, rooms, niches, and statues in order. [Perseus text](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A2007.01.0068%3Abook%3D11%3Achapter%3D2)

## 4. Medieval and Renaissance antecedents

### Albert the Great and Thomas Aquinas

**[T-source tradition/R]** The Dominican integration of artificial memory into prudence descends from Albert’s *De bono* and Aquinas’s *Summa theologiae* II–II, q. 49, a. 1. Aquinas recommends:

1. appropriate but unusual sensible likenesses;
2. orderly arrangement;
3. anxious or affectionate attachment;
4. frequent meditation.

Rosselli’s vivid torments, hierarchy, rehearsal, and affect correspond closely to these precepts.

### Boncompagno da Signa

His *Rhetorica novissima* and memory discussions already move mnemonic practice toward moral, religious, and visionary images.

### Jacobus Publicius

*Ars memorativa*, printed with his rhetorical works in Venice in 1482, includes cosmic and diagrammatic mnemonic places and visual alphabets.

### Peter of Ravenna

*Phoenix, sive artificiosa memoria*, first printed Venice, 1491/92, popularized a portable, personal art of places and images.

### Johannes Romberch

*Congestorium artificiose memorie*, Venice, 1520; expanded edition 1533. This is Rosselli’s closest printed predecessor:

- Dominican authorship;
- classical authorities;
- physical places;
- bodily loci;
- visual alphabets;
- encyclopedic lists;
- Thomist justification.

Rosselli expands Romberch’s apparatus into a more fully illustrated Christian cosmos.

### Lodovico Dolce

*Dialogo nel quale si ragiona del modo di accrescere et conservar la memoria*, Venice, 1562, translates and adapts much Romberchian material into Italian. Dolce recommends Hell, Limbo, Purgatory, and contemporary painted mythologies as image resources.

## Yates’s account and the scholarship since

## 1. Yates’s presentation

### Chapter V: a late Dominican memory manual

**[Y]** Yates places Rosselli beside Romberch as one of the two principal Dominican writers who made artificial memory publicly available during the sixteenth century. She treats both as transmitters of a medieval, Thomist, “Dantesque” tradition.

Her summary accurately identifies:

- the eleven major infernal regions;
- the central well;
- heretics, Jews, idolaters, and hypocrites;
- seven deadly sins;
- punishments and gestures as mnemonic material;
- Paradise’s gem wall, throne, angelic orders, and saints;
- zodiacal places and Metrodorus;
- Nicolaus Alexius’s verses;
- actual ecclesiastical buildings;
- the human body as a place;
- the visual alphabet.

### Decline thesis

Yates then characterizes later manuals as increasingly trivial:

> “The memory rules become more and more detailed; alphabetical lists and visual alphabets encourage trivial elaborations.”

She calls some of their advice childish games or a kind of crossword puzzle for idle cloister hours.

**[R/correction]** Later scholarship has generally rejected this as too teleological. Rosselli’s alphabets and lists are better understood within:

- contemporary information management;
- sermon composition;
- emblematic literacy;
- inventories and commonplace books;
- technical description;
- embodied cognition;
- the interaction of print and internal memory.

Bolzoni in particular shows that mnemonic images were not a decaying remnant displaced by print; print generated new relations among text, diagram, emblem, gallery, and memory.

### Chapter VII: exemplary practitioners

Yates quotes the list of Valla, Cicero, Aristotle, Plato, Phidias, Zeuxis, Atlas, Zoroaster, Ptolemy, Archimedes, Apollo, and Orpheus.

She then asks:

> “Are we now looking at Raphael’s ‘School of Athens’ as useful for memory?”

**[Y/speculation]** This is a suggestive analogy, not evidence that Raphael’s fresco was designed as a Rossellian memory system or that Rosselli used it. The general convention of placing an art beneath or beside its exemplary practitioner is well documented; a specific genetic connection is not.

### “Magia has moved up into the virtues”

**[Y]** Yates treats Rosselli’s association of Pythagoras and Zoroaster with *Magia* as a sign of movement toward the Hermetic memory of Camillo and Bruno.

**Correction:** Rosselli’s book remains structurally closer to orthodox rhetorical and Dominican memory than to Bruno’s astral magic. The appearance of *Magia* is evidence of Renaissance classificatory permeability, not proof that the *Thesaurus* is itself a magical operation.

---

## 2. Paolo Rossi

Rossi’s *Clavis universalis* appeared in 1960, before Yates’s book, and was translated as *Logic and the Art of Memory: The Quest for a Universal Language*.

His larger contribution is to shift the history from occult biography toward transformations in logic, classification, combinatorics, encyclopedism, and universal language. Rosselli occupies the textual-memory branch that later interacts with:

- Lullian combination;
- Ramist method;
- encyclopedic tables;
- seventeenth-century universal-language projects.

Rossi does not establish a direct Rosselli-to-Leibniz line. The defensible genealogy is a network: mnemonic classification and Lullian combinatorics both enter early-modern projects of method; Leibniz selectively transforms these materials into character and calculus.

---

## 3. Mary Carruthers

Carruthers’s *The Book of Memory* and *The Craft of Thought* revise the framework for medieval memory.

Her principal correction to Yates is methodological:

- medieval memory is not merely rote storage;
- it is a compositional and ethical craft;
- images are instruments of invention, meditation, judgment, and affect;
- architectural and bodily ordering are forms of thought.

Applied to Rosselli, this makes Hell and Paradise not simply repositories but “machines for thinking” devotionally and rhetorically. Rosselli’s system is late Renaissance in date but preserves the medieval monastic practice of making mental structures in which thought can be recomposed.

Carruthers does not, however, supply a full critical reconstruction of Rosselli’s text.

---

## 4. Lina Bolzoni

Bolzoni’s *The Gallery of Memory* places Rosselli within the interaction between literary and visual culture in the age of print. Her corrections are especially important:

- diagrams and alphabets are not merely degeneration;
- the printed image becomes a device for teaching the reader to manufacture a more elaborate inward image;
- memory treatises participate in emblem books, galleries, encyclopedic theaters, rhetorical machines, and image-text experiments;
- forgetting itself can be represented through violent clearing of images.

Bolzoni reproduces Rosselli’s manual alphabet and treats his sign terminology as evidence for increasingly explicit semiotic reflection.

---

## 5. Barbara Keller-Dall’Asta

*Heilsplan und Gedächtnis: Zur Mnemologie des 16. Jahrhunderts in Italien* gives the fullest sustained modern discussion specifically relevant to Rosselli, pp. 149–184.

Its organizing insight is the relation between mnemonic order and Christian *Heilsplan*, the history and structure of salvation. Rosselli’s cosmos is not accidentally Christian decoration attached to a neutral technique: salvation history supplies the macro-order of the mnemonic universe.

This corrects readings that isolate the Hell diagram as a bizarre curiosity.

---

## 6. Koji Kuwakino

Kuwakino’s *L’architetto sapiente* treats gardens, theaters, and cities as mnemonic architecture; pp. 243–84 discuss Rosselli. His 2024 article gives the most exact recent analysis of the infernal construction.

His principal contributions are:

- identifying Rosselli’s prose as technical ekphrasis;
- separating the stages of verbal construction, plate, and post-plate explanation;
- showing that the image is intentionally incomplete;
- emphasizing recursive interchange between place and figure;
- reading the author’s grammar—subjunctives, imperatives, and first-person plurals—as collaborative construction;
- comparing Rosselli’s totalized mental architecture with Fludd.

Kuwakino also corrects Yates’s impression of merely cumbersome accumulation: the abundance is functional, training the practitioner to build and animate a precise inner world.

---

## 7. Small, Hajdu, and Volkmann

- Jocelyn Penny Small’s *Wax Tablets of the Mind* emphasizes the differences between ancient oral-visual cognition and modern metaphors of storage. It cautions against treating ancient and Renaissance mnemonic systems as literal proto-computers.
- Helga Hajdu’s *Das mnemotechnische Schrifttum des Mittelalters* (1936) provided Yates with a foundational bibliography and medieval textual history.
- Ludwig Volkmann’s *Ars memorativa* (1937) reproduced numerous diagrams, including faculty-psychology and alphabetic material. Yates cites Volkmann’s plate 172 in connection with head diagrams.

These works contextualize Rosselli but do not replace direct reading of the 1579 edition.

---

## 8. Ong and Ramism

Walter J. Ong’s *Ramus, Method, and the Decay of Dialogue* established the importance of Ramist dichotomizing diagrams and curricular method.

Rosselli and Ramus represent competing but overlapping strategies:

- Rosselli: sensory images attached to reusable places;
- Ramus: dialectical disposition and branching order;
- both: visible organization, segmentation, and retrieval.

William Perkins’s *Antidicsonus* opposes the “vain” local memory associated with Metrodorus, Rosselli, Bruno (“Nolanus”), and Alexander Dicson to Ramist logical disposition. Yates’s account at p. 267 is correct in identifying Rosselli as a named polemical representative of image-memory, though not as a personal participant in the controversy.

---

## 9. Lull scholarship: Bonner and Fidora

Anthony Bonner and Alexander Fidora have substantially corrected older presentations of Ramon Llull.

Their work shows that:

- Llull’s Art is not originally a mnemonic imitation of classical loci;
- it is a combinatorial, demonstrative, missionary, and theological method grounded in divine dignities and relational principles;
- its figures are logical-operational, not simply storage wheels;
- later Renaissance thinkers conflated Lullism with memory arts.

Accordingly, Rosselli should not be described as straightforwardly “Lullian.” His cosmological circles are static mnemonic divisions, whereas Llull’s figures support systematic combination and demonstration.

The direct bridge is later reception. Tomaso Garzoni proposed combining Rosselli with Llull; Bruno and seventeenth-century encyclopedists operated in an environment where the two traditions were increasingly cross-read.

Relevant research infrastructure: [Ramon Llull Database, Universitat de Barcelona](https://orbita.bib.ub.edu/llull/).

---

## 10. Bruno scholarship: Sturlese, Ciliberto, Clucas, Gatti

Modern Bruno scholarship has complicated Yates’s “Hermetic magician” synthesis:

- Rita Sturlese’s philological work reconstructs Bruno’s sources and textual chronology.
- Michele Ciliberto stresses Bruno’s philosophy, infinity, civic-religious project, and polemics.
- Stephen Clucas distinguishes varieties of memory, logic, magic, and cognition rather than subsuming them under Hermeticism.
- Hilary Gatti situates Bruno in Renaissance philosophy, science, and politics.

For Rosselli the implication is negative but important: similarities to Bruno—cosmic scope, named inventors, circular diagrams, *umbrae*, Pythagoras, Zoroaster—do not demonstrate shared operative magic. Bruno’s rotating and combinatorial image systems transform materials that Rosselli deploys in a conventional local memory.

The Italian National Institute for Renaissance Studies’ “ideal library” records Rosselli as a source relevant to Bruno’s *De scrutinio specierum* and cites *Thesaurus* II.9, fol. 119r–v. [Biblioteca ideale di Giordano Bruno](https://bibliotecaideale.insr.it/23DeSpecierumScrutinioFonsFlowChart.php?addWorkTitle=yes)

## History and context

## 1. Chronology

### Ancient foundations

- Fifth century BCE: Simonides story; historical core uncertain.
- First century BCE: anonymous *Ad Herennium* systematizes places and images.
- First century BCE: Cicero transmits Simonides and the wax-tablet analogy.
- First century CE: Quintilian describes and criticizes extreme forms.

### Scholastic and mendicant transformation

- Thirteenth century: Albert the Great and Thomas Aquinas place artificial memory under prudence.
- Dominican and Franciscan preaching culture adapts mnemonic images to virtues, vices, salvation, Scripture, and sermon organization.
- Dante’s ordered afterworld emerges from the same broad culture of ethical division, scholastic hierarchy, pilgrimage, and visualized punishment.

### Print culture

- 1482: Publicius.
- 1491/92: Peter of Ravenna.
- 1520/1533: Romberch.
- 1550: Camillo’s *Idea del theatro* published posthumously.
- 1562: Dolce.
- 1575: Rosselli dies.
- 1579: *Thesaurus* published by his brother.
- 1582 onward: Bruno’s major mnemonic works.
- 1592: Gesualdo’s *Plutosofia*.
- 1610: Schenkel’s *Gazophylacium*.
- 1617–21: Fludd’s encyclopedic works.
- Seventeenth century: mnemonic, Lullian, Ramist, encyclopedic, and universal-language systems increasingly interact.

---

## 2. Dominican institutional setting

**[D]** Rosselli was a member of the Florentine Dominican convent of San Marco. The convent chronicle calls him:

- devout;
- intellectually gifted;
- prudent;
- chronically ill;
- subprior in several places;
- novice master;
- confessor to nuns.

He did not become prior because of ill health.

These offices explain the likely audiences and functions of the work:

- preachers;
- confessors;
- novices;
- theologians;
- religious educators.

The title page broadens the market to philosophers, physicians, jurists, orators, procurators, merchants, and lovers of letters. This is simultaneously a pedagogical claim and commercial positioning.

---

## 3. Franciscan and Lullian environments

Rosselli was Dominican, not Franciscan. Llull was a lay Franciscan tertiary in traditional accounts, though the exact institutional characterization of his status requires care. The key historical point is that mendicant intellectual networks cultivated:

- preaching;
- disputation;
- doctrinal organization;
- missionary argument;
- meditation;
- highly structured pedagogical aids.

Rosselli’s immediate method is Dominican-rhetorical. Lullism belongs to a neighboring combinatorial tradition that later authors tried to join to local memory.

---

## 4. Courts and academies

Rosselli’s publication belongs to Venetian print culture and is dedicated upward within Dominican hierarchy, not chiefly to a secular prince. In contrast:

- Camillo sought Francis I’s patronage;
- Bruno dedicated mnemonic books to Henry III and circulated through courts and universities;
- emblematic and encyclopedic memory flourished in academies.

Rosselli’s work nevertheless entered this broader courtly and learned market through print.

---

## 5. Universities and curricula

Memory was traditionally the fourth or fifth part of rhetoric, depending on the enumerative convention: invention, disposition, style, memory, delivery. It also belonged to:

- Aristotelian psychology;
- ethics through prudence;
- dialectic and method;
- preaching;
- legal and medical education.

Ramist reform challenged local-image memory by claiming that logical disposition supplies the proper order of recall. Rosselli’s 1579 book appeared at precisely the period when sensory mnemonic encyclopedism and anti-image method competed.

---

## 6. Dante and Florentine visual culture

### Dante

**[R]** Yates identifies Rosselli’s Hell as “Dantesque,” and Kuwakino describes it as inspired by Dante’s journey. The resemblances are real:

- descent and ascent;
- graded punishments;
- sin-specific topography;
- a journey through a moral cosmos;
- visual and affective intensity.

**[Disputed]** Rosselli’s eleven-part circular diagram is not a diagrammatic transcription of Dante’s nine circles. It combines doctrinal categories, capital sins, late-medieval iconography, and mnemonic requirements. “Dantesque” is an interpretive family resemblance, not a source citation proving direct copying.

### Nardo di Cione

Yates juxtaposes Rosselli’s Hell with Nardo di Cione’s fresco in the Strozzi Chapel, Santa Maria Novella. The fresco provides a plausible Florentine visual background of graded punishments and densely populated infernal space.

**[Y/R]** No document proves that Rosselli designed his plate from Nardo’s fresco. The comparison is contextual.

### Santa Maria Novella’s *Triumph of Saint Thomas*

The Spanish Chapel fresco places exemplary authorities under personifications of sciences. It is a strong visual analogue to Rosselli’s use of Cicero for rhetoric or Aristotle for dialectic.

Again, general tradition is documented; direct copying is unproved.

## People

## 1. Cosma Rosselli

**[D]** Born about 1535; died at Viterbo, 26 July 1575, aged forty. Florentine Dominican of San Marco. Subprior, novice master, confessor, invalid. Author of an unpublished-at-death work on artificial/local memory.

**Not the painter:** He must not be confused with Cosimo di Lorenzo Rosselli (1439/40–1507), the painter represented in Vasari.

## 2. Fra Damiano Rosselli

**[D]** Cosma’s brother and fellow Dominican. He arranged posthumous publication, composed or transmitted the dedication, and answered demand for the work. He died in 1593 according to the convent chronicle.

## 3. Nicolaus Alexius / Alegius of Perugia

**[T/D]** Dominican master and commissioner of the Holy Inquisition. Author of the mnemonic *carmina* on Hell, elements, and heavens.

## 4. Cardinal Vincenzo Giustiniani

**[T/D]** Dedicatee and former master general of the Dominican Order. His presence marks the book’s institutional orthodoxy and patronage strategy.

## 5. Johannes Romberch

Rosselli’s most important printed predecessor. Like Rosselli, a Dominican compiler and transmitter of classical, scholastic, bodily, architectural, and alphabetic mnemonic techniques.

## 6. Tomaso Garzoni

**[Y/T in Garzoni]** Garzoni announced an ambition to unite Rosselli’s artificial memory with Llull’s Art. This documents Rosselli’s reception as a standard authority soon after publication. It does not prove that Garzoni completed a functioning synthesis.

## 7. Giordano Bruno

Bruno’s systems were chronologically close and shared vocabulary and source traditions with Rosselli. Later polemicists grouped them, but no evidence found establishes Rosselli as Bruno’s teacher or associate.

## 8. Alexander Dicson and William Perkins

Dicson transmitted Bruno-inspired memory in England. Perkins’s *Antidicsonus* grouped Dicson, “Nolanus,” Metrodorus, and Rosselli as proponents of vain local-image arts opposed to Ramist method.

Rosselli was already dead; this is textual reception, not a personal quarrel.

## 9. Robert Fludd

**[R]** Fludd’s cosmic and theatrical memory can be productively compared with Rosselli. Kuwakino sees Fludd as clarifying how resident figures might interact with transient mnemonic images. Direct copying must be established passage by passage and should not be assumed from resemblance alone.

## Anecdotes: documented, inherited, and absent

### Simonides and the collapsed banquet hall

**[Tr/Legend]** Ancient sources relate that Simonides identified crushed banquet guests from their seating positions after Castor and Pollux called him outside. The story explains the discovery of ordered loci. It is ancient testimony, not independently verifiable biography.

### The auctioneer or auction memory

**[Tr]** Quintilian XI.2.24 says that people reportedly remembered, after an auction, what was sold to each buyer, supported by bankers’ records; Quintus Hortensius was credited with such an accomplishment:

> “*Et forsitan hoc sunt adiuti qui auctione dimissa quid cuique vendidissent … reddiderunt, quod praestitisse Q. Hortensium dicunt.*”

This is not an anecdote about Rosselli.

### “The professor’s party”

**Absence finding:** No securely sourced Rosselli anecdote corresponding to a professor demonstrating memory at a party was located. Such stories cluster around later itinerant mnemonists, Camillo, Lambert Schenkel, and nineteenth-century lecturers. Assigning one to Rosselli would be unsupported.

### “The student who lost his memory”

**Absence finding:** No identified contemporary source connects this anecdote to Rosselli. Stories of students damaged by excessive mnemonic practice occur in broader pedagogical polemic, especially humanist and Ramist attacks, but must not be silently transferred to him.

### “The accused magician”

**Absence finding:** No evidence found that Rosselli was accused of magic, prosecuted, denounced to the Inquisition, imprisoned, or censured.

Several other memory practitioners were suspected of occultism or imposture; Bruno was tried and executed for a complex set of theological propositions. Those histories cannot be used as evidence against Rosselli.

---

## The magic question

## 1. Is the *Thesaurus* magical?

### What is documented

Rosselli includes:

- cosmic and zodiacal places;
- Pythagoras and Zoroaster under *Magia*;
- terms such as *idola*, *umbrae*, and *simulacra* among names for images;
- celestial and supercelestial figures;
- a faculty-psychology diagram;
- visual alphabets and silent gestures.

None of those features alone constitutes operative magic.

### What is absent

The *Thesaurus* does not appear to prescribe:

- invocation of spirits;
- consecrated talismans;
- planetary suffumigations;
- magical seals intended to compel beings;
- necromancy;
- angelic conjuration;
- demonic pacts;
- astrological timing of image manufacture;
- *Ars notoria* prayers or ritual figures promising infused knowledge.

### Best classification

**[R]** The system is primarily:

- rhetorical;
- scholastic;
- devotional;
- encyclopedic;
- semiotic;
- pedagogical.

It borders Renaissance occult discourse through cosmology, exemplary magi, astrology, and “shadows,” but its actual operations remain orthodox local memory.

---

## 2. Yates’s Hermetic reading

Yates’s overarching thesis held that medieval artificial memory was transformed in the Renaissance through Ficinian and Hermetic magic, reaching Camillo, Bruno, and Fludd. Rosselli appears as a bridge: an orthodox Dominican manual beginning to admit modern, possibly occult material.

Later scholarship has qualified this:

- Hermeticism is one strand, not the master explanation for every circular image or cosmic scheme.
- Lullism cannot be reduced to memory or Hermeticism.
- Bruno’s memory works require close philological differentiation.
- Christian cosmological memory need not be magical.
- *Magia naturalis*, astrology, talismanic magic, ritual magic, and metaphorical “magic” are distinct categories.

For Rosselli, the evidence supports **adjacency and permeability**, not a demonstrated magical program.

---

## 3. The Inquisition

### Nicolaus Alexius

An inquisitorial commissioner supplied verse. This weighs heavily against the idea that the published work was covertly or obviously heretical.

### Formal approval

**Absence finding:** Alexius’s participation is not equivalent to a surviving formal inquisitorial examination. A complete census of approbations, licenses, and privileges in all copies remains desirable.

### Consequences

No inquisitorial consequence for author, editor, printer, or readers has been found.

---

## 4. *Ars notoria*

The *Ars notoria* promised rapid or infused mastery of disciplines through prayers, ritual figures, divine names, fasting, and visionary operations. Medieval theologians and later humanists disputed or condemned it as superstitious or demonic.

Rosselli’s method differs fundamentally:

- it demands construction, rehearsal, association, and exercise;
- its figures act cognitively, not sacramentally;
- it does not promise supernatural infusion;
- its diagrams are maps and sign-systems, not demonstrably ritual *notae*.

The shared word *notae* and the presence of diagrams do not establish derivation from the *Ars notoria*.

---

## 5. Talismanic interpretation

**[Disputed/unsupported]** Reading the Hell and Paradise plates as talismans would require evidence that their configuration was believed to channel celestial or spiritual power. Rosselli presents them as pictures to be inspected, mentally completed, and used as ordered places. No such talismanic instruction has been found.

---

## Afterlife

## 1. Immediate reception

### Garzoni

Garzoni’s proposed Rosselli–Llull synthesis documents Rosselli’s standing as a comprehensive memory authority.

### Bruno and Dicson

Rosselli’s procedures entered the same polemical field as Bruno’s and Dicson’s. Perkins’s hostile grouping shows recognizability across confessional and national lines.

### Schenkel

Lambert Schenkel expanded methods for constructing, filling, and violently clearing mnemonic locations. His work belongs to a practical afterlife in which Rosselli remained a possible source or comparator.

### Fludd

Fludd’s *ars rotunda* and *ars quadrata* distinguish celestial/intellectual from terrestrial/architectural places. This resembles the dual capacity of Rosselli’s cosmic and built loci, though Fludd’s system has its own metaphysics and theatrical apparatus.

---

## 2. Emblem books and visual culture

Rosselli’s sign-images belong to the same environment as:

- emblem books;
- hieroglyphic collections;
- personifications;
- imprese;
- rebuses;
- physiognomic illustrations;
- galleries of famous men;
- encyclopedic theaters.

Bolzoni’s strongest contribution is demonstrating that mnemonic images and printed images mutually transformed one another.

---

## 3. Dante, literature, and art

### Dante

Rosselli is evidence for the early-modern mnemonic usability of Dante’s moral topography, but not proof that Dante composed the *Commedia* from a formal Rossellian precursor.

### Frescoes

The fresco cycles of Hell, Paradise, virtues, vices, sciences, and famous practitioners provide a public iconographic repertoire comparable to Rosselli’s internal one. Direct dependence must be proved individually.

### Borges’s Funes

**[Modern analogy]** Borges’s “Funes the Memorious” dramatizes total, disabling memory without abstraction. It is an illuminating counter-image to Rosselli’s ordered selection, but there is no evidence Borges drew specifically on Rosselli.

### Umberto Eco

Eco discusses memory, combinatorics, perfect languages, encyclopedias, and semiotic systems. Rosselli belongs retrospectively in that intellectual archaeology. Claims of a direct influence require textual evidence and should not be assumed.

---

## 4. The modern memory-palace revival

Modern popular memory training retains the basic classical mechanism:

- familiar route;
- fixed locations;
- vivid and absurd images;
- serial mental traversal.

Rosselli’s special additions—Christian cosmic maps, taxonomic practitioners, bodies within bodies, manual alphabets—are usually absent.

Calling every modern “memory palace” Rossellian would therefore be misleading. Most modern systems descend from the shared classical method, often mediated through nineteenth- and twentieth-century manuals.

---

## 5. Memory athletes

Competitive mnemonists use:

- routes or loci;
- person–action–object systems;
- number-shape and number-rhyme codes;
- Major-system phonetics;
- image interaction;
- rapid clearing or reuse.

Functional analogies to Rosselli are strong:

- exemplary persons resemble person-based codes;
- active gestures resemble action encoding;
- nested anatomical places resemble dense routes;
- alphabetic animals resemble initial-letter encodings.

**[Modern]** No evidence suggests that contemporary athletes normally derive these techniques directly from the 1579 *Thesaurus*.

---

## 6. Computing and combinatorics

### What can legitimately be said

Rosselli’s system has features recognizable to information science:

- hierarchical addressing;
- stable storage locations;
- transient contents;
- multiple indexes;
- redundancy between image, word, verse, and spatial sequence;
- semantic typing of places;
- nested containers;
- erasure and reuse.

### What cannot

It is not a digital computer, formal programming language, database, or algorithm in the modern mathematical sense. These are useful analogies, not historical identities.

### Llull to Leibniz

The better-documented combinatorial genealogy runs:

- Llull’s finite alphabets and figures;
- Renaissance and seventeenth-century Lullism;
- mechanized or mathematized combination in Kircher and Izquierdo;
- Leibniz’s *ars combinatoria* and *characteristica universalis*.

Mnemonic classification interacted with that development. Rosselli represents the local-image and encyclopedic side, not a necessary direct ancestor of Leibniz’s calculus.

Yates herself says that memory, Lullian art, and “method” became entangled in the seventeenth century and that Leibniz supplies the most remarkable later case. This remains suggestive, but modern Lull scholarship rejects a simple linear “memory wheel → computer” story.

---

## Controversies and disputes

## 1. Identity of the author

### Error

Numerous modern catalogues and exhibition pages identify the author as “Cosimo Rosselli, 1439–1507,” the Renaissance painter.

### Documentary correction

The San Marco chronicle identifies the mnemonic author as a Dominican who died in 1575 aged forty. The title calls him a Florentine Dominican. His brother Damiano published the book.

**Verdict:** The painter attribution is false; it results from name collision.

---

## 2. Death date: 1575 or 1578?

Catalogues often give “–1578,” presumably inferred from publication preliminaries or an older authority file.

The convent chronicle gives:

> 26 July 1575, at Santa Maria ad Gradus in Viterbo, aged forty.

**Verdict:** 1575 is the stronger documented date. Authority records should be corrected unless contrary contemporary evidence emerges.

---

## 3. Authorship versus editorship

Cosma composed the work. Damiano brought it to print after Cosma’s death. Nicolaus Alexius supplied verse.

**Open textual issue:** Without an authorial manuscript, the extent of Damiano’s editorial intervention cannot be measured. Calling Damiano merely a printer’s intermediary may understate his role; calling him co-author overstates present evidence.

---

## 4. Publication date

The title page and bibliographical records give 1579.

References to 1574, 1575, 1578, or 1580 in later catalogues arise from:

- death dates;
- inventory dates;
- bookseller records;
- transcription errors;
- confusion between composition and publication.

**Verdict:** No verified separate early edition other than Venice 1579 was found.

---

## 5. “Eleven places”

Correct as the number of chief infernal subdivisions; incorrect if taken as the total number of mnemonic stations. The architecture, inhabitants, punishments, and bodies multiply loci recursively.

---

## 6. “Dantesque”

**Supported as reconstruction:** graded sin, journey, Hell-to-Paradise structure, and Florentine culture make Dante a compelling context.

**Not demonstrated:** exact derivation of Rosselli’s eleven divisions from Dante’s nine circles.

---

## 7. “Magia has become a virtue”

The textual fact is that *Magia* appears in the relevant exemplary list and receives Pythagoras and Zoroaster.

Yates’s “moved up into the virtues” is a historical interpretation. The precise taxonomy, syntax, and surrounding headings at fol. 113r should be critically edited before stronger claims are made.

---

## 8. Raphael’s *School of Athens*

Yates’s rhetorical question identifies a shared iconographic logic. No documentary evidence links Rosselli’s list to Raphael’s design. The claim is illuminating comparison, not established influence.

---

## 9. Earliest fingerspelling alphabet

Rosselli unquestionably prints early hand-letter diagrams.

The stronger “earliest known digital sign language” claim is unstable because:

- medieval monastic gesture systems predate him;
- hand mnemonics predate him;
- natural sign languages are not identical with artificial manual alphabets;
- priority depends on definition.

**Verdict:** “Among the earliest printed manual alphabets” is defensible; “first sign language” is not.

---

## 10. Phrenology

Bookseller descriptions sometimes call Rosselli’s faculty-head diagram “phrenological.”

That is anachronistic. Medieval faculty psychology located common sense, imagination, estimation, and memory in ventricular regions of the brain. Nineteenth-century phrenology inferred character from cranial protuberances.

**Verdict:** Rosselli prints a **ventricular faculty-psychology diagram**, not phrenology in the Gall–Spurzheim sense.

---

## 11. Magical or heretical status

No evidence found of magical accusation, censorship, prosecution, or heresy proceedings against Rosselli. The system’s publication by his Dominican brother, dedication to a Dominican cardinal, and verses by an inquisitorial commissioner favor an orthodox public identity.

This does not prove that every reader understood *Magia*, astrology, or image practice identically. It establishes the absence of documented consequences.

---

## 12. Influence on Bruno

There are textual and conceptual points of contact, and modern Bruno source projects register Rosselli. But broad similarity is not proof of direct dependence for every element.

**Verdict:** possible and sometimes textually traceable influence; no simple master–disciple relationship.

---

## 13. Yates’s deterioration narrative

Her description of Rosselli’s alphabetic material as trivial or childish reflects a mid-twentieth-century contrast between “great” philosophical systems and practical mnemonic detail.

Bolzoni, Carruthers, Keller-Dall’Asta, and Kuwakino show that such detail served:

- composition;
- image literacy;
- technical visualization;
- information organization;
- devotional exercise;
- controlled forgetting.

**Verdict:** substantially corrected, though Yates’s identification of Rosselli’s traditional sources remains valuable.

---

## 14. Direct line to computing

“Rosselli anticipated the computer” is a modern metaphor. The more defensible claim is that mnemonic and combinatorial arts contributed concepts of ordered representation, indexing, symbolic manipulation, and universal method later reconfigured in formal logic and computation.

---

## Open questions

1. **Authorial manuscript:** Does any Dominican archive preserve Rosselli’s draft, presentation copy, correspondence, or working diagrams?

2. **Damiano’s intervention:** Which passages, additions, illustrations, indexes, or corrections were added after Cosma’s death?

3. **Nicolaus Alexius:** Can his Dominican career and inquisitorial commissions be documented beyond the title and San Marco chronicle?

4. **Cardinal Giustiniani:** Did he materially sponsor publication, approve it, or merely receive the dedication?

5. **Printing history:** Who designed and cut the woodblocks? Were any blocks reused from earlier books?

6. **Copy census:** How many 1579 copies survive, and how do their inserted plates, cancellations, errata, and gatherings differ?

7. **Plate order:** Are the two Paradise impressions present in every complete copy? Do bindings preserve the intended relation between prose and foldout?

8. **Sources for Hell:** Beyond Dante, which theological, homiletic, cartographic, and pictorial models can be shown through verbal borrowing?

9. **Visual sources:** Can specific correspondences be established with Nardo di Cione, the Spanish Chapel, printed Dante diagrams, or contemporary Last Judgment imagery?

10. **Romberch dependence:** A line-by-line comparison is needed to separate direct borrowing from common classical and Dominican inheritance.

11. **Vernacular practice:** Did Rosselli teach the system orally in Italian while publishing it in Latin?

12. **Actual use:** Do annotated copies preserve readers’ assigned images, sermon plans, alphabets, or personal routes?

13. **Reception:** A systematic search of post-1579 mnemonic manuals, library inventories, preaching manuals, and university disputations would clarify his actual reach.

14. **Magia taxonomy:** A critical transcription of fols. 107r–123r is needed to determine exactly how *Magia* relates grammatically and conceptually to arts, sciences, and virtues.

15. **Manual alphabet priority:** Comparison with medieval finger alphabets, monastic signs, Boncompagno, Publicius, Romberch, and later deaf education could refine the priority claim.

16. **Translation:** The absence of a critical Latin edition with English translation is the greatest practical obstacle to further work.

---

## Overall assessment

Rosselli’s *Thesaurus* is best understood neither as a curiosity containing two pictures nor as an occult memory wheel. It is the most expansive late-sixteenth-century Dominican synthesis of classical local memory, scholastic faculty psychology, Christian moral topography, printed visual aids, alphabetic encoding, and encyclopedic classification.

Its distinctive achievement is scale and recursion. The practitioner can move from a place as large as the Christian universe to a celestial sphere, constellation, city, church, room, workshop, person, hand, finger, animal, tree, letter, or gesture. At every level, the same entity may become container or contained sign. Hell and Paradise are therefore not ornamental appendices: they are the poles of an ordered mnemonic cosmos.

Yates correctly recognized its Dominican and “Dantesque” importance and rescued its plates from obscurity. She underestimated, however, the technical intelligence of its lists, alphabets, and descriptive procedures; over-read isolated Renaissance markers as movement toward Hermetic magic; and relied on an authorial identity and chronology that modern catalogue practice has often made worse by confusing the friar with a painter. The documentary record instead gives a sickly but respected Florentine Dominican, dead at forty in 1575, whose brother and fellow friars ensured that his elaborate “treasury” survived.

# Rossellius's *Thesaurus artificiosae memoriae*: Research Dossier

## Basic identification

**Name:** *Thesaurus artificiosae memoriae*  
**Exponent:** Fra Cosma Rosselli, O.P. (Latinized *Cosmas Rossellius*), a Florentine Dominican  
**Date:** Written before Rosselli’s death on 26 July 1575; published posthumously in Venice in 1579  
**Source text:** *Thesaurus artificiosae memoriae … Authore R.P.F. Cosma Rossellio Florentino, sacri Ordinis Praedicatorum*, Venice, apud Antonium Paduanium [Antonio Padovani], 1579  
**Principle in one sentence:** Rosselli makes the ordered Christian cosmos—from Hell through the elements and celestial spheres to the Empyrean—an enormous reusable hierarchy of mnemonic places, supplemented by actual buildings, workshops, human bodies, animals, trees, alphabets, gestures, exemplary persons, and vivid images attached and retrieved through an orderly mental itinerary.

**Yates:** *The Art of Memory*, ch. V, pp. 121–23 (especially 122–23); plate 7a–b; ch. VII, pp. 163–64; also pp. 199, 209, 223, 256, 267. In the available Warburg scan, the relevant printed pages correspond to PDF pp. 73 and 97. [Warburg Institute scan of Yates](https://resources.warburg.sas.ac.uk/mnemosyne/fq/yates_memory.pdf)

### Evidentiary key

The following labels are used throughout:

- **[T] Documented text:** stated or visibly represented in Rosselli’s 1579 book.
- **[D] Documentary record:** early archival or bibliographical evidence independent of the treatise.
- **[R] Scholarly reconstruction:** an argument made by a named modern scholar.
- **[Y] Yates’s interpretation:** Frances Yates’s historical or iconographic reading.
- **[Tr] Tradition:** a claim transmitted by ancient or early-modern authorities without independent verification.
- **[Disputed]:** evidence or attribution is contested.
- **[Legend]:** an anecdote or genealogy lacking contemporary corroboration.
- **[Modern]:** a later analogy, adaptation, or invention that should not be attributed to Rosselli.

A central finding of this investigation is negative: despite the richness of Rosselli’s printed text, little biographical documentation survives, no authorial manuscript of the *Thesaurus* has been securely identified, and no evidence found connects Rosselli personally with magical practice, prosecution, or the Inquisition except through the thoroughly orthodox Dominican inquisitor who supplied mnemonic verses.

## The system in detail

## 1. What kind of book it is

### Bibliographical form

**[T/D]** The 1579 edition is a quarto of sixteen unnumbered preliminary pages, 145 numbered leaves plus an errata leaf. The ETH record describes twenty-six woodcuts and three folding plates; another detailed collation counts twenty-five full-page woodcuts or diagrams, one half-page diagram, and three plates. The apparent discrepancy results from counting sheets, impressions, and repeated or inserted plates differently. The volume’s own foliation is also irregular. [ETH e-rara bibliographical record](https://www.e-rara.ch/zut/content/titleinfo/4198306); [Folger catalogue record](https://catalog.folger.edu/record/381992)

The Folger collation is especially important:

> `[16], 145 [i.e. 147], [1] leaves; a–d⁴, A–R⁴ (plus two leaves, the second signed R3, bound after R3), S–2M⁴, 2N⁶.`

Its copy lacks some gatherings or leaves supplied by photostats from a Yale copy. Thus references such as “p. 33 recto” in Yates should properly be read as **folio 33r**, not page 33.

### Macrostructure

**[T]** The work is divided into two principal treatises:

1. **First part: places** (*de locis*), fols. 1r–77v.
2. **Second part: figures/images** (*de figuris*), fols. 78r–145r.

The first part proceeds from the largest possible locations—the cosmic regions—to successively smaller places:

- *loca amplissima*: Hell, the elementary world, celestial spheres, Empyrean/Paradise;
- *loca ampliora*;
- *loca communia amplissima*;
- *loca communia mediocria*;
- *loca communia minora*: workshops and professions;
- *loca communia minima*: persons, animals, and trees;
- practical cautions and conditions governing places.

The second part classifies figures as:

- natural;
- terrestrial and inanimate;
- vegetal;
- animal;
- aquatic, aerial, and fiery;
- celestial and supercelestial;
- artificial;
- alphabetic, numerical, gestural, or otherwise conventional;
- figures applied through direct likeness, association, linguistic resemblance, initial letters, rebus, narrative, or exemplary persons.

The printed table of contents can be followed leaf by leaf in the [ETH structured digitization](https://www.e-rara.ch/zut/content/structure/4198306).

---

## 2. The fundamental mechanism

### Places and figures

**[T]** Rosselli defines and subdivides *loci* before treating *figurae*. His terminology for images is unusually expansive:

> “*Figurae … multiplici appellatione ab huius artis peritis denominantur. Dicuntur enim etiam imagines, simulacra et idola; signa quoque, similitudines ac species; notae etiam vocitantur et memorandarum umbrae.*”  
> —*Thesaurus*, II.1, fol. 78r.

Gloss: “Figures are designated by many names by experts in this art: they are called images, likenesses and idols; also signs, similitudes and species; they are likewise called marks and shadows of things to be remembered.”

This passage is discussed by Lina Bolzoni in *I segni della memoria*. Its importance is semiotic: Rosselli does not restrict the mnemonic image to a literal picture. It may be a likeness, conventional sign, species, notation, or “shadow.”

**[T]** He explicitly permits a thing to function first as a place and then as an image:

> “*Nec mireris, quod eaedem res, quae pro locis serviunt in uno et eodem discursu minime alterato, ac in eodem tempore, etiam pro figuris deserviant. In consimilibus enim discursibus easdem res et figuras esse et loca non dedecet.*”  
> —Ibid., fol. 78r.

Gloss: “Do not be surprised that the same things which serve as places in one unchanged course, even at the same time, also serve as figures; in such courses it is not improper for the same things to be both figures and places.”

**[R]** Koji Kuwakino identifies this as a distinctive “interpenetration” or interchangeability of *loci* and *imagines agentes*. A person, animal, tree, constellation, or infernal circle can be a stable receptacle at one level and an active sign at another. [Kuwakino, “The ars memorativa and ecfrasis,” pp. 156–57](https://gal-studies.museogalileo.it/index.php/galilaeana/article/download/57/41)

### Fixing order

**[T]** Order is imposed hierarchically and itinerantly:

1. choose a stable domain;
2. divide it into major regions;
3. subdivide each region into numbered or otherwise unmistakable positions;
4. assign figures to those positions;
5. rehearse the route repeatedly;
6. retrieve by mentally traversing the same route.

The book offers several independent ordering systems:

- theological descent and ascent: Hell → elements → planets and spheres → Empyrean;
- concentric and radial order;
- architectural order through churches, convents, houses, rooms, doors, corners, altars, and furnishings;
- bodily order from head through limbs and digits;
- alphabetical order;
- lists of professions, plants, animals, philosophers, physicians, poets, wealthy men, and heroes;
- zodiacal and planetary order;
- verse as an auxiliary serial index.

### Retrieval

**[T/R]** Rosselli says surprisingly little about the moment of retrieval compared with the enormous labor he devotes to constructing places. The implied operation is classical: begin at the established starting point, traverse the sequence, and “repossess” what was deposited in each place.

**[R]** Kuwakino stresses this imbalance. Rosselli gives minute directions for building the mnemonic world but few extended demonstrations of a complete act of encoding and recall. That is not evidence that the system lacked a retrieval procedure; it means the procedure was assumed from the familiar Ciceronian tradition.

### Appropriate matching

**[T]** Places are not semantically indifferent. At fol. 51v Rosselli cautions against putting incongruous material into a domain—for example, the names of angels should not be assigned to Hell. Congruence reinforces recall: infernal material belongs in infernal places, celestial material in celestial places, trades in workshops, and sciences with their celebrated practitioners.

## 3. Rules for places

Rosselli’s prescriptions should be distinguished from those inherited from the *Ad Herennium*, Cicero, Quintilian, Peter of Ravenna, and Romberch.

### Hierarchical scale

**[T]** Rosselli distinguishes places by capacity:

- “very extensive” or world-sized places;
- large places;
- common places of several sizes;
- minimal places.

A minimal place is not simply a tiny patch of architecture. It may be a person, animal, or tree whose parts are themselves separately usable.

### Visibility and differentiation

**[T/Tr]** In agreement with the classical tradition, places must be distinct, clearly bounded, neither confused nor monotonously alike, and mentally visible. Repetition or insufficient differentiation causes one location to be mistaken for another.

### Moderate spacing

**[T/Tr]** The places should be separated by usable intervals. This descends from the *Ad Herennium*’s requirement that backgrounds be neither excessively close nor excessively far apart.

### Stability

**[T]** The system works only when its order is fixed before transient content is deposited in it. Cosmic, architectural, anatomical, zodiacal, and alphabetical structures are valuable because their internal relations are already stable.

### Actual buildings

**[T]** Rosselli permits or recommends places drawn from monasteries, abbeys, churches, houses, and similar familiar buildings. These are not merely metaphors: the practitioner is to learn their sequence so thoroughly that the mind can travel through them without hesitation.

This aspect places him firmly in the practical Dominican tradition. A preacher could use a familiar church or convent as a reusable matrix for sermon divisions, authorities, examples, objections, and conclusions.

### Imaginary places

**[T]** Rosselli also accepts deliberately fabricated places. The cosmological system is an extreme example: Hell and Paradise are made visually precise enough to be used like a known building.

At fol. 6r the reader is told, in effect:

> “*te oportet confingere*”

Gloss: “you must fashion [it] in imagination.”

**[R]** Kuwakino reads Rosselli’s elaborate descriptive method as “technical ekphrasis”: the prose gives construction instructions for a mental architecture, not simply literary ornament.

### Emptying and reuse

**[T]** Rosselli discusses deliberate forgetting or clearing of places. Bolzoni quotes his striking instruction: imagine someone returning home, casting the images onto the floor, then expelling or throwing them from the window.

This is an *ars oblivionalis* ancillary to the art of memory: the permanent place remains while temporary occupants are violently removed. It is neither iconoclasm in a doctrinal sense nor evidence of mental illness; it is a vivid erasure operation.

## 4. Rules for images

### Familiarity

**[T]** In the preface to the second book Rosselli limits himself to useful, recognizable figures:

> “*Primo enim figuras, non omnes, sed notiores nobis et familiariores, quae nostrae memoriae prodesse possint, ponere volumus.*”  
> —fol. 77v/78r vicinity; cited in scholarship as fol. 77r in some descriptions.

Gloss: “First, we wish to set down figures—not all of them, but those better known and more familiar to us—which can assist our memory.”

Foliation should be checked against the individual copy because inserted plates and misnumbering produce slight citation differences.

### Sensory force

**[T]** Images should be visible to the “eyes of the psyche,” affectively forceful, and capable of exciting the memory. Rosselli’s preferred language includes art, practice, imagination, motion, delight, horror, and astonishment.

### Congruity and association

**[T]** A figure may encode a thing through:

- direct resemblance;
- a characteristic instrument;
- profession or habitual action;
- a famous practitioner;
- first letter;
- sound resemblance;
- semantic association;
- emblematic or mythological convention;
- narrative episode;
- bodily gesture;
- written label;
- an accumulation of several of these.

### Variety and action

**[T/Tr]** Variety of posture, punishment, expression, gesture, clothing, and action increases discriminability. This is the direct heir of the *Ad Herennium*’s *imagines agentes*: active, striking images designed to “hit” the mind.

### The printed picture is a prompt, not the completed mental image

**[T/R]** Rosselli repeatedly asks the reader to add what cannot be fully represented by a crude woodcut—sound, smell, smoke, motion, cold, burning, demons, wounds, gestures, and the intensity of punishment. The physical plate scaffolds a richer internal spectacle.

## 5. Hell as artificial memory

### Structural position

**[T]** Hell is the first of the *loca communia amplissima*, the enormous common places through which Rosselli’s mnemonic cosmos begins. Its treatment proceeds through five stages:

1. general structure, fols. 2v–6r;
2. explanation of the usefulness of printed pictures, fol. 7r–v;
3. theological and sensory elaboration, fols. 7v–11v;
4. the woodcut, fol. 12r;
5. mnemonic verses by Nicolaus Alexius, fols. 13r–15r.

This five-part structure was reconstructed in detail by Kuwakino. [Kuwakino 2024, pp. 156–63](https://gal-studies.museogalileo.it/index.php/galilaeana/article/download/57/41)

### Eleven regions

**[T]** The infernal plan consists of a central fiery well and eleven surrounding subdivisions:

- four concentric, stair-like rings nearest the well:
  1. heretics;
  2. Jewish “infidels”;
  3. idolaters;
  4. hypocrites;
- seven outer sectors corresponding to the seven capital sins.

The seven labels visible in the plate include forms corresponding to pride, sloth, lust, envy, gluttony, avarice, and vainglory. Orthography is compressed in the engraving.

**Correction to oversimplified catalogue accounts:** “Hell is divided into eleven places” is true at the level of its principal *gironi*, but the system is recursively subdivisible. The damned, demons, walls, stairs, punishments, gestures, boat, river, cave, and body parts can all yield subsidiary places. Eleven is not the total storage capacity.

### Exact architectural instructions

**[T]** Rosselli locates the well in the center and describes outward construction. The four inner stone circuits are each approximately three or four *braccia* wide and about two *braccia* high. Walls separating the seven outer areas are assigned widths and heights in *braccia* or *palmae*; the outer circumferential wall is approximately three *palmae* high.

The prose uses exhortative subjunctives and first-person plurals—“let there be,” “let them be imagined,” “let them be placed,” “we add”—as though author and reader were building the structure together.

### Inhabitants and actions

**[T]**

- Heretics tear or mutilate Scripture.
- Jews are represented with iron faces, veiled eyes, and bent necks.
- Idolaters appear with ruined idols.
- Hypocrites occupy the fourth step.
- Lucifer emerges in the fiery torrent from the well.
- Demons act as guards and tormentors.
- The seven outer areas contain sinners punished according to the capital vice assigned to each.

These representations are documents of sixteenth-century Catholic polemic and anti-Jewish iconography, not neutral ethnography. Reporting them does not validate their theology or prejudice.

### Sensory completion

**[T]** Hell is the *carcer apostatarum spirituum*, “prison of apostate spirits.” Rosselli directs the reader to amplify the woodcut mentally with:

- thick smoke;
- red-hot fire;
- gnawing worms;
- screams and hideous noises;
- putrid stench;
- intolerable cold;
- demonic violence;
- punishments fitted to particular sins.

He writes:

> “*Nam cogita si potes omnia atrocissima tormenta, asperrimasque poenas a mundo condito a diversis tyrannis hominibus inflictas … nec tamen parem ullam invenies, quae etiam minimae inferni poenae vel sensus, vel damni coaequari possit.*”  
> —fol. 8v.

Gloss: “Think, if you can, of all the most atrocious torments and harshest punishments inflicted on human beings by tyrants since the creation of the world … yet you will find none equal to even the least punishment of Hell, whether of sense or of loss.”

And:

> “*Idcirco qualibet hac in pictura data poena, damnatis quaedam singulis, sed longe maior erit excogitanda.*”  
> —fol. 8v.

Gloss: “Therefore, whatever punishment is assigned to each of the damned in this picture, one far greater must be imagined.”

Yates’s famous paraphrase derives from the opening description:

> “the variety of punishments, inflicted in accordance with the diverse nature of the sins, the different situations of the damned, their varying gestures, will much help memory and give many places.”

Her adverb “cheerfully” is interpretive rather than textual.

### Peripheral features

**[T]** A river or lake surrounds the infernal region. A boat driven by demons carries souls to their places of punishment. A small marked opening, *peccatores caverna*, signals a tunnel through which souls descend; Rosselli admits that the subterranean route cannot itself be fully painted:

> “*attamen expostulabat ratio, ut illorum infelicium animarum ad Infernum descensus, qui pingi non poterat, hoc signo inspectoribus declaretur.*”  
> —fols. 11r–v.

Gloss: “Reason nevertheless required that the descent of those unhappy souls into Hell, which could not be painted, should be indicated to viewers by this sign.”

### Moral use

**[T]** The section closes with an address to the living viewer:

> “*Quaeso vos omnes lectores inspectoresque viventes: ad inferni profunda, cogitatu vestro descendite, poenasque universas perpendite …*”  
> —fol. 11v.

Gloss: “I beg all you living readers and viewers: descend in your thought to the depths of Hell, and weigh all its punishments …”

Thus the map has a double function:

- mnemonic storage;
- devotional and penitential admonition.

### Plate 7a / fol. 12r

**[T]** The woodcut reproduced by Yates as plate 7a is a roughly circular, diagrammatic Hell:

- a dark central well with Lucifer and flames;
- four concentric ring-steps carrying the four doctrinal categories;
- an outer annulus divided radially into seven vice-sectors;
- a surrounding river;
- a demon-driven boat;
- a small lateral cave or tunnel marker;
- labels printed directly into the architecture.

**[R]** Kuwakino emphasizes that Lucifer is depicted only schematically; the prose orders the practitioner to transform that crude mark into a smoky, flaming, active prince of demons.

## 6. The elementary and celestial universe

Hell is not an isolated “memory palace.” It begins an ascent through the whole created universe.

### Elementary world

**[T]** The diagram following the infernal material arranges the sublunary world in curved, amphitheatrical bands. It moves through regions of earth, water, air, and fire, with distinctions of purity, mixture, and position. The lowest band lies immediately above or around Hell.

### Celestial spheres

**[T]** Rosselli then supplies the ordered celestial spheres and constellations as places. The zodiac provides twelve familiar divisions; individual constellations and their parts can support further loci.

### Metrodorus of Scepsis

**[T/Tr]** Rosselli cites Metrodorus in association with a zodiacal system.

**[Tr/Disputed]** The claim descends through ancient testimony—especially Quintilian, *Institutio oratoria* XI.2.22—that Metrodorus found 360 places in the twelve signs through which the sun travels. Quintilian himself calls the achievement vanity and ostentation. No work by Metrodorus explaining the system survives; neither Rosselli nor modern historians can reconstruct its exact operation from primary Greek evidence.

The relevant Quintilian passage reads:

> “*Quo magis miror, quomodo Metrodorus in XII signis per quae sol meat trecenos et sexagenos invenerit locos. Vanitas nimirum fuit atque iactatio …*”

Gloss: “I therefore wonder all the more how Metrodorus found three hundred and sixty places in the twelve signs through which the sun travels. It was surely vanity and boasting.” [Perseus, Quintilian XI.2](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A2007.01.0068%3Abook%3D11%3Achapter%3D2)

Rosselli’s constellation places are documented; their direct descent from an actual Metrodoran technique is traditional, not demonstrable.

## 7. Paradise as artificial memory

### Location and sequence

**[T]** Paradise is introduced across fols. 29v–37r; its first large plate occurs at fol. 37v. Additional explanatory material runs through approximately fol. 51r, where the scheme appears again.

### Architecture

**[T]** The Empyrean is imagined as a circular or amphitheatrical celestial enclosure surrounded by a wall glittering with precious stones. Its center is the throne of Christ. Below and around it are ranked:

- the highest angelic orders;
- other angelic hierarchies;
- Apostles;
- Patriarchs;
- Prophets;
- Martyrs;
- Confessors;
- Virgins;
- Holy Hebrews;
- Innocents and Virtues in inner ranks;
- the innumerable multitude of saints.

The precise order is distributed between the diagram and the accompanying prose; some compressed labels are not fully intelligible without the explanation.

### Constructing the inhabitants

**[T]** The practitioner must form the places through art, rehearsal, and intense imagination; Christ’s throne should be envisioned so as to move the senses and excite memory. The spirits may be pictured:

> “as painters paint them.”

This direction is crucial. Rosselli does not demand an esoterically revealed angelic form. Conventional Catholic visual culture supplies serviceable images.

### Plate 7b / fols. 37v and c. 51r

**[T]** Yates’s plate 7b shows:

- a gem-walled enclosing circle;
- Christ’s throne at the center or apex of the inward hierarchy;
- concentric or stepped ranks;
- angelic labels in the inner celestial region;
- classes of saints distributed in outer sectors;
- compressed inscriptions such as *Apostoli*, *Patriarchae*, *Prophetae*, *Martyres*, *Confessores*, and *Virgines*.

**[Y]** Yates remarks:

> “There is nothing at all unusual about Rosellius’s Paradise, except that it is classed as ‘artificial memory’.”

That sentence understates the technical novelty. The iconography is conventional, but its reclassification as a reusable, measured hierarchy of mnemonic locations is precisely what makes it unusual as a cognitive instrument.

---

## 8. The mnemonic verses

### Nicolaus Alexius of Perugia

**[T/D]** The verses are attributed to:

> “*R.P.M. Nicolai Alexii Perusini Ordinis Praedicatorum, commissarii S. Inquisitionis*.”

That is, Reverend Father Master Nicolaus Alexius of Perugia, Dominican, commissioner of the Holy Inquisition.

The poems begin at:

- fol. 13r: Hell;
- later preliminary leaves: elements;
- fol. 27v: the heavens.

They turn spatial order into metrical/verbal order. Verse and diagram provide parallel retrieval paths: if the image-sequence falters, rhythm and verbal sequence can restore it, and vice versa.

### Orthodoxy

**[Y]** Yates writes:

> “These ‘carmina’ by an Inquisitor give an impressive air of great orthodoxy to the artificial memory.”

This is fair as an observation about presentation but remains interpretation. Alexius’s office does not by itself constitute formal ecclesiastical approval, an imprimatur, or a judgment on every mnemonic technique in the book.

**[D]** The convent chronicle says that “Master Nicolaus Alegius” had rendered Rosselli’s work into “most beautiful verses.” This independently confirms his collaboration.

## 9. Actual places: churches, convents, and houses

**[T]** Rosselli recommends familiar religious and domestic structures. A practitioner may mentally walk:

- into a church;
- past successive altars and chapels;
- around corners;
- through choir, sacristy, cloister, refectory, dormitory, or cells;
- through a house from vestibule into rooms;
- along known roads or urban circuits.

**[R]** The religious examples are institutionally significant. Rosselli had been a subprior, novice master, and confessor to nuns; churches and convents were not abstract examples but the spaces of his professional life.

**Absence of evidence:** no surviving record has been identified showing which particular convent Rosselli used as his personal mnemonic route.

---

## 10. Humans, animals, and trees as places

### Human bodies

**[T]** Rosselli treats the human figure as a *locus minimus* whose parts support subsidiary images. A stable traversal can proceed through head, forehead, eyes, ears, mouth, neck, shoulders, arms, hands, fingers, chest, abdomen, thighs, knees, legs, and feet.

This permits recursive storage: “a man” occupies one location in a larger building, while the man’s body itself provides many smaller locations.

### Alphabetical persons

**[T]** Lists include:

- philosophers;
- learned men;
- physicians;
- poets;
- men renowned for bodily strength;
- wealthy persons.

They are organized alphabetically and can function as places or figures.

### Animals and trees

**[T]** Animals and trees are likewise alphabetized. Their visible parts, attributes, names, habitats, and actions permit them to operate either as repositories or signs.

**[R]** This apparatus is encyclopedic but not yet a “universal language” in the seventeenth-century sense. It supplies a conventional image lexicon; it does not provide a logically exhaustive taxonomy comparable to Dalgarno or Wilkins.

---

## 11. Workshops and professions

**[T]** Rosselli gives alphabetized workshops or trades as intermediate-scale places. A workshop is useful because it already contains distinctive tools, actions, products, and practitioners. A smithy can hold images by forge, anvil, hammer, fire, bellows, and smith; other crafts are treated analogously.

The book even allows vernacular names for such workshops. That admission shows that effective mnemonic association takes precedence over an exclusively Latin scholarly vocabulary.

---

## 12. Visual, object, and gestural alphabets

### Shaped-letter alphabet

**[T]** Rosselli prints objects whose contours resemble letters: crescents, tools, croziers, limbs, and other familiar forms. This material derives substantially from the visual-alphabet tradition represented by Romberch.

### Initial-letter alphabet

**[T]** A word may be decomposed into letters represented by objects or animals beginning with the same letter. Rosselli’s famous example is:

> “*si pro A ponam Asinum, pro E Elephantem, pro R Rhinocerontem*”  
> —fol. 119v.

Gloss: “if for A I put an ass, for E an elephant, and for R a rhinoceros.”

Thus *AER* is memorized by an ass, elephant, and rhinoceros.

### Manual alphabet

**[T]** Several woodcuts show hands and fingers disposed to signify letters. Bolzoni reproduces it as “the silent alphabet of sign language.”

**[Disputed wording]** Booksellers and popular accounts often call this “the earliest known representation of a digital sign language.” That is too strong unless carefully qualified:

- the figures are genuinely early printed manual-letter forms;
- “digital” means finger-based, not electronic;
- they are not proof of a fully developed natural sign language;
- the claim to absolute priority depends on what counts as fingerspelling, monastic signs, chironomy, or a manual alphabet.

It is safer to call Rosselli’s plates **among the earliest printed systematic manual alphabets used for mnemonic or cryptographic purposes**.

### Cryptography

**[T]** Rosselli’s alphabets can support concealed or silent communication as well as private mnemonic substitution. Yet the work is not principally a cipher manual. Its encoding procedures remain subordinate to memory.

---

## 13. Famous persons as figures for arts and sciences

At fol. 113r Rosselli recommends an art’s most celebrated practitioner as its image:

> “For Grammar, Lorenzo Valla or Priscian; for Rhetoric, Marcus Tullius; for Dialectic Aristotle, and also for Philosophy; for Theology Plato; for Painting Phidias or Zeuxis; for Astrology Atlas, Zoroaster, or Ptolemy; for Geometry Archimedes; for Music Apollo or Orpheus …”

The Latin varies in case and abbreviation across the list, but this is the substance of Rosselli’s catalogue.

### How it works

**[T]** The relationship is metonymic rather than pictorial:

- Cicero signifies rhetoric because he is its paradigmatic practitioner.
- Archimedes signifies geometry through historical fame.
- Orpheus signifies music through mythic authority.
- Atlas signifies astrology or astronomy through iconographic convention.

The person can then be furnished with tools, inscriptions, gestures, companions, or objects encoding subdivisions of the discipline.

### “Magia”

**[T]** Pythagoras and Zoroaster appear in the list associated with *Magia*.

**[Y]** Yates writes that *Magia* “has moved up into the virtues” and takes this as evidence that the Dominican tradition is moving toward Renaissance occultism.

**Correction:** The text documents the label and the exemplary figures; it does not by that fact endorse demonic magic, talismanic operations, astral invocation, or Hermetic religion. Early-modern *magia* could mean ancient wisdom, natural philosophy, knowledge of occult properties, astrology, illicit conjuration, or several of these depending on context. Yates’s phrase compresses distinctions Rosselli does not explicitly resolve.

## 14. Worked examples and what is not Rosselli’s

### Rosselli’s own compact examples

The most securely identifiable examples include:

- *AER* encoded by ass–elephant–rhinoceros, fol. 119v;
- disciplines encoded by famous practitioners, fol. 113r;
- human, animal, and arboreal bodies subdivided into loci;
- detailed infernal and paradisal places;
- applications in the final chapter, fols. 132v onward.

Rosselli’s book contains examples of applying figures to words and things, but unlike the *Ad Herennium*, it does not organize its exposition around one canonical courtroom tableau.

### The lawsuit image

**[T-source ancestor, not Rosselli’s invention]** The famous lawsuit image belongs to the anonymous *Rhetorica ad Herennium* III.20.33. A prosecutor wishes to remember that a defendant killed a man by poison for an inheritance and that witnesses and accessories were involved. He imagines the sick victim in bed; the accused stands nearby holding a cup in the right hand, tablets in the left, and a ram’s testicles on the fourth finger. The cup signifies poison, tablets the will or inheritance, testicles the witnesses through a Latin sound association.

The Latin begins:

> “*Reum ad lectum eius adstituemus, dextra poculum, sinistra tabulas, medico testiculos arietinos tenentem …*”

Because manuscript texts and modern punctuation vary, the passage should be cited by section rather than isolated wording. [Latin and Caplan translation, *Ad Herennium* III.16–24](https://www.laits.utexas.edu/memoria/Ad_Herennium_Passages.html); [Latin, Teubner 1923 text](https://la.wikisource.org/wiki/Rhetorica_ad_Herennium/Liber_III)

Rosselli inherits the same operational principles—compression, action, objects, bodily placement, and phonetic association—but the lawsuit tableau is not evidence of a worked Rossellian demonstration unless he explicitly reproduces it in a passage not yet critically collated.

### Camillo’s grades and Bruno’s wheels

These are comparisons, not components of Rosselli’s own system:

- Camillo’s seven planetary grades structure a theatre of universal knowledge.
- Bruno’s rotating combinatorial wheels multiply lettered or imaged terms.
- Rosselli prints diagrams and circular cosmographies, but no evidence shows that his principal diagrams were physically rotating volvelles.

Calling Rosselli’s diagrams “wheels” without qualification risks importing Bruno or Lull into a largely static hierarchy.

## 2. Evidence for authorship and publication

A chronicle of the Dominican convent of San Marco supplies the strongest biographical documentation:

> “*Frater Cosmas Rossellius, filius huius conventus, vir fuit devotissimus et maximo ingenio praeditus, nec non et singulari prudentia, sed valetudinarius … Composuit autem opus … insigne de memoria artificiali seu locali, sed morte preventus in lucem edere non potuit. Huic autem defectui supplevit … germanus eius, frater Damianus Rossellus.*”

Gloss: “Brother Cosma Rosselli, a son of this convent, was a very devout man, endowed with great ability and singular prudence, but sickly … He composed a distinguished work on artificial or local memory but, prevented by death, could not bring it to light. His brother, Fra Damiano Rosselli, supplied this deficiency.”

The chronicle adds that Rosselli died at Santa Maria ad Gradus in Viterbo on **26 July 1575**, aged forty, after receiving the sacraments. [San Marco chronicle transcription](https://www.e-theca.net/emiliopanella/cronica3/mrc417.htm)

This corrects several catalogue errors:

- **[D]** Rosselli was born about 1535, not 1439.
- **[D]** He died in 1575, not 1578.
- **[Disputed/error]** Records giving 1439–1507 conflate him with the Florentine painter Cosimo Rosselli.
- **[D]** Fra Damiano Rosselli was his biological brother and posthumous editor, not co-author in the ordinary sense.
- **[D]** Nicolaus Alexius supplied verses.
- **[D]** The work’s publication was posthumous.

The title-page dedication is to Cardinal Vincenzo Giustiniani, a former master general of the Dominican Order. It situates the publication inside Dominican institutional patronage.

## 3. Classical sources

### Anonymous *Rhetorica ad Herennium*

Standard citation: III.16.28–24.40.

Principal modern edition:

- Friedrich Marx, ed., *Rhetorica ad Herennium*, Leipzig: Teubner, 1923.
- Harry Caplan, ed. and trans., *Rhetorica ad Herennium*, Loeb Classical Library 403, Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press, 1954.

Key precepts:

> “*Artificiosa memoria constat ex locis et imaginibus.*”

“Artificial memory consists of places and images.”

> “*Locos appellamus eos qui breviter, perfecte, insignite aut natura aut manu sunt absoluti … Imagines sunt formae quaedam et notae et simulacra eius rei quam meminisse volumus.*”

“We call places those backgrounds completed briefly, distinctly, and conspicuously by nature or art … Images are forms, marks, and representations of the thing we wish to remember.”

The work’s medieval attribution to Cicero is false. Rosselli nevertheless treats the *Ad Herennium* as Ciceronian, as Romberch did.

### Cicero, *De oratore*

Standard citation: II.86.351–88.360.

Standard Loeb:

- E. W. Sutton and H. Rackham, eds. and trans., *De oratore*, 2 vols., Loeb Classical Library 348–49.

Cicero supplies the Simonides story and the wax-tablet analogy:

> places are like wax tablets; images are like letters written upon them.

### Quintilian, *Institutio oratoria*

Standard citation: XI.2.1–51.

Standard modern edition:

- Donald A. Russell, ed. and trans., *The Orator’s Education*, 5 vols., Loeb Classical Library 124–27, 494, 2001.

Quintilian’s practical summary states:

> “*Opus est ergo locis, quae vel finguntur vel sumuntur, et imaginibus vel simulacris, quae utique fingenda sunt.*”

“We therefore need places, either invented or taken from reality, and images or likenesses, which must be fashioned.”

He describes assigning the first topic to the vestibule, the second to the atrium, then traversing impluvia, rooms, niches, and statues in order. [Perseus text](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A2007.01.0068%3Abook%3D11%3Achapter%3D2)

## 4. Medieval and Renaissance antecedents

### Albert the Great and Thomas Aquinas

**[T-source tradition/R]** The Dominican integration of artificial memory into prudence descends from Albert’s *De bono* and Aquinas’s *Summa theologiae* II–II, q. 49, a. 1. Aquinas recommends:

1. appropriate but unusual sensible likenesses;
2. orderly arrangement;
3. anxious or affectionate attachment;
4. frequent meditation.

Rosselli’s vivid torments, hierarchy, rehearsal, and affect correspond closely to these precepts.

### Boncompagno da Signa

His *Rhetorica novissima* and memory discussions already move mnemonic practice toward moral, religious, and visionary images.

### Jacobus Publicius

*Ars memorativa*, printed with his rhetorical works in Venice in 1482, includes cosmic and diagrammatic mnemonic places and visual alphabets.

### Peter of Ravenna

*Phoenix, sive artificiosa memoria*, first printed Venice, 1491/92, popularized a portable, personal art of places and images.

### Johannes Romberch

*Congestorium artificiose memorie*, Venice, 1520; expanded edition 1533. This is Rosselli’s closest printed predecessor:

- Dominican authorship;
- classical authorities;
- physical places;
- bodily loci;
- visual alphabets;
- encyclopedic lists;
- Thomist justification.

Rosselli expands Romberch’s apparatus into a more fully illustrated Christian cosmos.

### Lodovico Dolce

*Dialogo nel quale si ragiona del modo di accrescere et conservar la memoria*, Venice, 1562, translates and adapts much Romberchian material into Italian. Dolce recommends Hell, Limbo, Purgatory, and contemporary painted mythologies as image resources.

## 5. Translations and modern editions

**Absence finding:** No complete modern critical edition or complete published English translation of Rosselli’s *Thesaurus* was located.

Available access therefore consists of:

- facsimiles of the 1579 Latin edition;
- digitized page images;
- OCR of variable reliability;
- selected translations and quotations in Yates, Bolzoni, Keller-Dall’Asta, Kuwakino, and related scholarship;
- modern facsimile reprints without a critical apparatus.

This is a significant scholarly lacuna. Any definitive account still requires direct collation of multiple 1579 copies.

## Yates’s account and the scholarship since

## 1. Yates’s presentation

### Chapter V: a late Dominican memory manual

**[Y]** Yates places Rosselli beside Romberch as one of the two principal Dominican writers who made artificial memory publicly available during the sixteenth century. She treats both as transmitters of a medieval, Thomist, “Dantesque” tradition.

Her summary accurately identifies:

- the eleven major infernal regions;
- the central well;
- heretics, Jews, idolaters, and hypocrites;
- seven deadly sins;
- punishments and gestures as mnemonic material;
- Paradise’s gem wall, throne, angelic orders, and saints;
- zodiacal places and Metrodorus;
- Nicolaus Alexius’s verses;
- actual ecclesiastical buildings;
- the human body as a place;
- the visual alphabet.

### Decline thesis

Yates then characterizes later manuals as increasingly trivial:

> “The memory rules become more and more detailed; alphabetical lists and visual alphabets encourage trivial elaborations.”

She calls some of their advice childish games or a kind of crossword puzzle for idle cloister hours.

**[R/correction]** Later scholarship has generally rejected this as too teleological. Rosselli’s alphabets and lists are better understood within:

- contemporary information management;
- sermon composition;
- emblematic literacy;
- inventories and commonplace books;
- technical description;
- embodied cognition;
- the interaction of print and internal memory.

Bolzoni in particular shows that mnemonic images were not a decaying remnant displaced by print; print generated new relations among text, diagram, emblem, gallery, and memory.

### Chapter VII: exemplary practitioners

Yates quotes the list of Valla, Cicero, Aristotle, Plato, Phidias, Zeuxis, Atlas, Zoroaster, Ptolemy, Archimedes, Apollo, and Orpheus.

She then asks:

> “Are we now looking at Raphael’s ‘School of Athens’ as useful for memory?”

**[Y/speculation]** This is a suggestive analogy, not evidence that Raphael’s fresco was designed as a Rossellian memory system or that Rosselli used it. The general convention of placing an art beneath or beside its exemplary practitioner is well documented; a specific genetic connection is not.

### “Magia has moved up into the virtues”

**[Y]** Yates treats Rosselli’s association of Pythagoras and Zoroaster with *Magia* as a sign of movement toward the Hermetic memory of Camillo and Bruno.

**Correction:** Rosselli’s book remains structurally closer to orthodox rhetorical and Dominican memory than to Bruno’s astral magic. The appearance of *Magia* is evidence of Renaissance classificatory permeability, not proof that the *Thesaurus* is itself a magical operation.

---

## 2. Paolo Rossi

Rossi’s *Clavis universalis* appeared in 1960, before Yates’s book, and was translated as *Logic and the Art of Memory: The Quest for a Universal Language*.

His larger contribution is to shift the history from occult biography toward transformations in logic, classification, combinatorics, encyclopedism, and universal language. Rosselli occupies the textual-memory branch that later interacts with:

- Lullian combination;
- Ramist method;
- encyclopedic tables;
- seventeenth-century universal-language projects.

Rossi does not establish a direct Rosselli-to-Leibniz line. The defensible genealogy is a network: mnemonic classification and Lullian combinatorics both enter early-modern projects of method; Leibniz selectively transforms these materials into character and calculus.

---

## 3. Mary Carruthers

Carruthers’s *The Book of Memory* and *The Craft of Thought* revise the framework for medieval memory.

Her principal correction to Yates is methodological:

- medieval memory is not merely rote storage;
- it is a compositional and ethical craft;
- images are instruments of invention, meditation, judgment, and affect;
- architectural and bodily ordering are forms of thought.

Applied to Rosselli, this makes Hell and Paradise not simply repositories but “machines for thinking” devotionally and rhetorically. Rosselli’s system is late Renaissance in date but preserves the medieval monastic practice of making mental structures in which thought can be recomposed.

Carruthers does not, however, supply a full critical reconstruction of Rosselli’s text.

---

## 4. Lina Bolzoni

Bolzoni’s *The Gallery of Memory* places Rosselli within the interaction between literary and visual culture in the age of print. Her corrections are especially important:

- diagrams and alphabets are not merely degeneration;
- the printed image becomes a device for teaching the reader to manufacture a more elaborate inward image;
- memory treatises participate in emblem books, galleries, encyclopedic theaters, rhetorical machines, and image-text experiments;
- forgetting itself can be represented through violent clearing of images.

Bolzoni reproduces Rosselli’s manual alphabet and treats his sign terminology as evidence for increasingly explicit semiotic reflection.

---

## 5. Barbara Keller-Dall’Asta

*Heilsplan und Gedächtnis: Zur Mnemologie des 16. Jahrhunderts in Italien* gives the fullest sustained modern discussion specifically relevant to Rosselli, pp. 149–184.

Its organizing insight is the relation between mnemonic order and Christian *Heilsplan*, the history and structure of salvation. Rosselli’s cosmos is not accidentally Christian decoration attached to a neutral technique: salvation history supplies the macro-order of the mnemonic universe.

This corrects readings that isolate the Hell diagram as a bizarre curiosity.

---

## 6. Koji Kuwakino

Kuwakino’s *L’architetto sapiente* treats gardens, theaters, and cities as mnemonic architecture; pp. 243–84 discuss Rosselli. His 2024 article gives the most exact recent analysis of the infernal construction.

His principal contributions are:

- identifying Rosselli’s prose as technical ekphrasis;
- separating the stages of verbal construction, plate, and post-plate explanation;
- showing that the image is intentionally incomplete;
- emphasizing recursive interchange between place and figure;
- reading the author’s grammar—subjunctives, imperatives, and first-person plurals—as collaborative construction;
- comparing Rosselli’s totalized mental architecture with Fludd.

Kuwakino also corrects Yates’s impression of merely cumbersome accumulation: the abundance is functional, training the practitioner to build and animate a precise inner world.

---

## 7. Small, Hajdu, and Volkmann

- Jocelyn Penny Small’s *Wax Tablets of the Mind* emphasizes the differences between ancient oral-visual cognition and modern metaphors of storage. It cautions against treating ancient and Renaissance mnemonic systems as literal proto-computers.
- Helga Hajdu’s *Das mnemotechnische Schrifttum des Mittelalters* (1936) provided Yates with a foundational bibliography and medieval textual history.
- Ludwig Volkmann’s *Ars memorativa* (1937) reproduced numerous diagrams, including faculty-psychology and alphabetic material. Yates cites Volkmann’s plate 172 in connection with head diagrams.

These works contextualize Rosselli but do not replace direct reading of the 1579 edition.

---

## 8. Ong and Ramism

Walter J. Ong’s *Ramus, Method, and the Decay of Dialogue* established the importance of Ramist dichotomizing diagrams and curricular method.

Rosselli and Ramus represent competing but overlapping strategies:

- Rosselli: sensory images attached to reusable places;
- Ramus: dialectical disposition and branching order;
- both: visible organization, segmentation, and retrieval.

William Perkins’s *Antidicsonus* opposes the “vain” local memory associated with Metrodorus, Rosselli, Bruno (“Nolanus”), and Alexander Dicson to Ramist logical disposition. Yates’s account at p. 267 is correct in identifying Rosselli as a named polemical representative of image-memory, though not as a personal participant in the controversy.

---

## 9. Lull scholarship: Bonner and Fidora

Anthony Bonner and Alexander Fidora have substantially corrected older presentations of Ramon Llull.

Their work shows that:

- Llull’s Art is not originally a mnemonic imitation of classical loci;
- it is a combinatorial, demonstrative, missionary, and theological method grounded in divine dignities and relational principles;
- its figures are logical-operational, not simply storage wheels;
- later Renaissance thinkers conflated Lullism with memory arts.

Accordingly, Rosselli should not be described as straightforwardly “Lullian.” His cosmological circles are static mnemonic divisions, whereas Llull’s figures support systematic combination and demonstration.

The direct bridge is later reception. Tomaso Garzoni proposed combining Rosselli with Llull; Bruno and seventeenth-century encyclopedists operated in an environment where the two traditions were increasingly cross-read.

Relevant research infrastructure: [Ramon Llull Database, Universitat de Barcelona](https://orbita.bib.ub.edu/llull/).

---

## 10. Bruno scholarship: Sturlese, Ciliberto, Clucas, Gatti

Modern Bruno scholarship has complicated Yates’s “Hermetic magician” synthesis:

- Rita Sturlese’s philological work reconstructs Bruno’s sources and textual chronology.
- Michele Ciliberto stresses Bruno’s philosophy, infinity, civic-religious project, and polemics.
- Stephen Clucas distinguishes varieties of memory, logic, magic, and cognition rather than subsuming them under Hermeticism.
- Hilary Gatti situates Bruno in Renaissance philosophy, science, and politics.

For Rosselli the implication is negative but important: similarities to Bruno—cosmic scope, named inventors, circular diagrams, *umbrae*, Pythagoras, Zoroaster—do not demonstrate shared operative magic. Bruno’s rotating and combinatorial image systems transform materials that Rosselli deploys in a conventional local memory.

The Italian National Institute for Renaissance Studies’ “ideal library” records Rosselli as a source relevant to Bruno’s *De scrutinio specierum* and cites *Thesaurus* II.9, fol. 119r–v. [Biblioteca ideale di Giordano Bruno](https://bibliotecaideale.insr.it/23DeSpecierumScrutinioFonsFlowChart.php?addWorkTitle=yes)

## History and context

## 1. Chronology

### Ancient foundations

- Fifth century BCE: Simonides story; historical core uncertain.
- First century BCE: anonymous *Ad Herennium* systematizes places and images.
- First century BCE: Cicero transmits Simonides and the wax-tablet analogy.
- First century CE: Quintilian describes and criticizes extreme forms.

### Scholastic and mendicant transformation

- Thirteenth century: Albert the Great and Thomas Aquinas place artificial memory under prudence.
- Dominican and Franciscan preaching culture adapts mnemonic images to virtues, vices, salvation, Scripture, and sermon organization.
- Dante’s ordered afterworld emerges from the same broad culture of ethical division, scholastic hierarchy, pilgrimage, and visualized punishment.

### Print culture

- 1482: Publicius.
- 1491/92: Peter of Ravenna.
- 1520/1533: Romberch.
- 1550: Camillo’s *Idea del theatro* published posthumously.
- 1562: Dolce.
- 1575: Rosselli dies.
- 1579: *Thesaurus* published by his brother.
- 1582 onward: Bruno’s major mnemonic works.
- 1592: Gesualdo’s *Plutosofia*.
- 1610: Schenkel’s *Gazophylacium*.
- 1617–21: Fludd’s encyclopedic works.
- Seventeenth century: mnemonic, Lullian, Ramist, encyclopedic, and universal-language systems increasingly interact.

---

## 2. Dominican institutional setting

**[D]** Rosselli was a member of the Florentine Dominican convent of San Marco. The convent chronicle calls him:

- devout;
- intellectually gifted;
- prudent;
- chronically ill;
- subprior in several places;
- novice master;
- confessor to nuns.

He did not become prior because of ill health.

These offices explain the likely audiences and functions of the work:

- preachers;
- confessors;
- novices;
- theologians;
- religious educators.

The title page broadens the market to philosophers, physicians, jurists, orators, procurators, merchants, and lovers of letters. This is simultaneously a pedagogical claim and commercial positioning.

---

## 3. Franciscan and Lullian environments

Rosselli was Dominican, not Franciscan. Llull was a lay Franciscan tertiary in traditional accounts, though the exact institutional characterization of his status requires care. The key historical point is that mendicant intellectual networks cultivated:

- preaching;
- disputation;
- doctrinal organization;
- missionary argument;
- meditation;
- highly structured pedagogical aids.

Rosselli’s immediate method is Dominican-rhetorical. Lullism belongs to a neighboring combinatorial tradition that later authors tried to join to local memory.

---

## 4. Courts and academies

Rosselli’s publication belongs to Venetian print culture and is dedicated upward within Dominican hierarchy, not chiefly to a secular prince. In contrast:

- Camillo sought Francis I’s patronage;
- Bruno dedicated mnemonic books to Henry III and circulated through courts and universities;
- emblematic and encyclopedic memory flourished in academies.

Rosselli’s work nevertheless entered this broader courtly and learned market through print.

---

## 5. Universities and curricula

Memory was traditionally the fourth or fifth part of rhetoric, depending on the enumerative convention: invention, disposition, style, memory, delivery. It also belonged to:

- Aristotelian psychology;
- ethics through prudence;
- dialectic and method;
- preaching;
- legal and medical education.

Ramist reform challenged local-image memory by claiming that logical disposition supplies the proper order of recall. Rosselli’s 1579 book appeared at precisely the period when sensory mnemonic encyclopedism and anti-image method competed.

---

## 6. Dante and Florentine visual culture

### Dante

**[R]** Yates identifies Rosselli’s Hell as “Dantesque,” and Kuwakino describes it as inspired by Dante’s journey. The resemblances are real:

- descent and ascent;
- graded punishments;
- sin-specific topography;
- a journey through a moral cosmos;
- visual and affective intensity.

**[Disputed]** Rosselli’s eleven-part circular diagram is not a diagrammatic transcription of Dante’s nine circles. It combines doctrinal categories, capital sins, late-medieval iconography, and mnemonic requirements. “Dantesque” is an interpretive family resemblance, not a source citation proving direct copying.

### Nardo di Cione

Yates juxtaposes Rosselli’s Hell with Nardo di Cione’s fresco in the Strozzi Chapel, Santa Maria Novella. The fresco provides a plausible Florentine visual background of graded punishments and densely populated infernal space.

**[Y/R]** No document proves that Rosselli designed his plate from Nardo’s fresco. The comparison is contextual.

### Santa Maria Novella’s *Triumph of Saint Thomas*

The Spanish Chapel fresco places exemplary authorities under personifications of sciences. It is a strong visual analogue to Rosselli’s use of Cicero for rhetoric or Aristotle for dialectic.

Again, general tradition is documented; direct copying is unproved.

## People

## 1. Cosma Rosselli

**[D]** Born about 1535; died at Viterbo, 26 July 1575, aged forty. Florentine Dominican of San Marco. Subprior, novice master, confessor, invalid. Author of an unpublished-at-death work on artificial/local memory.

**Not the painter:** He must not be confused with Cosimo di Lorenzo Rosselli (1439/40–1507), the painter represented in Vasari.

## 2. Fra Damiano Rosselli

**[D]** Cosma’s brother and fellow Dominican. He arranged posthumous publication, composed or transmitted the dedication, and answered demand for the work. He died in 1593 according to the convent chronicle.

## 3. Nicolaus Alexius / Alegius of Perugia

**[T/D]** Dominican master and commissioner of the Holy Inquisition. Author of the mnemonic *carmina* on Hell, elements, and heavens.

## 4. Cardinal Vincenzo Giustiniani

**[T/D]** Dedicatee and former master general of the Dominican Order. His presence marks the book’s institutional orthodoxy and patronage strategy.

## 5. Johannes Romberch

Rosselli’s most important printed predecessor. Like Rosselli, a Dominican compiler and transmitter of classical, scholastic, bodily, architectural, and alphabetic mnemonic techniques.

## 6. Tomaso Garzoni

**[Y/T in Garzoni]** Garzoni announced an ambition to unite Rosselli’s artificial memory with Llull’s Art. This documents Rosselli’s reception as a standard authority soon after publication. It does not prove that Garzoni completed a functioning synthesis.

## 7. Giordano Bruno

Bruno’s systems were chronologically close and shared vocabulary and source traditions with Rosselli. Later polemicists grouped them, but no evidence found establishes Rosselli as Bruno’s teacher or associate.

## 8. Alexander Dicson and William Perkins

Dicson transmitted Bruno-inspired memory in England. Perkins’s *Antidicsonus* grouped Dicson, “Nolanus,” Metrodorus, and Rosselli as proponents of vain local-image arts opposed to Ramist method.

Rosselli was already dead; this is textual reception, not a personal quarrel.

## 9. Robert Fludd

**[R]** Fludd’s cosmic and theatrical memory can be productively compared with Rosselli. Kuwakino sees Fludd as clarifying how resident figures might interact with transient mnemonic images. Direct copying must be established passage by passage and should not be assumed from resemblance alone.

## Anecdotes: documented, inherited, and absent

### Simonides and the collapsed banquet hall

**[Tr/Legend]** Ancient sources relate that Simonides identified crushed banquet guests from their seating positions after Castor and Pollux called him outside. The story explains the discovery of ordered loci. It is ancient testimony, not independently verifiable biography.

### The auctioneer or auction memory

**[Tr]** Quintilian XI.2.24 says that people reportedly remembered, after an auction, what was sold to each buyer, supported by bankers’ records; Quintus Hortensius was credited with such an accomplishment:

> “*Et forsitan hoc sunt adiuti qui auctione dimissa quid cuique vendidissent … reddiderunt, quod praestitisse Q. Hortensium dicunt.*”

This is not an anecdote about Rosselli.

### “The professor’s party”

**Absence finding:** No securely sourced Rosselli anecdote corresponding to a professor demonstrating memory at a party was located. Such stories cluster around later itinerant mnemonists, Camillo, Lambert Schenkel, and nineteenth-century lecturers. Assigning one to Rosselli would be unsupported.

### “The student who lost his memory”

**Absence finding:** No identified contemporary source connects this anecdote to Rosselli. Stories of students damaged by excessive mnemonic practice occur in broader pedagogical polemic, especially humanist and Ramist attacks, but must not be silently transferred to him.

### “The accused magician”

**Absence finding:** No evidence found that Rosselli was accused of magic, prosecuted, denounced to the Inquisition, imprisoned, or censured.

Several other memory practitioners were suspected of occultism or imposture; Bruno was tried and executed for a complex set of theological propositions. Those histories cannot be used as evidence against Rosselli.

## The magic question

## 1. Is the *Thesaurus* magical?

### What is documented

Rosselli includes:

- cosmic and zodiacal places;
- Pythagoras and Zoroaster under *Magia*;
- terms such as *idola*, *umbrae*, and *simulacra* among names for images;
- celestial and supercelestial figures;
- a faculty-psychology diagram;
- visual alphabets and silent gestures.

None of those features alone constitutes operative magic.

### What is absent

The *Thesaurus* does not appear to prescribe:

- invocation of spirits;
- consecrated talismans;
- planetary suffumigations;
- magical seals intended to compel beings;
- necromancy;
- angelic conjuration;
- demonic pacts;
- astrological timing of image manufacture;
- *Ars notoria* prayers or ritual figures promising infused knowledge.

### Best classification

**[R]** The system is primarily:

- rhetorical;
- scholastic;
- devotional;
- encyclopedic;
- semiotic;
- pedagogical.

It borders Renaissance occult discourse through cosmology, exemplary magi, astrology, and “shadows,” but its actual operations remain orthodox local memory.

---

## 2. Yates’s Hermetic reading

Yates’s overarching thesis held that medieval artificial memory was transformed in the Renaissance through Ficinian and Hermetic magic, reaching Camillo, Bruno, and Fludd. Rosselli appears as a bridge: an orthodox Dominican manual beginning to admit modern, possibly occult material.

Later scholarship has qualified this:

- Hermeticism is one strand, not the master explanation for every circular image or cosmic scheme.
- Lullism cannot be reduced to memory or Hermeticism.
- Bruno’s memory works require close philological differentiation.
- Christian cosmological memory need not be magical.
- *Magia naturalis*, astrology, talismanic magic, ritual magic, and metaphorical “magic” are distinct categories.

For Rosselli, the evidence supports **adjacency and permeability**, not a demonstrated magical program.

---

## 3. The Inquisition

### Nicolaus Alexius

An inquisitorial commissioner supplied verse. This weighs heavily against the idea that the published work was covertly or obviously heretical.

### Formal approval

**Absence finding:** Alexius’s participation is not equivalent to a surviving formal inquisitorial examination. A complete census of approbations, licenses, and privileges in all copies remains desirable.

### Consequences

No inquisitorial consequence for author, editor, printer, or readers has been found.

---

## 4. *Ars notoria*

The *Ars notoria* promised rapid or infused mastery of disciplines through prayers, ritual figures, divine names, fasting, and visionary operations. Medieval theologians and later humanists disputed or condemned it as superstitious or demonic.

Rosselli’s method differs fundamentally:

- it demands construction, rehearsal, association, and exercise;
- its figures act cognitively, not sacramentally;
- it does not promise supernatural infusion;
- its diagrams are maps and sign-systems, not demonstrably ritual *notae*.

The shared word *notae* and the presence of diagrams do not establish derivation from the *Ars notoria*.

---

## 5. Talismanic interpretation

**[Disputed/unsupported]** Reading the Hell and Paradise plates as talismans would require evidence that their configuration was believed to channel celestial or spiritual power. Rosselli presents them as pictures to be inspected, mentally completed, and used as ordered places. No such talismanic instruction has been found.

## Afterlife

## 1. Immediate reception

### Garzoni

Garzoni’s proposed Rosselli–Llull synthesis documents Rosselli’s standing as a comprehensive memory authority.

### Bruno and Dicson

Rosselli’s procedures entered the same polemical field as Bruno’s and Dicson’s. Perkins’s hostile grouping shows recognizability across confessional and national lines.

### Schenkel

Lambert Schenkel expanded methods for constructing, filling, and violently clearing mnemonic locations. His work belongs to a practical afterlife in which Rosselli remained a possible source or comparator.

### Fludd

Fludd’s *ars rotunda* and *ars quadrata* distinguish celestial/intellectual from terrestrial/architectural places. This resembles the dual capacity of Rosselli’s cosmic and built loci, though Fludd’s system has its own metaphysics and theatrical apparatus.

---

## 2. Emblem books and visual culture

Rosselli’s sign-images belong to the same environment as:

- emblem books;
- hieroglyphic collections;
- personifications;
- imprese;
- rebuses;
- physiognomic illustrations;
- galleries of famous men;
- encyclopedic theaters.

Bolzoni’s strongest contribution is demonstrating that mnemonic images and printed images mutually transformed one another.

---

## 3. Dante, literature, and art

### Dante

Rosselli is evidence for the early-modern mnemonic usability of Dante’s moral topography, but not proof that Dante composed the *Commedia* from a formal Rossellian precursor.

### Frescoes

The fresco cycles of Hell, Paradise, virtues, vices, sciences, and famous practitioners provide a public iconographic repertoire comparable to Rosselli’s internal one. Direct dependence must be proved individually.

### Borges’s Funes

**[Modern analogy]** Borges’s “Funes the Memorious” dramatizes total, disabling memory without abstraction. It is an illuminating counter-image to Rosselli’s ordered selection, but there is no evidence Borges drew specifically on Rosselli.

### Umberto Eco

Eco discusses memory, combinatorics, perfect languages, encyclopedias, and semiotic systems. Rosselli belongs retrospectively in that intellectual archaeology. Claims of a direct influence require textual evidence and should not be assumed.

---

## 4. The modern memory-palace revival

Modern popular memory training retains the basic classical mechanism:

- familiar route;
- fixed locations;
- vivid and absurd images;
- serial mental traversal.

Rosselli’s special additions—Christian cosmic maps, taxonomic practitioners, bodies within bodies, manual alphabets—are usually absent.

Calling every modern “memory palace” Rossellian would therefore be misleading. Most modern systems descend from the shared classical method, often mediated through nineteenth- and twentieth-century manuals.

---

## 5. Memory athletes

Competitive mnemonists use:

- routes or loci;
- person–action–object systems;
- number-shape and number-rhyme codes;
- Major-system phonetics;
- image interaction;
- rapid clearing or reuse.

Functional analogies to Rosselli are strong:

- exemplary persons resemble person-based codes;
- active gestures resemble action encoding;
- nested anatomical places resemble dense routes;
- alphabetic animals resemble initial-letter encodings.

**[Modern]** No evidence suggests that contemporary athletes normally derive these techniques directly from the 1579 *Thesaurus*.

---

## 6. Computing and combinatorics

### What can legitimately be said

Rosselli’s system has features recognizable to information science:

- hierarchical addressing;
- stable storage locations;
- transient contents;
- multiple indexes;
- redundancy between image, word, verse, and spatial sequence;
- semantic typing of places;
- nested containers;
- erasure and reuse.

### What cannot

It is not a digital computer, formal programming language, database, or algorithm in the modern mathematical sense. These are useful analogies, not historical identities.

### Llull to Leibniz

The better-documented combinatorial genealogy runs:

- Llull’s finite alphabets and figures;
- Renaissance and seventeenth-century Lullism;
- mechanized or mathematized combination in Kircher and Izquierdo;
- Leibniz’s *ars combinatoria* and *characteristica universalis*.

Mnemonic classification interacted with that development. Rosselli represents the local-image and encyclopedic side, not a necessary direct ancestor of Leibniz’s calculus.

Yates herself says that memory, Lullian art, and “method” became entangled in the seventeenth century and that Leibniz supplies the most remarkable later case. This remains suggestive, but modern Lull scholarship rejects a simple linear “memory wheel → computer” story.

---

## Controversies and disputes

## 1. Identity of the author

### Error

Numerous modern catalogues and exhibition pages identify the author as “Cosimo Rosselli, 1439–1507,” the Renaissance painter.

### Documentary correction

The San Marco chronicle identifies the mnemonic author as a Dominican who died in 1575 aged forty. The title calls him a Florentine Dominican. His brother Damiano published the book.

**Verdict:** The painter attribution is false; it results from name collision.

---

## 2. Death date: 1575 or 1578?

Catalogues often give “–1578,” presumably inferred from publication preliminaries or an older authority file.

The convent chronicle gives:

> 26 July 1575, at Santa Maria ad Gradus in Viterbo, aged forty.

**Verdict:** 1575 is the stronger documented date. Authority records should be corrected unless contrary contemporary evidence emerges.

---

## 3. Authorship versus editorship

Cosma composed the work. Damiano brought it to print after Cosma’s death. Nicolaus Alexius supplied verse.

**Open textual issue:** Without an authorial manuscript, the extent of Damiano’s editorial intervention cannot be measured. Calling Damiano merely a printer’s intermediary may understate his role; calling him co-author overstates present evidence.

---

## 4. Publication date

The title page and bibliographical records give 1579.

References to 1574, 1575, 1578, or 1580 in later catalogues arise from:

- death dates;
- inventory dates;
- bookseller records;
- transcription errors;
- confusion between composition and publication.

**Verdict:** No verified separate early edition other than Venice 1579 was found.

---

## 5. “Eleven places”

Correct as the number of chief infernal subdivisions; incorrect if taken as the total number of mnemonic stations. The architecture, inhabitants, punishments, and bodies multiply loci recursively.

---

## 6. “Dantesque”

**Supported as reconstruction:** graded sin, journey, Hell-to-Paradise structure, and Florentine culture make Dante a compelling context.

**Not demonstrated:** exact derivation of Rosselli’s eleven divisions from Dante’s nine circles.

---

## 7. “Magia has become a virtue”

The textual fact is that *Magia* appears in the relevant exemplary list and receives Pythagoras and Zoroaster.

Yates’s “moved up into the virtues” is a historical interpretation. The precise taxonomy, syntax, and surrounding headings at fol. 113r should be critically edited before stronger claims are made.

---

## 8. Raphael’s *School of Athens*

Yates’s rhetorical question identifies a shared iconographic logic. No documentary evidence links Rosselli’s list to Raphael’s design. The claim is illuminating comparison, not established influence.

---

## 9. Earliest fingerspelling alphabet

Rosselli unquestionably prints early hand-letter diagrams.

The stronger “earliest known digital sign language” claim is unstable because:

- medieval monastic gesture systems predate him;
- hand mnemonics predate him;
- natural sign languages are not identical with artificial manual alphabets;
- priority depends on definition.

**Verdict:** “Among the earliest printed manual alphabets” is defensible; “first sign language” is not.

---

## 10. Phrenology

Bookseller descriptions sometimes call Rosselli’s faculty-head diagram “phrenological.”

That is anachronistic. Medieval faculty psychology located common sense, imagination, estimation, and memory in ventricular regions of the brain. Nineteenth-century phrenology inferred character from cranial protuberances.

**Verdict:** Rosselli prints a **ventricular faculty-psychology diagram**, not phrenology in the Gall–Spurzheim sense.

---

## 11. Magical or heretical status

No evidence found of magical accusation, censorship, prosecution, or heresy proceedings against Rosselli. The system’s publication by his Dominican brother, dedication to a Dominican cardinal, and verses by an inquisitorial commissioner favor an orthodox public identity.

This does not prove that every reader understood *Magia*, astrology, or image practice identically. It establishes the absence of documented consequences.

---

## 12. Influence on Bruno

There are textual and conceptual points of contact, and modern Bruno source projects register Rosselli. But broad similarity is not proof of direct dependence for every element.

**Verdict:** possible and sometimes textually traceable influence; no simple master–disciple relationship.

---

## 13. Yates’s deterioration narrative

Her description of Rosselli’s alphabetic material as trivial or childish reflects a mid-twentieth-century contrast between “great” philosophical systems and practical mnemonic detail.

Bolzoni, Carruthers, Keller-Dall’Asta, and Kuwakino show that such detail served:

- composition;
- image literacy;
- technical visualization;
- information organization;
- devotional exercise;
- controlled forgetting.

**Verdict:** substantially corrected, though Yates’s identification of Rosselli’s traditional sources remains valuable.

---

## 14. Direct line to computing

“Rosselli anticipated the computer” is a modern metaphor. The more defensible claim is that mnemonic and combinatorial arts contributed concepts of ordered representation, indexing, symbolic manipulation, and universal method later reconfigured in formal logic and computation.

---

## Open questions

1. **Authorial manuscript:** Does any Dominican archive preserve Rosselli’s draft, presentation copy, correspondence, or working diagrams?

2. **Damiano’s intervention:** Which passages, additions, illustrations, indexes, or corrections were added after Cosma’s death?

3. **Nicolaus Alexius:** Can his Dominican career and inquisitorial commissions be documented beyond the title and San Marco chronicle?

4. **Cardinal Giustiniani:** Did he materially sponsor publication, approve it, or merely receive the dedication?

5. **Printing history:** Who designed and cut the woodblocks? Were any blocks reused from earlier books?

6. **Copy census:** How many 1579 copies survive, and how do their inserted plates, cancellations, errata, and gatherings differ?

7. **Plate order:** Are the two Paradise impressions present in every complete copy? Do bindings preserve the intended relation between prose and foldout?

8. **Sources for Hell:** Beyond Dante, which theological, homiletic, cartographic, and pictorial models can be shown through verbal borrowing?

9. **Visual sources:** Can specific correspondences be established with Nardo di Cione, the Spanish Chapel, printed Dante diagrams, or contemporary Last Judgment imagery?

10. **Romberch dependence:** A line-by-line comparison is needed to separate direct borrowing from common classical and Dominican inheritance.

11. **Vernacular practice:** Did Rosselli teach the system orally in Italian while publishing it in Latin?

12. **Actual use:** Do annotated copies preserve readers’ assigned images, sermon plans, alphabets, or personal routes?

13. **Reception:** A systematic search of post-1579 mnemonic manuals, library inventories, preaching manuals, and university disputations would clarify his actual reach.

14. **Magia taxonomy:** A critical transcription of fols. 107r–123r is needed to determine exactly how *Magia* relates grammatically and conceptually to arts, sciences, and virtues.

15. **Manual alphabet priority:** Comparison with medieval finger alphabets, monastic signs, Boncompagno, Publicius, Romberch, and later deaf education could refine the priority claim.

16. **Translation:** The absence of a critical Latin edition with English translation is the greatest practical obstacle to further work.

---

## Overall assessment

Rosselli’s *Thesaurus* is best understood neither as a curiosity containing two pictures nor as an occult memory wheel. It is the most expansive late-sixteenth-century Dominican synthesis of classical local memory, scholastic faculty psychology, Christian moral topography, printed visual aids, alphabetic encoding, and encyclopedic classification.

Its distinctive achievement is scale and recursion. The practitioner can move from a place as large as the Christian universe to a celestial sphere, constellation, city, church, room, workshop, person, hand, finger, animal, tree, letter, or gesture. At every level, the same entity may become container or contained sign. Hell and Paradise are therefore not ornamental appendices: they are the poles of an ordered mnemonic cosmos.

Yates correctly recognized its Dominican and “Dantesque” importance and rescued its plates from obscurity. She underestimated, however, the technical intelligence of its lists, alphabets, and descriptive procedures; over-read isolated Renaissance markers as movement toward Hermetic magic; and relied on an authorial identity and chronology that modern catalogue practice has often made worse by confusing the friar with a painter. The documentary record instead gives a sickly but respected Florentine Dominican, dead at forty in 1575, whose brother and fellow friars ensured that his elaborate “treasury” survived.
