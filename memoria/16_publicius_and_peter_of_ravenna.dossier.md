# The first printed treatises: Publicius and Peter of Ravenna: Research Dossier

## Basic identification

**Name:** The first printed treatises: Jacobus Publicius and Peter of Ravenna  
**Catalogue position:** System 16 of 45  
**Exponents:** Jacobus Publicius; Petrus Ravennas, properly Pietro Francesco Tomai/Tomasi of Ravenna  
**Dates:** Publicius’s text circulated in manuscript by 1460 and was printed separately at Toulouse c. 1475–76 and Paris c. 1475–80; its famous illustrated Venetian compilation appeared on 30 November 1482, followed by a substantially reorganized edition dated 31 January 1485. Peter’s *Phoenix* was printed at Venice on 10 January 1491/92, conventionally cited as 1491.  
**Source texts:** Publicius, *Oratoriae artis epitomata*, containing *Institutiones oratoriae*, *Ars epistolandi*, and *Ars memorativa*; Peter, *Phoenix seu de artificiosa memoria*.  
**Principle in one sentence:** Publicius combines ordered real or imagined places with forceful corporeal images, figurative alphabets, and combinatorial diagrams, while Peter turns the same places-and-images tradition into a portable professional technique built from churches, alphabetically named human “place-guardians,” bodily grammatical coding, and personally exciting images.

**Yates:** *The Art of Memory*, ch. V, pp. 110–14 and 117–19; Peter’s English reception at ch. XII, p. 260; Bruno’s praise of Peter at ch. XI, p. 250; William Perkins’s attack at ch. XII, p. 277. Page references are to the 1966 Routledge and Kegan Paul pagination, retained in most later reprints.

### Necessary bibliographical correction

**[Documented bibliography; correction to Yates and to the catalogue label]** The Venetian Publicius of 1482 is not, strictly, the earliest printing of an independent memory treatise. Single copies survive of:

- *Ars memorativa*, Toulouse, probably Heinrich Turner, c. 1475–76: GW M36443; ISTC ip01093800; Paris, Bibliothèque Mazarine, Inc. 618-5.
- *Ars memorativa*, Paris, Petrus Caesaris and Johannes Stol, c. 1475–80: GW M36442.

The 1482 volume remains the first famous, extensively illustrated, integrated printed manual of rhetoric, letter-writing, and artificial memory, and the vehicle by which Publicius’s visual apparatus became influential. Luis Merino Jerez’s textual study distinguishes three printed stages rather than treating 1482 as an uncomplicated beginning. [Merino Jerez, “Iacobus Publicius’s *Ars memorativa*”](https://doi.org/10.14712/24646830.2020.31); [Biblissima, Toulouse edition](https://portail.biblissima.fr/fr/ark:/43093/oedataa3e2ccceec864962c1509474a7866c071cbbe27e).

## The system in detail

## 1. Publicius’s *Ars memorativa*

### 1.1 Purpose and psychology

**[Documented text]** Publicius presents memory as the “common parent and most faithful preserver” of the arts. The embodied soul’s intellectual light has been dimmed by its immersion in a corporeal prison, but “new precepts” and habitual exercise can restore its power.

The 1482 opening reads:

> *Haud ab re fore arbitror, si praeter maiorum consuetudinem, quae plurimis saeculis e mortalium usu recessere, in medium nunc lucemque referam.… At quod caduci fragilisque corpusculi coniunctione id luminis ei ademptum est, novis praeceptis et exercitatione consueta luce pristinoque iubare longe lateque splendescat.*

“Perhaps it will not be inappropriate if I now bring into public view and light things which, contrary to ancestral practice, have for many centuries withdrawn from mortal use.… What light has been taken from [the mind] by conjunction with the frail and perishable little body may, through new precepts and habitual exercise, shine again with its former radiance.”

Edition: Venice, Erhard Ratdolt, 1482, *Ars memorativa*, sig. c1r. The scanned Latin text begins at the corresponding section of the [1482 transcription and facsimile](https://la.wikisource.org/wiki/Ars_Oratoria._Ars_Epistolandi._Ars_Memorativa.).

**[Scholarly assessment]** The claim of novelty is promotional. Publicius rearranges inherited doctrines from the *Rhetorica ad Herennium*, Cicero, Quintilian, scholastic faculty psychology, Boncompagno, Albertus Magnus, Thomas Aquinas, and intervening fifteenth-century artes. Recent work treats the text as an evolving lecture manual rather than a wholly new authorial invention.

### 1.2 Natural and artificial memory

**[Documented text/tradition]** Like the *Ad Herennium*, Publicius distinguishes natural memory from memory strengthened by art. Artificial memory depends on:

1. *loca*—a stable ordered framework;
2. *imagines*—temporary representations deposited in those places;
3. repeated mental traversal;
4. removal or replacement of images when their work is done.

The fundamental distinction descends from *Rhetorica ad Herennium* III.16–24, especially III.16:

> *Constat igitur artificiosa memoria ex locis et imaginibus.*

“Artificial memory therefore consists of places and images.”

**[Documented classical source]** Cicero retells the Simonides origin-story in *De oratore* II.351–54; Quintilian gives the clearest architectural procedure in *Institutio oratoria* XI.2.17–22. Publicius himself retells Simonides immediately after his preface.

### 1.3 Places

Publicius discusses both real and fictive places.

#### Real places

**[Documented text; reconstructed from the printed redactions]** Suitable places are distinct, visible, moderately spaced, orderly, and neither excessively bright nor obscure. Buildings, rooms, corners, windows, columns, and other architectural features can supply a series. The user learns them in a fixed direction and must be able to begin from either end or enter at an intermediate point.

**[Inheritance]** These rules closely reproduce the ancient requirements that places be well lit, separated, moderately sized, and unmistakable. Medieval manuals often regularized architectural arrays more than the *Ad Herennium*, whose emphasis falls upon distinctiveness.

#### *Ficta loca*

**[Documented term; interpretation disputed]** Publicius permits *ficta loca*: places constructed by imagination where a sufficient real architecture is unavailable. The practitioner can build a mental city, palace, cloister, or cosmological order.

**[Yates’s interpretation]** Yates identifies Publicius’s principal fictive structure with the spheres of the universe: the elements, planetary spheres, fixed stars, higher celestial spheres, and *Paradisus*. She interprets it as a “Dantesque” memory of Hell, Purgatory, and Paradise.

**[Correction/qualification]** The printed diagram is demonstrably cosmological, but it does not itself map Dante’s three realms or prescribe a sequential mnemonic journey through *Inferno*, *Purgatorio*, and *Paradiso*. “Dantesque” and “Giottesque” are Yates’s historical analogies, not Publicius’s words. Publicius offers multiple place-making techniques; the cosmos is one visual and conceptual scaffold, not necessarily the master plan of his entire art.

### 1.4 Images

Publicius’s governing proposition is:

> *Intentiones simplices et spirituales facile a memoria labuntur, nisi corporeis similitudinibus coniungantur.*

“Simple and spiritual intentions easily slip from memory unless they are joined to corporeal similitudes.”

Edition cited by Yates: Venice 1485, sig. G4r; corresponding doctrine is present in the earlier redactions.

**[Documented text]** Images should be concrete, clearly bounded, and emotionally or visually exceptional. Publicius recommends:

- ridiculous movements;
- remarkable or astonishing gestures;
- savage, cruel, sorrowful, severe, or bewildered expressions;
- unusual bodily postures;
- familiar persons;
- personifications whose attributes disclose their meaning;
- transformations of words into visually or phonetically associated objects.

This is the late-medieval/early-Renaissance continuation of the *Ad Herennium* rule that ordinary things are forgotten while the base, dishonourable, unusual, great, unbelievable, or ridiculous remain.

### 1.5 Envy as a model image

**[Documented text and source]** Publicius invokes Ovid’s personification of Envy. In *Metamorphoses* II.775–82 Ovid describes her pallor, emaciation, rotten teeth, poisonous tongue, and snake-eating habits. Publicius’s version emphasizes the same repellent corporeality.

Representative Ovidian Latin:

> *Pallor in ore sedet, macies in corpore toto;  
> nusquam recta acies; livent rubigine dentes,  
> pectora felle virent, lingua est suffusa veneno;  
> risus abest, nisi quem visi movere dolores.*

“Pallor occupies her face, leanness her whole body; her gaze is never direct; her teeth are livid with decay, her breast green with gall, her tongue steeped in poison; she never smiles except at suffering she has seen.”

**[Yates’s interpretation, substantially confirmed]** Yates argues that this is not evidence of a newly humanist classical art but belongs to the medieval use of poets as repositories of morally forceful similitudes, particularly Albertus Magnus’s discussion of memorable poetic metaphor.

**[Qualification]** Publicius is not simply anti-humanist or untouched by Renaissance rhetoric: his career as a traveling lecturer, classical citations, printed rhetorical compendium, and collaboration—direct or indirect—with Ratdolt’s innovative press are thoroughly fifteenth-century humanist phenomena. Carruthers nevertheless judges the classical surface a “thin veneer” over a structure closer to Boncompagno than to the ancient handbook alone.

### 1.6 Words, syllables, and letters

Publicius does not restrict the art to remembering subject matter. He attempts *memoria verborum*, the notoriously laborious word-for-word branch.

**[Documented text]** He reports that experience has shown the combination of letters and material things to yield an almost divine advantage:

> *Commixtionem litterarum et rerum magnam, immensam et paene divinam utilitatem praestare experimento iam constat.*

“The combination of letters and things has already been established by experience to furnish a great, immeasurable, and almost divine usefulness.”

Words can be decomposed into letters or syllables, which are represented through:

- objects whose names begin with the required letter;
- objects shaped like the letter;
- combinations generated by turning a diagram or volvelle;
- conventional animal or object alphabets.

This trades semantic economy for exactness. A subject may require one vivid image; a verbal sequence may require several rebus-like elements.

### 1.7 Retrieval and forgetting

**[Documented/reconstructed procedure]**

1. Memorize the route and all its numbered or otherwise identifiable stations.
2. Translate each item into a striking image or a rebus.
3. place one principal image at each location, with subsidiary details if needed;
4. rehearse by walking the route mentally;
5. recall by revisiting the places in the same order;
6. reverse the route to test whether order belongs to the places rather than merely to verbal momentum;
7. erase, dismiss, or replace obsolete images so that old material does not interfere.

**[Context]** Medieval *ars oblivionalis*—controlled forgetting—is not a contrary art but the maintenance side of artificial memory. Carruthers’s work has especially corrected the modern picture of medieval memory as inert storage: the trained storehouse supports composition, rearrangement, judgment, and invention.

## 2. Publicius’s diagrams and printed apparatus

### 2.1 General collation

The Venice 1482 volume is a chancery quarto of 68 leaves, 31 lines per page:

- A2r–E6v: rhetoric;
- a1r–b8v: letter-writing;
- c1r–d8v: memory;
- c7r: key to mnemonic woodcuts;
- c7v–d3v: pictorial mnemonic aids;
- d7v: chessboard and colophon;
- d8 blank.

One bibliographical description records eleven illustrated pages, including the tree of rhetoric, forty-two alphabet roundels plus two unlettered roundels, an animal grid, an astronomical/combinatorial figure, and a chessboard. [Detailed collation and illustration record](https://www.christies.com/en/lot/lot-2031570).

### 2.2 Figurative alphabet

**[Documented object]** The celebrated alphabet consists of forty-two circular compartments, normally six per page within a rectangular framework. It supplies two figures for most consonants and three for vowels.

Two encoding principles coexist:

- **shape:** an object resembles the written letter;
- **initial sound/name:** an object’s Latin name begins with the letter.

Examples identified in modern analysis include:

- A: a folding ladder or *arta*, a narrow passage;
- B: a mandolin-like object or *babatum*;
- C: *cornu*, a horn, or a horseshoe-shaped object.

**[Documented priority with qualification]** This is the earliest known printed figurative alphabet in an *ars memorativa*. It is not the invention of visual alphabets: bodily, object, and animal alphabets circulated before print.

**[Variant]** The c. 1481 Cologne-stage printing had engravings with several moving components. Ratdolt’s 1482 treatment fixes almost all the images on the page. The relation among authorial conception, earlier engravings, and Ratdolt’s workshop remains unsettled.

### 2.3 Animal table

**[Documented object]** A full-page square is divided into twenty-five compartments, each occupied by an animal selected or ordered through its Latin name. It is a ready-made alphabetic repository: the animal supplies a memorable shape and an initial letter.

**[Later transmission]** Romberch reproduces and adapts object and animal alphabets in the *Congestorium artificiosae memoriae* (1520). At least one obscene or indecorous Publician image was toned down in Romberch’s replica, demonstrating moral as well as technical editorial intervention.

### 2.4 Concentric-circle device and volvelle

**[Documented object]** The relevant woodcut consists of a square around five concentric circles. Letters occupy the square and circular bands; the center was designed for a rotating paper component. In some surviving 1482 copies the movable part is absent, as volvelles were especially liable to loss.

A serpentine or worm-like rotating pointer allows vowels and consonants to be aligned in several directions.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** Publicius’s own directions are too compressed to reconstruct every operation with certainty. Romberch later supplies a more expansive account of using related circles and figurative alphabets to encode phrases.

**[Speculative reconstruction]** The resemblance of the rotating “worm” to the medieval cerebral *vermis*—the imagined valve controlling passage between the brain’s cognitive cells—has been proposed as a conceptual source. This is suggestive, not documented authorial intention.

### 2.5 Cosmological diagram

**[Documented object]** Yates’s Fig. 1 reproduces a vertically ordered geocentric cosmos: elemental and celestial regions rise in nested or stacked arcs toward the fixed stars and supercelestial regions, with *Paradisus* at the summit. It gives hierarchical, already ordered locations rather than the irregular architectural stations preferred in parts of the ancient tradition.

**[Yates’s reading]** The scheme returns the reader to a Dante- and Giotto-like world of Hell, Purgatory, cosmic ascent, virtue, vice, and Paradise.

**[Correction]** “Hell” and “Purgatory” should not be silently supplied as labels in the woodcut. The diagram is cosmological and potentially devotional; its conversion into a complete mnemonic map of Dante’s otherworld is Yates’s reconstruction.

### 2.6 Chessboard

**[Documented object]** A nearly full-page woodcut presents a chessboard with pieces in an opening arrangement. It is among the earliest printed representations of a chessboard.

**[Probable function]** Chess offers sixty-four fixed, addressable squares and familiar differentiated pieces. It can model ordered combinations or serve as a memory field.

**[Open question]** The surviving text does not explain the plate with enough precision to demonstrate one exclusive algorithm. Claims that it is a cryptographic machine, a Lullian calculator, or an occult board exceed the evidence.

## 3. Peter of Ravenna’s *Phoenix*

### 3.1 Author and occasion

Pietro Francesco Tomai/Tomasi, c. 1448–1509/10, was a jurist and itinerant professor associated with Padua, Pisa/Pistoia, Ferrara, Pavia, Bologna, Greifswald, Wittenberg, Cologne, Mainz, and probably Worms. He styled himself *memoriae magister*, *Petrus a memoria*, and *memorabili memoria praeditus*.

**[Documented publication]** The first edition was printed at Venice by Bernardinus de Choris of Cremona, 10 January 1491/92. The apparent discrepancy results from the Venetian calendar: January 1491 *more Veneto* falls in 1492 by modern reckoning.

**[Documented genre]** The title advertises uniqueness:

> *Et cum una sit phoenix et unus sit iste libellus, libello, si placet, Phoenicis nomen imponatis.*

“And since there is one Phoenix and this is the one little book [of its kind], if you please, give the book the name Phoenix.”

### 3.2 Peter’s place system

Peter begins from the familiar architectural art but renders it unusually practical.

**[Documented text]** The best site is quiet and little frequented; an unfrequented church or monastery is exemplary. Enter near the door, identify the first place, advance approximately five or six feet, establish the next, and continue in a stable circuit.

The practitioner should:

- walk through the building three or four times;
- inspect and fix each station;
- maintain a constant direction;
- make adjacent stations distinct;
- avoid places too large to apprehend at once;
- choose a locus roughly within bodily reach;
- keep the series sufficiently illuminated;
- add new buildings during travel.

**[Documented text]**

> *Et si quis locorum copiam habere cupiat, hoc ordine monasterium intret et illud totum locis impleat, aut in parietibus extra ecclesiam sibi loca comparet.*

“If anyone desires an abundance of places, let him enter a monastery in this manner and fill the whole of it with places, or prepare places for himself on the walls outside the church.”

Modern edition cited in scholarship: Pedro de Ravena, *Fénix de memoria artificial*, ed. and trans. Luis Merino Jerez, 2007, p. 142.

**[Comparison with Quintilian]** Quintilian XI.2.21 permits using a long journey and its landmarks as places. Peter’s practice of continually acquiring churches and monasteries while traveling is therefore classical in precedent, although his scale and publicity are personal.

### 3.3 Scale: the 100,000 places

Peter writes:

> *Ego autem, quia omnes homines Italiae copia rerum absque chartarum revolutione superare volui, in Sacris Scripturis, iure canonico civilique et aliis multarum rerum auctoritatibus, dum essem adolescens, mihi centum millia locorum paravi et nunc ipsis decem millia addidi…*

“Because I wished to surpass all the men of Italy in abundance of material without turning pages, while I was a young man I prepared for myself one hundred thousand places for Holy Scripture, canon and civil law, and authorities on many other subjects; and now I have added ten thousand more to them.”

**[Documented self-report]** The boast occurs in the *Phoenix*.

**[Not independently verified]** No inventory of 110,000 loci survives. The number may combine genuine sustained practice with promotional hyperbole.

**[Correction to Yates]** Yates says he began with 100,000 and had “added many more.” The text specifies another 10,000 in the cited redaction.

### 3.4 Place-guardians

Peter’s most characteristic refinement is the *custos loci*.

**[Documented text]** A familiar human figure permanently occupies each locus. This person is not necessarily the temporary image encoding the current content. Rather, the guardian anchors the place and acts with, holds, wears, touches, or suffers the temporary mnemonic object.

This distinction is important:

- **locus:** permanent spatial address;
- **guardian:** stable person identifying the address;
- **temporary image/object/action:** the information currently stored there.

Modern summaries often collapse guardian and image, obscuring Peter’s architecture.

### 3.5 Alphabetical persons and syllables

**[Documented text]** Guardians can be ordered alphabetically by their names. A known Eusebius supplies E; Thomas supplies T. Juxtaposing or successively traversing them can encode *ET*.

The same technique can expand from letters to syllables, using:

- personal names;
- objects beginning with the desired syllable;
- actions punning upon words;
- bodily position to mark inflection.

**[Later descent]** Bruno’s “Table Seal” in the *Explicatio triginta sigillorum* uses alphabetically arranged persons as stable carriers. Yates plausibly identifies Peter as an important source, but Bruno’s system is vastly more combinatorial and philosophically ambitious.

### 3.6 Grammatical coding on the body

**[Documented text]** Peter maps grammatical case, number, and related features onto the guardian’s body. A remembered object may be:

- in or associated with the right hand for one case;
- placed under or against the right leg for another;
- attached to the breast for another;
- displayed elsewhere to indicate additional cases;
- shown with a naked or clothed guardian to distinguish number in some examples.

One cited example has a naked girl touch bread with her right foot:

> *Si rem, ut panem, puellam nudam in loco sibi pedem dextrum cum pane tangentem…*

“If [you wish to remember] a thing, such as bread, [imagine] a naked girl in her place touching the bread with her right foot…”

**[Scholarly correction]** A later tradition, especially through Romberch, systematizes nudity as one number and clothing as another. The rule should not be attributed wholesale to every *Phoenix* redaction without collation. Scholarship has noted that the explicit generalized rule reported by Romberch is absent from some surviving Peter editions.

### 3.7 Familiar and erotic images

Peter divulges what he presents as a shameful secret: beautiful young women make especially effective guardians for him.

> *Hoc secretum diu prae verecundia tacui: si cito reminisci cupis, virgines pulcherrimas in locis colloca; memoria enim puellarum collocatione mirabiliter excitatur.*

“I long kept this secret silent out of modesty: if you desire to remember quickly, place very beautiful maidens in the loci; for memory is wonderfully excited by the placing of girls.”

The wording varies between editions; the doctrine is certain.

He adds, in substance, that the advice cannot help those who hate or despise women, and apologizes to religious and chaste readers.

He names a favorite:

> *Iuniperam Pistoriensium, mihi adolescenti carissimam, saepissime in locis collocavi…*

“I very frequently placed in my loci Junipera of Pistoia, who was most dear to me in my youth…”

**[Documented personal report]** Peter says the image of Junipera improved ease and regularity of recall.

**[Scholarly interpretation]** The technique exploits affect, desire, familiarity, and bodily attention.

**[Qualification]** Peter never explicitly prescribes sexual intercourse as a mnemonic action. Modern claims that his system is straightforwardly pornographic go beyond the language. Nudity, erotic attraction, violence, and intimate familiarity are present or strongly implied; the exact imagined action is often left unstated.

### 3.8 The testament example

Peter gives a legal example involving the rule governing witnesses to a will. The relevant image has a testator making a will before witnesses while a maiden tears it up:

> *Testatorem testamentum condentem praesentibus duobus testibus, et virginem unam illud lacerare…*

“A testator making a will in the presence of two witnesses, and one maiden tearing it up…”

Edition: Venice 1491/92, traditionally cited at sig. c3r.

**[Documented example]** This is Peter’s image.

**[Yates’s response]** Yates calls the connection baffling.

**[Later reconstruction]** The image probably encodes legal invalidity dramatically: the insufficiently witnessed instrument is destroyed. Other details may operate as numerical, alphabetical, grammatical, or punning cues lost when the example is isolated from Peter’s private conventions. That reconstruction is plausible but not demonstrable.

**[Correction to a common retelling]** Yates paraphrases the legal proposition as requiring seven witnesses while the scene contains two. Later discussions sometimes state that the proposition itself requires two witnesses. The discrepancy may reflect different legal categories or a misunderstood mnemonic contrast. It must not be “repaired” without collating the exact edition and its legal source.

### 3.9 Retrieval

Peter’s practical workflow is:

1. establish a large permanent series of places;
2. give many places permanent, familiar guardians;
3. select the required place by its order or alphabetical guardian;
4. make the guardian interact with an object, action, person, or written instrument representing the matter;
5. add bodily position, clothing, number, or gesture when grammatical/legal precision is needed;
6. mentally revisit the route;
7. read off the guardian’s action and decode the private association;
8. rehearse frequently and expand the architecture during travel.

Unlike Publicius’s printed figures, Peter’s first *Phoenix* supplies no comparable suite of mnemonic plates. Its “machinery” is primarily verbal and mental.

## The sources

## 1. Publicius: manuscripts and early print

### Manuscript transmission

**London, British Library, Add MS 28805**

- chiefly written in 1460;
- parchment, small quarto;
- Publicius’s *Ars oratoria*, fol. 7;
- *Ars epistolandi*, fol. 42;
- *Ars memoriae*, with figures, fol. 133;
- later ownership inscription: *Liber domini Thome Swalwell Dunelmensis monachi*;
- also includes an English rhetorical work dated 1460.

The catalogue does not say that Thomas Swalwell copied the whole manuscript in 1460; it says the volume was written chiefly in 1460 and later belonged to him. Therefore Yates’s “written in 1460 by Thomas Swatwell” and “probably a monk of Durham” compress palaeographical inference and ownership into authorship. His name is more accurately Swalwell, not Swatwell. [British Library catalogue](https://searcharchives.bl.uk/catalog/032-002020245).

**[Documented object; disputed interpretation]** One added diagram, reproduced by Volkmann as plate 145, was called “probably magical” by Yates. No evidence has been located that its copier practiced ritual magic, invoked spirits, or was investigated for heresy. “Magical” describes a modern iconographic impression, not a documented historical consequence.

Other manuscript witnesses and lecture-derived redactions have been identified in European collections; modern textual work uses sigla such as M, G, Z, Bod., NY, V82, and V85. Their relations show that Publicius repeatedly expanded and rearranged the work.

### Separate incunables

- Toulouse, Heinrich Turner, c. 1475–76: GW M36443; ISTC ip01093800; Mazarine Inc. 618-5.
- Paris, Petrus Caesaris and Johannes Stol, c. 1475–80: GW M36442.
- Cologne, Johann Guldenschaff, c. 1481: GW M36437.
- Paris, Antoine Caillaut, c. 1484–90: GW M36439.
- Lyon, printer associated with Baldwin of Savoy’s *Ars memoriae*, c. 1489/92.
- Further Paris/Lyon printings around 1495–1500.

### Ratdolt editions

- Venice: Erhard Ratdolt, 30 November 1482. ISTC ip01096000; GW M36431.
- Venice: Ratdolt, 31 January 1485. ISTC ip01097000; GW M36435.
- Augsburg: Ratdolt, 20 January 1490.

The 1485 text is not simply a second impression. It rebalances the three books, redistributes matter, adds a third section on exercising and strengthening memory, and incorporates medical regimens or remedies.

### Three textual stages

Merino Jerez reconstructs:

1. **Stage I, c. 1475–77:** Toulouse and Paris separate editions; a related later Lyon witness.
2. **Stage II, c. 1481–82:** an anonymous/title-poor version with movable diagrams, followed by Ratdolt’s 1482 rearrangement and largely fixed alphabet.
3. **Stage III, 1485:** doctrinal and editorial homogenization across three books; reprinted in 1490.

This overturns any model in which a stable manuscript was merely transferred to print in 1482.

### Manuscript copies after print

Yates records copies in:

- Vatican City, Biblioteca Apostolica Vaticana, Vat. lat. 5347, fol. 60;
- Paris, Bibliothèque nationale de France, lat. 8747, fol. 1.

**[Documented reception]** These are examples of print-to-manuscript transmission: admirers copied the printed handbook by hand. Manuscript form therefore does not necessarily imply pre-print composition.

### Sixteenth- and seventeenth-century editions

Yates lists Bologna 1492; Cologne 1506 and 1608; Venice 1526 and 1533; Vienna 1541 and 1600; Vicenza 1600. Bibliographical work adds or refines Leipzig and Erfurt issues.

Gregor Reisch incorporated Peter’s art into the *Margarita philosophica*, Book III, tractate II, chapter 23. Because the *Margarita* went through numerous editions, it multiplied Peter’s readership well beyond copies bearing the *Phoenix* title.

A searchable transcription based on the Venice 1533 Sessa edition is available at [Latin Wikisource](https://la.wikisource.org/wiki/Foenix).

### Translations

- Robert Copland, *An Art of Memory, That Otherwise Is Called the Phoenix*, London, c. 1548.
- French and other vernacular adaptations circulated, though their bibliographical status and dependence on complete versus excerpted Latin editions require individual collation.
- Luis Merino Jerez, ed. and Spanish trans., *Fénix de memoria artificial*, 2007, is the principal modern bilingual working edition cited by recent scholarship.

## Yates’s account and scholarship since

## 1. What Yates argues

Yates places the pair at a historical hinge.

### Publicius

Her main claims are:

- the 1482 rhetoric has an appended *Ars memorativa*;
- its mystically phrased preface and detachable position resemble medieval rhetorics;
- the cosmological places, Thomist corporeal similitudes, and Ovidian Envy belong to the medieval rather than a newly recovered classical tradition;
- therefore:

> “this first printed memory treatise is not a symptom of the revival of the classical art of memory as part of the Renaissance revival of rhetoric; it comes straight out of the mediaeval tradition.”

She treats the Swalwell manuscript as proof that the apparently Italian Renaissance book preserves an older northern-European manuscript world.

### Peter

Yates contrasts Peter with Publicius:

- Peter exploits the recovery of the complete Quintilian;
- he treats memory as a competitive practical advantage;
- he accumulates places in churches and during journeys;
- he uses familiar persons and erotic attraction;
- he advertises astonishing feats;
- he “laicised and popularised” the art.

### The four strands

Yates provisionally separates early printed arts into:

1. “straight mnemotechnic,” clarified by renewed access to Quintilian;
2. works still entangled with medieval ethical, theological, and devotional uses;
3. works touched by *Ars notoria* or magical traditions;
4. works transformed by Renaissance Hermetic and occult philosophy.

This is a heuristic, not a period classification used by the authors themselves.

## 2. What has been confirmed

### Carruthers

Mary Carruthers confirms the depth of Publicius’s medieval inheritance but reframes medieval memory as a craft of composition and invention. Her work makes several corrections to the older historiography:

- medieval memoria is not merely rote retention;
- “places” can be textual, architectural, diagrammatic, bodily, or conceptual;
- emotionally charged corporeal images belong to ordinary cognitive and rhetorical practice, not automatically to magic;
- monastic and scholastic memory is productive: material is gathered to be recomposed.

Carruthers’s observation that Publicius’s classicism is a thin veneer over a work closer to Boncompagno supports Yates’s continuity thesis, though without Yates’s sharp medieval/Renaissance dramatic contrast.

### Rossi

Paolo Rossi had already placed these manuals within the longer history from local memory to universal method and combinatorial logic. His emphasis on the transition from memory storage to artificial ordering remains valuable. He gives Peter an important place in the practical handbook tradition but does not make him the cause of later universal-language projects.

### Bolzoni

Lina Bolzoni’s work confirms that printed memory arts belong to a larger culture of interacting words and images. She complicates Yates by emphasizing:

- reading practices;
- rhetorical composition;
- iconographic repertories;
- galleries, theatres, emblems, and commonplace structures;
- continuity between mnemonic prescriptions and literary/visual production.

Publicius’s alphabet is therefore not only a residue of medieval memory; it is also an early experiment in what print can do with repeatable images, grids, movable paper, and cross-references.

### Small

Jocelyn Penny Small warns against reading ancient practices through Renaissance reconstructions. Quintilian’s architectural route is genuine, but neither Peter’s immense locus collections nor Publicius’s printed alphabets should be projected unchanged backward into Greek and Roman practice.

### Hajdu

Helga Hajdu’s pioneering *Das mnemotechnische Schrifttum des Mittelalters* remains fundamental for identifying manuscripts and continuities. Later scholarship corrects its cataloguing and editions but depends heavily on its recovery of the medieval corpus.

### Recent Publicius scholarship

Merino Jerez decisively corrects the simple Yates chronology:

- printing begins before 1482;
- the text existed in several configurations;
- 1482 is a major editorial redaction;
- 1485 is another doctrinally meaningful stage;
- illustration and movable apparatus vary across issues.

Recent research also attributes much of the polished visual program to Ratdolt’s expertise. That is a probability grounded in his work on Euclid, Regiomontanus, color printing, diagrams, and volvelles—not proof that Publicius had no role.

## 3. What has been corrected or rejected

- **“First printed treatise,” unqualified:** corrected by the Toulouse and Paris editions.
- **Stable authorial text first printed in 1482:** rejected by the three-stage transmission.
- **Publicius certainly Florentine:** disputed. He calls himself *Florentinus*, but Kristeller observed that his name is not Florentine and proposed Spanish or Portuguese origin.
- **Swalwell as the demonstrated 1460 copyist:** too definite; the manuscript’s ownership and production evidence must be distinguished.
- **Cosmological figure as an explicit Dante map:** not demonstrated.
- **Peter as purely classical and entirely detached from medieval memory:** rejected even by Yates’s own qualifications. Guardians, corporeal grammar, personal rebuses, and accumulated authorities belong to a mixed tradition.
- **Peter invented erotic mnemonics:** false as a priority claim. Erotic and sexually hazardous images occur in earlier medieval memory material. Peter’s novelty lies in explicit publicity and personal endorsement.
- **Peter’s images prescribe intercourse:** unsupported.
- **Peter had exactly 100,000 or 110,000 operational loci:** documented only as his claim.
- **Peter received “the first copyright”:** misleading. He received an early Venetian authorial printing privilege, not modern copyright.

## 4. Scholarship requested in the broader brief

Bonner and Fidora on Llull, Ong on Ramus, and Sturlese, Ciliberto, Clucas, and Gatti on Bruno matter primarily downstream. They do not show that Publicius or Peter was a Lullist, Ramist, or Hermetic magician.

- **Llull scholarship:** Bonner and Fidora distinguish Llull’s logical-theological Art from ordinary local memory. Similar circles do not establish derivation.
- **Ramus:** Ong shows that Ramist dichotomies and visual page layouts reorganized knowledge while often repudiating image-heavy artificial memory.
- **Bruno:** Sturlese, Ciliberto, Clucas, and Gatti have made Yates’s “Hermetic Bruno” insufficient as a total explanation. Bruno combines mnemonic practice, Lullian structures, logic, metaphysics, rhetoric, psychology, magic, and polemic.
- **Camillo:** Bolzoni and Stagno emphasize the theatre’s textual, architectural, courtly, and editorial histories, tempering an exclusively occult account.

These corrections matter because resemblance is not genealogy. Publicius’s concentric circles are not automatically Lullian; Peter’s alphabetical guardians are not already Bruno’s metaphysics.

## History and context

## 1. Classical descent

The immediate authoritative sources were:

- anonymous *Rhetorica ad Herennium* III.16–24;
- Cicero, *De oratore* II.350–60;
- Quintilian, *Institutio oratoria* XI.2.

The standard lawsuit image in the *Ad Herennium* places an ill man in bed, an accused person beside him, a cup, tablets, and ram’s testicles to encode poison, will, witnesses, and testimony. Peter’s damaged will belongs to this tradition of compressing a forensic proposition into an exaggerated scene, though it is not a direct reproduction.

## 2. Medieval descent

From late antiquity through the Middle Ages, artificial memory was absorbed into:

- rhetoric and preaching;
- the virtue of Prudence;
- moral psychology;
- meditation;
- scholastic accounts of internal senses;
- *ars dictaminis* and sermon composition;
- alphabetical and topical finding systems.

Albertus Magnus and Thomas Aquinas justified sensible images because intellectual content must enter embodied human cognition through phantasms. Their discussions did not make mnemonic images talismans.

Dominican and Franciscan schools trained preachers to retrieve Scripture, authorities, distinctions, exempla, and sermon structures. Publicius’s language emerges from that world even though he also belongs to traveling humanist pedagogy.

## 3. Universities and courts

Publicius lectured north of the Alps, with evidence associating him with Basel, Leipzig, and Erfurt during the 1460s. His identity and exact itinerary remain difficult to reconstruct.

Peter’s career was institutionally public:

- Italian law faculties;
- courts and civic elites;
- Duke Bogislaw X’s Greifswald;
- Frederick the Wise’s Wittenberg;
- Cologne’s legal and theological conflicts;
- Maximilian I’s court;
- testimonials from rulers and learned men.

His art was useful to a jurist because medieval and early-modern law depended on mastering texts, glosses, cases, distinctions, and authorities before searchable editions and modern indexes were dependable.

## 4. Print culture

Publicius demonstrates that print did not simply replace memory. It standardized aids for building memory:

- repeatable alphabets;
- grids;
- printed diagrams;
- detachable or movable volvelles;
- images that later authors could copy.

Peter demonstrates print’s ability to sell a persona and technique. His compact handbook, testimonials, dramatic claims, and authorial privilege form an early knowledge-product campaign.

## 5. Humanism and “laicization”

Yates’s opposition between medieval Publicius and secular Peter is useful but too clean.

- Publicius adopts humanist titles, classical exempla, and an innovative commercial press.
- Peter uses churches and monasteries as his preferred architecture, remembers Scripture, and later became a Franciscan tertiary.
- Neither author abandons Christian culture.
- Peter’s “laicization” means change of emphasis and audience: professional utility is foregrounded over Prudence, salvation, or sermon ethics.

## People

### Jacobus Publicius

**[Documented]** Traveling teacher, rhetorician, and possibly physician; authorial self-designation *Florentinus*.

**[Disputed]** Spanish or Portuguese origin. Kristeller’s proposal rests on the non-Florentine character of the name and lack of Italian documentation, not on a discovered Iberian birth record.

### Erhard Ratdolt

Printer of the 1482 and 1485 Venetian editions and the 1490 Augsburg reprint. His technical experience with mathematical diagrams and astronomical volvelles makes him a probable major contributor to the visual apparatus.

### Thomas Swalwell

Durham monk and later owner of BL Add MS 28805. The manuscript is strong evidence for English monastic reception, but not conclusive evidence that he personally copied every Publician section.

### Pietro Francesco Tomai/Tomasi

Jurist, teacher, itinerant lecturer, self-promoter, and mnemonic performer.

### Junipera of Pistoia

Known only through Peter’s personal mnemonic testimony. She may have been a historical beloved acquaintance, but no independent identification has been securely established.

### Patrons and witnesses

Testimonial material names or invokes:

- Eleonora of Aragon, Duchess of Ferrara;
- Bonifacio, Marquis of Montferrat;
- rulers, civic authorities, jurists, professors, and audiences who reportedly tested Peter;
- Emperor Frederick III, who conferred or confirmed Peter’s status as *miles aureatus*;
- Duke Bogislaw X;
- Frederick the Wise;
- Maximilian I.

Testimonials are contemporary evidence of reputation, not laboratory verification of every feat.

### Jacob van Hoogstraten and Cologne opponents

Peter clashed with the Cologne Dominican theologian Jacob van Hoogstraten and the theological faculty over propositions including the treatment of executed criminals’ bodies. The dispute involved suspected heterodoxy and disciplinary jurisdiction.

**[Correction]** This is not evidence that the *Phoenix* or its mnemonic method was condemned as magic.

### Bruno

Yates reports Bruno’s admiration for Peter. Bruno treats earlier mnemonic masters as authorities and develops alphabetical persons into vastly enlarged combinatorial systems.

### William Perkins

The English Puritan Perkins attacked artificial memory when it employed absurd, lascivious, or impious images. Peter’s attractive women became an obvious example for later moral criticism. The criticism targets imaginative pollution and ethics, not proof that Peter performed operative magic.

## Anecdotes and feats

Peter reports or his testimonial apparatus attributes to him the ability to retain:

- extensive Scripture;
- civil and canon law with glosses;
- approximately two hundred Ciceronian speeches or sayings;
- three hundred philosophical sayings;
- twenty thousand legal propositions;
- extremely large ordered stores without consulting books.

**[Status]** Contemporary self-report and publicity, sometimes supported by testimonials; scale independently unverified.

The story that his teacher listened “as if turned to stone,” then crossed himself defensively while others embraced Peter appears in later biographical tradition drawing on early accounts. It may preserve a contemporary performance narrative, but its theatrical details should not be treated as stenographic documentation.

### Requested but not securely traceable here

- **The auctioneer:** no secure primary-text connection to Publicius or Peter has been identified. Auctioneer feats belong to broader mnemonic anecdote collections.
- **The professor’s party:** no identifiable Publicius/Peter primary source was found.
- **The student who lost his memory:** no secure connection was found.
- **The accused magician:** Peter was attacked for allegedly heterodox legal propositions, but no primary record has been identified in which he was prosecuted as a magician because of the *Phoenix*.

These anecdotes should not be silently imported from other mnemonists such as Lambert Schenkel or confused with legends of Pietro d’Abano.

## The magic question

## 1. Publicius

### Was the printed system magical?

**[Documented text]** Publicius uses elevated language—memory is divine or almost divine—and includes cosmology and rotating figures.

**[Finding]** None of that by itself constitutes magic. His operational instructions require imagination, association, ordered places, repetition, and visual/verbal combination. They do not require:

- invocation of angels, demons, or spirits;
- astrological election;
- consecration;
- talismanic materials;
- divine names;
- ritual fasting or prayer;
- claims of infused supernatural knowledge.

### The Swalwell diagram

**[Yates’s interpretation]** An added diagram is “probably magical.”

**[Disputed]** Without a demonstrated ritual text or iconographic analysis, this is best classified as an occult-looking manuscript addition, not proof that Publicius’s art was magical.

## 2. Peter

### Was the *Phoenix* magical?

**[Documented text]** No. Its causal vocabulary is psychological and rhetorical: attention, familiarity, emotional arousal, ordered location, and exercise.

### Was Peter accused of magic?

**[Negative finding]** No reliable primary record has been located showing that Peter was accused, tried, or punished as a necromancer for his mnemonic performances.

The common statement that observers “believed him a necromancer” circulates widely in modern encyclopedic summaries, but the precise early source is rarely supplied. It may derive from dramatic biographical descriptions of audiences crossing themselves at his feats.

### Cologne heresy controversy

**[Documented but separate]** Peter encountered charges of heresy or theologically suspect propositions in Cologne. These concerned legal and theological matters—not an inquisitorial condemnation of artificial memory.

### Erotic images and sin

Perkins and other moralists could regard lascivious mnemonic imagery as spiritually dangerous. That makes the method morally suspect to them, not talismanic. Scholastic psychology readily explained why sexual images are memorable without invoking magic.

## 3. *Ars notoria*

The *Ars notoria* promised knowledge or eloquence through prayers, figures, divine names, ritual observance, and angelic assistance. It was repeatedly condemned or suspected.

Publicius and Peter’s systems differ categorically:

| Local artificial memory | *Ars notoria* |
|---|---|
| learned places | ritual figures or *notae* |
| invented images | sacred names and prayers |
| rehearsal | ritual observance |
| natural psychological causation | divine or angelic infusion |
| ordinary rhetorical art | suspect ritual art |

**[Historical complication]** Manuscript collectors could copy these materials beside one another, and later occult philosophers could combine mnemonic and magical techniques. Adjacency is not identity.

## Afterlife

## 1. Romberch

Johann Host von Romberch’s Dominican *Congestorium artificiosae memoriae* (Venice, 1520; revised 1533) cites Peter alongside Cicero, Quintilian, Thomas, and Petrarch. It transmits:

- Publicius-like alphabets;
- object grids;
- cosmological or hierarchical diagrams;
- elaborated rooms;
- Peter’s guardians and bodily coding;
- a fuller explanation of combinatorial letter devices.

Romberch is the clearest bridge from the incunable manuals to sixteenth-century system-building.

## 2. Reisch

Peter’s incorporation into *Margarita philosophica* III.ii.23 placed his method inside a university encyclopedia. This likely mattered more for diffusion than many stand-alone *Phoenix* editions.

## 3. Copland and English rhetoric

Robert Copland’s c. 1548 translation gave Peter an English vernacular afterlife. Thomas Wilson’s *Arte of Rhetorique* (1553) belongs to the same English rhetorical environment.

## 4. Bruno

Peter’s alphabetically ordered persons reappear conceptually in Bruno’s Table Seal. Publicius’s visual alphabets also belong to the genealogy of Bruno’s image-letter combinations.

**[Qualification]** Bruno does not simply enlarge Peter. His wheels, shadows, seals, planetary images, Lullian combinations, metaphysics, and magical theory form a different intellectual project. Yates’s Hermetic interpretation remains illuminating but is no longer accepted as exhaustive.

## 5. Fludd

Robert Fludd’s seventeenth-century memory systems preserve architectural theaters and visual alphabets. Their relation to Publicius is best understood through an intervening printed tradition—especially Romberch—rather than necessarily from direct use of the 1482 edition.

## 6. Ramism

Ramist method competed with image-heavy local memory by placing knowledge into visible dichotomous order on the printed page. Ong’s work shows how the page itself became a spatial apparatus. Ramism is thus partly a rival afterlife of the desire for addressable knowledge, not a direct descendant of Publicius’s images.

## 7. Emblem books and iconology

Publicius’s personifications and alphabetic images anticipate a print culture in which text and picture mutually decode one another. But Alciato’s three-part emblem—motto, pictura, epigram—is not simply a printed memory image. Bolzoni and later emblem scholarship emphasize affinity without reducing one genre to the other.

## 8. Dante and frescoes

Yates’s comparisons to Dante and Giotto capture shared cultural practices:

- hierarchical worlds;
- vivid bodily punishment and reward;
- personified virtues and vices;
- spatial moral order.

**[No direct proof]** No evidence establishes that Dante used Publicius’s system, which postdates him, or that Publicius copied a particular fresco cycle. The relationship is one of inherited mnemonic culture and analogy.

## 9. Borges, Eco, and modern literature

Borges’s Funes represents the nightmare of total, unselective memory—the opposite of an art that sorts, compresses, and deliberately forgets. Eco’s interest in universal languages, combinatorics, lists, and encyclopedic order belongs more directly to the Rossi lineage from Llull to Leibniz than to Peter alone.

## 10. Memory-palace revival and athletes

Modern competitors use loci, journeys, familiar buildings, person-action-object images, and numerical/alphabetic peg systems. These are structurally close to Peter:

- permanent locations;
- vivid or erotic/comic/violent interaction;
- rapid traversal;
- reusable routes.

**[Modern invention]** “PAO,” Major-system phonetics, standardized card decks, and timed sport protocols are modern techniques. They should not be credited to Peter merely because his guardians also interact with objects.

## 11. Computing and combinatorics

Publicius’s concentric device and letter-image combinations are addressable symbolic systems, but calling them computers is anachronistic.

The defensible lineage is conceptual:

1. Llull’s combinatorial Art uses letters and rotating figures to explore formally structured relations.
2. Renaissance authors combine Lullian, mnemonic, logical, and encyclopedic projects.
3. Leibniz imagines a *characteristica universalis* and a calculus of reasoning.
4. Mechanical and electronic computation later implement symbolic operations.

**[Correction]** Publicius’s volvelle did not lead in a documented straight line to the computer. Peter’s loci are closer to an indexed database metaphor than an ancestral data structure in the technical sense.

## Controversies and disputes

### 1. Who printed the first memory treatise?

**Older/Yates position:** Ratdolt’s Venice 1482 Publicius.

**Current bibliographical position:** Publicius’s separate Toulouse and Paris editions precede it. The honorific can be retained only as “first famous illustrated integrated memory treatise” or “first printed memory treatise known to Yates in that form.”

### 2. Publicius’s identity

**Documented:** he calls himself Florentine.

**Disputed:** Kristeller proposed Iberian origins. Evidence is circumstantial: name, itinerary, and documentary absence. No conclusive replacement identity exists.

### 3. Authorship of the images

**Documented:** the images occur in editions bearing Publicius’s work.

**Probable reconstruction:** Ratdolt and his workshop substantially designed or regularized the Venetian apparatus.

**Unknown:** which individual devised each alphabet figure, the chessboard’s exact mnemonic operation, and the surviving volvelle configuration.

### 4. Is the cosmos the memory system?

**Yates:** yes, centrally, and Dantesque.

**More cautious reading:** it is one supplied fictive-place scaffold within a plural art. The diagram’s exact use remains partly underexplained.

### 5. Was Publicius medieval or Renaissance?

The binary is false if taken absolutely.

- medieval in psychology, image doctrine, moral personification, and manuscript ancestry;
- Renaissance in itinerant humanism, classical packaging, print design, technical diagrams, and market.

### 6. Did Peter recover Quintilian?

**Yates’s reconstruction:** Peter’s travel loci suggest Quintilian.

**Evidence:** strong conceptual parallel; no explicit surviving statement proving that the journey technique came directly from Peter’s reading of the complete Quintilian rather than mediated tradition or practical experience.

### 7. Did Peter possess 100,000 places?

**Documented:** he says so.

**Unverified:** actual capacity.

**Legendary expansion:** later retellings transform the boast into near-perfect recall of all books or all law. Those claims must be traced individually to testimonials or biographies.

### 8. Peter’s “copyright”

**Documented:** Venetian authorial privilege, 3 January 1492, protecting his new art and chosen publication arrangements.

**Incorrect modern shorthand:** the first copyright. Venetian privileges were selective monopolies granted by public authority; they did not establish general, automatic authorial property. Sabellico had received a relevant authorial privilege in 1486. Peter’s privilege was nevertheless an important early precedent. [Kostylo, “From Gunpowder to Print”](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/master/gdc/gdcebookspublic/20/19/45/28/02/2019452802/2019452802.pdf).

### 9. Erotic technique

**Documented:** beautiful maidens, Junipera, shame, apology, excitation of memory.

**Reasonable inference:** erotic attraction was deliberately exploited.

**Disputed extension:** explicit coitus or pornography.

**Later elaboration:** Romberch and later mnemonic writers sometimes make bodily and clothing codes more systematic than Peter’s surviving text.

### 10. Magic and heresy

**Documented:** Cologne theological controversy; moral criticism of lascivious images; magical-looking material adjacent to mnemonic manuscripts.

**Unsupported conflation:** that Peter was inquisitorially prosecuted for mnemonic magic or that Publicius’s printed system was an *Ars notoria*.

### 11. Peter’s death date

Reference sources variously give 1508, 1509, or 1509/10 and place his death at Mainz or Worms. The newer *Neue Deutsche Biographie* gives 1509/10, probably Worms, and documents activity there in autumn 1509. “1508 at Mainz” is an older simplification.

### 12. “Purely mnemotechnical”

Yates is right about Peter’s emphasis, but “purely” suppresses:

- his use of churches;
- Scripture;
- canon law;
- religious testimonials and apologies;
- scholastic theories of image and affect;
- moral consequences of chosen imagery.

### 13. Direct genealogies

Claims of simple descent—Publicius → Romberch → Bruno → Fludd → Leibniz → computer—are too linear. Some links are textual and demonstrable; others are analogies within a shared European culture of diagrams, alphabets, combination, and ordered knowledge.

## Open questions

1. What is the complete stemma of Publicius’s manuscript and printed redactions?
2. Can the Paris and Toulouse single copies be dated more precisely through type, paper, and ownership evidence?
3. Which diagrams originated in Publicius’s lectures, and which were devised by printers?
4. What was the exact assembly and operation of each lost or incomplete volvelle?
5. Does a surviving copy preserve the complete 1482 moving mechanism?
6. What exactly does the chessboard encode?
7. Is Publicius’s cosmological hierarchy connected to a specific medieval diagram tradition?
8. Can the allegedly magical Swalwell figure be given a full codicological and iconographic reassessment?
9. Is there documentary evidence for Publicius’s Iberian origin?
10. What is the correct identification, if any, of “Cyrillus Caesar,” addressee of Publicius’s rhetoric?
11. Can Junipera of Pistoia be independently identified?
12. How much does the first *Phoenix* differ from the 1533 transcription commonly used online?
13. What precise legal doctrine is encoded by the testator, two witnesses, maiden, and torn testament?
14. Did Peter personally supervise all early editions?
15. Which claimed public memory performances have contemporary civic or university corroboration?
16. What is the earliest source for the necromancer story?
17. Did Bruno read Peter directly, through Reisch or Romberch, or through several channels?
18. How did female readers adapt a technique whose published examples presume a heterosexual male practitioner?
19. How widely were the movable diagrams actually cut out and used?
20. How did readers annotate, censor, or replace the indecorous alphabet images?
