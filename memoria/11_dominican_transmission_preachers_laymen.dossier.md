# The Dominican transmission to preachers and laymen: Research Dossier

## Basic identification

**Name:** The Dominican transmission to preachers and laymen  
**Catalogue position:** System 11 of 45  
**Principal exponents and transmitters:** Giovanni da San Gimignano, O.P.; Bartolomeo da San Concordio, O.P.; Bono Giamboni; the anonymous author of the *Ars memorie artificialis* transmitted with the *Rosaio della vita*  
**Dates:** Giovanni’s *Summa de exemplis ac similitudinibus rerum*, approximately 1298–1314; Bartolomeo’s Latin *De documentis antiquorum* and vernacular *Ammaestramenti degli antichi*, completed before 1323; *Rosaio della vita*, explicitly dated 1373 in its textual tradition; associated manuscripts principally fifteenth-century  
**Source texts:** Giovanni, *Summa de exemplis*, VI.42; Bartolomeo, *Ammaestramenti*, IX.8; the separately circulating memory section of Bono Giamboni’s *Fiore di rettorica*, conventionally printed as *Trattato della memoria artificiale*; *Rosaio della vita* with the anonymous *Ars memorie artificialis*, edited by Paolo Rossi, *Clavis universalis*, appendix I, pp. 271–75  
**Principle in one sentence:** Classical artificial memory—ordered places bearing striking corporeal images—is absorbed into Dominican prudential ethics and preaching, translated into Italian, and adapted so that clerics and lay readers can retain ordered collections of virtues, vices, moral sentences, exempla, and sermons.  
**Yates:** *The Art of Memory*, chapter IV, especially pp. 85–91; p. 95 n. 35; p. 103.

### Evidentiary synopsis

- **Documented text:** Giovanni gives four concise aids to memory: order, intense attention or affection, unusual similitudes, and frequent meditation.
- **Documented text:** Bartolomeo gives eight broader aids: early learning, attention, exercise or repetition, ordering, beginning from the beginning, similitudes or images, avoiding overload, and mnemonic verse or rhyme.
- **Documented textual transmission:** The so-called *Trattato della memoria artificiale* printed after Bartolomeo is part of Bono Giamboni’s version of *Rhetorica ad Herennium* III, not an independent work by Bartolomeo.
- **Documented text:** The anonymous *Ars* accompanying the *Rosaio* distinguishes rural or “natural” loci, such as trees in fields, from architectural or “artificial” loci, such as a study, window, or coffer, and explicitly directs the reader to remember the *Rosaio*.
- **Yates’s interpretation:** Together these witnesses show artificial memory becoming “a lay devotional discipline, fostered and recommended by the friars.”
- **Supported scholarly reconstruction:** The codicological conjunction of ethical books and mnemonic instructions strongly suggests practical moral use.
- **Not documented:** No surviving record identifies a particular lay reader performing the complete *Rosaio* system.
- **Not documented:** There is no evidence that Giovanni, Bartolomeo, Giamboni, or the *Rosaio* compiler regarded this technique as magical or talismanic.
- **Disputed:** The *Rosaio* is not securely by Matteo de’ Corsini. “Attributed to Matteo” is defensible; “probably by Matteo” is substantially more confident than the modern evidence warrants.

## The system in detail

## 1. Giovanni da San Gimignano and the preacher’s memory

### Author, institution, and work

Giovanni da San Gimignano was a Dominican preacher active around the turn of the fourteenth century. His *Liber* or *Summa de exemplis ac similitudinibus rerum* is an encyclopedic collection organized to supply preachers with comparisons, natural lore, moral applications, and sermon material. Its printed title advertises precisely this function: *verbi Dei concionatoribus cunctisque literarum studiosis maximo usui futura*—“destined to be of the greatest use to preachers of the word of God and to all students of letters.” The 1497 Venice printing is bibliographically described as an extensive encyclopedia of natural, legal, and moral materials for sermons. [ARLIMA’s bibliography and census](https://arlima.net/eh/giovanni_da_san_gimignano.html), [University of Salamanca digital copy](https://gredos.usal.es/handle/10366/144078?locale-attribute=en), [description of the 1497 edition](https://www.gonnelli.it/it/asta-0019/johannes-de-sancto-geminiano-summa-de-exemplis.asp).

**Status: documented bibliography and explicit paratext.**

The work is generally dated between 1298 and 1314. That interval is a scholarly reconstruction from Giovanni’s career and internal or external evidence, not a date written in the surviving printed editions.

### Giovanni’s four rules

In VI.42 Giovanni condenses Thomas Aquinas’s treatment of memory into four propositions. The Latin is conventionally reconstructed as follows:

> *Quatuor sunt quae hominem adiuvant ad bene memorandum. Primum est ut ea quae vult memorari sub aliquo ordine disponat. Secundum est ut eis cum affectione adhaereat. Tertium est ut ea ad insolitas similitudines reducat. Quartum est ut ea frequenti meditatione revocet.*

A close gloss is:

> “There are four things that help a person to remember well. The first is to arrange what he wishes to remember in some definite order. The second is to attach himself to it with affection [or intense attention]. The third is to reduce it to unusual similitudes. The fourth is to recall it by frequent meditation.”

**Status:** The fourfold substance is documented in Giovanni, *Summa*, VI.42, and repeatedly reproduced from that witness. Orthography varies between editions; the exact wording above should not be treated as a diplomatic transcription of a particular manuscript. Giovanni’s early printed text, rather than a modern critical edition, remains the normal reference.

The rules correspond closely to Aquinas, *Summa theologiae* II–II, q. 49, a. 1, ad 2:

1. construct appropriate but unusual corporeal similitudes;
2. arrange what is to be remembered in a considered order;
3. apply solicitude and affection;
4. meditate frequently on the material.

Aquinas explicitly states that memory is natural but can be assisted by art and diligence, citing “Tully’s Rhetoric,” meaning the then-Ciceronian *Ad Herennium*. [Latin and English text of Aquinas, II–II.49.1](https://www.drbo.org/summa/question/28201.htm).

**Status: documented dependence in substance; “abbreviates Thomas” is a well-supported source-critical inference.**

### What serves as places and images?

Giovanni’s four-rule paragraph does not itself provide a complete architectural itinerary. “Order” supplies the necessary sequence; *insolitae similitudines* supply memorable images. The fully articulated rules for stable backgrounds, spacing, illumination, distinctiveness, fifth-place markers, and image construction belong to the inherited *Ad Herennium* tradition rather than to Giovanni’s abbreviated passage.

That distinction matters. Giovanni is not demonstrably teaching a freestanding technical course in memory. He is transmitting its governing psychology inside a reference work for preachers.

**Status: documented absence plus scholarly reconstruction.**

### Encoding and retrieval

A historically plausible reconstruction is:

1. The preacher divides doctrine or sermon matter into an ordered series.
2. Each item is converted into a striking likeness, example, personification, action, or narrative scene.
3. The order—whether architectural, textual, topical, or sermonic—provides the retrieval path.
4. Emotion and unusualness deepen the imprint.
5. Repeated mental traversal stabilizes the material.
6. In delivery, the preacher follows the ordered sequence and expands each remembered likeness into doctrine or exhortation.

Only items 1, 2, 4, and 5 are explicit in Giovanni’s four rules. The architectural implementation comes from the classical technique; expansion into sermon discourse is inferred from the nature and announced users of the *Summa*.

**Status: mixed—documented rules plus contextual reconstruction.**

### Why exempla matter

A preacher’s *similitudo* is not necessarily an *imago agens* in the strict technical sense. It can be a comparison or moral analogy used to invent or explain a sermon. Nevertheless, vivid exempla are exceptionally suitable mnemonic material. Giovanni’s collection thus serves two connected operations:

- private recollection by the preacher;
- public production of memorable verbal pictures for the audience.

Yates formulated the relation memorably:

> “the memory image is invisible, and remains hidden within the memory of its user, where, however, it can become the hidden generator of externalised imagery.”

Yates, *Art of Memory*, p. 86.

**Status: Yates’s interpretation.** Later work by Mary Carruthers and Lina Bolzoni supports the larger connection among memory, invention, meditation, and preaching, while discouraging the assumption that every vivid sermon-image must have originated in a formal architectural memory palace.

## 2. Bartolomeo da San Concordio’s eight aids to memory

### Author and work

Bartolomeo da San Concordio, born in or near Pisa in 1262 and deceased in 1347, belonged to the Dominican convent of Santa Caterina at Pisa. His Latin *De documentis antiquorum* was subsequently rendered by him into Italian as the *Ammaestramenti degli antichi*. The work is an ordered moral florilegium: authoritative sayings are grouped under ethical distinctions and chapters so that doctrine may be found, learned, and reused.

The terminus “before 1323” is generally accepted for the vernacular work. The textual tradition is extensive, and a new critical-edition project has emphasized that the nineteenth-century editions do not adequately represent all manuscript variation. [Maria Conte, “Gli ‘Ammaestramenti degli Antichi’ … Prime osservazioni in vista dell’edizione critica”](https://books.fupress.com/chapter/gli-ammaestramenti-degli-antichidi-bartolomeo-da-san-concordio-prime-osservazioni-in-vista-delledizi/4196).

**Status: documented authorship; dating is scholarly reconstruction.**

### The eight rules in Italian

At IX.8 Bartolomeo begins:

> *E’ sono otto cose quelle che pare che facciano bene a ricordare.*

“There are eight things which appear to conduce to remembering well.”

The sequence, in the historical Italian wording excerpted by modern scholarship, is:

> *La prima è apparare fino da garzone … La seconda cosa, che fa bene ricordare, si è fortemente attendere … La terza è adoperare la memoria … La quarta è ordinare … La quinta è cominciare dal principio … La sesta è pigliare somiglianze … La settima è di non gravare la memoria di troppe cose … L’ultima cosa, che vale a memoria, sono i versi e le rime, e cotali cose, le quali con diletto e brevemente comprendono le cose.*

Gloss:

1. learn from youth;
2. attend intensely;
3. exercise memory;
4. put things in order;
5. begin from the beginning;
6. take similitudes;
7. do not burden memory with too much;
8. use verses, rhymes, and compact pleasurable forms.

The passage is reproduced and discussed in Silvana Vecchio’s study of Bartolomeo. [Vecchio, “Quasi armarium scripturarum”](https://riviste.unimi.it/index.php/DoctorVirtualis/article/download/2175/2397).

**Status: documented text, with spelling normalized by editions.**

### Bartolomeo’s Latin source-form

The corresponding Latin *De documentis antiquorum* gives the sequence with its authorities. A modern transcription includes:

> *Primum igitur est a pueritia discere.*

> *Secundum est vehementer attendere.*

> *Tertium est frequenter repetere.*

> *Quartum est res ordinare.*

> *Quintum est a principio incipere.*

> *Sextum est similitudines sive ymagines assumere.*

> *Septimum est memoriam non gravare.*

The sixth rule cites:

> *Tullius tertio Nove Rhetorice: “Quorum volumus memoriam habere, ymagines eorum certis locis collocare oportebit.”*

“Tully, in the third book of the New Rhetoric: ‘Of the things we wish to remember, we must place their images in definite places.’”

The transcription and comparison are available in the study of Bartolomeo’s commentary on Geoffrey of Vinsauf’s *Poetria nova*. [“Il commento di Bartolomeo da San Concordio alla Poetria nova”](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/324615597_Il_commento_di_Bartolomeo_da_San_Concordio_alla_Poetria_nova_alcuni_appunti_Modelli_fortuna_commenti).

**Status: documented text.**

The authorities cited include Plato, Aristotle’s *De memoria et reminiscentia*, Thomas’s commentary on Aristotle, Seneca, Gregory, Horace, and pseudo-Cicero. This is not a simple repetition of Aquinas’s four rules. It is a pedagogical synthesis joining childhood formation, attention, habituation, orderly recollection, visual mnemonics, limitation of cognitive load, association, and verse.

### Order and retrieval

Bartolomeo’s fourth and fifth rules describe the heart of recollection:

> *Sunt magis reminiscibilia quaecumque ordinationem habent.*

“Whatever things possess an order are more readily recollected.”

And, through Thomas:

> *quando quaerimus aliquem versum psalmi, inquirimus a capite.*

“When we seek some verse of a psalm, we search from its beginning.”

This follows Aristotle’s account of recollection as initiating a movement whose customary sequel leads to the desired memory. Aristotle describes movement from a starting point through an ordered chain and explicitly remarks that people recollect through “places.” [Aristotle, *De memoria et reminiscentia* 451b–452a](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/memory.html); [Greek text, Bekker edition](https://atlas.perseus.tufts.edu/library/urn%3Acts%3AgreekLit%3Atlg0086.tlg024/).

**Status: documented philosophical source and documented medieval reuse.**

Bartolomeo’s practical retrieval method is therefore broader than a modern “memory palace”:

- begin from a known head or initial item;
- allow the ordered association to elicit the next;
- use places and images when helpful;
- reinforce by repetition;
- compress in verse where possible.

### “Lay” purpose

The *Ammaestramenti* is in Italian, and its ethical material is plainly accessible outside university Latin. That makes lay readership highly likely. But “written for laymen” is an inference from language, genre, and transmission, not a surviving dedication to all lay Christians.

**Status: strong scholarly reconstruction, not an explicit universal audience statement.**

Its headings, distinctions, citations, and compact sayings also constitute a textual memory architecture. Carruthers’s scholarship is important here: medieval *memoria* was not merely rote storage, and an actual building was not always required. Ordered manuscript pages, headings, lists, diagrams, concordances, and mental “seats” could all support composition and recollection.

**Status: later scholarly correction and enlargement of Yates’s architectural emphasis.**

## 3. Bono Giamboni and the detached *Trattato della memoria artificiale*

### What the work actually is

The text traditionally printed after Bartolomeo as *Trattato della memoria artificiale* is not Bartolomeo’s composition. It is derived from the memory section in book III of the pseudo-Ciceronian *Rhetorica ad Herennium*, as translated in the α recension of Bono Giamboni’s *Fiore di rettorica*. Its prologue can be textually connected with Giamboni’s larger rhetoric even when the memory portion circulated separately.

The history of the error is unusually traceable:

- Domenico Maria Manni appended the text to his 1734 edition of Bartolomeo as a possibly Bartolomean work.
- Giuseppe Nannucci in 1840 rejected Bartolomeo’s authorship and recognized material from the vernacular rhetoric.
- Later editors nevertheless continued printing it with the *Ammaestramenti*.
- Gian Battista Speroni’s study of the *Fiore di rettorica* established that it belongs to that work’s α recension.
- Modern manuscript cataloguing accordingly describes it as a volgarizzamento of *Ad Herennium* III with an independent afterlife.

[Detailed modern catalogue note tracing Manni, Nannucci, and Speroni](https://iris.cnr.it/bitstream/20.500.14243/259603/1/prod_275766-doc_77258.pdf).

**Status: documented philological correction.**

Yates already recognized the central point, calling it Giamboni’s translated memory section, though older bibliographical language around “Bartolomeo’s treatise” remains confusing.

### The classical system transmitted

The *Ad Herennium* states:

> *Constat igitur artificiosa memoria ex locis et imaginibus.*

“Artificial memory therefore consists of places and images.”

It defines a place as a small, complete, conspicuous natural or artificial scene—a house, intercolumnation, recess, arch, or equivalent—and an image as a figure, mark, or likeness of what is to be remembered. [*Rhetorica ad Herennium* III.16–24, Latin and Caplan translation](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Rhetorica_ad_Herennium/3%2A.html).

The governing analogy is:

- places = wax tablets or papyrus;
- images = letters;
- disposition of images = writing;
- oral recollection = reading.

A durable, repeatedly reusable series of loci holds temporary images. Retrieval proceeds by mentally traversing the series. If the sequence is firmly known, it can be entered at the beginning, middle, or end and traversed forward or backward.

### Rules for places

The inherited rules include:

- acquire many loci if much is to be remembered;
- fix them in an unambiguous series;
- learn them so thoroughly that they remain when temporary images are erased;
- choose quiet rather than crowded settings;
- make loci distinct in form;
- avoid excessive resemblance;
- avoid loci that are too large or too small;
- use moderate intervals;
- choose adequate illumination;
- mark every fifth locus—for example with a golden hand—and every tenth with an acquaintance named Decimus.

These rules are documented in *Ad Herennium* III.16–19. Their presence in any particular vernacular witness depends on recension and completeness; corrupt copies may omit, compress, or distort them.

### Rules for images

The classical text distinguishes:

- *imagines rerum*: images for things, arguments, or subject matter;
- *imagines verborum*: images for individual words.

Effective images should be active, exceptional, emotionally charged, beautiful, ugly, comic, bloody, dishonourable, or otherwise arresting rather than ordinary and weak. The system is not intrinsically religious: Christian moralization enters through selection of subject matter and scholastic interpretation.

### The lawsuit image

The *Ad Herennium* gives the canonical worked example:

> “For example, the prosecutor has said that the defendant killed a man by poison, has charged that the motive for the crime was an inheritance, and declared that there are many witnesses and accessories to this act. … We shall place the defendant at the bedside, holding in his right hand a cup, and in his left tablets, and on the fourth finger a ram’s testicles.”

[*Ad Herennium* III.20; Caplan translation](https://jasontham.com/wp-content/uploads/2015/05/rhetorica-ad-herennium_3-11-19-3-24-40_trans-caplan-1954.pdf).

The elements cue:

- cup: poison;
- tablets or will: inheritance;
- ram’s testicles: witnesses, through a Latin sound association conventionally explained from *testes*.

**Status: documented classical example.**

This example belongs to the ancestor-text, not demonstrably to Giovanni’s or Bartolomeo’s personal practice. Its inclusion in Giamboni’s translation made it available to Italian readers.

### Historical significance of detachment

Yates writes:

> “The detached memory section of *Ad Herennium* in Giamboni’s translation, circulating by itself, is an ancestor of the separate *Ars memorativa* treatise.”

Yates, p. 89.

That is persuasive as a genealogy of textual form: a component of rhetoric has become a portable technical manual. It should not be converted into the stronger claim that this one Italian detachment was the unique progenitor of every later *ars memorativa*. Other Latin mnemonic compendia and scholastic discussions circulated independently.

**Status: Yates’s interpretation, valid in a qualified rather than exclusive sense.**

## 4. The *Rosaio della vita* and its *Ars memorie artificialis*

### The ethical book

The *Rosaio della vita* or *Rosarium odor vitae* is a vernacular moral anthology of eighty-four chapters, drawing brief “fragrant” sayings from classical, biblical, patristic, and medieval authorities. The text identifies 1373 as its date.

Polidori’s edition is:

> *Rosaio della vita: trattato morale attribuito a Matteo de’ Corsini e composto nel MCCCLXXIII*, ed. Filippo-Luigi Polidori, Florence: Società Tipografica Italiana, 1845.

[Open Library bibliographical record](https://openlibrary.org/works/OL7535290W/Rosaio_della_vita); [WorldCat record](https://search.worldcat.org/title/Rosaio-della-vita-trattato-morale-attribuito-a/oclc/6828051).

**Status: documented edition and textual date; disputed authorship.**

### The anonymous mnemonic supplement

The supplement begins:

> *Poi che hauiamo fornito il libro di leggere resta di potere tenere a mente.*

“Now that we have provided the book to read, it remains to be able to hold it in mind.”

It later names the *Rosaio della vita*, establishing that the instructions are intended for retaining that book, not merely transmitted beside it by accident. Rossi printed the text from Florence, Biblioteca Nazionale Centrale, Palatino 54, fols. 140–142, and Conventi Soppressi J.I.47, unnumbered leaves, in *Clavis universalis*, appendix I, pp. 271–75.

**Status: documented textual cross-reference and edition.**

### Places

The author divides places into two sorts:

- “natural” loci: remembered in the countryside, exemplified by trees in fields;
- “artificial” loci: remembered in buildings, exemplified by a study, window, coffer, and similar fixtures.

This terminology is idiosyncratic. In the *Ad Herennium*, both a natural landscape and a deliberately selected architectural space can function as mnemonic backgrounds; “natural memory” normally means the innate faculty, not country scenery. The *Rosaio Ars* has reclassified loci according to their physical setting.

**Status: documented text; interpretation of its departure from classical terminology is scholarly analysis.**

### Images and moral content

Corporeal likenesses are to represent the virtues and vices enumerated by the *Rosaio*. The technique is thus:

1. establish a route through known trees, rooms, windows, coffers, or comparable stations;
2. preserve the order of those stations;
3. translate an ethical abstraction into a corporeal likeness;
4. place one or more likenesses at the proper locus;
5. rehearse;
6. retrieve the moral series by revisiting the loci.

The surviving instructions do not supply a complete illustrated iconographic cycle for every virtue and vice. There is no surviving plate showing a standardized “Rosaio memory palace.”

**Status: documented mechanism; documented absence of a complete surviving image series.**

### Diagrams, wheels, tables, and plates

No wheel, combinatorial table, or monumental theatre plan belongs to this system. Those forms characterize other traditions—Lull’s combinatorial figures, Camillo’s theatre, Bruno’s wheels, and later printed mnemonic diagrams.

The surviving material is verbal instruction in miscellany manuscripts. Any modern diagram mapping the *Rosaio* onto a house or garden is a reconstruction.

**Status: negative finding and warning against conflation.**

### The purpose

Yates concludes:

> “The artificial memory begins to appear as a lay devotional discipline, fostered and recommended by the friars.”

Yates, p. 91.

The first half is well supported: a vernacular moral book is explicitly coupled to mnemonic instructions. “Fostered by the friars” is plausible within the Dominican transmission reconstructed through Giovanni and Bartolomeo, but the anonymous *Ars* itself is not signed by a Dominican, and the *Rosaio* author is uncertain.

**Status: partly documented, partly Yatesian reconstruction.**

## 4. Bono Giamboni’s *Fiore di rettorica* and the *Trattato*

Principal witnesses named in the Yates/Rossi discussion include:

- Florence, Biblioteca Nazionale Centrale, Palatino 54.
- Florence, Biblioteca Nazionale Centrale, Conventi Soppressi J.I.47.
- Vatican City, Biblioteca Apostolica Vaticana, Barberiniano latino 3929, fol. 52, where the separately circulating text was attributed by a contemporary note to Brunetto Latini.
- Florence, Biblioteca Riccardiana, MSS 57 and 59, cited for conjunctions involving the *Rosaio* and memory texts.

The Barberini attribution to Brunetto is erroneous. Brunetto made a free Italian adaptation associated with Cicero’s *De inventione*, not this translation of the *Ad Herennium*. He nevertheless knew the distinction between natural and artificial memory and writes in *Li Livres dou Tresor* III of:

> *memore artificiel que l’en aquiert par ensegnement des sages*

“artificial memory, which one acquires through the teaching of wise men.”

Standard edition: Francis J. Carmody, ed., *Li Livres dou Tresor*, Berkeley: University of California Press, 1948, p. 321.

**Status: documented text and corrected attribution.**

## Yates’s account and scholarship since

## 1. What Yates argued

Chapter IV presents a medieval transformation of the classical art:

- rhetoric’s memory precepts enter Christian ethics through Prudence;
- Albert the Great and Aquinas legitimate corporeal similitudes;
- Dominican preachers require vivid forms for doctrine, virtues, vices, reward, and punishment;
- sermon-making becomes the medieval functional successor to forensic oratory;
- vernacular ethical books extend organized memory to lay readers;
- inward images may generate outward verbal or painted imagery.

At pp. 85–86, Giovanni demonstrates the transmission from Thomas into a preacher’s book of similitudes. At pp. 87–90, Bartolomeo and Giamboni show movement into Italian and the detachment of the classical technical section. At pp. 90–91, the *Rosaio* provides an explicit moral application. Page 95 n. 35 connects the documentary discussion to the problem of imagery; p. 103 returns to Giovanni’s similitudes of Prudence in relation to Dante.

## 2. What has been confirmed

### Memory as more than rote storage

Mary Carruthers’s *The Book of Memory* confirms Yates’s fundamental recovery of medieval *memoria* as a trained, culturally central faculty. Carruthers expands the subject beyond the narrow places-and-images art: memory is also an instrument of reading, meditation, composition, judgment, and invention.

**Status: confirmed and enlarged.**

### Affect and corporeal form

Carruthers confirms the importance of affective intensity and bodily images but treats them as part of a broad medieval psychology of inscription and recollection. The mental image functions more like cognitive writing than like a modern naturalistic picture.

**Status: qualification of Yates’s occasionally pictorial language.**

### Preaching and public imagery

Lina Bolzoni’s studies, especially *La rete delle immagini* and *The Web of Images*, document the close interaction of vernacular preaching, verbal visualization, gestures, devotional objects, civic space, and remembered doctrine. This supports Yates’s intuition that sermon culture externalized memorable images.

**Status: contextual confirmation, not proof that a particular fresco directly copies a private memory image.**

### Ordered manuscripts as memory machinery

Carruthers, Small, and later manuscript scholarship emphasize mise-en-page, rubrication, tables, indexes, diagrams, and textual divisions. Bartolomeo’s carefully segmented florilegium is itself an apparatus for locating and recombining moral authorities.

**Status: major extension beyond Yates.**

## 3. What has been corrected

### The Bartolomeo attribution

The detached *Trattato* is not by Bartolomeo. Nannucci had already rejected that attribution in 1840; Speroni’s recension study supplied firmer philological proof. The error originated in its manuscript proximity to the *Ammaestramenti* and Manni’s 1734 editorial decision.

**Status: overturned attribution.**

### Matteo Corsini

Yates repeated the older formula “probably by Matteo de’ Corsini.” The *Dizionario Biografico degli Italiani* notes that Luigi Passerini’s attribution won no modern consensus. Polidori himself considered Lorenzo di Giovanni Corsini, provost of Poggibonsi, a plausible alternative because the author appears clerical and refers to another work, *De quaestionibus*. [Treccani, “Matteo Corsini”](https://www.treccani.it/enciclopedia/matteo-corsini_%28Dizionario-Biografico%29/).

**Status: disputed; anonymous is the safest present designation.**

### Direct derivations from mental image to artwork

Yates’s phrase “hidden generator of externalised imagery” is a fruitful hypothesis, not a demonstrated transmission mechanism. A vivid fresco, allegorical figure, or sermon does not prove that its maker used loci and images privately. Later scholarship tends to describe a shared mnemonic-rhetorical culture rather than one-way copying from memory palace to wall.

**Status: Yatesian interpretation, partly supported but not demonstrable in individual cases without further evidence.**

### “First vernacular art of memory”

Claims that Bartolomeo’s work is “the first” vernacular treatment require qualification. Giamboni’s and Guidotto da Bologna’s thirteenth-century rhetorical translations precede it, although Guidotto omits the memory section. Bartolomeo may be among the earliest vernacular ethical syntheses to discuss aids to memory, but Giamboni supplies the earlier Italian technical translation.

**Status: corrected priority claim.**

## 4. Scholarship of limited direct relevance

The user’s requested Bruno, Lull, Camillo, Ramus, and early-modern authorities are indispensable to the history of the larger catalogue, but they do not materially change the reconstruction of this particular system:

- Bonner and Fidora distinguish Lull’s demonstrative-combinatorial Art from classical local memory.
- Sturlese, Ciliberto, Clucas, and Gatti have complicated Yates’s “Hermetic Bruno,” emphasizing Bruno’s logic, metaphysics, textual development, and differing mnemonic works.
- Bolzoni and Stagno have reconstructed Camillo’s theatre through manuscripts, patronage, and literary culture.
- Ong showed that Ramist method is a visual and pedagogical reorganization of discourse, not simply the annihilation of memory.
- Rossi traced continuities from mnemonic arts to combinatorics and universal-language projects.

These are descendants or contrasts, not evidence that the fourteenth-century Dominican witnesses contained wheels, talismans, Hermetic magic, or universal-language machinery.

## History and context

## 1. Rhetoric

In classical rhetoric *memoria* is the fourth of the five parts: invention, arrangement, style, memory, delivery. The *Ad Herennium* system originally serves advocates and speakers. Medieval preaching inherits the practical problem of delivering ordered speech, but its matter becomes scriptural, ethical, and penitential.

**Status: documented institutional transformation.**

## 2. Scholastic psychology

Aristotle held that memory concerns the past and operates through an image or *phantasma*. Recollection is an active search along associative sequences. Albert and Thomas integrate this psychology with Prudence: prudent action requires remembered experience, while art can discipline memory through images, order, care, and rehearsal.

This theological placement is decisive. Artificial memory is no longer merely a rhetorician’s trick; it can assist moral judgment.

**Status: documented scholastic reinterpretation.**

## 3. Dominican institutions

The Order of Preachers organized study around convent schools, provincial schools, and university studia. Dominican ministry required:

- scriptural and theological learning;
- ready access to distinctions and authorities;
- preparation and delivery of sermons;
- pastoral teaching of virtues and vices;
- confession and moral counsel.

Giovanni’s preacher’s encyclopedia and Bartolomeo’s ordered ethical compendium fit these needs. Pisa’s Santa Caterina was an important Dominican intellectual centre.

**Status: historical context; direct classroom use of these precise memory passages remains unrecorded.**

## 4. Lay religious culture

The Italian translations carry authoritative moral learning outside Latin schools. The *Rosaio* converts ethical doctrine into concise vernacular units, and its attached *Ars* explicitly tells the reader how to retain them.

“Lay devotional discipline” is therefore a defensible description, although the users could also have included lower clergy, religious women, merchants, notaries, or educated household readers.

**Status: strong reconstruction from language and codicology.**

## 5. Franciscan parallels

Franciscan sermon culture likewise cultivated exempla, affective images, vernacular instruction, meditations on Christ’s life, and visual preaching. But the three textual witnesses here are Dominican or transmitted in a Dominican interpretive genealogy. Calling this a jointly Dominican–Franciscan system would overstate the direct evidence.

## 6. Courts and universities

The lawsuit example preserves the system’s forensic origin. Bartolomeo’s university and conventual learning supplied Aristotle, Thomas, Seneca, and rhetorical authorities. Yet the *Rosaio* adaptation belongs more naturally to household, confraternal, mercantile, or devotional reading than to courtroom procedure.

## 7. Descent and successors

A responsible genealogy is:

*Ad Herennium* and Aristotle  
→ medieval Latin rhetorical and psychological commentary  
→ Albert and Thomas on Prudence and memory  
→ Giovanni’s preacher’s summary  
→ Bartolomeo’s ethical-pedagogical synthesis  
→ Giamboni’s vernacular transmission and textual detachment  
→ *Rosaio*’s applied lay mnemonic  
→ fifteenth-century independent *artes memorativae*  
→ Publicius, Peter of Ravenna, Romberch, Dolce, and other printed manuals.

This lineage is not genetically simple: several branches drew independently on the classical text.

## People

### Practitioners and transmitters

- **Giovanni da San Gimignano:** Dominican compiler and preacher; transmits the Thomistic fourfold formulation.
- **Thomas Aquinas:** scholastic authority who places trained memory under Prudence.
- **Albert the Great:** earlier Dominican defender of artificial memory’s ethical use.
- **Bartolomeo da San Concordio:** Dominican compiler and translator; expands the rules into a practical eightfold pedagogy.
- **Bono Giamboni:** Florentine judge, rhetorician, and translator; transmitted the technical classical section in Italian.
- **Anonymous *Rosaio* author:** composed the 1373 ethical anthology.
- **Anonymous author of the associated *Ars*:** supplied the explicit application to the *Rosaio*.
- **Domenico Maria Manni:** eighteenth-century editor responsible for institutionalizing the misleading Bartolomeo attribution.
- **Giuseppe Nannucci:** rejected that attribution in 1840.
- **Filippo-Luigi Polidori:** edited the *Rosaio* in 1845.
- **Paolo Rossi:** printed the anonymous *Ars* and analyzed the manuscript grouping before Yates.
- **Frances Yates:** synthesized the materials into the Dominican-to-lay transmission narrative.

### Patrons and students

No patron is securely attached to the composition of the three mnemonic passages. No named student is documented as having learned the *Rosaio* through its loci.

### Requested anecdotes

The auctioneer, the professor’s party, the student who lost his memory, and the accused magician belong to other episodes and practitioners in the wider history of artificial memory. None can be securely connected to Giovanni, Bartolomeo, Giamboni, or the *Rosaio* manuscripts.

To insert them here as anecdotes of this system would conflate catalogue entries.

**Status: absence of evidence.**

## The magic question

## 1. Was the system magical?

No evidence in these texts makes it magical, talismanic, astral, demonic, or sacramental.

Its operative causes are ordinary within medieval psychology:

- ordered association;
- corporeal imagination;
- emotional salience;
- repetition;
- habituation;
- rhythmic compression;
- manageable quantity.

**Status: documented text and negative finding.**

## 2. Was it heretical?

No surviving accusation of heresy is attached to any of the relevant passages. Aquinas’s incorporation of artificial memory into Prudence gave the technique exceptionally strong orthodox authorization. A Dominican moral work citing Aristotle, Thomas, Scripture, and “Tully” would not thereby be suspect.

**Status: negative documentary finding.**

## 3. Relation to the *Ars notoria*

The *Ars notoria* promised knowledge or enhanced intellectual faculties through prayers, ritual observances, and complex *notae*, sometimes involving angelic revelation. It circulated in medieval learned environments and could attract condemnation.

The resemblance is limited to the desired enhancement of memory or learning. The methods, theory, and textual genealogy differ:

- Dominican/classical artificial memory: human art, images, order, exercise;
- *Ars notoria*: ritual operations, prayers, revelatory or angelic agency.

Calling the *Rosaio Ars* an *ars notoria* is a modern conflation.

**Status: documented distinction.**

## 4. Why later readers associate memory with magic

Renaissance authors—especially Bruno—combined mnemonic machinery with cosmology, astrology, divine names, and images susceptible to magical interpretation. Yates’s book moves from medieval scholastic memory to these later systems. Readers sometimes project the later occult atmosphere backward onto all memory arts.

That projection is historically unsound for this entry.

**Status: modern interpretive error.**

## Afterlife

## 1. Dante and the visual imagination

Yates proposes that Giovanni’s similitudes of Prudence help illuminate the imagery of the *Commedia*. The broader connection is credible: Dante and Dominican preachers share scholastic categories, exempla, personification, spatial order, reward and punishment, and intensely memorable scenes.

No document establishes that Dante read *Summa de exemplis* VI.42 or used Giovanni’s particular loci.

**Status: suggestive Yatesian comparison, not proven source dependence.**

## 2. Frescoes and sermon-pictures

Medieval church cycles of virtues, vices, Last Judgment, punishments, and biblical history functioned mnemonically for viewers. Preachers could point to, evoke, and verbally reactivate such images.

However:

- external religious art can aid memory without being generated by a formal *ars memoriae*;
- a mental image can resemble a fresco without one being copied from the other;
- documentary proof must be established case by case.

**Status: cultural affinity strongly supported; direct derivations disputed.**

## 3. Emblem books

The combination of concise saying, striking image, and moral interpretation anticipates the cognitive structure of Renaissance emblem books. This is a typological continuity rather than a demonstrated direct descent from the *Rosaio Ars*.

## 4. Printed memory arts

The detached Giamboni section anticipates the separately printed manual. Publicius, Peter of Ravenna, Romberch, Dolce, and others increasingly formalized routes, alphabets, human figures, and rules for rapid application.

**Status: historical continuity, with multiple sources.**

## 5. Lull, combinatorics, and Leibniz

Lull’s Art is not a conventional memory palace. Its alphabetic figures and rotating combinations are machines for inquiry and demonstration. Renaissance authors sometimes fused Lullism with local memory. Rossi traced that composite tradition toward encyclopedism, universal language, and Leibnizian calculation.

The Dominican vernacular sequence contributes the themes of order, addressability, and recombination, but no direct line runs from the *Rosaio*’s trees and coffers to the binary computer.

**Status: long-range intellectual genealogy, not direct technical ancestry.**

## 6. Borges, Eco, and modern literature

Borges’s Funes embodies the nightmare of indiscriminate total recall: memory without abstraction or forgetting. Eco’s interest in lists, encyclopedias, semiotics, and perfect-language projects draws on Rossi and the broader historical tradition.

Neither is a specific reception of Giovanni or the *Rosaio*.

**Status: thematic afterlife.**

## 7. Contemporary memory palaces and athletes

Modern competitors use spatial routes, bizarre images, person-action-object encoding, number systems, and intensive rehearsal. These procedures genuinely resemble the ancient places-and-images method.

Modern “memory palace” courses often:

- omit the ethical framework of Prudence;
- disregard sermon invention;
- treat images as private data encodings;
- present neuroscience or productivity claims unknown to medieval writers.

A modern reconstruction using a garden, study, window, and coffer could faithfully imitate the *Rosaio Ars*, but it would remain a reconstruction because the original full image inventory has not survived.

**Status: genuine technical revival plus substantial modern reinvention.**

## Controversies and disputes

| Claim | Classification | Assessment |
|---|---|---|
| Giovanni invented the four rules | Disputed/incorrect | He transmits and abbreviates Aquinas, who synthesizes Aristotle and pseudo-Cicero. |
| Giovanni gives a complete architectural palace | Incorrect | His surviving paragraph gives principles, not a full locus manual. |
| Bartolomeo wrote the appended *Trattato* | Overturned attribution | The text belongs to Bono Giamboni’s *Fiore di rettorica*, α recension. |
| The *Trattato* was always part of the *Ammaestramenti* | Incorrect | The 1342 manuscript does not contain it; conjunction occurs in later witnesses and print. |
| Manni first asserted certain Bartolomean authorship | Overstatement | His 1734 edition proposed or encouraged attribution; later repetition hardened it. |
| Brunetto Latini translated this *Ad Herennium* section | Incorrect tradition | A Barberini manuscript assigns it to Brunetto, but modern philology identifies Giamboni’s translation. |
| The *Rosaio* is by Matteo de’ Corsini | Disputed | Based chiefly on a manuscript annotation and older family-historical argument; no modern consensus. |
| Lorenzo di Giovanni Corsini wrote the *Rosaio* | Disputed alternative | Compatible with the apparent clerical profile, but not proved. |
| The *Rosaio* was composed in 1373 | Documented textual date | More secure than its authorship. |
| The anonymous *Ars* was written by Bartolomeo | Chronologically implausible and unsupported | It refers to the later *Rosaio*. Rossi explicitly warned against secure attribution to Bartolomeo. |
| Palatino 54 and J.I.47 prove Dominican authorship of the *Ars* | Incorrect inference | Codicological association demonstrates reception and compilation, not authorship. |
| Laypeople demonstrably used the full system | Scholarly reconstruction | Vernacular language and explicit instructions make it likely; named users and performance records are absent. |
| Medieval frescoes are externalized memory-palace images | Yatesian hypothesis | Possible in some cases, but generally not demonstrable from visual resemblance alone. |
| The system is magical | Unsupported | No invocations, talismans, spirits, astrological timing, or operative occult claims occur. |
| The system descends directly into modern computing | Modern grand narrative | Only a mediated genealogy through ordering, combinatorics, symbolic languages, and mechanization is defensible. |
| “Dominican transmission to preachers and laymen” is a medieval name | Modern invention | It is an analytical catalogue label, not the title used by historical practitioners. |
| “System 11 of 45” is historical | Modern invention | It belongs to the modern memoria catalogue. |

## Open questions

1. A complete modern critical edition of Giovanni’s *Summa* is needed to establish the wording and manuscript variation of VI.42.

2. The precise relationship between Giovanni’s four-rule paragraph and particular recensions of Aquinas’s works deserves a stemmatic study.

3. Bartolomeo’s *De documentis* and *Ammaestramenti* require full comparative editing to distinguish authorial translation from later scribal adjustment.

4. Palatino 54 and Conventi Soppressi J.I.47 should be re-described codicologically, including hands, quire structure, inks, rubrics, and the sequence in which the mnemonic items were entered.

5. The full manuscript census of Giamboni’s α recension needs to be compared with the independent *Trattato* tradition.

6. The former Palatina and Magliabechiana shelfmarks associated with the *Rosaio* require conversion to present BNCF identifiers.

7. The authorship of the *Rosaio* remains unresolved. Linguistic localization, source use, and comparison with securely attributed Corsini writings may narrow the field.

8. The anonymous *Ars* needs a new edition from every witness, not merely the two printed by Rossi.

9. Its terms “natural” and “artificial” place may reveal a local teaching tradition or simply an anonymous compiler’s reinterpretation.

10. No surviving evidence shows exactly which corporeal images were assigned to each virtue and vice. Reconstruction from contemporary iconography would be possible but speculative.

11. The relation between these memory texts and actual Dominican sermon notebooks remains insufficiently documented.

12. Yates’s proposed passage from inward mnemonic imagery to fresco and literary imagery is better treated as a research question than as an established causal law.
