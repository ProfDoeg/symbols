# Trecento painting and Dante as memory imagery: Research Dossier

## Basic identification

**Name:** Trecento painting and Dante as memory imagery  
**Catalogue position:** System 12 of 45  
**Exponents:** Ambrogio Lorenzetti; Giotto di Bondone; Dante Alighieri; retrospectively, Johannes Host von Romberch and Lodovico Dolce; Nardo di Cione as a later pictorial analogue  
**Dates:** Giotto’s Scrovegni/Arena Chapel, 1303–1305; Dante’s *Commedia*, approximately 1307–1321; Lorenzetti’s Sienese frescoes, 1338–1339, not properly “1337–40”; Nardo’s Strozzi Chapel frescoes, probably approximately 1354–1357; Romberch’s *Congestorium*, 1520, revised printing 1533; Dolce’s *Dialogo*, 1562, reprinted 1586  
**Principal sources:** *Divina Commedia*; Scrovegni Chapel, Padua; the Sala dei Nove frescoes, Palazzo Pubblico, Siena; Strozzi Chapel, Santa Maria Novella, Florence; Romberch, *Congestorium artificiose memorie*; Dolce, *Dialogo nel quale si ragiona del modo di accrescere, et conservar la memoria*  
**Principle in one sentence:** Frances Yates proposes that ordered Trecento cycles of virtues, vices, punishments, and celestial regions can be read “with the eyes of memory”—stable spatial sequences occupied by emotionally forceful corporeal images—but neither Dante nor the painters explicitly presents his work as an artificial-memory apparatus, so the claim is best treated as a historically informed interpretation rather than a documented system.  
**Yates:** *The Art of Memory*, Preface, p. xii; ch. IV, especially pp. 92–96; foundations at pp. 59–60 and 74–80; plates 2, 3a–b, and 8a. Pagination refers to the 1966 Routledge & Kegan Paul/University of Chicago text and may vary in later reprints.

## Evidentiary key

The labels used throughout mean:

- **[D] Documented:** expressly stated in a primary text, inscription, archival document, or surviving object.
- **[R] Scholarly reconstruction:** an inference supported by historical context, formal analysis, or reception evidence.
- **[T] Tradition/reception:** a claim made by premodern commentators, copyists, artists, or transmitters, whether or not historically provable.
- **[Y] Yates’s interpretation:** a proposition specifically advanced or strongly suggested by Yates.
- **[X] Disputed:** attribution, date, source, or interpretation on which scholarship materially disagrees.
- **[L] Legend:** an anecdote or belief transmitted without adequate contemporary documentation.
- **[M] Modern invention or analogy:** a recent application, performance, or comparison that should not be projected backward.

## The system in detail

### 1. What kind of “system” is this?

This catalogue item is not one historically unified mnemonic system comparable to the *Rhetorica ad Herennium*, Lull’s Art, Camillo’s Theatre, or Bruno’s wheels. It combines four different objects:

1. monumental paintings arranged in architectural space;
2. Dante’s ordered poetic cosmos;
3. later paintings of the afterlife influenced by Dante;
4. sixteenth-century mnemonic manuals explicitly proposing Hell as a store of places.

**[D]** Only the fourth category is unquestionably a technical artificial-memory system. Romberch’s treatise expressly divides artificial memory into *loci* and *imagines* and treats Hell as usable spatial material.

**[Y]** Yates’s originality is to connect all four categories. She asks whether the same habits of spatial ordering and forceful visualization that governed trained memory also conditioned Trecento literature and monumental art.

**[R]** Later work by Mary Carruthers and Lina Bolzoni substantially supports the broader proposition that medieval reading, composition, meditation, and image-making were mnemonic practices. It does not establish that Lorenzetti, Giotto, or Dante secretly encoded a textbook memory palace.

The safest formulation is therefore:

> The works are mnemonic in organization and reception; their identification as formal artificial-memory systems remains unproved.

### 2. The classical technical substrate

The governing model is the anonymous Latin *Rhetorica ad Herennium*, III.16–24, composed in the early first century BCE and attributed throughout much of the Middle Ages to Cicero.

**[D]** Its formula is:

> “Constat igitur artificiosa memoria ex locis et imaginibus.”  
> Artificial memory, then, consists of places and images.  
> —*Rhetorica ad Herennium* III.16.29, ed. and trans. Harry Caplan, Loeb Classical Library 403 (1954).

A *locus* is:

> “locus est ab natura aut manu brevis, perfectus, insignis, quem facile memoria naturali comprendere et amplecti queamus, ut aedes, intercolumnium, angulum, fornicem et alia quae his similia sunt.”

That is, a naturally or artificially bounded, complete, conspicuous setting easily grasped by memory—a house, space between columns, corner, arch, and the like.

An *imago* is:

> “forma, nota, simulacrum eius rei quam meminisse volumus.”

A form, mark, or likeness of what one wishes to remember.

The analogy governing storage and retrieval is:

> “Nam loci cerae aut cartae simillimi sunt, imagines litteris, dispositio et conlocatio imaginum scripturae, pronuntiatio lectioni.”

Places resemble wax tablets or papyrus; images resemble letters; their arrangement resembles writing; delivery resembles reading.

The places must remain stable. Images can be erased and replaced, just as writing is erased from wax.

#### Rules for places

**[D]** *Ad Herennium* III.16–19 prescribes places that are:

- clearly ordered;
- neither too bright nor too dim;
- neither crowded nor deserted in ways that distract;
- distinct from one another;
- moderate in size;
- separated by a suitable interval, conventionally about thirty feet;
- marked at every fifth place, for example by a golden hand, so the practitioner can count and reverse direction;
- familiar through repeated mental traversal.

Retrieval occurs by mentally walking the same ordered sequence and “reading” the images deposited there. A trained practitioner can proceed forward or backward.

#### Rules for images

**[D]** Images must be personally effective and exceptionally striking:

> “Imagines igitur nos in eo genere constituere oportebit quod genus in memoria diutissime potest haerere.”

They should be made memorable through exceptional beauty or ugliness, crowns or bloodstained clothing, deformity, ridicule, astonishment, action, or emotional violence. These are the celebrated *imagines agentes*, “active” or “acting images.”

#### The lawsuit image

The primary worked example compresses an accusation of poisoning, inheritance, witnesses, and accessories into one grotesque tableau:

> “Constituemus ipsum aegrum in lecto cubantem, si formam eius detinebimus; si eum non agnoverimus, aliquem aegrotum non de minimo loco sumemus, ut cito in mentem venire possit. Et reum ad lectum eius adstituemus, dextra poculum, sinistra tabulas, medico testiculos arietinos tenentem.”

The textual tradition and modern punctuation vary over the precise attachment of *medico/testiculos*, but the essential picture is stable: the accused stands at the sick man’s bed, with a cup, tablets, and ram’s testicles. The cup signifies poison; tablets signify the will or inheritance; *testiculi/testes* supplies the pun for witnesses. Other counts are placed successively in other backgrounds. This is a genuine technical mnemonic; nothing equally explicit survives from Giotto, Lorenzetti, or Dante.

### 3. The Thomistic transformation

Albertus Magnus and Thomas Aquinas placed trained memory within Prudence rather than treating it only as a rhetorician’s faculty.

**[D]** Thomas, *Summa theologiae* II–II, q. 49, a. 1, asks whether memory is a part of Prudence. His practical rules are:

> “Primo quidem, ut eorum quae vult memorari quasdam similitudines assumat convenientes, non tamen omnino consuetas; quia ea quae sunt inconsueta magis miramur, et sic in eis animus magis et vehementius detinetur.”

First, one should take suitable likenesses of the things to be remembered, but not wholly customary ones, because the unusual excites wonder and holds the mind more strongly.

> “Secundo, oportet ut homo ea quae vult memoriter tenere sua consideratione ordinate disponat.”

Second, one must arrange in an orderly manner what one wishes to retain.

> “Tertio, oportet ut homo sollicitudinem apponat et affectum adhibeat ad ea quae vult memorari.”

Third, one must apply attention and affect.

> “Quarto, oportet quod ea frequenter meditemur quae volumus memorari.”

Fourth, they must be repeatedly meditated.

Thomas explains corporeal similitudes through Aristotelian psychology: intellectual understanding in embodied human beings ordinarily works through phantasms. He also observes that Scripture teaches spiritual things under corporeal similitudes.

**[D]** This supports the historical legitimacy of vivid moral and theological imagery.

**[Y]** Yates makes it a direct interpretive bridge from scholastic memory doctrine to monumental painting.

**[R/X]** The bridge is plausible as intellectual history but lacks a documentary middle link: no commission contract, painter’s memorandum, or contemporary commentary says that Giotto’s or Lorenzetti’s figures were designed according to *Ad Herennium* or *Summa* II–II, q. 49.

### 4. Boncompagno and the memory of Hell

Boncompagno da Signa’s *Rhetorica novissima*, written at Bologna in 1235, offers an important intermediate witness.

**[D]** Book VIII defines artificial memory:

> “Artificialis memoria est suffragatrix et coadiutrix memorie naturalis, quia sibi tamquam domine famulatur; et dicitur artificialis ab arte, quia per subtilitatem ingenii artificialiter est inventa.”

Artificial memory assists natural memory and serves it as a handmaid.

Boncompagno then gives separate sections *De memoria paradisi* and *De memoria Inferni*. He is cautious about constructing Paradise, whose ineffable glory exceeds sense, and advises adherence to Scripture and Catholic teaching:

> “Non est hominis rerum ineffabilium subsidio alicuius artificii memorari, sed credere firmiter…”

It is not for man to remember ineffable things through an artificial aid but to believe firmly.

In discussing Hell he recalls seeing Etna/Vulcano and uses its physical terror as an image of infernal punishment.

**[D]** Boncompagno associates memory, repeated meditation, Paradise, Hell, and affectively powerful corporeal imagery.

**[X]** He does not set out Dante’s nine circles, a complete reusable route, or a pictorial floor plan. Saying that he created “Dante’s memory palace” would overstate the evidence.

### 5. Giotto’s Scrovegni Chapel

#### Date, place, and patronage

**[D]** Enrico Scrovegni commissioned the family chapel in Padua. Documentary limits place the work between the chapel’s dedication in 1303 and consecration in 1305. The official museum dates the fresco campaign 1303–1305, correcting the older shorthand “c. 1306.”

The lower registers of the nave contain fourteen monochrome personifications:

- Virtues: Prudence, Fortitude, Temperance, Justice, Faith, Charity, Hope.
- Vices: Foolishness, Inconstancy, Wrath, Injustice, Infidelity, Envy, Despair.

They lead toward the west-wall Last Judgment. The upper walls carry the narrative of Joachim and Anna, the Virgin, and Christ.

#### What serves as places?

**[Y]** Each simulated marble niche or panel is a *locus*. The physical chapel and the viewer’s progress through it supply the master order.

**[D]** The paintings really are regularly positioned in a fixed architectural sequence. Each figure occupies a differentiated fictive setting. The system is therefore objectively spatial and repeatable, even without assuming a technical mnemonic intention.

#### What serves as images?

**[Y/R]** The personifications function like *imagines agentes*:

- Charity offers or receives gifts in an open, upward movement.
- Envy is consumed by her own imagery: a serpent issues from her mouth and turns to bite or sting her face; flames rise around her.
- Despair hangs herself while a demon descends.
- Injustice sits amid an environment of violence.
- Hope rises toward an angelic crown.
- Wrath tears her clothing.

Their gestures and bodily consequences make abstract moral categories visually unforgettable.

#### Order and retrieval

**[Y]** A viewer mentally or physically proceeds along the walls, recognizes each moral image, compares opposed categories, and arrives at judgment. The Last Judgment supplies eschatological closure.

**[D]** This is a valid description of the cycle’s visual itinerary.

**[X]** It is not documented that viewers were told to reuse the niches for unrelated material, erase images, count every fifth locus, traverse backward, or perform any other operation characteristic of the classical method. Consequently Giotto’s cycle is a fixed didactic sequence, not demonstrably a general-purpose memory palace.

### 6. Ambrogio Lorenzetti’s Sienese cycle

#### Date and institutional setting

**[D]** The cycle occupies three walls of the Sala dei Nove in Siena’s Palazzo Pubblico, where the rotating government of the Nine met. Archival payment evidence dates Ambrogio’s work from February 1338 to May 1339. The often-repeated “1337–40” is an older approximate date.

The surviving signature reads in part:

> “Ambrosius Laurentii de Senis hic pinxit utrinque…”

Ambrogio Lorenzetti of Siena painted here on both sides.

**[R/X]** “Good and Bad Government” is a later convenient title. Patrick Boucheron and other scholars stress the historically transmitted antithesis “Peace and War,” and the inscriptions’ address to actual rulers.

#### Physical organization

The room supplies three large, fixed fields:

1. **Allegory of just/common government.**
2. **Effects of good government in city and countryside.**
3. **Tyranny/bad government and its effects.**

In the good-government allegory:

- Sapientia/Wisdom appears above.
- Justice is seated beneath Wisdom and operates two scales.
- Concord receives cords from Justice and passes a single rope through the line of citizens.
- A great male civic figure represents the *Comune* or common good of Siena.
- Peace, Fortitude, Prudence, Magnanimity, Temperance, and Justice sit beside him.
- Security appears over the well-governed countryside.

In the bad-government allegory:

- Tyranny is horned and fanged or tusked.
- Cruelty, Treason, Fraud, Fury, Division, and War surround him.
- Avarice, Pride, and Vainglory hover above.
- Justice lies bound below.
- Fear governs the devastated landscape.

#### Places, images, order, and retrieval

**[Y]** The allegorical thrones and differentiated civic and rural settings are loci. The personifications and narrative incidents are images.

**[Y]** Justice is a composite memory image because the large personification includes subordinate figures displaying distributive and commutative acts. Tyranny and his vices condense the opposed argument. The entire political doctrine is “set out in order and clothed in corporeal similitudes.”

**[Y]** Yates describes the vices above Tyranny as hovering “like bats.” The images’ moral ugliness supplies the exceptional quality demanded by *Ad Herennium*.

**[D]** The inscriptions explicitly instruct the governors to look. One reconstructed vernacular address begins:

> “Volgete li occhi a rimirar costei,  
> voi che reggiete…”

“Turn your eyes to look upon her, you who rule.”

The room is therefore indisputably didactic and addressed to magistrates.

**[R]** Its political lesson can be retrieved causally: justice produces concord; concord binds citizens to the common good; the virtues produce security and flourishing. Tyranny reverses the chain and produces fear, violence, and desolation.

**[X]** The interpretation of the central ruler, the precise sources of the program, the relation of Aristotelian, Thomist, Ciceronian, communal, legal, and vernacular traditions, and even the cycle’s conventional title have been disputed. Rubinstein emphasized Aristotelian and Thomistic political ideas; Quentin Skinner emphasized pre-humanist republican rhetoric and the common good; Boucheron and later scholars have emphasized the room’s performative warning to rulers and the danger of anachronistic naming.

None of these interpretations demonstrates a technical mnemonic commission. Yates’s reading remains one illuminating layer among political, legal, institutional, and iconographic accounts.

### 7. Dante’s *Commedia*

#### Date and textual form

**[R]** The poem was composed over many years, generally placed approximately 1307–1321. No autograph survives.

**[D]** It has three *cantiche*, each of thirty-three cantos after the introductory first canto of *Inferno*, for one hundred cantos. The poem uses *terza rima*. Its cosmos is rigidly ordered:

- Hell: vestibule, circles and subcircles descending toward Lucifer.
- Purgatory: ante-Purgatory, seven terraces corresponding to disordered loves or capital vices, Earthly Paradise.
- Paradise: planetary spheres, fixed stars, primum mobile, Empyrean.

The numerical, spatial, ethical, and narrative orders reinforce one another.

#### Places

**[Y/R]** The circles, ditches, rings, terraces, gates, spheres, and celestial rose constitute an exceptionally stable route. Each region differs in terrain, atmosphere, sound, light, guardians, inscriptions, and punishments.

The opening at Hell’s gate exemplifies an inscription attached to a decisive place:

> “Per me si va ne la città dolente,  
> per me si va ne l’etterno dolore,  
> per me si va tra la perduta gente.”  
> —*Inferno* III.1–3

“Through me one goes into the grieving city, through me into eternal pain, through me among the lost people.”

The verse does not call the gate a mnemonic locus, but it makes place, inscription, and doctrine inseparable.

#### Images

The poem’s inhabitants are unforgettable because identity and doctrine become bodily action:

- lustful souls blown ceaselessly by winds;
- diviners with heads twisted backward;
- suicides transformed into bleeding trees;
- sowers of division repeatedly split open;
- hypocrites in gilded lead cloaks;
- Ugolino gnawing Ruggieri’s skull;
- Satan’s three mouths chewing Judas, Brutus, and Cassius;
- the proud bent under stones;
- the envious with eyelids sewn shut;
- the avaricious lying face-down;
- the souls of Paradise appearing within a progressively clarified cosmology of light.

**[D]** These are punishments and revelations governed by theological and poetic justice, commonly called *contrapasso*, although Dante uses the term only once, at *Inferno* XXVIII.142.

**[Y/R]** They also satisfy the mnemonic demand for images that are violent, grotesque, beautiful, active, and emotionally charged.

#### Dante’s explicit language of remembering

Dante repeatedly represents the poem as an attempt to retrieve and reinscribe visionary experience.

At the opening:

> “O mente che scrivesti ciò ch’io vidi,  
> qui si parrà la tua nobilitate.”  
> —*Inferno* II.8–9

“O mind that wrote down what I saw, here your nobility will appear.”

At the threshold of Paradise:

> “O buono Appollo, a l’ultimo lavoro  
> fammi del tuo valor sì fatto vaso…”  
> —*Paradiso* I.13–14

The poet asks to become a vessel adequate to the final labor.

The clearest didactic injunction is:

> “Apri la mente a quel ch’io ti paleso  
> e fermalvi entro; ché non fa scïenza,  
> sanza lo ritenere, avere inteso.”  
> —*Paradiso* V.40–42

“Open your mind to what I reveal and fix it there; understanding without retaining does not make knowledge.”

**[D]** Retention is necessary to knowledge.

**[X]** This is not in itself a command to use architectural loci.

#### “Visible speech” and the terraces of Purgatory

On the terrace of pride, divinely made reliefs show examples of humility:

> “Colui che mai non vide cosa nova  
> produsse esto visibile parlare,  
> novello a noi perché qui non si trova.”  
> —*Purgatorio* X.94–96

“He for whom nothing is new produced this visible speech, new to us because it is not found here.”

The reliefs seem to speak, move, give off incense, and stir banners. The pavement in canto XII displays thirteen punished examples of pride. Dante’s repeated initial letters form an acrostic: the sequences beginning *Vedea*, *O*, and *Mostrava* produce **VOM**, “man.”

**[D]** The text deliberately integrates physical placement, sequential examples, verbal labels, visual illusion, emotional response, and letter-patterning.

**[R]** This is very strong evidence that Dante understood moral learning as ordered, affective visualization.

**[X]** It still does not prove that the whole poem was composed as a reusable *Ad Herennium* palace. The images teach the poem’s own matter; they are not empty pegs for arbitrary content.

#### Retrieval

**[Y]** Yates’s Dantean reader reconstructs the route and recalls the images in their places. Hell’s geography becomes an ordered store of sins and punishments; Purgatory an ascent through purgation; Paradise a cosmic ascent to unity.

**[R]** Bolzoni refines this account. The poem constructs memorability through:

- sharply ordered places;
- passion and bodily reaction;
- punishment inscribed on bodies;
- exempla;
- retrospective narration;
- repeated imperatives to attend, see, and retain;
- rhyme and numerical form;
- temporal interplay between the pilgrim’s experience and the poet’s recollection.

Bolzoni’s “memory of passion” is less like static storage than a process in which bodily emotion makes ethical knowledge retrievable.

### 8. Nardo di Cione’s Hell

#### Identification and dating

**[D]** The Strozzi Chapel in Santa Maria Novella contains *Last Judgment*, *Paradise*, and *Hell* frescoes generally attributed to Nardo di Cione, with related work by members or associates of the Cione workshop. Andrea di Cione, called Orcagna, signed and dated the altarpiece; a 1354 contract concerns that altarpiece.

**[R/X]** The wall paintings are conventionally dated approximately 1354–1357 because of their iconographic unity with the altarpiece, but no surviving contract explicitly commissions Nardo’s frescoes. Proposals that the murals began earlier or involved different hands remain part of the attribution history.

#### The diagram

Hell is organized into visually separated compartments. Individual classes of sinners occupy bounded regions and undergo punishments appropriate to their sins. Labels identify categories.

**[D]** The painted divisions are real, stable, and readable.

**[T/R]** The iconography is deeply Dantesque, although medieval Hell imagery also has non-Dantean scriptural, exegetical, homiletic, and pictorial sources.

**[Y]** Yates’s plate 8a calls attention to the compartments as an image of “the places of Hell.”

**[X]** The fresco does not supply instructions for loading and retrieving memories. It is a represented taxonomy of Hell, not a surviving mnemonic manual.

### 9. Romberch’s places of Hell

Johannes Host von Romberch, a German Dominican, first printed the *Congestorium artificiose memorie* in Venice in 1520.

**[D]** The title-page identifies it as a compilation of the precepts of memory useful to theologians, preachers, confessors, jurists, judges, advocates, notaries, physicians, philosophers, teachers, merchants, messengers, and scribes:

> “Omnium de memoria preceptiones aggregatim complectens…”

It is arranged in four treatises:

1. preliminary matters;
2. the nature and manufacture of places;
3. images and their fabrication;
4. applications to sciences, arguments, lectures, sermons, histories, verse, and secular affairs.

The 1533 table of contents includes:

> “De exemplificatione locorum fictorum”  
> On examples of fictitious places.

Romberch’s imaginary places include a diagrammatic universe and places of Hell containing images of sinners punished according to their sins and identified by inscriptions.

**[D]** This is the first item in the present dossier that clearly treats a represented Hell as material for artificial memory.

**[Y]** Yates calls Romberch’s suggestion “novel”: varied infernal punishments become varied memory loci.

**[X]** The printed pages do not name Nardo di Cione as the source of the scheme. Connecting the woodcut to Nardo is a visual analogy made by Yates, not a documented transmission.

#### Printed diagrams

The 1520 printing contains numerous woodcuts and, at fol. G1v, a surviving movable part or volvelle. Its place diagrams include:

- a city with distinguishable buildings alphabetically or sequentially designated;
- an abbey or monastic complex;
- interiors whose furniture and architectural members are numbered as loci;
- cosmic or zodiacal arrangements;
- an infernal arrangement with differentiated punishments.

The diagrams are not illusionistic artworks. They function as indexed templates: architecture supplies sequence; inscriptions or numbers stabilize identity; figures supply content.

The bibliographic record describes the first edition as [88] leaves, octavo, signatures A–L⁸, Gothic type, with woodcuts. The second edition has 104 leaves and illustrations.

### 10. Dolce’s Dantean gloss

Lodovico Dolce’s Italian *Dialogo*, first published in Venice in 1562, is substantially an adaptation and vernacularization of Romberch.

**[D]** In discussing fictitious places, Dolce explicitly adds Dante to Virgil as an aid:

> “la ingegnosa inventione di Virgilio e di Dante molto ci gioverà”

“The ingenious invention of Virgil and Dante will help us greatly.”

The exact spelling and punctuation differ among printings.

**[D]** This is firm sixteenth-century evidence that Dante’s imagined afterlife could be appropriated as a mnemonic store.

**[Y]** It is the strongest explicit textual support for Yates’s retrospective reading.

**[X]** It demonstrates reception, not Dante’s intention. Dolce wrote more than two centuries after Dante and was adapting a printed technical tradition. The inference “Dolce used Dante’s Hell as loci; therefore Dante constructed it as loci” does not follow without further evidence.

## The sources

### 1. *Rhetorica ad Herennium*

- Original language: Latin.
- Date: approximately 80s BCE.
- Medieval attribution: Cicero.
- Modern attribution: anonymous.
- Primary section: III.16.28–24.40.
- Standard edition/translation: Harry Caplan, *Rhetorica ad Herennium*, Loeb Classical Library 403, Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press, 1954.
- Important transmission fact: the false Ciceronian attribution increased its medieval authority.
- No authorial manuscript survives.

### 2. Aristotle and scholastic psychology

- Aristotle, *De memoria et reminiscentia*, in *Parva naturalia*.
- Greek text: W. D. Ross, *Parva Naturalia*, Oxford Classical Texts.
- Medieval Latin: *De memoria et reminiscentia*, translated and commented on in the scholastic schools.
- Governing idea: memory concerns sensory images or phantasms; recollection searches through an order.
- Albertus Magnus, *De bono*, tract. IV, q. 2, on Prudence and memory.
- Thomas Aquinas, *Summa theologiae* II–II, q. 49, a. 1; also commentary on Aristotle’s *De memoria*.
- Standard critical Latin: *Sancti Thomae Aquinatis Opera omnia*, Leonine edition.

### 3. Boncompagno da Signa

- *Rhetorica novissima*, Bologna, 1235.
- Original language: Latin.
- Relevant division: book VIII, *De memoria*, especially *De memoria paradisi* and *De memoria Inferni*.
- Modern electronic text: ALIM, Archivio della Latinità Italiana del Medioevo.
- The treatise is textual; it does not preserve a Dantean diagram.

### 4. Dante

- Original title: *Comedìa*; “Divina” became conventional through later reception.
- Original language: Florentine/Italian vernacular.
- No autograph survives.
- Earliest securely dated substantially complete witness: Piacenza, Biblioteca Comunale Passerini-Landi, MS Landiano 190, dated 1336.
- Important early witness: Milan, Biblioteca Trivulziana, MS Trivulziano 1080, copied by Francesco di ser Nardo in 1337.
- Total tradition: roughly 600 complete manuscripts and more than 800 when fragments and partial witnesses are counted.
- Standard edition: Giorgio Petrocchi, ed., *La Commedia secondo l’antica vulgata*, 4 vols., Milan: Mondadori, 1966–1967; revised Florence: Le Lettere, 1994.
- Major digital critical resource: Prue Shaw, *Dante’s Commedia: A Digital Edition*, with seven full manuscript transcripts, Petrocchi and Sanguineti texts, and word-by-word collation.
- Standard English translations include Charles S. Singleton, Robert and Jean Hollander, Allen Mandelbaum, and Robin Kirkpatrick.
- Manuscript diagrams and miniatures vary enormously and belong to the poem’s reception; no diagram can be identified as Dante’s own.

### 5. Giotto

- Surviving primary object: Scrovegni Chapel, Padua.
- Patron: Enrico Scrovegni.
- Date: 1303–1305.
- Medium: fresco, with extensive use of fictive architecture and monochrome simulated sculpture.
- No surviving program document calls the sequence an art of memory.
- The earliest literary reference to the frescoes occurs in Francesco da Barberino’s *Documenti d’Amore*, approximately 1308–1313.
- Relevant surviving “diagram” is the architecture itself: two opposed lower-wall sequences terminating in the west-wall Judgment.

### 6. Lorenzetti

- Surviving primary object: three-wall fresco cycle in the Sala dei Nove, Palazzo Pubblico, Siena.
- Date: February 1338–May 1339.
- Original language of inscriptions: Italian vernacular and Latin.
- Primary evidence: paintings, inscriptions, archival payment and governmental records.
- No surviving preparatory diagram or written mnemonic program is known.

### 7. Nardo di Cione

- Surviving primary object: Strozzi Chapel, Santa Maria Novella, Florence.
- Date: probably mid-1350s.
- Relevant paintings: *Hell*, *Paradise*, and *Last Judgment*.
- Related dated document: Andrea Orcagna’s 1354 altarpiece contract.
- No surviving mnemonic instructions accompany the fresco.

### 8. Romberch

**First edition**

Johannes Host von Romberch, *Congestorium artificiose memorie*, Venice: Giorgio Rusconi, 9 July 1520; [88] leaves, A–L⁸, octavo; woodcuts, including a movable diagram at G1v.

A catalogued copy is Milan, Biblioteca Nazionale Braidense, ECO.01.0721. The Wellcome Library also records a copy/digital surrogate.

**Second edition**

Venice: Melchiorre Sessa, July 1533; 104 leaves; illustrated; Library of Congress, Rare Book and Special Collections Division, call number BF383.H7.

**Variants**

- The two printings differ in setting, pagination/foliation, and details of presentation.
- References to “p. 18” in Yates refer to the 1533 printing’s recto and verso, not a stable chapter/page across editions.
- The 1520 edition contains a separately attached movable element; surviving copies may lack or differently preserve it.
- A full census of copy-specific states requires autopsy and comparison beyond available digital descriptions.

### 9. Dolce

## Yates’s account and scholarship since

### Yates’s argument

Yates warns in her Preface that the Dante hypothesis may be the book’s greatest surprise. Chapter IV develops the following sequence:

1. classical artificial memory required orderly places and striking images;
2. Albertus and Thomas moralized memory by making it part of Prudence;
3. corporeal similitudes could lead the mind to spiritual and ethical truths;
4. medieval didactic images therefore should be examined “with the eyes of memory”;
5. Giotto’s virtues and vices resemble active memory images in regularly ordered places;
6. Lorenzetti’s political allegory resembles an argument distributed among composite images;
7. compartmentalized Hell scenes resemble variegated loci;
8. Romberch and Dolce prove that Hell and Dante could later be consciously used in mnemonic practice;
9. Dante’s ordered universe may therefore be read as an immense memory system.

Her key formulations include:

> “the illusion of depth depends on the intense care with which the images have been placed on their backgrounds, or, speaking mnemonically, on their loci.”  
> —p. 93

> “This introduces the novel idea that the places of Hell, varied in accordance with the nature of the sins punished in them, could be regarded as variegated memory loci.”  
> —p. 94

> “That Dante’s Inferno could be regarded as a kind of memory system for memorising Hell and its punishments with striking images on orders of places, will come as a great shock, and I must leave it as a shock.”  
> —p. 95

Yates does not claim to have found a lost treatise by Dante. Her phrasing—“look,” “could be regarded,” “a kind of”—is deliberately conjectural. Later summaries often harden that conjecture into the false assertion that Dante demonstrably used a memory palace.

### What subsequent scholarship has confirmed

#### Carruthers

Mary Carruthers’s *The Book of Memory* (1990; 2nd ed. 2008) confirms the centrality of trained *memoria* to medieval culture but expands and corrects Yates’s framework.

**Confirmed:**

- medieval memory was trained, visual, affective, and locational;
- unusual images were instruments of moral thought;
- order and repeated mental traversal supported recollection;
- literary composition and reading were inseparable from memory.

**Corrected:**

- memory was not principally passive storage or verbatim reproduction;
- it was an art of invention, composition, judgment, and ethical formation;
- the classical architectural scheme was only one among several medieval mnemonic practices;
- “picture,” *imago*, *figura*, *similitudo*, and mental phantasm should not be collapsed into modern representational images.

Carruthers’s *The Craft of Thought* (1998) further argues that medieval rhetorical arts governed buildings, poems, pictures, and meditation. This broadens the case for mnemonic reading of Giotto and Dante while weakening the need to label either a literal *Ad Herennium* system.

#### Bolzoni

Lina Bolzoni’s *La stanza della memoria* and *The Gallery of Memory* trace the traffic among literary images, painted galleries, diagrams, and printed memory manuals. Her “Dante o della memoria appassionata,” *Lettere Italiane* 60.2 (2008), pp. 169–193, directly develops Yates’s conjecture.

Bolzoni distinguishes:

1. fifteenth- and sixteenth-century evidence that readers appropriated Dante mnemonically;
2. internal poetic devices that make the *Commedia* memorable.

Her emphasis on passion, embodied punishment, *contrapasso*, and the narrator’s temporal labor of recollection is a substantial confirmation with a different model: Dante’s memory is dynamic and affective, not merely a filing cabinet.

#### Rossi

Paolo Rossi’s *Clavis universalis*—translated as *Logic and the Art of Memory*—places mnemonic systems within histories of classification, encyclopedism, combinatorics, and universal language. Rossi supports continuity between spatial memory and early modern knowledge organization but is generally more cautious than Yates about treating every diagrammatic or imagistic order as Hermetic or magical.

#### Small

Jocelyn Penny Small’s *Wax Tablets of the Mind* insists on technical distinctions. A true loci system uses a stable setting repeatedly, traversed in the same order, while its deposited images can change. On that strict definition:

- Romberch supplies an artificial-memory system.
- Giotto and Lorenzetti supply fixed meaningful cycles.
- Dante supplies a fixed poetic cosmos.
- Calling all three “memory palaces” risks confusing rhetorical organization with a reusable mnemonic algorithm.

Small’s objection is methodological, not proof that the artworks had no mnemonic force.

#### Hajdu and Sabbatino

Helga Hajdu’s *Das mnemotechnische Schrifttum des Mittelalters* remains fundamental for manuscript treatises and medieval transmission. Her work shows that the technical tradition was real and diverse, but it does not document a Giotto–Dante–Lorenzetti school.

Ignazio Sabbatino’s work on Bruno and Renaissance memory pertains mainly to the later philosophical and magical transformation of memory. It supplies context for Yates’s larger narrative but no direct evidence for Trecento painterly intention.

#### Yates’s later position

Yates continued to defend the importance of images and memory in medieval culture. In her 1974 essay “Broken Images,” she described medieval didactic imagery as resting on faculty psychology and on the rule that beautiful or horrible images are memorable. This reinforces rather than retracts chapter IV.

No later Yates publication identified a lost Dantean memory treatise or upgraded the hypothesis to documentary fact.

### Scholarship not directly probative here

The user’s wider catalogue names Bonner, Fidora, Sturlese, Ciliberto, Clucas, Gatti, Stagno, and Ong. They are indispensable for Lull, Bruno, Camillo, and Ramus, but none supplies primary evidence that Giotto’s, Lorenzetti’s, or Dante’s work was commissioned or composed as a loci system.

Their relevance is comparative:

- Bonner and Fidora distinguish Lull’s demonstrative combinatorial Art from classical mnemonic loci.
- Sturlese, Ciliberto, Clucas, and Gatti have complicated Yates’s Hermetic reading of Bruno by restoring his works’ chronology, logic, psychology, polemics, and varied intellectual sources.
- Bolzoni and Stagno correct romantic reconstructions of Camillo by returning to texts, correspondence, patronage, and theatrical culture.
- Ong shows that Ramist spatial diagrams belong to dialectical partition and pedagogy, not simply to a war between “cold method” and “occult memory.”

The comparative lesson is important: visual order does not by itself prove one genealogically continuous “art of memory.”

## History and context

### Rhetoric and preaching

Artificial memory originated as one of rhetoric’s five parts: invention, arrangement, style, memory, and delivery. Medieval preaching, legal pleading, university disputation, and sermon composition all required organized recall.

Romberch’s title-page list of intended users documents this broad institutional range. His Dominican affiliation is significant: preaching orders had practical reasons to organize scriptural authorities, distinctions, exempla, sins, virtues, and sermon divisions.

### Scholastic psychology

Aristotelian faculty psychology placed sensible phantasms between perception and intellectual understanding. Albertus and Thomas integrated this psychology with Prudence, making cultivated memory a moral capacity.

This context explains why striking corporeal representations could be intellectually respectable. It does not mean every religious picture was a mnemonic cipher.

### Dominican and Franciscan environments

Santa Maria Novella was Dominican. Romberch was a Dominican Observant. Dominican preaching and scholastic education therefore provide genuine institutional continuity between medieval moral imagery and later memory manuals.

Giotto’s Padua patronage and Lorenzetti’s civic commission are different institutional contexts. Lorenzetti’s audience was a communal magistracy, not a convent classroom.

Franciscan devotional culture also cultivated affective visualization, especially meditation on Christ’s life and Passion. Carruthers’s work makes clear that monastic and mendicant “memory craft” extended beyond the architectural loci method.

### Civic government and law

Lorenzetti’s cycle is inseparable from Siena’s Nine, who governed from 1287 to 1355. Its rope of concord, seated magistrates, civic colors, legal forms, inscriptions, and causal contrast between justice and tyranny belong to communal political pedagogy.

Rubinstein connected the cycle to Aristotelian political theory and Thomas. Later scholarship added republican rhetoric, legal imagery, institutional performance, and the immediate danger of faction and signorial tyranny.

### Dante’s encyclopedic cosmos

Dante joined biblical eschatology, Aristotle, scholastic theology, classical epic, courtly poetry, political history, astronomy, ethics, and personal memory. The result resembles a *summa*, but in vernacular narrative verse.

**[R]** The poem’s memorable geography arises from multiple causes:

- the theological need to order moral states;
- the epic journey form;
- scholastic habits of division;
- biblical and visionary traditions;
- pilgrimage and ascent;
- rhetorical exempla;
- mnemonic techniques.

No single cause should be made exclusive.

### Descent and succession

A defensible genealogy is:

> Classical loci and images → medieval rhetorical and scholastic memory doctrine → preaching, meditation, exempla, and moral visualization → Dante’s and Trecento art’s ordered affective imagery → explicit Renaissance reuse of imagined cosmologies as loci in Romberch and Dolce.

A stronger genealogy—*Ad Herennium* directly instructing Giotto, Lorenzetti, and Dante—remains undocumented.

## People

### Practitioners and patrons

- **Dante Alighieri:** poet and narrator; no documentary evidence that he taught a separate artificial-memory course.
- **Giotto:** painter responsible for the Scrovegni program; the extent of his role in devising its theology is unknown.
- **Enrico Scrovegni:** patron; his family wealth and the chapel’s concern with sin, charity, judgment, and salvation are historically relevant.
- **Ambrogio Lorenzetti:** painter named in the Sienese inscription; evidently capable of translating a sophisticated political program into images.
- **The Nine:** institutional patron and primary audience of Lorenzetti’s admonitory cycle.
- **Nardo di Cione:** traditional and broadly accepted author of the Strozzi wall paintings; documentary attribution is less secure than customary captions imply.
- **Andrea di Cione/Orcagna:** maker of the documented Strozzi altarpiece, whose unity with the fresco program helps date them.
- **Johannes Host von Romberch:** Dominican compiler who unmistakably transmitted artificial memory.
- **Lodovico Dolce:** Venetian editor and writer who vernacularized Romberch and expressly named Dante as a mnemonic resource.

### Students, viewers, and users

The likely historical users were:

- clerics preparing sermons;
- students and university masters;
- magistrates and councillors;
- lawyers and notaries;
- readers and reciters of the *Commedia*;
- chapel visitors engaged in penitential examination.

These audiences are documented generally; particular acts of mnemonic traversal inside Giotto’s or Lorenzetti’s rooms are reconstructed rather than recorded.

### Anecdotes and legends

The auctioneer, professor’s party, student who lost his memory, and accused magician belong to other systems in Yates’s book and should not be imported here. No securely sourced anecdote records:

- a Sienese councillor using Lorenzetti’s figures as replaceable mnemonic pegs;
- Giotto demonstrating a memory feat from the chapel;
- Dante reciting the entire *Commedia* from a mental palace;
- Nardo being prosecuted for painting a mnemonic Hell.

Claims of this kind are **[L/M]** unless supported by a specific early witness.

Boccaccio’s portrait of Dante emphasizes extraordinary learning and memory. It is an important early tradition but not proof of technical loci practice.

## The magic question

### Was this system thought magical?

For the Trecento material, the answer is predominantly no.

**[D]** *Ad Herennium* treats artificial memory as rhetoric. Thomas makes memory part of Prudence. Boncompagno discusses Hell and Paradise within orthodox Christian limits. Giotto and Nardo painted chapels; Lorenzetti painted a government chamber; Dante’s poem is explicitly Christian even where its orthodoxy and politics provoked controversy.

### Talismanic operation

There is no evidence that the frescoes were talismans in the technical sense: objects consecrated under astrological conditions to attract spiritual powers. Their images were intended to teach, warn, move, and commemorate.

### Heresy

Dante’s political and theological positions were controversial, and *De monarchia* was condemned and burned in the fourteenth century because of its imperial argument. That does not document prosecution of the *Commedia* as a magical memory system.

No Inquisitorial process against Giotto, Lorenzetti, or Nardo for mnemonic imagery has been identified.

### Ars notoria

The *Ars notoria* promised knowledge or memory through prayers, figures, ritual observances, and angelic mediation. It was suspected or condemned by theologians, including Thomas Aquinas.

**[D]** This constitutes a neighboring but distinct tradition.

**[X]** Yates’s wide history can make technical memory and magical arts appear more continuous than they were. Nothing in the present evidence connects the Trecento cycles directly to *Ars notoria* ritual.

### Why Hell could look “magical” to modern readers

Grotesque demons, cosmic architecture, inscriptions, and intense images resemble later occult diagrams. That resemblance is not evidence of magic. In medieval Christianity, terror and wonder were orthodox tools of penitence and recollection.

## Afterlife

### Later visual culture

Dante rapidly generated manuscript illustrations, maps, diagrams, sermons, commentaries, and monumental images. Nardo’s Strozzi frescoes are among the earliest surviving large-scale Dantesque afterlife cycles.

Later milestones include:

- Botticelli’s approximately ninety-two surviving drawings, including the *Map of Hell*;
- printed diagrams in early Dante editions;
- Domenico di Michelino’s 1465 image of Dante, Florence, Hell, Purgatory, and the celestial spheres;
- illustrated incunabula and Renaissance commentaries;
- emblem books, moral tables, trees of virtues and vices;
- theatrical, cartographic, and diagrammatic renderings of the poem.

These images sometimes clarify structure and aid recollection, but each requires independent study before being called an artificial-memory device.

### Romberch and Dolce

Romberch converts inherited cosmological and infernal imagery into explicit mnemonic technology. Dolce then names Dante. This is the best-documented afterlife of the Trecento material within the technical art of memory.

### Camillo, Bruno, and Fludd

Yates sees “Dantesque” residues in:

- Camillo’s graded Theatre;
- Bruno’s populated celestial wheels and seals;
- Fludd’s theatrical and cosmic memory systems, including a Last Judgment image.

The link is morphological—ordered cosmos plus figures—more securely than genealogical. Camillo’s planetary grades, Bruno’s combinatorial wheels, and Fludd’s theatre introduce structures, philosophies, and sometimes magical ambitions absent from Dante’s documented program.

### Literature

- **Borges’s Funes:** a modern meditation on total recall without abstraction. It illuminates the difference between meaningful ordered memory and indiscriminate retention; it is not a historical descendant documented through Dante.
- **Umberto Eco:** repeatedly explored encyclopedias, labyrinths, lists, semiotic codes, and the dream of a perfect language. His medievalism makes comparison productive, but a direct line from Lorenzetti’s frescoes to Eco’s fiction should not be asserted without textual evidence.
- Dante’s own afterlife in Eliot, Pound, Primo Levi, Borges, Beckett, and many others repeatedly uses infernal places as frameworks for cultural and ethical memory.

### Modern memory palaces and athletes

Modern competitors use stable routes and extravagant person-action-object images. Their practice resembles the *Ad Herennium* far more directly than it resembles Lorenzetti. Some teachers and performers now use Dante’s circles as a ready-made palace.

**[M]** Contemporary shows and memory demonstrations advertised as revealing Dante’s “hidden mnemonic system” are imaginative applications inspired partly by Yates and Bolzoni. They are not new primary evidence about Dante’s intention.

### Computing and combinatorics

There is a legitimate conceptual analogy:

- addressable loci resemble memory addresses;
- images resemble stored data;
- a traversal rule resembles ordered retrieval;
- medieval classifications resemble hierarchical data structures.

But there is no direct technological genealogy from Giotto or Dante to digital memory.

The stronger historical line runs through Lull’s combinatorial Art, Renaissance adaptations, universal-language schemes, Leibniz’s *ars combinatoria*, formal logic, and eventually mechanical computation. Modern historians—especially Rossi and later Lull scholars—warn that Lull’s Art was a demonstrative logical-theological procedure, not simply a memory wheel or primitive computer.

## Controversies and disputes

### 1. Is there really one “system”?

**Verdict:** No, not as a documented historical object.

The catalogue entry is a Yatesian constellation: related works and later receptions that share ordered moral imagery. Treating it as one system devised by Ambrogio Lorenzetti would be false.

### 2. Did Dante consciously use the classical art of memory?

**Evidence for:**

- an exceptionally ordered universe;
- vivid, active images fixed to differentiated places;
- textual commands to see, attend, retain, and recall;
- scholastic and rhetorical culture in which trained memory was common;
- later readers’ explicit mnemonic appropriation.

**Evidence against or limiting:**

- no autograph, letter, commentary, or contemporary biography says he designed the poem as a loci apparatus;
- the sequence follows theological, cosmological, ethical, and narrative necessities;
- its images are semantically permanent, unlike replaceable classical memory images;
- Dolce is a sixteenth-century receiver, not a witness to Dante’s workshop.

**Finding:** **[R/X]** Dante consciously constructed memorability; conscious use of a formal loci system remains possible but unproved.

### 3. Did Trecento painters follow mnemonic rules?

The paintings exhibit properties praised by memory manuals: order, differentiation, corporeal similitude, emotional force, beauty, ugliness, action, inscriptions.

The missing evidence is operational: no rule for mentally loading, clearing, indexing, reversing, or reusing their places survives.

**Finding:** mnemonic function is plausible; technical construction is disputed.

### 4. Is Lorenzetti’s Justice a “composite memory image”?

This is Yates’s interpretive description, not a medieval title. Justice with subordinate operations certainly condenses a complex doctrine into one visual field. “Composite allegory” is documented by the object; “memory image” is an inferred functional category.

### 5. Are Lorenzetti’s frescoes Thomistic?

Rubinstein’s 1958 interpretation made Aristotelian-Thomistic political thought central. Later scholars have proposed:

- Ciceronian and pre-humanist republican rhetoric;
- legal iconography;
- communal ideology;
- vernacular political poetry;
- institutional warning and performance;
- peace/war rather than abstract good/bad government.

These are not mutually exclusive. What is disputed is priority and specificity of source.

### 6. The date “1337–40”

Archival work supports 1338–1339. “1337–40” is an approximate older range and should not be repeated as an exact date.

### 7. Giotto “c. 1306”

The official Scrovegni chronology gives 1303–1305. “c. 1306” is an older art-historical convention.

### 8. Nardo’s authorship and date

The frescoes are traditionally and generally attributed to Nardo. No direct wall-painting contract survives. The securely documented 1354 commission concerns Andrea Orcagna’s altarpiece. Dating and workshop participation are therefore reconstructed.

### 9. Nardo as Romberch’s source

No documentary evidence establishes this. Yates juxtaposes the works because both divide Hell into distinct places. Shared Dantean and broader infernal traditions can explain the resemblance without direct copying.

### 10. Dolce’s dependence

Dolce closely adapts Romberch, but his work is not a neutral translation. His vernacular style, examples, and addition of Dante are evidence of active cultural mediation.

### 11. Yates’s principal overreach

Yates’s actual prose is more cautious than its popular reception. Her limitations are:

- moving too readily from formal resemblance to historical genealogy;
- privileging the architectural loci model over other medieval memory practices;
- treating memory too much as storage rather than invention and composition;
- sometimes presenting “medieval memory” as more unified than manuscript and institutional evidence permits;
- using late Renaissance reception to illuminate—and occasionally to imply—the intentions of much earlier works.

Carruthers and Bolzoni correct these problems without making Yates irrelevant.

### 12. Magic and Hermeticism

No evidence makes this Trecento constellation Hermetic or talismanic. Later Bruno scholarship’s correction of Yates’s sweeping “Hermetic tradition” should not be back-projected into a claim that all imagistic memory was magical.

### 13. Modern “Dante memory palace” claims

Web courses, performances, productivity guides, and mnemonic reconstructions often say that the *Commedia* “hides” a memory palace. That formulation is modern. Bolzoni speaks of how the poem constructs memorability and how later readers used it, not of a proven secret code.

## Open questions

1. Can a complete comparison of surviving copies of Romberch’s 1520 and 1533 editions establish changes in the Hell diagram, captions, movable components, and page order?

2. What exact textual and iconographic sources were supplied to Lorenzetti by the Nine or their advisers?

3. Do archival records identify clerical, legal, or rhetorical advisers involved in the Sienese program?

4. How were the Scrovegni virtues and vices actually used in preaching, confession, or guided viewing?

5. Which early Dante commentaries most clearly describe the poem as a structure for retention rather than only as moral teaching?

6. What is the earliest text before Dolce that explicitly recommends Dante as a store of mnemonic places?

7. How much of Nardo’s Hell depends specifically on Dante, and how much on independent Last Judgment, sermon, and penitential traditions?

8. Do inscriptions now lost or damaged in Siena and the Strozzi Chapel materially alter reconstructed viewing sequences?

9. Can eye-level, doorway, seating, lighting, and traffic patterns in the Sala dei Nove reconstruct how successive magistrates encountered the frescoes?

10. Should “memory system” be reserved for reusable procedures, or can a fixed work designed to organize recollection qualify? Much of the disagreement is terminological.
