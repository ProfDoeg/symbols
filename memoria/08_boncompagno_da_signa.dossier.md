# Boncompagno da Signa's memory of Paradise and Hell: Research Dossier

## Basic identification

**Name:** Boncompagno da Signa’s memory of Paradise and Hell  
**Exponent:** Boncompagno da Signa—Latin *Boncompagnus de Signa*—a Tuscan master of grammar, rhetoric, and *ars dictaminis*, active chiefly at Bologna and for a time at Padua  
**Date:** substantially composed over a long period after being announced before 1215; publicly issued or “published” at Bologna in **1235**  
**Source text:** *Rhetorica novissima*, Book VIII, *De memoria*, especially VIII.1.1–26; Augusto Gaudenzi, ed., *Scripta anecdota glossatorum*, *Bibliotheca Iuridica Medii Aevi* II (Bologna, 1892), pp. 249–297, memory section pp. 275–280  
**Principle in one sentence:** Artificial memory strengthens natural memory through environmental discipline, ordered classification, stable spatial arrangements, imaginative signs, conspicuous personal features, and repeated mental exercise; remembrance of Paradise and Hell supplies a Christian moral imperative, but—contrary to the usual shorthand—Boncompagno expressly places the ineffable reality of Paradise beyond mnemonic artifice.  
**Yates:** Frances A. Yates, *The Art of Memory* (1966), Chapter III, pp. 57–60; recalled on pp. 76–77, 93–96; later Paradise-and-Hell diagrams discussed in Chapter V and reproduced as Plate 7 are those of **Cosmas Rossellius (1579), not Boncompagno**. [Warburg Institute scan of Yates](https://resources.warburg.sas.ac.uk/mnemosyne/fq/yates_memory.pdf)

### Evidentiary verdict at a glance

| Proposition | Status |
|---|---|
| Boncompagno discussed natural and artificial memory in Book VIII | **Documented text** |
| He gave no classical rules for architectural loci and *imagines agentes* | **Substantially documented**, but misleading if expanded into “he gave no memory technique” |
| Paradise and Hell themselves form Boncompagno’s mnemonic places | **Yates’s interpretation; not stated by Boncompagno and contradicted in the case of Paradise** |
| Virtues and vices are images arranged in Paradise/Hell | **Yatesian reconstruction**, not the wording or sequence of the primary text |
| The *signacula* are mainly virtues and vices | **Incorrectly selective**: they include names, bodies, dress, rank, behavior, fortunes, virtues, vices, crimes, and other distinguishing marks |
| Boncompagno possessed an imaginary alphabet | **Documented claim by Boncompagno**, but its form is undisclosed and its efficacy unverified |
| He memorized 500 students’ full identities in thirty days | **Documented autobiographical boast**, not independently corroborated |
| He designed a mnemonic classroom with fixed seats | **Documented prescription**, explicitly admitted never to have been built |
| His Paradise/Hell chapters directly influenced Albertus, Aquinas, Dante, Giotto, Romberch, Rossellius, or Fludd | **Possible genealogy, but no direct transmission evidence located** |
| The system was magical or talismanic | **No evidence**; astrology is acknowledged but subordinated to divine sovereignty |
| Boncompagno was prosecuted as a magician or by the Inquisition | **No evidence located** |
| His flying trick or mock magical announcement made him an accused magician | **Conflation**: the anecdotes portray deliberate satire of credulity, not prosecution |
| The surviving manuscripts contain a diagram of Boncompagno’s device | **No diagram identified** |
| Plate 7 in Yates reproduces Boncompagno’s Paradise and Hell | **False**: it reproduces Rossellius’s printed diagrams of 1579 |

## The system in detail

### 1. The scope of memory

Book VIII begins with definitions:

> *Memoria est gloriosum et admirabile nature donum, qua preterita recolimus, presentia complectimur, et futura per preterita similitudinarie contemplamur.*

“Memory is a glorious and wonderful gift of nature, by which we recall past things, embrace present things, and contemplate future things through their likeness to past things.”  
—*Rhetorica novissima* VIII.1.1; Gaudenzi, p. 275. [Latin text](https://www.scrineum.it/scrineum/wight/rn8.htm)

This temporal triad resembles the traditional components of prudence—*memoria*, *intelligentia*, and *providentia*—but Boncompagno does not explicitly identify it with that scheme here. Yates therefore correctly says that the definition “may suggest” prudence, not that dependence is demonstrable.

He continues:

> *Memoria naturalis est que a solo beneficio nature procedit, nullo artificio preeunte.*

“Natural memory is that which proceeds from nature’s gift alone, without any preceding artifice.”

> *Artificialis memoria est suffragatrix et coadiutrix memorie naturalis, quia sibi tamquam domine famulatur; et dicitur artificialis ab arte, quia per subtilitatem ingenii artificialiter est inventa.*

“Artificial memory is the supporter and helper of natural memory, because it serves it as a mistress; and it is called artificial from *ars*, because it has been devised artificially through subtlety of intelligence.”  
—VIII.1.2–3, Gaudenzi, p. 275.

**Documented text:** Boncompagno shares the foundational ancient distinction of *Rhetorica ad Herennium* III.16.28 between natural and artificial memory.

**Scholarly qualification:** The wording is Boncompagno’s and is much shorter than the contemporary academic commentaries on the *Ad Herennium*. Mary Carruthers and Sean Gallagher characterize Book VIII as a practical replacement for ancient precept, directed toward contemporary law students rather than toward Roman forensic declamation. [Carruthers and Ziolkowski, *The Medieval Craft of Memory*, pp. 103–17](https://dokumen.pub/the-medieval-craft-of-memory-an-anthology-of-texts-and-pictures-0812293428-9780812293425.html)

### 2. Memory belongs to more than rhetoric

At the beginning of Book VIII Boncompagno treats memory as necessary to all arts and professions. This accords with the structure of the whole *Rhetorica novissima*: a new, ambitious rhetoric for advocates, civic speakers, administrators, and students of law, not merely a manual of letter composition. Terence Tunberg calls it the most ambitious early-thirteenth-century Italian attempt to provide a theoretical foundation for public speech. [Tunberg, “What Is Boncompagno’s ‘Newest Rhetoric’?”](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/traditio/article/abs/what-is-boncompagnos-newest-rhetoric/136422C05EABDE2D980CC65A47D4C047)

**Documented context:** Book VIII stands between argumentation and rhetorical ornament in a thirteen-book work intended particularly for legal and civic practice.

### 3. The impaired human faculty

Boncompagno embeds memory in a Christian anthropology of the Fall:

> *Quare omnes amisimus incorruptibilitatis privilegium et gratiam memorandi.*

“Therefore we all lost the privilege of incorruptibility and the grace of remembering.”

He reports the philosophical doctrine that souls knew and remembered everything before embodiment, but immediately rejects it:

> *Sed opinio illa est penitus respuenda, quoniam est contra theologicam disciplinam.*

“But that opinion must be rejected completely, since it is contrary to theological teaching.”  
—VIII.1.5, pp. 275–76.

**Documented text:** The Fall explains the weakness of present human memory.

**Source reconstruction:** Carruthers and Gallagher relate the rejected doctrine to Platonic recollection, possibly transmitted through Calcidius or William of Conches. No precise immediate source has been demonstrated.

**Magic/heresy relevance:** Boncompagno is reporting an objectionable position, not endorsing the pre-existence of souls.

### 4. Faculty psychology: the posterior memory chamber

Human beings are *microcosmus* and *arbor inversa*, a “little world” and “inverted tree,” with roots above. The brain contains three cells or chambers; the principal seat of the soul may be in the brain while its natural operation is in the heart. Memory resides in the posterior chamber:

> *firmiter creditur, quod in posteriori cellula sit anime sedes et virtus in ea memorialis consistat.*

“It is firmly believed that the soul’s seat is in the posterior chamber and that the memorial power resides in it.”  
—VIII.1.6, p. 276.

This is medieval ventricular psychology, not a diagrammatic locus system. The phrase *cellula memorialis* becomes Boncompagno’s usual designation for the internal workspace or repository of memory.

### 5. Humoral memory

Boncompagno assigns mnemonic tendencies to the four complexions:

- **Sanguine:** remembers well because blood is temperate and friendly to nature.
- **Phlegmatic:** remembers poorly because phlegm is dense and viscous.
- **Choleric:** learns quickly but does not retain; what arrives suddenly departs suddenly.
- **Melancholic:** learns with difficulty but retains firmly because impressions placed in something hard and earthy are not easily moved.

In pathological melancholy, rising vapors can produce:

> *nigre imagines defunctorum, sepulcra et monstra infernalia*

“black images of the dead, tombs, and infernal monsters.”  
—VIII.1.7, p. 276.

**Documented text:** These are physiological/dream images, not recommended mnemonic images.

**Correction:** They should not be used as evidence that Boncompagno’s artificial memory was demonic, necromantic, or based upon infernal visualization.

### 6. Pathological forgetfulness

Boncompagno supplies comic or cautionary examples:

- a man who sometimes could not remember his own name;
- another who asked neighbors the names of his sons and looked for the animal on which he was riding;
- a man who put on a new leather garment but accused the cobblers of losing it;
- a priest who carried his servant’s name in his purse and asked his opponent to tell him the spots on the dice;
- people who asked others for objects already in their hands.

He concludes:

> *Huiusmodi autem oblivionis non prosunt artificia, quia semper sunt extraordinariis cogitationibus occupati.*

“Artificial devices do not help forgetfulness of this kind, because such people are continually occupied by disordered thoughts.”  
—VIII.1.8, p. 276.

**Documented anecdote:** The unnamed “student who lost his memory” requested in the general brief does not appear. These are Boncompagno’s own unnamed cases; their historicity cannot be independently checked.

### 7. Celestial influence, but no astral manipulation

Boncompagno believes heavenly signs affect the preservation and loss of memory because lower bodies are disposed according to higher ones. Yet:

> *Nos autem operationem superiorum corporum plenarie scire non possumus, nec aliquo valemus artificio permutare, quia solus Deus habet sub imperio suo naturam.*

“We cannot know fully the operations of the higher bodies, nor can we alter them by any artifice, because God alone has nature under his command.”  
—VIII.1.9, pp. 276–77.

**Documented:** belief in celestial causation.

**Not documented:** electional astrology for memory, talismans, astral images, or magical manipulation.

### 8. Impediments and physical supports

Memory is impeded by anxiety, ambition, concern over relatives, sexual entanglements, excessive food and wine, anger, pride, fear, noise, avarice, grief, overwork, excessive sex, viscous foods, spoiled wine, bad air, stench, too much sleep or wakefulness, excessive fasting, chronic disease, constraint, and old age.

Boncompagno then supplies *artificialia suffragia*:

- cheerful disposition and personal liberty;
- pure air;
- moderate diet suited to complexion;
- purging phlegmatic and viscous humors;
- *diamargariton* and *pliris*, medicinal compounds thought to strengthen the memory chamber;
- rosewater or *napium* on the face;
- spices and herbs smelled intermittently;
- clothing neither luxurious nor rough and shabby;
- pleasant places, nightingales, flowing brooks;
- agreeable friends;
- little conversation with women, preferably wise rather than beautiful.

The twelve principal supports are:

> *Meditatio, studium, disputatio, collatio, fabulatio, novitas, mutatio, consuetudo, emulatio, timor improperii, appetitus laudis et ambitio excellentie.*

“Meditation, study, disputation, conference, conversation, novelty, change, habit, rivalry, fear of reproach, desire for praise, and ambition for excellence.”  
—VIII.1.12, p. 277.

This is a regimen of physiology, attention, affect, social interaction, repetition, novelty, and motivation—not simply a memory palace.

### 9. Scripture and the world as memorial signs

Boncompagno argues against those who deny that natural memory can receive artificial aid. His examples include:

- the cockcrow recalling Christ’s words to Peter;
- the man carrying a pitcher as a sign to the disciples;
- the alabaster vessel and the woman remembered for anointing Christ;
- the Eucharistic command, “Do this in remembrance of me”;
- Judas’s thirty pieces and the naming of Aceldama;
- Abraham’s naming of the sacrificial place;
- Jacob’s stone and the naming of Bethel;
- Jacob and Laban’s memorial heap.

The cockcrow passage reads:

> *Unde tertius galli cantus fuit memoriale signum, quo recordatus est Petrus verbi Iesu.*

“Thus the third crow of the cock was a memorial sign through which Peter recalled Jesus’s word.”  
—VIII.1.13, p. 277.

Carruthers and Gallagher note that three cock-crowings occur in no individual Gospel; Boncompagno may reflect a conflated tradition rather than an error original to him.

The ensuing catalogue is exceptionally broad. It includes books, legal documents, wax-tablet inscriptions, epitaphs, paintings, images, sculpture, roadside crosses, churches, hospitals, bell towers, pillories, gibbets, chains, swords of justice, mutilations of criminals, boundary markers, bells, Greek wooden clappers, Islamic calls to prayer, horns, seals, religious clothing, alphabets, signs of ports and inns, military standards, heraldry, Hindu-Arabic numerals, astrolabes, clocks, papal seals, dice markings, colors, memorial knots, tally-stick notches, episcopal slaps at confirmation, blows administered to boys so events would remain memorable, lovers’ gestures, thieves’ signals, and gifts.

**Documented principle:** A “memorial sign” need not be a classical *imago agens*. It may be an object, noise, gesture, scar, penalty, inscription, ritual, social convention, token, or landmark that prompts recollection.

**Carruthers’s interpretation:** The list shows memory as a practical craft of composing and using signs. It resembles Augustine’s expansive semiotics in *De doctrina christiana* II more than a narrow architectural mnemonic.

### 10. Paradise

Boncompagno writes:

> *Viri sancti per itinera fidei gradientes firmiter asseverant, quod divina maiestas residet in excelso throno, cui assistunt Cherubini et Seraphini omnesque ordines angelorum.*

“Holy men, walking by the paths of faith, firmly assert that the divine majesty resides on a high throne, attended by Cherubim and Seraphim and all the orders of angels.”

But the decisive sentence is:

> *Non est hominis rerum ineffabilium subsidio alicuius artificii memorari, sed credere firmiter de gloria Dei et de resurrectione corporum secundum fidei catholice doctrinam.*

“It does not belong to man to remember ineffable things with the assistance of any artifice, but firmly to believe in the glory of God and the resurrection of bodies according to Catholic doctrine.”  
—VIII.1.14, p. 278; fuller reading preserved in the ALIM text. [ALIM edition](https://alim.unisi.it/dl/resource/18829)

He concludes with Romans 11:20, *Noli altum sapere, sed time*—“Do not aspire to know high things, but fear”—and leaves the subject to the Apocalypse and the Fathers.

**Primary-text conclusion:** The throne and angelic orders are matters of revealed belief. Boncompagno does **not** tell the reader to construct them as mnemonic loci.

**Major correction to Yates:** Paradise is expressly beyond the assistance of artificial memory. Tunberg made this correction forcefully in 1986, calling Yates’s reading selective.

### 11. Hell and Etna

> *Memini me vidisse montem qui litteraliter Ethna et vulgo Vulcanum vocatur, de quo cum iuxta ipsum navigaremus vidi proici sulphureos globos ignitos plurimum et candentes.*

“I remember seeing the mountain called Etna in learned usage and Vulcan among the common people; when we sailed near it, I saw fiery and intensely glowing sulphurous balls cast out.”

> *Unde multi putant, quod ibi sit os Inferni. Verumtamen ubicumque sit Infernus, firmissime credo, quod Sathan demoniorum princeps cum suis agminibus in ipsius voragine crucietur.*

“Thus many think that the mouth of Hell is there. Nevertheless, wherever Hell may be, I most firmly believe that Satan, prince of demons, is tormented in its abyss with his hosts.”  
—VIII.1.15, p. 278.

**Documented:** Etna supplies an autobiographical sensory analogue for infernal fire.

**Qualification:** Boncompagno reports that “many think” Etna is Hell’s mouth; he does not assert its geography as certain.

### 12. Heretics and the injunction to remember

Boncompagno tells a story about philosophers at Athens who denied bodily resurrection. They let corpses decay into soil, planted seed and vines in it, and treated the resulting growth as proof that bodies return to the elements. He claims contemporary imitators maintain that everything proceeds from and returns to the elements.

He replies:

> *Nos autem, qui fidem catholicam indubitanter credimus, invisibilium gaudiorum Paradisi et eternarum penarum Inferni debemus assidue memorari.*

“But we, who believe the Catholic faith without doubting, ought continually to remember the invisible joys of Paradise and the eternal punishments of Hell.”  
—VIII.1.16, p. 278.

This is the source of Yates’s capitalized translation:

> “WE MUST ASSIDUOUSLY REMEMBER THE INVISIBLE JOYS OF PARADISE AND THE ETERNAL TORMENTS OF HELL.”

**Documented:** continual moral recollection is enjoined.

**Not documented:** a technical itinerary through constructed places of Paradise and Hell.

**Tradition or anecdote:** No independent source for Boncompagno’s Athenian corpse-and-vine experiment has been identified. It should be treated as polemical exemplum or inherited story, not laboratory history.

### 13. The cryptic quadrangle and revolving genera

Immediately after the Paradise/Hell chapters, Boncompagno returns to artificial memory:

> *Revera potest aliqua imago in cellula memoriali formari, per quam potest notitia particularis taliter comprehendi secundum genera singulorum…*

“An image can indeed be formed in the memory chamber through which particular knowledge may be grasped according to the genera of individual things.”

The directions are deliberately obscure: imagine a quadrangle in the middle of the solar sphere, project a proportional line to its extremities, reflect the line through varied directions at a two-faced junction, and cause the members of genera to revolve by an orbicular art.

**Documented text:** quadrangle, solar sphere, lines, reflection, bifront junction, and orbital revolution.

**Open reconstruction:** No securely surviving diagram explains it. It could describe a mental geometrical classifier, a rotating conceptual figure, or a deliberately inflated “secret.” Treating it as a Lullian wheel would be anachronistic: Llull’s mature combinatorial Art is later, and no textual dependence is demonstrated.

Boncompagno acknowledges obscurity and says not all natural secrets can or should be published lest the envious gain the same prerogative. His carpenter analogy follows: trees in a forest are like artificial imaginations in memory cells, and the unlearned cannot comprehend them through words alone.

Carruthers interprets this as a craft analogy: memory, like carpentry, depends upon trained judgment and individual imaginative work, not rules alone.

### 14. Maps, hierarchy, and categorical descent

To remember provinces, cities, rivers, islands, deserts, and seas, inspect a labeled *mappamundi*, read Solinus, philosophers, poets, the Old Testament, and Roman histories.

Then imitate the ordering of the Fourth Lateran Council under Innocent III:

> *incipiens a se ipso tamquam a genere generalissimo et faciens descensum, conservatis gradatim quorumlibet dignitatibus et officiis, ad singulos tamquam ad species subalternas.*

“Beginning with himself as the most general genus and making a descent, preserving step by step the ranks and offices of each, down to individuals as subordinate species.”  
—VIII.1.18, p. 279.

The order extends from pope, emperors, kings, and prelates down to magistrates, householders, tavern keepers, and barbers.

**Order:** logical and social hierarchy supplies the retrieval tree.

**Retrieval:** begin at the superordinate class, descend through ranks and offices, and arrive at the individual.

**Historical anchor:** the council is securely identified as the Fourth Lateran Council of 1215—the only recent general council presided over by an Innocent within the relevant period.

### 15. The imaginary alphabet and the 500 students

> *Ad habendam memoriam plurium et diversorum nominum quoddam in memoriali cellula imaginarium constitui alphabetum, quod vix posset perituris chartulis annotari.*

“To remember many different names I established in the memory chamber a certain imaginary alphabet, which could scarcely be written upon perishable pages.”

> *Per illam siquidem imaginationem alphabeti … in XXX diebus quingentorum scholarium nomina memorie commendavi.*

“Through that imagining of an alphabet … in thirty days I committed the names of five hundred students to memory.”

He says he recalled each student’s first name, surname or cognomen, nickname, and place of origin before an astonished audience.

**Documented autobiographical claim:** the boast occurs in the text.

**Missing evidence:** the alphabet is not disclosed, diagrammed, independently witnessed, or experimentally recoverable.

**Likely integration:** personal features listed in VIII.1.23, regional grouping, fixed seats, names and nicknames probably worked together. That integration is a scholarly reconstruction, not Boncompagno’s explicit worked example.

### 16. The ideal classroom as external mnemonic architecture

The school should be:

- in open, pure air;
- away from women, markets, horses, ships, dogs, carts, noise, and stench;
- equal in length and width;
- neither too bright nor too dark;
- high-ceilinged;
- clean;
- painted green;
- entered through a single doorway;
- provided with easily climbed stairs;
- furnished with a raised master’s chair;
- supplied with two or three windows looking onto trees, gardens, and orchards.

There should be no paintings unless their notable forms recall the sciences being studied.

Students should sit where all are visible. Famous or senior students occupy the more honorable positions; students from the same or neighboring provinces sit together. Seats must remain fixed:

> *Sedendi autem ordo nullo modo varietur … sed unusquisque locum semper obtineat qui sibi fuerit assignatus.*

“The seating order must not be changed in any way … each person must always retain the place assigned to him.”  
—VIII.1.21, p. 279.

This provides real places, bodies, grouping, and stable order. The master retrieves a name from the student’s habitual spatial position plus province, status, appearance, and behavior.

Yet Boncompagno admits:

> *Ego vero numquam habui domum ita constructam, nec credo quod alicubi umquam fuerit taliter ordinata.*

“I myself never had a house so constructed, nor do I believe one was ever arranged this way anywhere.”

**Status:** documented design; explicitly unrealized thought experiment, not a historical Bologna lecture hall.

### 17. Negative salience

Offenses and accidents are remembered more strongly than favors. Boncompagno’s examples include the single student withdrawn after forty were provided, one failure after a hundred benefits, one insult after a thousand praises, one patient harmed after many cured, and the place where someone fell.

This is the closest analogue to the classical rule that unusual, violent, disgraceful, comic, or emotionally powerful images adhere to memory. Boncompagno generalizes it psychologically rather than instructing the reader to fabricate a grotesque courtroom tableau.

### 18. The *signacula*: what they really are

VIII.1.23 lists:

- first names, surnames, nicknames, and places of origin;
- ugliness, height, shortness, fatness, thinness, baldness, age, complexion;
- voice, accent, speech, speed, slowness;
- crooked nose, deformity, mutilation, moles, scars, limp, blindness, crossed eyes, hump, diseased eyes, leprosy;
- distinctive clothing and ridiculous names;
- occupation, order, rank, bodily strength, learning;
- wisdom, ignorance, sagacity, imprudence;
- holiness and perversity;
- kindness, credulity, gentleness, rage;
- cunning, simplicity, pride, humility;
- boldness, fear, magnanimity, pusillanimity;
- religion, lasciviousness, modesty;
- play, laughter, lament, joke, sorrow;
- wealth, poverty, victory, defeat, gain, loss;
- generosity, avarice, continence, luxury;
- “crime against nature,” heresy, theft, rapine, and falsity.

Then:

> *sunt memoriales note, quas directiones et signacula possumus appellare.*

“They are memorial marks, which we may call pointers and little signs.”

> *quasi per quosdam imaginarios et materiales funiculos specialia nomina eorumdem trahimus ad portum nostre memorie.*

“As if by imaginary and material cords, we draw their particular names into the harbor of our memory.”  
—VIII.1.23, p. 280.

**Correction to Yates:** The virtues and vices are elements within a much larger face-and-name retrieval inventory. The grammatical subject is the characteristics of identifiable persons. Boncompagno does not say that these abstract virtues and vices are moral images stationed along routes to Heaven and Hell.

### 19. Genera, species, and privileged exemplars

For animals:

- lion heads beasts;
- eagle heads birds;
- whale heads fish;
- basilisk heads reptiles and serpents.

The reader makes a *regularis descensus*, a regular descent, within the memory chamber, placing the more dignified first. Individual animals are differentiated by natural and accidental signs. The same order applies successively to crafts, tools, minerals, metals, stones, trees, herbs, fruits, liquids, and every genus of things.

This is an open-ended taxonomic memory, related to dialectical division and scholastic ordering. Its logic anticipates—but does not establish direct descent to—later Ramist dichotomies or encyclopedic classification.

### 20. The three keys

> *Prima clavis est in imaginatione nature. Secunda est in exercitatione ingenii. Tertia in operatione anime.*

“The first key is in nature’s imagination; the second in exercise of intelligence; the third in the operation of the soul.”

The soul joins middles to extremes, parts to wholes, and artificial memory to natural memory through laborious exercise.

The work ends by promising generosity while withholding secrets:

> *Verumtamen aliqua in armario dogmata remanserunt, que perituris non volui chartulis annotare.*

“Nevertheless certain teachings remained in my storehouse, which I did not wish to write on perishable pages.”  
—VIII.2.2, p. 280.

**Documented rhetoric of secrecy:** some teaching is withheld.

**Not proof of magic:** secrecy was also a professional strategy, an assertion of mastery, and an invitation to oral instruction.

## Places, images, order, and retrieval: reconstructed synopsis

| Element | Boncompagno’s actual provision |
|---|---|
| **Places** | the internal *cellula memorialis*; real-world places and monuments acting as signs; a labeled world map; the categories and ranks of a hierarchy; fixed student seats; pleasant physical surroundings |
| **Images/signs** | scriptural tokens, objects, pictures, sculpture, inscriptions, noises, gestures, clothing, scars, deformities, names, occupations, virtues, vices, crimes, personal accidents; an undisclosed imaginary alphabet; a cryptic quadrangular/orbicular image |
| **Order** | genus to species; superior to inferior; dignified exemplars first; fixed seating; geographical and national clusters; successive categories |
| **Encoding** | associate the target with name, origin, rank, appearance, voice, clothing, behavior, virtue, vice, or striking accident |
| **Retrieval** | enter at the general class, descend through subordinate groups, find the stable place or conspicuous characteristic, and “pull” the proper name into memory by an imaginary cord |
| **Maintenance** | repetition, study, debate, novelty, habit, rivalry, emotional arousal, good health, moderate diet, clean air, quiet, and pleasant visual surroundings |
| **Limit** | art cannot cure severe disordered forgetfulness; divine and ineffable reality must be believed rather than captured by an artificial system |

## The sources

### Primary text and edition

The standard printed text remains:

Boncompagnus de Signa, *Rhetorica novissima*, ed. Augusto Gaudenzi, in *Scripta anecdota glossatorum*, *Bibliotheca Iuridica Medii Aevi* II (Bologna: in aedibus Petri Virano, formerly Fratelli Treves, 1892), pp. 249–297; Book VIII, pp. 275–280.

The user’s “ed. Gaudenzi, pp. 255, 275–79” refers to:

- p. 255 for the larger work’s organization and scope;
- pp. 275–280 for memory—the complete section continues onto p. 280.

The edition is available through:

### Translation

The standard substantial English translation is Sean Gallagher’s “On Memory,” in Mary Carruthers and Jan M. Ziolkowski, eds., *The Medieval Craft of Memory: An Anthology of Texts and Pictures* (Philadelphia: University of Pennsylvania Press, 2002), pp. 103–117. It translates all of Book VIII’s memory section and supplies explanatory notes. [Accessible text](https://dokumen.pub/the-medieval-craft-of-memory-an-anthology-of-texts-and-pictures-0812293428-9780812293425.html)

Yates translates selected passages only.

### Manuscripts and textual tradition

Gaudenzi’s edition relied principally on:

- **V:** Venezia, Biblioteca Nazionale Marciana, **Lat. Z. 4185**, also cited as **Marc. lat., class XI, no. 8**.
- **M:** München, Bayerische Staatsbibliothek, **Clm 23499**, thirteenth century; *Rhetorica novissima* at fols. **81r–98r**.
- Gaudenzi is reported in newer bibliographic records as having used three manuscripts, although Tunberg’s detailed critique repeatedly describes the edition as based on V and M. This discrepancy should not be silently harmonized.

Franco-Lucio Schiavetto announced a newly identified witness in “Un nuovo manoscritto della *Rhetorica novissima* di Boncompagno,” *Studi medievali*, 3rd series, 33 (1992), pp. 825–828, and criticized Gaudenzi’s edition.

Recent work under Paolo Garbini’s direction has expanded attention to complete witnesses and excerpts. A new critical edition with translation and commentary is reported as in preparation for the *Edizione Nazionale dei Testi Mediolatini d’Italia*. Garbini’s 2021 project stresses previously unrecognized excerpt transmission. [Project description](https://iris.uniroma1.it/handle/11573/1617799)

The FAMA notice reports “80 manuscripts according to bibliography” but displays only Bern, Burgerbibliothek, Cod. 322 in its online manuscript field. That large number should be treated cautiously: it may aggregate bibliographical reports or confuse complete witnesses with excerpt transmission. It cannot be substituted for a checked handlist. [FAMA notice](https://fama.irht.cnrs.fr/en/oeuvre/254351)

Other witnesses or sigla used in recent editorial work include **P, Va, P², and Tr**, but a definitive public census and stemma for Book VIII were not located in the accessible sources consulted.

### Variants

The best-documented textual problems concern the work generally rather than Book VIII. Tunberg shows that Gaudenzi sometimes expanded abbreviations incorrectly and that M’s Book V is copied out of order. In Book VIII, the Scrineum transcription itself contains apparent slips—for example *dicitur naturalis ab arte* where sense and Gaudenzi require *dicitur artificialis ab arte*. ALIM reads the correct form.

**Finding:** No substantive manuscript variant has yet been published demonstrating an alternative Paradise/Hell memory system. Assertions that distinct manuscripts contain different diagrams or wheels remain unsupported.

### Early printings

No incunable or sixteenth-century edition of the *Rhetorica novissima* was identified. The work circulated in manuscript and first received its complete modern printed edition in Gaudenzi’s nineteenth-century collection. References online to a “Strassburg incunabulum” concern Boncompagno’s *Rota Veneris*, not the *Rhetorica novissima*.

### Surviving diagrams

No authorial or manuscript diagram of Book VIII’s quadrangle, solar sphere, imaginary alphabet, classroom, Paradise, or Hell was identified.

Yates’s Plate 7 depicts:

- **Hell as artificial memory**
- **Paradise as artificial memory**

but both are reproduced from Cosmas Rossellius, *Thesaurus artificiosae memoriae* (Venice, 1579), not from Boncompagno. Rossellius’s Hell contains a central well and eleven ordered regions; Paradise has a jeweled wall, Christ’s throne, angelic hierarchies, apostles, patriarchs, prophets, martyrs, confessors, virgins, holy Hebrews, and saints. [Yates’s description, pp. 121–22 and Plate 7](https://resources.warburg.sas.ac.uk/mnemosyne/fq/yates_memory.pdf)

## Yates’s account and the scholarship since

### Yates, 1966

Yates’s achievement was to notice a text previously marginal to histories of mnemonics and position it between the classical *Ad Herennium* and the ethical memory doctrines of Albertus Magnus and Thomas Aquinas.

She correctly observes:

- Boncompagno repeats the natural/artificial distinction.
- His discussion departs strikingly from the usual *Ad Herennium* rules.
- He connects memory with Christian anthropology, Scripture, Paradise, Hell, virtues, and vices.
- He belongs to the Bolognese *dictamen* milieu.
- His text may illuminate how artificial memory came to be read within ethics and prudence.

Her central formulation, however, goes farther:

> “No doubt connected with the primary necessity of remembering Paradise and Hell, as the chief exercise of memory, is the list of virtues and vices…”

The phrases “no doubt,” “chief exercise,” and the connection of the list to Paradise/Hell are Yates’s interpretation. Boncompagno does not make that connection.

At pp. 76–77 Yates hypothesizes that Boncompagno’s *signacula* could answer what became of the *Ad Herennium’s imagines agentes*: they were moralized into beautiful and hideous personifications ranged in a solemn building. This is explicitly speculative—“suggests an answer”—not textual documentation.

At pp. 93–96 she extends the hypothesis to Giotto, Dominican preaching, fresco cycles, Romberch, Rossellius, and Dante. Her language repeatedly signals conjecture: “I would suggest,” “could have been,” “it is indicated,” “if we were to reflect,” and “I believe so.”

### Tunberg’s correction, 1986

Tunberg’s decisive objection is that Yates read selectively. Paradise receives little emphasis and is explicitly declared inaccessible to mnemonic art. Artificial memory is Boncompagno’s chief concern, but it appears chiefly in the practical chapters after Paradise and Hell:

- the cryptic mental image;
- the *mappamundi*;
- hierarchical descent;
- the imaginary alphabet;
- fixed classroom seating;
- salient personal features;
- categorical classification.

This is the strongest direct correction of Yates located in the scholarship.

### Carruthers, 1990–2002

Carruthers reframed medieval memory as a craft of composition and invention rather than a technique of exact reproductive storage. For Boncompagno, the important points are:

- memory and imagination cooperate;
- mnemonic craft depends upon trained individual judgment;
- generalized precepts cannot exhaust an art;
- classification, signs, and environmental discipline matter as much as architectural loci;
- the “confusion” of *memoria* and *inventio* reflects medieval compositional practice;
- his lists resemble the working repertories of *ars dictaminis*;
- the ideal classroom and Lateran Council show a deliberately contemporary, practical rhetoric.

This both confirms Yates’s perception of Boncompagno’s originality and displaces her Paradise/Hell-centered reading.

Relevant works include:

- Mary Carruthers, *The Book of Memory* (Cambridge, 1990; 2nd ed. 2008).
- Mary Carruthers, “Boncompagno at the Cutting-Edge of Rhetoric: Rhetorical *Memoria* and the Craft of Memory,” *Journal of Medieval Latin* 6 (1996), pp. 44–64.
- Mary Carruthers, “Rhetorische memoria und die Praxis des Erinnerns,” in *Seelenmaschinen* (Vienna, 2000), pp. 15–36.
- Carruthers and Ziolkowski, *The Medieval Craft of Memory* (2002), pp. 103–117.

### Rossi, Small, Hajdu, Bolzoni, and others

- **Paolo Rossi**, *Clavis universalis*/*Logic and the Art of Memory*, supplies the broader history of memory arts, universal languages, Lullism, and combinatorics. It does not provide evidence that Boncompagno’s obscured orbicular device is Lullian.
- **Jocelyn Penny Small**, *Wax Tablets of the Mind*, is most useful for the ancient distinction between literacy, visualization, and mnemonic practice. Its principal subject is antiquity, not Boncompagno’s textual transmission.
- **Helga Hajdu**, *Das mnemotechnische Schrifttum des Mittelalters* (1936; reprint 1967), remains a pioneering catalogue but predates Yates, Carruthers, Tunberg, and the new manuscript discoveries.
- **Lina Bolzoni’s** work is indispensable for Renaissance memory, image, and literary culture, particularly Camillo and the *gallery of memory*. It does not establish Boncompagno-to-Camillo descent.
- **Walter Ong’s** work on Ramus clarifies the later conversion of memory and invention into diagrammatic dialectical order. Boncompagno’s genus-to-species descent is a useful precursor in kind, not proven ancestry.
- Scholarship on Llull by **Anthony Bonner** and **Alexander Fidora**, and on Bruno by **Rita Sturlese, Michele Ciliberto, Stephen Clucas, and Hilary Gatti**, corrects Yates’s broader narratives of Lullism and Hermetic memory but does not turn Boncompagno’s 1235 device into a Lullian or Brunian system.
- Work on Camillo by **Bolzoni and Stagno** concerns a later theater grounded in Renaissance textual, architectural, and mythological structures. No documentary line from Boncompagno’s Paradise/Hell chapters to Camillo has been shown.

**Scope finding:** Many scholars specified in the brief are essential to Yates’s complete forty-five-system genealogy but only indirectly relevant to Boncompagno. Invoking them as if they had all adjudicated this particular passage would manufacture a scholarly debate that does not exist.

## History and context

### Classical antecedents

Boncompagno inherits:

- the natural/artificial distinction from the *Ad Herennium*;
- the general concept that art and exercise strengthen nature;
- strikingness and emotional salience;
- memory as a component of rhetoric;
- taxonomic and topical methods from dialectic;
- signs, images, and inscriptions from wider rhetorical and educational culture.

He omits the canonical ancient prescription to select a sequence of architectural places and deposit extraordinary images representing matters or words.

### *Ars dictaminis* and Bologna

Bologna was a European center of professional rhetoric, law, notarial culture, and document production. Boncompagno’s “newest rhetoric” addresses advocates and legally trained speakers. The memory of names, ranks, offices, origins, legal signs, seals, penalties, and institutional order fits this professional environment.

Boncompagno claimed that “Cicero’s” rhetoric was scarcely taught except as a fable or mechanical art. Medieval “Cicero” here includes the pseudonymous *Ad Herennium*. Carruthers reads his departure from classical rules as deliberate modernization, not ignorance.

### Scholastic psychology

The posterior brain chamber, sensory impressions, humors, imagination, and the movement from particular to universal belong to pre- and early-scholastic psychology. Boncompagno predates the mature syntheses of Albert and Aquinas, and Book VIII does not quote Aristotle’s *De memoria et reminiscentia* in the way their works do.

### Dominicans and Franciscans

Yates proposed Bologna’s Dominican school as a bridge: Albert entered the Dominican Order in 1223, and she believed he studied in Bologna; Boncompagno praised Dominican and Franciscan preachers elsewhere.

**Caution:** Institutional proximity is not textual dependence. No record shows Albert attending Boncompagno’s teaching, reading this manuscript, or deriving his memory rules from it.

### Rhetoric to ethics

Yates’s larger thesis—that medieval readers joined *Ad Herennium* memory to prudence and ethics—is broadly sound. But Boncompagno’s Book VIII remains inside rhetoric and is heavily practical. Its moral eschatology coexists with rather than replaces professional mnemonics.

### What descends from it?

Documentable direct descendants are elusive. Later systems share:

- Paradise and Hell as ordered subjects;
- virtues and vices;
- visual alphabets;
- taxonomic descent;
- real architectural places;
- secrecy and boasts of virtuosity.

But these features also have independent classical, biblical, scholastic, monastic, and iconographic sources. Similarity is not proof of descent.

## People

### Boncompagno

Born at Signa around 1165–1175, teaching at Bologna by the 1190s, later active in Venice, Reggio, and Padua, and dead after 1240. He wrote rhetoric, letter-writing manuals, civic and legal works, satire, love-letter models, and an account of the siege of Ancona. [Treccani biography](https://www.treccani.it/enciclopedia/boncompagno-da-signa_%28Dizionario-Biografico%29/)

### Patron

The *Rhetorica novissima* was written at the request of Niccolò, bishop of Reggio, according to the biographical tradition. It was announced long before 1235, much of it probably composed in Venice, and issued at Bologna.

### Students

The 500 students are an autobiographical collective, not named individuals. They are both the alleged demonstration audience and the material of the mnemonic feat.

### Innocent III

His Fourth Lateran Council supplies the model of ordered social classification. Boncompagno does not say Innocent used an art of memory; he says the council’s ranked arrangement can be imitated mnemonically.

### Rivals and critics

Boncompagno’s writings attack glossators, rivals, plagiarists, and conservative Ciceronians. Bene da Firenze represented a rival rhetorical orientation. Salimbene called him:

> *more Florentinorum trufator maximus*

“in the Florentine manner, the greatest trickster.”

### The flying anecdote

Salimbene reports that, in mockery of the miracle-working Dominican Giovanni da Vicenza, Boncompagno announced that he would fly. Bologna assembled; Boncompagno appeared with wings and eventually dismissed the crowd, telling them to be satisfied that they had seen his face.

**Status:** near-contemporary chronicler’s anecdote, written decades later; plausible tradition, not connected to Book VIII’s memory system.

### Mock magic

In another satirical performance Boncompagno circulated a fabricated letter claiming a learned foreign magician would transform an ass into a lion, make it horned, give it eagle wings, and cause other marvels. The crowd was instructed to stare toward the midday sun—ensuring visual confusion.

**Status:** textually attested practical joke in the *Boncompagnus* tradition; evidence for parody of magical credulity, not for his practice of magic.

### Requested anecdotes not found

No reliable connection was found between this system and:

- an auctioneer;
- a professor’s party;
- a student who literally lost an acquired artificial memory;
- an accused mnemonic magician.

Those belong, if anywhere, to other memory-art traditions. Boncompagno’s own forgetful persons, 500-student boast, and mock-magical performances should not be merged with them.

## The magic question

### Was the system magical?

**No, on the available evidence.**

Book VIII includes celestial influence, a solar sphere, an orbicular operation, secrecy, medical compounds, infernal imagery, and an “imaginary alphabet.” These can sound occult when isolated. In context:

- celestial influence cannot be fully known or altered by human art;
- God alone governs nature;
- pre-existence of souls is rejected as theologically false;
- Paradise must be believed and cannot be captured by artifice;
- Hell is affirmed as Catholic doctrine;
- the Athenian materialists are condemned as heretics;
- the medicinal advice belongs to humoral regimen;
- the cryptic geometry does not prescribe invocation, consecration, spirits, planetary elections, characters, suffumigations, or talismans.

### Ars notoria

No dependence on the *Ars notoria* is demonstrated. Both traditions may promise extensive knowledge, use diagrams or figures, cultivate secrecy, and frame knowledge religiously, but Boncompagno gives no prayers, angelic names, ritual schedules, notae to be inspected devotionally, or revelatory operations characteristic of the *Ars notoria*.

### Heresy and consequences

The only explicit heretics are those denying resurrection and reducing bodies to elements. Boncompagno positions himself against them.

No trial, condemnation, prohibition, inquisitorial investigation, or ecclesiastical censure of Book VIII has been located.

### Why later readers may misclassify it

Yates’s later chapters connect memory to Renaissance occult philosophy; reading backward can make every circle, alphabet, secret, or celestial reference look proto-Brunian. That is methodologically unsafe. Boncompagno’s circle-like language is embedded in classification and imagination, not astral magic.

## Afterlife

### Albertus and Aquinas

Yates’s line—Boncompagno → Bolognese friars → Albert → Aquinas—is an attractive contextual hypothesis. Later scholarship supports a common intellectual environment but not direct textual descent.

Albert and Aquinas explicitly reconcile Aristotelian psychology with “Tullius” on places and images. Boncompagno neither performs that reconciliation nor supplies their detailed rules.

### Dante

Yates proposes the *Commedia* as a cosmic memory order of Hell, Purgatory, and Paradise. Later writers, including Roberto Antonelli and Luca Marcozzi, have explored connections between rhetorical memory, Boncompagno, and Dante.

**Documented:** Dante’s poem is profoundly ordered, visual, ethical, and eschatological; later memory treatises recommend Dante for remembering infernal punishments.

**Not documented:** Dante read Book VIII or constructed the *Commedia* from Boncompagno’s system.

The earliest explicit connection cited by Yates is much later: Ludovico Dolce’s 1562 adaptation of Romberch recommends Virgil and Dante for distinguishing infernal punishments.

### Giotto and frescoes

Yates suggests that Giotto’s virtues and vices in the Arena Chapel, framing the Last Judgment, behave like vivid memory images on differentiated loci.

**Status:** brilliant iconographic hypothesis; no commission document, artist’s statement, or contemporary commentary connects the frescoes to Boncompagno.

The same caution applies to Nardo di Cione’s Hell at Santa Maria Novella and Dominican chapter-house cycles. They are compatible with mnemonic use without proving mnemonic origin.

### Romberch and Rossellius

Johannes Romberch’s *Congestorium artificiose memorie* (1520) and Cosmas Rossellius’s *Thesaurus artificiosae memoriae* (1579) explicitly make Paradise, Purgatory, and Hell artificial-memory place systems. Here Yates has direct printed evidence.

Their detailed diagrams are later constructions. They should not be retrojected into Boncompagno.

### Fludd

Robert Fludd’s seventeenth-century memory system includes a Last Judgment image, virtues and vices, visual alphabets, theatrical places, and cosmic arrangements. Yates calls this a relic of the older tradition.

**Status:** broad structural affinity; no documentary Boncompagno–Fludd chain.

### Emblem books and visual alphabets

Boncompagno’s undisclosed imaginary alphabet belongs to the prehistory of later visual alphabets, but its exact relation cannot be established. Romberch, Rossellius, Peter of Ravenna, Giambattista della Porta, Bruno, and Fludd give clearer later evidence.

### Borges, Eco, and modern literature

Borges’s Funes represents limitless involuntary retention without classification or abstraction—the opposite of Boncompagno’s selective, hierarchical, cue-driven craft. Eco’s encyclopedic and semiotic interests make medieval memory relevant by analogy, but no specific Boncompagno reception was identified.

These are modern comparative echoes, not descendants.

### Memory palaces and athletes

Modern memory athletes use ordered routes, loci, conspicuous imagery, person-action-object encoding, and deliberate retrieval. Boncompagno’s stable seats, salient human features, emotional distinctiveness, and categorical descent are recognizably mnemonic, but he does not teach the standardized “method of loci” used in contemporary competition.

Any branded “Boncompagno Paradise-and-Hell memory palace” is a modern reconstruction.

### Computing and combinatorics

Boncompagno’s hierarchy resembles a tree-index; his alphabet resembles encoding; his orbicular genera suggest mechanical combination. These analogies are legitimate explanatory comparisons.

The celebrated intellectual genealogy from Llull through combinatorics to Leibniz and symbolic machines belongs to Lull’s demonstrable logical Art, not to Boncompagno. No evidence makes Boncompagno a link in the Llull–Leibniz–computer chain.

## Controversies and disputes

### 1. Is it truly a “memory of Paradise and Hell” system?

**Disputed modern naming.** The title is useful for cataloguing Yates’s narrative, but not Boncompagno’s own name for an integrated system. His rubric is simply *De memoria*. Paradise and Hell occupy three of twenty-six subsections.

### 2. Did he give no place-and-image rules?

**Half true.** He does not reproduce the classical loci-and-*imagines agentes* rules. But he gives:

- mental images;
- a memory chamber;
- maps;
- stable seats;
- classroom architecture;
- hierarchical and categorical ordering;
- conspicuous distinguishing signs;
- an imaginary alphabet;
- repetitive exercise.

The statement “we wait in vain” reflects Yates’s search for the *Ad Herennium* format, not an absence of technique.

### 3. Are Paradise and Hell mnemonic loci?

**Paradise:** expressly no, insofar as artificial comprehension of its ineffable reality is concerned.  
**Hell:** Etna is a sensory analogy and possible terrestrial mouth, but is not divided into mnemonic places.  
**Later tradition:** Romberch and Rossellius do construct such loci.

### 4. Are virtues and vices the system’s images?

No. They are some of many *notae* by which a person’s name may be retrieved. Yates excerpted the moral items and detached them from their onomastic and physiognomic context.

### 5. Influence on Albert and Aquinas

Possible but unproved. Yates’s evidence is chronological and institutional. Direct citation or manuscript use is absent.

### 6. Influence on Dante and Giotto

Possible cultural background; direct influence unproved. Yates’s argument is analogical and iconographic.

### 7. The imaginary alphabet

Its existence is asserted; its content is secret. Reconstructions using alphabetic objects, human-letter forms, Hebrew letters, or later visual alphabets are modern conjectures.

### 8. The quadrangle in the solar sphere

The passage is genuinely obscure. No consensus reconstruction or manuscript diagram was found. Calling it a wheel is descriptive shorthand; calling it Lullian, astrological, or computational goes beyond the evidence.

### 9. The date

**1235** is the date of publication/completion at Bologna. The work was announced before 1215 and worked on intermittently, probably in Venice and Reggio. Calling the entire text a composition suddenly written in 1235 is oversimplified.

### 10. Manuscript count

Older scholarship worked from two or three main witnesses; newer bibliographic resources report a far larger transmitted corpus including excerpts. Until the new critical edition and census appear, “eighty manuscripts” should not be presented as eighty complete copies of Book VIII.

### 11. Gaudenzi’s reliability

Gaudenzi remains the standard edition but is not critical by modern standards. Tunberg documents mistaken expansions and disorder in a source manuscript; Schiavetto and Garbini have called for a new edition.

### 12. Magical reputation

Boncompagno’s mock magic and flying performance foster the image of an eccentric wonder-worker. The contemporary evidence instead shows a satirist manipulating public expectation. No magical prosecution is documented.

### 13. Yates’s own later revisions

No later Yates publication was located in which she expressly withdrew or corrected the Boncompagno interpretation. The substantive correction entered the literature through Tunberg and the Carruthers-centered shift from “systems of loci” to memory as compositional craft.

## Open questions

1. What does the complete manuscript tradition reveal about VIII.1.17’s quadrangle, solar sphere, and line directions?
2. Do any manuscripts contain erased, omitted, marginal, or unfinished diagrams?
3. What are the full shelfmarks, dates, and textual relations of P, Va, P², Tr, Bern 322, and the newly identified excerpts?
4. Does the forthcoming Garbini critical edition alter the text of the Paradise clause or the imaginary alphabet?
5. What immediate source supplied the rejected doctrine of prenatal omniscience?
6. What is the source of the Athenian corpse-and-vine exemplum?
7. Can *diamargariton*, *pliris*, and *napium* be identified more exactly from contemporary pharmacological texts?
8. Is VIII.1.17 deliberately parodic—a satire on secret mnemonic masters—or a sincere but esoteric technical précis?
9. Was the 500-student feat independently reported by a pupil, rival, chronicler, or university document?
10. Can manuscript ownership demonstrate reception among Bolognese lawyers, Dominicans, or Franciscans?
11. Did later visual-alphabet writers ever cite Boncompagno directly?
12. When did modern cataloguers first begin calling this discrete complex “Boncompagno’s memory of Paradise and Hell”?
13. How much of Yates’s Giotto/Dante genealogy survives once *signacula* are restored to their immediate context: remembering names and persons?
