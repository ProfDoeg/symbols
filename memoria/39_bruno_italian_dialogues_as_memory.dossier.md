# Bruno's Italian dialogues as memory: *Cena, Eroici furori, Spaccio*: Research Dossier

## Basic identification

| Field | Identification |
|---|---|
| Catalogue designation | System 39 of 45: “Bruno’s Italian dialogues as memory” |
| Exponent | Giordano Bruno (Nola, 1548–Rome, 1600), former Dominican friar, philosopher, mnemonic teacher |

| Yates | *The Art of Memory* (1966), ch. XIV, pp. 308–319: *Cena*, pp. 309–313; *Furori*, pp. 313–314; *Spaccio*, pp. 314–319 |

### Governing finding

**[Documented text]** None of these three dialogues calls itself an *ars memoriae*, lays out a complete mnemonic curriculum, instructs readers to memorize the dialogue, or gives an explicit retrieval procedure comparable to Bruno’s *De umbris idearum*, *Cantus Circaeus*, *Ars reminiscendi*, *Explicatio triginta sigillorum*, or *De imaginum, signorum et idearum compositione*.

**[Yates’s interpretation]** The three dialogues nevertheless retain, in progressively different literary forms, structures characteristic of Bruno’s technical memory writings.

**[Later scholarly reconstruction]** The strongest post-Yates position is neither “the dialogues are mnemonic manuals” nor “memory is irrelevant.” Lina Bolzoni and Stephen Clucas treat mnemonic habits as part of Bruno’s poetics and psychology: the dialogues and the technical arts belong to the same intellectual project, but perform different literary and philosophical work. Clucas expressly argues that the *Spaccio* and *Furori* share with the memory treatises an effort to coordinate intellect, imagination, and affect for ethical and intellectual transformation. [Clucas’s article abstract](https://www.persee.fr/doc/xvii_0291-3798_2004_num_58_1_1969); [2024 bibliographic record](https://philpapers.org/rec/CLUMIL).

**Verdict:** “Bruno’s Italian dialogues as memory” is the name of Yates’s comparative reading, not a title or independently documented system devised by Bruno.

## The system in detail

## 1. *La cena de le ceneri*: London as an itinerary of places

### Text and structure

The *Cena* consists of five dialogues. Teofilo recounts an Ash Wednesday supper at which “the Nolan”—Bruno’s represented self—defends the Earth’s motion and an enlarged Copernican cosmology against the Oxford doctors Torquato and Nundinio.

The second dialogue narrates the journey to the supper; the subsequent dialogues supply the philosophical disputation. The geographical sequence is broadly:

1. Castelnau’s French embassy near Butcher Row.
2. The straight road westward along the Strand.
3. A diversion toward the Thames to obtain a boat.
4. Waiting for a decrepit boat.
5. A short and abortive river journey.
6. Disembarkation into dark, muddy lanes.
7. A near-return to the starting point.
8. A renewed westward journey.
9. The “pyramid” at Charing Cross.
10. Arrival at the unnamed supper house near the royal palace or Whitehall.
11. Entry through the hall, ascent upstairs, seating at table.
12. The debate.

The French embassy occupied a site off the Strand near Butcher Row, approximately in the vicinity of modern St Clement Danes. The normal westward route led toward Charing Cross and Whitehall. Contemporary travel by water was entirely plausible. [Historical topographical discussion](https://www.strandlines.london/2020/11/02/literary-testimonies-on-the-strand/).

### The explicit itinerary

**[Documented text]** Bruno writes:

> “Or, benché fussemo ne la strada diritta, pensando di far meglio, per accortar il camino, divertimmo verso il fiume Tamesi, per ritrovar un battello, che ne conducesse verso il palazzo.”

“Although we were on the straight road, thinking to do better and shorten the journey, we turned toward the River Thames to find a boat that would take us toward the palace.”

This is *Cena*, dialogue II; Aquilecchia’s Sansoni pagination begins the relevant narrative at pp. 26 ff. The passage is also preserved in the digital reconstruction of Bruno’s life and texts. [INSR transcription](https://vitadibruno.insr.it/index.php?id=774).

The boat, when it arrives, is:

> “una de le reliquie del diluvio”

“one of the relics of the Flood.”

After the boatmen put them ashore, the travellers discover that they have effectively circled back:

> “vintidui passi discosti da onde eravamo partiti”

“twenty-two paces from where we had started.”

At Charing Cross comes:

> “la piramide vicina a quella mansione dove tre strade concorreno”

“the pyramid near that dwelling where three streets meet.”

The pyramid is the Eleanor Cross at Charing Cross, not a Bruno-designed mnemonic monument. A contemporary urban reading of the episode is independently intelligible without memory theory. [Narrative and topographical synopsis](https://it.wikipedia.org/wiki/La_cena_de_le_ceneri); [older historical translation and discussion](https://resources.warburg.sas.ac.uk/pdf/acn414b2393959.pdf).

### What are the places and images?

**[Yates’s interpretation]**

- **Places:** embassy, Strand, river landing, boat, muddy lane, Charing Cross, supper house, hall, stair, table.
- **Images:** Bruno/Nolano, Teofilo, Florio, Guin/Gwinne, the boatmen, hostile Londoners, servants, Greville, Torquato and Nundinio.
- **Order:** fixed by the narrated journey and then by seating at the table.
- **Material retrieved:** the social, cosmological, religious, and polemical themes of the supper.

Yates’s memorable formulation is:

> “We can thus see in the *Cena* how the art of memory could as it were develop into literature; how the streets of memory places could become populated with characters, could become the backcloth for a drama.”

(*Art of Memory*, p. 312.)

### Mnemosyne and the seals

**[Documented text]** In the prefatory material Bruno invokes the Muse/Mnemosyne and speaks of something “hidden beneath thirty seals.” The number points toward the *Explicatio triginta sigillorum*, printed in London in 1583, whose thirty “seals” are intellectual and mnemonic procedures.

**[Yates’s interpretation]** This invocation signals that the dialogue issues from Bruno’s occult memory art.

**[Correction]** An invocation to Mnemosyne, even when joined to the thirty seals, does not convert the *Cena* into a memory manual. It establishes an intertextual relationship with Bruno’s mnemonic writings. The actual dialogue never instructs a practitioner to install its characters in the London places or to retrieve the cosmological argument by mentally retracing the route.

### Is the journey historical?

**[Documented conflict]** In the *Cena*, Greville is represented as host. During the Venetian proceedings Bruno stated that the Ash Wednesday supper took place in the French ambassador’s house.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** Possible explanations include fictional transposition, protective anonymity, conflation of more than one debate, or Bruno’s imperfect recollection eight years later.

**[Disputed]** Whether the entire river-and-street journey occurred is unknowable. Yates herself asks whether it was “entirely imaginary.” The topography is real; that does not authenticate the narrated incident.

### Allegorical meanings

**[Yates’s interpretation]** The decrepit, leaking boat may be the Church; the dark walled passage may recall the enclosure of a convent or monastery; the journey’s difficulty becomes the heroic philosopher’s passage through ignorance and hostility.

**[Documented text]** Bruno himself generalizes the ordeal:

> “Le cose ordinarie e facili son per il volgo ed ordinaria gente; gli uomini rari, eroichi e divini passano per questo camino de la difficoltà…”

“Ordinary and easy things are for the vulgar and ordinary people; rare, heroic and divine men pass by this road of difficulty…”

**[Disputed]** The ethical symbolism of difficulty is textual. The precise “boat = Church” and “lane = convent” equations are Yatesian hypotheses, not Bruno’s glosses.

### Retrieval and rules

No rules are supplied for:

- selecting appropriately spaced London loci;
- revisiting them in forward and reverse order;
- preventing crowded or obscure places;
- encoding words versus things;
- refreshing images;
- translating an argument into the characters of the dialogue.

Those rules exist in the classical and Renaissance memory tradition, but importing them here is an inference. Therefore:

**[Finding from absence]** The *Cena* offers a narratively ordered topography, not a reconstructable working mnemonic algorithm.

## 2. *De gli eroici furori*: verbal imprese and affective images

### Form

The work has two parts, each of five dialogues. It combines dialogue, lyric poetry, philosophical commentary, myths, and descriptions of imprese. It is dedicated to Philip Sidney.

The relevant sequence begins in part I, dialogue V and continues into part II. Contrary to the catalogue’s “about fifty emblems,” the commonly accepted count is **twenty-eight described imprese**. No pictures of those imprese are printed in the 1585 edition: the “body” of each device is verbalized. [Canone’s edition and census information](https://www.iliesi.cnr.it/prin/ipertesto_testi_prinILIESI.php?pagina=Ipertesto&scheda=Scheda_Furori.html); [description of the twenty-eight](https://it.wikipedia.org/wiki/De_gli_eroici_furori).

### Bruno’s instructions for reading the sequence

**[Documented text]** Cicada and Tansillo examine warriors carrying devices of their passions and fortunes. In the first device:

> “Mira come questi guerrieri portano l’imprese de gli affetti e fortune loro… Basta che attendiamo al senso de le imprese, al significato di quel che vi sta scritto, al motto che accompagna la figura, ed al poema che compisce la figura dichiarandone il senso.”

“See how these warriors carry the devices of their affections and fortunes… Let it suffice that we attend to the meaning of the devices, to what is written there, to the motto accompanying the figure, and to the poem that completes the figure by explaining its meaning.”

The components are consequently:

1. a verbally described image or *corpo*;
2. a motto or *lemma*, often Latin;
3. a poem;
4. dialogical commentary.

This four-part construction modifies the usual emblem/impresa form. [English text of the passage](https://www2.esotericarchives.com/bruno/furori.htm).

### Worked example: the first shield

**[Documented text]**

> “Porta un scudo diviso in quattro colori; nel cimiero è dipinta una fiamma sotto una testa di bronzo, da’ cui forami esce con gran forza un fumoso vento; e sopra vi sono scritte queste parole: *At regna senserunt tria*.”

“He carries a shield divided into four colors; upon its crest is painted a flame beneath a bronze head, from whose openings a smoky wind issues with great force; above are written the words *At regna senserunt tria*—‘But three realms felt it.’”

Tansillo interprets the flame, globe, water, vapor, and pressure. The image is not a simple rebus for one word. It is a compressed model of affective conflict extending through natural philosophy, psychology, and poetic experience.

### Other characteristic image-complexes

The twenty-eight devices draw on:

- eyes, stars, and reflected light;
- hearts and arrows;
- flames, furnaces, smoke, water, and tears;
- wheels, circles, and contraries;
- shields, helmets, warriors, and wounds;
- moth-like attraction to destructive light;
- the divided self;
- the hunter Actaeon, his dogs, and Diana;
- the nine blind men;
- Circe;
- Amphitrite, the all-containing sea and image of the One manifested in nature.

### Actaeon and Amphitrite

**[Documented text]** Actaeon’s dogs become thoughts directed toward divine objects. They consume the lower self, freeing the intellect from sense and bodily confinement:

> “gli cani, pensieri de cose divine, divorano questo Atteone…”

“the dogs, thoughts of divine things, devour this Actaeon…”

The transformed hunter sees:

> “l’Anfitrite, fonte de tutti numeri, de tutte specie, de tutte raggioni, che è la monade, vera essenza de l’essere de tutti…”

“Amphitrite, source of all numbers, all species and all forms, the monad, the true essence of the being of all things…”

The movement is from multiplicity toward unity, though the absolute source is seen in its natural manifestation rather than comprehended in itself.

### Is this a memory system?

**[Yates’s carefully qualified interpretation]**

> “we begin to see the love emblems of the *Eroici furori* in a new context, not, of course, as a memory system, but as traces of the memory methods in a literary work.”

(*Art of Memory*, p. 314.)

Thus even Yates expressly denies that the *Furori* is itself a mnemonic system.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** Its relationship to memory is more plausibly located in:

- sequential visualization;
- affect as an instrument of fixation;
- conversion of arguments into memorable image-complexes;
- the dynamic interaction of picture, motto, verse, and exegesis;
- the elevation of imagination from sensible multiplicity toward intellectual unity.

Bolzoni places this procedure within Renaissance “literary memory,” especially Bruno’s transformation of Petrarchan and Ariostan material. Her work confirms Yates’s broad insight that literary form can internalize mnemonic operations, while avoiding the claim that every ordered image is a technical locus. [Bolzoni, “Images of Literary Memory”](https://ricerca.sns.it/handle/11384/4283); [“Note su Bruno e Ariosto”](https://ricerca.sns.it/retrieve/e3aacdfd-fe31-4c98-e053-3705fe0acb7e/Note_bruno_ariosto.pdf).

### “Contractions”

**[Documented text]** Near the conclusion Bruno speaks of contractions or concentrations of the soul’s powers. Yates connects these to the *Sigillus sigillorum* and other mnemonic writings in which *contractio* denotes an inward concentration of attention and affect.

**[Qualification]** Contraction is a psychological and contemplative principle broader than memory storage. It helps explain how an image may transform the subject, but it does not supply a list of loci or a retrieval routine.

## 3. *Spaccio de la bestia trionfante*: the constellational reform

### Correct date

The item-specific brief dates the *Spaccio* to 1585. That is incorrect.

**[Documented bibliographical fact]** The first edition belongs to **1584**, printed in London by John Charlewood under a false “Paris” imprint. It precedes the *Furori* of 1585. The dedication to Sidney is genuine. [Treccani](https://www.treccani.it/enciclopedia/giordano-bruno_%28Enciclopedia-machiavelliana%29/); [Archivio Giordano Bruno](https://www.iliesi.cnr.it/AGB/index_bruno.php).

### Bruno’s declared plan

The *Spaccio* has an explanatory epistle and three dialogues, each divided into three parts. Sofia recounts to Saulino the council of the gods at which Jupiter reforms the sky.

**[Documented text]**

> “questo mondo… prima diviso come in tante sfere, e poi distinto in circa quarant’otto immagini… viene ad essere principio e soggetto del nostro lavoro.”

“This world… first divided into so many spheres and then distinguished into about forty-eight images… becomes the principle and subject of our work.”

Bruno states that Jupiter:

> “avea colmo di tante bestie, come di tanti vizii, il cielo, secondo la forma di quarant’otto famose imagini”

“had filled heaven with as many beasts as vices, according to the form of forty-eight famous images,”

and now intends to banish them:

> “ed in quelle medesime stanze facendo succedere le già tanto tempo bandite e tanto indegnamente disperse virtudi.”

“causing the virtues, so long banished and unworthily dispersed, to succeed them in those same stations.”

Most explicitly, Bruno says the dialogue establishes:

> “in numero e certo ordine tutte le prime forme de la moralità”

“in number and fixed order all the primary forms of morality.”

[Italian text](https://www.intratext.com/IXT/ITA1492/_P1.HTM); [complete PDF transcription](https://www.ousia.it/SitoOusia/SitoOusia/TestiDiFilosofia/TestiPDF/Bruno/SpaccioDeLaBestiaTrionfante.pdf).

### Places, images, order

- **Places:** the traditional forty-eight constellation regions of the ancient celestial sphere.
- **Old images:** Ursa Major and Minor, Draco, Cepheus, Cassiopeia, Andromeda, Perseus, Pegasus, the zodiacal animals, Orion, Eridanus, the southern constellations, and so forth.
- **Material removed:** named vices, corrupt customs, false religious attitudes, and undesirable passions.
- **Material installed:** Truth, Prudence, Wisdom, Law, Judgment, Fortitude, Magnanimity, Diligence, Labour, Memory, and other virtues.
- **Order:** broadly the inherited astronomical/mythographical ordering associated with Ptolemy and especially the illustrated *Poetica astronomica* attributed to Hyginus.
- **Narrative operator:** Jupiter deliberates; Momus provokes and criticizes; Mercury executes or announces assignments; Sofia reports the celestial proceedings to Saulino.
- **Retrieval:** a reader proceeds through the sky in the dialogue’s order, recalling at each constellation the expelled vice, replacement virtue, dispute, myth, and ethical-political application.

### Worked example: Ursa Major

**[Documented text]**

> “Dove dunque era l’Orsa, per ragion del luogo, per esser parte più eminente del cielo, si prepone la Verità…”

“Where the Bear was, because of the place, as the most eminent part of heaven, Truth is placed first…”

Truth’s companions or implications include Entity, Necessity, Goodness, Principle, Middle, End, and Perfection. Here the dignity of the celestial location helps determine the dignity of the new occupant.

### Worked example: Lyra

**[Documented text]** In the place of Lyra Bruno installs Memory, Mnemosyne, and the nine Muses. This is the dialogue’s most explicit internal acknowledgment of memory as a cosmic and cultural virtue.

**[Correction]** Memory is one virtue among the reformed principles, not proof by itself that the whole work was designed for rote retrieval.

### Worked example: Orion

The Orion episode attacks “imposture,” useless cleverness, miracle claims, and the inversion of nature and virtue. Orion is told he may perform wonders on earth, including walking on water.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** The passage is widely understood as an anticlerical and anti-Christian satire, especially an attack upon the theology of miraculous faith detached from natural law and ethical works.

**[Disputed nuance]** Whether Orion simply “equals Christ” is too reductive: the figure condenses Christological polemic, imposture, pedantry, and abused miraculous authority.

### Hyginus, Ptolemy, and Romberch

**[Documented source tradition]**

- Ptolemy’s *Almagest* catalogued stars under forty-eight constellations.
- Pseudo-Hyginus’s *Poetica astronomica* supplied an influential ordered mythography of the constellation figures.
- Johannes Romberch’s *Congestorium artificiose memorie* (first edition 1520; Venice edition 1533) recommended celestial or star orders and referred readers to Hyginus when they needed an extended series of constellation images.

Yates observes that Romberch does not say exactly what material should be placed upon those constellations. Her suggestion that a preacher might arrange virtues and vices there is explicitly conjectural. [Yates’s discussion](https://resources.warburg.sas.ac.uk/mnemosyne/fq/yates_memory.pdf); [Library of Congress record for Romberch](https://www.loc.gov/item/10019844/).

**[Yates’s interpretation]**

> “he has taken a system from the memory text-books, the use of Hyginus on the order of the constellations as a memory order, and has ‘occultised’ it into a ‘Seal’ of his own.”

(*Art of Memory*, p. 317.)

**[Post-Yates confirmation and correction]** Leo Catana demonstrated in detail that Hyginus is indeed a major source of the *Spaccio*’s constellation mythology. But Catana situates it within a literary lineage: Lucian’s *Assembly/Parliament of the Gods*, followed by Renaissance moral adaptations in Alberti and Niccolò Franco. Thus Hyginus is confirmed; exclusive derivation through Romberch’s mnemonic handbook is not. [Catana, “Bruno’s *Spaccio* and Hyginus’ *Poetica astronomica*”](https://researchprofiles.ku.dk/en/publications/leo-catana-brunos-spaccio-and-hyginus-poetica-astronomica/).

### Is it a “Seal”?

**[Documented text]** Bruno does not title the *Spaccio* a seal and does not tell the reader to operate it as one of the thirty seals.

**[Yates’s interpretation]** “Seal” denotes a Brunian transformation of inherited order into an inward, philosophically charged imaginal structure.

**[Best reconstruction]** The *Spaccio* is much closer to an operable mnemonic arrangement than either the *Cena* or *Furori*: it has stable places, ordered images, substitutions, and paired ethical matter. Yet it remains an allegorical dialogue rather than a technical set of user instructions.

Bibliographical form:

Bibliographical form:

> *Spaccio de la bestia trionfante proposto da Giove, effettuato dal consiglio, revelato da Mercurio, recitato da Sofia, udito da Saulino, registrato dal Nolano.*

- Actual production: London, John Charlewood, 1584.

### Textual controversy

**[Documented scholarly dispute]** The 2000 Mondadori *Dialoghi filosofici italiani*, edited by Michele Ciliberto, generated an acrimonious dispute with Aquilecchia and Alain-Philippe Segonds over its use and alteration of Aquilecchia’s text. Ciliberto defended his corrections, especially in the *Furori*; his critics alleged extensive new errors and insufficiently transparent dependence. This is an editorial controversy, not evidence of materially different mnemonic diagrams or alternative “systems.” [Contemporary record of the dispute](https://ilmanifesto.it/archivio/2000009915); [Ciliberto’s response](https://www.giordanobruno.info/michele-ciliberto-e-il-plagio-del-meridiano-mondadori/).

Translations modernize an exceptionally allusive Italian and must not replace the critical text when interpreting *impresa*, *imagine*, *luogo*, *sigillo*, *contrazione*, or *furore*.

## Diagrams, wheels, tables, and plates

**[Negative finding]**

- The *Cena* supplies no mnemonic plan or map of London.
- The *Spaccio* supplies no engraved celestial chart, wheel, or table pairing constellations with virtues.
- The *Furori* supplies no printed emblem pictures; it describes them in words.
- No manuscript variants preserve a lost set of authorially approved illustrations.
- Modern reconstructions that map the journey or diagram the constellation replacements are editorial visualizations, not surviving Brunian plates.

Bruno’s actual combinatorial wheels and mnemonic diagrams occur in his technical Latin works. Conflating those objects with the three dialogues produces a modern hybrid.

## Yates’s account and scholarship since

## What Yates established

Yates’s chapter does three important things:

1. It refuses to isolate Bruno’s famous vernacular dialogues from the difficult mnemonic writings.
2. It identifies three distinct literary transformations of spatial-image organization.
3. It treats artificial memory as a mode of composition, imagination, and ethical transformation, not merely a trick for verbatim recall.

Her three escalating claims are:

- *Cena*: memory places become dramatic literature.
- *Furori*: mnemonic images leave traces in love-emblem poetry, though the work is not a memory system.
- *Spaccio*: an inherited celestial memory order becomes an “occult” Brunian seal.

The complete chapter is available through the Warburg’s Yates resource. [Chapter XIV](https://resources.warburg.sas.ac.uk/mnemosyne/fq/yates_memory.pdf).

## What later work has confirmed

### Lina Bolzoni

Bolzoni confirms the importance of literary memory: texts, images, quotations, characters, and inherited poetry can be broken apart and recomposed into new imaginal structures. Her work on Bruno and Ariosto shows that literary reminiscence is neither passive influence nor necessarily a formal memory palace. It is an active practice of visual and verbal recombination.

### Stephen Clucas

Clucas gives the strongest recent defense of a qualified Yatesian connection. The technical works and dialogues share:

- a metaphysics of unity and multiplicity;
- imagination as mediator;
- affective discipline;
- ethical self-transformation;
- ascent from sensible species toward intellectual organization.

But Clucas rejects a choice between Sturlese’s purely semiotic machine and Yates’s astral talisman. Bruno’s images can be logically and ethically operative without every one being an astrological talisman.

### Leo Catana

Catana confirms Hyginus as a substantial source for the *Spaccio* while widening the genealogy to Lucianic moral satire. This materially corrects Yates’s near-exclusive emphasis on Romberch and memory manuals.

### Aquilecchia, Canone, Ciliberto, Granada, Ordine

Modern philology reconstructs the dialogue cycle, its language, intertexts, and philosophical sequence far more securely than was possible in 1966. It emphasizes the unity of Bruno’s *nova filosofia*, ethical reform, anti-pedantry, natural religion, and theory of knowledge.

## What has been corrected or rejected

### Rita Sturlese

Sturlese’s 1991 critical edition and 1992 study of *De umbris idearum* demonstrated that Yates had misplaced planetary images when reconstructing Bruno’s wheel. That error affected her claim that the mechanism directly embodied astral relations. Sturlese instead reconstructed a rigorous system for converting syllables or words into images.

The Warburg project summarizes the decisive correction: Yates’s talismanic interpretation rested partly on a misplacement of the planetary images. [Warburg, “Yates and Bruno’s Mnemonics”](https://warburg.sas.ac.uk/research-fellowships/research-projects/archived-research-projects/giordano-bruno/yates-brunos-mnemonics).

This correction directly concerns *De umbris*, not the London-dialogue chapter. Its consequence is methodological: one cannot infer that every Brunian image, constellation, or god is a talisman intended to draw down a planetary force.

### Michele Ciliberto

Ciliberto criticizes the reduction of Bruno to a single “Hermetic” key and insists upon chronological development and the plurality of Bruno’s philosophical registers. Magic and Hermeticism must not simply be identified. [Ciliberto, “Bruno nel XX secolo”](https://ricerca.sns.it/handle/11384/4117).

### Hilary Gatti

Gatti accepts that Yates reopened Bruno’s mnemonic and magical contexts, but emphasizes Bruno’s natural philosophy, mathematics, civic ethics, and relation to early modern science. She also notes that Sturlese’s operational correction does not exhaust the metaphysical theory that Bruno gives his mnemonic practice.

### Mary Carruthers, Paolo Rossi, Jocelyn Penny Small

These authors do not directly adjudicate every London-dialogue claim, but alter its framework:

- Carruthers treats medieval memoria as an inventive and compositional matrix, not a passive storehouse.
- Rossi situates memory arts within logic, method, and universal-language projects.
- Small reconstructs the material and cognitive practices behind ancient memory metaphors.
- Together they support the possibility of “literary memory” while discouraging the assumption that every vivid literary sequence is a cryptographic mnemonic apparatus.

### Lull scholarship: Anthony Bonner and Alexander Fidora

Modern Lull scholarship distinguishes Ramon Llull’s *ars*—a demonstrative, relational and missionary method—from ordinary artificial memory. Bruno adapted Lullian letters, figures, combinations, and relational thinking, but his mnemonic wheels are not simply Llull’s system continued unchanged. This weakens any linear “Lull invented the memory wheel; Bruno inherited it” story.

### Ong and Ramus

Walter J. Ong showed that Ramist method reorganized knowledge through dichotomous spatial diagrams and printed outlines, often in tension with image-rich mnemonic practice. Sidney’s association with Ramist intellectual circles supplies relevant context, but no evidence proves that Sidney adopted Bruno’s celestial system or that the *Furori* was written as his mnemonic textbook.

## Yates’s later position

Yates did not publish a comprehensive retraction of chapter XIV. Her later essays continued to connect Bruno’s memory, imagination, Hermeticism, and religious reform. Her formulations often remained deliberately tentative—“could,” “traces,” “I think justifiable”—a qualification frequently lost in popular summaries.

## History and context

### Classical rhetoric

The inherited art of memory rests on *loci* and *imagines*: an ordered series of places and striking images deposited in them. Cicero, Quintilian, and the anonymous *Rhetorica ad Herennium* transmitted the canonical story of Simonides and rules for artificial memory.

The notorious lawsuit image in *Ad Herennium*—a poisoned victim in bed, the accused holding a cup, tablets, and ram’s testicles—belongs to the general tradition, not to these dialogues. Bruno neither quotes nor reenacts that worked example here.

### Scholastic psychology

Medieval Aristotelian psychology held that intellectual cognition ordinarily begins from sensible forms or phantasms. Albert the Great and Thomas Aquinas incorporated artificial memory into prudence. This helps explain why ordered, affectively forceful images could be ethical instruments without being magical talismans.

### Dominican memory culture

Bruno and Romberch were Dominicans. Dominican preaching, confession, moral taxonomy, and pedagogical order provided fertile ground for systems of virtues and vices. Yet no institutional record shows the *Spaccio* being taught in a Dominican school—it is fiercely unorthodox and was produced after Bruno had left the order.

### Lullism and combinatorics

Llull’s lettered figures and relational combinations entered late-medieval and Renaissance logic. Bruno combined these with classical artificial memory, astronomical images, and his metaphysics of nature. In the technical works, wheels can generate or encode combinations; in the dialogues, combination becomes literary juxtaposition and transformation.

### Hermeticism and natural magic

The *Asclepius* passage on Egyptian statues animated by divine or demonic powers strongly influenced Bruno’s account of ancient Egyptian religion. The *Spaccio* contains gods perceived inwardly as statues or pictures and advocates a restoration of natural justice.

**[Yates’s interpretation]** This makes the constellation reform “Egyptian” and magical.

**[Qualification]** The dialogue’s use of living gods, statues, and celestial images is real; whether the reader is expected to use them talismanically is not stated.

### Emblem and impresa culture

The *Furori* belongs to the sixteenth-century culture of imprese: image-body, motto, and interpretive discourse. Bruno’s distinctive innovation is to withhold the actual picture and make dialogue and poem generate it mentally. That intensifies visualization but also foregrounds semantic instability.

### Courts and patronage

- Michel de Castelnau housed Bruno and received the dedication of the *Cena*.
- Philip Sidney received the *Spaccio* and *Furori*.
- Fulke Greville appears as host in the *Cena*.
- John Florio belongs to the ambassadorial household and participates in the journey.
- John Charlewood printed the dialogues clandestinely.
- Henry III of France earlier patronized Bruno and questioned him about whether his memory was natural or magical.

## People

### Bruno as practitioner

**[Documented]** Bruno taught memory publicly and privately, published multiple mnemonic works, and represented the art as joining memory, discovery, and intellectual ordering.

### Henry III

According to Bruno’s Venetian testimony, Henry III summoned him to determine whether his extraordinary memory was natural or magical. Bruno said it was acquired “by science” and presented the king with a memory book. The general episode is credible because Bruno himself reported it under examination; embellishments about spectacular court demonstrations are less secure.

### Giovanni Francesco Mocenigo: patron turned accuser

Mocenigo invited Bruno to Venice to learn “the secrets of memory and the others that he professes.” Dissatisfied with the instruction and fearing Bruno’s departure, he confined him and denounced him.

His first denunciation accused Bruno of grave theological errors and of being a magician:

> “ch’era un mago”

“that he was a magician.”

[Text of the denunciation](https://www.matdid.it/letture/giordano-bruno-denuncia-e-sentenza).

**[Documented]** Memory instruction caused the relationship and occasioned Bruno’s physical seizure.

**[Correction]** Bruno was not condemned merely for teaching a memory palace. The proceedings concerned Trinity, Incarnation, Eucharist, the soul, eternity and plurality of worlds, religious authority, magic, and other propositions. The final Roman trial record is incomplete, so any one-cause explanation is unsound.

### Sidney and Greville

Their presence anchors Bruno within elite Elizabethan literary and political culture.

**[Disputed]** There is no documentary evidence that Sidney became Bruno’s mnemonic pupil.

**[Disputed]** Suggestions that Greville’s or Sidney’s circle transmitted a Brunian constellation system directly to Shakespeare remain conjectural.

### Torquato and Nundinio

These names represent Oxford opponents in the *Cena*. Identification with particular historical doctors has been attempted but remains uncertain. They function literarily as pedantic Aristotelians whether or not they are portraits.

### The requested anecdotes

No reliable connection has been found between these three dialogues and:

- “the auctioneer”;
- “the professor’s party”;
- “the student who lost his memory.”

These anecdotes belong to other episodes or systems in the wider memory-art tradition. Importing them here would create a false provenance.

“The accused magician,” however, securely applies to Bruno through Mocenigo’s denunciation.

## The magic question

### Was the system magical?

The answer depends upon what “system” means.

1. **The narrative structures themselves:** not explicitly magical operations.
2. **Bruno’s wider philosophy of imagination:** closely connected with natural magic.
3. **Yates’s reconstruction:** an occult or Hermetic memory extending toward talismanic use.
4. **Sturlese’s operational reconstruction:** a regulated semiotic mechanism, not a set of astral talismans.
5. **Clucas’s synthesis:** logical, mnemonic, ethical, metaphysical, and in a qualified Brunian sense magical.

### Statues and inward gods

**[Documented text]** The *Spaccio* presents reforming gods as inward powers and sometimes as statue-like or pictorial forms.

**[Yates’s interpretation]** These are “magically animated statues” comparable to the statues of the Hermetic *Asclepius*.

**[Qualification]** Literary animation and inner visualization do not prove ritual consecration, spirit invocation, or manufacture of talismans.

### Ars notoria

Bruno knew of the *Ars notoria*, the medieval ritual art promising rapid knowledge through prayers, figures, and spiritual assistance. In the *Sigillus sigillorum* he criticizes credulous practitioners who imagine themselves suddenly made wise by such means.

**[Correction]** Bruno’s artificial memory is not simply the *Ars notoria*. It demands trained imagination, ordering, and intellectual labor even when framed through magic.

### Heresy and consequences

Mocenigo conflated memory secrets, magic, and theological suspicion. But the surviving evidence does not show that any one of the three dialogues’ alleged mnemonic arrangements was entered as an independent capital charge.

**[Legend]** “Bruno was burned for the art of memory.”

**Evidence:** none sufficient.

**[Legend/modern simplification]** “Bruno was burned for believing the Earth goes around the Sun.”

**Evidence:** cosmology entered the proceedings, but the trial encompassed a much broader group of philosophical and theological propositions. [Stanford Encyclopedia, including trial bibliography](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2025/entries/bruno/).

## Afterlife

### Literature

Yates proposed that the *Spaccio*’s celestial rhetoric might illuminate Shakespearean language, including Berowne’s speech in *Love’s Labour’s Lost*.

**[Disputed]** Bruno and Shakespeare were simultaneously connected to London’s theatrical-literary environment, but no meeting, borrowing document, or annotated Shakespearean copy survives.

Borges’s “Funes the Memorious” is a profound modern meditation on unlimited recollection and the loss of abstraction. It belongs to the conceptual afterlife of memory, but no direct textual descent from these dialogues has been demonstrated.

Umberto Eco discussed combinatorial arts, Llull, Bruno, and universal languages. Eco’s genealogies concern the dream of a complete symbolic order; they do not establish that modern programming directly descends from the *Spaccio*.

### Visual and emblematic culture

The *Furori* participates directly in impresa culture and indirectly in the broader emblem-book tradition. Later illustrators may realize the verbal images, but those realizations are post-Brunian interpretations.

The constellation reform also belongs beside moralized mythography, celestial atlases, and the personification of virtues in court art. There is no documented fresco cycle copied from the *Spaccio*.

### Memory-palace revival and memory athletes

Modern memory athletes use ordered routes and striking images closely analogous to classical loci and images. The *Cena* can readily be converted into a modern route, and the *Spaccio* into a forty-eight-station celestial palace.

**[Modern invention]** Such reconstructions are useful adaptations, not evidence that Bruno instructed his London readers to use these dialogues that way.

### Computing and combinatorics

A defensible genealogy is:

> Llullian finite alphabets and rotating figures → Renaissance combinatorial adaptations, including Bruno → seventeenth-century projects of universal method and language → Leibniz’s *ars combinatoria* and *characteristica universalis*.

**[Documented intellectual descent]** Leibniz read and discussed Llullian combinatorics.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** Bruno belongs to the intervening history of symbolic combination.

**[Modern myth]** “Bruno invented the computer” or “his wheels were mechanical computers.” They are rule-governed symbolic devices and therefore meaningful ancestors by analogy, not programmable digital machines.

## Open questions

1. Was the *Cena*’s journey composed from an actual failed trip, several London experiences, or wholly literary topography?
2. Can the unnamed supper house be identified securely?
3. How much of the *Spaccio*’s constellation order comes directly from a particular illustrated Hyginus edition?
4. Did Bruno consult Romberch at this stage, or did Yates infer the connection from structural similarity?
5. Were the *Furori*’s twenty-eight devices ever intended for illustration?
6. Did contemporary readers mentally visualize the devices as stable pictures or experience them as deliberately shifting verbal constructions?
7. How precisely do Bruno’s “contractions” in the *Furori* relate to the exercises of the *Sigillus sigillorum*?
8. What did Sidney, Greville, Florio, and Castelnau actually understand or accept of Bruno’s mnemonic philosophy?
9. How should “magic” be divided among ritual magic, astral magic, natural magic, psychological self-fashioning, and polemical accusation?
10. What was in the lost portions of Bruno’s Roman trial file, and would they clarify the relation between mnemonic teaching and the formal charges?
11. Can digital collation of all surviving first-edition copies identify press variants relevant to image-language, punctuation, or sequence?
12. To what degree does the *Spaccio*’s ordered reform support practical recollection, and to what degree does it instead dramatize the impossibility of fixing moral meanings permanently in a universe of *vicissitudine*?

### Governing finding

**[Documented text]** None of these three dialogues calls itself an *ars memoriae*, lays out a complete mnemonic curriculum, instructs readers to memorize the dialogue, or gives an explicit retrieval procedure comparable to Bruno’s *De umbris idearum*, *Cantus Circaeus*, *Ars reminiscendi*, *Explicatio triginta sigillorum*, or *De imaginum, signorum et idearum compositione*.

**[Yates’s interpretation]** The three dialogues nevertheless retain, in progressively different literary forms, structures characteristic of Bruno’s technical memory writings.

**[Later scholarly reconstruction]** The strongest post-Yates position is neither “the dialogues are mnemonic manuals” nor “memory is irrelevant.” Lina Bolzoni and Stephen Clucas treat mnemonic habits as part of Bruno’s poetics and psychology: the dialogues and the technical arts belong to the same intellectual project, but perform different literary and philosophical work. Clucas expressly argues that the *Spaccio* and *Furori* share with the memory treatises an effort to coordinate intellect, imagination, and affect for ethical and intellectual transformation. [Clucas’s article abstract](https://www.persee.fr/doc/xvii_0291-3798_2004_num_58_1_1969); [2024 bibliographic record](https://philpapers.org/rec/CLUMIL).

**Verdict:** “Bruno’s Italian dialogues as memory” is the name of Yates’s comparative reading, not a title or independently documented system devised by Bruno.

## The system in detail

## 1. *La cena de le ceneri*: London as an itinerary of places

### Text and structure

The *Cena* consists of five dialogues. Teofilo recounts an Ash Wednesday supper at which “the Nolan”—Bruno’s represented self—defends the Earth’s motion and an enlarged Copernican cosmology against the Oxford doctors Torquato and Nundinio.

The second dialogue narrates the journey to the supper; the subsequent dialogues supply the philosophical disputation. The geographical sequence is broadly:

1. Castelnau’s French embassy near Butcher Row.
2. The straight road westward along the Strand.
3. A diversion toward the Thames to obtain a boat.
4. Waiting for a decrepit boat.
5. A short and abortive river journey.
6. Disembarkation into dark, muddy lanes.
7. A near-return to the starting point.
8. A renewed westward journey.
9. The “pyramid” at Charing Cross.
10. Arrival at the unnamed supper house near the royal palace or Whitehall.
11. Entry through the hall, ascent upstairs, seating at table.
12. The debate.

The French embassy occupied a site off the Strand near Butcher Row, approximately in the vicinity of modern St Clement Danes. The normal westward route led toward Charing Cross and Whitehall. Contemporary travel by water was entirely plausible. [Historical topographical discussion](https://www.strandlines.london/2020/11/02/literary-testimonies-on-the-strand/).

### The explicit itinerary

**[Documented text]** Bruno writes:

> “Or, benché fussemo ne la strada diritta, pensando di far meglio, per accortar il camino, divertimmo verso il fiume Tamesi, per ritrovar un battello, che ne conducesse verso il palazzo.”

“Although we were on the straight road, thinking to do better and shorten the journey, we turned toward the River Thames to find a boat that would take us toward the palace.”

This is *Cena*, dialogue II; Aquilecchia’s Sansoni pagination begins the relevant narrative at pp. 26 ff. The passage is also preserved in the digital reconstruction of Bruno’s life and texts. [INSR transcription](https://vitadibruno.insr.it/index.php?id=774).

The boat, when it arrives, is:

> “una de le reliquie del diluvio”

“one of the relics of the Flood.”

After the boatmen put them ashore, the travellers discover that they have effectively circled back:

> “vintidui passi discosti da onde eravamo partiti”

“twenty-two paces from where we had started.”

At Charing Cross comes:

> “la piramide vicina a quella mansione dove tre strade concorreno”

“the pyramid near that dwelling where three streets meet.”

The pyramid is the Eleanor Cross at Charing Cross, not a Bruno-designed mnemonic monument. A contemporary urban reading of the episode is independently intelligible without memory theory. [Narrative and topographical synopsis](https://it.wikipedia.org/wiki/La_cena_de_le_ceneri); [older historical translation and discussion](https://resources.warburg.sas.ac.uk/pdf/acn414b2393959.pdf).

### What are the places and images?

**[Yates’s interpretation]**

- **Places:** embassy, Strand, river landing, boat, muddy lane, Charing Cross, supper house, hall, stair, table.
- **Images:** Bruno/Nolano, Teofilo, Florio, Guin/Gwinne, the boatmen, hostile Londoners, servants, Greville, Torquato and Nundinio.
- **Order:** fixed by the narrated journey and then by seating at the table.
- **Material retrieved:** the social, cosmological, religious, and polemical themes of the supper.

Yates’s memorable formulation is:

> “We can thus see in the *Cena* how the art of memory could as it were develop into literature; how the streets of memory places could become populated with characters, could become the backcloth for a drama.”

(*Art of Memory*, p. 312.)

### Mnemosyne and the seals

**[Documented text]** In the prefatory material Bruno invokes the Muse/Mnemosyne and speaks of something “hidden beneath thirty seals.” The number points toward the *Explicatio triginta sigillorum*, printed in London in 1583, whose thirty “seals” are intellectual and mnemonic procedures.

**[Yates’s interpretation]** This invocation signals that the dialogue issues from Bruno’s occult memory art.

**[Correction]** An invocation to Mnemosyne, even when joined to the thirty seals, does not convert the *Cena* into a memory manual. It establishes an intertextual relationship with Bruno’s mnemonic writings. The actual dialogue never instructs a practitioner to install its characters in the London places or to retrieve the cosmological argument by mentally retracing the route.

### Is the journey historical?

**[Documented conflict]** In the *Cena*, Greville is represented as host. During the Venetian proceedings Bruno stated that the Ash Wednesday supper took place in the French ambassador’s house.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** Possible explanations include fictional transposition, protective anonymity, conflation of more than one debate, or Bruno’s imperfect recollection eight years later.

**[Disputed]** Whether the entire river-and-street journey occurred is unknowable. Yates herself asks whether it was “entirely imaginary.” The topography is real; that does not authenticate the narrated incident.

### Allegorical meanings

**[Yates’s interpretation]** The decrepit, leaking boat may be the Church; the dark walled passage may recall the enclosure of a convent or monastery; the journey’s difficulty becomes the heroic philosopher’s passage through ignorance and hostility.

**[Documented text]** Bruno himself generalizes the ordeal:

> “Le cose ordinarie e facili son per il volgo ed ordinaria gente; gli uomini rari, eroichi e divini passano per questo camino de la difficoltà…”

“Ordinary and easy things are for the vulgar and ordinary people; rare, heroic and divine men pass by this road of difficulty…”

**[Disputed]** The ethical symbolism of difficulty is textual. The precise “boat = Church” and “lane = convent” equations are Yatesian hypotheses, not Bruno’s glosses.

### Retrieval and rules

No rules are supplied for:

- selecting appropriately spaced London loci;
- revisiting them in forward and reverse order;
- preventing crowded or obscure places;
- encoding words versus things;
- refreshing images;
- translating an argument into the characters of the dialogue.

Those rules exist in the classical and Renaissance memory tradition, but importing them here is an inference. Therefore:

**[Finding from absence]** The *Cena* offers a narratively ordered topography, not a reconstructable working mnemonic algorithm.

## 2. *De gli eroici furori*: verbal imprese and affective images

### Form

The work has two parts, each of five dialogues. It combines dialogue, lyric poetry, philosophical commentary, myths, and descriptions of imprese. It is dedicated to Philip Sidney.

The relevant sequence begins in part I, dialogue V and continues into part II. Contrary to the catalogue’s “about fifty emblems,” the commonly accepted count is **twenty-eight described imprese**. No pictures of those imprese are printed in the 1585 edition: the “body” of each device is verbalized. [Canone’s edition and census information](https://www.iliesi.cnr.it/prin/ipertesto_testi_prinILIESI.php?pagina=Ipertesto&scheda=Scheda_Furori.html); [description of the twenty-eight](https://it.wikipedia.org/wiki/De_gli_eroici_furori).

### Bruno’s instructions for reading the sequence

**[Documented text]** Cicada and Tansillo examine warriors carrying devices of their passions and fortunes. In the first device:

> “Mira come questi guerrieri portano l’imprese de gli affetti e fortune loro… Basta che attendiamo al senso de le imprese, al significato di quel che vi sta scritto, al motto che accompagna la figura, ed al poema che compisce la figura dichiarandone il senso.”

“See how these warriors carry the devices of their affections and fortunes… Let it suffice that we attend to the meaning of the devices, to what is written there, to the motto accompanying the figure, and to the poem that completes the figure by explaining its meaning.”

The components are consequently:

1. a verbally described image or *corpo*;
2. a motto or *lemma*, often Latin;
3. a poem;
4. dialogical commentary.

This four-part construction modifies the usual emblem/impresa form. [English text of the passage](https://www2.esotericarchives.com/bruno/furori.htm).

### Worked example: the first shield

**[Documented text]**

> “Porta un scudo diviso in quattro colori; nel cimiero è dipinta una fiamma sotto una testa di bronzo, da’ cui forami esce con gran forza un fumoso vento; e sopra vi sono scritte queste parole: *At regna senserunt tria*.”

“He carries a shield divided into four colors; upon its crest is painted a flame beneath a bronze head, from whose openings a smoky wind issues with great force; above are written the words *At regna senserunt tria*—‘But three realms felt it.’”

Tansillo interprets the flame, globe, water, vapor, and pressure. The image is not a simple rebus for one word. It is a compressed model of affective conflict extending through natural philosophy, psychology, and poetic experience.

### Other characteristic image-complexes

The twenty-eight devices draw on:

- eyes, stars, and reflected light;
- hearts and arrows;
- flames, furnaces, smoke, water, and tears;
- wheels, circles, and contraries;
- shields, helmets, warriors, and wounds;
- moth-like attraction to destructive light;
- the divided self;
- the hunter Actaeon, his dogs, and Diana;
- the nine blind men;
- Circe;
- Amphitrite, the all-containing sea and image of the One manifested in nature.

### Actaeon and Amphitrite

**[Documented text]** Actaeon’s dogs become thoughts directed toward divine objects. They consume the lower self, freeing the intellect from sense and bodily confinement:

> “gli cani, pensieri de cose divine, divorano questo Atteone…”

“the dogs, thoughts of divine things, devour this Actaeon…”

The transformed hunter sees:

> “l’Anfitrite, fonte de tutti numeri, de tutte specie, de tutte raggioni, che è la monade, vera essenza de l’essere de tutti…”

“Amphitrite, source of all numbers, all species and all forms, the monad, the true essence of the being of all things…”

The movement is from multiplicity toward unity, though the absolute source is seen in its natural manifestation rather than comprehended in itself.

### Is this a memory system?

**[Yates’s carefully qualified interpretation]**

> “we begin to see the love emblems of the *Eroici furori* in a new context, not, of course, as a memory system, but as traces of the memory methods in a literary work.”

(*Art of Memory*, p. 314.)

Thus even Yates expressly denies that the *Furori* is itself a mnemonic system.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** Its relationship to memory is more plausibly located in:

- sequential visualization;
- affect as an instrument of fixation;
- conversion of arguments into memorable image-complexes;
- the dynamic interaction of picture, motto, verse, and exegesis;
- the elevation of imagination from sensible multiplicity toward intellectual unity.

Bolzoni places this procedure within Renaissance “literary memory,” especially Bruno’s transformation of Petrarchan and Ariostan material. Her work confirms Yates’s broad insight that literary form can internalize mnemonic operations, while avoiding the claim that every ordered image is a technical locus. [Bolzoni, “Images of Literary Memory”](https://ricerca.sns.it/handle/11384/4283); [“Note su Bruno e Ariosto”](https://ricerca.sns.it/retrieve/e3aacdfd-fe31-4c98-e053-3705fe0acb7e/Note_bruno_ariosto.pdf).

### “Contractions”

**[Documented text]** Near the conclusion Bruno speaks of contractions or concentrations of the soul’s powers. Yates connects these to the *Sigillus sigillorum* and other mnemonic writings in which *contractio* denotes an inward concentration of attention and affect.

**[Qualification]** Contraction is a psychological and contemplative principle broader than memory storage. It helps explain how an image may transform the subject, but it does not supply a list of loci or a retrieval routine.

## 3. *Spaccio de la bestia trionfante*: the constellational reform

### Correct date

The item-specific brief dates the *Spaccio* to 1585. That is incorrect.

**[Documented bibliographical fact]** The first edition belongs to **1584**, printed in London by John Charlewood under a false “Paris” imprint. It precedes the *Furori* of 1585. The dedication to Sidney is genuine. [Treccani](https://www.treccani.it/enciclopedia/giordano-bruno_%28Enciclopedia-machiavelliana%29/); [Archivio Giordano Bruno](https://www.iliesi.cnr.it/AGB/index_bruno.php).

### Bruno’s declared plan

The *Spaccio* has an explanatory epistle and three dialogues, each divided into three parts. Sofia recounts to Saulino the council of the gods at which Jupiter reforms the sky.

**[Documented text]**

> “questo mondo… prima diviso come in tante sfere, e poi distinto in circa quarant’otto immagini… viene ad essere principio e soggetto del nostro lavoro.”

“This world… first divided into so many spheres and then distinguished into about forty-eight images… becomes the principle and subject of our work.”

Bruno states that Jupiter:

> “avea colmo di tante bestie, come di tanti vizii, il cielo, secondo la forma di quarant’otto famose imagini”

“had filled heaven with as many beasts as vices, according to the form of forty-eight famous images,”

and now intends to banish them:

> “ed in quelle medesime stanze facendo succedere le già tanto tempo bandite e tanto indegnamente disperse virtudi.”

“causing the virtues, so long banished and unworthily dispersed, to succeed them in those same stations.”

Most explicitly, Bruno says the dialogue establishes:

> “in numero e certo ordine tutte le prime forme de la moralità”

“in number and fixed order all the primary forms of morality.”

[Italian text](https://www.intratext.com/IXT/ITA1492/_P1.HTM); [complete PDF transcription](https://www.ousia.it/SitoOusia/SitoOusia/TestiDiFilosofia/TestiPDF/Bruno/SpaccioDeLaBestiaTrionfante.pdf).

### Places, images, order

- **Places:** the traditional forty-eight constellation regions of the ancient celestial sphere.
- **Old images:** Ursa Major and Minor, Draco, Cepheus, Cassiopeia, Andromeda, Perseus, Pegasus, the zodiacal animals, Orion, Eridanus, the southern constellations, and so forth.
- **Material removed:** named vices, corrupt customs, false religious attitudes, and undesirable passions.
- **Material installed:** Truth, Prudence, Wisdom, Law, Judgment, Fortitude, Magnanimity, Diligence, Labour, Memory, and other virtues.
- **Order:** broadly the inherited astronomical/mythographical ordering associated with Ptolemy and especially the illustrated *Poetica astronomica* attributed to Hyginus.
- **Narrative operator:** Jupiter deliberates; Momus provokes and criticizes; Mercury executes or announces assignments; Sofia reports the celestial proceedings to Saulino.
- **Retrieval:** a reader proceeds through the sky in the dialogue’s order, recalling at each constellation the expelled vice, replacement virtue, dispute, myth, and ethical-political application.

### Worked example: Ursa Major

**[Documented text]**

> “Dove dunque era l’Orsa, per ragion del luogo, per esser parte più eminente del cielo, si prepone la Verità…”

“Where the Bear was, because of the place, as the most eminent part of heaven, Truth is placed first…”

Truth’s companions or implications include Entity, Necessity, Goodness, Principle, Middle, End, and Perfection. Here the dignity of the celestial location helps determine the dignity of the new occupant.

### Worked example: Lyra

**[Documented text]** In the place of Lyra Bruno installs Memory, Mnemosyne, and the nine Muses. This is the dialogue’s most explicit internal acknowledgment of memory as a cosmic and cultural virtue.

**[Correction]** Memory is one virtue among the reformed principles, not proof by itself that the whole work was designed for rote retrieval.

### Worked example: Orion

The Orion episode attacks “imposture,” useless cleverness, miracle claims, and the inversion of nature and virtue. Orion is told he may perform wonders on earth, including walking on water.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** The passage is widely understood as an anticlerical and anti-Christian satire, especially an attack upon the theology of miraculous faith detached from natural law and ethical works.

**[Disputed nuance]** Whether Orion simply “equals Christ” is too reductive: the figure condenses Christological polemic, imposture, pedantry, and abused miraculous authority.

### Hyginus, Ptolemy, and Romberch

**[Documented source tradition]**

- Ptolemy’s *Almagest* catalogued stars under forty-eight constellations.
- Pseudo-Hyginus’s *Poetica astronomica* supplied an influential ordered mythography of the constellation figures.
- Johannes Romberch’s *Congestorium artificiose memorie* (first edition 1520; Venice edition 1533) recommended celestial or star orders and referred readers to Hyginus when they needed an extended series of constellation images.

Yates observes that Romberch does not say exactly what material should be placed upon those constellations. Her suggestion that a preacher might arrange virtues and vices there is explicitly conjectural. [Yates’s discussion](https://resources.warburg.sas.ac.uk/mnemosyne/fq/yates_memory.pdf); [Library of Congress record for Romberch](https://www.loc.gov/item/10019844/).

**[Yates’s interpretation]**

> “he has taken a system from the memory text-books, the use of Hyginus on the order of the constellations as a memory order, and has ‘occultised’ it into a ‘Seal’ of his own.”

(*Art of Memory*, p. 317.)

**[Post-Yates confirmation and correction]** Leo Catana demonstrated in detail that Hyginus is indeed a major source of the *Spaccio*’s constellation mythology. But Catana situates it within a literary lineage: Lucian’s *Assembly/Parliament of the Gods*, followed by Renaissance moral adaptations in Alberti and Niccolò Franco. Thus Hyginus is confirmed; exclusive derivation through Romberch’s mnemonic handbook is not. [Catana, “Bruno’s *Spaccio* and Hyginus’ *Poetica astronomica*”](https://researchprofiles.ku.dk/en/publications/leo-catana-brunos-spaccio-and-hyginus-poetica-astronomica/).

### Is it a “Seal”?

**[Documented text]** Bruno does not title the *Spaccio* a seal and does not tell the reader to operate it as one of the thirty seals.

**[Yates’s interpretation]** “Seal” denotes a Brunian transformation of inherited order into an inward, philosophically charged imaginal structure.

**[Best reconstruction]** The *Spaccio* is much closer to an operable mnemonic arrangement than either the *Cena* or *Furori*: it has stable places, ordered images, substitutions, and paired ethical matter. Yet it remains an allegorical dialogue rather than a technical set of user instructions.

## Diagrams, wheels, tables, and plates

**[Negative finding]**

- The *Cena* supplies no mnemonic plan or map of London.
- The *Spaccio* supplies no engraved celestial chart, wheel, or table pairing constellations with virtues.
- The *Furori* supplies no printed emblem pictures; it describes them in words.
- No manuscript variants preserve a lost set of authorially approved illustrations.
- Modern reconstructions that map the journey or diagram the constellation replacements are editorial visualizations, not surviving Brunian plates.

Bruno’s actual combinatorial wheels and mnemonic diagrams occur in his technical Latin works. Conflating those objects with the three dialogues produces a modern hybrid.

## Yates’s account and scholarship since

## What Yates established

Yates’s chapter does three important things:

1. It refuses to isolate Bruno’s famous vernacular dialogues from the difficult mnemonic writings.
2. It identifies three distinct literary transformations of spatial-image organization.
3. It treats artificial memory as a mode of composition, imagination, and ethical transformation, not merely a trick for verbatim recall.

Her three escalating claims are:

- *Cena*: memory places become dramatic literature.
- *Furori*: mnemonic images leave traces in love-emblem poetry, though the work is not a memory system.
- *Spaccio*: an inherited celestial memory order becomes an “occult” Brunian seal.

The complete chapter is available through the Warburg’s Yates resource. [Chapter XIV](https://resources.warburg.sas.ac.uk/mnemosyne/fq/yates_memory.pdf).

## What later work has confirmed

### Lina Bolzoni

Bolzoni confirms the importance of literary memory: texts, images, quotations, characters, and inherited poetry can be broken apart and recomposed into new imaginal structures. Her work on Bruno and Ariosto shows that literary reminiscence is neither passive influence nor necessarily a formal memory palace. It is an active practice of visual and verbal recombination.

### Stephen Clucas

Clucas gives the strongest recent defense of a qualified Yatesian connection. The technical works and dialogues share:

- a metaphysics of unity and multiplicity;
- imagination as mediator;
- affective discipline;
- ethical self-transformation;
- ascent from sensible species toward intellectual organization.

But Clucas rejects a choice between Sturlese’s purely semiotic machine and Yates’s astral talisman. Bruno’s images can be logically and ethically operative without every one being an astrological talisman.

### Leo Catana

Catana confirms Hyginus as a substantial source for the *Spaccio* while widening the genealogy to Lucianic moral satire. This materially corrects Yates’s near-exclusive emphasis on Romberch and memory manuals.

### Aquilecchia, Canone, Ciliberto, Granada, Ordine

Modern philology reconstructs the dialogue cycle, its language, intertexts, and philosophical sequence far more securely than was possible in 1966. It emphasizes the unity of Bruno’s *nova filosofia*, ethical reform, anti-pedantry, natural religion, and theory of knowledge.

## What has been corrected or rejected

### Rita Sturlese

Sturlese’s 1991 critical edition and 1992 study of *De umbris idearum* demonstrated that Yates had misplaced planetary images when reconstructing Bruno’s wheel. That error affected her claim that the mechanism directly embodied astral relations. Sturlese instead reconstructed a rigorous system for converting syllables or words into images.

The Warburg project summarizes the decisive correction: Yates’s talismanic interpretation rested partly on a misplacement of the planetary images. [Warburg, “Yates and Bruno’s Mnemonics”](https://warburg.sas.ac.uk/research-fellowships/research-projects/archived-research-projects/giordano-bruno/yates-brunos-mnemonics).

This correction directly concerns *De umbris*, not the London-dialogue chapter. Its consequence is methodological: one cannot infer that every Brunian image, constellation, or god is a talisman intended to draw down a planetary force.

### Michele Ciliberto

Ciliberto criticizes the reduction of Bruno to a single “Hermetic” key and insists upon chronological development and the plurality of Bruno’s philosophical registers. Magic and Hermeticism must not simply be identified. [Ciliberto, “Bruno nel XX secolo”](https://ricerca.sns.it/handle/11384/4117).

### Hilary Gatti

Gatti accepts that Yates reopened Bruno’s mnemonic and magical contexts, but emphasizes Bruno’s natural philosophy, mathematics, civic ethics, and relation to early modern science. She also notes that Sturlese’s operational correction does not exhaust the metaphysical theory that Bruno gives his mnemonic practice.

### Mary Carruthers, Paolo Rossi, Jocelyn Penny Small

These authors do not directly adjudicate every London-dialogue claim, but alter its framework:

- Carruthers treats medieval memoria as an inventive and compositional matrix, not a passive storehouse.
- Rossi situates memory arts within logic, method, and universal-language projects.
- Small reconstructs the material and cognitive practices behind ancient memory metaphors.
- Together they support the possibility of “literary memory” while discouraging the assumption that every vivid literary sequence is a cryptographic mnemonic apparatus.

### Lull scholarship: Anthony Bonner and Alexander Fidora

Modern Lull scholarship distinguishes Ramon Llull’s *ars*—a demonstrative, relational and missionary method—from ordinary artificial memory. Bruno adapted Lullian letters, figures, combinations, and relational thinking, but his mnemonic wheels are not simply Llull’s system continued unchanged. This weakens any linear “Lull invented the memory wheel; Bruno inherited it” story.

### Ong and Ramus

Walter J. Ong showed that Ramist method reorganized knowledge through dichotomous spatial diagrams and printed outlines, often in tension with image-rich mnemonic practice. Sidney’s association with Ramist intellectual circles supplies relevant context, but no evidence proves that Sidney adopted Bruno’s celestial system or that the *Furori* was written as his mnemonic textbook.

## Yates’s later position

Yates did not publish a comprehensive retraction of chapter XIV. Her later essays continued to connect Bruno’s memory, imagination, Hermeticism, and religious reform. Her formulations often remained deliberately tentative—“could,” “traces,” “I think justifiable”—a qualification frequently lost in popular summaries.

## History and context

### Classical rhetoric

The inherited art of memory rests on *loci* and *imagines*: an ordered series of places and striking images deposited in them. Cicero, Quintilian, and the anonymous *Rhetorica ad Herennium* transmitted the canonical story of Simonides and rules for artificial memory.

The notorious lawsuit image in *Ad Herennium*—a poisoned victim in bed, the accused holding a cup, tablets, and ram’s testicles—belongs to the general tradition, not to these dialogues. Bruno neither quotes nor reenacts that worked example here.

### Scholastic psychology

Medieval Aristotelian psychology held that intellectual cognition ordinarily begins from sensible forms or phantasms. Albert the Great and Thomas Aquinas incorporated artificial memory into prudence. This helps explain why ordered, affectively forceful images could be ethical instruments without being magical talismans.

### Dominican memory culture

Bruno and Romberch were Dominicans. Dominican preaching, confession, moral taxonomy, and pedagogical order provided fertile ground for systems of virtues and vices. Yet no institutional record shows the *Spaccio* being taught in a Dominican school—it is fiercely unorthodox and was produced after Bruno had left the order.

### Lullism and combinatorics

Llull’s lettered figures and relational combinations entered late-medieval and Renaissance logic. Bruno combined these with classical artificial memory, astronomical images, and his metaphysics of nature. In the technical works, wheels can generate or encode combinations; in the dialogues, combination becomes literary juxtaposition and transformation.

### Hermeticism and natural magic

The *Asclepius* passage on Egyptian statues animated by divine or demonic powers strongly influenced Bruno’s account of ancient Egyptian religion. The *Spaccio* contains gods perceived inwardly as statues or pictures and advocates a restoration of natural justice.

**[Yates’s interpretation]** This makes the constellation reform “Egyptian” and magical.

**[Qualification]** The dialogue’s use of living gods, statues, and celestial images is real; whether the reader is expected to use them talismanically is not stated.

### Emblem and impresa culture

The *Furori* belongs to the sixteenth-century culture of imprese: image-body, motto, and interpretive discourse. Bruno’s distinctive innovation is to withhold the actual picture and make dialogue and poem generate it mentally. That intensifies visualization but also foregrounds semantic instability.

### Courts and patronage

- Michel de Castelnau housed Bruno and received the dedication of the *Cena*.
- Philip Sidney received the *Spaccio* and *Furori*.
- Fulke Greville appears as host in the *Cena*.
- John Florio belongs to the ambassadorial household and participates in the journey.
- John Charlewood printed the dialogues clandestinely.
- Henry III of France earlier patronized Bruno and questioned him about whether his memory was natural or magical.

## People

### Bruno as practitioner

**[Documented]** Bruno taught memory publicly and privately, published multiple mnemonic works, and represented the art as joining memory, discovery, and intellectual ordering.

### Henry III

According to Bruno’s Venetian testimony, Henry III summoned him to determine whether his extraordinary memory was natural or magical. Bruno said it was acquired “by science” and presented the king with a memory book. The general episode is credible because Bruno himself reported it under examination; embellishments about spectacular court demonstrations are less secure.

### Giovanni Francesco Mocenigo: patron turned accuser

Mocenigo invited Bruno to Venice to learn “the secrets of memory and the others that he professes.” Dissatisfied with the instruction and fearing Bruno’s departure, he confined him and denounced him.

His first denunciation accused Bruno of grave theological errors and of being a magician:

> “ch’era un mago”

“that he was a magician.”

[Text of the denunciation](https://www.matdid.it/letture/giordano-bruno-denuncia-e-sentenza).

**[Documented]** Memory instruction caused the relationship and occasioned Bruno’s physical seizure.

**[Correction]** Bruno was not condemned merely for teaching a memory palace. The proceedings concerned Trinity, Incarnation, Eucharist, the soul, eternity and plurality of worlds, religious authority, magic, and other propositions. The final Roman trial record is incomplete, so any one-cause explanation is unsound.

### Sidney and Greville

Their presence anchors Bruno within elite Elizabethan literary and political culture.

**[Disputed]** There is no documentary evidence that Sidney became Bruno’s mnemonic pupil.

**[Disputed]** Suggestions that Greville’s or Sidney’s circle transmitted a Brunian constellation system directly to Shakespeare remain conjectural.

### Torquato and Nundinio

These names represent Oxford opponents in the *Cena*. Identification with particular historical doctors has been attempted but remains uncertain. They function literarily as pedantic Aristotelians whether or not they are portraits.

### The requested anecdotes

No reliable connection has been found between these three dialogues and:

- “the auctioneer”;
- “the professor’s party”;
- “the student who lost his memory.”

These anecdotes belong to other episodes or systems in the wider memory-art tradition. Importing them here would create a false provenance.

“The accused magician,” however, securely applies to Bruno through Mocenigo’s denunciation.

## The magic question

### Was the system magical?

The answer depends upon what “system” means.

1. **The narrative structures themselves:** not explicitly magical operations.
2. **Bruno’s wider philosophy of imagination:** closely connected with natural magic.
3. **Yates’s reconstruction:** an occult or Hermetic memory extending toward talismanic use.
4. **Sturlese’s operational reconstruction:** a regulated semiotic mechanism, not a set of astral talismans.
5. **Clucas’s synthesis:** logical, mnemonic, ethical, metaphysical, and in a qualified Brunian sense magical.

### Statues and inward gods

**[Documented text]** The *Spaccio* presents reforming gods as inward powers and sometimes as statue-like or pictorial forms.

**[Yates’s interpretation]** These are “magically animated statues” comparable to the statues of the Hermetic *Asclepius*.

**[Qualification]** Literary animation and inner visualization do not prove ritual consecration, spirit invocation, or manufacture of talismans.

### Ars notoria

Bruno knew of the *Ars notoria*, the medieval ritual art promising rapid knowledge through prayers, figures, and spiritual assistance. In the *Sigillus sigillorum* he criticizes credulous practitioners who imagine themselves suddenly made wise by such means.

**[Correction]** Bruno’s artificial memory is not simply the *Ars notoria*. It demands trained imagination, ordering, and intellectual labor even when framed through magic.

### Heresy and consequences

Mocenigo conflated memory secrets, magic, and theological suspicion. But the surviving evidence does not show that any one of the three dialogues’ alleged mnemonic arrangements was entered as an independent capital charge.

**[Legend]** “Bruno was burned for the art of memory.”

**Evidence:** none sufficient.

**[Legend/modern simplification]** “Bruno was burned for believing the Earth goes around the Sun.”

**Evidence:** cosmology entered the proceedings, but the trial encompassed a much broader group of philosophical and theological propositions. [Stanford Encyclopedia, including trial bibliography](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2025/entries/bruno/).

## Afterlife

### Literature

Yates proposed that the *Spaccio*’s celestial rhetoric might illuminate Shakespearean language, including Berowne’s speech in *Love’s Labour’s Lost*.

**[Disputed]** Bruno and Shakespeare were simultaneously connected to London’s theatrical-literary environment, but no meeting, borrowing document, or annotated Shakespearean copy survives.

Borges’s “Funes the Memorious” is a profound modern meditation on unlimited recollection and the loss of abstraction. It belongs to the conceptual afterlife of memory, but no direct textual descent from these dialogues has been demonstrated.

Umberto Eco discussed combinatorial arts, Llull, Bruno, and universal languages. Eco’s genealogies concern the dream of a complete symbolic order; they do not establish that modern programming directly descends from the *Spaccio*.

### Visual and emblematic culture

The *Furori* participates directly in impresa culture and indirectly in the broader emblem-book tradition. Later illustrators may realize the verbal images, but those realizations are post-Brunian interpretations.

The constellation reform also belongs beside moralized mythography, celestial atlases, and the personification of virtues in court art. There is no documented fresco cycle copied from the *Spaccio*.

### Memory-palace revival and memory athletes

Modern memory athletes use ordered routes and striking images closely analogous to classical loci and images. The *Cena* can readily be converted into a modern route, and the *Spaccio* into a forty-eight-station celestial palace.

**[Modern invention]** Such reconstructions are useful adaptations, not evidence that Bruno instructed his London readers to use these dialogues that way.

### Computing and combinatorics

A defensible genealogy is:

> Llullian finite alphabets and rotating figures → Renaissance combinatorial adaptations, including Bruno → seventeenth-century projects of universal method and language → Leibniz’s *ars combinatoria* and *characteristica universalis*.

**[Documented intellectual descent]** Leibniz read and discussed Llullian combinatorics.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** Bruno belongs to the intervening history of symbolic combination.

**[Modern myth]** “Bruno invented the computer” or “his wheels were mechanical computers.” They are rule-governed symbolic devices and therefore meaningful ancestors by analogy, not programmable digital machines.

## Controversies and disputes

| Claim | Status | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| The three dialogues form one artificial-memory system | **Yatesian/catalogue construction** | No such collective system is named by Bruno |
| *Spaccio* dates from 1585 | **Incorrect** | First edition is London, 1584 |
| The *Furori* contains about fifty emblems | **Incorrect or inflated** | Twenty-eight described imprese are normally counted |
| The *Furori* contains printed emblem plates | **Incorrect** | Images are verbally described |
| The London journey is a deliberate memory palace | **Disputed interpretation** | Ordered real topography exists; mnemonic instructions do not |
| The *Cena* journey happened exactly as narrated | **Unproved** | Bruno’s later testimony places the supper at Castelnau’s house |
| The boat represents the Church | **Yates’s allegory** | No explicit Brunian gloss |
| Hyginus is a source of the *Spaccio* | **Strongly supported** | Detailed verbal and sequential correspondences; Catana |
| Hyginus reached Bruno only through Romberch | **Unproved** | Bruno could use Hyginus directly; Lucianic and mythographic sources also matter |
| The *Spaccio* is an occult “seal” | **Yates’s interpretive term** | Strong structural analogy, but no explicit designation |
| Constellation images were astral talismans | **Disputed** | Yates’s broader reconstruction weakened by Sturlese’s correction |
| The dialogues and mnemonic treatises are unrelated | **Overturned by recent work** | Bolzoni and Clucas demonstrate shared poetics, psychology, and metaphysics |
| Bruno’s memory art was purely mechanical | **Too narrow** | It has logical and encoding functions but also ethical and metaphysical purposes |
| Bruno was arrested or executed for memory teaching | **Legend/simplification** | Memory brought him to Mocenigo; heresy proceedings were much broader |
| Sidney was Bruno’s student | **Tradition/speculation** | Dedications and social proximity, but no record of instruction |
| Shakespeare learned the zodiacal system from Bruno | **Speculation** | Possible milieu, no documentary chain |
| The works preserve lost ancient Egyptian magic | **Renaissance belief/Yatesian emphasis** | Bruno valued Egyptian religion through late-antique Hermetica; historical Egyptian descent is false |
| Bruno invented modern memory sport | **Modern invention** | Athletes principally use the classical method of loci, often through modern manuals |

## Open questions

1. Was the *Cena*’s journey composed from an actual failed trip, several London experiences, or wholly literary topography?
2. Can the unnamed supper house be identified securely?
3. How much of the *Spaccio*’s constellation order comes directly from a particular illustrated Hyginus edition?
4. Did Bruno consult Romberch at this stage, or did Yates infer the connection from structural similarity?
5. Were the *Furori*’s twenty-eight devices ever intended for illustration?
6. Did contemporary readers mentally visualize the devices as stable pictures or experience them as deliberately shifting verbal constructions?
7. How precisely do Bruno’s “contractions” in the *Furori* relate to the exercises of the *Sigillus sigillorum*?
8. What did Sidney, Greville, Florio, and Castelnau actually understand or accept of Bruno’s mnemonic philosophy?
9. How should “magic” be divided among ritual magic, astral magic, natural magic, psychological self-fashioning, and polemical accusation?
10. What was in the lost portions of Bruno’s Roman trial file, and would they clarify the relation between mnemonic teaching and the formal charges?
11. Can digital collation of all surviving first-edition copies identify press variants relevant to image-language, punctuation, or sequence?
12. To what degree does the *Spaccio*’s ordered reform support practical recollection, and to what degree does it instead dramatize the impossibility of fixing moral meanings permanently in a universe of *vicissitudine*?
