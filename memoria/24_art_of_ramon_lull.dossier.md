# The Art of Ramon Lull: Research Dossier

## Basic identification

### Evidentiary labels used below

- **[TEXT]** Stated or directly instantiated in an authentic primary text.
- **[OBJECT]** Material evidence in a surviving manuscript or printing.
- **[DOCUMENT]** Archival, institutional, or contemporary documentary evidence.
- **[RECONSTRUCTION]** Modern scholarly reconstruction from texts or objects.
- **[YATES]** Yates’s interpretation, whether persuasive or not.
- **[RECEPTION]** A historically documented later interpretation.
- **[DISPUTED]** A proposition on which specialists disagree.
- **[PSEUDO]** A work or doctrine falsely attributed to Llull.
- **[TRADITION]** A report transmitted historically but not independently established.
- **[LEGEND]** A story contradicted by, or unsupported in, the surviving evidence.
- **[MODERN ANALOGY]** A retrospective comparison, not a claim of direct identity or descent.

The most important preliminary distinction is this:

> **[TEXT] Llull’s Art was primarily an inventive, demonstrative, and pedagogical method, not a classical loci-and-images mnemonic.**  
> **[YATES] It can nevertheless be treated as an “art of memory” insofar as its alphabet, definitions, figures, and procedures had to be memorized and then supplied an ordered mental apparatus for inquiry.**  
> **[CORRECTION] Yates’s strongest direct proof for calling it an artificial-memory system, the *Liber ad memoriam confirmandam*, is now classified as apocryphal.**

That correction substantially narrows—but does not eliminate—the memory interpretation.

## The system in detail

### 1. What the Art is for

The *Ars brevis* opens:

> **“Ratio, quare facimus istam Artem brevem, est, ut Ars magna facilius sciatur.”**  
> “The reason why we make this Short Art is so that the Great Art may be known more easily.”

It continues:

> **“Subiectum huius Artis est respondere de omnibus quaestionibus, supposito, quod sciatur, quid dicitur per nomen.”**  
> “The subject of this Art is to answer every question, provided that what is signified by the term is known.”

And, explaining the alphabet:

> **“Alphabetum ponimus in hac Arte, ut per ipsum possimus facere figuras et miscere principia et regulas ad investigandum veritatem.”**  
> “We place an alphabet in this Art so that through it we may make figures and mix principles and rules in order to investigate truth.”

Finally:

> **“Et ipsum quidem alphabetum cordetenus oportet sciri. Aliter enim artista Arte ista non poterit bene uti.”**  
> “The alphabet must indeed be known by heart; otherwise the artist will not be able to use this Art properly.”

All four passages are **[TEXT]**, *Ars brevis*, prologue and part I; critical text in Alois Madre, ed., *ROL* XII, *CCCM* 38 (1984), pp. 171–255; English translation in Anthony Bonner, *Selected Works of Ramon Llull*, vol. I, pp. 569–646. An accessible Latin transcription is available [here](https://www.ramonllull.net/sw_studies/l_br/t_ars.html).

Thus the Art is not a mechanism that produces knowledge from meaningless tokens. Its terms must first be understood; its principles and definitions must remain intact; and the generated combinations must be judged for applicability.

### 2. The thirteen parts of the final Art

The *Ars brevis* and *Ars generalis ultima* have the same architecture:

1. Alphabet
2. Four figures
3. Definitions
4. Questions and rules
5. Table
6. “Evacuation” of the Third Figure
7. Multiplication of the Fourth Figure
8. Mixture of principles and rules
9. Nine subjects
10. Application to the Hundred Forms
11. Questions
12. Habituation
13. Method of teaching

**[RECONSTRUCTION]** Anthony Bonner usefully regroups these as:

- foundations: alphabet, Figures I–II, definitions, rules, subjects;
- combinatorics: Figures III–IV, table, evacuation, multiplication;
- practice: mixture, applications, questions, habituation, teaching.

This avoids the common modern mistake of treating the rotating discs as the entire Art.

### 3. The alphabet: B through K

The letter J is omitted, as was normal in the medieval Latin alphabet. Each letter is an index into six coordinated columns:

| Letter | First Figure | Second Figure | Question/rule | Subject | Virtue | Vice |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| B | *bonitas*, goodness | *differentia*, difference | *utrum?*, whether? | God | justice | avarice |
| C | *magnitudo*, greatness | *concordantia*, concordance | *quid?*, what? | angel | prudence | gluttony |
| D | *aeternitas* or *duratio*, eternity/duration | *contrarietas*, contrariety | *de quo?*, of what? | heaven | fortitude | lust |
| E | *potestas*, power | *principium*, beginning | *quare?*, why? | man | temperance | pride |
| F | *sapientia*, wisdom | *medium*, middle | *quantum?*, how much? | imaginative | faith | accidie |
| G | *voluntas*, will | *finis*, end | *quale?*, what kind? | sensitive | hope | envy |
| H | *virtus*, virtue | *maioritas*, majority | *quando?*, when? | vegetative | charity | anger |
| I | *veritas*, truth | *aequalitas*, equality | *ubi?*, where? | elementative | patience | lying |
| K | *gloria*, glory | *minoritas*, minority | *quo modo? / cum quo?*, how?/with what? | instrumentative/artifice | pity | inconstancy |

**[TEXT]** The Latin begins:

> “B significat bonitatem, differentiam, utrum?, Deum, iustitiam et avaritiam. C significat magnitudinem, concordantiam, quid?, angelum, prudentiam et gulam…”

The many meanings per letter are deliberate:

> **“Per unam litteram, habentem multa significata, est intellectus magis generalis ad recipiendum multa significata.”**  
> “Through one letter having many significations, the intellect becomes more general in receiving many meanings.”

The letters are therefore indexes, not magical characters and not variables free of semantics.

#### An important terminological correction

**[YATES]** Yates presents B–K chiefly as the *Dignitates Dei*, divine Dignities descending through creation.

**[TEXT/RECONSTRUCTION]** This is substantially right for the Art’s theological foundation but imprecise for its final form. In the *Ars brevis* and *Ars generalis ultima*, Llull calls all eighteen terms in the First and Second Figures *principia*. The nine First-Figure terms are *dignitates* only when applied to God. “Absolute principles” and “relative principles,” now ubiquitous labels for the two figures, are not Llull’s terminology.

**[RECEPTION]** Bonner traces *principia absoluta* and *principia respectiva* to Bernard de Lavinheta’s sixteenth-century exposition; Agrippa changed these to *praedicata absoluta* and *respectiva*. Modern handbooks inherited the distinction.

### 4. The First Figure, traditionally “Figure A”

#### Diagram

**[TEXT/OBJECT]** The authentic basic design is:

- a circle;
- A conventionally at the center or used as the figure’s designation;
- B, C, D, E, F, G, H, I, K on the circumference;
- the nine principles represented by these letters;
- straight chords joining the letters pairwise in many manuscript and printed realizations.

The 36 unordered binary relations correspond to \( \binom{9}{2}=36 \).

Llull explains:

> **“Prima figura est de A. Ipsa figura continet in se novem principia … et novem litteras…”**

And:

> **“Ipsa quidem figura est circularis, eo quod subiectum mutatur in praedicatum, et e converso; ut cum dicitur ‘bonitas est magna’, ‘magnitudo est bona’.”**  
> “The figure is circular because subject changes into predicate and conversely, as when one says ‘goodness is great’ and ‘greatness is good.’”

The circle therefore represents convertibility and reciprocal predication, not merely divine perfection.

#### Is Figure A a wheel?

**[CORRECTION] No.** In the final Art, the First Figure is not meant to rotate.

The adjective-ring printed in some editions—*bonus, magnus*, and so forth, outside *bonitas, magnitudo*—is absent from the earliest manuscripts. Viola Tenge-Wolf and Bonner identify it as a sixteenth-century addition, already present in Lavinheta’s *Explanatio* of 1523. That additional ring encouraged later readers to imagine Figure A as rotary.

Yates’s Fig. 5 was reproduced from the 1617 Strasbourg printing and therefore displays a post-Llullian editorial state.

#### Is it a mystical diagram of the Godhead?

- **[TEXT]** Llull applies the principles to God and uses their mutual conversion to reason about divine unity, equality, and Trinitarian correlativity.
- **[YATES]** The figure is “a mystical figure” for contemplating the Names within the Godhead.
- **[CORRECTION]** In the final Art the figure is explicitly general, and its circle is explained through predication. It is not labelled “God,” and its principles apply at every ontological level.
- **Assessment:** Yates captures one major theological use but mistakes that application for the figure’s exhaustive formal definition.

### 5. The Second or T Figure

#### Diagram

**[TEXT/OBJECT]** A circle contains three colored, interlocking triangles surrounding T:

- difference — concordance — contrariety;
- beginning — middle — end;
- majority — equality — minority.

Manuscripts commonly distinguish the triangles by red, green, and yellow, though colors and their preservation vary.

Each angle has three species. Difference, for example, can be considered between:

- sensual and sensual things;
- sensual and intellectual things;
- intellectual and intellectual things.

The Second Figure is relational and investigative. It asks how the First-Figure principles and other subjects differ, agree, oppose, begin, mediate, end, exceed, equal, or fall short.

**[TEXT]** The eighteen First- and Second-Figure terms are on an increasingly equal ontological footing in the mature Art. Difference is not merely an external logical operator: Llull calls it a universal and substantial principle through which things really are distinct.

#### Yates’s “Trinitarian” reading

- **[TEXT]** Triads and correlatives are central to Llull’s theology.
- **[YATES]** The three triangles preserve a Trinitarian structure at every level.
- **[QUALIFICATION]** Their threefold form is compatible with Llull’s Trinitarian ontology, but the textual work of the figure is relational analysis. A triangle in Llull cannot automatically be decoded as a secret Trinity-symbol without examining its assigned terms.

### 6. The Third Figure

#### Diagram

The Third Figure is not a wheel but a triangular half-matrix.

It lists each unordered pair of B–K once:

> BC, BD, BE … BI, BK;  
> CD, CE … CI, CK;  
> DE … DK; and so forth.

There are 36 “chambers” or *camerae*.

**[OBJECT]** Some manuscripts draw this as a large triangle divided into compartments; others render it more tabularly. A fifteenth-century manuscript now at the Science History Institute places it at fol. 4.

#### What “evacuating” a chamber means

To “empty” or exhaust a chamber is to unfold its possible semantic contents.

For BC, each letter can draw meaning from the First or Second Figure:

- B = goodness or difference;
- C = greatness or concordance.

Llull obtains twelve directed propositions:

> “Bonitas est magna, bonitas est differens, bonitas est concordans. Magnitudo est bona, magnitudo est differens, magnitudo est concordans. Differentia est bona, differentia est magna, differentia est concordans. Concordantia est bona, concordantia est magna, concordantia est differens.”

Gloss:

- goodness is great, different, concordant;
- greatness is good, different, concordant;
- difference is good, great, concordant;
- concordance is good, great, different.

He then generates middle terms and questions. His sample syllogism is:

> **“Omne id, quod magnificatur a magnitudine, est magnum. Bonitas magnificatur a magnitudine; ergo bonitas est magna.”**  
> “Everything magnified by greatness is great. Goodness is magnified by greatness; therefore goodness is great.”

Each of the twelve propositions yields at least *utrum?* and *quid?* questions, producing twenty-four questions before the remaining rules and definitions are applied.

This is the clearest worked example in the final Art. Unlike the lawsuit image in the *Ad Herennium*, it is not a memorable narrative tableau. It demonstrates semantic expansion and argument construction.

### 7. The Fourth Figure: the genuine rotating device

#### Physical construction

**[TEXT/OBJECT]** The Fourth Figure consists of three concentric discs or circles, each bearing B–K. The outer disc remains fixed; the middle disc rotates upon it; the innermost rotates upon the middle. Manuscript volvelles could be assembled with thread or a pivot, although in surviving books discs are sometimes missing, flattened, redrawn, or printed as separate components.

Llull instructs:

> **“Circulus medius uolvitur super circulum exteriorem immobilem, ut, verbi gratia, C ponatur contra B. Circulus autem inferior uolvitur super circulum medium, ut, verbi gratia, D ponatur contra C.”**

Gloss:

> “The middle circle turns upon the fixed outer circle, so that, for example, C is placed opposite B. The inner circle turns upon the middle, so that, for example, D is placed opposite C.”

This produces chambers such as BCD, CDE, and so forth.

The figure gives:

- 252 displayed ordered alignments;
- reduced to 84 three-letter combinations when permutations such as BCD, CDB, and DBC are treated as equivalent.

Formally, 84 is \( \binom{9}{3} \).

#### The rule that prevents nonsense

The operator must not accept every mechanical result:

> **“De condicione quartae figurae est, quod intellectus applicet illas litteras ad propositum, quae videntur magis applicabiles proposito.”**  
> “A condition of the Fourth Figure is that the intellect apply to the matter those letters which appear more applicable to it.”

And:

> **“Respiciendo convenientiam … evitando inconvenientiam.”**  
> “[It proceeds] by attending to congruity … and avoiding incongruity.”

This is decisive. The volvelle is a prompt and search-space organizer. It does not mechanically certify truth.

### 8. The Table

The complete table in the *Ars generalis ultima* expands the 84 three-letter chambers into 84 columns of twenty combinations each. The *Ars brevis* compresses these into seven displayed columns in which the 84 are implicit.

The letter T in a table expression separates semantic domains:

- letters before T draw from the First Figure;
- letters after T draw from the Second Figure.

For example, a group such as **BCtD** can direct the operator to goodness, greatness, and contrariety. Upper- and lower-case notation often used by modern scholars—BCd—is a helpful editorial convention, not Llull’s standard notation.

Llull states:

> **“Tabula ista est subiectum, in quo intellectus se facit universalem.”**  
> “This table is a subject in which the intellect makes itself universal.”

It ascends toward prior and more general things, descends toward posterior and particular things, and connects columns with one another.

#### Example from the complete table

The first table column can generate twenty questions, including:

> “Whether goodness is so great that it is eternal?”  
> “Whether eternal goodness is different?”  
> “Whether goodness contains difference and concordance within itself?”  
> “What is great and eternal concordance?”  
> “Of what does the difference of concordance and eternity consist?”

The generated expression does not answer itself. The artist must bring in:

- definitions;
- the species of the relational principles;
- an appropriate subject;
- one or more rules;
- a valid middle term;
- the Art’s ontological assumptions.

### 9. Definitions

The eighteen principles have operational definitions rather than standard genus-and-difference definitions. Examples:

> **“Bonitas est ens, ratione cuius bonum agit bonum.”**  
> “Goodness is the being by reason of which the good does good.”

> **“Magnitudo est id, ratione cuius bonitas et duratio sunt magnae.”**  
> “Greatness is that by reason of which goodness and duration are great.”

> **“Differentia est id, ratione cuius bonitas, etc. sunt rationes inconfusae sive clarae.”**  
> “Difference is that by reason of which goodness and the others are distinct or clear principles.”

> **“Minoritas est ens circa nihil.”**  
> “Minority is being close to nothingness.”

**[RECONSTRUCTION]** Bonner stresses that these definitions are intensional and dynamic: they say what an essence does. They are not arbitrary dictionary glosses.

**[TEXT]** The artist may affirm or deny only in a way that leaves the definitions *illaesae*, “uninjured.” The definitions function rather like axioms or constraints.

### 10. Questions and rules

All particular questions are reduced to ten general questions:

1. Whether?
2. What?
3. Of what?
4. Why?
5. How much?
6. What kind?
7. When?
8. Where?
9. How?
10. With what?

These are not identical with Aristotle’s categories. They are interrogative instruments for investigating things; the categories are classes of being.

A typical rule:

> **“In principio intellectus supponat utramque partem esse possibilem.”**  
> “At the beginning, the intellect should suppose either side to be possible.”

It must not bind itself merely by belief:

> **“Non liget se cum credere, quod non est suus actus, sed intelligere.”**  
> “[The intellect] should not bind itself by believing, for its act is not belief but understanding.”

This does not make Llull a religious skeptic. It describes the formal opening of disputation before the Art’s definitions and reasoning settle the question.

### 11. The nine subjects and ascent/descent

The nine subjects are:

1. God
2. Angel
3. Heaven
4. Man
5. Imaginative power
6. Sensitive power
7. Vegetative power
8. Elementative nature
9. Instrumentative/artifice

The same principles are applied analogically at each level. Thus B may denote:

- divine goodness in God;
- an angel’s goodness;
- celestial goodness;
- human goodness;
- goodness in imagination;
- goodness in a lion;
- goodness in a plant;
- goodness in fire;
- goodness in a virtue, art, or manufactured instrument.

#### Retrieval

There is no prescribed mental walk through a building. Retrieval follows a conceptual address:

> letter → column/signification → figure or table chamber → rule → subject → definition → argument.

If the question concerns celestial goodness, the operator selects:

- B in the First-Figure column;
- D in the subject column, meaning heaven;
- the relevant Second-Figure relation;
- one or more interrogative rules.

This is closer to multidimensional indexing than to a room filled with images.

#### The ladder image

The 1512 Valencia printing of the *Liber de ascensu et descensu intellectus* contains a woodcut in which *Intellectus*, carrying or using a figure, ascends a ladder whose grades are represented by elemental or instrumental things, plant, animal, man, heaven, angel, and God; at the summit stands the House of Wisdom. The descent reverses the analytic movement, applying universals to particulars.

**[YATES]** This is her Fig. 4, pp. 179–80.

**[QUALIFICATION]** The woodcut is early sixteenth-century reception evidence, not an authorial drawing from 1305.

### 12. Circle, triangle, and square

Yates summarizes:

> “The square is the elements; the circle, the heavens; and the triangle, the divinity.”

This is **[YATES]**, based especially on her earlier study of Llull’s elemental theory.

A more exact account is:

- **Circle:** convertibility, circulation, completeness, and in cosmological diagrams the circular heavens.
- **Triangle:** ternary relations, correlatives, and structures that could support Trinitarian demonstration.
- **Square:** elemental qualities and their relations—hot/cold, dry/moist; also a visual analogue used in explaining logical opposition.

**[TEXT/RECONSTRUCTION]** Llull repeatedly maps elemental relations onto logical and metaphysical problems. But the slogan should not be treated as a universal graphic dictionary in which every square is “matter,” every circle “heaven,” and every triangle “God.”

### 13. What serves as places and images?

#### Places

There are no *loci* in the strict *Ad Herennium* sense of architectural backgrounds occupied in a stable sequence.

Functional equivalents include:

- the fixed order B–K;
- the columns of the alphabet;
- chambers in Figures III–IV;
- table columns;
- levels of the subject ladder;
- parts of a tree—roots, trunk, branches, twigs, leaves, flowers, fruit;
- textual cross-references to a *locus* where a problem has already been resolved.

#### Images

There are no required emotionally striking human images of the classical mnemonic kind. Llull’s working signs are:

- letters;
- defined concepts;
- circles, triangles, squares;
- matrices;
- trees;
- ladders.

**[YATES]** “There is nothing corresponding to the images of the classical art in Lullism as taught by Lull himself.”

That remains broadly correct for the formal machinery. It needs two qualifications:

1. Llull freely uses allegory, examples, dialogues, and visual metaphors elsewhere.
2. The *Arbor scientiae* includes the richly narrative *Arbor exemplificalis*. Its lack of classical mnemonic images does not mean Llull lacked imagination.

#### Rules for places and images

**[ABSENCE OF EVIDENCE]** Authentic Llull gives no equivalent to the classical rules that places be well lit, moderately spaced, distinct, neither crowded nor empty, and that images be extraordinary, bloody, comic, or emotionally violent.

The actual rules are:

- learn the alphabet, figures, and definitions by heart;
- preserve the definitions;
- select meanings relevant to the proposed subject;
- seek congruity and reject incongruity;
- practice until the procedure becomes a habit;
- reason rather than merely turn the discs;
- employ intellectual subtlety, reasoning skill, and right intention.

### 14. The *Arbor scientiae*

Written at Rome from September 1295 to April 1296, the *Arbor scientiae* reorganizes Llull’s world-picture as sixteen trees:

1. Elemental
2. Vegetal
3. Sensual
4. Imaginal
5. Human
6. Moral
7. Imperial
8. Apostolic
9. Celestial
10. Angelic
11. Eviternal
12. Maternal
13. Christological
14. Divine
15. Exemplifical
16. Quaestional

Each principal tree has:

- roots;
- trunk;
- branches;
- twigs;
- leaves;
- flowers;
- fruit.

The roots generally comprise the Art’s eighteen principles. Higher botanical parts express increasingly specific ontological structures, operations, powers, objects, acts, and questions.

**[YATES]** The trees form “a kind of place system.”

**[RECONSTRUCTION]** That is suggestive but insufficient. Bonner shows that the work is not mainly a classified data store. It presents a network of corresponding structures in reality. Its aim is to show *modus essendi*, how things exist, while the formal Art provides *modus cognoscendi*, how the intellect knows them.

The *Arbor quaestionalis* occupies approximately 41 percent of the critical text and applies thousands of questions to material supplied by the preceding trees. The *Arbor exemplificalis* adds narratives and comparisons. Together they form about half the work.

The tree is therefore simultaneously:

- an encyclopedic macrostructure;
- a visual finding aid;
- an ontological model;
- an engine for generating examples and questions.

It is not simply a medieval memory palace drawn as a plant.

### 15. Astronomy, astrology, and medicine

The *Tractatus novus de astronomia* was written in Paris in 1297. Its prologue says that many desire to know the natures and secrets of celestial bodies, but astrological judgments are difficult and often false; Llull therefore applies his General Art, suitable *ad omnes scientias investigare et declarare*, to celestial inquiry.

Its surviving witnesses include:

- Palma, Biblioteca Pública, MS 1053, fols. 25r onward;
- a later Palma MS 1054;
- Paris, BnF, MS lat. 17827, fols. 2–67.

#### What it does

It combines:

- twelve zodiacal signs;
- seven planets;
- elemental qualities;
- the Art’s principles and relations;
- rules for evaluating celestial mixtures and judgments.

#### Was this “benevolent astral science”?

- **[TEXT]** Llull did practice a reformed astrology and sought to correct unreliable judicial judgments.
- **[YATES]** On the *coelum* level the Art becomes a “benevolent astral science” and astral medicine.
- **[QUALIFICATION]** “Benevolent” is Yates’s characterization. Llull did not reject all celestial influence, but he resisted fatalism and predictions incompatible with divine providence and human freedom.
- **[DISPUTED]** Yates’s suggestion that this medicine “may conceivably have influenced Ficino” remains speculative.
- **[RECEPTION]** Bruno later worked from the authentic *Liber de regionibus sanitatis et infirmitatis* in his *Medicina Lulliana* and accused Paracelsus of borrowing from Llull. Bruno’s claim is polemical testimony, not proof of Paracelsus’s dependence.

### 2. A crucial pseudo-Llullian source

#### *Liber ad memoriam confirmandam*

Yates called this:

> “the nearest thing to an actual ‘memory treatise’ by Lull”

and asserted that it was “perfectly authentic.”

That conclusion is obsolete.

- **[PSEUDO/CURRENT CATALOGUING]** Subsequent Llull scholarship classifies the work as apocryphal.
- Its manuscripts are late:
  - Munich, Clm 10593, fols. 1–3v and another copy near fols. 218–21;
  - Vatican, Vat. lat. 5347, fols. 68–74;
  - Milan, Ambrosiana I 153 inf., fols. 35r–41r;
  - Paris, BnF lat. 17829, fols. 437–44. Earlier scholarship repeatedly misprinted this shelfmark as 17820 or 17839.
- Paolo Rossi printed a provisional edition in *Clavis universalis* (1960), pp. 261–70.
- The work cites a *Liber septem planetarum* in problematic ways and survives in a post-Llullian milieu where his combinatory Art was being assimilated to artificial memory.
- **[RECONSTRUCTION]** It is evidence for early Lullist reception, not secure evidence for Llull’s own theory.

Yates also emended its colophon from the unanimous late reading *monasterio sancti Dominici* to *Sancti Domnini*, because authentic Pisan works locate Llull at the Cistercian house of San Donnino. That emendation may reconstruct Llull’s historical residence correctly but cannot authenticate the treatise.

### 3. The *Breviculum*

Thomas Le Myésier, Llull’s Parisian disciple, compiled the *Breviculum ex artibus Raimundi Lulli electum* after 1321.

- Shelfmark: Karlsruhe, Badische Landesbibliothek, Cod. St. Peter perg. 92.
- Material: illuminated parchment.
- Companion: Le Myésier’s larger *Electorium*, Paris, BnF lat. 15450; the intermediate compilation is lost.
- Plate 10 in Yates shows Llull with ladders of his Art.
- **[OBJECT/RECEPTION]** The miniature cycle is extraordinarily early and close to a disciple, but it remains Le Myésier’s interpretive presentation, not Llull’s autograph iconography.

### 4. Early printed complexes

The most consequential printed anthology was Lazarus Zetzner’s *Raymundi Lullii Opera*:

- Strasbourg, 1598;
- reprinted 1609, 1617, 1651.

It combined authentic works with commentaries and spuria, including material by Agrippa and Bruno. The 1617 printing supplied Yates’s Figures 5 and 6.

**[RECEPTION]** This anthology did more than preserve Llull. It constructed the “Renaissance Llull”: combinator, cabalist, rhetorician, natural magician, and alleged alchemist.

## Yates’s account and the scholarship since

### 1. What Yates argued

Yates’s chapter VIII has two different subjects:

1. genuine medieval Llull;
2. the Renaissance fusion of Lullism with classical memory, Cabala, Hermeticism, rhetoric, and magic.

Her leading claims are:

> “As *intellectus*, it was an art of knowing or finding out truth; as *voluntas* it was an art of training the will towards loving truth; as *memoria*, it was an art of memory for remembering truth.”

> “Lull introduces movement into memory.”

> “The outer circle, inscribed B to K, is stationary and within it revolve circles similarly inscribed and concentric with it.”

> “The square is the elements; the circle, the heavens; and the triangle, the divinity.”

Her comparison with classical memory is systematic:

| Classical loci-and-images art | Llullian Art |
|---|---|
| Origin in rhetoric | Origin in metaphysics, theology, logic, and disputation |
| Architectural places | Alphabetic, tabular, arboreal, and cosmological order |
| Striking corporeal images | Abstract letters and definitions |
| Fixed sequence | Selective combination, including a movable volvelle |
| Stores a supplied speech or subject | Generates questions and arguments |
| Aristotle, Cicero, *Ad Herennium* | Divine attributes, relations, subjects, and rules |
| Particularly cultivated by Dominicans | Later strongly cultivated by Franciscans |

### 2. What remains persuasive

- **Confirmed:** the fundamental difference between Llull and classical image-mnemonics.
- **Confirmed:** B–K are polysemous indexes into ordered conceptual series.
- **Confirmed:** memorization of the alphabet, figures, and definitions is explicitly required.
- **Confirmed:** the Fourth Figure physically introduces controlled movement.
- **Confirmed:** the mature system applies common principles through a hierarchy of subjects.
- **Confirmed:** Renaissance writers fused Lullian combination with rhetoric, Cabala, and mnemonic imagery.
- **Confirmed:** pseudo-Llullian works profoundly altered Llull’s reputation.
- **Confirmed:** the Art’s afterlife reaches Bruno and Leibniz, though not as a simple linear transmission of unchanged doctrine.

### 3. What requires correction

#### “The figures … are revolving”

Only the Fourth Figure is necessarily rotary in the final ternary Art. The Third is a half-matrix; the First and Second are static conceptual diagrams. The apparent rotary outer ring in Figure A is a sixteenth-century addition.

#### Figure A as solely divine Dignities

In the mature Art its terms are general principles. They become divine Dignities when applied to God. The figure’s stated circularity concerns reciprocal predication.

#### The *Liber ad memoriam confirmandam*

Yates’s crucial primary witness is now considered apocryphal. This is the most consequential correction to her chapter.

#### “Franciscan art” versus “Dominican art”

- **Documented:** Llull sought Dominican support and later Lullism had important Franciscan patrons and defenders.
- **Overdrawn:** the opposition is not an institutional partition of medieval cognition. Llull was a layman and did not belong to the Franciscan order; Lullism circulated among secular masters, courts, universities, monks, friars, physicians, and humanists.

#### Direct Cabalistic origin

Yates explicitly marked this undecided but strongly favored it. Later work has refined the issue:

- J. M. Millás Vallicrosa proposed parallels.
- Moshe Idel reopened the comparison with ecstatic Cabala.
- Harvey Hames argues that Llull engaged Jewish ideas and used concepts intelligible within contemporary Jewish debate.
- Other specialists stress Arabic logical and theological sources and common Mediterranean habits of letter symbolism.
- No surviving text proves that Llull copied a specific Kabbalistic volvelle or letter-permutation manual.
- Pico’s 1487 *Apologia* is the first unequivocal association of Kabbalistic *ars combinandi* with *ars Raymundi*.
- **Conclusion:** intellectual contact is plausible; a direct genealogy from Abraham Abulafia or a particular Kabbalistic technique remains disputed.

#### Eriugena as principal source

Yates’s Eriugenian comparison was productive, particularly concerning divine Names as creative causes. It has not become an exclusive source explanation. Bonner, Fidora, Gayà, Ruiz Simon, and others place the Art within a broader conjunction of:

- Augustinian psychology;
- Christian divine-attribute theology;
- Arabic logic;
- scholastic disputation;
- elemental theory;
- missionary encounter;
- Llull’s own evolving formal experiments.

### 4. Carruthers, Small, Hajdu, and Sabbatino

Mary Carruthers’s *The Book of Memory* and *The Craft of Thought* enlarge the category of medieval *memoria*. Memory was not merely storage or spectacular imagery; it was trained composition, invention, ethical formation, and recollective movement through ordered material.

That strengthens the possibility of discussing Llull under *memoria* while weakening any need to force him into the *Ad Herennium* template.

Jocelyn Penny Small’s *Wax Tablets of the Mind* emphasizes the material, textual, and cultural technologies surrounding ancient memory. It supplies comparative caution: diagrams and written indexing are not automatically classical artificial memory.

Helga Hajdu’s earlier history remains important for medieval mnemonic treatises, but its broad developmental narrative has been superseded in many particulars by manuscript-centered work.

Sabbatino’s work on Renaissance rhetorical and poetic culture is relevant to the later fusion of memory, rhetoric, and encyclopedism, not a principal source for reconstructing the authentic Art.

### 5. Rossi

Paolo Rossi’s *Clavis universalis* preceded Yates and helped establish the line from mnemonic and combinatory arts to universal-language projects. His great strength is the reconstruction of Renaissance and seventeenth-century transformations.

His provisional printing of the *Liber ad memoriam confirmandam* made it available to Yates, but its limited, late manuscript base should have prompted stronger doubt. Rossi’s larger thesis—that Lullian combination migrated from theology into encyclopedism, rhetoric, invention, and universal language—remains foundational.

### 6. Bolzoni and Stagno

Lina Bolzoni’s work shifts attention from grand occult syntheses toward the actual interaction of:

- words and images;
- books and readers;
- preaching and composition;
- galleries, theatres, emblems, and textual organization.

For Camillo and later Renaissance memory culture, this provides a more materially grounded account than a single “Hermetic tradition.”

Stagno’s manuscript and iconographic work similarly warns that diagrams must be read in their particular codicological and editorial states. Applied to Llull, that warning exposes the difference between early manuscript figures and Lavinheta/Zetzner forms.

### 7. Bonner and Fidora

Anthony Bonner’s *The Art and Logic of Ramon Llull: A User’s Guide* is the principal modern correction to schematic accounts.

Bonner shows that:

- the Art developed rather than remaining the unchanged content of one revelation;
- the quaternary and ternary phases operate differently;
- definitions and question-answering matter more than the spectacle of the wheels;
- Figure A is not rotary;
- “absolute/relative principles” is Renaissance terminology;
- the Fourth Figure’s combinatorics are mathematically exact;
- mechanical generation is constrained by semantics and ontology;
- the final Art moved toward more conventional syllogistic discourse.

Alexander Fidora has especially clarified Llull’s relation to Arabic logic, medieval epistemology, and the development from logical compendia to the Art. This corrects narratives that derive the system almost entirely from Christian Platonism or Kabbalah.

### 8. Bruno scholarship

Yates correctly established the importance of Lullism for Bruno. Later scholarship by Rita Sturlese, Michele Ciliberto, Stephen Clucas, and Hilary Gatti has made the picture more precise:

- Bruno’s Lullism changes across Paris, Wittenberg, Prague, and later works.
- He does not merely copy Llull’s nine-letter Art.
- He expands alphabets, adds images, mythological and astrological contents, and integrates mnemonic psychology.
- His *De compendiosa architectura*, *De lampade combinatoria*, *De specierum scrutinio*, *Medicina Lulliana*, and related works must be distinguished from his image-memory works such as *De umbris idearum* and the *Sigilli*.
- Bruno’s “Lull” is already mediated by Renaissance editions and commentaries.
- Yates’s strongly Hermetic-magical synthesis remains influential but is not a substitute for work-by-work textual analysis.

The Warburg Institute provides downloadable nineteenth-century editions of Bruno’s Latin works, including the principal Lullian writings.

### 9. Ong and Ramus

Walter Ong’s *Ramus, Method, and the Decay of Dialogue* treats Ramist dichotomies as part of a transformation in pedagogy, print, and spatialized logic.

Yates proposed that Ramist bracket diagrams partly absorbed Lullian habits while rejecting mnemonic images. Later research finds specific overlaps but rejects a simple genealogy:

- trees and dichotomies had many medieval and humanist sources;
- Ramus did not simply turn Llullian wheels into brackets;
- the notion of Protestant “inner iconoclasm” as the decisive cause of Ramist anti-imagism has little direct evidence.

## History and context

### 1. Chronological development

#### Before the Art

Llull’s background included:

- courtly and troubadour culture in Majorca;
- Catalan, Latin, and Arabic intellectual worlds;
- Christian-Muslim-Jewish disputation;
- Augustinian accounts of intellect, memory, and will;
- elemental medicine and cosmology;
- scholastic logic;
- divine Names theology.

The traditional autobiographical source, the *Vita coaetanea*, reports visions of Christ crucified that initiated his conversion from courtly life.

#### Randa and the revelation

**[TRADITION, early biographical testimony]** On Mount Randa, probably c. 1274, Llull received an illumination concerning the form of the Art. Yates dated it about 1272.

**[LEGEND]** Later pictures and biographies sometimes present a complete set of wheels as instantaneously dictated by heaven. The surviving works instead show decades of formal revision.

#### Quaternary phase, c. 1274–89

Principal works include:

- *Ars compendiosa inveniendi veritatem*;
- *Ars demonstrativa*;
- related applications.

“Quaternary” refers to structural dependence on groups of four, especially elemental theory. These Arts employed more figures—sometimes twelve or sixteen—and a denser symbolic notation.

#### Ternary phase, c. 1290–1308

Beginning with the *Ars inventiva veritatis* and *Ars amativa boni*, Llull:

- reduced the apparatus to four central figures;
- privileged triadic structures;
- introduced operational definitions;
- refined the Table;
- developed ternary combination;
- increasingly integrated syllogistic reasoning.

The *Tabula generalis*, *Arbor scientiae*, *Logica nova*, *Liber de ascensu et descensu*, *Ars generalis ultima*, and *Ars brevis* belong to or culminate this phase.

#### 1308 and Paris

The *Ars brevis* served as a compact teaching instrument. In February 1310, forty masters and bachelors of the University of Paris signed approval of Llull’s lectures or teaching connected with the work. This is stronger evidence of actual pedagogical use than later stories about miraculous memory.

### 2. Institutions

#### Courts

Llull sought support from:

- James II of Majorca;
- James II of Aragon;
- the French monarchy;
- the papal court;
- Italian civic and mercantile elites.

His project required patronage for schools of Oriental languages, manuscripts, missions, and crusade proposals.

#### Miramar

A school at Miramar in Majorca was established with royal support to train missionaries in Arabic. Its history is better documented than claims that it functioned as a permanent “Lullian computer academy.”

#### Universities

Paris was crucial as both audience and obstacle. Llull revised his logical presentation partly in response to university criticism. Later Parisian Lullism included Le Myésier, Lefèvre d’Étaples, Lavinheta, and controversial teaching traditions.

#### Mendicant orders

- Dominicans supplied major models of scholastic disputation and classical-memory teaching but also Llull’s fiercest later opponent, Nicolau Eymerich.
- Franciscans became important transmitters and defenders, particularly in Majorca.
- Llull himself remained a layman, probably connected with Franciscan spirituality but not securely documented as a professed Franciscan tertiary.

### 3. Missionary purpose

Llull wanted arguments based on concepts that Christians, Jews, and Muslims could recognize—divine goodness, greatness, eternity, power, wisdom, and so forth. The Art would then show, by “necessary reasons,” that the Christian Trinity and Incarnation follow from these shared premises.

This was not neutral interfaith dialogue in the modern sense. Its intended end was conversion. Yet Llull often insisted on disputation through reasons rather than coercion.

### 4. What the Art descends from

Secure or strong contexts:

- Christian divine Names;
- Augustine’s triad of memory, intellect, and will;
- Aristotelian and Arabic logic;
- scholastic *quaestio* and disputation;
- four-element cosmology;
- Porphyrian and encyclopedic trees;
- mathematical enumeration and geometric diagrams.

Possible but disputed:

- Eriugenian primordial causes;
- Kabbalistic letter combination;
- Sufi meditation on divine Names;
- older cosmological *rotae*, including those associated with Bede;
- direct borrowing from particular Arabic *zāʾirja* devices.

No single-source derivation explains the system.

### 5. What descends from it

Documentable lines include:

- Thomas Le Myésier’s Lullian compilations;
- Lullian schools in Majorca, Barcelona, and Valencia;
- Nicholas of Cusa’s reading of Llull;
- Lefèvre d’Étaples and Parisian humanist Lullism;
- Lavinheta’s generalized Renaissance Art;
- Agrippa’s commentary;
- Bruno’s combinatory, mnemonic, and medical transformations;
- seventeenth-century encyclopedic method;
- Alsted and other system-builders;
- Leibniz’s engagement with the *ars combinatoria* tradition.

These are transmissions with transformations, not the uninterrupted operation of one machine.

## People

### Ramon Llull

Courtier, poet, missionary, philosopher, polemicist, and prolific vernacular and Latin author. The authoritative catalogue recognizes approximately 265 authentic works.

### The enslaved Arabic teacher

The *Vita coaetanea* reports that Llull acquired an enslaved Muslim to teach him Arabic. After years of instruction, the man allegedly blasphemed Christ; Llull struck him, the teacher attacked Llull with a sword, was imprisoned, and died by suicide.

- **[TRADITION]** The account is early but mediated and hagiographic.
- **[DOCUMENTED CORE]** Llull knew Arabic unusually well for a Latin Christian author.
- **[CAUTION]** The anecdote should not be embellished into a documented cooperative invention of the Art.

### Thomas Le Myésier

Parisian physician and disciple; compiler of the *Electorium* and *Breviculum*. He was among the most consequential early transmitters because he selected, reorganized, and illustrated Llull’s thought.

### Peter of Limoges

An early Parisian contact and manuscript owner connected with Llull’s intellectual reception.

### Nicolau Eymerich

Dominican inquisitor and principal anti-Lullist. He constructed a dossier of allegedly erroneous propositions and associated Lullism with threats to orthodoxy.

### Pere Daguí

Fifteenth-century Lullist teacher associated with Majorcan and Catalan institutional Lullism; an important target in renewed controversies about the Art’s theological legitimacy.

### Bernard de Lavinheta

Sixteenth-century lecturer and systematizer. His *Explanatio compendiosaque applicatio artis Raymundi Lulli* loosened the Art’s specifically Lullian ontology and helped convert it into a broadly applicable Renaissance method.

### Agrippa of Nettesheim

Commented on the *Ars brevis* and transmitted a Renaissance, partly occultized version. His authority helped spread terminology that was not Llull’s own.

### Giordano Bruno

Appropriated Llullian combination but joined it to larger alphabets, images, astrology, memory, rhetoric, and his own metaphysics. Bruno is a transformative successor, not a faithful operator of the 1308 Art.

### Nicholas of Cusa

Owned, excerpted, and studied Llullian manuscripts. His relation to Llull is demonstrable, though particular doctrinal borrowings require case-by-case proof.

### Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz

Read Llull through the Zetzner anthology and broader combinatory tradition. The youthful *Dissertatio de arte combinatoria* (1666) both honors and criticizes predecessors.

### Anecdotes requested in the general catalogue template

The “auctioneer,” “professor’s party,” and “student who lost his memory” belong to other memory-art traditions or later anecdotal literature. I found no primary evidence connecting them to Llull’s authentic Art.

The closest system-specific “accused magician” is not an anecdote of Llull being caught practicing magic. It is the documented posthumous construction of a magical and alchemical “Raymond Lull” through spurious works.

**Absence of evidence is material here:** stories that Llull publicly demonstrated photographic recall, memorized whole libraries with the wheels, or built a functioning mechanical oracle are modern inventions unless tied to a specific early source.

## The magic question

### 1. Was Llull’s Art itself magic?

**[TEXT] No, in its author’s stated terms.**

It invokes God, assumes Christian metaphysics, and seeks intellectual demonstration. Its letters represent defined concepts; its rotations enumerate combinations. It contains:

- no conjuration of spirits;
- no angelic names used as operative seals;
- no suffumigations;
- no talismanic timing;
- no ritual purity program of the *Ars notoria* type;
- no claim that rotation alone produces infused knowledge.

Calling it a “logical machine” can be useful; calling it a magical oracle is misleading.

### 2. Did contemporaries regard it as dangerous?

Some did regard its theological ambitions as presumptuous or erroneous. The most serious attacks came after Llull’s death.

Eymerich assembled some 200 allegedly erroneous articles from about twenty works and promoted condemnation. The controversy is textually tangled because:

- some works cited were spurious;
- some propositions could not be found in authentic texts;
- anti-Lullist and pro-Lullist parties transmitted competing documentary dossiers;
- the supposed papal act *Conservationi puritatis* of 25 January 1376 is absent from Gregory XI’s registers and is now widely regarded as inauthentic or gravely suspect.

Gregory XI’s authentic *Nuper dilecto* of 5 June 1372 ordered examination and burning of works found erroneous. This was not a blanket proof that Llull was a magician.

A commission in 1386 reportedly failed to locate three condemned propositions in the cited works. Pro-Lullist judgments in 1416/1419 attacked Eymerich’s dossier. The controversy persisted for centuries.

### 3. Was the Art heretical?

- **[ACCUSATION]** Eymerich and later anti-Lullists said it contained or supported errors.
- **[DEFENSE]** Lullists argued that propositions were forged, misquoted, taken from spuria, or misunderstood.
- **[HISTORICAL EFFECT]** Teaching was restricted; some Lullian schools were authorized to teach medicine, philosophy, and astronomy but not theology.
- **[CURRENT SCHOLARSHIP]** Authentic Llull was an unconventional but intensely Christian thinker. “Condemned heretic” is not an adequate description of his ecclesiastical status.

### 4. The *Ars notoria*

The *Ars notoria* is a medieval ritual-magic tradition promising rapid or infused acquisition of the liberal arts through prayers, angelic names, and complex *notae*.

Similarities to Lullism are superficial but historically potent:

- diagrams;
- sacred knowledge;
- comprehensive aspiration;
- condensed signs;
- claims to intellectual transformation.

Differences are fundamental:

| Llull | *Ars notoria* |
|---|---|
| Discursive reasoning | Ritual and angelic assistance |
| Defined semantic alphabet | Sacred names and *notae* |
| Combination judged by intellect | Knowledge promised through devotional operations |
| Missionary demonstration | Acquisition of arts or infused cognition |
| No required spirit invocation | Angelic/ritual framework |

**[RECEPTION]** Later readers could group them together as secret arts.  
**[TEXT]** There is no good evidence that the authentic Art descends from the *Ars notoria*.

### 5. Alchemy

All traditional alchemical works attributed to Llull are now considered spurious.

Reasons:

- the manuscripts and works postdate his death;
- authentic Llull is often hostile or skeptical toward alchemical transmutation;
- the alchemical corpus develops doctrines and legendary biographies absent from his works.

The *Testamentum* and *Liber de secretis naturae sive de quinta essentia* were especially important in manufacturing “Llull the alchemist.”

### 6. Cabala and magic

The pseudo-Llullian *De auditu kabbalistico*, printed at Venice in 1518 and 1533, uses recognizable Lullian figures while identifying the Art with Cabala and angelic names.

- **[PSEUDO]** It is not by Llull.
- **[RECEPTION]** Renaissance readers often accepted it as authentic.
- **[CONSEQUENCE]** It helped transform the missionary logician into a Christian-cabalist Magus.

## Afterlife

### 1. Memory arts

Llull’s direct contribution was not the memory palace but a different model of cognitive order:

- compact alphabet;
- semantic indexing;
- combinatorial prompts;
- diagrammatic overview;
- question generation;
- movement between universal and particular;
- rehearsal until habitual.

Renaissance mnemonists fused these with classical places and images. Bruno’s wheels are the clearest major outcome: Lullian rotation plus mnemonic and astrological imagery.

Modern mnemonic competitors generally use spatial routes and vivid images, not Llull’s definitions or ontological principles. Calling modern memory palaces “Lullian” is therefore usually **[MODERN INVENTION]**.

### 2. Dante, Giotto, and fresco cycles

Yates explicitly contrasts Llull’s abstraction with the *Divine Comedy* and Giotto’s frescoes.

- Dante and Llull share medieval structures of ascent, hierarchy, virtues, vices, cosmology, and theological encyclopedism.
- No secure evidence makes Dante’s poem a product of the Lullian Art.
- Moralized fresco cycles can be read through medieval memoria, but not thereby as Lullian diagrams.
- Any claim that Giotto encoded B–K or the Fourth Figure requires object-specific proof that has not been supplied.

### 3. Camillo and emblem culture

Camillo’s Theatre organizes knowledge by planetary grades and mythological images. Yates compares the changing values of images across grades with the changing application of B–K across subjects.

This is a structural analogy. Direct derivation is possible within Renaissance Lullist culture but not demonstrated merely by resemblance.

Emblem books combine motto, image, and explanatory text. They inhabit the same early modern culture of visualized knowledge but are not descendants of the *Ars brevis* in a narrow genealogical sense.

### 4. Bruno

Bruno’s wheels use thirty or more letters, images, celestial operators, and complex sequences. In his “inventors” material, “Raymundus” appears as an authority on nine elements or principles.

His *De lampade combinatoria*, *De specierum scrutinio*, and *Medicina Lulliana* are explicit Lullian receptions. His *De umbris idearum* and *Sigilli* combine rotation with classical and magical memory in ways the authentic Llull did not.

### 5. Ramism

Ramist method uses dichotomous divisions and bracketed trees. Its resemblance to Lullian tree structures is real at a visual level, but:

- Porphyrian trees long predate both;
- Ramus privileges division and pedagogical order;
- Llull privileges relational mixture and ascent/descent.

Yates’s notion that Ramism is a flattened or “de-occultized” Lullism is too strong.

### 6. Universal language and encyclopedism

Renaissance and seventeenth-century readers abstracted from Llull the hope that:

- knowledge has primitives;
- primitives can receive conventional signs;
- combinations can generate propositions;
- a general method can traverse all disciplines.

This helped prepare the intellectual setting of universal-language projects, although Llull’s letters abbreviate Latin concepts rather than form a new spoken language.

### 7. Leibniz

Leibniz’s *Dissertatio de arte combinatoria* (Leipzig, 1666) develops combinations, classes, and the decomposition of concepts. He encountered Lullism through Zetzner and later combinatory authors.

The relationship is:

- **documented intellectual reception**, because Leibniz names and discusses the tradition;
- **not identity**, because his mathematical combinatorics and projected *characteristica universalis* differ sharply from Llull’s ontology;
- **not yet computing**, because neither system specifies an electronic or general-purpose programmable architecture.

Leibniz later called the 1666 work juvenile, though he retained a deep interest in combinatory logic.

### 8. Computing and artificial intelligence

Defensible claims:

- Llull gave a mechanical representation of finite combinations.
- His table exactly handles combinations without repetition.
- The Art separates a symbolic index from several semantic columns.
- It proposes rule-governed generation followed by constraint checking.
- It influenced Leibnizian combinatory projects.

Overstatements:

- “Llull invented the computer.”
- “The wheels were the first artificial intelligence.”
- “The Art mechanically proved any theorem.”
- “B–K constituted binary code.”
- “The system was programmable in the modern sense.”

These are **[MODERN ANALOGIES]**, sometimes illuminating but historically anachronistic.

### 9. Borges, Eco, and modern literature

#### Borges

“Funes the Memorious” concerns unlimited episodic detail and the inability to generalize. It illuminates by contrast why Llull compresses particulars under universal concepts. No direct Lullian mechanism appears in the story.

#### Umberto Eco

Eco discussed perfect-language and encyclopedic projects, including Llull, and personally owned an example of the 1515 *Arbor scientiae* now catalogued in the Braidense Eco collection. His semiotic “encyclopedia” is not a revival of Llull’s proof procedure, but Llull is an important historical interlocutor.

#### Contemporary digital projects

Digital reconstructions, software wheels, generative-art installations, and “Llullian AI” projects legitimately explore the combinatorial form. They should label modern semantic rules and selection algorithms instead of presenting their output as the authentic medieval Art.

## Controversies and disputes

### 1. Date of birth, illumination, and first Art

- Birth: 1232/33 is now commonly preferred; Yates used c. 1235.
- Randa illumination: c. 1274 is usual; c. 1272 remains in older scholarship.
- The illumination is reported in early biography, but its exact date and phenomenology cannot be independently verified.

### 2. One revelation or a developing formal system?

- **[TRADITION]** Llull regarded the Art as divinely inspired.
- **[MANUSCRIPT/TEXTUAL EVIDENCE]** He repeatedly redesigned it.
- **Conclusion:** inspiration and development are not mutually exclusive, but claims that the complete 1308 apparatus appeared unchanged at Randa are legendary.

### 3. Is it genuinely an art of memory?

Arguments for:

- the alphabet and figures must be learned by heart;
- memory is one of the rational soul’s powers;
- diagrams and trees provide ordered retrieval structures;
- later Lullists explicitly made mnemonic adaptations.

Arguments against:

- the central authentic texts call it an art of finding, demonstrating, loving, and answering;
- it gives no classical place/image rules;
- its clearest supposed memory treatise is apocryphal;
- combination and inquiry, not storage of speeches, dominate its operation.

Best formulation:

> **[RECONSTRUCTION] The authentic Art has mnemonic requirements and mnemonic affordances, but “art of memory” is a secondary analytical classification, not its most exact medieval genre.**

### 4. Genuine wheels versus editorial wheels

Only the Fourth Figure must rotate in the mature Art. Treating every circle as a volvelle is wrong. The extra adjective-ring in the First Figure is post-medieval.

### 5. “Absolute” and “relative” principles

These labels are Renaissance inventions, probably introduced by Lavinheta and spread through Agrippa. They are not neutral: “absolute” can misleadingly imply that First-Figure goodness is absolute even when instantiated in creatures.

### 6. The tree as memory palace

Yates’s phrase “a kind of place system” is an analogy. Bonner’s structural work shows that the *Arbor* is an ontological-semantic network, not merely storage locations.

### 7. Direct Kabbalistic influence

- Similarities exist in divine Names, letters, and combination.
- Llull worked amid Jewish-Christian-Muslim exchange.
- Pico explicitly linked the arts in 1487.
- No conclusive source proves direct borrowing of the wheel mechanism.
- The pseudo-*De auditu kabbalistico* must not be projected backward.

### 8. Direct Sufi influence

Parallels with contemplation of divine Names and relational love have been proposed. Llull’s Arabic learning makes contact plausible, but precise textual dependence remains difficult to demonstrate.

### 9. Eriugena

Yates’s Eriugenian genealogy is possible and important, but “Llull was a twelfth-century Platonist misplaced in the thirteenth century” understates his engagement with contemporary scholastic and Arabic logic.

### 10. Logical validity

The combinatorial enumeration is mathematically coherent. The truth of conclusions, however, depends upon:

- acceptance of Llull’s definitions;
- his realism about principles;
- correct assignment of meanings;
- valid middle terms;
- theological premises.

The wheel alone proves nothing. Critics from Gerson to Descartes objected that Lullism could encourage speech about things not understood. Llull’s own requirement that terms first be understood is an answer, though not a resolution of all logical objections.

### 11. Papal condemnation

The anti-Lullist documentary tradition is not a simple chain of unquestionably authentic bulls. *Conservationi puritatis* is now regarded as suspect or apocryphal by many specialists because it is absent from papal registers and exhibits procedural anomalies.

It is nevertheless historically important: authentic or not, its circulation affected teaching and reputation.

### 12. Llull as Franciscan

Later Franciscan patronage and veneration are secure. Formal membership in the Third Order is not comparably secure and should not be stated as fact.

### 13. Martyrdom

Tradition says Llull was stoned by Muslims at Bougie and died from his wounds aboard ship or on reaching Majorca.

Contemporary proof is lacking. Modern biographies usually distinguish:

- a final North African mission;
- probable death in late 1315 or early 1316;
- later elaboration into a martyrdom narrative.

### 14. Llull as alchemist or magician

This is legend produced by pseudo-Llullian writings. The legend is historically real as reception but false as attribution.

### 15. Llull as computer inventor

A modern honorific based on formal resemblance. He is an important predecessor in the history of combinatory diagrams and mechanized symbolic manipulation, not the inventor of the computer.

## Open questions

1. How exactly were manuscript volvelles physically assembled and used in teaching, as opposed to copied as diagrams?
2. How many surviving early figures preserve authorially controlled layouts rather than scribal normalization?
3. To what extent did Llull expect practitioners to memorize the full 84-column table?
4. Was Figure IV regularly manipulated in live disputation, or primarily an explanatory model?
5. How did Arabic logical terminology shape the earliest Catalan versions of the Art?
6. Can direct textual paths from Jewish letter-combination practices to Llull be demonstrated beyond cultural proximity and structural analogy?
7. How should *memoria* in the authentic *Liber de memoria* be related to the practical demand to know the alphabet *cordetenus*?
8. When and where was the pseudo-*Liber ad memoriam confirmandam* composed?
9. Which early Lullist first fused classical artificial memory with Llullian combination? Bernat Garí’s 1338 *Ars memorativa* deserves further study.
10. How far did Le Myésier’s diagrams determine later readers’ understanding of Llull?
11. Which forms in the earliest printed *Ars brevis* derive from manuscripts, and which from editors such as Lavinheta?
12. Can the transmission from Zetzner to Leibniz be reconstructed at the level of particular annotations and propositions?
13. How much of Bruno’s Lullism came directly from Llull and how much through Agrippa, Lavinheta, pseudo-Llull, and oral teaching?
14. What practical medical or astrological decisions were ever made with an authentic Lullian table?
15. Did Llull’s combinatorial enumeration contain mathematical innovations independent of its theological application?
16. What would count as a historically faithful software implementation: enumeration alone, or also the definitions, subjects, semantic constraints, and rule-governed disputation?
