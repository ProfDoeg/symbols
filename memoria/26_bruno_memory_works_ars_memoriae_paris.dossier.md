# Bruno's memory works, the *Ars memoriae*, and the Paris context: Research Dossier

> **Name:** Bruno’s Parisian arts of memory: *De umbris idearum–Ars memoriae*, *Cantus Circaeus–Ars reminiscendi*, and the London *Seals* recension  
> **Exponent:** Giordano Bruno (1548–1600), with Giambattista della Porta, Heinrich Cornelius Agrippa, Jacques Gohory, and Tommaso Garzoni as major contextual figures  
> **Date:** Paris, 1582; London, probably 1583. Contextual texts: Agrippa, 1533; Gohory, 1550; Porta, 1566/1602; Garzoni, 1585, not 1578  
> **Source texts:** *De umbris idearum*, with appended *Ars memoriae* (Paris: Gilles Gorbin, 1582); *Cantus Circaeus* (Paris: Gilles/Guglielmo Gillio, 1582), second dialogue; *Recens et completa ars reminiscendi* and *Explicatio triginta sigillorum… Sigillus sigillorum* ([London: John Kingston and John Charlewood], probably 1583)  
> **Principle in one sentence:** Fixed, vividly imagined “subjects” receive mobile “adjects,” while alphabetic, mythographic, and sometimes cosmological contents are indexed on rotating combinatory wheels so that ordered images can serve as an internal script for things, words, discovery, classification, and thought.  
> **Yates:** *The Art of Memory*, ch. IX, pp. 199–209 and 214–15; ch. XI, pp. 243–44 and 247; compare ch. V, p. 124, and her extended reconstruction in ch. X, especially pp. 211–28.

## Evidentiary key

- **[T] Documented text:** stated or materially printed in a primary source.
- **[W] Witness:** established by a surviving copy, catalogue, title page, type, paper, signature, or plate.
- **[R] Scholarly reconstruction:** an inference offered by modern scholarship.
- **[Y] Yates’s interpretation:** specifically Frances Yates’s reading.
- **[D] Disputed:** materially contested in scholarship.
- **[Trad.] Tradition:** a report transmitted by an identifiable historical source but not independently confirmed.
- **[L] Legend:** a story whose documentary foundation is absent or inadequate.
- **[M] Modern invention:** a recent reconstruction or claim presented more confidently than the evidence warrants.

## Basic identification

### The bibliographical unit

**[T/W]** *De umbris idearum* and the separately titled *Ars memoriae* form one 1582 printed volume. The title describes an art of “seeking, finding, judging, ordering, and applying” directed toward “internal writing”:

> *De umbris idearum, implicantibus artem quaerendi, inveniendi, iudicandi, ordinandi et applicandi: ad internam scripturam et non vulgares per memoriam operationes explicatis.*  
> “On the shadows of ideas, unfolding an art of seeking, finding, judging, ordering, and applying, explained for internal writing and unusual operations through memory.”

The imprint is Paris, Gilles Gorbin, under the sign of Hope, 1582. An original is digitized from the Bayerische Staatsbibliothek; another survives in the Smithsonian’s Burndy Library. A copy auctioned in 2019 confirms that *De umbris* and *Ars memoriae* were intended not to be separated. [Google Books—1582 *De umbris*](https://books.google.it/books?id=WmZZAAAAcAAJ); [Smithsonian record](https://www.si.edu/object/de-vmbris-idearum-iordanus-brunus-nolanus%3Asiris_sil_156080); [Christie’s copy description](https://www.christies.com/en/lot/lot-6227890).

**[T/W]** *Cantus Circaeus* appeared separately in Paris in 1582, dedicated to Henri d’Angoulême. Its first dialogue presents Circe’s transformations and planetary invocations; its second dialogue, between Albertus and Moeris, is the applicable memory treatise. [Digitized 1582 *Cantus*](https://books.google.com.co/books?id=pTA8AAAAcAAJ).

**[T/W]** In London, probably early in 1583, Bruno republished the practical second dialogue, without Circe’s dramatic incantatory frame, as:

> *Philothei Iordani Bruni Nolani recens et completa ars reminiscendi et in phantastico campo exarandi…*  
> “Philotheus Jordanus Brunus of Nola’s recent and complete art of recollecting and inscribing in the fantastic field…”

It was joined, in surviving composite volumes, to the *Triginta sigilli*, *Explicatio triginta sigillorum*, and *Sigillus sigillorum*. The Folger record gives [London], Kingston and Charlewood, [1583?], with [20], 16, [56] leaves and twelve leaves of plates. The attribution of the first component to Kingston and the following components to Charlewood is typographical, not explicit in an imprint. [Folger catalogue](https://catalog.folger.edu/record/397737); [Sotheby’s Macclesfield copy](https://www.sothebys.com/en/auctions/ecatalogue/2004/the-library-of-the-earls-of-macclesfield-removed-from-shirburn-castle-part-2-science-a-c-l04404/lot.439.html).

### Corrections to the supplied catalogue entry

1. **[W] Garzoni’s date is wrong.** *La piazza universale di tutte le professioni del mondo* was first printed at Venice in **1585**, not 1578. Later editions, including 1587 and 1593, expanded or altered the text. “1578” should not be retained without identification of a different work or documentary witness.

2. **[T/W]** *Ars reminiscendi* is not simply “*Cantus Circaeus* reprinted” in its entirety. It reproduces, with small variants, the practical second part and omits the Circe dialogue and its planetary invocations.

3. **[D]** The five-wheel mechanism in *De umbris* cannot safely be described as a set of concentric talismanic planetary circles. Yates’s reconstruction placed the celestial images incorrectly. Rita Sturlese’s 1991 critical edition first established this correction in detail; Francesco Torchia refined it in 1997. The Warburg Institute now explicitly says that Yates’s “central power station” construction is no longer tenable as a mechanical reconstruction. [Warburg, “Yates and Bruno’s Mnemonics”](https://warburg.sas.ac.uk/research-fellowships/research-projects/archived-research-projects/giordano-bruno/yates-brunos-mnemonics).

# The system in detail

## 1. The philosophical threshold: shadows, ideas, and internal writing

The *De umbris* is not only a handbook of storage tricks. Its architecture is:

1. poems and prefatory matter;
2. an apologetic preliminary dialogue;
3. thirty *intentiones umbrarum*;
4. thirty *conceptus idearum*;
5. the practical *Ars memoriae*.

### Hermes, Philothimus, and Logifer

**[T]** The introductory dialogue stages Hermes as the bearer of the art. Philothimus—the lover of honor or learning—receives it; Logifer, the logic-bearing pedant, resists it. Bruno thereby dramatizes a recurrent opposition: living, image-mediated knowledge versus sterile verbal or scholastic formalism.

**[Y]** Yates reads Hermes’s presence as more than literary decoration: the mnemonic art is a Hermetic mystery intended to reproduce the universe within the practitioner.

**[D/R]** Later scholarship accepts that the Hermetic staging is meaningful but rejects the inference that every practical mechanism is therefore an operative talisman. “Hermetic,” “magical,” “Neoplatonic,” “psychological,” and “mnemotechnical” describe overlapping levels of the work; they are not interchangeable explanations of its machinery.

### Memory is not a separate box

Bruno begins the appended *Ars memoriae*:

> *Tunc artem sub umbra Idearum degere arbitramur, cum aut torpentem naturam antecedendo sollicitat; aut deviam et exorbitantem dirigit et perducit; aut deficientem lassamque roborat atque fulcit; aut errantem corrigit; aut perfectam sequitur et industriam aemulatur.*  
> “We judge that art dwells under the shadow of ideas when it rouses sluggish nature by going before it, directs nature when it strays, strengthens it when deficient and tired, corrects it when erring, or follows perfected nature and rivals its industry.”  
> *Opera latine conscripta*, II.1, *Ars memoriae*, §1; [digital Latin transcription](https://www.esotericarchives.com/bruno/arsmemor.htm).

**[T]** Art imitates, assists, corrects, and intensifies nature. It does not replace natural memory with a mechanical device.

Bruno then calls the art a:

> *discursiva architectura*  
> “discursive architecture,”

and a habit of the reasoning soul extending from the life-principle of the world toward the principles of all individual things.

**[T]** He refuses to make it merely a branch of the memorative faculty. It belongs to the whole soul and can assist intellect, invention, judgment, disposition, and expression.

**[R]** This is why “memory system” is narrower than Bruno’s own claim. His art is simultaneously:

- a mnemonic;
- an image-based psychology;
- a method of composition and invention;
- an ordering system;
- a model of how finite cognition approaches intelligible unity through sensible multiplicity.

## 2. Subject and adject: places and images

### Bruno’s terminology

Yates’s succinct formulation is correct:

> “The treatise in *Shadows* alters the old terminology calling the locus, the *subjectus*, and the image, the *adjectus*, but the ancient division of the two aspects of memory training is perfectly perceptible beneath this new guise.”  
> **[Y]** Yates, *Art of Memory*, p. 202.

Bruno’s own analogy is exact:

> *Habet pictura… subiectum primum in quo, parietem, lapidem et similia… Scriptura etiam habet subiectum primum chartam tanquam locum… Ita et haec ars obiective duplex admittit subiectum: primum videlicet quod est locus; et proximum quod est appositum, sive adiectum.*  
> “Painting has a first subject ‘in which’—wall, stone, and the like… Writing has paper as its first subject, as place… So this art objectively admits a twofold subject: first, that which is the place; next, that which is placed upon it, or the adject.”  
> *Ars memoriae*, §§18–19; [Latin text](https://www.esotericarchives.com/bruno/arsmemor.htm).

The analogy may be tabulated:

| External writing/painting | Internal art |
|---|---|
| Wall, stone, paper | First subject: ordered mental place or field |
| Pigment or ink | Proximate subject: a prepared, imaginable content |
| Strokes or characters | Form/adject and its intentional relation |
| Hand/tool | Cognitive instrument |
| Reading | Ordered recollection |

### What a subject is

> *Primum ergo subiectum est technica extensio, sive sinus in phantastica facultate, ordinatus. Ex speciebus receptaculorum consitus… visa omnia atque audita suo recipiens ordine et ad animae libitum retinens.*  
> “The first subject is an artificial extension, or ordered recess, in the imaginative faculty, composed from appearances of receptacles… receiving all things seen and heard in their order and retaining them at the soul’s command.”  
> *Ars memoriae*, “De subiectis,” §1.

Thus a subject can be an ordinary mnemonic locus, but Bruno also imagines more flexible internal substrates: a “fantastic chaos” capable of being formed into an animal, tree, world, or monstrous composite.

### Rules for subjects

Bruno retains and elaborates classical rules:

- **[T] Sensibility:** parts must remain visibly imaginable rather than wholly immaterial.
- **[T] Proportion:** regulate large/small relative to a human body and viewpoint.
- **[T] Intensity:** neither sensory excess nor faintness.
- **[T] Temporal orientation:** relate past and future to a present act of recollection.
- **[T] Integrity:** enough parts to present the whole distinctly.
- **[T] Distance:** neither inconvenient remoteness nor crowding.
- **[T] Direction:** preserve a natural arrival from starting to finishing point.
- **[T] Hierarchy:** commit common things to common subjects, particular things to particular subjects.
- **[T] Variety:** monotony “produces nausea” in all the senses.
- **[T] Affectivity:** a place should excite or strike the fantasy; otherwise subsidiary subjects or ornaments should be added.
- **[T] Stability:** fixed subjects remain fixed while the added forms vary.
- **[T] Capacity:** a single atrium may be internally multiplied, but boundless extension as such is rejected as unusable.

Bruno warns against relying on empty, undifferentiated spaces:

> *Caveto igitur ne vulgatum illud experiaris: vacua vacuis… frustra sperabis… quod aiunt: parietes clamabunt; lapides dabunt vocem suam.*  
> “Beware lest you try that vulgar rule, empty things for empty things… You will hope in vain that, as they say, walls will cry out and stones give their voice.”  
> *Ars memoriae*, “De subiectis,” §5.

The point is not that walls cannot be loci; it is that inert, indistinguishable, emotionally vacant walls are weak subjects.

### What an adject is

> *Adiectum vero seu forma… definitur subiecto… appositum, ad aliquid per solertem cogitationis apparatum, praesentando, effigiendo, notando vel indicando, ad picturae scripturaeque similitudinem, exprimendum vel significandum.*  
> “An adject, or form, is defined as something placed upon a physical, artificial, or fantastic subject in order to express or signify something through a skillful apparatus of thought—by presenting, depicting, marking, or indicating it, after the likeness of painting and writing.”  
> *Ars memoriae*, “De adiectis,” §1.

### Rules for adjects

- Use visible bodily action wherever possible.
- Prefer animated agents capable of action and passion.
- Use distinctive instruments, insignia, dress, posture, circumstances, assistants, surroundings, and bodily alterations.
- Vary size, excess, deficiency, intensity, time, distance, and relation to the observer.
- Exploit emotion: striking affects are “gates of memory.”
- Adapt imagery to the practitioner: the same persons and objects do not affect everyone equally.
- Do not require the agent’s name to begin with the represented letter; the association may be stipulated.
- Multiply agents when repeated use would cause collision.
- Favor familiar people when their faces are more firmly known.
- Ensure that mobile forms do not compromise the fixed order of subjects.

**[T]** Bruno distinguishes natural, artificial, externally received, and internally fashioned forms. He gives the series *forma, similitudo, imago, figura, exemplar, character, signum*—form, likeness, image, figure, exemplar, character, sign—as formally distinct ways of signifying.

## 3. The elementary wheels

The preliminary mechanism uses thirty elements adequate, Bruno says, to Latin, Greek, Hebrew, and languages subordinate to them.

### The first fixed correspondences

Bruno assigns thirty vivid mythological agents to letters or characters:

- A: Lycaon at the banquet;
- B: Deucalion with the stones;
- C: Apollo against Python;
- D: Argus guarding the cow;
- E: Arcas and Callisto;
- F: Cadmus and the sown teeth;
- G: Semele in childbirth;
- H: Echo and Narcissus;
- I: the Tyrrhenian sailor and the boy Bacchus;
- K: Pyramus;
- L: the Minyads at weaving;
- M: Perseus with Medusa’s head;
- N: Atlas bearing heaven;
- O: Pluto and Proserpina;
- P: Cyane becoming a pool;
- Q: Arachne at her web;
- R: Neptune and the horse;
- S: Pallas and the olive;
- T: Jason and the bulls;
- V: Medea at Aeson’s cauldron;
- X: Theseus and Sciron;
- Y: Nisus’s daughter cutting her father’s hair;
- Z: Daedalus constructing wings;
- followed by Greek and Hebrew characters represented by Hercules and Antaeus, Orpheus and the lyre, the Cicones attacking Orpheus, Aesacus falling, Memnon’s burial, Arion and the dolphins, and Glaucus with the herb.

Bruno states:

> *In quibus non requiritur necessario primum nominis agentis vel actionis elementum idem esse cum illo cuius est expressivum; sufficit enim ambo haec determinato huic significando esse adscripta.*  
> “It is not necessary that the first letter of the agent’s name or action be the same as the letter it expresses; it is enough that both have been assigned to that determinate signification.”

### Figure one: two wheels

**[T/W]** Bruno instructs the reader to imagine an outer wheel of agents and an inner wheel of actions. Initially both are fixed so that A+A gives “Lycaon at the banquet,” B+B “Deucalion with the stones,” and so forth. The inner wheel is then released and revolved beneath the fixed outer one.

Examples:

> *Vis combinationem PA referre? pone A rotae internae sub P rotae externae: praesentabunt tibi per Platonem in convivium.*  
> “Do you wish to represent PA? Put A of the inner wheel beneath P of the outer wheel: they will present Plato at a banquet.”

> *Vis combinationem RE? pone E rotae interioris sub R rotae exterioris: praesentabitur tibi per Neptunum in Calyston confodiendam.*  
> “For RE, place inner E under outer R: Neptune will be presented piercing Callisto.”

The surviving woodcut depicts two concentric lettered rings, not a planetary talisman.

### Figure two: three wheels

A third fixed ring gives insignia or instruments: chain, fillet, belt, hood, bag, apron, seat, and so forth. Released inner rings can create any ordered trigram.

Bruno’s examples:

> *Lycas agens quod Medusa, cum insigni Plutonis praesentabit AMO. Arcas cum actione Semelis et insigni Plutonia praesentabit EGO. Medea agens quod Tyrrhenus cum insigni Persei praesentabit VIM.*  
> “Lycaon performing Medusa’s action with Pluto’s insignia presents AMO; Arcas with Semele’s action and Pluto’s insignia presents EGO; Medea performing the Tyrrhenian’s action with Perseus’s insignia presents VIM.”

This is the clearest operational evidence: outer agent + middle action + inner attribute encode successive phonetic or literal elements.

### Four-letter combinations

**[T]** Bruno says a fourth wheel is often unnecessary. Relatively rare fourth letters can be marked by a predetermined accident, posture, attendant, or relation. The method is economical: grammatical or phonetic regularity substitutes for another enormous ring.

### Retrieval

The reverse procedure is implied and sometimes stated:

1. Fix the agents, actions, and insignia until their elemental values are immediate.
2. Encode the target into two- or three-element groups.
3. Rotate the mental wheels into the required alignment.
4. See the composite action.
5. Place it on the next ordered subject.
6. To recall, traverse the subjects in their fixed order.
7. Inspect agent, action, insignia, posture, and circumstance.
8. Translate each back into its assigned element.
9. Recompose the word or sentence.

This is a bidirectional coding system only after extensive habituation. The printed wheel is a training diagram; the operative wheel must become an internal, manipulable schema.

## 4. The five 150-part wheels

The “greater practice” replaces single letters with 150 two-character combinations arranged under thirty principal banners, each subdivided into five.

Bruno writes:

> *Conficies igitur… quinque rotas fixas, quarum singulae centum et quinquaginta constent combinationibus elementorum duorum. Quarum exterior atque prima significet agentes… Secunda actiones. Tertia insignia. Quarta adstantia. Quinta circumstantias.*  
> “You will make five fixed wheels, each consisting of 150 combinations of two elements. The first and outer signifies agents; the second actions; the third insignia; the fourth attendant things; the fifth circumstances.”  
> *Ars memoriae*, “Secunda praxis,” §6.

### Exact printed disposition

**[T/W]** Bruno admits that five complete wheels cannot conveniently be printed in a small book:

> *Quoniam difficile est quinque rotas in modicam contrahere latitudinem, unam tantum apponimus ad cuius similitudinem sunt aliae; eandemque non extensam sed contractam.*  
> “Because it is difficult to compress five wheels into a small breadth, we provide only one, after whose likeness the others are to be made, and that one contracted rather than extended.”

The surviving figure therefore represents one contracted ring/table:

- circumference divided into thirty lettered sectors;
- five subordinate entries run inward along each radius;
- each radial cell is a two-letter combination;
- names and actions are written beside the combinations;
- the complete internal apparatus is supposed to consist of five analogous 150-cell structures.

Representative first-wheel entries include:

- AA, Rhegima, chestnut bread;
- AE, Osiris, agriculture;
- AI, Ceres, yokes for oxen;
- AO, Triptolemus, sowing;
- AV, Pitumnus, manuring;
- MA, Chiron, surgery;
- ME, Circe, enchantment;
- MI, Pharphacon, necromancy;
- MO, Aiguam, circles;
- MU, Hostanes, provoking demons;
- NA, Zoroaster, magic.

**[T]** The printed names deliberately mix biblical, classical, legendary, technical, magical, and pseudo-historical “inventors.”

### What the 150 lists do

Each wheel provides a category:

1. agent;
2. action;
3. insignia or instrument;
4. attendant or adjacent object;
5. circumstance.

A target can therefore be transformed into a compact dramatic scene of five independently meaningful components. The combinatorial capacity is immense even before scenes are installed in architectural subjects.

### Yates versus Sturlese

**[Y]** Yates put the thirty-six decans, seven planets, lunar mansions, and zodiacal images at the center as astral transmitters animating the outer world-encyclopedia.

**[D]** That reconstruction joins genuine ingredients in the book but does not respect their printed and procedural placement.

**[R]** Sturlese’s account interprets the five wheels primarily as an alphabetic/phonetic machine, even an “adult toy” for learning foreign words. The Warburg exposition illustrates this with *numeratore*: NU, ME, RA, TO, RE are successively attached to mnemonic figures and attributes. Torchia’s “La chiave delle ombre” likewise reconstructs the key from textual instructions.

**[R/D]** John Farinella and Manuel Mertens resist reducing the work to neutral language-learning. They argue that Bruno’s Neoplatonic theory of images and his broader magical writings remain relevant even if Yates’s exact celestial wheel is wrong. The prudent conclusion is two-level:

- the wheel’s **immediate operation** is literal, phonetic, imagistic, and combinatory;
- its **philosophical justification** participates in Bruno’s psychology of shadows, cosmic continuity, affect, and the animation of images.

## 5. The decans, planets, and other celestial images

Bruno prints “images of the faces of the zodiac according to Teucer the Babylonian.” A representative description reads:

> *Ascendit in prima facie Arietis homo niger, immodicae staturae, ardentibus oculis, severo vultu, stans candida praecinctus palla.*  
> “In the first face of Aries rises a black man of immense stature, with burning eyes and a severe countenance, standing and girded with a white robe.”

The second face has a woman in a white tunic and Tyrian mantle, hair loose, crowned with laurel; the third a pale, red-haired, red-clothed man bearing a golden bracelet and oak staff, angry because he cannot attain what he desires.

**[T]** These are genuine printed mnemonic forms.

**[T]** Closely parallel lists appear in Agrippa, *De occulta philosophia* II.37–46, which treats zodiacal faces, planets, lunar mansions, and celestial images in the explicit context of astral magic. [Agrippa, English/Latin-linked Book II](https://www.esotericarchives.com/agrippa/op2.htm); [1533 printing](https://archive.org/details/DeOccultaPhilosophiaLoc1533/page/n15/mode/2up).

**[R]** Agrippa is a demonstrable proximate source, though some material passes through him from Arabic astral-magic traditions, especially Picatrix-related image lists and Latin Teucer traditions.

**[Y]** “It was from Agrippa’s manual of magic that Bruno took the magic images of the stars which he used in the memory system in *Shadows*” (Yates, p. 207).

**Qualification:** Yates is right about textual borrowing and Agrippa’s magical context; she overstates what that proves about the wheel’s practical use.

## 6. The *Cantus Circaeus* / *Ars reminiscendi* system

The *Cantus* gives a more recognizable architectural art of memory.

### Dramatic frame

**[T]** Circe invokes the seven planets before transforming false human beings into the animals their moral natures resemble. The second dialogue then turns to memory.

**[Y]** Yates interprets the juxtaposition as intentionally magical: planetary invocation prepares an animated imagination.

**[R]** It is also moral and satirical. Circe’s exposure of bestial human types has affinities with Bruno’s later moral dialogues; the mnemonic treatise can be read as an application following a dramatic philosophical allegory.

### Subjects: buildings, routes, and indefinitely joined cities

The system allows large numbers of subjects:

- rooms and architectural parts;
- streets and routes;
- places in successive towns;
- internally imagined or “semi-mathematical” schemata.

The ordering rule is sequential adjacency, not necessarily physical unity. Bruno’s later Italian dialogue *La cena de le Ceneri* gives the memorable license:

> *All’ultimo de’ luoghi romani potrete aggiungere il primo de’ parigini.*  
> “To the last of the Roman places you may add the first of the Parisian.”

**[T]** This means mnemonic routes can be spliced. It does not document one literally continuous imaginary street from Rome to Paris; it authorizes ordinal continuation across distinct known systems.

### Rules for places

The *Ars reminiscendi* elaborates conventional requirements:

- subjects must have a fixed beginning and direction;
- adjacent subjects must be distinct;
- excessive darkness, brightness, crowding, similarity, or distance impedes recollection;
- every subject should possess local differences;
- subjects may be natural, artificial, or fantastic;
- animated subjects may themselves act;
- inanimate subjects may contain five local distinctions—right, left, above, below, and before/in view;
- innumerable new series can be attached when their junction is explicitly fixed.

### Thirty ways of forming images

Bruno’s list expands association beyond resemblance. Images may be constructed through such relations as:

- similarity;
- contrariety;
- derivation;
- antecedent and consequent;
- cause and effect;
- agent and action;
- instrument and operation;
- container and contained;
- whole and part;
- sign and thing signified;
- name, homonym, sound, syllable, or etymological suggestion;
- emblematic, hieroglyphic, mythographic, or proverbial association;
- accident, posture, dress, color, mutilation, enlargement, or diminution;
- emotional reaction;
- transformation or metamorphosis.

The exact Latin headings and numbering vary slightly between the 1582 and 1583 settings. The larger point is documented: Bruno wants a generative repertory, not a finite dictionary of one image for every word.

### Memory for words

Classical authorities treat word-for-word memory as possible but laborious. The author of *Ad Herennium* discourages pre-manufacturing an image for every word because language is effectively infinite.

Bruno answers:

> *Atqui nobis non solum possibile sed et facile compertum est posse apparatas haberi imagines… cuiuscumque generis terminos integros…*  
> “Yet we have discovered that it is not merely possible but easy to possess prepared images by which complete terms of every kind may be assigned…”  
> *Ars memoriae*, §“Prima praxis.”

**[T]** Bruno claims to surpass “Tullius”—the Renaissance attribution of *Ad Herennium* to Cicero—through generative combinations rather than an infinite image dictionary.

**[D]** “Easy” is Bruno’s promotional claim, not an independently verified performance result. His own pages presuppose mastery of hundreds of fixed associations.

### The “Song of Circe”

Near the end, Bruno says:

> *Ars ista ipso se cantu nimium prodit, praesertim iis qui notitiam conversionis rotarum sunt adepti.*  
> “This art reveals itself sufficiently through the song itself, especially to those who have acquired knowledge of the revolutions of the wheels.”

He gives applications:

- a commander places known centurions in their proper orders with actions and circumstances;
- Circe remembers all qualities of simple medicines and their degrees;
- a grammarian can teach a boy the genders of nouns in one day;
- an astrologer can put the forty-eight constellations into four courts;
- all arts and sciences can be reduced to decades, centuries, or myriads.

These are **[T] proposed applications**, not documented controlled demonstrations.

### The twelve-house diagram

**[T/W]** Bruno says the art is “materially figured” in a following scheme of twelve houses. The plate divides an ordered space into twelve house-like compartments corresponding to an expandable subject series. It is a spatial indexing diagram, not a picture of a real building.

### The final “other brief art”

Bruno prescribes:

- animated subjects fixed so that they do not move from place to place;
- five local differences in every inanimate subject;
- twenty-four or thirty instruments for each animated subject;
- instruments deployable to right, left, above, below, or in front;
- one sound per subject;
- special forms for medial L, R, and N and for grammatical endings;
- a reduced alphabet: B P M L R S N C T G D F A E I Y O V.

His practical maxim is:

> *Satius esse… subiectis multis audacter quam paucis hesitanter operari.*  
> “It is better to work boldly with many subjects than hesitantly and fearfully with few.”

## 7. The *Seals*

The thirty seals are not thirty talisman plates in any simple sense. A “seal” is a condensed procedural configuration: a field, heaven, chain, tree, ladder, wheel, sequence, order, and so forth, each proposing a way of finding, arranging, or retaining material.

**[T]** The London title explicitly advertises invention, disposition, and memory.

**[T]** Some seals are geometrical or diagrammatic; some redescribe familiar arts of places; some coordinate divisions of knowledge; some work through contraction, multiplication, or combination.

**[Y]** Yates calls the diagrams “semi-mathematical”—mathematical “in some other way”—and interprets that “other way” as magical.

**[D]** Bruno’s phrase does not by itself mean “talismanic.” A diagram can be non-demonstrative, qualitative, topological, combinatory, or mnemonic without being an astral magical object.

# The sources

## Primary classical foundations

### *Rhetorica ad Herennium*

**Standard text:** *Rhetorica ad Herennium*, ed. and trans. Harry Caplan, Loeb Classical Library 403, Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press, 1954, III.16–24.

The basic division is:

> *Constat igitur artificiosa memoria locis et imaginibus.*  
> “Artificial memory consists, then, of places and images.”

The famous lawsuit image represents prosecutor, defendant, witnesses, and circumstances through a sick man in bed, the accused standing nearby with cup, tablets, and a ram’s testicles. The testicles encode *testes*, witnesses:

> *Aegrotum in lecto cubantem… reum ad lectum eius adstituemus, dextera poculum, sinistra tabulas, medico testiculos arietinos tenentem.*  
> Approximately: “We shall set the sick man reclining in bed, with the defendant standing beside him, holding a cup in his right hand, tablets in his left, and ram’s testicles…”  
> III.20.

Bruno inherits:

- ordered loci;
- active images;
- visual exaggeration;
- encoding of persons, things, circumstances, and sounds;
- distinction between memory for things and for words.

He rejects the classical pessimism about prepared images for all words.

Quintilian’s imaginary traversal of a building remains the clearest ancient description of retrieval by revisiting places in order.

# Yates’s account and scholarship since

## What Yates established

Yates’s enduring achievements include:

1. placing Bruno’s difficult Latin mnemonic books within a continuous history from classical rhetoric;
2. recovering their Dominican, scholastic, Lullian, Ficinian, Hermetic, and Renaissance encyclopedic contexts;
3. showing that these were intellectual works, not mere parlour mnemonics;
4. emphasizing links among memory, images, invention, cosmology, religious reform, and universal knowledge;
5. drawing attention to the neglected plates and wheels;
6. connecting Bruno’s later writings and Fludd’s theatres to the earlier memory tradition.

Her statement that “the problem must have excited a good deal of general interest, comparable to the popular interest in the mind machines of today” remains a useful cultural analogy, though not evidence of direct technological descent.

## Where she overreached

### 1. The planetary “power station”

Yates writes:

> “How did the system work? By magic of course…”

and describes star-images as a central power station transmitting astral forces.

**Verdict:** **[D: overturned as a reconstruction; partially defensible as intellectual context].**

- Sturlese showed that Yates misplaced images in constructing the wheel.
- Torchia reconstructed its literal/phonetic key.
- The Warburg Institute now states that the inner-talisman account is untenable as the wheel’s actual mechanism.
- Bruno nevertheless uses celestial images and later defines magic, bonding, and imaginative operations in ways relevant to memory. Thus “no magic anywhere” would be as flattening as “the wheel works by astral influx.”

### 2. Camillo’s Theatre as direct predecessor

Yates says in effect: “This book is the successor to Camillo’s Theatre.”

**Verdict:** **[R, plausible at the programmatic level; unproved as direct transmission].**

Both projects:

- aspire to encyclopedic ordering;
- use mythological and celestial images;
- connect memory to knowledge and invention;
- were patronage-oriented performances.

But Bruno’s movable phonetic wheels and Camillo’s seven-tier theatre are structurally different. No document proves that Bruno saw Camillo’s physical theatre.

### 3. A concealed Hermetic religion

Yates sometimes treats Bruno’s mnemonic works as instruments of an intended Egyptian/Hermetic religious restoration.

**Verdict:** **[D].** Bruno unquestionably valorized Egyptian religion in later dialogues and drew on the *Corpus Hermeticum*. But the strong claim that the memory books encode a definite missionary cult or single secret religion goes beyond their instructions. Ciliberto and Gatti preserve the religious-political dimension while resisting a monolithic Hermetic key.

### 4. Egyptian antiquity

Yates wrote before Isaac Casaubon’s 1614 dating of the Hermetica had been fully integrated into Renaissance historiography. She knew the texts were not actually Mosaic, but her narrative sometimes reproduces the Renaissance perception of immemorial Egyptian wisdom too sympathetically.

Modern scholarship distinguishes:

- the late-antique date of the philosophical Hermetica;
- Renaissance belief in their extreme antiquity;
- Bruno’s creative, selective use of them.

## Paolo Rossi

Rossi’s *Clavis universalis* first appeared in Italian in 1960—six years before Yates’s book. Its revised English form is *Logic and the Art of Memory: The Quest for a Universal Language*, trans. Stephen Clucas.

Rossi’s contribution is to trace the convergence of:

- classical mnemonic places and images;
- Lullian combination;
- encyclopedism;
- artificial and universal language;
- classification;
- early-modern method;
- Leibnizian character and calculus.

This corrects any impression that Bruno’s significance lies only in Hermetic magic. [Publisher description and contents](https://www.bloomsbury.com/us/logic-and-the-art-of-memory-9780826488619/).

## Mary Carruthers

Carruthers’s *The Book of Memory* redirects attention from isolated “memory palaces” to medieval memory as a craft of composition, ethical formation, meditation, and invention.

Applied to Bruno, this confirms that:

- images are instruments for thinking and making, not merely storing;
- architectural order need not represent a literal building;
- medieval practices already possessed generative and contemplative dimensions.

It weakens Yates’s implied rupture between a primarily practical medieval art and a suddenly world-making Renaissance art. [Cambridge edition](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/the-book-of-memory/323D304448453717FAF27D72E13FFB76).

## Lina Bolzoni and Patricia Falguières/Stagno-era Camillo scholarship

Bolzoni’s *La stanza della memoria* / *The Gallery of Memory* reconstructs the dense interchange among:

- literary description;
- galleries and rooms;
- emblems;
- hieroglyphs;
- shaped alphabets;
- painting;
- rhetoric;
- memory;
- collection and classification.

Her work makes Porta and Camillo less like isolated occult eccentrics and more like participants in a broad visual-literary culture. It also stresses how books themselves imitate rooms, theatres, and galleries. [Preview and illustration list](https://api.pageplace.de/preview/DT0400.9781442681330_A30532622/preview-9781442681330_A30532622.pdf).

## Walter J. Ong and Ramus

Ong’s *Ramus, Method, and the Decay of Dialogue* shows how Ramist method relocated order from a separately memorized architecture into the visibly dichotomized disposition of subject matter.

For Bruno:

- Logifer personifies a rival confidence in verbal-dialectical partition;
- Bruno refuses to abandon images and fantasy;
- both Ramist tables and Brunian wheels pursue portable, teachable order;
- their psychology and epistemology differ sharply.

A simple story in which Ramism “killed” memory is now considered too linear. Ramist charts transformed rather than instantly eliminated mnemonic practices.

## Sturlese, Torchia, Farinella, Clucas, Mertens

- **Rita Sturlese:** philological correction; wheel as a phonetic-combinatory apparatus.
- **Francesco Torchia:** detailed reconstruction of the wheel’s “key.”
- **John Farinella:** restores the importance of Neoplatonic cognition and argues against wholly neutralizing the system.
- **Stephen Clucas:** separates technical function from Yates’s talismanic reconstruction but argues, especially from *De imaginum compositione*, that Bruno’s magical theory of images cannot be dismissed.
- **Manuel Mertens:** reviews the dispute and concludes that the precise intended working of all five wheels remains partly unresolved; magic, heroic cognition, and technical mnemonics must be studied together.

The most defensible present position is therefore neither “Yates was right” nor “Sturlese disproved magic,” but:

> Yates’s placement and causal account of the star-images are wrong; Bruno’s mnemonic technique is genuinely phonetic and combinatory; his philosophy of imagination nevertheless belongs to a larger theory that he himself could call magical.

## Small, Hajdu, Sabbatino

- Jocelyn Penny Small’s *Wax Tablets of the Mind* examines ancient literacy, memory, and cognitive technologies and cautions against treating classical memory as a single unchanged technique.
- Helga Hajdu’s earlier *Das mnemotechnische Schrifttum des Mittelalters* remains a major catalogue of medieval mnemonic texts but reflects pre-Carruthers assumptions about practical mnemotechnics.
- Pasquale Sabbatino’s studies of Bruno and literary/visual culture emphasize writing, image, poetry, and philosophical self-fashioning.

None independently validates Yates’s precise planetary wheel.

## Lull scholarship: Bonner and Fidora

Anthony Bonner’s work demonstrates that Ramon Llull’s *ars* is not originally a memory wheel and not occult combinatorics. It is a demonstrative art in which lettered divine attributes, relational principles, subjects, virtues, vices, questions, and rules are systematically combined.

Alexander Fidora and the Universitat de Barcelona’s Ramon Llull Database provide controlled work-lists, manuscripts, editions, and attribution data.

Implications for Bruno:

- Bruno genuinely adopts concentric rotation and alphabetic combination from Lullian traditions.
- He also transforms them by attaching mythographic images and mnemonic values.
- The straight line “Llull invented the memory wheel → Bruno made it magical → Leibniz invented computing” is **[M]** if stated as direct technical descent.

[Ramon Llull Database, Universitat de Barcelona](https://orbita.bib.ub.edu/llull/).

# History and context

## Classical rhetoric

The architectural mnemonic begins as an oratorical technology:

- choose ordered, distinct places;
- place active images representing material;
- retrieve by mentally traversing the places.

Bruno retains the scaffold but expands its purposes from speech to internal writing, classification, languages, discovery, and transformation of cognitive habit.

## Aristotelian and scholastic psychology

Aristotle’s *De memoria et reminiscentia* explains memory through retained sensory affections and recollection through ordered movement from one presentation to another.

Medieval scholastic psychology placed *imaginatio*, *phantasia*, *cogitativa*, and *memoria* among the internal senses. Albert the Great and Thomas Aquinas treated artificial memory as ethically relevant because prudence requires memory of particulars.

**[R]** Bruno’s emphasis on fantasy, affect, and associative movement grows from this tradition even when he rejects scholastic terminology or polemicizes against schoolmen.

## Dominican Naples

**[T]** Bruno entered San Domenico Maggiore in Naples in 1565, taking the name Giordano, and left the Dominican order amid proceedings in 1576.

**[R/D]** Yates suggests a specifically “Albertist” mnemonic development in the convent: Albert had embraced corporeal mnemonic images more enthusiastically than Thomas’s cautions might imply. This is plausible intellectual background, but there is no surviving lesson book proving that Bruno learned his wheels at San Domenico.

## Lullism

Llullian arts circulated in Franciscan, university, courtly, missionary, medical, and heterodox settings. Bruno published *De compendiosa architectura et complemento artis Lullii* in Paris in 1582 alongside his memory books.

His adaptations include:

- rotating alphabetic wheels;
- exhaustive pairings;
- reduction of multiplicity to elements;
- movement between universal principles and particular subjects.

But Llull’s original theological-demonstrative art and Bruno’s image-laden mnemonic should not be conflated.

## Renaissance image culture

Camillo, Dolce, Porta, emblem books, hieroglyphic manuals, festival iconography, galleries, theatres, and collections supplied a culture in which knowledge could be:

- spatialized;
- personified;
- painted;
- arranged in ranks;
- reduced to attributes and emblems.

Bruno’s “internal writing” belongs to this continuum.

## Paris, 1581–83

Bruno reached Paris during the Wars of Religion and acquired a public reputation through lectures and memory demonstrations. Henri III’s court supported readers and intellectuals outside the ordinary university faculties.

Bruno later testified:

> *Acquistai nome tale che il re Enrico terzo mi fece chiamare un giorno, ricercandomi se la memoria che avevo e che professava era naturale o pur per arte magica; al qual diedi satisfazione; e con quello che li dissi e feci provare a lui medesimo, conobbe che non era per arte magica ma per scienza.*  
> “I gained such a reputation that King Henri III summoned me one day, asking whether the memory I possessed and professed was natural or produced by magical art. I satisfied him; from what I told him and had him test himself, he recognized that it was not by magical art but by science.”  
> Venetian interrogation, 30 May 1592; text transmitted in the trial records.

He continues that he published *De umbris idearum*, dedicated it to the king, and became a salaried extraordinary reader.

Status of each element:

- king’s question: **[T]**, Bruno’s sworn retrospective testimony;
- Bruno’s demonstration: **[T/Trad.]**, self-report, not independently described;
- royal position or pension: **[T/R]**, consistent with the publication and later testimony;
- Bruno’s claim that the king accepted it as science: **[Trad.]**, reported only by Bruno;
- a formal professorial chair at the Sorbonne: **[L/M]**, not documented.

## Gohory’s Parisian prehistory

Gohory’s 1550 account of Camillo and his wish for powerful “images and seals” show that this vocabulary circulated in Paris before Bruno.

**[R]** It establishes a receptive milieu, not personal transmission. Gohory died in 1576, before Bruno’s arrival.

## Porta’s Naples

Porta’s Academy of Secrets and works on natural magic, ciphers, physiognomy, optics, theatre, and memory demonstrate that “memory” could coexist with natural magic without itself being presented as ritual sorcery.

Bruno and Porta share a Neapolitan environment and interest in:

- secrecy;
- signs;
- images;
- combinatory writing;
- theatre;
- natural and artificial marvels.

Direct personal collaboration is undocumented.

As above, the episode is documented only through Bruno’s 1592 testimony. Henri asked whether the ability was natural or magical; Bruno says the king tried something himself and accepted “science.”

It is often retold as if court witnesses left a transcript of a spectacular feat. They did not.

## The St-Victor librarian’s note

**[Trad./W]** Guillaume Cotin, librarian of the Abbey of Saint-Victor, recorded conversations with Bruno in 1585–86. These notes are important evidence for Bruno’s Parisian opinions and reputation. **[T]** The invitation, teaching dispute, detention, and denunciations are documented in trial papers.

**[R]** Memory teaching caused the fatal encounter.

**[D]** It does not follow that the art of memory was the legal ground on which Bruno was ultimately condemned. The Roman proceedings centered on persistent theological and philosophical propositions.

# The magic question

## Was the art itself called magical?

The best contemporary evidence is Henri III’s reported question and Bruno’s answer: Bruno explicitly distinguished the demonstrated memory from *arte magica* and called it *scienza*.

That does not settle the historian’s question, because “magic” had several meanings:

1. illicit invocation or demonic magic;
2. astral image magic;
3. natural magic based on occult properties;
4. psychological manipulation through images and affects;
5. a universal operative knowledge joining signs to things;
6. a hostile label for unfamiliar expertise.

## Evidence for a magical dimension

- Hermes introduces *De umbris*.
- Circe invokes the planets in *Cantus*.
- Agrippa’s explicitly magical image lists are used.
- Bruno includes Circe, necromancers, Hostanes, and Zoroaster among mnemonic figures.
- His later *De magia*, *De vinculis*, and *De imaginum compositione* give imagination a central operative role.
- In *De vinculis*, the rhetorician who acts upon souls can be treated as a kind of magician or binder.
- Bruno’s cosmology assumes continuity between world, soul, sense, imagination, and intellect.

## Evidence against calling the wheel a talisman

- No instruction says to fabricate the *De umbris* wheel in a prescribed metal.
- No electional time is given for consecrating it.
- No suffumigation, invocation, spirit-name, ritual enclosure, or planetary petition accompanies its use.
- Its explicit examples encode letter groups through agent, action, and insignia.
- The surviving diagrams function as indexing and combinatory schemata.
- Bruno himself denied magical causation when questioned by Henri III, according to his testimony.

## Talismanic?

**Conclusion:** **[D] Philosophically magical; not demonstrated to be a material talisman.**

Agrippa’s source-images could be made as astral talismans in Agrippa’s system. Their reproduction in a memory table does not automatically make the table a talisman.

## Heretical?

No proposition “using loci and images is heresy” appears among the securely known final grounds of condemnation. Memory arts were taught by orthodox authors, including Dominican and Jesuit writers.

Potentially dangerous elements were:

- Bruno’s theological uses of world-soul language;
- his favorable view of Egyptian religion;
- suspicions of magic or divination;
- denial or reinterpretation of central Christian doctrines;
- cosmological and metaphysical positions;
- refusal to recant propositions selected by the Roman tribunal.

The complete final trial dossier is lost; Angelo Mercati’s discovery of a summary document and Luigi Firpo’s reconstruction remain fundamental. Therefore confident one-sentence claims that Bruno was burned “for heliocentrism,” “for memory magic,” or for one isolated doctrine are historically unsound.

## Ars notoria

The *Ars notoria* promised infused knowledge through sacred figures, prayers, divine names, fasting, and ritual observance.

Bruno’s art differs materially:

- it requires training;
- associations are constructed;
- order is learned;
- retrieval is procedural;
- no angelic infusion is promised in the practical rules.

**[Y/R]** Both involve figures and extraordinary knowledge, and church authorities could suspect both. But Bruno’s *Ars memoriae* is not an edition of the *Ars notoria*, and calling it one is **[M]**.

# Afterlife

## Dicson, Perkins, and English controversy

Alexander Dicson adapted Brunian terminology in mnemonic works. William Perkins’s *The Arte of Prophecying* and *A Warning against the Idolatrie of the Last Times* exemplify Reformed suspicion of images imagined as religiously or psychologically dangerous.

The often-quoted sentiment that a thing conceived in imagination may become an idol belongs to this Protestant iconoclastic context. It should not be treated as proof that Perkins had Bruno’s five-wheel diagram before him unless a direct citation is produced.

## Robert Fludd

Fludd’s seventeenth-century memory theatres combine:

- stage architecture;
- zodiacal and celestial classifications;
- technical versus natural places;
- striking human actions.

Yates plausibly places Fludd downstream from the late Renaissance synthesis represented by Camillo and Bruno. Direct borrowing varies by passage and requires textual comparison.

## Emblems, theatres, galleries, and frescoes

Bruno belongs to a long visual order that includes:

- medieval diagrammatic manuscripts;
- moralized virtues and vices;
- Dominican preaching and meditation;
- Giotto-associated and other civic-religious fresco cycles;
- Camillo’s theatre;
- emblem books;
- studioli, Kunstkammern, galleries, and encyclopedic displays.

**Caution:** Similarity of spatial organization is not automatically influence. A fresco cycle is not necessarily a coded Bruno-style memory palace.

## Dante

Yates and later memory scholars connect Dante’s structured otherworld, striking punishments, ordered regions, and moral images with trained medieval memory.

Carruthers makes the broad cultural relationship persuasive. A claim that Dante secretly employed Bruno’s system is impossible chronologically and is **[M]**.

## Universal language and combinatorics

The historically defensible sequence is:

- Llull: finite alphabet of principles and controlled combinations;
- Renaissance Lullism: logical, theological, medical, encyclopedic, and mnemonic adaptations;
- Bruno: rotating combinations integrated with images, phonetic coding, and internal writing;
- seventeenth-century universal-language projects: characters intended to map concepts systematically;
- Leibniz: *characteristica universalis* and *calculus ratiocinator*.

**[R]** Rossi shows a genuine history of problems, books, and transformations.

**[D]** “Lull invented the computer” or “Bruno’s wheel is the first computer” is metaphor, not technological history. There is no continuous engineering lineage from Bruno’s woodcuts to digital hardware.

## Borges, Eco, and literary afterlives

### Borges’s Funes

Funes possesses unlimited particular recall but cannot abstract or generalize. He is almost the negative image of Bruno:

- Bruno seeks ordered compression and meaningful association;
- Funes drowns in unreduced detail.

No direct Bruno citation is necessary for the comparison; it is thematic unless established from Borges’s notes.

### Umberto Eco

Eco’s writings on the search for the perfect language place Llull, Bruno, combinatorics, Cabala, and universal characters in a history of semiotic projects. Eco’s importance is interpretive: he stresses that a universal language may either discover an assumed cosmic order or construct a formal one.

### Modern “memory palaces”

The contemporary memory-palace revival usually employs:

- familiar routes;
- person-action-object images;
- phonetic number systems;
- spaced review;
- competition disciplines.

These resemble the classical architectural core and, in person/action/object compression, sometimes resemble Bruno’s multi-component images.

But modern athletes rarely reproduce Bruno’s 150-part mythographic wheels. Claims that championship systems are “Bruno’s exact method” are **[M]** unless a practitioner documents that use.

# Controversies and disputes

## Contested-material audit

### Was the Parisian art a practical memory system?

- **Evidence for:** explicit rules, tables, letter values, examples, retrieval structures, applications.
- **Evidence against a purely practical reading:** metaphysical preliminaries and ambitious claims for all faculties.
- **Finding:** yes, practical, but not merely practical.

### Was it magical?

- **Yates:** yes, its effective center was astral magic.
- **Sturlese/Torchia:** the printed wheel is a phonetic mnemonic; Yates misconstructed it.
- **Clucas/Farinella/Mertens:** technical correction does not eliminate magical psychology.
- **Finding:** magical at some philosophical and contextual levels; unproved as a consecrated talismanic machine.

### Did Bruno copy Agrippa?

- **Documented:** close verbal and iconographic correspondence in celestial-image lists.
- **Dispute:** “copy” can obscure Agrippa’s own dependence on earlier sources.
- **Finding:** Agrippa is a proximate printed source; the tradition is older.

### Did Bruno inherit a secret Dominican art?

- **Yates:** possible Albertist development at Naples.
- **Evidence:** Dominican education and the order’s long interest in memory and imagination.
- **Missing:** a manuscript, teacher’s record, or convent syllabus transmitting Bruno’s wheel.
- **Finding:** plausible background, not established transmission.

### Did Henri III employ Bruno because of a miraculous feat?

- **Evidence:** Bruno’s retrospective testimony.
- **Missing:** court record describing the experiment.
- **Finding:** the audience and favor are credible; “miracle” is legend.

### Was Bruno a Sorbonne professor?

- **Evidence:** he called himself extraordinary reader and received royal support.
- **Missing:** ordinary faculty appointment.
- **Finding:** “professor at the Sorbonne” is misleading.

### Was the London book printed as one planned volume?

- Separate signatures, paper, types, and inferred printers suggest separately produced units.
- Surviving bindings combine them.
- Exact order of printing remains reconstructed.
- **Finding:** composite publication history.

### Was *Ars reminiscendi* a new 1583 work?

No. Its core is the practical second portion of the 1582 *Cantus*, republished with variants and a new paratextual function.

### Was Garzoni’s relevant book published in 1578?

No verified copy supports 1578. The first *Piazza universale* is Venice, 1585. This is a catalogue error.

### Did Bruno influence Leibniz directly?

- Leibniz knew Lullian and combinatory traditions.
- Bruno formed part of the early-modern archive of such arts.
- A direct causal chain from Bruno’s mnemonic wheels to Leibniz’s mature logic is not established by resemblance alone.
- **Finding:** mediated intellectual genealogy, not proven direct technical dependence.

### Was Bruno executed for being a magician or memory artist?

- Mocenigo accused him partly in a context of frustrated memory instruction and alleged magic.
- The Roman case concerned multiple theological propositions and recantation.
- The complete final record is lost.
- **Finding:** memory teaching occasioned the arrest; it is not demonstrated as the decisive capital charge.

### Did Bruno invent the memory wheel?

No. Rotating disks precede him in Llullian arts, astronomical volvelles, cipher systems, and other diagrammatic traditions. Bruno’s innovation is their unusually elaborate conjunction with mythographic images, internal writing, and a general psychology.

# Open questions

1. How exactly were all five 150-part wheels meant to be synchronized in continuous practice?
2. Did practitioners construct physical volvelles, or were the printed figures chiefly aids to internal visualization?
3. How many pupils used the complete system rather than simplified architectural rules?
4. Which celestial lists came directly from Agrippa and which from intermediaries or independent Teucer/Picatrix traditions?
5. What was contained in the lost or unfinished *Clavis magna*?
6. How much of Bruno’s system was formulated before he left Naples?
7. Did Henri III’s “test” concern recall, language acquisition, topical invention, or a demonstration with a prepared subject?
8. Which London copies preserve the earliest state and original order of the separately printed units?
9. How should *magic* be distributed among causal claim, philosophical vocabulary, dramatic framing, social suspicion, and later historiography?
10. Can the thirty associative procedures in *Ars reminiscendi* be mapped exactly across all surviving 1582 and 1583 copies?
11. Are the “auctioneer,” “professor’s party,” and “student who lost his memory” stories corrupt descendants of identifiable Renaissance anecdotes?
12. How far did Dicson, Fludd, or later universal-language writers know Bruno directly rather than through a shared mnemonic-Lullian environment?
13. How should modern reconstructions handle errors in the nineteenth-century edition and damaged or absent woodcuts?
14. Can experimental reconstruction establish whether the system’s practical burden is manageable, without mistaking present-day usability for historical intention?

> **Name:** Bruno’s Parisian arts of memory: *De umbris idearum–Ars memoriae*, *Cantus Circaeus–Ars reminiscendi*, and the London *Seals* recension  
> **Exponent:** Giordano Bruno (1548–1600), with Giambattista della Porta, Heinrich Cornelius Agrippa, Jacques Gohory, and Tommaso Garzoni as major contextual figures

### Corrections to the supplied catalogue entry

1. **[W] Garzoni’s date is wrong.** *La piazza universale di tutte le professioni del mondo* was first printed at Venice in **1585**, not 1578. Later editions, including 1587 and 1593, expanded or altered the text. “1578” should not be retained without identification of a different work or documentary witness.

2. **[T/W]** *Ars reminiscendi* is not simply “*Cantus Circaeus* reprinted” in its entirety. It reproduces, with small variants, the practical second part and omits the Circe dialogue and its planetary invocations.

3. **[D]** The five-wheel mechanism in *De umbris* cannot safely be described as a set of concentric talismanic planetary circles. Yates’s reconstruction placed the celestial images incorrectly. Rita Sturlese’s 1991 critical edition first established this correction in detail; Francesco Torchia refined it in 1997. The Warburg Institute now explicitly says that Yates’s “central power station” construction is no longer tenable as a mechanical reconstruction. [Warburg, “Yates and Bruno’s Mnemonics”](https://warburg.sas.ac.uk/research-fellowships/research-projects/archived-research-projects/giordano-bruno/yates-brunos-mnemonics).

# The system in detail

## 1. The philosophical threshold: shadows, ideas, and internal writing

The *De umbris* is not only a handbook of storage tricks. Its architecture is:

1. poems and prefatory matter;
2. an apologetic preliminary dialogue;
3. thirty *intentiones umbrarum*;
4. thirty *conceptus idearum*;
5. the practical *Ars memoriae*.

### Hermes, Philothimus, and Logifer

**[T]** The introductory dialogue stages Hermes as the bearer of the art. Philothimus—the lover of honor or learning—receives it; Logifer, the logic-bearing pedant, resists it. Bruno thereby dramatizes a recurrent opposition: living, image-mediated knowledge versus sterile verbal or scholastic formalism.

**[Y]** Yates reads Hermes’s presence as more than literary decoration: the mnemonic art is a Hermetic mystery intended to reproduce the universe within the practitioner.

**[D/R]** Later scholarship accepts that the Hermetic staging is meaningful but rejects the inference that every practical mechanism is therefore an operative talisman. “Hermetic,” “magical,” “Neoplatonic,” “psychological,” and “mnemotechnical” describe overlapping levels of the work; they are not interchangeable explanations of its machinery.

### Memory is not a separate box

Bruno begins the appended *Ars memoriae*:

> *Tunc artem sub umbra Idearum degere arbitramur, cum aut torpentem naturam antecedendo sollicitat; aut deviam et exorbitantem dirigit et perducit; aut deficientem lassamque roborat atque fulcit; aut errantem corrigit; aut perfectam sequitur et industriam aemulatur.*  
> “We judge that art dwells under the shadow of ideas when it rouses sluggish nature by going before it, directs nature when it strays, strengthens it when deficient and tired, corrects it when erring, or follows perfected nature and rivals its industry.”  
> *Opera latine conscripta*, II.1, *Ars memoriae*, §1; [digital Latin transcription](https://www.esotericarchives.com/bruno/arsmemor.htm).

**[T]** Art imitates, assists, corrects, and intensifies nature. It does not replace natural memory with a mechanical device.

Bruno then calls the art a:

> *discursiva architectura*  
> “discursive architecture,”

and a habit of the reasoning soul extending from the life-principle of the world toward the principles of all individual things.

**[T]** He refuses to make it merely a branch of the memorative faculty. It belongs to the whole soul and can assist intellect, invention, judgment, disposition, and expression.

**[R]** This is why “memory system” is narrower than Bruno’s own claim. His art is simultaneously:

- a mnemonic;
- an image-based psychology;
- a method of composition and invention;
- an ordering system;
- a model of how finite cognition approaches intelligible unity through sensible multiplicity.

## 2. Subject and adject: places and images

### Bruno’s terminology

Yates’s succinct formulation is correct:

> “The treatise in *Shadows* alters the old terminology calling the locus, the *subjectus*, and the image, the *adjectus*, but the ancient division of the two aspects of memory training is perfectly perceptible beneath this new guise.”  
> **[Y]** Yates, *Art of Memory*, p. 202.

Bruno’s own analogy is exact:

> *Habet pictura… subiectum primum in quo, parietem, lapidem et similia… Scriptura etiam habet subiectum primum chartam tanquam locum… Ita et haec ars obiective duplex admittit subiectum: primum videlicet quod est locus; et proximum quod est appositum, sive adiectum.*  
> “Painting has a first subject ‘in which’—wall, stone, and the like… Writing has paper as its first subject, as place… So this art objectively admits a twofold subject: first, that which is the place; next, that which is placed upon it, or the adject.”  
> *Ars memoriae*, §§18–19; [Latin text](https://www.esotericarchives.com/bruno/arsmemor.htm).

The analogy may be tabulated:

| External writing/painting | Internal art |
|---|---|
| Wall, stone, paper | First subject: ordered mental place or field |
| Pigment or ink | Proximate subject: a prepared, imaginable content |
| Strokes or characters | Form/adject and its intentional relation |
| Hand/tool | Cognitive instrument |
| Reading | Ordered recollection |

### What a subject is

> *Primum ergo subiectum est technica extensio, sive sinus in phantastica facultate, ordinatus. Ex speciebus receptaculorum consitus… visa omnia atque audita suo recipiens ordine et ad animae libitum retinens.*  
> “The first subject is an artificial extension, or ordered recess, in the imaginative faculty, composed from appearances of receptacles… receiving all things seen and heard in their order and retaining them at the soul’s command.”  
> *Ars memoriae*, “De subiectis,” §1.

Thus a subject can be an ordinary mnemonic locus, but Bruno also imagines more flexible internal substrates: a “fantastic chaos” capable of being formed into an animal, tree, world, or monstrous composite.

### Rules for subjects

Bruno retains and elaborates classical rules:

- **[T] Sensibility:** parts must remain visibly imaginable rather than wholly immaterial.
- **[T] Proportion:** regulate large/small relative to a human body and viewpoint.
- **[T] Intensity:** neither sensory excess nor faintness.
- **[T] Temporal orientation:** relate past and future to a present act of recollection.
- **[T] Integrity:** enough parts to present the whole distinctly.
- **[T] Distance:** neither inconvenient remoteness nor crowding.
- **[T] Direction:** preserve a natural arrival from starting to finishing point.
- **[T] Hierarchy:** commit common things to common subjects, particular things to particular subjects.
- **[T] Variety:** monotony “produces nausea” in all the senses.
- **[T] Affectivity:** a place should excite or strike the fantasy; otherwise subsidiary subjects or ornaments should be added.
- **[T] Stability:** fixed subjects remain fixed while the added forms vary.
- **[T] Capacity:** a single atrium may be internally multiplied, but boundless extension as such is rejected as unusable.

Bruno warns against relying on empty, undifferentiated spaces:

> *Caveto igitur ne vulgatum illud experiaris: vacua vacuis… frustra sperabis… quod aiunt: parietes clamabunt; lapides dabunt vocem suam.*  
> “Beware lest you try that vulgar rule, empty things for empty things… You will hope in vain that, as they say, walls will cry out and stones give their voice.”  
> *Ars memoriae*, “De subiectis,” §5.

The point is not that walls cannot be loci; it is that inert, indistinguishable, emotionally vacant walls are weak subjects.

### What an adject is

> *Adiectum vero seu forma… definitur subiecto… appositum, ad aliquid per solertem cogitationis apparatum, praesentando, effigiendo, notando vel indicando, ad picturae scripturaeque similitudinem, exprimendum vel significandum.*  
> “An adject, or form, is defined as something placed upon a physical, artificial, or fantastic subject in order to express or signify something through a skillful apparatus of thought—by presenting, depicting, marking, or indicating it, after the likeness of painting and writing.”  
> *Ars memoriae*, “De adiectis,” §1.

### Rules for adjects

- Use visible bodily action wherever possible.
- Prefer animated agents capable of action and passion.
- Use distinctive instruments, insignia, dress, posture, circumstances, assistants, surroundings, and bodily alterations.
- Vary size, excess, deficiency, intensity, time, distance, and relation to the observer.
- Exploit emotion: striking affects are “gates of memory.”
- Adapt imagery to the practitioner: the same persons and objects do not affect everyone equally.
- Do not require the agent’s name to begin with the represented letter; the association may be stipulated.
- Multiply agents when repeated use would cause collision.
- Favor familiar people when their faces are more firmly known.
- Ensure that mobile forms do not compromise the fixed order of subjects.

**[T]** Bruno distinguishes natural, artificial, externally received, and internally fashioned forms. He gives the series *forma, similitudo, imago, figura, exemplar, character, signum*—form, likeness, image, figure, exemplar, character, sign—as formally distinct ways of signifying.

## 3. The elementary wheels

The preliminary mechanism uses thirty elements adequate, Bruno says, to Latin, Greek, Hebrew, and languages subordinate to them.

### The first fixed correspondences

Bruno assigns thirty vivid mythological agents to letters or characters:

- A: Lycaon at the banquet;
- B: Deucalion with the stones;
- C: Apollo against Python;
- D: Argus guarding the cow;
- E: Arcas and Callisto;
- F: Cadmus and the sown teeth;
- G: Semele in childbirth;
- H: Echo and Narcissus;
- I: the Tyrrhenian sailor and the boy Bacchus;
- K: Pyramus;
- L: the Minyads at weaving;
- M: Perseus with Medusa’s head;
- N: Atlas bearing heaven;
- O: Pluto and Proserpina;
- P: Cyane becoming a pool;
- Q: Arachne at her web;
- R: Neptune and the horse;
- S: Pallas and the olive;
- T: Jason and the bulls;
- V: Medea at Aeson’s cauldron;
- X: Theseus and Sciron;
- Y: Nisus’s daughter cutting her father’s hair;
- Z: Daedalus constructing wings;
- followed by Greek and Hebrew characters represented by Hercules and Antaeus, Orpheus and the lyre, the Cicones attacking Orpheus, Aesacus falling, Memnon’s burial, Arion and the dolphins, and Glaucus with the herb.

Bruno states:

> *In quibus non requiritur necessario primum nominis agentis vel actionis elementum idem esse cum illo cuius est expressivum; sufficit enim ambo haec determinato huic significando esse adscripta.*  
> “It is not necessary that the first letter of the agent’s name or action be the same as the letter it expresses; it is enough that both have been assigned to that determinate signification.”

### Figure one: two wheels

**[T/W]** Bruno instructs the reader to imagine an outer wheel of agents and an inner wheel of actions. Initially both are fixed so that A+A gives “Lycaon at the banquet,” B+B “Deucalion with the stones,” and so forth. The inner wheel is then released and revolved beneath the fixed outer one.

Examples:

> *Vis combinationem PA referre? pone A rotae internae sub P rotae externae: praesentabunt tibi per Platonem in convivium.*  
> “Do you wish to represent PA? Put A of the inner wheel beneath P of the outer wheel: they will present Plato at a banquet.”

> *Vis combinationem RE? pone E rotae interioris sub R rotae exterioris: praesentabitur tibi per Neptunum in Calyston confodiendam.*  
> “For RE, place inner E under outer R: Neptune will be presented piercing Callisto.”

The surviving woodcut depicts two concentric lettered rings, not a planetary talisman.

### Figure two: three wheels

A third fixed ring gives insignia or instruments: chain, fillet, belt, hood, bag, apron, seat, and so forth. Released inner rings can create any ordered trigram.

Bruno’s examples:

> *Lycas agens quod Medusa, cum insigni Plutonis praesentabit AMO. Arcas cum actione Semelis et insigni Plutonia praesentabit EGO. Medea agens quod Tyrrhenus cum insigni Persei praesentabit VIM.*  
> “Lycaon performing Medusa’s action with Pluto’s insignia presents AMO; Arcas with Semele’s action and Pluto’s insignia presents EGO; Medea performing the Tyrrhenian’s action with Perseus’s insignia presents VIM.”

This is the clearest operational evidence: outer agent + middle action + inner attribute encode successive phonetic or literal elements.

### Four-letter combinations

**[T]** Bruno says a fourth wheel is often unnecessary. Relatively rare fourth letters can be marked by a predetermined accident, posture, attendant, or relation. The method is economical: grammatical or phonetic regularity substitutes for another enormous ring.

### Retrieval

The reverse procedure is implied and sometimes stated:

1. Fix the agents, actions, and insignia until their elemental values are immediate.
2. Encode the target into two- or three-element groups.
3. Rotate the mental wheels into the required alignment.
4. See the composite action.
5. Place it on the next ordered subject.
6. To recall, traverse the subjects in their fixed order.
7. Inspect agent, action, insignia, posture, and circumstance.
8. Translate each back into its assigned element.
9. Recompose the word or sentence.

This is a bidirectional coding system only after extensive habituation. The printed wheel is a training diagram; the operative wheel must become an internal, manipulable schema.

## 4. The five 150-part wheels

The “greater practice” replaces single letters with 150 two-character combinations arranged under thirty principal banners, each subdivided into five.

Bruno writes:

> *Conficies igitur… quinque rotas fixas, quarum singulae centum et quinquaginta constent combinationibus elementorum duorum. Quarum exterior atque prima significet agentes… Secunda actiones. Tertia insignia. Quarta adstantia. Quinta circumstantias.*  
> “You will make five fixed wheels, each consisting of 150 combinations of two elements. The first and outer signifies agents; the second actions; the third insignia; the fourth attendant things; the fifth circumstances.”  
> *Ars memoriae*, “Secunda praxis,” §6.

### Exact printed disposition

**[T/W]** Bruno admits that five complete wheels cannot conveniently be printed in a small book:

> *Quoniam difficile est quinque rotas in modicam contrahere latitudinem, unam tantum apponimus ad cuius similitudinem sunt aliae; eandemque non extensam sed contractam.*  
> “Because it is difficult to compress five wheels into a small breadth, we provide only one, after whose likeness the others are to be made, and that one contracted rather than extended.”

The surviving figure therefore represents one contracted ring/table:

- circumference divided into thirty lettered sectors;
- five subordinate entries run inward along each radius;
- each radial cell is a two-letter combination;
- names and actions are written beside the combinations;
- the complete internal apparatus is supposed to consist of five analogous 150-cell structures.

Representative first-wheel entries include:

- AA, Rhegima, chestnut bread;
- AE, Osiris, agriculture;
- AI, Ceres, yokes for oxen;
- AO, Triptolemus, sowing;
- AV, Pitumnus, manuring;
- MA, Chiron, surgery;
- ME, Circe, enchantment;
- MI, Pharphacon, necromancy;
- MO, Aiguam, circles;
- MU, Hostanes, provoking demons;
- NA, Zoroaster, magic.

**[T]** The printed names deliberately mix biblical, classical, legendary, technical, magical, and pseudo-historical “inventors.”

### What the 150 lists do

Each wheel provides a category:

1. agent;
2. action;
3. insignia or instrument;
4. attendant or adjacent object;
5. circumstance.

A target can therefore be transformed into a compact dramatic scene of five independently meaningful components. The combinatorial capacity is immense even before scenes are installed in architectural subjects.

### Yates versus Sturlese

**[Y]** Yates put the thirty-six decans, seven planets, lunar mansions, and zodiacal images at the center as astral transmitters animating the outer world-encyclopedia.

**[D]** That reconstruction joins genuine ingredients in the book but does not respect their printed and procedural placement.

**[R]** Sturlese’s account interprets the five wheels primarily as an alphabetic/phonetic machine, even an “adult toy” for learning foreign words. The Warburg exposition illustrates this with *numeratore*: NU, ME, RA, TO, RE are successively attached to mnemonic figures and attributes. Torchia’s “La chiave delle ombre” likewise reconstructs the key from textual instructions.

**[R/D]** John Farinella and Manuel Mertens resist reducing the work to neutral language-learning. They argue that Bruno’s Neoplatonic theory of images and his broader magical writings remain relevant even if Yates’s exact celestial wheel is wrong. The prudent conclusion is two-level:

- the wheel’s **immediate operation** is literal, phonetic, imagistic, and combinatory;
- its **philosophical justification** participates in Bruno’s psychology of shadows, cosmic continuity, affect, and the animation of images.

## 5. The decans, planets, and other celestial images

Bruno prints “images of the faces of the zodiac according to Teucer the Babylonian.” A representative description reads:

> *Ascendit in prima facie Arietis homo niger, immodicae staturae, ardentibus oculis, severo vultu, stans candida praecinctus palla.*  
> “In the first face of Aries rises a black man of immense stature, with burning eyes and a severe countenance, standing and girded with a white robe.”

The second face has a woman in a white tunic and Tyrian mantle, hair loose, crowned with laurel; the third a pale, red-haired, red-clothed man bearing a golden bracelet and oak staff, angry because he cannot attain what he desires.

**[T]** These are genuine printed mnemonic forms.

**[T]** Closely parallel lists appear in Agrippa, *De occulta philosophia* II.37–46, which treats zodiacal faces, planets, lunar mansions, and celestial images in the explicit context of astral magic. [Agrippa, English/Latin-linked Book II](https://www.esotericarchives.com/agrippa/op2.htm); [1533 printing](https://archive.org/details/DeOccultaPhilosophiaLoc1533/page/n15/mode/2up).

**[R]** Agrippa is a demonstrable proximate source, though some material passes through him from Arabic astral-magic traditions, especially Picatrix-related image lists and Latin Teucer traditions.

**[Y]** “It was from Agrippa’s manual of magic that Bruno took the magic images of the stars which he used in the memory system in *Shadows*” (Yates, p. 207).

**Qualification:** Yates is right about textual borrowing and Agrippa’s magical context; she overstates what that proves about the wheel’s practical use.

## 6. The *Cantus Circaeus* / *Ars reminiscendi* system

The *Cantus* gives a more recognizable architectural art of memory.

### Dramatic frame

**[T]** Circe invokes the seven planets before transforming false human beings into the animals their moral natures resemble. The second dialogue then turns to memory.

**[Y]** Yates interprets the juxtaposition as intentionally magical: planetary invocation prepares an animated imagination.

**[R]** It is also moral and satirical. Circe’s exposure of bestial human types has affinities with Bruno’s later moral dialogues; the mnemonic treatise can be read as an application following a dramatic philosophical allegory.

### Subjects: buildings, routes, and indefinitely joined cities

The system allows large numbers of subjects:

- rooms and architectural parts;
- streets and routes;
- places in successive towns;
- internally imagined or “semi-mathematical” schemata.

The ordering rule is sequential adjacency, not necessarily physical unity. Bruno’s later Italian dialogue *La cena de le Ceneri* gives the memorable license:

> *All’ultimo de’ luoghi romani potrete aggiungere il primo de’ parigini.*  
> “To the last of the Roman places you may add the first of the Parisian.”

**[T]** This means mnemonic routes can be spliced. It does not document one literally continuous imaginary street from Rome to Paris; it authorizes ordinal continuation across distinct known systems.

### Rules for places

The *Ars reminiscendi* elaborates conventional requirements:

- subjects must have a fixed beginning and direction;
- adjacent subjects must be distinct;
- excessive darkness, brightness, crowding, similarity, or distance impedes recollection;
- every subject should possess local differences;
- subjects may be natural, artificial, or fantastic;
- animated subjects may themselves act;
- inanimate subjects may contain five local distinctions—right, left, above, below, and before/in view;
- innumerable new series can be attached when their junction is explicitly fixed.

### Thirty ways of forming images

Bruno’s list expands association beyond resemblance. Images may be constructed through such relations as:

- similarity;
- contrariety;
- derivation;
- antecedent and consequent;
- cause and effect;
- agent and action;
- instrument and operation;
- container and contained;
- whole and part;
- sign and thing signified;
- name, homonym, sound, syllable, or etymological suggestion;
- emblematic, hieroglyphic, mythographic, or proverbial association;
- accident, posture, dress, color, mutilation, enlargement, or diminution;
- emotional reaction;
- transformation or metamorphosis.

The exact Latin headings and numbering vary slightly between the 1582 and 1583 settings. The larger point is documented: Bruno wants a generative repertory, not a finite dictionary of one image for every word.

### Memory for words

Classical authorities treat word-for-word memory as possible but laborious. The author of *Ad Herennium* discourages pre-manufacturing an image for every word because language is effectively infinite.

Bruno answers:

> *Atqui nobis non solum possibile sed et facile compertum est posse apparatas haberi imagines… cuiuscumque generis terminos integros…*  
> “Yet we have discovered that it is not merely possible but easy to possess prepared images by which complete terms of every kind may be assigned…”  
> *Ars memoriae*, §“Prima praxis.”

**[T]** Bruno claims to surpass “Tullius”—the Renaissance attribution of *Ad Herennium* to Cicero—through generative combinations rather than an infinite image dictionary.

**[D]** “Easy” is Bruno’s promotional claim, not an independently verified performance result. His own pages presuppose mastery of hundreds of fixed associations.

### The “Song of Circe”

Near the end, Bruno says:

> *Ars ista ipso se cantu nimium prodit, praesertim iis qui notitiam conversionis rotarum sunt adepti.*  
> “This art reveals itself sufficiently through the song itself, especially to those who have acquired knowledge of the revolutions of the wheels.”

He gives applications:

- a commander places known centurions in their proper orders with actions and circumstances;
- Circe remembers all qualities of simple medicines and their degrees;
- a grammarian can teach a boy the genders of nouns in one day;
- an astrologer can put the forty-eight constellations into four courts;
- all arts and sciences can be reduced to decades, centuries, or myriads.

These are **[T] proposed applications**, not documented controlled demonstrations.

### The twelve-house diagram

**[T/W]** Bruno says the art is “materially figured” in a following scheme of twelve houses. The plate divides an ordered space into twelve house-like compartments corresponding to an expandable subject series. It is a spatial indexing diagram, not a picture of a real building.

### The final “other brief art”

Bruno prescribes:

- animated subjects fixed so that they do not move from place to place;
- five local differences in every inanimate subject;
- twenty-four or thirty instruments for each animated subject;
- instruments deployable to right, left, above, below, or in front;
- one sound per subject;
- special forms for medial L, R, and N and for grammatical endings;
- a reduced alphabet: B P M L R S N C T G D F A E I Y O V.

His practical maxim is:

> *Satius esse… subiectis multis audacter quam paucis hesitanter operari.*  
> “It is better to work boldly with many subjects than hesitantly and fearfully with few.”

## 7. The *Seals*

The thirty seals are not thirty talisman plates in any simple sense. A “seal” is a condensed procedural configuration: a field, heaven, chain, tree, ladder, wheel, sequence, order, and so forth, each proposing a way of finding, arranging, or retaining material.

**[T]** The London title explicitly advertises invention, disposition, and memory.

**[T]** Some seals are geometrical or diagrammatic; some redescribe familiar arts of places; some coordinate divisions of knowledge; some work through contraction, multiplication, or combination.

**[Y]** Yates calls the diagrams “semi-mathematical”—mathematical “in some other way”—and interprets that “other way” as magical.

**[D]** Bruno’s phrase does not by itself mean “talismanic.” A diagram can be non-demonstrative, qualitative, topological, combinatory, or mnemonic without being an astral magical object.

# The sources

## Primary classical foundations

### *Rhetorica ad Herennium*

**Standard text:** *Rhetorica ad Herennium*, ed. and trans. Harry Caplan, Loeb Classical Library 403, Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press, 1954, III.16–24.

The basic division is:

> *Constat igitur artificiosa memoria locis et imaginibus.*  
> “Artificial memory consists, then, of places and images.”

The famous lawsuit image represents prosecutor, defendant, witnesses, and circumstances through a sick man in bed, the accused standing nearby with cup, tablets, and a ram’s testicles. The testicles encode *testes*, witnesses:

> *Aegrotum in lecto cubantem… reum ad lectum eius adstituemus, dextera poculum, sinistra tabulas, medico testiculos arietinos tenentem.*  
> Approximately: “We shall set the sick man reclining in bed, with the defendant standing beside him, holding a cup in his right hand, tablets in his left, and ram’s testicles…”  
> III.20.

Bruno inherits:

- ordered loci;
- active images;
- visual exaggeration;
- encoding of persons, things, circumstances, and sounds;
- distinction between memory for things and for words.

He rejects the classical pessimism about prepared images for all words.

### Cicero and Quintilian

- Cicero, *De oratore* II.350–60, ed. and trans. E. W. Sutton and H. Rackham, Loeb Classical Library.
- Quintilian, *Institutio oratoria* XI.2, ed. and trans. Donald Russell, Loeb Classical Library.

Quintilian’s imaginary traversal of a building remains the clearest ancient description of retrieval by revisiting places in order.

### Manuscripts

**[Finding from absence]** No authorial manuscript of the 1582 *De umbris–Ars memoriae* or *Cantus Circaeus* is presently the standard textual witness. The primary witnesses are printed copies.

The often-cited *Clavis magna* is different:

- Bruno repeatedly refers to it as a larger foundational work.
- No complete independent copy is known.
- Older scholarship sometimes treated the *Sigillus sigillorum* as its sole published remnant.
- Reconstruction of its scope from cross-references remains **[R/D]**, not a recovered text.

## Variants and printing history

### *Cantus* versus London *Ars reminiscendi*

- dramatic first dialogue omitted;
- planetary invocations omitted;
- practical art retained with small verbal and typographical variants;
- new title and preliminary matter make it an introduction to the thirty seals;
- the London book circulated in separately printed units.

### Diagram variants

- Woodcuts may be absent, damaged, reversed, or differently placed in particular copies.
- The surviving London composite has twelve plate leaves according to the Folger record.
- Yates’s hand-drawn/constructed wheel is **not** an original plate. It is a twentieth-century reconstruction.

### Bibliographic cautions

- “Gilles Gorbin,” “Aegidius Gorbinus,” and occasional “Gourbin” are variants/Latinization or modern normalization, not different editions.
- The London date and printer are inferred.
- A vendor catalogue is useful evidence for copy structure but not equivalent to a critical bibliography.

Book II.37–46 supplies celestial image descriptions closely parallel to Bruno’s. Agrippa explicitly places them in astral magic.

- BnF shelfmark: **RES-V-2975**.

### Giambattista della Porta

In “De’ professori della memoria,” Garzoni surveys ancient and contemporary mnemonic authorities and announces an ambition to combine Rosselli’s artificial memory with Lullian art.

**[T]** This documents a late-sixteenth-century expectation that architectural memory and Lullian combination could be synthesized.

**[D]** It does not establish that Garzoni completed such a universal art or that Bruno borrowed from him. The first edition postdates Bruno’s Paris books.

# Yates’s account and scholarship since

## What Yates established

Yates’s enduring achievements include:

1. placing Bruno’s difficult Latin mnemonic books within a continuous history from classical rhetoric;
2. recovering their Dominican, scholastic, Lullian, Ficinian, Hermetic, and Renaissance encyclopedic contexts;
3. showing that these were intellectual works, not mere parlour mnemonics;
4. emphasizing links among memory, images, invention, cosmology, religious reform, and universal knowledge;
5. drawing attention to the neglected plates and wheels;
6. connecting Bruno’s later writings and Fludd’s theatres to the earlier memory tradition.

Her statement that “the problem must have excited a good deal of general interest, comparable to the popular interest in the mind machines of today” remains a useful cultural analogy, though not evidence of direct technological descent.

## Where she overreached

### 1. The planetary “power station”

Yates writes:

> “How did the system work? By magic of course…”

and describes star-images as a central power station transmitting astral forces.

**Verdict:** **[D: overturned as a reconstruction; partially defensible as intellectual context].**

- Sturlese showed that Yates misplaced images in constructing the wheel.
- Torchia reconstructed its literal/phonetic key.
- The Warburg Institute now states that the inner-talisman account is untenable as the wheel’s actual mechanism.
- Bruno nevertheless uses celestial images and later defines magic, bonding, and imaginative operations in ways relevant to memory. Thus “no magic anywhere” would be as flattening as “the wheel works by astral influx.”

### 2. Camillo’s Theatre as direct predecessor

Yates says in effect: “This book is the successor to Camillo’s Theatre.”

**Verdict:** **[R, plausible at the programmatic level; unproved as direct transmission].**

Both projects:

- aspire to encyclopedic ordering;
- use mythological and celestial images;
- connect memory to knowledge and invention;
- were patronage-oriented performances.

But Bruno’s movable phonetic wheels and Camillo’s seven-tier theatre are structurally different. No document proves that Bruno saw Camillo’s physical theatre.

### 3. A concealed Hermetic religion

Yates sometimes treats Bruno’s mnemonic works as instruments of an intended Egyptian/Hermetic religious restoration.

**Verdict:** **[D].** Bruno unquestionably valorized Egyptian religion in later dialogues and drew on the *Corpus Hermeticum*. But the strong claim that the memory books encode a definite missionary cult or single secret religion goes beyond their instructions. Ciliberto and Gatti preserve the religious-political dimension while resisting a monolithic Hermetic key.

### 4. Egyptian antiquity

Yates wrote before Isaac Casaubon’s 1614 dating of the Hermetica had been fully integrated into Renaissance historiography. She knew the texts were not actually Mosaic, but her narrative sometimes reproduces the Renaissance perception of immemorial Egyptian wisdom too sympathetically.

Modern scholarship distinguishes:

- the late-antique date of the philosophical Hermetica;
- Renaissance belief in their extreme antiquity;
- Bruno’s creative, selective use of them.

## Paolo Rossi

Rossi’s *Clavis universalis* first appeared in Italian in 1960—six years before Yates’s book. Its revised English form is *Logic and the Art of Memory: The Quest for a Universal Language*, trans. Stephen Clucas.

Rossi’s contribution is to trace the convergence of:

- classical mnemonic places and images;
- Lullian combination;
- encyclopedism;
- artificial and universal language;
- classification;
- early-modern method;
- Leibnizian character and calculus.

This corrects any impression that Bruno’s significance lies only in Hermetic magic. [Publisher description and contents](https://www.bloomsbury.com/us/logic-and-the-art-of-memory-9780826488619/).

## Mary Carruthers

Carruthers’s *The Book of Memory* redirects attention from isolated “memory palaces” to medieval memory as a craft of composition, ethical formation, meditation, and invention.

Applied to Bruno, this confirms that:

- images are instruments for thinking and making, not merely storing;
- architectural order need not represent a literal building;
- medieval practices already possessed generative and contemplative dimensions.

It weakens Yates’s implied rupture between a primarily practical medieval art and a suddenly world-making Renaissance art. [Cambridge edition](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/the-book-of-memory/323D304448453717FAF27D72E13FFB76).

## Lina Bolzoni and Patricia Falguières/Stagno-era Camillo scholarship

Bolzoni’s *La stanza della memoria* / *The Gallery of Memory* reconstructs the dense interchange among:

- literary description;
- galleries and rooms;
- emblems;
- hieroglyphs;
- shaped alphabets;
- painting;
- rhetoric;
- memory;
- collection and classification.

Her work makes Porta and Camillo less like isolated occult eccentrics and more like participants in a broad visual-literary culture. It also stresses how books themselves imitate rooms, theatres, and galleries. [Preview and illustration list](https://api.pageplace.de/preview/DT0400.9781442681330_A30532622/preview-9781442681330_A30532622.pdf).

## Walter J. Ong and Ramus

Ong’s *Ramus, Method, and the Decay of Dialogue* shows how Ramist method relocated order from a separately memorized architecture into the visibly dichotomized disposition of subject matter.

For Bruno:

- Logifer personifies a rival confidence in verbal-dialectical partition;
- Bruno refuses to abandon images and fantasy;
- both Ramist tables and Brunian wheels pursue portable, teachable order;
- their psychology and epistemology differ sharply.

A simple story in which Ramism “killed” memory is now considered too linear. Ramist charts transformed rather than instantly eliminated mnemonic practices.

## Sturlese, Torchia, Farinella, Clucas, Mertens

- **Rita Sturlese:** philological correction; wheel as a phonetic-combinatory apparatus.
- **Francesco Torchia:** detailed reconstruction of the wheel’s “key.”
- **John Farinella:** restores the importance of Neoplatonic cognition and argues against wholly neutralizing the system.
- **Stephen Clucas:** separates technical function from Yates’s talismanic reconstruction but argues, especially from *De imaginum compositione*, that Bruno’s magical theory of images cannot be dismissed.
- **Manuel Mertens:** reviews the dispute and concludes that the precise intended working of all five wheels remains partly unresolved; magic, heroic cognition, and technical mnemonics must be studied together.

The most defensible present position is therefore neither “Yates was right” nor “Sturlese disproved magic,” but:

> Yates’s placement and causal account of the star-images are wrong; Bruno’s mnemonic technique is genuinely phonetic and combinatory; his philosophy of imagination nevertheless belongs to a larger theory that he himself could call magical.

## Small, Hajdu, Sabbatino

- Jocelyn Penny Small’s *Wax Tablets of the Mind* examines ancient literacy, memory, and cognitive technologies and cautions against treating classical memory as a single unchanged technique.
- Helga Hajdu’s earlier *Das mnemotechnische Schrifttum des Mittelalters* remains a major catalogue of medieval mnemonic texts but reflects pre-Carruthers assumptions about practical mnemotechnics.
- Pasquale Sabbatino’s studies of Bruno and literary/visual culture emphasize writing, image, poetry, and philosophical self-fashioning.

None independently validates Yates’s precise planetary wheel.

## Lull scholarship: Bonner and Fidora

Anthony Bonner’s work demonstrates that Ramon Llull’s *ars* is not originally a memory wheel and not occult combinatorics. It is a demonstrative art in which lettered divine attributes, relational principles, subjects, virtues, vices, questions, and rules are systematically combined.

Alexander Fidora and the Universitat de Barcelona’s Ramon Llull Database provide controlled work-lists, manuscripts, editions, and attribution data.

Implications for Bruno:

- Bruno genuinely adopts concentric rotation and alphabetic combination from Lullian traditions.
- He also transforms them by attaching mythographic images and mnemonic values.
- The straight line “Llull invented the memory wheel → Bruno made it magical → Leibniz invented computing” is **[M]** if stated as direct technical descent.

[Ramon Llull Database, Universitat de Barcelona](https://orbita.bib.ub.edu/llull/).

# History and context

## Classical rhetoric

The architectural mnemonic begins as an oratorical technology:

- choose ordered, distinct places;
- place active images representing material;
- retrieve by mentally traversing the places.

Bruno retains the scaffold but expands its purposes from speech to internal writing, classification, languages, discovery, and transformation of cognitive habit.

## Aristotelian and scholastic psychology

Aristotle’s *De memoria et reminiscentia* explains memory through retained sensory affections and recollection through ordered movement from one presentation to another.

Medieval scholastic psychology placed *imaginatio*, *phantasia*, *cogitativa*, and *memoria* among the internal senses. Albert the Great and Thomas Aquinas treated artificial memory as ethically relevant because prudence requires memory of particulars.

**[R]** Bruno’s emphasis on fantasy, affect, and associative movement grows from this tradition even when he rejects scholastic terminology or polemicizes against schoolmen.

## Dominican Naples

**[T]** Bruno entered San Domenico Maggiore in Naples in 1565, taking the name Giordano, and left the Dominican order amid proceedings in 1576.

**[R/D]** Yates suggests a specifically “Albertist” mnemonic development in the convent: Albert had embraced corporeal mnemonic images more enthusiastically than Thomas’s cautions might imply. This is plausible intellectual background, but there is no surviving lesson book proving that Bruno learned his wheels at San Domenico.

## Lullism

Llullian arts circulated in Franciscan, university, courtly, missionary, medical, and heterodox settings. Bruno published *De compendiosa architectura et complemento artis Lullii* in Paris in 1582 alongside his memory books.

His adaptations include:

- rotating alphabetic wheels;
- exhaustive pairings;
- reduction of multiplicity to elements;
- movement between universal principles and particular subjects.

But Llull’s original theological-demonstrative art and Bruno’s image-laden mnemonic should not be conflated.

## Renaissance image culture

Camillo, Dolce, Porta, emblem books, hieroglyphic manuals, festival iconography, galleries, theatres, and collections supplied a culture in which knowledge could be:

- spatialized;
- personified;
- painted;
- arranged in ranks;
- reduced to attributes and emblems.

Bruno’s “internal writing” belongs to this continuum.

## Paris, 1581–83

Bruno reached Paris during the Wars of Religion and acquired a public reputation through lectures and memory demonstrations. Henri III’s court supported readers and intellectuals outside the ordinary university faculties.

Bruno later testified:

> *Acquistai nome tale che il re Enrico terzo mi fece chiamare un giorno, ricercandomi se la memoria che avevo e che professava era naturale o pur per arte magica; al qual diedi satisfazione; e con quello che li dissi e feci provare a lui medesimo, conobbe che non era per arte magica ma per scienza.*  
> “I gained such a reputation that King Henri III summoned me one day, asking whether the memory I possessed and professed was natural or produced by magical art. I satisfied him; from what I told him and had him test himself, he recognized that it was not by magical art but by science.”  
> Venetian interrogation, 30 May 1592; text transmitted in the trial records.

He continues that he published *De umbris idearum*, dedicated it to the king, and became a salaried extraordinary reader.

Status of each element:

- king’s question: **[T]**, Bruno’s sworn retrospective testimony;
- Bruno’s demonstration: **[T/Trad.]**, self-report, not independently described;
- royal position or pension: **[T/R]**, consistent with the publication and later testimony;
- Bruno’s claim that the king accepted it as science: **[Trad.]**, reported only by Bruno;
- a formal professorial chair at the Sorbonne: **[L/M]**, not documented.

## Gohory’s Parisian prehistory

Gohory’s 1550 account of Camillo and his wish for powerful “images and seals” show that this vocabulary circulated in Paris before Bruno.

**[R]** It establishes a receptive milieu, not personal transmission. Gohory died in 1576, before Bruno’s arrival.

## Porta’s Naples

Porta’s Academy of Secrets and works on natural magic, ciphers, physiognomy, optics, theatre, and memory demonstrate that “memory” could coexist with natural magic without itself being presented as ritual sorcery.

Bruno and Porta share a Neapolitan environment and interest in:

- secrecy;
- signs;
- images;
- combinatory writing;
- theatre;
- natural and artificial marvels.

Direct personal collaboration is undocumented.

# People

## Practitioners and transmitters

- **Giordano Bruno:** author, teacher, demonstrator, and self-advertising master.
- **Henri III:** dedicatee and, by Bruno’s testimony, examiner of his memory.
- **Henri d’Angoulême:** dedicatee of *Cantus Circaeus*.
- **Michel de Castelnau, sieur de Mauvissière:** French ambassador in London and Bruno’s host/patron.
- **Giovanni Francesco Mocenigo:** Venetian patrician who sought instruction and denounced Bruno.
- **Giambattista Ciotti:** bookseller involved in transmitting Mocenigo’s invitation.
- **Alexander Dicson:** Scottish follower whose mnemonic works borrow Bruno’s vocabulary and ideas.
- **Raphael Egli and Johann Heinrich Hainzel:** later associates/patrons in the German-speaking lands.
- **Hieronymus Besler:** amanuensis for later magical and philosophical works.

## The king’s test

As above, the episode is documented only through Bruno’s 1592 testimony. Henri asked whether the ability was natural or magical; Bruno says the king tried something himself and accepted “science.”

It is often retold as if court witnesses left a transcript of a spectacular feat. They did not.

## The St-Victor librarian’s note

**[Trad./W]** Guillaume Cotin, librarian of the Abbey of Saint-Victor, recorded conversations with Bruno in 1585–86. These notes are important evidence for Bruno’s Parisian opinions and reputation. They do not provide a complete technical description of the 1582 art. Assertions that Cotin documented a particular superhuman performance need a precise folio citation; without one they are embellishments.

## Mocenigo: student and accuser

Mocenigo invited Bruno to Venice to teach him:

> *li secreti della memoria et li altri che egli professa*  
> “the secrets of memory and the other things he professes.”

When dissatisfied, he confined Bruno and denounced him to the Venetian Inquisition in May 1592.

**[T]** The invitation, teaching dispute, detention, and denunciations are documented in trial papers.

**[R]** Memory teaching caused the fatal encounter.

**[D]** It does not follow that the art of memory was the legal ground on which Bruno was ultimately condemned. The Roman proceedings centered on persistent theological and philosophical propositions.

## Johannes Nostitz

A later pupil remembered Bruno’s ability to speak extensively and extemporaneously on proposed topics. This is **[Trad.]**, a retrospective testimonial, compatible with trained topical memory but not proof that a specific wheel caused the performance. [McIntyre’s early biography reproducing the report](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/46901/old/46901-h/46901-h.htm).

## The auctioneer, professor’s party, and student who lost his memory

The item brief requests these anecdotes, but the dedicated source pass did not identify secure Bruno-specific primary witnesses matching those descriptions.

- **Auctioneer:** probably a displaced anecdote from the wider mnemonic tradition, where professional criers, auctioneers, or dealers memorize sequences; no verified connection to Bruno’s Paris art was found.
- **Professor’s party:** no primary Bruno witness located. It may be a modern retelling of court or university demonstrations.
- **Student who lost his memory:** no identified passage in *De umbris*, *Cantus*, the London *Ars reminiscendi*, or the principal trial documents.
- **Finding:** absence of a traceable witness makes these **[L] pending identification**, not facts to be silently imported.

# The magic question

## Was the art itself called magical?

The best contemporary evidence is Henri III’s reported question and Bruno’s answer: Bruno explicitly distinguished the demonstrated memory from *arte magica* and called it *scienza*.

That does not settle the historian’s question, because “magic” had several meanings:

1. illicit invocation or demonic magic;
2. astral image magic;
3. natural magic based on occult properties;
4. psychological manipulation through images and affects;
5. a universal operative knowledge joining signs to things;
6. a hostile label for unfamiliar expertise.

## Evidence for a magical dimension

- Hermes introduces *De umbris*.
- Circe invokes the planets in *Cantus*.
- Agrippa’s explicitly magical image lists are used.
- Bruno includes Circe, necromancers, Hostanes, and Zoroaster among mnemonic figures.
- His later *De magia*, *De vinculis*, and *De imaginum compositione* give imagination a central operative role.
- In *De vinculis*, the rhetorician who acts upon souls can be treated as a kind of magician or binder.
- Bruno’s cosmology assumes continuity between world, soul, sense, imagination, and intellect.

## Evidence against calling the wheel a talisman

- No instruction says to fabricate the *De umbris* wheel in a prescribed metal.
- No electional time is given for consecrating it.
- No suffumigation, invocation, spirit-name, ritual enclosure, or planetary petition accompanies its use.
- Its explicit examples encode letter groups through agent, action, and insignia.
- The surviving diagrams function as indexing and combinatory schemata.
- Bruno himself denied magical causation when questioned by Henri III, according to his testimony.

## Talismanic?

**Conclusion:** **[D] Philosophically magical; not demonstrated to be a material talisman.**

Agrippa’s source-images could be made as astral talismans in Agrippa’s system. Their reproduction in a memory table does not automatically make the table a talisman.

## Heretical?

No proposition “using loci and images is heresy” appears among the securely known final grounds of condemnation. Memory arts were taught by orthodox authors, including Dominican and Jesuit writers.

Potentially dangerous elements were:

- Bruno’s theological uses of world-soul language;
- his favorable view of Egyptian religion;
- suspicions of magic or divination;
- denial or reinterpretation of central Christian doctrines;
- cosmological and metaphysical positions;
- refusal to recant propositions selected by the Roman tribunal.

The complete final trial dossier is lost; Angelo Mercati’s discovery of a summary document and Luigi Firpo’s reconstruction remain fundamental. Therefore confident one-sentence claims that Bruno was burned “for heliocentrism,” “for memory magic,” or for one isolated doctrine are historically unsound.

## Ars notoria

The *Ars notoria* promised infused knowledge through sacred figures, prayers, divine names, fasting, and ritual observance.

Bruno’s art differs materially:

- it requires training;
- associations are constructed;
- order is learned;
- retrieval is procedural;
- no angelic infusion is promised in the practical rules.

**[Y/R]** Both involve figures and extraordinary knowledge, and church authorities could suspect both. But Bruno’s *Ars memoriae* is not an edition of the *Ars notoria*, and calling it one is **[M]**.

# Afterlife

## Dicson, Perkins, and English controversy

Alexander Dicson adapted Brunian terminology in mnemonic works. William Perkins’s *The Arte of Prophecying* and *A Warning against the Idolatrie of the Last Times* exemplify Reformed suspicion of images imagined as religiously or psychologically dangerous.

The often-quoted sentiment that a thing conceived in imagination may become an idol belongs to this Protestant iconoclastic context. It should not be treated as proof that Perkins had Bruno’s five-wheel diagram before him unless a direct citation is produced.

## Robert Fludd

Fludd’s seventeenth-century memory theatres combine:

- stage architecture;
- zodiacal and celestial classifications;
- technical versus natural places;
- striking human actions.

Yates plausibly places Fludd downstream from the late Renaissance synthesis represented by Camillo and Bruno. Direct borrowing varies by passage and requires textual comparison.

## Emblems, theatres, galleries, and frescoes

Bruno belongs to a long visual order that includes:

- medieval diagrammatic manuscripts;
- moralized virtues and vices;
- Dominican preaching and meditation;
- Giotto-associated and other civic-religious fresco cycles;
- Camillo’s theatre;
- emblem books;
- studioli, Kunstkammern, galleries, and encyclopedic displays.

**Caution:** Similarity of spatial organization is not automatically influence. A fresco cycle is not necessarily a coded Bruno-style memory palace.

## Dante

Yates and later memory scholars connect Dante’s structured otherworld, striking punishments, ordered regions, and moral images with trained medieval memory.

Carruthers makes the broad cultural relationship persuasive. A claim that Dante secretly employed Bruno’s system is impossible chronologically and is **[M]**.

## Universal language and combinatorics

The historically defensible sequence is:

- Llull: finite alphabet of principles and controlled combinations;
- Renaissance Lullism: logical, theological, medical, encyclopedic, and mnemonic adaptations;
- Bruno: rotating combinations integrated with images, phonetic coding, and internal writing;
- seventeenth-century universal-language projects: characters intended to map concepts systematically;
- Leibniz: *characteristica universalis* and *calculus ratiocinator*.

**[R]** Rossi shows a genuine history of problems, books, and transformations.

**[D]** “Lull invented the computer” or “Bruno’s wheel is the first computer” is metaphor, not technological history. There is no continuous engineering lineage from Bruno’s woodcuts to digital hardware.

## Borges, Eco, and literary afterlives

### Borges’s Funes

Funes possesses unlimited particular recall but cannot abstract or generalize. He is almost the negative image of Bruno:

- Bruno seeks ordered compression and meaningful association;
- Funes drowns in unreduced detail.

No direct Bruno citation is necessary for the comparison; it is thematic unless established from Borges’s notes.

### Umberto Eco

Eco’s writings on the search for the perfect language place Llull, Bruno, combinatorics, Cabala, and universal characters in a history of semiotic projects. Eco’s importance is interpretive: he stresses that a universal language may either discover an assumed cosmic order or construct a formal one.

### Modern “memory palaces”

The contemporary memory-palace revival usually employs:

- familiar routes;
- person-action-object images;
- phonetic number systems;
- spaced review;
- competition disciplines.

These resemble the classical architectural core and, in person/action/object compression, sometimes resemble Bruno’s multi-component images.

But modern athletes rarely reproduce Bruno’s 150-part mythographic wheels. Claims that championship systems are “Bruno’s exact method” are **[M]** unless a practitioner documents that use.

# Controversies and disputes

## Contested-material audit

### Was the Parisian art a practical memory system?

- **Evidence for:** explicit rules, tables, letter values, examples, retrieval structures, applications.
- **Evidence against a purely practical reading:** metaphysical preliminaries and ambitious claims for all faculties.
- **Finding:** yes, practical, but not merely practical.

### Was it magical?

- **Yates:** yes, its effective center was astral magic.
- **Sturlese/Torchia:** the printed wheel is a phonetic mnemonic; Yates misconstructed it.
- **Clucas/Farinella/Mertens:** technical correction does not eliminate magical psychology.
- **Finding:** magical at some philosophical and contextual levels; unproved as a consecrated talismanic machine.

### Did Bruno copy Agrippa?

- **Documented:** close verbal and iconographic correspondence in celestial-image lists.
- **Dispute:** “copy” can obscure Agrippa’s own dependence on earlier sources.
- **Finding:** Agrippa is a proximate printed source; the tradition is older.

### Did Bruno inherit a secret Dominican art?

- **Yates:** possible Albertist development at Naples.
- **Evidence:** Dominican education and the order’s long interest in memory and imagination.
- **Missing:** a manuscript, teacher’s record, or convent syllabus transmitting Bruno’s wheel.
- **Finding:** plausible background, not established transmission.

### Did Henri III employ Bruno because of a miraculous feat?

- **Evidence:** Bruno’s retrospective testimony.
- **Missing:** court record describing the experiment.
- **Finding:** the audience and favor are credible; “miracle” is legend.

### Was Bruno a Sorbonne professor?

- **Evidence:** he called himself extraordinary reader and received royal support.
- **Missing:** ordinary faculty appointment.
- **Finding:** “professor at the Sorbonne” is misleading.

### Was the London book printed as one planned volume?

- Separate signatures, paper, types, and inferred printers suggest separately produced units.
- Surviving bindings combine them.
- Exact order of printing remains reconstructed.
- **Finding:** composite publication history.

### Was *Ars reminiscendi* a new 1583 work?

No. Its core is the practical second portion of the 1582 *Cantus*, republished with variants and a new paratextual function.

### Was Garzoni’s relevant book published in 1578?

No verified copy supports 1578. The first *Piazza universale* is Venice, 1585. This is a catalogue error.

### Did Bruno influence Leibniz directly?

- Leibniz knew Lullian and combinatory traditions.
- Bruno formed part of the early-modern archive of such arts.
- A direct causal chain from Bruno’s mnemonic wheels to Leibniz’s mature logic is not established by resemblance alone.
- **Finding:** mediated intellectual genealogy, not proven direct technical dependence.

### Was Bruno executed for being a magician or memory artist?

- Mocenigo accused him partly in a context of frustrated memory instruction and alleged magic.
- The Roman case concerned multiple theological propositions and recantation.
- The complete final record is lost.
- **Finding:** memory teaching occasioned the arrest; it is not demonstrated as the decisive capital charge.

### Did Bruno invent the memory wheel?

No. Rotating disks precede him in Llullian arts, astronomical volvelles, cipher systems, and other diagrammatic traditions. Bruno’s innovation is their unusually elaborate conjunction with mythographic images, internal writing, and a general psychology.

# Open questions

1. How exactly were all five 150-part wheels meant to be synchronized in continuous practice?
2. Did practitioners construct physical volvelles, or were the printed figures chiefly aids to internal visualization?
3. How many pupils used the complete system rather than simplified architectural rules?
4. Which celestial lists came directly from Agrippa and which from intermediaries or independent Teucer/Picatrix traditions?
5. What was contained in the lost or unfinished *Clavis magna*?
6. How much of Bruno’s system was formulated before he left Naples?
7. Did Henri III’s “test” concern recall, language acquisition, topical invention, or a demonstration with a prepared subject?
8. Which London copies preserve the earliest state and original order of the separately printed units?
9. How should *magic* be distributed among causal claim, philosophical vocabulary, dramatic framing, social suspicion, and later historiography?
10. Can the thirty associative procedures in *Ars reminiscendi* be mapped exactly across all surviving 1582 and 1583 copies?
11. Are the “auctioneer,” “professor’s party,” and “student who lost his memory” stories corrupt descendants of identifiable Renaissance anecdotes?
12. How far did Dicson, Fludd, or later universal-language writers know Bruno directly rather than through a shared mnemonic-Lullian environment?
13. How should modern reconstructions handle errors in the nineteenth-century edition and damaged or absent woodcuts?
14. Can experimental reconstruction establish whether the system’s practical burden is manageable, without mistaking present-day usability for historical intention?
