# Descartes's reform of the art of memory: Research Dossier

## Basic identification

| Field | Identification |
|---|---|
| **Name** | Descartes’s “true art of memory” (*vera ars memoriae*), more accurately a pair of proposals for replacing or reforming contemporary artificial memory |
| **Exponent** | René Descartes (1596–1650) |
| **Date** | Usually assigned to 1619–1621 or, more cautiously, to the notebook used during 1619–1622; the individual memory note is undated |
| **Principal source** | The fragment conventionally called *Cogitationes privatae*, in René Descartes, *Œuvres*, ed. Charles Adam and Paul Tannery, vol. X, p. 230 (“Perlegens Lamberti Schenkelii lucrosas nugas …”) |
| **Related sources** | The lost *Studium bonae mentis*, known through Adrien Baillet and printed as fragments in AT X, 191–204, especially 200–201; letters to Isaac Beeckman of 26 March and 29 April 1619, AT X, 156–157 and 164–166; *Discours de la méthode*, part II, AT VI, 17–19; *Regulae ad directionem ingenii*, especially Rules VI–VII and XII–XVI, AT X, 379–387, 407–459 |
| **Immediate stimulus** | Lambert Thomas Schenkel’s printed art of memory, probably the *De memoria*/*De arte memoriae* material later incorporated into his *Gazophylacium artis memoriae* |
| **Principle in one sentence** | Instead of filling an arbitrary succession of mnemonic places with independent images, retain causes or relationally connected images from which the forgotten items can be regenerated in their proper order. |
| **Yates** | Frances A. Yates, *The Art of Memory* (1966), chapter XVII, pp. 373–375, with Schenkel treated earlier at pp. 299–302 |
| **Catalogue status** | “System 43 of 45” is a modern catalogue classification, not a designation used by Descartes or an early-modern source. |

### Evidentiary labels used below

- **[D] Documented:** directly stated in a surviving primary text or securely transmitted early witness.
- **[R] Reconstruction:** an inference made by editors or scholars from incomplete evidence.
- **[T] Tradition:** a report transmitted by an early biographer, disciple, critic, or institutional record, but not directly preserved in the author’s autograph.
- **[Y] Yates:** Frances Yates’s interpretation.
- **[X] Disputed:** a claim for which serious alternatives exist.
- **[L] Legend:** an anecdote or attribution whose documentary support is inadequate.
- **[M] Modern invention:** a recent elaboration presented as historical although absent from the early evidence.

The central conclusion should be stated at once: **Descartes never left a complete mnemonic system comparable to the *Ad Herennium*, Camillo’s Theatre, Bruno’s wheels, or Fludd’s theatres.** What survives is one compressed notebook entry offering two reforms, together with later writings that clarify why he increasingly substituted causal deduction, serial order, mathematical notation, and external writing for classical local memory. Any reconstruction more elaborate than that is necessarily conjectural.

## The system in detail

### 1. The surviving text

The primary passage is AT X, 230. In the spelling of the standard edition:

> **Perlegens Lamberti Schenkelii lucrosas nugas (lib. De arte memoriæ) cogitavi facile me omnia quæ detexi imaginatione complecti: quod fit per reductionem rerum ad causas; quæ omnes cum ad unam tandem reducantur, patet nulla opus esse memoria ad scientias omnes. Qui enim intelliget causas, elapsa omnino phantasmata causæ impressione rursus facile in cerebro formabit. Quæ vera est ars memoriæ, illius nebulonis arti plane contraria: non quod illa effectu careat, sed quod chartam melioribus occupandam totam requirat et in ordine non recto consistat: qui ordo in eo est, ut imagines ab invicem dependentes efformentur. Hoc ille omittit, nescio an consulto, quod est clavis totius mysterii.**

A close English gloss is:

> “While reading Lambert Schenkel’s profitable trifles—or lucrative nonsense—in the book *On the Art of Memory*, I thought that I could easily embrace in imagination everything I had discovered. This is done by reducing things to causes; since all these are ultimately reduced to one, it is evident that no memory is needed for all the sciences. For whoever understands the causes will readily form again in the brain, through the impression of the cause, phantasms that have completely vanished. This is the true art of memory, wholly opposed to the art of that muddle-headed fellow: not because his art lacks effect, but because it requires the whole sheet, which ought to be occupied by better things, and consists in an order that is not right. The right order consists in images being formed as dependent upon one another. He omits this—I do not know whether deliberately—which is the key to the whole mystery.”

The note continues:

> **Ipse excogitavi alium modum, si ex imaginibus rerum non inconnexarum addiscantur novæ imagines omnibus communes, vel saltem si ex omnibus simul una fiat una imago, nec solum habeatur respectus ad proximam, sed etiam ad alias, ut quinta respiciat 1º per hastam humi projectam, medium vero per scalam ex qua descendent, et secunda per telum quod ad illam projiciat, et tertia simili aliqua ratione in rationem significationis vel veræ vel fictitiæ.**

Gloss:

> “I myself devised another method: if, from the images of things that are not unconnected, new images common to them all are additionally learned—or at least if one single image is made simultaneously out of them all—then a relation will be had not only to the nearest image but also to the others: thus the fifth may refer to the first through a spear cast upon the ground, the middle through a ladder from which they descend, the second through a missile which it casts at the other, and the third through some similar relation, according to a meaning either real or fictitious.”

**[D]** This is the entire surviving explicit description of Descartes’s reformed art. It is not a chapter-length exposition and gives no finished inventory of places, alphabet, fixed image vocabulary, training regimen, or complete worked demonstration.

### 2. Two proposals, not necessarily one completed apparatus

#### Proposal A: causal regeneration

The first proposal proceeds as follows:

1. What has been discovered is grasped in imagination.
2. Things are reduced to their causes.
3. Causes are themselves reduced toward one cause or principle.
4. An understood cause leaves an “impression.”
5. From that impression, vanished *phantasmata* can be formed again in the brain.
6. Therefore science need not be retained as a mass of separately deposited items.

**[D]** Descartes explicitly calls this *vera ars memoriae*.

**[R]** The best reconstruction is that retrieval runs from a retained intelligible or imaginatively represented cause to its dependent effects. One does not “walk” through architectural loci; one reconstructs the chain. The order is intrinsic or explanatory rather than merely imposed.

**[X]** What “all are ultimately reduced to one” means is uncertain. Possible readings include:

- one first cause;
- one principle within a science;
- a unified method or *mathesis universalis*;
- the unity of interconnected sciences;
- the proportional or mathematical foundations of phenomena.

Nothing in the note identifies the ultimate “one” with a Hermetic One, the divine mind, Camillo’s supercelestial causes, or Bruno’s central principles.

**[Y]** Yates understood this as “curiously like a rationalisation of occult memory”: occult systems likewise reduced multiplicity to supposed causes whose impressed images organized subordinate images.

**[X]** Later scholarship has generally accepted the resemblance as suggestive but not as evidence of direct descent. Dennis Sepper’s reconstruction connects the passage more specifically with Descartes’s early work on imagination, proportion, mechanics, and mathematical figures. On that reading, the *Regulae* later replace arbitrary mnemonic images with lines, rectangles, and relational notation from which a proof can be reproduced.

#### Proposal B: common and cross-linked images

Descartes’s “other method” appears to concern a set of at least five items or images.

Instead of linking only item 1 to item 2, item 2 to item 3, and so forth, the mnemonist constructs:

- images common to several related things; or
- one composite image containing all of them;
- cross-relations between nonadjacent components.

The fragment names three connectors:

- a spear cast on the ground connecting the fifth with the first;
- a ladder from which the middle figures descend;
- a missile or arrow connecting the second with another figure.

The third is to be connected in a comparable way. The relation may be “real or fictitious.”

**[D]** These are the only concrete mnemonic images Descartes supplies.

**[R]** Retrieval probably begins with the composite image or any strongly marked member of it; the actions and spatial relations then cue other members. The design is more like a mnemonic network than a simple one-way chain.

**[R]** The passage may envisage five persons or agents incorporated into a single animated scene. The plural *descendent* suggests figures descending from the ladder. The syntax is compressed enough that the exact staging cannot be recovered.

**[D]** The connective significance need not reproduce a real causal relation. Descartes explicitly allows a signification “real or fictitious.”

**[Y]** Yates correctly observes that common, composite, interacting, and grotesquely active images are not novel. Classical and Renaissance manuals repeatedly recommend action, collision, extraordinary appearance, and association between mnemonic actors.

### 3. What serves as places?

There is no secure evidence that Descartes’s reform employs a palace, theatre, house, zodiac, or fixed architectural background.

- In the first proposal, the functional “places” are positions in a causal order.
- In the second, relative positions within a single composite scene provide order.
- Later Cartesian practice uses paper, algebraic signs, letters, lines, rectangles, and diagrams as external supports.

**[D]** The notebook criticizes Schenkel’s art for requiring *totam chartam*.

**Correction to Yates:** Yates rendered this as filling “the whole space.” But *charta* normally means sheet, paper, writing surface, map, or tablet—not “space” in the metaphysical or architectural sense. Dennis Sepper explicitly identifies “space” as Yates’s suggestion and stresses the literal sheet or tablet. This matters because the complaint can be read as one about representational economy: Schenkel’s multiplicity occupies the available writing or mnemonic surface that could hold more useful relations.

**[X]** It remains possible that Descartes uses *charta* metaphorically for the internal mnemonic field. But that should be argued, not silently assumed.

### 4. What serves as images?

The vocabulary is unstable:

- *imaginatio*: the faculty or activity by which discoveries are embraced;
- *phantasmata*: brain-dependent images capable of vanishing and being formed again;
- *imagines*: constructed mnemonic representations;
- causal “impression”: what allows a phantasm to be regenerated.

The images are therefore neither simply Platonic Ideas nor necessarily pictures of physical objects. They can be:

- representations of things;
- representations of relationships;
- composite scenes;
- figures or signs;
- in the later *Regulae*, mathematical diagrams.

**[D]** In Rule XII of the *Regulae*, Descartes says that one and the same knowing power is called remembering when it applies itself to imagination “clothed in diverse figures”:

> **Atque una et eadem est vis, quæ, si applicet se cum imaginatione ad sensum communem, dicitur videre, tangere, etc.; si ad imaginationem solam ut diversis figuris indutam, dicitur reminisci; si ad eamdem ut novas fingat, dicitur imaginari vel concipere; si denique sola agat, dicitur intelligere.**  
> —AT X, 416.

Gloss: the same power sees when applied with imagination to common sense, remembers when applied to imagination bearing previously formed figures, imagines when it makes new ones, and understands when acting alone.

This later psychology confirms that the early note’s images belong primarily to embodied imagination.

### 5. How order is fixed

Three kinds of order should be distinguished.

#### Classical local order

A stable series of loci supplies an arbitrary but reliable sequence. Retrieval is a mental tour.

#### Schenkel’s order

Schenkel taught conventional images, spatial arrangements, and routinized associations, although recent research cautions against reducing his practice to a purely architectural palace. His itinerant instruction included demonstrations, pedagogical exercises, and a partly proprietary “secret.”

#### Cartesian order

Descartes objects that Schenkel’s order is *non rectus*. The correct order is one in which images “are formed as dependent upon one another.”

That dependence may be:

- causal;
- logical;
- proportional;
- compositional;
- narratively or fictionally represented.

**[D]** Dependence, not mere proximity, is the expressed criterion.

**[R]** The later Cartesian method provides the best analogy: divide a difficulty; identify the simplest elements; arrange them in a series; pass gradually from the simple to the complex; and make enumerations sufficiently complete. In *Discours* II:

> **Le troisième, de conduire par ordre mes pensées, en commençant par les objets les plus simples et les plus aisés à connaître, pour monter peu à peu, comme par degrés, jusques à la connaissance des plus composés; et supposant même de l’ordre entre ceux qui ne se précèdent point naturellement les uns les autres.**

The “as if by degrees” language has a mnemonic resonance, but the passage describes methodical reasoning, not a secret memory palace.

### 6. Retrieval

The causal system retrieves by reconstruction:

```text
retained cause or principle
          ↓
recognized dependence
          ↓
re-formation of subordinate phantasms
          ↓
reproduction of the scientific sequence
```

The composite-image system retrieves by associative traversal:

```text
common image or any marked member
       ↙       ↓       ↘
   spear     ladder    missile
      ↓         ↓         ↓
 nonadjacent members of the same set
```

The first minimizes storage; the second multiplies retrieval routes.

### 7. Rules that can actually be extracted

Only the following rules are textually warranted:

1. Reduce things to their causes.
2. Reduce the multiplicity of causes as far as possible.
3. Understand rather than merely deposit.
4. Form images in dependence upon one another.
5. Prefer a common image for related things.
6. If possible, make one composite image of the whole set.
7. Connect distant as well as adjacent members.
8. Use actions or instruments—spear, ladder, missile—to mark relations.
9. A connector may signify a real relation or a fictitious one.
10. Do not expend the entire mnemonic or graphic field on an excessive multiplicity of independent items.

Rules concerning the size, lighting, spacing, privacy, number, or architectural character of loci are absent.

### 8. Diagrams, wheels, tables, and plates

**[D: negative finding]** No diagram, wheel, table, or plate belonging to Descartes’s “true art of memory” survives.

The notebook gives a verbal miniature—the five-part spear/ladder/missile scene—but neither Leibniz’s transmitted text nor Adam–Tannery prints a corresponding figure.

The geometric figures elsewhere in the youthful notes and *Regulae* are relevant analogues, not surviving diagrams of this mnemonic scheme.

### 9. Comparison with the requested canonical examples

There is no Cartesian equivalent of:

- the *Ad Herennium* lawsuit image;
- Camillo’s seven grades and theatrical gates;
- Bruno’s concentric mnemonic wheels;
- Fludd’s architectural stages.

Descartes’s spear–ladder–missile example is much shorter and lacks assigned memoranda. We cannot say what the five items were.

## The sources

## 1. The youthful notebook and its transmission

### The lost autograph

An inventory made at Stockholm after Descartes’s death mentions a small parchment register, commonly called notebook “C.” Early descriptions say that it could be used from both ends and contained materials under rubrics such as:

- *Parnassus*;
- *Olympica*;
- *Experimenta*;
- *Praeambula*;
- *Democritica*.

**[T]** Adrien Baillet saw or had access to papers derived from this material when preparing *La Vie de Monsieur Des-Cartes* (1691).

**[D/T]** Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz copied excerpts in Paris in June 1676. The dates 1 and 5 June occur in the transmitted record.

**[D]** The Cartesian autograph is lost.

**[D]** Leibniz’s intermediary copy used by Foucher de Careil is also now regarded as lost.

Consequently, the text at AT X, 230 descends through at least this chain:

```text
Descartes’s notebook
        ↓
Leibniz’s partial copy or extracts, 1676
        ↓
Foucher de Careil’s nineteenth-century transcription
        ↓
Adam–Tannery, vol. X
```

This is not a normal manuscript tradition in which several independent witnesses can be collated.

### The title *Cogitationes privatae*

**[X]** The title is probably not Descartes’s. Adam–Tannery notes that Foucher de Careil placed it over the fragments and that editorial invention is the more likely explanation.

Foucher de Careil first published the material in:

- Louis-Alexandre Foucher de Careil, *Œuvres inédites de Descartes, précédées d’une introduction sur la méthode*, 2 vols., Paris: Ladrange, 1859–1860.

The standard edition is:

- René Descartes, *Œuvres*, ed. Charles Adam and Paul Tannery, vol. X, Paris: Léopold Cerf, 1908, pp. 213–256; memory note at p. 230.
- Reissued in the revised Vrin–CNRS presentation, 1964–1974.

A modern critical witness to Leibniz’s Cartesian extracts appears in the Akademie-Ausgabe of Leibniz, series VII, vol. 8, no. 22, “Cartesii cogitationes privatae,” pp. 113–114 for the memory passage in the advance edition.

### Variants

There is no rich manuscript stemma. The major “variants” are editorial:

- spelling normalization;
- punctuation;
- Foucher de Careil’s title and ordering;
- translations of *lucrosas nugas*, *nebulonis*, and *chartam*;
- uncertainty about where Descartes’s various notebook rubrics began and ended.

Notable translation choices include:

- *lucrosas nugas*: “profitable trifles,” “lucrative nonsense,” or “money-making nonsense”;
- *nebulonis*: “muddle-headed fellow,” “rogue,” “charlatan,” or “nebulous fellow”;
- *chartam*: “sheet/paper/tablet,” rendered by Yates as “space”;
- *hastam* and *telum*: spear and projectile; some English accounts make the second specifically an arrow;
- *addiscantur*: “are additionally learned,” “are acquired,” or “are devised.”

### A parallel late copy or reformulation

AT XI, 649 records a related formulation:

> **Ars vera memoriæ est res per suas causas nosse; qui enim intelligit causas rerum, etiamsi res ipsæ elapsæ sint sive illarum phantasmata, facile denuo ab intellectu in cerebro nostro, per causæ impressionem formabuntur.**

Gloss:

> “The true art of memory is to know things through their causes; for whoever understands the causes of things, even if the things themselves or their phantasms have slipped away, will easily have them formed anew by the intellect in our brain through the impression of the cause.”

This supports the causal reading but does not add operational detail.

## 2. *Studium bonae mentis*

The *Studium bonae mentis* is lost. Baillet describes it as a youthful treatise about the desire for knowledge, the sciences, the mental dispositions required for learning, and the proper order of acquiring wisdom.

**[T]** Our sole substantial source is Baillet’s paraphrase and excerpts.

**[R/X]** Its date is variously placed in the early or middle 1620s. Assertions that it is precisely contemporaneous with the 1619 memory note are not secure.

In the fragments printed at AT X, 200–201, Baillet reports that Descartes:

- treated memory among the mental dispositions relevant to learning;
- doubted whether memory was truly distinct from understanding and imagination;
- distinguished intellectual from corporeal memory;
- held intellectual memory incapable of simple artificial augmentation in the manner of bodily memory;
- had not cultivated local memory extensively during his retreat.

Because the original is missing, Baillet’s wording must not automatically be quoted as Descartes’s verbatim French or Latin.

**Correction to Yates:** Her statement that Descartes “regarded” local memory as corporeal and outside us compresses different sources. The fullest surviving direct Cartesian statement about local memory being “outside us” occurs much later, in the letter to Meyssonnier discussed below; the *Studium* evidence comes through Baillet.

## 3. The Beeckman letters of 1619

### 26 March 1619: not Lull’s *ars brevis*

Descartes tells Beeckman that he is investigating a new science capable of solving questions concerning quantity:

> **… non Lullii Artem brevem, sed scientiam penitus novam …**

The precise continuation varies in translation, but the point is secure: Descartes distinguishes his project from the Lullian Short Art.

**[D]** This letter concerns mathematical method, not an exposition of the memory note.

### 29 April 1619: the Dordrecht Lullist

Descartes reports meeting at a Dordrecht inn a learned man who claimed to use Lull’s *Ars parva* so effectively that he could speak for twenty consecutive hours on any subject, always producing new material. Descartes asked whether the art consisted merely in arranging the conventional logical headings—subject, predicate, genera, species, whole, part, cause, effect, and similar topics. The man answered that it contained more subtle “keys” allegedly omitted from published explanations.

Descartes asked Beeckman to examine Agrippa’s commentary and look for the keys.

**[D]** In April 1619 Descartes was curious enough about Lullism to investigate it rather than simply dismiss it.

**[D]** This is not evidence that he became a Lullist.

**[R]** The anecdote supplies a plausible background for the memory note’s language of the “key to the whole mystery.” Secretive mnemonic teachers and Lullists both advertised concealed keys.

## 4. *Discours de la méthode*, part II

Published at Leiden by Jan Maire in 1637, part II includes the famous dismissal:

> **… pour la Logique, ses syllogismes et la plupart de ses autres instructions servent plutôt à expliquer à autrui les choses qu’on sait, ou même, comme l’art de Lulle, à parler, sans jugement, de celles qu’on ignore, qu’à les apprendre.**  
> —AT VI, 17.

Gloss: syllogisms and Lull’s art are better at expounding or talking about things than at discovering them; the Lullian machine permits speech without judgment concerning what one does not know.

Descartes then gives the four rules:

1. accept only what is clear and indubitable;
2. divide difficulties;
3. conduct thoughts in order from simple to complex;
4. make complete enumerations and general reviews.

**[D]** The third and fourth rules retain the memory tradition’s concern with order, division, completeness, and review.

**[R]** Calling the four rules an “art of memory” would nevertheless be misleading. Their explicit end is discovery and secure judgment.

## 5. The *Regulae*

The unfinished *Regulae ad directionem ingenii* was composed in stages, usually placed in the 1620s, with substantial portions often dated around 1627–1628. It was first published posthumously in Latin in *Opuscula posthuma* (Amsterdam, 1701), after a Dutch translation in 1684.

Relevant developments include:

- Rule VI: ordering things in series;
- Rule VII: continuous movement of thought and sufficient enumeration;
- Rule XII: cooperation of intellect, imagination, sense, and memory;
- Rules XIV–XVI: representation of relations by lines, figures, and abbreviated notation;
- the recommendation that not everything be retained internally when external marks can preserve it.

**[R]** Sepper reads these techniques as the mature transformation of the Schenkel note: arbitrary bows, scales, and dramatic agents give way to exactly reproducible geometrical figures.

This is an illuminating reconstruction, not an explicit cross-reference by Descartes.

## 6. Later texts on memory

### Letter to Mersenne, 17 June 1640

Descartes distinguishes bodily traces or folds in the brain from intellectual memory:

> **La mémoire intellectuelle a ses espèces à part qui ne dépendent nullement de ces plis.**

**[D]** He recognizes an intellectual memory independent of brain folds.

### Letter to Huygens, 13 October 1642

Descartes says that he finds in us an intellectual memory independent of the body and accordingly hopes for remembrance after death.

### Letter to Meyssonnier, 29 January 1640

Descartes argues that bodily memory is not confined to a single cerebral location and invokes local memory:

> **… tout ce qu’on nomme mémoire locale est hors de nous; en sorte que lorsque nous avons lu quelque livre, toutes les espèces qui peuvent servir à nous faire souvenir de ce qui est dedans ne sont pas en notre cerveau, mais il y en a aussi plusieurs dans le papier de l’exemplaire que nous avons lu … Mais, outre cette mémoire, qui dépend du corps, j’en reconnais encore une autre, du tout intellectuelle, qui ne dépend que de l’âme seule.**

This does not mean that an imagined palace literally exists outside the mind. His example concerns distributed cues: the visible arrangement on the paper of the particular copy read can help prompt recollection.

**Correction to Yates:** “Local memory is outside us” is not simply a metaphysical dismissal of the art. It forms part of an argument that embodied remembering may be distributed across brain, nerves, trained muscles, and environmental supports. Recent Cartesian memory scholarship treats this as more subtle than Yates’s “singularly crude idea.”

### Letter to Arnauld, 29 July 1648

Descartes distinguishes having a thought from recognizing it as one previously had. Intellectual memory enables the mind to reproduce an act and judge it to have occurred before.

**[D]** His later theory of personal and intellectual memory extends well beyond the 1619 mnemonic reform, but it confirms that memory cannot be reduced to static cerebral pictures.

## Yates’s account and the scholarship since

## 1. What Yates argues

In chapter XVII, “The Art of Memory and the Growth of Scientific Method,” Yates places Descartes after Renaissance occult memory, Ramism, Fludd, and Bacon.

Her principal claims are:

1. **[Y]** Descartes knew contemporary artificial memory through Schenkel.
2. **[Y]** He proposed reducing things to causes and organizing images through mutual dependence.
3. **[Y]** The causal proposal is new and “curiously like a rationalisation of occult memory.”
4. **[Y]** The common and interacting images are traditional mnemonic devices.
5. **[Y]** He probably did not use local memory much.
6. **[Y]** He treated local memory as corporeal and external, in contrast to intellectual memory.
7. **[Y]** His new science was not Lull’s Short Art but a science concerning quantity.
8. **[Y]** Nevertheless, his and Bacon’s concern with order belongs to the historical transformation of memory arts into scientific method.

Yates’s exact interpretive formulation is:

> “Curiously enough, Descartes’s suggested reform of memory is nearer to ‘occult’ principles than Bacon’s, for occult memory does reduce all things to their supposed causes whose images when impressed on memory are believed to organise the subsidiary images.”

She adds:

> “His brilliant new idea of organising memory on causes sounds curiously like a rationalisation of occult memory. His other notions about forming connected images are far from new and can be found in some form in nearly every text-book.”

And:

> “It seems unlikely that Descartes made much use of local memory …”

## 2. What has been confirmed

### Descartes’s engagement with mnemonic culture

Confirmed. The Schenkel note is unambiguous; the Lull letters show active investigation in 1619; the *Discours* later rejects Lullian verbal productivity without judgment.

### Order as the connecting problem

Broadly confirmed. Rossi, Yates, Sepper, and more recent work all identify a transition from storage to ordered generation. The *Regulae* and *Discours* make order, dependence, division, series, and review central.

### The traditional character of the composite images

Confirmed. Spearing, descending, striking, and combining figures belong to standard imagistic mnemonics. Nothing proves that Descartes invented those elementary devices.

### Memory and the rise of method

Confirmed in a limited genealogical sense. The same early-modern culture treated memory, dialectic, topical invention, encyclopedic ordering, teaching, and discovery as overlapping problems.

Not confirmed is the stronger deterministic claim that modern scientific method simply developed out of occult artificial memory.

## 3. Corrections and qualifications

### *Charta* is not securely “space”

Dennis Sepper’s philological correction is the most concrete correction to Yates’s rendering. *Totam chartam* means the whole sheet or tablet. “Space” encourages a more occult or architectural interpretation than the Latin requires.

### Schenkel is not merely a representative of a single occult tradition

Recent work on Schenkel as an itinerant teacher emphasizes:

- institutional examinations and teaching permissions;
- demonstrations before scholars and officials;
- commercial secrecy;
- printed certificates and testimonials;
- practical pedagogy;
- linguistic and educational applications.

His “mystery” could be a trade secret without being occult magic.

### Descartes’s psychology of memory is not adequately described as crude

Xavier Kieft and work by John Sutton, Stephen Gaukroger, and others distinguish:

- cerebral traces;
- motor and bodily habit;
- external retrieval cues;
- intellectual recognition;
- memory within the union of mind and body.

Yates’s few sentences telescope these layers.

### No direct line from Camillo, Bruno, or Fludd has been demonstrated

Yates’s comparison with occult causal images is typological. No surviving letter, library list, marginal note, or citation shows Descartes reading Camillo, Bruno’s mnemonic works, or Fludd’s memory treatises before writing AT X, 230.

### The second proposal may be more relational than Yates allows

Sepper reads the composite image as evidence for Descartes’s early “proportionalizing imagination.” The essential innovation may not be striking imagery but multidirectional representation of relations.

## 4. Rossi

Paolo Rossi’s *Clavis universalis*, later translated as *Logic and the Art of Memory: The Quest for a Universal Language*, treated the Descartes passage before or alongside Yates’s interpretation. Rossi stresses continuity between mnemonic principles of order and seventeenth-century method.

Rossi’s contribution is important because he situates Descartes in a history extending from Lullian combination and artificial memory to universal language and Leibniz.

**[R]** Rossi’s conclusion that memory principles helped shape Cartesian method is persuasive as contextual genealogy.

**[X]** It does not establish that the four rules of the *Discours* were derived from any identifiable memory manual.

## 5. Carruthers

Mary Carruthers’s *The Book of Memory* and *The Craft of Thought* do not chiefly revise this particular Descartes note. Their importance is methodological:

- medieval *memoria* was an instrument of composition and thought, not mere rote storage;
- images operated like cognitive writing;
- division, *brevitas*, order, and emotionally forceful images assisted invention and judgment;
- manuscript page layout and memory practice were interconnected.

Applied to Descartes, this weakens any clean opposition between “medieval memory” and “modern thought.” Descartes transforms an already generative craft.

## 6. Bolzoni and Stagno

Lina Bolzoni’s studies of the Renaissance gallery, theatre, preaching, literary imagery, and image-text relations likewise do not document a direct Camillo–Descartes transmission. They demonstrate that mnemonic images inhabited courts, academies, printed books, sermons, galleries, emblematic culture, and literary composition.

**[Correction to broad Yatesian narratives]** Renaissance memory practices were more socially and textually diverse than a single ascent from classical loci to Hermetic magic.

Stagno’s Camillo scholarship sharpens the study of manuscripts, editions, and the actual organization of the Theatre. It supplies no documentary evidence that Descartes borrowed Camillo’s grades or planetary gates.

## 7. Small and Hajdu

Jocelyn Penny Small’s *Wax Tablets of the Mind* emphasizes the interaction of literacy, cognition, and material writing in antiquity. This is particularly relevant to *charta*: external writing and internal memory should not be treated as simple opposites.

Helga Hajdu’s *Das mnemotechnische Schrifttum des Mittelalters* remains a foundational bibliography and survey. It predates Yates and helps identify the long technical tradition behind Schenkel. It does not establish an occult source for Descartes.

## 8. Lull scholarship: Bonner and Fidora

Anthony Bonner and Alexander Fidora have reconstructed Lull’s Art as a changing demonstrative and combinatorial system rooted in Lull’s theology, logical relations, divine dignities, and missionary program.

Their work corrects the habit of treating Lull primarily as a mnemonist or universal talking machine.

Therefore:

- Descartes’s innkeeper’s claim represents early-modern pedagogical Lullism, not necessarily Lull’s own aim.
- The *Discours* attacks a received “art of Lull” capable of producing discourse without knowledge.
- Descartes’s causal reduction is not itself Lullian merely because both projects seek unity and combinatorial order.

## 9. Bruno scholarship: Sturlese, Ciliberto, Clucas, and Gatti

Post-Yates Bruno scholarship has generally insisted on closer separation of:

- mnemonic technique;
- logic and combinatorics;
- ontology and psychology;
- Ficinian astral magic;
- talismanic operation;
- the metaphysics distinctive to Bruno.

Rita Sturlese in particular resists identifying Bruno’s mnemonic writings wholesale with talismanic magic. Stephen Clucas’s work on Alexander Dicson and Walter Warner highlights notation, characterization, and the storage of discourse. Hilary Gatti and Michele Ciliberto place Bruno’s arts within natural philosophy, reform, and intellectual conflict.

For Descartes, the result is negative but important: resemblance to “Brunian causal images” cannot substitute for evidence of textual contact.

## 10. Ramus and Ong

Walter J. Ong showed that Ramist method relied upon spatialized dichotomies, curricular order, and visual page design. Ramism did not simply abolish artificial memory; it displaced mnemonic work into diagrammed dialectical order.

Descartes’s series and reductions belong to a culture already saturated with such pedagogical ordering, even though his method is neither straightforwardly Ramist nor reducible to branching dichotomies.

## 11. Yates’s later revision

In *The Rosicrucian Enlightenment* (1972) and her 1979 essay “The Fear of the Occult,” Yates broadened the context. She asked whether fear of association with magicians, witches, and Rosicrucians affected the public presentation of early modern science.

She did not produce evidence that Descartes’s memory note was magical. Her later position is best described as a question about intellectual climate and strategic distancing.

## History and context

## 1. Classical rhetoric

The artificial memory inherited by Schenkel descends principally from:

- *Rhetorica ad Herennium* III.16–24;
- Cicero, *De oratore* II.350–360;
- Quintilian, *Institutio oratoria* XI.2.

The classical model compares places to writing tablets and images to letters. Order is supplied by an architectural itinerary.

Descartes preserves images, impression, order, and retrieval while attacking arbitrary sequential storage.

## 2. Scholastic psychology

Aristotle’s *De memoria et reminiscentia* and its medieval commentaries treated recollection as movement through an ordered succession: from one presentation, the soul proceeds to another by similarity, opposition, proximity, or causal relation.

Aquinas connected memory with prudence and recommended suitable similitudes, order, solicitude, and frequent meditation.

**[R]** Descartes’s causal passage can be situated within this tradition without requiring occult mediation. The movement from cause to effect was already a recognized route of recollection.

His vocabulary of *phantasmata*, impressions, brain, imagination, and intellect remains continuous with scholastic psychological problems even as his later dualism reorganizes them.

## 3. Jesuit schooling and La Flèche

Descartes studied rhetoric, philosophy, mathematics, and scholastic disputation at the Jesuit college of La Flèche. Classical rhetoric included *memoria* as one of the five parts of oratory, though no surviving school record proves that he was taught a particular Schenkelian course there.

**[R]** La Flèche supplied the rhetorical and scholastic vocabulary that made Schenkel immediately intelligible.

## 4. Courts, universities, and itinerant mnemonic teaching

Schenkel operated as a traveling professor. He sought authorization and testimonials in cities and universities, offered public demonstrations, and sold instruction while protecting parts of his method.

This institutional setting explains Descartes’s contemptuous *lucrosas nugas*: it can mean that Schenkel’s trifles were profitable to their seller.

Theological faculties and civic authorities were asked to judge whether such performances were natural, fraudulent, or illicit.

## 5. Lullism

Lullian arts offered finite alphabets and combinatorial figures for investigating relations among divine dignities and created predicates. Renaissance and early-modern versions could become:

- logic;
- topical invention;
- encyclopedism;
- missionary demonstration;
- universal language;
- extemporaneous speaking;
- mnemonic combination.

Descartes’s April 1619 correspondence shows interest in the alleged “keys” but also skepticism toward verbal fecundity ungrounded in knowledge.

## 6. Ramism

Ramist method ordered disciplines through definitions and dichotomous divisions displayed upon the page. This helped shift the burden of recollection from imagined architecture to visible curricular organization.

Descartes’s reliance upon series, division, and external notation belongs to this broader transformation, although he rejected school logic and does not cite Ramus as his source.

## 7. Hermeticism and natural magic

Camillo, Bruno, and Fludd constructed or interpreted memory structures through cosmology, astral correspondences, or universal causes. Yates sees Descartes’s reduction to causes as a rationalized survival of this movement.

**[X]** Similarity of form is real; historical transmission is unproved.

## 8. Universal science and universal language

Descartes’s early *mathesis universalis* seeks general order among quantities and relations. Later seventeenth-century projects by Comenius, Dalgarno, Wilkins, Kircher, and Leibniz sought universal characters, languages, or calculi.

Rossi’s broad genealogy runs:

```text
Lullian combination
→ Renaissance arts of memory and topical invention
→ ordered universal-language schemes
→ Leibnizian character and calculus
```

Descartes occupies an adjacent, critical position: he wants universal method but rejects combinatorial fluency without clear judgment.

## People

### René Descartes

Author of the fragment; critic of Schenkel and received Lullism; later architect of an alternative economy of cognition based on intuition, deduction, series, diagrams, and written notation.

### Lambert Thomas Schenkel

Born in 1547 and active into the 1620s, Schenkel was a teacher and promoter of artificial memory. His publications appeared under varying titles and in collections. His *Gazophylacium artis memoriae* assembled theory, practical instruction, testimonies, and related mnemonic works.

### Isaac Beeckman

Dutch natural philosopher, mathematician, physician, and Descartes’s early interlocutor. The letters to him preserve Descartes’s first explicit response to Lullism. Beeckman was asked to inspect Agrippa’s explanation for the missing “keys.”

### The Dordrecht Lullist

**[D/T]** He is reported in Descartes’s 29 April 1619 letter but not securely identified. His claim to speak for twenty hours on any subject exemplifies the promise—and, to Descartes, the danger—of mechanical topical fluency.

### Heinrich Cornelius Agrippa

His commentary or exposition of Lull was the work Descartes asked Beeckman to examine. Agrippa also criticized memory arts in *De incertitudine et vanitate scientiarum*, complicating any simple classification of him as merely an occult advocate.

### Johannes Paepp

Paepp published *Schenckelii detecti*, promising to reveal what Schenkel had concealed. Yates remarks that, had Descartes consulted Paepp, he would have known of this exposure.

**[D]** Paepp’s publication exists.

**[X]** No evidence shows that Descartes read it.

### Adrien Baillet

Descartes’s seventeenth-century biographer. Indispensable because he saw material now lost, but he paraphrased, moralized, harmonized, and defended Descartes. His testimony must be separated from verbatim Cartesian text.

### Leibniz

Copied the youthful fragments in 1676 and therefore preserved the memory note indirectly. Leibniz later pursued a combinatorial universal characteristic, but no direct line from this act of copying to his mature calculus can be assumed.

### Foucher de Careil

Found and published Leibniz’s excerpts in the nineteenth century and probably supplied the title *Cogitationes privatae*.

### Charles Adam and Paul Tannery

Editors of the standard *Œuvres*. They assembled the notebook fragments, Baillet’s evidence, correspondence, and editorial notes in volume X.

### Paolo Rossi and Frances Yates

The two modern historians most responsible for making the Schenkel fragment part of the history of method and memory.

## Anecdotes and their status

### The innkeeper who could speak for twenty hours

**[D]** Documented in Descartes’s letter, although the man’s claimed achievement was not independently verified.

### The accused magician

This belongs primarily to Schenkel’s biography. Later histories report that theological authorities at Douai examined his art and that the Antwerp inquisitor Jean Baxius questioned it, while the faculty protected Schenkel.

**[T/X]** The broad episode is transmitted through Schenkelian testimonials and later histories; precise dramatic claims that he “narrowly escaped execution” require caution.

### The auctioneer, the professor’s party, and the student who lost his memory

**[Negative finding]** No securely identified version of these anecdotes belongs to Descartes’s reform in the sources consulted. They occur in the wider anecdotal literature of prodigious memory, traveling teachers, and later mnemonic manuals. Attaching them to Descartes would be a catalogue conflation.

## The magic question

## 1. Was Descartes’s system magical?

The short answer is: **the text does not say so.**

It contains:

- no invocation;
- no angelic names;
- no planetary hours;
- no talisman;
- no consecration;
- no astrological calculation;
- no divine names used as operative characters;
- no claim that celestial powers enter the images;
- no *Ars notoria* prayers or figures.

The spear, ladder, and missile are ordinary mnemonic connectors.

**[D]** Descartes permits fictitious signification, but fiction is not magic.

## 2. Why Yates called it near occult memory

Yates’s comparison rests on structure:

- occult memory reduces multiplicity to higher causes;
- images of causes organize subordinate images;
- Descartes retains a cause from whose impression vanished phantasms return.

This is an interpretive analogy. It does not establish talismanic operation.

## 3. Schenkel and suspicion

Schenkel’s secrecy, commercial demonstrations, extraordinary claims, and language of mystery exposed him to suspicion. Authorities wanted to know whether prodigious memory came from:

- natural technique;
- fraud;
- demonic help;
- illicit magic.

His defenders represented the art as natural, ancient, and compatible with Aristotle, Cicero, Quintilian, and Aquinas.

**[Context]** “Secret” in this economy could mean proprietary pedagogical knowledge rather than occult ritual.

## 4. Descartes and Rosicrucianism

The young Descartes encountered the Rosicrucian controversy in Germany. His projected *Mathematical Thesaurus of Polybius Cosmopolitanus* contains a dedication to learned men and especially the famous “F.R.C.” in Germany (AT X, 214 and 241 in the transmitted fragments).

When he returned to Paris amid the Rosicrucian scare, he reportedly denied membership.

**[T]** Baillet transmits the denial and the concern over rumors.

**[X]** Descartes’s actual membership in a Rosicrucian fraternity is unsupported. The fraternity itself may never have existed as the organized body imagined by contemporaries.

**[X]** Influence of Rosicrucian reform rhetoric upon Descartes’s universal science remains possible but unproved.

### Yates’s later interpretation

Yates suggested that fear of occult accusations may have encouraged early modern thinkers to suppress or redescribe magical inheritances. This remains a historiographical hypothesis, not evidence that the memory note conceals a talismanic art.

## 5. Descartes’s dreams

Baillet’s report of the three dreams of 10–11 November 1619 derives from the lost *Olympica*. The dreams were interpreted as confirming a divinely guided vocation and “the foundations of a wonderful science.”

**[T]** The principal narrative is Baillet’s paraphrase of lost material.

**[X]** Susana Gómez López has argued that the *Olympica* may be a crafted literary fiction drawing on traditions of Platonic enthusiasm. Others treat it as an autobiographical record.

No surviving evidence connects the spear–ladder–missile image with the dreams.

## 6. The *Ars notoria*

The *Ars notoria* promised rapid acquisition of the liberal arts through prayers, ritual observance, and the contemplation of complex *notae*. It was repeatedly suspected or condemned as illicit magic.

Descartes’s reform differs fundamentally:

- it uses causal comprehension;
- it gives no ritual;
- it gives no sacred figures;
- it promises reconstruction, not infused omniscience.

**[M]** Claims that Descartes secretly adapted *Ars notoria* figures have no support in the surviving note.

## 7. Heresy and consequences

Descartes was later embroiled in theological and university controversies, particularly at Utrecht, and suppressed publication of *Le Monde* after Galileo’s condemnation. None of these events was caused by his youthful memory reform.

There is no record of:

- an inquisitorial proceeding against Descartes for artificial memory;
- condemnation of AT X, 230, which remained unpublished in his lifetime;
- confiscation of a Cartesian mnemonic diagram;
- a charge that the spear–ladder image was talismanic.

## Afterlife

## 1. In Descartes’s own work

The most defensible internal afterlife is not a palace but an economy of cognitive load.

### From storage to generation

The 1619 note says that causes can regenerate vanished phantasms. The later method similarly aims to regenerate results from principles rather than memorize conclusions.

### From arbitrary images to exact notation

The *Regulae* recommends lines and simple figures for representing relations. Marks on paper allow attention to remain fixed upon one or two relations at a time.

### From mnemonic itinerary to deductive series

Continuous intuition and orderly deduction replace the walk through loci. Review and enumeration preserve the whole.

### From internal storage to distributed supports

The later letter about local memory recognizes that paper, books, bodily habits, and cerebral traces may cooperate in recall.

## 2. Leibniz and combinatorics

Leibniz literally transmitted the fragment by copying it. His own universal characteristic and logical calculus also belong to the history of Lullian combination.

However:

- **[D]** Leibniz copied the note.
- **[D]** Leibniz pursued universal symbolic calculation.
- **[X]** It is not documented that AT X, 230 caused that project.
- **[R]** The episode is historically emblematic: a thinker seeking a universal calculus preserved Descartes’s criticism of mnemonic and Lullian excess.

## 3. Computing

A cautious analogy can be made:

- classical loci resemble addressed storage;
- Descartes’s first proposal resembles generative or compressed representation;
- causal dependencies resemble a directed graph;
- the composite image resembles associative indexing;
- external notation resembles offloading into an auxiliary memory.

These are modern analogies, not historical anticipations of computers.

**[M]** Calling Descartes’s memory reform a “neural network,” “database,” “compression algorithm,” or “hyperlink system” without qualification is anachronistic.

## 4. Literature and art

### Dante and the frescoes

Dante, medieval preaching, and fresco programs belong to the prehistory reconstructed by Yates, Carruthers, and Bolzoni. There is no documented direct descent from Descartes’s fragment to them; chronologically they precede it.

### Emblem books

The composite emblematic image supplies useful context for Descartes’s second proposal. Images containing multiple attributes could cue a network of propositions. No particular emblem has been identified as the model for the spear–ladder scene.

### Borges’s Funes

Borges’s “Funes the Memorious” dramatizes the opposite of Descartes’s economy: perfect retention without abstraction or general thought. The comparison is illuminating because Descartes wants fewer retained principles and more reconstruction.

There is no evidence Borges had AT X, 230 specifically in mind.

### Umberto Eco

Eco’s interest in perfect languages, combinatorial schemes, Lull, and encyclopedic semiosis belongs to the later reception of the tradition described by Rossi and Yates. A direct literary use of Descartes’s fragment should not be asserted without textual evidence.

## 5. Modern memory palaces and athletes

Contemporary memory athletes generally use loci, vivid interaction, numerical alphabets, and person–action–object systems. Their methods are closer to Schenkel and the classical tradition than to Descartes’s causal replacement.

The composite spear–ladder–missile scene resembles modern linked-image stories, but there is no historical chain from Descartes to modern competition rules.

## Controversies and disputes

## 1. Is it a “system”?

**[X]** The catalogue calls it a system; the primary evidence is a note. “Reform proposal” or “mnemonic experiment” is safer.

## 2. Date: 1619, 1619–1621, or 1619–1622?

The notebook began in 1619 and contained entries from subsequent years. The memory entry has no date.

- “1619” is plausible because of the Beeckman–Lull correspondence.
- “1619–1621” is conventional.
- “1619–1622” reflects the probable working life of the notebook.

An exact year cannot be demonstrated.

## 3. Authorship and title

The words are transmitted as Descartes’s, but the title *Cogitationes privatae* is probably Foucher de Careil’s. It should not be cited as an authorial book title without qualification.

## 4. Is Schenkel’s book securely identified?

Adam–Tannery identified it with Schenkel’s *De memoria* of 1595; Rossi notes its incorporation into the *Gazophylacium*. Multiple issues, reissues, translations, and collected forms circulated.

No ownership inscription or surviving copy proves which edition Descartes read.

## 5. “Profitable trifles” or “money-making nonsense”?

*Lucrosas nugas* may concede usefulness (“profitable”) or accuse Schenkel of commercial exploitation (“lucrative”). Descartes’s *nebulonis* is plainly contemptuous, but the exact joke cannot be recovered.

## 6. “Whole space” or “whole sheet”?

The Latin supports “whole sheet/paper.” Yates’s “space” is interpretive.

This is the clearest local error in her account.

## 7. Does the first proposal abolish memory?

Descartes says that no memory is needed for all the sciences, but he also speaks of causal impressions and re-formed phantasms.

Thus:

- it abolishes the need to retain every item separately;
- it does not abolish every temporal trace or every act of recognition;
- it transfers the burden to retained causes, understanding, imagination, and reconstruction.

## 8. Does “one cause” mean God?

**[X]** Possibly in a remote metaphysical sense, but the note does not say so. Treating the “one” as explicitly God, the Hermetic One, or a magical source is unwarranted.

## 9. Is the system occult?

No operative magic is documented. Yates’s resemblance is structural and interpretive.

## 10. Is it Lullian?

Descartes shares Lullism’s ambition for unity and relational order but explicitly distinguishes his new science from Lull’s *ars brevis* and later criticizes the received Lullian art.

Calling his reform “Lullian” without qualification obscures his opposition to mechanical fluency.

## 11. Is it Ramist?

It participates in the early-modern movement toward ordered, visible method. It is not a Ramist dichotomous table and Descartes does not name Ramus here.

## 12. Does it descend from Camillo or Bruno?

No direct evidence. Yates supplies a family resemblance: ascent toward causes and organization of lower images by higher principles.

## 13. Did Descartes neglect imagination?

Yates’s remark that the passage agrees with Descartes’s “lack of interest in imagination” has aged poorly. The early notebooks and *Regulae* assign imagination a central cognitive role. Sepper’s book is the fullest sustained correction.

The mature metaphysics restricts imagination relative to pure intellect, but restriction is not lack of interest.

## 14. Was local memory simply “outside us”?

The later letter says the aids called local memory may be outside us, including the patterned paper of a read book. That is closer to an account of environmentally supported memory than to a wholesale denial of mnemonic practice.

## 15. Did Descartes invent causal memory?

The exact declaration is his, but recollection through causal, logical, and associative sequences has ancient and scholastic precedents. His novelty lies in elevating causal reconstruction against the encyclopedic burden of contemporary artificial memory.

## 16. Did the memory art cause Cartesian method?

This remains a reconstruction.

Evidence for influence:

- direct reading of Schenkel;
- interest in Lull’s keys;
- vocabulary of order, dependence, images, series, and reduction;
- later preference for diagrams and economical notation.

Evidence against a strong derivation:

- no later citation of the memory note;
- no explicit claim that the four rules arose from mnemonics;
- multiple independent sources for method: mathematics, scholastic analysis, mechanics, algebra, dialectic, and personal practice.

## 17. Rosicrucian membership

A durable legend. Descartes knew of the movement and was associated with its reputation, but initiation or membership is undocumented.

## 18. Secret suppression

**[L/M]** Claims that Descartes destroyed a complete magical memory book, concealed a mnemonic wheel, or encoded its figures in analytic geometry are unsupported.

## 19. The catalogue’s lines of descent

The proposed chain “Camillo → Bruno → Schenkel/Fludd → Descartes → Leibniz” is a useful map of themes, not a demonstrated chain of book-borrowing or discipleship.

## Open questions

1. Which exact Schenkel edition or derivative did Descartes read?

2. Does *totam chartam* refer literally to a written mnemonic chart, metaphorically to the imaginative field, or polemically to both?

3. What were the five things in the spear–ladder–missile example?

4. Does *rerum non inconnexarum* mean genuinely related things, items already associated, or merely items capable of being brought into a common scene?

5. Is the “one” to which causes are reduced mathematical, metaphysical, theological, or methodological?

6. Did the AT XI, 649 formulation derive independently from Descartes, from a later Cartesian summary, or from the same lost notebook tradition?

7. How closely did Descartes know Schenkel’s practical instruction rather than only his printed exposition?

8. Had Descartes read Paepp’s exposure of Schenkel’s secrets? No evidence has yet established this.

9. Did the common-image technique influence the geometrical representations of the *Regulae*, or is the resemblance retrospective?

10. How much of Baillet’s account of the *Studium bonae mentis* preserves Descartes’s exact distinctions?

11. Can the lost Leibniz copy or another transcript be rediscovered in the Hanover holdings?

12. Was the memory entry written before or after the November 1619 dreams?

13. Did Descartes’s use of *clavis totius mysterii* parody the language of commercial mnemonic secrecy, Lullian keys, Rosicrucian mystery, or all three?

14. Does the passage represent a transient experiment abandoned by Descartes, or the germ of his mature economy of notation and deduction?

The decisive evidentiary result is therefore modest but substantial: Descartes did not leave a recoverable palace, theatre, wheel, or complete mnemonic curriculum. He left an exceptionally compressed proposal to replace the expensive storage of disconnected images with causal regeneration and relationally integrated representation. Yates correctly recognized its place at the threshold between artificial memory and method, but her occult analogy, her translation of *charta* as “space,” and her abbreviated treatment of Cartesian memory psychology require significant qualification.
