# Romberch's *Congestorium artificiose memorie*: Research Dossier

## Basic identification

| Field | Identification |
|---|---|
| **Name** | *Congestorium artificiose memorie*; modern normalized Latin often *Congestorium artificiosae memoriae* |
| **Exponent** | Johannes Host von Romberch, also Johannes Romberch de Kyrspe/Kierspe, O.P., a German Dominican associated with Cologne |
| **Date** | Completed at Cologne on 11 November 1513; first printed at Venice on 9 July 1520; second edition, Venice, July 1533, probably posthumous |
| **Source text** | *Congestorium artificiose memorie V.P.F. Ioannis Romberch de Kyrspe, regularis observantiae praedicatoriae: omnium de memoria praeceptiones aggregatim complectens* |
| **Principle in one sentence** | A comprehensive Thomist-rhetorical art of memory in which ordered real, imagined, cosmic, architectural, bodily, arboreal, and combinatorial places receive personally constructed images, symbols, gestures, letters, and inscriptions, enabling material to be recovered by mentally traversing the fixed order. |

### Evidentiary key

The following labels are used throughout:

- **[TEXT]** expressly documented by Romberch’s text or its material printing.
- **[CLASSICAL TEXT]** documented in an ancient source.
- **[BIBLIOGRAPHY]** documented by a library catalogue, surviving copy, colophon, or modern critical edition.
- **[RECONSTRUCTION]** a modern scholar’s reasoned reconstruction.
- **[YATES]** Frances Yates’s interpretation, rather than Romberch’s own assertion.
- **[DISPUTED]** contested attribution, dating, source identification, or interpretation.
- **[TRADITION]** a claim repeated in historical literature without equivalent direct evidence.
- **[LEGEND]** an anecdote or assertion whose historical occurrence has not been independently demonstrated.
- **[MODERN ANALOGY]** a later comparison, not a genealogically demonstrated descent.
- **[ABSENCE]** something sought but not found in the inspected evidence.

Two preliminary cautions are essential.

First, this is not a single “memory palace.” It is a manual encompassing several compatible place systems and many image-making procedures. Second, Yates’s “three different types of place systems” is a helpful synopsis, not Romberch’s exhaustive taxonomy. Recent study emphasizes a considerably richer apparatus: bodily places, trees, rooms, alphabetical lists, grammatical paradigms, astronomical sequences, professions, gestures, syllables, logical divisions, legal books, speeches, games, numbers, and movable combinatorial diagrams.

## The system in detail

## 1. Purpose, definition, and intended users

The title page advertises the work to theologians, preachers, confessors, jurists, judges, procurators, advocates, notaries, physicians, philosophers, teachers of the liberal arts, merchants, messengers, and couriers. **[TEXT]** It is therefore not addressed only to monks or contemplatives. Its applications extend from sermons and university disputation to litigation, debts, merchandise, page numbers, dice, cards, chess, and the rapid retention of spoken discourse.

Romberch explains why the work is a *Congestorium*:

> *Ex diversis comparavimus authorum libris, quorum canones et regulas in unum congessimus ad instar notariorum prothocollum diversarum causarum et negotiorum litis perscribentium … Cui proinde Congestorium artificiose memorative nomen imponimus.*

“We gathered [the material] from the books of different authors, whose canons and rules we collected into one, after the fashion of notaries writing a protocol of the different causes and business of a lawsuit … We therefore give it the name *Congestorium* of artificial memory.”  
—1520, prefatory epistle to Johann Grevenbach, f. A6r in the signed gathering; modern critical citation f. 6r. **[TEXT]**

This materially qualifies Yates’s joke that the title is appropriate because the book is “a strange congestion of memory material.” Romberch deliberately uses *congerere*—to gather or heap together—as a methodological term. Luis Merino Jerez argues that the title denotes something like an evidentiary notarial dossier: different authorities are collected without every disagreement being suppressed. **[RECONSTRUCTION]** The “congestion” is therefore partly a designed compilation and not merely intellectual decay.

Romberch defines the discipline as an aid to natural memory:

> *Ars memorativa dicitur, quandoquidem ingenii bonitas saepe imitatur artem; ars vero naturae commoda confirmat et auget.*

“It is called the mnemonic art because excellence of talent often imitates art, while art confirms and increases the advantages of nature.”  
—Tract I, ch. 4. **[TEXT]**

The art does not create the faculty of memory. It assists the natural power so that its acts are performed more easily and firmly. This is the conventional classical-scholastic distinction between *memoria naturalis* and *memoria artificialis*.

The book was initially written for Johann Grevenbach, whom Romberch calls a friend eager for every discipline. He says Grevenbach asked him to preserve the *artificium* they used in urgent or improvised matters. **[TEXT]** Whether Grevenbach is also the unnamed student in Romberch’s teaching anecdotes is possible but unproved. **[RECONSTRUCTION]**

## 2. Architecture of the treatise

The work contains four treatises:

1. **Seven preliminary chapters**: possibility, necessity, usefulness, detractors, suitable users, definitions and divisions of memory, foundations, impediments, and the need for places and images.
2. **Nine chapters on places**: their nature, kinds, number, marking, acquisition, conditions, construction, fictive places, examples, and erasure.
3. **Nineteen chapters on images**: definition, division, size, quality, action and passion, sources, signification, adaptation, letter-images, living alphabets, syllables, grammatical cases, words, metaphor, etymology, bodily movements, insignia, and combination.
4. **Fourteen chapters on applications**: natural and artificial things, concepts and words, the sciences, divisions of being, propositions, authorities and citation, questions, syllogisms, lectures, sermons, histories, verse, rapid retention of speech, commerce and games, reading, meditation, and repetition. **[TEXT]**

The fourth treatise is consequently much more than the abbreviated “encyclopaedic system” visible in Yates. Its practical range is one of the strongest corrections made by recent scholarship.

## 3. Psychological mechanism

Romberch’s psychology is late-scholastic and broadly Aristotelian-Thomist. Sensation supplies forms; the internal senses process them; phantasy supplies the corporeal similitudes without which embodied human understanding cannot ordinarily think; memory retains them; recollection searches them through order. **[TEXT/CONTEXT]**

The 1520 book includes a woodcut diagram of the external senses and powers of the soul, at f. 9v in the modern foliation. It relates sensory reception to internal faculties rather than depicting a supernatural channel of revelation. **[TEXT]**

Thomas Aquinas is indeed frequently cited, particularly the *Summa theologiae* II–II, q. 49, a. 1, ad 2 and the commentary on Aristotle’s *De memoria et reminiscentia*. Thomas’s four familiar mnemonic principles are:

- choose appropriate corporeal similitudes;
- arrange what is to be remembered in a considered order;
- apply solicitude or affective engagement;
- meditate frequently upon what has been stored.

Romberch embeds these principles within a much larger apparatus of loci, images, alphabets, lists, and exercises. **[TEXT]**

Retrieval is therefore neither photographic recall nor the passive reading of a universal code. The practitioner:

1. establishes a stable sequence of places;
2. gives each place a recognizable distinction;
3. converts the desired material into personally intelligible images;
4. deposits the images in order;
5. fixes them through attention, meditation, and repetition;
6. mentally traverses the series;
7. interprets or “reads” each image;
8. can traverse the places forward, backward, or from a chosen point.

Romberch claims to have demonstrated this flexibility by reciting the same material from its beginning, end, or middle:

> *Facile quippe est ex locis ordine collocatis materiam imaginatam ordinate et sapienter pronunciare, certitudinaliter de uno in alterum procedendo ordine recto et reflexo atque praepostero.*

“For it is easy, from places disposed in order, to pronounce imagined material orderly and intelligently, proceeding securely from one to another in direct, reversed, or inverted order.”  
—1520, f. 21v. **[TEXT: autobiographical claim]**

That he made the demonstration is Romberch’s testimony; no independent witness has been identified. It should not be promoted from a first-person claim to externally verified fact.

## 4. What serves as places

### 4.1 Definition

Romberch distinguishes common or inclusive place from proper or particular mnemonic place. In the second treatise he writes that the particular places used here are those in which one immediately inscribes the imagined thing:

> *Ita et nos in praesentiarum accipimus locos proprios sive particulares, illos quibus immediate rem imaginatam inscribimus: quales sunt parietes, fenestrae, columnae in cameris, aestuariis, cenaculis domorum … vel arbores, plantae, petrae.*

“We likewise presently take proper or particular places to be those in which we immediately inscribe the imagined thing: such as walls, windows, columns in chambers, heated rooms and dining rooms of houses … or trees, plants and rocks.”  
—Tract II, ch. 2. **[TEXT]**

Thus a *locus* need not be a whole room. It can be a bounded surface or recognizable feature within a larger containing place.

### 4.2 Natural, artificial, real, and fictive places

Romberch admits:

- natural places: trees, rocks, landscape features;
- artificial places: buildings, walls, windows, columns, furniture, streets, shops;
- real or previously perceived places;
- fictive places deliberately constructed in imagination;
- cosmic and theological places;
- bodily members;
- diagrammatic or logical places, such as branches of a tree.

Yates’s three groups—cosmos, zodiac/constellations, and real buildings—select the most visually arresting examples but do not exhaust Romberch’s classifications. **[YATES, corrected by TEXT]**

### 4.3 Rules for places

The second treatise, ch. 5, gives seven principal conditions with supplementary qualifications. Recent reconstruction from the 1520 edition yields:

1. **Order.** Places require a suitable, ordered disposition. The sequence begins at the viewer’s left:

   > *Id igitur in primis praecipimus, ut in debita ordinataque dispositione tibi loca vendices … a sinistra tamen sumendus est.*

   “First we prescribe that you appropriate places for yourself in a proper and ordered disposition … the order must nevertheless be taken from the left.”  
   —f. 21r. **[TEXT]**

2. **Distance.** Adjacent loci should be approximately five or six feet apart:

   > *Congrua quoque locorum expetitur distantia.*

   “An appropriate distance between places is also required.”  
   —f. 21v. **[TEXT]**

   Romberch says twice that experience confirmed Peter of Ravenna’s measure.

3. **Proportion.** The locus must suit the dimensions of the image:

   > *Ea quoque continua erit locorum quantitas, ne sit nimis arcta vel alta; imagines siquidem latitudinis, superficiei et longitudinis proportionem exigent.*

   “The continuous extent of the places should be such that it is neither too narrow nor too high, since images require proportion in breadth, surface and length.”  
   —f. 22r. **[TEXT]**

4. **Moderate illumination.** A place must be neither excessively dark nor excessively bright.

5. **Distinctiveness.** Places must not be confusingly similar. The text also warns against excessive circularity or roundness because it does not discriminate positions well.

6. **Availability and prior inspection.** Places should be “at hand,” stable, and ideally have been seen at least once empty of crowds.

7. **Fixity.** The chosen framework should not move or escape. A ship is a poor permanent locus because its physical and visual relations change. **[TEXT]**

The formulation “places must be spherical” sometimes appears through mistranscription or compressed paraphrase. The Latin says the opposite: excessively spherical or round places are insufficiently discriminating. **[TEXTUAL CORRECTION]**

### 4.4 Fifth and tenth markers

Romberch follows the ancient recommendation to mark every fifth place and adapts Peter of Ravenna’s expanded series:

- fifth: a golden hand;
- tenth: a golden cross;
- fifteenth: a silver hand;
- twentieth: an image of the number;
- the procedure continues analogically.

The *Ad Herennium* had proposed a golden hand at the fifth place and a person named Decimus at the tenth. Peter substituted a cross for Decimus, making the sequence more congenial to Christian practice. Romberch’s abbey plate displays the hand and cross. **[TEXT/TRANSMISSION]**

Yates remarks that the hand associates the fifth place with five fingers and imagines Memory “ticking off” places on the fingers. The five-finger association is plausible, but the specific account of Memory ticking them off is Yates’s explanatory prose, not Romberch’s statement. **[YATES/RECONSTRUCTION]**

### 4.5 The human reach rule

The human figure in the measuring woodcut stretches upward and sideways against a rectangular locus. Its purpose is to show that a place holding a human image should remain within the perceptual compass of a human figure and should not be so large that the image loses definition. **[TEXT/IMAGE]**

Yates writes that it “must not be larger than a man can reach.” This is an intelligible summary, but recent scholarship places the figure within a broader Renaissance discussion of human proportion. The 2024 critical study compares it with proportional figures attributed to Leonardo da Vinci, Francesco di Giorgio Martini, and Giacomo Andrea. **[RECONSTRUCTION]**

The rule comes substantially through Peter of Ravenna; it is not an original invention of Romberch. **[TEXTUAL DEPENDENCE]**

## 5. The monastery experiment: a documented practitioner’s error

This is the best evidence that Romberch did not merely copy rules but tested and revised a system.

He initially used the cells in a Dominican dormitory as places and became confused because the doors resembled one another. Someone advised:

> *Loca non fiant in cellis fratrum.*

“Let places not be made in the friars’ cells.”  
—f. 23v. **[TEXT]**

While Romberch was teaching this warning, one of his auditors objected that similar doors caused him no difficulty because he associated each friar with his office. The sacristan could be marked by sacristy instruments; the public lecturer by the books of his lecture; other friars by the tools or attributes of their work.

Romberch accepted the correction:

> *Et eo quidem libentius hac convincebar argumentatione quo et discipulum perficere didici.*

“I was the more willingly persuaded by this argument because I learned that a pupil too can perfect [his teacher].”  
—f. 23v. **[TEXT]**

He therefore recommends differentiating each cell door externally by an appropriate sign and internally by the occupant and office.

Important distinctions:

- That Romberch recounts the incident is **[TEXT]**.
- That the unnamed auditor was Grevenbach is **[RECONSTRUCTION]**, not established.
- That the incident precisely occurred as narrated is an autobiographical assertion, not independently documented.
- Similar warnings against friars’ cells appear in the earlier *Memoria fecunda* and Jacopo Ragona’s *Artificialis memoriae regulae*. Romberch’s initial “mistake” may therefore be a didactic *exemplum* shaped by tradition. **[DISPUTED/RECONSTRUCTION]**
- Cosmas Rossellius later repeats the story, showing textual reception, not independent corroboration. **[TRANSMISSION]**

## 6. The abbey memory system

The woodcut conventionally called the Abbey Memory System shows a monastic complex as a framework for ordered loci. Its spaces include the courtyard, library or book room, and chapel. Separate images present the objects to be deposited in these places. Every fifth position is marked by a hand and every tenth by a cross. **[TEXT/PLATE]**

The system should be understood operationally:

1. learn the abbey and its approach;
2. subdivide it into sequential, visually distinct loci;
3. mark the numerically significant positions;
4. associate each room with a semantic field where useful;
5. deposit active or otherwise memorable images;
6. retrieve by re-entering the complex mentally and following its fixed circuit.

The plate is not an architectural survey of a particular surviving abbey. No building has been securely identified as its prototype. **[ABSENCE]**

The objects in the associated woodcut are mnemonic stock, not necessarily the permanent contents of an actual monastic library or chapel. Their functions vary: some represent their ordinary referents, some work by name, shape, profession, pun, attribute, or a personal association.

A surviving copy of the 1533 edition at the University of Pennsylvania has the call number **GC5 H7958 520c 1533**. The Library of Congress copy is **BF383 .H7**. The NYPL digital copy is catalogued under **\*KB 1533 (Host von Romberch, J.)**. **[BIBLIOGRAPHY]**

## 7. The city, streets, shops, and rooms

The modern critical edition identifies:

- “Romberch’s city,” 1520 f. 28r;
- “Romberch’s rooms,” f. 29r.

The city woodcut represents an ordered street environment whose buildings and shops supply distinct visual places. A bookseller, *Bibliopola*, is among the shops; bibliographical literature sometimes describes it as an unusually early representation of a street-facing bookshop. **[TEXT/PLATE; priority claim DISPUTED]**

Yates later connects Bruno’s “shops” with this textbook tradition. The resemblance is real, but a direct act of borrowing by Bruno from a particular copy of Romberch has not been demonstrated. **[YATES/RECONSTRUCTION]**

Rooms may be subdivided by walls, windows, columns, corners, furniture, and bodies. The mental route must remain unambiguous. Similar rooms can be repaired by color, inscriptions, objects, persons, or professional attributes.

## 8. Cosmic places

### 8.1 Spheres of the universe

Romberch reproduces and explains a nested cosmological diagram derived substantially from Jacobus Publicius and related scholastic cosmographies. In the 1520 critical foliation it occurs at f. 25v; Yates, using 1533 pagination, cited pp. 17r ff. and 31r ff.

The diagram contains concentric regions for:

- earth;
- water;
- air;
- fire;
- the planetary heavens;
- the fixed stars;
- higher celestial spheres;
- the nine angelic orders.

At its foot appear abbreviated theological realms:

- **L.PA**: *locus Paradisi*, Paradise;
- **L.P**: *locus Paradisi terrestris*, Earthly Paradise;
- **PVR**: Purgatory;
- **IN**: *Infernus*, Hell.

Romberch defines such settings as *ficta loca*, imagined places. Paradise is divided into seats or stations for angelic choirs, patriarchs, prophets, apostles, martyrs, and other blessed orders. Purgatory and Hell are “common” or inclusive places, subdivided into particular loci. Images of sinners punished according to their sins occupy Hell, with inscriptions explaining or fixing the relevant matter. **[TEXT]**

These are mnemonic applications of the Christian cosmos, not instructions for astral magic.

### 8.2 Yates’s “Dantesque type”

Yates writes:

> “This type of artificial memory may be called the Dantesque type, not because the Dominican treatise is influenced by the *Divine Comedy*, but because Dante was influenced by such an interpretation of artificial memory.”

This is one of her most ambitious backward projections. **[YATES]**

What is documented:

- medieval mnemonic theory predates Dante;
- ordered images of virtues, vices, rewards, and punishments belong to preaching, devotional, rhetorical, and artistic traditions;
- Romberch and Rossellius explicitly treat Paradise, Purgatory, and Hell as mnemonic frameworks. **[TEXT/TRADITION]**

What is not documented:

- that Dante learned or composed the *Comedy* from a system identical to Romberch’s;
- that the divisions of the *Comedy* are the product of an otherwise recoverable Dominican memory manual;
- that Santa Maria Novella’s fresco cycle was designed as a working memory system.

Later scholarship, especially Carruthers and Bolzoni, supports the importance of trained, architectonic, affective memory in medieval composition but generally avoids Yates’s strong one-directional causal claim. **[SCHOLARLY CORRECTION]**

“Dantesque” should therefore be retained as Yates’s typological label, not as a documented historical name or proven descent.

## 9. Zodiac and constellations

Romberch recommends the zodiac as an immediately recognizable sequence of twelve places and names Metrodorus of Scepsis as ancient authority. His information comes through Cicero, *De oratore* II.360, and Quintilian, *Institutio oratoria* XI.2.22. **[TEXT/CLASSICAL TEXT]**

For a longer celestial sequence he recommends the constellation figures associated with Hyginus. Alphabetical tables of constellations help index the material. **[TEXT]**

Yates admits that Romberch does not specify what material is to be attached to the constellation figures and guesses that preachers might use them for sermons on virtues, vices, Heaven, and Hell. **[YATES/RECONSTRUCTION]** That guessed content must not be reported as Romberch’s instruction.

The use of celestial images is not itself evidence of talismanic astrology. In Romberch they serve as familiar, ordered pictures. **[TEXT]**

## 10. Trees and the *Hortus philosophiae*

At f. 66v the work presents the *Hortus philosophiae*, a tree-like hierarchy of the sciences. Recent analysis emphasizes that Romberch calls it a *hortus*, garden, rather than simply an *arbor*. The tree is the organizing component within a larger garden of diagrams. **[TEXT/RECONSTRUCTION]**

The figure places branches of knowledge in an ordered hierarchy. Knowing the position of a science in the tree enables the learner to attach and retrieve propositions concerning it. Romberch cites or alludes to established classifications associated with Robert Kilwardby, Hugh of Saint-Victor, scholastic divisions found in Albert the Great and Thomas Aquinas, and a contemporary instructional tradition represented by Poliziano. **[TEXT/SOURCE STUDY]**

The tree is “Lullian” in diagrammatic form only in a broad sense. It resembles widely diffused medieval branching diagrams; direct derivation from a specific work of Ramon Llull has not been established. Recent scholarship describes it as a pedagogical use of a diagrammatic convention associated with Lullism, not as proof that Romberch adopted Llull’s complete Art. **[DISPUTED/QUALIFIED]**

## 11. What serves as images

Romberch distinguishes images through several intersecting categories. They may be:

- representations of things, persons, animals, actions, passions, qualities, or relations;
- derived **from the thing** (*a re*) or **from the word** (*a verbo*);
- natural-looking or fictive;
- human, animal, artificial-object, or composite images;
- active figures engaged in a memorable action;
- bodily gestures;
- professional tools and insignia;
- metaphor, metonymy, transference, or etymology;
- letter-shapes and number-shapes;
- alphabetically indexed living creatures or persons;
- syllabic images;
- images associated with grammatical inflections;
- inscriptions made from pictorial letters;
- combinations of a principal image with subsidiary marks.

The essential requirement is not public symbolism but retrievability for the particular practitioner. An image is successful when its relation to the intended matter is clear, distinctive, durable, and rapidly decoded.

## 12. Rules for images

Romberch inherits the principal ancient rules from the *Ad Herennium* and reframes them through scholastic psychology:

1. images must be concrete enough to enter phantasy;
2. they should be unusual, affective, active, beautiful, ugly, comic, violent, splendid, shameful, or otherwise striking when ordinary images would fade;
3. image and locus must remain distinguishable;
4. the size of an image should suit its locus;
5. a single principal image may gather several subsidiary concepts;
6. action and passion clarify relations;
7. personally known persons and objects are often more effective than generic ones;
8. an image derived from a word must preserve an unmistakable phonetic or orthographic cue;
9. an image derived from a thing must capture the relevant meaning, not merely its sound;
10. associated images and inscriptions may be placed on a person’s head, limbs, clothing, or attributes;
11. the practitioner must rehearse;
12. difficult materials require adaptation to individual experience rather than blind copying.

Yates says that the images are “not illustrated nor … very clearly described.” That is true of the classical-style active images, but misleading if extended to the entire third treatise: the book illustrates letter-images, birds, number signs, Grammar, the quadrangle, a combinatorial device, and several applied systems. **[YATES, qualified]**

## 13. The *Ad Herennium* lawsuit image

The governing ancient example is *Rhetorica ad Herennium* III.20.33. The prosecutor alleges murder by poison, inheritance as motive, and multiple witnesses. The practitioner imagines the sick or dead man in bed and the accused beside him:

> *Et reum ad lectum eius adstituemus, dextra poculum, sinistra tabulas, medico testiculos arietinos tenentem.*

“And we shall place the defendant at his bedside, holding a cup in his right hand, tablets in his left, and ram’s testicles on the fourth finger.”  
—*Rhetorica ad Herennium* III.20.33; Latin text with Harry Caplan’s Loeb translation, 1954. **[CLASSICAL TEXT]**

The cup suggests poison; the tablets suggest the will or inheritance; *testiculi/testes* supplies a pun for witnesses. One image compresses the principal heads of the case.

Romberch transmits the underlying rule that a principal active image may gather associated signs. The lawsuit scene is the classical model for his composite imagery, but it should not be claimed that every surviving edition reproduces it as a woodcut. **[TRANSMISSION; ABSENCE of dedicated plate]**

## 14. Visual alphabets

### 14.1 Material or shape alphabet

Romberch supplies objects whose forms resemble letters. Yates gives the examples of compasses or a ladder for A and a hoe for N. The 1520 sequence occupies ff. 38v–43r in the modern foliation.

This alphabet descends substantially from Publicius, whose printed *Oratoriae artis epitomata* was the first printed mnemonic treatise to illustrate a visual alphabet. **[TEXTUAL DEPENDENCE]**

### 14.2 Living alphabet

A second alphabet associates each letter with the initial of an animal or bird:

- A: *anser*, goose;
- B: *bubo*, owl;
- P: *pica*, magpie;
- and so forth.

The full bird alphabet appears at f. 44r. The precise members reflect Latin vocabulary; it is not a language-neutral code. **[TEXT]**

### 14.3 Persons and professions

Known persons may be ordered by their names. Professions can be encoded through tools and insignia. This makes the system partly private: a practitioner’s acquaintances and professional world supply stronger images than arbitrary textbook figures.

Peter of Ravenna’s famous example is to remember **ET** as Eusebius before Thomas and **TE** by placing Eusebius behind Thomas. **[SOURCE TRANSMITTED BY YATES]**

### 14.4 Numbers

Romberch includes signs for numbers at f. 45r and two further full-alphabet/number plates at ff. 45v–46r. Objects are chosen by shape or conventional association. These anticipate, without being identical to, later number-shape systems. **[TEXT]**

### 14.5 Use and limitation

Yates believed the visual alphabets “were intended to be used for making inscriptions in memory.” That is true of some applications, especially Grammar, but Marta Ramos Grané’s analysis corrects Yates’s broader equation:

- *imagines a verbo* are images formed from the sound or literal form of a word;
- *memoria verborum* means retaining the words of a discourse, whether the images were constructed from sound or meaning;
- pictorial inscription is one resource within the system, not the definition of verbatim memory. **[SCHOLARLY CORRECTION]**

Romberch writes:

> *Pro inscriptione saltem, quae litteris realibus materialibus commode fieri poterit … ubi singula quis verba locare voluerit alicuius textus.*

“For an inscription, at least, which can conveniently be made with real material letters … when someone wishes to place the individual words of a text.”  
—f. 29v. **[TEXT]**

## 15. Grammar as a worked mnemonic image

The female personification of Grammar appears at f. 69v in the 1520 critical foliation; Yates cites 1533 pp. 82v–83r.

She carries the traditional attributes of Grammar, including a ladder and cutting instrument. Her body is also a secondary place-system bearing birds, tools, gestures, and pictorial inscriptions. The question being encoded is whether Grammar is a common or a special science.

The main cues include:

- *predicatio*: a *pica*, magpie, for P, with associated objects;
- *applicatio*: an *aquila*, eagle, on or near the arm;
- *continentia*: material-letter objects across the chest spelling or cueing C–O–N–T.

The bird on the arm is a revealing inconsistency. Romberch’s own alphabetical table gives A as *anser*, but the explanatory text says *aquila*. **[TEXTUAL VARIATION WITHIN THE SYSTEM]** This demonstrates that the lists are adaptable repertoires, not immutable universal characters.

Yates’s conclusion is broadly correct: a familiar personification can function simultaneously as:

- the image of a discipline;
- a bodily set of loci;
- an active figure;
- a support for subsidiary images;
- a tablet for pictorial inscriptions.

But her extension of this example to medieval virtue personifications, Holcot’s Penance, and fresco cycles is inferential. **[YATES/RECONSTRUCTION]**

## 16. The encyclopaedic sciences

Romberch advises forming a representative figure for each science, placing its principles and divisions on the body, and assigning the whole figure to a room.

For Theology:

> *Ut pro theologia consummatum egregiumque aliquem theologum …*

“For Theology [imagine] some consummate and excellent theologian …”  
—Tract IV, ch. 3. **[TEXT]**

The head receives *cognitio, amor, fruitio*—knowledge, love, fruition. Other members carry divine essence, act, form, relation, articles, precepts, sacraments, and further subdivisions. Parallel tables organize:

- theology;
- metaphysics and philosophy;
- moral philosophy;
- law;
- health or medicine;
- astronomy;
- geometry;
- arithmetic;
- music;
- logic;
- rhetoric;
- grammar.

This is not a completed universal encyclopedia containing every proposition. It is a generative template by which a user constructs disciplinary rooms, representative bodies, trees, and attached concepts. **[TEXT/RECONSTRUCTION]**

Yates described this as a “stupendous attempt to hold a vast *summa* of knowledge in memory” and conjectured a “distant echo” of Thomas Aquinas’s own memory system. **[YATES]** No text by Thomas describes an encyclopaedic bodily-room system of this kind. The dependence is conceptual and doctrinal, not evidence for a lost personal memory palace of Aquinas. **[ABSENCE/CORRECTION]**

## 17. Logical and combinatorial apparatus

The third treatise includes:

- a quadrangular diagram at f. 48v;
- Romberch’s combinatorial system at f. 49v;
- a singular/plural device at f. 50v;
- a tetragram at f. 61r.

Romberch attempts to explain a difficult diagram in Publicius and admits that he does not entirely understand his predecessor’s account. **[TEXT]** The diagram recombines letters or grammatical categories to generate cues for words and inflections.

The apparatus resembles Lullian wheels in its permutation of written elements, but its immediate source is Publicius and its purpose is mnemonic-linguistic. It is not the full Lullian *Ars*, whose letters signify divine dignities and relational principles in a demonstrative system. **[TEXTUAL DEPENDENCE/CORRECTION]**

This distinction matters to the supposed genealogy “Llull → Romberch → Bruno → Leibniz → computer.” There is a real history of combinatorial diagrams, but it is not a single uninterrupted technology or doctrine.

## 18. Memory for speech, reading, and citation

Romberch stresses that written material can be impressed through repetition, whereas immediate verbatim retention of a speaker is reserved for highly trained practitioners:

> *Quae ex libris desumimus crebra repetitione memoriae imprimuntur, sed contionatoris, declamatoris vel orantis verba ex tempore mox reponere exercitatissimi est …*

“What we take from books is impressed on memory by frequent repetition, but immediately storing the words of a preacher, declaimer, or speaker as they are delivered belongs to the very highly trained.”  
—f. 82v. **[TEXT]**

He adds:

> *Si diligens fueris auscultator et satis attentus et verba cum imaginibus conferas ac seriose et fortiter imprimas, optime audita commemorabis.*

“If you are a diligent and sufficiently attentive listener, connect words with images, and impress them earnestly and firmly, you will recall what you heard very well.”  
—f. 82v. **[TEXT]**

Recent scholarship accordingly associates Romberch’s *memoria verborum* as much with trained listening and aurality as with writing. **[RECONSTRUCTION]**

## 19. Erasing and reusing places

Tract II, ch. 9 treats evacuation of places by deleting images. The stable locus remains; temporary images are removed after use. This is the ordinary reusable-tablet principle of rhetorical memory. Long-term doctrinal frameworks, by contrast, may retain permanent index images.

The method therefore supports both:

- temporary storage for a speech, transaction, or lecture;
- durable organization of a science or body of doctrine.

## Yates’s account and scholarship since

## 1. What Yates got right

Yates correctly recognized:

- Romberch as one of the leading printed mnemonic compilers between Publicius/Peter of Ravenna and Rossellius;
- the four-part structure;
- the dependence on the three principal classical discussions of memory;
- the pervasive Thomist framework;
- the importance of Peter of Ravenna and Publicius;
- the coexistence of cosmic, astronomical, and architectural places;
- the use of Paradise, Purgatory, and Hell as artificial places;
- the importance of visual alphabets;
- Grammar as a rare illustrated composite mnemonic image;
- the encyclopaedic ambition of the fourth treatise;
- the work’s importance for understanding Dolce and later mnemonic books.

Her description remains exceptionally perceptive when treated as an interpretative essay rather than a critical edition.

## 2. What later work corrects

### “Strange congestion”

Merino Jerez shows that Yates’s phrase encouraged scholars to treat the book as an indiscriminate heap and to consult it mainly as a key to Publicius or Dolce. Romberch himself explains *Congestorium* through the ordered compilation of a notarial protocol. **[CORRECTION]**

### Excessive concentration on loci

Yates’s chapters foreground cosmic and architectural places while giving comparatively little attention to Romberch’s theory of image production and the extensive applications in Treatise IV. Recent work identifies the book as an integrated synthesis of places, images, language, disciplinary divisions, and practice. **[CORRECTION]**

### Memory for words

Yates associates visual inscription too directly with *memoria verborum*. Ramos Grané distinguishes:

- the object remembered—verbal wording;
- the route by which an image is created—from sound or from meaning;
- pictorial inscription as one optional device.

This is the clearest direct correction of a technical interpretation in Yates. **[CORRECTION]**

### “Dantesque” memory

The existence of moral-topographical mnemonic traditions is well supported. Direct derivation of Dante’s poem from an artificial-memory system identical to Romberch’s is not. **[YATES hypothesis, unproved]**

### Thomas’s lost system

Romberch’s Thomist citations are documented. A lost encyclopaedic image system personally used by Thomas is not. The Santa Maria Novella connection remains suggestive iconological speculation. **[YATES hypothesis]**

### Decline and debasement

Yates characterizes visual alphabets and expanding rule lists as “deterioration,” “trivial elaborations,” and a kind of crossword-puzzle mentality. Recent scholarship treats the same features as attempts to solve difficult problems involving exact wording, multilingual learning, rapid listening, classification, and teaching. The underlying evidence is unchanged; the value judgment has shifted. **[HISTORIOGRAPHICAL REVISION]**

## 3. Carruthers

Mary Carruthers retained Yates’s “congestion of lore” language and used Romberch especially to illuminate Publicius, Albertus Magnus, Thomas, and medieval locational heuristics. Her larger contribution was to replace a narrow “storage trick” account with memory as:

- composition;
- invention;
- ethical formation;
- meditation;
- trained recollection;
- a basis of reading and making.

Carruthers strengthens the medieval institutional and cognitive setting of Romberch but does not independently establish Yates’s proposed Dante or Thomas reconstructions.

## 4. Rossi

Paolo Rossi placed Romberch in the transition from rhetorical memory to encyclopaedic and universal-language projects. He emphasized the line through Peter of Ravenna, the wide circulation of the work, and its Italian reception through Dolce. Later Romberch-specific research finds this accurate but incomplete: Rossi devoted little attention to Romberch’s distinct synthesis and sometimes allowed Dolce’s success to eclipse the Latin source.

## 5. Bolzoni

Lina Bolzoni’s work on galleries, theatres, preaching, images, and encyclopaedic organization supports the larger claim that Renaissance memory systems were embedded in visual culture and literary composition. Her Camillo studies show why an encyclopaedic architectural system should not be reduced to a mechanical palace. They do not prove that Camillo copied Romberch, though both inhabited overlapping Venetian print and memory cultures.

## 6. Small

Jocelyn Penny Small’s *Wax Tablets of the Mind* stresses differences between ancient oral/visual practices and later literate reconstructions. Her discussion helps correct any assumption that “memory for words” always meant letter-by-letter encoding. Ramos Grané extends this warning directly to Romberch.

## 7. Hajdu and Volkmann

Helga Hajdu and Ludwig Volkmann treated Romberch before Yates. Volkmann reproduced visual alphabets and number-images from Romberch and other writers—plates 183–185 and 188 are especially relevant—but did not reconstruct their full operational role. Hajdu placed him in the long history of mnemonic doctrine. Yates’s presentation depended materially on this earlier German scholarship.

## 8. Vasoli, Torre, Merino Jerez, and Ramos Grané

These are the decisive Romberch-specific advances:

- Cesare Vasoli reconstructed the Dominican, scholastic, preaching, and Reuchlin contexts.
- Andrea Torre demonstrated that Dolce’s *Dialogo* is fundamentally a translation and adaptation of Romberch, not an independent mnemonic invention.
- Luis Merino Jerez reconstructed Romberch’s biography and epistolary context, clarified *Congestorium* as deliberate compilation, and challenged the Yates-derived neglect of its image theory.
- Marta Ramos Grané produced the first critical edition, analyzed the place rules, autobiographical error narrative, memory for words, Kabbalah references, *Hortus philosophiae*, diagrams, sources, and printing history.

## 9. Lull scholarship

Anthony Bonner and Alexander Fidora have shown that Llull’s Art is a formal demonstrative method grounded in combinations of dignities, principles, relations, questions, and subjects. It should not be conflated with the classical method of loci.

Romberch uses branching and combinatorial diagrams related to the broader Lullian afterlife, chiefly through intermediate sources such as Publicius. He does not present Llull’s complete system. **[CORRECTION]**

## 10. Bruno scholarship

Yates made the passage from mnemonic textbook to magical memory central to her Bruno narrative. Later work by Rita Sturlese, Michele Ciliberto, Stephen Clucas, Hilary Gatti, Marco Matteoli, and others has:

- provided more reliable texts and chronology;
- distinguished Bruno’s individual mnemonic works;
- treated his psychology, logic, metaphysics, language, and magic together;
- warned against reducing every wheel to Hermetic magic;
- shown both continuity with textbook mnemonic devices and radical philosophical transformation.

Bruno uses shops, bodies, alphabets, number-images, lists, wheels, and constellation orders recognizable from the Romberch–Publicius–Rossellius tradition. A direct borrowing is plausible in places, but “Bruno exercised himself using Romberch’s book” is generally repeated without a documented surviving annotated Bruno copy or explicit acknowledgment. **[TRADITION/UNPROVED]**

## 11. Camillo scholarship

Bolzoni and Paola Stagno clarify Camillo’s theatre through manuscript, print, iconographic, rhetorical, and patronage evidence. Yates’s linkage between Camillo’s theatre and a revival of Dominican memory in Venice is an attractive contextual possibility, not a proven causal chain.

## 12. Ramus and Ong

Walter J. Ong showed that Petrus Ramus’s method reorganized knowledge through dichotomous spatial outlines and dialectical order while rejecting the elaborate imagistic machinery of classical artificial memory. Yates memorably says Ramus “smashes the inner image of old Grammatica.” This is a metaphor for epistemic change, not an actual controversy between Ramus and Romberch.

## History and context

## 1. Chronology

- ca. 80s BCE: anonymous *Rhetorica ad Herennium* systematizes artificial memory.
- 55 BCE: Cicero’s *De oratore* gives the Simonides story and Metrodorus tradition.
- first century CE: Quintilian critically presents architectural memory.
- fourth century onward: the *Ad Herennium*, believed to be Cicero’s, becomes the *Rhetorica nova*.
- thirteenth century: Albert the Great and Thomas Aquinas incorporate artificial memory into Prudence and Aristotelian psychology.
- thirteenth–fifteenth centuries: preaching, devotional meditation, manuscript diagrams, alphabetic devices, and artes memoriae proliferate.
- 1482/1485: Publicius prints diagrams and a visual alphabet.
- 1491/92: Peter of Ravenna’s *Phoenix* popularizes practical memory.
- 1502/03: Reuchlin’s *Liber congestorum de arte praedicandi* circulates.
- November 1513: Romberch says the *Congestorium* is complete in Cologne.
- 1513–20: Romberch participates in the Reuchlin controversy and travels through Rome, Bologna, and Venice.
- 9 July 1520: first edition printed by Rusconi.
- early 1533: probable death of Romberch.
- July 1533: Sessa edition.
- 1562: Lodovico Dolce’s Italian *Dialogo* adapts the book; reprinted 1575 and 1586.
- 1579: Rossellius’s *Thesaurus artificiosae memoriae* develops many parallel systems.
- late sixteenth century: Bruno transforms textbook memory within his combinatorial philosophy and magic.
- seventeenth century: Schenkel, Paepp, Fludd, and others continue or transform the tradition.
- 1920s–30s: Volkmann and Hajdu restore Romberch to historical visibility.
- 1966: Yates makes him central to the Renaissance-memory narrative.
- 2001 onward: Torre, Vasoli, Merino Jerez, and Ramos Grané re-establish the primary-text and biographical record.
- 2024: first critical edition.
- 2025/26: first full Spanish translation advertised.

## 2. Dominican institutions

Romberch belonged to the observant Dominicans of Cologne. The convent and University of Cologne were major centers of scholastic theology and guardians of doctrinal orthodoxy. The University possessed censorship authority and opposed some humanist innovations, yet individual Dominicans engaged with Greek, Hebrew, classical rhetoric, and Italian print culture.

Romberch studied or worked in:

- Cologne’s Dominican and university environment;
- Rome, where he was connected with Silvestro Mazzolini da Prierio and acted in the Reuchlin affair;
- Bologna, where he continued theological study;
- Venice, where he edited scholastic works and printed the *Congestorium*.

Cardinal Domenico Grimani, to whom the printed book was dedicated, possessed a celebrated library rich in Latin, Greek, and Hebrew books. Romberch praises both the library and the palace rooms as exemplary mnemonic places. **[TEXT]**

## 3. Humanism and scholasticism

Romberch cannot be classified neatly as either an anti-humanist scholastic or a humanist rhetorician.

Documented scholastic features include:

- Thomas and Albert as authorities;
- Aristotelian faculty psychology;
- divisions of being and the sciences;
- Dominican institutional formation.

Documented humanist-facing features include:

- engagement with Cicero, Quintilian, Petrarch, Publicius, Peter of Ravenna, and Reuchlin;
- interest in Greek and Hebrew books;
- Venetian print and diagram culture;
- efforts to refine presentation and provide practical access to learning.

“Between scholasticism and humanism” is therefore a better description than either exclusive label.

## 4. Courts, universities, and professions

The juridical examples and notarial metaphor are functional, not decorative. Lawyers require ordered heads of a case, witnesses, authorities, and citations. Professors and students require divisions of sciences, propositions, syllogisms, lectures, and disputations. Preachers require themes, divisions, authorities, examples, and rapid oral delivery. Merchants require merchandise, debts, obligations, numbers, and page references.

The book’s institutional horizon includes convent school, university classroom, pulpit, confessional, law court, mercantile office, and print shop.

## 5. Franciscan and broader scholastic contexts

Artificial memory was not uniquely Dominican. Franciscans, Augustinians, secular masters, humanists, physicians, jurists, and itinerant teachers also transmitted it. The specific ethical elevation of memory under Prudence is strongly Albertine-Thomist, but the method of loci belongs to the common rhetorical curriculum.

## 6. Ramism and commonplaces

Ramist method replaced many moving, affective images with visible logical partitions. Printed commonplace books similarly externalized topical storage. This did not instantaneously destroy artificial memory; the traditions coexisted. Romberch’s own diagrams already show traffic between imagistic memory, logical division, and tabular organization.

## 7. Universal language

Visual alphabets and combinatorial figures may look like universal writing, but Romberch’s codes depend on Latin names, local acquaintances, and personal associations. They are not a universal language in the later sense. Rossi’s genealogy is best understood as a history of recurring ambitions to formalize thought, not proof that Romberch designed a universally decodable code.

## People

## 1. Johannes Host von Romberch

His birth is disputed:

- Nikolaus Paulus proposed about 1480 and Dominican entry around 1495.
- Cesare Vasoli proposed about 1485 and entry around 1505–06.
- The inference depends on an ambiguous autobiographical statement about an event twenty-five years earlier, possibly entry into the Order or priestly ordination. **[DISPUTED]**

He probably died late in 1532 or early in 1533 at Cologne. **[RECONSTRUCTION]**

His other work was largely theological, editorial, catechetical, and anti-Lutheran. The *Congestorium* is early and exceptional within his oeuvre.

## 2. Johann Grevenbach

Friend, dedicatee of the original 1513 composition, and requested recipient of the mnemonic art. Possibly represented as a student in the didactic voice of the book, though this is not certain.

## 3. Cardinal Domenico Grimani

Printed-edition dedicatee, patronal figure, Hebraist, book collector, and member of the commission judging Reuchlin. His palace and library are praised as ready-made mnemonic architecture.

## 4. Jacob Hoogstraten

Dominican inquisitor at Cologne, Romberch’s superior or powerful associate, and principal opponent of Reuchlin. Romberch acted as his attorney or agent in Rome.

## 5. Johannes Reuchlin

Christian Hebraist and author of *De verbo mirifico*, *De arte cabbalistica*, Hebrew studies, and the *Liber congestorum de arte praedicandi*. He was prosecuted in the controversy over Jewish books. Paradoxically, Romberch participated on Hoogstraten’s side while continuing to cite Reuchlin respectfully as an authority on preaching. **[TEXT/BIOGRAPHY]**

## 6. Silvestro Mazzolini da Prierio

Dominican theologian and papal official whom Romberch called his teacher in Rome. Associated with the anti-Reuchlin and later anti-Lutheran Dominican establishment.

## 7. Publicius and Peter of Ravenna

They are Romberch’s most important recent mnemonic predecessors:

- Publicius supplies major diagrams and alphabetic-combinatorial material.
- Peter supplies practical rules, distances, markers, known-person images, demonstrations, and much of the experiential persona of the memory master.

## 8. Lodovico Dolce

Dolce’s *Dialogo del modo di accrescere et conservar la memoria* of 1562 is substantially an Italian transformation of Romberch. He changes the manual into dialogue, omits much scholastic material, expands literary examples, and suppresses Romberch’s name except for an oblique reference to “a German.” **[TEXTUAL DEPENDENCE]**

Dolce’s adaptation helped the system circulate among readers who would not use difficult scholastic Latin.

## 9. Cosmas Rossellius

Dominican author of the *Thesaurus artificiosae memoriae* (1579). He expands celestial, infernal, paradisal, alphabetical, and architectural systems and transmits Romberch’s friars’-cells anecdote.

## The magic question

## 1. Is the *Congestorium* magical?

In its explicit theory and ordinary operations, no. **[TEXT]**

Its causal explanation is:

- natural memory;
- sensory perception;
- phantasy;
- corporeal similitudes;
- order;
- attention;
- repetition;
- habituation.

There is no instruction to summon spirits, consecrate talismans, capture stellar rays, pronounce divine names magically, or obtain knowledge without study.

## 2. Cosmic and zodiacal imagery

Cosmic spheres, zodiac signs, and constellations provide ordered loci. Celestial content alone does not make a system astral magic. Romberch does not give planetary hours, talismanic materials, suffumigations, invocations, or electional procedures. **[TEXT/ABSENCE]**

## 3. Kabbalah

Romberch explicitly mentions *Cabala* while discussing oral teaching:

> *Nosti siquidem quaedam praecepta a Deo Iudaeis per scripta tradita … alia autem per Cabalam, quam receptionem aiunt, sola traditione digniorum auribus indita.*

“You know that some precepts were delivered by God to the Jews in writing … but others through Kabbalah, which they call ‘reception,’ imparted by oral tradition to the ears of the worthier.”  
—1520, f. 30v, II.8. **[TEXT]**

He compares restricted oral Kabbalistic transmission with mnemonic arts that cannot be fully learned from a written manual. Elsewhere:

> *Sunt namque plurima saepe numero secreta litteris propter indignos non committenda, sed verbatim quibus ea favemus communicanda.*

“There are often many secrets that should not be committed to writing because of the unworthy, but should be communicated orally to those whom we favor.”  
—f. 23r. **[TEXT]**

Ramos Grané argues that Romberch’s tone is strikingly neutral and that he accommodates Kabbalah as a parallel of initiatory oral transmission. **[RECONSTRUCTION]** Her stronger suggestion that this places him in a Neoplatonic-Kabbalistic stream must remain interpretative: Romberch’s direct knowledge of Abulafian Kabbalah has not been demonstrated.

## 4. Alchemy

Romberch treats alchemy as a real but difficult and obscure art:

> *Quamquam non ignorem artem veram esse, est tamen tempus breve, experimentum fallax, ars vero difficilis.*

“Although I do not deny that the art is true, nevertheless time is short, experiment deceptive, and the art difficult.”  
—f. 56v. **[TEXT]**

His immediate concern is how alchemists transfer planetary names to metals and conceal meanings in specialized vocabulary. It is an example of metalepsis and coded naming, not an alchemical recipe.

## 5. Ars notoria

The *ars notoria* promised knowledge through prayers, divine names, figures, ritual observation, and angelic or divine assistance. Romberch’s method requires disciplined natural practice. No operational *ars notoria* ritual appears in the *Congestorium*. **[ABSENCE]**

Both systems could be described as “arts” involving figures, secrecy, and learning, but functional similarity at that level is insufficient to establish descent.

## 6. Heresy and the Index

Romberch’s name and *De idoneo verbi Dei ministro* appear in the Roman Index of 1557; his other works were included in the broader 1559 prohibition. **[DOCUMENTED RECEPTION]**

This is paradoxical because he was a vehement defender of Catholic orthodoxy and approved severe action against Lutheran reformers. Older biographical tradition speculated that:

- he converted late in life after contemplating the courage of condemned reformers; or
- the Index’s compilers mistook the evangelical-sounding title *De idoneo verbi Dei ministro* for a Lutheran work.

Neither explanation is documented. **[LEGEND/RECONSTRUCTION]**

There is no evidence that the *Congestorium* itself was prohibited because its memory method was magical, Kabbalistic, or heretical. **[ABSENCE]**

## 7. Consequences

No trial, imprisonment, censure, or ecclesiastical punishment has been found that was imposed upon Romberch for mnemonic practice. His involvement with inquisitorial institutions was principally as an associate of the accusers, not as an accused magician.

## Afterlife

## 1. Dolce

The clearest direct afterlife is textual. Dolce’s 1562 *Dialogo* translates, rearranges, modernizes, and selectively secularizes the *Congestorium*. It reproduces the cosmic diagram and visual-alphabet imagery using blocks descending from the Romberch editions. Reprints appeared in 1575 and 1586.

Dolce adds an explicit reference to Dante when discussing the memory of Hell. Consequently, any “Dante connection” in Dolce cannot automatically be projected backward into Romberch. **[TEXTUAL CORRECTION]**

## 2. Rossellius

Rossellius’s 1579 *Thesaurus* continues the Dominican textbook stream. His elaborate diagrams of Hell and Paradise make visually explicit what Romberch often described verbally. Rossellius’s Hell contains differentiated punishments for heretics, infidels, idolaters, hypocrites, and the seven deadly sins.

## 3. Bruno

Bruno’s works contain recognizable descendants or transformations of:

- visual alphabets;
- number-shapes;
- professions and shops;
- bodily loci;
- constellation sequences;
- combinatorial wheels;
- lists of agents, actions, attributes, and circumstances.

But Bruno’s systems often place these within a metaphysics of universal animation, magical bonds, celestial imagery, and combinatorial invention alien to Romberch’s ordinary textbook practice. **[CONTINUITY plus TRANSFORMATION]**

## 4. Fludd and Schenkel

Robert Fludd illustrates visual alphabets and architectural theatres; Lambert Schenkel and his students marketed practical memory instruction across Europe. They share the expanding textbook repertory to which Romberch contributed. Direct borrowing must be established passage by passage rather than inferred from any visual alphabet.

## 5. Ramus

Ramist outlines and dichotomies offered a competing model of order. Yet Romberch’s tree and disciplinary tables show that the border between imagistic memory and diagrammatic method was already permeable.

## 6. Preaching and emblem culture

Personification, attribute, motto, pictorial letter, and composite image overlap formally with:

- sermon imagery;
- devotional images;
- imprese;
- emblems;
- hieroglyphic collections;
- encyclopaedic frontispieces.

Romberch’s Grammar can look like an inscrutable emblem if its mnemonic decoding rule is lost, as Yates observed. Formal resemblance, however, is not proof that every emblem was designed as a mnemonic image.

## 7. Dante and frescoes

The broad medieval culture of architectonic, moral, and affective memory is relevant to Dante and didactic fresco cycles. Specific assertions that the *Comedy*, Giotto’s Arena Chapel, or the Spanish Chapel at Santa Maria Novella embodies a technical locus system remain reconstructions associated especially with Yates.

Romberch, born nearly two centuries later, can witness the survival of a tradition; he cannot independently prove the mechanism by which an earlier poem or fresco was composed.

## 8. Borges, Eco, and literary afterlives

### Borges’s Funes

“Funes the Memorious” is best read as an inversion of artificial memory: Funes retains limitless particulars but cannot abstract, classify, or forget. Romberch’s system depends precisely on selection, division, symbolic condensation, and erasure. The comparison is illuminating but not a documented literary borrowing from Romberch. **[MODERN ANALOGY]**

### Eco

Umberto Eco’s histories of perfect languages, semiotics, encyclopedias, and combinatorial thought discuss the larger tradition linking Lullism, memory, signs, and universal-language projects. This is an intellectual afterlife of the problem, not necessarily reception of the *Congestorium* itself.

## 9. Modern memory palaces and athletes

Modern competitors still use:

- fixed routes;
- striking interactive images;
- person-action-object combinations;
- number-shape systems;
- alphabetic pegs;
- reuse of loci;
- forward and reverse traversal.

These are structurally close to Romberch. Direct transmission is usually mediated by modern mnemonic manuals rather than continuous study of his Latin text. **[MODERN REVIVAL, indirect genealogy]**

Romberch’s five/ten markers resemble modern checkpointing; his known-person and profession images resemble person-action-object systems; his bird and object alphabets resemble alphabet pegs.

## 10. Computing and combinatorics

The defensible history is plural:

- Llull formalized combinations of symbolic terms for theological demonstration.
- Renaissance mnemonic writers adapted rotating or recombinable letters and figures.
- Bruno integrated combination with memory, logic, and natural magic.
- Seventeenth-century universal-language authors and Leibniz pursued formal character systems and calculable reasoning.
- Modern computing realizes symbolic operations mechanically.

The statement “Llull invented the computer” or “Romberch’s wheel is an early computer” is a modern metaphor. **[MODERN INVENTION]** No continuous engineering lineage connects Romberch’s volvelle to electronic computation.

## Controversies and disputes

## 1. Author’s name

“Johannes Romberch” is a toponymic or authorial form. “Johannes Host,” “Host von Romberch,” “Romberg,” “Romberch de Kyrspe,” and “Kierspe” occur in scholarship and catalogues. They designate the same Dominican. Exact birth name and the relation of the names to farm, village, or family remain imperfectly documented.

## 2. Birth and death

Birth: approximately 1480–85.  
Death: probably late 1532 or early 1533.  
Both remain reconstructed rather than established by a surviving birth or death record.

## 3. Date of the system

- 1513 is the stated completion date.
- 1520 is the first publication date.
- 1533 is the second edition used by Yates.

Calling it simply “a system of 1520” is bibliographically convenient but conceals its pre-Reuchlin-trial composition and possible Italian revision.

## 4. Number of editions

The specialist consensus located recognizes two Latin editions, 1520 and 1533. Claims for a 1553 Latin edition generally appear to be typographical errors for 1533. Dolce’s 1562 version is a separate Italian adaptation, not a third Latin edition. **[CORRECTION]**

## 5. Manuscript variants

No manuscript recension is known. Accordingly, “variants across manuscripts and editions” must here mean:

- no identified Romberch manuscript variants;
- differences between the 1520 and 1533 printed settings;
- internal inconsistencies such as *anser/aquila*;
- altered, omitted, or expanded material in Dolce;
- later reuse by Rossellius and others.

## 6. Source labeled “Petrarch”

A marginal attribution names “Franciscus Petrarcha” for an order rule. Andrea Torre and Ramos Grané observe that the precept does not occur in Petrarch and that Romberch normally calls him simply *Petrarcha*. They propose Franciscus Maturantius/Maturanzio as the intended source. **[TEXTUAL DISPUTE]**

This is a concrete correction to Yates’s broad statement that Romberch’s frequent invocation “absorbs Petrarch into the Dominican memory tradition.” Romberch does cite Petrarch elsewhere, but at least this particular attribution is probably erroneous.

## 7. Dependence on Peter of Ravenna

The practical doctrine follows Peter closely, but “entirely modeled” on the *Phoenix* is too strong. Romberch adds scholastic psychology, extensive source collation, diagrams, alphabets, disciplinary applications, a developed theory of images, and personal experiments.

## 8. Dependence on Publicius

Publicius supplies several major illustrations and devices. Romberch is sometimes an interpreter, sometimes a copier, and sometimes a critic. He expressly admits difficulty understanding the quadrangle. His work should not be treated as transparent evidence of what Publicius originally meant.

## 9. Originality

Older narratives oscillate between calling Romberch an unoriginal compiler and treating him as a major inventor. The text supports a middle position:

- few elementary principles are new;
- the synthesis, scale, pedagogical organization, image theory, professional applications, and some adaptations are distinctive;
- Romberch openly claims compilation, so dependence is not concealed plagiarism.

## 10. The “Dantesque” system

The places of the afterlife are documented. The direct Dante genealogy is disputed and unproved.

## 11. Thomas Aquinas’s own memory system

Thomas recommends artificial-memory principles. No surviving Thomas text sets out the Romberch-style encyclopedia conjectured by Yates.

## 12. Magic

The book contains Kabbalah, alchemy, cosmic imagery, secrecy, and combinatorial figures as topics or analogies. It lacks operational ritual magic. To call the entire *Congestorium* magical confuses it with Bruno’s transformation of related materials.

## 13. Romberch as heretic

His appearance in an Index is documented. His late conversion is legend. The cause of listing remains uncertain. His surviving polemical record is overwhelmingly anti-Lutheran.

## 14. Anti-Lutheran purpose of the *Congestorium*

Some booksellers and catalogue descriptions say that it was designed to combat Lutheranism. This is chronologically weak: Romberch dated its completion to 1513, before Luther’s public controversy. The 1520 publication occurred amid the Reformation, and the method could serve Catholic preaching, but an original anti-Lutheran purpose is not stated in the book. **[MODERN OVERSTATEMENT]**

## 15. Bruno’s personal use of Romberch

Textual parallels support influence from the same printed tradition. A personally owned or annotated Bruno copy has not been produced. The stronger biographical assertion remains unproved.

## 16. Visual alphabets as universal language

They are dependent on Latin vocabulary and personal knowledge. They encode rather than abolish language. Their inclusion in a history leading toward universal character is legitimate; describing them as already universal is not.

## Open questions

1. Is there a still-unidentified authorial or presentation manuscript behind the 1520 printing?
2. Which passages were revised between completion in Cologne in 1513 and publication in Venice in 1520?
3. Can the 1520 and 1533 editions be exhaustively collated beyond the “no significant differences” judgment?
4. Who designed and cut the twenty-three woodblocks?
5. Which blocks originated with Publicius, which were redrawn, and which were made specifically for Romberch?
6. Was the movable component supplied in every 1520 copy, or only in some?
7. What is the explanation of the additional end plate catalogued in the Braidense copy?
8. Can ownership annotations establish actual classroom, preaching, legal, or mercantile use?
9. Who was the auditor who corrected the friars’-cell system?
10. Does Romberch’s account preserve a real classroom exchange or adapt an earlier *exemplum*?
11. Which “Franciscus” lies behind the false or corrupt Petrarch attribution?
12. How much direct knowledge did Romberch possess of Hebrew and Kabbalah?
13. Did he read Reuchlin’s Kabbalistic works, or only absorb generalized reports?
14. How should the tetragram at f. 61r be interpreted within his image system?
15. Does the theological figure trampling a divine figure have a Kabbalistic context, or is that proposed connection overinterpretation?
16. What exact form of Publicius’s work did Romberch use?
17. Which edition of Peter of Ravenna lay before him?
18. Are some rules attributed to Peter absent from all known *Phoenix* editions because Romberch used a lost recension, oral teaching, or faulty attribution?
19. How widely was the Latin book actually read? Surviving-copy, citation, and annotation studies are needed to test Rossi’s “widely read throughout Europe.”
20. Can direct lines of borrowing from Romberch to Bruno or Fludd be demonstrated by unique errors rather than generic similarities?
21. Why exactly was Romberch entered in the Roman Index despite his anti-Lutheran record?
22. Was the Index listing an author-name error, a title-based suspicion, an unknown doctrinal objection, or a response to a lost work?
23. How far did Dolce’s omissions change the confessional and scholastic character of the system?
24. Did merchants, messengers, physicians, or notaries actually use the specialized applications advertised on the title page?
25. How did readers manipulate the volvelle, and do surviving copies preserve original attachment states?
26. Can digital reconstruction test the practical capacity and ambiguity of the Grammar image, city, abbey, and combinatorial apparatus without mistaking experimental usability for historical proof?

- **[TEXT]** expressly documented by Romberch’s text or its material printing.
- **[CLASSICAL TEXT]** documented in an ancient source.

- **[RECONSTRUCTION]** a modern scholar’s reasoned reconstruction.
- **[YATES]** Frances Yates’s interpretation, rather than Romberch’s own assertion.
- **[DISPUTED]** contested attribution, dating, source identification, or interpretation.
- **[TRADITION]** a claim repeated in historical literature without equivalent direct evidence.
- **[LEGEND]** an anecdote or assertion whose historical occurrence has not been independently demonstrated.

- **[ABSENCE]** something sought but not found in the inspected evidence.

Two preliminary cautions are essential.

First, this is not a single “memory palace.” It is a manual encompassing several compatible place systems and many image-making procedures. Second, Yates’s “three different types of place systems” is a helpful synopsis, not Romberch’s exhaustive taxonomy. Recent study emphasizes a considerably richer apparatus: bodily places, trees, rooms, alphabetical lists, grammatical paradigms, astronomical sequences, professions, gestures, syllables, logical divisions, legal books, speeches, games, numbers, and movable combinatorial diagrams.

## The system in detail

## 1. Purpose, definition, and intended users

The title page advertises the work to theologians, preachers, confessors, jurists, judges, procurators, advocates, notaries, physicians, philosophers, teachers of the liberal arts, merchants, messengers, and couriers. **[TEXT]** It is therefore not addressed only to monks or contemplatives. Its applications extend from sermons and university disputation to litigation, debts, merchandise, page numbers, dice, cards, chess, and the rapid retention of spoken discourse.

Romberch explains why the work is a *Congestorium*:

> *Ex diversis comparavimus authorum libris, quorum canones et regulas in unum congessimus ad instar notariorum prothocollum diversarum causarum et negotiorum litis perscribentium … Cui proinde Congestorium artificiose memorative nomen imponimus.*

“We gathered [the material] from the books of different authors, whose canons and rules we collected into one, after the fashion of notaries writing a protocol of the different causes and business of a lawsuit … We therefore give it the name *Congestorium* of artificial memory.”  
—1520, prefatory epistle to Johann Grevenbach, f. A6r in the signed gathering; modern critical citation f. 6r. **[TEXT]**

This materially qualifies Yates’s joke that the title is appropriate because the book is “a strange congestion of memory material.” Romberch deliberately uses *congerere*—to gather or heap together—as a methodological term. Luis Merino Jerez argues that the title denotes something like an evidentiary notarial dossier: different authorities are collected without every disagreement being suppressed. **[RECONSTRUCTION]** The “congestion” is therefore partly a designed compilation and not merely intellectual decay.

Romberch defines the discipline as an aid to natural memory:

> *Ars memorativa dicitur, quandoquidem ingenii bonitas saepe imitatur artem; ars vero naturae commoda confirmat et auget.*

“It is called the mnemonic art because excellence of talent often imitates art, while art confirms and increases the advantages of nature.”  
—Tract I, ch. 4. **[TEXT]**

The art does not create the faculty of memory. It assists the natural power so that its acts are performed more easily and firmly. This is the conventional classical-scholastic distinction between *memoria naturalis* and *memoria artificialis*.

The book was initially written for Johann Grevenbach, whom Romberch calls a friend eager for every discipline. He says Grevenbach asked him to preserve the *artificium* they used in urgent or improvised matters. **[TEXT]** Whether Grevenbach is also the unnamed student in Romberch’s teaching anecdotes is possible but unproved. **[RECONSTRUCTION]**

## 2. Architecture of the treatise

The work contains four treatises:

1. **Seven preliminary chapters**: possibility, necessity, usefulness, detractors, suitable users, definitions and divisions of memory, foundations, impediments, and the need for places and images.
2. **Nine chapters on places**: their nature, kinds, number, marking, acquisition, conditions, construction, fictive places, examples, and erasure.
3. **Nineteen chapters on images**: definition, division, size, quality, action and passion, sources, signification, adaptation, letter-images, living alphabets, syllables, grammatical cases, words, metaphor, etymology, bodily movements, insignia, and combination.
4. **Fourteen chapters on applications**: natural and artificial things, concepts and words, the sciences, divisions of being, propositions, authorities and citation, questions, syllogisms, lectures, sermons, histories, verse, rapid retention of speech, commerce and games, reading, meditation, and repetition. **[TEXT]**

The fourth treatise is consequently much more than the abbreviated “encyclopaedic system” visible in Yates. Its practical range is one of the strongest corrections made by recent scholarship.

## 3. Psychological mechanism

Romberch’s psychology is late-scholastic and broadly Aristotelian-Thomist. Sensation supplies forms; the internal senses process them; phantasy supplies the corporeal similitudes without which embodied human understanding cannot ordinarily think; memory retains them; recollection searches them through order. **[TEXT/CONTEXT]**

The 1520 book includes a woodcut diagram of the external senses and powers of the soul, at f. 9v in the modern foliation. It relates sensory reception to internal faculties rather than depicting a supernatural channel of revelation. **[TEXT]**

Thomas Aquinas is indeed frequently cited, particularly the *Summa theologiae* II–II, q. 49, a. 1, ad 2 and the commentary on Aristotle’s *De memoria et reminiscentia*. Thomas’s four familiar mnemonic principles are:

- choose appropriate corporeal similitudes;
- arrange what is to be remembered in a considered order;
- apply solicitude or affective engagement;
- meditate frequently upon what has been stored.

Romberch embeds these principles within a much larger apparatus of loci, images, alphabets, lists, and exercises. **[TEXT]**

Retrieval is therefore neither photographic recall nor the passive reading of a universal code. The practitioner:

1. establishes a stable sequence of places;
2. gives each place a recognizable distinction;
3. converts the desired material into personally intelligible images;
4. deposits the images in order;
5. fixes them through attention, meditation, and repetition;
6. mentally traverses the series;
7. interprets or “reads” each image;
8. can traverse the places forward, backward, or from a chosen point.

Romberch claims to have demonstrated this flexibility by reciting the same material from its beginning, end, or middle:

> *Facile quippe est ex locis ordine collocatis materiam imaginatam ordinate et sapienter pronunciare, certitudinaliter de uno in alterum procedendo ordine recto et reflexo atque praepostero.*

“For it is easy, from places disposed in order, to pronounce imagined material orderly and intelligently, proceeding securely from one to another in direct, reversed, or inverted order.”  
—1520, f. 21v. **[TEXT: autobiographical claim]**

That he made the demonstration is Romberch’s testimony; no independent witness has been identified. It should not be promoted from a first-person claim to externally verified fact.

## 4. What serves as places

### 4.1 Definition

Romberch distinguishes common or inclusive place from proper or particular mnemonic place. In the second treatise he writes that the particular places used here are those in which one immediately inscribes the imagined thing:

> *Ita et nos in praesentiarum accipimus locos proprios sive particulares, illos quibus immediate rem imaginatam inscribimus: quales sunt parietes, fenestrae, columnae in cameris, aestuariis, cenaculis domorum … vel arbores, plantae, petrae.*

“We likewise presently take proper or particular places to be those in which we immediately inscribe the imagined thing: such as walls, windows, columns in chambers, heated rooms and dining rooms of houses … or trees, plants and rocks.”  
—Tract II, ch. 2. **[TEXT]**

Thus a *locus* need not be a whole room. It can be a bounded surface or recognizable feature within a larger containing place.

### 4.2 Natural, artificial, real, and fictive places

Romberch admits:

- natural places: trees, rocks, landscape features;
- artificial places: buildings, walls, windows, columns, furniture, streets, shops;
- real or previously perceived places;
- fictive places deliberately constructed in imagination;
- cosmic and theological places;
- bodily members;
- diagrammatic or logical places, such as branches of a tree.

Yates’s three groups—cosmos, zodiac/constellations, and real buildings—select the most visually arresting examples but do not exhaust Romberch’s classifications. **[YATES, corrected by TEXT]**

### 4.3 Rules for places

The second treatise, ch. 5, gives seven principal conditions with supplementary qualifications. Recent reconstruction from the 1520 edition yields:

1. **Order.** Places require a suitable, ordered disposition. The sequence begins at the viewer’s left:

   > *Id igitur in primis praecipimus, ut in debita ordinataque dispositione tibi loca vendices … a sinistra tamen sumendus est.*

   “First we prescribe that you appropriate places for yourself in a proper and ordered disposition … the order must nevertheless be taken from the left.”  
   —f. 21r. **[TEXT]**

2. **Distance.** Adjacent loci should be approximately five or six feet apart:

   > *Congrua quoque locorum expetitur distantia.*

   “An appropriate distance between places is also required.”  
   —f. 21v. **[TEXT]**

   Romberch says twice that experience confirmed Peter of Ravenna’s measure.

3. **Proportion.** The locus must suit the dimensions of the image:

   > *Ea quoque continua erit locorum quantitas, ne sit nimis arcta vel alta; imagines siquidem latitudinis, superficiei et longitudinis proportionem exigent.*

   “The continuous extent of the places should be such that it is neither too narrow nor too high, since images require proportion in breadth, surface and length.”  
   —f. 22r. **[TEXT]**

4. **Moderate illumination.** A place must be neither excessively dark nor excessively bright.

5. **Distinctiveness.** Places must not be confusingly similar. The text also warns against excessive circularity or roundness because it does not discriminate positions well.

6. **Availability and prior inspection.** Places should be “at hand,” stable, and ideally have been seen at least once empty of crowds.

7. **Fixity.** The chosen framework should not move or escape. A ship is a poor permanent locus because its physical and visual relations change. **[TEXT]**

The formulation “places must be spherical” sometimes appears through mistranscription or compressed paraphrase. The Latin says the opposite: excessively spherical or round places are insufficiently discriminating. **[TEXTUAL CORRECTION]**

### 4.4 Fifth and tenth markers

Romberch follows the ancient recommendation to mark every fifth place and adapts Peter of Ravenna’s expanded series:

- fifth: a golden hand;
- tenth: a golden cross;
- fifteenth: a silver hand;
- twentieth: an image of the number;
- the procedure continues analogically.

The *Ad Herennium* had proposed a golden hand at the fifth place and a person named Decimus at the tenth. Peter substituted a cross for Decimus, making the sequence more congenial to Christian practice. Romberch’s abbey plate displays the hand and cross. **[TEXT/TRANSMISSION]**

Yates remarks that the hand associates the fifth place with five fingers and imagines Memory “ticking off” places on the fingers. The five-finger association is plausible, but the specific account of Memory ticking them off is Yates’s explanatory prose, not Romberch’s statement. **[YATES/RECONSTRUCTION]**

### 4.5 The human reach rule

The human figure in the measuring woodcut stretches upward and sideways against a rectangular locus. Its purpose is to show that a place holding a human image should remain within the perceptual compass of a human figure and should not be so large that the image loses definition. **[TEXT/IMAGE]**

Yates writes that it “must not be larger than a man can reach.” This is an intelligible summary, but recent scholarship places the figure within a broader Renaissance discussion of human proportion. The 2024 critical study compares it with proportional figures attributed to Leonardo da Vinci, Francesco di Giorgio Martini, and Giacomo Andrea. **[RECONSTRUCTION]**

The rule comes substantially through Peter of Ravenna; it is not an original invention of Romberch. **[TEXTUAL DEPENDENCE]**

## 5. The monastery experiment: a documented practitioner’s error

This is the best evidence that Romberch did not merely copy rules but tested and revised a system.

He initially used the cells in a Dominican dormitory as places and became confused because the doors resembled one another. Someone advised:

> *Loca non fiant in cellis fratrum.*

“Let places not be made in the friars’ cells.”  
—f. 23v. **[TEXT]**

While Romberch was teaching this warning, one of his auditors objected that similar doors caused him no difficulty because he associated each friar with his office. The sacristan could be marked by sacristy instruments; the public lecturer by the books of his lecture; other friars by the tools or attributes of their work.

Romberch accepted the correction:

> *Et eo quidem libentius hac convincebar argumentatione quo et discipulum perficere didici.*

“I was the more willingly persuaded by this argument because I learned that a pupil too can perfect [his teacher].”  
—f. 23v. **[TEXT]**

He therefore recommends differentiating each cell door externally by an appropriate sign and internally by the occupant and office.

Important distinctions:

- That Romberch recounts the incident is **[TEXT]**.
- That the unnamed auditor was Grevenbach is **[RECONSTRUCTION]**, not established.
- That the incident precisely occurred as narrated is an autobiographical assertion, not independently documented.
- Similar warnings against friars’ cells appear in the earlier *Memoria fecunda* and Jacopo Ragona’s *Artificialis memoriae regulae*. Romberch’s initial “mistake” may therefore be a didactic *exemplum* shaped by tradition. **[DISPUTED/RECONSTRUCTION]**
- Cosmas Rossellius later repeats the story, showing textual reception, not independent corroboration. **[TRANSMISSION]**

## 6. The abbey memory system

The woodcut conventionally called the Abbey Memory System shows a monastic complex as a framework for ordered loci. Its spaces include the courtyard, library or book room, and chapel. Separate images present the objects to be deposited in these places. Every fifth position is marked by a hand and every tenth by a cross. **[TEXT/PLATE]**

The system should be understood operationally:

1. learn the abbey and its approach;
2. subdivide it into sequential, visually distinct loci;
3. mark the numerically significant positions;
4. associate each room with a semantic field where useful;
5. deposit active or otherwise memorable images;
6. retrieve by re-entering the complex mentally and following its fixed circuit.

The plate is not an architectural survey of a particular surviving abbey. No building has been securely identified as its prototype. **[ABSENCE]**

The objects in the associated woodcut are mnemonic stock, not necessarily the permanent contents of an actual monastic library or chapel. Their functions vary: some represent their ordinary referents, some work by name, shape, profession, pun, attribute, or a personal association.

A surviving copy of the 1533 edition at the University of Pennsylvania has the call number **GC5 H7958 520c 1533**. The Library of Congress copy is **BF383 .H7**. The NYPL digital copy is catalogued under **\*KB 1533 (Host von Romberch, J.)**. **[BIBLIOGRAPHY]**

## 7. The city, streets, shops, and rooms

The modern critical edition identifies:

- “Romberch’s city,” 1520 f. 28r;
- “Romberch’s rooms,” f. 29r.

The city woodcut represents an ordered street environment whose buildings and shops supply distinct visual places. A bookseller, *Bibliopola*, is among the shops; bibliographical literature sometimes describes it as an unusually early representation of a street-facing bookshop. **[TEXT/PLATE; priority claim DISPUTED]**

Yates later connects Bruno’s “shops” with this textbook tradition. The resemblance is real, but a direct act of borrowing by Bruno from a particular copy of Romberch has not been demonstrated. **[YATES/RECONSTRUCTION]**

Rooms may be subdivided by walls, windows, columns, corners, furniture, and bodies. The mental route must remain unambiguous. Similar rooms can be repaired by color, inscriptions, objects, persons, or professional attributes.

## 8. Cosmic places

### 8.1 Spheres of the universe

Romberch reproduces and explains a nested cosmological diagram derived substantially from Jacobus Publicius and related scholastic cosmographies. In the 1520 critical foliation it occurs at f. 25v; Yates, using 1533 pagination, cited pp. 17r ff. and 31r ff.

The diagram contains concentric regions for:

- earth;
- water;
- air;
- fire;
- the planetary heavens;
- the fixed stars;
- higher celestial spheres;
- the nine angelic orders.

At its foot appear abbreviated theological realms:

- **L.PA**: *locus Paradisi*, Paradise;
- **L.P**: *locus Paradisi terrestris*, Earthly Paradise;
- **PVR**: Purgatory;
- **IN**: *Infernus*, Hell.

Romberch defines such settings as *ficta loca*, imagined places. Paradise is divided into seats or stations for angelic choirs, patriarchs, prophets, apostles, martyrs, and other blessed orders. Purgatory and Hell are “common” or inclusive places, subdivided into particular loci. Images of sinners punished according to their sins occupy Hell, with inscriptions explaining or fixing the relevant matter. **[TEXT]**

These are mnemonic applications of the Christian cosmos, not instructions for astral magic.

### 8.2 Yates’s “Dantesque type”

Yates writes:

> “This type of artificial memory may be called the Dantesque type, not because the Dominican treatise is influenced by the *Divine Comedy*, but because Dante was influenced by such an interpretation of artificial memory.”

This is one of her most ambitious backward projections. **[YATES]**

What is documented:

- medieval mnemonic theory predates Dante;
- ordered images of virtues, vices, rewards, and punishments belong to preaching, devotional, rhetorical, and artistic traditions;
- Romberch and Rossellius explicitly treat Paradise, Purgatory, and Hell as mnemonic frameworks. **[TEXT/TRADITION]**

What is not documented:

- that Dante learned or composed the *Comedy* from a system identical to Romberch’s;
- that the divisions of the *Comedy* are the product of an otherwise recoverable Dominican memory manual;
- that Santa Maria Novella’s fresco cycle was designed as a working memory system.

Later scholarship, especially Carruthers and Bolzoni, supports the importance of trained, architectonic, affective memory in medieval composition but generally avoids Yates’s strong one-directional causal claim. **[SCHOLARLY CORRECTION]**

“Dantesque” should therefore be retained as Yates’s typological label, not as a documented historical name or proven descent.

## 9. Zodiac and constellations

Romberch recommends the zodiac as an immediately recognizable sequence of twelve places and names Metrodorus of Scepsis as ancient authority. His information comes through Cicero, *De oratore* II.360, and Quintilian, *Institutio oratoria* XI.2.22. **[TEXT/CLASSICAL TEXT]**

For a longer celestial sequence he recommends the constellation figures associated with Hyginus. Alphabetical tables of constellations help index the material. **[TEXT]**

Yates admits that Romberch does not specify what material is to be attached to the constellation figures and guesses that preachers might use them for sermons on virtues, vices, Heaven, and Hell. **[YATES/RECONSTRUCTION]** That guessed content must not be reported as Romberch’s instruction.

The use of celestial images is not itself evidence of talismanic astrology. In Romberch they serve as familiar, ordered pictures. **[TEXT]**

## 10. Trees and the *Hortus philosophiae*

At f. 66v the work presents the *Hortus philosophiae*, a tree-like hierarchy of the sciences. Recent analysis emphasizes that Romberch calls it a *hortus*, garden, rather than simply an *arbor*. The tree is the organizing component within a larger garden of diagrams. **[TEXT/RECONSTRUCTION]**

The figure places branches of knowledge in an ordered hierarchy. Knowing the position of a science in the tree enables the learner to attach and retrieve propositions concerning it. Romberch cites or alludes to established classifications associated with Robert Kilwardby, Hugh of Saint-Victor, scholastic divisions found in Albert the Great and Thomas Aquinas, and a contemporary instructional tradition represented by Poliziano. **[TEXT/SOURCE STUDY]**

The tree is “Lullian” in diagrammatic form only in a broad sense. It resembles widely diffused medieval branching diagrams; direct derivation from a specific work of Ramon Llull has not been established. Recent scholarship describes it as a pedagogical use of a diagrammatic convention associated with Lullism, not as proof that Romberch adopted Llull’s complete Art. **[DISPUTED/QUALIFIED]**

## 11. What serves as images

Romberch distinguishes images through several intersecting categories. They may be:

- representations of things, persons, animals, actions, passions, qualities, or relations;
- derived **from the thing** (*a re*) or **from the word** (*a verbo*);
- natural-looking or fictive;
- human, animal, artificial-object, or composite images;
- active figures engaged in a memorable action;
- bodily gestures;
- professional tools and insignia;
- metaphor, metonymy, transference, or etymology;
- letter-shapes and number-shapes;
- alphabetically indexed living creatures or persons;
- syllabic images;
- images associated with grammatical inflections;
- inscriptions made from pictorial letters;
- combinations of a principal image with subsidiary marks.

The essential requirement is not public symbolism but retrievability for the particular practitioner. An image is successful when its relation to the intended matter is clear, distinctive, durable, and rapidly decoded.

## 12. Rules for images

Romberch inherits the principal ancient rules from the *Ad Herennium* and reframes them through scholastic psychology:

1. images must be concrete enough to enter phantasy;
2. they should be unusual, affective, active, beautiful, ugly, comic, violent, splendid, shameful, or otherwise striking when ordinary images would fade;
3. image and locus must remain distinguishable;
4. the size of an image should suit its locus;
5. a single principal image may gather several subsidiary concepts;
6. action and passion clarify relations;
7. personally known persons and objects are often more effective than generic ones;
8. an image derived from a word must preserve an unmistakable phonetic or orthographic cue;
9. an image derived from a thing must capture the relevant meaning, not merely its sound;
10. associated images and inscriptions may be placed on a person’s head, limbs, clothing, or attributes;
11. the practitioner must rehearse;
12. difficult materials require adaptation to individual experience rather than blind copying.

Yates says that the images are “not illustrated nor … very clearly described.” That is true of the classical-style active images, but misleading if extended to the entire third treatise: the book illustrates letter-images, birds, number signs, Grammar, the quadrangle, a combinatorial device, and several applied systems. **[YATES, qualified]**

## 13. The *Ad Herennium* lawsuit image

The governing ancient example is *Rhetorica ad Herennium* III.20.33. The prosecutor alleges murder by poison, inheritance as motive, and multiple witnesses. The practitioner imagines the sick or dead man in bed and the accused beside him:

> *Et reum ad lectum eius adstituemus, dextra poculum, sinistra tabulas, medico testiculos arietinos tenentem.*

“And we shall place the defendant at his bedside, holding a cup in his right hand, tablets in his left, and ram’s testicles on the fourth finger.”  
—*Rhetorica ad Herennium* III.20.33; Latin text with Harry Caplan’s Loeb translation, 1954. **[CLASSICAL TEXT]**

The cup suggests poison; the tablets suggest the will or inheritance; *testiculi/testes* supplies a pun for witnesses. One image compresses the principal heads of the case.

Romberch transmits the underlying rule that a principal active image may gather associated signs. The lawsuit scene is the classical model for his composite imagery, but it should not be claimed that every surviving edition reproduces it as a woodcut. **[TRANSMISSION; ABSENCE of dedicated plate]**

## 14. Visual alphabets

### 14.1 Material or shape alphabet

Romberch supplies objects whose forms resemble letters. Yates gives the examples of compasses or a ladder for A and a hoe for N. The 1520 sequence occupies ff. 38v–43r in the modern foliation.

This alphabet descends substantially from Publicius, whose printed *Oratoriae artis epitomata* was the first printed mnemonic treatise to illustrate a visual alphabet. **[TEXTUAL DEPENDENCE]**

### 14.2 Living alphabet

A second alphabet associates each letter with the initial of an animal or bird:

- A: *anser*, goose;
- B: *bubo*, owl;
- P: *pica*, magpie;
- and so forth.

The full bird alphabet appears at f. 44r. The precise members reflect Latin vocabulary; it is not a language-neutral code. **[TEXT]**

### 14.3 Persons and professions

Known persons may be ordered by their names. Professions can be encoded through tools and insignia. This makes the system partly private: a practitioner’s acquaintances and professional world supply stronger images than arbitrary textbook figures.

Peter of Ravenna’s famous example is to remember **ET** as Eusebius before Thomas and **TE** by placing Eusebius behind Thomas. **[SOURCE TRANSMITTED BY YATES]**

### 14.4 Numbers

Romberch includes signs for numbers at f. 45r and two further full-alphabet/number plates at ff. 45v–46r. Objects are chosen by shape or conventional association. These anticipate, without being identical to, later number-shape systems. **[TEXT]**

### 14.5 Use and limitation

Yates believed the visual alphabets “were intended to be used for making inscriptions in memory.” That is true of some applications, especially Grammar, but Marta Ramos Grané’s analysis corrects Yates’s broader equation:

- *imagines a verbo* are images formed from the sound or literal form of a word;
- *memoria verborum* means retaining the words of a discourse, whether the images were constructed from sound or meaning;
- pictorial inscription is one resource within the system, not the definition of verbatim memory. **[SCHOLARLY CORRECTION]**

Romberch writes:

> *Pro inscriptione saltem, quae litteris realibus materialibus commode fieri poterit … ubi singula quis verba locare voluerit alicuius textus.*

“For an inscription, at least, which can conveniently be made with real material letters … when someone wishes to place the individual words of a text.”  
—f. 29v. **[TEXT]**

## 15. Grammar as a worked mnemonic image

The female personification of Grammar appears at f. 69v in the 1520 critical foliation; Yates cites 1533 pp. 82v–83r.

She carries the traditional attributes of Grammar, including a ladder and cutting instrument. Her body is also a secondary place-system bearing birds, tools, gestures, and pictorial inscriptions. The question being encoded is whether Grammar is a common or a special science.

The main cues include:

- *predicatio*: a *pica*, magpie, for P, with associated objects;
- *applicatio*: an *aquila*, eagle, on or near the arm;
- *continentia*: material-letter objects across the chest spelling or cueing C–O–N–T.

The bird on the arm is a revealing inconsistency. Romberch’s own alphabetical table gives A as *anser*, but the explanatory text says *aquila*. **[TEXTUAL VARIATION WITHIN THE SYSTEM]** This demonstrates that the lists are adaptable repertoires, not immutable universal characters.

Yates’s conclusion is broadly correct: a familiar personification can function simultaneously as:

- the image of a discipline;
- a bodily set of loci;
- an active figure;
- a support for subsidiary images;
- a tablet for pictorial inscriptions.

But her extension of this example to medieval virtue personifications, Holcot’s Penance, and fresco cycles is inferential. **[YATES/RECONSTRUCTION]**

## 16. The encyclopaedic sciences

Romberch advises forming a representative figure for each science, placing its principles and divisions on the body, and assigning the whole figure to a room.

For Theology:

> *Ut pro theologia consummatum egregiumque aliquem theologum …*

“For Theology [imagine] some consummate and excellent theologian …”  
—Tract IV, ch. 3. **[TEXT]**

The head receives *cognitio, amor, fruitio*—knowledge, love, fruition. Other members carry divine essence, act, form, relation, articles, precepts, sacraments, and further subdivisions. Parallel tables organize:

- theology;
- metaphysics and philosophy;
- moral philosophy;
- law;
- health or medicine;
- astronomy;
- geometry;
- arithmetic;
- music;
- logic;
- rhetoric;
- grammar.

This is not a completed universal encyclopedia containing every proposition. It is a generative template by which a user constructs disciplinary rooms, representative bodies, trees, and attached concepts. **[TEXT/RECONSTRUCTION]**

Yates described this as a “stupendous attempt to hold a vast *summa* of knowledge in memory” and conjectured a “distant echo” of Thomas Aquinas’s own memory system. **[YATES]** No text by Thomas describes an encyclopaedic bodily-room system of this kind. The dependence is conceptual and doctrinal, not evidence for a lost personal memory palace of Aquinas. **[ABSENCE/CORRECTION]**

## 17. Logical and combinatorial apparatus

The third treatise includes:

- a quadrangular diagram at f. 48v;
- Romberch’s combinatorial system at f. 49v;
- a singular/plural device at f. 50v;
- a tetragram at f. 61r.

Romberch attempts to explain a difficult diagram in Publicius and admits that he does not entirely understand his predecessor’s account. **[TEXT]** The diagram recombines letters or grammatical categories to generate cues for words and inflections.

The apparatus resembles Lullian wheels in its permutation of written elements, but its immediate source is Publicius and its purpose is mnemonic-linguistic. It is not the full Lullian *Ars*, whose letters signify divine dignities and relational principles in a demonstrative system. **[TEXTUAL DEPENDENCE/CORRECTION]**

This distinction matters to the supposed genealogy “Llull → Romberch → Bruno → Leibniz → computer.” There is a real history of combinatorial diagrams, but it is not a single uninterrupted technology or doctrine.

## 18. Memory for speech, reading, and citation

Romberch stresses that written material can be impressed through repetition, whereas immediate verbatim retention of a speaker is reserved for highly trained practitioners:

> *Quae ex libris desumimus crebra repetitione memoriae imprimuntur, sed contionatoris, declamatoris vel orantis verba ex tempore mox reponere exercitatissimi est …*

“What we take from books is impressed on memory by frequent repetition, but immediately storing the words of a preacher, declaimer, or speaker as they are delivered belongs to the very highly trained.”  
—f. 82v. **[TEXT]**

He adds:

> *Si diligens fueris auscultator et satis attentus et verba cum imaginibus conferas ac seriose et fortiter imprimas, optime audita commemorabis.*

“If you are a diligent and sufficiently attentive listener, connect words with images, and impress them earnestly and firmly, you will recall what you heard very well.”  
—f. 82v. **[TEXT]**

Recent scholarship accordingly associates Romberch’s *memoria verborum* as much with trained listening and aurality as with writing. **[RECONSTRUCTION]**

## 19. Erasing and reusing places

Tract II, ch. 9 treats evacuation of places by deleting images. The stable locus remains; temporary images are removed after use. This is the ordinary reusable-tablet principle of rhetorical memory. Long-term doctrinal frameworks, by contrast, may retain permanent index images.

The method therefore supports both:

- temporary storage for a speech, transaction, or lecture;
- durable organization of a science or body of doctrine.

## 2. Manuscripts

## Yates’s account and scholarship since

## 1. What Yates got right

Yates correctly recognized:

- Romberch as one of the leading printed mnemonic compilers between Publicius/Peter of Ravenna and Rossellius;
- the four-part structure;
- the dependence on the three principal classical discussions of memory;
- the pervasive Thomist framework;
- the importance of Peter of Ravenna and Publicius;
- the coexistence of cosmic, astronomical, and architectural places;
- the use of Paradise, Purgatory, and Hell as artificial places;
- the importance of visual alphabets;
- Grammar as a rare illustrated composite mnemonic image;
- the encyclopaedic ambition of the fourth treatise;
- the work’s importance for understanding Dolce and later mnemonic books.

Her description remains exceptionally perceptive when treated as an interpretative essay rather than a critical edition.

## 2. What later work corrects

### “Strange congestion”

Merino Jerez shows that Yates’s phrase encouraged scholars to treat the book as an indiscriminate heap and to consult it mainly as a key to Publicius or Dolce. Romberch himself explains *Congestorium* through the ordered compilation of a notarial protocol. **[CORRECTION]**

### Excessive concentration on loci

Yates’s chapters foreground cosmic and architectural places while giving comparatively little attention to Romberch’s theory of image production and the extensive applications in Treatise IV. Recent work identifies the book as an integrated synthesis of places, images, language, disciplinary divisions, and practice. **[CORRECTION]**

### Memory for words

Yates associates visual inscription too directly with *memoria verborum*. Ramos Grané distinguishes:

- the object remembered—verbal wording;
- the route by which an image is created—from sound or from meaning;
- pictorial inscription as one optional device.

This is the clearest direct correction of a technical interpretation in Yates. **[CORRECTION]**

### “Dantesque” memory

The existence of moral-topographical mnemonic traditions is well supported. Direct derivation of Dante’s poem from an artificial-memory system identical to Romberch’s is not. **[YATES hypothesis, unproved]**

### Thomas’s lost system

Romberch’s Thomist citations are documented. A lost encyclopaedic image system personally used by Thomas is not. The Santa Maria Novella connection remains suggestive iconological speculation. **[YATES hypothesis]**

### Decline and debasement

Yates characterizes visual alphabets and expanding rule lists as “deterioration,” “trivial elaborations,” and a kind of crossword-puzzle mentality. Recent scholarship treats the same features as attempts to solve difficult problems involving exact wording, multilingual learning, rapid listening, classification, and teaching. The underlying evidence is unchanged; the value judgment has shifted. **[HISTORIOGRAPHICAL REVISION]**

## 3. Carruthers

Mary Carruthers retained Yates’s “congestion of lore” language and used Romberch especially to illuminate Publicius, Albertus Magnus, Thomas, and medieval locational heuristics. Her larger contribution was to replace a narrow “storage trick” account with memory as:

- composition;
- invention;
- ethical formation;
- meditation;
- trained recollection;
- a basis of reading and making.

Carruthers strengthens the medieval institutional and cognitive setting of Romberch but does not independently establish Yates’s proposed Dante or Thomas reconstructions.

## 4. Rossi

Paolo Rossi placed Romberch in the transition from rhetorical memory to encyclopaedic and universal-language projects. He emphasized the line through Peter of Ravenna, the wide circulation of the work, and its Italian reception through Dolce. Later Romberch-specific research finds this accurate but incomplete: Rossi devoted little attention to Romberch’s distinct synthesis and sometimes allowed Dolce’s success to eclipse the Latin source.

## 5. Bolzoni

Lina Bolzoni’s work on galleries, theatres, preaching, images, and encyclopaedic organization supports the larger claim that Renaissance memory systems were embedded in visual culture and literary composition. Her Camillo studies show why an encyclopaedic architectural system should not be reduced to a mechanical palace. They do not prove that Camillo copied Romberch, though both inhabited overlapping Venetian print and memory cultures.

## 6. Small

Jocelyn Penny Small’s *Wax Tablets of the Mind* stresses differences between ancient oral/visual practices and later literate reconstructions. Her discussion helps correct any assumption that “memory for words” always meant letter-by-letter encoding. Ramos Grané extends this warning directly to Romberch.

## 7. Hajdu and Volkmann

Helga Hajdu and Ludwig Volkmann treated Romberch before Yates. Volkmann reproduced visual alphabets and number-images from Romberch and other writers—plates 183–185 and 188 are especially relevant—but did not reconstruct their full operational role. Hajdu placed him in the long history of mnemonic doctrine. Yates’s presentation depended materially on this earlier German scholarship.

## 8. Vasoli, Torre, Merino Jerez, and Ramos Grané

These are the decisive Romberch-specific advances:

- Cesare Vasoli reconstructed the Dominican, scholastic, preaching, and Reuchlin contexts.
- Andrea Torre demonstrated that Dolce’s *Dialogo* is fundamentally a translation and adaptation of Romberch, not an independent mnemonic invention.
- Luis Merino Jerez reconstructed Romberch’s biography and epistolary context, clarified *Congestorium* as deliberate compilation, and challenged the Yates-derived neglect of its image theory.
- Marta Ramos Grané produced the first critical edition, analyzed the place rules, autobiographical error narrative, memory for words, Kabbalah references, *Hortus philosophiae*, diagrams, sources, and printing history.

## 9. Lull scholarship

Anthony Bonner and Alexander Fidora have shown that Llull’s Art is a formal demonstrative method grounded in combinations of dignities, principles, relations, questions, and subjects. It should not be conflated with the classical method of loci.

Romberch uses branching and combinatorial diagrams related to the broader Lullian afterlife, chiefly through intermediate sources such as Publicius. He does not present Llull’s complete system. **[CORRECTION]**

## 10. Bruno scholarship

Yates made the passage from mnemonic textbook to magical memory central to her Bruno narrative. Later work by Rita Sturlese, Michele Ciliberto, Stephen Clucas, Hilary Gatti, Marco Matteoli, and others has:

- provided more reliable texts and chronology;
- distinguished Bruno’s individual mnemonic works;
- treated his psychology, logic, metaphysics, language, and magic together;
- warned against reducing every wheel to Hermetic magic;
- shown both continuity with textbook mnemonic devices and radical philosophical transformation.

Bruno uses shops, bodies, alphabets, number-images, lists, wheels, and constellation orders recognizable from the Romberch–Publicius–Rossellius tradition. A direct borrowing is plausible in places, but “Bruno exercised himself using Romberch’s book” is generally repeated without a documented surviving annotated Bruno copy or explicit acknowledgment. **[TRADITION/UNPROVED]**

## 11. Camillo scholarship

Bolzoni and Paola Stagno clarify Camillo’s theatre through manuscript, print, iconographic, rhetorical, and patronage evidence. Yates’s linkage between Camillo’s theatre and a revival of Dominican memory in Venice is an attractive contextual possibility, not a proven causal chain.

## 12. Ramus and Ong

Walter J. Ong showed that Petrus Ramus’s method reorganized knowledge through dichotomous spatial outlines and dialectical order while rejecting the elaborate imagistic machinery of classical artificial memory. Yates memorably says Ramus “smashes the inner image of old Grammatica.” This is a metaphor for epistemic change, not an actual controversy between Ramus and Romberch.

## History and context

## 1. Chronology

- ca. 80s BCE: anonymous *Rhetorica ad Herennium* systematizes artificial memory.
- 55 BCE: Cicero’s *De oratore* gives the Simonides story and Metrodorus tradition.
- first century CE: Quintilian critically presents architectural memory.
- fourth century onward: the *Ad Herennium*, believed to be Cicero’s, becomes the *Rhetorica nova*.
- thirteenth century: Albert the Great and Thomas Aquinas incorporate artificial memory into Prudence and Aristotelian psychology.
- thirteenth–fifteenth centuries: preaching, devotional meditation, manuscript diagrams, alphabetic devices, and artes memoriae proliferate.
- 1482/1485: Publicius prints diagrams and a visual alphabet.
- 1491/92: Peter of Ravenna’s *Phoenix* popularizes practical memory.
- 1502/03: Reuchlin’s *Liber congestorum de arte praedicandi* circulates.
- November 1513: Romberch says the *Congestorium* is complete in Cologne.
- 1513–20: Romberch participates in the Reuchlin controversy and travels through Rome, Bologna, and Venice.
- 9 July 1520: first edition printed by Rusconi.
- early 1533: probable death of Romberch.
- July 1533: Sessa edition.
- 1562: Lodovico Dolce’s Italian *Dialogo* adapts the book; reprinted 1575 and 1586.
- 1579: Rossellius’s *Thesaurus artificiosae memoriae* develops many parallel systems.
- late sixteenth century: Bruno transforms textbook memory within his combinatorial philosophy and magic.
- seventeenth century: Schenkel, Paepp, Fludd, and others continue or transform the tradition.
- 1920s–30s: Volkmann and Hajdu restore Romberch to historical visibility.
- 1966: Yates makes him central to the Renaissance-memory narrative.
- 2001 onward: Torre, Vasoli, Merino Jerez, and Ramos Grané re-establish the primary-text and biographical record.
- 2024: first critical edition.
- 2025/26: first full Spanish translation advertised.

## 2. Dominican institutions

Romberch belonged to the observant Dominicans of Cologne. The convent and University of Cologne were major centers of scholastic theology and guardians of doctrinal orthodoxy. The University possessed censorship authority and opposed some humanist innovations, yet individual Dominicans engaged with Greek, Hebrew, classical rhetoric, and Italian print culture.

Romberch studied or worked in:

- Cologne’s Dominican and university environment;
- Rome, where he was connected with Silvestro Mazzolini da Prierio and acted in the Reuchlin affair;
- Bologna, where he continued theological study;
- Venice, where he edited scholastic works and printed the *Congestorium*.

Cardinal Domenico Grimani, to whom the printed book was dedicated, possessed a celebrated library rich in Latin, Greek, and Hebrew books. Romberch praises both the library and the palace rooms as exemplary mnemonic places. **[TEXT]**

## 3. Humanism and scholasticism

Romberch cannot be classified neatly as either an anti-humanist scholastic or a humanist rhetorician.

Documented scholastic features include:

- Thomas and Albert as authorities;
- Aristotelian faculty psychology;
- divisions of being and the sciences;
- Dominican institutional formation.

Documented humanist-facing features include:

- engagement with Cicero, Quintilian, Petrarch, Publicius, Peter of Ravenna, and Reuchlin;
- interest in Greek and Hebrew books;
- Venetian print and diagram culture;
- efforts to refine presentation and provide practical access to learning.

“Between scholasticism and humanism” is therefore a better description than either exclusive label.

## 4. Courts, universities, and professions

The juridical examples and notarial metaphor are functional, not decorative. Lawyers require ordered heads of a case, witnesses, authorities, and citations. Professors and students require divisions of sciences, propositions, syllogisms, lectures, and disputations. Preachers require themes, divisions, authorities, examples, and rapid oral delivery. Merchants require merchandise, debts, obligations, numbers, and page references.

The book’s institutional horizon includes convent school, university classroom, pulpit, confessional, law court, mercantile office, and print shop.

## 5. Franciscan and broader scholastic contexts

Artificial memory was not uniquely Dominican. Franciscans, Augustinians, secular masters, humanists, physicians, jurists, and itinerant teachers also transmitted it. The specific ethical elevation of memory under Prudence is strongly Albertine-Thomist, but the method of loci belongs to the common rhetorical curriculum.

## 6. Ramism and commonplaces

Ramist method replaced many moving, affective images with visible logical partitions. Printed commonplace books similarly externalized topical storage. This did not instantaneously destroy artificial memory; the traditions coexisted. Romberch’s own diagrams already show traffic between imagistic memory, logical division, and tabular organization.

## 7. Universal language

Visual alphabets and combinatorial figures may look like universal writing, but Romberch’s codes depend on Latin names, local acquaintances, and personal associations. They are not a universal language in the later sense. Rossi’s genealogy is best understood as a history of recurring ambitions to formalize thought, not proof that Romberch designed a universally decodable code.

## People

## 1. Johannes Host von Romberch

His birth is disputed:

- Nikolaus Paulus proposed about 1480 and Dominican entry around 1495.
- Cesare Vasoli proposed about 1485 and entry around 1505–06.
- The inference depends on an ambiguous autobiographical statement about an event twenty-five years earlier, possibly entry into the Order or priestly ordination. **[DISPUTED]**

He probably died late in 1532 or early in 1533 at Cologne. **[RECONSTRUCTION]**

His other work was largely theological, editorial, catechetical, and anti-Lutheran. The *Congestorium* is early and exceptional within his oeuvre.

## 2. Johann Grevenbach

Friend, dedicatee of the original 1513 composition, and requested recipient of the mnemonic art. Possibly represented as a student in the didactic voice of the book, though this is not certain.

## 3. Cardinal Domenico Grimani

Printed-edition dedicatee, patronal figure, Hebraist, book collector, and member of the commission judging Reuchlin. His palace and library are praised as ready-made mnemonic architecture.

## 4. Jacob Hoogstraten

Dominican inquisitor at Cologne, Romberch’s superior or powerful associate, and principal opponent of Reuchlin. Romberch acted as his attorney or agent in Rome.

## 5. Johannes Reuchlin

Christian Hebraist and author of *De verbo mirifico*, *De arte cabbalistica*, Hebrew studies, and the *Liber congestorum de arte praedicandi*. He was prosecuted in the controversy over Jewish books. Paradoxically, Romberch participated on Hoogstraten’s side while continuing to cite Reuchlin respectfully as an authority on preaching. **[TEXT/BIOGRAPHY]**

## 6. Silvestro Mazzolini da Prierio

Dominican theologian and papal official whom Romberch called his teacher in Rome. Associated with the anti-Reuchlin and later anti-Lutheran Dominican establishment.

## 7. Publicius and Peter of Ravenna

They are Romberch’s most important recent mnemonic predecessors:

- Publicius supplies major diagrams and alphabetic-combinatorial material.
- Peter supplies practical rules, distances, markers, known-person images, demonstrations, and much of the experiential persona of the memory master.

## 8. Lodovico Dolce

Dolce’s *Dialogo del modo di accrescere et conservar la memoria* of 1562 is substantially an Italian transformation of Romberch. He changes the manual into dialogue, omits much scholastic material, expands literary examples, and suppresses Romberch’s name except for an oblique reference to “a German.” **[TEXTUAL DEPENDENCE]**

Dolce’s adaptation helped the system circulate among readers who would not use difficult scholastic Latin.

## 9. Cosmas Rossellius

Dominican author of the *Thesaurus artificiosae memoriae* (1579). He expands celestial, infernal, paradisal, alphabetical, and architectural systems and transmits Romberch’s friars’-cells anecdote.

## The magic question

## 1. Is the *Congestorium* magical?

In its explicit theory and ordinary operations, no. **[TEXT]**

Its causal explanation is:

- natural memory;
- sensory perception;
- phantasy;
- corporeal similitudes;
- order;
- attention;
- repetition;
- habituation.

There is no instruction to summon spirits, consecrate talismans, capture stellar rays, pronounce divine names magically, or obtain knowledge without study.

## 2. Cosmic and zodiacal imagery

Cosmic spheres, zodiac signs, and constellations provide ordered loci. Celestial content alone does not make a system astral magic. Romberch does not give planetary hours, talismanic materials, suffumigations, invocations, or electional procedures. **[TEXT/ABSENCE]**

## 3. Kabbalah

Romberch explicitly mentions *Cabala* while discussing oral teaching:

> *Nosti siquidem quaedam praecepta a Deo Iudaeis per scripta tradita … alia autem per Cabalam, quam receptionem aiunt, sola traditione digniorum auribus indita.*

“You know that some precepts were delivered by God to the Jews in writing … but others through Kabbalah, which they call ‘reception,’ imparted by oral tradition to the ears of the worthier.”  
—1520, f. 30v, II.8. **[TEXT]**

He compares restricted oral Kabbalistic transmission with mnemonic arts that cannot be fully learned from a written manual. Elsewhere:

> *Sunt namque plurima saepe numero secreta litteris propter indignos non committenda, sed verbatim quibus ea favemus communicanda.*

“There are often many secrets that should not be committed to writing because of the unworthy, but should be communicated orally to those whom we favor.”  
—f. 23r. **[TEXT]**

Ramos Grané argues that Romberch’s tone is strikingly neutral and that he accommodates Kabbalah as a parallel of initiatory oral transmission. **[RECONSTRUCTION]** Her stronger suggestion that this places him in a Neoplatonic-Kabbalistic stream must remain interpretative: Romberch’s direct knowledge of Abulafian Kabbalah has not been demonstrated.

## 4. Alchemy

Romberch treats alchemy as a real but difficult and obscure art:

> *Quamquam non ignorem artem veram esse, est tamen tempus breve, experimentum fallax, ars vero difficilis.*

“Although I do not deny that the art is true, nevertheless time is short, experiment deceptive, and the art difficult.”  
—f. 56v. **[TEXT]**

His immediate concern is how alchemists transfer planetary names to metals and conceal meanings in specialized vocabulary. It is an example of metalepsis and coded naming, not an alchemical recipe.

## 5. Ars notoria

The *ars notoria* promised knowledge through prayers, divine names, figures, ritual observation, and angelic or divine assistance. Romberch’s method requires disciplined natural practice. No operational *ars notoria* ritual appears in the *Congestorium*. **[ABSENCE]**

Both systems could be described as “arts” involving figures, secrecy, and learning, but functional similarity at that level is insufficient to establish descent.

## 6. Heresy and the Index

Romberch’s name and *De idoneo verbi Dei ministro* appear in the Roman Index of 1557; his other works were included in the broader 1559 prohibition. **[DOCUMENTED RECEPTION]**

This is paradoxical because he was a vehement defender of Catholic orthodoxy and approved severe action against Lutheran reformers. Older biographical tradition speculated that:

- he converted late in life after contemplating the courage of condemned reformers; or
- the Index’s compilers mistook the evangelical-sounding title *De idoneo verbi Dei ministro* for a Lutheran work.

Neither explanation is documented. **[LEGEND/RECONSTRUCTION]**

There is no evidence that the *Congestorium* itself was prohibited because its memory method was magical, Kabbalistic, or heretical. **[ABSENCE]**

## 7. Consequences

No trial, imprisonment, censure, or ecclesiastical punishment has been found that was imposed upon Romberch for mnemonic practice. His involvement with inquisitorial institutions was principally as an associate of the accusers, not as an accused magician.

## Afterlife

## 1. Dolce

The clearest direct afterlife is textual. Dolce’s 1562 *Dialogo* translates, rearranges, modernizes, and selectively secularizes the *Congestorium*. It reproduces the cosmic diagram and visual-alphabet imagery using blocks descending from the Romberch editions. Reprints appeared in 1575 and 1586.

Dolce adds an explicit reference to Dante when discussing the memory of Hell. Consequently, any “Dante connection” in Dolce cannot automatically be projected backward into Romberch. **[TEXTUAL CORRECTION]**

## 2. Rossellius

Rossellius’s 1579 *Thesaurus* continues the Dominican textbook stream. His elaborate diagrams of Hell and Paradise make visually explicit what Romberch often described verbally. Rossellius’s Hell contains differentiated punishments for heretics, infidels, idolaters, hypocrites, and the seven deadly sins.

## 3. Bruno

Bruno’s works contain recognizable descendants or transformations of:

- visual alphabets;
- number-shapes;
- professions and shops;
- bodily loci;
- constellation sequences;
- combinatorial wheels;
- lists of agents, actions, attributes, and circumstances.

But Bruno’s systems often place these within a metaphysics of universal animation, magical bonds, celestial imagery, and combinatorial invention alien to Romberch’s ordinary textbook practice. **[CONTINUITY plus TRANSFORMATION]**

## 4. Fludd and Schenkel

Robert Fludd illustrates visual alphabets and architectural theatres; Lambert Schenkel and his students marketed practical memory instruction across Europe. They share the expanding textbook repertory to which Romberch contributed. Direct borrowing must be established passage by passage rather than inferred from any visual alphabet.

## 5. Ramus

Ramist outlines and dichotomies offered a competing model of order. Yet Romberch’s tree and disciplinary tables show that the border between imagistic memory and diagrammatic method was already permeable.

## 6. Preaching and emblem culture

Personification, attribute, motto, pictorial letter, and composite image overlap formally with:

- sermon imagery;
- devotional images;
- imprese;
- emblems;
- hieroglyphic collections;
- encyclopaedic frontispieces.

Romberch’s Grammar can look like an inscrutable emblem if its mnemonic decoding rule is lost, as Yates observed. Formal resemblance, however, is not proof that every emblem was designed as a mnemonic image.

## 7. Dante and frescoes

The broad medieval culture of architectonic, moral, and affective memory is relevant to Dante and didactic fresco cycles. Specific assertions that the *Comedy*, Giotto’s Arena Chapel, or the Spanish Chapel at Santa Maria Novella embodies a technical locus system remain reconstructions associated especially with Yates.

Romberch, born nearly two centuries later, can witness the survival of a tradition; he cannot independently prove the mechanism by which an earlier poem or fresco was composed.

## 8. Borges, Eco, and literary afterlives

### Borges’s Funes

“Funes the Memorious” is best read as an inversion of artificial memory: Funes retains limitless particulars but cannot abstract, classify, or forget. Romberch’s system depends precisely on selection, division, symbolic condensation, and erasure. The comparison is illuminating but not a documented literary borrowing from Romberch. **[MODERN ANALOGY]**

### Eco

Umberto Eco’s histories of perfect languages, semiotics, encyclopedias, and combinatorial thought discuss the larger tradition linking Lullism, memory, signs, and universal-language projects. This is an intellectual afterlife of the problem, not necessarily reception of the *Congestorium* itself.

## 9. Modern memory palaces and athletes

Modern competitors still use:

- fixed routes;
- striking interactive images;
- person-action-object combinations;
- number-shape systems;
- alphabetic pegs;
- reuse of loci;
- forward and reverse traversal.

These are structurally close to Romberch. Direct transmission is usually mediated by modern mnemonic manuals rather than continuous study of his Latin text. **[MODERN REVIVAL, indirect genealogy]**

Romberch’s five/ten markers resemble modern checkpointing; his known-person and profession images resemble person-action-object systems; his bird and object alphabets resemble alphabet pegs.

## 10. Computing and combinatorics

The defensible history is plural:

- Llull formalized combinations of symbolic terms for theological demonstration.
- Renaissance mnemonic writers adapted rotating or recombinable letters and figures.
- Bruno integrated combination with memory, logic, and natural magic.
- Seventeenth-century universal-language authors and Leibniz pursued formal character systems and calculable reasoning.
- Modern computing realizes symbolic operations mechanically.

The statement “Llull invented the computer” or “Romberch’s wheel is an early computer” is a modern metaphor. **[MODERN INVENTION]** No continuous engineering lineage connects Romberch’s volvelle to electronic computation.

## Controversies and disputes

## 1. Author’s name

“Johannes Romberch” is a toponymic or authorial form. “Johannes Host,” “Host von Romberch,” “Romberg,” “Romberch de Kyrspe,” and “Kierspe” occur in scholarship and catalogues. They designate the same Dominican. Exact birth name and the relation of the names to farm, village, or family remain imperfectly documented.

## 2. Birth and death

Birth: approximately 1480–85.  
Death: probably late 1532 or early 1533.  
Both remain reconstructed rather than established by a surviving birth or death record.

## 3. Date of the system

- 1513 is the stated completion date.
- 1520 is the first publication date.
- 1533 is the second edition used by Yates.

Calling it simply “a system of 1520” is bibliographically convenient but conceals its pre-Reuchlin-trial composition and possible Italian revision.

## 4. Number of editions

The specialist consensus located recognizes two Latin editions, 1520 and 1533. Claims for a 1553 Latin edition generally appear to be typographical errors for 1533. Dolce’s 1562 version is a separate Italian adaptation, not a third Latin edition. **[CORRECTION]**

## 5. Manuscript variants

No manuscript recension is known. Accordingly, “variants across manuscripts and editions” must here mean:

- no identified Romberch manuscript variants;
- differences between the 1520 and 1533 printed settings;
- internal inconsistencies such as *anser/aquila*;
- altered, omitted, or expanded material in Dolce;
- later reuse by Rossellius and others.

## 6. Source labeled “Petrarch”

A marginal attribution names “Franciscus Petrarcha” for an order rule. Andrea Torre and Ramos Grané observe that the precept does not occur in Petrarch and that Romberch normally calls him simply *Petrarcha*. They propose Franciscus Maturantius/Maturanzio as the intended source. **[TEXTUAL DISPUTE]**

This is a concrete correction to Yates’s broad statement that Romberch’s frequent invocation “absorbs Petrarch into the Dominican memory tradition.” Romberch does cite Petrarch elsewhere, but at least this particular attribution is probably erroneous.

## 7. Dependence on Peter of Ravenna

The practical doctrine follows Peter closely, but “entirely modeled” on the *Phoenix* is too strong. Romberch adds scholastic psychology, extensive source collation, diagrams, alphabets, disciplinary applications, a developed theory of images, and personal experiments.

## 8. Dependence on Publicius

Publicius supplies several major illustrations and devices. Romberch is sometimes an interpreter, sometimes a copier, and sometimes a critic. He expressly admits difficulty understanding the quadrangle. His work should not be treated as transparent evidence of what Publicius originally meant.

## 9. Originality

Older narratives oscillate between calling Romberch an unoriginal compiler and treating him as a major inventor. The text supports a middle position:

- few elementary principles are new;
- the synthesis, scale, pedagogical organization, image theory, professional applications, and some adaptations are distinctive;
- Romberch openly claims compilation, so dependence is not concealed plagiarism.

## 10. The “Dantesque” system

The places of the afterlife are documented. The direct Dante genealogy is disputed and unproved.

## 11. Thomas Aquinas’s own memory system

Thomas recommends artificial-memory principles. No surviving Thomas text sets out the Romberch-style encyclopedia conjectured by Yates.

## 12. Magic

The book contains Kabbalah, alchemy, cosmic imagery, secrecy, and combinatorial figures as topics or analogies. It lacks operational ritual magic. To call the entire *Congestorium* magical confuses it with Bruno’s transformation of related materials.

## 13. Romberch as heretic

His appearance in an Index is documented. His late conversion is legend. The cause of listing remains uncertain. His surviving polemical record is overwhelmingly anti-Lutheran.

## 14. Anti-Lutheran purpose of the *Congestorium*

Some booksellers and catalogue descriptions say that it was designed to combat Lutheranism. This is chronologically weak: Romberch dated its completion to 1513, before Luther’s public controversy. The 1520 publication occurred amid the Reformation, and the method could serve Catholic preaching, but an original anti-Lutheran purpose is not stated in the book. **[MODERN OVERSTATEMENT]**

## 15. Bruno’s personal use of Romberch

Textual parallels support influence from the same printed tradition. A personally owned or annotated Bruno copy has not been produced. The stronger biographical assertion remains unproved.

## 16. Visual alphabets as universal language

They are dependent on Latin vocabulary and personal knowledge. They encode rather than abolish language. Their inclusion in a history leading toward universal character is legitimate; describing them as already universal is not.

## Open questions

1. Is there a still-unidentified authorial or presentation manuscript behind the 1520 printing?
2. Which passages were revised between completion in Cologne in 1513 and publication in Venice in 1520?
3. Can the 1520 and 1533 editions be exhaustively collated beyond the “no significant differences” judgment?
4. Who designed and cut the twenty-three woodblocks?
5. Which blocks originated with Publicius, which were redrawn, and which were made specifically for Romberch?
6. Was the movable component supplied in every 1520 copy, or only in some?
7. What is the explanation of the additional end plate catalogued in the Braidense copy?
8. Can ownership annotations establish actual classroom, preaching, legal, or mercantile use?
9. Who was the auditor who corrected the friars’-cell system?
10. Does Romberch’s account preserve a real classroom exchange or adapt an earlier *exemplum*?
11. Which “Franciscus” lies behind the false or corrupt Petrarch attribution?
12. How much direct knowledge did Romberch possess of Hebrew and Kabbalah?
13. Did he read Reuchlin’s Kabbalistic works, or only absorb generalized reports?
14. How should the tetragram at f. 61r be interpreted within his image system?
15. Does the theological figure trampling a divine figure have a Kabbalistic context, or is that proposed connection overinterpretation?
16. What exact form of Publicius’s work did Romberch use?
17. Which edition of Peter of Ravenna lay before him?
18. Are some rules attributed to Peter absent from all known *Phoenix* editions because Romberch used a lost recension, oral teaching, or faulty attribution?
19. How widely was the Latin book actually read? Surviving-copy, citation, and annotation studies are needed to test Rossi’s “widely read throughout Europe.”
20. Can direct lines of borrowing from Romberch to Bruno or Fludd be demonstrated by unique errors rather than generic similarities?
21. Why exactly was Romberch entered in the Roman Index despite his anti-Lutheran record?
22. Was the Index listing an author-name error, a title-based suspicion, an unknown doctrinal objection, or a response to a lost work?
23. How far did Dolce’s omissions change the confessional and scholastic character of the system?
24. Did merchants, messengers, physicians, or notaries actually use the specialized applications advertised on the title page?
25. How did readers manipulate the volvelle, and do surviving copies preserve original attachment states?
26. Can digital reconstruction test the practical capacity and ambiguity of the Grammar image, city, abbey, and combinatorial apparatus without mistaking experimental usability for historical proof?
