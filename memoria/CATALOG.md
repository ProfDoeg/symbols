# Catalogue of memory systems in Yates, *The Art of Memory*

**What this is.** A merged catalogue of every memory system, device and doctrine that Frances Yates presents in *The Art of Memory*, grouped at the scale a research dossier would take. It was built from four readers' granular catalogues (part 1, Chapters I–IV, 34 entries; part 2, Chapters V–IX, 68 entries; part 3, Chapters X–XIII, 47 entries; part 4, Chapters XIV–XVII, 27 entries). The 176 reader entries have been folded into 45 systems; nothing has been dropped. Where a reader made a separate entry for a part of a larger system (each grade of Camillo's Theatre, each wheel of the *Shadows*, each of Bruno's Seals), it survives here as a named component under the system, tagged with its reader location as `[part.entry]`. Overlaps between readers (Metrodorus in parts 1 and 2; the Ars reminiscendi of *Circe* in parts 2 and 3; Schenkel, Paepp and Perkins in parts 3 and 4) are merged into one system and cited in both places.

**Source edition.** Frances A. Yates, *The Art of Memory* (London: Routledge and Kegan Paul, 1966); Routledge 1999 reprint as *Selected Works*, vol. III. Page numbers are the printed page numbers of that edition. Plate and figure numbers follow Yates's List of Illustrations. Quotations under "In Yates's words" are Yates's own unless a source is named in parentheses; they are the readers' transcriptions from the pypdf extraction of the text (see Extraction notes at the end).

**Order.** Yates's order of introduction: classical, medieval, the printed treatises, Camillo, Lull, Bruno's *Shadows*, Ramism, the Seals, Dicson and England, the late Bruno, Fludd and the Globe, Bacon, Descartes, Leibniz.

**Companion file.** `systems.json` in this directory lists the 45 systems with slug, exponent, date, source text, pages and component names, for tooling.

## 1. Simonides, the *Dialexeis* and Cicero's version of the art

Simonides of Ceos (c. 556–468 BC), legendary inventor; the anonymous sophist *Dialexeis* (c. 400 BC); Cicero, *De oratore* (55 BC) and *De inventione* (c. 86–82 BC).

**Principle.** After the collapse of Scopas's banqueting hall Simonides identified the corpses by remembering where each guest had sat, and inferred that order is the key to memory and sight the strongest sense: one selects places, forms images of the things to be remembered, and stores the images in the places, so that the order of the places preserves the order of the things. The *Dialexeis* fragment, the earliest treatise, already has the two kinds (things and names) and the rule "what you hear, place on what you know": names dissected etymologically (Chrysippus on gold and horse), things placed on gods and heroes (courage on Mars and Achilles). Cicero's *De oratore* gives a "potted version" of the rules through Antonius (many well-lighted places in order; active, sharply defined, unusual images; memory for things by "masks" on places; memory for words with more images) and defends the art against the "inert". His *De inventione* makes memory a part of Prudence, the doctrine that later made the artificial memory a moral duty. This is the founding layer every later system reworks; its distinctness from its neighbour (2) is that it is legend, fragment and elegant summary rather than a complete set of rules.

**Components.**
- The Simonides method: places and images (loci and imagines) `[1.1]`
- The *Dialexeis* sophist mnemonic (Hippias of Elis) `[1.13]`
- Cicero's version of the art (*De oratore*) `[1.9]`
- Memory as a part of Prudence (the Tullian ethical frame) `[1.10]`

**Yates.** Ch. I, pp. 1–4, 17–21, 26; Ch. II, pp. 27–31; the Prudence frame recalled Ch. III pp. 54–55, 57, 61–62, 73–74 and Ch. IV pp. 95, 97, 102. Pl. 1 (Tullius before Rhetoric, Santa Maria Novella), Pl. 4a.

**In Yates's words.**
- "he realised that orderly arrangement is essential for good memory." (p. 2)
- "Memory for things; memory for words (or names)! Here are the technical terms for the two kinds of artificial memory already in use in 400 B.C." (p. 30)
- "Cicero is a believer and a defender. He evidently had by nature a fantastically acute visual memory." (p. 19)
- "it rides with Prudence in a chariot of which Tullius is the driver, whipping up his two horses of the First and Second Rhetorics." (p. 67)

**Descends from / leads to.** Every later system; directly to 2 (the full rules), 3 (Quintilian's abbreviation of Cicero), 5 (Cicero's *Tusculans* transposing memoria and inventio into proofs of the soul's divinity; Plato's satire possibly aimed at sophist etymologies), 7 (Martianus and Alcuin know the Prudence frame), 9–11 (Prudence as the scholastic warrant), 12 (Dante's three parts of Prudence), 14 (Petrarch uses *De oratore*). Yates on p. 6: even Bruno's *De umbris idearum* is "going through yet once again the old, old business of rules for places, rules for images".

**Dossier note.** Primary texts named by the readers: Cicero, *De oratore* II, lxxxvi–lxxxviii, 351–360 and I, xxxiv, 157; *De inventione* II, liii, 160; Quintilian XI, ii, 11–16 for the variant; the Parian Chronicle entry for 477 BC; the *Dialexeis* in Diels, *Fragmente der Vorsokratiker* II, p. 345, with *Greater Hippias* 285D–286A and *Lesser Hippias* 368D; Pliny, Aelian, Ammianus, Suidas as attestations. The 1470 Venice *Rhetorica nova et vetus* pairing is the medieval form.

## 2. The *Ad Herennium* artificial memory

Anonymous Roman rhetoric teacher, c. 86–82 BC; *Rhetorica ad Herennium* III, xvi–xxiv; attributed to Cicero ("Tullius", the "Second Rhetoric") throughout the Middle Ages.

**Principle.** "Constat igitur artificiosa memoria ex locis et imaginibus": places are a house, an intercolumnar space, a corner, an arch, formed in deserted buildings, unlike one another, moderate in size and lighting, about thirty feet apart, in a numbered series marked at every fifth locus, reusable like wax tablets, and thought may construct fictitious places at will. Images must be *imagines agentes*, striking similitudes, active human figures of exceptional beauty or singular ugliness, crowned, bloodstained or comic, because nature is stirred by the novel; the teacher gives method and a few examples only. Memory for things sets one composite image on one locus (the poisoned man, the defendant with cup, tablets and ram's testicles); memory for words needs far more places and works by sound-cues (Domitius lashed by the Reges for "domum itionem reges"). The Greek method of a fixed stock of ready-made images for words, possibly shorthand *notae*, is reported and rejected. It is the "only complete source" for the classical art and the plan of every later *Ars memorativa* treatise.

**Components.**
- The *Ad Herennium* artificial memory and its rules for places `[1.4]`
- *Ad Herennium* rules for images: the imagines agentes `[1.5]`
- *Ad Herennium* memory for things (memoria rerum): the lawsuit image `[1.6]`
- *Ad Herennium* memory for words (memoria verborum): Domitius/Reges and Aesopus/Cimber `[1.7]`
- The Greek method of ready-made images for words (shorthand notae) `[1.8]`

**Yates.** Ch. I, pp. 4–17, 24–25; medieval transmission Ch. III, pp. 54–56, 63, 66–67, 77; Ch. II, pp. 41–43 for the notae and Metrodorus.

**In Yates's words.**
- "The artificial memory is established from places and images ... the stock definition to be forever repeated down the ages." (p. 6)
- "Who is that man moving slowly in the lonely building, stopping at intervals with an intent face? He is a rhetoric student forming a set of memory loci." (p. 8)
- "They appear to be completely amoral, their function being solely to give an emotional impetus to memory by their personal idiosyncracy or their strangeness." (pp. 16–17)
- "by a kind of inner stenography, the shorthand symbols were written down inwardly and memorised on the memory places." (p. 15)

**Descends from / leads to.** From 1 (lost Greek treatises; Simonides "invented, or codified" rules of the *Dialexeis* kind). To 3 (Quintilian omits the imagines agentes), 4 (the ram's testicles possibly Aries; the notae branch Metrodorus may have used), 6 (Ars Notoria as "bastard descendant" of the notae branch), 9–10 (Albertus quotes the imagines agentes in full; Thomas misreads *solitudo* as *sollicitudo*), 11 (the Rosaio's natural/artificial places from the real/fictitious distinction), 12 (Giotto's varied backgrounds), 13 (medieval memory for words as inscriptions), 15–18 (the plan of every treatise), 40 (Fludd's polemic against fictitious places).

**Dossier note.** The readers give *Ad Herennium* III, xvi–xix (places), xxii (images), xx, 33 (things), xxi, 34 and xxiv, 40 (words), xxiii, 38 (the Greek method); Cicero, *De inventione* I, vii, 9 for res/verba; Quintilian XI, ii, 23–26 and *De oratore* II, lxxxviii, 360 for the notae; Milne, *Greek Shorthand Manuals* on Tironian notes. Yates regrets that the treatise never gives a whole set of images on places (pp. 10–11); a dossier would need the Albertine and Thomist readings of the corrupt text (system 9–10).

## 3. Quintilian's mnemonic and his simpler precepts

Quintilian, *Institutio oratoria* XI, ii (first century AD); with the anonymous professor's party feat and Hortensius's auctioneer.

**Principle.** Quintilian gives "the clearest description of the process": a spacious building memorised in order (forecourt, atrium, impluvium, bedrooms, statues), signs such as an anchor or a weapon set on the places, and recital by running through the places "demanding what has been entrusted to them"; places may be public buildings, a long journey, a city, or pictures. He allows the simple object-placing form (Hortensius after an auction; the elder Seneca and Simplicius reciting backwards), which Yates matches with a modern professor's independently discovered party trick. But he doubts images for a connected speech, dismisses Charmadas and Metrodorus ("may keep their systems for themselves"), and prefers simpler precepts: learn a passage from the same tablets on which it was written, the mind's eye fixed on the page, with private marks against hard passages. His text was not generally accessible before Poggio's find of 1416, so the medieval tradition was not founded on it; after 1470 it clarified the "straight mnemotechnic".

**Components.**
- Quintilian's architectural mnemonic (the building walked through) `[1.2]`
- The object-placing mnemonic: the professor's party feat and Quintilian's auctioneer `[1.3]`
- Quintilian's simpler precepts: memorising from the written tablet or page `[1.11]`

**Yates.** Ch. I, pp. 3–4, 16, 21–26; Ch. III, pp. 51–53 (Martianus recommends the same), 56; recalled Ch. V p. 111.

**In Yates's words.**
- "We have to think of the ancient orator as moving in imagination through his memory building whilst he is making his speech" (p. 3)
- "The perplexed student of the art of memory is grateful to Quintilian." (p. 23)
- "instead of attempting to visualise shorthand notae on some vast place system it visualises ordinary writing as actually placed on the tablet or page." (p. 25)

**Descends from / leads to.** From 1–2 (quotes Cicero's abbreviation). To 4 (the only source for Metrodorus's 360 places), 7 (Martianus's Quintilianist advice; his sword image is the "Quintilian type"), 13 (Holcot's images on the page combine page and image), 16 (Peter of Ravenna adds places on journeys), 20 (the humanist coolness of Erasmus and Melanchthon), 29 (Ramus quotes XI, ii, 36 on division and composition; Ong's spatialised page traced to the tablet), 34 (Watson's "straight exposition").

**Dossier note.** *Institutio oratoria* XI, ii, 4 (wax-seal theory), 17–22 (the building), 23–26 (critique, auctioneer), 32–33 (tablet method); Seneca, *Controversiae* I, praef. 2; Augustine, *De anima* IV, vii. Yates's point on the 1416 recovery and the 1470 printing (pp. 26, 56, 111) fixes the chronology a dossier must respect.

## 4. Metrodorus of Scepsis's zodiacal memory

Metrodorus of Scepsis, Greek rhetor at Mithridates's court (fl. c. 100–70 BC); known only through Quintilian, Cicero, Pliny, Strabo, Plutarch; revived as a place system by Romberch (1520) and Rossellius (1579).

**Principle.** Quintilian says Metrodorus "found three hundred and sixty places in the twelve signs through which the sun moves"; L. A. Post's reconstruction, endorsed by Yates, divides the zodiac into 12 signs and 36 decans with their decan-figures, ten loci under each decan, yielding a numbered series 1–360 in which any place is findable by calculation. The astrological images serve as the order of places as memorised buildings do in the ordinary method; if Quintilian is right, shorthand *notae* were then "written" on them. Pliny says he could repeat what he heard in the very same words. Romberch revives the twelve signs as "an easily memorised order of places", naming Metrodorus as authority, and extends it with Hyginus's constellation images when more places are needed; Rossellius repeats it with mnemonic verses. Every later celestial memory invokes Metrodorus, and the decan images, "certainly believed to be magical images", are the root the Renaissance occult systems grow from.

**Components.**
- Metrodorus of Scepsis's zodiacal memory (360 places in the twelve signs) `[1.12]`
- The zodiac and the constellations as place system (Metrodorus of Scepsis revived; Romberch with Hyginus) `[2.12]`

**Yates.** Ch. I, pp. 19–20, 23–25; Ch. II, pp. 39–42; Ch. IV, p. 100; Ch. V, pp. 116–117, 122 (Romberch, Rossellius); recalled Ch. IX pp. 206, 217; Ch. XII p. 267 (Perkins's "Scepsian"). Descendants illustrated at Pl. 11–12, Fig. 8, Pl. 16.

**In Yates's words.**
- "Every subsequent user of a celestial memory system will invoke Metrodorus of Scepsis as the classical authority for bringing the stars into memory." (p. 39)
- "It had to do with memory for words, possibly through memorising the notae or symbols of shorthand, and was connected with the zodiac. That is all we really know." (p. 42)
- "As another type of place system, Romberch envisages using the signs of the zodiac as giving an easily memorised order of places. He gives the name of Metrodorus of Scepsis as the authority on this." (p. 116)

**Descends from / leads to.** From 2 (notae branch; the ram's testicles perhaps Aries). To 6 ("a memory based on the zodiac ... might give rise to rumours of magical powers"), 9 (Albertus's nocturnal ram, "we might wonder whether Albertus thought that the ram was Aries"), 10 (Thomist inscribed orders likened to writing on the zodiac), 17–18 (Romberch and Rossellius), 26 (Porta condemns him), 27–28 ("The Metrodorian system has turned into a magical system" in Bruno), 30 (Riccio's rational zodiac on the body), 33–34 (Dicson's "Scepsian" art; Perkins's label), 39 (the *Spaccio*), 40 (Fludd's zodiac), 42 (Bacon's target: "Metrodorian" systems).

**Dossier note.** Sources: Quintilian XI, ii, 22; *De oratore* II, lxxxviii, 360; Pliny, *Natural History* VII, 24; Strabo XIII, i, 55; Plutarch, *Lucullus*; L. A. Post, *Classical Weekly* XV (1932). Romberch, *Congestorium* pp. 25 recto ff. and 33 verso (Hyginus); Rossellius, *Thesaurus* p. 22 verso. Yates connects the decan images to Egyptian temple archives and the Palazzo Schifanoja via her *Giordano Bruno and the Hermetic Tradition*, pp. 45–8.

## 5. The philosophers' memory: Aristotle, Plato, Augustine

Aristotle, *De memoria et reminiscentia* and *De anima*; Plato, *Theaetetus*, *Phaedo*, *Phaedrus*; Augustine, *Confessions* X (d. 430). Doctrines, not techniques, which Yates treats as the ground of the scholastic and Renaissance forms of the art.

**Principle.** For Aristotle the soul never thinks without a mental picture; memory is a store of images stamped like a seal on wax, and reminiscence is a deliberate hunt by association (similar, contrary, contiguous) and by order from a starting point, "some use places for the purposes of recollecting"; the scholastics conflated this with Tullius's rules as their philosophical justification, though Yates doubts Aristotle meant more than illustration. For Plato the wax block receives perceptions, but true knowledge is recollection of the Ideas seen before birth, so "a Platonic memory would have to be organised ... in relation to the realities", and writing is "an elixir not of memory but of reminding"; this is the ground the Renaissance Neoplatonists build on. Augustine enters memory as "fields and spacious palaces" holding treasuries of images, the arts by presence, the affections by notions, and God "in no place"; Yates reads the capacity and organisation as a memory trained on the classical mnemonic in the buildings of the antique world, possibly "a Christianised artificial memory". "Aristotle is essential for the scholastic and mediaeval form of the art; Plato is essential for the art in the Renaissance."

**Components.**
- Aristotle's doctrine of memory and reminiscence (as used by the scholastics) `[1.14]`
- Plato's memory: the wax block, anamnesis, and the Theuth story `[1.15]`
- Augustine's memory: the fields and spacious palaces (*Confessions* X) `[1.17]`

**Yates.** Ch. II, pp. 31–39, 44–49; scholastic use Ch. III, pp. 61, 63–64, 67–68, 70–72, 75; Fig. 9 (faculty psychology after Romberch) belongs to Ch. XI.

**In Yates's words.**
- "Aristotle's work thus became for them a kind of memory treatise, to be conflated with the rules of Tullius and which provided philosophical and psychological justifications for those rules." (p. 32)
- "The grandiose attempt to do just this, within the framework of the art of memory, was made by the Neoplatonists of the Renaissance." (p. 37)
- "one gains, I think, quite strongly the impression that Augustine's was a trained memory, trained on the lines of the classical mnemonic." (p. 46)

**Descends from / leads to.** To 9–10 (Albertus's ars reminiscendi and inspired melancholy; Thomas's second and fourth precepts; Bartolomeo's "Aristotile in libro memoria"), 15 (the 'Democritus' treatises rest on Aristotelian association), 21 (Camillo's Theatre "based (so he believes) on archetypes of reality"), 24 (Lull's three powers of the Augustinian soul), 33 (Dicson's dialogues built on the Theuth story, used by "a disciple of Bruno for the 'Egyptian' inner writing", p. 39 ref. p. 268), 45 (Leibniz's three Aristotelian laws for the *nota*). Augustine's three powers of the soul join Tullius's Prudence at p. 79.

**Dossier note.** Loci: *De memoria* 449b–452a (450b 1–10, 451b 18–20, 452a 8–25); *De anima* 427b, 431b, 432b; *Topica* 163b 24–30; *De insomniis* 458b 20–22; Diogenes Laertius V, 26 for a lost book on mnemonics. *Theaetetus* 191 C–D; *Phaedo* 75 B–D; *Phaedrus* 249 E–250 D and 274 C–275 B; Cicero, *Tusculans* I, xxiv–xxv, 59–65. *Confessions* X, 8–9, 17, 25–26; III, 4; *De Trinitate* IX, 6, xi (the arch at Carthage). The Albertine and Thomist commentaries (systems 9–10) are the medieval readings.

## 6. Neopythagorean memory-training and the *Ars Notoria*

Apollonius of Tyana as described by Philostratus; the medieval *Ars Notoria* attributed to Apollonius or Solomon; with the magical treatises Yates notes among the printed books (Weczdorff, Simon aus Weida).

**Principle.** The Pythagorean revival made memory a religious discipline: Apollonius practised silence, stored all in memory, sang a hymn to memory; a Brahmin wears seven planetary rings; Dionysius of Miletus was accused of "Chaldaean arts". The *Ars Notoria* practitioner gazes at curiously marked figures called *notae*, one for each art or science, while reciting magical prayers, hoping to gain knowledge or memory of all the arts; Yates suggests it descends, transformed underground, from the shorthand-notae branch of the classical art. Among the printed treatises she notes "traces of infiltration of mediaeval forms of magic memory, such as the Ars notoria", with heavily magical frontispieces and diagrams, and Thomas's condemnation of the *Ars notoria* is recalled when Bruno is reached. It is the "lazy magic" against which Yates sets Bruno's industrious system-building.

**Components.**
- Neopythagorean memory-training and the Ars Notoria `[1.16]`
- The magical infiltration of the printed treatises (Weczdorff, *Ars memorandi nova secretissima*; Nicolas Simon aus Weida, *Ludus artificialis oblivionis*) — the second half of `[2.9]`, whose "straight mnemotechnic" label is placed under system 16

**Yates.** Ch. II, pp. 42–43; Ch. V, pp. 114 (n. 25), 127 (n. 57, Erasmus's Colloquy); Ch. IX, p. 204 (n. 16, Thomas's condemnation, *Summa* II, II, q. 96); Ch. XIII, p. 306; Ch. XV, p. 328 n. 17 (the Toulouse art "with the ring of Solomon").

**In Yates's words.**
- "The practitioner of the Ars Notoria gazed at figures or diagrams curiously marked and called 'notae' whilst reciting magical prayers." (p. 43)
- "The Ars Notoria is perhaps a bastard descendant of the classical art of memory, or of that difficult branch of it which used the shorthand notae." (p. 43)
- "In some there are traces of infiltration of mediaeval forms of magic memory, such as the Ars notoria into the art." (p. 114)

**Descends from / leads to.** From 2 (notae branch) and 4 (the awe of a zodiacal memory). To 26–28 (Bruno's Hermetic memory, contrasted at p. 306), 40 (the magical art Fludd heard of at Toulouse, possibly Belot's; see Appendix).

**Dossier note.** Philostratus, *Life of Apollonius* I, 14; III, 16, 41; *Lives of the Sophists* on Dionysius; Iamblichus, Porphyry, Diogenes Laertius on Pythagorean memory; Thorndike, *History of Magic* II, ch. 49 for the *Ars Notoria*; Volkmann's plates 168–71 for Weczdorff and Simon aus Weida; Erasmus's Colloquy against the *Ars notoria*.

## 7. Martianus Capella and Alcuin: the art handed on and lost

Martianus Capella of Carthage, *De nuptiis Philologiae et Mercurii* (fifth century AD); Alcuin, *De rhetorica et de virtutibus* for Charlemagne (c. 800).

**Principle.** Martianus gives, under memoria as the fourth part of rhetoric, an "incomprehensibly potted version of the rules in a setting of allegory": the Simonides story; precepts pondered in well-lighted places where "images of things" are set (a veiled girl for a wedding, a sword for a murderer); places as wax, images as letters; then the Quintilianist advice to write the matter down, divide it, set notae, murmur it by night. Rules for places are reduced to one and the imagines agentes are not given, though Yates notes his personified liberal arts "conform remarkably well" to the rules for images. He lodged the art in its niche among the liberal arts for the Middle Ages. Alcuin, drawing on *De inventione* and Julius Victor (who dismisses places and images), teaches Charlemagne the four virtues and the parts of Prudence but has no precepts for memory beyond "exercise in memorising, practice in writing, application to study, and the avoidance of drunkenness": the First Rhetoric without the Second. Cassiodorus, Isidore and Bede do not mention the art at all.

**Components.**
- Martianus Capella's compressed art (*De nuptiis Philologiae et Mercurii*) `[1.18]`
- Alcuin's precepts: the art lost (Carolingian rhetoric) `[1.19]`

**Yates.** Ch. III, pp. 50–54, 57.

**In Yates's words.**
- "He thus handed on the art to the Middle Ages firmly lodged in its correct niche in the scheme of the liberal arts." (p. 50)
- "The personified liberal arts conform remarkably well to the rules for images in the artificial memory" (p. 52)
- "The artificial memory has disappeared! Its rules have gone, replaced by 'avoid drunkenness'!" (p. 53)

**Descends from / leads to.** From 1–3 (Cicero, Quintilian's tablet method). To 9 (Albertus's nocturnal ram perhaps from "too much memory work in the night ... as advised by Martianus"), 10 (the twelfth–fourteenth-century vogue of *Ad Herennium* MSS that follows), 13 (page-visualising combined with images in Holcot), 17 (Romberch's inscribed Grammatica as the printed continuation of the personified arts). John of Salisbury's *Metalogicon* is noted as "closer to what was later to be Lull's view" (p. 56 n. 16; see Appendix).

**Dossier note.** *De nuptiis*, ed. Dick, pp. 268–70; Alcuin, ed. Howell pp. 136–9, 146; Julius Victor in Halm, *Rhetores latini* p. 440. The liberal arts as figures appear in Pl. 1 and the Vienna MS 2639 (system 10).

## 8. Boncompagno da Signa's memory of Paradise and Hell

Boncompagno da Signa, Bolognese *dictamen* master; *Rhetorica Novissima*, Bologna 1235.

**Principle.** Memory belongs to all arts, not rhetoric only. Boncompagno defines natural and artificial memory after *Ad Herennium* but gives no place-and-image rules; instead, the corruption of memory by the Fall, the humours and stellar influence, a list of "memory signs" in Scripture (the cock-crow reminding Peter), the memory of Paradise (the divine throne, the angelic orders) and of Hell (Etna seen from the sea), with the injunction to remember assiduously the joys of Paradise and the torments of Hell, and a list of virtues and vices as "memorial notes" (*signacula*). Yates proposes it as the Bolognese background of Albertus and Thomas and the seed of the medieval habit of treating Heaven and Hell, virtues and vices, as the things memory chiefly holds. Its "mystical rhetoric" foreshadows the later tradition (metaphor "invented in the Earthly Paradise").

**Components.**

## 9. Albertus Magnus's *ars memorandi*

Albertus Magnus, Dominican (entered 1223); *De bono*, tract. IV (Prudence), q. 2; commentary on *De memoria et reminiscentia*.

**Principle.** Albertus examines "the ars memorandi which Tullius gives in the Second Rhetoric" under memory as a part of Prudence and is "the first to make this conflation" of Aristotle with Tullius: artificial memory belongs to reminiscence, in the rational soul. Places should be real buildings rather than an imaginary camp or city (Yates infers a church; "solemn and rare" places move most), and the five place rules are confirmed. The memory image contains its *intentio* (an image of Justice contains the intention to seek it); *metaphorica* are preferred to *propria* because "the wonderful moves the memory more"; the imagines agentes are quoted in full into a treatise on ethics. In the commentary the rule for dark and solitary places becomes a mystical retirement in which a ram with huge horns and testicles charges out of the darkness, and reminiscence belongs to the hot, inspired melancholy of the *Problemata*. The artificial memory pertains "to the moral man and to the speaker", the warrant for its later lay use.

**Components.**
- Albertus Magnus's ars memorandi (*De bono* and the *De memoria* commentary) `[1.21]`

**Yates.** Ch. III, pp. 61–70; recalled pp. 90, 97–99, 104; Ch. IX pp. 204–205 (the "huge ram in the night skies" against Thomas's caution, and a possible Albertist development in Bruno's Naples convent).

**In Yates's words.**

## 10. Thomas Aquinas's four precepts and the scholastic transformation

Thomas Aquinas, *Summa Theologiae* II, II, qq. 48–49 and the commentary *In De memoria et reminiscentia*; with Yates's reconstruction of the "basic Thomist method" from the Vienna MS 2639 and the Santa Maria Novella fresco.

**Principle.** Four precepts: assume convenient corporeal similitudes, not too familiar, because "simple and spiritual intentions slip easily from the soul unless ... linked to some corporeal similitudes"; place them in a considered order so that from one point one passes to the next; dwell with solicitude and cleave with affection (a misreading of *solitudo* as *sollicitudo*); meditate frequently. Images first, then places; order, "really the order of the argument", replaces the classical irregular loci. Yates's synthesis: the things the Middle Ages wished to remember were salvation and damnation, the imagines agentes were moralised into virtues and vices as corporeal similitudes of spiritual intentions "memorised as ranged in order in some 'solemn' building", so that the Summa might be corporealised into a Gothic cathedral of images on ordered places. Its concrete form is orders of inscribed figures on a regular series of places: in the Vienna MS Prudence holds a circle inscribed with her eight Thomist parts, Temperance's parts are on a tree, Fortitude's on her castle, Justice's in her book, like Metrodorus writing on the zodiac images. Thomas "became something like its patron saint", cited by treatises from Ragone (1434) to Feinaigle (1813).

**Components.**

**In Yates's words.**

- "the abstract Summa might be corporealised in memory into something like a Gothic cathedral full of images on its ordered places." (p. 79)

## 11. The Dominican transmission to preachers and laymen

Giovanni di San Gimignano, O.P., *Summa de exemplis* (1298–1314); Bartolomeo da San Concordio, O.P., *Ammaestramenti degli antichi* (before 1323) with Bono Giamboni's *Trattato della memoria artificiale*; the *Rosaio della vita* (1373) with its *Ars memorie artificialis*.

**Principle.** San Gimignano abbreviates Thomas into four things that help a man remember (order, affection, unusual similitudes, frequent meditation) inside a summa of similitudes for preachers; Yates notes the memory image is invisible but "can become the hidden generator of externalised imagery", and that sermons were the medieval transformation of oratory the art chiefly served. Bartolomeo's vernacular ethical treatise gives eight rules under memoria as a part of Prudence, quoted from Thomas, Aristotle and "Tullio nel terzo della nuova Rettorica"; the detached *Ad Herennium* memory section in Giamboni's translation that travelled with it is "an ancestor of the separate Ars memorativa treatise", and laymen were to memorise the book's virtues and vices by the art. The *Rosaio*'s rules expand *Ad Herennium* with "natural places" memorised in the country (trees in fields) and "artificial places" in buildings (a study, a window, a coffer) on which corporeal similitudes of the virtues and vices are set: the art "as a lay devotional discipline, fostered and recommended by the friars".

## 12. Trecento painting and Dante as memory imagery

Ambrogio Lorenzetti (Siena, 1337–40); Giotto (Arena Chapel, c. 1306); Romberch's places of Hell and Nardo di Cione's Hell fresco; Dante's *Divina Commedia* read "as a kind of memory system".

**Principle.** Yates proposes to look at Trecento didactic art "with the eyes of memory". Lorenzetti's Justice sits with secondary figures illustrating her parts "after the manner of a composite memory image", the tyrannical vices hovering like bats: an argument "set out in order and clothed in corporeal similitudes". Giotto's virtues and vices are dramatic, beautiful or grotesque imagines agentes, each standing out from a varied background as the place rules require, regularly placed as Thomist order allows, framing the Last Judgment as Boncompagno enjoined. Hell divided into many inscribed places, each punishment fitting its sin, makes "the places of Hell ... variegated memory loci" with the damned as striking images (Romberch's text, Dolce's gloss, Nardo di Cione's compartments). Dante's poem then becomes "a summa of similitudes and exempla, ranged in order and set out upon the universe": orders of places in Hell, Purgatory and Paradise, striking images on them, the three parts of Prudence as its structure, Memory as "the converting power, the bridge between the abstraction and the image". Yates leaves the Dante reading "as a shock".

**Components.**
- Lorenzetti's Good and Bad Government read as memory imagery `[1.27]`
- Giotto's virtues and vices (Arena Chapel) as imagines agentes on variegated loci `[1.28]`
- Hell and Paradise as memory places (Romberch's places of Hell; Nardo di Cione) `[1.29]`
- Dante's *Divine Comedy* as a memory system `[1.30]`

**Yates.** Ch. IV, pp. 92–96; Preface p. xii; anticipated pp. 59–60; Pl. 2 (Lorenzetti, Justice and Peace), Pl. 3a–b (Giotto, Charity and Envy), Pl. 8a (Nardo di Cione, The Places of Hell).

**In Yates's words.**

## 13. The English friars' invisible pictures: Ridevall and Holcot

John Ridevall, O.F.M., *Fulgentius metaforalis* (early fourteenth century); Robert Holcot, O.P., *Moralitates* and the commentary on Hosea.

**Principle.** The friars' "pictures" are descriptions of images never meant to be painted: "invisible memory images, held within the memory ... being used for quite practical mnemonic purposes". Ridevall's Idolatry is a prostitute, blind, ears mutilated, trumpeted, deformed, diseased, each feature keyed to a point of a sermon and fixed by a mnemonic verse; his pagan gods are moralised memory images (Saturn Prudence, Juno memoria, Neptune intelligentia, Pluto providentia). Holcot's "sham antique" pictures (Penance as a naked man with a five-thonged scourge carrying five inscribed sentences; Friendship as a youth in green with inscriptions) memorise sentences as written on the image, which Yates takes to be what the Middle Ages understood by "memory for words". Holcot also places such images, Cupid with bow and arrows included, in imagination on the pages of the prophet's text at the point he will comment on: the page as place system, the moralised pagan image as image. The artificial memory becomes "a hitherto unsuspected medium through which pagan imagery survived".

**Components.**
- The English friars' invisible 'pictures': Ridevall and Holcot (inscribed memory images) `[1.31]`
- Holcot's memory images placed on the pages of a text `[1.32]`

**Yates.** Ch. IV, pp. 96–99; Pl. 4c (Penance, Biblioteca Casanatense 1404, a later illustration "we ought not to be seeing").

**In Yates's words.**
- "These invisible 'pictures' provide us with specimens of invisible memory images, held within the memory, not intended to be externalised, and being used for quite practical mnemonic purposes." (p. 96)
- "the memorising of words or sentences as placed or written on the memory images was perhaps what the Middle Ages understood by 'memory for words'." (p. 98)

## 14. Petrarch's *Rerum memorandarum libri*

Francesco Petrarca, *Rerum memorandarum libri*, c. 1343–45; Petrarch as one of the "Professors of Memory" of the later treatises.

**Principle.** An ethical treatise designed for memorisation on the plan of *De inventione*: preludes to virtue (leisure, solitude, study, doctrine), then Prudence under memoria, intelligentia, providentia, then the other virtues. Under memoria Petrarch cites the classical art ("Memory is of two kinds, one for things, one for words"), Seneca reciting backwards, Latro Portius's memory "good both by nature and by art", Themistocles refusing the art; he uses *De oratore* rather than *Ad Herennium*. Romberch credits him with place rules (no perturbation must disturb the order of places; places of medium size; use many places) and thereby absorbs the poet into the Dominican memory tradition, and Gesualdo, Garzoni, Agrippa, Schenkel and Diderot's *Encyclopédie* keep him among the memory authorities. Yates supposes pagan images as his corporeal similitudes, perhaps the virtues in chariots with their exempla as in the *Trionfi*.

**Components.**

**In Yates's words.**

## 15. The fifteenth-century manuscript treatises

Anonymous and named manuscript *Artes memorativae* of the fifteenth century: Lodovico da Pirano (Padua, from c. 1422), Luca Braga (1477), the 'pater reuerende' treatises, the anonymous Dominican *De memoria artificiali* (Marciana VI, 238) with Jacopo Ragone and Matthew of Verona, the Vienna codex 5395.

**Principle.** The manuscript tradition behind the printed treatises. The 'Democritus' type follows the *Ad Herennium* plan but assigns the invention to Democritus, ignores the striking human figures and Thomas, rests its image rules on Aristotelian association, and carries diagrams of rows of towers for the "multiplication of places" (Yates suspects a Byzantine channel). The 'pater reuerende' treatises supply long lists of familiar objects (a paternoster, an anvil, a helmet, a lantern, a tripod) as prefabricated memory images to be memorised on sets of places. The anonymous Dominican treatise holds "the whole order of the universe and the roads to Heaven and Hell" with only "devout images" and "sacred histories", saints with attributes on loci, in text nearly identical with Romberch's. The Vienna codex shows a rare row of human memory figures (Prudence at the head, Christ at the centre, Hell's mouth beneath, faces to resemble known persons, subsidiary images as memory for words) and plans of memory rooms with five places, four corners and a centre, the regular layout Yates takes as the normal medieval and later interpretation of places.

## 16. The first printed treatises: Publicius and Peter of Ravenna

Jacobus Publicius, *Oratoriae artis epitome* with *Ars memorativa* (Venice 1482, the first printed memory treatise); Peter of Ravenna (Petrus Tommai), *Phoenix, sive artificiosa memoria* (Venice 1491); with Yates's sorting of the printed treatises into strands.

**Principle.** Publicius's *ficta loca* are the spheres of the universe (elements, planets, fixed stars, higher spheres, "Paradisus" at the top), a Dantesque memory of Hell, Purgatory and Paradise on the cosmic order; his images follow Thomas and the Ad Herennian strikingness with Ovid's Envy as the model; he is the first to print a visual alphabet, and he "comes straight out of the mediaeval tradition", not the humanist revival. Peter of Ravenna forms loci in an unfrequented church, walked round three or four times, adds places on journeys until he claims 100,000 and more; images resemble known people, and persons in alphabetical order stand for syllables (Eusebius before Thomas = ET); he laicised and popularised the art and stressed its purely mnemotechnical side, with the rule that a locus be no larger than a man can reach. Yates sorts the printed treatises into four strands: the "straight mnemotechnic" better understood after the recovery of Quintilian; those entangled with medieval uses; those touched by *Ars notoria* magic; those touched by the Hermetic transformation.

**Components.**
- Publicius's *Ars memorativa*: the spheres of the universe as imaginary places `[2.7]`

## 17. Romberch's *Congestorium artificiose memorie*

Johannes Romberch, O.P. (German), *Congestorium artificiose memorie*, Venice 1520 (Yates uses the 1533 edition).

**Principle.** "A strange congestion of memory material" in four parts: introduction, places, images, an encyclopaedic system; Romberch knows all three classical sources, cites Petrarch and Peter of Ravenna, and rests on Thomas "on nearly every other page". He envisages three types of place system, all called artificial memory: the cosmos (spheres of elements, planets, stars and angelic orders, with Paradise, Earthly Paradise, Purgatory and Hell marked at the foot, Hell's places holding sinners punished by their sins, the "Dantesque type"); the zodiac and Hyginus's constellations (system 4); and real buildings, the abbey with courtyard, library and chapel supplying numbered loci marked with a hand at each fifth and a cross at each tenth place, stocked with memory objects and backed by alphabetical lists. A locus holding a human image must be no larger than a man can reach. Visual alphabets (objects shaped like letters, birds in alphabetical order, persons, number-objects) are used for inscriptions in memory: Grammatica holds a Pica for *predicatio* and an Aquila for *applicatio*, personifications recalling things and inscriptions memorising words. The encyclopaedic fourth part imagines a figure for every science (a perfect theologian with *cognitio, amor, fruitio* on his head), each subject in a memory room, a possible "distant echo" of Thomas's own system.

**Components.**
- Romberch's *Congestorium*: plan of the book and the three types of place system `[2.10]`
- The cosmos as place system: the 'Dantesque' type `[2.11]`

## 21. Camillo's Memory Theatre: the structure

Giulio Camillo Delminio (c. 1480–1544); the wooden theatre shown at Venice (1532) and built at Paris for Francis I (1534); *L'Idea del Theatro*, dictated to Muzio, printed Florence and Venice 1550; Yates's plan reconstructed from it (the Folder).

**Principle.** A Vitruvian theatre reversed: the solitary spectator stands on the stage and looks at the auditorium, where seven gangways (the seven planets) cross seven rising grades (the stages of creation), and on each gangway at each grade stands an imaginary decorated gate, seven times seven memory places stocked with images, the greatest things lowest. The whole rests on seven pillars, the seven Sephiroth of the supercelestial world, the "seven measures" of the *Pimander*, the Ideas, as "eternal places" against the orators' "frail places" (Kether and Hokmah omitted); since the Sephiroth are remote, the seven planets with their strongly differentiated images occupy the first grade, Sol represented by a pyramid with Apollo above. The upper grades are the Banquet (simple elements), the Cave (mixtures), the Gorgon Sisters (the interior man, three souls), Pasiphe and the Bull (soul joined to body), the Sandals of Mercury (natural operations), Prometheus (all arts and sciences), so that an image keeps its theme but changes meaning by grade (Juno suspended = air, from simple element to windmills). Beneath the images, drawers of papers hold Ciceronian speeches on the subject each image recalls: the image is the thing, the stored text the words, "a highly ornamental filing cabinet". Yates shows from Barbaro's Vitruvius that the theatre's seven gangways and five doors are fixed by four zodiacal triangles, so the building itself mirrors the twelve signs, which Camillo distorted by moving the doors from the *frons scaenae* to the gangways.

**Components.**

- The first grade: the seven planets `[2.24]`
- The six upper grades: Banquet, Cave, Gorgon Sisters, Pasiphe and the Bull, Sandals of Mercury, Prometheus `[2.25]`
- The planetary series: the same image changing meaning by grade `[2.26]`
- The drawers: Ciceronian speeches stored under the images `[2.27]`

## 22. Camillo's Memory Theatre: the occult engine

Yates's interpretation of the Theatre's "secret" (Ch. VI pp. 151–158; Ch. VII pp. 160–163): the Sun series, the memory images as inner talismans, Ficino's and Pico's precepts, and the Dantesque residue.

**Principle.** The great central series of Sol shows Ficinian sun mysticism with magical solarianism: the Sun as *statua Dei* and image of the Trinity, the light hierarchy Sol, Lux, Lumen, Splendor, Color, Generatio, Argus as the world vivified by astral spirit, the Golden Chain under the Sandals, the Cock and Lion story proving Camillo's "solar virtue", the solar spiritus identified with the spirit of Christ. The basic planetary images are supposed to be inner talismans drawing astral spiritus into memory, their power running through the subsidiary images of each series and unifying memory from its causes; Camillo's own gloss on the Egyptian statues locates the magic in perfect proportion, so the magic of a memory image would be its proportions. Ficino's letter adds proportion, harmony and connection to the classical demand for order, his projected *imago mundi* supplies an astral memory building, Pico's "theatre of the world" may have suggested the form; so Camillo developed rather than invented occult memory. Beneath the surface the old places survive: Cybele vomiting fire is Hell, Europa and the Bull true religion, the Mouth of Tartarus Purgatory, the sphere of ten circles the Earthly Paradise, the wolf–lion–dog heads Prudence's three times; these coffers "must have been full of sermons. Or of cantos of the Divine Comedy."

## 24. The Art of Ramon Lull

Ramon Lull (c. 1235–1316); the Art in its versions from c. 1272 to the *Ars generalis ultima* and *Ars brevis* (1305–8); *Liber de ascensu et descensu intellectus*; *Arbor scientiae*; *Tractatus de astronomia*.

**Principle.** An art founded on the Dignitates Dei (Bonitas, Magnitudo, Eternitas, Potestas, Sapientia, Voluntas, Virtus, Veritas, Gloria = B to K) as primordial causes issuing into creation, used by intellect to find truth, by will to love it, by memory to remember it. No images: concepts are carried by a letter notation on geometrical figures, some of which revolve. The A figure sets B to K on a wheel joined by triangulations for meditating the Names in the Godhead; the T figure holds the relata (differentia, concordia, contrarietas; principium, medium, finis; majoritas, equalitas, minoritas) as triangles in a circle; the combinatory figure, a stationary outer circle with concentric revolving inner circles, reads off combinations of the letters, the *ars combinatoria* in its simplest form, derived from cosmological *rotae*. The artista carries the figures up and down the ladder of the nine subjects (Deus, Angelus, Coelum, Homo, Imaginativa, Sensitiva, Vegetativa, Elementativa, Instrumentativa), measuring the same proportions on each level; square is the elements, circle the heavens, triangle the divinity, and the logical square of opposition is identified with the square of the elements. The *Arbor scientiae* schematises the encyclopaedia as trees rooted in B to K, "a kind of place system"; on the level *coelum* the Art manipulates the signs and planets with B to K in a benevolent, wholly imageless astral science and medicine. Contrasted point by point with the classical art; associated with the Franciscans as the classical art with the Dominicans; a missionary Art meant to demonstrate the Trinity to Jews and Muslims.

## 25. Lullism as memory and the Renaissance Lull

Lull's own doctrine of the Art as memoria and his *Liber ad memoriam confirmandam* (Pisa, c. 1308); Lavinheta (1523) and Salzinger (1721–42) as its readers; the Cabalist and alchemical Lull of the Renaissance and Yates's "grand Renaissance aim".

**Principle.** Since the three powers of the soul are one, the Art as memory consists in memorising the whole Art, its principles, terminology and operations; the diagrams are in some sense places, and memory "is becoming a method of investigation", quite unlike the classical art that "seeks only to memorise what is given"; no striking images. The *Liber ad memoriam confirmandam* divides memory into natural (received at generation, according to the reigning planet) and artificial, the latter either medicines and plasters or frequent going-over "like an ox chewing the cud"; the Tullian places and images are pointedly omitted, and three times the reader is sent to the "Book of the Seven Planets" for the key. Lavinheta assigns the classical places and images to *sensibilia* and the *Ars generalis* to *intelligibilia*, making the classical art the inferior discipline; Salzinger identifies the Book of the Seven Planets with the *Tractatus de astronomia* and reads memory as retained through the seven planets as "seven instruments", so that the sixteenth century could find in Lull a memory based on the celestial seven, without images. Meanwhile Pico declared the Cabalist *ars combinandi* with revolving Hebrew alphabets (Abulafia) like the *ars Raymundi*; the Pseudo-Lullian *De auditu kabbalistico* identified B to K with the Sephiroth; Pseudo-Lullian alchemical figures set combinatory wheels at the root and planetary wheels at the top of a Lullian tree, making Lull a Magus. The "grand Renaissance aim" was to set magic images of the stars on the revolving Lullian figures: Camillo's Theatre only anticipates it, "static in a building"; Bruno achieves it.

## 26. Bruno's memory works, the *Ars memoriae*, and the Paris context

Giordano Bruno (1548–1600), Dominican at Naples 1563–76: *De umbris idearum* (*Shadows*, Paris 1582), *Cantus Circaeus* (*Circe*, Paris 1582), the *Ars reminiscendi* reprinted in *Seals* (London 1583); with the context of Porta, Agrippa, Gohorry and Garzoni.

**Principle.** *Shadows*, *Circe* and *Seals* each contain an "art of memory" on the old plan of rules for places and rules for images, with all the classical precepts and their accrued elaborations, under new terminology (locus = *subjectus*, image = *adjectus*) and a philosophy of the magically animated imagination; *Shadows* is presented by Hermes to Philotheus against Logifer the pedant. The *Ars reminiscendi* of *Circe*, introduced by the sorceress's incantations to the seven planets (omitted in the English reprint), expands the rules like a normal treatise: very large numbers of places (the last Roman place joined to the first Parisian), a list of thirty ways of forming images by association, a claimed better memory for words than "Tullius", and "semi-mathematical" subjects, diagrams mathematical "in some other way", i.e. magical, as place systems. Around it: Porta's *Ars reminiscendi* (Naples 1566/1602), a treatise without overt magic that reads Dido's painted rooms as her memory system, uses palaces, theatres or geometrical figures as places, images from Michelangelo, Raphael and Titian, hieroglyphs, and condemns Metrodorus; Agrippa's *De occulta philosophia* II, whose decan, planet and mansion images Bruno copies; Gohorry's 1550 description of Camillo's wooden amphitheatre and his wish for bolder "images and seals"; Garzoni's announced ambition of a universal memory combining Rossellius and Lull. ## 27. The Thirty and the shadows of ideas

Bruno's thirty-lettered Lullian wheels, the philosophy of *De umbris idearum* (1582), and the *sigilli*, intentions, concepts and thirty mythological images that accompany them; *De compendiosa architectura artis Lullii* (Paris 1582).

**Principle.** Lullian concentric circles divided into thirty segments carrying the Latin alphabet plus Greek and Hebrew letters, more than any genuine Art; the Thirty are Lullian Dignities Cabalised as Sephiroth ("the Jewish Cabalists reduce to ten sephiroth and we to thirty"), the Trinitarian use rejected for the Tetragrammaton multiplied to the universe, and Yates believes the wheels doubled as practical Cabala conjuring the spirits of the air, the "architecture" of Lullian figures replacing the memory building. Star-images stand between the Ideas and the elemental world and are the "shadows of ideas", nearer reality than physical shadows: imprinted on memory and set on revolving wheels, "the lower things will arrange themselves in memory once we have arranged there the images of the higher things"; the Metrodorian system "has turned into a magical system". Images are graded by nearness to reality, the least opaque being *sigilli*, the theory of the 150 astral seals transmitting astral force. Two preliminary sets of thirty gnomic statements, the intentions of the shadows and the concepts of ideas, headed by the thirty letters and illustrated with thirty-lettered wheels (one with a sun at its centre), expound the road from multiplicity to the One, the intentions being the Lullian *voluntas* element. A further list of thirty mythological images from Lycaon to Glaucus, lettered for the wheels, is a separate system of the Thirty Statues type.

- The shadows of ideas: star-images as intermediaries; Metrodorus made magical `[2.62]`
- The *sigilli*: degrees of potency of memory images `[2.66]`
- The thirty intentions of the shadows and the thirty concepts of ideas `[2.67]`
- The thirty mythological images (Lycaon to Glaucus): a separate system `[2.68]`

## 28. The *Shadows* wheel system: 150 images on concentric wheels

Bruno, *De umbris idearum* (Paris 1582): the four lists of 150 items marked Aa, Ae, Ai, Ao, Au through thirty letters, reconstructed by Yates as concentric revolving wheels (Pl. 11).

**Principle.** Each wheel is cut into thirty segments (the letters) and each segment into five (the vowels), 150 divisions; four lists of 150 items so marked are set on concentric wheels revolving in the Lullian manner, the fives revolving independently within the thirties, so that images "form and reform in ever changing combinations". The central wheel is the "astral power station": 36 decan images after "Teucer the Babylonian" (Aries: a huge dark man with burning eyes in white; a woman; a man with sphere and staff), 49 planet images seven to a planet (Saturn as a stag-headed man on a dragon holding an owl eating a snake), the Draco lunae with the 28 mansions of the moon, and 36 house images of Bruno's own composing, "the whole sky with all its complex astrological influences". Next out, a "strangely jumbled" wheel of plants, birds, animals, stones, metals, artefacts and sacred objects, the lower creation moving in dependence on the celestial wheel; then 150 adjectives in the accusative (knotty, counterfeited, involved, formless, famous), unexplained; then the outer wheels of 150 inventors with their inventions from Rhegima (bread from chestnuts) through Osiris, Ceres, the magical arts (Circe fascination, Zoroaster magic), Theut writing, Plato, Raymundus, Bruno himself ("Ior. in clauim & umbras") to Melicus (Simonides) "in memoriam", the first of each five linking back and the last forward. "The revolving wheels of the Lullian Art have become the places for the reception of the images."

## 29. The Ramist method as an art of memory

Pierre de la Ramée (Peter Ramus, 1515–1572); *Dialecticae institutiones* (Paris 1543), *Scholae in liberales artes*, *Aristotelicae animadversiones*, *De religione Christiana*.

**Principle.** Memory is abolished as a part of rhetoric and with it the artificial memory of places and images; every subject is set out in "dialectical order", a schematic outline descending by dichotomies from generals to specials, and memorised in that order from the printed epitome (the first: Penelope's complaint in Ovid). Order is kept by the logical analysis itself; there are no buildings and no images, the "natural" stimulus being the abstract order Ramus holds natural to the mind, so "we smash the inner image of old Grammatica". Yates sees an element of spatial visualisation in the schematised page (Ong), traced to Quintilian's tablet and to bracketed Lullist manuscripts rather than to print; an "inner iconoclasm" matching the outer Protestant one; and, behind the rationalism, a Neoplatonic ascent-and-descent mysticism (Prometheus to Socrates as *prisca theologia*, the "golden chain"). Ramus quotes Quintilian: "the true art of memory is one and the same as dialectics." Citolini's imageless *Tipocosmia*, said to be stolen from Camillo's papers, is the "undigested and controversial" hint that a memory movement begun by Camillo on the occult level was continued by Sturm and Ramus "rationalised by omitting the images" (see Appendix).

**In Yates's words.**
- "The art of memory (says Quintilian) consists entirely in division and composition... For the true art of memory is one and the same as dialectics." (Ramus, p. 233)
- "Under Ramism, we smash the inner image of old Grammatica, and teach little boys to do so, substituting for her the imageless Ramist epitome of Grammar memorised from the printed page." (p. 234)

## 30. Agostino del Riccio's *Arte della memoria locale*

## 32. The Seal of Seals and the image-Seals (Zeuxis, Phidias, Husbandman, Fountain and Mirror)

Bruno, *Sigillus sigillorum*, the fourth item of the 1583 London volume, said to form part of the *Clavis magna*; with the Seals that carry the theory of the imagination.

**Principle.** Zeuxis the Painter is the principle "use images" and ties the Seals to *Shadows*: "the images of Teucer the Babylonian supply me with the indications of three hundred thousand propositions", and Bruno "knows a double picture", images and notae formed "from strange descriptions" (the astral images) and "edifices" feigned as need requires with sensible images for non-sensible things (normal classical memory, galvanised by affiliation to astral systems); painter, poet and philosopher are made one, "to understand is to speculate with images". Phidias the Sculptor moulds memory statues within, in wax, by addition of small stones, or "sculpturing the rough and formless stone as though by subtraction", releasing forms from the "inform chaos of memory". The Husbandman cultivates the Field with the Hermetic sentence that as the world is the image of God, man is the image of the world, so an art reproducing the divine organisation in memory taps the powers of the cosmos. The Fountain and the Mirror is the unifying stage, "one knowledge in one subject". The Seal of Seals is not a place-and-image system but the visionary apotheosis the Thirty lead up to (the reverse of *Shadows*): Simonides made a mystagogue leading from the celestial to the supercelestial; cognition as one imaginative process in four grades with the doors open between them, against the treatises' faculty psychology (Romberch's diagram of the head); vital images charged with affects, above all Love, unifying memory; a fifth grade of fifteen "contractions"; Thomas ranked with Zoroaster and Paul; solitude; four guides, Love, Art, Mathesis, Magic, leading to Light. ## 33. Alexander Dicson's *De umbra rationis*

Alexander Dicson of Errol ("Dicsono Arelio"), Scot, d. c. 1604; *De umbra rationis* (London, Vautrollier, dated 1583, early 1584), reprinted as *Thamus* (Leiden 1597); defended in *Defensio pro Alexandro Dicsono* (1584, "Heius Scepsius"); his teaching as reported by Hugh Platt (1592).

**Principle.** A close imitation of *Shadows*: loci are "subjects", images "adjuvants" or most often "umbrae", the image a shadow of the divine light sought through its shadows, vestiges and seals; the Ad Herennian rules repeated "muffled in an obscure mystique"; memory based on the order of the signs of the zodiac, repeated, without the decan list; traces of Bruno's inventors (Theutates letters, Nereus hydromancy, Chiron medicine); the Egyptians, not Simonides, invented the art, "if it is separated from Egypt it can effect nothing". As taught to Hugh Platt after Bruno left England, it was places in sets of ten with images made lively, "to animate the umbras or ideas rerum memorandarum" (Bellona with her fiery eyes), a simple form of the straight mnemotechnic without initiation into the mysteries. ## 34. The English controversy: Perkins, Watson, Sidney

William Perkins ("G. P. Cantabrigiensis"), *Antidicsonus* and *Libellus de memoria* (London 1584); Thomas Watson, *Compendium memoriae localis* (c. 1585); Sir Philip Sidney, *Defence of Poetrie* (c. 1583–85).

**Principle.** Perkins, Puritan Ramist, holds there are two arts of memory, one with places and "umbrae", the other by logical disposition as Ramus teaches, and only the latter is true: the method uses "true places", generals highest, subalterns middle, specials lowest, its "images" clear divisions, not fugitive shadows; the classical art is rejected as impious (the "animation" of images arouses carnal affects; Peter of Ravenna's libidinous images), a triple burden (places, images, thing), astrologically false (the "astronomy" of the Scepsians, on the Ramist ground that astronomy is a special art and memory a general one), and "barbarism and Dunsicality"; "a thing faigned in the mind by imagination is an idol". The *Prophetica* prescribes the method for memorising sermons. Watson's *Compendium* is "a perfectly straight exposition of the classical art as a rational mnemotechnic", disassociated in its preface from "the mystical and deeply learned Sigilli of the Nolan" and the "Umbra artificiosa of Dicson", and to publish a local memory then "amounted to taking up a position against Puritan Ramism". Sidney says verse is remembered because it is "a certain room divided into many places, well and throughly known", every word in its natural seat in the line, proof he did not memorise poetry by the Ramist method.

## 35. The *Figuratio* and the Thirty Statues

Bruno, *Figuratio Aristotelici physici auditus* (Paris 1586, "sub Rosa rubra", dedicated to Piero Del Bene); *Lampas triginta statuarum* (Wittenberg c. 1588, unfinished, unpublished in his lifetime, Noroff manuscript).

**Principle.** The *Figuratio* teaches how to memorise Aristotle's physics through mythological memory images (the Arbor Olympica, Minerva, Thetis as matter, Apollo as form, the "superior Pan" as nature, Cupid as motion, Saturn as time, Jupiter as prime mover) placed on a "curious looking" horoscope-like place system of the Seals type; the reader is told to choose a Seal, perhaps the Painter, perhaps the Sculptor. Yates argues the images, animated with divine proportions and in contact with cosmic powers, contain Bruno's own living philosophy, so "the memory system by which the physics is to be 'figured' is in itself a contradiction of the physics. The book is a Seal." The Statues then do what the *Figuratio* advised with the Seal of Phidias: towering mythological statues sculptured within, beginning with "infigurable" concepts (Orcus, the Abyss, the thirst for the infinite) and passing to Apollo (the Monad), Saturn (Time), Prometheus (efficient cause), Sagittarius (intention), Coelius, Vesta, Venus and Cupid (love), Minerva (the mens, memory and reminiscence) with her Ladder; Yates believes the thirty were to be revolved on Lullian wheels, images replacing letters. Their theory is threefold: ancient truths declared through fables to be remembered; sensibilia retained more easily than intelligibilia (Thomist); talismanic statues in the manner of the *Asclepius* applied inwardly. "They are his philosophy."

## 36. *De imaginum, signorum et idearum compositione* (*Images*)

Bruno, *De imaginum, signorum et idearum compositione*, Frankfort 1591, probably written at Hainzell's castle near Zurich, dedicated to Johann Heinrich Hainzell; in three parts.

**Principle.** Part I gives the Ad Herennian rules once more "in a yet more mystifying form", called a method ("We institute a method, not about things but about the significance of things"): rules for images first, a visual alphabet of objects straight out of Romberch (O a sphere, A a ladder or compasses, I a column), images lively and charged with affects "so that they may pass through the doors of the storehouse of memory", then rules for places partly in Latin verse with Romberch's reach-of-a-man rule. Its architectural system is "of terrible complexity": twenty-four atria each of nine places, fifteen fields of nine, thirty cubicles, in which everything in the lower world is memorised, plants, stones, metals, animals, arts, inventions, "all things which can be said, known, or imagined", the encyclopaedic wheels of *Shadows* redistributed into rooms, "worked from above by celestial mechanics". Part II supplies that celestial part: twelve "principles", Jupiter, Saturn, Mars, Mercury, Minerva, Apollo, Aesculapius, Sol, Luna, Venus, Cupid, Tellus, under the "ineffable and infigurable Optimus Maximus", great statues each surrounded by talismanic images composed to draw their powers into the psyche, Ficinian talismanic magic applied to memory images. The diagram of the twenty-four atria, a square plan of rooms related to the compass points inside a circle Yates takes for the heavens, realises the "double picture" of Zeuxis simultaneously, "the memory temple of an astral religion", the letters on the central circle possibly reading "Alta Astra". Part III is a second, German, listing of Thirty Seals, "if possible, even more obscure", with "mathesistical" place systems and no Seal of Seals.

## 37. Campanella's City of the Sun as local memory

Tommaso Campanella, *Città del Sole*, written c. 1602 in the Inquisition's prisons at Naples, first published in Latin 1623; his letters repeatedly offering the City or a model of it for local memory.

**Principle.** A round Utopian city with a round temple at its centre depicting all the stars with their relations to things below, and concentric circular walls on which are painted all mathematical figures, animals, birds, fishes, metals, human inventions and activities, statues of great men and founders of religions on the outermost wall: an encyclopaedic lay-out with a "celestial" organising basis, "using the world as a book", the children learning by walking the walls of images dependent on the central causal sun-temple images. ## 38. Schenkel and Paepp: the occult memory detected

Lambert Schenkel (1547–c. 1603), *De memoria* (Douai 1593) and *Gazophylacium* (Strasburg 1610; French *Le Magazin des Sciences*, Paris 1623); Johannes Paepp, *Schenkelius detectus* (Lyons 1617), *Eisagoge* and *Crisis* (Lyons 1619).

**Principle.** Schenkel, Louvain-trained public memory performer later accused of magic, who charged fees and withheld the full secrets from his books, teaches "basically the classical art" in the Romberch/Rossellius text-book stream, with long sections on places (diagrams of rooms containing memory places) and images, constant quotation of Thomas to attach it to the Dominican tradition, and a well-referenced history of the art (Simonides, Metrodorus, Tullius, Petrarch, Pico); "it could be a rational mnemotechnic", but obscure and citing suspect authors such as Trithemius. Paepp, his disciple, reveals the "arcane philosophical mysteries" hidden in it as Brunian, with long lists of magic images reminiscent of *Images*; his key passage runs from Thomas's *Summa* on order in memory, to the fifth treatise of the *Pimander* on the order of the universe as revelation of God, to the *Timaeus*, to Cicero on order, to *Ad Herennium* on places and images, and back to Aristotle and Thomas on frequent meditation: the thought-sequence by which the Tullian and Thomist places and images "turned into the magico-religious techniques of the occult memory". Together they are evidence that memory teaching could carry a Hermetic sect's message. Descartes's reform (system 43) was written "on reading through Schenkel's profitable trifles".

## 39. Bruno's Italian dialogues as memory: *Cena*, *Eroici furori*, *Spaccio*

Bruno, *La cena de le ceneri* (London 1584); *De gli eroici furori* (London 1585); *Spaccio della bestia trionfante* (London 1585); the last two dedicated to Sidney.

**Principle.** Yates reads the London dialogues as the art of memory developing into literature. In the *Cena* the places are real streets and waterways of Elizabethan London (the French embassy in Butcher Row, the Strand, the Thames, Charing Cross "the pyramid near the mansion where three streets meet", a house near Whitehall) traversed from the embassy to the Supper, on which Bruno "remembers" the themes of the debate on the Copernican Sun, the interlocutors populating the places; the opening invocation to Mnemosyne "hidden beneath the thirty seals" marks it as issuing from occult memory. The *Eroici furori* is a succession of about fifty emblems, Petrarchan conceits of eyes, stars, arrows and impresa shields, each in a poem and commentary, which Yates reads, "not, of course, as a memory system", as memory images charged with the affect of love carrying the soul's intentions, ending in Circe and a vast figure of Amphitrite "like some great memory statue". The *Spaccio* uses the forty-eight constellations in Hyginus's order, Romberch's text-book memory order, as the ground plan of a sermon on virtues and vices, a vice expelled and a virtue mounting on each constellation, the reforming gods perceived inwardly as statues or pictures: a Romberch system "'occultised' ... into a 'Seal' of his own".

- The love-emblems of the *De gli eroici furori* as memory images `[4.2]`
- The forty-eight constellations of the *Spaccio della bestia trionfante* (Hyginus's order occultised into a Seal) `[4.3]`

**Yates.** Ch. XIV, pp. 308–319 (*Cena* pp. 309–313; *Furori* pp. 313–314; *Spaccio* pp. 314–319).

**In Yates's words.**
- "We can thus see in the Cena how the art of memory could as it were develop into literature; how the streets of memory places could become populated with characters, could become the backcloth for a drama." (p. 312)

## 40. Fludd's Theatre memory system (with Willis's *Mnemonica*)

Robert Fludd, *Utriusque Cosmi Maioris scilicet et Minoris Historia*, Tomus Secundus (De Microcosmi Historia), sectio 2, *Ars memoriae*, Oppenheim, De Bry, 1619; with John Willis, *Mnemonica; sive Ars reminiscendi* (London 1618; English 1621, 1661) as possible immediate source.

**Principle.** Fludd divides the art into the *ars rotunda*, the "natural" and superior art whose fantasy operates "through ideas", spirits, shadows, souls, angels, demons, effigies of the stars, images of gods and virtues and vices "made into shadows", with the ethereal heavens as its common place, requiring daemonic or Holy-Spirit assistance; and the *ars quadrata*, the "artificial" art of corporeal, active images (the imagines agentes) in buildings or rooms, which must be real places, never the fictitious ones *Ad Herennium* allowed. The Theatre system combines them: the great common place is the eighth sphere or zodiac enclosing the seven planetary circles and the elements, into which are set "theatres" (stages, not playhouses), two flanking Aries on the diagram, an eastern theatre light and bright for the day and a western dark and obscure for the night, presumably a pair for each sign, twenty-four in all, so that their images are "astrally activated". Within each theatre five doors, about equidistant, and five feigned columns opposite them of distinct shapes (round, square, hexagon, square, round) and colours (white, red, green, blue, black) give ten places for things and words; two subsidiary theatres, rooms with one wall left out, of different materials, connect through their battlements. The celestial images are the "principal ideas", the planets through the signs (Saturn imaged differently in each sign, the same for the other planets); the "less principal images" in the theatres come in sets of five (Jason, Medea, Paris, Daphne, Phoebus; Medea collecting herbs on the white door, killing her brother on the red), with alphabetical lists, visual alphabets and number-images "in continuity with the old memory tradition". An introductory picture shows the "eye of imagination" beside five loci with an obelisk, Babel, Tobias and the Angel, a ship and the Last Judgment; a diagram of the three worlds shows sense impressions becoming *umbrae* in a *mundus imaginabilis* reflecting the zodiac, then the *mens* with the hierarchies and the Trinity, then the seat of memory receiving all three worlds. Willis's *Mnemonica* supplies the humble original: sets of identical one-level "theatres" or "repositories", divided by a column into two rooms, imagined in different colours which every image must share (a bushel with handles of pure gold in the golden theatre), a straight mnemotechnic "inner shopping list", with day things deposited before sleep and night things after.

## 41. The Globe Theatre reconstructed from Fludd's *Theatrum Orbi*

Yates's Chapter XVI: Fludd's engraving (Pl. 17) and text read, after Richard Bernheimer's "Another Globe Theatre" (1958), as a picture of the stage of the second Globe (rebuilt 1614).

**Principle.** Because Fludd insists on real, not fictitious, places, the theatre in the engraving is taken to be a real stage in a real public theatre. It shows the tiring-house wall from beneath the stage "heavens": three entrances at ground level (the central one with hinged doors on an inner room), two on the upper level giving on a battlemented terrace, and a projecting bay window or "chamber" in the centre. The side walls with their boxes and the five columns are "mnemonic distortions" that crush the stage into an enclosed memory room; removed, two posts rise to the painted heavens, which are the zodiac diagram facing the engraving, so the round art's common place is the real theatre's "heavens". The five column bases (round, square, hexagon, square, round) are read, with Hester Thrale's "hexagonal in form without ... round within", as the geometrical forms of the building, and the five entrances as the classical *frons scaenae* transposed to a multilevel stage; a plan is drawn from four Vitruvian triangles in a circle with Fortune-contract dimensions, "Oriens" at the top of the heavens giving a stage at the east end "like the altar in a church". The subsidiary theatres become moveable scenic screens with entrances (the Capulet orchard, the Friar's cell). Camillo distorts the Vitruvian theatre by moving the five doors into the auditorium; Fludd "stands with his back to the auditorium and looking towards the stage". Yates asks whether the twenty-four theatres in the zodiac are "an elaborate casket deliberately contrived to conceal his allusion to the Globe" for initiates such as James I, and answers that occult memory "is not to be entirely accounted for as a cipher".

## 42. Bacon's art of memory: prenotions and emblems

Francis Bacon, *Advancement of Learning* (II, xv, 2; II, x, 2; II, xvii, 14), *Novum Organum* II, xxvi, *De augmentis* V, v, *Sylva Sylvarum* Century X, 956; Aubrey on the Gorhambury windows.

**Principle.** Bacon builds the art "upon two intentions; the one prenotion, the other emblem", his version of places and images: prenotion "dischargeth the indefinite seeking" and directs the search to what "hath congruity with our place of memory" (a door, a corner, a window, familiar persons, or anything arranged in order, animals, herbs, words, letters, historical personages); emblem "reduceth conceits intellectual to images sensible, which strike the memory more" (a sportsman hunting the hare for invention, an apothecary ranging boxes for disposition). The art is turned from "ostentation prodigious" to "business and occasions", above all to memorising particulars of natural history in order so the judgment can work on them, order "turning into something like classification". "Men are best places", and the image's power is tied to "force of imagination" tricks. Aubrey's report of the Gorhambury gallery's painted panes of beast, bird and flower as "topiques for locall use" is one of the few known buildings designed for local memory. Bacon's attack on the microcosm doctrine "fantastically strained by Paracelsus" targets Metrodorian systems such as Fludd's; his demand for "real characters" seeds the universal-language movement; he rejects Lull's method as "a method of imposture".

## 43. Descartes's reform of the art of memory

René Descartes, *Cogitationes privatae* (1619–21), the note written "on reading through Schenkel's profitable trifles"; fragments of the *Studium bonae mentis* in Baillet; *Discours de la méthode* II; letter to Beeckman (1619).

**Principle.** Two proposals for "the true art of memory". First, "the reduction of things to their causes": since all can be reduced to one, one need not remember all the sciences; "when one understands the causes all vanished images can easily be found again in the brain through the impression of the cause", and images should be "formed in dependence on one another", the "key to the whole mystery" Schenkel omits, whose art fills the space "with too many things and not in the right order". Second, from unconnected images to compose "new images common to them all", or one image referring to all the others (the fifth to the first by a spear thrown on the ground, the middle by a ladder, the second by an arrow), notions "far from new" found in nearly every text-book. Yates finds the first "curiously like a rationalisation of occult memory", which does reduce all things to their supposed causes whose impressed images organise the subsidiary images. Descartes otherwise neglected local memory as "corporeal memory" and "outside of us", against an intellectual memory incapable of increase; his method is "not an ars brevis of Lull, but a new science ... concerning quantity", and he rejects Lull as a way "to speak without judgment of those things of which one is ignorant".

**Components.**
- Descartes's reform of the art of memory (reduction of images to their causes; interdependent images), prompted by Schenkel `[4.19]`

**In Yates's words.**

- "his brilliant new idea of organising memory on causes sounds curiously like a rationalisation of occult memory. His other notions about forming connected images are far from new and can be found in some form in nearly every text-book." (p. 374)

## 44. Seventeenth-century methods: Lullism mathematicised and the universal language

Cornelius Gemma, *De arte cyclognomica* (Antwerp 1569); the Lullian Art as judged by Bacon, Descartes and the 1632 Bureau d'Adresse; Johann-Heinrich Alsted (1588–1638), *Systema mnemonicum duplex* (1610) and *Clavis artis Lullianae* (1633); J. A. Comenius, *Orbis sensualium pictus* (Nuremberg 1658); the universal-language movement (Comenius, Bisterfield, Dalgarno, Wilkins); Sebastian Izquierdo, *Pharus Scientiarum* (Leyden 1659) and Athanasius Kircher, *Ars magna sciendi* (Amsterdam 1669).

**Principle.** "These two great mediaeval arts, which the Renaissance had tried to combine, turn into methods in the seventeenth century." Gemma's "circular method" for knowing everything is "a compound of Lullism, Hermetism, Cabalism, and the art of memory", showing how unsurprising Descartes's title would have seemed. Lullism offered "a universal art or method which, because based on reality, could be applied for the solution of all problems", a geometrical logic with revolving wheels and a letter notation, rejected by both Bacon and Descartes yet sharing their aims. Alsted, "encyclopaedist, Lullist, Cabalist, Ramist", defines method as "the mnemonic instrument which proceeds from generals to specials" and "calls the Lullian circles places corresponding to the places of the art of memory", assimilating Lullism to Cabalism through the *De auditu kabbalistico* while wishing to free it from "idle dreams" and return to Lavinheta: a reformed version of Brunian procedures. Comenius, Alsted's pupil, turns the Solarian pedagogy of the City of the Sun into a picture primer teaching Latin, German, Italian and French by pictures "arranged in the order of the world", "a perfectly rational, and extremely original and valuable, language primer". The universal-language enthusiasts seek "real characters" in contact with the notions they express, "translating into rational terms" Bruno's magic images directly in contact with reality. Izquierdo surveys the universal-art projects and insists Lullism be "mathematicised", substituting combinations of numbers for combinations of letters; Kircher likewise; "a presage of Leibniz's use of the principles of the combinatoria as a calculus".

## 45. Leibniz: *Mnemonica*, *combinatoria*, *characteristica*

G. W. Leibniz: *Dissertatio de arte combinatoria* (1666); *Nova methodus discendae docendaeque jurisprudentiae* (1667); the *characteristica universalis* project (Couturat; the *Fundamenta calculi ratiocinatoris*, *Introductio ad Encyclopaediam arcanam*, and related essays and letters).

**Principle.** In the *Nova methodus* "Mnemonica provides the matter of an argument; Methodologia gives it form; and Logica is the application of the matter to the form": Mnemonica is "the joining of the image of some sensible thing to the thing to be remembered", the image a *nota* connected by likeness, unlikeness or association; things seen are better remembered than heard, Egyptian and Chinese hieroglyphs are of the nature of memory images, and distribution "in cells or places" helps, for which Alsted and Frey are to be consulted; "a little memory treatise by Leibniz", with the emblem-figure of the *Disputatio de casibus* title-page read as a local memory for law suits and the 1678 remark on series of personages (patriarchs, apostles, emperors). The *Dissertatio de arte combinatoria* grasps Lullism "as a natural logic" (the square of the elements joined to the logical square), interpreted with arithmetic and Bacon's "inventive logic", the *notae* serving "as an alphabet", a universal writing better than the Egyptian and Chinese pictures "for memory"; its preface lists Agrippa, Alsted, Kircher and "Jordanus Brunus", who called the Art a "combinatoria"; Leibniz said the germ of his later thinking was here. The *characteristica* assigns a "character" to every essential notion, drawn from an encyclopaedia of all knowledge, and combines the characters logically "to form a universal art or calculus for the solution of all problems", so that disputants would say "Let us calculate"; characters are signs "written, or delineated, or sculptured", the more useful the closer to the thing, the notae of the arithmeticians preferred over Dee's *Monas*, Chinese and Egyptian figures and the Adamic language. Yates reads it as the search for "images for things" since Simonides transferred to mathematical symbolism, the infinitesimal calculus "but a fragment, or a specimen"; encyclopaedia, characters and calculus were never completed. Bound up with it are church-reunion plans and an "Order of Charity" whose rules quote the Rosicrucian *Fama*.

- Leibniz's *Mnemonica* (the *nota*; the "little memory treatise"; the emblem figure of the *Disputatio de casibus*; series of personages) `[4.25]`

## Appendix: mentions

Things the readers flagged as marginal, as analogues rather than arts, or as bare mentions. Pages are Yates's.

**Flagged marginal by the part-3 reader (kept out of the system count).**
- Citolini's *Tipocosmia* (Venice 1561), said to have been "stolen" from Camillo's papers: an encyclopaedic setting-out of subjects in order "but without the images", Yates's "undigested and controversial" hint of a rhetorical-methodological memory movement running from Camillo through Sturm to Ramus; Betussi (*Raverta*) and Liruti for the theft. Ch. X, pp. 238–239; suspected of theft of the Theatre's papers, p. 144. `[3.2]`
- Dee's *Monas hieroglyphica* (1564), the composite sign of the seven planetary characters: not an art of places and images, but "perhaps, in his eyes, a unified arrangement of significant signs, infused with astral power" composing the psyche into a One, offered as the only English preparation for Bruno's memory (Sidney, Greville and Dyer were Dee's pupils); Dee as the only English representative of the Lullist revival (Digby MS. 197); Dee's thirty concentric circles and *Clavis angelicae* (Cracow 1584). Ch. XI, pp. 262–263; Ch. IX p. 211; also among the impresa examples p. 124 and rejected by Leibniz p. 384. `[3.31]`
- The Rosicrucian vault and Royal Arch symbolism as occult memory (hypothesis "explicitly outside the book's scope"): the *Fama Fraternitatis* (Cassel 1614) with its rotae and a sacred vault whose walls, ceiling and floor are divided into compartments with figures or sentences, "something like an occult use of artificial memory"; the columns, arches and geometrical figures of speculative masonry possibly descending from the imaginary architecture of Camillo and Bruno rather than from operative guilds; the rumoured "Giordanisti". Ch. XIII, pp. 303–305 (Waite's translation pp. 75, 77; B. E. Jones, *Freemasons' Book of the Royal Arch*, 1957; Knoop and Jones, *The Genesis of Freemasonry*, 1947); Freemasonry indexed at pp. 286, 303–5 only. `[3.47]`

**Mentions in parts 1–2.**
- John of Salisbury, *Metalogicon*, a non-Ad-Herennian approach "closer to what was later to be Lull's view" (p. 56 n. 16).

**Mentions in part 3 (not counted as systems by that reader).**

- The *Ars notoria* as "the lazy magic" contrasted with Bruno's industry (p. 306); the "Giordanisti" (p. 303); Del Rio's commendation of Dicson (1600); the Family of Love (pp. 285–286).

**Mentions in part 4.**
- Bruno's Latin poems (*De immenso*, *De triplici minimo*, *De monade*) and the German Thirty Seals, excluded by Yates from further discussion (pp. 308 n. 1, 388).
- Jean Belot's "highly magical artificial memory" (mentioning Lull, Agrippa, Bruno; *Oeuvres*, Lyons 1654, pp. 329 ff.); R. Saunders's *Physiognomie and Chiromancie ... whereunto is added the Art of Memory* (London 1653, 1671, dedicated to Elias Ashmole, based on Belot); the *ars notoria*-like art "with the ring of Solomon" Fludd heard of at Toulouse (p. 328 n. 17).
- The magical theatre of the English Wagner Book of 1592, with posts, tiring house and heavens "spotted with golden teares which men callen Stars" (p. 345 n. 10).
- Fludd's "Temple of Music" in Tomus Primus (p. 349); Fludd's geomancy at Avignon 1601–2 and his account of learning the art at Nîmes, perfecting it at Avignon and teaching the Duc de Guise at Marseilles (p. 336).
- Dee's Preface to Billingsley's Euclid (1570) on Vitruvius, Alberti, the "Brasen Vesels" under theatre seats, and architecture "in mynde and Imagination" (pp. 360–363), a source for the Globe's design, not a memory art.
- The 1632 Bureau d'Adresse conference "De la méthode": the method of the Cabalists, the method of Ramon Lull, the method of ordinary philosophy (pp. 369–370).
- Andreae's *Christianopolis* (Strasburg 1619), picture-decorated for instructing youth, given only as an ancestor of the *Orbis pictus* (p. 378).
- Adam Bruxius, *Simonides Redivivus* (Leipzig 1610) and J. C. Frey, *Opera* (Paris 1645–6), memory authors known to Leibniz (pp. 380–381).
- The Rosicrucians: Fludd as self-declared disciple of the Brotherhood, Michael Maier as courier to De Bry (pp. 321, 324–5); Leibniz's "Rosicrucian aura" and Order of Charity (pp. 387–8). No Rosicrucian or Masonic memory theatre is described in Chapters XIV–XVII.
- Yates's closing remark that "Books on the art of memory continued to appear, still recognisably in the classical tradition" after Leibniz, and that "another book could probably be written" on later survivals (p. 389); Feinaigle, *The New Art of Memory* (1813), is named at pp. 82–83 and 336 as a late witness.

## Extraction notes

The four readers worked from a pypdf extraction of the book (`private_notes/yates_art_of_memory.txt`) and flagged its garbling so that nothing in their entries, or in this catalogue, rests on a corrupt passage. Part 1: pdf page 46 (printed p. 31) is scrambled, with footnotes and footer before the body and the word "places" displaced in the *Topica* quotation; the Greek of the *Dialexeis* examples (p. 30), Aristotle's "τόπων" (p. 34) and "τόποι" (p. 72) are garbled; footnote 21 on pp. 88–89 runs across the page break and is printed out of order; scattered OCR slips ("Institutio oratorio", "Simonidcs", "die/tiie" for "the") affect nothing. Part 2: Camillo's plan of the Theatre ("see Folder") is absent, pdf pages 167–168 being blank; the Sephiroth–planets–angels table (p. 148), Lull's B-on-each-level table (p. 179) and Bruno's inventor groups (pp. 218–221) came through with their columns run together, and were rebuilt from Yates's surrounding prose; printed page numbers for pp. 193 and 195 are missing and inferred; Plates 5–12 and Figs. 1–8 appear only as captions. Part 3: on pdf pages 260 (p. 231) and 272 (p. 243) the chapter openings are displaced to the bottom of the page and the drop-capital lost ("URING the period...", "T must have been soon after..."); footnote numerals are frequently garbled ("J2", "4»", "i960"); the Third Captain of Riccio is labelled "Pl. 13c", almost certainly a misread of 13e; Yates's own ordering (Seal 12 before 9, Seal 26 before 25) is preserved, and she does not enumerate all thirty Seals. Part 4: every chapter opening (pp. 308, 320, 342, 368) is garbled by the drop-capital layout; on pp. 330–333 "the/there/theatre" is rendered "die/diere/dieatre"; on pp. 354–358 "frons scaenae" is "from scaenae"; Fludd's Latin in footnote 28 (p. 332) is noisy; the captions for Pls. 13–14 (Riccio, Seals, *Figuratio*, *Images*) are physically inserted between pp. 320 and 321; Willis "formed sets" (p. 336) is probably "formed of sets"; the lettering on the central circle of Pl. 14d is unexplained in the text and inaccurately reproduced in the nineteenth-century edition. Quotations in this catalogue are the readers' transcriptions with OCR slips silently corrected only where they noted it.
