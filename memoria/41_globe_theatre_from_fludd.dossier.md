# The Globe Theatre reconstructed from Fludd's *Theatrum Orbi*: Research Dossier

## Basic identification

**Name:** The Globe Theatre reconstructed from Robert Fludd’s *Theatrum Orbi*  
**Catalogue position:** System 41 of 45  
**Exponent:** Frances A. Yates’s 1966 reconstruction, developed from Richard Bernheimer’s 1958 identification  
**Date:** Fludd’s engraving and mnemonic treatise, 1619; second Globe, 1614; Bernheimer’s article, 1958; Yates’s reconstruction, 1966  
**Primary source:** Robert Fludd, *Utriusque cosmi maioris scilicet et minoris metaphysica, physica atque technica historia*, Tomus II, Tractatus I, Sectio II, Portio III, *De animae memorativae scientia, quae vulgo Ars memoriae vocatur*, especially Liber I, chapters V–X, pp. 48–55, and Liber II, pp. 63–69; Oppenheim, Johann Theodor de Bry/Hieronymus Galler, 1619.  
**Principal image:** the perspective engraving inscribed **THEATRVM ORBI**, p. 55; Yates, Plate 17.  
**Principle in one sentence:** Fludd coordinates celestial loci—the zodiac and seven planetary spheres—with architecturally ordered “theatres” containing five doors and five corresponding columns; Yates conjectured that the engraved theatre preserved, beneath mnemonic alterations, the stage wall of Shakespeare’s second Globe.

**Yates:** *The Art of Memory*, chapter XV, pp. 320–341, for Fludd’s system; chapter XVI, pp. 342–367, for the Globe hypothesis; Plates 15–20 and Figure 11. The relevant chapter divisions are: Bernheimer, pp. 345–346; engraving, pp. 346–349; entrances, chamber, and terrace, pp. 349–352; subsidiary theatres, pp. 353–354; column bases and ground plan, pp. 354–359; Dee and Vitruvius, pp. 360–363; orientation and levels, pp. 364–365; concealment and Camillo, pp. 366–367.

### Evidentiary verdict

**Documented:** Fludd describes celestial theatres, five doors, five corresponding columns, differentiated shapes and colours, theatrical actions used as images, and a combined *ars rotunda* and *ars quadrata*.

**Documented:** The printed engraving exists, is headed *THEATRVM ORBI*, and depicts a structure resembling an early modern stage or ceremonial screen: three lower openings, two upper openings, battlements, a projecting central bay, side walls, and five column bases.

**Documented:** A preparatory manuscript image survives in Frankfurt, Universitätsbibliothek Johann Christian Senckenberg, MS lat. qu. 15, fol. 67r. This materially strengthens the attribution of the design to Fludd’s publication programme, but not its identification with the Globe.

**Scholarly reconstruction:** The image draws upon theatre architecture and theatrical performance as a model for memory.

**Disputed and now generally rejected:** That the engraving records the second Globe specifically.

**Unsupported:** That its five doors, bay window, terrace, orientation, posts, “heavens,” or geometrical bases are reliable archaeological evidence for the Globe.

**Modern invention:** Yates’s Plate 20 and Figure 11 are twentieth-century conjectural transformations of Fludd’s plate, not reproductions of an early modern document.

## The system in detail

### 1. Its place in Fludd’s encyclopedia

Fludd’s encyclopedia is organized around the traditional correspondence of macrocosm and microcosm. The first great division treats the larger world; the second treats humanity as the lesser world. The mnemonic art belongs to the “technical history” of the microcosm because memory is one of the arts by which humanity imitates, contains, and reorganizes the larger creation.

At the head of the technical section, the human microcosm stands beneath a triangular divine glory and above an ape, Fludd’s emblem of *ars* as the “ape of nature.” Around him appear Prophecy, Geomancy, Memory, Genethliology, Physiognomy, Chiromancy, and the “Science of Pyramids.” Memory is represented by five loci containing images.

The treatise begins on p. 48:

> *De animae memorativae scientia, quae vulgo Ars memoriae vocatur.*

“On the science of the remembering soul, commonly called the Art of Memory.”

**Documented text:** Fludd says that he first became interested in memory at Nîmes, improved his knowledge at Avignon, and taught it at Marseille to Charles de Lorraine, fourth Duke of Guise, and the duke’s brother. This belongs to his travels around 1601–1602. It shows that Fludd practised and transmitted mnemonic techniques before the publication of his encyclopedia.

**Yates’s inference:** France may have transmitted knowledge of Camillo or Bruno to Fludd.

**Unproved:** No surviving document identifies Fludd’s French mnemonic teacher, proves that he had seen Camillo’s physical theatre, or shows direct instruction from a Brunian practitioner.

### 2. Natural and artificial memory

Fludd begins from the traditional division between natural memory and artificial memory. Artificial improvement may occur through:

1. physical or medicinal assistance;
2. the operations of fantasy upon incorporeal “ideas”;
3. the arrangement of corporeal images in places.

The last two become his “round” and “square” arts.

This is continuous with the classical distinction between *loci* and *imagines*, but Fludd embeds it in a psychology of imagination and a macrocosm–microcosm cosmology.

### 3. *Ars rotunda*

Fludd defines the common place of the round art:

> *Locus communis artis rotundae est pars mundi aetherea, scilicet orbes coelestes, numerando ab octava sphaera et finiendo in sphaera Lunae.*

“The common place of the round art is the ethereal part of the world, namely the celestial circles, counting from the eighth sphere and ending with the sphere of the Moon.”  
—Fludd, *Ars memoriae*, p. 54.

Its places are therefore the eighth sphere or zodiac and the seven planetary spheres down to the Moon.

Its proper images are incorporeal or quasi-incorporeal forms:

> spirits, shadows, souls, angels, demons, stellar effigies, gods and goddesses endowed with celestial powers, virtues and vices imagined as shadows.

Fludd expressly distinguishes these “ideas” from Platonic Ideas in the divine mind. He means comparatively simple or spiritual forms apprehended by imagination rather than bodies composed of the four elements.

**Documented:** Fludd associates this part of the art with celestial powers and says that its practice involves the assistance of *daemones* or the metaphysical operation of the Holy Spirit, with fantasy concurring in the act.

**Yates’s interpretation:** These are “magicised or talismanic images,” and the art seeks to activate memory through astral power.

**Necessary qualification:** Early modern *daemon* could signify an intermediary spirit or power and does not automatically mean a Christian devil. Fludd contrasts this method with a reportedly darker art involving the “ring of Solomon,” encountered through rumours at Toulouse.

### 4. *Ars quadrata*

The square art uses corporeal things:

- human figures;
- animals;
- manufactured or natural objects;
- active scenes;
- rooms, buildings, walls, and architectural subdivisions.

Fludd states that ordinary people find a square chamber easier to impress upon the mind than purely contemplative ideas:

> *conclavis aut cubiculi quadrati species facilius imprimuntur hominum literarum ignarorum conceptui, quam ideae sola contemplatione consideratae.*

“The forms of an apartment or square room are more easily impressed on the conception of people unversed in letters than ideas considered through contemplation alone.”  
—Fludd, p. 51.

The square art is easier but, in Fludd’s hierarchy, inferior to the round art. It is artificial because its places are human constructions.

### 5. The rule of real places

Fludd’s much-discussed rule is that the architectural places of the square art should be based on buildings actually known to the practitioner. Invented palaces multiply the work of memory and weaken the image.

His optical analogy is:

> *quo propinquius et magis immediate procedit imaginationis similitudo a realitate sensus, eo sit in hac arte certior, validior, durabilior et efficacior.*

“The nearer and more immediately the likeness in imagination proceeds from sensory reality, the surer, stronger, more durable, and more effective it is in this art.”  
—Fludd, p. 52.

He compares repeated mental mediation to an image successively reflected through mirrors: at every remove it loses clarity.

**Documented rule:** Use a real house, castle, palace, room, or other place personally and securely known.

**Yates’s crucial inference:** Because Fludd requires real places, the engraved theatre must reproduce a particular real theatre.

**Correction:** The text does not make that inference. As Koji Kuwakino stresses, the real-building rule is introduced for the sublunary *ars quadrata*. The *Theatrum Orbi* illustrates the celestial theatres of the *ars rotunda*, whose locus is expressly the ethereal world. Moreover, an international Latin book could not assume that its dispersed readers personally knew one London playhouse. A generic, adaptable theatre-model fits Fludd’s instructions better than a concealed portrait of the Globe.

### 6. Hierarchy of places

Recent scholarship reconstructs the system as nested rather than as a single freestanding “memory theatre”:

- *ars rotunda*: celestial spheres → zodiacal sectors → eastern and western theatres;
- *ars quadrata*: known building → rooms → walls, floor, and ceiling → smaller “theatres” or fields;
- within each theatre: doors, columns, and subsidiary places;
- within those places: active persons, objects, letters, numbers, names, or scenes.

This hierarchical compression resembles the systems of Cosma Rosselli and later John Willis more closely than Yates’s picture of one occult building.

### 7. Eastern and western theatres

Fludd imagines two theatres in relation to each zodiacal sign:

- an eastern, white, bright, shining theatre for day;
- a western, black, obscure theatre for night.

The zodiac diagram on p. 54 shows the eighth sphere with twelve signs, seven inner planetary circles, the elemental sphere at the centre, and two small stage-like structures beside Aries.

Fludd uses the first-person future *imaginabimur*: “we shall imagine.” The diagram supplies the ordered celestial framework.

**Probable reconstruction:** Two theatres multiplied by twelve signs yield twenty-four celestial theatres.

**Not completely explicit:** Fludd does not provide a single fully worked table enumerating theatres 1–24. Yates’s “twenty-four identical theatres” is a plausible reconstruction from the text and diagram, not a phrase to be quoted as Fludd’s own exhaustive specification.

### 8. The five doors and five columns

Fludd says:

> *Quodlibet theatrum quinque portas inter se distinctas et quasi aequidistantes habebit, quarum usum postea explicabimus.*

“Each theatre will have five doors distinct from one another and approximately equidistant; their use we shall explain later.”  
—Fludd, p. 55; spelling and punctuation normalized.

The corresponding columns are described at p. 63:

> *His pratis oppositae fingantur quinque columnae, quae itidem debent figura et colore distingui; figura enim duarum extremarum erit circularis et rotunda, media autem columna habebit figuram hexagoneam, et quae his intermedia sunt quadratam possidebunt figuram.*

“Opposite these fields let five columns be imagined, which likewise ought to be distinguished by shape and colour: the shape of the two outermost will be circular and round; the middle column will have a hexagonal shape; and those between them will possess a square shape.”

The order is therefore:

1. round;
2. square;
3. hexagonal;
4. square;
5. round.

The doors and corresponding columns are coloured:

1. white;
2. red;
3. green;
4. blue;
5. black.

These ten loci are distinguished by position, geometry, and colour.

**Documented:** Five doors and five opposing columns serve as ordered mnemonic loci.

**Documented:** Shape and colour prevent confusion among similar places.

**Yates’s inference:** The three lower and two upper openings shown in the plate constitute the Globe’s actual five entrances and reproduce a Vitruvian five-door *frons scaenae* on two levels.

**Problem:** Fludd’s text requires five mnemonic doors but does not say that they are evidence for the Globe, that they derive from Vitruvius’s five stage openings, or that the depicted upper apertures were actor entrances in a London theatre.

### 9. Images and actions

Fludd criticizes empty loci. An architectural place should contain a differentiating inhabitant, figure, picture, or action even before temporary material is attached. The image must be vivid and unambiguous.

His examples include mythological figures and dramatic actions, among them Medea:

- Medea killing her brother;
- Medea gathering magical herbs.

The remembered material—words, names, sentences, things, virtues, vices, mythological persons—may be attached to a column, nailed to a door, carried by an actor, or incorporated into the scene.

Fludd also supplies:

- visual alphabets in which human bodies or objects resemble letters;
- number-images;
- alphabetical lists of persons and things;
- mythological actors;
- virtues and vices;
- occupational and historical figures.

These provide a stock repertoire from which the practitioner constructs *imagines agentes*.

### 10. Fixing order and retrieval

The order is fixed by several simultaneous systems:

1. the astronomical order of zodiac and planets;
2. east before west, day before night;
3. the fixed sequence of doors and opposing columns;
4. left-to-right or otherwise rehearsed movement through the theatre;
5. stable colours;
6. stable geometrical shapes;
7. alphabetical or numerical image lists.

Encoding proceeds by translating the subject into an active visual episode and placing it at a known locus. Retrieval reverses the process: the practitioner mentally enters the appropriate sphere and theatre, traverses its doors and columns in the learned order, sees the deposited action, and translates the action back into words or things.

**Documented principle:** Stable places preserve order; striking images carry content.

**Scholarly reconstruction:** Fludd never supplies a modern algorithmic walk-through of an entire twenty-four-theatre store. Detailed retrieval must be inferred from his examples and the inherited rhetorical art.

## The sources

### 1. Primary printed text

Full bibliographical form:

Robert Fludd, *Utriusque cosmi maioris scilicet et minoris metaphysica, physica atque technica historia, in duo volumina secundum cosmi differentiam divisa*, Tomus secundus, *De supernaturali, naturali, praeternaturali et contranaturali microcosmi historia, in tractatus tres distributa*, Oppenheim: sumptibus Johann Theodor de Bry, typis Hieronymus Galler, 1619.

The memory treatise is:

*De animae memorativae scientia, quae vulgo Ars memoriae vocatur*, Tomus II, Tractatus I, Sectio II, Portio III.

Important pages:

- p. 48: title and autobiographical account;
- pp. 50–51: round and square arts;
- pp. 51–53: real and fictitious places;
- p. 54: zodiacal scheme;
- p. 55: eastern and western theatres and *THEATRVM ORBI*;
- pp. 57–58: architectural subdivisions and subsidiary theatre;
- p. 63: five columns, their shapes and colours;
- pp. 64–69: theatrical actions and image repertories.

The relevant section is sometimes catalogued without a separate dated title page. The encyclopedia was issued in parts between 1617 and the 1620s; consequently library catalogues sometimes give 1617–1621 or 1617–1638 for collected sets. The memory section itself belongs to the 1619 microcosm printing.

### 2. Preparatory manuscript

**Documented, newly important evidence:** Frankfurt am Main, Universitätsbibliothek Johann Christian Senckenberg, **MS lat. qu. 15**, the illustrated printer’s copy of *De technica microcosmi historia*.

Relevant folios:

- fol. 66r: preparatory zodiac drawing corresponding to printed p. 54;
- fol. 67r: preparatory *Theatrum Orbi[s]* corresponding to printed p. 55.

Ute Frietsch has established that this was a master copy used in preparing the printed section. It contains pen-and-ink drawings, pasted materials, authorial or workshop corrections, and German production instructions.

The preparatory theatre is attributed cautiously to “Robert Fludd in collaboration with Johann Theodor de Bry and workshop (?)”; the final etching is attributed cautiously to Matthäus Merian the Elder or the de Bry workshop.

**Consequence:** Yates wondered how Fludd communicated a London drawing to German engravers and conjectured an intermediary such as Michael Maier. The Frankfurt manuscript replaces much of that speculation with physical evidence: an illustrated printer’s copy crossed the author–publisher–workshop chain.

**What it does not establish:** It does not identify the represented building as the Globe. On the contrary, the presence of the image already within the designed manuscript programme strengthens its status as a constructed mnemonic diagram.

### 3. The inscription

The engraving reads:

> **THEATRVM ORBI**

Grammatically, “theatre of the world” would normally be *theatrum orbis*.

Yates proposed that *orbi* was an intentional dative: “a theatre for the world,” meaning a theatre placed within or assigned to the celestial world.

Later interpretations include:

- *orbi* as a simple grammatical or workshop error;
- an abbreviated or damaged *orbis*;
- *theatre for the sphere*, referring to the preceding *orbes coelestes*;
- a possible incomplete *orbi[s terrarum]*;
- an echo of the commonplace *theatrum mundi*.

Frietsch observes that the anomalous wording already appears in the manuscript and was carried into the print. That rules out an independent typesetter’s or final engraver’s innovation, but does not decide whether the anomaly was intentional.

**Disputed:** Yates’s dative interpretation remains possible but is unnecessary.

**No evidence:** Nothing in the wording secretly spells “Globe Theatre.”

### 4. Description of the p. 55 plate

The image is a one-point perspective interior or stage-like enclosure. It contains:

- a rear wall occupying the width of the composition;
- three openings at lower level;
- a broad central lower opening with paired leaves shown partly open;
- two smaller flanking lower doors;
- an upper gallery or terrace;
- two upper doorways;
- a crenellated parapet;
- a central projecting bay or oriel-like structure;
- the inscription *THEATRVM ORBI* on that projection;
- lateral walls returning toward the viewer;
- low box-like apertures or compartments in those walls;
- a ruled or tiled floor;
- five column bases advancing across the foreground;
- geometric forms associated with the ordered mnemonic stations.

The plate does **not** depict:

- an entire circular playhouse;
- an exterior;
- a visible audience;
- two structural stage posts;
- a projecting apron stage;
- a third gallery level;
- a roof or painted “heavens”;
- a named London location;
- actors performing Shakespeare.

Those elements enter Yates’s reconstructed Plate 20, not Fludd’s surviving image.

### 5. Other comparative primary sources

The most relevant are:

- Vitruvius, *De architectura* V.6–9, on theatre planning, orientation, acoustics, and the *scaenae frons*;
- Daniele Barbaro’s Italian and Latin Vitruvius editions, Venice, 1556 and 1567, illustrated by Andrea Palladio;
- John Dee’s “Mathematicall Praeface” to Henry Billingsley’s English Euclid, London, 1570;
- John Willis, *Mnemonica; sive Ars reminiscendi*, London, 1618;
- Willis, *The Art of Memory*, London, 1621;
- Willis, *Mnemonica: or The Art of Memory*, London, 1661;
- the contract for Philip Henslowe and Edward Alleyn’s Fortune playhouse, 1600;
- Arend van Buchell’s copy of Johannes de Witt’s sketch of the Swan theatre, Utrecht University Library, MS 842, fol. 132r;
- Cosma Rosselli, *Thesaurus artificiosae memoriae*, Venice, 1579;
- Giulio Camillo, *L’idea del theatro*, Florence and Venice, 1550;
- Giordano Bruno, *De umbris idearum*, Paris, 1582; *Ars reminiscendi* and *Explicatio triginta sigillorum*, London, 1583; *De imaginum, signorum et idearum compositione*, Frankfurt, 1591.

### 6. Editions and translations

There is no universally adopted modern critical edition of Fludd’s complete *Ars memoriae* comparable to the critical editions available for Bruno or Ramon Llull. Modern discussions commonly cite the 1619 folio directly.

Frances Yates translates substantial passages in chapters XV–XVI, but her renderings occur inside an interpretative argument and are not a complete edition.

Koji Kuwakino’s 2024 study provides a fresh structural reading of the Latin treatise. Ute Frietsch reproduces and compares manuscript and printed states.

## Yates’s account and the scholarship since

### 1. Bernheimer, 1958

Richard Bernheimer’s “Another Globe Theatre,” *Shakespeare Quarterly* 9.1 (1958), pp. 19–29, brought Fludd’s plate into modern Globe reconstruction.

Bernheimer wrote that Shakespeareans would recognize:

> “a lower and an upper stage, two entrance doors flanking an inner stage, battlements fitted for scenes of siege, and a bay window” suitable for Juliet.

**Status:** This is architectural identification by resemblance. Bernheimer did not possess documentary proof connecting Fludd, the engraver, or the plate with the Globe.

Bernheimer also treated the engraving’s relationship to the “round” art inadequately. Yates’s major correction was to show that “round” referred to the celestial component of the mnemonic system, not necessarily to the visible geometry of the pictured room.

### 2. Yates’s reconstruction

Yates’s three central statements are:

> “The engraving represents that part of the stage of the Globe which would be covered by the stage ‘heavens.’”  
> —p. 347.

> “My explanation of these features is that they are distortions of the real stage introduced for mnemonic purposes.”  
> —p. 348.

> “Encouraged by Mrs. Thrale, I believe that Fludd is stating through the shapes of the five column bases the geometrical forms used in the construction of the Globe, namely the hexagon, the circle, and the square.”  
> —p. 355.

Her reasoning proceeds as follows:

1. Fludd insists on real rather than fictitious places.
2. He compares his theatre with a public theatre where comedies and tragedies are acted.
3. His plate therefore embodies a real public stage.
4. The Globe was the best-known English public theatre and its name meant “world.”
5. Fludd’s first volume was dedicated to James I, patron of the King’s Men.
6. The plate’s lower central opening, upper bay, battlements, and five doors answer disputed needs in Shakespearean staging.
7. The side walls and five columns would obstruct spectators and therefore must be mnemonic distortions.
8. Remove the side walls and three of the five columns.
9. Substitute two load-bearing posts copied from Fludd’s “Temple of Music.”
10. Extend the floor into an apron stage.
11. Add a zodiacal “heavens” above.
12. Read the round, square, and hexagonal bases as a key to the Globe’s plan.
13. Generate a plan with Vitruvian triangles and dimensions borrowed from the Fortune contract.
14. Orient the stage from the word *Oriens* on the zodiac scheme.
15. Interpret subsidiary theatres as movable scenic screens or “mansions.”

This creates Yates’s Plate 20 and Figure 11.

### 3. Immediate criticism: Brownlow

F. W. Brownlow replied in the *New York Review of Books* in July 1966. His central objection was methodological: Yates could obtain the Globe only by radically changing the evidence.

She had to:

- remove the side walls;
- remove three columns;
- reconstruct the seating;
- enlarge or transform the central opening;
- turn the bay window into an acting chamber;
- add stage posts and heavens;
- extend the stage.

The result resembled a generic Elizabethan stage precisely because the distinctive evidence had been selectively removed or altered.

Brownlow concluded that the plate might preserve a modified screen-wall of a kind common in early modern halls, pageants, and stages, but that its specificity to the Globe rested on faith.

This remains the strongest concise objection to the reconstruction.

### 4. Immediate criticism: Wickham

Glynne Wickham argued in November 1966 that Yates had moved without adequate evidence from an unlocalized image to one particular London playhouse among many.

He noted that the plate’s rectangular enclosure might fit:

- the square Fortune;
- the Red Bull;
- the rectangular indoor Blackfriars;
- or no commercial playhouse at all.

His positive alternative was a royal tiltyard or Court of Chivalry setting, perhaps Whitehall or Greenwich. The large central gates, smaller flanking doors, crenellation, gallery, royal viewing window, and temporary canvas architecture could suit courtly combats and siege entertainments.

**Status:** Wickham’s tiltyard identification is itself a conjecture. It refutes exclusivity more successfully than it establishes a new provenance.

Yates replied that Wickham ignored the mnemonic context: the image is certainly a “theatre” within a theatre-memory system. On that narrow point she was right. But “theatre mnemonic” does not entail “Globe portrait.”

### 5. Yates’s later development

In *Theatre of the World* (1969), Yates expanded the argument connecting:

- Vitruvius;
- John Dee;
- Elizabethan theatre architecture;
- Fludd;
- Inigo Jones;
- Hermetic and Rosicrucian currents.

She continued to treat Fludd’s image as evidence for the Globe while acknowledging the provisional character of her theatre-historical reconstruction.

Her own closing words in *The Art of Memory* deserve emphasis. She called the chapter a “first attempt,” expected criticism by specialists, and requested more research on the German production of the engraving and on Vitruvian influence.

### 6. Modern manuscript research: Frietsch

Ute Frietsch’s work in 2022–2024 substantially changes the publication history.

Confirmed:

- Fludd actively designed and organized visual material.
- MS lat. qu. 15 was the illustrated master copy for the printed *De technica microcosmi historia*.
- The theatre and zodiac diagrams existed in manuscript before final engraving.
- De Bry’s workshop and probably Matthäus Merian transformed supplied visual designs into polished prints.

Corrected:

- One need not posit a lost sketch of the Globe independently copied by a German engraver.
- Yates’s Michael Maier courier hypothesis is possible in general but no longer necessary to explain the plates.
- The imagery belongs to a coherent authorial–editorial programme.

Frietsch explicitly rejects the Globe identification as overly “concretistic.” She distinguishes two round celestial theatres and one square mnemonic theatre rather than treating the system as a single Globe-derived room.

### 7. Kuwakino, 2024

Koji Kuwakino reconstructs Fludd’s places as a hierarchical art of architectural visualization and *ekphrasis*.

His decisive correction is that Fludd’s rule about personally known, real buildings applies to the *ars quadrata*. The p. 55 theatre belongs to the celestial scheme discussed on p. 54 and is initially an abstract model. Its fixed features organize memory, while readers may adapt others.

Kuwakino also observes that the international Latin readership at Oppenheim makes a uniquely London-specific model unsuitable as a common locus: most readers could not have known the Globe directly.

This is the clearest text-based refutation of Yates’s foundational premise.

### 8. Carruthers, Bolzoni, Rossi, Small, Ong, and others

These scholars are indispensable for the larger history of memory but do not all endorse or directly adjudicate the Fludd–Globe identification.

- **Mary Carruthers** shifted attention from “photographic storage” to medieval memory as composition, invention, ethical formation, and trained recollection. This supports reading Fludd’s architecture as a generative mental instrument rather than a literal building plan.
- **Lina Bolzoni** demonstrated how memory theatres, galleries, emblematic images, preaching, literature, and collection practices interacted. Her work cautions against isolating a diagram from its textual and performative uses.
- **Paolo Rossi** traced the passage from mnemonic arts through Lullism and combinatorics to projects for universal knowledge and language. This contextualizes Fludd’s system without requiring a Globe cipher.
- **Jocelyn Penny Small** emphasized ancient cognitive and material practices and the historical distance between Greek/Roman memory theory and Renaissance reconstructions.
- **Walter J. Ong** showed how Ramist spatial diagrams replaced or reconfigured imagistic loci through dichotomous order. Fludd represents a rival visual-cosmological tradition, not Ramist method.
- **Alfredo Bonner and Alexander Fidora** have corrected the once-common treatment of Llull’s Art as principally a mnemonic wheel-system. Llull’s figures are logical-combinatorial instruments for demonstrating relations among divine dignities, not simply storage wheels.
- **Rita Sturlese, Michele Ciliberto, Stephen Clucas, and Hilary Gatti** have complicated the Yatesian picture of Bruno as primarily a Hermetic magician. Bruno’s mnemonic works involve logic, ontology, cognition, rhetoric, atomism, and combinatorial invention as well as magic.
- **Bolzoni and Stagno** have sharpened study of Camillo through textual, rhetorical, visual, and collecting contexts, reducing reliance on the romantic picture of a completed occult machine.
- **William Engel, Rory Loughnane, and Grant Williams** document the breadth of memory practice in Renaissance England without making the Globe identification a settled fact.

The collective post-Yates correction is methodological: cosmological imagery cannot be reduced to magical talismanry, mnemonic architecture cannot automatically be treated as architectural documentation, and resemblance is not provenance.

## History and context

### 1. Classical rhetoric

Fludd’s immediate technical ancestry is the classical artificial memory preserved in:

- pseudo-Cicero, *Rhetorica ad Herennium* III.16–24;
- Cicero, *De oratore* II.350–360;
- Quintilian, *Institutio oratoria* XI.2.

The classical method establishes an ordered series of places and deposits distinctive images in them. Recollection is a mental traversal.

The *Ad Herennium*’s famous lawsuit image uses a sick man in bed, the accused beside him holding a cup, tablets, and a ram’s testicles: cup for poisoning, tablets for the will, testicles for witnesses. It exemplifies violent, concrete, punning *imagines agentes*.

That example is an ancestor of Fludd’s theatrical Medea-scenes, but it does not occur in Fludd’s *Theatrum Orbi* section.

### 2. Scholastic psychology and preaching

Medieval writers integrated artificial memory into ethics, preaching, confession, and prudence. Thomas Aquinas treated memory as a component of prudence; Albertus Magnus discussed vivid corporeal similitudes. Dominican and Franciscan educational environments used ordered divisions, images, exempla, and affective visualization.

Fludd retains:

- faculty psychology;
- the inward image;
- virtues and vices;
- Hell imagery;
- moral and religious recollection.

His opening diagram includes Tobias and the Angel and the Last Judgment with the damned entering Hell’s mouth.

### 3. Renaissance theatres and galleries

Giulio Camillo’s theatre arranged universal knowledge through seven grades, seven planetary structures, and mythological images in a reversed Vitruvian theatre: the spectator stood on the stage and faced the knowledge disposed through the auditorium.

Fludd reverses that orientation. His mnemonic operator faces the stage-like wall.

Yates’s elegant contrast—Camillo looking from stage to auditorium, Fludd standing with his back to the auditorium—is interpretatively useful, but it does not prove the physical existence of either reconstructed arrangement in the form she imagines.

### 4. Lullism and combinatorics

Fludd’s round form superficially resembles Lullian wheels, but Yates herself observed that this memory system does not conspicuously employ Lullian letter-combination. Llull appears in Fludd’s image lists as a representative of alchemy, not necessarily as the system’s operative source.

The deeper affinity lies in the ambition to order total knowledge. From Llull through Renaissance Lullism, Kircher, and Leibniz came projects for combinatorial discovery and universal language. Fludd belongs to that encyclopedic environment, but his zodiacal theatres are not a direct step-by-step ancestor of digital computers.

### 5. Hermeticism and macrocosm–microcosm

Fludd accepted a Christianized Hermetic cosmology:

- elemental world;
- celestial world;
- supercelestial or angelic world;
- humanity as microcosm;
- divine order reflected downward and recollected upward.

Yates interpreted the combined memory system as an attempt to reconstitute the cosmic order within the practitioner. That broad interpretation remains plausible.

Her stronger language—astral activation, talismanic images, a “memory Seal” comparable with Bruno—must be marked as interpretation. Fludd’s text supports spiritual and celestial efficacy, but not every diagram is thereby a magical talisman.

### 6. Ramism

Ramist method arranged knowledge through visible dichotomies and definitions. It offered a disciplined alternative to crowded image-loci. Protestant critics could treat spectacular artificial memory as morally dangerous, deceitful, or “impious,” especially where zodiacal images or spirits were involved.

Fludd’s richly pictorial, cosmological system stands near the opposite pole from the austere textbook Ramism studied by Ong.

### 7. Public theatre and court spectacle

Fludd explicitly analogizes memory with a public theatre:

> a theatre is that in which actions of words, sentences, parts of speech, or subjects are demonstrated as in a public theatre where comedies and tragedies are acted.

This proves conceptual dependence on performance.

It does not decide whether the architectural image derives from:

- the Globe;
- another public playhouse;
- an indoor theatre;
- a court tiltyard;
- a festival stage;
- a generic *scaenae frons*;
- mnemonic treatise conventions;
- or a composite made from several sources.

## People

### Robert Fludd

Physician, natural philosopher, alchemical experimenter, astrologer, diagram-maker, defender of Rosicrucian writings, and author of the system.

His autobiographical testimony documents mnemonic study and teaching in southern France. There is no evidence that he was an actor, Globe shareholder, or theatre architect.

### Johann Theodor de Bry

Publisher responsible for the lavish Oppenheim programme. The de Bry firm had long experience transforming drawings and travel reports into engravings.

### Hieronymus Galler

Printer of the Oppenheim volumes.

### Matthäus Merian the Elder

Probable engraver or etcher of many Fludd images, including perhaps the printed theatre. Attribution of individual plates should remain qualified where unsigned.

### Michael Maier

Rosicrucian and alchemical author associated with de Bry and England. Yates proposed him as a possible carrier of Fludd’s material to Oppenheim.

**Status:** plausible contact; not documented as the transmitter of the theatre drawing.

### Charles de Lorraine, Duke of Guise, and his brother

Fludd says he taught them mathematics and memory at Marseille.

**Documented practitioner anecdote:** This is the best evidence for actual instruction in Fludd’s art.

### James I

The first volume was dedicated to James. The King’s Men were under royal patronage.

**Yates’s interpretation:** The Globe allusion may have been intended to interest or flatter James.

**Against:** No dedication, letter, payment, court record, or contemporary testimony says that James received a concealed Globe diagram.

### John Dee

Yates proposed Dee’s 1570 Euclidean preface as a channel by which Vitruvius and architectural geometry reached English builders, including James Burbage.

**Disputed:** Dee promoted architecture, perspective, mechanics, and Vitruvius; no document shows him teaching Burbage or designing the Theatre or Globe.

### James Burbage and Peter Street

Burbage built the Theatre; Street dismantled its timbers and constructed the first Globe, then contracted to build the Fortune.

They are relevant to the material history of London playhouses. Neither is connected in surviving documents with Fludd’s mnemonic plate.

### Hester Thrale

Yates quoted Thrale’s eighteenth-century recollection that the Globe was “hexagonal without and round within.”

**Problem:** Thrale wrote long after the Globe had disappeared. Her statement is antiquarian tradition, not eyewitness evidence, and cannot securely decode Fludd’s five column bases.

### William Foster

Foster attacked Fludd and insinuated that his works were printed abroad because English universities and bishops would not permit magical books. Fludd replied that English printers had demanded £500, whereas overseas publication cost him nothing and was executed as he wished.

This exchange documents suspicion of Fludd’s works as magical, but also supplies an ordinary economic explanation for foreign publication.

### Mersenne and Kepler

Marin Mersenne attacked Fludd’s reliance on “Egyptian” or Hermetic teaching. Johannes Kepler criticized his hieroglyphic images and non-mathematical manipulation of number.

They were critics of Fludd’s epistemology and cosmology, not witnesses to a Globe cipher.

### The requested anecdotes

The “auctioneer,” “professor’s party,” “student who lost his memory,” and “accused magician” belong to other episodes and systems in the long history of memory arts. No primary or early secondary source located connects these anecdotes with Fludd’s *Theatrum Orbi* or with the Globe reconstruction.

**Absence of evidence:** They should not be imported into this dossier as Fludd anecdotes merely because they occur elsewhere in Yates or later mnemonic folklore.

## The magic question

### 1. Was the system magical?

The answer depends on the category being used.

**Documented:** Fludd’s round art employs stellar effigies, gods, goddesses, angels, *daemones*, virtues, vices, and the celestial spheres.

**Documented:** He says fantasy must concur in a metaphysical act and allows either spiritual intermediaries or the Holy Spirit as operative assistance.

**Documented:** He places memory beside geomancy, astrology, physiognomy, and chiromancy.

Thus the system is not merely a secular memory trick.

### 2. Was it talismanic?

**Yates:** Yes, or nearly so. She interprets its stellar images as “magicised or talismanic” and sees Fludd following Bruno’s astral memory.

**Qualification:** Fludd does not clearly instruct the reader, in this section, to manufacture or consecrate physical talismans. The operation occurs through imagination, celestial correspondences, and inward images. “Talismanic” is a defensible analytical comparison, not Fludd’s unequivocal procedural label.

### 3. Was it heretical?

Fludd presented his philosophy as Christian and defended a lawful, pious, natural or divine magic. Critics considered its Hermetic, Rosicrucian, spirit-invoking, and cosmological elements suspect.

William Foster’s accusation shows that “magical book” was a live polemical category. Mersenne’s attacks show that learned Catholic controversialists rejected Fludd’s Hermetic foundations.

**No documented consequence specific to the memory theatre:** No evidence located shows Fludd tried by the Inquisition, formally prosecuted for this mnemonic system, or punished for the *Theatrum Orbi*.

### 4. Rosicrucianism

Fludd defended the Rosicrucian manifestos and was widely regarded as a Rosicrucian philosopher.

**Documented:** His publications support Rosicrucian ideas.

**Disputed:** Membership in a concrete, organized Rosicrucian fraternity is unproved.

**Yates’s speculation:** The Globe may have been concealed within an elaborate mnemonic “casket” intelligible to initiates, perhaps including James I.

**Evidence:** None beyond thematic congruence, secrecy rhetoric, dedication, and wordplay on “Globe/world.”

### 5. *Ars notoria*

The *Ars notoria* promised accelerated or infused knowledge through prayers, sacred names, figures, and angelic assistance. Fludd distinguishes his art from a darker Solomonic practice associated with a ring.

Yates suggested that the Toulouse report might concern something like the *Ars notoria*.

**Status:** comparative conjecture. Fludd does not identify the reported art by that title.

## Afterlife

### 1. Theatre reconstruction

Bernheimer and Yates made the plate famous among Shakespeare scholars. Yates’s reconstruction influenced popular images of the Globe, experimental theatre designs, and the broader cultural association between Shakespeare’s theatre and Renaissance memory.

It did not become secure archaeological evidence. The modern Shakespeare’s Globe, opened in 1997, was based on a much larger body of evidence: excavation, contracts, Hollar, the Swan drawing, stage directions, carpentry practice, and comparative playhouse archaeology.

### 2. Modern theatre and installation art

Fludd’s theatre has been repeatedly reproduced in exhibitions, performance studies, architectural installations, and visual-art projects. Contemporary artists often treat it as:

- an archive of images;
- a stage for cultural memory;
- a model of mental architecture;
- an analogue of montage;
- a predecessor of Aby Warburg’s *Mnemosyne Atlas*.

These are productive receptions, not evidence about Fludd’s original use.

### 3. Literature

Dante’s mnemonic landscapes, emblem literature, moralized architecture, and Renaissance theatre supplied the wider tradition from which modern writers draw.

Borges’s “Funes the Memorious” explores the opposite of trained artificial memory: limitless particular recall without abstraction. It is an illuminating contrast, not a direct descendant of Fludd.

Umberto Eco’s labyrinthine libraries and semiotic encyclopedias knowingly evoke memory-palace, combinatorial, and universal-language traditions. Again, the relation is cultural and conceptual rather than a demonstrable line from p. 55.

### 4. Modern mnemonic practice

Contemporary “memory palace” training and memory-sport methods preserve the basic classical mechanism:

- fixed familiar route;
- ordered loci;
- exaggerated active images;
- deliberate retrieval traversal.

They ordinarily omit Fludd’s zodiac, metaphysical spirits, and macrocosm–microcosm theory.

Calling every modern memory palace a “Fludd theatre” is therefore a modern simplification.

### 5. Computing and combinatorics

The defensible genealogy is:

- ancient ordered loci and symbolic encoding;
- medieval systems of division and concordance;
- Llull’s combinatorial figures;
- Renaissance universal theatres and taxonomies;
- Ramist diagrams;
- seventeenth-century universal-language projects;
- Leibniz’s *characteristica universalis* and *calculus ratiocinator*;
- later formal logic and computing.

**Disputed popular claim:** “Llull invented the computer.”

Llull produced rule-governed combinatorial devices and influenced later formal projects. He did not build a programmable electronic or mechanical computer in the modern sense.

Fludd’s nested theatres resemble address spaces and structured information retrieval, but describing them as computer memory is analogy, not technological descent.

## Controversies and disputes

### 1. Is it the Globe?

**Yates/Bernheimer:** Yes, or very probably.

**Brownlow:** At most a generic modified stage or screen-wall.

**Wickham:** Perhaps a tiltyard or courtly combat theatre.

**Frietsch:** The Globe identification is an over-literal, rejected reading of a conceptual image.

**Kuwakino:** The plate belongs to celestial theatres and is an abstract mnemonic model; Fludd’s real-place rule does not support Yates’s conclusion.

**Present evidentiary judgment:** No provenance, label, eyewitness account, architectural measurement, stage direction, or textual reference ties it specifically to the Globe. The identification should be catalogued as a brilliant but unsupported twentieth-century hypothesis.

### 2. First or second Globe?

Yates specifies the second Globe because it was rebuilt in 1614 and the plate appeared in 1619.

That chronology only makes the identification possible. It does not prove it. Nor is it certain that the second Globe exactly reproduced the first internally.

### 3. Five doors

The five doors are textually secure mnemonic loci.

Their conversion into five real Globe entrances depends on circular reasoning: they are treated as real because the image is assumed to show the Globe, while the image is identified as the Globe because it shows the required entrances.

### 4. The “inner stage”

Yates interpreted the partly open central doors as evidence for a large inner stage and the bay above as an upper acting chamber.

These are possible theatrical uses of such architecture. But the image was made for a memory treatise, and no evidence establishes that the openings reproduce functional Globe spaces.

### 5. Battlements and Juliet’s window

Bernheimer’s attractive associations—siege scenes and Juliet at the window—are recognitions based on Shakespearean stage needs.

They are not annotations supplied by Fludd. The same architectural motifs occur in court festivals, pageants, halls, temporary scenery, and generic theatre imagery.

### 6. The subsidiary theatres

Yates interpreted Fludd’s smaller stage illustrations as movable screens used at the Globe to mark places such as the Capulet orchard or Friar Laurence’s cell.

**Documented:** Fludd has subsidiary mnemonic theatres.

**Possible theatrical analogy:** Early modern performance used temporary properties and localizing structures.

**Unsupported:** No source identifies Fludd’s subsidiary images as Globe scenery.

### 7. Geometry and the column bases

Yates read round–square–hexagon–square–round as a concealed construction key for a Globe that was polygonal outside and circular inside.

The text supplies a direct mnemonic explanation: unlike shapes prevent loci from being confused.

The mnemonic explanation is therefore prior and sufficient. An additional architectural cipher is possible only as speculation.

### 8. The Fortune dimensions

The 1600 Fortune contract specifies a square building eighty feet externally, a fifty-five-foot yard, a forty-three-foot stage, galleries, stage cover, and construction “with such-like stairs, conveyances and divisions” as the Globe.

Yates used the forty-three-foot stage and an inferred eighty-six-foot Globe diameter to construct her plan.

**Problem:** The Fortune was expressly square; similarity clauses do not authorize simple conversion of all its dimensions into a circular Globe. Later archaeology and reconstruction scholarship treat the contract as essential but potentially misleading when mechanically transferred.

### 9. Four Vitruvian triangles

Yates generated the Globe plan from four intersecting equilateral triangles in a circle, connecting the result with zodiacal *trigona*, Barbaro, and Palladio.

**Status:** modern geometrical reconstruction. Neither the Fortune contract nor Fludd’s plate supplies this plan.

### 10. Orientation

Yates read *Oriens* at the top of Fludd’s celestial diagram as evidence that the Globe stage lay at the east, “like the altar in a church.”

**Problem:** *Oriens* belongs naturally to an astronomical diagram of rising and setting. Transferring its orientation to the physical Globe assumes the conclusion.

### 11. Dee as theatre architect’s intellectual source

Dee’s preface documents the English availability of Vitruvian architectural mathematics and practical geometry.

It does not document contact with Burbage or Street. The proposed chain Dee → Burbage → Globe → Fludd remains a scholarly reconstruction.

### 12. Camillo’s influence

There is a strong typological relationship:

- theatre as universal ordering structure;
- planetary organization;
- mythological images;
- cosmological ascent;
- memory as totalizing knowledge.

Direct textual borrowing by Fludd from *L’idea del theatro* has not been demonstrated at every point. The possibility of mediation through French, Italian, Brunian, or general mnemonic culture remains open.

### 13. Willis’s influence

Willis’s *Mnemonica* appeared in London in 1618, one year before Fludd’s printed section. Willis employs repeated “theatres” or repositories, colour differentiation, and practical active images.

A direct influence is chronologically possible. But Fludd had practised memory since his French travels, and the printer’s manuscript may predate final publication by an uncertain period.

**Verdict:** Close contemporary parallel; direct dependence unproved.

### 14. Was the engraving deliberately obscure?

Yates asked whether the twenty-four theatres constituted an “elaborate casket” concealing a Globe allusion from everyone except initiates.

Fludd’s system is genuinely difficult, and secrecy was culturally meaningful. Yet textual difficulty, compressed exposition, and composite visual design sufficiently explain much of the obscurity.

**No positive evidence:** There is no key, testimony, initiation record, or contemporary decipherment of a Globe secret.

### 15. Was the Globe itself a memory theatre?

Modern criticism sometimes connects Hamlet’s:

> “whiles memory holds a seat / In this distracted globe”

with theatre-memory language.

This is a suggestive literary interpretation. “Globe” can mean head, world, theatre, or the Globe playhouse, and “seat” can carry theatrical and mental resonances.

It does not prove that the Globe was designed as a mnemonic machine or that Shakespeare knew Fludd’s 1619 plate.

## Open questions

1. Can handwriting analysis more precisely distinguish Fludd’s hand from those of amanuenses and workshop personnel in MS lat. qu. 15?

2. Who executed the preparatory theatre drawing and who cut or etched the final plate?

3. What is the exact relation between manuscript and print in the inscription *THEATRVM ORBI*? Is material now illegible after *ORBI*?

4. Does the engraving combine multiple visual sources—public stage, court festival, tiltyard, mnemonic room, and classical *scaenae frons*?

5. Can a precise stemma be established between Rosselli, Camillo, Bruno, Willis, and Fludd’s nested architectural places?

6. When was MS lat. qu. 15 assembled relative to Willis’s 1618 *Mnemonica*?

7. Were Fludd’s eastern and western theatres intended as twenty-four discrete repositories, or as two repeatable templates successively attached to twelve zodiacal signs?

8. How exactly were planetary spheres coordinated with doors, columns, colours, alphabetic lists, and dramatic images during retrieval?

9. Does *orbi* mean “for the sphere,” “for the world,” an unfinished phrase, or simply erroneous Latin?

10. Did any seventeenth-century reader report actually using Fludd’s complete celestial system? None has yet been identified here.

11. Is there any documentary evidence that Fludd visited the Globe? None has been located.

12. Did James I ever see or comment upon the memory engraving? None has been located.

13. Can surviving copies reveal meaningful states or corrections in the theatre plate? A systematic census and plate comparison remains desirable.

# The Globe Theatre reconstructed from Fludd's *Theatrum Orbi*: Research Dossier

## Basic identification

**Name:** The Globe Theatre reconstructed from Robert Fludd’s *Theatrum Orbi*  
**Catalogue position:** System 41 of 45  
**Exponent:** Frances A. Yates’s 1966 reconstruction, developed from Richard Bernheimer’s 1958 identification  
**Date:** Fludd’s engraving and mnemonic treatise, 1619; second Globe, 1614; Bernheimer’s article, 1958; Yates’s reconstruction, 1966  
**Primary source:** Robert Fludd, *Utriusque cosmi maioris scilicet et minoris metaphysica, physica atque technica historia*, Tomus II, Tractatus I, Sectio II, Portio III, *De animae memorativae scientia, quae vulgo Ars memoriae vocatur*, especially Liber I, chapters V–X, pp. 48–55, and Liber II, pp. 63–69; Oppenheim, Johann Theodor de Bry/Hieronymus Galler, 1619.  
**Principal image:** the perspective engraving inscribed **THEATRVM ORBI**, p. 55; Yates, Plate 17.  
**Principle in one sentence:** Fludd coordinates celestial loci—the zodiac and seven planetary spheres—with architecturally ordered “theatres” containing five doors and five corresponding columns; Yates conjectured that the engraved theatre preserved, beneath mnemonic alterations, the stage wall of Shakespeare’s second Globe.

**Yates:** *The Art of Memory*, chapter XV, pp. 320–341, for Fludd’s system; chapter XVI, pp. 342–367, for the Globe hypothesis; Plates 15–20 and Figure 11. The relevant chapter divisions are: Bernheimer, pp. 345–346; engraving, pp. 346–349; entrances, chamber, and terrace, pp. 349–352; subsidiary theatres, pp. 353–354; column bases and ground plan, pp. 354–359; Dee and Vitruvius, pp. 360–363; orientation and levels, pp. 364–365; concealment and Camillo, pp. 366–367.

### Evidentiary verdict

**Documented:** Fludd describes celestial theatres, five doors, five corresponding columns, differentiated shapes and colours, theatrical actions used as images, and a combined *ars rotunda* and *ars quadrata*.

**Documented:** The printed engraving exists, is headed *THEATRVM ORBI*, and depicts a structure resembling an early modern stage or ceremonial screen: three lower openings, two upper openings, battlements, a projecting central bay, side walls, and five column bases.

**Documented:** A preparatory manuscript image survives in Frankfurt, Universitätsbibliothek Johann Christian Senckenberg, MS lat. qu. 15, fol. 67r. This materially strengthens the attribution of the design to Fludd’s publication programme, but not its identification with the Globe.

**Scholarly reconstruction:** The image draws upon theatre architecture and theatrical performance as a model for memory.

**Disputed and now generally rejected:** That the engraving records the second Globe specifically.

**Unsupported:** That its five doors, bay window, terrace, orientation, posts, “heavens,” or geometrical bases are reliable archaeological evidence for the Globe.

**Modern invention:** Yates’s Plate 20 and Figure 11 are twentieth-century conjectural transformations of Fludd’s plate, not reproductions of an early modern document.

## The system in detail

### 1. Its place in Fludd’s encyclopedia

Fludd’s encyclopedia is organized around the traditional correspondence of macrocosm and microcosm. The first great division treats the larger world; the second treats humanity as the lesser world. The mnemonic art belongs to the “technical history” of the microcosm because memory is one of the arts by which humanity imitates, contains, and reorganizes the larger creation.

At the head of the technical section, the human microcosm stands beneath a triangular divine glory and above an ape, Fludd’s emblem of *ars* as the “ape of nature.” Around him appear Prophecy, Geomancy, Memory, Genethliology, Physiognomy, Chiromancy, and the “Science of Pyramids.” Memory is represented by five loci containing images.

The treatise begins on p. 48:

> *De animae memorativae scientia, quae vulgo Ars memoriae vocatur.*

“On the science of the remembering soul, commonly called the Art of Memory.”

**Documented text:** Fludd says that he first became interested in memory at Nîmes, improved his knowledge at Avignon, and taught it at Marseille to Charles de Lorraine, fourth Duke of Guise, and the duke’s brother. This belongs to his travels around 1601–1602. It shows that Fludd practised and transmitted mnemonic techniques before the publication of his encyclopedia.

**Yates’s inference:** France may have transmitted knowledge of Camillo or Bruno to Fludd.

**Unproved:** No surviving document identifies Fludd’s French mnemonic teacher, proves that he had seen Camillo’s physical theatre, or shows direct instruction from a Brunian practitioner.

### 2. Natural and artificial memory

Fludd begins from the traditional division between natural memory and artificial memory. Artificial improvement may occur through:

1. physical or medicinal assistance;
2. the operations of fantasy upon incorporeal “ideas”;
3. the arrangement of corporeal images in places.

The last two become his “round” and “square” arts.

This is continuous with the classical distinction between *loci* and *imagines*, but Fludd embeds it in a psychology of imagination and a macrocosm–microcosm cosmology.

### 3. *Ars rotunda*

Fludd defines the common place of the round art:

> *Locus communis artis rotundae est pars mundi aetherea, scilicet orbes coelestes, numerando ab octava sphaera et finiendo in sphaera Lunae.*

“The common place of the round art is the ethereal part of the world, namely the celestial circles, counting from the eighth sphere and ending with the sphere of the Moon.”  
—Fludd, *Ars memoriae*, p. 54.

Its places are therefore the eighth sphere or zodiac and the seven planetary spheres down to the Moon.

Its proper images are incorporeal or quasi-incorporeal forms:

> spirits, shadows, souls, angels, demons, stellar effigies, gods and goddesses endowed with celestial powers, virtues and vices imagined as shadows.

Fludd expressly distinguishes these “ideas” from Platonic Ideas in the divine mind. He means comparatively simple or spiritual forms apprehended by imagination rather than bodies composed of the four elements.

**Documented:** Fludd associates this part of the art with celestial powers and says that its practice involves the assistance of *daemones* or the metaphysical operation of the Holy Spirit, with fantasy concurring in the act.

**Yates’s interpretation:** These are “magicised or talismanic images,” and the art seeks to activate memory through astral power.

**Necessary qualification:** Early modern *daemon* could signify an intermediary spirit or power and does not automatically mean a Christian devil. Fludd contrasts this method with a reportedly darker art involving the “ring of Solomon,” encountered through rumours at Toulouse.

### 4. *Ars quadrata*

The square art uses corporeal things:

- human figures;
- animals;
- manufactured or natural objects;
- active scenes;
- rooms, buildings, walls, and architectural subdivisions.

Fludd states that ordinary people find a square chamber easier to impress upon the mind than purely contemplative ideas:

> *conclavis aut cubiculi quadrati species facilius imprimuntur hominum literarum ignarorum conceptui, quam ideae sola contemplatione consideratae.*

“The forms of an apartment or square room are more easily impressed on the conception of people unversed in letters than ideas considered through contemplation alone.”  
—Fludd, p. 51.

The square art is easier but, in Fludd’s hierarchy, inferior to the round art. It is artificial because its places are human constructions.

### 5. The rule of real places

Fludd’s much-discussed rule is that the architectural places of the square art should be based on buildings actually known to the practitioner. Invented palaces multiply the work of memory and weaken the image.

His optical analogy is:

> *quo propinquius et magis immediate procedit imaginationis similitudo a realitate sensus, eo sit in hac arte certior, validior, durabilior et efficacior.*

“The nearer and more immediately the likeness in imagination proceeds from sensory reality, the surer, stronger, more durable, and more effective it is in this art.”  
—Fludd, p. 52.

He compares repeated mental mediation to an image successively reflected through mirrors: at every remove it loses clarity.

**Documented rule:** Use a real house, castle, palace, room, or other place personally and securely known.

**Yates’s crucial inference:** Because Fludd requires real places, the engraved theatre must reproduce a particular real theatre.

**Correction:** The text does not make that inference. As Koji Kuwakino stresses, the real-building rule is introduced for the sublunary *ars quadrata*. The *Theatrum Orbi* illustrates the celestial theatres of the *ars rotunda*, whose locus is expressly the ethereal world. Moreover, an international Latin book could not assume that its dispersed readers personally knew one London playhouse. A generic, adaptable theatre-model fits Fludd’s instructions better than a concealed portrait of the Globe.

### 6. Hierarchy of places

Recent scholarship reconstructs the system as nested rather than as a single freestanding “memory theatre”:

- *ars rotunda*: celestial spheres → zodiacal sectors → eastern and western theatres;
- *ars quadrata*: known building → rooms → walls, floor, and ceiling → smaller “theatres” or fields;
- within each theatre: doors, columns, and subsidiary places;
- within those places: active persons, objects, letters, numbers, names, or scenes.

This hierarchical compression resembles the systems of Cosma Rosselli and later John Willis more closely than Yates’s picture of one occult building.

### 7. Eastern and western theatres

Fludd imagines two theatres in relation to each zodiacal sign:

- an eastern, white, bright, shining theatre for day;
- a western, black, obscure theatre for night.

The zodiac diagram on p. 54 shows the eighth sphere with twelve signs, seven inner planetary circles, the elemental sphere at the centre, and two small stage-like structures beside Aries.

Fludd uses the first-person future *imaginabimur*: “we shall imagine.” The diagram supplies the ordered celestial framework.

**Probable reconstruction:** Two theatres multiplied by twelve signs yield twenty-four celestial theatres.

**Not completely explicit:** Fludd does not provide a single fully worked table enumerating theatres 1–24. Yates’s “twenty-four identical theatres” is a plausible reconstruction from the text and diagram, not a phrase to be quoted as Fludd’s own exhaustive specification.

### 8. The five doors and five columns

Fludd says:

> *Quodlibet theatrum quinque portas inter se distinctas et quasi aequidistantes habebit, quarum usum postea explicabimus.*

“Each theatre will have five doors distinct from one another and approximately equidistant; their use we shall explain later.”  
—Fludd, p. 55; spelling and punctuation normalized.

The corresponding columns are described at p. 63:

> *His pratis oppositae fingantur quinque columnae, quae itidem debent figura et colore distingui; figura enim duarum extremarum erit circularis et rotunda, media autem columna habebit figuram hexagoneam, et quae his intermedia sunt quadratam possidebunt figuram.*

“Opposite these fields let five columns be imagined, which likewise ought to be distinguished by shape and colour: the shape of the two outermost will be circular and round; the middle column will have a hexagonal shape; and those between them will possess a square shape.”

The order is therefore:

1. round;
2. square;
3. hexagonal;
4. square;
5. round.

The doors and corresponding columns are coloured:

1. white;
2. red;
3. green;
4. blue;
5. black.

These ten loci are distinguished by position, geometry, and colour.

**Documented:** Five doors and five opposing columns serve as ordered mnemonic loci.

**Documented:** Shape and colour prevent confusion among similar places.

**Yates’s inference:** The three lower and two upper openings shown in the plate constitute the Globe’s actual five entrances and reproduce a Vitruvian five-door *frons scaenae* on two levels.

**Problem:** Fludd’s text requires five mnemonic doors but does not say that they are evidence for the Globe, that they derive from Vitruvius’s five stage openings, or that the depicted upper apertures were actor entrances in a London theatre.

### 9. Images and actions

Fludd criticizes empty loci. An architectural place should contain a differentiating inhabitant, figure, picture, or action even before temporary material is attached. The image must be vivid and unambiguous.

His examples include mythological figures and dramatic actions, among them Medea:

- Medea killing her brother;
- Medea gathering magical herbs.

The remembered material—words, names, sentences, things, virtues, vices, mythological persons—may be attached to a column, nailed to a door, carried by an actor, or incorporated into the scene.

Fludd also supplies:

- visual alphabets in which human bodies or objects resemble letters;
- number-images;
- alphabetical lists of persons and things;
- mythological actors;
- virtues and vices;
- occupational and historical figures.

These provide a stock repertoire from which the practitioner constructs *imagines agentes*.

### 10. Fixing order and retrieval

The order is fixed by several simultaneous systems:

1. the astronomical order of zodiac and planets;
2. east before west, day before night;
3. the fixed sequence of doors and opposing columns;
4. left-to-right or otherwise rehearsed movement through the theatre;
5. stable colours;
6. stable geometrical shapes;
7. alphabetical or numerical image lists.

Encoding proceeds by translating the subject into an active visual episode and placing it at a known locus. Retrieval reverses the process: the practitioner mentally enters the appropriate sphere and theatre, traverses its doors and columns in the learned order, sees the deposited action, and translates the action back into words or things.

**Documented principle:** Stable places preserve order; striking images carry content.

**Scholarly reconstruction:** Fludd never supplies a modern algorithmic walk-through of an entire twenty-four-theatre store. Detailed retrieval must be inferred from his examples and the inherited rhetorical art.

## The sources

### 1. Primary printed text

Full bibliographical form:

Robert Fludd, *Utriusque cosmi maioris scilicet et minoris metaphysica, physica atque technica historia, in duo volumina secundum cosmi differentiam divisa*, Tomus secundus, *De supernaturali, naturali, praeternaturali et contranaturali microcosmi historia, in tractatus tres distributa*, Oppenheim: sumptibus Johann Theodor de Bry, typis Hieronymus Galler, 1619.

The memory treatise is:

*De animae memorativae scientia, quae vulgo Ars memoriae vocatur*, Tomus II, Tractatus I, Sectio II, Portio III.

Important pages:

- p. 48: title and autobiographical account;
- pp. 50–51: round and square arts;
- pp. 51–53: real and fictitious places;
- p. 54: zodiacal scheme;
- p. 55: eastern and western theatres and *THEATRVM ORBI*;
- pp. 57–58: architectural subdivisions and subsidiary theatre;
- p. 63: five columns, their shapes and colours;
- pp. 64–69: theatrical actions and image repertories.

The relevant section is sometimes catalogued without a separate dated title page. The encyclopedia was issued in parts between 1617 and the 1620s; consequently library catalogues sometimes give 1617–1621 or 1617–1638 for collected sets. The memory section itself belongs to the 1619 microcosm printing.

### 2. Preparatory manuscript

**Documented, newly important evidence:** Frankfurt am Main, Universitätsbibliothek Johann Christian Senckenberg, **MS lat. qu. 15**, the illustrated printer’s copy of *De technica microcosmi historia*.

Relevant folios:

- fol. 66r: preparatory zodiac drawing corresponding to printed p. 54;
- fol. 67r: preparatory *Theatrum Orbi[s]* corresponding to printed p. 55.

Ute Frietsch has established that this was a master copy used in preparing the printed section. It contains pen-and-ink drawings, pasted materials, authorial or workshop corrections, and German production instructions.

The preparatory theatre is attributed cautiously to “Robert Fludd in collaboration with Johann Theodor de Bry and workshop (?)”; the final etching is attributed cautiously to Matthäus Merian the Elder or the de Bry workshop.

**Consequence:** Yates wondered how Fludd communicated a London drawing to German engravers and conjectured an intermediary such as Michael Maier. The Frankfurt manuscript replaces much of that speculation with physical evidence: an illustrated printer’s copy crossed the author–publisher–workshop chain.

**What it does not establish:** It does not identify the represented building as the Globe. On the contrary, the presence of the image already within the designed manuscript programme strengthens its status as a constructed mnemonic diagram.

### 3. The inscription

The engraving reads:

> **THEATRVM ORBI**

Grammatically, “theatre of the world” would normally be *theatrum orbis*.

Yates proposed that *orbi* was an intentional dative: “a theatre for the world,” meaning a theatre placed within or assigned to the celestial world.

Later interpretations include:

- *orbi* as a simple grammatical or workshop error;
- an abbreviated or damaged *orbis*;
- *theatre for the sphere*, referring to the preceding *orbes coelestes*;
- a possible incomplete *orbi[s terrarum]*;
- an echo of the commonplace *theatrum mundi*.

Frietsch observes that the anomalous wording already appears in the manuscript and was carried into the print. That rules out an independent typesetter’s or final engraver’s innovation, but does not decide whether the anomaly was intentional.

**Disputed:** Yates’s dative interpretation remains possible but is unnecessary.

**No evidence:** Nothing in the wording secretly spells “Globe Theatre.”

### 4. Description of the p. 55 plate

The image is a one-point perspective interior or stage-like enclosure. It contains:

- a rear wall occupying the width of the composition;
- three openings at lower level;
- a broad central lower opening with paired leaves shown partly open;
- two smaller flanking lower doors;
- an upper gallery or terrace;
- two upper doorways;
- a crenellated parapet;
- a central projecting bay or oriel-like structure;
- the inscription *THEATRVM ORBI* on that projection;
- lateral walls returning toward the viewer;
- low box-like apertures or compartments in those walls;
- a ruled or tiled floor;
- five column bases advancing across the foreground;
- geometric forms associated with the ordered mnemonic stations.

The plate does **not** depict:

- an entire circular playhouse;
- an exterior;
- a visible audience;
- two structural stage posts;
- a projecting apron stage;
- a third gallery level;
- a roof or painted “heavens”;
- a named London location;
- actors performing Shakespeare.

Those elements enter Yates’s reconstructed Plate 20, not Fludd’s surviving image.

### 5. Other comparative primary sources

The most relevant are:

- Vitruvius, *De architectura* V.6–9, on theatre planning, orientation, acoustics, and the *scaenae frons*;
- Daniele Barbaro’s Italian and Latin Vitruvius editions, Venice, 1556 and 1567, illustrated by Andrea Palladio;
- John Dee’s “Mathematicall Praeface” to Henry Billingsley’s English Euclid, London, 1570;
- John Willis, *Mnemonica; sive Ars reminiscendi*, London, 1618;
- Willis, *The Art of Memory*, London, 1621;
- Willis, *Mnemonica: or The Art of Memory*, London, 1661;
- the contract for Philip Henslowe and Edward Alleyn’s Fortune playhouse, 1600;
- Arend van Buchell’s copy of Johannes de Witt’s sketch of the Swan theatre, Utrecht University Library, MS 842, fol. 132r;
- Cosma Rosselli, *Thesaurus artificiosae memoriae*, Venice, 1579;
- Giulio Camillo, *L’idea del theatro*, Florence and Venice, 1550;
- Giordano Bruno, *De umbris idearum*, Paris, 1582; *Ars reminiscendi* and *Explicatio triginta sigillorum*, London, 1583; *De imaginum, signorum et idearum compositione*, Frankfurt, 1591.

### 6. Editions and translations

There is no universally adopted modern critical edition of Fludd’s complete *Ars memoriae* comparable to the critical editions available for Bruno or Ramon Llull. Modern discussions commonly cite the 1619 folio directly.

Frances Yates translates substantial passages in chapters XV–XVI, but her renderings occur inside an interpretative argument and are not a complete edition.

Koji Kuwakino’s 2024 study provides a fresh structural reading of the Latin treatise. Ute Frietsch reproduces and compares manuscript and printed states.

## Yates’s account and the scholarship since

### 1. Bernheimer, 1958

Richard Bernheimer’s “Another Globe Theatre,” *Shakespeare Quarterly* 9.1 (1958), pp. 19–29, brought Fludd’s plate into modern Globe reconstruction.

Bernheimer wrote that Shakespeareans would recognize:

> “a lower and an upper stage, two entrance doors flanking an inner stage, battlements fitted for scenes of siege, and a bay window” suitable for Juliet.

**Status:** This is architectural identification by resemblance. Bernheimer did not possess documentary proof connecting Fludd, the engraver, or the plate with the Globe.

Bernheimer also treated the engraving’s relationship to the “round” art inadequately. Yates’s major correction was to show that “round” referred to the celestial component of the mnemonic system, not necessarily to the visible geometry of the pictured room.

### 2. Yates’s reconstruction

Yates’s three central statements are:

> “The engraving represents that part of the stage of the Globe which would be covered by the stage ‘heavens.’”  
> —p. 347.

> “My explanation of these features is that they are distortions of the real stage introduced for mnemonic purposes.”  
> —p. 348.

> “Encouraged by Mrs. Thrale, I believe that Fludd is stating through the shapes of the five column bases the geometrical forms used in the construction of the Globe, namely the hexagon, the circle, and the square.”  
> —p. 355.

Her reasoning proceeds as follows:

1. Fludd insists on real rather than fictitious places.
2. He compares his theatre with a public theatre where comedies and tragedies are acted.
3. His plate therefore embodies a real public stage.
4. The Globe was the best-known English public theatre and its name meant “world.”
5. Fludd’s first volume was dedicated to James I, patron of the King’s Men.
6. The plate’s lower central opening, upper bay, battlements, and five doors answer disputed needs in Shakespearean staging.
7. The side walls and five columns would obstruct spectators and therefore must be mnemonic distortions.
8. Remove the side walls and three of the five columns.
9. Substitute two load-bearing posts copied from Fludd’s “Temple of Music.”
10. Extend the floor into an apron stage.
11. Add a zodiacal “heavens” above.
12. Read the round, square, and hexagonal bases as a key to the Globe’s plan.
13. Generate a plan with Vitruvian triangles and dimensions borrowed from the Fortune contract.
14. Orient the stage from the word *Oriens* on the zodiac scheme.
15. Interpret subsidiary theatres as movable scenic screens or “mansions.”

This creates Yates’s Plate 20 and Figure 11.

### 3. Immediate criticism: Brownlow

F. W. Brownlow replied in the *New York Review of Books* in July 1966. His central objection was methodological: Yates could obtain the Globe only by radically changing the evidence.

She had to:

- remove the side walls;
- remove three columns;
- reconstruct the seating;
- enlarge or transform the central opening;
- turn the bay window into an acting chamber;
- add stage posts and heavens;
- extend the stage.

The result resembled a generic Elizabethan stage precisely because the distinctive evidence had been selectively removed or altered.

Brownlow concluded that the plate might preserve a modified screen-wall of a kind common in early modern halls, pageants, and stages, but that its specificity to the Globe rested on faith.

This remains the strongest concise objection to the reconstruction.

### 4. Immediate criticism: Wickham

Glynne Wickham argued in November 1966 that Yates had moved without adequate evidence from an unlocalized image to one particular London playhouse among many.

He noted that the plate’s rectangular enclosure might fit:

- the square Fortune;
- the Red Bull;
- the rectangular indoor Blackfriars;
- or no commercial playhouse at all.

His positive alternative was a royal tiltyard or Court of Chivalry setting, perhaps Whitehall or Greenwich. The large central gates, smaller flanking doors, crenellation, gallery, royal viewing window, and temporary canvas architecture could suit courtly combats and siege entertainments.

**Status:** Wickham’s tiltyard identification is itself a conjecture. It refutes exclusivity more successfully than it establishes a new provenance.

Yates replied that Wickham ignored the mnemonic context: the image is certainly a “theatre” within a theatre-memory system. On that narrow point she was right. But “theatre mnemonic” does not entail “Globe portrait.”

### 5. Yates’s later development

In *Theatre of the World* (1969), Yates expanded the argument connecting:

- Vitruvius;
- John Dee;
- Elizabethan theatre architecture;
- Fludd;
- Inigo Jones;
- Hermetic and Rosicrucian currents.

She continued to treat Fludd’s image as evidence for the Globe while acknowledging the provisional character of her theatre-historical reconstruction.

Her own closing words in *The Art of Memory* deserve emphasis. She called the chapter a “first attempt,” expected criticism by specialists, and requested more research on the German production of the engraving and on Vitruvian influence.

### 6. Modern manuscript research: Frietsch

Ute Frietsch’s work in 2022–2024 substantially changes the publication history.

Confirmed:

- Fludd actively designed and organized visual material.
- MS lat. qu. 15 was the illustrated master copy for the printed *De technica microcosmi historia*.
- The theatre and zodiac diagrams existed in manuscript before final engraving.
- De Bry’s workshop and probably Matthäus Merian transformed supplied visual designs into polished prints.

Corrected:

- One need not posit a lost sketch of the Globe independently copied by a German engraver.
- Yates’s Michael Maier courier hypothesis is possible in general but no longer necessary to explain the plates.
- The imagery belongs to a coherent authorial–editorial programme.

Frietsch explicitly rejects the Globe identification as overly “concretistic.” She distinguishes two round celestial theatres and one square mnemonic theatre rather than treating the system as a single Globe-derived room.

### 7. Kuwakino, 2024

Koji Kuwakino reconstructs Fludd’s places as a hierarchical art of architectural visualization and *ekphrasis*.

His decisive correction is that Fludd’s rule about personally known, real buildings applies to the *ars quadrata*. The p. 55 theatre belongs to the celestial scheme discussed on p. 54 and is initially an abstract model. Its fixed features organize memory, while readers may adapt others.

Kuwakino also observes that the international Latin readership at Oppenheim makes a uniquely London-specific model unsuitable as a common locus: most readers could not have known the Globe directly.

This is the clearest text-based refutation of Yates’s foundational premise.

### 8. Carruthers, Bolzoni, Rossi, Small, Ong, and others

These scholars are indispensable for the larger history of memory but do not all endorse or directly adjudicate the Fludd–Globe identification.

- **Mary Carruthers** shifted attention from “photographic storage” to medieval memory as composition, invention, ethical formation, and trained recollection. This supports reading Fludd’s architecture as a generative mental instrument rather than a literal building plan.
- **Lina Bolzoni** demonstrated how memory theatres, galleries, emblematic images, preaching, literature, and collection practices interacted. Her work cautions against isolating a diagram from its textual and performative uses.
- **Paolo Rossi** traced the passage from mnemonic arts through Lullism and combinatorics to projects for universal knowledge and language. This contextualizes Fludd’s system without requiring a Globe cipher.
- **Jocelyn Penny Small** emphasized ancient cognitive and material practices and the historical distance between Greek/Roman memory theory and Renaissance reconstructions.
- **Walter J. Ong** showed how Ramist spatial diagrams replaced or reconfigured imagistic loci through dichotomous order. Fludd represents a rival visual-cosmological tradition, not Ramist method.
- **Alfredo Bonner and Alexander Fidora** have corrected the once-common treatment of Llull’s Art as principally a mnemonic wheel-system. Llull’s figures are logical-combinatorial instruments for demonstrating relations among divine dignities, not simply storage wheels.
- **Rita Sturlese, Michele Ciliberto, Stephen Clucas, and Hilary Gatti** have complicated the Yatesian picture of Bruno as primarily a Hermetic magician. Bruno’s mnemonic works involve logic, ontology, cognition, rhetoric, atomism, and combinatorial invention as well as magic.
- **Bolzoni and Stagno** have sharpened study of Camillo through textual, rhetorical, visual, and collecting contexts, reducing reliance on the romantic picture of a completed occult machine.
- **William Engel, Rory Loughnane, and Grant Williams** document the breadth of memory practice in Renaissance England without making the Globe identification a settled fact.

The collective post-Yates correction is methodological: cosmological imagery cannot be reduced to magical talismanry, mnemonic architecture cannot automatically be treated as architectural documentation, and resemblance is not provenance.

## History and context

### 1. Classical rhetoric

Fludd’s immediate technical ancestry is the classical artificial memory preserved in:

- pseudo-Cicero, *Rhetorica ad Herennium* III.16–24;
- Cicero, *De oratore* II.350–360;
- Quintilian, *Institutio oratoria* XI.2.

The classical method establishes an ordered series of places and deposits distinctive images in them. Recollection is a mental traversal.

The *Ad Herennium*’s famous lawsuit image uses a sick man in bed, the accused beside him holding a cup, tablets, and a ram’s testicles: cup for poisoning, tablets for the will, testicles for witnesses. It exemplifies violent, concrete, punning *imagines agentes*.

That example is an ancestor of Fludd’s theatrical Medea-scenes, but it does not occur in Fludd’s *Theatrum Orbi* section.

### 2. Scholastic psychology and preaching

Medieval writers integrated artificial memory into ethics, preaching, confession, and prudence. Thomas Aquinas treated memory as a component of prudence; Albertus Magnus discussed vivid corporeal similitudes. Dominican and Franciscan educational environments used ordered divisions, images, exempla, and affective visualization.

Fludd retains:

- faculty psychology;
- the inward image;
- virtues and vices;
- Hell imagery;
- moral and religious recollection.

His opening diagram includes Tobias and the Angel and the Last Judgment with the damned entering Hell’s mouth.

### 3. Renaissance theatres and galleries

Giulio Camillo’s theatre arranged universal knowledge through seven grades, seven planetary structures, and mythological images in a reversed Vitruvian theatre: the spectator stood on the stage and faced the knowledge disposed through the auditorium.

Fludd reverses that orientation. His mnemonic operator faces the stage-like wall.

Yates’s elegant contrast—Camillo looking from stage to auditorium, Fludd standing with his back to the auditorium—is interpretatively useful, but it does not prove the physical existence of either reconstructed arrangement in the form she imagines.

### 4. Lullism and combinatorics

Fludd’s round form superficially resembles Lullian wheels, but Yates herself observed that this memory system does not conspicuously employ Lullian letter-combination. Llull appears in Fludd’s image lists as a representative of alchemy, not necessarily as the system’s operative source.

The deeper affinity lies in the ambition to order total knowledge. From Llull through Renaissance Lullism, Kircher, and Leibniz came projects for combinatorial discovery and universal language. Fludd belongs to that encyclopedic environment, but his zodiacal theatres are not a direct step-by-step ancestor of digital computers.

### 5. Hermeticism and macrocosm–microcosm

Fludd accepted a Christianized Hermetic cosmology:

- elemental world;
- celestial world;
- supercelestial or angelic world;
- humanity as microcosm;
- divine order reflected downward and recollected upward.

Yates interpreted the combined memory system as an attempt to reconstitute the cosmic order within the practitioner. That broad interpretation remains plausible.

Her stronger language—astral activation, talismanic images, a “memory Seal” comparable with Bruno—must be marked as interpretation. Fludd’s text supports spiritual and celestial efficacy, but not every diagram is thereby a magical talisman.

### 6. Ramism

Ramist method arranged knowledge through visible dichotomies and definitions. It offered a disciplined alternative to crowded image-loci. Protestant critics could treat spectacular artificial memory as morally dangerous, deceitful, or “impious,” especially where zodiacal images or spirits were involved.

Fludd’s richly pictorial, cosmological system stands near the opposite pole from the austere textbook Ramism studied by Ong.

### 7. Public theatre and court spectacle

Fludd explicitly analogizes memory with a public theatre:

> a theatre is that in which actions of words, sentences, parts of speech, or subjects are demonstrated as in a public theatre where comedies and tragedies are acted.

This proves conceptual dependence on performance.

It does not decide whether the architectural image derives from:

- the Globe;
- another public playhouse;
- an indoor theatre;
- a court tiltyard;
- a festival stage;
- a generic *scaenae frons*;
- mnemonic treatise conventions;
- or a composite made from several sources.

## People

### Robert Fludd

Physician, natural philosopher, alchemical experimenter, astrologer, diagram-maker, defender of Rosicrucian writings, and author of the system.

His autobiographical testimony documents mnemonic study and teaching in southern France. There is no evidence that he was an actor, Globe shareholder, or theatre architect.

### Johann Theodor de Bry

Publisher responsible for the lavish Oppenheim programme. The de Bry firm had long experience transforming drawings and travel reports into engravings.

### Hieronymus Galler

Printer of the Oppenheim volumes.

### Matthäus Merian the Elder

Probable engraver or etcher of many Fludd images, including perhaps the printed theatre. Attribution of individual plates should remain qualified where unsigned.

### Michael Maier

Rosicrucian and alchemical author associated with de Bry and England. Yates proposed him as a possible carrier of Fludd’s material to Oppenheim.

**Status:** plausible contact; not documented as the transmitter of the theatre drawing.

### Charles de Lorraine, Duke of Guise, and his brother

Fludd says he taught them mathematics and memory at Marseille.

**Documented practitioner anecdote:** This is the best evidence for actual instruction in Fludd’s art.

### James I

The first volume was dedicated to James. The King’s Men were under royal patronage.

**Yates’s interpretation:** The Globe allusion may have been intended to interest or flatter James.

**Against:** No dedication, letter, payment, court record, or contemporary testimony says that James received a concealed Globe diagram.

### John Dee

Yates proposed Dee’s 1570 Euclidean preface as a channel by which Vitruvius and architectural geometry reached English builders, including James Burbage.

**Disputed:** Dee promoted architecture, perspective, mechanics, and Vitruvius; no document shows him teaching Burbage or designing the Theatre or Globe.

### James Burbage and Peter Street

Burbage built the Theatre; Street dismantled its timbers and constructed the first Globe, then contracted to build the Fortune.

They are relevant to the material history of London playhouses. Neither is connected in surviving documents with Fludd’s mnemonic plate.

### Hester Thrale

Yates quoted Thrale’s eighteenth-century recollection that the Globe was “hexagonal without and round within.”

**Problem:** Thrale wrote long after the Globe had disappeared. Her statement is antiquarian tradition, not eyewitness evidence, and cannot securely decode Fludd’s five column bases.

### William Foster

Foster attacked Fludd and insinuated that his works were printed abroad because English universities and bishops would not permit magical books. Fludd replied that English printers had demanded £500, whereas overseas publication cost him nothing and was executed as he wished.

This exchange documents suspicion of Fludd’s works as magical, but also supplies an ordinary economic explanation for foreign publication.

### Mersenne and Kepler

Marin Mersenne attacked Fludd’s reliance on “Egyptian” or Hermetic teaching. Johannes Kepler criticized his hieroglyphic images and non-mathematical manipulation of number.

They were critics of Fludd’s epistemology and cosmology, not witnesses to a Globe cipher.

### The requested anecdotes

The “auctioneer,” “professor’s party,” “student who lost his memory,” and “accused magician” belong to other episodes and systems in the long history of memory arts. No primary or early secondary source located connects these anecdotes with Fludd’s *Theatrum Orbi* or with the Globe reconstruction.

**Absence of evidence:** They should not be imported into this dossier as Fludd anecdotes merely because they occur elsewhere in Yates or later mnemonic folklore.

## The magic question

### 1. Was the system magical?

The answer depends on the category being used.

**Documented:** Fludd’s round art employs stellar effigies, gods, goddesses, angels, *daemones*, virtues, vices, and the celestial spheres.

**Documented:** He says fantasy must concur in a metaphysical act and allows either spiritual intermediaries or the Holy Spirit as operative assistance.

**Documented:** He places memory beside geomancy, astrology, physiognomy, and chiromancy.

Thus the system is not merely a secular memory trick.

### 2. Was it talismanic?

**Yates:** Yes, or nearly so. She interprets its stellar images as “magicised or talismanic” and sees Fludd following Bruno’s astral memory.

**Qualification:** Fludd does not clearly instruct the reader, in this section, to manufacture or consecrate physical talismans. The operation occurs through imagination, celestial correspondences, and inward images. “Talismanic” is a defensible analytical comparison, not Fludd’s unequivocal procedural label.

### 3. Was it heretical?

Fludd presented his philosophy as Christian and defended a lawful, pious, natural or divine magic. Critics considered its Hermetic, Rosicrucian, spirit-invoking, and cosmological elements suspect.

William Foster’s accusation shows that “magical book” was a live polemical category. Mersenne’s attacks show that learned Catholic controversialists rejected Fludd’s Hermetic foundations.

**No documented consequence specific to the memory theatre:** No evidence located shows Fludd tried by the Inquisition, formally prosecuted for this mnemonic system, or punished for the *Theatrum Orbi*.

### 4. Rosicrucianism

Fludd defended the Rosicrucian manifestos and was widely regarded as a Rosicrucian philosopher.

**Documented:** His publications support Rosicrucian ideas.

**Disputed:** Membership in a concrete, organized Rosicrucian fraternity is unproved.

**Yates’s speculation:** The Globe may have been concealed within an elaborate mnemonic “casket” intelligible to initiates, perhaps including James I.

**Evidence:** None beyond thematic congruence, secrecy rhetoric, dedication, and wordplay on “Globe/world.”

### 5. *Ars notoria*

The *Ars notoria* promised accelerated or infused knowledge through prayers, sacred names, figures, and angelic assistance. Fludd distinguishes his art from a darker Solomonic practice associated with a ring.

Yates suggested that the Toulouse report might concern something like the *Ars notoria*.

**Status:** comparative conjecture. Fludd does not identify the reported art by that title.

## Afterlife

### 1. Theatre reconstruction

Bernheimer and Yates made the plate famous among Shakespeare scholars. Yates’s reconstruction influenced popular images of the Globe, experimental theatre designs, and the broader cultural association between Shakespeare’s theatre and Renaissance memory.

It did not become secure archaeological evidence. The modern Shakespeare’s Globe, opened in 1997, was based on a much larger body of evidence: excavation, contracts, Hollar, the Swan drawing, stage directions, carpentry practice, and comparative playhouse archaeology.

### 2. Modern theatre and installation art

Fludd’s theatre has been repeatedly reproduced in exhibitions, performance studies, architectural installations, and visual-art projects. Contemporary artists often treat it as:

- an archive of images;
- a stage for cultural memory;
- a model of mental architecture;
- an analogue of montage;
- a predecessor of Aby Warburg’s *Mnemosyne Atlas*.

These are productive receptions, not evidence about Fludd’s original use.

### 3. Literature

Dante’s mnemonic landscapes, emblem literature, moralized architecture, and Renaissance theatre supplied the wider tradition from which modern writers draw.

Borges’s “Funes the Memorious” explores the opposite of trained artificial memory: limitless particular recall without abstraction. It is an illuminating contrast, not a direct descendant of Fludd.

Umberto Eco’s labyrinthine libraries and semiotic encyclopedias knowingly evoke memory-palace, combinatorial, and universal-language traditions. Again, the relation is cultural and conceptual rather than a demonstrable line from p. 55.

### 4. Modern mnemonic practice

Contemporary “memory palace” training and memory-sport methods preserve the basic classical mechanism:

- fixed familiar route;
- ordered loci;
- exaggerated active images;
- deliberate retrieval traversal.

They ordinarily omit Fludd’s zodiac, metaphysical spirits, and macrocosm–microcosm theory.

Calling every modern memory palace a “Fludd theatre” is therefore a modern simplification.

### 5. Computing and combinatorics

The defensible genealogy is:

- ancient ordered loci and symbolic encoding;
- medieval systems of division and concordance;
- Llull’s combinatorial figures;
- Renaissance universal theatres and taxonomies;
- Ramist diagrams;
- seventeenth-century universal-language projects;
- Leibniz’s *characteristica universalis* and *calculus ratiocinator*;
- later formal logic and computing.

**Disputed popular claim:** “Llull invented the computer.”

Llull produced rule-governed combinatorial devices and influenced later formal projects. He did not build a programmable electronic or mechanical computer in the modern sense.

Fludd’s nested theatres resemble address spaces and structured information retrieval, but describing them as computer memory is analogy, not technological descent.

## Controversies and disputes

### 1. Is it the Globe?

**Yates/Bernheimer:** Yes, or very probably.

**Brownlow:** At most a generic modified stage or screen-wall.

**Wickham:** Perhaps a tiltyard or courtly combat theatre.

**Frietsch:** The Globe identification is an over-literal, rejected reading of a conceptual image.

**Kuwakino:** The plate belongs to celestial theatres and is an abstract mnemonic model; Fludd’s real-place rule does not support Yates’s conclusion.

**Present evidentiary judgment:** No provenance, label, eyewitness account, architectural measurement, stage direction, or textual reference ties it specifically to the Globe. The identification should be catalogued as a brilliant but unsupported twentieth-century hypothesis.

### 2. First or second Globe?

Yates specifies the second Globe because it was rebuilt in 1614 and the plate appeared in 1619.

That chronology only makes the identification possible. It does not prove it. Nor is it certain that the second Globe exactly reproduced the first internally.

### 3. Five doors

The five doors are textually secure mnemonic loci.

Their conversion into five real Globe entrances depends on circular reasoning: they are treated as real because the image is assumed to show the Globe, while the image is identified as the Globe because it shows the required entrances.

### 4. The “inner stage”

Yates interpreted the partly open central doors as evidence for a large inner stage and the bay above as an upper acting chamber.

These are possible theatrical uses of such architecture. But the image was made for a memory treatise, and no evidence establishes that the openings reproduce functional Globe spaces.

### 5. Battlements and Juliet’s window

Bernheimer’s attractive associations—siege scenes and Juliet at the window—are recognitions based on Shakespearean stage needs.

They are not annotations supplied by Fludd. The same architectural motifs occur in court festivals, pageants, halls, temporary scenery, and generic theatre imagery.

### 6. The subsidiary theatres

Yates interpreted Fludd’s smaller stage illustrations as movable screens used at the Globe to mark places such as the Capulet orchard or Friar Laurence’s cell.

**Documented:** Fludd has subsidiary mnemonic theatres.

**Possible theatrical analogy:** Early modern performance used temporary properties and localizing structures.

**Unsupported:** No source identifies Fludd’s subsidiary images as Globe scenery.

### 7. Geometry and the column bases

Yates read round–square–hexagon–square–round as a concealed construction key for a Globe that was polygonal outside and circular inside.

The text supplies a direct mnemonic explanation: unlike shapes prevent loci from being confused.

The mnemonic explanation is therefore prior and sufficient. An additional architectural cipher is possible only as speculation.

### 8. The Fortune dimensions

The 1600 Fortune contract specifies a square building eighty feet externally, a fifty-five-foot yard, a forty-three-foot stage, galleries, stage cover, and construction “with such-like stairs, conveyances and divisions” as the Globe.

Yates used the forty-three-foot stage and an inferred eighty-six-foot Globe diameter to construct her plan.

**Problem:** The Fortune was expressly square; similarity clauses do not authorize simple conversion of all its dimensions into a circular Globe. Later archaeology and reconstruction scholarship treat the contract as essential but potentially misleading when mechanically transferred.

### 9. Four Vitruvian triangles

Yates generated the Globe plan from four intersecting equilateral triangles in a circle, connecting the result with zodiacal *trigona*, Barbaro, and Palladio.

**Status:** modern geometrical reconstruction. Neither the Fortune contract nor Fludd’s plate supplies this plan.

### 10. Orientation

Yates read *Oriens* at the top of Fludd’s celestial diagram as evidence that the Globe stage lay at the east, “like the altar in a church.”

**Problem:** *Oriens* belongs naturally to an astronomical diagram of rising and setting. Transferring its orientation to the physical Globe assumes the conclusion.

### 11. Dee as theatre architect’s intellectual source

Dee’s preface documents the English availability of Vitruvian architectural mathematics and practical geometry.

It does not document contact with Burbage or Street. The proposed chain Dee → Burbage → Globe → Fludd remains a scholarly reconstruction.

### 12. Camillo’s influence

There is a strong typological relationship:

- theatre as universal ordering structure;
- planetary organization;
- mythological images;
- cosmological ascent;
- memory as totalizing knowledge.

Direct textual borrowing by Fludd from *L’idea del theatro* has not been demonstrated at every point. The possibility of mediation through French, Italian, Brunian, or general mnemonic culture remains open.

### 13. Willis’s influence

Willis’s *Mnemonica* appeared in London in 1618, one year before Fludd’s printed section. Willis employs repeated “theatres” or repositories, colour differentiation, and practical active images.

A direct influence is chronologically possible. But Fludd had practised memory since his French travels, and the printer’s manuscript may predate final publication by an uncertain period.

**Verdict:** Close contemporary parallel; direct dependence unproved.

### 14. Was the engraving deliberately obscure?

Yates asked whether the twenty-four theatres constituted an “elaborate casket” concealing a Globe allusion from everyone except initiates.

Fludd’s system is genuinely difficult, and secrecy was culturally meaningful. Yet textual difficulty, compressed exposition, and composite visual design sufficiently explain much of the obscurity.

**No positive evidence:** There is no key, testimony, initiation record, or contemporary decipherment of a Globe secret.

### 15. Was the Globe itself a memory theatre?

Modern criticism sometimes connects Hamlet’s:

> “whiles memory holds a seat / In this distracted globe”

with theatre-memory language.

This is a suggestive literary interpretation. “Globe” can mean head, world, theatre, or the Globe playhouse, and “seat” can carry theatrical and mental resonances.

It does not prove that the Globe was designed as a mnemonic machine or that Shakespeare knew Fludd’s 1619 plate.

## Open questions

1. Can handwriting analysis more precisely distinguish Fludd’s hand from those of amanuenses and workshop personnel in MS lat. qu. 15?

2. Who executed the preparatory theatre drawing and who cut or etched the final plate?

3. What is the exact relation between manuscript and print in the inscription *THEATRVM ORBI*? Is material now illegible after *ORBI*?

4. Does the engraving combine multiple visual sources—public stage, court festival, tiltyard, mnemonic room, and classical *scaenae frons*?

5. Can a precise stemma be established between Rosselli, Camillo, Bruno, Willis, and Fludd’s nested architectural places?

6. When was MS lat. qu. 15 assembled relative to Willis’s 1618 *Mnemonica*?

7. Were Fludd’s eastern and western theatres intended as twenty-four discrete repositories, or as two repeatable templates successively attached to twelve zodiacal signs?

8. How exactly were planetary spheres coordinated with doors, columns, colours, alphabetic lists, and dramatic images during retrieval?

9. Does *orbi* mean “for the sphere,” “for the world,” an unfinished phrase, or simply erroneous Latin?

10. Did any seventeenth-century reader report actually using Fludd’s complete celestial system? None has yet been identified here.

11. Is there any documentary evidence that Fludd visited the Globe? None has been located.

12. Did James I ever see or comment upon the memory engraving? None has been located.

13. Can surviving copies reveal meaningful states or corrections in the theatre plate? A systematic census and plate comparison remains desirable.
