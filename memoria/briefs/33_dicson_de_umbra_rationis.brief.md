Alexander Dicson of Errol ("Dicsono Arelio"), Scot, d. c. 1604; *De umbra rationis* (London, Vautrollier, dated 1583, early 1584), reprinted as *Thamus* (Leiden 1597); defended in *Defensio pro Alexandro Dicsono* (1584, "Heius Scepsius"); his teaching as reported by Hugh Platt (1592).

**Principle.** A close imitation of *Shadows*: loci are "subjects", images "adjuvants" or most often "umbrae", the image a shadow of the divine light sought through its shadows, vestiges and seals; the Ad Herennian rules repeated "muffled in an obscure mystique"; memory based on the order of the signs of the zodiac, repeated, without the decan list; traces of Bruno's inventors (Theutates letters, Nereus hydromancy, Chiron medicine); the Egyptians, not Simonides, invented the art, "if it is separated from Egypt it can effect nothing". The introductory dialogues (Mercurius, Thamus, Theutates, Socrates), built on the *Phaedrus* Theuth story and *Corpus Hermeticum* XIII and IV, present the "inner writing" of occult memory as Egyptian regenerative wisdom against a Socrates who is a satire on Ramus; Perkins called it the "Scepsian" artificial memory, after Metrodorus. As taught to Hugh Platt after Bruno left England, it was places in sets of ten with images made lively, "to animate the umbras or ideas rerum memorandarum" (Bellona with her fiery eyes), a simple form of the straight mnemotechnic without initiation into the mysteries. Yates links its "underground" Hermetic form to possible secret groups.

**Components.**
- Alexander Dicson, *De umbra rationis* (the "Scepsian" Brunian art of memory) `[3.32]`
- Dicson's taught practice: places in tens and "animated umbrae" (per Hugh Platt) `[3.36]`

**Yates.** Ch. XII, pp. 266–273 (system p. 268; dialogues pp. 268–272), 281–286; Ch. XV p. 336 (his teaching in London); Ch. XIV p. 318.

**In Yates's words.**
- "the loci are called 'subjects' and the images 'adjuvants' or more frequently 'umbra'. Clearly he is using Bruno's terminology." (p. 268)
- "Dicson's art of memory is but a fragmentary impression of the systems and expositions of Shadows from which it is nevertheless unmistakably derived." (p. 268)
- "He seems to have been taught a simple form of the straight mnemotechnic which he did not know was a classical art but thought was 'Maister Dickson's art'." (p. 285)
