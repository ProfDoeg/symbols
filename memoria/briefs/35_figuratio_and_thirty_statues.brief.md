Bruno, *Figuratio Aristotelici physici auditus* (Paris 1586, "sub Rosa rubra", dedicated to Piero Del Bene); *Lampas triginta statuarum* (Wittenberg c. 1588, unfinished, unpublished in his lifetime, Noroff manuscript).

**Principle.** The *Figuratio* teaches how to memorise Aristotle's physics through mythological memory images (the Arbor Olympica, Minerva, Thetis as matter, Apollo as form, the "superior Pan" as nature, Cupid as motion, Saturn as time, Jupiter as prime mover) placed on a "curious looking" horoscope-like place system of the Seals type; the reader is told to choose a Seal, perhaps the Painter, perhaps the Sculptor. Yates argues the images, animated with divine proportions and in contact with cosmic powers, contain Bruno's own living philosophy, so "the memory system by which the physics is to be 'figured' is in itself a contradiction of the physics. The book is a Seal." The Statues then do what the *Figuratio* advised with the Seal of Phidias: towering mythological statues sculptured within, beginning with "infigurable" concepts (Orcus, the Abyss, the thirst for the infinite) and passing to Apollo (the Monad), Saturn (Time), Prometheus (efficient cause), Sagittarius (intention), Coelius, Vesta, Venus and Cupid (love), Minerva (the mens, memory and reminiscence) with her Ladder; Yates believes the thirty were to be revolved on Lullian wheels, images replacing letters. Their theory is threefold: ancient truths declared through fables to be remembered; sensibilia retained more easily than intelligibilia (Thomist); talismanic statues in the manner of the *Asclepius* applied inwardly. "They are his philosophy."
