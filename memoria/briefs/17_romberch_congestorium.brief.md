Johannes Romberch, O.P. (German), *Congestorium artificiose memorie*, Venice 1520 (Yates uses the 1533 edition).

**Principle.** "A strange congestion of memory material" in four parts: introduction, places, images, an encyclopaedic system; Romberch knows all three classical sources, cites Petrarch and Peter of Ravenna, and rests on Thomas "on nearly every other page". He envisages three types of place system, all called artificial memory: the cosmos (spheres of elements, planets, stars and angelic orders, with Paradise, Earthly Paradise, Purgatory and Hell marked at the foot, Hell's places holding sinners punished by their sins, the "Dantesque type"); the zodiac and Hyginus's constellations (system 4); and real buildings, the abbey with courtyard, library and chapel supplying numbered loci marked with a hand at each fifth and a cross at each tenth place, stocked with memory objects and backed by alphabetical lists. A locus holding a human image must be no larger than a man can reach. Visual alphabets (objects shaped like letters, birds in alphabetical order, persons, number-objects) are used for inscriptions in memory: Grammatica holds a Pica for *predicatio* and an Aquila for *applicatio*, personifications recalling things and inscriptions memorising words. The encyclopaedic fourth part imagines a figure for every science (a perfect theologian with *cognitio, amor, fruitio* on his head), each subject in a memory room, a possible "distant echo" of Thomas's own system.

**In Yates's words.**
- "Romberch envisages three different types of place systems, as all belonging to artificial memory." (p. 115)
- "This type of artificial memory may be called the Dantesque type, not because the Dominican treatise is influenced by the Divine Comedy, but because Dante was influenced by such an interpretation of artificial memory" (p. 116)
- "The visual alphabets illustrated in the memory treatises, were I believe, intended to be used for making inscriptions in memory." (p. 119)
- "If we did not know the mnemonic explanation of Romberch's Grammar, she might seem like some inscrutable emblem." (p. 123)
