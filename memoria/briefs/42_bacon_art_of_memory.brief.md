**Principle.** Bacon builds the art "upon two intentions; the one prenotion, the other emblem", his version of places and images: prenotion "dischargeth the indefinite seeking" and directs the search to what "hath congruity with our place of memory" (a door, a corner, a window, familiar persons, or anything arranged in order, animals, herbs, words, letters, historical personages); emblem "reduceth conceits intellectual to images sensible, which strike the memory more" (a sportsman hunting the hare for invention, an apothecary ranging boxes for disposition). The art is turned from "ostentation prodigious" to "business and occasions", above all to memorising particulars of natural history in order so the judgment can work on them, order "turning into something like classification". "Men are best places", and the image's power is tied to "force of imagination" tricks. Aubrey's report of the Gorhambury gallery's painted panes of beast, bird and flower as "topiques for locall use" is one of the few known buildings designed for local memory. Bacon's attack on the microcosm doctrine "fantastically strained by Paracelsus" targets Metrodorian systems such as Fludd's; his demand for "real characters" seeds the universal-language movement; he rejects Lull's method as "a method of imposture".
