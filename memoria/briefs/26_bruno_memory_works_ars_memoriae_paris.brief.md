THREADS TO PULL: the catalogue entry follows, built from Yates by four readers; every line of it is a lead to verify against the primary text and the scholarship:

Giordano Bruno (1548–1600), Dominican at Naples 1563–76: *De umbris idearum* (*Shadows*, Paris 1582), *Cantus Circaeus* (*Circe*, Paris 1582), the *Ars reminiscendi* reprinted in *Seals* (London 1583); with the context of Porta, Agrippa, Gohorry and Garzoni.

**Principle.** *Shadows*, *Circe* and *Seals* each contain an "art of memory" on the old plan of rules for places and rules for images, with all the classical precepts and their accrued elaborations, under new terminology (locus = *subjectus*, image = *adjectus*) and a philosophy of the magically animated imagination; *Shadows* is presented by Hermes to Philotheus against Logifer the pedant. The *Ars reminiscendi* of *Circe*, introduced by the sorceress's incantations to the seven planets (omitted in the English reprint), expands the rules like a normal treatise: very large numbers of places (the last Roman place joined to the first Parisian), a list of thirty ways of forming images by association, a claimed better memory for words than "Tullius", and "semi-mathematical" subjects, diagrams mathematical "in some other way", i.e. magical, as place systems. Around it: Porta's *Ars reminiscendi* (Naples 1566/1602), a treatise without overt magic that reads Dido's painted rooms as her memory system, uses palaces, theatres or geometrical figures as places, images from Michelangelo, Raphael and Titian, hieroglyphs, and condemns Metrodorus; Agrippa's *De occulta philosophia* II, whose decan, planet and mansion images Bruno copies; Gohorry's 1550 description of Camillo's wooden amphitheatre and his wish for bolder "images and seals"; Garzoni's announced ambition of a universal memory combining Rossellius and Lull. "This book is the successor to Camillo's Theatre."

**Yates.** Ch. IX, pp. 199–209, 214–215; Ch. XI, pp. 243–244, 247; Ch. V p. 124 (Agrippa).

**In Yates's words.**
- "The treatise in Shadows alters the old terminology calling the locus, the subjectus, and the image, the adjectus, but the ancient division of the two aspects of memory training is perfectly perceptible beneath this new guise" (p. 202)
- "He recommends as place systems what he calls 'semi-mathematical' subjects, that is diagrammatical figures which are not mathematical in the normal way, but in some other way." (p. 247)
- "it was from Agrippa's manual of magic that Bruno took the magic images of the stars which he used in the memory system in Shadows." (p. 207)
- "The problem must have excited a good deal of general interest, comparable to the popular interest in the mind machines of today." (p. 208)
