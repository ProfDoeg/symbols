THREADS TO PULL: the catalogue entry follows, built from Yates by four readers; every line of it is a lead to verify against the primary text and the scholarship:

Aristotle, *De memoria et reminiscentia* and *De anima*; Plato, *Theaetetus*, *Phaedo*, *Phaedrus*; Augustine, *Confessions* X (d. 430). Doctrines, not techniques, which Yates treats as the ground of the scholastic and Renaissance forms of the art.

**Principle.** For Aristotle the soul never thinks without a mental picture; memory is a store of images stamped like a seal on wax, and reminiscence is a deliberate hunt by association (similar, contrary, contiguous) and by order from a starting point, "some use places for the purposes of recollecting"; the scholastics conflated this with Tullius's rules as their philosophical justification, though Yates doubts Aristotle meant more than illustration. For Plato the wax block receives perceptions, but true knowledge is recollection of the Ideas seen before birth, so "a Platonic memory would have to be organised ... in relation to the realities", and writing is "an elixir not of memory but of reminding"; this is the ground the Renaissance Neoplatonists build on. Augustine enters memory as "fields and spacious palaces" holding treasuries of images, the arts by presence, the affections by notions, and God "in no place"; Yates reads the capacity and organisation as a memory trained on the classical mnemonic in the buildings of the antique world, possibly "a Christianised artificial memory". "Aristotle is essential for the scholastic and mediaeval form of the art; Plato is essential for the art in the Renaissance."

**Components.**
- Aristotle's doctrine of memory and reminiscence (as used by the scholastics) `[1.14]`
- Plato's memory: the wax block, anamnesis, and the Theuth story `[1.15]`
- Augustine's memory: the fields and spacious palaces (*Confessions* X) `[1.17]`

**Yates.** Ch. II, pp. 31–39, 44–49; scholastic use Ch. III, pp. 61, 63–64, 67–68, 70–72, 75; Fig. 9 (faculty psychology after Romberch) belongs to Ch. XI.

**In Yates's words.**
- "Aristotle's work thus became for them a kind of memory treatise, to be conflated with the rules of Tullius and which provided philosophical and psychological justifications for those rules." (p. 32)
- "The grandiose attempt to do just this, within the framework of the art of memory, was made by the Neoplatonists of the Renaissance." (p. 37)
- "one gains, I think, quite strongly the impression that Augustine's was a trained memory, trained on the lines of the classical mnemonic." (p. 46)
