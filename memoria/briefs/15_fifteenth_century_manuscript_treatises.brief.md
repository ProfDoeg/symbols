Anonymous and named manuscript *Artes memorativae* of the fifteenth century: Lodovico da Pirano (Padua, from c. 1422), Luca Braga (1477), the 'pater reuerende' treatises, the anonymous Dominican *De memoria artificiali* (Marciana VI, 238) with Jacopo Ragone and Matthew of Verona, the Vienna codex 5395.

**Principle.** The manuscript tradition behind the printed treatises. The 'Democritus' type follows the *Ad Herennium* plan but assigns the invention to Democritus, ignores the striking human figures and Thomas, rests its image rules on Aristotelian association, and carries diagrams of rows of towers for the "multiplication of places" (Yates suspects a Byzantine channel). The 'pater reuerende' treatises supply long lists of familiar objects (a paternoster, an anvil, a helmet, a lantern, a tripod) as prefabricated memory images to be memorised on sets of places. The anonymous Dominican treatise holds "the whole order of the universe and the roads to Heaven and Hell" with only "devout images" and "sacred histories", saints with attributes on loci, in text nearly identical with Romberch's. The Vienna codex shows a rare row of human memory figures (Prudence at the head, Christ at the centre, Hell's mouth beneath, faces to resemble known persons, subsidiary images as memory for words) and plans of memory rooms with five places, four corners and a centre, the regular layout Yates takes as the normal medieval and later interpretation of places.

**Yates.** Ch. V, pp. 106–109.

**In Yates's words.**
- "One type of memory treatise may be called the 'Democritus' type from the peculiarity that such treatises assign the invention of the art of memory to Democritus and not to Simonides." (p. 106)
- "They are, I believe, as it were prefabricated memory images to be memorised on sets of places. This is almost certainly an old mediaeval tradition" (p. 107)
- "An anonymous treatise, probably also by a Dominican, gives a most solemn description of how to remember the whole order of the universe and the roads to Heaven and Hell by the artificial memory." (p. 108)
- "The regular arrangement of the places in such memory rooms ... was, I believe, a normal interpretation of places, both in the Middle Ages and in later times." (p. 109)

**Descends from / leads to.** From 2, 5 (Aristotelian association), 8 (Boncompagno as precedent for the object lists), 10–11, 13 (inscriptions on figures). To 17 (Romberch's Abbey with the objects "in action", Pl. 5a–b; his memory rooms for each science; his Dantesque cosmos), 18 (Rossellius), 20 (Dürer's "chamber must have more than four corners"), 31 (Daedalus's memory-object list in the Seals), 40 (Fludd's five loci).
