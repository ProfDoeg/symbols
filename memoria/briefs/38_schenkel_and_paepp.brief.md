Lambert Schenkel (1547–c. 1603), *De memoria* (Douai 1593) and *Gazophylacium* (Strasburg 1610; French *Le Magazin des Sciences*, Paris 1623); Johannes Paepp, *Schenkelius detectus* (Lyons 1617), *Eisagoge* and *Crisis* (Lyons 1619).

**Principle.** Schenkel, Louvain-trained public memory performer later accused of magic, who charged fees and withheld the full secrets from his books, teaches "basically the classical art" in the Romberch/Rossellius text-book stream, with long sections on places (diagrams of rooms containing memory places) and images, constant quotation of Thomas to attach it to the Dominican tradition, and a well-referenced history of the art (Simonides, Metrodorus, Tullius, Petrarch, Pico); "it could be a rational mnemotechnic", but obscure and citing suspect authors such as Trithemius. Paepp, his disciple, reveals the "arcane philosophical mysteries" hidden in it as Brunian, with long lists of magic images reminiscent of *Images*; his key passage runs from Thomas's *Summa* on order in memory, to the fifth treatise of the *Pimander* on the order of the universe as revelation of God, to the *Timaeus*, to Cicero on order, to *Ad Herennium* on places and images, and back to Aristotle and Thomas on frequent meditation: the thought-sequence by which the Tullian and Thomist places and images "turned into the magico-religious techniques of the occult memory". Together they are evidence that memory teaching could carry a Hermetic sect's message. Descartes's reform (system 43) was written "on reading through Schenkel's profitable trifles".
