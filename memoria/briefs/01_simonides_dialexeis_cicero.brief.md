Simonides of Ceos (c. 556–468 BC), legendary inventor; the anonymous sophist *Dialexeis* (c. 400 BC); Cicero, *De oratore* (55 BC) and *De inventione* (c. 86–82 BC).

**Principle.** After the collapse of Scopas's banqueting hall Simonides identified the corpses by remembering where each guest had sat, and inferred that order is the key to memory and sight the strongest sense: one selects places, forms images of the things to be remembered, and stores the images in the places, so that the order of the places preserves the order of the things. The *Dialexeis* fragment, the earliest treatise, already has the two kinds (things and names) and the rule "what you hear, place on what you know": names dissected etymologically (Chrysippus on gold and horse), things placed on gods and heroes (courage on Mars and Achilles). Cicero's *De oratore* gives a "potted version" of the rules through Antonius (many well-lighted places in order; active, sharply defined, unusual images; memory for things by "masks" on places; memory for words with more images) and defends the art against the "inert". His *De inventione* makes memory a part of Prudence, the doctrine that later made the artificial memory a moral duty. This is the founding layer every later system reworks; its distinctness from its neighbour (2) is that it is legend, fragment and elegant summary rather than a complete set of rules.

**Components.**
- The Simonides method: places and images (loci and imagines) `[1.1]`
- The *Dialexeis* sophist mnemonic (Hippias of Elis) `[1.13]`
- Cicero's version of the art (*De oratore*) `[1.9]`
- Memory as a part of Prudence (the Tullian ethical frame) `[1.10]`

**In Yates's words.**
- "he realised that orderly arrangement is essential for good memory." (p. 2)
- "Memory for things; memory for words (or names)! Here are the technical terms for the two kinds of artificial memory already in use in 400 B.C." (p. 30)
- "Cicero is a believer and a defender. He evidently had by nature a fantastically acute visual memory." (p. 19)
- "it rides with Prudence in a chariot of which Tullius is the driver, whipping up his two horses of the First and Second Rhetorics." (p. 67)

**Descends from / leads to.** Every later system; directly to 2 (the full rules), 3 (Quintilian's abbreviation of Cicero), 5 (Cicero's *Tusculans* transposing memoria and inventio into proofs of the soul's divinity; Plato's satire possibly aimed at sophist etymologies), 7 (Martianus and Alcuin know the Prudence frame), 9–11 (Prudence as the scholastic warrant), 12 (Dante's three parts of Prudence), 14 (Petrarch uses *De oratore*). Yates on p. 6: even Bruno's *De umbris idearum* is "going through yet once again the old, old business of rules for places, rules for images".
