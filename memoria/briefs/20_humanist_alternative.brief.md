THREADS TO PULL: the catalogue entry follows, built from Yates by four readers; every line of it is a lead to verify against the primary text and the scholarship:

Erasmus, *De ratione studii* (1512); Melanchthon, *Rhetorica elementa* (1534); Agrippa, *De vanitate scientiarum* (1530) cap. X; Dürer's letter to Pirckheimer (1506).

**Principle.** The rational pole against which Yates sets Camillo and Bruno: Quintilianist coolness. Erasmus allows that places and images may help but grounds the best memory on "study, order, and care"; Melanchthon forbids mnemonic devices and prescribes learning by heart in the normal way as the sole art; Agrippa's *De vanitate* condemns the art (Simonides invented, Metrodorus perfected it; the modern treatises "an unworthy catalogue by obscure men"), read by Yates as a safety device beside his own *De occulta philosophia*; Dürer's "chamber must have more than four corners which is to contain all the gods of memory" is the artist's aside. Viglius's letters to Erasmus about Camillo's wooden theatre carry the same scepticism.

**Components.**
- The humanist alternative: study, order and care; learning by heart `[2.21]`
- Agrippa's 'vanity' of the art (the *De vanitate* half of `[2.54]`; the *De occulta philosophia* image lists are under system 28)

**Yates.** Ch. V, pp. 124, 126–128; Ch. VI, p. 132; Ch. IX pp. 206–207.

**In Yates's words.**
- "Though I do not deny that memory can be helped by places and images, yet the best memory is based on three most important things, namely study, order, and care." (Erasmus, p. 127)
- "Melanchthon forbids students to use any mnemotechnical devices and enjoins learning by heart in the normal way as the sole art of memory." (p. 127)
- "A chamber must have more than four corners which is to contain all the gods of memory." (Dürer, p. 126)
