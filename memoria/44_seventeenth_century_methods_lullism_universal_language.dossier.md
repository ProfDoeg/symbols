# Seventeenth-century methods: Lullism mathematicised and the universal language: Research Dossier

## Basic identification

**Catalogue designation:** System 44 of 45: “Seventeenth-century methods: Lullism mathematicised and the universal language.”

**Exponents:** Cornelius Gemma (1535–1578/79); Johann Heinrich Alsted (1588–1638); Johann Amos Comenius (1592–1670); Johann Heinrich Bisterfeld (1605–1655); George Dalgarno (c. 1616–1687); John Wilkins (1614–1672); Sebastián Izquierdo (1601–1681); Athanasius Kircher (1602–1680).

**Dates and principal texts:**

- Cornelius Gemma, *De arte cyclognomica*, Antwerp, Christophe Plantin, 1569.
- Johann Heinrich Alsted, *Systema mnemonicum duplex*, Frankfurt am Main, 1610.
- Alsted, *Trigae canonicae*, Frankfurt, 1612; *Theatrum scholasticum*, Herborn, 1620; *Methodus admirandorum mathematicorum*, Herborn, 1623.
- Alsted, *Clavis artis Lullianae et verae logices*, Strasbourg, 1633, incorporating a preface dated 1609; reprinted 1652.
- Comenius, *Orbis sensualium pictus*, Nuremberg, Michael Endter, 1658.
- Sebastián Izquierdo, *Pharus scientiarum*, **Lyon**, Claude Bourgeat and Michel Liétard, 1659.
- George Dalgarno, *Ars signorum*, London, 1661.
- Bisterfeld, *Bisterfeldius redivivus*, The Hague, 1661, containing the posthumous *Alphabeti philosophici libri tres*.
- John Wilkins, *An Essay towards a Real Character, and a Philosophical Language*, London, 1668.
- Athanasius Kircher, *Ars magna sciendi*, Amsterdam, Johannes Janssonius van Waesberge and the widow of Elizaeus Weyerstraet, 1669.

**Principle in one sentence:** A family of seventeenth-century projects converted the fixed places and striking images of rhetorical memory, and the lettered figures and rotating combinations of Ramon Llull’s Art, into ordered encyclopedias, taxonomies, numerical combination procedures, pedagogical pictures, and “real characters” intended not merely to retain knowledge but to classify, communicate, and sometimes generate it.

**Yates:** *The Art of Memory*, chapter XVII, principally pp. 368–83, especially pp. 369–70, 374–79 and 383; antecedents at pp. 189, 194, 197, 209. The pagination is that of the 1966 Routledge and Kegan Paul edition and its standard reprints. A searchable Warburg Institute scan confirms the passages and references cited below. [Warburg Institute scan](https://resources.warburg.sas.ac.uk/mnemosyne/fq/yates_memory.pdf)

### Status of the catalogue entry

The catalogue’s singular word “system” is potentially misleading. **Documented fact:** no single author promulgated a unified system jointly called “Lullism mathematicised and the universal language.” The entry is a retrospective historical grouping made from Yates’s concluding chapter.

The component projects differ fundamentally:

| Project | Primary operation | Mnemonic “places” | Mnemonic “images/signs” | Intended result |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Gemma | Analogical circular traversal | Ordered sectors and cosmological/intellectual circles | Concepts, faculties, cosmic grades, letters and diagrams | Universal discovery and ordering |
| Alsted | Encyclopedic method plus artificial memory | Physical loci, topical divisions, tables, Lullian circles | Conventional images, words, letters, common notions | Learning, recollection, invention, exposition |
| Comenius | Illustrated, ordered nomenclature | The serial chapters and numbered objects in each scene | Woodcut pictures keyed to words | Sensory-language education |
| Bisterfeld | Philosophical alphabet and definitions | Taxonomic order | Significant character or word-as-definition | Universal intelligibility |
| Dalgarno | Taxonomic encoding | Classes, genera and species | Systematic letter-groups/signs | Written and spoken philosophical language |
| Wilkins | Forty-genus taxonomy | Hierarchical tables | “Real character” strokes and pronounceable syllables | International and scientific language |
| Izquierdo | Mathematical combinatorics | Ordered numerical or symbolic arrays | Terms, numbers and combinations | A general instrument of knowing |
| Kircher | Expanded Lullian combinatorial machinery | Alphabetic fields, tables, trees and wheels | Letters, divine attributes, subjects and predicates | Almost unlimited discourse and encyclopedic summary |

This dossier therefore reconstructs a **transformation complex**, not a single memory palace.

## The system in detail

## 1. The Lullian substrate

### What seventeenth-century writers inherited

**Documented primary-text structure:** Ramon Llull’s mature Art, especially the *Ars generalis ultima* (1305–08) and *Ars brevis* (1308), employed a controlled alphabet, fixed semantic assignments, geometrical figures, and regulated combinations. In the *Ars brevis* the alphabet B–K represents, according to the column consulted, nine divine dignities or principles, nine relational principles, nine questions, nine subjects, nine virtues, and nine vices. Figure A arranges the divine dignities around a circle; Figure T distributes relational principles through triangles; a third figure connects pairs; the fourth uses three concentric, rotatable circles to produce ternary letter combinations.

The mature nine dignities normally appear as:

> **B bonitas; C magnitudo; D aeternitas sive duratio; E potestas; F sapientia; G voluntas; H virtus; I veritas; K gloria.**

“B goodness; C greatness; D eternity or duration; E power; F wisdom; G will; H virtue; I truth; K glory.”

**Documented function:** these are not arbitrary pegs standing for an unlimited vocabulary. They are fundamental features of reality because every creature manifests the divine dignities in finite form. Combination is therefore constrained by a metaphysics and by logical rules of concordance, contrariety, beginning, middle and end.

**Modern correction to older descriptions:** Anthony Bonner and later Lull scholarship stress that Llull’s figures are components of a demonstrative and disputational Art, not mechanical truth machines that make propositions true merely by rotating discs. Figures generate questions and possible predications; definitions and rules decide whether a combination is appropriate. The [Ramon Llull Database](https://www.ub.edu/llulldb/presentacio) records 265 authentic works and distinguishes them from hundreds of pseudonymous alchemical and other texts.

### Places, images, order, retrieval

Strictly speaking, mature Llull has neither architectural loci nor the emotionally violent agent-images prescribed by the *Rhetorica ad Herennium*.

- **Places:** positions in figures, alphabetic columns, compartments in tables, and positions in a combination.
- **Images:** normally letters and geometric relations rather than imagined persons or actions.
- **Order:** alphabetic and diagrammatic; the same B–K sequence is fixed across semantic columns.
- **Retrieval:** formulate a question, identify its terms, select the relevant principles and rule, inspect permitted combinations, and develop the resulting middle terms into an argument.
- **Invention:** more important than verbatim recollection. The figures prompt structured investigation.

Calling the Lullian letters “memory images” is therefore an analogical Renaissance and modern-historiographical extension, not Llull’s simple identification of his Art with classical artificial memory.

### The pseudo-Lullian complication

*De auditu kabbalistico*—also printed as *Opusculum Raymundinum de auditu kabbalistico*—was composed after Llull and falsely attributed to him. **Documented reception:** the Strasbourg 1598 Zetzner collection printed it as Lullian, helping readers including Bruno, Alsted and Kircher treat Lullism as naturally allied to Cabala.

**Confirmed correction:** modern Lull bibliography excludes it from the authentic corpus. The Llull database’s separation of 265 authentic works from large pseudo-Lullian corpora makes the old attribution untenable. Yates already calls it pseudo-Lullian, so on this point she did not perpetuate the Renaissance attribution; her more speculative suggestion is that its preface helped propagate the term “method.”

## 2. Cornelius Gemma’s *De arte cyclognomica* (1569)

### Identity and purpose

Gemma, son of the mathematician and instrument-maker Gemma Frisius, was professor of medicine at Louvain. The full title supplies an unusually precise program:

> *De arte cyclognomica, tomi III. Doctrinam ordinum universam, unaque philosophiam Hippocratis, Platonis, Galeni, et Aristotelis in unius communissimae ac circularis methodi speciem referentes, quae per animorum triplices orbes ac sphaerae caelestis similitudinem fabricatos, non medicinae tantum arcana pandit mysteria, sed et inveniendis constituendisque artibus ac scientiis caeteris viam compendiariam patefecit.*

“Three books on the cyclognomic art, reducing the whole doctrine of orders, together with the philosophy of Hippocrates, Plato, Galen and Aristotle, to the form of one most general circular method, which—constructed through the threefold circles of minds and after the likeness of the celestial sphere—not only opens the mysteries of medicine but provides a short road for discovering and establishing the other arts and sciences.”

That is primary-text evidence for a universal circular **method of ordering and invention**. It is not merely Yates’s characterization. A complete digitization survives from the Museum Plantin-Moretus copy. [Wellcome scan and catalogue](https://wellcomecollection.org/works/rx9bzs7w/images?id=rtvp9hdu)

### How the method works

**Scholarly reconstruction, principally Stephen Clucas and Thomas Leinkauf:** Gemma begins from correspondences among three domains:

1. divine/intelligible order;
2. the human soul and its faculties;
3. the natural and celestial world.

The Art moves circularly because cosmic process and intellectual return are circular. Analogical correspondences permit movement from a known order to a less-known one. The human mind can reproduce in abbreviated form the order that proceeds from the divine mind into nature.

The operative structure is thus:

1. reduce a subject to its most general principles;
2. assign its members to ordered divisions or sectors;
3. compare corresponding places in the circles;
4. descend from universal causes to particulars or ascend from effects;
5. establish analogies among disciplines;
6. recombine relations to disclose questions or possible explanations.

### Places and images

**Documented/scholarly reconstruction:**

- The **places** are the determinate positions of the diagrams: sectors, rings, triads and correspondences.
- The **images** are not predominantly Ad-Herennium-style actors. They are cosmological, anatomical and geometrical schemata, lettered terms, the celestial sphere, the structure of the eye, and the threefold organization of soul/world.
- Fixity comes from cosmic hierarchy and the diagram’s geometry.
- Retrieval means returning to the relevant sector and following its analogical relations.

Clucas emphasizes that one of Gemma’s *typi* maps metaphysical divisions of the cosmos while simultaneously supplying mnemonic places. Gemma is therefore genuinely relevant to artificial memory, but the work’s center is universal method rather than a handbook of mnemonic feats. [Clucas discussion](https://eprints.bbk.ac.uk/19502/1/Clucas_pre-proof%20version.pdf)

### Diagrams

The Plantin book “bristles with tables and schemata.” The major types are:

- concentric or superimposed circles;
- radial partitions;
- triadic diagrams linking soul, cosmos and discipline;
- tables of paired or correlated concepts;
- a diagram of the eye and hollow optic nerve;
- cosmological spheres;
- volvelles or diagrams whose text instructs one orb to be rotated around another.

A surviving Plantin woodblock bears the legend:

> *Hic orbis circa superiorem voluitur, sic ut singuli circulorum ordines singulis quam maxime quadrent.*

“This circle is turned around the upper one, so that the individual orders of the circles correspond as closely as possible to the individual [orders above].”

It was printed in book II, p. 146. The block survives as Museum Plantin-Moretus object **MPM.HB.03593**, cut by Antoni van Leest between June 1568 and March 1569. [Museum Plantin-Moretus record](https://search.museumplantinmoretus.be/Details/collect/354709)

This is unusually strong material evidence: the diagram was not merely metaphorical but designed as a movable correspondence device.

### Gemma and Bruno

**Yates’s interpretation:** Gemma’s compound of “Lullism, Hermetism, Cabalism, and the art of memory” may have influenced Bruno.

**Later scholarship:** Clucas gives the philosophical relationship more substance, locating Gemma and Bruno in a Platonic diagrammatic lineage involving Cusanus, Ficino and Charles de Bovelles. Yet direct dependence remains **probable or possible, not proved**. A shared Antwerp, Lullian and diagrammatic environment does not by itself demonstrate that Bruno read this particular book.

### Limits of reconstruction

No passage located in Gemma supplies a simple exercise comparable to the *Ad Herennium* lawsuit scene. No evidence has emerged of a pupil memorizing a speech by physically turning Gemma’s wheel. The best reconstruction is of a visual-analogical instrument for ordered thought, with mnemonic utility, not a conventional place-and-image drill.

## 3. Bacon, Descartes and the Paris Bureau d’Adresse

### Bacon

Bacon separates intellectual work into invention, judgment, memory and transmission. In *The Advancement of Learning* II.xvii.14 he calls the Lullian Art:

> “not a lawful method, but a method of imposture,” devised so that people might “make a show of learning” which they did not possess.

This is **documented rejection**, not rejection of universal method as such. Bacon wanted an art of discovering arts grounded in interrogating nature; he rejected Lullian topical machinery as a producer of fluent appearances.

He similarly criticizes conventional artificial memory as capable of prodigious exhibition but “barren” in serious business. Yet he retains order, commonplaces and visible signs within a reformed art of retaining knowledge. [Full text of *Advancement*](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/5500/5500-h/5500-h.htm)

Yates’s statement that Bacon “used” local memory depends partly on John Aubrey’s report that Gorhambury’s gallery windows displayed beasts, birds and flowers, followed by Aubrey’s own conjecture: “perhaps his Lordship might use them as topiques for locall use.” **Evidentiary label:** contemporary-biographical tradition plus Aubrey’s explicit guess, not proof of Bacon’s practice.

### Descartes

In the *Discours de la méthode*, part II, Descartes dismisses the Lullian Art as enabling one “to speak without judgment of things of which one is ignorant”:

> *l’art de Lulle, pour parler sans jugement de celles qu’on ignore.*

But his earlier notes show engagement rather than simple ignorance:

- Letter to Isaac Beeckman, 26 March 1619, AT X, 156–57: Descartes announces a *scientia penitus nova* capable of generally solving questions of continuous or discrete quantity.
- In the private *Cogitationes* associated with 1619–21, he criticizes Lambert Schenkel’s mnemonic profusion and proposes reducing things to their causes, so vanished images can be recovered through the cause’s impression.
- He also considers constructing connected images whose members refer to one another.

**Yates’s interpretation:** Descartes rationalized something structurally close to occult causal memory.

**Caution:** Descartes explicitly distinguished his projected quantitative science from an *ars brevis* of Llull. The resemblance lies in universality, order and reduction, not demonstrated technical descent.

### The Bureau d’Adresse

Around 1632 Théophraste Renaudot’s Paris conference discussed:

1. the method of Cabalists, descending from archetypal through intellectual to elemental worlds;
2. Llull’s method based on divine attributes;
3. ordinary philosophical method.

The published *Recueil général des questions traitées ès conférences du Bureau d’Adresse*, Lyon, 1633–66, volume I, pp. 7 ff., grouped this under “De la méthode.”

**Yates’s inference:** this shows how unsurprising Descartes’s title would have sounded in 1637.

**Correction in emphasis:** it demonstrates the breadth of *méthode* as a contemporary term, not that Descartes borrowed his title or procedure from this conference.

## 4. Johann Heinrich Alsted

### Institutional setting and intellectual profile

Alsted taught at the Calvinist academy or *Hohe Schule* of Herborn: first in its pedagogium, then philosophy, and from 1619 theology. The institution was a center of Reformed encyclopedism, Ramism and educational reform. Comenius studied under him there.

Calling Alsted “encyclopaedist, Lullist, Cabalist, Ramist,” as Yates does, is broadly confirmed, but these were ingredients he compared and harmonized rather than an undifferentiated creed.

### *Systema mnemonicum duplex* (1610)

The full title distinguishes:

- *Systema minus*: four books in concise preceptive order.
- *Systema maius*: seven books with fuller method and explanatory commentaries.

It promises:

> *artis memorativae praecepta plene et methodice traduntur … et a pseudo-memoristarum, pseudo-Lullistarum, pseudo-Cabbalistarum imposturis discernitur atque vindicatur.*

“The precepts of the memorative art are transmitted fully and methodically … and [the art] is distinguished and vindicated from the impostures of pseudo-mnemonists, pseudo-Lullists and pseudo-Cabalists.”

The surviving first edition is physically enormous: two parts, [16], 136; 830, [2], 731, [1] pages, with eleven folding tables, octavo. The digitized copy derives from the Biblioteca Nazionale Centrale di Firenze. [Wellcome bibliographical record](https://wellcomecollection.org/works/a8gna6ww)

### The duplex structure

**Documented from title and organization; clarified by Rossi and later Alsted studies:** the work joins a shorter practical digest to a much larger theoretical, pedagogical and encyclopedic system. Memory is treated as part of the whole *modus studendi*: how to teach, learn, establish schools, arrange subjects and recall them.

This breadth matters. Alsted is not simply providing a bag of visual mnemonics. He makes memory the architecture of curriculum.

### Places, images and order

Alsted retains conventional distinctions between natural and artificial memory and between places and images, but subjects them to method.

His decisive definition, quoted by Rossi and Yates from *Systema*, p. 5, is:

> *Methodus est instrumentum mnemonicum procedens a generalibus ad specialia.*

“Method is a mnemonic instrument proceeding from generals to particulars.”

He treats Lullian circles as kinds of places corresponding to mnemonic loci. On this model:

- **Places:** architectural loci may still be used, but logical divisions, disciplinary tables, commonplaces and Lullian circles also become places.
- **Images:** sensory images, significant words, letters, diagrams and topical headings.
- **Order:** general-to-special, coordinated with Ramist dichotomy, Aristotelian definition, encyclopedic hierarchy and Lullian combination.
- **Storage:** assign matter to the correct generic and specific location.
- **Retrieval:** recover the general heading, descend through its subdivisions, and use images or signs as cues.
- **Invention:** compare neighboring or combinable headings; the storehouse becomes a finding-machine.

### *Clavis artis Lullianae* (1633)

The work presents itself as a key to both Lull’s Art and “true logic.” Its bibliographical situation needs precision:

- the commonly cited printing is Strasbourg, 1633;
- its preface bears the date 1609;
- a 1652 reprint survives;
- therefore “1609” is not safely treated as the publication date of the cited 1633 artifact.

The digitized 1633 copy is from the Bavarian State Library. [Google Books record](https://books.google.com/books/about/Clavis_artis_Lullianae.html?id=iP9BAAAAcAAJ)

Alsted criticizes Agrippa and Bruno for falsehoods and obscurities said to have deformed the divine Art. He seeks a cleaner line through Bernard de Lavinheta, whose works he edited in 1612.

**Important complication:** in the same publishing milieu, Alsted issued Bruno’s *Artificium perorandi*, composed at Wittenberg in 1587, in Frankfurt in 1612. Thus his hostility was discriminating: he rejected Brunian corruptions of Lullism but could transmit a non-Lullian rhetorical work by Bruno.

### Diagrams and tables

The eleven folding tables in *Systema* are not incidental decoration. They externalize knowledge through:

- synoptic divisions;
- tabular topical sequences;
- alphabetic and numerical schemes;
- circular or Lullian relationships;
- hierarchical curricular maps.

The *Clavis* explains figures, principles, questions, subjects, triangles and combination. Its circles are increasingly conceptual “places”: positions into which predicates and questions can be inserted.

### Ramism

Yates rightly detects Ramist influence in the descent from general to special. Walter Ong’s *Ramus, Method, and the Decay of Dialogue* remains fundamental for the migration from dialectical disputation to visualized curricular order. Later work corrects any simple opposition between Ramist Protestantism and imagistic memory: Calvinist authors including Alsted could combine Ramist method with loci, images and Lullian figures. [Recent reassessment](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/17496977.2021.1981695)

### Alsted’s reform program

Yates describes a retreat from Renaissance occultism. That is partly correct, but “disenchantment” is too linear.

- Alsted rejects “pseudo-” practitioners.
- He still calls Llull a mathematician and Cabalist.
- He accepts the spurious *De auditu kabbalistico*.
- He maintains correspondence theories and Christian universalism.
- He combines these with Ramist pedagogy and encyclopedic discipline.

The result is not simply magic becoming reason. It is confessional and pedagogical regulation of inherited techniques.

## 5. Comenius and *Orbis sensualium pictus* (1658)

### Primary artifact

The first edition is *Orbis sensualium pictus*, Nuremberg, 1658, printed by Michael Endter, Latin and German, with the German rendered by Sigmund von Birken. The illustrations are attributed to Paulus Creutzberger. [Bibliotheca Augustana edition record](https://www.hs-augsburg.de/~harsch/Chronologia/Lspost17/Comenius/com_o000.html)

The title defines it as:

> *Omnium fundamentalium in mundo rerum et in vita actionum pictura et nomenclatura.*

“A picture and nomenclature of all the fundamental things in the world and actions in life.”

The motto is:

> *Omnia sponte fluant; absit violentia rebus.*

“Let everything flow spontaneously; let violence be absent from things.”

### Mechanism

Each chapter presents:

1. a woodcut scene;
2. numbers placed beside objects or actions within it;
3. a facing or adjacent verbal description;
4. the same numbers embedded in the verbal text;
5. parallel language columns.

The learner moves repeatedly between pictured thing, printed number and word.

- **Places:** the ordered sequence of approximately 150 thematic chapters and the numbered positions inside each scene.
- **Images:** ordinary depictions of things and actions, not fantastic mnemonic agents.
- **Order:** creation and the human world—God/world, elements and heavens, plants, animals, human body and trades, social and civic life, religion and judgment.
- **Encoding:** the numeral binds a visible object to its name and proposition.
- **Retrieval:** seeing the picture cues the word; seeing or hearing the word can reactivate the pictured object and its context.
- **Pedagogical rule:** teach words with things; where things cannot be physically presented, present their images.

This is artificial memory in a broad pedagogical sense, but the book does not teach a learner to build a private memory palace.

### Editions and variants

**Documented:** the first edition was bilingual Latin-German. Later editions added or substituted vernaculars; English translation followed in 1659 under Charles Hoole. The catalogue’s suggestion that a single 1658 book taught Latin, German, Italian and French conflates the multilingual publication history. Some later editions are trilingual or multilingual.

Image blocks, captions, spelling, chapter organization and languages vary across printings. It is unsafe to treat a nineteenth-century English-Latin *Orbis* as visually identical to Nuremberg 1658.

### Campanella and Andreae

Yates says:

> “there can be no doubt that the *Orbis pictus* came straight out of Campanella’s *City of the Sun*.”

She connects Comenius’s pictures to Campanella’s concentric city walls, covered with images through which children learn the world, and also to Johann Valentin Andreae’s illustrated educational city in *Christianopolis* (1619).

**Assessment:** this is one of the dossier’s most important disputed overstatements.

- A relationship among pansophic pedagogy, the world-as-book, Campanella, Andreae and Comenius is plausible.
- Direct linear derivation of the *Orbis* “straight” from Campanella is not established by the structural similarity alone.
- Illustrated pedagogical, emblematic, natural-historical, catechetical and craft-book traditions provide additional contexts.
- Later studies emphasize the book’s ordered, morally interpreted “mirror” of creation and its debt to emblematic natural history.
- The safer formula is: Campanella’s painted city is a notable analogue and possible influence within a wider visual-pedagogical ecology.

A later study explicitly identifies Yates’s claim and instead emphasizes the *Orbis* as a conservative ordered *speculum* whose images and moral readings belong partly to emblem culture. [Miller, “Beasts of the New Jerusalem”](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/007327530804600204)

### Was it a universal language?

Not by itself. It is a multilingual illustrated nomenclature. Its principle that words be learned through things supports the later philosophical-language aspiration, but its pictures remain conventional representations and its words remain natural-language words.

## 6. Comenius, Bisterfeld and “word as definition”

### Comenian pansophy

Comenius’s larger program sought an ordered account of all things, universal education and repair of human affairs. In *Via lucis* and the manuscript *Panglottia*, language reform becomes part of pansophic reform.

A key principle reconstructed by recent scholarship is **“word as definition”**: the root should signify the proximate genus and modifications should encode the specific difference. A word’s form would disclose where its referent belongs.

### Bisterfeld

Johann Heinrich Bisterfeld, another Herborn-associated encyclopedist and later court intellectual in Transylvania, contributed to this continental lineage. His posthumous *Alphabeti philosophici libri tres*, in *Bisterfeldius redivivus* (The Hague, 1661), treats a philosophical alphabet in which signs or words reveal structured concepts.

Recent scholarship places Bisterfeld and Marin Mersenne between earlier mystical or Cratylist traditions and Comenius’s mature formulation. It therefore corrects the impression that the English movement arose directly and solely from Bacon or from mnemonic images. [Pavlas, “Word as definition”](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/17597536.2024.2307681)

### Mnemonic character

A definitional word functions as a compressed retrieval address:

\[
\text{word} = \text{genus marker} + \text{difference marker} + \text{grammatical marker}.
\]

Remember the classification and one can reconstruct the word; decode the word and one can recover the classification. This reciprocity is why philosophical languages can properly be discussed beside mnemonic systems.

## 7. George Dalgarno’s *Ars signorum* (1661)

### Primary text and artifact

Full title:

> *Ars signorum, vulgo character universalis et lingua philosophica.*

“The Art of Signs, commonly called a universal character and philosophical language.”

London, printed by J. Hayes, 1661; [18], 127 pages and one folded leaf; Wing D128. A Bodleian copy is shelfmarked **Savile Cc 18**. [Museum of the History of Science catalogue](https://www.mhs.ox.ac.uk/gatt/catalog1ca6.html?num=42); [Folger record](https://catalog.folger.edu/record/466109)

David Cram and Jaap Maat’s 2001 edition supplies the Latin text, first complete English translation, commentary, and Dalgarno’s unpublished autobiographical tract. [Modern edition record](https://books.google.com/books/about/George_Dalgarno_on_Universal_Language.html?id=oP9QEAAAQBAJ)

### System

Dalgarno classifies notions into a limited number of general classes and assigns systematic letters to subdivisions. Radical forms encode semantic location; grammatical modifications make the system speakable.

- **Places:** positions in the taxonomy.
- **Images/signs:** alphabetic forms whose components have assigned semantic values.
- **Order:** genus → subordinate class → species.
- **Storage:** assign a concept to its proper taxonomic cell.
- **Retrieval:** decode the letters to recover the class, or traverse the class to construct the word.
- **Mnemonic advantage:** related concepts have related forms.
- **Mnemonic liability:** related forms are easy to confuse, and reclassification changes the linguistic address.

### Patrons and dispute

Dalgarno began with an international shorthand and entered Samuel Hartlib’s reform network. Sir Cheney Culpeper offered interest and possible patronage, asking whether the characters might be rational “down to the last jot” and perhaps identical with Adam’s language.

Dalgarno interacted with Wilkins, Seth Ward and John Wallis. By 1661 he asserted priority. The later dispute with Wilkins concerns collaboration, borrowing, taxonomy and different educational aims.

**Contested claim:** “Wilkins plagiarized Dalgarno” is too blunt. There was contact and shared development; Dalgarno felt wronged; both also worked from common philosophical-language and taxonomic problems. Modern scholarship reconstructs competing models rather than a conclusively proven one-way theft. [Subbiondo on the dispute](https://benjamins.com/catalog/sihols.112.10sub)

## 8. John Wilkins’s *Essay* (1668)

### Primary text

*An Essay towards a Real Character, and a Philosophical Language*, London, printed for Samuel Gellibrand and John Martyn, printer to the Royal Society, 1668: [19], 454, [162] pages and two plates. [EEBO-TCP full text](https://quod.lib.umich.edu/e/eebo/A66045.0001.001?view=toc); [Folger record](https://catalog.folger.edu/record/499625)

It appeared under Royal Society imprimatur. Contributors included John Ray in botany; other fellows supplied specialized vocabularies.

### The taxonomy

Wilkins divides the universe of discourse into forty principal genera. Each genus subdivides into differences, generally six, and each difference into species, generally up to nine.

A spoken word encodes position:

- initial consonant: broad family or genus group;
- vowel: genus;
- following consonant: difference;
- final vowel or modifier: species;
- additional markers: grammatical and relational information.

A standard example is:

- **Zi**: beasts;
- **Zit**: rapacious beasts of the dog kind;
- **Zitα** or its alphabetic equivalent: dog.

The written “real character” makes comparable distinctions through a central generic stroke plus spatially differentiated affixes.

### Places, images and retrieval

- **Places:** the taxonomic tables, not buildings.
- **Images:** graphic strokes and pronounceable syllables.
- **Fixed order:** the forty genera and their numbered differences/species.
- **Retrieval from concept:** determine genus, difference and species; concatenate their signs.
- **Retrieval from sign:** separate graphic or phonetic components; trace them back through the tables.
- **Rule:** the character should signify the thing or notion through its classified position rather than merely spell an English or Latin word.
- **Reality claim:** “real” means notion-directed and cross-linguistic, not magically identical with the essence of the object.

### Character versus language

Recent scholarship corrects the tendency to privilege the written scheme. Wilkins and Dalgarno designed both visual and articulated media and treated “language” as encompassing writing and speech. Their characters do not bypass all language in an instantaneous picture; they require learned conventions and grammatical operations. [“Effable characters”](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/17597536.2024.2311957)

### Practical limits

- Any false taxonomy is built permanently into the vocabulary.
- Closely related words become dangerously similar.
- Users must master an encyclopedic classification before speaking easily.
- culturally contested entities need fixed locations;
- synonymy, metaphor, polysemy and changing scientific knowledge strain the scheme.

The Royal Society appointed a committee to consider the work but produced no formal report. **Corrective scholarship:** absence of a report should not be read as simple indifference; some committee members participated in revision attempts after Wilkins’s death. [Vivian Salmon](https://benjamins.com/catalog/hl.1.2.02sal)

## 9. Sebastián Izquierdo’s *Pharus scientiarum* (1659)

### Bibliographical correction

The first edition is **Lugduni—Lyon—not Leiden**, 1659, in two folio volumes, published by Claude Bourgeat and Michel Liétard. The catalogue identification “Leyden” repeats an error found in Yates. The surviving catalogued copy described by the CCFr belonged to the Jesuit royal college at La Flèche and bears the old marking “143.” [CCFr record](https://ccfr.bnf.fr/portailccfr/ark:/16871/0019204578)

The title promises a “science of science” and:

> *Ars demum legitima ac prorsus mirabilis sciendi, omnesque scientias in infinitum propagandi ac methodice dirigendi … aperte et absque involucris mysteriorum in lucem proditur.*

“At last, a legitimate and altogether marvelous art of knowing, of extending all sciences indefinitely and directing them methodically … is brought into the light openly and without coverings of mysteries.”

The language is triumphal and “marvelous,” but explicitly contrasts open demonstration with hidden mystery.

### Combination as instrument

At *Pharus*, p. 319, Izquierdo writes:

> *Hoc est potissimum atque praestantissimum omnium sciendi instrumentum: immo unicum, per quod immediate fabrica sciendi construitur, et absque ullo termino semper augetur.*

“This is the most powerful and excellent of all instruments of knowing—indeed the unique one through which the structure of knowledge is immediately constructed and increased without limit.”

The antecedent is *combinatio*. The key primary section is Disputation XXIX, *De Combinatione*.

### What “mathematicised” means

Yates’s phrase is substantively correct if carefully limited. Izquierdo:

- defines different kinds of selection and arrangement;
- calculates numbers of possible combinations;
- enumerates combinations in systematic orders;
- substitutes general numerical procedures for dependence on Llull’s B–K alphabet;
- uses the result as a tool for combining terms and propositions.

Modern historians of combinatorics identify in §§48–51 a rule equivalent to:

\[
\binom{m+n-1}{n}
\]

for combinations with unlimited repetition. In §§81–84 he lists combinations of \(m\) letters taken \(n\) at a time, and provides larger tables for selected cases. He treats what modern taxonomy calls tuples, variations, multicombinations and combinations. [History of combinatorics survey](https://api.pageplace.de/preview/DT0400.9780191630620_A23590107/preview-9780191630620_A23590107.pdf)

### Places and images

Izquierdo’s principal machinery is not an image system.

- **Places:** positions in ordered lists and tables; positions occupied by terms in a proposition.
- **Images/signs:** letters or numbers as replaceable symbols.
- **Order:** lexicographic and numerical procedures.
- **Retrieval:** reproduce the generation rule and locate or regenerate a combination.
- **Invention:** systematically exhaust allowed combinations, then submit them to logical and disciplinary judgment.

This is a decisive movement from visual association toward generative symbolic procedure. But semantic truth remains dependent on definitions and metaphysics; arithmetic enumeration alone does not validate a proposition.

### Edition and translation

Ramón Ceñal edited Disputation XXIX with Latin text, Spanish translation and a long historical introduction:

Sebastián Izquierdo, *La combinatoria: “Pharus scientiarum” (1659), Disputatio XXIX, De Combinatione*, ed. and trans. Ramón Ceñal, Madrid, Instituto de España, 1974, 317 pp. [Edition record](https://books.google.com/books/about/La_Combinatoria_de_Sebasti%C3%A1n_Izquierdo.html?id=33ChNQAACAAJ)

### Influence

Izquierdo’s discussion influenced Kircher and was known within the field from which Leibniz’s 1666 *Dissertatio de arte combinatoria* emerged. Precise one-way claims need caution: Mersenne, Clavius and other combinatorial traditions also intervene. “Izquierdo invented Leibniz’s calculus” is a modern exaggeration.

## 10. Athanasius Kircher’s *Ars magna sciendi* (1669)

### Primary artifact

Full title:

> *Ars magna sciendi, in XII libros digesta, qua nova et universali methodo per artificiosum combinationum contextum de omni re proposita plurimis et prope infinitis rationibus disputari, omniumque summaria quaedam cognitio comparari potest.*

“The Great Art of Knowing, divided into twelve books, by which, through a new and universal method and an artificial network of combinations, any proposed matter can be discussed in very many and almost infinite ways, and a certain summary knowledge of all things can be acquired.”

Amsterdam, 1669. ETH Zürich supplies a persistent digital record. [e-rara/ETH](https://doi.org/10.3931/e-rara-12297); a full scan is also [available here](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/6/6f/Athanasii_Kircheri_e_Soc._Jesu_Ars_magna_sciendi%2C_in_XII_libros_digesta_-_qua_nova_%26_universali_methodo_per_artificiosum_combinationum_contextum_de_omni_re_proposita_plurimis_%26_prope_infinitis_%28IA_gri_33125008657112%29.pdf).

### Origin story

Kircher reports that Emperor Ferdinand III asked him to assess or clarify Llull’s Art. He likens treasures concealed under difficult Lullian forms to riches within a rough Silenus and proposes correction and extension.

**Status:** authorial report in Kircher’s preface; credible evidence of courtly commission or encouragement, but also conventional patronal self-presentation.

### Machinery

Kircher retains nine principal Lullian letters and associates them with divine attributes and logical categories. He adds:

- *Alphabetum Artis Magnae*;
- a *Tabula generalis*;
- branching trees;
- concentric wheels;
- linear combinations;
- elaborate networks connecting every member of one series with members of another;
- combination tables;
- subject-predicate and question structures.

The diagrams include visually dense line meshes now recognizable as graph-like representations. Calling them “graphs” is a retrospective mathematical description, not Kircher’s term.

### Worked combinatorial example

Kircher discusses arrangements of letters such as **ALA** and **SARA**. Where some letters repeat, he divides the factorial number of arrangements by the factorials of repeated elements. The scan states in substance that for ALA, with two identical letters, the six arrangements of three objects are divided by two, yielding three distinct arrangements; for SARA, 24 is divided by two, yielding twelve.

This is a genuine worked combinatorial procedure, materially different from simply rotating a Lullian wheel. The searchable scan displays the Latin proposition and arrangements. [Kircher scan](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/6/6f/Athanasii_Kircheri_e_Soc._Jesu_Ars_magna_sciendi%2C_in_XII_libros_digesta_-_qua_nova_%26_universali_methodo_per_artificiosum_combinationum_contextum_de_omni_re_proposita_plurimis_%26_prope_infinitis_%28IA_gri_33125008657112%29.pdf)

### Places, signs and retrieval

- **Places:** cells and positions in alphabetic, tabular, arboreal and circular structures.
- **Images:** letters, divine dignities, logical relations and line connections; relatively few classical mnemonic actors.
- **Order:** fixed alphabets, categorical tables and combinatorial rules.
- **Retrieval:** identify subject and logical relation, consult the table, trace connections, or regenerate combinations.
- **Invention:** expand the possible modes of treating a question.
- **Validation:** combination produces candidate discourse; it does not guarantee empirical truth.

### Relation to Izquierdo and Llull

Kircher explicitly works in the Lullian tradition but reforms it. The numerical-combinatorial detail also owes much to Izquierdo. Paolo Rossi’s formulation—Llullian art becoming neutral combinatorial technique—captures part of the change, although Kircher’s theological framework remains strong.

## Yates’s account and scholarship since

## What Yates argues

Yates’s chapter XVII is explicitly a sketch. She says the chapter is “insufficient” but necessary to carry the history from Simonides to Leibniz.

Her governing thesis is:

> “These two great mediaeval arts, which the Renaissance had tried to combine, turn into methods in the seventeenth century and play their part in the methodological revolution.”

The two arts are classical artificial memory and Llull’s Art.

Her sequence is:

1. Gemma exemplifies an occult and circular “method.”
2. Bacon and Descartes reject Llull yet share his universal ambition.
3. Alsted combines memory, Ramism, Lullism and Cabala while reforming occult excess.
4. Comenius rationalizes Campanella’s magical visual pedagogy.
5. real-character projects rationalize magical images thought to contact reality;
6. Izquierdo and Kircher mathematize Lullism;
7. Leibniz converts mnemonic-combinatorial inheritance into a mathematical *characteristica* and calculus.

## What remains strong

- Memory techniques, diagrammatic logic, encyclopedism and method should be studied together.
- Lullian reception is indispensable to Alsted, Izquierdo, Kircher and Leibniz.
- Seventeenth-century writers did not observe a clean modern boundary between mnemonic, logical, pedagogical and theological instruments.
- Tables and diagrams are operational, not decorative.
- Alsted did assimilate Lullian circles to places and method to a mnemonic instrument.
- Izquierdo did develop explicitly mathematical combinatorics.
- Universal characters often advertised mnemonic advantages.
- The chain from Lullian combination to early modern combinatorial logic is historically real, though not exclusive.

## Corrections and qualifications

### 1. A transition too linear

Yates’s repeated pattern—occult Renaissance source transformed into rational modern method—can become teleological. Later scholarship shows coexistence:

- rational enumeration inside theological projects;
- imagery inside Protestant/Ramist pedagogy;
- metaphysical realism inside apparently formal languages;
- practical educational goals inside pansophy.

The period is better represented as **recomposition** than disenchantment.

### 2. Gemma was under-read

Yates admits she had not reconstructed cyclognomy and suggests Izquierdo might help a future investigator. Clucas and Leinkauf have since shown it to be a coherent Platonic method of analogical inference across logic, physics and metaphysics. Yates’s ingredient list is suggestive but not a working description.

### 3. Comenius and Campanella

“There can be no doubt” and “came straight out” exceed the evidence. Later scholarship places *Orbis pictus* among multiple traditions: sensory pedagogy, emblem, illustrated natural history, nomenclature, catechesis and pansophy.

### 4. Bacon’s “real characters”

Yates attributes the universal-language movement to Bacon’s demand for characters “really in contact” with notions. Bacon did discuss characters for things and cross-linguistic signs, but the stronger ontological phrase belongs to Yates’s reconstruction. Wilkins’s signs remain stipulated and taxonomic, not naturally bonded to essences.

### 5. Dalgarno and Wilkins

Yates compresses their projects into a single current. Modern editions reveal substantial differences, a priority dispute, different grammars and divergent educational assumptions.

### 6. Bibliographical errors

- Izquierdo’s *Pharus* is Lyon 1659, not Leiden.
- Wilkins is 1668, not 1669.
- Alsted’s *Clavis* must distinguish the 1609 preface from the 1633 printing.
- Alsted’s publication of Bruno’s *Artificium perorandi* is normally dated 1612, though Yates’s surrounding prose can make the chronology seem closer to 1610.

### 7. Llull and Cabala

Yates correctly identifies *De auditu kabbalistico* as pseudo-Lullian but sometimes allows Renaissance conflations to color her description of authentic Llull. Bonner, Fidora, Charles Lohr and the critical-edition tradition sharply distinguish Llull’s Christian apologetic Art from later Christian Cabala and pseudo-Lullian alchemy.

### 8. Leibniz and calculus

The strongest Yates sentence says Leibniz’s significant signs became mathematical symbols whose combinations “were to produce” infinitesimal calculus. That formulation conflates distinct strands.

- Lullian and combinatorial traditions influenced Leibniz’s universal-characteristic projects.
- Izquierdo and Kircher belong to his combinatorial background.
- Infinitesimal calculus arose through mathematical work on series, tangents, quadratures and symbolic notation, not by straightforwardly converting a memory wheel.
- “Lull invented computing” and “Kircher’s wheels became the computer” are retrospective popular simplifications.

## The requested scholars: relevance and limits

- **Paolo Rossi**, *Clavis universalis* / *Logic and the Art of Memory*: indispensable direct source for the passage from mnemonic images to combinatorial logic and universal language; more philosophically attentive to inference than Yates.
- **Walter Ong**, *Ramus, Method, and the Decay of Dialogue*: essential for Ramist visual order and “method”; not a full treatment of Alsted’s mnemonic synthesis.
- **Mary Carruthers**, *The Book of Memory*: corrects the notion of medieval memory as passive storage, emphasizing compositional invention; mainly medieval and not a direct account of Izquierdo or Wilkins.
- **Lina Bolzoni**, *The Gallery of Memory*: crucial for Renaissance visual culture and the porous boundary between image, text and memory; more directly relevant to Camillo and Italian traditions than to Izquierdo.
- **Jocelyn Penny Small**, *Wax Tablets of the Mind*: important for ancient literacy and memory; indirect here.
- **Helga Hajdu**, *Das mnemotechnische Schrifttum des Mittelalters*: foundational catalogue of medieval mnemonic writing; indirect for the seventeenth-century language schemes.
- **Anthony Bonner** and **Alexander Fidora**: decisive for authentic Llull, the logical function of the Art and its scholarly transmission.
- **Sturlese, Ciliberto, Clucas and Gatti**: chiefly correct the Yatesian picture of Bruno and Renaissance natural philosophy. Clucas is directly important for Gemma; the others supply context rather than primary evidence for Alsted or Wilkins.
- **Bolzoni and Stagno**: indispensable for Camillo and visual theaters but only genealogically relevant here.
- **Yates’s later writings:** she continued to use the broad Hermetic/mnemonic framework; no later wholesale retraction of chapter XVII was found.

## History and context

## Chronology

### c. 1274–1308: Llull constructs and revises the Art

Llull develops figures and alphabets to demonstrate Christian truths and organize disputation. His own program belongs to medieval apologetics, Franciscan-associated spirituality, university disputation and missionary controversy.

### Fourteenth–fifteenth centuries: reception and opposition

Lullist schools and manuscript networks arise in Majorca, Paris, Barcelona, Valencia and Italy. The Dominican inquisitor Nicholas Eymerich attacks propositions attributed to Llull; Lullists defend his orthodoxy. Pseudo-Lullian alchemical texts multiply.

### Late fifteenth–sixteenth centuries: Cabala, printing and encyclopedism

Pico’s comparison of Cabalistic *ars combinandi* with Llull encourages Christian-Cabalist assimilation. Printed collections mix authentic and spurious works. Lavinheta systematizes the Art for Renaissance disciplines. Agrippa and Bruno combine Lullian elements with occult philosophy, rhetoric and artificial memory.

### 1569: Gemma

At Catholic Louvain and Plantin’s Antwerp press, medicine, celestial order, Platonism, Aristotelianism and Lullian diagrammatics become cyclognomic method.

### 1598–1633: German printed Lullism

Zetzner’s Strasbourg compilations transmit authentic and pseudo-Lullian material. Alsted at Herborn integrates it with Ramism, pedagogy and encyclopedism.

### 1619–58: pansophy and visual education

Comenius carries Herborn encyclopedism into a trans-European Protestant reform network. Campanella and Andreae provide parallel utopian models. *Orbis pictus* converts universal order into a child’s numbered picture-text.

### 1650s–60s: philosophical languages

Hartlib’s network, Oxford intellectual circles and the emerging Royal Society encourage real-character proposals. Dalgarno and Wilkins design systematic languages; Bisterfeld and Comenius develop continental equivalents.

### 1659–69: numerical combinatorics

Jesuit university and court networks transmit Izquierdo and Kircher. Combination becomes formally countable and deployable across subjects while retaining theological-universal ambitions.

### 1666 onward: Leibniz

Leibniz’s *Dissertatio de arte combinatoria* names modern Lullists, studies combination, and projects an alphabet of human thoughts. His later *characteristica universalis* and *calculus ratiocinator* preserve the ideal of decomposing concepts and calculating relations, though no complete universal language is realized.

## Institutions

- **Franciscan settings:** Llull was a lay tertiary, not simply a Franciscan schoolman; later Franciscan support mattered to his cult and defense.
- **Dominican settings:** Dominican opposition is associated above all with Eymerich, but “the Dominicans” were not uniformly anti-Lullist.
- **University of Paris:** central to medieval disputation and early Lullian reception.
- **University of Louvain:** Gemma’s medical and astronomical chair.
- **Plantin press, Antwerp:** material production of Gemma’s elaborate figures.
- **Herborn Academy:** Alsted’s Reformed encyclopedism and Comenius’s education.
- **Hartlib circle:** correspondence-based patronage of educational, agricultural and linguistic reform.
- **Oxford philosophical circle/Royal Society:** Wilkins, Ward, Wallis, Ray and language reform.
- **Jesuit colleges:** Izquierdo’s education and teaching; the La Flèche provenance of one *Pharus* copy illustrates institutional circulation.
- **Collegio Romano:** Kircher’s teaching, museum and international Jesuit correspondence.
- **Habsburg court:** Ferdinand III’s interest in Kircher’s reform of Llull.
- **Transylvanian court:** Bisterfeld’s career and the eastward circulation of Herborn pansophy.

## The magic question

## Authentic Llull

Llull’s mature Art is theological and metaphysical but not “magic” in the ordinary sense of invoking spirits or talismanic forces. It aims at necessary reasoning from principles shared across creation.

**Disputed historiographical label:** describing it as “magical combinatorics” imports later occult receptions into the authentic works.

## Pseudo-Llull, Cabala and alchemy

The strongest magic associations come from:

- pseudo-Lullian alchemical works;
- *De auditu kabbalistico*;
- Renaissance Christian Cabala;
- Agrippa;
- Bruno;
- analogies between divine names, celestial causes and significant signs.

Alsted inherited some of this mixture while policing “pseudo-” forms. Kircher accepted aspects of Christian Cabala and universal correspondence but condemned illicit divination and demonic magic elsewhere.

## Gemma

Gemma cites Hermes Trismegistus, Ficino, Orphic and Pythagorean material and constructs cosmic correspondences. “Hermetic” is documented as intellectual source; “talismanic” is not established. No instruction in *Cyclognomica* located here tells the user to consecrate a wheel, summon a spirit or capture a planetary force.

## Alsted

Alsted’s polemic distinguishes legitimate mnemonic/Lullian/Cabalist learning from imposture. He was not prosecuted as a magician. His confessional and educational career is inconsistent with portraying the *Systema* as a secret magical manual.

## Comenius

*Orbis pictus* is Christian and providential, not talismanic. Yates calls its alleged Campanellan ancestor magical; the child’s picture book itself uses ordinary didactic pictures. No accusation of sorcery resulted from its numbered scenes.

## Universal languages

Some contemporaries connected real characters with:

- Adamic language;
- divine naming;
- recovery from Babel;
- direct expression of notions.

But Dalgarno’s and Wilkins’s signs are conventional constructions. “Real” did not mean occultly efficacious. Culpeper’s question about Adamic language documents speculation, not the designers’ possession of Adam’s tongue.

## Izquierdo and Kircher

Izquierdo calls his Art *mirabilis* and a universal key, but also says it is presented openly and without mysterious coverings. His combination theory is arithmetic and logical.

Kircher’s wider oeuvre includes Cabala, magic, seals and occult correspondences, yet *Ars magna sciendi* principally offers authorized Christian encyclopedism and combinatorics. No Inquisition prosecution of Kircher for this book is documented.

## Heresy and consequences

The main heresy controversy belongs to Llull’s reception, especially Eymerich’s *Directorium inquisitorum* and the long Lullist campaign at Rome. The seventeenth-century authors in this dossier were not collectively persecuted for artificial memory. Statements that “the universal-language inventors were accused magicians” are unsupported generalization.

## Ars notoria

The *Ars notoria* promises knowledge through prayers, ritual observance and inspection of complex *notae*. It is historically relevant because it links diagrams, memory, angelic revelation and accusations of illicit art. But there is no demonstrated textual identity between its notae and the tables of Wilkins or the arithmetic combinations of Izquierdo. Resemblance at the level “diagram plus universal knowledge” is insufficient evidence of descent.

## Afterlife

## Leibniz

Leibniz knew Lullian and modern combinatorial writers, including Alsted and Kircher, and cites mnemonic authorities. His youthful *Dissertatio de arte combinatoria* (1666) precedes Kircher’s 1669 *Ars magna sciendi* but belongs to the same active environment and could know Izquierdo.

The legitimate line is:

\[
\text{Lullian decomposition}
\rightarrow \text{Renaissance/Alstedian encyclopedism}
\rightarrow \text{Izquierdo’s enumerative combination}
\rightarrow \text{Leibnizian characteristic projects}.
\]

This is not a single chain of exclusive causation. Scholastic logic, algebra, legal classification, Mersennean combinatorics and contemporary mathematics also matter.

## Classification and information organization

Wilkins’s hierarchical semantics anticipates features of:

- thesaurus organization;
- faceted classification;
- controlled vocabularies;
- semantic encoding;
- ontology engineering.

The analogy is structural. Modern systems generally treat classifications as revisable data models; Wilkins sought a comparatively stable map of reality.

## Computing

### Documented continuities

- finite alphabets;
- symbolic decomposition;
- combinatorial generation;
- address-like taxonomic positions;
- rule-governed transformations;
- tabular external memory.

### Disputed or retrospective claims

- Llull did not build a programmable digital computer.
- Kircher’s wheels are not stored-program machines.
- Wilkins’s language is not a modern semantic web.
- Leibniz’s binary arithmetic and calculating-machine work cannot be derived solely from Lullian wheels.
- “Artificial intelligence began with Llull” is a modern genealogical slogan. It can illuminate formal resemblance but must not substitute for institutional and technical history.

## Literature

### Borges

“The Analytical Language of John Wilkins” uses an imaginary Chinese encyclopedia to expose the contingency of all classification. Borges’s target is not only Wilkins; it is the dream that one taxonomic grid transparently reproduces the universe. Borges nevertheless recognizes the attraction of semantically articulate words. [Background on the essay and Wilkins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Analytical_Language_of_John_Wilkins)

### Eco

Umberto Eco’s *The Search for the Perfect Language* situates Llull, Cabala, Comenius, Dalgarno, Wilkins and Leibniz within Europe’s recurring search for Adamic, universal and philosophical languages. Eco generally weakens the claim that any system escapes convention: even “perfect” languages presuppose a culturally produced encyclopedia.

### Funes

Borges’s Funes remembers every detail but cannot readily abstract. His predicament reverses the seventeenth-century program: unlimited retention without general concepts defeats thought. Alsted, Wilkins and Izquierdo seek precisely the general ordering Funes lacks. Any claim that Borges consciously modeled Funes on Alsted requires evidence not located here.

## Visual culture, emblems and pedagogy

Comenius’s numbered scenes have an afterlife in:

- illustrated primers;
- object lessons;
- bilingual picture dictionaries;
- encyclopedias for children;
- museum and classroom labeling.

They also inherit the emblem book’s combination of image, title and explanatory text, though *Orbis* makes the image-text relation more systematically instructional.

## Modern memory palaces and athletes

Contemporary memory athletes mostly use loci, person-action-object encodings and number-shape or major systems. They descend more directly from classical artificial memory than from Wilkins’s real character or Izquierdo’s combinatorial theory.

Digital “memory palace” products that cite Llull or Kircher often blend historically separate systems. That is a **modern invention**, sometimes useful in practice but not evidence of seventeenth-century continuity.

## Controversies and disputes

| Claim | Status | Evidence and assessment |
|---|---|---|
| Gemma invented a single memory wheel for knowing everything | Misleading simplification | His work contains multiple circles, tables and analogical procedures across three books. |
| Gemma directly influenced Bruno | Possible, not proved | Chronology and conceptual parallels fit; no decisive borrowing document located. |
| *De auditu kabbalistico* is by Llull | False attribution/tradition | Accepted by many Renaissance Lullists; excluded by modern critical bibliography. |
| Alsted published *Clavis* in 1609 | Bibliographically imprecise | The preface is dated 1609; the cited edition is Strasbourg 1633. |
| Alsted rejected Bruno entirely | False | He attacked Bruno’s Lullian corruption yet published *Artificium perorandi*. |
| Ramism eliminated imagistic memory among Protestants | Overturned generalization | Alsted and other Reformed authors combined them. |
| *Orbis pictus* directly and solely descends from *City of the Sun* | Yates’s strong interpretation; disputed | Analogy and possible influence exist, but multiple visual-pedagogical traditions intervene. |
| *Orbis pictus* first appeared in four languages | False for editio princeps | Nuremberg 1658 was Latin-German; additional languages belong to later editions. |
| Bacon demanded signs ontologically touching things | Interpretive exaggeration | Bacon proposed characters for things; stronger contact language is Yatesian. |
| Dalgarno was the first fully elaborated seventeenth-century philosophical-language author | Defensible within a defined corpus | Cram and Maat support it; earlier projects existed but were less complete or differently framed. |
| Wilkins plagiarized Dalgarno | Disputed | Collaboration, rivalry and shared materials are documented; exclusive derivation is not. |
| *Pharus scientiarum* was published at Leiden | Error | Title-page and catalogues give Lyon, 1659. |
| Izquierdo simply replaced every Lullian letter by a number | Too crude | He developed general enumeration rules and retained logical-semantic structures. |
| Izquierdo anticipated modern combinatorics | Confirmed with qualification | Specific enumeration results are mathematically significant; notation and framework are premodern. |
| Kircher’s combinatorial diagrams automatically discover truth | False | They generate arrangements and topics; truth still requires interpretation and judgment. |
| Kircher copied Izquierdo | Partial and reductive | Influence is substantial, but Kircher also draws directly on Llull and wider traditions. |
| Lullism produced Leibniz’s infinitesimal calculus | Overturned as causal claim | Lullism informs combinatorial and characteristic ambitions, not the mathematical derivation of calculus. |
| The systems were condemned as magic | Unsupported as a general claim | Individual antecedents attracted heresy or magic allegations; these printed systems generally circulated in recognized schools, courts and orders. |
| These are early computers | Modern analogy | Useful for finite-state combination and symbolic addressing; anachronistic if taken literally. |

### When the influential readings appear

- **Renaissance identification of Llull with Cabala:** encouraged by Pico and spread through pseudo-Lullian printing, especially *De auditu* and Zetzner collections.
- **Alsted as harmonizer of Lull, Ramus and Cabala:** visible in Alsted’s own titles and system; systematized by Carreras y Artau and Rossi.
- **Memory → universal language → scientific method:** most influentially formulated by Paolo Rossi in 1960 and Yates in 1966.
- **Gemma → Bruno possibility:** advanced by Yates, later made philosophically more plausible by Clucas, but not converted into certain textual dependence.
- **Campanella → Comenius direct line:** forcefully spread by Yates; later contextual work qualifies it.
- **Lull → computer genealogy:** popularized in twentieth- and twenty-first-century histories of combinatorics, computing and “thinking machines”; valid as conceptual ancestry only with explicit caveats.
- **Wilkins as absurd classifier:** enormously spread through Borges; literary brilliance should not replace reading Wilkins’s tables, grammar and Royal Society setting.

## Open questions

1. A modern critical edition and full English translation of Gemma’s *De arte cyclognomica* remains desirable.
2. Gemma’s diagrams need a complete census across surviving copies, including movable parts, cancellation states and reader annotations.
3. The exact readership linking Gemma to Bruno remains incompletely documented.
4. Alsted’s vast mnemonic corpus still lacks a comprehensive modern study integrating all eleven *Systema* tables with the *Clavis*, *Theatrum* and encyclopedia.
5. The publication history of the *Clavis*—1609 preface, 1633 issue, 1652 reprint—needs copy-level bibliographical clarification.
6. A systematic comparison of Alsted’s “places” across architectural loci, Ramist tables and Lullian circles would refine Yates’s central claim.
7. Direct textual evidence for Campanella’s influence on the design of *Orbis pictus* should be separated from broad pansophic affinity.
8. Early *Orbis* editions require plate-by-plate comparison; later illustrations are too often reproduced as though they were those of 1658.
9. Bisterfeld’s role between Herborn, Comenius and English projectors is still underrepresented in Anglophone accounts.
10. Dalgarno-Wilkins priority should continue to be reconstructed from correspondence and drafts, not merely from publication dates.
11. The Royal Society’s planned revision of Wilkins deserves fuller manuscript study.
12. Izquierdo’s full two-volume logic, not only Disputation XXIX, needs integration into histories of memory.
13. The exact paths by which Leibniz encountered Izquierdo require finer dating; citation, indirect knowledge and conceptual influence must be distinguished.
14. Kircher’s hand-designed diagrams, engravers’ interventions and practical assembly need material-book analysis.
15. No evidence found establishes that any of these authors performed the celebrated public memory feats associated with earlier professional mnemonists.
16. No evidence found connects the catalogue’s auctioneer, professor’s party, amnesiac student or accused magician anecdotes to this particular system.
17. “Real character” needs further comparative study as a technology of external memory, not merely as failed language planning.
18. The most productive modern comparison may be with revisable ontologies and generative notation, provided it is labeled analogy rather than direct technological descent.
