### Status of the “system”

**Documented text:** Bacon unquestionably describes the traditional architecture of artificial memory—ordered places and sensible images—and redescribes its two operative principles as *prenotion* and *emblem*.

**Important qualification:** Bacon does not provide a complete instructional manual, a numbered palace, a full image alphabet, a wheel, or a worked sequence demonstrating encoding and recall. “Bacon’s system” is therefore a convenient historiographical name for a reformulation and proposed reform of inherited mnemonic practice, not the title of a separate Baconian *ars memoriae*.

**Yates’s interpretation:** Bacon accepted and personally practised local memory, redirected it from spectacular feats toward scientific investigation, and helped transform mnemonic order into classification.

**Later correction:** Rhodri Lewis and Richard Yeo show that Bacon’s mature program for natural history distrusts unaided internal memory and depends on written records, notebooks, schedules, and “tables of discovery.” Bacon retained artificial memory as a genuine psychological phenomenon, but his research method increasingly externalized the storage and rearrangement of particulars. [Yates’s text](https://resources.warburg.sas.ac.uk/mnemosyne/fq/yates_memory.pdf), [Yeo’s study](https://research-repository.griffith.edu.au/server/api/core/bitstreams/a9e72cee-eab1-5aa0-a130-5b482e361d7a/content), [Lewis’s article record](https://philpapers.org/rec/LEWAKO).

## The system in detail

### 1. Bacon’s division of intellectual operations

Bacon correlates the three principal faculties with three great divisions of learning:

- memory with history;
- imagination with poesy;
- reason with philosophy.

Within logic—the arts governing the intellectual faculties—he distinguishes invention, judgment, memory or retention, and tradition or delivery. Memory is consequently not an isolated curiosity. It stands between judgment and delivery as the faculty that keeps available what inquiry has acquired.

In *Advancement* II.xv.1, custody or retention is divided between **writing** and **memory**. Writing itself requires:

1. characters or visible signs;
2. a good order of entry, especially a properly designed commonplace system.

Bacon values commonplace books but complains that existing headings carry “the face of a school and not of a world”: they reproduce pedagogical or verbal categories rather than divisions responsive to nature and action.

That context matters. Bacon’s artificial memory is only one part of a wider architecture of storage:

```text
Custody of knowledge
├── Writing
│   ├── characters/signs
│   └── ordered entries and commonplaces
└── Memory
    ├── natural memory
    └── artificial aids
        ├── prenotion
        └── emblem
```

### 2. Bacon’s criticism of the received art

In *Advancement* II.xv.2 Bacon says:

> “An art there is extant of it; but it seemeth to me that there are better precepts than that art, and better practices of that art than those received.”

He concedes that traditional mnemonics can be raised to “points of ostentation prodigious,” including:

- repeating a great number of names or words after one hearing;
- pouring out verses or rhymes extempore;
- making a satirical similitude of everything;
- turning everything into a jest;
- contradicting everything by cavillation.

But these feats are “barren,” because they are not readily applicable to “the serious use of business and occasions.” He compares them to tumbling, tightrope walking, and dancing: wonders of the mind corresponding to wonders of the body, “matters of strangeness without worthiness.” The complete passage is available in the [1605 text](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/5500/pg5500-images.html).

**Documented text:** Bacon condemns ostentatious mnemonic performance, not artificial memory itself.

**Yates’s inference:** The condemned displays probably include Renaissance occult mnemonic systems and their claims to prodigious power.

**Qualification:** Bacon names no mnemonic practitioner here. Bruno, Fludd, Lambert Schenkel, and occult “Magi” are not mentioned in this passage.

### 3. Prenotion

Bacon’s English formulation, *Advancement* II.xv.3, reads:

> “This art of memory is but built upon two intentions; the one prenotion, the other emblem. Prenotion dischargeth the indefinite seeking of that we would remember, and directeth us to seek in a narrow compass, that is, somewhat that hath congruity with our place of memory.”

“Intention” here means an operative direction or function of the art. “Prenotion” is not merely foreknowledge of the content. It is a constraint upon search.

The 1623 Latin expansion makes the logic more explicit:

> “Cum quis aliquid reminisci aut revocare in memoriam nititur, si nullam praenotionem habeat aut perceptionem ejus quod quaerit, quaerit certe et molitur, et hac illac discurrit, tanquam in infinito.”

Gloss: When someone tries to recollect or recall something but has no previous notion or perception of what he seeks, he searches, struggles, and runs this way and that as though in an infinite field.

Bacon calls the remedy *abscissio infiniti*, “the cutting-off” or “severance of the infinite.”

Three examples appear in *Novum organum* II.26:

1. **Order or distribution.** If the material belongs to a known series, the missing item must fit that order.
2. **Artificial-memory places.** If an image was placed in a fixed locus, the required image must agree with that locus.
3. **Verse.** If a word is missing, metre, rhythm, rhyme, syntax, or verbal sequence limits the candidates.

The original Latin is:

> “Atque ex isto manipulo trium instantiarum, videlicet ordinis, locorum artificialis memoriae, et versuum, constituitur species una auxilii ad Memoriam. Species autem illa Abscissio Infiniti recte vocari possit.”

Gloss: From this cluster of three instances—order, the places of artificial memory, and verse—one kind of aid to memory is constituted; it may rightly be called the severance of infinity. [Latin text](https://www.logicmuseum.com/authors/baconfrancis/novumorganum-II-1-37.htm).

### 4. What serves as a place

*Novum organum* II.26 provides Bacon’s most extensive list:

> “Loci in memoria artificiali, qui aut possunt esse loci secundum proprium sensum, veluti janua, angulus, fenestra, et similia, aut possunt esse personae familiares et notae, aut possunt esse quidvis ad placitum (modo in ordine certo ponantur), veluti animalia, herbae; etiam verba, literae, characteres, personae historicae, et caetera.”

Gloss: Places in artificial memory can be literal places—a door, corner, window, and the like—or familiar and known persons, or anything chosen at will, provided it is put in a fixed order: animals, plants, words, letters, characters, historical persons, and so forth.

Thus Bacon’s *locus* need not be architectural. It may be:

- a door;
- a corner;
- a window;
- a familiar person;
- a distinguishing sign or mark;
- an ordered series of animals;
- plants or herbs;
- words;
- letters;
- characters;
- historical personages;
- anything else capable of stable serial order.

The necessary property is not architecture but **fixed addressability**: *modo in ordine certo ponantur*, “provided they are placed in a certain order.”

This is closely related to the classical doctrine that places must be stable while images may be temporarily deposited and removed. Bacon does not repeat the classical rules about intervals, lighting, size, distinctness, or avoiding crowded and overly familiar public places. Those rules belong to the inherited manuals, especially *Rhetorica ad Herennium* III.29–32 and Quintilian XI.ii; they should not be attributed to Bacon unless explicitly marked as antecedents.

### 5. What serves as an image or emblem

The English definition is:

> “Emblem reduceth conceits intellectual to images sensible, which strike the memory more.”

In *De augmentis* V.v:

> “Emblema vero deducit intellectuale ad sensibile: sensibile autem semper fortius percutit memoriam, atque in ea facilius imprimitur quam intellectuale; adeo ut etiam brutorum memoria per sensibile excitetur, per intellectuale minime.”

Gloss: The emblem brings the intellectual down to the sensible; the sensible always strikes memory more forcibly and is impressed upon it more easily than the intellectual—so much so that even the memory of animals is aroused by sensible things, never by intellectual ones.

This is not “emblem” in only the narrow bibliographical sense of an emblem-book plate with motto and epigram. It is any sensory representation capable of standing for an abstract concept.

Bacon’s examples are:

> “Itaque facilius retineas imaginem venatoris leporum persequentis, aut pharmacopoei pyxides ordinantis, aut paedanti orationem habentis, aut pueri versus memoriter recitantis, aut mimi in scena agentis, quam ipsas notiones inventionis, dispositionis, elocutionis, memoriae, actionis.”

Gloss:

- a hunter pursuing a hare = **invention**;
- an apothecary arranging boxes = **disposition**;
- a schoolmaster or pedant delivering a speech = **elocution**;
- a boy reciting verses from memory = **memory**;
- an actor performing on stage = **action** or delivery.

The examples form a miniature mnemonic encoding of the five traditional parts of rhetoric: *inventio, dispositio, elocutio, memoria, actio*.

This is Bacon’s only compact worked set. He gives no numbered loci for the figures and no narrative showing a practitioner walking through them.

### 6. How order is fixed

Order can be supplied by:

- a physical route through a building;
- serial features such as doors, corners, and windows;
- an already memorized list;
- alphabetic order;
- a zoological or botanical sequence;
- a series of famous persons;
- metrical and verbal order;
- a written table or set of headings.

The fixed order performs two jobs:

1. it restricts the search space;
2. it supplies the succession in which items are retrieved.

**Documented text:** Bacon explicitly requires “a certain order.”

**Scholarly reconstruction:** Retrieval works by mentally presenting each place in sequence, allowing the place to prompt the congruent image and the image to recover its intellectual content. This follows classical practice and fits Bacon’s language, but Bacon does not himself spell out a complete traversal algorithm.

### 7. Congruity between place and image

Bacon says the image should have “congruity with our place of memory.” The Latin expansion says:

> “talem esse debere imaginem, qualis aliquatenus conveniat cum loco; id quod vellicat memoriam, et aliquo modo munit et ducit ad rem quam quaerimus.”

Gloss: the image ought in some degree to agree with the place; this plucks or twitches memory and in some way supports and leads it to the thing sought.

The key term is *vellicat*: the fit between image and locus “tugs” memory. Bacon does not require a natural or metaphysical correspondence. A practical associative fit suffices.

### 8. Bacon’s most concrete example: “men are best places”

*Sylva sylvarum* X.956 states:

> “We finde in the Art of Memory, that Images visible work better then other conceits; As if you would remember the word Philosophy, you shall more surely do it by imagining that such a Man (for Men are best places) is reading upon Aristotle’s Physicks, then if you should imagine him to say, I will go study Philosophy.”

The encoding is:

```text
Place: a particular familiar man
Visible image: that man reading Aristotle’s Physics
Target: “philosophy”
```

The weaker alternative is to imagine the man merely saying, “I will go study philosophy.” A visible action is more effective than imagined words because “the more lustrous the imagination is, it filleth and fixeth the better.” [First-edition-derived text and scan](https://echo-old.mpiwg-berlin.mpg.de/ECHOdocuView?mode=imagepath&pn=241&tocMode=figures&url=%2Fmpiwg%2Fonline%2Fpermanent%2Flibrary%2FWX8HY2V2%2Fpageimg&viewMode=text).

The exact force of “men are best places” is unusual. A person can be both the stable address and the animated bearer of the image. Bacon thereby treats familiar human figures as mnemonic loci, not merely as images placed inside architectural loci.

### 9. Retrieval

A reconstructable Baconian recall operation is:

1. Establish an ordered series of loci.
2. Translate each abstract target into a sensible, preferably visible, scene.
3. Choose a scene that has some congruity with its locus.
4. During recall, use the desired position, topic, or class as a prenotion.
5. Search only the relevant locus or bounded series.
6. Let the locus cue the image.
7. Read the abstract notion back from the image.

This is consistent with modern distinctions between retrieval cues, constrained search, and elaborative imagery, but those terms are explanatory analogies, not Bacon’s psychology.

### 10. Rules Bacon actually gives—and does not give

#### Explicit rules

- Arrange loci in a determinate order.
- A locus may be architectural, personal, verbal, alphabetic, natural-historical, or historical.
- Choose loci for convenience; Bacon admits that some kinds are more apt than others.
- Give the image some congruity with the locus.
- Convert intellectual notions into sensible images.
- Prefer visible, vivid, or “lustrous” action to verbal declaration.
- Use order to cut off an indefinite search.
- Adapt the art to useful business rather than spectacular performance.
- Do not entrust long and exact scientific work to unaided memory; use writing and ordered tables.

#### Not supplied by Bacon

- a canonical number of loci;
- prescribed intervals between loci;
- rules for lighting, size, solitude, or architectural perspective;
- a permanent image alphabet;
- methods for erasing and reusing images;
- instructions for verbatim memorization;
- a wheel or combinatorial mechanism;
- talismanic or astrological image lists;
- a complete curriculum of practice;
- evidence of named students trained by Bacon.

### 11. Diagrams, wheels, tables, and plates

**Finding:** None of Bacon’s four principal mnemonic passages contains a mnemonic diagram, wheel, table, or plate.

The *Novum organum* first edition has its famous engraved title page showing a ship passing beyond the Pillars of Hercules. That is a programmatic emblem of intellectual discovery, not a diagram of prenotion-and-emblem memory.

Bacon’s “tables” belong to inductive inquiry—notably tables of presence, absence, and degrees—not to an illustrated memory palace. Their relationship to artificial memory is interpretive:

- Rossi treated the written tables as a rationalized, externalized successor to mnemonic places.
- Yates spoke more loosely of mnemonic order becoming “something like classification.”
- Yeo emphasizes that tables relieve internal memory and permit continual rearrangement, comparison, and correction.

- James Spedding, Robert Leslie Ellis, and Douglas Denon Heath, eds., *The Works of Francis Bacon*, vol. III. London, 1857–74; memory discussion at pp. 398–400.

## 3. *De dignitate et augmentis scientiarum* (1623)

First edition:

> *Francisci Baronis de Verulamio, Vice-Comitis Sancti Albani, De dignitate et augmentis scientiarum, libri IX*. London: John Haviland, 1623.

This is not a simple translation of the *Advancement*. It is a revised and greatly enlarged Latin recasting. William Rawley assisted with the Latin, in close collaboration with Bacon. The art of memory appears in V.v, Spedding I, pp. 648–49.

Standard edition:

- Spedding, Ellis, and Heath, *Works*, I, especially pp. 643–49.
- English translation in *Works*, IV, pp. 435–37.

The principal variation from 1605 is expansion. The Latin version explains prenotion through:

- ordered prose and verse;
- prearranged mnemonic places;
- the *abscissio infiniti*;
- the five images for the five rhetorical offices.

It also begins with a stronger warning that writing is indispensable for long, exact, inductive research.

An accessible scan of Spedding’s Latin text contains the relevant passage at p. 649: [*Works*, vol. I](https://proyectoscio.ucv.es/wp-content/uploads/2015/06/Bacon-Works1.pdf).

## 4. *Sylva sylvarum* (1627)

First edition:

> *Sylva Sylvarum: or A Naturall Historie in Ten Centuries*. London: printed by J. H. for William Lee, 1627; published posthumously by William Rawley.

The mnemonic observation is experiment 956 in Century X. It is embedded in a series of inquiries into imagination, fascination, the “binding of thoughts,” dreams, and action at a distance.

Textual caution is necessary because Rawley organized and published the work after Bacon’s death. Recent scholarship treats the *Sylva* as a layered working natural history, not a polished treatise. Nothing in the publication history, however, gives positive reason to reject experiment 956 as Baconian.

## 5. John Aubrey, *Brief Lives*

Aubrey composed his biographical collections between 1669 and 1696. The principal manuscript witnesses are among his papers at the Bodleian Library; Andrew Clark’s edition was explicitly edited from Aubrey’s manuscripts:

- John Aubrey, *“Brief Lives,” Chiefly of Contemporaries*, ed. Andrew Clark, 2 vols. Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1898.
- Oliver Lawson Dick, ed., *Aubrey’s Brief Lives*. London, 1949; revised reprints. Bacon at p. 14 in the edition cited by Yates.

Aubrey writes:

> “Over this Portico is a stately Gallerie, whose Glasse-windowes are all painted: and every pane with severall figures of beest, bird, or flower: perhaps his Lordship might use them as Topiques for Locall memorie.”

He continues:

> “Here his Lordship much meditated, his servant Mr. Bushell attending him with his pen and inke horne to sett downe his present Notions.”

The full description also includes emblematic paintings with mottoes, life-size portraits, and a semicylindrical roof painted with Greek and Roman emperors and heroes. [Aubrey’s text](https://home.hiwaay.net/~paul/bacon/biographies/aubrey.html), [Clark’s first volume](https://archive.org/details/briefliveschiefl01aubruoft).

### The evidentiary status of Gorhambury

- The painted gallery is **documented by Aubrey**, writing decades after Bacon’s death.
- Its use as mnemonic loci is **Aubrey’s conjecture**, signalled by “perhaps.”
- The statement that Bacon “himself used” the windows is **Yates’s stronger inference**.
- The claim that the building was “designed for local memory” is **not demonstrated** by architectural documents.
- Architectural history indicates that the gallery wing was substantially the work of Sir Nicholas Bacon and existed by the 1570s, before Francis inherited Gorhambury. Francis added the portico and altered the estate, but ownership does not prove that he commissioned every painted pane. [Historic England](https://historicengland.org.uk/listing/the-list/list-entry/1000417), [English Heritage](https://www.english-heritage.org.uk/visit/places/old-gorhambury-house/history/).

No secure surviving plan maps individual panes into an operative mnemonic order. Some painted glass associated with Gorhambury survives, but no complete Baconian memory itinerary has been reconstructed.

## Yates’s account and the scholarship since

## 1. What Yates says

In chapter XVII, “The Art of Memory and the Growth of Scientific Method,” Yates argues that seventeenth-century “method” emerged in a culture still shaped by Ramism, Lullism, Cabala, and artificial memory.

Her Baconian claims are:

1. Bacon had “a very full knowledge” of artificial memory.
2. He “himself used it.”
3. Aubrey’s Gorhambury description is rare evidence of architecture designed for local memory.
4. Prenotion and emblem are Bacon’s version of places and images.
5. Bacon accepts the ancient doctrine that an active image strikes memory.
6. His sensible-image theory agrees with the Thomistic maxim that intellectual things are remembered through sensible likenesses.
7. Because Bacon retains images, he is influenced by Ramism but is not a strict Ramist.
8. The art is redirected from prodigious display toward business and scientific inquiry.
9. Its ordered arrangement of particulars becomes “something like classification.”
10. Bacon generally excludes occult memory, yet *Sylva* 956 retains an ambiguous connection between vivid mnemonic images and the “force of imagination.”
11. Bacon’s real characters contribute to the universal-language movement.
12. Bacon’s hostility to Lull’s impostural method does not prevent elements of inventive logic and combinatorial aspiration from reaching later thinkers, especially Leibniz.

Yates frankly admits a central limitation:

> “How he proposed to improve it is not clear.”

That admission should govern reconstruction. [Chapter XVII online](https://resources.warburg.sas.ac.uk/mnemosyne/fq/yates_memory.pdf).

## 2. Paolo Rossi

Rossi’s work preceded Yates:

- *Francesco Bacone: dalla magia alla scienza* (Bari, 1957);
- *Clavis universalis: arti della memoria e logica combinatoria da Lullo a Leibniz* (Milan/Naples, 1960);
- English translations: *Francis Bacon: From Magic to Science* and *Logic and the Art of Memory*.

Rossi’s major contribution was to connect:

```text
mnemonic places
→ topical order
→ tables and classifications
→ universal characters
→ combinatorial logic
```

Rossi is more attentive than Yates to the externalization of knowledge. For him Bacon largely transfers the organizing function of mnemonic places into written tables and classificatory instruments.

**Confirmed:** Bacon does connect memory, ordered commonplaces, written tables, and the management of particulars.

**Corrected:** The phrase “mnemonic order becomes classification” risks suggesting that Bacon expected the investigator to memorize natural history internally. His explicit rule is nearly the opposite: natural and experimental history is too copious and exact for “naked” memory and must be written.

## 3. Walter J. Ong and Ramism

Ong’s *Ramus, Method, and the Decay of Dialogue* (1958) describes Ramist method as a visual, dichotomizing organization of knowledge on the page. Ramist charts function mnemonically while suppressing the classical apparatus of corporeal images.

Yates used Ong to sharpen the contrast:

- Ramism: order, spatialized print, dichotomies, few or no fantastic images;
- Bacon: ordered analysis plus continued endorsement of sensible images.

This establishes that Bacon is not simply a Ramist. Yet the contrast is not absolute. Bacon’s written heads, tables, and distributive structures share early modern habits of spatial organization even while rejecting the rigidity of Ramist partitions.

## 4. Mary Carruthers

Carruthers’s *The Book of Memory* (1990; 2nd ed. 2008) and *The Craft of Thought* (1998) revise the large historical framework in which Bacon should be placed. Memory in medieval culture was not passive storage but an instrument of composition, judgment, meditation, and invention. Text, page layout, images, and architecture were cooperating instruments rather than opposed “internal” and “external” media.

This corrects any simple narrative in which:

```text
medieval memory = rote storage
Renaissance memory = magic
Bacon = modern discovery
```

Bacon’s claim that ordered custody enables invention has deep classical and medieval precedents. His novelty lies less in discovering that memory can think than in subordinating records to a collective, revisable program of natural inquiry. [Carruthers overview](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/book-of-memory/introduction/B185E76088F1B134F33C4133B4D17E95).

## 5. Lina Bolzoni and the culture of images

Bolzoni’s *La stanza della memoria* and *The Gallery of Memory* document the dense interaction of preaching, rhetoric, emblem books, galleries, literary composition, and visible images in sixteenth-century Italy.

Applied to Bacon, this scholarship cautions against treating “emblem” as a merely private mental picture. Bacon inhabited a material culture of:

- painted galleries;
- devices and impresa;
- emblematic page design;
- mottoes;
- commonplace books;
- theatrical and courtly spectacle.

It strengthens the plausibility that Bacon thought with emblematic forms. It does not prove a particular Gorhambury mnemonic practice.

## 6. Jocelyn Penny Small

Small’s *Wax Tablets of the Mind* (1997) uses cognitive evidence to reassess classical claims about literacy, mental imagery, and verbatim recall. Her findings reinforce distinctions between:

- remembering order;
- remembering subject matter;
- reconstructing verbal expression;
- memorizing exact words.

For Bacon, this helps explain why verse, orderly loci, and concrete imagery constrain retrieval without necessarily storing a transcript. Small does not establish a separate Baconian system.

## 7. Richard Yeo

Yeo’s “Between Memory and Paperbooks” (2007) is the clearest correction to Yates and Rossi on scientific use.

Bacon writes in *Novum organum* I.101 that even after a suitable natural history has been collected, the intellect cannot handle it “off hand and by memory” any more than one can calculate an ephemeris by memory. Inquiry must proceed in writing, with materials arranged in “Tables of Discovery.”

Yeo concludes that Bacon moved toward:

- external storage;
- revisable written records;
- tables and schedules;
- collaborative collection;
- comparison of particulars;
- relief of individual memory.

Yeo nevertheless identifies tensions. Bacon encouraged collective natural history but distrusted delegated commonplacing, because “one man’s Notes will little profit another” when their “conceits” differ. [Yeo, pp. 8–13](https://research-repository.griffith.edu.au/server/api/core/bitstreams/a9e72cee-eab1-5aa0-a130-5b482e361d7a/content).

## 8. Rhodri Lewis

Rhodri Lewis, “A Kind of Sagacity: Francis Bacon, the *Ars Memoriae* and the Pursuit of Natural Knowledge,” *Intellectual History Review* 19.2 (2009), pp. 155–175, gives the most directly focused post-Yates treatment.

Lewis situates Bacon’s mnemonic ideas within early seventeenth-century English mnemonic manuals, rhetoric, natural history, and “sagacity”—the trained ability to detect relations among particulars. He resists equating the scientific method with a memory palace. Artificial memory supplies models of ordered retrieval and associative acuity, but paper technologies are indispensable to Bacon’s inquiry.

## 9. Andrew Hiscock

Hiscock’s “‘This art of memory’: Francis Bacon, memory and the discourses of power” places memory within Bacon’s political, rhetorical, and epistemological career. Bacon’s control of records, precedents, historical examples, and counsel is part of the practical “business and occasions” against which he measures mnemonic worth. [Hiscock chapter](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/299A38C2CC9FF3994DA9845326F205E2/9781139028035c8_p219-245_CBO.pdf/this_art_of_memory_francis_bacon_memory_and_the_discourses_of_power.pdf).

## 10. Yates’s later qualification

Reviewing the English translation of Rossi in 1968, Yates reaffirmed the transition from magic toward science but made its ambiguity more explicit. Bacon, she wrote, stands “between two worlds”: he knows Renaissance imaginative magic but begins to detach himself from it. She also conceded that Rossi’s work had priority in identifying the Baconian transformation of memory and classification. [“Bacon’s Magic”](https://www.nybooks.com/articles/1968/02/29/bacons-magic/).

## 11. Relevance of the other named scholars

- Hajdu’s history of mnemonics belongs to the broad historiography of the art but does not overturn the textual account of Bacon.
- Sabbatino’s work on Italian Renaissance memory and literary culture contextualizes the emblematic and rhetorical background rather than documenting Baconian practice.
- Bonner and Fidora correct older pictures of genuine Lullism: Llull’s art is a logical and demonstrative procedure grounded in divine dignities, not simply a revolving memory machine. This makes Bacon’s attack on “Raymundus Lullius” partly an attack on Renaissance and pseudo-Lullian reception.
- Sturlese, Ciliberto, Clucas, and Gatti have complicated Yates’s strongly Hermetic reading of Bruno. Bruno’s mnemonic works involve logic, ontology, psychology, rhetoric, and combinatorics as well as magic. This weakens any easy equation “Brunian memory = occult imposture” behind Bacon’s unnamed criticism.
- Bolzoni and Stagno have greatly refined reconstruction of Camillo’s theatre from manuscripts, publications, and court culture. Their work underscores how much more fully specified Camillo’s architectural-cosmological program is than Bacon’s two principles.
- None of this later work produces evidence that Bacon possessed a hidden wheel, full palace, or unpublished mnemonic manual.

## History and context

### Classical rhetoric

Bacon’s immediate technical inheritance is the classical *loci et imagines* tradition:

- *Rhetorica ad Herennium* III.16–24;
- Cicero, *De oratore* II.350–360;
- Quintilian, *Institutio oratoria* XI.ii.

The traditional analogy is:

```text
places = wax tablets or writing surface
images = letters written upon them
```

The famous lawsuit image in the *Ad Herennium*—a sick man in bed, the accused at his side holding a cup in one hand and specified objects in the other—is not quoted by Bacon. It remains an antecedent example of converting the circumstances of a case into one shocking composite image.

Bacon preserves the classical core but reduces it to two psychological functions: bounded search and sensible impact.

### Aristotelian and scholastic psychology

Aristotle held that thinking does not occur without a *phantasma*, a sense-derived mental presentation. Thomas Aquinas therefore approved corporeal similitudes for remembering intellectual things.

Yates identifies Bacon’s emblem with this Thomistic principle. That is historically plausible, but Bacon explains it naturalistically: sensible things impress both human and animal memory more readily. He does not cite Aquinas in the mnemonic passage.

### Humanism, rhetoric, law, and the court

Bacon was trained at Trinity College, Cambridge, and Gray’s Inn and spent his career in law, counsel, Parliament, and royal administration. These settings demanded:

- command of precedents;
- retrieval of commonplaces;
- extempore speech;
- orderly briefs;
- management of written records;
- emblematic and ceremonial presentation.

His phrase “business and occasions” is therefore concrete. Artificial memory had to help counsel, inquiry, administration, or composition—not merely astonish an audience.

### Ramism

Bacon accepted orderly distribution but rejected systems that impose premature or schoolish partitions. He was influenced by the culture of method associated with Ramus without accepting Ramist binary method as the sovereign logic of discovery.

### Lullism

Bacon’s *Advancement* II.xvii.14 calls the art attributed to Ramon Llull:

> “not a lawful method, but a method of imposture.”

He says it enables people “speedily” to make a show of learning and compares its collections of terms to a second-hand clothing shop containing scraps of everything and nothing of worth.

**Documented:** Bacon rejects what he calls Llull’s art.

**Correction from modern Lull studies:** His caricature fits late medieval and Renaissance Lullist, pseudo-Lullist, and encyclopedic adaptations better than Llull’s original Catalan and Latin logical projects.

### Hermeticism and natural magic

Bacon rejects the Paracelsian overextension of the microcosm doctrine and attacks magical systems built on untested correspondences. Nevertheless, he retains a category of legitimate “natural magic”: knowledge of hidden forms and powers derived from sound natural philosophy.

His attitude is discriminating rather than simply anti-magical:

- ceremonial and superstitious claims are suspect;
- reports of fascination or imagination may contain natural effects;
- experiment should separate operative fact from fable;
- mnemonic images work through sense and imagination, without requiring astral talismans.

### Universal language and real characters

Bacon distinguishes ordinary words from signs capable of expressing things or notions across languages. Chinese characters and numerical signs serve as analogies for characters readable despite linguistic difference.

Rossi and Yates connect this demand with the mnemonic search for stable signs. Later universal-language schemes by George Dalgarno and John Wilkins combine:

- taxonomic organization;
- “real characters”;
- concise notation;
- mnemonic assistance;
- aspirations to discovery.

**Documented influence:** Bacon was an important authority for seventeenth-century universal-language advocates.

**Disputed extension:** Memory art was one contributing tradition, not necessarily the single cause of the universal-language movement. Contemporary logic, mathematics, cryptography, pedagogy, missionary linguistics, and print culture were also decisive.

## People

### Francis Bacon

Author of the formulations, but not securely documented as a teacher of an organized Baconian mnemonic school.

### William Rawley

Bacon’s chaplain, literary executor, and editor. Rawley helped prepare the Latin *De augmentis* and published *Sylva sylvarum* after Bacon’s death. His role is essential to the transmission of the expanded and posthumous evidence.

### John Aubrey

Antiquary and biographer whose later description preserves the Gorhambury evidence. His wording distinguishes observation from speculation: the painted windows existed; their mnemonic use was “perhaps.”

### Thomas Bushell

Aubrey reports that Bushell attended Bacon with pen and inkhorn while Bacon meditated in the gallery. This supports Bacon’s dependence on amanuensis and writing more directly than it proves mental use of the windows.

### Sir Nicholas Bacon

Francis’s father and builder of the older Gorhambury house. Since the gallery wing predates Francis’s ownership, Nicholas is an important complication in attributing its entire visual program to Francis.

### James I

Patron and dedicatee of the *Advancement* and *Novum organum*. Bacon’s intellectual reform was framed as a royal and institutional project, not a private feat of memory.

### Anthony Bacon

Francis’s brother and predecessor in ownership of Gorhambury. His place in the estate’s history further cautions against treating every feature as Francis’s personal mnemonic commission.

### Ramus, Llull, Bruno, Fludd, Schenkel

These are comparative figures, not documented Baconian teachers:

- Ramus: method without traditional images;
- Llull: combinatorial logical art, harshly criticized by Bacon;
- Bruno: complex image-based memory, logic, and magic;
- Fludd: cosmic and theatrical memory contemporary with Bacon;
- Schenkel: popular performer and teacher of local memory, directly discussed by Descartes but not by Bacon in the cited texts.

### The requested anecdotes

- **Auctioneer:** Quintilian’s auctioneer recalls sold objects by returning mentally to their locations. It is a classical example, not a Bacon anecdote.
- **Professor’s party or classroom performance:** Yates uses examples of mnemonic display in earlier chapters; no securely documented Baconian professor demonstrates prenotions and emblems at a party.
- **Student who lost his memory:** No such person is attached to Bacon’s system in the consulted primary sources.
- **Accused magician:** Bacon was not prosecuted for mnemonic magic. The best-known accusations in the larger tradition concern figures such as Giordano Bruno, John Dee, and memory teachers suspected of occult practice; none proves consequences for Bacon’s method.
- **Gorhambury meditation:** The strongest Baconian anecdote is Aubrey’s image of Bacon meditating while Bushell writes down his “present Notions.”

Absence here is substantive: the Baconian system lacks the lineage of named pupils, trial narratives, and performance testimonials found around Pietro da Ravenna, Schenkel, Bruno, or later mnemonists.

## The magic question

### Was the system itself magical?

On the direct evidence, no supernatural operation is required:

- prenotion constrains search;
- order cues sequence;
- sensible images produce stronger impressions;
- emotional force and vividness aid retention.

These are psychological claims.

### Why Yates detects a magical residue

Experiment 956 occurs amid investigations of “the force of imagination.” Bacon considers card tricks, the binding of thoughts, fascination, sympathy, dreams, effects on sleepers, and possible action on living bodies. He says visible mnemonic images work better and proposes transferring that observation to experiments concerning suggestion.

Yates therefore argues that Bacon still sees connections between mnemonic images and a Renaissance tradition in which imagination could exert real power.

### Necessary distinction

Bacon does not say in experiment 956 that a mnemonic image:

- draws down a planetary power;
- functions as a talisman;
- acts at a distance;
- changes external nature;
- invokes a spirit.

Instead, he proposes experimental inquiry into whether imagination has effects and repeatedly notes uncertainty. His phrase “I have no opinion of these things” is important. He is collecting trials rather than certifying occult causation.

### Heresy and the Inquisition

There is no evidence that Bacon’s prenotion-and-emblem doctrine was condemned as heretical, investigated by the Inquisition, or placed on an index. Bacon’s political fall in 1621 arose from bribery charges, not mnemonic or magical practice.

### Ars notoria

The medieval *Ars notoria* promised infused knowledge through prayers, figures, and ritual observances. Bacon’s method has no documented ritual connection with it. Both concern extraordinary intellectual acquisition, but similarity of ambition is not evidence of transmission.

### Rosicrucian and cipher legends

Later Baconian and occult literature has attributed to Bacon:

- Rosicrucian leadership;
- secret societies;
- hidden ciphers;
- Shakespearean authorship;
- concealed teachings encoded in typography and emblems.

These are later traditions or legends. They do not supply evidence for a hidden mnemonic system behind prenotion and emblem. The first edition of *De augmentis* does contain Bacon’s discussion of biliteral cipher, but that is cryptography, not proof that every typographical variation conceals a message.

## Afterlife

### Seventeenth-century scientific information management

The most defensible Baconian afterlife lies in:

- paper books and commonplace systems;
- natural-history questionnaires;
- tabular records;
- collaborative collecting;
- the Hartlib circle;
- the Oxford Philosophical Society;
- the Royal Society;
- Robert Boyle and Robert Hooke.

Yeo shows that later Baconians debated whether information should be condensed into memory or made retrievable through external indexes and tables. Hooke ultimately confronted the mismatch between the immense quantity of scientific particulars and the limited capacity of individual memory.

### Universal languages

Comenius, Samuel Hartlib, George Dalgarno, Seth Ward, and John Wilkins pursued taxonomic signs and universal characters. Bacon’s criticism of ordinary language and call for signs of things helped legitimate the project.

The mnemonic hope was that a rational character would simultaneously:

- name a thing;
- show its taxonomic place;
- make its essential relations intelligible;
- facilitate learning and recollection.

### Lull to Leibniz to machines

The usual genealogy is:

```text
Llull’s finite letters and combinatorial figures
→ Renaissance Lullism and encyclopedic arts
→ Baconian invention, classification, and real characters
→ seventeenth-century universal languages
→ Leibniz’s characteristica universalis and calculus ratiocinator
→ later histories of symbolic logic and computation
```

This is an intellectual genealogy, not a direct engineering lineage. Llull did not construct a computer; Bacon did not design a formal calculus; Leibniz’s calculating machines belong to a related but distinct history of mechanical arithmetic.

Bonner and Fidora’s scholarship requires distinguishing genuine Llull from pseudo-Lullian universal-memory and alchemical texts. Modern claims that Bacon’s mnemonic art “invented computing” are retrospective simplifications.

### Emblem books, galleries, and visual culture

Bacon’s emblematic examples belong to the same visual economy as:

- Andrea Alciato’s emblem tradition;
- courtly devices and imprese;
- painted galleries;
- illustrated natural histories;
- Comenius’s *Orbis sensualium pictus*.

Direct influence from Bacon’s mnemonic paragraph on a particular artwork is rarely demonstrable.

### Dante and fresco cycles

Dante, medieval preaching imagery, and didactic frescoes are antecedent or parallel traditions, not Bacon’s afterlife. Carruthers and Bolzoni make plausible the general view that ordered visual programs could function as instruments of memory and meditation. They do not turn every allegorical cycle into a designed Baconian palace.

### Borges and Eco

Borges’s “Funes the Memorious” explores unlimited, unclassified memory: Funes cannot readily abstract because every particular remains irreducibly distinct. This is a striking conceptual inverse of Bacon’s *abscissio infiniti*, but no direct Baconian borrowing is established.

Umberto Eco’s work on encyclopedias, signs, lists, perfect languages, and labyrinths intersects the Bacon–Llull–Wilkins–Leibniz genealogy. His *The Search for the Perfect Language* treats the universal-language tradition directly. Literary resonance should be distinguished from documentary influence by this specific mnemonic passage.

### Contemporary memory palaces and athletes

Modern memory athletes employ:

- ordered routes;
- person-action-object scenes;
- exaggerated sensory imagery;
- familiar persons as pegs;
- constrained retrieval.

These procedures resemble Bacon’s principles, particularly “men are best places,” but normally descend through the continuous classical and popular mnemonic tradition rather than through documented direct study of Bacon.

Terms such as “retrieval cue,” “search-set reduction,” “elaborative encoding,” and “dual coding” make Bacon intelligible to modern readers. They are modern interpretations, not historical components of his art.

## Controversies and disputes

### 1. Did Bacon devise a distinct system?

**Documented:** He renamed and analyzed two governing functions of artificial memory.

**Against a strong attribution:** He says the art already exists, supplies no full manual, and promises only that better practice can be drawn from his axioms.

**Conclusion:** “Bacon’s art” is best understood as a reform program and conceptual analysis, not a wholly invented mnemonic architecture.

### 2. Did Bacon practise local memory?

**Evidence for:** Aubrey’s gallery report; Bacon’s precise technical knowledge; *Sylva* 956’s practical example.

**Evidence against certainty:** Aubrey says “perhaps”; he wrote decades later; no mnemonic notebook, route, or testimony by Bacon identifies the panes as his loci.

**Status:** Plausible but unproved.

### 3. Was Gorhambury designed as a memory palace?

**Yates:** treats it as rare evidence of a building designed for local memory.

**Correction:** The observed paintings are real, but their mnemonic purpose is conjectural; important portions of the house and gallery originated under Sir Nicholas Bacon.

**Status:** Disputed reconstruction, often repeated as fact.

### 4. Did artificial memory become scientific classification?

**Yates:** yes, “something like classification.”

**Rossi:** mnemonic places are rationalized into tables organizing reality.

**Yeo and Lewis:** Bacon’s science requires external records precisely because internal memory cannot retain and rearrange sufficient particulars.

**Best synthesis:** The functional ideas of address, order, bounded search, and retrieval help illuminate Bacon’s information practices. But the tables of induction are not simply memory palaces on paper, and Bacon’s natural histories were not conventional taxonomies.

### 5. Did Bacon reject images?

No. His definition of emblem, five rhetorical examples, and *Sylva* 956 explicitly endorse sensible and visible images.

He nevertheless rejects dependence on inner imagery for large-scale natural history. Acceptance of image-based mnemonics and insistence on written scientific records coexist.

### 6. Was Bacon a Ramist?

Not in any strict sense. He shares Ramist concern with method, distribution, and visual order but rejects rigid anticipatory divisions and retains sensible images.

### 7. Was Bacon attacking Bruno or Fludd?

Yates suggests that his attack on prodigious ostentation and the strained microcosm had occult mnemonic systems in view.

No cited Bacon passage names Bruno or Fludd in this connection. Fludd’s major memory publications are contemporary with, not clear sources for, the 1605 formulation.

**Status:** Contextually plausible but undocumented identification.

### 8. Was Bacon’s “emblem” talismanic?

No direct evidence supports talismanic activation. Bacon’s examples are ordinary narrative images: hunting, arranging boxes, speaking, reciting, acting.

Yates’s association with magical images is a broad historical interpretation prompted by the *Sylva* context, not a property specified in the mnemonic rules.

### 9. Does “real character” descend directly from occult signs?

Rossi and Yates trace a meaningful continuity from mnemonic and magical signs to rational universal characters. Later scholars accept overlap but resist monocausal descent. Mathematics, cryptography, comparative language, pedagogy, taxonomy, and print all contributed.

### 10. Did Bacon’s method directly produce Leibnizian computation?

Leibniz read and engaged Baconian projects, Lullism, universal-language schemes, and combinatorics. A direct straight line from prenotion and emblem to digital computing is modern teleology.

### 11. Are there lost diagrams or manuscript variants?

No evidence located in the consulted catalogues establishes:

- a lost Baconian mnemonic wheel;
- a complete diagrammatic system;
- authorial manuscript variants materially changing prenotion and emblem;
- a secret image alphabet;
- a school of Baconian mnemonic students.

The principal textual development is visible between the brief English statement of 1605 and its Latin expansion in 1623, with the independent natural-philosophical treatment in *Novum organum* II.26.

## Open questions

1. Who supplied the exact Latin phrasing of *De augmentis* V.v—Bacon, Rawley, or collaborative revision—and do surviving working papers permit finer attribution?

2. Can the surviving Gorhambury glass be securely dated, commissioned, and assigned between Sir Nicholas, Anthony, and Francis Bacon?

3. Is it possible to reconstruct the original order of the gallery panes? Without their serial order, mnemonic use cannot be tested.

4. Did Bacon’s servants or amanuenses leave evidence describing his compositional routines beyond Aubrey’s Bushell anecdote?

5. Which contemporary English mnemonic manuals Bacon read remains incompletely documented. Verbal parallels with period textbooks establish familiarity with the tradition, not necessarily a particular source.

6. How far does *prenotion* derive from scholastic accounts of recollection and how far from topical rhetoric?

7. Does *abscissio infiniti* describe mnemonic recall only, or is it a local expression of Bacon’s more general method of restricting inquiry through ordered instances?

8. How should Bacon’s praise of striking images be reconciled with his warnings against idols, premature abstractions, and the seductive power of fables?

9. Is “classification” the right term for Bacon’s tables? Recent natural-history scholarship stresses that he often resisted traditional taxonomies in favor of histories of qualities, motions, processes, and experiments.

10. How much did later Baconians inherit from Bacon himself and how much did they retroactively label existing commonplace, tabular, and mnemonic practices “Baconian”?

11. Can reception studies identify readers who annotated the memory passages in surviving copies of the 1605, 1620, or 1623 editions?

12. The major negative finding remains: no evidence currently supports a fully specified secret Baconian memory palace beyond the published principles and Aubrey’s conjecture.
