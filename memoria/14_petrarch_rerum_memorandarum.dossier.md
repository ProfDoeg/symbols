# Petrarch's *Rerum memorandarum libri*: Research Dossier

## Basic identification

| Field | Identification |
|---|---|
| **Name** | *Rerum memorandarum libri* (“Books of Memorable Things”; also transmitted as *De rebus memorandis*) |
| **Exponent** | Francesco Petrarca / Petrarch (1304–1374) |
| **Date** | Begun in 1343, probably in spring or summer; substantially abandoned by early 1345. “c. 1343–45” is the safest concise date. |
| **Source text** | Petrarch, *Rerum memorandarum libri*, especially I, proem and 1–37; II.1–13, *De memoria*; critical ed. Giuseppe Billanovich, Florence: Sansoni, 1945 [often bibliographically dated 1943], pp. 44–45 and 60; modern Latin–Italian ed. Marco Petoletti, Florence: Le Lettere, 2014. |
| **Principle in one sentence** | Petrarch arranges morally memorable exempla under a Ciceronian hierarchy of virtues—beginning with Prudence divided into memory of the past, understanding of the present, and foresight of the future—but the surviving text supplies examples of memory and notices of artificial memory, not a working place-and-image mnemonic system. |
| **Yates** | Frances A. Yates, *The Art of Memory* (1966), ch. IV, pp. 101–104; ch. V, p. 115; ch. XIV, p. 314. |

### Evidentiary conclusion

**[Documented text]** The *Rerum memorandarum libri* is an unfinished ethical-exemplary compilation whose architecture derives from Cicero’s classification of the virtues. Its section *De memoria* distinguishes memory for things from memory for words and includes several classical anecdotes involving natural and artificial memory.

**[Not documented]** The surviving work does **not** prescribe mnemonic buildings, ordered loci, image construction, image placement, interval rules, illumination rules, alphabetic images, a retrieval walk, diagrams, wheels, or tables.

**[Early-modern tradition]** Johannes Romberch nevertheless listed Petrarch among writers on the art and attached his name to rules about the order, dimensions, and number of mnemonic places.

**[Yates’s interpretation]** Yates inferred that the book was “designed for memorisation” and imagined that Petrarch may have represented virtues as pagan or triumphal images.

**[Current assessment]** The book can securely be called a morally ordered repository of things worth remembering and evidence of Petrarch’s familiarity with classical discourse about artificial memory. Calling it a reconstructable Petrarchan “system of artificial memory” goes beyond the surviving evidence.

## The system in detail

## 1. What Petrarch actually devised

### 1.1 The macrostructure

The work begins with what Billanovich called the *praeludia virtutum*, the conditions or preliminaries of virtuous intellectual life:

1. *otium* — leisure;
2. *solitudo* — solitude;
3. *studium* — study;
4. *doctrina* — learning or instruction.

Petrarch then proceeds to Prudence, the first cardinal virtue. The governing division is stated at II.1:

> **“Hec … trifariam … diducitur in preteritorum memoriam, presentium intelligentiam et providentiam futurorum.”**

“Prudence is divided threefold into memory of the past, understanding of the present, and foresight of the future.”  
—*Rerum memorandarum libri* II.1.2; Billanovich, p. 44; online Latin text in [Book II](https://la.wikisource.org/wiki/Rerum_memorandarum_libri/II).

**[Documented text]** This comes from Cicero, *De inventione* II.160: *prudentia* consists of *memoria, intelligentia,* and *providentia*. It is a logical and ethical division, not itself a mnemonic itinerary.

Petrarch announces that he will proceed “by parts”:

> **“Et quoniam per partes commodissime omnis tractatus absolvitur, a memoria quidem … initium repetendum est.”**

“Since every treatment is most conveniently completed part by part, a beginning must be made from memory.”

That phrase supplies the actual mechanism of textual order: hierarchical division followed by serial exempla.

### 1.2 Intended complete architecture

Billanovich’s reconstruction, accepted in broad outline by Yates and later editors, is:

- preliminaries to virtue;
- Prudence:
  - memory;
  - understanding;
  - foresight;
- Justice;
- Fortitude;
- Temperance;
- probably corresponding vices after the virtues.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** Only a fraction was completed. The extant four books do not complete Prudence in the originally projected proportions; Justice and Fortitude are absent, while only a fragment associated with Temperance survives. Consequently, neither the final scale nor every subdivision can be recovered.

**[Yates’s interpretation]** Because Bartolomeo da San Concordio’s *Ammaestramenti degli antichi* employs a similar progression—preliminaries, virtues, and vices—and places artificial-memory advice under memory, Yates treated Petrarch’s compilation as a parallel ethical work designed for memorization.

**[Qualification]** Structural comparability does not prove identical mnemonic practice. Petrarch’s text never directs a reader to translate this hierarchy into loci and images.

## 2. What serves as “places”

### 2.1 In Petrarch’s surviving instructions

Nothing is explicitly designated as a series of artificial-memory loci.

There is no rule corresponding to:

- choose a building;
- mark five or ten stations;
- keep the stations distinct;
- traverse them in a fixed direction;
- place one striking image in each;
- revisit the circuit to retrieve the subject matter.

**[Absence of evidence]** No surviving passage in the *Rerum memorandarum libri* gives such directions. Neither Billanovich’s critical text nor the modern complete edition identifies a lost diagram or an incomplete locus chapter.

### 2.2 The book’s headings as textual “places”

One may describe the hierarchy of headings—virtue, part of virtue, Roman examples, foreign examples, modern examples—as textual compartments. The *De memoria* section, for example, proceeds through:

- Roman examples;
- external or Greek examples;
- modern examples.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** These headings could aid retrieval in a codex or in learned recall. Medieval and Renaissance readers frequently used rubrics, divisions, marginal signs, excerpt books, and topical classifications as mnemonic supports.

**[Important distinction]** Calling a rubric a “place” in a broad sense is reasonable; identifying it as one of Petrarch’s imagined architectural *loci* is not documented.

### 2.3 Places attributed to Petrarch by Romberch

Romberch’s *Congestorium artificiose memorie* appeared in 1520; the accessible scan and Wikisource transcription used here reproduce the Venice, Melchiorre Sessa, 1533 edition.

Romberch gives Petrarch three roles:

1. an author who recommends strengthening weak memory by diligence and art;
2. one of the recognized writers or authorities on memory;
3. a follower of the rule requiring numerous mnemonic places.

In the second treatise, chapter 3, Romberch writes, in abbreviated print:

> **“Divus Aquinas multos habendos consulit locos … [quem] … Franciscus Petrarcha, Petrus Ravennas … sequuntur.”**

“Saint Thomas counsels that many places should be possessed … and Francis Petrarch, Peter of Ravenna … follow him.”  
—Romberch, *Congestorium*, tract II, ch. 3, ff. 19v–20r in Yates’s citation; [transcription](https://la.wikisource.org/wiki/Congestorium_Artificiosae_Memoriae/Tractatus_Secundus/Capitulum_3).

The chapter explains the rationale: writers who wish to write more require a larger tablet, wax surface, or paper; likewise, a person wishing to retain many things requires many places.

**[Documented Romberch]** Romberch explicitly associates Petrarch with multiplicity of loci.

**[Textual problem]** Aquinas, *Summa theologiae* II–II, q.49, a.1, does not prescribe a numerical abundance of loci in the form Romberch implies. No extant Petrarchan work has been found containing Romberch’s locus rule.

**[Yates’s report]** Yates also says Romberch credits Petrarch with:

- warning that no perturbation should disturb the order of loci;
- recommending loci of medium size, proportioned to their images;
- advising the use of many places.

The first two occur in the locus discussion at *Congestorium* ff. 27v–28r; the third at ff. 19v–20r.

**[Disputed attribution]** These are “Petrarchan” only as Romberch transmits them. They are not verified quotations from the *Rerum memorandarum libri*.

## 3. What serves as “images”

### 3.1 In Petrarch’s text

Petrarch gives no rules for making mnemonic images. The exempla themselves supply memorable verbal portraits:

- Caesar simultaneously reading, writing, listening, and dictating;
- Fabius Maximus retaining Roman and foreign wars;
- Lucullus remembering things and Hortensius words;
- Seneca repeating thousands of items in normal or reverse order;
- Latro “writing in his mind”;
- Themistocles burdened by unforgettable experience;
- Cineas learning the Roman senators’ names;
- Cyrus knowing every soldier by name;
- Mithridates knowing numerous languages;
- Charmadas or Tarmadas reciting books removed from libraries.

**[Documented text]** These are narrated examples of extraordinary memory.

**[Not documented]** Petrarch never says that the reader should transform these figures into *imagines agentes*, install them in loci, or use them as mnemonic ciphers.

### 3.2 Yates’s imagined pictures

Yates asks:

> “What were they like, the corporeal similitudes, the invisible ‘pictures’ which Petrarch would have placed in memory for Prudence and her parts?”

She continues:

> “One wonders whether the virtues rode through Petrarch’s memory in chariots, with the famous ‘examples’ of them marching in their train as in the *Trionfi*.”

—Yates, ch. IV, pp. 103–104; [online scan, text at pp. 103–104](https://www.aproged.pt/biblioteca/Yatestheartofmemory.pdf).

**[Yates’s interpretation/speculation]** Her interrogative grammar matters: she is not reporting a Petrarchan instruction or a surviving image cycle.

**[Possible cultural analogy]** Personified virtues in processions, triumphs, fresco cycles, manuscripts, and Petrarch’s vernacular *Trionfi* could provide memorable moral imagery. Albertus Magnus had defended affectively forceful corporeal similitudes in moral recollection.

**[Unproved]** No manuscript diagram, marginal instruction, letter, or eyewitness account shows Petrarch placing chariot-borne virtues in an artificial memory.

## 4. How order is fixed

### 4.1 Textual order

Order is fixed by:

1. the Ciceronian four-virtue scheme;
2. the subdivision of Prudence;
3. treatment *per partes*;
4. topical rubrics;
5. division of exempla into Roman, foreign, and modern;
6. sequences of named persons within each subsection.

This makes the book an ethical and historical *congeries*, Petrarch’s own word:

> **“memorandarum rerum congeriem amplector.”**

“I embrace a collection of memorable things.”  
—*Rerum memorandarum libri*, surviving fragment *De modestia* 1.2; [Latin text](https://petrarca.letteraturaoperaomnia.org/petrarca_rerum_memorandarum_libri.html).

**[Documented text]** The work is a deliberately arranged store of examples.

**[Scholarly interpretation]** Its structure belongs simultaneously to moral philosophy, rhetorical exemplum literature, historical compilation, and the culture of commonplace organization.

### 4.2 Artificial-locus order

**[Romberch’s system, not demonstrably Petrarch’s]** Romberch insists that loci possess a stable series and that disturbance of their order compromises retrieval. In his full system, loci and images function like a writing surface and marks upon it.

**[Disputed attribution]** Romberch’s attachment of that rule to Petrarch cannot presently be traced to Petrarch’s extant text.

## 5. How retrieval works

### 5.1 Retrieval in the book

The reader retrieves material by moving down a classificatory tree:

> virtue → component of virtue → geographical/temporal class → exemplary person → deed or saying.

For example:

> Prudence → memory → foreign examples → Themistocles → refusal of the art of memory.

**[Documented structure]** This is visible in the headings and succession of chapters.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** A medieval or humanist reader could internalize this outline, use the headings as topical cues, or transfer examples into a commonplace book.

**[Not documented]** Petrarch gives no account of mentally walking the structure backward or forward.

### 5.2 Retrieval in Romberch

In the classical system adopted by Romberch, the practitioner revisits a stable ordered series of loci; an image deposited at each locus cues the associated word, proposition, subject, or argument.

**[Historical context]** That is a genuine place-and-image method, but its detailed application to Petrarch’s moral catalogue is Yates’s extrapolation.

## 6. The memory exempla

### 6.1 Caesar

Petrarch begins *De memoria* with Julius Caesar:

> **“Divo Iulio Cesari … datum scio, ut memoria polleret eximia.”**

“I know that even Caesar’s enemies conceded that he possessed an exceptional memory.”

He reports Caesar reading, writing, listening, and dictating simultaneously, including dictating four letters while writing a fifth, or seven when not writing.

**[Documented text]** *RML* II.2.

**[Source criticism]** Petrarch combines classical notices, principally Cicero, Pliny, and Suetonius. It is an exemplum of capacity and mental agility, not a worked mnemonic exercise.

### 6.2 Fabius Maximus

Fabius retained knowledge of both domestic and foreign wars. Petrarch adds that those who remember many things from past time may become, unless dull-minded, “in some way prophets of the future.”

**[Documented text]** *RML* II.3.

**[Conceptual importance]** Memory is not merely storage. It feeds prudential prediction, linking *memoria* with *providentia*.

### 6.3 Lucullus and Hortensius: things and words

> **“Sed cum duplex sit memoria, una rerum, verborum altera…”**

“But since memory is twofold, one of things, the other of words…”  
—*RML* II.4; Billanovich, p. 44.

Lucullus exemplifies *memoria rerum*, Hortensius *memoria verborum*; Cicero is invoked as witness.

**[Documented text]** This repeats a fundamental distinction of classical rhetoric.

**[Qualification]** Petrarch identifies types of remembered content. He does not repeat the *Ad Herennium*’s detailed method for inventing images for either type.

### 6.4 Scipio

Lucius Scipio is said to have returned the names of the entire Roman people:

> **“Mira et spatiosa capacitas, ubi tam multa et tam varia nomina locum invenissent!”**

“A wondrous and capacious capacity, in which so many and varied names could find a place!”

**[Documented text]** *RML* II.5.

**[Possible mnemonic resonance]** *Locum invenissent* can evoke storage-space language.

**[Not sufficient evidence]** It is not an instruction to assign names to architectural loci.

### 6.5 Seneca’s reverse recitation

Petrarch reports Seneca the Elder’s claim:

> **“duo milia nominum recitata eodem ordine reddidisse”**

“He repeated two thousand names in the same order,”

and more than two hundred verses, supplied one by each pupil:

> **“ordine retrogrado recitasse.”**

“He recited them in reverse order.”  
—*RML* II.6; Billanovich, pp. 44–45.

**[Documented Petrarch]** The feat is explicitly narrated.

**[Primary classical source]** Seneca the Elder, *Controversiae* I, preface 2–3.

**[Interpretive caution]** Reverse recall is compatible with ordered-locus training but does not prove that Seneca used loci in this performance. Petrarch does not say how it was accomplished.

### 6.6 Porcius Latro

Petrarch writes:

> **“memoriam illi et nature validam et artificio munitam tribuit.”**

“[Seneca] attributes to him a memory strong by nature and fortified by art.”  
—*RML* II.7; Billanovich, p. 45.

Latro did not need to read what he had written before speaking; writing once was enough. He boasted:

> **“scribere se in animo”**

“that he wrote in his mind.”

Petrarch compares his speech to reading a book held before his eyes.

**[Documented text]** Natural aptitude and art cooperate.

**[Strongest Petrarchan evidence for training]** This passage explicitly recognizes *artificium* as fortifying natural memory.

**[Still absent]** It does not specify what that art consisted of.

### 6.7 The anonymous plagiarist or claimant

Seneca tells of a listener who retained a newly recited poem so rapidly that he claimed authorship, relying on his ability to reproduce it when its real author could not.

**[Documented text]** *RML* II.8.

**[Ethical ambiguity]** The memory is admirable; the use of it is dishonorable. Petrarch includes “the good and the bad” because memorable acts can be morally mixed.

### 6.8 Themistocles refuses the art

> **“artificiosam memoriam contempsisse … malle se obliviscendi artem discere.”**

“He despised artificial memory … [and said] that he preferred to learn an art of forgetting.”  
—*RML* II.9; Billanovich, p. 60.

Petrarch explains that everything seen or heard clung to Themistocles; he wished to unload his overburdened mind rather than add new cargo.

**[Documented text]** Petrarch knew the story of an explicitly “artificial” memory.

**[Classical source]** Cicero, *De oratore* II.299 and *De finibus* II.104 preserve variants of the story.

**[Yates’s inference]** Because *De oratore* proceeds elsewhere to approve the place-and-image art, Yates assumes Petrarch knew the broader context.

**[Reasonable but indirect]** Familiarity with the book is highly likely; personal practice of its technique is not thereby demonstrated.

### 6.9 Cineas, Cyrus, Mithridates, Tarmadas

Petrarch’s remaining foreign examples include:

- Cineas greeting the Roman Senate by name the day after arriving;
- Cyrus remembering the names of all his soldiers;
- Mithridates knowing the languages of the peoples under his rule;
- Tarmadas or Charmadas reciting any volume taken from a library “in the manner of one reading.”

On Tarmadas:

> **“non scribebat in animo tantum, sed sculpebat.”**

“He did not merely write in his mind; he carved there.”  
—*RML* II.12.

**[Documented text]** Petrarch’s governing metaphors are writing, reading, engraving, capacity, cargo, and storage.

**[Later scholarship]** Bolzoni and Torre show that such metaphors—writing in the mind, storehouse, hook, wound, impression, internalized library—are central to Petrarch’s broader memory practice and are more securely documented than Yates’s hypothetical chariots.

### 6.10 The unnamed modern friend

Petrarch describes a contemporary companion whose military memory seemed inexhaustible. Seeing or hearing something once was sufficient. Years later, he could correct Petrarch’s altered wording and identify:

- the time of the conversation;
- the oak under which it occurred;
- the river;
- the seashore;
- the mountain summit.

**[Documented text]** *RML* II.13.

**[Biographical uncertainty]** The text as transmitted here withholds or has lost the identifying name; suggested identifications must therefore remain conjectural unless supported by independent evidence.

**[Mnemonic relevance]** This is associative episodic recall—words joined to times and places—but Petrarch presents it as an innate military faculty, not as deliberate locus construction.

## 8. Worked examples and apparatus

### 8.1 Worked examples

There is no Petrarchan equivalent of:

- the bloodstained lawsuit image in *Ad Herennium* III.33;
- Camillo’s seven grades and mythological gates;
- Lullian revolving figures;
- Bruno’s letter wheels;
- Fludd’s theater stages.

The Seneca, Latro, Themistocles, and Tarmadas narratives are examples **about** memory, not demonstrations of encoding.

### 8.2 Diagrams, wheels, tables, and plates

**[Documented absence]**

- No mnemonic diagram survives as part of the authorial text.
- No wheel belongs to the work.
- No table maps virtues to loci or images.
- No plate depicts the conjectural triumphal procession.
- Billanovich’s two plates concern textual and manuscript evidence, not a mnemonic mechanism.
- BnF Latin 6069T contains decorated initials, but these are not diagrams of an artificial-memory system.

**[Romberch distinction]** Romberch’s book does contain diagrams, including cosmic and architectural locus schemes. These illustrate Romberch’s synthetic system and cannot be retrojected into Petrarch’s work.

## The sources

## 1. Primary Petrarchan text

### 1.1 Critical editions

1. **Giuseppe Billanovich, ed.**  
   Francesco Petrarca, *Rerum memorandarum libri*. Edizione nazionale delle opere di Francesco Petrarca, XIV. Florence: Sansoni, 1945, cxliii + 312 pp., with portrait and two plates.

   The title page is often catalogued as 1943, and Yates cites “Florence, 1943.” Bibliographical records also give 1945. A contemporary review of Petoletti explains that Billanovich completed the work in 1941 and that publication occurred across the 1943–45 period. The edition remains foundational.

2. **Marco Petoletti, ed. and Italian trans.**  
   Francesco Petrarca, *Rerum memorandarum libri*. *Opere*, III, *Opere storiche*, I. Florence: Le Lettere, 2014. 524 pp. ISBN 978-88-6087-573-0.

   This supplies the Latin text, the first complete Italian translation, introduction, and commentary. Bibliographical description is confirmed by [PubliRES](https://publires.unicatt.it/it/publications/francesco-petrarca-rerum-memorandarum-libri-10/) and [ARLIMA](https://www.arlima.net/eh/francesco_petrarca/rerum_memorandarum_libri.html).

3. **Earlier printed text:**  
   Petrarch, *Opera omnia*, Basel: Sebastian Henricpetri, 1581, and other collected Latin editions. These are useful for reception, but Billanovich and Petoletti supersede them for textual work.

### 1.2 Online Latin text

A searchable transcription is available at:

- [Letteratura Italiana: complete Latin text](https://petrarca.letteraturaoperaomnia.org/petrarca_rerum_memorandarum_libri.html)
- [Latin Wikisource: complete work](https://la.wikisource.org/wiki/Rerum_memorandarum_libri)
- [Latin Wikisource: Book II](https://la.wikisource.org/wiki/Rerum_memorandarum_libri/II)

**[Textual caution]** These online transcriptions are convenient reading texts, not substitutes for the apparatus of Billanovich or Petoletti.

### 1.3 Translation status

There is no established complete English translation comparable to the Loeb translations of the classical rhetorical sources.

Petoletti’s 2014 edition contains a complete Italian translation. Giuseppe Fracassetti attempted an Italian translation in 1860, *Libri delle cose memorabili*, but left it incomplete and unpublished; its autograph survives in the Fracassetti archive at Fermo. The project is documented in [“Da Petrarca a Fracassetti”](https://cris.unibo.it/handle/11585/973417).

The English glosses in this dossier are literal working translations.

Digital facsimiles:

The manuscript contains decorated or filigreed initials, including the initial recorded at f.17 in [Biblissima’s illumination record](https://portail.biblissima.fr/fr/ark:/43093/ifdata549939ce12d7f2842513eded2f4844da53d06a89).

**[Documented manuscript evidence]** The decorative initials articulate textual divisions.

**[Absence]** The catalogue and facsimile do not identify a memory wheel, locus plan, image table, or authorial mnemonic drawing.

### 2.2 Manuscript variants and the critical text

Billanovich’s edition is based on a recensio of the manuscript tradition and remains necessary for variant readings. Petoletti revisits the text and commentary.

For the memory chapters, differences in spelling and names include the familiar medieval forms:

- *michi* for classical *mihi*;
- *memorie* for *memoriae*;
- *Cirus* for Cyrus;
- *Tarmadas* beside classical Charmadas;
- orthographic variation in *artificiosam/artificialem* contexts.

**[Textual finding]** No reported variant transforms the chapter into a technical ars memorativa or supplies the missing locus-and-image instructions.

**[Open bibliographical limit]** A complete shelfmark-by-shelfmark stemmatic catalogue could not responsibly be reconstructed from open catalogue records alone; it belongs to Billanovich’s introduction and Petoletti’s editorial discussion. Claims that a particular manuscript preserves a lost Petrarchan memory diagram should therefore be rejected unless matched to a folio and edition apparatus.

## 3. Early printing

### 3.1 Editio princeps

The first printed edition is:

- Francesco Petrarca, *Rerum memorandarum libri*;
- [Louvain: Johannes de Westfalia], **1483**;
- ISTC/IP records and library descriptions sometimes vary in the precise assignment of printer or year.

A digitized copy is catalogued by Utrecht University as:

- [*Francisci Petrarche poete laureati rerum memorandarum liber primus–quartus* (1483)](https://dbc.library.uu.nl/handle/1874/461651).

**[Documented reception]** The early printing helped detach the work from a single manuscript setting and made its rubrics and exemplary order available to humanist readers.

### 3.2 False or confused attribution in associated incunable literature

A work entitled *De Christianarum rerum memoria*, attributed to Haymo of Halberstadt and printed at Louvain in the 1480s, was at times bound or catalogued with Petrarch and could be attributed to him.

**[Bibliographical confusion]** This is not part of the authentic *Rerum memorandarum libri* and should not be used to supply Petrarch with an otherwise missing technical mnemonic treatise.

## 4. Petrarch’s classical sources

### 4.1 Cicero, *De inventione*

- Cicero, *De inventione* II.159–165, especially II.160.
- Latin/English standard edition: H. M. Hubbell, trans., *De Inventione; De Optimo Genere Oratorum; Topica*, Loeb Classical Library 386. Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press, 1949.

This provides the hierarchy of Prudence as *memoria, intelligentia,* and *providentia*.

### 4.2 Cicero, *De oratore*

- *De oratore* II.299: Themistocles and the art of forgetting.
- II.350–360: Simonides and the place-and-image art.
- Standard edition: E. W. Sutton and H. Rackham, trans., *De Oratore*, 2 vols., Loeb Classical Library 348–349. Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press, 1942.

**[Documented dependence]** Petrarch draws on the Themistocles tradition and on Cicero’s reports of Lucullus and Hortensius.

**[Yates’s correction to a simplified genealogy]** Petrarch is not relying only on the pseudo-Ciceronian *Ad Herennium*; his wording and examples show direct engagement with *De oratore*.

### 4.3 *Rhetorica ad Herennium*

- *Rhetorica ad Herennium* III.28–40.
- Standard edition and translation: Harry Caplan, Loeb Classical Library 403. Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press, 1954.

This is the fullest ancient Latin account of artificial memory:

- loci like wax tablets;
- images like written letters;
- distinct, moderately sized, well-lit places;
- striking images;
- separate treatment of things and words.

**[Historical relevance]** Its rules explain what medieval and Renaissance readers meant by *memoria artificialis*.

**[Direct-dependence limit]** Petrarch’s *De memoria* does not quote its locus rules or reproduce its worked lawsuit image.

### 4.4 Quintilian

- Quintilian, *Institutio oratoria* XI.2.
- Standard edition: Donald A. Russell, ed. and trans., *The Orator’s Education*, Loeb Classical Library 494. Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press, 2001.

Quintilian describes loci while expressing reservations about elaborate image systems.

### 4.5 Seneca the Elder

- Seneca the Elder, *Controversiae* I, preface, especially 2–3 and 18–24.
- Standard edition: Michael Winterbottom, trans., *Declaimers*, Loeb Classical Library 463–464. Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press, 1974.

This is the source for Seneca’s feats and the memory of Porcius Latro.

### 4.6 Valerius Maximus

- Valerius Maximus, *Facta et dicta memorabilia*, especially VIII.7, on natural capacities and intellectual distinction.
- Standard edition: D. R. Shackleton Bailey, ed. and trans., *Memorable Doings and Sayings*, Loeb Classical Library 492–493. Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press, 2000.

**[Documented generic ancestry]** Valerius is the major model for a collection arranged as memorable deeds and sayings. Recent genre scholarship emphasizes this relationship more strongly than Yates’s mnemonic reading.

## 5. Romberch

### 5.1 Editions

Johannes Host von Romberch, *Congestorium artificiose memorie*:

- first published Venice, Giorgio Rusconi, 1520;
- reprinted Venice, Melchiorre Sessa, July 1533.

Digital witnesses:

- [Library of Congress, 1533 edition](https://www.loc.gov/item/10019844/)
- [Internet Archive/Wikisource edition index](https://la.wikisource.org/wiki/Congestorium_Artificiosae_Memoriae)
- [Prefatory letters and list of authorities](https://la.wikisource.org/wiki/Congestorium_Artificiosae_Memoriae/Carmina_et_Epistulae)
- [Treatise I, chapter 4](https://la.wikisource.org/wiki/Congestorium_Artificiosae_Memoriae/Tractatus_Primus/Capitulum_4)
- [Treatise II, chapter 3](https://la.wikisource.org/wiki/Congestorium_Artificiosae_Memoriae/Tractatus_Secundus/Capitulum_3)

### 5.2 Petrarch quotation from *De remediis*

Romberch quotes a genuinely Petrarchan counsel, not from *Rerum memorandarum libri* but from *De remediis utriusque fortunae*:

> **“Si tibi memoria fluxa fuerit, diligentia et artificio eam stringe.”**

“If your memory is unstable, bind it by diligence and art.”

He continues that industry meets the defects of memory and intellect and that one should not trust an unfaithful memory.

**[Documented Petrarchan statement]** Petrarch recommends art and disciplined effort as remedies for a weak memory.

**[Interpretive limit]** The quoted words do not identify loci and images as the art in question.

### 5.3 Romberch’s authority list

Romberch’s prefatory material places Petrarch among:

- Seneca;
- Cicero;
- Quintilian;
- Jacobus Publicius;
- Peter of Ravenna;
- several more recent teachers.

**[Early-modern tradition]** This is decisive evidence that by 1520 Petrarch’s name functioned as mnemonic authority.

**[Not evidence of a lost work by itself]** Lists of authorities can conflate authors who theorized memory, displayed it, supplied moral examples, recommended diligence, or actually wrote mnemonic manuals.

## Yates’s account and the scholarship since

## 1. Yates, chapter IV, pp. 101–104

Yates’s argument has four stages.

### 1.1 Petrarch’s early-modern reputation

She begins from reception: Gesualdo, Garzoni, Agrippa, Schenkel, Romberch, and an annotation in the Lucca *Encyclopédie* all associate Petrarch with memory.

Her historical question is sound:

> What generated such a persistent reputation?

### 1.2 Identification of the *Rerum memorandarum libri*

Yates proposes that no lost treatise need be assumed. The answer may lie in an extant work readers had ceased to understand as they once did.

**[Confirmed]** The book prominently organizes Prudence under memory, understanding, and foresight and contains classical artificial-memory references.

**[Not demonstrated]** These references alone do not prove that the whole compilation was encoded through loci and images.

### 1.3 Comparison with Bartolomeo da San Concordio

Yates notes the structural resemblance to the Dominican *Ammaestramenti degli antichi*.

**[Valuable comparison]** Both works are moral compilations organized around virtues and exemplary sayings.

**[Qualification after Carruthers and Bolzoni]** Medieval mnemonic culture extended far beyond a single classical architectural technique. A text may be composed for recollection, meditation, preaching, quotation, or ethical formation without embodying a strict place-and-image apparatus.

### 1.4 The triumphal-image hypothesis

Yates wonders whether Petrarch used pagan corporeal similitudes and virtues in chariots.

**[Not confirmed]** No later discovery has produced Petrarch’s supposed internal procession or its external diagram.

**[Productive heuristic]** The hypothesis stimulated study of Petrarch’s visual metaphors, the *Trionfi*, manuscripts, marginalia, and the traffic between moral exempla and imagery.

---

## 2. Yates, chapter V, p. 115

Yates calls Romberch’s book “a strange congestion of memory material” and observes:

> “By his frequent citation of the name of Petrarch, he absorbs the poet into the Dominican memory tradition.”

**[Confirmed reception history]** Romberch was a Dominican, his system repeatedly invokes Aquinas, and he lists or quotes Petrarch.

**[Qualification]** “Absorbs” accurately describes Romberch’s construction of authority; it should not be converted into proof that Petrarch himself belonged institutionally or technically to a Dominican school of mnemonics.

---

## 3. Yates, chapter XIV, p. 314

Discussing Bruno’s *Eroici furori*, Yates asks whether the tradition associating Petrarch with memory may have interpreted Petrarchan conceits as memory images.

**[Yates’s interpretation]** Bruno’s emotionally charged eyes, stars, Cupid’s arrows, emblems, and devices resemble the affectively powerful images required by mnemonic theory.

**[Disputed extension]** Bruno’s adaptation of Petrarchan lyric language does not establish that Petrarch devised those conceits as technical mnemonic images.

**[Current safer formulation]** Petrarchism supplied Bruno and emblem culture with a reusable store of vivid verbal-visual figures; Bruno’s own mnemonic and magical theory could transform that inherited imagery for purposes Petrarch never stated.

---

## 4. Mary Carruthers

Mary Carruthers, *The Book of Memory: A Study of Memory in Medieval Culture*, first ed. 1990; second ed. Cambridge University Press, 2008, changed the governing question.

Her work stresses that medieval *memoria* was:

- a trained cognitive faculty;
- a basis of composition and ethical judgment;
- organized through division, association, affect, and habitual recollection;
- supported by page layout, rubrication, imagery, textual “places,” and architectural loci without being reducible to one system.

**[Correction to Yates’s framing]** Yates often narrates “the art of memory” as a relatively unified line of place-and-image transmission. Carruthers shows a plurality of memorial practices embedded in reading, composition, prayer, and moral formation.

**[Application to Petrarch]** This supports viewing the *RML* as a structured memorial book while discouraging the claim that it must conceal an undocumented architectural palace.

Carruthers’s later *The Craft of Thought* (1998) likewise emphasizes meditative composition and crafted cognitive places.

---

## 5. Lina Bolzoni

Bolzoni’s 2004 essay “Petrarca e le tecniche della memoria (a proposito del *De remediis*)” begins from the same Ciceronian triad but extends the inquiry through Petrarch’s later writings.

She shows that in *De remediis*:

- memory, intellect, and foresight may become causes of suffering under Fortune;
- Petrarch presents memory ambivalently, as power and burden;
- moral dialogue, reading, internalized authors, and mental writing are more central than a mechanical display of loci.

The article is available through the [Scuola Normale repository](https://ricerca.sns.it/handle/11384/5044) and [direct PDF](https://ricerca.sns.it/retrieve/handle/11384/5044/61785/Petrarca_e_le_tecniche_memoria.pdf).

**[Confirmation of Yates]** The Ciceronian Prudence model matters deeply to Petrarch.

**[Correction/expansion]** Petrarchan memory must be studied across *De remediis*, letters, reading practices, and metaphors—not inferred principally from later technical manuals.

Bolzoni’s *The Gallery of Memory* broadens this into the relationship among memory arts, literary organization, galleries, emblems, and printed images.

---

## 6. Andrea Torre

Andrea Torre, *Petrarcheschi segni di memoria: Spie, postille, metafore* (Pisa: Edizioni della Normale, 2008), is the most directly relevant post-Yates monograph.

Its documented object is Petrarch’s:

- annotation of manuscripts;
- marginal signs;
- selection and internalization of passages;
- metaphors of treasure, writing, wounds, hooks, rooms, and libraries;
- later reception in Petrarchism.

The publisher summarizes the study as an inquiry into how far classical mnemonics and medieval *sancta memoria* influenced Petrarch’s intellectual and moral choices: [publisher’s description](https://edizioni.sns.it/prodotto/petrarcheschi-segni-di-memoria-spie-postille-metafore/).

**[Major advance beyond Yates]** Torre replaces the hypothetical reconstruction of one hidden “system” with materially testable evidence: books Petrarch owned, marks he made, metaphors he repeatedly used, and the later transformation of Petrarchan signs.

Paolo Rossi’s review reaches the key conclusion:

> Petrarch’s scattered references do not by themselves explain the enormous later tradition that made him an authority.

See [“Petrarca e l’arte della memoria”](https://st.ilsole24ore.com/art/cultura/2011-09-21/petrarca-arte-memoria-163121.shtml).

---

## 7. Paolo Rossi

Rossi’s *Clavis universalis*—translated as *Logic and the Art of Memory*—places Renaissance memory arts within projects for ordered, universal knowledge.

For Petrarch specifically, Rossi helps document the long bibliographical chain from memory treatises to encyclopedias. His strength here is reception history rather than proof of a lost Petrarchan manual.

**[Correction to over-linear histories]** The early-modern mnemonic tradition continually recombined rhetoric, logic, pedagogy, encyclopedism, and claims of wondrous performance. An authority’s name could travel farther than his actual doctrine.

---

## 8. Jocelyn Penny Small

Small’s *Wax Tablets of the Mind: Cognitive Studies of Memory and Literacy in Classical Antiquity* (London/New York: Routledge, 1997) questions overly literal accounts of ancient mnemonic practice and examines the interaction between literacy, internal memory, visual organization, and wax-tablet metaphors.

**[Relevance]** Petrarch’s “writing in the mind” and “carving” metaphors belong to a long cognitive-literary tradition. They cannot automatically be decoded as proof of a physical or imaginary building.

---

## 9. Hajdu and Yates’s dependence

Helga Hajdu, *Das mnemotechnische Schrifttum des Mittelalters* (Vienna, 1936), was Yates’s most important predecessor for medieval mnemonic manuals.

**[Historiographical fact]** Yates explicitly called Hajdu’s book the best account known to her of the ancient and medieval tradition.

**[Limit]** Hajdu’s catalogue-driven reconstruction and Yates’s synthetic narrative preceded the broader manuscript, reading-history, and material-book approaches developed by Carruthers, Bolzoni, and Torre.

---

## 10. Scholars named in the general brief but not directly probative here

The work of Bonner and Fidora on Lull; Sturlese, Ciliberto, Clucas, and Gatti on Bruno; Bolzoni and Stagno on Camillo; and Ong on Ramus is fundamental to those distinct systems. It does not supply missing evidence for Petrarch’s loci or images.

**[Methodological finding]** Importing conclusions from Lullian combinations, Brunian magical images, Camillo’s theater, or Ramist dialectical order into the *RML* would collapse systems that differ in purpose, machinery, chronology, and evidence.

## History and context

## 1. Classical rhetoric

The immediate conceptual ancestry is:

- Simonides’ legendary discovery of ordered places;
- *Ad Herennium* III on loci and images;
- Cicero’s *De oratore* on Simonides and Themistocles;
- Cicero’s *De inventione* on Prudence;
- Quintilian’s qualified treatment;
- Seneca the Elder’s demonstrations of prodigious recall;
- Valerius Maximus’s thematic collections of memorable deeds and sayings.

Petrarch uses two strands simultaneously:

1. **ethical classification**, chiefly from *De inventione*;
2. **exemplary history and memory anecdotes**, from Cicero, Seneca, Pliny, Suetonius, Valerius, and others.

He does not reproduce the full technical strand of the *Ad Herennium*.

---

## 2. Scholastic psychology and ethics

Albertus Magnus and Thomas Aquinas made memory a component of Prudence and interpreted artificial-memory precepts through Aristotelian psychology:

- recollection proceeds from one remembered thing to another;
- order assists recollection;
- sensible likenesses help intellectual intentions;
- affectively forceful or unusual forms adhere more strongly.

**[Contextual influence]** Petrarch inherits a culture in which *memoria* is an intellectual and moral habit, not merely verbatim recall.

**[Yates’s phrase]** His book “comes straight out of scholasticism with its pious use of artificial memory as a part of Prudence.”

**[Qualification]** Petrarch was also a forceful critic of aspects of contemporary scholastic culture. The work’s Ciceronian and Valerian humanism is not exhausted by the label “scholastic.”

---

## 3. Dominican and Franciscan traditions

Bartolomeo da San Concordio, a Dominican, arranged moral teachings for ready recollection. Romberch later placed Petrarch in a Dominicanized genealogy centered on Aquinas.

Filippo Gesualdo, a Franciscan, repeated the Petrarch–Romberch association in *Plutosofia* (Padua, 1592).

**[Institutional history]** Memory doctrine crossed order boundaries. Dominicans and Franciscans both transmitted, adapted, and taught mnemonic material.

**[Not documented]** Petrarch was neither a Dominican nor a Franciscan memory master, and no institutional record makes him a teacher within either school.

---

## 4. Courts

Petrarch composed the work amid courtly and curial networks:

- Avignon and the papal environment;
- King Robert of Naples, who examined Petrarch before his coronation;
- the Colonna circle;
- Azzo da Correggio and other patrons;
- rulers, soldiers, orators, and papal figures appearing among the modern exempla.

**[Functional setting]** At courts, memory served diplomacy, counsel, oratory, genealogy, legal knowledge, and the performance of learning.

**[Textual manifestation]** The unnamed companion’s military memory and the prominence of rulers show the practical prestige of recollection.

---

## 5. Universities

The *RML* belongs to a learned world shaped by university rhetoric, law, theology, medicine, and philosophy, but Petrarch did not write it as a university mnemonic textbook.

**[Documented context]** He studied law and read scholastic, patristic, and classical sources.

**[Distinctive humanist movement]** His collecting and philological restoration of ancient authors redirected learned memory toward historical recovery, imitation, and conversation with classical writers.

---

## 6. The commonplace and exemplum tradition

Petrarch’s closest formal ancestors include:

- Valerius Maximus;
- medieval *florilegia*;
- collections of *dicta et facta*;
- preacher’s exempla;
- moral summae;
- topical notebooks.

Its descendants include:

- humanist exemplary histories;
- commonplace books;
- printed moral anthologies;
- collections organized for quotation and composition;
- early-modern encyclopedic memory books.

The work is thus a bridge between medieval moral compilation and humanist historical exemplarity.

---

## 7. Lullism, Hermeticism, Ramism, and universal language

### Lullism

Ramon Llull’s combinatorial art manipulates letters and divine dignities through figures and combinations. It is not a direct source of the *RML*.

**[No evidence]** Petrarch’s work has no Lullian wheel or combinatorial alphabet.

### Hermeticism

Ficino, Camillo, and Bruno later connected memory with Neoplatonic and Hermetic cosmology.

**[No evidence]** The *RML* predates Ficino’s Latin *Corpus Hermeticum* and contains no Hermetic celestial-memory program.

### Ramism

Ramus later replaced or marginalized the image-locus art by emphasizing dialectical order and method.

**[Retrospective analogy only]** Petrarch’s hierarchical organization can look “methodical,” but it is not Ramist.

### Universal language and computation

Lullian combination contributed to traditions leading through Bruno and Leibniz toward formal symbolic calculation.

**[No direct Petrarch–machine line]** The *RML* contributes to encyclopedic ordering and retrievable classification, not to rotating combinatorics. Any direct “Petrarch to the computer” lineage is modern overstatement.

---

## People

## 1. Petrarch

Petrarch is simultaneously:

- compiler;
- moral historian;
- reader and textual critic;
- collector of classical examples;
- theorist of memory’s value and burden;
- later-constructed mnemonic authority.

**[Documented]** He knew classical accounts of artificial memory and recommended art and diligence to support a weak memory.

**[Undocumented]** He is not known to have operated a public memory school or demonstrated a proprietary palace system.

## 3. Transmitters

- **Johannes Romberch:** made Petrarch an authority within a Dominican-Thomist mnemonic compilation.
- **Filippo Gesualdo:** said “Petrarch, whom Romberch follows on memory,” perpetuating the association.
- **Tommaso Garzoni:** included Petrarch among celebrated “professors of memory.”
- **Cornelius Agrippa:** listed Petrarch among modern memory authorities while criticizing the art.
- **Lambert Schenkel:** claimed Petrarch avidly revived and cultivated the art.
- **Diodati, annotating the Lucca *Encyclopédie*:** retained Petrarch in the historical notice on memory.
- **Frances Yates:** recovered this chain for twentieth-century scholarship.
- **Carruthers, Bolzoni, and Torre:** reframed it through medieval memorial culture, reading practices, and material evidence.

---

## 4. Patrons and students

No securely documented pupil learned a distinct mnemonic system from Petrarch. Likewise, no patron is recorded as commissioning a Petrarchan ars memorativa.

**[Documented broader patronage]** King Robert, the Colonna, the Visconti, Azzo da Correggio, and others supported or intersected with Petrarch’s literary life.

**[Legend risk]** Patronage of Petrarch must not be recast as patronage of an undocumented memory machine.

## 5. Anecdotes requested in the general catalogue

### The auctioneer

No auctioneer anecdote belongs to the *RML* or its verified Petrarchan reception. Such stories belong to other mnemonic performers and should not be imported.

### The professor’s party

No securely sourced “professor’s party” anecdote is connected with Petrarch’s book.

### The student who lost his memory

No such Petrarchan case occurs in the relevant primary text.

### The accused magician

Two different traditions must be distinguished.

1. **Petrarch:** accused or mocked as a necromancer because of solitude, book learning, and admiration for Virgil.
2. **Lambert Schenkel:** later accused of magic because of spectacular mnemonic performances.

Neither accusation demonstrates that the *RML* was a magical memory system.

## The magic question

## 1. Is the *Rerum memorandarum libri* magical?

No.

**[Documented text]**

- no invocation;
- no planetary timing;
- no talisman;
- no angelic names;
- no magical seals;
- no consecration;
- no spirit operation;
- no *notae*;
- no ritual purity regimen;
- no claim to infused universal knowledge.

Its ethical hierarchy and exempla are Christian-humanist and rhetorical.

---

## 2. Pagan imagery

Yates conjectured that Petrarch may have used pagan images because classical subjects moved him intensely and Albertus had licensed morally useful corporeal similitudes.

**[Yates’s interpretation]** This would be mnemonic imagery, not necessarily operative magic.

**[No evidence]** The *RML* does not tell readers to animate, astralize, consecrate, or invoke pagan figures.

---

## 3. Petrarch accused of necromancy

Petrarch complained that powerful but ignorant people regarded him as a magician because he lived alone and read Virgil:

> **“Perhaps … I appear to many as a necromancer and magician.”**

He explains that his admiration for Virgil generated the suspicion. The episode is discussed in *Petrarch’s Genius* with references to Petrarch’s letters and his examination by King Robert: [open scholarly text](https://pub-ucpec2-prd.cdlib.org/ucpressebooks/view?anchor.id=0&brand=eschol&chunk.id=d0e1510&doc.view=content&docId=ft167nb0qn&toc.depth=1).

**[Documented self-report]** Petrarch says the accusation circulated.

**[Context]** Medieval Naples possessed legends of Virgil the magician; intense Virgilian study could therefore appear suspect to non-scholars.

**[Not documented]** There was no Inquisition trial of Petrarch for practicing artificial memory, and the *RML* was not condemned as heretical.

---

## 4. Petrarch’s attitude to magic and divination

Petrarch repeatedly attacks astrologers, diviners, superstition, and magical pretensions. In the *RML* he sometimes reports prophecies, dreams, and magical reputations as memorable historical material, while marking uncertainty with formulas such as *fama est* or “new writers assert.”

**[Documented nuance]** Recording a magical anecdote is not endorsement.

**[Self-positioning]** Petrarch calls himself an enemy of divination and magic.

---

## 5. The *Ars notoria*

The *Ars notoria* promised extraordinary knowledge or memory through prayers, ritual observances, and contemplation of complex *notae*.

**[Historical distinction]**

| *RML* | *Ars notoria* |
|---|---|
| Moral exempla | Ritual acquisition of arts |
| Ciceronian virtue hierarchy | Prayers, angelic mediation, figures |
| Ordinary literary composition | Claims of preternatural infusion |
| No operative images | Contemplated *notae* |
| No ecclesiastical condemnation known | Periodically suspected or condemned |

**[Conclusion]** Their connection is only that both belong to the much wider medieval history of memory and knowledge.

---

## 6. Agrippa’s criticism

In *De incertitudine et vanitate scientiarum*, ch. X, Agrippa describes artificial memory as characters, places, and representations but warns that monstrous images may confuse natural memory and cause madness. He repeats Themistocles’ desire for an art of forgetting.

See [English text, chapter X](https://www.alchemy-texts.com/book/the-vanity-of-arts-and-sciences/).

**[Reception irony]** Agrippa can list Petrarch as an authority while attacking exaggerated claims for the art. Inclusion in an authority list therefore does not mean agreement about efficacy or magic.

---

## Afterlife

## 1. Early-modern mnemonic authority

The traceable chain is:

> Petrarch’s texts and reputation → Romberch → Gesualdo/Garzoni/Agrippa → Schenkel → encyclopedic notices → Yates.

### Romberch

By 1520, Petrarch appears in a formal genealogy of mnemonic authors.

### Gesualdo

Filippo Gesualdo’s *Plutosofia* (Padua, 1592) describes a large memory system and refers to Petrarch through Romberch. Bibliographical record: [Folger Shakespeare Library](https://catalog.folger.edu/record/358556).

### Garzoni

Tommaso Garzoni’s *Piazza universale di tutte le professioni del mondo* first appeared in Venice in 1585 and included a discourse on memory professionals. Accessible 1593 text: [Italian Wikisource](https://it.wikisource.org/wiki/La_piazza_universale_di_tutte_le_professioni_del_mondo).

### Schenkel

Lambert Schenkel, active around 1600, marketed memory instruction across Europe and presented Petrarch as an eager reviver of the art.

**[Tradition]** Schenkel’s historical claim is evidence of Petrarch’s reputation in 1600, not independent evidence about 1343.

### *Encyclopédie*

A note attributed to Diodati in the Lucca edition, vol. X, p. 263, preserved Petrarch’s name in the history of memory.

**[Reception]** By the eighteenth century the association had become encyclopedic lore.

---

## 2. Petrarchism, emblems, and Bruno

Petrarch’s lyric vocabulary—eyes, stars, darts, fire, ice, wounds, bonds—became a shared European figurative repertoire.

Bruno’s *Eroici furori* organizes poems and commentaries around emotionally charged emblems and Petrarchan conceits.

**[Documented literary afterlife]** Bruno uses Petrarchan language.

**[Yates’s interpretation]** These conceits operate like affectively charged mnemonic images.

**[Disputed]** There is no evidence that Bruno received a technical Petrarchan mnemonic system rather than literary Petrarchism plus independent mnemonic doctrine.

---

## 3. The *Trionfi* and visual art

Petrarch’s *Trionfi* furnished processional allegories of:

- Love;
- Chastity;
- Death;
- Fame;
- Time;
- Eternity.

These generated illuminated manuscripts, cassone paintings, engravings, tapestries, and monumental imagery.

**[Documented afterlife]** The triumphal figures formed memorable ordered visual sequences.

**[Yates’s speculative retrojection]** Because these images resemble possible moral *imagines agentes*, she imagined similar chariots in Petrarch’s memory.

**[Safer conclusion]** The *Trionfi* demonstrate Petrarch’s capacity to construct serial personified imagery; they do not document the encoding procedure of the *RML*.

---

## 4. Dante and fresco cycles

Dante’s ordered otherworld and late-medieval virtue-and-vice frescoes have often been interpreted through mnemonic culture.

**[Broader scholarly context]** Their structured spaces, vivid punishments, personifications, and serial narratives are highly memorable and compatible with medieval memorial practice.

**[Contested strong thesis]** It is difficult to prove that a particular poem or fresco was designed as a technical artificial-memory scheme unless explicit instructions or use evidence survive.

Petrarch belongs in this visual-moral environment, but no specific fresco cycle is securely the diagram of his *RML*.

---

## 5. Commonplace books and encyclopedias

The work’s strongest structural afterlife lies in:

- topical excerpting;
- headings and subheadings;
- ethical taxonomies;
- collections of examples;
- indexes;
- libraries of quotations;
- encyclopedic “places.”

**[Historical continuity]** Here “place” moves between mental locus, textual topic, heading, index entry, and storage location.

---

## 6. Borges and Eco

### Borges’s Funes

Funes remembers everything but cannot abstract effectively.

**[Modern literary echo]** This parallels Petrarch’s Themistocles: perfect retention can become an intolerable burden.

**[No direct-source claim]** No evidence proves Borges constructed Funes directly from *RML* II.9.

### Umberto Eco

Eco’s fiction and essays repeatedly explore encyclopedias, libraries, signs, lists, and the tension between memory and oblivion.

**[Thematic afterlife]** The *RML* belongs to the genealogy of ordered cultural memory relevant to Eco.

**[No direct-system continuity]** Eco is not reproducing a Petrarchan locus technique.

---

## 7. Contemporary memory palaces and athletes

Modern memory athletes commonly use:

- familiar spatial routes;
- vivid or bizarre images;
- person–action–object systems;
- number or card encodings;
- spaced review.

**[Historical continuity]** These practices descend principally from the classical locus tradition recovered and repeatedly reformulated after antiquity.

**[Petrarch’s limited place]** His distinction between things and words, praise of art strengthening nature, and examples of ordered feats belong to the historical conversation. No technique used by athletes can be uniquely traced to the *RML*.

---

## 8. Computing and combinatorics

A defensible lineage is:

> ancient topical and mnemonic order → medieval classifications and Lullian combination → Renaissance encyclopedic and combinatorial arts → Leibnizian *ars combinatoria* → much later formal logic and computing.

**[Documented intellectual history]** Leibniz explicitly engaged Lullian combination.

**[Overstatement to avoid]** Petrarch did not design a programmable wheel, formal code, addressing system, or computer architecture.

**[Conceptual analogy]** The *RML*’s hierarchy resembles a browsable data taxonomy:

```text
Virtue
└── Prudence
    ├── Memory
    │   ├── Roman examples
    │   ├── Foreign examples
    │   └── Modern examples
    ├── Understanding
    └── Foresight
```

This is a useful modern analogy, not a historical claim that the codex is a computer.

---

## Controversies and disputes

## 1. Is it an artificial-memory “system”?

### Position A: Yates

The ethical hierarchy supplies the things to be remembered; references to artificial memory identify the intended mnemonic culture; therefore the work was probably designed for memorization.

### Position B: cautious revision

The work is organized and memorable, and Petrarch knew artificial memory, but no places, images, encoding algorithm, or retrieval instructions survive.

### Verdict

**[Best-supported]** It is a memorially useful ethical taxonomy, not a recoverable technical system in the sense of the *Ad Herennium*, Camillo, Lull, Bruno, or Fludd.

---

## 2. Did Petrarch write a lost *Ars memorativa*?

Yates acknowledges the possibility and then says it is unnecessary.

**Evidence for:**

- Romberch lists Petrarch among authors whose books on the art he had seen or among recognized authorities;
- later writers treat Petrarch as a reviver or professor of memory;
- Romberch attaches detailed rules to his name.

**Evidence against:**

- no title, incipit, manuscript, explicit self-reference, library record, or independent contemporary witness has been identified;
- genuine Petrarchan passages quoted by Romberch come from *De remediis*;
- early-modern authority lists routinely telescope disparate kinds of testimony.

### Verdict

**[Disputed hypothesis]** A lost treatise is possible in the abstract but unsupported. The burden of proof lies with a manuscript or precise early citation.

---

## 3. Are Romberch’s place rules authentically Petrarchan?

### Evidence for

Romberch says so and was closer in time to manuscript materials now lost.

### Evidence against

- the wording has not been located in Petrarch’s extant corpus;
- the rules are conventional in the classical and Renaissance tradition;
- Romberch is avowedly a compiler;
- his attribution to Aquinas of numerous loci is itself imprecise;
- Gesualdo’s later statement depends on Romberch rather than independent Petrarchan evidence.

### Verdict

**[Unverified early-modern attribution]** Cite the rules as “Romberch attributed to Petrarch,” never simply “Petrarch taught.”

---

## 4. Did Petrarch rely on the *Ad Herennium*?

**[Yates’s correction]** The relevant memory anecdotes show the importance of *De oratore*, especially Themistocles, rather than exclusive reliance on *Ad Herennium*.

**[Possible broader familiarity]** Petrarch may have known the pseudo-Ciceronian rhetoric, widely attributed to Cicero.

**[Textual result]** The *RML*’s memory section does not reproduce the *Ad Herennium*’s technical locus-and-image exposition.

---

## 5. Scholastic or humanist?

Yates emphasizes continuity with scholastic moral memory. Other scholarship emphasizes Valerius Maximus, classical recovery, biography, praise, historical exemplarity, and Petrarch’s humanist canon.

### Verdict

**[False binary]** The work is both:

- medieval in its ethical psychology and culture of memorial formation;
- humanist in its classical sources, historical voice, philological ambition, and inclusion of modern exempla.

---

## 6. Dating

The conventional dating “1343–45” rests on internal references, biography, and Billanovich’s textual reconstruction. Petoletti’s edition describes the work as begun in spring 1343 and abandoned within roughly two years. A recent discussion likewise places composition from summer 1343 to early 1345: [Insula Europea](https://www.insulaeuropea.eu/2022/05/24/un-nuovo-livio-di-petrarca-il-manoscritto-arch-s-pietro-c-132-della-biblioteca-apostolica-vaticana/).

**[Documented scholarly consensus]** 1343–45.

**[Qualification]** Individual passages may have undergone revision, and the absence of an authorially completed final form complicates exact dating.

---

## 7. Why was it unfinished?

Proposals include:

- the scale of the projected moral encyclopedia became unmanageable;
- competing projects displaced it;
- Petrarch’s intellectual priorities changed;
- the Valerian exemplary format became less satisfactory;
- the work’s increasingly evident untimeliness led to abandonment.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** No single surviving statement by Petrarch definitively explains the abandonment.

---

## 8. Was the book meant for memorization?

### Evidence supporting

- its title, “things to be remembered”;
- systematic ethical divisions;
- discrete short exempla;
- rubrics;
- artificial-memory references;
- resemblance to moral compilations used in preaching and study.

### Evidence limiting

- “memorable” can mean noteworthy rather than assigned to mnemonic loci;
- no instruction tells readers to memorize the book;
- no encoding scheme survives;
- exemplum collections also served consultation, composition, and ethical reading.

### Verdict

**[Probable general function]** It was designed to make moral-historical material memorable and reusable.

**[Unproved technical function]** It was not demonstrably designed for memorization by a particular artificial-memory palace.

---

## 9. The virtues in chariots

**Origin of reading:** Yates, 1966, phrased as “One wonders whether…”

**Carriers:** later summaries of Yates sometimes report it without the interrogative qualification.

**Evidence:**

- Petrarch’s *Trionfi*;
- widespread virtue personification;
- medieval memorable imagery;
- Albertus’s defense of corporeal similitudes.

**Counterevidence:**

- no statement in the *RML*;
- no diagram;
- no relevant manuscript image sequence;
- no user testimony.

### Verdict

**[Yates’s evocative conjecture]**, sometimes hardened by later popular retelling into **legend**.

---

## 10. Magic

**Origin of magical interpretation:** not Petrarch’s text but the broader Renaissance history in which artificial memory later combined with Hermetic, astral, and magical images.

**Evidence against for this work:**

- ordinary moral-rhetorical sources;
- no ritual apparatus;
- Petrarch’s hostility to magic;
- no prosecution or condemnation.

### Verdict

**[Modern conflation]** Calling the *RML* a magical memory system imports Bruno or the *Ars notoria* into a nonmagical work.

## Open questions

1. Did Romberch possess a Petrarchan manuscript, excerpt, or pseudo-Petrarchan mnemonic tract no longer identifiable?

2. Can the exact verbal source of each rule attributed to Petrarch at *Congestorium* ff. 19v–20r and 27v–28r be traced to *De remediis*, a letter, Publicius, Peter of Ravenna, or another intermediate compilation?

3. Did early readers annotate copies of the *RML* with artificial-memory loci or images?

4. Can ownership records establish which Dominican or Franciscan libraries transmitted the work alongside mnemonic manuals?

5. How did rubrics and page layout vary across the full manuscript tradition, and did those variants materially affect retrieval?

6. Who is Petrarch’s unnamed contemporary with the extraordinary military and episodic memory?

7. Did Gesualdo consult Romberch alone, or did he know an independent Petrarchan memory tradition?

8. At what point did “Petrarch possessed a remarkable memory” become “Petrarch wrote or taught an ars memorativa”?

9. How widely was the possibly pseudo-Petrarchan *De Christianarum rerum memoria* confused with the authentic work?

10. Can the reception of Petrarch’s lyric conceits in emblem books be tied to explicit mnemonic commentary rather than general Petrarchism?

11. Do Petrarch’s marginal signs in manuscripts of Cicero, Seneca, Valerius, and Augustine correspond systematically to the headings or examples of the *RML*?

12. Would a complete comparison between Billanovich’s apparatus and Petoletti’s text alter any memory passage materially? The available evidence suggests not, but this deserves a dedicated apparatus-level collation.

---

## Final assessment

Petrarch’s *Rerum memorandarum libri* is securely a book of morally memorable matter, organized by Ciceronian divisions and rich in classical testimony about natural and artificial memory. It recognizes the distinction between memory for things and words, art’s capacity to strengthen nature, reverse-order recall, and the burden of remembering too much.

It is not, in its surviving form, a technical place-and-image manual. Its “places” are demonstrably textual categories and only conjecturally architectural loci; its “images” are vivid narrated persons and deeds, not explicitly constructed *imagines agentes*. No diagram, wheel, table, image alphabet, or retrieval itinerary survives.

The technical “Petrarchan system” begins with Romberch, nearly two centuries later. Romberch quotes genuine advice from *De remediis*, lists Petrarch among mnemonic authorities, and attaches Petrarch’s name to conventional locus rules. That reception is historically real even where the attribution is textually unverifiable.

Yates brilliantly recovered this forgotten reputation and correctly connected Petrarch’s book with Prudence, exemplarity, and classical artificial memory. Her further reconstruction—pagan corporeal similitudes and virtues in triumphal chariots—remains an explicitly imaginative hypothesis. Carruthers, Bolzoni, and especially Torre have made possible a more secure account: Petrarch’s memory culture is visible in organization, reading, annotation, internalized authors, mental writing, affective impressions, and the book as a storehouse. That is a richer but less mechanically reconstructable system than Yates proposed.

# Petrarch's *Rerum memorandarum libri*: Research Dossier

## Basic identification

| Field | Identification |
|---|---|
| **Name** | *Rerum memorandarum libri* (“Books of Memorable Things”; also transmitted as *De rebus memorandis*) |
| **Exponent** | Francesco Petrarca / Petrarch (1304–1374) |
| **Date** | Begun in 1343, probably in spring or summer; substantially abandoned by early 1345. “c. 1343–45” is the safest concise date. |
| **Source text** | Petrarch, *Rerum memorandarum libri*, especially I, proem and 1–37; II.1–13, *De memoria*; critical ed. Giuseppe Billanovich, Florence: Sansoni, 1945 [often bibliographically dated 1943], pp. 44–45 and 60; modern Latin–Italian ed. Marco Petoletti, Florence: Le Lettere, 2014. |
| **Principle in one sentence** | Petrarch arranges morally memorable exempla under a Ciceronian hierarchy of virtues—beginning with Prudence divided into memory of the past, understanding of the present, and foresight of the future—but the surviving text supplies examples of memory and notices of artificial memory, not a working place-and-image mnemonic system. |
| **Yates** | Frances A. Yates, *The Art of Memory* (1966), ch. IV, pp. 101–104; ch. V, p. 115; ch. XIV, p. 314. |

### Evidentiary conclusion

**[Documented text]** The *Rerum memorandarum libri* is an unfinished ethical-exemplary compilation whose architecture derives from Cicero’s classification of the virtues. Its section *De memoria* distinguishes memory for things from memory for words and includes several classical anecdotes involving natural and artificial memory.

**[Not documented]** The surviving work does **not** prescribe mnemonic buildings, ordered loci, image construction, image placement, interval rules, illumination rules, alphabetic images, a retrieval walk, diagrams, wheels, or tables.

**[Early-modern tradition]** Johannes Romberch nevertheless listed Petrarch among writers on the art and attached his name to rules about the order, dimensions, and number of mnemonic places.

**[Yates’s interpretation]** Yates inferred that the book was “designed for memorisation” and imagined that Petrarch may have represented virtues as pagan or triumphal images.

**[Current assessment]** The book can securely be called a morally ordered repository of things worth remembering and evidence of Petrarch’s familiarity with classical discourse about artificial memory. Calling it a reconstructable Petrarchan “system of artificial memory” goes beyond the surviving evidence.

---

## The system in detail

## 1. What Petrarch actually devised

### 1.1 The macrostructure

The work begins with what Billanovich called the *praeludia virtutum*, the conditions or preliminaries of virtuous intellectual life:

1. *otium* — leisure;
2. *solitudo* — solitude;
3. *studium* — study;
4. *doctrina* — learning or instruction.

Petrarch then proceeds to Prudence, the first cardinal virtue. The governing division is stated at II.1:

> **“Hec … trifariam … diducitur in preteritorum memoriam, presentium intelligentiam et providentiam futurorum.”**

“Prudence is divided threefold into memory of the past, understanding of the present, and foresight of the future.”  
—*Rerum memorandarum libri* II.1.2; Billanovich, p. 44; online Latin text in [Book II](https://la.wikisource.org/wiki/Rerum_memorandarum_libri/II).

**[Documented text]** This comes from Cicero, *De inventione* II.160: *prudentia* consists of *memoria, intelligentia,* and *providentia*. It is a logical and ethical division, not itself a mnemonic itinerary.

Petrarch announces that he will proceed “by parts”:

> **“Et quoniam per partes commodissime omnis tractatus absolvitur, a memoria quidem … initium repetendum est.”**

“Since every treatment is most conveniently completed part by part, a beginning must be made from memory.”

That phrase supplies the actual mechanism of textual order: hierarchical division followed by serial exempla.

### 1.2 Intended complete architecture

Billanovich’s reconstruction, accepted in broad outline by Yates and later editors, is:

- preliminaries to virtue;
- Prudence:
  - memory;
  - understanding;
  - foresight;
- Justice;
- Fortitude;
- Temperance;
- probably corresponding vices after the virtues.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** Only a fraction was completed. The extant four books do not complete Prudence in the originally projected proportions; Justice and Fortitude are absent, while only a fragment associated with Temperance survives. Consequently, neither the final scale nor every subdivision can be recovered.

**[Yates’s interpretation]** Because Bartolomeo da San Concordio’s *Ammaestramenti degli antichi* employs a similar progression—preliminaries, virtues, and vices—and places artificial-memory advice under memory, Yates treated Petrarch’s compilation as a parallel ethical work designed for memorization.

**[Qualification]** Structural comparability does not prove identical mnemonic practice. Petrarch’s text never directs a reader to translate this hierarchy into loci and images.

---

## 2. What serves as “places”

### 2.1 In Petrarch’s surviving instructions

Nothing is explicitly designated as a series of artificial-memory loci.

There is no rule corresponding to:

- choose a building;
- mark five or ten stations;
- keep the stations distinct;
- traverse them in a fixed direction;
- place one striking image in each;
- revisit the circuit to retrieve the subject matter.

**[Absence of evidence]** No surviving passage in the *Rerum memorandarum libri* gives such directions. Neither Billanovich’s critical text nor the modern complete edition identifies a lost diagram or an incomplete locus chapter.

### 2.2 The book’s headings as textual “places”

One may describe the hierarchy of headings—virtue, part of virtue, Roman examples, foreign examples, modern examples—as textual compartments. The *De memoria* section, for example, proceeds through:

- Roman examples;
- external or Greek examples;
- modern examples.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** These headings could aid retrieval in a codex or in learned recall. Medieval and Renaissance readers frequently used rubrics, divisions, marginal signs, excerpt books, and topical classifications as mnemonic supports.

**[Important distinction]** Calling a rubric a “place” in a broad sense is reasonable; identifying it as one of Petrarch’s imagined architectural *loci* is not documented.

### 2.3 Places attributed to Petrarch by Romberch

Romberch’s *Congestorium artificiose memorie* appeared in 1520; the accessible scan and Wikisource transcription used here reproduce the Venice, Melchiorre Sessa, 1533 edition.

Romberch gives Petrarch three roles:

1. an author who recommends strengthening weak memory by diligence and art;
2. one of the recognized writers or authorities on memory;
3. a follower of the rule requiring numerous mnemonic places.

In the second treatise, chapter 3, Romberch writes, in abbreviated print:

> **“Divus Aquinas multos habendos consulit locos … [quem] … Franciscus Petrarcha, Petrus Ravennas … sequuntur.”**

“Saint Thomas counsels that many places should be possessed … and Francis Petrarch, Peter of Ravenna … follow him.”  
—Romberch, *Congestorium*, tract II, ch. 3, ff. 19v–20r in Yates’s citation; [transcription](https://la.wikisource.org/wiki/Congestorium_Artificiosae_Memoriae/Tractatus_Secundus/Capitulum_3).

The chapter explains the rationale: writers who wish to write more require a larger tablet, wax surface, or paper; likewise, a person wishing to retain many things requires many places.

**[Documented Romberch]** Romberch explicitly associates Petrarch with multiplicity of loci.

**[Textual problem]** Aquinas, *Summa theologiae* II–II, q.49, a.1, does not prescribe a numerical abundance of loci in the form Romberch implies. No extant Petrarchan work has been found containing Romberch’s locus rule.

**[Yates’s report]** Yates also says Romberch credits Petrarch with:

- warning that no perturbation should disturb the order of loci;
- recommending loci of medium size, proportioned to their images;
- advising the use of many places.

The first two occur in the locus discussion at *Congestorium* ff. 27v–28r; the third at ff. 19v–20r.

**[Disputed attribution]** These are “Petrarchan” only as Romberch transmits them. They are not verified quotations from the *Rerum memorandarum libri*.

---

## 3. What serves as “images”

### 3.1 In Petrarch’s text

Petrarch gives no rules for making mnemonic images. The exempla themselves supply memorable verbal portraits:

- Caesar simultaneously reading, writing, listening, and dictating;
- Fabius Maximus retaining Roman and foreign wars;
- Lucullus remembering things and Hortensius words;
- Seneca repeating thousands of items in normal or reverse order;
- Latro “writing in his mind”;
- Themistocles burdened by unforgettable experience;
- Cineas learning the Roman senators’ names;
- Cyrus knowing every soldier by name;
- Mithridates knowing numerous languages;
- Charmadas or Tarmadas reciting books removed from libraries.

**[Documented text]** These are narrated examples of extraordinary memory.

**[Not documented]** Petrarch never says that the reader should transform these figures into *imagines agentes*, install them in loci, or use them as mnemonic ciphers.

### 3.2 Yates’s imagined pictures

Yates asks:

> “What were they like, the corporeal similitudes, the invisible ‘pictures’ which Petrarch would have placed in memory for Prudence and her parts?”

She continues:

> “One wonders whether the virtues rode through Petrarch’s memory in chariots, with the famous ‘examples’ of them marching in their train as in the *Trionfi*.”

—Yates, ch. IV, pp. 103–104; [online scan, text at pp. 103–104](https://www.aproged.pt/biblioteca/Yatestheartofmemory.pdf).

**[Yates’s interpretation/speculation]** Her interrogative grammar matters: she is not reporting a Petrarchan instruction or a surviving image cycle.

**[Possible cultural analogy]** Personified virtues in processions, triumphs, fresco cycles, manuscripts, and Petrarch’s vernacular *Trionfi* could provide memorable moral imagery. Albertus Magnus had defended affectively forceful corporeal similitudes in moral recollection.

**[Unproved]** No manuscript diagram, marginal instruction, letter, or eyewitness account shows Petrarch placing chariot-borne virtues in an artificial memory.

## 4. How order is fixed

### 4.1 Textual order

Order is fixed by:

1. the Ciceronian four-virtue scheme;
2. the subdivision of Prudence;
3. treatment *per partes*;
4. topical rubrics;
5. division of exempla into Roman, foreign, and modern;
6. sequences of named persons within each subsection.

This makes the book an ethical and historical *congeries*, Petrarch’s own word:

> **“memorandarum rerum congeriem amplector.”**

“I embrace a collection of memorable things.”  
—*Rerum memorandarum libri*, surviving fragment *De modestia* 1.2; [Latin text](https://petrarca.letteraturaoperaomnia.org/petrarca_rerum_memorandarum_libri.html).

**[Documented text]** The work is a deliberately arranged store of examples.

**[Scholarly interpretation]** Its structure belongs simultaneously to moral philosophy, rhetorical exemplum literature, historical compilation, and the culture of commonplace organization.

### 4.2 Artificial-locus order

**[Romberch’s system, not demonstrably Petrarch’s]** Romberch insists that loci possess a stable series and that disturbance of their order compromises retrieval. In his full system, loci and images function like a writing surface and marks upon it.

**[Disputed attribution]** Romberch’s attachment of that rule to Petrarch cannot presently be traced to Petrarch’s extant text.

---

## 5. How retrieval works

### 5.1 Retrieval in the book

The reader retrieves material by moving down a classificatory tree:

> virtue → component of virtue → geographical/temporal class → exemplary person → deed or saying.

For example:

> Prudence → memory → foreign examples → Themistocles → refusal of the art of memory.

**[Documented structure]** This is visible in the headings and succession of chapters.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** A medieval or humanist reader could internalize this outline, use the headings as topical cues, or transfer examples into a commonplace book.

**[Not documented]** Petrarch gives no account of mentally walking the structure backward or forward.

### 5.2 Retrieval in Romberch

In the classical system adopted by Romberch, the practitioner revisits a stable ordered series of loci; an image deposited at each locus cues the associated word, proposition, subject, or argument.

**[Historical context]** That is a genuine place-and-image method, but its detailed application to Petrarch’s moral catalogue is Yates’s extrapolation.

## 6. The memory exempla

### 6.1 Caesar

Petrarch begins *De memoria* with Julius Caesar:

> **“Divo Iulio Cesari … datum scio, ut memoria polleret eximia.”**

“I know that even Caesar’s enemies conceded that he possessed an exceptional memory.”

He reports Caesar reading, writing, listening, and dictating simultaneously, including dictating four letters while writing a fifth, or seven when not writing.

**[Documented text]** *RML* II.2.

**[Source criticism]** Petrarch combines classical notices, principally Cicero, Pliny, and Suetonius. It is an exemplum of capacity and mental agility, not a worked mnemonic exercise.

### 6.2 Fabius Maximus

Fabius retained knowledge of both domestic and foreign wars. Petrarch adds that those who remember many things from past time may become, unless dull-minded, “in some way prophets of the future.”

**[Documented text]** *RML* II.3.

**[Conceptual importance]** Memory is not merely storage. It feeds prudential prediction, linking *memoria* with *providentia*.

### 6.3 Lucullus and Hortensius: things and words

> **“Sed cum duplex sit memoria, una rerum, verborum altera…”**

“But since memory is twofold, one of things, the other of words…”  
—*RML* II.4; Billanovich, p. 44.

Lucullus exemplifies *memoria rerum*, Hortensius *memoria verborum*; Cicero is invoked as witness.

**[Documented text]** This repeats a fundamental distinction of classical rhetoric.

**[Qualification]** Petrarch identifies types of remembered content. He does not repeat the *Ad Herennium*’s detailed method for inventing images for either type.

### 6.4 Scipio

Lucius Scipio is said to have returned the names of the entire Roman people:

> **“Mira et spatiosa capacitas, ubi tam multa et tam varia nomina locum invenissent!”**

“A wondrous and capacious capacity, in which so many and varied names could find a place!”

**[Documented text]** *RML* II.5.

**[Possible mnemonic resonance]** *Locum invenissent* can evoke storage-space language.

**[Not sufficient evidence]** It is not an instruction to assign names to architectural loci.

### 6.5 Seneca’s reverse recitation

Petrarch reports Seneca the Elder’s claim:

> **“duo milia nominum recitata eodem ordine reddidisse”**

“He repeated two thousand names in the same order,”

and more than two hundred verses, supplied one by each pupil:

> **“ordine retrogrado recitasse.”**

“He recited them in reverse order.”  
—*RML* II.6; Billanovich, pp. 44–45.

**[Documented Petrarch]** The feat is explicitly narrated.

**[Primary classical source]** Seneca the Elder, *Controversiae* I, preface 2–3.

**[Interpretive caution]** Reverse recall is compatible with ordered-locus training but does not prove that Seneca used loci in this performance. Petrarch does not say how it was accomplished.

### 6.6 Porcius Latro

Petrarch writes:

> **“memoriam illi et nature validam et artificio munitam tribuit.”**

“[Seneca] attributes to him a memory strong by nature and fortified by art.”  
—*RML* II.7; Billanovich, p. 45.

Latro did not need to read what he had written before speaking; writing once was enough. He boasted:

> **“scribere se in animo”**

“that he wrote in his mind.”

Petrarch compares his speech to reading a book held before his eyes.

**[Documented text]** Natural aptitude and art cooperate.

**[Strongest Petrarchan evidence for training]** This passage explicitly recognizes *artificium* as fortifying natural memory.

**[Still absent]** It does not specify what that art consisted of.

### 6.7 The anonymous plagiarist or claimant

Seneca tells of a listener who retained a newly recited poem so rapidly that he claimed authorship, relying on his ability to reproduce it when its real author could not.

**[Documented text]** *RML* II.8.

**[Ethical ambiguity]** The memory is admirable; the use of it is dishonorable. Petrarch includes “the good and the bad” because memorable acts can be morally mixed.

### 6.8 Themistocles refuses the art

> **“artificiosam memoriam contempsisse … malle se obliviscendi artem discere.”**

“He despised artificial memory … [and said] that he preferred to learn an art of forgetting.”  
—*RML* II.9; Billanovich, p. 60.

Petrarch explains that everything seen or heard clung to Themistocles; he wished to unload his overburdened mind rather than add new cargo.

**[Documented text]** Petrarch knew the story of an explicitly “artificial” memory.

**[Classical source]** Cicero, *De oratore* II.299 and *De finibus* II.104 preserve variants of the story.

**[Yates’s inference]** Because *De oratore* proceeds elsewhere to approve the place-and-image art, Yates assumes Petrarch knew the broader context.

**[Reasonable but indirect]** Familiarity with the book is highly likely; personal practice of its technique is not thereby demonstrated.

### 6.9 Cineas, Cyrus, Mithridates, Tarmadas

Petrarch’s remaining foreign examples include:

- Cineas greeting the Roman Senate by name the day after arriving;
- Cyrus remembering the names of all his soldiers;
- Mithridates knowing the languages of the peoples under his rule;
- Tarmadas or Charmadas reciting any volume taken from a library “in the manner of one reading.”

On Tarmadas:

> **“non scribebat in animo tantum, sed sculpebat.”**

“He did not merely write in his mind; he carved there.”  
—*RML* II.12.

**[Documented text]** Petrarch’s governing metaphors are writing, reading, engraving, capacity, cargo, and storage.

**[Later scholarship]** Bolzoni and Torre show that such metaphors—writing in the mind, storehouse, hook, wound, impression, internalized library—are central to Petrarch’s broader memory practice and are more securely documented than Yates’s hypothetical chariots.

### 6.10 The unnamed modern friend

Petrarch describes a contemporary companion whose military memory seemed inexhaustible. Seeing or hearing something once was sufficient. Years later, he could correct Petrarch’s altered wording and identify:

- the time of the conversation;
- the oak under which it occurred;
- the river;
- the seashore;
- the mountain summit.

**[Documented text]** *RML* II.13.

**[Biographical uncertainty]** The text as transmitted here withholds or has lost the identifying name; suggested identifications must therefore remain conjectural unless supported by independent evidence.

**[Mnemonic relevance]** This is associative episodic recall—words joined to times and places—but Petrarch presents it as an innate military faculty, not as deliberate locus construction.

## 8. Worked examples and apparatus

### 8.1 Worked examples

There is no Petrarchan equivalent of:

- the bloodstained lawsuit image in *Ad Herennium* III.33;
- Camillo’s seven grades and mythological gates;
- Lullian revolving figures;
- Bruno’s letter wheels;
- Fludd’s theater stages.

The Seneca, Latro, Themistocles, and Tarmadas narratives are examples **about** memory, not demonstrations of encoding.

### 8.2 Diagrams, wheels, tables, and plates

**[Documented absence]**

- No mnemonic diagram survives as part of the authorial text.
- No wheel belongs to the work.
- No table maps virtues to loci or images.
- No plate depicts the conjectural triumphal procession.
- Billanovich’s two plates concern textual and manuscript evidence, not a mnemonic mechanism.
- BnF Latin 6069T contains decorated initials, but these are not diagrams of an artificial-memory system.

**[Romberch distinction]** Romberch’s book does contain diagrams, including cosmic and architectural locus schemes. These illustrate Romberch’s synthetic system and cannot be retrojected into Petrarch’s work.

## 4. Petrarch’s classical sources

### 4.1 Cicero, *De inventione*

- Cicero, *De inventione* II.159–165, especially II.160.
- Latin/English standard edition: H. M. Hubbell, trans., *De Inventione; De Optimo Genere Oratorum; Topica*, Loeb Classical Library 386. Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press, 1949.

This provides the hierarchy of Prudence as *memoria, intelligentia,* and *providentia*.

### 4.2 Cicero, *De oratore*

- *De oratore* II.299: Themistocles and the art of forgetting.
- II.350–360: Simonides and the place-and-image art.
- Standard edition: E. W. Sutton and H. Rackham, trans., *De Oratore*, 2 vols., Loeb Classical Library 348–349. Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press, 1942.

**[Documented dependence]** Petrarch draws on the Themistocles tradition and on Cicero’s reports of Lucullus and Hortensius.

**[Yates’s correction to a simplified genealogy]** Petrarch is not relying only on the pseudo-Ciceronian *Ad Herennium*; his wording and examples show direct engagement with *De oratore*.

### 4.3 *Rhetorica ad Herennium*

- *Rhetorica ad Herennium* III.28–40.
- Standard edition and translation: Harry Caplan, Loeb Classical Library 403. Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press, 1954.

This is the fullest ancient Latin account of artificial memory:

- loci like wax tablets;
- images like written letters;
- distinct, moderately sized, well-lit places;
- striking images;
- separate treatment of things and words.

**[Historical relevance]** Its rules explain what medieval and Renaissance readers meant by *memoria artificialis*.

**[Direct-dependence limit]** Petrarch’s *De memoria* does not quote its locus rules or reproduce its worked lawsuit image.

### 4.4 Quintilian

- Quintilian, *Institutio oratoria* XI.2.
- Standard edition: Donald A. Russell, ed. and trans., *The Orator’s Education*, Loeb Classical Library 494. Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press, 2001.

Quintilian describes loci while expressing reservations about elaborate image systems.

### 4.5 Seneca the Elder

- Seneca the Elder, *Controversiae* I, preface, especially 2–3 and 18–24.
- Standard edition: Michael Winterbottom, trans., *Declaimers*, Loeb Classical Library 463–464. Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press, 1974.

This is the source for Seneca’s feats and the memory of Porcius Latro.

### 4.6 Valerius Maximus

- Valerius Maximus, *Facta et dicta memorabilia*, especially VIII.7, on natural capacities and intellectual distinction.
- Standard edition: D. R. Shackleton Bailey, ed. and trans., *Memorable Doings and Sayings*, Loeb Classical Library 492–493. Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press, 2000.

**[Documented generic ancestry]** Valerius is the major model for a collection arranged as memorable deeds and sayings. Recent genre scholarship emphasizes this relationship more strongly than Yates’s mnemonic reading.

## 5. Romberch

### 5.1 Editions

Johannes Host von Romberch, *Congestorium artificiose memorie*:

- first published Venice, Giorgio Rusconi, 1520;
- reprinted Venice, Melchiorre Sessa, July 1533.

Digital witnesses:

- [Library of Congress, 1533 edition](https://www.loc.gov/item/10019844/)
- [Internet Archive/Wikisource edition index](https://la.wikisource.org/wiki/Congestorium_Artificiosae_Memoriae)

- [Treatise I, chapter 4](https://la.wikisource.org/wiki/Congestorium_Artificiosae_Memoriae/Tractatus_Primus/Capitulum_4)
- [Treatise II, chapter 3](https://la.wikisource.org/wiki/Congestorium_Artificiosae_Memoriae/Tractatus_Secundus/Capitulum_3)

### 5.2 Petrarch quotation from *De remediis*

Romberch quotes a genuinely Petrarchan counsel, not from *Rerum memorandarum libri* but from *De remediis utriusque fortunae*:

> **“Si tibi memoria fluxa fuerit, diligentia et artificio eam stringe.”**

“If your memory is unstable, bind it by diligence and art.”

He continues that industry meets the defects of memory and intellect and that one should not trust an unfaithful memory.

**[Documented Petrarchan statement]** Petrarch recommends art and disciplined effort as remedies for a weak memory.

**[Interpretive limit]** The quoted words do not identify loci and images as the art in question.

### 5.3 Romberch’s authority list

Romberch’s prefatory material places Petrarch among:

- Seneca;
- Cicero;
- Quintilian;
- Jacobus Publicius;
- Peter of Ravenna;
- several more recent teachers.

**[Early-modern tradition]** This is decisive evidence that by 1520 Petrarch’s name functioned as mnemonic authority.

**[Not evidence of a lost work by itself]** Lists of authorities can conflate authors who theorized memory, displayed it, supplied moral examples, recommended diligence, or actually wrote mnemonic manuals.

## Yates’s account and the scholarship since

## 1. Yates, chapter IV, pp. 101–104

Yates’s argument has four stages.

### 1.1 Petrarch’s early-modern reputation

She begins from reception: Gesualdo, Garzoni, Agrippa, Schenkel, Romberch, and an annotation in the Lucca *Encyclopédie* all associate Petrarch with memory.

Her historical question is sound:

> What generated such a persistent reputation?

### 1.2 Identification of the *Rerum memorandarum libri*

Yates proposes that no lost treatise need be assumed. The answer may lie in an extant work readers had ceased to understand as they once did.

**[Confirmed]** The book prominently organizes Prudence under memory, understanding, and foresight and contains classical artificial-memory references.

**[Not demonstrated]** These references alone do not prove that the whole compilation was encoded through loci and images.

### 1.3 Comparison with Bartolomeo da San Concordio

Yates notes the structural resemblance to the Dominican *Ammaestramenti degli antichi*.

**[Valuable comparison]** Both works are moral compilations organized around virtues and exemplary sayings.

**[Qualification after Carruthers and Bolzoni]** Medieval mnemonic culture extended far beyond a single classical architectural technique. A text may be composed for recollection, meditation, preaching, quotation, or ethical formation without embodying a strict place-and-image apparatus.

### 1.4 The triumphal-image hypothesis

Yates wonders whether Petrarch used pagan corporeal similitudes and virtues in chariots.

**[Not confirmed]** No later discovery has produced Petrarch’s supposed internal procession or its external diagram.

**[Productive heuristic]** The hypothesis stimulated study of Petrarch’s visual metaphors, the *Trionfi*, manuscripts, marginalia, and the traffic between moral exempla and imagery.

---

## 2. Yates, chapter V, p. 115

Yates calls Romberch’s book “a strange congestion of memory material” and observes:

> “By his frequent citation of the name of Petrarch, he absorbs the poet into the Dominican memory tradition.”

**[Confirmed reception history]** Romberch was a Dominican, his system repeatedly invokes Aquinas, and he lists or quotes Petrarch.

**[Qualification]** “Absorbs” accurately describes Romberch’s construction of authority; it should not be converted into proof that Petrarch himself belonged institutionally or technically to a Dominican school of mnemonics.

---

## 3. Yates, chapter XIV, p. 314

Discussing Bruno’s *Eroici furori*, Yates asks whether the tradition associating Petrarch with memory may have interpreted Petrarchan conceits as memory images.

**[Yates’s interpretation]** Bruno’s emotionally charged eyes, stars, Cupid’s arrows, emblems, and devices resemble the affectively powerful images required by mnemonic theory.

**[Disputed extension]** Bruno’s adaptation of Petrarchan lyric language does not establish that Petrarch devised those conceits as technical mnemonic images.

**[Current safer formulation]** Petrarchism supplied Bruno and emblem culture with a reusable store of vivid verbal-visual figures; Bruno’s own mnemonic and magical theory could transform that inherited imagery for purposes Petrarch never stated.

---

## 4. Mary Carruthers

Mary Carruthers, *The Book of Memory: A Study of Memory in Medieval Culture*, first ed. 1990; second ed. Cambridge University Press, 2008, changed the governing question.

Her work stresses that medieval *memoria* was:

- a trained cognitive faculty;
- a basis of composition and ethical judgment;
- organized through division, association, affect, and habitual recollection;
- supported by page layout, rubrication, imagery, textual “places,” and architectural loci without being reducible to one system.

**[Correction to Yates’s framing]** Yates often narrates “the art of memory” as a relatively unified line of place-and-image transmission. Carruthers shows a plurality of memorial practices embedded in reading, composition, prayer, and moral formation.

**[Application to Petrarch]** This supports viewing the *RML* as a structured memorial book while discouraging the claim that it must conceal an undocumented architectural palace.

Carruthers’s later *The Craft of Thought* (1998) likewise emphasizes meditative composition and crafted cognitive places.

---

## 5. Lina Bolzoni

Bolzoni’s 2004 essay “Petrarca e le tecniche della memoria (a proposito del *De remediis*)” begins from the same Ciceronian triad but extends the inquiry through Petrarch’s later writings.

She shows that in *De remediis*:

- memory, intellect, and foresight may become causes of suffering under Fortune;
- Petrarch presents memory ambivalently, as power and burden;
- moral dialogue, reading, internalized authors, and mental writing are more central than a mechanical display of loci.

The article is available through the [Scuola Normale repository](https://ricerca.sns.it/handle/11384/5044) and [direct PDF](https://ricerca.sns.it/retrieve/handle/11384/5044/61785/Petrarca_e_le_tecniche_memoria.pdf).

**[Confirmation of Yates]** The Ciceronian Prudence model matters deeply to Petrarch.

**[Correction/expansion]** Petrarchan memory must be studied across *De remediis*, letters, reading practices, and metaphors—not inferred principally from later technical manuals.

Bolzoni’s *The Gallery of Memory* broadens this into the relationship among memory arts, literary organization, galleries, emblems, and printed images.

---

## 6. Andrea Torre

Andrea Torre, *Petrarcheschi segni di memoria: Spie, postille, metafore* (Pisa: Edizioni della Normale, 2008), is the most directly relevant post-Yates monograph.

Its documented object is Petrarch’s:

- annotation of manuscripts;
- marginal signs;
- selection and internalization of passages;
- metaphors of treasure, writing, wounds, hooks, rooms, and libraries;
- later reception in Petrarchism.

The publisher summarizes the study as an inquiry into how far classical mnemonics and medieval *sancta memoria* influenced Petrarch’s intellectual and moral choices: [publisher’s description](https://edizioni.sns.it/prodotto/petrarcheschi-segni-di-memoria-spie-postille-metafore/).

**[Major advance beyond Yates]** Torre replaces the hypothetical reconstruction of one hidden “system” with materially testable evidence: books Petrarch owned, marks he made, metaphors he repeatedly used, and the later transformation of Petrarchan signs.

Paolo Rossi’s review reaches the key conclusion:

> Petrarch’s scattered references do not by themselves explain the enormous later tradition that made him an authority.

See [“Petrarca e l’arte della memoria”](https://st.ilsole24ore.com/art/cultura/2011-09-21/petrarca-arte-memoria-163121.shtml).

---

## 7. Paolo Rossi

Rossi’s *Clavis universalis*—translated as *Logic and the Art of Memory*—places Renaissance memory arts within projects for ordered, universal knowledge.

For Petrarch specifically, Rossi helps document the long bibliographical chain from memory treatises to encyclopedias. His strength here is reception history rather than proof of a lost Petrarchan manual.

**[Correction to over-linear histories]** The early-modern mnemonic tradition continually recombined rhetoric, logic, pedagogy, encyclopedism, and claims of wondrous performance. An authority’s name could travel farther than his actual doctrine.

---

## 8. Jocelyn Penny Small

Small’s *Wax Tablets of the Mind: Cognitive Studies of Memory and Literacy in Classical Antiquity* (London/New York: Routledge, 1997) questions overly literal accounts of ancient mnemonic practice and examines the interaction between literacy, internal memory, visual organization, and wax-tablet metaphors.

**[Relevance]** Petrarch’s “writing in the mind” and “carving” metaphors belong to a long cognitive-literary tradition. They cannot automatically be decoded as proof of a physical or imaginary building.

---

## 9. Hajdu and Yates’s dependence

Helga Hajdu, *Das mnemotechnische Schrifttum des Mittelalters* (Vienna, 1936), was Yates’s most important predecessor for medieval mnemonic manuals.

**[Historiographical fact]** Yates explicitly called Hajdu’s book the best account known to her of the ancient and medieval tradition.

**[Limit]** Hajdu’s catalogue-driven reconstruction and Yates’s synthetic narrative preceded the broader manuscript, reading-history, and material-book approaches developed by Carruthers, Bolzoni, and Torre.

---

## 10. Scholars named in the general brief but not directly probative here

The work of Bonner and Fidora on Lull; Sturlese, Ciliberto, Clucas, and Gatti on Bruno; Bolzoni and Stagno on Camillo; and Ong on Ramus is fundamental to those distinct systems. It does not supply missing evidence for Petrarch’s loci or images.

**[Methodological finding]** Importing conclusions from Lullian combinations, Brunian magical images, Camillo’s theater, or Ramist dialectical order into the *RML* would collapse systems that differ in purpose, machinery, chronology, and evidence.

## History and context

## 1. Classical rhetoric

The immediate conceptual ancestry is:

- Simonides’ legendary discovery of ordered places;
- *Ad Herennium* III on loci and images;
- Cicero’s *De oratore* on Simonides and Themistocles;
- Cicero’s *De inventione* on Prudence;
- Quintilian’s qualified treatment;
- Seneca the Elder’s demonstrations of prodigious recall;
- Valerius Maximus’s thematic collections of memorable deeds and sayings.

Petrarch uses two strands simultaneously:

1. **ethical classification**, chiefly from *De inventione*;
2. **exemplary history and memory anecdotes**, from Cicero, Seneca, Pliny, Suetonius, Valerius, and others.

He does not reproduce the full technical strand of the *Ad Herennium*.

---

## 2. Scholastic psychology and ethics

Albertus Magnus and Thomas Aquinas made memory a component of Prudence and interpreted artificial-memory precepts through Aristotelian psychology:

- recollection proceeds from one remembered thing to another;
- order assists recollection;
- sensible likenesses help intellectual intentions;
- affectively forceful or unusual forms adhere more strongly.

**[Contextual influence]** Petrarch inherits a culture in which *memoria* is an intellectual and moral habit, not merely verbatim recall.

**[Yates’s phrase]** His book “comes straight out of scholasticism with its pious use of artificial memory as a part of Prudence.”

**[Qualification]** Petrarch was also a forceful critic of aspects of contemporary scholastic culture. The work’s Ciceronian and Valerian humanism is not exhausted by the label “scholastic.”

---

## 3. Dominican and Franciscan traditions

Bartolomeo da San Concordio, a Dominican, arranged moral teachings for ready recollection. Romberch later placed Petrarch in a Dominicanized genealogy centered on Aquinas.

Filippo Gesualdo, a Franciscan, repeated the Petrarch–Romberch association in *Plutosofia* (Padua, 1592).

**[Institutional history]** Memory doctrine crossed order boundaries. Dominicans and Franciscans both transmitted, adapted, and taught mnemonic material.

**[Not documented]** Petrarch was neither a Dominican nor a Franciscan memory master, and no institutional record makes him a teacher within either school.

---

## 4. Courts

Petrarch composed the work amid courtly and curial networks:

- Avignon and the papal environment;
- King Robert of Naples, who examined Petrarch before his coronation;
- the Colonna circle;
- Azzo da Correggio and other patrons;
- rulers, soldiers, orators, and papal figures appearing among the modern exempla.

**[Functional setting]** At courts, memory served diplomacy, counsel, oratory, genealogy, legal knowledge, and the performance of learning.

**[Textual manifestation]** The unnamed companion’s military memory and the prominence of rulers show the practical prestige of recollection.

---

## 5. Universities

The *RML* belongs to a learned world shaped by university rhetoric, law, theology, medicine, and philosophy, but Petrarch did not write it as a university mnemonic textbook.

**[Documented context]** He studied law and read scholastic, patristic, and classical sources.

**[Distinctive humanist movement]** His collecting and philological restoration of ancient authors redirected learned memory toward historical recovery, imitation, and conversation with classical writers.

---

## 6. The commonplace and exemplum tradition

Petrarch’s closest formal ancestors include:

- Valerius Maximus;
- medieval *florilegia*;
- collections of *dicta et facta*;
- preacher’s exempla;
- moral summae;
- topical notebooks.

Its descendants include:

- humanist exemplary histories;
- commonplace books;
- printed moral anthologies;
- collections organized for quotation and composition;
- early-modern encyclopedic memory books.

The work is thus a bridge between medieval moral compilation and humanist historical exemplarity.

---

## 7. Lullism, Hermeticism, Ramism, and universal language

### Lullism

Ramon Llull’s combinatorial art manipulates letters and divine dignities through figures and combinations. It is not a direct source of the *RML*.

**[No evidence]** Petrarch’s work has no Lullian wheel or combinatorial alphabet.

### Hermeticism

Ficino, Camillo, and Bruno later connected memory with Neoplatonic and Hermetic cosmology.

**[No evidence]** The *RML* predates Ficino’s Latin *Corpus Hermeticum* and contains no Hermetic celestial-memory program.

### Ramism

Ramus later replaced or marginalized the image-locus art by emphasizing dialectical order and method.

**[Retrospective analogy only]** Petrarch’s hierarchical organization can look “methodical,” but it is not Ramist.

### Universal language and computation

Lullian combination contributed to traditions leading through Bruno and Leibniz toward formal symbolic calculation.

**[No direct Petrarch–machine line]** The *RML* contributes to encyclopedic ordering and retrievable classification, not to rotating combinatorics. Any direct “Petrarch to the computer” lineage is modern overstatement.

---

## People

## 1. Petrarch

Petrarch is simultaneously:

- compiler;
- moral historian;
- reader and textual critic;
- collector of classical examples;
- theorist of memory’s value and burden;
- later-constructed mnemonic authority.

**[Documented]** He knew classical accounts of artificial memory and recommended art and diligence to support a weak memory.

**[Undocumented]** He is not known to have operated a public memory school or demonstrated a proprietary palace system.

## 3. Transmitters

- **Johannes Romberch:** made Petrarch an authority within a Dominican-Thomist mnemonic compilation.
- **Filippo Gesualdo:** said “Petrarch, whom Romberch follows on memory,” perpetuating the association.
- **Tommaso Garzoni:** included Petrarch among celebrated “professors of memory.”
- **Cornelius Agrippa:** listed Petrarch among modern memory authorities while criticizing the art.
- **Lambert Schenkel:** claimed Petrarch avidly revived and cultivated the art.
- **Diodati, annotating the Lucca *Encyclopédie*:** retained Petrarch in the historical notice on memory.
- **Frances Yates:** recovered this chain for twentieth-century scholarship.
- **Carruthers, Bolzoni, and Torre:** reframed it through medieval memorial culture, reading practices, and material evidence.

---

## 4. Patrons and students

No securely documented pupil learned a distinct mnemonic system from Petrarch. Likewise, no patron is recorded as commissioning a Petrarchan ars memorativa.

**[Documented broader patronage]** King Robert, the Colonna, the Visconti, Azzo da Correggio, and others supported or intersected with Petrarch’s literary life.

**[Legend risk]** Patronage of Petrarch must not be recast as patronage of an undocumented memory machine.

## 5. Anecdotes requested in the general catalogue

### The auctioneer

No auctioneer anecdote belongs to the *RML* or its verified Petrarchan reception. Such stories belong to other mnemonic performers and should not be imported.

### The professor’s party

No securely sourced “professor’s party” anecdote is connected with Petrarch’s book.

### The student who lost his memory

No such Petrarchan case occurs in the relevant primary text.

### The accused magician

Two different traditions must be distinguished.

1. **Petrarch:** accused or mocked as a necromancer because of solitude, book learning, and admiration for Virgil.
2. **Lambert Schenkel:** later accused of magic because of spectacular mnemonic performances.

Neither accusation demonstrates that the *RML* was a magical memory system.

## The magic question

## 1. Is the *Rerum memorandarum libri* magical?

No.

**[Documented text]**

- no invocation;
- no planetary timing;
- no talisman;
- no angelic names;
- no magical seals;
- no consecration;
- no spirit operation;
- no *notae*;
- no ritual purity regimen;
- no claim to infused universal knowledge.

Its ethical hierarchy and exempla are Christian-humanist and rhetorical.

---

## 2. Pagan imagery

Yates conjectured that Petrarch may have used pagan images because classical subjects moved him intensely and Albertus had licensed morally useful corporeal similitudes.

**[Yates’s interpretation]** This would be mnemonic imagery, not necessarily operative magic.

**[No evidence]** The *RML* does not tell readers to animate, astralize, consecrate, or invoke pagan figures.

---

## 3. Petrarch accused of necromancy

Petrarch complained that powerful but ignorant people regarded him as a magician because he lived alone and read Virgil:

> **“Perhaps … I appear to many as a necromancer and magician.”**

He explains that his admiration for Virgil generated the suspicion. The episode is discussed in *Petrarch’s Genius* with references to Petrarch’s letters and his examination by King Robert: [open scholarly text](https://pub-ucpec2-prd.cdlib.org/ucpressebooks/view?anchor.id=0&brand=eschol&chunk.id=d0e1510&doc.view=content&docId=ft167nb0qn&toc.depth=1).

**[Documented self-report]** Petrarch says the accusation circulated.

**[Context]** Medieval Naples possessed legends of Virgil the magician; intense Virgilian study could therefore appear suspect to non-scholars.

**[Not documented]** There was no Inquisition trial of Petrarch for practicing artificial memory, and the *RML* was not condemned as heretical.

---

## 4. Petrarch’s attitude to magic and divination

Petrarch repeatedly attacks astrologers, diviners, superstition, and magical pretensions. In the *RML* he sometimes reports prophecies, dreams, and magical reputations as memorable historical material, while marking uncertainty with formulas such as *fama est* or “new writers assert.”

**[Documented nuance]** Recording a magical anecdote is not endorsement.

**[Self-positioning]** Petrarch calls himself an enemy of divination and magic.

---

## 5. The *Ars notoria*

The *Ars notoria* promised extraordinary knowledge or memory through prayers, ritual observances, and contemplation of complex *notae*.

**[Historical distinction]**

| *RML* | *Ars notoria* |
|---|---|
| Moral exempla | Ritual acquisition of arts |
| Ciceronian virtue hierarchy | Prayers, angelic mediation, figures |
| Ordinary literary composition | Claims of preternatural infusion |
| No operative images | Contemplated *notae* |
| No ecclesiastical condemnation known | Periodically suspected or condemned |

**[Conclusion]** Their connection is only that both belong to the much wider medieval history of memory and knowledge.

---

## 6. Agrippa’s criticism

In *De incertitudine et vanitate scientiarum*, ch. X, Agrippa describes artificial memory as characters, places, and representations but warns that monstrous images may confuse natural memory and cause madness. He repeats Themistocles’ desire for an art of forgetting.

See [English text, chapter X](https://www.alchemy-texts.com/book/the-vanity-of-arts-and-sciences/).

**[Reception irony]** Agrippa can list Petrarch as an authority while attacking exaggerated claims for the art. Inclusion in an authority list therefore does not mean agreement about efficacy or magic.

## Afterlife

## 1. Early-modern mnemonic authority

The traceable chain is:

> Petrarch’s texts and reputation → Romberch → Gesualdo/Garzoni/Agrippa → Schenkel → encyclopedic notices → Yates.

### Romberch

By 1520, Petrarch appears in a formal genealogy of mnemonic authors.

### Gesualdo

Filippo Gesualdo’s *Plutosofia* (Padua, 1592) describes a large memory system and refers to Petrarch through Romberch. Bibliographical record: [Folger Shakespeare Library](https://catalog.folger.edu/record/358556).

### Garzoni

Tommaso Garzoni’s *Piazza universale di tutte le professioni del mondo* first appeared in Venice in 1585 and included a discourse on memory professionals. Accessible 1593 text: [Italian Wikisource](https://it.wikisource.org/wiki/La_piazza_universale_di_tutte_le_professioni_del_mondo).

### Schenkel

Lambert Schenkel, active around 1600, marketed memory instruction across Europe and presented Petrarch as an eager reviver of the art.

**[Tradition]** Schenkel’s historical claim is evidence of Petrarch’s reputation in 1600, not independent evidence about 1343.

### *Encyclopédie*

A note attributed to Diodati in the Lucca edition, vol. X, p. 263, preserved Petrarch’s name in the history of memory.

**[Reception]** By the eighteenth century the association had become encyclopedic lore.

---

## 2. Petrarchism, emblems, and Bruno

Petrarch’s lyric vocabulary—eyes, stars, darts, fire, ice, wounds, bonds—became a shared European figurative repertoire.

Bruno’s *Eroici furori* organizes poems and commentaries around emotionally charged emblems and Petrarchan conceits.

**[Documented literary afterlife]** Bruno uses Petrarchan language.

**[Yates’s interpretation]** These conceits operate like affectively charged mnemonic images.

**[Disputed]** There is no evidence that Bruno received a technical Petrarchan mnemonic system rather than literary Petrarchism plus independent mnemonic doctrine.

---

## 3. The *Trionfi* and visual art

Petrarch’s *Trionfi* furnished processional allegories of:

- Love;
- Chastity;
- Death;
- Fame;
- Time;
- Eternity.

These generated illuminated manuscripts, cassone paintings, engravings, tapestries, and monumental imagery.

**[Documented afterlife]** The triumphal figures formed memorable ordered visual sequences.

**[Yates’s speculative retrojection]** Because these images resemble possible moral *imagines agentes*, she imagined similar chariots in Petrarch’s memory.

**[Safer conclusion]** The *Trionfi* demonstrate Petrarch’s capacity to construct serial personified imagery; they do not document the encoding procedure of the *RML*.

---

## 4. Dante and fresco cycles

Dante’s ordered otherworld and late-medieval virtue-and-vice frescoes have often been interpreted through mnemonic culture.

**[Broader scholarly context]** Their structured spaces, vivid punishments, personifications, and serial narratives are highly memorable and compatible with medieval memorial practice.

**[Contested strong thesis]** It is difficult to prove that a particular poem or fresco was designed as a technical artificial-memory scheme unless explicit instructions or use evidence survive.

Petrarch belongs in this visual-moral environment, but no specific fresco cycle is securely the diagram of his *RML*.

---

## 5. Commonplace books and encyclopedias

The work’s strongest structural afterlife lies in:

- topical excerpting;
- headings and subheadings;
- ethical taxonomies;
- collections of examples;
- indexes;
- libraries of quotations;
- encyclopedic “places.”

**[Historical continuity]** Here “place” moves between mental locus, textual topic, heading, index entry, and storage location.

---

## 6. Borges and Eco

### Borges’s Funes

Funes remembers everything but cannot abstract effectively.

**[Modern literary echo]** This parallels Petrarch’s Themistocles: perfect retention can become an intolerable burden.

**[No direct-source claim]** No evidence proves Borges constructed Funes directly from *RML* II.9.

### Umberto Eco

Eco’s fiction and essays repeatedly explore encyclopedias, libraries, signs, lists, and the tension between memory and oblivion.

**[Thematic afterlife]** The *RML* belongs to the genealogy of ordered cultural memory relevant to Eco.

**[No direct-system continuity]** Eco is not reproducing a Petrarchan locus technique.

---

## 7. Contemporary memory palaces and athletes

Modern memory athletes commonly use:

- familiar spatial routes;
- vivid or bizarre images;
- person–action–object systems;
- number or card encodings;
- spaced review.

**[Historical continuity]** These practices descend principally from the classical locus tradition recovered and repeatedly reformulated after antiquity.

**[Petrarch’s limited place]** His distinction between things and words, praise of art strengthening nature, and examples of ordered feats belong to the historical conversation. No technique used by athletes can be uniquely traced to the *RML*.

---

## 8. Computing and combinatorics

A defensible lineage is:

> ancient topical and mnemonic order → medieval classifications and Lullian combination → Renaissance encyclopedic and combinatorial arts → Leibnizian *ars combinatoria* → much later formal logic and computing.

**[Documented intellectual history]** Leibniz explicitly engaged Lullian combination.

**[Overstatement to avoid]** Petrarch did not design a programmable wheel, formal code, addressing system, or computer architecture.

**[Conceptual analogy]** The *RML*’s hierarchy resembles a browsable data taxonomy:

```text
Virtue
└── Prudence
    ├── Memory
    │   ├── Roman examples
    │   ├── Foreign examples
    │   └── Modern examples
    ├── Understanding
    └── Foresight
```

This is a useful modern analogy, not a historical claim that the codex is a computer.

## Controversies and disputes

## 1. Is it an artificial-memory “system”?

### Position A: Yates

The ethical hierarchy supplies the things to be remembered; references to artificial memory identify the intended mnemonic culture; therefore the work was probably designed for memorization.

### Position B: cautious revision

The work is organized and memorable, and Petrarch knew artificial memory, but no places, images, encoding algorithm, or retrieval instructions survive.

### Verdict

**[Best-supported]** It is a memorially useful ethical taxonomy, not a recoverable technical system in the sense of the *Ad Herennium*, Camillo, Lull, Bruno, or Fludd.

---

## 2. Did Petrarch write a lost *Ars memorativa*?

Yates acknowledges the possibility and then says it is unnecessary.

**Evidence for:**

- Romberch lists Petrarch among authors whose books on the art he had seen or among recognized authorities;
- later writers treat Petrarch as a reviver or professor of memory;
- Romberch attaches detailed rules to his name.

**Evidence against:**

- no title, incipit, manuscript, explicit self-reference, library record, or independent contemporary witness has been identified;
- genuine Petrarchan passages quoted by Romberch come from *De remediis*;
- early-modern authority lists routinely telescope disparate kinds of testimony.

### Verdict

**[Disputed hypothesis]** A lost treatise is possible in the abstract but unsupported. The burden of proof lies with a manuscript or precise early citation.

---

## 3. Are Romberch’s place rules authentically Petrarchan?

### Evidence for

Romberch says so and was closer in time to manuscript materials now lost.

### Evidence against

- the wording has not been located in Petrarch’s extant corpus;
- the rules are conventional in the classical and Renaissance tradition;
- Romberch is avowedly a compiler;
- his attribution to Aquinas of numerous loci is itself imprecise;
- Gesualdo’s later statement depends on Romberch rather than independent Petrarchan evidence.

### Verdict

**[Unverified early-modern attribution]** Cite the rules as “Romberch attributed to Petrarch,” never simply “Petrarch taught.”

---

## 4. Did Petrarch rely on the *Ad Herennium*?

**[Yates’s correction]** The relevant memory anecdotes show the importance of *De oratore*, especially Themistocles, rather than exclusive reliance on *Ad Herennium*.

**[Possible broader familiarity]** Petrarch may have known the pseudo-Ciceronian rhetoric, widely attributed to Cicero.

**[Textual result]** The *RML*’s memory section does not reproduce the *Ad Herennium*’s technical locus-and-image exposition.

---

## 5. Scholastic or humanist?

Yates emphasizes continuity with scholastic moral memory. Other scholarship emphasizes Valerius Maximus, classical recovery, biography, praise, historical exemplarity, and Petrarch’s humanist canon.

### Verdict

**[False binary]** The work is both:

- medieval in its ethical psychology and culture of memorial formation;
- humanist in its classical sources, historical voice, philological ambition, and inclusion of modern exempla.

---

## 6. Dating

The conventional dating “1343–45” rests on internal references, biography, and Billanovich’s textual reconstruction. Petoletti’s edition describes the work as begun in spring 1343 and abandoned within roughly two years. A recent discussion likewise places composition from summer 1343 to early 1345: [Insula Europea](https://www.insulaeuropea.eu/2022/05/24/un-nuovo-livio-di-petrarca-il-manoscritto-arch-s-pietro-c-132-della-biblioteca-apostolica-vaticana/).

**[Documented scholarly consensus]** 1343–45.

**[Qualification]** Individual passages may have undergone revision, and the absence of an authorially completed final form complicates exact dating.

---

## 7. Why was it unfinished?

Proposals include:

- the scale of the projected moral encyclopedia became unmanageable;
- competing projects displaced it;
- Petrarch’s intellectual priorities changed;
- the Valerian exemplary format became less satisfactory;
- the work’s increasingly evident untimeliness led to abandonment.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** No single surviving statement by Petrarch definitively explains the abandonment.

---

## 8. Was the book meant for memorization?

### Evidence supporting

- its title, “things to be remembered”;
- systematic ethical divisions;
- discrete short exempla;
- rubrics;
- artificial-memory references;
- resemblance to moral compilations used in preaching and study.

### Evidence limiting

- “memorable” can mean noteworthy rather than assigned to mnemonic loci;
- no instruction tells readers to memorize the book;
- no encoding scheme survives;
- exemplum collections also served consultation, composition, and ethical reading.

### Verdict

**[Probable general function]** It was designed to make moral-historical material memorable and reusable.

**[Unproved technical function]** It was not demonstrably designed for memorization by a particular artificial-memory palace.

---

## 9. The virtues in chariots

**Origin of reading:** Yates, 1966, phrased as “One wonders whether…”

**Carriers:** later summaries of Yates sometimes report it without the interrogative qualification.

**Evidence:**

- Petrarch’s *Trionfi*;
- widespread virtue personification;
- medieval memorable imagery;
- Albertus’s defense of corporeal similitudes.

**Counterevidence:**

- no statement in the *RML*;
- no diagram;
- no relevant manuscript image sequence;
- no user testimony.

### Verdict

**[Yates’s evocative conjecture]**, sometimes hardened by later popular retelling into **legend**.

---

## 10. Magic

**Origin of magical interpretation:** not Petrarch’s text but the broader Renaissance history in which artificial memory later combined with Hermetic, astral, and magical images.

**Evidence against for this work:**

- ordinary moral-rhetorical sources;
- no ritual apparatus;
- Petrarch’s hostility to magic;
- no prosecution or condemnation.

### Verdict

**[Modern conflation]** Calling the *RML* a magical memory system imports Bruno or the *Ars notoria* into a nonmagical work.

## Open questions

1. Did Romberch possess a Petrarchan manuscript, excerpt, or pseudo-Petrarchan mnemonic tract no longer identifiable?

2. Can the exact verbal source of each rule attributed to Petrarch at *Congestorium* ff. 19v–20r and 27v–28r be traced to *De remediis*, a letter, Publicius, Peter of Ravenna, or another intermediate compilation?

3. Did early readers annotate copies of the *RML* with artificial-memory loci or images?

4. Can ownership records establish which Dominican or Franciscan libraries transmitted the work alongside mnemonic manuals?

5. How did rubrics and page layout vary across the full manuscript tradition, and did those variants materially affect retrieval?

6. Who is Petrarch’s unnamed contemporary with the extraordinary military and episodic memory?

7. Did Gesualdo consult Romberch alone, or did he know an independent Petrarchan memory tradition?

8. At what point did “Petrarch possessed a remarkable memory” become “Petrarch wrote or taught an ars memorativa”?

9. How widely was the possibly pseudo-Petrarchan *De Christianarum rerum memoria* confused with the authentic work?

10. Can the reception of Petrarch’s lyric conceits in emblem books be tied to explicit mnemonic commentary rather than general Petrarchism?

11. Do Petrarch’s marginal signs in manuscripts of Cicero, Seneca, Valerius, and Augustine correspond systematically to the headings or examples of the *RML*?

12. Would a complete comparison between Billanovich’s apparatus and Petoletti’s text alter any memory passage materially? The available evidence suggests not, but this deserves a dedicated apparatus-level collation.

---

## Final assessment

Petrarch’s *Rerum memorandarum libri* is securely a book of morally memorable matter, organized by Ciceronian divisions and rich in classical testimony about natural and artificial memory. It recognizes the distinction between memory for things and words, art’s capacity to strengthen nature, reverse-order recall, and the burden of remembering too much.

It is not, in its surviving form, a technical place-and-image manual. Its “places” are demonstrably textual categories and only conjecturally architectural loci; its “images” are vivid narrated persons and deeds, not explicitly constructed *imagines agentes*. No diagram, wheel, table, image alphabet, or retrieval itinerary survives.

The technical “Petrarchan system” begins with Romberch, nearly two centuries later. Romberch quotes genuine advice from *De remediis*, lists Petrarch among mnemonic authorities, and attaches Petrarch’s name to conventional locus rules. That reception is historically real even where the attribution is textually unverifiable.

Yates brilliantly recovered this forgotten reputation and correctly connected Petrarch’s book with Prudence, exemplarity, and classical artificial memory. Her further reconstruction—pagan corporeal similitudes and virtues in triumphal chariots—remains an explicitly imaginative hypothesis. Carruthers, Bolzoni, and especially Torre have made possible a more secure account: Petrarch’s memory culture is visible in organization, reading, annotation, internalized authors, mental writing, affective impressions, and the book as a storehouse. That is a richer but less mechanically reconstructable system than Yates proposed.
