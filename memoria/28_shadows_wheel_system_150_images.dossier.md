# The Shadows wheel system: 150 images on concentric wheels: Research Dossier

> **Name:** The “Shadows” wheel system; more precisely, the *secunda praxis* of the *Ars memoriae* printed with *De umbris idearum*.  
> **Exponent:** Giordano Bruno (1548–1600).  
> **Date:** Paris, 1582.  
> **Source:** *De umbris idearum … ad internam scripturam*, followed by *Ars memoriae Iordani Bruni*, Paris: apud Aegidium [Gilles] Gorbinum, 1582; nineteenth-century edition, *Opera latine conscripta*, II.1, pp. 1–177, especially pp. 107–8 and 123–32; modern critical edition, ed. Rita Sturlese, Florence: Olschki, 1991; *Opere mnemotecniche*, I, ed. Michele Ciliberto, Marco Matteoli, Rita Sturlese, and Nicoletta Tirinnanzi, Milan: Adelphi, 2004.  
> **Principle in one sentence:** Bruno assigns every consonant–vowel pair in a 30 × 5 alphabet to vivid agents, actions, attributes, objects, and circumstances, then aligns five 150-member mnemonic series so that words and propositions can be translated into composite mental scenes and retrieved by reversing that encoding.  
> **Yates:** *The Art of Memory*, ch. IX, especially pp. 208 and 212–225; Plate 11; Plates 12a–b; Fig. 8; recalled at pp. 249, 334, and 383.

## Evidentiary key

- **[D] Documented:** stated or visibly present in a primary source.
- **[SR] Scholarly reconstruction:** an inference supported by textual, bibliographical, or iconographic evidence.
- **[Y] Yates’s interpretation:** Yates’s own reconstruction or historical thesis.
- **[T] Tradition:** reported in an ancient or early-modern source but not independently verifiable.
- **[Disputed]:** materially contested in scholarship.
- **[L] Legend:** narratively transmitted without adequate contemporary documentation.
- **[MI] Modern invention:** a recent analogy or reconstruction that should not be attributed to Bruno.

## Basic identification

### Title, printing, and physical composition

**[D]** The 1582 title page reads:

> *Iordanus Brunus Nolanus, De umbris idearum. Implicantibus artem, quaerendi, inveniendi, iudicandi, ordinandi, & applicandi: ad internam scripturam, & non vulgares per memoriam operationes explicatis. Ad Henricum III … Parisiis, apud Aegidium Gorbinum, sub insigne Spei, e regione gymnasii Cameracensis. M.D.LXXXII. Cum privilegio regis.*

Gloss: “Giordano Bruno of Nola, On the Shadows of Ideas, comprising an art of seeking, discovering, judging, ordering, and applying, explained for inner writing and uncommon operations through memory.”

The digitized 1582 copy identifies Gilles/Ægidius Gorbin as printer-bookseller and specifies his shop opposite the Collège de Cambrai. The volume is dedicated to Henri III of France. A digitized copy derives from the Bayerische Staatsbibliothek; another Google scan records Ghent University provenance. The Folger also catalogs a copy of the Paris edition. [The 1582 scan](https://books.google.it/books?id=WmZZAAAAcAAJ), [Ghent copy record](https://books.google.com/books?id=j4IPAAAAQAAJ), [Folger catalog](https://catalog.folger.edu/record/370428).

**[D]** The commonly cited “*De umbris idearum*” actually contains distinguishable components:

1. dedicatory matter and poems;
2. the *Dialogus praelibatorius apologeticus* among Hermes, Philothimus, and Logifer;
3. thirty *intentiones umbrarum*;
4. thirty *conceptus idearum*;
5. the practical *Ars memoriae*;
6. additional abbreviated arts and diagrams.

The practical lists behind Yates’s Plate 11 belong to the *Ars memoriae*, not simply to the philosophical sixty theses.

**[SR]** Bibliographers normally treat *De umbris* and *Ars memoriae* as one publishing unit because the latter follows in the same 1582 volume without a separate conventional title-page. “The Shadows system” is therefore convenient but imprecise.

### The first major correction to the catalogue description

**[D]** Bruno’s own instruction is:

> *Conficies igitur ad similitudinem trium praedictarum rotarum: quinque rotas fixas, quarum singulae centum et quinquaginta constent combinationibus elementorum duorum. Quorum exterior atque prima significet agentes sub inventorum nomine. Secunda actiones. Tertia insignia. Quarta adstantia. Quinta circumstantias.*

Gloss: “You will therefore construct, after the likeness of the three wheels described above, five fixed wheels, each consisting of 150 combinations of two letters. The outer and first signifies agents under the names of inventors; the second actions; the third insignia; the fourth things standing by; the fifth circumstances.” *Ars memoriae*, *Secunda praxis*, VI; nineteenth-century *Op. lat.* II.1, approximately pp. 122–24. The searchable Latin transcription preserves the passage and the ensuing tables. [Latin text](https://www.esotericarchives.com/bruno/arsmemor.htm).

That sentence establishes three important points:

- **[D]** five wheels, not four;
- **[D]** each has 150 two-letter combinations;
- **[D]** Bruno calls them *rotas fixas*, “fixed wheels.”

**[Y/Disputed]** Yates instead combined the astronomical, natural-object, adjective, and inventor lists into one vast set of concentric revolving rings. That construction is Plate 11 of *The Art of Memory*, not a surviving plate from 1582. Yates explicitly calls it “my attempt to excavate the ‘secret’ of *Shadows*” and admits that “we shall never understand this thing in detail.” Her identification of a single “astral power station” at the center is consequently a conjectural interpretation, not Bruno’s caption or diagram.

**[SR]** Rita Sturlese’s 1991 critical edition, followed in different ways by Francesco Torchia and Marco Matteoli, reconstructs a practical phonetic and verbal encoding apparatus. The Warburg Institute now expressly says that Yates misplaced the planetary images and that her magical concentric reconstruction “is not completely accurate.” [Warburg reassessment](https://warburg.sas.ac.uk/research-fellowships/research-projects/archived-research-projects/giordano-bruno/yates-brunos-mnemonics).

# The system in detail

## 1. Two distinct practices

Bruno’s exposition is easiest to understand if its two practical levels are separated.

### The *prima praxis*: elemental or syllabic combinations

**[D]** Thirty base agents are attached to thirty letters or characters. Bruno begins:

- A — Lycaon
- B — Deucalion
- C — Apollo
- D — Argus

The next ring supplies characteristic actions:

- AA — Lycaon at a banquet
- BB — Deucalion with the stones
- CC — Apollo with Python
- DD — Argus with cattle

A third supplies transferable insignia:

- AAA — Lycaon at the banquet with a chain
- BBB — Deucalion with the stones and a headband
- CCC — Apollo and Python with a baldric
- DDD — Argus and the cattle with a hood.

**[D]** Rotation or reassignment lets one create hybrid images:

- BAA: Deucalion performing Lycaon’s banquet action and bearing Lycaon’s chain;
- MAD: Perseus at a banquet with a hood;
- CAD: Apollo at a banquet with a hood;
- COD: Apollo with Proserpina and a hood.

These examples are described in *Ars memoriae*, around *Op. lat.* II.1, pp. 107–12. The Warburg Institute gives the combinations and explains their function. [Warburg reconstruction](https://warburg.sas.ac.uk/research-fellowships/research-projects/archived-research-projects/giordano-bruno/yates-brunos-mnemonics).

**[D]** What changes across the rings is grammatical and pictorial function:

1. **agent** — the principal acting figure;
2. **action** — what the agent does;
3. **insignium** — a salient carried or worn sign;
4. in the enlarged system, **adstans** — an accompanying thing or figure;
5. **circumstantia** — setting or surrounding circumstance.

**[SR]** This is recognizably an early form of multi-component image encoding. The modern “person–action–object” analogy is useful but anachronistic: Bruno’s five grammatical-pictorial functions do not coincide exactly with modern PAO systems.

### The *secunda praxis*: complete terms

Bruno describes the second practice as:

> *Secunda praxis quae terminorum simplicium est ad quamcumque compositionem de pluribus elementorum combinationibus praesentandam.*

Gloss: “The second practice is one of simple terms, for representing any composition by several combinations of elements.”

**[D]** He directs the reader to expand from thirty to 150 items by placing five vowel subdivisions under each of thirty consonantal signs:

> *Sicut ad triginta elementorum numerum triginta tibi statueras agentes, actiones et insignia, circumstantias et adstantia: ita nunc ordinate disponas centum quinquaginta; quod fit singula adsistentium elementorum per quinarium subsistentium deducendo.*

Gloss: “As for thirty elements you had established thirty agents, actions, insignia, circumstances and attendants, so now arrange 150 in order; this is done by deriving beneath each of the standing elements five subsidiary elements.”

The address system is thus:

`AA, AE, AI, AO, AV; BA, BE, BI, BO, BV …`

through thirty consonantal or symbolic headings. In normalized modern descriptions U is usually written where Bruno’s typography uses V.

## 2. What serves as places

### Bruno’s answer

**[D]** Bruno does not simply discard the classical distinction between *loci* and *imagines*. In the *Ars memoriae* he defines the primary mnemonic subject as:

> *Primum ergo subiectum est technica extensio, sive sinus in phantastica facultate, ordinatus. Ex speciebus receptaculorum consitus … visa omnia atque audita suo recipiens ordine et ad animae libitum retinens.*

Gloss: “The primary subject is an artificial extension, or ordered recess, in the imaginative faculty, constructed from representations of receptacles, receiving everything seen and heard in its order and retaining it at the soul’s command.”

The *subiectum* is therefore the receiving place or mnemonic substrate; the *adiectum* is the form deposited upon it.

**[D]** Bruno compares the art to internal painting and internal writing:

> *Est pictura intrinseca, cum rerum et operum memorandorum producit imagines. Est etiam scriptura intrinseca, cum rationum et verborum ordinat atque tribuit signa, notas et characteres.*

Gloss: “It is inward painting when it produces images of things and deeds to be remembered; it is inward writing when it orders and assigns signs, marks, and characters to arguments and words.”

**[D]** He claims an innovation beyond fixed material architecture:

> *Nec locis materialibus … ultra indiguimus, nec ordini locorum memorandorum ordinem adstrinximus: sed puro phantasiae architecto innixi, ordini rerum memorandarum locorum ordinem adligavimus.*

Gloss: “We no longer required material places, nor did we bind the order of things to be remembered to an already existing order of places; relying on the pure architect of fantasy, we bound the order of the places to the order of the things to be remembered.”

### Yates’s answer

**[Y]** Yates’s memorable formulation is that “the revolving wheels of the Lullian Art have become the places for the reception of the images.” This captures Bruno’s substitution of a formal address-grid for a walked building, but it overstates the evidence when applied to the single Plate 11 super-wheel. Bruno still discusses imaginary buildings, rooms, subjects, and receptacles elsewhere in the same treatise.

### Corrected formulation

**[SR]** In the second practice the stable two-letter cells function as addresses. The vivid scenes indexed to those cells function both as alphabetic code-signs and as mnemonic imagery. A separate architectural or scenic background can receive the composite. Thus “place” operates at several levels:

- the ordered two-letter address;
- the fixed position on a table or imagined wheel;
- the image that serves as a reusable mnemonic substrate;
- a larger mental room or scene into which the composite image is inserted.

## 3. How order is fixed

**[D]** Order is fixed by the thirty-character series and its five-vowel subdivisions. Each image has a unique address such as `NA`, `NE`, `NI`, `NO`, `NV`.

**[D]** Bruno insists on separation, visible difference, proportion, and repeated review. Places must be:

- visibly or imaginatively apprehensible;
- proportioned to human scale;
- neither too intense nor too faint;
- spatially distinct;
- separated sufficiently that images do not overwrite one another;
- varied rather than monotonously uniform;
- repeatedly traversed until recall becomes habitual.

He compares overlapping images to “letters laid over letters” and “seals over seals,” which erase or confuse one another.

**[D]** Bruno also recommends animation. Empty places should not be paired with empty forms:

> *Caveto igitur ne vulgatum illud experiaris: vacua vacuis. Frustra enim sperabis … quod parietes clamabunt: lapides dabunt vocem suam.*

Gloss: “Take care not to experience the proverb ‘empty things with empty things.’ You will wait in vain for the walls to cry out and the stones to give their voice.”

**[D]** Images should differ in mode of subsistence, size, shape, dress, attitude, position, action, passion, giving, receiving, adding, subtracting, binding, and separating. This develops the classical *imagines agentes* rule.

## 4. How encoding and retrieval work

### Encoding

**[SR, grounded in Bruno]**

1. Break a word into encodable consonant–vowel or comparable phonetic units.
2. Find each unit’s two-letter address.
3. Take, in sequence, the agent, action, insignium, attendant, and circumstance belonging to those addresses.
4. Fuse them into one striking composite scene.
5. Place or contemplate that scene at its mnemonic address or in a chosen larger subject.
6. Repeat until the image-answer connection becomes automatic.

### Sturlese’s worked reconstruction: *numeratore*

**[SR]** Sturlese’s celebrated example encodes *numeratore* through successive syllables:

- `NU` → Apis;
- `ME` → *in tapeta*, engaged with a carpet or tapestry;
- `RA` → *deploratus*, lamenting or deplored;
- `TO` → *compedes*, fetters;
- `RE` → a circumstance describing a woman upon a many-headed hydra.

The resulting scene is approximately: Apis works a tapestry, in a lamentable condition, wearing fetters, before a woman extending empty hands while riding a three-necked, many-headed hydra.

The Warburg summary renders it as “the god Apis weaving a rug and wearing rags with wood blocks on his feet, with, in the background, a woman stretching out her hands and riding a hydra with many heads.” [Warburg, lines 337–41](https://warburg.sas.ac.uk/research-fellowships/research-projects/archived-research-projects/giordano-bruno/yates-brunos-mnemonics).

**[Disputed]** This example demonstrates plausible reversible word encoding, but it does not prove that foreign-language vocabulary was the system’s sole or even principal purpose. Sturlese’s “adult toy for learning foreign words” formulation is deliberately deflationary. Bruno promises applications to arguments, sciences, classification, discovery, and all operations of the soul.

### Retrieval

**[D/SR]** Recall reverses the procedure:

1. reactivate the composite scene;
2. identify its indexed agent, action, attribute, attendant, and background;
3. translate those components back into their letter-pairs;
4. reassemble the word, proposition, or ordered subject.

The reliability of retrieval depends on prior mastery of the 150-image dictionaries. The principal practical objection, already voiced by ancient and early-modern critics, is that one may have to remember more coding apparatus than the target material.

## 5. The five 150-member series

### Series I: agents under the names of inventors

**[D]** The first supplied wheel begins:

> `AA Rhegima — panem castanearum`  
> `AE Osiris — in agriculturam`  
> `AI Ceres — in iuga bouum`  
> `AO Triptolemus — serit`  
> `AV Pitumnus — stercorat`

Gloss: Rhegima, chestnut bread; Osiris, agriculture; Ceres, ox-yokes; Triptolemus, sowing; Pitumnus, manuring.

The sequence proceeds through agriculture, tools, crafts, medicine, divination, religion, music, warfare, writing, astronomy, philosophy, and memory. [Latin table, lines 439–616](https://www.esotericarchives.com/bruno/arsmemor.htm).

Selected entries include:

- `ME Circe — fascinatrix`: Circe, enchantress;
- `MI Pharphacon — necromantus`: necromancer;
- `MV Hostanes — demonum lacessitor`: provoker or summoner of demons;
- `NA Zoroaster — in magiam`: magic;
- `NE Suah — chyromantus`: chiromancy;
- `NI Chaldaeus — pyromantus`: pyromancy;
- `NO Attalus — in hydromantiam`: hydromancy;
- `XE Theut — in literas`: writing;
- `ZI Xenophanes — in mundos innumeros`: innumerable worlds;
- `ZO Plato — in ideas et ab ideis`: Ideas and derivation from Ideas;
- `ZV Raymundus — in novem elementa`: Ramon Llull’s nine elements;
- `ψA Ior. — in clavim et umbras`: Jordanus, the Key and the Shadows;
- `DV Melicus — in memoriam`: Melicus/Simonides, memory.

**[Y/SR]** Yates correctly recognized `Ior.` as Bruno inserting himself among the inventors. Her further narrative—that the entire procession deliberately climaxes in Bruno before returning cyclically to Simonides—is plausible literary interpretation, not an explicit instruction.

**[Y/Disputed]** Yates proposed that the first member of each group of five links backward and the fifth forward. Some local transitions support this, but Bruno does not state it as a universal rule and corrupt printing complicates the sequence.

### Series II: actions

**[D]** Bruno says the second wheel signifies *actiones*. Some action material appears attached to the inventor entries themselves, which contributed to Yates’s reading of the inventor and invention as two adjacent rings.

**[SR]** Sturlese’s collation showed that the functions must be interpreted grammatically across the five-wheel mechanism, not as independent cosmic ontological bands.

### Series III: insignia or notable adjuncts

**[D]** The lists include attachable or wearable objects such as:

- chain;
- ring;
- wing;
- bundle;
- noose;
- quiver;
- baldric;
- sieve;
- millstone;
- yoke;
- purse;
- fan;
- sceptre;
- scourge;
- flask;
- trumpet;
- sword;
- trophy;
- cowl;
- linen cloth;
- golden girdle;
- clock.

Headings such as *collo aptabilia* and *ad pedes aptabilia* classify items by where they can be attached.

### Series IV: attendants, materials, objects, and environments

**[D]** The “natural-object” table begins:

> `aa Oliua; ae laurus; ai myrtus; ao rosmarinum; av cypressus.`

It later mixes plants, animals, metals, weather phenomena, weapons, sacred furnishings, body parts, monsters, and constructed objects: lightning, sword, axe, arrow, triple tiara, altar, seven candlesticks, owl, hydra, furnace, tomb, mirror, Pandora’s jar, cornucopia.

**[Y]** Yates treated this as the wheel of the lower creation moving under the astronomical wheel.

**[SR]** The table certainly supplies concrete, visually memorable objects. Its role in the syntax of the five-wheel composition is better documented than the claimed cosmological dependence.

### Series V: adjectives, states, or circumstances

**[D]** The adjective list begins:

> `AA nodosum; AE mentitum; AI involutum; AO informe; AV famosum.`

Gloss: knotty; false or feigned; involved/wrapped; formless; famous.

It continues with terms such as inactive, unworthy, clothed, recumbent, enchanted, bound, struck, festive, expected, rooted, broken, woven, mysterious, portentous, shipwrecked, chained, wandering, putrid, pestilential, lascivious, ravenous, and stupefying.

**[Y]** Yates confessed that she could not explain either the accusative case or the “extraordinary selection.”

**[SR]** Later grammatical reconstruction makes the accusative intelligible: these neuter accusatives can qualify an implied object or supply a transformable predicate/state within a composite scene. They are operational descriptors, not necessarily an ontological “third cosmic level.”

## 6. The astronomical lists

The astronomical material occupies 150 indexed positions:

- 36 decan images;
- 49 planetary images, seven per planet;
- the lunar dragon plus 28 lunar mansions;
- 36 images for the twelve astrological houses, three per house.

The arithmetic is exact: 36 + 49 + 29 + 36 = 150.

### The decans

The heading is:

> *Imagines facierum signorum ex Teucro Babylonico quae ad usum praesentis artis quam commode trahi possunt.*

Gloss: “Images of the faces of the signs from Teucer the Babylonian, which may conveniently be adapted to the use of the present art.”

For Aries Bruno writes:

> *Ascendit in prima facie Arietis homo niger, immodicae staturae, ardentibus oculis, severo vultu, stans candida praecinctus palla.*

Gloss: “In the first face of Aries there rises a dark man of enormous stature, with burning eyes and a severe expression, standing wrapped in a white mantle.”

> *In secunda mulier non invenusta, alba induta tunica, pallio vero Tyrio colore intincto superinduta, soluta coma, et lauro coronata.*

Gloss: “In the second, a not-unattractive woman in a white tunic and a Tyrian-purple mantle, with loose hair and a laurel crown.”

> *In tertia homo pallidus, ruffi capilli, rubris indutus vestibus, in sinistra auream gestans armillam, et ex robore baculum in dextra …*

Gloss: “In the third, a pale red-haired man in red clothing, bearing a golden bracelet in his left hand and an oaken staff in his right …”

**Correction:** the third Aries figure holds a bracelet and staff in the surviving Latin transcription, not a “sphere and staff.” Yates’s English précis or the nineteenth-century plate may have encouraged the latter reading.

For Gemini:

> *In prima Geminorum facie, vir paratus ad serviendum, virgam habens in dextera. Vultu hilari atque iocundo.*

Gloss: “In the first face of Gemini, a man ready to serve, holding a rod in his right hand, with a cheerful and pleasant expression.”

[Decan text and images](https://www.esotericarchives.com/bruno/arsmemor.htm).

### The planets

Bruno’s heading for Saturn states that the seven images come “from Egyptian and Persian philosophers” and can serve as places or subjects:

> *Sequuntur septem Saturni imagines ex Aegyptiis et Persis philosophis, quae etiam pro locis et subiectis usuvenire possunt.*

The first Saturn image is:

> *Homo cervini vultus super draconem, habens bubonem in dextra qui serpentem deglutit.*

Gloss: “A man with a stag’s face upon a dragon, holding in his right hand an owl which swallows a serpent.”

Other planetary images include Jupiter with a ram’s head seated on a wheel; Mars riding a lion; the Sun as a crowned woman in a golden chariot; Mercury with a serpent-entwined sceptre; and the Moon as a horned woman riding a dolphin.

**[D]** These are explicitly permitted to function *pro locis et subiectis*—as mnemonic places or subjects. That phrase is decisive: the celestial images are not merely “content”; they can furnish reusable mnemonic substrates.

**[SR]** Most correspond closely to Cornelius Agrippa, *De occulta philosophia* II.37–44. Yates’s source identification is broadly correct.

**[Disputed]** Borrowing images from a book of celestial magic does not by itself establish that every mnemonic use was talismanic. Textual genealogy and operative intention are separate questions.

### Lunar dragon and mansions

**[D]** Bruno gives the *Draco lunae* and 28 lunar-mansion images. Agrippa II.46 is the immediate printed source for much of the series.

**[Y]** Yates makes these images the mechanism by which zodiacal and planetary influence passes into the lower world.

**[SR]** That reflects contemporary astrological theory, but Bruno’s practical chapter also treats the images as indexed mnemonic material. Both meanings may coexist; neither licenses the assertion that physically turning the table changes astral influence.

### The houses

**[D]** Three images are supplied for each of twelve houses: foundation and birth; possessions; siblings; parents and burial; children and gifts; sickness and service; marriage; death; religion and journeys; rule; benefactions; confinement and enemies.

Examples include:

> *Primae domus imago prima, homo fundamenta iaciens … fonti scaturienti proximus.*

“A man laying foundations … near a spring.”

> *Imago secunda. Vir ad mensam sedens, et eius uxor parturiens, et obstetrices duae iuxta illam.*

“A man seated at table, his wife giving birth, with two midwives beside her.”

> *Imago tertia. Nuda mulier volvens rotam, vestem habens ante oculos, stans super globum, sub quo agitantur fluctuantes aquae.*

“A naked woman turning a wheel, with a cloth before her eyes, standing on a globe beneath which waters surge.”

**[Y/SR]** Yates compared the series to Leonhard Reymann’s *Nativität-Kalender* (Nuremberg, 1515), but judged Bruno’s versions extensively altered and partly original. That remains a reasonable source comparison, though “of Bruno’s own composing” should be stated as inference rather than fact.

## 7. Diagrams, wheels, tables, and plates

### Surviving 1582 material

**[D]** The original printing contains woodcut diagrams and tables. It is Bruno’s earliest surviving printed book with woodcuts; Mino Gabriele’s *Corpus iconographicum* inventories the engravings. [Gabriele text](https://dokumen.pub/corpus-iconographicum-le-incisioni-nelle-opere-a-stampa-9788845916670.html).

Important surviving figures include:

- elementary concentric wheels illustrating combinations among agents, actions, and insignia;
- the contracted diagram preceding the 150-member tables;
- zodiac-sign woodcuts accompanying the decans;
- planetary headings and indexed image lists;
- a twelve-house horoscope diagram;
- later *figura fecunda* and related diagrams.

**[D]** Bruno explains why only one contracted wheel is printed:

> *Quoniam difficile est quinque rotas in modicam contrahere latitudinem, unam tantum apponimus ad cuius similitudinem sunt aliae: eandemque non extensam sed contractam.*

Gloss: “Since it is difficult to compress five wheels into a narrow space, we print only one, after whose likeness the others are made, and that one contracted rather than extended.”

This is the textual warrant for reconstructing five analogous devices. It is not warrant for Yates’s precise cosmological nesting.

### Yates Plate 11

**[Y]** Plate 11 is an enormous circular reconstruction:

- thirty radial sectors;
- each sector divided into five vowel cells;
- a central astronomical band;
- surrounding natural-object and adjective bands;
- outer inventor and invention bands;
- letters and text distributed around the circumference.

Yates states that it is her own “plan,” designed to communicate the “general lay-out” and “appalling complexity.” The inscriptions were never intended to be fully readable at book size.

**[Disputed]** Plate 11’s chief error is functional placement: it turns coordinate dictionaries into ontologically concentric cosmic levels and makes the astronomical series the central driver.

### Yates Plates 12a–b

**[D/Y]** Plates 12a–b reproduce pages from the 1886 Naples edition, not directly from the 1582 exemplar:

- 12a: heading for Teucer’s images, an Aries zodiac cut, and the first three decans;
- 12b: Taurus and Gemini with their three decans and letter-pair labels.

They are reproductions of an edition that itself reproduced or reset the earlier material. They should not be described as autonomous Renaissance talismanic plates.

### Fig. 8

**[D]** Fig. 8 reproduces Bruno’s smaller combinatory wheels. These are authentic evidence that Bruno worked with rotating or mutually alignable circular diagrams.

**[Disputed]** Authentic smaller wheels do not prove that every subsequent 150-entry table was mentally or physically nested and revolved in the exact manner shown by Plate 11.

# The sources

## Primary Brunian texts

### *De umbris idearum* / *Ars memoriae*

- **Editio princeps:** Giordano Bruno, *De umbris idearum … Ars memoriae*, Paris: Gilles Gorbin, 1582.
- **Nineteenth-century edition:** *Opera latine conscripta publicis sumptibus edita*, ed. Francesco Fiorentino et al., vol. II.1, Naples: Domenico Morano, 1886, pp. 1–177.
- **Critical edition:** Giordano Bruno, *De umbris idearum*, ed. Rita Sturlese, Florence: Leo S. Olschki, 1991, 249 pp.
- **Modern collected edition:** *Opere mnemotecniche*, vol. I, ed. Michele Ciliberto, Marco Matteoli, Rita Sturlese, and Nicoletta Tirinnanzi, Milan: Adelphi, 2004.
- **Spanish translation:** *Las sombras de las ideas*, introduction and translation associated with Jordi Raventós and modern Spanish Brunian scholarship, Madrid: Siruela, 2009.
- **Online Latin transcription:** Joseph H. Peterson, 1997–2019, based on Morano 1886—not a critical substitute for Sturlese. [*De umbris*](https://www.esotericarchives.com/bruno/umbris.htm), [*Ars memoriae*](https://www.esotericarchives.com/bruno/arsmemor.htm).

### Related Bruno works

- *Cantus Circaeus ad eam memoriae praxim ordinatus quam ipse iudiciariam appellat*, Paris, 1582.
- *De compendiosa architectura et complemento artis Lullii*, Paris, 1582.
- *Ars reminiscendi*, *Explicatio triginta sigillorum*, and *Sigillus sigillorum*, London, 1583.
- *De imaginum, signorum et idearum compositione*, Frankfurt, 1591.
- *De magia* and *De vinculis in genere*, manuscript works, composed principally in the late 1580s–early 1590s and first printed much later.
- Lost or unlocated: *Clavis magna*, repeatedly cited by Bruno as the fuller foundation of his art.

### Manuscripts and early copies

**[D]** *De umbris* survives as an early printed book, not as an authorial manuscript. No autograph manuscript of the work or of its five completed 150-image wheels is known.

**[D]** The census of early Bruno printing is Rita Sturlese, *Bibliografia, censimento e storia delle antiche stampe di Giordano Bruno*, Florence: Olschki, 1987. The Folger’s copy of that census is PQ4615.B5 Z7. [Catalog record](https://catalog.folger.edu/record/47835).

Digitized or catalogued first-edition witnesses include:

- Bayerische Staatsbibliothek copy digitized by Google Books;
- Ghent University copy, Google Books ID `j4IPAAAAQAAJ`;
- Folger Shakespeare Library copy, catalog record 370428;
- copies inventoried through the Archivio Giordano Bruno and Sturlese census.

**Absence finding:** a complete, freely searchable online concordance tying every known 1582 witness to a current shelfmark was not available in the sources consulted. Shelfmarks should therefore be checked against Sturlese’s census and current institutional catalogs rather than inferred from scan IDs.

## Classical mnemonic sources

### *Rhetorica ad Herennium*

Anonymous, first century BCE, III.16–24 (modern sectioning III.28–40). Standard English translation: Harry Caplan, Loeb Classical Library 403, Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press, 1954.

The classic lawsuit image is:

> *Aegrotum in lecto cubantem … reum ad lectum eius adstituemus, dextra poculum, sinistra tabulas, medico testiculos arietinos tenentem.*

The textual versions vary slightly in punctuation and transmitted wording. Caplan’s translation gives the defendant at the bedside “holding in his right hand a cup, and in his left tablets, and on the fourth finger a ram’s testicles.” Cup = poison; tablets = will/inheritance; ram’s testicles = *testes*, witnesses. The remaining counts are placed successively in ordered backgrounds. [Latin and Loeb translation](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Rhetorica_ad_Herennium/3%2A.html).

**[D]** The source distinguishes:

- *memoria rerum*: images summarize matters or arguments;
- *memoria verborum*: images encode verbal expression.

Sturlese’s correction depends on taking Bruno’s renewed *memoria verborum* seriously.

### Cicero

Cicero, *De oratore* II.350–60. Standard Loeb: E. W. Sutton and H. Rackham.

**[T]** Cicero attributes the origin of the art to Simonides’ recognition of the positions of guests killed in a collapsed banquet hall. He concludes that persons seeking to train memory must select places and form mental images of what they wish to remember, placing images in locations so that spatial order preserves the order of things. [Loeb passages](https://www.laits.utexas.edu/memoria/Cicero.html).

### Quintilian

Quintilian, *Institutio oratoria* XI.2. Standard modern Loeb: Donald A. Russell, 2001; older Loeb translation by H. E. Butler.

**[D]** Quintilian describes memorizing a spacious building, assigning ideas to rooms and furnishings, and mentally retracing the route. He accepts that memory can be trained but doubts the efficiency of elaborate word-by-word imagery. [Latin and English](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Quintilian/Institutio_Oratoria/11B%2A.html).

## Lullian sources

Ramon Llull’s authentic Art developed across several phases:

- *Ars compendiosa inveniendi veritatem*, c. 1274;
- *Ars demonstrativa*, 1283;
- *Ars inventiva veritatis*, 1290;
- *Ars generalis ultima*, 1305–08;
- *Ars brevis*, 1308.

**[D]** Llull’s letters stand for divine dignities, relations, questions, subjects, virtues, and vices; rotating figures produce ordered combinations for demonstration and disputation.

**[Correction]** Llull’s Art is not originally an art of memory and not simply a combinatorial randomizer. Anthony Bonner and the modern Barcelona school emphasize its demonstrative, relational, and missionary-logical purposes. Bruno consciously transforms it.

The authoritative open research gateway is the Universitat de Barcelona’s Ramon Llull Database, created by Anthony Bonner and containing authentic, apocryphal, and Lullist works plus manuscripts by city and library. [Llull DB](https://www.ub.edu/llulldb/), [SEP overview](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/llull/).

## Immediate astrological and encyclopedic sources

- Heinrich Cornelius Agrippa, *De occulta philosophia libri tres*, II.37: decans; II.37–44: planetary images; II.46: lunar mansions.
- Polydore Vergil, *De inventoribus rerum*, Venice, 1499 and later editions: major source for the inventor catalogue.
- Leonhard Reymann, *Nativität-Kalender*, Nuremberg, 1515: comparative source for house imagery.
- Astrological decan traditions attributed to Teucer of Babylon and transmitted through Arabic and Latin intermediaries.
- Cecco d’Ascoli’s commentary on Sacrobosco: mentions a *Liber de umbris idearum* attributed in learned-magic tradition to Solomon.

**[Disputed]** Yates’s claim that Bruno took his title from Cecco’s necromantic reference is possible but not demonstrated by a verbal quotation or citation from Bruno.

# Yates’s account and scholarship since

## Yates’s reconstruction

Yates’s Chapter IX proceeds through five claims:

1. **[Y]** Bruno fused classical architectural memory with Lullian wheels.
2. **[Y]** The 150-entry tables were concentric rings on one enormous wheel.
3. **[Y]** Astral images occupied the center and powered the lower levels.
4. **[Y]** These images were inward talismans or *sigilli* that drew stellar power into the psyche.
5. **[Y]** The entire mechanism was a Hermetic “mind machine” anticipating later mechanical combination.

Her strongest formulations include:

> “No one has realised that the lists of images given in the book, each list consisting of 150 images in sets of thirty are intended to be set out on concentric wheels.”

> “This heavily inscribed central wheel is the astral power station, as it were, which works the whole system.”

> “How did the system work? By magic, of course.”

> “He puts the image of himself on the wheel, for has he not himself produced a very great invention?”

> “Bruno’s list of images of inventors … is … absolutely within the orthodox tradition of the classical art.”

The last claim remains substantially sound: named, animated persons are excellent *imagines agentes*. The first three are reconstructions; the fourth is the main contested thesis.

## What later work confirmed

### Practical sophistication

**[Confirmed]** Bruno did create a technically sophisticated synthesis of alphabetic coding, vivid imagery, grammar, and circular tabulation.

### Classical inheritance

**[Confirmed]** The *Ars memoriae* explicitly treats subjects/places, added images, vividness, separation, visual scale, repetition, and internal writing. Its debt to the *Ad Herennium*, Cicero, medieval mnemonic commentary, and Dominican practice is real.

### Lullian transformation

**[Confirmed, qualified]** Bruno adapted circular combination and alphabetic notation from Lullism. Bonner and Fidora’s work makes the qualification essential: Bruno’s thirty-letter, image-saturated phonetic apparatus is not “genuine Llull” but an aggressive Renaissance re-engineering.

### Astral sources

**[Confirmed]** Agrippa and related astrological traditions supplied many decan, planetary, and lunar images. Yates’s source tracing remains valuable.

### Philosophical ambition

**[Confirmed]** Bruno says the higher form of the art orders all operations of the soul and opens a way toward discovering many faculties. Stephen Clucas therefore warns against reducing the system to a trick for vocabulary alone. Bruno unites practice, psychology, and metaphysics. [Clucas article](https://www.persee.fr/doc/xvii_0291-3798_2004_num_58_1_1969).

## What was corrected or overturned

### Rita Sturlese

**[Correction]** Sturlese’s 1991 edition demonstrated that Yates misplaced functions and insufficiently distinguished *memoria rerum* from *memoria verborum*. The five wheels are coordinated syntactic/image dictionaries capable of encoding terms.

**[Correction]** The adjectives’ grammatical form is functional rather than inexplicable.

**[Correction]** “Five fixed wheels” conflicts with Yates’s picture of independently turning cosmic bands.

**[Disputed]** Sturlese’s foreign-language-learning emphasis is illuminating but may be too narrow for Bruno’s declared universal ambitions.

### Francesco Torchia

**[Correction/alternative]** Torchia, “La chiave delle ombre,” *Intersezioni* 17.1 (1997), pp. 131–51, objects that a purely spelling-oriented mechanism imposes excessive mnemonic overhead. He restores the inventors and planetary figures as semantically generative anchors for discourse on arts and sciences.

### Stephen Clucas

**[Middle position]** Clucas rejects a simple Yatesian talismanic engine but also resists eliminating magic, transformation, and contemplation. Bruno’s images participate in a philosophy of an interconnected universe and in disciplines intended to reform or “domesticate” the soul.

### Hilary Gatti

**[Correction]** Gatti’s *Giordano Bruno: Philosopher of the Renaissance* describes Sturlese as “undoing” Yates’s distortion of the technical system, while retaining the larger importance of Bruno’s natural philosophy, imagination, and magic. Yates’s astronomy-centered interpretation cannot be made the sole key to Bruno.

### Michele Ciliberto, Marco Matteoli, Nicoletta Tirinnanzi

**[Current philological tendency]** The Adelphi *Opere mnemotecniche* situates the works in Bruno’s evolving philosophy, lexicon, theory of nature, and theory of cognition. It replaces the search for one secret Hermetic key with analysis of multiple practices, textual strata, and philosophical functions.

### Paolo Rossi

**[Different emphasis]** Rossi’s *Clavis universalis* and *Logic and the Art of Memory* place Bruno within the longer history of artificial method, classification, combinatorics, and universal language. Rossi does not require Yates’s “astral power station” to explain why the wheels matter historically. [Rossi book record](https://books.google.com/books/about/Logic_and_the_Art_of_Memory.html?id=j2w4sxTH7TAC).

### Mary Carruthers

**[Contextual correction]** Carruthers does not principally reconstruct Bruno. Her importance is that she corrected the larger Yatesian picture of medieval memory as a corridor leading toward Renaissance occultism. Medieval *memoria* was an ethical, rhetorical, compositional, and cognitive craft—a means of invention and judgment, not mere rote storage. Bruno inherits that productive conception.

### Lina Bolzoni and Lina Bolzoni–Camillo scholarship

**[Contextual correction]** Bolzoni’s *The Gallery of Memory* and studies of Giulio Camillo show that images, theaters, emblematic structures, commonplace systems, and literary invention formed a broad sixteenth-century culture. Bruno was not a lone magician suddenly converting a dead classical trick into cosmic magic.

### Jocelyn Penny Small

**[Contextual correction]** *Wax Tablets of the Mind* stresses ancient practices, cognitive metaphors, and differences between oral, textual, and visual memory. It cautions against treating ancient mnemonic testimony as a single uniform technique.

### Walter Ong and Ramus scholarship

**[Contextual correction]** Ong’s *Ramus, Method, and the Decay of Dialogue* explains Ramist spatial dichotomies and printed method as an alternative organization of knowledge. Ramism did not merely “destroy memory”: it relocated mnemonic work into visible analytical order.

### Hajdu and Sabbatino

**[Historiographical importance]** Helga Hajdu’s *Das mnemotechnische Schrifttum des Mittelalters* and Nicola Sabbatino’s work on Renaissance mnemonics helped establish textual continuities before Yates. They are important correctives to claims of total medieval disappearance or sudden Renaissance rediscovery.

### Yates’s own qualifications

**[D/Y]** Yates repeatedly signals uncertainty: “I believe,” “perhaps,” “did he intend,” “we shall never understand this thing in detail.” Popular retellings often convert these questions into certainties. Her speculative mode is part of the evidence and should be preserved.

# History and context

## Rhetoric and scholastic psychology

**[D]** Artificial memory was the fourth or fifth part of rhetoric, depending on enumeration: invention, arrangement, style, memory, delivery. Classical practice ordered places, inserted images, and retrieved them by mental traversal.

**[D]** Aristotelian psychology made thought dependent upon *phantasmata*: the intellect does not think without images. Medieval writers incorporated this into education, meditation, preaching, and prudence.

**[SR]** Bruno radicalizes the productive side of memory. The mnemonic image is not merely a label attached to stored information; it becomes a transformable vehicle of thought.

## Dominican formation

**[D]** Bruno entered the Dominican convent of San Domenico Maggiore in Naples. Dominican authorities such as Albert the Great and Thomas Aquinas had sanctioned artificial memory when morally disciplined.

**[T]** Bruno later reported displaying his mnemonic ability in Rome before Pope Pius V and Cardinal Rebiba. The report survives in trial documents, but no independent papal record confirms the demonstration.

## Lullism

**[D]** Llull’s Art originated in the Franciscan and missionary environment of medieval Majorca and Mediterranean disputation. It sought necessary relations among divine dignities and created procedures usable in interreligious demonstration.

**[Correction]** The Art’s circles are not simply “memory wheels.” Bruno combines Lullian combination with mnemonic imagery in a way Llull did not.

## Courts, universities, and patronage

- Paris: Bruno lectured on divine attributes and memory and secured the interest of Henri III.
- Toulouse: he taught philosophy before moving to Paris.
- French court: *De umbris* is dedicated to Henri III.
- London and Oxford: Bruno participated in learned and courtly networks around Michel de Castelnau, Philip Sidney, John Dee, and the visiting Polish nobleman Albert Łaski.
- Wittenberg, Prague, Helmstedt, Frankfurt: later stages in Bruno’s teaching, patronage, and publication.
- Venice: Giovanni Mocenigo invited him to teach memory; their failed patron–client relation precipitated Bruno’s arrest.

## Hermeticism and natural magic

**[D]** Hermes is a character in the prologue. Bruno also employs images with astrological-magical pedigrees and later writes explicitly on magic and bonds.

**[Correction]** After Isaac Casaubon dated the principal *Corpus Hermeticum* texts to late antiquity in 1614, Renaissance belief in their extreme Egyptian antiquity became untenable. Bruno did not know this chronology.

**[Disputed]** “Hermetic” remains relevant, but the strong Yates thesis—that a coherent Hermetic religion provides the master key to Bruno—is no longer generally accepted.

## Ramism

Alexander Dicson, a Scottish follower of Bruno, published *De umbra rationis* and *De memoriae virtute*. A writer signing “G. P. Cantabrigiensis,” traditionally identified with William Perkins, attacked the Brunian current.

**[Disputed attribution]** Yates accepted the identification of G. P. with Perkins; later work treats attribution and confessional interpretation more cautiously.

**[D]** Perkins’s preaching manual condemns the “animation of images” as impious because it provokes absurd and fleshly fantasies. This is genuine Protestant criticism of affective imagery, but it does not show universal Calvinist acceptance of Ramism. [Recent reassessment](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/17496977.2021.1981695).

## Universal language and combinatorics

**[SR]** The line Llull → Renaissance Lullism → Bruno → seventeenth-century combinatorics → Leibniz is a history of transformations, not a simple transmission of one device.

Leibniz discusses Lullian wheels in *De arte combinatoria* (1666). His *characteristica universalis* and calculus of reasoning seek unambiguous conceptual elements and operations. Bruno’s images remain semantically and affectively dense; Leibniz’s ideal signs aim at formal calculability.

**[MI if stated strongly]** Llull and Bruno did not build programmable digital computers, artificial intelligence, or binary machines. Calling their diagrams “computers” is a retrospective analogy.

# People

## Giordano Bruno

**[D]** Author, teacher, self-promoter, and principal practitioner. His insertion of `Ior. in clavim et umbras` places his own mnemonic invention inside the inventory of civilization.

## Henri III

**[D/T]** Bruno testified before the Venetian Inquisition:

> “Il re Enrico terzo mi fece chiamare un giorno, ricercandomi se la memoria che avevo e che professava era naturale o pur per arte magica; al quale diedi sodisfazione … E doppo questo feci stampar un libro de memoria, sotto titolo *De umbris idearum*, il qual dedicai a Sua Maestà; e con questa occasione mi feci lettor straordinario e provisionato.”

Gloss: Henri III summoned him to ask whether his memory was natural or obtained by magical art; Bruno satisfied him that it came by science, printed *De umbris*, and became a salaried extraordinary reader.

**Status:** documented as Bruno’s sworn retrospective testimony; not independently documented dialogue. [Biographical archive](https://vitadibruno.insr.it/index.php?id=300).

## Giovanni Mocenigo

**[D]** Mocenigo invited Bruno to Venice to teach him memory and other “secrets.” Dissatisfied and fearful that Bruno would depart, he detained and denounced him in 1592.

**[Correction]** Memory was the occasion of the relationship and denunciation. It was not by itself the judicial cause of Bruno’s execution.

## Alexander Dicson

**[D]** Scottish student or follower who adapted Brunian memory in England and became a target of anti-mnemonic polemic.

## Agrippa, Ficino, Llull, Polydore Vergil

These are intellectual transmitters rather than personal teachers:

- Agrippa supplies extensive celestial-image lists.
- Ficino supplies theories of spirit, image, sympathy, and astral mediation.
- Llull supplies alphabetic combination and circular figures.
- Polydore supplies inventor lore.

## Critics inside Bruno’s prologue

Bruno caricatures opponents as Doctor Bobus, Magister Anthoc, Pharfacon, Roccus, Clyster, Carpophorus, and others. They object that:

- there is no art of memory, only repetition;
- extraordinary memory-users are possessed or monstrous;
- artificial memory burdens rather than relieves natural memory;
- medicines and regimens work better than images;
- existing arts of Cicero, Thomas, Albert, and Llull yield nothing.

**[D]** These speeches occur in Bruno’s satirical dialogue.

**[Disputed]** They may lampoon identifiable Parisian or scholastic adversaries, but secure one-to-one identification is lacking. They are not reliable transcripts of real conversations.

## Anecdotes requested

### The professor’s party

**[T/Y]** Yates says she was told of a professor who asked students at parties to name objects, mentally placed them on the windowsill, desk, wastebasket, and other room locations, and later recited them in order. She gives no name, date, institution, or independent witness. It is an illustrative modern anecdote, not evidence about Bruno. [Yates text](https://api.pageplace.de/preview/DT0400.9781136353611_A23838754/preview-9781136353611_A23838754.pdf).

### The auctioneer

**[Absence finding]** No documented auctioneer anecdote was found in Bruno’s *De umbris*, trial records, or the relevant Yates chapter. Modern accounts sometimes compare rapid memory performers to auctioneers; this is not part of the Bruno tradition.

### The student who lost his memory

**[Absence finding]** No securely traceable “student who lost his memory” episode belongs to the *De umbris* system. Stories of memory collapse occur in the wider mnemonic literature, including discussions of overloaded artificial memory and Quintilian’s observations that memory can disappear and return, but attaching one to Bruno would be unsupported.

### The accused magician

**[D/T]** Bruno is the relevant accused figure, but the precise charge must be handled carefully. Henri III reportedly asked whether his memory was magical; Bruno said it was scientific. Mocenigo later denounced Bruno for a much wider body of statements and conduct. “Accused magician because of the wheel” is a compressed later legend.

# The magic question

## Was the apparatus magical?

### Evidence for a magical reading

- Hermes introduces the book.
- Bruno uses decan, planetary, and lunar images transmitted in astrological-magic manuals.
- Agrippa is a major proximate source.
- Bruno distinguishes signs, notes, characters, and seals whose efficacy can appear *praeter naturam, supra naturam*, and even *contra naturam*.
- Bruno later wrote explicit works on magic and *vincula*.
- The prologue guards the art as knowledge to be communicated selectively.
- His cosmos treats imagination, sympathy, spirit, and natural bonds as real causal structures.

These facts support saying that the work inhabits the conceptual world of Renaissance natural magic.

### Evidence against Yates’s talismanic mechanism

- Bruno expressly calls the memory demonstrated to Henri III *scientia*, not *ars magica*.
- The relevant practical chapter explains alphabetic and grammatical encoding in detail.
- It calls for five fixed 150-member wheels, not a rotating astral hierarchy.
- The celestial images are said to be usable “as places and subjects,” an ordinary mnemonic role.
- No ritual of consecration, election of astrological time, suffumigation, invocation, or fabrication of physical talismans is prescribed for the five wheels.
- No primary passage says that rotating the wheels draws down stellar rays or changes external events.
- Yates reaches “How did the system work? By magic, of course” through analogy with Ficino and Agrippa, not through an explicit operating instruction.

### Best-supported conclusion

**[SR]** The system is a mnemonic-combinatory technology embedded in Bruno’s metaphysics and in a culture that admitted natural magic. Some images carry magical meanings and may support contemplation or psychic transformation. The narrower claim that the five-wheel word-encoding mechanism itself operates talismanically is unproved and, in Yates’s strong form, largely rejected.

## Heresy and consequences

**[D]** Bruno was tried in Venice and Rome and executed at Rome on 17 February 1600.

**[Correction]** It is misleading to say he was burned “for heliocentrism,” “for science,” or “for his memory wheel.” The surviving documentation points to multiple theological and philosophical accusations. The final Roman sentence survives, but the complete trial summary and exact eight propositions demanded for recantation have not survived intact.

**[D]** Memory teaching helped bring Bruno into Mocenigo’s house and thus into Venetian custody.

**[Y/Disputed]** Yates writes that the art of memory stands “at the very centre of the life and death of Bruno.” It is biographically central, but causally too strong if taken to mean the mnemonic system was the capital charge.

## Ars notoria

The *Ars notoria* is a medieval ritual tradition promising rapid acquisition of arts through prayers, fasts, divine names, and contemplation of complex *notae*.

**[D]** It was frequently suspected or condemned as illicit magic; Thomas Aquinas rejected it in *Summa contra Gentiles* III.106.

**[Correction]** Bruno’s wheel is not an *Ars notoria* diagram. Both traditions concern artificial acquisition of knowledge and potent signs, but their procedures, textual genealogies, and theological frameworks differ.

# Afterlife

## Bruno’s own later systems

### *Thirty Seals*

Bruno’s London works replace a single exposition with thirty “seals” or procedural perspectives: Field, Heaven, Chain, Tree, Ladder, Standard, and others.

**[D]** The seals combine loci, images, orders, diagrams, encyclopedic classifications, and meditative exercises.

**[Y]** Yates treats them as further stages of magical memory. Later scholarship more often sees a plurality of logical-mnemonic and philosophical operations.

### *De imaginum compositione*

Bruno’s 1591 final mnemonic work organizes images into atria and rooms and gives rules for composing images, signs, and ideas.

**[SR]** It confirms that Bruno’s mature concern was the controlled composition of semantically productive images. It does not confirm every feature of Plate 11.

## Visual culture

### Palazzo Schifanoia

The fifteenth-century Ferrara frescoes combine zodiac signs, decans, planetary gods, and courtly life. Aby Warburg used them to reconstruct the migration of astrological images.

**[SR]** They illuminate the decan tradition behind Bruno, but there is no evidence Bruno copied the fresco program directly.

### Santa Maria Novella

The Spanish Chapel’s frescoes arrange personified arts, virtues, sciences, and exemplary practitioners.

**[SR]** Yates uses this as a visual analogue for placing practitioners under fields of knowledge. Direct descent to Bruno’s inventor wheel is not documented.

### Emblem books

**[SR]** Emblems unite motto, image, and explanatory text in ways congenial to mnemonic composition. Bruno participates in the same culture of visually charged commonplaces, though no single emblem book explains the entire apparatus.

## Literature

### Dante

**[Disputed]** Yates and later scholars have identified mnemonic architectures in Dante’s ordered punishments, virtues, and visionary images.

**[Correction]** There is no documentary evidence that Dante used Bruno’s system, which postdates him by more than two centuries. The relationship is genealogical through medieval *memoria*, not direct.

### Borges’s “Funes the Memorious”

Funes remembers every detail but cannot abstract or generalize.

**[SR]** He is an illuminating inversion of Bruno: perfect storage without productive classification becomes intellectual paralysis. There is no documented textual dependence on *De umbris*.

### Umberto Eco

Eco’s writings on perfect languages, encyclopedias, signs, and combinatorial projects explicitly engage Llull, Renaissance universal language, and Bruno.

**[SR]** Eco belongs to the historiographical afterlife, not to a continuous practitioner tradition.

## Modern mnemonic revival

Modern memory athletes commonly use:

- method of loci;
- person–action–object systems;
- number–consonant coding;
- linking and elaborative imagery.

**[SR]** These resemble parts of Bruno’s practice structurally, especially composite agents/actions/objects, but there is little evidence that elite competitors learned their methods directly from *De umbris*.

**[D]** Controlled studies support the efficacy of loci training. A 2021 meta-analysis of thirteen randomized trials reported a medium effect, and neuroimaging studies found durable behavioral improvement and altered network organization after training. [Meta-analysis](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1747021821993457), [neural study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7929507/).

**[Correction]** These findings validate core spatial-imagery principles, not Bruno’s astrological cosmology or the practical efficiency of memorizing five 150-item dictionaries.

## Computing and combinatorics

### Llull to Leibniz

**[D]** Leibniz knew Lullian combinatorics and discussed Lullian wheels. His 1666 *Dissertatio de arte combinatoria* sought systematic combination of concepts.

**[SR]** Rossi and Yates rightly place Llull and Bruno in the prehistory of symbolic method and universal language.

### “Mind machine”

**[Y/MI]** Yates compares Bruno’s system to a machine performing work of the brain. This is a twentieth-century heuristic.

**[Correction]** Bruno’s device lacks:

- a formal semantics;
- error-controlled algorithms;
- autonomous execution;
- storage separate from a trained human imagination;
- hardware implementing general symbolic computation.

The historically defensible lineage is conceptual: finite primitives, positional notation, permutation, classification, and hopes for universal discovery. The claim that Bruno invented a computer or AI is modern promotional mythology. A computer-science history of Llull’s contribution similarly treats him as a conceptual predecessor, not a digital engineer. [Llull and computer science](https://www.iiia.csic.es/~sierra/wp-content/uploads/2019/02/Llull.pdf).

# Controversies and disputes

## 1. Was there one giant concentric wheel?

- **Yates:** yes, reconstructed as Plate 11.
- **Primary text:** prescribes five fixed wheels after the likeness of earlier wheels and prints one contracted exemplar.
- **Sturlese:** coordinated phonetic-verbal apparatus.
- **Torchia:** broader discursive system with semantic anchors.
- **Verdict:** the general wheel form is documented; Plate 11’s exact concentric hierarchy is not.

## 2. Did the five vowel cells rotate independently?

- **Yates:** yes; cites the diagram at *Op. lat.* II.1, p. 123.
- **Primary evidence:** earlier combinatory figures rotate; the second practice calls its five 150-member wheels *fixae*.
- **Verdict:** alignability and permutation are documented in Bruno’s broader practice; independent micro-rotation of every five-cell block in Plate 11 is not securely established.

## 3. Was the astronomical list central?

- **Yates:** it is the central “astral power station.”
- **Primary text:** astronomical figures can serve as *loci* and *subiecta*; the inventor series is explicitly the first/outer agent wheel.
- **Verdict:** celestial images are important and potentially foundational subjects, but their physical and causal centrality is Yates’s construction.

## 4. Four lists or five?

- **Catalogue/Yatesian shorthand:** four principal lists of 150, sometimes with inventor and invention split.
- **Primary text:** five functional wheels—agents, actions, insignia, attendants, circumstances.
- **Verdict:** five is textually correct. “Four lists” describes only the conspicuous printed blocks selected by Yates.

## 5. 150 astronomical “magic images”?

- **Arithmetic:** exactly 150 indexed celestial figures.
- **Source:** many derive from Agrippa’s celestial magic.
- **Dispute:** whether mnemonic use makes them talismanic.
- **Verdict:** “150 celestial images with magical pedigrees” is documented; “150 operative mental talismans” is Yates’s hypothesis.

## 6. Does *rotas fixas* mean physically immobile?

Possibilities include:

1. fixed reference dictionaries contrasted with movable encoding alignments;
2. fixed as mentally installed and memorized;
3. printed or imagined wheels whose contents remain fixed although their relative positions can change.

**Open philological issue:** *fixas* cannot simply be ignored, but it need not mean no manipulation whatsoever.

## 7. Was the art mainly for foreign words?

- **Sturlese:** phonetic word learning is a central concrete use.
- **Torchia/Clucas:** the system also supports discourse, invention, contemplation, and organization of sciences.
- **Bruno:** explicitly promises both verbal and intellectual applications.
- **Verdict:** word encoding is the best-demonstrated procedure, but exclusivity is unproved.

## 8. Did Bruno derive the title from a lost Solomonic magic book?

- **Evidence:** Cecco d’Ascoli mentions a *Liber de umbris idearum* in a necromantic context.
- **Yates:** treats it as Bruno’s title source.
- **Problem:** Bruno does not cite that book here; “shadows of ideas” also fits Platonic and Neoplatonic language.
- **Verdict:** possible allusion, not established derivation.

## 9. Did Bruno really possess prodigious memory?

- **Evidence:** Bruno’s testimony, patronage, teaching reputation, and mnemonic publications.
- **Missing:** controlled demonstrations or independent verbatim records of feats.
- **Verdict:** documented reputation and professional activity; magnitude of natural performance unknown.

## 10. Was he executed for magic, memory, or cosmology?

- **Documented:** broad heresy trial; memory teaching led to Mocenigo; Bruno wrote on magic.
- **Legend:** martyr solely for heliocentrism or for a forbidden memory machine.
- **Verdict:** neither single-cause legend is defensible.

## 11. Are the inventor attributions historical?

Rhegima, Osiris, Ceres, Theut, Zoroaster, and Melicus belong to ancient and Renaissance inventor traditions.

**[T/L]** These entries document what Bruno or his sources credited, not actual historical invention. “Theut invented writing” is Platonic-Egyptian tradition; “Simonides invented memory” is the classical foundation story.

## 12. Did the system directly lead to Leibniz or computers?

- **Documented:** Leibniz engaged Lullian and combinatorial traditions.
- **Possible:** indirect access to Brunian or Bruno-adjacent materials.
- **Unproved:** a direct line from Plate 11 to Leibniz’s calculus.
- **Modern invention:** Bruno as creator of AI, a software algorithm, or a literal universal computer.

# Open questions

1. How exactly were the five 150-item dictionaries coordinated in live teaching?
2. Did Bruno use physical volvelles, loose paper wheels, memorized tables, or different media for different students?
3. What did the lost *Clavis magna* contain?
4. Were the errors, duplicated letter headings, and missing item numbers in the 1582 tables compositorial accidents or traces of revision?
5. Can every inventor entry be traced to Polydore Vergil, Pliny, Diodorus, biblical chronography, or another source?

7. Does *fixae* describe static tables, memorized anchors, or fixed content on movable discs?
8. How much of the system was publicly printed and how much supplied orally?
9. Did Henri III learn and use the method, or only test Bruno’s claims?
10. Can Dicson’s works reveal classroom practice omitted from Bruno’s deliberately compressed exposition?
11. Are the house images substantially Bruno’s inventions or transformations of a still-unidentified illustrated almanac?
12. How should the higher metaphysical art of sixty intentions and concepts be related operationally to the lower phonetic practice?
13. Can experimental reconstruction measure whether the five-wheel technique repays its enormous setup cost?
14. Does the astronomical sequence possess semantic relations beyond serving as a 150-address peg list?
