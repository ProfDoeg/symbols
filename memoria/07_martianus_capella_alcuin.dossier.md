### Evidentiary key

The labels used below have precise meanings:

- **[Text]** directly stated in a primary source.
- **[Manuscript]** directly evidenced by a surviving witness or catalogue.
- **[Reconstruction]** a scholarly inference from sources, transmission, vocabulary, or context.
- **[Yates]** Frances Yates’s interpretation or formulation.
- **[Tradition]** a historically transmitted report whose factual basis may be incomplete.
- **[Disputed]** a live disagreement about attribution, date, meaning, or influence.
- **[Legend]** an anecdote or causal story unsupported by adequate contemporary evidence.
- **[Modern invention]** a recent elaboration mistakenly presented as ancient or medieval.

The central conclusion is straightforward but important:

> **Martianus’s two paragraphs document an authentic, operational core of classical artificial memory. Alcuin documents neither a continuation nor a prohibition of that mechanism. He follows Julius Victor’s non-local program of exercise, writing, and orderly composition. “The art was lost” is therefore a useful Yatesian description of what disappears from Alcuin’s chapter, not proof that spatial or imaginal mnemonic practice disappeared from Carolingian culture.**

## The system in detail

## 1. Martianus Capella’s compressed artificial memory

### 1.1 Position in the work

**[Text]** *De nuptiis Philologiae et Mercurii* is a nine-book prosimetrical encyclopedia. Books I–II narrate the marriage of Mercury and Philology; Books III–IX introduce the seven liberal arts as personified female attendants:

1. Grammar
2. Dialectic
3. Rhetoric
4. Geometry
5. Arithmetic
6. Astronomy
7. Harmony or Music

Rhetoric speaks in Book V. At §442 she identifies her five parts as *materia/inventio*, *dispositio*, *elocutio*, *memoria*, and *pronuntiatio*. At §§538–539 she reaches memory. The relevant text occupies only two sections immediately before her treatment of delivery.

**[Yates]** Yates calls this an “incomprehensibly potted version of the rules,” meaning not that the Latin is literally unintelligible but that an entire technical tradition has been compressed so severely that a reader without Cicero, the *Ad Herennium*, or Quintilian would have difficulty reconstructing its full practice.

That judgment is substantially fair. Martianus gives:

- the Simonides origin story;
- the indispensable role of order;
- places;
- images of things or thoughts;
- two elementary image examples;
- the wax/letters analogy;
- complementary non-spatial study practices.

He does **not** give:

- the full architectural rules for selecting places;
- intervals between loci;
- an explicit rule against excessively bright, dim, crowded, or similar places;
- sets of five or ten loci;
- the hand or other marker for every fifth place;
- a distinction between ordinary and extraordinary images;
- the *Ad Herennium*’s grotesque “active images”;
- a worked conversion of an entire speech or sequence into loci;
- a detailed method for word-for-word memory.

### 1.2 The Latin text

The following text is cited by the standard section numbers, which are more stable than pagination. It appears in Dick, pp. 268–270; compare Willis, Book V, §§538–539. A searchable Latin text and the Stahl–Johnson–Burge translation are available in parallel online.

#### §538: nature, art, Simonides, places, and images

> *Sequuntur praecepta memoriae, quae quamvis naturalis sit, arte tamen adiuvari posse non dubium est. Haec paucis praeceptis, multa exercitatione constat, cuius utilitas est, ut res et verba non tarde, sed celeriter firmiterque comprehendantur. Nec solum quae ipsi invenerimus retinenda sunt, sed etiam quae adversarius in causa disseruerit.*

“Next come the precepts of memory. Although memory is natural, there is no doubt that it can be assisted by art. The art consists of few rules but much practice; its utility is that things and words may be grasped quickly and securely. We must retain not only what we ourselves have devised but also what an opponent has argued in a case.”

**[Text]** Memory is simultaneously natural and trainable. This is the classical distinction between *memoria naturalis* and an acquired or “artificial” memory, although Martianus does not here reproduce the *Ad Herennium*’s formal definition of the two kinds.

> *Simonides huius rei praecepta invenisse perhibetur, poeta idemque philosophus: cum enim convivii locus subito corruisset, nec possent propinqui obtritos internoscere, discumbentium ordinem nominaque memoria recordante suggessit.*

“Simonides, poet and philosopher, is said to have discovered the rules of this subject: when a banquet hall suddenly collapsed and the relatives could not distinguish those crushed beneath it, his memory supplied the order and names of the diners.”

**[Text/Tradition]** Martianus explicitly marks the Simonides story as received tradition—*perhibetur*, “he is said.” The collapse, identification of mangled bodies, and inference from seating order already appear in Cicero, *De oratore* II.351–354, and Quintilian, *Institutio oratoria* XI.2.11–16. It is not independently verifiable biography of Simonides.

> *Quo admonitus intellexit ordinem esse, qui memoriae praecepta conferret.*

“Prompted by this, he understood that order was what supplied the principle of memory.”

**[Text]** Order is the system’s governing mechanism. The remembered arrangement of diners demonstrates that an ordered spatial sequence can preserve a corresponding sequence of people or ideas.

> *Is vero in locis illustribus meditandus est, in quibus species rerum sententiarumque imagines collocandae sunt…*

“That order is to be rehearsed in conspicuous/distinct places, in which forms of things and images of thoughts are to be placed…”

The wording deserves care.

- *locis* unquestionably means mnemonic places or topics.
- *illustribus* can suggest illuminated, conspicuous, vivid, notable, or clearly distinguishable.
- Stahl–Johnson–Burge render it “distinct topics.”
- Yates’s “well-lighted places” draws the phrase toward the fuller classical locus rule.

**[Disputed translation]** “Well-lighted” is possible in the context of the classical prescriptions, but “conspicuous” or “clearly distinguishable” is lexically safer. Martianus does not explicitly repeat the *Ad Herennium*’s balanced requirement that loci be neither too bright nor too dim. Thus the catalogue’s statement that the rules for places are “reduced to one” is essentially correct, but that one rule should not be made more exact than Martianus’s wording permits.

### 1.3 What serves as places

**[Text]** Martianus says only *loci illustres*. In practice these are imagined places that can:

1. be mentally rehearsed in a stable order;
2. receive a deposited image;
3. return that image when revisited.

No particular building is prescribed. There are no rooms, porticoes, doorways, columns, or intervals in his surviving instruction.

**[Reconstruction from the classical tradition]** A rhetorically trained late-antique reader could supply what Martianus omits: a familiar building or sequence of architectural locations, visited in a fixed route. Cicero and the *Ad Herennium* make this background much clearer. But that richer architecture must not be quoted as if it were in Martianus.

### 1.4 What serves as images

Martianus calls them:

> *species rerum sententiarumque imagines*

“forms of things and images of thoughts/sentences.”

This phrasing is broader than a picture of a physical object. The image must stand for a matter, proposition, or segment of discourse.

He gives two examples:

> *veluti nuptiarum velatam flammeo nubentem, aut homicidae gladium vel arma detineas*

“For example, for a wedding you might retain a bride veiled with the *flammeum*; for a murderer, a sword or weapons.”

The orange-red *flammeum* is the traditional Roman bridal veil. The image works by immediately presenting the distinctive sign of the subject:

| Matter to recall | Image |
|---|---|
| wedding | a veiled bride, specifically wearing the *flammeum* |
| murder/murderer | sword or weapons |

**[Text]** These are semantic images—images for things—not phonetic rebuses for individual words.

**[Text]** They are also simple and conventional, not bizarre. Martianus gives no instruction equivalent to the *Ad Herennium*’s demand for exceptionally beautiful, shameful, bloody, ridiculous, or otherwise emotionally striking *imagines agentes*.

### 1.5 Deposit and retrieval

> *quas species locus tamquam depositas reddat*

“which forms the place returns as though they had been deposited there.”

This is Martianus’s clearest description of retrieval.

1. Fix an ordered series of distinct places.
2. Convert the matters to be remembered into visual forms.
3. place or “deposit” each form in its proper locus.
4. During recollection, revisit the places in order.
5. Each locus “returns” its deposited image.
6. The image cues the corresponding matter or proposition.

The verb *reddat* makes the place an active retrieval cue. Martianus does not explicitly explain reverse traversal, selective access, reuse of loci, or clearing images after use.

### 1.6 The wax-and-letters analogy

> *Nam sicut id quod conscribitur, cera continetur et litteris, sic quod memoriae mandatur, in locis tamquam in cera paginaque signatur; imaginibus vero quasi litteris rerum recordatio continetur.*

“For just as what is written is held in wax and letters, so what is entrusted to memory is marked in places as though in wax or on a page; recollection of things is contained in images as though in letters.”

The correspondences are:

| Writing | Artificial memory |
|---|---|
| wax/tablet or page | places |
| letters | images |
| written message | remembered things |
| reading the inscription | traversing the places and recognizing images |

**[Text]** This is a spatial-writing model: loci supply the stable receiving surface; images supply differentiated signs.

**[Historical context]** The analogy descends from classical rhetorical accounts. The *Ad Herennium* III.17 says that places are like wax tablets or papyrus and images like letters; Cicero, *De oratore* II.354, gives essentially the same mapping.

**[Later scholarship]** Jocelyn Penny Small’s *Wax Tablets of the Mind* places such analogies in the material environment of ancient writing technologies. Mary Carruthers emphasizes that medieval *memoria* is not merely a storage bin: it is a trained matrix for composition and invention. Martianus’s *cera paginaque* formula is unusually valuable because it explicitly joins mental place to both erasable tablet and written page.

### 1.7 The second method: writing, division, signs, murmuring, night

At §539 Martianus shifts from local memory to study practice:

> *Sed, ut diximus, magnam exercitationem res laboremque conquirit…*

“But, as we have said, the matter requires much practice and labor…”

He then prescribes:

> *ut scribamus ipsi quae facile volumus retinere*

“that we write out ourselves what we wish to retain easily.”

> *si longiora fuerint, quae sunt ediscenda, divisa per partes facilius inhaerescant*

“if what is to be learned is longer, it adheres more readily when divided into parts.”

> *apponere notas rebus singulis oportebit his, quae volumus maxime retinere*

“we should attach signs or marks to the individual matters that we especially wish to retain.”

> *nec voce magna legenda sunt, sed murmure potius meditanda*

“They should not be read in a loud voice, but rather rehearsed in a murmur.”

> *et nocte magis quam interdiu maturius excitari memoriam manifestum est, cum et late silentium iuvat, nec foras a sensibus avocatur intentio*

“It is evident that memory is more readily aroused at night than by day, since the deep silence helps and attention is not drawn outward by the senses.”

Finally:

> *est quidem memoria rerum atque verborum, sed non semper ediscenda sunt verba, nisi spatium meditandi tempus indulserit; sat eritque res ipsas animo tenuisse…*

“There is indeed memory for things and for words, but words need not always be learned unless time permits extended rehearsal; it will be enough to hold the matters themselves in mind.”

This is not a second complete “memory palace.” It is a cluster of complementary disciplines:

- autographic copying;
- segmentation or chunking;
- marginal or textual *notae*;
- low-voiced repetition;
- nocturnal concentration;
- prioritizing memory for substance over verbatim wording.

**[Text]** Martianus acknowledges both *memoria rerum* and *memoria verborum* but favors memory for things when time is limited.

**[Reconstruction]** The local-image procedure and page-based procedure could be combined, but Martianus does not explicitly demonstrate such a combination.

**[Yates]** Yates identifies the latter advice as “Quintilianist,” correctly in broad terms. Quintilian XI.2 recommends writing, division, silent or low rehearsal, and dependence on the internal order of a well-composed speech. Direct verbal dependence in every clause is harder to establish than general affiliation.

### 1.8 Rules actually present, and rules absent

#### Rules Martianus states

**Places**

- They must be *illustres*: conspicuous, distinct, or clearly graspable.
- They must possess an order.
- They receive and return deposited images.

**Images**

- They are visual *species*.
- They stand for things or thoughts.
- They are placed in loci.
- A salient attribute may represent a subject: veil for wedding; sword for murder.

**Study**

- Practice and labor are indispensable.
- Write material yourself.
- Divide long material into portions.
- Add signs to especially important items.
- Rehearse in a murmur.
- Use the quiet of night.
- Prefer memory for substance when verbatim learning is impractical.

#### Rules not stated

- No exact size of loci.
- No fixed distance between loci.
- No instruction to choose deserted buildings.
- No prohibition on overly bright or dim places.
- No numerical indexing of every fifth locus.
- No grotesque or emotionally violent image rule.
- No explicit rule for resemblance, substitution, or phonetic pun.
- No example of memorizing a sequence longer than one concept.
- No instruction for erasing or replacing images.
- No claim that the images possess occult force.

## 2. The relation to the classical sources

Martianus is neither the inventor nor the fullest transmitter of the technique.

### 2.1 The *Rhetorica ad Herennium*

**[Text]** The anonymous *Rhetorica ad Herennium*, probably composed in the 80s BCE and later misattributed to Cicero, provides the fullest surviving ancient Latin account, Book III.16–24.

Its central scheme is:

> *Constat igitur artificiosa memoria ex locis et imaginibus.*

“Artificial memory therefore consists of places and images.”

It specifies:

- solitary, clearly differentiated places;
- moderate scale and illumination;
- regularly spaced loci;
- extraordinary, active, emotionally striking images;
- memory for things and memory for words;
- repeated mental traversal;
- periodic numerical markers.

The famous lawsuit example—an image of a sick man in bed, an accused person standing beside him with cup and tablets, and a ram’s testicles on a finger—belongs to the *Ad Herennium*, not to Martianus.

**[Correction to template expectations]** Martianus gives no lawsuit image. Quoting the *Ad Herennium* example as one of “Martianus’s examples” would conflate two systems.

### 2.2 Cicero, *De oratore*

**[Text]** Cicero II.351–360 transmits the Simonides banquet story and explains that people seeking to train memory must select places and form mental images. Cicero stresses order and the analogy of places to wax and images to letters.

Martianus’s §538 is extremely close to this conceptual skeleton.

### 2.3 Quintilian, *Institutio oratoria* XI.2

**[Text]** Quintilian retells Simonides and describes architectural loci, but he also records objections to excessively elaborate image systems. He gives strong practical advice on writing, division, rehearsal, and following the structural sequence of a speech.

Martianus’s §539 belongs especially to this pragmatic line.

### 2.4 Julius Victor

C. Julius Victor, probably fourth century, is the decisive bridge to Alcuin. In *Ars rhetorica* 23, Halm p. 440, he writes:

> *Memoria est firma animi rerum ac verborum ad inventionem perceptio.*

“Memory is the mind’s firm grasp of things and words relating to invention.”

He then states:

> *Ad hanc obtinendam tradunt plerique locorum et simulacrorum quasdam observationes, quae mihi non videntur habere effectum.*

“To acquire it, many people transmit certain observations about places and images, which do not seem to me to be effective.”

Victor instead recommends:

> *Exercenda est memoria ediscendis ad verbum quam plurimis et tuis scriptis et alienis…*

“Memory should be exercised by learning as much as possible word for word, both your own writings and those of others…”

He also advocates *divisio et compositio*: a speech coherently divided and composed is remembered because each succeeding part prompts the next.

**[Text]** Julius Victor does not merely omit artificial memory. He mentions loci and images and rejects their efficacy.

**[Source criticism]** Alcuin’s memory passage is demonstrably assembled from Victor’s chapter, but Alcuin omits Victor’s explicit dismissal of loci and images. Therefore:

- Alcuin’s source knew the method.
- Alcuin probably encountered at least Victor’s notice of it.
- Alcuin chose not to reproduce either the method or Victor’s criticism.
- We cannot infer that Alcuin had never heard of loci and images.

This is a substantial qualification to a literal reading of “the art has disappeared.”

## 3. Alcuin’s non-local memory program

### 3.1 Work, setting, and audience

**[Text]** The *Disputatio de rhetorica et de virtutibus sapientissimi regis Carli et Albini magistri* is a pedagogical dialogue between Charlemagne (*Karolus*) and Alcuin (*Albinus*).

**[Reconstruction]** It belongs to the intellectual program of the Carolingian court: training rulers and administrators in speech, civil judgment, disputation, moral conduct, and Christian kingship.

**[Disputed date]**

- Conventional dating: 793–796.
- Copeland and Sluiter use the broader c. 790–800.
- Eva Rädler-Bohn has argued for c. 784–790.
- The text represents Charlemagne as king, but titulature, political setting, and the relation between its rhetoric and virtue sections admit more than one reconstruction.

It should not simply be dated “c. 800” without noting the uncertainty.

### 3.2 Memory as a part of rhetoric

At §4:

> *Artis rhetoricae partes quinque sunt: inventio, dispositio, elocutio, memoria, pronuntiatio.*

“The parts of rhetoric are five: invention, arrangement, style, memory, and delivery.”

Alcuin defines:

> *memoria est firma animi rerum ac verborum ad inventionem perceptio*

“Memory is the mind’s firm grasp of things and words relating to invention.”

**[Text]** This is the same formula found in Julius Victor.

Later, at §39, Charlemagne asks:

> *Quid dicis de nobilissima, ut reor, rhetoricae parte, memoria?*

“What do you say about memory, which I consider the noblest part of rhetoric?”

Alcuin answers with the Ciceronian commonplace:

> *Quid aliud nisi quod Marcus Tullius dicit? quod thesaurus est omnium rerum memoria, quae nisi custos cogitatis inventisque rebus et verbis adhibeatur, intellegimus omnia, etiamsi praeclara fuerint, in oratore peritura.*

“What else but what Marcus Tullius says: memory is the treasury of all things; unless it is applied as guardian to thoughts and to matters and words discovered, we understand that everything in the orator, however excellent, will perish.”

The underlying passage is Cicero, *De oratore* I.18, transmitted to Alcuin through Julius Victor.

### 3.3 The surviving precepts

Charlemagne asks:

> *Suntne aliqua eius praecepta, quomodo vel illa optinenda sit vel augenda?*

“Are there any precepts by which it may be acquired or increased?”

Alcuin replies:

> *Non habemus eius alia praecepta, nisi ediscendi exercitationem et scribendi usum et cogitandi studium et ebrietate cavenda, quae omnibus bonis studiis maxime nocet, quae non solum corpori aufert sanitatem, sed etiam menti adimit integritatem.*

“We have no other precepts for it except practice in learning by heart, the use of writing, application to thought, and guarding against drunkenness, which is especially harmful to every good study, since it not only takes health from the body but integrity from the mind.”

The four precepts are:

1. *ediscendi exercitatio* — practice in learning by heart;
2. *scribendi usus* — habitual writing;
3. *cogitandi studium* — diligent thought or meditation;
4. *ebrietate cavenda* — avoidance of drunkenness.

Charlemagne answers:

> *Sufficiunt haec praecepta, si quis idoneus est ad haec implenda; nam, ut video, tam ardua sunt sensu quam rara verbis.*

“These precepts suffice, if someone is capable of carrying them out; for, as I see, they are as arduous in substance as they are few in words.”

**[Text]** The dialogue does not present the list as trivial. The rules are few, but their execution is difficult.

### 3.4 How order and retrieval work in Alcuin

There is no artificial sequence of loci. Retrieval depends on:

- repeated memorization;
- writing;
- attentive thought;
- coherent composition;
- disciplined bodily habits;
- constant practice.

At §§42–43 Alcuin argues that persistent exercise can almost take the place of nature:

> *exercitatio ingenium et naturam saepe vincit, et usus omnium magistrorum praecepta superabit*

“Exercise often overcomes talent and nature, and practice will surpass the precepts of all teachers.”

**[Text]** This is a theory of habituation, not spatial storage. The structure of discourse and the learner’s practiced mind provide the order.

### 3.5 Memory as part of Prudence

At §§44–45 Charlemagne asks about the four virtues. Prudence has three parts:

> *Memoriam, intellegentiam, providentiam.*

“Memory, understanding, foresight.”

Alcuin defines:

> *Memoria est, per quam animus repetit illa quae fuerunt; intellegentia, per quam ea perspicit quae sunt; providentia, per quam futurum aliquid praevidetur ante quam fiat.*

“Memory is that by which the mind recalls what has been; understanding that by which it perceives what is; foresight that by which something future is seen before it occurs.”

This division comes from Cicero, *De inventione* II.160.

Alcuin therefore preserves two conceptual memories:

| Context | Function |
|---|---|
| Rhetoric | firm possession of discovered things and words |
| Prudence | mental recovery of past things |

**[Text]** He does not connect either definition to loci and images.

**[Yates]** This matters enormously to Yates. In later medieval thought, she argues, the pseudo-Ciceronian *Ad Herennium* became paired with Cicero’s *De inventione*. Memory as part of Prudence and memory as a rhetorical art could then converge. Alcuin has the *De inventione* “first rhetoric,” but not the *Ad Herennium*’s effective “second rhetoric.”

### 3.6 What Alcuin does not say

Alcuin gives:

- no Simonides story;
- no places;
- no images;
- no wax-and-letters analogy;
- no architectural itinerary;
- no image rules;
- no examples of symbolic encoding;
- no warning that mnemonic images are magical;
- no theological rejection of artificial memory.

**[Text]** Silence is the only evidence for the absence of the local technique from this treatise.

**[Correction]** “Alcuin abolished artificial memory” is not documented. Nor does he say that the method is pagan, false, magical, dangerous, or heretical.

## Diagrams, tables, wheels, and plates

## 4. Martianus: no mnemonic diagram survives as part of §§538–539

**[Textual finding]** The memory chapter itself contains no diagram, wheel, table, architectural plan, or series of pictured loci. The system is verbal.

**[Manuscript finding]** Manuscripts of *De nuptiis* frequently contain diagrams, particularly for dialectic, astronomy, music, geometry, and cosmology. These demonstrate the work’s visual and diagrammatic reception, but they are not diagrams of the loci-and-images procedure.

**[Warning]** It is an error to treat every liberal-arts personification or scientific diagram in a Martianus manuscript as a mnemonic diagram. A picture can be memorable or pedagogically useful without being evidence of an encoded architectural memory system.

## 5. The personified liberal arts

At the beginning of Book V, §§425–427, Rhetoric enters with trumpets. She is:

- exceptionally tall;
- confident and beautiful;
- helmeted;
- crowned with royal majesty;
- armed with weapons that flash like lightning;
- dressed in Roman fashion;
- clothed in a garment bearing figures or devices;
- girdled with a jeweled belt.

The Latin begins:

> *ecce quaedam sublimissimi corporis ac fiduciae grandioris, vultus etiam decore luculenta femina insignis ingreditur, cui galeatus vertex… arma in manibus… fulminea quadam coruscatione renidebant.*

“Behold, there entered a distinguished woman of loftiest stature and great confidence, splendid in the beauty of her face; her head was helmeted … and the weapons in her hands shone with a lightning brilliance.”

When she strikes her arms together, the noise resembles thunder. She can move assemblies, senates, courts, theaters, cities, and armies.

### Are these mnemonic images?

**[Yates]** “The personified liberal arts conform remarkably well to the rules for images in the artificial memory” (*Art of Memory*, p. 52).

The observation is perceptive:

- the figures are visually concrete;
- they carry distinctive attributes;
- they are dramatic;
- they evoke strong affect;
- they differentiate abstract disciplines;
- they occur in an ordered series.

**[But not text]** Martianus never tells the reader to install the seven women in mnemonic loci. They are characters in the wedding allegory, not a worked memory palace.

**[Reconstruction]** The allegorical forms could have functioned mnemonically for readers, artists, and teachers. Their aptitude for recollection helps explain their long iconographic life.

**[Disputed inference]** Their conformity to mnemonic-image rules does not demonstrate that Martianus designed the entire allegory as an artificial-memory system.

## 6. Surviving illustrated witnesses

### Vatican City, Biblioteca Apostolica Vaticana, Urb. lat. 329

**[Manuscript]** A richly illuminated late-fifteenth-century manuscript, dated broadly 1476–1500, made for the Urbino/Montefeltro milieu. It contains:

- f. 1v: title within an ornamented circle;
- f. 2r: Martianus, bearded, opening a book, within an illuminated initial;
- f. 25v: Grammar;
- f. 33: Dialectic;
- f. 46: Rhetoric;
- f. 65: Geometry;
- f. 88v: Arithmetic;
- f. 113v or 132, depending on catalogue description and sequence: Astronomy;
- f. 149v: Music.

Catalogues identify Gherardo di Giovanni and workshop collaborators; the figures are related iconographically to the so-called Mantegna Tarocchi.

**[Correction]** These miniatures are nearly a millennium later than Martianus. They document Renaissance reception, not the original fifth-century appearance of the text.

### Leiden, Universiteitsbibliotheek, Voss. Lat. F. 48

**[Manuscript]** A ninth-century witness central to the oldest Carolingian gloss tradition. The Huygens digital edition supplies manuscript images, transcription, and glosses. The manuscript is important evidence for the intense Carolingian study of Martianus.

**[Finding]** Its existence directly weakens any broad proposition that Martianus’s instructional world was simply forgotten in Alcuin’s century.

### Cologne, Dombibliothek, Cod. 193

**[Manuscript]** Preserves Martianus with glosses and illustrations; a digital edition places the base text beside material associated with the Cologne gloss corpus, Eriugena, and Remigius.

### Vienna, Österreichische Nationalbibliothek, Cod. 2639

**[Manuscript]** Frequently reproduced for its personifications of the liberal arts and cited by Yates in relation to medieval imagery. Its liberal-arts images are evidence for the visualization of disciplines, not a surviving diagram of Martianus’s memory instructions.

### Other diagrammatic witnesses

Numerous Martianus manuscripts contain cosmological, astronomical, musical, and logical diagrams. A ninth-century manuscript displayed by Google Arts & Culture is described as containing explanatory diagrams in Book VI. Such diagrams externalize ordered knowledge, but none is known to provide a sequence of mnemonic loci for §§538–539.

## 7. Alcuin’s didactic diagrams

Artur Costrino’s critical study established that some early manuscripts of the *Disputatio* transmit rhetorical diagrams ignored by modern printed editions.

**[Manuscript/scholarship]**

- Diagrams occur in some of the earliest ninth- and tenth-century witnesses.
- They classify rhetorical knowledge and its divisions.
- They participated in the text’s school reception.
- They are not diagrams of an artificial memory palace.
- No surviving Alcuin diagram restores places and images to §39.

The distinction is crucial: diagrammatic organization can support memory, but it does not automatically constitute the classical *ars memoriae*.

## Yates’s account and scholarship since

## 14. What Yates argues

### 14.1 Martianus as transmitter

Yates opens Chapter III against the political catastrophe of the fifth-century West: the sack of Rome, Vandal expansion, and the end of ancient institutions. She describes *De nuptiis* as preserving the educational arrangement of the seven liberal arts.

Her central formulation is:

> “He thus handed on the art to the Middle Ages firmly lodged in its correct niche in the scheme of the liberal arts.”  
> —Yates, p. 50

**[Yates]** The “handing on” has two meanings:

1. Martianus preserves a compressed textual recipe for artificial memory.
2. He locates it canonically as the fourth part of Rhetoric, herself one of the seven liberal arts.

The first is documented. The second is a convincing structural observation.

### 14.2 Living rhetorical tradition in North Africa

Yates suggests that Martianus could have acquired artificial memory from living rhetorical education in Carthage rather than directly from a particular copy of the *Ad Herennium*.

**[Reconstruction]** This is plausible but unprovable. Martianus’s immediate textual sources for §§538–539 have not been identified with sufficient precision to establish oral instruction as opposed to literary compilation.

**[Older hypothesis]** Yates also cites the suggestion by Friedrich Marx and Harry Caplan that the *Ad Herennium* experienced a late-antique revival in North Africa before returning to Italy.

That diffusion model is not required to explain Martianus. Similar material existed in Cicero, Quintilian, and rhetorical handbooks.

### 14.3 The allegorical arts as memory images

Yates writes:

> “The personified liberal arts conform remarkably well to the rules for images in the artificial memory.”  
> —p. 52

**[Yates]** This is a functional and morphological comparison, not a quotation from Martianus.

**[Confirmed in limited form]** Later scholarship on medieval memory agrees that striking personifications, attributes, diagrams, and ordered visual structures can assist recollection and invention.

**[Not demonstrated]** No textual or manuscript evidence shows Martianus assigning the seven arts to a route of loci. “The whole *De nuptiis* is a memory palace” is a modern extrapolation.

### 14.4 Alcuin and disappearance

Yates contrasts Martianus with Alcuin:

> “The artificial memory has disappeared! Its rules have gone, replaced by ‘avoid drunkenness’!”  
> —p. 53

Her rhetorical point is accurate at the level of Alcuin’s explicit chapter:

- loci are gone;
- images are gone;
- Simonides is gone;
- the wax analogy is gone;
- sobriety appears.

But it becomes misleading if expanded into a historical claim that mnemonic visualization disappeared from Carolingian education.

### 14.5 The two rhetorics

Yates sees the later medieval art as arising from the conjunction of:

- Cicero’s *De inventione*, later the “Old” or “First Rhetoric,” with its account of the virtues;
- pseudo-Cicero’s *Ad Herennium*, the “New” or “Second Rhetoric,” with its detailed artificial memory.

Alcuin uses *De inventione* for Prudence but follows Julius Victor for rhetorical memory. Hence Yates’s metaphor: Alcuin possesses only one horse of the later two-horse vehicle.

This is a productive reconstruction of the later doctrinal synthesis, though medieval manuscripts and teaching practices were more varied than a two-book model can capture.

## 15. Carruthers: the principal correction

Mary Carruthers’s *The Book of Memory* and *The Craft of Thought* substantially revised the field created by Yates.

### What Carruthers confirms

- Ancient and medieval memory is trained, not merely innate.
- Writing and memory are mutually constitutive.
- Wax, page, grid, architectural place, division, and image belong to a shared craft vocabulary.
- Images are instruments of thought and composition, not simply passive storage tags.
- Ordered visual forms were central to medieval intellectual work.

### What Carruthers corrects

**[Scholarly correction]** Yates often narrates a bounded ancient “artificial memory” disappearing and later reappearing. Carruthers reconstructs a much broader *memoria*: lectio, murmured reading, rumination, textual division, page layout, numerical ordering, liturgical repetition, mental architecture, ethical formation, and invention.

On this account, Alcuin’s writing, memorization, meditation, and habit are not the ruins left after memory has vanished. They are themselves components of medieval memorial craft.

Carruthers therefore changes the question from:

> Did Alcuin preserve the complete *Ad Herennium* system?

to:

> What practices enabled Carolingian readers to possess, retrieve, reorganize, and compose from learned material?

The answer includes but is not limited to loci and images.

### Important limitation

Carruthers’s broader category should not erase the specific textual fact Yates identified: Alcuin’s *Disputatio* supplies no instructions for spatial loci and imaginal deposits.

Both propositions can be true:

- **Yates is right locally:** the classical architectural recipe is absent from Alcuin’s chapter.
- **Carruthers is right culturally:** disciplined, visual, textual, and compositional memory did not disappear.

## 16. Small, Rossi, Bolzoni, Hajdu, Sabbatino, and Ong

### Jocelyn Penny Small

*Wax Tablets of the Mind* (1997) situates ancient memory language among actual technologies of literacy. It supports reading Martianus’s wax/page analogy materially rather than as an empty metaphor. Wax tablets encouraged habits of inscription, ordering, erasure, rewriting, and visible segmentation.

### Paolo Rossi

*Clavis universalis*—translated as *Logic and the Art of Memory*—traces the movement from rhetorical memory toward combinatorial systems, encyclopedic ordering, universal language, and method. Rossi is more directly pertinent to Lull, Camillo, Bruno, and Leibniz than to Martianus or Alcuin, but his longue durée helps distinguish:

- a fixed local-image system;
- classificatory diagrams;
- logical combination;
- encyclopedic architectures;
- universal-language projects.

Martianus belongs primarily to rhetorical and encyclopedic transmission, not to mechanical combination.

### Lina Bolzoni

*The Gallery of Memory* concentrates on Renaissance literary and visual culture. Its relevance here is retrospective: personified arts, painted figures, emblems, and spatially arranged knowledge become available as compositional machines. Bolzoni does not supply evidence that Martianus’s own allegory was already Camillo’s theatre in embryo.

### Helga Hajdu

Hajdu’s history of mnemonic doctrine was a major predecessor to Yates. She treated the technical lineage more schematically. Later work has corrected the tendency to infer actual practice directly from the mere survival of prescriptive texts.

### Andrea Torre, Saverio Sabbatino, and related Italian scholarship

Italian work on literary memory emphasizes the permeability among rhetoric, iconology, commentary, and poetic composition. Its strongest contribution to the present dossier is methodological: resemblance between an allegorical figure and a mnemonic image establishes affordance, not necessarily authorial intention.

### Walter J. Ong

Ong’s work on Ramus belongs much later. Ramist dichotomies and page diagrams replace or reorganize aspects of rhetorical memory through visual method. Alcuin’s classificatory dialogue and its diagrams are remote predecessors only in the very general sense that both organize doctrine for teaching. No direct Alcuin-to-Ramus line is documented.

## 18. Dedicated contested-material pass

Searches were conducted for combinations of Martianus, Alcuin, Yates, artificial memory, dispute, attribution, dating, magic, heresy, images, and transmission. The principal contested points found are these.

### 18.1 Did the art “disappear”?

**[Yates]** Yes, from Alcuin’s explicit memory rules.

**[Criticism]** Carol Gibson-Wood, studying the Utrecht Psalter, objects that Yates’s conclusion is not fully founded: *ediscendi exercitatio* could encompass mnemonic practices even though Alcuin does not name their rules.

**Assessment:** The criticism is valid against a broad cultural claim but not against a narrow textual claim. Alcuin’s words do not prove that exercise included loci; they merely leave the possibility open.

### 18.2 Did Alcuin know the local-image art?

**[Documented]** His principal source, Julius Victor, explicitly mentions and rejects loci and images.

**[Strong reconstruction]** Alcuin probably knew at least this notice because his surrounding language closely follows Victor.

**[Unknown]** Whether he knew the *Ad Herennium* itself.

**[Correction to Yates]** “Alcuin did not seem to know” is appropriately cautious when applied to the *Ad Herennium*. It should not become “Alcuin had never heard of places and images.”

### 18.3 Was the art suppressed as pagan or magical?

No primary evidence says so.

### 18.4 Was Martianus himself pagan?

**[Disputed]** His work is saturated with classical gods, Neoplatonic cosmology, ritual language, and allegory. Gregory of Tours’s phrase *Martianus noster* has sometimes been used to claim Christian affiliation. Neither proves a secure confession.

The memory passage itself is religiously neutral.

### 18.5 When did Martianus write?

**[Disputed]**

- Traditional early chronology: after the sack of Rome in 410, before Vandal capture of Carthage in 439—sometimes narrowed to before 429.
- Alan Cameron argued for approximately 410–415.
- Danuta Shanzer and others have supported a later fifth-century date.
- S. J. B. Barnish proposed a late-fifth-century Roman context.

Yates’s dramatic placement amid the catastrophes of 410–439 reflects the conventional chronology available to her. It is not settled fact.

### 18.6 Was Martianus an advocate?

**[Probable reconstruction]** Internal evidence supports association with legal/rhetorical culture in Carthage. Biographical claims remain sparse and often rest on difficult passages in a deliberately comic work.

### 18.7 Was he from Madaura?

**[Rejected older attribution]** The identification appears to derive from Hugo Grotius’s confusion of Martianus with the *Madaurensis* mentioned by Cassiodorus—Apuleius. Manuscript titles generally call Martianus *Afer Carthaginiensis*.

### 18.8 Did the personified arts originate as mnemonic images?

**[Yatesian hypothesis]** They conform to mnemonic-image rules.

**[Documented]** They are vivid ordered allegorical figures.

**[Not documented]** That Martianus constructed them by applying the *Ad Herennium*’s image rules.

### 18.9 Are the manuscript diagrams mnemonic?

**[Disputed category]** They certainly aid learning and recollection. But the surviving diagrams are overwhelmingly disciplinary and scientific, not technical plans of an architectural *ars memoriae*.

## History and context

## 19. Late-antique rhetorical education

Martianus’s memory chapter belongs to forensic and deliberative rhetoric. Its opening concern is practical: an orator must retain both his own material and the opponent’s statements.

The institutional background includes:

- municipal and imperial law courts;
- rhetorical schools;
- training in declamation and civil causes;
- late-antique encyclopedic compendia;
- North African literary education.

**[Reconstruction]** A Carthaginian legal or rhetorical milieu is highly plausible. The claim that Martianus personally learned loci from a living master is possible but undocumented.

## 20. The seven liberal arts

Martianus did not invent every one of the seven disciplines, nor was he the first author to juxtapose all of them. His decisive contribution was to present a memorable and enormously influential literary architecture in which the seven arts speak in sequence.

The later labels *trivium* and *quadrivium* should not be retrojected too mechanically into his own vocabulary. The grouping became foundational in medieval curricula through a wider network including:

- Augustine;
- Martianus;
- Boethius;
- Cassiodorus;
- Isidore;
- Carolingian school authors.

## 21. Cassiodorus, Isidore, and Bede

Yates says Cassiodorus, Isidore, and Bede do not mention the artificial art.

This must be stated narrowly.

**[Confirmed in the relevant rhetorical summaries]** They do not transmit a complete loci-and-images recipe comparable to Martianus or the *Ad Herennium*.

**[Qualification]** Their works participate extensively in ordering knowledge, textual division, numerical structure, diagram, computus, exegesis, and disciplined recollection. “They say nothing about artificial memory” is too broad if *artificial memory* is used in Carruthers’s expanded sense.

## 22. The Carolingian court and schools

Alcuin taught within the courtly circle associated with Charlemagne and later served at Tours. The *Disputatio* joins rhetoric to royal morality.

The dialogue’s institutions are:

- the palace school or royal scholarly circle;
- episcopal and monastic schools;
- scriptoria;
- courts and councils;
- administrative government;
- theological disputation.

Its rhetorical education is not an antiquarian hobby. Speech, legal classification, debate, moral authority, and royal government are integrated.

## 23. Rhetoric and virtue

Earlier scholarship sometimes treated the virtue section as an awkward appendage to the rhetoric.

Wallach interpreted the work as a *via regia* or mirror for princes. Costrino has shown that Julius Victor’s structure and Alcuin’s source use help explain the apparent duality.

**[Textual structure]** The transition from rhetoric to virtues is prepared by the dialogue’s concern with the honorable conduct and formation of the speaker.

Memory therefore belongs to both:

- competent speech;
- prudent moral judgment.

## 24. The actual Carolingian reception of Martianus

The strongest correction to a simple story of loss is the manuscript record.

Sinéad O’Sullivan’s work shows:

- dense Carolingian annotation of *De nuptiis*;
- wide geographical distribution;
- copying in important centers;
- multiple gloss corpora;
- intermixture among gloss traditions;
- diagrams and illustrations;
- approximately twenty manuscripts of the oldest gloss tradition, chiefly from the second quarter of the ninth through the tenth century.

Prominent readers or commentators include:

- John Scottus Eriugena;
- Remigius of Auxerre;
- scholars formerly grouped under “Dunchad” or Martin of Laon;
- anonymous teachers and students at Auxerre, Corbie, Reims, Lorsch, Cologne, and related centers.

**[Correction]** Martianus was not merely placed on a shelf by late antiquity and rediscovered centuries later. His text became an exceptionally active Carolingian school object.

**[Negative finding]** Published work on the gloss traditions has not yet established that §§538–539 themselves generated a distinct Carolingian practice of architectural mnemonic training.

## 25. From Martianus toward later medieval memory

A defensible line of descent is:

1. Classical rhetoric formalizes natural and artificial memory.
2. Martianus preserves a compressed loci-and-images account within Rhetoric.
3. His encyclopedia and personified arts receive intense Carolingian study.
4. Alcuin preserves *memoria* as rhetorical faculty and part of Prudence but follows Julius Victor’s practice-centered account.
5. From the eleventh and especially twelfth century, the *Ad Herennium* circulates extensively under Cicero’s name, often beside *De inventione*.
6. Scholastic moral theory connects memory with Prudence.
7. Later memory treatises redevelop architectural, imaginal, alphabetic, devotional, and diagrammatic techniques.

This is a network, not a provable single chain from Martianus through Alcuin to every later system.

## People

## 26. Martianus Capella

Almost nothing securely biographical is known beyond the work and its subscriptions.

Likely or traditional identifications include:

- African;
- associated with Carthage;
- trained in rhetoric and law;
- possibly an advocate;
- author of one surviving encyclopedic work.

Claims that he was a proconsul, a Christian teacher, or a participant in a pagan cultural resistance exceed the evidence.

## 27. Simonides

**[Tradition]** Poet and reputed inventor of mnemonic places after the banquet-hall disaster.

**[Historical caution]** The story is a foundational explanatory legend transmitted by Cicero, Quintilian, Pliny, and later authors. It may encode real Greek practices of spatial ordering, but the narrated accident is not independently documented.

## 28. Securus Memor Felix

A sixth-century rhetor/editor whose subscription records correction of corrupt exemplars in Rome.

He is a practitioner of textual criticism, not a documented practitioner of the memory system.

## 29. Alcuin

Alcuin of York was:

- educated in the Northumbrian tradition;
- recruited to Charlemagne’s circle;
- teacher, poet, theologian, exegete, and administrator;
- a central figure in Carolingian educational reform;
- later abbot of Saint-Martin at Tours.

His pedagogical method favors question-and-answer dialogue, definition, division, repetition, and moral formation.

## 30. Charlemagne

In the dialogue, Charlemagne is both student and active interrogator. He calls memory the noblest part of rhetoric, asks whether it can be acquired or increased, and accepts the brevity and difficulty of Alcuin’s precepts.

**[Literary caution]** The dialogue is not a stenographic transcript of a lesson. It stages royal learning and may idealize both monarch and master.

## 31. Julius Victor

Victor is the immediate source of much of Alcuin’s account of rhetoric, including the memory definition and practice-centered approach.

His rejection of loci is especially significant because Alcuin silently removes it.

## 32. Carolingian commentators

John Scottus Eriugena and Remigius of Auxerre are crucial to Martianus’s reception. The oldest gloss corpus was once attributed to Dunchad, later to Martin of Laon, but is now regarded as the work of multiple scholars.

**[Contested attribution]** Treating the oldest gloss tradition as the unitary work of one famous author is no longer tenable.

## 33. Anecdotes requested in the general template

The auctioneer, professor’s party, student who lost his memory, and accused magician belong to other memory-art dossiers or later mnemonic lore.

**[Negative finding]** No primary source connects those anecdotes to Martianus’s §§538–539 or Alcuin’s *Disputatio*. They should not be imported merely to enliven this dossier.

The only use anecdote inside this system is the Simonides banquet story, itself traditional rather than securely historical.

## The magic question

## 34. Is Martianus’s procedure magical?

**[Text]** No. The mechanism is natural memory assisted by *ars*, order, visual representation, writing, and practice.

There are:

- no planetary correspondences;
- no spirits;
- no consecration;
- no talismanic images;
- no divine names;
- no ritual purity;
- no claim of supernatural acquisition;
- no forbidden books.

The mythological setting of *De nuptiis* is an allegorical literary frame. It does not make the memory instructions a magical rite.

## 35. Are the personifications talismanic?

No primary evidence supports this.

**[Yatesian background]** Yates’s wider history moves toward Renaissance systems in which images may carry astral or magical power. Her observation that Martianus’s arts resemble mnemonic images does not mean she proves them talismanic.

**[Modern invention]** Describing Martianus’s Rhetoric as an “occult memory goddess” or his seven arts as “planetary memory seals” would be unsupported conflation with Ficino, Camillo, or Bruno.

## 36. Is Alcuin suppressing magic or paganism?

There is no evidence that he omits loci and images because he regards them as magical.

The simplest source-based explanation is:

1. he follows Julius Victor;
2. Victor prefers learning, writing, division, and composition;
3. Alcuin adapts this program to moral and courtly education.

A theological objection cannot be ruled out absolutely, but it is not stated.

## 37. Relation to the *Ars notoria*

The *Ars notoria* is a later medieval ritual tradition promising infused knowledge through prayers, figures, names, and contemplative procedures.

There is no documented line from Martianus §§538–539 or Alcuin §39 to the *Ars notoria*.

The common concern with acquiring the liberal arts is insufficient to establish descent:

| Martianus/Alcuin | *Ars notoria* |
|---|---|
| training and practice | ritual and prayer |
| natural faculty aided by art | extraordinary or divinely mediated acquisition |
| semantic images in ordered places | sacred figures and verbal operations |
| no spirits or angelic mediation | traditions may invoke angels or divine names |

## 38. Accusations, heresy, and the Inquisition

**[Negative finding]**

- Martianus is not known to have been prosecuted for this art.
- Alcuin was not accused of mnemonic magic.
- No Inquisitorial consequence is attached to either memory passage.
- Neither passage is known to have been censored because of loci or images.

Later controversies surrounding ritual magic, demonic invocation, Bruno, or heterodox cosmology must not be back-projected onto this system.

## Afterlife

## 39. The liberal-arts image tradition

Martianus’s most visible afterlife lies less in his two mnemonic paragraphs than in the personified arts.

Ordered female figures of the arts appear in:

- manuscript illumination;
- cathedral sculpture and glass;
- encyclopedic diagrams;
- school imagery;
- fresco cycles;
- the *Hortus deliciarum* tradition;
- Herrad of Landsberg’s image of Philosophy and the arts;
- Andrea da Firenze’s Spanish Chapel frescoes;
- humanist manuscripts;
- the so-called Mantegna Tarocchi;
- Gregor Reisch’s *Margarita philosophica*;
- later emblematic and encyclopedic frontispieces.

**[Influence caution]** Some descend directly or indirectly from Martianus; others participate in a larger iconographic tradition drawing also on Boethius, Cassiodorus, Isidore, Alain de Lille, scholastic curricula, and local programs.

## 40. Dante

Dante’s ordered moral and cosmological imagery belongs to a culture saturated with trained memory, allegory, number, and place. Carruthers and others have made a strong case for reading medieval literary architecture through memorial craft.

**[Reconstruction]** Dante may be illuminated by the tradition Martianus helped transmit.

**[Not documented]** No evidence shows Dante copying Martianus §§538–539 as a practical mnemonic manual.

## 41. Frescoes and cathedral programs

Yates famously relates medieval didactic imagery to artificial memory. Later scholarship accepts that ordered visual cycles facilitate recollection but is more cautious about calling every cycle a “memory palace.”

A fresco series can have several functions simultaneously:

- instruction;
- contemplation;
- liturgical orientation;
- institutional identity;
- moral persuasion;
- mnemonic prompting.

Formal similarity is not proof of a prescribed mnemonic walk.

## 42. Emblem books

The pairing of a striking image, motto, and explanatory text resembles the fundamental mnemonic relation between image and matter. Yet emblem books are a sixteenth-century genre with their own rhetorical and publishing history.

Martianus is part of the remote culture of personification and attribute, not a sufficient direct source.

## 43. Lull, Camillo, Bruno, and Fludd

These later systems differ sharply:

- **Ramon Llull:** combinatorial letters, figures, and logical principles;
- **Giulio Camillo:** a theater organized by planetary and mythological grades;
- **Giordano Bruno:** elaborate image fields, combinatorial wheels, and philosophical transformation;
- **Robert Fludd:** theater, cosmology, and engraved architectural schemes.

Martianus supplies:

- an encyclopedic ordering of disciplines;
- vivid personifications;
- the idea that memory joins order, place, and image.

He does not supply:

- rotating wheels;
- combinatorial alphabets;
- astral grades;
- occult correspondences;
- a theater structure.

Any genealogical line must pass through many intervening sources, particularly the *Ad Herennium*, medieval moralized memory, Lullism, Renaissance Platonism, and Hermeticism.

## 44. Ramus

Ramist method arranges knowledge into dichotomous visual trees. It often shifts the burden traditionally borne by rhetorical memory into dialectical organization and page display.

Alcuin’s definitions and divisions provide a remote analogy, but Ramus’s intellectual setting is sixteenth-century humanism and dialectical reform. Ong’s work does not establish direct dependence on Alcuin.

## 45. Borges and Eco

### Borges’s “Funes the Memorious”

Funes possesses almost limitless natural retention but lacks abstraction and productive forgetting. That is nearly the inverse of Martianus and Alcuin:

- their memory depends on division and selected signs;
- Funes cannot subordinate detail to conceptual order.

Any link is thematic, not genealogical.

### Umberto Eco

Eco’s interest in encyclopedias, semiotics, medieval classifications, and universal language places Martianus within the prehistory of organized signs. Direct influence should be demonstrated work by work rather than assumed.

## 46. Modern memory palaces

Contemporary memory athletes use familiar routes, loci, and bizarre images. Structurally this resembles Cicero and the *Ad Herennium* far more fully than Martianus’s compressed account.

Martianus nevertheless offers an unusually concise ancient description of the same retrieval logic:

> an ordered place “returns” the image deposited in it.

Modern systems add standardized routes, competition disciplines, person-action-object codes, phonetic systems, and rapid reuse. These are modern elaborations, not recoveries of details found in Martianus.

## 47. Computing and combinatorics

Martianus’s places-and-images structure can be compared analogically to indexed storage:

- locus = address;
- image = encoded datum;
- traversal = ordered query;
- retrieval cue = address-content association.

This is a useful explanatory analogy, not historical descent.

The often-invoked history “Lull → Leibniz → computer” has genuine elements:

- Lull devised combinatorial figures;
- Leibniz pursued a *characteristica universalis* and formal calculation;
- later logic contributed to computing.

But Martianus’s two paragraphs contain no combinatorial machine. They concern storage and cueing, not symbolic calculation.

## 49. What Yates got right

- She identified the exact survival of loci and images in Martianus.
- She recognized the importance of its canonical location within Rhetoric and the liberal arts.
- She saw the mnemonic potential of the personified disciplines.
- She correctly observed the absence of loci and images from Alcuin’s explicit memory precepts.
- She correctly connected Alcuin’s virtue scheme to *De inventione*.
- She correctly identified Julius Victor as central to Alcuin’s rhetorical sources.
- She highlighted the later significance of pairing *De inventione* with the *Ad Herennium*.

## 50. What needs correction or qualification

### “Firmly lodged”

True textually, but it does not establish continuous practical teaching of the local art. Manuscript survival and practical use are different questions.

### “Well-lighted places”

The Latin supports conspicuous or distinct places; the fuller illumination rule comes from comparison with other classical sources.

### “The personified arts conform to image rules”

They do, but no authorial mnemonic program is stated.

### “The artificial memory has disappeared”

It has disappeared from Alcuin’s explicit instructions. The broader culture of trained memory has not.

### “Alcuin did not know the second rhetoric”

Possible for the *Ad Herennium* as a text. Yet through Julius Victor he probably knew that observations about loci and images existed.

### The dramatic collapse narrative

Yates’s transition from Vandal destruction to mnemonic loss is powerful historical writing. It can imply a causal relation that the evidence does not establish. Compendia select, abbreviate, and alter material for many reasons besides civilizational collapse.

## 51. Legend and modern embellishment

### Simonides’s banquet

Ancient explanatory legend, not modern invention; transmitted by multiple classical sources but historically unverifiable.

### Martianus deliberately encoded the whole encyclopedia as a memory palace

Modern speculation unless supported by a specific argument. No route of loci is described.

### Alcuin prohibited pagan images

Unsupported modern inference.

### Monks secretly preserved a forbidden magical memory art

Legend/modern occult narrative. The manuscript evidence instead shows overt copying and commentary on Martianus.

### Night study caused Albertus Magnus’s “nocturnal ram”

Yates suggests humorously that Albertus’s strange nocturnal experience might reflect excessive night memory work of the sort Martianus recommends. This is speculative association, not evidence of influence or causation.

### The seven arts are prototypes of Bruno’s talismanic images

Modern conflation.

## Open questions

1. **What exact source or source-combination did Martianus use for §§538–539?**  
   The mixture resembles Cicero, the *Ad Herennium*, and Quintilian, but a precise lost handbook or epitome may lie behind it.

2. **How should *locis illustribus* be translated?**  
   Collation of textual variants and the usage of *illustris* elsewhere in Martianus may sharpen the choice among bright, conspicuous, and clearly differentiated.

3. **How were §§538–539 glossed in each Carolingian commentary family?**  
   A complete comparison of the oldest gloss tradition, Eriugena, Remigius, and anonymous scholia could reveal whether teachers expanded the compressed rules.

4. **Do any manuscripts add diagrams, marginal hands, numbered loci, or visual examples specifically beside §§538–539?**  
   No standard published account establishes such a witness. A systematic manuscript-image survey remains desirable.

5. **Did Alcuin omit Julius Victor’s rejection of loci deliberately?**  
   The omission might indicate indifference, abbreviation, disagreement, or simple adaptation. No explicit explanation survives.

6. **Does *ediscendi exercitatio* include imaginal mnemonics?**  
   Gibson-Wood considers that possible. The phrase itself does not specify the exercise.

7. **How independent are Alcuin’s four precepts?**  
   The precise sources of *cogitandi studium* and the sobriety clause merit line-by-line comparison with health regimens, monastic discipline, and late-antique rhetorical manuals.

8. **How did readers actually use liberal-arts personifications?**  
   Textual vividness, manuscript illustration, classroom exposition, and deliberate mnemonic placement may overlap, but their proportions are rarely recoverable.

9. **What chronology best fits Martianus?**  
   The early fifth-century and late fifth-century hypotheses alter the political and institutional background of his educational project.

10. **Can “artificial memory” be used consistently across Yates and Carruthers?**  
    Yates usually means the technical loci-and-images art; Carruthers often means the larger trained craft of medieval remembrance and invention. Many apparent disagreements arise from this difference of scale.

---

## Final assessment

Martianus Capella preserves the minimum viable form of classical local memory:

> ordered, distinct places receive visual signs; in recollection each place returns its deposited sign, just as wax or a page bears letters.

That is a real system, but a severely abbreviated one. Its only worked image examples are a bride’s veil for marriage and a sword or arms for homicide. The full architectural rules and emotionally heightened *imagines agentes* must be recovered from Cicero and especially the *Ad Herennium*, not attributed to Martianus.

Alcuin retains memory’s dignity, its place among the five parts of rhetoric, and its ethical place within Prudence. What he does not retain is the spatial-image recipe. His positive program—memorization, writing, meditation, repeated practice, coherent order, and sobriety—comes principally through Julius Victor. Because Victor explicitly knew and rejected loci and images, Alcuin’s silence cannot prove ignorance.

Yates’s phrase “the art handed on and lost” remains excellent as a description of the contrast between two short textbook passages. It fails as a complete history of early medieval memory. Manuscripts, glosses, diagrams, murmured reading, writing, division, liturgy, and learned composition show that memorial culture remained vigorous. The more exact conclusion is:

> **The local-image rules were handed on by Martianus, omitted by Alcuin, and not demonstrably operative in Alcuin’s own treatise; the larger arts of trained memory were transformed, not lost.**
