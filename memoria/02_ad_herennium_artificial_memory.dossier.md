# The Ad Herennium artificial memory: Research Dossier

## Basic identification

| Field | Identification |
|---|---|
| **Name** | The artificial memory of the *Rhetorica ad Herennium*; later the “Tullian,” “Ciceronian,” or architectural art of memory |
| **Catalogue position** | System 2 of 45 in the stated *memoria* catalogue |
| **Exponent** | An anonymous Roman rhetoric teacher, conventionally called the *Auctor ad Herennium* |
| **Date** | Conventionally c. 86–82 BCE, though the exact date remains disputed |
| **Source text** | *Rhetorica ad C. Herennium* III.xvi–xxiv, §§28–40; Latin text in Friedrich Marx’s editions and subsequent editions by Harry Caplan, Guy Achard, Gualtiero Calboli, and Thierry Fögen |
| **Principle in one sentence** | Artificial memory strengthens natural memory by fixing a reusable, ordered sequence of clearly differentiated places and temporarily depositing in them personally effective, vividly active images representing either the substance or the words of a discourse. |
| **Yates** | *The Art of Memory* (1966), ch. I, pp. 4–17 and 24–25; Greek precursors and *notae*, ch. II, pp. 41–43; medieval transmission, ch. III, pp. 54–56, 63, 66–67, 77 |

### Evidentiary key

The following labels are used throughout:

- **[TEXT]** Directly documented in a primary source.
- **[MANUSCRIPT]** Documented by a manuscript, catalogue, collation, or textual apparatus.
- **[RECONSTRUCTION]** A scholarly inference from incomplete evidence.
- **[TRADITION]** A historical attribution or interpretation transmitted by earlier writers.
- **[YATES]** Frances Yates’s interpretation or synthesis.
- **[DISPUTED]** Seriously contested attribution, dating, genealogy, or interpretation.
- **[LEGEND]** A story transmitted as exemplary history but not independently verifiable.
- **[MODERN]** A modern adaptation, analogy, or retrospective use of the tradition.

A central caution governs the dossier: the *Ad Herennium* itself contains no occult cosmology, planetary wheel, talisman, ritual invocation, or surviving authorial diagram. Camillo’s theatre, Lull’s figures, Bruno’s wheels, and Fludd’s plates belong to later systems. They descend partly from, combine with, or have been interpreted through the *Ad Herennium* tradition, but they are not components of the ancient system.

## The system in detail

## 1. Memory within rhetoric

**[TEXT]** The author places memory among the five traditional functions of the orator: invention, arrangement, style, delivery, and memory. At III.28 he calls it:

> *thesaurum inventorum atque ad omnium partium rhetoricae custodem, memoriam*

“memory, the treasury of things invented and the guardian of all the parts of rhetoric.”

The wording is important. Memory is not initially offered as a universal metaphysics or as a private parlor feat. Its immediate institutional setting is rhetorical education, especially the preparation and delivery of speeches.

The author distinguishes two kinds:

> *Sunt igitur duae memoriae: una naturalis, altera artificiosa. Naturalis est ea, quae nostris animis insita est et simul cum cogitatione nata; artificiosa est ea, quam confirmat inductio quaedam et ratio praeceptionis.*

“There are therefore two memories: one natural, the other artificial. Natural memory is that implanted in our minds and born together with thought; artificial memory is that which a certain training and rational system of instruction strengthens.”

**[TEXT]** The artificial art neither creates a mental faculty from nothing nor makes natural ability unnecessary. Training develops nature; natural aptitude remains necessary. The author postpones proving that memory is teachable and never supplies the promised proof in the surviving treatise.

### The fundamental definition

> *Constat igitur artificiosa memoria locis et imaginibus.*

“Artificial memory, therefore, consists of places and images.” (*Ad Herennium* III.29)

Yates calls this “the stock definition to be forever repeated down the ages.” That is substantially correct as a description of the later Latin mnemonic tradition, although “every” later memory treatise is too absolute: some medieval, Ramist, pedagogical, numerical, phonetic, and associational systems do not follow the entire Ad Herennium plan.

## 2. What is a *locus*?

### Definition

**[TEXT]**

> *Locos appellamus eos, qui breviter, perfecte, insignite aut natura aut manu sunt absoluti, ut eos facile naturali memoria conprehendere et amplecti queamus: ut aedes, intercolumnium, angulum, fornicem et alia, quae his similia sunt.*

“We call places those that have been completed, naturally or by human workmanship, within a limited compass, completely and conspicuously, so that we can easily grasp and embrace them with natural memory: for example, a house, an intercolumnar space, a corner, an arch, and other similar things.” (III.29)

Caplan translates *loci* as “backgrounds.” “Places” is more literal and preserves the vocabulary of the later tradition. A place is not necessarily an entire building. It is a perceptually bounded station within a larger ordered setting.

### Real and fictitious places

**[TEXT]** Places may be:

1. naturally formed;
2. made by human work;
3. already known from experience; or
4. deliberately constructed in thought.

The decisive passage is III.32:

> *Cogitatio enim quamvis regionem potest amplecti et in ea situm loci cuiusdam ad suum arbitrium fabricari et architectari.*

“Thought can embrace any region whatsoever and can at will fabricate and architect within it the situation of some place.”

**[TEXT]** Consequently, the ancient art expressly permits imaginary architecture. The modern term “memory palace” captures one common implementation, but it is not the ancient name of the system and should not imply that a palace is required.

**[YATES]** Yates dramatizes this process by asking who is walking slowly through a lonely building and stopping with an intent expression: a rhetoric student forming memory loci. The scene is her reconstruction, not an ancient anecdote.

### Rules for places

The rules form a coherent perceptual design.

#### A. Numerous

**[TEXT]** A person wishing to remember many things must prepare many loci. One image or image-complex is then deposited at each station.

#### B. Fixed in an unambiguous series

> *ex ordine locos conparare*

“prepare the places in order.”

**[TEXT]** The sequence must be so thoroughly learned that the practitioner can begin at any station and proceed forward, backward, or from the middle. The author compares this with naming acquaintances standing in a line.

This gives the system:

- serial recall;
- reverse recall;
- access from an intermediate point;
- a stable positional index independent of the changing contents.

The text does not describe simultaneous random access in the computing sense. It describes practiced navigability through an ordered series.

#### C. Permanently learned; temporarily furnished

**[TEXT]**

> *imagines, sicuti litterae delentur, ubi nihil utimur; loci, tamquam cera, remanere debent.*

“The images, like letters, are erased when we cease to use them; the places, like wax, ought to remain.”

The places are the reusable support. Images are replaceable inscriptions. Earlier in the passage the author expands the comparison:

> *Nam loci cerae aut cartae simillimi sunt, imagines litteris, dispositio et conlocatio imaginum scripturae, pronuntiatio lectioni.*

“Places are very like wax tablets or papyrus, images like letters, the arrangement and positioning of the images like writing, and delivery like reading.” (III.30)

**[TEXT]** Retrieval is thus figured as inward reading, not merely as spontaneous association.

**[SCHOLARSHIP]** Jocelyn Penny Small and Mary Carruthers emphasize that this comparison belongs to a culture in which writing and trained memory supported one another. It should not be turned into a simple opposition between purely oral memory and external writing. Medieval reading, note-making, page layout, meditation, and composition were themselves memorial practices.

#### D. Numbered every fifth place

**[TEXT]** Each fifth station should be marked. The fifth may contain a golden hand; the tenth, an acquaintance named Decimus:

> *quintum quemque placet notari: quod genus, si in quinto loco manum auream conlocemus, si in decumo aliquem notum, cui praenomen sit Decumo...*

This is a positional checksum. *Decimus* supplies a name-number pun for ten. Similar markers can recur at subsequent fifth stations.

The author does not prescribe Roman numerals painted on doors. That is a modern pedagogical simplification.

#### E. Deserted rather than crowded

**[TEXT]**

> *commodius est in derelicta, quam in celebri regione locos conparare... solitudo conservat integras simulacrorum figuras.*

“It is more convenient to prepare places in a deserted than in a crowded region … solitude preserves intact the outlines of the images.”

Moving crowds interfere with the distinctiveness of the artificial figures. The rule concerns attentional and imaginal interference.

#### F. Unlike one another

**[TEXT]** A long succession of identical intercolumnar spaces creates confusion. Loci should differ in form and character so the user can distinguish what belongs at each.

#### G. Moderate in size

**[TEXT]** Very large places make images vague; very small ones cannot readily receive an arrangement of images.

#### H. Moderately illuminated

**[TEXT]** Places should be neither too dark nor too bright. Darkness obscures images; glare makes them visually indistinct.

#### I. Moderately spaced

**[TEXT]**

> *Intervalla locorum mediocria placet esse, fere paulo plus aut minus pedum tricenum.*

“The intervals between places should be moderate, approximately thirty feet, a little more or less.”

Too near and too far are both said to weaken the “inner eye of thought,” just as they weaken ordinary sight. “Thirty feet” is approximate, not an invariant geometrical module.

### The *solitudo/sollicitudo* episode

**[TEXT]** The transmitted ancient rule is *solitudo conservat integras simulacrorum figuras*: solitude preserves the figures.

**[YATES]** Yates reports that Thomas Aquinas or the textual tradition available to him converted *solitudo* into *sollicitudo*, “solicitude,” and interpreted emotional concern as helping an image adhere.

**[DISPUTED/CAUTION]** The broader medieval principle that affect and concentrated intention strengthen memory is well documented in scholastic psychology. But the claim that a single scribal corruption generated that principle is too simple. Manuscripts, glosses, and scholastic argument must be distinguished: medieval authors already possessed Aristotelian and Augustinian accounts of attention, intention, and affect. A corrupt or differently read lemma may have reinforced an existing psychology rather than created it.

## 3. What is an *imago*?

### Definition

**[TEXT]**

> *Imagines sunt formae quaedam et notae et simulacra eius rei, quam meminisse volumus.*

“Images are certain forms, marks, and representations of the thing we wish to remember.”

The three nouns resist reduction to a modern mental “picture”:

- *forma* — form or shape;
- *nota* — mark, sign, identifying cue;
- *simulacrum* — likeness or representation.

An image can therefore work iconically, symbolically, through punning, through a person’s name, through theatrical action, or through a composite scene.

### Two kinds of likeness

**[TEXT]**

> *Duplices igitur similitudines esse debent, unae rerum, alterae verborum.*

“There must therefore be two kinds of likenesses, one of things and the other of words.”

The distinction is functional:

- **Memory for things, *memoria rerum***, stores the subject matter, arguments, conceptual divisions, or gist.
- **Memory for words, *memoria verborum***, attempts to cue individual words or short verbal segments.

“Things” does not simply mean physical objects. In rhetorical theory *res* includes the matter, proposition, argument, or thought expressed in words. Cicero’s *De inventione* I.9 similarly distinguishes what a discourse says from the words in which it says it.

## 4. The rules for effective images

### Images must be individually selected

**[TEXT]** Different people are struck by different likenesses. The author therefore rejects a universal stock catalogue and says:

> *sibi quemque suo commodo convenit imagines conparare.*

“Each person should prepare images according to his own convenience.”

A teacher should teach how to find images and provide one or two examples, not compile images for every possible word. This explains why the treatise gives no complete furnished memory route.

### Images must be sharply characterized

**[TEXT]** The governing paragraph, III.37, reads:

> *Id accidet, si quam maxime notatas similitudines constituemus; si non multas nec vagas, sed aliquid agentes imagines ponemus; si egregiam pulcritudinem aut unicam turpitudinem eis adtribuemus; si aliquas exornabimus, ut si coronis aut veste purpurea … aut si qua re deformabimus, ut si cruentam aut caeno oblitam aut rubrica delibutam inducamus … aut ridiculas res aliquas imaginibus adtribuamus.*

“This will happen if we establish likenesses as strikingly marked as possible; if we place images that are not numerous or vague but are doing something; if we give them exceptional beauty or singular ugliness; if we adorn some, for example with crowns or purple clothing … or disfigure them in some way, introducing one bloodstained, smeared with mud, or daubed with red paint … or attach comic things to the images.”

The rules are:

- few elements rather than a vague crowd;
- clear differentiation;
- visible action;
- extreme beauty or ugliness;
- unusual costume;
- bodily deformation or staining;
- humor, absurdity, scandal, wonder, or emotional force.

### *Imagines agentes*

The famous expression is:

> *non multas nec vagas, sed aliquid agentes imagines*

“images not numerous or vague, but doing something.”

**[TEXT]** Grammatically, *agentes* means acting, active, or doing something. The ancient text does not capitalize it as the name of an esoteric class.

**[YATES]** Yates’s rendering “active images” and her emphasis on *imagines agentes* successfully identify the dynamic core of the advice.

**[SCHOLARSHIP]** Carruthers broadens the interpretation: medieval mnemonic imagery is not simply a representational picture gallery but a tool for affective, ethical, and compositional work. An image “acts” partly because it provokes, organizes, and directs recollection and judgment.

### Why strangeness works

**[TEXT]** Nature remembers what is unusual:

> *si quid videmus aut audimus egregie turpe aut honestum, inusitatum, magnum, incredibile, ridiculum, id diu meminisse consuevimus.*

“If we see or hear something exceptionally disgraceful or honorable, unusual, great, unbelievable, or laughable, we are accustomed to remember it for a long time.”

The author contrasts daily sunrise and sunset with an eclipse. Art should imitate nature:

> *Imitetur ars igitur naturam.*

“Let art therefore imitate nature.”

This is a rudimentary theory of distinctiveness and arousal, not evidence for talismanic efficacy.

### Rehearsal

**[TEXT]** Images and loci are not self-operating. The user must repeatedly traverse the original places in the mind to refresh the images and practice placing images every day. The concluding warning is severe:

> *in nemonicis minimum valet doctrina, nisi industria, studio, labore, diligentia conprobatur.*

“In mnemonics theory is of very little value unless confirmed by industry, devotion, labor, and care.”

Any modern presentation promising effortless or photographic recall contradicts the source.

## 5. Worked example: memory for things

### The lawsuit image

**[TEXT]** The imagined case contains four propositions:

1. a man was killed by poison;
2. the defendant was responsible;
3. inheritance supplied the motive;
4. witnesses and accomplices exist.

The Latin in Marx’s text is:

> *Rei totius memoriam saepe una nota et imagine simplici conprehendimus … aegrotum in lecto cubantem faciemus ipsum illum, de quo agetur … Et reum ad lectum eius adstituemus, dextera poculum, sinistra tabulas, medico testiculos arietinos tenentem: hoc modo et testium et hereditatis et veneno necati memoriam habere poterimus.* (III.33)

A close gloss is:

> “We often comprehend the memory of an entire matter by one mark and a single image … We shall make the person involved lie ill in bed … and station the defendant at his bedside, holding a cup in his right hand, tablets in his left, and ram’s testicles on the *medicus* finger. In this way we can retain the witnesses, the inheritance, and the person killed by poison.”

The decoding is:

- sick or dying man → poisoning and victim;
- defendant at the bed → accused agent;
- cup → poison;
- tablets → will or inheritance documents;
- testicles (*testiculi*) → witnesses (*testes*).

The scene compresses an entire legal allegation into a single enacted tableau.

### Which invalid?

**[TEXT/TEXTUAL PROBLEM]** If the practitioner knows the victim’s appearance, the victim should be represented directly. If not, the text says to substitute someone memorable. Caplan translates “a man of the lowest class,” while the Latin tradition around *non de minimo loco* is textually awkward. The principle is nevertheless clear: use a recognizable person rather than an indeterminate body.

### “Fourth finger,” “middle finger,” or “doctor”?

**[DISPUTED TEXT]** The phrase *medico testiculos arietinos tenentem* is difficult if punctuation and syntax are not supplied. *Digitus medicus* or *medicinalis* could designate a finger conventionally associated with medicine and the heart—commonly the fourth or ring finger in modern numbering. Caplan renders “on the fourth finger a ram’s testicles.” Some older readings and translations make the image more syntactically or anatomically bizarre.

The secure mnemonic is the pun *testiculi/testes*. The precise staging of the ram’s testicles is less secure.

### Is the ram Aries?

**[YATES/RECONSTRUCTION]** Yates tentatively wonders whether the ram’s testicles could hint at Aries and therefore at an astrological mnemonic tradition.

**[DISPUTED]** Nothing in the passage says Aries, zodiac, decan, star, or celestial influence. The straightforward verbal cue is *testiculi* → *testes*. An astrological interpretation is possible only as speculation and has no compelling textual necessity.

### Is the image “amoral”?

**[YATES]** Yates calls the classical images “completely amoral,” their function being to supply emotional force through oddity.

**[CORRECTION]** In this forensic example, the image is instrumentally mnemonic rather than a moral allegory. But the author’s list explicitly includes the *honestum* as well as the *turpe*, and the larger treatise concerns civic persuasion, praise, blame, virtue, and vice. “Not yet systematically moralized in the medieval Christian manner” is more defensible than “completely amoral.”

## 6. Worked example: memory for words

The author quotes a verse:

> *Iam domum itionem reges Atridae parant.*

“And now the kings, sons of Atreus, prepare their homecoming.”

The verse is probably a schoolroom or dramatic example; its original source is unidentified.

### First locus

**[TEXT]**

> *in loco constituere manus ad caelum tollentem Domitium, cum a Regibus Marciis loris caedatur*

“Place in the locus Domitius raising his hands to heaven while being beaten with thongs by the Marcii Reges.”

The names supply sound-cues:

- *Domitium* → *domum itionem*;
- *Reges* → *reges*.

The scene’s violence and action make it memorable.

### Second locus

**[TEXT]** Place the actors Aesopus and Cimber dressing to play Agamemnon and Menelaus in *Iphigenia*:

- the actors costumed as Agamemnon and Menelaus → *Atridae*, sons of Atreus;
- their theatrical preparation → *parant*, “they prepare.”

This is not one picture per orthographic word. It is a flexible series of personal-name puns, semantic representations, dramatic roles, and enacted predicates.

### Preliminary repetition

**[TEXT]** The verse is first repeated two or three times; only then are the verbal cues installed. Artificial memory stimulates and supports natural memory. It does not encode an entirely unknown line without prior acquaintance.

### Purpose

**[TEXT]** The author concedes that word-memory is laborious. It is included chiefly as difficult training to strengthen the more useful memory for things, not primarily so that students can recite poetry verbatim.

This sharply qualifies modern claims that the method guarantees effortless word-perfect recall.

## 7. Retrieval mechanics reconstructed

The full cycle can be reconstructed directly from the text:

1. **Prepare a route.** Learn a large sequence of distinct loci.
2. **Index it.** Mark every fifth locus, with a special tenth marker.
3. **Overlearn the places.** They must remain when particular images are erased.
4. **Analyze the speech.** Divide it into propositions or, for harder training, verbal segments.
5. **Invent personal cues.** Select likenesses meaningful to the individual user.
6. **Make them active.** Convert abstract material into sharply delineated scenes.
7. **Deposit sequentially.** One major image-complex or cue-group goes in each successive locus.
8. **Rehearse.** Traverse the route mentally and refresh the images.
9. **Deliver.** During speech, begin at the needed station and mentally inspect each locus.
10. **Erase and reuse.** Remove the temporary imagery while retaining the route.

**[RECONSTRUCTION]** The treatise does not give timings, capacity limits, a complete sample speech, procedures for erasing stubborn images, or a formal method for resolving cue collisions. These details in modern manuals are later inventions or practical extrapolations.

## 8. The rejected Greek stock-image method

**[TEXT]**

> *Scio plerosque Graecos, qui de memoria scripserunt, fecisse, ut multorum verborum imagines conscriberent … paratas haberent.*

“I know that most Greeks who wrote about memory arranged to list images corresponding to many words, so that learners might have them ready-made.”

The author rejects it because:

- words are effectively innumerable;
- even a thousand prepared images would be inadequate;
- ready-made lists discourage personal invention;
- an image striking to one person may be inconspicuous to another;
- the teacher’s duty is to impart a method, not exhaustive products.

### Were these shorthand signs?

**[YATES]** Yates suggests that the Greek images may have been shorthand *notae*, inwardly written on memory places; she calls the process a kind of “inner stenography.”

**[DISPUTED]** This is possible but unproved. The author calls mnemonic images *formae, notae, simulacra*, but *nota* can mean any identifying mark or cue. It does not by itself establish stenography. The surviving Greek shorthand manuals edited by H. J. M. Milne are valuable evidence for ancient shorthand systems, but no manuscript links their signaries directly to the rejected image catalogues of *Ad Herennium* III.38.

Quintilian XI.2.19 likewise speaks of marking material with a *signum* that recalls either a whole subject or a word. That supports cueing, not necessarily stenographic encoding.

### The lineage to *Ars notoria*

**[YATES]** Yates calls the medieval *Ars notoria* a “bastard descendant” of the *notae* branch.

**[CORRECTION]** Modern scholarship by Claire Fanger and Julien Véronèse treats the *Ars notoria* as a medieval theurgic ritual tradition, flourishing from the late twelfth or early thirteenth century, in which prayers, ritual disciplines, and complex figures called *notae* promise infused knowledge through angelic or divine assistance. Its *notae* have their own textual etymology and ritual function. Direct descent from the Ad Herennium’s Greek ready-made images has not been demonstrated. Similar vocabulary and a shared desire for rapid knowledge are insufficient to prove genealogy.

## The sources

## 1. The primary Latin text

The memory section is *Rhetorica ad Herennium* III.xvi.28–xxiv.40. The common reference systems should not be confused:

- Roman chapter divisions: III.xvi–xxiv;
- Arabic section divisions: §§28–40;
- Loeb pagination: approximately pp. 205–225.

### Standard editions

1. **Friedrich Marx, ed.**, *Incerti auctoris De ratione dicendi ad C. Herennium libri IV*, Leipzig: B. G. Teubner, 1894. This is the major critical edition, with extensive prolegomena and apparatus.

2. **Friedrich Marx, ed.**, *Rhetorica ad Herennium*, Leipzig: Teubner, 1923; reprinted under W. Trillitzsch, 1964. The *editio minor* supplied the Latin text reproduced by several digital sources.

3. **Harry Caplan, ed. and trans.**, *[Cicero] Ad C. Herennium de ratione dicendi*, Loeb Classical Library 403, Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press, 1954. Latin and English. Caplan relies substantially on Marx but supplies a valuable introduction, bibliography, notes, and manuscript stemma.

4. **Gualtiero Calboli, ed.**, *Cornifici Rhetorica ad C. Herennium*, Bologna: Pàtron, 1969; later revised work and commentary. The title preserves Calboli’s support for Cornifician attribution, which remains disputed.

5. **Guy Achard, ed. and trans.**, *Rhétorique à Herennius*, Collection des Universités de France, Paris: Les Belles Lettres, 1989/1990. Latin with French translation and notes.

6. **Thierry Fögen, ed. and trans.**, *Rhetorica ad Herennium*, Stuttgart: Franz Steiner, 1998, Latin-German edition and commentary.

### Translations

- Harry Caplan’s Loeb translation remains the standard readily accessible English version.
- Guy Achard supplies the standard Budé French edition.
- Italian, Spanish, Portuguese, and German translations exist, but textual decisions should be checked against Marx, Achard, Calboli, or Fögen rather than inferred from a modern translation.

The online LacusCurtius version reproduces Caplan’s Latin-English text and notes. The Latin Wikisource text identifies Marx 1923 as its edition, but it is not a substitute for a critical apparatus.

## 2. Authorship

### Cicero

**[TRADITION]** The work was attributed to Marcus Tullius Cicero in late antiquity and throughout the Middle Ages. As “Tullius,” its author acquired Cicero’s authority. The pairing with Cicero’s genuine youthful *De inventione* reinforced the attribution.

**[DOCUMENTED RECEPTION]** Medieval manuscripts call *De inventione* the *Rhetorica vetus*, *prima rhetorica*, or “First Rhetoric,” and *Ad Herennium* the *Rhetorica nova*, *secunda rhetorica*, or “Second Rhetoric.”

**[MODERN CONSENSUS]** Cicero did not write it. Differences in terminology, doctrine, examples, style, and political perspective, together with the uncertain relationship between it and *De inventione*, rule out confident Ciceronian authorship.

### Cornificius

**[DISPUTED]** Renaissance and modern scholars have proposed Q. Cornificius, partly because Quintilian attributes certain rhetorical terminology and examples to a Cornificius. Caplan argues that Quintilian’s Cornificius belongs later and that parallels are better explained by a shared rhetorical source. Calboli has been more receptive to Cornifician authorship. No manuscript colophon securely identifies Cornificius as author, and current reference works retain *auctor incertus*.

### Other candidates

Various Marian orators, teachers, and public figures have been proposed. None has attained consensus. “Anonymous Roman rhetoric teacher” describes the authorial persona and pedagogical character of the work; it is not a discovered biography.

### The addressee

**[TEXT]** The work is addressed to a Gaius Herennius.

**[DISPUTED]** Several men named Herennius are known from the period, but none can be securely identified as this addressee.

### Original title

**[DISPUTED]** *Rhetorica ad Herennium* is the conventional title. Caplan notes Marx’s plausible reconstruction *De ratione dicendi*, based on the author’s opening description, but the original title is unknown.

## 3. Dating

### Conventional date: 86–82 BCE

**[RECONSTRUCTION]** Caplan’s influential dating rests on internal historical allusions:

- the death of P. Sulpicius Rufus in 88 BCE gives a terminus after that event;
- reference to Marius’s seventh consulship points to 86 BCE;
- the jury composition reflected in an example appears pre-Sullan;
- no event securely later than the Sullan constitutional settlement appears.

Thus c. 86–82 BCE remains the common handbook date.

### Problems

**[DISPUTED]**

- Internal examples can have been collected earlier than final composition.
- A teaching manual may preserve deliberately archaic examples.
- Rhythmic-clausula analysis has been used to question the conventional window.
- The relative dating of *Ad Herennium* and Cicero’s *De inventione* remains uncertain.
- Similarities between them may come from a common Greek or Latin teaching source rather than direct borrowing in either direction.

Accordingly, “late 80s BCE” is safer than treating 86–82 as an archival fact.

## 4. Greek background

**[RECONSTRUCTION]** The treatise’s rhetorical doctrine is overwhelmingly Greek in ancestry but deliberately Romanized in terminology and examples. Caplan describes it as a synthesis of pre-Aristotelian, Peripatetic, Stoic, Hermagorean, and possibly Epicurean teaching.

No Greek source containing this exact memory chapter survives.

### Simonides

**[LEGEND/TEXTUAL TRADITION]** Cicero’s *De oratore* II.351–354 and Quintilian XI.2.11–16 tell the famous story: after a banquet hall collapsed, Simonides identified the mangled dead by remembering where each guest had sat. From this he supposedly discovered that orderly places preserve the order of things.

The story beautifully explains the method, but no contemporary source proves Simonides actually devised it. Yates appropriately says “invented, or codified.” “Simonides invented the memory palace” is tradition, not established biography.

### The *Dialexeis*

**[TEXT/RECONSTRUCTION]** The late fifth- or early fourth-century BCE *Dissoi Logoi* or *Dialexeis* includes brief advice on memory using attention, repetition, and associative linkage. It does not preserve the complete architectural system of *Ad Herennium*. It is evidence for an early Greek culture of trained memory, not proof of a direct textual chain.

### Aristotle

**[TEXT]** Aristotle’s *De memoria et reminiscentia* explains recollection through ordered associative search and starting points. It does not prescribe the Ad Herennium’s architectural image system.

**[MEDIEVAL SYNTHESIS]** Albertus Magnus and Thomas Aquinas united Aristotelian accounts of memory and reminiscence with “Tullius’s” practical rules. Yates credits Albert with making this fusion explicit. Later scholarship generally accepts the importance of the fusion but places it within a much broader monastic and scholastic memory culture.

### Cicero and Quintilian

- **Cicero, *De oratore* II.350–360 [TEXT]:** gives the Simonides narrative, places and images, the writing-tablet analogy, the importance of order, and examples of famous trained memories.
- **Quintilian, *Institutio oratoria* XI.2 [TEXT]:** explains the architectural itinerary clearly but doubts that elaborate systems are equally useful for continuous verbal discourse. He prefers division, attentive rehearsal, textual cues, and visualizing the written page in many circumstances.

Yates rightly treats these as the other two major Latin witnesses, but *Ad Herennium* is the fullest surviving ancient rule set.

## 5. Manuscript transmission

### Scale

**[MANUSCRIPT]** Ruth Taylor’s 1993 inventory counted 583 manuscripts from the ninth through the sixteenth centuries—far beyond the “more than one hundred” known or emphasized in older accounts. This is a significant correction to Caplan’s and Yates’s numerical impression.

### Principal early witnesses

Taylor identifies the oldest witnesses as members of the *Mutili* recension, characterized by loss of the opening five chapters and other lacunae:

- **Bern, Burgerbibliothek, MS 433**, second half of the ninth century;
- **Würzburg, Universitätsbibliothek, M. p. misc. f. 2**, dated approximately 842–855;
- **Paris, Bibliothèque nationale de France, lat. 7714**, third quarter of the ninth century;
- **St Petersburg, National Library of Russia, F.v.** — Taylor’s article gives the complete shelfmark in its manuscript list.

The absence of the beginning does not remove Book III’s memory chapter.

### *Mutili* and *Integri*

**[MANUSCRIPT]** Marx constructed a bipartite stemma:

- *Mutili* — early witnesses lacking the opening portion and other text;
- *Integri* — complete witnesses.

**[DISPUTED]** Taylor argues that the history behind the complete witnesses is more complicated than a simple late branch. The “complete” text may preserve material with a late-antique prehistory not adequately represented by Marx’s bipartite model. A printed stemma is a scholarly hypothesis, not a surviving ancient diagram.

### Association with *De inventione*

**[MANUSCRIPT/TRADITION]** By the twelfth century, manuscripts commonly paired the works. This physical and curricular association made the anonymous work Cicero’s “Second” or “New Rhetoric.”

### Vernacular transmission

**[TEXTUAL TRADITION]**

- The Italian *Fiore di rettorica* or *Rettorica nuova di Tullio*, associated with Guidotto da Bologna and Bono Giamboni, was composed before 1266 in an early form.
- Jean d’Antioche translated or adapted the Ciceronian rhetorical corpus into French in 1282.
- A Greek translation of the memory section survives; it has been attributed to Maximus Planudes or Theodore Gaza, but Caplan stresses that neither attribution rests on strong evidence.

Vernacular versions adapt and abridge. They must not automatically be cited as witnesses to the author’s original wording.

---

## 6. Early printings

### Editio princeps

**[PRINT]** Venice, Nicolas Jenson, 1470, edited by Ognibene/Omnibonus Leonicenus. It was printed under Ciceronian attribution and commonly joined to *De inventione*.

Bibliographical identifiers include:

- GW 6709;
- Goff C-672 for *Ad Herennium*;
- Hain 5057;
- BMC V 166;
- BSB-Ink A-817.

The Library of Congress holds a vellum copy in the Lessing J. Rosenwald Collection, no. 214. The Bibliothèque Mazarine digitizes **Inc. 18 A**. The Biblioteca Nacional de España records related Jenson holdings and later composite editions.

The Jenson book has:

- 68 leaves for *Ad Herennium*;
- 30 lines per page;
- Jenson’s Roman type;
- spaces and painted initials supplied by hand in individual copies.

### Later incunabula

Among the many subsequent editions:

- Venice, 1476;
- Italian *Rhetorica nova* printings in the 1470s;
- Venice, Piero Quarenghi and Troilo Zani, 15 April 1493, with commentary;
- Venice, Philippus Pincius, 1500;
- numerous sixteenth-century editions with commentaries by Francesco Maturanzio/Maturanti, Antonio Mancinelli, and others.

The printing history continued the medieval Ciceronian attribution long after philological doubts began to emerge.

---

## 7. Diagrams, tables, wheels, and plates

### In the ancient memory chapter

**[ABSENCE OF EVIDENCE]** No diagram, wheel, table, theatre plan, or illustrated image series is prescribed by the text. No authorial illustration survives. The system is explained verbally.

### In manuscripts

Some manuscripts contain decorated initials, marginal schemata, scholia, ownership marks, or commentary. These are witnesses to reading and teaching, not necessarily diagrams transmitted from the anonymous author.

### In editions

Caplan’s introduction includes a modern schematic stemma of manuscript relations. It is an editorial reconstruction, not a mnemonic diagram.

### Later illustrated arts

The following must be kept separate:

- **Lull’s lettered figures and rotating combinatorial devices:** thirteenth-century logical machinery, not Ad Herennium diagrams.
- **Publicius, Romberch, and Rosselli:** Renaissance printed mnemonic alphabets, architectural diagrams, and image inventories.
- **Camillo’s theatre:** a sevenfold encyclopedic theatre reconstructed chiefly from *L’Idea del Theatro* and reports.
- **Bruno’s wheels:** printed combinatorial and imagistic machinery mixing mnemonic, Lullian, astrological, and philosophical materials.
- **Fludd’s theatres:** elaborate engravings in *Utriusque cosmi historia*.

Their use of places, images, order, striking figures, and mental architecture shows reception or recombination. It does not prove that the Roman manual originally contained lost wheels.

## Yates’s account and scholarship since

## 1. What Yates got right

### The centrality of the text

**[YATES—CONFIRMED WITH QUALIFICATION]** Yates calls *Ad Herennium* the only complete surviving source for the classical artificial memory and the plan underlying the later *ars memorativa*. It is unquestionably the most detailed surviving ancient set of rules and was the most authoritative Latin source in the central and later Middle Ages.

The qualification is that later memory culture was never produced by this text alone. It also drew on:

- Aristotle and his commentators;
- Augustine;
- monastic meditation;
- biblical typology;
- preaching;
- manuscript layout and textual division;
- dialectic and commonplace traditions;
- Lullism;
- humanist rhetoric;
- medical psychology;
- later emblematic, combinatorial, and occult traditions.

### The reusable places/image distinction

Yates accurately foregrounds the wax-tablet analogy and the permanence of loci versus the erasability of images.

### Emotional distinctiveness

Her emphasis on active, shocking, comic, beautiful, ugly, and bloody images is directly supported by III.35–37.

### The incomplete gallery

Yates regrets that the author never supplies a complete sequence of images arranged through a building. That absence is real and methodologically significant. It results partly from the author’s insistence that effective images must be personally invented.

### Medieval authority

Yates correctly identifies the force of the attribution to “Tullius” and the curricular union of the “Old” and “New” Rhetorics.

---

## 2. Where Yates overreaches or requires revision

### “Every” later treatise follows the same plan

**[YATES—OVERSTATED]** Many Renaissance *artes memorativae* repeat rules for loci, images, memory for things, and memory for words. But the historical field also contains:

- monastic meditative architectures;
- diagrammatic and tabular systems;
- logical topical orders;
- alphabetic and numerical codes;
- phonetic systems;
- Lullian combinatorics;
- Ramist method;
- ritual arts promising infused knowledge.

These cannot all be reduced to the fourfold Ad Herennium plan.

### A single continuous “art”

**[CORRECTION]** Carruthers shows that medieval *memoria* was a culture of composition, ethical formation, reading, and invention—not merely the preservation of an antique technical recipe. Practices resembling loci and images operated within materially and institutionally different settings.

### Printing “destroyed” memory

**[YATES—REVISED]** Yates often dramatizes print as displacing the great medieval artificial memories, followed paradoxically by their occult Renaissance resurgence.

Bolzoni shows that print could disseminate and intensify the art. Printed books multiplied image repertories, emblematic structures, indexes, theatres, diagrams, and practical mnemonic manuals. The relation is transformation and remediation, not simple replacement.

### Images as purely interior pictures

**[CORRECTION]** Carruthers and Small caution against treating ancient and medieval mental imagery as if it were a modern private cinema. Image, mark, textual cue, gesture, diagram, page disposition, bodily motion, and affect could cooperate.

### Medieval churches and frescoes as memory systems

**[YATES—SUGGESTIVE BUT OFTEN UNPROVED]** Yates proposes that varied settings in Giotto, virtue-and-vice cycles, Dante’s ordered afterlife, and church sculpture may externalize mnemonic habits.

Later scholarship generally accepts that memorial habits illuminate such works. But proving that a particular fresco was designed as a technical sequence of Ad Herennium loci requires documentary or structural evidence. Visual order and memorable imagery alone do not prove formal mnemonic programming.

### The *notae*–shorthand theory

**[YATES—SPECULATIVE]** No direct evidence identifies the Greek ready-made images with the shorthand systems preserved in later papyri.

### The *Ars notoria* genealogy

**[YATES—UNDEMONSTRATED]** Fanger and Véronèse reconstruct it as a distinctive ritual and theurgic tradition. Yates’s genealogical metaphor should not be repeated as fact.

### Astrological Aries in the lawsuit image

**[YATES—SPECULATIVE]** The pun on witnesses is sufficient; astrology is not stated.

### Renaissance “Hermetic man”

**[YATES—MAJOR HISTORIOGRAPHICAL REVISION]** Yates’s works made Hermetic, magical, and mnemonic materials historically visible. But later Bruno and esotericism scholarship—Ciliberto, Sturlese, Gatti, Clucas, Hanegraaff, and others—rejects a single coherent “Hermetic tradition” as the master explanation. Renaissance authors mixed Platonism, Aristotelianism, Christianity, Lullism, astrology, magic, rhetoric, mathematics, and polemic in locally specific ways.

---

## 3. Carruthers

Mary Carruthers’s *The Book of Memory* (1990; revised 2008) and *The Craft of Thought* (1998) shift the question from “How was information stored?” to “How did trained memory make thought and composition possible?”

Her principal corrections are:

- memory was an inventive and compositional faculty;
- medieval reading was designed for internalization;
- vivid images were affective instruments;
- ethical judgment and prudence depended on an abundant, well-organized memory;
- architectural mnemonic practice belonged inside a broader craft of meditation and composition;
- books and memory were partners, not simple rivals.

This does not refute the mechanics Yates describes. It changes their cultural meaning.

---

## 4. Small

Jocelyn Penny Small’s *Wax Tablets of the Mind* (1997) situates mnemonic imagery within ancient cognition, literacy, note-taking, visual convention, and the material practices of writing. Her work resists romantic claims that ancient memory was a wholly oral, quasi-photographic power later destroyed by writing.

For *Ad Herennium*, the wax-tablet analogy should be taken as evidence of interaction between internal organization and literate technology.

---

## 5. Rossi

Paolo Rossi’s *Clavis universalis* first appeared in Italian in 1960, before Yates’s book, and in expanded forms; the English translation is *Logic and the Art of Memory: The Quest for a Universal Language*.

Rossi traces two partly independent traditions:

- classical memory by places and images;
- logical-combinatorial arts, especially Lullism.

Their Renaissance interweaving contributed to projects for encyclopedic order, universal language, and a “key” to knowledge. Rossi is a safeguard against treating Lull’s Art as simply an Ad Herennium memory wheel.

---

## 6. Bolzoni and Stagno

Lina Bolzoni’s work, including *La stanza della memoria* and *The Gallery of Memory*, reconstructs the intense traffic among:

- literary composition;
- images;
- emblems;
- galleries;
- collections;
- printed books;
- rhetorical memory;
- courtly performance.

She shows why printing did not merely extinguish mnemonic culture.

Scholarship by Bolzoni and Valeria Stagno also subjects Camillo’s theatre to closer textual and archival reconstruction. Yates’s imaginative plan remains influential, but no complete physical theatre or authoritative architect’s drawing survives. Reports, Camillo’s *Idea del Theatro*, and manuscripts must be kept distinct.

---

## 7. Hajdu

Helga Hajdu’s *Das mnemotechnische Schrifttum des Mittelalters* (1936) was one of Yates’s major guides to medieval mnemonic treatises. Its cataloguing work remains valuable, but the field has expanded enormously through manuscript study. Its narrative should not be treated as a complete inventory.

---

## 8. Ong and Ramus

Walter J. Ong’s *Ramus, Method, and the Decay of Dialogue* appeared in 1958 and was therefore not simply “post-Yates.” Ong reconstructs Ramist method as spatialized logical order: material is arranged through definitions and dichotomous division rather than populated with fantastic images.

**[HISTORICAL RELATION]** Ramism did not merely abolish memory. It relocated mnemonic force into methodical disposition, printed layout, branching classification, and repeated logical order.

Yates’s opposition between Ramist abstract order and Brunian imagistic memory is illuminating but polemically sharp. Actual classrooms and books could combine methods.

---

## 9. Lull scholarship

Anthony Bonner and Alexander Fidora have clarified that Ramon Llull’s *Ars* was designed as a demonstrative and missionary method grounded in divine dignities, relational principles, letter notation, and combinatorial figures.

**[CORRECTION]** Llull’s Art is not originally an Ad Herennium memory system. Later Lullists and Renaissance mnemonists used it mnemonically, and Bruno fused combinatorial and imagistic operations, but origin and later appropriation must be distinguished.

---

## 10. Bruno scholarship

Work by Michele Ciliberto, Rita Sturlese, Leen Spruit, Hilary Gatti, Stephen Clucas, Giovanni Aquilecchia, and others has:

- established better texts and chronologies;
- located memory books within Bruno’s philosophy of nature and cognition;
- complicated the idea of a single Hermetic key;
- distinguished Brunian mnemonics from ordinary loci practice;
- shown the importance of Lullian combinatorics without reducing Bruno to Llull;
- treated his magic as philosophically and polemically specific rather than a generic occult atmosphere.

Yates’s great achievement was to insist that Bruno’s memory books matter. Her weaker claim is that “Hermeticism” alone explains them.

---

## History and context

## 1. Greek and Roman rhetoric

The system belongs first to professional rhetorical pedagogy. A speaker needed to:

- retain a planned argument;
- preserve order;
- adapt delivery;
- speak without continuous dependence on a written script;
- recover after interruption;
- move among proofs and rebuttals.

The legal example reflects the prominence of forensic rhetoric. The named actors and aristocratic families in the word-memory example presume a socially situated Roman student with knowledge of public personalities and theater.

The art is therefore personal but not culturally neutral. What counts as an instantly recognizable figure depends on the user’s society.

---

## 2. Late antiquity

Jerome cites the work as Tullian. Martianus Capella gives a compressed account of places and images within his allegorical encyclopedia. Julius Victor mentions the technique skeptically, judging observations about loci and images of little use.

**[CORRECTION TO SIMPLE CONTINUITY]** Late-antique rhetorical education could transmit mnemonic practice independently of direct reading of *Ad Herennium*. Textual transmission and living pedagogical tradition are not identical.

---

## 3. Carolingian and monastic settings

Lupus of Ferrières knew the work in the ninth century. Yates contrasts this with Alcuin’s dialogue on rhetoric, which gives Charlemagne general health advice rather than the full art of loci and images.

Monasteries preserved manuscripts, but monastic memory practices were not simply dormant classical rhetoric. Psalmody, lectio divina, biblical concordance, liturgical repetition, and meditative composition created their own ordered memory culture.

---

## 4. Twelfth- and thirteenth-century revival

The *Ad Herennium* became increasingly important after about 1100 and eventually rivaled or surpassed *De inventione* as a practical rhetorical authority. It was useful because, unlike *De inventione*, it covered delivery, memory, and style as well as invention.

Institutional settings included:

- cathedral and monastic schools;
- schools of *ars dictaminis*;
- universities;
- Dominican and Franciscan study;
- preaching schools;
- legal and notarial education.

---

## 5. Scholastic psychology and prudence

Cicero’s *De inventione* defined memory, intelligence, and foresight as parts of prudence. The pseudo-Ciceronian “Second Rhetoric” supplied rules for storing things. The pairing encouraged a powerful medieval synthesis:

- memory was not merely rote retention;
- a rightly furnished memory supplied cases for judgment;
- images of virtues, vices, rewards, and punishments could direct conduct;
- recollection became part of prudential action.

Albertus Magnus and Thomas Aquinas integrated Ad Herennium rules with Aristotle’s psychology. Their endorsement gave artificial memory moral and scholastic legitimacy.

---

## 6. Preaching and the mendicant orders

Dominican and Franciscan preachers required large stores of:

- biblical passages;
- distinctions;
- exempla;
- moral classifications;
- authorities;
- sermon divisions.

Orders of vivid images, alphabetic schemes, trees, towers, biblical buildings, bodily figures, and inscriptions assisted retrieval and composition.

**[CAUTION]** It is rarely possible to prove that a surviving sermon was delivered by mentally walking through a particular building. Treatises and explicit testimony establish availability; individual performance often remains reconstruction.

---

## 7. Humanism and the courts

Humanists returned to Cicero and Quintilian but did not simply discard medieval technique. Memory teachers offered systems at courts and universities, often advertising prodigious capacity.

Peter of Ravenna’s *Phoenix sive artificiosa memoria* (Venice, 1491) became a major printed manual. He promoted erotic or personally affecting images, boasted of vast legal and textual memory, and adapted the lawsuit tradition.

Courtly patronage rewarded systems that promised:

- fluent oratory;
- rapid learning;
- command of quotations;
- encyclopedic display;
- political and social prestige.

---

## 8. Renaissance theatres, wheels, and encyclopedias

### Camillo’s grades

Giulio Camillo’s *L’Idea del Theatro*, published posthumously in 1550, describes a theatre organized through seven vertical divisions and seven grades. The user stands where the stage would normally be and looks toward an amphitheatrical arrangement containing the ordered universe.

**[TEXT]** Camillo contrasts the “frail places” in which ancient orators temporarily stored speeches with “eternal places” suited to the eternal nature of things.

**[RECONSTRUCTION]** Yates reconstructs the seven grades through planetary, mythological, cabalistic, and cosmological associations. No fully authoritative diagram of the completed theatre survives. Modern images presenting a single exact Camillo plan are reconstructions.

Camillo’s system preserves Ad Herennium principles—ordered places, images, differentiated stations—but changes their object from a particular speech to encyclopedic and cosmic knowledge.

### Bruno’s wheels

Bruno’s *De umbris idearum* (Paris, 1582) prints combinatorial wheels whose concentric rings carry letters and whose accompanying lists coordinate celestial, animal, vegetal, mineral, human, technical, and linguistic imagery.

**[TEXT]** The figures are printed components of Bruno’s own system.

**[HISTORICAL SYNTHESIS]** They combine:

- Lullian letter-combination;
- Ad Herennium-like image placement;
- astrological and mythological repertories;
- Bruno’s theory of shadows, bonds, imagination, and universal relations.

The wheels are not latent diagrams recovered from the Roman text.

---

## 9. Ramism

Ramus reduces rhetoric largely to style and delivery and transfers invention and judgment to dialectic. Memory becomes the consequence of correct logical arrangement and repeated method.

The Ramist tree is an external, printed, classificatory technology. It competes with active imagery but shares the conviction that order makes knowledge retrievable.

---

## 10. Universal language and combinatorics

Rossi traces a long convergence:

1. rhetorical places and signs;
2. Lullian letters and combinations;
3. Renaissance encyclopedic theatres;
4. universal-character projects;
5. Leibniz’s *characteristica universalis* and *calculus ratiocinator*.

**[CAUTION]** “Lull invented the computer” and “the memory palace became RAM” are modern genealogical slogans. Lull and Leibniz genuinely belong to histories of formal combination and symbolic calculation. That does not establish a direct engineering lineage to electronic computing.

---

## People

## 1. The anonymous teacher

Everything biographical is inferred from the work:

- Roman;
- rhetorically trained;
- familiar with Greek teaching;
- concerned to Latinize it;
- pedagogically practical;
- probably sympathetic to Marian or popularis examples, though the author’s precise politics remain disputed.

Calling him a “professor” is a convenient modern analogy, not his documented office.

## 2. Gaius Herennius

The named recipient, otherwise unidentified.

## 3. Cicero

Not the author, but the most consequential attributed author. His genuine *De oratore* independently transmits the method, and medieval belief that “Tullius” wrote both rhetorics magnified the authority of the artificial memory.

## 4. Quintilian

A critical transmitter. He acknowledges that places can cue objects and examples but doubts the usefulness of excessive image-making for connected discourse.

### The auctioneer

**[TEXT]** Quintilian XI.2.24 describes an auctioneer who can remember what has been sold by mentally attaching the objects to places. It is a practical example of loci used for a sequence of concrete things.

### Yates’s professor at parties

**[YATES/ANECDOTE]** Yates says she had been told of a professor who asked students at parties to name objects and later astonished them by reciting the list. He had placed the objects on the windowsill, desk, wastebasket, and other room locations.

No name, date, institution, or independent witness is supplied. It is a modern anecdote illustrating that the method works for ordered object lists, not evidence about Roman practice.

## 5. Albertus Magnus and Thomas Aquinas

They transformed the authority of “Tullius” by integrating the art with Aristotelian recollection and prudence. Later biographies celebrate Thomas’s extraordinary memory.

Claims that Thomas remembered everything he ever read are hagiographical testimony, not experimentally verifiable fact.

## 6. Peter of Ravenna

A jurist and professional mnemonist who claimed enormous feats, including thousands of legal points, hundreds of speeches, and extensive canon law.

**[TRADITION/SELF-ADVERTISEMENT]** Many feats come from his own promotional writing. His real legal training and influence are documented; the exact capacities cannot be independently verified.

## 7. Giulio Camillo

Patronized by Francis I of France and sought by elite admirers. Contemporary reports say he constructed a wooden theatre in Venice and allowed selected visitors inside.

The often-retold story that a lion approached Camillo and then submitted or withdrew is found in Giuseppe Betussi’s dialogues of 1544. It is a contemporary literary anecdote, not proof of magical power.

## 8. Giordano Bruno and Giovanni Mocenigo

Mocenigo invited Bruno to Venice to teach him memory and invention. Dissatisfied, he confined and denounced Bruno in May 1592.

**[DOCUMENTED]** Inquisition testimony links their conflict to promised memory instruction.

**[CORRECTION]** Bruno was not executed simply “for teaching the memory palace.” The surviving trial materials involve a wide range of theological, cosmological, sacramental, and magical accusations. The final complete trial dossier is lost, so any single-cause account is unreliable.

## 9. Lambert Schenkel

Schenkel taught memory publicly in late sixteenth-century Europe and was accused of sorcery at Louvain, yet secured approvals elsewhere, including at Douai. Stories that he or his students lost their natural memory through mnemonic practice belong to polemical literature about artificial memory; individual versions require checking against the relevant pamphlet or testimonial.

## 10. Alleged students who lost memory

**[OPEN/LEGEND]** Stories that an artificial-memory student lost or damaged his natural memory circulate in early modern criticism and later histories. No such case occurs in *Ad Herennium*. Without a named person and a traceable early text, the catalogue lead should be classified as an unverified cautionary anecdote, not a medical event.

## The magic question

## 1. Was the Ad Herennium system magical?

**[TEXT: NO]** Nothing in III.28–40 invokes:

- gods;
- angels;
- demons;
- celestial powers;
- talismans;
- ritual purity;
- incantations;
- planetary hours;
- infused knowledge.

The method is explained through natural memory, attention, resemblance, emotion, order, rehearsal, and disciplined practice. In its source context it is a rhetorical technique.

The fictional construction of places is an act of imagination, not the creation of an astral temple.

## 2. Was it morally suspect?

The ancient author does not treat bizarre or obscene imagery as religiously dangerous. Its purpose is memorability.

Later Christian authors faced a different question: can deliberately shocking, violent, comic, or erotic images be morally safe? Answers varied. Some moralized imagery toward virtues, vices, heaven, hell, and Scripture. Others warned that lustful or impious images could corrupt the imagination.

## 3. The *Ars notoria*

The *Ars notoria* was repeatedly criticized or condemned because it promised accelerated or infused knowledge through prayers, ritual observance, and angelic figures.

Thomas Aquinas rejects occult procedures that seek knowledge through improper spiritual powers, while endorsing natural artificial memory. The distinction matters:

- Ad Herennium memory: natural faculty trained by human art;
- *Ars notoria*: alleged supernaturally infused knowledge through ritual mediation.

Yates’s proposed descent from mnemonic *notae* is conjectural.

## 4. Renaissance talismanic and astral memory

Camillo, Bruno, and Fludd placed classical mnemonic devices in cosmological frameworks. In some systems, images did more than remind: they were supposed to conform the psyche to cosmic order or make celestial forces mentally operative.

**[YATES]** This is the center of her interpretation of “occult memory.”

**[CORRECTION]** Not all Renaissance imagery was talismanic; not all astrology implied demonic magic; and not all memory wheels performed the same operation. Each author’s textual claims must be examined separately.

## 5. Bruno and the Inquisition

Bruno’s reputation for memory helped bring him to Mocenigo and thus contributed causally to his arrest. But the claim “Bruno was burned for his memory system” is false or, at minimum, grossly misleading. Memory teaching was the occasion of the fatal patronage relationship, not the sole doctrinal charge.

## 6. Enemies and critics

Criticism came from several directions:

- Quintilian: practical skepticism about over-elaboration;
- humanists such as Erasmus: suspicion of burdensome or magical shortcuts;
- Ramists: preference for dialectical order;
- Protestant polemicists such as William Perkins: attacks on impious, astrological, or “Scepsian” memory;
- Catholic authorities: opposition when memory arts crossed into prohibited magic or angelic ritual;
- later empiricists and educators: charges of artificiality, nonsense, and injury to understanding.

The target was not always the same “art.”

---

## Afterlife

## 1. Dante

**[YATES/RECONSTRUCTION]** Dante’s ordered otherworld, moral classifications, memorable punishments, and differentiated landscapes are compatible with a culture trained in mnemonic disposition. Dante explicitly knew the language of the *prima rhetorica*.

**[CAUTION]** No text by Dante says that the *Commedia* is a formal Ad Herennium memory palace. The relationship is contextual and structural, not demonstrated as a technical blueprint.

## 2. Frescoes and churches

Virtue-and-vice cycles and distinct architectural settings could assist communal and individual recollection.

**[DISPUTED]** Yates sometimes moves too quickly from “memorable ordered imagery” to “artificial-memory system.” Iconography, liturgy, preaching, pilgrimage, and architectural function provide alternative or complementary explanations.

## 3. Emblem books

Renaissance emblems combine image, motto, and explanatory text in compact memorable units. They share the mnemonic virtues of brevity, peculiarity, and semantic compression. Direct borrowing from *Ad Herennium* varies by author and cannot be assumed from formal resemblance alone.

## 4. Literature

### Borges’s “Funes the Memorious”

**[MODERN ANALOGY]** Funes possesses uncontrollable, nearly total particular memory. He cannot readily abstract or generalize. This is almost the inverse of the disciplined Ad Herennium art, which selects salient cues and compresses a whole matter into a single usable image.

### Umberto Eco

Eco’s writings on signs, encyclopedias, lists, universal languages, and combinatorial schemes engage the Lull–Renaissance–Leibniz tradition. His *The Search for the Perfect Language* is particularly relevant. These are intellectual afterlives, not direct continuations of Roman rhetorical training.

## 5. The “memory palace” revival

Modern manuals commonly retain:

- a familiar route;
- stable loci;
- striking imagery;
- sequential retrieval;
- reuse of locations.

They often omit:

- rhetorical theory;
- the distinction between *res* and *verba*;
- the civic and forensic purpose;
- daily disciplined training;
- the recommendation that images be individually invented.

The phrase “memory palace” has become a broad popular label. It can obscure the ancient text’s allowance for streets, arches, corners, intercolumnar spaces, and invented landscapes.

## 6. Memory athletes

Modern competitors combine loci with:

- person-action-object systems;
- number-shape and number-rhyme codes;
- phonetic major systems;
- card and binary-image vocabularies;
- preconstructed routes.

The active, bizarre composite images strongly resemble the Ad Herennium principle. Fixed universal image dictionaries, however, resemble the Greek stock method the author rejects—though athletes personalize them through practice.

## 7. Experimental psychology and neuroscience

Modern studies generally find that method-of-loci training improves ordered recall. Neuroimaging studies associate expert mnemonic performance with spatial-navigation and imagery networks.

A recent systematic review and meta-analysis concludes that the method can be effective, while rating much of the comparative evidence low or very low in certainty because of small samples, heterogeneity, and risk of bias.

This supports the practical principle, not ancient theories about an “inner eye” in their original physiological form.

## 8. Computing

Useful analogies include:

- loci as addresses;
- images as mutable contents;
- fifth-place marks as indexing aids;
- traversal as retrieval;
- erasure and reuse as storage management.

**[MODERN INVENTION]** These are retrospective analogies. Roman practitioners did not invent computer RAM. Nor is electronic random access equivalent to an internally rehearsed route, even though the route can be entered from multiple points.

## 9. Lull to Leibniz to the machine

A defensible historical sequence is:

- Llull devised formal lettered combinations for demonstrative and missionary reasoning;
- Renaissance thinkers merged combinatorics with encyclopedic and mnemonic schemes;
- seventeenth-century authors sought universal characters and calculable reasoning;
- Leibniz explicitly engaged Lullian combination and universal-character projects;
- later historians recognize these projects as part of the prehistory of symbolic computation.

The direct contribution of the Ad Herennium is the durable idea that knowledge can be assigned to stable positions and manipulated through signs. It is one ancestor among several, not a solitary origin.

## Controversies and disputes

## 1. “The oldest complete art of memory”

**Status:** substantially correct, with qualification.

It is the fullest surviving ancient prescriptive account. It may not be the first written account, and it depends on lost Greek teaching.

## 2. Simonides as inventor

**Status:** ancient foundation legend.

Carried by Cicero, Quintilian, and later writers. No contemporary documentation survives.

## 3. Cicero as author

**Status:** late-antique and medieval tradition; rejected by modern scholarship.

Spread by manuscript attribution, curricular pairing, vernacular adaptation, and early printing.

## 4. Cornificius as author

**Status:** disputed scholarly hypothesis.

Supported through parallels with material Quintilian assigns to Cornificius; opposed through chronology and common-source explanations.

## 5. Exact date 86–82 BCE

**Status:** strong conventional reconstruction, not certainty.

Derived from historical allusions and silence; questioned by composition history and clausulae.

## 6. Dependence between *Ad Herennium* and *De inventione*

**Status:** unresolved.

Shared dependence on a common source is widely favored. Direct borrowing in either direction has not been demonstrated conclusively.

## 7. A Rhodian source

**Status:** reconstruction.

Caplan and earlier scholarship infer a Greek, possibly Rhodian, rhetorical synthesis. No source manuscript survives.

## 8. The lawsuit’s finger

**Status:** textual and translational difficulty.

The image’s cup, tablets, and *testiculi/testes* pun are secure; exact anatomical placement is less so.

## 9. Aries

**Status:** Yatesian speculation.

No explicit astrological wording.

## 10. Greek stock images as shorthand

**Status:** plausible but unproved conjecture.

Ancient shorthand existed; direct identity with mnemonic catalogues is unsupported.

## 11. *Ars notoria* as descendant

**Status:** unproved genealogy.

Modern ritual-magic scholarship reconstructs a distinct medieval textual tradition.

## 12. “Imagines agentes” as technical occult entities

**Status:** modern reification.

The phrase means images doing something. Later magical image theories may intensify it, but the ancient grammar is ordinary.

## 13. The system as “amoral”

**Status:** Yates’s interpretive contrast.

The rules are instrumentally mnemonic; their larger rhetorical setting is not devoid of ethical content.

## 14. Medieval art as an external memory palace

**Status:** sometimes illuminating, rarely proven wholesale.

Individual programs require individual evidence.

## 15. Printing killed memory

**Status:** overturned as a simple causal thesis.

Print changed mnemonic practice and simultaneously disseminated it.

## 16. Camillo built the exact theatre shown in modern diagrams

**Status:** false certainty.

A physical structure is contemporaneously reported, but its complete form is unrecoverable. Most circulating diagrams are scholarly or popular reconstructions.

## 17. Bruno’s wheels are Ad Herennium wheels

**Status:** false.

They are Brunian syntheses with Lullian and other sources.

## 18. Bruno died for teaching memory

**Status:** legend or polemical simplification.

Memory teaching drew him to Mocenigo; his prosecution concerned numerous alleged heresies and magical practices.

## 19. The system produces photographic memory

**Status:** modern marketing invention.

The ancient source demands rehearsal, cue creation, and labor.

## 20. Memory athletes directly preserve an unbroken lineage

**Status:** generally unproved.

There are genuine textual revivals and rediscoveries, but modern competitive systems also arose from nineteenth- and twentieth-century pedagogical, phonetic, and performance traditions.

---

## Open questions

1. What exact Greek handbook or oral school tradition underlies III.28–40?

2. Does the author translate a single source or synthesize several mnemonic doctrines?

3. What is the original wording at the corrupt or difficult points in the lawsuit image?

4. How precisely should *breviter* be understood in the definition of loci: small-scale, bounded, concise, or quickly apprehensible?

5. How much practical Roman use can be inferred from a prescriptive textbook?

6. Were the Greek stock images pictorial scenes, written signs, shorthand symbols, syllabic cues, or a mixture?

7. What is the exact history of the Greek translation of the memory chapter, and was Planudes, Gaza, or neither responsible?

8. Can the complete-witness tradition be pushed securely into late antiquity, as Taylor proposes?

9. Which medieval manuscripts contain significant memory glosses, and how do their readings vary geographically and institutionally?

10. When and where did *solitudo* become *sollicitudo* or acquire attention-based glossing?

11. Which claimed medieval visual programs can be documented as consciously mnemonic rather than merely ordered and memorable?

12. How widely did actual students practice reverse or mid-route retrieval?

13. Did teachers normally reuse a single route or maintain multiple specialized routes?

14. How were unwanted or intrusive images deliberately removed?

15. Can modern experimental work separate the effects of spatial order, bizarreness, emotional arousal, embodiment, and rehearsal?

16. How much of the modern “memory palace” descends textually from early modern manuals, and how much was independently reinvented?

---

## Conclusions

The Ad Herennium artificial memory is a disciplined rhetoric of internal placement. Its machinery is austere:

- a permanently learned architecture;
- an exact sequence;
- numbered checkpoints;
- temporary, personally meaningful images;
- action, peculiarity, and emotion;
- repeated traversal;
- retrieval as inward reading.

Its historical power came from adaptability. Medieval scholastics made it serve prudence; preachers made it serve Scripture and moral instruction; humanists made it serve courtly and forensic display; Renaissance encyclopedists joined it to theatres and galleries; Bruno joined mnemonic imagery to combinatorial philosophy; Ramists relocated memory into diagrammatic method; later teachers turned it into commercial mnemonics; memory athletes turned it into high-speed encoding.

The ancient system itself was not magical. Its later descendants and neighbors could be devotional, cosmological, astrological, theurgic, polemical, or magical. Confusing those later formations with the Roman source is the central historical error to avoid.

Yates remains indispensable because she recovered the scale of this afterlife and made its imagery intellectually serious. Later scholarship has replaced her single dramatic arc with a denser history of manuscripts, institutions, reading practices, mixed methods, and local transformations. Her map opened the territory; it is no longer the territory’s final survey.
