# Albertus Magnus’s *ars memorandi*: Research Dossier

## Basic identification

| Field | Identification |
|---|---|
| **Name** | Albertus Magnus’s *ars memorandi*—not an independent handbook, but a scholastic examination and endorsement of the artificial memory attributed to “Tullius” |
| **Exponent** | Albertus Magnus / Albert the Great, O.P. (ca. 1200–1280) |

### Evidentiary synopsis

- **Documented text:** Albert explicitly examines “*ars memorandi quam Tullius tradit in Secunda Rhetorica*”—the art taught by Tullius in the “Second Rhetoric,” meaning the anonymous *Rhetorica ad Herennium*.
- **Documented text:** He accepts the classical division into natural and artificial memory, places and images, memory for things and memory for words, and the rules for choosing places and striking images.
- **Documented text:** He reclassifies the technique as *reminiscentia*, rationally directed recollection, and therefore as pertinent to prudence.
- **Yates’s interpretation:** Albert was the first to conflate Aristotle’s analysis of recollection with the pseudo-Ciceronian architectural mnemonic.
- **Scholarly correction:** Albert’s discussion is unusually early and systematic, but “first” is unsafe without qualification. Earlier Latin pedagogical and rhetorical traditions—Hugh of Saint-Victor, John of Garland, Boncompagno da Signa, monastic ordering practices, and glosses on the rhetorical corpus—had already related ordered recall to intellectual or moral formation. Albert’s distinctive achievement is the explicit scholastic reconciliation of the *Ad Herennium* with Aristotelian faculty psychology.
- **Negative finding:** Albert supplies no wheel, table, alphabetic key, combinatorial diagram, or illustrated plate. Those belong to other memory systems. No surviving diagram can securely be called “Albertus Magnus’s memory diagram.”
- **Negative finding:** There is no evidence that Albert himself called the ram an image of Aries, treated the mnemonic as talismanic, or prescribed astral images for memory.

## The system in detail

## 1. What kind of “system” is this?

Albert did not invent an entirely new apparatus. He transmitted, interrogated, and philosophically naturalized the classical architectural mnemonic.

Its principal ingredients came from four textual complexes:

1. Cicero’s *De inventione* II.53.160 supplied the moral classification of memory as a part of prudence.
2. The anonymous *Rhetorica ad Herennium* III.16–24 supplied artificial memory, places, images, memory for things, memory for words, and the spectacular-image rules.
3. Aristotle’s *De memoria et reminiscentia*, especially 451b–453a, supplied recollection as an active search beginning from an ordered starting point.
4. Avicenna, Averroes, and the Latin faculty-psychology tradition supplied the internal senses: common sense, imagination, estimation, and memory.

Albert’s own contribution is the synthesis. A technique inherited under rhetoric becomes explicable through psychology and defensible under ethics.

This is why “Albertus’s *ars memorandi*” should be understood as shorthand for an Albertine theory and ratification of an existing art, not as the title of an independently circulating manual.

## 2. The scholastic structure of *De bono* IV, q. 2

The question contains two articles.

### Article 1: What memory is

Albert begins from Cicero:

> *Prudentia est rerum bonarum et malarum neutrarumque scientia. Partes eius: memoria, intelligentia, providentia. Memoria est, per quam animus repetit illa quae fuerunt…*

“Prudence is knowledge of things good, bad, and indifferent. Its parts are memory, understanding, and foresight. Memory is that through which the mind recalls what has been.”  
—Cicero, *De inventione* II.53.160.

Albert must answer an apparent category error:

- memory seems to be a sensitive cognitive power;
- prudence is a rational and moral virtue;
- therefore memory cannot simply be a “part” of prudence.

His answer is functional. Mere retention belongs to sensitive memory; purposive recovery of past particulars for present deliberation belongs to rational recollection. Memory becomes part of prudence insofar as it enters the activity of *reminiscentia*.

The governing formulation is:

> *Dicimus, quod memoria est pars prudentiae, secundum quod memoria cadit in rationem reminiscentiae.*

“We say that memory is a part of prudence insofar as memory enters into the character of recollection.”  
—Albertus, *De bono* IV, q. 2, art. 1, sol.; Editio Coloniensis XXVIII, pp. 245–246.

Albert further distinguishes memory as a cognitive disposition from memory as a moral disposition. Remembered particulars make experience possible; experience informs deliberation; prudence must judge actions, and actions occur in particulars rather than universals alone.

### Article 2: The art taught by “Tullius”

Article 2 puts the art on trial through twenty-one numbered objections or problems and corresponding solutions. Albert quotes or paraphrases the *Ad Herennium*, raises philosophical and practical objections, and then vindicates the rules.

His initial classification is decisive:

> *Quod autem dicit de memoria artificiali, quae inductione et rationali praecepto confirmatur … non est memoriae, sed reminiscentiae, sicut dicit Philosophus in libro De memoria et reminiscentia.*

“What he says about artificial memory, which is established by induction and rational precept … belongs not [simply] to memory but to recollection, as the Philosopher says in the book *On Memory and Recollection*.”  
—*De bono* IV, q. 2, art. 2; ed. Col. XXVIII, p. 246.

**Documented text:** artificial memory is an acquired habit, governed by reason and deliberate practice.

**Yates’s formulation:** “So far as I know, Albertus was the first to make this conflation.”

**Safer reconstruction:** Albert provides the earliest presently famous, fully explicit scholastic harmonization of the pseudo-Ciceronian art with Aristotle’s account of recollective search. Priority over every lost, glossed, or unedited classroom treatment has not been demonstrated.

## 3. What serves as places?

The classical answer, accepted by Albert, is *loci*: stable, distinctly apprehended backgrounds into which temporary images are placed.

Albert’s *De bono* recognizes both:

- a natural place, such as a field;
- an artificial or humanly made place, such as a building.

The text’s terminology distinguishes what is memorable *natura* and *manu*. Albert explains the latter as something constructed, not as an imaginary geometry.

### The five rules for places

Albert reproduces and confirms the five chief Herenian rules:

1. **Choose quiet or solitary places.** Crowds and traffic disturb the concentration required to imprint the sequence.
2. **Keep the places dissimilar.** Repeated identical arches or intercolumniations produce confusion.
3. **Keep each place moderate in size.** It must be graspable as a distinct unit, neither too large nor too small.
4. **Use moderate illumination.** Excessive brightness dazzles; excessive darkness conceals.
5. **Separate places by a moderate interval.** The inherited recommendation is approximately thirty feet.

The point is not physical measurement for its own sake. Each locus must remain perceptually discriminable from its neighbors.

### Real versus imaginary places

Albert puzzles over the description of a good locus as both *brevis* and *perfectus*. His answer is that “brief” means cognitively bounded: it must not stretch the mind through enormous imagined spaces such as a whole camp or city.

Yates infers from this that Albert recommends only real places memorized through direct experience.

That conclusion needs qualification:

- **Documented:** Albert rejects expansive imagined camps and cities as mentally distending.
- **Documented:** he accepts fields, temples, hospitals, and buildings as possible place systems, chosen according to what most affects the individual.
- **Scholarly reconstruction:** his safest model is a bounded, previously apprehended environment.
- **Overstatement:** the text does not establish a universal ban on every invented locus. Albert’s category *loca imaginabilia* includes places as present in imagination, whether their initial form entered through sense or was subsequently recomposed.
- **Yates’s speculation:** because Albert calls “solemn and rare” places most moving, perhaps he had a church in mind.
- **Evidence:** none beyond the suitability of a church as one possible solemn, distinctive building. Albert does not prescribe “use a church.”

The key individualizing qualification is that one person may be most moved by a field, another by a temple, another by a hospital. The physical kind of place can vary; the formal five rules remain.

## 4. What serves as images?

The images are corporeal similitudes placed within the ordered backgrounds.

Albert retains the classical distinction:

- *memoria rerum*: images stand for things, topics, arguments, or conceptual contents;
- *memoria verborum*: images cue individual words or verbal sequences.

He regards memory for words as possible but cumbersome and vulnerable to obscurity. His deepest interest lies in images capable of carrying intelligible and moral *intentiones*.

### Image, form, and *intentio*

In Avicennian faculty psychology, the sheep perceives not only the visible form of a wolf but its non-sensible *intentio*—dangerousness, hostility, something-to-flee. The estimative faculty apprehends that intention; memory retains it.

Albert asks whether artificial memory therefore requires:

- one image to represent the sensible form, and
- another to represent the *intentio*.

His answer is no. The mnemonic image carries its intention within it.

The wolf is Albert’s genuine example elsewhere in his psychology. The oft-repeated example “an image of Justice contains the intention to seek Justice” is not Albert’s worked example.

- **Documented:** the memory image can include an *intentio*.
- **Documented:** Albert illustrates animal estimation with the sheep or lamb’s apprehension of the wolf.
- **Yates’s stated deduction:** a rational image of Justice might contain an intention to acquire Justice.
- **Catalogue distortion:** turning Yates’s hypothetical Justice example into Albert’s own wording.

This is an important correction because Yates herself explicitly marked the Justice example as her deduction.

## 5. The rules for images

Albert accepts images that are:

- concrete and corporeal;
- distinct rather than vague;
- few enough to avoid confusion;
- emotionally or perceptually forceful;
- extraordinary, beautiful, ugly, bloody, comic, obscene, or otherwise unusual;
- metaphorically capable of compressing several related matters into one scene.

The source behind him states:

> *Id accidet, si quam maxime notatas similitudines constituemus; si non multas nec vagas, sed aliquid agentes imagines ponemus; si egregiam pulchritudinem aut unicam turpitudinem eis attribuemus … aut si qua re deformabimus, ut si cruentam aut caeno oblitam aut rubrica delibutam inducamus … aut ridiculas res aliquas imaginibus attribuamus.*

“This will happen if we establish likenesses as striking as possible; if we set up images that are neither numerous nor vague but active; if we give them exceptional beauty or singular ugliness … or disfigure them, making one bloody, smeared with mud, or daubed with red pigment … or give them something ridiculous.”  
—*Rhetorica ad Herennium* III.22.37.

Albert imports these *imagines agentes* into a scholastic treatise on prudence. Yates memorably writes that they “stroll mysteriously, like players, out of antiquity into the scholastic treatise on memory as a part of Prudence.”

Albert’s philosophical defense is:

> *Propria magis certificent de re, tamen metaphorica plus movent animam et ideo plus conferunt memoriae.*

“Although literal expressions give more precise information about the thing, metaphorical ones move the soul more, and therefore contribute more to memory.”  
—*De bono* IV, q. 2, art. 2, solutions to points 16–18; ed. Col. XXVIII, p. 251.

He adds that the marvelous is more moving than the customary and invokes Aristotle’s observation that wonder stands near the beginning of philosophy and that myth is composed of wonders.

This does not mean that the image magically transmits knowledge. It means that affective salience strengthens retention and supplies a route of recollective search.

## 6. Fixing order and retrieving content

Albert does not give a new numbered architectural itinerary. He accepts the classical mechanism and supplies its Aristotelian rationale.

### Encoding

1. Select a stable sequence of distinguishable loci.
2. Know their order in advance.
3. Translate what is to be retained into one or more corporeal similitudes.
4. Make those images conspicuous, active, and personally affecting.
5. Place each image in its assigned locus.
6. Rehearse the locus sequence.

### Retrieval

1. Begin from a determinate starting point.
2. Mentally traverse the places in their established succession.
3. Perceive the image assigned to each place.
4. Move from the image’s sensible form to its associated *intentio*, topic, or words.
5. Continue until the desired item or sequence is recovered.

Aristotle supplies the logic:

> *Διὸ τάχιστα καὶ κάλλιστα γίνονται ἀπ᾽ ἀρχῆς αἱ ἀναμνήσεις· ὡς γὰρ ἔχουσι τὰ πράγματα πρὸς ἄλληλα τῷ ἐφεξῆς, οὕτω καὶ αἱ κινήσεις.*

“Therefore recollections occur most quickly and successfully from a beginning; for as the things stand successively to one another, so do the movements.”  
—Aristotle, *De memoria et reminiscentia* 452a7–10.

**Documented synthesis:** places furnish the *principium determinatum*; their sequence directs the internal movements; images furnish associative termini.

**Important negative finding:** Albert does not explicitly describe reverse traversal, random access from every fifth locus, reusable numerical place markers, or the elaborate place-management procedures found in later manuals. Those are inherited or later elaborations, not demonstrably Albert’s own instructions.

## 7. Worked example: the inheritance-poisoning lawsuit

The foundational example comes from *Ad Herennium* III.20.33. A man has allegedly poisoned another for an inheritance; witnesses know of the transaction.

The classical image is approximately:

> *Aegrotum in lecto cubantem faciemus ipsum illum, de quo agetur, si formam eius detinebimus; si eum non agnoverimus, aliquem aegrotum non de minimo loco sumemus, ut cito in mentem venire possit. Et reum ad lectum eius adstituemus, dextera poculum, sinistra tabulas, medico testiculos arietinos tenentem.*

“We shall make the sick man lie in bed, representing the victim, if we know his appearance; if not, we shall use some notable sick man. We shall place the defendant beside the bed, holding a cup in his right hand, tablets in his left, and ram’s testicles with the fourth finger.”  
—*Rhetorica ad Herennium* III.20.33; punctuation and interpretation vary.

The cues are:

- cup → poison;
- tablets or document → will or inheritance;
- ram’s testicles → witnesses, through wordplay involving *testes*.

### Albert’s corrupt reading

The transmitted Latin available to Albert had *medico*, which he understood as “physician,” rather than *medico [digito]*, “with the medical/fourth finger.” His resulting scene contains:

- the sick man in bed;
- the accused man with cup and document;
- a physician holding a ram’s testicles.

That bizarre third figure then enters Albert’s later commentary in transformed form.

**Documented textual history:** Albert’s misunderstanding depends on a corrupt or ambiguously punctuated Herenian text.

**Yates’s useful correction:** the original scene does not require a doctor.

**Modern caution:** editions differ in how they reconstruct the notoriously compressed final phrase. The underlying mnemonic pun on *testes*—testicles/witnesses—is secure; the exact syntax is less so.

## 8. The ram in the dark

In *De memoria et reminiscentia*, Albert describes people seeking recollection as withdrawing from public light into secluded obscurity because public sensory images are dispersed and confused, while in quiet darkness they can be unified and set in order.

The Borgnet text reads, in substance:

> *Reminisci volentes recedunt a luce publica ad obscuram solitudinem … propter quod Tullius in arte memorandi … praecipit imaginari et quaerere loca obscura modicum habentia luminis … sicut si velimus recordari eorum quae nobis in causa opponuntur, imaginemur aliquem arietem magnis cornibus et testiculis contra nos in obscuro venientem.*

“Those wishing to recollect withdraw from public light into obscure solitude … therefore Tullius in the art of memory directs us to imagine and seek dark places having little light … as, if we wish to remember what is brought against us in a lawsuit, we imagine a ram with great horns and testicles coming toward us in the darkness.”  
—Albertus, *De memoria et reminiscentia*, tr. II, Borgnet IX, p. 108; compare Editio Coloniensis VII/2A.

Albert explains the horns as adversaries and the testicles as the dispositions or testimony of witnesses.

The scene is best understood as a secondary deformation of the already corrupt lawsuit image:

1. the *Ad Herennium* supplies ram’s testicles;
2. Albert’s *De bono* adds a physician through misreading;
3. the commentary detaches the ram from the bedside tableau;
4. the solitary ram becomes a composite image for adversaries and witnesses.

Yates exclaims, “This ram gives one rather a fright!”

Her description of it as a “mysterious vision in the night” is interpretation, not Albert’s terminology.

### Does Albert violate his own lighting rule?

Not necessarily. The classical rule prohibits a locus that is excessively dark. Albert’s commentary recommends dim, secluded conditions during recollective concentration. “Little light” is not total darkness. Nevertheless, his fusion of:

- quiet surroundings,
- moderate darkness,
- interior concentration,
- and the lawsuit image

does produce a more dramatic scene than the *Ad Herennium* intended.

## 9. Melancholy and recollection

Albert’s commentary also relates memory to humoral physiology.

In the standard medical account:

- dryness helps retain impressions;
- coldness helps stabilize them;
- hence melancholics were often considered good at retaining memories.

Albert writes in *De bono* that goodness of memory is associated with the dry and cold.

For active recollection, however, he invokes a different melancholy—*melancholia fumosa et fervens*, smoky and hot—connected to the pseudo-Aristotelian *Problemata* XXX.1. Dryness retains many phantasms; heat sets them in motion. The combination allegedly supports investigative recollection.

- **Documented:** Albert contrasts retention with mobile, searching recollection through humoral language.
- **Yates’s interpretation:** this is the “intellectual” or “inspired” melancholy of the *Problemata*; therefore Albert’s *ars reminiscendi* may be a prerogative of inspired melancholics.
- **Overreach:** Albert does not say that only inspired melancholics can practice artificial memory. His physiological explanation describes aptitude, not an initiation requirement.
- **Historical context:** Renaissance theories of Saturnian genius later magnified the “inspired melancholy” theme, making it easy to read Ficinian or Brunian concepts backward into Albert.

## The sources

## 1. Albertus Magnus, *De bono*

### Critical edition

*Alberti Magni ordinis fratrum praedicatorum De bono*, ed. Heinrich Kühle, Carl Feckes, Bernhard Geyer, Wilhelm Kübel, *Opera omnia*, Editio Coloniensis, vol. XXVIII (Münster: Aschendorff, 1951).

Relevant passage:

- tract. IV, q. 2, art. 1: pp. 245–246;
- tract. IV, q. 2, art. 2: pp. 246–252;
- place rules: principally pp. 247, 250–251;
- lawsuit and word-images: pp. 247–248, 251;
- *imagines agentes*: p. 248;
- final endorsement: pp. 251–252.

The complete volume contains 329 pages. It was the first volume issued in the modern Editio Coloniensis.

### Publication history

**Documented:** *De bono* was not included as a complete printed work in the seventeenth-century Lyon or nineteenth-century Borgnet editions. The Albertus-Magnus-Institut describes it as previously unprinted when the critical edition appeared in 1951.

**Documented:** the editors knew eleven witnesses and constituted the edition chiefly from five. The text is unfinished or imperfectly revised; the editors made numerous conjectures and suspected some defects could go back to Albert’s working copy.

This matters for the memory chapter: Albert’s mistaken Herenian readings may belong to his exemplar, while some lesser verbal irregularities may reflect the unstable transmission of *De bono* itself.

### Manuscripts

A complete census requires the prolegomena to Editio Coloniensis XXVIII and Winfried Fauser’s catalogue, *Die Werke des Albertus Magnus in ihrer handschriftlichen Überlieferung* (1982). Open catalogues presently verify at least:

- **Köln, Dombibliothek, Cod. 1024**, fifteenth century: *De bono*; fol. 1r is digitally reproduced.
- **Brussels, KBR, MS 77–78**, fifteenth century: described as containing Albertus’s *Summa/De bono* and *Super tertium Sententiarum*; associated by ownership evidence with Henricus van Loen and the Carthusians of Herne.

The 1951 editors’ eleven witnesses should not be silently replaced by an improvised list assembled from modern catalogue keyword matches. Several catalogues confuse Albert’s *De bono*, Philip the Chancellor’s *Summa de bono*, Ulrich of Strasbourg’s *De summo bono*, and Ambrose’s *De bono mortis*.

### Illustrations

Köln Cod. 1024 has decorated manuscript presentation, but no identified mnemonic diagram for IV, q. 2. Albert’s text describes no plate. Its loci and images are mental and verbal.

## 2. Albertus Magnus, *De memoria et reminiscentia*

### Older printed edition

*Beati Alberti Magni Opera omnia*, ed. Auguste Borgnet, vol. IX (Paris: Louis Vivès, 1890), pp. 97–119.

Key passages:

- p. 97: programmatic distinction of memory and recollection and Albert’s complaint that Latin writers have misunderstood them;
- p. 108: dark solitude and the ram;
- p. 117: hot, smoky melancholy and recollective mobility.

The Borgnet text is freely searchable in the University of Waterloo Alberti Magni e-corpus.

### Current critical edition

Albertus Magnus, *De sensu et sensato cuius secundus liber est De memoria et reminiscentia*, Editio Coloniensis VII/2A. The critical edition supersedes Borgnet for wording and textual variants.

### Genre

This is not a line-by-line commentary in the modern sense. Albert calls his method an exposition of Peripatetic doctrine and frequently inserts *digressiones*. He synthesizes Aristotle with Avicenna, Averroes, Alexander of Aphrodisias, Themistius, and al-Fārābī.

The work contains no wheels or mnemonic plates.

## 3. *Rhetorica ad Herennium*

Standard citation: [Cicero], *Rhetorica ad Herennium*, ed. and trans. Harry Caplan, Loeb Classical Library 403 (Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press, 1954), especially III.16.28–24.40.

Albert and his contemporaries believed it to be Cicero’s and called it the “Second Rhetoric,” distinguishing it from the “First Rhetoric,” *De inventione*.

Modern scholarship rejects Ciceronian authorship. “Tullius” in Albert therefore identifies an auctoritas as medieval readers understood it, not the historical author.

Important passages:

- III.16.28: memory as treasury and guardian;
- III.16.29: natural and artificial memory;
- III.16.29–17.30: places and images;
- III.19.32: place rules;
- III.20.33: lawsuit image;
- III.20.34–21.35: memory for things and words;
- III.21.34: Domitius/Marcii Reges and Aesop/Cimber;
- III.22.37: *imagines agentes*.

The controlling metaphor is:

> *Nam loci cerae aut chartae simillimi sunt, imagines litteris, dispositio et conlocatio imaginum scripturae, pronuntiatio lectioni.*

“For places are very like wax tablets or papyrus, images like letters, the arrangement and placement of images like writing, and delivery like reading.”  
—*Ad Herennium* III.17.30.

## 4. Cicero, *De inventione*

Standard modern edition: Cicero, *De inventione; De optimo genere oratorum; Topica*, trans. H. M. Hubbell, Loeb Classical Library 386 (Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press, 1949).

The crucial passage is II.53.160. It does not teach architectural memory. Its role is taxonomic: *memoria*, *intelligentia*, and *providentia* are parts of prudence.

Albert’s synthesis depends upon treating the genuine Ciceronian ethical classification and the pseudo-Ciceronian mnemonic rules as the work of one authority.

## 5. Aristotle, *De memoria et reminiscentia*

Greek: *Περὶ μνήμης καὶ ἀναμνήσεως*, in the *Parva naturalia*.

Useful editions:

- Aristotle, *Parva Naturalia*, ed. and trans. W. S. Hett, Loeb Classical Library 288.
- David Bloch, *Aristotle on Memory and Recollection: Text, Translation, Interpretation, and Reception in Western Scholasticism* (Leiden: Brill, 2007).

Key passages:

- 449b–451a: memory requires a phantasm and awareness of pastness;
- 451a–b: recollection is neither initial learning nor mere retention;
- 451b–452a: search moves through associations and sequences;
- 452a: ordered arrangement, starting points, and ease of recollection.

Albert knew Aristotle through medieval Latin translation and interpretive traditions, not through direct reading of the Greek quoted above.

## Textual variants and corruptions

The most consequential recoverable variants are in Albert’s copy of the *Ad Herennium*.

### The lawsuit phrase

Albert’s *medicus* or *medico* generated a physician where the classical text referred to the *medicus digitus*, the fourth finger. The existence of a doctor in Albert’s *De bono* is therefore:

- **documented in Albert’s reception;**
- **not original to the classical mnemonic;**
- **probably caused by textual corruption or syntactic misunderstanding.**

### The memory-for-words verse

The *Ad Herennium* encodes:

> *Iam domum itionem reges Atridae parant.*

In the first locus, Domitius is beaten by the Marcii Reges, cueing *domum itionem reges*; in the second, Aesop and Cimber are costumed as Agamemnon and Menelaus in *Iphigenia*, cueing *Atridae parant*.

Albert’s text apparently read:

- *ultionem* for *itionem*;
- a syntactically confused version in which Aesop, Cimber, and a wandering Iphigenia were rearranged.

Albert admits that these metaphorical words are obscure and difficult to remember, yet still defends the general principle because the marvelous moves memory.

This episode is evidence of textual difficulty, not evidence that medieval practitioners routinely used incomprehensible images.

## Yates’s account and scholarship since

## 1. What Yates argued

Yates’s chapter makes five principal claims.

### A. Transfer from rhetoric to prudence

She argues that medieval scholasticism moved artificial memory from its minor place under rhetoric into the ethical life as a component of a cardinal virtue.

**Confirmed, with qualification:** Albert explicitly embeds it in prudence. But Yates’s dramatic “transfer” can conceal the persistence of rhetorical uses and the long monastic habit of treating memory as formative rather than merely reproductive.

### B. Albert as the first Aristotelian–Tullian synthesizer

Her exact caution—“So far as I know”—is important.

**Largely confirmed as a description of Albert’s distinctiveness; not proved as absolute priority.** Later work has enlarged the pre-Albertine field and shown that medieval memoria was already intertwined with pedagogy, ethics, meditation, textual layout, and invention.

### C. Real buildings, perhaps churches

**Partly reconstruction, partly speculation.** Bounded and distinctive places are textual; the exclusive restriction to real buildings is stronger than the evidence; the church is Yates’s conjecture.

### D. Images are morally potentiated through *intentiones*

**Confirmed at the psychological level.** But the Justice example is Yates’s construction, not Albert’s.

### E. The ram and melancholy hint at something proto-magical

Yates describes Albert as approaching “superstitious awe,” contrasts him with Thomas, and later wonders whether Bruno encountered an Albertist, astral mnemonic in the Dominican convent at Naples.

**Not confirmed.** No primary text connects Albert’s ram to Aries or makes it talismanic. Yates frames this as a question, but later retellings often promote it into a fact.

## 2. Mary Carruthers

Carruthers’s *The Book of Memory* is the most important correction of scale and context.

Her contributions include:

- translating *De bono* IV, q. 2 in full in Appendix B;
- treating memory as the matrix of reading, composition, meditation, ethics, and invention;
- showing that medieval memory images function like internal writing and need not be pictorial in the modern representational sense;
- situating Albert among a broad range of monastic, cathedral-school, university, preaching, and book-design practices;
- stressing that memoria is productive—material is stored so that it can be recomposed, judged, and used.

Carruthers confirms the importance of Albert’s architectural mnemonic but corrects the impression that medieval artificial memory can be understood as a continuous succession of spectacular occult systems.

Her phrase “architectural mnemonic” is often more exact than the modern “memory palace,” which suggests a single imaginary palace and obscures fields, streets, churches, cloisters, page grids, diagrams, and other ordered supports.

## 3. Paolo Rossi

Rossi’s *Clavis universalis* appeared in Italian in 1960, before Yates’s book; the expanded English translation is *Logic and the Art of Memory*.

Rossi’s central relevance is genealogical:

- classical associative memory;
- medieval and Renaissance memory arts;
- Lullian combination;
- sixteenth-century topical logic;
- universal-language and encyclopedia projects;
- Leibnizian characteristic and calculus.

For Albert specifically, Rossi recognizes the *De bono* and Aristotelian commentary as central points in the scholastic legitimation of artificial memory. His larger trajectory discourages a direct Albert-to-computer line: Albert contributed to one mnemonic-psychological branch, while Lullian combination supplied a structurally different branch that later converged with logical and encyclopedic projects.

## 4. Jocelyn Penny Small

Small’s *Wax Tablets of the Mind* concerns classical antiquity rather than Albert directly. Its importance is methodological:

- ancient “memory for words” should not be assumed to mean modern verbatim memorization;
- physical writing, page organization, oral performance, and internal retrieval interacted;
- ancient feats and prescriptions must be separated from experimentally demonstrated practice;
- the method’s effectiveness depends upon task, training, cue distinctiveness, and prior organization.

Small therefore checks romantic accounts in which every premodern scholar possesses photographic memory or an unlimited internal palace.

## 5. Hajdu and later German research

Helga Hajdu’s *Das mnemotechnische Schrifttum des Mittelalters* (1936) catalogued the medieval technical tradition and emphasized continuity from Roman sources. Later German work, including the *Documenta Mnemonica* editions, has greatly expanded manuscript study and differentiated individual treatises, compilations, translations, and print families.

A useful correction emerging from this scholarship is that Albert’s actual *ars memorandi* section remains closely tied to its single technical source, the *Ad Herennium*. The ethical framing is important, but the detailed mnemonic content is primarily an exercise in reconciling that source with Aristotle.

## 6. Bolzoni, Stagno, Camillo, and emblematic culture

Lina Bolzoni’s work explains how memory images became entangled with preaching, devotional visualization, literary invention, emblems, galleries, and theaters.

This strengthens Yates’s insight that mnemonic images participate in culture, not merely rote learning. It weakens any simple claim that Albert directly caused Camillo’s theater. Camillo’s system arose from a sixteenth-century fusion of rhetoric, Neoplatonism, mythography, Kabbalah, Hermeticism, and courtly patronage. Albert belongs to its deep background only through the legitimated Christian use of corporeal and metaphorical images.

Stagno’s and Bolzoni’s Camillo scholarship likewise favors historically specific reconstruction over a single occult-memory lineage.

## 7. Ong and Ramism

Walter J. Ong’s *Ramus, Method, and the Decay of Dialogue* explains the sixteenth-century movement from rhetorical and dialectical disputation toward spatially tabulated “method.”

Ramus rejected or marginalized the ornate image-and-place art in favor of dialectical order and schematic division. Albert is not a Ramist precursor. The contrast is instructive:

- Albert: sensory images, individual affect, associative places, prudential recovery;
- Ramism: dichotomous division, visible method, curricular order, reduced dependence on bizarre images.

## 8. Lull scholarship: Bonner and Fidora

Anthony Bonner and Alexander Fidora have corrected accounts that treat Ramon Llull’s Art as merely an advanced mnemonic.

Llull’s letters, figures, and rotating combinations served demonstrative, theological, missionary, and logical purposes. Memory could aid their use, but combination is not identical with the Herenian place-image technique.

Therefore:

- Albert’s art and Llull’s Art belong to overlapping cultures of ordered cognition;
- no evidence shows Albert’s *ars memorandi* using Lullian wheels;
- histories from Lull to Leibniz and computing must not absorb Albert’s technique into the combinatorial lineage without distinction.

## 9. Bruno scholarship: Sturlese, Ciliberto, Clucas, Gatti

Post-Yates Bruno scholarship has complicated the thesis of a unitary “Hermetic tradition.”

- Michele Ciliberto emphasizes Bruno’s philosophy, language, civic-religious ambitions, and historical transformations.
- Rita Sturlese’s philological work distinguishes texts, editions, chronology, and sources.
- Stephen Clucas studies Bruno’s mnemonic and magical languages while resisting reduction to one Hermetic key.
- Hilary Gatti emphasizes Bruno’s philosophy, cosmology, and intellectual setting.

These scholars do not establish an Albertine occult-memory school at Naples. Bruno’s invocation of Albertus reflects Albert’s enormous authority in natural philosophy and in works genuinely or spuriously attributed to him. It does not prove that Bruno inherited his wheels from Albert’s ram.

## History and context

## 1. Classical rhetoric

The remote ancestor is the story of Simonides identifying banquet victims by remembering where each had sat.

- **Tradition:** transmitted by Cicero, Quintilian, and others.
- **Historical core:** Simonides was an ancient poet associated with memory.
- **Legendary element:** the precise banquet catastrophe and divine intervention cannot be independently verified.
- **Mnemonic lesson:** order preserves identities after appearances have been destroyed.

The mature architectural method is documented in the *Ad Herennium*, Cicero’s *De oratore*, and Quintilian XI.2.

## 2. Early medieval memoria

Medieval memory was cultivated through:

- memorization of Scripture and Psalms;
- monastic meditation;
- numerical and calendrical ordering;
- divisions and headings;
- page layout;
- concordances;
- florilegia;
- alphabetic indexing;
- allegorical buildings, trees, ladders, arks, and diagrams.

This broader culture did not depend solely on continuous access to the *Ad Herennium*. Albert’s significance lies in bringing the classical technical recipe into a developed scholastic psychology.

## 3. Bologna and the *ars dictaminis*

Yates suggests contact with the Bolognese rhetorical tradition, especially Boncompagno da Signa.

Her chain is:

- Albert entered the Dominicans in 1223;
- he probably studied at the Dominican house in Bologna;
- Bologna was a center of legal rhetoric and letter-writing;
- Boncompagno praised the preaching orders;
- therefore Albert likely knew this environment.

This is plausible context but not direct documentary transmission. No surviving note says that Boncompagno taught Albert the art.

## 4. Paris and the university

*De bono* probably arose from disputed-question teaching in Paris between ca. 1240 and 1246/47. It belongs to Albert’s early moral-theological summa project and precedes his mature commentaries.

The work’s form—objections, authorities, solutions—is university procedure. Artificial memory is not presented as a secret imparted to initiates but as a public scholastic problem subject to rational objections.

## 5. Dominican institutions

For Dominicans, memory was central to:

- Biblical study;
- preaching without continual dependence on a book;
- organizing authorities and distinctions;
- disputation;
- moral self-examination;
- pastoral application of general principles to particular cases.

Albert’s conclusion broadens the user group:

> *Dicimus, quod ars memorandi optima est, quam tradit Tullius, et praecipue in memorabilibus pertinentibus ad vitam et iudicium, et illae memoriae praecipue pertinent ad ethicum et rhetorem…*

“We say that the best art of remembering is that which Tullius teaches, especially for memorable matters pertaining to life and judgment; such memories pertain chiefly to the moral philosopher and the speaker.”  
—*De bono* IV, q. 2, art. 2, sol.; ed. Col. XXVIII, pp. 251–252.

Yates interprets *ethicus et rhetor* as a warrant for later lay use. That is reasonable but indirect. Albert names functions, not clerical estates: ethical agent and orator.

## 6. Thomas Aquinas

Thomas studied under Albert at Cologne and later treated memory in:

- his commentary on Aristotle’s *De memoria et reminiscentia*;
- *Summa theologiae* II–II, q. 49, art. 1, ad 2.

Thomas’s four mnemonic precepts are:

1. choose suitable, unusual corporeal similitudes;
2. arrange what is to be remembered in a considered order;
3. apply solicitude and affection;
4. meditate frequently.

He states:

> *Intentiones simplices et spirituales facilius ex anima elabuntur, nisi quibusdam similitudinibus corporalibus quasi alligentur.*

“Simple and spiritual intentions slip more easily from the soul unless they are bound, as it were, to corporeal similitudes.”

Thomas continues Albert’s integration but is less entangled in the corrupt lawsuit imagery.

Yates’s contrast—Thomas is cautious while Albert verges on “superstitious awe”—is rhetorically sharp but not a neutral description. Both authors naturalize mnemonics within Aristotelian psychology; neither makes it ritual magic.

## 7. Later medieval reception

Albert and Thomas became authorities for later mnemonic writers, including preachers, jurists, students, notaries, and lay intellectuals. The tradition appears in:

- Thomas Bradwardine;
- fifteenth-century Central European mnemonic manuscripts;
- Jacobus Publicius;
- Petrus Ravennas;
- Johannes Romberch;
- Cosmas Rossellius.

Transmission was rarely a pure Albertine school. Writers combined classical rules, scholastic psychology, alphabets, numerical schemes, grammar, devotional imagery, and increasingly printed diagrams.

## People

### Albertus Magnus

A Dominican theologian, philosopher, naturalist, teacher, and later bishop of Regensburg. The traditional entry date into the Dominicans is 1223, though details of his earliest biography rely on later sources.

His authorship of *De bono* and the *De memoria* paraphrase is secure. His authorship of many magical, alchemical, astrological, and occult books printed under his name is not.

### Thomas Aquinas

Documented student of Albert. Early biographies praise his extraordinary memory and claim:

- as a student he retained what his teacher said;
- he remembered material read in different monasteries;
- he could draw upon it in compiling the *Catena aurea*.

These reports are hagiographical testimonies, not controlled demonstrations. The claim that Albert personally “trained Thomas’s memory” is plausible tradition rather than a documented lesson record.

### Boncompagno da Signa

An earlier Bolognese rhetorician who treated natural and artificial memory. Yates sees him as contextual preparation for Albert. Direct teacher-pupil transmission is unproved.

### Hugh of Saint-Victor and John of Garland

Important predecessors in ordered learning and rhetorical memoria. They help disprove the idea that Albert created medieval artificial memory ex nihilo.

### Petrarch

Yates imagines that Petrarch could have used pagan triumphal images under Albert’s authority for striking metaphorical images. This is suggestive interpretation. No surviving Petrarchan note documents an Albertine palace populated by the *Trionfi*.

### Giordano Bruno

A former Dominican who named Albertus among authoritative writers on memory and defended possession of a book of magical images by appealing to Albert’s authority.

That defense is documented in the Bruno trial materials. It proves rhetorical appeal to Albert’s reputation, not dependence on *De bono* IV, q. 2.

### The requested anecdotes

The following belong to the wider catalogue, not securely to Albert’s system:

- **The auctioneer:** associated with later professional mnemonic demonstrations or classical anecdotes, not Albert’s texts.
- **The professor’s party:** not found in the consulted Albertine primary texts.
- **The student who lost his memory:** a late biographical tradition says Albert himself suffered a catastrophic loss of memory near the end of his life, sometimes while lecturing in 1278. The story is hagiographical and poorly documented by contemporary evidence.
- **The accused magician:** accusations of magical methods belong more directly to later mnemonic teachers such as Lambert Schenkel, and to Bruno’s Inquisition context. Albert acquired a posthumous reputation as a magician but was not prosecuted for his *ars memorandi*.
- **Absence finding:** none of these anecdotes can responsibly be presented as a documented episode of someone practicing Albert’s *De bono* mnemonic.

## The magic question

## 1. Is the system itself magical?

No, not in its stated theory or operations.

The causal account is naturalistic by medieval standards:

- perception produces corporeal forms;
- imagination retains or composes phantasms;
- estimation attaches intentions;
- memory conserves them;
- rational recollection searches an ordered series;
- emotionally forceful images adhere more strongly.

There are:

- no invocations;
- no angelic names;
- no consecrations;
- no astrological election;
- no talisman;
- no claim of infused knowledge;
- no promise of learning a science without study.

## 2. Comparison with the *Ars notoria*

The *Ars notoria* is a ritual-magic tradition promising rapid or divinely assisted acquisition of arts and sciences through prayers, observances, and inspection of complex *notae* or figures.

Its superficial similarities to artificial memory are:

- concern with memory and learning;
- visual forms;
- disciplined inspection;
- claims to organize the sciences.

Its decisive differences are causal and ritual. Albert’s art strengthens natural faculties through practice. The *Ars notoria* seeks extraordinary infusion or angelic assistance.

Thomas Aquinas condemned the *Ars notoria* as illicit and superstitious in *Summa theologiae* II–II, q. 96, art. 1.

**Yates’s broader hypothesis:** the *Ars notoria* may descend from, or preserve a magicalized branch of, classical mnemonic *notae*.

**Status:** disputed and unproved. Similar vocabulary does not establish direct descent.

## 3. The ram as Aries

Yates asks whether Albert’s ram could be Aries and the image a stellar or talismanic one. She immediately signals the chronological and evidentiary difficulty.

- **Documented:** Albert describes a ram with horns and testicles.
- **Documented:** the ram derives intelligibly from the lawsuit image.
- **No evidence:** the word *Aries* as zodiacal sign, a planetary correspondence, or a talismanic operation in the mnemonic passage.
- **Verdict:** Yates’s playful speculation, frequently reified by later occult retellings; not a supported reading.

## 4. Albert as magician

Albert’s posthumous magical reputation grew from several causes:

- genuine expertise in natural philosophy, minerals, plants, astrology, and occult qualities;
- discussion of licit and illicit magic;
- attribution of spurious works, including *Secreta Alberti*, *De secretis mulierum*, alchemical texts, charms, and books of experiments;
- fifteenth-century Dominican vitae;
- early modern print marketing;
- later *Grand Albert* and *Petit Albert* traditions.

David J. Collins’s work shows that Dominicans themselves participated in shaping Albert’s “Magnus/Magus” afterlife, partly in contexts of Observant reform.

This afterlife must be separated from *De bono*. The authenticated mnemonic chapter neither identifies itself as a secret nor attracted an accusation of heresy.

## 5. Consequences

There is no known inquisitorial proceeding against Albert for artificial memory. His canonization history, orthodoxy, and Dominican authority instead helped legitimate natural mnemonic practice.

Bruno’s later appeal to Albert shows the reverse problem: Albert’s prestige could be invoked to defend material that Albert may not have written or endorsed.

## Afterlife

## 1. Dante and monumental art

Yates proposed that medieval schemes of virtues, vices, Hell, Purgatory, and Paradise could act as memory places and images, and she connected this possibility with Dante, Giotto, and moral fresco cycles.

Later scholarship accepts that:

- medieval literature and monumental art are deeply structured by memoria;
- ordered spaces, personifications, exemplary histories, and affective images support recollection and meditation;
- Dominican and Franciscan preaching interacted with visual programs.

But a direct claim such as “Dante built the *Commedia* with Albert’s five place rules” is unproved. Similar structures may arise from shared rhetorical, exegetical, liturgical, scholastic, and architectural cultures.

## 2. Emblem books and the Renaissance image

Albert’s defense of metaphorical, marvelous, corporeal images supplied authoritative language compatible with:

- preaching exempla;
- personifications;
- imprese;
- emblems;
- triumphal imagery;
- galleries and theaters;
- illustrated memory manuals.

Compatibility is not direct descent. Sixteenth-century emblem culture has its own humanist, hieroglyphic, numismatic, mythographic, and print histories.

## 3. Camillo

Giulio Camillo’s Memory Theatre uses a physical or imagined theater arranged in grades, with planetary and mythological images.

Albert contributes only deep background:

- place-image architecture;
- confidence in corporeal metaphor;
- ordered ascent or traversal;
- legitimating intellectual authority.

Camillo’s seven planetary grades and its Neoplatonic-Hermetic cosmology are not in Albert.

## 4. Bruno

Bruno preserves the distinction between mnemonic subject or place and attached image while transforming it through:

- Lullian-style rotating combinations;
- mythological and astral imagery;
- philosophical “shadows”;
- magical and cosmological theories;
- enormous combinatorial alphabets.

Yates’s proposed Albertist continuity is possible only at the general Dominican and mnemonic level. No textual chain connects the ram passage with Bruno’s wheels.

## 5. Ramus

Ramist method displaced spectacular associative memory in many educational environments with branching topical order. It represents an alternative reorganization of memory, logic, and pedagogy, not a refinement of Albert’s images.

## 6. Leibniz and the machine

The defensible genealogy is branching:

```text
Classical loci and images
        │
        ├── Albert and Thomas: psychology, prudence, rhetoric
        │       └── later architectural and image-based mnemonics
        │
        └── combinatorial and topical traditions
                ├── Ramon Llull
                ├── Renaissance wheels and universal arts
                ├── universal-language projects
                └── Leibnizian characteristic and calculus
```

The line “Albert → Lull → Leibniz → computer” is misleading:

- Llull was Albert’s contemporary, not Albert’s mnemonic descendant.
- Lullian combination is structurally different from storing images in loci.
- Leibniz drew on logical, algebraic, encyclopedic, and combinatorial sources.
- Modern computing uses addressable storage and symbolic operations, but resemblance to loci is analogical, not a documented technological descent.

## 7. Borges, Eco, and modern literature

Borges’s “Funes the Memorious” explores perfect retention without abstraction. It illuminates, by contrast, why Albert values rational recollection rather than indiscriminate storage. Funes is not based specifically on Albert.

Umberto Eco repeatedly engages lists, encyclopedias, signs, labyrinths, medieval learning, and universal-language projects. His intellectual genealogy is closer to semiotics, Llull, combinatorics, and encyclopedism than to Albert’s ram.

## 8. Modern memory palaces and athletes

Modern competitors still use recognizable descendants of:

- ordered familiar routes;
- vivid interacting images;
- phonetic and numerical encodings;
- repeated retrieval practice.

This confirms the practical plausibility of the core place-image method. It does not validate Albert’s ventricular physiology, humoral theory, or moral classification.

“Memory palace” is now a broad popular label. Albert himself speaks of places, fields, temples, hospitals, and buildings; he does not name one universal palace.

## Controversies and disputes

## 1. Did Albert invent the system?

No.

- **Documented:** the technical rules come overwhelmingly from the *Ad Herennium*.
- **Albert’s innovation:** philosophical reconciliation and ethical authorization.
- **Legend/modern simplification:** “Albertus Magnus invented the memory palace.”

## 2. Was he the first to combine Aristotle and Cicero?

- **Yates:** probably yes, cautiously stated.
- **Evidence for:** Albert’s discussion is early, explicit, and unusually complete.
- **Evidence against absolute priority:** earlier school traditions already combined ordering, recollection, rhetoric, and moral training; unedited glosses may predate him.
- **Verdict:** first major surviving systematic synthesis, not demonstrably the first occurrence.

## 3. Were his loci exclusively real?

- **Yates:** deduces real buildings from Albert’s rejection of imaginary camps and cities.
- **Text:** favors bounded, manageable loci and accepts corporeal/imaginable places.
- **Verdict:** real, previously known environments are preferred; an absolute prohibition of constructed mental places is not established.

## 4. Did he prescribe churches?

- **Yates:** suggests a church because solemn and rare places move strongly.
- **Text:** gives temple among possible examples but also field and hospital.
- **Verdict:** church/temple is one possible locus system, not the prescribed Albertine architecture.

## 5. Does an image of Justice contain an intention to seek Justice?

- **Albert:** images contain associated *intentiones*; wolf-danger is the secure model.
- **Yates:** invents Justice as an explanatory example and labels it her deduction.
- **Verdict:** sound illustration of Albert’s theory, but not a quotation or worked example from Albert.

## 6. Is the ram mystical?

- **Text:** an image used while recollecting material from a lawsuit.
- **Yates:** “mystical obscurity,” “mysterious vision.”
- **Alternative reading:** ordinary advice about sensory withdrawal fused with a corrupted mnemonic scene.
- **Verdict:** “mystical” describes Yates’s response, not an explicit theological category.

## 7. Is the ram Aries?

- **Evidence:** none in the passage.
- **Origin of reading:** Yates’s speculative question in 1966.
- **Spread:** occult histories and popular summaries that omit her question marks.
- **Verdict:** unsupported.

## 8. Did Albert’s melancholy theory make mnemonics an inspired gift?

- **Text:** hot/dry melancholy can favor mobile recollection.
- **Yates:** perhaps *ars reminiscendi* is a prerogative of inspired melancholics.
- **Problem:** artificial memory is defined as a trainable rational habit; restricting it to a temperament contradicts its general pedagogical function.
- **Verdict:** temperament may affect aptitude; exclusive inspired status is not documented.

## 9. Was the technique heretical?

No known medieval authority condemned Albert’s place-image technique as heretical.

Confusion arises from combining it with:

- the *Ars notoria*;
- astral talismans;
- necromancy;
- spurious magical books attributed to Albert;
- Bruno’s magical memory.

These are historically adjacent but doctrinally distinct.

## 10. Are there Albertine diagrams?

No securely identified mnemonic diagram accompanies either authenticated treatment.

Images of Köln Cod. 1024’s decorated opening document a manuscript of *De bono*, not a diagram of its memory system. Wheels reproduced beside discussions of Albert generally belong to Llull, Publicius, Romberch, Rossellius, Bruno, or later authors.

## 11. Did Albert personally train Thomas in the art?

Thomas studied under Albert. Thomas later taught closely related mnemonic principles. Early biographies praise Thomas’s memory.

No surviving testimony records an Albertine memory lesson or gives its exercises. “Albert trained Thomas’s artificial memory at Cologne” is plausible tradition, not documented fact.

## 12. Did *De bono* circulate widely as a mnemonic manual?

Probably not as an independent manual.

- The complete work survives in a limited manuscript tradition.
- Its memory section was embedded in a moral-theological question.
- Later authority may have travelled through excerpts, scholastic commonplaces, Thomas, and general attribution rather than direct copying of IV, q. 2.
- A reception study must distinguish citation of “Albertus” from direct dependence on this chapter.

## Open questions

1. **Priority:** Do pre-1240 glosses on the *Ad Herennium* explicitly identify artificial memory with Aristotelian *reminiscentia*?
2. **Albert’s exemplar:** Can the precise manuscript family of the corrupt *medico*, *ultionem*, and Iphigenia readings be reconstructed?
3. **Relation between the two works:** Did Albert consciously revise the bedside lawsuit image into the charging ram, or was the commentary relying on a different corrupt rhetorical witness?
4. **Chronology:** How much time separates *De bono* from *De memoria et reminiscentia*, and did Albert’s faculty psychology materially change?
5. **Manuscript use:** Are there marginal diagrams, place lists, or reader annotations beside IV, q. 2 in any of the eleven known *De bono* witnesses?
6. **Classroom reception:** Was the passage excerpted in Dominican *studia*, preaching manuals, or university collections?
7. **Thomas:** Can verbal parallels demonstrate direct dependence on *De bono*, rather than common dependence on Cicero, the *Ad Herennium*, and Aristotle?
8. **Lay reception:** When is Albert first explicitly cited by a lay mnemonic author as authority for metaphorical images?
9. **Magic:** When was Yates’s tentative Aries question first repeated as a positive assertion?
10. **Spurious Albertina:** Which pseudo-Albertine magical works were read alongside authentic psychological works, and by whom?
11. **Humoral practice:** Did any practitioner adapt Albert’s hot/dry melancholy theory into regimen—diet, sleep, environment, or medication—for mnemonic training?
12. **Visual culture:** Can any specific Dominican pictorial program be linked through documentary evidence to *De bono* IV, q. 2 rather than to the general culture of preaching and memoria?

---

## Conclusions

Albertus Magnus’s *ars memorandi* is neither a secret medieval memory palace of his invention nor an occult wheel. It is a philosophically ambitious defense of the classical art of places and images.

Its historical importance lies in four moves:

1. Albert places trained memory within prudence.
2. He interprets artificial memory as rational recollection rather than passive storage.
3. He explains mnemonic images through the internal senses and their capacity to retain *intentiones*.
4. He defends extraordinary metaphorical images because affective wonder makes them adhere.

Yates identified these moves with exceptional clarity. Her treatment remains indispensable for the textual drama: the corrupt physician, the charging ram, the imagines agentes entering ethics, and the contrast with Thomas. The chief corrections are equally clear:

- the church is conjectural;
- the Justice example is hers;
- “first” requires qualification;
- inspired melancholy is not an exclusive qualification;
- the ram is not demonstrably Aries;
- no Albertine magical memory school at Bruno’s Naples convent has been documented;
- and Albert’s system contains no wheels, diagrams, or talismans.

The enduring Albertine principle is simpler and more consequential: rational judgment depends on recoverable experience, and recoverable experience is strengthened when abstract intentions are deliberately bound to ordered, corporeal, emotionally effective forms.

# Albertus Magnus’s *ars memorandi*: Research Dossier

## Basic identification

| Field | Identification |
|---|---|
| **Name** | Albertus Magnus’s *ars memorandi*—not an independent handbook, but a scholastic examination and endorsement of the artificial memory attributed to “Tullius” |
| **Exponent** | Albertus Magnus / Albert the Great, O.P. (ca. 1200–1280) |
| **Date** | *De bono*: probably Paris, ca. 1240–1246/47; *De memoria et reminiscentia*: later, during Albert’s systematic Aristotelian paraphrase project, conventionally placed in the 1250s |
| **Principal text** | *De bono*, tractatus IV, quaestio 2, “De partibus prudentiae,” especially art. 2, “De arte memorandi”; critical edition: *Alberti Magni Opera omnia*, Editio Coloniensis XXVIII, ed. | **Companion text** | *De memoria et reminiscentia*, in the Aristotelian *Parva naturalia* paraphrases; older edition: *Opera omnia*, ed. Auguste Borgnet, IX (Paris: Vivès, 1890), pp. 97–119, especially pp. 108 and 117; new critical edition: Editio Coloniensis VII/2A |
| **Principle in one sentence** | Artificial memory is disciplined recollection: corporeal, emotionally forceful images are attached to an ordered series of imaginable places so that rational search can proceed from a determinate beginning through a stable sequence, thereby making remembered experience serviceable to prudence, ethics, and rhetoric. |
| **Yates** | Frances A. Yates, *The Art of Memory* (1966), ch. III, pp. 61–70; recalled at pp. 90, 97–99, 104; and ch. IX, pp. 204–205. Pagination follows the original Routledge/Chicago text. |

### Evidentiary synopsis

- **Documented text:** Albert explicitly examines “*ars memorandi quam Tullius tradit in Secunda Rhetorica*”—the art taught by Tullius in the “Second Rhetoric,” meaning the anonymous *Rhetorica ad Herennium*.
- **Documented text:** He accepts the classical division into natural and artificial memory, places and images, memory for things and memory for words, and the rules for choosing places and striking images.
- **Documented text:** He reclassifies the technique as *reminiscentia*, rationally directed recollection, and therefore as pertinent to prudence.
- **Yates’s interpretation:** Albert was the first to conflate Aristotle’s analysis of recollection with the pseudo-Ciceronian architectural mnemonic.
- **Scholarly correction:** Albert’s discussion is unusually early and systematic, but “first” is unsafe without qualification. Earlier Latin pedagogical and rhetorical traditions—Hugh of Saint-Victor, John of Garland, Boncompagno da Signa, monastic ordering practices, and glosses on the rhetorical corpus—had already related ordered recall to intellectual or moral formation. Albert’s distinctive achievement is the explicit scholastic reconciliation of the *Ad Herennium* with Aristotelian faculty psychology.
- **Negative finding:** Albert supplies no wheel, table, alphabetic key, combinatorial diagram, or illustrated plate. Those belong to other memory systems. No surviving diagram can securely be called “Albertus Magnus’s memory diagram.”
- **Negative finding:** There is no evidence that Albert himself called the ram an image of Aries, treated the mnemonic as talismanic, or prescribed astral images for memory.

## The system in detail

## 1. What kind of “system” is this?

Albert did not invent an entirely new apparatus. He transmitted, interrogated, and philosophically naturalized the classical architectural mnemonic.

Its principal ingredients came from four textual complexes:

1. Cicero’s *De inventione* II.53.160 supplied the moral classification of memory as a part of prudence.
2. The anonymous *Rhetorica ad Herennium* III.16–24 supplied artificial memory, places, images, memory for things, memory for words, and the spectacular-image rules.
3. Aristotle’s *De memoria et reminiscentia*, especially 451b–453a, supplied recollection as an active search beginning from an ordered starting point.
4. Avicenna, Averroes, and the Latin faculty-psychology tradition supplied the internal senses: common sense, imagination, estimation, and memory.

Albert’s own contribution is the synthesis. A technique inherited under rhetoric becomes explicable through psychology and defensible under ethics.

This is why “Albertus’s *ars memorandi*” should be understood as shorthand for an Albertine theory and ratification of an existing art, not as the title of an independently circulating manual.

## 2. The scholastic structure of *De bono* IV, q. 2

The question contains two articles.

### Article 1: What memory is

Albert begins from Cicero:

> *Prudentia est rerum bonarum et malarum neutrarumque scientia. Partes eius: memoria, intelligentia, providentia. Memoria est, per quam animus repetit illa quae fuerunt…*

“Prudence is knowledge of things good, bad, and indifferent. Its parts are memory, understanding, and foresight. Memory is that through which the mind recalls what has been.”  
—Cicero, *De inventione* II.53.160.

Albert must answer an apparent category error:

- memory seems to be a sensitive cognitive power;
- prudence is a rational and moral virtue;
- therefore memory cannot simply be a “part” of prudence.

His answer is functional. Mere retention belongs to sensitive memory; purposive recovery of past particulars for present deliberation belongs to rational recollection. Memory becomes part of prudence insofar as it enters the activity of *reminiscentia*.

The governing formulation is:

> *Dicimus, quod memoria est pars prudentiae, secundum quod memoria cadit in rationem reminiscentiae.*

“We say that memory is a part of prudence insofar as memory enters into the character of recollection.”  
—Albertus, *De bono* IV, q. 2, art. 1, sol.; Editio Coloniensis XXVIII, pp. 245–246.

Albert further distinguishes memory as a cognitive disposition from memory as a moral disposition. Remembered particulars make experience possible; experience informs deliberation; prudence must judge actions, and actions occur in particulars rather than universals alone.

### Article 2: The art taught by “Tullius”

Article 2 puts the art on trial through twenty-one numbered objections or problems and corresponding solutions. Albert quotes or paraphrases the *Ad Herennium*, raises philosophical and practical objections, and then vindicates the rules.

His initial classification is decisive:

> *Quod autem dicit de memoria artificiali, quae inductione et rationali praecepto confirmatur … non est memoriae, sed reminiscentiae, sicut dicit Philosophus in libro De memoria et reminiscentia.*

“What he says about artificial memory, which is established by induction and rational precept … belongs not [simply] to memory but to recollection, as the Philosopher says in the book *On Memory and Recollection*.”  
—*De bono* IV, q. 2, art. 2; ed. Col. XXVIII, p. 246.

**Documented text:** artificial memory is an acquired habit, governed by reason and deliberate practice.

**Yates’s formulation:** “So far as I know, Albertus was the first to make this conflation.”

**Safer reconstruction:** Albert provides the earliest presently famous, fully explicit scholastic harmonization of the pseudo-Ciceronian art with Aristotle’s account of recollective search. Priority over every lost, glossed, or unedited classroom treatment has not been demonstrated.

## 3. What serves as places?

The classical answer, accepted by Albert, is *loci*: stable, distinctly apprehended backgrounds into which temporary images are placed.

Albert’s *De bono* recognizes both:

- a natural place, such as a field;
- an artificial or humanly made place, such as a building.

The text’s terminology distinguishes what is memorable *natura* and *manu*. Albert explains the latter as something constructed, not as an imaginary geometry.

### The five rules for places

Albert reproduces and confirms the five chief Herenian rules:

1. **Choose quiet or solitary places.** Crowds and traffic disturb the concentration required to imprint the sequence.
2. **Keep the places dissimilar.** Repeated identical arches or intercolumniations produce confusion.
3. **Keep each place moderate in size.** It must be graspable as a distinct unit, neither too large nor too small.
4. **Use moderate illumination.** Excessive brightness dazzles; excessive darkness conceals.
5. **Separate places by a moderate interval.** The inherited recommendation is approximately thirty feet.

The point is not physical measurement for its own sake. Each locus must remain perceptually discriminable from its neighbors.

### Real versus imaginary places

Albert puzzles over the description of a good locus as both *brevis* and *perfectus*. His answer is that “brief” means cognitively bounded: it must not stretch the mind through enormous imagined spaces such as a whole camp or city.

Yates infers from this that Albert recommends only real places memorized through direct experience.

That conclusion needs qualification:

- **Documented:** Albert rejects expansive imagined camps and cities as mentally distending.
- **Documented:** he accepts fields, temples, hospitals, and buildings as possible place systems, chosen according to what most affects the individual.
- **Scholarly reconstruction:** his safest model is a bounded, previously apprehended environment.
- **Overstatement:** the text does not establish a universal ban on every invented locus. Albert’s category *loca imaginabilia* includes places as present in imagination, whether their initial form entered through sense or was subsequently recomposed.
- **Yates’s speculation:** because Albert calls “solemn and rare” places most moving, perhaps he had a church in mind.
- **Evidence:** none beyond the suitability of a church as one possible solemn, distinctive building. Albert does not prescribe “use a church.”

The key individualizing qualification is that one person may be most moved by a field, another by a temple, another by a hospital. The physical kind of place can vary; the formal five rules remain.

## 4. What serves as images?

The images are corporeal similitudes placed within the ordered backgrounds.

Albert retains the classical distinction:

- *memoria rerum*: images stand for things, topics, arguments, or conceptual contents;
- *memoria verborum*: images cue individual words or verbal sequences.

He regards memory for words as possible but cumbersome and vulnerable to obscurity. His deepest interest lies in images capable of carrying intelligible and moral *intentiones*.

### Image, form, and *intentio*

In Avicennian faculty psychology, the sheep perceives not only the visible form of a wolf but its non-sensible *intentio*—dangerousness, hostility, something-to-flee. The estimative faculty apprehends that intention; memory retains it.

Albert asks whether artificial memory therefore requires:

- one image to represent the sensible form, and
- another to represent the *intentio*.

His answer is no. The mnemonic image carries its intention within it.

The wolf is Albert’s genuine example elsewhere in his psychology. The oft-repeated example “an image of Justice contains the intention to seek Justice” is not Albert’s worked example.

- **Documented:** the memory image can include an *intentio*.
- **Documented:** Albert illustrates animal estimation with the sheep or lamb’s apprehension of the wolf.
- **Yates’s stated deduction:** a rational image of Justice might contain an intention to acquire Justice.
- **Catalogue distortion:** turning Yates’s hypothetical Justice example into Albert’s own wording.

This is an important correction because Yates herself explicitly marked the Justice example as her deduction.

## 5. The rules for images

Albert accepts images that are:

- concrete and corporeal;
- distinct rather than vague;
- few enough to avoid confusion;
- emotionally or perceptually forceful;
- extraordinary, beautiful, ugly, bloody, comic, obscene, or otherwise unusual;
- metaphorically capable of compressing several related matters into one scene.

The source behind him states:

> *Id accidet, si quam maxime notatas similitudines constituemus; si non multas nec vagas, sed aliquid agentes imagines ponemus; si egregiam pulchritudinem aut unicam turpitudinem eis attribuemus … aut si qua re deformabimus, ut si cruentam aut caeno oblitam aut rubrica delibutam inducamus … aut ridiculas res aliquas imaginibus attribuamus.*

“This will happen if we establish likenesses as striking as possible; if we set up images that are neither numerous nor vague but active; if we give them exceptional beauty or singular ugliness … or disfigure them, making one bloody, smeared with mud, or daubed with red pigment … or give them something ridiculous.”  
—*Rhetorica ad Herennium* III.22.37.

Albert imports these *imagines agentes* into a scholastic treatise on prudence. Yates memorably writes that they “stroll mysteriously, like players, out of antiquity into the scholastic treatise on memory as a part of Prudence.”

Albert’s philosophical defense is:

> *Propria magis certificent de re, tamen metaphorica plus movent animam et ideo plus conferunt memoriae.*

“Although literal expressions give more precise information about the thing, metaphorical ones move the soul more, and therefore contribute more to memory.”  
—*De bono* IV, q. 2, art. 2, solutions to points 16–18; ed. Col. XXVIII, p. 251.

He adds that the marvelous is more moving than the customary and invokes Aristotle’s observation that wonder stands near the beginning of philosophy and that myth is composed of wonders.

This does not mean that the image magically transmits knowledge. It means that affective salience strengthens retention and supplies a route of recollective search.

## 6. Fixing order and retrieving content

Albert does not give a new numbered architectural itinerary. He accepts the classical mechanism and supplies its Aristotelian rationale.

### Encoding

1. Select a stable sequence of distinguishable loci.
2. Know their order in advance.
3. Translate what is to be retained into one or more corporeal similitudes.
4. Make those images conspicuous, active, and personally affecting.
5. Place each image in its assigned locus.
6. Rehearse the locus sequence.

### Retrieval

1. Begin from a determinate starting point.
2. Mentally traverse the places in their established succession.
3. Perceive the image assigned to each place.
4. Move from the image’s sensible form to its associated *intentio*, topic, or words.
5. Continue until the desired item or sequence is recovered.

Aristotle supplies the logic:

> *Διὸ τάχιστα καὶ κάλλιστα γίνονται ἀπ᾽ ἀρχῆς αἱ ἀναμνήσεις· ὡς γὰρ ἔχουσι τὰ πράγματα πρὸς ἄλληλα τῷ ἐφεξῆς, οὕτω καὶ αἱ κινήσεις.*

“Therefore recollections occur most quickly and successfully from a beginning; for as the things stand successively to one another, so do the movements.”  
—Aristotle, *De memoria et reminiscentia* 452a7–10.

**Documented synthesis:** places furnish the *principium determinatum*; their sequence directs the internal movements; images furnish associative termini.

**Important negative finding:** Albert does not explicitly describe reverse traversal, random access from every fifth locus, reusable numerical place markers, or the elaborate place-management procedures found in later manuals. Those are inherited or later elaborations, not demonstrably Albert’s own instructions.

## 7. Worked example: the inheritance-poisoning lawsuit

The foundational example comes from *Ad Herennium* III.20.33. A man has allegedly poisoned another for an inheritance; witnesses know of the transaction.

The classical image is approximately:

> *Aegrotum in lecto cubantem faciemus ipsum illum, de quo agetur, si formam eius detinebimus; si eum non agnoverimus, aliquem aegrotum non de minimo loco sumemus, ut cito in mentem venire possit. Et reum ad lectum eius adstituemus, dextera poculum, sinistra tabulas, medico testiculos arietinos tenentem.*

“We shall make the sick man lie in bed, representing the victim, if we know his appearance; if not, we shall use some notable sick man. We shall place the defendant beside the bed, holding a cup in his right hand, tablets in his left, and ram’s testicles with the fourth finger.”  
—*Rhetorica ad Herennium* III.20.33; punctuation and interpretation vary.

The cues are:

- cup → poison;
- tablets or document → will or inheritance;
- ram’s testicles → witnesses, through wordplay involving *testes*.

### Albert’s corrupt reading

The transmitted Latin available to Albert had *medico*, which he understood as “physician,” rather than *medico [digito]*, “with the medical/fourth finger.” His resulting scene contains:

- the sick man in bed;
- the accused man with cup and document;
- a physician holding a ram’s testicles.

That bizarre third figure then enters Albert’s later commentary in transformed form.

**Documented textual history:** Albert’s misunderstanding depends on a corrupt or ambiguously punctuated Herenian text.

**Yates’s useful correction:** the original scene does not require a doctor.

**Modern caution:** editions differ in how they reconstruct the notoriously compressed final phrase. The underlying mnemonic pun on *testes*—testicles/witnesses—is secure; the exact syntax is less so.

---

## 8. The ram in the dark

In *De memoria et reminiscentia*, Albert describes people seeking recollection as withdrawing from public light into secluded obscurity because public sensory images are dispersed and confused, while in quiet darkness they can be unified and set in order.

The Borgnet text reads, in substance:

> *Reminisci volentes recedunt a luce publica ad obscuram solitudinem … propter quod Tullius in arte memorandi … praecipit imaginari et quaerere loca obscura modicum habentia luminis … sicut si velimus recordari eorum quae nobis in causa opponuntur, imaginemur aliquem arietem magnis cornibus et testiculis contra nos in obscuro venientem.*

“Those wishing to recollect withdraw from public light into obscure solitude … therefore Tullius in the art of memory directs us to imagine and seek dark places having little light … as, if we wish to remember what is brought against us in a lawsuit, we imagine a ram with great horns and testicles coming toward us in the darkness.”  
—Albertus, *De memoria et reminiscentia*, tr. II, Borgnet IX, p. 108; compare Editio Coloniensis VII/2A.

Albert explains the horns as adversaries and the testicles as the dispositions or testimony of witnesses.

The scene is best understood as a secondary deformation of the already corrupt lawsuit image:

1. the *Ad Herennium* supplies ram’s testicles;
2. Albert’s *De bono* adds a physician through misreading;
3. the commentary detaches the ram from the bedside tableau;
4. the solitary ram becomes a composite image for adversaries and witnesses.

Yates exclaims, “This ram gives one rather a fright!”

Her description of it as a “mysterious vision in the night” is interpretation, not Albert’s terminology.

### Does Albert violate his own lighting rule?

Not necessarily. The classical rule prohibits a locus that is excessively dark. Albert’s commentary recommends dim, secluded conditions during recollective concentration. “Little light” is not total darkness. Nevertheless, his fusion of:

- quiet surroundings,
- moderate darkness,
- interior concentration,
- and the lawsuit image

does produce a more dramatic scene than the *Ad Herennium* intended.

## 9. Melancholy and recollection

Albert’s commentary also relates memory to humoral physiology.

In the standard medical account:

- dryness helps retain impressions;
- coldness helps stabilize them;
- hence melancholics were often considered good at retaining memories.

Albert writes in *De bono* that goodness of memory is associated with the dry and cold.

For active recollection, however, he invokes a different melancholy—*melancholia fumosa et fervens*, smoky and hot—connected to the pseudo-Aristotelian *Problemata* XXX.1. Dryness retains many phantasms; heat sets them in motion. The combination allegedly supports investigative recollection.

- **Documented:** Albert contrasts retention with mobile, searching recollection through humoral language.
- **Yates’s interpretation:** this is the “intellectual” or “inspired” melancholy of the *Problemata*; therefore Albert’s *ars reminiscendi* may be a prerogative of inspired melancholics.
- **Overreach:** Albert does not say that only inspired melancholics can practice artificial memory. His physiological explanation describes aptitude, not an initiation requirement.
- **Historical context:** Renaissance theories of Saturnian genius later magnified the “inspired melancholy” theme, making it easy to read Ficinian or Brunian concepts backward into Albert.

*Beati Alberti Magni Opera omnia*, ed. Auguste Borgnet, vol. IX (Paris: Louis Vivès, 1890), pp. 97–119.

### Current critical edition

### Genre

This is not a line-by-line commentary in the modern sense. Albert calls his method an exposition of Peripatetic doctrine and frequently inserts *digressiones*. He synthesizes Aristotle with Avicenna, Averroes, Alexander of Aphrodisias, Themistius, and al-Fārābī.

The work contains no wheels or mnemonic plates.

## 3. *Rhetorica ad Herennium*

Standard citation: [Cicero], *Rhetorica ad Herennium*, ed. and trans. Harry Caplan, Loeb Classical Library 403 (Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press, 1954), especially III.16.28–24.40.

Albert and his contemporaries believed it to be Cicero’s and called it the “Second Rhetoric,” distinguishing it from the “First Rhetoric,” *De inventione*.

Modern scholarship rejects Ciceronian authorship. “Tullius” in Albert therefore identifies an auctoritas as medieval readers understood it, not the historical author.

Important passages:

- III.16.28: memory as treasury and guardian;
- III.16.29: natural and artificial memory;
- III.16.29–17.30: places and images;
- III.19.32: place rules;
- III.20.33: lawsuit image;
- III.20.34–21.35: memory for things and words;
- III.21.34: Domitius/Marcii Reges and Aesop/Cimber;
- III.22.37: *imagines agentes*.

The controlling metaphor is:

> *Nam loci cerae aut chartae simillimi sunt, imagines litteris, dispositio et conlocatio imaginum scripturae, pronuntiatio lectioni.*

“For places are very like wax tablets or papyrus, images like letters, the arrangement and placement of images like writing, and delivery like reading.”  
—*Ad Herennium* III.17.30.

## 4. Cicero, *De inventione*

Standard modern edition: Cicero, *De inventione; De optimo genere oratorum; Topica*, trans. H. M. Hubbell, Loeb Classical Library 386 (Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press, 1949).

The crucial passage is II.53.160. It does not teach architectural memory. Its role is taxonomic: *memoria*, *intelligentia*, and *providentia* are parts of prudence.

## 5. Aristotle, *De memoria et reminiscentia*

Greek: *Περὶ μνήμης καὶ ἀναμνήσεως*, in the *Parva naturalia*.

Useful editions:

- Aristotle, *Parva Naturalia*, ed. and trans. W. S. Hett, Loeb Classical Library 288.
- David Bloch, *Aristotle on Memory and Recollection: Text, Translation, Interpretation, and Reception in Western Scholasticism* (Leiden: Brill, 2007).

Key passages:

- 449b–451a: memory requires a phantasm and awareness of pastness;
- 451a–b: recollection is neither initial learning nor mere retention;
- 451b–452a: search moves through associations and sequences;
- 452a: ordered arrangement, starting points, and ease of recollection.

Albert knew Aristotle through medieval Latin translation and interpretive traditions, not through direct reading of the Greek quoted above.

## Textual variants and corruptions

The most consequential recoverable variants are in Albert’s copy of the *Ad Herennium*.

### The lawsuit phrase

Albert’s *medicus* or *medico* generated a physician where the classical text referred to the *medicus digitus*, the fourth finger. The existence of a doctor in Albert’s *De bono* is therefore:

- **documented in Albert’s reception;**
- **not original to the classical mnemonic;**
- **probably caused by textual corruption or syntactic misunderstanding.**

### The memory-for-words verse

The *Ad Herennium* encodes:

> *Iam domum itionem reges Atridae parant.*

In the first locus, Domitius is beaten by the Marcii Reges, cueing *domum itionem reges*; in the second, Aesop and Cimber are costumed as Agamemnon and Menelaus in *Iphigenia*, cueing *Atridae parant*.

Albert’s text apparently read:

- *ultionem* for *itionem*;
- a syntactically confused version in which Aesop, Cimber, and a wandering Iphigenia were rearranged.

Albert admits that these metaphorical words are obscure and difficult to remember, yet still defends the general principle because the marvelous moves memory.

This episode is evidence of textual difficulty, not evidence that medieval practitioners routinely used incomprehensible images.

## Yates’s account and scholarship since

## 1. What Yates argued

Yates’s chapter makes five principal claims.

### A. Transfer from rhetoric to prudence

She argues that medieval scholasticism moved artificial memory from its minor place under rhetoric into the ethical life as a component of a cardinal virtue.

**Confirmed, with qualification:** Albert explicitly embeds it in prudence. But Yates’s dramatic “transfer” can conceal the persistence of rhetorical uses and the long monastic habit of treating memory as formative rather than merely reproductive.

### B. Albert as the first Aristotelian–Tullian synthesizer

Her exact caution—“So far as I know”—is important.

**Largely confirmed as a description of Albert’s distinctiveness; not proved as absolute priority.** Later work has enlarged the pre-Albertine field and shown that medieval memoria was already intertwined with pedagogy, ethics, meditation, textual layout, and invention.

### C. Real buildings, perhaps churches

**Partly reconstruction, partly speculation.** Bounded and distinctive places are textual; the exclusive restriction to real buildings is stronger than the evidence; the church is Yates’s conjecture.

### D. Images are morally potentiated through *intentiones*

**Confirmed at the psychological level.** But the Justice example is Yates’s construction, not Albert’s.

### E. The ram and melancholy hint at something proto-magical

Yates describes Albert as approaching “superstitious awe,” contrasts him with Thomas, and later wonders whether Bruno encountered an Albertist, astral mnemonic in the Dominican convent at Naples.

**Not confirmed.** No primary text connects Albert’s ram to Aries or makes it talismanic. Yates frames this as a question, but later retellings often promote it into a fact.

---

## 2. Mary Carruthers

Carruthers’s *The Book of Memory* is the most important correction of scale and context.

Her contributions include:

- translating *De bono* IV, q. 2 in full in Appendix B;
- treating memory as the matrix of reading, composition, meditation, ethics, and invention;
- showing that medieval memory images function like internal writing and need not be pictorial in the modern representational sense;
- situating Albert among a broad range of monastic, cathedral-school, university, preaching, and book-design practices;
- stressing that memoria is productive—material is stored so that it can be recomposed, judged, and used.

Carruthers confirms the importance of Albert’s architectural mnemonic but corrects the impression that medieval artificial memory can be understood as a continuous succession of spectacular occult systems.

Her phrase “architectural mnemonic” is often more exact than the modern “memory palace,” which suggests a single imaginary palace and obscures fields, streets, churches, cloisters, page grids, diagrams, and other ordered supports.

## 3. Paolo Rossi

Rossi’s *Clavis universalis* appeared in Italian in 1960, before Yates’s book; the expanded English translation is *Logic and the Art of Memory*.

Rossi’s central relevance is genealogical:

- classical associative memory;
- medieval and Renaissance memory arts;
- Lullian combination;
- sixteenth-century topical logic;
- universal-language and encyclopedia projects;
- Leibnizian characteristic and calculus.

For Albert specifically, Rossi recognizes the *De bono* and Aristotelian commentary as central points in the scholastic legitimation of artificial memory. His larger trajectory discourages a direct Albert-to-computer line: Albert contributed to one mnemonic-psychological branch, while Lullian combination supplied a structurally different branch that later converged with logical and encyclopedic projects.

## 4. Jocelyn Penny Small

Small’s *Wax Tablets of the Mind* concerns classical antiquity rather than Albert directly. Its importance is methodological:

- ancient “memory for words” should not be assumed to mean modern verbatim memorization;
- physical writing, page organization, oral performance, and internal retrieval interacted;
- ancient feats and prescriptions must be separated from experimentally demonstrated practice;
- the method’s effectiveness depends upon task, training, cue distinctiveness, and prior organization.

Small therefore checks romantic accounts in which every premodern scholar possesses photographic memory or an unlimited internal palace.

## 5. Hajdu and later German research

Helga Hajdu’s *Das mnemotechnische Schrifttum des Mittelalters* (1936) catalogued the medieval technical tradition and emphasized continuity from Roman sources. Later German work, including the *Documenta Mnemonica* editions, has greatly expanded manuscript study and differentiated individual treatises, compilations, translations, and print families.

A useful correction emerging from this scholarship is that Albert’s actual *ars memorandi* section remains closely tied to its single technical source, the *Ad Herennium*. The ethical framing is important, but the detailed mnemonic content is primarily an exercise in reconciling that source with Aristotle.

## 6. Bolzoni, Stagno, Camillo, and emblematic culture

Lina Bolzoni’s work explains how memory images became entangled with preaching, devotional visualization, literary invention, emblems, galleries, and theaters.

This strengthens Yates’s insight that mnemonic images participate in culture, not merely rote learning. It weakens any simple claim that Albert directly caused Camillo’s theater. Camillo’s system arose from a sixteenth-century fusion of rhetoric, Neoplatonism, mythography, Kabbalah, Hermeticism, and courtly patronage. Albert belongs to its deep background only through the legitimated Christian use of corporeal and metaphorical images.

Stagno’s and Bolzoni’s Camillo scholarship likewise favors historically specific reconstruction over a single occult-memory lineage.

## 7. Ong and Ramism

Walter J. Ong’s *Ramus, Method, and the Decay of Dialogue* explains the sixteenth-century movement from rhetorical and dialectical disputation toward spatially tabulated “method.”

Ramus rejected or marginalized the ornate image-and-place art in favor of dialectical order and schematic division. Albert is not a Ramist precursor. The contrast is instructive:

- Albert: sensory images, individual affect, associative places, prudential recovery;
- Ramism: dichotomous division, visible method, curricular order, reduced dependence on bizarre images.

## 8. Lull scholarship: Bonner and Fidora

Anthony Bonner and Alexander Fidora have corrected accounts that treat Ramon Llull’s Art as merely an advanced mnemonic.

Llull’s letters, figures, and rotating combinations served demonstrative, theological, missionary, and logical purposes. Memory could aid their use, but combination is not identical with the Herenian place-image technique.

Therefore:

- Albert’s art and Llull’s Art belong to overlapping cultures of ordered cognition;
- no evidence shows Albert’s *ars memorandi* using Lullian wheels;
- histories from Lull to Leibniz and computing must not absorb Albert’s technique into the combinatorial lineage without distinction.

## 9. Bruno scholarship: Sturlese, Ciliberto, Clucas, Gatti

Post-Yates Bruno scholarship has complicated the thesis of a unitary “Hermetic tradition.”

- Michele Ciliberto emphasizes Bruno’s philosophy, language, civic-religious ambitions, and historical transformations.
- Rita Sturlese’s philological work distinguishes texts, editions, chronology, and sources.
- Stephen Clucas studies Bruno’s mnemonic and magical languages while resisting reduction to one Hermetic key.
- Hilary Gatti emphasizes Bruno’s philosophy, cosmology, and intellectual setting.

These scholars do not establish an Albertine occult-memory school at Naples. Bruno’s invocation of Albertus reflects Albert’s enormous authority in natural philosophy and in works genuinely or spuriously attributed to him. It does not prove that Bruno inherited his wheels from Albert’s ram.

## History and context

## 1. Classical rhetoric

The remote ancestor is the story of Simonides identifying banquet victims by remembering where each had sat.

- **Tradition:** transmitted by Cicero, Quintilian, and others.
- **Historical core:** Simonides was an ancient poet associated with memory.
- **Legendary element:** the precise banquet catastrophe and divine intervention cannot be independently verified.
- **Mnemonic lesson:** order preserves identities after appearances have been destroyed.

The mature architectural method is documented in the *Ad Herennium*, Cicero’s *De oratore*, and Quintilian XI.2.

## 2. Early medieval memoria

Medieval memory was cultivated through:

- memorization of Scripture and Psalms;
- monastic meditation;
- numerical and calendrical ordering;
- divisions and headings;
- page layout;
- concordances;
- florilegia;
- alphabetic indexing;
- allegorical buildings, trees, ladders, arks, and diagrams.

This broader culture did not depend solely on continuous access to the *Ad Herennium*. Albert’s significance lies in bringing the classical technical recipe into a developed scholastic psychology.

## 3. Bologna and the *ars dictaminis*

Yates suggests contact with the Bolognese rhetorical tradition, especially Boncompagno da Signa.

Her chain is:

- Albert entered the Dominicans in 1223;
- he probably studied at the Dominican house in Bologna;
- Bologna was a center of legal rhetoric and letter-writing;
- Boncompagno praised the preaching orders;
- therefore Albert likely knew this environment.

This is plausible context but not direct documentary transmission. No surviving note says that Boncompagno taught Albert the art.

## 4. Paris and the university

*De bono* probably arose from disputed-question teaching in Paris between ca. 1240 and 1246/47. It belongs to Albert’s early moral-theological summa project and precedes his mature commentaries.

The work’s form—objections, authorities, solutions—is university procedure. Artificial memory is not presented as a secret imparted to initiates but as a public scholastic problem subject to rational objections.

## 5. Dominican institutions

For Dominicans, memory was central to:

- Biblical study;
- preaching without continual dependence on a book;
- organizing authorities and distinctions;
- disputation;
- moral self-examination;
- pastoral application of general principles to particular cases.

Albert’s conclusion broadens the user group:

> *Dicimus, quod ars memorandi optima est, quam tradit Tullius, et praecipue in memorabilibus pertinentibus ad vitam et iudicium, et illae memoriae praecipue pertinent ad ethicum et rhetorem…*

“We say that the best art of remembering is that which Tullius teaches, especially for memorable matters pertaining to life and judgment; such memories pertain chiefly to the moral philosopher and the speaker.”  
—*De bono* IV, q. 2, art. 2, sol.; ed. Col. XXVIII, pp. 251–252.

Yates interprets *ethicus et rhetor* as a warrant for later lay use. That is reasonable but indirect. Albert names functions, not clerical estates: ethical agent and orator.

## 6. Thomas Aquinas

Thomas studied under Albert at Cologne and later treated memory in:

- his commentary on Aristotle’s *De memoria et reminiscentia*;
- *Summa theologiae* II–II, q. 49, art. 1, ad 2.

Thomas’s four mnemonic precepts are:

1. choose suitable, unusual corporeal similitudes;
2. arrange what is to be remembered in a considered order;
3. apply solicitude and affection;
4. meditate frequently.

He states:

> *Intentiones simplices et spirituales facilius ex anima elabuntur, nisi quibusdam similitudinibus corporalibus quasi alligentur.*

“Simple and spiritual intentions slip more easily from the soul unless they are bound, as it were, to corporeal similitudes.”

Thomas continues Albert’s integration but is less entangled in the corrupt lawsuit imagery.

Yates’s contrast—Thomas is cautious while Albert verges on “superstitious awe”—is rhetorically sharp but not a neutral description. Both authors naturalize mnemonics within Aristotelian psychology; neither makes it ritual magic.

## 7. Later medieval reception

Albert and Thomas became authorities for later mnemonic writers, including preachers, jurists, students, notaries, and lay intellectuals. The tradition appears in:

- Thomas Bradwardine;
- fifteenth-century Central European mnemonic manuscripts;
- Jacobus Publicius;
- Petrus Ravennas;
- Johannes Romberch;
- Cosmas Rossellius.

Transmission was rarely a pure Albertine school. Writers combined classical rules, scholastic psychology, alphabets, numerical schemes, grammar, devotional imagery, and increasingly printed diagrams.

## People

### Albertus Magnus

A Dominican theologian, philosopher, naturalist, teacher, and later bishop of Regensburg. The traditional entry date into the Dominicans is 1223, though details of his earliest biography rely on later sources.

His authorship of *De bono* and the *De memoria* paraphrase is secure. His authorship of many magical, alchemical, astrological, and occult books printed under his name is not.

### Thomas Aquinas

Documented student of Albert. Early biographies praise his extraordinary memory and claim:

- as a student he retained what his teacher said;
- he remembered material read in different monasteries;
- he could draw upon it in compiling the *Catena aurea*.

These reports are hagiographical testimonies, not controlled demonstrations. The claim that Albert personally “trained Thomas’s memory” is plausible tradition rather than a documented lesson record.

### Boncompagno da Signa

An earlier Bolognese rhetorician who treated natural and artificial memory. Yates sees him as contextual preparation for Albert. Direct teacher-pupil transmission is unproved.

### Hugh of Saint-Victor and John of Garland

Important predecessors in ordered learning and rhetorical memoria. They help disprove the idea that Albert created medieval artificial memory ex nihilo.

### Petrarch

Yates imagines that Petrarch could have used pagan triumphal images under Albert’s authority for striking metaphorical images. This is suggestive interpretation. No surviving Petrarchan note documents an Albertine palace populated by the *Trionfi*.

### Giordano Bruno

A former Dominican who named Albertus among authoritative writers on memory and defended possession of a book of magical images by appealing to Albert’s authority.

That defense is documented in the Bruno trial materials. It proves rhetorical appeal to Albert’s reputation, not dependence on *De bono* IV, q. 2.

### The requested anecdotes

The following belong to the wider catalogue, not securely to Albert’s system:

- **The auctioneer:** associated with later professional mnemonic demonstrations or classical anecdotes, not Albert’s texts.
- **The professor’s party:** not found in the consulted Albertine primary texts.
- **The student who lost his memory:** a late biographical tradition says Albert himself suffered a catastrophic loss of memory near the end of his life, sometimes while lecturing in 1278. The story is hagiographical and poorly documented by contemporary evidence.
- **The accused magician:** accusations of magical methods belong more directly to later mnemonic teachers such as Lambert Schenkel, and to Bruno’s Inquisition context. Albert acquired a posthumous reputation as a magician but was not prosecuted for his *ars memorandi*.
- **Absence finding:** none of these anecdotes can responsibly be presented as a documented episode of someone practicing Albert’s *De bono* mnemonic.

## The magic question

## 1. Is the system itself magical?

No, not in its stated theory or operations.

The causal account is naturalistic by medieval standards:

- perception produces corporeal forms;
- imagination retains or composes phantasms;
- estimation attaches intentions;
- memory conserves them;
- rational recollection searches an ordered series;
- emotionally forceful images adhere more strongly.

There are:

- no invocations;
- no angelic names;
- no consecrations;
- no astrological election;
- no talisman;
- no claim of infused knowledge;
- no promise of learning a science without study.

## 2. Comparison with the *Ars notoria*

The *Ars notoria* is a ritual-magic tradition promising rapid or divinely assisted acquisition of arts and sciences through prayers, observances, and inspection of complex *notae* or figures.

Its superficial similarities to artificial memory are:

- concern with memory and learning;
- visual forms;
- disciplined inspection;
- claims to organize the sciences.

Its decisive differences are causal and ritual. Albert’s art strengthens natural faculties through practice. The *Ars notoria* seeks extraordinary infusion or angelic assistance.

Thomas Aquinas condemned the *Ars notoria* as illicit and superstitious in *Summa theologiae* II–II, q. 96, art. 1.

**Yates’s broader hypothesis:** the *Ars notoria* may descend from, or preserve a magicalized branch of, classical mnemonic *notae*.

**Status:** disputed and unproved. Similar vocabulary does not establish direct descent.

## 3. The ram as Aries

Yates asks whether Albert’s ram could be Aries and the image a stellar or talismanic one. She immediately signals the chronological and evidentiary difficulty.

- **Documented:** Albert describes a ram with horns and testicles.
- **Documented:** the ram derives intelligibly from the lawsuit image.
- **No evidence:** the word *Aries* as zodiacal sign, a planetary correspondence, or a talismanic operation in the mnemonic passage.
- **Verdict:** Yates’s playful speculation, frequently reified by later occult retellings; not a supported reading.

## 4. Albert as magician

Albert’s posthumous magical reputation grew from several causes:

- genuine expertise in natural philosophy, minerals, plants, astrology, and occult qualities;
- discussion of licit and illicit magic;
- attribution of spurious works, including *Secreta Alberti*, *De secretis mulierum*, alchemical texts, charms, and books of experiments;
- fifteenth-century Dominican vitae;
- early modern print marketing;
- later *Grand Albert* and *Petit Albert* traditions.

David J. Collins’s work shows that Dominicans themselves participated in shaping Albert’s “Magnus/Magus” afterlife, partly in contexts of Observant reform.

This afterlife must be separated from *De bono*. The authenticated mnemonic chapter neither identifies itself as a secret nor attracted an accusation of heresy.

## 5. Consequences

There is no known inquisitorial proceeding against Albert for artificial memory. His canonization history, orthodoxy, and Dominican authority instead helped legitimate natural mnemonic practice.

Bruno’s later appeal to Albert shows the reverse problem: Albert’s prestige could be invoked to defend material that Albert may not have written or endorsed.

## Afterlife

## 1. Dante and monumental art

Yates proposed that medieval schemes of virtues, vices, Hell, Purgatory, and Paradise could act as memory places and images, and she connected this possibility with Dante, Giotto, and moral fresco cycles.

Later scholarship accepts that:

- medieval literature and monumental art are deeply structured by memoria;
- ordered spaces, personifications, exemplary histories, and affective images support recollection and meditation;
- Dominican and Franciscan preaching interacted with visual programs.

But a direct claim such as “Dante built the *Commedia* with Albert’s five place rules” is unproved. Similar structures may arise from shared rhetorical, exegetical, liturgical, scholastic, and architectural cultures.

## 2. Emblem books and the Renaissance image

Albert’s defense of metaphorical, marvelous, corporeal images supplied authoritative language compatible with:

- preaching exempla;
- personifications;
- imprese;
- emblems;
- triumphal imagery;
- galleries and theaters;
- illustrated memory manuals.

Compatibility is not direct descent. Sixteenth-century emblem culture has its own humanist, hieroglyphic, numismatic, mythographic, and print histories.

## 3. Camillo

Giulio Camillo’s Memory Theatre uses a physical or imagined theater arranged in grades, with planetary and mythological images.

Albert contributes only deep background:

- place-image architecture;
- confidence in corporeal metaphor;
- ordered ascent or traversal;
- legitimating intellectual authority.

Camillo’s seven planetary grades and its Neoplatonic-Hermetic cosmology are not in Albert.

## 4. Bruno

Bruno preserves the distinction between mnemonic subject or place and attached image while transforming it through:

- Lullian-style rotating combinations;
- mythological and astral imagery;
- philosophical “shadows”;
- magical and cosmological theories;
- enormous combinatorial alphabets.

Yates’s proposed Albertist continuity is possible only at the general Dominican and mnemonic level. No textual chain connects the ram passage with Bruno’s wheels.

## 5. Ramus

Ramist method displaced spectacular associative memory in many educational environments with branching topical order. It represents an alternative reorganization of memory, logic, and pedagogy, not a refinement of Albert’s images.

## 6. Leibniz and the machine

The defensible genealogy is branching:

```text
Classical loci and images
        │
        ├── Albert and Thomas: psychology, prudence, rhetoric
        │       └── later architectural and image-based mnemonics
        │
        └── combinatorial and topical traditions
                ├── Ramon Llull
                ├── Renaissance wheels and universal arts
                ├── universal-language projects
                └── Leibnizian characteristic and calculus
```

The line “Albert → Lull → Leibniz → computer” is misleading:

- Llull was Albert’s contemporary, not Albert’s mnemonic descendant.
- Lullian combination is structurally different from storing images in loci.
- Leibniz drew on logical, algebraic, encyclopedic, and combinatorial sources.
- Modern computing uses addressable storage and symbolic operations, but resemblance to loci is analogical, not a documented technological descent.

## 7. Borges, Eco, and modern literature

Borges’s “Funes the Memorious” explores perfect retention without abstraction. It illuminates, by contrast, why Albert values rational recollection rather than indiscriminate storage. Funes is not based specifically on Albert.

Umberto Eco repeatedly engages lists, encyclopedias, signs, labyrinths, medieval learning, and universal-language projects. His intellectual genealogy is closer to semiotics, Llull, combinatorics, and encyclopedism than to Albert’s ram.

## 8. Modern memory palaces and athletes

Modern competitors still use recognizable descendants of:

- ordered familiar routes;
- vivid interacting images;
- phonetic and numerical encodings;
- repeated retrieval practice.

This confirms the practical plausibility of the core place-image method. It does not validate Albert’s ventricular physiology, humoral theory, or moral classification.

“Memory palace” is now a broad popular label. Albert himself speaks of places, fields, temples, hospitals, and buildings; he does not name one universal palace.

## Controversies and disputes

## 1. Did Albert invent the system?

No.

- **Documented:** the technical rules come overwhelmingly from the *Ad Herennium*.
- **Albert’s innovation:** philosophical reconciliation and ethical authorization.
- **Legend/modern simplification:** “Albertus Magnus invented the memory palace.”

## 2. Was he the first to combine Aristotle and Cicero?

- **Yates:** probably yes, cautiously stated.
- **Evidence for:** Albert’s discussion is early, explicit, and unusually complete.
- **Evidence against absolute priority:** earlier school traditions already combined ordering, recollection, rhetoric, and moral training; unedited glosses may predate him.
- **Verdict:** first major surviving systematic synthesis, not demonstrably the first occurrence.

## 3. Were his loci exclusively real?

- **Yates:** deduces real buildings from Albert’s rejection of imaginary camps and cities.
- **Text:** favors bounded, manageable loci and accepts corporeal/imaginable places.
- **Verdict:** real, previously known environments are preferred; an absolute prohibition of constructed mental places is not established.

## 4. Did he prescribe churches?

- **Yates:** suggests a church because solemn and rare places move strongly.
- **Text:** gives temple among possible examples but also field and hospital.
- **Verdict:** church/temple is one possible locus system, not the prescribed Albertine architecture.

## 5. Does an image of Justice contain an intention to seek Justice?

- **Albert:** images contain associated *intentiones*; wolf-danger is the secure model.
- **Yates:** invents Justice as an explanatory example and labels it her deduction.
- **Verdict:** sound illustration of Albert’s theory, but not a quotation or worked example from Albert.

## 6. Is the ram mystical?

- **Text:** an image used while recollecting material from a lawsuit.
- **Yates:** “mystical obscurity,” “mysterious vision.”
- **Alternative reading:** ordinary advice about sensory withdrawal fused with a corrupted mnemonic scene.
- **Verdict:** “mystical” describes Yates’s response, not an explicit theological category.

## 7. Is the ram Aries?

- **Evidence:** none in the passage.
- **Origin of reading:** Yates’s speculative question in 1966.
- **Spread:** occult histories and popular summaries that omit her question marks.
- **Verdict:** unsupported.

## 8. Did Albert’s melancholy theory make mnemonics an inspired gift?

- **Text:** hot/dry melancholy can favor mobile recollection.
- **Yates:** perhaps *ars reminiscendi* is a prerogative of inspired melancholics.
- **Problem:** artificial memory is defined as a trainable rational habit; restricting it to a temperament contradicts its general pedagogical function.
- **Verdict:** temperament may affect aptitude; exclusive inspired status is not documented.

## 9. Was the technique heretical?

No known medieval authority condemned Albert’s place-image technique as heretical.

Confusion arises from combining it with:

- the *Ars notoria*;
- astral talismans;
- necromancy;
- spurious magical books attributed to Albert;
- Bruno’s magical memory.

These are historically adjacent but doctrinally distinct.

## 10. Are there Albertine diagrams?

No securely identified mnemonic diagram accompanies either authenticated treatment.

Images of Köln Cod. 1024’s decorated opening document a manuscript of *De bono*, not a diagram of its memory system. Wheels reproduced beside discussions of Albert generally belong to Llull, Publicius, Romberch, Rossellius, Bruno, or later authors.

## 11. Did Albert personally train Thomas in the art?

Thomas studied under Albert. Thomas later taught closely related mnemonic principles. Early biographies praise Thomas’s memory.

No surviving testimony records an Albertine memory lesson or gives its exercises. “Albert trained Thomas’s artificial memory at Cologne” is plausible tradition, not documented fact.

## 12. Did *De bono* circulate widely as a mnemonic manual?

Probably not as an independent manual.

- The complete work survives in a limited manuscript tradition.
- Its memory section was embedded in a moral-theological question.
- Later authority may have travelled through excerpts, scholastic commonplaces, Thomas, and general attribution rather than direct copying of IV, q. 2.
- A reception study must distinguish citation of “Albertus” from direct dependence on this chapter.

---

## Open questions

1. **Priority:** Do pre-1240 glosses on the *Ad Herennium* explicitly identify artificial memory with Aristotelian *reminiscentia*?
2. **Albert’s exemplar:** Can the precise manuscript family of the corrupt *medico*, *ultionem*, and Iphigenia readings be reconstructed?
3. **Relation between the two works:** Did Albert consciously revise the bedside lawsuit image into the charging ram, or was the commentary relying on a different corrupt rhetorical witness?
4. **Chronology:** How much time separates *De bono* from *De memoria et reminiscentia*, and did Albert’s faculty psychology materially change?
5. **Manuscript use:** Are there marginal diagrams, place lists, or reader annotations beside IV, q. 2 in any of the eleven known *De bono* witnesses?
6. **Classroom reception:** Was the passage excerpted in Dominican *studia*, preaching manuals, or university collections?
7. **Thomas:** Can verbal parallels demonstrate direct dependence on *De bono*, rather than common dependence on Cicero, the *Ad Herennium*, and Aristotle?
8. **Lay reception:** When is Albert first explicitly cited by a lay mnemonic author as authority for metaphorical images?
9. **Magic:** When was Yates’s tentative Aries question first repeated as a positive assertion?
10. **Spurious Albertina:** Which pseudo-Albertine magical works were read alongside authentic psychological works, and by whom?
11. **Humoral practice:** Did any practitioner adapt Albert’s hot/dry melancholy theory into regimen—diet, sleep, environment, or medication—for mnemonic training?
12. **Visual culture:** Can any specific Dominican pictorial program be linked through documentary evidence to *De bono* IV, q. 2 rather than to the general culture of preaching and memoria?

## Conclusions

Albertus Magnus’s *ars memorandi* is neither a secret medieval memory palace of his invention nor an occult wheel. It is a philosophically ambitious defense of the classical art of places and images.

Its historical importance lies in four moves:

1. Albert places trained memory within prudence.
2. He interprets artificial memory as rational recollection rather than passive storage.
3. He explains mnemonic images through the internal senses and their capacity to retain *intentiones*.
4. He defends extraordinary metaphorical images because affective wonder makes them adhere.

Yates identified these moves with exceptional clarity. Her treatment remains indispensable for the textual drama: the corrupt physician, the charging ram, the imagines agentes entering ethics, and the contrast with Thomas. The chief corrections are equally clear:

- the church is conjectural;
- the Justice example is hers;
- “first” requires qualification;
- inspired melancholy is not an exclusive qualification;
- the ram is not demonstrably Aries;
- no Albertine magical memory school at Bruno’s Naples convent has been documented;
- and Albert’s system contains no wheels, diagrams, or talismans.

The enduring Albertine principle is simpler and more consequential: rational judgment depends on recoverable experience, and recoverable experience is strengthened when abstract intentions are deliberately bound to ordered, corporeal, emotionally effective forms.
