# The English friars’ invisible pictures: Ridevall and Holcot: Research Dossier

## Basic identification

| Field | Identification |
|---|---|
| Catalogue number | System 13 of 45 |
| Conventional name | The English friars’ invisible pictures |
| Exponents | John Ridevall, O.F.M.; Robert Holcot, O.P. |
| Date | Oxford, chiefly the 1330s; Holcot’s *Moralitates* probably after c. 1334 and before 1342 |
| Principal texts | Ridevall, *Fulgencius/Fulgentius metaforalis*; Holcot, commentary on the Twelve Minor Prophets, especially Hosea and Nahum; *Moralitates*; related passages in *Super Sapientiam* |
| Classical point of departure | Fulgentius, *Mitologiae*; the Third Vatican Mythographer; Remigius of Auxerre on Martianus Capella; biblical, patristic and classical commonplace collections |
| Principle in one sentence | A preacher compresses the divisions of a moral or exegetical discourse into the conspicuous attributes of a verbally described personification—often reinforced by rhyme, inscriptions and verbal echoes with Scripture—so that reconstructing the figure helps reconstruct the sermon. |
| Yates | Frances A. Yates, *The Art of Memory* (1966), ch. IV, pp. 96–99; plate 4c |
| Present critical verdict | Holcot’s pictures are securely reconstructable as preacherly compositional and mnemonic devices; Ridevall’s figures were likewise memorable, indexed moralizations, but Yates’s presentation of his whole mythography as a formal artificial-memory “system” goes beyond the surviving instructions. Neither author supplies an Ad Herennium-style sequence of loci or a formal rulebook for constructing places. |

### Evidentiary labels used below

- **[Text]** Directly stated or materially present in a medieval or early printed source.
- **[Manuscript]** Codicological evidence.
- **[Reconstruction]** A modern scholar’s inference from textual structure or transmission.
- **[Yates]** Frances Yates’s interpretation.
- **[Disputed]** An attribution, date or functional interpretation for which serious alternatives exist.
- **[Tradition]** A premodern report repeated without secure access to its alleged ancient source.
- **[Legend]** A story unsupported by contemporary evidence.
- **[Modern invention]** A recent embellishment or conflation not found in the historical witnesses.
- **[Negative finding]** No evidence was located for the proposed feature.

The distinction matters especially here. “Invisible pictures” is Yates’s memorable category, not Ridevall’s or Holcot’s name for a formally defined art.

## The system in detail

### 1. What kind of “system” is it?

**[Text]** Ridevall and Holcot repeatedly present a figure and enumerate its attributes. Each bodily feature, garment, object, defect or inscription is then interpreted as one division of a virtue, vice or sermon topic.

**[Reconstruction]** The basic retrieval path is:

> sermon theme → remembered personification → ordered bodily attributes or clauses → moral propositions → supporting authorities and biblical texts.

This is a system of **topical compression and sermon composition**, not demonstrably a complete classical place-system.

**[Negative finding]** Neither Ridevall’s *Fulgencius metaforalis* nor Holcot’s *Moralitates* gives rules comparable to *Ad Herennium* III.16–24 for:

- selecting and spacing architectural loci;
- numbering loci;
- repeatedly walking a fixed route;
- distinguishing background places from deposited images;
- erasing and reusing images;
- training through daily rehearsal;
- translating every word of a continuous text into images.

**[Yates]** Yates nevertheless interpreted the personifications through the classical theory of *imagines agentes*: striking, grotesque, mobile images impressed upon memory. For Holcot’s Hosea commentary she proposed an additional page-place system: the biblical page supplies the locus and the personification supplies the image.

**[Later scholarship]** Kimberly Rivers, Mary Carruthers, John T. Slotemaker and Jeffrey C. Witt substantially support a mnemonic and compositional function for Holcot’s pictures, but describe it more precisely as medieval preaching practice. Ralph Hanna’s complete edition sharply qualifies claims that Ridevall’s work circulated as “memory diagrams”: its medieval tabula is better evidence for consultation and excerpting by preachers. [Liverpool University Press’s description of Hanna’s edition](https://utpdistribution.com/9781836245209/john-ridewall-fulgentius-metaforalis/) explicitly contrasts manuscript consultation with the frequently asserted “set of memory diagrams.”

### 2. Ridevall’s *Fulgencius metaforalis*

#### Form and organization

**[Text]** The complete work, as reconstructed by Ralph Hanna, consists of two books:

**Book I**

1. Idolatry  
2. Saturn / Prudence  
3. Jupiter / Love and Friendship  
4. Juno / Memory  
5. Neptune / Intelligence  
6. Pluto / Foresight  
7. Apollo / Truth  
8. Phaethon / Ambition  
9. Mercury / Eloquence  
10. Danaë / Modesty and Women’s Greed  
11. Ganymede / Sodomy  
12. Perseus / Courage  
13. Alcestis / Marital Continence  

**Book II**

1. Paris / Injustice  
2. Minerva / Contemplative Life  
3. Juno / Worldly Life  
4. Venus / The Life of Pleasure  

There are also two inserted discussions in the received text.

This is important because Hans Liebeschütz’s 1926 edition, the text available to Smalley and Yates, printed only the opening portion. Hanna established that its base witness, Venice, Biblioteca Nazionale Marciana, MS Z. lat. 494 (1790), preserves roughly one-third of the work. [Hanna’s edition and contents](https://www.jstor.org/stable/jj.38238767).

#### The image of Idolatry

Ridevall’s compact descriptive verse is:

> **Mulier notata, oculis orbata, aure mutilata, cornu ventilata, vultu deformata et morbo vexata.**

—Ridevall, *Fulgencius metaforalis*, I.1; Liebeschütz, 1926, in the chapter *Ydolatria*.

A close gloss is:

> “A marked woman, deprived of her eyes, mutilated in the ear, proclaimed by a horn, deformed in face, and afflicted by disease.”

“Prostitute” is supplied by the ensuing interpretation and by Yates/Smalley’s paraphrase; *mulier notata* itself means a notorious, branded or marked woman rather than lexically “prostitute.”

**[Text]** The six clauses establish a determinate sequence. Ridevall then moralizes them:

1. the dishonoured or meretricious woman signifies idolatry’s infidelity;
2. blindness signifies error and failure to perceive divine truth;
3. the mutilated ear signifies failure to hear or obey God’s word;
4. the sounding horn publicizes criminality or infamy;
5. the deformed face signifies disfigurement of the divine likeness;
6. disease signifies disordered or excessive attachment.

**[Reconstruction]** Rhyme and parallel morphology—*notata/orbata/mutilata/ventilata/deformata/vexata*—make the order orally recoverable. Remembering the verse regenerates the six headings; remembering each attribute regenerates its exposition.

**[Yates]** Yates calls it an invented, moving memory image and treats each feature as a mnemonic cue.

**[Qualification]** The text itself does not say “put this image in a locus,” “use it to memorize,” or “retrieve the sermon by mentally inspecting it.” The mnemonic function is strongly plausible from its compact rhyme and preacherly structure, but the explicit operational language belongs chiefly to modern reconstruction.

#### The gods as moral schemata

The opening sequence associates pagan gods with the three temporal parts of prudence:

- Saturn: *prudentia*;
- Juno: *memoria*;
- Neptune: *intelligentia*;
- Pluto: *providentia*.

The scheme draws upon the Ciceronian division of prudence into memory, understanding and foresight, but it is not merely a one-to-one table. Each deity’s traditional appearance and narrative are decomposed into further attributes.

For Pluto, the verse printed by Liebeschütz reads:

> **Ligno coronatus, opibus dilatatus, inferis praelatus, Cerbero delatus, aetati ligatus, Furiis armatus, et Fatis vallatus.**

A gloss:

> “Crowned with wood, enlarged by riches, set over the underworld, attended by Cerberus, bound to age, armed with the Furies, and enclosed by the Fates.”

Ridevall then says:

> **Istae sunt partes septem picturae Plutonicae, quibus correspondent totidem proprietates providentiae.**

> “These are the seven parts of the Plutonic picture, to which correspond the same number of properties of foresight.”

This is exceptionally strong evidence for an ordered image-to-doctrine mapping. A contemporary review of Liebeschütz already warned, however, that *pingere* and *pictura* may be metaphorical here rather than instructions for a material illustration. [Review in *Revue belge de Philologie et d’Histoire*](https://www.persee.fr/doc/rbph_0035-0818_1928_num_7_2_6518_t1_0609_0000_2).

#### Order and retrieval

Ridevall’s order operates at several levels:

1. **[Text]** book and chapter sequence;
2. **[Text]** a deity or personification as the chapter’s principal organizing figure;
3. **[Text]** a rhyming descriptive verse;
4. **[Text]** a fixed series of attributes;
5. **[Text]** a corresponding series of moral properties;
6. **[Manuscript]** later alphabetical tabulae enabling readers to find moral themes independently of the mythological chapter;
7. **[Reconstruction]** preacherly extraction: consult the tabula, locate an apt *moralitas* or *integumentum*, and incorporate it into a sermon.

The last point is supported by Hanna’s edition of the “A tabula.” Hanna interprets it as helping a user locate useful moralizations not readily found from the names of mythological figures—most probably a preacher collecting amplificatory material. The tabula is an access technology, but not a revolving mnemonic wheel.

### 3. Holcot’s rhymed pictures in the Minor Prophets

#### Type I: rhymed, enumerative pictures

**[Text]** Holcot’s commentary on the Twelve Prophets contains twenty-six rhymed pictures not duplicated in the *Moralitates*. Slotemaker and Witt follow Kimberly Rivers in calling these “Type I”: compact rhymed descriptions whose clauses lead into ordered explanations.

The most fully analyzed modern example is the picture of Patience in the commentary on Nahum 1:3, *Dominus patiens*.

**[Text/Reconstruction]** Its ten attributes cue ten properties:

1. sitting contentedly → sufficiency or contentment;
2. fed with common food → endurance of conditions that nourish patience;
3. joyful face → joy;
4. turned toward everyone → patience toward all, including enemies;
5. clothed in purple → mercy, kindness, modesty and humility;
6. defended from enemies → silence and peace during trouble;
7. one hand shrivelled, the other extended → readiness to endure;
8. without feet → completion or perfection through trials;
9. without a tongue → disciplined speech;
10. without ears → ignoring or forgiving insults.

Holcot often links the wording of a clause to the wording of a biblical authority. The cue is therefore simultaneously visual, rhythmic and verbal. Slotemaker and Witt show, for example, that *extenta* can recall passages using *extendere*. Their conclusion is that a friar could recover both a division of the theme and the scriptural text supporting it. [Slotemaker and Witt, discussion of the pictures](https://dokumen.pub/robert-holcot-great-medieval-thinkers-1nbsped-9780199391240-9780199391257-0199391246.html).

#### The page as place

**[Text]** Holcot introduces pictures at specific textual lemmata in his running commentary. Idolatry appears where Hosea supplies a relevant cue; Cupid appears where the prophetic text invites a treatment of love.

**[Yates]** From this arrangement Yates concluded that Holcot “places such images, in imagination, on the pages of a Scriptural text.” The page becomes a locus, the picture an image, and its attributes the heads of the intended exposition.

**[Qualification]** What is directly documented is attachment of a *pictura* to a textual lemma. The stronger claim that Holcot internally visualized a physical manuscript opening and deposited a moving Cupid upon it is Yates’s psychological reconstruction. No surviving instruction says to memorize page coordinates, margins, ruling or openings in the manner of a formal page-grid mnemonic.

A safer description is **textual localization**: Hosea’s words provide an ordered sequence of retrieval cues because the commentary follows Scripture in order.

#### Cupid

Yates writes:

> “He even places on the text of the prophet an image of Cupid, complete with bow and arrows!”

—Yates, *Art of Memory*, ch. IV, p. 99.

**[Text]** Cupid and his attributes are moralized in Holcot’s commentary.

**[Yates]** Its importance for her lies in a Christian friar using a vivid pagan god as a working mnemonic image rather than merely mentioning a classical fable.

**[Negative finding]** No evidence was found that the Cupid figure was used as a talisman, invoked as a spirit, or believed to exert occult power.

### 4. Holcot’s *Moralitates*

#### Function and date

**[Text]** The *Moralitates* are a repertory of moralized stories and pictures for preachers.

**[Dating reconstruction]** Smalley placed them after the less developed picture-technique of the Twelve Prophets commentary, c. 1334, and before the compiler of the earliest *Gesta Romanorum* witness used them by 1342. This yields approximately 1334–1342. The argument depends on direction of borrowing and remains a reconstruction rather than a dated colophon.

Manuscripts vary substantially:

- Basel, Universitätsbibliothek, A II 26: twenty items, fols. 138rb–140va;
- Basel, A V 33: forty-one;
- Basel, B VIII 10: forty-five;
- Madrid, Universidad Complutense, BH MSS 133: forty-four, probably once more;
- Basel 1586 printing: forty-eight.

The Basel manuscripts also differ in ordering. [Notes on the Basel witnesses](https://slotemaker.wordpress.com/category/holcots-moralitates/); [Madrid catalogue](https://manipulus.ucm.es/handle/20.500.12238/189?mode=full).

#### Type II pictures

Rivers calls the shorter, generally unrhymed pictures in the *Moralitates* “Type II.” They frequently abandon a tightly enumerated verse for a prose description followed by interpretation.

This is not necessarily a decline in mnemonic sophistication. It may reflect a different use: a preacher selects a compact allegorical unit from a repertory rather than reconstructing a long exegetical lecture.

### 5. Penance

Holcot’s Latin begins:

> **Imago penitentie, quam depinxerunt sacerdotes dee Veste secundum Remigium: penitentia depingebatur ad modum unius hominis nudati per totum corpus, qui tenebat in manu sua quoddam flagellum, quinque folia ferens, in quo scribebantur quinque versus sive sententie …**

—*Moralitates*, Moralitas XXII in Basel 1586, p. 728, as transcribed by Smalley.

Gloss:

> “The image of Penance, which the priests of the goddess Vesta painted, according to Remigius: Penance was painted in the form of a man naked over his whole body, holding in his hand a scourge bearing five strips, on which five verses or sentences were written …”

**[Text]** The five strips physically segment the five statements. A remembered body and object thus provide hierarchy:

> Penance → scourge → five strips → five written propositions.

**[Tradition/fictional attribution]** Holcot attributes the figure to Vesta’s priests and Remigius.

**[Source criticism]** Smalley checked Remigius of Auxerre’s commentary on Martianus Capella and found no such passage; moreover, nothing in the relevant classical narrative naturally yields Christian penance. “According to Remigius” is therefore pseudonymous authority, confusion or deliberate antiquizing—not documentation of an ancient image.

**[Yates]** This “sham antique” quality confirms for Yates that Holcot invented a memorable figure while giving it classical prestige.

**[Manuscript]** A fifteenth-century illustrated witness shows a naked man holding a nail scourge and a larger whip, inscriptions running along the strands. The same complex includes another Penance as an armed man and associated mnemonic verses. [Casanatense manuscript description](https://www.omifacsimiles.com/brochures/cima39.pdf).

### 6. Friendship

Holcot’s description reads:

> **Pictura amoris sive amicitiae. Narrat Fulgentius in quodam libro de gestis Romanorum quod Romani verum amorem sive veram amicitiam hoc modo descripserunt, scilicet: quod imago amoris vel amicitiae depicta erat instar iuvenis cuiusdam valde pulchri, induti habitu viridi. Facies eius et caput discooperta erant sive nudata, et in fronte ipsius erat hoc scriptum: HYEMS ET AESTAS. Erat latus eius apertum, ita ut videretur cor, in quo scripta erant haec verba: LONGE ET PROPE. Et in fimbria vestimenti eius erat scriptum: MORS ET VITA. Similiter ista imago habebat pedes nudos.**

—Basel: Jacob Ryter, 1586, Moralitas XXVI, p. 731; the Venice 1514 edition has it as Moralitas XX, fols. 23r–v.

Gloss:

> “The picture of love or friendship. Fulgentius relates in a certain book on Roman deeds that the Romans represented true love or friendship in this way: the image was like a very beautiful young man dressed in green. His face and head were uncovered, and upon his forehead was written ‘WINTER AND SUMMER.’ His side was open so that the heart could be seen, upon which were written ‘FAR AND NEAR.’ On the hem of his clothing was written ‘DEATH AND LIFE.’ The image likewise had bare feet.”

The interpretation makes each feature a proposition:

- youth: genuine friendship must not age or fail in adversity;
- green: continuing freshness;
- uncovered face and head: true friendship cannot remain concealed;
- winter and summer: adversity and prosperity;
- exposed heart: openness between friends;
- far and near: affection survives absence;
- death and life: friendship endures beyond death;
- bare feet: readiness and freedom from impediment.

**[Text]** This is a particularly clear instance of “words on images.”

**[Yates]** She proposed that such inscriptions show what medieval writers understood by “memory for words.”

**[Qualification]** It does not encode the exact wording of an arbitrary continuous text. It stores short antithetical formulas at semantically appropriate bodily positions. In classical terminology it is closer to *memoria rerum*—remembering ordered subject matter—with selected verbal anchors.

**[False ancient authority]** The cited work by Fulgentius, *de gestis Romanorum*, is not extant and the description is absent from authentic Fulgentius. The alleged Roman antiquity is unsupported.

### 7. What are the places?

There are three defensible answers, with different evidentiary status:

1. **The body as micro-topography — [Reconstruction strongly supported].** Head, forehead, ear, face, side, heart, hand, garment hem and feet provide stable positions.
2. **The verse as serial place — [Text/Reconstruction].** Rhymed clauses impose sequence independently of pictorial space.
3. **The biblical text or page as place — [Yates, partly supported].** A scriptural lemma fixes where a picture enters the commentary; visualization of the actual page remains inferential.

There is no documented architectural itinerary. Calling this a “memory palace” would be a modernizing distortion.

## The sources

### 1. Ridevall: primary text and editions

#### Ralph Hanna, 2023

John Ridewall, *Fulgencius metaforalis*, ed. and trans. Ralph Hanna, Exeter Medieval Texts and Studies, Liverpool University Press, 2023, 464 pp., DOI 10.3828/9781837644759.

This is now the standard edition:

- complete reconstructed text;
- facing English translation;
- line numbering by book and chapter;
- manuscript variants;
- identification of biblical and non-biblical sources;
- manuscript descriptions;
- edition of a medieval alphabetical tabula;
- commentary on organization and reception.

Its principal correction is that Liebeschütz unknowingly edited a fragment.

#### Hans Liebeschütz, 1926

Hans Liebeschütz, *Fulgentius Metaforalis: Ein Beitrag zur Geschichte der antiken Mythologie im Mittelalter*, ed. Fritz Saxl, Studien der Bibliothek Warburg 4, Leipzig and Berlin: Teubner, 1926, vii + 140 pp., 32 plates.

Still valuable for:

- the first critical presentation;
- photographs of illustrated witnesses;
- the opening chapters used by Smalley and Yates;
- the appended *De imaginibus deorum*/*Libellus* material.

It is no longer adequate as an edition of the entire work.

### 4. Early printings

Bibliographies give:

- Venice, 1505, an early edition cited in older catalogues;
- Paris, 1510;
- Venice: Simon de Luere, 1514, first securely documented standalone printed *Moralitates* in recent iconographic scholarship;
- Basel: Jacob Ryter, 1586, appended to Holcot’s *Super Sapientiam*, pp. 709–748; forty-eight items.

Because numbering and order differ, citations must specify the edition. Friendship is Moralitas XX in Venice 1514 but XXVI in Basel 1586.

### 5. Surviving diagrams and illustrations

**[Negative finding]** No fourteenth-century authorial diagram, wheel or table establishing a memory system survives.

What survives instead:

- rhymed verbal descriptions;
- alphabetical tabulae;
- marginal classification as *pictura* or *imago*;
- spaces reserved for pictures;
- fifteenth-century painted realizations;
- later printed and emblematic adaptations.

**[Manuscript]** Pal. lat. 1726 contains a prefatory picture cycle, followed separately by texts. It includes two Friendship figures. One is a green-clad, barefoot youth pointing toward a red heart; another bears the antitheses *hyems et aestas*, *longe et prope*, *vita et mors*. These are materializations by later readers, not proof that Holcot required paintings.

**[Manuscript]** The Casanatense Penance image shows the naked man and inscribed scourge. Yates’s phrase “we ought not to be seeing” it means that, under her reconstruction, an image designed for internal imagination has escaped into material illustration. That is rhetorically effective but too absolute: blank illustration spaces and the eventual cycles show that medieval readers did consider externalization compatible with the texts.

## Yates’s account and scholarship since

### 1. What Yates says

Yates’s central statements are:

> “These invisible ‘pictures’ provide us with specimens of invisible memory images, held within the memory, not intended to be externalised, and being used for quite practical mnemonic purposes.”

—p. 96.

> “The memorising of words or sentences as placed or written on the memory images was perhaps what the Middle Ages understood by ‘memory for words’.”

—p. 98.

> “He even places on the text of the prophet an image of Cupid, complete with bow and arrows!”

—p. 99.

She concludes:

> “The preference of these English friars for the fables of the poets as memory images … suggests that the artificial memory may be a hitherto unsuspected medium through which pagan imagery survived in the Middle Ages.”

A searchable scan of the relevant chapter is available through the [Warburg Institute](https://resources.warburg.sas.ac.uk/mnemosyne/fq/yates_memory.pdf).

### 2. What Yates got right

**Confirmed or strengthened:**

- The images are organized collections of attributes, not ornamental literary flourishes.
- Rhyme aids retention.
- Holcot’s pictures function within preaching and composition.
- Their grotesque or conspicuous features make them memorable.
- Inscribed bodies can retain short verbal units.
- Classical gods and pseudo-classical figures were Christianized rather than simply suppressed.
- Holcot’s scriptural lemmata give the images ordered textual positions.
- Later manuscripts materially realized figures initially transmitted in words.
- The technique became an important conduit between medieval moralized mythology and Renaissance iconography.

Slotemaker and Witt reconstruct a much more exact mnemonic operation than Yates did for Holcot’s Patience: each of ten clauses cues a property and, through shared words, relevant scriptural authorities.

### 3. What requires correction

#### “None of the manuscripts is illustrated”

**[Corrected]** Yates herself immediately noted Saxl’s illustrated exceptions, so her categorical sentence really means that the main English transmission was unillustrated. Later cataloguing documents several continental illustrated complexes.

#### “Not intended to be externalised”

**[Disputed]** This is plausible for Holcot’s initial lecturing and for unillustrated English witnesses, but intention cannot be proved. Some manuscript pages reserve spaces for illustrations, and fifteenth-century readers converted descriptions into pictures. It is safer to say that the mnemonic operation did not require material painting.

#### Ridevall as a formal artificial-memory manual

**[Overstated]** Ridevall supplies moralized mythography, rhymed attribute lists and preacherly finding aids. He does not provide a classical regimen of loci. Hanna specifically cautions against treating the manuscripts as sets of “memory diagrams.”

#### “Memory for words”

**[Disputed]** Holcot’s inscriptions preserve formulas such as *hyems et aestas* or five sentences on scourge-strips. They do not demonstrate verbatim storage of long discourse. Carruthers’s category of *imagines rerum*, compositional images for the divisions of a theme, is more exact.

#### The page as literal locus

**[Plausible but unproved]** Attachment to successive scriptural lemmata is documented; internal visualization of manuscript pages is not.

#### Invented from nothing

**[Corrected]** Yates, following the difficulty earlier scholars had locating sources, called the images invented memory figures. Later work shows more extensive dependence on the Third Vatican Mythographer, Remigius, Fulgentius, biblical language, sermon collections and possibly the *Fasciculus morum*. The finished combinations may be inventive even when their components are inherited.

### 4. Smalley

Beryl Smalley, *English Friars and Antiquity in the Early Fourteenth Century* (Oxford, 1960), especially pp. 109–132 for Ridevall and 133–202 for Holcot, was Yates’s indispensable immediate source.

Smalley established:

- the Oxford friars’ unusual density of classical citation;
- the preacherly setting;
- Holcot’s “sham antique” authorities;
- the chronology of Holcot’s writings;
- the likelihood that the pictures were aural and mnemonic aids;
- the absence of the claimed Penance picture in Remigius;
- the relation to *Gesta Romanorum*.

Her approach has since been criticized as primarily descriptive and source-hunting, but its factual foundation remains substantial.

### 5. Carruthers

Mary Carruthers reframed medieval memory as an art of composition, ethical formation and invention, rather than a trick for verbatim repetition. Her concise assessment is that a textual picture can serve memory as well as a painted one because imagination can “paint” it without material constraints.

For Holcot, she calls the figures emblematic *imagines rerum*: devices connecting the divisions of Faith, Sloth, Patience or another theme. This confirms Yates’s mnemonic insight while weakening her equation with literal “memory for words.”

### 6. Rivers

Kimberly A. Rivers, *Preaching the Memory of Virtue and Vice: Memory, Images, and Preaching in the Late Middle Ages* (Turnhout: Brepols, 2010), provides the most sustained study of this specific preaching environment. [Publisher record](https://www.brepols.net/products/IS-9782503515250-1).

Her major contributions are:

- distinction between Holcot’s rhymed, enumerative Type I and shorter prose Type II pictures;
- reconstruction of the preacher’s memory;
- comparison with sermon theory and practice;
- attention to Ridevall’s *Lectura super Apocalypsim* as well as the *Fulgencius*;
- possible influence of the Franciscan *Fasciculus morum* on some forms.

### 7. Slotemaker and Witt

John T. Slotemaker and Jeffrey C. Witt, *Robert Holcot* (Oxford, 2016), pp. 233–249 on the *Moralitates* and pp. 142–162 on the Twelve Prophets, confirm that Holcot’s pictures are mnemonic but insist on their exegetical content.

Their analysis of Patience demonstrates:

- ordered cueing;
- exact links between descriptive vocabulary and biblical proof texts;
- the pictures’ role in reconstructing an exposition;
- no evidence for painted versions before the fifteenth century;
- later illustrations as a distinct stage in reception.

They also correct several inherited attributions: the *Ymagines Fulgentii* derives from Ridevall; the adjacent *Aenigmata* is anonymous rather than Holcot’s. [OUP chapter record](https://academic.oup.com/book/36275/chapter-abstract/316605877).

### 8. Hanna

Hanna’s 2023 Ridevall edition is the largest post-Yates correction:

- *Fulgencius* was about three times longer than Liebeschütz’s text suggested;
- its structure extends well beyond the prudence group;
- more than one hundred manuscripts preserve it or forms of it;
- its principal mythographic sources can be identified;
- the tabula documents preacherly retrieval;
- “memory diagram” language should not replace analysis of actual codices.

His 2021 Holcot edition likewise moves discussion from Smalley’s extracts to a critically presented Latin text with translation.

### 9. Scholarship named in the general brief but not directly determinative

Bolzoni, Rossi, Small, Ong, Bonner, Fidora, Sturlese, Ciliberto, Clucas, Gatti and Stagno are essential for Camillo, Lull, Bruno, Ramus and the larger history of memory. They do not provide major independent corrections to the Ridevall–Holcot dossier. Importing their wheels, theatres, alphabetic combinations or occult cosmologies into this case would obscure its preacherly character.

There are:

- no Lullian rotating figures;
- no Camillo theatre and graded ascent;
- no Bruno-style wheel combinations;
- no Ramist dichotomous chart;
- no Fluddian architectural theatre.

The pertinent comparison is functional, not genealogically demonstrated.

## History and context

### 1. Mendicant and university setting

Ridevall was an Oxford Franciscan and probably regent master in the 1330s. Hanna reconstructs his access to a rich institutional library, plausibly Oxford Greyfriars, from the breadth and physical pattern of his citations.

Holcot was a Dominican theologian associated with Oxford, Cambridge, Northampton and the intellectual circle of Richard de Bury. His Minor Prophets lectures belong to his Oxford regency; *Super Sapientiam* became exceptionally successful, surviving in roughly 175 manuscripts and numerous early printings. [Holcot overview](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/holkot/).

The institutions mattered:

- friars required reusable sermon material;
- university biblical lectures proceeded lemma by lemma;
- students needed to convert lectures into preaching;
- mendicant mobility rewarded compact, internally recoverable structures;
- convent libraries and commonplace books enabled extraction and recombination.

### 2. Rhetoric and scholastic psychology

The remote ancestry lies in classical rhetoric:

- *Rhetorica ad Herennium* III.16–24;
- Cicero, *De oratore* II.350–360;
- Quintilian, *Institutio oratoria* XI.2.

Those works distinguish places and images and recommend striking *imagines agentes*.

The proximate medieval setting includes:

- Aristotle’s claim that thinking occurs with phantasms;
- scholastic faculty psychology;
- Augustine’s capacious notion of memory;
- Albert the Great and Thomas Aquinas on artificial memory;
- the preacher’s division of a theme;
- exempla, distinctions and similitudes;
- lectio, meditation and mental composition.

**[Reconstruction]** Holcot’s body-pictures translate scholastic divisiones into sensuous form. They are simultaneously outlines, concordances and imaginative prompts.

### 3. Mythography

Ridevall belongs to the moralized-Fulgentian tradition:

- Fabius Planciades Fulgentius, *Mitologiae*;
- Remigius of Auxerre on Martianus Capella;
- the Third Vatican Mythographer;
- *Ovide moralisé*;
- Pierre Bersuire’s *Ovidius moralizatus*;
- later mythographic and emblematic compilations.

Hanna finds that much of Ridevall’s mythographic detail comes not directly from Fulgentius but from Remigius and the Third Vatican Mythographer.

### 4. Preaching

The method is adapted to sermon performance:

- the verse can be memorized;
- the body fixes divisions;
- each division opens into authorities;
- the moralized story interests an educated audience;
- the image can be abbreviated or expanded;
- tabulae permit topical retrieval.

By the late fourteenth and fifteenth centuries, Siegfried Wenzel’s work indicates that moralized *picturae* had become part of the general rhetorical idiom of preaching rather than a peculiarity of English friars.

## People

### John Ridevall

**[Documented]** English Franciscan, active at Oxford in the 1330s; author of *Fulgencius metaforalis* and associated with an Apocalypse commentary.

**[Disputed]** Works once or sometimes assigned to him include an Ovid commentary more usually connected with Pierre Bersuire and a commentary on Walter Map sometimes assigned to John of Wales.

**[Negative finding]** No contemporary anecdote survives of Ridevall publicly demonstrating the technique or performing feats of memory.

### Robert Holcot

**[Documented]** English Dominican theologian and biblical commentator, c. 1290–1349.

**[Tradition]** He is said to have died while ministering during the Black Death. The death date is accepted; the pastoral circumstances are transmitted as a story rather than securely established by a contemporary eyewitness.

**[Disputed]** His role in Richard de Bury’s *Philobiblon* has long been debated. It is irrelevant to the authorship of the pictures and should not be used as proof of his mnemonic practices.

### Beryl Smalley

Recovered the “classicizing friars” as an identifiable intellectual formation and supplied Yates with her primary textual framework.

### Fritz Saxl

Located and analyzed illustrated continental compilations. His 1942 “A Spiritual Encyclopaedia of the Later Middle Ages” made the later materialization of the pictures central to art-historical discussion. [Bibliographic record](https://philpapers.org/rec/SAXASE).

### Frances Yates

Synthesized Smalley and Saxl within a history of artificial memory. Her category “invisible pictures” remains illuminating, though too categorical as a statement of authorial intention.

### Practitioners, patrons, students and enemies

**[Documented collectively]** Intended and actual users were friars, university students, biblical lecturers, preachers, compilers, rubricators, indexers and later illustrators.

**[Negative finding]** No named student is documented as losing his memory through using this system. No auctioneer or professor’s-party anecdote belongs to Ridevall or Holcot. Those anecdotes derive from other memory traditions and must not be silently transferred here.

**[Negative finding]** No patron commissioned the system in the sense that Francis I patronized Camillo or Henry III engaged Bruno.

## The magic question

### Was it magical?

The best source-based answer is **no evidence of operative magic**.

**[Text]**

- The figures are moralized within orthodox Christian preaching.
- Pagan gods are subordinated to virtues, vices and biblical teaching.
- The procedure uses rhyme, bodily attributes, inscriptions, textual concordance and allegory.
- No spirit invocation, planetary timing, suffumigation, consecration, magical name or talismanic operation is prescribed.

### Pagan imagery and idolatry

The image of Idolatry is itself an attack on false worship. Ridevall’s mythography justifies pagan fables by uncovering a Christian or ethical *integumentum*. Holcot repeatedly domesticates classical material for moral instruction.

**[Tension, not prosecution]** The friars’ false or loose attributions to Fulgentius, Roman priests and Remigius could be criticized as bad source practice or fictional antiquarianism. That does not make them magical.

### Ars notoria

The *Ars notoria* promised knowledge and enhanced faculties through prayers, divine names, ritual observances and complex *notae*. It was controversial and sometimes condemned.

Ridevall and Holcot differ fundamentally:

| *Ars notoria* | Ridevall–Holcot |
|---|---|
| Knowledge claimed through revelation or ritual | Knowledge organized through study and preaching |
| Sacred names and prayers | Rhyme, attributes, biblical quotations |
| Diagrammatic *notae* may possess operative force | Pictures signify moral propositions |
| Susceptible to magical classification | Treated as moralized rhetoric and exegesis |

**[Negative finding]** No medieval source was found accusing Ridevall or Holcot of practising the *Ars notoria*, necromancy or image magic.

### Heresy and consequences

**[Negative finding]** No Inquisition proceeding, university condemnation or ecclesiastical penalty is known concerning these pictures.

Holcot did participate in controversial scholastic debates, including questions about divine foreknowledge, future contingents, faith and intuitive cognition. Those controversies must not be conflated with an accusation that his mnemonic pictures were heretical.

### The “accused magician”

**[Modern conflation if applied here]** Stories of memory masters accused of magic belong elsewhere—for example to later Renaissance practitioners or to reputations surrounding extraordinary mnemonic performance. No such documented episode attaches to Ridevall or Holcot.

## Afterlife

### 1. Immediate medieval reception

Ridevall’s work circulated quickly in England and then across Europe. Its materials were:

- excerpted;
- abridged;
- indexed;
- detached from the continuous commentary;
- combined with Holcot;
- combined with *Gesta Romanorum*, moralized Seneca and Ovid;
- eventually illustrated.

Derivative titles include *Ymagines Fulgentii*, *Imagines Fulgentii moralizatae*, *De imaginibus virtutum* and material beginning *Prudentia depingebatur*. Attribution is unstable because scribes joined anonymous extracts from several authors.

### 2. *Gesta Romanorum*

Smalley argued that the compiler of the *Gesta Romanorum* borrowed from Holcot’s collected *Moralitates*, rather than Holcot borrowing from the *Gesta*. Her evidence is distributional: stories shared with Holcot occur in the *Moralitates* but not correspondingly across his earlier commentaries.

This direction of borrowing supplies the terminus before 1342, but remains a scholarly reconstruction.

### 3. Catalan reception

Francesc Eiximenis used Holcot’s Friendship in *Dotzè del Crestià*, ch. 807, “Qui figura vera amistat en semblança d’una imatge,” 1385–1386.

A Barcelona clerk, Bernat d’Esplugues, owned a copy of the *Moralitates*. Anselm Turmeda in 1398 praised preaching that turned the fables of great poets into good moral instruction. These cases show the technique moving beyond Oxford into courtly, civic and Catalan religious culture.

### 4. Material picture-books

The decisive fifteenth-century transformation was from verbal picture to image-text ensemble. Pal. lat. 1726 and Casanatense 1404 do not reveal the original format; they show how later readers reinterpreted it.

The stages are:

> verbal personification → excerpt collection → rubricated *imago/pictura* → reserved illustration space → painted cycle → printed iconography.

### 5. Friendship and Renaissance iconology

Holcot’s Friendship had a long and unusually traceable afterlife:

- fifteenth-century manuscript paintings;
- Gregor Reisch, *Margarita philosophica*;
- humanist and emblematic treatments;
- Johannes Sambucus, *Emblemata* (1564), *Vera amicitia*;
- Nicolaus Reusner’s *Emblemata*;
- *Mikrokosmos/Parvus mundus* traditions;
- Cesare Ripa, *Iconologia*, especially illustrated editions;
- seventeenth-century emblem collections;
- Orthodox marriage-sermon books printed at Lviv in 1644 and 1645.

Ripa changes Holcot’s male youth into a female personification, retains the exposed heart and paired inscriptions, and adds features from other emblematic sources: white dress, myrtle and pomegranate, and the elm embraced by a vine. The transmission is therefore compilation, not faithful copying.

The three Holcot antitheses remain recognizable:

- *hyems et aestas*;
- *longe/procul et prope*;
- *mors et vita*.

Ernst Gombrich treated this descent as evidence that Ripa’s iconology grew out of medieval didacticism. [Discussion of Holcot and Ripa](https://www.fondazioneterradotranto.it/2013/08/19/quella-seconda-mensola-del-balcone-del-castello-di-nardo-56/).

### 6. Dante and frescoes

**[No demonstrated direct descent]** Dante’s memory, moral topography and personification belong to a broader common culture, but no evidence shows that he used Ridevall or Holcot.

**[Disputed/general analogy]** Yates speculated that medieval artificial memory could illuminate grotesque figures and moral fresco cycles. This is suggestive at the level of shared habits—ordered personification, vivid vice and virtue, inscription—but direct dependence must be proved work by work.

### 7. Later literature

Borges’s Funes and Eco’s libraries concern total memory, textual order and the dangers of indiscriminate recall. They are useful comparative afterlives of memory culture, but:

**[Negative finding]** no textual evidence was located that Borges or Eco specifically drew on Ridevall’s Idolatry or Holcot’s pictures.

### 8. Modern memory palaces and athletes

Modern competitors use loci, striking images and associative coding. Holcot’s bodies could be adapted effectively to such practice.

**[Modern analogy]** Presenting Ridevall–Holcot as a medieval “memory palace,” or teaching their figures as a documented precursor of competitive memory, is anachronistic. Their primary task was ethical and homiletic invention, not rapid storage of cards or numbers.

### 9. Computing and combinatorics

There is no demonstrated line:

> Ridevall/Holcot → Lull → Leibniz → computer.

Lull’s combinatorial art developed independently before these Oxford works. Leibniz’s interest in Lullian combination belongs to another genealogy. Ridevall and Holcot do share abstract features with information systems:

- hierarchical records;
- embodied indexing;
- fixed field positions;
- keyword concordance;
- topical lookup;
- recombinable modules.

Those are illuminating structural analogies, not documentary descent.

## Controversies and disputes

### 1. Is this truly an “artificial-memory system”?

**For:** rhyme, ordered attributes, conspicuous figures, inscriptions, scriptural word-links and preacherly use produce a robust mnemonic mechanism.

**Against:** no explicit loci rules, rehearsal instructions or declaration of an *ars memoriae* survives.

**Verdict:** Holcot’s pictures are documented mnemonic-compositional devices within preaching; “system of artificial memory” is a useful but secondary classification. For Ridevall, the mnemonic reading is plausible and influential but should not replace “moralized mythographic commentary.”

### 2. Were the pictures invisible?

**Yates:** originally internal and not meant for material depiction.

**Evidence:** early English manuscripts are unillustrated; later continental manuscripts illustrate them; some witnesses reserve spaces.

**Verdict:** material pictures were unnecessary, and probably absent from the initial instructional situation. Absolute non-intention is unprovable.

### 3. Are the images ancient?

**Textual claim:** Holcot invokes Fulgentius, Roman practice, Vesta’s priests and Remigius.

**Evidence:** key pictures are absent from the alleged sources.

**Verdict:** “sham antique” is justified. The attributions are medieval learned fictions, misunderstandings or prestige devices.

### 4. Did Ridevall devise the technique?

He is an early and important exponent, not demonstrably its inventor. Rhymed *picturae*, virtues and vices, moralized mythology, sermon distinctions and embodied allegory have older roots. The precise direction of influence among Ridevall, Holcot and the *Fasciculus morum* requires individual textual comparison.

### 5. Did Holcot borrow Idolatry directly from Ridevall?

Smalley and Yates say yes, based on close similarity and chronology. Hanna’s fuller text strengthens the need to compare complete versions, but direct borrowing, shared source and circulating extract must still be distinguished. The broader Oxford mendicant milieu makes all three possible.

### 6. How many manuscripts?

Statements range from nine direct witnesses to “more than one hundred manuscripts in some form.” These figures count different things:

- direct witnesses of the continuous commentary;
- fragments;
- excerpted *Imagines*;
- derivative condensations;
- composite picture-books.

They are not necessarily contradictory.

### 7. *Fulgentius* or *Fulgencius*?

*Fulgentius metaforalis* is the traditional scholarly spelling used by Liebeschütz and Yates. Hanna prints *Fulgencius metaforalis*, reflecting the work’s textual form. Both titles identify the same work; citations should follow the edition used.

### 8. Ridevall or Holcot as author of *Imagines Fulgentii*

Composite manuscripts sometimes assign Ridevall-derived *Imagines* and anonymous *Aenigmata* to Holcot. Modern scholarship separates them:

- *Imagines Fulgentii*: derived from Ridevall;
- *Aenigmata*: anonymous;
- *Moralitates*: Holcot.

The Madrid manuscript catalogue explicitly records this correction.

### 9. Date of the *Moralitates*

Smalley’s c. 1334–before 1342 date depends on:

- development from the Minor Prophets commentary;
- direction of borrowing with *Gesta Romanorum*;
- time allowed for circulation.

It is a strong relative chronology, not an authorial date.

### 10. Yates and “memory for words”

Her proposal remains possible in a limited sense: exact short phrases are visibly assigned to locations on an image.

It becomes misleading if expanded to mean that Holcot encoded continuous discourse verbatim. The dominant function is topical and compositional.

### 11. The Casanatense plate

Yates identifies plate 4c as Holcot’s Penance and calls it a later illustration that “we ought not to be seeing.”

The identification is secure at the level of textual correspondence. What is not secure is the implication that its painter violated the intended ontology of the image. The illustrator may instead have understood *pictura* literally or converted a mnemonic text into a teaching picture.

### 12. The Koblenz “1268” problem

A catalogue page can misleadingly display “1268” beside a volume containing Holcot. Since Holcot’s works postdate that year by decades, 1268 cannot be the production date of his *Moralitates*. It derives from a copied colophon in another section or from the exemplar’s textual history.

### 13. Magic, heresy and persecution

No evidence supports these claims for this system. Any account that supplies an Inquisition trial, accusation of sorcery or talismanic use without a new primary citation is legend or modern conflation.

### 14. Famous mnemonic anecdotes

The auctioneer, professor’s party, student who lost his memory and accused magician do not occur in the Ridevall–Holcot sources examined. Including them as episodes of this system would be modern invention.

## Open questions

1. Can the direction of borrowing between Ridevall and Holcot be demonstrated clause by clause from Hanna’s complete editions?
2. Which of Holcot’s twenty-six prophetic pictures are original combinations, and which derive from sermon aids now unedited?
3. Does any early witness contain reader annotations explicitly saying *ad memoriam*, *memoriter*, or equivalent?
4. How consistently do verse order, attribute order and exegetical order correspond across all manuscripts?
5. Did scribes alter bodily attributes because they misunderstood the mnemonic structure?
6. Were reserved blank spaces intended for illustrations, diagrams, decorated initials or merely rubrication?
7. How many of Hanna’s “more than one hundred” witnesses contain the continuous *Fulgencius*, and how many contain only derivative *Imagines*?
8. Can the Oxford Greyfriars library reconstruction be tested against surviving medieval catalogues?
9. Is Holcot’s fictive citation of Remigius deliberate prosopopoeia, memory failure, a lost intermediary, or scribal corruption?
10. Did preachers recite the rhymed *pictura* aloud to congregations, or use it privately to generate prose?
11. How early did Friendship acquire actual inscriptions in pictures rather than merely in descriptive text?
12. What exact manuscript stands between Holcot and Eiximenis?
13. Can the transformation of the green youth into Ripa’s female Amicitia be mapped through every surviving illustrated witness?
14. How should “memory diagram” be defined so that indices, body schemas and actual diagrams are not conflated?
15. Does the complete *Fulgencius* require a revised account of the relative priority of Ridevall, Holcot and later moralized mythography?

# The English friars’ invisible pictures: Ridevall and Holcot: Research Dossier

## Basic identification

| Field | Identification |
|---|---|
| Catalogue number | System 13 of 45 |
| Conventional name | The English friars’ invisible pictures |
| Exponents | John Ridevall, O.F.M.; Robert Holcot, O.P. |
| Date | Oxford, chiefly the 1330s; Holcot’s *Moralitates* probably after c. 1334 and before 1342 |
| Principal texts | Ridevall, *Fulgencius/Fulgentius metaforalis*; Holcot, commentary on the Twelve Minor Prophets, especially Hosea and Nahum; *Moralitates*; related passages in *Super Sapientiam* |
| Classical point of departure | Fulgentius, *Mitologiae*; the Third Vatican Mythographer; Remigius of Auxerre on Martianus Capella; biblical, patristic and classical commonplace collections |
| Principle in one sentence | A preacher compresses the divisions of a moral or exegetical discourse into the conspicuous attributes of a verbally described personification—often reinforced by rhyme, inscriptions and verbal echoes with Scripture—so that reconstructing the figure helps reconstruct the sermon. |
| Yates | Frances A. Yates, *The Art of Memory* (1966), ch. IV, pp. 96–99; plate 4c |
| Present critical verdict | Holcot’s pictures are securely reconstructable as preacherly compositional and mnemonic devices; Ridevall’s figures were likewise memorable, indexed moralizations, but Yates’s presentation of his whole mythography as a formal artificial-memory “system” goes beyond the surviving instructions. Neither author supplies an Ad Herennium-style sequence of loci or a formal rulebook for constructing places. |

### Evidentiary labels used below

- **[Text]** Directly stated or materially present in a medieval or early printed source.
- **[Manuscript]** Codicological evidence.
- **[Reconstruction]** A modern scholar’s inference from textual structure or transmission.
- **[Yates]** Frances Yates’s interpretation.
- **[Disputed]** An attribution, date or functional interpretation for which serious alternatives exist.
- **[Tradition]** A premodern report repeated without secure access to its alleged ancient source.
- **[Legend]** A story unsupported by contemporary evidence.
- **[Modern invention]** A recent embellishment or conflation not found in the historical witnesses.
- **[Negative finding]** No evidence was located for the proposed feature.

The distinction matters especially here. “Invisible pictures” is Yates’s memorable category, not Ridevall’s or Holcot’s name for a formally defined art.

## The system in detail

### 1. What kind of “system” is it?

**[Text]** Ridevall and Holcot repeatedly present a figure and enumerate its attributes. Each bodily feature, garment, object, defect or inscription is then interpreted as one division of a virtue, vice or sermon topic.

**[Reconstruction]** The basic retrieval path is:

> sermon theme → remembered personification → ordered bodily attributes or clauses → moral propositions → supporting authorities and biblical texts.

This is a system of **topical compression and sermon composition**, not demonstrably a complete classical place-system.

**[Negative finding]** Neither Ridevall’s *Fulgencius metaforalis* nor Holcot’s *Moralitates* gives rules comparable to *Ad Herennium* III.16–24 for:

- selecting and spacing architectural loci;
- numbering loci;
- repeatedly walking a fixed route;
- distinguishing background places from deposited images;
- erasing and reusing images;
- training through daily rehearsal;
- translating every word of a continuous text into images.

**[Yates]** Yates nevertheless interpreted the personifications through the classical theory of *imagines agentes*: striking, grotesque, mobile images impressed upon memory. For Holcot’s Hosea commentary she proposed an additional page-place system: the biblical page supplies the locus and the personification supplies the image.

**[Later scholarship]** Kimberly Rivers, Mary Carruthers, John T. Slotemaker and Jeffrey C. Witt substantially support a mnemonic and compositional function for Holcot’s pictures, but describe it more precisely as medieval preaching practice. Ralph Hanna’s complete edition sharply qualifies claims that Ridevall’s work circulated as “memory diagrams”: its medieval tabula is better evidence for consultation and excerpting by preachers. [Liverpool University Press’s description of Hanna’s edition](https://utpdistribution.com/9781836245209/john-ridewall-fulgentius-metaforalis/) explicitly contrasts manuscript consultation with the frequently asserted “set of memory diagrams.”

### 2. Ridevall’s *Fulgencius metaforalis*

#### Form and organization

**[Text]** The complete work, as reconstructed by Ralph Hanna, consists of two books:

**Book I**

1. Idolatry  
2. Saturn / Prudence  
3. Jupiter / Love and Friendship  
4. Juno / Memory  
5. Neptune / Intelligence  
6. Pluto / Foresight  
7. Apollo / Truth  
8. Phaethon / Ambition  
9. Mercury / Eloquence  
10. Danaë / Modesty and Women’s Greed  
11. Ganymede / Sodomy  
12. Perseus / Courage  
13. Alcestis / Marital Continence  

**Book II**

1. Paris / Injustice  
2. Minerva / Contemplative Life  
3. Juno / Worldly Life  
4. Venus / The Life of Pleasure  

There are also two inserted discussions in the received text.

This is important because Hans Liebeschütz’s 1926 edition, the text available to Smalley and Yates, printed only the opening portion. Hanna established that its base witness, Venice, Biblioteca Nazionale Marciana, MS Z. lat. 494 (1790), preserves roughly one-third of the work. [Hanna’s edition and contents](https://www.jstor.org/stable/jj.38238767).

#### The image of Idolatry

Ridevall’s compact descriptive verse is:

> **Mulier notata, oculis orbata, aure mutilata, cornu ventilata, vultu deformata et morbo vexata.**

—Ridevall, *Fulgencius metaforalis*, I.1; Liebeschütz, 1926, in the chapter *Ydolatria*.

A close gloss is:

> “A marked woman, deprived of her eyes, mutilated in the ear, proclaimed by a horn, deformed in face, and afflicted by disease.”

“Prostitute” is supplied by the ensuing interpretation and by Yates/Smalley’s paraphrase; *mulier notata* itself means a notorious, branded or marked woman rather than lexically “prostitute.”

**[Text]** The six clauses establish a determinate sequence. Ridevall then moralizes them:

1. the dishonoured or meretricious woman signifies idolatry’s infidelity;
2. blindness signifies error and failure to perceive divine truth;
3. the mutilated ear signifies failure to hear or obey God’s word;
4. the sounding horn publicizes criminality or infamy;
5. the deformed face signifies disfigurement of the divine likeness;
6. disease signifies disordered or excessive attachment.

**[Reconstruction]** Rhyme and parallel morphology—*notata/orbata/mutilata/ventilata/deformata/vexata*—make the order orally recoverable. Remembering the verse regenerates the six headings; remembering each attribute regenerates its exposition.

**[Yates]** Yates calls it an invented, moving memory image and treats each feature as a mnemonic cue.

**[Qualification]** The text itself does not say “put this image in a locus,” “use it to memorize,” or “retrieve the sermon by mentally inspecting it.” The mnemonic function is strongly plausible from its compact rhyme and preacherly structure, but the explicit operational language belongs chiefly to modern reconstruction.

#### The gods as moral schemata

The opening sequence associates pagan gods with the three temporal parts of prudence:

- Saturn: *prudentia*;
- Juno: *memoria*;
- Neptune: *intelligentia*;
- Pluto: *providentia*.

The scheme draws upon the Ciceronian division of prudence into memory, understanding and foresight, but it is not merely a one-to-one table. Each deity’s traditional appearance and narrative are decomposed into further attributes.

For Pluto, the verse printed by Liebeschütz reads:

> **Ligno coronatus, opibus dilatatus, inferis praelatus, Cerbero delatus, aetati ligatus, Furiis armatus, et Fatis vallatus.**

A gloss:

> “Crowned with wood, enlarged by riches, set over the underworld, attended by Cerberus, bound to age, armed with the Furies, and enclosed by the Fates.”

Ridevall then says:

> **Istae sunt partes septem picturae Plutonicae, quibus correspondent totidem proprietates providentiae.**

> “These are the seven parts of the Plutonic picture, to which correspond the same number of properties of foresight.”

This is exceptionally strong evidence for an ordered image-to-doctrine mapping. A contemporary review of Liebeschütz already warned, however, that *pingere* and *pictura* may be metaphorical here rather than instructions for a material illustration. [Review in *Revue belge de Philologie et d’Histoire*](https://www.persee.fr/doc/rbph_0035-0818_1928_num_7_2_6518_t1_0609_0000_2).

#### Order and retrieval

Ridevall’s order operates at several levels:

1. **[Text]** book and chapter sequence;
2. **[Text]** a deity or personification as the chapter’s principal organizing figure;
3. **[Text]** a rhyming descriptive verse;
4. **[Text]** a fixed series of attributes;
5. **[Text]** a corresponding series of moral properties;
6. **[Manuscript]** later alphabetical tabulae enabling readers to find moral themes independently of the mythological chapter;
7. **[Reconstruction]** preacherly extraction: consult the tabula, locate an apt *moralitas* or *integumentum*, and incorporate it into a sermon.

The last point is supported by Hanna’s edition of the “A tabula.” Hanna interprets it as helping a user locate useful moralizations not readily found from the names of mythological figures—most probably a preacher collecting amplificatory material. The tabula is an access technology, but not a revolving mnemonic wheel.

### 3. Holcot’s rhymed pictures in the Minor Prophets

#### Type I: rhymed, enumerative pictures

**[Text]** Holcot’s commentary on the Twelve Prophets contains twenty-six rhymed pictures not duplicated in the *Moralitates*. Slotemaker and Witt follow Kimberly Rivers in calling these “Type I”: compact rhymed descriptions whose clauses lead into ordered explanations.

The most fully analyzed modern example is the picture of Patience in the commentary on Nahum 1:3, *Dominus patiens*.

**[Text/Reconstruction]** Its ten attributes cue ten properties:

1. sitting contentedly → sufficiency or contentment;
2. fed with common food → endurance of conditions that nourish patience;
3. joyful face → joy;
4. turned toward everyone → patience toward all, including enemies;
5. clothed in purple → mercy, kindness, modesty and humility;
6. defended from enemies → silence and peace during trouble;
7. one hand shrivelled, the other extended → readiness to endure;
8. without feet → completion or perfection through trials;
9. without a tongue → disciplined speech;
10. without ears → ignoring or forgiving insults.

Holcot often links the wording of a clause to the wording of a biblical authority. The cue is therefore simultaneously visual, rhythmic and verbal. Slotemaker and Witt show, for example, that *extenta* can recall passages using *extendere*. Their conclusion is that a friar could recover both a division of the theme and the scriptural text supporting it. [Slotemaker and Witt, discussion of the pictures](https://dokumen.pub/robert-holcot-great-medieval-thinkers-1nbsped-9780199391240-9780199391257-0199391246.html).

#### The page as place

**[Text]** Holcot introduces pictures at specific textual lemmata in his running commentary. Idolatry appears where Hosea supplies a relevant cue; Cupid appears where the prophetic text invites a treatment of love.

**[Yates]** From this arrangement Yates concluded that Holcot “places such images, in imagination, on the pages of a Scriptural text.” The page becomes a locus, the picture an image, and its attributes the heads of the intended exposition.

**[Qualification]** What is directly documented is attachment of a *pictura* to a textual lemma. The stronger claim that Holcot internally visualized a physical manuscript opening and deposited a moving Cupid upon it is Yates’s psychological reconstruction. No surviving instruction says to memorize page coordinates, margins, ruling or openings in the manner of a formal page-grid mnemonic.

A safer description is **textual localization**: Hosea’s words provide an ordered sequence of retrieval cues because the commentary follows Scripture in order.

#### Cupid

Yates writes:

> “He even places on the text of the prophet an image of Cupid, complete with bow and arrows!”

—Yates, *Art of Memory*, ch. IV, p. 99.

**[Text]** Cupid and his attributes are moralized in Holcot’s commentary.

**[Yates]** Its importance for her lies in a Christian friar using a vivid pagan god as a working mnemonic image rather than merely mentioning a classical fable.

**[Negative finding]** No evidence was found that the Cupid figure was used as a talisman, invoked as a spirit, or believed to exert occult power.

### 4. Holcot’s *Moralitates*

#### Function and date

**[Text]** The *Moralitates* are a repertory of moralized stories and pictures for preachers.

**[Dating reconstruction]** Smalley placed them after the less developed picture-technique of the Twelve Prophets commentary, c. 1334, and before the compiler of the earliest *Gesta Romanorum* witness used them by 1342. This yields approximately 1334–1342. The argument depends on direction of borrowing and remains a reconstruction rather than a dated colophon.

Manuscripts vary substantially:

- Basel, Universitätsbibliothek, A II 26: twenty items, fols. 138rb–140va;
- Basel, A V 33: forty-one;
- Basel, B VIII 10: forty-five;
- Madrid, Universidad Complutense, BH MSS 133: forty-four, probably once more;
- Basel 1586 printing: forty-eight.

The Basel manuscripts also differ in ordering. [Notes on the Basel witnesses](https://slotemaker.wordpress.com/category/holcots-moralitates/); [Madrid catalogue](https://manipulus.ucm.es/handle/20.500.12238/189?mode=full).

#### Type II pictures

Rivers calls the shorter, generally unrhymed pictures in the *Moralitates* “Type II.” They frequently abandon a tightly enumerated verse for a prose description followed by interpretation.

This is not necessarily a decline in mnemonic sophistication. It may reflect a different use: a preacher selects a compact allegorical unit from a repertory rather than reconstructing a long exegetical lecture.

### 5. Penance

Holcot’s Latin begins:

> **Imago penitentie, quam depinxerunt sacerdotes dee Veste secundum Remigium: penitentia depingebatur ad modum unius hominis nudati per totum corpus, qui tenebat in manu sua quoddam flagellum, quinque folia ferens, in quo scribebantur quinque versus sive sententie …**

—*Moralitates*, Moralitas XXII in Basel 1586, p. 728, as transcribed by Smalley.

Gloss:

> “The image of Penance, which the priests of the goddess Vesta painted, according to Remigius: Penance was painted in the form of a man naked over his whole body, holding in his hand a scourge bearing five strips, on which five verses or sentences were written …”

**[Text]** The five strips physically segment the five statements. A remembered body and object thus provide hierarchy:

> Penance → scourge → five strips → five written propositions.

**[Tradition/fictional attribution]** Holcot attributes the figure to Vesta’s priests and Remigius.

**[Source criticism]** Smalley checked Remigius of Auxerre’s commentary on Martianus Capella and found no such passage; moreover, nothing in the relevant classical narrative naturally yields Christian penance. “According to Remigius” is therefore pseudonymous authority, confusion or deliberate antiquizing—not documentation of an ancient image.

**[Yates]** This “sham antique” quality confirms for Yates that Holcot invented a memorable figure while giving it classical prestige.

**[Manuscript]** A fifteenth-century illustrated witness shows a naked man holding a nail scourge and a larger whip, inscriptions running along the strands. The same complex includes another Penance as an armed man and associated mnemonic verses. [Casanatense manuscript description](https://www.omifacsimiles.com/brochures/cima39.pdf).

### 6. Friendship

Holcot’s description reads:

> **Pictura amoris sive amicitiae. Narrat Fulgentius in quodam libro de gestis Romanorum quod Romani verum amorem sive veram amicitiam hoc modo descripserunt, scilicet: quod imago amoris vel amicitiae depicta erat instar iuvenis cuiusdam valde pulchri, induti habitu viridi. Facies eius et caput discooperta erant sive nudata, et in fronte ipsius erat hoc scriptum: HYEMS ET AESTAS. Erat latus eius apertum, ita ut videretur cor, in quo scripta erant haec verba: LONGE ET PROPE. Et in fimbria vestimenti eius erat scriptum: MORS ET VITA. Similiter ista imago habebat pedes nudos.**

—Basel: Jacob Ryter, 1586, Moralitas XXVI, p. 731; the Venice 1514 edition has it as Moralitas XX, fols. 23r–v.

Gloss:

> “The picture of love or friendship. Fulgentius relates in a certain book on Roman deeds that the Romans represented true love or friendship in this way: the image was like a very beautiful young man dressed in green. His face and head were uncovered, and upon his forehead was written ‘WINTER AND SUMMER.’ His side was open so that the heart could be seen, upon which were written ‘FAR AND NEAR.’ On the hem of his clothing was written ‘DEATH AND LIFE.’ The image likewise had bare feet.”

The interpretation makes each feature a proposition:

- youth: genuine friendship must not age or fail in adversity;
- green: continuing freshness;
- uncovered face and head: true friendship cannot remain concealed;
- winter and summer: adversity and prosperity;
- exposed heart: openness between friends;
- far and near: affection survives absence;
- death and life: friendship endures beyond death;
- bare feet: readiness and freedom from impediment.

**[Text]** This is a particularly clear instance of “words on images.”

**[Yates]** She proposed that such inscriptions show what medieval writers understood by “memory for words.”

**[Qualification]** It does not encode the exact wording of an arbitrary continuous text. It stores short antithetical formulas at semantically appropriate bodily positions. In classical terminology it is closer to *memoria rerum*—remembering ordered subject matter—with selected verbal anchors.

**[False ancient authority]** The cited work by Fulgentius, *de gestis Romanorum*, is not extant and the description is absent from authentic Fulgentius. The alleged Roman antiquity is unsupported.

### 7. What are the places?

There are three defensible answers, with different evidentiary status:

1. **The body as micro-topography — [Reconstruction strongly supported].** Head, forehead, ear, face, side, heart, hand, garment hem and feet provide stable positions.
2. **The verse as serial place — [Text/Reconstruction].** Rhymed clauses impose sequence independently of pictorial space.
3. **The biblical text or page as place — [Yates, partly supported].** A scriptural lemma fixes where a picture enters the commentary; visualization of the actual page remains inferential.

There is no documented architectural itinerary. Calling this a “memory palace” would be a modernizing distortion.

**[Negative finding]** No fourteenth-century authorial diagram, wheel or table establishing a memory system survives.

**[Manuscript]** The Casanatense Penance image shows the naked man and inscribed scourge. That is rhetorically effective but too absolute: blank illustration spaces and the eventual cycles show that medieval readers did consider externalization compatible with the texts.

## Yates’s account and scholarship since

### 1. What Yates says

Yates’s central statements are:

> “These invisible ‘pictures’ provide us with specimens of invisible memory images, held within the memory, not intended to be externalised, and being used for quite practical mnemonic purposes.”

—p. 96.

> “The memorising of words or sentences as placed or written on the memory images was perhaps what the Middle Ages understood by ‘memory for words’.”

—p. 98.

> “He even places on the text of the prophet an image of Cupid, complete with bow and arrows!”

—p. 99.

She concludes:

> “The preference of these English friars for the fables of the poets as memory images … suggests that the artificial memory may be a hitherto unsuspected medium through which pagan imagery survived in the Middle Ages.”

A searchable scan of the relevant chapter is available through the [Warburg Institute](https://resources.warburg.sas.ac.uk/mnemosyne/fq/yates_memory.pdf).

### 2. What Yates got right

**Confirmed or strengthened:**

- The images are organized collections of attributes, not ornamental literary flourishes.
- Rhyme aids retention.
- Holcot’s pictures function within preaching and composition.
- Their grotesque or conspicuous features make them memorable.
- Inscribed bodies can retain short verbal units.
- Classical gods and pseudo-classical figures were Christianized rather than simply suppressed.
- Holcot’s scriptural lemmata give the images ordered textual positions.
- Later manuscripts materially realized figures initially transmitted in words.
- The technique became an important conduit between medieval moralized mythology and Renaissance iconography.

Slotemaker and Witt reconstruct a much more exact mnemonic operation than Yates did for Holcot’s Patience: each of ten clauses cues a property and, through shared words, relevant scriptural authorities.

### 3. What requires correction

#### “None of the manuscripts is illustrated”

**[Corrected]** Yates herself immediately noted Saxl’s illustrated exceptions, so her categorical sentence really means that the main English transmission was unillustrated. Later cataloguing documents several continental illustrated complexes.

#### “Not intended to be externalised”

**[Disputed]** This is plausible for Holcot’s initial lecturing and for unillustrated English witnesses, but intention cannot be proved. Some manuscript pages reserve spaces for illustrations, and fifteenth-century readers converted descriptions into pictures. It is safer to say that the mnemonic operation did not require material painting.

#### Ridevall as a formal artificial-memory manual

**[Overstated]** Ridevall supplies moralized mythography, rhymed attribute lists and preacherly finding aids. He does not provide a classical regimen of loci. Hanna specifically cautions against treating the manuscripts as sets of “memory diagrams.”

#### “Memory for words”

**[Disputed]** Holcot’s inscriptions preserve formulas such as *hyems et aestas* or five sentences on scourge-strips. They do not demonstrate verbatim storage of long discourse. Carruthers’s category of *imagines rerum*, compositional images for the divisions of a theme, is more exact.

#### The page as literal locus

**[Plausible but unproved]** Attachment to successive scriptural lemmata is documented; internal visualization of manuscript pages is not.

#### Invented from nothing

**[Corrected]** Yates, following the difficulty earlier scholars had locating sources, called the images invented memory figures. Later work shows more extensive dependence on the Third Vatican Mythographer, Remigius, Fulgentius, biblical language, sermon collections and possibly the *Fasciculus morum*. The finished combinations may be inventive even when their components are inherited.

### 4. Smalley

Beryl Smalley, *English Friars and Antiquity in the Early Fourteenth Century* (Oxford, 1960), especially pp. 109–132 for Ridevall and 133–202 for Holcot, was Yates’s indispensable immediate source.

Smalley established:

- the Oxford friars’ unusual density of classical citation;
- the preacherly setting;
- Holcot’s “sham antique” authorities;
- the chronology of Holcot’s writings;
- the likelihood that the pictures were aural and mnemonic aids;
- the absence of the claimed Penance picture in Remigius;
- the relation to *Gesta Romanorum*.

Her approach has since been criticized as primarily descriptive and source-hunting, but its factual foundation remains substantial.

### 5. Carruthers

Mary Carruthers reframed medieval memory as an art of composition, ethical formation and invention, rather than a trick for verbatim repetition. Her concise assessment is that a textual picture can serve memory as well as a painted one because imagination can “paint” it without material constraints.

For Holcot, she calls the figures emblematic *imagines rerum*: devices connecting the divisions of Faith, Sloth, Patience or another theme. This confirms Yates’s mnemonic insight while weakening her equation with literal “memory for words.”

### 6. Rivers

Kimberly A. Rivers, *Preaching the Memory of Virtue and Vice: Memory, Images, and Preaching in the Late Middle Ages* (Turnhout: Brepols, 2010), provides the most sustained study of this specific preaching environment. [Publisher record](https://www.brepols.net/products/IS-9782503515250-1).

Her major contributions are:

- distinction between Holcot’s rhymed, enumerative Type I and shorter prose Type II pictures;
- reconstruction of the preacher’s memory;
- comparison with sermon theory and practice;
- attention to Ridevall’s *Lectura super Apocalypsim* as well as the *Fulgencius*;
- possible influence of the Franciscan *Fasciculus morum* on some forms.

### 7. Slotemaker and Witt

John T. Slotemaker and Jeffrey C. Witt, *Robert Holcot* (Oxford, 2016), pp. 233–249 on the *Moralitates* and pp. 142–162 on the Twelve Prophets, confirm that Holcot’s pictures are mnemonic but insist on their exegetical content.

Their analysis of Patience demonstrates:

- ordered cueing;
- exact links between descriptive vocabulary and biblical proof texts;
- the pictures’ role in reconstructing an exposition;
- no evidence for painted versions before the fifteenth century;
- later illustrations as a distinct stage in reception.

They also correct several inherited attributions: the *Ymagines Fulgentii* derives from Ridevall; the adjacent *Aenigmata* is anonymous rather than Holcot’s. [OUP chapter record](https://academic.oup.com/book/36275/chapter-abstract/316605877).

### 8. Hanna

Hanna’s 2023 Ridevall edition is the largest post-Yates correction:

- *Fulgencius* was about three times longer than Liebeschütz’s text suggested;
- its structure extends well beyond the prudence group;
- more than one hundred manuscripts preserve it or forms of it;
- its principal mythographic sources can be identified;
- the tabula documents preacherly retrieval;
- “memory diagram” language should not replace analysis of actual codices.

His 2021 Holcot edition likewise moves discussion from Smalley’s extracts to a critically presented Latin text with translation.

### 9. Scholarship named in the general brief but not directly determinative

Bolzoni, Rossi, Small, Ong, Bonner, Fidora, Sturlese, Ciliberto, Clucas, Gatti and Stagno are essential for Camillo, Lull, Bruno, Ramus and the larger history of memory. They do not provide major independent corrections to the Ridevall–Holcot dossier. Importing their wheels, theatres, alphabetic combinations or occult cosmologies into this case would obscure its preacherly character.

There are:

- no Lullian rotating figures;
- no Camillo theatre and graded ascent;
- no Bruno-style wheel combinations;
- no Ramist dichotomous chart;
- no Fluddian architectural theatre.

The pertinent comparison is functional, not genealogically demonstrated.

## History and context

### 1. Mendicant and university setting

Ridevall was an Oxford Franciscan and probably regent master in the 1330s. Hanna reconstructs his access to a rich institutional library, plausibly Oxford Greyfriars, from the breadth and physical pattern of his citations.

Holcot was a Dominican theologian associated with Oxford, Cambridge, Northampton and the intellectual circle of Richard de Bury. His Minor Prophets lectures belong to his Oxford regency; *Super Sapientiam* became exceptionally successful, surviving in roughly 175 manuscripts and numerous early printings. [Holcot overview](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/holkot/).

The institutions mattered:

- friars required reusable sermon material;
- university biblical lectures proceeded lemma by lemma;
- students needed to convert lectures into preaching;
- mendicant mobility rewarded compact, internally recoverable structures;
- convent libraries and commonplace books enabled extraction and recombination.

### 2. Rhetoric and scholastic psychology

The remote ancestry lies in classical rhetoric:

- *Rhetorica ad Herennium* III.16–24;
- Cicero, *De oratore* II.350–360;
- Quintilian, *Institutio oratoria* XI.2.

Those works distinguish places and images and recommend striking *imagines agentes*.

The proximate medieval setting includes:

- Aristotle’s claim that thinking occurs with phantasms;
- scholastic faculty psychology;
- Augustine’s capacious notion of memory;
- Albert the Great and Thomas Aquinas on artificial memory;
- the preacher’s division of a theme;
- exempla, distinctions and similitudes;
- lectio, meditation and mental composition.

**[Reconstruction]** Holcot’s body-pictures translate scholastic divisiones into sensuous form. They are simultaneously outlines, concordances and imaginative prompts.

### 3. Mythography

Ridevall belongs to the moralized-Fulgentian tradition:

- Fabius Planciades Fulgentius, *Mitologiae*;
- Remigius of Auxerre on Martianus Capella;
- the Third Vatican Mythographer;
- *Ovide moralisé*;
- Pierre Bersuire’s *Ovidius moralizatus*;
- later mythographic and emblematic compilations.

Hanna finds that much of Ridevall’s mythographic detail comes not directly from Fulgentius but from Remigius and the Third Vatican Mythographer.

### 4. Preaching

The method is adapted to sermon performance:

- the verse can be memorized;
- the body fixes divisions;
- each division opens into authorities;
- the moralized story interests an educated audience;
- the image can be abbreviated or expanded;
- tabulae permit topical retrieval.

By the late fourteenth and fifteenth centuries, Siegfried Wenzel’s work indicates that moralized *picturae* had become part of the general rhetorical idiom of preaching rather than a peculiarity of English friars.

## People

### John Ridevall

**[Documented]** English Franciscan, active at Oxford in the 1330s; author of *Fulgencius metaforalis* and associated with an Apocalypse commentary.

**[Disputed]** Works once or sometimes assigned to him include an Ovid commentary more usually connected with Pierre Bersuire and a commentary on Walter Map sometimes assigned to John of Wales.

**[Negative finding]** No contemporary anecdote survives of Ridevall publicly demonstrating the technique or performing feats of memory.

### Robert Holcot

**[Documented]** English Dominican theologian and biblical commentator, c. 1290–1349.

**[Tradition]** He is said to have died while ministering during the Black Death. The death date is accepted; the pastoral circumstances are transmitted as a story rather than securely established by a contemporary eyewitness.

**[Disputed]** His role in Richard de Bury’s *Philobiblon* has long been debated. It is irrelevant to the authorship of the pictures and should not be used as proof of his mnemonic practices.

### Beryl Smalley

Recovered the “classicizing friars” as an identifiable intellectual formation and supplied Yates with her primary textual framework.

### Fritz Saxl

Located and analyzed illustrated continental compilations. His 1942 “A Spiritual Encyclopaedia of the Later Middle Ages” made the later materialization of the pictures central to art-historical discussion. [Bibliographic record](https://philpapers.org/rec/SAXASE).

### Frances Yates

Synthesized Smalley and Saxl within a history of artificial memory. Her category “invisible pictures” remains illuminating, though too categorical as a statement of authorial intention.

### Practitioners, patrons, students and enemies

**[Documented collectively]** Intended and actual users were friars, university students, biblical lecturers, preachers, compilers, rubricators, indexers and later illustrators.

**[Negative finding]** No named student is documented as losing his memory through using this system. No auctioneer or professor’s-party anecdote belongs to Ridevall or Holcot. Those anecdotes derive from other memory traditions and must not be silently transferred here.

**[Negative finding]** No patron commissioned the system in the sense that Francis I patronized Camillo or Henry III engaged Bruno.

## The magic question

### Was it magical?

The best source-based answer is **no evidence of operative magic**.

**[Text]**

- The figures are moralized within orthodox Christian preaching.
- Pagan gods are subordinated to virtues, vices and biblical teaching.
- The procedure uses rhyme, bodily attributes, inscriptions, textual concordance and allegory.
- No spirit invocation, planetary timing, suffumigation, consecration, magical name or talismanic operation is prescribed.

### Pagan imagery and idolatry

The image of Idolatry is itself an attack on false worship. Ridevall’s mythography justifies pagan fables by uncovering a Christian or ethical *integumentum*. Holcot repeatedly domesticates classical material for moral instruction.

**[Tension, not prosecution]** The friars’ false or loose attributions to Fulgentius, Roman priests and Remigius could be criticized as bad source practice or fictional antiquarianism. That does not make them magical.

### Ars notoria

The *Ars notoria* promised knowledge and enhanced faculties through prayers, divine names, ritual observances and complex *notae*. It was controversial and sometimes condemned.

Ridevall and Holcot differ fundamentally:

| *Ars notoria* | Ridevall–Holcot |
|---|---|
| Knowledge claimed through revelation or ritual | Knowledge organized through study and preaching |
| Sacred names and prayers | Rhyme, attributes, biblical quotations |
| Diagrammatic *notae* may possess operative force | Pictures signify moral propositions |
| Susceptible to magical classification | Treated as moralized rhetoric and exegesis |

**[Negative finding]** No medieval source was found accusing Ridevall or Holcot of practising the *Ars notoria*, necromancy or image magic.

### Heresy and consequences

**[Negative finding]** No Inquisition proceeding, university condemnation or ecclesiastical penalty is known concerning these pictures.

Holcot did participate in controversial scholastic debates, including questions about divine foreknowledge, future contingents, faith and intuitive cognition. Those controversies must not be conflated with an accusation that his mnemonic pictures were heretical.

### The “accused magician”

**[Modern conflation if applied here]** Stories of memory masters accused of magic belong elsewhere—for example to later Renaissance practitioners or to reputations surrounding extraordinary mnemonic performance. No such documented episode attaches to Ridevall or Holcot.

## Afterlife

### 1. Immediate medieval reception

Ridevall’s work circulated quickly in England and then across Europe. Its materials were:

- excerpted;
- abridged;
- indexed;
- detached from the continuous commentary;
- combined with Holcot;
- combined with *Gesta Romanorum*, moralized Seneca and Ovid;
- eventually illustrated.

Derivative titles include *Ymagines Fulgentii*, *Imagines Fulgentii moralizatae*, *De imaginibus virtutum* and material beginning *Prudentia depingebatur*. Attribution is unstable because scribes joined anonymous extracts from several authors.

### 2. *Gesta Romanorum*

Smalley argued that the compiler of the *Gesta Romanorum* borrowed from Holcot’s collected *Moralitates*, rather than Holcot borrowing from the *Gesta*. Her evidence is distributional: stories shared with Holcot occur in the *Moralitates* but not correspondingly across his earlier commentaries.

This direction of borrowing supplies the terminus before 1342, but remains a scholarly reconstruction.

### 3. Catalan reception

Francesc Eiximenis used Holcot’s Friendship in *Dotzè del Crestià*, ch. 807, “Qui figura vera amistat en semblança d’una imatge,” 1385–1386.

A Barcelona clerk, Bernat d’Esplugues, owned a copy of the *Moralitates*. Anselm Turmeda in 1398 praised preaching that turned the fables of great poets into good moral instruction. These cases show the technique moving beyond Oxford into courtly, civic and Catalan religious culture.

### 4. Material picture-books

The decisive fifteenth-century transformation was from verbal picture to image-text ensemble. Pal. lat. 1726 and Casanatense 1404 do not reveal the original format; they show how later readers reinterpreted it.

The stages are:

> verbal personification → excerpt collection → rubricated *imago/pictura* → reserved illustration space → painted cycle → printed iconography.

### 5. Friendship and Renaissance iconology

Holcot’s Friendship had a long and unusually traceable afterlife:

- fifteenth-century manuscript paintings;
- Gregor Reisch, *Margarita philosophica*;
- humanist and emblematic treatments;
- Johannes Sambucus, *Emblemata* (1564), *Vera amicitia*;
- Nicolaus Reusner’s *Emblemata*;
- *Mikrokosmos/Parvus mundus* traditions;
- Cesare Ripa, *Iconologia*, especially illustrated editions;
- seventeenth-century emblem collections;
- Orthodox marriage-sermon books printed at Lviv in 1644 and 1645.

Ripa changes Holcot’s male youth into a female personification, retains the exposed heart and paired inscriptions, and adds features from other emblematic sources: white dress, myrtle and pomegranate, and the elm embraced by a vine. The transmission is therefore compilation, not faithful copying.

The three Holcot antitheses remain recognizable:

- *hyems et aestas*;
- *longe/procul et prope*;
- *mors et vita*.

Ernst Gombrich treated this descent as evidence that Ripa’s iconology grew out of medieval didacticism. [Discussion of Holcot and Ripa](https://www.fondazioneterradotranto.it/2013/08/19/quella-seconda-mensola-del-balcone-del-castello-di-nardo-56/).

### 6. Dante and frescoes

**[No demonstrated direct descent]** Dante’s memory, moral topography and personification belong to a broader common culture, but no evidence shows that he used Ridevall or Holcot.

**[Disputed/general analogy]** Yates speculated that medieval artificial memory could illuminate grotesque figures and moral fresco cycles. This is suggestive at the level of shared habits—ordered personification, vivid vice and virtue, inscription—but direct dependence must be proved work by work.

### 7. Later literature

Borges’s Funes and Eco’s libraries concern total memory, textual order and the dangers of indiscriminate recall. They are useful comparative afterlives of memory culture, but:

**[Negative finding]** no textual evidence was located that Borges or Eco specifically drew on Ridevall’s Idolatry or Holcot’s pictures.

### 8. Modern memory palaces and athletes

Modern competitors use loci, striking images and associative coding. Holcot’s bodies could be adapted effectively to such practice.

**[Modern analogy]** Presenting Ridevall–Holcot as a medieval “memory palace,” or teaching their figures as a documented precursor of competitive memory, is anachronistic. Their primary task was ethical and homiletic invention, not rapid storage of cards or numbers.

### 9. Computing and combinatorics

There is no demonstrated line:

> Ridevall/Holcot → Lull → Leibniz → computer.

Lull’s combinatorial art developed independently before these Oxford works. Leibniz’s interest in Lullian combination belongs to another genealogy. Ridevall and Holcot do share abstract features with information systems:

- hierarchical records;
- embodied indexing;
- fixed field positions;
- keyword concordance;
- topical lookup;
- recombinable modules.

Those are illuminating structural analogies, not documentary descent.

## Controversies and disputes

### 1. Is this truly an “artificial-memory system”?

**For:** rhyme, ordered attributes, conspicuous figures, inscriptions, scriptural word-links and preacherly use produce a robust mnemonic mechanism.

**Against:** no explicit loci rules, rehearsal instructions or declaration of an *ars memoriae* survives.

**Verdict:** Holcot’s pictures are documented mnemonic-compositional devices within preaching; “system of artificial memory” is a useful but secondary classification. For Ridevall, the mnemonic reading is plausible and influential but should not replace “moralized mythographic commentary.”

### 2. Were the pictures invisible?

**Yates:** originally internal and not meant for material depiction.

**Evidence:** early English manuscripts are unillustrated; later continental manuscripts illustrate them; some witnesses reserve spaces.

**Verdict:** material pictures were unnecessary, and probably absent from the initial instructional situation. Absolute non-intention is unprovable.

### 3. Are the images ancient?

**Textual claim:** Holcot invokes Fulgentius, Roman practice, Vesta’s priests and Remigius.

**Evidence:** key pictures are absent from the alleged sources.

**Verdict:** “sham antique” is justified. The attributions are medieval learned fictions, misunderstandings or prestige devices.

### 4. Did Ridevall devise the technique?

He is an early and important exponent, not demonstrably its inventor. Rhymed *picturae*, virtues and vices, moralized mythology, sermon distinctions and embodied allegory have older roots. The precise direction of influence among Ridevall, Holcot and the *Fasciculus morum* requires individual textual comparison.

### 5. Did Holcot borrow Idolatry directly from Ridevall?

Smalley and Yates say yes, based on close similarity and chronology. Hanna’s fuller text strengthens the need to compare complete versions, but direct borrowing, shared source and circulating extract must still be distinguished. The broader Oxford mendicant milieu makes all three possible.

### 6. How many manuscripts?

Statements range from nine direct witnesses to “more than one hundred manuscripts in some form.” These figures count different things:

- direct witnesses of the continuous commentary;
- fragments;
- excerpted *Imagines*;
- derivative condensations;
- composite picture-books.

They are not necessarily contradictory.

### 7. *Fulgentius* or *Fulgencius*?

*Fulgentius metaforalis* is the traditional scholarly spelling used by Liebeschütz and Yates. Hanna prints *Fulgencius metaforalis*, reflecting the work’s textual form. Both titles identify the same work; citations should follow the edition used.

### 8. Ridevall or Holcot as author of *Imagines Fulgentii*

Composite manuscripts sometimes assign Ridevall-derived *Imagines* and anonymous *Aenigmata* to Holcot. Modern scholarship separates them:

- *Imagines Fulgentii*: derived from Ridevall;
- *Aenigmata*: anonymous;
- *Moralitates*: Holcot.

The Madrid manuscript catalogue explicitly records this correction.

### 9. Date of the *Moralitates*

Smalley’s c. 1334–before 1342 date depends on:

- development from the Minor Prophets commentary;
- direction of borrowing with *Gesta Romanorum*;
- time allowed for circulation.

It is a strong relative chronology, not an authorial date.

### 10. Yates and “memory for words”

Her proposal remains possible in a limited sense: exact short phrases are visibly assigned to locations on an image.

It becomes misleading if expanded to mean that Holcot encoded continuous discourse verbatim. The dominant function is topical and compositional.

### 11. The Casanatense plate

Yates identifies plate 4c as Holcot’s Penance and calls it a later illustration that “we ought not to be seeing.”

The identification is secure at the level of textual correspondence. What is not secure is the implication that its painter violated the intended ontology of the image. The illustrator may instead have understood *pictura* literally or converted a mnemonic text into a teaching picture.

### 12. The Koblenz “1268” problem

A catalogue page can misleadingly display “1268” beside a volume containing Holcot. Since Holcot’s works postdate that year by decades, 1268 cannot be the production date of his *Moralitates*. It derives from a copied colophon in another section or from the exemplar’s textual history.

### 13. Magic, heresy and persecution

No evidence supports these claims for this system. Any account that supplies an Inquisition trial, accusation of sorcery or talismanic use without a new primary citation is legend or modern conflation.

### 14. Famous mnemonic anecdotes

The auctioneer, professor’s party, student who lost his memory and accused magician do not occur in the Ridevall–Holcot sources examined. Including them as episodes of this system would be modern invention.

## Open questions

1. Can the direction of borrowing between Ridevall and Holcot be demonstrated clause by clause from Hanna’s complete editions?
2. Which of Holcot’s twenty-six prophetic pictures are original combinations, and which derive from sermon aids now unedited?
3. Does any early witness contain reader annotations explicitly saying *ad memoriam*, *memoriter*, or equivalent?
4. How consistently do verse order, attribute order and exegetical order correspond across all manuscripts?
5. Did scribes alter bodily attributes because they misunderstood the mnemonic structure?
6. Were reserved blank spaces intended for illustrations, diagrams, decorated initials or merely rubrication?
7. How many of Hanna’s “more than one hundred” witnesses contain the continuous *Fulgencius*, and how many contain only derivative *Imagines*?
8. Can the Oxford Greyfriars library reconstruction be tested against surviving medieval catalogues?
9. Is Holcot’s fictive citation of Remigius deliberate prosopopoeia, memory failure, a lost intermediary, or scribal corruption?
10. Did preachers recite the rhymed *pictura* aloud to congregations, or use it privately to generate prose?
11. How early did Friendship acquire actual inscriptions in pictures rather than merely in descriptive text?
12. What exact manuscript stands between Holcot and Eiximenis?
13. Can the transformation of the green youth into Ripa’s female Amicitia be mapped through every surviving illustrated witness?
14. How should “memory diagram” be defined so that indices, body schemas and actual diagrams are not conflated?
15. Does the complete *Fulgencius* require a revised account of the relative priority of Ridevall, Holcot and later moralized mythography?
