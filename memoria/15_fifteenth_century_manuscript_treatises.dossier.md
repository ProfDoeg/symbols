# The fifteenth-century manuscript treatises: Research Dossier

## Basic identification

**Name:** The fifteenth-century manuscript *artes memorativae*—a modern grouping, not the title of a single work and not demonstrably a single “school.”

**Principal exponents and witnesses:** Lodovico Strassoldo da Pirano, OFM; Matthaeus of Verona, OP; Jacopo Ragone/Ragona of Vicenza; Luca Braga; the anonymous *Ars memoriae artificialis, pater reverende* family; an anonymous devotional *De memoria artificiali* in Venice; and an illustrated German-language mnemonic manuscript now associated with the Austrian National Library.

**Date:** Approximately 1420–1477. The principal dated points are Matthaeus of Verona’s first redaction of 2 October 1420 and revision of 28 August 1423; Lodovico’s probable composition during his teaching at Padua, about 1422–24; the *Memoria fecunda*, Bologna, 19 July 1425; Ragone’s treatise, 1434; and Braga’s work, Padua, 1477.

**Principal texts and manuscripts:**

- Lodovico, *Regule memorie artificialis*: Venice, Biblioteca Nazionale Marciana, Lat. VI, 274 (old X, 226), fols. 5r–15r; Marciana Lat. XIV, 292, fols. 180r–194v and 209v–213r; Vatican City, BAV, Vat. lat. 5347, fols. 1r–17v; Vienna, ÖNB, Cod. 3130, fols. 63r–67r; and additional witnesses listed below.
- Matthaeus of Verona, *De arte memorandi*: especially Naples, Biblioteca Nazionale, V.C.20, ending at fol. 310v; Munich, BSB, Clm 14260; St Paul im Lavanttal, Stiftsbibliothek, Cod. 137/4.
- Jacopo Ragone, *Artificialis memoriae regulae*: London, British Library, Additional MS 10,438, among other Latin and vernacular witnesses.
- Anonymous devotional *De memoria artificiali*: Venice, Marciana Lat. VI, 238, fols. 1r ff.
- Luca Braga: London, British Library, Additional MS 10,438, fols. 19r ff.
- Illustrated Vienna manuscript: cited by Yates as “Vienna National Library, Codex 5395,” after Ludwig Volkmann. The present ÖNB *Tabulae* catalogue instead describes **Cod. 5393** as *Tractatus de arte memorativa cum figuris pictis*, while Cod. 5395 is catalogued as an astrological excerpt from Haly Abenragel. This discrepancy must be checked against the physical manuscript, accession history, and Volkmann’s original citation; “Cod. 5395” should not be repeated as an unquestionably current shelfmark. [ÖNB manuscript catalogue](https://manuscripta.at/_scripts/php/cat_onb2pdf.php?cat1=tabulae&lit=8148&ms_code=AT8500-5393)

**Principle in one sentence:** Fixed, ordered architectural or object-loci function like a writing surface, upon which temporary, vivid, moving, personally intelligible images are mentally placed; recollection consists in traversing the loci in their established order and interpreting the images.

**Yates:** *The Art of Memory* (1966), Chapter V, especially pp. 106–109; supplemented and partly revised in “Lodovico da Pirano’s Memory Treatise” (1976), pp. 111–22.

### Evidentiary labels used below

- **[Text]** Directly stated or shown in a primary text or manuscript.
- **[Manuscript]** Physical or catalogue evidence concerning a witness.
- **[Reconstruction]** A modern scholarly inference from textual comparison or historical context.
- **[Yates]** Frances Yates’s interpretation.
- **[Tradition]** An attribution or narrative transmitted by premodern sources but not independently established.
- **[Disputed]** A claim seriously challenged or left unresolved in scholarship.
- **[Legend]** An anecdote or attribution unsupported by contemporary evidence.
- **[Modern invention]** A modern synthesis or application not attested in the medieval sources.
- **[Absence]** No supporting evidence was found in the specified corpus.

## The system in detail

## 1. The common classical mechanism

The family is united less by a unique doctrine than by a technical grammar inherited from the pseudo-Ciceronian *Rhetorica ad Herennium*, Book III.

### Places and images

**[Text: *Ad Herennium* III.16.29]**

> *Constat igitur artificiosa memoria ex locis et imaginibus. Locos appellamus eos qui breviter, perfecte, insignite aut natura aut manu sunt absoluti, ut eos facile naturali memoria comprehendere et amplecti queamus: ut aedes, intercolumnium, angulum, fornicem, et alia quae his similia sunt. Imagines sunt formae quaedam et notae et simulacra eius rei quam meminisse volumus.*

Gloss: “Artificial memory therefore consists of places and images. Places are settings, naturally or artificially formed, compact, complete and conspicuous enough to be readily grasped by natural memory—a house, an intercolumnar space, a corner, an arch, and the like. Images are forms, signs, or likenesses of what we wish to remember.”

Standard edition: *Rhetorica ad Herennium*, ed. and trans. Harry Caplan, Loeb Classical Library 403 (Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press, 1954), III.16.29. A modern discussion reproducing the Latin and Caplan’s translation is available in the notes to [“Remembering Doomsday”](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/02666280802045699).

Places are the durable support; images are the erasable writing. The remembered material is recovered by mentally visiting the places, ordinarily forward but also backward or from an intermediate point.

### Order

**[Text: *Ad Herennium* III.17–18]**

The places must:

- form an unambiguous sequence;
- be learned securely before images are deposited;
- be neither too similar nor too crowded;
- be moderately sized;
- be moderately illuminated;
- stand at moderate intervals;
- normally be empty when first learned;
- receive conspicuous markers at every fifth and tenth position.

The famous fifth-place marker is a golden hand; the tenth may be a person named Decimus. The fifteenth-century manuscripts repeatedly preserve, alter, or concretize this rule.

### Images

**[Text: *Ad Herennium* III.20.32]**

Effective images are exceptional rather than ordinary: beautiful or ugly, comic or cruel, crowned or bloodied, deformed or otherwise emotionally arresting. They should be active—later manuscripts call them *imagines agentes*—because action binds image, place, and intended meaning.

The anonymous Roman author distinguishes:

- *memoria rerum*: remembering subject matter, propositions, arguments, or narrative units;
- *memoria verborum*: remembering words or verbal sequences.

The fifteenth-century treatises elaborate both categories by applying medieval grammatical and logical operations to syllables, letters, names, numbers, dates, merchandise, arguments, scholastic divisions, and devotional histories.

### The lawsuit image

The requested lawsuit example belongs to the classical source, not uniquely to the fifteenth-century manuscripts.

**[Text: *Ad Herennium* III.20.33]**

> *Si accusator dixerit ab reo hominem veneno necatum et hereditatis causa factum arguerit et eius rei multos dixerit testes et conscios esse, si hoc primum, ut ad defendendum nobis expeditum sit, meminisse volemus, in primo loco rei totius imaginem conformabimus: aegrotum in lecto cubantem faciemus ipsum illum de quo agetur, si formam eius detinebimus; si eum non agnoverimus, aliquem aegrotum non de minimo loco sumemus, ut cito in mentem venire possit. Et reum ad lectum eius adstituemus, dextra poculum, sinistra tabulas, medico testiculos arietinos tenentem.*

Gloss: To remember an accusation that a defendant poisoned a man for an inheritance, one imagines the sick victim in bed; the accused stands beside him holding a cup in his right hand and tablets in his left, while holding ram’s testicles with a finger—the punning *testiculi* recalls the witnesses, the cup the poison, and the tablets the will.

This exemplifies the method inherited by Ragone and the anonymous vernacular texts: reduce an argument to agents, objects, actions, and phonetic or semantic cues, then stage them in a fixed locus.

## 2. Lodovico da Pirano

### Author, date and institutional setting

**[Documented/reconstruction]** Lodovico Strassoldo or Traversari da Pirano was a Conventual Franciscan, born around 1390. He studied at Padua and taught philosophy, theology, and rhetoric there in the 1420s and early 1430s. He later participated in Franciscan administration and probably the Council of Basel, became bishop of Senj and then Forlì, resigned the latter see, and died around 1450 or 1451. The composition of the *Regule* is conventionally placed in 1422–24 because it fits his Paduan teaching period, not because the surviving text carries an explicit composition date. [Treccani biography](https://www.treccani.it/enciclopedia/ludovico-da-pirano_%28Dizionario-Biografico%29/); [Croatian Biographical Lexicon](https://bl.lzmk.hr/clanak/ludovik-iz-pirana)

### Attribution

**[Manuscript]** Only Marciana Lat. VI, 274 explicitly names Lodovico as author. Other longer witnesses are anonymous.

**[Disputed qualification]** “By Lodovico” therefore describes an attributed textual family. It does not establish that every paragraph, diagram, or recension was written by him.

### Opening attribution to Democritus

**[Text: Lodovico, ed. Ziliotto, p. 212]**

> *Democritus Atheniensis philosophus huius artis primus inventor fuit; qui dum existimaret memoriam nostram valde fragilem ac variabilem esse, ideo ad subsidium nostre memorie hanc artem edidit.*

Gloss: “Democritus, the Athenian philosopher, was the first inventor of this art; judging our memory very fragile and variable, he produced this art as an aid to it.”

**[Tradition, not history]** No surviving ancient text establishes Democritus as the inventor of the architectural art of memory. The Simonides narrative is the ancient tradition transmitted by Cicero and Quintilian. Lodovico’s statement is evidence for a fifteenth-century attribution, not for Democritean authorship.

### Places

Lodovico preserves the classical writing analogy and the *Ad Herennium* rules:

- number and abundance of places;
- a stable order;
- empty or visually uncluttered places;
- marking each fifth place;
- distinctiveness;
- moderate size;
- moderate illumination;
- moderate distance.

**[Text/reconstruction]** The teaching is not merely architectural. A numbered list of one hundred familiar objects provides a finer-grained system inside, or in conjunction with, larger architectural spaces. Examples include a *paternoster* or rosary, anvil, helmet, lantern, tripod, foodstuffs, household articles, tools, and commercial objects.

**[Correction to Yates]** Yates called these “prefabricated memory images.” Later analysis distinguishes more carefully between:

1. the architectural container;
2. permanent, numbered **loci-objects** placed within it;
3. temporary images of the current material placed upon or made to interact with those objects.

The objects thus function more like addressable sub-loci than like the disposable images of the thing presently being memorized.

### Images and association

Lodovico distinguishes three broad image-forming procedures:

1. **Similarity:** a person, object, quality, action, name, or sound directly resembles or conventionally indicates the target.
2. **Partial similarity or linguistic manipulation:** syllables and letters are transposed, added, removed, or altered.
3. **Dissimilarity or imposed signification:** opposites, habitual associations, metonymies, arbitrary alphabetic codes, or a memorable bearer of a quality represents the target.

Examples recorded in the edition include:

> *Idolum in toto dissimile per impositionem … Per alphabetum: ut si imponerem aliqua animalia vel aliquas res ad representanda elementa alphabeti, ut puta si ego imponerem quod unus asinus representaret mihi hanc litteram A, et unus bos significaret hanc litteram B…*

Gloss: “An image wholly dissimilar by imposition … through the alphabet: if I assign animals or other things to represent the alphabetic elements—for example, an ass for A and an ox for B—I may compose images representing letters, syllables or words.” Lodovico prudently limits such combinations to short words to avoid confusion. The Latin is quoted in Lodovico Dolce’s annotated *Dialogo* discussion of Lodovico’s rules. [Annotated text](https://dokumen.pub/dialogo-del-modo-di-accrescere-e-conservar-la-memoria-88-7642-103-3.html)

### Things, words and retrieval

**[Text]** Contrary to Yates’s later suggestion that the “Greek” line was primarily interested in word-memory, Lodovico knows and uses both *memoria rerum* and *memoria verborum*. For a history, one imagines the principal person performing a characteristic action. For names and foreign words, phonetic, syllabic, alphabetic, metonymic, or personally imposed cues are used.

**[Correction]** Heimann-Seelbach concludes that Yates’s description of Lodovico as a representative of an essentially word-oriented Greek current is untenable: the treatise explicitly integrates traditional subject-memory. [*Ars und scientia*, preview pp. 29–32](https://api.pageplace.de/preview/DT0400.9783110937862_A19893224/preview-9783110937862_A19893224.pdf)

### The towers and “multiplication of places”

**[Manuscript]** Marciana VI, 274, the witness printed by Ziliotto, lacks the tables and tower diagrams. Marciana XIV, 292 and Vat. lat. 5347 preserve additional diagrams and tabular material.

**[Yates]** She describes them as “rows of towers” for multiplying places.

**[Later reconstruction]** Recent work treats the principal drawing as a geometrically organized arrangement of **nine towers**, connecting it with mathematical and geometrical interests shared by Lodovico, Matthaeus of Verona, and Giovanni Fontana. It is not a Lullian wheel, talisman, or cosmological diagram. The exact operation should be reconstructed from the manuscript captions and recensional context rather than from Yates’s small reproduction alone. Valentina Cacopardo explicitly redirects attention from a hypothetical Byzantine tradition to this Paduan geometrical setting. [Cacopardo dissertation, Part I](https://sas-space.sas.ac.uk/9846/1/VCacopardoMemoryandImaginationPhDthesis.pdf)

### Recensions and manuscripts

Heimann-Seelbach lists:

- Dubrovnik, Dominican Monastery, MS 36 V 19, fols. 2r–10r;
- Paris, BnF, Latin 8749, formerly NAL 468;
- Paris, BnF, Latin 8747, fols. 49r–56v;
- Parma, Biblioteca Palatina, Pal. 746, fols. 29r–33v;
- Rome, Biblioteca Nazionale, Gesuitico 973 (3102), fols. 53v–57v;
- Vatican City, BAV, Vat. lat. 5347, fols. 1r–17v;
- Vatican City, Archivio di San Pietro, B 125, fols. 30r–43r;
- Venice, Marciana Lat. VI, 274 (old X, 226), fols. 5r–15r;
- Venice, Marciana Lat. XIV, 292, fols. 180r–181r and 209v–213r, with the full text at 182r–194v;
- Vienna, ÖNB, Cod. 3130, fols. 63r–67r.

Treccani additionally distinguishes a longer anonymous recension from a shorter recension carrying Lodovico’s name, and lists Assisi, Biblioteca Comunale, MS 562, fols. 24v–25v. It also notes a fifteenth-century vernacular version at Dubrovnik. [Treccani](https://www.treccani.it/enciclopedia/ludovico-da-pirano_%28Dizionario-Biografico%29/)

**[Textual finding]** Marciana XIV, 292 is not simply “the illustrated copy at fol. 182 ff.” It has a prologue fragment at 180r–v, a brief Italian regimen for natural memory at 180v–181r, the full text at 182r–194v, and further related material at 209v–213r.

## 3. Matthaeus of Verona

### Identification and date

**[Text/manuscript]** Matthaeus identifies himself as:

> *frater Matheus de Verona, ordinis predicatorum baccalariorum minimus*

Gloss: “Brother Matthew of Verona, the least of the bachelors of the Order of Preachers.”

His colophon in Naples, Bibl. Naz. V.C.20 reads:

> *Explicit ars memorandi completa de 1423 die 28 augusti per fr. Matheum de Verona, que potest dici correptorium et supplementum prime complete de 1420, die secundo octubris.*

Gloss: “Here ends the art of remembering, completed by Brother Matthew of Verona on 28 August 1423, which may be called a correction and supplement of the first [version], completed on 2 October 1420.”

He was prior of the Dominican convent at Verona in 1415, a bachelor at Padua in 1419, lectured on Peter Lombard’s *Sentences* in 1421–22, and became master of theology in 1422. The modern critical text and German translation appear in Sabine Seelbach and Angelika Kemper, eds., *Zentrale Gedächtnislehren des Spätmittelalters* (2019). [Edition preview](https://api.pageplace.de/preview/DT0400.9783110575231_A33546025/preview-9783110575231_A33546025.pdf)

### Definition and structure

**[Text]**

> *Memoria artificialis est scientia anime recolendi memorata…*

Gloss: “Artificial memory is a science of the soul for recollecting things committed to memory.”

The work treats three things:

> *de locis, ymaginibus et memorabilibus*

“places, images, and things to be remembered.”

### Scholastic classification of places

Matthaeus describes loci through an Aristotelian classificatory vocabulary:

- substantial versus accidental components of the art;
- complex versus simple loci;
- natural-general loci: valley, mountain;
- natural-particular loci: tree, lion;
- artificial-general loci: city, house, monastery;
- artificial-particular loci: door, fireplace, furniture or tool.

Twenty larger subdivisions, typically rooms or contexts, each hold five associated loci-objects, producing a one-hundred-place matrix. Groups are semantically coherent: writing room, kitchen, knightly equipment, feast, bakery, and so forth.

The classical requirements of moderate size, distance, illumination, emptiness, and fifth-place marking remain operative.

### Knowledge as architecture

A discipline can occupy an imaginary house, with its subdivisions assigned to rooms or objects. Grammar, for example, may be divided into orthography, prosody, syntax or *diasynthetica*, and etymology.

### Image theory

Matthaeus makes *similitudo* a technical and explicitly differentiated relation:

- likeness in word and meaning;
- partial phonetic resemblance;
- semantic association;
- bodily gesture;
- metaphor and metonymy;
- even total dissimilarity used contrastively.

His celebrated example of complete dissimilarity is:

> an Ethiopian with white teeth for whiteness.

**[Reconstruction]** The example adapts a logical problem from Porphyry’s *Isagoge* and shows that this branch is not merely practical household mnemonics. It merges rhetorical image construction with Aristotelian–Porphyrian semantics.

### Word-memory

Letters and syllables may be transformed by:

- transposition;
- addition;
- subtraction;
- mutation.

Alphabetic images may derive from letter-shape, initial sound, or alphabetic number. Technical words can be converted into familiar visual terms: *nominativus* through an object with a similar sound; *genitivus* through *genu*, “knee”; January through *janua parva*, “a little door.”

### Applications

The treatise covers:

- scholastic subject divisions;
- propositions and syllogisms;
- arguments;
- texts and verses;
- natural history;
- accounting and credit;
- cards, dice, chess and board games;
- foreign or otherwise unfamiliar words.

**[Correction to Yates]** This breadth makes “scholastic tradition” accurate but incomplete. The same Dominican text accommodates university logic, domestic material culture, gaming, commerce, and oral recitation.

## 4. Jacopo Ragone or Ragona of Vicenza

### Author and patron

**[Text/manuscript]** The treatise opens:

> *Iussu tuo princeps illustrissime artificialis memoriae regulas…*

“By your command, most illustrious prince, [I set forth] the rules of artificial memory…”

It is dated to 1434 and addressed to Gianfrancesco I Gonzaga, ruler and from 1433 marquis of Mantua.

**[Documented]** Ragone was a Vicentine nobleman and was made count palatine by Emperor Sigismund in 1436.

**[Reconstruction]** A client relationship with Gonzaga is plausible but not independently proved. No other surviving work by Ragone is presently known. Cacopardo notes eighteenth-century reports of three copies once held in Vicenza and Padua, not all of which have been securely identified today. [Cacopardo, pp. 152–54](https://sas-space.sas.ac.uk/9846/1/VCacopardoMemoryandImaginationPhDthesis.pdf)

### What counts as a locus

Ragone makes an unusually explicit distinction:

> *Loci sunt non anguli, ut existimant aliqui, sed imagines fixe supra quibus, sicut supra carta, alie pinguntur imagines delebiles, sicut litterae; unde loci sunt sicuti materia. Imagines vero sicuti forma. Differunt ergo sicut fixum et non fixum.*

Gloss: “Places are not corners, as some think, but fixed images upon which, as upon paper, other erasable images are painted like letters. Thus loci are as matter, while images are as form: they differ as fixed from unfixed.”

This is important. Ragone’s hundred household objects are **fixed mnemonic addresses** within architectural rooms, not merely a list of images to be replaced each time.

### Architectural rules

**[Text]**

> *Expedit etiam ut in locis servetur modus, ne sit inter illos distantia nimis brevis vel nimium remota, sed moderata ut puta sex vel octo aut decem pedum vel circa, iuxta magnitudinem camere, ne sit in illis nimia claritas vel obscuritas, sed lux mediocris.*

Gloss: “Moderation should be preserved in the places: their distance must be neither too short nor too great, but moderate—say six, eight, or ten feet, according to the size of the room; there should be neither excessive brightness nor darkness, but moderate light.”

The practitioner should establish a house of up to twenty distinguishable rooms—halls, bedrooms, kitchens and stairs. Ragone warns against selecting the friars’ cells because they are too similar:

> *cave ne assumas cellas fratrum propter nimiam illarum similitudinem*

The rooms should be empty of movable things. Doorways are also discouraged as loci.

A tiny space should not be forced to contain an enormous image:

> *violentia aborret cogitatio*

“thought recoils from violence.” A spider’s hole is unsuitable for an imagined horse.

### The hundred loci-objects

Five familiar objects are fixed in each of twenty rooms. The surviving list uses vernacular Venetian vocabulary inside the Latin text. The first ten in the derivative illustrated Venetian manuscript are:

1. rosary or *paternoster*;
2. Savoy cabbage;
3. cheese;
4. bread;
5. fish;
6. garlic;
7. apple;
8. coif;
9. holy-water bucket;
10. abacus.

The objects should be domestic, familiar, and visually affecting:

> *Talia obiecta que loci dicuntur debent esse per se immutantia visus et res tibi familiares et domestice.*

Gloss: “Such objects, which are called loci, should in themselves affect the sight and be things familiar and domestic to you.”

### Images must move

> *Res qualiscunque sit semper locanda est cum motu aut ridiculoso aut admirativo, crudeli aut fetido vel alio modo insueto…*

Gloss: “Whatever the thing, it must always be placed with motion—comic, astonishing, cruel, foul-smelling, or in some other unusual fashion.”

The reason is psychological: strange, disgusting, cruel or surprising things strike the senses, command attention, and are therefore more readily recalled.

### Known names

To remember a rich man named Peter, the practitioner imagines a personally known Peter—friend, enemy or acquaintance—doing something ridiculous or unusual in the first place. In the second, a known Albert might hang himself or perform some other ugly or exceptional act.

**[Text, Ragone; parallel anonymous vernacular]**

> *Cum vis recordari unius divitis qui nominatur Petrus, immediate ponas unum Petrum quem tu cognoscas … qui Petrus faciat aliquid ridiculum in illo loco, vel aliquid inusitatum.*

The close vernacular parallel reads:

> *quando tu te volli aricordare de uno Piero richo … metamo uno Piero lo qual tu cognoschi … el qual Piero façe qualche solaço in quello luogho hover alguna cossa no usada.*

The fifth and tenth loci are marked:

> *per quintum vel decimum locum debes ponere unam manum auream aut unum imperatorem…*

A golden hand or magnificently dressed emperor occupies the fifth or tenth place. [Laura Ramello, pp. 887–89](https://iris.unito.it/retrieve/handle/2318/51220/167856/atti%20sifr.pdf)

### Syllabic examples

For the unfamiliar name *Abraam*:

- A → *agnus*, lamb;
- BRA → *bracha*, breeches;
- AM → Ambrose.

The breeches are placed on the lamb’s head, while Ambrose tries to take them off. The action fixes both order and syllabification.

For *Chain*, Ragone suggests a familiar wooden basin, *cadinus*, using sound resemblance.

For *Malafaza Noradu*, he uses personalized fantasy: an exceptionally ugly-faced man represents *mala faza*; the second name is prompted through *nurus/nuora*, daughter-in-law. This is not etymology but deliberately private mnemonic sound-play.

### Patronal example

A familiar or eminent Ludovico is placed at the first locus-object, the rosary. He may tie it to his nose or ears, bite it, or tear it apart. Cacopardo plausibly identifies the intended Ludovico with Gianfrancesco Gonzaga’s son.

**[Reconstruction]** The identification is contextually strong but not explicit in the quoted rule.

### The palazzo drawing

**[Manuscript/Yates]** British Library Additional MS 10,438 contains a drawing of a *palazzo* used for forming memory places.

**[Limitation]** A complete folio-level description could not be verified from an openly accessible full-resolution digitization during this research. Yates’s notice should therefore be cited as a report, not treated as a substitute for direct codicological inspection.

### Reassessment

Earlier scholarship often treated Ragone as a mediocre compiler whose rough Latin reflected conceptual weakness.

**[Later reconstruction]** Cacopardo argues that this misses his innovation: he combines classical loci, Thomistic-Aristotelian psychology, the *Memoria fecunda*, Lodovico, and Matthaeus into a flexible court manual. Unlike visually prescriptive illuminated manuscripts, Ragone deliberately leaves the temporary images to the user’s imagination. His vernacular objects, card figures, patronal names, and commercial examples adapt the art to Gonzaga court culture. [Cacopardo, pp. 152–70](https://sas-space.sas.ac.uk/9846/1/VCacopardoMemoryandImaginationPhDthesis.pdf)

## 5. The anonymous *pater reverende* family

### Incipit

> *Ars memoriae artificialis, pater reverende, est ea qualiter homo ad recordandum de pluribus pervenire potest per memoriam artificialem de quibus recordari non possit per memoriam naturalem.*

Gloss: “Artificial memory, reverend father, is that by which a person may come, through artificial memory, to remember more things than he can remember by natural memory.”

The nearly verbal Venetian translation begins:

> *L’arte della artificiosa memoria, reverendo padre, è come lo homo possa pervegnire per la memoria artificial a recordarse de più cosse delle qual el non se possa arecordare per la memoria naturale.*

**[Correction]** This incipit is not the title of a single stable treatise. It marks a broad, internally variable textual family.

### Yale Marston MS 30

A significant vernacular witness is Beinecke Library, Marston MS 30, a paper manuscript of fifty folios, probably from the first half of the fifteenth century. It contains:

- fols. 1r–33r: an epitome of the *Rhetorica ad Herennium*;
- fols. 33r–46r: *Una altra arte de memoria*;
- fols. 45v–46r: full-page drawings of a left and a right hand used as mnemonic systems.

The mnemonic portion covers:

- learning the art;
- order and form of loci;
- images;
- embassies;
- arguments;
- sermons;
- testaments or texts;
- multiplying and changing loci;
- known and unknown names;
- letters and syllables;
- months, days and abacus figures;
- merchandise;
- a second abbreviated art;
- natural-memory remedies;
- left and right mnemonic hands.

### Merchant’s example: saffron weighing 137 pounds

A merchant arrives by sea and must pay customs on goods. Fearing that officials will report a fraudulent weight, he encodes the earlier weight of a saffron sack—137 pounds.

The number is divided into 1 and 37:

- 1 corresponds to a string or sack of *paternosters*;
- 37 corresponds to a small sack;
- the saffron sack is imagined together with these objects in the first prepared locus.

The primary vernacular explains:

> *tu te vuole arecordare che lo çafrano pesa … libre 137: partisi queste 3 figure in doe parte … la prima figura … 1 … e 37 … uno sacheto el qual sempre significa 37.*

This is a positional mnemonic code based on a previously memorized hundred-object list. It is practical, reusable, and capable of storing quantities, commodity types, dates, debtors and creditors. [Ramello, pp. 889–90](https://iris.unito.it/retrieve/handle/2318/51220/167856/atti%20sifr.pdf)

### Natural-memory remedies

Marston 30 also gives ointment recipes involving ivy gum and flowers, rosemary, turpentine, sage, betony, salt, old bear fat, marjoram and other substances. The user is to anoint the temples two or three times monthly, fast and abstain from sex that day, and seek the Creator’s grace.

**[Text]** This is medical or regimen material attached to the mnemonic art.

**[Not evidence of magic]** Premodern pharmacology may appear implausible today, but an herbal ointment is not thereby talismanic or demonic. No invocation, spirit-operation, astrological suffumigation, or magical character is reported in the passage.

## 6. The *Memoria fecunda*

Though omitted from Yates’s pp. 106–09 grouping, it is essential to the genealogy.

### Date and manuscripts

**[Text]** Vienna, ÖNB Cod. 4444, fol. 327v dates the work:

> *Data est hec ars memorativa … Bononie anno domini 1425 die 19 mensis julii.*

“This art of memory was given at Bologna on 19 July 1425.”

Heimann-Seelbach records at least fifteen witnesses in Basel, Berlin, Erfurt, Munich, Olomouc, Salzburg, Vienna and Wrocław. [Witness list and analysis](https://api.pageplace.de/preview/DT0400.9783110937862_A19893224/preview-9783110937862_A19893224.pdf)

### Definition

> *Memoria artificialis est dispositio ymaginaria in mente rerum sensibilium, supra quas memoria naturalis reflexa per eas commovetur et adiuvatur.*

Gloss: “Artificial memory is an imaginary disposition in the mind of sensible things, upon which natural memory, turning back through them, is stirred and assisted.”

### Contents and variants

The work has:

1. a theological prologue;
2. locus and image doctrine;
3. methods for remembering different forms of text and knowledge.

It includes conventional images for virtues and sins, liberal arts, senses and powers of the soul; phonetic decomposition of unfamiliar words; letters; numbers; months; and, in some witnesses, commercial and credit data.

**[Manuscript variation]** The passages on numbers and credit are absent from witnesses with expanded theological prologues. This shows active selection for differing audiences rather than passive copying.

**[Contested incipit]** Older catalogues sometimes gave *Intellectualis filii verbi* as the opening. Heimann-Seelbach explains that *Memoria fecunda deus pater eternus* was written around a large initial in Munich Clm 19876 and was overlooked by later readers or scribes.

---

## 7. The anonymous devotional *De memoria artificiali*

### Manuscript

Venice, Marciana Lat. VI, 238, fols. 1r ff.; copied in the fifteenth century. Yates thought the underlying text might be earlier.

### Content

**[Text as reported by Yates]** The author insists that artificial memory should serve devout meditation and spiritual consolation and says he will use only:

> *devotae imagines*  
> *sacrae historiae*

rather than fables or:

> *vana phantasmata*

“empty fantasies.”

Saints with their attributes are assigned to loci. The universe, salvation history, and roads to Heaven and Hell become ordered material for recollection.

### Dominican attribution

**[Yates/reconstruction]** Yates calls the author “probably also a Dominican,” partly because sections closely resemble Johannes Romberch’s later Dominican *Congestorium artificiose memorie*.

**[Not demonstrated]** Textual resemblance to a Dominican author does not alone establish authorship or institutional provenance. The attribution should remain “anonymous, possibly Dominican.”

### Relationship to Romberch

**[Textual reconstruction]** The near identity of passages suggests that Romberch’s printed work drew on a late-medieval manuscript tradition represented by Marciana VI, 238, or that both descend from a common exemplar.

**[Direction unresolved]** Without a critical edition and stemma, one cannot identify the exact intermediary or assume direct copying.

## 8. Luca Braga, Padua, 1477

### Source

London, British Library, Additional MS 10,438, fols. 19r ff.

**[Text/Yates]** Braga names Democritus but also Simonides and Thomas Aquinas.

### Consequence

This single fact weakens Yates’s taxonomy. If a “Democritus type” is defined by rejection or absence of Simonides and Thomas, Braga is not a clean member. His work instead displays the late-century agglomeration of classical, scholastic and legendary authorities.

### State of scholarship

**[Absence]** No reliable complete modern edition or full transcription of Braga’s text was located in the openly accessible sources consulted here. Heimann-Seelbach explicitly left it to Romance philology. Claims about its complete rules, diagrams, or dependence should therefore remain provisional.

## 9. The illustrated Vienna manuscript

### Shelfmark problem

Yates, following Volkmann, cites “Vienna National Library, Codex 5395.” The modern catalogue entry accessible through Manuscripta.at describes Cod. 5393 as *Tractatus de arte memorativa cum figuris pictis* and Cod. 5395 as astrology.

**[Disputed]** Possibilities include:

- a typographical error in Volkmann or Yates;
- renumbering;
- catalogue-layout misreading;
- transfer or rebinding.

The physical codex and ÖNB acquisition concordance must decide the matter.

### Surviving images

Volkmann reproduces the material in *Ars Memorativa*, Jahrbuch der Kunsthistorischen Sammlungen in Wien, new series 3 (1929), pp. 111–203, especially his pp. 124–31 and plates/figures cited by Yates as 115–124. The article is digitized by Heidelberg University Library. [Volkmann, digitized article](https://digi.ub.uni-heidelberg.de/diglit/jbksak1929/0179)

### Row of figures

The sequence includes:

- a crowned female figure at the head;
- male and female personifications;
- a devil or grotesque demonic figure;
- a central Christ;
- the mouth of Hell beneath Christ;
- numerous subsidiary objects or inscriptions attached to or surrounding bodies;
- a final inscription:

> *Ex locis et imaginibus ars memorativa constat Tullius ait.*

“Tullius says that the art of memory consists of places and images.”

One male figure is labelled:

> *Brueder Ottell*

“Brother Ottell.”

A female face may be patterned after a known virgin or woman:

> *Margaretha, Dorothea, Appolonia, Lucia, Anastasia, Agnes, Benigna, Beatrix … Anna, Martha, Maria, Elizabeth…*

### Interpretation

**[Yates]** The crowned woman is “almost certainly Prudence”; the remaining figures “probably” represent virtues and vices; Christ and Hell indicate a discourse about the ways to Heaven and Hell; the subsidiary figures probably encode words.

Every major noun in that reading needs qualification:

- **Prudence:** plausible iconographic identification, not an explicit surviving label cited by Yates.
- **Virtues and vices:** plausible series-level interpretation, not demonstrated figure by figure.
- **Heaven/Hell discourse:** strongly supported by Christ and Hell-mouth imagery.
- **Memory for words:** possible, but the precise verbal payload has not been decoded.
- **Brother Ottell:** documentary evidence of using a personally known face, assuming the label belongs to the figure as Volkmann reports.

### Memory rooms

Plans show a room divided into five loci:

- one in each of four corners;
- one in the centre.

**[Yates]** This regular fivefold arrangement represents a normal medieval and later interpretation of memory places.

**[Qualification]** Fivefold room-organization is well attested in Matthaeus, Ragone, Romberch and later works. “Normal” should mean recurrent and influential, not universal: classical authorities warned against excessive similarity, and medieval systems also use roads, churches, monasteries, towns, hands, alphabets, games and object matrices.

## Yates’s account and scholarship since

## What Yates argued

In Chapter V, pp. 106–09, Yates proposed the following sequence:

1. A “Democritus type” credited Democritus rather than Simonides.
2. It neglected the striking human figures of the *Ad Herennium*.
3. It concentrated on Aristotelian association.
4. It usually omitted Thomas Aquinas.
5. Lodovico exemplified the type.
6. Byzantine transmission might explain its Greek or non-Thomist features.
7. Lists beginning with a *paternoster* were “prefabricated memory images.”
8. The anonymous Dominican system Christianized the art through saints and sacred history.
9. The Vienna figures showed a debased survival of medieval image-writing.
10. Five-locus rooms represented a normal medieval and Renaissance practice.

Her language was repeatedly cautious:

> “may be called”;  
> “a possible source”;  
> “I put this forward only as a hypothesis”;  
> “I believe”;  
> “probably”;  
> “almost certainly.”

Later retellings have often hardened these provisional formulations into facts.

## Confirmed or substantially strengthened

### The fifteenth-century flowering

**[Confirmed]** The independent *ars memorativa* did flourish as a distinct genre in the early fifteenth century. Heimann-Seelbach’s survey documents roughly eighty-five texts in about 260 witnesses, far beyond Yates’s sample. [*Ars und scientia*](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/de/document/doi/10.1515/9783110937862/html)

### Classical inheritance with medieval innovation

**[Confirmed and refined]** These texts preserve *Ad Herennium* loci and images but expand them through scholastic psychology, grammatical transformations, numeric matrices, commercial practice, games, preaching, meditation and vernacular pedagogy.

### Dominican and Franciscan settings

**[Confirmed]** Matthaeus was a Dominican university theologian; Lodovico was a Franciscan teacher and bishop. Religious institutions were genuine production and transmission sites.

### Object lists and fivefold grouping

**[Confirmed, terminology corrected]** Lists of one hundred objects and groups of five are real and influential. Later scholarship shows that the objects often function as fixed loci or loci-markers, not simply disposable “prefabricated images.”

### Manuscript-to-print continuity

**[Confirmed]** Romberch and other printed authors inherited substantial material from manuscript traditions. The exact genealogies are more branching and compilatory than Yates could establish.

## Corrected or overturned

### The “Democritus type”

**[Overturned as a discrete school]** Heimann-Seelbach shows that Democritus appears in Lodovico, Pietro da Orvieto, *Memoria fecunda*, *Attendentes nonnulli*, Antonius de Mercatello, Jacobus Publicius and other texts belonging to different functional and intellectual groupings. Citation of Democritus alone cannot define a separate “Greek type.”

### Lodovico as word-memory specialist

**[Corrected]** Lodovico incorporates *memoria rerum* as well as verbal techniques. Yates’s later claim that the supposed Greek line cared for words but not things is contradicted by the text.

### Byzantine channel

**[Unsubstantiated]** Yates’s hypothesis rested on:

- Lodovico’s knowledge of Greek;
- attribution to Democritus;
- the existence of a Greek translation of the *Ad Herennium* memory section;
- perceived deviation from a Thomist Latin line.

No manuscript genealogy, Greek exemplar, translation sequence, shared error, or identifiable Byzantine technical vocabulary connects that Greek translation with Lodovico’s treatise. The hypothesis is historically imaginable but evidentially unsupported.

### Johannes Andreas Quirinus

**[Corrected]** Kristeller recorded “Jo. Andr. Quirinus, *de memoria artificiali*” at Marciana XIV, 292, fol. 179. Heimann-Seelbach found only a heading or entry on an otherwise blank page, followed by natural-memory recipes and then Lodovico’s text. No Quirinus treatise is presently known. Yates treated an apparent catalogue entry as a textual witness.

### “Troilus Boncompagno” as author

**[Corrected]** The work is anonymous and addressed to a Troilus Boncompagnus, probably the man attested giving a speech at Padua around 1455. He is not established as its author.

### Ragone as crude simplifier

**[Substantially revised]** His rough Latin and borrowing are real, but Cacopardo demonstrates deliberate adaptation to a courtly, vernacular and practical audience. Compilation and innovation are not mutually exclusive.

### Vienna Codex 5395

**[Bibliographical correction requiring confirmation]** Current catalogue data point to Cod. 5393, while 5395 contains astrology. A direct institutional verification remains necessary.

## Relation to Carruthers, Bolzoni, Rossi and Small

Mary Carruthers’s *The Book of Memory* and *The Craft of Thought* shift emphasis away from mnemonic “storage” alone toward memory as composition, ethical formation, meditation and invention. That framework clarifies why sacred histories and conventional virtues are not decorative additions: they organize thought and action.

Lina Bolzoni’s work on preaching and image-networks situates mnemonic practice among sermons, devotional pictures, emblematic figures and vernacular culture rather than treating it as an isolated occult technique.

Paolo Rossi first assembled important evidence for Ragone and the anonymous texts and correctly emphasized psychological observations in locus rules. His broad genealogy from memory arts through Lullism to universal-language projects remains valuable, but the direct line from these object systems to combinatory logic is contextual, not a demonstrated chain of manuscript descent.

Jocelyn Penny Small’s *Wax Tablets of the Mind* cautions against treating verbal descriptions, material images and interior mental images as interchangeable. This is particularly important for Yates’s reading of the Vienna drawings: a manuscript illustration can teach or prompt an internal image without being the internal image itself.

## Scholarship not directly determinative here

Bonner and Fidora on Llull; Sturlese, Ciliberto, Clucas and Gatti on Bruno; Bolzoni and Stagno on Camillo; and Ong on Ramus are essential to other catalogue systems. They do not provide direct evidence for Lodovico’s authorship, the *pater reverende* stemma, or the Vienna figures. Importing their conclusions into this corpus would risk turning later analogies into fifteenth-century facts.

## History and context

## Classical rhetoric

The foundational sequence is:

- pseudo-Cicero, *Rhetorica ad Herennium* III;
- Cicero, *De oratore* II.350–60;
- Quintilian, *Institutio oratoria* XI.2.

The manuscripts extract *memoria* from rhetoric’s five offices and make it an independent art.

## Aristotelian psychology

Aristotle’s *De memoria et reminiscentia* explains memory as involving a sensible image and recollection as an ordered search from one item to another through similarity, contrast or contiguity.

Thomas Aquinas integrated this psychology with rhetorical loci and images, especially in his commentary on Aristotle and his treatment of memory as a part of prudence. Matthaeus and Ragone reflect this world even where Thomas is not named.

**[Correction to Yates’s binary]** “Rhetorical” and “Aristotelian” are not separate systems here. The manuscripts repeatedly combine architectural rhetoric with Aristotelian association and scholastic semantics.

## Padua

Padua is the principal early centre:

- Matthaeus wrote and revised his treatise there;
- Lodovico taught there;
- Braga wrote there;
- Pietro da Orvieto’s and related texts circulated in the same wider northern Italian environment.

The university supplied logic, grammar, theology, optics and geometry; the city and its students supplied houses, classrooms, churches, games, merchants and civic objects.

## Dominican and Franciscan schools

The Dominicans provide Matthaeus, probably some devotional compilers, and later Romberch. The Franciscans provide Lodovico.

**[Important qualification]** The techniques were not proprietary “Dominican” or “Franciscan” arts. Texts circulated across orders, universities, courts, urban readers and vernacular communities.

## Courts

Ragone’s dedication and examples place the art at the Gonzaga court. Its contents suit patronal culture:

- dynastic names;
- card figures;
- embassies;
- speeches;
- debts and merchandise;
- flexible use by a lay prince or secretary.

## Commerce

The merchant carrying saffron and fearing customs fraud is documentary evidence that mnemonic manuals imagined commercial users. It does not prove how many real merchants adopted the technique.

## From manuscript to print

The manuscript systems feed into:

- Jacobus Publicius;
- Pietro da Ravenna;
- Johannes Romberch;
- Cosmas Rossellius;
- later vernacular manuals.

Romberch’s abbey diagrams combine architectural places, object-loci and vivid actions. Fifth and tenth loci are marked by a hand and cross. This is a printed elaboration of practices already visible in Matthaeus, Lodovico and Ragone.

## Lullism, Hermeticism and Ramism

**[No direct descent demonstrated]**

- Lodovico’s towers are not demonstrated to be Lullian combinatory wheels.
- The manuscripts contain no securely identified Hermetic talismanic operation.
- Ramus’s dialectical order belongs to a later criticism and reorganization of memory, not to the original construction of these texts.

The broader historical trajectory brings architectural mnemonics into contact with Lullian combination, Renaissance magic and Ramist method, but these contacts should not be retrojected into every fifteenth-century diagram.

## People

### Practitioners and transmitters

- **Lodovico da Pirano:** Franciscan teacher, theologian, rhetorician and later bishop.
- **Matthaeus of Verona:** Dominican prior, bachelor, lecturer on the *Sentences*, and master of theology.
- **Jacopo Ragone:** Vicentine nobleman and count palatine; compiler and adapter for the Gonzaga court.
- **Luca Braga:** author or scribe associated with a Paduan treatise of 1477; biography remains obscure.
- **Anonymous *pater reverende* compilers:** probably clerical in some instances, but the commercial and vernacular adaptations show a broader readership.
- **Anonymous devotional author:** possibly Dominican.
- **Johannes Romberch:** later Dominican transmitter in print.
- **Baccio Ziliotto:** first editor of Lodovico’s attributed text.
- **Ludwig Volkmann:** first major publisher of the Vienna images.
- **Paolo Rossi, Frances Yates, Sabine Heimann-Seelbach, Angelika Kemper, Laura Ramello and Valentina Cacopardo:** principal modern reconstructors.

### Patrons and implied users

- Gianfrancesco I Gonzaga;
- possibly members of the Gonzaga household, including Ludovico Gonzaga;
- university *scolares*;
- friars and preachers;
- students learning grammar, logic and foreign terminology;
- merchants, customs payers and creditors;
- envoys and speakers.

### Documented anecdotes or examples

- Peter, one’s friend or enemy, behaves ridiculously in a locus.
- Albert hangs himself or performs an ugly action.
- Ludovico ties a rosary to his nose or ears or bites it.
- Ambrose removes breeches from a lamb to encode *A-bra-am*.
- A merchant remembers that saffron weighed 137 pounds.
- A personally known “Brother Ottell” apparently becomes a mnemonic figure.
- Familiar women or saints supply recognizable faces.

### Requested anecdotes not belonging to this system

The auctioneer with prodigious memory, the professor’s party, the student who lost his memory, and the accused magician are anecdotes associated with other memory authors or later traditions in Yates’s wider history.

**[Absence]** None was traced to Lodovico, Matthaeus, Ragone, Braga, Marciana VI, 238, or the Vienna illustrated manuscript. Assigning them to this system would be erroneous.

## The magic question

## Was the system magical?

For the central technical texts, the best answer is: **not in the operative sense established by the surviving evidence.**

### Evidence against a magical classification

**[Text]**

- The mechanism is psychological and rhetorical: attention, association, order, similarity, contrast and sensory vividness.
- The images are deliberately constructed by the user.
- The houses and objects are mnemonic addresses.
- The results sought are sermons, arguments, names, numbers, merchandise, texts and devotional histories.
- Matthaeus invokes God, Mary and Dominic in a conventional scholastic prologue.
- The devotional author rejects *vana phantasmata* in favour of sacred histories.
- Ragone explains effectiveness through how unusual images strike the senses.
- No spirits, planetary intelligences, consecrations, talismans, magical seals, suffumigations or coercive ritual are documented in the passages examined.

### Medicine and regimen

The herbal memory ointments occupy the medical-regimen border. Their efficacy is doubtful by modern standards, but that does not make them magic in their own categories.

### Sacred images

Saints with attributes and a cosmic road to Heaven and Hell are devotional mnemonics. They can be theological without being talismanic.

### Ars notoria

The *Ars notoria* promises extraordinary knowledge through prayers, ritual observance and inspection of complex *notae*. It was sometimes criticized as illicit or demonic.

**[Distinction]** No textual genealogy has been demonstrated between the *notae* of the *Ars notoria* and Lodovico’s towers, Ragone’s household objects, or the Vienna five-locus rooms. Visual complexity alone is not proof of magical descent.

### Heresy, accusation and Inquisition

**[Absence]** No evidence was found that Lodovico, Matthaeus, Ragone, Braga, the *pater reverende* compilers, or the anonymous devotional author was prosecuted for these mnemonic systems.

**[Absence]** No contemporary source examined calls the systems heretical or talismanic.

**[Misleading association]** Later memory figures such as Giordano Bruno faced accusations entangled with theology, cosmology and magic. Bruno’s history cannot be projected backward onto these manuals.

### Consequences

The lack of recorded prosecution is itself significant. The art operated openly in university, mendicant and courtly environments. Its Christian, Aristotelian and rhetorical explanations supplied legitimate intellectual categories.

## Afterlife

## Printed memory manuals

The clearest descendants are Publicius, Pietro da Ravenna, Romberch and Rossellius. They preserve:

- ordered architecture;
- fifth- and tenth-place markers;
- known people as images;
- grotesque action;
- alphabetic images;
- rooms organized in fives;
- object-loci;
- applications to disciplines and texts.

## Preaching, devotional art and frescoes

Medieval sermons, virtue-and-vice cycles, saints’ attributes, Last Judgments and diagrammatic devotional books share mnemonic functions.

**[Caution]** Formal resemblance does not prove that a particular fresco was designed from a specific *ars memorativa*. Bolzoni and Carruthers support a common culture of image-based meditation and preaching, not a universal hidden memory-palace code.

## Dante

Dante’s ordered moral landscapes proved exceptionally compatible with later mnemonic organization. Romberch and subsequent writers could map cosmological or moral knowledge onto structured places.

**[Disputed]** It does not follow that the *Divine Comedy* was composed by using Lodovico’s object list or the Vienna rooms.

## Emblem books

The combination of concise text, personification, attribute and striking action resembles emblematic practice and supplied reusable habits of reading. The relationship is cultural and technical rather than a simple one-text descent.

## Lull, Leibniz and combinatorics

Llull’s combinatory figures predate these treatises. Later authors brought mnemonic loci and alphabetic codes into contact with Lullian wheels, universal-language projects and formal combination. Rossi’s “Llull to Leibniz” genealogy concerns this broader convergence.

**[Correction]** The nine-tower diagram is not established as a direct ancestor of Leibnizian computation.

## Literature

Borges’s “Funes the Memorious” explores unlimited retention without abstraction—the inverse problem of the medieval arts, which depend on selection, division and ordered retrieval.

Eco’s novels and essays play with libraries, encyclopedic structures, semiotics and impossible classification. These are literary afterlives, not continuations of a particular Lodovican regimen.

## Modern memory palaces and athletes

Contemporary competitive mnemonists use:

- routes or journeys;
- fixed stations;
- person-action-object encodings;
- exaggerated, violent, comic or sexual imagery;
- reusable number and card codes.

The family resemblance to Ragone is striking. Yet modern PAO systems, Major-system phonetics, spaced-repetition software and standardized competition disciplines are **modern constructions**, not recovered verbatim from the fifteenth-century texts.

## Computing

A memory palace resembles addressed storage only metaphorically:

- loci act as addresses;
- images act as mutable contents;
- traversal acts as retrieval;
- alphabetic and numeric matrices act as codes.

This is historically illuminating, but a medieval mental locus is not random-access machine memory. Claims that Lodovico “invented computing” are modern invention.

## Open questions

1. What is the correct current shelfmark and codicological history of Volkmann’s illustrated Vienna manuscript—5393, 5395, or an older concordant number?

2. Can the Vienna human figures be decoded image by image from their accompanying text rather than through Yates’s iconographic conjectures?

3. What exact information do the subsidiary figures and banderoles encode: propositions, words, syllables, virtues and vices, or mixed material?

4. What is the full stemma of Lodovico’s long and short recensions, and which passages can securely be assigned to the named author?

5. Is Marciana VI, 274 the earliest or merely the only attributive witness?

6. What is the exact relation between Pietro da Orvieto, Lodovico, Matthaeus, *Memoria fecunda*, Ragone and the anonymous *pater reverende* family: direct borrowing, a lost common source, or serial compilation?

7. Did Boncompagno’s object miscellanies directly generate the one-hundred-object matrices, or are they only an earlier analogy?

8. What does a full philological edition of Luca Braga reveal about his simultaneous use of Democritus, Simonides and Aquinas?

9. Does the Greek translation of the *Ad Herennium* memory section share any diagnostic readings with an Italian manuscript? Until such parallels are found, the Byzantine hypothesis remains unsupported.

10. Can Marciana VI, 238 be dated linguistically earlier than its surviving copy, and can its relationship to Romberch be stemmatically demonstrated?

11. Are the tower diagrams purely geometric multiplication devices, architectural projections, or combinations of both?

12. How did actual readers mark, rehearse or adapt these systems? Marginalia and ownership history may supply firmer evidence of use than prescriptive texts.

13. How widely did merchants employ the techniques? The saffron example proves an intended application, not a social statistic.

14. Were the herbal recipes originally part of the arts or later miscellany additions?

15. Can the lost or eighteenth-century-reported Ragone witnesses be identified in present collections?

The central finding is that Yates correctly recognized a rich manuscript substrate beneath the printed Renaissance arts, but her “Democritus type” is not sustainable as a discrete Greek or Byzantine school. The recoverable history is instead a densely interconnected northern-Italian and Central-European tradition in which classical rhetoric, Aristotelian psychology, scholastic classification, mendicant devotion, court culture, vernacular pedagogy and commercial practice continually recombined.
