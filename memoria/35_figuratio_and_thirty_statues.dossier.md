# The Figuratio and the Thirty Statues: Research Dossier

> **Name:** *Figuratio Aristotelici physici auditus* and *Lampas triginta statuarum*  
> **Exponent:** Giordano Bruno (1548–1600)  
> **Date:** *Figuratio*: Paris, 1586. *Lampas*: first redaction probably Wittenberg, spring 1587; revised redaction copied at Padua, 22 October 1591. “c. 1588” is serviceable only as a broad date for the project, not as the best date for either surviving witness.  
> **Source texts:** *Figuratio*, in *Opera latine conscripta*, I.4, pp. 129–221, especially pp. 129–40; *Lampas*, *Opera latine conscripta*, III, pp. 1–258; now critically edited with Italian translation by Nicoletta Tirinnanzi in Bruno, *Opere magiche* (2000/2003), canonical paragraphs [1]–[440], pp. 928–1484.  
> **Principle in one sentence:** Concrete mythological figures—first fifteen mnemonic images for the concepts of Aristotelian physics, later a mobile repertory of thirty or more conceptual “statues,” fields, trees, and personifications—make abstract terms memorable and simultaneously supply middle terms for philosophical invention.  
> **Yates:** *The Art of Memory*, ch. XIII, pp. 287–93 and plate 14c.

## Evidentiary key

The labels used below distinguish different kinds of claim:

- **[TEXT]** expressly stated or depicted in a primary source.
- **[WITNESS]** physical or catalogical evidence from a manuscript or early printing.
- **[RECONSTRUCTION]** a conclusion based on textual history or comparison.
- **[YATES]** Frances Yates’s interpretation, whether or not subsequently accepted.
- **[SCHOLARSHIP]** a modern scholarly interpretation supported by specified evidence.
- **[DISPUTED]** a reading for which serious alternatives remain.
- **[TRADITION]** a report inherited from an older source, not independently verified.
- **[LEGEND]** a story or attribution for which adequate contemporary evidence is lacking.
- **[MODERN INVENTION]** a later construction sometimes projected back onto Bruno.

## Basic identification

### The *Figuratio*

**[WITNESS]** The full title is:

> *Iordani Bruni Nolani Figuratio Aristotelici physici auditus ad eiusdem intelligentiam atque retentionem per quindecim imagines explicanda.*

The surviving 1586 octavo was printed at Paris:

> *Parisiis, ex Typographia Petri Chevillot, in vico S. Ioannis Lateranensis, sub Rosa rubra.*

Its collation is [8], 42, 14, [2] leaves, signatures ã⁸ A–E⁸ F² A–B⁸. It is dedicated to Piero Del Bene, abbot of Belleville and a person connected with the French court. A digitized copy from the Smithsonian Libraries is complete, including the diagrams.

**[TEXT]** The title promises an explanation of Aristotle’s *Physical Lectures* “through fifteen images” for both understanding and retention. It is therefore not merely an epitome of the *Physics*. It consists of:

1. a prefatory mnemonic apparatus;
2. fifteen emblematic or statue-like figures;
3. a schematic system of places;
4. a six-book digest of Aristotelian natural philosophy.

**[SCHOLARSHIP]** Marco Matteoli’s close comparison with Aristotle, Averroes, Philoponus, Aquinas, and the Latin Aristotelian tradition confirms that it is a carefully constructed teaching text, but one in which Bruno repeatedly alters definitions and emphases. It is neither an innocent transcription of Aristotle nor a simple encoded statement of Bruno’s mature cosmology.

### The *Lampas*

**[WITNESS]** The work normally called *Lampas triginta statuarum* was not printed during Bruno’s lifetime. Neither principal manuscript begins with an independent author-and-title page; the heading *Praefatio in lampadem triginta statuarum* supplies the conventional title.

**[RECONSTRUCTION]** Nicoletta Tirinnanzi dates the first redaction to Wittenberg, probably spring 1587. Bruno was then teaching as a private lecturer at the Lutheran university and working intensively on Aristotle, dialectic, and Lullism.

**[TEXT]** Bruno calls the project an:

> *ars inventiva per triginta statuas*  
> “an art of invention through thirty statues.”

**[TEXT]** At the end of the later manuscript is the subscription:

> *Anno 1591 / Mensi Octobre / Die lunae 22 / Paduae.*  
> “In the year 1591, in October, Monday the 22nd, at Padua.”

This dates Besler’s copy, not necessarily the conception of the system.

# The system in detail

## 1. The *Figuratio*: fifteen images for physics

### Purpose

**[TEXT]** The work’s declared purposes are *intelligentia* and *retentio*: understanding and retention. Bruno is “figuring” the principal subjects of natural philosophy so that a student can recover their ordered exposition.

**[TEXT]** The opening directs the reader to choose an appropriate procedure from the *Explicatio triginta sigillorum*, mentioning in particular painterly and sculptural modes. The relevant principle is the Seal of Zeuxis or that of Phidias: form a synthetic or statue-like internal image whose parts carry the distinctions to be remembered.

### Places

**[TEXT]** The places are not a conventional walk through a familiar house. The printed diagram reproduced by Yates as plate 14c is a circular, radial arrangement:

- a central circular region;
- surrounding bands or compartments;
- sectors divided by straight lines;
- alphabetic or verbal indications assigned to the compartments;
- small circles or nodes disposed around the circumference.

It looks “horoscope-like” because it shares the radial geometry of astrological figures, but the mere visual resemblance does not establish an astrological operation.

**[YATES]** Yates calls it “one of those horoscope-like diagrams such as are to be seen in *Seals*” and infers that the images are “magically animated” and “in contact with cosmic powers.”

**[CORRECTION]** The diagram is documentary evidence for ordered schematic loci. It is not documentary evidence that spirits, planets, or astral rays activate them. No invocation, election of an astrological hour, planetary suffumigation, or talismanic consecration accompanies it.

### Images

The fifteen figures organize concepts drawn principally from the opening books of Aristotle’s *Physics*. The best-known include:

- the *Arbor Olympica*, an Olympian tree functioning as a divisional and connective schema;
- Minerva;
- Thetis for matter;
- Apollo for form;
- a superior Pan for nature;
- Cupid for motion;
- Saturn for time;
- Jupiter for the first mover.

**[TEXT]** These are visually or verbally articulated figures, not merely arbitrary substitute symbols. Their bodies, attributes, positions, family relations, and associated myths carry divisions of the philosophical term.

**[SCHOLARSHIP]** Matteoli calls them kinds of *statuae*. Their mnemonic function depends on the same feature that will dominate the *Lampas*: one figure is divisible into meaningful members, actions, attributes, and relations.

### Fixing order

**[RECONSTRUCTION]** Order operates at several levels:

1. the numerical succession of the fifteen figures;
2. the fixed geometry of the printed place diagram;
3. the ordered members of each image;
4. the order of Aristotle’s subject matter;
5. trees and partitions supplying genus/species relations.

Retrieval proceeds by mentally surveying the loci, recognizing the assigned deity, and unpacking its attributes into the corresponding physical concepts.

There is no evidence that a user had to rotate a physical paper device. Nor does Bruno provide a first-person report of a student actually performing the complete sequence.

### The physics remembered—and rewritten

**[TEXT]** The body of the work is divided into six books, not Aristotle’s canonical eight, and compresses or rearranges the material. It uses Aristotle and the commentarial tradition but sometimes introduces positions incompatible with strict Aristotelianism.

**[YATES]** Yates asks why Bruno should want anyone to memorize “the dead and empty Aristotelian physics” and concludes:

> “The memory system by which the physics is to be ‘figured’ is in itself a contradiction of the physics. The book is a Seal.”

**[SCHOLARSHIP/CORRECTION]** Later philological study makes the relationship less binary. The book is simultaneously:

- a usable summary of Aristotle;
- an exercise in Bruno’s art of memory;
- a revision of key physical vocabulary;
- evidence that Bruno continued to engage seriously with Aristotelian texts even after the Italian dialogues.

Calling the whole work a concealed refutation captures part of its tension but flattens its pedagogical and exegetical work.

## 2. The *Lampas*: from memory to invention

### Declared task

**[TEXT]** Bruno distinguishes several ways of finding or producing knowledge: resolution, formation (like wax impressed by different moulds), combination, preparation, and “objectation,” likened to presenting changing objects to a mirror.

He announces a method that will avoid wandering by chance:

> *Propositum igitur est eiusmodi inveniendi viam sternere, qua ex proximioribus atque propriis … facillime omnium et propriissime procedamus.*  
> “Our purpose, therefore, is to lay out a way of discovery by which we may proceed most easily and appropriately from premises that are nearer and proper [to the matter].”  
> (*Opere magiche*, *Lampas* [2], pp. 932–33.)

The core description is:

> *Haec quidem constat triginta statuis, in quibus triginta intentiones continentur …*  
> “This [art] consists of thirty statues, in which thirty concepts or intentions are contained.”  
> (*Lampas* [4], pp. 938–39.)

And its epistemic direction is:

> *Ordo erit procedendi a notioribus nobis sensibilibus et phantasiabilibus ad intelligibilia et contemplabilia universalia…*  
> “The order of procedure will be from sensibles and objects of imagination better known to us, toward universal intelligibles and objects of contemplation.”  
> (*Lampas* [5], pp. 938–41.)

This is the most explicit rule of the system.

### What serves as places

**[TEXT]** The principal “places” are not architectural rooms. They include:

- *solia*, seats or thrones occupied by statues;
- *campi*, fields associated with deities or concepts;
- the articulated body of a statue;
- trees, roots, trunks, branches, and fruits;
- left, middle, and right;
- paired limbs;
- workshops, mountains, horns, arrows, knots, ladders, and other internal structures.

Bruno writes:

> *Mitto quod videbitur de perlustratione statuarum per solia, quae commutationem et vicissitudinem quandam suscipere videbuntur.*  
> “I leave aside what will become apparent from surveying the statues through their seats, which will appear capable of a certain interchange and alternation.”  
> (*Lampas* [4], pp. 938–39.)

**[SCHOLARSHIP]** Tirinnanzi therefore describes the system as a *discursiva architectura*: an interior, reconfigurable architecture of discourse. The statues are conceptual nodes, not immovable exhibits in a literal museum.

### What serves as images

The primary images are mythological persons or complexes. The central sequence, reconstructed from the headings, includes:

1. Apollo — unity or monad;
2. Saturn — principle;
3. Prometheus — agent and efficient cause;
4. Vulcan’s workshop — the production and articulation of form;
5. Vulcan — formal cause;
6. Thetis — material cause;
7. Sagittarius — final cause or intention;
8. Mount Olympus — end;
9. field of Coelius/Vesta — natural goodness and moral good;
10. field of Ocean — magnitude;
11. Mars — virtue or power in action;
12. field of Earth — potency;
13. field of Juno — the middle;
14. Demogorgon — relation, concurrence, or habitual connection;
15. horn of Achelous — possession or having;
16. field of Minerva — knowledge;
17. ladder of Minerva — habits and grades of cognition;
18. field of Venus — concord;
19. Venus — concord in will;
20. Cupid’s arrows or knots — concord in action;
21. Cupid — differences and attributes of will.

The later logical architecture adds trees and personifications such as Atlas and Enceladus. Thus “thirty statues” is the name of an organizing method, not a clean illustrated portfolio containing exactly thirty engraved classical statues.

### The six infigurables

Before the sensible statues Bruno treats two opposed triads of *infigurabilia*: principles that lack an adequate sensible figure.

**[TEXT/SCHOLARSHIP]** The superior triad belongs to light, actuality, and formative power; the inferior triad to darkness, privation, receptivity, and matter. Among the dark figures or approximations are Orcus, the abyss or immense gulf, and Night or prime matter.

A representative formulation is:

> *In infimo gradu est paupertas seu inopia, indignitas, defectus et potentia triplex: passiva, receptiva, subiectiva.*  
> “At the lowest degree are poverty or need, unworthiness, defect, and threefold potency: passive, receptive, and subjective.”

The paradox is deliberate: the system begins by explaining how the imagination can approach what cannot properly be pictured.

**[CORRECTION]** Yates’s phrasing “beginning with Orcus, the Abyss, the thirst for the infinite” catches the affective imagery but can suggest that Orcus is simply statue number one. Textually these opening *infigurabilia* form a metaphysical prologue or frame for the statues.

### Building and reading a statue

Each statue can be analyzed through:

- genealogy;
- sex, age, dress, and posture;
- right, center, and left;
- tools and weapons;
- actions suffered and performed;
- companions and enemies;
- dwelling place;
- myths;
- causes, effects, powers, habits, and privations;
- thirty subordinate articles, conditions, descriptions, or contemplations.

The statue of Saturn, for example, unfolds “principle” through origin, generation, succession, temporal priority, and consumption. Bruno invokes the familiar ancient etymological allegory in which Saturn eats his children because time consumes its products.

Prometheus is the privileged figure of efficient causality. He models natural and artificial production and, reflexively, the memory artist’s formation of living inner images.

For Vulcan, Bruno says that one may contemplate in order his:

> *genus, indolem, fortunam, connubia, officium, inventionem, artem, locum, opera, affectionem … instrumenta, nutrimentum, elementum…*  
> “birth, disposition, fortune, marriages, office, inventions, art, place, works, affections … instruments, nourishment, element…”

From these, different definitions of form can be produced. The forge is both a mnemonic scene and a machine for generating distinctions.

### Retrieval and invention

This is not merely storage-and-recall.

**[TEXT]** Bruno claims no proposed question can escape at least one of the thirty ideas, and that each idea supplies a “number of middle terms” through its parts and circumstances.

**[TEXT]** On the utility of the Promethean or causal statue:

> *Ex hac statua non solum licet elicere modos et rationes quibus principium dicitur, verum etiam ordinem inquirendi de quacunque re…*  
> “From this statue one may derive not only the modes and senses in which ‘principle’ is said, but also an order for investigating any thing…”  
> (*Lampas* [118]–[119]; Italian edition pp. 1088–91.)

One asks of a thing:

- whence it originates;
- by what seed;
- under what authority;
- in what order;
- with what efficacy;
- through what instrument;
- in what genus.

Retrieval is therefore topical and inferential. A statue provides interrogative routes and middle terms for constructing an argument.

### A worked application

**[TEXT]** The manuscript concludes by applying the architecture to the proposition *anima non est accidens*, “the soul is not an accident.” Bruno runs the question through appropriate fields and distinctions, including the object’s eternity, the insufficiency of sense, concord of soul with divine things, and the soul’s principle of internal motion.

The conclusion states that the example shows:

> how it is possible to dispute about any subject by proceeding through a discursive chain according to the principles established.

This is the closest thing in the *Lampas* to a complete worked retrieval/invention exercise.

### Are the statues on wheels?

**[YATES]** Yates writes:

> “The Thirty Statues are, I believe, supposed to be revolved on Lullian combinatory wheels.”

She reasons from:

- Bruno’s Wittenberg Lullian works;
- the recurrent number thirty;
- the movable image wheels of *De umbris idearum*;
- Lullian *principia* and *relata* in the *Lampas*;
- the statement that the statues’ seats can change.

**[DISPUTED/CORRECTED]** No surviving *Lampas* witness supplies a wheel, instructions for concentric rotation, lettered rings, or a permutation table. Bruno says the seats can undergo *commutatio* and *vicissitudo*, but that need not mean literal rotation.

Modern work by Tirinnanzi, Michele Ciliberto, Michele Cambi, Leen Spruit, and Manuel Mertens generally preserves the combinatorial connection while treating the *Lampas* as an imaginative logic or mobile conceptual architecture. Yates’s wheel is a plausible extrapolation, not a documented component.

## 3. Rules for places and images

The two texts do not repeat the textbook lists of rules familiar from the *Ad Herennium*. Their operative rules can nevertheless be extracted.

### Places

**[TEXT/RECONSTRUCTION]**

- Places must impose order on complex material.
- A place can be geometric, bodily, mythic, verbal, or classificatory.
- Its internal parts must be distinguishable.
- The same conceptual object can be inspected under different loci.
- In the *Lampas*, order is deliberately mutable when invention requires a different chain.
- A place need not be a real building.

### Images

**[TEXT/RECONSTRUCTION]**

- Begin from what can be sensed or imagined.
- Choose an image whose received myths contain many usable relations.
- Treat every attribute as semantically significant.
- Use striking, active figures rather than pale abstractions.
- Divide the image into members and circumstances.
- Preserve enough generality that the image can apply to many special cases.
- Move from the sensible figure to universal intelligibles.
- Combine images according to the user’s *ingenium* and the problem at hand.

### Classical example requested in the brief

The celebrated lawsuit image belongs to *Rhetorica ad Herennium* III.20.33, not to either Brunian text:

> *Reum ad lectum eius aegri constituemus, dextra poculum, sinistra tabulas, medico testiculos arietinos tenentem.*  
> “We shall place the defendant at the sick man’s bedside, holding a cup in his right hand, tablets in his left, and ram’s testicles with his little finger.”

The cup recalls poison, the tablets the will, and the ram’s testicles the witnesses (*testes*).

**[CONTEXT]** Bruno inherits the principle of the composite *imago agens*, but his deities do more than cue the elements of one narrative. They function as reusable semantic matrices.

Camillo’s seven grades and Bruno’s earlier concentric wheels likewise belong to predecessor or parallel systems. They are not worked mechanisms reproduced in the *Figuratio* or *Lampas*.

# The sources

## Primary texts and editions

### *Figuratio Aristotelici physici auditus*

1. **Paris, 1586**, Pierre Chevillot, *sub Rosa rubra*. This is the lifetime printing and chief primary witness.
2. **Felice Tocco and Girolamo Vitelli**, eds., *Iordani Bruni Nolani Opera latine conscripta*, vol. I, part IV, Florence/Neapolitan state edition, 1890–91, pp. 129 ff.
3. **Marco Matteoli, Rita Sturlese, and Nicoletta Tirinnanzi**, in *Opere mnemotecniche*, directed by Michele Ciliberto, Milan: Adelphi, 2004. This is the modern critical framework for Bruno’s mnemonic works.

The 1586 images and diagram survive in printed impressions, not merely as later reconstructions.

### *Lampas triginta statuarum*

Two principal witnesses survive.

- **A — Augsburg witness.** First redaction, written by an unidentified German pupil during the Wittenberg period. It traveled with a printed *De lampade combinatoria lulliana* and manuscript *Animadversiones circa lampadem Lullianam*. It probably belonged to Johann Heinrich Heinzel/Hainzell, later dedicatee of *De imaginum compositione*.
- **M — Moscow/Norov witness.** Russian State Library, MS Norov 36. The volume contains some autograph Bruno material but the *Lampas*, ff. 99r–160r, is in Hieronymus Besler’s hand. It represents the later redaction and ends with the Padua subscription of 22 October 1591.

Older scholarship sometimes confusingly called the Augsburg collection “Augustan” and placed it at Erlangen. Erlangen manuscripts 1215 and 1279 are especially relevant to the *Libri physicorum Aristotelis explanati*, not a license to identify every “Augustan” witness with Erlangen. Modern catalogical work places the early *Lampas* witness at Augsburg.

Editions:

1. Tocco and Vitelli, *Opera latine conscripta*, III (1891), pp. 1–258. First publication.
2. Michele Ciliberto, dir.; Nicoletta Tirinnanzi, ed., trans., and commentary, *Opere magiche*, Milan: Adelphi, 2000; revised printing 2003, pp. 928–1503. This collates A, M, and the older editor’s transcript N and records extensive variants.
3. Claudio D’Antonio, ed., *Il quarto libro della Clavis Magna, ovvero l’arte di inventare con trenta statue*, Rome: Di Renzo, 2002. Useful but its “fourth book of the *Clavis magna*” framing should not be mistaken for a surviving Brunian title page.

### Important manuscript variation

**[WITNESS]**

- A is the earlier redaction.
- M is later and entirely copied by Besler in the *Lampas* section.
- M rearranges, omits, and reformulates passages rather than merely correcting spelling.
- The apparatus records substantive alternatives: *statua/idolum*, different numbering of the modes of investigation, omissions of Lullian or Archytan references, and different formulations concerning matter and form.
- The title and some divisions are editorially stabilized.
- The work’s apparent incompleteness is structural: it contains a conclusion and worked application, but its promised symmetry and exhaustive thirtyfold development are not carried through consistently.

**[RECONSTRUCTION]** It is therefore better to speak of an unfinished or imperfectly systematized treatise preserved in two authorially connected redactions—not an accidentally truncated manuscript.

# Yates’s account and scholarship since

## What Yates argued

In chapter XIII, pp. 287–93, Yates joins the two works into a single developmental narrative:

1. the *Figuratio* applies the classical art to Aristotle;
2. its divine figures covertly carry Bruno’s anti-Aristotelian philosophy;
3. its place figure is a magical, horoscope-like Seal;
4. the *Lampas* performs the “Seal of Phidias,” sculpting colossal inner statues;
5. the statues are inward equivalents of the magically animated statues in the *Asclepius*;
6. they are meant to revolve on Lullian wheels;
7. consequently they do not merely illustrate Bruno’s philosophy:

> “They are his philosophy.”

Yates’s reconstruction drew additional support from her 1964 *Giordano Bruno and the Hermetic Tradition*, where Bruno’s art of memory forms part of a Hermetic religious and magical program.

## What has been confirmed

**[CONFIRMED]**

- The *Figuratio* is a genuine mnemonic application, not decorative mythology.
- The Seal of Phidias is an essential antecedent for the statue method.
- The same mythological figures recur across Bruno’s mnemonic, philosophical, and literary works.
- The *Lampas* combines artificial memory, dialectical invention, Aristotelian terminology, and Renaissance Lullism.
- The figures carry Bruno’s philosophical commitments and are not detachable mnemonic labels.
- The passage from sensible image to intelligible structure is central.
- Prometheus is a reflexive emblem of the artist who forms living images.
- Bruno’s memory art belongs to his philosophy of imagination and cannot be reduced to rote tricks.

## What has been corrected or qualified

### Hermeticism

**[CORRECTION]** Paolo Rossi, Hilary Gatti, Michele Ciliberto, Rita Sturlese, Stephen Clucas, and later Bruno scholars have generally rejected an exclusively Hermetic key. Bruno’s sources also include:

- Aristotle and Greek/Arabic/Latin commentators;
- scholastic logic and psychology;
- Lucretius and atomism;
- Nicholas of Cusa;
- Ficino and Neoplatonism;
- Renaissance mythography;
- Lullism;
- contemporary pedagogical and disputational practices.

The Hermetic materials are real; their historiographical monopoly is not.

### Talismanic activation

**[DISPUTED]** Yates moves quickly from “astrological-looking” geometry and divine images to talismanic animation. Clucas and Sturlese insist on distinguishing:

- an image used to attract or bind an external power;
- an image used to organize imagination and cognition;
- natural magic grounded in occult qualities;
- explicit ritual or demonic magic.

The *Lampas* discusses magic among modes of acquiring knowledge and even describes sudden knowledge through an alien *bonus vel malus daemon* as akin to *ars notoria*. But it chooses another method: objectation, imagination, division, and discourse. The text does not tell the user to ensoul the statues ritually.

### Lullian wheels

**[CORRECTION]** Yates’s “I believe” must be retained. The work is combinatorial in a broad sense and responds to Bruno’s Lullian writings, but no wheels survive in either witness.

### Aristotle

**[CORRECTION]** Matteoli and Tirinnanzi show that Bruno’s dialogue with Aristotle is much more sustained than Yates’s “dead physics versus living magical universe” opposition allows. The *Lampas* resembles a transformed philosophical lexicon and an image-based counterpart to Aristotle’s *Metaphysics* V.

### Static memory palace

**[CORRECTION]** The system is not a gallery through which one always walks in a fixed order. The seats may be exchanged, and the route changes with the question. It is closer to a reconfigurable topical index than a single memorized building.

## Relation to the requested broader scholarship

- **Mary Carruthers** and **Janet Coleman/Small** illuminate medieval memory as composition, recollection, and craft rather than mere storage. This supports reading Bruno’s statues as generative.
- **Lina Bolzoni** shows how Renaissance galleries, theaters, emblems, preaching, and literary images made knowledge spatial and visible. Her work supports the cultural plausibility of Bruno’s “museum,” while discouraging a purely occult reading.
- **Paolo Rossi** emphasizes the transition from mnemonic images and Lullian combination toward arts of method and universal language.
- **Walter J. Ong** situates Ramus’s spatial dichotomies and visual method as a competing simplification of dialectic. Bruno’s richly affective images are nearly the opposite of Ramist austerity.
- **Anthony Bonner** and **Alexander Fidora** distinguish historical Llull’s demonstrative art from Renaissance pseudo-Lullian encyclopedism and occult accretions. Bruno’s Lullism is a Renaissance transformation, not straightforward transmission.
- **Ciliberto, Sturlese, Clucas, and Gatti** restore philosophical, philological, and institutional specificity to Bruno.
- **Tirinnanzi** provides the decisive modern account of the *Lampas* redactions, its Aristotle, sources, structure, and mobile discursive architecture.
- **Matteoli** provides the most detailed recent reconstruction of the *Figuratio* as a mnemonic rewriting of physics.

Camillo specialists Bolzoni and Stagno are relevant chiefly by comparison: Camillo’s fixed theater grades and cosmic proportions help explain the Renaissance aspiration to make knowledge visible, but no documentary chain proves that Bruno directly copied the *Lampas* from a particular Camillo diagram.

# History and context

## Classical rhetoric

The remote technical ancestry is the Greek and Roman artificial memory described by the anonymous *Rhetorica ad Herennium*, Cicero, and Quintilian: orderly places plus vivid images.

Bruno keeps the vivid, composite image but loosens the classical requirement of a stable architectural route. His figures become instruments of conceptual analysis.

## Scholastic psychology

The maxim underlying the procedure is Aristotelian-Thomist:

> *nihil est in intellectu quod non prius fuerit in sensu*  
> “Nothing is in the intellect that was not previously in sense.”

Bruno’s own formulation—proceed from *sensibilia et phantasiabilia* to *intelligibilia*—is documentary. Yet his imagination is more productive than the passive storehouse sometimes attributed to scholastic psychology.

Dominican memory teaching is relevant biographically and institutionally: Bruno was trained in the Dominican convent of San Domenico Maggiore in Naples. But no evidence proves that his fifteen-image system was an official Dominican curriculum.

## Romberch’s precursor anecdote

Johann Host von Romberch’s *Congestorium artificiosae memoriae* (Venice, 1520), ff. 7v–8r, reports:

> *Adolescens quidam huius artis fere rudis … in parietibus aliquot figuras parum ad rem facientes depinxit, quibus Aristotelis De auditu physico comprehenderet…*

A young man, scarcely trained in memory, drew apparently irrelevant figures on walls and used them to master Aristotle’s *Physics*.

**[TRADITION]** This is evidence that image-based memorization of the *Physics* circulated before Bruno. It does not establish that Bruno knew the student, saw the walls, or copied the precise scheme.

## Lullism

Historical Llull’s art used:

- divine attributes and relational principles;
- letters serving as abbreviated concepts;
- figures and tables;
- systematic combinations;
- demonstrative and missionary purposes.

Bruno’s Wittenberg works—*De lampade combinatoria lulliana*, *Animadversiones*, and *De progressu et lampade venatoria logicorum*—adapt these techniques to dialectic and invention.

The “thirty” structures derive not directly from Llull’s normal nine-letter alphabet but from Bruno’s own repeated architecture of thirty shadows, seals, bonds, and statues.

## Renaissance mythography

The statue descriptions draw on Ovid, Virgil, Hesiod, Cicero, Boccaccio, Vincenzo Cartari, and especially Natale Conti’s *Mythologiae*. These handbooks made a god’s genealogies, attributes, physical descriptions, and allegorical meanings available as a compact semantic archive.

## Institutions and patrons

- **Paris and the royal lectorate:** environment of the *Figuratio*, Piero Del Bene, and Bruno’s confrontation with university Aristotelians.
- **Collège de Cambrai:** the 1586 anti-Peripatetic theses, delivered or defended through Jean Hennequin, caused public opposition.
- **Wittenberg University:** setting for the first *Lampas*, Lullian lamps, logical instruction, and lectures on Aristotle.
- **Prague and Rudolf II’s court:** Bruno advertised a coming *Lampas Cabalistica* in connection with *De specierum scrutinio*.
- **Helmstedt, Zurich, Frankfurt:** further teaching, copying, and revision.
- **Venice and Padua:** Besler’s final copy and Mocenigo’s disastrous attempt to purchase Bruno’s secrets of memory and invention.

# People

## Giordano Bruno

The deviser, lecturer, and reviser. His claim was not simply to remember more material but to furnish an instrument for generating ordered discourse.

## Piero Del Bene

Dedicatee of the *Figuratio* and of Bruno’s Mordente dialogues. His patronage places the work within courtly and learned Parisian networks.

## Jean Hennequin

A disciple associated with the public presentation of Bruno’s anti-Aristotelian theses at Cambrai. The *Figuratio* and the *Acrotismus* should be read against this teaching and disputational episode.

## Hieronymus Besler

Student, secretary, and copyist. His clean Padua transcript preserves the later *Lampas* redaction. It is not an autograph, although the containing Norov codex includes some autograph Bruno leaves.

## Johann Heinrich Heinzel/Hainzell

Augsburg patrician, occult and alchemical patron, probably associated with the early manuscript and later dedicatee of *De imaginum, signorum et idearum compositione*.

## Giovanni Francesco Mocenigo

The Venetian nobleman who invited Bruno to teach him memory and invention. After becoming dissatisfied, he confined Bruno in his house and denounced him to the Venetian Inquisition in May 1592.

**[DOCUMENTED ANECDOTE]** Bruno testified that Mocenigo had invited him after contacts made through the Frankfurt book trade and had promised payment for instruction in memory and invention.

**[DISPUTED MOTIVE]** Popular accounts say Mocenigo wanted “magic and nothing else.” The denunciations do associate Bruno with magic, but the surviving documents also refer specifically to memory, invention, geometry, and books. His exact expectations cannot be reconstructed confidently.

## The requested catalogue anecdotes

- **The auctioneer:** no securely documented connection to these two systems was located.
- **The professor’s party:** no securely documented connection.
- **The student who lost his memory:** no securely documented *Figuratio/Lampas* case.
- **The accused magician:** Bruno himself is the documented case, but the memory art was the occasion for his return and betrayal, not demonstrably the doctrinal ground of his death sentence.

Absence here matters: anecdotes from other memory treatises should not be silently transferred to Bruno.

# The magic question

## What the texts say

**[TEXT]** The *Lampas* expressly mentions *ars notoria*. It describes a mode in which nature or an intelligence works through a human subject, making someone suddenly learned through another intelligence, *bonus vel malus daemon*. Bruno compares this with the notorious art and then identifies the eighth method—objectation or presentation to a mirror—as the method he will develop.

Thus the work knows and classifies occult acquisition of knowledge but does not prescribe it as the statue method.

## Statue magic and the *Asclepius*

The Hermetic *Asclepius* describes Egyptian priests making statues capable of receiving souls or divine powers.

**[YATES]** Bruno internalizes this: the memory artist makes animated divine statues within the imagination.

**[DISPUTED]** The analogy is philosophically suggestive but not an instruction found in the *Lampas*. Its statues are “living” principally because myths, passions, and relations make them cognitively active. Whether that should be called magical depends on the definition:

- under a broad Renaissance definition of imagination acting through natural sympathies: plausibly magical;
- under a strict definition requiring ritual operations or spirit invocation: unproved;
- as literal talismans installed in the brain: **[MODERN INVENTION]**.

## Heresy and prosecution

**[DOCUMENTED]** Mocenigo accused Bruno of religious errors and magical pretensions after the failed memory lessons. Bruno was arrested in Venice in 1592, transferred to Rome in 1593, condemned as an impenitent heretic, and executed in 1600.

**[DOCUMENTED LIMIT]** The complete Roman trial record and final detailed sentence do not survive. The surviving summary and Venetian depositions emphasize major theological and philosophical errors: Trinity, Incarnation, Christ, transubstantiation, plurality of worlds, eternity or structure of the cosmos, transmigration, and related propositions.

**[CORRECTION]** It cannot be documented that Bruno was burned “for the art of memory,” for heliocentrism alone, or specifically for the Thirty Statues.

**[SCHOLARSHIP]** Gatti notes that magic/Hermeticism forms only one part of the reconstructed charges. Firpo and later editors show that the final cause was cumulative obstinate heresy. The invitation to teach memory caused the circumstances of arrest; it is not equivalent to the formal ground of condemnation.

# Afterlife

## Immediate and early-modern reception

Because the *Lampas* remained in manuscript until 1891, its direct early-modern influence was necessarily limited. The related printed works had wider reach:

- *De umbris idearum*;
- *Ars memoriae*;
- *Explicatio triginta sigillorum*;
- *De lampade combinatoria lulliana*;
- *De specierum scrutinio*;
- *De imaginum compositione*.

Bruno’s students and editors—including Besler, Raphael Egli, and Johann Heinrich Alsted—helped transmit adjacent logical, rhetorical, and mnemonic materials.

## Ramism and universal method

Ramist diagrams offered visibly ordered knowledge with fewer mythic images. Bruno and Ramus thus represent competing early-modern answers to information overload:

- Ramus: dichotomous order, textual economy, curricular clarity;
- Bruno: rich imagery, multiple relations, mobile combination, and affective force.

## Literature and visual culture

**[SCHOLARLY COMPARISON, NOT DIRECT DESCENT]**

- Dante’s moralized landscapes and personifications belong to the longer culture of memorable images, but the *Lampas* does not descend directly from a documented Dantean schema.
- Renaissance fresco cycles, triumphs, emblem books, and mythographic galleries share the practice of making abstract order visible.
- Bacon’s mythological interpretation in *De sapientia veterum* may be compared with Bruno’s philosophical fables. Rossi and Yates suggested anticipation, but no direct borrowing from the unpublished *Lampas* can be established.
- Borges’s Funes is a literary meditation on indiscriminate total recall—the opposite problem from Bruno’s ordered, selective invention.
- Eco’s work on encyclopedias, semiotics, and perfect languages gives Llull and combinatorics an important genealogy, but Eco did not revive the *Lampas* as a practical apparatus.

## From Llull to Leibniz to computing

**[DOCUMENTED GENEALOGY WITH QUALIFICATIONS]**

Llull’s formal combinations influenced early-modern discussions of universal method; Renaissance Lullists including Bruno transformed them; Leibniz explicitly studied Lullian combinatorial art while seeking a *characteristica universalis* and *calculus ratiocinator*.

**[DISPUTED]** “Llull invented the computer” or “Bruno’s wheel became the computer” is anachronistic. The legitimate continuity is conceptual:

- finite symbolic elements;
- rule-governed combinations;
- exhaustive or semi-exhaustive search;
- mechanical aids to reasoning.

Modern databases, knowledge graphs, and faceted search resemble the *Lampas* functionally: a query moves through reusable categories and relations. That resemblance is not documentary descent.

## Modern memory palaces and athletes

Contemporary memory athletes commonly use loci and vivid images. Their practice descends broadly from classical artificial memory through early-modern and modern mnemonic manuals.

There is no evidence that leading competitive systems use Bruno’s exact fifteen figures or thirty statues. Claims that Bruno possessed a lost photographic-memory secret are **[LEGEND/MODERN INVENTION]**.

# Controversies and disputes

## 1. Dating the *Lampas*

- **Older broad date:** c. 1588 or 1586–88.
- **Tirinnanzi:** first redaction probably spring 1587 at Wittenberg.
- **M witness:** copied/revised in 1591 at Padua.
- **Verdict:** distinguish composition, revision, and surviving copy.

## 2. Is it the promised *Lampas Cabalistica*?

Bruno’s 1588 *De specierum scrutinio* announces a forthcoming work under that title.

- **[RECONSTRUCTION]** Tocco and later scholars identify it with the *Lampas triginta statuarum*.
- **Evidence:** date, “lamp” vocabulary, Lullian context, thirtyfold inventive art.
- **Problem:** no surviving manuscript bears *Lampas Cabalistica* as its independent title.
- **Verdict:** probable, not certain.

## 3. Exactly thirty statues?

The work has thirty governing intentions, but its named apparatus includes fields, workshop, mountain, ladder, horn, arrows, trees, and later logical giants.

- **Verdict:** “thirty statues” is a programmatic numerical architecture, not an exact inventory of thirty freestanding visual sculptures.

## 4. Is the work incomplete?

- Yates calls it incomplete.
- It has an explicit ending and dated subscription.
- Its architecture is uneven and some promised developments are not symmetrically realized.
- **Verdict:** finished enough to circulate and be copied, but unfinished as the perfectly exhaustive system it projects.

## 5. Is the system mnemonic, logical, or metaphysical?

All three, but in different proportions:

- **mnemonic:** sensible images stabilize complex terms;
- **logical:** each figure supplies distinctions and middle terms;
- **metaphysical:** the order of figures embodies Bruno’s accounts of unity, matter, form, relation, cognition, and soul.

Treating it as only a memory trick or only a coded metaphysics misses explicit parts of the text.

## 6. Yates’s talismanic interpretation

- **Evidence for:** divine images, astral-looking schemes elsewhere, Bruno’s magic, *Asclepius* interests, affective animation.
- **Evidence against:** no consecration ritual, no planetary timing, no surviving talismanic directions, explicit logical and pedagogical explanations.
- **Verdict:** powerful interpretive analogy, not a demonstrated operating rule.

## 7. Yates’s revolving wheels

- **Evidence for:** Bruno’s Lullism and other literal wheels; *commutatio* and *vicissitudo* of seats.
- **Against:** absence of wheels or rotation instructions in A and M.
- **Verdict:** disputed reconstruction. It must never be reported as a primary-source fact.

## 8. Natale Conti as source

Tirinnanzi’s source notes demonstrate repeated parallels with Natale Conti, Cartari, classical poets, Ficino, Agrippa, and Aristotelian commentators.

- **Verdict:** Conti is an important but not exclusive source. Claims that Bruno copied the statues wholesale from one mythographer are unsupported.

## 9. “They are his philosophy”

Yates’s sentence remains illuminating if understood to mean that image and thought are inseparable in the method.

It is misleading if taken to mean that every accessory of every myth is Bruno’s doctrinal assertion. Some attributes are mnemonic materials inherited from mythography and only provisionally mapped onto concepts.

## 10. Bruno as martyr for science or magician

Both simple portraits are legendary reductions.

- He defended an infinite universe and plurality of worlds.
- He also wrote seriously on magic, images, bonds, and occult qualities.
- He was prosecuted as a theologico-philosophical heretic.
- The surviving evidence does not reduce his condemnation to either Copernican astronomy or memory magic.

# Open questions

1. Can the Augsburg witness’s full modern shelfmark and codicological history be established from a newly digitized institutional catalogue rather than inherited descriptions?
2. How much of the difference between A and M represents Bruno’s deliberate revision, and how much Besler’s normalization?
3. Was *Lampas Cabalistica* certainly the *Thirty Statues*, or a related lost project?
4. Did Bruno intend an external manipulable aid, now lost, to accompany the mobile seats?
5. How exactly were the thirty governing intentions correlated with the numerous fields, statues, trees, and logical personifications?
6. Were the *Figuratio* images actually used by Bruno’s Paris students? No student notebook documenting performance has yet been identified.
7. How should “magic” be delimited when the imagination is both a natural cognitive faculty and, elsewhere in Bruno, a medium of occult bonds?
8. Can a full stemmatic and digital comparison of the *Figuratio*, the two *Lampas* redactions, and the *Libri physicorum* reveal the direction of borrowing among them?
9. Did Bacon or later encyclopedists know the *Figuratio* directly? Resemblance is known; a documentary chain is not.
10. How much of the *Lampas*’s conceptual inventory comes from Aristotle’s *Metaphysics* V, and how much from late-medieval terminist and university teaching traditions?
