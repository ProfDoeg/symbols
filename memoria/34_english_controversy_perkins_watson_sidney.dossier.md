# The English controversy: Perkins, Watson, Sidney: Research Dossier

## Basic identification

**Name:** The English controversy over artificial memory: Perkins, Watson, Sidney  
**Catalogue number:** 34 of 45  
**Principal exponents:** “G. P. Cantabrigiensis,” conventionally identified as William Perkins; Thomas Watson; Sir Philip Sidney  
**Dates:** 1583–1585 for the principal documents; Perkins returned to the issue in 1592  
**Source texts:**

- “G. P. Cantabrigiensis,” *Antidicsonus cuiusdam Cantabrigiensis G. P. Accessit Libellus, in quo dilucidè explicatur impia Dicsoni artificiosa memoria* (London: Henry Middleton for John Harrison, 1584), STC 19064.
- “G. P. Cantabrigiensis,” *Libellus de memoria, verissimaque bene recordandi scientia. Huc accessit eiusdem admonitiuncula ad A. Disconum, de artificiosae memoriae, quam publicè profitetur, vanitate* (London: Robert Waldegrave, 1584).
- Alexander Dicsone, *De umbra rationis & iudicii, sive de memoriae virtute prosopopoeia* (London: Thomas Vautrollier, title dated 1583; dedication dated 1 January, hence probably issued early in 1584).
- “Heius Scepsius” [Alexander Dicsone], *Defensio pro Alexandro Dicsono Arelio adversus quendam G. P. Cantabrigiensem* (London: Thomas Vautrollier for Francis Coldock, 1584), STC 21809.
- Thomas Watson, *Artificiosae memoriae libellus*, British Library, Sloane MS 3731, fols. 3–43, dedication dated 6 June 1583.
- Thomas Watson, *Compendium memoriae localis* ([London: probably Thomas Vautrollier, alternatively John Wolfe], [c.1585]), STC 25118.10.
- Sir Philip Sidney, *An Apologie for Poetrie* and *The Defence of Poesie* (both London, 1595), composed probably c.1580–83.
- William Perkins, *Prophetica, sive de sacra et unica ratione concionandi tractatus* (Cambridge, 1592), sig. F8r; English translation, *The Arte of Prophecying* (1607).

**Principle in one sentence:** The controversy opposed a Brunian–Dicsonean art of ordered places and affectively “animated” images to a Ramist memory grounded in logical division and disposition, while Watson defended a practical, non-cosmological classical local memory and Sidney treated metrical verse itself as an ordered “room” in which each word has a fixed seat.

**Yates:** Frances A. Yates, *The Art of Memory* (London: Routledge & Kegan Paul; Chicago: University of Chicago Press, 1966), chapter XII, especially pp. 267 and 273–284; retrospective references at chapter XIV, p. 318, and chapter XV, p. 338.

### Evidentiary key

- **[Text]** Directly documented in a primary source.
- **[Bibliographical record]** Established by a manuscript or rare-book catalogue rather than by the argument of a text.
- **[Scholarly reconstruction]** A reasoned inference by a modern editor or historian.
- **[Yates]** Yates’s interpretation, not automatically the primary text’s claim.
- **[Disputed]** Supported and challenged in scholarship.
- **[Tradition]** Repeated early testimony whose literal accuracy cannot be independently established.
- **[Legend]** An anecdote or causal claim without adequate contemporary documentation.
- **[Modern invention]** A retrospective label or relationship not present in the historical witnesses.
- **[Absence]** Something looked for but not found in the surviving evidence.

## The system in detail

There is not, strictly speaking, one “Perkins–Watson–Sidney system.” The catalogue heading compresses three different positions within one unusually concentrated English debate:

1. Dicsone’s abbreviated version of Bruno’s imagistic and cosmological memory;
2. the anti-local, Ramist “true memory” advocated by G. P. and later explicitly by Perkins;
3. Watson’s deliberately practical reconstruction of classical local memory;
4. Sidney’s analogy between the ordered places of a memory room and the fixed positions of words in verse.

The common subject is therefore not a single machine but a dispute about what kind of order properly makes knowledge memorable.

### 1. The Ramist alternative of G. P.

#### Two arts of memory

**[Text through Yates’s transcription and later translation]** In the dedicatory epistle to Thomas Moufet preceding the *Antidicsonus*, G. P. distinguishes two arts:

- one that employs places and *umbrae*, “shadows” or imagistic substitutes;
- another that proceeds by logical disposition as taught by Petrus Ramus.

Yates translates the conclusion:

> “There are two kinds of arts of memory, one using places and ‘umbra’, the other by logical disposition as taught by Ramus. The former is utterly vain; the latter is the only true method.”

The opposition is not simply between memory and logic. Ramist method is itself called the genuine art of memory. What G. P. rejects is an independent mnemonic apparatus imposed upon subject matter from outside.

#### What serves as places?

**[Text]** In the *Admonitiuncula*, summarized and partly translated by Yates, the Ramist method is said to employ *true places*. These are not rooms, walls, zodiacal signs, or fictional architectures. They are the intrinsic positions occupied by concepts in a correctly divided discipline:

- *generalia* or generals in the highest place;
- *subalterna* or intermediate species in middle places;
- *specialia* or lowest species at the bottom.

The spatial language survives, but it has become taxonomic. A “place” is a node in an ordered analysis of the subject.

#### What serves as images?

**[Text]** The Ramist equivalent of images consists in clear, distinct divisions of the subject. G. P. contrasts these with Dicsone’s fugitive *umbrae*. A good diagram or division does not resemble the thing sensuously; it displays its logical relations.

Thus, in reconstructed form:

```text
Highest/general term
├── first subordinate member
│   ├── first special
│   └── second special
└── second subordinate member
    ├── first special
    └── second special
```

No such modern tree is printed here as a facsimile of the 1584 pamphlet. It represents the Ramist logic described in the text. Ramist editions commonly rendered dichotomies typographically with braces, but one should not infer a particular surviving chart in the *Antidicsonus* without inspecting the copy.

#### How order is fixed

**[Text]** Order is “natural” insofar as it follows the relations believed to be inherent in the subject: first the most general definition, then division into subordinate kinds, then division down to particulars. Memory follows understanding. To retrieve an item, the learner mentally traverses the analytical structure.

This corresponds to the Ramist rules of *methodus*: proceed from what is universal and more evident to what is subordinate and particular; distribute each discipline without importing material belonging to another art.

#### Why astronomy is excluded

**[Text/Yates]** G. P. attacks the celestial or zodiacal structure associated with Metrodorus of Scepsis, Bruno, and Dicsone. The objection is methodological as well as polemical: astronomy is a particular art, whereas memory is a general instrument applicable to every art. Therefore astronomical figures cannot properly constitute the universal art of remembering.

**[Correction to an over-theological reading]** Joshua Reid’s later study of Perkins and imagination stresses that in the anonymous pamphlets this objection is not developed chiefly as a biblical attack on astrology. It is framed through Ramist rules against mixing disciplines. Yates recognized the technical argument, but her larger narrative made the religious implications more determinative than the pamphlets themselves consistently do.

#### The triple burden

**[Text, confirmed by the signed *Prophetica*]** A local memory burdens the practitioner with three acts:

1. remembering the places;
2. remembering the images;
3. remembering what the images signify.

The Ramist alternative claims to remember the matter directly through its organization.

#### Moral objection to animated images

In *Prophetica*, Perkins writes, in the received English translation:

> “The animation of the images which is the key of memory is impious: because it calls up absurd thoughts, insolent, prodigious and the like which stimulate and light up depraved carnal affections.”

He then adds the triple-burden argument. The Latin passage is at *Prophetica* (Cambridge, 1592), sig. F8r. The modern English version available online renders it:

> “Artificial memory aids, like those which depend on remembering places and images can teach us how to commit sermons to memory easily, but they cannot be approved…”

**[Text]** This is important: Perkins does not deny that local memory works. He says explicitly that it can make retention easy. His objections are moral and cognitive.

#### Peter of Ravenna

**[Text/Yates]** G. P. attacks the affective, erotic mnemonic recommendations of Pietro da Ravenna. Pietro’s *Phoenix sive artificiosa memoria* recommends placing images of beautiful women in loci because their presence excites his memory. Perkins treats this as proof that “animation” stimulates corrupt carnal affection.

Pietro’s advice is real, not a Protestant fabrication. But Yates’s description of him as transformed by the “whirligig of time” from a respected medieval authority into a lewd figure is her historical irony, not Perkins’s terminology.

#### “A thing feigned in the mind … is an idol”

**[Text, but not a definition of every mental image]** Perkins’s *Warning against the Idolatrie of the Last Times* argues that when the mind gives God a bodily form—his example is God imagined “like an old man sitting in heaven in a throne with a sceptre”—an internal idol has been erected.

**[Yates]** Yates brings this doctrine into explanatory conjunction with Perkins’s attack on mnemonic imagery and calls Ramism an “inner iconoclasm.”

**[Correction]** Later scholarship shows that Perkins did not reject imagination as a faculty, all internal pictures, or all visual organization. His theology gives imagination legitimate psychological functions. The prohibition is especially directed against false representations of God and against deliberately provocative mnemonic images. “A thing feigned in the mind is an idol” should not be detached from its theological object and converted into a universal ban on imagery.

### 2. Perkins’s memory for sermons

#### The prescribed procedure

**[Text]** In *Prophetica*, a preacher should impress upon memory:

- doctrinal proofs;
- applications of doctrine;
- illustrations used in application;
- the order in which the material will be expounded.

The preacher does so through axiomatic, syllogistic, and methodical arrangement. The skeleton of the sermon, rather than a sequence of fantastic pictures, supplies the cues.

#### Retrieval

The preacher begins from the governing proposition or doctrine. Its division calls up the proofs; each proof leads to its application; each application leads to its illustration. Retrieval is consequently semantic and hierarchical.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** This resembles a structured outline more closely than a modern “memory palace,” but calling it merely an outline can understate the extent to which Ramists regarded orderly disposition itself as a psychological instrument of memory.

### 3. Watson’s classical local memory

Watson is not a Ramist opponent of local memory. He presents the most technically explicit system in this dossier.

#### Natural memory and recollection

In *Compendium*, chapter 2, Watson compares memory to a book containing many things and recollection to reading that book:

> *Memoriam igitur libro cuidam in se multa complectenti, quae non inspiciuntur; reminiscentiam vero … eiusdem codicis lectioni comparemus.*

“Let us compare memory to a book containing many things which are not being looked at, and recollection to the reading of that same volume.”

He adds:

> *Organum enim virtutis memorativae … per imagines aliquid solummodo continere possit…*

“The organ of the memorative power can contain something only through images.”

Memory is a habit or retained store; recollection is its activation.

#### Natural images

Chapter 3 divides natural images into:

- *verae imagines*, true images of things previously perceived;
- *fortuitae imagines*, constructed or contingent images of things never directly seen, such as Hector killed by Achilles;
- *mutuatae imagines*, borrowed images for incorporeal things.

Watson’s example of a borrowed image is theological:

> *ego pro divinitatis imagine infinitum quendam splendorem capio … et uterque tamen falsas umbras arripimus. Nam vera divinitatis imago penitus ignoratur.*

“I take a certain infinite radiance as an image of divinity; another figures it by another image; yet both of us grasp false shadows, for the true image of divinity is wholly unknown.”

This is a striking complication of any simple Protestant/imagist opposition. Watson accepts the necessity of an image for thought while recognizing its inadequacy.

#### Artificial memory

Chapter 4 defines artificial memory as natural imagery joined by invention to an aid that has no real-world connection with the matter remembered. The aid may be:

- places;
- concatenation or a chain;
- another invented device.

Watson then restates the classical principle:

> *artificiosam memoriam ex locis et imaginibus constare.*

“Artificial memory consists of places and images.”

#### The five-object example

The learner must remember:

> *lapidem, arborem, piscem, avem, equum*  
> “a stone, a tree, a fish, a bird, a horse.”

Watson chooses a familiar wall containing five formally distinct stations:

1. a door;
2. a window;
3. a cupboard;
4. an iron hook;
5. a deep crack.

He places:

- a stone so large that it blocks the whole door;
- a tree whose root has grown through the far side of the wall and whose branches project through the window;
- a fish hidden in the cupboard, wearing a helmet;
- a bird whose beak is smashing the iron hook;
- a horse whose tail is firmly trapped in the crack.

In Watson’s Latin:

> *lapidis imaginem … in ostio parietis … imaginando forsan quod lapis unus totum hoc ostium implendo obstruat.*

“I shall place the image of the stone at the door of the wall, imagining that one stone fills and blocks the whole door.”

And:

> *piscem includam, ipsumque ibi latitantem in capite galeam gerere imaginabor.*

“I shall shut the fish in the cupboard and imagine it hiding there with a helmet on its head.”

Retrieval occurs by mentally extending thought through the known locations; the objects occur in the order in which they were installed.

#### True and fictive places

Chapter 5 distinguishes:

- **true places**, existing outside thought:
  - artificial structures such as buildings and their parts;
  - natural features such as rocks, mountains, valleys, meadows, rivers, and woods;
- **fictive places**, existing only in thought.

A practitioner may take rooms in one or several familiar buildings until a hundred loci are collected. Each room receives a persistent distinguishing sign—a sword, bed, counting table, bath, and so forth. The image to be remembered acts upon the sign, is acted upon by it, or uses it as an instrument.

Another method assigns five loci to every room, so twenty rooms supply one hundred places.

#### Watson’s constructed thirty-two-place figure

Watson says that he personally uses fictive places for larger collections. His printed text preserves mnemonic verses specifying a quadrangular ground-plan crossed at right angles by two pairs of rivers. The divisions produce thirty-two loci. Each area is imagined as a triangular-fronted building with:

- an upper terrace or solar;
- a door;
- four front windows;
- equal streets separating it from its neighbors;
- an assigned mechanical or otherwise distinctive activity;
- a numeral fixing its order.

The surviving online edition displays the relevant diagram from the printed version. It is a plan rather than a revolving wheel.

#### Two manuscript-only diagrams

**[Text/manuscript collation]** Sloane MS 3731 contains two additional schemes absent from the printed *Compendium*.

1. **CENTURIA QUADRATA TYPOGRAPHICA**, “Typographical Square Century”:
   - a large square ruled ten-by-ten into one hundred smaller squares;
   - Watson’s verse directs that the areas be doubled and alternating spaces marked with two colors;
   - the manuscript was not colored, so the intended two-color operation cannot be reconstructed with certainty.

2. **ALIA CENTURIA QUAE CIRCULARIS EST**, “Another Century, Which Is Circular”:
   - a circular spiderweb;
   - a spider at the center;
   - radii extending outward;
   - concentric circles crossing the radii;
   - one hundred wedge-shaped panels.

Watson’s verse says:

> *Tela tibi, solers qualem sibi texit arachne,  
> centenos poterit suppeditare locos.*

“Here is a web for you, such as the clever spider weaves for itself; it can supply a hundred places.”

He immediately identifies a defect:

> *quod par sit sedi nulla plataea sua.*

No area has a square or properly proportioned “street” corresponding to its wedge-shaped seat.

These are not Brunian rotating wheels. They are static plans for multiplying ordered loci.

#### Watson’s nine laws of places

Chapter 6 supplies nine rules:

1. Many things require many places.
2. Places should be of moderate size; huge places enlarge images excessively, tiny ones cannot contain them.
3. Places should be neither too bright nor too dark.
4. They should be removed from crowds.
5. Intervals should be moderate, allowing easy mental progression.
6. They should not be so close that images merge.
7. Order must be inviolable and secured by number.
8. Empty places must frequently be traversed in thought.
9. They should periodically remain empty so that old images are not confused with new ones.

Numbering permits recall from the beginning, from the middle, or backward from the end. Some practitioners mark every fifth locus.

#### Images for simple things

Chapter 8 says concrete things supply their own likenesses: a horse represents a horse and a human a human. Incorporeal things require proportional images:

- a book for knowledge;
- a laurel crown for virtue;
- scales or a sword for justice;
- fetters for slavery;
- gold rings for liberty.

Watson openly recognizes ambiguity: a sword might represent justice or itself. Natural memory must resolve the intended reference.

#### Images for complex things

A complete narrative should not be translated word by word or object by object. One chooses a salient cue for a coherent episode.

For the plague of frogs in Exodus, one frog suffices to recall the connected narrative. To remember all the plagues, however, one chooses a distinct sign for each and assigns the signs to separate loci.

Thus artificial memory supplies the cue, but natural memory supplies the narrative’s internal order. Watson’s distinction is technically important: an image of a simple object resembles it, while an image of a complex argument is merely an auxiliary sign.

#### Rules for effective images

Chapter 10 follows the *Rhetorica ad Herennium*. Weak images concern ordinary, trivial, daily matters. Strong images are:

- great;
- unheard-of;
- incredible;
- new;
- rare;
- admirable;
- conspicuously ugly;
- exceptionally beautiful;
- disastrous;
- ridiculous;
- otherwise remarkable.

The practitioner should use as few images as possible. They should act rather than remain idle.

#### The lawsuit image

Watson closely adapts the famous example from *Ad Herennium* III.20. An accuser alleges that a defendant poisoned a man to obtain an inheritance; the fact is supposedly attested by many witnesses.

Watson directs the advocate to imagine the victim sick in bed and the defendant beside him:

> *dextra poculum, sinistra tabulas testamenti, digito vero suo, qui medicus appellatur, tenentem testiculos arietinos.*

“In his right hand he holds a cup, in his left the tablets of the will, and with the finger called the physician he holds a ram’s testicles.”

The cup cues poison; the will-tablets cue inheritance; the ram’s testicles cue *testes*, “witnesses,” through a visual-verbal pun. This is essentially the ancient lawsuit image, locally adjusted by Watson.

#### Concatenation

Watson also describes a chain method. To remember man, horse, stone, fire, pig, tree:

- the man grips the horse’s tail;
- the horse bites a stone so forcefully that fire flies out;
- the fire burns the pig’s bristles;
- the enraged pig overturns the tree.

He concedes three advantages:

- no preconstructed series of loci is required;
- one item leads immediately to the next;
- the chain has potentially unlimited capacity.

He nevertheless prefers places because chains can be hard to construct, adjacent items can become confused, and one cannot immediately say what number an item occupies without starting at the beginning.

#### Memory for words

Watson gives three methods:

1. represent the thing signified by the word;
2. use a rebus, cognate, or associated word;
3. divide a word into syllables and represent each syllable.

His artificial word *acelipo* is represented by:

- *aqua* → *a*;
- *cera* → *ce*;
- *liber* → *li*;
- *pomum* → *po*.

He admits that this syllabic procedure is of limited utility.

#### Practical applications

Watson explicitly mentions:

- lecturing in schools;
- preaching;
- courtroom pleading;
- memorizing an opponent’s speech;
- retaining sciences, laws, and statutes permanently;
- retaining a lecture, sermon, or legal plea for two or three days.

A premeditated speech is easier than an opponent’s disorderly one. For heard discourse, the practitioner identifies:

- its beginning;
- narration;
- divisions;
- arguments;
- discontinuous refutations;
- especially striking words.

The cues should be mentally reviewed several times.

### 4. Sidney’s verse-room

Sidney does not give a complete mnemonic system. He makes a technical analogy within an argument for verse.

The 1595 *Defence* reads:

> “Lastly even they that have taught the Art of memory, have shewed nothing so apt for it, as a certain roome divided into many places, well & thoroughly knowne: Now that hath the verse in effect perfectly, everie word having his natural seat, which must needs make the word remembred.”

His surrounding argument is equally important:

- pleasure has an affinity with memory;
- verse is tightly interdependent, so the loss of one word disrupts the whole and calls recollection back;
- rhyme and measure allow the preceding word to suggest the following word;
- educational rules from grammar through logic, mathematics, and medicine are consequently cast in verse.

Verse therefore internalizes the two chief mnemonic requirements:

1. a stable order;
2. an error signal when an expected element is missing.

**[Yates]** Yates calls this an “interesting adaptation of local memory” and concludes that Sidney did not memorize poetry by the Ramist method.

**[Correction]** The first statement is secure; the second exceeds the evidence. Sidney says verse functions *like* a well-known divided room. He does not report his own mnemonic practice, reject Ramist disposition, or identify which art he used to learn poems. A person could approve Ramist analysis for arguments and recognize the spatial or positional mnemonic properties of meter.

## The sources

### The four-document pamphlet controversy

#### Dicsone, *De umbra rationis*

**[Bibliographical record]** Printed by Thomas Vautrollier in London. The title page is dated 1583, but the dedication to Robert Dudley, Earl of Leicester, is dated the Kalends of January. On modern reckoning it was probably issued early in 1584.

**[Text/Yates]** The title deliberately echoes Bruno’s *De umbris idearum* (Paris, 1582). Dicsone dramatizes memory in a prosopopoeia and adapts Brunian “shadows.” His Socrates mocks dialectical pretensions.

**[Yates/disputed]** Yates interprets this Socrates as a satire upon Ramus. The identification is plausible because G. P. responded as a defender of Ramus, but the characterization is not sufficiently individualized to constitute proof.

#### G. P., *Antidicsonus*

Full imprint:

> *Antidicsonus cuiusdam Cantabrigiensis G. P. Accessit Libellus, in quo dilucidè explicatur impia Dicsoni artificiosa memoria.*

London: Henry Middleton for I. Harrison, 1584; 48 pages, octavo; STC 19064. The appended *Libellus* occupies pp. 33–48 and has a separate title page. The BnF holds the volume as Z-54564, with microfilm MFILM Z-54564.

The dedication is to physician Thomas Moufet or Muffett. Its Latin closing appeal, quoted by Yates, says that Moufet’s name may repel:

> *Scepsianos impetus totamque Dicsoni scholam effervescentem in me atque erumpentem.*

“The assaults of the Scepsians and the whole school of Dicsone boiling up and breaking out against me.”

#### Dicsone, *Defensio*

Full title:

> *Heii Scepsii Defensio pro Alexandro Dicsono Arelio adversus quendam G. P. Cantabrigien. Ad illustrissimum Robertum Dudleum, comitem Licestaraeum. Sub umbra illius, quam desideraveram, sedi.*

London: Thomas Vautrollier for Francis Coldock, 1584; [4], 72 pages; STC 21809. The Folger record points to an EEBO reproduction of the Cambridge University Library copy.

“Heius Scepsius” is Dicsone’s pseudonym. “Scepsius” proudly appropriates G. P.’s attempt to associate him with Metrodorus of Scepsis and zodiacal memory. The phrase *sub umbra illius … sedi*, “I sat beneath the shadow I had desired,” continues the polemical play on shadows.

#### G. P., *Libellus de memoria*

Full title:

> *Libellus de memoria, verissimaque bene recordandi scientia. Authore G. P. Cantabrigiense. Huc accessit eiusdem admonitiuncula ad A. Disconum, de artificiosae memoriae, quam publicè profitetur, vanitate.*

London: Robert Waldegrave, 1584. The surviving Folger catalogue record attributes it to Perkins with qualification.

### Watson’s manuscript

The correct shelfmark is:

> **British Library, Sloane MS 3731, fols. 3–43.**

The item is entitled *Artificiosae memoriae libellus*. Its dedication to Albertus Łaski is dated:

> *Sexto Idus Iunii, 1583*  
> 6 June 1583.

Watson signs himself:

> *Vestrae celsitudinis humillimus orator, Thomas Watson.*

The online editor considers the hand Watson’s and the manuscript a formal presentation copy. It was later bound with a letter of November 1586 in which William Lewis transmitted it to jurist Francis Beaumont, saying that an unnamed friend had given it to him.

**[Correction]** Yates printed “Sloane 3751.” That is erroneous. The manuscript used by the modern edition is Sloane 3731.

### Watson’s printed edition

> *Compendium memoriae localis. Autore Thoma Watsono Londinensi, I. V. studioso.*

The pamphlet has no printed place, printer, or date. STC assigns [London], [Thomas Vautrollier], [1585?]; the Folger/EEBO record gives [56] pages and signatures A–G⁴. The reproduced Huntington copy lacks G4 and has a mutilated G3.

The date c.1585 derives principally from the dedication to Henry Noel, which refers to Watson’s Latin *Amyntas*. The online editor suggests John Wolfe as another possible printer because of the numerous errors. Neither printer has been demonstrated from a signed imprint.

The manuscript and print are substantially the same work, but:

- they have different dedicatees;
- the presentation manuscript is explicitly dated 1583;
- the printed version has stylistic and verbal revisions;
- two large locus diagrams in the manuscript are omitted from print;
- the online edition supplies a chapter-by-chapter collation.

### Sidney’s textual witnesses

Four principal witnesses are recognized:

1. Penshurst manuscript, De L’Isle MS 1226;
2. Norwich manuscript, recovered in a commonplace book;
3. Henry Olney’s *An Apologie for Poetrie* (1595);
4. William Ponsonby’s *The Defence of Poesie* (1595), printed by Thomas Creede.

The Ponsonby copy underlying the Oregon transcription is British Library C.57.b.38. A standard critical edition is Katherine Duncan-Jones and Jan van Dorsten, eds., *Miscellaneous Prose of Sir Philip Sidney* (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1973).

Composition dating remains approximate. c.1580–83 is safer than asserting c.1583–85. Publication was posthumous.

### No wheels in Perkins or Sidney

**[Absence]** No evidence was found that Perkins’s mnemonic method employed a rotating wheel. His system is analytical and taxonomic.

**[Absence]** Sidney’s passage contains no diagram.

**[Text]** Watson supplies static ground-plans and grids. Calling them “Brunian wheels” would be misleading.

## Yates’s account and the scholarship since

### Yates’s chapter XII narrative

Yates presents the dispute as the English collision of Brunian and Ramist memory. Her sequence is:

1. Bruno’s London memory books and Oxford encounter create the immediate intellectual setting.
2. Dicsone publishes a close English imitation of Bruno.
3. G. P., identified as William Perkins, defends Ramus.
4. Dicsone replies as “Heius Scepsius.”
5. Perkins restates his anti-imagistic position in another pamphlet.
6. Perkins’s signed *Prophetica* confirms the attribution and applies Ramist method to sermons.
7. Watson publishes an orthodox local mnemonic amid this “red-hot” dispute.
8. Sidney’s verse-room shows that a strongly Ramist-associated courtier could also value local-memory principles.

Yates calls the controversy potentially “one of the most basic of all Elizabethan controversies.” She sees the contestants as representing:

- “profound Egypt” versus “superficial Greece,” from Dicsone’s perspective;
- superstitious Egypt versus reformed Puritan Greece, from Perkins’s;
- Hermetic, affective imagination versus rational, emotionally “aseptic” dialectic.

### What Yates established securely

Several conclusions have survived well:

- **[Confirmed]** Dicsone’s title and doctrine depend closely on Bruno.
- **[Confirmed]** G. P. explicitly champions Ramus as the true art of memory.
- **[Confirmed]** the disagreement concerns both cognition and morality.
- **[Confirmed]** Perkins’s *Prophetica* independently repeats the attack on local memory.
- **[Confirmed]** Watson presents a comparatively plain classical mnemonic.
- **[Confirmed]** Sidney knew the architectural vocabulary of artificial memory.
- **[Confirmed]** Ramist method was not merely formal logic; it was also a pedagogical and memorial program.

Yates was indebted here to Walter J. Ong’s *Ramus, Method, and the Decay of Dialogue* (1958), especially its demonstration that printed spatial disposition and method were central to Ramist pedagogy.

### Perkins’s attribution

**[Yates]** Yates says the identity of G. P. “will become certain” and treats the signed *Prophetica* parallel as proof.

**[Disputed bibliographical history]** Nineteenth-century Cambridge bibliography proposed Gerard Peeters or Pesters, a Trinity College man whose initials fit. The older conjecture appears in Cooper’s *Athenae Cantabrigienses* and Bowes’s *Catalogue of Cambridge Books*. It required an undergraduate Peeters to have produced both technically knowledgeable pamphlets.

**[Present consensus]** Modern Perkins editors, the BnF, Folger, and most scholarship attribute the texts to William Perkins, but responsible catalogues retain “attributed” or a question mark because the title pages say only G. P.

Evidence for Perkins includes:

- Cambridge affiliation and initials;
- intense Ramism;
- theological hostility to impious image-making;
- the close arguments and vocabulary of the signed *Prophetica*;
- appropriate chronology: Perkins took the M.A. in 1584;
- the improbability that two Cambridge G. P.s independently held the same distinctive position.

The parallel is strong cumulative evidence, not a signed confession or archival payment record. “Certain” is therefore bibliographically stronger than the surviving external evidence permits.

### “Inner iconoclasm”

**[Yates]** Yates connects Perkins’s external campaign against religious images with destruction of images in the mind. Her memorable phrase is “inner iconoclasm.” She invokes his warning that an imagined bodily form of God becomes an idol.

**[Later correction]** Joshua Reid, “William Perkins, the imagination in Calvinist theology and ‘inner iconoclasm’ after Frances Yates” (2022), argues:

- the 1584 pamphlets concentrate more on Ramist disciplinary method than on iconoclasm;
- Perkins does not reject imagination *tout court*;
- Calvinists and Ramists were not uniformly hostile to mnemonic imagery;
- Reformed scholars such as Johann Heinrich Alsted combined Ramist organization with traditions indebted to Bruno;
- English thinkers Nicholas Hill and Walter Warner likewise complicate any clean Ramist/Brunian confessional divide.

Reid concludes that Yates’s general equation—Calvinist iconoclasm produces rejection of mental imagery—is not sustainable. Yet he does not erase Perkins’s genuine moral attack on animated mnemonic pictures.

The best-supported formulation is:

> Perkins opposed particular uses of imagined images because they could misrepresent God, arouse corrupt affections, and distract memory; his Ramism supplied a non-local alternative. That is not identical with rejecting the psychological faculty of imagination.

### Watson: orthodox alternative, covert Hermeticist, or satirist?

#### Yates’s reading

Yates describes the *Compendium* as:

> “a perfectly straight exposition of the classical art as a rational mnemotechnic.”

She reads its preface as carefully dissociating Watson from Bruno’s “mystical and deeply learned *Sigilli*” and Dicsone’s “artificial shadow.” In the heat of 1584–85, publication of local memory could therefore look like taking a position against Puritan Ramism.

#### What the preface says

Watson writes:

> *Siquidem valde vereor ne, si nugae meae vel cum mysticis et doctissimis Nolani Sigillis, vel cum artificiosa Dicsoni Umbra conferantur, maiorem authori infamiam quam lectori utilitatem adferant.*

“I greatly fear that, if my trifles are compared either with the mystical and most learned Seals of the Nolan or with Dicsone’s artificial Shadow, they may bring more disgrace to their author than benefit to the reader.”

The tone is ambiguous. It can be humble praise, ironic distance, or both.

#### Charles Nicholl’s reading

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** Nicholl treated the reference as evidence that Watson acknowledged Bruno’s mastery and participated in an arcane intellectual milieu.

#### The Birmingham editor’s correction

Dana F. Sutton’s online edition argues that Watson is doing nearly the reverse:

- the technical substance is practical and Ciceronian;
- Watson regards *Ad Herennium* as Cicero’s;
- nothing in the operative system is astrological, talismanic, Hermetic, or theurgic;
- “mystical and learned” may carry sarcasm;
- Watson may be deflating Bruno by reducing memory to an ordinary technical art.

The editor goes further and suggests Watson may have delivered the treatise in Bruno’s presence as a practical joke or studied insult.

**[Disputed/speculative]** The anti-Brunian technical contrast is strong. The practical-joke scenario is ingenious but undocumented. The manuscript does not name Bruno, and no witness records such a performance.

### Was Watson’s lecture delivered before Łaski and Bruno?

**[Text]** The manuscript dedication is dated 6 June 1583 and addressed to Łaski.

**[Context]** Łaski shortly afterward visited Oxford with Leicester and Sidney; Bruno was associated with the party.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** Certain oral features—*vobis ante oculos ponam*, “I shall put before your eyes,” Watson’s self-description as *orator*, and a precise date—suggest delivery rather than merely presentation.

**[Disputed]** There is no attendance list and no eyewitness report. It is plausible that Watson delivered it before Łaski. Bruno’s presence is possible but not established.

### Sidney and Ramism

Yates correctly notes Sidney’s Ramist connections:

- Sir William Temple was his friend;
- Temple dedicated his 1584 edition of Ramus’s *Dialecticae libri duo* to Sidney;
- Abraham Fraunce, Sidney’s protégé, wrote Ramist works.

Sidney also belonged to the circle to which Bruno dedicated Italian dialogues, and Dicsone apparently attended him.

**[Correction]** These affiliations need not force Sidney into one camp. Patronage, acquaintance, use of verse, and commitment to a single mnemonic doctrine are different things. His room analogy proves knowledge of local-memory theory, not exclusive practice.

### Carruthers

Mary Carruthers’s *The Book of Memory* and *The Craft of Thought* broadened the object of study beyond Yates’s dominant line from classical places-and-images to Renaissance occult memory. Carruthers emphasizes:

- memory as a craft of composition and invention, not passive storage;
- textual division, numbering, rubrication, page layout, and learned order;
- meditation and ethical formation;
- the compatibility of imagistic, verbal, and schematic techniques.

Applied here, Carruthers makes Perkins less anomalous: logical divisio and textual order belong to a much larger memorial culture. She also helps explain why Watson repeatedly says natural memory completes what artificial cues begin.

### Rossi

Paolo Rossi’s *Clavis universalis*, later translated as *Logic and the Art of Memory*, situates mnemonic systems within classification, combinatorics, and the universal-language project. Rossi is less willing than Yates to let occult religion dominate the story. For the English controversy, his approach supports seeing both camps as rival information architectures:

- Dicsone: imagistic and cosmological;
- Perkins: taxonomic and logical;
- Watson: spatial and rhetorical.

### Small

Jocelyn Penny Small’s *Wax Tablets of the Mind* cautions against treating ancient memory theory as a stable, continuous technique unchanged across cultures. It stresses the material, oral, visual, and textual circumstances of ancient mnemonic practices. That warning matters because Watson’s “Ciceronian” art is a sixteenth-century reconstruction based substantially on *Ad Herennium*, not transparent survival of Roman practice.

### Bolzoni, Stagno, Bonner, Fidora, Sturlese, Ciliberto, Clucas, and Gatti

These scholars are essential to the wider catalogue—Camillo, Lull, and Bruno—but only some bear directly on this English episode:

- Lina Bolzoni and Paola Stagno refine the reconstruction of Camillo’s theater and warn against treating every architectural memory as a single Hermetic species.
- Anthony Bonner and Alexander Fidora separate Llull’s demonstrative, relational Art from later pseudo-Lullian magical or mnemonic appropriations.
- Rita Sturlese’s textual work on Bruno and Michele Ciliberto’s philosophical interpretations make it unsafe to reduce Bruno to “Hermetic magician.”
- Stephen Clucas demonstrates hybrid English receptions in which atomism, Ramism, Bruno, and mathematical interests overlap.
- Hilary Gatti emphasizes Bruno’s philosophy, rhetoric, literary strategies, and political context.

Their collective consequence is methodological: Dicsone’s Brunian derivation is real, but “Brunian,” “Hermetic,” “magical,” “Catholic,” and “anti-Ramist” are not interchangeable categories.

### Yates’s later work

Yates returned to these issues in *The Occult Philosophy in the Elizabethan Age* (1979). She continued to foreground occult philosophy, Christian Cabala, and Hermeticism. No explicit later retraction of the Perkins “inner iconoclasm” thesis was found. Her later work modifies the surrounding intellectual map more than it formally revises chapter XII.

## History and context

### Classical rhetoric

Watson’s immediate canonical sources are:

- pseudo-Cicero, *Rhetorica ad Herennium* III.16–24;
- Cicero, *De oratore* II.350–360;
- Quintilian, *Institutio oratoria* XI.2.

The core principles are ancient:

- places resemble wax tablets or writing material;
- images resemble letters;
- order of places preserves order of discourse;
- extraordinary and affective images adhere better than ordinary ones.

Watson repeats the lawsuit image and the wax/writing analogy. He believes *Ad Herennium* is Cicero’s, as did many Renaissance readers.

### Scholastic psychology

Watson’s account presupposes Aristotelian faculty psychology:

- sense supplies phantasms;
- imagination retains or constructs images;
- intellect cannot ordinarily think without a phantasm;
- recollection searches an ordered store.

Yet his theory is eclectic and practical rather than a scholastic commentary. The relation between natural and artificial memory is central: artificial signs do not eliminate ordinary associative and narrative memory.

### Renaissance printed memory manuals

Watson belongs to the practical tradition represented by:

- Jacobus Publicius;
- Pietro da Ravenna;
- Johannes Romberch;
- Cosmas Rossellius;
- later Lambert Schenkel.

G. P. expressly associates Dicsone with Metrodorus, Rossellius, Bruno, and other ostentatious *memoriographi*. His polemic therefore attacks a recognizable printed profession as well as Dicsone personally.

### Ramism

Ramus redistributed the rhetorical canon. In the Ramist settlement:

- invention and disposition became functions of dialectic;
- rhetoric was reduced chiefly to style and delivery;
- memory ceased to require a separate imagistic art because correct method made discourse memorable.

Printing favored this pedagogy: divisions, brackets, headings, tables, and visible hierarchy converted discourse into inspectable spatial order.

**[Qualification]** Ramism was not simply caused by print, nor did it abolish speech. Perkins’s purpose was preaching, a profoundly oral activity. Print and diagrams helped prepare oral performance.

### Cambridge and Oxford

Cambridge was a major English center of Ramism. Perkins studied and taught there and became an influential preacher at Great St Mary’s.

Oxford supplied the spectacular context of Łaski’s visit in June 1583. Bruno participated in Oxford disputations and later represented the university as scholastically hostile. Sidney accompanied Łaski. Watson’s dated address falls immediately before this visit.

### Court, embassy, and patronage

The networks overlap:

- Bruno resided with French ambassador Michel de Castelnau;
- Łaski received elaborate court and university hospitality;
- Leicester patronized or was addressed by Dicsone;
- Sidney was Leicester’s nephew;
- Bruno dedicated Italian dialogues to Sidney;
- Watson dedicated the manuscript to Łaski and the print to Henry Noel;
- G. P. dedicated *Antidicsonus* to Thomas Moufet.

The debate moved through courts, embassy households, printing houses, universities, and legal circles rather than through a single school.

### Legal culture

Watson calls himself *I. V. studiosus*, a student of both laws, and repeatedly uses courtroom examples. His worked lawsuit image and advice about remembering an adversary’s case make local memory a professional forensic tool.

The suggestion that he belonged to an Inn of Court is unsupported by surviving admissions records. His legal interests are documented; institutional membership is not.

### Confessional context

Perkins was a Reformed theologian traditionally called Puritan, though W. B. Patterson stresses his role as an apologist working within the Elizabethan Church. Dicsone’s publisher Vautrollier was a Huguenot who also printed Ramist works.

This fact alone defeats a simple equation:

```text
Protestant printer → Ramist text
Catholic printer → Brunian text
```

The printing and patronage networks crossed confessional and philosophical lines.

## People

### William Perkins

Perkins (1558–1602) took his B.A. at Cambridge in 1581 and M.A. in 1584. He became a major theologian, casuist, teacher, and preacher.

**[Documented]** His signed *Prophetica* rejects local memory and prescribes methodical recollection for preaching.

**[Attributed]** He is almost certainly G. P., but the 1584 books are pseudonymous.

### Alexander Dicsone

Dicsone or Dickson (bap. 1558, d. 1603/4), from Errol in Scotland, studied at St Andrews, became a follower of Bruno, and later worked as a political agent.

In 1592 the English diplomat Robert Bowes described him as:

> “Dickson, master of the art of memory, and sometime attending on Mr. Philip Sidney.”

This is valuable evidence that Dicsone’s relation to Sidney was not invented by Yates. The exact nature and duration of “attending” remain unknown.

### Thomas Watson

Watson (1557?–1592) was a poet, Latinist, translator, and legal student, later associated with Christopher Marlowe. His *Hekatompathia* and Latin *Amyntas* made him an important Elizabethan literary figure.

His memory treatise displays genuine technical interest, not a passing metaphor.

### Sir Philip Sidney

Sidney (1554–1586) was poet, courtier, soldier, Protestant political thinker, and literary patron. He knew or patronized people on both sides of the later historiographical divide:

- Ramists such as William Temple and Abraham Fraunce;
- Bruno, who dedicated works to him;
- Dicsone, who reportedly attended him.

### Thomas Moufet

The physician and naturalist Thomas Moufet was the dedicatee of *Antidicsonus*. G. P. hoped his authority would shield the work from the “school of Dicsone.”

### Henry Noel

Watson dedicated the printed *Compendium* to Henry Noel, a courtier and patron. The reference to *Amyntas* helps date the edition.

### Albertus Łaski

Adalbert or Olbracht Łaski, Voivode of Sieradz, is the dedicatee of Watson’s manuscript. English contemporaries inaccurately styled him a “Pfalzgraf” or Palatine in a German sense. His visit connected court spectacle, Oxford, Sidney, John Dee, and Bruno.

### The alleged anecdotes

The requested motifs—the auctioneer, the professor’s party, the student who lost his memory, and the accused magician—do not belong securely to the Perkins–Watson–Sidney documents.

- **The auctioneer:** **[Absence]** No contemporary auctioneer anecdote was found in these sources.
- **The professor’s party:** **[Absence]** No securely documented party at which Perkins, Watson, Sidney, and Bruno debated memory was found. Łaski’s receptions and the conjectured Watson recital may be the ingredients from which a modern composite story could arise.
- **The student who lost his memory:** **[Absence]** Watson says those wholly lacking natural memory cannot become masters of the art, but gives no narrative about a student who lost his memory.
- **The accused magician:** **[Misplaced tradition]** This motif belongs more readily to Pietro da Ravenna, Lambert Schenkel, Bruno, and other prodigious mnemonists. There is no evidence that Watson or Sidney was prosecuted as a magician for mnemonic practice.
- **Bruno reciting a Hebrew psalm forward and backward before the pope:** **[Legend]** This appears in popular biographical retellings but is not a secure component of the English controversy.

## The magic question

### Was Dicsone’s memory “magic”?

**[Text]** Dicsone borrowed Bruno’s language of shadows and celestial organization and deliberately aligned himself with the Scepsian/zodiacal tradition.

**[Perkins’s charge]** G. P. calls it *impia artificiosa memoria*, “impious artificial memory,” and treats its astrology, fictive places, and animated images as false and morally dangerous.

**[Yates]** Yates interprets Dicsone as expressing a Hermetic cult and sees the controversy as religious “at bottom.”

**[Qualification]** “Magic” covers several different claims:

1. use of astrological images;
2. belief that images correspond to celestial forces;
3. a transformative contemplative practice;
4. ordinary mnemonic images that an opponent denounces as impious;
5. ritual invocation or operative spirit magic.

The first three can be relevant to Bruno and, in abbreviated form, Dicsone. The fifth has not been demonstrated for Watson, Perkins, or Sidney.

### Was Watson’s memory magical?

**[Text]** No. Its operating instructions require no talismans, celestial timing, invocations, spirits, divine names, or occult correspondences.

Its bizarre images are conventional *imagines agentes*, psychologically striking cues. The ram’s testicles in the lawsuit image are a pun, not a talisman.

**[Disputed interpretation]** Watson’s deferential reference to Bruno’s “mystical and most learned Seals” has encouraged an occult reading. The technical chapters strongly support the contrary conclusion: whatever his personal curiosity, this treatise teaches practical local memory.

### Was Perkins frightened by magic?

Perkins regarded Dicsone’s art as impious and elsewhere wrote extensively against witchcraft and idolatry. Nevertheless:

- the pamphlets’ astrological objection is largely disciplinary;
- no prosecution or Inquisition followed;
- England had no Roman Inquisition;
- the controversy remained a print polemic;
- Dicsone answered publicly and continued in political employment.

It is therefore misleading to narrate the episode as an English inquisitorial trial.

### Heresy and consequences

No participant in this particular dispute was formally convicted of heresy because of these memory texts.

Bruno’s later Roman trial and execution in 1600 cannot simply be attributed to his art of memory. His prosecution involved a much wider body of theological and philosophical propositions. Yates’s narrative makes his death symbolically relevant, but it is not a direct consequence of the Perkins controversy.

### Ars notoria

The *Ars notoria* promised accelerated or infused knowledge through prayers, figures, divine names, and ritual observance. Neither Perkins’s methodical sermon memory nor Watson’s local system is an *ars notoria*.

Perkins would almost certainly have classified illicit angelic or spirit-assisted arts with forbidden magic. But **[Absence]** the sources examined do not establish that he accused Watson’s ordinary local memory of being *ars notoria*.

## Afterlife

### Seventeenth-century preaching and knowledge organization

Perkins’s *Prophetica* had a substantial afterlife in Reformed preaching. Its division of doctrine, proof, application, and illustration helped structure sermons and sermon notes. Ramist and post-Ramist tables became instruments for:

- teaching;
- catechesis;
- commonplacing;
- doctrinal analysis;
- recollection.

This is a direct institutional afterlife of the methodical side of the controversy.

### Hybrid systems

Later scholars such as Alsted show that the supposed victory of one side was incomplete. Encyclopedic tables, topical places, combinatorial structures, emblematic pictures, and conventional mnemonics could coexist.

The history is consequently not:

```text
memory palace → destroyed by Ramism → modern logic
```

It is closer to:

```text
classical loci ─┐
scholastic order ├─→ competing and hybrid early-modern systems
Ramist division ┤
Lullian combination ┤
emblems and images ┘
```

### Literature

Sidney’s verse-room is an early literary formulation of positional redundancy: meter, rhyme, and syntax let one part cue another.

Possible wider echoes include:

- patterned stanza forms;
- emblem books combining motto, picture, and exposition;
- commonplace books;
- allegorical architectures;
- later literary memory palaces.

But no direct documentary chain has been established from Watson’s *Compendium* to Borges’s “Funes the Memorious,” Umberto Eco, or modern fiction.

Borges’s Funes is in significant respects the opposite of the trained Ramist or classical mnemonist: he cannot abstract or forget. The comparison is illuminating but typological, not genealogical.

### Art and architecture

The controversy participates in a culture that understood:

- buildings as ordered information spaces;
- diagrams as visible arguments;
- images as instruments of emotion and knowledge;
- page layout as a memory support.

No particular fresco cycle has been demonstrated to descend from Perkins or Watson. Associating every Renaissance architectural image with the art of memory would repeat one of the broadest and least testable uses of Yates’s thesis.

### Universal language and computing

The broad sequence from Llull through Renaissance combinatorics to Leibniz is historically important, but Perkins’s bifurcating method and Watson’s locus plans are not computers in a strict sense.

Legitimate analogies include:

- addressable storage locations;
- hierarchical data structures;
- indexed retrieval;
- error-detecting redundancy in verse;
- combinatorial generation.

**[Modern analogy]** Calling Watson’s loci “memory addresses” or Ramist trees “data structures” can clarify function.

**[Modern invention if literalized]** Neither author conceived electronic storage, algorithms in the modern formal sense, or a programmable machine. The path from Llull to Leibniz is neither a continuous engineering genealogy nor a direct line through Perkins.

### Modern memory-palace revival

Contemporary mnemonic teaching and memory sports use familiar routes, numbered loci, exaggerated images, and reusable palaces. These practices resemble Watson far more closely than Perkins.

Modern competitors generally treat the technique as cognitive training rather than cosmic, talismanic, or theological practice. Their systems confirm Watson’s pragmatic distinction between stable loci and temporary images, but do not prove his psychological theory in every detail.

## Controversies and disputes

### 1. Is G. P. William Perkins?

**Status:** Strong consensus, not autograph certainty.

- First title-page claim: only “G. P. Cantabrigiensis.”
- Nineteenth-century alternative: Gerard Peeters/Pesters.
- Yates: William Perkins, said to become certain through internal comparison.
- Modern editions and most catalogues: Perkins, sometimes qualified as attributed.
- Best evidence: close agreement with *Prophetica*, chronology, institutional identity, theology, and Ramism.
- Missing evidence: signed copy, authorial letter, printer’s account, or contemporary explicit identification.

### 2. Is Watson’s manuscript Sloane 3751?

**Status:** No.

- Yates: Sloane 3751.
- Modern manuscript edition: Sloane 3731, fols. 3–43.
- The catalogue brief repeats Yates’s error.
- This is a factual shelfmark correction, not an interpretive dispute.

### 3. When did Watson write and print the work?

**Status:** Manuscript securely dated; print conjecturally dated.

- Manuscript dedication: 6 June 1583.
- Printed edition: undated.
- c.1585 follows the reference to *Amyntas*.
- Assertions of “1585 or 1586” or simply “1583” for the print should be marked conjectural.
- The work itself therefore predates the full Perkins–Dicsone pamphlet exchange in manuscript form.

This corrects the notion that Watson necessarily composed his system as a reaction to the controversy. He may have revised or printed it in that environment, but the original address came first.

### 4. Who printed Watson?

**Status:** Unresolved.

- STC: Thomas Vautrollier.
- Online editor: John Wolfe is another possibility, partly because of the error pattern.
- No printed imprint settles the matter.

### 5. Was Watson siding against Puritan Ramism?

**Status:** Plausible reception claim, not explicit declaration.

He teaches exactly what Perkins rejects: places, fictive loci, acting images, grotesque affect, and local retrieval. Publishing it after 1584 could look oppositional. But Watson never names Perkins or Ramus in the treatise, and the manuscript predates their public quarrel.

### 6. Was Watson praising Bruno?

**Status:** Tonally ambiguous.

The phrases *mysticis et doctissimis Nolani Sigillis* and *artificiosa Dicsoni Umbra* can be sincere honorifics. The following reference to “my trifles” and possible “infamy” can be conventional modesty or irony. Technical content favors distance from Bruno, but it cannot determine tone conclusively.

### 7. Did Watson mock Bruno in person?

**Status:** Speculative.

- Evidence: date, oral phrasing, Łaski dedication, possible overlap of social circles.
- Missing: eyewitness testimony, a venue, attendance list, Watson’s statement, or Bruno’s response.
- The “practical joke” version first appears as a modern editorial reconstruction and should not be repeated as fact.

### 8. Was Dicsone’s Socrates Ramus?

**Status:** Plausible but unproven satire.

G. P. evidently felt Ramus attacked. Yet the fictional Socrates is not a detailed portrait, and Dicsone does not identify him as Ramus.

### 9. Was the dispute “at bottom religious”?

**Status:** Yates’s influential but overgeneralized interpretation.

Religion is certainly present:

- *impia* appears in G. P.’s titles;
- Perkins objects to corrupt passions;
- both sides claim moral seriousness;
- Perkins’s later theology condemns interior idols.

But technical issues are equally explicit:

- proper division of disciplines;
- whether astronomical loci can be general;
- natural versus fictive order;
- cognitive burden;
- clarity and direct retrieval.

Later work has overturned the claim that Ramism, Calvinism, and rejection of imagination form a necessary package.

### 10. Did Sidney’s remark prove he did not use Ramist memory?

**Status:** Unsupported inference.

The passage proves he knew local-memory doctrine and saw an analogy with verse. It does not record his personal memorization habits. Yates’s conclusion is possible, not demonstrated.

### 11. Was Sidney a Ramist or Brunian?

**Status:** False dichotomy.

His network and writing show selective engagement with both milieus. Patronage and intellectual hospitality do not equal doctrinal adherence.

### 12. Are Watson’s diagrams Brunian wheels?

**Status:** No.

They are:

- a thirty-two-place fictive ground-plan;
- a hundred-square grid;
- a hundred-cell circular spiderweb.

Nothing indicates rotating concentric letter-wheels of the Lullian-Brunian type.

### 13. Did Perkins reject all visualization?

**Status:** No.

He rejects an external, emotionally provocative image apparatus and false images of God. His own method depends upon visible or mentally graspable logical relations. Later analysis properly distinguishes visual organization from mimetic fantasy.

### 14. Is “Puritan Ramist” sufficient identification?

**Status:** Useful but reductive.

Perkins was Ramist and a major Reformed preacher. “Puritan” is conventional, but recent biography emphasizes his embeddedness in the established Elizabethan Church. Neither label fully explains his mnemonic theory.

### 15. Is the controversy a landmark in the birth of modern rationality?

**Status:** Historiographical construction.

Ong and Yates saw movement from oral-affective dialogue toward visual, printed, analytical method. That remains suggestive. But Watson’s system is already highly analytical, and Perkins’s method serves oral preaching. Later hybrid systems prevent a clean story of replacement.

## Open questions

1. Can external evidence beyond doctrinal parallelism settle the authorship of the two G. P. pamphlets?
2. Can type, ornaments, paper, or compositorial habits determine the printer and date of Watson’s *Compendium*?
3. What was the original function of the uncolored two-color instruction in Watson’s “typographical century”?
4. Was Sloane MS 3731 actually delivered orally on 6 June 1583, or merely presented?
5. If delivered, where, and who attended?
6. Did Bruno ever read or respond to Watson?
7. Does the print’s removal of two diagrams reflect expense, practical revision, printer limitations, or a conceptual change?
8. How widely was Watson’s pamphlet owned and annotated?
9. Did legal practitioners use his courtroom technique, or is its forensic application primarily authorial self-positioning?
10. Does surviving correspondence clarify Dicsone’s service to Sidney?
11. How far does Dicsone’s system reproduce Bruno’s actual mechanisms rather than only his terminology and philosophical posture?
12. Can verbal comparison between *Antidicsonus*, *Libellus de memoria*, and Perkins’s authenticated Latin works quantify the attribution?
13. Did Sidney’s “natural seat” derive directly from a particular mnemonic manual, or from broadly circulating doctrine?
14. How did readers reconcile Perkins’s prohibition of affectively animated images with the vivid exempla, metaphors, and emotional appeals of Reformed preaching?
15. Did the controversy materially change English mnemonic publication, or was its later importance largely created by Yates?
16. No documentary source yet ties the requested auctioneer, lost student, or professor’s-party anecdotes to this episode. Their origins should be traced separately before inclusion in its reception history.
