# The philosophers' memory: Aristotle, Plato, Augustine: Research Dossier

## Basic identification

**Name:** The philosophers’ memory: Aristotle, Plato, Augustine  
**Catalogue position:** System 5 of 45  
**Exponents:** Aristotle; Plato; Augustine of Hippo  
**Dates:** Plato, ca. 427–347 BCE; Aristotle, 384–322 BCE, relevant works chiefly fourth century BCE; Augustine, 354–430 CE, *Confessions* ca. 397–401  
**Source texts:** Aristotle, *De memoria et reminiscentia* 449b4–453b11, with *De anima* 427b, 431b, 432b, *Topica* 163b24–30, and *De insomniis* 458b20–22; Plato, *Theaetetus* 191c–196c, *Phaedo* 72e–77a, especially 75b–d, and *Phaedrus* 249e–250d, 274c–275b; Augustine, *Confessions* X.8–26, with *De Trinitate* IX–XI and *Confessions* XI on memory and time.  
**Principle in one sentence:** Sensation leaves images or traces in memory; deliberate recollection recovers what is absent by traversing an ordered chain, while Plato and Augustine enlarge remembering into a metaphysical and theological ascent from sensible signs toward intelligible truth.

**Yates:** *The Art of Memory*, ch. II, printed pp. 31–39 and 44–49; ch. III, pp. 61, 63–64, 67–68, 70–72, 75. Her governing formula is that “Aristotle is essential for the scholastic and mediaeval form of the art; Plato is essential for the art in the Renaissance.” [The digitized Warburg copy preserves this passage](https://resources.warburg.sas.ac.uk/mnemosyne/fq/yates_memory.pdf).

### Evidentiary verdict at the outset

- **[Documented text]** Each author gives a substantial account of memory or recollection.
- **[Documented text]** Aristotle explicitly mentions people who employ “places” in recollecting and compares memory impressions to an image or seal-impression.
- **[Documented text]** Plato uses the wax block and aviary as models of cognition; presents learning as recollection in the *Phaedo* and *Phaedrus*; and distinguishes memory from the reminder supplied by writing.
- **[Documented text]** Augustine speaks of the fields, palaces, caverns, chambers, treasuries, and images of memory.
- **[No documentary evidence]** None of the three texts supplies a complete practical artificial-memory system comparable to *Ad Herennium* III.16–24: there is no numbered route, no prescribed architectural background, no rules for intervals or image formation, and no worked encoding of a speech.
- **[Yates’s interpretation]** Together they constitute the philosophical foundation subsequently joined to the rhetorical *ars memoriae*.
- **[Scholarly reconstruction]** Medieval readers—especially Albert the Great and Thomas Aquinas—made that union explicit; it was not already a single integrated “system” in Aristotle, Plato, or Augustine.
- **[Disputed]** Yates’s inference that Augustine personally possessed a classically trained artificial memory.
- **[Modern invention]** “The philosophers’ memory” and “system 5 of 45” are catalogue labels, not historical names found in any primary source.

## The system in detail

## 1. Aristotle: image, time, association, and ordered search

### 1.1 Memory belongs to the past

In *De memoria* 449b4–451a17 Aristotle distinguishes:

- perception of what is present;
- expectation or opinion concerning the future;
- memory of what has happened.

**[Documented text]** The defining temporal awareness is not merely possession of an image but recognition that its object was perceived or thought **before**. Memory is therefore neither sensation nor judgment simply, but a state or affection arising from them after time has elapsed.

The Greek begins:

> **Ἡ δὲ μνήμη τοῦ γενομένου.**

“Memory is of what has happened” (*De memoria* 449b15; Greek text in W. D. Ross’s 1955 edition, with Beare’s translation in the [Aristotle Reader](https://johnhboyer-sys.github.io/aristotle-reader/Mem/book/1/)).

Animals capable of perceiving time can remember. Recollection, by contrast, requires a more complex capacity to initiate and traverse a sequence; Aristotle associates it especially with human deliberation.

### 1.2 No thinking without a phantasm

**[Documented text]** *De anima* III.7, 431a14–17, says that the thinking soul thinks forms in images or *phantasmata*. III.8, 432a8–10, supplies the famous proposition conventionally rendered:

> **διὸ οὐδέποτε νοεῖ ἄνευ φαντάσματος ἡ ψυχή.**

“Therefore the soul never thinks without a phantasm.”

Here *phantasma* does not mean only a consciously fabricated mnemonic picture. It is the internal appearance left or reactivated by perception—the representational medium in which embodied human cognition operates.

**[Correction to a common summary]** “The soul never thinks without an image” is serviceable, but “image” can misleadingly suggest a little picture consciously inspected. Sorabji and later philosophy-of-mind scholarship emphasize that Aristotle’s *phantasia* covers appearances and representational states more broadly. Richard Sorabji’s revised *Aristotle on Memory* explicitly treats the modern dispute about mental imagery; David Bloch similarly reconstructs memory within Aristotle’s psychology rather than treating the work as a mnemonic manual. [Sorabji’s edition](https://books.google.com/books?id=10UEAQAAIAAJ); [review and contents of Bloch’s study](https://bmcr.brynmawr.edu/2008/2008.08.19/).

### 1.3 The seal in wax

At *De memoria* 450a25–450b11 Aristotle asks how an affection produced by an absent thing can be remembered. Perception produces a movement and, as it were, impresses a type:

> **οἷον τύπον τινὰ τοῦ αἰσθήματος**

“as it were, a certain imprint of the percept.”

The comparison is to people sealing with rings. The resulting *phantasma* can be considered in two ways:

1. as an affection or picture present in the soul;
2. as an image **of** something formerly perceived.

This “double aspect” answers the puzzle. Just as a painted figure may be viewed as paint or as a portrait of Coriscus, the internal image may be considered as a present mental occurrence or as representing a past object.

**What serves as places?** Nothing in this account. The receiving bodily-soul faculty is figuratively like wax, but Aristotle does not divide it into mnemonic loci.

**What serves as images?** Perceptually generated *phantasmata* or traces. These are natural psychological products, not necessarily the extraordinary agents recommended by *Ad Herennium*.

**How is retrieval authenticated?** By the added awareness that the represented perception or thought occurred previously. An image without this temporal reference may be imagination, not memory.

**Rules for images:** Aristotle supplies causal conditions, not an artisan’s rules. Excessively fluid or excessively hard “receiving surfaces”—the very young, the old, and persons in certain bodily states—do not retain a clear impression. This is physiological analogy, not advice to make grotesque or emotionally violent images.

### 1.4 Recollection as a search

At 451b10–22 Aristotle distinguishes passive remembering from active *anamimnēskesthai*. Recollection is a search: a person deliberately originates movements whose sequence may culminate in the wanted memory.

The search proceeds:

- from the same;
- from something similar;
- from something contrary;
- from something contiguous or closely connected.

**[Documented text, later standardized terminology]** Aristotle describes these relations, but the neat textbook formula “the three laws of association—similarity, contrast, contiguity” is a later systematization. Nineteenth-century associationist psychology explicitly adopted and reorganized the Aristotelian scheme, sometimes reducing contrast to similarity or contiguity. [Wundt’s historical discussion](https://psychclassics.yorku.ca/Wundt/Outlines/sec16.htm).

Frequency or habit makes one movement follow another more readily. An ordered series—Aristotle gives mathematical propositions as an example—is easier to recover than a disorderly collection.

### 1.5 Starting points and “places”

The central passage is *De memoria* 452a7–16. One must seize a beginning from which the desired sequence can follow:

> **διὸ καὶ τῶν τόπων ἀναμιμνήσκεταί τινες**

Approximately: “For this reason some recollect by means of places.”

The reading and syntax are difficult; translations vary between “some use places for recollecting” and “some recollect from places.” Yates’s translation presents the line as direct evidence of mnemonic loci.

Aristotle’s example proceeds:

> milk → white → air → moist → autumn.

The precise cosmological logic is uncertain. The likely connecting notions are that milk is white; air may be associated with whiteness or transparency; air with moisture; and moistness with the season sought. Ross thought the text might be corrupt or at least resistant to certain reconstruction. Yates candidly reports the difficulty and treats the artificial mnemonic only as an illustration of Aristotle’s theory, not as the theory’s subject.

**What serves as places?**

- **[Documented text]** A *topos* can function as a stable point of departure.
- **[Plausible reconstruction]** Aristotle is alluding to an already existing Greek method of loci.
- **[Not documented here]** He does not say that the places are rooms, columns, doorways, or a memorized building.
- **[Yates’s interpretation]** The “places” are the loci of artificial memory, invoked because their fixed order exemplifies recollective sequence.
- **[Alternative reading]** The term may include ordinary contextual places—where something was said or experienced—as natural retrieval cues. Thomas Aquinas later explicitly mentions both experiential places and Cicero’s artificially ordered loci.

### 1.6 The alphabetic series

Immediately afterward, 452a16–25 uses a sequence represented by letters. Because the transmitted letters differ between manuscripts and the argument is compressed, editors have proposed numerous rearrangements.

Its general logic is recoverable:

1. represent an ordered sequence as positions such as A–B–C–D–E–F–G–H;
2. begin near a middle point;
3. if the sought item is not reached in one direction, move in the other;
4. a middle point is efficient because it permits traversal both forward and backward.

**[Documented principle]** Recollection depends on recoverable order and a suitable starting point.

**[Textually disputed detail]** The exact letter sequence and which desired item follows which node. Ross’s *Parva Naturalia*, pp. 247–48, and Bloch’s textual notes should be consulted before quoting a particular diagram as uniquely Aristotelian. Yates herself notes “many variations in the manuscripts.”

**Diagram as reconstructable, not transmitted artwork:**

```text
A — B — C — D — E — F — G — H
                ↑
        useful middle beginning
```

This is an editorial visualization of the argument. No authorial wheel, architectural plate, or graphical manuscript tradition belongs to the treatise.

### 1.7 *Topica* VIII.14

At *Topica* 163b24–30 Aristotle compares dialectical preparation to trained memory: just as mention of mnemonic “places” immediately brings the things themselves to mind, arranging propositions under numbered headings makes them readily available in disputation.

**[Documented text]** This is stronger evidence than *De memoria* that Aristotle knew a trained practice using indexed places.

**[Limit]** It remains an analogy, not a practical handbook. The “places” here are also close to dialectical topics or classified headings, so architectural and logical place should not be collapsed without qualification.

### 1.8 Lost Aristotelian mnemonic writing

Diogenes Laertius V.26 includes among works attributed to Aristotle something conventionally rendered *Mnemonics* or *On Memory*, one book.

- **[Ancient catalogue tradition]** A memory-related work circulated under Aristotle’s name or was listed in an Aristotelian bibliography.
- **[Disputed attribution and identity]** It cannot securely be identified with a technical mnemonic manual; it might duplicate or describe the surviving *De memoria*.
- **[Legend if stated strongly]** “Aristotle wrote a lost complete art of memory.” No surviving quotation establishes its contents.

## 2. Plato: wax, anamnesis, living knowledge, and the reminder

### 2.1 The wax block in the *Theaetetus*

At *Theaetetus* 191c–e Socrates asks the interlocutors to imagine:

> **κήρινον ἐκμαγεῖον ἐν ταῖς ψυχαῖς**

“a waxen impression-block in our souls.”

It is a gift of Mnemosyne, mother of the Muses. Perceptions and thoughts are stamped upon it as seal-rings make impressions. What remains legible can be remembered and known; what is erased or was never impressed is forgotten or unknown.

People differ because their wax differs:

- larger or smaller;
- purer or mixed;
- harder or softer;
- properly tempered or badly constituted.

Clear, deep, well-spaced impressions support quick learning and reliable judgment. A small, muddy, hard, or overly soft tablet produces confused, shallow, overlapping, or rapidly effaced traces.

**Function in the dialogue:** The model attempts to explain false judgment as a mismatch—taking the present perception of one person for the stored imprint of another.

**Crucial limitation:** Socrates later shows that the model cannot explain every kind of false judgment, particularly mistakes involving items already known independently of perception. It is a provisional model within an aporetic dialogue, not Plato’s final neuropsychology. The *Theaetetus* entry in the Stanford Encyclopedia emphasizes both its function and its limitations. [SEP, “Plato on Knowledge in the Theaetetus”](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/plato-theaetetus/).

**Places:** The wax is a receptive field, not a route of discrete loci.

**Images:** Impressions of perceptions and thoughts.

**Order:** Spatial separation prevents overlap, but no ordered itinerary is prescribed.

**Retrieval:** Match a current perception with the correct retained imprint.

### 2.2 The aviary variant

At *Theaetetus* 197c–200c Socrates substitutes an aviary filled with birds representing pieces of knowledge. Learning is acquiring birds; knowing is possessing them; recalling or using knowledge is catching the right bird.

This distinguishes:

- having knowledge dispositionality;
- actively grasping the relevant item;
- accidentally seizing the wrong item.

The aviary too fails to solve the regress: how does one know that one has caught the right knowledge-bird without already possessing a higher-order knowledge?

**Variant within the primary text:** Wax explains traces and recognition; the aviary explains possession versus active retrieval. Neither is an artificial mnemonic prescription.

### 2.3 Anamnesis in the *Phaedo*

In *Phaedo* 73c–76e, recollection occurs when perceiving one object causes thought of another—whether similar or dissimilar. The worked example is equality:

- equal sticks or stones are perceived;
- sensible equals fall short of perfect Equality;
- yet the perceiver judges that they fall short;
- therefore the standard “the Equal itself” was known before the sensible comparison;
- learning is the recovery of knowledge possessed before birth.

A compact Greek statement at 75e is:

> **ἡ μάθησις ἀνάμνησις τυγχάνει οὖσα**

“Learning turns out to be recollection.”

**[Documented dramatic argument]** Plato’s Socrates uses anamnesis to support the soul’s prenatal existence.

**[Interpretive dispute]** Whether Plato intends a literal episodic memory of disembodied vision, an argument about a priori conceptual capacities, or a mythically expressed epistemology remains contested.

**[Yates’s interpretation]** True knowledge aligns sensible impressions with “moulds” of intelligible Forms already latent in memory.

**[Correction]** The *Phaedo* does not itself describe Forms as literal seal-moulds stored in a wax palace. That is Yates’s synthesis of the *Phaedo* with the *Theaetetus* metaphor.

### 2.4 Anamnesis in the *Phaedrus*

At 249b–250d the soul’s apprehension of sensible beauty can awaken recollection of Beauty itself, formerly beheld in the soul’s divine procession. The philosopher gathers many perceptions into one rational unity:

> **κατ’ εἶδος λεγόμενον**

“according to a form.”

The earthly image is not an arbitrary mnemonic sign. Beauty is unusually luminous among sensible manifestations and produces awe, desire, and philosophical mania.

**[Yates’s interpretation]** A genuinely Platonic artificial memory would organize its images according to ontological realities rather than trivial verbal associations.

**[Documented text]** Plato does not prescribe building such a memory. The Renaissance program described by Yates belongs to Ficino, Camillo, Bruno, and other Neoplatonizing interpreters who joined Platonic metaphysics to mnemonic architecture.

### 2.5 Theuth, Thamus, and writing

At *Phaedrus* 274c–275b Theuth presents writing to King Thamus as a drug that will make Egyptians wiser and improve memory. Thamus replies that it will cause forgetfulness because learners will rely on external marks rather than recollecting from within:

> **οὔκουν μνήμης ἀλλὰ ὑπομνήσεως φάρμακον**

“a drug not of memory but of reminder.”

The authoritative living discourse of a knower can answer questions and adapt to souls; written discourse circulates indiscriminately and cannot defend or explain itself.

**Evidence labels:**

- **[Documented text]** The distinction is between *mnēmē* and *hypomnēsis*, memory and reminder.
- **[Traditional narrative]** Theuth and Thamus are presented as an Egyptian tale; it is not independent documentation of an Egyptian debate.
- **[Interpretive caution]** Plato wrote dialogues; the passage is not a simple prohibition of writing. It defines writing’s subordinate role within dialectical education.
- **[Modern extension]** Applying the passage directly to search engines, smartphones, or artificial intelligence is an analogy, not an ancient prediction. The continuing philosophical relevance to external and distributed memory is recognized in current memory scholarship. [SEP, “Memory”](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/memory/).

## 3. Augustine: the immeasurable interior and the search for God

### 3.1 Date and purpose

*Confessions* was composed around 397–401, not in 430. The catalogue’s “d. 430” is Augustine’s death date.

Book X changes from autobiographical retrospection to present self-examination before God and readers. Chapters 8–26 examine memory as Augustine searches for where and how God is known.

### 3.2 Fields and vast palaces

*Confessions* X.8.12 opens:

> **venio in campos et lata praetoria memoriae**

“I come into the fields and vast palaces of memory.”

There are:

> **thesauri innumerabilium imaginum**

“treasuries of innumerable images.”

Perceptions enter through their respective sensory “doors.” The remembered objects themselves are not physically present; their images are available. Augustine asks memory to bring something forth:

- some things appear immediately;
- some emerge after delay;
- irrelevant candidates rush forward;
- they are dismissed until the wanted item arrives.

The Latin text is accessible in a chaptered critical-use format through [James O’Donnell’s Georgetown resources](https://faculty.georgetown.edu/jod/conf/text10.html) and in continuous form at [The Latin Library](https://thelatinlibrary.com/augustine/conf10.shtml). A public English translation gives the architectural passage in [Book X](https://web.english.upenn.edu/~cavitch/pdf-library/Augustine_ConfessionsX.pdf).

### 3.3 What memory contains

Augustine’s inventory is more discriminating than “a store of pictures.”

| Contents | Mode of presence in memory |
|---|---|
| Bodies, places, faces, sounds, smells, tastes | Through images or representations |
| Enlarged, diminished, recombined imagined scenes | Transformations of sensory material |
| Liberal arts and learned principles | The things or intelligible contents themselves, not merely sensory images |
| Numbers and mathematical relations | Present as themselves, in Augustine’s account |
| Emotions | Retained as notions without necessarily re-experiencing the emotion |
| Memory itself | Present to itself |
| Forgetfulness | Paradoxically remembered, perhaps through an image |
| Happiness | Somehow already recognizable by all who seek it |
| God | Known in memory but not contained as a spatial image or localized object |

At X.14 Augustine calls memory the belly of the mind—*memoria quasi venter est animi*—where joy and sorrow can be retained without presently tasting their sweetness or bitterness.

At X.17.26:

> **magna vis est memoriae ... profunda et infinita multiplicitas**

“Great is the power of memory … a deep and infinite multiplicity.”

Augustine ranges through its “fields,” “caves,” and “innumerable caverns,” but finds no boundary.

### 3.4 Search and retrieval

Retrieval resembles interrogation:

1. issue an intention or question;
2. memory presents candidates;
3. attention recognizes or rejects them;
4. the correct recollection is acknowledged when found.

In X.18–19 Augustine invokes the woman who lost a coin in Luke 15. One cannot search for what is wholly unknown; one must retain enough recognition to identify the missing thing. This is Augustine’s Christian response to the structure of Meno’s paradox.

**Places:** Architectural spaces are the controlling metaphor.

**Images:** Sensory representations occupy the metaphorical palace, but arts, numbers, affections, and God do not all exist there in the same imagistic fashion.

**Order:** Augustine never gives a numbered spatial route. His exposition is ordered rhetorically and philosophically—from sensible things through intellectual contents and affect, to happiness, truth, and God—but this is not presented as a reusable itinerary for memorizing other material.

**Retrieval:** Intentional search, candidate recognition, and an ascent through kinds of content.

### 3.5 God “in no place”

At X.25–26 Augustine asks where God was found. God is neither outside memory nor contained there like a picture in a chamber. The decisive Latin is:

> **et nusquam locus**

“and nowhere is there place.”

God is found “in” memory insofar as truth is known and loved, yet transcends spatial location and the created mind.

**[Correction of a mnemonic reading]** The climax does not perfect a method of loci. It exposes the inadequacy of spatial storage metaphors for God. Recent theological readings emphasize memory’s failure to mediate the Creator fully and the need for divine or Christological mediation. [Kevin Grove, *Augustine on Memory*](https://academic.oup.com/book/39176/chapter-abstract/338646991); [“Beyond Mnemotechnics”](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/236772883_Beyond_Mnemotechnics_Confession_and_Memory_in_Augustine).

### 3.6 *De Trinitate*

In *De Trinitate* IX–XI Augustine searches for images of the Trinity in the mind:

- mind, knowledge, love;
- memory, understanding, will.

The famous triad *memoria–intelligentia–voluntas* is not a classical method of loci. It is a psychological-theological analogy for the inseparable distinction of the divine persons.

**[Documented text]** Memory becomes an element of the soul’s trinitarian self-relation.

**[Later reception]** Lullian and scholastic systems appropriated the three powers; that does not make Augustine the inventor of Lull’s combinatorial Art.

### 3.7 The Carthage arch

In *De Trinitate* XI Augustine uses recollection of an arch at Carthage to distinguish:

- the absent external object;
- its image retained in memory;
- the present act of internal vision;
- the will directing attention.

It is a psychological example, not evidence that the arch was a station in Augustine’s mnemonic route.

### Manuscript condition and variants

The *Parva Naturalia* survives in the broader Byzantine Aristotelian manuscript tradition and in medieval Latin translation. Ross collated the principal witnesses available to him; Bloch reconsidered the stemma and argued for a renewed critical treatment.

**Materially important variant:** The letter-series at 452a16–25 is unstable. Its exact nodes and directional relations differ, which makes over-precise diagrams unsafe.

**Surviving diagrams:** None intrinsic to the work. Alphabetic sequences are verbal symbols in the text, not wheels or plates.

### Latin transmission

The treatise became available in Latin during the twelfth- and thirteenth-century recovery of Aristotle. James of Venice is associated with translation of much of the *Parva Naturalia*; later versions and the *translatio vetus/nova* tradition require witness-by-witness qualification. Arabic philosophical psychology—especially Avicenna’s faculty theory—mediated the interpretation received by Albert.

David Bloch’s study is the principal modern monograph specifically tracing the Latin scholastic reception. Its central finding, summarized by reviewers, is that newly translated Aristotle had to compete with Arabic theory, Augustinian usage, and an existing Latin vocabulary rather than entering an empty field. [Bloch review](https://bmcr.brynmawr.edu/2008/2008.08.19/).

## Yates’s account and the scholarship since

## 1. What Yates argued

### Aristotle

Yates reads *De anima* and *De memoria* as the psychology that made artificial memory intelligible:

1. all embodied thinking requires images;
2. perceptions stamp images like seals in wax;
3. recollection proceeds associatively and in order;
4. mnemonic places demonstrate how an ordered sequence can be searched.

Her historically stronger claim concerns reception: medieval schoolmen turned Aristotle’s treatise into “a kind of memory treatise,” combining it with the falsely Ciceronian *Ad Herennium*. [Yates’s formulation](https://resources.warburg.sas.ac.uk/mnemosyne/fq/yates_memory.pdf).

Her historically cautious qualification is equally important: she says it is “very doubtful” that Aristotle meant to provide philosophical grounding for artificial memory. He appears to use mnemonic practice as an illustration.

### Plato

Yates opposes a “trivial” sophist’s mnemonic to Platonic recollection. She argues that Renaissance Neoplatonists attempted to organize artificial memory according to archetypal realities. Camillo’s Theatre is her principal example.

This is a genealogy of later interpretation, not a claim that Plato designed Camillo’s Theatre.

### Augustine

Yates infers from the palatial language and the rhetoric of images, treasuries, and search that Augustine probably possessed a trained classical memory. She then speculates about a Christianized art in which Faith, Hope, Charity, virtues, vices, or liberal arts might occupy images and church loci.

Yates marks this as impression and speculation: “one gains, I think,” and “might.” The rhetoric of certainty sometimes attached to the claim by later popular accounts exceeds her wording.

## 2. What later work confirms

### Carruthers

Mary Carruthers, *The Book of Memory* (1990; 2nd ed. 2008), confirms the broad importance of Aristotelian image-psychology and trained mnemonic ordering for medieval intellectual life. She substantially enlarges the field beyond explicit memory manuals:

- medieval *memoria* is a compositional and ethical craft;
- images, layout, textual divisions, affect, and habit all support invention;
- memory is not merely verbatim storage;
- monastic meditation and rhetorical composition are as important as public oratory.

This supports Yates’s insistence that “mnemotechnics” is too narrow. It also weakens any simple genealogy in which an unchanged classical palace passes directly into medieval practice.

### Rossi, Bolzoni, and Small

Paolo Rossi’s *Clavis universalis* and English *Logic and the Art of Memory* confirm the importance of memory arts to classification, method, universal language, and combinatorics, while differentiating rhetorical loci from Lullian and logical systems.

Lina Bolzoni’s *The Gallery of Memory* relocates Renaissance memory within preaching, visual culture, literary invention, emblematic images, galleries, collections, and reading practices. For the present dossier her work supports a rich afterlife for Plato and Augustine but does not prove that either practised the *Ad Herennium* technique.

Jocelyn Penny Small’s *Wax Tablets of the Mind* challenges narratives that treat ancient literacy as simply destroying oral memory or artificial mnemonics as an all-purpose ancient system. Her cognitive-history approach stresses actual reading, writing, notes, performance, and material media. The wax tablet is both object and cognitive metaphor; one must not infer a fully spatial memory palace from every occurrence of imprint language. [Contemporary review information](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/pdf/10.1086/449516).

### Sorabji and Bloch

Sorabji and Bloch confirm that Aristotle provides the earliest sustained surviving philosophical analysis of remembering and recollecting. They refine or correct the simplified Yatesian picture:

- *phantasma* is not necessarily a consciously visual mnemonic emblem;
- recollection is a causally structured search;
- the strict dichotomy between memory and recollection is more complicated than “passive storage versus rational retrieval”;
- medieval interpretation was mediated by Latin semantics and Arabic faculty psychology;
- the 452a text is genuinely problematic.

Bloch’s work is especially corrective because it treats the scholastic reception as a collision of traditions, not a straightforward discovery that Aristotle “confirmed Cicero.”

### Augustine scholarship

Gerard O’Daly, Janet Coleman, James O’Donnell, Paige Hochschild, Kevin Grove, and other Augustine scholars place *Confessions* X within:

- ancient philosophical psychology;
- Plotinus and Neoplatonism;
- scriptural exegesis;
- Augustine’s theory of time;
- the self’s search for God;
- trinitarian anthropology;
- rhetoric and confession.

O’Donnell notes Augustine’s originality in claiming that memory contains intelligible contents and not merely images. His commentary explicitly raises Yates’s “ancient memory technique” but does not turn the palace passage into evidence of a formal itinerary. [O’Donnell’s commentary](https://faculty.georgetown.edu/jod/conf/comm10.html).

Hochschild treats Aristotle’s contribution as grounding understanding in sensation and marking temporal succession, while emphasizing Augustine’s distinct theological anthropology. [Hochschild chapter abstract](https://academic.oup.com/book/9155/chapter-abstract/155764629).

Grove argues that memory in *Confessions* X finally fails to mediate God adequately; this reverses the tendency to see the passage as celebrating an all-mastering mnemonic palace. [Grove](https://academic.oup.com/book/39176/chapter-abstract/338646991).

Johannes van Oort proposes specific parallels between Augustine’s palaces, sensory doors, chambers, and the Coptic Manichaean *Kephalaia*. This is a serious but not universally accepted reconstruction of an additional source environment. [Van Oort’s article and abstract](https://hts.org.za/index.php/hts/article/view/1922).

## 3. What is corrected or overturned

- **Overturned as a literal taxonomy:** Aristotle, Plato, and Augustine did not transmit one “system.”
- **Corrected:** “No thinking without images” does not mean all knowledge is stored as pictures.
- **Corrected:** Plato’s wax block is a provisional model that the *Theaetetus* tests and finds insufficient.
- **Corrected:** Platonic anamnesis should not automatically be treated as a mnemonic technique.
- **Corrected:** Augustine’s “palaces” do not establish a fixed method-of-loci route.
- **Corrected:** Augustine’s ascent culminates in the non-spatiality and transcendence of God.
- **Qualified:** Yates’s account of medieval conflation remains broadly persuasive, but later scholarship supplies a more complicated Arabic, monastic, exegetical, and material-textual background.
- **Unproven:** Augustine’s personal training in the classical artificial memory.
- **Unproven:** Specific Christian images located in specific churches before the scholastic evidence.
- **Unproven:** Direct descent from Plato’s wax tablet to Aristotle’s seal analogy. Plato is an immediate antecedent, but both participate in an older Greek imprint vocabulary; Aeschylus already uses inner-writing language.

Bonner and Fidora on Lull; Sturlese, Ciliberto, Clucas, and Gatti on Bruno; Bolzoni and Stagno on Camillo; Ong on Ramus; and Sabbatino on later Renaissance culture are essential for downstream systems. They do not provide primary evidence that Aristotle, Plato, or Augustine operated wheels, theatres, talismans, or Ramist tables.

**[Absence finding]** Importing conclusions about Lull, Bruno, Camillo, or Ramus backward into this item would confuse reception with origin. There are no Lullian wheels, Camillan grades, Brunian seals, or Ramist dichotomies in these philosophical texts.

## History and context

### Greek philosophical and rhetorical worlds

Plato and Aristotle worked in an environment where:

- poets preserved cultural memory;
- sophists advertised teachable intellectual powers;
- dialectic required command of arguments;
- public speaking rewarded reliable recollection;
- wax tablets provided an everyday model of inscription and erasure;
- Greek mnemonic practices using *topoi* already existed.

Plato distrusts possession of information without dialectical understanding. Aristotle naturalizes memory as an embodied capacity and recollection as structured search.

### Roman rhetoric

The complete surviving artificial-memory system appears not in these philosophers but in:

- anonymous *Rhetorica ad Herennium* III.16–24, ca. 80s BCE;
- Cicero, *De oratore* II.350–360;
- Quintilian, *Institutio oratoria* XI.2.

Here fixed places and vivid images are explicitly constructed. The lawsuit image—sick man, accused person, cup, tablets, ram’s testicles—belongs to *Ad Herennium*, not to Aristotle, Plato, or Augustine.

### Late antiquity and Augustine

Augustine was trained as a grammarian and rhetorician at Madauros, Carthage, Rome, and Milan. He knew Cicero deeply, especially the lost *Hortensius*; he knew Latin Platonist texts, probably in translations associated with Marius Victorinus; his direct knowledge of Aristotle was limited. Recent work stresses that any “Aristotelian” element in Augustine may arrive through an eclectic Latin and Neoplatonic tradition rather than direct study of *De memoria*. [Study of Augustine’s limited Aristotle](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/traditio/article/topics-of-aristotles-protrepticus-in-augustine-of-hippo-the-transmission-of-cicero-and-the-context-of-their-use/0D05E61AC931B23939DABCC0FBEBC296).

### Medieval institutions

The decisive synthesis occurred in the thirteenth-century schools:

- newly translated Aristotle entered cathedral schools and universities;
- Avicennian internal-sense psychology supplied imagination, estimative power, and memory;
- Dominican scholars Albert the Great and Thomas Aquinas treated artificial memory under Prudence;
- Franciscan and Augustinian traditions developed partly different accounts of cognition, illumination, and will;
- preaching, confession, disputation, and moral instruction rewarded ordered, emotionally forceful memory.

Thomas comments at Aristotle’s mention of places that Cicero teaches one to imagine an ordered set of loci and distribute *phantasmata* upon them. This is direct documentary evidence of conflation.

### Descent and descendants

```text
Greek poetic and rhetorical memory practices
        │
        ├── Plato: imprint, anamnesis, critique of writing
        │       └── Neoplatonism → Augustine → Ficino/Camillo/Renaissance Platonism
        │
        └── Aristotle: phantasm, temporal trace, associative search
                └── Arabic psychology + Latin Aristotle
                        └── Albert and Aquinas
                                └── scholastic ethical ars memoriae
                                        └── preaching, images, diagrams, treatises

Roman rhetorical loci and images ────────────────────────────────┘
```

The diagram is a historical synthesis, not a stemma of direct textual borrowing.

## The magic question

### Aristotle

Memory and recollection in *De memoria* are natural functions of an embodied soul.

- **[Documented]** No spirits, talismans, astral powers, divine names, or ritual operations are required.
- **[Context]** Aristotle mentions divination only while distinguishing future expectation from memory; he reports it as something “some say,” not as mnemonic doctrine.
- **Verdict:** Not magical.

### Plato

Anamnesis is metaphysical and religious in imagery: prenatal vision, soul, divine procession, and inspired love.

- **[Documented]** It is not a ritual mnemonic operation.
- **[Tradition]** Later Platonists read the myths as metaphysical truth.
- **[Renaissance interpretation]** Neoplatonic magicians could treat images as links to cosmic realities, but that is a later synthesis.
- **Verdict:** Sacred or metaphysical in setting, not an operative magical art in these passages.

### Augustine

Augustine’s memory is theological. God is sought through and beyond the mind.

- **[Documented]** No talisman, seal, magical diagram, angelic invocation, or ritual vision is prescribed.
- **[Documented]** Augustine elsewhere opposes divination, illicit magic, and demon-mediated arts.
- **[Disputed reconstruction]** Manichaean imagery may have influenced Book X’s vocabulary, but influence is not continued adherence.
- **Verdict:** Christian philosophical theology, not magic.

### Ars notoria and later accusations

The *Ars notoria* is a medieval ritual art promising knowledge or memory through prayers, angelic names, fasting, and inspection of figures.

- **[Historical relationship]** It shares the broad goal of acquiring or retaining knowledge.
- **[No direct descent demonstrated]** It is not derived in any simple textual line from the three philosophical accounts here.
- **[Category error]** Calling Augustine’s “palaces” an *Ars notoria* operation is unsupported.

No Inquisition case, heresy trial, or accusation of mnemonic sorcery follows from these particular doctrines. The accused-magician stories belong to later figures such as practitioners of occult or “Egyptian” memory.

## Afterlife

### Medieval memory

Aristotle supplied the language of *phantasmata*, bodily cognition, association, and starting points. Plato supplied recollection of higher realities. Augustine supplied a capacious interior, moral self-examination, and the triad of memory, understanding, and will.

In Albert and Thomas, mnemonic images become morally useful “corporeal similitudes” for spiritual intentions. Order and repeated meditation produce prudent action.

### Dante, preaching, and fresco

Yates and Carruthers connect medieval moral imagery, preaching divisions, Hell and Paradise, and Dante’s architecture with trained memorial culture.

- **[Strong contextual reconstruction]** Medieval works were created and received in a culture of trained memory.
- **[Disputed if literalized]** The *Divine Comedy* or a fresco cycle is not thereby proven to be a secretly encoded personal memory palace.
- **[No authorial declaration]** Dante does not present the *Comedy* as an *Ad Herennium* route.

### Emblem books, galleries, and theatres

Platonic ascent and Augustinian interiority provided conceptual authority for Renaissance arrangements of images. Camillo’s Theatre claimed to organize the universe by archetypal grades; Bruno combined mnemonic imagery with combinatorial and cosmological structures. Those are new systems, not merely repetitions of Plato.

Bolzoni’s work shows that literary texts, galleries, collections, emblematic images, and preaching mutually informed one another. The result is a culture of visualized knowledge rather than one uniform secret technique.

### Literature

- **Borges, “Funes the Memorious”:** an anti-Aristotelian or hyper-Aristotelian nightmare of retaining every percept without abstraction. The connection is thematic, not documented borrowing from *De memoria* alone.
- **Eco:** repeatedly stages libraries, encyclopedias, signs, combinatorics, and memory; his work self-consciously belongs to the learned afterlife of medieval and Renaissance semiotics.
- **Modern “memory palace” fiction:** usually derives from popularized classical loci, often mediated by Yates, not directly from Augustine.

### Computing and combinatorics

The line “Lull → Leibniz → computer” needs strict labels.

- **[Documented]** Lull developed finite alphabets and combinatorial figures for systematic inquiry.
- **[Documented]** Leibniz studied universal characteristic and combinatorial calculation.
- **[Historical reconstruction]** These projects form part of the prehistory of formal languages and mechanized reasoning.
- **[Disputed teleology]** Neither Lull’s wheels nor Leibniz’s characteristica is a digital computer.
- **[No direct role]** Aristotle’s memory chains, Plato’s anamnesis, and Augustine’s palaces are conceptual ancestors of ideas about representation and retrieval, not machine designs.

Modern information retrieval uses association, indexing, graph traversal, and addressable storage; similarities to loci are illuminating analogies but do not establish a technical lineage.

### Modern method-of-loci research and memory athletes

Unlike the metaphysical claims, the practical method of loci is experimentally testable.

A 2021 meta-analysis found substantial support for its effectiveness across predominantly university-based randomized studies. [Twomey and Kroneisen](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1747021821993457).

Neuroimaging studies associate loci training with hippocampal, parahippocampal, and retrosplenial systems involved in spatial navigation. Six weeks of training produced durable recall gains and connectivity patterns more similar to those of memory athletes. [Dresler et al., 2017](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0896627317300879); [Wagner et al., 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7929507/).

- **[Modern empirical finding]** Spatial mnemonic training works.
- **[Not confirmation of metaphysics]** This does not validate prenatal knowledge of Forms, an immaterial storehouse, or divine illumination.
- **[Historical caution]** Modern athletes generally practise the rhetorical loci-and-images technique, not “Augustine’s palace” as Augustine described it.

## Controversies and disputes

### 1. Is this a system at all?

**Best-supported answer:** It is a retrospectively assembled family of doctrines.

Yates’s narrative insight is that later arts required philosophical accounts of why images and order aid memory. The catalogue turns that genealogy into a “system.” This is useful analytically but historically anachronistic if taken literally.

### 2. Did Aristotle teach the method of loci?

- **For:** *De memoria* and *Topica* refer to mnemonic places.
- **Against:** Neither gives the technique’s rules.
- **Consensus-compatible judgment:** Aristotle knew of trained place-memory and used it illustratively; the surviving texts are philosophical psychology, not a mnemonic manual.

### 3. Are Aristotle’s three laws of association exact doctrines?

The relations of similarity, contrariety, and nearness/order are textual. Calling them three capitalized “Laws of Association” is a later historiographical abstraction disseminated through associationist psychology and textbooks.

### 4. Does *phantasma* mean a mental picture?

Sometimes it is pictorial; not always. Translating it uniformly as “image” risks importing modern picture-in-the-head theory. Sorabji’s later work foregrounds this dispute.

### 5. Is Plato’s recollection literal?

Possible readings include:

- literal prenatal acquaintance with Forms;
- a mythic defense of immortality;
- an account of a priori concepts;
- recovery of latent rational structure;
- pedagogical elicitation.

The dialogues do not settle the modern dispute. Augustine himself moved away from straightforward preexistence, developing illumination instead. The Stanford Encyclopedia notes the difficulty of determining whether early Augustine ever held literal recollection. [SEP, Augustine](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/augustine/).

### 6. Does Plato condemn all writing?

No. The dialogue criticizes writing when treated as self-sufficient wisdom. It permits written reminders for one who already knows and values living dialectic. “Plato predicted that smartphones destroy memory” is modern rhetoric.

### 7. Was Augustine classically trained in artificial memory?

- **Evidence for:** professional rhetorical education; familiarity with Cicero; architectural and treasury language; ordered rhetorical exposition; command of scripture.
- **Evidence against:** no statement that he constructed loci; no prescribed route; no image-encoding example; no explicit citation of *Ad Herennium*’s mnemonic section; God finally eludes spatial placement.
- **Yates’s status:** suggestive inference, explicitly tentative.
- **Later correction:** memory should be read primarily within confession, theology, scriptural search, time, selfhood, and Neoplatonic ascent.
- **Verdict:** Possible, not demonstrated.

### 8. Are Augustine’s palaces literally antique buildings?

No building is identified. The Carthage arch is a separate example about remembered perception. “Augustine walked through actual Roman buildings inside his head” is an unsupported literalization.

### 9. Is Book X Manichaean?

Van Oort finds parallels with Manichaean sensory doors, chambers, storehouse personnel, memory, and beauty.

- **Evidence:** Augustine’s long Manichaean formation and lexical/conceptual parallels with the *Kephalaia*.
- **Difficulty:** architectural and storage metaphors were widespread; parallel does not prove direct borrowing.
- **Verdict:** A significant scholarly proposal, not settled consensus.

### 10. Did Augustine fuse Plato and Aristotle directly?

Augustine knew Platonist philosophy much more substantially than Aristotle. Similarity to Aristotelian psychology may reflect shared ancient vocabulary, Cicero, doxography, Plotinus, Porphyry, or other mediation.

### 11. Are there diagrams or hidden plates?

No. All diagrams attached to this item are modern explanatory reconstructions or later scholastic/Renaissance adaptations.

### 12. Magic and heresy

No primary evidence makes these doctrines talismanic. Renaissance magical memory may claim Platonic or Hermetic authorization, but retroactively calling Platonic recollection or Augustine’s memory palace “occult technology” is a modern conflation.

### 13. Legends generated by popular memory culture

Common modern inventions include:

- Plato invented the memory palace;
- Aristotle codified its three rules;
- Augustine stored sermons in an internal cathedral;
- medieval monks universally used identical architectural palaces;
- the method was suppressed as witchcraft;
- Lull invented the computer;
- neuroscience has proved Augustine’s metaphysics.

Each statement either lacks evidence or compresses several distinct traditions into a marketable origin story.

## Open questions

1. What precise Greek mnemonic practice lies behind Aristotle’s plural “some” who use places?
2. Is 452a’s milk-to-autumn sequence recoverable by cosmological association, or is the transmitted text corrupt?
3. Can a better stemma settle the alphabetic sequence at 452a16–25?
4. What was the lost Aristotelian work listed by Diogenes Laertius?
5. How literally should Plato’s prenatal vision and recollection be read?
6. How much early Academic mnemonic theory lies behind both Plato’s wax and Aristotle’s imprint?
7. Which Latin rhetorical mnemonic texts did Augustine actually read?
8. Does Augustine’s architectural language reflect formal mnemonic education, common rhetorical metaphor, scripture, Neoplatonism, Manichaean discourse, or some combination?
9. Can verbal or structural parallels with the *Kephalaia* be shown to be distinctive rather than part of a broader ancient storehouse metaphor?
10. At what point did commentators first explicitly identify Augustine’s *praetoria memoriae* with the technical method of loci?
11. How far did medieval practitioners distinguish contextual recollection—remembering where something happened—from fabricated artificial loci?
12. Which modern popular accounts first converted Yates’s tentative inference into the claim that Augustine certainly used a memory palace?
13. How should modern cognitive findings about spatial navigation be related to ancient philosophical descriptions without turning analogy into retrospective neuroscience?
