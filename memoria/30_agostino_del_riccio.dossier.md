# Agostino del Riccio's *Arte della memoria locale*: Research Dossier

## Basic identification

**Name:** *Arte della memoria locale*  
**Exponent:** Fra Agostino del Riccio, O.P.  
**Date:** 1595  
**Source text:** Florence, Biblioteca Nazionale Centrale di Firenze, MS Nazionale II.I.13, formerly Magliabechiano Classe V, 9  
**Principle in one sentence:** Stable, familiar places—churches, streets, rooms, objects, or the ordered parts of a human body—are traversed in a fixed direction and populated with personally effective images, so that mentally retracing the route retrieves the deposited material in sequence.  
**Yates:** Frances A. Yates, *The Art of Memory* (London, 1966), ch. XI, pp. 244–246; recalled at pp. 313–14; plates 13a–f.

### Evidentiary labels used

- **[TEXT]** directly stated or depicted in Riccio’s manuscript.
- **[DOCUMENT]** established by archival or bibliographical evidence independent of the treatise.
- **[RECONSTRUCTION]** a modern scholar’s contextual or functional inference.
- **[YATES]** Yates’s interpretation.
- **[TRADITION]** reported by Riccio or another historical witness without independent confirmation.
- **[DISPUTED]** contested, internally inconsistent, or inadequately demonstrated.
- **[LEGEND]** anecdote or reputation transmitted without firm documentation.
- **[MODERN ANALOGY]** a later comparison, not a historical line of influence.
- **[ABSENCE]** something sought but not found in the surviving evidence.

The most important correction to the catalogue entry is numerical. **[TEXT]** The modern transcription identifies an introductory royal image followed by seven illustrated rules, with headings extending from a “first illustration” to an “eighth illustration.” **[YATES]** Yates says that the manuscript has “seven drawings.” Unless the manuscript’s physical organization makes one heading non-pictorial, the accessible transcription points to **eight illustrated units**, not seven. Likewise, Riccio’s Via della Scala demonstration contains **27 named deposits**, and he says that his own full route contains more than eighty places; Yates’s plate 13e evidently reproduces only twelve symbols.

## The system in detail

### 1. Purpose and audience

**[TEXT]** The treatise is dedicated at f. 1r:

> «Alla gioventù fiorentina studiosa di Lettere.»

“To the Florentine youth devoted to letters.”

At f. 5r Riccio widens the audience to preachers, orators, students, and effectively everyone, young and old. The manuscript repeatedly addresses the reader as *benigno lettore*, *industrioso giovanetto*, and *predicator mio amantissimo*. It is therefore both a beginner’s manual and a vocational tool for preaching, study, confession, conversation, and civic display.

The aims include:

- retaining sermons, speeches, concepts, sententiae, histories, examples, and rhetorical ornaments;
- memorizing councils, canon law, patristic teachings, excommunications, and difficult cases;
- retaining lectures and arguments for academic *circoli*;
- learning grammar and Donatus;
- remembering military stratagems;
- remembering names immediately after hearing them;
- ordering natural history—rivers, fishes, plants, flowers, stones, animals, birds;
- producing learned conversation at banquets;
- displaying the dignities and learned citizens of Florence.

**[TEXT]** Riccio’s epistemic maxim is striking:

> «quel che sappiamo a mente ci fa honore e veramente si può dir di sapere» (f. 13v).

“What we know by heart brings us honour and can truly be called knowledge.”

That sentence explains why his memory art connects preaching, encyclopedism, collecting, sociability, and civic prestige.

### 2. The governing allegory: king, counsellors, captains, servant

**[TEXT]** Riccio personifies the art as a court or household. Local memory is a rich king; the rules subordinated to it appear as counsellors, captains, and a servant. Each rule is prefaced by a symbolic picture and title.

**[RECONSTRUCTION]** This is more than decoration. The illustrations themselves create a miniature memory system: personified offices distinguish functions, while their attributes—globe, book, objects, geometrical lines, food—compress the associated instructions.

#### The King

**[TEXT; f. 5r]** Memory is represented as:

> «un re richissimo che con una mano si tocca la memoria».

“A very rich king who with one hand touches his memory.”

**[YATES]** Plate 13a shows him striking or touching his brow. Yates interprets the gesture as calling up local memory for preachers, orators, students, and every class of person.

The king’s wealth anticipates Riccio’s later metaphor of the royal palace whose categorized rooms contain antiquities, tapestries, silver, jewels, arms, and provisions.

### 3. First rule: natural and artificial memory

**[TEXT]** The first rule distinguishes *memoria naturale*, an innate faculty, from *memoria artificiale* or *locale*, perfected through practice. Riccio’s practical premise is summarized by the pseudo-Ciceronian maxim:

> «l’arte fa perfetta la na(tural) cosa».

“Art perfects the natural thing.”

Riccio or the modern transcriber associates this with *Rhetorica ad Herennium* III.16. The wording is not a literal standard Latin quotation; it is a vernacular maxim expressing the classical distinction.

**Retrieval principle:** natural aptitude supplies the capacity; repeated ordered emplacement trains and enlarges it.

### 4. Second rule: places—the First Counsellor

**[TEXT; ff. 6–14v]** The heading is:

> «Seconda regola nominata “luoghi et consiglier primo”».

The illustration shows a counsellor resting a hand on a world map containing cities, towns, castles, houses, shops, churches, palaces, streets, squares, and convents.

#### Rules for places

Riccio enumerates seven conditions:

> «In prima, i luoghi debbono esser luminosi; secondario, fermi; terzo, habbino distanza ragionevole; quarto, che detti luoghi seguitino ordinatamente l’uno doppo l’altro; quinto, si tien’ ordine a locare a mano ritta o mancina …; sesto, ci sono i luoghi comuni che non ritengono sempre le cose …; settimo, ci sono i luoghi ch’insegnano ritenere a mente tutto quel che s’è inparato».

In concise form:

1. Places must be sufficiently illuminated.
2. They must be stable.
3. They should be reasonably spaced.
4. They must follow one another in an intelligible order.
5. One must preserve a directional convention—rightward or leftward.
6. Some places are temporary and can be reused.
7. Other places support durable retention and should not be cleared.

Useful architectural markers include:

> «porte, finestre, statue, pile, cantonate, altari, sepolcri, tabernacoli, acquai, cammini, scale, banchi, armadi, pitture dipinte ne’ muri».

Doors, windows, statues, basins, corners, altars, tombs, tabernacles, sinks or water places, fireplaces, stairs, benches, cupboards, and wall paintings.

#### Distance

**[TEXT]** In churches, squares, and streets, Riccio recommends approximately six or eight braccia between loci; he also reports a prescription of eight or ten. In small, intimately familiar rooms, shops, or on the body, loci can be less than a braccio apart.

This is an empirical qualification of the textbook rule: effective familiarity matters more than an invariant measurement.

#### Direction and circuit

Start at a conspicuous entrance; proceed consistently to the right or left; continue around the structure until returning to the starting point. Riccio refuses to dogmatize about right versus left, comparing them to the mutually assisting “sisters” of painting and sculpture.

#### Worked example: the Beatitudes

**[TEXT; ff. 7–8]** Riccio maps the eight Beatitudes onto an ordered domestic or architectural route. For “Blessed are those who hunger and thirst after justice,” he places four perpetually hungry friends at a door:

> «Beati qui esuriunt et sitiunt iustiziam … metti a l’uscio che va in camera quattro tuoi amici, che sempre habbin fame».

For the persecuted he places five martyrs at the next corner. The number of figures helps mark ordinal position as well as content.

#### Worked example: apostolic predicates

**[TEXT; ff. 8–9]** Riccio deposits a sequence of objects in a compact room:

- a burning lamp for the apostles as the light of the world;
- a stone column for “columns”;
- salt on a stool for “salt”;
- three stones for “precious stones”;
- a judge on a stone for “judges”;
- bones at a corner for “bones of Christ”;
- four doves at a window;
- a holy-water bucket for “fishermen,” because fishing occurs in water;
- painted clouds;
- two imaginary oxen in a cupboard;
- four lions on a bench;
- two paper stars on a nail for the twelve stars of the Apocalyptic Woman’s crown.

The examples mix resemblance, pun, causal association, scriptural iconography, number, and personal familiarity.

#### Santa Maria Novella and the theological virtues

**[TEXT, as quoted by Yates from f. 6]** Beginning at the high altar, Riccio places Charity; proceeding around the church, Hope goes on the Ciodi altar and Faith on the Gaddi altar; the circuit continues through chapel altars, holy-water stoup, tombs, and other landmarks until it returns to its beginning.

**[YATES]** This is “the good old fashioned way of using the art, to remember virtues.”

**[QUALIFICATION]** The example certainly is devotional and Dominican. But Riccio’s surviving text also emphasizes encyclopedic storage, natural history, collecting, professional utility, and social performance. “Good old fashioned” captures one use but not the whole system.

#### Temporary versus permanent repositories

For daily preaching Riccio recommends seven independent place systems, one for every day:

- Sunday: Santa Maria Novella;
- Monday: San Marco;
- Tuesday: Santa Croce;
- Wednesday: Santo Spirito;
- Thursday: the Carmine;
- Friday: Santa Trinita;
- Saturday: Santissima Annunziata.

After seven days one returns to Santa Maria Novella, by which time the transient images should have faded.

Some practitioners allegedly use one or two systems and imagine an agile servant sweeping out yesterday’s figures. Riccio prefers seven separate systems because a week’s delay reduces interference.

For durable knowledge, he says not to remove the material but to revisit it frequently.

#### The royal palace as classified storehouse

**[TEXT; f. 13v]**

> «fa’ come fanno i re potentissimi e ricchissimi che hanno ne’ lor gran palazzi molte stanze: in una sarà l’anticagle, in un’altra i panni d’arazzo, in un’altra la loro argenteria, in un’altra le gioie; in altre stanze l’armi da guerra, sì offensive come defensive; in altre le vettovaglie …».

“Do as the most powerful and wealthy kings do, who have many rooms in their great palaces: in one will be antiquities, in another tapestries, in another their silver, in another jewels; in other rooms weapons of war, offensive and defensive; in others provisions….”

The user who wishes to be universal should similarly maintain multiple rooms for sermons, discourses, concepts, sayings, histories, and whatever discipline he professes.

**[BOLZONI’S RECONSTRUCTION]** Lina Bolzoni reads this as a meeting between the classical *thesaurus eloquentiae* and late-Renaissance collecting. The mental room and the collection-room organize abundance by type. Unlike Camillo’s metaphysical theatre or Citolini’s elaborately topical storehouse, Riccio’s categories remain comparatively practical. [Bolzoni, *The Gallery of Memory*, pp. 251–54](https://pdfcoffee.com/the-gallery-of-memory-literary-and-iconographic-models-in-the-age-of-the-printing-press-bolzoni-lina-pdf-free.html).

### 5. Third rule: images—the Second Counsellor

**[TEXT; ff. 15–32]** The heading is:

> «Terza regola nominata “figure et consiglier secondo”».

The pictured man holds a book and is surrounded by heterogeneous objects: the transcription’s description includes a statue on a pedestal, a marble bust, and a lectern. Yates’s plate 13c reproduces this unit.

#### Kinds and rules of images

Riccio distinguishes:

- **real figures:** lion, ox, falcon, and similar things;
- **imaginary or fictive figures:** Hydra, or a goose with three necks and three heads;
- **artworks:** figures invented or executed by painters and sculptors;
- **actual objects or statues:** especially memorable things already seen.

He says:

> «le figure imaginarie muovano manco, però noi ci serviamo delle figure reali e materiali».

“Imaginary figures move [the mind] less; therefore we use real and material figures.”

This is an important divergence from the most sensational reading of the *Ad Herennium*. Riccio accepts monsters but prefers objects with perceptual and personal force. He explicitly relativizes selection:

> «a mme mi muoverà una figura et a te un’altra».

“One image will move me and another you.”

#### Niccolò Gaddi’s gallery

Riccio describes an unusual double-bodied or double-headed marble figure, viewable from two sides, in the gallery left by Niccolò Gaddi. The collection supplies ready-made memorable images.

**[DOCUMENT]** Niccolò Gaddi (1537–1591) was a Florentine collector and patron connected with Santa Maria Novella.

**[RECONSTRUCTION]** For Benzi and Bolzoni, this is evidence that Riccio’s art belongs not only to sermon mnemonics but to the culture of galleries, natural specimens, precious stones, and organized collections.

#### Wordplay: Florentine family names

**[TEXT]**

- *Pescioni*: put three fish (*pesci*) in a corner.
- *Ricci*: put two hedgehogs (*ricci*) in a corner.
- *Asini*: tie two asses (*asini*) to a door.

These are phonetic or rebus images, not semantic representations of the families.

#### Alphabetical image dictionaries

Riccio provides alphabetic lists for rivers and lakes, fishes, precious and other stones, herbs, flowers, trees and fruits, land animals, birds, cities, Florentine families, mechanical and liberal arts, occupations, honoured men, illustrious women, male saints, and female saints.

For A in the river alphabet:

> «Arno, Anbra, Asapo, Arco».

The reader is expected to augment the lists. Riccio proposes additional alphabets for poisonous animals, legumes and seeds, fruits, textile colours, numbers, armour and weapons, philosophers, poets, and authors in every discipline.

**[TEXTUAL FEATURE]** The manuscript leaves gaps in these lists for supplementation.

**[RECONSTRUCTION]** They are open-ended working instruments, not finished universal dictionaries.

### 6. Fourth rule: the First Captain or Straight Line

**[TEXT; cited by Yates at f. 33]** The first captain places an ordered vertical sequence on a human body. The body itself supplies intrinsically ordered loci: hair, forehead, eyebrows, eyes, mouth, chin, throat, breasts, torso, belt, knee, leg, foot, shoe.

Riccio also places the twelve zodiacal signs upon the bodily regions they were conventionally believed to govern.

**Retrieval:** scan the body vertically in the memorized order; the anatomical sequence cues the zodiacal or other deposited sequence.

**[YATES]** The figure shows a man crossed by a vertical line, with zodiac signs placed according to astrological melothesia.

**[QUALIFICATION]** Neither Yates nor the accessible transcription shows Riccio claiming that zodiacal powers cause recollection. The zodiac supplies a familiar sequence and a map of the body.

**[ABSENCE]** No wheel is involved in this rule.

### 7. Fifth rule: the Second Captain or Circular Line

**[TEXT; f. 35]** The second captain is a man with arms and legs extended within a circle. The user distributes the four elements and cosmic spheres around bodily loci so that a mental circuit retrieves the cosmological order.

Yates transcribes the sequence as:

- earth—feet;
- water—knee;
- air—flank;
- fire—arm;
- Moon—right hand;
- Mercury—right forearm;
- Venus—right shoulder;
- Sun—head;
- Mars—left shoulder;
- Jupiter—left forearm;
- Saturn—left hand;
- sphere of fixed stars—“left shoulder”;
- crystalline sphere—waist;
- *primum mobile*—knees;
- Paradise—under the left foot.

**[DISPUTED/TRANSCRIPTION PROBLEM]** Yates’s printed list assigns both Mars and the fixed stars to the left shoulder, while other positions suggest a continuous descending circuit. This may be Riccio’s repetition, Yates’s error, or a modern OCR problem. It requires direct inspection of f. 35 and cannot responsibly be silently corrected.

**[YATES]** This is “puerile, but not magical”: a rational use of a traditional cosmological sequence.

**[RECONSTRUCTION]** Its operative resemblance to a wheel is limited. The enclosing circle and bodily circuit order the sequence, but there is no evidence of independently rotating concentric disks or combinatorial permutations of the Lullian/Brunian kind.

### 8. Sixth rule: Third Captain, Transverse Line

**[TEXT; ff. 39–45]** Heading:

> «Sesta regola da me detta “linea traversale e(t) nominativo terzo”».

Riccio calls this “the best of all and the most used.” *Transverse* means that it can pass through sites of any shape—streets, squares, churches, quadrangular, hexagonal, octagonal, or irregular spaces.

#### Dominican cell: Job 14:1–2

The six loci are:

1. Door: Riccio’s mother who bore him — *homo natus de muliere*.
2. First corner: an old man covered with *brevi* (briefs) — *brevi vivens tempore*.
3. Small altar: a sick man covered in sores — *repletur multis miseriis*.
4. Second corner: Riccio’s sister adorned with quickly fading flowers — *quasi flos egreditur et conteritur*.
5. Window: a friend holding an umbrella for shade — *fugit velut umbra*.
6. Next corner: a courtier falling from his lord’s favour and losing favour and life — *nunquam in eodem statu permanet*.

Riccio then recites the text without naming loci or images, and afterward in reverse order. This is the clearest surviving account of retrieval:

1. encode phrase by phrase;
2. attach each phrase to an image;
3. place images on a known ordered route;
4. traverse once forward;
5. traverse backward;
6. eventually recite the material directly, while the places remain the recovery structure.

**Correction to Yates:** Job gives six clauses or “miseries” here, not seven. Yates calls them “the seven miseries of man.”

#### Via della Scala: 27 objects

The route begins near Santa Maria Novella and proceeds to the right. Among the first loci:

1. street-corner tabernacle: a religious with a cross — cross;
2. Casavecchi house-door: a large red star — star;
3. Jacopo dal Borgho’s door: a golden sun — sun;
4. blocked convent door with Marian image and Acciaiuoli arms: an Acciaiuoli duke holding a golden flower-vase — vase;
5. church of the *donne mal maritate*: a porphyry door — door;
6. former Bartolini garden entrance: an imaginary silver mountain — mountain;
7. Ripoli convent church door: a clock;
8. convent entrance: an official reading with spectacles;
9. garden corner: an astrologer with compasses;
10. wall corner: an armed man with a mace;
11. facing wall: a shepherd with shears;
12. Acciaiuoli garden entrance: St Barbara with a tower;
13. simples-garden entrance: a hammer;
14. first house: a large bell;
15. blind alley: a drummer;
16. weavers’ house: a standard-bearer;
17. Rucellai garden: a man reading a book;
18. Via Polverosa corner: a trumpeter;
19. San Martino convent corner: a widow;
20. small door: a confessor;
21. parlour door: a doctor;
22. factor’s door: a gardener;
23. Via dell’Albero: a Turk;
24. Via de’ Canacci: a Corsican dog, *can corso*;
25. Via Benedetta: St Benedict in a porphyry niche;
26. Via Porcella: St Paul on a column;
27. hospital loggia/church: Saints Dominic and Francis.

Riccio recites the resulting list forward and backward. The transcription preserves several slips in numbering and repetition—valuable evidence that this is a practical manuscript rather than a typographically normalized textbook.

> «Io ho locati questi ventisette nomi in via della Scala per via d’esempio ma io, quando vi loco … piglio più di ottanta luoghi.»

“I have located these twenty-seven names in Via della Scala by way of example, but when I locate there I take more than eighty places.”

**Correction:** Plate 13e shows twelve small symbols, but the accompanying written demonstration is not a twelve-item system.

#### Piazza del Granduca: evergreen plants

Riccio creates a 32-place botanical circuit around what is now Piazza della Signoria, using Santo Romolo, shops, corners, the Loggia, its columns and statues, Perseus, the palace entrance, David, the lion, the fountain, and government offices.

Plants deposited include palm, dwarf palm, orange, myrtle, cedar, laurel, lemon, Adam’s apple, cypress, strawberry tree, juniper, pine, savin, fir, holm oak, box, olive, bitter orange, viburnum, yew or *nasso*, mastic, oleander, royal laurel, cork oak, holly, teucrium, wild olive, broom, rosemary, ivy, and myrtle.

Again Riccio recites forward and backward.

This example is especially important because it ties the memory art directly to Riccio’s botanical and horticultural writing.

### 9. Seventh rule: Food and Servant

**[TEXT; ff. 45–48]** Heading:

> «Settima regola da me nominata “cibo et servitore”».

The associated image shows a man with food and drink.

Riccio compares memorization to distributing food across meals:

> «sicome si divide il mangiar che facciamo il giorno più volte, così e non altrimenti si viene ad inparare per memoria locale».

“As we divide the eating we do several times during the day, so and in no other way should one learn through local memory.”

Attempting two hundred concepts or two hundred articles of Thomas immediately after rising would exhaust memory, just as consuming a whole day’s food at breakfast would overload the stomach. The student should number the material in a notebook and learn ten items at a time—on rising, an hour later, before dinner, after dinner, and through the day.

This rule adds **temporal spacing** to spatial order.

#### Promised feats

Riccio claims that a disciplined user can acquire one or two hundred concepts a day; learn lists of popes and emperors; discuss rivers, fishes, precious stones, plants, flowers, and Florentine worthies; and learn the plants in Dioscorides rapidly.

At ff. 47r–48r he reports:

> «questo è intervenuto a un dei miei carissimi compagni che ha questa memoria locale che in un’ora ha imparato a mente un libro».

“This happened to one of my dearest companions, who has this local memory and in one hour learned a book by heart.”

He also describes a small boy taught by him who astonished learned men, retained many memorized things, and could recite material he could scarcely read.

These are **[TEXT: autobiographical claims]**, not independently documented demonstrations.

## The sources

### 1. The primary manuscript

**[DOCUMENT]**

Agostino del Riccio, *Arte della memoria locale*, Florence, Biblioteca Nazionale Centrale di Firenze:

- current shelfmark: **MS Nazionale II.I.13**;
- former shelfmark: **Magliabechiano Classe V, 9**;
- language: Tuscan Italian;
- date stated and consistently reported: **1595**;
- unpublished in Riccio’s lifetime;
- no early printed edition has been identified.

The principal cited folios are:

- f. 1r: dedication;
- f. 5r: king and audience;
- f. 6: First Counsellor/Santa Maria Novella;
- ff. 7–14v: place rules and applications;
- f. 15: image-rule illustration;
- f. 16r: real and imaginary images/Gaddi;
- f. 33: Straight Line;
- f. 35: Circular Line;
- f. 39: Transverse Line illustration;
- ff. 40–45: cell, Via della Scala, and Piazza examples;
- f. 45: Food and Servant illustration;
- ff. 46–48: spaced practice and testimonials.

### 2. Online transcription

Utzima Benzi prepared a normalized diplomatic transcription hosted by Emilio Panella. Its principles include preserving many original spellings and semi-Latinisms, expanding abbreviations, marking supplied text, inserting modern paragraphs for intelligibility, and identifying folio transitions.

This is the best accessible primary-text surrogate found in the research, but it is **not a facsimile and not a full critical edition**. Editorial paragraphing, punctuation, capitalization, and some identifications are modern.

### 3. Surviving illustrations

The transcription’s headings imply:

1. introductory image of the King;
2. First Rule;
3. First Counsellor/Places;
4. Second Counsellor/Images;
5. First Captain/Straight Line;
6. Second Captain/Circular Line;
7. Third Captain/Transverse Line;
8. Food and Servant.

Yates’s plate 13 reproduces six photographs:

- 13a: King;
- 13b: First Counsellor;
- 13c: Second Counsellor;
- 13d: Second Captain/Circular Line;
- 13e: Third Captain/Transverse Line;
- 13f: Food and Servant.

**[ABSENCE]** Yates does not reproduce the First Captain/Straight Line. Her plate caption groups 13a–f generally as “Pictures Illustrating the Principles of the Art of Memory.”

**Correction of catalogue note:** “Third Captain = plate 13c” is demonstrably a catalogue transcription error. It is **plate 13e**. Plate 13c is the Second Counsellor.

### 4. Other Riccio manuscripts relevant to reconstruction

The memory treatise should be read alongside Riccio’s manuscript encyclopedic-naturalistic production:

- *Agricoltura sperimentale*, BNCF, Targioni-Tozzetti 56, vols. I–III;
- *Agricoltura teorica*, in the same complex;
- *Istoria delle pietre*, Biblioteca Riccardiana, Ricc. 230;
- *Libro di Pietre et Agricoltura*, Modena, Biblioteca Estense, Campori 624γ B.2.9;
- *Agricoltura sperimentale*, Biblioteca Estense, It. 400, H.3–5;
- another agricultural manuscript, Florence, Careggi medical library, R 210.2;
- *Trattato di agricoltura*, BNCF Nuovi Acquisti 1072.

These are not textual witnesses of the *Arte della memoria locale*. They establish the broader taxonomic environment in which Riccio’s alphabets and botanical memory routes operated.

### 5. Manuscript and edition variants

**[ABSENCE]** No second manuscript witness, authorial redaction, sixteenth-century printing, or early translation of the *Arte della memoria locale* was located.

Accordingly:

- there is no established stemma;
- there are no print-state variants;
- variation currently means differences among the manuscript, Benzi’s normalized transcription, Yates’s English paraphrase, and later summaries;
- several numerical problems—seven/eight pictures, six/seven miseries, twelve/27/80 Via della Scala items—arise at this level of mediation.

## Yates’s account and scholarship since

### Yates, chapter XI, pp. 244–246

Yates introduces Riccio while trying to explain Bruno’s *Thirty Seals*. Her sequence is:

1. identify Riccio as a Dominican of Santa Maria Novella;
2. call his work an unpublished 1595 manual for “studious young gentlemen”;
3. describe the King and two Counsellors;
4. summarize the bodily Straight and Circular Lines;
5. summarize Via della Scala, the Dominican cell, and Food and Servant;
6. declare the system nonmagical and late, weak, traditional;
7. use its personified diagrams as the explanatory analogue for Bruno’s seals.

Her core comparative claim is:

> “his idea of presenting the principles and various techniques of the art through little symbolic pictures, with titles, exactly corresponds to what Bruno does in *Seals*.”

She concludes that Bruno had “magicised” a Dominican mode of presentation with Lullism and Cabala.

### What remains confirmed

- Riccio was a Dominican associated with Santa Maria Novella.
- The text is dated 1595.
- Places and images follow the classical local-memory framework.
- Churches and devotional matter are prominent.
- Zodiac and cosmic orders function as memory sequences.
- The symbolic court figures summarize rules.
- The manuscript is a useful formal comparison with Bruno’s named seals.
- Nothing in Riccio’s text demonstrates talismanic or operative astral magic.

### What requires correction

1. **“Seven drawings.”** The transcription numbers eight illustrated headings. Direct codicological inspection is needed, but Yates’s count cannot simply be repeated.

2. **Via della Scala.** Yates’s plate has twelve objects; the text gives 27 and says Riccio ordinarily uses over 80 loci.

3. **Job’s “seven miseries.”** The manuscript enumerates six clauses from Job 14:1–2.

4. **“This friar has not heard of exciting Renaissance transformations.”** This is rhetoric, not evidence. Riccio knew gallery culture, emblematic presentation, cosmological diagrams, expanding image alphabets, encyclopedic natural history, and possibly wrote a lost book of *imprese*. His system is conservative in mechanism but embedded in late-Renaissance collecting and encyclopedism.

5. **“Enfeeblement.”** Later scholarship has largely replaced this decline narrative with functional and cultural analysis. Riccio’s art is not metaphysically ambitious like Camillo’s or Bruno’s, but it is pedagogically detailed, urban, socially situated, and linked to collecting and natural history.

6. **Influence on Bruno.** Riccio wrote in 1595; Bruno’s *Triginta sigilli* appeared in 1583. Riccio therefore cannot have supplied Bruno’s form. Yates means that both reflect a Dominican pedagogical tradition, but she offers no direct textual chain proving that Bruno learned precisely this convention in his convent.

### Rossi

Paolo Rossi treated Riccio within the history of artificial memory, combinatorics, and universal method. His broad framework helps distinguish ordinary local memory from Lullian combinatorial arts. Riccio has ordered repositories and finite lists; he does not generate propositions by rotating symbolic alphabets.

### Bolzoni

Lina Bolzoni’s major correction is contextual. In *La stanza della memoria* and *The Gallery of Memory*, she treats Riccio’s royal rooms as evidence for the convergence of memory, collecting, rhetorical storage, and classification. She rejects the implication that Riccio is simply alien to encyclopedic aspiration: his universalism is practical and taxonomic rather than Camillian or Brunian.

### Benzi

Utzima Benzi’s 2007 study and online transcription provide the fullest item-specific treatment found. Benzi:

- documents Riccio’s life and Dominican records;
- reconstructs the seven-rule organization;
- emphasizes the images’ emblematic function;
- connects the treatise with his natural-history manuscripts;
- identifies the practical, pedagogical, and Florentine-civic purposes;
- treats forward and backward repetition as central to secure retrieval.

### Kuwakino and Brunon

Koji Kuwakino and Hervé Brunon situate Riccio’s garden and agricultural projects within mnemonic architecture and the cataloguing of the world. The garden, city, theatre, and collection can all function as ordered spatial schemas. This does not prove that Riccio’s ideal garden was literally a fully encoded memory palace, but it shows continuity between his mnemonic and natural-historical methods.

### Carruthers, Small, Hajdu, Ong, Bonner, Fidora, Sturlese, Ciliberto, Clucas, Gatti, Stagno

These scholars provide necessary comparative frameworks but, in the materials located, do not substantially revise the codicology or detailed operation of Riccio’s manuscript.

- Mary Carruthers establishes medieval memory as compositional, ethical, and inventive rather than rote storage.
- Jocelyn Penny Small stresses the material and cognitive interaction of writing, visualization, and recollection.
- Helga Hajdu’s earlier history remains important for classical and medieval techniques.
- Walter Ong explains Ramist spatial order as a reorganization of discourse that normally suppresses the emotionally charged mnemonic image.
- Anthony Bonner and Alexander Fidora distinguish genuine Lullian combinatorial method from Renaissance pseudo-Lullism.
- Rita Sturlese, Michele Ciliberto, Stephen Clucas, and Hilary Gatti complicate strongly “Hermetic” readings of Bruno and insist on textual, logical, philosophical, and historical specificity.
- Lina Bolzoni and Lucia Stagno’s work on Camillo and visual culture helps prevent treating every theatre, diagram, or emblem as the same mnemonic technology.

**[ABSENCE]** No post-1966 scholar located attributes Riccio’s treatise to another author, argues seriously for a date other than 1595, identifies a lost printed edition, or produces evidence that Riccio was prosecuted for this memory art.

## History and context

### Classical rhetoric

Riccio’s immediate technical ancestry is the classical art of places and images known through:

- pseudo-Cicero, *Rhetorica ad Herennium* III.16–24;
- Cicero, *De oratore* II.350–360;
- Quintilian, *Institutio oratoria* XI.2.

The classical scheme supplies:

- natural versus artificial memory;
- ordered, visually distinct loci;
- images attached to things or words;
- forward traversal;
- sufficiently vivid and differentiated places;
- practice and review.

Riccio’s compact-room experiments resemble Quintilian’s house-route, while his church and city routes extend it to institutional and urban scale.

### Scholastic psychology and Dominican memory

Albert the Great and Thomas Aquinas incorporated trained recollection into prudence and Aristotelian faculty psychology. Medieval Dominican practice legitimated corporeal similitudes as aids through which the mind retrieves intellectual and moral matter.

Riccio’s sermons, Beatitudes, apostles, Job, saints, virtues, Thomas’s *Summa*, convents, and daily preaching calendar belong securely to this lineage.

### Franciscan, Lullian, and combinatorial traditions

Riccio’s circle and zodiac can look superficially “Lullian,” but the difference is structural:

- Lullian wheels combine letters and divine dignities to investigate necessary relations.
- Riccio’s bodily circuit is a static serial index.
- No rotating disks, combinatorial chambers, or logical production occur.
- No dependence on a specific Lull text is demonstrated.

The Franciscan/Lullian tradition is therefore context, not a documented source.

### Hermeticism

**[ABSENCE]** Riccio does not invoke Hermes Trismegistus, Ficinian spiritus, planetary demons, talismanic capture of celestial power, Cabala, or ascent through astral images.

The cosmic-body diagrams share common cosmological imagery with Hermetic and astrological works, but shared imagery does not prove shared magical operation.

### Ramism

Riccio’s alphabetic and taxonomic lists belong to the broader sixteenth-century pressure toward ordered knowledge. Yet he retains artificial images and traversed places—precisely what Ramist method tended to replace with dialectical division and visible outline. He is therefore adjacent to, not an instance of, Ramism.

### Universal knowledge and collecting

Riccio repeatedly promises *universalità*. His universality means having enough differentiated repositories to command many fields in conversation, preaching, and study. The royal palace, Gaddi gallery, stone lists, plant lists, and botanical Piazza circuit link memory with the late-Medicean collection and cabinet.

This is a modest universal-language project only by analogy. He supplies expandable vernacular dictionaries of images but no universally valid symbolic grammar.

### Institutions

The system’s institutional geography is unusually concrete:

- Dominican convents at Santa Maria Novella and San Marco;
- Franciscan Santa Croce;
- Augustinian Santo Spirito;
- Carmelite Carmine;
- Vallombrosan Santa Trinita;
- Servite Annunziata;
- Florentine streets, monasteries, hospitals, workshops, gardens, galleries, and government buildings;
- sermon cycles and Lenten preaching;
- university or conventual lecture repetition;
- Medici collections and natural-history workshops.

The city itself becomes an index of church, court, craft, gender, charity, government, and natural knowledge.

## People

### Agostino del Riccio

**[DOCUMENT]** Born in Florence in 1541, originally named Pietro, son of Francesco del Riccio. The Dominican vestition record says he received the habit on 27 September 1558, aged seventeen, and made solemn profession on 8 September 1559.

He spent time at San Marco, then at the sanctuary of Madonna della Quercia near Viterbo, and later lived at Santa Maria Novella. He worked with the convent’s lands and gardens and cultivated interests in agriculture, botany, medicine, stones, architecture, and art.

The Santa Maria Novella chronicle records his death on 18 December 1598 and states that his likeness appeared in the great cloister’s scene of the death of St Dominic.

### Niccolò Gaddi

Collector and patron whose gallery provided Riccio with memorable sculptural forms. His role is documented by Riccio’s reference and independent art-historical study.

### Francesco Panigarola

**[TRADITION in Riccio]** Riccio says the celebrated preacher possessed one hundred thousand loci and, according to further report, ten thousand more. The wording—*dicono*, “they say”—marks hearsay.

Panigarola’s own *Regole per far la memoria locale*, composed approximately 1575–1578, places him in the same practical Counter-Reformation mnemonic culture. The exact total of 100,000 or 110,000 loci remains unverified.

### Riccio’s companion and child pupil

**[TEXTUAL ANECDOTE]** One companion allegedly memorized a book in an hour; a small boy allegedly astonished learned audiences despite scarcely being able to read the material he recited.

**Evidence status:** authorial testimonial only. No name, dated performance record, or independent corroboration was found.

### Giuseppo Fiammingo

Riccio praises the gardener and herbalist Giuseppe Fiammingo for bringing many flowers to Florence. He connects mnemonic conversation at banquets with the botanical wealth encouraged under Grand Duke Francesco I.

### Patrons and implied users

The dedicatees are Florentine literary youth, but the actual user community includes preachers, prelates, confessors, students, grammarians, soldiers, artisans, nobles, poor people, and social diners.

### The requested stock anecdotes

The auctioneer, professor’s party, amnesiac student, and accused magician are not documented episodes in Riccio’s treatise or its known reception. They belong to other memory-art dossiers or later mnemonic folklore. Assigning them to Riccio would be a modern conflation.

## The magic question

### Was Riccio’s system magical?

The strongest answer is: **the surviving text provides no evidence that Riccio thought it magical or talismanic.**

Reasons:

- Places work through familiarity, illumination, stability, spacing, and order.
- Images work through resemblance, phonetic pun, vividness, emotion, personal association, and repetition.
- Retrieval works by forward and reverse traversal.
- The zodiac supplies a conventional order.
- The elements and spheres are objects of memorization, not invoked powers.
- Practice is explicitly divided into timed doses.
- The examples are doctrinally orthodox, practical, botanical, civic, and social.

### Astrology versus astral magic

The zodiacal body is based on melothesia—the traditional assignment of signs to bodily regions. Such imagery could appear in astrological, medical, mnemonic, artistic, or magical contexts. Context determines function.

Here the signs are ordinal markers. No operation, prayer, election, planetary hour, consecration, spirit, talisman, or causal astral influx is specified.

### Yates’s verdict

Yates says “no suspicion need be attached” to the zodiac and calls the cosmic-body exercise “puerile, but not magical.” This negative conclusion is supported by the text. Her evaluative term “puerile” is not an evidentiary finding.

### Heresy and Inquisition

**[ABSENCE]**

- No accusation of magic or heresy against Riccio was found.
- No Inquisition proceeding concerning the *Arte della memoria locale* was found.
- No censorship, prohibition, confiscation, or Index listing was found.
- No evidence links the treatise to the *Ars notoria*.

A 1569 conventual record restricted Riccio from celebrating public Mass because of excessive prolixity in pronouncing the words of consecration, which caused scandal, astonishment, and irritation. This is a genuine disciplinary document, but it concerns liturgical conduct—not magic, mnemonic practice, or heresy.

### Bruno as a misleading shadow

Bruno was accused and executed for a complex body of theological and philosophical positions. The fact that both men were Dominicans and used memory images does not transfer Bruno’s inquisitorial history to Riccio.

Yates’s contrast—Riccio’s tame Dominican mnemonic versus Bruno’s magicized seals—is heuristic. It is not evidence that Riccio’s system was investigated and cleared by authorities.

## Afterlife

### Direct reception

**[ABSENCE]** No named line of pupils, printed transmission, early translation, or demonstrable adoption of Riccio’s particular seven-rule court allegory was found.

His memory treatise survived principally as a manuscript. His natural-history works were better known to eighteenth- and nineteenth-century Tuscan scholars.

### Modern rediscovery

A tentative chronology is:

- 1722: Giovanni Negri briefly notices Riccio as a Florentine writer.
- 1751 onward: Giovanni Targioni-Tozzetti uses Riccio’s natural-history work.
- 1845: Vincenzo Marchese discusses Dominican artists and writers.
- 1920s: Ernst Kris brings Riccio into study of Renaissance stonework and rustic style.
- 1955: Yates mentions the manuscript and the Bruno comparison in “The Ciceronian Art of Memory.”
- 1958–60s: Paolo Rossi places Riccio within the wider history of mnemonic systems.
- 1966: Yates’s *Art of Memory* gives the system its most influential English-language description.
- 1979–96: Barocchi, Heikamp, Gnoli, and Sironi reconstruct Riccio’s art, stone, garden, and natural-history environment.
- 1984–2001: Bolzoni develops the memory/collection interpretation.
- 2001–07: Kuwakino, Brunon, and Benzi treat his garden, encyclopedism, and mnemonic architecture in detail.
- 2007: Benzi publishes the item-specific study and electronic transcription.
- 2021–24: scholarship on stones, collections, and mnemonic ekphrasis continues to use Riccio as a local, practical case.

### Art, fresco, and emblem

Riccio’s virtues in Santa Maria Novella fit a longer tradition in which church architecture, fresco cycles, personifications, and moral classifications reinforced recollection. But no surviving fresco has been shown to have been painted from his treatise.

His lost or unlocated book of *imprese*, if it existed as described, would strengthen the emblematic connection. **[ABSENCE]** Since the text has not been found, its contents cannot be reconstructed.

### Dante, Borges, Eco, and modern memory palaces

These are comparative afterlives of the art of memory generally, not documented effects of Riccio:

- Dante’s ordered moral and cosmological spaces predate him.
- Borges’s Funes represents involuntary total recall, almost the opposite of selective mnemonic ordering.
- Eco’s semiotic and encyclopedic interests belong to a modern intellectual reception of combinatorics and memory.
- Contemporary memory palaces reproduce Riccio’s central mechanism: fixed routes, distinctive loci, personally vivid images, deliberate retrieval.
- Memory athletes use comparable spatial encoding but are not known to descend specifically from Riccio.

### Computing and combinatorics

Riccio’s system resembles computer addressing only as a **[MODERN ANALOGY]**:

- locus ≈ address;
- image ≈ retrieval key;
- route ≈ index order;
- multiple convents ≈ separate workspaces;
- permanent versus swept loci ≈ long-term versus reusable storage;
- alphabetic lists ≈ extensible lookup tables.

The historically documented “Lull to Leibniz to machine” line concerns formal combination and symbolic calculus. Riccio’s art is a serial storage-and-retrieval discipline, not a calculating machine. Connecting him directly to Leibniz or computing would overstate the evidence.

## Controversies and disputes

### 1. Seven or eight illustrations?

- **Yates:** seven drawings.
- **Modern transcription:** introductory king plus seven numbered rules, with illustration headings reaching “eighth illustration.”
- **Finding:** unresolved without examining the codex’s page layout, but eight illustrated units are reconstructable from the transcription.

### 2. Plate 13 labels

- 13c is the Second Counsellor/Images.
- 13d is the Second Captain/Circular Line.
- 13e is the Third Captain/Transverse Line.
- The catalogue’s “Third Captain, plate 13c” is a modern copying error.
- No Yates plate is assigned to the First Captain/Straight Line.

### 3. Twelve, 27, or 80 Via della Scala places?

All three numbers have different referents:

- twelve: objects visible in Yates’s cropped/reproduced plate;
- 27: deposits in the written worked example;
- over 80: Riccio’s claim for his normal full route.

### 4. Six or seven miseries of man?

Riccio explicitly enumerates six clauses from Job 14:1–2. Yates’s “seven miseries” is an error.

### 5. Conservative or encyclopedic?

- **Yates:** traditional, enfeebled, unaware of Renaissance transformations.
- **Bolzoni/Benzi/Kuwakino/Brunon:** technically traditional but culturally intertwined with collecting, visual alphabets, gardens, natural history, and universal knowledge.
- **Finding:** the later interpretation explains more of the manuscript. Traditional mechanism and encyclopedic purpose are compatible.

### 6. Did Riccio model Bruno?

Chronology excludes direct influence from Riccio’s 1595 manuscript upon Bruno’s 1583 *Seals*. Yates proposes a shared Dominican mode of instruction. No common immediate source or conventual curriculum document has yet been identified.

### 7. Was Riccio’s zodiac magical?

No operative magic is present. Calling all zodiacal imagery magical would erase distinctions among cosmology, medicine, astrology, mnemonics, and talismanic practice.

### 8. Is “local memory” only spatial?

No. Riccio adds:

- personally variable image selection;
- semantic and phonetic association;
- numerical marking;
- forward and backward retrieval;
- temporal spacing;
- multiple repositories to prevent interference;
- periodic review;
- categorical organization for permanent knowledge.

### 9. Reliability of feats

Panigarola’s 100,000 places, the one-hour book, the nearly illiterate child prodigy, and the promise of 200 concepts a day are promotional or exemplary claims. They are valuable evidence for expectations surrounding mnemonic virtuosity, not verified performance measurements.

# Agostino del Riccio's *Arte della memoria locale*: Research Dossier

## Basic identification

**Name:** *Arte della memoria locale*  
**Exponent:** Fra Agostino del Riccio, O.P.  
**Date:** 1595  
**Source text:** Florence, Biblioteca Nazionale Centrale di Firenze, MS Nazionale II.I.13, formerly Magliabechiano Classe V, 9  
**Principle in one sentence:** Stable, familiar places—churches, streets, rooms, objects, or the ordered parts of a human body—are traversed in a fixed direction and populated with personally effective images, so that mentally retracing the route retrieves the deposited material in sequence.  
**Yates:** Frances A. Yates, *The Art of Memory* (London, 1966), ch. XI, pp. 244–246; recalled at pp. 313–14; plates 13a–f.

### Evidentiary labels used

- **[TEXT]** directly stated or depicted in Riccio’s manuscript.
- **[DOCUMENT]** established by archival or bibliographical evidence independent of the treatise.
- **[RECONSTRUCTION]** a modern scholar’s contextual or functional inference.
- **[YATES]** Yates’s interpretation.
- **[TRADITION]** reported by Riccio or another historical witness without independent confirmation.
- **[DISPUTED]** contested, internally inconsistent, or inadequately demonstrated.
- **[LEGEND]** anecdote or reputation transmitted without firm documentation.
- **[MODERN ANALOGY]** a later comparison, not a historical line of influence.
- **[ABSENCE]** something sought but not found in the surviving evidence.

The most important correction to the catalogue entry is numerical. **[TEXT]** The modern transcription identifies an introductory royal image followed by seven illustrated rules, with headings extending from a “first illustration” to an “eighth illustration.” **[YATES]** Yates says that the manuscript has “seven drawings.” Unless the manuscript’s physical organization makes one heading non-pictorial, the accessible transcription points to **eight illustrated units**, not seven. Likewise, Riccio’s Via della Scala demonstration contains **27 named deposits**, and he says that his own full route contains more than eighty places; Yates’s plate 13e evidently reproduces only twelve symbols.

## The system in detail

### 1. Purpose and audience

**[TEXT]** The treatise is dedicated at f. 1r:

> «Alla gioventù fiorentina studiosa di Lettere.»

“To the Florentine youth devoted to letters.”

At f. 5r Riccio widens the audience to preachers, orators, students, and effectively everyone, young and old. The manuscript repeatedly addresses the reader as *benigno lettore*, *industrioso giovanetto*, and *predicator mio amantissimo*. It is therefore both a beginner’s manual and a vocational tool for preaching, study, confession, conversation, and civic display.

The aims include:

- retaining sermons, speeches, concepts, sententiae, histories, examples, and rhetorical ornaments;
- memorizing councils, canon law, patristic teachings, excommunications, and difficult cases;
- retaining lectures and arguments for academic *circoli*;
- learning grammar and Donatus;
- remembering military stratagems;
- remembering names immediately after hearing them;
- ordering natural history—rivers, fishes, plants, flowers, stones, animals, birds;
- producing learned conversation at banquets;
- displaying the dignities and learned citizens of Florence.

**[TEXT]** Riccio’s epistemic maxim is striking:

> «quel che sappiamo a mente ci fa honore e veramente si può dir di sapere» (f. 13v).

“What we know by heart brings us honour and can truly be called knowledge.”

That sentence explains why his memory art connects preaching, encyclopedism, collecting, sociability, and civic prestige.

### 2. The governing allegory: king, counsellors, captains, servant

**[TEXT]** Riccio personifies the art as a court or household. Local memory is a rich king; the rules subordinated to it appear as counsellors, captains, and a servant. Each rule is prefaced by a symbolic picture and title.

**[RECONSTRUCTION]** This is more than decoration. The illustrations themselves create a miniature memory system: personified offices distinguish functions, while their attributes—globe, book, objects, geometrical lines, food—compress the associated instructions.

#### The King

**[TEXT; f. 5r]** Memory is represented as:

> «un re richissimo che con una mano si tocca la memoria».

“A very rich king who with one hand touches his memory.”

**[YATES]** Plate 13a shows him striking or touching his brow. Yates interprets the gesture as calling up local memory for preachers, orators, students, and every class of person.

The king’s wealth anticipates Riccio’s later metaphor of the royal palace whose categorized rooms contain antiquities, tapestries, silver, jewels, arms, and provisions.

### 3. First rule: natural and artificial memory

**[TEXT]** The first rule distinguishes *memoria naturale*, an innate faculty, from *memoria artificiale* or *locale*, perfected through practice. Riccio’s practical premise is summarized by the pseudo-Ciceronian maxim:

> «l’arte fa perfetta la na(tural) cosa».

“Art perfects the natural thing.”

Riccio or the modern transcriber associates this with *Rhetorica ad Herennium* III.16. The wording is not a literal standard Latin quotation; it is a vernacular maxim expressing the classical distinction.

**Retrieval principle:** natural aptitude supplies the capacity; repeated ordered emplacement trains and enlarges it.

### 4. Second rule: places—the First Counsellor

**[TEXT; ff. 6–14v]** The heading is:

> «Seconda regola nominata “luoghi et consiglier primo”».

The illustration shows a counsellor resting a hand on a world map containing cities, towns, castles, houses, shops, churches, palaces, streets, squares, and convents.

#### Rules for places

Riccio enumerates seven conditions:

> «In prima, i luoghi debbono esser luminosi; secondario, fermi; terzo, habbino distanza ragionevole; quarto, che detti luoghi seguitino ordinatamente l’uno doppo l’altro; quinto, si tien’ ordine a locare a mano ritta o mancina …; sesto, ci sono i luoghi comuni che non ritengono sempre le cose …; settimo, ci sono i luoghi ch’insegnano ritenere a mente tutto quel che s’è inparato».

In concise form:

1. Places must be sufficiently illuminated.
2. They must be stable.
3. They should be reasonably spaced.
4. They must follow one another in an intelligible order.
5. One must preserve a directional convention—rightward or leftward.
6. Some places are temporary and can be reused.
7. Other places support durable retention and should not be cleared.

Useful architectural markers include:

> «porte, finestre, statue, pile, cantonate, altari, sepolcri, tabernacoli, acquai, cammini, scale, banchi, armadi, pitture dipinte ne’ muri».

Doors, windows, statues, basins, corners, altars, tombs, tabernacles, sinks or water places, fireplaces, stairs, benches, cupboards, and wall paintings.

#### Distance

**[TEXT]** In churches, squares, and streets, Riccio recommends approximately six or eight braccia between loci; he also reports a prescription of eight or ten. In small, intimately familiar rooms, shops, or on the body, loci can be less than a braccio apart.

This is an empirical qualification of the textbook rule: effective familiarity matters more than an invariant measurement.

#### Direction and circuit

Start at a conspicuous entrance; proceed consistently to the right or left; continue around the structure until returning to the starting point. Riccio refuses to dogmatize about right versus left, comparing them to the mutually assisting “sisters” of painting and sculpture.

#### Worked example: the Beatitudes

**[TEXT; ff. 7–8]** Riccio maps the eight Beatitudes onto an ordered domestic or architectural route. For “Blessed are those who hunger and thirst after justice,” he places four perpetually hungry friends at a door:

> «Beati qui esuriunt et sitiunt iustiziam … metti a l’uscio che va in camera quattro tuoi amici, che sempre habbin fame».

For the persecuted he places five martyrs at the next corner. The number of figures helps mark ordinal position as well as content.

#### Worked example: apostolic predicates

**[TEXT; ff. 8–9]** Riccio deposits a sequence of objects in a compact room:

- a burning lamp for the apostles as the light of the world;
- a stone column for “columns”;
- salt on a stool for “salt”;
- three stones for “precious stones”;
- a judge on a stone for “judges”;
- bones at a corner for “bones of Christ”;
- four doves at a window;
- a holy-water bucket for “fishermen,” because fishing occurs in water;
- painted clouds;
- two imaginary oxen in a cupboard;
- four lions on a bench;
- two paper stars on a nail for the twelve stars of the Apocalyptic Woman’s crown.

The examples mix resemblance, pun, causal association, scriptural iconography, number, and personal familiarity.

#### Santa Maria Novella and the theological virtues

**[TEXT, as quoted by Yates from f. 6]** Beginning at the high altar, Riccio places Charity; proceeding around the church, Hope goes on the Ciodi altar and Faith on the Gaddi altar; the circuit continues through chapel altars, holy-water stoup, tombs, and other landmarks until it returns to its beginning.

**[YATES]** This is “the good old fashioned way of using the art, to remember virtues.”

**[QUALIFICATION]** The example certainly is devotional and Dominican. But Riccio’s surviving text also emphasizes encyclopedic storage, natural history, collecting, professional utility, and social performance. “Good old fashioned” captures one use but not the whole system.

#### Temporary versus permanent repositories

For daily preaching Riccio recommends seven independent place systems, one for every day:

- Sunday: Santa Maria Novella;
- Monday: San Marco;
- Tuesday: Santa Croce;
- Wednesday: Santo Spirito;
- Thursday: the Carmine;
- Friday: Santa Trinita;
- Saturday: Santissima Annunziata.

After seven days one returns to Santa Maria Novella, by which time the transient images should have faded.

Some practitioners allegedly use one or two systems and imagine an agile servant sweeping out yesterday’s figures. Riccio prefers seven separate systems because a week’s delay reduces interference.

For durable knowledge, he says not to remove the material but to revisit it frequently.

#### The royal palace as classified storehouse

**[TEXT; f. 13v]**

> «fa’ come fanno i re potentissimi e ricchissimi che hanno ne’ lor gran palazzi molte stanze: in una sarà l’anticagle, in un’altra i panni d’arazzo, in un’altra la loro argenteria, in un’altra le gioie; in altre stanze l’armi da guerra, sì offensive come defensive; in altre le vettovaglie …».

“Do as the most powerful and wealthy kings do, who have many rooms in their great palaces: in one will be antiquities, in another tapestries, in another their silver, in another jewels; in other rooms weapons of war, offensive and defensive; in others provisions….”

The user who wishes to be universal should similarly maintain multiple rooms for sermons, discourses, concepts, sayings, histories, and whatever discipline he professes.

**[BOLZONI’S RECONSTRUCTION]** Lina Bolzoni reads this as a meeting between the classical *thesaurus eloquentiae* and late-Renaissance collecting. The mental room and the collection-room organize abundance by type. Unlike Camillo’s metaphysical theatre or Citolini’s elaborately topical storehouse, Riccio’s categories remain comparatively practical. [Bolzoni, *The Gallery of Memory*, pp. 251–54](https://pdfcoffee.com/the-gallery-of-memory-literary-and-iconographic-models-in-the-age-of-the-printing-press-bolzoni-lina-pdf-free.html).

### 5. Third rule: images—the Second Counsellor

**[TEXT; ff. 15–32]** The heading is:

> «Terza regola nominata “figure et consiglier secondo”».

The pictured man holds a book and is surrounded by heterogeneous objects: the transcription’s description includes a statue on a pedestal, a marble bust, and a lectern. Yates’s plate 13c reproduces this unit.

#### Kinds and rules of images

Riccio distinguishes:

- **real figures:** lion, ox, falcon, and similar things;
- **imaginary or fictive figures:** Hydra, or a goose with three necks and three heads;
- **artworks:** figures invented or executed by painters and sculptors;
- **actual objects or statues:** especially memorable things already seen.

He says:

> «le figure imaginarie muovano manco, però noi ci serviamo delle figure reali e materiali».

“Imaginary figures move [the mind] less; therefore we use real and material figures.”

This is an important divergence from the most sensational reading of the *Ad Herennium*. Riccio accepts monsters but prefers objects with perceptual and personal force. He explicitly relativizes selection:

> «a mme mi muoverà una figura et a te un’altra».

“One image will move me and another you.”

#### Niccolò Gaddi’s gallery

Riccio describes an unusual double-bodied or double-headed marble figure, viewable from two sides, in the gallery left by Niccolò Gaddi. The collection supplies ready-made memorable images.

**[DOCUMENT]** Niccolò Gaddi (1537–1591) was a Florentine collector and patron connected with Santa Maria Novella.

**[RECONSTRUCTION]** For Benzi and Bolzoni, this is evidence that Riccio’s art belongs not only to sermon mnemonics but to the culture of galleries, natural specimens, precious stones, and organized collections.

#### Wordplay: Florentine family names

**[TEXT]**

- *Pescioni*: put three fish (*pesci*) in a corner.
- *Ricci*: put two hedgehogs (*ricci*) in a corner.
- *Asini*: tie two asses (*asini*) to a door.

These are phonetic or rebus images, not semantic representations of the families.

#### Alphabetical image dictionaries

Riccio provides alphabetic lists for rivers and lakes, fishes, precious and other stones, herbs, flowers, trees and fruits, land animals, birds, cities, Florentine families, mechanical and liberal arts, occupations, honoured men, illustrious women, male saints, and female saints.

For A in the river alphabet:

> «Arno, Anbra, Asapo, Arco».

The reader is expected to augment the lists. Riccio proposes additional alphabets for poisonous animals, legumes and seeds, fruits, textile colours, numbers, armour and weapons, philosophers, poets, and authors in every discipline.

**[TEXTUAL FEATURE]** The manuscript leaves gaps in these lists for supplementation.

**[RECONSTRUCTION]** They are open-ended working instruments, not finished universal dictionaries.

### 6. Fourth rule: the First Captain or Straight Line

**[TEXT; cited by Yates at f. 33]** The first captain places an ordered vertical sequence on a human body. The body itself supplies intrinsically ordered loci: hair, forehead, eyebrows, eyes, mouth, chin, throat, breasts, torso, belt, knee, leg, foot, shoe.

Riccio also places the twelve zodiacal signs upon the bodily regions they were conventionally believed to govern.

**Retrieval:** scan the body vertically in the memorized order; the anatomical sequence cues the zodiacal or other deposited sequence.

**[YATES]** The figure shows a man crossed by a vertical line, with zodiac signs placed according to astrological melothesia.

**[QUALIFICATION]** Neither Yates nor the accessible transcription shows Riccio claiming that zodiacal powers cause recollection. The zodiac supplies a familiar sequence and a map of the body.

**[ABSENCE]** No wheel is involved in this rule.

### 7. Fifth rule: the Second Captain or Circular Line

**[TEXT; f. 35]** The second captain is a man with arms and legs extended within a circle. The user distributes the four elements and cosmic spheres around bodily loci so that a mental circuit retrieves the cosmological order.

Yates transcribes the sequence as:

- earth—feet;
- water—knee;
- air—flank;
- fire—arm;
- Moon—right hand;
- Mercury—right forearm;
- Venus—right shoulder;
- Sun—head;
- Mars—left shoulder;
- Jupiter—left forearm;
- Saturn—left hand;
- sphere of fixed stars—“left shoulder”;
- crystalline sphere—waist;
- *primum mobile*—knees;
- Paradise—under the left foot.

**[DISPUTED/TRANSCRIPTION PROBLEM]** Yates’s printed list assigns both Mars and the fixed stars to the left shoulder, while other positions suggest a continuous descending circuit. This may be Riccio’s repetition, Yates’s error, or a modern OCR problem. It requires direct inspection of f. 35 and cannot responsibly be silently corrected.

**[YATES]** This is “puerile, but not magical”: a rational use of a traditional cosmological sequence.

**[RECONSTRUCTION]** Its operative resemblance to a wheel is limited. The enclosing circle and bodily circuit order the sequence, but there is no evidence of independently rotating concentric disks or combinatorial permutations of the Lullian/Brunian kind.

### 8. Sixth rule: Third Captain, Transverse Line

**[TEXT; ff. 39–45]** Heading:

> «Sesta regola da me detta “linea traversale e(t) nominativo terzo”».

Riccio calls this “the best of all and the most used.” *Transverse* means that it can pass through sites of any shape—streets, squares, churches, quadrangular, hexagonal, octagonal, or irregular spaces.

#### Dominican cell: Job 14:1–2

The six loci are:

1. Door: Riccio’s mother who bore him — *homo natus de muliere*.
2. First corner: an old man covered with *brevi* (briefs) — *brevi vivens tempore*.
3. Small altar: a sick man covered in sores — *repletur multis miseriis*.
4. Second corner: Riccio’s sister adorned with quickly fading flowers — *quasi flos egreditur et conteritur*.
5. Window: a friend holding an umbrella for shade — *fugit velut umbra*.
6. Next corner: a courtier falling from his lord’s favour and losing favour and life — *nunquam in eodem statu permanet*.

Riccio then recites the text without naming loci or images, and afterward in reverse order. This is the clearest surviving account of retrieval:

1. encode phrase by phrase;
2. attach each phrase to an image;
3. place images on a known ordered route;
4. traverse once forward;
5. traverse backward;
6. eventually recite the material directly, while the places remain the recovery structure.

**Correction to Yates:** Job gives six clauses or “miseries” here, not seven. Yates calls them “the seven miseries of man.”

#### Via della Scala: 27 objects

The route begins near Santa Maria Novella and proceeds to the right. Among the first loci:

1. street-corner tabernacle: a religious with a cross — cross;
2. Casavecchi house-door: a large red star — star;
3. Jacopo dal Borgho’s door: a golden sun — sun;
4. blocked convent door with Marian image and Acciaiuoli arms: an Acciaiuoli duke holding a golden flower-vase — vase;
5. church of the *donne mal maritate*: a porphyry door — door;
6. former Bartolini garden entrance: an imaginary silver mountain — mountain;
7. Ripoli convent church door: a clock;
8. convent entrance: an official reading with spectacles;
9. garden corner: an astrologer with compasses;
10. wall corner: an armed man with a mace;
11. facing wall: a shepherd with shears;
12. Acciaiuoli garden entrance: St Barbara with a tower;
13. simples-garden entrance: a hammer;
14. first house: a large bell;
15. blind alley: a drummer;
16. weavers’ house: a standard-bearer;
17. Rucellai garden: a man reading a book;
18. Via Polverosa corner: a trumpeter;
19. San Martino convent corner: a widow;
20. small door: a confessor;
21. parlour door: a doctor;
22. factor’s door: a gardener;
23. Via dell’Albero: a Turk;
24. Via de’ Canacci: a Corsican dog, *can corso*;
25. Via Benedetta: St Benedict in a porphyry niche;
26. Via Porcella: St Paul on a column;
27. hospital loggia/church: Saints Dominic and Francis.

Riccio recites the resulting list forward and backward. The transcription preserves several slips in numbering and repetition—valuable evidence that this is a practical manuscript rather than a typographically normalized textbook.

> «Io ho locati questi ventisette nomi in via della Scala per via d’esempio ma io, quando vi loco … piglio più di ottanta luoghi.»

“I have located these twenty-seven names in Via della Scala by way of example, but when I locate there I take more than eighty places.”

**Correction:** Plate 13e shows twelve small symbols, but the accompanying written demonstration is not a twelve-item system.

#### Piazza del Granduca: evergreen plants

Riccio creates a 32-place botanical circuit around what is now Piazza della Signoria, using Santo Romolo, shops, corners, the Loggia, its columns and statues, Perseus, the palace entrance, David, the lion, the fountain, and government offices.

Plants deposited include palm, dwarf palm, orange, myrtle, cedar, laurel, lemon, Adam’s apple, cypress, strawberry tree, juniper, pine, savin, fir, holm oak, box, olive, bitter orange, viburnum, yew or *nasso*, mastic, oleander, royal laurel, cork oak, holly, teucrium, wild olive, broom, rosemary, ivy, and myrtle.

Again Riccio recites forward and backward.

This example is especially important because it ties the memory art directly to Riccio’s botanical and horticultural writing.

### 9. Seventh rule: Food and Servant

**[TEXT; ff. 45–48]** Heading:

> «Settima regola da me nominata “cibo et servitore”».

The associated image shows a man with food and drink.

Riccio compares memorization to distributing food across meals:

> «sicome si divide il mangiar che facciamo il giorno più volte, così e non altrimenti si viene ad inparare per memoria locale».

“As we divide the eating we do several times during the day, so and in no other way should one learn through local memory.”

Attempting two hundred concepts or two hundred articles of Thomas immediately after rising would exhaust memory, just as consuming a whole day’s food at breakfast would overload the stomach. The student should number the material in a notebook and learn ten items at a time—on rising, an hour later, before dinner, after dinner, and through the day.

This rule adds **temporal spacing** to spatial order.

#### Promised feats

Riccio claims that a disciplined user can acquire one or two hundred concepts a day; learn lists of popes and emperors; discuss rivers, fishes, precious stones, plants, flowers, and Florentine worthies; and learn the plants in Dioscorides rapidly.

At ff. 47r–48r he reports:

> «questo è intervenuto a un dei miei carissimi compagni che ha questa memoria locale che in un’ora ha imparato a mente un libro».

“This happened to one of my dearest companions, who has this local memory and in one hour learned a book by heart.”

He also describes a small boy taught by him who astonished learned men, retained many memorized things, and could recite material he could scarcely read.

These are **[TEXT: autobiographical claims]**, not independently documented demonstrations.

## The sources

### 1. The primary manuscript

**[DOCUMENT]**

Agostino del Riccio, *Arte della memoria locale*, Florence, Biblioteca Nazionale Centrale di Firenze:

- current shelfmark: **MS Nazionale II.I.13**;
- former shelfmark: **Magliabechiano Classe V, 9**;
- language: Tuscan Italian;
- date stated and consistently reported: **1595**;
- unpublished in Riccio’s lifetime;
- no early printed edition has been identified.

The principal cited folios are:

- f. 1r: dedication;
- f. 5r: king and audience;
- f. 6: First Counsellor/Santa Maria Novella;
- ff. 7–14v: place rules and applications;

- f. 39: Transverse Line illustration;
- ff. 40–45: cell, Via della Scala, and Piazza examples;
- f. 45: Food and Servant illustration;
- ff. 46–48: spaced practice and testimonials.

Utzima Benzi prepared a normalized diplomatic transcription hosted by Emilio Panella. Its principles include preserving many original spellings and semi-Latinisms, expanding abbreviations, marking supplied text, inserting modern paragraphs for intelligibility, and identifying folio transitions.

This is the best accessible primary-text surrogate found in the research, but it is **not a facsimile and not a full critical edition**. Editorial paragraphing, punctuation, capitalization, and some identifications are modern.

### 4. Other Riccio manuscripts relevant to reconstruction

The memory treatise should be read alongside Riccio’s manuscript encyclopedic-naturalistic production:

- *Agricoltura sperimentale*, BNCF, Targioni-Tozzetti 56, vols. I–III;
- *Agricoltura teorica*, in the same complex;
- *Istoria delle pietre*, Biblioteca Riccardiana, Ricc. 230;
- *Libro di Pietre et Agricoltura*, Modena, Biblioteca Estense, Campori 624γ B.2.9;
- *Agricoltura sperimentale*, Biblioteca Estense, It. 400, H.3–5;
- another agricultural manuscript, Florence, Careggi medical library, R 210.2;
- *Trattato di agricoltura*, BNCF Nuovi Acquisti 1072.

These are not textual witnesses of the *Arte della memoria locale*. They establish the broader taxonomic environment in which Riccio’s alphabets and botanical memory routes operated.

### 5. Manuscript and edition variants

**[ABSENCE]** No second manuscript witness, authorial redaction, sixteenth-century printing, or early translation of the *Arte della memoria locale* was located.

Accordingly:

- there is no established stemma;
- there are no print-state variants;
- variation currently means differences among the manuscript, Benzi’s normalized transcription, Yates’s English paraphrase, and later summaries;
- several numerical problems—seven/eight pictures, six/seven miseries, twelve/27/80 Via della Scala items—arise at this level of mediation.

## Yates’s account and scholarship since

### Yates, chapter XI, pp. 244–246

Yates introduces Riccio while trying to explain Bruno’s *Thirty Seals*. Her sequence is:

1. identify Riccio as a Dominican of Santa Maria Novella;
2. call his work an unpublished 1595 manual for “studious young gentlemen”;
3. describe the King and two Counsellors;
4. summarize the bodily Straight and Circular Lines;
5. summarize Via della Scala, the Dominican cell, and Food and Servant;
6. declare the system nonmagical and late, weak, traditional;
7. use its personified diagrams as the explanatory analogue for Bruno’s seals.

Her core comparative claim is:

> “his idea of presenting the principles and various techniques of the art through little symbolic pictures, with titles, exactly corresponds to what Bruno does in *Seals*.”

She concludes that Bruno had “magicised” a Dominican mode of presentation with Lullism and Cabala.

### What remains confirmed

- Riccio was a Dominican associated with Santa Maria Novella.
- The text is dated 1595.
- Places and images follow the classical local-memory framework.
- Churches and devotional matter are prominent.
- Zodiac and cosmic orders function as memory sequences.
- The symbolic court figures summarize rules.
- The manuscript is a useful formal comparison with Bruno’s named seals.
- Nothing in Riccio’s text demonstrates talismanic or operative astral magic.

### What requires correction

1. **“Seven drawings.”** The transcription numbers eight illustrated headings. Direct codicological inspection is needed, but Yates’s count cannot simply be repeated.

2. **Via della Scala.** Yates’s plate has twelve objects; the text gives 27 and says Riccio ordinarily uses over 80 loci.

3. **Job’s “seven miseries.”** The manuscript enumerates six clauses from Job 14:1–2.

4. **“This friar has not heard of exciting Renaissance transformations.”** This is rhetoric, not evidence. Riccio knew gallery culture, emblematic presentation, cosmological diagrams, expanding image alphabets, encyclopedic natural history, and possibly wrote a lost book of *imprese*. His system is conservative in mechanism but embedded in late-Renaissance collecting and encyclopedism.

5. **“Enfeeblement.”** Later scholarship has largely replaced this decline narrative with functional and cultural analysis. Riccio’s art is not metaphysically ambitious like Camillo’s or Bruno’s, but it is pedagogically detailed, urban, socially situated, and linked to collecting and natural history.

6. **Influence on Bruno.** Riccio wrote in 1595; Bruno’s *Triginta sigilli* appeared in 1583. Riccio therefore cannot have supplied Bruno’s form. Yates means that both reflect a Dominican pedagogical tradition, but she offers no direct textual chain proving that Bruno learned precisely this convention in his convent.

### Rossi

Paolo Rossi treated Riccio within the history of artificial memory, combinatorics, and universal method. His broad framework helps distinguish ordinary local memory from Lullian combinatorial arts. Riccio has ordered repositories and finite lists; he does not generate propositions by rotating symbolic alphabets.

### Bolzoni

Lina Bolzoni’s major correction is contextual. In *La stanza della memoria* and *The Gallery of Memory*, she treats Riccio’s royal rooms as evidence for the convergence of memory, collecting, rhetorical storage, and classification. She rejects the implication that Riccio is simply alien to encyclopedic aspiration: his universalism is practical and taxonomic rather than Camillian or Brunian.

### Benzi

Utzima Benzi’s 2007 study and online transcription provide the fullest item-specific treatment found. Benzi:

- documents Riccio’s life and Dominican records;
- reconstructs the seven-rule organization;
- emphasizes the images’ emblematic function;
- connects the treatise with his natural-history manuscripts;
- identifies the practical, pedagogical, and Florentine-civic purposes;
- treats forward and backward repetition as central to secure retrieval.

### Kuwakino and Brunon

Koji Kuwakino and Hervé Brunon situate Riccio’s garden and agricultural projects within mnemonic architecture and the cataloguing of the world. The garden, city, theatre, and collection can all function as ordered spatial schemas. This does not prove that Riccio’s ideal garden was literally a fully encoded memory palace, but it shows continuity between his mnemonic and natural-historical methods.

### Carruthers, Small, Hajdu, Ong, Bonner, Fidora, Sturlese, Ciliberto, Clucas, Gatti, Stagno

These scholars provide necessary comparative frameworks but, in the materials located, do not substantially revise the codicology or detailed operation of Riccio’s manuscript.

- Mary Carruthers establishes medieval memory as compositional, ethical, and inventive rather than rote storage.
- Jocelyn Penny Small stresses the material and cognitive interaction of writing, visualization, and recollection.
- Helga Hajdu’s earlier history remains important for classical and medieval techniques.
- Walter Ong explains Ramist spatial order as a reorganization of discourse that normally suppresses the emotionally charged mnemonic image.
- Anthony Bonner and Alexander Fidora distinguish genuine Lullian combinatorial method from Renaissance pseudo-Lullism.
- Rita Sturlese, Michele Ciliberto, Stephen Clucas, and Hilary Gatti complicate strongly “Hermetic” readings of Bruno and insist on textual, logical, philosophical, and historical specificity.
- Lina Bolzoni and Lucia Stagno’s work on Camillo and visual culture helps prevent treating every theatre, diagram, or emblem as the same mnemonic technology.

**[ABSENCE]** No post-1966 scholar located attributes Riccio’s treatise to another author, argues seriously for a date other than 1595, identifies a lost printed edition, or produces evidence that Riccio was prosecuted for this memory art.

## History and context

### Classical rhetoric

Riccio’s immediate technical ancestry is the classical art of places and images known through:

- pseudo-Cicero, *Rhetorica ad Herennium* III.16–24;
- Cicero, *De oratore* II.350–360;
- Quintilian, *Institutio oratoria* XI.2.

The classical scheme supplies:

- natural versus artificial memory;
- ordered, visually distinct loci;
- images attached to things or words;
- forward traversal;
- sufficiently vivid and differentiated places;
- practice and review.

Riccio’s compact-room experiments resemble Quintilian’s house-route, while his church and city routes extend it to institutional and urban scale.

### Scholastic psychology and Dominican memory

Albert the Great and Thomas Aquinas incorporated trained recollection into prudence and Aristotelian faculty psychology. Medieval Dominican practice legitimated corporeal similitudes as aids through which the mind retrieves intellectual and moral matter.

Riccio’s sermons, Beatitudes, apostles, Job, saints, virtues, Thomas’s *Summa*, convents, and daily preaching calendar belong securely to this lineage.

### Franciscan, Lullian, and combinatorial traditions

Riccio’s circle and zodiac can look superficially “Lullian,” but the difference is structural:

- Lullian wheels combine letters and divine dignities to investigate necessary relations.
- Riccio’s bodily circuit is a static serial index.
- No rotating disks, combinatorial chambers, or logical production occur.
- No dependence on a specific Lull text is demonstrated.

The Franciscan/Lullian tradition is therefore context, not a documented source.

### Hermeticism

**[ABSENCE]** Riccio does not invoke Hermes Trismegistus, Ficinian spiritus, planetary demons, talismanic capture of celestial power, Cabala, or ascent through astral images.

The cosmic-body diagrams share common cosmological imagery with Hermetic and astrological works, but shared imagery does not prove shared magical operation.

### Ramism

Riccio’s alphabetic and taxonomic lists belong to the broader sixteenth-century pressure toward ordered knowledge. Yet he retains artificial images and traversed places—precisely what Ramist method tended to replace with dialectical division and visible outline. He is therefore adjacent to, not an instance of, Ramism.

### Universal knowledge and collecting

Riccio repeatedly promises *universalità*. His universality means having enough differentiated repositories to command many fields in conversation, preaching, and study. The royal palace, Gaddi gallery, stone lists, plant lists, and botanical Piazza circuit link memory with the late-Medicean collection and cabinet.

This is a modest universal-language project only by analogy. He supplies expandable vernacular dictionaries of images but no universally valid symbolic grammar.

### Institutions

The system’s institutional geography is unusually concrete:

- Dominican convents at Santa Maria Novella and San Marco;
- Franciscan Santa Croce;
- Augustinian Santo Spirito;
- Carmelite Carmine;
- Vallombrosan Santa Trinita;
- Servite Annunziata;
- Florentine streets, monasteries, hospitals, workshops, gardens, galleries, and government buildings;
- sermon cycles and Lenten preaching;
- university or conventual lecture repetition;
- Medici collections and natural-history workshops.

The city itself becomes an index of church, court, craft, gender, charity, government, and natural knowledge.

## People

### Agostino del Riccio

**[DOCUMENT]** Born in Florence in 1541, originally named Pietro, son of Francesco del Riccio. The Dominican vestition record says he received the habit on 27 September 1558, aged seventeen, and made solemn profession on 8 September 1559.

He spent time at San Marco, then at the sanctuary of Madonna della Quercia near Viterbo, and later lived at Santa Maria Novella. He worked with the convent’s lands and gardens and cultivated interests in agriculture, botany, medicine, stones, architecture, and art.

The Santa Maria Novella chronicle records his death on 18 December 1598 and states that his likeness appeared in the great cloister’s scene of the death of St Dominic.

### Niccolò Gaddi

Collector and patron whose gallery provided Riccio with memorable sculptural forms. His role is documented by Riccio’s reference and independent art-historical study.

### Francesco Panigarola

**[TRADITION in Riccio]** Riccio says the celebrated preacher possessed one hundred thousand loci and, according to further report, ten thousand more. The wording—*dicono*, “they say”—marks hearsay.

Panigarola’s own *Regole per far la memoria locale*, composed approximately 1575–1578, places him in the same practical Counter-Reformation mnemonic culture. The exact total of 100,000 or 110,000 loci remains unverified.

### Riccio’s companion and child pupil

**[TEXTUAL ANECDOTE]** One companion allegedly memorized a book in an hour; a small boy allegedly astonished learned audiences despite scarcely being able to read the material he recited.

**Evidence status:** authorial testimonial only. No name, dated performance record, or independent corroboration was found.

### Giuseppo Fiammingo

Riccio praises the gardener and herbalist Giuseppe Fiammingo for bringing many flowers to Florence. He connects mnemonic conversation at banquets with the botanical wealth encouraged under Grand Duke Francesco I.

### Patrons and implied users

The dedicatees are Florentine literary youth, but the actual user community includes preachers, prelates, confessors, students, grammarians, soldiers, artisans, nobles, poor people, and social diners.

### The requested stock anecdotes

The auctioneer, professor’s party, amnesiac student, and accused magician are not documented episodes in Riccio’s treatise or its known reception. They belong to other memory-art dossiers or later mnemonic folklore. Assigning them to Riccio would be a modern conflation.

## The magic question

### Was Riccio’s system magical?

The strongest answer is: **the surviving text provides no evidence that Riccio thought it magical or talismanic.**

Reasons:

- Places work through familiarity, illumination, stability, spacing, and order.
- Images work through resemblance, phonetic pun, vividness, emotion, personal association, and repetition.
- Retrieval works by forward and reverse traversal.
- The zodiac supplies a conventional order.
- The elements and spheres are objects of memorization, not invoked powers.
- Practice is explicitly divided into timed doses.
- The examples are doctrinally orthodox, practical, botanical, civic, and social.

### Astrology versus astral magic

The zodiacal body is based on melothesia—the traditional assignment of signs to bodily regions. Such imagery could appear in astrological, medical, mnemonic, artistic, or magical contexts. Context determines function.

Here the signs are ordinal markers. No operation, prayer, election, planetary hour, consecration, spirit, talisman, or causal astral influx is specified.

### Yates’s verdict

Yates says “no suspicion need be attached” to the zodiac and calls the cosmic-body exercise “puerile, but not magical.” This negative conclusion is supported by the text. Her evaluative term “puerile” is not an evidentiary finding.

### Heresy and Inquisition

**[ABSENCE]**

- No accusation of magic or heresy against Riccio was found.
- No Inquisition proceeding concerning the *Arte della memoria locale* was found.
- No censorship, prohibition, confiscation, or Index listing was found.
- No evidence links the treatise to the *Ars notoria*.

A 1569 conventual record restricted Riccio from celebrating public Mass because of excessive prolixity in pronouncing the words of consecration, which caused scandal, astonishment, and irritation. This is a genuine disciplinary document, but it concerns liturgical conduct—not magic, mnemonic practice, or heresy.

### Bruno as a misleading shadow

Bruno was accused and executed for a complex body of theological and philosophical positions. The fact that both men were Dominicans and used memory images does not transfer Bruno’s inquisitorial history to Riccio.

Yates’s contrast—Riccio’s tame Dominican mnemonic versus Bruno’s magicized seals—is heuristic. It is not evidence that Riccio’s system was investigated and cleared by authorities.

## Afterlife

### Direct reception

**[ABSENCE]** No named line of pupils, printed transmission, early translation, or demonstrable adoption of Riccio’s particular seven-rule court allegory was found.

His memory treatise survived principally as a manuscript. His natural-history works were better known to eighteenth- and nineteenth-century Tuscan scholars.

### Modern rediscovery

A tentative chronology is:

- 1722: Giovanni Negri briefly notices Riccio as a Florentine writer.
- 1751 onward: Giovanni Targioni-Tozzetti uses Riccio’s natural-history work.
- 1845: Vincenzo Marchese discusses Dominican artists and writers.
- 1920s: Ernst Kris brings Riccio into study of Renaissance stonework and rustic style.
- 1955: Yates mentions the manuscript and the Bruno comparison in “The Ciceronian Art of Memory.”
- 1958–60s: Paolo Rossi places Riccio within the wider history of mnemonic systems.
- 1966: Yates’s *Art of Memory* gives the system its most influential English-language description.
- 1979–96: Barocchi, Heikamp, Gnoli, and Sironi reconstruct Riccio’s art, stone, garden, and natural-history environment.
- 1984–2001: Bolzoni develops the memory/collection interpretation.
- 2001–07: Kuwakino, Brunon, and Benzi treat his garden, encyclopedism, and mnemonic architecture in detail.
- 2007: Benzi publishes the item-specific study and electronic transcription.
- 2021–24: scholarship on stones, collections, and mnemonic ekphrasis continues to use Riccio as a local, practical case.

### Art, fresco, and emblem

Riccio’s virtues in Santa Maria Novella fit a longer tradition in which church architecture, fresco cycles, personifications, and moral classifications reinforced recollection. But no surviving fresco has been shown to have been painted from his treatise.

His lost or unlocated book of *imprese*, if it existed as described, would strengthen the emblematic connection. **[ABSENCE]** Since the text has not been found, its contents cannot be reconstructed.

### Dante, Borges, Eco, and modern memory palaces

These are comparative afterlives of the art of memory generally, not documented effects of Riccio:

- Dante’s ordered moral and cosmological spaces predate him.
- Borges’s Funes represents involuntary total recall, almost the opposite of selective mnemonic ordering.
- Eco’s semiotic and encyclopedic interests belong to a modern intellectual reception of combinatorics and memory.
- Contemporary memory palaces reproduce Riccio’s central mechanism: fixed routes, distinctive loci, personally vivid images, deliberate retrieval.
- Memory athletes use comparable spatial encoding but are not known to descend specifically from Riccio.

### Computing and combinatorics

Riccio’s system resembles computer addressing only as a **[MODERN ANALOGY]**:

- locus ≈ address;
- image ≈ retrieval key;
- route ≈ index order;
- multiple convents ≈ separate workspaces;
- permanent versus swept loci ≈ long-term versus reusable storage;
- alphabetic lists ≈ extensible lookup tables.

The historically documented “Lull to Leibniz to machine” line concerns formal combination and symbolic calculus. Riccio’s art is a serial storage-and-retrieval discipline, not a calculating machine. Connecting him directly to Leibniz or computing would overstate the evidence.

## Controversies and disputes

### 1. Seven or eight illustrations?

- **Yates:** seven drawings.
- **Modern transcription:** introductory king plus seven numbered rules, with illustration headings reaching “eighth illustration.”
- **Finding:** unresolved without examining the codex’s page layout, but eight illustrated units are reconstructable from the transcription.

### 2. Plate 13 labels

- 13c is the Second Counsellor/Images.
- 13d is the Second Captain/Circular Line.
- 13e is the Third Captain/Transverse Line.
- The catalogue’s “Third Captain, plate 13c” is a modern copying error.
- No Yates plate is assigned to the First Captain/Straight Line.

### 3. Twelve, 27, or 80 Via della Scala places?

All three numbers have different referents:

- twelve: objects visible in Yates’s cropped/reproduced plate;
- 27: deposits in the written worked example;
- over 80: Riccio’s claim for his normal full route.

### 4. Six or seven miseries of man?

Riccio explicitly enumerates six clauses from Job 14:1–2. Yates’s “seven miseries” is an error.

### 5. Conservative or encyclopedic?

- **Yates:** traditional, enfeebled, unaware of Renaissance transformations.
- **Bolzoni/Benzi/Kuwakino/Brunon:** technically traditional but culturally intertwined with collecting, visual alphabets, gardens, natural history, and universal knowledge.
- **Finding:** the later interpretation explains more of the manuscript. Traditional mechanism and encyclopedic purpose are compatible.

### 6. Did Riccio model Bruno?

Chronology excludes direct influence from Riccio’s 1595 manuscript upon Bruno’s 1583 *Seals*. Yates proposes a shared Dominican mode of instruction. No common immediate source or conventual curriculum document has yet been identified.

### 7. Was Riccio’s zodiac magical?

No operative magic is present. Calling all zodiacal imagery magical would erase distinctions among cosmology, medicine, astrology, mnemonics, and talismanic practice.

### 8. Is “local memory” only spatial?

No. Riccio adds:

- personally variable image selection;
- semantic and phonetic association;
- numerical marking;
- forward and backward retrieval;
- temporal spacing;
- multiple repositories to prevent interference;
- periodic review;
- categorical organization for permanent knowledge.

### 9. Reliability of feats

Panigarola’s 100,000 places, the one-hour book, the nearly illiterate child prodigy, and the promise of 200 concepts a day are promotional or exemplary claims. They are valuable evidence for expectations surrounding mnemonic virtuosity, not verified performance measurements.

## Open questions

1. Does direct inspection of BNCF II.I.13 confirm eight drawings, or is one numbered “illustration” merely a rubric without an independent image?
2. What are the exact dimensions, quire structure, hands, watermarks, inks, and stages of revision?
3. Are all images by Riccio, by a collaborator, or by more than one hand?
4. Does f. 35 really repeat “left shoulder,” or is this Yates’s mistranscription?
5. What is the complete mapping of zodiac signs to bodily loci at f. 33?
6. Can the Via della Scala route be reconstructed against late-sixteenth-century cadastral records?
7. Which of its landmarks, shops, tabernacles, and convent doors survive archaeologically?
8. Are the Ciodi and Gaddi altars identified consistently in contemporary plans of Santa Maria Novella?
9. Does Riccio borrow verbally from Panigarola, Rosselli, Romberch, Dolce, Publicius, or another vernacular mnemonic manual?
10. Who is the unnamed “famous writer” whose successful local memory Riccio praises—Cosmas Rosselli or someone else?
11. Can Riccio’s companion or child pupil be identified from conventual, household, or court records?
12. Can the lost treatise on *imprese* be located in an uncatalogued or differently attributed manuscript?
13. Does the electronic transcription include every cancelled phrase, marginal symbol, correction, and graphic relation?
14. Was the treatise intended for presentation to a named Florentine household or academy?
15. How did Riccio’s permanent repositories interact in practice with his manuscript “universal tables” of plants and stones?
16. Did later Tuscan naturalists know the mnemonic treatise, or only the agricultural and lithological manuscripts?
