### Evidentiary key

- **[Text]** directly documented in a primary source.
- **[Reconstruction]** a conclusion drawn by modern scholarship from primary evidence.
- **[Yates]** Frances Yates’s interpretation.
- **[Tradition]** a report circulating among early witnesses but not independently demonstrated.
- **[Disputed]** an attribution, genealogy, or interpretation challenged in scholarship.
- **[Legend]** a story unsupported or substantially embellished.
- **[Modern analogy]** a retrospective comparison, not evidence of direct historical descent.
- **[Negative finding]** something sought but not attested in the relevant Ramist evidence.

## The system in detail

### 1. What Ramus removed from rhetoric

Classical Latin rhetoric normally recognized five functions:

1. *inventio*, finding arguments;
2. *dispositio*, arranging them;
3. *elocutio*, verbal style;
4. *memoria*, memorization;
5. *pronuntiatio* or *actio*, delivery.

**[Text]** Ramus and Talon redistributed these operations. Dialectic received *inventio* and *iudicium/dispositio*; rhetoric was confined largely to style and delivery. There was no independent Ramist book of mnemonic precepts corresponding to Book III of the *Rhetorica ad Herennium*.

**[Correction]** It is therefore imprecise to say simply that Ramus “abolished memory.” He abolished *memoria* as a separate rhetorical art and the familiar technical apparatus of arbitrary places and images. He did not deny the importance of retaining knowledge. His dialectic repeatedly explains how correct disposition assists or “comprehends” memory.

Annarita Angelini’s recent formulation is particularly exact: memory “loses its identity as an autonomous discipline and is entirely absorbed by the doctrine of method.” This corrects the stronger meaning often attached to Yates’s word “abolished.” [Angelini, “Memory as shadow of the method”](https://gal-studies.museogalileo.it/index.php/galilaeana/article/download/45/37/686).

### 2. The positive replacement: division and composition

In the *Scholae rhetoricae*, Book XIX, Ramus accepts what he takes to be the sound part of Quintilian’s teaching:

> **“Ars memoriae … tota posita est in divisione et compositione.”**  
> “The art of memory is wholly situated in division and composition.”

He then asks which discipline supplies an art of ordering, dividing, and composing things. His answer is dialectic:

> **“Vera igitur memoriae ars, et Dialecticae propria omnino est.”**  
> “Therefore the true art of memory belongs wholly and properly to dialectic.”

**[Text]** This occurs in the *Scholae rhetoricae*, XIX, in the posthumous *Scholae in liberales artes*, Basel, 1578, col. 309. Yates translates and discusses it on pp. 232–33.

**[Source qualification]** Ramus is adapting rather than merely quoting Quintilian. Quintilian, *Institutio oratoria* XI.2.36, says that division and composition greatly assist memory. He does not identify memory without remainder with dialectic. Ramus turns a practical observation into a disciplinary claim.

### 3. Memory as the shadow of disposition

The 1543 *Dialecticae institutiones* contains two important formulations:

> **“dispositionis umbra quaedam est memoria”**  
> “Memory is, as it were, the shadow of disposition.”

And:

> **“iudicii gradus perinde ac umbra lucente sole corpus sequitur”**  
> “[Memory] follows the grades of judgment as a shadow follows a body when the sun shines.”

**[Text]** *Dialecticae institutiones*, fols. 43r–v, as identified and transcribed by Angelini.

The metaphor is crucial. A shadow:

- has no independent ordering power;
- reproduces the articulation of the body it follows;
- appears wherever the light of reason illuminates a properly disposed subject.

Memory therefore does not determine the order. Correct judgment or disposition determines it, and recollection retraces it.

### 4. “Natural” and “artificial” do not mean what they first appear to mean

Ramus distinguishes natural rational capacity from the acquired discipline that guides it. Method is an *artificialis methodus* or learned habit, but it is “natural” in the stronger sense that it claims to follow the real order of things and the mind’s native rational movement.

**[Text]** In the French *Dialectique*:

> **“Toute vraye doctrine et science doibt proceder par des choses generalles, et descendre degrez à degrez aux specialles.”**  
> “Every true doctrine and science must proceed from general things and descend degree by degree to particulars.”

The standard critical edition is Michel Dassonville’s *Dialectique (1555)*, Geneva: Droz, 1964, around pp. 121–23.

This produces a characteristic sequence:

```text
subject or art
├── first principal part
│   ├── subordinate kind
│   │   ├── species
│   │   └── species
│   └── subordinate kind
└── second principal part
    ├── subordinate kind
    └── subordinate kind
```

The bifurcation is visually conspicuous, but **the diagram is not identical with method**. Method is the ordered relation of propositions; the branching table displays that relation. Modern historians sometimes conflate the two. The current *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy* entry explicitly cautions against doing so. [SEP, “Petrus Ramus,” §3.4](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/win2024/entries/ramus/).

### 5. The laws governing a proper art

In mature Ramism an art should obey three celebrated requirements:

- **Lex veritatis**: every precept must be true and necessary.
- **Lex iustitiae**: each art must contain only what properly belongs to its subject; material belonging to another discipline must be removed.
- **Lex sapientiae**: universal and more general propositions precede subordinate and more particular ones.

**[Reconstruction]** These laws explain the treatment of memory. Since memory is not peculiar to ornate speech, the law of justice excludes it from rhetoric. Since mnemonic success depends on ordered propositions, its proper assistance belongs to dialectical disposition. Since general rules should precede particular members, the law of wisdom fixes the direction of pedagogical presentation.

### 6. What functions as “places”

Ramus rejects the architectural places of the classical technique but writes:

> **“veros locos pro rerum gradibus et generibus distinctos”**  
> “true places distinguished according to the grades and genera of things.”

**[Text]** In his attack on artificial memory in the dialectical *Scholae*, cited in Angelini, Ramus contrasts:

- artificial, external signs and simulacra;
- the order inherent in things;
- “true places,” meaning structural grades and genera;
- “truest images,” meaning the things or concepts themselves.

Thus the Ramist analogue of a locus is a position in a taxonomy or argument:

```text
animal
→ human
→ Greek
→ Socrates
```

Retrieval means restoring the item by following its intelligible address in that order, not mentally walking through a building.

**[Caution]** This is “local memory” only in an analogical or topical sense. Walter Ong called the whole Ramist organization of the arts “a system of local memory,” and Yates adopted the phrase. Ramus himself expressly opposes the ordinary mnemonic *loci et imagines*.

### 7. What functions as “images”

Ramus says:

> **“rerumque verissimas imagines adhibemus”**  
> “we employ the truest images of the things.”

This does not reinstate pictorial memory. It is a polemical redefinition: the thing rightly conceived is a truer representation than an invented grotesque substitute.

There are consequently three different senses of “image” that must not be confused:

1. **Classical mnemonic image:** an imagined actor, object, injury, costume, or action installed in a place.
2. **Ramist conceptual image:** the true conception or representation of the thing itself.
3. **Page image:** the visual impression of the bracketed epitome.

Only the second and third remain.

### 8. How order is fixed

Ramist order is determined through *analysis*:

1. Determine the subject or question.
2. Gather arguments through dialectical invention.
3. distinguish what is essential from what belongs accidentally or to another art.
4. formulate propositions or axioms.
5. group only homogeneous propositions.
6. rank them by clarity and generality.
7. descend continuously from genus to species, general to special.
8. expose the completed arrangement in a linear or branching epitome.
9. learn the matter in that same order.

The mature definition states:

> **“Methodus est dianoia variorum axiomatum homogeneorum … unde … memoriaque comprehenditur.”**  
> “Method is the reasoning arrangement of diverse homogeneous axioms … through which their agreement is judged and memory comprehends them.”

**[Text]** *Dialecticae libri duo*, late form, pp. 72–73 in editions cited by Angelini.

### 9. How retrieval works

Ramus supplies no separate algorithm comparable to “walk from the doorway clockwise and inspect each image.”

The reconstructable retrieval procedure is instead:

- recall the most general proposition;
- recall its first division;
- choose the relevant branch;
- descend through successive subordinate divisions;
- recover the specific proposition from its structural relation to its superiors and siblings;
- reverse direction where explanation or analysis requires ascent from particular to general.

**[Reconstruction]** Because every member is relationally located, forgetting one item need not destroy the entire sequence: its parent category and coordinate members may regenerate its place.

**[Negative finding]** Ramus gives no rules about locus size, illumination, intervals, human scale, five-place markers, bizarre clothing, blood, sexual excitation, or emotional intensity. Those belong to the local-image tradition he rejects.

### 10. Ramus’s alphabet analogy

A 1566 prelection compares methodical order to alphabetic sequence: if propositions bore the marks *a, b, c*, one would place the *a*-marked item first, *b* second, and continue similarly.

**[Text]** *Praelectiones* associated with *Dialecticae libri duo* (1566), p. 374, quoted by Angelini.

The analogy stresses:

- seriality;
- stable relative position;
- uninterrupted progression;
- independence from an imagined building.

### 11. The chain

Ramus repeatedly represents method as an unbroken chain whose links bind propositions:

> **“nil aptius, nil compactius, nil firmius”**  
> “nothing could be more fitted, compact, or firm.”

The image ultimately invokes Homer’s golden chain between heaven and earth.

**[Text]** Chain imagery is present in Ramus’s discussions of method.

**[Yates]** Yates interprets it as evidence that Ramist method is not merely utilitarian pedagogy but a Neoplatonic ascent and descent between particulars, universals, human mind, and divine reason.

**[Qualification]** The image does support metaphysical and religious readings. It does not by itself prove membership in an esoteric *prisca theologia*, initiation, operative magic, or dependence on Hermetic texts.

## The worked example: Penelope’s complaint

### 1. Identification

The first printed Ramist epitome is found at the end of the *Dialecticae institutiones* of September 1543, fol. 57, continuing into fol. 58.

It analyzes the beginning of Ovid’s *Heroides* I, Penelope’s letter to Ulysses. Yates describes it as a scheme for learning twenty-eight verses.

The opening is:

> **“Haec tua Penelope lento tibi mittit, Ulixe; / nil mihi rescribas attamen: ipse veni.”**  
> “Your Penelope sends this to you, slow-returning Ulysses; write nothing back to me—come yourself.”

### 2. What the diagram looks like

**[Text/object]** The surviving 1543 printing presents the analysis as a typographical epitome:

- verbal headings stand to one side;
- braces or brackets extend from inclusive propositions;
- successive subdivisions move laterally and vertically;
- coordinated members appear on the same rank;
- subordinate members are indented;
- the whole makes the argumentative relation of the verses visible at a glance.

It is not:

- a circular wheel;
- a memory palace;
- an emblematic plate;
- an allegorical figure of Penelope;
- a pictorial representation of Troy or Ithaca.

The poem’s complaint is dissolved into its dialectical argument. The learner is supposed to remember the verses by understanding and rehearsing this disposition.

### 3. What the example demonstrates

**[Text plus reconstruction]**

- Poetry, no less than logic or theology, is analyzable into arguments.
- Memorization follows argumentative structure.
- The printed page externalizes that structure.
- The same structural pattern can then be internalized.
- The verse’s rhythm and imagery are not themselves analyzed as mnemonic agents.

Yates perceptively asks what has happened to Ovid’s rhythm and poetic imagery. The answer is that the epitome privileges propositional sequence over the poem’s acoustic and imagistic organization.

### 4. Limits of the evidence

No surviving classroom record proves exactly how a particular pupil recited the scheme. Ong reconstructs its pedagogical purpose from Ramus’s directions and the textbook form. Yates follows Ong.

It is reasonable to infer oral rehearsal, but the diagram itself does not document:

- how many repetitions were required;
- whether pupils copied it;
- whether teachers pointed to branches;
- whether the table was retained as a visual afterimage;
- whether every word or chiefly the argument was recalled.

## Comparison with the classical artificial memory

The *Rhetorica ad Herennium*’s celebrated lawsuit image encodes a legal case through a sick man, a cup, tablets, a ram’s testicles, and related visual cues. It exemplifies violent or extraordinary *imagines agentes*.

Ramus supplies no comparable picture. His direct answer to the older system is:

> **“externis et commentitiis … signis et simulachris”**  
> “external and fabricated signs and simulacra.”

He complains that image systems:

- impose artificial signs on memory;
- multiply forms without limit when words are encoded;
- confuse recollection;
- substitute invented associations for the order inherent in the subject.

The Ramist equivalent of the lawsuit image would be a structured division of the case:

```text
legal question
├── fact
│   ├── illness/death
│   └── alleged poison
├── persons
│   ├── victim
│   ├── accused
│   └── witnesses/heirs
├── evidence
│   ├── cup
│   ├── document
│   └── testimony
└── issue
    ├── motive
    └── proof
```

This illustration is a **modern reconstruction**, not a Ramus diagram.

## The sources

### A. Ramus’s own works

#### 1. *Dialecticae partitiones*, Paris, 1543

- Printer: Jacques Bogard.
- Ramus’s earliest printed reconstruction of dialectic.
- Divides dialectic initially into *natura*, *doctrina*, and *exercitatio*.
- Contains no worked literary examples comparable to the subsequent *Institutiones*.
- Ong calls it the first printed edition in the sequence, although Jacques Charpentier later numbered the *Institutiones* as a “second edition.”

**[Bibliographical complication]** “First” and “second” edition can refer either to distinct titles or to successive states of Ramus’s evolving dialectic.

#### 2. *Dialecticae institutiones*, Paris, September 1543

Full title:

*Petri Rami Veromandui Dialecticae institutiones, ad celeberrimam et illustrissimam Lutetiae Parisiorum Academiam.*

- Paris: Jacques Bogard.
- Octavo.
- Fifty-eight folios.
- Fol. 13 is reportedly misnumbered 31.
- The Penelope epitome appears at fol. 57.
- The polemic against places and images continues at fols. 57v–58r.
- Memory as the shadow of judgment occurs around fols. 43r–v.
- A final dichotomous conspectus is the earliest known printed Ramist dialectical table.

The BnF’s BP16 record distinguishes two states: in state B the title page was reset and passages printed in italic in the other state were changed to roman. [BnF BP16 record](https://bp16.bnf.fr/ark:/12148/cb43984489r/).

#### 3. Manuscript transcript of the 1543 *Institutiones*

**[Text/object]** Vatican City, Biblioteca Apostolica Vaticana, MS Ottob. lat. 2172:

- late-sixteenth-century manuscript;
- approximately 28.5 × 19 cm;
- five preliminary and fifty-seven numbered leaves;
- text concludes on fol. 55v;
- remaining leaves blank;
- catchwords imitate printed-book practice;
- a second hand added scattered objections.

**[Conclusion]** Ong judged it a transcript from a printed copy, not an authorial manuscript and not evidence that the 1543 work originated in this form.

#### 4. Earlier illuminated manuscript associated with the *Partitiones*

Ong reports an illuminated manuscript in the Bibliothèque nationale de France titled *Petri Rami Dialecticae partitiones ad Franciscum Valesium Christianissimum Gallorum regem*.

**[Reconstruction]** Ong considered this possibly closer to the earliest state of the work than the first printed edition.

**[Caution]** A firm shelfmark and full modern codicological description were not established in the accessible records consulted here. It should not be confused with the Vatican transcript.

#### 5. *Dialectici commentarii tres*, Paris, 1544

- Published under Audomarus Talon’s name while Ramus was prohibited from teaching or writing dialectic.
- First version to give the later dialectic something close to its lasting structure.
- First to treat method explicitly as such.
- Introduces centered headings and blocked spatial organization.
- Three divisions: invention, disposition, exercise.

**[Attribution]** Substantially Ramist and probably produced under Ramus’s direction; title-page attribution to Talon is not a reliable measure of sole authorship. Ramus and Talon routinely collaborated and exchanged texts.

#### 6. *Rhetoricae distinctiones in Quintilianum*, Paris, 1549

This is Ramus’s extended attack on Quintilian’s allocation of dialectical material to rhetoric. It is indispensable for understanding why invention, arrangement, and memory cannot remain rhetorical in his disciplinary economy.

Modern text and translation: Carole Newlands, “Arguments in Rhetoric against Quintilian: Translation and Text of Peter Ramus’s *Rhetoricae distinctiones in Quintilianum*,” *Humanistica Lovaniensia* 24 (1975).

#### 7. *Dialectique*, Paris, 1555

- French vernacular recasting of Ramus’s dialectic.
- Printed by André Wechel.
- Important for the mature formula of descent from generals to specials.
- Critical edition: Michel Dassonville, Geneva: Droz, 1964.
- A later scholarly edition, *Dialectique 1555: un manifeste de la Pléiade*, appeared in Paris in 1996.

The French edition makes method available outside a wholly Latin university readership and is an important episode in vernacular intellectual standardization.

#### 8. *Dialecticae libri duo*

The two-book Latin dialectic repeatedly changed during Ramus’s lifetime and after his death. Its two great parts are:

- *inventio*;
- *iudicium* or *dispositio*.

Within disposition lie:

- axiomatic judgment;
- syllogism;
- method.

The concise textbook and the much larger *Scholae* serve different purposes: the former supplies precepts; the latter supplies historical criticism, argument, commentary, and justification.

#### 9. *Scholae in liberales artes*, Basel, 1578

- Basel: Episcopius and heirs of Nicolaus Episcopius.
- 1,182 columns.
- Surviving digitized copy: Augsburg Staats- und Stadtbibliothek, shelfmark **2 Phil 78**.
- The *Scholae rhetoricae*, Book XIX, col. 309, contains the decisive “true art of memory” passage.
- The dialectical *Scholae* contain the fullest polemic against *loci et imagines* and substitute real genera, grades, and things.

This is a posthumous collection. It should not be cited as though Ramus personally supervised the 1578 physical edition, though its component lectures and discussions represent his teaching.

#### 10. *Commentariorum de religione Christiana libri quatuor*

- First edition: Frankfurt: Andreas Wechel, 1576.
- BSB shelfmark: **Dogm. 812**.
- VD16 **L 463**.
- BSB digital object **bsb00029791**.
- Some catalogues and Yates cite a Frankfurt 1577 edition or issue.

**[Correction]** The item-specific brief’s *De religione Christiana* (1577) should be normalized to the full title and differentiated from the demonstrable 1576 Frankfurt first edition.

### B. Ancient sources

#### Quintilian, *Institutio oratoria* XI.2

Ramus’s proximate classical warrant. Significant sections include:

- XI.2.4: hesitation about assigning a distinct “art” to memory;
- XI.2.11–22: Simonides and the local art;
- XI.2.23–26: limited utility of storing material objects;
- XI.2.27–29: disadvantages of elaborate images;
- XI.2.32: visualization of the written tablet/page;
- XI.2.36: division and composition;
- XI.2.40–44: repetition, delivery, and learning by parts.

Standard current edition:

Donald A. Russell, ed. and trans., *The Orator’s Education*, Loeb Classical Library, 5 vols., Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press, 2001; Book XI in vol. 5.

An accessible Latin text and the older H. E. Butler Loeb translation are available through LacusCurtius. [Quintilian XI.2](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Quintilian/Institutio_Oratoria/11B%2A.html).

#### *Rhetorica ad Herennium* III.16–24

The canonical Latin exposition of natural and artificial memory, places, images for things and words, and the rules for striking images.

Standard edition:

Harry Caplan, ed. and trans., *Rhetorica ad Herennium*, Loeb Classical Library 403, Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press, 1954.

Ramus rejects this tradition’s technical center while retaining its insistence on order.

#### Cicero

- *De oratore* II.350–360: Simonides, places, images, visual cognition.
- *De inventione* II: the relation of memory to prudence inherited by scholastic writers.

#### Aristotle

- *De memoria et reminiscentia*: order, association, and recollection as a search from a starting point.
- *Posterior Analytics*: demonstrative organization and generality.
- *Physics* I.1, 184a10–22: the disputed relation between what is better known to us and better known by nature.

Ramus claimed ancient and natural warrant while rejecting the accumulated scholastic Aristotelian curriculum.

### C. Lullist comparison

Ramon Llull’s authentic Art is a combinatorial and demonstrative system founded on principles such as the divine dignities, not originally a local-image mnemonic. Relevant textual families include the *Ars generalis ultima*, *Ars brevis*, and works of ascent and descent.

Yates cites the *Liber ad memoriam confirmandam* for division into generals and specials.

**[Textual warning]** The authenticity and dating of works in the vast pseudo-Lullian corpus must be checked individually in the Universitat de Barcelona’s Ramon Llull Database. A title’s attribution to “Raymond Lull” in an early printing is not sufficient evidence of authorship.

Standard modern orientation:

- Anthony Bonner, *The Art and Logic of Ramon Llull: A User’s Guide*, Leiden: Brill, 2007.
- Alexander Fidora and Josep E. Rubio, eds., *Raimundus Lullus: An Introduction to His Life, Works and Thought*, Turnhout: Brepols, 2008.
- *Raimundi Lulli Opera Latina* series.

### D. The Lullist diagram adduced by Yates

Paris, Bibliothèque nationale de France, **MS lat. 15450**, Thomas Le Myésier’s Lullist compendium, especially fol. 99v.

Yates says its heavily bracketed organization resembles later Ramist epitomes.

**[Documented comparison]** The visual resemblance is real.

**[Disputed genealogy]** Resemblance does not prove that Ramus copied this manuscript or any particular Lullist exemplar. Bracketed division also belongs to broader manuscript, scholastic, Porphyrian, rhetorical, and pedagogical traditions.

## Diagrams, tables, wheels, and plates

### Ramus’s own visual apparatus

The Ramist corpus uses:

- synoptic tables;
- braces and brackets;
- branching divisions;
- centered headings;
- indentation;
- coordinated typographical blocks;
- general tables of an art.

It does not characteristically use:

- rotating volvelles;
- concentric combinatorial wheels;
- astrological sectors;
- architectural theatres;
- personified mnemonic figures;
- talismanic seals.

### The 1543 general table

Ong identifies the table at fol. 57 as the first in the long series of printed Ramist dichotomies. Its visual grammar is:

- one inclusive head;
- a first division;
- two coordinated branches where the matter permits;
- repeated secondary division;
- terminal members placed at the outer or lower ends of brackets.

**[Correction]** Ramus did not require that reality always divide into exactly two ontological species. Binary display was a favored expository instrument. Later “Ramist trees” could become more rigid than Ramus’s actual practice.

### Savigny’s *Tableaux accomplis de tous les arts libéraux* (1587)

Christophe de Savigny’s folio encyclopedia contains sixteen paired treatments:

- a prose “partition” arranged in decreasing generality;
- a facing “tableau” showing the partitions diagrammatically.

Its disciplines include grammar, rhetoric, dialectic, arithmetic, geometry, optics, music, cosmography, astronomy, geography, physics, medicine, ethics, jurisprudence, history, and theology.

The dialectic tableau is revealing:

- an open book forms the trunk;
- two branches stand for invention and disposition/judgment;
- unlike other tableaux, it lacks a dense repertory of disciplinary symbols;
- an inscription says that memory follows dialectical order as a shadow follows bodies when the sun shines.

**[Reconstruction]** Angelini interprets the nearly imageless dialectic tableau as both a declaration of Ramist dematerialization and evidence that first-generation Ramists still needed an encyclopedic visible structure.

**[Attribution disputed]** “Christophe de Savigny” remains biographically obscure; the possibility of a pseudonym or collaborative production has not been excluded.

### No Ramist wheels

**[Negative finding]** There is no Ramist counterpart to Llull’s rotating figures or Bruno’s concentric wheels. Later authors combined Ramism, Lullism, Aristotelianism, and Brunian devices, but those hybrids should not be projected backward into Ramus’s 1543 system.

## Variants across editions

Ramus treated his textbooks as revisable teaching instruments. The following changes matter:

| Stage | Structure and mnemonic significance |
|---|---|
| *Partitiones* (1543) | Dialectic divided into nature, doctrine, exercise; no worked examples |
| *Institutiones* (1543) | Expanded examples; Penelope analysis; earliest printed dichotomous conspectus; explicit memory language |
| *Dialectici commentarii* (1544) | Invention, disposition, exercise; method explicitly treated; more spatially blocked page |
| Later *Institutiones* and *Scholae* | Dialectic reorganized and repeatedly argued against rivals |
| *Dialectique* (1555) | Vernacular formulation; clear “generals to specials” rule |
| *Dialecticae libri duo* | Stable two-part textbook: invention and judgment/disposition; method increasingly central |
| Late/posthumous form | Method defined through homogeneous axioms and as comprehending memory |
| *Scholae* (1578) | Full historical and polemical justification, including the critique of artificial memory |

**[Important finding]** The system was not devised once in 1543 and thereafter mechanically reproduced. Ramus revised terminology, divisions, examples, and the relation of judgment to disposition and method throughout his career.

## Yates’s account

### 1. Her central thesis

At pp. 231–32 Yates writes that Ramus:

- abolished memory as a part of rhetoric;
- abolished artificial memory;
- replaced it with memorization through dialectical order;
- arranged every subject from generals through dichotomies to specials;
- had pupils memorize the schematic epitome.

This remains a powerful overview if “abolished” is restricted to autonomous rhetorical and local-image memory.

### 2. Ramism as transformed artificial memory

Yates’s most durable insight is that Ramism should not be discussed only as logic. It belongs in memory history because it makes reliable disposition the principal aid to recollection:

> “a transformation which keeps and intensifies the principle of order but does away with the ‘artificial’ side”

**[Yates]** The quotation is from p. 236.

**[Confirmed, with qualification]** Rossi, Ong, and Angelini all recognize that memory and method become inseparable. But Ramus himself still calls dialectic an art and speaks of “true places” and “truest images.” The opposition is not simply artifice versus nature; it is arbitrary mnemonic artifice versus an artificial discipline claiming to imitate natural and real order.

### 3. The printed page and spatial visualization

Yates follows Ong in observing that learning a bracketed epitome has a spatial-visual dimension. She disagrees with his stronger association of that form with print, proposing continuity with schematized manuscripts.

Subsequent book and memory history favors a mixed account:

- manuscripts already used tables, trees, diagrams, concordances, initials, rubrics, and page architecture;
- printing standardized and multiplied these devices;
- Ramist pedagogy intensified their use;
- the change cannot be reduced to a sudden Gutenberg revolution.

Mary Carruthers and Jocelyn Penny Small are particularly important here. Their work shows that page layout, textual segmentation, headings, and ordered composition were mnemonic long before Ramus.

### 4. “We smash the inner image of old Grammatica”

At p. 234 Yates contrasts Romberch’s personified old woman *Grammatica* with the imageless Ramist outline:

> “Under Ramism, we smash the inner image of old Grammatica”

This is rhetorically memorable but historically hazardous.

- **[Text]** Ramus rejects fabricated mnemonic images.
- **[Yates]** The smashing of an internal personification is Yates’s metaphor.
- **[Negative finding]** Ramus does not instruct pupils to visualize an icon of Grammar and destroy it.
- **[Correction]** Replacing one mnemonic method does not prove the suppression of imagination in general.

### 5. Inner iconoclasm

Yates connects Ramus’s hostility to mnemonic images with Protestant attacks on religious images. She cites his *Commentariorum de religione Christiana*, where he invokes the biblical prohibition on images and condemns Catholic devotional practice.

There is a real conjunction:

- Ramus converted to Protestantism;
- he rejected arbitrary mnemonic simulacra;
- he condemned religious images used as objects of worship.

But the causal inference is weak.

**[Overturned as a general thesis]** Recent work on William Perkins and Reformed visual culture shows:

- Calvinists did not uniformly adopt Ramism;
- some Ramists accepted imagistic mnemonics;
- some Calvinists combined Ramism, Lullism, and Bruno;
- Alsted was Reformed yet developed a *Mnemonica sacra*;
- hostility to idolatry did not entail hostility to every mental or pedagogical image.

The most direct reassessment is Koen Vermeir’s/related recent discussion in “William Perkins, the imagination in Calvinist theology and ‘inner iconoclasm’ after Frances Yates.” [Full article](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/17496977.2021.1981695).

### 6. Lullist ancestry

Yates proposes that:

- Lullism and Ramism both join logic and memory;
- both ascend and descend between generals and specials;
- bracketed Lullist manuscripts may lie behind the Ramist epitome;
- the Renaissance revival of Lullism probably contributed to Ramism.

**[Partly confirmed]** Later Ramists unquestionably combined Lullian and Ramist materials. Savigny and Alsted show that classificatory memory and encyclopedia-building could bring the traditions together.

**[Disputed]** Direct genetic dependence of Ramus’s 1543 method on Llull is not demonstrated. Bonner and Fidora’s work makes clear how different Llull’s relational combinatorics is from a simple dichotomizing taxonomy. Yates knew the difference but still used “Lullism” too elastically at points.

### 7. Camillo and Citolini

Yates suggests that Giulio Camillo may have initiated a rhetorical-methodological-memory movement that Sturm and Ramus rationalized by removing images. Her bridge is Alessandro Citolini’s *Tipocosmia*, an imageless ordering of the world allegedly derived from Camillo.

Yates herself calls these suggestions “undigested and controversial.”

The evidence separates as follows:

- **[Text]** Citolini’s *Tipocosmia* is organized encyclopedically and mnemonically without Camillo’s surviving repertory of images.
- **[Text/tradition]** Giuseppe Betussi’s *Il Raverta* (1544) notes Citolini’s long association with Camillo.
- **[Tradition]** Later writers including Fontanini, Zeno, and Liruti accused Citolini or Camillo’s followers of appropriating the master’s papers.
- **[Disputed]** No surviving dossier proves that Citolini stole the *Tipocosmia* from Camillo.
- **[Speculation]** No documentary chain shows Ramus deriving dialectical method from Camillo via Sturm or Citolini.
- **[Negative finding]** “Ramus rationalized Camillo by omitting the images” remains a suggestive Yatesian scenario, not an established genealogy.

The Italian biographical scholarship describes the plagiarism problem as complex and possibly colored by Citolini’s later religious heterodoxy. [Treccani, “Alessandro Citolini”](https://www.treccani.it/enciclopedia/alessandro-citolini_%28Dizionario-Biografico%29/).

### 8. Neoplatonic mysticism

Yates reads the Prometheus story, divine light, ascent and descent, Virgil’s *spiritus intus alit*, and the golden chain as a Ramist *prisca theologia*.

Later scholarship has moved in two directions:

- Some historians have found Ramus more deeply shaped by Christian Platonism, Cusan themes, evangelical reform, and aspirations to restore a golden age than Ong’s austere pedagogical account allowed.
- Others warn that Renaissance commonplace metaphors of light, chain, ascent, and ancient wisdom do not automatically constitute Hermeticism or esoteric initiation.

Simon J. G. Burton’s *Ramism and the Reformation of Method* (Oxford, 2024) supports a substantial Christian-Platonic and reforming dimension, including method as a route toward recovered harmony and Eden. It does not make Ramus an operative magician. [Oxford chapter abstract](https://academic.oup.com/book/56290/chapter-abstract/445243469).

## Scholarship since Yates

### Walter J. Ong

Although published in 1958 and therefore preceding Yates, Ong is the indispensable modern foundation:

- *Ramus, Method, and the Decay of Dialogue*;
- *Ramus and Talon Inventory*.

Ong established:

- the extraordinary instability of the Ramist bibliography;
- Ramus and Talon’s collaboration;
- the centrality of the epitome;
- the visual/spatial organization of Ramist thought;
- the relation between method, classroom, and print culture.

His thesis that print helped transform discourse into spatially arranged objects remains influential, but manuscript studies have weakened any sharp print/manuscript rupture.

### Paolo Rossi

Rossi’s *Clavis universalis* (1960), translated as *Logic and the Art of Memory* (2000), interprets Ramus as a crucial stage in moving mnemonic problems into general method:

- memory is absorbed into logic;
- method becomes a response to mnemonic reliability;
- the line continues toward Bacon, Descartes, universal languages, and encyclopedic classification.

Yates acknowledged Rossi but complained in 1968 that the original book could not use Ong’s then-new work fully. [Yates, “Bacon’s Magic”](https://www.nybooks.com/articles/1968/02/29/bacons-magic/).

### Mary Carruthers

Carruthers’s *The Book of Memory* broadens the field beyond architectural loci and bizarre images. Medieval memory involved:

- textual division;
- ordered composition;
- meditative reconstruction;
- page and manuscript architecture;
- moral judgment;
- invention as well as storage.

Her important correction is that Ramus’s move was not wholly unprecedented. Albert the Great and Thomas Aquinas had already drawn memory toward prudential and intellectual judgment while retaining images.

Ramus’s novelty lies more in disciplinary redistribution, simplification, and systematic curricular application than in discovering that rational order aids recollection.

### Jocelyn Penny Small

*Wax Tablets of the Mind* challenges simple technological determinism. Ancient and medieval memory practices cannot be inferred directly from modern assumptions about literacy or page perception. Her work supports caution about calling the Ramist epitome a wholly new printed-memory technology.

### Lina Bolzoni, Paolo Rossi, and Camillo scholarship

Bolzoni’s *The Gallery of Memory* reconstructs the interaction of images, preaching, collection, rhetoric, and literary composition. Her approach undermines any binary in which mnemonic images are irrational residues and outlines are modern reason. Early modern actors often combined both.

Bolzoni and Monica Stagno’s Camillo scholarship emphasizes textual transmission, visualization, performance, and the instability of the Theatre’s reconstruction. That makes Yates’s proposed Camillo-to-Ramus line even harder to demonstrate.

### Lull scholarship: Bonner and Fidora

Bonner’s formal reconstruction of Llull’s Art shows:

- relations and correlatives are central;
- figures serve discovery and demonstration, not merely storage;
- the alphabet is semantically loaded;
- the combinatorial operation differs fundamentally from a Ramist binary outline.

Fidora and collaborators have strengthened textual chronology and the distinction between authentic Llull and pseudo-Lullian traditions.

Result: Yates was right that Renaissance Lullism forms part of the environment of universal method, but “Lullist influence” must be demonstrated title by title and mechanism by mechanism.

### Bruno scholarship: Sturlese, Ciliberto, Clucas, Gatti

Modern Bruno scholarship has corrected a purely “Hermetic magician” Bruno:

- Rita Sturlese’s philology establishes textual and chronological precision.
- Michele Ciliberto places the works in Bruno’s philosophical and political development.
- Stephen Clucas demonstrates overlaps among Bruno, Ramism, mathematics, atomism, and English intellectual networks.
- Hilary Gatti stresses philosophy, infinity, science, and intellectual freedom.

This matters because Yates’s sharp opposition—Ramus as abstract order, Bruno as magical image—is heuristically useful but historically porous. Their followers combined components of both.

### Ramus after Ong: Hotson, Freedman, Meerhoff, Skalnik, Angelini, Burton

Later work has shifted attention from whether Ramus produced good formal logic to:

- inexpensive curricula;
- minor schools and *gymnasia illustria*;
- confessional migration;
- the needs of poor students;
- encyclopedism;
- textbook hybridization;
- political and ecclesiastical reform.

Angelini’s 2024 article provides the most direct new account of Ramist memory: neither simple abolition nor survival as an independent technique, but a “shadow” pervading properly organized knowledge.

## History and context

### 1. Rhetorical ancestry

Ramus descends from a tradition in which memory was both:

- retention of a prepared discourse;
- an ordered storehouse supporting invention.

He rejects the dominant local-image mechanism but preserves:

- sequence;
- division;
- composition;
- topical location;
- ordered retrieval;
- the connection between memory and judgment.

### 2. Scholastic ancestry

Medieval schools trained memory through:

- divisions and subdivisions;
- *distinctiones*;
- concordances;
- trees;
- numbered questions;
- alphabetic finding systems;
- manuscript mise-en-page;
- moral images;
- Aristotelian association and order.

Ramist tables simplify and republish this environment as much as they destroy it.

### 3. Humanist dialectic

Ramus’s more immediate intellectual background includes:

- Lorenzo Valla’s hostility to technical scholastic Latin;
- Rudolph Agricola’s relocation of rhetorical invention into dialectic;
- humanist reading of Cicero and Quintilian;
- the conviction that logic should reflect ordinary learned discourse;
- pedagogical analysis of poets, historians, and orators.

Agricola is better documented as a direct structural predecessor than Llull or Camillo.

### 4. Institutions

#### University of Paris

Ramus studied and taught within a curriculum governed by the arts faculty, ecclesiastical privileges, scholastic commentaries, and competitive teaching fees. His reform attacked both intellectual doctrines and institutional livelihoods.

His 1543 books were condemned after opposition from Paris masters. A royal decree temporarily prohibited him from teaching philosophy.

#### Collège Royal

In 1551 Ramus obtained a royal professorship in philosophy and eloquence. The royal chairs operated with greater freedom from university statutes and embodied the French monarchy’s humanist educational policy.

#### Protestant institutions

After converting in 1561, Ramus sought positions and allies in Protestant territories. Ramist curricula later spread especially through:

- Reformed academies;
- Huguenot networks;
- Scottish universities;
- German and Central European gymnasia;
- Dutch schools;
- English Puritan education;
- New England colleges.

**[Correction]** Ramism was not inherently Calvinist. Catholics used it; Lutherans sometimes used it; late Lutheran scholasticism often rejected it; Reformed writers also combined it with Aristotle and Llull.

### 5. Courts and patrons

Ramus depended on royal and aristocratic protection, notably the Cardinal of Lorraine before his Protestant conversion. Ramist instruction reached courtly circles. Yates notes its teaching to Mary, Queen of Scots, through the broader Guise environment.

### 6. From oral contest to visual syllabus

Ong’s broad historical claim is that Ramism converts agonistic, dialogical logic into a silent, inspectable spatial object.

There is evidence for:

- emphasis on the synopsis;
- textbook compression;
- reduced dependence on extended commentary;
- classroom recitation from ordered precepts.

But Ramus remained a lecturer, commentator, polemicist, and analyst of speeches. The culture did not suddenly cease to be oral.

## People

### Pierre de La Ramée

Born at Cuts in Picardy in 1515. Contemporary biographical evidence comes chiefly from:

- Nicolas Nancel, student, colleague, and friend;
- Theophilus Banosius;
- Johannes Thomas Freigius.

Nancel reports that the poor young Ramus supported himself by serving wealthier students and slept only about three hours nightly, damaging his eyes.

**[Tradition with close witness]** Nancel is the best-informed source but still wrote commemorative biography.

### Omer Talon

Ramus’s collaborator in rhetoric and dialectic. The attribution of individual works between them is often porous. Talon’s *Rhetorica* is substantially a revision of Ramus’s earlier rhetorical textbook.

Nancel reports that Ramus once produced a rapid reply to an opponent and issued it under Talon’s name although Talon had scarcely seen it. The anecdote explains why title-page authorship in this circle requires scrutiny.

### Jacques Charpentier

A major academic enemy. He attacked Ramus’s dialectic, disputed his edition history, and participated in the institutional conflict surrounding Ramus’s teaching.

**[Controversy]** Later reports sometimes implicate Charpentier morally or practically in Ramus’s death. The circumstances are difficult to reconstruct, and confessional martyrology amplified personal blame.

### Johannes Sturm

Humanist educator at Strasbourg, associated with Hermogenes and pedagogical method. Yates treats him as a possible mediator between Camillo’s milieu and Ramus.

**[Disputed]** Contact and shared educational interests are real; transmission of a Camillian memory program to Ramus is not documented.

### Christophe de Savigny

Associated with Luigi Gonzaga, Duke of Nevers and Rethel. His *Tableaux* offer the fullest sixteenth-century visual encyclopedia built around Ramist order.

### William Perkins

Cambridge theologian and influential Reformed preacher. His *Arte of Prophecying* recommends logical disposition and attacks the animation of mnemonic images as spiritually dangerous because extraordinary fantasies can inflame corrupt passions.

### “G. P. Cantabrigiensis”

Author of *Libellus de memoria* and an attack on Alexander Dicson’s artificial memory.

**[Attribution disputed]** Yates identified the initials with William Perkins. Later scholars have questioned whether the identification is sufficiently demonstrated. Arguments based chiefly on initials, place, theology, and intellectual affinity do not equal proof of authorship.

### Alexander Dicson

Scottish follower of Bruno who defended an imagistic artificial memory. His controversy with the Cambridge critic supplies Yates’s principal confrontation between Brunian and Ramist memory.

### William Ames

A follower of Perkins who explicitly characterized Ramist logical ordering as an art of memory. This is strong reception evidence that contemporaries could recognize “Ramist memory” without pretending Ramus retained the classical mnemonic apparatus.

### Johann Heinrich Alsted

A Reformed encyclopedist who combined Ramist, Lullian, Aristotelian, biblical, and occasionally Brunian materials. He is decisive counterevidence to the claim that Calvinism, Ramism, and imagelessness formed an inseparable package.

### Francis Bacon

Influenced by Ramist and anti-Ramist debates but not simply a Ramist. His *ministratio ad memoriam*, tables, topics, and search for “real characters” preserve a more complicated role for images and particulars.

### Anecdotes sought in the brief

#### The auctioneer

**[Text/tradition, not Ramist]** Quintilian reports that local memory might help someone recall which auctioned object was sold to which purchaser; Hortensius allegedly performed such a feat. Yates connects this with an earlier anecdote about a professor entertaining students by remembering objects.

This illustrates the limited task for which Quintilian concedes local images can work. It is not an anecdote about Ramus using his method.

#### The professor’s party

**[Secondary retelling]** Yates’s “professor” example belongs to the classical-memory discussion and resembles the auctioneer procedure. It is not evidence for Ramist classroom performance.

#### The student who lost his memory

**[Negative finding]** No securely identifiable Ramus episode matching this description appears in the primary Ramist biographical and mnemonic material consulted. It belongs, if at all, to another system in the larger memory catalogue or to later mnemonic lore.

#### The accused magician

**[Negative finding for Ramus]** Ramus was accused of intellectual impiety, undermining philosophy and religion, and later of Protestant error; there is no sound evidence that he was prosecuted as a magician for his memory system. “The accused magician” belongs more naturally to the histories of Llullists, Bruno, or practitioners of the *Ars notoria*.

## The magic question

### 1. Was Ramist memory magical?

No, not in its documented technique.

It requires no:

- spirits;
- astral correspondences;
- planetary images;
- suffumigations;
- consecrations;
- seals;
- angelic names;
- prayers for infused knowledge;
- talismans;
- ritual timing.

Its operative terms are logical and pedagogical: invention, proposition, syllogism, disposition, method, genus, species, exercise.

### 2. Was it mystical?

Possibly in a limited philosophical-theological sense.

Ramus describes:

- divine light;
- Promethean fire;
- reason as natural to humanity;
- ascent and descent;
- the golden chain;
- return from shadow toward intellectual illumination.

**[Text]** The metaphors are authentic.

**[Interpretation disputed]** Calling them “mysticism” depends on definition. They support a Christian-Platonic metaphysics of reason and reform. They do not demonstrate ritual or talismanic practice.

### 3. Was it heretical?

The mnemonic method itself was not prosecuted as heresy.

Ramus’s troubles arose from:

- attacks on Aristotle and university teaching;
- jurisdictional and professional conflict;
- royal and ecclesiastical politics;
- his later Huguenot allegiance.

His 1543 works were condemned and his teaching restricted, but not because a tribunal found a forbidden magical art of memory in the Penelope diagram.

### 4. Ramus’s death

Ramus was killed in Paris during the St Bartholomew’s Day violence of August 1572.

- **[Documented]** He was a conspicuous Huguenot and died during the massacre.
- **[Early Protestant tradition]** His death became a martyr narrative.
- **[Disputed]** Personal academic enemies, especially Charpentier, have sometimes been cast as instigators.
- **[Legendary embellishment]** Highly dramatic accounts of mutilation, defenestration, and deliberate scholarly revenge vary and cannot all be reconciled.

His death materially aided the confessional reception of Ramism, but it does not prove that his logical method was itself Protestant doctrine.

### 5. Ars notoria

The medieval *Ars notoria* promised rapid or infused acquisition of the liberal arts through prayers, ritual observance, and inspection of complex *notae*.

Ramist method is nearly its inverse:

- *Ars notoria*: divine or angelic infusion, ritual figures.
- Ramus: ordinary instruction, division, rehearsal, rational habit.

There is no evidence that Ramus secretly practiced the *Ars notoria*. Placing both under “artificial memory” is a modern taxonomic convenience, not evidence of a continuous operative tradition.

### 6. Perkins and impious images

Perkins’s objection to activated mnemonic images is genuinely religious: extravagant images may excite sinful passions.

But this is evidence for Perkins, not automatically for Ramus. Ramus’s primary objection is predominantly epistemic and disciplinary: arbitrary signs confuse; inherent order assists.

## Reception and descent

### Sixteenth century

Ramist textbooks spread because they were:

- comparatively concise;
- teachable;
- reproducible;
- adaptable to many arts;
- suitable for institutions without great libraries or large faculties;
- available in Latin and vernacular forms.

The method shaped analysis of:

- rhetoric;
- grammar;
- poetry;
- theology;
- law;
- ethics;
- mathematics;
- preaching.

### England and Scotland

Roland MacIlmaine published an English translation of Ramus’s dialectic in 1574. Ramist logical disposition influenced:

- Gabriel Harvey;
- Abraham Fraunce;
- William Perkins;
- Puritan sermon method;
- pedagogical analysis of literature.

### William Perkins

Perkins’s preaching method breaks scriptural material into propositions, doctrines, reasons, and uses. Logical disposition aids both preacher and hearer.

Yates reads Perkins as the Calvinist, Ramist opposite of Bruno and Dicson. Later scholarship shows that this polarity captures one dispute, not the whole Reformed intellectual landscape.

### Sidney

Yates contrasts the imagistic richness of Philip Sidney’s poetic theory with Ramist abstraction. This is an interpretive contrast, not proof that Sidney consciously staged a contest between two formally exclusive memory systems.

### Alsted and encyclopedism

Alsted’s encyclopedias carry Ramist descent from general to special into large-scale knowledge architecture, but combine it with non-Ramist materials. He exemplifies hybrid rather than pure Ramism.

### Comenius

Comenius inherits the dream of ordered universal instruction but restores a major place to sensory images, especially in the *Orbis sensualium pictus*. He is thus an heir to encyclopedic method, not an orthodox continuer of Ramus’s anti-image polemic.

### Decline

By the later seventeenth century, pure Ramist textbooks lost ground to:

- renewed Aristotelian scholasticism;
- Cartesian method;
- Baconian natural history;
- mixed logical systems;
- new encyclopedic and scientific classifications.

Yet Ramist habits survived in outline culture, preaching, textbooks, and diagrams after explicit allegiance faded.

## Afterlife

### 1. Literature and art

#### Dante and medieval frescoes

Dante, scholastic moral imagery, and fresco cycles predate Ramus. Their relevance is ancestral and contrastive:

- they embody knowledge through emotionally and morally charged images;
- Ramus reduces reliance on personified figures;
- Yates’s Grammatica contrast dramatizes this transition.

There is no evidence that Dante anticipated Ramist dichotomy in any direct sense.

#### Emblem books

Emblems unite image, motto, and explanatory text. They flourished alongside Ramism, refuting any simple chronological story in which schemata replaced images throughout European culture.

#### Borges’s “Funes the Memorious”

Funes remembers particulars without effective abstraction. A Ramist would regard such memory as defective because it lacks general ordering and discriminating judgment.

This is a **modern interpretive comparison**, not historical influence.

#### Umberto Eco

Eco’s writing on perfect languages, Llull, encyclopedias, and semiotics illuminates the tension between:

- a tree-like taxonomy;
- combinatorial systems;
- open-ended encyclopedic networks.

Any line from Ramus to Eco is conceptual reception, not a single continuous mnemonic tradition.

### 2. Modern memory-palace revival

Contemporary memory athletes generally use:

- method of loci;
- person-action-object systems;
- numerical encodings;
- exaggerated images.

These practices are much closer to the *Ad Herennium* than to Ramus.

Possible Ramist echoes include:

- hierarchical notes;
- structured outlines;
- concept maps;
- chunking;
- taxonomic rehearsal.

**[Modern invention]** Calling every outline a “Ramist memory palace” conflates opposed techniques.

### 3. Computing and combinatorics

#### Llull to Leibniz

There is a serious history connecting Lullian combinatorics, early modern universal-language projects, and Leibniz’s *ars combinatoria*. But the line is neither purely Ramist nor equivalent to modern computing.

#### Ramist contribution

Ramist tables contribute historically to:

- formal decomposition;
- hierarchical data;
- visual trees;
- rule-governed classification;
- modular curricula.

#### Modern analogy

A Ramist outline resembles:

- a tree data structure;
- a nested menu;
- a filesystem hierarchy;
- a decision tree;
- an ontology.

But those similarities do not demonstrate direct descent. Binary trees in modern computing arise from mathematical logic, algorithmics, and engineering traditions with multiple genealogies.

#### What Ramus lacks

Ramist method has no explicit:

- executable notation;
- general calculus of combinations;
- truth-table semantics;
- binary arithmetic;
- programmable machine;
- algorithm for discovering genuinely new propositions.

“Ramus invented the computer tree” is a modern myth.

## Controversies and disputes

### 1. Did Ramus abolish memory?

**Verdict:** misleading without qualification.

- **Yates:** yes, as part of rhetoric and as artificial local-image memory.
- **Primary evidence:** memory survives as the shadow and consequence of disposition.
- **Angelini:** it is absorbed and universalized, not simply eliminated.

### 2. Is Ramist method itself an art of memory?

**Verdict:** defensible in Ramus’s own terms, but not in the narrow Ciceronian sense.

Ramus explicitly calls dialectic the true art of memory. Yet it lacks the defining apparatus of classical artificial memory. “Art of memory” here means an art whose correct ordering necessarily aids recollection.

### 3. Are dichotomous tables the method?

**Verdict:** no.

They display methodical divisions. Method resides in the ordered relation of homogeneous propositions. A table can be badly or arbitrarily divided and therefore fail to embody true method.

### 4. Did print create Ramism?

**Verdict:** print greatly amplified it but did not create visual division.

- Ong emphasizes printed spatialization.
- Yates points to manuscript schemata.
- Carruthers and Small supply strong evidence for pre-print mnemonic page architecture.

### 5. Does Ramism derive from Llull?

**Verdict:** possible environmental influence; direct descent unproved.

Visual resemblance, shared ascent/descent vocabulary, and later hybrids are evidence of affinity and reception. They do not identify the concrete source from which Ramus derived his first epitome.

### 6. Does Ramism derive from Camillo?

**Verdict:** speculative.

Yates proposed a possible reaction or rationalization. No surviving letter, lecture note, or testimony demonstrates it.

### 7. Did Citolini steal Camillo’s papers?

**Verdict:** early-modern accusation, not established fact.

Association with Camillo and structural similarities are documented. Theft is transmitted by hostile or later testimony and may have been sharpened by confessional controversy.

### 8. Was Ramism Protestant or Calvinist?

**Verdict:** not inherently.

Ramus became a Huguenot; martyr reception mattered; Ramism flourished in Reformed settings. But Catholic and mixed-confessional use, as well as Ramist-Aristotelian and Ramist-Lullian syntheses, disprove an exclusive identity.

### 9. Was Ramism an “inner iconoclasm”?

**Verdict:** a powerful Yatesian metaphor, overturned as a general causal explanation.

The metaphor describes the removal of mnemonic personifications. It cannot account for the variety of Reformed visual, mnemonic, and encyclopedic practice.

### 10. Was Ramus a Neoplatonic mystic?

**Verdict:** Christian-Platonic and illuminationist elements are real; “mystic” remains interpretive.

The Promethean and golden-chain passages justify more than a purely technical reading. They do not justify identifying Ramism with Bruno’s operative mnemonic magic.

### 11. Was Ramus wholly anti-Aristotelian?

**Verdict:** his polemical identity was anti-Aristotelian, but his conceptual debts were substantial.

He attacked scholastic Aristotle while claiming the true Aristotle or natural reason where useful. His laws, genus/species ordering, demonstration, and method debates remain deeply entangled with Aristotelian materials.

### 12. Did Ramist method discover new knowledge?

**Verdict:** disputed and generally doubtful in its strongest form.

Ramus emphasized finding arguments and properly displaying accepted truths. Critics objected that ordering and abridgment are not discovery. Later encyclopedists expanded the apparatus, but Ramus supplied no experimental research program comparable to Bacon’s.

### 13. Is the famous thesis “Everything Aristotle said is false” documented?

The formula *Quaecutnque ab Aristotele dicta essent commentitia esse* is attached by later biographical tradition to Ramus’s master’s thesis.

**[Disputed]** The wording is famous but no contemporary thesis text survives to verify that he defended precisely this universal proposition. It should be treated as biographical tradition, not verbatim documentation.

### 14. Was Ramus murdered by Charpentier?

**Verdict:** possible hostile involvement has been alleged, but later martyrology exceeds secure evidence.

The certain facts are Ramus’s Huguenot identity, academic enmities, and death during the massacre. Fine-grained narratives of conspiracy require source-by-source scrutiny.

### 15. Was the Ramist tree always binary?

**Verdict:** no.

Binary branching is characteristic and visually memorable, but later textbook stereotypes can exaggerate its universality and rigidity.

### 16. Does Ramism lead directly to modern computing?

**Verdict:** no direct linear genealogy.

There are histories of formalization and diagrammatic knowledge connecting Llull, Ramus, Leibniz, encyclopedism, logic, and machines. Each transition involves transformations and independent sources. The direct “Ramus-to-computer” story is a modern compression.

## What Yates got right

- Ramus consciously confronts and rejects local-image artificial memory.
- Dialectical order is intended to strengthen memory.
- The Penelope epitome has an explicitly mnemonic pedagogical function.
- The page and diagram introduce a spatial-visual component even without representational pictures.
- Ramism and Lullism belong to the early modern search for universally applicable method.
- Ramus and Bruno represent genuinely contrasting emphases.
- The history of memory is essential to the history of method.
- Ramus’s metaphors of light, shadow, chain, ascent, and descent complicate a purely secular-rationalist account.

## What needs correction

- Memory is absorbed, not simply abolished.
- Ramist rejection of mnemonic images does not equal rejection of imagination in general.
- Protestant iconoclasm does not explain Ramism’s whole mnemonic theory.
- Calvinists did not uniformly oppose imagistic memory.
- Ramism, Lullism, and Brunian practice were sometimes combined.
- The Ramist epitome did not originate solely from print culture.
- Lullist or Camillian derivation is less securely documented than Yates implies.
- Ramus’s Platonism should not be casually equated with Hermetic magic.
- The contrast between “medieval images” and “modern abstract reason” is too clean.

## Open questions

1. What is the exact stemma of the 1543 *Partitiones*, *Institutiones*, and any earlier presentation manuscript?
2. Can the illuminated BnF manuscript reported by Ong be securely identified by current shelfmark and fully digitized?
3. How did actual pupils use the Penelope epitome: copying, chanting, pointing, oral recitation, or visual rehearsal?
4. Did Ramus encounter specific Lullist manuscripts, or only a generalized culture of schematized division?
5. What documentary contact, if any, links Ramus to Camillo beyond shared French intellectual networks?
6. How much of the anti-image polemic belongs to Ramus and how much to the later editorial shape of the *Scholae*?
7. Did early editions vary materially in the arrangement of the Penelope table, not merely typeface and title-page state?
8. How should “true images of things” be related to Renaissance theories of mental species, concepts, and signs?
9. Is “memory as shadow” primarily psychological, logical, pedagogical, or metaphysical in Ramus’s changing terminology?
10. How widely did teachers reproduce Ramist tables on walls, boards, commonplace books, and student manuscripts?
11. Which purportedly Ramist schoolbooks used nonbinary, circular, emblematic, or hybrid displays?
12. Can the authorship of Savigny’s *Tableaux* be resolved?
13. Can “G. P. Cantabrigiensis” be securely identified, or should the Perkins attribution be abandoned?
14. Which elements of Ramism reached Bacon directly, and which came through mixed late-sixteenth-century logical culture?
15. How far did New England “Ramist” practice preserve mnemonic aims rather than merely textbook terminology?

The most defensible final characterization is therefore not that Ramus destroyed memory, but that he attempted to deprive it of a separate body, iconography, and architecture. Memory became the shadow cast by ordered judgment: less a storehouse one walks through than the recoverable articulation of knowledge itself.
