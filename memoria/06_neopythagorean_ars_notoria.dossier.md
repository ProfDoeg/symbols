# Neopythagorean memory-training and the Ars Notoria: Research Dossier

## Basic identification

**Catalogue number:** 6 of 45  
**Name:** Neopythagorean memory-training and the medieval *Ars notoria*  
**Ancient exponent:** Apollonius of Tyana, as represented by Philostratus  
**Medieval attributions:** Solomon as divinely inspired author; “Apollonius” as compiler, redactor, or translator; sometimes Euclid of Thebes and other legendary authorities  
**Dates:** Apollonius, first century CE; Philostratus’s *Life*, early third century CE; *Ars notoria*, probably late twelfth-century northern Italy, with manuscripts from about 1225 onward; glossed recension, fourteenth century; first Latin printing, around 1600; Robert Turner’s English translation, 1657  
**Associated printed mnemonic books:** Jodocus Wetzdorf/Weczdorff, *Ars memorandi nova secretissima*, Strasbourg, about 1500–1501—not about 1600, as Yates reported; Nicolaus Simonis ex Weyda, *Ludus artificialis oblivionis*, Leipzig, 1510  
**Principal sources:** Philostratus, *Vita Apollonii* I.14, III.16, III.41; Philostratus, *Lives of the Sophists* I.22.2 on Dionysius of Miletus; medieval *Ars notoria/Flores aurei*; Thomas Aquinas, *Summa theologiae* II-II, q.96, a.1; *Grandes Chroniques de France* for 1323; John of Morigny, *Liber florum caelestis doctrinae*  
**Yates:** *The Art of Memory*, ch. II, pp. 42–43; ch. V, p.114 and notes; ch. IX, p.204; ch. XIII, p.306; ch. XV, p.328 n.17.

**Principle in one sentence:** Neopythagorean discipline cultivates recollection through silence, attention, repetition, and devotion to Mnemosyne, whereas the medieval *Ars notoria* seeks divinely infused memory, understanding, eloquence, and curricular knowledge through ascetic preparation, repeated prayers, mysterious names, prescribed times, and the ritual inspection of discipline-specific diagrams called *notae*.

### Evidentiary verdict at the outset

- **Documented text:** Philostratus makes Apollonius’s five-year silence and exceptional memory part of his Pythagorean formation.
- **Documented medieval tradition:** The *Ars notoria* claims Solomonic revelation and an Apollonian transmission.
- **Scholarly reconstruction:** The extant Latin work probably arose in northern Italy, plausibly around Bologna, in the late twelfth or early thirteenth century.
- **Disputed:** Its exact date, place, compositional stages, and the relationship of its oldest recensions.
- **Unsupported tradition:** No evidence connects the historical Apollonius of Tyana with the medieval Latin work.
- **Yates’s interpretation:** It may be a “bastard descendant” of the shorthand-*notae* branch of classical artificial memory.
- **Later correction:** The diagrams are not demonstrated descendants of Tironian shorthand, and the rite is structurally unlike the classical locus–image mnemonic.
- **Modern invention:** Calling it a medieval “memory palace,” a technique of photographic memory, a part originally belonging to the *Lemegeton*, or an ancestor of contemporary “download learning” goes beyond the evidence.

## The system in detail

## 1. Apollonius’s Neopythagorean memory discipline

### 1.1 What Philostratus actually describes

Philostratus wrote the *Life of Apollonius of Tyana* under the Severan dynasty, roughly two centuries after its subject. It is a literary-philosophical biography, not Apollonius’s notebook or a technical mnemonic manual. Philostratus says that Apollonius adopted Pythagorean rules, including abstinence and five years of silence.

At *Vita Apollonii* I.14 the Greek reads:

> **καὶ τὴν μὲν φωνὴν κατεῖχεν, οἱ δ᾽ ὀφθαλμοὶ καὶ ὁ νοῦς πλεῖστα μὲν ἀνεγίνωσκον, πλεῖστα δὲ ἐς μνήμην ἀνελέγοντο.**

Gloss: “He restrained his voice, while his eyes and mind read much and gathered much into memory.”

Philostratus continues:

> **τὸ τοι μνημονικὸν ἑκατοντούτης γενόμενος καὶ ὑπὲρ τὸν Σιμωνίδην ἔρρωτο, καὶ ὕμνος αὐτῷ τις ἐς τὴν Μνημοσύνην ᾔδετο, ἐν ᾧ πάντα μὲν ὑπὸ τοῦ χρόνου μαραίνεσθαί φησιν, αὐτόν γε μὴν τὸν χρόνον ἀγήρω τε καὶ ἀθάνατον παρὰ τῆς Μνημοσύνης εἶναι.**

Gloss: “Even when he had reached a hundred, his faculty of memory remained stronger than Simonides’; he used to sing a hymn to Mnemosyne, saying that everything is withered by Time, but that Time itself is made ageless and immortal by Memory.”

The episode is securely present in the Greek text; the historicity of its particulars is not. The comparison with Simonides deliberately places Apollonius beside the legendary founder of the classical art of memory.

### 1.2 Places, images, order, and retrieval

No architectural places, mnemonic images, alphabet, or retrieval itinerary is given.

The recoverable procedure is:

1. Adopt Pythagorean silence.
2. Attend visually and mentally to what is heard or read.
3. refrain from immediately discharging thought in speech.
4. collect material inwardly “into memory.”
5. retain discipline over emotional reactions.
6. honor Memory as the power that preserves persons, events, and time.

The five-year silence is therefore an ascetic discipline that produces concentrated attention and self-command. To reconstruct a hidden locus-system from it would exceed the text.

**Documented text:** disciplined silence, reading, attentive observation, retention, exceptional old-age memory, a hymn to Mnemosyne.

**Scholarly inference:** silence removes distraction and forces internal rehearsal.

**Not documented:** a Simonidean architectural mnemonic practiced by Apollonius.

### 1.3 The seven rings

At *Vita Apollonii* III.41 the Indian sage Iarchas gives Apollonius seven rings “named after the seven stars,” to be worn successively on the corresponding days of the week.

This is an astrological gift, not a memory algorithm. Later magical tradition magnified the passage: Agrippa states that the rings bore planetary virtues, names, and seals and credits them with Apollonius’s longevity. Those specifications are not all in Philostratus.

**Documented in Philostratus:** seven stellar or planetary rings and weekday rotation.

**Later magical elaboration:** engraved planetary names, virtues, seals, preservation of youth, and extraordinary longevity.

**Yates’s use:** the story helps her establish an atmosphere in which memory, Pythagorean discipline, astrology, and magical reputation coexist.

**Correction:** the rings do not function as mnemonic places or images in Philostratus.

### 1.4 Philostratus III.16

In the Indian-sage sequence, Apollonius and Iarchas discuss knowledge and self-knowledge. This passage contributes to the ideal of wisdom attained through purification and philosophical discipline. It does not prescribe a technical memory system.

### 1.5 Dionysius of Miletus and “Chaldaean arts”

In *Lives of the Sophists* I.22.2 Philostratus reports a rumor that Dionysius taught his students memory by “Chaldaean arts”:

> **ὡς Χαλδαίοις τέχναις τοὺς ὁμιλητὰς τὸ μνημονικὸν ἀναπαιδεύοντος**

Gloss: “that he retrained his pupils’ mnemonic faculty by Chaldaean arts.”

Philostratus rejects the allegation:

> **τέχναι μνήμης οὔτε εἰσὶν οὔτ᾽ ἂν γένοιντο, μνήμη μὲν γὰρ δίδωσι τέχνας, αὐτὴ δὲ ἀδίδακτος καὶ οὐδεμιᾷ τέχνῃ ἁλωτός**

Gloss: “There are no arts of memory, nor could there be: memory gives arts, but is itself untaught and capturable by no art.”

He explains the students’ powers naturally. Dionysius repeated admired performances; able students impressed them on their minds and reproduced them through practice.

This is an important counter-witness to Yates’s narrative. Philostratus knew both the language of magical mnemonic accusation and a naturalistic explanation based on repetition, aptitude, and rehearsal.

**Documented:** the accusation and Philostratus’s explicit denial.

**Misleading catalogue shorthand:** saying simply that Dionysius “was accused of Chaldaean arts” omits that Philostratus refutes the allegation.

## 2. The medieval *Ars notoria*

### 2.1 Objective

The rite promises some combination of:

- strengthening memory;
- increasing *ingenium*, intellectual capacity;
- improving understanding;
- producing eloquence;
- granting perseverance;
- rapidly acquiring the seven liberal arts;
- acquiring philosophy, theology, medicine, and sometimes mechanical or “exceptive” arts;
- receiving visions or angelic confirmation.

Its model is not “store an image at a place and retrieve it later.” It is illumination: God, often through angels, perfects the operator’s mental faculties or infuses knowledge.

### 2.2 Legendary authorship

The text’s internal narrative makes Solomon the recipient of heavenly wisdom. An angel—called Pamphilius in some forms of the tradition—is said to reveal the work. Solomon composes a *Liber florum caelestis doctrinae*. Apollonius then transmits, selects, glosses, translates, or reorganizes the prayers as the *Flores aurei*, depending on the recension.

Yale Mellon MS 1 begins:

> **Incipit prohemium, sive exceptiones quas magnus Appollonius flores aureos ad eruditionem et coniunctionem omnium scientiarum… ab angelo sapientissimo Salomoni.**

Approximate gloss: “Here begins the prologue, or selections, which the great Apollonius [called] Golden Flowers for the learning and conjunction of all sciences … [revealed] to Solomon by a most wise angel.”

The damaged syntax and textual instability counsel against treating this as a simple authorial colophon.

**Documented manuscript attribution:** Solomon and Apollonius.

**Tradition:** Apollonius translated primordial prayers from Greek, Hebrew, Chaldean, or Arabic.

**Historical verdict:** pseudepigraphy. No Greek antique archetype or first-century witness is known.

### 2.3 What serves as “places”

Strictly speaking, there are no classical *loci*.

The functional substitutes are:

- the ordered curriculum of sciences;
- the sequence of general and special prayers;
- the numbered or discipline-specific *notae*;
- prescribed days, hours, and cycles;
- bodily and devotional states;
- in glossed versions, a carefully regulated sequence of inspection, recitation, fasting, confession, and repetition.

Each discipline occupies a position in a ritual-curricular matrix, not in an imagined building. The “place” is thus a compound of textual location, diagram, calendar position, and ritual stage.

### 2.4 What serves as “images”

The *notae* are intricate pen-drawn figures containing combinations of:

- circles and concentric circular zones;
- crosses;
- arcs, radiating lines, and spokes;
- squares, polygons, and compartmented fields;
- letter sequences;
- divine or angelic names;
- *voces mysticae* or *verba ignota*;
- short prayers and instructions;
- occasional figurative or quasi-figurative elements.

They are not normally narrative *imagines agentes* of the *Ad Herennium* type. They do not depict a grotesque actor whose features encode propositions. Nor is each component consistently decodable as a rebus.

The operator inspects the figure while reciting associated prayers. The figure is a ritual object and visual focus; whether it also organizes semantic material varies by manuscript and cannot be assumed.

### 2.5 The oldest surviving diagram set

Yale, Beinecke Library, Mellon MS 1, France or northern Italy, about 1225, contains approximately thirty-six surviving drawings, though the text calls for thirty-two. They occupy fifteen pages, fols. 10v–17v. The designs are chiefly in red, made with compass and ruler but also including freehand elements; minute brown writing supplies labels and prayers.

Its list says:

> **Quinque sunt generales figure. Septem sunt philosophie. Tres gramatice. Due dialectice. Quattuor rethorice. Quinque theologie. Una musice. Una reprehensionis et taciturnitatis. Una ad exceptivas. Una de mirabilibus. Una iusticie pacis et timoris. Una inexplicabilis hominibus. Et ita sunt in summa xxxij.**

Gloss:

> “There are five general figures; seven of philosophy; three of grammar; two of dialectic; four of rhetoric; five of theology; one of music; one of reproof and silence; one for the exceptive arts; one concerning marvels; one of justice, peace, and fear; one inexplicable to human beings: thus thirty-two in all.”

This is the clearest primary evidence for the curricular structure.

### 2.6 How the order is fixed

The order comes from several overlapping schemes:

1. **General preparation:** prayers intended to perfect memory, understanding, eloquence, and perseverance.
2. **Curricular hierarchy:** grammar, dialectic, rhetoric, arithmetic, geometry, music, astronomy, philosophy, theology, and variant additions.
3. **Figure-to-prayer correspondence:** a *nota* is assigned one or more orations.
4. **Liturgical or planetary timing:** particular prayers or figures belong to specified days and hours.
5. **Repetition count:** prayers must be said prescribed numbers of times.
6. **Ascetic sequence:** confession, fasting, chastity, bathing or washing, clean garments, and devotional preparation may precede inspection.
7. **Visionary verification:** dreams or visions may confirm permission, progress, or failure.

The details differ among recensions. There is no single procedure valid for every manuscript.

### 2.7 Inspection and retrieval

The usual operative verb is *inspectio*: deliberate looking at a *nota*. In later explanatory material, inspection may be divided into repeated sessions and coordinated with prayer.

The hoped-for “retrieval” is not normally:

> locus → image → encoded proposition.

It is:

> correctly prepared operator → prayer and inspection → divine or angelic illumination → strengthened faculties or infused command of a subject → fluent recall and exposition.

The practitioner was nevertheless often expected to read or study. The rhetoric of instantaneous learning should not erase the text’s long, laborious regimen.

### 2.8 Rules for the operator

Across the principal versions, recurrent requirements include:

- orthodox profession of faith;
- prayer directed to God and holy angels rather than coercive conjuration;
- confession and repentance;
- fasting or restricted diet;
- sexual continence and bodily purity;
- clean clothing and a clean location;
- avoidance of ridicule, frivolity, drunkenness, or play during inspection;
- strict observance of times and repetitions;
- correct pronunciation where possible;
- restraint concerning the mysterious words;
- humility and worthy intention;
- perseverance over extended cycles.

The printed English text warns that on days devoted to the notes of theology or philosophy the practitioner must neither “laugh nor play, nor sport.” It supplies a cautionary Solomonic story: Solomon once inspected the figures while drunk and treated the sacrament irreverently, whereupon an angel announced divine punishment. This is internal didactic legend, not a historical event.

### 2.9 The prayers

The prayers combine conventional Christian language with strings of obscure words. A representative programmatic claim in the tradition is that the art permits every science to be:

> **intelligibiliter et memorabiliter … cum subtilissima celeritate et brevi temporis spatio**

Gloss: “grasped intelligibly and memorably … with the most subtle speed and in a brief space of time.”

The strange words were variously represented as Hebrew, Greek, Chaldean, Arabic, primordial, angelic, or too profound for translation. Modern philology finds mixtures of recognizable divine names, corrupt transliterations, invented syllables, and irrecoverably damaged forms.

**Documented belief of the text:** the words possess divinely established efficacy.

**Aquinas’s interpretation:** their opacity is evidence that they are conventional signs in a demonic compact.

**Modern philological finding:** some fragments are corrupt multilingual sacred vocabulary; there is no demonstrated single lost language behind them.

### 2.10 What the *notae* do

Three interpretations should be kept separate:

- **Internal ritual theory:** the *nota*, prayer, divine names, timing, and angelic mediation form a sacramental-like instrument through which God grants knowledge.
- **Psychological reconstruction:** prolonged attention, fasting, repetition, expectancy, rehearsal, and visual concentration may alter attention and subjective confidence.
- **Yatesian mnemonic reconstruction:** the figures descend from classical memory notation or shorthand.

Only the first is explicitly the text’s own theory. The second is a modern explanatory hypothesis. The third remains conjectural.

### 2.11 Worked examples—or their absence

The *Ars notoria* does not give a worked encoding comparable to the *Ad Herennium* lawsuit image. It does not show how “poison,” “inheritance,” and “witnesses” become particular visual details. Nor does it provide Camillo’s spatial grades or Bruno’s lettered wheel-combinations.

Its nearest equivalents are assignments:

- three figures to grammar;
- two to dialectic;
- four to rhetoric;
- seven to philosophy;
- five to theology;
- individual figures to music, silence, justice, marvels, or exceptive arts.

The absence of semantic worked examples is significant. It weakens the claim that the *notae* are ordinary mnemonic codes.

## The sources

## 3. Ancient sources

### 3.1 Philostratus, *Life of Apollonius*

Standard modern edition:

- Christopher P. Jones, ed. and trans., *Philostratus: The Life of Apollonius of Tyana*, 2 vols., Loeb Classical Library 16–17, Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press, 2005.
- Older Loeb: F. C. Conybeare, 2 vols., 1912, repeatedly reprinted.

Relevant passages:

- I.13–14: Pythagorean discipline and five-year silence;
- I.14: collection of material into memory, superiority to Simonides, hymn to Mnemosyne;
- III.16: Indian wisdom and self-knowledge;
- III.41: seven rings assigned to the stars and weekdays.

Textual caution: Philostratus claimed to use memoirs attributed to Damis, but the nature and existence of this source remain disputed. The *Life* is evidence for a third-century representation of Apollonius, not transparent documentation of first-century practice.

### 3.2 Philostratus, *Lives of the Sophists*

Standard edition:

- Wilmer Cave Wright, ed. and trans., *Philostratus and Eunapius: Lives of the Sophists*, Loeb Classical Library 134, Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press, 1921/1922.

Relevant passage:

- I.22.2, Dionysius of Miletus: rumor of Chaldaean memory instruction, emphatically rejected in favor of natural aptitude and repeated practice.

### 3.3 Other Pythagorean witnesses

Later biographies of Pythagoras by Porphyry and Iamblichus associate Pythagorean life with silence, recollection, moral examination, dietary discipline, and ordered daily review. Diogenes Laertius preserves biographical traditions about Pythagoras but no technical locus–image system.

A famous Pythagoreanizing practice is evening recollection of the day’s deeds:

> “Where did I transgress? What did I accomplish? What duty did I leave undone?”

That practice belongs to moral self-audit and ordered recollection. It should not be silently converted into a classical artificial-memory architecture.

## 4. *Ars notoria* manuscripts and editions

### 4.1 Earliest witness

**New Haven, Yale University, Beinecke Rare Book and Manuscript Library, Mellon MS 1**

- Shelfmark: Mellon MS 1
- Title: *Ars notoria, sive Flores aurei*
- Date and origin: France or northern Italy, about 1225
- Material: parchment
- Extent: 18 surviving folios of 20
- Figures: fols. 10v–17v; about thirty-six surviving drawings, while the text enumerates thirty-two
- Significance: earliest securely dated, richly diagrammatic witness presently known.

The catalogue’s suggestion that it may have been copied from a “considerably older manuscript” is a codicological inference, not proof of an ancient or even eleventh-century archetype.

### 4.2 Major manuscript families

Julien Véronèse’s critical work distinguishes principal textual states conventionally called:

- **Version A:** the earlier, relatively short form;
- **Version A2:** a related early adaptation identified through manuscript comparison;
- **Version B or glossed version:** an expanded and reorganized form, especially important from the fourteenth century;
- **Ars nova:** a supplementary sequence that could circulate with the older material;
- related abridgments and transformations, including an *Ars brevis*, material attributed to Thomas of Toledo, and an *Ars Paulina* distinct from the later *Lemegeton* text.

These are modern editorial classifications, not titles consistently used by medieval scribes.

Important witnesses discussed in the scholarship include:

- Yale, Mellon MS 1;
- Munich, Bayerische Staatsbibliothek, Clm 276, fols. 1–26, *Apollonii Flores aurei*;
- Paris, Bibliothèque nationale de France, MS lat. 9336, a major glossed/version-B witness;
- Oxford, Bodleian Library, MS Ashmole 208, copied and used by Simon Forman; its colophon dates completion to 14 July 1601;
- Amsterdam, Bibliotheca Philosophica Hermetica/Ritman Library, MS M 242;
- additional manuscripts in London, Oxford, Glasgow, Paris, Munich, Prague, Vienna, Erfurt, and other European collections.

Counts have risen with cataloguing: Véronèse reported about forty witnesses in 2002 and subsequently surveyed roughly fifty. The exact total depends on whether fragments, excerpts, derivatives, and modern copies are counted.

### 4.3 The critical Latin edition

Julien Véronèse, *L’Ars notoria au Moyen Âge: introduction et édition critique*, Florence: SISMEL–Edizioni del Galluzzo, 2007, *Salomon Latinus* 1.

This supersedes Thorndike and early printed texts for reconstructing the medieval Latin tradition. It edits the principal versions rather than presenting one late witness as “the original.”

### 4.4 Modern translation

Matthias Castle, *Ars Notoria: The Notory Art of Solomon: A Medieval Treatise on Angelic Magic and the Art of Memory*, Rochester, VT: Inner Traditions, 2023.

This is the first full modern English translation explicitly based on Véronèse’s critical Latin edition. Its historical and linguistic proposals should still be distinguished from Véronèse’s established manuscript findings.

### 4.5 Early modern Latin printing

A Latin *Ars notoria* was printed around 1600 in a collection circulated under Agrippa’s name and subsequently reprinted by the Bering brothers. It omits the manuscript tradition’s indispensable diagrams and much commentary. Its exemplar cannot now be securely identified.

That defective printed form became the basis for Robert Turner’s English translation:

- Robert Turner, *Ars Notoria: The Notory Art of Solomon, Shewing the Cabalistical Key of Magical Operations, the Liberal Sciences, Divine Revelation, and the Art of Memory*, London, 1657.

Turner’s version is historically influential but is not a critical translation. It inherits omissions and disorder from the Latin printing and modifies Catholic language.

### 4.6 John of Morigny’s derivative

John of Morigny, a Benedictine monk educated in theology and canon law, practiced the *Ars notoria* and composed a transformed work usually called the *Liber florum caelestis doctrinae* or *Flowers of Heavenly Teaching*, beginning in the first decade of the fourteenth century.

Modern edition:

- Claire Fanger and Nicholas Watson, eds. and trans., *John of Morigny: Liber florum caelestis doctrinae—The Flowers of Heavenly Teaching*, Toronto: Pontifical Institute of Mediaeval Studies, 2015.

John removes or replaces suspect *verba ignota*, emphasizes authorization by the Virgin Mary, supplies visionary autobiography, and develops new figures and prayers. His work is a derivative and critique of the *Ars notoria*, not merely another manuscript of it.

## 5. Wetzdorf and Nicolaus Simonis

### 5.1 Jodocus Wetzdorf/Weczdorff

Full title:

> *Ars memorandi nova secretissima continens praecepta paucissimis bonarum artium militibus visa*

Bibliographic facts:

- author named in the dedicatory preface: Jodocus Wetzdorf de Triptis;
- place and printer: Strasbourg, Johann Grüninger;
- date: about 1500–1501;
- extent: four unnumbered leaves;
- illustrations: title woodcut and one figure woodcut;
- identifiers: ISTC iw00013000; GW M51520; BSB-Ink W-26;
- Munich copy: Bayerische Staatsbibliothek, 4 Inc.c.a. 1466 b, Beiband 6;
- Strasbourg copy: Bibliothèque nationale et universitaire, K 3802.

**Correction of Yates:** she dates it “circa 1600.” Incunable and paper evidence place it around 1500–1501. This is a century-level error, probably inherited from the source or copy available to her.

The treatise provides an alphabetically organized stock of words usable as a “memory house.” Later analysis compares it with Conrad Celtis’s alphabetic lists. This is conventional verbal and figural mnemonics with conspicuous visual packaging—not the *Ars notoria* ritual.

### 5.2 Nicolaus Simonis ex Weyda

Full title:

> *Ludus artificialis oblivionis gratia Dei omniumque vitiorum fugativus, virtutum ac scientiarum lucrativus, memoriam tum arte tum medicinalibus salvans, cum speculo et annulo utilissimo philosophorum*

Publication:

- Leipzig, 1510;
- author: Nicolaus Simonis ex Weyda, doctor of arts and philosophy at Erfurt.

The book combines:

- artificial memory;
- remedies or medical counsel for memory;
- moral and religious framing;
- alphabetic and syllabic notation;
- bodily coding;
- a philosophical mirror and ring.

A worked grammatical example, fol. 9r, says:

> **Imaginari cupiens casus nominis: si nominativi sit concavitatem manus advertit, si genitivi pollicem, si dativi indicem. Si in dextra manu est singularis, in sinistra pluralis.**

Gloss:

> “To imagine the case of a noun: for the nominative attend to the hollow of the hand, for the genitive to the thumb, for the dative to the index finger. If it is on the right hand it is singular; on the left, plural.”

Here the body supplies an ordered code: hand region signifies grammatical case, right/left signifies number. Other positions and gestures encode verbal mood, tense, person, and number.

### 5.3 Surviving printed diagrams

Ludwig Volkmann reproduced the books’ frontispieces and diagrams in “Ars Memorativa,” *Jahrbuch der Kunsthistorischen Sammlungen in Wien*, new series 3 (1929), figures—not “plates”—168–171.

The Simon title woodcut bears:

> **Figuralis omnium litterarum et syllabarum totiusque numeri facilis annotatio**

Gloss: “Easy figural notation of all letters and syllables and of the entire number-system.”

Volkmann describes a fantastic human figure carrying letters, numbers, bodily signs, and miscellaneous symbols. The figure serves as a coding surface.

**Yates’s interpretation:** the imagery is “heavily magical” and shows infiltration by medieval magical memory.

**Correction:** religious titles, rings, symbolic bodies, and elaborate woodcuts establish a magical or occult visual register, but do not prove direct textual descent from the *Ars notoria*. Wetzdorf and Simon teach recognizable mnemonic encodings; the *Ars notoria* seeks illumination by prayer. “Infiltration” is a suggestive comparison, not a demonstrated transmission.

## Yates’s account and the scholarship since

## 6. What Yates says

### 6.1 Chapter II, pp. 42–43

Yates connects the late-antique reverence for memory with the Pythagorean revival. She summarizes Apollonius’s silence, prodigious memory, hymn to Memory, the Indian rings, and the accusation against Dionysius.

She then introduces the medieval work:

> “The practitioner of the *Ars Notoria* gazed at figures or diagrams curiously marked and called ‘notae’ whilst reciting magical prayers.”

This is substantially accurate, though radically compressed.

She proposes:

> “The *Ars Notoria* is perhaps a bastard descendant of the classical art of memory, or of that difficult branch of it which used the shorthand *notae*.”

The qualifying “perhaps” matters. Yates presents a hypothesis, not a proven genealogy.

### 6.2 Chapter V, p. 114

Discussing printed memory treatises, she writes:

> “In some there are traces of infiltration of mediaeval forms of magic memory, such as the *Ars notoria*, into the art.”

Her possible examples are Weczdorff and Nicolaus Simonis. She relies on Volkmann’s reproductions.

### 6.3 Chapter V, p. 127 n.57

Yates invokes Erasmus’s satirical treatment of shortcuts to learning. The broader target is the promise that ritual or mechanical tricks can replace disciplined education. Any anecdote attributed to Erasmus must be checked against the particular *Colloquy* and edition; it should not automatically be treated as an eyewitness report of *Ars notoria* practice.

### 6.4 Chapter IX, p. 204

Yates recalls Aquinas’s condemnation while discussing magical images and memory. Thomas’s argument is more precise than a simple condemnation of “magic”: the figures and unknown words lack natural causal power and are not divinely instituted signs; therefore, if efficacious, they signify a tacit demonic compact.

### 6.5 Chapter XIII, p. 306

Yates contrasts Bruno’s enormous constructive labor with:

> “the lazy magic of the *Ars notoria*, the practitioner of which just stares at a magical *nota* whilst reciting magical prayers.”

This is polemically memorable but historically unfair. The glossed rite demands fasting, purity, repeated recitation, calendrical observance, sustained inspection, and sometimes months of work. It promises accelerated or infused learning, but its performance is anything but effortless.

### 6.6 Chapter XV, p. 328 n.17

Yates mentions an allegedly magical art heard of by Robert Fludd at Toulouse, involving the “ring of Solomon.” The identification with the *Ars notoria*, Belot, or a particular Solomonic art remains uncertain.

## 7. What later work confirms

Later scholarship confirms that:

- *notae* and prayers are central;
- the goal includes memory and the acquisition of curricular sciences;
- the work circulated among clerics, monks, university students, and educated magical practitioners;
- its Christian language did not protect it from condemnation;
- it belongs to a broad Solomonic current of learned ritual magic;
- its visual forms affected later angelic and visionary works;
- it sometimes mingled with more ordinary memory techniques in manuscripts and print culture.

## 8. What later work corrects or overturns

### 8.1 Véronèse

Véronèse transformed the subject by replacing a handful of late witnesses with a comparative manuscript history. His work establishes:

- a late twelfth- or early thirteenth-century origin, probably in northern Italy;
- multiple recensions rather than a single stable text;
- extensive clerical and university copying;
- a major fourteenth-century glossing and reorganization;
- a carefully structured theurgic ritual;
- a more complex angelology and sacramental logic than Yates’s summary suggests.

His work does **not** demonstrate a first-century origin or direct descent from antique shorthand.

### 8.2 Fanger and Watson

Fanger and Watson show through John of Morigny that practitioners could be learned, self-critical, autobiographical, and deeply engaged in Christian discernment. John’s revisions expose the unstable boundary between prayer, theurgy, superstition, and heresy.

The discovery and edition of surviving *Liber florum* manuscripts overturn the older impression that John’s project was simply extinguished in 1323.

### 8.3 Michael Camille

Camille’s study of the *Ars notoria* images emphasizes their manuscript materiality, visual opacity, and ritual use. The *notae* are not merely illustrations of propositions; their partly unreadable or non-discursive form contributes to their authority.

### 8.4 Carruthers

Mary Carruthers’s major correction to Yates concerns medieval memory culture as a whole. She observes that Yates left the impression that medieval interest in artificial memory arose chiefly from occult involvement. In fact, trained memory was commonplace in monastic reading, preaching, composition, meditation, and schooling.

For this dossier, the consequence is decisive: the *Ars notoria* is an exceptional ritual technology embedded in a much larger nonmagical memorial culture. It must not stand for “medieval memory” as such.

### 8.5 Rossi

Paolo Rossi’s *Clavis universalis* first appeared in 1960, six years before Yates. It traces mnemonic arts, combinatory logic, encyclopedism, and universal-language projects from Lull to Leibniz. Rossi helps separate several genealogies that Yates sometimes binds into a single dramatic “Hermetic” story.

The *Ars notoria* belongs primarily to ritual illumination, not directly to the Lullian combinatory lineage.

### 8.6 Bolzoni and Small

Lina Bolzoni reconstructs the literary, visual, and printed ecology of Renaissance memory: galleries, tableaux, emblematic bodies, theater, rhetoric, and book layout. Jocelyn Penny Small’s *Wax Tablets of the Mind* stresses ancient cognitive practices and the physical technologies of reading and writing.

Neither provides evidence for an antique *Ars notoria*. Their work instead warns against treating every diagram, tablet, emblem, and mnemonic image as one continuous technology.

### 8.7 Hajdu, Sabbatino, and older mnemonic historiography

Older histories by Helga Hajdu and Pasquale Sabbatino remain useful for assembling mnemonic treatises and literary contexts, but they predate the modern codicological reconstruction of the *Ars notoria*. Where they rely on printed witnesses, attributional traditions, or broad intellectual genealogies, Véronèse and Fanger must take precedence.

### 8.8 Bonner and Fidora on Lull

Anthony Bonner and Alexander Fidora emphasize the logical, theological, and demonstrative structures of Ramon Llull’s Art. Llull’s letters, figures, and rotating combinatorial devices operate through explicitly defined principles and relations.

That differs fundamentally from a *nota* whose efficacy resides in prayer, revelation, inspection, and divine or angelic action. Any line “*Ars notoria* → Lull → computer” is therefore unproved.

### 8.9 Bruno scholarship

Rita Sturlese, Michele Ciliberto, Stephen Clucas, Hilary Gatti, and others have reduced the dominance of Yates’s “Hermetic magician” explanation of Bruno. Bruno’s memory works combine mnemonic technique, logic, metaphysics, psychology, language, and sometimes magic; magic is not their single defining feature.

This also weakens Yates’s rhetorical contrast between “lazy” *Ars notoria* and industrious Bruno. They are different intellectual technologies, not merely easy and difficult forms of the same magical mnemonic.

### 8.10 Camillo, Ramus, and the limits of relevance

Bolzoni and Linda Stagno deepen understanding of Giulio Camillo’s theater and its visual, rhetorical, and courtly settings. Walter Ong’s work on Ramus explains diagrammatic method, curricular ordering, and the reorganization of knowledge.

These illuminate the later history of knowledge diagrams, but no evidence establishes Camillo’s theater or Ramist method as descendants of the *notae*.

## History and context

## 9. Ancient background

The Apollonian material belongs to:

- the revival of Pythagorean identity under the Roman Empire;
- philosophical biography;
- ascetic self-fashioning;
- praise of natural memory;
- the association of Mnemosyne with preservation and immortality;
- contemporary suspicions that exceptional learning came from astrology or “Chaldaean” magic.

It does not supply the medieval ritual’s missing genealogy.

## 10. Medieval intellectual context

The *Ars notoria* arose when the schools and universities were organizing knowledge into formal curricula. Its promised subjects map that world:

- trivium: grammar, dialectic, rhetoric;
- quadrivium: arithmetic, geometry, music, astronomy;
- philosophy;
- theology;
- medicine and practical arts in some recensions;
- “exceptive arts,” including divinatory subjects, in some witnesses.

The likely northern Italian setting is significant because Bologna combined legal, notarial, medical, and arts instruction with intense manuscript culture. Yet “Bologna” remains a scholarly reconstruction, not a signed place of composition.

## 11. Institutions and users

The evidence points especially to:

- monasteries and cathedral schools;
- university arts faculties;
- clerical intellectual networks;
- physicians and astrologers;
- educated lay or courtly collectors in the early modern period.

Dominican theology furnished major criticism through Aquinas. Benedictine life furnished John of Morigny. Franciscan and Dominican manuscript networks participated more generally in the transmission of learned magic and mnemonic arts, but the *Ars notoria* cannot be assigned to one order.

## 12. Relationship to classical rhetoric

Classical artificial memory normally requires:

1. an ordered set of places;
2. vivid images assigned to them;
3. repeated mental traversal;
4. retrieval by revisiting the sequence.

The *Ars notoria* instead requires:

1. bodily and spiritual purification;
2. prayers and sacred or mysterious words;
3. inspection of *notae*;
4. calendrical and numerical observance;
5. divine or angelic illumination.

Both involve trained attention, visual artifacts, order, and memory. That structural resemblance does not establish descent.

## 13. Relationship to scholastic psychology

Scholastic psychology ordinarily treats knowledge as acquired through sense, imagination, intellect, repetition, and instruction. Infused knowledge is possible by divine gift but is not controllable by a recipe.

The *Ars notoria* attempts to ritualize such infusion. Aquinas objects precisely because a created sequence of figures and unknown words cannot compel a supernatural gift.

## 14. Relationship to Lullism

Llull’s figures rotate explicitly defined terms to generate propositions and demonstrations. They are semantic and combinatorial. *Ars notoria* figures are ritual and often semantically opaque.

Possible shared environment:

- diagrammatic thought;
- encyclopedic aspiration;
- aspiration to comprehensive knowledge;
- letters and figures as cognitive instruments.

No direct line of borrowing has been demonstrated.

## 15. Hermeticism and astral magic

Some manuscripts coordinate the rite with celestial time, and the Apollonian legend encourages an astral reading. Nevertheless:

- the central prayers invoke God and angels;
- the rite generally petitions rather than coerces;
- the figures are not straightforward planetary talismans;
- the seven rings in Philostratus are not *notae*;
- Renaissance Hermeticism should not be projected backward as the system’s original framework.

## 16. Ramism and universal language

Ramist diagrams arrange curriculum by dichotomies and visible order. Universal-language schemes assign stable concepts to characters. The *Ars notoria* does neither systematically.

The resemblance lies in the dream of mastering knowledge through visible order. The causal genealogy from *notae* to Ramus, Wilkins, Leibniz, or computing is not documented.

## People

## 17. Apollonius of Tyana

Historical first-century philosopher and itinerant sage, subsequently surrounded by miracle, magic, alchemical, astrological, and Solomonic traditions. He is the exponent of Philostratus’s ascetic memory ideal, not the demonstrable medieval author.

## 18. Philostratus

Third-century sophist and biographer. He praises Apollonius but also rejects the claim that Dionysius taught memory by magic. His texts preserve both sides of the ancient debate: marvelous sage biography and rationalizing criticism.

## 19. Julia Domna

Philostratus says the empress Julia Domna encouraged the *Life* and made Damis’s memoirs available. Her role as patron belongs to Philostratus’s account; the authenticity and form of the Damis material remain disputed.

## 20. Iarchas

A literary Indian Brahmin king or sage in Philostratus. He gives the seven rings. There is no independent evidence for him as a historical teacher of Apollonius.

## 21. Solomon

Biblical king transformed in late antique and medieval tradition into master of demons, angels, occult names, seals, and revealed wisdom. His *Ars notoria* authorship is pseudepigraphic but central to the text’s authority.

## 22. The medieval “Apollonius”

The name may invoke Apollonius of Tyana, but medieval texts sometimes circulated names more flexibly than modern authorship permits. It is safest to write “Pseudo-Apollonius” unless a manuscript explicitly identifies the Tyanean.

## 23. Thomas Aquinas

The most influential critic. He knew enough of the practice to identify fasting, prayers, inspection of figures, and unknown words. His condemnation provided a durable theological framework for classifying it as illicit and ineffective.

## 24. John of Morigny

A practitioner, critic, reviser, and visionary. He reports unsuccessful and troubling experiments, seeks Marian authorization, and constructs a replacement art. His autobiography is the richest surviving evidence for the subjective world of a medieval *Ars notoria* practitioner.

## 25. Simon Forman

The Elizabethan astrologer-physician copied and annotated a glossed *Ars notoria*. Oxford, Bodleian, Ashmole MS 208 bears the completion date 14 July 1601. His ownership proves early modern practical interest; it does not prove that every prescribed operation was completed.

## 26. Wetzdorf and Nicolaus Simonis

Both are authors of early printed artificial-memory books. Their relationship to *Ars notoria* is analogical or syncretic, not a demonstrated direct succession.

## 27. Anecdotes requested in the catalogue

### The auctioneer

No securely identified *Ars notoria* source found in the consulted primary and critical literature records an auctioneer using this rite. Auctioneer feats belong to the wider anecdotal history of trained natural memory.

### The professor’s party

No primary witness located connects such an anecdote specifically to *Ars notoria*. It may derive from Renaissance mnemonic biography or Erasmus’s satire; attribution to this system should be suspended until the exact text is identified.

### The student who lost his memory

Stories of mnemonic students suffering confusion or losing natural memory circulate in criticism of artificial memory. John of Morigny reports failure, fear, temptation, and visionary disturbance, but that is not securely the same anecdote.

### The accused magician

Two documented cases are relevant:

- Dionysius of Miletus was rumored to teach memory by Chaldaean arts, but Philostratus rejects the charge.
- John of Morigny’s work was denounced as a renamed revival of condemned *Ars notoria*; a book was publicly burned in Paris in 1323.

Simon Forman was repeatedly suspected of illicit magic, but his surviving *Ars notoria* copy is not itself evidence of a prosecution specifically for using that book.

## The magic question

## 28. Was it magical?

### By its practitioners

The text often presents itself as holy wisdom, prayer, divine revelation, or an *ars* granted to Solomon—not as demonic magic. It prefers invocation and supplication to coercive conjuration.

### By Aquinas

Yes: illicit and ineffective magical observance.

Aquinas writes:

> **ars notoria et illicita est, et inefficax**

Gloss: “The notory art is both unlawful and ineffective.”

His reason is exact:

> **inspectione quarundam figurarum, et prolatione quorundam ignotorum verborum**

Gloss: “the inspection of certain figures and the utterance of certain unknown words.”

These things have no natural power to cause knowledge and are not sacramental signs instituted by God. Therefore they must be empty conventional signs implicated in:

> **pacta quaedam significationum cum Daemonibus**

Gloss: “certain compacts of signs agreed with demons.”

Aquinas also says that divine infusion of wisdom is possible, as in Solomon’s case, but is distributed freely by the Holy Spirit, not produced by fixed observances.

### By chroniclers

The *Grandes Chroniques de France* calls it:

> **une heresie et sorcerie condampnée, qui est nommée en latin Ars notoria**

Gloss: “a condemned heresy and sorcery called in Latin *Ars notoria*.”

The chronicle explains that different figures corresponded to sciences, were inspected at specified times with fasting and prayers, and were supposed to pour knowledge into the practitioner. It alleges that the unknown names were believed to be names of devils.

### By modern historians

“Learned ritual magic” or “Christian theurgy” is generally more precise than “demonic grimoire.” These labels describe the rite’s operation and social classification without accepting either its self-defense or its opponents’ demonology.

## 29. Talismanic?

The *notae* can look like seals or talismans, and some later derivatives integrate astrological image theory. But their primary prescribed use is inspection accompanied by prayer, not necessarily manufacture under a celestial configuration to capture stellar rays.

Thus:

- **visually talismanic:** often;
- **ritually analogous to talismans:** sometimes;
- **technically identical to astral talismans:** no.

## 30. Heretical?

Aquinas calls it superstitious and unlawful, not a doctrinal heresy in the narrowest sense. The Parisian chronicle uses “heresy and sorcery” polemically for John of Morigny’s derivative.

Consequences included:

- theological condemnation;
- danger of confiscation;
- destruction of at least one related book in 1323;
- concealment, abbreviation, rewriting, or orthodox reframing;
- removal of suspect names and diagrams in some transmissions.

There is no evidence of a Europe-wide inquisitorial campaign against every owner.

## 31. John of Morigny and the burned book

The *Grandes Chroniques* says that a monk of Morigny revived the condemned art under a new title and pretended to receive Marian authorization. It reports that his book was burned at Paris.

Later research complicates this account:

- John himself criticized aspects of the *Ars notoria*;
- his *Liber florum* is a distinct rewriting;
- surviving manuscripts show continued revision and circulation;
- sixteenth-century antiquarians falsely credited him with inventing the *Ars notoria*;
- nineteenth-century summaries reduced the episode to a monk caught with a forbidden book.

The chronicle is evidence for condemnation and public representation, not an impartial account of John’s intentions.

## Afterlife

## 32. Medieval derivatives

The strongest documented afterlife lies in:

- John of Morigny’s *Liber florum*;
- the *Liber iuratus Honorii* or *Sworn Book of Honorius*, whose northern recension incorporates prayers related to the glossed *Ars notoria*;
- abridged and reorganized *Artes notoriae*;
- intersections with notarial shorthand, cipher, angelic invocation, and university encyclopedism;
- late medieval arts of visions, crosses, and angelic instruction.

## 33. Early modern survival

The tradition continued through:

- manuscripts owned or copied by astrologer-physicians such as Simon Forman;
- the defective Latin print around 1600;
- Turner’s English translation of 1657;
- insertion into later Solomonic collections;
- eighteenth-century manuscript copies and occult compilations.

The diagrams were often omitted in print, transforming a visual-ritual book into an obscure sequence of prayers.

## 34. Dante, frescoes, and emblem books

There is no demonstrated direct influence of the *Ars notoria* on Dante. Dante’s arts of memory, moral topographies, and visionary structures belong to broader medieval mnemonic, theological, rhetorical, and diagrammatic cultures.

Likewise, no general genealogy from *notae* to moralizing fresco cycles has been established. Frescoes and emblem books share visual condensation, ordered knowledge, and symbolic figures, but those traits are widespread.

**Finding:** suggestive analogy, no documented transmission.

## 35. Camillo, Bruno, and Fludd

Camillo, Bruno, and Fludd pursue totalizing visual or spatial systems. Yet:

- Camillo constructs a theater with ordered grades and mythological images;
- Bruno builds combinatorial mnemonic machinery with lettered wheels and image sets;
- Fludd constructs cosmic theaters and architectural mnemonic spaces;
- *Ars notoria* petitions for illumination through prayer and diagram inspection.

Yates’s history encourages readers to see one continuous occult current. Later scholarship favors multiple interacting traditions.

## 36. Borges, Eco, and modern literature

Borges’s Funes suffers from limitless episodic retention; he does not practice the *Ars notoria*. Eco repeatedly explores encyclopedias, signs, combinatorics, and artificial languages. These are thematic echoes of total knowledge, not proven receptions of the medieval rite.

## 37. Modern memory palaces and athletes

Contemporary memory competitors predominantly use:

- loci;
- peg systems;
- person-action-object encoding;
- phonetic number systems;
- deliberate rehearsal.

That descends from classical and modern mnemonic technique, not from angelic illumination. Modern presentations of *Ars notoria* as “the original memory palace” are historically false.

## 38. Computing and combinatorics

The defensible historical line is:

> Llull’s explicitly combinatorial figures → Renaissance and seventeenth-century combinatory projects → Leibnizian universal-characteristic and calculus ambitions → much later histories of formal logic and computation.

The *Ars notoria* participates in the aspiration to comprehensive knowledge but does not perform formal combinatorial calculation. A line:

> *Ars notoria* → Llull → Leibniz → computer

is a modern narrative, not a documented chain.

## 39. Contemporary occult revival

Modern facsimiles and practical editions have restored diagrams omitted from Turner. Contemporary practitioners often interpret the work through psychology, angel magic, altered states, “egregores,” or accelerated learning.

These are modern receptions. Claims that digital display invalidates the figures, that the rite guarantees photographic memory, or that its prayers can teach modern programming are neither medieval rules nor experimentally established findings.

## Controversies and disputes

## 40. Was Apollonius the author?

**Status:** legend/pseudepigraphy.

Evidence for:

- medieval prologues name Apollonius;
- his ancient reputation made him a plausible authority for astrology and magical wisdom.

Evidence against:

- no antique Greek text;
- no reference in Philostratus;
- no continuous transmission from late antiquity;
- medieval Latin curricular and liturgical structures;
- earliest witnesses are about 1225.

## 41. Was Solomon the author?

**Status:** sacred attribution, historically unsupported.

The attribution supplies biblical precedent for divinely infused wisdom. It is comparable to widespread medieval Solomonic pseudepigraphy.

## 42. When and where did it originate?

**Best reconstruction:** late twelfth century, northern Italy, plausibly the Bologna region.

Evidence:

- early-thirteenth-century manuscript witnesses;
- curricular structure associated with emerging universities;
- possible earlier reference in Gervase of Tilbury;
- Italian scribal and intellectual context.

Uncertainties:

- no authorial manuscript;
- no securely localized archetype;
- earlier textual layers may have circulated independently.

## 43. Does it descend from shorthand *notae*?

**Yates’s hypothesis:** perhaps.

Evidence favoring some association:

- the word *nota*;
- letters and signs within the figures;
- medieval intersections among notation, notarial learning, cipher, and mnemonic abbreviation;
- a later *Ars notoria notarie* explicitly connects notory art with notarial and Tironian signs.

Evidence against direct descent:

- earliest ritual *notae* are complex prayer figures, not a decipherable shorthand inventory;
- no stemma links Tironian notes to the diagrams;
- the operative theory is illumination rather than abbreviation;
- the semantics of many marks are intentionally obscure.

**Verdict:** possible lexical or cultural interaction; direct descent unproved.

## 44. Is it an art of memory?

Three answers coexist:

- **Yes, in medieval purpose:** improving memory is an explicit objective.
- **No, in the narrow classical sense:** it lacks a systematic locus–image encoding and retrieval procedure.
- **Yes, as ritual cognitive technology:** it disciplines attention and seeks transformed intellectual faculties.

Calling it an “art of memory” is therefore legitimate only if the meaning is stated.

## 45. Are the *notae* mnemonic diagrams?

**Disputed.**

Some may structure disciplines or support recollection. But the text treats inspection as efficacious beyond ordinary cognition. Their opacity, sacred names, and prescribed ritual use distinguish them from transparent teaching diagrams.

## 46. Is the rite “lazy magic”?

**Yates’s interpretation, overturned as description.**

It promises extraordinary gains but demands an arduous regimen. “Lazy” captures an external criticism of seeking knowledge without normal study; it does not accurately describe the labor of performance.

## 47. Was Wetzdorf printed around 1600?

**No.**

Yates’s “circa 1600” is contradicted by modern incunable catalogues, printer attribution, watermark evidence, ISTC, GW, and the Munich copy. The correct date is about 1500–1501.

## 48. Are Wetzdorf and Simon evidence of direct *Ars notoria* infiltration?

**Disputed.**

Their magical-looking frontispieces, rings, religious language, bodily figures, and claims of secrecy support Yates’s comparison. Their actual mnemonic procedures, however, include alphabetic and grammatical coding unlike the angelic rite. No direct borrowing has been demonstrated.

## 49. Was John of Morigny executed?

**Legend or unsupported inference.**

The source reports the burning of a book, not John’s execution. Nineteenth-century retellings speculated about abjuration or penance. Surviving revisions suggest he continued working after an earlier condemnation.

## 50. Did the University of Paris formally condemn John in exactly 1323?

The chronicle places the burning in 1323. Scholarship also identifies an earlier censorship phase around 1315. The responsible institutional mechanisms and exact relationship between the events remain under study. “The University condemned John in 1323” is plausible shorthand but less precise than the evidence warrants.

## 51. Are the mysterious words Hebrew, Chaldean, Greek, or Arabic?

**Textual tradition:** yes, or derived from primordial sacred languages.

**Philological result:** some recognizable fragments, much corruption and invention, no single recoverable source language.

**Modern notarikon theory:** an interesting proposal, not established consensus.

## 52. Did the figures cause visions?

**Practitioner testimony:** John of Morigny reports visions within a derived practice.

**Historical finding:** ritual contexts were designed to elicit visionary confirmation.

**Empirical claim:** supernatural causation is not historically verifiable.

## 53. Did Yates identify one continuous occult memory tradition?

Yates did not always claim literal unbroken transmission, but her narrative encourages it. Later work finds overlapping but distinct traditions:

- classical rhetorical mnemonics;
- monastic and scholastic memory craft;
- Neopythagorean discipline;
- Solomonic ritual magic;
- Lullian combinatorics;
- Renaissance emblematic and theatrical memory;
- Ramist method;
- universal-language projects.

Their interactions must be proved case by case.

## Open questions

1. What was the exact relationship among the earliest A, A2, and lost antecedent forms?
2. Did a pre-1200 collection of prayers circulate without figures?
3. How much of the system’s curricular organization originated in Bologna?
4. Were individual *notae* copied as stable visual objects, or freely recomposed by scribes?
5. Can recurring graphic elements be assigned stable meanings across manuscripts?
6. Does the term *nota* derive primarily from “mark,” “making known,” mnemonic notation, notarial culture, or several simultaneous associations?
7. What precise earlier work did Gervase of Tilbury know?
8. Which manuscripts show actual use—wear, corrections, repetition marks, added calendars—as opposed to collecting?
9. How did users reconcile ordinary study with promises of infused knowledge?
10. What was the institutional process behind the 1315 and 1323 condemnations of John of Morigny’s work?
11. Which prayers passed from the glossed *Ars notoria* into the *Liber iuratus*, and at what stage?
12. Can Wetzdorf’s and Simon’s magical imagery be tied to specific ritual-magic books, rather than to a general visual culture of secrecy?
13. Which “professor’s party,” “auctioneer,” and “student who lost his memory” anecdotes were intended by the catalogue’s compilers? No source-specific identification was recoverable from the leads supplied.
14. What did Yates consult for her erroneous “circa 1600” dating of Wetzdorf?
15. To what extent did omission of the *notae* from early printed editions alter the practice and its later interpretation?
16. How far do present-day reconstructions silently combine incompatible medieval recensions?
