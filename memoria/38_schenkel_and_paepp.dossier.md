# Schenkel and Paepp: the occult memory detected: Research Dossier

## Basic identification

**Name:** Schenkel and Paepp: the occult memory detected  
**Catalogue position:** System 38 of 45  
**Exponents:** Lambert Thomas Schenkel—also Schenckel, Schenckels, Schenkelius, *Dusilvius/Ducissilvius*—and Johannes/Joannes/Jan Paepp, Paëpp, or Päepp  
**Dates:** Schenkel’s first memory publications, 1593; expanded *Gazophylacium*, title-dated 1610, with some catalogues assigning its manufacture to 1609 or recording a 1611 issue; Paepp’s *Schenckelius detectus*, 1617; *Eisagoge*, *Artificiosae memoriae fundamenta*, and *Crisis*, 1618–19  
**Principal texts:** Schenkel, *De memoria libri duo* (Douai, 1593); *Gazophylacium artis memoriae* (Strasbourg, 1610); French *Le Magazin des sciences, ou le vray art de mémoire* (Paris, 1623); Paepp, *Schenckelius detectus* (Lyons and Cologne issues, 1617); *Eisagoge seu introductio facilis in praxim artificiosae memoriae*, *Artificiosae memoriae fundamenta*, and *Crisis Iani Phaosphori* (Lyons, 1618–19).  
**Principle in one sentence:** A commercially guarded elaboration of the classical art of ordered loci and striking images—extended to letters, syllables, words, academic subjects, and rapid public demonstrations—which Paepp decoded, supplemented with Bruno-derived imagery, and framed through Thomist, Platonic, and Hermetic statements about cosmic order.  
**Yates:** *The Art of Memory*, Chapter XIII, pp. 299–302; Chapter XV, p. 338; Chapter XVII, pp. 374 and 380–81 (pagination of the 1966 Routledge & Kegan Paul edition and its photographic reprints). The searchable Warburg scan confirms the principal discussion at pp. 299–302 and the later Descartes and Leibniz passages. [Warburg Institute scan](https://resources.warburg.sas.ac.uk/mnemosyne/fq/yates_memory.pdf)

### Evidentiary labels used below

- **[DT] Documented text:** stated or shown in a primary text, contemporary letter, privilege, approbation, or surviving copy.
- **[SR] Scholarly reconstruction:** an inference or reconstruction made by a modern scholar from primary evidence.
- **[TR] Tradition:** a report transmitted by historical compilers but not presently anchored in a contemporary document.
- **[YI] Yates’s interpretation:** Yates’s historical or philosophical reading, rather than a statement made by Schenkel or Paepp.
- **[DISPUTED]** competing readings or unresolved bibliographical facts.
- **[LEGEND]** a picturesque story for which the recoverable evidentiary chain is late, defective, or absent.
- **[MODERN]** a modern analogy, adaptation, or invention.

The labels matter especially here. “Occult” in *Schenckelius detectus* can mean hidden or withheld; it does not by itself mean supernatural magic. Yates repeatedly moves between those meanings.

## The system in detail

### 1. What Schenkel actually inherited

**[DT]** Schenkel’s basic apparatus belongs to the ancient artificial memory transmitted through the pseudo-Ciceronian *Rhetorica ad Herennium*, Cicero’s *De oratore*, Quintilian, Aristotle’s *De memoria et reminiscentia*, and medieval commentaries by Albert the Great and Thomas Aquinas. Its two indispensable components are:

1. an ordered, firmly memorized series of **places**;
2. memorable **images** temporarily or permanently attached to those places.

The compact ancient definition is *constat igitur artificiosa memoria ex locis et imaginibus*—“artificial memory therefore consists of places and images”—*Ad Herennium* III.29.

**[DT]** Schenkel’s definition of memory, reproduced in the *Gazophylacium*, is conventional rather than occult:

> *Memoria est firma perceptio rerum et verborum atque ordinis eorum, et fidelis retentio.*

“Memory is the firm apprehension and faithful retention of things, words, and their order.”

**[DT/SR]** The classical art was never merely a collection of bizarre pictures. The order of loci supplies serial position; the image makes otherwise elusive material sensibly present; mental traversal supplies retrieval. Schenkel’s novelty lies less in this principle than in its scale, codification, vocational applications, commercial delivery, and alphabetic or syllabic encodings.

### 2. Places

#### Selection

**[DT]** In the *Gazophylacium*, as in Romberch and Rossellius, a place is a stable spatial receptacle capable of holding an image. The most serviceable places come from buildings—rooms, walls, corners, windows, doors, niches, furniture, and other distinct architectural features.

**[DT]** The surviving Strasbourg book contains woodcut illustrations and spatial diagrams. Its bibliographical description is “[8] leaves, 378 pages, [7] leaves; illustrated with woodcuts”; the Berlin copy is shelfmark **SBB Nn 8292**, VD17 **39:139181Y**. [Deutsche Digitale Bibliothek/SBB record and scan](https://www.deutsche-digitale-bibliothek.de/item/B4Y7IQYINA64RI224QZKW2YU5Y3HVH33)

**[DT/SR]** The diagrams should be read as plans or schemas of addressable surfaces, not realistic perspective views of an imaginary occult temple. Yates accurately says that Schenkel provides “diagrams of rooms containing memory places,” but her context encourages the reader to compare them with Camillo and Bruno. The more economical explanation is textbook pedagogy: the plate teaches the pupil how to divide an ordinary visual field into stable stations.

#### Order

**[DT]** The practitioner learns the places before loading them. Their sequence must be:

- fixed;
- distinct;
- readily surveyed;
- repeatable forward and backward;
- separated sufficiently to prevent one image from contaminating its neighbor;
- available for repeated reuse.

**[DT/SR]** Schenkel’s printed shorthand assigns words and abbreviations to architectural or mnemonic elements. A later English transcription of the tables includes *domus* (house), *angulus* (corner), *sedes* (seat), *locus*, *ostium* (door), *receptaculum*, *fenestra* (window), *convexum*, and *latus*. The table also couples numerical values with concrete or geometrical cues: one with a wax candle or wine, two with a swan, three with a triangle, four with a quadrangle, five with a hand, six with a star, seven with a rule or axe, eight with a cup, nine with a hook or related form, and ten with a ring or circle. The late transcription is not a substitute for the early book, but it corresponds to the tabular material reproduced from the Schenkel–Sommer tradition. [Richard Grey/Feinaigle-era survey](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Memory_Systems_New_and_Old/Chapter_2)

#### Scale

**[DT/TR]** Schenkel and his authorized teacher Martin Sommer advertised enormous capacities. Sommer claimed that a trained user could classify 25,000 disordered portraits in the time required to say a *Pater noster*. This is an advertisement, not an independently verified measurement.

**[SR]** Satoko Kusukawa and later historians of diagrams have noted the general early-modern movement from mentally inhabitable loci toward increasingly large paper schemas. A modern conference abstract specifically characterizes Schenkel as pushing the palace toward “millions of loci” and then supplementing it with dichotomous, Ramist-looking study diagrams. That is a useful description of his system’s direction, but “millions” should not be treated as a demonstrated human capacity.

### 3. Images

#### General rules

**[DT]** Schenkel repeats the classical requirement that images be visibly apprehensible and connected with what is to be remembered. The inherited rules favor:

- human actors, preferably personally known;
- action rather than static resemblance;
- concrete instruments;
- unusual scale, motion, violence, beauty, ugliness, comedy, impropriety, or emotional force;
- a clear relation—natural, verbal, symbolic, contrary, causal, or punning—between image and target.

**[DT]** The lineage is *Ad Herennium* III.35–37: everyday things are easily forgotten, whereas something exceptionally base, dishonorable, unusual, great, unbelievable, or ridiculous adheres longer. Thomas gives the psychological rationale: *spirituales et simplices intentiones facile ex anima elabuntur, nisi quibusdam similitudinibus corporalibus quasi alligentur*—spiritual and simple intentions easily slip from the soul unless bound to bodily likenesses—*Summa theologiae* II–II, q.49, a.1, ad 2.

**[DT/SR]** Schenkel’s language is often cryptographically transposed. Paepp’s 1617 volume ends with a *clavicula*, a “little key,” for decoding it. Manuel Mertens demonstrates that this is not unbreakable cryptography but a barrier to uninitiated readers and a sales device.

#### A worked Schenkel example: “oma”

**[DT]** The *Gazophylacium*, p. 6, gives an encoded instruction deciphered by Paepp’s key. To remember the invented syllabic sequence **oma**:

- choose a known person whose name begins with **o**, “Oliver”;
- imagine Oliver performing an action with a *matula*, an earthen vessel;
- have him pour it out or smash it;
- Oliver supplies **o** and *matula* supplies **ma**.

The resulting scene—Oliver breaking a pot—encodes **o-ma**.

The partly encoded Latin reads:

> *Imago primae litterae ponatur in loco … ut si exprimendum esset hoc vocabulum oma … Oliverius, qui matulam effundit vel frangit…*

“The image of the first letter is put in a place … so, if the word *oma* is to be expressed … Oliver pours out or breaks a pot.”

Mertens verifies the example against *Gazophylacium*, p. 6, and the *clavicula* appended to *Schenckelius detectus*. [Mertens, *Magic and Memory in Giordano Bruno*](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/isbn/9789004372672/html?lang=en)

#### Persons, actions, and instruments

**[DT/SR]** The “oma” example exposes the system’s generative grammar:

| Mnemonic component | Function |
|---|---|
| familiar person | initial letter, class, or stable agent |
| action | medial letters, grammatical relation, or conceptual link |
| instrument/object | additional syllable or semantic specification |
| fixed locus | ordinal address |
| exaggerated interaction | associative binding |

This resembles Bruno’s agent–action–attribute machinery, but it also follows ordinary rebus and *memoria verborum* practice. Similarity does not prove direct Brunian transmission.

### 4. Memory for things and memory for words

**[DT]** Like the ancient handbooks, the Schenkel corpus distinguishes:

- *memoria rerum*: remembering subjects, arguments, divisions, causes, propositions, and the order of discourse;
- *memoria verborum*: remembering exact wording, letters, syllables, names, numbers, or arbitrary verbal sequences.

**[DT/SR]** Memory for things uses images representing the meaning of an argument. Memory for words requires a more cumbersome phonetic code. Schenkel’s alphabetic agents and objects enable the latter, but at the cost of learning a second symbolic vocabulary.

**[DT]** His vocational advertisements promise applications to:

- sermons and public speeches;
- legal briefs and opposing arguments;
- books read in libraries;
- dictation to multiple scribes;
- languages and grammar;
- scholastic disciplines;
- names, portraits, sentences, and numbers.

**[DT/TR]** Sommer’s prospectus says a lawyer with a hundred cases can answer any client as though he had just read the brief, and can retain both his own arguments and his adversary’s refutations. It also promises alternating dictation on unrelated subjects to ten or thirty writers. These are documented promotional claims, not documented performance results.

### 5. Training and retrieval

**[DT/TR]** Later histories, apparently drawing upon Sommer’s printed prospectus, report a course of nine lessons or nine days, followed by approximately half an hour’s daily exercise.

**[DT/SR]** The operational sequence is reconstructable:

1. Construct and overlearn an ordered stock of loci.
2. Learn standard alphabets of persons, objects, actions, numbers, and disciplinary signs.
3. Analyze the target into things, words, letters, syllables, propositions, or divisions.
4. Select an appropriate image or compose an agent–action–instrument scene.
5. Place it at the next locus.
6. Bind it through vivid action and repeated attention.
7. Retrieve by mentally surveying the ordered places.
8. Decode the person, action, object, abbreviation, or disciplinary sign.
9. Clear or reuse temporary places after the information is no longer needed.

**[DT]** Thomas’s fourth memory precept—frequent meditation on what is to be retained—supplies rehearsal. Paepp explicitly closes his philosophical sequence by returning to Aristotle and Thomas on repetition.

### 6. Disciplinary and Ramist organization

**[DT/SR]** Schenkel’s later materials do more than store lists. They divide grammar, rhetoric, logic, physics, metaphysics, and related subjects into hierarchies. Abbreviations in the surviving or transcribed tables—*Gr.*, *La.*, *Rh.*, *LG.*, *Ph.*, *Mph.*, *Sy.*, *Prosodia*—show a study apparatus organized by discipline.

**[SR]** This is the point of contact with Ramism: knowledge is divided into branching heads and subheads, then localized or symbolized. Walter Ong’s history of Ramus helps explain the cultural setting, but it does not prove that Schenkel simply adopted a particular Ramist diagram. The system is hybrid: classical spatial order, scholastic divisions, humanist grammar, and printed tables.

### 7. What Paepp “detects”

#### The title’s meaning

**[DT]** *Schenckelius detectus: seu memoria artificialis hactenus occultata … nunc primum … luce donata* means approximately:

> “Schenkel detected—or uncovered: artificial memory, hitherto concealed and long desired by many, now for the first time brought to light.”

Here *occultata* means concealed. The title promises exposure of a trade secret.

**[YI/DISPUTED]** Yates turns this bibliographical “uncovering” into the detection of an occult-philosophical undercurrent. That move is ingenious, but the title alone cannot support it.

#### Paepp’s textual sequence

**[DT]** In *Artificiosae memoriae fundamenta*, pp. 26–27, Paepp moves through the following authorities:

1. Thomas Aquinas, *Summa* II–II, q.49, a.1, on order and corporeal similitudes;
2. “the fifth sermon of Trismegistus in the *Pimander*,” actually Corpus Hermeticum V in Ficino’s Latin arrangement, on the visible and invisible God;
3. Plato’s *Timaeus*, concerning cosmic order;
4. Cicero, *De oratore* II, on order as memory’s great aid;
5. pseudo-Cicero, *Ad Herennium* III, on loci and images;
6. Aristotle and Thomas on frequent meditation.

**[DT]** The Hermetic treatise opens from the invisibility of God to his manifestation through the ordered cosmos. Paepp’s juxtaposition is real.

**[YI]** Yates reads the sequence as showing “a transition from the places and images of the artificial memory to the order of the universe ecstatically perceived as a religious experience.” That is a historical interpretation of adjacency, not Paepp’s explicit declaration that a mnemonic exercise is a Hermetic rite.

#### Bruno

**[DT]** Paepp cites *Jordanus Brunus* and quotes or paraphrases *De umbris idearum*. Yates’s references are *Eisagoge*, pp. 36–113, and *Crisis*, pp. 12–13.

**[DT/YI]** Paepp supplies lists of agents, actions, and images comparable to those in Bruno’s *De imaginum, signorum et idearum compositione*. Yates calls them “magic images.” The textual resemblance is observable; “magic” is her classification and must be tested image by image.

**[DT]** *Schenckelius detectus*, p. 21, says that *arcana philosophiae mysteria*—“arcane mysteries of philosophy”—are contained in the art.

**[DISPUTED]** That sentence supports an esoteric or initiatory self-presentation, but not necessarily talismanic astral magic. *Arcanum* can signify advanced, restricted, or commercially protected knowledge.

## The sources

### A. Schenkel’s primary corpus

#### 1593: *De memoria libri duo*

**[DT]** Full bibliographical form: Lambertus Thomas Schenckelius, *De memoria libri duo*, Douai, apud Jacobum Boscardum, 1593, octavo, approximately 28 leaves in some descriptions.

**[DT]** A French companion or version appeared at Arras in 1593 as *Introduction du Traicté de la mémoire, divisé en deux livres*, printed by Guillaume de la Rivière.

**[DT/SR]** Clément Poupard uses surviving approbation brochures and the copy bound at the Royal Library of Belgium, **KBR VH 8847 A LP**, to reconstruct the circumstances of publication and Schenkel’s early demonstrations.

**[DISPUTED]** “Two books” can mislead. In the French presentation Schenkel says:

> *Le second livre est pour ceux là seulement qui auront appris l’art de l’Auteur.*

“The second book is only for those who have learned the author’s art.”

Thus the public book advertises a withheld practical book or section. Surviving issues and later compilations do not all contain identical matter.

#### 1594–97: approbations and promotional dossiers

**[DT]** Important ephemeral titles include:

- *Copia speciminum artis memoriae Bruxellae, Leodii, Tornaci et alibi editorum* (Liège, Christian Ouwerx, 1595);
- *De memoria liber secundus, in quo est Ars Memoriae* (no place or printer, 1595);
- testimonies and approbations printed in later collections.

These documents are important because they record named witnesses, dates, students, cities, and demonstrations. They are also advertisements assembled by Schenkel and therefore require corroboration.

#### 1609/10: *Gazophylacium artis memoriae*

**[DT]** Title:

> *Gazophylacium artis memoriae, in quo duobus libris omnia et singula ea quae ad absolutam huius cognitionem inserviunt recondita habentur…*

“Treasury of the art of memory, in which, in two books, are stored all the individual things serving toward complete knowledge of it.”

Title-page imprint: Strasbourg, Anton Bertram, 1610. Google’s digitized copy derives from Rome’s Biblioteca Alessandrina. [Google Books](https://books.google.com/books?id=b1EtrKzta34C&output=html_text)

**[DISPUTED]** The Berlin catalogue assigns the item to 1609 while reproducing the familiar 1610 title tradition; other bibliographies cite Strasbourg 1611. These may represent printing completion, issue, or catalogue normalization rather than three substantively revised editions. A signature-by-signature census remains needed.

**[DT]** Contents include:

- a first book on the utility and marvelous effects of trained memory;
- a second on its foundations, method, and practice;
- texts associated with “Joannes Austriacus,” Girolamo Marafioti, and Johann Spangenberg/Herd;
- historical exempla and authorities;
- architectural and mnemonic diagrams;
- ciphered practical instructions.

#### 1614 and 1619 apologetic/teaching texts

**[DT]** *Brevis tractatus de utilitatibus et effectibus admirabilibus artis memoriae* circulated in 1614.

**[DT]** A Strasbourg collection printed by Eberhard Zetzner in 1619 contains:

- *Methodus sive declaratio … quo modo Latina lingua sex mensium spatio doceri*;
- *Brevis tractatus*;
- *Elogia*;
- *Brevissima refutatio*;
- *Apologia in quosdam*.

The Malines/Stedelijke Erfgoedbibliotheek collection is cited by Mertens as shelfmark beginning **M**; published catalogues should be consulted for the complete modern call number. It is the essential source for Schenkel’s own account of his accusation and business disputes.

#### 1623: French *Magazin*

**[DT]** *Le Magazin des sciences, ou le vray art de mémoire descouvert par Schenckelius*, translated or adapted by Adrian Le Cuirot, Paris, 1623. Yates used this extensively.

**[DISPUTED]** Some catalogues give 1625, and the degree to which Le Cuirot translates, abridges, rearranges, or supplements the *Gazophylacium* needs systematic collation. Calling it simply “the French translation” conceals this problem.

#### Later witnesses

- *Gazophylacium* issue, Venice, Antonio Padovano, 1617: 58 pages in duodecimo. [IxTheo catalogue](https://ixtheo.de/Record/329777041)
- Cologne *Schenckelius detectus* issues and the 1643 Cologne reprint.
- *Variorum de arte memoriae tractatus sex*, Frankfurt and Leipzig, 1678.
- Johann Christoph Klüber, *Compendium der Mnemonik … nach L. Schenckel und M. Sommer*, Erlangen, 1804.

### C. Manuscripts

**[Negative finding]** No authorial manuscript of the mnemonic treatises by Schenkel or Paepp emerged from this search. This system is transmitted principally through printed books, Sammelbände, handwritten annotations in printed copies, privileges, approbations, and correspondence.

**[DT]** Relevant archival witnesses include:

- Schenkel’s autograph petition, Brussels, April 1624, published from the Belgian record by René Hoven;
- letters of Laevinus Torrentius, edited by Marie Delcourt and Jean Hoyoux, vol. II, letters 1032 and 1083;
- KBR **VH 8847 A LP**, an important composite witness;
- annotated copies at the Wellcome, Getty, Folger, Berlin Staatsbibliothek, and Senate House Library.

**[Open bibliographical problem]** A union census of all extant copies, bindings, marginalia, reset pages, and diagram states does not appear to have been published.

## Yates’s account and the scholarship since

### What Yates says

**[YI]** Yates makes five principal claims:

1. Schenkel is a “post-Brunian” itinerant memory teacher.
2. His published system looks conventional: classical places and images enlarged through the Romberch–Rossellius textbook tradition.
3. His obscurity, secrecy, and citation of Trithemius make an occult undercurrent possible.
4. Paepp “lets the cat out of the bag” by naming Bruno and joining classical/scholastic mnemonic texts to Hermetic contemplation.
5. The pair suggests that commercial memory teaching could transmit “a Hermetic religious message, or a Hermetic sect.”

Her most cautious sentence deserves emphasis:

> “It could be a rational mnemotechnic…”

This is a hypothesis framed against another hypothesis, not a finding that Schenkel practiced astral magic.

### What Yates got right

**[Confirmed]**

- Schenkel’s main operational mechanism is classical loci and images.
- His books contain extensive rules, rooms, places, image material, authorities, and historical exempla.
- His instruction was obscure and deliberately incomplete.
- Paepp supplies a deciphering key.
- Paepp names and uses Bruno.
- Paepp juxtaposes Thomas, the *Pimander*, Plato, Cicero, the *Ad Herennium*, and Aristotle.
- Schenkel was accused of magic.
- Descartes read and criticized Schenkel.
- Leibniz knew Schenkel’s work or reputation.

### Major corrections

#### Schenkel did name Bruno

**[Correction: DT]** Yates says Schenkel never names Bruno. Mertens has shown that Schenkel does name him, specifically in connection with obscure writing. This directly overturns one of Yates’s premises for treating Paepp as the first indiscreet revealer. [Mertens, 2018 bibliographical record](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/isbn/9789004372672/html?lang=en)

#### The accusation preceded the books Yates associates with suspicion

**[Correction: DT/SR]** Yates’s chronology implies that suspicions arose after Schenkel became known through his publications. Schenkel was already under suspicion by January 1593, probably November 1592, at the beginning of his mnemonic career. The accusation therefore cannot have been caused by occult contents in the 1610 *Gazophylacium*. Poupard and Mertens reconstruct the chronology from the 1590s approbations, Torrentius’s letters, and Schenkel’s apologetic collection. [Poupard, 2024](https://journals.openedition.org/cahierscfv/4927)

#### There was an actual investigation and acquittal

**[DT]** Schenkel says the investigators concluded:

> *artem memoriae ab omni superstitione et magia esse liberrimam.*

“The art of memory was wholly free from superstition and magic.”

This is corroborated by Torrentius’s letters to Henricus Cuyckius. The University of Louvain cleared the art on 3 June 1593.

#### No torture or completed Inquisition trial is documented

**[LEGEND]** Nineteenth- and early twentieth-century mnemonic histories say Schenkel “barely escaped the torture of the Inquisition.” The recoverable contemporary evidence supports denunciation, inquiry, hostile preaching, ecclesiastical examination, and acquittal—not imprisonment, torture, or a formal capital trial. The stronger tale appears to be an inflation of the documented accusation.

#### Secrecy was substantially commercial

**[Correction: DT/SR]** Henri IV’s privilege of 14 November 1601 permitted Schenkel to teach throughout France and prohibited former auditors from teaching the art for twenty years without his permission, under a penalty of 1,000 écus:

> *sans son expres congé … à peine de mille escus d’amende.*

Mertens connects the cipher, withheld second book, oaths, licensed teachers, and restricted circulation to the economics of an itinerant course. This does not exclude intellectual esotericism, but it supplies a documented motive that Yates underweights.

#### “Hermetic sect” remains speculative

**[YI/DISPUTED]** No membership list, rule, correspondence, ritual, confession, prosecution dossier, or self-description establishes a Schenkel–Paepp Hermetic sect. Paepp’s citation sequence and Brunian borrowings document eclectic intellectual appropriation. They do not by themselves establish sectarian organization or religious conversion.

### Poupard’s pedagogical correction

**[SR]** Clément Poupard’s 2024 study moves the center of gravity from an intellectual genealogy of great occult thinkers to labor, teaching, and institutions. Schenkel was a financially precarious schoolmaster turned itinerant instructor. His courses operated near schools, colleges, academies, and universities but were not institutionalized within them.

Poupard’s documented itinerary includes:

- Brussels, Antwerp, Douai, Louvain, Liège, Mons, Tournai, and Beaumont, 1592–95;
- Marburg, 1602;
- Paris, 1606;
- Toulouse, Castres, and other French cities, 1609–10;
- Prague, 1616;
- Freiburg im Breisgau, 1620;
- activity still documented after 1624 and probably after 1627.

This corrects Yates’s death date “circa 1603.”

### Carruthers, Bolzoni, Rossi, Small, Hajdu, Ong

**[Contextual scholarship]**

- **Mary Carruthers** shifts medieval memory away from a crude storage model. Memory was compositional, ethical, affective, and inventive. Applied to Schenkel, this cautions against treating loci as a passive filing cabinet.
- **Lina Bolzoni** reconstructs the visual, literary, and social culture of memory theaters and galleries. Her work makes Schenkel’s rooms intelligible as part of a much broader culture of organized imagery, while not establishing a Camillo-to-Schenkel chain.
- **Paolo Rossi**, *Clavis universalis* (1960; English *Logic and the Art of Memory*), first highlighted Paepp’s Bruno references through Nicola Badaloni and connected memory arts to combinatorics and universal language. Yates expressly relied on him.
- **Jocelyn Penny Small**, *Wax Tablets of the Mind*, corrects overly uniform accounts of ancient literacy and memory. The ancient art operated within changing oral, written, and visual practices.
- **Henrik Hajdu**, *Das mnemotechnische Schrifttum des Mittelalters* (1936), supplied Yates with important bibliographical antecedents but belongs to an older developmental narrative.
- **Walter Ong**, *Ramus, Method, and the Decay of Dialogue*, explains the rise of spatialized, dichotomous textbook method. His account illuminates Schenkel’s disciplinary tables, though “Ramist” should not become a blanket attribution.
- **Heimann-Seelbach; Doležalová, Kiss, and Wójcik** emphasize the widespread pedagogical and rhetorical functions of medieval and early-modern mnemonics. This weighs against treating Hermetic use as the normal or defining form.

### Bruno scholarship and its bearing here

**[Correction by analogy and source criticism]** Rita Sturlese’s 1991 critical work on *De umbris idearum* corrected Yates’s reconstruction of Bruno’s wheel, particularly her placement of planetary images. Francesco Torchia and later François Quiviger argue that the wheel can function as a phonetic and combinatorial mnemonic rather than an inner astral talisman. [Warburg Institute, “Yates and Bruno’s Mnemonics”](https://warburg.sas.ac.uk/research-fellowships/research-projects/archived-research-projects/giordano-bruno/yates-brunos-mnemonics)

This matters for Paepp: if Bruno’s machinery is not automatically magical, resemblance to Bruno cannot automatically make Paepp’s lists magical.

Michele Ciliberto, Hilary Gatti, Simonetta Bassi, Rita Sturlese, Stephen Clucas, Leen Spruit, and Manuel Mertens have replaced the idea of a single coherent “Hermetic Bruno” with a more heterogeneous Bruno: mnemonic, metaphysical, natural-philosophical, rhetorical, magical, polemical, and pedagogical. Mertens nonetheless preserves a real, carefully delimited relation between Bruno’s theory of imagination and his magic.

### Yates’s later position

**[DT]** Yates did not retract the Schenkel–Paepp argument. In later defenses of the history of occult philosophy she maintained that historians had excluded relevant esoteric materials. Her later writing generally emphasizes the provisional and exploratory nature of her reconstructions. No specific later correction by Yates of the Schenkel chronology or the claim that Schenkel never named Bruno has been located.

## History and context

### Classical rhetoric

**[DT]** The founding narrative is Simonides identifying banquet victims by remembering where each had sat. Cicero and Quintilian transmit the story; the *Ad Herennium* supplies the fullest rules.

The famous lawsuit image in *Ad Herennium* III.33 is not a Schenkel example but a remote ancestor. To remember a poisoning case involving inheritance and witnesses, the practitioner places in the sick man’s room an image of the accused holding a cup in one hand, tablets in the other, and a ram’s testicles on a finger. The cup recalls poison, tablets the will, and testicles (*testiculi*) the witnesses (*testes*). It demonstrates compression by pun, attribute, and dramatic tableau.

### Scholastic psychology

**[DT]** Aristotle explains recollection as a purposive search moving through an ordered associative sequence. Albert and Thomas Christianize and moralize the art as part of prudence.

Thomas’s four aids are:

1. corporeal similitudes for intellectual intentions;
2. orderly arrangement;
3. attentive or affective engagement;
4. frequent meditation.

**[SR]** Schenkel’s constant citation of Thomas does not prove Dominican membership or control. It establishes doctrinal legitimacy and supplies a psychology of image, order, attention, and repetition.

### Dominican and Franciscan streams

**[DT]** Johannes Romberch and Cosmas Rossellius were Dominicans; their printed handbooks transmitted architectural diagrams, alphabets, orders of places, and rules for images. Girolamo Marafioti was a Franciscan Observant whose hand-based signs and figures entered later compilations.

**[SR]** Schenkel’s book is best described as an eclectic commercial compilation downstream of these traditions, rather than a secret Dominican manual.

### Louvain and the Catholic Low Countries

**[DT]** Nicolaus Mameranus’s *Oratio pro memoria et eloquentia in integrum restituenda* was delivered at Louvain in 1560 and printed at Brussels in 1561. It documents a local revival of interest before Schenkel.

**[DT]** Schenkel matriculated at Louvain in 1564 and studied at Cologne in 1565. He taught in Latin schools and became rector of the public school at Malines. His institutional world included municipal schools, Catholic clergy, universities, printers, courts, and princely patrons.

### Patrick Lenan

**[DT/SR]** Schenkel acknowledged that he learned the art from the Irishman Patrick Lenan, whom he met at Antwerp in 1592. Lenan studied at Douai and Louvain, later entered the Jesuits, and served in the Irish mission. This is a major correction to accounts portraying Schenkel as sole inventor.

**[Open question]** The precise sources of Lenan’s system—Jesuit, Louvain, Italian, or independent textbook study—remain unidentified.

### Lullism

**[Distinction]** Ramon Llull’s authentic Art is not originally a memory palace. It combines letters, divine dignities, relations, questions, subjects, and rotating figures for demonstration and invention. Later Renaissance authors fused it with artificial memory.

Bonner and Fidora’s scholarship has been crucial in separating authentic Lullian logic from later “pseudo-Lullian” alchemy, Cabala, and encyclopedic memory. Schenkel’s own system is primarily local and imagistic; Paepp’s Brunian associations bring it nearer the Renaissance Lull–memory hybrid.

### Hermeticism

**[DT]** Paepp quotes Ficino’s Latin *Pimander*, treating cosmic order as divine manifestation.

**[Historical correction]** Isaac Casaubon dated the Hermetic writings to post-Christian antiquity in 1614, but this did not instantly eliminate their philosophical authority. Paepp’s 1619 use belongs to the transitional moment after Casaubon.

**[DISPUTED]** Citation of Hermes proves reading or reuse, not adherence to a “Hermetic religion.”

### Ramism and universal method

**[SR]** Schenkel’s disciplinary reductions and Descartes’s response belong to a broader search for methods that replace accumulated images with causal or logical order. Ramist dichotomies, commonplace books, indexes, and tables increasingly externalized what mnemonic architecture had organized internally.

## People

### Lambert Thomas Schenkel

**[DT]** Born at ’s-Hertogenbosch on 7 March 1547, son of the physician and humanist Dominicus Schenkel. He married Anne Smeyers in 1574.

**[DT]** He taught at Elten, Tienen, Antwerp, and Malines and served as rector at Malines. During Calvinist control he operated a private boarding school and reportedly moved approximately fifty pupils to France.

**[DT]** Debt and loss of office preceded his turn to itinerant mnemonic teaching. Poupard therefore describes his mobility as a financially motivated professional strategy, not merely a philosophical mission.

**[Correction]** He did not die circa 1603. He was alive in 1624, and Poupard finds evidence placing his activity after 1627. The exact death date remains unknown.

### Johannes Paepp

**[DT]** Paepp describes himself as *Galbaicus*. His exact biography is much thinner than Schenkel’s.

**[DISPUTED]** Many catalogues assign him a death date around 1613, plainly difficult to reconcile with authorial privileges and works dated 1617–19 unless those works were posthumous, the date is wrong, or a bibliographical identity has been conflated. No decisive contemporary death record was found.

### Patrick Lenan

Teacher or immediate transmitter to Schenkel; later Jesuit. His role was largely missed by Yates.

### Martin Sommer

**[DT]** Schenkel licensed Sommer, called *Somerius Silesius*, to teach for shared profit. Schenkel later complained that Sommer disappeared owing him more than 200 gold ducats:

> *communi lucro … 200 aureos ducatos excedere…*

**[TR/Correction]** Later English histories sometimes say Sommer “betrayed” Schenkel and published the secret in 1619. The documented quarrel concerns profit, unauthorized or defective publications, and failure to remit money. Paepp—not Sommer—is the author of the 1617 *Schenckelius detectus*.

### Jean/Joannes Baxius

**[DT]** Antwerp inquisitorial figure who denounced the mnemonic teaching as magical or superstitious. Martin Del Rio praises his zeal against magical heresy in *Disquisitionum magicarum libri sex*.

**[DT]** Schenkel reports Bax preaching that “weeds” were being sown in the city under the name of memory.

### Laevinus Torrentius

**[DT]** Bishop of Antwerp; patron and defender. His letters of 28 January and 10 June 1593 recommend Schenkel and defend the art as ancient and lawful. He had earlier awarded Schenkel 150 florins for a dedicatory poem, so his testimony was supportive but not disinterested.

### Henricus Cuyckius

Theologian at Louvain and recipient of Torrentius’s letters; part of the network through which ecclesiastical legitimacy was sought.

### Henri IV of France

**[DT]** Granted the 1601 teaching and publication privilege. Later memory books inflate the penalty to 4,000 francs; the reproduced privilege says 1,000 écus.

### Descartes

**[DT]** In the *Cogitationes privatae*, Descartes writes, in Leibniz’s surviving copy of the notes, that reading Schenkel’s *nugae* prompted a “true art of memory” based on reducing things to causes. He objects that Schenkel fills space with too many unrelated images; proper images should depend on one another. Yates translates *nugae* as “profitable trifles,” though *nugae* alone is closer to “trifles” or “nonsense.” [Yates scan, pp. 373–74](https://resources.warburg.sas.ac.uk/mnemosyne/fq/yates_memory.pdf)

### Leibniz

**[DT]** Hanover manuscripts **Phil. VI.19** and **Phil. VII.B.III.7**, cited by Couturat and Rossi, mention Schenkel. Leibniz’s published work defines mnemonic signs as sensible *notae* joined to things by likeness, unlikeness, or connection.

**[YI]** Yates makes Schenkel one link in a long chain from ancient images, through Llull and Bruno, to Leibniz’s universal characteristic. The historical acquaintance is documented; a direct causal path to calculus is a much stronger and disputed interpretation.

### Anecdotes requested in the brief

- **The accused magician:** documented in outline; later torture narrative is legend.
- **The student who lost his memory:** no securely identifiable Schenkel–Paepp source was found.
- **The professor’s party:** no securely identifiable Schenkel–Paepp source was found.
- **The auctioneer:** no securely identifiable Schenkel–Paepp anecdote was found. This may belong to a different catalogue entry or later professional-memory literature.
- **Public feats:** promotional brochures record pupils repeating disconnected words, names, sentences, and numbers forward, backward, and in selected order. Because Schenkel published the testimonials himself, they are documented claims but not controlled observations.

Absence from this dossier is deliberate: attaching attractive floating anecdotes to Schenkel without a traceable witness would reproduce precisely the legendary method the brief asks to expose.

## The magic question

### What was called magic?

**[DT]** Bax regarded the teaching as suspect in the legal and confessional climate created by anti-witchcraft measures in the Spanish Netherlands.

**[DT]** Schenkel, Torrentius, named examiners, and Louvain authorities said it was not magic.

**[DT]** Schenkel used secrecy, cipher, initiation rhetoric, “secrets,” and marvelous performance claims.

**[DT]** Paepp cited Hermes, Plato, Bruno, and “arcane philosophical mysteries.”

These are four distinct facts. They should not be collapsed.

### Natural memory versus supernatural agency

**[DT]** Schenkel’s stated machinery requires ordinary faculties: perception, imagination, association, spatial order, attention, and repetition. No demon, angel, planetary spirit, consecrated talisman, fumigation, prayer cycle, or astrological election is necessary to make the “oma” example work.

**[Negative finding]** No operational ritual equivalent to the *Ars notoria* was located in Schenkel’s mnemonic instructions.

### Ars notoria

**[Context]** The medieval *Ars notoria* promises infused knowledge through prayers, sacred names, fasting, ritual schedules, and the contemplation of complex *notae*. It was condemned or treated with suspicion by some theologians, including Thomas.

**[Distinction]** Both the *Ars notoria* and Schenkel promise accelerated learning and use figures or signs. But similarity of aspiration does not establish descent. Schenkel’s documented exercises are mnemonic encodings; the *Ars notoria* invokes supernatural illumination.

### Were the images talismans?

**[DISPUTED]** Yates’s broader model treated certain Renaissance memory images, especially Bruno’s, as inward talismans capable of receiving astral influence. Later reconstruction of Bruno’s wheels has weakened the claim that their mnemonic operation was inherently talismanic.

For Schenkel there is still less evidence. His actors, objects, letters, rooms, and disciplinary abbreviations behave as mnemonic cues. Paepp’s Hermetic passages give them cosmological interpretation, but no surviving instruction says to consecrate them or draw down celestial power.

### Heresy and consequences

**[DT]** The accusation was potentially serious because magic could be assimilated to heresy. Bax belonged to an anti-magic network praised by Del Rio.

**[DT]** The immediate consequences were examination and the production of defenses and approbations.

**[SR]** Schenkel turned danger into marketing capital. The suggestion of marvelous power attracted customers; formal acquittal reassured authorities.

**[LEGEND]** “Narrow escape from the rack” is unsupported by the currently identified contemporary record.

### The strongest defensible conclusion

The Schenkel system was:

- **technically:** a natural mnemonic;
- **socially:** secretive and commercial;
- **rhetorically:** presented as marvelous and arcane;
- **intellectually:** open to scholastic, Hermetic, Brunian, and encyclopedic interpretation;
- **legally:** accused of magic and officially defended as nonmagical;
- **not demonstrated to be:** a talismanic rite or the teaching apparatus of an organized Hermetic sect.

## Afterlife

### Seventeenth-century mnemonics

Schenkel’s books and Paepp’s disclosure fed a crowded market including Martin Sommer, Arnold Backhaus/Backhusy, Adam Bruxius, Johann Justus Winckelmann, Johann Heinrich Alsted, John Willis, and later German compilations.

Yates asks whether Willis’s *Mnemonica* might hide more than its practical surface, by analogy with Paepp’s “detection.” **[YI]** No equivalent documentary revelation has established such an occult Willis.

### Descartes: from stored images to causal reconstruction

**[DT]** Descartes’s objection is structural. Schenkel stores too many independent phantasms. Descartes wants dependency: knowing causes should regenerate effects.

**[SR]** This is a transition from mnemonic storage toward method, explanation, and relational organization. It anticipates neither modern databases nor neural networks in any literal sense.

### Leibniz: characters and combination

**[DT]** Leibniz knew Lullism and mnemonic theory and sought a *characteristica universalis* and *calculus ratiocinator*.

**[DISPUTED]** “Llull to Leibniz to the computer” is a legitimate history of aspirations—formal signs, decomposition, combination, mechanical reasoning—but a misleading linear genealogy if it suggests that Llull or Schenkel designed a programmable computer.

### Literature and visual culture

#### Dante and medieval frescoes

**[SR/DISPUTED]** Yates proposed that scholastic memory rules helped shape Dante’s vivid moral imagery and cycles of virtues and vices in preaching and fresco. Carruthers and Bolzoni support the larger culture of mnemonic composition, but direct proof that a particular fresco cycle was designed as a technical locus system is rare.

There is no direct Schenkel-to-Dante relation; Dante is an ancestor in Yates’s reconstructed history.

#### Emblem books

**[SR]** Emblems unite picture, motto, and explanatory text and therefore resemble mnemonic image practice. They are part of the same visual-rhetorical ecology. Not every emblem is a memory image, and no universal dependence on Schenkel exists.

#### Borges’s Funes

**[MODERN analogy]** “Funes the Memorious” explores the pathology of total, undifferentiated retention. Schenkel instead depends on selection, coding, and order. Borges’s story is a conceptual counter-image, not a documented reception of Schenkel.

#### Eco

**[MODERN reception]** Umberto Eco’s studies of the perfect language and his fiction inherit the history of encyclopedic signs, Llullism, Hermetic interpretation, and combinatorial schemes. Direct dependence on Schenkel is far weaker than dependence on Llull, Bruno, Kircher, and the modern scholarship of Rossi and Yates.

### Modern memory-palace revival and memory athletes

**[MODERN]** Competitive mnemonists use ordered routes, person–action–object systems, number codes, and vivid interaction. These are structurally close to Schenkel’s person–action–instrument encodings.

**[Distinction]** Modern PAO systems are not necessarily copied from Schenkel. They can arise independently from the same constraints of encoding arbitrary sequences.

Modern experimental work supports the practical efficacy of loci for serial recall and finds durable changes after training. It does not validate claims about 25,000 portraits during a prayer or universal mastery after nine lessons. [Modern method-of-loci study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7929507/)

### Computing

A careful genealogy distinguishes:

- **documented transmission:** Llull → Renaissance Lullism → Bruno and early modern combinatorics → Leibniz;
- **functional resemblance:** mnemonic addresses resemble indexed storage; agent–action systems resemble compositional codes;
- **modern metaphor:** “memory palace” interfaces and spatial data stores;
- **false equivalence:** treating a mental locus as a computer address or Schenkel’s cipher as executable code.

## Controversies and disputes

### 1. Schenkel’s dates

**Old account:** 1547–c.1603.  
**Evidence now:** born 7 March 1547; alive in 1624; activity probably documented after 1627.  
**Verdict:** Yates’s death date is obsolete.

### 2. Inventor or transmitter?

**Tradition:** Schenkel invented a remarkable system.  
**Documented correction:** he said he learned it from Patrick Lenan.  
**Verdict:** Schenkel was its most successful codifier, entrepreneur, and transmitter, not its sole inventor.

### 3. Gazophylacium 1609, 1610, or 1611?

**Evidence:** title-page and catalogue records disagree.  
**Verdict:** cite the inspected copy’s imprint; do not merge records without collation.

### 4. Paepp’s life dates

**Catalogue tradition:** died c.1613.  
**Problem:** works and privileges dated 1617–19 appear authorially framed.  
**Verdict:** unresolved identity or dating problem; “d. c.1613” should carry a question mark.

### 5. Did Paepp expose Schenkel?

**Documented:** he supplied a key and clearer practical exposition.  
**Yates:** he exposed a Brunian-Hermetic secret.  
**Correction:** Schenkel himself named Bruno, and much secrecy protected income.  
**Verdict:** practical/commercial exposure is certain; exposure of a sectarian occult doctrine is unproved.

### 6. Are Paepp’s images magical?

**Evidence:** Brunian borrowing, Hermes, arcana, elaborate image lists.  
**Counterevidence:** no consecration or spirit-operation is required; later Bruno scholarship reads major mnemonic machinery non-talismanically.  
**Verdict:** philosophically esoteric in places; operationally magical status unproved.

### 7. Was Schenkel an accused sorcerer?

**Documented:** yes, in the ordinary historical sense of having been accused and examined.  
**Legendary enlargement:** torture, imprisonment, or near execution.  
**Verdict:** accusation true; lurid consequences unverified.

### 8. Did Henri IV give a monopoly?

**Documented:** a twenty-year protective privilege covering teaching and unauthorized dissemination.  
**Legend:** a 4,000-franc fine.  
**Primary wording:** 1,000 écus.  
**Verdict:** privilege real; later monetary retellings unreliable.

### 9. Was the system effective?

**Documented:** demonstrations, testimonials, pupils, repeat engagements, and a durable publishing market.  
**Limitation:** evidence is largely promotional and selected by Schenkel.  
**Modern support:** loci and imagery work; the spectacular numerical claims have not been independently replicated.  
**Verdict:** credible mnemonic core, unverified advertising maxima.

### 10. Schenkel to Descartes and Leibniz

**Documented:** both knew or referred to him.  
**Yates:** the stream leads toward modern scientific method.  
**Verdict:** reception is real; strong causal claims about modern science, universal language, or calculus remain historiographical interpretation.

### 11. The “Yates thesis”

Yates’s achievement was to recover connections among rhetoric, memory, imagery, magic, Llullism, and universal method. Her weakness was treating proximity, shared vocabulary, and symbolic form as evidence of a coherent Hermetic current.

For this case, the strongest post-Yates correction is not “there was no occult dimension.” It is:

> There were several dimensions—natural mnemonic technique, scholastic psychology, secrecy, commerce, confessional suspicion, Bruno reception, Hermetic citation, and marvelous publicity—and their coexistence does not make them one hidden doctrine.

## Open questions

1. What is the exact stemma of the 1593 Latin and French texts, the 1609/10/11 *Gazophylacium*, and the 1617 Venetian issue?
2. Does a complete copy-by-copy census reveal altered diagrams, censored passages, or different cipher keys?
3. What is Paepp’s correct identity, birthplace, and death date?
4. Was *Ianus Phaosphorus* always Paepp, or was the name used editorially after his death?
5. What exactly did Paepp take from each Bruno work, and what came independently through Romberch, Rossellius, Ravellin, or Marafioti?
6. Can the *Crisis* be digitized and collated page by page with the *Eisagoge* and *Schenckelius detectus*?
7. What was Patrick Lenan’s source?
8. Are student notebooks or manuscript locus inventories hidden in Sammelbände containing Schenkel’s promotional brochures?
9. Can the named public demonstrations be corroborated in university, municipal, court, or personal records not selected by Schenkel?
10. What precise legal authority did Bax possess, and was the inquiry technically inquisitorial, episcopal, municipal, or mixed?
11. Is Schenkel’s 1624 petition the last secure autograph document?
12. What does “after 1627” rest upon—new publication, personal appearance, reprint attribution, or archival testimony?
13. Did the French *Magazin* translate every diagram and encoded passage, or regularize the system?
14. How many of the supposedly “magic” images in Paepp have specifically astrological, talismanic, or ritual sources?
15. Did Descartes read Schenkel directly, a Sommer version, or a composite edition?
16. Which Schenkel text did Leibniz consult?
17. Can the scattered Hannover references be newly edited against the current Akademie edition?
18. Do the “auctioneer,” “professor’s party,” and “student who lost his memory” anecdotes belong to another historical memory system? No evidence presently connects them to Schenkel or Paepp.
