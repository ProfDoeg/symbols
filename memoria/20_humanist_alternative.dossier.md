# The humanist alternative: Erasmus, Melanchthon, Agrippa, Durer: Research Dossier

## Basic identification

**Catalogue name:** The humanist alternative: Erasmus, Melanchthon, Agrippa, Dürer  
**Catalogue number:** System 20 of 45  
**Exponents:** Desiderius Erasmus; Philipp Melanchthon; Heinrich Cornelius Agrippa von Nettesheim; Albrecht Dürer  
**Dates:** 1511–12; 1531–34; 1526/1530; September 1506  
**Principal sources:** Erasmus, *De ratione studii*; Melanchthon, *Elementorum rhetorices libri duo*; Agrippa, *De incertitudine et vanitate scientiarum et artium*, chapter 10; Dürer, letter to Willibald Pirckheimer, September 1506  
**Principle in one sentence:** Memory is secured primarily by understanding, rational arrangement, repetition, and attentive practice—not by multiplying imaginary buildings and striking images—while elaborate mnemonic shortcuts range from occasionally useful aids to pedagogical distractions, frauds, or spiritually dangerous promises.

**Yates:** *The Art of Memory*, chapter V, pp. 124, 126–28; chapter VI, p. 132; chapter IX, pp. 206–07 in the 1966 Routledge and Kegan Paul pagination. The relevant core discussion occupies pp. 124–28 in the original pagination, reproduced on pp. 74–76 of the Warburg PDF.

### Immediate bibliographical corrections

- **[Documented text]** Erasmus’s *De ratione studii* first appeared in an unauthorized or defective Paris printing dated 20 October **1511**, not 1512. Erasmus’s fuller authorized text appeared in Paris on 15 July 1512, followed by editions at Leuven/Aalst and Strasbourg. “1512” is therefore a defensible date for the standard expanded text but not the first appearance. The Morgan Library’s copy of the 1511 printing is PML 63849. [Morgan Library catalogue](https://www.themorgan.org/printed-books/169994)

- **[Documented text]** Erasmus’s triad is *intellectu, ordine, cura*: “understanding, order, and care.” Yates translates *intellectu* as “study,” which conveys part of the educational sense but is not literal.

- **[Documented bibliography]** Melanchthon’s work is normally titled *Elementorum rhetorices libri duo*, not *Rhetorica elementa*. It was first printed at Wittenberg by Georg Rhau in **1531**; copies and catalogues also date an early issue to 1532. Yates cites a Venetian 1534 edition, and the item-specific date “1534” derives from that witness rather than from the composition or first publication. [Utrecht 1531 copy](https://dbc.library.uu.nl/handle/20.500.14918/399744)

- **[Documented bibliography]** Agrippa announced *De vanitate* as substantially completed in a letter of 16 September **1526**, but the first securely documented printing is Johannes Grapheus, Antwerp, **1530**. The BnF copy is Arsenal 4-S-196. [BnF catalogue](https://catalogue.bnf.fr/ark:/12148/cb39310206z)

- **[Correction to the catalogue]** Yates’s own note dates the relevant Dürer letter to **September 1506**, not simply “1506” and not the surviving letter of 28 February 1506 sometimes returned by web searches. Her source was the older edition *Dürers Briefe, Tagebücher und Reime*, ed. Moriz Thausing, 1899, pp. 54–55. The standard modern collection is Hans Rupprich, ed., *Albrecht Dürer: Schriftlicher Nachlaß*, vol. I (Berlin, 1956). Yates’s reference appears at *Art of Memory*, p. 126 n. 56. [Yates, searchable Warburg PDF](https://resources.warburg.sas.ac.uk/mnemosyne/fq/yates_memory.pdf)

- **[Interpretive correction]** These four witnesses do not jointly describe a single artificial-memory mechanism. “The humanist alternative” is Yates’s historical grouping of reservations and alternatives. Erasmus articulates a positive pedagogy; Melanchthon drastically reduces *memoria* as a teachable rhetorical art; Agrippa delivers a satirical and religiously charged attack; Dürer makes a joke. Only Agrippa discusses the traditional places-and-images system at any length.

## The system in detail

### 1. What kind of “system” is this?

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** The reconstructable system is an anti-system or reduced system:

1. Comprehend the material.
2. Divide and arrange it coherently.
3. rehearse it repeatedly.
4. test recall rather than trusting recognition.
5. repair omissions after testing.
6. use places and images, if at all, as subordinate aids rather than as the foundation of learning.

It has no prescribed palace, wheel, theatre, zodiac, alphabet of images, talisman, or combinatorial table. Its “places” are principally the intelligible divisions of the subject; its “images” are optional and unspecified.

This is closest to Quintilian’s final recommendation in *Institutio oratoria* 11.2.40:

> *Si quis tamen unam maximamque a me artem memoriae quaerat, exercitatio est et labor: multa ediscere, multa cogitare, et si fieri potest cotidie, potentissimum est; nihil aeque vel augetur cura vel negligentia intercidit.*

“If anyone asks me for the single greatest art of memory, it is exercise and labour: learning much by heart, thinking much, and, if possible, doing so every day are most powerful; nothing grows so much through care or perishes so much through neglect.”

**[Documented genealogy]** Erasmus’s *cura* and repeated self-testing closely resemble this Quintilianic conclusion. Melanchthon’s contraction of rhetoric to invention, arrangement, and style likewise assumes that memory follows understanding and disposition more readily than it follows a separate technical apparatus.

### 2. Erasmus

#### Text and immediate setting

The relevant passage occurs near the close of *De ratione studii ac legendi interpretandique auctores*, a concise curriculum for Latin and Greek education. In Jean-Claude Margolin’s critical edition it appears in *Desiderii Erasmi Roterodami Opera omnia*, series I, vol. 2 (Amsterdam: North-Holland, 1971), pp. 79–151, at approximately p. 118. Yates used the Froben *Opera omnia* of 1540, vol. I, p. 466.

The central text reads:

> *Neque negligenda memoria, lectionis thesaurus. Eam tametsi locis et imaginibus adiuvari non inficior, tamen tribus rebus potissimum constat optima memoria: intellectu, ordine, cura.*

“Nor should memory, the treasury of reading, be neglected. Although I do not deny that it may be helped by places and images, nevertheless the best memory consists above all in three things: understanding, order, and care.”

The following practical advice, with orthography varying between editions, prescribes repeated reading and examination:

> *Quae meminisse velis … crebrius relegenda, deinde saepius a nobis ipsis exigenda, ut si quid forte suffugerit, id restituatur.*

“What you wish to remember should be reread rather frequently and then repeatedly demanded back from ourselves, so that, if anything happens to have escaped, it may be restored.”

#### What serves as places?

- **[Documented text]** Erasmus concedes that conventional *loci* can help.
- **[Absence of evidence]** He supplies no architectural rules, number of places, intervals, lighting conditions, directional route, or worked example.
- **[Scholarly reconstruction]** His preferred functional substitute is *ordo*: the logical, grammatical, rhetorical, or thematic arrangement of material.
- **[Caution]** It would overstate the text to identify *ordo* with Ramist dichotomies. Erasmus wrote before Ramus’s mature method and does not present binary trees here.

#### What serves as images?

- **[Documented text]** Erasmus concedes *imagines* without defining them.
- **[Absence of evidence]** He gives no rules for vividness, beauty, ugliness, violence, familiarity, similarity, rebus construction, or images for words.
- **[Interpretation]** Images are allowable auxiliary prompts, not the organizing foundation.

#### Fixing order and retrieval

- **Encoding:** understand the passage or subject.
- **Ordering:** arrange its parts intelligibly.
- **Consolidation:** reread frequently.
- **Retrieval:** compel oneself to reproduce it without the book.
- **Error correction:** restore whatever was missed, then repeat.

The decisive operation is *a nobis ipsis exigenda*: material is to be “exacted” or “required back” from oneself. In modern terminology this resembles retrieval practice, but calling Erasmus an inventor of the modern testing effect would be **[modern analogy]**, not a documented historical claim.

#### Is Erasmus hostile to all mnemonic imagery?

No.

- **[Documented text]** *Non inficior* expressly refuses a total denial.
- **[Yates’s interpretation]** Erasmus is “distinctly cool” toward the art and represents a Quintilianist humanist tendency.
- **[Correction]** “Coolness” is better than “prohibition.” The passage subordinates rather than abolishes places and images.
- **[Broader evidence]** Erasmus used proverbial, exemplary, and commonplace-based forms of textual organization throughout his pedagogy. These can support memory without constituting a classical artificial memory.

### 3. Erasmus and the *Ars notoria*

Yates adds that Erasmus was “strongly against all magical short cuts to memory,” citing the colloquy commonly called *Ars notoria*, translated by Craig R. Thompson in *The Colloquies of Erasmus* (Chicago, 1965), pp. 458–61.

**[Documented text/tradition]** The *Ars notoria* promised rapid or infused mastery of disciplines through prayers, ritual observance, divine names, and diagrammatic *notae*. It was repeatedly suspected or condemned as illicit magic.

**[Documented Erasmus]** Erasmus satirizes the desire to acquire learning without ordinary study.

**[Important distinction]** His rejection of the *Ars notoria* does not prove rejection of all visualization. The target is the promise of supernatural or near-instant intellectual acquisition.

### 4. Melanchthon

#### Text

At the beginning of *Elementorum rhetorices libri duo*, book I, under *De officiis oratoris*, Melanchthon lists:

> *Quinque igitur numerantur officia oratoris: inventio, dispositio, elocutio, memoria, pronunciatio.*

“Five duties of the orator are therefore numbered: invention, arrangement, style, memory, and delivery.”

He immediately minimizes the independent teachability of the last two:

> *Memoria parum admodum ab arte adiuvatur.*

“Memory is helped very little indeed by art.”

And delivery is chiefly acquired by imitation in actual practice:

> *Et quid maxime in agendo deceat, in foro discendum est imitatione.*

“What is most becoming in delivery must be learned in the forum by imitation.”

He concludes that almost all the teachable art is expended on invention, arrangement, and style:

> *Et in his tribus partibus fere tota ars consumitur.*

“In these three parts almost the whole art is contained.”

These sentences are printed in *Corpus Reformatorum*, vol. XIII, col. 419, and occur around A8v–B1r in early editions.

#### Did Melanchthon “forbid mnemonic devices”?

- **[Yates’s statement]** “Melanchthon forbids students to use any mnemotechnical devices and enjoins learning by heart in the normal way as the sole art of memory.”
- **[Primary-text correction]** The immediately cited sentence says that memory is helped very little by art. That is a dismissal or marginalization, but it is not itself a formal prohibition.
- **[Likely source of Yates’s stronger wording]** A fuller instruction in the edition she consulted may have associated memory with ordinary *ediscere* or learning by heart. Nevertheless, the categorical English verb “forbids” is stronger than *parum admodum ab arte adiuvatur*.
- **[Best conclusion]** Melanchthon removes technical memory from the substantial curriculum of rhetoric rather than constructing an alternative mnemonic apparatus.

#### Places, images, order, retrieval

- **Places:** none specified.
- **Images:** none specified.
- **Order:** *dispositio*—arrangement—is a major teachable part of rhetoric.
- **Retrieval:** ordinary memorization after invention, disposition, and verbal formulation.
- **Rules:** no independent system of mnemonic rules.
- **Diagrams:** the original core passage contains no memory diagram.

Later pedagogical editions added questions, scholia, indices, or synoptic representations. For example, Martin Crusius’s annotated edition, *Philippi Melanchthonis Elementorum rhetorices libri duo … quaestionibus et scholiis explicati* (Basel, 1574), visually schematized rhetorical divisions. Such diagrams document the reception of Melanchthon’s orderly textbook, not an image-based memory system devised by him.

### 5. Agrippa

#### Text and chapter

Chapter 10 of *De incertitudine et vanitate scientiarum et artium atque excellentia verbi Dei declamatio invectiva* is headed:

> *De arte memorativa.*

“On the art of memory.”

The chapter begins, in early-print spelling:

> *Inter has artes numeranda est ars memorativa, quae, ut ait Cicero, nihil aliud est quam quaedam doctrina et praeceptio, veluti tenuis quaedam membrana, constans characteribus, locis atque imaginibus, a Simonide Melico primum inventa, a Metrodoro Scepsio consummata.*

“Among these arts must be numbered the art of memory, which, as Cicero says, is nothing other than a certain teaching and body of precepts, like a thin membrane consisting of characters, places, and images, first invented by Simonides of Ceos and perfected by Metrodorus of Scepsis.”

The printed forms “Simonides Melicus/Melito” vary or have been corrupted in transcription and translation. The intended traditional figure is Simonides of Ceos, celebrated as a lyric or “melic” poet.

Agrippa’s principal objection is that the art presupposes good natural memory and can overload it:

> *certius est eam nunquam bene succedere posse, ubi non praecesserit bona memoria naturalis; quam interdum monstrosis quibusdam imaginibus ita confundit, ut pro nova memoria insaniam et phrenesim inducat.*

“It is more certain that it can never succeed well where a good natural memory has not preceded it; and sometimes it so confuses that memory with monstrous images that, instead of a new memory, it induces madness and frenzy.”

He complains that innumerable characters and images for things and words burden natural memory. He invokes the traditional anecdote that Themistocles, offered an art of memory, replied that he needed an art of forgetting because he remembered what he would rather forget.

He then names ancient and modern writers:

> *De hac arte meminit Cicero … Quintilianus … et Seneca. Ex recentioribus Franciscus Petrarcha … Matthaeolus Veronensis, Petrus Ravennas, Hermannus Buschius, et alii catalogo indigni, utpote obscuri.*

“Cicero, Quintilian, and Seneca mention this art. Among the more recent writers, Francesco Petrarch, Matteo of Verona, Peter of Ravenna, Hermannus Buschius, and others unworthy of a catalogue, being obscure.”

The chapter ends by ridiculing contemporary professional teachers who extract money from credulous youths and by comparing ostentatious memory to a house empty within but displaying a market of words outside.

#### The system Agrippa describes

Agrippa’s hostile summary nevertheless identifies its components:

- *characteres*: marks or signs;
- *loci*: ordered places;
- *imagines*: representations attached to those places;
- natural memory as the necessary substrate;
- images both for things and for words;
- public display or extemporaneous discourse as a claimed result.

He gives no rules for constructing a locus series and no worked placement. His object is exposure, not instruction.

#### Simonides and Metrodorus

- **[Ancient tradition]** Cicero, Quintilian, and the *Ad Herennium* transmit the architectural art; Cicero tells the Simonides banquet story.
- **[Documented uncertainty]** Simonides’ role is legendary or etiological, not established by a surviving work of Simonides.
- **[Ancient testimony]** Metrodorus of Scepsis was reputed to use the twelve zodiacal signs, divided into many subdivisions, as mnemonic places.
- **[Agrippa’s rhetoric]** The inventor/perfecter sequence compresses a much more complicated ancient tradition.

#### The Themistocles anecdote

- **[Ancient literary tradition]** Cicero, *De oratore* 2.299, and Plutarch, *Themistocles* 2, preserve versions of the reply: Themistocles would prefer an art of forgetting.
- **[Not eyewitness history]** It is an exemplary anecdote transmitted centuries after the statesman.
- **[Agrippa’s use]** It authorizes the claim that total retention can be a burden rather than a virtue.
- **[Afterlife]** The anecdote becomes an important counter-myth to Simonides: the wish to forget stands against the promise of unlimited storage.

#### Agrippa’s “modern catalogue”

Agrippa’s sneer is historically valuable because it identifies a market:

- Petrarch, or mnemonic material circulating under his authority;
- Matteo da Verona;
- Jacobus Publicius and/or related Italian traditions, depending on how particular editions and names are resolved;
- Peter of Ravenna, whose *Phoenix sive artificiosa memoria* became a major printed manual;
- Hermannus Buschius;
- unnamed itinerant teachers selling mnemonic instruction.

**[Documented text]** Agrippa alleges fraud and monetary exploitation.

**[Absence of corroboration]** The chapter does not identify particular cheated students, contracts, or prosecutions. His “rascally fellows” are a satirist’s collective portrait, not a prosopographical list.

### 6. Dürer

Yates translates Dürer’s joke:

> “A chamber must have more than four corners which is to contain all the gods of memory. I am not going to cram my head full of them; that I leave to you; for I believe that however many chambers there might be in the head, you would have something in each of them. The Margrave would not grant an audience long enough!”

#### Interpretation

- **[Documented letter]** Dürer teases Pirckheimer about preparing or possessing an enormous amount of material for an address to a margrave.
- **[Metaphor]** The “chamber” is also the head; its corners evoke mnemonic locations.
- **[Probable learned joke]** *Gods of memory* suggests a crowd of learned authorities, themes, or memory images.
- **[Yates’s interpretation]** The passage gives “an amusing glimpse of a humanist orator preparing a speech to be memorised by the art.”
- **[Correction]** It does not say that Dürer is the orator, that he constructed the chamber, or that Pirckheimer literally employed a palace. Dürer says he will leave the head-cramming to Pirckheimer.
- **[Absence of evidence]** No Dürer mnemonic treatise, locus plan, image alphabet, or diagram survives.
- **[Disputed inference]** Treating Dürer as an “exponent” of a memory system is therefore misleading. He is a witness to the currency of mnemonic architectural language in learned joking.

#### Dating problem

Yates’s note says “letter dated September, 1506.” A commonly indexed digital letter dated 28 February 1506, Rupprich no. 3, does not contain the passage. The catalogue’s nonspecific “letter to Pirckheimer (1506)” should therefore be replaced with “September 1506, as cited by Yates; verify against the relevant letter in Rupprich rather than the February letter.” [Digital text of the February letter](https://www.uni-giessen.de/de/fbz/fb05/germanistik/absprache/sprachverwendung/gloning/tx/dsn1-b03.htm)

## Diagrams, wheels, tables, and plates

### Surviving apparatus

There is no common diagram belonging to this “system.”

- **Erasmus:** no mnemonic diagram in the relevant passage.
- **Melanchthon:** no place-image diagram in the original treatment of *memoria*; later school editions contain analytical or synoptic pedagogical matter.
- **Agrippa:** chapter 10 contains prose only. Its references to characters, places, and images describe the conventional art without reproducing its apparatus.
- **Dürer:** the letter contains no known mnemonic drawing attached to the sentence.

### What should not be imported

- Camillo’s seven planetary grades belong to Camillo’s theatre.
- Lullian letter wheels belong to Lullism and its transformations.
- Bruno’s concentric combinatorial wheels belong to *De umbris idearum*, *Ars reminiscendi*, *Cantus Circaeus*, and related works.
- Agrippa’s planetary tables, magical squares, seals, and image catalogues belong to *De occulta philosophia*, especially books II–III—not to *De vanitate* chapter 10.
- Dürer’s magic square in *Melencolia I* is not identified by Dürer as a memory table.

Connecting any of these mechanically to the four-text “humanist alternative” is **[conflation]** unless separately demonstrated.

## The sources

## Erasmus: manuscripts and print

### Composition and manuscripts

- **[Scholarly reconstruction]** Erasmus developed the work from educational activity in Paris around 1496–97.
- A dedicatory letter to Thomas Grey, conventionally Allen Epistle 66, is associated with the early form.
- **[Open question]** A complete authorial manuscript corresponding exactly to the 1512 printed recension is not normally cited as surviving. The early printed tradition is therefore primary for the established text.

### Early editions

1. *Praeterea Herasmi Roterodami ratio studii ac legendi interpretandique auctores*, appended to Agostino Dati, Paris, Georges Biermant/Jean Granjon, 20 October 1511. Morgan Library, PML 63849.
2. Paris, Josse Bade, 15 July 1512: fuller and more authoritative recension.
3. Leuven/Aalst, Thierry Martens, October 1512.
4. Strasbourg, Matthias Schürer, 1512; Bayerische Staatsbibliothek digital copy represented in Google Books.
5. Basel, Froben, *Opera omnia*, 1540, I, p. 466—the edition cited by Yates.

### Standard edition and translation

- Jean-Claude Margolin, ed., “De ratione studii,” in *Opera omnia Desiderii Erasmi Roterodami*, I-2 (Amsterdam: North-Holland, 1971), pp. 79–151.
- Brian McGregor, trans., “On the Method of Study,” in *Collected Works of Erasmus*, vol. 24, *Literary and Educational Writings 2* (Toronto: University of Toronto Press, 1978), pp. 661–91.
- Jean-Claude Margolin’s French translation appeared in *Bulletin de l’Association Guillaume Budé* 1976.

### Textual variation

The important variation is recensional rather than diagrammatic: the short, defective 1511 printing versus the expanded 1512 form. Orthography varies—*adiuvari/adjuvari*, *inficior/inficior*—without altering the argument. Some secondary quotations silently change *intellectu* to a word meaning “study,” following Yates’s translation.

## Melanchthon: manuscripts and print

### Early editions

1. *Elementorum rhetorices libri duo*, Wittenberg: Georg Rhau, 1531. Utrecht University Library, B oct 53 dl 3.
2. Early Wittenberg issues dated or catalogued 1532, including VD16 M 3102.
3. Paris: Robert Estienne, 1534.
4. Venice, 1534—the edition cited by Yates, p. 4v.
5. Numerous revised or school editions, including Wittenberg 1539, Basel 1574 with Martin Crusius’s questions and scholia, and Wittenberg 1572.

### Standard collected text and translation

- Karl Gottlieb Bretschneider and Heinrich Ernst Bindseil, eds., *Corpus Reformatorum*, vol. XIII, *Philippi Melanthonis Opera quae supersunt omnia* (Halle, 1846), cols. 413–506; the relevant passage is col. 419.

### Variants

- The principal variation is continued authorial revision and pedagogical expansion.
- Page references cannot safely be transferred between editions.
- The 1534 witness used by Yates should not be called the first edition.
- Later schematic presentations illustrate reception in schools; they are not evidence that Melanchthon adopted architectural mnemonics.

## Agrippa: manuscripts and print

### Composition

Agrippa announced the work to Jean Chapelain on 16 September 1526. The extent to which the surviving printed text was complete then is uncertain; revisions were probably made before publication.

### Early editions

1. *De incertitudine et vanitate scientiarum et artium atque excellentia verbi Dei declamatio*, Antwerp: Johannes Grapheus, 1530. BnF Arsenal 4-S-196; Wellcome EPB/B/7193.
2. Paris: “apud Sorbonam,” Jean Pierre, 1531.
3. Cologne and other sixteenth-century reprints.
4. German translation, 1534.
5. Italian translation, 1543.
6. English translation, James Sanford, *Of the Vanitie and Uncertaintie of Artes and Sciences*, London, 1569.
7. French translation, 1582.
8. English translation, *The Vanity of Arts and Sciences*, London, 1676.

### Editions

No single modern chapter-level critical edition has displaced consultation of the early printings and *Opera*:

- *Henrici Cornelii Agrippae … Opera*, 2 vols., Lyon, traditionally dated c. 1600; *De vanitate* in vol. II.
- Paola Zambelli and Marc van der Poel should be consulted for textual and generic context.
- The 1676 English version is useful reception evidence but frequently expands, paraphrases, modernizes names, and intensifies insults; it is not a substitute for the Latin.

### Variants and corruptions

- Simonides’ epithet appears in unstable or misunderstood forms.
- Names in the catalogue of modern mnemonic authors vary across type, transcription, and translation.
- Chapter numbering is stable as chapter X, but pagination varies completely.
- The older English “thin Membrane” is a literal but obscure rendering of Agrippa’s metaphor; it should not be converted into a claim that an actual mnemonic parchment diagram accompanied the text.

## Yates’s account and scholarship since

### Yates’s argument

On pp. 124–28 Yates constructs a historical pivot:

1. Printing makes vast internally stored textual structures less necessary.
2. Humanist philology dissolves the medieval “First and Second Rhetoric of Tullius” by showing that the *Ad Herennium* is not Cicero’s.
3. Dürer’s joke shows the lay art still socially familiar.
4. Quintilian encourages a cooler, exercise-based pedagogy.
5. Erasmus admits places and images but prefers understanding/order/care.
6. Melanchthon virtually banishes mnemonic technique from rhetoric.
7. Nevertheless, the art does not die; it is transformed in Neoplatonic, Hermetic, and occult forms by Camillo and Bruno.

At p. 132 Yates places Erasmus’s sceptical correspondent Viglius opposite Camillo. At pp. 206–07 she considers later reforms by Bacon and Descartes: visible images still work, but the art is naturalized and redirected toward method.

### What remains persuasive

- **[Confirmed]** Humanist recovery of Quintilian furnished an authoritative alternative to medieval *Ad Herennium*-based instruction.
- **[Confirmed]** Valla and Raphael Regius undermined the attribution of *Ad Herennium* to Cicero.
- **[Confirmed]** Erasmus subordinated loci and images to comprehension, order, care, rehearsal, and testing.
- **[Confirmed with qualification]** Melanchthon gave *memoria* little independent technical content.
- **[Confirmed]** Traditional mnemonic books and teachers continued despite elite criticism.
- **[Confirmed]** Agrippa’s two major works cannot be understood by assuming a simple chronological conversion from magician to sceptic.

### What requires correction or qualification

#### “Printing destroyed memory”

- **[Yates’s large thesis]** Print weakened the need for enormous internal stores.
- **[Later correction]** Carruthers, Bolzoni, Small, and others show continuity and interaction rather than clean replacement. Manuscript practices, commonplace books, marginal annotation, visual page layout, indexes, and printed diagrams externalized and reorganized mnemonic activity.
- **[Best formulation]** Print altered the economy of memory. It did not make trained recollection obsolete.

#### Humanism versus medieval memory

- **[Yates]** Erasmus sees the art as medieval cobwebs.
- **[Evidentiary problem]** Those phrases are Yates’s historical narration, not Erasmus’s words in *De ratione studii*.
- **[Later scholarship]** Humanism retained florilegia, commonplace headings, imitation, ordered excerpting, and intensive memorization. The opposition is therefore between kinds of mnemonic discipline, not memory and non-memory.

#### Melanchthon “forbids”

The primary sentence supports marginalization, not necessarily formal prohibition. Melanchthon’s *dispositio*, dialectical topics, theological *loci communes*, and school organization all foster structured retention. His alternative is imageless or minimally imagistic ordering, but it is not yet identical to Ramus’s method.

#### Agrippa’s “safety device”

- **[Yates’s interpretation]** The denunciation in *De vanitate* could protect Agrippa beside the dangerous *De occulta philosophia*.
- **[Weakness]** Agrippa continued revising and published the expanded *Occult Philosophy* in 1533, after *De vanitate*.
- **[Nauert]** Magic and scepticism coexist throughout Agrippa’s career.
- **[Bowen/Korkowski]** The book belongs to paradoxical declamation and satire.
- **[Van der Poel]** Calling it a declamation does not make it unserious; Agrippa presented probable, disputable arguments rather than dogmatic propositions.
- **[Keefer, Zambelli, Lehrich]** The two works can be understood as complementary critiques of fallen human reason and attempts to recover legitimate divine or natural wisdom.
- **[Current cautious conclusion]** Political self-protection may have influenced Agrippa’s rhetoric, but “safety device” is not demonstrated as the purpose of chapter 10.

### Carruthers, Bolzoni, Rossi, Small, Hajdu, and Ong

- **Mary Carruthers:** demonstrates that medieval memory was an art of composition and invention, not merely rote storage. This corrects any account that treats “understanding and order” as exclusively modern-humanist discoveries.
- **Lina Bolzoni:** shows the interaction among internal memory, writing, print, images, collecting, emblems, and theatrical structures. Her work undermines a simple print-versus-memory binary.
- **Paolo Rossi:** traces continuity from memory arts and Lullism into method, classification, universal language, and Leibniz. His account supports Yates’s long chronology while generally treating magic as only one strand.
- **Jocelyn Penny Small:** stresses material supports, literacy, tablets, pages, and cognitive practice, making external inscription part of—not simply the enemy of—memory.
- **Helga Hajdu:** provided one of Yates’s main pre-1966 surveys and remains important for identifying texts, though later studies supply more precise manuscript and institutional contexts.
- **Walter J. Ong:** explains the Ramist conversion of rhetoric and dialectic into visual, dichotomous method. Ong helps distinguish Melanchthon’s curricular reduction from fully developed Ramist spatial schematization.

### Scholarship named in the broader brief but not directly probative here

Bonner and Fidora on Lull, Sturlese, Ciliberto, Clucas, and Gatti on Bruno, and Bolzoni and Stagno on Camillo are indispensable for the systems against which Yates defines the humanist alternative. They do not, however, supply evidence that Erasmus, Melanchthon, or Dürer possessed hidden wheels or theatres. Importing their reconstructions into this item would erase the very distinction under study.

## History and context

### Classical rhetoric

The traditional five parts were invention, disposition, style, memory, and delivery. Three principal ancient Latin witnesses survive:

- *Rhetorica ad Herennium* 3.16–24: the fullest rules for places and images;
- Cicero, *De oratore* 2.350–60: Simonides and the principles of order and visualization;
- Quintilian, *Institutio oratoria* 11.2: description followed by reservations and emphasis on exercise.

The humanist alternative privileges Quintilian’s reservations and his final commendation of labour.

### Scholastic psychology and prudence

Medieval Dominican authors integrated artificial memory into *prudentia*. Albert the Great and Thomas Aquinas interpreted memory through Aristotle’s *De memoria et reminiscentia*. Images were justified by the soul’s dependence on sensible forms; orderly recollection served moral deliberation.

Erasmus’s formula does not simply abandon this inheritance. “Understanding” and “order” were already central to medieval mnemonic theory, although Erasmus shifts emphasis from a moralized architectural art to philological study and pedagogical exercise.

### Humanist philology

Lorenzo Valla and Raphael Regius challenged Cicero’s authorship of *Ad Herennium*. That removed one pillar of the medieval synthesis: Cicero remained authority for memory as part of prudence, but the detailed locus rules were no longer his own.

This did not invalidate the rules logically; it reduced their prestige within Ciceronian humanism.

### Universities and schools

- Erasmus wrote for tutors, schoolmasters, and elite pupils moving through the new Greek-and-Latin curriculum.
- Melanchthon’s textbook served the Lutheran university and school network centred on Wittenberg.
- His reduction of mnemonic technique was institutional: rhetoric teaching concentrated on forms usable in textual interpretation, preaching, disputation, and civic speech.
- Agrippa’s declamation attacks university disciplines, courtly pretension, itinerant professionals, and scholastic authorities from the unstable position of a court intellectual, physician, lawyer, and theological controversialist.
- Pirckheimer belonged to Nuremberg’s patrician and humanist culture; Dürer’s joke presumes shared familiarity with learned rhetorical performance.

### Relationship to Ramism

Melanchthon precedes and partially prepares the setting in which memory migrates into ordered disposition. Ramus later makes method—the progression from general to particular through dichotomous division—the effective art of memory.

- **[Genealogical reconstruction]** Erasmus → rational order and rehearsal.
- Melanchthon → reduced autonomous *memoria*, enlarged pedagogical importance of disposition.
- Ramus → diagrammatic method and dialectical organization.
- Puritan and Protestant pedagogies → suspicion of extravagant sensory imagery, though never uniform iconoclasm.

This is a family resemblance, not a documented chain of direct borrowing at every step.

## People

### Practitioners and critics

- **Erasmus:** teacher, textual critic, editor, and cautious user of ordered learning.
- **Melanchthon:** university professor, textbook writer, organizer of Protestant education.
- **Agrippa:** learned practitioner of occult philosophy who also attacked mnemonic professionals.
- **Dürer:** artist and joking observer, not a demonstrated mnemonic master.
- **Willibald Pirckheimer:** intended butt of Dürer’s joke; a learned humanist credited with an exceptionally well-stocked head.
- **Peter of Ravenna:** successful promoter of printed artificial memory and one of Agrippa’s targets.
- **Hermannus Buschius:** humanist author named dismissively by Agrippa.
- **Viglius Zuichemus:** Erasmus’s correspondent and eyewitness to Camillo’s wooden theatre.
- **Giulio Camillo:** the spectacular counterexample whose claims made Erasmus suspicious.
- **Francis I:** Camillo’s royal patron.
- **Johannes Grapheus:** printer of Agrippa’s 1530 declamation.
- **Georg Rhau:** printer of Melanchthon’s 1531 rhetoric.

### Viglius’s report on Camillo

In letter 2632, 28 March 1532, Viglius reports rumours that Camillo had constructed an amphitheatre organizing everything found in Cicero into fixed places, orders, and grades. He initially considered it a *fabula*.

In the more famous eyewitness letter, Epistle 2657, 8 June 1532, he describes:

> *Opus est ligneum, multis signatum imaginibus, loculis plenum; varii in eo ordines ac gradus…*

“The work is wooden, marked with many images, full of little boxes; in it are various orders and grades.”

He reports Camillo’s descriptions of it as a constructed or “windowed” mind and soul. Viglius admires the labour yet doubts the result and concludes, in substance, that its chief product may be a mass of papers extracted from Cicero.

**[Documented]** The letters are real contemporary evidence.

**[Interpretive caution]** Viglius is not neutral: Erasmus had asked him to investigate a potential literary adversary. His scepticism cannot simply be converted into an objective technical report.

## The magic question

### Erasmus

- **Ordinary places and images:** permitted as limited aids.
- **Magical memory:** rejected.
- **Ars notoria:** satirized as a shortcut promising learning without study.
- **Consequence:** pedagogical ridicule rather than prosecution.

### Melanchthon

Melanchthon’s sentence about art helping memory very little is pedagogical, not an accusation of sorcery. His broader Lutheran theology was suspicious of illicit invocation and superstition, but the *Elementa* passage does not call classical loci demonic, talismanic, or heretical.

### Agrippa

Agrippa creates the central paradox:

- In *De vanitate* 10, he attacks monstrous images, overload, ostentation, fraud, and frenzy.
- In *De occulta philosophia*, he catalogues celestial images, correspondences, numbers, characters, seals, and ritual powers.
- The memory chapter itself does not state that classical artificial memory is demonic.
- “Monstrous” here primarily describes cognitively grotesque images and their effects; it need not mean demons.
- His later reputation as a black magician caused neutral or ambivalent materials to be read retrospectively as necromancy.

### Was *De vanitate* a recantation?

- **[Traditional reading]** Agrippa repudiated his magical youth.
- **[Yates]** The condemnation may function as protective camouflage.
- **[Modern correction]** Agrippa revised and published *De occulta philosophia* after *De vanitate*. The two positions coexist.
- **[Disputed]** Whether this coexistence expresses irony, sceptical fideism, a hierarchy of legitimate and illegitimate magic, strategic ambiguity, or genuine unresolved contradiction.
- **[Unsupported legend]** Agrippa secretly encoded a magical memory system in chapter 10. No evidence establishes this.

### Censure and consequences

*De vanitate* provoked condemnation by theological authorities, including Louvain and Parisian critics; Agrippa answered in his *Apologia*. Its offences extended far beyond memory: it attacked the reliability and corruption of virtually every art, profession, and school.

The chapter on memory was not the principal basis of a known heresy prosecution.

### Dürer and magic

Dürer’s “gods of memory” is comic hyperbole. It is not evidence of polytheistic worship, talismanic installation, or heresy. Linking the sentence to the planetary magic square in *Melencolia I* is possible as iconographical speculation but not established by the letter.

## Afterlife

### Educational memory

The most direct descendants are mundane but historically powerful:

- comprehension before memorization;
- ordered outlines;
- division of a speech into parts;
- repeated recitation;
- self-examination;
- commonplace books;
- printed headings, indices, and marginal summaries;
- Ramist and post-Ramist diagrams.

These displaced some functions of a memory palace without eliminating spatial organization.

### Commonplace books and print

The commonplace book gives each topic a textual “place.” Extracts are entered under stable headings and retrieved by conceptual classification. This is external memory, but it preserves the principle that recollection depends on ordered addresses.

Bolzoni’s work is especially important here: page, cabinet, gallery, theatre, emblem, and internal image become interacting forms rather than sequential replacements.

### Bacon, Descartes, and Leibniz

Yates’s pp. 206–07 show that the humanist criticism did not terminate artificial memory.

- Bacon valued visible images but wanted memory arts assessed by their actual powers and ends.
- Descartes considered how a mnemonic art might be grounded in causal connections rather than arbitrary images.
- Leibniz connected *mnemonica*, method, logical form, and significant notation.

Paolo Rossi’s central contribution is to trace these continuities without making every form of classification a literal memory palace.

### Lull, combinatorics, and computing

The line “Lull to Leibniz to the computer” is best handled in levels:

- **[Documented]** Lull used finite alphabets, figures, and rule-governed combinations.
- **[Documented]** Renaissance and seventeenth-century authors adapted Lullism to encyclopaedia, method, and universal language.
- **[Documented]** Leibniz knew Lullian and mnemonic traditions and sought a *characteristica universalis* and calculus of reasoning.
- **[Scholarly reconstruction]** These projects form part of the prehistory of formal symbolic processing.
- **[Modern analogy]** A Lullian wheel can look like a mechanical computer.
- **[Overstatement]** Lull “invented the computer” or Erasmus’s ordering rules directly produced computing. Neither is historically defensible.

### Art and literature

- **Dante and fresco cycles:** relevant to the medieval moral-memory background, not direct descendants of Erasmus.
- **Emblem books:** combine image, motto, and explanatory text in memorable units; the relationship to mnemonic practice is strong but not every emblem was designed as a locus image.
- **Dürer’s *Melencolia I*:** engages number, geometry, imagination, learned labour, and melancholy. Its 4×4 magic square is documented; its status as a memory device is not.
- **Borges’s Funes:** a literary realization of Themistocles’ problem—perfect retention without useful abstraction or forgetting. The connection is thematic; there is no need to posit direct borrowing from Agrippa.
- **Umberto Eco:** his work on lists, encyclopaedias, semiotics, and perfect languages consciously re-engages Lullian and mnemonic histories.
- **Modern memory-palace revival:** returns to classical loci and vivid images, the very techniques Erasmus allowed but subordinated.
- **Memory athletes:** demonstrate that loci remain effective for competitive serial recall. Their aims differ from Erasmus’s formation of judgment and Agrippa’s concern with genuine learning.

### Modern educational psychology

Erasmus’s rereading plus compelled self-reproduction resembles spaced review and retrieval practice.

This is a useful **[modern comparison]**, not proof of experimental anticipation. Erasmus neither isolated variables nor conducted controlled trials.

## Controversies and disputes

### 1. Is there actually a “humanist alternative” system?

**Verdict:** useful historiographical category, not a jointly authored historical system.

Yates assembled heterogeneous evidence to dramatize a rational pole opposite Camillo and Bruno. Erasmus gives a method; Melanchthon truncates an art; Agrippa satirizes it; Dürer jokes about it.

### 2. Erasmus: “study” or “understanding”?

**Documented reading:** *intellectu*.  
**Literal translation:** understanding or comprehension.  
**Yates:** “study.”  
**Effect:** Yates makes the triad sound more purely laborious; the Latin gives cognition a distinct role.

### 3. Erasmus: opponent or moderate?

He is a moderate in *De ratione studii*: loci and images can help. He becomes an opponent when the technique claims magical, automatic, or effortless acquisition.

### 4. Melanchthon’s date

- 1531: securely catalogued Wittenberg printing.
- 1532: another early Wittenberg issue or bibliographical dating.
- 1534: Parisian, Venetian, and other reprints; Yates’s cited witness.
- Calling 1534 the date of the work is inaccurate.

### 5. Did Melanchthon ban all mnemonics?

The cited text says art helps memory very little. “Ban” is an interpretive intensification. His own topical and dispositive pedagogy remained mnemonic in a broad sense.

### 6. Agrippa’s intellectual consistency

Competing readings include:

- genuine conversion from magic;
- scepticism;
- evangelical fideism;
- paradoxical declamation;
- satirical attack with serious reforming intent;
- esoteric concealment;
- coherent hierarchy distinguishing corrupt human arts from divinely grounded wisdom.

The chronology rules out the simplest recantation story but not ambivalence or strategic rhetoric.

### 7. Agrippa and insanity

- **Documented:** he claims excessive artificial images can induce *insania et phrenesis*.
- **Not documented:** an identified student driven mad by a particular memory course.
- **Genre warning:** *De vanitate* systematically exaggerates the pathologies of each art.

### 8. Dürer as practitioner

The letter proves knowledge of mnemonic language. It does not prove use. Yates’s “humanist orator preparing a speech” likely refers to Pirckheimer rather than Dürer, and even Pirckheimer’s literal use remains inferential.

### 9. Dürer letter date and identity

The relevant letter is September 1506 according to Yates’s note. The widely accessible 28 February letter is not it. Digital catalogues must not be joined merely through matching sender, recipient, and year.

### 10. Print as cause of decline

Printing provided external storage and navigational devices, but mnemonic education, recitation, preaching, oral examination, and artificial-memory manuals continued. “Destruction” is a suggestive macrohistory, not a settled causal law.

### 11. Magic and iconophobia

Protestant dislike of magical invocation and, in some environments, religious imagery could reinforce suspicion of extravagant mnemonic images. Yet Melanchthon’s rhetoric passage argues from limited efficacy, not iconoclasm. Conflating pedagogical economy with a theological ban on images is unsupported.

### 12. The catalogue’s descent lines

- “From Quintilian”: strongly supported.
- “To Viglius and Erasmus against Camillo”: supported as a polemical constellation.
- “To Bruno’s Magister Psicoteus”: plausible literary contextualization, not a portrait with proven one-to-one identity.
- “To Ramism”: broad intellectual continuity, not simple direct derivation.
- “To Perkins”: Protestant mnemonic reform provides context, but each text needs separate reconstruction.

## Open questions

1. What is the exact German wording, manuscript status, and shelfmark of the September 1506 Dürer letter in Rupprich’s apparatus?
2. Does the stronger “forbids” formulation attributed to Melanchthon reflect additional wording in Yates’s Venetian 1534 copy, or is it entirely her paraphrase?
3. How substantially does Erasmus’s memory paragraph differ between the defective 1511 printing and authorized 1512 recensions?
4. Which individual author is concealed by each unstable name in Agrippa’s catalogue, especially the forms rendered “Mareol,” “Matthaeolus,” or “Veronensis”?
5. To what extent did Melanchthon’s pupils convert his *dispositio* and *loci* into explicit mnemonic classroom diagrams?
6. Can payments, advertisements, or student notes corroborate Agrippa’s claim about itinerant mnemonic teachers exploiting youths?
7. Did Pirckheimer own or annotate a contemporary mnemonic manual that would turn Dürer’s joke into evidence of actual practice?
8. How did confessional school ordinances distinguish ordinary memorization, topical order, classical loci, and prohibited magical shortcuts?
9. Is Yates’s sharp rational/Hermetic polarity sustainable when Erasmus’s figurative pedagogy and Agrippa’s continuing occult work are considered together?
10. How far did the commonplace book function as a material substitute for internal loci, and how often did teachers explicitly describe it that way?

## Conclusions

The fullest reconstructable account is deliberately modest in machinery. There is no shared palace or wheel. Erasmus supplies the positive core: understand, arrange, attend, reread, recite, detect omissions, restore them. Places and images are admissible but secondary. Melanchthon institutionalizes a similar economy by removing memory from the substantial teachable content of rhetoric and letting invention, disposition, and style carry much of its work. Agrippa attacks the traditional apparatus as cognitively overloading, ostentatious, commercially fraudulent, and incapable of producing genuine learning without natural ability. Dürer’s letter proves that architectural mnemonic language had become material for jokes among humanists, not that the artist devised a system.

Yates correctly perceived a rationalizing pole within Renaissance memory culture and correctly connected it with Quintilian, philology, and later method. She overstated its unity, the abruptness of its break with medieval practice, Melanchthon’s prohibition, and perhaps the protective function of Agrippa’s scepticism. Later scholarship replaces a story of extinction with one of redistribution: memory moved among mind, ordered discourse, manuscript, printed page, commonplace book, diagram, theatre, and combinatorial instrument.
