# Emblem and impresa as externalised memory images: Research Dossier

## Basic identification

**Name:** Emblem and *impresa* as externalised memory images  
**Catalogue position:** System 19 of 45 in the *memoria* catalogue  
**Exponent:** Frances A. Yates’s interpretive suggestion—not a system explicitly devised under this name by an early-modern author  
**Date:** Antecedents in antiquity and the Middle Ages; principal evidence c. 1499–1572  
**Principal source texts:** *Hypnerotomachia Poliphili* (Venice, 1499); Girolamo Ruscelli, *Le imprese illustri* (Venice, 1566; enlarged posthumous edition, 1572); Achille Bocchi, *Symbolicarum quaestionum de universo genere* (Bologna, 1555; revised 1574); John Dee, *Monas hieroglyphica* (Antwerp, 1564); Alessandro Farra, *Settenario dell’humana riduttione* (Casalmaggiore and Venice, 1571)  
**Principle in one sentence:** An emblem or *impresa* fixes a personal, ethical, political, religious, or cosmological intention in a memorable compound of image and words, and can therefore be interpreted—by analogy rather than by an explicit historical rule—as a mnemonic image made material and public.  
**Yates:** *The Art of Memory*, ch. V, pp. 123–24; ch. VII, pp. 169–70 and n. 25; the Bocchi engraving used as the book’s frontispiece.

### Evidentiary verdict

The expression “emblem and *impresa* as externalised memory images” is a useful modern classification, but it does **not** name a documented Renaissance *ars memoriae*. No surviving author among Colonna, Giovio, Ruscelli, Bocchi, Dee, or Farra supplies:

- a stable series of mnemonic places;
- instructions for depositing the emblems in those places;
- an ordered mental itinerary for retrieving information;
- rules for clearing and reusing the places;
- a worked mnemonic demonstration comparable to the lawsuit image in the *Rhetorica ad Herennium*.

Those are decisive absences.

What the sources do document is a family resemblance between:

1. the classical and scholastic theory that an immaterial intention is retained through a corporeal likeness;
2. the *impresa*, which makes an inward intention visible through a figurative “body” and verbal “soul”;
3. the emblem book, which makes moral and intellectual commonplaces retrievable through repeated encounters with word-image complexes;
4. the hieroglyphic or symbolic ambition to compress extensive knowledge into a single visible sign;
5. printed books and works of art functioning as durable external stores from which an attentive reader may reconstruct meanings.

Yates’s conclusion is therefore best labeled **interpretation**, not documented historical practice. Later work by Mary Carruthers, Lina Bolzoni, Paolo Rossi, Jocelyn Penny Small, Daniel Russell, John Manning, Elizabeth See Watson, Anne Rolet, and others makes the broad word–image–memory relationship considerably stronger while making the claim that this was a distinct mnemonic “system” considerably weaker.

## Evidence labels used below

- **[DT] Documented text:** stated or visibly instantiated in a primary source.
- **[MO] Material object:** established by a surviving manuscript, edition, engraving, or catalogue record.
- **[SR] Scholarly reconstruction:** a conclusion argued from historical evidence but not stated by the primary author.
- **[YI] Yates’s interpretation:** an inference proposed by Frances Yates.
- **[TR] Tradition:** a premodern report or attribution transmitted by later witnesses.
- **[D] Disputed:** substantial scholarly disagreement exists.
- **[L] Legend:** an anecdote or attribution without adequate contemporary evidence.
- **[MI] Modern invention:** later terminology or synthesis projected back onto the material.
- **[AE] Absence of evidence:** an expected feature has not been located in the relevant texts.

# The system in detail

## 1. What the alleged system is—and is not

### 1.1 The genuine classical mnemonic template

The comparison begins with the artificial memory described in the anonymous *Rhetorica ad Herennium*, III.16–24.

**[DT] Places and images.** The Latin text states:

> *Constat igitur artificiosa memoria ex locis et imaginibus.*

“Artificial memory therefore consists of places and images.”

A *locus* is:

> *locus est… sedes complete et absolute… ut aedes, intercolumnium, angulus, fornix et alia quae his similia sunt.*

“A place is a complete and distinct setting … such as a house, an intercolumnar space, a corner, an arch, and things of that sort.”

An image is:

> *imago est forma, nota, simulacrum eius rei quam meminisse volumus.*

“An image is the form, mark, or likeness of the thing we wish to remember.”

The standard modern text and translation are Harry Caplan’s Loeb edition, *[Cicero], Rhetorica ad Herennium* (Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press, 1954), III.16.29–24.40, pp. 205–25. The Latin and Caplan translation are available together in the University of Chicago transcription and the University of Texas selection. [Loeb text and translation](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Rhetorica_ad_Herennium/3%2A.html); [parallel memory passages](https://www.laits.utexas.edu/memoria/Ad_Herennium_Passages.html).

**[DT] Ordering.** Places must be memorized in a fixed order so that the user can traverse them forward or backward without confusion. Every fifth place should be specially marked—for example by a golden hand or a named acquaintance—to make counting and orientation easy.

**[DT] Retrieval.** One mentally revisits the places in their established sequence. Each placed image cues the corresponding thing or phrase. The author compares the places to wax tablets, the images to letters, their disposition to writing, and recollection to reading:

> *Nam loci cerae aut chartae simillimi sunt, imagines litteris, dispositio et collocatio imaginum scripturae, pronuntiatio lectioni.*

“For the places are very like wax tablets or papyrus, the images like letters, the arrangement and placing of the images like writing, and delivery like reading.”

**[DT] Rules for places.** They should be distinct, moderately sized, neither crowded nor deserted, neither glaring nor dark, spaced at suitable intervals, and familiar enough to be mentally surveyed.

**[DT] Rules for images.** They should be active, singular, emotionally forceful, beautiful or ugly, comic, bloody, crowned, muddy, or otherwise exceptional:

> *imagines agentes… egregia pulchritudine aut unica turpitudine… aliqua re deformemus.*

“Active images … of exceptional beauty or singular ugliness … or let us disfigure them in some fashion.”

These rules are important because they identify exactly what an emblem book does **not** supply: emblem pages contain striking images and inscriptions, but the books do not instruct readers to attach them to a pre-memorized sequence of loci.

### 1.2 The lawsuit image

The *Ad Herennium*’s celebrated example concerns a prosecution in which the defendant is accused of poisoning a man for an inheritance, with witnesses and accomplices implicated. The mnemonic image is constructed as follows:

> *aegrotum in lecto cubantem faciemus ipsum illum de quo agitur, si formam eius detinebimus; si eum non agnoverimus, at aliquem aegrotum non de minimo loco sumemus, ut cito in mentem venire possit. Et reum ad lectum eius adstituemus, dextera poculum, sinistra tabulas, medico testiculos arietinos tenentem.*

“We shall picture the sick man lying in bed as the very person concerned, if we know his appearance; if we do not, we shall take some sick man of no mean rank, so that he may come quickly to mind. We shall place the defendant beside his bed, holding a cup in his right hand, tablets in his left, and ram’s testicles on his little finger.”

The cup cues poisoning, the tablets the will or inheritance, and the testicles—*testiculi*—the witnesses, *testes*. This is a precisely specified internal image placed in a locus and decoded by visual resemblance, association, and verbal pun.

No comparable placement or retrieval instruction accompanies the heliotrope in Ruscelli, Bocchi’s Mercury, or Dee’s monad.

### 1.3 Aquinas’s mediation

Yates’s bridge from classical memory image to *impresa* is Thomas Aquinas, *Summa theologiae* II–II, q. 49, a. 1, ad 2.

The Latin reads:

> *Necesse est ad huiusmodi intentiones spirituales aliquas similitudines corporales assumere, quia simplices et spirituales intentiones facilius ex anima elabuntur nisi quibusdam similitudinibus corporalibus quasi alligentur.*

“For intentions of this kind that are spiritual it is necessary to assume certain corporeal likenesses, because simple and spiritual intentions slip more readily from the soul unless they are bound, as it were, to corporeal likenesses.”

Thomas is explaining why memory belongs to prudence and why sensible images assist the retention of intelligible or spiritual intentions. He cites “Tully’s Rhetoric,” meaning the *Ad Herennium*, then still attributed to Cicero. [Latin/English *Summa*, II–II, q. 49](https://www.newadvent.org/summa/3049.htm).

**[YI]** Yates says that this formula “defines” the *impresa*: a spiritual intention is remembered through a sensible similitude.

**Qualification:** Thomas does not mention *imprese*, emblems, badges, emblem books, or public devices. The proposed relation is typological and historical-contextual, not a demonstrated line of direct borrowing.

## 2. Emblem and impresa: components and operations

### 2.1 The impresa

In sixteenth-century Italian theory, an *impresa* was normally a personal device: a figurative design and a short motto expressing an intention, aspiration, condition, difficulty, loyalty, or concealed relation.

Paolo Giovio’s *Dialogo dell’imprese militari et amorose*, composed before his death in 1552 and first printed at Rome in 1555, gives the canonical rules. The first printing apparently had no illustrations; illustrated editions followed, notably Lyon, Guillaume Rouillé, 1559. A digitized early text is available through [Google Books](https://books.google.it/books?id=Buy1qabRZA8C); a later printing containing Ruscelli’s discourse is preserved as an [Internet Archive scan](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File%3ARagionamento_di_mons._Paolo_Giovio_sopra_i_motti%2C_%26_disegni_d%27arme%2C_%26_d%27amore%2C_che_communemente_chiamano_imprese_-_con_vn_discorso_di_Girolamo_Ruscelli%2C_intorno_allo_stesso_soggetto_%28IA_ragionamentodimo00giov%29.pdf).

Giovio’s five conditions include:

> *l’inventione o vero impresa, s’ella debba avere del buono, bisogna c’habia cinque conditioni; prima, giusta proportione d’anima e di corpo… quinta, richiede il motto, che è l’anima del corpo.*

“If the invention or device is to be good, it must have five conditions: first, a proper proportion between soul and body … fifth, it requires the motto, which is the soul of the body.”

The usual formulation of the rules is:

1. proper proportion between “soul” and “body,” meaning motto and picture;
2. neither so obscure that a sibyl is needed nor so obvious that everyone immediately understands it;
3. visual attractiveness;
4. avoidance, as a rule, of the complete human figure;
5. a short motto, commonly in a language other than the bearer’s vernacular, supplying the “soul.”

**[DT]** These are rules for composing and judging a device.

**[AE]** They are not rules for mnemonic places, serial order, mental traversal, or recollection.

### 2.2 The emblem

The emblem-book form conventionally combines:

- an inscription or *inscriptio*;
- a pictorial figure or *pictura*;
- an epigram or explanatory *subscriptio*.

That tripartite normalization developed around Andrea Alciato’s *Emblematum liber* from 1531 onward, but was neither absolute nor identical in every edition. Printers, translators, and commentators changed titles, images, order, and explanatory matter.

An emblem usually presents a moral, political, religious, erotic, or learned commonplace rather than the bearer-specific intention characteristic of an *impresa*. The boundary remained porous: sixteenth-century authors regularly used *symbolum*, *emblema*, *impresa*, *devise*, and *hieroglyphicum* with overlapping senses.

### 2.3 What serves as “places”

There are four possible answers, which must not be conflated.

1. **[DT] In classical artificial memory:** an imagined or remembered architectural sequence supplies the places.
2. **[SR] In medieval book-memory:** divisions of a page, manuscript opening, diagram, textual section, or architectural visualization can function as orienting places.
3. **[SR] In emblem books:** the materially fixed page, opening, numbered symbol, book, and sequence can serve as external finding structures.
4. **[YI] In Yates’s analogy:** the emblematic picture itself has “gone outward”; she does not demonstrate that emblem-book pages were inwardly memorized as loci.

A printed emblem book fixes order bibliographically: signatures, pagination, book divisions, symbol numbers, headings, indices, and sequence. That permits retrieval by reopening the book. This is external storage, not necessarily trained artificial memory.

### 2.4 What serves as “images”

The relevant visible units are:

- the emblematic picture;
- the motto;
- the interaction of picture and motto;
- explanatory verse or prose;
- framing architecture;
- hieroglyphic or pseudo-hieroglyphic inscriptions;
- typographic arrangement;
- the entire page as a composite visual-semantic object.

**[SR]** Bolzoni’s work supports describing such pages as engines of association and invention. Words prompt images; images prompt texts; both can call up literary, biblical, mythological, and personal material.

**[AE]** Nothing establishes that every emblematic image was intended to be an *imago agens* in the technical *Ad Herennium* sense.

### 2.5 How order is fixed

In a genuine locus-system, order inheres in the memorized route. In the works studied here, it inheres primarily in:

- narrative progression in the *Hypnerotomachia*;
- numerical sequence and five-book division in Bocchi;
- book, page, owner, and commentary in Ruscelli;
- a sequence of twenty-four theorems in Dee;
- the number seven and cosmological correspondences in Farra.

These are textual or cosmological orders. They can assist memory, but they are not interchangeable with an architectural locus-series.

### 2.6 How retrieval works

Three different retrieval operations should be distinguished:

- **Mnemonic retrieval:** mentally traverse places to recover deposited content.
- **Emblematic interpretation:** move between motto, picture, epigram, and intertexts to reconstruct significance.
- **Book retrieval:** use page order, headings, index, or visual recognition to find an item again.

Yates’s suggestion is most defensible if “memory” includes the second and third operations. It is least defensible if it means that Ruscelli, Bocchi, Dee, or Colonna taught the first.

# Principal primary cases

## 3. Vienna, Österreichische Nationalbibliothek, Cod. 5393

### Identification

**[MO]** Vienna, Österreichische Nationalbibliothek, Cod. 5393; earlier shelfmark Salisb. 292; composite paper manuscript, fifteenth century, its parts assigned broadly to the first and second halves of the century. The codex consists principally of conciliar acts, letters, and materials concerning the councils of Pisa, Constance, and Basel, followed by a figurative mnemonic tract.

The University of Vienna catalogue describes the figures not as ordinary illustrations but as variable pictured mnemonic contents designed for memorization within a fixed system of places. [Bibliotheca Gemnicensis description](https://bibliotheca-gemnicensis.univie.ac.at/handschriften/sammelhandschrift-konzilsakten-fig%C3%BCrlicher-mnemotechnik-traktat); [manuscripta.at record](https://manuscripta.at/_scripts/php/msDescription2_m1_NEU.php?ID=13539&IDinitia=).

### What Yates says

Yates cites Ludwig Volkmann’s discussion, p. 130, for advice to memorize Boethius’s *Consolation of Philosophy*. She then speculates that Lady Philosophy might come alive and wander through “the palaces of memory.”

### Correction and clarification

**[DT/MO]** Cod. 5393 is genuine evidence for illustrated mnemonic practice and belongs near the later block-book *Ars memorandi*. It demonstrates the possibility of pictorializing doctrinal or textual material.

**[AE]** It is not an emblem book and does not document the Renaissance *impresa*.

**[YI]** The passage from Boethian memorization to the animated Lady Philosophy and thence to the *Hypnerotomachia* is Yates’s imaginative historical conjecture.

**[D]** Dating the entire codex as a single unit is unsafe because it is composite. Any argument must identify the relevant leaves and codicological unit rather than cite “Cod. 5393” indiscriminately.

## 4. *Hypnerotomachia Poliphili*

### 4.1 Bibliographical facts

**[MO]** *Hypnerotomachia Poliphili, ubi humana omnia non nisi somnium esse docet* was printed at Venice by Aldus Manutius for Leonardo Crasso in December 1499. It has 234 unnumbered leaves and approximately 168–172 woodcuts, depending on whether ornamental or repeated blocks are counted separately. The Library of Congress copy is Incun. 1499 .C6; ISTC ic00767000; Goff C-767; GW 7223. [Digitized 1499 copy](https://archive.org/details/hypnerotomachiap00colo); [Seville copy, shelfmark A 336/080](https://archive.org/details/A336080); [Biblissima record](https://portail.biblissima.fr/fr/ark%3A/43093/edataab8adef95ecd3a1883dd162bf6ebb57ccdc368db).

The colophon gives the printing date; the final material says that the dream was completed on 1 May 1467. Whether this is a genuine date of composition, a fictional date, or a retrospective pose remains disputed.

### 4.2 Authorship

The decorated initials of the chapters form the acrostic:

> *POLIAM FRATER FRANCISCVS COLVMNA PERAMAVIT.*

“Brother Francesco Colonna loved Polia exceedingly.”

Another concealed signature has been read from Polia’s epitaph as *F.C.I.*, “Franciscus Columna invenit.”

**[DT]** The acrostic exists.

**[SR]** Maria Teresa Casella and Giovanni Pozzi identified the author with the Venetian Dominican Francesco Colonna of SS. Giovanni e Paolo; their documentary case was set out in *Francesco Colonna: Biografia e opere* (Padua, 1959) and underlies the Pozzi–Ciapponi critical edition.

**[D]** Alternative candidates include Francesco Colonna, lord of Palestrina; Leon Battista Alberti; and the Servite Eliseo da Treviso. Liane Lefaivre argued for Alberti; Maurizio and Stefano Colonna have argued for the Roman lord. The Biblissima notice records the competing attributions. [Authorship bibliography](https://portail.biblissima.fr/fr/ark%3A/43093/edataab8adef95ecd3a1883dd162bf6ebb57ccdc368db); [discussion of the Dominican attribution](https://www.persee.fr/doc/mefr_1123-9891_1994_num_106_2_4336); [Lefaivre project](https://mitp-content-server.mit.edu/books/content/sectbyfn/books_pres_0/4196/HP.zip/hypcredt.htm).

**Correction to Yates:** “written by a Dominican before 1500” was defensible in 1966 because Yates followed Casella and Pozzi, but should now be phrased “anonymously printed in 1499 and commonly, though not unanimously, attributed to the Venetian Dominican Francesco Colonna.”

### 4.3 Narrative and visual organization

Poliphilo falls asleep, awakens inside a dream, and moves through forests, ruins, pyramidal and triumphal architecture, gardens, palaces, processions, fountains, temples, islands, and ceremonies in pursuit of Polia. His path is sequential and strongly spatial.

The book’s visual field contains:

- architectural plans and elevations;
- triumphal processions;
- statues, reliefs, fountains, altars, tombs, obelisks, pyramids, and temples;
- invented or pseudo-antique inscriptions;
- Latin, Greek, Hebrew, and pseudo-hieroglyphic signs;
- rebus-like translations between objects and verbal meanings;
- geometrically organized gardens;
- typographic shapes integrated with woodcuts;
- erotic and sacrificial imagery.

One well-known rebus involves an eye, vulture, altar, and other objects interpreted conceptually rather than phonetically in a strict Egyptian manner. Another porphyry tablet shows a woman crowned or girdled with a serpent, holding wings and a tortoise; Logistica glosses it as the reconciliation of speed and delay. These inventions later circulated in emblematic culture. [Overview of the image-language](https://artsandculture.google.com/story/images-in-the-library-%E2%80%93-hieroglyphs-emblems-rebuses-kunstbibliothek-staatliche-museen-zu-berlin/qgWxL9T6nf4ZJw?hl=en); [emblematic afterlife of the wings and tortoise](https://emblemstudies.com/2015/12/eotm006/).

### 4.4 Is this a memory palace?

**[YI]** Yates writes:

> “Perhaps an artificial memory gone out of control into wild imaginative indulgence might be one of the stimuli behind such a work as the *Hypnerotomachia Polyphili*.”

She points especially to Petrarchan triumphs, antiquarian monuments, inscriptions, and a Hell divided into places corresponding to sins and punishments.

**[DT]** The book unquestionably organizes experience as an itinerary through striking places populated by striking figures and inscribed monuments.

**[SR]** Its word–image reversibility, visualized architecture, and sequential journey are compatible with contemporary memorial reading practices. Bolzoni’s broader model of the printed page as an interactive field strengthens this restrained conclusion.

**[AE]** The book never says that Poliphilo’s route is to be used as a mnemonic route, never supplies material to be placed in it, and never gives recall instructions.

**[D]** “Memory palace” is therefore an interpretive description. The work is also—and much more securely—a dream romance, erotic allegory, antiquarian encyclopedia, architectural fantasy, typographic experiment, and initiation narrative.

### 4.5 “Hell divided into places”

Yates’s wording is unusually confident relative to her evidence. The book contains moralized spaces, punishments, frightening monuments, and inscriptions, but later scholarship has not established that its infernal geography derives directly from a memory treatise.

**[YI]** The division into morally differentiated spaces resembles the organizational logic Yates had attributed to medieval mnemonic diagrams and to Dante.

**[AE]** No textual statement by Colonna identifies these spaces as *loci memoriae*.

### 4.6 Diagrams and plates

The illustrations are woodcuts embedded in the typographic composition rather than detachable mnemonic tables. Important types include:

- a dark forest path marking disorientation;
- ruins whose measurements are narrated in obsessive detail;
- an elephant bearing an obelisk;
- a monumental pyramid and colossal portal;
- triumphal cars populated by allegorical figures;
- inscriptions shown as though cut into marble;
- geometric gardens and architectural ground plans;
- processions whose ordered figures could function like visual inventories;
- Polia and Poliphilo moving across sequentially rendered landscapes.

The 1888 Appell facsimile reproduces 168 woodcuts photographically from the first Aldine edition. [Heidelberg facsimile](https://digi.ub.uni-heidelberg.de/diglit/appell1888/0011).

### 4.7 Editions and variants

- **1499, Venice, Aldus Manutius for Leonardo Crasso:** first edition; original woodcuts and decorated initials; chapter initials preserve the authorship acrostic.
- **1545, Venice, heirs of Aldus:** substantially a reprint using the earlier blocks, with a new Italian title page and roman initials replacing some woodcut initials. The replacement threatens the visibility of the acrostic as a typographic experience, although the textual sequence remains.
- **1546, Paris, Jacques Kerver:** French adaptation associated with Jean Martin; redesigned illustrations and fourteen or so additional cuts. Images become more conventionally illustrative and French in style.
- **1554/1553, 1561, 1600 French reissues:** altered title matter and continuing adaptation.
- **1592, London, Simon Waterson:** *Hypnerotomachia, The Strife of Love in a Dreame*, translated by “R.D.,” incomplete and freely adapted.

The second Aldine is bibliographically close to the first; the French editions materially reinterpret the visual programme. [Comparison of Italian and French woodcuts](https://blogs.unimelb.edu.au/librarycollections/2014/01/20/hypnerotomachia-poliphili-woodcuts-in-the-italian-and-french-editions/); [BnF record for the 1546 French edition](https://bp16.bnf.fr/ark%3A/12148/cb43985569n/); [edition history](https://mitp-content-server.mit.edu/books/content/sectbyfn/books_pres_0/4196/HP.zip/hyptext6.htm).

## 5. Girolamo Ruscelli, *Le imprese illustri*

### 5.1 Text and editions

Ruscelli’s theory developed through several publications:

- *Discorso intorno all’inventioni dell’imprese, dell’insegne, de’ motti e delle livree*, printed with Giovio;
- *Le imprese illustri*, Venice, 1566;
- posthumous enlarged edition: *Le imprese illustri … con la giunta di altre imprese, tutto riordinato et corretto da Francesco Patrizi*, Venice, Comin da Trino di Monferrato, 1572.

The 1572 edition contains three books, engraved title pages, a portrait, coats of arms and device engravings; eleven leaves between numbered leaves 191 and 193 are unnumbered, producing the catalogue description “[4], 288 [i.e. 298] leaves.” [Internet Archive copy](https://archive.org/details/leimpreseillustr00rus); [Folger catalogue record, CR1962 .R8 1572 Cage](https://catalog.folger.edu/record/75837); [BnF catalogue](https://catalogue.bnf.fr/ark%3A/12148/cb31270909w).

### 5.2 The heliotrope device

At 1572, pp./leaves 209 ff. in Yates’s citation, Ruscelli discusses a heliotropic plant turned toward the sun. The motto is:

> *MENS EADEM.*

“The mind remains the same,” or “the same intention.”

The plant draws on the story of Clytie in Ovid, *Metamorphoses* IV.256–70:

> *illa suum, quamvis radice tenetur, / vertitur ad solem; mutataque servat amorem.*

“Though held by her root, she turns toward her sun and, transformed, preserves her love.”

Ruscelli’s exposition multiplies the associations: constancy, love, the relation of lower things to celestial sources, illumination, the divine mind, Hermes Trismegistus, and Cabalistic or hieroglyphic learning. A study of the heliotrope tradition confirms Ruscelli’s page reference and the motto *Mens eadem*. [Heliotrope discussion](https://theses.gla.ac.uk/72502/1/10646767.pdf).

### 5.3 Is it a mnemonic image?

**[DT]** The device makes an inward state—steadfast orientation—visible through the flower’s repeated turning toward the sun.

**[YI]** For Yates this is precisely Aquinas’s “spiritual intention” fastened to a corporeal likeness.

**[SR]** The visual-verbal complex can cue a chain of Ovidian, moral, erotic, Hermetic, and theological associations.

**[AE]** Ruscelli does not instruct the bearer or reader to install the heliotrope in a memory locus. Its primary documented functions are self-presentation, interpretation, praise, and learned concealment.

### 5.4 Ruscelli and Camillo

Yates cites Ruscelli’s later *Trattato del modo di comporre in versi nella lingua italiana* (Venice, 1594 edition, p. 14) as evidence that Ruscelli knew Giulio Camillo.

**[TR/DT]** Ruscelli’s report is evidence of acquaintance with Camillo’s reputation or teaching.

**[AE]** Acquaintance does not prove that *Le imprese illustri* was constructed as Camillo’s mnemonic theatre in book form.

### 5.5 The Inquisition

Ruscelli was prosecuted by the Venetian Inquisition in connection with the unauthorized publication of the satirical *Capitolo delle lodi del fuso* in 1554 and was fined.

**[DT/SR]** This is relevant to his publishing career.

**Correction:** There is no evidence that he was prosecuted because his *imprese* were mnemonic, Hermetic, magical, or talismanic. Treating his prosecution as a consequence of “occult memory” would be a false connection.

## 6. Achille Bocchi, *Symbolicarum quaestionum*

### 6.1 Bibliographical facts

**[MO]** Achille Bocchi, *Symbolicarum quaestionum de universo genere quas serio ludebat libri quinque*, Bologna: in aedibus Novae Academiae Bocchianae, 1555. The book contains five books, extensive Latin verse, and approximately 151 copper engravings associated principally with Giulio Bonasone, probably after designs including those of Prospero Fontana. [1555 digital copy, BSB-ID 991038937869707356](https://www.digitale-sammlungen.de/de/details/bsb11347818); [Internet Archive copy](https://archive.org/details/bocchiibononsymb00bocc); [Walters copy, accession 92.391](https://art.thewalters.org/object/92.391/).

The second edition, Bologna, 1574, revised the text and images; Agostino Carracci reworked or engraved some plates. [Metropolitan Museum copy, 33.77.5](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/346208).

The 1555 issue has known plate-state and ordering variants: symbols LXXXIX and XCI are transposed in some copies; details were altered in plates XXV, XLIX, and CXLIX; some copies preserve manuscript additions or corrections. Such differences matter because a numbered emblem sequence is partly an editorial and material construction.

### 6.2 Structure

Each *symbolum* commonly combines:

- a number;
- an engraved scene;
- a short lemma or motto;
- Latin verse;
- learned references and dedicatory or dialogic framing.

Bocchi’s *serio ludere*—“to play seriously”—encourages the reader to move through several semantic levels. The book treats ethics, politics, physics, metaphysics, theology, dialectic, love, life, and death under mythological and symbolic veils.

Elizabeth See Watson’s *Achille Bocchi and the Emblem Book as Symbolic Form* and Anne Rolet’s critical edition show that this is not merely an album of detached pictures: its ordered books, academic setting, poems, interlocutors, and iconographic echoes create a deliberately interconnected intellectual programme.

### 6.3 Symbol LXII: Mercury and the seven-branched lamp

The engraving singled out by Yates shows:

- a standing male figure wearing Mercury’s winged petasus;
- his right index finger pressed to his lips, enjoining silence;
- a seven-branched golden lampstand held in the other hand;
- seven flames;
- a circular disc or nimbus above the head;
- the inscription around it:

> *MONAS MANET IN SE.*

“The One remains in itself.”

The lemma is commonly given as:

> *SILENTIO DEUM COLE.*

“Worship God in silence.”

The accompanying Latin poem identifies or assimilates the figure to Mercurius Trismegistus. The menorah/apocalyptic lamp becomes a symbol of sevenfold illumination and higher knowledge. A modern iconographic study describes the plate in these terms and identifies it as Symbol 62 in Book III. [Iconographic study](https://ricerca.unich.it/retrieve/e4233f16-6eb9-2860-e053-6605fe0a460a/01_10578_SAGGIO_LEONE_OK.pdf).

### 6.4 Yates’s frontispiece

Yates reproduced this engraving as the frontispiece to *The Art of Memory*. This creates a potential bibliographical trap.

**[MO]** The image occurs inside Bocchi’s book at p. CXXXVIII in Yates’s citation; it is not the original title-page frontispiece of the 1555 volume.

**[MI]** Calling it “Bocchi’s frontispiece” without qualification confuses its position in Yates’s modern book with its position in Bocchi’s.

### 6.5 Bocchi and Camillo

Yates reports that Bocchi belonged to Camillo’s circle and that one symbol was dedicated to Camillo.

**[SR]** Social and intellectual contact is plausible and supported by the shared northern Italian academy culture.

**[AE]** The existence of a dedication does not turn the *Symbolicarum quaestionum* into a manual of Camillo’s theatre.

### 6.6 Memory status

**[DT]** The book requires readers to identify figures, reconcile verbal and visual signs, recall classical and biblical texts, and discover concealed relations.

**[SR]** It encourages “planned associative thinking,” to use William Heckscher’s later formulation, and can function as a repertory for invention, meditation, teaching, and recollection.

**[YI]** Mercury’s sevenfold lamp provides Yates with a symbolic analogue to Camillo’s seven grades.

**[AE]** Neither the Mercury plate nor the book supplies mnemonic loci or a retrieval walk.

## 7. John Dee, *Monas hieroglyphica*

### 7.1 Bibliographical facts

**[MO]** John Dee, *Monas hieroglyphica Ioannis Dee, Londinensis … mathematice, magice, cabalistice, anagogiceque explicata*, Antwerp: Guillaume Silvius, 1564. The work contains 28 numbered leaves and a sequence of twenty-four theorems, with an engraved title page and geometrical-symbolic diagrams. [Library of Congress, Q155 .D31](https://www.loc.gov/item/11023473/); [Wellcome copy, EPB/B/1728](https://wellcomecollection.org/works/rzge7jad).

Later printings include a 1591 Frankfurt edition issued by Johann Wechel and Peter Fischer. [1591 scan](https://archive.org/details/b3335084x).

### 7.2 The monad glyph

The central sign superimposes or derives:

- a point and circle for the sun;
- a crescent for the moon;
- a cross of the four elements;
- the zodiacal sign of Aries;
- relations to Mercury and the planetary signs;
- numerical, geometrical, astronomical, alchemical, Cabalistic, and anagogical meanings.

Dee’s title explicitly promises four modes of explanation:

> *mathematice, magice, cabalistice, anagogiceque explicata.*

“Explained mathematically, magically, Cabalistically, and anagogically.”

In theorems I–IV Dee begins from the point, straight line, circle, sun, and moon. Subsequent constructions show how the glyph contains planetary characters and cosmological relations. The diagrams are not mnemonic wheels but demonstrations of graphical generation and symbolic inclusion.

### 7.3 Exact visual programme

The engraved title page frames the monad in an egg-like or oval enclosure amid scrolls, columns, celestial signs, and Latin inscriptions. Within the text the monad is repeatedly decomposed and recombined. The graphic argument proceeds theorem by theorem:

1. point;
2. line and circle;
3. solar and lunar components;
4. elemental cross;
5. planetary and Mercurial derivations;
6. Aries and fire;
7. numerical and alchemical transformations.

Dee warned his printer to reproduce the figures accurately and restrict circulation among the indiscriminate. That warning shows that he regarded graphical exactness and controlled readership as essential.

### 7.4 Memory status

**[DT]** Dee calls the work mathematical, magical, Cabalistic, and anagogical.

**[DT]** The glyph is a visual compression of numerous relations.

**[YI]** Yates says it is “a composite symbol of the seven planets, based on the character for Mercury,” moving in the same “country of the mind” as Bocchi’s Mercury and lampstand.

**[AE]** Dee does not call the monad an artificial-memory image and gives no locus instructions.

The glyph is closer to a cosmological notation, meditative diagram, alchemical signature, and synthetic symbol than to an *Ad Herennium* image. It may aid memory because its parts graphically entail one another, but mnemonic usefulness is not its declared governing purpose.

### 7.5 Magic and reception

Dee openly uses *magice*. For him, however, *magia* could mean an exalted knowledge of natural, mathematical, celestial, and divine correspondences. It should not automatically be translated as demonic sorcery.

**[DT]** The text presents itself as magical and Cabalistic.

**[SR]** Its secrecy, alchemical readings, royal dedication to Maximilian II, and later reception among alchemists made occult interpretations inevitable.

**[AE]** No surviving evidence shows Dee being prosecuted for the *Monas* itself.

**[L/MI]** Modern occult accounts often claim that the glyph is a talisman guaranteed to produce transformation merely by being displayed. Dee’s text offers no simple operational consecration ritual and no such guarantee.

## 8. Alessandro Farra, *Settenario dell’humana riduttione*

### 8.1 Bibliography

Two 1571 states or issues require distinction:

- *Settenario dell’humana riduttione*, Casalmaggiore: Farra di Bartoli, 1571;
- Venice: Cristoforo Zanetti, 1571.

The Folger copy is *PQ4621.F14 S3 1571 Cage*: [28], 280 leaves, octavo, with index, printer’s device, and initials. [Folger catalogue](https://catalog.folger.edu/record/126149); [digitized copy](https://books.google.com/books?id=tWhK92Uw4NwC).

### 8.2 Contents

Farra organizes his philosophical anthropology and cosmology around sevenfold patterns. The book invokes:

- God and creation;
- soul, mind, body, and spirit;
- heavens, planets, and celestial order;
- Plato, Plotinus, Pythagoras, Orpheus, Homer, Hermes, Zoroaster, and Iamblichus;
- Hebrew Cabala and *En Soph*;
- Christian doctrine;
- hieroglyphs, symbols, and *imprese*.

### 8.3 Relation to Camillo and the impresa

Yates calls Farra a disciple of Camillo and says that the *Settenario* contains a philosophy of the *impresa*. The sevenfold architecture certainly belongs to the same Venetian environment in which Camillo’s seven grades, planetary order, rhetoric, Cabala, and image theory circulated.

**[SR]** Farra is strong evidence that *impresa* theory could be absorbed into cosmological and theological speculation.

**[AE]** The work is not an operational memory manual. No complete set of mnemonic places, rules for image deposition, or recall exercise has been identified.

**Caution:** Farra’s *Settenario* should not be confused with a work by “Giovanni Battista Farra.” The author is Alessandro Farra, described on the title page as an Alessandrian jurist from Castellazzo.

# Yates’s account and scholarship since

## 9. Yates, chapter V, pp. 123–24

Yates begins with late-medieval and early-print mnemonic figures. Romberch’s personified Grammar has letters and inscriptions distributed over her body. Yates observes that, if the explanatory mnemonic text had been lost, the figure might appear to be an inscrutable emblem.

That is a valuable methodological point: an image’s operative function can disappear when the accompanying instructions disappear. Conversely, however, it does not follow that every inscrutable emblem once possessed a lost mnemonic instruction.

She then moves:

1. from Romberch’s documented mnemonic diagram;
2. to the Vienna Boethius manuscript;
3. to the possibility of imagined Lady Philosophy;
4. to the *Hypnerotomachia*;
5. to the *impresa*.

Her key statements are:

> “Perhaps an artificial memory gone out of control into wild imaginative indulgence might be one of the stimuli behind such a work as the *Hypnerotomachia Polyphili*.”

and:

> “The impresa, in particular, is the attempt to remember a spiritual intention through a similitude; the words of Thomas Aquinas define it exactly.”

The verbal signals matter:

- “perhaps” marks speculation;
- “might be” marks possible stimulus, not proof;
- “one of the stimuli” allows multiple causes;
- “defines it exactly” is much stronger and is the most contestable sentence.

The relevant pages can be checked in the [digitized *Art of Memory*](https://www.aproged.pt/biblioteca/Yatestheartofmemory.pdf).

## 10. Yates, chapter VII, pp. 169–70

Here Yates places Camillo’s theatre in Venetian Renaissance culture. She says:

> “The impresa is related to the memory image, as already suggested, and in commentaries on imprese there is frequently to be found a blend of Hermetic-Cabalist mysticism.”

Her examples are:

- Ruscelli’s heliotrope;
- Bocchi’s Mercury and seven-branched candlestick;
- Dee’s planetary monad;
- Farra’s sevenfold philosophy.

This is less a genealogy than a constellation of analogues joined by:

- composite images;
- compressed intentions;
- sevenfold structure;
- Mercury/Hermes;
- Cabalistic and Hermetic interpretation;
- Camillo’s reported personal connections.

## 11. What later scholarship confirms

### 11.1 Mary Carruthers

Carruthers confirms that medieval *memoria* was not mere rote storage. It was a disciplined craft of composition, ethical formation, meditation, and invention. Mental images, page layouts, diagrams, architectural forms, and verbal ornaments could all participate.

Her most important correction is that memory should not be reduced to passive reproduction:

> “The goal of rhetorical mnemotechnical craft was not to give students a prodigious memory … but to give an orator the means and wherewithal to invent his material.”

Thus emblematic images may be related to *memoria* because they support associative invention, not necessarily because they encode a fixed item for verbatim recall. [Discussion of Carruthers’s correction](https://hts.org.za/index.php/hts/article/view/3222/7556); [review of *The Book of Memory*](https://www.persee.fr/doc/ahess_0395-2649_1993_num_48_6_279234_t1_1616_0000_001).

Carruthers also warns against treating the *Ad Herennium*’s locus-system as the sole medieval art of memory. Learned mnemonics included textual division, alphabetic and numerical ordering, etymology, meditation, and compositional “duct.” [Carruthers on inventional mnemonics](https://www.connotations.de/article/mary-carruthers-inventional-mnemonics-and-the-ornaments-of-style-the-case-of-etymology/).

### 11.2 Lina Bolzoni

Bolzoni strongly confirms the interactive field Yates glimpsed among memory, image, reading, writing, and *impresa*. Her *La stanza della memoria* (Turin: Einaudi, 1995), translated by Jeremy Parzen as *The Gallery of Memory* (Toronto, 2001; reissued 2020), treats the printed page as a workshop where images become internally visible and mental images seek visible form.

For Bolzoni:

- mnemonic treatises and illustrated books exchange visual models;
- images may serve invention as much as retention;
- love imagery and devices can act like emotionally charged *imagines agentes*;
- the reader moves reversibly from text to image and image to text;
- private phantasms can collide with standardized emblematic repertoires.

This supports “externalised memory image” as an analytical description. It does not establish a separate historical system with rules. [Publisher’s account of *The Gallery of Memory*](https://utpdistribution.com/9781487524951/the-gallery-of-memory/); [Bolzoni and Volterrani, *Con parola brieve e con figura*](https://books.google.com/books?id=PuQ_AQAAIAAJ).

### 11.3 Paolo Rossi

Rossi’s *Clavis universalis* appeared in Italian in 1960, six years before Yates; it was later revised and translated as *Logic and the Art of Memory: The Quest for a Universal Language*. Rossi traces the movement from rhetorical memory to combinatorial arts, universal languages, encyclopedias, and method.

Rossi’s emphasis makes emblematic forms historically significant as instruments of ordering and communication, but he is more attentive than Yates to the shift from internal images toward method, classification, printed reference, and symbolic language. [Treccani on Rossi and the chronology of *Clavis universalis*](https://www.treccani.it/enciclopedia/paolo-rossi_%28altro%29/).

### 11.4 Jocelyn Penny Small

Small’s *Wax Tablets of the Mind* examines memory alongside the material and cognitive technologies of reading and writing in antiquity. Her framework is crucial here: written and pictorial objects do not merely “express” inner memory; they change storage, indexing, navigation, and retrieval.

An emblem book is consequently better described as an external cognitive artifact than as an internal memory palace printed on paper. [BMCR review](https://bmcr.brynmawr.edu/1998/1998.10.19/).

### 11.5 Emblem scholarship

Daniel Russell, Peter Daly, John Manning, Michael Bath, Alison Adams, Karl Enenkel, Elizabeth See Watson, Anne Rolet, and the Society for Emblem Studies have emphasized:

- genre instability;
- dependence on printers and artists;
- the difference between emblem and *impresa*;
- the reader’s active interpretive labor;
- the importance of *commonplaces*, rhetoric, education, and social display;
- material variation between editions.

Watson and Rolet in particular show that Bocchi’s book has its own symbolic, academic, poetic, and political architecture. It need not be explained as a disguised Camillian memory treatise.

## 12. What later scholarship corrects or overturns

### 12.1 There is no single “system”

The principal correction is taxonomic. The catalogue item unites several genres:

- mnemonic manuscript diagram;
- dream romance;
- personal device collection;
- neo-Latin emblem book;
- hieroglyphic cosmological treatise;
- sevenfold philosophical compendium.

They share cultural materials but not an operating procedure.

### 12.2 The *Hypnerotomachia* attribution is not settled beyond dispute

Yates stated the Dominican identity as established. Casella and Pozzi remain influential, but Alberti and Roman-Colonna alternatives have been defended. The Venetian Dominican is still the most common attribution in catalogues, not an uncontested fact.

### 12.3 Hermeticism is not sufficient proof of mnemonics

Yates often treats Hermetic-Cabalistic imagery, sevenfold order, and connection to Camillo as mutually reinforcing signs of occult memory. Later scholarship separates them more carefully. A work may be:

- Hermetic without being mnemonic;
- mnemonic without being Hermetic;
- diagrammatic without being a memory wheel;
- sevenfold because of planets, creation, sacraments, virtues, or biblical lamps rather than Camillo.

### 12.4 External artifacts do not simply reproduce internal images

Carruthers and Small encourage a bidirectional account. Manuscripts, pages, diagrams, and emblems train perception and invention; internal images also guide the production of books and art. “Externalization” is therefore a modern cognitive metaphor, not an uncomplicated historical description.

### 12.5 Yates’s “wild imaginative indulgence”

This phrase should not be mistaken for a contemporary reception judgment. It is Yates’s characterization. Later study treats the *Hypnerotomachia*’s visual-verbal excess as structured by rhetoric, architecture, antiquarianism, erotic psychology, and dream poetics—not simply as mnemonic loss of control.

# History and context

## 13. Classical rhetoric

The nearest documented ancestor is the rhetorical artificial memory of the *Ad Herennium*, Cicero’s *De oratore* II.350–60, and Quintilian’s *Institutio oratoria* XI.2.

Cicero tells the Simonides banquet story: after a roof collapse mangled the guests, Simonides identified the bodies by recalling where each person had sat. From this arose the principle that order belongs to places and content to images.

**[TR]** Cicero is the earliest surviving full narration, not an eyewitness. Simonides’s invention is an ancient origin tradition, not independently documented biography.

Quintilian reports the technique but voices practical reservations about elaborate image systems. This already proves that there was no single unanimous ancient attitude.

## 14. Scholastic psychology

Aristotle’s *De memoria et reminiscentia* describes recollection as movement through an ordered series. Medieval Aristotelian psychology explains memory through sensible species, imagination, estimation, and retained intentions.

Albertus Magnus and Thomas Aquinas placed memory within prudence. Thomas’s four mnemonic recommendations were conventionally summarized as:

1. adopt suitable corporeal similitudes;
2. arrange what is to be remembered in considered order;
3. attend to it affectively;
4. meditate or rehearse it frequently.

This moralized and intellectualized memory. It also made striking images legitimate within Christian prudence, though ecclesiastical writers could still distrust excessive curiosity or illicit arts.

## 15. Dominican and Franciscan institutions

Yates emphasized Dominican mediation because Albert and Thomas belonged to that order and because the likely author of the *Hypnerotomachia* was a Dominican.

The institutional picture is broader:

- Dominicans trained preachers in scriptural division, exempla, distinctions, and ordered recollection.
- Franciscans likewise cultivated preaching, diagrammatic theology, affective meditation, and combinatory or alphabetic forms.
- Universities preserved Aristotle, Cicero, and pseudo-Ciceronian rhetoric.
- Courts transformed heraldry, badges, liveries, medals, and mottoes into personal *imprese*.
- Academies made symbolic interpretation a communal learned practice.
- Printing multiplied standardized images and made external repertoires portable.

## 16. Lullism

Ramon Llull’s *Art* used letters, tables, trees, and rotating figures to combine divine dignities, principles, questions, subjects, virtues, and vices.

**Correction:** Llull’s Art was designed primarily as a demonstrative and missionary logic, not as a classical place-memory. Anthony Bonner and Alexander Fidora have repeatedly clarified that its combinatorial figures belong to Llull’s logical and theological project. Later authors nevertheless absorbed Lullian wheels into mnemonic, encyclopedic, Cabalistic, and universal-language schemes.

The relevance to the present dossier lies in the ambition to make relations visible and manipulable. That ambition is shared by Dee and, more loosely, by complex emblem books.

Calling Llull’s circles “the first computer” is **[MI]** metaphor. Bonner cautiously calls the Art a distant precursor of computer science because it is a rule-governed generative system, not because Llull built an electronic or even arithmetic computer. [Historical study of Llull’s graphs and lattices](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.hm.2025.02.001); [Llull and computing assessment](https://www.iiia.csic.es/~sierra/wp-content/uploads/2019/02/Llull.pdf).

## 17. Hermeticism and hieroglyphs

Ficino’s translation of the *Corpus Hermeticum*, Pico’s Christian Cabala, the rediscovery of Horapollo, and Pierio Valeriano’s *Hieroglyphica* encouraged belief in compact signs capable of presenting meanings simultaneously rather than discursively.

Early-modern “Egyptian hieroglyphs” were largely interpretive inventions based on Greek late-antique reports, monuments, coins, medals, and fantasy. They were not decipherments of phonetic Egyptian writing.

This culture helps explain:

- the pseudo-hieroglyphs of the *Hypnerotomachia*;
- Bocchi’s synthetic classical-biblical images;
- Dee’s monad;
- Ruscelli’s expansive interpretation of natural objects.

## 18. Ramism

Petrus Ramus reorganized knowledge through dichotomous division and method. Walter J. Ong’s *Ramus, Method, and the Decay of Dialogue* presents Ramism as part of a transition toward visually ordered, diagrammatic, print-centered pedagogy.

The relation to emblematic memory is indirect:

- Ramist tables externalize order;
- emblem pages externalize associative images;
- both exploit the visible page;
- Ramism generally minimizes the fantastically affective image favored by the *Ad Herennium*.

Yates proposed intersections among Ramism, Lullism, and memory. Ong’s account supports the importance of page-space but not a single subterranean occult genealogy.

## 19. Universal language

From hieroglyphic signs and Lullian combinations arose projects for characters that would represent things, notions, or relations across linguistic boundaries. The line runs—not continuously, but through recurrent appropriations—from Llull to Renaissance combinatorics, Kircher, Dalgarno, Wilkins, and Leibniz.

Leibniz’s *characteristica universalis* and *calculus ratiocinator* sought formal representation and calculable reasoning. Emblems contributed a cultural dream of compressed visual intelligibility; they were not, however, a formal calculus.

# People

## 20. Practitioners and transmitters

### Anonymous author of the *Rhetorica ad Herennium*

Codified the fullest surviving ancient rules for places and active images. The medieval attribution to Cicero greatly enhanced its authority.

### Thomas Aquinas

Integrated art-assisted memory into prudence and supplied the formula on binding spiritual intentions to corporeal similitudes.

### Francesco Colonna—or the author concealed under that name

Produced the *Hypnerotomachia*’s dream itinerary and composite verbal-visual archaeology. Whether Venetian Dominican, Roman lord, Alberti, or another author remains disputed.

### Aldus Manutius and Leonardo Crasso

Aldus printed the 1499 *Hypnerotomachia*; Crasso financed or commissioned the edition. The visual programme was a collaborative publishing achievement, not simply the solitary author’s mental imagery.

### Paolo Giovio

Established the influential critical vocabulary for the *impresa* as a proportioned union of verbal soul and pictorial body.

### Girolamo Ruscelli

Expanded *impresa* theory through long interpretive commentaries. His role as Venetian *poligrafo* and editor made him a mediator among courtly display, print, antiquarian learning, and esoteric speculation.

### Achille Bocchi

Humanist, Greek lecturer, and leader of the Accademia Bocchiana in Bologna. His academy enjoyed elite patronage, including Cardinal Alessandro Farnese.

### Giulio Bonasone, Prospero Fontana, and Agostino Carracci

Artists and engravers central to the material form and later revision of Bocchi’s symbols. Their participation shows why the “memory image” cannot be assigned exclusively to an authorial mind.

### John Dee

Mathematician, astrologer, natural philosopher, collector, and later angelic experimenter. He dedicated the *Monas* to Emperor Maximilian II.

### Alessandro Farra

Jurist and philosophical writer who combined Christian theology, Platonism, Hermeticism, Cabala, hierarchy, and the number seven.

### Giulio Camillo

The connective figure in Yates’s argument. His lost or partly reconstructed theatre arranged universal knowledge under planetary grades and mythological images. Ruscelli claimed knowledge of him; Farra is presented as a disciple; Bocchi moved in related circles.

## 21. Patrons and publics

The emblem and *impresa* inhabited:

- ducal and royal courts;
- aristocratic households;
- academies;
- universities;
- printing houses;
- festivals, tournaments, armor, clothing, jewelry, medals, façades, and painted interiors.

An *impresa* could be carried on a shield or garment, painted in a room, engraved in a book, or exchanged in learned conversation. This physical portability is the strongest literal sense in which it was “external.”

## 22. Critics and enemies

Criticism came from several directions:

- Quintilian doubted cumbersome mnemonic image-making.
- Protestant and Catholic reformers could distrust pagan, erotic, or occult images, though both confessions used emblematic pedagogy.
- Inquisitorial authorities scrutinized unauthorized, heterodox, satirical, magical, and divinatory books.
- Humanist theorists criticized *imprese* that were too obscure, too obvious, badly proportioned, or dependent on forced explanations.
- Modern scholars criticize Yates for treating suggestive cultural adjacency as genealogy.

## 23. Requested anecdotes: relevance audit

### The auctioneer

No securely documented Renaissance “auctioneer” anecdote belongs to this emblem-*impresa* cluster. Modern descriptions sometimes compare rapid memory performers to auctioneers; Harry Lorayne was described this way, but that is a twentieth-century performance context. It is **[MI]** if attached to Colonna, Ruscelli, Bocchi, Dee, or Farra.

### The professor’s party

The historically relevant party is Simonides’s banquet, told by Cicero and Quintilian. It is an ancient etiological tradition, not an eyewitness report. “Professor’s party” is not a source-supported designation.

### The student who lost his memory

No primary case fitting that description was located in these five principal texts. Stories of students, scholars, or prodigious mnemonists losing memory circulate in later mnemonic and medical literature, but importing one here without identification would be irresponsible.

### The accused magician

Dee was popularly and polemically associated with magic, especially after his angelic conversations with Edward Kelley. Yet no evidence shows that the *Monas* was prosecuted as a mnemonic device.

Giordano Bruno was prosecuted and executed by the Roman Inquisition in 1600, but reducing his trial to “memory magic” is false. The surviving trial summary concerns multiple theological and philosophical propositions.

Peter of Ravenna was a conspicuous professional memory teacher, but he was not the “accused magician” of this particular dossier.

**Finding:** These anecdotes appear to belong to a master template covering other memory systems. Except for Simonides’s banquet, they cannot safely be assigned to system 19.

# The magic question

## 24. Was the classical art magical?

The *Ad Herennium* presents artificial memory as rhetorical technique, not magic. Its grotesque and emotionally violent images are psychological devices modeled on the greater memorability of unusual experiences.

**[DT]** No invocation, talisman, spirit, astrological election, or consecration is prescribed.

## 25. Was Aquinas’s memory art magical?

No. Aquinas incorporates it into prudence. Corporeal likenesses aid an embodied human intellect.

**[DT]** Its legitimacy is philosophical and moral.

**Qualification:** Medieval theologians distinguished acceptable mnemonic images and meditation from illicit divination, necromancy, or spirit magic.

## 26. Was the *Hypnerotomachia* magical or heretical?

The book contains pagan gods, erotic initiation, sacrifices, dream allegory, antiquarian ritual, invented inscriptions, and hieroglyphs.

- **[DT]** These elements are present.
- **[SR]** Neoplatonic, antiquarian, erotic, architectural, and possibly initiatory readings are legitimate.
- **[AE]** No operational magical rite is identified as the purpose of the book.
- **[AE]** No documented prosecution of the book or author for this text is established.
- **[L]** Claims that it is a coded Templar, Rosicrucian, Masonic, alchemical, or satanic manual generally postdate the evidence and vary mutually.

## 27. Were imprese talismans?

Some devices were worn on bodies, armor, jewelry, seals, or buildings and could acquire apotropaic or devotional force. Astrological, Cabalistic, alchemical, or Hermetic commentaries could make them resemble talismans.

But genre is not operation:

- an emblem represents or teaches;
- an *impresa* declares or conceals an intention;
- a talisman is normally made under specified correspondences or rites to produce an effect.

**[AE]** Ruscelli gives no general rule that an *impresa* must be astrologically elected, consecrated, or causally efficacious.

## 28. Bocchi’s Mercury

Bocchi’s silent Mercury with seven lamps is esoteric in iconography.

- **[DT]** It combines Mercury/Hermes, silence, unity, and sevenfold illumination.
- **[SR]** It participates in Christian-Hermetic and Cabalistic symbolism.
- **[AE]** It is not accompanied by a ritual directing the reader to use the engraving as a talisman.
- **[YI]** Yates reads it as an emblematic statement of Camillo-like Hermetic memory.

## 29. Dee’s monad

Dee’s case is different because the title explicitly claims magical and Cabalistic explanation.

- **[DT]** The work is self-described as *magice* and *cabalistice*.
- **[DT]** Dee associates the glyph with cosmic and alchemical transformation.
- **[SR]** Contemplation of the glyph was intended to transform understanding.
- **[D]** Whether Dee intended physical alchemical practice, primarily spiritual alchemy, cryptographic cosmology, or all of these remains debated.
- **[AE]** The *Monas* contains no standard grimoire-like evocation procedure.

## 30. Farra

Farra’s Cabala and Hermetic authorities make the *Settenario* esoteric by modern classification, but it is written within a Christian universal philosophy.

- **[DT]** Cabalistic, Hermetic, Neoplatonic, and Christian materials coexist.
- **[AE]** No evidence of prosecution for the *Settenario* has been established.
- **[AE]** No talismanic activation procedure governs its *imprese*.

## 31. Ars notoria

The *Ars notoria* promises knowledge or memory through prayers, ritual observance, divine names, and complex figures or *notae*. It was repeatedly suspected or condemned because its efficacy could be interpreted as dependent on illicit spirits.

Thomas Aquinas rejected attempts to acquire knowledge by such ritual figures in *Summa contra gentiles* III.104–06 and related discussions.

The distinction is fundamental:

- *Ad Herennium*: natural faculties assisted by art and practice;
- Aquinas’s mnemonic images: corporeal cues assisting prudence;
- *Ars notoria*: ritual and revelatory acquisition;
- emblem and *impresa*: figurative communication and interpretation;
- Dee’s monad: esoteric philosophical symbol, adjacent to but not identical with ritual magic.

Yates’s wide category of “occult memory” sometimes makes these boundaries appear more permeable than the primary texts warrant.

# Afterlife

## 32. Dante and moral topography

Yates proposed that Dante’s ordered Hell, Purgatory, and Paradise could be interpreted through the medieval art of memory: morally classified places, unforgettable punishments, and emotionally forceful figures.

**[YI]** This is an influential reading.

**[SR]** Carruthers and later medievalists support the broader idea that Dante wrote within a memorial and meditational culture.

**[AE]** Dante never identifies the *Commedia* as a technical locus-system.

The safest claim is that Dante’s poem and artificial memory share a culture of ordered moral visualization; direct derivation remains disputed.

## 33. Frescoes and painted cycles

Medieval and Renaissance fresco cycles externalize ordered theological, civic, and ethical knowledge across walls and architectural compartments. Viewers move through space and encounter memorable figures.

This makes them analogous to memory architecture. Yet each cycle requires its own evidence: patronage, inscriptions, preaching context, liturgy, or textual programme. “Looks like a memory palace” is not proof that it was designed as one.

## 34. Emblem books

The emblem tradition expanded across Europe through Alciato, Sambucus, Junius, Whitney, Vaenius, Rollenhagen, Saavedra Fajardo, and many others.

Its principal mnemonic effects were:

- repetition of memorable image–motto pairs;
- indexing of moral commonplaces;
- portable repertoires for writers, preachers, painters, and courtiers;
- association of abstract doctrine with concrete objects;
- cross-reference among classical, biblical, heraldic, and vernacular traditions.

The *Hypnerotomachia* influenced individual emblematic motifs; it was not simply the first emblem book.

## 35. Bruno’s *Eroici furori*

Bruno’s *De gli eroici furori* (London, 1585) includes emblem-like shields, mottos, poems, and prose expositions. Yates interpreted these as outward literary counterparts to Bruno’s inner memory images.

Later Bruno scholarship by Michele Ciliberto, Leen Spruit, Rita Sturlese, Hilary Gatti, Stephen Clucas, and others treats the work through philosophy, poetics, ethics, religion, and visual language. The mnemonic dimension remains illuminating but cannot exhaust the dialogue.

## 36. Fludd and later diagrammatic encyclopedism

Robert Fludd’s engravings unite cosmology, music, anatomy, Cabala, architecture, and theatre. Some explicitly depict stages, chambers, and mnemonic structures. They are a more direct case of externalized memory architecture than the works in system 19.

The emblem-*impresa* cluster helped normalize the assumption that a printed plate could gather a world of relations into one visible field.

## 37. Borges’s “Funes the Memorious”

Borges’s 1942 story, collected in *Ficciones* in 1944, imagines a man unable to forget. Funes develops an extravagant private numbering system but cannot generalize because every instance remains irreducibly particular.

The story is not descended demonstrably from Yates, whose book appeared in 1966. It belongs instead to the long paradox of perfect memory: total retention may destroy abstraction and thought.

Any claim that Borges modeled Funes directly on Renaissance emblematic memory requires evidence not presently available.

## 38. Umberto Eco

Eco’s fiction and semiotics repeatedly engage encyclopedias, labyrinths, signs, codes, libraries, and the tension between finite signs and unlimited interpretation. He was deeply familiar with medieval and early-modern semiotic traditions.

Connections to Llull, Bruno, universal language, and emblematic culture are intellectually substantial, but an exact direct descent from Yates’s “externalised *impresa*” formulation should not be presumed without textual citation.

## 39. Memory-palace revival and athletes

Modern memory competitors use familiar routes, vivid images, person–action–object codes, and reusable locus sets. This is genuinely continuous in operating principle with the classical method of loci, though modern competitors usually learn through recent manuals and communities rather than an unbroken guild tradition.

Experimental work finds that method-of-loci training can improve ordered recall and alter functional connectivity patterns, although recent systematic review rates parts of the evidence low or very low in quality because of bias and small studies. [Mnemonic-training study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7929507/); [systematic review and meta-analysis](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12514325/).

Modern competitors’ systems are not descendants specifically of Ruscelli’s heliotrope or Bocchi’s Mercury.

## 40. Computing and combinatorics

The most defensible chain is:

1. Llull created rule-governed combinatorial figures for theological demonstration.
2. Renaissance Lullists generalized their use.
3. Early-modern universal-language theorists sought systematic characters.
4. Leibniz studied Llull and proposed a universal characteristic and calculus.
5. Formal logic and computing later realized mechanized symbol manipulation in forms Llull could not have anticipated.

Emblems contributed to the culture of visible compression; Lullian combinations contributed more directly to formal generation. Calling an emblem a “data visualization” or a monad glyph “software” is useful analogy but **[MI]**, not historical terminology.

# Controversies and disputes

## 41. Did Yates discover a forgotten system?

No single forgotten system bearing this name has been found.

**Best formulation:** Yates identified a historical problem—the traffic between inner images, printed images, symbolic interpretation, and Renaissance memory culture—and proposed a suggestive family resemblance.

## 42. Is an impresa “exactly” Aquinas’s corporeal similitude?

**For the claim:**

- an *impresa* embodies an inward intention in a sensible figure;
- it is designed to be retained and recognized;
- motto and image stabilize interpretation;
- the culture knew scholastic theories of image and intention.

**Against the claim:**

- Aquinas discusses memory and prudence, not courtly devices;
- the technical scholastic meaning of *intentio* is not identical to the bearer’s declared intention;
- an *impresa* communicates to others, whereas Thomas explains retention within the soul;
- Giovio’s own genealogy and rules do not cite Aquinas.

**Verdict:** strong analogy; unproved direct definition.

## 43. Did the *Hypnerotomachia* derive from artificial memory?

**For:**

- sequential travel through architecturally differentiated places;
- striking figures and ceremonies;
- inscriptions and rebuses;
- moralized topography;
- a likely Dominican author familiar with a culture shaped by scholastic memory;
- pronounced reversibility of word and image.

**Against:**

- no mention of *ars memoriae*;
- no deposit-and-recall procedure;
- dream romance and triumphal/allegorical literature independently explain the itinerary;
- Vitruvian and Alberti-derived architectural discourse explains much of the spatial form;
- attribution to a Dominican remains disputed.

**Verdict:** plausible contextual influence, unproved textual derivation.

## 44. Was the author a Dominican?

**[D]**

- The acrostic says *frater Franciscus Columna*.
- Casella and Pozzi identify a fitting Venetian Dominican.
- Documentary and linguistic evidence has sustained that identification in much scholarship.
- Lefaivre attributes the work to Alberti.
- Calvesi and Stefano Colonna advocate the Roman Francesco Colonna.
- Other candidates have appeared.

No theory is established merely by decoding the acrostic, because more than one Francesco Colonna can be proposed and *frater* itself has been contested.

## 45. Who designed the woodcuts?

Attributions have included Benedetto Montagna, Botticelli, Mantegna-related workshops, and other Venetian artists.

**[AE]** No definitive contemporary document names the designer or cutter.

The illustrations should therefore be cited as anonymous Venetian woodcuts produced for the Aldine edition, unless a specific attribution is explicitly labeled disputed.

## 46. Are Bocchi’s symbols a memory theatre?

Bocchi’s numbered five-book architecture and Camillo connections make the comparison attractive. But Rolet and Watson demonstrate a self-sufficient emblematic, poetic, academic, and philosophical organization.

**Verdict:** mnemonic affordance and shared milieu, not proven identity.

## 47. Is Bocchi’s Mercury the book’s frontispiece?

No, not in the 1555 original. It is the frontispiece to Yates’s *The Art of Memory*. In Bocchi it is an internal numbered symbol. This is a minor but persistent bibliographical error.

## 48. Is Dee’s monad a seven-planet mnemonic?

It contains the planetary characters through graphical derivation and can assist their recollection. Dee, however, presents a philosophical, mathematical, magical, Cabalistic, and alchemical synthesis, not a lesson for memorizing the seven planets.

**Verdict:** mnemonic by affordance; cosmological by declared purpose.

## 49. Are emblem books external memory?

Three senses must be separated:

- **Strong historical claim:** authors explicitly called them artificial memory—generally unsupported here.
- **Functional claim:** they store and cue recoverable cultural information—well supported.
- **Cognitive-theoretical claim:** they participate in distributed or extended cognition—defensible modern analysis, but **[MI]** as terminology.

## 50. Did Hermeticism produce modern science and computing?

Yates’s wider thesis made Renaissance magic a major precursor to active, operative attitudes toward nature. It corrected histories that excluded occult philosophy altogether.

Later historians generally reject any single-origin account. Mathematics, artisanal practice, instruments, institutions, theology, natural philosophy, commerce, state patronage, and humanism all mattered.

The Lull-to-Leibniz relationship is documented; “emblem to computer” is primarily analogy.

# Dedicated contested-material pass

The following propositions were specifically tested against searches combining Yates, emblem, *impresa*, dispute, attribution, dating, magic, and heresy.

## 51. “Emblem and impresa as externalised memory images” as a period phrase

**Finding: [AE/MI].** No primary or established early-modern title using this wording was found. It is a catalogue-level synthesis based on Yates and later external-memory vocabulary.

## 52. Direct contemporary identification of *impresa* and *imago agens*

**Finding: [AE].** No passage in Giovio, Ruscelli, Bocchi, Dee, or Farra was located that equates an *impresa* with the *Ad Herennium*’s *imago agens*.

Bolzoni and later interpreters make the comparison analytically.

## 53. A lost mnemonic key to the *Hypnerotomachia*

**Finding: [AE/L].** No manuscript key, authorial commentary, or contemporary reader’s manual identifying the narrative as a memory palace has been found. Modern “code-breaking” claims frequently presuppose alchemical, Masonic, Rosicrucian, erotic, architectural, or political master keys; none commands consensus.

## 54. Heresy prosecution for emblematic memory

**Finding: [AE].** No principal author here is documented as prosecuted specifically for using emblematic images as artificial memory.

- Ruscelli’s legal trouble concerned publication.
- Dee’s reputation for conjuration arose principally from his broader career and angelic experiments.
- Colonna’s alleged irregularities do not prove prosecution of the *Hypnerotomachia* as magical memory.
- Bocchi’s and Farra’s books passed through ordinary mechanisms of learned publication.

## 55. Talismanic activation

**Finding: [AE].** None of the core emblematic books provides a general ritual for consecrating its plates as talismans. Dee’s monad is the closest to explicitly magical symbolic operation, but it still lacks a simple talisman manual’s recipe.

## 56. The “Dominican-authored *Hypnerotomachia*”

**Finding: [D].** Common and well-supported attribution, not settled fact. Yates’s categorical phrasing should be corrected.

## 57. The date “before 1500”

**Finding: [MO].** Printing in December 1499 is certain. Completion in 1467 is stated in the book but its historical interpretation is disputed. “Before 1500” is correct for publication but evasive where exact dating is available.

## 58. Ruscelli 1572 as authorial final form

**Finding: [MO/D].** Ruscelli died before the 1572 edition. It was reordered, corrected, and enlarged by Francesco Patrizi. Claims based on the 1572 arrangement must not automatically be attributed to Ruscelli’s final personal design.

## 59. Bocchi 1555 versus 1574

**Finding: [MO].** The second edition is not a neutral reprint: text, plates, states, ordering, and engraving work differ. A cited image should be tied to the edition.

## 60. “Mercurius with the seven-branched candlestick”

**Finding: [DT].** The figure, lamp, silence gesture, and sevenfold symbolism are visible and textually supported.

**Qualification:** Identifying the lamp specifically as the menorah, the seven churches of Revelation, the seven planets, Camillo’s seven grades, or all simultaneously is interpretive. Bocchi’s syncretic programme encourages multiplication of meanings.

## 61. “Wild imaginative memory”

**Finding: [YI].** This is Yates’s phrase, not a known Renaissance classification. Later writers who repeat it are transmitting Yates unless they provide independent evidence.

# Open questions

1. Can the exact mnemonic leaves of ÖNB Cod. 5393 be fully edited with their Boethian source correspondences, rather than known largely through Volkmann and catalogue summaries?

2. Do surviving annotated copies of the *Hypnerotomachia* contain explicit references to *ars memoriae*, *loci*, *imagines agentes*, or Aquinas?

3. Can reader annotations in copies of Ruscelli show actual memorization practices rather than interpretation and ownership?

4. Which of Ruscelli’s long Hermetic-Cabalistic expansions belong to his 1566 text, and which reflect Patrizi’s 1572 reordering or additions?

5. What is the precise textual basis for identifying Bocchi’s Symbol LXII simultaneously with Hermes Trismegistus, the Apocalypse, the Jewish menorah, planetary sevens, and Camillo?

6. Which plates in individual 1555 Bocchi copies occupy altered states, and how do those changes affect interpretation?

7. Does Farra ever explicitly connect *impresa*, *memoria*, *reminiscentia*, and *locus* in the same argumentative passage? A searchable critical edition is needed.

8. Did Dee’s readers annotate the monad as an aid to memory, or primarily as an alchemical, astrological, religious, and cryptographic sign?

9. Can a direct documentary chain be demonstrated between Camillo’s teaching and any particular emblem-book architecture?

10. How did physical uses of *imprese*—on clothing, shields, rings, rooms, and façades—alter recall compared with book-bound devices?

11. When did the modern expression “externalised memory image” first become standard in scholarship, and how much derives from Yates, Warburgian iconology, cognitive archaeology, or extended-mind theory?

12. Can modern experimental work distinguish memory prompted by a picture alone, a motto alone, and the deliberately enigmatic interaction of the two?

---

# Conclusions

The fullest defensible reconstruction yields a spectrum rather than a single technique.

At one end stands the documented classical *ars memoriae*: fixed places, active images, deliberate encoding, ordered mental traversal, and recall. At the other stand public and printed symbols whose chief documented functions are communication, concealment, moral instruction, learned play, praise, meditation, and cosmological synthesis.

Between them lies a historically real culture of memory:

- Aquinas explains why spiritual intentions require corporeal likenesses.
- Manuscript and printed mnemonic figures make internal cues visible.
- The *Hypnerotomachia* turns reading into an itinerary through striking places, figures, and inscriptions.
- The *impresa* gives an inward intention a figurative body and verbal soul.
- Ruscelli expands a device into a network of recoverable mythological and esoteric associations.
- Bocchi constructs a numbered academy of symbolic questions.
- Dee compresses a cosmology into a generative glyph.
- Farra organizes philosophical return through sevenfold correspondences.

Yates saw this continuity before most historians were prepared to take it seriously. Her insight survives: memory, imagination, rhetoric, symbolic art, and the material book cannot be cleanly separated.

Her stronger historical implication does not survive intact. These objects are not demonstrated fragments of one occult mnemonic system. They are heterogeneous artifacts that share techniques of visual compression, affective salience, spatial or numerical order, interpretive retrieval, and the conversion of invisible intentions into durable sensible form.

“Externalised memory images” is therefore best retained as a critical lens—with its modern and Yatesian status always declared.
