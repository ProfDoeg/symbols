### Evidentiary verdict at the outset

**[Documented text]** Aquinas indisputably gives four aids to memory and grounds them in Aristotelian psychology, the ethical virtue of prudence, and material taken partly from the *Ad Herennium*.

**[Documented text]** He calls for corporeal likenesses, order, attentive-affective impression, and repetition.

**[Not documented]** Aquinas does not describe a complete architectural memory palace, specify a sequence of rooms, draw a mnemonic diagram, provide an encoded specimen argument, or say that he memorized the *Summa* in a cathedral.

**[Yates’s interpretation]** The “basic Thomist method,” the “inner Gothic cathedral,” the treatment of monumental virtue-and-vice cycles as externalizations of mnemonic images, and the direct mnemonic interpretation of Vienna 2639 and the Spanish Chapel are reconstructions made by Frances Yates.

**[Later correction]** Subsequent scholarship supports Yates’s broad insight that medieval memory was ethical, affective, visual, ordered, and fundamental to composition. It does not establish the particular Vienna manuscript or the Spanish Chapel as records of an Aquinas-designed mnemonic system. Research on Vienna 2639 identifies its poems and images with an Augustinian-eremitical and Italian visual-textual context; the codex is evidence for ordered scholastic allegory, not independently documented evidence of Aquinas’s private memory practice.

## The system in detail

## 1. What Aquinas actually wrote

The operative passage is *Summa theologiae* II–II, q. 49, a. 1, reply to objection 2. Question 48 enumerates the components of prudence; question 49 examines eight “integral parts” individually. Article 1 asks whether memory is one of them.

Aquinas first explains why memory belongs to prudence:

> *Prudentia est circa contingentia operabilia… Experimentum autem est ex pluribus memoriis… Unde consequens est quod ad prudentiam requiritur plurium memoriam habere.*

“Prudence concerns contingent matters of action… Experience results from many memories… Therefore prudence requires the possession of many memories.”

**[Documented text]** This is an ethical and epistemological claim. Prudence needs remembered particulars because practical affairs do not proceed from universally necessary premises alone.

Aquinas then answers the objection that memory is natural and therefore cannot be an acquired component of prudence:

> *Sicut prudentia aptitudinem quidem habet ex natura, sed eius complementum est ex exercitio vel gratia, ita etiam, ut Tullius dicit, in sua rhetorica, memoria non solum a natura proficiscitur, sed etiam habet plurimum artis et industriae.*

“Just as prudence has a natural aptitude but receives its completion from exercise or grace, so also, as Tully says in his Rhetoric, memory not only arises from nature but owes much to art and diligence.”

**[Documented text]** Aquinas distinguishes natural aptitude from trainable practice. His “Tully” here is the medievally Ciceronian *Rhetorica ad Herennium*, now treated as anonymous.

He then states the four aids:

> *Et sunt quatuor per quae homo proficit in bene memorando.*

“And there are four things through which a person advances in remembering well.”

### First precept: suitable, unusual, corporeal likenesses

> *Quorum primum est ut eorum quae vult memorari quasdam similitudines assumat convenientes, nec tamen omnino consuetas, quia ea quae sunt inconsueta magis miramur, et sic in eis animus magis et vehementius detinetur; ex quo fit quod eorum quae in pueritia vidimus magis memoremur.*

“First, for the things one wishes to remember, one should assume certain suitable likenesses, though not entirely customary ones, because we wonder more at unfamiliar things, and the mind is held in them more fully and intensely; hence we remember more readily things seen in childhood.”

He supplies the psychological rationale:

> *Ideo autem necessaria est huiusmodi similitudinum vel imaginum adinventio, quia intentiones simplices et spirituales facilius ex anima elabuntur nisi quibusdam similitudinibus corporalibus quasi alligentur, quia humana cognitio potentior est circa sensibilia.*

“The invention of such likenesses or images is necessary because simple and spiritual intentions slip more easily from the soul unless they are, as it were, bound to certain corporeal likenesses, since human cognition is stronger with respect to sensible things.”

> *Unde et memorativa ponitur in parte sensitiva.*

“Hence the power of memory is assigned to the sensitive part.”

**[Documented text]** This is the strongest textual basis for a “Thomist mnemonic.” Abstract or noncorporeal content is to be tethered to sensible, corporeal likenesses.

**[Important qualification]** *Intentio* is not merely “an idea” in modern English. In scholastic faculty psychology, an intention can be a significance apprehended in or through a sensible presentation—classically, the sheep’s apprehension of the wolf as something to flee. Yates’s rendering “simple and spiritual intentions” is verbally faithful but loads the phrase with a specifically religious resonance stronger than the passage by itself requires.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** The precept can support images of virtues, vices, doctrines, arguments, or actions, but Aquinas gives no specimen image here.

### Second precept: considered order

> *Secundo, oportet ut homo ea quae memoriter vult tenere sua consideratione ordinate disponat, ut ex uno memorato facile ad aliud procedatur.*

“Second, a person must arrange in an orderly manner, by considered reflection, the things to be retained in memory, so that from one remembered item one may proceed easily to another.”

Aquinas cites Aristotle:

> *A locis videntur reminisci aliquando, causa autem est quia velociter ab alio in aliud veniunt.*

“They sometimes appear to recollect from places; the cause is that they pass rapidly from one thing to another.”

**[Documented text]** Retrieval is associative traversal. One begins with something available and follows an established succession until reaching what was lost.

**[Yates’s interpretation]** Yates says that “the places of the artificial memory are thus given a rational grounding in Aristotelian theory of reminiscence based on order and association” (*Art of Memory*, p. 72).

**[Correction]** Aquinas’s immediate injunction is to arrange the things to be remembered. He does not explicitly say here that “order replaces places,” that the order must be the argumentative divisions of a *summa*, or that classical architectural loci should be discarded. Those are interpretive extrapolations.

### Third precept: attention and affect

> *Tertio, oportet ut homo sollicitudinem apponat et affectum adhibeat ad ea quae vult memorari, quia quo aliquid magis fuerit impressum animo, eo minus elabitur.*

“Third, one must apply solicitude and bring affect to the things one wishes to remember, because the more strongly something has been impressed upon the mind, the less it slips away.”

Aquinas then attributes to “Tully”:

> *Sollicitudo conservat integras simulacrorum figuras.*

“Solicitude preserves intact the forms of the images.”

But the *Ad Herennium* reads:

> *Frequentia et obambulatio hominum conturbat et infirmat imaginum notas; solitudo conservat integras simulacrorum figuras.*

“The crowding and passing to and fro of people confuses and weakens the marks of the images; solitude preserves intact the forms of the images.”

**[Documented textual divergence]** *Sollicitudo*, “care, attention, concern,” stands in Aquinas where the received classical text has *solitudo*, “solitude.”

**[Yates’s interpretation]** Yates calls this a misreading or misremembering. It transforms a practical direction—choose uncrowded loci—into a direction to invest the memory-content with care and feeling.

**[Caution]** The existence of the different readings is certain. The historical mechanism is not. Without a full collation of the *Ad Herennium* exemplar or textual family available to Aquinas, one cannot decide whether he personally misread, quoted from memory, deliberately adapted the sentence, or inherited a variant.

**[Documented text]** The affective component does not depend entirely on the faulty citation: Aquinas himself explicitly writes *affectum adhibeat* and explains intensity of impression.

### Fourth precept: frequent meditation

> *Quarto, oportet quod ea frequenter meditemur quae volumus memorari.*

“Fourth, we must frequently meditate upon the things we wish to remember.”

> *Consuetudo est quasi natura; unde quae multoties intelligimus cito reminiscimur, quasi naturali quodam ordine ab uno ad aliud procedentes.*

“Custom is like nature; hence things we understand many times we quickly recollect, proceeding from one to another in a kind of natural order.”

**[Documented text]** Repeated traversal turns a deliberately constructed association into a habitual or quasi-natural sequence.

## 2. Aquinas’s fuller account of retrieval

The commentary on Aristotle supplies the mechanism which the compact *Summa* passage presupposes.

> *Causa autem reminiscendi est ordo motuum, qui relinquuntur in anima ex prima impressione eius, quod primo apprehendimus.*

“The cause of recollecting is the order of movements left in the soul from the first impression of what we originally apprehended.”

Aquinas distinguishes succession:

> *Unus motus natus est post alium nobis occurrere… vel ex necessitate vel ex consuetudine.*

“One movement is naturally apt to occur to us after another… either by necessity or by custom.”

Retrieval is described as a hunt:

> *Reminiscentia nil est aliud quam inquisitio alicuius quod a memoria excidit. Et ideo reminiscendo venamur…*

“Recollection is nothing other than the search for something which has fallen out of memory. Therefore in recollecting we hunt…”

One proceeds:

> *Ex aliquo priori, quod in memoria habetur…*

“From some prior thing which is retained in memory…”

The starting point can be a known time or thing. Aquinas gives actual examples:

- Starting from today, yesterday, and the third day, one traverses the sequence to recover what happened four days earlier.
- Starting from the eighth day, one may proceed toward the seventh and then the fourth.
- Socrates prompts Plato by similarity in wisdom.
- Hector prompts Achilles by contrariety.
- Father prompts son by proximity or relationship.
- A forgotten verse is best recovered by beginning at its opening.

Association thus operates through:

- similarity;
- contrariety;
- proximity;
- social relationship;
- place;
- time;
- habitual succession;
- necessary or logical succession.

Well-ordered material is most recollectable:

> *Illa sunt magis reminiscibilia, quaecumque sunt bene ordinata, sicut mathematica et theoremata mathematicorum, quorum secundum concluditur ex primo.*

“Those things are more recollectable which are well ordered, like mathematics and mathematical theorems, of which the second is concluded from the first.”

At the end of lectio 5 Aquinas derives four *documenta utilia*:

> *Primum est, ut studeat quae vult retinere in aliquem ordinem deducere. Secundo ut profunde et intente eis mentem apponat. Tertio ut frequenter meditetur secundum ordinem. Quarto ut incipiat reminisci a principio.*

“First, let one strive to reduce what one wishes to retain to some order. Second, let one apply the mind to it deeply and attentively. Third, let one meditate frequently according to that order. Fourth, let one begin recollecting from the beginning.”

**[Documented text]** These are not identical in wording or sequence to the four precepts of the *Summa*. Notably, the commentary’s concluding four instructions do not mention invented corporeal images. They concern order, concentration, repetition, and starting points.

**[Correction to catalogue language]** It is therefore misleading to speak of one fully codified “system” appearing identically in both works. The *Summa* combines artificial-memory language with ethical prudence; the commentary gives a more strictly Aristotelian account of associative search.

## 3. Places, images, fixing order, and retrieval

### What serves as places?

**[Documented text]** Aquinas acknowledges that recollection may proceed “from places,” but supplies no rules for constructing an architectural locus series.

**[Classical source]** The explicit place system belongs to the *Ad Herennium*. It defines places as clearly bounded natural or artificial backgrounds:

> *Locos appellamus eos, qui breviter, perfecte, insignite aut natura aut manu sunt absoluti… ut aedes, intercolumnium, angulum, fornicem.*

“We call places those backgrounds which are completed on a small scale, completely and distinctly, either by nature or by hand… such as a house, an intercolumnar space, a corner, an arch.”

Its place rules include:

- arrange loci in a fixed order;
- memorize the loci thoroughly;
- leave them stable while changing the images;
- mark every fifth place, for example with a golden hand;
- mark the tenth with someone named Decimus;
- prefer quiet or deserted surroundings;
- make adjacent places visually dissimilar;
- use places of moderate size;
- avoid excessive brightness or darkness;
- maintain a moderate interval between loci;
- traverse them forward or backward.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** A Thomist trained in the *Ad Herennium* could use these classical architectural places while applying Aquinas’s ethical and psychological rationale. Aquinas himself does not restate most of these rules.

### What serves as images?

**[Documented text]** “Suitable,” “not entirely customary” corporeal likenesses which bind abstract or spiritual *intentiones*.

**[Classical inheritance]** The *Ad Herennium* recommends active, striking images—*imagines agentes*—made memorable through extraordinary beauty or ugliness, crowns or purple, blood, mud, red paint, disfigurement, or comic action.

**[Yates’s interpretation]** Medieval personifications of virtues and vices are Christianized *imagines agentes*: sensible figures representing moral abstractions and moving the will toward salvation or away from damnation.

**[Qualification]** This is plausible as a cultural genealogy, not a demonstrated identity. Allegorical personification has many sources besides artificial memory: scriptural exegesis, Prudentius’s *Psychomachia*, liturgy, preaching, moral theology, encyclopedic classification, devotional practice, and workshop iconography.

### How is order fixed?

Aquinas permits or implies several forms:

1. logical or demonstrative order;
2. chronological order;
3. habitual sequence;
4. spatial succession;
5. associative chains of similitude, contrast, or proximity;
6. the articulated order of subject matter.

**[Yates’s interpretation]** In scholastic culture, “really the order of the argument” comes to replace or dominate the irregular architectural loci of classical rhetoric.

**[Correction]** Aquinas does not explicitly say that argumentative order replaces loci. Medieval practice employed many simultaneous ordering schemes: numbered divisions, alphabetic series, biblical chronology, trees, ladders, wheels, diagrams, architectural metaphors, body parts, and literal or imagined places.

### How does retrieval work?

1. Recall a known starting point.
2. Activate its associated mental movement.
3. Follow customary, logical, temporal, spatial, similar, contrary, or contiguous successors.
4. Continue until the missing content reappears.
5. Frequent rehearsal makes this passage rapid and quasi-natural.
6. A trained recollector may move through a sequence intentionally; an association may also arise accidentally.

This is better described as ordered associative search than as passive storage.

## 4. Worked examples and absent apparatus

### Examples Aquinas gives

Aquinas’s commentary gives examples of retrieval, not picturesque mnemonic encodings:

- today → yesterday → earlier days;
- Socrates → Plato;
- Hector → Achilles;
- father → son;
- opening of a verse → remaining verse;
- first theorem → succeeding theorem.

### Examples Aquinas does not give

**[Absence of evidence]** Neither principal Aquinas text contains:

- the *Ad Herennium* lawsuit image;
- a model set of rooms;
- a virtue placed inside a church;
- a mnemonic encoding of an article of the *Summa*;
- an alphabet of human figures;
- a wheel;
- a memory table;
- a memory theatre;
- a claimed feat of verbatim recall.

The famous lawsuit image belongs to *Ad Herennium* III.33–34. To remember that the defendant poisoned a man for an inheritance, with witnesses and accessories involved, the author imagines the sick man in bed, the accused beside him holding a cup in his right hand, tablets in his left, and a ram’s testicles on a finger. The cup signifies poisoning, the tablets the will or inheritance, and the testicles suggest witnesses through a Latin sound association.

**[Correction]** It is a classical comparison image used to explain artificial memory. It is not an Aquinas example.

### Diagrams, wheels, tables, and plates

**[Documented text]** The Aquinas passages have no authorial diagrams.

**[Absence of evidence]** No surviving wheel, table, architectural plan, or plate can securely be attributed to Aquinas as an apparatus for these four precepts.

**[Later reception]** Trees, wheels, visual alphabets, buildings, and tabular diagrams flourish in later mnemonic and scholastic works, but their mere compatibility with Aquinas’s prescriptions does not prove descent from a lost Thomist diagram.

### Manuscript and printing situation

The *Summa* survives in a vast manuscript tradition; no single manuscript is “the manuscript” of the four precepts. The passage belongs to the normal textual tradition of the *Secunda secundae*. A stemmatic investigation of *sollicitudo* would require consultation of the Leonine apparatus and relevant early witnesses.

Early print milestones for the *Summa* include:

- Mainz, Peter Schöffer, 1467: *Secunda secundae*, commonly identified as the first printed portion of the *Summa*;
- Rome, 1470s, and Venice, 1480s–90s: early editions and expanding complete sets;
- the Roman edition of 1570 associated with Pius V and the commentary of Cardinal Cajetan;
- the Leonine critical edition from 1882 onward.

**[Open bibliographical caution]** Copy-specific shelfmarks differ by holding institution. Claims about the earliest print should distinguish the separately printed *Secunda secundae* from the first complete multi-part printed *Summa*.

### Date, provenance, and contents

**[Older scholarship/Yates]** Yates treated the manuscript as an early-fourteenth-century witness whose virtue figures illustrated a “basic Thomist method.”

**[Later manuscript scholarship]** Research summarized in Andreas Büttner’s *Das Bild des Ordenslehrers und die Allegorie des Wissens* associates the codex’s titular poems with the Augustinian Hermits and places it within a more complex network of Italian poems and allegorical image cycles. Schlosser had already connected its *tituli* with Augustinian-eremitical institutional art.

**[Correction]** “Thomist” describes correspondences between the labeled parts of virtues and Aquinas’s classification. It does not establish Dominican authorship, Aquinas’s direction, or mnemonic use.

### Description of the relevant figures

As reproduced and interpreted by Yates:

- Prudence bears a circular device labeled with her eight component parts;
- Temperance is associated with a branching tree bearing subdivisions;
- Fortitude carries or is embodied through a fortified architectural structure;
- Justice presents her parts in or on a book.

**[Documented artifact]** These are ordered combinations of personification, attribute, inscription, and diagrammatic arrangement.

**[Yates’s interpretation]** They resemble the imagined practice of putting inscriptions onto striking corporeal figures in an ordered series.

**[Alternative explanation]** They are also fully explicable as illustrated scholastic classification: a visual synopsis of divisiones intended for reading, teaching, display, or meditation. No rubric calls them memory places or directs a reader to deposit and retrieve information through them.

### Variants and related visual traditions

Schlosser introduced the Vienna virtues and liberal arts into scholarship in 1896 as comparanda for Italian monumental programs. Later writers connected them to:

- the Chantilly mnemonic or didactic *canzone*;
- a Milanese/North Italian miniature associated with Nicolò di Giacomo;
- the Cortellieri Chapel fresco tradition;
- lost intermediaries hypothesized to explain differences.

**[Disputed reconstruction]** Proposed lost models are hypothetical. Similarity among image cycles establishes circulation of iconographic and textual formulas, not automatically a mnemonic genealogy.

## 6. The Chantilly poem

Yates cites:

- Léon Dorez, “La Canzone delle Virtù e delle Scienze,” 1894, concerning an illustrated didactic poem associated with the virtues and sciences.

**[Relevance]** It shows how scholastic classifications could be converted into personified, inscribed, memorable sequences.

**[Qualification]** A mnemonic function is possible but must not be inferred merely because a diagram or poem is orderly.

## 7. Andrea di Bonaiuto, Spanish Chapel

Work:

- Andrea di Bonaiuto, also called Andrea da Firenze;
- chapter house, later the Spanish Chapel, Dominican Santa Maria Novella, Florence;
- generally dated 1365–69.

The *Triumph of St Thomas Aquinas* presents:

- Thomas enthroned centrally;
- an open book emitting or displaying wisdom;
- theological and cardinal virtues above;
- prophets, evangelists, apostles, or authoritative teachers around him;
- defeated doctrinal opponents below;
- fourteen female personifications of sacred disciplines and liberal arts in an ordered lower register;
- a historical exemplar seated below each discipline.

The wider chapel includes the Church Militant and Triumphant, Dominican preaching and conversion, sacramental and ecclesial imagery, and the road to salvation.

[Harvard Villa I Tatti image record](https://cria.itatti.harvard.edu/item/09.04.00111)  
[Study of the fresco’s Averroes iconography](https://doi.org/10.13130/interfaces-06-02)

**[Documented artifact]** The program is Dominican, scholastic, hierarchical, didactic, and closely associated with the authority of Aquinas.

**[Probable context]** The program was devised with learned Dominican participation; Fra Jacopo Passavanti has often been proposed, though precise authorship is not conclusively documented.

**[Yates’s interpretation]** Its virtues and sciences are publicly visible counterparts of ordered mnemonic images.

**[Correction]** The fresco is not a diagram for operating Aquinas’s four precepts, and no documentary source says it was designed as a memory palace. It is evidence that Dominican scholastic knowledge could be spatialized and personified in monumental art.

**[Dating correction]** The fresco is not simply “mid-fourteenth century” in the loose sense sometimes given: the accepted working date is 1365–69. It therefore postdates Aquinas by about ninety years.

## Yates’s account and scholarship since

## 1. Chapter III: the scholastic transformation

Yates’s argument proceeds through Albert the Great and Aquinas:

1. Cicero’s three parts of prudence—memory, intelligence, foresight—gave memory an ethical foothold.
2. Albert and Aquinas reconciled artificial memory with Aristotle’s psychology.
3. Images became corporeal vehicles for intellectual or spiritual intentions.
4. Unusual images retained the classical power of the striking *imago agens*.
5. Order became rational and associative.
6. Aquinas’s *sollicitudo* introduced attention and affect.
7. Christian memory increasingly concerned virtues, vices, salvation, damnation, heaven, hell, and the articles of faith.
8. Medieval didactic imagery may therefore embody the operations of artificial memory.

Her central formulations include:

> “The places of the artificial memory are thus given a rational grounding in Aristotelian theory of reminiscence based on order and association.” (p. 72)

> “The abstract *Summa* might be corporealised in memory into something like a Gothic cathedral full of images on its ordered places.” (p. 79)

The second statement is explicitly speculative: Yates introduces it as an “extraordinary thought” and immediately warns against excessive supposition.

## 2. Chapter IV: imagery and the “basic Thomist method”

Yates traces Aquinas’s authority through Ragone, Publicius, Romberch, Garzoni, Gesualdo, Paepp, Schenkel, Gratarolo/Fulwood, and Feinaigle. She then examines medieval pictures and manuscripts as possible evidence of the method.

Her culminating reconstruction is:

> “The basic Thomist method may have been orders of images with inscriptions on them memorised in the order of the carefully articulated argument.” (p. 101)

> “So might the vast inner memory cathedrals of the Middle Ages have been built.” (p. 101)

The modal language—“may have been,” “might”—is essential.

### Yates’s evidentiary steps

- Aquinas authorizes images and order.
- The Vienna figures combine images, inscriptions, and scholastic subdivisions.
- The Spanish Chapel combines architecture, personification, doctrinal hierarchy, and Aquinas’s authority.
- Panofsky compared the articulation of Gothic architecture with scholastic method.
- Therefore an inward, imagistic counterpart to the scholastic *summa* is conceivable.

### What Yates does not prove

- that Aquinas himself used the Vienna images;
- that the manuscript descends from a Dominican memory manual;
- that the Spanish Chapel was designed as a mnemonic route;
- that a cathedral was Aquinas’s own set of loci;
- that each article of the *Summa* was assigned to a physical place;
- that medieval viewers were instructed to rehearse the fresco as an artificial-memory system.

## 3. Mary Carruthers

Carruthers’s *The Book of Memory* (1990; 2nd ed. 2008) confirms the centrality of trained memory but reframes it.

Her strongest correction appears in *The Craft of Thought*:

> “The goal of rhetorical mnemotechnical craft was not to give students a prodigious memory… but to give an orator the means and wherewithal to invent his material… *Memoria* is most usefully thought of as a compositional art.”

Carruthers argues that Yates overemphasized storage and exact repetition and underrepresented:

- invention;
- composition;
- meditation;
- the movement or *ductus* through a work;
- monastic practices earlier than the thirteenth century;
- commonplace structures, textual divisions, grids, marginal signs, and nonarchitectural places;
- memory as an ordinary craft rather than an exotic secret.

[Cambridge sample of *The Craft of Thought*](https://assets.cambridge.org/0521795419/sample/0521795419WS.pdf)

**[Confirmed]** Images, emotion, order, repeated mental movement, and ethics were fundamental.

**[Corrected]** Medieval memory was not merely a static storehouse, nor did it depend exclusively on the formal architectural art described in the *Ad Herennium*.

**[Corrected]** “Place” may be textual, rhetorical, numerical, bodily, schematic, or compositional—not only a room in a building.

## 4. Janet Coleman

Coleman’s *Ancient and Medieval Memories* reconstructs the philosophical history more broadly, examining how medieval writers transformed classical texts within changing social, educational, and institutional settings.

**[Correction of emphasis]** Aquinas’s account belongs to a theory of embodied cognition: the human soul acts through a body, memory belongs to sensitive power, and recollection uses ordered phantasms. This philosophical problem cannot be reduced to the techniques of a rhetoric manual.

[Book contents and publication record](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/ancient-and-medieval-memories/919B1544566017B2EAAFA13223E44BEC)

## 5. Paolo Rossi

Rossi’s *Clavis universalis*—translated as *Logic and the Art of Memory*—places the mnemonic tradition beside projects for encyclopedic order, combination, and universal language.

**[Contribution]** Rossi helps distinguish:

- classical local memory;
- scholastic classification;
- Lullian combinatorics;
- Renaissance encyclopedism;
- universal-character projects.

**[Correction to simple genealogy]** A line from Aquinas to Ramus, Lull, Bruno, and Leibniz is not a single continuous system. These traditions intersect, borrow, and compete, but their operations differ.

[Publication record](https://books.google.com/books/about/Logic_and_the_Art_of_Memory.html?id=qMSvAwAAQBAJ)

## 6. Hajdu and Heimann-Seelbach

Helga Hajdu’s *Das mnemotechnische Schrifttum des Mittelalters* (1936) was Yates’s principal predecessor for medieval mnemonic texts.

Sabine Heimann-Seelbach’s work greatly expanded the manuscript census, finding dozens of fifteenth-century mnemonic treatises and demonstrating that the period between Aquinas and the major printed manuals was not empty.

**[Correction]** Yates’s impression of a poorly documented medieval interval partly reflected the state of cataloguing. The textual field is larger, more heterogeneous, and less reducible to one Dominican scheme.

## 7. Bolzoni and later art-literature scholarship

Lina Bolzoni’s *The Gallery of Memory* and related studies strengthen the case that images, inscriptions, spatial arrangements, preaching, emblems, and literary composition interacted profoundly in late medieval and Renaissance culture.

**[Confirmed]** Visual and verbal ordering systems could function as instruments of meditation, persuasion, invention, and remembrance.

**[Qualification]** Such studies usually support a field of practices, not the literal existence of Yates’s imagined Thomist cathedral.

## 8. Panofsky

Erwin Panofsky’s *Gothic Architecture and Scholasticism* compared scholastic “manifestation” and articulation—parts, parts of parts, systematic division—with High Gothic architectural organization.

**[Yates’s synthesis]** Yates fused Panofsky’s analogy with Aquinas’s corporeal likenesses to imagine the *Summa* inwardly rebuilt as a cathedral.

**[Disputed]** Panofsky’s thesis concerns structural habits and historical “mental habit,” not an assertion that scholastics literally used cathedrals as mnemonic loci. Yates’s memory-cathedral is a further interpretive step.

## 9. Yates’s own later position

In later writing, including “The Art of Memory in the Middle Ages” and “Broken Images,” Yates continued to defend the relation between medieval didactic imagery and memory. She presented material images as instruments for teaching and retaining *intelligibilia* through *sensibilia*.

[“Broken Images,” 1974](https://www.nybooks.com/articles/1974/05/30/broken-images/)  
[Warburg-hosted essay](https://resources.warburg.sas.ac.uk/mnemosyne/fq/yates_memory.pdf)

She nevertheless consistently used conjectural language where direct documentation was absent.

## History and context

## 1. Classical rhetoric

Artificial memory was one of rhetoric’s five traditional departments:

1. invention;
2. arrangement;
3. style;
4. memory;
5. delivery.

The *Ad Herennium* divided memory into natural and artificial:

> *Sunt igitur duae memoriae: una naturalis, altera artificiosa.*

“There are therefore two memories, one natural and the other artificial.”

Artificial memory consists of:

> *Constat igitur artificiosa memoria locis et imaginibus.*

“Artificial memory consists of places and images.”

Aquinas inherits the claim that art and diligence can strengthen a natural faculty, but he relocates the discussion within prudence.

## 2. Scholastic psychology

The decisive Aristotelian framework is:

- all human cognition begins from sense;
- memory belongs to the sensitive soul;
- memory retains past apprehension under an aspect of pastness;
- phantasms mediate thought;
- recollection is an intentional search through ordered movements;
- repeated sequences become habitual;
- prudence applies universal understanding to particular action.

**[Documented doctrine]** This explains why corporeal likenesses can assist abstract cognition without turning the images into magical objects.

## 3. Ethics and prudence

Aquinas’s immediate purpose is not to write a mnemonic manual. It is to explain why *memoria* is an integral part of *prudentia*.

The practical syllogism requires access to experienced particulars. A badly formed or dishonest memory corrupts deliberation; a trained, faithful memory supports sound judgment.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** The moralization of artificial memory follows not simply because Christians wanted to memorize hell, but because remembering past particulars was constitutive of virtuous practical reasoning.

## 4. Dominican institutions

Albert the Great and Aquinas taught within the Dominican studium system, in which preaching, disputation, scriptural exposition, and theological teaching demanded substantial trained memory.

Relevant centers included:

- Paris;
- Cologne;
- Italian Dominican convents;
- Naples;
- Santa Maria Novella in Florence.

**[Documented institutional context]** Dominican education valued ordered division, disputation, preaching aids, and compilation.

**[Not documented]** No Dominican statute has been identified commanding friars to memorize the *Summa* by placing virtue figures in cathedral loci.

## 5. Franciscans and Lullism

Yates sometimes stages a contrast between Dominican Aristotelian memory and Franciscan-associated Lullism.

**[Correction]** Ramon Llull was a layman and later Franciscan tertiary, but Lullism cannot simply be equated with “the Franciscan memory art.” Llull’s alphabetic figures combine divine attributes, logical relations, questions, subjects, and combinatorial procedures. Their main purpose is demonstrative and missionary, not storage by loci and images.

Anthony Bonner and Alexander Fidora have emphasized the logical, epistemic, and argumentative functions of Llull’s Art. A Dominican-versus-Franciscan binary obscures cross-order transmission and later hybridization.

## 6. Universities, sermons, courts, and books

Memory practices served:

- university lectures and disputations;
- sermon composition and delivery;
- legal pleading;
- biblical study;
- private meditation;
- compilation and indexing;
- public moral instruction;
- examination and teaching.

Carruthers’s work makes clear that manuscript layout—rubrics, initials, tables, numbered distinctions, marginal signs, concordances—was part of memorial culture. The book did not simply replace memory; it trained and cued it.

## People

## Thomas Aquinas

**[Documented]** Author of the four-precept passage and the Aristotelian commentary.

**[Tradition]** Later mnemonic writers invoked his authority.

**[Legend]** His prodigious memory became part of saintly biography. Such reports support his reputation for learning but do not document a particular locus system.

## Albert the Great

Aquinas’s teacher and a major earlier commentator on memory and prudence. Albert more explicitly wrestled with metaphorical images and the compatibility of the *Ad Herennium* with Aristotle.

**[Yates’s reading]** Albert and Aquinas jointly effect the scholastic transformation, though Thomas is more cautious and ethically focused.

## “Tully”

For Aquinas and most medieval readers, “Tully” could cover both Cicero’s authentic *De inventione* and the *Ad Herennium*. The latter’s mnemonic doctrine was therefore received under Cicero’s prestige.

**[Modern correction]** The author of the *Ad Herennium* is unknown.

## Andrea di Bonaiuto and the Dominican programmers

Andrea painted the Spanish Chapel cycle. Learned friars almost certainly shaped its theology.

**[Tradition/reconstruction]** Fra Jacopo Passavanti is often suggested as intellectual adviser.

**[Open question]** The precise division of responsibility between painter, patron, convent, and theological adviser is not securely documented.

## Julius von Schlosser

Schlosser brought Vienna 2639 into modern art-historical discussion and considered its inscriptions in relation to the Augustinian Hermits.

**[Historiographical importance]** Yates used his publication, but her “basic Thomist method” is her own mnemonic reinterpretation rather than Schlosser’s demonstrated conclusion.

## Jacopo Ragone

Ragone’s 1434 *Artificialis memoriae regulae*, dedicated to Gianfrancesco Gonzaga, opens by saying that artificial memory is perfected through loci and images “as Cicero teaches and as St Thomas Aquinas confirms.”

Yates cited British Museum, Additional MS 10,438, f. 2v. More recent work also studies Victoria and Albert Museum, National Art Library MS KRP.A.22, ff. 1r–30v. [Recent study](https://sas-space.sas.ac.uk/6183/1/EnglThe%20Art%20of%20Memory%20as%20cultural%20transfer%20-%20odf.pdf)

**[Documented reception]** By the fifteenth century Thomas functioned as an authoritative guarantor of artificial memory.

## Jacobus Publicius

His *Oratoriae artis epitome* with an appended *Ars memorativa*, Venice 1482/1485, represents the passage of manuscript mnemonic instruction into print.

**[Documented reception]** Publicius cites or draws authority from Aquinas, though his illustrated techniques go well beyond the four precepts.

## Johannes Romberch

The Dominican Romberch’s *Congestorium artificiosae memoriae* appeared in Venice in 1520; Yates often cites the 1533 edition. It contains architectural loci, visual alphabets, diagrams, and encyclopedic classification.

[Library of Congress record](https://www.loc.gov/item/10019844/)

**[Reception]** Romberch is genuinely within a Dominican mnemonic tradition and cites Thomas.

**[Qualification]** His apparatus is a sixteenth-century synthesis, not a surviving diagram by Aquinas.

## Cosimo Rosselli

A Dominican author of the *Thesaurus artificiosae memoriae*, Venice, Antonio Padovano, 1579.

[Salamanca digital record](https://www.cervantesvirtual.com/obra/thesaurus-artificiosae-memoriae/)  
[Folger copy, BF383 .R7 1579 Cage](https://catalog.folger.edu/record/381992)

Rosselli systematizes relations between images and things through similarity, metonymy, contrast, names, hieroglyphs, and conventional symbolism.

## Garzoni, Gesualdo, Paepp, Schenkel, Gratarolo, Fulwood, Feinaigle

Yates’s reception chain includes:

- Tommaso Garzoni, *Piazza universale*, Venice, 1578, discourse LX;
- Filippo Gesualdo, *Plutosofia*, Padua, 1592;
- Lambert Schenkel, *Gazophylacium*, Strasbourg, 1610;
- Johannes Paepp, *Artificiosae memoriae fundamenta ex Aristotele, Cicerone, Thoma Aquinate…*, Lyon, 1619;
- Guglielmo Gratarolo, *De memoria reparanda*, 1553;
- William Fulwood, *The Castel of Memorie*, London, 1562;
- Gregor von Feinaigle, *The New Art of Memory*, London, 1812/1813.

Yates quotes the chain on pp. 82–83. [Relevant pages](https://resources.warburg.sas.ac.uk/mnemosyne/fq/yates_memory.pdf)

**[Documented reception]** Thomas’s name remained an authority.

**[Qualification]** Citation does not prove faithful transmission of his precise doctrine. Often “Aristotle, Cicero, and Thomas” functions as an authorization formula.

## Catalogue anecdotes

The requested auctioneer, professor’s party, student who lost his memory, and accused magician are not attached in the verified primary material to Aquinas’s four precepts.

**[Catalogue contamination]** These anecdotes belong to other figures and systems in the wider memory-art tradition—classical feats, Renaissance demonstrations, pedagogical legends, or accusations against occult mnemonists.

**[Absence of evidence]** No reliable source found in this pass connects Aquinas personally with those episodes. Assigning them to “system 10” would create a false history.

## The magic question

## 1. Were the four precepts magical?

No, in Aquinas’s own terms.

**[Documented text]** Their causes are natural and psychological:

- sensible likenesses hold attention;
- novelty produces wonder;
- intensity deepens impression;
- order supports associative passage;
- repetition makes a sequence habitual.

There are no:

- celestial correspondences;
- consecrations;
- invocations;
- talismanic materials;
- divine names;
- angelic intermediaries;
- claims of infused knowledge;
- claims that images exert occult force.

## 2. Aquinas on the *Ars notoria*

In *Summa theologiae* II–II, q. 96, a. 1, Aquinas asks whether practices intended to acquire knowledge through prohibited observances are lawful. He explicitly discusses the *ars notoria*.

His verdict is negative: intellectual knowledge is not naturally caused by inspecting figures, reciting unknown words, fasting in specially prescribed ways, or observing ritual forms. Where such ceremonies purport to deliver knowledge, he sees either futility or implicit demonic involvement.

[Discussion and translation context](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/defining-magic/thomas-aquinas/42D2E66BCAEB6609F52ED8CDC4AA7600)  
[English translation of q. 96](https://freddoso.com/summa-translation/Part%202-2/st2-2-ques96.pdf)

**[Documented distinction]**

| Licit mnemonic art | Condemned *ars notoria* |
|---|---|
| Works through natural cognition | Claims extraordinary or infused knowledge |
| Requires exercise and repetition | Promises acquisition without proportionate study |
| Uses intelligible likenesses | Uses unknown words, figures, ritual observances |
| Strengthens a natural faculty | Implies hidden spiritual agency |
| Serves prudence | Falls under superstition |

## 3. Yates’s position

Yates explicitly says Aquinas’s rules are “very carefully framed to exclude magic” and that no one following them in their intended spirit would turn them into magical art.

**[Confirmed]** This distinction is strongly supported by q. 96.

**[Disputed broader genealogy]** Yates speculated that the *Ars notoria* might descend from classical memory or shorthand *notae*. Modern scholarship treats that genealogy cautiously. Similar vocabulary and a common promise of knowledge do not demonstrate direct descent.

## 4. Heresy and consequences

**[Absence of evidence]** No evidence indicates that Aquinas was accused of heresy or magic for the four precepts.

**[Documented context]** The *Ars notoria* and related ritual texts could be condemned; John of Morigny’s visionary works were burned at Paris in 1323. Such events demonstrate the peril surrounding ritual acquisition of knowledge, not the condemnation of ordinary artificial memory.

**[Later confusion]** Modern occult writing sometimes presents all elaborate memory images as magical. That collapses Aquinas’s explicit distinction between natural cognitive art and superstitious ritual.

## Afterlife

## 1. Medieval preaching and devotional composition

The most defensible afterlife is not a single unchanged machine but a family of practices:

- converting doctrine into memorable figures;
- dividing sermons into ordered parts;
- using biblical and moral exempla;
- arranging virtues and vices spatially;
- rehearsing material affectively;
- retrieving a discourse from its divisions.

## 2. Dante

Yates calls Dante a supreme example of medieval memory imagery: Hell, Purgatory, and Paradise are rigorously ordered spaces populated by unforgettable moral images.

**[Yates’s interpretation]** The *Commedia* can be read as an immense memory system.

**[Supported analogy]** Dante’s poem is spatial, sequential, ethical, affective, and designed for recollectable visualization.

**[Not documented]** Dante never says he constructed the poem by applying Aquinas’s four precepts or by using a formal *Ad Herennium* palace.

## 3. Giotto, Lorenzetti, and fresco cycles

Virtue-and-vice cycles in the Arena Chapel, civic personifications in Siena, Last Judgments, and Dominican cycles share features recommended for memory:

- vivid personification;
- contrast;
- emotional force;
- regular placement;
- inscriptions;
- narrative and moral sequence.

**[Plausible cultural interaction]** Such works participated in a mnemonic pedagogy.

**[Disputed stronger thesis]** They should not automatically be decoded as literal external memory palaces.

## 4. Emblem books and imprese

The emblem’s combination of image, motto, and explanatory text resembles Aquinas’s binding of intellectual content to corporeal likeness.

**[Historical affinity]** Sixteenth-century theorists drew upon rhetoric, allegory, hieroglyphics, and memory.

**[Overstatement to avoid]** The impresa was not “defined exactly” by Aquinas; later authors could appropriate Thomist language to explain a more recent genre.

## 5. Ramism

Peter Ramus relocated memory toward dialectical method, division, and orderly disposition, rejecting or marginalizing some elaborate image practices.

Walter Ong interpreted Ramism as part of a transformation toward spatialized, typographic, dichotomous organization. Carruthers observes that Ramus’s relocation of memory into ordered dialectic repeats, in a different form, a move already made when Albert and Aquinas subordinated it to Aristotelian structures.

**[Correction to Yates]** Ramism did not simply eliminate imagination. Recent scholarship disputes the equation “Ramism + Protestantism = inner iconoclasm.”

## 6. Lull, Bruno, and combinatorics

Llull’s figures combine concepts through lettered mechanisms; Bruno later hybridized Lullian combination, classical loci, celestial images, and metaphysical speculation.

**[Historical connection]** Aquinas, Albert, Cicero, and Llull could all be cited by later mnemonic authors.

**[Structural distinction]**

- Aquinas: natural psychology and ethical prudence;
- *Ad Herennium*: ordered loci and striking images;
- Llull: combinatorial demonstration;
- Bruno: highly generative, metaphysical and sometimes magical image systems.

The line is braided rather than linear.

## 7. Leibniz and universal language

Rossi traces the convergence of mnemonic classification and combinatorial logic into early-modern projects for a universal character and a calculus of concepts.

**[Documented intellectual genealogy]** Leibniz knew Lullian and combinatorial traditions.

**[Qualification]** “Aquinas’s memory palace led to the computer” is a modern teleological slogan. Aquinas supplied no computational notation, algorithm, or machine design.

## 8. Borges and Eco

Borges’s Funes represents pathological total recall without abstraction or productive ordering—the inverse of Aquinas’s useful, selective, ordered memory.

Eco repeatedly engaged medieval semiotics, lists, encyclopedias, libraries, Lullian combination, and artificial languages.

**[Literary afterlife]** These are reflective modern appropriations, not continuations of an institutional Thomist mnemonic school.

## 9. Modern memory palaces and athletes

Modern competitors use loci, striking imagery, person-action-object systems, and rapid traversal.

**[Genuine continuity]** The principles of novelty, vivid association, fixed order, and rehearsal closely resemble ancient and Thomist prescriptions.

**[Modern invention]** Standardized decks, timed disciplines, PAO encodings, and neuroscience-based training programs are modern developments. Calling them “Aquinas’s system” would be branding rather than history.

## Controversies and disputes

## 1. Is this a complete “system”?

**Verdict: disputed terminology.**

Aquinas gives principles for strengthening memory and a theory of recollection. He does not provide the operational detail normally expected of a full mnemonic system.

The phrase “Thomas Aquinas’s four precepts” is accurate. “The Thomist memory palace” is a reconstruction.

## 2. Did Aquinas invent the four rules?

**Verdict: no, not ex nihilo.**

- unusual images and natural/artful memory come from the *Ad Herennium*;
- order and associative pursuit come from Aristotle;
- memory as part of prudence comes through Cicero’s *De inventione* and related ethical classifications;
- Aquinas’s distinctive contribution is their compact integration into prudence and scholastic psychology.

## 3. Did images come before places?

**Verdict: only partly supportable.**

Aquinas’s *Summa* paragraph mentions likenesses first and ordered arrangement second. That order differs from the *Ad Herennium*’s standard formulation “places and images.”

It does not prove that Aquinas systematically reversed the classical technique. His commentary also recognizes recollection “from places.”

## 4. Did argumentative order replace classical loci?

**Verdict: Yatesian reconstruction.**

Aquinas says to arrange material so that one remembered thing leads to another and praises mathematical sequence. He never commands the abandonment of architectural loci.

## 5. Is *sollicitudo* a personal misreading?

**Verdict: textual fact, causal explanation uncertain.**

The divergence from *solitudo* is real. Whether it was:

- Aquinas’s visual error;
- a lapse of memory;
- an inherited manuscript variant;
- a deliberate ethical adaptation;
- or an already circulating quotation

remains unresolved without fuller manuscript evidence.

## 6. Are “spiritual intentions” necessarily Christian doctrines?

**Verdict: overreading if made exclusive.**

The phrase permits spiritual or abstract content, but *intentio* has a technical psychological meaning. Yates’s salvation-centered interpretation is possible within the *Summa*’s theological setting, not forced by the vocabulary alone.

## 7. Did Aquinas memorize the *Summa* as a cathedral?

**Verdict: no evidence.**

This is Yates’s memorable thought experiment, created by combining Aquinas with Panofsky. It is neither a medieval report nor an inference securely grounded in material evidence.

## 8. Is Vienna 2639 a Thomist memory manuscript?

**Verdict: doubtful as a strict identification.**

The manuscript contains personified and diagrammatically organized virtues whose subdivisions overlap scholastic, including Thomist, categories. But:

- no mnemonic instructions accompany them;
- its institutional associations appear Augustinian rather than Dominican;
- later art historians place it within a broader Italian image-and-*titulus* tradition;
- its images may be pedagogical, devotional, encyclopedic, or commemorative without being loci in an artificial-memory operation.

## 9. Is the Spanish Chapel a memory palace?

**Verdict: plausible analogy, unproved function.**

It is ordered, spatial, allegorical, Dominican, affective, and didactic. That makes mnemonic reception likely in the broad sense. No contract, program, rubric, or contemporary report identifies it as a formal locus system.

## 10. Did Aquinas “revive” the art in 1323?

**Verdict: false modern summary.**

A modern medical-history abstract claims the art was revived in 1323 by Aquinas. Aquinas died in 1274; 1323 was the year of his canonization. Memory practice had not simply vanished after Rome’s sack in 410. [The problematic abstract](https://doi.org/10.1212/wnl.40.2.346)

This is a clear example of legend generated by compressed secondary narration.

## 11. Was Thomas the “patron saint” of memory?

**Verdict: Yates’s metaphor grounded in reception.**

Later mnemonic authors repeatedly cite him; that justifies the metaphor. There was no formal ecclesiastical declaration appointing him patron saint of artificial memory.

## 12. Was medieval memory static?

**Verdict: substantially overturned.**

Carruthers has shown memory as a craft of invention, composition, meditation, and recombination. Aquinas’s own hunting metaphor and flexible associative routes support the dynamic reading.

## 13. Was the medieval system wholly derived from the *Ad Herennium*?

**Verdict: overturned as too narrow.**

The *Ad Herennium* was crucial, but medieval memorial culture also drew on:

- Aristotle;
- Augustine;
- monastic meditation;
- biblical exegesis;
- liturgy;
- computus;
- florilegia;
- diagrams;
- manuscript layout;
- preaching and legal practice;
- encyclopedic and pedagogical traditions.

## 14. Was the system magical or proto-Hermetic?

**Verdict: no for Aquinas.**

Aquinas’s naturalized memory art and the *Ars notoria* are expressly opposed. Later authors could hybridize mnemonic practices with astrology, talismans, Cabala, Hermeticism, and ritual magic, but that does not retroactively make the four precepts occult.

## Open questions

1. Which *Ad Herennium* textual form did Aquinas consult, and does any pre-Aquinas witness read *sollicitudo*?
2. Did Aquinas quote the sentence directly, through a florilegium, through Albert, or from memory?
3. How consistently do early manuscripts of *Summa* II–II q. 49 transmit *sollicitudo*?
4. Can documentary evidence identify the intellectual programmer of the Spanish Chapel?
5. What precise textual sources underlie each inscription in Vienna 2639?
6. What is the exact codicological date and place of production of the Vienna codex’s illustrated appendix?
7. Are the virtue images contemporary with the main text or a distinct campaign of compilation and illumination?
8. Did any medieval reader annotate the Vienna figures with explicit mnemonic directions?
9. How do the Vienna images relate stemmatically to the Chantilly poem, Milanese miniatures, and lost monumental cycles?
10. How often do fourteenth- and fifteenth-century memory treatises quote Aquinas directly rather than repeat an authorization formula?
11. Do Dominican constitutions, sermon manuals, or *studia* records prescribe architectural loci?
12. How much of the “ethical transformation” belongs specifically to scholasticism, and how much continues ancient rhetoric’s already moral and affective functions?
13. Can reception studies distinguish images intended to be memorable from images intended to operate as a formal artificial memory?
14. How should *intentiones simplices et spirituales* be translated without erasing its technical faculty-psychological sense?
15. Is Yates’s cathedral best retained as a historical hypothesis, a phenomenological model of scholastic culture, or merely a powerful metaphor?

## Overall assessment

The securely reconstructable Thomist practice is compact but consequential:

1. Translate difficult-to-retain content into suitable, unusual corporeal likenesses.
2. arrange the content in a recoverable order;
3. impress it through concentrated attention and affect;
4. rehearse it until traversal becomes habitual;
5. when something is lost, begin from a known point and hunt through relations of sequence, similarity, contrast, time, place, or proximity.

Aquinas did not leave a memory cathedral. He left a philosophical and ethical warrant for making thought memorable through the body, imagination, order, emotion, and habit.

Yates’s lasting achievement was to recognize that this warrant mattered for medieval culture and later memory arts. Her overreach was to turn correspondences among psychology, scholastic divisions, manuscripts, frescoes, and Gothic architecture into the outline of a particular “basic Thomist method” for which no operating manual survives. Later scholarship has replaced that singular imagined machine with a richer ecology of memory practices: textual, oral, architectural, affective, diagrammatic, devotional, rhetorical, and compositional.
