# Lullism as memory and the Renaissance Lull: Research Dossier

## Basic identification

**Name:** Lullism as artificial memory; the Renaissance “Lull”  
**Catalogue position:** System 25 of 45  
**Historical exponent:** Ramon Llull (Raimundus Lullus, c. 1232–1315/16)  
**Principal transmitters and transformers:** Thomas Le Myésier; Nicholas of Cusa; Giovanni Pico della Mirandola; Pietro Mainardi; Bernard de Lavinheta; Remigius Rufus; Giordano Bruno; Heinrich Cornelius Agrippa; Johann Heinrich Alsted; Ivo Salzinger  
**Date:** Llull’s genuine Art, c. 1274–1308; *Arbor scientiae*, 1295–96; Pseudo-Llullian *Liber ad memoriam confirmandam*, probably late fifteenth or early sixteenth century, falsely dated “Pisa, c. 1308”; Lavinheta’s *Explanatio*, 1523; Mainz *Opera*, 1721–42  
**Principal texts:** *Ars compendiosa inveniendi veritatem*; *Ars demonstrativa*; *Ars generalis ultima*; *Ars brevis*; *Arbor scientiae*, especially the “Tree of Man”; *Liber de memoria*; Pseudo-Llull, *Liber ad memoriam confirmandam*; Lavinheta, *Explanatio compendiosaque applicatio artis Raymundi Lulli*; Mainardi, *De auditu kabbalistico*; Salzinger, *Revelatio secretorum Artis*  
**Principle in one sentence:** Genuine Lullian “memory” is principally the internalized command of a finite alphabet of metaphysical principles, definitions, questions, figures, and combinatorial procedures through which the practitioner can reconstruct and test arguments; the Renaissance converted this logical-theological Art into an increasingly Cabalistic, alchemical, encyclopedic, and sometimes magical “Lull.”

**Yates:** *The Art of Memory* (1966), chapter VIII, “Lullism as an Art of Memory,” especially pp. 173–198; the catalogue brief’s pp. 177, 183–86, 188–98 cover the decisive passages. Her discussion continues directly into chapter IX on Bruno.

### Evidentiary key

- **[T] Documented text:** stated in a primary source.
- **[M] Manuscript or material evidence:** established by manuscripts, printings, ownership, or surviving diagrams.
- **[R] Scholarly reconstruction:** a modern inference supported by textual or historical evidence.
- **[Y] Yates’s interpretation:** Yates’s synthesis, whether still tenable or not.
- **[D] Disputed:** a live or historically important scholarly controversy.
- **[Trad.] Tradition/reception:** what later readers believed, independent of historical truth.
- **[L] Legend:** a story not adequately documented.
- **[MI] Modern invention or retrospective analogy:** a recent extrapolation, often useful but not evidence of medieval intention.

The most important correction should be stated immediately:

> **[R, now prevailing] The *Liber ad memoriam confirmandam* is not accepted as a genuine work of Ramon Llull.**

Yates called it “a perfectly authentic and genuine work by Lull” and dated it to Pisa about 1308. Alois Madre and Charles Lohr’s 1996 examination and edition of all five surviving witnesses classified it as Pseudo-Llull and situated it in the Lullist tradition around the turn of the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries. No known manuscript predates the sixteenth century. The modern Universitat de Barcelona Llullian tradition and subsequent scholarship accordingly treat it as apocryphal. This overturns the strongest piece of evidence for a distinct memory manual written by Llull himself, though it does not erase genuine Llullian discussions of *memoria* or the authentic Art’s mnemonic utility. [Madre–Lohr edition and study](https://ibdigital.uib.es/greenstone/sites/localsite/collect/studiaLulliana/index/assoc/Studia_L/ulliana_/1996v036/p099-1.dir/Studia_Lulliana_1996v036p099.pdf)

## The system in detail

## 1. What exactly is “Lullism as memory”?

It is necessary to separate four historically different things.

### 1.1 The authentic Art of Ramon Llull

**[T]** Llull’s Art is a general method of finding and demonstrating truth, not primarily a device for retaining speeches, names, or lists. Its basic elements are:

1. a finite alphabet, normally B–K in the final Art;
2. tables assigning several meanings to each letter;
3. absolute principles or divine dignities;
4. relative principles;
5. subjects or levels of being;
6. virtues and vices;
7. definitions;
8. questions or rules;
9. geometrical figures;
10. systematic combinations called “compartments”;
11. procedures of ascent, descent, mixture, and demonstration.

The final Art is represented chiefly by the *Ars generalis ultima* of 1305–08 and its short handbook, the *Ars brevis* of 1308. The mature Art is not a random-word generator. It is an ontological and argumentative discipline: permitted combinations must be interpreted through definitions, the hierarchy of subjects, and Llull’s theological axioms. Modern scholarship, especially Anthony Bonner, emphasizes that its diagrams represent structured relations rather than mechanically producing conclusions on their own. [Bonner, *The Art and Logic of Ramon Llull*](https://uberty.org/wp-content/uploads/2015/12/Anthony_Bonner_The_art_and_logic_of_Ramon_Llull.pdf); [Stanford Encyclopedia, “Ramon Llull”](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/llull/)

### 1.2 Genuine Llullian memoria

**[T]** Memory is one of the three Augustinian powers of the rational soul:

- *memoria*;
- *intellectus*;
- *voluntas*.

In the *Liber contemplationis*, Llull describes them as three noble maidens:

> “The first remembers that which the second understands and the third wills; the second understands that which the first remembers and the third wills; the third wills that which the first remembers and the second understands.”

Yates translated this from the Mainz *Opera*, X, p. 530. The point is reciprocal operation, not three independent storage compartments.

**[T/R]** Because the three powers are mutually involved, memory in genuine Lullian texts retains what intellect understands and will desires. Practically, learning the Art means learning its alphabet, vocabulary, definitions, figures, and argumentative procedures until they can be reproduced and applied without the book.

**[Y]** Yates therefore formulated:

> “The Lullian Art as memory consists in memorising the Art as a whole, in all its aspects and operations.”

This is a strong interpretive summary rather than a sentence written by Llull. It remains broadly useful if “as memory” is not inflated into the claim that Llull designed a separate mnemonic system equivalent to the *Ad Herennium*.

### 1.3 Lullism used as an artificial memory

**[T]** In the “Tree of Man” of the *Arbor scientiae*, Llull says that his treatment of memory could be used in an *ars memorativa*:

> “Et iste tractatus memoriae, quem hic damus, posset poni in arte memorativa, quae posset fieri secundum ea quae hic dicuntur.”

Gloss: “And this treatment of memory which we give here could be put into an art of memory, which could be made according to what is said here.”

Yates cites the Catalan *Arbre de ciència*, *Obres essencials*, I, p. 619. The critical Latin edition is *Raimundi Lulli Opera Latina* (ROL), vols. XXIV–XXVI, no. 65, ed. Pere Villalba Varneda, *Corpus Christianorum, Continuatio Mediaevalis* 180A–C (Turnhout: Brepols, 2000). [UB lexical and edition record](https://nggl.ub.edu/obres/iii23-arbsci)

**[T]** Llull also composed three tree-shaped works on memory, intellect, and will. The *Liber de memoria* ends, in Yates’s transcription:

> “Locuti sumus de memoria, dando doctrinam de memoria artificiali, ut artificialiter possit attingere sua obiecta.”

Gloss: “We have spoken of memory, giving a doctrine of artificial memory so that it may attain its objects artificially.”

Yates consulted Paris, BnF, MS lat. 16116. Her “unpublished” description was accurate in 1966; a modern dossier should not infer from that alone that the work remains unedited without checking the current ROL and Llull Database records.

### 1.4 The Renaissance composite “Lull”

**[Trad.]** From the fifteenth century onward, authentic works became mixed with:

- pseudo-Lullian memory treatises;
- pseudo-Lullian alchemy;
- Christian-Cabalist texts;
- pseudo-Lullian rhetoric;
- Renaissance commentaries;
- Bruno’s Lullian and mnemonic adaptations.

The resulting “Lull” was historically powerful but textually composite. It is this received author—not always the historical Mallorcan Llull—who became a Cabalist, alchemist, universal encyclopedist, secret master, and proto-magician.

## 2. What serves as places, and what as images?

### 2.1 Places

**[T/R]** Authentic Lullian texts do not provide an architectural route of classical mnemonic places. Possible functional equivalents are:

- the fixed letter sequence B–K;
- the positions on circular figures;
- the cells of the alphabet table;
- the 36 binary compartments of the Third Figure;
- the ternary arrangements of the Fourth Figure;
- branches, roots, trunks, boughs, leaves, flowers, and fruits in the *Arbor scientiae*;
- the hierarchy of nine subjects;
- the sequence of ten questions or rules.

**[Y]** Yates cautiously says that Llull “seems to have thought of the diagrams of the Art as in some sense ‘places.’” She calls the trees “a kind of place system.” That is analogy, not an explicit Llullian identification of a geometrical chamber with an *Ad Herennium* locus.

**[R]** Bonner’s relational interpretation is more exact: the figures externalize possible relations among terms. They can aid recollection because positions and connections are fixed, but their primary logical function should not be reduced to storage.

### 2.2 Images

**[T]** Genuine Llullian Art contains abundant visualization but almost no system of emotionally striking human mnemonic agents comparable to the *Ad Herennium*. Its normal signs are:

- letters;
- circles;
- triangles;
- squares;
- relational lines;
- ladders;
- trees;
- tables.

**[Y, substantially confirmed]** Yates’s distinction between “visual memory” and the classical *imagines agentes* is sound. Llull’s diagrams are visible; they are not ordinarily grotesque, violent, erotic, or theatrically animated mnemonic images.

**Qualification:** Llull’s literary works teem with allegories, personifications, exemplary narratives, and imagined dialogues. It would therefore be too strong to call Llull’s thought “imageless.” The narrower claim is that the technical Art does not prescribe a classical inventory of striking images attached to architectural places.

## 3. The mature alphabet and table

In the *Ars brevis*, the alphabet B–K carries multiple correlated series. Spellings vary between manuscripts, editions, and translations, but the usual mature table is:

| Letter | Absolute principle | Relative principle | Question/rule | Subject | Virtue | Vice |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| B | goodness | difference | whether? | God | justice | avarice |
| C | greatness | concordance | what? | angel | prudence | gluttony |
| D | duration/eternity | contrariety | of what? | heaven | fortitude | lust |
| E | power | beginning | why? | man | temperance | pride |
| F | wisdom | middle | how much? | imagination | faith | sloth |
| G | will | end | what kind? | sensitive being | hope | envy |
| H | virtue | majority | when? | vegetative being | charity | anger |
| I | truth | equality | where? | element | patience | lying |
| K | glory | minority | how/with what? | instrument | piety | inconstancy |

**[T]** The same letter therefore changes semantic value according to the column in which it is read. B can mean goodness, difference, *utrum*, God, justice, or avarice. This is controlled polysemy, not secret substitution.

**Retrieval:** A trained practitioner begins with a question and subject, then consults mentally or materially the corresponding letter meanings, definitions, and allowed combinations. The alphabet is an address system. The figures expose relations, but interpretation supplies the argument.

## 4. The diagrams and wheels

## 4.1 Figure A

**[T/M]** The mature Figure A is a circle with nine letters B–K distributed around its circumference. The letters stand for the nine absolute principles:

> *bonitas, magnitudo, duratio, potestas, sapientia, voluntas, virtus, veritas, gloria.*

Lines connect the letters so that every principle can be considered with every other. The completed graph represents 36 unordered binary pairings.

**Function:** It asserts the mutual convertibility, concordance, and activity of the dignities, especially in God, and provides relational combinations for arguments.

## 4.2 Figure T

**[T/M]** Figure T is arranged in three ternary groupings, conventionally distinguished by three triangles:

- difference, concordance, contrariety;
- beginning, middle, end;
- majority, equality, minority.

The figure organizes relative principles. Some editions use colors to distinguish the triangles, but color is not equally preserved in all manuscript and printed transmission.

## 4.3 The Third Figure

**[T/M]** The Third Figure displays all 36 unordered pairings of the nine letters:

> BC, BD, BE … IK.

Each pairing forms a “compartment.” Since each letter may refer to several rows of the alphabet, a single compartment opens multiple propositions, but only subject to the definitions and rules.

Modern graph theory can represent this as the edges of the complete graph on nine vertices. **[MI]** That is a mathematically illuminating translation, not Llull’s terminology or self-understanding.

## 4.4 The Fourth Figure

**[T/M]** The Fourth Figure consists of three concentric circles. The outer circle is fixed; the inner circles can rotate. Each bears the letter series, allowing ternary combinations.

In printed exemplars it may survive as:

- three superimposed paper disks;
- a printed diagram intended to be cut out;
- a flat representation of the three circles;
- a volvelle fastened by a thread or pin;
- a damaged diagram whose central disks have been lost.

The sequence and admissibility of combinations are not arbitrary. The mature Art’s tabulation eliminates repetition and cyclic equivalence, yielding 252 ternary arrangements under its conventions.

**[Y]** Yates made movement psychologically and historically central: revolving wheels “introduce movement into memory.”  
**[R]** Later formal analysis regards movement as a device for systematic relational enumeration. Its mnemonic effect is real but secondary to its logical work.

## 4.5 The alphabet table

**[T/M]** The table fixes the multiple meanings of B–K in parallel columns. It is arguably a stronger mnemonic instrument than the physical volvelle, because the user must know which semantic register is active.

## 4.6 Trees

**[T/M]** The *Arbor scientiae* organizes knowledge into sixteen trees. Fourteen map the hierarchy of created and divine reality; the final two gather examples and questions.

The major trees include:

- elemental;
- vegetal;
- sensual;
- imaginal;
- human;
- moral;
- imperial;
- apostolic;
- celestial;
- angelical;
- eviternal;
- maternal, concerning Mary;
- Christological, divine-human;
- divine.

Each tree develops through a repeated morphology: roots, trunk, branches, twigs, leaves, flowers, and fruits. The roots regularly draw upon the principles and relations of the Art. This repeated anatomy fixes order across different sciences.

**[Y]** Yates reproduces a tree from the Lyon 1515 edition as figure 7 and describes the entire encyclopedia as a forest whose roots are the principles B–K.

**[R]** The tree is simultaneously:

- a division diagram;
- an ontological model;
- an encyclopedic index;
- an aid to exposition and recall.

Calling it a “memory palace” is a modern metaphor and should not be attributed to Llull.

## 5. How order is fixed

Order is secured by redundancy among several structures:

1. alphabetical order, B–K;
2. serial order of the principles;
3. geometric position on the figures;
4. binary order in 36 compartments;
5. ternary order in the Fourth Figure;
6. definitional order;
7. the ladder or hierarchy of subjects;
8. the ten questions;
9. the repeated anatomy of each tree;
10. ascent from particulars to universals and descent from universals to particulars.

The system therefore does not depend on remembering one arbitrary association. A forgotten relation can often be reconstructed from another structure.

**[R]** This is where Lullism differs most sharply from the classical art. A classical mnemonic normally retrieves something previously deposited. A trained Lullist attempts to regenerate the relevant argumentative possibilities from a finite grammar.

## 6. How retrieval and investigation work

A simplified reconstruction of mature practice is:

1. Formulate a question using one of the rules: whether, what, of what, why, how much, what kind, when, where, how/with what.
2. Identify the relevant subject: God, angel, heaven, man, imagination, sensitive nature, vegetative nature, elements, or instruments.
3. Select the absolute and relative principles involved.
4. Locate their letter pairing in the Third Figure.
5. Apply the definitions.
6. Test whether the principles concord or conflict in the given subject.
7. If needed, generate a ternary combination with the Fourth Figure.
8. ascend from a particular case to universal principles or descend from principles to the case;
9. articulate the resulting middle term or argument;
10. test the answer against the Art’s axioms and its theological hierarchy.

Llull insists that the mechanism cannot replace reasoning. In the *Ars generalis ultima*, section XIII on teaching, he directs the teacher to explain through reasons rather than authority and adds:

> “Ars habet tres amicos, videlicet subtilitatem intellectus, rationandi habilitatem et bonam intentionem; sine quibus nullus potest ipsam addiscere.”

Gloss: “The Art has three friends: intellectual subtlety, skill in reasoning, and good intention; without these no one can learn it.”

The English wording in modern summaries derives from Yanis Dambergs’s translation; the section is conveniently summarized in the [Stanford Encyclopedia account](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/llull/).

## 7. Rules for places and images

### Genuine Llull

There is no authenticated Llullian list comparable to the *Ad Herennium*’s rules that places should be moderate in size, well lit, distinct, and regularly spaced, or that images should be exceptional and emotionally striking.

Instead, the practical rules are:

- learn the alphabet and definitions;
- preserve the serial order;
- know the figures;
- know the subjects and rules;
- practice combinations;
- reason rather than appeal mechanically to authority;
- repeatedly apply the Art;
- maintain proper intellectual and moral intention.

### Lavinheta

**[T]** Lavinheta explicitly bifurcates memory:

- *sensibilia* may be retained through classical places and sensible images;
- *intelligibilia*, especially speculative matters remote from sense and imagination, require the *Ars generalis*.

His crucial passage, as cited by Yates from Alsted’s 1612 edition, pp. 653–56, says that for intelligibles one needs the Art of the Doctor Illuminatus, who:

> “omnia in locis suis colligit, multa sub paucis comprehendens.”

Gloss: “collects all things in their places, comprehending many things under a few.”

Here *loci* are ordered conceptual positions, not necessarily rooms in a building.

**[Y/D]** Yates calls Lavinheta’s terminology a “curious misuse” because scholastic image theory used sensible forms precisely to remember intelligibles. That criticism assumes the Thomist framework Lavinheta is deliberately ranking below Lullism. It may be better read as a polemical redefinition rather than simple misuse.

## 8. Worked examples

### 8.1 What the relevant sources do not contain

The following famous examples do **not** belong to the Lullian source complex:

- the lawsuit image from *Rhetorica ad Herennium* III.20/33;
- Camillo’s seven grades and their mythological contents;
- Bruno’s 150 astral images;
- the auctioneer anecdote;
- the professor’s party;
- the student who lost his memory.

They belong to other systems or later anecdotal traditions. Their absence here is a substantive finding. Importing them would disguise the very distinction that makes Lullism important.

### 8.2 A genuine Lullian type of example

Llull commonly asks questions such as whether God’s goodness is great, whether divine attributes agree, or how one dignity acts through another. The letters are expanded into definitions; the conclusion follows from the necessary co-essentiality of the dignities in God.

This is not “retrieve an image of God’s goodness.” It is “retrieve the definitions and relational procedure through which goodness and greatness are tested in the subject God.”

### 8.3 The Pseudo-Llullian ox

The *Liber ad memoriam confirmandam* gives the dossier’s clearest practical mnemonic example:

> “Bos enim cum maxima velocitate recipit herbas et sine masticatione ad stomachum remittit; quas postmodum remugit … Ita est de studente … debet in ore mentis masticare, ut in memoria radicetur et habituetur.”

Gloss: “The ox takes in grass very quickly and sends it to the stomach without chewing; afterward it brings it up again … So it is with the student … he must chew it in the mouth of the mind so that it may take root and become habitual in memory.”

The author advises reviewing Monday’s lesson on Tuesday while studying Tuesday’s, then reviewing both on Wednesday. The promise is extravagant: a person who follows the method for one year will become more learned than one who merely attends lectures for six.

This metaphor derives not uniquely from Llull but from Alan of Lille’s *Doctrinale minus*:

> “Sic documenta tui si vis retinere magistri, / saepe recorderis quod semel aure capis.”

Gloss: “If you wish to retain your teacher’s lessons, recall often what you once receive by ear.”

## The sources

## 9. Authentic primary texts

### 9.1 *Llibre de contemplació en Déu / Liber contemplationis in Deum*

- Catalan composition, c. 1273–74; Latin transmission.
- Early locus for systematic combinatory and diagrammatic thought.
- Yates used the Mainz *Opera*, vol. X, p. 530, for the allegory of memory, intellect, and will.
- Standard Catalan editions appear in *Obres de Ramon Llull* and *Obres essencials*.

### 9.2 *Ars compendiosa inveniendi veritatem*

- Usually dated c. 1274.
- First major formulation of the Art.
- Quaternary phase: more figures, sixteen-element structures, elemental correspondences.
- Surviving diagrams include Figure A and the psychologically important Figure S.

### 9.3 *Ars demonstrativa*

- c. 1283.
- Quaternary Art with an elaborate ensemble of figures.
- Contains elemental, theological, psychological, and relational structures.
- First printed in the Mainz edition; the work had largely receded from the main Renaissance printed tradition before Salzinger.

### 9.4 *Tabula generalis*

- 1293–94.
- Important transitional work toward the simplified ternary Art.

### 9.5 *Arbor scientiae / Arbre de ciència*

- Written in Rome, 1295–96.
- Catalan and Latin transmission.
- Standard Latin critical edition: Pere Villalba Varneda, ed., *Raimundi Lulli Opera Latina*, XXIV–XXVI, CCCM 180A–C (Turnhout: Brepols, 2000).
- Early Latin printings include Barcelona editions of 1482 and 1505 and the influential Lyon 1515 edition.
- The UB Llull Database records a Barcelona 1505 edition printed by Pere Posa, 182 folios. [Llull Database entry](https://www.ub.edu/llulldb/bibliografia/873)
- Later editions include Lyon 1635. [Llull Database entry](https://www.ub.edu/llulldb/bibliografia/2549)

### 9.6 *Ars generalis ultima*

- Begun 1305; completed 1308.
- Standard critical edition: *ROL* XIV, CCCM 75, ed. Alois Madre (Turnhout: Brepols, 1986).
- The mature systematic exposition.

### 9.7 *Ars brevis*

- Pisa, January 1308.
- Condensed manual of the final Art.
- Standard critical edition: *ROL* XII, CCCM 38, ed. Alois Madre (Turnhout: Brepols, 1984), pp. 171–255.
- Modern translation in Anthony Bonner, *Doctor Illuminatus: A Ramon Llull Reader*.
- Its figures became the most portable and most frequently reproduced form of Lullism.

### 9.8 *Liber de memoria*

- Yates’s manuscript: Paris, BnF, MS lat. 16116.
- Part of the trilogy *De memoria*, *De intellectu*, *De voluntate*.
- Any assertion about its present editorial status should now be checked against the live [Ramon Llull Database](https://www.ub.edu/llulldb/).

## 10. The *Liber ad memoriam confirmandam*

### 10.1 Attribution and date

**[Trad./Y]** Title and colophon attribute the work to Llull and place it at Pisa in a monastery of “Sancti Dominici.” Rossi and Yates accepted a 1307–08 date. Yates emended the monastery to San Donnino because authentic Pisan colophons locate Llull there.

**[R, prevailing]** Madre and Lohr reject the attribution. Their reasons include:

- no witness earlier than the sixteenth century;
- language and doctrine unlike authentic Llull;
- dependence on non-Llullian university study culture;
- explicit citations of authorities atypical of Llull;
- its accompanying *Liber de septem planetis* is itself Pseudo-Llullian;
- its close fit with early-modern Lullist synthesis.

The false Pisan colophon was an attribution strategy. Yates’s emendation from *Dominici* to *Donnini* repaired the forged setting instead of questioning the attribution.

### 10.2 Manuscripts

Madre–Lohr identify five witnesses:

1. **A:** Milan, Biblioteca Ambrosiana, MS I 153 inf., fols. 35r–39r, sixteenth/seventeenth century.
2. **M1:** Munich, Bayerische Staatsbibliothek, Clm 10593, fols. 218r–221r, seventeenth century.
3. **M2:** the same codex, fols. 1r–3v, seventeenth century.
4. **R:** Paris, Bibliothèque nationale de France, MS lat. 17829, fols. 437r–444r, seventeenth century.
5. **V:** Vatican City, Biblioteca Apostolica Vaticana, Vat. lat. 5347, fols. 68r–74r, sixteenth century.

A lost Barcelona manuscript was cited by Arias de Loyola and Johannes a Sancto Antonio.

Yates printed the Paris shelfmark as lat. 17820; the critical study gives **lat. 17829**. This is a concrete bibliographic correction.

### 10.3 Editions

- Paolo Rossi, “*Liber ad memoriam confirmandam*,” *Rivista critica di storia della filosofia* 13/19 (bibliographies vary in reporting the volume/year), 1958, pp. 273–79.
- Rossi, *Clavis universalis* (Milan–Naples, 1960), appendix I, pp. 261–70.
- Alois Madre and Charles Lohr, “Pseudo-Raimundus Lullus, *Liber ad memoriam confirmandam*: Zeuge der lullistischen Tradition an der Wende des 15./16. Jahrhunderts,” *Studia Lulliana* 36 (1996), pp. 99–121. This is the critical five-witness edition and controlling attribution study.

### 10.4 Structure and doctrine

The treatise opens:

> “Ratio, quare praesentem volumus colligere tractatum, est, ut memoria hominum, quae labilis est et caduca, modo rectificetur meliori.”

Gloss: “The reason why we wish to compile the present treatise is that human memory, which is unstable and perishable, may be corrected by a better method.”

Its alphabet begins:

> “B significat memoriam naturalem; C significat capacitatem; D significat discretivam.”

Gloss: “B signifies natural memory; C capacity; D discrimination.”

It divides the student’s cognitive equipment into:

- *capacitas*, located toward the front of the head and associated with fantasy;
- *memoria*, located toward the rear;
- *discretiva*, located at the crown and ruling the others like a queen.

This localization belongs to the medieval internal-senses tradition, though its exact arrangement is not standard Aristotelian doctrine.

### 10.5 Its study regimen

The student should:

- pray to God, the Holy Spirit, and the Virgin;
- moderate eating, drinking, and sleeping;
- devote most time to study;
- preview the lecture before attending;
- listen attentively;
- mentally revolve each proposition while the next is being taught;
- divide material into two, three, or four parts;
- review material frequently;
- use remembered texts and reasons to exercise judgment.

These are pedagogical and devotional rules, not a developed method of places and images.

### 10.6 Natural and artificial memory

The text says:

> “Memoria … secundum antiquos … alia est naturalis, alia est artificialis.”

Gloss: “Memory, according to the ancients, is one kind natural and another artificial.”

Natural memory is influenced by bodily matter and by the planet reigning at generation:

> “secundum quod influentia alicuius planetae superioris regnat.”

Artificial memory is twofold:

1. medicines and plasters;
2. repeated mental rehearsal.

The medical method is rejected as dangerous because excessive heat or dryness can damage the brain and cause madness:

> “homo ad dementiam demergitur.”

The approved method is repetition:

> “per saepissimam reiterationem firmiter confirmatur.”

This echoes Aristotle, *De memoria et reminiscentia* 452a28–29, as transmitted through scholastic commentary.

### 10.7 “Book of the Seven Planets”

All five witnesses refer three times to a *Liber septem planetarum*. Near the beginning:

> “vade ad quintum subiectum, per B C D designatum in Libro septem planetarum, quia ibi tractavimus miraculose, et notitiam omnium habebis entium naturalium.”

Gloss: “Go to the fifth subject, designated by B C D in the Book of the Seven Planets, for there we have treated wondrously, and you will have knowledge of all natural beings.”

**[Y]** Yates followed Salzinger in connecting this to Llull’s genuine *Tractatus novus de astronomia*.  
**[R]** Madre–Lohr identify the cited work as a Pseudo-Llullian *Liber de septem planetis*. The reference is therefore evidence for the apocryphal complex, not a hidden key supplied by authentic Llullian astronomy.

## 11. Lavinheta

### 11.1 Person and institutions

Bernard de Lavinheta, a Franciscan-associated theologian and one of the leading French Lullists, worked in the orbit of Paris, Lyon, Cologne, and Salamanca. Yates calls him the holder of a newly founded Sorbonne chair of Lullism. That institutional description is frequently repeated, but the exact legal form and duration of a permanent “chair” require more precise documentary confirmation than Yates supplies.

### 11.2 Principal printing

*Practica compendiosa artis Raymundi Lul. Explanatio compendiosaque applicatio artis illuminati doctoris magistri Raymundi Lulli ad omnes facultates*, Lyon: Jean Moylin for Simon Vincent, completed 1 March and printed 30 May 1523.

The colophon reads:

> “finiuit Bernardus de Lauinheta hoc opus in conventu sancti Francisci alias sancti Bonaventure Lugduni prima Martij … 1523.”

The volume has nine books and approximately 264 folios; surviving copies commonly appear to lack fols. CCLX–CCLXI because of a foliation or gathering anomaly rather than loss of text. [Bibliographical study and transcription](https://publicacions.iec.cat/repository/pdf/00000330/00000084.pdf)

Johann Heinrich Alsted re-edited Lavinheta’s works at Cologne in 1612. Yates cites its memory appendix at pp. 653–56.

### 11.3 Lavinheta’s synthesis

Lavinheta knew the pseudo-Llullian *Liber ad memoriam confirmandam* and incorporated it as authoritative Llull. His classification of sensible and intelligible memory converts the Lullian Art into a higher mnemonic discipline:

- the classical art stores sensory particulars;
- the Lullian Art compresses intellectual knowledge under a few universal terms.

This is historically one of the clearest points at which “the Art as memory” becomes an explicit Renaissance doctrine.

## 12. Pico, Mainardi, and *De auditu kabbalistico*

### 12.1 Pico’s comparison

In the *Apologia* to the *Conclusiones*, Pico writes:

> “In universali autem duas scientias hoc etiam nomine honorificarunt … una quae dicitur … ars combinandi … et est simile quid, sicut apud nostros dicitur ars Raymundi, licet forte diverso modo procedant.”

Gloss: “They honored two sciences in general with this name … one called the art of combining … and it is something similar to what among us is called the Art of Raymond, although perhaps they proceed in a different way.”

The qualification—“perhaps in a different way”—matters. Pico asserts resemblance, not identity. His immediate Jewish background was the combinatory or ecstatic Kabbalah associated with Abraham Abulafia and related texts. [Study of Pico’s wording and sources](https://ojs.ppke.hu/verbum/article/download/1007/1316?inline=1)

### 12.2 Was Llull himself a Cabalist?

- **[Y/D]** Yates believed a Cabalist element probably existed in Lullism “from the start.”
- **[D]** Erhard-Wolfram Platzeck, Gershom Scholem, José María Millás Vallicrosa, Moshe Idel, Harvey Hames, and others have identified affinities or possible channels.
- **[R]** Modern Llull scholarship generally distinguishes resemblance and contact from proof of direct technical borrowing.
- **[T]** Llull knew Jewish and Islamic interlocutors and worked in a multilingual Mediterranean intellectual environment.
- **Absence of evidence:** no authenticated Llullian text says that its letters or wheels derive from *Sefer Yetzirah*, Abulafia, or the Sephiroth.
- **[R]** Llull’s dignities also have strong Christian, Arabic theological, Augustinian, Victorine, and scholastic genealogies.

Moshe Idel’s formulation is appropriately cautious: affinities exist, and influence is possible, but the route and extent remain contested. [Idel, “Dignitates and Kavod”](https://ibdigital.uib.es/greenstone/sites/localsite/collect/studiaLulliana/index/assoc/Studia_L/ulliana_/1996v036/p069-1.dir/Studia_Lulliana_1996v036p069.pdf)

### 12.3 *De auditu kabbalistico*

The work was printed as:

*Opusculum Raymundinum de auditu kabbalistico sive ad omnes scientias introductorium*, Venice: Bernardino de Vitalibus, 2 December 1518; reprinted Venice, 1533, and repeatedly through sixteenth- and seventeenth-century Lullian anthologies.

The 1518 edition has 52 leaves, illustrations, and volvelles. A digitized Folger copy is B765.L8 D2 1518 Cage, with contemporary annotations and manicules. [Folger catalogue and digital images](https://catalog.folger.edu/record/339767)

**[R, prevailing]** Paola Zambelli demonstrated in 1965 that the author was probably the physician Pietro Mainardi (1456–1529), active at Ferrara and Padua. The title page itself contains evidence of his editorial-authorial role. [Braidense catalogue record](https://opac.bibliotecabraidense.org/opac_braidense/opaclib?db=solr_braidense&do_cmd=search_show_cmd&fname=none&format=unimarc&from=42&nentries=1&resultForward=opac%2Fbraidense%2Ffull_simili.jsp&rpnlabel=+Nomi+%3D++Lull%2C+Ram%C3%B3n+&rpnquery=%40attrset+bib-1++%40attr+1%3D1003+%40attr+4%3D1+%22+Lull%2C+Ram%C3%B3n%22&searchForm=opac%2Fbraidense%2Ferror.jsp&select_db=solr_braidense&simili_id=RMLE038875&totalResult=74)

The work maps Lullian letters and principles onto Cabalist terminology, Sephiroth, and angelic names. Yet it is only superficially Jewish in technical content; much of it is Christianized Lullism renamed “Cabala.”

**[Trad.]** Bruno and later Lullists believed it genuine. Zetzner’s anthology perpetuated that belief.

## 13. Pseudo-Lullian alchemy

### 13.1 Attribution

**[T]** Authentic Llull criticized alchemy in some contexts and did not write the great alchemical corpus attributed to him.

**[R, settled in principle]** The alchemical works are posthumous, beginning in the fourteenth century and expanding over several centuries. Individual authorships and dates remain difficult, but Llull’s authorship is rejected.

Important pseudo-Lullian titles include:

- *Testamentum*;
- *Codicillus*;
- *Liber de secretis naturae seu de quinta essentia*;
- *Apertorium*;
- *Clavicula*;
- *Compendium animae transmutationis*.

The corpus adapted Lullian terminology, elemental theory, and diagrams to transmutation, distillation, medicine, and quintessence. The major modern authority is Michela Pereira.

### 13.2 The alchemical tree

A fifteenth-century pseudo-Lullian diagram described by F. Sherwood Taylor and Yates has:

- lettered combinatory circles at the roots;
- a Lullian tree rising through elemental or material processes;
- zodiacal and seven-planet circles near or at the crown.

Yates correctly notes that no genuine Lullian Art uses so many letters in this way. The figure is reception evidence, not an authentic Llull diagram.

A Princeton manuscript feature preserves comparable Lullian-style wheels and trees connected with the *Book of the Secrets of Nature or Quintessence*. [Princeton, “The Philosophical Tree”](https://dpul.princeton.edu/alchemy/feature/the-philosophical-tree)

### 13.3 Camillo

Camillo quotes the pseudo-Lullian *Testamentum* in *L’Idea del Theatro*. Therefore:

- **[T]** Camillo cited an alchemical work as Llull’s.
- **[Trad.]** Camillo accepted or exploited the alchemical Llull.
- **[Y/D]** This does not prove that Camillo’s seven-grade Theatre was structurally derived from the Art.
- **[Y]** Yates cautiously says the Theatre anticipates the fusion later achieved by Bruno but remains “static in a building.”

## 14. Salzinger and the Mainz edition

Ivo Salzinger edited the incomplete Mainz *Opera omnia* of Ramon Llull, 1721–42. He placed his own *Revelatio secretorum Artis* in volume I.

At p. 154 he describes:

> “ars memorandi, per quam omnia huius Artis secreta in his septem instrumentis retinebis.”

Gloss: “an art of remembering through which you will retain all the secrets of this Art in these seven instruments.”

He identifies the seven instruments with the planets and the pseudo-treatise’s “Book of the Seven Planets” with Llull’s *Tractatus de astronomia*.

**[Trad.]** Salzinger’s synthesis is a major document in eighteenth-century esoteric Lullism.

**[Y]** Yates hypothesized that if sixteenth-century readers interpreted Llull similarly, they could have derived a celestial-seven memory from Lullism.

**[D/R]** This is conjectural. Yates admits that the two relevant works were not printed in the Renaissance, although manuscript circulation and Pirovano’s 1507 quotation of the astronomical material made indirect transmission possible.

**Correction:** Since the memory treatise is Pseudo-Llullian and its cited planetary book is also Pseudo-Llullian, Salzinger’s identification no longer reconstructs Llull’s secret. It reconstructs Salzinger’s synthesis of genuine and apocryphal Lullism.

## Yates’s account and the scholarship since

## 15. Yates’s argument, chapter VIII

Yates’s chapter proceeds through seven theses.

### Thesis 1: The Art belongs to memory because the soul’s powers are one

**Status:** **[Y, substantially defensible]** as an interpretation of Llullian psychology, provided it is not made into a claim that every operation of the Art is mnemonic.

### Thesis 2: The authentic mnemonic task is to memorize the whole Art

**Status:** **[Y/R, broadly confirmed but requires narrowing].** Llull expected mastery of the alphabet, definitions, and figures. Bonner confirms the centrality of learning the relational system. But the primary aim is invention and demonstration; “art of memory” is only one aspect.

### Thesis 3: Diagrams are “in some sense places”

**Status:** **[Y/D].** Plausible as a functional analogy. Not explicit doctrine. “Place” also has dialectical and ontological meanings in Llull, so the classical mnemonic resonance should not dominate.

### Thesis 4: Lullism turns memory into investigation

Yates writes:

> “memory in memorising such procedures is becoming a method of investigation, and a method of logical investigation.”

**Status:** **[Y, still illuminating].** Rossi’s history of the transition from mnemonic order to universal method supports the larger trajectory. Bonner shows more precisely how the Art finds relational middle terms. The phrase “memory becomes investigation” remains Yates’s synthesis, not Llull’s formula.

### Thesis 5: Genuine Lullism excludes striking images

**Status:** **[Y, generally confirmed].** The technical Art favors letters and abstract figures. Later scholarship adds that Llull’s literary-allegorical imagination remains rich, and that diagrams are not non-images in a broad semiotic sense.

### Thesis 6: Llull’s own memory treatise rejects “Tullian” rules

Yates writes:

> “But he deliberately leaves out the ‘Tullian’ rules.”

**Status:** **Overturned as a claim about Llull.** The *Liber ad memoriam confirmandam* is Pseudo-Llullian. Its omission of places and images is evidence about an early-modern Lullist author, not about Ramon Llull’s deliberate opposition to Dominican mnemonic practice.

Yates’s further speculation that the omission reflects conflict with Pisan Dominicans is consequently unsupported.

### Thesis 7: The Renaissance sought to put magical star images on Lullian wheels

Yates writes:

> “it was to be a grand Renaissance aim to bring together Lullism and the classical art of memory by using magic images of the stars on the Lullian figures.”

**Status:** **[Y/D].**

- Strongly supported for Bruno’s particular synthesis.
- Partly supported by pseudo-Lullian alchemical and Cabalist diagrams.
- Too broad if made the governing purpose of Renaissance Lullism as a whole.
- Lavinheta, Lefèvre d’Étaples, Bovelles, Agrippa, Alsted, and university Lullists had differing logical, theological, pedagogical, encyclopedic, medical, and apologetic agendas.
- Camillo’s direct structural dependence remains uncertain.

## 16. Rossi

Paolo Rossi’s *Clavis universalis* preceded Yates and profoundly shaped the problem. Rossi traced the arts of memory, topical invention, Lullian combination, universal language, and encyclopedic method from Llull to Leibniz.

Important corrections and developments:

- Rossi first edited the *Liber ad memoriam confirmandam*, but from only three witnesses.
- He accepted authenticity while entertaining the possibility of later tampering.
- Madre–Lohr’s complete collation made pseudonymity the better conclusion.
- Rossi’s larger history of combinatory method remains valuable independently of that attribution.

English edition: Paolo Rossi, *Logic and the Art of Memory: The Quest for a Universal Language*, trans. Stephen Clucas (London/Chicago: Athlone/University of Chicago Press, 2000).

## 17. Carruthers, Small, Hajdu, Bolzoni, and Ong

### Mary Carruthers

Carruthers’s *The Book of Memory* corrected the tendency to identify medieval memory almost exclusively with an image-and-locus technique. Medieval *memoria* was an ethical, compositional, meditative, and inventive practice embedded in reading and textual organization.

Applied to Llull:

- Yates is right that the Art differs from the classical rhetorical mnemonic;
- but “memory as invention” is less unprecedented than Yates suggests;
- medieval memory already generated composition and judgment rather than merely preserving data.

### Jocelyn Penny Small

*Wax Tablets of the Mind* stresses material writing practices, visual organization, and the difference between ancient testimony and modern reconstructions. Its caution is relevant to claims that revolving diagrams directly model psychic motion: the surviving artifact and its cognitive interpretation must be distinguished.

### Helga Hajdu

Hajdu’s early study of medieval artificial memory helped define the field Yates inherited. Later scholarship has broadened the evidence beyond a linear survival of the *Ad Herennium*.

### Lina Bolzoni

Bolzoni situates memory within image culture, preaching, literary composition, galleries, theatres, and early-modern encyclopedism. Her work on Camillo shows that the Theatre cannot be reduced to a single Lullian or Hermetic source; it is an active machine of textual and visual invention.

### Walter J. Ong

Ong’s *Ramus, Method, and the Decay of Dialogue* makes Ramist method a reorganization of knowledge through dichotomous visual order rather than simply a “memory system.” Lullism and Ramism overlap in their ambition to order the sciences, but differ sharply:

- Llullian order is relational and combinatory;
- Ramist order is predominantly divisional and dichotomous;
- the Lullian wheel generates or tests relations;
- the Ramist chart descends through partitions.

Yates’s broad movement from memory to method remains useful, but Ong prevents “method” from being treated as a single uniform development.

---

## 18. Bonner and Fidora

Anthony Bonner’s work is the most important technical correction to simplified pictures of the Art.

### Bonner’s principal corrections

1. The Art is a logic-like relational system, not chiefly a mnemonic.
2. The letters are variables or relational labels, not inherently mystical ciphers.
3. The figures must be read with definitions and rules.
4. The early and mature Arts differ substantially.
5. The Art changed in response to teaching experience and Parisian criticism.
6. Llull’s combinatorics are constrained, not an indiscriminate enumeration of every possible word.
7. The system is tied to metaphysical realism and apologetic goals.

Alexander Fidora and collaborators place Llull’s logic within medieval traditions of demonstration, topics, language, interreligious disputation, and Arabic philosophy. This weakens Yates’s contrast between a “Platonist” Llull and “scholasticism.” Llull was unconventional, but he participated deeply in scholastic questions and institutions.

---

## 19. Bruno scholarship after Yates

Yates decisively restored memory and Lullism to Bruno studies, but her “Hermetic magician” model has been revised.

### Ciliberto

Michele Ciliberto emphasizes Bruno’s philosophy, ontology, infinity, civil religion, and intellectual transformations. Magic is important but not an exhaustive explanation.

### Sturlese

Rita Sturlese’s philological work on Bruno’s Latin texts and manuscripts has refined chronology, textual relations, and the place of Lullian works in Bruno’s career.

### Clucas

Stephen Clucas has shown the interaction of memory, language, sign theory, natural philosophy, and manuscript culture. He resists treating every formal resemblance as direct occult transmission.

### Gatti

Hilary Gatti emphasizes Bruno’s philosophy of nature, mathematics, images, and intellectual freedom. The mnemonic works must be read as philosophy and epistemology as well as magic.

### Result

- **Confirmed:** Bruno deliberately combined Lullian figures with imagistic mnemonic systems.
- **Corrected:** the system is not exhausted by Ficinian astral magic.
- **Disputed:** how literally operative or talismanic Bruno intended particular images to be.
- **Documented:** Bruno represented his memory to Henri III as scientific rather than magically acquired.
- **Documented:** his accuser Mocenigo invited him to Venice to teach memory.
- **Not established:** that memory instruction itself was the juridical cause of Bruno’s execution.

## History and context

## 20. Intellectual traditions

### 20.1 Rhetoric

The classical art derives principally from:

- *Rhetorica ad Herennium* III.16–24;
- Cicero, *De oratore* II.350–60;
- Quintilian, *Institutio oratoria* XI.2.

Its architecture contains ordered places and vivid images. Genuine Lullism did not descend directly from this technique, although later Lullists such as Lavinheta explicitly compared and combined them.

### 20.2 Aristotelian psychology

Aristotle’s *De memoria et reminiscentia* treats memory as dependent on images and bodily affection and recollection as an ordered search. Medieval faculty psychology localized imagination, memory, and estimation in internal brain “cells.”

The Pseudo-Llullian treatise’s front/back/crown localization and repetition rule belong to this broad tradition.

### 20.3 Augustinian psychology

Augustine’s triad memory–intellect–will, especially in *De trinitate*, is central to Llull’s anthropology. The unity-in-distinction of the powers parallels the Trinity.

### 20.4 Topics and scholastic logic

Llull’s *inventio* should be compared with Aristotelian and Boethian topical logic: finding a middle term or argumentative locus. Bonner stresses this dialectical background. The Art aims at necessary rather than merely probable demonstrations.

### 20.5 Divine names and dignities

The dignities belong to a wide tradition including:

- Augustine;
- Pseudo-Dionysius;
- Anselm;
- Victorine theology;
- Arabic *ṣifāt* or divine attributes;
- medieval Jewish treatments of divine names;
- scholastic transcendentals.

**[D]** Yates’s specific emphasis on John Scotus Eriugena as a channel was pioneering but too exclusive. She herself admitted that she could not establish the exact transmission.

### 20.6 Cabala

Pico made the explicit Renaissance comparison between Abulafian letter combination and the *ars Raymundi*. Mainardi then materially fused the vocabularies in *De auditu kabbalistico*.

That Renaissance fusion is documented; direct thirteenth-century derivation remains disputed.

### 20.7 Hermeticism and magic

The pseudo-Lullian and Brunian traditions placed letters, wheels, planetary structures, divine names, and images within an occult synthesis. This was not one stable doctrine but a field spanning:

- natural magic;
- astrology;
- talismanic imagery;
- angelic names;
- Cabala;
- alchemy;
- medicine;
- mnemonic psychology.

### 20.8 Universal language

Because a small alphabet could represent general concepts and combine them, Lullism became a precedent for projects to classify all knowledge and create a universal symbolic language. Later figures include:

- Agrippa;
- Bruno;
- Alsted;
- Kircher;
- Comenius;
- Dalgarno;
- Wilkins;
- Leibniz.

The line is a reception genealogy, not the uninterrupted development of a single technical system.

## 21. Institutions

### Majorca

Llull’s home environment supplied:

- Catalan court culture;
- contact with Arabic;
- Jewish, Christian, and Muslim intellectual proximity;
- royal patronage;
- early Lullist schools;
- later institutional defense of his orthodoxy and cult.

### Paris

The University of Paris was the premier arena in which Llull sought recognition. His difficult first reception helped provoke simplification of the early Art. Thomas Le Myésier, a canon, physician, and Sorbonne associate, became his disciple and editor.

### Mendicant orders

**Dominicans:**

- Albert the Great and Thomas Aquinas helped integrate Aristotelian memory theory with the classical art.
- Bartolomeo da San Concordio transmitted Thomist memory teaching at Pisa.
- Nicolau Eymerich became the great anti-Lullist inquisitor.
- Bruno later entered the Dominican Order.

**Franciscans:**

- Lullism frequently flourished in Franciscan settings.
- Lavinheta completed his *Explanatio* at the Franciscan convent of St Bonaventure in Lyon.
- Franciscan interest in divine attributes, will, and the Immaculate Conception helped create receptive contexts.

Llull himself was a layman associated with Franciscan spirituality, not simply a Franciscan friar.

### Courts and patrons

Llull repeatedly sought royal and papal support for missionary schools and crusading projects. Renaissance Lullists found patrons among French and imperial courts. Bruno dedicated mnemonic works to Henri III and sought patronage through teaching.

### Universities

Paris, Padua, Ferrara, Cologne, Salamanca, and later German academies acted as sites where Lullism was:

- taught;
- attacked;
- reconciled with Aristotle;
- joined to medicine;
- absorbed into Cabala;
- reformulated as method.

## People

## 22. Practitioners, transmitters, critics, and patrons

### Ramon Llull

Courtier turned missionary, author, logician, contemplative, and organizer. He claimed that the Art’s principles were revealed to him in an illumination on Mount Randa.

**[T/Trad.]** The autobiographical *Vita coaetanea* records the illumination.  
**[R]** Historians can document the autobiographical claim, not supernatural causation.

### Thomas Le Myésier

Llull’s major early disciple and compiler of the *Electorium* and *Breviculum*. The *Breviculum*, Karlsruhe, Badische Landesbibliothek, Cod. St. Peter perg. 92, preserves twelve famous miniatures. One shows Llull and the ladders of his Art.

Le Myésier records pedagogical difficulty: students suffered eye strain and confusion from the sixteen figures of the *Ars demonstrativa*. This helps explain the mature Art’s simplification. [Recent analysis of the diagrammatic development](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.hm.2025.02.001)

### Nicholas of Cusa

Owned and studied numerous Lullian manuscripts. He transmitted genuine philosophical Lullism into fifteenth-century learned culture. Direct doctrinal dependence must be established work by work.

### Jacques Lefèvre d’Étaples and Charles de Bovelles

Major figures in French humanist Lullism; editors, commentators, and adapters rather than simply occultists.

### Giovanni Pico della Mirandola

Made the epochal comparison between Cabalist combination and the *ars Raymundi*. His 900 *Conclusiones* were printed in late 1486; thirteen were initially attacked by Innocent VIII. [Recent study of the *Conclusiones*](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/10.1086/725098)

### Pietro Mainardi

Probable author of *De auditu kabbalistico*. A physician working in Ferrara and Padua; a key architect of the Cabalist pseudo-Llull.

### Bernard de Lavinheta

Systematizer of the Art and explicit theorist of its superiority for memorizing intelligibles.

### Remigius Rufus

Lavinheta’s disciple and author of the pseudo-Lullian *In Rhetoricen Isagoge*, first printed Paris, 1515. The work ends with a model oration mystically encompassing the universe and the sciences.

### Giordano Bruno

The most consequential synthesizer of:

- Dominican artificial memory;
- classical places and images;
- Lullian wheels;
- astral imagery;
- combinatory invention;
- natural magic;
- Cabalist vocabulary.

### Johann Heinrich Alsted

Editor of Lavinheta and author of works on encyclopedic and Lullian method. He wished to recover a more disciplined Lullism but continued to transmit pseudo-Lullian material such as *De auditu*.

### Ivo Salzinger

Editor, interpreter, and esoteric systematizer. His Mainz edition preserved important texts while also projecting a “secret” architecture onto them.

### Nicolau Eymerich

Dominican inquisitor and tireless enemy of Lullism. In 1376 he assembled one hundred condemned propositions attributed to Llull and incorporated the attack into the *Directorium inquisitorum*.

Subsequent commissions found that some propositions were absent from Llull’s writings. The legality, authenticity, and scope of the papal condemnation long remained disputed. [Study of the controversy and later censorship](https://www.scielo.org.mx/scielo.php?pid=S0185-25232013000100005&script=sci_arttext)

## 23. Anecdotes and their status

### The Dominican vision

The *Vita coaetanea* says that Llull saw a vision in a Dominican church telling him that salvation required joining the Dominicans and abandoning his Art. He chose to preserve the Art even at risk to himself:

> he preferred that he alone be damned rather than that an Art capable of saving many should be lost.

**Status:** **[T as autobiographical tradition; D as literal event].** It is not independent evidence of Dominican hostility to Llullian memory, still less of a dispute over places and images. Yates’s link between the vision and the Pisan memory treatise collapses once that work is recognized as pseudo-Llullian.

### Llull’s illumination on Mount Randa

**Status:** **[T as Llullian biographical claim; Trad. as miracle].** It explains the Art’s divine authority within Lullist tradition but cannot establish supernatural revelation historically.

### Bruno before Pius V

Bruno reportedly told the librarian Guillaume Cotin that Pope Pius V and Cardinal Rebiba summoned him to Rome to demonstrate artificial memory, where he recited the Psalm *Fundamenta* in Hebrew.

**Status:** **[T as a recorded report by Cotin; not independently verified].**

### Henri III and the magic question

Bruno told the Venetian inquisitors that Henri III asked whether his memory was natural or obtained by magic. Bruno answered that it was achieved “not by magical art but by science,” then dedicated *De umbris idearum* to the king and received a lectureship.

**Status:** **[T as Bruno’s trial testimony].** It documents both suspicion and Bruno’s denial, not a judicial finding that the mnemonic was non-magical.

### Mocenigo

Giovanni Mocenigo invited Bruno to Venice promising good treatment if Bruno taught him the art of memory. Mocenigo later denounced him.

**Status:** **[T].**  
**Legendary inflation:** “Bruno was burned for teaching memory” is false. Memory brought him into the patronage relationship that led to his arrest, but the Roman proceedings concerned a broad complex of theological and philosophical accusations.

### The auctioneer, professor’s party, and amnesiac student

No reliable connection to Llull, Lavinheta, or Salzinger was found in the primary Lullian corpus or the critical studies consulted. They should not be included as Lullian anecdotes merely because they circulate in general histories of memory. **Status: [L or other-system material].**

## The magic question

## 24. Was genuine Lullism magical?

### Llull’s own Art

**[T/R]** Ordinarily, no—not in the sense of spells, talismans, demonic invocation, or coercive astral magic.

Its letters are abbreviations; its circles display relations; its demonstrations depend on metaphysical and theological principles. Llull’s religious aims were missionary and apologetic.

### Astronomy and medicine

Llull did apply the Art to:

- the elements;
- zodiacal and planetary relations;
- humoral medicine;
- diagnosis and health.

He distinguished his “new astronomy” from judicial astrology. The presence of planets does not by itself prove talismanic practice.

### Abstract or geometrical magic

**[Y/D]** Yates asks whether the figures themselves might constitute a kind of “abstract or geometrical magic.” This is suggestive but not a native Llullian category. It risks making unfamiliar diagrammatic reasoning magical by definition.

### Cabalist and alchemical reception

**[Trad.]** Renaissance readers did treat the expanded Lull as a repository of:

- divine names;
- angelic names;
- occult correspondences;
- alchemical secrets;
- planetary structures.

This reception was real even though many source texts were spurious.

---

## 25. Heresy, condemnation, and Inquisition

### Eymerich’s attack

Eymerich accused Llull of numerous theological errors, especially concerning:

- rational demonstration of the Trinity;
- the relation of faith and knowledge;
- divine attributes;
- eschatology;
- supposed revelation of the Art.

He even suggested that the apparition associated with Llull’s revelation might have been diabolical rather than Christ.

### Gregory XI’s bull

A bull attributed to Gregory XI condemned propositions extracted by Eymerich. Its exact authenticity, transmission, and juridical force became a centuries-long controversy.

What can safely be said:

- anti-Lullist proceedings occurred;
- Eymerich circulated condemned propositions;
- royal, municipal, Franciscan, and Lullist defenders contested him;
- later examinations found misquotation or propositions not present in Llull’s works;
- later printed indexes sometimes listed Llull while authorized editions and local cult continued.

### The 1419 “definitive sentence”

A later decision was used by Lullists to invalidate Eymerich’s case. Its scope and procedural history should not be simplified into “the Church cleared Llull once and for all.” The record reflects competing jurisdictions and continuing reception.

### Mexican Inquisition, 1665–69

A Spanish translation of the *Arbor scientiae* printed in Brussels was examined in New Spain. Censors objected to passages concerning original sin and the spiritual punishment of sincere Jews and Muslims. The episode documents continued suspicion of propositions in a genuine Llullian work, alongside elaborate printed defenses of his orthodoxy. [Full study](https://www.scielo.org.mx/scielo.php?pid=S0185-25232013000100005&script=sci_arttext)

## 26. Ars notoria

The *Ars notoria* promised accelerated or divinely infused knowledge through prayers, ritual observance, and inspection of complex figures or *notae*. It was associated with Solomon and sometimes condemned as necromantic.

Similarities to Lullism include:

- total knowledge;
- compressed figures;
- divine assistance;
- disciplined contemplation.

Differences are decisive:

- the *Ars notoria* relies on ritual prayers and visionary figures;
- Llull’s Art relies on definitions, principles, and reasoning;
- there is no evidence that Llull derived his figures from the *Ars notoria*;
- later occult readers could nevertheless assimilate both under the category of secret arts.

Calling the authentic Art an *Ars notoria* is therefore **[MI/Trad.]**, not textual description.

## Afterlife

## 27. Bruno’s fusion

Bruno’s principal mnemonic works are:

- *De umbris idearum*, Paris, 1582;
- *Cantus Circaeus*, Paris, 1582;
- *Ars reminiscendi* and *Explicatio triginta sigillorum*, London, 1583;
- *Sigillus sigillorum*, London, 1583;
- *Lampas triginta statuarum*, manuscript, probably Wittenberg c. 1587;
- *De imaginum, signorum et idearum compositione*, Frankfurt, 1591.

Bruno places image series upon concentric wheels and combines them through Lullian-type mechanisms. Unlike authentic Llull, he deliberately employs mythological, astral, animal, human, and sometimes magical images.

Yates’s strongest historical conclusion is therefore secure in a limited form: **Bruno really did fuse Lullian combination with an imagistic art of memory.**

But Bruno’s alphabetic wheels also support:

- topical invention;
- classification;
- word and concept formation;
- metaphysical ascent;
- psychological discipline;
- poetic composition.

They are not simply “memory wheels.”

---

## 28. Alsted, encyclopedism, and Ramism

Alsted’s encyclopedic works attempted to coordinate:

- Lullian universal principles;
- Ramist division;
- topical logic;
- mnemonic order;
- theological encyclopedia.

The seventeenth century increasingly translated the older “arts” into *methodus*. Yates saw this as a bridge to scientific method; Rossi traced it into universal-language projects. The relation is genuine but diffuse: it is not a direct causal chain from a medieval memory wheel to modern science.

---

## 29. Leibniz

Leibniz’s *Dissertatio de arte combinatoria* appeared at Leipzig in 1666. It explicitly discusses Llull and the Lullian tradition while criticizing arbitrary selection of primitive terms.

Documented continuities include:

- a finite alphabet of basic concepts;
- systematic combination;
- the hope of finding propositions by calculation;
- universal science;
- encyclopedic organization.

Leibniz’s work also depended on mathematical combinatorics, contemporary logic, Hobbes, Bisterfeld, and other universal-language projects. “Llull invented the computer” is therefore anachronistic.

A precise formulation is:

- **[T]** Leibniz knew and discussed Lullian combinatory art.
- **[R]** Lullism helped shape the genealogy of his *ars combinatoria* and *characteristica universalis*.
- **[MI]** Llull as the inventor of computation, artificial intelligence, or the digital computer is retrospective.
- **[R/MI]** Modern graph theory and computer science can fruitfully formalize parts of the Art without proving that Llull possessed those disciplines.

Recent historians note that the Art’s central achievement is relational organization, not numerical combinatorics alone. [Study of the mathematical question](https://narpan.net/bibliotecadigital/articles/doc_download/10-.html); [recent graph analysis](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.hm.2025.02.001)

## 30. Literature and visual culture

### Dante and the frescoes

Yates contrasts Lull’s abstract notation with Dante and Giotto’s affective corporeal similitudes. Direct influence of Llullian wheels on Dante or Giotto has not been demonstrated. Claims of a Lullian key to the *Divine Comedy* remain speculative unless tied to specific textual transmission.

### Emblem books

Lullian trees, lettered circles, and combinatory tables contributed to the broad early-modern culture of symbolic diagrams. Emblem books, however, normally join motto, picture, and epigram; that structure is not derived solely from Llull.

### Borges

Borges’s “Funes the Memorious” depicts a memory incapable of abstraction or forgetting. This is almost the inverse of Lullian memory, which compresses multiplicity under general principles. A comparison is intellectually fruitful but **[MI]** unless a direct textual source is demonstrated.

### Umberto Eco

Eco explicitly engaged Lullism in:

- *The Search for the Perfect Language*;
- essays on Cabalism, Lullism, and secret writing;
- *Foucault’s Pendulum*.

The computer called Abulafia and the novel’s combinatorial “Plan” deliberately juxtapose Cabalist permutation, Lullian wheels, occult historiography, and modern computation. Eco’s treatment is knowingly about the generation of false connections as well as real intellectual genealogy.

### Contemporary memory palaces

Modern popular mnemonic training chiefly descends from classical loci, Renaissance mnemonic manuals, and modern competitive practice. It normally does not reproduce Llull’s metaphysical alphabet.

### Memory athletes

Memory athletes use:

- routes;
- person–action–object codes;
- number-shape or number-rhyme systems;
- vivid images;
- chunking.

That practice is structurally closer to the *Ad Herennium* than to authentic Lullism. Lullian analogy becomes stronger only where users employ generative matrices or reconstruct information from conceptual relations.

## Controversies and disputes

## 31. What Yates got wrong

### 31.1 Authenticity of the memory treatise

Yates:

> “It is a perfectly authentic and genuine work by Lull.”

**Verdict:** overturned. Madre–Lohr classify it as Pseudo-Llull.

### 31.2 Pisa, 1308

**Verdict:** the colophon’s setting is part of the pseudo-attribution; it does not date composition.

### 31.3 San Donnino versus San Domenico

Yates emended the unanimous manuscript reading *Sancti Dominici* to *Sancti Donnini* by comparison with authentic Pisan colophons.

**Verdict:** ingenious but circular. The discrepancy is better evidence against authenticity.

### 31.4 Deliberate rejection of the Dominican art

Yates inferred that Llull knew the *Ad Herennium* rules but intentionally omitted places and images, perhaps in opposition to Pisan Dominican memory teaching.

**Verdict:** unsupported as biography. It describes the strategy of a Pseudo-Llullian writer, not securely Llull’s decision.

### 31.5 “Book of the Seven Planets” as Llull’s astronomy

**Verdict:** corrected. The direct referent is a Pseudo-Llullian planetary book, not necessarily the genuine *Tractatus novus de astronomia*.

### 31.6 Cabala “from the start”

**Verdict:** disputed. Affinities and possible contacts remain, but direct borrowing is unproved.

### 31.7 Llull as essentially non-scholastic and Eriugenian

**Verdict:** substantially revised. Llull was not a conventional university master, but his Art engages scholastic logic, demonstration, faculty psychology, and disputation. Direct Eriugenian transmission remains uncertain.

### 31.8 The “grand Renaissance aim”

**Verdict:** too totalizing. Accurate for Bruno and some occult Lullism; not a comprehensive description of Renaissance Lullism.

## 32. What Yates got right or made newly visible

1. Lullism belongs in the history of memory even though it differs radically from classical loci.
2. The figures have mnemonic as well as logical effects.
3. Renaissance readers conflated authentic and spurious Lullian texts.
4. *De auditu kabbalistico* was crucial to the Cabalist Lull.
5. Pseudo-Lullian alchemy helped create Llull the Magus.
6. Lavinheta explicitly treated the Art as superior memory for intelligibles.
7. Bruno fused Lullian wheels with classical and astral images.
8. Memory, universal method, encyclopedia, and combinatory logic formed an important early-modern constellation.
9. The later history of Lullism cannot be reconstructed solely from authentic Llull.

## 34. Manuscript and edition variants

### *Liber ad memoriam confirmandam*

Madre–Lohr find two broad textual groupings, with M1–M2 often preserving better readings and R, V, and A supplying alternatives. Variants include:

- titles: *Liber ad memoriam confirmandam*, *Tractatus de memoria*, *Perutilis … Tractatus de memoria*;
- *Dominici* in the colophon;
- *memoria artificialis* versus *artificiosa*;
- *emplastris* corrupted to *epistolis* in one Munich witness;
- Alan of Lille corrupted to “Alonius” or replaced with Aristotle;
- the Aristotelian citation omitted in the Ambrosiana manuscript;
- differing readings of *capacitas*, *discretiva*, and lecture instructions.

All five contain the planetary references. That consistency shows that these are not a casual interpolation in one late witness; they belong to the recoverable archetype of the pseudo-treatise.

### Authentic Art diagrams

Variants arise from:

- quaternary versus ternary phase;
- 16-letter versus nine-letter systems;
- different coloring;
- copying errors in lines;
- immovable drawings versus functional volvelles;
- printers flattening layered disks;
- omitted or missing movable pieces;
- rearranged alphabet tables;
- vernacular versus Latin terminology.

One should never treat “the Lullian wheel” as a single invariant diagram.

## 35. Legend versus documentation

### Legend: Llull was a master alchemist

- First spread through posthumous works under his name.
- Reinforced by manuscript and print anthologies.
- Accepted by Camillo, Bruno, Salzinger, and many early-modern readers.
- No evidence in authentic works supports authorship of the major alchemical corpus.

### Legend: Llull invented a logical machine

- The movable figure is documented.
- Calling it a “machine” is early-modern and modern reception language.
- It does not autonomously reason.

### Legend: Llull derived the Art from Cabala

- Pico first made the explicit comparison.
- Scholem, Millás, Yates, Platzeck, Idel, and Hames developed differing versions.
- Contact is plausible; direct technical derivation remains unproved.

### Legend: Bruno died for the art of memory

- Mocenigo’s invitation to teach memory is documented.
- The denunciation and trial involved extensive theological charges.
- Memory was causally involved in his return and arrest, not the sole charge or reason for execution.

### Legend: the wheels generate every possible truth

- They enumerate formal combinations.
- Definitions, subjects, axioms, and trained reasoning determine meaningful or admissible use.
- Llull expressly denies that the mechanism works without intellectual subtlety and reasoning.

## Open questions

1. Can the *Liber ad memoriam confirmandam* be dated more narrowly through its university vocabulary, sources, and relationship to Lavinheta’s circle?
2. Who wrote its companion *Liber de septem planetis*, and do the surviving works preserve the exact referenced recension?
3. Did Lavinheta know a manuscript substantially earlier than the five now identified?
4. What was the precise status of Lavinheta’s teaching position in Paris?
5. How widely were physical volvelles actually assembled and used rather than merely viewed as diagrams?
6. How did oral teaching modify the written instructions of the Art?
7. Which diagrams in pseudo-Lullian alchemy derive from specific authentic figures, and which merely imitate their visual authority?
8. How much did Abulafian combination reach Llull directly, indirectly through converted Jews or Arabic culture, or not at all?
9. Can a definite chain connect Llull’s dignities to Eriugena, rather than demonstrate only doctrinal resemblance?
10. How did different confessional settings—Franciscan, Dominican, humanist, Reformed—alter the meaning of “Lullian memory”?
11. Which surviving copies of *De auditu kabbalistico* retain intact movable disks, pins, threads, or user annotations?
12. Did Camillo know *De auditu* directly, or only the wider Christian-Cabalist Lull?
13. To what degree did Bruno use the wheels operationally in teaching, rather than as philosophical-literary exposition?
14. How far did Leibniz know authentic Llull as opposed to the Zetzner anthology’s composite author?
15. Can modern computational reconstructions distinguish Llull’s actual relational logic from operations imposed by graph theory or programming?
16. What is the current critical-edition status of the *Liber de memoria* trilogy, beyond the manuscript and pre-1966 reports used by Yates?
17. Are the “auctioneer,” “professor’s party,” and “student who lost his memory” traceable to distinct catalogued systems rather than to Lullism? No connection was found in the sources consulted here.
