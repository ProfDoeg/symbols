The central finding is that Dicson’s mnemonic procedure is real and fairly complete, but it is less mechanically Brunian than Yates’s famous summary suggests. The work contains no engraved wheel, zodiacal diagram, decan table, or illustrated plate. Its zodiac is primarily a numbering system, and its operational core remains recognizably classical local memory.

## The system in detail

## 1. Form and architecture of the book

**[TEXT]** The first edition consists of:

1. a dedication to Robert Dudley, Earl of Leicester;
2. an address by Dicson to the reader;
3. a commendatory address by “Ioannes Adamus Lincolniensis”;
4. a short dialogue among Thamus, Mercurius, and Theutates;
5. a much longer dialogue between Thamus and Socrates;
6. Thamus’s systematic discourse on memory in twenty-two chapters.

The title calls the work a *prosopopoeia*, a personification or speech placed in another’s mouth. The surviving text looks like selected sections of a larger dialogue: it begins abruptly in conversation, and after Thamus’s discourse it ends without a resumed dramatic conclusion. The proposition that portions of a larger work have been omitted is a reasonable **[RECONSTRUCTION]**, not a statement by Dicson.

3. *De memoriae subiecto*

## 2. What memory is

**[TEXT: Chapter I, first edition pp. 35–36 approximately]** Dicson defines forgetting morally and cosmologically:

> *Oblivio nequitia est impotentis animi, atque demissi, eademque materiae soboles prima…*

“Forgetfulness is the depravity of a powerless and abject mind, the first offspring of matter.”

Memory, by contrast, is:

> *potentia quidem illa, et actus, et umbra, remigium … alarum animi*

“a power, an act, and a shadow—the oarage of the soul’s wings.”

This is not merely an empirical psychology. **[TEXT]** Dicson connects memory with the soul’s passage from matter and darkness toward light. Yet the actual mnemonic prescriptions that follow are conventional enough to be practiced without accepting that metaphysics.

**[YATES]** Yates treats this language as evidence that the mnemonic image is a metaphysical shadow of divine ideas.

**[QUALIFICATION]** Dicson certainly makes light, shadow, vestige, horizon, and seal into philosophical terms. But his technical definition of an *umbra* is simply a mentally fashioned visible representation assigned to a place. The metaphysical and practical meanings coexist; it does not follow that each mnemonic image was a talisman or astral vehicle.

## 3. The causes and alleged origin of memory

**[TEXT: Chapter II; first edition p. 37]** Dicson distinguishes three sources of mnemonic power:

- nature;
- powers or influences “from above”;
- doctrine and reason, which awaken and strengthen latent nature.

He writes:

> *licet omnis a Deo et uno procedat, in triplici tamen nunc esto varietate. Nunc enim naturae parentis reputamus officium. Nunc superum radios et efficacias sentimus, nunc rationis et doctrinae consilia succurrunt.*

“Although all proceeds from God and the One, let it now be considered in a threefold variety: the work of parent Nature; the rays and powers of the higher beings; and the counsels of reason and teaching.”

The word *coniuratio* follows in Dicson’s discussion. In context it means a conjunction or sworn alliance of natural and taught powers, not necessarily ritual conjuration.

### Egypt, Simonides, and the Druids

Dicson rejects the usual Greek origin story:

> *Imo Chius ille primus eam invenisse et protulisse creditur. At falsum… Quod si separetur etiam Aegiptus, nihil tamen effecerit ille. Et enim si Celtarum replicentur annales… multo ante in Druidum schola viguisse reperias.*

“That man of Chios is believed to have first discovered and published it. But this is false … If Egypt be separated from it, he will have accomplished nothing. For if the annals of the Celts are unfolded … you will find that it flourished much earlier in the school of the Druids.”

“Chius” means Simonides of Ceos, not Chios strictly speaking; the phrasing is geographically loose.

- **[TEXT]** Dicson claims Egyptian and Druidic priority.
- **[TRADITION]** Renaissance prisca-theologia narratives routinely assigned arts to primordial sages.
- **[NO EVIDENCE]** No Egyptian manual of the classical place-and-image technique is known, nor is there documentary evidence that Druids practiced Dicson’s system.
- **[YATES]** “If it is separated from Egypt it can effect nothing” becomes a central indication of occult Egyptian memory.
- **[CORRECTION]** It is evidence for Dicson’s origin myth and ideological framing, not evidence about the actual ancient origin of artificial memory.

## 4. The psychological model

**[TEXT: Chapters III, XIX]** Memory belongs to embodied, temporal beings. Celestial souls do not remember discursively because they know without the temporal absence that makes recollection necessary. Human beings, by contrast, experience time and require memory.

Dicson later offers a concentric cognitive model:

> *Habet etenim cerebrum suam sphaeram, et distinctos orbes, sensum, visionem, cogitationem, memoriam. Atque haec quidem in centro est, in horizonte vero sensuum instrumenta.*

“The brain has its sphere and its distinct circles: sensation, vision, thought, and memory. Memory is at the center, while the instruments of sense are at the horizon.”

Nothing reaches the center except through the intervening circles. This is a conceptual diagram expressed verbally; the book supplies no drawn cerebral sphere.

The stages are approximately:

```text
external object
→ sensory instrument at the “horizon”
→ sensation
→ internal visualization
→ thought/imaginative shaping
→ memory at the center
```

**[RECONSTRUCTION]** This combines Aristotelian-scholastic internal-sense psychology with Neoplatonic language. Dicson does not provide a textbook mapping to the usual *sensus communis–imaginatio–cogitativa–memoria* faculties, but that background makes his account intelligible.

## 5. What serves as places: *subiecta*

**[TEXT: Chapters VI–VII]**

> *Est ergo subiectum sinus et receptaculum umbrarum.*

“A subject is a hollow or receptacle of shadows.”

A *subiectum* is therefore the mnemonic background conventionally called a *locus*. Dicson distinguishes:

- **absolute or principal subjects**: stable things made by nature or human hands;
- **assisting or auxiliary subjects**: added by visualization, capable of movement, used both to support animation and to mark number and order.

Possible larger fields for absolute subjects include:

- the universe;
- the earth;
- a containing environment;
- a polity;
- a household and its parts.

In practical operation, however, the examples are architectural: an intercolumniation can contain a sword; distinct spaces are arranged at regular intervals.

### Rules for selecting subjects

**[TEXT: Chapter X]** Dicson’s rules reproduce the classical tradition with his own terminology:

1. **They must be sensible or capable of a sensible form.**  
   One cannot place or retrieve what cannot be mentally pictured.

2. **They must be neither dazzlingly bright nor excessively dark.**

   > *nec nimis illustria … nec vehementer etiam obscura sunto*

3. **They should be moderately sized.**  
   A place too large will not hold a determinate image; one too narrow seems to repel it.

4. **Intervals must be moderate and approximately equal or parallel.**

5. **The intervening areas should be mentally suppressed.**  
   This prevents accidental wandering between places.

6. **The subjects must differ in form and nature.**  
   Similar places cause mnemonic confusion.

7. **Their sequence must be faultless.**  
   The user must know what occupies every ordinal position.

8. **They are divided into groups or “tribes.”**

9. **Each receives an auxiliary guard or marker.**

These closely parallel *Ad Herennium* III.29–32 and Quintilian XI.2: moderate size, adequate light, distinctiveness, intervals, and fixed order. Yates’s statement that Dicson repeats the Ad Herennian rules is therefore substantially confirmed.

## 6. Auxiliary images: *adiuvantia*

**[TEXT: Chapters VII–IX]** Dicson’s terminology is more precise than Yates’s shorthand sometimes implies. An *adiuvans* is not simply another name for every mnemonic image. It is an auxiliary element attached to an absolute place, used:

- to multiply or differentiate the place;
- to indicate its number and order;
- to provide an instrument with which the principal mnemonic figure can act;
- to prompt recovery if the principal shadow fails to appear.

Example:

> *Esto igitur in intercolumnio gladius.*

“Let there be, then, a sword in the space between the columns.”

The sword remains associated with that station. Different figures can then use it:

- a ruler threatens seditionists with it;
- Alledius hands it out while arming slaves;
- a personification of natural self-defense extends it;
- innovators pay the penalty by it;
- Astraea bears it as her attribute.

This is an important mechanism. A fixed accessory stabilizes the locus, while a variable agent performs the action required by the thing being memorized.

Dicson calls all such auxiliaries “mathematical” not because they are immaterial geometrical entities, but because they signify the number and order of the subjects.

## 7. What serves as images: *umbrae*

**[TEXT: Chapter XI]**

> *Est igitur umbra efficta rei species credita subiecto, ad firmam illa quidem perceptionem assumpta.*

“A shadow is a fashioned appearance of a thing entrusted to a subject, adopted for its firm apprehension.”

The mnemonic chain is:

```text
thing to remember
→ fashioned visible species (“shadow”)
→ assigned to a subject/place
→ sharpened by thought and action
→ retrieved through the place’s order
```

The shadow is not the eternal form in the divine mind; that is light. Nor is it the celestial form or the external object itself. It is the representation polished by sense and thought.

### Simple shadows

**[TEXT: Chapter XII]** A simple shadow represents a visible thing directly:

> *homo hominem, Alledius Alledium, et beluam belua designat*

“A man signifies a man, Alledius Alledius, and an animal an animal.”

Thus, if a known person is to be remembered, imagine that person. If an animal is to be remembered, picture it directly.

### Modified shadows

**[TEXT: Chapters XIII–XV]** Abstract, invisible, auditory, relational, or conceptual material requires a constructed or transferred image. Dicson calls this *conformatio*, *modificatio*, or *inflexio*.

The transfer is functional rather than ornamental. Unlike a decorous rhetorical metaphor, a mnemonic image can be extreme because its purpose is recovery, not elegance.

Methods include:

- **cause for effect or effect for cause**;
- **inventor or practitioner for an art**;
- **attribute for institution or condition**;
- **opposite for opposite**;
- **associate for associate**;
- **part for whole**;
- **species for genus**;
- **analogy or allegory**;
- **sound and word association**.

### Dicson’s worked examples

**[TEXT: Chapters XV–XVI]**

#### Inventors and arts

> *Theutates, pyromantiam Vulcanus, hydromantiam Nereus, ortum et occasum Apollo, eamque quae chirurgia dicitur Chiron, hinc bella et molitiones Minerva, providentiam Prometheus, Epimetheus poenitentiam praestare possit.*

In gloss:

- Theutates represents mathematics and letters;
- Vulcan represents pyromancy;
- Nereus represents hydromancy;
- Apollo represents rising and setting;
- Chiron represents surgery—not medicine in general in this exact passage;
- Minerva represents wars and military works;
- Prometheus represents foresight;
- Epimetheus represents hindsight or repentance.

**[YATES]** These are “traces of Bruno’s list of inventors.”

**[QUALIFICATION]** The correspondence is strong, especially in the use of mythic inventors as categorical image-agents. But inventor-images were not uniquely Bruno’s creation, and Dicson’s selection is shorter and partly different. Direct dependence on *De umbris idearum* is very likely; exact one-to-one copying is not demonstrated.

#### Effects as vestiges of an invisible cause

An unseen God is represented through the created order:

> *Quis oculos tornavit? Quis nares perfodit? Quis nervos produxit et alligavit? … nisi qui non videtur Deus? Ecce ut ipsum infinitum, incomprehensum ab effectis ostenditur.*

“Who fashioned the eyes? Who bored the nostrils? Who drew out and joined the nerves? … Who but the unseen God? Thus the infinite and incomprehensible is indicated from effects.”

Dicson then says that God, eternity, world, time, generation, gods, and agents can be recovered:

> *quasi ex vestigiis et sigillis*

“as if from vestiges and seals.”

This phrase supports Yates’s metaphysical reading, but it occurs in a discussion of effect-to-cause imagery; *sigilla* here need not mean magical seals.

#### Political constitutions

- a ruler consulting the common good represents kingship;
- one who does not represents tyranny;
- a virtuous few represent aristocracy;
- a vicious few represent oligarchy;
- the people united for common utility represent polity;
- the people acting against patricians represent democracy.

#### Accidents and conventional signs

- the swallow or bean-sowing represents spring;
- the toga represents peace;
- arms and weapons represent war.

#### Opposites and correlatives

- a robber can evoke a parricide;
- heirs evoke a testator;
- the person causing loss evokes the sufferer;
- wife evokes husband;
- father-in-law evokes son-in-law;
- tenant evokes landlord;
- Anaxagoras evokes Pericles;
- adulterer evokes Hippolytus;
- slave evokes freedman;
- hot evokes cold;
- prodigal evokes thrifty;
- prudent evokes fool;
- prosperous evokes unfortunate;
- blind evokes seeing;
- sober evokes drunken.

The opposite is sometimes made memorable through ridicule.

#### Analogy and allegory

- a deeply rooted tree that cannot be shaken represents virtue;
- a storm-battered ship represents a distressed commonwealth;
- storms and whirlwinds represent dangers and disasters;
- gold tested in fire represents the maxim that fidelity is tested by adverse fortune;
- nuptial flutes represent marriage;
- a troop represents cavalry;
- roof, walls, and fortifications represent a house or city;
- Mercury, Aesculapius, Prometheus, and Chiron can stand for wise persons.

## 8. Numerical and zodiacal order

**[TEXT: Chapter XVI; first edition pp. 69 ff.]**

The zodiac is introduced as a system for numbering positions:

> *numerorum exhibitio quaeritur … eius primum, in decurias quae ex Zodiaci signorum ordine reputentur, esto divisio.*

“Numerical representation is sought … let there first be a division into tens reckoned according to the order of the signs of the Zodiac.”

Procedure:

1. Assign one decade to each zodiacal sign.
2. Divide each sign into ten distinct image-positions.
3. Twelve signs × ten positions = **120**.
4. Apply the same procedure to the planets and the image of the lunar dragon to reach **200**.
5. Use these positions to remember the numbers of books, chapters, and comparable ordered material.

Dicson anticipates the objection that a crab or fishes do not naturally supply ten clear subdivisions:

> *quasi vero cancrum pro Cancro, pro Piscibus pisces effingi velim.*

“As though I wished a crab to be fashioned for Cancer or fishes for Pisces.”

Instead, one must use the signs’ “proper images” and vary them.

### What “proper images” means

**[DISPUTED]** Yates inferred that Dicson alludes to the traditional astrological images associated with the signs and lunar mansions, though he omits Bruno’s explicit decan lists. This is plausible because:

- Dicson says his readers know what and how many these images are;
- he mentions zodiac, planets, lunar dragon, and lunar-mansion images;
- the system is numerically close to zodiacal mnemonic traditions attributed to Metrodorus.

But the surviving text neither enumerates nor illustrates the images. We cannot reconstruct Dicson’s complete set with certainty.

### Was it a wheel?

- **[TEXT]** No wheel is prescribed in *De umbra rationis*.
- **[TEXT]** No movable volvelles survive in either printing examined through catalogues and digital witnesses.
- **[YATES/RECONSTRUCTION]** The system is related to Bruno’s rotating-wheel systems because its categories and terminology derive from *Shadows*.
- **[CORRECTION]** Calling Dicson’s own system “a set of Brunian wheels” is unwarranted. Its explicitly described order can be maintained as a linear or grouped sequence.

## 9. Word memory

**[TEXT: Chapter XVII]** Dicson favors remembering things rather than exact wording, citing the Horatian principle that words follow a well-mastered subject. Nevertheless, he provides devices for verbal memory.

One method assigns thirty letters or elements to thirty known persons, with each person’s characteristic effects and attributes. Combinations of persons and attributes construct syllables or words. Gesture can indicate fourth or fifth elements in a compound.

He also proposes separate bodies for letters, with five accompanying consonantal configurations and additional effects or attributes for liquids and finals. The details are compressed, and Dicson tells each practitioner to prepare a personally suitable repertory.

**[RECONSTRUCTION]** This resembles Bruno’s combinatory alphabets but lacks Bruno’s full tabulation and wheels. It is best understood as a flexible personal visual alphabet rather than a reconstructable universal code.

## 10. The law of shadows

**[TEXT: Chapter XVIII]** Rules for images include:

- first seek a visually apprehensible form;
- keep the object at its natural scale if possible;
- otherwise modify it through attributes;
- choose among the many possible shadows according to opportunity;
- keep images dissimilar;
- revise any image that resembles another too closely;
- subordinate elegance to mnemonic effectiveness.

Dicson’s rules resemble the classical advice that images must be distinct, exceptional, and readily visible. Unlike *Ad Herennium*, he does not reproduce the famous lawsuit image as his own worked example.

### The lawsuit image

The user’s template asks for the *Ad Herennium* lawsuit image. It belongs to the ancestor system, not to Dicson’s text. In *Rhetorica ad Herennium* III.33, a prosecution involving poisoning, inheritance, and witnesses is compressed into a bedside tableau: the accused holds a cup in one hand, tablets in the other, and a ram’s testicles on a finger to cue the witnesses (*testes*). Dicson does not quote or adapt that tableau. Its relevance is structural: multiple details are condensed into one extraordinary active scene.

## 11. Method and hierarchy

**[TEXT: Chapter XX]** Order is established hierarchically:

> *una si possit idea statuatur, quae mox in partes communes sit distribuenda. Hae rursus, in alias minus communes, illa, in proprias, propriae tandem in tribus secernantur.*

“Let one idea, if possible, be established; then divide it into common parts, these again into less common parts, those into proper parts, and finally separate the proper parts into tribes.”

The pattern is:

```text
one governing idea
├── general divisions
│   ├── less-general divisions
│   │   ├── particular divisions
│   │   │   └── tribes / groups of stations
```

Additional third and tenth differentiations can be added with auxiliaries and a “golden chain.”

**[TEXT]** The phrase *aurea catena* appears, but Dicson gives no diagram explaining it.

**[RECONSTRUCTION]** It may mean a continuous chain of ordinal auxiliary markers linking one station to the next.

**[CAUTION]** It should not automatically be identified with Homer’s cosmic golden chain, a magical chain of spirits, or a later occult “association chain.”

## 12. Animation

**[TEXT: Chapter XXI]** This is the decisive act:

> *Primum igitur ne dormiatur in umbris.*

“First, then, let there be no sleeping among the shadows.”

The images should display:

- sharp or violent deeds;
- conspicuous circumstances;
- exaggeration;
- forceful motion;
- strong emotional effects;
- transformation of the observer into the matter and of the matter into itself;
- the apparent life and breathing of the inanimate.

Dicson concludes:

> *ea praesertim [solertia], quae efficit, ut inanima quae sunt, quasi vivere et spirare videantur.*

“Especially admirable is the skill that makes inanimate things appear to live and breathe.”

This is not a marginal flourish: animation supplies the force that drives images from thought into memory.

## 13. Retrieval

Dicson never gives a single numbered retrieval demonstration comparable to “walk to locus 1, decode A; locus 2, decode B.” The operation nevertheless follows clearly from his definitions.

**[RECONSTRUCTION, strongly text-based]:**

1. Establish a stable series of subjects.
2. Know their order and grouping independently of the temporary content.
3. Mark stations with fixed auxiliaries.
4. Convert the material into simple or modified shadows.
5. Make the shadows act with or upon the auxiliaries.
6. During recall, survey the subjects in order.
7. Observe each animated scene.
8. Decode direct likeness, cause, effect, attribute, opposite, associate, part, allegory, letter, or number.
9. If the main image fails, use the fixed auxiliary and its consequences as a secondary cue.

That the order could be traversed backward as well as forward is standard in classical artificial memory, but Dicson does not state it explicitly in the surviving passage. It should not be attributed to him without qualification.

## The taught system according to Hugh Plat

## 1. Correct bibliography

The item-specific brief gives “Platt 1592, pp. 81–83.” The extant book is:

**Hugh Plat, *The Jewell House of Art and Nature* (London: Peter Short for William Ponsonby, 1594), section 98, pp. 81–85, sigs. N1r–N3r.**

The Folger record identifies the 1594 printing, STC 19991.5; the Cambridge anthology likewise dates and locates the passage in 1594. [Folger catalogue](https://catalog.folger.edu/record/408450); [Cambridge critical anthology entry](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/memory-arts-in-renaissance-england/hugh-plat-the-jewel-house-of-art-and-nature-1594/7438D33ACFBCBAAE008DCF79C07A2413).

No verified 1592 edition of *The Jewell House* has been found. **The 1592 date in the supplied catalogue entry is an error**, perhaps confused with the date of Bowes’s report on Dicson.

## 2. Plat’s description

Plat’s heading reads:

> “The Art of Memory which Master Dickson the Scot did teach of late years in England, and whereof he hath written a figurative and obscure treatise, set down briefly and in plain terms according to his own demonstration, with the special uses thereof.”

**[TEXT]** Plat says places are prepared in groups of ten. Objects or figures are attached to them and made lively. His examples of convenient place-images include ordinary, gross, or comic objects such as a dunghill, tub, night-gown, and ape.

He describes the process as:

> “to animate the umbras or ideas rerum memorandarum. But herein everie man may best please his owne witte and memorie.”

That is, “to animate the shadows or ideas of the things to be remembered.”

One exemplary image is:

> “Bellona staring with her fierie eies and portraied in all points according to the usual description of the Poets.”

The fiery-eyed war goddess represents martial material by a conventional poetic personification made emotionally vivid.

## 3. Uses claimed by Plat

**[TEXT/REPORT]** Plat treats the art pragmatically. It can help with:

- errands;
- matters arising in conversation or at table;
- ordered lists;
- possibly cards or games;
- retaining miscellaneous business.

He also complains that the practical result did not match the “great and swelling art” under which it had been advertised or sold.

**[YATES]** Plat received only a “simple form of the straight mnemotechnic” and was not initiated into Dicson’s Hermetic mysteries.

**[CORRECTION]** The first half is plausible; the second is an argument from silence. Plat may have ignored, rejected, misunderstood, or never been taught Dicson’s metaphysics. Nothing documents a secret higher initiation from which he was excluded.

## 4. Relationship between book and oral teaching

The oral system agrees closely with the treatise:

| Plat | Dicson |
|---|---|
| Places in tens | decuries; tenfold division |
| Vivid personifications | simple and modified shadows |
| Fixed ordinary objects | auxiliaries attached to subjects |
| “Animate the umbras” | Chapter XXI, *De animatione* |
| Personal choice of images | each person prepares suitable persons, bodies, effects, and attributes |
| Practical list memory | books, chapters, matters, words, numbers |

Thus Plat is a valuable independent witness that Dicson publicly taught an operational art rather than merely publishing metaphysical allegory.

## 4. *Defensio pro Alexandro Dicsono* (1584)

“Scepsius” aligns the defender with Metrodorus of Scepsis, the ancient mnemonist associated by Quintilian with zodiacal places. It is also a witty appropriation of an adversary’s label.

## 5. Perkins/G. P. texts

The controversy comprises two related anti-Dicsonian publications:

1. *Antidicsonus cuiusdam Cantabrigiensis G.P.*, with *Libellus in quo dilucide explicatur impia Dicsoni artificiosa memoria*; London: Henry Middleton, 1584.
2. *Libellus de memoria, verissimaque bene recordandi scientia*, followed by *Admonitiuncula ad A. Disconum de artificiosae memoriae … vanitate*; London: Robert Waldegrave, 1584.

The second survives in searchable EEBO-TCP transcription. Its admonition begins:

> *quem aspexisse multos audio, probasse vix ullos*

“I hear that many have looked at your little book, but scarcely any have approved it.”

It accuses Dicson of deliberately cultivating obscurity and hints that his real purpose is to make money. [EEBO-TCP text](https://quod.lib.umich.edu/e/eebo/A08777.0001.001).

### Attribution to William Perkins

- **[TRADITION/YATES]** G. P. is William Perkins, the Cambridge Puritan theologian.
- **[DOCUMENT]** Perkins’s later bibliography and theological writings are consistent with the attribution.
- **[DISPUTED/HISTORICAL]** Older cataloguing also proposed Gerard Peeters or left G. P. anonymous.
- **[CURRENT CONSENSUS]** Perkins is probable, but “signed G. P., attributed to William Perkins” is more exact than presenting a named title page.

## Yates’s account and scholarship since

## 1. Yates’s reading

Yates makes five principal claims.

### A. Dicson is an ardent disciple of Bruno

**[YATES]** *De umbra rationis* is a “close imitation” of Bruno’s *De umbris idearum*.

**Assessment:** substantially confirmed.

Evidence includes:

- Bruno’s terminology of shadows;
- mythological inventor-images;
- zodiacal organization;
- “vestiges” and “seals”;
- the contrast between inner writing and external letters;
- the Hermetic framework;
- Bruno’s praise of Dicson and use of “Dicsono” as an interlocutor.

But the technical core also descends directly from classical and Renaissance local-memory manuals.

### B. The imagery is an occult route toward divine light

Yates writes that Dicson’s image is a shadow of divine light sought through shadows, vestiges, and seals.

**Assessment:** textually grounded but rhetorically intensified.

Dicson explicitly distinguishes primordial mental light from generated mnemonic shadows and speaks of God through effects, vestiges, and seals. That supports a Neoplatonic-Hermetic epistemology. It does not prove that every image was intended to exert occult force.

### C. Socrates is a satire on Ramus

**[YATES]** Following John Durkan, Yates identifies Dicson’s pedantic Socrates with Peter Ramus: Ramists claimed to restore Socratic dialectic, while Dicson makes Socrates the enemy of higher Egyptian wisdom.

**Assessment:** plausible, not proved.

Dana Sutton notes that the portrait is not closely individualized. Much of Dicson’s attack comes from Aristophanes’ *Clouds*, from anti-Socratic commonplaces, and from objections to rationalism more generally. Perkins’s reaction makes an anti-Ramist application likely, but Dicson never writes “Socrates is Ramus.” [Philological Museum introduction](https://philological.cal.bham.ac.uk/dickson/intro.html).

### D. The controversy is “at bottom” religious

**[YATES]** Brunian occult memory opposes the morally and theologically sanitized order of Puritan Ramism.

**Assessment:** partly confirmed, partly over-totalized.

Perkins calls Dicson’s memory “impious,” objects to emotionally provocative images, criticizes Peter of Ravenna’s erotic mnemonic, and associates the art with scholastic barbarism. Religious and moral commitments plainly matter. But the controversy is also about:

- clarity of Latin;
- pedagogical economy;
- whether memory should follow the logical structure of subject matter;
- astrology’s status;
- money and public teaching;
- Ramist classification of the arts.

It need not be reduced to a confrontation between two coherent religions.

### E. Plat was not initiated into the mysteries

**[YATES]** Plat learned only the elementary technique.

**Assessment:** first clause plausible, initiation claim unverified. Plat’s account is practical, but no document identifies a secret curriculum.

## 2. Yates’s bibliographical observations

Yates accurately recognized:

- the 1583/early-1584 dating problem;
- the four pamphlets in the controversy;
- Basson’s 1597 reprints;
- Plat’s report;
- the Bowes letter identifying Dicson as “master of the art of memory” formerly attending Sidney.

She also wrote that she did not know why Basson reprinted the works. That remains a more responsible position than later claims that the printing proves membership in an occult fraternity.

## 3. John Durkan

John Durkan, “Alexander Dickson and S.T.C. 6823,” *The Bibliotheck* 3 (1962), pp. 183–190, was foundational for Yates. Durkan recovered state-paper evidence for Dicson’s later career and proposed the Ramist significance of the Socrates episode.

Durkan’s documentary work remains important, but some political inferences were later refined by Peter Beal.

## 4. Peter Beal and the documentary Dicson

Peter Beal’s research substantially enlarges Dicson’s life beyond Yates:

- “Alexander Dicsone, Elizabethan Philosopher, Propagandist, Spy: A Checklist of his Writings,” *The Library*, 7th ser., 2 (2001), pp. 120–130.
- “Philip Sidney’s Letter to Queen Elizabeth and that ‘False Knave’ Alexander Dicsone,” *English Manuscript Studies* 11 (2002), pp. 1–51.
- ODNB article on Alexander Dicsone.

The Catalogue of English Literary Manuscripts records autograph letters, political compilations, and books owned or inscribed by Dicson. [CELM introduction](https://celm.folger.edu/introductions/DicsoneAlexander.html); [CELM records](https://celm.folger.edu/authors/dicsonealexander.html).

Most strikingly:

- Dicson owned at least four books by Bruno;
- one bore a personal inscription from Bruno;
- surviving copies include Bruno’s works signed by Dicson;
- Dicson compiled or handled political manuscripts;
- his later career involved shifting Catholic, Scottish, and English political interests.

This provides firmer evidence for a personal Brunian connection than thematic similarity alone.

## 5. Bruno scholarship

### Rita Sturlese

Sturlese’s bibliographical and copy-specific work confirmed material links between Bruno and Dicson, including a newly identified Bruno autograph and Dicson provenance. This shifts the discussion from Yates’s intellectual pattern-recognition toward book history and documented contact.

Relevant works:

- Rita Sturlese, *Bibliografia, censimento e storia delle antiche stampe di Giordano Bruno*, Florence: Olschki, 1987.
- “Un nuovo autografo del Bruno,” *Rinascimento* 27 (1987), pp. 387–391.

### Hilary Gatti

Gatti accepts the importance of images in Bruno’s natural philosophy and notes Perkins’s attack on Dicson. Her work tends to treat Brunian memory as epistemological and scientific as well as magical: images connect thought to perceived natural phenomena. [Gatti, *Essays on Giordano Bruno* excerpt](https://dokumen.pub/essays-on-giordano-bruno-course-booknbsped-9781400836932.html).

### Stephen Clucas

Clucas’s work qualifies a purely Hermetic reading by locating Bruno’s memory in contemporary psychology, natural philosophy, language, and debates over imagination. This makes Dicson’s distinction between sensation, vision, thought, and central memory especially important.

### Giovanni Aquilecchia, Michele Ciliberto, and Bruno editions

Post-Yates Bruno scholarship has emphasized philological context, the internal development of Bruno’s philosophy, and the political-literary purposes of the Italian dialogues. It does not erase Hermetic sources but rejects using “Hermeticism” as a complete explanation.

Standard Bruno sources relevant to Dicson include:

- *De umbris idearum* (Paris, 1582);
- *Ars memoriae* appended to it;
- *Cantus Circaeus* (Paris, 1582);
- *Explicatio triginta sigillorum* (London, 1583);
- *Sigillus sigillorum* (London, 1583);
- *De la causa, principio et uno* (London, 1584);
- *La cena de le Ceneri* (London, 1584).

The Warburg Institute provides scans and bibliographical materials for Bruno and Yates. [Warburg Bruno resources](https://resources.warburg.sas.ac.uk/mnemosyne/Bruno/).

## 6. Mary Carruthers, Lina Bolzoni, Paolo Rossi, Jocelyn Penny Small

These scholars do not all treat Dicson extensively, but they correct the historical frame within which Yates placed him.

### Carruthers

Mary Carruthers, *The Book of Memory* and *The Craft of Thought*, demonstrates that medieval memoria was not merely storage. It was compositional, ethical, affective, and inventive. Dicson’s animated images therefore do not require an exclusively Brunian or magical explanation; they also continue a broad tradition of affective cognitive craft.

### Bolzoni

Lina Bolzoni’s *The Gallery of Memory* situates Renaissance memory images amid preaching, emblem, literary invention, galleries, and textual visualization. Dicson belongs to a culture in which mental images, rhetorical figures, and material images constantly interacted.

### Rossi

Paolo Rossi’s *Logic and the Art of Memory* places memory arts within projects for method, classification, combinatorics, and universal knowledge. The Dicson–Perkins dispute is thus not merely magic against religion; it is also image-based topical invention against Ramist logical order.

### Small

Jocelyn Penny Small’s *Wax Tablets of the Mind* warns against treating ancient memory theory as a single unchanging “method of loci.” Ancient testimony is fragmentary, and Renaissance authors reconstructed it creatively. This is particularly relevant to Dicson’s confident Egyptian and Druidic genealogy.

## 7. Walter Ong and Ramism

Walter J. Ong, *Ramus, Method, and the Decay of Dialogue* (1958), especially the account of Ramist printing and pedagogy, provides the essential background.

Ramist memory is not simply “natural memory.” It locates mnemonic help in logical division, dichotomous disposition, and the ordered structure of the matter itself. Perkins quotes or adapts Ramist statements that places and images are inept and that dialectical disposition alone teaches order.

Yates’s contrast between “emotionally aseptic” Ramist order and affectively charged images is memorable and often illuminating. It is also polemical: Ramist diagrams are themselves visual artifacts, and Protestant pedagogy did not abandon all imagery.

## 8. Scholarship listed in the requested template but not directly probative

The works of Hajdu, Sabbatino, Bonner, Fidora, Stagno, and much Camillo or Llull scholarship illuminate the broader history but do not furnish direct evidence about Dicson’s text.

- Hajdu is important for the prehistory and social status of medieval mnemonic arts.
- Sabbatino and Bolzoni clarify Camillo’s theater and literary context.
- Bonner and Fidora correct older accounts of Llull by emphasizing his demonstrative art, alphabetic figures, and theological logic rather than treating it as simply a mnemonic machine.
- Stagno’s work on galleries and collections helps contextualize spatial ordering.
- Carruthers and Small correct teleological narratives of a single unchanged art.
- Ong remains directly relevant because Perkins’s alternative is Ramist.

The absence of direct Dicson treatment should be stated rather than manufacturing a scholarly consensus from adjacent fields.

## History and context

## 1. Classical rhetoric

Dicson’s technical substrate is the classical art of memory:

- places function like wax tablets;
- images function like letters;
- order allows retrieval;
- extraordinary action makes an image adhere;
- visible representation can cue things or words.

The traditional line is:

```text
Simonides tradition
→ Cicero / Ad Herennium / Quintilian
→ medieval rhetoric, preaching, and devotion
→ Renaissance printed memory manuals
→ Bruno
→ Dicson and the Perkins controversy
```

Metrodorus’s zodiac supplies an especially suggestive ancient precedent, but no Metrodoran text survives. Renaissance claims to restore his “Scepsian” method are reconstructions.

## 2. Scholastic psychology

Dicson’s model presupposes the Aristotelian view that intellect normally receives material through sense and phantasm. Invisible or abstract things must be rendered perceptible by internal images.

His sequence of sense, vision, thought, and memory resonates with scholastic accounts of internal senses. Yet his terminology is idiosyncratic, and one should not force it into a single Thomist anatomical diagram.

## 3. Lullism and combinatorics

Dicson’s thirty letter-agents, categorical combinations, and hierarchical division exist in a culture transformed by Ramon Llull’s alphabetic figures.

However:

- **[NO DIRECT TEXTUAL PROOF]** Dicson does not name Llull in the technical chapters examined.
- **[RECONSTRUCTION]** Bruno’s combinatory arts transmitted Lullian habits into Dicson’s environment.
- **[CORRECTION TO OLDER HISTORIES]** Llull’s own art was devised as a demonstrative and missionary logic, not originally as a memory palace.

For current Llull bibliography and manuscript data, the appropriate authority is the Universitat de Barcelona’s Ramon Llull Database. [Ramon Llull Database](https://orbita.bib.ub.edu/llull/).

## 4. Hermeticism and Neoplatonism

Dicson draws on:

- the Platonic Theuth story;
- *Corpus Hermeticum* IV and XIII;
- probably CH XVI;
- the hierarchy of one, mind, soul, nature, light, shadows, vestiges;
- regeneration in the divine mixing bowl;
- Egyptian wisdom as prior to Greek disputation.

The Renaissance still generally accepted the Hermetic writings as extremely ancient. Isaac Casaubon’s redating of much of the Greek Hermetica to the post-Christian period came only in 1614.

Thus Dicson’s Egyptian genealogy was historically intelligible, though not historically correct.

## 5. Ramism and universities

Ramus’s method was especially influential in Protestant schools and at Cambridge. Its promise was that knowledge properly divided according to nature becomes memorable without a vast private stock of arbitrary images.

Perkins, a Cambridge theologian, attacks Dicson from this institutional setting. The dispute concerns which faculty owns order:

- Dicson: visual places, imagination, auxiliary numbering, and animation;
- Perkins: dialectical analysis and the intrinsic hierarchy of propositions.

## 6. Courts and patronage

Dicson dedicated both *De umbra rationis* and his *Defensio* to Robert Dudley, Earl of Leicester.

**[DOCUMENT]** Robert Bowes reported in 1592:

> “Dickson, master of the art of memory, and sometime attending on Mr. Philip Sidney, deceased, has come to court.”

This documents his reputation and some association with Sidney. It does not establish that Sidney was formally his student.

Bruno praised Dicson in the Italian dialogues as a learned, honorable, lovable, well-born, faithful friend, and made “Dicsono” a participant in *De la causa*.

## 7. Printing networks

Thomas Vautrollier was a Huguenot printer who also printed Ramist works. That he printed Dicson’s anti-Ramist texts shows that printer confessional identity did not mechanically determine every title.

Thomas Basson, an English printer established at Leiden, reprinted *Thamus* and the *Defensio* in 1597.

**[YATES]** Basson liked mysteries and may have been connected with the Family of Love.

**[DISPUTED]** Neither the reprint nor Basson’s later family publishing connections proves that Dicson’s books circulated through a continuous esoteric fraternity.

## People

## Alexander Dicson

**[DOCUMENT]** A Scot from the Errol area, probably born around 1558; educated at St Andrews and possibly on the Continent; in London by 1583; associated with Bruno, Leicester, and Sidney; later active in Scottish and international political business.

His name appears as:

- Dicson;
- Dicsone;
- Dickson;
- Dicsonus Arelius;
- Bruno’s Italian “Dicsono.”

By 1591 he declared himself Catholic. He served or negotiated for Francis Hay, Earl of Erroll, and carried politically sensitive correspondence. Later he worked in James VI’s interest, including on an unpublished succession tract.

The description “spy” is defensible as a shorthand for aspects of his activity, but “political agent of shifting allegiance” is more exact.

## Giordano Bruno

Bruno was not merely an inferred intellectual ancestor.

**[DOCUMENT]** Dicson owned Bruno books, at least one personally inscribed. Bruno praised him warmly and wrote him into an Italian dialogue. Direct instruction remains probable but is not documented in a surviving classroom record.

## Robert Dudley, Earl of Leicester

Patron named in the dedications. Whether Leicester paid for Dicson’s research or instruction is unknown.

## Philip Sidney

Bowes says Dicson had “attended” Sidney. That can indicate service, presence in a circle, or another dependent relationship. It does not by itself mean “student” or “patron.”

## Hugh Plat

Plat is the clearest witness to actual instruction. He translates Dicson’s obscure vocabulary into a usable recipe but also portrays the teaching as oversold.

## William Perkins / G. P.

Probably the Cambridge author of the attacks. His objection was moral, theological, epistemological, and pedagogical. He did not accuse Dicson in a surviving criminal proceeding; he accused the art in print.

## Thomas Watson

Watson’s *Compendium memoriae localis*, probably printed around 1585–1586, offers a clearer classical local memory. Its relationship to Dicson and Bruno is contextual rather than demonstrably that of disciple to master. A manuscript version survives in British Library **Sloane MS 3731** according to recent scholarship; Yates printed a different Sloane number in some discussions, and the catalogue should be checked before citation.

## Ioannes Adamus Lincolniensis

The commendatory speaker praises Dicson for restoring memory from “mad barbarism.” His precise identity and intention remain uncertain.

Dana Sutton provocatively reads this as praise for stripping away contemporary magical excess. That is an interpretive suggestion, not an established identification of Adams’s target.

## Thomas Basson

English printer at Leiden; reprinted both Dicson texts in 1597. His precise reasons are unknown.

## Martin Del Rio

Yates reports that Del Rio’s *Disquisitionum magicarum libri sex* (1600) commended Dicson’s *Thamus* as not lacking wit and acumen. This is noteworthy: a major Catholic demonologist could praise the dialogue without treating its mnemonic procedure as prohibited magic.

## Anecdotes: documented, misplaced, and absent

The supplied general template names “the auctioneer, the professor’s party, the student who lost his memory, the accused magician.” None belongs demonstrably to Dicson’s system.

### Documented for Dicson

- Plat took or observed lessons and later published the procedure.
- Dicson was publicly remembered as “master of the art of memory.”
- He was associated with Sidney.
- He owned Bruno books.
- An opponent accused him of obscurity, impiety, vanity, and profiteering.
- His method was allegedly useful for errands, conversation, and games.

### Not documented for Dicson

- No contemporary account says he performed an auctioneer’s feat.
- No identified professor’s-party anecdote is attached to him.
- No student is documented as losing natural memory through his lessons.
- No record has been found of Dicson being tried before an Inquisition as a magician.
- No source says his images physically moved, summoned spirits, or conferred supernatural recall.

These stories belong to other mnemonists, medieval exempla, or modern composite histories unless a specific witness can be produced.

## The magic question

## 1. Was the system magical?

The answer depends on the meaning of “magical.”

### As a practical mnemonic technique

**No necessary magic is present.**

Its operative acts are:

- visualization;
- ordered location;
- association;
- personification;
- exaggeration;
- emotional salience;
- repeated mental traversal.

No ritual timing, suffumigation, divine name, spirit invocation, talismanic consecration, planetary prayer, or astrological election is prescribed.

### As a cosmological and religious philosophy

**Yes, it is explicitly Hermetic and Neoplatonic in framing.**

Dicson speaks of:

- rays and higher powers;
- the One and God;
- mind over reason;
- ascent from matter;
- shadows, vestiges, and seals;
- Hermetic regeneration;
- Egyptian wisdom;
- zodiacal and lunar images.

Calling this “occult philosophy” is historically reasonable.

### As astral magic

**Not proved.**

The zodiac organizes numbered images. Dicson does not instruct the practitioner to capture planetary influence in a talisman. The system might coexist with astral assumptions, but mnemonic use of zodiacal order is not itself astral magic.

## 2. Perkins’s charge of impiety

**[TEXT]** Perkins’s title calls the memory *impia*. His objections include:

- arbitrary images displace the natural logical order of truth;
- violent, grotesque, or erotic images excite disorderly passions;
- Peter of Ravenna recommended a beloved woman as a mnemonic image;
- astrology is an inappropriate basis for a general art;
- the art smells of scholastic “barbarism” and “Dunsicality”;
- Dicson’s prose is obscure and commercially self-serving.

Yates is right that confessional and moral hostility mattered. Yet Perkins’s case is not proof that Dicson performed magic; it tells us what a Ramist Protestant opponent classified as impious.

## 3. Papistry and heresy

Dicson’s later Catholic profession and political service made suspicions of “papistry” biographically plausible. But:

- *De umbra rationis* does not proclaim Roman Catholic dogma;
- its Hermetic regeneration is not orthodox Catholic sacramental theology;
- Vautrollier, its printer, was a Huguenot;
- no ecclesiastical condemnation of the book has been located;
- no inquisitorial prosecution of Dicson for this art is documented.

## 4. Ars notoria

The *Ars notoria* promises infused knowledge through prayers, divine names, ritual observance, and inspection of *notae*. Dicson’s art differs fundamentally.

Shared features:

- inward visualization;
- claims about perfected memory;
- sacral or cosmic language.

Absent from Dicson:

- prescribed liturgical cycles;
- angelic invocation;
- ritual fasting;
- notory prayers;
- guaranteed supernatural infusion of the liberal arts.

Connecting the two is a broad history-of-magic comparison, not evidence that *De umbra rationis* transmits the *Ars notoria*.

## 5. Consequences

The known consequences were literary and reputational:

- a four-part pamphlet controversy;
- charges of impiety and vanity;
- Dicson’s public defense;
- later repetition of the debate by Plat, Del Rio, Yates, and historians.

There is no documented imprisonment, excommunication, or prosecution arising from the memory treatise.

## Afterlife

## 1. Immediate reception

The best evidence of reception is:

- Perkins’s rapid attacks in 1584;
- Dicson’s *Defensio*;
- Plat’s 1594 report;
- Basson’s two 1597 reprints;
- ownership of *Thamus* by learned collectors.

Yates reports that Henry Percy, ninth Earl of Northumberland, owned a copy. Edinburgh University’s copy later stood among memory-related books associated with William Drummond’s intellectual environment, though it is reportedly not itself part of the core Drummond collection.

“Considerable circulation” is possible, but four polemical texts and a reprint do not establish mass readership.

## 2. Later British memory arts

Dicson and Plat form part of the transition to seventeenth-century English manuals:

- William Basse, *A Help to Memory and Discourse* (1620);
- John Willis, *Mnemonica* or *The Art of Memory*;
- Henry Herdson, *Ars memoriae* (1651);
- Richard Saunders;
- Marius D’Assigny.

Direct borrowing from Dicson must be demonstrated case by case. Much of what these authors share comes independently from the classical place-and-image tradition.

## 3. Emblems, galleries, and literature

Dicson’s technique belongs to the same visual culture as:

- emblem books;
- mythographical handbooks;
- personified virtues and vices;
- painted galleries;
- iconological attributes;
- allegorical pageants.

The relation is cultural and structural. No surviving fresco or emblem plate has been identified as an illustration of *De umbra rationis*.

## 4. Dante, Camillo, and the frescoes

These are important to the general art of memory but should not be claimed as Dicson’s direct sources without evidence.

- Dante’s ordered otherworld and vivid punishments can be read mnemonically; Dicson does not cite Dante.
- Giulio Camillo’s theater organizes knowledge in graded, planetary spaces; Dicson neither describes a theater nor reproduces Camillo’s seven grades.
- Dominican fresco cycles use ordered personifications; Dicson’s figures share that iconographic world, but no direct visual borrowing has been traced.

## 5. Borges, Eco, and literary memory

Borges’s Funes possesses overwhelming involuntary particular memory—the opposite of an ordered selective art. Eco’s semiotic and combinatorial fictions revisit libraries, encyclopedias, signs, and universal languages. These are illuminating retrospective comparisons, not documented receptions of Dicson.

## 6. Modern memory palaces and athletes

Modern competitors routinely use:

- ordered routes;
- person-action-object scenes;
- exaggeration;
- violence, humor, and erotic salience;
- numerical peg systems.

These resemble Dicson’s places, auxiliaries, modified shadows, and animation. There is no need to posit direct descent: both draw on durable associative principles.

A responsible formulation is “structural continuity,” not “modern athletes use Dicson’s system.”

## 7. Computing and combinatorics

The broad intellectual genealogy often proposed is:

```text
Llull’s finite alphabets and combinatory figures
→ Renaissance Lullism and Bruno
→ universal-method and universal-language projects
→ Leibniz’s ars combinatoria
→ formal logic and mechanized symbolic calculation
```

Dicson is a minor witness inside that history because his thirty elements, categorical hierarchy, and recombination show how mnemonic images could become symbolic operators.

But:

- he designed no calculating machine;
- his method is not binary;
- no line of direct influence from *De umbra rationis* to Leibniz has been demonstrated;
- “Dicson anticipated the computer” is a **[MODERN INVENTION]**.

## Controversies and disputes

## 1. Dicson, Dicsone, or Dickson?

All forms occur. “Dicson” follows the Latin title and Yates; “Dicsone” is common in archival scholarship; “Dickson” is the modern form. They designate the same person.

## 2. Errol and *Arelius*

*Arelius* is conventionally read as “of Errol.” This is well established, though the exact relationship between birthplace, family origin, and territorial designation should be handled carefully.

## 3. 1583 or 1584?

- Title page: 1583.
- Dedication: 1 January.
- Modern bibliographers: 1583/84 or early 1584.
- Best citation: “London, 1583 [probably issued early 1584 new style].”

## 4. Date of Plat

The correct publication date is **1594**. The supplied 1592 date is not supported by the surviving edition.

## 5. Date of Dicson’s death

Older accounts say “about 1604.” William Fowler reported the death on 11 October 1603, while Thomas Murray’s memorial verses appeared in 1604. The safest formulation is “died in 1603; commemorated in print in 1604,” unless a document proves the report premature.

## 6. Was “Heius Scepsius” Dicson?

The attribution is accepted by catalogues and fits the self-defense, dedication, and Scepsian mnemonic identity. It is highly probable.

## 7. Was G. P. William Perkins?

Probable current attribution, not an explicit title-page identification. Older Gerard Peeters attributions and anonymous cataloguing explain why some records remain cautious.

## 8. Was Dicson Bruno’s student?

- **Documented:** personal association, praise, inscribed books, intellectual dependence.
- **Probable reconstruction:** Bruno taught or directly influenced him.
- **Not documented:** a dated lesson, payment, curriculum, or declaration “I studied memory under Bruno.”

“Disciple” is safe intellectually; “formal pupil” is less secure.

## 9. Is *De umbra rationis* merely an abridgment of *De umbris idearum*?

No.

It borrows Bruno’s philosophical lexicon and several mnemonic structures, but it:

- recasts them in a dialogue with Thamus and Socrates;
- foregrounds Plato’s critique of writing;
- develops its own subject/auxiliary distinction;
- gives its own political, relational, and causal examples;
- lacks Bruno’s full wheel apparatus and decan repertory.

It is derivative but not a simple epitome.

## 10. Does the text prescribe decan images?

Not explicitly. It says the signs have proper images and assigns lunar-mansion images as group leaders. Yates’s comparison with Bruno’s decans is plausible but cannot supply Dicson’s missing repertory.

## 11. Does it contain diagrams?

No surviving diagram has been identified. Statements that Dicson’s book “shows” wheels generally confuse it with Bruno’s illustrated works.

## 12. Is the zodiac magical?

It may be cosmologically meaningful, but its explicit technical function is numerical and ordinal. Astrology is part of the controversy because Perkins treats its use as false or illicit; the text does not prescribe operative astrology.

## 13. Is Socrates definitely Ramus?

No. It is a strong contextual reading first promoted through Durkan and Yates, reinforced by Perkins’s response, but never made explicit by Dicson.

## 14. Was Dicson a Catholic secret agent using memory for espionage?

- Catholic and politically active: documented.
- Political intermediary or intelligence agent: documented in broad terms.
- Memory as useful to an agent: obvious possibility.
- Use of this system to memorize secret dispatches: **no direct evidence**.

The last claim is an attractive modern reconstruction that should not be narrated as fact.

## 15. Family of Love, Rosicrucianism, and Freemasonry

Yates and later writers linked Basson’s environment, Hermetic memory, Familist networks, Rosicrucian publications, and early Scottish Masonry.

Evidence exists for overlapping printers, patrons, books, and intellectual interests. Evidence does not establish:

- Dicson’s membership in the Family of Love;
- a Rosicrucian order in 1583 teaching his system;
- a continuous lodge transmission from Bruno through Dicson;
- Dicson as founder of speculative Freemasonry.

These are disputed genealogies, often expanded in modern esoteric literature.

## 16. Did Del Rio approve a magical art?

Del Rio’s praise of the *Thamus* for wit does not mean he endorsed every doctrine or recognized it as licit magic. It may refer to the anti-Socratic dialogue or its ingenuity.

## 17. What Yates got wrong or overstated

1. She makes a plausible Bruno relationship sound mechanically complete.
2. She tends to merge *adiuvantia* with mnemonic images generally, whereas Dicson gives auxiliaries a distinct stabilizing and ordinal role.
3. She speaks as if Dicson’s zodiac necessarily reproduces Bruno’s astral images; the text is less explicit.
4. Her statement that Plat was not initiated into mysteries is unsupported.
5. Her speculation about underground Hermetic groups exceeds the evidence.
6. Her religious polarization of Brunian and Ramist memory is illuminating but too binary.
7. “Considerable circulation” is possible but not quantified.
8. The political-agent evidence does not prove that mnemonic instruction served espionage.
9. As in her larger Bruno thesis, she relied heavily on the Renaissance belief in an ancient Egyptian Hermes. Post-Casaubon historical dating and post-Yates scholarship distinguish Renaissance reception from the actual late-antique origin of the Hermetica.

## 18. What Yates got decisively right

1. Dicson is indispensable evidence for Bruno’s immediate British reception.
2. The terminology and philosophy of shadows are plainly Brunian.
3. The treatise combines classical memory rules with Hermetic language.
4. The Perkins controversy reveals that artificial memory could be a confessional and moral flashpoint.
5. Plat’s report proves practical oral teaching.
6. The Theuth/Thamus dialogue deliberately contrasts exterior inscription with inner mnemonic writing.
7. The zodiac and mythological inventor-images connect Dicson with Bruno’s mnemonic world even though the systems are not identical.

## Open questions

1. Has anyone completed a full census and textual collation of all surviving 1583 copies?
2. Do copies contain stop-press corrections or variant gatherings not captured in Sutton’s transcription?
3. Is *Thamus* a page-for-page reprint, a revised text, or a selectively reset version? A formal collation remains desirable.
4. Does “consideratio prima” imply a lost second consideration?
5. What exact image repertory did Dicson mean by the “proper images” of signs, planets, lunar dragon, and lunar mansions?
6. Was that repertory taught orally but withheld from print?
7. What precisely was the “golden chain” connecting stations?
8. Can the thirty letter-agents be matched systematically to a Brunian table?
9. Can surviving copies owned by Dicson identify annotations relevant to his mnemonic practice?
10. Did Sidney actually receive instruction, or did Dicson merely attend him in another capacity?
11. Can G. P.’s attribution to Perkins be proved through type, paper, archival payment, or verbal parallels?
12. Who was Ioannes Adamus Lincolniensis?
13. Why did Basson reprint both works in 1597?
14. Did Plat learn directly from Dicson, attend a public demonstration, or receive the procedure through an intermediary?
15. Are there unpublished Plat notebooks recording the lessons?
16. Did Dicson use memory in political activity? No operational record has yet been identified.
17. Did any seventeenth-century author explicitly cite *De umbra rationis* as a technical source?
18. Is Del Rio’s favorable phrase directed to the mnemonic doctrine, the dialogue, or Dicson’s polemical wit?
19. Can the claims connecting Dicson to early Scottish Masonry be supported by evidence earlier than modern historical reconstruction?
20. Are any promised or projected writings implied by the abrupt dialogue preserved anonymously in manuscript?

## The system in detail

## 1. Form and architecture of the book

**[TEXT]** The first edition consists of:

1. a dedication to Robert Dudley, Earl of Leicester;
2. an address by Dicson to the reader;
3. a commendatory address by “Ioannes Adamus Lincolniensis”;
4. a short dialogue among Thamus, Mercurius, and Theutates;
5. a much longer dialogue between Thamus and Socrates;
6. Thamus’s systematic discourse on memory in twenty-two chapters.

The title calls the work a *prosopopoeia*, a personification or speech placed in another’s mouth. The surviving text looks like selected sections of a larger dialogue: it begins abruptly in conversation, and after Thamus’s discourse it ends without a resumed dramatic conclusion. The proposition that portions of a larger work have been omitted is a reasonable **[RECONSTRUCTION]**, not a statement by Dicson.

The chapters of the technical discourse are:

1. *Oblivio, memoria*  
2. *De memoriae causis*  
3. *De memoriae subiecto*  
4. *De arte*  
5. [on preparation/providence: the chapter heading is imperfect or unstable in modern transcription]  
6. *De subiectis*  
7. *De subiecto absoluto*  
8. *De adiuvante*  
9. *In prolepsi*

## 2. What memory is

**[TEXT: Chapter I, first edition pp. 35–36 approximately]** Dicson defines forgetting morally and cosmologically:

> *Oblivio nequitia est impotentis animi, atque demissi, eademque materiae soboles prima…*

“Forgetfulness is the depravity of a powerless and abject mind, the first offspring of matter.”

Memory, by contrast, is:

> *potentia quidem illa, et actus, et umbra, remigium … alarum animi*

“a power, an act, and a shadow—the oarage of the soul’s wings.”

This is not merely an empirical psychology. **[TEXT]** Dicson connects memory with the soul’s passage from matter and darkness toward light. Yet the actual mnemonic prescriptions that follow are conventional enough to be practiced without accepting that metaphysics.

**[YATES]** Yates treats this language as evidence that the mnemonic image is a metaphysical shadow of divine ideas.

**[QUALIFICATION]** Dicson certainly makes light, shadow, vestige, horizon, and seal into philosophical terms. But his technical definition of an *umbra* is simply a mentally fashioned visible representation assigned to a place. The metaphysical and practical meanings coexist; it does not follow that each mnemonic image was a talisman or astral vehicle.

## 3. The causes and alleged origin of memory

**[TEXT: Chapter II; first edition p. 37]** Dicson distinguishes three sources of mnemonic power:

- nature;
- powers or influences “from above”;
- doctrine and reason, which awaken and strengthen latent nature.

He writes:

> *licet omnis a Deo et uno procedat, in triplici tamen nunc esto varietate. Nunc enim naturae parentis reputamus officium. Nunc superum radios et efficacias sentimus, nunc rationis et doctrinae consilia succurrunt.*

“Although all proceeds from God and the One, let it now be considered in a threefold variety: the work of parent Nature; the rays and powers of the higher beings; and the counsels of reason and teaching.”

The word *coniuratio* follows in Dicson’s discussion. In context it means a conjunction or sworn alliance of natural and taught powers, not necessarily ritual conjuration.

### Egypt, Simonides, and the Druids

Dicson rejects the usual Greek origin story:

> *Imo Chius ille primus eam invenisse et protulisse creditur. At falsum… Quod si separetur etiam Aegiptus, nihil tamen effecerit ille. Et enim si Celtarum replicentur annales… multo ante in Druidum schola viguisse reperias.*

“That man of Chios is believed to have first discovered and published it. But this is false … If Egypt be separated from it, he will have accomplished nothing. For if the annals of the Celts are unfolded … you will find that it flourished much earlier in the school of the Druids.”

“Chius” means Simonides of Ceos, not Chios strictly speaking; the phrasing is geographically loose.

- **[TEXT]** Dicson claims Egyptian and Druidic priority.
- **[TRADITION]** Renaissance prisca-theologia narratives routinely assigned arts to primordial sages.
- **[NO EVIDENCE]** No Egyptian manual of the classical place-and-image technique is known, nor is there documentary evidence that Druids practiced Dicson’s system.
- **[YATES]** “If it is separated from Egypt it can effect nothing” becomes a central indication of occult Egyptian memory.
- **[CORRECTION]** It is evidence for Dicson’s origin myth and ideological framing, not evidence about the actual ancient origin of artificial memory.

## 4. The psychological model

**[TEXT: Chapters III, XIX]** Memory belongs to embodied, temporal beings. Celestial souls do not remember discursively because they know without the temporal absence that makes recollection necessary. Human beings, by contrast, experience time and require memory.

Dicson later offers a concentric cognitive model:

> *Habet etenim cerebrum suam sphaeram, et distinctos orbes, sensum, visionem, cogitationem, memoriam. Atque haec quidem in centro est, in horizonte vero sensuum instrumenta.*

“The brain has its sphere and its distinct circles: sensation, vision, thought, and memory. Memory is at the center, while the instruments of sense are at the horizon.”

Nothing reaches the center except through the intervening circles. This is a conceptual diagram expressed verbally; the book supplies no drawn cerebral sphere.

The stages are approximately:

```text
external object
→ sensory instrument at the “horizon”
→ sensation
→ internal visualization
→ thought/imaginative shaping
→ memory at the center
```

**[RECONSTRUCTION]** This combines Aristotelian-scholastic internal-sense psychology with Neoplatonic language. Dicson does not provide a textbook mapping to the usual *sensus communis–imaginatio–cogitativa–memoria* faculties, but that background makes his account intelligible.

## 5. What serves as places: *subiecta*

**[TEXT: Chapters VI–VII]**

> *Est ergo subiectum sinus et receptaculum umbrarum.*

“A subject is a hollow or receptacle of shadows.”

A *subiectum* is therefore the mnemonic background conventionally called a *locus*. Dicson distinguishes:

- **absolute or principal subjects**: stable things made by nature or human hands;
- **assisting or auxiliary subjects**: added by visualization, capable of movement, used both to support animation and to mark number and order.

Possible larger fields for absolute subjects include:

- the universe;
- the earth;
- a containing environment;
- a polity;
- a household and its parts.

In practical operation, however, the examples are architectural: an intercolumniation can contain a sword; distinct spaces are arranged at regular intervals.

### Rules for selecting subjects

**[TEXT: Chapter X]** Dicson’s rules reproduce the classical tradition with his own terminology:

1. **They must be sensible or capable of a sensible form.**  
   One cannot place or retrieve what cannot be mentally pictured.

2. **They must be neither dazzlingly bright nor excessively dark.**

   > *nec nimis illustria … nec vehementer etiam obscura sunto*

3. **They should be moderately sized.**  
   A place too large will not hold a determinate image; one too narrow seems to repel it.

4. **Intervals must be moderate and approximately equal or parallel.**

5. **The intervening areas should be mentally suppressed.**  
   This prevents accidental wandering between places.

6. **The subjects must differ in form and nature.**  
   Similar places cause mnemonic confusion.

7. **Their sequence must be faultless.**  
   The user must know what occupies every ordinal position.

8. **They are divided into groups or “tribes.”**

9. **Each receives an auxiliary guard or marker.**

These closely parallel *Ad Herennium* III.29–32 and Quintilian XI.2: moderate size, adequate light, distinctiveness, intervals, and fixed order. Yates’s statement that Dicson repeats the Ad Herennian rules is therefore substantially confirmed.

## 6. Auxiliary images: *adiuvantia*

**[TEXT: Chapters VII–IX]** Dicson’s terminology is more precise than Yates’s shorthand sometimes implies. An *adiuvans* is not simply another name for every mnemonic image. It is an auxiliary element attached to an absolute place, used:

- to multiply or differentiate the place;
- to indicate its number and order;
- to provide an instrument with which the principal mnemonic figure can act;
- to prompt recovery if the principal shadow fails to appear.

Example:

> *Esto igitur in intercolumnio gladius.*

“Let there be, then, a sword in the space between the columns.”

The sword remains associated with that station. Different figures can then use it:

- a ruler threatens seditionists with it;
- Alledius hands it out while arming slaves;
- a personification of natural self-defense extends it;
- innovators pay the penalty by it;
- Astraea bears it as her attribute.

This is an important mechanism. A fixed accessory stabilizes the locus, while a variable agent performs the action required by the thing being memorized.

Dicson calls all such auxiliaries “mathematical” not because they are immaterial geometrical entities, but because they signify the number and order of the subjects.

## 7. What serves as images: *umbrae*

**[TEXT: Chapter XI]**

> *Est igitur umbra efficta rei species credita subiecto, ad firmam illa quidem perceptionem assumpta.*

“A shadow is a fashioned appearance of a thing entrusted to a subject, adopted for its firm apprehension.”

The mnemonic chain is:

```text
thing to remember
→ fashioned visible species (“shadow”)
→ assigned to a subject/place
→ sharpened by thought and action
→ retrieved through the place’s order
```

The shadow is not the eternal form in the divine mind; that is light. Nor is it the celestial form or the external object itself. It is the representation polished by sense and thought.

### Simple shadows

**[TEXT: Chapter XII]** A simple shadow represents a visible thing directly:

> *homo hominem, Alledius Alledium, et beluam belua designat*

“A man signifies a man, Alledius Alledius, and an animal an animal.”

Thus, if a known person is to be remembered, imagine that person. If an animal is to be remembered, picture it directly.

### Modified shadows

**[TEXT: Chapters XIII–XV]** Abstract, invisible, auditory, relational, or conceptual material requires a constructed or transferred image. Dicson calls this *conformatio*, *modificatio*, or *inflexio*.

The transfer is functional rather than ornamental. Unlike a decorous rhetorical metaphor, a mnemonic image can be extreme because its purpose is recovery, not elegance.

Methods include:

- **cause for effect or effect for cause**;
- **inventor or practitioner for an art**;
- **attribute for institution or condition**;
- **opposite for opposite**;
- **associate for associate**;
- **part for whole**;
- **species for genus**;
- **analogy or allegory**;
- **sound and word association**.

### Dicson’s worked examples

**[TEXT: Chapters XV–XVI]**

#### Inventors and arts

> *Theutates, pyromantiam Vulcanus, hydromantiam Nereus, ortum et occasum Apollo, eamque quae chirurgia dicitur Chiron, hinc bella et molitiones Minerva, providentiam Prometheus, Epimetheus poenitentiam praestare possit.*

In gloss:

- Theutates represents mathematics and letters;
- Vulcan represents pyromancy;
- Nereus represents hydromancy;
- Apollo represents rising and setting;
- Chiron represents surgery—not medicine in general in this exact passage;
- Minerva represents wars and military works;
- Prometheus represents foresight;
- Epimetheus represents hindsight or repentance.

**[YATES]** These are “traces of Bruno’s list of inventors.”

**[QUALIFICATION]** The correspondence is strong, especially in the use of mythic inventors as categorical image-agents. But inventor-images were not uniquely Bruno’s creation, and Dicson’s selection is shorter and partly different. Direct dependence on *De umbris idearum* is very likely; exact one-to-one copying is not demonstrated.

#### Effects as vestiges of an invisible cause

An unseen God is represented through the created order:

> *Quis oculos tornavit? Quis nares perfodit? Quis nervos produxit et alligavit? … nisi qui non videtur Deus? Ecce ut ipsum infinitum, incomprehensum ab effectis ostenditur.*

“Who fashioned the eyes? Who bored the nostrils? Who drew out and joined the nerves? … Who but the unseen God? Thus the infinite and incomprehensible is indicated from effects.”

Dicson then says that God, eternity, world, time, generation, gods, and agents can be recovered:

> *quasi ex vestigiis et sigillis*

“as if from vestiges and seals.”

This phrase supports Yates’s metaphysical reading, but it occurs in a discussion of effect-to-cause imagery; *sigilla* here need not mean magical seals.

#### Political constitutions

- a ruler consulting the common good represents kingship;
- one who does not represents tyranny;
- a virtuous few represent aristocracy;
- a vicious few represent oligarchy;
- the people united for common utility represent polity;
- the people acting against patricians represent democracy.

#### Accidents and conventional signs

- the swallow or bean-sowing represents spring;
- the toga represents peace;
- arms and weapons represent war.

#### Opposites and correlatives

- a robber can evoke a parricide;
- heirs evoke a testator;
- the person causing loss evokes the sufferer;
- wife evokes husband;
- father-in-law evokes son-in-law;
- tenant evokes landlord;
- Anaxagoras evokes Pericles;
- adulterer evokes Hippolytus;
- slave evokes freedman;
- hot evokes cold;
- prodigal evokes thrifty;
- prudent evokes fool;
- prosperous evokes unfortunate;
- blind evokes seeing;
- sober evokes drunken.

The opposite is sometimes made memorable through ridicule.

#### Analogy and allegory

- a deeply rooted tree that cannot be shaken represents virtue;
- a storm-battered ship represents a distressed commonwealth;
- storms and whirlwinds represent dangers and disasters;
- gold tested in fire represents the maxim that fidelity is tested by adverse fortune;
- nuptial flutes represent marriage;
- a troop represents cavalry;
- roof, walls, and fortifications represent a house or city;
- Mercury, Aesculapius, Prometheus, and Chiron can stand for wise persons.

## 8. Numerical and zodiacal order

**[TEXT: Chapter XVI; first edition pp. 69 ff.]**

The zodiac is introduced as a system for numbering positions:

> *numerorum exhibitio quaeritur … eius primum, in decurias quae ex Zodiaci signorum ordine reputentur, esto divisio.*

“Numerical representation is sought … let there first be a division into tens reckoned according to the order of the signs of the Zodiac.”

Procedure:

1. Assign one decade to each zodiacal sign.
2. Divide each sign into ten distinct image-positions.
3. Twelve signs × ten positions = **120**.
4. Apply the same procedure to the planets and the image of the lunar dragon to reach **200**.
5. Use these positions to remember the numbers of books, chapters, and comparable ordered material.

Dicson anticipates the objection that a crab or fishes do not naturally supply ten clear subdivisions:

> *quasi vero cancrum pro Cancro, pro Piscibus pisces effingi velim.*

“As though I wished a crab to be fashioned for Cancer or fishes for Pisces.”

Instead, one must use the signs’ “proper images” and vary them.

### What “proper images” means

**[DISPUTED]** Yates inferred that Dicson alludes to the traditional astrological images associated with the signs and lunar mansions, though he omits Bruno’s explicit decan lists. This is plausible because:

- Dicson says his readers know what and how many these images are;
- he mentions zodiac, planets, lunar dragon, and lunar-mansion images;
- the system is numerically close to zodiacal mnemonic traditions attributed to Metrodorus.

But the surviving text neither enumerates nor illustrates the images. We cannot reconstruct Dicson’s complete set with certainty.

### Was it a wheel?

- **[TEXT]** No wheel is prescribed in *De umbra rationis*.
- **[TEXT]** No movable volvelles survive in either printing examined through catalogues and digital witnesses.
- **[YATES/RECONSTRUCTION]** The system is related to Bruno’s rotating-wheel systems because its categories and terminology derive from *Shadows*.
- **[CORRECTION]** Calling Dicson’s own system “a set of Brunian wheels” is unwarranted. Its explicitly described order can be maintained as a linear or grouped sequence.

## 9. Word memory

**[TEXT: Chapter XVII]** Dicson favors remembering things rather than exact wording, citing the Horatian principle that words follow a well-mastered subject. Nevertheless, he provides devices for verbal memory.

One method assigns thirty letters or elements to thirty known persons, with each person’s characteristic effects and attributes. Combinations of persons and attributes construct syllables or words. Gesture can indicate fourth or fifth elements in a compound.

He also proposes separate bodies for letters, with five accompanying consonantal configurations and additional effects or attributes for liquids and finals. The details are compressed, and Dicson tells each practitioner to prepare a personally suitable repertory.

**[RECONSTRUCTION]** This resembles Bruno’s combinatory alphabets but lacks Bruno’s full tabulation and wheels. It is best understood as a flexible personal visual alphabet rather than a reconstructable universal code.

## 10. The law of shadows

**[TEXT: Chapter XVIII]** Rules for images include:

- first seek a visually apprehensible form;
- keep the object at its natural scale if possible;
- otherwise modify it through attributes;
- choose among the many possible shadows according to opportunity;
- keep images dissimilar;
- revise any image that resembles another too closely;
- subordinate elegance to mnemonic effectiveness.

Dicson’s rules resemble the classical advice that images must be distinct, exceptional, and readily visible. Unlike *Ad Herennium*, he does not reproduce the famous lawsuit image as his own worked example.

### The lawsuit image

The user’s template asks for the *Ad Herennium* lawsuit image. It belongs to the ancestor system, not to Dicson’s text. In *Rhetorica ad Herennium* III.33, a prosecution involving poisoning, inheritance, and witnesses is compressed into a bedside tableau: the accused holds a cup in one hand, tablets in the other, and a ram’s testicles on a finger to cue the witnesses (*testes*). Dicson does not quote or adapt that tableau. Its relevance is structural: multiple details are condensed into one extraordinary active scene.

## 11. Method and hierarchy

**[TEXT: Chapter XX]** Order is established hierarchically:

> *una si possit idea statuatur, quae mox in partes communes sit distribuenda. Hae rursus, in alias minus communes, illa, in proprias, propriae tandem in tribus secernantur.*

“Let one idea, if possible, be established; then divide it into common parts, these again into less common parts, those into proper parts, and finally separate the proper parts into tribes.”

The pattern is:

```text
one governing idea
├── general divisions
│   ├── less-general divisions
│   │   ├── particular divisions
│   │   │   └── tribes / groups of stations
```

Additional third and tenth differentiations can be added with auxiliaries and a “golden chain.”

**[TEXT]** The phrase *aurea catena* appears, but Dicson gives no diagram explaining it.

**[RECONSTRUCTION]** It may mean a continuous chain of ordinal auxiliary markers linking one station to the next.

**[CAUTION]** It should not automatically be identified with Homer’s cosmic golden chain, a magical chain of spirits, or a later occult “association chain.”

## 12. Animation

**[TEXT: Chapter XXI]** This is the decisive act:

> *Primum igitur ne dormiatur in umbris.*

“First, then, let there be no sleeping among the shadows.”

The images should display:

- sharp or violent deeds;
- conspicuous circumstances;
- exaggeration;
- forceful motion;
- strong emotional effects;
- transformation of the observer into the matter and of the matter into itself;
- the apparent life and breathing of the inanimate.

Dicson concludes:

> *ea praesertim [solertia], quae efficit, ut inanima quae sunt, quasi vivere et spirare videantur.*

“Especially admirable is the skill that makes inanimate things appear to live and breathe.”

This is not a marginal flourish: animation supplies the force that drives images from thought into memory.

## 13. Retrieval

Dicson never gives a single numbered retrieval demonstration comparable to “walk to locus 1, decode A; locus 2, decode B.” The operation nevertheless follows clearly from his definitions.

**[RECONSTRUCTION, strongly text-based]:**

1. Establish a stable series of subjects.
2. Know their order and grouping independently of the temporary content.
3. Mark stations with fixed auxiliaries.
4. Convert the material into simple or modified shadows.
5. Make the shadows act with or upon the auxiliaries.
6. During recall, survey the subjects in order.
7. Observe each animated scene.
8. Decode direct likeness, cause, effect, attribute, opposite, associate, part, allegory, letter, or number.
9. If the main image fails, use the fixed auxiliary and its consequences as a secondary cue.

That the order could be traversed backward as well as forward is standard in classical artificial memory, but Dicson does not state it explicitly in the surviving passage. It should not be attributed to him without qualification.

## 1. Correct bibliography

The item-specific brief gives “Platt 1592, pp. 81–83.” The extant book is:

The Folger record identifies the 1594 printing, STC 19991.5; the Cambridge anthology likewise dates and locates the passage in 1594. [Folger catalogue](https://catalog.folger.edu/record/408450); [Cambridge critical anthology entry](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/memory-arts-in-renaissance-england/hugh-plat-the-jewel-house-of-art-and-nature-1594/7438D33ACFBCBAAE008DCF79C07A2413).

No verified 1592 edition of *The Jewell House* has been found. **The 1592 date in the supplied catalogue entry is an error**, perhaps confused with the date of Bowes’s report on Dicson.

## 2. Plat’s description

Plat’s heading reads:

> “The Art of Memory which Master Dickson the Scot did teach of late years in England, and whereof he hath written a figurative and obscure treatise, set down briefly and in plain terms according to his own demonstration, with the special uses thereof.”

**[TEXT]** Plat says places are prepared in groups of ten. Objects or figures are attached to them and made lively. His examples of convenient place-images include ordinary, gross, or comic objects such as a dunghill, tub, night-gown, and ape.

He describes the process as:

> “to animate the umbras or ideas rerum memorandarum. But herein everie man may best please his owne witte and memorie.”

That is, “to animate the shadows or ideas of the things to be remembered.”

One exemplary image is:

> “Bellona staring with her fierie eies and portraied in all points according to the usual description of the Poets.”

The fiery-eyed war goddess represents martial material by a conventional poetic personification made emotionally vivid.

## 3. Uses claimed by Plat

**[TEXT/REPORT]** Plat treats the art pragmatically. It can help with:

- errands;
- matters arising in conversation or at table;
- ordered lists;
- possibly cards or games;
- retaining miscellaneous business.

He also complains that the practical result did not match the “great and swelling art” under which it had been advertised or sold.

**[YATES]** Plat received only a “simple form of the straight mnemotechnic” and was not initiated into Dicson’s Hermetic mysteries.

**[CORRECTION]** The first half is plausible; the second is an argument from silence. Plat may have ignored, rejected, misunderstood, or never been taught Dicson’s metaphysics. Nothing documents a secret higher initiation from which he was excluded.

## 4. Relationship between book and oral teaching

The oral system agrees closely with the treatise:

| Plat | Dicson |
|---|---|
| Places in tens | decuries; tenfold division |
| Vivid personifications | simple and modified shadows |
| Fixed ordinary objects | auxiliaries attached to subjects |
| “Animate the umbras” | Chapter XXI, *De animatione* |
| Personal choice of images | each person prepares suitable persons, bodies, effects, and attributes |
| Practical list memory | books, chapters, matters, words, numbers |

Thus Plat is a valuable independent witness that Dicson publicly taught an operational art rather than merely publishing metaphysical allegory.

## Yates’s account and scholarship since

## 1. Yates’s reading

Yates makes five principal claims.

### A. Dicson is an ardent disciple of Bruno

**[YATES]** *De umbra rationis* is a “close imitation” of Bruno’s *De umbris idearum*.

**Assessment:** substantially confirmed.

Evidence includes:

- Bruno’s terminology of shadows;
- mythological inventor-images;
- zodiacal organization;
- “vestiges” and “seals”;
- the contrast between inner writing and external letters;
- the Hermetic framework;
- Bruno’s praise of Dicson and use of “Dicsono” as an interlocutor.

But the technical core also descends directly from classical and Renaissance local-memory manuals.

### B. The imagery is an occult route toward divine light

Yates writes that Dicson’s image is a shadow of divine light sought through shadows, vestiges, and seals.

**Assessment:** textually grounded but rhetorically intensified.

Dicson explicitly distinguishes primordial mental light from generated mnemonic shadows and speaks of God through effects, vestiges, and seals. That supports a Neoplatonic-Hermetic epistemology. It does not prove that every image was intended to exert occult force.

### C. Socrates is a satire on Ramus

**[YATES]** Following John Durkan, Yates identifies Dicson’s pedantic Socrates with Peter Ramus: Ramists claimed to restore Socratic dialectic, while Dicson makes Socrates the enemy of higher Egyptian wisdom.

**Assessment:** plausible, not proved.

Dana Sutton notes that the portrait is not closely individualized. Much of Dicson’s attack comes from Aristophanes’ *Clouds*, from anti-Socratic commonplaces, and from objections to rationalism more generally. Perkins’s reaction makes an anti-Ramist application likely, but Dicson never writes “Socrates is Ramus.” [Philological Museum introduction](https://philological.cal.bham.ac.uk/dickson/intro.html).

### D. The controversy is “at bottom” religious

**[YATES]** Brunian occult memory opposes the morally and theologically sanitized order of Puritan Ramism.

**Assessment:** partly confirmed, partly over-totalized.

Perkins calls Dicson’s memory “impious,” objects to emotionally provocative images, criticizes Peter of Ravenna’s erotic mnemonic, and associates the art with scholastic barbarism. Religious and moral commitments plainly matter. But the controversy is also about:

- clarity of Latin;
- pedagogical economy;
- whether memory should follow the logical structure of subject matter;
- astrology’s status;
- money and public teaching;
- Ramist classification of the arts.

It need not be reduced to a confrontation between two coherent religions.

### E. Plat was not initiated into the mysteries

**[YATES]** Plat learned only the elementary technique.

**Assessment:** first clause plausible, initiation claim unverified. Plat’s account is practical, but no document identifies a secret curriculum.

## 2. Yates’s bibliographical observations

Yates accurately recognized:

- the 1583/early-1584 dating problem;
- the four pamphlets in the controversy;
- Basson’s 1597 reprints;
- Plat’s report;
- the Bowes letter identifying Dicson as “master of the art of memory” formerly attending Sidney.

She also wrote that she did not know why Basson reprinted the works. That remains a more responsible position than later claims that the printing proves membership in an occult fraternity.

- “Philip Sidney’s Letter to Queen Elizabeth and that ‘False Knave’ Alexander Dicsone,” *English Manuscript Studies* 11 (2002), pp. 1–51.
- ODNB article on Alexander Dicsone.

The Catalogue of English Literary Manuscripts records autograph letters, political compilations, and books owned or inscribed by Dicson. [CELM introduction](https://celm.folger.edu/introductions/DicsoneAlexander.html); [CELM records](https://celm.folger.edu/authors/dicsonealexander.html).

Most strikingly:

- Dicson owned at least four books by Bruno;
- one bore a personal inscription from Bruno;
- surviving copies include Bruno’s works signed by Dicson;
- Dicson compiled or handled political manuscripts;
- his later career involved shifting Catholic, Scottish, and English political interests.

This provides firmer evidence for a personal Brunian connection than thematic similarity alone.

## 5. Bruno scholarship

### Rita Sturlese

Sturlese’s bibliographical and copy-specific work confirmed material links between Bruno and Dicson, including a newly identified Bruno autograph and Dicson provenance. This shifts the discussion from Yates’s intellectual pattern-recognition toward book history and documented contact.

Relevant works:

- Rita Sturlese, *Bibliografia, censimento e storia delle antiche stampe di Giordano Bruno*, Florence: Olschki, 1987.
- “Un nuovo autografo del Bruno,” *Rinascimento* 27 (1987), pp. 387–391.

### Hilary Gatti

Gatti accepts the importance of images in Bruno’s natural philosophy and notes Perkins’s attack on Dicson. Her work tends to treat Brunian memory as epistemological and scientific as well as magical: images connect thought to perceived natural phenomena. [Gatti, *Essays on Giordano Bruno* excerpt](https://dokumen.pub/essays-on-giordano-bruno-course-booknbsped-9781400836932.html).

### Stephen Clucas

Clucas’s work qualifies a purely Hermetic reading by locating Bruno’s memory in contemporary psychology, natural philosophy, language, and debates over imagination. This makes Dicson’s distinction between sensation, vision, thought, and central memory especially important.

### Giovanni Aquilecchia, Michele Ciliberto, and Bruno editions

Post-Yates Bruno scholarship has emphasized philological context, the internal development of Bruno’s philosophy, and the political-literary purposes of the Italian dialogues. It does not erase Hermetic sources but rejects using “Hermeticism” as a complete explanation.

Standard Bruno sources relevant to Dicson include:

- *De umbris idearum* (Paris, 1582);
- *Ars memoriae* appended to it;
- *Cantus Circaeus* (Paris, 1582);
- *Explicatio triginta sigillorum* (London, 1583);
- *Sigillus sigillorum* (London, 1583);
- *De la causa, principio et uno* (London, 1584);
- *La cena de le Ceneri* (London, 1584).

The Warburg Institute provides scans and bibliographical materials for Bruno and Yates. [Warburg Bruno resources](https://resources.warburg.sas.ac.uk/mnemosyne/Bruno/).

## 6. Mary Carruthers, Lina Bolzoni, Paolo Rossi, Jocelyn Penny Small

These scholars do not all treat Dicson extensively, but they correct the historical frame within which Yates placed him.

### Carruthers

Mary Carruthers, *The Book of Memory* and *The Craft of Thought*, demonstrates that medieval memoria was not merely storage. It was compositional, ethical, affective, and inventive. Dicson’s animated images therefore do not require an exclusively Brunian or magical explanation; they also continue a broad tradition of affective cognitive craft.

### Bolzoni

Lina Bolzoni’s *The Gallery of Memory* situates Renaissance memory images amid preaching, emblem, literary invention, galleries, and textual visualization. Dicson belongs to a culture in which mental images, rhetorical figures, and material images constantly interacted.

### Rossi

Paolo Rossi’s *Logic and the Art of Memory* places memory arts within projects for method, classification, combinatorics, and universal knowledge. The Dicson–Perkins dispute is thus not merely magic against religion; it is also image-based topical invention against Ramist logical order.

### Small

Jocelyn Penny Small’s *Wax Tablets of the Mind* warns against treating ancient memory theory as a single unchanging “method of loci.” Ancient testimony is fragmentary, and Renaissance authors reconstructed it creatively. This is particularly relevant to Dicson’s confident Egyptian and Druidic genealogy.

## 7. Walter Ong and Ramism

Walter J. Ong, *Ramus, Method, and the Decay of Dialogue* (1958), especially the account of Ramist printing and pedagogy, provides the essential background.

Ramist memory is not simply “natural memory.” It locates mnemonic help in logical division, dichotomous disposition, and the ordered structure of the matter itself. Perkins quotes or adapts Ramist statements that places and images are inept and that dialectical disposition alone teaches order.

Yates’s contrast between “emotionally aseptic” Ramist order and affectively charged images is memorable and often illuminating. It is also polemical: Ramist diagrams are themselves visual artifacts, and Protestant pedagogy did not abandon all imagery.

## 8. Scholarship listed in the requested template but not directly probative

The works of Hajdu, Sabbatino, Bonner, Fidora, Stagno, and much Camillo or Llull scholarship illuminate the broader history but do not furnish direct evidence about Dicson’s text.

- Hajdu is important for the prehistory and social status of medieval mnemonic arts.
- Sabbatino and Bolzoni clarify Camillo’s theater and literary context.
- Bonner and Fidora correct older accounts of Llull by emphasizing his demonstrative art, alphabetic figures, and theological logic rather than treating it as simply a mnemonic machine.
- Stagno’s work on galleries and collections helps contextualize spatial ordering.
- Carruthers and Small correct teleological narratives of a single unchanged art.
- Ong remains directly relevant because Perkins’s alternative is Ramist.

The absence of direct Dicson treatment should be stated rather than manufacturing a scholarly consensus from adjacent fields.

## History and context

## 1. Classical rhetoric

Dicson’s technical substrate is the classical art of memory:

- places function like wax tablets;
- images function like letters;
- order allows retrieval;
- extraordinary action makes an image adhere;
- visible representation can cue things or words.

The traditional line is:

```text
Simonides tradition
→ Cicero / Ad Herennium / Quintilian
→ medieval rhetoric, preaching, and devotion
→ Renaissance printed memory manuals
→ Bruno
→ Dicson and the Perkins controversy
```

Metrodorus’s zodiac supplies an especially suggestive ancient precedent, but no Metrodoran text survives. Renaissance claims to restore his “Scepsian” method are reconstructions.

## 2. Scholastic psychology

Dicson’s model presupposes the Aristotelian view that intellect normally receives material through sense and phantasm. Invisible or abstract things must be rendered perceptible by internal images.

His sequence of sense, vision, thought, and memory resonates with scholastic accounts of internal senses. Yet his terminology is idiosyncratic, and one should not force it into a single Thomist anatomical diagram.

## 3. Lullism and combinatorics

Dicson’s thirty letter-agents, categorical combinations, and hierarchical division exist in a culture transformed by Ramon Llull’s alphabetic figures.

However:

- **[NO DIRECT TEXTUAL PROOF]** Dicson does not name Llull in the technical chapters examined.
- **[RECONSTRUCTION]** Bruno’s combinatory arts transmitted Lullian habits into Dicson’s environment.
- **[CORRECTION TO OLDER HISTORIES]** Llull’s own art was devised as a demonstrative and missionary logic, not originally as a memory palace.

For current Llull bibliography and manuscript data, the appropriate authority is the Universitat de Barcelona’s Ramon Llull Database. [Ramon Llull Database](https://orbita.bib.ub.edu/llull/).

## 4. Hermeticism and Neoplatonism

Dicson draws on:

- the Platonic Theuth story;
- *Corpus Hermeticum* IV and XIII;
- probably CH XVI;
- the hierarchy of one, mind, soul, nature, light, shadows, vestiges;
- regeneration in the divine mixing bowl;
- Egyptian wisdom as prior to Greek disputation.

The Renaissance still generally accepted the Hermetic writings as extremely ancient. Isaac Casaubon’s redating of much of the Greek Hermetica to the post-Christian period came only in 1614.

Thus Dicson’s Egyptian genealogy was historically intelligible, though not historically correct.

## 5. Ramism and universities

Ramus’s method was especially influential in Protestant schools and at Cambridge. Its promise was that knowledge properly divided according to nature becomes memorable without a vast private stock of arbitrary images.

Perkins, a Cambridge theologian, attacks Dicson from this institutional setting. The dispute concerns which faculty owns order:

- Dicson: visual places, imagination, auxiliary numbering, and animation;
- Perkins: dialectical analysis and the intrinsic hierarchy of propositions.

## 6. Courts and patronage

Dicson dedicated both *De umbra rationis* and his *Defensio* to Robert Dudley, Earl of Leicester.

**[DOCUMENT]** Robert Bowes reported in 1592:

> “Dickson, master of the art of memory, and sometime attending on Mr. Philip Sidney, deceased, has come to court.”

This documents his reputation and some association with Sidney. It does not establish that Sidney was formally his student.

Bruno praised Dicson in the Italian dialogues as a learned, honorable, lovable, well-born, faithful friend, and made “Dicsono” a participant in *De la causa*.

## 7. Printing networks

Thomas Vautrollier was a Huguenot printer who also printed Ramist works. That he printed Dicson’s anti-Ramist texts shows that printer confessional identity did not mechanically determine every title.

Thomas Basson, an English printer established at Leiden, reprinted *Thamus* and the *Defensio* in 1597.

**[YATES]** Basson liked mysteries and may have been connected with the Family of Love.

**[DISPUTED]** Neither the reprint nor Basson’s later family publishing connections proves that Dicson’s books circulated through a continuous esoteric fraternity.

## People

## Alexander Dicson

**[DOCUMENT]** A Scot from the Errol area, probably born around 1558; educated at St Andrews and possibly on the Continent; in London by 1583; associated with Bruno, Leicester, and Sidney; later active in Scottish and international political business.

His name appears as:

- Dicson;
- Dicsone;
- Dickson;
- Dicsonus Arelius;
- Bruno’s Italian “Dicsono.”

By 1591 he declared himself Catholic. He served or negotiated for Francis Hay, Earl of Erroll, and carried politically sensitive correspondence. Later he worked in James VI’s interest, including on an unpublished succession tract.

The description “spy” is defensible as a shorthand for aspects of his activity, but “political agent of shifting allegiance” is more exact.

## Giordano Bruno

Bruno was not merely an inferred intellectual ancestor.

**[DOCUMENT]** Dicson owned Bruno books, at least one personally inscribed. Bruno praised him warmly and wrote him into an Italian dialogue. Direct instruction remains probable but is not documented in a surviving classroom record.

## Robert Dudley, Earl of Leicester

Patron named in the dedications. Whether Leicester paid for Dicson’s research or instruction is unknown.

## Philip Sidney

Bowes says Dicson had “attended” Sidney. That can indicate service, presence in a circle, or another dependent relationship. It does not by itself mean “student” or “patron.”

## Hugh Plat

Plat is the clearest witness to actual instruction. He translates Dicson’s obscure vocabulary into a usable recipe but also portrays the teaching as oversold.

## William Perkins / G. P.

Probably the Cambridge author of the attacks. His objection was moral, theological, epistemological, and pedagogical. He did not accuse Dicson in a surviving criminal proceeding; he accused the art in print.

## Thomas Watson

Watson’s *Compendium memoriae localis*, probably printed around 1585–1586, offers a clearer classical local memory. Its relationship to Dicson and Bruno is contextual rather than demonstrably that of disciple to master. A manuscript version survives in British Library **Sloane MS 3731** according to recent scholarship; Yates printed a different Sloane number in some discussions, and the catalogue should be checked before citation.

## Ioannes Adamus Lincolniensis

The commendatory speaker praises Dicson for restoring memory from “mad barbarism.” His precise identity and intention remain uncertain.

Dana Sutton provocatively reads this as praise for stripping away contemporary magical excess. That is an interpretive suggestion, not an established identification of Adams’s target.

## Thomas Basson

English printer at Leiden; reprinted both Dicson texts in 1597. His precise reasons are unknown.

## Martin Del Rio

Yates reports that Del Rio’s *Disquisitionum magicarum libri sex* (1600) commended Dicson’s *Thamus* as not lacking wit and acumen. This is noteworthy: a major Catholic demonologist could praise the dialogue without treating its mnemonic procedure as prohibited magic.

## Anecdotes: documented, misplaced, and absent

The supplied general template names “the auctioneer, the professor’s party, the student who lost his memory, the accused magician.” None belongs demonstrably to Dicson’s system.

### Documented for Dicson

- Plat took or observed lessons and later published the procedure.
- Dicson was publicly remembered as “master of the art of memory.”
- He was associated with Sidney.
- He owned Bruno books.
- An opponent accused him of obscurity, impiety, vanity, and profiteering.
- His method was allegedly useful for errands, conversation, and games.

### Not documented for Dicson

- No contemporary account says he performed an auctioneer’s feat.
- No identified professor’s-party anecdote is attached to him.
- No student is documented as losing natural memory through his lessons.
- No record has been found of Dicson being tried before an Inquisition as a magician.
- No source says his images physically moved, summoned spirits, or conferred supernatural recall.

These stories belong to other mnemonists, medieval exempla, or modern composite histories unless a specific witness can be produced.

## The magic question

## 1. Was the system magical?

The answer depends on the meaning of “magical.”

### As a practical mnemonic technique

**No necessary magic is present.**

Its operative acts are:

- visualization;
- ordered location;
- association;
- personification;
- exaggeration;
- emotional salience;
- repeated mental traversal.

No ritual timing, suffumigation, divine name, spirit invocation, talismanic consecration, planetary prayer, or astrological election is prescribed.

### As a cosmological and religious philosophy

**Yes, it is explicitly Hermetic and Neoplatonic in framing.**

Dicson speaks of:

- rays and higher powers;
- the One and God;
- mind over reason;
- ascent from matter;
- shadows, vestiges, and seals;
- Hermetic regeneration;
- Egyptian wisdom;
- zodiacal and lunar images.

Calling this “occult philosophy” is historically reasonable.

### As astral magic

**Not proved.**

The zodiac organizes numbered images. Dicson does not instruct the practitioner to capture planetary influence in a talisman. The system might coexist with astral assumptions, but mnemonic use of zodiacal order is not itself astral magic.

## 2. Perkins’s charge of impiety

**[TEXT]** Perkins’s title calls the memory *impia*. His objections include:

- arbitrary images displace the natural logical order of truth;
- violent, grotesque, or erotic images excite disorderly passions;
- Peter of Ravenna recommended a beloved woman as a mnemonic image;
- astrology is an inappropriate basis for a general art;
- the art smells of scholastic “barbarism” and “Dunsicality”;
- Dicson’s prose is obscure and commercially self-serving.

Yates is right that confessional and moral hostility mattered. Yet Perkins’s case is not proof that Dicson performed magic; it tells us what a Ramist Protestant opponent classified as impious.

## 3. Papistry and heresy

Dicson’s later Catholic profession and political service made suspicions of “papistry” biographically plausible. But:

- *De umbra rationis* does not proclaim Roman Catholic dogma;
- its Hermetic regeneration is not orthodox Catholic sacramental theology;
- Vautrollier, its printer, was a Huguenot;
- no ecclesiastical condemnation of the book has been located;
- no inquisitorial prosecution of Dicson for this art is documented.

## 4. Ars notoria

The *Ars notoria* promises infused knowledge through prayers, divine names, ritual observance, and inspection of *notae*. Dicson’s art differs fundamentally.

Shared features:

- inward visualization;
- claims about perfected memory;
- sacral or cosmic language.

Absent from Dicson:

- prescribed liturgical cycles;
- angelic invocation;
- ritual fasting;
- notory prayers;
- guaranteed supernatural infusion of the liberal arts.

Connecting the two is a broad history-of-magic comparison, not evidence that *De umbra rationis* transmits the *Ars notoria*.

## 5. Consequences

The known consequences were literary and reputational:

- a four-part pamphlet controversy;
- charges of impiety and vanity;
- Dicson’s public defense;
- later repetition of the debate by Plat, Del Rio, Yates, and historians.

There is no documented imprisonment, excommunication, or prosecution arising from the memory treatise.

## Afterlife

## 1. Immediate reception

The best evidence of reception is:

- Perkins’s rapid attacks in 1584;
- Dicson’s *Defensio*;
- Plat’s 1594 report;
- Basson’s two 1597 reprints;
- ownership of *Thamus* by learned collectors.

Yates reports that Henry Percy, ninth Earl of Northumberland, owned a copy. Edinburgh University’s copy later stood among memory-related books associated with William Drummond’s intellectual environment, though it is reportedly not itself part of the core Drummond collection.

“Considerable circulation” is possible, but four polemical texts and a reprint do not establish mass readership.

## 2. Later British memory arts

Dicson and Plat form part of the transition to seventeenth-century English manuals:

- William Basse, *A Help to Memory and Discourse* (1620);
- John Willis, *Mnemonica* or *The Art of Memory*;
- Henry Herdson, *Ars memoriae* (1651);
- Richard Saunders;
- Marius D’Assigny.

Direct borrowing from Dicson must be demonstrated case by case. Much of what these authors share comes independently from the classical place-and-image tradition.

## 3. Emblems, galleries, and literature

Dicson’s technique belongs to the same visual culture as:

- emblem books;
- mythographical handbooks;
- personified virtues and vices;
- painted galleries;
- iconological attributes;
- allegorical pageants.

The relation is cultural and structural. No surviving fresco or emblem plate has been identified as an illustration of *De umbra rationis*.

## 4. Dante, Camillo, and the frescoes

These are important to the general art of memory but should not be claimed as Dicson’s direct sources without evidence.

- Dante’s ordered otherworld and vivid punishments can be read mnemonically; Dicson does not cite Dante.
- Giulio Camillo’s theater organizes knowledge in graded, planetary spaces; Dicson neither describes a theater nor reproduces Camillo’s seven grades.
- Dominican fresco cycles use ordered personifications; Dicson’s figures share that iconographic world, but no direct visual borrowing has been traced.

## 5. Borges, Eco, and literary memory

Borges’s Funes possesses overwhelming involuntary particular memory—the opposite of an ordered selective art. Eco’s semiotic and combinatorial fictions revisit libraries, encyclopedias, signs, and universal languages. These are illuminating retrospective comparisons, not documented receptions of Dicson.

## 6. Modern memory palaces and athletes

Modern competitors routinely use:

- ordered routes;
- person-action-object scenes;
- exaggeration;
- violence, humor, and erotic salience;
- numerical peg systems.

These resemble Dicson’s places, auxiliaries, modified shadows, and animation. There is no need to posit direct descent: both draw on durable associative principles.

A responsible formulation is “structural continuity,” not “modern athletes use Dicson’s system.”

## 7. Computing and combinatorics

The broad intellectual genealogy often proposed is:

```text
Llull’s finite alphabets and combinatory figures
→ Renaissance Lullism and Bruno
→ universal-method and universal-language projects
→ Leibniz’s ars combinatoria
→ formal logic and mechanized symbolic calculation
```

Dicson is a minor witness inside that history because his thirty elements, categorical hierarchy, and recombination show how mnemonic images could become symbolic operators.

But:

- he designed no calculating machine;
- his method is not binary;
- no line of direct influence from *De umbra rationis* to Leibniz has been demonstrated;
- “Dicson anticipated the computer” is a **[MODERN INVENTION]**.

## Controversies and disputes

## 1. Dicson, Dicsone, or Dickson?

All forms occur. “Dicson” follows the Latin title and Yates; “Dicsone” is common in archival scholarship; “Dickson” is the modern form. They designate the same person.

## 2. Errol and *Arelius*

*Arelius* is conventionally read as “of Errol.” This is well established, though the exact relationship between birthplace, family origin, and territorial designation should be handled carefully.

## 3. 1583 or 1584?

- Title page: 1583.
- Dedication: 1 January.
- Modern bibliographers: 1583/84 or early 1584.
- Best citation: “London, 1583 [probably issued early 1584 new style].”

## 4. Date of Plat

The correct publication date is **1594**. The supplied 1592 date is not supported by the surviving edition.

## 5. Date of Dicson’s death

Older accounts say “about 1604.” William Fowler reported the death on 11 October 1603, while Thomas Murray’s memorial verses appeared in 1604. The safest formulation is “died in 1603; commemorated in print in 1604,” unless a document proves the report premature.

## 6. Was “Heius Scepsius” Dicson?

The attribution is accepted by catalogues and fits the self-defense, dedication, and Scepsian mnemonic identity. It is highly probable.

## 7. Was G. P. William Perkins?

Probable current attribution, not an explicit title-page identification. Older Gerard Peeters attributions and anonymous cataloguing explain why some records remain cautious.

## 8. Was Dicson Bruno’s student?

- **Documented:** personal association, praise, inscribed books, intellectual dependence.
- **Probable reconstruction:** Bruno taught or directly influenced him.
- **Not documented:** a dated lesson, payment, curriculum, or declaration “I studied memory under Bruno.”

“Disciple” is safe intellectually; “formal pupil” is less secure.

## 9. Is *De umbra rationis* merely an abridgment of *De umbris idearum*?

No.

It borrows Bruno’s philosophical lexicon and several mnemonic structures, but it:

- recasts them in a dialogue with Thamus and Socrates;
- foregrounds Plato’s critique of writing;
- develops its own subject/auxiliary distinction;
- gives its own political, relational, and causal examples;
- lacks Bruno’s full wheel apparatus and decan repertory.

It is derivative but not a simple epitome.

## 10. Does the text prescribe decan images?

Not explicitly. It says the signs have proper images and assigns lunar-mansion images as group leaders. Yates’s comparison with Bruno’s decans is plausible but cannot supply Dicson’s missing repertory.

## 11. Does it contain diagrams?

No surviving diagram has been identified. Statements that Dicson’s book “shows” wheels generally confuse it with Bruno’s illustrated works.

## 12. Is the zodiac magical?

It may be cosmologically meaningful, but its explicit technical function is numerical and ordinal. Astrology is part of the controversy because Perkins treats its use as false or illicit; the text does not prescribe operative astrology.

## 13. Is Socrates definitely Ramus?

No. It is a strong contextual reading first promoted through Durkan and Yates, reinforced by Perkins’s response, but never made explicit by Dicson.

## 14. Was Dicson a Catholic secret agent using memory for espionage?

- Catholic and politically active: documented.
- Political intermediary or intelligence agent: documented in broad terms.
- Memory as useful to an agent: obvious possibility.
- Use of this system to memorize secret dispatches: **no direct evidence**.

The last claim is an attractive modern reconstruction that should not be narrated as fact.

## 15. Family of Love, Rosicrucianism, and Freemasonry

Yates and later writers linked Basson’s environment, Hermetic memory, Familist networks, Rosicrucian publications, and early Scottish Masonry.

Evidence exists for overlapping printers, patrons, books, and intellectual interests. Evidence does not establish:

- Dicson’s membership in the Family of Love;
- a Rosicrucian order in 1583 teaching his system;
- a continuous lodge transmission from Bruno through Dicson;
- Dicson as founder of speculative Freemasonry.

These are disputed genealogies, often expanded in modern esoteric literature.

## 16. Did Del Rio approve a magical art?

Del Rio’s praise of the *Thamus* for wit does not mean he endorsed every doctrine or recognized it as licit magic. It may refer to the anti-Socratic dialogue or its ingenuity.

## 17. What Yates got wrong or overstated

1. She makes a plausible Bruno relationship sound mechanically complete.
2. She tends to merge *adiuvantia* with mnemonic images generally, whereas Dicson gives auxiliaries a distinct stabilizing and ordinal role.
3. She speaks as if Dicson’s zodiac necessarily reproduces Bruno’s astral images; the text is less explicit.
4. Her statement that Plat was not initiated into mysteries is unsupported.
5. Her speculation about underground Hermetic groups exceeds the evidence.
6. Her religious polarization of Brunian and Ramist memory is illuminating but too binary.
7. “Considerable circulation” is possible but not quantified.
8. The political-agent evidence does not prove that mnemonic instruction served espionage.
9. As in her larger Bruno thesis, she relied heavily on the Renaissance belief in an ancient Egyptian Hermes. Post-Casaubon historical dating and post-Yates scholarship distinguish Renaissance reception from the actual late-antique origin of the Hermetica.

## 18. What Yates got decisively right

1. Dicson is indispensable evidence for Bruno’s immediate British reception.
2. The terminology and philosophy of shadows are plainly Brunian.
3. The treatise combines classical memory rules with Hermetic language.
4. The Perkins controversy reveals that artificial memory could be a confessional and moral flashpoint.
5. Plat’s report proves practical oral teaching.
6. The Theuth/Thamus dialogue deliberately contrasts exterior inscription with inner mnemonic writing.
7. The zodiac and mythological inventor-images connect Dicson with Bruno’s mnemonic world even though the systems are not identical.

## Open questions

1. Has anyone completed a full census and textual collation of all surviving 1583 copies?
2. Do copies contain stop-press corrections or variant gatherings not captured in Sutton’s transcription?
3. Is *Thamus* a page-for-page reprint, a revised text, or a selectively reset version? A formal collation remains desirable.
4. Does “consideratio prima” imply a lost second consideration?
5. What exact image repertory did Dicson mean by the “proper images” of signs, planets, lunar dragon, and lunar mansions?
6. Was that repertory taught orally but withheld from print?
7. What precisely was the “golden chain” connecting stations?
8. Can the thirty letter-agents be matched systematically to a Brunian table?
9. Can surviving copies owned by Dicson identify annotations relevant to his mnemonic practice?
10. Did Sidney actually receive instruction, or did Dicson merely attend him in another capacity?
11. Can G. P.’s attribution to Perkins be proved through type, paper, archival payment, or verbal parallels?
12. Who was Ioannes Adamus Lincolniensis?
13. Why did Basson reprint both works in 1597?
14. Did Plat learn directly from Dicson, attend a public demonstration, or receive the procedure through an intermediary?
15. Are there unpublished Plat notebooks recording the lessons?
16. Did Dicson use memory in political activity? No operational record has yet been identified.
17. Did any seventeenth-century author explicitly cite *De umbra rationis* as a technical source?
18. Is Del Rio’s favorable phrase directed to the mnemonic doctrine, the dialogue, or Dicson’s polemical wit?
19. Can the claims connecting Dicson to early Scottish Masonry be supported by evidence earlier than modern historical reconstruction?
20. Are any promised or projected writings implied by the abrupt dialogue preserved anonymously in manuscript?
