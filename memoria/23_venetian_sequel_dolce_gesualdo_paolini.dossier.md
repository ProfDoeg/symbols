# The Venetian sequel: Dolce, Gesualdo, Paolini: Research Dossier

## Basic identification

**Catalogue name:** The Venetian sequel: Dolce, Gesualdo, Paolini  
**Memoria catalogue:** system 23 of 45  
**Exponents:** Lodovico Dolce; Filippo Gesualdo, O.F.M. Conv.; Fabio Paolini  
**Dates:** 1562; 1589; 1592  
**Principal texts:**

- Lodovico Dolce, *Dialogo di M. Lodovico Dolce, nel quale si ragiona del modo di accrescere, & conservar la memoria* (Venice: Giovanni Battista and Melchiorre Sessa, 1562).
- Fabio Paolini, *Hebdomades, sive Septem de Septenario libri, habiti in Uranicorum Academia in unius Vergilii versus explicatione* (Venice: Francesco de Franceschi, 1589).
- Filippo Gesualdo, *Plutosofia … nella quale si spiega l’arte della memoria, con altre cose notabili pertinenti, tanto alla memoria naturale, quanto all’artificiale* (Padua: Paolo Meietti, 1592).

**Principle in one sentence:** A Venetian and Veneto-Paduan convergence in which the classical-scholastic architectural art of places and images was vernacularized and adapted to printed pictures by Dolce, cosmologically elevated through Ficino and Camillo by Gesualdo, and associated by Paolini with a sevenfold, musically proportioned, potentially astral art of oratory.

**Yates:** *The Art of Memory*, chapter VII, “Camillo’s Theatre and the Venetian Renaissance,” principally pp. 163–69; compare pp. 94–95, 115 n. 26, and 196 n. 40 in the 1966 English edition.

### Evidentiary warning

“The Venetian sequel” is **Yates’s historiographical grouping**, not the name of a historical school or a single system used by the three writers. Dolce supplies a practical mnemonic manual; Gesualdo another, philosophically ornamented manual; Paolini does not give a comparable operative memory system at all. The strongest claim—that Paolini reveals a concealed magical function of Camillo’s Theatre—is explicitly an inference by Yates.

## The system in detail

# I. Lodovico Dolce’s *Dialogo*

## 1. Authorship, form, and derivation

**[Documented text]** Dolce casts his instructions as a dialogue between **Hortensio** and **Fabrizio**. The dedicatory letter addresses the Venetian jurist, philosopher, and orator Filippo Terzo. The dialogue form evokes Cicero’s *De oratore* and converts technical Latin mnemonic doctrine into cultivated Italian.

**[Established scholarly finding]** Andrea Torre’s critical edition demonstrates that the work is substantially a vernacular adaptation—often close to translation—of Johannes Host von Romberch’s *Congestorium artificiosae memoriae* (Venice, 1520), supplemented from Pietro da Ravenna, Jacobus Publicius, Thomas Aquinas, Cicero, Quintilian, and other authorities.

It is therefore misleading to call Dolce the inventor of the apparatus. His originality lies chiefly in:

- vernacularization and dialogic presentation;
- selection, compression, and reordering;
- adaptation to contemporary Venetian visual and print culture;
- insertion of Dante and Titian;
- making Romberch’s scholastic manual usable by a lay Italian reading public.

Yates called the book “Romberch in Ciceronian Italian.” That is rhetorically apt, though Torre shows that “translation” must include active rewriting and cultural adaptation.

## 2. What serves as places

Dolce distinguishes places by origin and scale.

### By origin

**[Documented text: Dolce, Torre ed., pp. 37–39]**

> “Le cose vere … sono … rupi, monti, colli, fiumi, prati, selve … Le imaginarie e invisibili sono il Cielo, il Paradiso, l’Inferno, e’l Purgatorio. Quelle che hanno luogo dall’arte sono le case, i palazzi, le chiese, i monasteri…”

Gloss: Real loci may be natural features; imaginary or invisible loci include Heaven, Paradise, Hell, and Purgatory; artificial loci include houses, palaces, churches, and monasteries.

This creates three practical reservoirs:

1. actual natural landscapes;
2. actual human constructions;
3. mentally constructed or scripturally and poetically described worlds.

### By scale

**[Documented text]** Dolce distinguishes:

- **grandissimi / comunissimi:** cities, castles, monasteries, churches, abbeys, colleges, theatres, fortresses, houses;
- **maggiori:** rooms, halls, dining rooms, sleeping chambers;
- **grandi** or immediate loci: walls, windows, columns, altars, and other surfaces upon which the image is directly “inscribed.”

The terminology is not perfectly intuitive: “grandissimi” contain “maggiori,” which contain the immediate image-bearing loci.

### Real and imaginary itineraries

**[Documented text, pp. 57–76]** For real places, the practitioner enters a city, castle, monastery, church, or house and proceeds from the left, “toward the course of the sun,” moving regularly to the right. Windows, columns, wall divisions, chapels, choirs, sacristies, and doors articulate the sequence. One must return by the door rather than leap irregularly through the structure.

> “Volendo adunque fare i tuoi luoghi, entra nella città, o castello, o monasterio, o casa; e ciò che troverai a mano sinistra … con diligenza noterai…”

Gloss: To make loci, enter the chosen building and carefully register what first appears on the left, then the second locus and so onward.

**[Documented text]** Imaginary places are built by analogy with real ones. Dolce’s woodcut city furnishes a model: enter the imagined city, encounter the abbey, barber’s house, and other major structures in alphabetical order, and subdivide each into lesser loci.

## 3. How order is fixed

Order is the indispensable skeleton.

**[Documented text]**

- Follow a stable route, ordinarily left-to-right.
- Do not jump to the centre of a room or use an irregular path.
- Mark every fifth locus.
- Use stronger signs for decades or other important intervals.
- Rehearse the empty route three or four times before depositing images.
- Revisit it frequently after use.
- Keep different bodies of subject matter in distinct large systems where useful.
- Place the first component of a word nearer the locus than the second.

Dolce repeats the classical recommendation that every fifth locus receive a marker. His examples include:

- a golden hand at the fifth;
- a cross, coin, or other conspicuous sign at the tenth;
- a silver or golden hand at the fifteenth;
- numerical characters or crosses at the twentieth and thirtieth.

The source is ultimately *Ad Herennium* III.31:

> “Et, ne forte in numero locorum falli possimus, quintum quemque placet notari…”

Gloss: So that we do not lose count of the loci, every fifth one should be marked.

Dolce’s woodcut sequence visually incorporates hands, crosses, numbers, and heraldic or distinguishing signs.

## 4. Rules for good places

**[Documented synthesis of Dolce/Romberch]**

A useful locus should be:

- clearly bounded;
- neither excessively large nor too small;
- sufficiently illuminated;
- not crowded by competing objects;
- separated from adjacent loci;
- distinctive but integrated into one fixed route;
- proportionate to the image placed within it;
- thoroughly familiar before use.

Dolce rejects using cramped corners merely because a room appears to supply four corners and a centre. Congestion and darkness obscure images.

> “I luoghi troppo grandi fanno le imagini come vaganti, et i troppo piccioli non le possono capere; ché in una picciol finestra la mente non concederà un camelo.”

Gloss: In an overlarge locus the image seems to wander; a locus too small cannot contain it—the mind will not put a camel in a tiny window.

This is a particularly clear Renaissance formulation of **mnemonic scale matching**.

## 5. What serves as images

Dolce uses several kinds.

### Images for things

These signify a subject through resemblance, attribute, action, association, myth, profession, or conventional iconography.

### Images for words, letters, and syllables

When a thing has no readily imaginable form, Dolce recommends phonetic or alphabetic encoding:

- letters represented by familiar persons whose names begin with them;
- letters embodied by animals;
- occupational images representing syllables;
- foreign alphabets or “real letters” as supplements;
- combinations of images whose spatial relation fixes letter order.

His personal alphabet includes such names as:

> “Angela, Beatrice, Cecilia, Elisabetta, Faustina, Giulia, Helena, Irene, Laura…”

An alternative male alphabet begins Antonio, Bernardo, Corrado, Dominico, Emilio, Federico, Giovanni, and so forth.

An animal alphabet begins:

> “Anser … Buffo … Corvo; Dragone; Ericius … Falcone; Graculus … Harpia; Ibis…”

Dolce then gives a syllabic alphabet using figures such as *Abbate*, *Accolito*, *Advocato*, *Africano*, *Ambasciadore*, *Barbiere*, *Bombardiere*, *Chirurgo*, *Cuoco*, *Dottore*, and *Duca*.

**[Documented text]** Spatial precedence records phonetic precedence. If Eusebio must precede Tommaso, Eusebio is placed touching or nearer the locus; reversing the positions reverses the encoded sequence.

## 6. Rules for good images

Dolce preserves the classical and medieval demand for emotionally active imagery.

**[Documented text, Torre ed., around pp. 94–99]**

> “Se … vediamo o udiamo alcuna cosa che sia pienamente sozza e pienamente bella, sopramodo dishonesta o honesta, incredibile, grande, e ridicola, di questa a lungo abbiamo costume di ricordarci.”

Gloss: We long remember what is extremely filthy or beautiful, extraordinarily dishonourable or honourable, incredible, huge, or ridiculous.

Pietro da Ravenna’s images are summarized as:

> “rare e maravigliose … disusate, giocose, horrende, di bello, di brutto aspetto, e che alcuna cosa facciano.”

Gloss: rare, marvellous, unfamiliar, playful, horrifying, beautiful or ugly, and performing some action.

Hence images should be:

- animate rather than inert;
- large enough to command attention;
- emotionally charged;
- beautiful, ugly, comic, frightening, scandalous, or otherwise exceptional;
- engaged in an intelligible action;
- proportionate to their loci;
- personally familiar where possible;
- rehearsed through mental inspection.

## 7. Retrieval

**[Documented reconstruction]** Retrieval is serial mental traversal:

1. enter the established city, building, landscape, body, or imaginary region;
2. follow the learned itinerary;
3. inspect the image at each locus;
4. decode its direct, symbolic, phonetic, or syllabic significance;
5. reconstruct the subject, proposition, wording, or sequence.

Dolce stresses that loci alone do not yield their contents automatically:

> “Se non v’interviene il discorso, quel luogo non ci renderà quello che v’avremo deposto…”

Gloss: Unless reasoning or active mental traversal intervenes, the locus will not return what was deposited in it.

This qualification matters. The art is not represented as a mechanically infallible camera; it requires attention, inference, rehearsal, and trained decoding.

## 8. Worked examples

### Dante’s Hell

**[Documented Dolcean addition]**

> “Onde potremo imaginarci lo Inferno … distinguendolo nel Limbo … nel ricetto de’ fanciulli … e aggiungendovi il Purgatorio … ordineremo diverse magioni.… In che ci gioverà assai l’ingeniosa inventiva di Virgilio e di Dante.”

Gloss: Hell, Limbo, the abode of unbaptized children, and Purgatory may be divided into separate dwellings; Virgil’s and Dante’s imaginative invention supplies the layout.

**[Scholarly finding: Torre]** Dante is Dolce’s addition to Romberch’s Virgilian material. The *Commedia’s* spatial divisions and contrapasso make it an inventory of memorable loci and punishments.

**[Correction to Yates]** Yates’s phrase “cosmic diagram for Dantesque artificial memory” can suggest that Romberch himself supplied a specifically Dantean diagram. The diagram derives from Romberch, but the explicit recommendation of Dante here is Dolce’s adaptation.

### Titian’s Europa and Adonis

**[Documented text, 1562 fol. 86r; Torre ed. p. 146]**

> “Come chi volesse raccordarsi della favola di Europa, potrebbe valersi dell’esempio della pittura di Tiziano; et altretanto di Adone e di qual si voglia altra favola, o historia profana o sacra, eleggendo specialmente quelle figure che dilettano e quindi sogliono la memoria eccitare.”

Gloss: To remember Europa, one can use Titian’s painting; likewise for Adonis or any profane or sacred story, choosing especially images that delight and therefore stimulate memory.

Dolce adds that illustrated books are useful image banks, singling out books from Gabriel Giolito’s press.

**[Scholarly interpretation: Bolzoni]** This is evidence of accommodation between manuscript-era mnemonic practice and print: pictures in books and celebrated paintings become standardized suppliers of internal images.

**[Caution]** The passage does not say that the physical painting itself is a locus, still less a talisman. It is a model for forming a mental image.

### The *Ad Herennium* lawsuit image

The classical example encodes a poisoning lawsuit involving inheritance, witnesses, and an accused person. In *Ad Herennium* III.33, the mnemonic image places the sick or murdered man in bed; the accused stands beside him holding a cup, tablets, and a ram’s testicles. The cup signifies poison, tablets the will or inheritance, and the testicles (*testiculi*) pun on witnesses (*testes*).

**[Source genealogy]** This is a classical example inherited through the tradition, not an invention peculiar to Dolce. Dolce transmits the distinction between images of things and images of words through Romberch’s compilation, but his distinctive worked examples are Dante, Titian, alphabets, buildings, bodies, and classificatory tables.

## 9. Diagrams and woodcuts

The 1562 edition has **23 woodcut figures, 13 full-page**, and several blocks previously used in Romberch’s 1520 *Congestorium*.

They include:

- a cosmographical/world diagram used to organize geographic or cosmic loci;
- a schematic imagined city and its nested architectural places;
- numbered spatial sequences with hands, crosses, and distinguishing markers;
- human figures whose members function as ordered loci;
- a female figure personifying Grammar, covered or surrounded by letter forms and alphabetic cues;
- animal alphabets;
- alphabetic and syllabic tables;
- square and circular combinatory devices for arranging vowels, consonants, and syllables.

**[Documented bibliographical fact]** The surviving blocks show physical continuity between Latin Dominican mnemonic printing and Dolce’s vernacular commercial edition.

**[Yates’s description, p. 163]**

> “And Romberch’s diagrams are exactly reproduced; we see once again his cosmic diagram for Dantesque artificial memory, and the antiquated figure of Grammar, stuck over with visual alphabets.”

**[Correction]** “Exactly” is broadly true of reused cuts, not of the entire textual function surrounding them. Dolce changes their vernacular framing and supplies new contemporary examples.

## 10. Natural memory

Dolce also surveys causes of good and bad natural memory and traditional regimens: temperament, diet, sleep, exercise, medicines, and substances believed to aid the brain. Alphabetical lists of plants, compounds, precious stones, waters, winds, animals, and professions double as encyclopedic mnemonic resources.

These medical passages belong to premodern humoral medicine. Their presence does not transform the architectural art into magic.

# II. Filippo Gesualdo’s *Plutosofia*

## 1. Identity and publication

Filippo Gesualdo of Castrovillari, a Conventual Franciscan, lived approximately 1550–1618/19. He became minister general of the Friars Minor Conventual in 1593 and later bishop of Cariati and Cerenzia.

**First edition:** Padua: Paolo Meietti, 1592, quarto, [6], 64 leaves, illustrated.  
**Second edition:** Vicenza, 1600, expanded or reset in 136 pages in surviving digitizations.

The title means approximately “wisdom of wealth” or “wealth-wisdom.”

**[Documented authorial explanation]** Gesualdo calls artificial memory the treasure or riches of human knowledge; the title characterizes the art as a storehouse rather than identifying a deity named Pluto as its magical patron.

## 2. Structure

The 1592 book consists of **twenty lectures** (*lettioni*). Its central practical material is indebted to:

- *Ad Herennium*;
- Pietro da Ravenna;
- Romberch;
- the scholastic psychology of memory and imagination;
- contemporary encyclopedic and bibliographical organization.

The eighteenth lecture constructs a “library of memory.” The twentieth teaches an art of forgetting.

## 3. The three levels of memory

**[Documented text, reported and partially quoted by Yates]** Gesualdo opens with Ficino’s *De vita* and presents memory through exalted analogies:

1. memory is like **Ocean**, father of waters, because thoughts and words flow from it;
2. memory is like **heaven**, with its lights and operations;
3. memory is the **divine element or image of God in the soul**.

Elsewhere it is compared with the highest celestial sphere, the zodiac, and the supercelestial sphere of the Seraphim.

**[Yates’s interpretation, pp. 164–65]**

> “Clearly Gesualdo’s memory moves amongst the three worlds, in a manner similar to that shown in the lay-out of the Theatre.”

The “three worlds” are the elemental, celestial, and supercelestial/intelligible worlds familiar from Ficinian and Renaissance Neoplatonic cosmology.

**[Assessment]** Gesualdo’s language is certainly cosmological and devotional. The stronger assertion that it consciously reproduces Camillo’s Theatre is an analogy made by Yates; no demonstrated passage says, “these three levels are Camillo’s Theatre.”

## 4. Practical system

After the cosmological opening, Gesualdo returns to conventional architectural mnemonic technique.

### Places

He recommends:

- actual or imagined buildings;
- rooms and architectural subdivisions;
- the human body;
- a mentally fabricated city;
- a “library” whose components classify knowledge;
- sacred pictures and ranked personages as ordering devices.

### Images

Images should be:

- vivid;
- active;
- strongly differentiated;
- attached to stable loci;
- inspected repeatedly;
- connected by resemblance, sound, etymology, association, or action.

A striking exercise appears at fol. 23r:

> “con la mente, starti a contemplarle in quelli luoghi, come se vi fossero, & passando salutarle, toccarle, chiamarle per nome, ragionar seco, mirarle dinanzi, e rimirarle da’ lati…”

Gloss: Mentally contemplate the persons as if physically present; pass them, greet and touch them, call them by name, converse with them, and inspect them from the front and sides.

**[Scholarly interpretation: Bolzoni]** This makes mnemonic visualization bodily and theatrical. The remembered image is not merely seen but encountered socially and kinetically.

### The Library of Memory

In lecture XVIII, Gesualdo proposes an organized mental library. The practitioner places pictures of selected saints, angels, and human worthies in architecturally ordered spaces:

> “il formatore … giornalmente collochi il tutto nelli formati Luoghi…”

and:

> “il formatore di questa Libraria vi ponga Quadri di Santi, eleggendosi un certo numero di Prencipi del Paradiso, Angeli, & Humani…”

Gloss: The maker should daily place material in the constructed loci; in the library he should install pictures of saints, choosing a fixed number of heavenly princes, angels, and human beings.

**[Recent scholarship: Baldi and Kuwakino]** This lecture also appropriates Conrad Gesner’s bibliographical classifications. It is therefore not merely a mystical building: it translates printed bibliography and subject taxonomy into spatial memory.

This is an important correction to a purely Yatesian reading. Gesualdo is as much concerned with information organization as with ascent through cosmic levels.

## 5. The art of forgetting

Lecture XX is one of the most original sections.

**[Documented text]** Artificial oblivion is justified:

> “non in quanto è distruttiva, ma in quanto, per certa consequenza accidentale, è perfettiva della rimembranza.”

Gloss: not insofar as it destroys, but insofar as, by an accidental consequence, it perfects recollection.

Methods include imagining:

- the route plunged into darkness;
- rooms empty and stripped;
- images whitewashed or covered with white, green, or black cloth;
- loci filled with straw, hay, timber, or merchandise;
- new images displacing old ones, like one nail driving out another;
- a violent storm, hail, dust, collapsing houses, and flood;
- a terrifying enemy and armed company entering the palace, beating its inhabitants, smashing images, and driving living figures through doors and windows;
- calm weather returning after the destructive episode, revealing empty loci.

This is not neurological erasure. It is deliberate interference with access cues.

**[Modern interpretation]** It resembles cue suppression or replacement, but describing it as a scientifically validated memory-erasure method would be a modern invention.

## 6. Diagrams

The 1592 *Plutosofia* contains woodcut illustrations of:

- architectural loci;
- a human or quasi-anatomical figure divided into mnemonic regions;
- spatial or tabular ordering schemes.

Surviving exhibition descriptions emphasize their relationship to architectural and anatomical visualization.

**[Limitation]** No census establishing meaningful diagrammatic variants between every surviving 1592 copy and the 1600 edition was located. Claims that the figures encode a hidden magical machine are unsupported by the consulted evidence.

# III. Fabio Paolini’s *Hebdomades*

## 1. What the book is—and is not

Paolini’s work is not principally a mnemonic handbook. Its title means:

> *Weeks, or Seven Books concerning the Number Seven, delivered in the Academy of the Uranici in explanation of one verse of Virgil.*

The verse is *Aeneid* VI.646:

> “Obloquitur numeris septem discrimina vocum.”

A defensible gloss is: “He accompanies [the song] rhythmically with the seven distinctions/pitches of the voice.”

The line concerns Orpheus and the seven-stringed lyre. Paolini turns it into an encyclopedic investigation of seven.

**[Documented structure]** Seven books, each with seven discourses or chapters: forty-nine divisions.

**[Correction]** It should not be presented as a complete third architectural memory system parallel to Dolce’s or Gesualdo’s. Its relevance is its fusion of rhetoric, music, number symbolism, cosmology, Ficinian magic, Cabala, and Camillo.

## 2. Institutional setting

The discourses originated in 1587 in or around Venice’s **Accademia degli Uranici** and the intellectual milieu of the Biblioteca Marciana.

Paolini taught rhetoric, poetry, Greek, and Latin. Archival evidence records him and Lombardo Amulio among public lecturers connected with the Marciana. He studied at Padua and knew Antonio Riccobono, Francesco Robortello, Bernardino Partenio, and Gioseffo Zarlino.

The academy’s name, “Uranici,” signals celestial or Uranian aspirations, but an academic celestial name is not itself evidence of ritual magic.

## 3. Sevenfold correspondences

Paolini accumulates correspondences involving:

- seven planetary spheres;
- seven musical intervals or distinctions;
- seven strings;
- seven days;
- seven ages and physiological cycles;
- seven liberal or intellectual divisions;
- seven rhetorical qualities or “Ideas” associated with Hermogenes;
- biblical and angelic sevens;
- the sevenfold construction of Camillo’s Theatre.

At p. 27 he quotes *L’Idea del Theatro*, p. 14, on the Theatre’s sevenfold planetary construction.

At pp. 313–14 he gives planetary angels in a form connected with Trithemius’s *De septem secundeis*.

At p. 24 he says Julius Caesar Scaliger displayed Hermogenean forms:

> “quasi in Theatrum”

—“as though in a theatre.”

## 4. Planetary oratory

D. P. Walker reconstructed Paolini’s theory as an application of Ficino’s musical magic to speech.

**[Documented intellectual ingredients]**

- Ficino held that appropriately composed music and hymns could attract or accommodate celestial influences.
- Paolini treats voice, rhythm, verbal sound, figures of speech, rhetorical forms, and Hermogenes’ Ideas within a sevenfold cosmology.
- Paolini relates the proper mixture of oratorical forms to cosmic and musical proportion.
- He cites Camillo and Trithemius.

**[Walker’s reconstruction]** Just as a proper mixture of tones could give music a planetary affinity or power, a proper mixture of rhetorical “forms” could give an oration celestial efficacy.

**[Yates’s stronger inference, p. 168]**

> “the Theatre also magically activated the speeches which the orator remembered by it, infusing them with planetary virtue through which they would have magical effects on the hearers.”

She proposes that a perfectly proportioned image—Apollo, for example—could generate a perfectly proportioned and therefore magically effective speech about the sun.

**[Disputed/undemonstrated]** No passage located in Camillo says that his mnemonic image of Apollo literally injects astral force into a remembered speech. Paolini does not provide Yates’s Apollo thought experiment. This is a synthetic Yatesian reconstruction from:

- Camillo’s seven planetary grades;
- Ficinian sympathetic magic;
- Paolini’s theory of musical-rhetorical proportion;
- Renaissance doctrines of the magically animated statue in the *Asclepius*.

It is ingenious and historically possible, but not a documented “second secret” in Camillo’s own words.

## 5. Musicological correction

Grantley McDonald’s detailed examination confirms Paolini’s extensive use of Ficino and ancient musical-ethical discourse, but finds his command of technical music theory “considerable, if patchy.” Paolini sometimes manipulates authorities through forced arithmetic to preserve the sovereignty of seven—for example, reconciling seven musical intervals with nine Muses.

This qualifies the impression of a perfectly coherent mathematical system. *Hebdomades* is an erudite, associative, rhetorical encyclopedia structured by seven, not a consistently derived acoustic science.

## 6. Diagrams and tables

The consulted descriptions do not establish a major surviving wheel or mnemonic plate comparable to Bruno’s wheels or Dolce’s reused woodcuts. The operative diagram is chiefly the book’s **architectonic arrangement of seven by seven** and its chains of correspondences.

Calling *Hebdomades* a “wheel system” would be a modern conflation with Lull or Bruno.

## The sources

# Primary genealogy

## Classical foundations

### Aristotle

*De memoria et reminiscentia*, especially 449b–453b. Memory belongs to the image-bearing part of the soul; recollection proceeds by ordered associations.

Standard editions include:

- Aristotle, *Parva Naturalia*, ed. W. D. Ross, Oxford Classical Texts.
- Aristotle, *On Memory*, in the Loeb *On the Soul; Parva Naturalia; On Breath*, trans. W. S. Hett.

### Cicero

*De oratore* II.350–60: Simonides, places, images, visual order, and the analogy of wax tablets.

### *Rhetorica ad Herennium*

Book III.16–24, especially III.29–40: the fullest ancient account of artificial memory. Its important distinction is between:

- *memoria rerum*: images for things;
- *memoria verborum*: images for words.

The striking-image rule and lawsuit example are at III.33–37.

Standard edition:

- *Rhetorica ad Herennium*, trans. Harry Caplan, Loeb Classical Library 403.

### Quintilian

*Institutio oratoria* XI.2: buildings, orderly mental traversal, reservations about memorizing every word through elaborate imagery.

## Medieval and early Renaissance sources

- Thomas Aquinas, *Summa theologiae* II–II, q. 49, a. 1, ad 2: memory as part of prudence; spiritual intentions should be clothed in sensible similitudes; orderly arrangement and solicitude assist memory.
- Albertus Magnus, *De bono*, tractate IV; commentary on memory and prudence.
- Boncompagno da Signa, *Rhetorica novissima* (1235), on remembering Heaven, Paradise, and infernal punishment.
- Jacobus Publicius, *Oratoriae artis epitoma* / *Ars memorativa* (Venice, 1482 and later editions): visual alphabets and combinatory figures.
- Pietro da Ravenna, *Phoenix sive artificiosa memoria* (Venice, 1491): many loci, personal alphabets, erotic and emotionally charged images, professional claims of prodigious recall.
- Johannes Host von Romberch, *Congestorium artificiosae memoriae* (Venice, Giorgio de Rusconi, 1520): Dolce’s principal source.

## Yates’s account and scholarship since

# What Yates argued

Yates’s narrative moves through three stages.

### Dolce: survival plus modernization

At pp. 163–64, the old Dominican apparatus survives almost intact in Venice, but receives contemporary Italian cultural images: Dante and Titian.

This remains substantially correct, with the qualification that Torre’s edition provides a much more exact account of Dolce’s editorial agency.

### Gesualdo: Neoplatonism infiltrating the old art

At pp. 164–65, Gesualdo’s Ficinian opening makes memory operate across elemental, celestial, and supercelestial worlds, recalling Camillo’s Theatre.

The textual observations are sound. The precise dependence on Camillo is less firmly demonstrated than the common Ficinian cosmology.

### Paolini: disclosure of magical planetary oratory

At pp. 166–69, Yates follows Walker and then goes beyond him. Paolini supplies the context, she argues, in which Camillo’s sevenfold memory Theatre could not only store speeches but infuse them with celestial power.

This is the chapter’s most famous and most speculative step.

# What later scholarship confirms

## Andrea Torre

Confirms the massive dependence of Dolce on Romberch but replaces the simple label “translation” with a detailed history of rewriting, adaptation, print culture, pedagogy, religious imagery, and vernacularization.

## Lina Bolzoni

Confirms that sixteenth-century memory practice interacted dynamically with:

- painting;
- illustrated books;
- iconological repertories;
- preaching;
- literature;
- libraries and knowledge classification.

Her work makes Dolce’s Titian passage and Gesualdo’s mental library culturally central rather than picturesque marginalia.

## Paolo Rossi

Places Renaissance memory within the longer history of universal method, topical logic, combinatorics, encyclopedism, and artificial language. This helps distinguish the alphabetic and classificatory machinery of Dolce and Gesualdo from Yates’s dominant magical narrative.

## Mary Carruthers

Her account of medieval memory as compositional, ethical, inventive, and affective corrects any idea that loci merely store inert data. Dolce’s reasoning, Gesualdo’s conversations with images, and the generation of speech are better understood as **trained invention** as well as storage.

## Jocelyn Penny Small

Emphasizes the relations among writing, visual organization, literacy, and mental imagery. This supports reading printed diagrams as cognitive prompts, not literal pictures resident unchanged in the brain.

## Koji Kuwakino and Diego Baldi

Their work on architectural mnemonics, excerpting, and Gesualdo’s eighteenth lecture shows the importance of bibliography and knowledge management. Gesualdo’s “library” engages Conrad Gesner’s *Pandectae* and the organization of books, not only Ficinian ascent.

## D. P. Walker and Grantley McDonald

Walker remains the basis for the magical-oratory reading of Paolini. McDonald confirms Paolini’s Ficinian interests and his engagement with music while exposing technical weaknesses, opportunistic arithmetic, and the rhetorical character of his “science” of seven.

## Fabio Vendruscolo and archival scholarship

Corrects Paolini’s biography:

- birth around 1550 rather than about 1535;
- death in September 1604, not 1605;
- documented teaching and degrees;
- no secure evidence that he knew Arabic, despite older biography;
- a more exact chronology of his Venetian appointments and Scaliger controversy.

# Scholarship named in the brief but not directly corrective here

Carruthers, Bolzoni, Rossi, Small, and Walker directly illuminate this dossier. Ong’s Ramus, Bonner and Fidora on Lull, and Sturlese, Ciliberto, Clucas, and Gatti on Bruno are essential for the broader history but do not, in the consulted material, establish a specific textual correction to Dolce, Gesualdo, or Paolini.

Likewise, the requested “Yates’s own later revisions” require qualification. *Theatre of the World* and later writings extend her Camillo–Vitruvius–Globe framework, but no explicit later withdrawal or textual demonstration of the “magically activated speech” hypothesis was found.

## History and context

# Rhetoric and scholastic psychology

The operative base remains the ancient rhetorical art:

> stable places + vivid images + fixed order + mental traversal.

Medieval scholasticism relocates memory under **prudence**, giving it ethical and devotional significance. Thomas Aquinas’s rule that intellectual intentions be clothed in sensible images legitimates corporeal imagery for preachers and theologians.

Romberch’s Dominican compilation belongs to this world. Gesualdo’s Franciscan status shows that by the late sixteenth century the technique crossed institutional and order boundaries.

# Humanism and vernacular print

Dolce was a prolific Venetian *poligrafo*: editor, translator, dramatist, art critic, and publishing professional. His book represents commercial print culture translating a specialist Latin technique for a lay vernacular audience.

Its recommendation of illustrated Giolito books is historically significant. Print does not instantly destroy artificial memory; it supplies reproducible visual materials for it.

# Dante and sacred topography

Hell and Purgatory offer ordered moral geographies. This does not prove that Dante composed the *Commedia* from a formal memory palace, though mnemonic habits illuminate its structures.

**[Disputed inference]** “Dante’s poem is itself an ars memoriae manual” is stronger than the evidence warrants. Dolce documents its later use as a source of loci.

# Painting and Venetian visual culture

Titian’s mythological *poesie* offered intense, emotionally charged figures. Dolce’s art theory praises painting’s capacity to make absent actions present and to stir bodily affect—the same properties desired of mnemonic images.

The relation is functional, not necessarily occult.

# Ficino, Hermeticism, and natural magic

Ficino’s *De vita libri tres* explains how music, words, images, odors, colours, and appropriately composed objects may acquire celestial affinities. Its safeguards distinguish licit natural or spiritual magic from demon-conjuring, though the boundary remained controversial.

Gesualdo adopts Ficinian cosmological language. Paolini engages much more directly with astral music, Cabala, planetary angels, and number symbolism.

# Camillo

Giulio Camillo’s Theatre organized the universe through seven planetary columns and seven ascending grades, joining rhetoric, memory, mythology, Cabala, and Neoplatonic cosmology.

Dolce had edited or prefaced Camillo’s posthumous works. Paolini quotes *L’Idea del Theatro*. Gesualdo shares its cosmological vocabulary, although direct dependence is less clear.

# Lullism and combinatorics

Dolce’s alphabetic squares and circles descend immediately from Publicius and Romberch, not necessarily from a direct reading of Ramon Llull. Yet the culture of rotating letters and combinable terms belongs to the wider afterlife of Lullian figures.

Bonner and Fidora have stressed that Llull’s authentic Art was originally a theological demonstrative method, not simply a mnemonic wheel. Renaissance “Lullism” often detached the combinatory figures from Llull’s missionary and logical purposes.

# Ramism

Ramus replaced image-heavy mnemonic rhetoric with dichotomous method and orderly disposition. Ong showed that Ramist method was itself spatial and visual, though hostile to the luxuriant classical image tradition.

Dolce and Gesualdo represent not a world untouched by print and method, but an alternative in which architecture, taxonomy, picture, and alphabet remained intertwined.

## People

### Lodovico Dolce

Venetian publishing professional and mediator between Latin learning and vernacular readers. He knew the circles of Aretino, Titian, Giolito, and Camillo.

**[Documented legal history, not demonstrably caused by mnemonics]** Dolce faced Holy Office proceedings in 1558 and 1565 concerning his editorial associations with suspect works. He was acquitted. No evidence found ties those proceedings to the *Dialogo della memoria*.

### Filippo Terzo

Dedicatee of Dolce’s *Dialogo*, praised as a Venetian jurist, philosopher, and orator possessing exceptional natural memory. The dedication situates mnemonic study in the world of civic eloquence and legal disputation.

### Johannes Host von Romberch

Dominican compiler whose *Congestorium* furnished Dolce’s structure, scholastic authorities, and woodcuts.

### Pietro da Ravenna

The exemplary virtuoso behind prescriptions for huge stocks of loci, familiar-person alphabets, emotionally provocative images, and professional mnemonic performance.

His claims of 100,000 or 110,000 loci are **self-advertising testimony**, not independently verified measurement.

### Filippo Gesualdo

Conventual Franciscan teacher and administrator. His later ecclesiastical advancement argues against treating *Plutosofia* as an openly heterodox manual.

### Fabio Paolini

Humanist, teacher, physician, ecclesiastic, and academician. His career included a dispute over a benefice, imprisonment by the papal nuncio, teaching at Venice, and controversy with Joseph Justus Scaliger.

### Joseph Justus Scaliger

Criticized Paolini’s unpublished commentary on Cicero under the pseudonym Yvo Villiomarus Aremoricus. Paolini answered pseudonymously as Chyameus/Chianeus Oligenius.

This is a documented literary controversy, not evidence that Scaliger accused him of magic.

### Gioseffo Zarlino and Venetian colleagues

Paolini records travelling by gondola to dine in company including Zarlino, Bernardino Partenio, Fabrizio Cecchoni, and Lorenzo Massa. This is useful evidence of learned social networks; it does not demonstrate that Zarlino endorsed Paolini’s occult conclusions.

The auctioneer, professor’s party, student who lost his memory, and accused magician belong to other figures or other episodes in the long history of memory arts. No secure primary evidence connects those anecdotes to these three authors. Importing them here would create a false dossier.

## The magic question

# Dolce

**Classification: ordinarily non-magical mnemonic technique.**

His system depends on imagination, affect, association, order, rehearsal, and scholastic psychology. Sacred and cosmic places do not by themselves make a system magical. Titian’s paintings stimulate memory through delight and vividness, not talismanic radiation.

Dolce’s astrological, medical, or cosmographic commonplaces reflect sixteenth-century natural philosophy. No incantation, spirit-binding rite, consecrated talisman, or *Ars notoria* operation is prescribed.

# Gesualdo

**Classification: cosmological and devotional, with Ficinian language; no demonstrated operative astral ritual.**

Yates is right that *Plutosofia* places memory within a Neoplatonic universe. But Gesualdo’s practical instructions remain architectural and associative.

There is no evidence located of:

- an Inquisition case caused by *Plutosofia*;
- condemnation of the book as heretical;
- talismanic consecration of its diagrams;
- angelic invocation;
- use of the *Ars notoria*.

As a senior Franciscan and later bishop, Gesualdo operated within Catholic institutions. That does not prove every proposition harmless, but it weighs against calling the work a clandestine grimoire.

# Paolini

**Classification: the strongest magical component, but its precise operation is disputed.**

Documented ingredients include:

- Ficino’s astral and musical magic;
- Cabalistic interests;
- planetary angels;
- Trithemius;
- Hermetic and Neoplatonic authorities;
- the doctrine that proportioned sound affects listeners;
- Camillo’s planetary seven.

Yet three levels must be separated:

1. **documented:** Paolini discusses celestial correspondences and magical theories;
2. **Walker’s reconstruction:** these theories imply a celestial potency in properly mixed oratorical forms;
3. **Yates’s inference:** Camillo’s mnemonic images activated stored speeches and made them magically effective.

Only the first is directly secure; the second is strong contextual interpretation; the third remains speculative.

# Ars notoria

The *Ars notoria* promised infused knowledge through prayers, ritual observance, and inspection of complex *notae*. It was repeatedly suspected or condemned because its efficacy could be attributed to demons.

The systems here differ fundamentally:

- Dolce teaches trained encoding and rehearsal.
- Gesualdo teaches architecture, imagery, classification, and deliberate cue destruction.
- Paolini theorizes sympathetic celestial efficacy in rhetoric but does not simply reproduce an *Ars notoria* ritual.

Conflating every diagrammatic memory art with the *Ars notoria* is a modern category error.

## Afterlife

# Emblems, iconology, and art

Dolce’s recommendation of paintings and illustrated books anticipates the extensive exchange among:

- mnemonic manuals;
- emblem books;
- mythographies;
- personifications;
- iconological dictionaries;
- preaching images;
- festival and theatrical design.

Cesare Ripa’s later *Iconologia* supplied standardized figures that could readily become mnemonic images, though it is not simply an ars memoriae manual.

# Literature

### Dante

Documented in Dolce as a usable map of Hell and Purgatory. Claims that Dante used exactly Dolce’s later system are anachronistic.

### Borges’s Funes

“Funes the Memorious” explores limitless retention without abstraction. The comparison with memory arts is illuminating but inverse: classical artificial memory selects and orders; Funes cannot forget or generalize.

### Umberto Eco

Eco repeatedly engaged encyclopedias, signs, libraries, universal languages, and combinatorics. His historical studies of perfect languages belong especially to the Lull–Leibniz line. Direct descent from Gesualdo or Paolini requires case-by-case textual proof.

# From Lull to Leibniz to machines

A defensible genealogy is:

- Lull: finite symbolic alphabets and combinatory figures directed toward demonstrative theology;
- Renaissance Lullism: increasingly encyclopedic and universalized combinations;
- Bruno: mobile wheels and combinatorial image systems;
- seventeenth-century projects: universal character, classification, and mechanical combination;
- Leibniz: *characteristica universalis* and *calculus ratiocinator*.

This is an intellectual genealogy, not a straight technological chain from a Lullian wheel to the computer. Digital computing additionally depends on formal logic, algebra, engineering, and electronic technologies absent from Renaissance arts.

Dolce’s phonetic alphabets are encoding schemes; Gesualdo’s library is a knowledge architecture; Paolini’s sevenfold encyclopedia is a correspondence system. They are distant conceptual analogues of indexing and data structures, not proto-computers.

# Modern memory palaces and athletes

Modern competitors continue to use:

- ordered routes;
- person-action-object combinations;
- exaggerated, comic, violent, erotic, or grotesque images;
- numerical and phonetic codes.

This closely resembles ancient and Renaissance practice at the functional level. Modern systems usually omit scholastic faculties, cosmic hierarchies, and astral efficacy.

## Controversies and disputes

| Claim | Status | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Dolce invented the system in 1562 | **False/overstatement** | The text substantially adapts Romberch, Publicius, Pietro da Ravenna, and classical sources. |
| Dolce merely translated Romberch word for word | **Too simple** | Torre documents rewriting, new dialogue form, Venetian examples, Dante, Titian, and print adaptation. |
| Romberch’s diagram was originally Dantean | **Disputed wording** | Romberch supplied the diagram; Dolce explicitly adds Dante as a guide to imagined infernal loci. |
| Titian’s paintings functioned as magical talismans | **Unsupported** | Dolce recommends them as vivid image models. |
| Gesualdo consciously rebuilt Camillo’s three worlds | **Yatesian inference** | Shared Ficinian cosmology is documented; exact architectural dependence is not demonstrated. |
| *Plutosofia* is mainly a magical work | **Misleading** | Most of it concerns conventional loci, images, physiology, order, libraries, and forgetting. |
| Gesualdo invented an effective technology for erasing memory | **Modern exaggeration** | He gives imagined cue-obscuring and image-replacement exercises, not verified erasure. |
| Paolini’s *Hebdomades* is a complete mnemonic system | **Misclassification** | It is a seven-by-seven commentary and encyclopedia with mnemonic and Camillan relevance. |
| Paolini had mastered musical mathematics | **Qualified/corrected** | McDonald finds substantial but patchy knowledge and forced numerical reasoning. |
| Paolini knew Arabic | **Older biographical tradition, now doubted** | Vendruscolo reports that medical editing does not establish Arabic competence. |
| Paolini was born c. 1535 and died in 1605 | **Corrected** | Archival research supports 1550–September 1604. |
| Camillo’s Theatre magically activated speeches | **Yates’s interpretation** | Built from Walker, Paolini, Ficino, and Camillo; no direct statement by Camillo proves it. |
| The Apollo image generated a solar speech | **Yates’s hypothetical example** | Not a worked example documented in Camillo or Paolini. |
| The Uranici practised ceremonial conjuration | **Unsupported** | Their celestial interests and citations do not prove ritual practice. |
| Dolce’s or Gesualdo’s work was prosecuted for heresy | **No evidence found** | Dolce’s separate Holy Office cases concerned other editorial matters; none located for *Plutosofia*. |
| The three writers constituted a self-conscious “Venetian sequel” | **Modern historiographical construction** | The phrase and narrative grouping come from Yates. |
| Their diagrams are direct ancestors of computers | **Metaphorical genealogy** | There is continuity in encoding and combination, but no direct engineering descent. |

### What Yates got wrong—or left insufficiently proved

1. She compressed Dolce too tightly into “Romberch translated,” underplaying the cultural work of adaptation.
2. She blurred the difference between a reused cosmographic woodcut and Dolce’s specifically Dantean textual addition.
3. She read Gesualdo mainly through Camillo and Ficino, underplaying bibliography, libraries, classification, and the art of forgetting.
4. She treated Paolini as evidence for a secret of Camillo that Paolini cannot directly prove.
5. Her “Apollo image → solar speech” is a reconstruction, not primary testimony.
6. She presented the number seven as unusually explanatory without sufficiently testing forced or commonplace uses of seven.
7. Like much scholarship of her period, she relied on older biographical authorities for Paolini that archival research has corrected.

### What she saw exceptionally well

1. The survival of medieval mnemonic machinery inside sophisticated late Renaissance print culture.
2. The importance of Venetian painting and illustrated books for mnemonic imagery.
3. The intersection of memory, rhetoric, music, cosmology, and Ficinian magic.
4. Paolini’s importance for contextualizing Camillo.
5. The need to treat occult philosophy as intellectually serious rather than as mere irrational debris.

## Open questions

1. A full stemmatic comparison of the 1562, 1575, and 1586 *Dialogo* editions, including every reset diagram and textual correction, remains to be published or at least was not located.
2. The exact bibliographical relation between all surviving 1592 copies and the 1600 *Plutosofia* requires copy-by-copy collation.
3. Gesualdo’s precise use of Ficino needs a source-critical list of quotations, not only Yates’s summary.
4. The extent of direct Camillan borrowing in Gesualdo remains less certain than the shared cosmological vocabulary.
5. Paolini’s lost commentary on Hermogenes might have clarified the relation between seven Ideas, memory, and rhetorical composition.
6. No secure autograph of Paolini is known; additional lecture notes or academy records could distinguish performed discourse from printed revision.
7. Whether Paolini’s hearers understood planetary oratory literally, analogically, aesthetically, or apologetically remains unknown.
8. Yates’s magical-activation hypothesis needs a line-by-line comparison of *Hebdomades*, Camillo, Ficino, Trithemius, and the Latin *Asclepius*.
9. The actual use of these manuals by students, lawyers, preachers, and orators is much less documented than their prescriptive literature.
10. No verified user notebook reconstructing a complete working Dolce or Gesualdo palace was found.
11. There is no adequate evidence that the three authors regarded themselves as members of a unified mnemonic school.
12. There is no documented chain from these particular works to Borges, Eco, modern memory athletes, or computing; those are comparative afterlives unless direct citations can be produced.
