# Leibniz: Mnemonica, combinatoria, characteristica: Research Dossier

## Basic identification

**Name:** *Mnemonica, ars combinatoria, characteristica universalis*

**Exponent:** Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz (1646–1716)

**Date:** 1666–1667 for the first public formulations; continuously revised through the 1670s–1690s, with related work extending to Leibniz’s death in 1716.

**Principal source texts:**

- *Dissertatio de arte combinatoria* (Leipzig: Johann Simon Fick and Johann Polycarp Seubold, 1666).
- *Nova methodus discendae docendaeque jurisprudentiae* (Frankfurt: Johann David Zunner, 1667), General Part, §§22–26, especially §§22–24.
- Leibniz’s unpublished *Mnemonica, sive praecepta varia de memoria excitanda* and excerpts from Lambert Schenkel and Adam Bruxius, formerly cited under Hanover classifications Phil. VI.19 and Phil. VII.B.III.7.
- *Consilium de encyclopaedia nova conscribenda methodo inventoria* (15 June 1679), A VI,4, no. 81.
- *Calculus ratiocinator seu artificium facile et infallibiliter ratiocinandi* (1679), A VI,4, no. 68.
- *Introductio ad Encyclopaediam arcanam* (probably 1684; formerly dated 1679 or 1683–85), A VI,4, no. 126.
- *Fundamenta calculi ratiocinatoris* (probably 1688), A VI,4, no. 192, pp. 917–922.
- *De modis combinandi characteres* (probably 1688), A VI,4, no. 193.
- Related essays and letters collected by Louis Couturat in *Opuscules et fragments inédits de Leibniz* and discussed in *La logique de Leibniz*.

**Principle in one sentence:** Sensible signs (*notae*) support memory by being associated with remembered things; concepts are analyzed into elementary terms, assigned characters, and recombined under explicit rules so that remembering, discovering, and judging can increasingly be externalized as orderly symbolic operations.

**Yates:** *The Art of Memory*, Chapter XVII, especially pp. 369 and 380–388 in the original 1966 Routledge and Kegan Paul pagination; the title-page emblem discussed there is her Plate 11.

### Evidentiary status

- **Documented text:** Leibniz explicitly defines *Mnemonica*, *nota*, sensible association, visual precedence, “cells” or places, topics, combination, and formal judgment in *Nova methodus* §§22–25.
- **Documented text:** The 1666 *Dissertatio* treats combinations arithmetically and proposes their application to logic, law, theology, music, and other sciences.
- **Documented text:** Later manuscripts propose an alphabet of human thoughts, characteristic signs, encyclopedic analysis, and calculi for reasoning.
- **Scholarly reconstruction:** These dispersed undertakings form one evolving program involving encyclopedia, universal characteristic, and rational calculus.
- **Yates’s interpretation:** The whole program is the rationalized heir of the classical art of memory combined with Lullism.
- **Disputed:** That the *characteristica universalis* “comes straight out of” the memory tradition, or that Leibniz’s infinitesimal calculus was generated by that tradition.
- **Disproved or very poorly supported:** That Leibniz belonged to a Rosicrucian fraternity at Nuremberg.
- **Modern invention:** Treating Leibniz’s project as a completed programming language, digital computer, artificial-intelligence architecture, or operational “universal memory palace.”

## The system in detail

## 1. Three related projects, not one finished apparatus

The catalogue title conveniently joins three strands, but it can mislead if it suggests that Leibniz published a single integrated mnemonic machine.

### 1.1 *Mnemonica*

This is the art of retaining propositions and material through signs, sensory associations, verbal devices, pictures, and orderly placement.

### 1.2 *Ars combinatoria*

This is the systematic enumeration and recombination of terms, cases, or conditions. Its purposes include:

- ensuring completeness;
- finding possibilities that ordinary thought overlooks;
- classifying legal and logical cases;
- producing arguments from topical relations;
- testing whether combinations are possible, impossible, useful, or redundant.

### 1.3 *Characteristica universalis*

This is the projected exact system of characters corresponding to elementary concepts and their lawful compositions. It would serve simultaneously as:

- a universal script;
- a perspicuous representation of concepts;
- an aid to memory;
- an instrument of discovery;
- a medium of demonstration;
- the language in which a *calculus ratiocinator* could operate.

**Documented conclusion:** Leibniz repeatedly places mnemonic, combinatorial, characteristic, methodological, logical, and encyclopedic arts within the same *scientia generalis*.

**Necessary qualification:** No surviving text gives a completed character set, completed encyclopedia, and complete universal calculus. There is consequently no single reconstructable “Leibniz memory machine” analogous to a fully described architectural mnemonic.

## 2. The *Nova methodus*: memory as a didactic faculty

The first edition appeared at Frankfurt in autumn 1667. The authoritative modern edition is *Sämtliche Schriften und Briefe*, Series VI, vol. 1, no. 10; a revised overview table survives from Leibniz’s later preparations for a second edition. A separate later supplement and revision are printed in A VI,2.

### 2.1 The faculties

In General Part §22 Leibniz distinguishes three specifically human habits:

> “*Habitus hominibus proprii sunt vel Memoriae, vel Inventionis, vel Judicii*.”

“Habits proper to human beings concern memory, invention, or judgment.”

From these come three didactic arts:

> “*Mnemonica, Topica, Analytica*.”

One can remember, make, and judge propositions. Topics and analytics together constitute logic; method is then added:

> “*Mnemonica praestat materiam, methodologia formam, logica applicationem materiae ad formam*.”

“Mnemonics supplies the matter, methodology the form, and logic the application of matter to form.”  
—*Nova methodus*, General Part, §22; 1667 ed., pp. 10–11; A VI,1, no. 10.

This is not merely a memory tip. It gives memory a structural position in intellectual activity: it supplies the content upon which ordering and judgment work.

### 2.2 The fundamental mnemonic sign

Section 23 states:

> “*Mnemonicae fundamentum est res aliqua sensibilis, quae dicitur Nota cum re memoranda certa relatione juncta*.”

“The foundation of mnemonics is some sensible thing, called a *nota*, joined by a definite relation to the thing to be remembered.”

The relation is either:

1. **comparison**—especially likeness or unlikeness; or
2. **connection**—such as whole and part, one part and another, cause and effect, sign and signified.

This passage is decisive because it shows that a *nota* is not necessarily a conventional written symbol. It is any sensible item linked appropriately to remembered content.

Leibniz relates this to words:

> “*Verba enim non tantum signa sunt cogitationis meae praesentis ad alios, sed et notae cogitationis meae praeteritae ad me ipsum*.”

“Words are not only signs of my present thought to others, but also marks of my past thought to myself.”

He attributes this insight to Hobbes’s *De corpore*. Words therefore have both communicative and autonoetic functions: they convey present thought outward and cue past thought inward.

### 2.3 Which signs work best?

Leibniz says the most mnemonic signs are those that are most strongly sensible. His examples are:

- words heard with pleasure;
- verse;
- striking or “clapping” terms used in school vocabularies;
- allusions and puns;
- phonetic affinities among languages;
- Wilhelmus Schickard’s devices for learning Hebrew roots;
- visual signs, which are stronger than auditory ones;
- Egyptian hieroglyphs;
- Chinese characters;
- pictures;
- the distribution of things into “cells” and definite places.

He writes:

> “*Non tantum autem notae auditus, sed et multo magis notae visus ad memoriam pertinent*.”

“Not only auditory signs, but still more visual signs, belong to memory.”

### 2.4 What serves as places?

Leibniz mentions:

> “*dispositiones rerum per cellulas, in obvia conclavis parte, et reliquae in certa loca distributiones*.”

“Arrangements of things in cells, on a conspicuous part of a room, and other distributions into definite places.”

The passage confirms familiarity with spatial mnemonics but does not prescribe:

- the dimensions or spacing of loci;
- a mandatory architectural route;
- the classical five-locus interval;
- lighting rules;
- restrictions on repeated loci;
- a numbered palace;
- a fixed procedure for forward and backward recitation.

Instead, Leibniz directs readers to Johann Heinrich Alsted’s *Thesaurus mnemonicus* and Jean-Cécile Frey’s *Via ad scientias et artes*.

**Documented text:** Spatial arrangement is useful.

**Absence of evidence:** Leibniz does not provide a complete local-memory rulebook here.

**Yates’s interpretation:** This passage places him squarely inside the tradition descending from the *Ad Herennium*.

**Correction:** The resemblance is genuine, but Leibniz’s immediate presentation is eclectic and didactic. It combines rhetoric, pedagogy, signs, language learning, pictures, charts, and encyclopedic order rather than merely reproducing the classical architectural method.

### 2.5 What serves as images?

The category is unusually broad:

- the visible shape of a word;
- a pictorial representation;
- a hieroglyph;
- a Chinese character as Leibniz understood it;
- an emblem;
- an arranged classroom object;
- a conventional character;
- a sound association;
- a rhyme;
- a sensible sign related by resemblance, contrast, causal connection, part-whole relation, or signification.

Unlike the anonymous author of the *Ad Herennium*, Leibniz does not insist in this passage on grotesque, violent, beautiful, bloody, comic, or emotionally extraordinary human images.

**Important difference:** Leibniz’s *nota* generalizes “image” into the broader category of a perceptible sign.

## 3. Order and retrieval

### 3.1 Mnemonic retrieval

The cue is encountered or mentally revived; its established relation leads to the remembered content. Retrieval works through:

- similarity;
- dissimilarity;
- contiguity or connection;
- cause and effect;
- whole and part;
- sign and signified;
- position in a visible or conceptual arrangement;
- verbal or phonetic association.

Yates calls the first three relations “the three Aristotelian laws of association.” That formulation is interpretive shorthand. Leibniz’s actual list is more articulated, including mereological and causal relations.

### 3.2 Topical retrieval

Section 24 moves from memory to invention:

> “*Topicae seu artis inventivae fundamentum sunt loci, id est relationes transcendentes, ut totum, causa, materia, simile*.”

“The foundations of Topics, or the art of invention, are places—that is, transcendent relations such as whole, cause, matter, and similar.”

Here *loci* are no longer primarily architectural places. They are logical or metaphysical relations. Propositions arise by connecting things through those relations and applying combinatorial art.

This passage marks the critical transition:

| Mnemonic locus | Topical locus |
|---|---|
| A cell, picture-position, room, or sensible cue | A general relation such as cause, whole, matter, or similarity |
| Retrieves something deposited | Generates or locates possible propositions |
| Primarily memorial | Primarily inventive |
| Spatial or perceptual | Logical and relational |

The two senses coexist. Their analogy—ordered places in perception and ordered relations in thought—is central to Rossi’s and Yates’s histories.

### 3.3 Analytical checking

Section 25 reduces analysis, or the art of judging, to two broad rules:

> “*Ut nulla vox admittatur, nisi explicata; ut nulla propositio, nisi probata*.”

“Admit no term unless it is explained, and no proposition unless it is proved.”

Thus the sequence is:

1. Mnemonics supplies retrievable matter.
2. Topics and combination generate propositions.
3. Method arranges them.
4. Analysis tests their terms and proofs.
5. A future characteristic would render these operations visible and calculable.

## 4. The “little memory treatise”

Yates, following Couturat and Paolo Rossi, reports unpublished Hanover material headed *Mnemonica, sive praecepta varia de memoria excitanda*—“Mnemonics, or various precepts for stimulating memory.”

The older classifications cited by Yates are:

- Hanover, Phil. VI.19;
- Hanover, Phil. VII.B.III.7.

The latter included material concerning Adam Bruxius’s *Simonides Redivivus* (Leipzig, 1610); the former included mnemonic extracts or notes associated with Lambert Schenkel.

**Documented archival fact:** Leibniz collected or excerpted mnemonic authors and left notes on memory.

**Caution:** “A little memory treatise by Leibniz” may imply more authorial unity than the papers warrant. Some material is excerpt, bibliographical note, or compilation rather than an original finished treatise.

**Open archival problem:** Modern reassignment of every old Phil. shelfmark to current LH/Akademie classification should be checked directly against the Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz Bibliothek catalogue. Digitized finding aids do not presently expose the entire mnemonic dossier as a continuous facsimile.

## 5. The *Disputatio de casibus perplexis* emblem

Leibniz defended *Disputatio inauguralis de casibus perplexis in jure* at Altdorf in November 1666 and received the doctorate in February 1667.

Its title-page emblem shows a branching juridical situation in which claimants or competing legal principles converge upon a disputed object. Yates reproduces the figure as Plate 11 and proposes that it can be read as a “local memory” for lawsuits: the emblem simultaneously represents persons, claims, relationships, and the disputed thing.

### Status of this reading

- **Documented:** The emblem exists in the early printing.
- **Documented:** Leibniz’s thesis analyzes legally perplexing cases.
- **Yates’s interpretation:** The picture is effectively a local-memory diagram.
- **Not documented:** Leibniz nowhere says in the thesis that a student should memorize lawsuits by depositing images in this emblem.
- **Plausible reconstruction:** In the context of §23 of the *Nova methodus*, emblematic legal diagrams could function as visual *notae*.
- **Overstatement to avoid:** It is not a published, worked mnemonic exercise comparable to the lawsuit image in *Ad Herennium* III.33.

The title-page emblem therefore belongs in the dossier as a possible application, not as proof of an explicit procedure.

## 6. The 1678 “series of personages”

Yates reports a 1678 remark in which Leibniz recommends ordered series of persons—patriarchs, apostles, rulers, or emperors—as supports for memorizing historical succession.

**Probable function:** A familiar finite series supplies ordinal pegs. New material can be associated with Abraham, Isaac, Jacob; Peter, Andrew, James; Augustus, Tiberius, Caligula, and so forth.

**Tradition:** Such lists had long been used in chronicle tables, genealogies, pedagogical diagrams, saints’ cycles, illustrated histories, and mnemonic alphabets.

**Caution:** The brief reference does not establish a unique Leibnizian system. It is an application of serial peg-memory using culturally fixed lists.

## 7. The *Dissertatio de arte combinatoria* (1666)

## 7.1 Publication and status

The original title promises:

> “*nova etiam Artis Meditandi, seu Logicae Inventionis semina sparguntur*.”

“New seeds of an art of thinking, or logic of invention, are also scattered.”

The work grew from a Leipzig philosophical disputation. Leibniz was nineteen when he composed it and twenty when it appeared.

The first edition is:

Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz, *Dissertatio de arte combinatoria* (Leipzig: Johann Simon Fick and Johann Polycarp Seubold, 1666), quarto, [8], 78, [2] pages. The BnF catalogue records a copy as V-12141 and digital copy NUMM-62578. A Biblioteca Nazionale Centrale di Firenze copy is digitized through Internet Archive.

A bookseller reprinted it at Frankfurt in 1690 without Leibniz’s authorization or clear notice that it was a second edition. In a letter to Morell of 1 October 1697 Leibniz complained that parts smelled of the schoolboy—“*sentent presque son petit garçon*”—and that the reprint made it appear to be current work. He nevertheless continued to defend its fundamental aim.

### 7.2 Sources named in the preface

Leibniz surveys predecessors including:

- Ramon Llull;
- Heinrich Cornelius Agrippa;
- Giordano Bruno—“*Jordanus Brunus*”;
- Johann Heinrich Alsted;
- Athanasius Kircher;
- earlier mathematicians of permutations and combinations.

Leibniz remarks that Bruno had called the art *combinatoria*. This is direct evidence that he knew Bruno by name and situated his project in a received combinatorial literature.

**Not established:** Direct study of every Brunian mnemonic work.

**Not established:** Adoption of Bruno’s astral images, magical seals, or operational ritual.

### 7.3 Combinations and variations

Leibniz distinguishes operations broadly corresponding to:

- combinations without regard to order;
- arrangements or variations where order matters;
- repetitions;
- classes of combinations;
- partitions of numbers.

He uses terminology such as *complexiones*, *variationes*, *classis*, and *caput variationis*. The mathematical portion contains youthful errors, but Eberhard Knobloch has shown that Leibniz’s later unpublished combinatorial work developed results of considerable importance, including work related to partitions and symmetric functions.

### 7.4 The alphabet of thought

The logical ambition is to analyze composite concepts into simple terms and treat these as letters of an alphabet. Complex concepts would be comparable to words formed from letters.

The basic scheme is:

1. Identify primitive terms.
2. Assign marks or numbers to them.
3. Represent a compound concept by the composition or product of its primitives.
4. Enumerate possible combinations.
5. eliminate contradictions and redundancies;
6. use the remainder to discover possible propositions or cases.

This is already more than memorization. A mnemonic alphabet recalls; a combinatorial alphabet generates; a characteristic alphabet should also display logical structure and enable proof.

### 7.5 Worked applications in the *Dissertatio*

Leibniz applies combination to several domains.

#### Law

Legal cases can be generated from combinations of relevant circumstances, parties, rights, acts, and exceptions. The practical object is completeness: the jurist should not overlook a possible case.

#### Logic

Given a subject and primitive predicates, combinations enumerate possible complex predicates and propositions. Traditional syllogistic modes also become objects of systematic classification.

#### Theology

Leibniz applies combinations to divine attributes and to his youthful mathematical-style proof of God’s existence. This use is philosophical theology, not mnemonic magic.

#### Music

Combinations can enumerate dispositions of stops or registers in an organ and possible musical configurations.

#### Poetry and language

Letters, words, and metrical units can be permuted. Leibniz recognizes that mechanically possible strings greatly exceed meaningful ones.

#### Geometry

Combinations help enumerate figures, arrangements, and incidences.

### 7.6 The diagram of the elements

The most important surviving printed diagram joins two squares.

One square places the four elements at the vertices:

- fire;
- air;
- water;
- earth.

Its diagonals join contraries:

- fire and water;
- air and earth.

A second square places the four primary qualities at its corners:

- hot;
- cold;
- dry;
- moist.

The sides distinguish possible combinations; the diagonals mark impossible or contrary conjunctions. The adjacent qualities generate the elements:

- hot + dry = fire;
- hot + moist = air;
- cold + moist = water;
- cold + dry = earth.

The figure combines:

- Aristotelian elemental physics;
- the traditional logical square of opposition;
- Lullian letter-and-relation diagrams;
- Leibniz’s concern with formally enumerable combinations.

**Documented:** The diagram survives in printed copies.

**Scholarly reconstruction:** Teun Koetsier reads it as evidence that the early Leibniz remained closer to Llull’s cosmological art than a modern combinatorial mathematician would be.

**Qualification:** It is a static relational diagram, not a surviving set of concentric movable wheels. Describing Leibniz’s printed figure as “Bruno’s wheel rationalized” goes beyond the object.

## 8. From *ars combinatoria* to *characteristica universalis*

## 8.1 The encyclopedic prerequisite

The characteristic requires an inventory of elementary concepts. Leibniz consequently connects it to an encyclopedia that would:

- collect established knowledge;
- define terms;
- distinguish primitive from derivative concepts;
- order sciences;
- record observations and experiments;
- expose lacunae;
- supply material for invention.

The encyclopedia is therefore not merely a reference book. It is the database and semantic analysis needed before valid characteristic signs can be assigned.

**Documented:** Leibniz repeatedly connects the characteristic to encyclopedic analysis.

**Scholarly reconstruction:** Couturat’s influential interpretation treats encyclopedia, characteristic, and calculus as three inseparable pillars.

**Qualification:** Leibniz sometimes experiments with formal calculi despite lacking the complete encyclopedia. The logical manuscripts are therefore partial feasibility studies rather than pieces of one completed master notation.

## 8.2 What is a character?

For Leibniz, a character is a perceptible sign used in reasoning. Characters can be written, drawn, engraved, or otherwise formed. Their value increases when their internal construction represents the structure of what they signify.

Examples or comparanda include:

- numerals;
- algebraic notation;
- geometrical diagrams;
- chemical signs;
- musical notation;
- astronomical symbols;
- logical letters;
- Egyptian hieroglyphs as Leibniz understood them;
- Chinese writing as Leibniz understood it;
- John Wilkins’s and George Dalgarno’s philosophical-language projects;
- John Dee’s *Monas hieroglyphica*, usually as an example of an obscure rather than adequate universal sign.

A mere label is useful for memory. A well-formed characteristic sign should do more: its parts and operations should mirror conceptual composition.

## 8.3 *Fundamenta calculi ratiocinatoris*

The manuscript is now dated approximately 1688 and edited as A VI,4, no. 192, pp. 917–922.

Leibniz begins from the dependence of reasoning upon signs. Ordinary languages aid thought but contain ambiguities and cannot by themselves expose errors through the construction of their expressions. Arithmetic and algebra provide the model: one can inspect and transform written characters according to rules.

A calculus is defined in terms of transformations of formulas under prescribed rules. Reasoning can then become the connection and substitution of characters.

The famous conclusion is:

> “*Calculemus*.”

“Let us calculate.”

The surrounding claim is that where a controversy can be decided from given data, disputants should set aside verbal quarrels, take up pens, and calculate.

**Documented:** Leibniz makes this proposal.

**Not documented:** He possessed a universal decision procedure for every philosophical, political, religious, or empirical controversy.

**Logical limitation:** Even a successful formal calculus requires agreed premises, definitions, and data. Leibniz acknowledged that empirical truth and the analysis of primitive concepts posed separate difficulties.

## 8.4 *Introductio ad Encyclopaediam arcanam*

The critical edition dates this work probably to 1684, A VI,4, no. 126. Earlier scholarship often assigned it to 1679 or broadly to 1683–85.

It defines general science as a science of what is thinkable and lists, among its components:

> “*Artem reminiscendi seu Mnemonicam, Artem characteristicam seu symbolicam, Artem Combinatoriam … Artem Lullianam, Caballam sapientum, Magiam naturalem*.”

“The art of recollecting or mnemonics, characteristic or symbolic art, combinatorial art … Lullian art, the cabala of the wise, natural magic.”

The list is genuine and important. But it is a classificatory inventory of arts that general science must absorb, reform, or place—not an avowal that the universal characteristic is itself magical.

The text also says the sciences form a continuum “like the ocean.” Modern scholarship emphasizes this fluid organization over the claim that Leibniz simply inherited a fixed Renaissance occult encyclopedia.

## 9. Was there a wheel?

No complete Leibnizian mnemonic wheel comparable to those in Llull or Bruno survives as the operating center of this system.

What survives instead is:

- the printed elemental square;
- genealogical or branching models;
- tables of combinations;
- arrays of letters and numbers;
- logical matrices;
- tables of definitions and authors;
- schemes for characteristic numbers;
- diagrams and machines in other mathematical or technical projects.

**Documented absence:** Neither the *Nova methodus* nor the mature characteristic manuscripts supplies a canonical set of rotating mnemonic disks.

**Modern conflation:** Illustrations on websites often place a Lullian or Brunian volvelle beside Leibniz and imply that it is “Leibniz’s universal-characteristic wheel.” Unless the provenance is stated, that is misleading.

## 3. Manuscript variants and dating

The characteristic project survives largely as drafts rather than authorially finished books. Variants matter because:

- the same proposal is reformulated under different titles;
- dates assigned by Couturat, Gerhardt, and twentieth-century editors have sometimes been revised;
- the term *characteristica universalis* does not denote one stable document;
- “universal language,” “general characteristic,” “symbolic art,” and “calculus” overlap without always being identical;
- papers once treated as fragments of one work are now edited as separate manuscripts.

A particularly clear example is the *Introductio ad Encyclopaediam arcanam*: older accounts give 1679 or 1683; the A VI,4 concordance proposes 1684 with a question mark. *Fundamenta* is generally placed in 1688, sometimes 1688–89.

## Yates’s account and scholarship since

## 1. Yates’s argument

Yates’s concluding chapter traces the survival and transformation of artificial memory into seventeenth-century method. She says at p. 369:

> “Our history which began with Simonides must not end before Leibniz.”

At pp. 380–381 she treats the *Nova methodus* definition of mnemonic signs and writes:

> “Here the mind of the great Leibniz is moving on lines which take us straight back to *Ad Herennium*.”

At p. 382:

> “Leibniz is emerging out of a Renaissance tradition.”

She specifies that tradition as repeated attempts to combine Lullism with classical artificial memory.

At p. 384 she argues that new mathematical characters made the infinitesimal calculus possible and calls that calculus a fragment or specimen of the incomplete universal characteristic.

At p. 387 she compares Leibniz with Bruno’s four guides—Love, Art, Mathesis, and Magic—while proposing that in Leibniz:

- magic is removed;
- genuine mathematics replaces occult *mathesis*;
- art becomes calculus;
- charitable or irenic love remains.

At p. 388 she interprets the monad as a “living mirror” of the universe and sees marks of the Hermetic tradition.

### Her chain of descent

Yates’s implicit genealogy is:

Classical local memory  
→ medieval scholastic association  
→ Llull’s combinatorial figures  
→ Renaissance image magic and Bruno’s wheels  
→ seventeenth-century encyclopedism and real characters  
→ Leibniz’s characteristic and calculus.

### Her strongest contribution

Yates correctly insists that Leibniz’s logical symbolism did not emerge in an intellectual vacuum. His texts explicitly mention:

- mnemonics;
- places;
- images and emblems;
- hieroglyphs;
- Chinese writing;
- Llull;
- Bruno;
- Alsted;
- Kircher;
- universal language;
- encyclopedia;
- combinatorial invention.

### Her principal overreach

She sometimes turns contextual affinity into a genetic claim. Her key sentence is expressly conditional:

> “If … Leibniz’s *characteristica* as a whole comes straight out of the memory tradition…”

The “if” is crucial. Later retellings often omit it and present the genealogy as established fact.

---

## 2. Paolo Rossi

Rossi’s *Clavis universalis* appeared in Italian in 1960, before Yates’s book. The English version is *Logic and the Art of Memory: The Quest for a Universal Language*, trans. Stephen Clucas (2000).

Rossi gives greater weight than Yates to:

- encyclopedic classification;
- logic of discovery;
- the passage from images to orderly concepts;
- philosophical languages;
- the transformation rather than occult survival of mnemonics.

Rossi’s line from Llull to Leibniz remains influential because it identifies real textual intermediaries: Agrippa, Bruno, Alsted, Comenius, Kircher, Schenkel, Baconian projects, and philosophical-language schemes.

**Confirmed:** Leibniz knew and cited several members of this sequence.

**Still reconstructed:** A direct causal line from Bruno’s mnemonic wheels to the mature characteristic.

---

## 3. Carruthers, Small, Bolzoni, and the enlarged history of memory

Mary Carruthers’s *The Book of Memory* and Jocelyn Penny Small’s *Wax Tablets of the Mind* complicate Yates’s concentration on striking images and architectural loci. They emphasize:

- textual layout;
- division and rubrication;
- ethical formation;
- habitual recollection;
- oral-written interaction;
- the page as a memory surface;
- compositional memory, not just verbatim storage.

This correction matters for Leibniz. His lists, tables, excerpts, indices, encyclopedic plans, commonplace practices, and archival methods fit a broad culture of ordered memory better than a narrow chain of fantastic images.

Lina Bolzoni’s *The Gallery of Memory* likewise shows that literary, emblematic, devotional, and pictorial culture cannot be reduced to occult mnemonic systems. Leibniz’s emblematic references and legal diagrams belong to this larger visual-textual ecology.

**Correction to Yates:** The history does not run exclusively through the classical place-and-image technique.

**Confirmation of Yates:** Visual organization, emblem, classification, and memory remained deeply interwoven in early modern intellectual work.

---

## 4. Lull scholarship: Bonner and Fidora

Anthony Bonner’s studies, especially *The Art and Logic of Ramon Llull*, have clarified that Llull’s Art was not primarily an artificial-memory system. It was a demonstrative and missionary method whose alphabet, figures, conditions, and combinatorial procedures were intended to reason about divine dignities and principles.

Alexander Fidora and other recent Lull scholars emphasize:

- the Art’s scholastic and interreligious setting;
- its changing phases;
- the difference between authentic Llull and pseudo-Llull;
- the logical and theological functions of the figures;
- the danger of reading the Art backward from Renaissance occult Lullism.

**Correction to Yates:** Calling Llull’s Art a variant of classical artificial memory is too restrictive.

**Relevant confirmation:** Renaissance and seventeenth-century readers did combine Lullian figures with mnemonic, Cabalistic, encyclopedic, and magical projects. Leibniz inherited that reception as well as elements of the authentic logical Art.

**Open point:** Which Llullian texts Leibniz read directly, rather than through Agrippa, Bruno, Alsted, Kircher, or secondary compilations, remains text-specific.

---

## 5. Bruno scholarship

Work by Rita Sturlese, Michele Ciliberto, Hilary Gatti, Stephen Clucas, and the Warburg Institute’s Bruno project has corrected the image of a single, transparent “Hermetic Bruno.”

The corrections include:

- Bruno’s mnemonic works must be reconstructed from their precise Latin texts and diagrams.
- Their figures have distinct functions and should not all be treated as interchangeable Lullian wheels.
- The cognitive burden of Bruno’s image combinations is substantial.
- Magic, metaphysics, rhetoric, psychology, and combinatory technique do not collapse into one operation.
- Yates’s Bruno-centered account of Renaissance Hermetism has been highly productive but is not a settled explanatory consensus.

**For Leibniz:** His citation of “Jordanus Brunus” is evidence of awareness; it is not evidence that he adopted Bruno’s magical imagery or belonged to a Brunian society.

---

## 6. Ramus and Ong

Walter J. Ong’s *Ramus, Method, and the Decay of Dialogue* made dichotomous charts, classroom method, and spatialized logic central to the history of early modern thought.

Leibniz does not simply become a Ramist. He criticizes oversimplified method and rejects the idea that one universal dichotomous outline can substitute for discovery. Yet Ramist and post-Ramist pedagogical culture is part of the environment in which:

- knowledge was tabulated;
- arts were divided;
- curricula were schematized;
- visible order supported recollection;
- dialectic absorbed functions formerly assigned to rhetoric.

**Scholarly reconstruction:** Leibniz’s program can be read as a hybrid of mnemonic, topical, combinatorial, and dialectical-methodical traditions.

**Caution:** Yates’s direct Bruno-to-Leibniz line underplays the independent importance of legal method, scholastic logic, Ramist pedagogy, Hobbes, Bacon, mathematical notation, and contemporary universal-language projects.

---

## 7. Later logical scholarship

Couturat’s recovery of Leibniz as a precursor of mathematical logic was foundational but tended to systematize fragmentary manuscripts into a single stable “logic of Leibniz.”

Later scholarship distinguishes more carefully among:

- *characteristica universalis*;
- *lingua characterica*;
- *calculus ratiocinator*;
- formal calculi for limited domains;
- universal language;
- encyclopedia;
- characteristic geometry;
- algebraic and arithmetic models.

Volker Peckhaus argues that *characteristica* and *calculus* should not automatically be identified. A characteristic supplies articulated expressions; a calculus supplies transformation rules. One can devise limited calculi without possessing the universal semantic inventory.

Bertran-San Millán further cautions against a simple direct line to Frege and Schröder: much of Leibniz’s logical Nachlass was unavailable until long after his death, and later logicians reconstructed his ambitions through partial editions.

**Correction to popular history:** Leibniz did not hand an operative symbolic logic directly to Boole, Frege, or modern computer science.

## History and context

## 1. Classical rhetoric

The remote ancestry includes:

- the Simonides story in Cicero and Quintilian;
- *Rhetorica ad Herennium* III.16–24;
- Cicero, *De oratore* II.350–360;
- Quintilian, *Institutio oratoria* XI.2;
- Aristotle, *De memoria et reminiscentia*;
- rhetorical topics.

The classical method fixes order by a stable sequence of places and retrieves speech by inspecting the images deposited there.

Leibniz preserves:

- signs attached to things;
- visual superiority;
- ordered places;
- association;
- topics as “places.”

He changes the emphasis toward:

- conceptual analysis;
- exhaustive enumeration;
- external notation;
- invention;
- proof.

## 2. Scholastic psychology

Medieval faculty psychology treated memory as an internal sense involving phantasms. Scholastic discussions of resemblance, contrariety, contiguity, cause, and ordered recollection form part of the background to Leibniz’s relational account.

Leibniz’s youthful psychology is not simply Thomist. It also absorbs:

- Hobbesian sign theory;
- mechanistic explanation;
- Aristotelian terminology;
- Protestant pedagogy;
- mathematical models.

## 3. Lullism

Llull supplied:

- letters for elementary principles;
- figures displaying relations;
- combinatorial enumeration;
- an art intended to discover and demonstrate;
- the hope of resolving religious disputes rationally.

Leibniz explicitly acknowledges Llull but criticizes received Lullian practice for failing to find the true primitives and valid rules.

## 4. Baconian invention

Francis Bacon criticized arts that merely decorate or retrieve what is already known and called for a genuine *ars inveniendi*. Leibniz’s *Dissertatio* similarly aims to make combination productive rather than merely memorial.

The Baconian contribution is therefore not pictorial but programmatic:

- organized natural history;
- tables;
- exhaustive collection;
- systematic invention;
- reform of learning;
- collaborative institutions.

## 5. Universal-language projects

Leibniz worked in a century of projects by:

- Francis Lodwick;
- George Dalgarno;
- John Wilkins;
- Cave Beck;
- Athanasius Kircher;
- Johann Joachim Becher;
- Comenius and his circle.

These sought either:

- a language easier to learn;
- a script comprehensible across spoken languages;
- a taxonomy of things;
- a philosophical grammar;
- a representation whose visible construction expressed meaning.

Leibniz judged ordinary language too equivocal and purely pictorial systems too cumbersome. His ideal characters would be conventional enough to manipulate but structurally motivated enough to display analysis.

## 6. Institutions

### Universities

- Leipzig provided Leibniz’s early training in philosophy, law, and scholastic disputation.
- Altdorf granted his doctorate in law.
- Jena and Erhard Weigel’s mathematical pedagogy formed part of his early mathematical environment.

### Courts and administration

Leibniz’s projects were tied to service at Mainz and Hanover. His concerns were practical:

- legal codification;
- diplomacy;
- church reunion;
- libraries;
- mines and engineering;
- state statistics;
- learned correspondence;
- dynastic history.

### Academies

He promoted learned societies and helped found the Berlin Academy. The encyclopedia and characteristic were repeatedly imagined as collaborative undertakings too large for one person.

### Confessional institutions

Catholic, Lutheran, and Reformed scholars all appear in his networks. Church-reunion projects encouraged the hope that disputes could be separated into:

- verbal disagreements;
- disagreements over facts;
- formally decidable consequences;
- irreducible commitments.

This is the political and theological setting of “Let us calculate.”

## Leibniz

He is not known as a public memory performer in the style of Lambert Schenkel. His practice was that of a jurist, polymath, librarian, excerptor, indexer, correspondent, and designer of symbolic methods.

- Antoine Arnauld, Christiaan Huygens, Henry Oldenburg, Joachim Bouvet, and many others supplied problems, knowledge, or criticism.

## Critics and correctives

- Leibniz himself criticized the mathematical defects of the 1666 dissertation.

- Modern logicians distinguish aspiration from formal accomplishment.

## Anecdotes

### The Nuremberg alchemical trick

Johann Georg von Eckhart’s biography, written in 1717 and published later, says Leibniz heard of a society conducting secret chemical experiments. To gain admission, he allegedly assembled obscure expressions from alchemical books into an impressive letter. The group accepted him and made him secretary. Eckhart says Leibniz later recounted the trick laughingly.

Status:

- **Near-contemporary testimony:** Eckhart wrote fifty years after the event but had served Leibniz.
- **Partly corroborated:** Leibniz later said he first learned chemistry at Nuremberg and referred to access to secret alchemical writings “as if to Eleusinian mysteries.”
- **Not corroborated:** Exact membership, rules, name, laboratory, duties, salary, and duration.
- **Legendary elaboration:** That Leibniz became master, director, chief experimentalist, or “life and soul” of a Rosicrucian lodge.

### The auctioneer, professor’s party, lost-memory student, accused magician

These anecdotes belong to other branches of the memory-art tradition and are not securely documented episodes in Leibniz’s mnemonic practice. No primary evidence located connects Leibniz’s system to an auctioneer’s feat, a professor’s party demonstration, a student losing his natural memory through artificial memory, or an accusation that Leibniz’s mnemonic practice was sorcery.

**Absence of evidence is significant:** Repeating such portable memory legends under Leibniz’s name would create a false biography.

## The magic question

## 1. Was *Mnemonica* magical?

No. In *Nova methodus* §23 it is a natural and pedagogical art based on sensation, association, language, pictures, and order.

No incantation, planetary spirit, talismanic consecration, demon, or astral operation is prescribed.

## 2. Was the *ars combinatoria* magical?

Not in Leibniz’s stated procedure. It is mathematical, logical, juridical, and heuristic.

Its historical sources include authors who discussed magic, Cabala, or occult arts. Historical adjacency is not operational identity.

## 3. Was the *characteristica* talismanic?

Leibniz wanted signs that reveal conceptual structure, but this is not the same as a talisman that acts through occult sympathy.

He contrasts rigorous mathematical characters with obscure supposedly universal symbols and with the failures of magical books. The desired “true key” would replace—not perfect—the ineptitudes of magical clavicles.

## 4. Why does “natural magic” appear in the *Introductio*?

Because Leibniz lists many arts to be incorporated into general science. In the seventeenth century *magia naturalis* could denote knowledge of hidden natural powers and surprising effects without invoking demons.

Possible readings:

- **Documented:** Natural magic is named.
- **Historical-context reading:** It is a conventional category in encyclopedic taxonomies.
- **Reformist reading:** General science would sift genuine natural effects from superstition.
- **Yatesian reading:** The list preserves the architecture of Renaissance occult encyclopedism.
- **Unsupported reading:** The characteristic is itself a magical ritual.

## 5. Rosicrucian affiliation

This is the most important contested issue.

### Documentary base

Eckhart says Leibniz associated with a Nuremberg group interested in secret chemical experiments and the philosopher’s stone. He does not call it Rosicrucian.

### Growth of the story

- Late eighteenth- and nineteenth-century writers identified members, director, foundation date, laboratory, and affiliations without adequate documentation.
- Guhrauer’s 1846 biography called the group Rosicrucian.
- That label was repeated by Couturat, popular biographies, encyclopedias, and Yates.
- Yates found apparent parallels between Leibniz’s learned-society plans and Rosicrucian reform ideals.

### Later correction

G. M. Ross’s “Leibniz and the Nuremberg Alchemical Society” found:

- no surviving contemporary institutional records;
- no secure membership list;
- no proof that the group was Rosicrucian;
- tension between a search for the philosopher’s stone and Rosicrucian manifesto criticism of vulgar gold-making;
- explicitly skeptical or hostile references to Rosicrucianism in Leibniz’s own later writings.

Ross concludes that Leibniz’s youthful interest in alchemy is credible but the Rosicrucian affiliation is a myth.

### Verdict

- **Documented or highly probable:** Young Leibniz acquired practical or textual knowledge of chemistry/alchemy in Nuremberg.
- **Plausible tradition:** He gained access by displaying command of alchemical terminology.
- **Disputed:** A formally constituted secret alchemical society and Leibniz’s official secretaryship.
- **Unsupported/legend:** Membership in a Rosicrucian fraternity.
- **No evidence:** Heresy trial or Inquisitorial consequences for the mnemonic-combinatorial project.

## 6. The Order of Charity and the *Fama*

Leibniz drafted plans for learned or charitable societies. Yates notes that proposed rules for an “Order of Charity” resemble or quote provisions in the Rosicrucian *Fama*, especially service, healing, modest public conduct, and reformist fraternity.

Possible explanations include:

- direct borrowing from the *Fama*;
- borrowing through Andreae’s Christian-society literature;
- common Protestant reform vocabulary;
- conventional rules for charitable learned communities.

**Yates’s interpretation:** Evidence of a Rosicrucian aura around Leibniz’s social and intellectual projects.

**Correction:** Literary borrowing or parallel rules do not demonstrate initiation into an order.

## 7. Ars notoria

No evidence shows Leibniz practicing the medieval *Ars notoria*, whose prayers and figures promised divinely infused knowledge.

Structural resemblance—the hope for accelerated mastery through signs—is insufficient. Leibniz’s characteristic requires collaborative analysis, definitions, experiments, and formal operations rather than angelic infusion.

## Afterlife

## 1. Eighteenth-century philosophy and logic

Leibniz’s relevant manuscripts remained largely unpublished. Christian Wolff systematized demonstrative philosophy but did not transmit a finished universal characteristic. Encyclopedists, taxonomists, and language planners continued related aspirations.

This limited circulation is why influence must be traced through specific publications rather than assumed from retrospective similarity.

## 2. Nineteenth-century symbolic logic

Boole, De Morgan, Peirce, Schröder, and Frege realized parts of the dream of formal reasoning, but by independent technical routes.

Frege described his *Begriffsschrift* in relation to Leibniz’s ideal of a characteristic. Nevertheless:

- Frege did not possess the full modern Academy corpus;
- Leibniz’s manuscripts did not directly furnish predicate logic;
- Frege’s notation and theory differ profoundly from Leibniz’s sketches.

**Documented reception:** Leibniz served as an acknowledged predecessor and programmatic ideal.

**Disputed causal claim:** Modern logic developed directly out of Leibniz’s characteristic.

## 3. Computing

Leibniz contributed separately but relatedly to:

- mechanical calculation;
- binary arithmetic;
- formal manipulation of characters;
- combinatorics;
- the dream of delegating routine reasoning to rules.

These justify his place in the prehistory of computing.

### Important corrections

- He did not build a general-purpose programmable computer.
- His stepped reckoner was a numerical machine, not an implementation of the universal characteristic.
- Binary arithmetic was not derived from the *Yijing*. Leibniz developed binary notation before Joachim Bouvet sent him the Fu Xi/Shao Yong diagram; he interpreted the hexagrams afterward as a remarkable correspondence.
- The hexagram relation was formal and theological for Leibniz, not an engineering lineage from Chinese divination to digital electronics.
- Boole, Shannon, relay logic, electronic switching, and stored-program computing supply indispensable later steps.

The claim “Llull invented the computer, Bruno programmed it, and Leibniz digitized it” is a modern mnemonic slogan, not documented technological history.

## 4. Literature and art

### Dante and fresco cycles

Dante, scholastic imagery, Dominican and Franciscan fresco programs, and moralized visual cycles belong to the long prehistory of ordered conceptual memory. They are not descendants of Leibniz.

### Emblem books

Leibniz explicitly valued emblems and pictures as didactic supports. His legal title-page belongs to emblematic print culture, though it does not prove a formal emblem-memory course.

### Borges’s Funes

Borges’s “Funes the Memorious” explores limitless particular memory and inability to abstract. It offers an illuminating counterpoint to Leibniz:

- Funes remembers every detail but cannot generalize.
- Leibniz seeks primitive concepts and economical characters precisely to master multiplicity through abstraction.

No direct Leibnizian source is required for the comparison.

### Umberto Eco

Eco’s *The Search for the Perfect Language* places Leibniz within the history of Adamic language, philosophical languages, Cabala, taxonomy, and universal communication. Eco generally treats the characteristic as a historically situated project, not a completed formal language.

### Modern memory palaces and athletes

Modern competitors use:

- routes through familiar spaces;
- person-action-object systems;
- number-shape and number-rhyme codes;
- highly vivid images;
- spaced practice.

These descend more directly from classical local memory and modern mnemonic pedagogy than from the *characteristica universalis*.

A modern app marketed as a “Leibniz memory palace,” or a deck assigning concepts to universal symbols, is a contemporary construction unless it follows a documented Leibniz text.

## 5. Information architecture and artificial intelligence

Leibniz’s program resembles modern concerns with:

- ontologies;
- knowledge representation;
- formal languages;
- theorem proving;
- symbolic artificial intelligence;
- databases;
- semantic decomposition;
- automated reasoning.

These are legitimate conceptual analogies. They are not identity claims.

The recent interpretation that the universal characteristic “externalizes” and reorganizes memory is a scholarly cognitive reconstruction: signs stabilize relationships so the mind need not retain and recompute everything internally.

## Controversies and disputes

## 1. Is this genuinely a system of artificial memory?

**Yes, in a limited sense:** *Nova methodus* explicitly gives mnemonic principles and recommends visual signs and places.

**No, if “system” means a complete architectural code:** Leibniz supplies no full palace, image alphabet, or training sequence.

**Best description:** A mnemonic component embedded in a larger didactic, topical, combinatorial, encyclopedic, and logical program.

## 2. Did Leibniz derive the characteristic from classical memory?

- **Evidence for continuity:** *notae*, visual precedence, association, loci, emblems, memory authors.
- **Evidence for other origins:** scholastic logic, law, arithmetic, Hobbes, Bacon, universal-language projects, algebra, scientific notation.
- **Yates:** Strong genealogical continuity.
- **Current cautious position:** Artificial memory is one source and analogy among several.

## 3. Did Llull invent combinatorial logic?

Llull used letters and figures to combine principles systematically. But:

- his goals were theological and demonstrative;
- his “combinations” are not modern combinatorics;
- the Art changed substantially over his career;
- pseudo-Lullian works shaped later reception.

Leibniz’s 1666 mathematics goes beyond Llull numerically while retaining a Lullian ideal of general invention.

## 4. Was Bruno the missing link?

- **Documented:** Leibniz names Bruno.
- **Plausible:** Bruno helped establish *combinatoria* as a term and model.
- **Unproved:** Bruno’s mnemonic wheels directly caused Leibniz’s characteristic.
- **Unsupported:** Leibniz adopted Brunian astral magic.

## 5. Is the infinitesimal calculus a fragment of the characteristic?

Leibniz frequently cites successful mathematical notation as evidence of what good characters can accomplish. The differential and integral signs are extraordinarily productive characters.

But two claims must be separated:

1. **Documented and defensible:** Calculus notation exemplifies Leibniz’s general philosophy of signs.
2. **Yates’s stronger genealogy:** The calculus arose through the transformation of mnemonic “images for things.”
   
The second is not established by the mathematical manuscripts. The calculus developed through concrete work on curves, series, quadratures, tangents, and contemporary mathematics.

## 6. Were Chinese characters ideographic memory images?

Leibniz and many contemporaries believed Chinese writing approached a script of things or concepts shared across spoken languages.

Modern linguistics rejects the simple ideographic model. Chinese characters participate in a language-specific morphosyllabic writing system and often include phonetic components.

Thus:

- **Documented historical belief:** Leibniz treated Chinese writing as relevant to universal characters and memory.
- **Incorrect as modern linguistics:** Chinese script is not a transparent language-independent inventory of things.

## 7. Egyptian hieroglyphs

Leibniz wrote before Champollion’s decipherment. He inherited Renaissance and early modern conceptions of hieroglyphs as condensed intellectual images.

Modern Egyptology shows that hieroglyphic writing includes phonetic, lexical, and determinative functions.

Yates is right about the historical importance of the Renaissance belief, not about that belief’s linguistic accuracy.

## 8. Adamic language

Leibniz entertained the tradition that Adam’s names had been close to the nature of things, but he did not claim that the original language was recoverable simply by occult archaeology. His mature comparative linguistics was historical and empirical.

- **Tradition:** Adam named creatures truly.
- **Leibnizian speculation:** An original language may have had a privileged relation to reality.
- **Characteristic project:** Construct a better future instrument rather than confidently restore Edenic speech.
- **Legend:** Leibniz discovered or encoded the language of Adam.

## 9. Monad as Hermetic mirror

Leibniz calls every substance a living mirror of the universe. Mirror and microcosm language has Hermetic and Neoplatonic precedents, but also scholastic, theological, optical, and mathematical histories.

- **Yates’s interpretation:** “Obvious marks” of Hermetic tradition.
- **Correction:** Lexical or structural resemblance does not establish a unique Hermetic source.
- **More secure context:** Leibniz’s theory concerns expression, perception, substance, and universal relational order.

## 10. Rosicrucian Leibniz

The evidentiary chain is unusually clear:

1. Eckhart, 1717: an alchemical group.
2. Later biographical elaboration.
3. Guhrauer, 1846: Rosicrucian identification.
4. Couturat and later writers repeat it.
5. Yates links it to charitable-society plans.
6. G. M. Ross, 1970s: documentary dismantling of the Rosicrucian attribution.

The historically responsible label is **legend based on a plausible alchemical episode**.

## 11. Did Leibniz complete the universal characteristic?

No.

He produced:

- programmatic statements;
- partial logical calculi;
- characteristic numbers;
- notational experiments;
- algebraic and geometrical characteristics;
- encyclopedia plans;
- examples of analysis and formal inference.

He did not produce:

- an agreed list of all primitive concepts;
- a complete lexicon;
- a universal grammar;
- a calculus encompassing all reasoning;
- a general machine executing it.

## 12. “Let us calculate”

The quotation is authentic, but popular versions often omit its condition: the controversy must be decidable from data. Leibniz did not promise mechanical resolution of every disagreement regardless of premises, evidence, or interpretation.

## 13. Yates’s own caution

Yates says her book is neither complete nor final and acknowledges the conditional nature of her broadest Leibniz thesis. Later simplifications are often more categorical than Yates herself.

## Open questions

1. What is the exact present shelfmark and material composition of every paper formerly grouped as Hanover Phil. VI.19 and Phil. VII.B.III.7?

2. Which mnemonic notes are original formulations by Leibniz, which are excerpts, and which are later archival groupings?

3. Can the 1678 person-series reference be identified and edited with its full immediate context rather than through Couturat and Rossi’s excerpts?

4. Did Leibniz read Bruno’s *De umbris idearum*, *Ars reminiscendi*, *Explicatio triginta sigillorum*, or *De specierum scrutinio* directly, or did he know Bruno chiefly through bibliographical and Lullist transmission?

5. Which editions of Llull were available to Leibniz during 1665–66?

6. How much did Erhard Weigel’s mathematical pedagogy contribute to the early combinatorial project?

7. Should Leibniz’s practical filing, excerpting, indexing, and library cataloguing be treated as part of his art of memory?

8. How were mnemonic cells implemented in seventeenth-century classrooms—painted wall tables, manuscript grids, portable charts, or imagined places?

9. Is the *Disputatio de casibus* title-page demonstrably didactic, or merely emblematic and ornamental?

10. How precisely did Leibniz distinguish a merely conventional sign, a mnemonic *nota*, an expressive character, and a formula within a calculus at different dates?

11. Were the “Order of Charity” parallels to the *Fama* direct quotation, common dependence on Andreae, or shared reformist convention?

12. What parts of the characteristic were known to nineteenth-century logicians before Gerhardt and Couturat, and through which publications?

13. To what extent is the unity of “encyclopedia–characteristic–calculus” Leibniz’s own stable doctrine rather than Couturat’s reconstruction from heterogeneous papers?
