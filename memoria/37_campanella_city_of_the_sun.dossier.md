# Campanella's City of the Sun as local memory: Research Dossier

## Basic identification

**Name:** *The City of the Sun as local memory*  
**Exponent:** Tommaso Campanella (Giovanni Domenico Campanella, 1568–1639), Dominican friar, philosopher, astrologer, political conspirator, and prisoner of the Spanish government and Roman Inquisition.  
**Date:** Italian composition conventionally dated 1601–1602, probably early 1602; revised Italian recensions followed. Campanella prepared the Latin *Civitas Solis* around 1613–1614.  
**Source text:** *La città del Sole: Dialogo poetico*; first printed as *Civitas Solis, vel de reipublicae idea. Dialogus poeticus*, appendix to the *Politica* in *Realis philosophiae epilogisticae partes quatuor*, Frankfurt, 1623, pp. 415–464.  
**Principle in one sentence:** A seven-ringed city converts an ordered encyclopedia of nature, arts, peoples, and celestial causes into permanent architectural places and visible images, so that children learn by walking, looking, hearing explanations, and correlating terrestrial things with the heavens.  
**Yates:** Frances A. Yates, *The Art of Memory* (London: Routledge & Kegan Paul, 1966), ch. XIII, pp. 297–298; ch. XVII, pp. 377–378; compare the reconstruction of Camillo’s theatre, p. 130 n. 4.

### Evidentiary verdict

The following distinctions govern the dossier:

- **Documented text:** The painted walls, their fixed concentric order, the inscriptions, specimens, instructors, and ambulatory teaching are explicit in *La città del Sole*.
- **Documented text, outside the dialogue:** Campanella’s letters explicitly promise a rapid method that will make “the world itself” serve “as book and as local memory.”
- **Strong scholarly reconstruction:** The City’s walls can therefore be understood as an implementation, model, or dramatization of that program.
- **Yates’s interpretation:** The entire City is a Renaissance occult memory system comparable with Camillo and Bruno.
- **Disputed or unproved:** No surviving sentence by Campanella says simply, “the City of the Sun is my system of local memory.” The letters do not unambiguously identify the painted City with the promised method.
- **Incorrect if stated literally:** The temple images do not straightforwardly “cause” all the pictures below in the operational manner of Bruno’s combinatory wheels. That is Yates’s cosmological reading, not an instruction in the primary text.
- **Unproved:** No architectural model commissioned by Campanella, no mnemonic wheel, no user manual, and no Campanellan diagram of the City is known to survive.

The CNR’s critical-text catalogue cautiously describes the painted walls as “a true encyclopedia of the sciences,” not expressly as a technical *ars memoriae*. It dates the work 1601–1602 and identifies Tobias Adami as editor of the 1623 Latin printing. [Archivio Tommaso Campanella, critical-text notice](https://www.iliesi.cnr.it/ATC/schede.php?sc=t_3&tp=1&tx=27)

## The system in detail

### 1. Architectural framework: the places

The City stands on a hill and is divided into seven enormous concentric circuits, named for the seven planets. Four roads and four gates, oriented to the cardinal points, cut through the circuits. At the summit is a circular temple.

This gives the prospective mnemonic apparatus several kinds of stable place:

1. The central temple and its dome.
2. The seven planetary circuits.
3. The inner and outer face of each circuit wall.
4. The four cardinal routes and gates.
5. The galleries or *rivellini* adjoining the walls.
6. The successive painted objects and accompanying inscriptions.
7. Actual specimens—minerals, plants, and other objects—placed beside some representations.

**Documented text:** these are architectural and pedagogical divisions.  
**Scholarly reconstruction:** they function like ordered mnemonic *loci*.  
**Not documented:** Campanella never provides a numerical locus-key, an instruction to deposit arbitrary speech-images, or a formal retrieval drill comparable to classical mnemonic manuals.

The order is unusually resistant to confusion because it is doubly indexed:

- radially, from circumference toward center or center toward circumference;
- serially, by wall number;
- binarily, by inner versus outer face;
- cosmologically, through the seven planets and the celestial–terrestrial correspondences;
- taxonomically, by discipline or natural kingdom.

The City is thus closer to a permanent encyclopedic museum or illustrated curriculum than to a reusable chain of empty places.

### 2. The temple: cosmic synopsis

At the summit stands a circular temple resting on columns. It has no enclosing wall in the ordinary sense. Two globes are placed over the altar: one represents the heavens and the other the earth. Seven lamps, named for the planets, burn in the temple. The dome displays the major stars, with their names and influences.

The Italian text says that Wisdom has caused the exterior walls of the temple and the curtains lowered during preaching to be painted with every star, each supplied with three verses.

> “Nelle mura del tempio esteriori e nelle cortine, che si calano quando si predica per non disperdersi la voce, vi sta ogni stella con tre versi per una.”

Gloss: “On the exterior walls of the temple and on the curtains lowered when preaching so that the voice will not be lost, every star appears, with three verses for each.”

The Latin text correspondingly describes:

> “In muris templi et in vela, quae demittuntur quando concionatur … sunt stellae depictae suis quaeque magnitudinibus, viribus et motibus, tribus versiculis explicatae.”

Gloss: “On the temple walls and on the hangings lowered during preaching … the stars are depicted, each with its magnitude, powers, and motions explained in three little verses.”

**Documented:** celestial pictures, explanatory verse, globes, planetary lamps.  
**Yates’s interpretation:** this is the causal, celestial center from which the lower encyclopedic images derive meaning.  
**Qualification:** the dialogue certainly correlates stars and sublunary things, but it does not give a Brunian procedure for transmitting stellar powers through the picture series.

### 3. First circuit: mathematics and terrestrial geography

On the inner face of the first wall appear mathematical figures, said to exceed those discovered by Euclid and Archimedes. Each has its proposition or definition written beside it.

> “Nel dentro del primo girone tutte le figure matematiche, più che non scrisse Euclide ed Archimede, con la lor proposizione significante.”

Gloss: “On the inside of the first circuit are all the mathematical figures—more than Euclid and Archimedes wrote—with the proposition signifying each.”

On the outer face is a single great representation of the whole Earth, followed by maps or tablets of individual regions. These give customs, laws, origins, political strength, and alphabets.

The Latin recension emphasizes a synoptic world image:

> “In exteriori convexitate primo est ingens totius terrae descriptio una intuitu.”

Gloss: “On the exterior convex surface there is first an immense representation of the whole Earth at a single view.”

**Mnemonic function:** mathematics is given a stable visual sequence; geography is nested as world → regions → peoples → laws → scripts.

### 4. Second circuit: celestial and terrestrial maps

The second circuit presents the heavens and the Earth in greater differentiation. The stars and their relations are diagrammed; terrestrial countries, climates, rivers, mountains, and peoples are correspondingly displayed.

Later summaries sometimes merge this material with the first wall. That variation usually arises from paraphrase or from differences in punctuation and division, not from evidence that Campanella designed multiple incompatible wall-programs.

### 5. Third circuit: minerals, waters, plants, and fishes

On the inside are metals, stones, minerals, waters, and botanical specimens or pictures, with explanations of:

- their origins;
- properties and powers;
- medicinal uses;
- resemblance or correspondence to stars;
- affinity with metals and parts of the human body.

The text gives unusually vivid examples:

> “Nel dentro del terzo vi sono tutte le sorti d’erbe ed arbori del mondo pinte, e pur in teste di terra sopra il rivellino, e le dichiarazioni dove prima si ritrovaro, e le virtù loro, e le simiglianze c’hanno con le stelle, con li metalli e con le membra umane, e l’uso loro in medicina.”

Gloss: “On the inside of the third are painted all kinds of herbs and trees in the world, and some are also planted in earthen pots upon the gallery, with explanations of where they were first found, their virtues, their resemblances to the stars, metals, and human members, and their medical use.”

On the outside appear fishes and aquatic creatures. Campanella plays on vernacular names that resemble artifacts or ecclesiastical persons:

> “mi stupii quando trovai pesce vescovo e catena e chiodo e stella, appunto come son queste cose tra noi.”

Gloss: “I was amazed when I found bishop-fish, chain-fish, nail-fish, and star-fish, exactly resembling those things among us.”

These are not artificial mnemonic grotesques invented to encode words. They are images of supposedly real natural objects whose striking forms make them naturally memorable.

### 6. Fourth circuit: birds and terrestrial animals

The fourth circuit displays birds on one face and terrestrial animals on the other, classified by form, behavior, habitat, generation, and use.

The Latin text says of the birds:

> “In quarto interiori omnes avium species depictae sunt, earum naturae, magnitudines, mores, colores, vita.”

Gloss: “On the fourth interior wall all species of birds are depicted, with their natures, sizes, habits, colors, and life.”

The City is said to possess the one genuine phoenix. That is a **fictional assertion within the utopia**, not a documented zoological belief unique to Campanella and not a mnemonic rule.

### 7. Fifth circuit: animals and human anatomy

The fifth circuit continues the zoological series and moves toward the human body. Larger land animals appear with their uses and correspondences; anatomy, bodily members, diseases, and remedies lead toward the human sciences.

The organizing movement across the circuits is broadly from abstract/cosmic and elemental knowledge through natural species to humanity, arts, history, and religion. Precise modern diagrams often impose more symmetry on the sequence than the prose warrants.

### 8. Sixth circuit: arts, inventors, laws, heroes, religions

The sixth and outermost encyclopedic circuit displays the mechanical arts and their instruments, inventors of sciences and weapons, legislators, heroes, and founders of religions.

Among those honored are Moses, Osiris, Jupiter, Mercury, Muhammad, and others; Christ and the apostles occupy a privileged position. Images or statues of famous persons stand in a historical and comparative sequence.

Campanella also describes a “book of heroes” in which the names of living benefactors are entered; statues are normally erected only after death. Thus the mnemonic environment includes both fixed monumental images and an updatable civic register.

**Documented:** exemplary persons and founders are represented.  
**Interpretive:** these figures work like classical mnemonic *imagines agentes*.  
**Qualification:** the text calls them portraits, statues, or histories, not technically “memory images,” and supplies no bizarre actions of the kind recommended by the *Ad Herennium*.

### 9. The supervising intelligence: Wisdom

The prince called *Sin*, “Wisdom,” governs all sciences and their officers:

> “Il Sapienza ha fatto pingere in tutte le muraglie, su li rivellini, dentro e di fuori, tutte le scienze.”

Gloss: “Wisdom has had all the sciences painted on all the walls, upon the galleries, inside and outside.”

Beneath Wisdom are named officials corresponding to grammar, logic, physics, medicine, astrology, astronomy, geometry, cosmography, music, perspective, arithmetic, poetry, rhetoric, painting, sculpture, history, politics, and morals.

This administrative hierarchy mirrors the intellectual hierarchy. Knowledge is not merely stored; it is embodied in offices and social practice.

### 10. Learning and retrieval

The clearest operational account concerns children:

> “Li fanciulli senza fastidio, giocando, si trovano a saper tutte le scienze istoricamente prima che abbin dieci anni.”

Gloss: “The children, without weariness and as if at play, find that they know all the sciences historically before they are ten.”

In another passage, children learn language and the alphabet while walking around the walls. After they have surveyed the mathematics painted there, instructors take them to formal readings of the sciences. Magistrates explain the pictures.

Retrieval therefore works through:

- repeated bodily movement along a fixed route;
- recognition of visible figures;
- short verbal captions;
- oral explanation;
- classification by wall and domain;
- repeated association between picture, name, property, use, and celestial correspondence;
- later practical work in crafts, agriculture, military exercises, and government.

The system is multimodal: spatial, visual, verbal, oral, tactile, and practical.

**Important limitation:** the dialogue explains acquisition much better than recall. It never says, for example, “to remember a discourse, mentally begin at the north gate and inspect each picture in turn.” Classical local-memory retrieval is inferred from the architecture and the letters.

### 11. Rules for places and images

No formal rule-list appears. The following are reconstructable implicit rules.

#### Places

- They must be permanently and publicly fixed.
- Their order must be evident.
- They should be large enough to contain distinct subjects.
- Inner and outer faces should be distinguishable.
- They must support regular physical traversal.
- Their relation to the cosmic and disciplinary order should remain stable.

#### Images

- Images should resemble the thing taught.
- Captions should be brief; several celestial pictures have three-verse explanations.
- The picture may be accompanied by a real specimen.
- It should communicate nature, properties, relations, history, and uses.
- Images should be interpreted by trained teachers.
- Sensible things take precedence over merely verbal definitions.

These are **reconstructed principles**, not Campanella’s explicitly numbered mnemonic rules.

### 12. No “worked mnemonic example” in the strict sense

Unlike the *Ad Herennium*’s lawsuit image, Camillo’s grades, or Bruno’s wheel combinations, the City supplies no demonstration in which a proposition is encoded into a deliberately fabricated memory image and then decoded.

The bishop-fish, chain-fish, nail-fish, and star-fish are the closest things to memorable examples, but they are presented as natural-history specimens. The City is an illustrated curriculum, not a surviving handbook of image composition.

## The sources

### Italian textual tradition

Campanella composed the work in Italian, probably in Castel Nuovo after his 1601 life sentence. Dating it precisely to 1602 derives chiefly from biographical and textual reconstruction associated with Luigi Amabile and later Luigi Firpo. The modern CNR catalogue properly calls the exact date uncertain while retaining 1601–1602 as most probable. [CNR textual notice](https://www.iliesi.cnr.it/ATC/schede.php?sc=t_3&tp=1&tx=27)

Eighteen Italian manuscript copies were known in the survey published by Germana Ernst in 2009. They belong to different recensional stages and were copied by several hands; no complete autograph of the dialogue is known. [Ernst, “Campanella autografo”](https://www.autografi.net/volumi/ALI_500_1/ALI_500_1-08_Ernst_Campanella_2009.pdf)

The earliest surviving witness is:

- **Trento, Biblioteca comunale, BCT1-1538**, an anonymous copy bound into a composite manuscript. Its *Città del Sole* fascicle is the earliest known witness and is associated with 1602. It came to the library with Antonio Mazzetti’s collection. A digital facsimile is available. [Trento manuscript facsimile](https://it.wikisource.org/wiki/File:Campanella_-_La_Citt%C3%A0_del_Sole,_manoscritto,_1602.djvu)

The manuscript was identified by Luigi Firpo in 1943. It is a carefully written continuous prose copy, not a design portfolio. It contains **no authoritative architectural plan or mnemonic diagram**.

Other important witnesses include copies at Lucca, Florence, Rome, and elsewhere. The Lucca material is especially important because Gaspare Scioppio assembled or supervised copies of Campanella’s works and correspondence. Spampanato’s account describes a Lucca codex containing the *Aforismi politici*, *Città del Sole*, and twenty-three letters, including several autographs and copies in Scioppio’s hand. [Spampanato, manuscript note](https://it.wikisource.org/wiki/Lettere_%28Campanella%29/Nota)

### Latin editions

1. **Frankfurt, 1623:** *Realis philosophiae epilogisticae partes quatuor*, edited by Tobias Adami. *Civitas Solis* follows the *Politica*, pp. 415–464, with its own title. This is the first printing in any language.

2. **Paris, 1637:** revised *Philosophia realis*, containing the revised Latin *Civitas Solis*. Campanella supervised this late presentation of his philosophy more closely.

3. **Utrecht, 1643:** *Civitas Solis* was printed with Joseph Hall’s *Mundus alter et idem* and Francis Bacon’s *Nova Atlantis*. This juxtaposition helped establish its later classification as utopian fiction. A searchable Latin transcription is available through [Bibliotheca Augustana](https://www.tha.de/~harsch/Chronologia/Lspost17/Campanella/cam_ci00.html) and [Latin Wikisource](https://la.wikisource.org/wiki/Civitas_solis).

The Latin is Campanella’s adaptation, not a mechanically literal translation. It clarifies, expands, and sometimes apologetically Christianizes the Italian text. References to Aquinas and other modifications help position the work within acceptable Catholic political philosophy.

### Modern standard editions and translations

- Tommaso Campanella, *La città del Sole*, ed. Luigi Firpo; revised by Germana Ernst and Laura Salvetti Firpo, Rome–Bari: Laterza, 1997; revised ed. 2006, pp. 3–59.
- Tommaso Campanella, *La Città del Sole—Civitas Solis: edizione complanare del manoscritto della prima redazione italiana (1602) e dell’ultima edizione a stampa*, ed. Tonino Tornitore, Milan: Unicopli, 1998.
- Tommaso Campanella, *The City of the Sun: A Poetical Dialogue*, ed. and trans. Daniel J. Donno, Berkeley: University of California Press, 1981. Donno prints Italian and English and provides a substantial introduction. [Publisher’s introduction](https://content.ucpress.edu/title/9780520040366/9780520040366_intro.pdf)
- A nineteenth-century Italian version is digitized at [Italian Wikisource](https://it.wikisource.org/wiki/La_Citt%C3%A0_del_Sole_%281863%29), but it should not replace Firpo/Ernst for textual work.
- A public-domain English translation is accessible in [*Ideal Commonwealths*](https://www.files.ethz.ch/isn/125498/5032_Campanella.pdf); its diction and occasional simplifications require checking against Italian or Latin.

### Campanella’s letters

The catalogue’s references are to Vincenzo Spampanato, ed., *Lettere di Tommaso Campanella*, Scrittori d’Italia 103 (Bari: Laterza, 1927), especially pp. 27–28, 160, and 194.

Two related formulations are crucial:

> “far il mondo libro e memoria locale d’ogni arte, salvo della teologia, che vuol autorità assai”

Gloss: “to make the world the book and local memory of every art, except theology, which greatly requires authority.”

And:

> “facendo che ’l mondo stesso serva per libro e per memoria locale”

Gloss: “making the world itself serve as book and as local memory.”

In the fuller promise, Campanella undertakes—under penalty of his life—to teach natural and moral philosophy, logic, rhetoric, poetics, politics, astrology, medicine, and cosmography within a year, enabling suitable pupils to surpass those who had spent ten years in ordinary schools.

**Documented:** a claimed rapid-teaching method based on the world as book and local memory.  
**Yates/Rossi reconstruction:** the painted City supplies the architectural implementation.  
**Unproved:** that every cited letter expressly calls *La città del Sole* itself the promised mnemonic instrument.

In 1637 Campanella wrote to Cardinal Richelieu and expressed the hope that Richelieu might establish the City he had described. The surviving letter invokes the two divine books, nature and Scripture, and calls the world God’s living statue. [Letter to Richelieu](https://it.wikisource.org/wiki/Pagina:Campanella,_Tommaso_%E2%80%93_Lettere,_1927_%E2%80%93_BEIC_1776819.djvu/378)

### Surviving diagrams and reconstructions

No authorial plan, wheel, table, or engraved plate forms part of the 1623 text. The City is verbally described. Modern circular plans are reconstructions.

Accordingly:

- the familiar seven-ring plan is textually warranted in outline;
- the precise placement of every subject is partly reconstructive;
- perfectly symmetrical modern charts are not primary evidence;
- identifying the City with Camillo’s theatre or Bruno’s *Alta Astra* is comparative interpretation, not a transmitted diagrammatic genealogy.

## Yates’s account and the scholarship since

### Yates, chapter XIII

At pp. 297–298 Yates places Campanella after Bruno and emphasizes their shared Dominican formation at Naples. She quotes or paraphrases the letters as evidence that:

> “his City of the Sun, or perhaps some model of it, could be used for ‘local memory’, as a very quick way of knowing everything ‘using the world as a book’.”

The phrase “or perhaps some model of it” is important. Yates herself registers uncertainty about whether the literary City, a physical teaching model, or a related method was meant.

She continues:

> “It is much simpler than Bruno’s systems because static in a City (as Camillo’s system is static in a Theatre) and does not attempt Bruno’s awful complexities.”

This is an effective structural comparison:

- Camillo: a fixed theatre whose grades and images organize knowledge;
- Campanella: a fixed city whose circuits and pictures organize knowledge;
- Bruno: mobile or combinatory systems in which alphabets and image series are manipulated.

But “static” must be qualified. The pictures are fixed; the pupils, instructors, civic offices, specimens, ceremonies, and celestial observations are active.

Yates compares the round temple with the *Alta Astra* altar in Bruno’s *De imaginum, signorum et idearum compositione*:

> “certain basic similarities between ‘local memory’ as conceived by Bruno and Campanella … may become apparent.”

The cautious “may” is often lost in derivative accounts. No documentary proof establishes that Campanella copied this particular Brunian configuration.

### Yates, chapter XVII

At pp. 377–378 Yates connects the Solarian pedagogy with Andreae and Comenius:

> “The pedagogic method of the highly occult Solarians, and the whole plan of their City and its images, was a form of local memory, with its places and images.”

She then claims that Comenius’s *Orbis sensualium pictus* “came straight out of Campanella’s City of the Sun” and treats it as a rationalized descendant of a magical memory system.

### What later work confirms

Paolo Rossi’s studies confirm the larger importance of encyclopedic ordering, Lullism, visual pedagogy, and universal-language projects in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries. Rossi also treats Campanella’s sensible encyclopedia as opposed to—or at least corrective of—a merely verbal Lullian art. Campanella wanted reasoning through sensible objects, *non per vocabula tantum, ut Lullio mos est*—“not merely through words, as is Lull’s custom.”

Lina Bolzoni’s work on Renaissance memory culture confirms that painted rooms, galleries, theatres, emblematic sequences, and encyclopedic image programs could function simultaneously as decoration, pedagogy, meditation, and mnemonic ordering. That supports Yates’s broad category while warning against forcing every iconographic program into a single technical model.

Mary Carruthers and Jocelyn Penny Small reinforce a further correction: premodern memory was not simply a storage trick. It was an inventive, compositional, ethical, and interpretive practice. Campanella’s City forms citizens by arranging what they repeatedly see. That is closer to a civic “matrix of recollection” than to rote data storage.

### What later work qualifies or corrects

1. **Yates’s Hermetic master narrative is too exclusive.** Campanella’s natural philosophy, Telesian sensism, Catholic universalism, Dominican education, classical utopia, civic iconography, astrology, and contemporary pedagogical reform all contribute. Hermeticism is one strand, not a sufficient explanation.

2. **“Local memory” is not the work’s declared genre.** The title calls it a poetic dialogue and idea of a philosophical republic. Its printed position is within political philosophy. The mnemonic reading is illuminating but secondary.

3. **Campanella’s images are primarily referential and didactic.** Bruno’s images frequently serve as symbolic operators within combinatory systems. The City’s pictures ordinarily represent the very objects being learned.

4. **The Comenius claim is stronger than the evidence.** Similarities are real: encyclopedic scope, words joined to images, sensory pedagogy, graded instruction, and the “world in pictures.” Yet Comenius also draws explicitly on Aristotelian and scholastic sensory psychology, humanist language teaching, Baconian reform, Johann Heinrich Alsted, Wolfgang Ratke, and earlier illustrated textbooks. Gordon L. Miller accurately reports Yates’s claim but places Comenius within a broader millenarian and pedagogical movement. [Miller, “Beasts of the New Jerusalem”](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/007327530804600204)

5. **Direct descent is not demonstrated by likeness alone.** “Campanella → *Orbis pictus*” should be labeled a probable or possible influence within a network, not an established single-source derivation.

6. **Lull should not be reduced to occult wheels.** Work by Anthony Bonner and Alexander Fidora has re-established the logical, theological, demonstrative, and missionary purposes of Llull’s Art. Campanella’s criticism of reasoning through terms does not justify treating Lullism merely as verbal combinatorics.

7. **Bruno should not be used as the automatic missing link.** Studies by Rita Sturlese, Michele Ciliberto, Stephen Clucas, and Hilary Gatti differentiate Bruno’s mnemonic works by date, purpose, ontology, and audience. Shared Dominican background does not prove direct transmission from a particular Brunian memory work.

### Yates’s later position

Yates continued to treat Campanella within the Hermetic and Rosicrucian reform constellation, particularly in *Giordano Bruno and the Hermetic Tradition* and *The Rosicrucian Enlightenment*. She did not substantially retract the local-memory reading. Her later work broadened its political and religious setting but retained a strong line from Renaissance occult philosophy to seventeenth-century reform.

## History and context

### Classical local memory

The classical art described in the anonymous *Rhetorica ad Herennium*, Cicero’s *De oratore*, and Quintilian’s *Institutio oratoria* requires an ordered series of places and vivid images deposited in them. The orator mentally revisits the places to retrieve the images.

Campanella retains:

- ordered places;
- visible images;
- sequential traversal;
- association of image and verbal matter.

He changes:

- private imagined architecture into public civic architecture;
- temporary speech-images into permanent encyclopedia pictures;
- solitary recollection into collective education;
- arbitrary symbolic encoding into largely mimetic representation;
- rhetorical recall into the formation of a whole society.

The *Ad Herennium*’s famous lawsuit image—an ill man in bed, the defendant beside him holding cup and tablets, and a ram’s testicles on a finger—has no close equivalent here. Campanella’s pictures are memorable chiefly because the world itself is various and wonderful.

### Scholastic psychology and Dominican institutions

As a Dominican, Campanella inherited an institutional culture in which Aristotle’s psychology, Aquinas, preaching, disputation, and trained memory remained important. Dominican convents supplied actual ordered spaces—churches, cloisters, chapels, statues, tombs—that contemporary mnemonic manuals recommended as loci.

Nevertheless, Campanella rejected the servile authority of bookish Aristotelianism and insisted on nature as a primary divine book. His pedagogy combines scholastic classification with a Telesian appeal to sensation.

### Lullism

The City shares Lullism’s encyclopedic and missionary ambition: all knowledge should form a unified, teachable order capable of supporting universal religious reform.

It differs operationally:

- Llull’s Art combines letters and principles through figures;
- Campanella foregrounds sensible things and images;
- the City offers a taxonomic promenade rather than a demonstrative combinatorial calculus.

There is no Campanellan mnemonic wheel in *Civitas Solis*.

### Hermeticism and the *Picatrix*

Yates argues that the deepest model is the magical City of Adocentyn in the *Picatrix*, traditionally built by Hermes Trismegistus. That city contains planetary images and structures intended to channel celestial powers.

Similarities include:

- a cosmically ordered city;
- central solar symbolism;
- planetary divisions;
- images relating heaven to things below;
- priestly knowledge of celestial influences.

But direct dependence remains disputed:

- Campanella does not cite the *Picatrix* in the dialogue;
- circular ideal cities, sevenfold cosmic orders, and planetary correspondences had many other sources;
- Plato, More, monastic architecture, civic fresco programs, astrology, Revelation, and natural philosophy provide nearer or parallel models.

Thus the Adocentyn genealogy is **Yates’s influential hypothesis**, not documented transmission.

### Ramism and encyclopedism

Ramist method sought visible division, ordered presentation, and curricular economy. Walter J. Ong showed that Ramism transforms discourse into spatialized diagrams and dichotomies. Campanella was not simply a Ramist, but the desire to make all knowledge surveyable belongs to the same early-modern crisis of information organization.

His arrangement is not Ramist in form: it is circular, analogical, cosmological, and sensory rather than a branching binary outline.

### Universal language and the book of nature

The walls contain foreign alphabets, maps, objects, pictures, and concise explanations. This participates in the movement toward:

- universal encyclopedias;
- real characters;
- philosophical languages;
- visual dictionaries;
- reform of education by things rather than words.

The City does not possess a fully designed universal character. Its images are intended to cross some linguistic barriers, but instructors and captions remain necessary.

## People

### Tommaso Campanella

Campanella devised the City and translated it into Latin. His self-presentation as an extraordinarily rapid teacher and encyclopedic thinker is documented in his letters, but the success of his promised one-year curriculum is not independently verified.

### Tobias Adami

Adami met Campanella in Naples, carried texts north, edited the Frankfurt publications, and gave *Civitas Solis* its first printed circulation. He is the principal early transmitter in print.

### Gaspare Scioppio

Scioppio acted as advocate, correspondent, copyist, and organizer of manuscripts. His surviving copies helped preserve both the Italian dialogue and letters. His role matters because the manuscript tradition is not a simple chain descending from a single autograph.

### Luigi Amabile, Norberto Bobbio, Luigi Firpo, Germana Ernst

- Amabile established foundational biographical and trial research and helped anchor the 1602 dating.
- Bobbio collated ten of the eleven Italian manuscripts then accessible and emphasized the distinct Italian and Latin traditions.
- Firpo discovered the Trento witness and produced the fundamental critical work.
- Ernst and Laura Salvetti Firpo revised the Laterza edition and refined the intellectual, political, and textual history.

An early review notes that older readers often mistook Latin for the original and that the widely circulated nineteenth-century Italian text was actually retranslated from Latin. [Luigi Einaudi’s discussion of Bobbio’s edition](https://www.luigieinaudi.it/doc/delle-utopie-a-proposito-della-citta-del-sole/)

### Pupils and teachers inside the fiction

Four leaders and four elders supervise young children. Specialist magistrates explain the pictures. Children walk the walls, learn alphabets, hear lectures, enter workshops, and are observed to discover their natural aptitudes.

These are fictional practitioners, not documented students of Campanella’s method.

### Patrons

Campanella repeatedly sought patrons capable of publishing or realizing his reform:

- German Protestant intellectuals around Adami facilitated publication;
- Urban VIII protected him and consulted him on astrology;
- French patrons supported his final exile;
- Richelieu was invited to found the City.

No patron is known to have built it.

### Anecdotes requested in the general template

The auctioneer, professor’s party, student who lost his memory, and accused magician belong to other memory traditions or exponents. No reliable Campanellan version has been located. Importing them into this dossier would create a false tradition.

## The magic question

### Was the City itself called magic?

Not by Campanella in the dialogue. It is called a poetic dialogue and philosophical republic.

The City is, however, deeply astrological. Human generation, medicine, agriculture, worship, government, and historical expectation are coordinated with celestial configurations. The pictures explicitly record resemblances between stars, metals, plants, animals, and human members.

“Magical” may therefore mean three different things:

1. **Historically documented natural magic:** manipulating natural sympathies and celestial conditions.
2. **Yates’s category:** an image-filled structure intended to align mind and society with cosmic powers.
3. **Modern loose usage:** anything esoteric or astrologically decorated.

Only the first two are analytically defensible.

### Campanella’s actual magical practice

Campanella wrote *Del senso delle cose e della magia* and defended a pansensist natural philosophy in which the world is a living divine statue. His later *De siderali fato vitando* described procedures intended to avert harmful astral influence.

In 1628 Urban VIII, fearing astrological predictions connected with eclipses, consulted Campanella. The reported countermeasures included a sealed room, white cloths, lamps and torches representing planets, appropriate music, colors, plants, stones, perfumes, and astrologically prepared drinks. Campanella framed this as philosophical or natural practice, not demonic superstition.

The unauthorized or hostile 1629 publication of this material risked compromising him and the pope. Later research describes the controversy and his defenses against charges of superstition and heresy. [Germana Ernst, “The stars and the life of man”](https://iris.uniroma3.it/handle/11590/272992)

This establishes that Campanella practiced and theorized astral ritual. It does **not** prove that the City’s wall paintings were talismans.

### Accusations and Inquisition

Campanella faced multiple proceedings:

- 1592: inquiry concerning his defense of Telesian philosophy;
- 1594: arrest in Padua on accusations of heresy; some accusations concerning demonic practice collapsed or were shown unreliable;
- 1599 onward: prosecution for the Calabrian conspiracy, sedition, prophecy, and religious heterodoxy;
- later Roman scrutiny of *Atheismus triumphatus*, pansensism, predestination, astrology, and related writings.

His imprisonment followed the rebellion case and associated religious charges, not the publication of *Civitas Solis*, which did not appear until 1623.

A prison testimony reported by Amabile has Felice Gagliardo say in 1606 that Campanella instructed companions to address the rising and setting Sun:

> “O sacrosanto sole, lampa del cielo, padre de la natura…”

Gloss: “O most holy Sun, lamp of heaven, father of nature…”

This is a reported deposition in a hostile judicial setting, not an uncontested transcript of Campanella’s own liturgy. [Firpo’s notes reproducing the testimony](https://it.wikisource.org/wiki/Poesie_%28Campanella,_1915%29/Annotazioni)

### Ars notoria

There is no evidence that *La città del Sole* transmits the medieval *Ars notoria* with its prayers, angelic names, ritual schedules, and *notae*. Both promise accelerated acquisition of the sciences, but resemblance of ambition is insufficient to establish descent.

The contrast is substantial:

- *Ars notoria*: revelation, prayer, fasting, ritual figures;
- City: pictures, captions, teachers, walking, lectures, work, and sensory experience.

Any claim that the City is an *Ars notoria* handbook is unsupported.

### Talismanic status of the images

Yates reads the City against Ficinian and *Picatrix* traditions of astral images. Yet the dialogue does not instruct that the wall pictures be made at elected astrological times, engraved in particular materials, consecrated, or animated by ritual—the usual evidentiary markers of talismanic manufacture.

Best verdict: **cosmological and astrologically correspondent, possibly conceived within a natural-magical worldview, but not demonstrably a city-sized collection of operative talismans.**

## Afterlife

### Andreae’s *Christianopolis*

Johann Valentin Andreae’s *Christianopolis* appeared in 1619, before the printed *Civitas Solis* but after Italian manuscripts were circulating. Both offer geometrically ordered Christian learned communities. Direct influence is chronologically possible only through manuscript or personal transmission and remains debated. Shared dependence on More, ideal-city discourse, Protestant and Catholic reform, and encyclopedic pedagogy must also be considered.

### Comenius

Comenius’s *Orbis sensualium pictus* (1658) joins labeled pictures with words in a graded encyclopedia for children. The resemblance to Campanella is strong:

- the sensible world becomes a curriculum;
- pictures reduce drudgery;
- children acquire an overview before advanced study;
- knowledge is ordered encyclopedically;
- language is attached to things.

Yates’s “came straight out of” formulation overstates what can be established. A safer genealogy is:

**Campanella + Andreae/Alsted + realist pedagogy + illustrated-book traditions + Aristotelian sensory psychology → Comenian visual education.**

Comenius made a portable, printed classroom book where Campanella imagined a civic environment.

### Museums, galleries, frescoes, and emblem books

The City belongs retrospectively with:

- medieval church portals and fresco cycles;
- Dominican didactic painting;
- maps and cosmographies;
- natural-history cabinets;
- emblem books;
- encyclopedic galleries;
- Jesuit museums and theatres of the world.

Direct influence on any specific fresco cycle requires independent documentary proof. Many visual encyclopedias predate Campanella.

### Dante

Dante’s ordered otherworld, planetary ascent, encyclopedic poem, and vivid exemplary figures invited mnemonic readings long before Yates. Campanella knew Dante and wrote philosophical poetry in the vernacular. But no evidence makes Dante’s *Commedia* the specific architectural blueprint for the seven walls.

### Borges, Eco, and literary memory

Borges’s “Funes the Memorious” concerns limitless, disabling particular memory—the opposite of Campanella’s socially organized classification. The comparison is thematic, not genealogical.

Umberto Eco’s interest in encyclopedias, perfect languages, libraries, Lullian combinatorics, and the semiotics of culture makes the City a relevant precursor. Direct literary echoes must be identified work by work; the general resemblance does not prove borrowing.

### Modern memory palaces and athletes

Memory athletes normally use private, reusable routes and striking arbitrary images. Campanella’s City instead supplies public, permanent, referential images. A modern practitioner could convert its rings into a memory palace, but doing so would be a **modern adaptation**, not a recovered Campanellan exercise.

### Computing and combinatorics

The broad history “Llull → Bruno → Leibniz → computing” concerns formal combination and symbolic calculus. Campanella occupies a neighboring but different branch:

- Llull: limited alphabets and combinatory figures;
- Bruno: expanded mnemonic and ontological combinations;
- Campanella: spatial, sensory encyclopedia;
- Leibniz: universal characteristic and calculable reasoning;
- computing: formal symbolic operations implemented mechanically.

Calling the City an early computer is a modern metaphor. It has addressing, hierarchy, and linked representations, but no algorithm for mechanically transforming inputs.

## Controversies and disputes

### 1. Date of composition

**Traditional scholarly date:** early 1602.  
**Evidence:** biographical chronology, references in letters and poems, manuscript history.  
**Qualification:** no autograph dated “1602” conclusively closes the question; the CNR catalogue uses 1601–1602 and says the exact date is uncertain.

### 2. “Written in the Inquisition’s prisons”

Broadly true but imprecise. Campanella was held in Neapolitan castles under intertwined secular and ecclesiastical proceedings. Castel Nuovo was not simply a Roman-Inquisition prison. His long captivity resulted especially from the Spanish sedition trial and life sentence.

### 3. Original language

The Italian is original. The Latin is Campanella’s later adaptation. The mistaken belief that Latin was original spread because Latin was printed first and nineteenth-century Italian editions were translated back from it.

### 4. Is the City literally a mnemonic system?

- **For:** fixed ordered places, images, captions, traversal, rapid learning; letters explicitly invoke *memoria locale*.
- **Against a strict identification:** no technical mnemonic instructions, arbitrary encoding, or mental retrieval drill.
- **Best conclusion:** it is intentionally mnemonic pedagogy and is reconstructable as a local-memory environment; calling it a complete technical memory art goes beyond the surviving instructions.

### 5. Was there a physical model?

Yates says “or perhaps some model of it.” Campanella repeatedly sought practical realization, including in his appeal to Richelieu. But no surviving model, commission, payment record, plan, or eyewitness description has been identified.

Status: **possible proposal, no extant artifact.**

### 6. Bruno’s direct influence

Shared Dominican education at Naples is documented in the broad institutional sense; they did not belong to the convent at the same time as master and pupil. Similar solar, planetary, and image structures exist. Direct derivation from Bruno’s *Images* or *Shadows* is unproved.

### 7. Camillo’s direct influence

The comparison of static theatre and static city is Yates’s. No citation by Campanella of Camillo’s theatre establishes transmission. Both participate in Renaissance architectural encyclopedism.

### 8. The *Picatrix* and Adocentyn

Yates’s proposed archetype is suggestive but unsupported by an explicit citation or traceable copy used by Campanella. “There can be no doubt” is too strong by current evidentiary standards.

### 9. Are the wall images magical?

Astrological correspondences are explicit. Talismanic manufacture and ritual activation are not. The City’s educational pictures should not automatically be equated with the ritual apparatus used for Urban VIII.

### 10. Direct source of *Orbis pictus*

Possible influence is credible; exclusive or demonstrable direct descent is not. Comenius’s independent intellectual and pedagogical sources are too numerous for Yates’s categorical formula.

### 11. Was the utopia the program of the 1599 rebellion?

Campanella preached a coming renewal, natural law, common life, and an astrologically announced transformation. The later City preserves significant affinities. But the surviving dialogue is a post-defeat literary and philosophical construction. Treating every civic detail as the rebels’ literal constitution exceeds the trial evidence.

### 12. Did the City cause Campanella’s persecution?

No. The major imprisonment began before composition and long before publication. His conspiracy, prophecies, suspected heterodoxy, Telesianism, and later controversial doctrines caused scrutiny. The City is evidence of the world that emerged from those conflicts, not the originating corpus delicti.

### 13. “System 37 of 45”

This is a modern catalogue identifier, not a historical name or Campanellan enumeration.

## Open questions

1. Which precise letters did Yates associate with the City rather than with Campanella’s broader teaching project?
2. Did Campanella envisage a portable model, a full-scale city, illustrated scrolls, or ordinary classroom plates?
3. Can the revisions between the earliest Italian witness and the 1637 Latin edition reveal increasing or decreasing mnemonic emphasis?
4. Were the three-verse star captions ever separately composed?
5. Did Adami or Campanella’s German correspondents explicitly read the work as an art of memory?
6. Is there documentary evidence that Comenius read *Civitas Solis* before planning the *Orbis pictus*, rather than absorbing related ideas through Alsted or Andreae?
7. Can a specific *Picatrix* manuscript, translation, intermediary, or citation be connected to Campanella?
8. Were the “real specimens” beside the pictures intended merely for object teaching, or also as repositories of occult signatures?
9. How should the seven planetary circuits be reconciled with the six principal painted encyclopedic wall-series?
10. Did Campanella’s pupils or prison companions ever use his promised local-memory method and leave an independent report?
11. Are any early marginalia in the 1623, 1637, or 1643 printings explicitly mnemonic?
12. How much of Yates’s reading derives from Campanella, and how much from her prior reconstruction of Bruno and Camillo?

The most responsible final characterization is therefore:

> **Campanella’s City is a documented visual and ambulatory encyclopedia, explicitly allied by his correspondence with the project of making the world serve as “book and local memory.” Its treatment as a complete occult memory system is Yates’s powerful but only partly demonstrable reconstruction.**
