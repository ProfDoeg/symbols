# Hebrew numerals and gematria: Research Dossier

## Scope, method, and evidence labels

This dossier concerns the Hebrew alphabetic numeral system and gematria. The user’s general template also names the Ishango and Lebombo bones, Uruk tokens, Egyptian, Babylonian, Indian, Chinese, Roman, Maya, and Hindu-Arabic materials. Those objects are not ancestors or transmission stages of Hebrew alphabetic numerals, except where Egyptian, Aramaic, Phoenician, and Greek notation directly bear on Hebrew practice. Treating the Bakhshali manuscript, Gwalior zero, Dresden Codex, or Chinese counting rods as Hebrew origins would manufacture a relationship for which there is no evidence.

Claims are marked as follows:

- **[A — documented artefact]**: surviving inscription, coin, manuscript, or printed object.
- **[T — documented text]**: an identifiable literary source, though the surviving witness may be later than its composition.
- **[R — scholarly reconstruction]**: the prevailing or a significant historical inference from the evidence.
- **[D — disputed]**: substantially contested attribution, date, reading, origin, or interpretation.
- **[TR — tradition]**: a claim transmitted within Jewish or another historical tradition, not independently established as an event.
- **[L — legend]**: a story or claimed ancient fact unsupported by adequate evidence.
- **[M — modern invention]**: a recent system or assertion presented as ancient without historical documentation.

Dates are BCE/CE. Hebrew runs right-to-left; tables necessarily place it inside left-to-right Markdown.

## Basic identification

| Field | Identification |
|---|---|
| Name | Hebrew alphabetic numerals; Hebrew letter-numerals; in numerical exegesis, **gematria**, גימטריא or גמטריה |
| Base | Decimal organization: units 1–9, decades 10–90, hundreds 100–400 |
| Structural type | **Ciphered-additive alphabetic system**: distinct signs represent powers-of-ten multiples; their values are normally added. It is neither positional nor multiplicative in its ordinary form |
| Signs | The twenty-two Hebrew consonant letters: א–ת; optionally the five final forms ך ם ן ף ץ for 500–900 in later “large-value” practice |
| Zero | No zero sign and no empty positional place in classical alphabetic notation |
| Earliest secure decimal alphabetic attestation | Alexander Jannaeus year-25 coinage, conventionally 79/78 or 78 BCE |
| Possible earlier letter-label use | First ten letters on Hasmonean/Simon Maccabaeus issues, approximately 141–136 BCE |
| Earlier Hebrew number notation | Number words and, in Iron Age administration, Egyptian-hieratic-derived or related nonalphabetic numeral signs |
| Region | Judaea/Palestine initially; subsequently Jewish communities throughout the Mediterranean, Near East, North Africa, Europe, and the diaspora; modern residual use especially in Israel and Jewish religious printing |
| Main periods | Hellenistic/Hasmonean adoption; Roman and rabbinic expansion; medieval manuscript and mathematical use; early-modern print, chronograms, and Kabbalah; modern restricted use |
| Present status | Living but specialized: Hebrew calendar dates, biblical chapters and verses, Talmud folios, lists, school classes, religious citations, commemorative dates, coins/medals, and gematria. Hindu-Arabic digits dominate ordinary calculation |

Chrisomalis classifies systems of this sort as **ciphered-additive**, not positional: each order has separate ciphers, but the value of a string is normally its sum. His standard comparative treatment dates the Hebrew system to the late Hellenistic period and distinguishes it from the earlier hieratic-derived Hebrew notation. [Chrisomalis, *Numerical Notation*](https://nzdr.ru/data/media/biblio/kolxoz/M/MPop/Chrisomalis%20S.%20Numerical%20notation..%20A%20comparative%20history%20%28CUP%2C%202010%29%28ISBN%200521878187%29%28O%29%28498s%29_MPop_.pdf)

# The system in detail

## 1. The signs and their order

### Standard values: *mispar hekhreḥi*

**[Documented continuing convention]** The consonants are divided into three decimal series. The first nine letters mean 1–9; the next nine mean 10–90; the remaining four mean 100–400.

The allocation follows the inherited consonantal order. It is not based on the modern pronunciation, the visual complexity of the letters, or mystical meanings of their names. **[M]** Online diagrams claiming that the values derive from the number of angles in each glyph are modern inventions; the values instead follow alphabetic order in three decimal cycles.

### The final letters

Five letters have special word-final shapes:

| Ordinary form | Final form | Ordinary value | Later extended value |
|:---:|:---:|---:|---:|
| כ | ך | 20 | 500 |
| מ | ם | 40 | 600 |
| נ | ן | 50 | 700 |
| פ | ף | 80 | 800 |
| צ | ץ | 90 | 900 |

Two practices must not be conflated:

1. **Ordinary numeration and standard gematria:** ך, ם, ן, ף, and ץ retain 20, 40, 50, 80, and 90.
2. **Later extended or “large” gematria, *mispar gadol*:** they may be valued 500–900.

**[R/D]** The extended sequence is a later adaptation that makes Hebrew resemble a full 27-sign Greek alphabetic array. It is not secure evidence that final letters possessed those values when the Hebrew system arose. Historical hundreds above 400 were normally compounded: 500 = תק, 600 = תר, 700 = תש, 800 = תת, 900 = תתק. Gandz’s foundational study and Cajori’s older survey discuss the historical distinction. [Gandz, “Hebrew Numerals,” DOI record](https://doi.org/10.2307/3622115); [Cajori, *A History of Mathematical Notations*](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/A_History_of_Mathematical_Notations/Volume_1/Hebrews)

## 2. How numbers are formed

The normal algorithm is:

1. Select the largest appropriate value.
2. Continue with smaller values.
3. Add them.
4. Write the resulting sequence in Hebrew direction, conventionally largest value first.
5. Add numeral punctuation where needed.

Examples:

- 11 = יא = 10 + 1
- 27 = כז = 20 + 7
- 154 = קנד = 100 + 50 + 4
- 578 = תקעח = 400 + 100 + 70 + 8
- 999 = תתקצט = 400 + 400 + 100 + 90 + 9

It is thus decimal in organization but not a base-ten positional system. In 154, the value of ק is 100 because ק is intrinsically the 100-sign, not because it occupies a hundreds column.

Order is conventional rather than arithmetically indispensable: since addition is commutative, rearranging the letters ordinarily leaves the sum unchanged. Nevertheless, normal numeral writing descends from the greatest value to the least. Gematria, by contrast, normally preserves the spelling of the word being evaluated.

## 3. Numeral punctuation: geresh and gershayim

- A single-letter numeral normally receives a **geresh** after the letter: א׳, י׳, ק׳.
- A multi-letter numeral normally receives **gershayim** before the last letter: י״א, כ״ז, קנ״ד.
- Unicode provides distinct characters:
  - ׳ = U+05F3 HEBREW PUNCTUATION GERESH
  - ״ = U+05F4 HEBREW PUNCTUATION GERSHAYIM

They are not typographically identical to ASCII apostrophe `'` and quotation mark `"`, although keyboard practice frequently substitutes the latter. Unicode explicitly encodes the Hebrew punctuation separately. [Unicode Standard, Hebrew chapter](https://www.unicode.org/versions/Unicode16.0.0/core-spec/chapter-9/); [Unicode Hebrew chart](https://unicode.org/charts/PDF/U0590.pdf)

**[R]** These punctuation marks are disambiguators, not operators. Gershayim does not multiply the preceding letters or make them a new positional digit. It signals that the group is functioning as a numeral, abbreviation, or initialism rather than as an ordinary word.

Older manuscripts vary widely: dots, overbars, spaces, enlarged letters, or context may serve the same purpose. Therefore absence of modern gershayim does not prove that a medieval string was not numerical.

## 4. The special forms of 15 and 16

The mechanically expected forms are:

- 15 = יה, 10 + 5
- 16 = יו, 10 + 6

The conventional forms are instead:

- 15 = ט״ו, 9 + 6
- 16 = ט״ז, 9 + 7

**[TR, securely documented convention]** יה is a divine name, *Yah*, and also the opening of יהוה. יו can evoke a divine-name component found in theophoric names. Jewish scribal convention therefore avoids both in ordinary numeral use. Gesenius records the convention; modern Hebrew usage continues it. [Gesenius, §5](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Gesenius%27_Hebrew_Grammar/5._The_Consonants%3A_their_Forms_and_Names)

**[Open historical question]** The religious explanation is old and coherent, but the exact date at which both substitutions became universal is poorly documented in accessible artefact catalogues. One should not project the fully standardized modern rule automatically onto every Hasmonean or early rabbinic witness.

The substitutions survive in names of calendar observances:

- ט״ו בשבט, *Tu bi-Shvat*, the fifteenth of Shevat
- ט״ו באב, *Tu be-Av*, the fifteenth of Av

Here *Tu* is the conventional reading of טו, not a separate Hebrew word meaning fifteen.

### Thousands and beyond

There have been several conventions.

- A geresh may mark a letter as thousands: א׳ = 1,000, ב׳ = 2,000, and so forth, where context establishes the magnitude.
- A number may be divided into groups, each succeeding group representing thousands or millions.
- Hebrew calendar dates usually omit the millennium.
- Medieval writers developed dots or strokes to distinguish orders; practice was not uniform.
- Modern technical writing normally abandons this ambiguity and uses Hindu-Arabic positional figures.

Thus 5,786 may be written in full as ה׳תשפ״ו, but much Jewish usage writes only תשפ״ו, literally 786, with the fifth millennium understood. The omission is called the **minor era**, לפרט קטן (*li-frat qatan*); explicit inclusion is the **major era**, לפרט גדול.

There is no intrinsic largest representable integer. Repetition can extend the additive series, and thousands-group conventions can be iterated. What the unextended alphabet supplies directly is only 1–400; that is not the same as saying that 400 is the largest writable number.

Modern number words include:

- 0: אֶפֶס, *efes*
- 100: מֵאָה, *meʾah*
- 1,000: אֶלֶף, *elef*
- 10,000: עֲשֶׂרֶת אֲלָפִים
- 1,000,000: מִילְיוֹן, *milyon*
- 1,000,000,000: מִילְיַארְד, *milyard*

These lexical forms belong to Hebrew language, not to the internal architecture of alphabetic notation. The Academy of the Hebrew Language defines מיליון as \(10^6\). [Academy terminology database](https://terms.hebrew-academy.org.il/munnah/48991_2/million)

## 7. Zero and absence

### Classical alphabetic notation

**[Documented structural fact]** There is no zero sign. Since the system is additive, no placeholder is required:

- 101 = קא, 100 + 1
- nothing needs to occupy a tens position.

This differs from a positional system, in which 101 must distinguish an empty tens place.

The often-repeated phrase “Hebrew numerals had no zero” is true only about the classical alphabetic notation. It becomes misleading if expanded into any of the following claims:

- that Hebrew speakers lacked a word for nothing;
- that Jewish mathematicians could not understand zero;
- that no Hebrew mathematical work used zero;
- that Hebrew letters could never be adapted to positional arithmetic.

### Ibn Ezra’s positional system

**[T]** Abraham ibn Ezra’s twelfth-century *Sefer ha-Mispar* explicitly describes the Indian decimal system. He substitutes א–ט for the nine nonzero digits and uses a “wheel,” עיגול, as zero/placeholder. His worked example writes the equivalent of 4032 ÷ 30 and explains how zeros occupy missing ranks. This is a positional use of Hebrew letters, structurally different from ordinary gematria. [Digital *Sefer ha-Mispar* edition](https://mispar.ethz.ch/wiki/%D7%A1%D7%A4%D7%A8_%D7%94%D7%9E%D7%A1%D7%A4%D7%A8_/_%D7%90%D7%91%D7%A8%D7%94%D7%9D_%D7%90%D7%91%D7%9F_%D7%A2%D7%96%D7%A8%D7%90)

Accordingly, the accurate formulation is:

> Classical Hebrew alphabetic numeration has no zero because it is nonpositional; medieval Hebrew mathematicians nevertheless adopted and taught positional zero.

## 8. Fractions

Classical alphabetic numerals do not possess a single native fraction syntax equivalent to a modern horizontal numerator/denominator bar.

Practices include:

- lexical forms such as חצי, “half,” and רבע, “quarter”;
- ordinal-derived denominators: שליש, “third”; חמישית, “fifth”; עשירית, “tenth”;
- mixed verbal and numerical expressions;
- astronomical sexagesimal ranks;
- medieval fraction layouts borrowed through Arabic mathematical traditions;
- by the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries, increasingly common fraction bars.

**[T]** Ibn Ezra’s *Sefer ha-Mispar* treats fractions as a separate mathematical subject. The open research platform’s transcription shows positional zeros used in missing fractional ranks. [Posen Library description](https://www.posenlibrary.com/entry/book-number)

**[R]** A fraction such as ¾ can be written in modern Hebrew mathematical typography as `3/4` or `¾`, while its traditional-language expression is three quarters. Writing ג״ד and assuming that it inherently means 3/4 would be ambiguous and historically unjustified without a manuscript’s specific convention.

## 9. Ligatures and abbreviations

Hebrew numerals normally concatenate ordinary letters; they do not require dedicated numeral ligatures. Important qualifications are:

- gershayim visually binds a multi-letter numeral as a unit;
- scribes sometimes joined letters or used cursive forms, but these are handwriting phenomena, not a separate arithmetic syntax;
- ״ and ׳ also mark abbreviations, so context remains necessary;
- thousands notation can look identical to a one-letter numeral;
- title-page chronograms may mark only the letters that count, using dots, larger type, a different font, or an acrostic.

The Unicode Hebrew block encodes the letters and punctuation but no separate set of “Hebrew numeral digits.” The same encoded א is used as a consonant, list label, numeral, or gematria component.

## 10. Famous numbers

### 18: life

חי, *ḥai*, “life”:

- ח = 8
- י = 10
- total = 18

**[Documented cultural practice]** Gifts and charitable donations are often made in multiples of 18. The arithmetic is ancient-system-compatible; the modern fundraising prominence of multiples such as $18, $36, or $180 should not automatically be projected into antiquity.

### 26: the Tetragrammaton

יהוה:

- י = 10
- ה = 5
- ו = 6
- ה = 5
- total = 26

This value is important in Jewish mystical and devotional numerology. It also helps explain why numerical uses adjacent to divine names became religiously sensitive.

### 318: Eliezer

אליעזר:

- א 1 + ל 30 + י 10 + ע 70 + ז 7 + ר 200 = 318.

**[T/TR]** Genesis 14:14 says Abraham mobilized 318 retainers. Genesis Rabbah 43:2 and Babylonian Talmud Nedarim 32a interpret the number as Abraham’s servant Eliezer alone, because אליעזר totals 318. This is a securely documented rabbinic interpretation, but **not evidence that the author of Genesis encoded Eliezer**. The latter is a disputed historical inference.

### 30: an ordinary Nazirite term

יהיה, *yihyeh*, “he shall be”:

- י 10 + ה 5 + י 10 + ה 5 = 30.

**[T]** Babylonian Talmud Nazir 5a invokes this value in support of a thirty-day unspecified Nazirite term. [Nazir 5a](https://www.sefaria.org/Nazir.5a)

### 613: commandments

תרי״ג:

- ת 400 + ר 200 + י 10 + ג 3 = 613.

**[TR/T]** The count of 613 commandments is a rabbinic enumeration, classically associated with Makkot 23b–24a and later systematized by authorities such as Maimonides. תרי״ג consequently became both a numeral and an adjective in expressions about the commandments.

### 666: a useful boundary case

Revelation 13:18 calls 666 “the number of a man.” A leading reconstruction reads Nero Caesar in Hebrew letters, נרון קסר, as 666; a textual variant gives 616, compatible with a Latinized spelling lacking the final nun.

**[D]** This is one of the strongest historical gematria proposals in early Christian literature, but it remains an interpretation of a Greek Christian text employing a Semitic spelling. It is not proof that 666 held an established Jewish mystical meaning. The recently published comparative study of Hebrew/Greek names and number symbolism discusses the Roman-era context. [Open Book Publishers volume](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10219892/1/obp.0463.pdf)

# Origins: dated and placed

## 1. Before alphabetic numerals: number words

**[T]** The Hebrew Bible ordinarily writes numbers as words rather than letter-numerals. The Mesha inscription, closely related linguistically to ancient Hebrew, likewise writes such quantities lexically. The Siloam inscription writes its measurements in words.

**[Finding from absence]** No surviving biblical manuscript supplies evidence that the original biblical authors wrote quantities with the later א–ת cipher and that copyists subsequently expanded them. Proposed solutions to biblical numerical discrepancies based on confusion between alphabetic numeral letters are therefore speculative and often anachronistic.

## 2. Iron Age Hebrew: Egyptian-hieratic-derived signs

### Samaria ostraca

- **Date:** normally early eighth century BCE, with regnal-year chronology debated.
- **Place:** Samaria/Sebaste, capital of the northern kingdom of Israel.
- **Discovery:** 1910, Harvard expedition under George Andrew Reisner.
- **Context:** royal or administrative archive, found in fill beneath the “Ostraca House.”
- **Publication:** George A. Reisner, Clarence S. Fisher, and David G. Lyon, *Harvard Excavations at Samaria, 1908–1910* (1924).
- **Corpus:** conventionally 102 inscribed sherds.
- **Notation:** number words for some regnal years and hieratic-derived numerical signs for others.

**[A]** These are the earliest secure Hebrew inscriptions containing graphic numerals. They do **not** use א = 1, י = 10, ק = 100 in the later gematria arrangement. Their signs descend from Egyptian hieratic administrative notation. Chrisomalis notes that 10,000 could be expressed with the hieratic 10 sign plus the Hebrew lexical word “thousand,” a mixed notation proving that the writer was not simply an Egyptian scribe. [Chrisomalis](https://nzdr.ru/data/media/biblio/kolxoz/M/MPop/Chrisomalis%20S.%20Numerical%20notation..%20A%20comparative%20history%20%28CUP%2C%202010%29%28ISBN%200521878187%29%28O%29%28498s%29_MPop_.pdf); [Harvard excavation report](https://etana.org/sites/default/files/coretexts/17084.pdf)

The ostraca were read and published through the Harvard expedition’s scholarly team. Modern epigraphers including Christopher Rollston and Stefan Jakob Wimmer have refined the analysis of Hebrew scribal practice and hieratic numerals.

**Museum numbers:** No single current online museum catalogue provides a dependable one-to-one inventory table for the entire corpus. The excavation report uses excavation/catalogue numbers. Where modern popular accounts assign a single museum number to “the Samaria Ostraca,” that should be treated cautiously.

### Other Iron Age materials

Hieratic-derived numerals occur on additional Hebrew ostraca and weights, including material from Judaean sites such as Arad and Lachish. A recently published Lachish ostracon from the second half of the seventh century BCE contains a recognizable hieratic 10. [New Lachish ostracon report](https://jjar.huji.ac.il/sites/default/files/jjar/files/jjar08_02_lachish-ostracon.pdf)

**[D]** W. H. Shea proposed a thirteenth-century BCE jar-handle attestation. Chrisomalis observes that both its date and palaeographic interpretation are questionable and that Semitic epigraphers have not generally accepted it. It cannot serve as a secure first.

## 3. Phoenician and Aramaic neighbours

**[R]** During the Persian period, Phoenician-Aramaic numeral signs increasingly dominate Levantine administrative writing. These signs were nonalphabetic cumulative-additive marks, although the same scripts also used letters for serial labels.

A crucial control is the bilingual ostracon from Khirbet el-Qom:

- **Date:** year 6 of Ptolemy II, 277 BCE.
- **Place:** Khirbet el-Qom, Judaea/Idumaea.
- **Object:** Aramaic-Greek receipt.
- **Content:** a receipt for 32 zuz/drachmas.
- **Evidence:** the Greek side employs Greek alphabetic numerals; the Aramaic side still uses nonalphabetic Aramaic number signs.

**[A/R]** This makes straightforward Hebrew-to-Greek priority difficult: in the same regional document, Greek already possesses alphabetic numerals while Aramaic does not. [Text and translation](https://www.attalus.org/docs/other/inscr_209.html); [comparative study](https://doi.org/10.1017/s001781602510076x)

## 4. From letter-labels to decimal alphabetic numerals

### Simon Maccabaeus issues, approximately 141–136 BCE

**[A/D]** Some shekels associated in scholarship with Simon use letters of the first decade to designate years. These may be true numerical letters, or only **ordinal labels**—A, B, C-style serial notation. A sequence using only the first ten letters cannot by itself establish the later decimal values כ = 20, ל = 30, and so forth.

### The possible year-40 seal

A seal reading approximately “Jonathan the High Priest, Jerusalem” also carries מ.

**[D]** Frank Moore Cross and Georges Ifrah discussed the possibility that מ means “year 40,” perhaps 103 BCE, which would make it an early Semitic letter-numeral. But the object’s reading and interpretation are not secure enough to displace the year-25 coin as the first unambiguous decimal example.

### Alexander Jannaeus year 25, 79/78 or 78 BCE

The decisive object class is the bilingual coinage of Alexander Jannaeus:

- **Ruler:** Alexander Jannaeus, ruled 103–76 BCE.
- **Mint:** Jerusalem/Judaea.
- **Date legend:** שנת כה, “year 25.”
- **Numeral:** כה = 20 + 5.
- **Greek parallel:** ΚΕ = 20 + 5 on bilingual variants.
- **Tübingen object:** Institut für Klassische Archäologie, Sammlung Müller J087, object ID2774; bronze prutah, 1.03 g, 13 mm.
- **British Museum object:** 1905,1016.10, a probable contemporary imitation, dated by the museum to 78 BCE/year 25.

**[A]** Because כ demonstrably means 20 rather than “the eleventh item,” this is the earliest secure Hebrew/Jewish use of the mature decimal alphabetic structure now used in gematria. [Tübingen catalogue](https://www.ikmk.uni-tuebingen.de/object?id=ID2774); [British Museum 1905,1016.10](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/C_1905-1016-10)

The precise conversion of Jannaeus’s regnal year varies by one or two years among catalogues—78 BCE in the British Museum and Tübingen records, 80/79 BCE in some numismatic catalogues. The numeral reading “year 25” is firmer than the modern-calendar conversion.

## 5. Was the system borrowed from Greek?

### Prevailing reconstruction

**[R]** Hebrew adopted the structural principle from Greek Milesian/alphabetic numeration during the Hellenistic period, approximately 200–78 BCE. Evidence:

- Greek alphabetic notation is earlier.
- Greek and Hebrew both assign separate signs to 1–9, 10–90, and 100s.
- The 277 BCE bilingual ostracon contrasts Greek letter-numerals with Aramaic nonalphabetic signs.
- The Jannaeus coin carries matching Greek ΚΕ and Semitic כה for 25.
- Hellenistic Judaea was saturated with Greek administrative and numismatic practice.

### Qualification

Hebrew did not borrow the Greek glyphs. It assigned values to its own inherited alphabet in the same decimal pattern. Nor does Greek influence mean that Jews lacked earlier number notation; Hebrew scribes already had number words and hieratic/Aramaic signs.

### Alternative and older proposals

- **[D] Semitic priority:** Older scholarship sometimes held that Greeks borrowed alphabetic numerical values with the alphabet from Phoenicia. Expanding archaeological evidence has weakened that view because secure Greek alphabetic numerals precede secure full Semitic decimal letter-numerals.
- **[R/D] Egyptian ancestry of the structural idea:** Chrisomalis argues that Greek alphabetic numerals themselves may have been structurally inspired by Egyptian Demotic ciphers through trade in Asia Minor. This would make the chain approximately Egyptian Demotic → Greek alphabetic → Hebrew alphabetic, but the Greek-to-Hebrew stage is substantially better evidenced than the proposed Demotic-to-Greek stage. [Chrisomalis, “Egyptian Origin of the Greek Alphabetic Numerals”](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/abs/egyptian-origin-of-the-greek-alphabetic-numerals/B21F16056A89CCDF000EE98CAC4E43AE)
- **[M/L] primordial Hebrew mathematics:** Claims that the alphabet’s original letter shapes encoded their gematria values in Bronze Age Egypt, that alef was designed as “one,” or that the Hebrew alphabet began as a complete cosmological number code have no accepted archaeological sequence behind them.

## 6. Roman-period consolidation

### First Jewish Revolt, 66–70 CE

**[A]** Revolt shekels and bronze coins employ Paleo-Hebrew inscriptions and letter-dates:

- א above the chalice: year 1, 66/67.
- ש ב or שנת שתים: year 2.
- ש ד: year 4.
- שנה ה or abbreviated forms: year 5.

A Walters Art Museum silver shekel, accession 59.518, reads ד ש, “year four,” and dates to 69–70 CE. [Walters 59.518](https://art.thewalters.org/object/59.518/)

These coins deliberately revive archaizing Paleo-Hebrew forms. Their letters are numerical by function even though their shapes differ from modern square Hebrew.

### Bar Kokhba Revolt, 132–135 CE

**[A]** Overstruck coins use Paleo-Hebrew legends and regnal/revolt dates such as “year one” and “year two of the freedom of Israel.” This continues the association of Hebrew letter-dating with political sovereignty.

### Judaean Desert documents

Documents from Qumran, Wadi Murabbaʿat, Naḥal Ḥever, and related sites preserve Hebrew-Aramaic numeral notation in administrative and calendrical contexts. Their evidence shows overlap rather than instantaneous replacement: older abstract signs, written number words, and alphabetic forms coexist.

## 7. Rabbinic period

The Mishnah was redacted around 200 CE; the Palestinian Talmud in late antiquity and Babylonian Talmud approximately fifth–sixth centuries, with later transmission.

**[T]** Mishnah Avot 3:18 attributes to Rabbi Eleazar Ḥisma the saying that calculations of calendrical cycles and *gematriot* are “desserts” or secondary embellishments of wisdom. The precise sense of *gematriot* here has been debated—it may include mathematical calculations rather than only numerical word-exegesis.

**[T]** Rabbinic texts explicitly use numerical gematria:

- Nedarim 32a: 318 = Eliezer.
- Nazir 5a: יהיה = 30.
- Berakhot 8a: תוצאות = 903 kinds of death.
- Shabbat 10b: נא = 51 in comparison with Sodom’s 52 years.
- Shabbat 70a: אלה הדברים contributes to the traditional count of 39 Sabbath labours.

These texts prove rabbinic use of the technique. They do not prove that each interpretation was intended by the biblical writer.

# Arithmetic and instruments

## 1. A notation is not a calculating machine

Hebrew letter-numerals are effective for labels, totals, dates, and short accounts, but cumbersome for long multiplication or division. There is no evidence for a distinct “Hebrew abacus” structurally tied to א–ת in the way positional digits are tied to written algorithms.

**[R]** Ancient Jewish merchants and administrators likely calculated with the same material and mental repertoire available in the surrounding Mediterranean and Near Eastern cultures:

- fingers and memorized tables;
- counters or pebbles;
- dust or ruled counting surfaces;
- written intermediate totals;
- sexagesimal astronomical tables;
- later Indian positional algorithms transmitted through Arabic scholarship.

The Greek *abakion*, Roman counting board, and later European abacus provide comparative context, but a Roman abacus in a museum is not automatically evidence of Jewish use.

## 2. Biblical and rabbinic arithmetic

Biblical arithmetic is embedded in measurements, taxes, censuses, architectural descriptions, calendars, weights, and proportional distributions. Rabbinic literature contains:

- calendar calculation;
- areas and volumes;
- mixture and purity ratios;
- commercial arithmetic;
- inheritance and damages;
- astronomical cycles.

These problems were generally stated in words. Letter-numerals served as concise written numbers but did not define the algorithms.

## 3. *Mishnat ha-Middot*

**[T/D]** *Mishnat ha-Middot*, “Treatise of Measures,” is the earliest known Hebrew geometrical treatise. It includes mensurational problems and uses \(3\frac17\) for π, in contrast to the rough value 3 implicit in some biblical and mishnaic examples.

Its date is disputed:

- Hermann Schapira and Solomon Gandz argued for an early, possibly mishnaic stratum.
- Other scholarship places it in the post-Talmudic or Judaeo-Arabic milieu because of affinities with Greek and Islamic mensuration.

The surviving textual history cannot securely establish a tannaitic mathematical textbook. [Posen Library](https://www.posenlibrary.com/entry/treatise-measures); [digitized 1880 edition](https://sammlungen.ub.uni-frankfurt.de/freimann/content/titleinfo/4994522)

## 4. Abraham ibn Ezra and *Sefer ha-Mispar*

Abraham ibn Ezra (1089–1164), born in Tudela and active across Christian Europe after leaving al-Andalus, was a biblical commentator, poet, grammarian, astronomer, and mathematician.

**[T]** His *Sefer ha-Mispar* is a seven-part arithmetic textbook covering:

- numeration;
- addition and subtraction;
- multiplication and division;
- fractions;
- proportions or related numerical operations;
- square roots.

Its historically important maneuver is to explain Indian positional numeration using Hebrew letters א–ט as the nine digits and a circle for zero. Hence the same Hebrew character can operate in two incompatible systems:

- כ = 20 in ordinary alphabetic numeration;
- a letter such as ב can mean the digit 2 whose value depends on its column in Ibn Ezra’s adapted positional system.

A British Library witness is Or 10785, fols. 1r–36r, with an additional mathematical text on multiplication, division, and roots at fols. 37r–39v. [British Library Or 10785](https://searcharchives.bl.uk/catalog/032-003392201)

## 5. Arabic-to-Hebrew mathematical transmission

Jewish scholars translated and adapted Arabic arithmetic, astronomy, geometry, and algebra into Hebrew. Notable figures and texts include:

- Abraham bar Ḥiyya, early twelfth century, whose mathematical and astronomical writing transmitted Arabic science into Latin and Hebrew settings;
- Abraham ibn Ezra, *Sefer ha-Mispar*;
- Moses ibn Tibbon’s 1271 Hebrew translation of al-Ḥaṣṣār’s arithmetic, *Sefer ha-Ḥeshbon*;
- Levi ben Gershom/Gersonides (1288–1344), *Maʿaseh Ḥoshev* (1321);
- Mordecai Comtino (1402–1482), *Sefer ha-Ḥeshbon we-ha-Middot*;
- Elijah Mizraḥi (c. 1455–1525), whose printed *Sefer ha-Mispar* appeared at Constantinople in 1534.

The ETH *Mispar* project catalogues this corpus and its vocabulary. [Mispar research platform](https://mispar.ethz.ch/wiki/Main_Page)

## 6. Calculation practice and direction

Ordinary Hebrew alphabetic numerals follow right-to-left script direction. Medieval positional adaptations could arrange the units-first description according to the writer’s directional framework while retaining decimal ranks. Modern Hebrew mathematical expressions use international positional digits and are processed left-to-right inside right-to-left prose.

This mixed direction is typographically awkward but not a new numeral system.

# Transmission and replacement

## 1. A concise transmission diagram

Egyptian hieratic administrative signs  
→ Iron Age Hebrew hieratic-derived numerals

Phoenician/Aramaic cumulative signs  
→ Persian/Hellenistic Levantine administrative notation

Egyptian Demotic?  
→ **[D/R]** Greek alphabetic system  
→ **[R]** Hebrew alphabetic system, late Hellenistic Judaea

Indian positional notation  
→ Arabic mathematical scholarship  
→ medieval Hebrew scientific writing  
→ modern international digits in Hebrew

These are multiple episodes, not one continuous march from ancient Egypt to modern Hebrew.

## 2. Greek and Hasmonean mediation

Hellenistic coinage supplied both a model and an administrative setting. The Jannaeus year-25 bilingual issue is unusually strong evidence because Greek ΚΕ and Hebrew/Aramaic כה appear in corresponding roles.

## 3. From Judaea to the Jewish diaspora

Rabbinic literature made numerical letter-values part of the shared learned tradition. As Hebrew manuscripts circulated through Babylonia, Palestine, North Africa, Italy, Iberia, France, Germany, Yemen, and elsewhere, the notation was used for:

- sections and lists;
- manuscript quires;
- colophons and dates;
- biblical and rabbinic references;
- astronomical tables;
- mnemonic totals;
- gematria.

Hebrew codicology records Arabic numeral or word signatures added opposite Hebrew quire signatures, probably for non-Jewish binders. This is direct evidence of coexistence between systems rather than a clean succession. [National Library of Israel codicology survey](https://web.nli.org.il/sites/NLI/English/collections/manuscripts/hebrewcodicology/Documents/HC%20ENGLISH%20ACCUMULATED%201-5%2019.7.17%20%28Autosaved%29.pdf)

## 4. Printing and the Talmud page

Daniel Bomberg, a Christian printer in Venice employing Jewish editors and scholars, issued the first complete printed Babylonian Talmud in 1520–1523.

**[A/R]** Bomberg’s foliation and page architecture became canonical:

- the Talmud text occupies the centre;
- Rashi appears on the inner side;
- Tosafot on the outer;
- each leaf is a *daf*;
- its two sides are *amud alef* and *amud bet*;
- citations use Hebrew numeral folios, for example ברכות ב ע״א, Berakhot 2a.

The first textual leaf is daf ב because the title leaf effectively occupies the first folio. Later printings reproduced Bomberg’s page breaks so closely that citations remain stable across editions. [History of printing the Talmud](https://jewishaction.com/jewish-world/history/printing-the-shas/)

This is among the most consequential survivals of Hebrew numerals: millions of modern citations depend on sixteenth-century foliation.

## 5. Replacement by positional digits

Hindu-Arabic figures gradually prevailed for extended computation because they offer:

- place value;
- zero;
- compact multiplication and division;
- compatibility with commercial and scientific algorithms;
- international printing standards.

Replacement was functional, not total. Hebrew numerals remained advantageous where numbers also function as letters, labels, memorial formulae, or sacred-text references.

## 6. Present-day distribution

In contemporary Israel, Hindu-Arabic digits dominate:

- prices and money;
- addresses;
- phone numbers;
- ages;
- civil dates;
- scientific and engineering notation;
- most ordinary pagination.

Hebrew alphabetic numerals remain common for:

- Hebrew calendar dates;
- holidays and days of a Hebrew month;
- biblical chapter and verse numbers;
- Talmud folios;
- numbered clauses, school grades, and list labels;
- rabbinic works;
- memorial inscriptions;
- government or military operations named by letters;
- gematria and symbolic gifts.

**[A/T]** In 1999 the Israeli Supreme Court held that Western numerals could be placed on gravestones in Orthodox cemeteries, where Hebrew letters had previously been required by burial authorities. Chrisomalis cites this as an unusually clear modern conflict over the social jurisdiction of a numeral system.

# People

## Ancient rulers, scribes, and excavators

### Anonymous Iron Age administrators

The Samaria ostraca’s writers are unnamed palace or estate scribes. Their mixed use of words and hieratic-derived signs supplies better evidence for actual Israelite bureaucratic numeracy than later stories about biblical sages inventing gematria.

### Ptolemy II Philadelphus, ruled 285–246 BCE

His year-6 dating anchors the Khirbet el-Qom bilingual receipt to 277 BCE. The object captures Greek and Aramaic notation side by side.

### Simon Maccabaeus, active second century BCE

Coin legends conventionally associated with his regime use initial letters as year indicators. Whether they demonstrate full decimal gematria is disputed.

### Alexander Jannaeus, ruled 103–76 BCE

His bilingual year-25 coinage provides the earliest secure full-decimal Hebrew/Aramaic letter-number.

### George Andrew Reisner, 1867–1942

Directed the 1909–1910 Harvard work at Samaria and excavated the ostraca archive in 1910. Fisher and Lyon joined in the definitive excavation publication.

### Frank Moore Cross, 1921–2012

Discussed early West Semitic inscriptions and the disputed year-40 seal as possible evidence for alphabetic numerals.

### Solomon Gandz, 1883–1954

Published “Hebrew Numerals” in *Proceedings of the American Academy for Jewish Research* 4 (1932–33), pp. 53–112, still a fundamental historical study.

### Stephen Chrisomalis

His *Numerical Notation: A Comparative History* (Cambridge, 2010) supplies the current standard cross-cultural typology and distinguishes ciphered-additive Hebrew notation from positional systems.

## Rabbis and exegetes

### Rabbi Eleazar Ḥisma, second century CE

Traditionally credited in Mishnah Avot 3:18 with the remark on calendar calculations and *gematriot* as embellishments of wisdom.

### Rabbi Eliezer ben Yose ha-Gelili, second century CE

The Baraita of Thirty-Two Rules is traditionally attributed to him. Rule 29 in the transmitted compilation is gematria; rule 30 is notarikon.

**[D]** The attribution of the entire surviving compilation is not secure. The examples cite later authorities, and the text survives through medieval transmission. Moshe Zucker argued from Geniza material for composition by Samuel ben Ḥofni Gaon (d. 1013); A. Greenbaum challenged that conclusion. [Encyclopedia entry](https://www.encyclopedia.com/religion/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/baraita-32-rules)

### Saadia Gaon, 882/892–942

His 931 commentary on *Sefer Yetzirah* interprets its letter combinations and defends 231 gates rather than the manuscript variant 221.

### Shabbatai Donnolo, 913–c. 982

Produced an early commentary on *Sefer Yetzirah*, combining Jewish, medical, astronomical, and cosmological learning.

### Judah ben Barzillai of Barcelona, late eleventh–early twelfth century

Wrote an extensive commentary on *Sefer Yetzirah*, preserving early interpretive traditions.

### Abraham ibn Ezra, 1089–1164

Introduced Indian positional arithmetic in Hebrew dress, including a true placeholder zero. The anecdotal image of Ibn Ezra merely “doing gematria” understates his technical engagement with place-value algorithms.

### Judah he-Ḥasid, c. 1150–1217, and Eleazar of Worms, c. 1176–1238

Central figures of the German Pietists, *Ḥasidei Ashkenaz*. Their writings greatly expanded mystical and exegetical gematria, including elaborate letter combinations and numerical correspondences.

### Abraham Abulafia, 1240–after 1291

Developed an ecstatic-prophetic Kabbalah centred on letter permutation, pronunciation, writing, and numerical transformation.

### Joseph Gikatilla, 1248–after 1305

Used gematria extensively. The title *Ginnat Egoz* was interpreted through the initials גנת as gematria, notarikon, and temurah.

### Jacob ben Jacob ha-Kohen, thirteenth century

Associated with the transmission of Ashkenazi numerical and angelological traditions into Spanish Kabbalah.

### Isaac Luria, 1534–1572, and Ḥayyim Vital, 1542–1620

Lurianic Kabbalah reinvigorated systems of divine-name and letter interpretation. Gematria is present, although later Sarugian and other expositors often use it more expansively than the core Lurianic writings.

### Shabbetai Zevi, 1626–1676, and Nathan of Gaza, 1643–1680

Sabbatean writers used gematria to support messianic identification and date calculations. These computations document the movement’s rhetoric; they do not independently validate its prophetic claims.

## Printers and bibliographers

### The Soncino family

Their 1484 Soncino Talmud uses גמרא, “Gemara,” as a chronogram for 244, understood as 5244 AM = 1483/84 CE. The Jewish Encyclopedia calls it the earliest printed Hebrew chronogram.

### Daniel Bomberg, c. 1483–c. 1549

Standardized the printed Talmud’s foliation and layout.

### Judah al-Ḥarizi, 1165–1225

A Hebrew poem dated 1205 is commonly identified as the earliest known literary Hebrew chronogram, though epigraphic and manuscript discoveries could alter that priority.

# Culture

## 1. Gematria: definition and limits

Gematria reads a Hebrew word, name, or phrase as the sum—or under special methods, another function—of its letters’ values.

It has at least four historically distinct uses:

1. **Numeral notation:** letters directly write quantities.
2. **Mnemonic or rhetorical calculation:** a total helps remember or decorate a teaching.
3. **Exegetical gematria:** equal values connect biblical expressions.
4. **Mystical or magical gematria:** totals disclose divine names, cosmological structures, angels, ritual correspondences, or predictions.

Calling every Hebrew letter-number “Kabbalah” collapses these separate practices.

## 2. Gematria in the Hebrew Bible

**[Finding]** The Bible nowhere names gematria as a method and its transmitted consonantal text ordinarily writes numbers as words.

### Proposed examples

- Genesis 14:14, 318 = אליעזר.
- Deuteronomy 32:1–6, initials allegedly totaling 345, משה.
- Ezekiel 4–5, letter sums allegedly coordinating 390.
- Ecclesiastes 7:27 and other passages alleged to contain numerical wordplay.

**[D]** Some scholars and religious interpreters regard selected examples as deliberate. Others observe that:

- the alphabetic numeral system is not securely attested until the late second/first century BCE;
- Hebrew spelling was variable;
- long texts yield accidental numerical equivalences easily;
- the rabbinic interpretation may be creative reception rather than authorial code.

The older *Jewish Encyclopedia* concludes that numerical gematria cannot be positively demonstrated in the Bible. A recent *Jewish Bible Quarterly* article argues for biblical examples but openly acknowledges the prevailing chronological objection. [Jewish Encyclopedia](https://sefaria.jewishencyclopedia.com/articles/6571-gematria); [Jewish Bible Quarterly](https://www.jewishbible.org/articles/gematria-in-the-hebrew-bible/)

The responsible conclusion is not “the Bible certainly contains no numerical artistry,” but “specific authorial gematria requires independent constraints stronger than a matching total.”

## 3. Gematria in the Talmud

Rabbinic sources use gematria openly, but with differing authority.

- Some passages present it as support for law.
- Many employ it in aggadah, homily, or mnemonic reasoning.
- The Baraita’s classification treats it as one interpretive technique among many.
- Avot’s “desserts of wisdom” has been read both as praise and as a limitation.

The method was never a single universally controlling rule capable of overriding ordinary grammar, context, or received law.

## 4. *Sefer Yetzirah*

*Sefer Yetzirah*, “Book of Formation,” organizes creation through:

- ten *sefirot belimah*;
- twenty-two foundation letters;
- three “mothers”;
- seven “doubles”;
- twelve “simples”;
- a wheel and combinations traditionally called 231 gates.

**[T]** The work undeniably makes number, letter, permutation, speech, and cosmology mutually constitutive. It is therefore fundamental to the later culture of letter mysticism.

**[D — date]** Proposed dates range from the early centuries CE to the early Islamic period. Earlier scholarship often placed it between approximately the second and sixth centuries. Joseph Dan and Tzahi Weiss have advanced later settings, with Weiss arguing for a seventh-century Christian-Syriac intellectual context. The earliest extensive commentaries are tenth century, including Saadia, Dunash ibn Tamim, and Donnolo. That gap prevents confident claims that the present text is a first- or second-century composition.

**[D — 221/231 gates]** Twenty-two letters yield 231 unordered pairs:

\[
\binom{22}{2}=231.
\]

Yet manuscripts also read 221. Early commentators including Saadia criticized 221 and calculated 231, while recent manuscript research argues that 221 was not merely an isolated scribal slip. [Research summary on 221/231](https://cris.iucc.ac.il/en/publications/%D7%A0%D7%9E%D7%A6%D7%90%D7%95-%D7%99%D7%95%D7%A6%D7%90%D7%95%D7%AA-%D7%91%D7%A8%D7%9C%D7%90-%D7%A9%D7%A2%D7%A8%D7%99%D7%9D-%D7%A0%D7%90-%D7%9E%D7%90%D7%AA%D7%99%D7%9D-%D7%A2%D7%A9%D7%A8%D7%99%D7%9D-%D7%95%D7%90%D7%97%D7%93-%D7%A2%D7%9C-%D7%A9%D7%99%D7%98%D7%95%D7%AA-%D7%94/)

*Sefer Yetzirah* should not simply be called “a book of gematria.” Its central techniques are cosmological classification and letter combination; later readers developed them into fuller gematriatic systems.

## 5. Notarikon and temurah

These are related but distinct.

### Notarikon

A word’s letters are expanded as initials or endings of other words, or a phrase is compressed into an acronym. Its name is normally connected with Latin *notarius*, a shorthand writer, mediated through Greek or Aramaic usage.

**[T]** Notarikon appears in rabbinic interpretive traditions and is listed adjacent to gematria in the Baraita of Thirty-Two Rules. It is not inherently numerical.

### Temurah

תמורה means substitution or exchange. Letters are systematically replaced or permuted.

Examples include:

- **Atbash:** א ↔ ת, ב ↔ ש, ג ↔ ר, etc.
- **Albam:** division into paired halves.
- other kabbalistic substitution alphabets.

Jeremiah’s ששך for Babylon has long been interpreted as Atbash:

- ב → ש
- ב → ש
- ל → כ

**[D]** This is a plausible ancient cryptographic substitution, but it is not numerical gematria. Later Kabbalah grouped gematria, notarikon, and temurah as a family of letter sciences.

## 6. Medieval and kabbalistic expansion

German Pietists used gematria for:

- prayer-word counts;
- divine names;
- angel names;
- ethical and ritual correspondences;
- explanations of liturgical wording.

Spanish Kabbalists used it for:

- relations among divine names and *sefirot*;
- permutations;
- prophetic meditation;
- cosmological equivalences.

**[R]** Gematria became central to some schools of Kabbalah, but not uniformly. Gershom Scholem’s encyclopedia account emphasizes that intensive numerical practice entered much thirteenth-century Kabbalah especially through Ashkenazi influence. [Encyclopaedia Judaica article](https://www.encyclopedia.com/philosophy-and-religion/bible/bible-general/gematria)

## 7. Principal gematria methods

| Method | Rule | Example or consequence |
|---|---|---|
| *Mispar hekhreḥi* | Standard 1–400 values | חי = 18 |
| *Mispar gadol* | Finals ךםןףץ = 500–900 | final letters differ from medial equivalents |
| *Mispar siduri* | Alphabetic positions 1–22 | כ = 11, not 20 |
| *Mispar katan* | Reduce tens/hundreds to units or take digit roots, depending on source | ק may count as 1 |
| *Mispar kolel* | Add one for the word or whole expression | convention varies |
| *Milui* | Spell out each letter’s name and total the expanded spelling | א becomes אלף |
| *Kidmi* | Sum cumulative preceding values | definitions vary |
| Atbash | Replace first with last, second with penultimate | cryptographic, not primarily additive |
| *Temurah* systems | Substitute/permutate by a fixed alphabet table | many medieval variants |
| *Notarikon* | Read initials, finals, or expansions | nonnumeric unless combined with gematria |

**Caution:** Names and definitions were not standardized across all periods. Modern calculator sites often present twenty-five, fifty, or hundreds of “ancient methods” assembled from heterogeneous books. A named modern menu is not evidence that every method formed one classical system.

## 8. Chronograms

A chronogram is a word, phrase, or verse whose selected letter-values state a year.

### Manuscripts and poetry

**[T]** A Hebrew poem by Judah al-Ḥarizi dated 1205 is frequently cited as the earliest literary Hebrew chronogram.

### Printed books

**[A/T]** Hebrew printers made chronograms a major bibliographical art:

- גמרא = ג 3 + מ 40 + ר 200 + א 1 = 244.
- Under the minor era, 244 means AM 5244.
- AM 5244 corresponds to 1483/84 CE, the Soncino Talmud’s date.

Later chronograms use entire biblical verses. Only selected letters might count, indicated by:

- enlarged type;
- dots;
- a line above;
- different fonts;
- textual instructions such as “according to the minor era.”

This practice causes real cataloguing difficulties. Damage, missing emphasis, or an omitted millennium can shift a date by centuries. [Jewish Encyclopedia, “Chronogram”](https://www.jewishencyclopedia.com/articles/4372-chronogram)

### Tombstones

Hebrew epitaphs commonly encode death dates through biblical phrases relevant to the deceased’s name, virtues, or circumstances.

**[D]** Abraham Firkovich claimed very early Crimean Jewish chronograms, including sixth-century examples. Scholars have judged many of his inscriptions or dates to be forged or altered. They cannot securely move the history of Hebrew chronograms back to 582 CE.

## 9. Calendars

Hebrew dates generally total the letters of the year and omit the thousands:

- תשפ״ו = 400 + 300 + 80 + 6 = 786;
- understood in the present millennium as 5786 AM.

A full version is ה׳תשפ״ו. Because ה׳ can also mean 5, context supplies “five thousand.”

Days of months use the same forms:

- א׳ בתשרי, first of Tishri;
- ט״ו בשבט, fifteenth of Shevat;
- ט״ז בניסן, sixteenth of Nisan.

The Hebrew calendar itself is lunisolar; its calendrical mathematics is independent of whether dates are printed as תשפ״ו or 5786.

## 10. Law and liturgy

Gematria is found mainly in aggadic, exegetical, mnemonic, and mystical reasoning. It occasionally supports legal discussions, but classical jurisprudence does not ordinarily permit unconstrained numerical coincidences to overturn explicit text or established halakhah.

In liturgy it appears through:

- fixed word and letter counts;
- acrostics;
- divine-name totals;
- numerically structured poems;
- mystical prayer manuals.

Whether a particular prayer was originally designed around a numerical total must be demonstrated from stable wording. Variant rites often change the count.

## 11. Manuscript and print culture

Hebrew letters served as:

- quire signatures;
- folio numbers;
- section headings;
- running references;
- catchword systems;
- printers’ dates;
- censorship-era evasions and abbreviations.

The reuse of the alphabet economized type: no separate numeral font was needed. Conversely, the shared signs produced ambiguity, which geresh, gershayim, dots, and layout resolved.

## 12. Coins and state symbolism

Ancient Jewish rebels used Paleo-Hebrew letters and dates to assert political and cultic identity. Modern Israeli coinage and commemorative issues sometimes place Hebrew dates alongside international digits. The Bank of Israel presents Great Revolt examples with alef for year one and shin-he for year five. [Bank of Israel currency exhibition](https://kamakama.gov.il/en/bank-of-israel/education-and-visitors-center/the-currency-exhibit-development-of-money/part3/)

## 13. Literature, art, and popular culture

Gematria appears in:

- piyyut and riddling poetry;
- kabbalistic diagrams;
- amulets;
- Hasidic homilies;
- modern fiction about hidden codes;
- visual art built around חי, 18, 26, or letter permutations;
- charitable appeals requesting multiples of 18.

Popular “Bible code” culture frequently treats any equal sum as intentional. Historiically controlled analysis asks instead:

1. Is the spelling stable in the earliest witnesses?
2. Was the numerical method available at that date?
3. Is the proposed method independently signalled?
4. Is the match statistically selective?
5. Does it explain something that ordinary language cannot?
6. Are alternative equal-value words being ignored?

# Samaritan and Aramaic parallels

## 1. Samaritan

Samaritan script descends from Paleo-Hebrew rather than from the Jewish square script. It has twenty-two consonants and is encoded in Unicode U+0800–U+083E.

**[Documented modern/historical convention]** Samaritan letters can receive numerical values corresponding to the same alphabetic sequence. Unicode specifically notes that U+0824 SAMARITAN MODIFIER LETTER SHORT A after a numerically used letter can indicate thousands, analogous to Hebrew geresh. [Unicode 17, Chapter 9](https://unicode.org/versions/Unicode17.0.0/core-spec/chapter-9/)

**[R]** This parallel reflects shared alphabetic order and long contact; it does not prove that the mature decimal Hebrew gematria system existed in pre-schism Israel.

Samaritan literature also develops theology of letters. Comparisons between *Memar Marqah*, rabbinic sources, and *Sefer Yetzirah* are legitimate fields of study, but direct borrowing in any particular direction remains textually complex.

## 2. Aramaic

“Aramaic numerals” can mean several different things:

- old nonalphabetic cumulative signs;
- letters as ordinal labels;
- later alphabetic values;
- Syriac alphabetic numerals;
- Aramaic language written in Jewish Hebrew script.

The Jewish alphabet itself is historically derived from Imperial Aramaic script. Consequently “Hebrew letters” and “Aramaic letters” may refer to nearly identical written signs in late-antique Jewish documents.

**[R]** Syriac alphabetic numerals emerged later, probably through Greek and/or Hebrew models. Their order follows the Semitic alphabet, which favours Hebrew/Aramaic mediation, while Byzantine Christian context favours Greek influence. Chrisomalis treats both as plausible.

## 3. Arabic abjad

Arabic abjad numerals preserve the old Semitic alphabetic ordering in mnemonic strings such as *abjad hawwaz…*. They assign units, tens, hundreds, and thousands to Arabic letters.

They are structurally related to Hebrew and Greek alphabetic systems but are not the source of the late-second-century BCE Hebrew system. Their historical florescence is later. Conversely, modern “Arabic numerals” 0–9 are Indian-derived positional digits transmitted through Arabic mathematics, not abjad letters.

# Controversies and disputes

## 1. Who invented alphabetic numerals?

### Greek-priority position

**Evidence:**

- earlier secure Greek attestations;
- full Greek 1–900 structure;
- bilingual Khirbet el-Qom evidence;
- Jannaeus’s matched ΚΕ/כה;
- Hellenistic date of Hebrew adoption.

This is the prevailing reconstruction.

### Semitic-priority position

Older historians reasoned that because Greeks borrowed their alphabet from Phoenician, they might also have borrowed numerical values. The difficulty is chronological: early Phoenician letters may label sequences, but a full 1–9, 10–90, 100–900 system is not securely earlier than Greek.

### Egyptian structural model

Chrisomalis proposes that Greek alphabetic numeration adapted the ciphered decimal structure of Egyptian Demotic numerals. This does not mean alpha was copied from a Demotic “one” sign; the structural assignments, rather than the glyphs, are at issue.

**Conclusion:** Greek-to-Hebrew is the best-supported immediate transmission; deeper origin remains reconstructive.

## 2. Earliest Hebrew alphabetic numeral

Candidates must be ranked:

1. Simon-era first-decade letters, 141–136 BCE — **secure letters, uncertain full decimal status**.
2. Jonathan seal with מ as year 40, possibly 103 BCE — **disputed reading/function**.
3. Alexander Jannaeus כה, year 25, 79/78 BCE — **earliest secure decimal compound**.

Calling 78 BCE simply the “first use of Hebrew letters as numbers” erases the earlier letter-label evidence; calling Simon’s letters a complete gematria system overstates it.

## 3. Did biblical writers use gematria?

### For deliberate use

- some proposed matches are thematically elegant;
- ancient Near Eastern cultures practiced number symbolism;
- methods can exist before their first surviving name or artefact;
- Saul Lieberman warned that acquiring a Greek name does not prove that a practice itself was newly invented.

### Against secure attribution

- no explicit biblical mention;
- numbers are written lexically;
- secure alphabetic decimal notation is later than much of the Bible;
- orthographic variants alter totals;
- post hoc searches generate coincidences.

**Finding:** isolated deliberate numerical wordplay is possible; a comprehensive biblical gematria code is not demonstrated.

## 4. Meaning and etymology of גימטריא

### From Greek γεωμετρία, “geometry”

This is the conventional dictionary derivation. Rabbinic *gimṭariya* could have broadened from “measurement/calculation” to numerical reckoning.

### From γράμμα or γραμματεία

Older scholars proposed derivation or contamination from Greek terms for “letter,” “writing,” or “scribal knowledge,” which fit letter-calculation semantically.

### Assessment

**[D]** The word is certainly Greek or Greek-mediated in form and cultural setting, but its exact immediate model is disputed. The *Jewish Encyclopedia* sets out both derivations. Claims deriving it from “Khem-matria,” Egyptian “black-land mathematics,” are modern folk etymology unsupported by historical phonology. [Jewish Encyclopedia etymology](https://sefaria.jewishencyclopedia.com/articles/6571-gematria)

## 5. Did gematria “give rise” to the numeral system?

No. That reverses the evidentiary order.

- Alphabetic numeration gives words calculable values.
- Exegetes then exploit those values as gematria.

Numerical wordplay could conceivably have accompanied the system’s adoption, but it is not the demonstrated reason that administrative letter-numerals were created.

## 6. Are final letters ancient digits 500–900?

### Extended-gematria view

Later tables assign ךםןףץ to 500–900 and produce a symmetrical 27-sign array.

### Historical view

Ordinary early Hebrew has twenty-two letters, and large hundreds were compounded with ת. Final forms are contextual shapes, not five original alphabetic letters. Their separate high values are later.

**Conclusion:** authentic traditional method, but not an original feature securely attested on the Jannaeus coin or in Iron Age Hebrew.

## 7. “Hebrew numerals have no zero”

True for the classical additive system; false as a statement about Hebrew mathematical culture. Ibn Ezra explicitly uses a circular placeholder in a positional decimal system.

This parallels the problem with “Roman numerals have no zero”: a nonpositional notation does not need a placeholder, while its users may still represent nothing in calculation or language.

## 8. Are 15 and 16 “forbidden”?

The normal forms are ט״ו and ט״ז. Yet “forbidden” can exaggerate:

- The substitutions are a strong scribal and cultural convention.
- יה remains a legitimate divine name and occurs as such.
- יו occurs inside words and names.
- Context, manuscript date, and community matter.
- Writing the mechanically additive forms is not evidence that the writer rejects Judaism or that the numeral becomes magically sacred.

The convention is reverential avoidance, not a mathematical limitation.

## 9. Is every equal sum meaningful?

No historical source treats all equal totals as equally authoritative. Given a twenty-two-letter alphabet, many words inevitably collide.

A persuasive historical gematria requires:

- a documented method;
- a textually stable spelling;
- a plausible historical setting;
- thematic relevance;
- evidence of selection or signalling;
- preferably independent early commentary.

A calculator match alone documents only arithmetic.

## 10. *Sefer Yetzirah*: ancient patriarchal book?

**[TR]** Traditional attributions connect the work to Abraham, and some printed editions call it the teaching of Abraham our father.

**[R/D]** Linguistic, philosophical, and manuscript evidence places composition many centuries later. Even the wide scholarly range—late antique to early Islamic—excludes literal Abrahamic authorship as history.

The Abraham attribution remains important evidence for how the work was authorized and read.

## 11. 231 versus 221 gates

The mathematically elegant 231 equals all unordered pairs of twenty-two letters. The existence of 221 manuscripts, however, means editors cannot simply erase the variant. It may reflect an alternative combinatorial practice or a textual development not yet fully reconstructed.

## 12. Chronogram “firsts” and Firkovich

Firkovich’s purported early Crimean dates once appeared to extend chronogram practice deep into late antiquity. Because he altered or forged inscriptions and manuscript data, each associated date requires independent physical and palaeographic verification. The safer firsts are the 1205 literary example and fifteenth-century printed examples.

## 13. Ifrah: usefulness and known risks

Georges Ifrah’s *Universal History of Numbers* collected an immense range of material and popularized non-Western numeral histories. It is valuable as a map to examples, not a final authority.

Reviews by historians including Joseph Dauben and later comparisons by Chrisomalis criticize:

- insufficient source control;
- confident diffusion chains;
- errors of chronology;
- dependence on secondary illustrations;
- weak separation of speculation from documentation.

Where Ifrah’s claim about an early Hebrew seal is repeated here, it is labelled disputed and checked against later epigraphic discussion.

## 14. Modern “English gematria” and calculators

Systems assigning A=1, B=2, or Hebrew-style decades to English letters are **[M]** modern ciphers. Some descend from early-modern Christian Cabala or nineteenth-century occultism; many calculator variants are twenty-first-century constructions.

They may be used creatively, but “gematria” in their name does not establish ancient Jewish pedigree.

## 15. Statistical Torah codes

The equidistant-letter-sequence “Bible code” is not classical gematria. It is a modern computational method. Published statistical claims and rebuttals concern text selection, spelling, search flexibility, and multiple comparisons. Equal-value gematria and skip-letter codes should not be conflated.

## 16. The requested global-origin objects

For the record:

- Ishango and Lebombo bones: prehistoric tally interpretations, unrelated to Hebrew alphabetic numeration.
- Uruk tokens: Mesopotamian accounting, not a Hebrew letter-value source.
- Narmer macehead: Egyptian numerical imagery, not an ancestor demonstrated for gematria.
- Rhind Papyrus and Plimpton 322: Egyptian and Babylonian mathematics, useful only as remote comparative background.
- Brahmi, Bakhshali, and Gwalior: Indian positional-numeral history relevant to Ibn Ezra’s later adopted decimal arithmetic, not to the Hasmonean alphabetic cipher.
- Nine Chapters, counting rods, suanpan, soroban: East Asian traditions with no demonstrated role in Hebrew numeral formation.
- Maya zero and Dresden Codex: independent Mesoamerican development.
- Quipu: Andean accounting, independent.
- Roman counters, medieval abacists, Fibonacci, Sacrobosco, Pacioli, and Recorde: relevant to the broader European transition to Hindu-Arabic arithmetic, but not stages in the origin of א–ת numeration.

**Absence of evidence is the finding:** no credible source connects these artefacts directly to Hebrew gematria.

# Open questions

1. **Exact transition chronology:** When did Jewish scribes move from older hieratic/Aramaic signs to full alphabetic values outside coins?

2. **First-decade labels:** Were Simon-era letters understood only ordinally, or as the first part of an already complete decimal system?

3. **The year-40 seal:** Is מ numerical, and is its proposed chronological era correct?

4. **Early punctuation:** When did the modern differentiation between geresh for one letter and gershayim for multiple letters become standard?

5. **Fifteen and sixteen:** What are the earliest securely dated manuscript or inscriptional examples of טו and טז used specifically to avoid יה and יו?

6. **Final-letter values:** When and in which circles were ךםןףץ first systematically assigned 500–900?

7. **Biblical intentionality:** Can any proposed biblical gematria survive controls for spelling variants, textual chronology, and chance?

8. ***Sefer Yetzirah*’s date:** Does its intellectual vocabulary point primarily to Roman Palestine, late-antique Mesopotamia, or an early Islamic Christian-Syriac environment?

9. **The 221 reading:** What combinatorial operation, if any, originally produced 221 gates?

10. **Samaritan transmission:** Did Samaritan alphabetic numeration arise independently from shared order, through Jewish contact, or from a wider late-antique convention?

11. **Material calculation:** What direct archaeological evidence can identify counting boards or counters specifically used by Jewish administrators rather than by the surrounding population generally?

12. **Digital change:** Will Unicode-correct geresh/gershayim remain common, or will ASCII quotation marks and international digits further reduce the distinct visual system?
