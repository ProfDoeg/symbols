# Greek numerals (acrophonic and alphabetic): Research Dossier

## Basic identification

| Field | Attic acrophonic notation | Greek alphabetic notation |
|---|---|---|
| Principal names | Attic, acrophonic, formerly “Herodianic” numerals | Ionic, Ionian, Milesian, Alexandrian, alphabetic Greek numerals |
| Base | Decimal, structured by 5 and 10 | Decimal |
| Type | Cumulative-additive, with multiplicative/ligatured signs for five times a power of ten; non-positional | Ciphered-additive: separate signs for 1–9, 10–90, and 100–900; non-positional in its ordinary form |
| Basic signs | Ι 1, Π 5, Δ 10, 𐅄 50, Η 100, 𐅅 500, Χ 1,000, 𐅆 5,000, Μ 10,000, 𐅇 50,000 | α–θ 1–9; ι–ϟ 10–90; ρ–ϡ 100–900 |
| Special letters | None beyond locally varying acrophonic signs and monetary/metrological signs | ϛ or ϝ/Ͷ for 6; ϟ/ϙ for 90; ϡ/ͳ for 900 |
| Zero | No ordinary zero and no need for a placeholder | No ordinary zero in cardinal notation; Greek astronomers later used an empty-place sign in sexagesimal tables |
| Main period | Securely attested from the sixth/fifth centuries BCE; dominant in Attic public accounts through the Hellenistic period, with sporadic later survival | Probably created in Ionia in the sixth century BCE; widely dominant from the Hellenistic period through Byzantium; restricted use continues today |
| Region | Attica, with related but locally different systems across mainland Greece, the islands, and colonies | Origin normally placed in Greek-speaking western Asia Minor; subsequently the Greek Mediterranean, Ptolemaic and Roman Egypt, Byzantium, and modern Greece |
| Main functions | Money, weights, measures, inventories, tribute and public accounts; labels on counting boards | Accounts, dates, page/chapter numbering, mathematics, astronomy, manuscripts, coins, inscriptions, calendars, biblical numbering and isopsephy |
| Modern status | Antiquarian and epigraphic only | Still used for enumeration and ordinal-like labels, much as Roman numerals are used in western Europe |

**Evidence label — scholarly synthesis.** “Acrophonic” is the analytically preferable name because Π, Δ, Η, Χ, and Μ are initials of number words, whereas “Herodianic” rests on a late grammatical tradition. “Alphabetic,” “Ionic,” and “Milesian” refer to the second system’s structure or probable place of development. The typology here follows Stephen Chrisomalis, for whom the first system is cumulative-additive and the second ciphered-additive. The Oxford Classical Dictionary describes Greek acrophonic notation as part of a Greco-Italic 5–10 family and regards the alphabetic notation as structurally unrelated to it and probably inspired by Egyptian Demotic practice. [Chrisomalis, “Numerical notation,” Oxford Classical Dictionary](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/61673/chapter-abstract/548065162)

## The system in detail

## 1. Attic acrophonic numerals

### 1.1 The principle

The ordinary Attic inventory is:

| Value | Sign | Description and derivation |
|---:|:---:|---|
| 1 | Ι | A single vertical stroke; not acrophonic in the strict sense |
| 5 | Π or 𐅃 | Initial of **πέντε**, “five” |
| 10 | Δ | Initial of **δέκα**, “ten” |
| 50 | 𐅄 | Composite of Π and Δ: “five tens” |
| 100 | Η | Initial of older Attic **ΗΕΚΑΤΟΝ**, later ἑκατόν, “hundred” |
| 500 | 𐅅 | Composite of Π and Η: “five hundreds” |
| 1,000 | Χ | Initial of **χίλιοι**, “thousand” |
| 5,000 | 𐅆 | Composite of Π and Χ |
| 10,000 | Μ | Initial of **μύριοι**, “ten thousand” |
| 50,000 | 𐅇 | Composite of Π and Μ |

Unicode encodes the principal composite Attic signs as:

- 𐅃 U+10143, ATTIC FIVE
- 𐅄 U+10144, ATTIC FIFTY
- 𐅅 U+10145, ATTIC FIVE HUNDRED
- 𐅆 U+10146, ATTIC FIVE THOUSAND
- 𐅇 U+10147, ATTIC FIFTY THOUSAND

The plain signs Ι, Π, Δ, Η, Χ, and Μ are represented by ordinary Greek capitals. Unicode deliberately encodes separate characters only where an acrophonic sign cannot adequately be treated as an ordinary Greek letter or glyph variant. [Unicode 17, §7.2.3](https://unicode.org/versions/Unicode17.0.0/core-spec/chapter-7/); [Unicode Ancient Greek Numbers names list](https://www.unicode.org/charts/nameslist/n_10140.html)

**Evidence label — documented signs plus linguistic reconstruction.** The inscriptions establish the shapes and values. Their derivation from number-word initials is exceptionally strong because the correspondence is systematic and agrees with the archaic Attic use of Η for /h/. It is nevertheless an etymological reconstruction, not an ancient inventor’s explanation.

### 1.2 Additive and multiplicative features

Numbers were normally made by repeating signs and adding their values. Four repetitions were possible before the relevant five-sign was used:

- ΙΙΙΙ = 4
- ΠΙΙΙΙ = 9
- ΔΔΔΔ = 40
- 𐅄ΔΔΔΔ = 90

The composite signs 𐅄, 𐅅, 𐅆, and 𐅇 embody multiplication by five graphically. A traditional drawing describes 𐅄 as a Π-like frame containing Δ; 𐅅 similarly contains Η, and so forth. Their shapes vary considerably between local scripts and individual inscriptions.

There is no place value. Ι after Δ does not occupy a “ones column” in the modern positional sense: it is simply another sign whose value is added. Because addition is commutative, sign order did not change value, although descending order was conventional and easier to read.

**Evidence label — documented artefacts.** The system is found in public accounts and tribute lists. The statement that its structure is “mixed” is a modern typological description, not an ancient label.

### 1.5 Monetary and fractional signs

Attic accounts did not record pure abstract numbers alone. Signs could identify talents, drachmas, staters, obols, and subdivisions, and compound forms joined a numerical multiplier to a unit sign. Unicode includes, among others:

- 𐅀 U+10140: Attic one quarter
- 𐅁 U+10141: Attic one half
- 𐅂 U+10142: Attic one drachma
- 𐅈 U+10148: five talents
- 𐅉 U+10149: ten talents
- 𐅊 U+1014A: fifty talents
- 𐅋 U+1014B: one hundred talents
- 𐅌 U+1014C: five hundred talents
- 𐅍 U+1014D: one thousand talents
- 𐅎 U+1014E: five thousand talents

The wider Unicode block also contains local signs from Delphi, Epidaurus, Hermione, Thespiae, Troezen, Cyrene, Messene, Naxos, and other regions. It should not be mistaken for one uniform pan-Hellenic font. [Unicode/TLG encoding proposal](https://unicode.org/L2/L2003/03075r2-tlg-numeric.pdf)

**Correction of a common simplification.** It is fair to say that the ordinary numeral sequence had no general fraction syntax comparable to modern `a/b`. It is false to infer that fractions could not be recorded: monetary and metrological systems supplied unit and subdivision signs, including halves and quarters.

### 1.6 Range

With the standard signs repeated no more than four times, the ordinary repertoire reaches 99,999:

`𐅇ΜΜΜΜ𐅆ΧΧΧΧ𐅅ΗΗΗΗ𐅄ΔΔΔΔΠΙΙΙΙ`

= 50,000 + 40,000 + 5,000 + 4,000 + 500 + 400 + 50 + 40 + 5 + 4.

There is no mathematical upper bound if further signs, unit compounds, or repetitions are admitted, but the canonical Attic inventory was optimized for actual fiscal quantities rather than arbitrary enormous integers.

## 2. The alphabetic or Milesian system

### 2.1 Complete sign inventory

The alphabet is divided into three enneads, groups of nine.

#### Units

| Value | Capital | Lowercase | Name |
|---:|:---:|:---:|---|

| 2 | Β | β | beta |
| 3 | Γ | γ | gamma |
| 4 | Δ | δ | delta |
| 5 | Ε | ε | epsilon |
| 6 | Ϛ, Ϝ, or ΣΤ | ϛ, ϝ, στ | stigma/digamma; modern substitute *st* |
| 7 | Ζ | ζ | zeta |

| 9 | Θ | θ | theta |

#### Hundreds

| Value | Capital | Lowercase | Name |
|---:|:---:|:---:|---|
| 100 | Ρ | ρ | rho |
| 200 | Σ | σ | sigma |
| 300 | Τ | τ | tau |
| 400 | Υ | υ | upsilon |
| 500 | Φ | φ | phi |

| 700 | Ψ | ψ | psi |
| 800 | Ω | ω | omega |
| 900 | Ϡ or Ͳ | ϡ or ͳ | sampi |

The three additional signs are necessary because 24 ordinary Ionic letters cannot fill three groups of nine:

- **6:** archaic digamma ϝ/Ϝ, later a cursive numeral form called stigma ϛ/Ϛ; modern printing often writes στʹ.
- **90:** koppa ϙ/Ϙ, later numeral koppa ϟ/Ϟ.
- **900:** sampi ͳ/Ͳ or ϡ/Ϡ.

**Evidence label — documented system; terminology partly retrospective.** The numeral functions are secure. The historical relationship between alphabetic sampi and the earlier phonetic letter conventionally called sampi is palaeographically complicated. Its numeric value is secure even where the exact genealogy of the glyph is disputed.

### 2.2 Numeral marks

Because letters and numerals use the same shapes, scribes developed disambiguating marks:

- an overline in manuscripts: `α̅`, `ιβ̅`, `χξϛ̅`;
- a right-hand numeral stroke, **dexia keraia**: `αʹ`, `ιβʹ`, `χξϛʹ`;
- a lower-left numeral sign for thousands: `͵α`, `͵β`, etc.

Unicode distinguishes:

- U+0374 `ʹ`, GREEK NUMERAL SIGN;
- U+0375 `͵`, GREEK LOWER NUMERAL SIGN.

The modern keraia is not merely an acute accent, even when fonts make the two look alike. [Unicode, Greek numeral-sign discussion](https://unicode.org/versions/Unicode16.0.0/core-spec/chapter-22/)

Early inscriptions do not consistently mark numerals. Context, spacing, strokes, or flanking marks can distinguish them. An apparent “word” may therefore be a number and vice versa.

### 2.3 Ordering and composition

A conventional numeral is written from the highest value to the lowest:

`ψκδʹ` = 700 + 20 + 4 = 724.

The order was not absolutely invariant. Attic examples exist in which units precede tens. Consequently, “always descending” is a pedagogical normalization, not an exceptionless ancient rule.

Repeated signs are normally unnecessary inside 1–999 because every multiple of 1, 10, and 100 has its own cipher. Thus 30 is λʹ, not ιιιʹ.

The thousands convention effectively reuses the 1–9 signs with a multiplier of 1,000. Myriads could be written with Μ and a multiplier placed above, beside, or in relation to it. Manuscript practice is not typographically uniform.

### 2.6 Fractions

Greek mathematical texts use several overlapping practices.

1. **Unit fractions.** A numeral with a special stroke or accent-like mark could mean its reciprocal:

   - γ′ = 1/3
   - δ′ = 1/4
   - ζ′ = 1/7

   The appearance is dangerously similar to the cardinal keraia in modern transcription; context is essential.

2. **Special half sign.** A half was often represented by a special sign conventionally transcribed `∠ʹ` or by a form related to the word ἥμισυ, rather than simply β′.

3. **General fractions.** Numerator and denominator might be written in sequence, distinguished by reversed accents, doubled strokes, placement, or explanatory words such as μέρος or ἐν μορίῳ. There was no single universal slash convention.

4. **Egyptian-style sums of unit fractions.** Earlier Greek mathematical practice sometimes expressed a fraction as a sum of distinct unit fractions. This reflects a genuine relation to Egyptian fractional technique, but individual Greek procedures must be established text by text.

5. **Diophantus.** The manuscripts of the *Arithmetica* employ abbreviated verbal notation and several fractional constructions. Modern expressions such as `47/90` may correspond to language meaning “47 in a ninetieth part,” not to a modern fraction bar. [Diophantus, *Arithmetica* I, Greek text and notes based on Tannery](https://web.calstatela.edu/faculty/hmendel/Ancient%20Mathematics/Diophantus/Arithmetica/Diophantus.I.1-6.html)

### 2.7 Zero and empty places

The ordinary alphabetic system has no zero sign because it is not positional. A missing power simply is not written:

- 101 = ραʹ, not “1-0-1”;
- 700 = ψʹ, not “7-0-0.”

That statement must be separated from Greek astronomical notation.

**Documented text/manuscript tradition.** Hellenistic and Roman astronomers represented sexagesimal places with alphabetic numerals from 1 to 59. In the tradition represented by Ptolemy’s *Almagest*, an empty sexagesimal place could be marked by a small circular sign, often with an overbar. Unicode encodes the papyrological zero sign as 𐆊, U+1018A.

**Important qualification.** This was a positional placeholder inside astronomical tables and could also denote a genuinely zero tabular entry. It did not become a freely operating number with general arithmetic rules comparable to Brahmagupta’s zero. The later sign sometimes resembles ο, but “Ptolemy simply used omicron because οὐδέν means nothing” is an attractive conjecture, not a documented ancient explanation. Omicron already meant 70 in ordinary alphabetic notation, though that value cannot occur inside a single sexagesimal digit. [Unicode Ancient Greek Numbers](https://www.unicode.org/charts/nameslist/n_10140.html)

### 2.8 Largest expressible numbers

Ordinary alphabetic notation directly and compactly expresses 1–999. The thousands mark extends the same inventory through 9,999; myriad notation permits at least 99,999,999 by treating 10,000 as a named grouping unit.

This is a conventional limit, not a limit on Greek thought.

In the *Sand Reckoner*, Archimedes, writing in the third century BCE, says that some people regard the named numbers ending at “a myriad of myriads” (10^8). He then constructs:

- **orders** based on powers of 10^8;
- **periods** made from vast sequences of orders;
- ultimately a naming scheme reaching approximately \(10^{8\cdot10^{16}}\).

**Documented text.** This is an explicit mathematical extension, not an inference from fragmentary notation. Its purpose was to show that even the imagined number of grains of sand filling the cosmos is finite and nameable. [Archimedes, *Sand Reckoner*, Greek and aligned translations](https://reader.humanitext.ai/en/library/tlg0552.tlg006.humanitext-grc1/); [Heath translation](https://sacred-texts.com/cla/archim/sand/sandreck.htm)

## 3. Worked famous numbers

### 3.1 300

Alphabetic:

`τʹ` = 300.

Acrophonic:

`ΗΗΗ` = 100 + 100 + 100.

The association of “300” with the Spartans at Thermopylae is literary and modern-cultural; it does not make τʹ or ΗΗΗ a special sacred numeral.

### 3.2 666, the number of the Beast

`χξϛʹ` = 600 + 60 + 6 = 666.

Revelation 13:18 instructs the reader to “calculate” the Beast’s number. Most manuscripts give 666, either in words or numeral letters.

A significant ancient variant is:

`χιϛʹ` = 600 + 10 + 6 = 616.

**Documented manuscript evidence.** Papyrus 115, P.Oxy. LXVI 4499, generally dated palaeographically to the late third or early fourth century CE, supports 616 in the surviving passage; Codex Ephraemi Rescriptus also supports 616. Irenaeus, writing around 180 CE, knew the 616 variant but preferred 666 on the authority of the copies he considered accurate.

**Scholarly reconstruction.** “Neron Caesar” can produce 666 in Hebrew letters, while the Latinized “Nero Caesar” produces 616. This elegantly explains both readings and is widely influential, but the Apocalypse does not explicitly identify Nero, and alternative ancient and modern solutions exist.

### 3.3 888, Jesus

`ωπηʹ` = 800 + 80 + 8 = 888.

The name ΙΗΣΟΥΣ gives:

Ι 10 + Η 8 + Σ 200 + Ο 70 + Υ 400 + Σ 200 = 888.

**Documented late-second-century text.** Irenaeus reports this calculation while criticizing Valentinian numerical speculation. Thus “Jesus = 888” is genuinely ancient, although Irenaeus is a hostile witness to the theology attached to it. [Irenaeus, *Against Heresies*](https://openscriptorium.com/books/irenaeus-plain)

### 3.4 “I love the woman whose number is 731”

`ψλαʹ` = 700 + 30 + 1 = 731.

**Documented graffito.** A Greek graffito from Smyrna reads in translation, “I love a woman whose number is 731.” The number conceals a name whose letters add to that total. The name cannot be recovered uniquely because many letter combinations have the same sum. [Ancient Graffiti Project, AGP-SMYT00242](https://ancientgraffiti.org/Graffiti/graffito/AGP-SMYT00242)

### 3.5 Archimedes’ approximation of π

The familiar Archimedean bounds are:

\[
3+\frac{10}{71}<\pi<3+\frac17.
\]

A normalized alphabetic rendering of 22/7 is `κβʹ / ζʹ`, but the modern slash and the compression “π = 22/7” are not Archimedes’ own notation and slightly misstate his result: 22/7 is an upper bound.

## Origins

## 4. Before the two classical systems

### 4.1 Bronze Age Aegean numerals

**Documented artefacts.** Linear A and Linear B tablets used separate numeral and metrological signs centuries before the Greek alphabet. Linear B is attested in Mycenaean administrative archives of the second millennium BCE, including Knossos, Pylos, Thebes, and Mycenae. Its numerals are decimal and additive, with distinct signs for powers of ten.

**Scholarly conclusion.** These systems demonstrate a long Aegean tradition of written accounting but are not the immediate graphic ancestors of Attic Π–Δ–Η notation or of the 27-sign Milesian system. The collapse of the palace administrations was followed by a substantial discontinuity in writing.

### 4.2 Tally ancestry

Chrisomalis places Greek acrophonic, Etruscan, and Roman notation in a broader Greco-Italic family ultimately grounded in tallying and organized around 5 and 10.

**Scholarly reconstruction.** The stroke for one and the grouping at five strongly support a tally background. Direct transmission from a particular surviving bone, tablet, or eastern numeral inscription has not been demonstrated.

### 4.3 Explicit negative findings

The Ishango and Lebombo bones, Uruk tokens, Narmer macehead, Bakhshali manuscript, Gwalior zero, Brāhmī inscriptions, Chinese rods, quipu, and Dresden Codex belong to other numeral histories. No credible primary or specialist source makes any of them a direct ancestor or transmission stage of Greek acrophonic or alphabetic notation. Their omission from the Greek genealogy is therefore evidentiary, not accidental.

Likewise:

- Greek numerals did not travel India → Baghdad → al-Andalus → Italy as the Hindu-Arabic digits did.
- The 1299 Florentine regulation is not an episode in the transmission of Greek numerals.
- Suanpan, soroban, quipu, and Maya Long Count mechanisms are comparative analogues, not Greek instruments.
- Claims connecting Greek letter shapes to digit-counting angles concern modern Hindu-Arabic numeral folklore and are irrelevant to the documented genesis of αʹ, βʹ, γʹ or Ι, Π, Δ.

## 5. The acrophonic system

### 5.1 Date and earliest evidence

The precise first example remains difficult because short tally-like strokes are often ambiguous. Current syntheses place recognizable Greek acrophonic systems in circulation by the middle of the sixth century BCE and securely by its final decades.

**Scholarly reconstruction.** Dating is based on letter forms, archaeological context, and integration into monetary and metrological texts, rather than an inscription announcing the invention.

**Documented fifth-century corpus.** The Athenian tribute lists are the most imposing surviving series. When the Delian League treasury was moved to Athens in 454/3 BCE, annual lists recorded the sixtieth portion, the *aparchē*, dedicated to Athena. The series IG I³ 259 onward runs, with losses, into the late fifth century. The first list, IG I³ 259, includes fragments found on and near the Acropolis and held as EM 6870. [Attic Inscriptions Online, IG I³ 259](https://www.atticinscriptions.com/inscription/IGI3/259)

A major assembled tribute-list monument is catalogued by the Epigraphical Museum under EM 6647 + 13453 + 13454. The values beside community names are written with acrophonic monetary notation.

**Evidence label — documented artefact.** The lists prove mature official use by 454/3 BCE. They do not prove invention at Athens or in 454.

### 5.2 Initial letters and the Attic alphabet

The sign Η for 100 is especially informative. Before Athens adopted the Ionic alphabet officially in 403/2 BCE, Attic Η represented /h/, so Η was the first letter of ΗΕΚΑΤΟΝ. After Ionic spelling made Η the vowel eta, the old numeral remained fossilized.

**Scholarly inference.** This favors creation or stabilization of the sign before the alphabetic reform. It does not date the entire system to a single year.

### 5.3 Local Greek systems

“Attic numerals” are one regional member of a broader family. Other Greek cities used signs formed from their own alphabets and number or unit words. Their symbols for 10, 100, 1,000, monetary units, and fractions could differ.

**Correction.** It is misleading to treat every acrophonic sign in Unicode as an Attic numeral. Unicode’s U+10140–U+1018F block deliberately includes non-Attic forms.

### 5.4 Solon and Herodian

A grammatical text transmitted under the name of Aelius Herodianus says that such signs could be seen in laws of Solon.

- **Tradition:** the signs belonged to Solonian legislation.
- **Problem:** Herodian lived roughly eight centuries after Solon; the attribution and exact authorship of the grammatical material are uncertain.
- **Evidence:** no autograph law of Solon survives.
- **Conclusion:** this is ancient testimony to perceived antiquity, not proof of sixth-century Solonian invention.

The name “Herodianic numerals” spread in modern literature because the transmitted grammatical passage was once treated as their first description. Marcus N. Tod preferred “acrophonic” in his systematic epigraphic studies.

## 6. The alphabetic system

### 6.1 Why “Ionic” or “Milesian”?

The fully developed system presupposes an ordered series of 27 signs. Its association with Ionia and Miletus rests on the geographic distribution and scripts of early evidence, followed by its later spread.

Older histories sometimes dated it to the eighth century BCE or imagined that Greeks received letter-values with the Phoenician alphabet.

**Current evidentiary position.** The securely datable evidence does not support an eighth-century origin. Moreover, the earliest West Semitic alphabetic numeral systems presently known are later than Greek alphabetic notation. A sixth-century BCE origin is therefore the mainstream reconstruction.

### 6.2 Earliest attestations

The earliest alleged cases are small inscriptions, graffiti, vase marks, coins, and abecedaria whose interpretation is sometimes uncertain: a letter may be a label, sequence mark, abbreviation, or true cardinal numeral.

Current specialist syntheses state that apparent Ionic alphabetic numerals occur in early-sixth-century contexts and that the system was circulating by the mid-sixth century. In Attica it appears in private inscriptions before it becomes the dominant public notation.

**Absence-of-evidence finding.** No surviving “invention tablet” gives the system as a complete dated table. The date is reconstructed from dispersed objects. The late attestation of ϡ = 900 is especially unsurprising because 900 occurs much less often than small numbers.

### 6.3 Egyptian Demotic hypothesis

Stephen Chrisomalis argued in 2003 that the organizing principle came from Egyptian Demotic numerals:

- Demotic used a ciphered-additive decimal arrangement with signs for 1–9, tens, and hundreds.
- Greek alphabetic notation has the same unusual structural profile.
- Ionian merchants were active at Naucratis and elsewhere in the Nile Delta in the early sixth century BCE.
- Greeks could adopt the structure while replacing Demotic signs with their own alphabet.

[Chrisomalis, “The Egyptian origin of the Greek alphabetic numerals,” *Antiquity* 77](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/abs/egyptian-origin-of-the-greek-alphabetic-numerals/B21F16056A89CCDF000EE98CAC4E43AE)

**Evidence label — scholarly reconstruction, influential but not directly documented.** The structural parallel and historical contact are real. No bilingual manual or intermediate object explicitly records the borrowing. “Probably deriving from Egyptian Demotic practices” is a reasoned historical inference, not an established event witnessed by a text.

### 6.4 Phoenician-origin theory

**Older scholarly theory.** Since Greeks borrowed alphabetic writing from Phoenician, some scholars assumed that numerical letter-values arrived with it.

**Counter-evidence.**

- Early Phoenician and related West Semitic numerical notations were not organized as the later full alphabetic value system.
- Secure Semitic alphabetic numeral systems appear later than the Greek one.
- A Greek–Aramaic bilingual ostracon of 277 BCE reportedly uses alphabetic numerals in Greek while the Aramaic side retains non-alphabetic number signs.

The modern consensus therefore separates the Phoenician origin of Greek alphabetic writing from the later origin of Greek alphabetic numeration.

### 6.5 Relationship to the acrophonic system

The alphabetic system did not evolve by simply adding letters to Ι Π Δ Η Χ Μ. The principles differ:

- acrophonic signs abbreviate number words and repeat;
- alphabetic signs receive values from their position in a 27-sign sequence and normally do not repeat.

Both could coexist for centuries. In Attica, acrophonic notation remained normal in public documents long after alphabetic numerals were known privately. Outside Attica, alphabetic numerals largely displaced local acrophonic systems during the Hellenistic period. The Oxford Classical Dictionary dates continuing official Attic acrophonic use to about 100 BCE, with sporadic later examples. [OCD, “numbers, Greek”](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/61673/chapter-abstract/548061657)

## Arithmetic and instruments

## 7. Written numerals were usually records, not algorithms

A numeral notation and a calculating technique are not the same thing. Neither ΙΙΙΙ𐅄 nor ψκδʹ tells the user how to carry tens in the way a written positional algorithm does.

Chrisomalis emphasizes that ancient notations were often representational and mnemonic, while calculation occurred mentally, verbally, with tables, or on boards. This explains how sophisticated Greek mathematics flourished without written decimal place-value algorithms.

## 8. Finger reckoning

**Documented literary and iconographic tradition, incompletely reconstructable.** Greeks and Romans counted and signalled numbers with finger positions. Later Latin and Byzantine texts preserve elaborate conventions, but it is unsafe to project every medieval hand sign unchanged into Classical Athens.

Fingers were useful for:

- counting small quantities;
- silently signalling numbers;
- retaining partial results;
- coordinating transactions.

The English word **digit**, from Latin *digitus*, “finger,” reflects the general association between fingers and number symbols, but it does not descend specifically from Greek alphabetic numerals.

## 9. Pebbles and counting boards

Greek **ψῆφος**, *psēphos*, means “pebble,” including a voting or reckoning pebble. Related words connect counting, voting, and numerical calculation. **Ἰσοψηφία**, isopsephy, literally evokes “equal pebble-count”: two expressions have equal numerical totals.

The Greek **ἄβαξ**, *abax*, is the ancestor, through Latin, of English **abacus**. Its deeper etymology is uncertain. A derivation from a Northwest Semitic word for dust has often been proposed, but modern etymologists regard the semantic case as weak rather than proven. [Online Etymology Dictionary, “abacus”](https://www.etymonline.com/word/abacus)

### 9.1 The Salamis board

**Documented artefact.**

- Material: white marble.
- Find: Salamis, 1846.
- Date: commonly around 300 BCE, though exact dating varies.
- Present location: Epigraphical Museum, Athens.
- Inventory: EM 11515.
- Epigraphic publication: IG II² 2777.
- Features: parallel ruled lines, a dividing line, and three sets of acrophonic monetary signs along its margins.

[Computer History Museum record citing EM 11515](https://www.computerhistory.org/revolution/artifact/1/128)

**Scholarly reconstruction.** Counters were probably placed on the ruled surface so that successive lines or spaces represented units, fives, tens, and higher values. Its marginal sequences name monetary values in descending order.

**Earlier interpretation.** The slab was once described as a gaming board.

**Disputed/modern reconstructions.** Detailed procedures for multiplication, division, negative numbers, or floating-point-style mantissas and exponents are modern proposals. The artefact has no surviving counters and no ancient instruction manual; its function as a reckoning board is very plausible, but a particular modern simulation is not itself ancient evidence.

### 9.2 How carrying could work

A plausible decimal/five-based board procedure is:

1. Place pebbles on a line representing units.
2. Exchange five unit pebbles for one pebble in a five-unit position.
3. Exchange two fives for one ten.
4. Repeat at higher powers.
5. Read the result using the marginal acrophonic labels.

This is a reconstruction from the board’s layout and comparative counting-board practice. It is not a verbatim ancient algorithm.

## 10. Tables and school exercises

Surviving Greek papyri and wooden tablets contain addition, doubling, multiplication, division, and fraction tables.

- A Cairo mathematical papyrus has a doubling series 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64, 128, 256, accounts, and geometrical drawings. [Aish, “A Greek Mathematical Papyrus from the Cairo Museum”](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1515/apf-2016-0004/html)
- A wooden tablet acquired by Otto Rubensohn in 1904 and held in Berlin contains six columns of divisions on one side and multiplication and addition on the other. [Berlin Papyrus Database, “Mathematische Merktafel”](https://berlpap.smb.museum/mathematische-merktafel/)
- The Louvre catalogue records a Greek mathematical tablet containing a multiplication table. [Louvre collections record](https://collections.louvre.fr/en/ark%3A/53355/cl010002616)
- Jacques Sesiano catalogued and analyzed the corpus of Greek multiplication tables. [Sesiano, “Greek Multiplication Tables,” *SCIAMVS* 21](https://www.sciamvs.org/files/SCIAMVS_21_083-140_Sesiano.pdf)

**Documented pedagogical practice.** These objects show that students memorized and copied tables and that alphabetic numerals could support routine arithmetic. They caution against the claim that Greeks “could not calculate” with their numerals.

## 11. Euclid, Nicomachus, and number language

Euclid’s *Elements* generally proves propositions about numbers using verbal and geometric reasoning, not columns of written arithmetic. Books VII–IX treat units, ratios, primes, greatest common divisors, and figurate or perfect-number results.

Nicomachus of Gerasa, around the first or second century CE, systematized arithmetic classifications in the *Introduction to Arithmetic*. Numbers could be represented by letter-numerals, but the mathematical arguments were still largely rhetorical.

**Evidence label — texts.** These works document mathematical concepts and proof styles. They are not elementary account books explaining pebble placement.

## 12. Diophantus

Diophantus of Alexandria probably worked in the third century CE, though his dates remain uncertain. The *Arithmetica* survives partly in Greek and partly in Arabic transmission.

His notation includes abbreviations for:

- the unknown number, ἀριθμός;
- its square, δύναμις;
- cube, κύβος;
- higher powers and reciprocals;
- subtraction or deficiency;
- numerical coefficients written alphabetically.

**Documented manuscript tradition.** This is syncopated algebra: neither wholly rhetorical nor fully symbolic in the modern sense.

**Caution.** Printed symbols differ among Tannery, Heath, Ver Eecke, modern Unicode approximations, and the manuscripts. Statements that “Diophantus invented algebraic symbolism” compress a gradual scribal history and may attribute later standardized glyphs to the author himself.

## 13. Ptolemy and sexagesimal computation

Claudius Ptolemy, second century CE, used sexagesimal tables for astronomy and trigonometry in the *Mathematical Syntaxis*, later called the *Almagest*.

Each sexagesimal digit from 1 to 59 was written as a Greek alphabetic numeral. Positional order distinguished degrees, minutes, seconds, or successive fractional places; an empty place could receive the circular zero sign.

Example in normalized form:

- λα = 31 within one sexagesimal place;
- κ = 20 in another;
- an intervening 𐆊 denotes an empty place.

**Transmission.** The technique ultimately belongs to the Babylonian sexagesimal astronomical inheritance, mediated and transformed by Greek astronomers including Hipparchus. The Greek letter shapes themselves are not Babylonian borrowings.

## 14. Archimedes

Archimedes of Syracuse, third century BCE, manipulated extremely large number concepts without positional written digits. The *Sand Reckoner* deliberately expands Greek number naming. Other works calculate areas, volumes, ratios, and approximations through geometric reasoning.

The work demonstrates a crucial distinction:

- ordinary numeral signs had practical limits;
- the language of recursively defined units could express numbers far beyond them.

## Transmission and replacement

## 15. Classical coexistence

From the fifth into the third century BCE:

- Attic public finance favored acrophonic notation.
- Private inscriptions and scholarly contexts increasingly used alphabetic numerals.
- Other regions maintained local acrophonic varieties.
- Alphabetic notation spread with Ionic writing and Hellenistic administration.

This was not an overnight reform.

## 16. Hellenistic expansion

The conquests of Alexander and the successor kingdoms spread Greek language and administration from Egypt to western Asia. The alphabetic system’s compactness made it suitable for papyri, accounts, dates, tables, and manuscript organization.

In Ptolemaic Egypt it coexisted with:

- Egyptian Demotic numerals;
- hieratic traditions;
- later Roman administrative practices;
- eventually Coptic notation.

## 17. Descendant and neighboring alphabetic systems

Greek alphabetic notation became a powerful model for assigning numerical values to letters.

### 17.1 Hebrew

Hebrew letter-numerals appear securely later than Greek alphabetic notation, around the late Hellenistic period. Greek influence is probable at the structural level, although Jewish alphabetic and exegetical traditions developed distinctive conventions.

### 17.2 Coptic

Coptic writing adapted Greek letters and added Demotic-derived characters. Coptic numerical practice drew on both Greek alphabetic and Egyptian traditions and remained in use for many centuries. [Cambridge Genizah Research Unit, “‘Coptic’ numerals and Genizah studies”](https://genizahfragments.lib.cam.ac.uk/2021/06/02/coptic-numerals-and-genizah-studies/)

### 17.3 Armenian and Georgian

Christian Armenia and Georgia developed alphabetic numeral systems in late antiquity, structurally comparable to Greek but using their own scripts.

### 17.4 Gothic, Glagolitic, and Cyrillic

Greek ecclesiastical culture helped transmit alphabetic numeral principles to Gothic and later Slavic scripts. Cyrillic numerals follow Greek values closely, including special treatment of thousands and overlining.

### 17.5 Syriac and Arabic abjad

Syriac and Arabic letter-value systems share the broad alphabetic-numeral principle. Their exact development cannot be reduced to a simple one-step copying event. Arabic **abjad** order preserves an older Semitic letter sequence and differs from the ordinary modern alphabetic order.

**Correction.** These abjad numerals are not the “Arabic numerals” 0–9. The latter are of Indian positional ancestry.

## 18. Roman and Byzantine periods

Greek alphabetic numerals persisted under Roman rule, especially in Greek-speaking provinces. Roman numerals did not universally replace them.

Byzantine manuscripts used them for:

- dates;
- regnal and indiction years;
- biblical chapter numbers;
- canon tables;
- page and quire numbering;
- astronomical and calendrical tables;
- lexicographical and scholastic enumeration.

The Byzantine era also produced extensive manuscript variation in overlines, ligatures, numeral koppa, stigma, and sampi.

## 19. Encounter with Hindu-Arabic numerals

Indian positional numerals reached the Islamic scholarly world and then Latin Europe by routes involving Baghdad, al-Andalus, North Africa, and Italy. Greek-speaking scholars eventually encountered the same system through Arabic and Latin science and later print culture.

This was a replacement pressure, not the origin of Greek numerals.

Alphabetic numerals nevertheless persisted because they served functions beyond arithmetic:

- compact ordinals;
- traditional dates;
- liturgical numbering;
- manuscript divisions;
- culturally Greek alternatives to Latin Roman numerals.

## 20. Printing and modern Greek

Greek typography regularized the keraia and lower thousands sign. Modern Greece normally uses Hindu-Arabic digits for cardinal quantities, science, commerce, years, and measurements. Alphabetic numerals survive in:

- chapter and section enumeration;
- list items;
- school classes or groups;
- monarchs, patriarchs, and similarly numbered officeholders;
- historical dates and editions;
- legal subdivisions;
- ecclesiastical and biblical references.

Examples:

- αʹ, βʹ, γʹ = first, second, third item;
- στʹ = sixth;
- ͵βιεʹ = 2015.

The replacement of stigma by `στ` is a typographic accommodation: modern users recognize sigma-tau as the historical spelling source of the ligature, while many fonts or keyboards lack a convenient ϛ.

## People

## 21. Ancient administrators and craftsmen

The earliest users are mostly anonymous:

- stonecutters who engraved tribute and treasury accounts;
- *hellenotamiai* and other financial officials;
- sanctuary treasurers;
- merchants and money changers;
- scribes preparing accounts;
- teachers and pupils copying tables.

Their work is better documented materially than biographically.

## 22. Solon

Solon, traditionally active as Athenian lawgiver in the early sixth century BCE, was associated by the Herodianic grammatical tradition with acrophonic signs in his laws.

**Tradition, not secure artefact.** The claim demonstrates later belief in the notation’s antiquity. It does not establish that Solon invented it or that a Herodianic observer saw an original Solonian inscription.

## 23. Herodian

Aelius Herodianus, second century CE, was a major grammarian. A transmitted text under his name describes the older signs, giving rise to “Herodianic numerals.”

**Textual caution.** The attribution and transmission of the relevant grammatical material are less secure than the conventional label suggests.

## 24. Archimedes

Archimedes, ca. 287–212 BCE, constructed the extended number system in the *Sand Reckoner*. His aim was conceptual rather than bureaucratic: to defeat the assertion that the grains of sand were literally innumerable.

## 25. Hipparchus

Hipparchus, second century BCE, is a central figure in the adoption and development of Babylonian-derived sexagesimal astronomy in Greek. Much of his work is lost and reconstructed through Ptolemy and later sources.

**Scholarly reconstruction.** Specific notational practices cannot always be assigned securely to Hipparchus rather than later copyists or Ptolemy.

## 26. Ptolemy

Claudius Ptolemy, ca. 100–170 CE, made extensive use of alphabetic-sexagesimal tables. His works became a principal route by which Greek astronomical numeration entered Arabic and Latin science.

## 27. Diophantus

Diophantus, probably third century CE, combined alphabetic numerical coefficients with abbreviated algebraic terminology. His works passed into Arabic and, in the Renaissance, Greek and Latin editions.

The famous epitaph giving his age through fractional stages is preserved in the later *Greek Anthology*. Solving it yields 84. It is traditional biographical evidence, not a contemporary inscription.

## 28. Irenaeus

Irenaeus of Lyons, ca. 130–ca. 202 CE, preserves:

- the calculation Jesus = 888;
- polemics against Gnostic letter-and-number interpretation;
- testimony that both 666 and 616 circulated for Revelation 13:18.

He is invaluable but partisan: his purpose was refutation, not neutral documentation.

## 29. Marcus N. Tod

Marcus Niebuhr Tod, 1878–1974, pioneered systematic modern study of Greek numeral inscriptions:

- “The Greek Numeral Notation,” *Annual of the British School at Athens* 18 (1911–1912), 98–132;
- “Further Notes on the Greek Acrophonic Numerals,” BSA 28;
- “The Greek Acrophonic Numerals,” BSA 37;
- “The Alphabetic Numeral System in Attica,” BSA 45.

Tod assembled geographically dispersed epigraphic evidence and helped displace casual, Athens-only accounts.

## 30. Stephen Chrisomalis

Stephen Chrisomalis reframed numeral systems comparatively in *Numerical Notation: A Comparative History* (2010). His Egyptian-Demotic hypothesis for the alphabetic system has become influential and is reflected in recent reference works, though it remains a reconstruction rather than a documented borrowing.

## Culture

## 31. Public finance and law

The strongest Classical use-context is administrative:

- tribute assessments;
- temple inventories;
- construction accounts;
- wages;
- quantities of metal;
- talents, drachmas, obols, weights and measures.

The Athenian tribute lists are political monuments as well as accounts. Their numeral signs made imperial revenue visible on the Acropolis.

Legal texts could number clauses or record penalties and payments. The Herodianic report about Solon belongs to this remembered association of old signs with law.

## 32. Coins

Greek coins may bear:

- alphabetic date numerals;
- regnal years;
- mint or magistrate marks that resemble numerals;
- denominations expressed through local signs or abbreviations.

**Methodological warning.** A letter on a coin is not automatically a number. It can be an ethnic abbreviation, magistrate initial, control mark, mint signature, or die series. Identification requires the coin series and die context.

## 33. Calendars

Alphabetic numerals numbered:

- days of a month;
- Olympiads;
- regnal years;
- indiction cycles;
- ecclesiastical years;
- Easter tables.

Because ancient Greek calendars were locally variable, a numeral alone does not identify a modern Gregorian date.

## 34. Isopsephy

**Isopsephy** assigns each letter its alphabetic numeral value and adds the total. Two names or phrases with the same total are *isopsephic*.

Uses include:

- riddles concealing personal names;
- love graffiti;
- epigrams balanced by equal numerical totals;
- magical and amuletic texts;
- theological interpretation;
- chronographic or dedicatory play.

The practice becomes especially visible in the Hellenistic and Roman periods. The term’s literal association with equal pebble-counts preserves the physical metaphor of reckoning.

### 34.1 Pompeii and Smyrna

Greek graffiti from Roman cities include declarations of love for someone “whose number is” a stated total. Such texts demonstrate everyday recreational isopsephy outside learned theological literature.

**Documented artefacts; unresolved identities.** Even a perfectly read number rarely reveals one unique name.

### 34.2 Leonides of Alexandria

Leonides of Alexandria, first century CE, composed isopsephic epigrams whose lines or couplets balance numerically. These are literary demonstrations, not secret codes accidentally discovered by modern readers. [Cambridge, *Poetry and Number in Graeco-Roman Antiquity*](https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/D024A7FBFCE9A2E66B4C4400634D3DBB/9781009123044AR.pdf/Poetry_and_Number_in_Graeco-Roman_Antiquity.pdf)

## 35. Revelation 13:18

The Greek text asks the intelligent reader to calculate the Beast’s number. This presupposes familiarity with name-values.

### Evidence levels

- **Text:** most witnesses give 666.
- **Ancient variant:** 616 was already known to Irenaeus.
- **Manuscript artefact:** P115 and Codex Ephraemi support 616.
- **Strong scholarly reconstruction:** Nero/Neron Caesar explains both totals through Hebrew spelling.
- **Tradition and legend:** innumerable identifications—from emperors and popes to modern politicians and technologies—have been generated by changing language, spelling, or calculation rules.
- **Finding:** a proposed solution is evidentially weak if the spelling or value scheme is selected only after the target person has been chosen.

## 36. Jesus 888

The value 888 became a Christian numerical symbol contrasted with 666. Irenaeus proves that it was already in circulation in the second century, although he reports it in a polemical account of Valentinian interpretation.

Later Christian writers elaborated 888 as:

- an “eighth day” number;
- a number of resurrection and new creation;
- a numerical counterpart to Jesus’ Greek name.

The arithmetic is objective given the spelling ΙΗΣΟΥΣ; the theology is interpretive tradition.

## 37. Gematria and isopsephy

“Gematria” is normally used for Jewish letter-number interpretation; “isopsephy” for Greek. The practices influenced one another in the multilingual Hellenistic and Roman world, but the terms should not erase differences in language, alphabet, text, and exegetical convention.

Claims that all early Greek myths, temple dimensions, or philosophical terms were originally constructed from hidden letter totals require independent contemporary evidence. The mere ability to calculate a total proves only that a total can be calculated.

## 38. Liturgy and manuscripts

Greek numeral letters remain visible in:

- biblical chapter headings;
- lectionaries;
- canon tables;
- hymn and ode enumeration;
- liturgical books;
- marginal reference systems;
- patriarchal and conciliar numbering.

Nomina sacra—contracted sacred names such as ΙΣ̅ or ΘΣ̅—use overlines too, but they are abbreviations, not automatically numerals. Context distinguishes the two practices.

## 39. Chronograms

A chronogram encodes a date in selected numeral letters. Greek alphabetic values make Greek chronograms possible, although the practice is more conspicuous in some Latin, Hebrew, Arabic, and later Christian traditions.

**Caution.** Not every inscription containing high-value letters is a deliberate chronogram. Selection marks, textual explanation, or a plausible contemporary convention are needed.

## 40. Literature and art

Greek number symbolism appears in:

- Pythagorean and Neopythagorean speculation;
- Platonic and Neoplatonic commentary;
- riddling epigrams;
- magical papyri;
- Christian exegesis;
- architectural inscriptions;
- modern historical novels, games, logos, and occult literature.

The numeral system enabled these readings but did not dictate them. Historical users ranged from accountants to mystics.

## 41. Typography

Manuscript and print forms vary:

- majuscule versus minuscule;
- overline versus keraia;
- digamma versus stigma;
- ligature ϛ versus στ;
- older koppa ϙ versus numeral koppa ϟ;
- sampi variants ͳ and ϡ;
- enclosing and conjoined myriad forms.

Greek numeral signs are not “old-style figures” in the typographic sense of lowercase Hindu-Arabic digits with ascenders and descenders. That term belongs to later European Arabic-digit typography.

## Controversies and disputes

## 42. Who invented Greek numerals?

No named inventor is securely attested.

- **Acrophonic notation:** probably emerged incrementally from tally and abbreviation practices.
- **Alphabetic notation:** probably devised or standardized in an Ionian commercial environment.
- **Solon:** attached by later tradition to old numeral use, not securely to invention.
- **Pythagoras:** later traditions connect him with arithmetic and the abacus, but no contemporary evidence makes him inventor of either Greek notation.

Any precise inventor’s name is therefore tradition or legend.

## 43. Are the acrophonic signs really initials?

For Π, Δ, Η, Χ, and Μ the explanation is strong and systematic. The sign Ι for 1 is a tally stroke, not plausibly an initial of the standard Greek word for one.

A nineteenth-century source that attempts to derive Ι from an alleged old Greek word beginning with iota illustrates the danger of forcing every sign into acrophony. Modern comparative work treats the one-stroke as abstract/tally-derived.

## 44. Did alphabetic numerals come from Phoenicia?

### Case for

- Greek letters themselves derive from Phoenician.
- Later Semitic scripts possess alphabetic numeral systems.
- The archaic extra letters occupy historically meaningful positions.

### Case against

- Early West Semitic numeral notation used separate cumulative signs.
- Secure Semitic alphabetic numerals postdate Greek alphabetic numerals.
- The distinctive three-ennead decimal structure resembles Egyptian Demotic more closely.

### Assessment

Phoenician ancestry explains the alphabetic signs and their order, but not necessarily the decision to assign the three decimal series. Egyptian structural stimulus is presently the stronger scholarly reconstruction.

## 45. Egyptian origin: established or speculative?

Chrisomalis’s hypothesis explains both form and historical opportunity. Critics may properly observe that:

- structural similarity does not prove direct borrowing;
- the earliest Greek evidence is fragmentary;
- no transitional bilingual notation has been found;
- independent invention is possible.

The responsible wording is therefore “probably inspired by Egyptian Demotic,” not “proved copied from Egypt.”

## 46. The date of the Milesian system

Older dates ranged from the eighth century BCE to the fifth. Current evidence favors the sixth century, but individual “earliest” objects remain contestable because a sequence letter is not necessarily a cardinal numeral.

The especially late secure appearance of ϡ = 900 does not prove that the system originally stopped at 800; high values are statistically rare, and early material is sparse. Conversely, reconstructing a complete 27-sign table before any full table survives remains an inference.

## 47. What was sampi?

Sampi has:

- an archaic phonetic history in some Ionic alphabets;
- an alphabetic-sequence function;
- a later numeral value of 900;
- several glyph forms.

The name *sampi* is late and of disputed etymology, often explained from a phrase like ὡς ἂν πῖ, “like pi,” but this is not securely established. The exact line from archaic phonetic sign to numeral 900 remains palaeographically debated.

## 48. Digamma or stigma for six?

The sixth place originally corresponded to digamma, ϝ. In cursive and medieval writing its numeral form developed into ϛ, later called stigma and associated visually or historically with the στ ligature.

It is misleading either to say:

- “stigma was always the ancient sixth letter,” or
- “στ is simply two independently valued letters, 200 + 300.”

When marked as one numeral unit, στʹ conventionally means 6 in modern Greek enumeration.

## 49. Did Greek numerals have zero?

Three different questions are often conflated.

1. **Ordinary cardinal alphabetic notation:** no zero sign needed.
2. **Astronomical sexagesimal tables:** an empty-place/zero sign existed.
3. **Zero as an abstract number with general arithmetic rules:** Greek sources do not supply a Brahmagupta-like systematic arithmetic of zero.

Thus both “Greek numerals had no zero” and “the Greeks invented zero” are misleading without qualification.

## 50. Was Ptolemy’s sign an omicron?

In later manuscripts it can look like ο, and omicron cannot mean 70 within a single sexagesimal digit limited to 0–59. But early forms are small circles with extended overbars and differ from normal omicron.

The derivation from οὐδέν, “nothing,” is plausible wordplay but is not established by Ptolemy’s explicit testimony. The sign may instead continue Babylonian/astronomical empty-place practice in a locally adapted shape.

## 51. Did Babylonian zero pass directly to Greece?

Greek astronomy unquestionably absorbed Babylonian sexagesimal methods. A historical connection between Babylonian placeholder practice and Greek astronomical empty-place marking is therefore plausible.

What is not documented is a single named transmitter or an object showing one sign changing graphically into the other. The positional practice can be borrowed even if the glyph is newly designed.

## 52. Did the numeral system impede Greek mathematics?

This is a modern progress narrative, not an ancient finding.

Against it:

- Greeks developed rigorous geometry and number theory;
- Archimedes named enormous numbers;
- Ptolemy used functional sexagesimal place notation;
- Diophantus used syncopated algebra;
- papyri preserve practical tables.

For it, in a limited sense:

- alphabetic numerals do not support the transparent written carrying algorithms of a ten-digit positional system;
- routine written multiplication and division are less mechanically obvious.

The balanced conclusion is that calculation moved across speech, memory, tables, diagrams, and boards. Written notation was only one component.

## 53. “Letters were invented as numbers”

Modern numerological movements sometimes assert that Greek letters were originally Egyptian number-cosmograms, that entire vocabularies were generated from letter totals, or that selected Egyptian monuments encode the later 27-value system.

- **Documented fact:** Greek letters have numerical values in the alphabetic system.
- **Documented fact:** Greek writing derives substantially from a Phoenician alphabetic model ultimately connected with earlier West Semitic writing.
- **Plausible reconstruction:** the numeral system’s three-ennead structure was inspired by Demotic notation.
- **Unsupported leap:** numerical values generated the phonetic alphabet or the Greek language.
- **Modern invention:** elaborate diagrams matching letter totals to unrelated monuments without historically transmitted rules.

Such claims should be recorded as modern esoteric traditions, not silently presented as epigraphy.

## 54. “Every matching total proves intention”

Given 27 values, many words and phrases inevitably share totals. Intention is strongest where:

- an ancient author explicitly states the equation;
- an inscription calls attention to “the number”;
- paired poetic lines demonstrably balance;
- variants are constrained by normal contemporary spelling.

It is weak where spellings, languages, titles, omitted letters, or counting rules are adjusted after the desired total is known.

## 55. The “angles equal digit values” legend

The familiar internet graphic claiming that modern digit 1 has one angle, 2 two angles, and so on is a modern invention. It concerns stylized Hindu-Arabic digits, not Greek numerals. Historical digit forms do not match the diagram, and the zero is usually forced into the pattern.

There is no corresponding ancient evidence that α, β, γ or Ι, Π, Δ received values from counting their angles.

## 56. Ifrah, Menninger, and broad universal histories

Karl Menninger’s *Number Words and Number Symbols* remains rich in comparative examples and illustrations but predates much current archaeological and palaeographic work.

Georges Ifrah’s *Universal History of Numbers* is expansive and stimulating, but reviewers including Joseph Dauben and Stephen Chrisomalis have criticized its unsourced narratives, diffusionist leaps, inconsistent chronology, and repetition of attractive legends.

Best practice is:

- use Menninger and Ifrah to locate topics and traditional claims;
- verify object dates in museum catalogues and inscriptions;
- verify transmission claims in specialist work;
- treat Chrisomalis 2010 as the standard comparative synthesis, not as infallible primary evidence.

## Open questions

1. Which ambiguous sixth-century marks are true cardinal alphabetic numerals rather than labels, abbreviations, or sequence letters?

2. Can a securely stratified object narrow the creation of the complete three-ennead system more closely than “early/mid-sixth century BCE”?

3. Was the alphabetic system first standardized specifically at Miletus, elsewhere in Ionia, or among mobile Greek merchants in Egypt?

4. Will a bilingual Greek–Demotic account be found that demonstrates structural transfer directly?

5. Did the 900 sign belong to the system from its creation, or was the final ennead completed gradually?

6. What is the exact palaeographic relationship between archaic phonetic sampi, alphabetic ordering signs, and numeral sampi?

7. How much of the notation in surviving Ptolemaic and Diophantine manuscripts is authorial, and how much reflects later copyists?

8. What exact algorithms were performed on the Salamis board? Its accounting function is highly likely, but detailed reconstructions remain underdetermined.

9. How widespread was finger reckoning among Classical Greek merchants and schoolchildren, as distinct from the better documented Roman and Byzantine conventions?

10. How did local acrophonic systems interact with coin denominations and metrological reforms city by city?

11. When did particular Byzantine conventions—overline, keraia, lower thousands mark, and standardized stigma—become dominant in each manuscript tradition?

12. How common is active knowledge of the full system, especially ϟ and ϡ, among Greek speakers today? Official usage demonstrates survival, but not general numeracy.

## Chronological outline

| Date | Development | Evidence status |
|---|---|---|
| Second millennium BCE | Linear A and Linear B decimal accounting in the Aegean | Documented tablets; no proven direct graphic descent |
| Eighth century BCE | Greek alphabet adapted from Phoenician | Documented inscriptions; not yet secure evidence for alphabetic numerals |
| Mid-sixth century BCE | Greek acrophonic systems circulating; probable creation of alphabetic numerals in Ionian/Egyptian-contact setting | Mixed artefactual evidence and reconstruction |
| Late sixth century BCE | Mature acrophonic use in several Greek regions | Inscriptions |
| 454/3 BCE onward | Athenian tribute lists use acrophonic monetary notation | Secure dated inscriptions, IG I³ 259 ff. |
| 403/2 BCE | Athens officially adopts Ionic alphabet | Dated historical/epigraphic context |
| Fifth–fourth centuries BCE | Alphabetic numerals increasingly visible alongside acrophonic notation | Inscriptions and graffiti |
| Third century BCE | Alphabetic notation spreads widely; Archimedes writes *Sand Reckoner* | Textual tradition |
| ca. 300 BCE | Conventional date of Salamis counting board | Surviving artefact, EM 11515 |
| Second–first centuries BCE | Local acrophonic systems decline; alphabetic numerals become dominant | Epigraphic pattern |
| First century CE | Isopsephic graffiti and poetry; Revelation’s 666 | Artefacts and texts |
| ca. 130–170 CE | Ptolemy’s alphabetic-sexagesimal astronomy and zero/empty-place sign | Text/manuscript tradition |
| ca. 180 CE | Irenaeus reports Jesus 888 and 666/616 controversy | Dated literary testimony |
| Third century CE | Diophantus’s syncopated algebra | Manuscript tradition |
| Third–fourth centuries CE | P115 preserves 616 at Revelation 13:18 | Papyrus artefact |
| Late antiquity | Greek model influences Coptic, Gothic, Armenian, Georgian and later Slavic notation | Comparative textual and epigraphic evidence |
| Byzantine period | Numerals used in manuscripts, liturgy, calendars, astronomy and administration | Extensive manuscript evidence |
| Renaissance–modern era | Hindu-Arabic numerals dominate calculation and ordinary cardinals | Printed and administrative evidence |
| Present | Greek letter-numerals survive in enumeration, traditional ordinals, legal, historical and ecclesiastical use | Documented contemporary practice |
