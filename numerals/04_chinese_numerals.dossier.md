# Chinese numerals, counting rods and the abacus: Research Dossier

## Basic identification

| Field | Identification |
|---|---|
| Name | Chinese character numerals; Chinese rod numerals, *suànchóu* / *suànzǐ* 算籌／算子; Chinese abacus, *suànpán* 算盤; abacus calculation, *zhūsuàn* 珠算 |
| Base | Decimal throughout. Ordinary written Chinese groups higher powers primarily by myriads, \(10^4\), rather than by thousands. |
| Type | Three related but distinct systems: (1) ordinary characters: decimal multiplicative-additive; (2) rod numerals: decimal positional notation with ciphered digits 1–9 and originally a blank zero; (3) abacus: positional decimal calculating apparatus, normally with beads worth one and five. |

| Region | Origin in northern and central China; subsequently used or adapted in Japan, Korea and Vietnam. Chinese characters and Sino-derived number words remain active in East Asia, while rod calculation was largely displaced by the abacus and written Indo-Arabic arithmetic. |
| Present status | Chinese characters coexist with Indo-Arabic digits. Financial numerals remain legally and commercially useful. ### Evidentiary labels used

- **[A—documented artefact]**: surviving excavated or catalogued object.
- **[T—documented text]**: claim made or exemplified in a surviving text.
- **[R—scholarly reconstruction]**: modern inference from artefacts, texts or linguistic structure.
- **[D—disputed]**: serious disagreement over dating, interpretation, priority or transmission.
- **[Trad—tradition]**: premodern historical or cultural attribution not independently demonstrated.
- **[L—legend]**: story unsupported or contradicted by contemporary evidence.
- **[M—modern invention/convention]**: recent standardization, encoding or retrospective presentation.

The labels concern the status of the evidence, not the cultural importance of the claim.

# The system in detail

## 1. Three systems that must not be conflated

“Chinese numerals” can mean three different things.

### 1.1 Chinese character numerals

These write number words in the ordinary script:

> 三千四百五十九  
> *sān qiān sì bǎi wǔ shí jiǔ*  
> \(3\times1,000+4\times100+5\times10+9=3,459\)

This is a **multiplicative-additive** notation. A digit precedes a power word and multiplies it; the resulting terms are added. It is decimal, but it is not positional in the strict sense in which “3459” is positional: 千, 百 and 十 expressly name the powers.

Stephen Chrisomalis classifies classical Chinese notation as multiplicative-additive. Unicode more cautiously calls it “decimal-based” rather than a decimal radix notation, because power-signs remain explicit. [Unicode’s description of CJK numerals](https://unicode.org/versions/Unicode16.0.0/core-spec/chapter-22/) and Chrisomalis’s comparative typology should therefore take precedence over loose statements that ordinary 一二三 notation itself was positional.

### 1.2 Counting-rod numerals

Small rods were placed, and later rod-shaped signs were written, as digits in successive decimal positions. Adjacent columns alternated orientation so that boundaries remained intelligible:

- ones, hundreds, ten-thousands: one orientation;
- tens, thousands, hundred-thousands: the other.

Thus rods form a **ciphered-positional decimal system**. A digit’s value depends on its place.

### 1.3 The abacus

The *suanpan* is an instrument rather than a written numeral system. Each vertical rod or wire is a decimal position. On the familiar two-five form:

- each lower or “earth” bead brought to the beam counts 1;
- each upper or “heaven” bead brought to the beam counts 5;
- columns normally increase by powers of ten from right to left.

A two-five column can temporarily hold 0–15. That redundancy facilitates intermediate calculations and also accommodates the traditional sixteen-*liang* pound. The reduced Japanese *soroban* eventually adopted one upper and four lower beads: exactly the resources needed for decimal digits 0–9.

Empress Wu promulgated novel characters in 689–690, including a circle-like 〇 meaning “star,” but identity of shape is not proof that she invented mathematical zero.

廿 and 卅 are ancient, not modern shorthand; their antecedents occur in early script traditions. They remain visible in calendars, dates, calligraphy and place or event names. 卌 is rarer but historically real. They should not be interpreted as positional digits.

### Suzhou or *huāmǎ* numerals

A commercial descendant of rod notation used:

> 〇 〡 〢 〣 〤 〥 〦 〧 〨 〩

for 0–9, supplemented by 十, 廿 and 卅 or commercial unit annotations. To keep successive strokes distinguishable, forms or orientations could alternate. They were once common in shops, account books and price markings, particularly in southern China and Hong Kong, but are now marginal.

**[M]** Unicode originally called 〡–〩 “Hangzhou numerals.” It later acknowledged that “Suzhou” was the appropriate designation, but immutable character-name policy prevents renaming the encoded characters. This is a documented modern naming error, not evidence of ancient Hangzhou origin. [Unicode’s CJK chapter](https://www.unicode.org/versions/Unicode12.1.0/ch22.pdf).

### Worked rod numbers

Using Unicode’s Song-oriented alternation and writing highest place first:

- 17: 𝍩𝍦
- 59: 𝍭𝍨
- 365: 𝍢𝍮𝍤
- 3,459: 𝍫𝍣𝍭𝍨
- 6,039: 𝍮 𝍫𝍨 — with a blank hundreds position
- 60,390: 𝍥 𝍢𝍱  — blanks for absent places as required

Spaces are semantically crucial but typographically fragile. A copied manuscript or modern line-breaking process can erase the distinction between 107, 1,007 and 10,007. Alternating orientation helps identify adjacent powers, but it does not solve an arbitrary run of empty places.

**[R/D]** Modern diagrams often put the rods on a ruled checkerboard. Martzloff emphasizes that no ancient Chinese text securely describes such a universal counting board and no ancient gridded board has been conclusively excavated as the standard calculating surface. Literary references instead permit rods to be spread on tables, beds or the ground. “Counting surface” is therefore safer than “counting board,” unless a specific source identifies a board.

## 7. Zero: four different questions

Histories of zero become confused when these are treated as the same invention:

1. recognition that a collection may contain nothing;
2. an empty positional place;
3. a written placeholder;
4. zero treated as an arithmetic number with explicit operational rules.

### 7.1 Zero in ordinary character numerals

Ancient multiplicative-additive notation did not require a zero sign. One could write 一百五, with explicit powers conveying the structure, although such abbreviation may be ambiguous by modern standards.

零 originally had non-numerical senses involving residual or scattered rain and later “remainder.” It became the spoken and written bridge across an absent internal power: 一百零五.

〇 is primarily the digit-like circle. Modern PRC convention uses it especially in years and identifiers: 二〇二六.

### 7.2 Zero with rods

**[R strongly supported]** An unoccupied decimal position functioned as a placeholder on the calculating surface. This is operational place-value zero, but not a material zero token.

**[Important qualification]** A blank is not the same as a written zero digit, and an empty place is not by itself a philosophical or arithmetic theory of zero. It also disappears when the rods are cleared.

### 7.3 First secure Chinese written circle zero

A round 〇 occurs as a positional placeholder in Qin Jiushao’s 數書九章, *Mathematical Treatise in Nine Sections*, dated 1247, and in Li Ye’s 測圓海鏡, *Sea Mirror of Circle Measurements*, 1248. Unicode consequently describes the written zero in surviving rod-number texts as a Song-period development.

**[D]** Proposals for its origin include:

- transformation of a square vacancy marker □;
- graphic representation of the vacant rod place;
- borrowing or stimulus from Indian numeral notation, known in Tang China;
- convergence of indigenous vacancy practice and imported written zero.

The present evidence does not select one with certainty.

### 7.4 Zero as a number

Chinese rod practice made absent positions and “nothing entering” operationally intelligible, but the surviving early Chinese corpus does not present a Brahmagupta-style systematic arithmetic of zero as an independently signed number. The *Nine Chapters*’ positive-negative rules mention operations in which “nothing” or an empty entry is involved, but reading this as a complete modern zero algebra is anachronistic.

Thus “China had no zero” is false if it denies empty placeholders or absence; “China had invented modern zero by the Han” is also too strong if it implies a written digit with all later arithmetic properties.

## 8. Fractions, mixed numbers and decimals

### Linguistic form

Modern and classical Chinese commonly order a fraction as:

> denominator + 分之 + numerator

Thus:

- 二分之一: “of two parts, one” = \(1/2\)
- 三分之二: “of three parts, two” = \(2/3\)
- 五又三分之一: \(5+\frac13\)

The denominator-first order reflects partitioning a unit into equal parts before specifying how many are taken.

### Early computational notation

**[T/R]** The *Nine Chapters* gives systematic procedures for reducing, comparing, adding, subtracting, multiplying and dividing fractions. On a rod surface, numerator and denominator could be arranged in separate rows or described verbally. Printed reconstructions often render them vertically, numerator above denominator, but the surviving verbal text should not automatically be treated as a facsimile diagram.

The reduction rule is essentially the Euclidean algorithm: repeatedly subtract or divide to find a common measure, then divide numerator and denominator by it.

### Decimal fractions

Rod place value naturally permits extension to positions to the right of a units position, provided the unit point is known. Chinese metrological systems also possessed decimal subdivisions. Nonetheless a modern decimal point and standardized written sequence such as 3.1416 are later notational conventions. Modern Chinese reads the point as 點/点 *diǎn*: 三點一四一六.

### Historical instability

**[T/D]** Chinese sources transmitted several large-number progressions:

- a lower series in which each term was ten times the preceding;
- a middle series involving powers of \(10^4\);
- a higher or “upper” series in which scales could be squared successively.

Consequently 兆 has not always denoted the same number. Modern engineering usage also encounters 兆 as the SI-style prefix mega-, \(10^6\), while ordinary mainland counting often treats it as \(10^{12}\). Context is essential.

There is no mathematical “largest number” the system can express. Productive multiplication and addition, or positional rods, permit arbitrarily long finite integers. 載 at \(10^{44}\) is merely the conventional end of one inherited lexical list. Buddhist translations added enormous named numbers and recursive cosmological sequences; these are lexicons, not upper numerical bounds.

# Origins: dated and placed

## 1. Before writing

Notched bones, cords and tallies are widespread human technologies. They demonstrate counting or record-keeping but rarely identify a spoken numeral system.

**[Finding]** No accepted archaeological chain connects the Lebombo or Ishango bones, Mesopotamian tokens, Egyptian numerals, or any other remote tally tradition directly to Chinese character or rod numerals. Similar strokes for one, two and three are predictable independent developments.

Claims that Chinese numerals “come from” the *Yijing* trigrams, knotted cords, Mesopotamia or India require transmission evidence that is presently absent for the Shang-period signs.

## 2. Late Shang oracle bones, c. 1250/1200–1046 BCE

### The objects

**[A]** Numerals occur in inscriptions cut on ox scapulae and turtle plastrons excavated principally at Yinxu near Anyang, Henan. The inscriptions record divinations, dates, sacrifices, tribute, warfare, harvests and quantities of persons, animals and objects.

The British Library’s Couling–Chalfant group, shelfmarks **Or 7694/1506–2125**, is catalogued as late Shang, c. 1300–1050 BCE. Individual example **Or 7694/1595** has been digitized. The British Museum catalogues many oracle bones as 1200–1050 BCE, including **OA+.170**, **OA+.157**, **OA+.211** and **Franks.5279.d**. The Metropolitan Museum’s oracle bone is **67.43.16**. Taiwan’s National Palace Museum catalogues a late-Shang ox-scapula fragment as **中雜000004N000000000**, c. 1300–1046 BCE. These objects document the corpus and its dating; not every listed bone necessarily contains a complete numeral series.

### Numerical structure

**[A/T]** Shang inscriptions employ unit signs and explicit signs for ten, hundred, thousand and probably myriad. Quantities are constructed multiplicatively and additively. One to four are transparent stroke tallies; higher graphs are conventional signs rather than simply five through nine scratches.

**[R]** Chrisomalis describes Shang notation as decimal multiplicative-additive. It already displays the structural ancestry of later written Chinese numerals, but it is not a positional rod notation.

### Discovery and decipherment

Oracle bones entered scholarly notice in 1899. Tradition credits Wang Yirong with recognizing that “dragon bones” sold medicinally bore ancient writing; Liu E published rubbings in *Tieyun canggui* (1903), Sun Yirang produced early decipherments, and Luo Zhenyu and Wang Guowei established historical readings and links to Shang rulers. Li Ji’s Academia Sinica excavations at Anyang from 1928 to 1937 supplied controlled archaeological context.

**[Trad with documentary core]** The vivid story that Wang Yirong noticed characters on medicine purchased for malaria is repeatedly told, but details vary and were recorded after the event. The 1899 recognition and subsequent scholarly publication are secure; the exact pharmacy anecdote is not contemporary documentation.

### “Earliest attestation”

No single surviving bone can responsibly be crowned “the first Chinese numeral.” The corpus was produced across reign groups and survives fragmentarily. The defensible statement is:

> The earliest substantial, securely readable Chinese written numeral corpus is the late-Shang oracle-bone corpus, especially the Anyang/Yinxu inscriptions of approximately the thirteenth to eleventh centuries BCE.

Earlier marks on Neolithic pottery are real artefacts, but reading them as a developed Chinese numeral notation is disputed.

## 3. Western Zhou to Warring States, c. 1045–221 BCE

Bronze inscriptions continue numeral characters in records of gifts, military actions, land and ritual vessels. Bamboo and wooden documents become increasingly important late in the period.

The word 籌 *chóu* denotes counters, tallies or calculating rods. A famous line transmitted in the *Daodejing* says:

> 善數不用籌策  
> “One skilled in reckoning does not use counting rods/tallies.”

**[T with dating qualification]** The received text is traditionally associated with Laozi in the sixth century BCE, but modern scholarship generally places much of its formation in the late Warring States, with excavated Guodian and Mawangdui witnesses showing a complex textual history. It documents that rod or tally reckoning was culturally intelligible by the late first millennium BCE, not that Laozi invented it.

### Archaeological rods

Reports describe more than forty bamboo pieces from **Chu grave 15 at Zuojia Gongshan, Changsha**, excavated in 1954 and dated to the Warring States. Their association with weighing and writing equipment supports identification as counters.

**[D/R]** Slender sticks in graves can have several functions. Identification as mathematical rods is strongest where the assemblage, dimensions, container and associated texts agree. Claims of far earlier rods made only from undiagnostic sticks deserve caution.

## 4. Qin and early Han mathematical manuscripts

### *Suan shu shu*, “Writings on Reckoning”

**[A/T]** In 1983–84 excavators recovered 190 bamboo strips bearing the *Suan shu shu* from **Zhangjiashan tomb M247**, Jiangling, Hubei. Associated calendrical and legal texts date the sealing of the tomb to **186 BCE**, so the manuscript must be earlier.

It contains practical procedures concerning fractions, proportions, areas, grain, labor and taxation. It demonstrates a mature administrative mathematical culture before the received *Nine Chapters* reached its final form.

### Other manuscripts

Mathematical material from Shuihudi, Fangmatan, Juyan and other Qin-Han sites documents multiplication tables, administrative accounting, metrology and numerical writing. A wooden document from Fenghuangshan tomb 10 has been cited as an early written imitation of a rod digit, but the exact reading and typological significance should be checked against its excavation publication rather than repeated from modern diagrams.

## 5. Han counting rods

**[A]** Bundles interpreted as counting rods survive from Han tombs, including bone rods from Qianyang, Shaanxi, and rods among the furnishings of tombs at Mawangdui, Changsha. A lacquer box from Mawangdui tomb 3, closed in 168 BCE, contained game pieces and rods.

**[D]** Mawangdui’s patterned board is usually identified as a *liubo* game board. Its presence near rods does not establish that it was the standardized gridded “counting board” depicted in many modern histories.

The Han evidence establishes physical counters and mature procedural mathematics. It does not preserve a complete ancient calculating session frozen in place.

## 6. *The Nine Chapters on the Mathematical Art*

### Date and formation

**[T]** The anonymous 九章算術 *Jiǔzhāng Suànshù* contains 246 problems in nine thematic chapters:

1. field measurement;
2. grain exchange;
3. proportional distribution;
4. extraction of square and cube roots;
5. construction volumes;
6. equitable transport and taxation;
7. excess and deficit;
8. rectangular arrays or systems of linear equations;
9. right triangles.

**[R/D]** Its strata are commonly placed between approximately 200 BCE and the first century CE. Economic institutions, measures, terminology and comparison with excavated manuscripts indicate compilation over generations. The Chinese Text Project assigns a broad Western Han–Xin range, 120 BCE–20 CE; other scholars allow completion as late as the first century CE.

### Traditional history

Liu Hui’s 263 preface says ancient mathematical learning descended from the legendary Bao Xi/Fuxi and the Duke of Zhou; that Qin book-burning in 213 BCE damaged the tradition; and that the Han figures Zhang Cang and Geng Shouchang restored or edited the work.

- **[T]** Liu Hui really makes these claims.
- **[Trad]** Fuxi and the Duke of Zhou are culture-hero attributions.
- **[D]** Zhang Cang and Geng Shouchang may have contributed to textual formation, but contemporary Han catalogues do not securely confirm Liu’s precise editorial story.
- **[R]** Excavated *Suan shu shu* material shows that the *Nine Chapters* emerged from a broader procedural tradition rather than from one authorial event.

The work had canonical standing no later than the later Han. A Cambridge study notes a reference on an official copper measuring vessel dated 179 CE.

## 7. Liu Hui, 263 CE

Liu Hui of the state of Wei wrote his commentary in 263. It is the earliest securely dated, extensive body of Chinese mathematical reasoning by a named author.

His commentary:

- explains why algorithms work;
- treats fractions and proportional reasoning;
- justifies area and volume transformations by dissection;
- develops polygonal approximation to π;
- analyzes elimination procedures;
- attends to exactness, approximation and units.

Calling the work “without proofs” reflects an overly narrow identification of proof with Euclidean proposition style. Karine Chemla and Guo Shuchun show that verification and justification are embedded in procedures and commentary.

## 8. Tang canon and Indian contact

Li Chunfeng led an imperial group that annotated the mathematical classics; the revised canon was presented in **656 CE** and used in the Tang mathematical school and examinations.

In **718**, the Indian-descended Tang astronomer Gautama Siddhārtha, Chinese name 瞿曇悉達 *Qútán Xīdá*, compiled or translated the 九執曆 *Jiuzhi li*, “Nine Seizers Calendar,” which described Indian arithmetic and astronomical procedures.

**[T/R]** The work supplies the earliest surviving complete Chinese description of Indian decimal digits including a zero mark, although details of its textual transmission and the exact glyph shown differ among editions.

**[Finding]** Indian written positional arithmetic was known at the Tang court but did not replace rod computation. This is direct evidence of contact, not direct proof that one civilization’s earlier positional tradition generated the other.

## 9. Song-Yuan written rod numerals and zero

Song printing made rod configurations reproducible on paper. Works of the twelfth and thirteenth centuries show increasingly explicit written rod digits, polynomial arrays and equation procedures.

Major figures include:

- Jia Xian, active c. 1050: root-extraction and coefficient methods, known largely through later texts;
- Qin Jiushao, c. 1202–1261: *Shu shu jiu zhang*, 1247;
- Li Ye, 1192–1279: *Ce yuan hai jing*, 1248;
- Yang Hui, c. 1238–1298: arithmetical and combinatorial works;
- Zhu Shijie, active c. 1280–1303: *Suanxue qimeng* (1299) and *Siyuan yujian* (1303).

The circle 〇 is securely attested as a written positional zero in Qin Jiushao’s 1247 work. Li Ye’s 1248 diagrams contain circles among rod-number coefficients.

## 10. Rise of the bead abacus

### Early references

Xu Yue, c. 160–220, is traditionally credited with *Shushu jiyi*, “Notes on Traditions of Arithmetic Methods,” usually dated around 190. Its transmitted form has a commentary attributed to Zhen Luan in the sixth century.

It lists several computing devices, including 珠算 *zhūsuàn*, “bead reckoning.”

**[T/D]** The term is early in the received textual tradition, but its device need not have been the later framed two-five *suanpan*. Descriptions are obscure and may refer to beads placed or strung in a different apparatus. Claims that the familiar modern Chinese abacus is securely documented in 190 therefore overstate the evidence.

A possible abacus-like object in Zhang Zeduan’s *Along the River During the Qingming Festival* has been proposed.

**[D]** The detail is too small and its identification as an abacus remains contested.

A secure use of the word 算盤 *suanpan* is commonly cited from Tao Zongyi’s *Chuogeng lu*, completed around **1366**, near the end of the Yuan.

### Secure visual and material evidence

- **[T]** Cheng Dawei’s *Suanfa tongzong*, first printed in **1592**, contains the earliest widely accepted printed illustration of the recognizable abacus and extensive mnemonic rules.
- **[A]** The British Museum owns a porcelain model of a two-five suanpan, object **1909,0611.1**, dated c. **1522–1566**, probably Jiajing reign, Jingdezhen.
- **[A]** The Science Museum Group’s Chinese suanpan, object **1863-20**, was made before 1863.
- **[A]** Smithsonian object **MA.309527**, c. 1925, has fifteen bamboo rods, two upper beads and five lower beads.

The National Museum of China notes that no Yuan physical suanpan has yet verified claims of widespread Yuan use, while Ming textual and visual evidence is secure.

# Arithmetic and instruments

## 1. Fingers, tallies and mental tables

Chinese texts assume memorized multiplication tables. The phrase 九九 *jiǔjiǔ*, “nine nines,” became a name for the table. Early excavated multiplication tables show that decimal products were memorized and written well before the imperial period was mature.

Finger reckoning certainly existed as an ordinary human practice. Later commercial manuals contain elaborate finger mnemonics, but there is no basis for projecting every late hand-sign system into the Shang.

## 2. Setting out rods

A calculator placed a number in decimal columns, alternating orientations. Operations were performed locally:

- addition: combine rods in each place and exchange ten unit-rods for one in the next place;
- subtraction: remove rods and borrow from the next place;
- multiplication: arrange multiplicand, multiplier and product in separate rows, shifting partial products by place;
- division: place divisor and dividend in aligned rows, subtract multiples and shift;
- roots: use a place-by-place algorithm with changing divisor expressions;
- elimination: arrange coefficients in rectangular columns and combine them.

The rods are both numbers and manipulable objects. The procedure is therefore not well described as either purely written or purely mechanical.

### Simple addition

To calculate 247 + 185:

1. Set 247 in three places.
2. Add 5 to the units: 7 + 5 = 12; leave 2 and carry 1 ten.
3. Tens become 4 + 8 + 1 = 13; leave 3 and carry 1 hundred.
4. Hundreds become 2 + 1 + 1 = 4.
5. Result: 432, 四百三十二.

The physical exchanges make carrying visible.

### Multiplication by place shifting

To multiply 23 by 14:

\[
23\times 14=(23\times4)+(23\times10)=92+230=322.
\]

With rods, the second partial product is displaced one place left. Some Chinese manuals describe multiplying from high or low positions in orders different from later school algorithms, but the place-value logic is the same.

## 3. Positive and negative rods

The *Nine Chapters*, chapter 8, gives 正負術, rules for positive and negative entries in elimination.

The received rule includes:

> 同名相除，異名相益，正無入負之，負無入正之

Approximately:

> Like signs subtract; unlike signs add. From nothing subtracting a positive makes a negative; from nothing subtracting a negative makes a positive.

**[T]** This is the earliest surviving systematic rule-set for operating with signed quantities in solving simultaneous linear problems.

### Color

Later tradition and commentary associate red and black rods with signs.

**[D]** The assignment is not uniform across modern accounts. Some say red positive and black negative; others report the reverse or note that the *Nine Chapters*’ base text specifies operational categories rather than an archaeologically preserved color code. Negative numbers could also be marked by slanting a rod or by written signs in later manuscripts. “The Chinese always used red for positive and black for negative” is therefore unsafe.

Modern accounting’s association of red with deficits should not be projected backward automatically.

## 4. The *Nine Chapters* worked style

A typical problem gives:

1. concrete data;
2. an answer;
3. a “procedure” 術;
4. in the received edition, Liu Hui’s explanation.

For systems of equations, grain bundles or yields form columns. Successive column operations eliminate unknowns. Calling this “Gaussian elimination” is a useful modern structural comparison, but **[M/R]** it does not mean the ancient authors used modern matrix notation or the conceptual language of linear algebra.

For right triangles, the 勾股 *gou-gu* rule corresponds to \(a^2+b^2=c^2\). Again “Pythagorean theorem” is comparative terminology, not evidence of transmission from Pythagoras.

## 5. The abacus in operation

On a two-five suanpan, only beads touching the beam count. A canonical representation of 6 in one column is one upper bead worth 5 plus one lower bead worth 1.

### Addition: 7 + 8

One common mnemonic decomposition is:

\[
7+8=7+(10-2)=15.
\]

On the units column:

1. set 7;
2. add one bead in the next column, representing ten;
3. subtract 2 from the units;
4. result: 15.

Abacus tables encode such complementary operations as memorized rhymes. Multiplication and division use shifted multiplicands, divisors and result columns; square and cube roots are also possible. UNESCO’s heritage description explicitly includes addition, subtraction, multiplication, division, powers and roots.

### Why two upper and five lower beads?

A single decimal digit requires only one five-bead and four one-beads. Extra beads:

- simplify intermediate states and older algorithms;
- allow a column to hold 10–15 before normalization;
- permit hexadecimal reckoning for sixteen-*liang* weight relations.

The familiar architecture is thus redundant by deliberate design.

## 6. From suanpan to soroban

The Chinese instrument reached Japan by the Muromachi period, broadly the fifteenth–sixteenth centuries. The precise carrier and year are unknown.

Japanese evidence includes:

- pictorial depictions around the beginning of the Edo period;
- Mōri Shigeyoshi’s *Warizan sho*, 1622;
- Yoshida Mitsuyoshi’s popular *Jinkōki*, 1627, influenced by Cheng Dawei;
- continuing competitive and pedagogical culture.

The Japanese *soroban* reduced the older two-five form:

1. two-five;
2. one-five;
3. ultimately one-four, especially in the twentieth century.

The word *soroban* is generally related to Chinese *suanpan*, though the exact phonological route may involve intermediate forms and is not fully demonstrated.

## 7. Korea and Vietnam

China’s written and computational traditions moved with Literary Chinese, state administration, Buddhist learning and examinations.

- Korea used counting rods (*sangi*) and later the *jupan* abacus. Modern Korean has both native Korean and Sino-Korean number vocabularies. Indo-Arabic digits dominate ordinary numerical writing; Hanja numerals remain recognizable in formal, historical, ceremonial and numismatic settings.
- Japan retains kanji numerals in vertical prose, legal instruments, ceremonial dates, addresses, traditional documents and compounds, beside Arabic digits.
- Vietnam historically used Literary Chinese and chữ Nôm. Modern quốc ngữ writes native and Sino-Vietnamese number words in the Latin alphabet, and Arabic digits dominate. Chinese numeral characters remain in historical documents, monuments, temples, calligraphy and Hán-Nôm scholarship rather than ordinary national notation.

The statement “Chinese numerals remain in daily use in Japan and Korea” is true only with qualification: the **Sino-derived vocabulary and characters** persist to differing degrees, while positional calculation is overwhelmingly performed with Indo-Arabic digits.

# Transmission and replacement

## 1. Indigenous continuity

**[R, mainstream]** The ordinary character notation is a direct structural continuation of Shang and Zhou numeral writing. There is no demonstrated foreign source for the core signs or their multiplicative-additive grammar.

Rod calculation is commonly reconstructed as an indigenous development out of counters and decimal written language. The old power words could be omitted once places on a surface carried the powers.

## 2. Across East Asia

Chinese mathematical books and institutions circulated through:

- diplomatic and monastic contacts;
- the adoption of Literary Chinese;
- governmental codes and examination systems;
- Buddhist translation networks;
- merchant practice.

The Tang mathematical classics influenced Japanese court learning. Later Song-Yuan works, including Zhu Shijie’s *Suanxue qimeng*, travelled to Korea and Japan. Cheng Dawei’s *Suanfa tongzong* reached Japan soon after its 1592 publication and deeply influenced early Edo commercial arithmetic.

## 3. Contact with India and Central Asia

Buddhism created sustained translation traffic from India and Central Asia to China. Astronomers of Indian and Central Asian descent served at the Tang court. Gautama Siddhārtha’s 718 work is the clearest surviving numeral-related contact.

What can be stated securely:

- China possessed decimal multiplicative-additive numerals long before this contact.
- China used positional rod arrangements before the surviving Tang description of Indian digits.
- Indian decimal positional written numerals with a zero sign entered Tang scholarly awareness.
- China did not simply abandon rods for the Indian glyphs.
- The Song circle zero may have been influenced by imported notation, indigenous vacancy practice, or both.

What cannot presently be demonstrated:

- a documented transmission of Chinese rod digits into the formative Brahmi–Indian digit tradition;
- a documented transmission of Indian positional structure into pre-Han Chinese rod calculation;
- a single inventor, journey or manuscript connecting the two early systems.

## 4. Replacement by the abacus

Between the Yuan and Ming periods, bead reckoning became increasingly prominent in commerce. By the sixteenth century abacus manuals were abundant and rod calculation was becoming specialist or antiquarian knowledge.

Possible reasons include:

- beads remain fixed in a portable frame;
- wind, sleeves or accidental contact do not scatter them;
- a merchant can suspend and resume a calculation;
- mnemonic abacus rules are teachable at scale;
- commercial arithmetic values speed and portability.

**[R]** These explanations are plausible social reconstructions; no single contemporary text announces a formal nationwide replacement.

## 5. Encounter with European mathematics

Jesuit collaboration, especially Matteo Ricci (1552–1610) and Xu Guangqi (1562–1633), brought European geometry, astronomy and written methods into late-Ming China. Their Chinese *Elements* appeared in 1607.

European/Indo-Arabic digits became increasingly visible through astronomy, missionary printing, maritime commerce and later modern schooling. The decisive institutional replacement of rods and much traditional abacus arithmetic by written international numerals belongs chiefly to the nineteenth and twentieth centuries.

Abacus use nevertheless continued in shops, banks and schools well into the twentieth century. Electronic calculators reduced its commercial necessity but did not eliminate it.

## 6. Present survival

- Chinese publications routinely mix Arabic digits and characters.
- Legal and financial instruments retain anti-fraud forms.
- Classical-style vertical writing often favors character numerals.
- Suzhou numerals survive as heritage or niche market signs.
- Soroban education remains organized in Japan.
- Chinese zhusuan entered UNESCO’s Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity in **2013**, file **00853**.
- Mental abacus training converts imagined bead movements into rapid arithmetic.

# People

## Shang diviners and scribes, thirteenth–eleventh centuries BCE

Their names sometimes survive in divination formulas. They recorded calendrical dates, sacrificial inventories and quantified predictions. They are the earliest identifiable professional users of the written numeral tradition, though not “mathematicians” in the later textual sense.

## Wang Yirong, Liu E, Sun Yirang, Luo Zhenyu and Wang Guowei

- **Wang Yirong** (1845–1900): traditionally credited with recognizing oracle-bone writing in 1899.
- **Liu E** (1857–1909): published *Tieyun canggui* in 1903.
- **Sun Yirang** (1848–1908): made early systematic readings.
- **Luo Zhenyu** (1866–1940): collected, published and localized the material to the Shang capital area.
- **Wang Guowei** (1877–1927): connected inscriptions with received royal genealogies.
- **Li Ji** (1896–1979): directed major scientific excavations at Anyang.

## Zhang Cang and Geng Shouchang

- **Zhang Cang**, c. 253–152 BCE, was a Han statesman and calendrical expert.
- **Geng Shouchang**, active first century BCE, was an administrator and astronomer.

Their editing of the *Nine Chapters* is **[Trad/D]**, derived principally from Liu Hui’s retrospective preface.

## Liu Hui

Active in the state of Wei; commentator of 263. His surviving intellectual identity rests almost entirely on his mathematical writing. Anecdotal biographies are sparse, which is itself significant: later celebration should not be mistaken for contemporary biography.

## Li Chunfeng

602–670. Astronomer, historian and official who supervised the Tang recension of mathematical classics, submitted in 656. The canon linked mathematical mastery to state education.

## Gautama Siddhārtha

Active early eighth century. An astronomer from an Indian-descended family at the Tang court. His 718 *Jiuzhi li* is central evidence for knowledge of Indian numeral practice in China.

## Qin Jiushao

c. 1202–1261. Official and author of the 1247 *Mathematical Treatise in Nine Sections*. His biography includes accusations of corruption and violence in hostile sources.

**[T/biographical dispute]** The mathematical work is secure; moral assessments often depend on factional official histories. The “good and bad” cannot be responsibly balanced as equivalent evidence without attention to those political contexts.

## Li Ye

1192–1279. Scholar under the Jin and Mongol transition. His 1248 *Sea Mirror of Circle Measurements* uses polynomial procedures and written rod arrangements with zero circles.

## Yang Hui

Active c. 1261–1275. Preserved and explained earlier algorithms, including coefficient diagrams now popularly called “Pascal’s triangle.” The Chinese diagram predates Pascal, but naming it after Pascal is a later European convention; calling it “Yang Hui’s triangle” corrects local history but does not prove Yang invented it.

## Zhu Shijie

Active late thirteenth–early fourteenth century. His *Jade Mirror of the Four Unknowns* (1303) represents the high development of polynomial rod algebra.

## Cheng Dawei

1533–1606. Merchant, traveller and mathematical compiler from Anhui. His 1592 *Suanfa tongzong* assembled practical problems, abacus rules, tables, verse mnemonics and historical lore. Its durability—reprinted for centuries—illustrates the abacus’s commercial success.

## Xu Guangqi and Matteo Ricci

Their collaboration in 1607 translated the first six books of Euclid into Chinese. It did not instantly replace indigenous mathematics; it began a prolonged encounter between proof cultures, astronomical requirements and computational practices.

# Culture

## 1. Administration and law

Numbers appear throughout early Chinese administration: levies, grain, labor, land, household registers, punishments, calendars and military strength. Excavated legal and mathematical manuscripts show that numeracy was an administrative technology, not merely elite recreation.

Financial numerals remain a security script. A payment written 壹仟 cannot be converted into 參仟 by adding two strokes as easily as 一千 can become 三千.

## 2. Calendars

Chinese calendrical dates combined:

- regnal years;
- lunar months and days;
- sexagenary stem-branch designations;
- occasionally numbered sequences.

The heavenly stems and earthly branches are ordinal cycles, not ordinary numeral signs. Treating 甲 as “1,” 乙 as “2,” and so forth is a contextual ordering convention analogous to “A, B, C,” not a general Chinese alphabetic numeral system.

Modern years may be read digit by digit:

> 二〇二六年 — year 2026.

Traditional dates may use 廿 for the twentieth day: 廿五 “the twenty-fifth.”

## 3. Coins and paper money

Early coin legends name weight or denomination: 半兩 “half-*liang*,” 五銖 “five-*zhu*.” Wang Mang’s reforms used denominations such as 小布一百 through 大布黃千.

From the Tang, coin legends commonly used reign titles plus 通寶 “circulating treasure,” rather than printing a continuously numbered absolute year. Song coins sometimes marked minting years with numerals on reverses. Modern machine-struck coins and banknotes increasingly combined Chinese character denominations, financial numerals and Arabic digits.

The Palace Museum’s “Ten Spades” documents denominations from 一百 to 黃千; the National Museum of China preserves extensive currency series.

## 4. Literature

Number characters are productive literary and organizational devices:

- titles such as *Nine Chapters* 九章;
- canonical groupings such as Five Classics 五經 and Four Books 四書;
- parallelism and graded enumeration;
- riddles exploiting the form or sound of 一, 十, 八 and other graphs;
- poems containing multiplication-table expressions.

Numbers in titles are not necessarily exact inventories; they can signify completeness, multiplicity or canonical organization.

## 5. Divination and cosmology

The *Yijing* associates broken and unbroken lines, trigrams, hexagrams, odd/even values and cosmological transformations. Later numerological traditions connected numbers with directions, elements, tones and calendrical cycles.

**[T]** These systems were culturally influential.

**[D/L]** They do not demonstrate that binary arithmetic, Leibnizian positional notation or modern computer science existed in ancient China. Leibniz did correspond about the hexagrams and interpreted them through his binary arithmetic, but that early-modern reinterpretation is not identical to the ancient divinatory system.

The Luoshu magic square—traditionally revealed on a turtle from the Luo River—is a major cultural diagram.

- **[Trad/L]** The miraculous turtle is legend.
- **[T]** Magic-square arrangements occur in transmitted mathematical and cosmological traditions.
- **[D]** Dating every later nine-cell numerical diagram to remote antiquity is unjustified.

## 6. Auspicious and inauspicious numbers

Sound associations underlie many modern preferences:

- 八 *bā* resembles 發/发 *fā*, “prosper”;
- 九 *jiǔ* resembles 久 *jiǔ*, “long-lasting”;
- 四 *sì* resembles 死 *sǐ*, “death”;
- 六 may suggest smooth progress in modern idiom.

These are socially real and affect phone numbers, addresses, gifts, auction prices and wedding dates.

**[Qualification]** They vary by language, dialect, period and community. Present-day marketing narratives should not automatically be projected into the Shang or Han.

## 7. Typography

Chinese numerals participate in ordinary calligraphic styles: seal, clerical, regular, running and cursive. Vertical typesetting ordinarily runs top to bottom in columns ordered right to left, although individual number strings may be set according to publishing convention.

“Old-style figures” properly describes Western typefaces whose 0–9 digits have ascending and descending forms. Chinese characters do not possess an exact native equivalent. Relevant Chinese typographic contrasts are instead:

- ordinary versus financial characters;
- full-width ０１２３ versus ASCII 0123;
- vertical versus horizontal composition;
- formal versus cursive graphs;
- ideographic 〇 versus the Arabic digit 0.

## 8. Gematria, isopsephy and chronograms

Chinese culture had rich numerology, rebus play, stroke counting and calendrical encoding, but it did not normally assign every Chinese graph a fixed numeral value in the manner of Hebrew gematria or Greek isopsephy.

Chronogram-like dating can occur through cyclical characters, riddles and literary allusion. It is structurally unlike the European chronogram, where selected Roman-numeral letters in a phrase are summed.

**[Finding]** Calling Chinese stroke-number divination “Chinese gematria” may be a useful loose comparison but is not a historically indigenous unified system.

# Controversies and disputes

## 1. Did China invent decimal place value before India?

### Needham–Lam position

Joseph Needham and Wang Ling emphasized the sophistication and antiquity of decimal rod reckoning. Lam Lay Yong and Ang Tian Se argued more strongly that the Hindu-Arabic system ultimately developed from Chinese rod numerals, citing:

- decimal base;
- nine nonzero digit configurations;
- alternating positional columns;
- an empty place serving as zero;
- similarity between rod algorithms and later written algorithms;
- the chronological priority of Chinese procedural texts over surviving Indian positional manuscripts.

### Critical position

Jean-Claude Martzloff and other critics answer:

- similarity of decimal algorithms does not establish transmission;
- a physical rod configuration is not the same as a written numeral script;
- the standard gridded counting board is partly reconstructed;
- early Chinese sources often state numbers in characters, not preserved rod glyphs;
- no document traces the rod signs through Central Asia into Brahmi numeral forms;
- Indian numerals have their own epigraphic sequence and a written zero tradition;
- the shapes of early Indian digits do not follow straightforwardly from the Chinese alternating rod sets.

### Assessment

**[D]** Chinese priority in the *use of decimal positional counters* is strongly defensible relative to currently surviving texts and artefacts. Chinese priority in the ancestry of the Hindu-Arabic written numeral system is not established.

The two claims are different. A culture can develop positional manipulation with counters without being the graphic ancestor of another culture’s written digits.

## 2. Who invented zero?

Potential claimants vary because “zero” has several meanings.

- Babylonian scribes used a sexagesimal placeholder, initially a blank and later a special mark, but not consistently a terminal zero.
- Chinese rod calculators used an empty decimal place.
- Maya scribes possessed shell-like zero signs in vigesimal calendrical notation.
- Indian mathematicians developed a written decimal zero and explicit arithmetic rules; Brahmagupta’s *Brahmasphuṭasiddhānta* of 628 gives rules for zero and debts/fortunes.
- Chinese Song texts use 〇 as a written positional zero.

**Assessment:** there was no single undifferentiated invention. Babylonian, Chinese, Maya and Indian achievements answer different historical questions. The Hindu-Arabic zero used globally descends through the South Asian–Islamic transmission, but this does not erase independent Babylonian, Chinese or Maya placeholders.

## 3. Was the Chinese blank a “true zero”?

- If “zero” means an empty placeholder in positional computation: yes.
- If it means a permanent written sign: initially no.
- If it means an independently manipulable number with rules: early evidence is incomplete.
- If it means the origin of modern 0: unproved.

The expression “true zero” often conceals a chosen definition and should be replaced by the precise function.

## 4. Was 〇 invented in China or imported?

Evidence for indigenous development:

- a long tradition of empty rod places;
- square vacancy marks in written layouts;
- circle and void symbolism available within China;
- need for a durable printed placeholder.

Evidence for external stimulus:

- Indian positional numerals and a zero mark were known in Tang China;
- the written circle appears late relative to that contact;
- trans-Eurasian astronomical exchange was active.

**[D]** Shape alone cannot decide the matter. Circles are easy to invent independently. No surviving Chinese text announces “we borrowed this zero,” and no developmental sequence conclusively derives 〇 from □.

## 5. Red and black negatives

Popular histories state categorically that red rods were positive and black rods negative, often calling this the origin of “in the red.”

Problems:

- textual evidence for signed operations is firmer than evidence for a universal color convention;
- descriptions and reconstructed color assignments vary;
- modern “red ink = debt” conventions need not descend from Han rods.

**Assessment:** colored differentiation is plausible and textually attested in later tradition; universal assignment and direct modern etymology are disputed.

## 6. The counting board

Needham, educational reconstructions and museum displays frequently show a square grid.

Martzloff observes that:

- no standard ancient calculating board is securely preserved;
- ancient and medieval descriptions do not require a ruled board;
- sources mention rods laid on an ordinary surface.

**Assessment:** decimal places require spatial alignment, but not necessarily a purpose-built checkerboard. A “virtual grid” created by spacing is sufficient.

## 7. How old is the suanpan?

Competing dates arise by conflating different evidence:

- Han references to bead reckoning;
- an ambiguous Song painting detail;
- Yuan occurrences of the word 算盤;
- Ming images and objects of the recognizable framed device.

**Assessment:** bead reckoning has early textual ancestry; the familiar suanpan is securely documented only in the later medieval/early modern period, especially from the fourteenth to sixteenth centuries.

The statement “the modern suanpan was invented in 190 CE” is therefore overconfident.

## 8. Fuxi invented arithmetic

Liu Hui’s preface traces reckoning and the “nine nines” to Bao Xi/Fuxi.

- **[T]** This is a genuine third-century account of tradition.
- **[Trad/L]** Fuxi is a culture hero placed in mythic antiquity.
- **Evidence:** no contemporary inscription names him as inventor, and the historical multiplication-table tradition is documented much later.

The story is important evidence for how Chinese scholars legitimized mathematics, not for an exact invention date.

## 9. Empress Wu or Zhu Yuanzhang invented financial numerals

Both stories explain complex numerals as state anti-fraud reforms.

- Empress Wu did promulgate new graphs.
- Ming law strongly valued secure accounting.
- Complex numeral words and graphs predate both rulers.

**Assessment:** particular governments may have regulated or popularized forms; invention by one ruler is tradition, not established fact.

## 10. “Ancient China invented binary”

The claim usually maps broken and unbroken *Yijing* lines to 0 and 1.

- Leibniz saw a binary correspondence in the hexagram arrangement communicated to him.
- Ancient diviners manipulated two line types and combinatorial patterns.
- They did not use those lines as an unrestricted positional base-two numeral notation with Leibniz’s arithmetic.

**Assessment:** the structural analogy is real; identity with modern binary numeration is a retrospective invention.

## 11. “Chinese numerals were created from rod shapes”

One through three are obviously stroke-like in both scripts. Some later character signs resemble conventionalized rod patterns.

But oracle-bone character numerals substantially predate the best evidence for positional rod notation, and 5–9 cannot all be derived by simply copying the later rod digits.

**Assessment:** interaction between written signs and calculating practice is likely; a one-way derivation of the entire character series from rods is not demonstrated.

## 12. The “angles determine digit values” legend

This story concerns modern Hindu-Arabic digit shapes: 1 supposedly has one angle, 2 two angles, and so on.

- no medieval Indian or Arabic manuscript explains the digits this way;
- digit forms changed gradually and do not preserve the proposed angle counts;
- diagrams are redesigned retroactively to make the counts work.

It is a modern didactic legend and has no role in the origin of Chinese numerals.

## 13. Ifrah’s sweeping reconstructions

Georges Ifrah’s *Universal History of Numbers* popularized an enormous comparative archive and drew attention to neglected systems. It is valuable as a source-finder and history of popular interpretation.

Reviews by historians including Joseph Dauben and discussions drawing on Martzloff identify problems:

- reliance on secondary compilations;
- overly confident diffusion narratives;
- reconstructions presented more vividly than their evidence permits;
- a misleading early date for Chinese written zero, traceable partly to a typographical or interpretive error in the use of Needham;
- reconstruction of a counting table from much later illustration.

Accordingly, Ifrah should be cited for claims he makes, but not used alone to establish dates or provenance.

# Open questions

1. **Exactly when did positional rod notation emerge?**  
   Rod-like artefacts and decimal texts survive, but early configurations rarely remain in their working positions.

2. **Was there ever a standard ancient ruled counting board?**  
   Alignment is certain; a dedicated universal physical grid is not.

3. **How should individual excavated sticks be distinguished from game pieces, tally sticks, divination implements or writing tools?**  
   Contextual archaeological publication remains decisive.

4. **When did 〇 become general rather than specialist mathematical notation?**  
   Its thirteenth-century mathematical presence is secure; diffusion into dates, ordinary numerals and financial practice was gradual.

5. **Did Tang knowledge of Indian zero materially influence Song notation?**  
   Contact is documented, but an explicit chain is missing.

6. **How much of the *Nine Chapters* predates the Han?**  
   Comparison with newly excavated Qin-Han manuscripts continues to alter proposed strata.

7. **How consistently were colors used for signs?**  
   Texts, printed conventions and archaeological preservation do not yet provide a simple universal history.

8. **Why did rod algebra decline so rapidly once the abacus spread?**  
   Commercial utility is persuasive, but the relationship among institutions, printing, pedagogy and occupational specialization needs fuller local study.

9. **What were the actual transmission routes into Korea, Japan and Vietnam?**  
   Books and terminology can often be dated; the itineraries of practitioners are much less visible.

10. **How long will suanpan, soroban and Suzhou numerals remain living rather than reconstructed traditions?**  
    Heritage programs, competitions and mental-abacus education provide continuity, but ordinary commercial use has sharply contracted.
