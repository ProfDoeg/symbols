# Maya numerals and the vigesimal Long Count: Research Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary method

This dossier concerns the numeral traditions used by Maya-speaking peoples, especially the bar-and-dot notation and the modified-vigesimal calendar chronology conventionally called the Long Count. The Long Count was not exclusively Maya in its earliest surviving phase: its oldest inscriptions occur in Zoque/Isthmian or culturally mixed regions west of the Maya lowlands. “Maya numeral” and “Maya Long Count” are therefore convenient but potentially misleading labels.

Claims are marked as follows:

- **[Artefact]** Directly preserved object or inscription.
- **[Documented text]** Primary or near-primary written testimony.
- **[Reconstruction]** Broad scholarly interpretation supported by comparative or contextual evidence.
- **[Disputed]** Materially contested identification, date, reading, or priority claim.
- **[Tradition]** Indigenous or later historical tradition whose documentary status differs from archaeological demonstration.
- **[Legend]** Story lacking adequate historical evidence.
- **[Modern convention/invention]** Scholarly, pedagogical, commercial, or popular practice not securely attributable to ancient scribes.
- **[Absence of evidence]** A proposition for which the surviving corpus supplies no adequate confirmation.

Dates converted into the Gregorian or Julian calendar depend upon a correlation constant. Unless stated otherwise, modern Gregorian equivalents use the widely accepted Goodman–Martínez–Thompson correlation near Julian Day 584,283. A one- or two-day difference among publications is therefore not necessarily an error.

## Basic identification

| Field | Identification |
|---|---|
| Name | Maya numerals; bar-and-dot numeration; Long Count or “Initial Series” chronology |
| Base | Ordinary numeration: vigesimal, base 20. Long Count: mixed-radix sequence \(1,20,360,7,200,144,000,\ldots\), with 18 rather than 20 winalob making one tun |
| Type | The elementary digits are additive and quinary-grouped; stacked numerals can be ciphered-positional; calendrical inscriptions also use a mixed or multiplicative-additive form in which coefficients accompany named period glyphs |
| Elementary signs | Dot = 1; horizontal bar = 5; several zero/completion signs, famously shell-like; complete head variants for 0–19; occasional full-figure variants |
| Direction | Simple positional columns normally have units at the bottom and ascending positions above. Monumental Long Counts can run through paired glyph blocks in normal Maya reading order |
| Period | Precursors by at least the Middle–Late Formative period; securely dated Long Counts from the first century BCE; Maya monumental florescence roughly AD 250–900; codical use into the Postclassic; lexical and ritual vigesimal counting survives today |
| Region | Mesoamerica: chiefly present-day southern Mexico, Guatemala, Belize, western Honduras, and El Salvador; early Long Count monuments also occur in the Isthmian/Gulf and Pacific-slope interaction zones |
| Unicode | U+1D2E0–U+1D2F3, MAYAN NUMERAL ZERO through NINETEEN; introduced as twenty atomic digit characters. Full Maya hieroglyphic writing remains a separate and much harder encoding problem ([Unicode chart](https://unicode.org/charts/PDF/U1D2E0.pdf); [Unicode proposal](https://www.unicode.org/wg2/docs/n4804-mayan-numerals.pdf)) |

### A crucial distinction

“The Maya numeral system” was not one absolutely uniform notation.

1. Bar-and-dot digits are additive inside each digit: dots count ones and bars count fives.
2. Ordinary stacked notation can be genuinely base-20 positional.
3. Long Count chronology normally couples numeral coefficients with explicit time-unit glyphs. Its values are calendrical rather than pure powers of twenty below the tun.
4. Distance numbers in codices and inscriptions have their own conventions and may be written vertically, horizontally, or compactly.
5. Head variants are not a second mathematical system; they are elaborate allographs of numeral values.
6. Spoken Maya number systems are related but not identical across Mayan languages and periods.

Stephen Chrisomalis consequently warns against describing every Long Count as though it were simply a modern positional numeral with an anomalous “360 place.” A useful structural analysis treats the higher series as vigesimal tun coefficients, with winal and k’in as separately specified subdivisions of the tun. That interpretation avoids making the fourth written coefficient intrinsically mean 7,200 in every context ([Chrisomalis, *Numerical Notation*, chapter 9](https://nzdr.ru/data/media/biblio/kolxoz/M/MPop/Chrisomalis%20S.%20Numerical%20notation..%20A%20comparative%20history%20%28CUP%2C%202010%29%28ISBN%200521878187%29%28O%29%28498s%29_MPop_.pdf)).

# The system in detail

## 1. The signs

### Dot

**[Artefact]** A circular dot has the value one. Several dots are normally arranged in a horizontal row, although orientation changes with available space.

In words: a small filled circle or bead.

Unicode:

- 1: 𝋡
- 2: 𝋢
- 3: 𝋣
- 4: 𝋤

### Bar

**[Artefact]** A horizontal bar has the value five. Two or three bars are stacked for ten and fifteen. Dots are normally placed above the bars.

In words: a thick horizontal stroke, often rounded or decorated at its ends.

Unicode:

- 5: 𝋥
- 10: 𝋪
- 15: 𝋯

The internal construction is additive:

\[
17=5+5+5+1+1.
\]

It is therefore a **quinary-grouped additive digit notation embedded in a vigesimal positional or coefficient system**. Calling it “base five” is incorrect: five is a sub-grouping sign, not the principal radix.

### Zero and completion signs

**[Artefact]** Maya scribes used several graphic zeros:

- a shell- or seed-shaped sign, often drawn as an elongated oval with internal lobes;
- flower-like forms;
- a quatrefoil or completion form;
- a head variant, often with a hand or jaw-related diagnostic;
- elaborated signs incorporating the calendrical period being completed.

Unicode’s 𝋠 is a standardized modern character based on a common horizontal zero form, not a facsimile of every ancient zero.

**[Disputed interpretation]** Earlier epigraphers debated whether these signs meant arithmetical “zero,” “twenty,” or “completion.” Förstemann and Morley treated shell forms as zeros; Goodman and Maudslay sometimes read the relevant sign as twenty; Gordon, Spinden, and Thompson emphasized completion. Modern epigraphy recognizes contextual overlap: the signs can function as zero coefficients/placeholders while their linguistic or ritual reading may express “completion,” “nothing,” or *mi(h)*, depending on glyph and context. The equation “every shell-shaped sign was pronounced zero” is therefore too simple. The history of this debate is surveyed in Signa L. Cook’s “Maya Concepts of Zero” and can already be seen in Thompson’s 1950 synthesis ([Cook](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/267958689_51_Maya_Concepts_of_Zero); [Thompson](https://www.mesoweb.com/publications/Thompson/Thompson1950-text-hires.pdf)).

### Ordering within a digit

The normal horizontal form places dots above bars. A value of 13 is three dots over two bars, not an arbitrary collection of thirteen marks. In narrow glyph blocks, bars and dots can be rotated or rearranged.

**[Modern convention]** Unicode assigns one atomic character to each complete digit 0–19. Ancient scribes did not type an atomic “seventeen”: 𝋱 is a digital encoding of the composite dot-and-bar configuration.

## 3. Pure vigesimal place value

In ordinary mathematical description, positions ascend upward:

```text
top        coefficient × 20²
middle     coefficient × 20¹
bottom     coefficient × 20⁰
```

Thus:

```text
𝋡     1 × 400
𝋡     1 × 20
𝋩     9 × 1
```

equals 429.

The value is

\[
1(400)+1(20)+9=429.
\]

Blank space alone is unsafe because it can collapse the distinction between, for example, one unit and one twenty. A zero sign makes the empty position explicit.

## 4. Long Count place values

In the standard five-position chronology:

| Position | Conventional name | Composition | Days | Approximate solar duration |
|---|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 | k’in | basic day | 1 | 1 day |
| 2 | winal/uinal | 20 k’in | 20 | 20 days |
| 3 | tun | 18 winal | 360 | 0.986 year |
| 4 | k’atun | 20 tun | 7,200 | 19.71 years |
| 5 | b’ak’tun | 20 k’atun | 144,000 | 394.26 years |
| 6 | piktun | 20 b’ak’tun | 2,880,000 | c. 7,885 years |
| 7 | kalabtun | 20 piktun | 57,600,000 | c. 157,700 years |
| 8 | k’inchiltun | 20 kalabtun | 1,152,000,000 | c. 3.15 million years |
| 9 | alautun | 20 k’inchiltun | 23,040,000,000 | c. 63.1 million years |

The five conventional fields are written:

\[
b’ak’tun.k’atun.tun.winal.k’in.
\]

For example:

\[
9.12.11.5.18 =
9(144{,}000)+12(7{,}200)+11(360)+5(20)+18
=1{,}386{,}478\text{ days}.
\]

### Is the system “base 18”?

No. Only one regrouping is by eighteen:

\[
18\times20=360.
\]

All higher regroupings are by twenty. “Modified vigesimal,” “mixed-radix,” or “vigesimal tun count with subdivisions” is more exact than either “base 18” or unqualified “base 20.”

### Why the exception?

**[Reconstruction]** The obvious functional explanation is calendrical: 18 months of 20 days give a 360-day computing year close to the 365-day haab’. This is strongly supported by the system’s organization but is not an ancient Maya author’s explicit explanation.

**[Modern embellishment]** Claims that the Maya selected 360 because they knew an exact tropical year of 365.2422 days, or designed the exception to encode a modern astronomical constant, require independent inscriptional evidence and usually exceed what the sources demonstrate.

## 5. Long Count notation as coefficients plus period signs

Monumental dates commonly place a coefficient beside a glyph naming the period. A date could therefore be visually closer to:

```text
9 [B’AK’TUN]
12 [K’ATUN]
11 [TUN]
5 [WINAL]
18 [K’IN]
```

than to a bare modern string `9.12.11.5.18`.

The date was often preceded by an Initial Series Introductory Glyph and followed by:

- the corresponding 260-day Tzolk’in date;
- lunar information in a Supplementary Series;
- the 365-day haab’ position;
- sometimes a nine-day “Lord of the Night” coefficient.

This redundancy let scribes and modern epigraphers cross-check damaged coefficients.

## 6. Head variants, full figures, ligatures, and abbreviation

### Head variants

**[Artefact]** Each number 0–19 could be written as a deity-like or personified head. Distinguishing features—such as particular jaws, forehead elements, headdresses, or spots—identify the value. Goodman systematized their numerical values in the 1890s using Maudslay’s photographs and Annie Hunter’s drawings.

They are allographs, not spoken portraits or rebuses in every occurrence. Head numeral 10, for example, signifies the same coefficient as two bars.

### Full-figure numerals

**[Artefact]** Especially elaborate inscriptions can represent a numeral as a complete anthropomorphic figure whose head carries the identifying traits of its head variant. These are prestigious calligraphic alternatives, not a separate arithmetic.

### Compact and ligatured dates

**[Artefact]** Scribes sometimes:

- fused a coefficient with the period glyph;
- substituted the appropriate numbered head for an ordinary bar-and-dot coefficient;
- omitted predictable period glyphs;
- recorded period endings in abbreviated forms;
- used Calendar Round or Short Count expressions rather than a complete five-place Long Count.

A “zero tun” can be expressed through a completion construction rather than by mechanically placing an independent shell before a separate tun sign. Consequently, visual transcription into `0` conceals glyphic morphology.

### Vertical and horizontal orientation

Codices commonly arrange place-value numbers vertically with the lowest value at the bottom. Monumental Long Counts usually follow the two-column Maya reading order: paired blocks from left to right, then downward. Bar-and-dot forms can rotate to fit a glyph block.

### The same number can differ in a Long Count

Pure-vigesimal 400 is:

```text
𝋡
𝋠
𝋠
```

But a Long Count interval of 400 days is:

```text
1 tun, 2 winal, 0 k’in
```

because \(360+40=400\). This is the most important practical warning when converting modern numbers into “Maya numerals.”

## 8. Famous numbers worked out

### 13.0.0.0.0: completion of thirteen b’ak’tuns

```text
𝋭  b’ak’tun
𝋠  k’atun
𝋠  tun
𝋠  winal
𝋠  k’in
```

\[
13×144{,}000=1{,}872{,}000\text{ days}.
\]

In the dominant GMT correlation, its modern recurrence fell on 21 December 2012.

### Tres Zapotes Stela C: 7.16.6.16.18

```text
𝋧  b’ak’tun
𝋰  k’atun
𝋦  tun
𝋰  winal
𝋲  k’in
```

\[
7(144{,}000)+16(7{,}200)+6(360)+16(20)+18
=1{,}125{,}698\text{ days}.
\]

It corresponds approximately to early September 32 BCE, depending on correlation. The official Mexican catalogue gives 3 September 32 BCE ([Museo Nacional de Antropología](https://mna.inah.gob.mx/detalle_pieza_mes.php?id=353)).

### Tikal Stela 29: 8.12.14.8.15

```text
𝋨  b’ak’tun
𝋬  k’atun
𝋮  tun
𝋨  winal
𝋯  k’in
```

This dates to AD 292 under GMT and was long considered the earliest secure Long Count in the Maya lowlands. In 2026, Stela 46 at El Palmar displaced it by more than a century ([Penn Museum account of Tikal](https://www.penn.museum/sites/expedition/maya-long-count-numbers/); [El Palmar study](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0956536126100984)).

### Dresden Venus supernumber: 1,366,560 days

As a Long Count interval:

\[
9.9.16.0.0
\]

because

\[
9(144{,}000)+9(7{,}200)+16(360)=1{,}366{,}560.
\]

```text
𝋩  b’ak’tun
𝋩  k’atun
𝋰  tun
𝋠  winal
𝋠  k’in
```

It is divisible by 260, 365, 584, 2,920, and 37,960, interlocking the ritual calendar, haab’, canonical Venus synodic period, five-Venus cycle, and the full Venus table ([Šprajc and Sánchez Nava discussion](https://www.scielo.org.mx/scielo.php?lng=es&nrm=iso&pid=S0185-25742020000200095&script=sci_arttext_plus&tlng=en)).

### Pakal’s death: 9.12.11.5.18

The Palenque record associates this Long Count with 6 Etz’nab 11 Yax and the death of K’inich Janaab’ Pakal in AD 683:

```text
𝋩  b’ak’tun
𝋬  k’atun
𝋫  tun
𝋥  winal
𝋲  k’in
```

## 9. Fractions

**[Absence of evidence]** No generally accepted Maya notation comparable to modern vulgar fractions `½`, `⅓`, or a fraction bar has been demonstrated in the surviving corpus.

Several distinctions matter:

- Winal and k’in can be treated as subdivisions—or in Thompson’s language, “fractions”—of the 360-day tun. That does not make them written rational fractions.
- Calendar and astronomical tables express ratios through integer cycles, repeated addition, common multiples, and correction intervals.
- Linguistic expressions for halves or portions undoubtedly existed in Maya languages, but this is not evidence for a formal written fraction calculus.
- Claims that particular shell details or dots beneath bars encode decimal points or fractional places are **[modern invention or unsupported speculation]** unless tied to a specific deciphered text.

## 10. Largest expressible numbers

Mathematically, a positional vigesimal notation is unbounded if more positions can be added.

**[Documented text]** Diego de Landa wrote in the sixteenth century that the Yucatec count continued from 8,000 to 160,000 and repeatedly by twenties “until they reach an uncountable figure,” and that counting was done “on the ground or a flat surface” ([Landa, section XXIV](https://sacred-texts.com/nam/maya/ybac/ybac28.htm)).

**[Artefact]** Maya monuments and the Dresden Codex contain expanded calculations extending far beyond the ordinary five-place Long Count. Thompson reported a correctly handled date more than 500 million years into the mythic past.

**[Terminological caution]** The names *piktun*, *kalabtun*, *k’inchiltun*, and *alautun* are conventional epigraphic labels transmitted through colonial lexical sources and early scholarship; their exact ancient readings and morphology are not equally secure. The Classic Maya Dictionary explicitly calls *baktun* a fictive conventional term rather than a securely attested Classic word formed with a root *ba-* “400” ([Calendar Unit Overview](https://mayaglyphs.org/CWLhtml/%40CALENDAR%20UNIT%20OVERVIEW%40logo.html)).

There is no largest value inherent in the system, although a particular inscription, manuscript page, or conventional series has a finite highest place.

# Origins: dated and placed

## Before bar-and-dot numeration

No evidence connects Maya numerals historically with the Lebombo or Ishango bones, Mesopotamian tokens, Egyptian numerals, Babylonian place value, Chinese rods, or Indian zero.

- **[Artefact, unrelated]** Lebombo and Ishango are marked bones from Africa interpreted as tallies or patterned marks.
- **[Artefact, unrelated]** Near Eastern tokens and Uruk numerical tablets belong to independent Mesopotamian accounting traditions.
- **[Artefact, unrelated]** Narmer-period Egyptian numerical signs are decimal and non-positional.
- **[Artefact, unrelated]** The Bakhshali manuscript, Brahmi numerals, Gwalior zero, Chinese rods, Roman abaci, quipu, suanpan, and soroban belong to separate Old World or Andean histories.
- **[Absence of evidence]** No credible archaeological route carries any of those notations into pre-Columbian Mesoamerica.

Their inclusion in universal histories of zero provides comparison, not genealogy. Diffusionist claims invoking Phoenicians, Egyptians, Hindus, Chinese voyagers, Atlantis, or extraterrestrials are **[legend/pseudohistory]**.

## Formative-period precursors

### San José Mogote Monument 3, Oaxaca, c. 600–500 BCE

**[Artefact]** A Zapotec monument traditionally read as recording the calendrical name “1 Earthquake,” with a dot coefficient. Chrisomalis treats it as the earliest well-attested Zapotec numeral, while the identification of the accompanying glyph and exact chronology rests on archaeological interpretation.

This is a single-dot calendrical coefficient, not yet a demonstrated positional Long Count.

### Monte Albán Stela 12, Oaxaca

**[Artefact]** Often cited as an early combined bar-and-dot expression, including a bar plus dots. Its significance is that the quinary bar-and-dot convention existed outside Maya writing.

### San Andrés and Olmec-region evidence

**[Disputed]** Incised objects from San Andrés, Tabasco, around the Middle Formative have been proposed as early writing and calendrical notation. Whether individual marks are numerals and whether the script should be called Olmec remain debated.

### La Blanca figurine, Guatemala, c. 700–500 BCE

**[Disputed, newly published]** A Middle Preclassic figurine bears incisions that investigators propose may be an early numerical or day-name expression. The marks’ intentional numerical status is plausible but not certain. The evidence strengthens a picture of broadly shared early Mesoamerican numerical conventions rather than a simple one-way invention by a single ethnic group ([Latin American Antiquity study](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/latin-american-antiquity/article/numbers-and-bodies-potential-early-numeration-on-a-middle-preclassic-figurine-from-la-blanca-guatemala/8CA5E11708F80ABBBD7117697E0CB304)).

## Who invented bar-and-dot notation?

**[Reconstruction]** The system was indigenous to Mesoamerica and circulated among multilingual scribal communities. Bar-and-dot numerals occur in Zapotec, Isthmian/Epi-Olmec, Maya, and later central Mexican traditions.

**[Disputed]** Older “mother culture” models credited the Olmec; other scholars argued for Zapotec priority. Chrisomalis notes that the earliest securely recognized numeral is Zapotec but remains agnostic about whether the mature bar-and-dot system was invented by Olmec- or Zapotec-associated communities. Archaeological priority of an object is not necessarily proof of invention.

A recent historical synthesis concludes that the early origin, the quinary-vigesimal structure, and increasing use over time favor local Mesoamerican development. Pan-regional borrowing makes a unique ethnic inventor presently unrecoverable.

## Earliest surviving Long Count inscriptions

### Chiapa de Corzo Stela 2

- **Place:** Chiapa de Corzo, Chiapas, Mexico.
- **Object:** Limestone wall-panel fragment conventionally called Stela 2.
- **Long Count:** `7.16.3.2.13`.
- **Converted date:** December 36 BCE under a common GMT correlation; some exhibition material says 37 BCE.
- **Repository:** Museo Regional de Chiapas, Tuxtla Gutiérrez.
- **Catalogue/exhibition numbers:** `10-342633`; former exhibition number `2414.117`.
- **Status:** **[Artefact; reading substantially accepted, cultural attribution qualified].**

The surviving inscription is the earliest presently known complete or reconstructable Long Count date. The object is not unambiguously Maya: Chiapa de Corzo is generally associated with Zoque culture and Isthmian interaction. Calling it “the earliest Maya date” confuses the history of a shared Mesoamerican chronological technology with later Maya florescence ([LACMA/Olmec exhibition catalogue](https://www-images.lacma.org/s3fs-public/module-uploads/E4E_Olmec.pdf)).

### Tres Zapotes Stela C

- **Place:** Tres Zapotes, Veracruz, Mexico.
- **Material:** Basalt.
- **Long Count:** `7.16.6.16.18`.
- **Converted date:** early September 32 BCE.
- **Discovery:** lower half found by Matthew W. Stirling in 1939; upper fragment found locally in 1969.
- **Repositories:** portions associated with the Museo Nacional de Antropología and Museo de Sitio de Tres Zapotes; the upper fragment is known as the Covarrubias Stela.
- **Status:** **[Artefact; date secure after restoration of the missing upper coefficient].**

When Stirling proposed that the damaged date began with 7 rather than 1, it placed the monument before the Common Era—an implication many specialists initially resisted because it upset established chronology. The 1969 discovery of the upper half preserved the coefficient seven and vindicated the earlier reading. The monument is conventionally Epi-Olmec/Isthmian rather than Maya ([official MNA catalogue](https://mna.inah.gob.mx/detalle_pieza_mes.php?id=353); [INAH site-museum guide](https://lugares.inah.gob.mx/images/museos/guias/18_MS_TresZapotes_2022_US.pdf)).

### Other early non-lowland or frontier dates

Late Preclassic Long Counts occur at:

- El Baúl, Guatemala;
- Takalik Abaj, Guatemala;
- La Mojarra, Veracruz;
- the Tuxtla Statuette;
- other Pacific-slope and Isthmian monuments.

Several are damaged or controversial. La Mojarra Stela 1 carries two substantial Isthmian-script dates, approximately AD 143 and 156 under the GMT family of correlations ([George Stuart, *La Estela 1 de La Mojarra*](https://www.mesoweb.com/bearc/cmr/RRAMW16.pdf)).

### Earliest securely Maya lowland dates

Until 2026, Tikal Stela 29, `8.12.14.8.15` = AD 292, was the standard earliest secure Maya-lowland Long Count.

**[New artefact reading; partly reconstructed]** High-resolution scanning of El Palmar Stela 46 in Campeche has produced a probable date `8.7.1.0.0 4 Ajaw 8 Sotz`, corresponding to 31 August AD 180. The final two coefficients are flower-like zeros, but erosion means the authors do not entirely exclude alternatives such as `8.7.0.5.0`. It is now the best candidate for the earliest Long Count in the central Maya lowlands, 112 years earlier than Tikal Stela 29 ([Tsukamoto et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0956536126100984); [INAH announcement](https://inah.gob.mx/boletines/monumento-de-el-palmar-en-campeche-revela-la-fecha-de-cuenta-larga-mas-antigua-en-las-tierras-bajas-mayas)).

This distinction resolves an apparent contradiction:

- Chiapa de Corzo, 36 BCE: earliest known Long Count overall.
- El Palmar, AD 180: earliest presently proposed in the Maya lowlands.
- Tikal, AD 292: former earliest securely legible Maya-lowland example.

## Origin of the Long Count and zero

**[Reconstruction]** The distribution of the earliest monuments suggests that the Long Count developed in the interaction zone linking the Gulf Coast, Isthmus of Tehuantepec, Chiapas, Oaxaca, the Pacific slope, and early Maya communities.

**[Disputed]** It has been called:

- an Olmec invention;
- an Epi-Olmec/Isthmian invention;
- a Maya invention transmitted westward;
- a shared product of cross-cultural scribal networks.

The oldest surviving monuments postdate the main Olmec florescence, so “the Olmec invented the Long Count zero” is not demonstrated merely because Tres Zapotes lies in the Olmec heartland.

A recent mathematical reconstruction proposes an early **bijective** positional system using digits 1–20, followed by a transitional redundant system, and only later a canonical 0–19 notation. This is an ingenious model based on anomalous early coefficients and representational redundancy, not a preserved indigenous exposition ([Rojo-Garibaldi, Rangoni and Siehler](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8022160/)).

# Arithmetic and instruments

## What the evidence actually preserves

No Maya equivalent of the Rhind Mathematical Papyrus, *Nine Chapters*, Brahmagupta’s *Brāhmasphuṭasiddhānta*, al-Khwarizmi’s arithmetic, or Fibonacci’s *Liber Abaci* survives.

**[Absence of evidence]**

- No surviving Maya schoolbook systematically defines algorithms for addition, subtraction, multiplication, division, fractions, or roots.
- No securely identified Maya abacus survives.
- No surviving text tells us, step by step, how a Classic-period apprentice learned arithmetic.
- No named ancient Maya mathematician is securely attached to a general textbook.

What survives is applied computation embedded in calendrical, astronomical, dynastic, divinatory, and tribute-related material.

## Finger and body reckoning

**[Reconstruction]** Base twenty is commonly explained as counting fingers and toes. The explanation is typologically plausible and vigesimal number vocabularies are often body-linked.

**[Evidence qualification]** It is not established by a Classic Maya text saying “we use base twenty because humans have twenty digits.” Artistic hand signs and ethnographic counting practices make body reckoning probable, but the specific origin story remains reconstructed.

**[Modern linguistic evidence]** Contemporary Tseltal and other Maya communities preserve vigesimal structures in oral calculation, commercial transactions, and embroidery work, and describe finger-counting practices ([Solares Rojas study](https://psyjournals.ru/en/journals/chp/archive/2010_n4/32901)).

## Ground or flat-surface calculation

**[Documented text]** Landa’s report, composed around 1566 from colonial Yucatec information, states:

> “They do all their counting on the ground or a flat surface.”

This is the strongest direct evidence for a counting-board-like practice. It does not specify a standardized board, grid, bead material, or formal algorithm.

**[Reconstruction]** Counters may have been cacao beans, pebbles, kernels, or other small objects, moved through vigesimal columns. Such reconstructions are computationally workable and consistent with Landa, but illustrations of a canonical “Maya abacus” are generally modern.

## Addition and subtraction with bars and dots

A plausible written or counter-based addition algorithm is:

1. Combine dots and bars within corresponding places.
2. Exchange five dots for one bar.
3. Exchange four bars for one dot in the next vigesimal place.
4. In Long Count arithmetic, exchange 18 winal units for one tun; elsewhere exchange 20 lower units for one higher unit.

Example, \(17+8=25\):

```text
17 = three bars + two dots
 8 = one bar + three dots
combined = four bars + five dots
five dots → one bar
five bars = twenty-five
four bars → one dot in the twenties place,
leaving one bar in units
```

Result:

```text
𝋡
𝋥
```

that is \(1×20+5=25\).

**[Reconstruction]** This algorithm is natural from the notation and is widely used in modern teaching. Surviving ancient pages show the results of sophisticated calculations but do not provide this verbal classroom recipe.

Subtraction reverses the exchanges. Multiplication can be performed as repeated addition or by partial products; division as repeated subtraction or partition. Again, the precise ancient workflow is incompletely documented.

## Calendar arithmetic

The clearest surviving practice is addition and subtraction of day intervals.

A distance number could be added to a base Calendar Round or Long Count date. Because the 260-day and 365-day calendars operated simultaneously, the scribe updated:

- the Long Count;
- the Tzolk’in coefficient modulo 13;
- the sequence of 20 day names;
- the haab’ day and month;
- lunar or planetary positions where relevant.

The inscriptions sometimes supply both starting and resulting dates, making the arithmetic checkable. Morley’s early handbook worked through examples such as `4.8.3.1`, equal to 31,741 days in Long Count place values ([Morley, chapter 4](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/An_Introduction_to_the_Study_of_the_Maya_Hieroglyphs/Chapter_4)).

## Dresden Codex calculations

### The manuscript

**[Artefact]** The Dresden Codex is an accordion-fold manuscript of bark paper coated with a writing surface. It is conventionally catalogued in Dresden as **Mscr.Dresd.R.310**; Fleischer’s 1831 catalogue listed it as no. 451. It is a Postclassic manuscript, probably copied from older exemplars. It entered the Royal Library in Dresden in 1739 through Johann Christian Götze.

### Venus table

Pages 24 and 46–50 contain a Venus table organized around:

- canonical synodic period: 584 days;
- five synodic periods: \(5×584=2,920\) days;
- 65 synodic periods: \(65×584=37,960\) days;
- 104 haab’ years: \(104×365=37,960\);
- 146 Tzolk’in cycles: \(146×260=37,960\).

The table divides a canonical Venus cycle into phases whose traditional scholarly totals are 236, 90, 250, and 8 days, although interpretation of observational meaning and correction schemes has evolved.

**[Artefact plus reconstruction]** The integers are preserved; identifying sections as heliacal appearances, disappearances, and corrections is an astronomical reconstruction supported by pattern and iconography.

### Eclipse table

Pages 51–58 carry an eclipse warning table based on sequences of lunar intervals. The Dresden library describes it as predicting possible solar and lunar eclipses at intervals of five or six lunations. It is better understood as an eclipse-season table than as a list of guaranteed local observations ([SLUB content guide](https://www.slub-dresden.de/en/explore/manuscripts/the-dresden-maya-codex/content)).

### Serpent Series and huge numbers

Pages 61–69 contain enormous “Serpent Numbers” and mythic-era calculations. They show that Maya scribes could manipulate day counts spanning millions and, in some readings, hundreds of millions of years.

**[Disputed]** Astronomical identifications involving precession, planetary conjunctions, or specific ancient observations vary in evidentiary strength. The numerical strings are artefacts; the claimed physical phenomenon behind every string is not.

## Xultun tables

**[Artefact]** Numerical tables painted on a wall at Xultun, Guatemala, date to the early ninth century. They contain large calendrical multiples and demonstrate that computation also occurred on architectural working surfaces, not only in surviving codices.

**[Reconstruction]** The chamber is frequently described as a scribal workshop. That interpretation is strong but remains archaeological rather than stated in a caption.

## Commerce

**[Documented text]** Landa says the 20–400–8,000 sequence was much used “for the contracting of cacao.” This connects vigesimal arithmetic with commerce.

**[Absence of evidence]** No preserved Maya merchant ledger comparable to Old World account books survives. Claims about specific interest rates, market algorithms, or standardized cacao-bean abaci require caution.

## Comparison with other calculating traditions

Roman calculi and counter-casting, Chinese counting rods, the suanpan and Japanese soroban, Andean quipu and yupana, Indian dust-board arithmetic, and the European algorist–abacist contest illuminate how written numerals can coexist with manipulatives. They do not establish Maya borrowing.

The English words *calculus*, *digit*, *cipher*, *zero*, *algorithm*, and *abacus* descend from Latin, Indian-Arabic, and Mediterranean histories—not from Maya languages. No common English mathematical word is securely derived from Maya numeral terminology.

# Transmission, transformation, and replacement

## Within ancient Mesoamerica

**[Reconstruction]** Bar-and-dot notation spread across language boundaries through elite calendars, writing, ritual exchange, trade, and political display. Its wide occurrence is better modeled as a Mesoamerican scribal convention than as a uniquely Maya ethnic possession.

The Long Count was especially prominent in:

- Isthmian and Pacific-slope inscriptions during the Late Preclassic;
- Classic Maya royal monuments;
- codical astronomical computation.

## Classic-period political use

Long Count dates anchored accessions, births, deaths, warfare, captures, dedications, ancestor rituals, and period-ending ceremonies in linear time. They placed rulers within both dynastic history and mythic chronology.

This was not a neutral timestamping technology. The monument’s patron controlled labor, stone, public placement, ritual performance, and the authorized account of the past.

## Decline of monumental Long Counts

After the ninth-century political transformations conventionally called the Classic Maya collapse, many southern lowland centers ceased erecting dated stelae. Among the last known monumental Long Count dates are Toniná Monument 101, AD 909, and Itzimté Stela 6, AD 910.

This was not the “death of the Maya,” who remained numerous and culturally diverse. Nor did calendrical knowledge vanish. In many regions:

- Calendar Round dating continued;
- k’atun-based historical reckoning persisted;
- Postclassic codices preserved bar-and-dot arithmetic;
- colonial Books of Chilam Balam recorded k’atun sequences in alphabetic Maya.

## Conquest and manuscript destruction

**[Documented text]** Landa reported that missionaries found books containing what they judged “superstitions and lies of the devil” and burned them, causing the Maya profound grief.

**[Documented event]** The Maní inquisition and auto-da-fé of 1562 destroyed books and ritual objects and involved coercive interrogations and punishments.

**[Qualification]** The often repeated exact statement that Landa personally burned “27 codices and 5,000 idols” is derived through colonial reports and later summaries; quantities and categories vary. The destruction at Maní was catastrophic, but it was not necessarily the sole cause of manuscript loss. Tropical preservation, warfare, earlier destruction, missionary campaigns elsewhere, concealment, and ordinary decay also contributed.

Four pre-Columbian or early-contact Maya screenfolds are now generally accepted:

- Dresden Codex;
- Madrid Codex;
- Paris Codex;
- Maya Codex of Mexico, formerly called the Grolier Codex.

The Madrid museum still describes its codex alongside Dresden and Paris as one of three traditional surviving originals; the authenticated Maya Codex of Mexico makes four under current scholarship ([Museo de América](https://www.cultura.gob.es/museodeamerica/en/coleccion/america-prehispanica/c-dice-trocortesiano.html); [INAH Maya Codex of Mexico](https://www.codicemayademexico.inah.gob.mx/)).

## Colonial replacement

Alphabetic writing in Latin script and European/Arabic numerals increasingly replaced hieroglyphic notation in administration, church life, taxation, and commerce. Roman numerals entered some ecclesiastical contexts, but the decisive numerical replacement was the Hindu-Arabic decimal system carried by Spanish colonial institutions.

This was not “India–Baghdad–al-Andalus–Italy” transmission within Maya history. That route explains the numerals the Spaniards brought, not the origin of the displaced Mesoamerican system.

## Survival among Maya daykeepers

**[Tradition and documented modern practice]** Maya daykeepers, especially in Guatemala, continue the 260-day Chol Q’ij/Tzolk’in and related calendrical practices. Knowledge is transmitted through ritual office, community instruction, almanacs, and modern cultural organizations.

**[Qualification]** Continuous use of the five-place monumental Long Count is less securely demonstrated than continuous or revived use of the 260-day count. Modern daykeepers may calculate or display Long Count dates, particularly in pan-Maya cultural revival, but one should not conflate all contemporary Maya calendars with uninterrupted daily use of Classic monumental notation.

The Smithsonian’s *Living Maya Time* project documents present-day Maya calendrical authorities and explicitly rejects treating Maya civilization as extinct ([Smithsonian calendar guide](https://maya.nmai.si.edu/calendar/calendar-system)).

## Modern public use

### Guatemalan banknotes

**[Documented modern law]** Guatemalan currency legislation requires denominations to appear in both Arabic and Maya numeral systems. The banknotes therefore provide an official modern civic use of bar-and-dot numerals ([Banco de Guatemala](https://banguat.gob.gt/page/la-legislacion-sobre-billetes-y-monedas-1)).

### Education and identity

Maya numerals appear in:

- Guatemalan and Mexican school materials;
- Indigenous-language publications;
- university and cultural-organization emblems;
- monuments and public artworks;
- calendars, jewelry, tattoos, logos, and digital converters.

These uses range from Indigenous revitalization to tourism and commercial stylization. A vertically stacked modern birthday is not automatically an ancient calendrical inscription; its correct structure depends on whether the maker intends pure vigesimal notation or a Long Count.

### Unicode

The numerals were encoded in Unicode at U+1D2E0–U+1D2F3 following a proposal emphasizing both historical use and modern publishing, including Guatemalan banknotes and Maya-language materials. Unicode standardizes glyph identities but does not settle every epigraphic allograph or provide the full logosyllabic script.

# People

## Indigenous scribes, rulers, and daykeepers

The overwhelming majority of ancient calculators and scribes remain unnamed. Their work survives on monuments, ceramics, walls, and codices, often commissioned by rulers whose names are known.

### K’inich Janaab’ Pakal, 603–683

Palenque ruler whose life, death, ancestry, and mythic historical framework are preserved in elaborate dated inscriptions, especially the Temple of the Inscriptions. The astronomical and calendrical computations were almost certainly the work of trained scribes attached to court institutions.

### Bahlam Ajaw of Tortuguero, seventh century

His Monument 6 contains the much-publicized reference to the completion of the thirteenth b’ak’tun in 2012. The monument primarily concerns the ruler’s historical and ritual world; it is not a freestanding doomsday prophecy.

### Unnamed Xultun calculators

Painted tables on the walls at Xultun preserve the work of scribes or calendar specialists. Recent scholarship sometimes attempts to isolate individual hands or mathematical authors, but personal names remain uncertain.

## Colonial witnesses

### Diego de Landa, 1524–1579

Franciscan friar and later bishop of Yucatán. He was involved in the violent suppression of Maya religious practice and the destruction of books at Maní, yet his *Relación de las cosas de Yucatán*, surviving in a later abridged copy, preserves indispensable information about counting, calendars, and glyphs.

This dual role is historical fact, not a paradox that cancels either side of the evidence.

### Maya informants and scribes

Landa’s information depended upon Maya speakers, including literate or knowledgeable assistants, but the surviving text does not consistently credit them. Treating the *Relación* as Landa’s solitary discovery reproduces the colonial erasure of its Indigenous sources.

## Rediscovery and decipherment

### Johann Christian Götze, 1692–1749

Director of the Royal Library in Dresden who acquired the Dresden Codex in Vienna in 1739. Its path from Mesoamerica to Europe before that date is undocumented.

### Charles Étienne Brasseur de Bourbourg, 1814–1874

Found and published the surviving manuscript of Landa’s *Relación* in the nineteenth century. His work restored crucial data to scholarship, although some of his broader historical speculations were unreliable.

### Ernst Wilhelm Förstemann, 1822–1906

Dresden librarian who made foundational breakthroughs in the numerical, calendrical, and astronomical interpretation of the Dresden Codex. In 1886 he published results identifying its number system, calendar structure, and large computations. Later work clarified the Venus and eclipse tables ([SLUB decipherment history](https://www.slub-dresden.de/en/explore/manuscripts/the-dresden-maya-codex/deciphering)).

### Alfred Percival Maudslay, 1850–1931

Produced exceptionally important photographs, casts, plans, and publications of Maya monuments in the *Biologia Centrali-Americana*. His records made comparative study possible far from the sites.

### Annie Hunter, 1860–1927

Prepared precise drawings from Maudslay’s photographs and casts. Her visual scholarship was essential to Goodman and later epigraphers but was often subordinated to male authorship in older histories.

### Joseph T. Goodman, 1838–1917

Using monumental evidence, independently reconstructed much of Long Count chronology and recognized numeral head variants. His *The Archaic Maya Inscriptions* appeared in 1897. He also proposed a calendar correlation later modified by Juan Martínez Hernández and J. Eric S. Thompson.

### Juan Martínez Hernández, 1866–1959

Mexican scholar who revised Goodman’s correlation in 1926.

### J. Eric S. Thompson, 1898–1975

Systematized calendrical epigraphy and the GMT correlation. His catalogues and 1950 *Maya Hieroglyphic Writing* remain indispensable historiography.

Thompson also resisted phonetic decipherment and promoted an overly calendrical, priestly, and nonhistorical view of Maya texts. His authority delayed acceptance of Knorozov’s approach.

### Yuri Knorozov, 1922–1999

Published his decisive phonetic analysis in 1952. Working from reproductions and Landa’s so-called alphabet, he recognized that Landa’s signs usually represented syllabic values rather than alphabetic letters.

### The Knorozov controversy

**[Documented scholarly dispute]**

- Knorozov argued that Maya writing was logosyllabic and used phonetic complementation.
- Thompson attacked the method, partly within Cold War intellectual politics and partly because it contradicted his own model.
- Later readings by Tatiana Proskouriakoff, Heinrich Berlin, David Kelley, Michael Coe, Linda Schele, Peter Mathews, Floyd Lounsbury, Nikolai Grube, David Stuart, and others confirmed the central phonetic principle.
- Knorozov did not single-handedly decipher every glyph; modern decipherment combined his phonetic insight with calendrical work, structural analysis, dynastic history, archaeology, and Mayan linguistics.

PBS’s formulation that Knorozov proposed signs “stood for phonetic sounds, much like English letters” is pedagogically useful but imprecise: the basic phonetic units were generally syllabic, not alphabetic ([PBS timeline](https://www.pbs.org/wgbh/nova/mayacode/time-06.html)).

# Culture

## Calendar and cosmology

The Long Count linked linear elapsed time to two repeating calendars:

- Tzolk’in: 13 coefficients × 20 named days = 260 days;
- haab’: 18 months × 20 days, plus five Wayeb’ days = 365 days.

The combined Calendar Round repeats after:

\[
\operatorname{lcm}(260,365)=18{,}980\text{ days}=52\text{ haab’ years}.
\]

The Long Count distinguished events more than 52 years apart and placed historical reigns inside a far larger sacred chronology.

## Era date

Classic texts place the current creation-era base at:

`13.0.0.0.0 4 Ajaw 8 Kumk’u`

conventionally correlated with 11 or 13 August 3114 BCE, depending on correlation and Gregorian/Julian convention.

**[Tradition recorded in artefacts]** This is a mythological era anchor, not an archaeological claim that Maya civilization or humanity began in 3114 BCE.

## Period endings

Round-number endings—especially tun, k’atun, and b’ak’tun completions—were occasions for:

- monument dedication;
- incense scattering;
- wrapping or raising stones;
- offerings;
- accession and legitimacy statements;
- connections between rulers and divine beings.

Zero therefore had cultural force as completion and renewal, not only “nothing.”

## Law, genealogy, and political memory

Long Count dates appear in narratives of:

- accessions and royal descent;
- warfare and capture;
- tribute and alliance;
- deaths, burials, and ancestor rites;
- building dedication.

The date authenticated a political account by embedding it in cosmic order. No surviving Maya statutory law code uses the Long Count in the manner of a Roman consular date or modern gazette, but dated monuments publicly asserted rights, succession, and legitimate rule.

## Astronomy and divination

The Dresden Venus and eclipse tables were not modern secular astronomy. Numerical regularities were integrated with deities, auguries, warfare imagery, sacrifice, and ritual timing.

Likewise, an eclipse table identifies intervals of danger or ritual concern; it is not simply an observatory log.

## Coins and money

Pre-Columbian Maya economies used commodities, tribute goods, cacao, textiles, valuables, and market exchange rather than a general mint-based coinage system. Consequently:

- **[Absence of evidence]** there are no ancient Maya coins bearing Long Count mint dates;
- **[Modern documented use]** Guatemalan banknotes display Maya numeral denominations.

## Literature

Colonial Books of Chilam Balam preserve Maya historical and calendrical knowledge in Latin alphabet manuscripts. They use k’atun cycles and retrospective chronicles but are not simple copies of Classic carved Long Counts.

Modern literature transformed the calendar into themes of cyclical time, lost wisdom, apocalypse, and extraterrestrial intervention. These representations often say more about twentieth-century Euro-American esotericism than ancient Maya texts.

## Art and typography

Ancient numeral typography ranged from spare dots and bars to elaborate deity heads and full figures. Choice of form could communicate:

- available space;
- medium;
- scribal virtuosity;
- monument prestige;
- iconographic associations of particular numbers.

Unicode’s clean horizontal digits are **[modern typographic normalization]**. Likewise, computer fonts that render every dot as an identical circle and every bar as a ruler-straight rectangle suppress historical calligraphy.

“Old-style figures” in European typography have no historical connection to Maya numerals.

## Gematria, isopsephy, and chronograms

Hebrew gematria, Greek isopsephy, Arabic abjad numerals, and European chronograms assign numerical values to alphabetic letters. Maya numeral signs do not operate on that principle.

**[Absence of evidence]** There is no demonstrated Maya system in which every ordinary syllabogram or logogram has a fixed numerical value and words are totaled like gematria.

Some glyphs combine numerical and phonetic readings, and day names are numbered, but that is not equivalent to alphabetic numerology.

# Controversies and disputes

## 1. Who invented zero?

### What can be said securely

- **[Artefact]** Babylonians used placeholder signs in sexagesimal notation, ultimately including an internal placeholder, but did not normally treat it as a freely operating cardinal number with modern rules.
- **[Artefact]** Mesoamerican scribes independently used zero/completion coefficients in calendrical position.
- **[Text and artefact]** Indian mathematics developed the decimal place-value zero and explicit arithmetic rules that entered the Islamic and European traditions.
- **[Conclusion]** There is no single adequate answer unless “zero” is defined: empty place, written placeholder, number, arithmetic operator, or philosophical concept.

### Maya priority

The Maya—or more cautiously, Mesoamerican scribal communities—developed their zero independently of the Old World. The earliest secure Long Count dates require empty positions conceptually, but early inscriptions do not always preserve a shell-shaped zero.

The claim “the Maya invented zero in 36 BCE” overstates the evidence:

- Chiapa de Corzo Stela 2 contains a Long Count date;
- its cultural identity is not securely Maya;
- the earliest date of a surviving notation is not necessarily the date of invention;
- zero signs and completion expressions evolved in multiple graphic forms.

## 2. Was Maya zero a true number?

Three levels should be distinguished:

1. **Placeholder:** marks an empty coefficient.
2. **Ordinal/completion state:** no units of the new period have elapsed.
3. **Cardinal zero:** participates in arithmetic as a quantity.

The corpus securely demonstrates the first two. Large calculations and interval arithmetic support the third in practice, but no preserved Maya treatise gives algebraic rules such as Brahmagupta’s operations with zero.

Thus “only a placeholder” is too dismissive, while “the Maya formulated modern zero theory” is unsupported.

## 3. Did the Maya invent positional notation before India?

**[Disputed framing]** Mesoamerican positional notation is independent and early. But asking which civilization “first invented place value” collapses unlike systems:

- Babylonian sexagesimal place value;
- Chinese rod place value;
- Maya mixed-radix calendrical notation;
- Indian decimal notation with zero.

Chronology depends on which features count. The Maya achievement does not need global priority to be significant.

## 4. Olmec versus Zapotec versus Maya origin

Evidence for each position:

- **Olmec case:** early symbols and calendrical practices in the Gulf region; later Long Counts in an Olmec heartland.
- **Zapotec case:** some of the earliest securely identified numeral coefficients and bar-and-dot forms are from Oaxaca.
- **Maya case:** the richest positional, zero-bearing, and astronomical corpus is Maya.
- **Network model:** early Mesoamerica was multilingual and interconnected; separate communities could contribute different components.

Present evidence favors shared development and borrowing over an identifiable lone inventor.

## 5. Is the Long Count purely positional?

Many introductions say yes. Chrisomalis and earlier specialists such as Michael Closs emphasize that monumental dates pair coefficients with named units and that the tun-centered structure is not equivalent to a bare numeral with place multipliers \(1,20,360,7200\).

The safest classification is:

- bar-and-dot digit: additive;
- pure stacked number: ciphered-positional base 20;
- Long Count date: mixed-radix and often multiplicative-additive through explicit unit glyphs;
- compact Long Count transcription: functionally positional for calculation.

## 6. Why thirteen b’ak’tuns?

Many Classic inscriptions write the era base with 13 b’ak’tuns, and 2012 completed thirteen from that base. But inscriptions also contemplate higher periods and dates beyond 2012.

**[Disputed]** Whether a grand cycle inherently contains 13 or 20 b’ak’tuns depends upon textual context and scholarly model. Palenque material has been used to argue that the piktun behaves as 20 b’ak’tuns. Thirteen therefore has ritual and era-structuring importance without making the numeral system terminate at 13.

## 7. The 2012 “end of the world”

### Artefact

Tortuguero Monument 6 refers to the completion of the thirteenth b’ak’tun and an event involving the deity Bolon Yokte’. The monument survives in several fragments divided among museums and collections; Berthold Riese published an important reconstruction in 1978 ([Riese](https://www.revistas-filologicas.unam.mx/estudios-cultura-maya/index.php/ecm/article/view/513)).

La Corona Hieroglyphic Stairway 12 also mentions the 2012 period ending as a future anniversary anchoring a seventh-century event.

### What the texts do not say

They do not say:

- the world will be destroyed;
- time will stop;
- a planet will collide with Earth;
- the magnetic poles will reverse;
- extraterrestrials will return;
- a new biological consciousness will automatically begin.

The Smithsonian concludes that no ancient inscription predicts a catastrophe on 21 December 2012 ([NMAI, “Meaning of 2012”](https://maya.nmai.si.edu/2012-resetting-count/meaning-of-2012)).

### Growth of the legend

**[Modern invention]** The apocalypse narrative emerged through a combination of:

- Western millenarian expectations;
- New Age cycle theories;
- selective readings of Aztec “Five Suns” mythology;
- archaeoastronomical speculation;
- José Argüelles’s *The Mayan Factor*;
- Frank Waters’s *Mexico Mystique*;
- popular occult publishing;
- internet circulation;
- films such as Roland Emmerich’s *2012*.

UNAM researchers specifically identify Waters as an important popularizer of the nonexistent prophecy ([UNAM statement](https://www.dgcs.unam.mx/boletin/bdboletin/2012_140.html)).

### Indigenous responses

Contemporary Maya speakers and daykeepers expressed a range of views—celebration, renewal, political critique, indifference, or rejection of commercial appropriation. “The Maya believed…” cannot legitimately reduce those voices to one position.

## 8. Förstemann versus Goodman priority

Förstemann’s work on the Dresden Codex preceded Goodman’s mature monumental study. Goodman claimed independent discoveries and did make major advances, particularly with head numerals and monumental chronology. Thompson later argued that Goodman understated his likely access to published accounts of Förstemann.

The fair historical allocation is:

- Förstemann: codical number system, calendrics, astronomical cycles;
- Goodman: monumental Long Count structure, head variants, early correlation;
- Martínez Hernández and Thompson: subsequent correlation refinements.

## 9. Knorozov and Cold War mythology

Two myths distort the controversy:

- **Legend:** Knorozov deciphered Maya writing from scratch using only one book.
- **Legend:** Western scholars rejected him solely because he was Soviet.

He depended on published codices, Landa, earlier epigraphy, and linguistic reasoning. Cold War hostility mattered, but Thompson also had substantive—though ultimately mistaken—theoretical commitments. Knorozov’s central logosyllabic insight was vindicated through decades of collaborative work.

## 10. “Maya precision” myths

Popular claims often state that Maya astronomers calculated the modern tropical year or Venus period more accurately than contemporary observatories.

The codices unquestionably show sophisticated cycle arithmetic. But:

- canonical integer cycles are not identical with physical constants;
- table design may prioritize commensurability and ritual repeatability;
- accuracy depends on which interval is compared;
- numerical coincidence alone does not prove observational derivation.

The good evidence is impressive enough without manufactured decimal precision.

## 11. The shell zero as literal seashell

The zero often resembles a shell and has long been called the shell glyph.

**[Disputed iconographic description]** Some variants resemble an *Oliva* shell, others a flower, seed, hand, or completion sign. “Shell” is a useful visual label, not a proof that every zero sign depicts the same zoological object or carried one uniform spoken name.

## 12. Absence and “no zero”

A blank location can function as implicit absence, but blank space alone is ambiguous. Early notations may alternate between omission, a coefficient twenty, and explicit zero/completion signs.

Therefore:

- “The Maya always had zero from the invention of bar-and-dot notation” is unproved.
- “The Maya had no zero because it meant completion” is false as a description of function.
- “A numeral system cannot be positional without zero” is mathematically false; bijective systems are possible.

## 13. Museum-number problem

Many Mesoamerican monuments are catalogued principally by site and monument number rather than a universally published accession number.

Known examples:

- Chiapa de Corzo Stela 2: Museo Regional de Chiapas `10-342633`, ex. `2414.117`.
- Dresden Codex: SLUB Dresden `Mscr.Dresd.R.310`; Fleischer catalogue no. 451.

# Open questions

1. Where, when, and in which linguistic community did the mature bar-and-dot convention first arise?
2. Did positional notation emerge before the explicit zero sign through a bijective 1–20 system?
3. What were the exact Classic Maya readings of all zero and completion allographs?
4. How should the b’ak’tun and higher period glyphs be transliterated without perpetuating early scholarly inventions?
5. Did all scribal schools use identical exchange rules in non-calendrical arithmetic?
6. What physical counters or boards accompanied written calculation?
7. How much everyday accounting existed on perishable media now lost?
8. Were surviving codical astronomical tables primarily predictive, retrospective, ritual, or intentionally all three?
9. How were corrections to Venus and eclipse schemes selected in practice?
10. Which claimed astronomical alignments are intentional rather than numerical coincidence?
11. How should scholars distinguish continuous Indigenous calendrical transmission from twentieth- and twenty-first-century reconstruction?
12. Will future imaging recover additional coefficients on damaged early monuments such as El Palmar Stela 46?
13. Are still-undeciphered signs on Formative monuments numerical, calendrical, linguistic, or decorative?
14. How much of the apparent transition from Long Count to shorter counts reflects real abandonment, and how much reflects loss of perishable books?
15. Which individual scribes or mathematical specialists can eventually be identified by signature, title, handwriting, or archaeological context?

# Assessment of frequently cited general works

## Chrisomalis

Stephen Chrisomalis’s *Numerical Notation: A Comparative History* (Cambridge University Press, 2010) is the strongest comparative starting point used here. Its virtue is structural precision: it separates additive signs, ciphered digits, multiplicative unit notation, and place value, and refuses to infer invention directly from an artefact’s ethnic label.

## Menninger

Karl Menninger’s *Zahlwort und Ziffer*—English translation *Number Words and Number Symbols*—remains a wide-ranging illustrated classic. It is valuable for comparative vocabulary, body counting, and graphic forms, but many historical claims predate modern Mesoamerican epigraphy and should be checked against later specialist work.

Edition consulted bibliographically: Karl Menninger, *Number Words and Number Symbols: A Cultural History of Numbers*, translated by Paul Broneer, Cambridge, MA: MIT Press, 1969; reprints by Dover.

## Ifrah

Georges Ifrah’s *Histoire universelle des chiffres* and English *Universal History of Numbers* are visually rich and unusually broad. They preserve comparative examples neglected elsewhere.

They are not reliable as a final authority on priority or diffusion. Joseph Dauben’s detailed review documents misdating, mistranslation, uncritical repetition, and conclusions stronger than the cited scholarship permits. Dauben also criticizes editorial problems in the English version ([AMS review](https://www.ams.org/notices/200202/rev-dauben.pdf)).

## Thompson and Morley

Thompson’s *Maya Hieroglyphic Writing* and Morley’s introductory manual are primary documents in the history of decipherment and still useful compilations. Their phonetic and cultural models are outdated. Numerical examples must be separated from their assumptions about a peaceful, priest-dominated, nonhistorical Maya civilization.

## Förstemann

Förstemann’s commentaries are foundational historical sources for the decipherment of Maya calendrical arithmetic. Later scholars have corrected readings, but his identification of numerical and astronomical structures remains a landmark.
