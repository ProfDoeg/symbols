# Anacaona: Research Dossier

> **Traditional class:** historically grounded mortal cacica of Xaraguá/Jaragua, later transformed into a martyred culture hero, Indigenous resistance heroine, poet-ancestor, and national or pan-Caribbean symbol. She is not securely attested as a goddess, demigod, canonized saint, or regularly worshipped lwa.
>
> **Earliest secure attestation:** Peter Martyr d’Anghiera’s Latin *De orbe novo*, First Decade, composed from reports received between the 1490s and 1510 and printed at Seville in 1511. It identifies Anacaona as the sister and influential co-ruler of Beuchius/Behechío, widow of Caunaboa/Caonabó, and participant in Bartolomé Colón’s reception at Xaraguá. The First Decade predates the surviving narratives of Oviedo and Las Casas, although Martyr was a court compiler, not an eyewitness in Hispaniola. [Peter Martyr’s text and publication history](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/12425/12425-h/12425-h.htm)

## Evidentiary key

- **[EA] Secure early attestation:** sixteenth-century testimony or documentary evidence, though not necessarily eyewitness.
- **[CE] Colonial elaboration:** later sixteenth- or seventeenth-century narrative dependent on testimony, institutional interests, or earlier chronicles.
- **[RV] Regional/literary variant:** a geographically or artistically specific reworking.
- **[LS] Learned systematization:** antiquarian, philological, or historiographic construction.
- **[F] Folklore or reported living tradition:** recorded as belief or practice but not evidence for the sixteenth century.
- **[MSR] Modern scholarly reconstruction:** inference from the surviving evidence.
- **[MI] Modern invention or unsupported popular claim.**

The labels assess the age and character of a claim, not whether its source was impartial. All surviving early narratives were produced inside the Spanish imperial world; none preserves Anacaona’s own words.

## 1. Identification, Names and Epithets

### Identification

**[EA]** Anacaona was a high-ranking Indigenous woman of late-fifteenth- and early-sixteenth-century Hispaniola. Peter Martyr, Gonzalo Fernández de Oviedo, Bartolomé de las Casas, and Diego Méndez consistently associate her with Xaraguá in the island’s southwest; call her the sister of its ruler Behechío; and identify her as the widow of Caonabó, ruler of Maguana. Martyr and Las Casas describe her as influential in her brother’s government. Oviedo says that after Caonabó’s removal she returned to Xaraguá and was obeyed there as a ruler. All three chroniclers attach exceptional political, rhetorical, or poetic ability to her.

**[EA/MSR]** This convergence makes her existence and high status substantially better documented than those of a purely mythical heroine. Her “heroic” dossier nevertheless consists largely of mediated colonial narratives and later literary transformations. There is no Indigenous manuscript, inscription, securely identified portrait, tomb, relic, or surviving composition by her.

### Name forms

The principal early forms are:

- **Anacaona** — Peter Martyr, Oviedo, and Las Casas.
- **Anacoana** — occasional early-modern transposition in Oviedo and later copies.
- **Nacaona / Naçaoana** — Diego Méndez’s testament as transmitted in Martín Fernández de Navarrete’s documentary collection; this may be a scribal or editorial form.
- **Anakaona** — modern Haitian, Dominican, and pan-Indigenous orthographic variant.
- **Ana Caona** — modern analytic division used to support the “flower + gold” interpretation.
- **Xaraguá / Jaraguá** — early Spanish spellings for her political territory; the initial *x* represents an older Spanish sound subsequently written *j*.
- **Yaguana / La Yaguana** — the settlement or district traditionally connected with present-day Léogâne.

### Etymology

**[EA]** The component *caona* is securely early. Columbus, Las Casas, and Peter Martyr record a related Hispaniolan word for gold or a gold alloy; comparative Arawakan evidence supports its Indigenous status. A linguistic study of pre-Columbian Hispaniolan contact vocabulary consequently treats *caona* as securely Taíno.

**[LS, disputed]** “Anacaona = Flor de Oro / Fleur d’Or / Golden Flower,” analyzed as *ana* “flower” + *caona* “gold,” became standard in nineteenth-century Antillean antiquarianism. It appears in later word lists and supplies the title and imagery of numerous modern works.

**[MSR, disputed]** José Juan Arrom objected that no dependable early Arawakan vocabulary establishes *ana* as “flower.” Comparative vocabularies instead record forms such as *totocolo* for “flower,” while *annaká/annakán* can mean “middle” or “center.” He tentatively reconstructed something like “center/heart of the celestially precious.” That reconstruction is itself conjectural. The defensible conclusion is therefore:

- *caona* probably evokes gold or preciousness;
- “Golden Flower” is culturally powerful but philologically insecure;
- the full original sense of *Anacaona* is unrecoverable from the surviving evidence.

The linguistic dispute is documented in the [study of Hispaniolan contact vocabulary](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/315688760_Encuentros_linguisticos_en_La_Espanola_-_antes_de_Colon) and [Arrom’s critique of “Flor de Oro”](https://tiboko.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/11/Jose_Juan_Arrom_y_la_busqueda_de_nuestra.pdf).

### Titles and epithets

- **cacica** — Oviedo’s direct political designation; a Spanish feminization of *cacique*.
- **reina / señora** — “queen” and “lady,” used by Las Casas and later historians. These translate rank into European political vocabulary and should not be treated as exact Taíno constitutional terminology.
- **“la mayor señora de la isla”** — Diego Méndez’s testament: the greatest lady on the island, obeyed and served by the executed caciques.
- **“notable mujer,” “muy prudente,” “graciosa,” “palanciana”** — Las Casas’s characterizations: notable, prudent, gracious, courtly in speech and bearing.
- **poet, composer, “vates” of the areítos** — Peter Martyr’s Third Decade says she was reputed among the outstanding composers of *areítos* or rhythms.
- **Flor de Oro / Fleur d’Or / Golden Flower** — nineteenth-century learned-literary epithet based on the disputed etymology.
- **Queen Anacaona / Reine Anacaona** — modern memorial and literary title.
- **martyr of Indigenous resistance** — modern historical and political characterization, not an early title.

### Ethnic and national identifications

**[EA]** The chroniclers did not call her Haitian or Dominican in the modern national sense. They placed her among the Indigenous inhabitants of Hispaniola and within Xaraguá and Maguana.

**[LS/MSR]** “Taíno” is the accepted modern umbrella classification, but its use as the self-name of one politically uniform fifteenth-century people is debated. The island contained multiple polities and linguistic or cultural communities.

**[MI if projected backward]** Calling Anacaona a citizen or queen of the Republic of Haiti or Dominican Republic is anachronistic. Her territory lay principally in what is now Haiti, while the colonial execution narrative places her death at or under the authority of Santo Domingo. Her subsequent memory belongs to both sides of the island and to the wider Caribbean.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Earliest record

**[EA] Peter Martyr d’Anghiera, *De orbe novo*, First Decade.** Martyr assembled reports at the Spanish court, apparently including testimony from returning participants. The First Decade was written in stages and printed in 1511. It says:

- Anacaona was Behechío’s sister and Caonabó’s widow;
- she possessed substantial authority in her brother’s government;
- she advised accommodation with the Spaniards after Caonabó’s fate;
- she and Behechío ceremonially welcomed Bartolomé Colón;
- she had her own canoe and following.

What this earliest layer does **not** provide is a birth year, a physical portrait, the “Golden Flower” etymology, a surviving poem, or an autobiographical speech.

### Primary and early-modern corpus

#### Peter Martyr, *De orbe novo decades*

- **Language:** Latin.
- **Composition:** reports written from 1493 onward; First Decade substantially complete by 1510.
- **Publication:** First Decade, 1511; first three Decades, 1516; complete eight-Decade edition, 1530.
- **Role:** influential ruler, diplomatic intermediary, widow, patron or composer of areítos.
- **Later notice:** the Third Decade, Book IX, calls her outstanding at composing areítos and reports that, after Behechío’s death, she caused his most beautiful wife or concubine Guanahattabenecheua to be buried alive with him; a Franciscan allegedly prevented further burials. This disturbing episode is unique or nearly unique to Martyr’s tradition and must not be silently harmonized with Las Casas’s idealized portrait. [The passage in English translation](https://www.fulltextarchive.com/book/De-Orbe-Novo-Volume-1-of-2-/7/)

#### Gonzalo Fernández de Oviedo, *Historia general y natural de las Indias*

- **Language:** Spanish.
- **Publication/history:** first major portion printed in 1535; Oviedo continued revising into the 1540s; parts remained unpublished until the nineteenth century.
- **Witness status:** Oviedo reached the Indies after the massacre and relied on participants and colonial testimony. He was a royal chronicler and openly defended Ovando’s government.
- **Role:** widow of Caonabó, sister and successor-like ruler of Xaraguá, mother of Aguaimota, alleged organizer of a conspiracy, prisoner and victim of judicial execution.
- **Distinctive version:** Oviedo accepts the conspiracy as proven and calls the massacre “justice.” He says more than forty caciques were burned; Anacaona was hanged three months later; her nephew Guaorocaya was hunted down and hanged. [Oviedo, Book III, chapter XII](https://ems.kcl.ac.uk/print/e026.html)

Oviedo also inserts a sexualized and hostile excursus about her. That passage is evidence for colonial misogyny and Oviedo’s rhetoric, not independent evidence about her private sexual life.

#### Diego Méndez de Segura, testament or deathbed relation

- **Language:** Spanish.
- **Date:** Méndez died in 1536; the relation survives through later documentary publication, notably Navarrete.
- **Role:** Méndez says Ovando detained him at Xaraguá while “84 caciques, lords of vassals” and “Nacaona, the greatest lady of the island” were burned or hanged.
- **Value and limit:** closer to a participant’s recollection than the literary chronicles, but preserved through later copying and publication. Its “84” differs from Oviedo’s “more than forty” and Las Casas’s approximately eighty. [Transcribed in Emiliano Tejera’s documentary lexicon](https://tiboko.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/11/Palabras-Indigenas-de-la-Isla-de-Santo-Domingo-por-Emiliano-Tejera.pdf)

#### Bartolomé de las Casas, *Historia de las Indias*

- **Language:** Spanish.
- **Composition:** principally 1527–1561.
- **First full publication:** 1875–1876.
- **Witness status:** Las Casas was in Hispaniola during the general period but does not establish himself as an eyewitness to Anacaona’s execution.
- **Role:** he praises her prudence, generosity, diplomacy, and friendship toward Christians; recounts the magnificent Xaraguá reception of Bartolomé Colón; and condemns Ovando’s massacre as treachery.
- **Distinctive version:** the conspiracy was a pretext; the assembled leaders were deceived, confined, bound, burned, and attacked by cavalry; Anacaona was hanged. [Las Casas’s account of the earlier reception](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/50351/50351-h/50351-h.htm)

#### Las Casas, *Brevísima relación de la destrucción de las Indias*

- **Language:** Spanish.
- **Printed:** 1552.
- **Role:** compressed denunciation of the Xaraguá massacre and Anacaona’s hanging.
- **Purpose:** polemical advocacy against colonial atrocities; invaluable but rhetorically concentrated.

#### Francisco López de Gómara, *Historia general de las Indias*

- **Language:** Spanish.
- **Printed:** 1552.
- **Role:** retells conquest history without firsthand American experience; subordinate to earlier testimony.

#### Antonio de Herrera y Tordesillas, *Historia general de los hechos de los castellanos*

- **Language:** Spanish.
- **Publication:** 1601–1615.
- **Role:** official synthesis. Herrera records disapproval of the massacre at court but preserves a conspiracy framework. This is a seventeenth-century learned systematization, not fresh eyewitness evidence. A comparison of Oviedo, Las Casas, and Herrera appears in this [study of terror in the conquest](https://www.scielo.cl/scielo.php?pid=S0717-71942012000200001&script=sci_arttext).

### Lost Indigenous corpus

**[EA]** Peter Martyr’s assertion that Anacaona composed areítos is the oldest evidence for her poetic reputation. Areíto combined sung or recited words, rhythm, dance, historical memory, and ceremony. No words or melody securely attributable to her survive.

**[MI]** Texts marketed as a verbatim “Areíto de Anacaona” are not recoveries of her work unless accompanied by an independently verifiable early provenance. The famous melody later bearing that title belongs to the modern-invention problem discussed below.

## 3. The Story

### Parentage and birth

**[EA]** No early source names Anacaona’s parents. The chroniclers identify Behechío as her brother but supply no genealogy above them.

**[MI]** The exact birth year **1474**, the frequent formulation “born at Yaguana in 1474,” and a precisely calculated age at death do not occur in the early corpus. They are modern biographical filling. “Born in or associated with Yaguana/Xaraguá” is plausible; the year is not documented.

### Marriage to Caonabó

**[EA]** Peter Martyr, Oviedo, and Las Casas agree that Anacaona had been the wife of Caonabó, the powerful ruler associated with Maguana. After his capture by Alonso de Ojeda and death while being shipped toward Spain, Anacaona returned to her brother’s territory.

**[MSR]** The marriage is sometimes interpreted as a political alliance between major polities. That is reasonable but not explicitly stated by the early sources.

**[MI]** Detailed modern accounts assigning Caonabó a Lucayan birth, a conquest of Maguana in precisely 1470, and a political marriage arranged in 1475 exceed the secure evidence unless separately sourced.

### The destruction of La Navidad

**[EA]** Caonabó was blamed by Spanish narratives for the destruction of Columbus’s settlement at La Navidad and the deaths of its occupants.

**[MI]** Early sources do not securely say that Anacaona planned, ordered, or personally participated in that attack. Modern biographies often convert her marriage into complicity. The inference is possible but undocumented.

### Return to Xaraguá and reception of Bartolomé Colón

**[EA]** Peter Martyr and Las Casas describe Bartolomé Colón’s journey to Xaraguá in the later 1490s. Behechío and Anacaona received him with large ceremonial dances, songs, food, cotton, and formal hospitality. Las Casas describes thirty women bearing branches, dancing and singing before the visitors; he does not say that all the words were composed by Anacaona.

Behechío initially objected that his country produced no gold. Bartolomé Colón accepted cotton, cassava bread, and other products as tribute. Las Casas credits both Behechío and Anacaona with generosity and records Anacaona accompanying the party to inspect the Spanish ship. [The narrative begins at chapter CXIV](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/50351/50351-h/50351-h.htm)

**[EA/MSR]** Martyr portrays Anacaona as advising accommodation because Caonabó’s fate demonstrated Spanish power. This makes her early recorded political role diplomatic rather than simplistically “pro-Spanish” or “anti-Spanish.”

### Poet of the areítos

**[EA]** Martyr’s Third Decade calls her reputedly one of the leading composers of areítos. This establishes a sixteenth-century poetic reputation, not surviving authorship: no composition can be quoted or reconstructed.

**[LS]** Later writers recast her as “the first poet of Haiti,” “first Caribbean woman poet,” or national poet-ancestor. Such descriptions are legitimate reception history but impose modern national and literary categories on oral ceremonial performance.

### Death of Behechío and succession

**[EA]** Later passages in Martyr and Oviedo treat Anacaona as the dominant ruler after her brother’s death. Oviedo says that she was as feared and honored as Behechío and that nothing was done contrary to her command.

**[EA, hostile variant]** Martyr says she ordered Guanahattabenecheua, Behechío’s most beautiful wife or concubine, buried alive with him and would have buried others except for a Franciscan’s intervention. No equivalent passage in Las Casas confirms it. It could preserve a mortuary practice, hostile report, misunderstanding, or sensational court gossip; the evidence cannot decide.

**[MI]** The precise year **1500** for Behechío’s death and Anacaona’s formal accession is conventional rather than securely documented.

### Ovando’s expedition

**[EA]** In 1503 Governor Nicolás de Ovando marched into Xaraguá with a major armed force. Oviedo specifies seventy cavalry and two hundred infantry. Other retellings give totals near 300–370. The expedition’s scale is more secure than an exact count.

The central conflict is motive:

- **Oviedo’s version [EA, pro-colonial]:** Ovando received information that Anacaona and allied caciques were secretly preparing rebellion and the killing of Christians.
- **Las Casas’s version [EA, anti-conquest]:** the alleged plot was fraudulent; Ovando planned a treacherous destruction of peacefully assembled leaders.
- **Herrera [CE]:** preserves the conspiracy explanation while acknowledging the scandal the killings caused.
- **Modern historical reconstruction [MSR]:** scholars commonly place the action within Ovando’s elimination of autonomous Indigenous leadership, enforcement of tribute and labor, and consolidation of the southwest. A recent archaeological-historical synthesis describes her execution as part of the destruction of Indigenous leadership in the region. [Valcárcel-Rojas, 2023](https://www.revistasipgh.org/index.php/rearam/article/download/4310/6487/22151)

No surviving trial dossier independently proves the alleged conspiracy.

### The massacre of Xaraguá

**[EA]** Anacaona assembled regional leaders and received Ovando ceremonially. During or after festivities, Spanish forces surrounded the principal house. The leaders were bound; the structure was burned; cavalry and infantry attacked people outside.

The principal numerical variants are:

- **more than forty caciques** — Oviedo;
- **about eighty** — Las Casas;
- **eighty-four burned or hanged, with Anacaona** — Diego Méndez.

These figures should remain separate. They probably refer to different reckonings or were shaped by memory and rhetoric.

Oviedo names Diego Velázquez and Rodrigo Mejía Trillo as officers entrusted with the prisoners. His narrative says Anacaona herself came with her daughter Aguaimota to watch the Spanish mock combat and asked that the caciques be summoned. This detail makes the subsequent entrapment particularly stark even in Oviedo’s justificatory account.

### Trial and execution

**[EA]** Oviedo says the alleged treason was discovered, the chiefs were sentenced, and Anacaona was hanged three months later. Las Casas treats the proceedings as invented justification and says she was hanged “to do her honor”—a bitterly ironic description of differential execution: elite hanging rather than burning.

**Date variants:**

- **1503:** Oviedo explicitly dates the expedition to 1503; much modern scholarship follows him.
- **1504:** later summaries sometimes date the hanging to 1504, reasoning from the continuation of Ovando’s western campaign into February 1504 or from the reported three-month imprisonment.
- **Best statement:** the massacre occurred in 1503; the execution followed, probably late in 1503 or possibly early in 1504. A precise day is unattested.

**Place variants:**

- Oviedo’s sequence implies capture at Xaraguá and execution after three months but does not unambiguously document every movement.
- Later historians commonly say she was taken in chains to Santo Domingo and publicly hanged there.
- Diego Méndez’s wording has also been used to argue that the executions occurred while Ovando remained at Xaraguá.

No execution-site archaeology, contemporary burial record, or body identification resolves this.

### The body

**Absence of evidence:** no early source states where Anacaona’s body was buried, exposed, or removed. There is no authenticated tomb, grave, relic, or set of remains. Claims to know her burial place are therefore modern invention unless accompanied by new archaeological documentation.

### Immediate aftermath

**[EA]** Oviedo records that Anacaona’s nephew Guaorocaya fled into the Bahoruco mountains, was hunted by 130 Spaniards, captured, and hanged. Spanish operations continued against western communities until early 1504. Ovando subsequently founded Santa María de la Vera Paz as a colonial settlement in the conquered region.

**[LS/MI]** Later genealogical narratives connect Anacaona’s daughter Higüemota to Hernando de Guevara, their daughter Mencía, and Mencía’s marriage to Enriquillo. Some links derive from colonial genealogical testimony, but popular retellings frequently collapse generations or make Enriquillo Anacaona’s direct nephew. Each relationship requires separate proof; it cannot simply be read backward from the nineteenth-century novel *Enriquillo*.

### Every documented interaction with another named divine being or hero

No securely early source records Anacaona interacting with a named zemí, deity, supernatural being, or culture hero. Claims that she was a “zemí interpreter” occur in modern summaries but have not been located in the early passages examined here.

The named human figures important to her heroic tradition are:

- **Behechío** — sister, counselor, ceremonial co-host, and eventual successor-like ruler; Peter Martyr, First Decade (printed 1511), Las Casas’s *Historia*, and Oviedo. **[EA]**
- **Caonabó** — wife and political consort; after his capture and death she returned to Xaraguá; Peter Martyr, Las Casas, Oviedo. **[EA]**
- **Bartolomé Colón** — received him at Xaraguá, joined the ceremonial hospitality, negotiated or supported tribute in cotton and cassava, and visited his vessel; Peter Martyr and Las Casas. **[EA]**
- **Aguaimota/Higüemota** — mother and daughter appear together immediately before the Spanish seizure at Xaraguá; Oviedo, Book III, chapter XII. **[EA]**
- **Nicolás de Ovando** — ceremonial host turned prisoner and victim; Ovando ordered the seizure of the chiefs and her later hanging; Oviedo and Las Casas, composed mid-sixteenth century. **[EA, opposed interpretations]**
- **Diego Velázquez de Cuéllar** — Oviedo names him as one of the officers who received and bound the assembled caciques under Ovando’s plan; Oviedo. **[EA]**
- **Rodrigo Mejía Trillo** — same role as Velázquez in Oviedo’s massacre narrative. **[EA]**
- **Guaorocaya** — nephew who fled and continued resistance before capture and execution; Oviedo. **[EA]**
- **Guanahattabenecheua** — Anacaona allegedly ordered her burial alive with Behechío; Peter Martyr, Third Decade. **[EA, isolated hostile report]**
- **Christopher Columbus** — no secure face-to-face interaction is narrated. Their famous visual pairing is a nineteenth-century monument, not an ancient or early-colonial meeting tradition. **[MI as interaction; RV as iconography]**
- **Alonso de Ojeda** — captured Caonabó, profoundly affecting Anacaona’s political circumstances, but no early direct encounter with Anacaona is established. **[EA indirect only]**
- **Hernando de Guevara** — later genealogical and romantic narratives connect him with Higüemota, not securely with Anacaona herself. Scenes of direct negotiation are later elaboration. **[CE/RV]**
- **Enriquillo/Guarocuya** — kinship and ideological succession are central to later Dominican writing, especially Galván; no securely documented personal meeting survives. **[CE/RV]**
- **Hatuey** — later national and pan-Caribbean narratives make him a subordinate, survivor, or continuator of Xaraguá’s resistance. The early sources do not securely document a personal Anacaona–Hatuey encounter. **[LS/RV]**
- **Salomé Ureña’s Guacanagarix and other caciques** — interact with Anacaona inside the 1880 epic’s constructed Indigenous cycle; they are literary interactions, not independent fifteenth-century evidence. **[RV]**

## 5. Cult, Tomb and Ritual

### Cult

**Absence of early evidence:** no sixteenth-century source records sacrifices, prayers, a shrine, priesthood, festival, oracle, relic cult, or posthumous invocation directed to Anacaona. The sources call her a living cacica and poet, not a divine being.

**Absence in standard Vodou documentation:** the major ethnographic literature on Haitian Vodou does not establish a widespread, historically continuous Anacaona lwa cult. Vodou is locally variable, so this is not proof that no household or congregation has ever honored her. It does mean that categorical internet lists presenting “Anacaona” as a standard nationwide lwa are unsupported.

**[F]** Modern reports occasionally describe ceremonies honoring Anacaona or “Indian” spirits, and contemporary practitioners may incorporate Indigenous ancestors into local devotions. Such a ceremony would document modern ancestral veneration, not an unbroken Taíno cult.

### Areíto as ritual

**[EA]** The areíto was simultaneously social memory, dance, song, and ceremony. Anacaona’s reputation as an areíto composer places her within ritual performance during life, but it does not show that later areítos worshipped her.

### Tomb and relics

No authenticated tomb, execution marker, grave, body, or relic survives. No excavated structure can be identified as her residence. The modern monument at Léogâne is commemorative, not a tomb.

### Civic commemoration

**[RV]** Léogâne is popularly called *la Cité de la Reine Anacaona*. Place Anacaona, Lycée Anacaona, businesses, schools, streets, and cultural organizations institutionalize her memory. Moreau de Saint-Méry’s late-eighteenth-century topography documents Léogâne as a colonial town and preserves geographical history, but it is not independent evidence for a precise house or birthplace. His 1798 volume is digitized [here](https://books.google.com/books/about/Description_topographique_physique_civil.html?id=qbhIAAAAcAAJ).

**[MSR/disputed geography]** The common equation “Yaguana = present Léogâne” is a durable local and historiographical identification, but the scale of early settlement, political district, and later town should not be collapsed into one exact archaeological point.

## 6. Historical Evolution of the Figure

### c. 1496–1511: diplomatic ruler in conquest intelligence

The earliest narrative layer emphasizes diplomacy, rank, ceremony, tribute, and political prudence. Martyr’s Anacaona tries to preserve Xaraguá through accommodation after Caonabó’s defeat. She belongs to a colonial report network rather than to an autonomous Indigenous written archive.

### 1510s–1550s: poet, dangerous ruler, and massacre victim

Martyr adds her poetic renown and the alleged mortuary killing. Oviedo constructs a dangerous conspirator justly punished by a capable governor. Las Casas constructs the counter-image: a prudent and gracious ally murdered by treachery. These are not merely factual discrepancies; they are rival moral and imperial histories.

### Seventeenth and eighteenth centuries: official synthesis and geographical memory

Herrera canonized a moderated imperial account. Xaraguá and Yaguana persisted in chronicles and geographical writing, while Anacaona herself had no demonstrable public cult. Moreau de Saint-Méry’s late-colonial descriptions belong to the history of the region that later claimed her, not to eyewitness testimony about her.

### Early nineteenth century: post-independence Indigenous ancestry

Independent Haiti and the emerging Dominican national tradition looked beyond the colonial period for pre-European figures. Coriolan Ardouin’s “Floranna la fiancée,” written by a poet who died in 1835 and published posthumously, places a queenly Anacaona over an idealized, sensuous Xaraguá. Its surviving text begins “Anacaona, la Reine” and surrounds her with women, flowers, moonlight, innocence, and ceremony. [Text of “Floranna la fiancée”](https://sites.google.com/view/phareshatiens/poets-by-name/coriolan-ardouin/floranna-la-fianc%C3%A9e)

Thomas Madiou’s *Histoire d’Haïti*, volume I (1847), made the Indigenous period part of a Haitian national history stretching from 1492 through revolution. Beaubrun Ardouin’s works likewise installed pre-Columbian geography and conquest in the island’s historical prologue. These historians remain crucial for Haitian historiography, but they wrote more than three centuries after Anacaona.

### Late nineteenth century: Dominican epic and monumental subordination

Salomé Ureña’s **“Anacaona” (1880)** is a thirty-nine-part Spanish epic poem. It reconstructs a lost Indigenous world, makes Anacaona and Caonabó heroic national ancestors, and places the conquest within a Dominican patriotic and Hispanophone literary framework. It is reception, not recovered oral tradition. [National-library record](https://bd.bnphu.gob.do/items/6bfb53a7-4765-444b-9e47-d0ca52118a5a/full) and [critical discussion](https://revistas.intec.edu.do/index.php/ciso/article/download/1048/html?inline=1).

The 1886 Columbus monument in Santo Domingo by French sculptor Ernest Guilbert places a kneeling or ascending Indigenous woman—identified in Dominican memory as Anacaona—at Columbus’s pedestal. A description already published in 1893 cautiously said the figure was “probably intended for Anacaona.” Thus the identification is old but was not entirely explicit. Jennifer Baez reads the racialized, gendered subordination of the woman beneath Columbus as both nationalist symbol and erasure of Indigenous and Black agency. [Monument study](https://art.washington.edu/research/publications/anacaona-writes-back-columbus-monument-santo-domingo-site-erasures)

### Haitian theatre, 1870s–1930s

A substantial Haitian theatrical corpus developed:

- Alcibiade Fleury-Battier’s lost **Anacaona**, reportedly staged with great success in the late nineteenth century; Massillon Coicou’s surviving commentary helps document it.
- Massillon Coicou’s **L’Oracle** (1893), a symbolic encounter among “Indian,” “African,” and “Spaniard.” It participates in the same Indigenous-national genealogy but is not itself a surviving Anacaona biography.
- Henry Chauvet’s **La Fille du Kacik / Les Quisqueyennes** (1890s), where Anacaona is not necessarily the protagonist.
- Vergniaud Leconte’s **Une princesse aborigène**, presenting her as intermediary or negotiator.
- Frédéric Burr-Reynaud and Dominique Hyppolite’s verse drama **Anacaona**, variously dated 1927 or published/performed around 1931.

The specialist study *Figures d’Anacaona dans le théâtre haïtien* inventories this material, including lost plays and reception. [Review and corpus description](https://mimesisjournals.com/ojs/index.php/ponts/article/download/821/658)

### Mid-twentieth century: decolonization and the false areíto

An “Areíto de Anacaona” circulated as purported Indigenous music. Alejo Carpentier reported finding that its melody was identical to the Spanish children’s romance “Estaba el señor Don Gato.” He sent the comparison to Fernando Ortiz; the attribution collapsed. This is a textbook **[MI] modern invention**: a European-derived melody retrospectively authenticated as Anacaona’s lost song. Carpentier’s account is preserved in his published lectures. [Relevant passage](https://www.scribd.com/document/1017008291/CONFERENCIAS-Alejo-Carpentier-1987-01-01-Editorial-Letras-Cubanas-9d5934e8656a885f1d3e7775debb2cfb-Anna-s-Archive)

Carpentier’s *El reino de este mundo* (1949) does **not** make Anacaona a character. Its importance here is comparative: it transforms Mackandal, Boukman, Henri Christophe, and Haitian revolutionary memory through the “marvelous real.” Treating it as an Anacaona narrative would be erroneous.

### 1970s–present: pan-Caribbean heroine

Tite Curet Alonso’s song **“Anacaona,”** recorded by Cheo Feliciano for his 1971 solo debut, became the most internationally influential popular treatment. Its slain, sorrowful queen is a pan-Caribbean emblem of conquest. The song is modern salsa, not a preserved areíto; invocations of an “areíto” are artistic framing. [Recording date](https://music.apple.com/us/song/1465787418) and [musicological discussion](https://cmpr.edu/wp-content/uploads/2020/02/Musike_6.pdf).

Jean Métellus’s French-language play **Anacaona** (1986; staged at Chaillot in 1988) interprets the conquest from a Haitian diasporic and anti-colonial position. Danielle Legros Georges’s poem **“Anacaona”** (2001) and Edwidge Danticat’s young-adult novel **Anacaona: Golden Flower, Haiti, 1490** (2005) give her an imagined first-person or recovered voice. Scholarship treats these as postcolonial efforts to establish kinship with the land before European conquest. [Comparative study](https://www.jstor.org/stable/26527980)

## 7. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Literature and theatre

- **Coriolan Ardouin, “Floranna la fiancée”** — composed before 1835; published posthumously, including in an 1881 collection. Anacaona presides over an idealized female ceremonial community. **[RV]**
- **Salomé Ureña, “Anacaona”** — 1880 Dominican epic poem. It creates speeches, scenes, motives, and a comprehensive Indigenous world absent from the chronicles. **[RV]**
- **Alcibiade Fleury-Battier, *Anacaona*** — late-nineteenth-century Haitian play, text now apparently lost; remembered through performance history and Coicou’s commentary. **[RV; incomplete record]**
- **Massillon Coicou, *L’Oracle*** — 1893; symbolically connects the Indian, African, and European components of Haitian history. Coicou should not be credited with a surviving poem titled *Anacaona* on the evidence reviewed. **[RV]**
- **Manuel de Jesús Galván, *Enriquillo*** — serialized 1879–1882; makes Anacaona’s family part of a providential Dominican colonial genealogy. Historical details are heavily romanticized. **[RV]**
- **Burr-Reynaud and Hyppolite, *Anacaona*** — 1927/1931 Haitian verse drama. **[RV]**
- **Jean Métellus, *Anacaona*** — 1986; anti-colonial historical tragedy. **[RV]**
- **Danielle Legros Georges, “Anacaona”** — 2001; Haitian diasporic poetic recovery. **[RV]**
- **Edwidge Danticat, *Anacaona: Golden Flower, Haiti, 1490*** — 2005; fictional diary. Its childhood, internal voice, chronological precision, and translations of the name are declared imaginative reconstruction, not primary testimony. **[RV]**
- **Ofelia Berrido, *Anacaona*** — twenty-first-century Dominican illustrated poetry that treats Taíno vocabulary and Anacaona as elements of national and spiritual memory. [Dominican Academy discussion](https://academia.org.do/2020/02/24/anacaona-de-ofelia-berrido-una-ofrenda-estetica-y-simbolica/) **[RV]**
- **Laurent Binet, *Civilizations*** — 2019 alternate history; Anacaona appears in a consciously counterfactual world. **[MI/fiction]**

### Music and dance

- **“Anacaona,” Tite Curet Alonso/Cheo Feliciano** — 1971. Seminal salsa reception.
- **“Anakaona,” Ansy and Yole Dérose** — 1983 Haitian popular-music tribute.
- **“Anacaona,” Irka Mateo** — 2009 Dominican musical reinvention.
- Ballets and dance-school productions in Haiti and the diaspora continue to embody her as queen, dancer, and resistance figure. These performances frequently conflate the historical areíto with modern choreography. **[RV]**

### Visual culture

- **Santo Domingo Columbus monument, 1886:** Anacaona is traditionally identified as the Indigenous woman climbing or kneeling at Columbus’s pedestal. Her physical subordination makes the sculpture an especially contested image of mestizo or Hispanist nationalism.
- **Léogâne monument, 2015:** a monumental crowned figure with staff and sunflower was erected in Place Sainte-Rose during a German-funded post-earthquake rebuilding project. Its base narrates Spanish arrival and violence; the plaque identifies her as *Fleur d’Or* and claims Léogâne as birthplace. This is modern civic iconography, not a historical likeness. A detailed visual study describes its construction and unveiling. [Study of the Léogâne statue](https://escholarship.org/content/qt2cx6b4hf/qt2cx6b4hf_noSplash_58a04bdd935cb5239d696d4838723c08.pdf?t=pxm2j9)
- **Julianny Ariza Vólquez, *Anacaona, Yuíza, Yuíza, Anacaona* (2023):** a contemporary sculpture pairing two remembered Caribbean cacicas and explicitly critiquing their marginalization. [Museum record](https://artsandculture.google.com/asset/anacaona-yu%C3%ADza-yu%C3%ADza-anacaona-julianny-ariza-v%C3%B3lquez/5gE1Ioi5JqYDuw)

No surviving sixteenth-century portrait was made from life. Modern faces, crowns, shell garments, feather headdresses, and sunflowers are iconographic choices.

### National and political appropriation

**Haitian:** Anacaona supplies an Indigenous prehistory to a state created through an African-descended anti-slavery revolution. Writers use her to join Indigenous dispossession to enslavement and later imperialism. Léogâne’s civic identity is particularly attached to her.

**Dominican:** nineteenth-century poetry and the Columbus monument made her an Indigenous ancestor of Dominican nationality. This could authorize resistance to conquest, but it could also support Hispanist narratives in which a feminized Indigenous population yields to Spain while African ancestry is visually or rhetorically suppressed.

**Pan-Caribbean and diasporic:** salsa, Chicano reception, Haitian-American literature, and contemporary art detach her from a single national claim and make her a symbol of Indigenous survival, women’s political leadership, colonial betrayal, and cultural memory.

**Caution:** “Haitian hero” and “Dominican hero” describe modern reception. Neither nation existed in 1503.

### Documented modern inventions and overstatements

- Exact birth in **1474** — unsupported by the early corpus.
- A recoverable verbatim body of her poems — no such manuscript or transcription exists.
- The traditional “Areíto de Anacaona” melody — European *Don Gato* melody misattributed as Indigenous.
- A direct role in planning the destruction of La Navidad — not established.
- A standardized office of “zemí interpreter” personally documented for her — not located in the early sources reviewed.
- Conversion to Christianity, baptismal name, or sainthood — unsupported.
- Ovando offering life in exchange for sexual submission or concubinage — a modern dramatic accretion, not in Oviedo or Las Casas.
- A known tomb or authenticated portrait — none.
- An unbroken Vodou cult identifying her as a canonical lwa — unproven.
- “First woman executed in the New World” or “first Indigenous poet of the Americas” — rhetorical superlatives, not demonstrable historical findings.

## 8. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Historicity

There is no serious evidentiary reason to reduce Anacaona to a wholly invented person: Martyr, Oviedo, Las Casas, and Méndez independently or semi-independently place a woman of that name and kinship at Xaraguá. Debate concerns the reliability of individual episodes, the political structure translated as “kingdom,” and the extent to which the chronicles created exemplary types—the prudent queen, lascivious woman, conspirator, or innocent martyr.

### Conspiracy or colonial pretext

This is the central early dispute.

- Oviedo accepts secret rebellion and judicial punishment.
- Las Casas calls the operation treacherous extermination.
- Herrera retains conspiracy while recording courtly outrage.
- Modern historians emphasize that Ovando’s campaign destroyed autonomous leadership and opened the region to colonial control.

No surviving depositions or original trial record permit independent review. Oviedo’s phrase “the truth of the treason was learned” is an assertion, not the evidence itself. Las Casas’s moral indictment is powerful but also shaped by his broader argument against conquest abuses.

### Numbers and chronology

The forty/eighty/eighty-four variants reflect distinct sources, not a total that can responsibly be averaged. Likewise, “1503” and “1504” should not be harmonized into an invented exact date. Oviedo’s 1503 expedition and the campaign’s continuation into early 1504 explain the ambiguity.

### Political terminology

“Five Taíno kingdoms of Hispaniola” is a useful schoolroom scheme but risks making fluid, nested Indigenous leadership resemble centralized European monarchies. *Cacique*, *cacica*, “king,” “queen,” “province,” and “vassal” are colonial translations. Archaeology can document settlement and material networks but cannot yet reconstruct Anacaona’s constitutional powers.

### Poet and authorship

Martyr’s near-contemporary statement makes the poetic reputation early, but “poet” translates an oral, musical, choreographic office into a European literary category. There is no textual basis for attributing any surviving Spanish or Creole lyric to her.

### Etymology

“Golden Flower” has overwhelming cultural currency but weak philological foundations. Arrom’s alternative is also reconstructive rather than certain. Modern writers who use *Golden Flower* are participating in a nineteenth-century tradition, whether or not they know its date.

### Gendered colonial writing

Las Casas makes Anacaona exceptionally prudent, graceful, generous, and friendly to Christians; Oviedo both recognizes her authority and subjects her to sexualized invective. Modern feminist and postcolonial scholarship studies this polarity as a struggle over whether powerful Indigenous women could appear as political agents rather than exotic bodies or tragic victims.

### Island-wide versus national ownership

Maria Cristina Fumagalli’s comparative work emphasizes that Anacaona’s Xaraguá predates and crosses the later Haiti–Dominican Republic border. Haitian and Dominican texts selectively nationalize her. Her reception is therefore evidence not only of Indigenous remembrance but of competing modern accounts of race, language, territory, and belonging. [Comparative chapter](https://doi.org/10.5949/liverpool/9781781381601.003.0004)

### State of the evidence

- No Taíno-authored text survives.
- No original trial dossier has been identified.
- Martyr compiled testimony at court.
- Oviedo relied on colonial informants and official ideology.
- Las Casas wrote decades later and selected material for moral argument.
- Méndez’s short recollection survives through later transmission.
- Later histories often copy one another without declaring dependence.
- Archaeology supports complex Indigenous occupation of western Hispaniola but has not identified Anacaona personally.

### Relevance audit of the item-specific comparative corpus

The sources named for Mackandal, Bois Caïman, and Boukman do **not** supply sixteenth-century evidence about Anacaona:

- the 1758 Makandal proceedings concern the poisoned-maroon conspiracy;
- Antoine Dalmas’s 1814 narrative is a source for Bois Caïman/Boukman traditions;
- Thomas Madiou and Beaubrun Ardouin are nineteenth-century historians of Haiti;
- Carolyn Fick, David Geggus, and Laurent Dubois analyze the Haitian Revolution;
- Carpentier’s *El reino de este mundo* reimagines the revolutionary era.

They matter comparatively because Haitian memory repeatedly links Indigenous dispossession with maroon and revolutionary resistance. They must not be cited as if they corroborated Anacaona’s deeds. Carpentier is directly relevant only to the false “Areíto de Anacaona” and to the later poetics of Haitian historical myth.

### Principal modern scholarship used

- José Juan Arrom on Taíno onomastics and the disputed “Golden Flower” etymology.
- Roberto Valcárcel-Rojas on Indigenous women, conquest, and Anacaona’s destruction as regional leadership.
- Jennifer Baez on the Columbus–Anacaona monument, racialized gender, nationalism, and erasure.
- Maria Cristina Fumagalli on Haitian and Dominican literary reconstructions and the problem of imposing the modern border on Xaraguá.
- Scholarship on Anacaona in Haitian theatre and on postcolonial Haitian diasporic writing.
- Linguistic research on *caona* and early Hispaniolan/Arawakan vocabulary.
- Musicological work on Tite Curet Alonso and Cheo Feliciano.

### Documentary conclusion

The securely recoverable Anacaona is a named, high-ranking Xaraguán woman: Behechío’s sister, Caonabó’s widow, a diplomatic actor, and a reputed composer of areítos, killed under Ovando after the mass seizure of western leaders. Even those propositions come through colonial narrators. Her exact birth, appearance, private voice, poems, trial, burial, and intentions are lost.

The legendary Anacaona is far more expansive: Golden Flower, innocent queen, conspirator, poet-mother, anti-colonial martyr, Haitian ancestor, Dominican ancestor, salsa heroine, diasporic speaker, and sometimes imagined spirit. None of those later forms should be discarded. They document how successive communities have made the silence left by conquest politically and artistically meaningful.
