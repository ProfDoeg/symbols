# Faust: Research Dossier

> **Traditional class:** Faust is not a heroized dead, demigod, epic king, or recipient of cult. He is a **mortal learned magician and legendary overreacher**, subsequently recast as a tragic antihero, knowledge-seeker, lover, national-cultural type, and emblem of modernity.  
> **Earliest secure attestation:** Johannes Trithemius’s Latin letter of **20 August 1507**, naming a living itinerant claimant as “*Magister Georgius Sabellicus, Faustus iunior*.” This attests a boastful practitioner of astrology and magic—not a written diabolical contract, Mephistopheles, twenty-four-year term, Gretchen, or supernatural death.

## Evidentiary key

- **[EA] Secure early attestation:** contemporary or near-contemporary documentary evidence.
- **[LE] Early-modern legendary accretion:** sixteenth- or seventeenth-century story attached to Faust.
- **[LC] Later canonical elaboration:** influential literary transformation, especially Marlowe or Goethe.
- **[FV] Folkloric or regional variant:** oral, popular-theatrical, puppet-play, or localized tradition.
- **[LS] Learned systematization:** later ordering or synthesis of scattered traditions.
- **[SI] Syncretistic identification:** equation with another legendary, demonic, biblical, or classical figure.
- **[MSR] Modern scholarly reconstruction:** inference rather than primary testimony.
- **[MI] Modern invention or reception:** demonstrably modern adaptation, explanation, memorial, or usage.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

Faust begins in the record as an itinerant German-speaking astrologer, magician, prognosticator, and reputed charlatan active during the early Reformation. Within several decades of his probable death, Protestant authors converted scattered accusations and anecdotes into an admonitory legend: an arrogant theologian invokes a spirit, signs away his soul for a fixed term, misuses demonic powers for knowledge, travel, sex, illusion, and practical jokes, and is finally torn apart by the Devil. Marlowe transformed this material into Renaissance tragedy; traveling actors and puppet theatres returned it to continental popular performance; Goethe made Faust’s restless striving, seduction of Gretchen, journey through classical myth, political enterprise, death, and contested salvation the center of a vast modern drama. The result is not a recoverable biography but a sequence of historically distinguishable Fausts.

### Names

- **“Magister Georgius Sabellicus, Faustus iunior”**—“Master Georg Sabellicus, Faust the Younger”—is the form reported by Trithemius in 1507. **[EA]**
- Trithemius also transmits grandiose self-descriptions: *fons necromanticorum* (“fount of necromancers”), *astrologus*, *magus secundus*, *chiromanticus*, *agromanticus*, *pyromanticus*, and a boast of superiority in hydromancy. These are hostile reportage of titles allegedly used by the man, not independently certified accomplishments. **[EA]**
- **Georg/Jörg Faust** is the name most often reconstructed for the historical practitioner. **Johann Faust** becomes standard in later records and in the chapbook. Whether every “Georg,” “Johann,” and “Faustus” notice concerns one person remains disputed. **[MSR]**
- A Heidelberg matriculation entry for **Georgius Helmstetter** in 1483 has been proposed as the future Faust because later university notation and biographical reconstruction can be made to fit him. Frank Baron argued that this identification deserved renewed attention; it is not a document explicitly equating Helmstetter with Faust. **[MSR]**
- Latin **Faustus** means “favorable,” “fortunate,” or “auspicious.” German **Faust** means “fist,” from Middle High German *vûst*. Scholarship disagrees over whether the legendary name represents a German surname, a Latinizing learned name, or a professional pseudonym. The convenient interpretation “fortunate” is philologically possible but not proof that the bearer deliberately chose it. **[MSR]**
- **Sabellicus** may be an assumed humanist or occult title invoking the Sabines, whom learned tradition associated with sorcery; derivation from an otherwise hypothesized family name *Sabel/Zavel* is less generally accepted. **[MSR]**
- **Faustus junior** implies an elder bearer or model, but no securely identified “Faustus senior” survives. Attempts to identify the senior Faust with Simon Magus, a medieval magician, or another contemporary are speculative. **[MSR]**
- The forms **Faust**, **Faustus**, **Doctor Faust**, **Doctor Johann Faust**, and Marlowe’s **Doctor Faustus** belong to successive German and Latinizing/English traditions. Goethe’s protagonist is called **Heinrich Faust**, a first name not securely attached to the historical practitioner. **[LC]**
- The spirit’s name appears in early forms including **Mephostophiles/Mephostophilis** and German **Mephostophiles**; Goethe standardizes **Mephistopheles**. The demon is not present in the secure lifetime documentation and marks the transition from suspect magician to fully literary legend. **[LE/LC]**

### Classification and identifications

Faust was never canonically classified as a saint, demigod, or cult hero. The 1587 *Historia* calls him a notorious magician and black artist; Marlowe makes him a tragic scholar; Goethe makes him a representative modern striver. Equations with Simon Magus, Theophilus of Adana, Cyprian the Magician, Don Juan, Prometheus, or Job are comparative or literary constructions, not early identifications. **[LS/SI]**

The recurring description of Faust as an “Arthurian” or broadly medieval hero is taxonomically misleading. His legend developed in the early-modern Holy Roman Empire and Protestant print culture. It later absorbed medieval pact narratives and, through Goethe, classical and biblical structures, but Faust is not a member of an Arthurian genealogy. **[MSR]**

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### Documentary horizon, 1507–1540

1. **Johannes Trithemius to Johann Virdung, 20 August 1507; Latin.** Trithemius reports “Georgius Sabellicus, Faustus junior,” whom he considers a foolish, boastful vagabond. He says Faust claimed mastery of necromancy, astrology, chiromancy, divination by earth, fire, and water, and boasted that he could reproduce the works of Plato and Aristotle or restore lost works of classical authors. Trithemius also reports scandalous claims involving boys. Because the account is polemical and partly hearsay, it securely proves reputation and self-advertisement, not magical performance or every accusation. **[EA]**

2. **Conrad Mutianus Rufus, letter of 3 October 1513; Latin.** Mutianus says that “Georgius Faustus, *Helmitheus Hedebergensis*,” a chiromancer and boastful fool, had appeared in Erfurt and attracted crowds. The wording has helped generate the Helmstetter/Heidelberg hypothesis. **[EA/MSR]**

3. **Bamberg episcopal accounts, 1520.** Accounts record payment to “Doctor Faustus” for a nativity or horoscope for Bishop Georg III Schenk von Limpurg. This is unusually strong evidence that someone of that name worked professionally as an astrologer and received elite patronage. It does not attest demonology. **[EA]**

4. **Ingolstadt and related municipal material, 1528.** A “Doctor Jörg Faustus von Heidelberg” was ordered to leave Ingolstadt and reportedly promised not to retaliate against the authorities. The record attests official suspicion, not a pact. **[EA]**

5. **Nuremberg council record, 1532.** The authorities denied “Doctor Faustus, the great sodomite and necromancer” safe conduct. The language records an official accusation; it does not establish the alleged sexual crime or supernatural practice. **[EA]**

6. **Philipp Begardi, *Index sanitatis*, Worms, 1539; German.** Begardi describes Faust as a famous, bold man who called himself a philosopher of philosophers but deceived many patients and accomplished little. This supports a reputation in medicine, alchemy, or healing and contemporary charges of fraud. **[EA]**

7. **Philipp von Hutten to his brother, 15 January 1540; German.** Hutten mentions a “philosopher Faustus” whose unfavorable prognostication concerning the Venezuela expedition had proved wrong thus far. This is the latest firmly dated lifetime notice generally accepted in the dossier. **[EA]**

### Death traditions

Johannes Manlius’s *Locorum communium collectanea*, printed in **1562/1563**, attributes recollections about Faust to Philipp Melanchthon. It places Faust’s gruesome death in a Württemberg village and says the Devil killed him after Faust’s period of sorcery. Because the printed testimony is posthumous and filtered through Manlius, it is evidence for the legend by the 1560s, not a contemporary death certificate. **[LE]**

Johann Weyer’s *De praestigiis daemonum* (**1563**) places Faust’s death at the village of **Staufen in Breisgau**, saying he was found dead beside his bed with his face turned backward. Later tradition locates the event at the *Gasthaus zum Löwen* and attributes it to an alchemical explosion or demonic violence. The Staufen location is early but still posthumous; the explosion is an explanatory reconstruction, not securely recorded fact. **[LE/MSR]**

No authenticated grave, skeleton, tomb inscription, or contemporary death record identifies Faust. The “Faust room,” inn traditions, plaques, and museums at Staufen or Knittlingen are sites of memory, not archaeological proof. **[MI]**

### Principal literary corpus

- **Wolfenbüttel manuscript, *Historia und Geschicht Doctor Johannis Faustj*; German; undated sixteenth-century manuscript.** Its exact priority relative to the printed book is debated. It preserves material belonging to the narrative complex from which the 1587 print emerged. **[LE]**
- **Anonymous, *Historia von D. Johann Fausten*, printed by Johann Spies, Frankfurt, 1587; German.** First securely dated printed Faust book and decisive canonical collection. Modern critical editions compare the first print, Wolfenbüttel manuscript, and the 1587–1590 print variants. **[LE]**
- **Revised *Historia von Doct. Johann Fausti*, 1589, including the “Erfurt chapters”; German.** This younger version reorganizes and enlarges the cycle. **[LE]**
- **P. F., Gent., *The Historie of the Damnable Life, and Deserved Death of Doctor Iohn Faustus*, 1592; English.** Translation/adaptation of the German book and Marlowe’s immediate narrative source. **[LE]**
- **Christopher Marlowe, *The Tragical History of Doctor Faustus*; English; probably composed 1588–1592, performed by 1592–1593.** The earliest extant print is the short **A-text of 1604**; the longer, substantially variant **B-text appeared in 1616**, incorporating revisions and probably additions by other hands. Neither text can simply be called Marlowe’s untouched original. **[LC]**
- **Georg Rudolf Widmann, *Warhafftige Historien von den grewlichen und abschewlichen Sünden und Lastern Doctor Johann Fausti*, Hamburg, 1599; German.** A more expansive and aggressively moralizing Protestant compilation. **[LE]**
- **Johann Nikolaus Pfitzer, *Das ärgerliche Leben und schreckliche Ende des vielberüchtigten Ertz-Schwartzkünstlers D. Johannis Fausti*, 1674; German.** A major conduit from the chapbook tradition toward later popular and literary Fausts. **[LE]**
- **Popular actors’ plays and puppet plays, seventeenth–nineteenth centuries; German and other European languages.** English companies helped circulate Marlovian material in German-speaking Europe. Surviving scripts are mostly late; therefore claims that any nineteenth-century puppet text exactly preserves a sixteenth-century performance are unsafe. Johann Georg Geisselbrecht’s performances, documented from 1786 to 1826 and printed from 1832, are important late witnesses. **[FV]**
- **Gotthold Ephraim Lessing, Faust fragments, principally 1750s–1770s; German.** Lessing planned a Faust saved through the divinely sanctioned desire for knowledge, anticipating the reversal of automatic damnation. **[LC]**
- **Goethe, *Urfaust*, composed chiefly in the 1770s; manuscript recovered and published in 1887.** It centers on the scholar and Gretchen material but lacks the final architecture of the completed drama. **[LC]**
- **Goethe, *Faust. Ein Fragment*, 1790; German.** First published stage of Goethe’s project. **[LC]**
- **Goethe, *Faust. Der Tragödie erster Teil*, 1808.** Establishes the heavenly wager, conditional compact, Gretchen tragedy, and her final salvation. **[LC]**
- **Goethe, *Faust. Der Tragödie zweiter Teil*, completed in 1831 and published posthumously in 1832.** Extends Faust through imperial finance, Helen, Euphorion, colonization, murder, blindness, death, and salvation. **[LC]**
- **Nikolaus Lenau, *Faust*, 1836; German.** A pessimistic poetic reworking ending in spiritual defeat. **[MI]**
- **Thomas Mann, *Doktor Faustus*, 1947; German.** Recasts Faust as the composer Adrian Leverkühn, whose twenty-four productive years, syphilis, demonic colloquy, and collapse parallel the cultural catastrophe of modern Germany. **[MI]**
- **Mikhail Bulgakov, *Мастер и Маргарита* (*The Master and Margarita*), written 1928–1940, first published in censored form 1966–1967.** It is not a retelling with Faust as a character. Goethe’s drama supplies its epigraph, Woland’s name and Mephistophelean resonances, Margarita, the poodle imagery, and a transformed configuration of Devil, lover, writer, judgment, and grace. **[MI/SI]**

## 3. The story

### A. The historical-reputation cycle

**[EA]** The documentary Faust travels through German-speaking territories advertising expertise in astrology, divination, magic, and healing. He attracts clients but also denunciations from abbots, humanists, physicians, and municipalities. No contemporary source records a signed infernal contract.

**[LE]** Posthumous narrators convert his unexplained or violent death into the Devil’s collection of a servant. Reports of bodily mutilation and a twisted corpse provide the bridge to the chapbook’s spectacular ending.

### B. The 1587 *Historia*

**Origin and education.** The book makes Johann Faust the son of a poor peasant from Roda near Weimar, raised by a wealthy kinsman in Wittenberg, and trained in theology. His intellectual gifts turn into arrogance, curiosity, and rejection of Scripture. This genealogy is literary and conflicts with later Knittlingen and Heidelberg reconstructions. **[LE]**

**Conjuration and pact.** Faust goes to a forest near Wittenberg, draws a circle, and summons a spirit amid noise and fire. After negotiation, the spirit Mephostophiles serves him on condition that Faust renounce God and surrender body and soul after **twenty-four years**. Faust signs in his own blood. The contract, fixed term, and named serving demon are securely attested in the 1587 literary tradition, not in lifetime records. **[LE]**

**Demonic hierarchy.** Mephostophiles says that he serves Lucifer and cannot finally act independently of infernal authority. The book’s demonology draws on Christian exempla and contemporary Protestant polemic rather than documenting folk belief in a historically worshipped Mephistopheles. **[LE/LS]**

**Knowledge and disputation.** Faust questions the spirit about hell, creation, astronomy, and cosmology. Mephostophiles can answer much but refuses or distorts theological questions. Faust’s promised omniscience therefore proves counterfeit. **[LE]**

**Travel and spectacle.** Faust flies through Europe, visits Rome and Constantinople, mocks the pope, eats and drinks invisibly at papal expense, performs at imperial courts, and creates illusions. Geographic description, jest-book motifs, anti-Catholic satire, and wonder-tales are accumulated around his name. **[LE]**

**Alexander and Helen.** At Emperor Charles V’s request, Faust causes Alexander the Great and Alexander’s wife to appear. Later he brings forth Helen of Troy, takes her as companion, and fathers a son named **Justus Faustus** in some forms of the cycle. These are magical apparitions within the story, not claims of literal classical survival. **[LE/SI]**

**Pranks and violence.** Faust deceives peasants, horse-dealers, innkeepers, and rivals; removes or appears to remove body parts; consumes prodigious food and drink; and uses magic for revenge. The cycle does not present an ethically pure seeker of knowledge. **[LE]**

**Repentance frustrated.** An old Christian urges Faust to repent. Faust briefly attempts it, but Mephostophiles terrifies him into renewing the pact. The story’s Protestant exemplum insists both on the horror of apostasy and Faust’s culpable despair. **[LE]**

**Death.** At the end of twenty-four years Faust warns students and companions. During the night they hear tumult; in the morning his blood and brains are on the walls and his mutilated body lies outside or on a dung heap, depending on textual form. His students bury the remains. Damnation is unambiguous. **[LE]**

### C. Marlowe

Marlowe retains Wittenberg, necromancy, blood bond, twenty-four years, Mephistophilis, Lucifer, travel, papal mockery, Alexander, Helen, failed repentance, and dismemberment. **[LC]**

He intensifies the inner drama. Faustus rejects logic, medicine, law, and theology as insufficient; the Good and Evil Angels externalize conflict; his blood congeals when he tries to sign; “Homo, fuge” appears on his arm; and Mephistophilis describes hell not merely as a place but as separation from God. **[LC]**

Faustus asks for a wife, but Mephistophilis supplies a devil in female form. His later embrace of Helen—“Was this the face that launched a thousand ships?”—substitutes an apparition for repentance. In the final hour he begs time to stop, seeks annihilation rather than judgment, and is carried away. The Chorus interprets him as the branch that might have grown straight but was cut. **[LC]**

The A- and B-texts differ in wording, comic material, theology, spectacle, and length. A celebrated difference—whether it is never too late “if Faustus **can** repent” or “if Faustus **will** repent”—changes the apparent balance between predestination and voluntary refusal. Editorial choice therefore affects interpretation. **[LC]**

### D. Puppet and folk theatre

Popular performances preserve the conjuring, compact, travels, court tricks, despair, and infernal death while expanding comic servants such as Kasper or Hanswurst. Because surviving written scripts are late and mutable, “the original German puppet play” is a misleading singular. **[FV]**

Goethe recalled encountering the story through popular print and puppet theatre, but later scripts cannot automatically be projected backward as the exact production he saw. **[FV/MSR]**

### E. Goethe, Part One

**Heavenly wager.** The Lord permits Mephistopheles to test Faust, in a structure consciously resembling Job. Faust does not simply sell his soul for twenty-four years. The pact becomes a conditional wager: if he ever tells a passing moment to remain because it is perfectly satisfying, he will die and belong to Mephistopheles. **[LC/SI]**

**Scholar’s crisis.** Heinrich Faust has studied philosophy, law, medicine, and theology but believes he truly knows nothing. He turns to magic, invokes the Earth Spirit, is rejected as not its equal, and prepares poison. Easter bells and childhood memory interrupt his suicide. **[LC]**

**Mephistopheles and rejuvenation.** A black poodle follows Faust home and becomes Mephistopheles. After the compact, the witch’s potion restores Faust’s youth and inflames desire. **[LC]**

**Gretchen tragedy.** Faust courts the young Margarete (“Gretchen”) through Mephistopheles. He supplies a sleeping potion that kills her mother. Gretchen becomes pregnant; her brother Valentin confronts Faust and is killed by Faust with Mephistopheles’ assistance. Abandoned and socially condemned, Gretchen drowns her infant, is imprisoned, and becomes mentally disordered. Faust attempts a rescue, but she rejects flight, submits to divine judgment, and is declared “saved” by a voice from above. The sexual exploitation, maternal death, fratricidal violence, and infanticide are central, not incidental. **[LC]**

### F. Goethe, Part Two

**Imperial economy.** Faust and Mephistopheles enter an indebted emperor’s court. Mephistopheles proposes paper money ostensibly backed by buried treasure, producing temporary prosperity and exposing monetary value as political magic. **[LC]**

**Helen and classical myth.** To satisfy the emperor, Faust descends toward the mysterious Mothers and evokes Paris and Helen. He becomes obsessed with Helen. Wagner, independently assisted by Mephistopheles, produces Homunculus in a flask; the party enters the Classical Walpurgis Night. Chiron carries Faust, and Manto guides him toward Persephone’s realm. **[LC/SI]**

Helen is united with Faust in a deliberately synthetic classical-medieval setting. Their son **Euphorion**, often read as bearing Byronic features, tries to fly, falls, and dies. Helen disappears, leaving her garments. These figures belong to Goethe’s mythopoetic construction, not the chapbook. **[LC]**

**War, land, and murder.** Faust aids the emperor in war and receives coastal territory. He attempts to reclaim land from the sea. When the elderly Philemon and Baucis obstruct his desired vista, Mephistopheles’ agents remove them; their house and chapel burn, and they die. Faust’s claimed constructive project is inseparable from dispossession and killing. **[LC]**

**Death and salvation.** Care blinds Faust. Hearing laborers whom he imagines building a free community—although they are digging his grave—he anticipates a future moment he could bid remain and dies. Mephistopheles claims the soul, but angels distract the devils and carry it away. Gretchen, now a penitent, participates in the final ascent. Goethe thus reverses the chapbook’s mandatory damnation without acquitting Faust of the destruction caused by his striving. **[LC]**

### G. Mann and Bulgakov

Mann’s Adrian Leverkühn contracts syphilis, experiences or imagines a demonic conversation, accepts twenty-four years of artistic genius at the cost of human love, and collapses into mental incapacity. The pact is at once theological possibility, disease symptom, artistic allegory, and analogy for Germany’s bargain with destructive power. The narrator Serenus Zeitblom is partial and not perfectly reliable. **[MI]**

Bulgakov does not reproduce Faust’s biography. Margarita, rather than the Master, enters a bargain-like alliance with Woland; Woland is more judicial and less simply destructive than Goethe’s Mephistopheles; the Master and Margarita receive “peace,” not Goethe’s celestial “light.” Explicit Goethean traces include the epigraph from Mephistopheles, Woland’s name, poodle imagery, and a final reference to making a homunculus. **[MI/SI]**

## 4. Relations with gods, demons, heroes, and legendary figures

Faust has no stable parentage, consort, or descendants across the tradition.

| Tradition | Parentage or kinship |
|---|---|
| 1587 *Historia* | Son of poor peasants near Weimar; raised by a prosperous kinsman in Wittenberg. **[LE]** |
| Historical reconstruction | Birth around 1480, often assigned to Knittlingen; proposed identification with Georg Helmstetter. Neither parentage nor birthplace is securely established by a contemporary birth record. **[MSR]** |
| Chapbook extension | Helen becomes Faust’s companion; Justus Faustus is their magically implicated son in later forms. **[LE]** |
| Goethe | No operative genealogy; Gretchen is lover and mother of his unnamed child; Helen is a second, mythic consort; Euphorion is their son. **[LC]** |
| Mann | Adrian Leverkühn is an analog, not Faust’s descendant or reincarnation. **[MI]** |

### Documented named interactions

The following inventory covers direct or explicitly staged interactions in the principal Faust corpus. Merely comparative pairings are marked accordingly.

- **God/The Lord** — In Goethe’s “Prologue in Heaven,” the Lord discusses Faust with Mephistopheles and permits his testing; Faust does not hear the exchange. *Faust I*, 1808. **[LC/SI]**
- **The Devil/Lucifer** — In the *Historia*, Faust’s contract ultimately belongs to Lucifer’s infernal government; Lucifer also appears in demonic spectacle. 1587. In Marlowe, Lucifer confronts Faustus, suppresses thoughts of Christ, and displays the Seven Deadly Sins. c. 1588–1592; A-text 1604, B-text 1616. **[LE/LC]**
- **Mephostophiles/Mephistophilis/Mephistopheles** — Serving spirit, tempter, guide, magical provider, coercive creditor, and final claimant. *Historia*, 1587; Marlowe, c. 1588–1592; Goethe, 1808/1832. **[LE/LC]**
- **Beelzebub** — Named within the infernal hierarchy rather than developed as Faust’s independent companion; associated with the power Faust invokes or serves in early English/German forms. *Historia*, 1587; Marlowe, A/B texts. **[LE/LC]**
- **Good Angel** — Urges Faustus to abandon magic and repent. Marlowe, c. 1588–1592; 1604/1616. **[LC]**
- **Evil Angel** — Encourages necromancy and despair. Marlowe, c. 1588–1592; 1604/1616. **[LC]**
- **The Seven Deadly Sins** — Appear before and address Faustus as infernal entertainment after his wavering. Marlowe, 1604/1616. **[LC/LS]**
- **Earth Spirit (*Erdgeist*)** — Faust invokes it; it appears and rejects his claim to equality. Goethe’s *Faust I*, 1808. **[LC]**
- **Helen of Troy** — Faust causes or receives her apparition in the *Historia* (1587) and Marlowe (1604/1616); Goethe makes her Faust’s consort and mother of Euphorion in *Faust II* (1832). The sexual and familial union is a later transformation, not early classical tradition. **[LE/LC/SI]**
- **Paris** — Faust evokes Paris with Helen before the emperor and attempts to seize Helen’s image, causing the apparition to explode or vanish. Goethe’s *Faust II*, 1832. **[LC]**
- **Alexander the Great** — Faust conjures or simulates Alexander’s presence for Emperor Charles V. *Historia*, 1587; Marlowe, 1604/1616. **[LE/LC]**
- **Alexander’s wife** — Appears with Alexander in the chapbook and Marlowe. Her inconsistent or non-historical identification underscores that the scene is illusionistic legend, not historiography. 1587; 1604/1616. **[LE/LC]**
- **Chiron** — The centaur carries Faust during the Classical Walpurgis Night and discusses Helen. Goethe’s *Faust II*, 1832. **[LC/SI]**
- **Manto** — The prophetic daughter of Asclepius leads Faust toward the underworld route associated with Persephone so that he may seek Helen. Goethe’s *Faust II*, 1832. **[LC/SI]**
- **Persephone** — Faust’s quest for Helen is referred to her underworld authority through Manto, but Goethe does not stage an extended face-to-face dialogue. *Faust II*, 1832. **[LC/SI]**
- **Homunculus** — Meets Faust after Wagner’s artificial creation and accompanies the party toward the Classical Walpurgis Night. *Faust II*, 1832. **[LC/MI within Goethe’s new mythology]**
- **Euphorion** — Faust and Helen’s son; his ecstatic attempt at flight ends in a fatal fall. *Faust II*, 1832. **[LC]**
- **Philemon and Baucis** — Goethe transfers the hospitable couple from Ovid into Faust’s reclamation landscape. Faust desires their property; Mephistopheles’ agents kill them while carrying out removal. *Faust II*, 1832. **[LC/SI]**
- **The Mater Gloriosa/Virgin-associated celestial figure** — Faust’s soul is drawn upward in the final mystical scene under her authority. *Faust II*, 1832. The nomenclature is Goethean and should not be treated as evidence of historical Marian devotion to Faust. **[LC/SI]**
- **Gretchen as *Una Poenitentium*** — The saved penitent intercedes for and guides Faust’s ascending soul. *Faust II*, 1832. **[LC]**
- **Job** — No direct encounter. The Lord–Mephistopheles wager in Goethe structurally recalls the Book of Job; calling Faust “Job” is comparison, not identification. 1808. **[SI/MSR]**
- **Theophilus of Adana** — No meeting or historical identification. His older Christian pact-and-recovery legend is a possible narrative antecedent. Direct transmission into the Faust book remains debated. **[MSR]**
- **Simon Magus** — No direct interaction. Humanist and scholarly comparisons arise because both are condemned magicians; equation with Faust senior is unsupported. **[LS/MSR]**
- **Prometheus** — No direct meeting in the canonical texts. Faust’s Promethean classification is a modern critical analogy based on forbidden aspiration and creation. **[MSR]**
- **Don Juan** — No early interaction. “Faust and Don Juan” is a later comparative pairing of intellectual and erotic transgression, notably developed in nineteenth- and twentieth-century criticism and adaptations. **[MI]**
- **Woland** — Faust never meets Woland. Bulgakov’s figure is a transformed descendant of Goethe’s Mephistophelean tradition, not an episode in Faust’s own story. 1928–1940/1966–1967. **[MI/SI]**

## 5. Cult, tomb, and ritual

There is **no evidence of historical religious cult** to Faust: no consecrated heroon, altar, votive deposit, feast, sacrifice, oracle, relic cult, liturgy, or theophoric naming practice. Absence is significant because Faust belongs to Christian demonological and literary legend rather than ancient hero cult. **[EA: negative finding]**

Claims sometimes described loosely as “Faust cult” are cultural commemoration:

- **Staufen im Breisgau** preserves the tradition that Faust died at the *Löwen* inn. Its early basis is Weyer’s 1563 report; particular rooms, plaques, and explosion narratives are later localization. **[LE/MI]**
- **Knittlingen** claims association with Faust’s birth and houses the Faust Museum, established in the old town hall in 1980. The museum documents reception; it is not a shrine or proof of birthplace. **[MI]**
- Sites in Wittenberg, Erfurt, Leipzig, Auerbachs Keller, and Prague have attached Faust stories or commercial memorials to particular buildings. Their evidentiary value must be judged individually; most document tourism and literary memory rather than the historical man’s presence. **[FV/MI]**
- Performances, anniversaries, museums, street names, monuments, and literary societies constitute secular commemoration, not worship. **[MI]**

No authenticated tomb or bodily relic survives. The chapbook’s burial by students is narrative; later Staufen death localization supplies no demonstrable grave. **[LE/EA: negative finding]**

## 6. Historical evolution

### 1507–1540: living controversial practitioner

Contemporary sources describe a mobile claimant to magical, astrological, medical, and divinatory expertise. Hostile witnesses call him fool, necromancer, fraud, sodomite, or braggart; accounts and patrons show that his services nevertheless had a market. The infernal contract is absent. **[EA]**

### 1540s–1586: demonization after death

Oral anecdotes, Protestant demonology, jest motifs, and accounts of a violent death coalesce. Manlius and Weyer make the Devil central. Martin Luther’s reported references to a Faust-like conjurer and devil’s ally belong to this confessional environment, though their textual transmission is later and should not be inflated into a personal encounter. **[LE]**

### 1587–1674: print canon and tragedy

The Spies *Historia* fixes the major sequence—Wittenberg theologian, conjuration, blood contract, Mephostophiles, twenty-four years, travels, courtly apparitions, Helen, failed repentance, dismemberment. Marlowe rapidly translates admonitory narrative into tragic theatre. Widmann and Pfitzer expand moral exposition. **[LE/LC]**

### Seventeenth–eighteenth centuries: popular theatre and rehabilitation

Actors’ plays and puppet theatre internationalize Faust and enlarge comic material. Enlightenment writers reconsider whether the thirst for knowledge is itself damnable. Lessing’s projected salvation of the seeker marks a fundamental ideological turn. **[FV/LC]**

### 1770s–1832: Goethean transformation

Goethe replaces the fixed twenty-four-year sale with a conditional wager, attaches the independent infanticide tradition of Gretchen to Faust, and ultimately saves both. Part Two makes Faust participate in classical myth, monetary policy, war, technology, land reclamation, colonial violence, and mystical redemption. **[LC]**

### Nineteenth century: European master myth

Translations, Delacroix’s 1828 lithographs, operas, symphonic works, poems, paintings, and popular theatre make Goethe’s version internationally dominant. Faust becomes a symbol of scientific ambition, industrial development, subjective restlessness, and the supposedly “German spirit.” **[MI]**

### 1871–1945: nationalization and ideological appropriation

Wilhelmine readings praised *Tat*, work, mastery of nature, and expansion. Racial-nationalist critics increasingly presented Faust as the dynamic German type. Nazi Germanists and propagandists appropriated Goethe: Baldur von Schirach in 1937 called *Faust* the quintessential German book, while Alfred Rosenberg treated Faust as an expression of the German racial essence. Some antisemitic readings cast Mephistopheles as a “Jewish spirit”; this was documented but never the only or universally adopted Nazi interpretation. A 1932 *Stürmer* caricature visually mobilized that equation. **[MI: extremist appropriation]**

### 1943–postwar: indictment of German modernity

Mann’s *Doktor Faustus* interprets artistic genius, lovelessness, disease, and Germany’s catastrophe through the pact. East German reception often reclaimed Faust as progressive labor and humanity moving beyond bourgeois limitations; West German debate gradually confronted the nationalist and fascist inheritance of Goethe scholarship. **[MI]**

### Late twentieth century–present

Faust is global shorthand for bargains involving science, weapons, capitalism, colonialism, technology, celebrity, and political power. Contemporary productions can cast him as financier, colonial exploiter, technocrat, or authoritarian entrepreneur. These are legitimate receptions but not recovered “ancient meanings.” **[MI]**

## 7. Modern reception and reinvention

### Literature and drama

- **Lessing’s fragments**, 1750s–1770s: the knowledge-seeker potentially saved. **[LC]**
- **Goethe**, 1770s–1832: definitive expansion from damnation tale to world drama. **[LC]**
- **Christian Dietrich Grabbe, *Don Juan und Faust***, 1829: explicit pairing of intellectual and erotic overreachers. **[MI]**
- **Nikolaus Lenau, *Faust***, 1836: alienation, nature, and defeat. **[MI]**
- **Paul Valéry, *Mon Faust***, begun during the Second World War and published unfinished in 1946: reflexive modern intellectual Faust. **[MI]**
- **Thomas Mann, *Doktor Faustus***, 1947: composer Adrian Leverkühn and Germany’s disastrous pact. **[MI]**
- **Bulgakov, *The Master and Margarita***, 1928–1940/1966–1967: creative reconfiguration rather than direct retelling. **[MI]**
- **Randy Newman, *Faust***, 1993 album and 1995 musical: satirical American reworking of God, Devil, and modern innocence. **[MI]**

### Music

- **Louis Spohr, *Faust***, 1816: early Romantic opera drawing more on popular Faust material than directly on Goethe. **[MI]**
- **Hector Berlioz, *Huit scènes de Faust***, 1829, and *La Damnation de Faust*, 1846. **[MI]**
- **Robert Schumann, *Szenen aus Goethes Faust***, composed 1844–1853. **[MI]**
- **Franz Liszt, *Eine Faust-Symphonie***, 1854–1857. **[MI]**
- **Charles Gounod, *Faust***, 1859: focuses on the Gretchen story and profoundly shaped the operatic popular image. **[MI]**
- **Arrigo Boito, *Mefistofele***, 1868, revised 1875–1876: centers the demon and draws from both Goethe parts. **[MI]**
- **Gustav Mahler, Symphony No. 8**, premiered 1910: its second part sets the closing scene of Goethe’s *Faust II*. **[MI]**
- **Ferruccio Busoni, *Doktor Faust***, left unfinished at his death in 1924 and completed by Philipp Jarnach: deliberately returns to chapbook and puppet traditions rather than merely adapting Goethe. **[MI]**
- **Alfred Schnittke, *Historia von D. Johann Fausten***, 1995: opera based on the early book tradition. **[MI]**

### Images

Eugène Delacroix produced seventeen lithographs for Albert Stapfer’s French translation of *Faust I*, published in **1828**. Their demonic, nocturnal imagery influenced later visual expectations; Goethe admired them. These images illustrate Goethe rather than the sixteenth-century historical Faust. **[MI]**

Later stage designs, book illustration, posters, and film repeatedly standardize the black-clad scholar, red or dark Mephistopheles, magic circle, blood signature, poodle, Gretchen at the spinning wheel, and infernal seizure. No securely contemporary portrait of the historical Faust is known. **[EA: negative finding/MI]**

### Film and media

- **Georges Méliès** repeatedly used Faust material in short trick films around 1897–1904, emphasizing transformation and spectacle. **[MI]**
- **F. W. Murnau, *Faust—Eine deutsche Volkssage***, 1926: combines chapbook, Marlowe, Goethe, and cinematic invention. **[MI]**
- **Richard Burton and Nevill Coghill, *Doctor Faustus***, 1967: film adaptation of Marlowe. **[MI]**
- **Jan Švankmajer, *Lekce Faust* (*Faust*)**, 1994: combines live action, marionettes, folklore, Marlowe, and Goethe. **[MI]**
- **Alexander Sokurov, *Faust***, 2011: a corporeal, grotesque reimagining and fourth film in his tetralogy on power. **[MI]**
- Comics, role-playing games, anime, video games, and speculative fiction commonly use “Faust” or “Faustian” for a scholar, alchemist, artificial creator, or person who exchanges autonomy for power. Such characters are modern descendants, not textual variants of the 1587 story unless an adaptation says so. **[MI]**

### “Faustian bargain”

Modern English uses **Faustian** for an exchange in which a person gains knowledge, power, wealth, survival, or short-term advantage at the cost of moral integrity, freedom, humanity, or ultimate ruin. The phrase compresses several incompatible plots: the chapbook’s unconditional blood contract, Marlowe’s twenty-four-year bargain, Goethe’s conditional wager, and modern secular compromises. It should not be projected back into the 1507 evidence. **[MI]**

### Psychological and theoretical readings

Faust has been read as Renaissance overreacher, divided Christian conscience, Enlightenment subject, capitalist entrepreneur, colonial developer, scientist, artist, addict, narcissist, and modern humanity. These are dated interpretations rather than recoverable components of the earliest legend. **[MSR/MI]**

Rank’s psychoanalytic work on heroic birth is only marginally applicable because Faust lacks a stable miraculous birth narrative. Raglan’s hero-pattern and Campbell’s monomyth fit poorly: Faust has no consistent royal birth, initiatory victory, return with a boon, or hero cult. Structural comparison can illuminate selected episodes but becomes misleading when it erases the confessional print culture that formed the legend. **[MSR]**

## 8. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Was there one historical Faust?

The conservative conclusion is that a real itinerant practitioner operated between at least 1507 and 1540, but his name, birthplace, education, and the unity of all notices remain uncertain. “Johann Georg Faust” is a retrospective composite label. **[MSR]**

Frank Baron’s Georg Helmstetter hypothesis connects the 1483 Heidelberg student with later “Faustus Heidelbergensis” evidence. It gives the practitioner an educational history and possible origin but depends on identification across differently named records. Critics emphasize the lack of an explicit contemporary equation. **[MSR]**

The elaborate genealogy printed in the *Neue Deutsche Biographie*—including specific parents, illegitimacy, and extended relatives—goes considerably beyond what the secure early notices establish and should not be repeated as settled fact. **[MSR]**

### What killed him?

- **Demonic violence:** attested as legend by Manlius and Weyer, 1562–1563. **[LE]**
- **Alchemy accident/explosion:** plausible modern rationalization of a violent death but not securely documented by a contemporary record. **[MSR]**
- **Ordinary death subsequently sensationalized:** possible but evidentially unprovable. **[MSR]**
- **Staufen, c. 1540/1541:** the strongest early localization, still posthumous. **[LE]**

No corpse or tomb resolves the question.

### When did the Faust book take shape?

The relationship among lost oral and written material, the Wolfenbüttel manuscript, and Spies’s 1587 print remains debated. The manuscript preserves older-looking material but is itself undated; “the Faust book of about 1580” is a textual reconstruction, whereas **1587** is the first indisputable printed date. **[MSR]**

The *Historia* is not merely folk transcription. It is a shaped Protestant admonitory work drawing on demonology, travel writing, jest books, theological dispute, and classical-magical exempla. **[LS]**

### Marlowe’s texts

The 1604 A-text and 1616 B-text differ too substantially to be treated as transparent copies of one original. The B-text likely incorporates theatrical revision and additions; the A-text may also be shortened or memorially affected. Scholarship has alternately privileged their brevity, theatricality, theology, and possible closeness to Marlowe. A responsible account cites which text carries a disputed line or episode. **[MSR]**

### Puppet-play priority

Older scholarship sometimes imagined an ancient, stable German puppet play feeding Goethe directly. Current evidence favors a mobile, reciprocal circulation among chapbooks, Marlowe, actors’ theatre, fairground performance, and puppetry. Most surviving puppet scripts are eighteenth- or nineteenth-century witnesses. **[MSR]**

### The pact’s antecedents

Theophilus of Adana, Simon Magus, Cyprian and Justina, medieval contracts with the Devil, and tales such as *Mariken van Nieumeghen* demonstrate that soul-bargain narrative structures predate Faust. Similarity does not by itself prove a direct line of transmission into the 1587 book. **[MSR]**

### Goethe’s ending

Faust’s salvation has generated opposing readings:

- salvation through continual striving;
- grace overriding merit;
- Gretchen’s intercession and the “eternal feminine”;
- an ironic salvation that does not erase murder, exploitation, and delusion;
- critique or endorsement of technological-colonial modernity.

The text supports tension among these positions. It does not straightforwardly declare every act of striving morally good. **[MSR]**

### Nationalist and antisemitic readings

Nineteenth- and twentieth-century critics made Faust a national type. Nazi appropriation is documented, but scholarship warns against assuming one unified Nazi interpretation. The explicit Mephistopheles-as-Jew reading existed in Dietrich Eckart, Georg Schott, popular propaganda, and related discourse; silence or ambiguity in other nationalist writers should not be converted into evidence of universal acceptance. **[MI/MSR]**

### State of the evidence

The principal early evidence is dispersed among correspondence, municipal and episcopal records, printed demonology, chapbooks, and later recollection. Faust’s own writings, if any, have not been securely authenticated. Magical works marketed under his name—notably grimoires such as a supposed *Höllenzwang*—are pseudepigraphic products of later occult publishing, not demonstrated autograph compositions. **[LE/MI]**

Major discoveries and editions include publication of early documentary testimonies, the nineteenth-century editing of the 1587 print, Gustav Milchsack’s 1892 edition of the Wolfenbüttel manuscript, modern editions by H. G. Haile, and the Füssel–Kreutzer critical edition comparing early states. **[MSR]**

## 10. Sources and assessment

### Primary and critical texts

The documentary core should be read through editions that reproduce Trithemius, Mutianus, account books, municipal records, Begardi, Hutten, Manlius, and Weyer. Frank Baron’s documentary work is especially valuable for distinguishing the historical practitioner from the literary composite, though individual identifications remain arguments rather than raw facts.

For the legend’s first canonical state, the indispensable text is the **1587 Spies edition**, checked against the **Wolfenbüttel manuscript** and 1587–1589 variants. The Füssel–Kreutzer critical edition provides the 1587 text, variant materials, sources, and early reception. Riedl and Roloff’s edition documents the younger 1589 version.

For Marlowe, a two-text edition is essential. The Folger digital A-text accurately records the first surviving 1604 quarto; any discussion of variants must also consult the 1616 B-text.

Goethe’s *Faust* must be treated as several composition states—*Urfaust*, 1790 fragment, Part One, and Part Two—not as a book written in a single moment. Modern digital genetic editions add manuscript evidence to the conventional 1808/1832 publication history.

### Findings from the contested-material pass

- **Historicity:** probable real practitioner; composite conventional name; no secure continuous biography.  
- **Birth and parentage:** Knittlingen and the elaborate Gerlach/Faust genealogy are reconstructions, not securely documented birth facts.  
- **Death:** Staufen tradition appears by 1563; demonic murder is legend; alchemical explosion is plausible but unproved.  
- **Tomb:** none authenticated.  
- **Cult:** none attested; museums and memorial sites are secular reception.  
- **Name:** Latin *faustus* and German *Faust* are both relevant; intentional pseudonymy remains disputed.  
- **Contract:** absent from lifetime evidence; canonical in 1587.  
- **Mephistopheles:** literary demon appearing with the developed legend, not an attested historical associate or pre-Christian deity.  
- **Grimoires:** later pseudepigrapha, not authenticated Faust autographs.  
- **Nationalism:** Faust’s conversion into the essence of “German striving” is chiefly nineteenth- and twentieth-century reception.  
- **Extremism:** Nazi and antisemitic appropriations are documented but internally varied; they are not the meaning of Goethe’s text.  
- **Bulgakov:** demonstrably Faustian and Goethean, but not a direct Faust biography.  
- **“Faustian bargain”:** modern abstraction combining distinct contracts and wagers from the chapbook, Marlowe, Goethe, and later culture.
