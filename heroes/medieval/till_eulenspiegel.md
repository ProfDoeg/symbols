# Till Eulenspiegel: Research Dossier

**Traditional class:** mortal folkloric trickster and wandering *Schalk*—an antihero of the German *Schwankbuch*, not a demigod, epic king, saint, or heroized dead. Later authors recast him as social critic, Protestant rebel, national hero, revolutionary, and children’s prankster. No ancient or medieval cult is securely attested.

**Earliest secure attestation:** three Latin letters exchanged in **1411** by the papal officials Dietrich von Nieheim and Johannes Schele mention *Ulenspiegel* and allude to his stone-sowing jest. This securely proves circulation of an Eulenspiegel name and story by 1411, but not the existence of the supposed fourteenth-century man. The earliest surviving substantial text is the Strasbourg *Ulenspiegel* edition conventionally dated **1510–1511**, preserved incompletely; the first complete, securely dated edition is Strasbourg **1515**.

## Evidentiary labels

- **[EARLY]** Secure early evidence, before or within the earliest print tradition.
- **[CANON]** The sixteenth-century German *Schwankbuch* tradition.
- **[VARIANT]** Regional or translation-specific variant.
- **[ACCRETION]** Later premodern addition or reinterpretation.
- **[FOLKLORE]** Locally collected oral or popular tradition.
- **[RECONSTRUCTION]** Modern scholarly hypothesis.
- **[REINVENTION]** Deliberate modern literary, artistic, or political transformation.
- **[MATERIAL]** Surviving manuscript, print, image, monument, or archaeological object.
- **[NEGATIVE FINDING]** No documentary evidence has been identified for the asserted practice or relationship.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

Till Eulenspiegel is the protagonist of a late-medieval/early-modern comic prose cycle centered on a peasant’s son from the Brunswick region who travels through northern and central Europe. He repeatedly obeys figurative commands literally, exploits equivocal words, exposes professional pretension, destroys materials entrusted to him, cheats hosts and clergy, and uses food, excrement, nudity, animals, and bodily disgust as weapons. **[CANON]** The earliest book does not consistently make him a moral reformer or champion of the poor: his victims include craftsmen, peasants, women, clergy, Jews, innkeepers, invalids, and animals, and many suffer without having committed an antecedent wrong. The familiar benign “wise fool holding society’s mirror” is substantially a later interpretation.

### Etymology

1. **Owl + mirror.** Middle Low German *ūle*/*ule*, “owl,” plus *spēgel*, “mirror,” yields the rebus displayed by the early woodcuts: Till carries an owl and mirror. **[CANON/MATERIAL]** Story 40 says he marks his work with an owl and a mirror; the final grave image repeats the emblems.

2. **Obscene imperative.** A widely accepted but not demonstrable reconstruction hears Low German *ulen* “wipe” and *spegel* “rear/buttocks,” hence approximately “wipe the arse” or “kiss my arse.” **[RECONSTRUCTION]** This suits the book’s scatology and repeated display of Till’s buttocks, but the linguistic pathway and original intention remain disputed. It should not be presented as a certain medieval etymology.

3. **Moral “mirror.”** Interpretations in which the wise owl holds up a mirror to society draw on medieval mirror literature and later moralization. **[ACCRETION]** The early book certainly permits such readings, but it never consistently defines Till as an altruistic moral educator.

4. **Iconographic ambiguity.** Medieval owls could signify darkness, folly, marginality, or demonic association as well as wisdom. **[RECONSTRUCTION]** Selecting only Athena-like wisdom retrospectively sanitizes the early imagery.

### Epithets and classifications

- *Schalk*, *Schelm*: rogue, mischief-maker. **[CANON]**
- *eines Bauern Sohn*: “a peasant’s son.” **[CANON]** Prominent in later sixteenth-century titles.
- “Narr” or court fool: common in later pictures and children’s books. **[ACCRETION]** Early Till is not regularly an appointed fool and initially lacks the fixed cap-and-bells costume.
- “Flemish spirit” or immortal spirit of Flanders: De Coster’s formulation. **[REINVENTION]**
- Protestant freedom fighter, Geuzen partisan: De Coster, 1867. **[REINVENTION]**
- Germanic/Niedersächsisch racial hero: nationalist and National Socialist appropriation. **[MODERN INVENTION/POLITICAL APPROPRIATION]**

There is no Arthurian affiliation in the primary tradition. **[NEGATIVE FINDING]** He is sometimes grouped editorially with Faust, Tannhäuser, the Pied Piper, or legendary medieval heroes, but no early text makes him a knight of Arthur, a companion of Tristan, Parzival, Lohengrin, Robin Hood, or one of the Nine Worthies.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### 2.1 The 1411 letters

**[EARLY]** Dietrich von Nieheim and Johannes Schele exchanged three Latin letters in 1411 which mention *Ulenspiegel* and evoke his sowing of stones. The correspondence also jokes about reading Eulenspiegel material instead of more edifying or classical works. It establishes:

- that the name already denoted a recognized comic character;
- that at least one later printed motif circulated by 1411;
- that some written Eulenspiegel material may then have existed.

It does **not** establish:

- a complete fifteenth-century Eulenspiegel book;
- that the 1510–1515 collection descended unchanged from such a book;
- that a historical Till performed the deed;
- that the character’s supposed life dates, parents, birthplace, and grave were known in 1411.

The 1959 *Neue Deutsche Biographie* treated these letters as proof that Eulenspiegel was already attached to particular jests early in the fifteenth century. More recent transmission scholarship likewise calls the correspondence the earliest secure textual witness.

### 2.2 Hermann Bote’s world chronicle

**[EARLY/MATERIAL]** Hermann Bote’s autograph Brunswick world chronicles, begun in the 1490s and continued in the early sixteenth century, enter the death of *Ulenspeygel* at Mölln in 1350, in one account from plague. The chronicle is independent of the surviving 1515 book in genre, but its proximity to the print tradition complicates claims of full independence. Its unknown source could have been local report, a grave tradition, circulating tales, or material used in compiling the prose book.

### 2.3 The Strasbourg print witnesses

1. **Strasbourg, c. 1510–1511.** **[MATERIAL/CANON]** Peter Honegger discovered sixteen leaves—apparently proof sheets—in a reused binding in 1969 and published a facsimile in 1973. Bernd Ulrich Hucker acquired a much larger, though incomplete, copy in 1975. Typographical and woodcut comparison places the edition before the 1515 and 1519 impressions, though details of dating have been disputed.

2. **Strasbourg, 1515.** **[MATERIAL/CANON]** *Ein kurtzweilig lesen von Dyl Vlenspiegel geboren vß dem land zu Brunßwick. Wie er sein leben volbracht hatt. XCVI seiner geschichten.* Printed in Johannes Grüninger’s Strasbourg shop. It is the earliest substantially complete, explicitly dated witness. Its eighty-five narrative woodcuts, title image, and grave image established the visual cycle.

3. **Strasbourg, 1519.** **[CANON]** A fuller witness frequently used by editors to supply material defective or absent in other early copies.

The printed title promises ninety-six histories. Counting is unstable: one reconstruction of the early text counts ninety-four actual narrative units, while modernized tables of contents normally number ninety-six. **[TEXTUAL VARIANT]** “Ninety-five tales” is therefore not a universally valid description; it may arise from counting the death-and-burial material differently. The surviving 1515 presentation is conventionally cited as **XCVI**.

### 2.4 Language and authorship

The surviving text is Early New High German with conspicuous Low German/North German matter. **[RECONSTRUCTION]** Peter Honegger’s 1973 attribution to the Brunswick customs official and chronicler **Hermann Bote** rested especially on:

- the sequence **ERMAN B** or **HERMAN B** formed by initials around the final Mölln histories;
- detailed Brunswick topography;
- North German vocabulary and geography;
- Bote’s record of Eulenspiegel’s death in his chronicle;
- thematic and linguistic parallels with Bote’s didactic and political works.

Herbert Blume and others regard Bote’s authorship or compilation as overwhelmingly probable. Jürgen Schulz-Grobert’s 1999 study objects that the surviving text is a product of the Strasbourg print environment, that the acrostic need not name Bote uniquely, and that one need not posit a lost Low German archetype. He emphasizes collaboration or redaction among people associated with Grüninger’s press. **The defensible formulation is “probably compiled or authored in an earlier form by Hermann Bote, substantially shaped in Strasbourg,” not “certainly written by Bote.”**

Earlier attributions to Thomas Murner, Johannes Pauli, Sebastian Brant, and others are part of the history of scholarship but lack comparable cumulative evidence. **[DISCREDITED OR MINORITY RECONSTRUCTION]**

### 2.5 European transmission

- **Dutch/Flemish:** The oldest extant Michiel Hillen van Hoochstraten edition is now generally placed around **1526–1532**, though older scholarship dated it to 1512, 1519, or c. 1520. **[VARIANT]**
- **English:** *Howleglas*, probably printed by Jan van Doesborch or from his Antwerp milieu, survives fragmentarily and is commonly dated to the early sixteenth century. **[VARIANT]**
- **French:** Paris editions appeared in **1532**, c. **1536**, and **1539**, announcing translation from Flemish. **[VARIANT]**
- These Dutch, French, and English texts form an “Antwerp group”: approximately forty-six stories compress about sixty German episodes and include material from *Salomon and Marcolf*. The English version has an additional verse tale. **[VARIANT]**
- **German expansions:** Frankfurt editions of **1549** and **1555** contain 102 histories; a 1593 edition likewise advertises 102. **[ACCRETION]**
- **Hans Sachs:** dramatized “Eulenspiegel and the blind men” in **1553** and also treated the fur-washing story. **[ACCRETION]**
- Danish, Swedish, Czech, Polish, and Latin versions carried the cycle across Europe during the early-modern period. Their titles and tale counts vary; none should automatically be used to reconstruct the lost German archetype.

### 2.6 Folklore and nineteenth-century collections

**[FOLKLORE/ACCRETION]** Regional tales continued to migrate onto Till. Wendish collections, for example, call him *Hansspiegel* in one tale and attach miniature or magical-seeming feats to him. Ludwig Bechstein’s nineteenth-century legend collection retells his upright burial and grave inscription. Such collections document nineteenth-century belief and localization; they are not witnesses to a fourteenth-century biography.

Grimm’s *Deutsche Sagen* belongs to the broader antiquarian context, but it is not the foundational primary corpus for Till in the way the 1411 letters and Strasbourg prints are. The Arthurian sources named in the comparative brief—Chrétien, Wolfram, Gottfried, Béroul, Thomas, the *Schwanritter* tradition, and the Nine Worthies—contain no Till Eulenspiegel. **[NEGATIVE FINDING]**

## 3. The story

### 3.1 Birth, baptism, and childhood

**[CANON: Histories 1–9]** Till is born near the forest called Melme, at Kneitlingen in Brunswick territory, to the peasant **Claus Ulenspiegel** and **Ann Witcken/Witcken**. A godfather named Till is supplied in some recensions. He is carried to baptism at Ampleben; returning home, his carrier falls from a footbridge into filthy water, and at home he is washed in a kettle. The narrator therefore jokes that he was baptized three times: sacramentally, in the muddy pool, and in warm water.

As a small child he exposes his backside from his father’s horse and, when seated facing forward, grimaces at the villagers. Complaints cause the family to move. The child tricks other boys out of their shoes, cheats a baker, participates in gluttonous contests, torments chickens by making them struggle over tied bait, and escapes thieves by hiding in a beehive.

**Development:** The opening sequence creates a pseudo-biographical frame for pre-existing jests. The triple baptism, obscene exposure, animal cruelty, and early deceit mark him from the outset as socially inverted; the book does not narrate a lost innocent youth.

### 3.2 Literal obedience and service

**[CANON: especially Histories 10–15, 40–54, 60–64, 73]** The most characteristic mechanism is malicious literalism:

- Ordered to defecate where he finds *Henep* (“hemp”), he does so in *Senep* (“mustard”).
- Told to “clear” a merchant’s house, he carries its contents outside.
- As a smith’s servant he takes workshop commands literally and ruins bellows, hammers, and tongs.
- Told by a shoemaker to cut leather “large and small, as the swineherd drives them through the gate,” he cuts animal silhouettes.
- Told to sew “under the tub,” he sits physically beneath it.
- At a baker’s in Brunswick, asked what kind of creature he is and told sarcastically to bake owls and monkeys, he produces bread in those shapes; these become one of the few pranks with a profitable outcome.
- As a brewer, told to boil “Hopf,” he boils the brewer’s dog named Hopf instead of hops.
- For a Berlin furrier he makes literal wolves rather than wolf-skin garments.
- In Brunswick he boils a tanner’s leather with the chairs and benches.
- At a Hamburg barber’s he enters through the window because he was told to come “through” it.

**Function:** The tales expose the danger of formulaic speech and the asymmetry of master-servant labor. Yet Till regularly destroys scarce materials and harms uninvolved animals. Reading every episode as justified class revenge is a modern moral systematization.

### 3.3 Public performance and learned imposture

**[CANON: Histories 16–34]**

- At Magdeburg he announces that he will fly from the town hall. When a crowd gathers, he laughs at people foolish enough to believe that a man can fly.
- He poses as a physician and deceives a learned doctor connected with the bishop of Magdeburg.
- As tower watchman for the Count of Anhalt, he gives warnings at the wrong times, excusing himself through literal constructions.
- The King of Denmark is made to pay for golden horseshoes.
- Till defeats the King of Poland’s professional fool in an obscene contest.
- Banished by the Duke of Lüneburg, he stands inside the carcass of his horse or sits on soil he has bought, claiming to remain on his own ground rather than the duke’s territory.
- He promises the Landgrave of Hesse a marvelous painting visible only to those of legitimate birth, a close relative of the “invisible cloth” international tale type.
- At Prague University he answers impossible questions with sophistical counterclaims.
- At Erfurt he undertakes to teach a donkey to read; oats placed between a psalter’s leaves make the animal turn pages and vocalize “I-A.”
- He carries a skull as a supposed relic and receives offerings.
- At Rome, the pope regards his behavior as heretical; Till escapes through equivocation.

These episodes expand the peasant rogue into an international traveler confronting courts, universities, churches, and civic crowds. The institutional satire is real, but the named rulers are usually types rather than securely identifiable historical contemporaries.

### 3.4 Food, money, and excrement

**[CANON]** Scatology is structural, not incidental:

- Till contaminates plums, beds, baths, rooms, tables, food, tools, and an apothecary’s container with excrement.
- At Frankfurt he sells his feces to Jews as “prophet berries.” The tale depends on anti-Jewish stereotypes and greed mythology and should be identified plainly as an antisemitic episode in the early book.
- He makes diners reject food by dripping or appearing to drip substances from his backside.
- He pays an innkeeper for the smell of roast meat with the sound of money—an internationally circulating tale not exclusive to him.
- He places emetic in an apple.
- He flays a dog and tenders its skin to an innkeeper.
- He tricks a priest into putting his hands into feces during the testament scene.
- He threatens or humiliates women innkeepers, including setting one bare-bottomed in hot ashes.

**Assessment:** Later children’s adaptations systematically omit or soften these episodes. The resulting amiable prankster is a nineteenth- and twentieth-century editorial creation.

### 3.5 Trades, towns, and victims

The itinerary includes Kneitlingen, Ampleben, Brunswick, Hildesheim, Magdeburg, Einbeck, Bernburg/Anhalt, Lüneburg, Prague, Erfurt, Nuremberg, Bamberg, Rome, Frankfurt, Quedlinburg, Wismar, Berlin, Leipzig, Lübeck, Dresden, Bremen, Hanover, Hamburg, Cologne, Rostock, Eisleben, Mariental, and Mölln. **[CANON]**

The geography is not a reliable travel diary. Some institutions are anachronistic: the Erfurt university, for example, was founded in 1392, decades after the legendary death date of 1350. **[EVIDENCE AGAINST BIOGRAPHICAL READING]** Other stories can be traced to older or international jest traditions. The route is a compiler’s narrative geography, not proof of an individual’s movements.

### 3.6 Illness, testament, death, and burial

**[CANON: final Mölln cycle]** In Mölln Till becomes ill, defecates into an apothecary’s box, and is taken to the Hospital of the Holy Spirit. Asked to repent, he regrets only tricks that he failed to perform. During his testament he promises property to friends, Mölln’s council, and the priest, but the supposed treasure involves a final excrement trick.

He dies during or after this sequence. Pigs overturn or disturb the bier during the funeral. He has stipulated that neither clergy nor laity should bury him, so **Beguines** perform the burial. His coffin slips or is lowered so that he stands upright; the community leaves him that way because inversion suits him. The grave image shows an owl and mirror and gives 1350.

**Variants:**

- The world chronicle says he died at Mölln in 1350, in one version of plague. **[EARLY]**
- The prose book emphasizes final pranks, disrupted funeral, Beguines, and upright burial. **[CANON]**
- Bechstein and later folklore consolidate these details into a localized grave legend. **[FOLKLORE]**
- De Coster’s Ulenspiegel does not die: the spirit of Flanders cannot be buried. **[REINVENTION]**
- Strauss has Till tried and hanged, followed by music suggesting his continuing vitality. **[REINVENTION]**
- Kehlmann’s *Tyll* displaces the figure into the seventeenth century and again treats survival ambiguously. **[REINVENTION]**

## 5. Grave, cult, and ritual

### 5.1 Mölln grave tradition

The 1515 book depicts or describes a grave marker at Mölln bearing the year 1350 and owl-and-mirror emblems. Bote’s world chronicle also assigns death at Mölln to 1350. **[EARLY/CANON]** Together these establish a grave tradition by around 1500, not the authenticity of a burial made in 1350.

A memorial stone is now mounted at the west side of St Nicholas’ Church in Mölln. The visible stone is not an untouched fourteenth-century gravestone. Accounts state that an earlier stone was damaged and renewed in the seventeenth century; architectural analysis has also suggested that the object may originally have served another function, perhaps as the backing of a religious image. **[CONTESTED MATERIAL]**

The often-reproduced Low German epitaph—“In the year 1350 this stone was raised; Tyl Ulenspiegel lies buried beneath … all who pass must become as I am”—is documented through early-modern and antiquarian transmission. It cannot simply be dated to 1350 from its wording.

### 5.2 Historicity positions

**Affirmative reconstruction:** Gerhard Cordes’s 1959 *Neue Deutsche Biographie* considered the existence of a historical bearer likely or virtually certain, citing:

- Eulenspiegel-like surnames in the Brunswick region;
- Bote’s chronicle notice;
- the Mölln grave tradition;
- the 1411 letters;
- apparent independence among some death notices.

Bernd Ulrich Hucker later proposed a specific historical crystallization figure, *Tilo dictus Ulenspegel*, and investigated Tile von Kneitlingen and related documentary names. **[RECONSTRUCTION]**

**Critical position:**

- No contemporary fourteenth-century document securely identifies the legendary protagonist.
- The 1411 letters attest a story-character sixty-one years after the alleged death, not a man.
- A surname or byname does not connect its bearer to the tales.
- The grave’s physical fabric and inscription are not securely fourteenth-century.
- The prose itinerary contains later institutions and tales borrowed from an international reservoir.
- No prank in the book can be assigned confidently to a demonstrable historical Till.

**Conclusion:** A historical catalyst is possible, but “Till Eulenspiegel, born c. 1300 and died 1350” is a traditional dossier assembled around a literary character, not a verified life.

### 5.3 Cult and ritual

- No medieval chapel, altar, votive inscription, relic translation, sacrificial practice, liturgical office, confraternity, pilgrimage indulgence, or feast dedicated to Till is documented. **[NEGATIVE FINDING]**
- The Hospital of the Holy Spirit and St Nicholas’ Church are historical religious institutions, but their presence in the death tradition does not constitute a Till cult.
- The alleged armor once displayed at Mölln’s town hall belongs to antiquarian relic-making, not verified medieval relic veneration. **[ACCRETION]**
- Modern statues, fountains, museums, festivals, tourism, and touching or visiting memorials are commemorative reception, not hero cult in the historical-religious sense.
- Mölln and Schöppenstedt operate museums; Kneitlingen, Brunswick, Bernburg, and other places monumentalize local episodes. **[MODERN COMMEMORATION]**

## 6. Historical evolution

### I. Before 1411: hypothetical formation

**[RECONSTRUCTION]** Individual literal-obedience, trickster, invisible-art, food-smell, false-relic, and scatological tales circulated independently. Nothing securely proves that they were then attached to a man named Till.

### II. 1411–c. 1500: recognizable tale-bearer

**[EARLY]** The Nieheim–Schele correspondence establishes *Ulenspiegel* as a known comic name. Bote’s chronicle fixes death at Mölln in 1350. During this period, regional tales were probably being attached to the figure.

### III. c. 1510–1519: print canonization

**[CANON]** Grüninger’s Strasbourg shop turns heterogeneous tales into a pseudo-life from birth through burial, supplies a geographic itinerary, and fixes the owl-and-mirror visual rebus. The book combines oral matter, literary exempla, international joke types, local knowledge, and editorial composition.

### IV. 1520s–seventeenth century: European adaptation

**[VARIANT]** Dutch, English, French, Polish, Scandinavian, Czech, and Latin versions abridge, combine, moralize, and add stories. The Antwerp group reshapes about sixty German episodes into forty-six or forty-seven tales. German editions expand to 102 histories. The name becomes lexicalized in French *espiègle*.

### V. Eighteenth century: moralization and pictorial modernization

**[ACCRETION]** Editions turn the history sequence into a more continuous “life,” add moral explanations, and dress Till as a fashionable young man or recognizable fool. Leonhard Bramer’s c. 1646 drawings and later engravings loosen the one-woodcut-per-tale format. Images of any man looking at or displaying his backside begin to receive folk identification as Eulenspiegel, even without textual proof.

### VI. Nineteenth century: folk hero and national symbol

Romantic nationalism and the category of the “Volksbuch” recast the collection as the authentic wit of a people. Scholarly editions—especially Lappenberg’s 1854 edition and later facsimiles—recover early texts but sometimes overconfidently assign authorship.

Charles De Coster’s **1867** *La Légende et les aventures héroïques, joyeuses et glorieuses d’Ulenspiegel et de Lamme Goedzak* relocates Till from fourteenth-century Saxony to sixteenth-century Flanders. Claes is burned by the Inquisition; Till carries his father’s ashes symbolically on his breast, joins the Geuzen struggle, serves the cause of William of Orange, and becomes the deathless spirit of Flanders. **[REINVENTION]** The Protestant patriot is De Coster’s creation, not a recovered medieval version.

### VII. 1895–1945: musical canon and ideological competition

Richard Strauss completed *Till Eulenspiegels lustige Streiche*, Op. 28, in May **1895**; Franz Wüllner conducted the premiere in Cologne on **5 November 1895**. Strauss’s rondo-like tone poem gives Till two recurring musical identities, follows him through market disorder, flirtation, clerical mockery, trial, and hanging, and ends with an epilogue implying that the rogue’s spirit survives. **[REINVENTION]**

Other works include Emil von Reznicek’s opera *Till Eulenspiegel* (1902), Walter Braunfels’s *Ulenspiegel* (1913), Ballets Russes treatment in 1916, and Gerhart Hauptmann’s postwar verse epic, developed in the 1920s and published in 1928.

### VIII. Political appropriation

De Coster’s Till served Belgian liberal patriotism, Flemish cultural assertion, socialism, and later separatist nationalism. During the 1920s–40s, some Flemish nationalist productions stripped away comedy and made him an authoritarian national warrior. Marnix Beyen documents his adoption in anti-Belgian theatre, Verdinaso and VNV environments, and wartime propaganda. Some versions became openly antidemocratic and antisemitic. **[POLITICAL REINVENTION]**

In National Socialist Germany, Ernst August Roloff’s *Ewiger Eulenspiegel* (1940) treated him as a racially pure embodiment of combative life-affirmation; Peter Osten’s *Till ist wieder im Lande* made him a model Hitler Youth figure. A Nazi-era Kneitlingen monument depicts the blond, heroicized Lower Saxon promoted by regional cultural politics. **[MODERN INVENTION/EXTREMIST APPROPRIATION]**

The figure also supported resistance, left-wing satire, and intercultural reconciliation. “Till” was therefore not inherently fascist, liberal, socialist, or separatist; rival movements selected different reinventions.

### IX. After 1945: divided German and international afterlives

- East German versions emphasized anticlericalism, popular resistance, and conflict with feudal power.
- The DEFA/French film *Les Aventures de Till l’Espiègle* appeared in **1956**, with Gérard Philipe.
- Rainer Simon’s **1973–74** film, developed from Christa and Gerhard Wolf’s treatment, placed Till amid early-sixteenth-century conflict and sharpened social criticism.
- Belgian comics by Ray Goossens, Willy Vandersteen, and George van Raemdonck used both the comic duo and De Coster’s rebel tradition.
- Children’s editions by Erich Kästner and others removed feces, cruelty, religious hostility, and sexual material, consolidating the harmless jester. **[MODERN SANITIZATION]**
- Daniel Kehlmann’s *Tyll* (**2017**) relocates him to the Thirty Years’ War and uses the indestructible performer to traverse religious violence, war, and historical memory. **[REINVENTION]**

### Literature

- Nikolai Karetnikov and Pavel Lungin, samizdat opera *Till Eulenspiegel* (1983; official release 1990).

### Visual art

- Strasbourg 1515: title woodcut of mounted Till with owl and mirror; eighty-five story illustrations and a grave image. **[CANON/MATERIAL]**

Claims that every medieval or early-modern image of a person exposing his backside represents Till are unsound. The RDK catalog notes several sculptures so identified by local tradition without iconographic proof. **[CONTESTED IDENTIFICATION]**

### Film and comics

- *Till Eulenspiegel und der Bäcker von Braunschweig* (1954), puppet animation.
- *Till Eulenspiegel als Türmer* (1955), puppet animation.

- George van Raemdonck’s adaptation (1964).

### Commercial and lexical afterlife

Eulenspiegel names museums, restaurants, publishing ventures, satirical periodicals, tourism programs, awards, streets, fountains, schools, and consumer products. French *espiègle* became detached from the proper name and now ordinarily means playfully mischievous. These are reception phenomena, not evidence for medieval biography or cult.

## 8. Historiography and scholarly debates

### 8.1 Was there a historical Till?

The affirmative case is cumulative but indirect: 1411 recognition, c. 1500 death notice, local name forms, and Mölln’s grave tradition. The skeptical case stresses the absence of a contemporary record and the migratory nature of the tales. Scholarship can responsibly speak of a possible historical crystallization figure; it cannot reconstruct a verified life.

The proposed identity *Tile von Kneitlingen* is especially uncertain. A documentary Tile existed, but connecting him to *Ulenspiegel* depends on later association and onomastic inference. **[RECONSTRUCTION, NOT IDENTIFICATION]**

### 8.2 Is the grave authentic?

Three separate propositions must be kept apart:

1. Mölln possessed an Eulenspiegel grave tradition by around 1500: **well attested**.
2. A memorial stone descended from an earlier object: **plausible but materially complicated**.
3. The stone marks the verified grave of a man who died in 1350: **unproved**.

Claims that the current object is simply “Till’s gravestone from 1350” collapse all three.

### 8.3 Who wrote the book?

- **Bote hypothesis:** acrostic, Brunswick geography, chronicle notice, vocabulary, professional knowledge, and thematic parallels.
- **Strasbourg workshop hypothesis:** High German form, editorial intervention, humanist and printing-house networks, and the weakness of treating the acrostic as a signature.
- **Current cautious synthesis:** Bote is the strongest named candidate for compiler or author of a source-stage; Strasbourg redaction is undeniable; sole authorship remains unproved.

### 8.4 Was there a lost Low German print?

Older research often posited a Low German manuscript or print before the Strasbourg High German edition. The discovery of the c. 1510–1511 Strasbourg witnesses weakened the need for such a lost edition. Some scholars retain a Low German source-stage; others argue that Low German material was incorporated directly in Strasbourg. **[OPEN TEXTUAL QUESTION]**

### 8.5 How many stories?

The title’s **XCVI** does not settle the number of independent narrative units. Early copies are incomplete; headings, combined episodes, and the division of the Mölln death sequence differ. Later editions expand to 102. Any statement that “the original consists of exactly ninety-five stories” requires identification of the edition and counting system.

### 8.6 Oral tradition versus literary compilation

Some episodes are demonstrably international tale types or derive from literary traditions such as *Salomon and Marcolf*. Others contain precise North German localization. The collection is therefore neither a transparent transcript of folk memory nor a wholly original authored novel. It is a printed composite whose pseudo-biographical sequence gives migratory jests a single protagonist.

### 8.7 Social meaning

Major readings include:

- class conflict between mobile worker and guild master;
- carnivalesque inversion of authority;
- linguistic critique through literal obedience;
- urban anxiety about itinerancy and fraud;
- bodily grotesque and scatological degradation;
- anticlerical satire;
- moral warning against disorder;
- celebration of vernacular cleverness.

No single reading covers every tale. The protagonist sometimes reveals hypocrisy, but he also abuses the weak, participates in antisemitic humor, harms animals, destroys livelihoods, and attacks without provocation.

### 8.8 Discredited claims that continue to circulate

- “The present Mölln stone is unquestionably from 1350.” **Unsupported.**
- “Bote’s authorship is proved by a complete signature.” **Overstated.**
- “All ninety-five pranks were performed by one historical man.” **Unsupported.**
- “Till was a Protestant rebel against Spain.” **De Coster’s 1867 invention.**
- “He was a medieval court jester in cap and bells.” **Later iconographic simplification.**
- “He always punished only the powerful and guilty.” **Contradicted by the early tales.**
- “He belonged to Arthurian or Germanic pagan mythology.” **No primary evidence.**
- “Modern visits to his memorial demonstrate a medieval hero cult.” **Category error.**

## 10. Sources and assessment

### Primary and material witnesses

- Dietrich von Nieheim–Johannes Schele correspondence, 1411: earliest named attestation, known through edited letter material and modern transmission scholarship.
- Hermann Bote, Brunswick world chronicles, autograph manuscripts begun c. 1493: earliest death notice.
- *Ein kurtzweilig lesen von Dyl Vlenspiegel*, Strasbourg c. 1510–11 fragments: earliest surviving book witness.
- Strasbourg 1515 and 1519 editions: foundational German prose cycle.
- Early Dutch, English *Howleglas*, and French editions: independent evidence for rapid European adaptation.
- Charles De Coster, *La Légende d’Ulenspiegel* (1867): primary source for the Flemish rebel.
- Richard Strauss, autograph and critical edition of *Till Eulenspiegels lustige Streiche* (1895): primary musical reinvention.
- Mölln memorial stone and church setting: material evidence for commemoration, not proof of the alleged burial.

### Principal scholarship consulted

- Gerhard Cordes, “Eulenspiegel, Till,” *Neue Deutsche Biographie* 4 (1959): historicity, early record, and tradition.
- Friedrich Thöne and Thomas Poensgen, “Eulenspiegel,” *Reallexikon zur Deutschen Kunstgeschichte* VI (1970): iconography and illustrated editions.
- Peter Honegger, *Ulenspiegel: Ein Beitrag zur Druckgeschichte und zur Verfasserfrage* (1973): early fragments and Bote attribution.
- Jürgen Schulz-Grobert, *Das Straßburger Eulenspiegelbuch* (1999): Strasbourg production and critique of the simple Bote model.
- Krystyna Wierzbicka-Trwoga, “Ulenspiegel in Dutch, French, and English” (2023): current comparative account of early translations.
- Marnix Beyen, *Held voor alle werk: De vele gedaanten van Tijl Uilenspiegel*: Belgian, Flemish-nationalist, fascist, socialist, and wartime appropriations.
- Richard Strauss critical-edition documentation: composition, print history, form, and premiere.
