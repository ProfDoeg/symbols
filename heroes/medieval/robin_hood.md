# Robin Hood: Research Dossier

> **Traditional class:** mortal outlaw hero; in the earliest texts a violent but devout English yeoman and leader of forest outlaws, not a demigod, saint, nobleman, or object of cult. Later tradition successively makes him a May-game lord, dispossessed earl, loyalist of Richard I, Saxon resistance hero, national hero, redistributive champion, and commercial folk icon.
>
> **Earliest secure attestation:** William Langland’s Middle English *Piers Plowman*, B-text, Passus V, composed about 1377–79. Sloth says that he cannot recite the Lord’s Prayer properly but knows “rymes of Robyn Hood.” This proves that recognizable Robin Hood narratives circulated by the late fourteenth century; it supplies no biography, date, forest, companions, or deeds. The first surviving narrative, *Robin Hood and the Monk*, is preserved in Cambridge University Library MS Ff.5.48, copied about 1450.

## Evidentiary key

- **[EA] Securely attested early material:** documentary or literary evidence before approximately 1500.
- **[LC] Later canonical elaboration:** a later element that became central to the received legend.
- **[RV] Regional variant:** geographically localized or competing local tradition.
- **[LS] Learned systematization:** an antiquarian, chronicle, editorial, or scholarly effort to turn scattered stories into history.
- **[SI] Syncretistic identification:** merger with another figure, ritual role, or narrative tradition.
- **[EM] Medieval or early-modern accretion:** post-formative material before approximately 1700.
- **[F] Folklore:** oral, customary, place, grave, or popular-belief tradition not independently verified as history.
- **[MSR] Modern scholarly reconstruction:** a hypothesis or interpretation, not primary narrative evidence.
- **[MI] Modern invention or reinvention:** material created principally from the eighteenth century onward.

Dates refer to composition or first attestation where reasonably established; manuscript and print dates are distinguished when important.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### Orientation

**[EA]** The earliest recoverable Robin Hood is a famous subject of popular “rhymes,” apparently known below the level of clerical learning by about 1377. Fifteenth-century poems make him an English outlaw, usually a yeoman, intensely devoted to the Virgin Mary, commanding Little John, Much the Miller’s Son, and Will Scarlet or Scathlock. He inhabits either Sherwood or, more often in the early substantial narratives, Barnsdale. He hunts deer, waylays wealthy travelers and churchmen, holds coercive forest dinners, excels with the longbow, uses disguise, fights savagely, and clashes repeatedly with the sheriff. He is not yet the Earl of Huntingdon, a Saxon patriot, Maid Marian’s courtly lover, Richard the Lionheart’s partisan, or an indiscriminate benefactor of the poor.

**[LC]** The familiar composite—green-clad noble outlaw of Sherwood, robbed of his lands by Prince John, lover of Marian, friend of Friar Tuck, defender of the poor, and loyal servant of Richard I—is the product of Tudor drama, seventeenth-century balladry, eighteenth-century antiquarian editing, Walter Scott, nineteenth-century children’s literature, and twentieth-century film.

### Forms of the name

- **[EA]** *Robyn Hood*, *Robyn Hode*, *Robert Hood*, *Robert Hude*, and *Robertus Hood* are medieval and early-modern spellings. *Robin* is the ordinary diminutive of *Robert*, from continental Germanic *Hrōdebert*, conventionally “bright-fame” or “fame-bright.” *Hod/Hode/Hood* was a real medieval surname and could refer to a hood-maker or wearer; documentary bearers need not have any connection to the legend.
- **[EA/MSR]** David Crook found that William son of Robert le Fevre, a Berkshire fugitive named normally in a 1261 eyre record, was called *William Robehod* when his case was copied in 1262. Similar fused forms—*Robehod*, *Rabunhod*, *Robinhood*—occur later. The evidence strongly suggests that “Robin Hood” could function as an outlaw nickname by 1262. It does not establish whether the nickname derived from an already existing hero, from a generic criminal label, or from some lost intermediate tradition.
- **[MSR]** J. C. Holt regarded the 1262 evidence and later criminal aliases as grounds for placing the formation of the legend before the late thirteenth century. Stephen Knight stresses that names and aliases do not recover a single originating person.
- **[LS, discredited]** “Robin of the Wood,” *Hood* as a dialect form of *wood*, derivation from the Norse god Höðr, identification with Woden, and derivation from a Germanic sprite called Hodekin were nineteenth- and early-twentieth-century proposals. No linguistic or textual chain establishes them.
- **[LS/MI, discredited]** William Stukeley constructed “Robert Fitzooth,” claimant to the earldom of Huntingdon, partly by treating *Fitzooth* as a precursor of *Hood*. The genealogy is antiquarian fabrication, not medieval evidence.
- **[MI]** *Robin of Locksley/Loxley* is not an early name. Loxley associations grew through local antiquarianism and modern romance; Scott’s *Ivanhoe* (1819) calls him “Locksley” before revealing him as Robin Hood.
- **[LC]** *Robert, Earl of Huntingdon* is established dramatically by Anthony Munday’s *The Downfall of Robert, Earl of Huntington* and *The Death of Robert, Earl of Huntington* (performed 1598–99; printed 1601). Earlier narrative ballads call Robin a yeoman. Richard Grafton’s *Chronicle at Large* (1569) had already moved toward gentrification by suggesting that Robert Hood might have been raised to an earldom.
- **[MI]** “Robin of Sherwood,” “the Prince of Thieves,” and comparable advertising titles are modern literary, theatrical, and screen epithets rather than medieval titles.

### Places embedded in the identity

- **[EA]** Sherwood is named in *Robin Hood and the Monk* and in an early-fifteenth-century scribble, “Robyn hod in scherewod stod.”
- **[EA]** Barnsdale is named in *A Gest of Robyn Hode*, *Robin Hood and Guy of Gisborne*, Walter Bower’s *Scotichronicon*, and Andrew of Wyntoun’s chronicle tradition. It is more frequent than Sherwood in substantial pre-1500 witnesses.
- **[EA]** A 1429 legal formula, “Robin Hode in Barnsdale stood,” treats that statement as proverbial obviousness.
- **[MSR]** The oldest written evidence therefore does not authorize an exclusively Nottinghamshire or exclusively Yorkshire Robin. The geography was plural before later tourism and civic identity hardened it.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### Pre-narrative witnesses

1. **[EA] *Piers Plowman*, B-text, c. 1377–79, Middle English.** Sloth knows “rymes of Robyn Hood” but not his prayers. It proves circulation and popular recognizability, not historicity or story content.
2. **[EA] *Friar Daw’s Reply*, c. 1402.** It uses the proverb that many speak of Robin Hood but never shot his bow—evidence that the name had become proverbial.
3. **[EA] *Dives and Pauper*, c. 1405–10.** It complains that people prefer tales or songs of Robin Hood to religious instruction, documenting clerical unease and popular performance.
4. **[EA] Lincoln Cathedral MS 132, first quarter of the fifteenth century.** The line “Robyn hod in scherewod stod” is the earliest compact placement in Sherwood, but it is not a narrative.
5. **[EA] Andrew of Wyntoun, *Orygynale Cronykil of Scotland*, c. 1420.** Under 1283, Little John and Robert Hood are commendable forest outlaws in Barnsdale and Inglewood. This is retrospective chronicle placement, not contemporary evidence.
6. **[EA] Exeter civic record, 1426.** The first generally accepted documentary record of a “Robin Hood” game. It demonstrates enacted custom but does not preserve its script.
7. **[EA] Walter Bower’s continuation of *Scotichronicon*, c. 1440.** Under 1266, Bower places “Robert Hood” and Little John among the dispossessed after Simon de Montfort’s defeat. He calls Robin a celebrated murderer or cutthroat whom foolish people honor in comedies and tragedies. Bower is evidence for fifteenth-century reputation and performance, not for events in 1266.

### The four early narrative texts

1. **[EA] *Robin Hood and the Monk*, Middle English; CUL MS Ff.5.48, c. 1450.** Robin quarrels with Little John, goes to St Mary’s Church in Nottingham, is betrayed by a monk, captured by the sheriff, and rescued by Little John and Much. The rescuers murder the monk and his young page, deceive the king, kill the jailer, and free Robin. The king grudgingly praises Little John’s fidelity. This is the earliest surviving full narrative.
2. **[EA] *Robin Hood and the Potter*, Middle English; CUL MS Ee.4.35, later fifteenth century.** A potter defeats Robin in a fight; Robin exchanges clothes with him, sells pots cheaply in Nottingham, charms the sheriff’s wife, wins an archery demonstration, and tricks the sheriff into the greenwood. The tone is comic and trickster-like.
3. **[EA] *A Gest of Robyn Hode*, Middle English; probably assembled in the later fifteenth century, surviving chiefly in prints from about 1500 onward.** In eight “fyttes,” Robin aids the indebted knight Sir Richard at the Lee, humiliates the abbot of St Mary’s York, kills or defeats the sheriff, encounters a disguised King Edward, enters royal service, tires of court, returns to the forest, and eventually dies at Kirklees through the treachery of a prioress and Sir Roger of Doncaster. It is the closest thing to a medieval synthetic cycle.
4. **[EA/EM] *Robin Hood and Guy of Gisborne*, probably rooted in the late fifteenth century but preserved in the seventeenth-century Percy Folio.** Guy, wearing a horsehide, hunts Robin. Robin kills and beheads him, mutilates the face, assumes his identity, rescues Little John, and kills the sheriff. Its late manuscript does not permit every detail to be assigned securely to the fifteenth century.

### Plays, chronicles, prints, and ballad canon

- **[EA] *Robyn Hod and the Shryff off Notyngham*, c. 1475 or later fifteenth century.** A fragmentary dramatic text involving Robin, the sheriff, a knight, and a friar; it is important evidence that theatrical episodes and the fighting-friar motif were already developing.
- **[EM] John Major, *Historia Majoris Britanniae* (Latin, 1521).** Major dates Robin and Little John to Richard I’s captivity in 1193–94, says they robbed only the wealthy, spared women, did not despoil the poor, and enriched them from abbots’ goods. This is the first influential Richard-I chronology and an early explicit moralization.
- **[EM] John Leland, *Collectanea*, assembled in the 1530s.** Records the Kirklees burial tradition.
- **[EM] William Copland’s *A Mery Geste of Robyn Hoode* and associated plays, approximately 1549–69.** Preserves *Robin Hood and the Potter* and *Robin Hood and the Friar* in performable form; marginal speech labels call the friar Tuck.
- **[EM] Richard Grafton, *Chronicle at Large* (1569).** Expands Major, gentrifies Robin, describes sickness, bloodletting and betrayal at “Bircklies”/Kirklees, and reports a tomb supposedly visible in Grafton’s time.
- **[EM] William Warner, *Albion’s England* (1586).** Contains the early formula that the outlaws “took from rich to give the poor.”
- **[EM] John Stow, *Annales of England* (1592).** Says Robin spared poor men’s goods and relieved them abundantly with wealth stolen from abbeys and rich churls. This is the first fully explicit prose formulation of habitual redistribution.
- **[LC] Anthony Munday’s Huntingdon plays, 1598–99/1601.** Transform Robin into a dispossessed earl loyal to Richard I; integrate Marian as Matilda/Fitzwalter; greatly influence later aristocratic romance.
- **[EM] Martin Parker, *A True Tale of Robin Hood*, registered 1631–32.** A broadside pseudo-biography synthesizing noble birth, outlawry, charity, royal relations, death, and chronology.
- **[EM] Percy Folio MS, c. 1650.** Preserves *Robin Hood and Guy of Gisborne* and other ballad material.
- **[EM] Forresters Manuscript, British Library Additional MS 71158, c. 1670s; rediscovered in 1993.** Contains twenty-one Robin Hood ballads, thirteen substantially different from printed counterparts and four in their earliest known versions. Its discovery materially altered the textual record.
- **[LS] Joseph Ritson, *Robin Hood* (1795).** The first enormously influential antiquarian collection; its radical introduction helped construct Robin as a popular opponent of tyranny.
- **[LS] Francis James Child, *The English and Scottish Popular Ballads* (1882–98).** Canonized thirty-eight Robin Hood ballads as Child 117–154. They include the *Gest*; Guy of Gisborne; the Monk; the Potter; the Butcher; the Curtal Friar; Little John; Will Scarlet; Allen-a-Dale; the Bishop; Queen Katherine; the Beggars; the Tanner; the Tinker; the Ranger; the Shepherd; the Golden Arrow; rescues of condemned men; the King’s disguise; Robin’s death; and Parker’s *True Tale*. Child’s numbers identify editorially collected ballads, not a single medieval cycle.

### Related outlaw literature

**[MSR]** *The Tale of Gamelyn*, *Fouke le Fitz Waryn*, the Adam Bell–Clym of the Clough–William of Cloudesley cycle, Eustace the Monk, and Hereward supplied analogues for outlaw fellowship, forest conflict, disguise, resistance, and reconciliation. Resemblance does not prove that any one was Robin’s original.

## 3. The story: episodes and variants

### Birth, status, and outlawry

- **[EA]** The early ballads give Robin no birth story and usually call him a “good yeoman.” They do not explain his outlawry.
- **[EM]** Munday makes him Robert, Earl of Huntingdon, deprived through courtly and familial intrigue.
- **[EM/LC]** Parker’s *True Tale* turns prodigality and debt into causes of outlawry.
- **[MI]** Scott makes “Locksley” a Saxon archer opposing Norman arrogance. Later novels and films add confiscated estates, Crusader service, unjust taxation, Norman oppression, or Prince John’s conspiracy.
- **Finding:** No secure medieval source makes Robin either a Saxon noble or a crusader.

### Religion

- **[EA]** In *Monk*, Robin risks entering Nottingham because he has not heard Mass; he worships at a church of St Mary.
- **[EA]** In the *Gest*, Robin refuses to harm women, husbandmen, yeomen, or worthy knights and commands reverence for the Virgin Mary. He targets bishops and archbishops not as a secular revolutionary but as wealthy, corruptible churchmen.
- **[EA]** The *Gest* says he maintains a chapel dedicated to Mary Magdalene in Barnsdale.
- **Interpretation:** Early anticlericalism is directed against rich prelates and monastic officials; it is not evidence that Robin was pagan, Protestant before the Reformation, or generally anti-Christian.

### Sir Richard at the Lee

**[EA]** In the *Gest*, Little John, Much, and Will bring Sir Richard to dinner. He admits poverty: he mortgaged his lands to St Mary’s Abbey, York, after his son killed a knight. Robin lends him £400, clothes him, equips him, and later refuses repayment after extracting an equivalent sum from the abbot. The beneficiary is a temporarily distressed knight, not a peasant. This is charity and anti-abbatial justice, but not yet a program of equal redistribution.

### Contests, recruitment, and comic reversal

- **[EA]** Robin frequently loses an initial fight: the potter beats him; later ballads have Little John, the tanner, tinker, ranger, shepherd, butcher, and friar challenge, deceive, or defeat him.
- **[EM]** The familiar bridge fight with Little John belongs chiefly to *Robin Hood and Little John*, traceable in its surviving form to about 1680–85. Little John already exists in the earliest chronicles and all four principal early ballads, but his recruitment at a bridge is later.
- **Function [MSR]:** The “meet one’s match, then recruit him” structure expands the band and allows ritual humiliation of its leader.

### The sheriff and Nottingham

- **[EA]** The sheriff is Robin’s recurring institutional enemy, though medieval sheriffs administered counties rather than merely cities.
- **[EA]** In *Monk*, the sheriff arrests Robin after the monk’s betrayal.
- **[EA]** In *Potter*, Robin tricks the sheriff through disguise and the sheriff’s wife’s favor.
- **[EA]** In the *Gest*, Little John enters the sheriff’s service, steals his silver and plate, and brings him to Robin; later the sheriff betrays Sir Richard and is killed by Robin.
- **[EA/EM]** In *Guy of Gisborne*, the sheriff employs or cooperates with Guy; Robin ultimately shoots the sheriff.
- **[MI]** Naming the sheriff “Philip Marc” is a modern historical concretization based on an actual sheriff serving under John, not a medieval narrative tradition.

### Guy of Gisborne

**[EA/EM]** Guy wears an entire horsehide, tail and mane included. Robin fights him with swords, kills him, cuts off his head, mutilates his face, and impersonates him. This violence is neither sanitized nor incidental: the hero desecrates a corpse to achieve rescue and revenge. Modern film usually converts Guy into an armored aristocratic lieutenant and rival for Marian.

### The king

- **[EA]** In *Monk*, an unnamed king is deceived by Little John and Much but admires John’s loyalty.
- **[EA]** In the *Gest*, “King Edward” enters the greenwood disguised as an abbot. Robin compels him to dine, recognizes him after receiving a blow, submits, is pardoned, and serves at court before returning to the forest. The poem does not specify Edward I, II, or III.
- **[EM]** Major relocates Robin to Richard I’s reign; Munday makes Richard his rightful sovereign and John an antagonist.
- **[MI]** Scott and film canonize the opposition “good Richard versus usurping Prince John.” It is absent from the formative ballads.

### Marian

- **[EA: absence]** Marian does not appear in the four early narrative ballads or the *Gest*.
- **[SI/EM]** Robin and Marian were separate May or pastoral figures before their traditions merged. Kingston records of 1509–10 are the first clear documentation of Marian in a Robin Hood game.
- **[SI, disputed]** Adam de la Halle’s French *Jeu de Robin et Marion* (c. 1283) has a shepherd Robin and shepherdess Marion, but no demonstrated transmission connects them directly to the English outlaw. Similar names alone are insufficient.
- **[EM]** In late-sixteenth-century games Marian could be bawdy and male-performed. Munday then dignified her as noble Matilda/Marian and Robin’s romantic partner.
- **[EM/MI]** *Robin Hood and Maid Marian*, known in later broadside tradition, makes her an armed disguise-wearing combatant who fights Robin before recognition. Modern portrayals vary between passive noblewoman and independent outlaw.

### Friar Tuck

- **[EA/contested]** Royal writs of 1417 and 1429 name Robert Stafford, Sussex chaplain, alias “Frere Tuk,” accused of serious disorder. This proves the alias existed; it does not prove Stafford joined Robin Hood.
- **[EA]** A fighting friar appears in the late-fifteenth-century dramatic fragment, but the earliest four ballads do not name Tuck as a companion.
- **[SI/EM]** May games and Copland’s mid-sixteenth-century plays fuse the bellicose friar with the festive, gluttonous, sexually comic friar. Later ballads add the river-carrying contest.
- **[MI]** The fat, jovial band chaplain is principally a post-medieval literary and screen consolidation.

### Will Scarlet and Much

- **[EA]** Will Scarlet or variants—Scarlet, Scathlock, Scarlock—appears already in *Monk* and the *Gest*. The names may reflect variant characters or textual substitution; no secure genealogy exists.
- **[EA]** Much the Miller’s Son appears in *Monk* and the *Gest*. In *Monk* he kills the monk’s young page to prevent disclosure. Later children’s versions usually omit the act.
- **[EM/MI]** Will becomes Robin’s cousin or fashionable swordsman in later narratives; Much is frequently reduced or omitted.

### Allen-a-Dale

**[EM]** The bride-rescue story enters surviving record in the seventeenth-century broadside *Robin Hood and Allen-a-Dale*: Robin prevents a forced marriage and restores the bride to Allen. It is not securely medieval. Pyle later uses Tuck as the priest who conducts the marriage.

### Death at Kirklees

- **[EA]** The *Gest* gives the earliest surviving death notice: Robin goes to Kirklees to be bled; the prioress, described as his kinswoman, and Sir Roger of Doncaster cause his death. The poem gives few medical details.
- **[EM]** Grafton (1569) says illness led Robin to a Yorkshire nunnery, where he was betrayed and bled to death, then buried beside the highway under an inscribed stone.
- **[EM]** *Robin Hood’s Death* elaborates the episode: weakened by excessive bleeding, Robin summons Little John, forbids vengeance on a woman, shoots a last arrow, and asks to be buried where it lands. Some texts make Red Roger participate in violence; later retellings add locked rooms or severed bowstrings.
- **[F]** The monument called Robin Hood’s Grave near Kirklees is a real post-medieval monument, not an excavated or authenticated medieval grave. Its pseudo-archaic inscription is generally regarded as late or forged. No identified human remains establish Robin’s burial.
- **[F/MI]** Claims that the grave possesses supernatural powers, that opening it released misfortune, or that nearby prioress Elizabeth de Stainton was Robin’s murderer are late folklore. Historic England notes that Stainton’s actual slab was found in 1706 or 1712; identifying her with the legendary killer is erroneous.

## 4. Relations with divine beings and named heroes

### Kinship and descent

**[EA]** The formative poems give Robin no parents, wife, child, divine ancestor, or securely named blood relative. The Kirklees prioress is called a relative in the death tradition but is unnamed.

**[EM]** Munday identifies Robin as Robert, Earl of Huntingdon, and Marian as Matilda, daughter of Robert Fitzwalter. These are literary genealogies.

**[LS/MI]** Stukeley’s Fitzooth descent from Earl Waltheof and later pedigrees are unsupported antiquarian constructions.

**[MI]** Modern novels, films, and television supply many mutually incompatible parents, spouses, children, and successors. These belong to individual adaptations, not inherited medieval genealogy.

## 5. Cult, tomb, ritual, and belief

### Was there a cult?

**Finding:** Robin Hood received no demonstrable medieval religious cult. There is no authenticated shrine, saint’s office, votive deposit, sacrificial rite, healing oracle, relic dossier, canonization tradition, or excavated *heroon*. The Virgin and Mary Magdalene are objects of Robin’s devotion; Robin is not himself worshipped in the early evidence.

### May games and parish ritual

- **[EA]** Exeter’s 1426 record is the earliest commonly accepted Robin Hood game. During the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries Robin became a May lord in games, processions, morris dances, archery, mock combats, collections, and parish fundraising.
- **[EA/EM]** These events were not uniform rites. Surviving records encompass civic pageants, improvised fights, dramatic episodes, church ales, Whitsun festivities, and revenue-raising collections.
- **[SI]** Robin’s outlaw company absorbed or was staged beside Little John, a fighting friar, Marian, fools, hobby-horses, dancers, pipers, and drummers.
- **[EM]** Kingston records for 1509–10 first securely place Marian in Robin’s game. By the later sixteenth century she could be presented as a sexually comic, cross-dressed role.
- **[EM]** Reformers condemned the games for disorder, sexuality, Sabbath violation, or misuse of church space and money. Suppression after the Reformation was uneven rather than instantaneous.
- **Interpretive limit:** Calling the games a remnant of pagan vegetation sacrifice goes beyond the records. They document festive custom and fundraising, not worship of a dying god.

### Kirklees grave and relic claims

- **[F]** Kirklees Priory is archaeologically and historically real. Historic England’s listing documents the medieval Cistercian nunnery and later remains.
- **[EM/F]** Leland in the 1530s reports a Kirklees burial tradition; Grafton in 1569 describes a visible stone and crosses. These are sixteenth-century testimonies, centuries later than any supposed lifetime.
- **[F]** The present railed monument called Robin Hood’s Grave is post-medieval and its inscription is not reliable medieval epigraphy.
- **[F]** No scientifically authenticated bones, grave goods, or stratified burial connect the monument to Robin Hood.
- **[F/MI]** The last-arrow burial story explains the grave’s position narratively. It is not archaeological evidence.
- **[F]** Place-names—Robin Hood’s Bay, Well, Hill, Chair, Stride, Ball, and others—testify to diffusion of the legend, but most cannot date its origin and may have been attached after the hero became famous.

### Civic commemoration

**[MI]** Nottingham’s bronze Robin Hood by James Woodford was commissioned in 1949 and unveiled below Nottingham Castle on 24 July 1952. It is civic monumentalization and tourism, not cult. The statue’s archer in feathered cap and forest costume embodies the nineteenth- and twentieth-century composite rather than a fifteenth-century yeoman.

## 6. Historical evolution of the figure

### Before 1377: lost formation

**[MSR]** Criminal aliases from 1262 onward imply that “Robin Hood” had recognizable outlaw associations before the surviving poems. Whether an individual generated the alias or an already-circulating figure generated it cannot be recovered. No extant twelfth- or thirteenth-century narrative names the hero.

### 1377–1450: popular rhymes become visible

**[EA]** Clerical allusions, proverbs, Scottish chronicles, and civic games reveal a broadly circulating figure before any complete surviving story. Robin is already proverbial, performative, and associated with Little John.

### 1450–1500: heterogeneous ballad hero

**[EA]** *Monk*, *Potter*, *Gest*, *Guy*, and dramatic fragments display no single personality. Robin can be rash, devout, murderous, comic, courtly, generous, or peripheral while Little John performs the decisive action. Barnsdale and Sherwood coexist.

### 1500–1600: print, festival, moralization, and gentrification

**[EM]** Cheap print consolidates the cycle. May games incorporate Marian and Tuck. Major places Robin under Richard I and emphasizes protection of poor people and women. Grafton supplies pseudo-historical detail and a tomb. Warner and Stow state redistribution explicitly. Munday transforms the yeoman into the Earl of Huntingdon and gives the tradition its courtly romance.

### Seventeenth century: broadside expansion

**[EM]** Encounters with tradesmen, friars, beggars, bishops, queens, and recruits multiply. Robin repeatedly loses a comic fight, reveals himself, and recruits the victor. Garlands and broadsides organize an episodic repertoire rather than a continuous biography.

### Eighteenth century: antiquarian recovery

**[LS]** Editors collect texts but also manufacture coherence. William Stukeley’s pedigree and similar “historical” lives turn literary accretions into alleged fact. Ritson’s 1795 collection gives Robin radical, anti-clerical, liberty-loving political force during the era of the French Revolution.

### Nineteenth century: national and juvenile hero

**[MI]** Scott’s *Ivanhoe* (1819) makes Locksley a Saxon patriot resisting Norman power while supporting Richard. Thomas Love Peacock’s *Maid Marian* (1822), later operatic and theatrical works, and Tennyson’s *The Foresters* (1892) enlarge the romance. Howard Pyle’s *The Merry Adventures of Robin Hood* (1883) synthesizes ballads into a decorous children’s classic with archaizing prose and influential illustrations. The period standardizes Lincoln green, the feathered cap, noble motives, Merrie England, and the “national inheritance.”

### Twentieth century: screen canon

**[MI]** Percy Stow’s *Robin Hood and His Merry Men* (1908) begins the surviving cinema lineage. Douglas Fairbanks’s *Robin Hood* (1922) monumentalizes the athletic, aristocratic swashbuckler. Michael Curtiz and William Keighley’s Technicolor *The Adventures of Robin Hood* (1938), with Errol Flynn, fixes the visual grammar: green costume, Marian romance, Prince John, Guy of Gisborne, Norman tyranny, dazzling archery, and Richard’s restoration.

**[MI]** The British television *Adventures of Robin Hood* (1955–60) makes him a weekly champion of the oppressed. Disney’s animated *Robin Hood* (1973) recasts the hero as a fox and Prince John as a lion, creating one of the most durable children’s versions. *Robin of Sherwood* (1984–86) introduces mystical paganized elements and Herne. *Robin Hood: Prince of Thieves* (1991), *Men in Tights* (1993), BBC’s *Robin Hood* (2006–09), Ridley Scott’s *Robin Hood* (2010), and the 2018 film repeatedly reconfigure race, class, gender, religion, violence, and state power.

### Twenty-first century

**[MI]** Robin now serves simultaneously as public-domain adventure hero, children’s character, tax metaphor, financial brand, redistribution label, tourism emblem, fantasy avatar, and political analogy. The multiplicity is not corruption of a fixed original; variation has characterized the record from its first narratives.

## 7. Modern reception and reinvention

### Literature and illustration

- **[MI]** Scott’s *Ivanhoe* (1819): Saxon “Locksley,” Richard loyalist, Norman/Saxon national history.
- **[MI]** Peacock’s *Maid Marian* (1822): satirical romance and expanded Marian.
- **[MI]** Ritson’s editions, especially the 1795 collection and Victorian reprints: radical antiquarian Robin.
- **[MI]** Pyle’s *Merry Adventures* (1883): the major children’s synthesis; excludes much of the early brutality and supplies a warmly communal greenwood.
- **[MI]** Tennyson’s *The Foresters* (1892): poetic drama linking Robin, Marian, Richard, John, and relief of the poor.
- **[MI]** Twentieth- and twenty-first-century fiction repeatedly gives Marian independent command, makes Robin a crusader veteran, or relocates the structure to science fiction and post-apocalyptic settings.

### Theatre, opera, and music

- **[EM]** Munday’s plays created the durable Huntingdon/Richard/Marian configuration.
- **[MI]** Reginald De Koven and Harry B. Smith’s comic opera *Robin Hood* (1890) was a major American stage success and helped popularize the romantic operetta image.
- **[MI]** Television themes—especially “Robin Hood, Robin Hood, riding through the glen” from the 1955 series—converted the legend into mass-media shorthand.

### Film and television

- **[MI] 1908:** Percy Stow, *Robin Hood and His Merry Men*, the earliest screen production generally listed by the British Film Institute.
- **[MI] 1922:** Douglas Fairbanks, directed by Allan Dwan; vast sets and athletic spectacle.
- **[MI] 1938:** Errol Flynn, directed by Michael Curtiz and William Keighley; the decisive color-film template.
- **[MI] 1955–60:** ITV’s *The Adventures of Robin Hood*; episodic social justice.
- **[MI] 1973:** Disney; anthropomorphic fox, comic taxation, sentimental romance.
- **[MI] 1976:** *Robin and Marian*; aging, disillusioned lovers and a tragic ending.
- **[MI] 1984–86:** *Robin of Sherwood*; supernatural and neo-pagan atmosphere.
- **[MI] 1991:** *Prince of Thieves*; crusader backstory, Azeem, Hollywood action.
- **[MI] 1993:** Mel Brooks’s *Men in Tights*; parody of the screen canon.
- **[MI] 2006–09:** BBC series; explicit contemporary political analogies.
- **[MI] 2010 and 2018:** attempts to restart the legend as militarized historical or franchise action.

### Politics and nationalism

- **[LS/MI]** Ritson’s 1795 Robin was compatible with radical critique of monarchy, aristocracy, and clerical wealth.
- **[MI]** Nineteenth-century writers turned Robin into an English national hero embodying liberty, archery, woodland, common law, and reconciliation between social ranks.
- **[MI]** Scott’s Saxon-versus-Norman framework naturalized an ethnic-national reading unknown to the early ballads.
- **[MI]** Socialist and labor rhetoric employs Robin as a redistributor; conservative and libertarian rhetoric recasts him as an enemy of corrupt taxation who “returns” property to taxpayers. Neither use exhausts the early evidence.
- **[MI]** “Robin Hood tax” names modern proposals for levies on financial transactions or wealth. This is metaphorical political branding.
- **Finding:** Unlike some legendary heroes, Robin has not developed a single stable extremist appropriation. His semantic flexibility allows mutually opposed movements to claim him. Claims that he was inherently socialist, nationalist, libertarian, pagan, or monarchist select one historical layer and treat it as timeless.

### Psychological, structural, and comparative readings

- **[MSR]** Otto Rank, Lord Raglan, and Joseph Campbell formulated heroic biography patterns, but Robin fits them poorly in his earliest form: there is no miraculous birth, divine parent, exposed infant, monster combat, sacred marriage, or apotheosis.
- **[MSR]** Eric Hobsbawm’s “social bandit” model made Robin the archetypal noble robber: admired by peasants, hostile to oppressive power, yet not a revolutionary organizer. Critics note that the early poems’ audiences, class values, and targets are more complicated; Holt saw affinities with yeomen, lesser gentry, and household retainers rather than a direct peasant-revolt program.
- **[MSR]** Knight shifts inquiry from a single historical original to successive cultural meanings: yeoman, forest lord, gentleman, national hero, media hero.
- **[LS/MI, discredited]** Margaret Murray and other ritualists treated Robin as leader of a surviving pagan witch cult or sacrificed vegetation god. The witch-cult thesis is discredited; the Robin evidence consists of late festive materials interpreted through an assumed survival model.
- **[MSR]** Modern gender and queer readings analyze the overwhelmingly male greenwood fellowship, cross-dressed Marian performances, masculine testing, and later displacement of women. These are datable critical methods, not recovered authorial declarations.

### Commercial use

**[MI]** The name labels investment products, redistribution algorithms, pubs, tourism, beer, transport, charities, festivals, video games, comics, and sports clubs. “Robin Hood” has become a productive common metaphor for transferring resources downward; “Sheriff of Nottingham” can signify the inverse.

## 8. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Historicity

#### Robert Hod of 1225–26

**[Historical record]** A Robert Hod failed to appear in a York assize proceeding; his goods, valued at 32 shillings and 6 pence, were forfeited. His name was subsequently rendered in forms resembling Hobbehod.

**[MSR: Holt/Owen position]** The name, outlaw status, northern venue, and early date make him a possible stimulus for the tradition. Holt called him the only then-discovered candidate meeting minimal requirements.

**[Criticism]** Nothing in the record connects him with archery, Barnsdale, Sherwood, Little John, the sheriff of Nottingham, charity, the king, or any recorded adventure. A possible candidate is not an identification.

#### The Wakefield Robin

**[LS/MSR]** Joseph Hunter in 1852 proposed a Robert Hood of Wakefield associated with the period of Thomas of Lancaster’s rebellion. He connected this man with a “Robyn Hode” employed as a royal chamber porter under Edward II and with Edward’s northern journey in 1323, which resembles the king’s itinerary in the *Gest*.

**[Criticism]** The chain contains unsupported identities: the Wakefield tenant is not proved to have been outlawed; the confiscated property is not securely his; the royal porter is not proved identical with the Wakefield man. Holt’s examination of the chamber record showed that the porter was already in royal service before Edward reached Nottingham, undermining Hunter’s scenario of recruitment from the greenwood.

#### The 1262 outlaw nickname

**[Historical record]** Crook’s Berkshire record transforms William son of Robert le Fevre into “William Robehod” in a later entry.

**[MSR]** This is powerful evidence that a Robin-Hood-like label had criminal currency by 1262 and that the legend or stereotype is older than Langland.

**[Criticism]** It cannot determine whether the label came from a specific earlier outlaw, a stock character, wordplay, or an already collective tradition.

#### Other candidates

**[LS/MSR]** Robert of Wetherby, Roger Godberd, Robert de Kyme, Fulk fitzWarin, members of the Coterel gang, and numerous Robert/Robin Hoods have been proposed. Each supplies some combination of outlawry, geography, resistance, or name; none supplies a demonstrable genealogical bridge to the ballads.

**Current evidentiary conclusion:** A historical outlaw—or several—may have contributed deeds or a name. No candidate can be identified as “the” Robin Hood. The secure object of study is the documented legend.

### Dating and transmission

- **[MSR]** Manuscript date is not necessarily composition date. *Guy of Gisborne*, preserved in a seventeenth-century manuscript, may contain late-medieval material; the degree cannot be measured line by line.
- **[MSR]** The *Gest* may compile previously independent tales. Its earliest surviving witnesses are print fragments and editions around 1500, while its language and structures point earlier.
- **[MSR]** Oral transmission explains loss and variation but cannot license undated reconstruction. Assertions that a particular story existed in the twelfth century require evidence beyond the general likelihood of oral storytelling.
- **[MSR]** The 1993 discovery of the Forresters Manuscript demonstrated that broadside and manuscript streams could coexist and vary substantially; printed texts were not the entire tradition.

### Audience and social politics

- **Holt [MSR]:** The ballads express values of yeomen, retainers, and lesser-gentry households; they are not straightforward peasant-revolt propaganda.
- **Rodney Hilton/Hobsbawm line [MSR]:** Robin can be read through class conflict and social banditry, especially because he attacks rich ecclesiastics and unjust officials.
- **Ohlgren [MSR]:** Urban, mercantile, guild, and dramatic contexts deserve greater weight.
- **Knight [MSR]:** Each period constructs a socially useful Robin; searching for one fixed ideology obscures the evidence.
- **Balanced finding:** The *Gest* respects kingship, hierarchy, worthy knights, yeomen, and property while humiliating corrupt officials and church magnates. It offers selective justice, not a manifesto abolishing rank.

### Barnsdale versus Sherwood

**[EA]** Both are early. Barnsdale predominates in the *Gest*, *Guy*, Wyntoun, and Bower; Sherwood appears in *Monk* and the Lincoln line.

**[MSR]** Nottingham’s role and the sheriff favor the Sherwood complex, while Yorkshire abbeys, Kirklees, Wakefield, and Barnsdale favor the northern one. Attempts to eliminate either region falsify the plural evidence.

### Pagan Robin

**[LS, discredited]** Nineteenth-century solar mythology, Woden/Höðr theories, Frazerian dying-god models, and Margaret Murray’s witch-cult thesis connected Robin, Marian, the May games, horns, greenwood, and seasonal ritual.

**Why rejected:** The derivations are linguistically weak; the earliest texts are explicitly Christian; May-game evidence is late and varied; no continuous pre-Christian ritual record exists. Contemporary Pagan and fantasy uses are genuine modern religious or artistic reception, not proof of medieval antiquity.

### The “rob the rich” question

- **[EA]** The *Gest* orders restraint toward worthy social groups, lends money to an impoverished knight, and contemplates giving to a poor traveler. It does not state a general redistribution program.
- **[EM]** Major in 1521 says Robin robbed the wealthy, spared the poor, and enriched them from abbots’ plunder.
- **[EM]** Warner in 1586 supplies “took from rich to give the poor”; Stow in 1592 makes habitual relief explicit.
- **Conclusion:** The ethical tendency has late-medieval roots, but the slogan and general policy are Tudor developments. The modern claim that redistribution defines the original Robin is anachronistic.

### Editions and major studies

- **Joseph Ritson, 1795 [LS]:** foundational collection but ideologically forceful and antiquarian by modern standards.
- **Francis James Child, 1882–98 [LS]:** establishes the numbered ballad canon and variant method.
- **R. B. Dobson and J. Taylor, *Rymes of Robyn Hood* (1976) [MSR]:** major documentary anthology integrating texts and historical context.
- **J. C. Holt, *Robin Hood* (1982; revised 1989) [MSR]:** archival and social-history landmark; favors an early-thirteenth-century emergence and a non-peasant milieu.
- **Stephen Knight, *Robin Hood: A Complete Study of the English Outlaw* (1994) and *A Mythic Biography* (2003) [MSR]:** redirects study toward literary evolution, ideology, gender, and reception.
- **Knight and Thomas Ohlgren, *Robin Hood and Other Outlaw Tales* (1997) [MSR]:** critical teaching edition and basis of the Rochester digital corpus.
- **David Crook, *Robin Hood: Legend and Reality* (2020) [MSR]:** renews archival inquiry and challenges some literary-geographical reconstructions.
- **Stephen H. Rigby, ed., *Historians on Robin Hood* (2025) [MSR]:** recent synthesis of dating, audience, law, gender, performance, social banditry, and regional diffusion.

## 10. Sources and conclusions

### Primary-source base

The firmest reconstruction rests on:

- Langland’s *Piers Plowman* and early clerical allusions;
- Wyntoun and Bower, read as fifteenth-century historiography rather than contemporary medieval reportage;
- *Robin Hood and the Monk*, *Robin Hood and the Potter*, *A Gest of Robyn Hode*, and *Robin Hood and Guy of Gisborne*;
- the late-fifteenth-century play fragment;
- civic and royal records concerning games and the Frere Tuk alias;
- Major, Leland, Grafton, Warner, Stow, Munday, Parker, the Percy Folio, and the Forresters Manuscript;
- Ritson and Child as editorial monuments rather than transparent medieval witnesses.

### Final evidentiary findings

1. **Robin Hood is securely a legendary subject by c. 1377–79, but the tradition was probably older.**
2. **The 1262 “William Robehod” record establishes an early outlaw label, not a historical Robin’s identity.**
3. **No proposed individual—Robert Hod of 1225, the Wakefield Robert Hood, or any other candidate—can be demonstrated to be the hero.**
4. **Barnsdale and Sherwood are both early; Barnsdale is at least as deeply rooted in the formative record.**
5. **Little John, Much, Will Scarlet, the sheriff, Marian devotion, archery, disguise, clerical robbery, royal encounter, and Kirklees death all have medieval roots, though not always in the same text.**
6. **Maid Marian and Friar Tuck entered through converging festive, dramatic, and ballad traditions rather than belonging to the earliest preserved company.**
7. **Richard I, Prince John, Earl of Huntingdon, Locksley, Saxon resistance, the green-clad romantic noble, and the unified Merry Men are successive accretions.**
8. **The early hero does not consistently “rob the rich to give to the poor.” Major, Warner, and Stow make that principle explicit during the sixteenth century.**
9. **There is a tomb tradition but no authenticated grave, body, relic, or cult.**
10. **Pagan-god and witch-cult Robin theories are modern constructions without continuous medieval evidence.**
11. **The historical record is not the life of one man but the continuing production of an adaptable outlaw hero across manuscripts, performance, print, landscape, scholarship, politics, tourism, and screen media.**
