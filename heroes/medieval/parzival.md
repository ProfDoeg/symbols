# Parzival (Perceval): Research Dossier

> **Traditional class:** mortal Arthurian hero and Grail knight; in some continuations, Grail king or saint-like ascetic, but never a god, demigod, or independently attested heroized dead.  
> **Earliest secure attestation:** Chrétien de Troyes’s unfinished Old French verse romance *Perceval, ou Le Conte du Graal*, composed for Philip of Flanders approximately 1180–1190, most probably in the 1180s. No securely dated inscription, image, cult, tomb, genealogy, or chronicle names this literary Perceval before Chrétien.

## 1. Identification, Names and Epithets

Perceval is the naïve Welsh youth who leaves an isolated childhood to become an Arthurian knight, encounters a wounded king and a mysterious procession containing a grail, fails to ask whom the grail serves, and must recover from that failure. That is the securely attested late-twelfth-century nucleus in Chrétien. Medieval authors then made him, variously, the successful healer and Grail king; the descendant of a sacred Grail lineage; a married dynastic ruler; one of three celibate Grail achievers subordinate to Galahad; a Welsh vengeance hero encountering a severed head rather than a Grail; and a comic English crusading knight. Wagner finally remade him as the “pure fool” who redeems the Grail brotherhood through compassion.

### Etymologies

The name’s origin is unresolved.

- **Welsh derivation:** Rachel Bromwich and other Celticists have treated French *Perceval* as an approximation of Welsh *Peredur*. *Peredur* is an independently attested Welsh personal name, and a warrior of that name appears in the transmitted text of *Y Gododdin*. But the surviving *Peredur fab Efrawg* romance is later than Chrétien, while the date and textual layers of *Y Gododdin* are disputed. Neither fact proves that Chrétien’s hero descends directly from the warrior. This is a **modern scholarly reconstruction**, not an attested medieval identification. A study of Welsh oral-literary relations describes the French form as an approximation altered by folk etymology, but that reconstruction remains inferential ([Oral Tradition](https://journal.oraltradition.org/wp-content/uploads/files/articles/5i/5_1_complete.pdf)).

- **Old French folk etymology:** medieval and later interpreters associated the name with *perce val* (“pierce the valley”) or *par ce val* (“through this valley”). Wolfram plays on an interpretation resembling “right through the middle.” These are **medieval learned or poetic etymologies**, not secure linguistic derivations.

- **“Pure fool”:** Joseph Görres transmitted an alleged Persian/Arabic analysis *fal parsi* or *parsi fal*, “pure fool.” Wagner adopted the spelling **Parsifal** and made “the pure fool, knowing through compassion” central to his opera. The etymology is linguistically untenable and is a **demonstrably modern invention**, although the dramatic concept itself became enormously influential.

### Epithets and classifications

- **li Galois / the Welshman:** Chrétien and later French tradition; ethnic-literary designation, not proof of a historical Welsh birthplace.
- **nice**, meaning ignorant, simple, or uncultivated: describes the forest-bred youth in Chrétien; it does not mean morally idiotic in the modern sense.
- **Grail knight/Grail king:** applicable only in those continuations and adaptations in which he completes the quest.
- **“pure fool” (*der reine Tor*):** Wagnerian, not medieval.
- **Red Knight:** Perceval acquires the slain Red Knight’s red armor in Chrétien and Wolfram, but “the Red Knight” is principally the defeated opponent’s identity, not Perceval’s universal epithet.

No primary source identifies Perceval with a pagan god, historical saint, or classical hero. Comparisons with Pryderi, Bran, Lleu, Perseus, or initiation gods belong to **modern comparative reconstruction**.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Earliest evidence

Chrétien’s *Conte du Graal* is the first secure evidence. The poem names its patron as **Philip, count of Flanders**, who died on crusade in 1191, providing a terminus ante quem. The work breaks off after roughly 9,000 verses during Gawain’s adventures. Whether Chrétien died, abandoned it, or left an ending now lost is unknown. Its unfinished state is a manuscript fact; the cause is not.

The surviving manuscript witnesses are later than composition. The Bibliothèque nationale de France lists numerous manuscripts, including BnF français 794, 1429, 1450, 1453, 12576, and 12577; BnF français 12577 was copied around 1330 ([BnF manuscript survey](https://gallica.bnf.fr/selections/fr/html/manuscrits/le-conte-du-graal); [BnF illustrated witness](https://fantasy.bnf.fr/fr/grand/fan_231.php)). Keith Busby’s edition collates the manuscript tradition and corrects the limitations of Hilka’s and Lecoy’s reconstructed or single-witness texts ([edition record](https://books.google.com/books/about/Le_Roman_de_Perceval_ou_Le_Conte_du_Graa.html?id=kKGa9MbWBRoC)).

What this evidence does **not** establish:

- It does not establish a historical Perceval.
- It does not show a pre-Christian Grail cult.
- Chrétien’s *graal* is not explicitly the cup of the Last Supper.
- It does not give Perceval a completed return to the castle.
- It does not call the wounded host “the Grail King”; he is the Fisher King, while an older king is served by the grail.

## 3. The Story: Cycles and Variants

### Chrétien’s unfinished story

**Securely attested early material, c. 1180s.** Perceval’s unnamed father and elder brothers have been disabled or killed through warfare. His unnamed mother withdraws with him to the **Waste Forest**, concealing knighthood from him. When he encounters knights, he mistakes them for angels. She reluctantly gives him rudimentary advice: aid women, seek honorable company, and attend churches. He applies her counsel mechanically and often destructively.

At Arthur’s court, a red-armored knight has insulted the king and taken his cup. Perceval demands knighthood and the man’s armor. He kills the Red Knight with a javelin and cannot remove the armor until Yonet helps him. The killing is not a bloodless initiation: Perceval takes equipment from a corpse and leaves wearing the dead man’s identity.

At Gornemant de Goort’s castle, he is trained in arms and instructed not to chatter or ask too many questions. At Beaurepaire he relieves **Blancheflor**, whose people are starving under siege, defeating Anguinguerron and Clamadeu. He and Blancheflor share a bed, but Chrétien does not narrate a marriage.

Returning toward his mother, Perceval enters the Fisher King’s castle. A boy carries a bleeding lance; attendants carry candlesticks; a maiden carries a radiant grail; another carries a silver carving dish. Remembering Gornemant’s injunction, Perceval does not ask why the lance bleeds or whom the grail serves. The next morning the castle is deserted.

A maiden—Perceval’s cousin—reveals his name to him and says his mother died from grief after his departure. She tells him that asking would have healed the wounded king and prevented suffering. At Arthur’s court, an ugly damsel publicly curses him for the omitted questions and announces adventures. The narrative then alternates between Perceval and Gawain.

Five years later, Perceval has forgotten God while performing feats of arms. On Good Friday penitents direct him to a hermit, who proves to be his maternal uncle. Perceval confesses. The hermit attributes his failure partly to the sin arising from his mother’s death and reveals that the grail sustains the Fisher King’s aged father with a single consecrated wafer. The poem then returns to Gawain and breaks off. Consequently, every medieval description of Chrétien’s Perceval as actually healing the king is a **continuator’s conclusion mistakenly projected backward**.

### The French continuations

**Later canonical elaboration, c. 1190–1230.** The four continuations do not form a single authorially planned sequel. Different manuscript assemblages connect and reorder them.

- The First Continuation primarily develops Gawain’s Grail visit, a broken sword, and adventures of revenge and marvel.
- Wauchier’s Second brings Perceval back to the quest and multiplies trials, castles, kinship revelations, and partial advances.
- Gerbert imposes penitential and moral tests and greatly extends the road to completion.
- Manessier supplies a decisive ending: Perceval learns the history of the broken sword and Grail calamity, avenges the dead, heals the Fisher King by completing the sword and asking correctly, succeeds as Grail king, and later retires to a hermitage. Manuscripts that place Gerbert before Manessier manufacture a huge composite life never composed by one author.

### Wolfram’s *Parzival*

**Regional canonical elaboration, c. 1200–1210.** Wolfram adds an extensive prehistory. Parzival’s father **Gahmuret**, an Angevin prince, marries the Black queen **Belacane** in Zazamanc and fathers the black-and-white **Feirefiz**, then abandons her. He later marries **Herzeloyde**, wins fame abroad, and dies before Parzival’s birth. Wolfram’s racialized marvel of Feirefiz’s “magpie” skin belongs to medieval romance ethnography; it should neither be suppressed nor mistaken for observation.

Herzeloyde raises Parzival at Soltane in ignorance. He meets knights, leaves her, and she dies of grief. He kills **Ither**, Wolfram’s Red Knight, with a javelin, takes his armor, trains with **Gurnemanz**, saves and marries **Condwiramurs**, then leaves her while she is pregnant.

At Munsalvaesche, the wounded **Anfortas** gives him a sword. Parzival sees the bleeding lance and the Grail carried by **Repanse de Schoye**, but fails to ask Anfortas what afflicts him. Here the Grail is a stone, called *lapsit exillis* by Trevrizent; it supplies food, preserves its guardians, and receives a dove’s wafer on Good Friday ([Wolfram’s wording and location](https://wiki.uni-konstanz.de/mediaewiki/index.php/Der_Gral_im_Parzival_%28Wolfram_von_Eschenbach%2C_Parzival%29)). Proposed translations—“stone from exile,” “fallen stone,” *lapis ex caelis*, or alchemical stone—are **modern conjectures**; Wolfram’s phrase is corrupt, invented, or deliberately enigmatic.

The Grail messenger **Cundrie** curses Parzival publicly. He renounces trust in God and wanders for nearly five years. On Good Friday he meets the hermit **Trevrizent**, confesses, learns the Grail genealogy, and is reconciled to God. After combats involving Gawan and his unknown half-brother Feirefiz, Cundrie announces his election. He returns, asks Anfortas “Uncle, what troubles you?”, heals him, becomes Grail king, and reunites with Condwiramurs and their twin sons **Kardeiz** and **Loherangrin**. Feirefiz converts and marries Repanse; their descendants are linked to Prester John. Loherangrin becomes the Swan Knight.

Wolfram repeatedly invokes **Kyot the Provençal**, allegedly the discoverer at Toledo of an Arabic book by the astrologer Flegetanis. No such book survives; no known Guiot supplies Wolfram’s plot. Most scholarship treats Kyot as fictional source-play or an unverifiable claim, not evidence for an Arabic *Parzival*.

### Welsh *Peredur*

**Regional variant, thirteenth-century composition in four later manuscripts.** Efrawg and six sons die in warfare; Peredur’s mother raises the surviving boy in isolation. He meets Arthur’s knights, receives instruction, and enters Arthurian society. He encounters marvels corresponding loosely to Chrétien’s procession, but the central objects are a bleeding spear and a severed head. The long version eventually explains them through a family vengeance plot against the witches of Gloucester. Other adventures include the Empress of Constantinople and the hero’s long residence or rule with her.

The short and long witnesses differ substantially, suggesting growth rather than a single fixed Welsh archetype. The tale may preserve Welsh narrative matter; it also responds to French romance. Recent analysis argues for a shorter Welsh story later influenced by material returning from Chrétien, but this remains a reconstruction rather than a demonstrable stemma ([study of the “inappropriate questions” problem](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1515/jias-2021-0002/html)).

### Vulgate, Malory, and the death tradition

**Thirteenth-century theological systematization.** The Vulgate *Queste* makes Galahad the predestined, virginal supreme achiever. Perceval remains one of the three successful companions with Galahad and Bors. His sister sacrifices her blood to heal a leprous woman and dies; the companions carry her body toward Sarras. Galahad beholds the Grail mysteries and dies. Perceval then becomes a hermit and dies after a short religious life; Bors alone returns.

Malory repeats this conclusion: Percivale lives as a hermit for a year and two months, dies, and is buried at Sarras beside his sister and Galahad ([Malory, Book XVII, chapter 23](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Le_Morte_d%27Arthur/Volume_II/Book_XVII/Chapter_XXIII)). This is a **literary death and literary tomb**, not evidence of burial, relics, or cult.

**Middle English regional variant.** *Sir Perceval of Galles* omits the Grail. Its hero restores his mother Acheflour, defeats a giant and a sultan’s forces, marries Lufamour, and eventually dies fighting on crusade. This irreconcilable fate must stand beside—rather than be harmonized with—Grail kingship, hermitage at Sarras, Constantinopolitan rule, and Wolfram’s open-ended reign.

## 5. Cult, Tomb and Ritual

### Cult

There is no reliable evidence that Perceval received medieval religious cult:

- no securely identified shrine or *heroon*;
- no altar, votive inscription, relic catalogue, liturgical office, feast day, sacrifice, pilgrimage, or miracle collection;
- no archaeologically identified grave;
- no medieval testimony that worshippers treated him as a saint or ancestral hero.

This negative result distinguishes him from cult heroes such as Achilles or saint-like legendary rulers with claimed relics.

### Literary tombs and ritual

- Malory’s Sarras burial beside Galahad and Percivale’s sister is **late-medieval fiction**, not a claimed excavated tomb.
- The Grail procession has ceremonial form, but Chrétien never calls it a Mass, mystery initiation, or survival of pagan ritual.
- Wolfram’s Good Friday dove and wafer make the stone Grail part of a poetic Christian economy.
- Wagner’s *Bühnenweihfestspiel* (“festival play for the consecration of the stage”) deliberately constructs quasi-liturgical theatre. Bayreuth performance culture is documented reception, not continuation of a medieval Perceval cult.

Modern claims locating Perceval’s grave, the “historical Grail castle,” or a surviving Grail brotherhood are unsupported unless tied to demonstrable modern tourism, esotericism, or fiction.

## 6. Historical Evolution of the Figure

### Before c. 1180

A personal name **Peredur** and various Welsh heroic traditions existed, but no surviving evidence securely supplies Chrétien’s plot. Any “original pagan Perceval myth” is hypothetical.

### c. 1180–1190: emergence

Chrétien creates the first documented conjunction of:

- isolated forest upbringing;
- naïve entry into knighthood;
- the Red Knight’s armor;
- a wounded fishing host;
- bleeding lance and grail procession;
- failed questions;
- wandering, religious lapse, confession, and an unfinished quest.

### c. 1190–1230: explosive expansion

French continuators repeatedly attempt to finish the open poem. Robert de Boron and prose redactors identify the Grail with Christ’s Passion and Joseph of Arimathea. Wolfram supplies a complete family history, the Grail stone, the religious crisis, Kyot, Feirefiz, and a dynastic resolution. *Perlesvaus* renders the story more violent and crusading.

### Thirteenth century: competition and displacement

The Vulgate cycle makes **Galahad**, not Perceval, the supreme Grail achiever. Perceval becomes one member of a purified trio. Welsh, Dutch, German, and later English works detach, alter, or suppress the Grail material.

### Fourteenth–fifteenth centuries: vernacular regionalization

*Sir Perceval of Galles* turns the hero into a comic family-and-crusade protagonist. Malory consolidates the Galahad-centered version for English readers. The English poem survives uniquely in Lincoln Cathedral MS 91; its hero dies on crusade rather than in Sarras or as Grail king.

### Early print to eighteenth century

Wolfram’s *Parzival* was printed in 1477 but, like much Middle High German courtly literature, ceased to command its medieval readership. Antiquarian and philological rediscovery in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries restored the text to national literary history.

### Nineteenth century: Romantic, national, and musical remaking

Medieval philology, Arthurian revival, and Wagner transformed Parzival from one variable Grail hero into an emblem of German spiritual art. This is cultural reception, not recovery of a pristine national myth.

### Twentieth–twenty-first centuries

Philology shifted attention from a lost archetype toward manuscript plurality, adaptation, patronage, gender, race, theology, and narrative ethics. The Bern digital edition explicitly treats multiple manuscript versions; sixteen complete witnesses and over seventy fragments resist reduction to one stable medieval text ([digital-edition introduction](https://parzival.unibe.ch/introduction)).

## 7. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Wagner

Richard Wagner composed *Parsifal* between 1877 and January 1882; it premiered at Bayreuth on 26 July 1882 ([Richard Wagner Museum](https://www.wagnermuseum.de/informationen/aktuelles/ausstellung-der-parsifal-partitur-in-der-schatzkammer-des-richard-wagner-museums/)). His principal medieval source was Wolfram, but he radically compressed and altered it:

- Parzival’s parents, wife, children, half-brother, secular knighthood, and long genealogy disappear.
- The Grail is again a vessel rather than Wolfram’s stone.
- The sacred spear becomes the instrument that wounded Amfortas and later heals him.
- Kundry combines messenger, penitent wanderer, temptress, and witness to Christ.
- Compassion rather than genealogical election becomes Parsifal’s defining knowledge.
- The invented “pure fool” etymology becomes dramatic theology.

Wagner’s work acquired German national prestige, but its politics are not reducible to simple Nazi endorsement. Wilhelmine arguments over exclusive Bayreuth performance rights made it a disputed national-cultural possession before 1914 ([Oxford, “Embracing the Grail”](https://academic.oup.com/gh/article-abstract/30/3/372/558100)). Hitler admired Wagner generally, yet Alfred Rosenberg and some Nazi ideologues rejected *Parsifal* as excessively Christian and pacifist. Claims that it was a straightforward Nazi ideological opera erase this documented conflict.

### Literature and scholarship shaping reception

- **Tennyson, “The Holy Grail” in *Idylls of the King* (1869):** belongs principally to the Galahad tradition; Percivale serves as narrator and failed quester.
- **Jessie Weston, *From Ritual to Romance* (1920):** interpreted the Grail story through vegetation ritual and the dying/reviving king. This is a **modern ritualist reconstruction**, not medieval testimony.
- **T. S. Eliot, *The Waste Land* (1922):** explicitly credited Weston for the poem’s title, plan, and symbolism. Eliot later called the apparatus conducive to “bogus scholarship”; the poem’s Fisher King pattern is reception rather than evidence for the romance’s origins ([University of Toronto edition and annotations](https://rpo.library.utoronto.ca/content/waste-land-1)).
- **Emma Jung and Marie-Louise von Franz, *The Grail Legend* (1960):** Jungian interpretation of individuation and psychic wholeness; influential psychology, not historical reconstruction.
- **Robert Bresson, *Lancelot du Lac* (1974):** treats the failed aftermath of the Grail quest rather than retelling Perceval’s youth.
- **Éric Rohmer, *Perceval le Gallois* (1978):** deliberately theatrical film based closely on Chrétien’s language and narrative conventions.
- **Hans-Jürgen Syberberg, *Parsifal* (1982):** centenary cinematic confrontation with Wagner, German culture, gender, and theatricality.
- **John Boorman, *Excalibur* (1981):** merges Perceval and Galahad functions and popularizes the question as healing the king and land.
- **The Fisher King* (1991):** relocates Grail healing to modern New York; it is an openly modern analogy, not medieval tradition.
- Novels, comics, role-playing games, and video games frequently turn Percival into a generic Round Table knight. Such appearances belong to **commercial modern reception** unless they preserve a specifically sourced medieval episode.

Campbell’s “monomyth” can easily assimilate Perceval’s separation, initiation, ordeal, and return, but Campbell did not discover the historical origin of *Perceval*. Rankian birth-pattern analysis, Jungian individuation, structuralist initiation, and ritualist vegetation readings are datable analytical systems imposed upon a multiform corpus.

## 8. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Celtic-origins debate

Roger Sherman Loomis’s *Celtic Myth and Arthurian Romance* (1927) and *Arthurian Tradition and Chrétien de Troyes* (1949) argued that Grail romance reworked Celtic narrative: magic vessels, wounded rulers, waste lands, severed heads, otherworldly castles, and Welsh/Irish analogues. Loomis’s strength was the accumulation of correspondences; his weakness was that correspondences do not establish a recoverable chain of transmission. Even sympathetic contemporary assessment acknowledged that the Celtic-origin theory lacked direct textual proof while possessing a body of converging comparisons ([1951 review](https://www.persee.fr/doc/roma_0035-8029_1951_num_72_285_3263_t1_0118_0000_2)).

Opposing or qualifying approaches emphasize:

- the surviving Welsh *Peredur* manuscripts postdate Chrétien;
- the long Welsh text contains demonstrably composite material;
- French-to-Welsh influence may explain major shared episodes;
- Christian eucharistic and aristocratic-romance contexts can explain much without reconstructing pagan ritual;
- oral intermediaries are possible but, by definition, not directly recoverable.

The most defensible position is reciprocal and agnostic: Brittonic names and narrative matter contributed to Arthurian romance, but no extant “Celtic original of *Perceval*” can be reconstructed as a documentary fact.

### Historicity

A Peredur appears among warriors associated with *Y Gododdin*, and later genealogical traditions contain persons of that name. But:

1. *Y Gododdin* survives in a thirteenth-century manuscript with disputed layers.
2. A shared personal name does not demonstrate identity with the forest-reared Grail hero.
3. No early annal or genealogy attributes the Grail narrative, the Red Knight episode, or Arthurian membership to a historical Peredur.
4. “Perceval was a sixth-century British warrior” is therefore a **modern historicizing conjecture**, not an established result.

### What was Chrétien’s grail?

In Chrétien, *un graal* is a splendid serving object carried by a maiden; the subsequent hermit scene reveals that it carries a wafer sustaining the old king. Chrétien does not identify it as the Last Supper cup or the vessel catching Christ’s blood. Robert de Boron’s Joseph tradition creates that explicit Passion history. Calling Chrétien’s object “the Holy Grail chalice” without qualification imports a later system backward.

### Why does Perceval fail?

The sources offer overlapping, not identical answers:

- **Chrétien:** he obeys Gornemant’s advice against excessive speech; later revelations connect his silence to the moral consequences of abandoning his mother.
- **Wolfram:** immaturity, failure of compassion, inherited kinship obligations, and estrangement from God converge.
- **Continuations:** failure becomes a mechanism requiring renewed questions, sword repair, vengeance, or purification.
- **Wagner:** the failure becomes lack of conscious compassion; redemption comes when Parsifal understands Amfortas’s pain.

Modern claims that the episode is simply an initiation taboo, fertility rite, Christian catechism, or lesson in curiosity each select one interpretive structure from a more complicated textual history.

### Kyot

Wolfram claims that Kyot found Flegetanis’s Arabic account in Toledo and traced the Grail lineage through Angevin records. No external witness verifies either author or book. Attempts to identify Kyot with the trouvère Guiot de Provins have not produced a Grail source. Kyot is best classified as **an unverified medieval source-claim, probably literary fiction**, not proof of lost Arabic historiography.

### Manuscripts and editions

The textual record is unusually plural. Chrétien’s poem survives in witnesses that incorporate different continuations. Wolfram survives in four distinguishable textual groupings. Editorial history moved from Lachmannian reconstruction toward version-conscious and manuscript-centered approaches. Accordingly, “the complete medieval Perceval story” is an editorial composite, not a single medieval work.

### Cult, tomb, and occult claims

The dedicated contested-material search found no credible archaeological or documentary evidence for a Perceval cult, shrine, relic, or historical tomb. Claims connecting *lapsit exillis* conclusively with the Philosopher’s Stone, a meteorite, Lucifer’s crown, the Kaaba stone, or a Templar relic are later esoteric constructions. Wolfram says it is a life-sustaining stone and gives the enigmatic name; he does not state most of those modern identifications.

## 10. Sources and Evidentiary Conclusions

### Primary and critical foundations

The indispensable primary corpus consists of Chrétien’s *Conte du Graal* and its manuscript continuations; Robert de Boron and the *Didot-Perceval*; *Perlesvaus*; Wolfram’s *Parzival* and *Titurel*; Middle Welsh *Peredur*; the Vulgate and Post-Vulgate Grail quests; *Moriaen*; *Sir Perceval of Galles*; and Malory. Manuscript catalogues and critical editions must be preferred to modern synthetic “complete Grail” narratives because the latter regularly combine incompatible versions.

### Final evidentiary assessment

- **Secure earliest fact:** Perceval is documented as a literary Arthurian hero in Chrétien’s late-twelfth-century romance.
- **Secure early story:** isolated upbringing, encounter with knights, Red Knight killing, chivalric schooling, Blancheflor, Fisher King, lance-and-grail procession, silence, accusation, five-year wandering, and hermit-confession.
- **Later canonical elaboration:** completed return, healing, Grail kingship, Joseph-of-Arimathea history, Galahad’s supremacy, and death at Sarras.
- **German regional elaboration:** named ancestry, Condwiramurs, Feirefiz, Anfortas, Trevrizent, Kyot, *lapsit exillis*, and Lohengrin.
- **Welsh regional variant:** Peredur, severed head, witches, vengeance, Angharad, and Constantinopolitan ending.
- **English regional variant:** comic naïveté, no Grail, family restoration, and crusading death.
- **Absence of evidence:** no verified historical Perceval, cult, tomb, relic, shrine, feast, or ancient image.
- **Modern reconstruction:** derivation from a particular *Y Gododdin* warrior, a recoverable pagan Celtic initiation myth, vegetation ritual, or a historical Grail dynasty.
- **Modern invention:** the Persian “pure fool” etymology, Wagner’s integrated spear-and-compassion theology, and occult identifications of the Grail stone presented as established medieval fact.
