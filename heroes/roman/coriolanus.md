# Coriolanus: Research Dossier

> **Traditional class:** Mortal Roman warrior and political traitor of heroic legend; an exemplary figure in Roman historiography, not a demigod and not a securely attested recipient of hero cult.  
> **Earliest secure attestation:** The earliest recoverable source is Quintus Fabius Pictor, writing in Greek in the late third century BCE, but only through Livy’s citation of his version of Coriolanus’ old age (*Ab urbe condita* 2.40.10–11). The earliest extant text is Cicero, *Brutus* 41–42, written in 46 BCE. There is no contemporary fifth-century BCE inscription, image, tomb, or public record naming Coriolanus.

## Evidentiary key

- **[EA] Securely attested early material:** ancient evidence securely dated, without implying that its narrated event is historical.
- **[LC] Later canonical elaboration:** especially the Augustan and imperial narrative tradition.
- **[RV] Regional or authorial variant.**
- **[LS] Learned systematization or antiquarian inference.**
- **[EM] Medieval or early-modern accretion.**
- **[MSR] Modern scholarly reconstruction.**
- **[MI] Modern invention or creative reinvention.**
- **[NEG] Negative finding:** the specified corpus or material record supplies no evidence for the claim.

The labels date the evidence, not the supposed lifetime of Coriolanus.

## 1. Identification, Names and Epithets

Coriolanus is the protagonist of a Roman legend set during the first decades of the Republic, conventionally around 493–488 BCE. In its canonical form, he is a superb but arrogant Roman aristocratic warrior: he distinguishes himself in the capture of the Volscian city Corioli, receives the victory-name *Coriolanus*, opposes concessions to the plebs during a grain shortage, is prosecuted or driven into exile, defects to the Volsci, devastates Roman territory, and is stopped outside Rome not by male ambassadors or priests but by an embassy of women led by his mother and wife. The sources then disagree whether the Volsci kill him, he kills himself, or he survives among them into old age. The legend functions successively as a narrative about aristocratic pride, civic treason, maternal authority, women’s intervention in war, class conflict, and the limits of heroic individualism.

### Names

- **Latin:** *Cn. Marcius Coriolanus* in Livy; expanded as *Gnaeus Marcius Coriolanus*. **[LC]** Livy 2.33 first introduces him as *Cn. Marcius*, the manuscripts’ conventional abbreviation for *Gnaeus*.  
- **Greek:** Γάϊος Μάρκιος / Γάιος Μάρκιος Κοριολανός, *Gaios Markios Koriolanos*, in Dionysius and Plutarch. **[RV]** These Greek authors make his praenomen Gaius rather than Gnaeus; Tim Cornell’s *Oxford Classical Dictionary* entry preserves the disagreement.
- **Early-modern English:** “Caius Martius Coriolanus.” **[EM]** Shakespeare followed Thomas North’s 1579 English Plutarch, itself translated through Jacques Amyot’s French version.
- **Modern vernaculars:** Italian *Coriolano*, French *Coriolan*, German *Coriolan*, English *Coriolanus*. **[EM/MI]**

### Components and etymology

- **Marcius:** the nomen of the *gens Marcia*. Ancient genealogical tradition connected the Marcii with the fourth king, Ancus Marcius, and through him with Numa Pompilius. **[LS]** Plutarch, *Coriolanus* 1, gives Coriolanus an illustrious patrician ancestry. This is family genealogy transmitted many centuries after the supposed events, not contemporary evidence.
- **Coriolanus:** a Latin toponymic cognomen, conventionally “the man of Corioli.” **[LC]** Livy 2.33.9 says the capture of Corioli produced the name; Plutarch 11 dramatizes its public bestowal by the consul Cominius. The suffix is not an independent word meaning “victorious”; the circulating derivation “Corioli + *lanus*, victorious” is **[MI]** false etymology.
- An older alternative held that the cognomen meant that Marcius came from, settled at, or otherwise belonged to Corioli. **[LS/MSR]** Nineteenth-century reference works record that suggestion, but the extant narrative tradition overwhelmingly makes it an earned victory-name.

### Status and epithets

Coriolanus has no divine epithet, patronymic formula, or cult title. He is variously framed as:

- *vir* and soldier of exceptional *virtus* in Livy; **[LC]**
- a proud, inadequately educated warrior whose excellence is ruined by anger and contempt in Plutarch; **[LC]**
- a civic traitor comparable with Themistocles in Cicero; **[EA/LS]**
- a tragic aristocratic hero in Shakespeare and German Romanticism; **[EM/MI]**
- an anti-popular military leader whose apparent indispensability is exposed as an illusion in Brecht. **[MI]**

He was never identified with a god or foreign hero through *interpretatio*. Cicero and Plutarch compare him with Themistocles, but neither equates them.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Earliest recoverable evidence

#### Fabius Pictor, late third century BCE

**[EA, indirectly preserved]** Livy 2.40.10–11 says that “Fabius, the oldest authority” reported Coriolanus living into old age among the Volsci and lamenting that exile was more wretched for an old man. This is almost certainly Quintus Fabius Pictor, Rome’s earliest historical annalist, active during the Second Punic War and writing in Greek. His work is lost.

What the fragment establishes:

- a Coriolanus tradition existed by the late third century BCE;
- it already placed him in Volscian exile;
- it contained a memorable saying about old age.

What it does **not** establish:

- that Coriolanus existed;
- that Fabius already narrated every element found in Livy;
- that the capture of Corioli, grain crisis, trial, women’s embassy, or Fortuna cult was in Fabius.

#### Cicero, 46–44 BCE

The earliest surviving attestations are:

- **[EA]** *Brutus* 41–42, 46 BCE: Coriolanus is a voluntary exile who led the Volscian war and ended his resentment by a voluntary death. Cicero compares him directly with Themistocles and acknowledges that Atticus held a different account of his death.
- **[EA]** *Laelius de amicitia* 36 and 42–43, 44 BCE: Coriolanus is the paradigm proving that friendship cannot justify bearing arms against one’s country; Cicero again says that he and Themistocles killed themselves.
- **[EA]** *Ad Atticum* 9.10.3, 49 BCE, alludes to the disputed Themistocles–Coriolanus analogy in the context of civil war.

Cicero assumes that educated readers know the legend. His surviving notices emphasize exile, treason, comparison with Themistocles, and death—not the intervention of the women.

### Virgil, Servius, Ovid, and other specified corpora

- **Virgil’s *Aeneid*:** **[NEG]** Coriolanus does not appear in Virgil’s catalogue of future Romans or elsewhere by name.
- **Servius’ commentary on Virgil:** **[NEG]** It is valuable for early Roman religion and genealogy but supplies no independent Coriolanus dossier.
- **Ovid’s *Fasti* and *Metamorphoses*:** **[NEG]** Neither narrates Coriolanus’ career. Ovidian evidence cannot be used to authenticate the women’s embassy or his death.
- **Plutarch’s *Romulus* and *Publicola*:** **[NEG/context]** These Lives supply comparative material about the early Republic and the Valerii, not an earlier attestation of Coriolanus.
- **Fasti Capitolini:** **[NEG]** Coriolanus is not attested there as consul or triumphator.
- **Lapis Niger, Lupercal, and the so-called heroon of Aeneas at Lavinium:** **[NEG]** These are important evidence for archaic Rome, Roman foundation traditions, and elite commemoration, but none names, depicts, or materially attests Coriolanus. Their inclusion in a general Roman-heroes bibliography must not be converted into evidence for him.

### Manuscript distance

No authorial manuscript survives. Livy’s first decade depends principally on medieval codices; Dionysius’ early books likewise survive through Byzantine manuscript transmission; Plutarch’s *Lives* survive in medieval Greek manuscripts grouped into complex families. The chronological gulf between the alleged fifth-century events, the first annalistic version in the late third century BCE, the extant late-republican/Augustan narratives, and their medieval manuscripts is fundamental.

## 3. The Story

### Parentage and youth

**[LC] Plutarch 1–3** calls him Gaius Marcius, descended from the patrician Marcii and raised by his widowed mother Volumnia. Losing his father early, he supposedly developed extraordinary attachment to her. Plutarch uses his story to argue that an excellent nature without education may produce both great energy and savage defects.

**[RV] Livy** calls the mother Veturia and does not narrate this psychological childhood. In Livy, Volumnia is the wife. Plutarch instead calls the wife Vergilia.

### Lake Regillus

**[LC] Plutarch 3** places the adolescent Marcius at the battle of Lake Regillus against the restored Tarquinian cause. He saves a fallen Roman, earns the oak civic crown, and begins a career in which every battle supposedly brings a new distinction. Livy does not attach him to Regillus. The episode is therefore a Plutarchan or pre-Plutarchan elaboration, not independently corroborated early evidence.

### Capture of Corioli

**[LC] Livy 2.33.4–9**, under the consul Postumus Cominius, says the Romans attacked Corioli. When Volscians from Antium threatened the Roman camp and Corioli’s defenders made a sortie, Marcius drove the latter through the open gates, entered the town, and helped capture it. His fame overshadowed the consul’s campaign, and the exploit produced the cognomen *Coriolanus*.

**[LC/RV] Plutarch 8–11** magnifies the exploit. Marcius enters almost alone, rallies the Romans inside the city, then returns wounded to help Cominius defeat the relieving Volscian army. He refuses money and most captured goods, accepting only a horse and the release of a Volscian host-friend. Cominius publicly proposes the surname Coriolanus.

**Historical assessment [MSR]:** Corioli was a real Latin/Volscian settlement in Roman tradition, but no contemporary record links its capture with Marcius. Some scholars consider the single-warrior city capture a heroic narrative pattern. Others retain a possible memory of a mobile aristocratic war leader operating in the fragmented warfare of early fifth-century Latium.

### Political conflict and grain

After the first secession of the plebs and creation of the tribunate:

- **[LC] Livy 2.34–35** makes Coriolanus a fierce opponent of the tribunes. During scarcity, grain arrived from Sicily and elsewhere. He urged the Senate to make cheap distribution conditional upon the plebs surrendering powers gained through secession. The tribunes denounced this as an attack on liberty.
- **[LC] Dionysius 7.20–67** expands the episode into fifteen speeches and a long constitutional contest. Marcius becomes the spokesman of a systematic aristocratic case against tribunician power.
- **[LC] Plutarch 12–21** combines grain politics, personal contempt, candidacy for the consulship, popular reversal, and prosecution. His Coriolanus displays his scars to voters but cannot perform ordinary conciliation.

The developed conflict-of-the-orders setting is suspect because it resembles later republican constitutional argument. The Marcii known historically were plebeian, which sits awkwardly with a primordial patrician champion. This does not prove that every element was invented, but it makes the political biography a likely retrojection. **[MSR]**

### Trial and exile

- **Livy 2.35:** the tribunes summon Coriolanus; he does not appear on the appointed day and goes into exile among the Volsci. **[LC]**
- **Dionysius 7.64:** he is condemned by tribal vote after an elaborate trial. The transmitted numbers are problematic and may reflect later tribal arrangements. **[LC/LS]**
- **Plutarch 20–21:** three tribes’ votes produce conviction; he departs without outward humiliation. **[LC/RV]**
- **Cicero, *Brutus* 41:** calls him a voluntary exile, showing that this classification predates Livy. **[EA]**

“Exiled by the plebs” is therefore a useful shorthand but not a uniform source statement: formal conviction, threatened prosecution, and voluntary withdrawal coexist.

### Alliance with the Volsci

**[LC] Livy 2.35** has Coriolanus go to Antium and enter the house of his former enemy Attius Tullius. Together they devise war against Rome.

**[LC/RV] Plutarch 22–24** gives the scene tragic coloration: Coriolanus enters Tullus Aufidius’ house in disguise, sits silently at the hearth, unveils himself, and asks Aufidius either to use his service against Rome or kill him. Aufidius embraces him.

The two then use a religious incident at the Roman games to inflame the Volsci:

- a herald orders Volscians to leave Rome before sunset;
- Tullus represents the expulsion as an insult;
- Volscian communities prepare for war.

Livy 2.36–38 associates this sequence with the repeated Great Games following Titus Latinius’ revelatory dream. Plutarch 24–27 integrates it directly into the Coriolanus plot. The manipulation is principally attributed to Tullus, not to divine instruction received by Coriolanus.

### Volscian campaign

**[LC] Livy 2.39** says Coriolanus spared patrician lands while devastating plebeian property, intensifying Roman class suspicion. He captured or received a chain of Latin communities and encamped at the Cluilian Trenches, five miles from Rome.

**[LC] Dionysius 8.1–36** supplies the longest itinerary, strategic speeches, and negotiations. The geographical sequence has been mined for historical memories of Volscian expansion, but the narratives differ, and some towns or political conditions are anachronistic. **[MSR]**

The plausible historical background is the genuine Volscian pressure upon Latium in the fifth century BCE. It cannot establish the individual commander.

### Failed male embassies

In Livy 2.39–40:

1. senators approach Coriolanus; he demands harsh terms;
2. a second senatorial mission fails;
3. priests in sacred dress fail;
4. only the women succeed.

Dionysius and Plutarch greatly amplify the exchanges. Their structure deliberately opposes unsuccessful male civic and religious authority to successful maternal and marital supplication. **[LC]**

### Veturia, Volumnia, Vergilia, and Valeria

**Livy’s arrangement [LC]:**

- mother: **Veturia**;
- wife: **Volumnia**;
- two young sons;
- an unnamed initiative among Roman matrons.

Veturia rebukes him: before accepting an embrace she must know whether she has come to a son or an enemy. Coriolanus yields to his mother’s anger, his family’s embraces, and the women’s lamentation.

**Dionysius’ arrangement [LC/RV]:**

- Veturia and Volumnia retain roles broadly corresponding to Livy;
- **Valeria**, sister of Publicola, initiates or organizes the women’s intervention;
- extended speeches and ritual details turn the embassy into a formal matronal *supplicatio*.

**Plutarch’s arrangement [LC/RV]:**

- mother: **Volumnia**;
- wife: **Vergilia**;
- Valeria helps lead the matrons;
- two sons accompany them.

Plutarch’s Coriolanus greets his family and hears his mother’s long speech. When he yields, he tells her that she has won a victory fortunate for Rome but destructive to him.

Shakespeare follows Plutarch/North and therefore uses Volumnia for the mother and Virgilia for the wife. The frequent claim that Shakespeare simply “swapped” Livy’s names obscures his actual dependence on a different ancient version.

### Retreat and death

The sources preserve irreconcilable endings:

- **Old age among the Volsci:** Fabius Pictor, quoted by Livy 2.40.10–11. **[EA fragment]**
- **Unspecified death amid Volscian hatred:** Livy 2.40 first notes that accounts varied. **[LC]**
- **Suicide:** Cicero, *Brutus* 42 and *Laelius* 42. **[EA/RV]**
- **Murder by Volscian opponents:** Dionysius 8.57–59 and Plutarch 39. Tullus/Aufidius’ faction accuses him of betraying Volscian interests and kills him at a public meeting. **[LC/RV]**
- **Honorable funeral after murder:** Plutarch 39 says the Volsci nevertheless buried him splendidly and allowed their women to mourn for ten months. **[LC]**

Shakespeare adopts murder at Aufidius’ instigation but transforms it into a theatrical climax in which conspirators stab Coriolanus after Aufidius calls him “boy.” **[MI]** Collin instead has him die by suicide, and Beethoven’s overture musically follows that tragic conception. **[MI]**

No ancient source securely identifies a grave, body, relic, or place of burial.

### Documented interactions: named divine beings and heroic figures

The category is applied broadly to named actors inside the legendary cycle. There is no evidence that Coriolanus personally meets an Olympian-style deity.

- **Ancus Marcius** — Coriolanus is presented as a remote descendant through the Marcii; Plutarch, *Coriolanus* 1, c. 100–120 CE. **[LC/LS]**
- **Numa Pompilius** — remote genealogical ancestor through Ancus Marcius; Plutarch 1. **[LC/LS]**
- **Tarquinius Superbus** — no direct meeting is narrated securely; Coriolanus supposedly fights the Tarquinian side’s forces at Lake Regillus; Plutarch 3. **[LC]**
- **Postumus Cominius Auruncus** — Roman consul and commander at Corioli; praises and rewards Marcius and, in Plutarch, bestows the cognomen; Livy 2.33, Dionysius 6.92–94, Plutarch 8–11. **[LC]**
- **Tullus/Attius Tullius Aufidius** — Volscian rival, host, ally, co-commander, later accuser and instigator of death; Livy 2.35–40, Dionysius 8, Plutarch 22–39. **[LC; name-form RV]**
- **Menenius Agrippa** — political mediator and, especially in later dramatic tradition, an older friend; involved in Roman negotiations; Dionysius 7–8, Plutarch, Shakespeare. **[LC; relationship enlarged EM]**
- **Sicinius** — plebeian tribune and prosecutor/opponent; Dionysius and Plutarch; Shakespeare expands the role. **[LC/EM]**
- **Junius Brutus, tribune** — plebeian political opponent in the later narrative; Dionysius/Plutarch and Shakespeare. He is not Lucius Junius Brutus, expeller of the kings. **[LC/EM]**
- **Veturia** — mother in Livy and Dionysius; confronts and persuades him to spare Rome; Livy 2.40, Dionysius 8.39–54. **[LC]**
- **Volumnia** — wife in Livy/Dionysius but mother in Plutarch; participates in the successful supplication; Livy 2.40, Dionysius 8, Plutarch 33–36. **[LC/RV]**
- **Vergilia** — wife in Plutarch; joins the women’s embassy with their children; Plutarch 33–36. **[LC/RV]**
- **Valeria** — sister of Publicola and organizer or leader of the matronal mission in Dionysius and Plutarch; *Roman Antiquities* 8.39–44; Plutarch 33. **[LC/RV]**
- **Publicola (Publius Valerius Publicola)** — no direct interaction with Coriolanus is securely narrated; he enters the cycle genealogically as Valeria’s brother. **[NEG/direct; LC/indirect]**
- **Titus Latinius** — no personal encounter with Coriolanus; Latinius’ divinely compelled report causes repetition of the games used in the Volscian grievance; Livy 2.36–38, Plutarch 24–25. **[LC/indirect]**
- **Jupiter** — no direct encounter. Jupiter communicates through Latinius’ dream concerning the games; the resulting repetition becomes part of Tullus’ political scheme; Livy 2.36, Plutarch 24. **[LC/indirect]**
- **Fortuna Muliebris** — no narrated personal epiphany or conversation with Coriolanus. Her temple and cult are etiologically attached to the women’s success in turning him back; Livy 2.40, Dionysius 8.55–56, Valerius Maximus. **[LC/cult aition]**
- **Themistocles** — comparative pairing, not a meeting: both are celebrated commanders exiled by ungrateful cities who turn against their homelands; Cicero, *Brutus* 41–42 and *Laelius* 42; Plutarch’s moral comparisons reinforce the type. **[EA/LS]**
- **Alcibiades** — literary pair, not a meeting. Plutarch juxtaposes their ambition, anger, political defects, and relations with the people in the paired *Lives*. **[LC/LS]**
- **Fortuna, Mars, or other gods as personal patrons** — no source states that one guides, arms, fathers, rescues, or punishes him. The apparent connection with Mars in *Marcius* is a nomen, not a narrated divine relationship. **[NEG]**

## 5. Cult, Tomb and Ritual

### No cult of Coriolanus

There is no securely attested:

- temple, altar, priesthood, festival, sacrifice, games, oracle, or votive inscription dedicated to Coriolanus;
- *heroon* or heroized grave;
- recovery or translation of his bones;
- ancient divine image identifiable as Coriolanus.

He is an exemplary legendary mortal, not an attested cult hero. **[NEG]**

### Temple of Fortuna Muliebris

The religious monument belongs to Fortuna and the Roman matrons, not to Coriolanus.

- **Livy 2.40.12 [LC]** says a temple of Fortuna Muliebris was built and dedicated as a memorial of the women’s intervention.
- **Dionysius 8.55–56 [LC]** gives a fuller foundation account: the state funds a temple and cult image; the women use their collected money for a second image; Valeria receives priestly prominence; the goddess’ image is said to speak its approval. The speaking statue is a religious marvel tradition, not archaeological evidence.
- **Valerius Maximus [LC/LS]** transmits the exemplary and miraculous aspects.
- **Festus and later topographic testimony [LS]** place the sanctuary at the fourth milestone of the Via Latina.
- The festival/dedication was associated with early July in the calendar tradition; claims that every detail can be projected securely into 488/487 BCE exceed the evidence.

Topographic studies treat it as a boundary sanctuary near the old *ager Romanus*. Archaeological and epigraphic evidence confirms a sanctuary of Fortuna Muliebris on the Via Latina and later rebuilding, including imperial-period patronage. It does not prove that Coriolanus caused its foundation. Modern scholarship consequently distinguishes:

1. the sanctuary’s historical existence;
2. the later literary aition linking it to a women’s embassy;
3. the unverified fifth-century biography attached to that aition.

The reverse possibility—that the cult and its ritual prominence generated or reshaped the Coriolanus episode—is a major **[MSR]** hypothesis.

### Tomb and body

Dionysius and Plutarch give him a Volscian funeral, but neither supplies an archaeologically recoverable tomb. No site at Antium, Corioli, Rome, or elsewhere can be identified as his grave. Claims of “the tomb of Coriolanus” lack ancient epigraphic or archaeological authentication. **[NEG/MI where presented as fact]**

### Images

No securely identified ancient portrait or narrative image of Coriolanus is known. His recognizable iconography begins in post-classical art and normally selects one scene: the armored commander confronted by kneeling or supplicating women and children outside Rome.

## 6. Historical Evolution of the Figure

### Fifth–fourth centuries BCE: alleged setting, no contemporary record

The social setting—Volscian pressure, unstable warfare, debt, grain shortages, and elite/plebeian conflict—contains historically plausible components. No contemporary document names Coriolanus. **[NEG]**

### Late third–second centuries BCE: annalistic formation

Fabius Pictor’s lost history demonstrates that the tradition had formed by the late third century BCE. Family memorial traditions, public monuments, dramatic narrative, and Greek historiographic models may all have contributed, but no source permits their shares to be isolated. **[EA/MSR]**

### First century BCE: republican exemplum

Cicero treats Coriolanus as already proverbial: the great commander whose resentment turns against the homeland. His comparison with Themistocles places Roman memory into a Greek exemplary framework. **[EA/LS]**

### Augustan period: canonical shaping

Livy creates the durable compact narrative; Dionysius turns it into an extended constitutional and rhetorical drama. Both connect martial arrogance, the conflict of the orders, matronal intervention, and religious commemoration. Their agreement proves a shared developed tradition, not fifth-century factuality. **[LC]**

### Imperial period: ethical biography and epitome

Plutarch reshapes the material around nature, education, anger, maternal dependence, and political incompetence, pairing Coriolanus with Alcibiades. Valerius Maximus extracts moral examples. Dio and epitomators condense the story into a standard episode in Rome’s early republican memory. **[LC/LS]**

### Medieval transmission

Coriolanus survives principally through Latin historical epitomes, Livian traditions, moral exempla, and Plutarch’s eventual revival. He does not acquire a major independent medieval cult, saintly identity, or vernacular folklore cycle. **[NEG/EM]**

### Renaissance and early modernity

Humanist recovery of Plutarch and Livy makes the women’s embassy an important artistic subject. It serves as an exemplar of maternal authority, female civic virtue, mercy, and the defeat of martial rage. Luca Signorelli’s fresco, about 1509, belongs to this phase. **[EM]**

Thomas North’s 1579 Plutarch transmits the Greek-name version to Shakespeare. Shakespeare’s *Coriolanus*, probably written 1607–08 and first printed in the 1623 First Folio, becomes the principal source of the modern figure. **[EM]**

### Eighteenth–nineteenth centuries

Political adaptations repeatedly align Coriolanus with contemporary crises:

- Nahum Tate’s adaptation, 1682, radicalizes the ending. **[MI]**
- John Dennis’ *The Invader of His Country*, 1719, addresses Jacobite politics. **[MI]**
- James Thomson’s *Coriolanus*, 1745, again responds to Jacobitism. **[MI]**
- Heinrich Joseph von Collin’s *Coriolan*, 1804, makes him a German Romantic tragic hero. **[MI]**
- Beethoven’s *Coriolan Overture*, Op. 62, performed in 1807 for Collin’s play, becomes the best-known non-dramatic musical reception. **[MI]**

### Twentieth–twenty-first centuries

The figure becomes a testing ground for fascism, militarism, class politics, authoritarian charisma, mass politics, and the political responsibilities of artists. Brecht rewrites the play from 1951 onward; Günter Grass responds to Brecht and the East German uprising of 1953 in *Die Plebejer proben den Aufstand* (1966). Ralph Fiennes’ 2011 film relocates Shakespeare’s language to a visually contemporary Balkan-style war. **[MI]**

## 7. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Literature and theatre

Shakespeare’s tragedy is not simply the ancient biography dramatized. It expands Menenius and Volumnia, sharpens the plebeian crowd scenes, places the hero’s body and wounds at the center of electoral ritual, intensifies the homoerotic rivalry with Aufidius, and supplies the fatal taunt “boy.” These are early-modern dramaturgical constructions. **[EM/MI]**

Brecht’s unfinished *Coriolan*, composed chiefly in 1951–53, rejects the premise that the city depends upon a unique military genius. The plebeians and collective military organization become historically effective; the tragedy shifts from the fall of a great individual toward analysis of class power. Brecht died in 1956 before finishing it. A completed theatrical version by Manfred Wekwerth and Joachim Tenschert opened in Frankfurt in 1962; the Berliner Ensemble staged it in 1964. **[MI]**

Grass’ 1966 play stages a fictionalized Brecht rehearsing *Coriolan* during the 17 June 1953 East German uprising, exposing the tension between revolutionary aesthetics and refusal to support actual workers. **[MI]**

### Music and opera

- Beethoven, *Coriolan Overture*, Op. 62, 1807: composed for Collin, not directly for Shakespeare or Plutarch. Its opposed musical materials are conventionally heard as martial resolve confronted by maternal pleading. **[MI]**
- Ján Cikker, opera *Coriolanus*, composed in the early 1970s and premiered in Prague in 1974: a twentieth-century operatic recasting of Shakespeare. **[MI]**

### Visual art

- Renaissance cassone and fresco cycles make the women’s embassy a scene of female virtue and civic concord. **[EM]**
- Luca Signorelli, *Coriolanus Persuaded by His Family to Spare Rome*, about 1509, painted for the Petrucci palace in Siena. **[EM]**
- The “Master of Coriolanus,” active about 1500, takes a modern scholarly name from bronze plaquettes associated with Coriolanus subjects; this is an art-historical convenience, not an ancient cult title. **[EM/LS]**
- Seventeenth- and eighteenth-century paintings and prints repeatedly show Veturia/Volumnia kneeling before the armored general. Their inconsistent titles often mix Livian and Plutarchan names. **[EM]**
- A 1703–04 painting at Castello Bufalini explicitly depicts Veturia and Volumnia obtaining peace. **[EM]**

### Film and television

- *Coriolano: eroe senza patria* (1964), starring Gordon Scott, is a loose sword-and-sandal reinvention. **[MI]**
- BBC *The Spread of the Eagle* (1963) and BBC Television Shakespeare (1984 broadcast cycle; production commonly dated 1983–84) bring Shakespeare’s version to television. **[MI]**
- Ralph Fiennes’ *Coriolanus* (2011) uses Shakespeare’s text in a contemporary militarized state with television news and modern weaponry. **[MI]**

### Political appropriation

Coriolanus has been usable from mutually hostile political positions:

- royalist and anti-Jacobite adaptations treat him as a warning against rebellion or treason;
- Romantic reception admires isolated heroic will;
- anti-democratic readings identify with his contempt for the populace;
- Marxist and Brechtian readings expose the class system that produces and then discards him;
- modern productions invoke fascism, military dictatorship, austerity, food politics, populism, and elite disdain.

Descriptions of Coriolanus as “proto-fascist” are modern analogies, not ancient classifications. There is no documented continuous nationalist cult or modern extremist organization centered on the ancient Coriolanus comparable to appropriations of Sparta, Achilles, or Roman imperial symbols. **[NEG]** His political potency lies chiefly in Shakespearean performance and criticism.

### Commercial and popular naming

Suzanne Collins’ Coriolanus Snow in *The Hunger Games* series is a documented modern reuse of the resonant Roman/Shakespearean name. Snow is not a continuation of the Roman legend and should not be cited as a variant tradition about Gaius/Gnaeus Marcius. **[MI]**

## 8. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Was Coriolanus historical?

Three broad positions recur.

#### Complete or near-complete rejection

Nineteenth-century hypercritical historians argued that essentially every feature was unhistorical. Reasons include:

- the long gap before surviving sources;
- heroic narrative patterns;
- constitutional and electoral anachronisms;
- the implausibly developed conflict-of-the-orders politics;
- discrepant names, chronology, campaigns, and deaths;
- the possibility that the story explains the Fortuna Muliebris sanctuary retrospectively.

This position remains defensible as a judgment on the biography, though certainty that no individual underlies it goes beyond the evidence.

#### Historical nucleus, legendary political biography

A common modern position allows a possible fifth-century war leader or *condottiere* who changed allegiance during Roman–Volscian warfare, while regarding the capture of Corioli, cognomen ceremony, tribunal politics, speeches, and women’s embassy as heavily reworked. **[MSR]**

This reconstruction is plausible because mobile aristocratic war bands and unstable loyalties fit archaic central Italy. It is not independently attested.

#### Aetiological and structural legend

Other studies emphasize the tale’s relation to Fortuna Muliebris, boundary ritual, matronal religious authority, and the repeated pattern:

1. male civic institutions fail;
2. women cross the boundary;
3. maternal speech defeats martial force;
4. cult memorializes restored civic order.

On this account, the sanctuary may be older than the full biography, with Coriolanus supplying an explanatory narrative. **[MSR]**

### Patrician or plebeian?

The canonical sources make him an aristocratic, normally patrician champion. Yet historically attested Marcii of the middle and late Republic are plebeian. Proposed explanations include:

- later authors projected their own patrician–plebeian categories backward;
- a branch of Marcii was once patrician;
- the family promoted or appropriated the legend;
- “patrician Coriolanus” is a narrative construction needed for the conflict.

No proposal is demonstrable. The contradiction should not be harmonized away.

### Chronology and synchronism

Cicero compares Coriolanus with Themistocles and places the Volscian conflict roughly twenty years before Themistocles’ treasonous exile. Ancient accounts also connect the grain crisis with Sicilian rulers in chronologically inconsistent ways. Such Greek synchronisms are learned chronological constructions, not direct contemporary links. **[LS]**

### The name as evidence

A victory cognomen supposedly granted in the early fifth century is itself problematic. Roman naming practices were later regularized, and stories explaining surnames through single exploits are often retrospective. Yet a toponymic name can preserve genuine geographical association without validating the explanatory exploit. **[MSR]**

### Source dependence

Plutarch’s narrative agrees closely with Dionysius in many places and probably depends directly or indirectly upon the same developed tradition. Their agreement therefore cannot simply be counted as two independent witnesses. Livy is much shorter and explicitly knows multiple death versions. Cicero proves that the treason-and-death paradigm predated the Augustan historians, while Fabius proves a still earlier exile tradition.

### Comparative theories

- Themistocles and Alcibiades are ancient moral-comparative frameworks, not proof of Greek invention.
- Dumézilian readings have sought Indo-European structures in Roman heroic material. Applied to Coriolanus, such analysis can illuminate narrative opposition—warrior force, civic sovereignty, fertility/grain, and female mediation—but does not date the story or prove common Indo-European descent. **[MSR]**
- Structural and gender-focused studies read the matrons’ crossing of civic boundaries as controlled exceptional action that ultimately restores male civic order. **[MSR]**
- Psychoanalytic readings concentrate on the hero’s dependence on his mother and inability to form an autonomous civic self, especially in Shakespeare. These interpret the literary character, not an ancient person. **[MSR]**
- Campbell’s generalized monomyth is a poor fit: Coriolanus does not complete a stable departure–initiation–return pattern, and forcing him into it erases the Roman political and cultic specificity. **[MSR]**

### Archaeological evidence

Archaeology supports the broad world in which the story was placed:

- conflict and cultural interaction among Romans, Latins, and Volscians;
- archaic and republican sanctuaries;
- a real Fortuna Muliebris cult site on the Via Latina.

It has not produced Coriolanus himself. Neither the Lapis Niger nor Lavinium’s so-called heroon nor the Lupercal supplies a cross-reference to him. **[NEG]**

### Discredited or unsupported claims still circulating

- “Coriolanus is a securely documented Roman general.” **Unsupported:** he is securely documented only as a later tradition.
- “The Fasti Capitolini list his office.” **False/unsupported.**
- “His tomb has been discovered.” **Unsupported.**
- “He received hero cult.” **Unsupported.**
- “Coriolanus means ‘victorious at Corioli’ because *lanus* means victorious.” **Modern false etymology.**
- “Shakespeare invented the wife/mother name reversal.” **Misleading:** he followed Plutarch through North.
- “The temple proves the women actually stopped his army.” **Invalid inference:** it proves a sanctuary and later association, not the event.
- “Livy, Dionysius, and Plutarch are independent contemporary witnesses.” **False:** all wrote roughly five to six centuries after the traditional date and share earlier historiographic traditions.

## 10. Sources and Assessment

### Principal primary editions and translations consulted

- Cicero, *Brutus* and *Laelius de amicitia*.
- Livy, *Ab urbe condita* 2.33–40.
- Dionysius of Halicarnassus, *Roman Antiquities* 7.20–67 and 8.1–62, Earnest Cary’s Loeb translation.
- Plutarch, *Life of Coriolanus* and *Comparison of Alcibiades and Coriolanus*, Bernadotte Perrin’s Loeb translation.
- Cassius Dio, fragmentary early books.
- Shakespeare, *Coriolanus*, First Folio text as represented by the Folger edition.

### Central modern scholarship

The major historical questions are treated in T. J. Cornell’s work on early Rome and his reference entry on Coriolanus; studies of Roman historiography and the conflict of the orders; scholarship on Fortuna Muliebris and women’s ritual; topographic work on the Via Latina sanctuary; and studies of Dionysius’ classicizing construction of the legend. Wiseman’s work on Roman mythmaking, including *Remus*, is methodologically relevant: early Roman narratives must be studied as datable acts of memory and literary construction rather than mined uncritically for biography. Dumézil and comparativist readings belong to the history of interpretation, not the primary evidence.

### Final evidentiary judgment

Coriolanus is securely attested as a Roman legendary figure from the late third century BCE indirectly and from Cicero directly. The secure historical record is the record of people telling, reshaping, monumentalizing, painting, performing, and politicizing his story. A fifth-century war leader may lie behind it, but no contemporary name, tomb, image, inscription, or cult confirms him. The Fortuna Muliebris sanctuary is real; the claim that Coriolanus’ retreat caused it is literary aition. The contradictions—Gnaeus or Gaius, Veturia or Volumnia, Volumnia or Vergilia, conviction or voluntary exile, murder or suicide or old age—are not noise to eliminate. They are the documentary history of Coriolanus.
