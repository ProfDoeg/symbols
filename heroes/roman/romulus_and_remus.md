# Romulus and Remus: Research Dossier

> **Traditional class:** Romulus and Remus are divine-born mortal twins, exposed-child heroes, rival founders and Roman culture heroes. Romulus becomes first king, lawgiver and conqueror and—under the canonical late-Republican/early-imperial synthesis—is translated into heaven and identified with the god Quirinus. Remus dies before the foundation and ordinarily receives neither kingship nor an independently attested public cult, although Ovid connects his ghost etiologically with the Lemuria.
>
> **Earliest secure attestation:** the first unambiguous, independently datable evidence for the developed wolf-and-twins legend is the sculptural group added by the curule aediles Gnaeus and Quintus Ogulnius to a bronze she-wolf beside the *ficus Ruminalis* in **296 BCE**, recorded by Livy 10.23.11–12. Earlier Greek founder genealogies, possible fourth-century images and an anonymous “Romos/Rhomos” are disputed or do not securely attest the canonical twins. The archaic Lapis Niger inscription, conventionally dated around the sixth century BCE, does **not** name Romulus or Remus.

## Evidentiary key

- **[EA] Secure early attestation:** independently datable Republican text, inscription, coin or monument.
- **[LC] Later canonical elaboration:** principally first century BCE through second century CE.
- **[RV] Regional or minority variant.**
- **[LS] Learned antiquarian systematization or rationalization.**
- **[SI] Syncretistic identification.**
- **[MA] Medieval or early-modern accretion or reception.**
- **[F] Folklore or weakly localized tradition.**
- **[MSR] Modern scholarly reconstruction:** an argument, not an ancient fact.
- **[MI] Modern invention or demonstrably modern attribution.**

Dates assigned to legendary events below are dates claimed by ancient chronography, not independently verified historical dates.

## 1. Identification, names and epithets

### Identification

**[LC]** In the Augustan canonical account, Romulus and Remus are sons of the Vestal Rhea Silvia, daughter of the deposed Alban king Numitor, and Mars—or, in Livy’s deliberately skeptical formulation, sons attributed to Mars because divine paternity made rape more honorable. King Amulius exposes them in the flooded Tiber; a she-wolf nurses them beneath the *ficus Ruminalis*; the shepherd Faustulus and his wife Larentia raise them; they restore Numitor and attempt to found a colony. Rival auguries and a dispute over the wall end with Remus’ death. Romulus founds Rome, opens an asylum, orders or enables the mass abduction of women at the Consualia, wars with their communities, shares power with the Sabine Titus Tatius, then reigns alone. He disappears in a storm and is either translated to heaven as Quirinus or murdered and dismembered by senators. Livy 1.3–16, Dionysius 1.76–2.63 and Plutarch, *Romulus* 3–29 are the fullest ancient narrative complexes ([Livy, Books 1–2](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus:text:1999.02.0151); [Dionysius, Book I](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Dionysius_of_Halicarnassus/1D*.html); [Plutarch](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Plutarch/Lives/Romulus*.html)).

### Names and scripts

- **[EA/LC] Latin:** *Rōmulus*; *Remus*. The vowel of *Remus* was short in Latin. Republican and imperial inscriptions render the names in Roman capitals, **ROMVLVS**, **REMVS**.
- **[EA/LC] Greek:** Ῥωμύλος (*Rhōmylos*) commonly renders Romulus; Ῥῶμος (*Rhōmos*, “Romos/Romus”) appears in Greek founder traditions and can denote a different founder, an ancestor, or eventually Remus. Dionysius preserves numerous incompatible Greek genealogies and recognizes that “Romos” was not always the canonical Remus.
- **[LC] Patronymics and titles:** *Martis filius*, “son of Mars,” appears in the Augustan Fasti Triumphales; *rex*, “king”; *conditor urbis*, “founder of the city”; *pater patriae* or civic father in later political analogy; *Quirinus* after apotheosis. The Fasti record “Romulus, son of Mars, king” triumphing over the Caeninenses ([Fasti Triumphales](https://www.attalus.org/translate/fasti.html)).
- **[LC] Remus:** occasionally *Remus frater* or Romulus’ *geminus*, “twin”; Ovid’s phrase *Remo cum fratre Quirinus* deliberately preserves both the dead brother and the deified survivor.

### Etymology

**[LS] Ancient explanations**

- Plutarch, *Romulus* 4, records derivation from Latin *ruma*, “teat/breast,” because the twins sucked the wolf’s teats; this associates their names with *Rumina*, a nursing divinity, and the *ficus Ruminalis*.
- Greek writers connected *Rōmē* with Greek ῥώμη, “strength.” This is Greek explanatory wordplay, not a demonstrated linguistic derivation.
- Servius, commenting on *Aeneid* 8.63 and 8.90, reports *Rumon* as an old name of the Tiber and relates it to the river’s erosion of its banks. Whether *Rumon* is genuinely archaic or antiquarian reconstruction remains disputed.
- Varro and later antiquarians generated several etymologies for *Quirinus*: from Sabine *curis*, “spear”; from Cures; or from *Quirites*. Ovid, *Fasti* 2.475–480, presents all three rather than deciding among them ([Ovid, Fasti II](https://www.poetryintranslation.com/PITBR/Latin/OvidFastiBkTwo.php)).

**[MSR] Modern positions**

- The transparent morphology of *Romulus* permits “Roman” or “little Roman,” suggesting an eponym constructed from *Roma*; this does not itself date the construction.
- Proposed Etruscan connections include the gentilic forms *Rumelna/Rumlna*. The direction of borrowing—Etruscan to Latin or Latin to Etruscan—is disputed.
- Derivation of Rome from a pre-Latin river-name *Rumon* remains possible but unproved.
- T. P. Wiseman derives Remus programmatically from *remorari*, “to delay,” making him the political “latecomer.” This is part of Wiseman’s 1995 reconstruction of a late fourth-/early third-century invention and has not become consensus.
- Comparative Indo-European derivation of Remus from reconstructed *Yemo*, “twin,” connects him with the primordial sacrificed twin of comparative mythology. The phonology and the historical route remain disputed; it cannot establish that the Roman story is archaic.

### Quirinus

**[EA/SI]** Quirinus was an independently worshipped Roman god before the surviving canonical texts identified him with Romulus. His flamen belonged to the major priesthoods, and his festival, the Quirinalia, fell on 17 February. The temple dedicated by Lucius Papirius Cursor in 293 BCE is historically attested by Livy 10.46.7–8. Modern reference scholarship generally dates the assimilation “Romulus = Quirinus” no earlier than the third century BCE; it is therefore unsafe to project the equation into Rome’s supposed eighth-century foundation ([Oxford Classical Dictionary, “Quirinus”](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/61673/chapter-abstract/550492894); [Vé, temple chronology](https://www.persee.fr/doc/bude_0004-5527_2010_num_1_1_2361)).

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### The earliest evidence

1. **Possible fourth-century BCE images — [EA? disputed].** Etruscan or central-Italian mirrors have been read as the wolf and twins. Identification and dating are not always secure, and an image of nursing twins does not by itself establish every element of the Roman narrative.

2. **Ogulnian monument, 296 BCE — [EA].** Livy 10.23.11–12 says the Ogulnii placed infant figures beneath an already existing she-wolf near the sacred fig tree. This is the first completely unambiguous and datable Roman attestation of wolf, twins and Roman civic location. Because Livy wrote nearly three centuries later, the monument is known textually, but the aediles and dedication belong to a historically documented Republican context. Wiseman makes it the anchor for his late-development thesis; critics agree about its evidentiary importance without accepting his complete reconstruction ([BMCR review of Wiseman](https://bmcr.brynmawr.edu/1997/1997.05.18/)).

3. **Romano-Campanian didrachms, early third century BCE — [EA].** Coins showing the she-wolf and twins establish that the image had become state currency iconography by the early third century. Precise dating varies among numismatists.

4. **Fabius Pictor, late third century BCE — [EA, fragmentary].** Rome’s earliest historian wrote in Greek during the Second Punic War. His foundation account is lost but was used by Dionysius and Plutarch. It already included much of the familiar narrative. Because it survives only through later citations, exact wording and dependence are unrecoverable.

5. **Naevius and Ennius, third–second centuries BCE — [EA, fragmentary].** Naevius’ *Bellum Punicum* and Ennius’ *Annales* integrated Rome’s Trojan and Alban past. Ennius treated the twins’ augury and Romulus’ divine future; surviving fragments do not provide a continuous narrative.

6. **Lapis Niger complex — [EA monument; LS identification].** The archaic cippus below the black pavement is among the oldest Latin inscriptions and refers to a *rex*, but it does not name Romulus. Literary antiquarians variously connected the place with Romulus’ death or grave, Faustulus, or Hostus Hostilius. Calling it “Romulus’ tomb” is therefore an ancient or modern attribution to an archaic sanctuary, not an inscribed identification ([German Archaeological Institute record](https://arachne.dainst.org/entity/10553)).

### Principal surviving corpus

- **Cicero, *De re publica* 2.4–20; *De legibus*; first century BCE — [LS].** Rationalizes Romulus as a prudent statesman, treats his apotheosis as ancient civic belief and uses the monarchy in constitutional argument.
- **Varro, *De lingua Latina* and lost *Antiquitates*; first century BCE — [LS].** Preserves etymologies, ritual topography and cultic explanations; much survives only through quotation.
- **Virgil, *Aeneid*, 29–19 BCE — [LC].** Romulus appears prospectively: Jupiter promises Rome’s imperial future and the “Roman, son of Mars”; the shield of Aeneas includes the wolf nursing the twins; book 8 maps the Lupercal and archaic Rome. Virgil does not retell the continuous childhood story.
- **Livy, *Ab urbe condita* 1.3–16, composed from the 20s BCE — [LC].** The most influential compact historical synthesis. Livy flags uncertainty, gives rape as the plain alternative to Mars’ paternity, and presents both apotheosis and senatorial murder.
- **Propertius 4.1, 4.4; late first century BCE — [LC].** Uses primitive Rome, Tarpeia and the founder landscape in Augustan elegy.
- **Ovid, *Fasti* 2–5, composed around 2–8 CE — [LC/LS].** Supplies festival aetiologies: Lupercalia, Quirinalia, Parilia and Lemuria/“Remuria”; offers alternative etymologies and gives Remus’ ghost a role in instituting rites.
- **Ovid, *Metamorphoses* 14.805–851, about 8 CE — [LC].** Mars asks Jupiter to fulfil his promise; Romulus becomes Quirinus; Juno and Iris bring Hersilia to him, and she becomes Hora ([Ovid, Metamorphoses XIV](https://www.theoi.com/Text/OvidMetamorphoses14.html)).
- **Dionysius of Halicarnassus, *Roman Antiquities* 1.72–2.63, published 7 BCE — [LC/LS].** The most extensive collector of Greek and Roman variants; gives institutions, wars and rationalistic alternatives.
- **Fasti Capitolini and Fasti Triumphales, Augustan inscriptions — [LC/state systematization].** Fix Romulus within an official king-list and triumphal chronology. They are Augustan commemorative documents, not eighth-century records.
- **Plutarch, *Life of Romulus*, late first/early second century CE — [LC/LS].** Explicitly collates incompatible origins, parents, etymologies, killing narratives and dates. It depends on earlier lost historians, including Diocles of Peparethus and Fabius Pictor.
- **Florus, *Epitome* 1.1; second century CE — [LC].** Condenses Romulus into imperial origin history.
- **Cassius Dio, *Roman History* books 1–2, early third century CE, surviving in fragments and epitomes — [LC].** Preserves additional rationalizations and even a tradition of an earlier Rome founded by earlier figures named Romulus and Remus ([Dio fragments](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/e/roman/texts/cassius_dio/1*.html)).
- **Festus/Paul the Deacon, *De verborum significatu*, second century CE epitome and eighth-century epitome — [LS].** Essential but textually damaged evidence for archaic terms, Remuria, the Lapis Niger and cult topography.
- **Servius, commentary on Virgil, fourth–fifth centuries CE — [LS].** Preserves otherwise lost antiquarian variants and etymologies. The manuscript tradition is divided between shorter and expanded versions.
- **Augustine, *City of God* 3.6; 15.5, 413–426 CE — [late-antique Christian reinterpretation].** Makes fratricide emblematic of the violent earthly city, comparing Romulus and Remus with Cain and Abel.
- **Origo gentis Romanae, probably fourth century CE — [LS].** A late compilation of Roman origins with older material embedded in it.

## 3. The story: episodes and variants

### Alban ancestry and conception

**[LC canonical]** Procas’ sons Numitor and Amulius dispute Alba Longa. Amulius usurps Numitor, kills or neutralizes his male heirs and forces Numitor’s daughter Rhea Silvia—also called Ilia—into Vestal service. She conceives twins by Mars; Livy 1.4 says she named Mars either because she believed it or because a god made the violation seem more honorable.

**[RV]** Plutarch records variants in which the mother is a slave impregnated after an oracle ordered a virgin to have intercourse with a phallic apparition from the hearth; the mistress substitutes the slave. Other Greek genealogies make a *Romos* a son or descendant of Aeneas, Odysseus, Latinus, Roma or Emathion. These are competing founder constructions, not missing pieces of one family tree.

### Exposure, Tiber and wolf

**[LC canonical]** Amulius orders the infants drowned. The flooded Tiber prevents servants from reaching the main current, so they abandon the trough or basket in shallow water. It comes to rest near the *ficus Ruminalis* at the foot of the Palatine. A she-wolf nurses them; a woodpecker sometimes brings food. Faustulus discovers them and Larentia raises them.

**[LS rationalization]** Livy and Plutarch report that *lupa*, “she-wolf,” could also mean prostitute and that Larentia may have been nicknamed *lupa*. This does not prove the animal version arose from a mistranslation; it demonstrates that late sources already rationalized the marvel.

**[RV]** Dionysius reports more than one rescuer and localizes the discovery within a still-visible sacred landscape. The woodpecker, like the wolf, belongs to Mars in later explanation.

### Youth and recognition

**[LC]** The twins grow into leaders among shepherds, attack robbers and divide spoils. During the Lupercalia or a related running game, Remus is captured by Numitor’s men and accused of raiding. Faustulus reveals the exposure; Numitor recognizes his grandsons; the brothers kill Amulius and restore Numitor.

**[RV]** Accounts differ on whether Faustulus always knew the boys’ identity, how Numitor recognized them, which twin was captured and whether the young men’s bands corresponded to named Lupercalian colleges.

### Choosing the site and augury

**[LC]** Numitor allows the brothers to establish a colony where they were raised. Romulus favors the Palatine; Remus the Aventine or a place called Remoria. They consult birds. Remus sees six vultures first; Romulus sees twelve later. The dispute is whether priority or number conveys sovereignty.

**[RV]** Dionysius says Romulus initially lies that he has seen birds; twelve then appear. Livy presents rival partisans acclaiming one twin for priority and the other for number. The traditions preserve an actual ambiguity in augural interpretation rather than a single uncontested verdict.

### The wall and death of Remus

- **[LC canonical] Romulus kills Remus:** Remus contemptuously jumps across the new wall; Romulus kills him and declares, in substance, “So perish whoever else shall leap over my walls.” Livy 1.7 attributes this version directly to *fama*.
- **[RV] Celer kills Remus:** Dionysius and Plutarch preserve the variant that Celer, Romulus’ overseer or companion, strikes Remus with a spade or mattock.
- **[RV] General fighting:** Remus, Faustulus and/or others die in a melee between factions rather than a single ritualized blow.
- **[LS/MSR] Foundation sacrifice:** Ancient narratives sometimes make Remus’ blood the cost of the walls; modern comparativists interpret him as a sacrificed twin or foundation victim. No Roman ritual document identifies his death as a historically performed human sacrifice.
- **[MSR] Political myth:** Wiseman reads Remus as a late plebeian co-founder, first honored and then killed in patrician counter-narrative. Reviewers accept the value of dating individual variants but regard his exact reconstruction—especially heroic human sacrifice in 296 BCE—as underdetermined.

### Foundation

**[LC/LS]** Romulus ploughs the *sulcus primigenius*, lifting the plough where gates will stand; establishes the *pomerium*; sacrifices; organizes citizens; and is elected king. Varro’s canonical calculation placed the foundation on 21 April 753 BCE. Other ancient chronographers gave other years. Plutarch reports the astrologer Tarutius’ retrocalculation of Romulus’ conception, birth and foundation—an explicit learned exercise, not documentary biography.

### Asylum, population and Sabine women

**[LC]** Romulus opens an asylum to fugitives, slaves, exiles and criminals. Neighboring communities refuse intermarriage. He announces games for Consus/Neptune Equester; Romans seize unmarried women from attending communities. Ancient euphemisms such as “rape of the Sabines” use *raptio*, abduction, but the tradition entails forced seizure and coerced marriage.

**[LC variants]** Livy emphasizes that Romulus tells the women they will have legal marriage, civic status and children; this speech legitimates rather than negates the violence. Plutarch gives varying totals—30, 527, 683 and others—and reports that only one married woman, Hersilia, was taken accidentally in one version. Dionysius supplies formal embassy speeches and constitutional consequences.

### Wars and Titus Tatius

**[LC]** Caenina, Antemnae and Crustumerium attack. Romulus kills Caenina’s king Acron in single combat and dedicates the *spolia opima* to Jupiter Feretrius. The Sabine king Titus Tatius attacks Rome. Tarpeia admits the Sabines to the citadel for “what they wear on their left arms”; they crush her with their shields.

**[RV]** Tarpeia is a traitor greedy for bracelets, a deceiver intending to demand shields, or a patriotic victim. Her story belongs to the Romulan war cycle but is not an interaction with Remus.

**[LC]** During the battle Romulus vows a temple to Jupiter Stator. The abducted women, led in many accounts by Hersilia, enter between fathers and husbands and compel peace. Romans and Sabines unite; Romulus and Tatius rule jointly. The people take or receive the name *Quirites*.

### Sole reign and institutions

**[LC/LS]** After Tatius is killed at Lavinium and Romulus declines to avenge him fully, Romulus again rules alone. Later historians credit him with the Senate, patricians and clients, tribes and curiae, cavalry centuries, priesthoods, military discipline and laws of marriage and paternal power. The lists vary and often project late-Republican institutions backward. Dionysius’ long constitutional portrait is Greek political historiography, not an archival law code from the eighth century BCE.

**[LC]** Romulus wages wars against Fidenae, Veii and other neighbors. Fasti credit him with triumphs; Dionysius makes him celebrate three. Precise campaigns are traditional king-history, not contemporary records.

### Disappearance, murder and apotheosis

- **[LC divine translation]** At the Goat’s Marsh (*Palus Caprae*), darkness, storm and thunder conceal Romulus; Mars carries him to heaven. Ovid dramatizes Mars asking Jupiter to fulfil a prior promise.
- **[LC epiphany]** Julius Proculus swears that Romulus appeared to him in superhuman form, announced that he was Quirinus and promised Rome dominion. Livy admits that Proculus’ authority calmed the army and populace.
- **[LC murder]** Senators kill and dismember Romulus, hiding pieces beneath their robes. Motives include resentment of tyranny, unequal treatment, cruelty and unilateral rule. Dionysius calls this the more plausible human account.
- **[RV] Popular murder:** A minority version assigns the killing to dissatisfied citizens.
- **[LC/LS]** No securely identified body exists. Traditions locating a grave by the Rostra or beneath the black stone conflict directly with bodily assumption.

### Remus after death

**[LC]** Remus has no continuous posthumous career. Ovid, *Fasti* 5, has his bloodied ghost appear to Faustulus and Larentia, asking that a day honor him; *Remuria* is then offered as the former name of Lemuria. Ovid explicitly supplies alternative etymology, so this cannot establish an archaic cult.

## 4. Relations with gods, heroes and named legendary figures

### Competing genealogies

### Consorts and descendants

- **Romulus–Hersilia — [LC]:** Hersilia is usually Romulus’ Sabine wife and leader/intercessor of the women. Some traditions instead make her wife of Hostus Hostilius.
- **Prima — [RV]:** A daughter attributed to Romulus in a fragmentary or antiquarian tradition.
- **Avilius/Aollius — [RV]:** A son attributed to Romulus and Hersilia in some later sources.
- **Remus — absence of evidence:** no stable ancient consort, child or dynasty is documented.

### Documented interactions, one figure per line

The list separates actual narrative contact from genealogy or cultic identification.

- **Aeneas — [LC/LS]:** Romulus and Remus descend from Aeneas through Ascanius and the Alban kings in the canonical genealogy; other Greek traditions make Romos his son or companion. Livy 1.1–3; Dionysius 1.72–73; first century BCE.
- **Ascanius/Iulus — [LC]:** dynastic ancestor of the twins through Alba Longa. Livy 1.3; Dionysius 1; first century BCE.
- **Mars — [LC]:** father of the twins; protects their martial identity and carries or obtains Romulus’ admission to heaven. Livy 1.4, Ovid *Fasti* 2 and *Metamorphoses* 14; first century BCE–first century CE.
- **Jupiter — [LC]:** validates augury, receives Romulus’ vows as Stator and Feretrius, and authorizes his apotheosis. Livy 1.10, 1.12; Ovid *Fasti* 2; first century BCE–first century CE.
- **Jupiter Feretrius — [LC/LS]:** receives Acron’s armor as *spolia opima* and a temple from Romulus. Livy 1.10; Dionysius 2.34; first century BCE.
- **Jupiter Stator — [LC/LS]:** halts the Roman flight after Romulus’ vow in the Sabine battle. Livy 1.12; Dionysius 2.50; first century BCE.
- **Quirinus — [SI]:** Romulus is identified with this older god after disappearance. Cicero, Livy, Ovid and Dionysius; identification securely current by the late Republic, probably developing from the third century BCE.
- **Rhea Silvia/Ilia — [LC]:** mother of both twins, raped or impregnated by Mars and imprisoned or cast into water in competing versions. Livy 1.3–4; Dionysius 1.76–79.
- **Vesta — [LC/LS]:** Rhea Silvia is Vesta’s priestess; Dionysius debates whether Romulus founded Vesta’s Roman cult but rejects details on topographical grounds. Dionysius 2.64–66.
- **Tiber/Tiberinus — [LC/SI]:** the river receives the exposed infants; in some variants the river-god marries or rescues Rhea Silvia. Livy 1.4; Ovidian and later tradition.
- **Rumina — [LS]:** nursing goddess associated etymologically with the fig and the twins’ suckling; antiquarian Roman tradition.
- **Faunus — [LS/cultic association]:** god associated with the Lupercal and Lupercalia; no early narrative says he personally rescues the twins.
- **Consus — [LC/cultic interaction]:** Romulus stages games of Consus as the occasion for abducting the women. Livy 1.9; Plutarch 14.
- **Neptune Equester — [SI]:** Greek or learned identification of the god honored at the abduction festival. Plutarch 14; imperial-era account.
- **Hercules/Heracles — [LC/topographical predecessor]:** Evander’s Hercules episode precedes the twins at the future site of Rome; Romulus later incorporates or honors the cult in some accounts. Livy 1.7; Dionysius 1–2.
- **Evander — [LC/LS]:** culture hero established at the future Roman site before Romulus; some antiquarians made Evander founder of the Lupercalia. Livy 1.5–7; Dionysius 1.32, 1.79.
- **Acron — [LC]:** king of Caenina killed in single combat by Romulus; his armor becomes the first *spolia opima*. Livy 1.10; Plutarch 16.
- **Titus Tatius — [LC]:** enemy Sabine king, reconciled co-ruler and, in some versions, Hersilia’s father. Livy 1.10–14; Dionysius 2.36–52.
- **Hersilia/Hora — [LC/SI]:** Romulus’ wife and Sabine peacemaker; after his apotheosis Juno sends Iris for her, and Quirinus receives and renames her Hora. Ovid *Metamorphoses* 14.829–851.
- **Juno — [LC]:** orders Hersilia’s translation to join Quirinus. Ovid *Metamorphoses* 14.
- **Iris — [LC]:** guides Hersilia to Romulus/Quirinus. Ovid *Metamorphoses* 14.
- **Numitor — [LC]:** maternal grandfather; recognizes the twins, is restored by them and advises their foundation. Livy 1.3–6; Dionysius 1.76–85.
- **Amulius — [LC]:** great-uncle and usurper who orders their exposure; killed by the twins and their followers. Livy 1.3–6.
- **Faustulus — [LC]:** shepherd foster-father and revealer of the twins’ identity; in variants dies in the brothers’ factional fighting. Livy 1.4–5; Dionysius 1.
- **Acca Larentia — [LC/LS]:** foster-mother; sometimes rationalized as the human “she-wolf.” Livy 1.4; Plutarch 4–5.
- **Celer — [RV]:** kills Remus for crossing the wall in one major variant, or supervises construction. Dionysius 1.87; Plutarch 10.
- **Tarpeia — [LC/RV]:** admits Tatius’ Sabines into the citadel and is crushed by their shields; motives vary. Livy 1.11; Propertius 4.4; Dionysius 2.38–40.
- **Hostus Hostilius — [RV]:** Roman champion killed in the Sabine war; in one genealogy Hersilia is his wife and ancestress of Tullus Hostilius. Livy 1.12; Plutarch 18.
- **Julius Proculus — [LC]:** claims to encounter the vanished Romulus and announces his identity as Quirinus. Livy 1.16; Dionysius 2.63; Plutarch 28.
- **Numa Pompilius — [LC]:** succeeds Romulus after the interregnum and regularizes religious institutions; no secure narrative meeting occurs. Livy 1.17–21.
- **Romulus Silvius — absence of direct interaction:** an Alban king in the genealogy; resemblance of name does not establish a meeting with the twins.
- **Castor and Pollux/Dioscuri — [MSR comparative]:** modern scholars compare the twins with divine twin traditions, but no canonical ancient episode makes Romulus and Remus interact with the Dioscuri.
- **Cain and Abel — [late-antique Christian equation/comparison]:** Augustine compares civic fratricide with biblical fratricide; they do not meet in ancient narrative. *City of God* 15.5.
- **Aeneas and Romulus as joint founders — [LC political iconography]:** Augustan art and literature pair ancestral immigrant and native founder conceptually; they do not meet in the canonical chronology.

## 5. Cult, tomb and ritual

### Romulus–Quirinus

**[EA/SI]** Quirinus had public cult, a major flamen and the Quirinalia. A temple was dedicated on the Quirinal in 293 BCE by Lucius Papirius Cursor; Augustus rebuilt it in 16 BCE. That evidence securely establishes Quirinus’ cult, but only the later identification makes every Quirinal rite “cult of Romulus.”

**[LC]** Dionysius 2.63 says Numa ordered Romulus honored under the name Quirinus with temple and annual sacrifices. This is a late explanatory history of the cult, not contemporary evidence from Numa’s supposed reign.

**Remus:** No independently attested temple, priesthood or annual state sacrifice to Remus is known. Ovid’s derivation of Lemuria from “Remuria” is learned poetic aetiology, not proof of a public hero cult.

### Lupercal and Lupercalia

**[EA cult / LC mythical connection]** The Lupercalia on 15 February was a genuine ancient Roman ritual involving the Luperci, sacrifice and running with hide-strips. Ancient authorities disagreed whether Evander, Romulus and Remus or another power founded it. Its connection with the twins’ naked youthful running is mythic aetiology, not evidence that the festival originated with historical twins.

**[LC topography]** Dionysius located the Lupercal against the Palatine on the road toward the Circus and associated it with a spring and the wolf’s cave.

**[MSR disputed discovery]** In 2007 a richly decorated circular grotto under the House of Augustus was publicized as the Lupercal, especially by Andrea Carandini. Fausto Zevi, Adriano La Regina and subsequent topographical analysis objected that its shell mosaic and domed form resemble an imperial nymphaeum, that it lies too high and too far from the Circus and ancient flood zone, and that ancient route descriptions point farther southwest. A detailed modern study concludes that the 2007 chamber is **not** the Lupercal and places the likely sanctuary near Santa Anastasia and the Circus Maximus ([Topography of the Lupercalia](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/papers-of-the-british-school-at-rome/article/topography-of-the-lupercalia/91F6642CFE4C0303D50E9F604F7EA3E4)).

### Ficus Ruminalis and wolf image

**[EA/LC]** The sacred fig beside which the twins were exposed was shown near the Palatine; another fig in the Comitium acquired linked traditions. Tacitus, *Annals* 13.58, records public anxiety when the Comitium tree withered in 58 CE. Multiple trees and transfers complicate any attempt to identify a single botanical relic.

### Lapis Niger and “tomb of Romulus”

**[EA archaeology]** The Comitium complex contains archaic altars, bases and an inscribed cippus. The inscription regulates sacred conduct and mentions a king; it contains no readable dedication to Romulus.

**[LS ancient variants]** Festus and scholiasts associated the black stone or nearby monuments with:

- the place where Romulus was killed;
- the grave of Romulus;
- the grave of Faustulus;
- the grave of Hostus Hostilius.

The contradictions show loss of the monument’s original meaning by the literary period.

**[MSR]** Giacomo Boni’s excavation in 1899 revived identification with Romulus. In 2020 officials presented a sixth-century-BCE tufa chest and altar-like cylinder in the vicinity as a possible cenotaph connected with later founder cult. No bones, name or dedication identifies it as Romulus’ burial. “Tomb of Romulus discovered” is therefore an overstatement; “archaic monument later associated with traditions of Romulus” is defensible ([Antiquity editorial](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/editorial/77000D0DD81A8DBBB730A2003F974E3E)).

### Lavinium

**[EA archaeology, not twins’ tomb]** Lavinium’s so-called *heroon of Aeneas*—a monumentalized archaic burial—demonstrates how a real early tomb could be reinterpreted as the shrine of a legendary founder. It is important comparative evidence for Roman-Latin founder cult, but it is not archaeological evidence for Romulus’ or Remus’ body.

### Capitoline Wolf

**[MA object with early-modern additions]** The famous *Lupa Capitolina* was long dated to the fifth century BCE and attributed to an Etruscan workshop. Technical examination, casting method and radiocarbon measurements of organic material in its casting core instead place the wolf most plausibly in the **eleventh–twelfth centuries CE**. The suckling twins were added in the late fifteenth century, traditionally associated with Antonio del Pollaiuolo. The statue is consequently not the Ogulnian monument and not independent Republican evidence. The 2012 radiocarbon report gave an eleventh–twelfth-century calibrated range ([2012 radiocarbon abstract](https://pure.au.dk/portal/files/47740858/27.06.12_radiocarbon_2012_abstracts_book.pdf); [assessment of the statue](https://s3-eu-west-1.amazonaws.com/s3-euw1-ap-pe-ws4-cws-documents.ri-prod/9781138543898/case-studies/chapter-1/The-Capitoline-Wolf.pdf)).

## 6. Historical evolution

### Before 300 BCE: inaccessible formation

**[MSR]** Archaeology documents Iron Age settlements, burials, increasing integration around the Forum and major urban change in the seventh–sixth centuries BCE. It does not identify either twin. Foundation in one day by one ruler is not an archaeological conclusion.

**[EA?]** Wolf symbolism, sacred fig and local topography may predate the complete narrative, but the surviving evidence cannot demonstrate the order in which these elements combined.

### Circa 300–200 BCE: first secure civic myth

**[EA]** The Ogulnian monument of 296 BCE and early-third-century coinage show the nursing twins as a Roman civic emblem. Fabius Pictor then gives the legend historiographic form in Greek. This period also sees the documented temple of Quirinus and expanding engagement with Greek foundation historiography.

### Second–first centuries BCE: historical and political systematization

**[EA/LS]** Ennius, annalists, Varro and Cicero integrate the myth with constitutional history, ritual and chronology. Competing dates are reconciled into king lists; Romulus becomes an exemplary lawgiver but can also signify tyranny and fratricide.

### Augustan period

**[LC]** Virgil, Livy, Propertius, Ovid and the Fasti make Romulus central to Rome’s providential imperial history. Augustus could be praised as a second founder while avoiding the politically dangerous title “Romulus.” Aeneas embodies piety and ancestry; Romulus embodies city-foundation, conquest and apotheosis. The literature preserves disturbing alternatives—rape, murder and senatorial conspiracy—rather than eliminating them.

### Imperial period

**[LC]** Plutarch and Dionysius expand comparative and constitutional interpretation. Emperors use founder imagery; Hadrian’s coinage and monumental art circulate the wolf and twins. Maxentius later invokes Rome’s founders and names his dead son Romulus, tying dynastic memory to the eternal city.

### Christian late antiquity

**[late-antique reinterpretation]** Christian writers preserve the story while reversing its moral value. Augustine treats Romulus’ murder of Remus as an emblem of the earthly city’s origins in violence. The legend survives because Roman universal history remains indispensable even when its gods are rejected.

### Middle Ages

**[MA]** Chronicles transmit Romulus as a pagan king and founder. The late seventh- or early eighth-century Northumbrian Franks Casket depicts the twins with two wolves and labels them in runes, placing Rome’s origin within a Christian-Germanic universal history ([Franks Casket study](https://ray.yorksj.ac.uk/id/eprint/5955/)). The two-wolf image is a medieval transformation, not the canonical Roman scene.

### Renaissance to nineteenth century

**[MA]** Humanist recovery of Livy and Plutarch restores the founders to civic and artistic prominence. The Capitoline wolf receives Renaissance twins. Rubens paints the discovery of the infants and the Roman-Sabine reconciliation; Johann Adolf Hasse’s *Romolo ed Ersilia* is produced in 1765; Jacques-Louis David’s *Les Sabines* (1799) makes Hersilia’s intervention an image of reconciliation after revolutionary civil conflict.

### Twentieth and twenty-first centuries

**[modern reception]** Archaeology, Fascist *romanità*, mass cinema, television, sport and tourism make the twins ubiquitous. At the same time, source criticism dismantles confident identifications of the Capitoline Wolf and the alleged 2007 Lupercal.

## 7. Modern reception and reinvention

### Visual art, opera, film and television

- **[MA] Peter Paul Rubens, *Romulus and Remus*, approximately 1615–1616:** dramatizes Faustulus’ discovery in a luxuriant baroque landscape; the Capitoline Museums preserve the painting ([Musei Capitolini](https://www.museicapitolini.org/en/percorsi/percorsi_per_sale/pinacoteca_capitolina/sala_di_santa_petronilla_la_grande_pittura_del_seicento_a_roma/romolo_e_remo)).
- **[MA] Rubens/workshop, *Reconciliation of the Romans and Sabines*, approximately 1625:** focuses on women interrupting male war ([Alte Pinakothek](https://www.sammlung.pinakothek.de/en/artwork/wq4jX0qLWo)).
- **[MA] Johann Adolf Hasse, *Romolo ed Ersilia*, 1765:** operatic celebration composed for the wedding of Archduke Leopold.
- **[MA] Jacques-Louis David, *The Intervention of the Sabine Women*, 1799:** Hersilia separates Romulus and Tatius; David conceived it amid post-Revolutionary conflict as reconciliation.
- **[MI] *Romolo e Remo* / *Duel of the Titans*, 1961:** peplum cinema turns the brothers’ conflict into heroic adventure and single combat.
- **[MI] Matteo Rovere, *Il primo re*, 2019:** deliberately violent, naturalistic reinvention using reconstructed archaic Latin; it is not a screen translation of a single ancient source ([RAI](https://www.raiplay.it/programmi/ilprimore)).
- **[MI] *Romulus*, television series, from 2020:** expands the foundation into serial political fantasy in reconstructed archaic language.
- **[MI] *Alien: Romulus*, 2024:** uses the name and sibling/foundational resonance in science fiction; it is reception, not adaptation of the Roman plot.

### Fascism and nationalism

**[documented modern appropriation]** Fascist Italy made *romanità*—a claimed continuity between ancient Rome and Mussolini’s state—a major visual and political language. The she-wolf functioned as an easily reproduced emblem of fertility, martial origin, imperial destiny and the capital. Mussolini’s government distributed replicas abroad, including to American cities bearing classical names. This appropriation did not create the emblem, but it selected and monumentalized it as a symbol of imperial rebirth. The same image later served the 1960 Rome Olympics, AS Roma and municipal branding; use of the wolf today is therefore not automatically Fascist, though specific Fascist contexts and inscriptions remain identifiable.

### Psychological and comparative readings

- **[MSR] Otto Rank, 1909:** treated the exposed royal infant as part of the hero-birth pattern.
- **[MSR] Georges Dumézil:** interpreted Romulus/Quirinus within an Indo-European tripartite ideology; Quirinus represented organized civic productivity or the “third function,” while Jupiter and Mars embodied sovereignty and force. The model was enormously influential but is criticized for selecting parallels and sometimes treating late Roman evidence as inherited Indo-European structure.
- **[MSR] Lord Raglan, 1936:** abstracted heroic biographies into a recurring ritual pattern; Romulus scores highly because exposure, royal descent, return and violent or mysterious death already resemble the template.
- **[MSR] Joseph Campbell, 1949:** the monomyth can accommodate separation and return, but Romulus and Remus do not neatly follow a single “hero’s journey.” Applying Campbell mechanically suppresses the political and ritual specificity of the Roman variants.
- **[MSR] Comparative primordial twins:** scholars compare Remus with sacrificed *Yemo*, Romulus with *Manu*, or both with Indo-European twin founders. Structural resemblance is real evidence for comparison, not proof of direct descent.
- **[MSR] René Girard and later fratricide readings:** foundation through exclusionary violence makes Remus a victim whose death creates civic unity. This is interpretation, not an attested Roman ritual explanation.

### Commercial and scientific naming

**[MI]** The twins name products, schools, sports organizations and astronomical bodies. The moons of asteroid 87 Sylvia were named Romulus and Remus in the twenty-first century. Such naming attests modern recognizability, not survival of cult.

## 8. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Historicity

**[MSR majority position]** No contemporary inscription, securely identified grave, personal object or eighth-century text demonstrates historical individuals named Romulus and Remus. Archaeology supports substantial settlement and political transformation in the conventional foundation horizon, but this is not proof of the biographical legend.

**[MSR historicizing minority]** Andrea Carandini interprets an eighth-century Palatine wall and associated deposits as the *murus Romuli* and argues that the literary foundation preserves a historical core, perhaps even an individual founder. Critics answer that dating a wall near the legendary period does not identify its builder and that using the narrative to name the archaeology risks circular reasoning.

**[MSR cautious reconstruction]** T. J. Cornell accepts that traditions may preserve institutions and memories but treats the canonical life as accumulated legend. Alexandre Grandazzi gives greater weight to the convergence between eighth-century archaeological change and foundation tradition while distinguishing that convergence from proof of the twins.

### When was Remus invented?

**[MSR Wiseman]** In *Remus: A Roman Myth* (1995), T. P. Wiseman argues that Remus emerged in the late fourth or early third century BCE as a plebeian co-founder, reflecting the political incorporation of plebeians. The Ogulnii’s monument in 296 BCE is the first unequivocal evidence; Remus’ name evokes delay, and his death reflects counter-politics or transformed sacrifice ([Wiseman book record](https://books.google.com/books/about/Remus.html?id=7LPNHRUlWacC)).

**Criticism:** Tim Cornell and other reviewers agree that the extant record becomes clear around 300 BCE but object that first surviving evidence is not necessarily invention, that the proposed etymology is speculative and that Remus’ death is represented as murder rather than an unmistakable *devotio*. Comparative twin and fratricide motifs also make a purely event-specific invention unnecessary. The responsible conclusion is: **late-fourth-/early-third-century formation is plausible; precise invention in 296 BCE is unproved.**

### Greek versus Italian formation

**[MSR]** Greek historiography supplied eponymous founders, genealogical competition and exposure-story forms. Italian elements include the Palatine landscape, wolf, fig, Lupercal, augury and Roman boundary ritual. Claims that the myth is simply “copied from Greece” or purely indigenous exceed the mixed evidence.

### Quirinus and Romulus

**[MSR]** Older scholarship often treated Quirinus straightforwardly as Romulus’ deified form. Cult history indicates the reverse chronological problem: Quirinus is an archaic god whose worship predates surviving evidence for the equation. The assimilation probably helped give Romulus a divine afterlife and Quirinus a narrative biography.

### Lapis Niger and founder cult

**[MSR competing readings]**

- Filippo Coarelli reconstructs the Comitium complex as a founder-related *heroon* and links ancient testimony to the Lapis Niger.
- Skeptical archaeologists emphasize that the inscription does not name Romulus, ancient explanations conflict and excavation did not reveal an identifiable royal burial.
- The 2020 tufa chest is compatible with a cenotaph or ritual focus but cannot establish whose cult it served.

### Human sacrifice

**[LC/MSR]** The death of Remus has been interpreted as boundary sacrifice, scapegoating or a transformed historical rite. Roman sources call it killing in anger, punishment or factional violence. No excavated victim or ritual text connects a human sacrifice to Remus. The sacrificial interpretation must remain labeled comparative reconstruction.

### Capitoline Wolf

**[discredited older attribution]** The traditional fifth-century-BCE Etruscan dating remains common in older books and tourist materials. Medieval casting technique and radiocarbon evidence now strongly favor an eleventh–twelfth-century wolf. Identifying it with the Ogulnian wolf is demonstrably untenable.

### The “found Lupercal”

**[disputed modern attribution]** The 2007 claim remains widespread online. Topography, elevation and imperial decorative form weigh against it. The discovered chamber is real; its identification as the twins’ cave is not established.

### Manuscripts and lost evidence

The earliest Roman historians survive only in fragments quoted centuries later. Livy’s first decade survives through medieval manuscripts; Dionysius’ early books survive but much of the complete history does not; Dio’s regal books are fragmentary; Festus survives in a damaged manuscript and Paul’s abridgment; Servius exists in shorter and expanded recensions. Consequently, apparent “first appearances” may reflect survival rather than actual invention.

## 10. Sources and assessment

### Primary evidence in recommended editions

- Livy, *Ab urbe condita* 1 and 10: use the Oxford Classical Text or Teubner Latin; B. O. Foster’s Loeb and modern translations are serviceable.
- Dionysius of Halicarnassus, *Roman Antiquities* 1–2: Jacoby/modern fragment collections should be consulted for his lost Greek authorities; Earnest Cary’s Loeb remains accessible online.
- Plutarch, *Life of Romulus*: use a modern critical Greek text alongside the Loeb translation.
- Virgil, *Aeneid* 1, 6 and 8: Nicholas Horsfall’s commentaries are especially valuable for antiquarian and source problems.
- Ovid, *Fasti* 2–5 and *Metamorphoses* 14: use critical Latin editions; poetic aetiology must not be mistaken for archival ritual history.
- Cicero, *De re publica* 2; Varro, *De lingua Latina*; Festus/Paul; Servius on *Aeneid* 1 and 8; Cassius Dio fragments; Fasti Capitolini and Triumphales.
- Archaeological corpora: the Lapis Niger inscription in *Corpus Inscriptionum Latinarum* I²; Republican coinage in Michael Crawford, *Roman Republican Coinage*.

### Major scholarship

- T. J. Cornell, *The Beginnings of Rome* (1995): critical synthesis of texts and archaeology.
- T. P. Wiseman, *Remus: A Roman Myth* (1995): foundational stratigraphic study of the variants; its precise political reconstruction remains contested.
- Alexandre Grandazzi, *The Foundation of Rome* (English translation, 1997): argues for meaningful convergence between tradition and archaeology without making the canonical biography documentary fact.
- Georges Dumézil, *Archaic Roman Religion*: comparativist analysis of Quirinus and Roman ideological structures.
- Filippo Coarelli, studies of the Forum and Comitium: influential reconstruction of founder monuments.
- Nicholas Horsfall, Virgilian commentaries: essential for literary and antiquarian detail.
- Mary Beard, John North and Simon Price, *Religions of Rome*: cultic context and methodological caution.
- Cristina Mazzoni, *She-Wolf: The Story of a Roman Icon* (2010): reception of the wolf image.
- Brill’s *New Pauly*, entries “Romulus,” “Remus,” “Quirinus,” “Lupercalia” and “Roma”: concise bibliographic orientation, not substitutes for primary evidence.

### Documentary conclusion

The evidence securely establishes that Romans possessed a developed wolf-and-twins foundation story by **296 BCE** and made it a state emblem during the third century BCE. It does not establish that either twin lived in the eighth century BCE. Between Fabius Pictor and the Augustan writers, the tradition became a dense account of divine ancestry, exposure, augury, boundary violence, coerced marriage, political incorporation, monarchy and apotheosis. Its contradictions are not noise: they record successive Roman arguments about who could belong to the city, whether power rested on divine favor or force, whether the founder was liberator or tyrant, and whether Rome’s origin culminated in godhood or senatorial murder.

The cultic record is asymmetrical. Quirinus had real public cult and was eventually identified with Romulus; Remus had no comparable securely documented cult. The Lapis Niger and Palatine landscape preserve genuine archaic sanctuaries, but none bears the twins’ names. The famous Capitoline Wolf is medieval, its infants Renaissance additions; the grotto announced in 2007 is probably an imperial nymphaeum rather than the Lupercal. These corrections do not diminish the legend’s historical importance. They relocate that importance from an unverifiable eighth-century biography to the documented ways Republican, imperial, Christian, medieval, Fascist and contemporary communities repeatedly remade Rome’s founders.
