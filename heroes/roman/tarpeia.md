# Tarpeia: Research Dossier

## Evidentiary key

The following labels distinguish the status of claims throughout:

- **[Early attestation]** securely attested Republican evidence, including fragments reliably attributed to early annalists.
- **[Material evidence]** coin, inscription, relief, or archaeological context.
- **[Canonical elaboration]** late-Republican or early-imperial literary formulation that became standard.
- **[Variant]** an ancient alternative not harmonized with the standard account.
- **[Learned systematization]** antiquarian, etymological, scholiastic, or rationalizing reconstruction.
- **[Cult claim]** ancient testimony for tomb, rite, monument, or worship; not necessarily archaeologically confirmed.
- **[Medieval/early-modern reception]** post-antique reuse before modern scholarship.
- **[Modern reconstruction]** scholarly hypothesis rather than ancient testimony.
- **[Modern invention]** a recent claim lacking ancient evidence.

## Summary identification

**Class:** Tarpeia is principally a **mortal maiden of Roman foundation legend**, not a demigoddess. Fabius Pictor, Cincius Alimentus, Livy, Dionysius, Propertius, and Plutarch place her during Romulus’s war with Titus Tatius and the Sabines. Varro, Propertius, and later writers call her a Vestal or associate her with Vesta; that status is not present in every version and may be a late-Republican elaboration. Piso’s account and reported annual libations potentially make her a **heroized dead or patriotic heroine**, but no excavated shrine or dedicatory inscription verifies a cult. Older modern scholarship’s assertion that she was originally a goddess is reconstruction, not an ancient classification.

**Earliest secure attestation:** **[Early attestation]** The earliest datable literary evidence is embedded in the lost histories of **Quintus Fabius Pictor** and **Lucius Cincius Alimentus**, writing in the late third century BCE, whose disagreement with **Lucius Calpurnius Piso Frugi** is reported by Dionysius of Halicarnassus, *Roman Antiquities* 2.38–40. Because their original texts are lost, the earliest surviving contemporary object that unquestionably depicts the story is the denarius of **L. Titurius L. f. Sabinus, 89 BCE**: Tatius appears on the obverse and Tarpeia, between shield-bearing soldiers, on the reverse. The coin establishes that the shield-death narrative and its Sabine setting were publicly recognizable by 89 BCE. [Dionysius 2.38–40](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/e/roman/texts/dionysius_of_halicarnassus/2b%2A.html); [Museum Victoria coin record](https://collections.museumsvictoria.com.au/items/74265).

No contemporary evidence identifies a historical woman behind the legend. Tarpeia belongs to Rome’s remembered foundation past, conventionally placed in the eighth century BCE but documented only many centuries later.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### Name and forms

- **Latin:** *Tarpeia*, conventionally pronounced with four syllables, feminine of *Tarpeius*. The adjectival forms are *Tarpeius, -a, -um*: *mons Tarpeius*, *saxum Tarpeium*, *rupes Tarpeia*, *arx Tarpeia*, and Virgil’s *Tarpeia sedes*.
- **Greek:** ancient Greek authors transliterate the name, normally printed as **Ταρπηία** or **Ταρπεία** according to author and editorial convention; transliteration: *Tarpēia/Tarpeia*.
- **English:** Tarpeia; older English also uses “Tarpeian maid.”
- **Italian:** Tarpea.
- **French:** Tarpéia or Tarpeia.
- **German:** Tarpeia.

**[Early attestation]** The money of L. Titurius Sabinus does not label the central woman by name; recognition depends on the pictorial narrative and its pairing with Tatius. **[Canonical elaboration]** Livy names the commander *Spurius Tarpeius* and calls the woman *huius filia*—“his daughter”—without supplying her personal name at the beginning of the episode; the traditional name follows from the larger tradition. Dionysius directly calls her Tarpeia.

### Etymology

Three different questions have often been conflated:

1. Did the woman give her name to the hill and rock?
2. Was she invented to explain an older place-name?
3. Was *Tarpeius* originally a family, Sabine, or otherwise non-Latin name?

**[Learned systematization]** Varro, *De lingua Latina* 5.41, says the hill was once called Tarpeian after the Vestal Tarpeia, killed there by Sabine weapons and buried there; the continuing name of the *Tarpeium saxum* preserved her memory. His Latin reads in part: *hic mons ante Tarpeius dictus a virgine Vestale Tarpeia*—“this hill was formerly called Tarpeian from the Vestal virgin Tarpeia.” [Varro’s Latin text](https://latin.packhum.org/loc/684/1/5/1314-1316%2C1366-1368%2C1421-1423%2C1456-1458%2C1473-1475%2C1989-1991%2C2046-2048%2C2336-2338%2C2507-2509%2C2599-2601%2C2622-2624%2C2693-2695%2C2753-2755%2C2816-2818%2C2841-2843%2C2893-2895%2C2923-2925%2C3132-3134%2C3667-3669%2C3846-3848%2C3967-3969%2C4066-4068%2C4144-4146%2C4213-4215%2C4232-4234%2C4276-4278%2C4294-4296%2C4869-4871%2C4921-4923%2C4993-4995%2C5094-5096%2C5571-5573%2C5665-5667%2C5711-5713%2C5928-5930).

**[Variant]** Propertius 4.4.93 instead says the mountain took its cognomen from the commander Tarpeius: *a duce Tarpeio mons est cognomen adeptus*. The poem has nevertheless already called the grave Tarpeia’s at 4.4.1.

**[Modern reconstruction]** Platner and Ashby considered it more likely that *Tarpeius* derived from a *gens Tarpeia* living nearby and that the woman was incorporated into an aetiological legend. This is plausible Roman onomastic reasoning, not an independently attested fact. [Topographical discussion](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Gazetteer/Places/Europe/Italy/Lazio/Roma/Rome/_Texts/PLATOP%2A/Tarpeius_Mons.html).

**[Modern reconstruction]** Giacomo Devoto proposed a Sabine or related Italic form reconstructed as something like *Tarqueia*, linking *tarqu-* with “rock” or “height.” Other scholars have connected Tarpeia with Tarquinius or the Vestal Tarquinia. Neither proposal commands consensus, and the phonetic and historical steps remain disputed.

No ancient author supplies a linguistic etymology of the personal name comparable to modern comparative reconstruction. The secure statement is only that Romans themselves connected the woman, the *mons Tarpeius*, and the *saxum/rupes Tarpeia*.

### Titles and descriptions

Tarpeia has no stable cult epithet. Ancient descriptions include:

- *virgo*—“maiden,” in the narrative tradition.
- *virgo Vestalis* or *Vestalis*—“Vestal virgin,” explicitly in Varro and Propertius and assumed by later treatments.
- *mala puella*—“wicked girl,” Propertius 4.4.17.
- *proditrix*—“betrayer/traitress,” the moral category applied in later summaries rather than a fixed proper epithet.
- “daughter of Tarpeius,” the normal patronymic identification.
- “daughter of Tatius,” in the rejected Antigonus-type variant reported by Plutarch.
- A patriotic deceiver of the enemy in Piso’s version.

**[Modern invention]** “Goddess Tarpeia,” “warrior goddess Tarpeia,” and “Sabine earth goddess” are not securely ancient titles. They summarize particular modern reconstructions based on the alleged tomb, libations, image, and shield mound.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### Earliest recoverable tradition

**Fabius Pictor and Cincius Alimentus, late third century BCE; Latin annalistic tradition preserved in Greek.**  
**[Early attestation]** Dionysius 2.38 attributes to both historians the version in which Tarpeia desired the Sabines’ gold bracelets and rings. They are the earliest securely datable named Roman authorities. Their works survive only in fragments and quotations, so the exact wording and narrative extent cannot be recovered.

**Antigonus, uncertain identification and date.**  
**[Variant]** Plutarch, *Romulus* 17.5, names Antigonus among those who made Tarpeia Tatius’s daughter, held by Romulus under compulsion and acting at her father’s command. If the author is Antigonus of Carystus, the evidence could be third-century BCE; the identification is uncertain, so this cannot safely displace Fabius and Cincius as the earliest secure witnesses.

**Simylus, uncertain Hellenistic date.**  
**[Variant]** Plutarch quotes Greek verses in which Tarpeia betrays Rome not to the Sabines but to the **Boii/Gauls**, desiring marriage to their king. Her shield burial remains. Plutarch calls the version absurd, but his quotation preserves genuine evidence for a different placement of the story in Rome’s Gallic wars. [Plutarch, *Romulus* 17](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Plutarch/Lives/Romulus%2A.html).

### Republican material and literary evidence

**L. Calpurnius Piso Frugi, annals composed in the later second century BCE, probably after his censorship of 120 BCE.**  
**[Variant: patriotic Tarpeia]** Piso said Tarpeia intended to disarm the Sabines, warned Romulus through a messenger, and asked for their shields under the ambiguous formula “what they wore on their left arms.” The messenger defected and exposed her plan. Tatius killed her with the shields while literally satisfying the agreement. Piso also reported a tomb or monument and annual Roman libations. The account survives through Dionysius.

**Denarius of L. Titurius L. f. Sabinus, 89 BCE.**  
**[Material evidence]** Obverse: bearded head of Tatius, marked *SABIN*. Reverse: Tarpeia kneeling or sinking amid shields, arms raised, flanked by two soldiers; star within crescent above; *L. TITVRI* below. The image does not disclose whether she is traitor or patriotic agent. Its pairing with Tatius and its issue during the Social War make Sabine/Roman integration politically relevant, but the precise message remains debated. [Metropolitan Museum record](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/256794); [Walters Art Museum record](https://art.thewalters.org/detail/1053/denarius-of-lucius-titurius-sabinus/).

**Varro, *De lingua Latina* 5.41, composed in the mid-first century BCE.**  
**[Learned systematization]** Tarpeia is a Vestal, killed and buried on the hill, whose earlier name and surviving rock commemorate her. Varro gives no motive and therefore does not explicitly call her either traitor or heroine.

### Augustan corpus

**Livy, *Ab urbe condita* 1.11, written late first century BCE.**  
**[Canonical elaboration]** Spurius Tarpeius commands the Roman citadel. Tatius corrupts his unmarried daughter with gold when she leaves the walls to fetch water for rites. After admitting the Sabines, she is crushed beneath their arms. Livy offers three explanations:

1. the death disguised the citadel’s capture as force;
2. it warned that no promise need be kept to a traitor;
3. the Sabines’ bracelets inspired the formula “what they wore on their left hands,” but they gave her shields;
4. alternatively, she deliberately demanded the shields to disarm them and was killed when her trick was detected.

Livy neither names Piso nor endorses one explanation without residue. His phrase *additur fabula* marks the bracelet story as an added tale, and recent textual study argues that the episode deliberately exposes the uncertainty of foundation legend. [Livy’s Latin, 1.11](https://latin.packhum.org/loc/914/1/11/338-342%2C1068-1072); [study of Livy’s fictional marking](https://epub.ub.uni-muenchen.de/59562/).

**Virgil, *Aeneid* 8.347 and 8.652–654, composed 20s BCE.**  
**[Canonical topography, not a narrative of Tarpeia]** Evander leads Aeneas to the *Tarpeia sedes*; later the shield of Aeneas shows the *Tarpeiae … arcis*. Virgil uses Tarpeia adjectivally to locate the primitive Capitoline and does not retell her betrayal. Servius’s late-antique commentary supplies antiquarian explanation. [Aeneid 8.347–358](https://latintutorial.com/read/vergil/aeneid/book-8/347-358).

**Propertius, *Elegies* 4.4, approximately 16 BCE.**  
**[Canonical elaboration: erotic variant]** This is the fullest poetic treatment. Tarpeia is a Vestal who fetches ritual water from a spring in a grove of Silvanus. She sees Tatius and falls in love with his body, royal armor, and blond crest. She fantasizes about marriage, Sabine motherhood, and peace; neglects or betrays Vesta’s fire; arranges the opening of the citadel; and is crushed beneath shields. Tatius condemns her as unfit to be queen because she betrayed her city. Propertius superimposes Roman love elegy upon national foundation narrative and makes erotic desire, not merely greed, the mechanism of civic treason. [Latin text](https://la.wikisource.org/wiki/Elegiae_%28Propertius%29/Liber_IV); [English translation](https://www.poetryintranslation.com/PITBR/Latin/PropertiusBkFour.php).

**Ovid, *Fasti* 1.259–262, begun under Augustus and revised in exile.**  
**[Canonical allusion]** In Janus’s description of the Sabine assault, “Saturnia”—Juno—opened a gate after Tarpeia had given the armed Sabines access. Ovid compresses Tarpeia into an exemplum of bribery, “having received bracelets,” while shifting the decisive divine opening of Rome to Juno. [*Fasti* Book 1](https://www.theoi.com/Text/OvidFasti1.html).

**Ovid, *Metamorphoses* 14.775–804, completed about 8 CE.**  
**[Canonical allusion with divine displacement]** Tarpeia’s betrayal is mentioned briefly, but Juno removes the gate’s bars and Venus and the Naiads generate a hot sulphurous flood to impede the Sabines. Tarpeia is thus one human element in a divine contest, not the episode’s psychological center.

**Denarius of P. Petronius Turpilianus, conventionally dated 19–18 BCE.**  
**[Material evidence]** The obverse bears Augustus; the reverse reproduces Tarpeia half buried by shields with raised arms. The British Museum notes that the type derives from Titurius Sabinus’s Republican issue. Its meaning may concern punishment, foundation memory, Augustan moral discourse, or the reappropriation of Republican imagery; the coin itself supplies no explanatory legend. [British Museum record](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/C_R-6023).

**Basilica Aemilia frieze, date disputed, commonly placed in a late-Republican or Augustan rebuilding.**  
**[Material evidence]** A marble relief shows Tarpeia frontally between two men who raise shields over her; shields or stone-like forms already cover her lower body. It belonged to a longer cycle of Rome’s legendary beginnings. The find is often interpreted within Augustan moral and marriage ideology, but both the date of the surviving architectural phase and the scene’s specific message are contested. The identification of the Tarpeia scene itself is secure. Original fragments are held in the Antiquarium Forense; a reconstructed copy has been displayed at the basilica. [Object record](https://lupa.at/33188); [iconographic study](https://www.nm.cz/en/periodicals/nl/71-1-2/k-zobrazeni-tarpeie-na-rimskych-mincich-image-of-tarpeia-on-roman-coins).

### Imperial and late-antique corpus

**Dionysius of Halicarnassus, *Roman Antiquities* 2.38–40, published 7 BCE.**  
**[Learned systematization]** Dionysius preserves the most valuable source-critical dossier, explicitly separating Fabius and Cincius’s venal Tarpeia from Piso’s patriotic Tarpeia. He favors Piso because of the reported monument, burial, and annual libations but ends by leaving judgment to the reader.

**Valerius Maximus, *Memorable Deeds and Sayings* 9.6.1, reign of Tiberius.**  
**[Canonical moral exemplum]** Tarpeia appears among examples of treachery or perfidy: greed obtains a fatal reward. The story is reduced to moral instruction.

**Florus, *Epitome* 1.1, probably second century CE.**  
**[Canonical epitome]** Tarpeia’s treason enables the Sabines to enter the fortress, and the shields become her reward.

**Plutarch, *Romulus* 17–18, late first or early second century CE.**  
**[Learned systematization]** Plutarch tells the greedy-bracelet version, explicitly denies that Tarpeia commanded the fortress, and makes her daughter of the commander Tarpeius. Tatius initiates the shield burial because he needs but despises a traitor. Plutarch then catalogs the Antigonus and Simylus variants. He says Tarpeia was buried there and the hill bore her name until a Tarquin consecrated it to Jupiter; her bones were removed, while the cliff retained her name as the place from which criminals were hurled.

**Cassius Dio, early third century CE, Book 1 fragment preserved through Byzantine excerptors.**  
**[Variant]** Tarpeia goes down for water, is seized and brought before Tatius, and is induced to betray the fortress. This makes capture and coercion more explicit than the standard voluntary negotiation. Because early Dio survives through epitomes and excerpts, its wording and completeness are uncertain. [Dio, Book 1 fragments](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/e/roman/texts/cassius_dio/1%2A.html).

**Festus/Paulus, ultimately drawing on Verrius Flaccus, imperial to Carolingian textual transmission.**  
**[Cult claim; fragmentary evidence]** A difficult notice has been read as referring to an image or statue of Tarpeia in connection with Jupiter Stator. Because the text is epitomized and damaged, it cannot securely prove either a formal temple cult or the image’s date and character.

**Servius, commentary on *Aeneid* 8, fourth–fifth century CE.**  
**[Late-antique learned systematization]** Servius explains Virgil’s Tarpeian topography with the inherited betrayal narrative and the hill’s change of name. The commentary is evidence for the story’s late-antique school transmission, not an independent account of regal Rome.

### Negative corpus findings

- **Fasti Capitolini:** **[Absence of evidence]** They record magistrates and triumphs, not Tarpeia; they provide no independent confirmation.
- **Lapis Niger/Forum cippus:** **[Material evidence; negative finding]** This late-sixth-century BCE inscription contains an archaic reference to a *rex* but does not name Tarpeia, Tatius, or Tarpeius. It demonstrates regal-period institutions and literacy, not the Tarpeia story. [Epigraphic overview](https://catalogimages.wiley.com/images/db/pdf/9781405198196.excerpt.pdf).
- **Lupercal:** **[Negative finding]** Its archaeology and literary traditions concern Romulus, Remus, the wolf, and the Lupercalia. No excavated dedication connects it to Tarpeia.
- **Lavinium heroon:** **[Negative finding]** The mound conventionally associated with Aeneas belongs to a different cultic and legendary complex. Tarpeia has no documented interaction with Aeneas at Lavinium.
- No archaic vase, tablet, funerary inscription, or securely identified eighth-to-fourth-century BCE monument names Tarpeia.

## 3. The story and its variants

### Parentage and position

**[Early attestation]** Fabius and Cincius apparently made Tarpeia the daughter of the Roman commander of the citadel; Dionysius does not preserve his personal name in the quoted summary.

**[Canonical elaboration]** Livy names her father **Spurius Tarpeius**, commander of the Roman *arx*. Plutarch stresses that Tarpeius, not Tarpeia, held command.

**[Variant]** Antigonus’s version makes her **Tatius’s daughter**, detained by Romulus, so that opening the fortress is filial and Sabine rather than treasonous.

No ancient source supplies a mother, sibling, spouse, or child as an established fact.

### Vestal status

**[Later canonical elaboration]** Varro and Propertius explicitly make her a Vestal; Livy’s statement that she left the walls for water needed for rites permits but does not require that identification. Dionysius calls her a maiden and does not emphasize a Vestal office.

**[Modern reconstruction]** Scholars disagree whether Vestal status was original. Arguments for lateness note that the earliest recoverable summaries concern greed and the commander’s daughter, while the Vestal identity intensifies sexual and civic transgression in late-Republican writing. Arguments for antiquity point to water gathering, ritual topography, and Varro’s antiquarian claim. Neither position can be demonstrated from archaic evidence.

### The departure for water

- **Livy:** she happened to go outside the walls to fetch water for sacred rites.
- **Dio fragment:** she went down for water, was seized, and brought to Tatius.
- **Propertius:** she repeatedly draws Vesta’s water from a grove and spring associated with Silvanus, using ritual service as the setting for erotic vision.

**[Development]** A brief narrative convenience becomes, in Propertius, a charged opposition among water, Vestal fire, sexuality, and enemy territory.

### Motives

#### Greed for gold

**[Early attestation]** Fabius and Cincius said she coveted Sabine bracelets and rings. Livy calls the bracelets massive and the rings conspicuously jeweled. Plutarch makes this the principal account. Ovid’s *Fasti* compresses it to bribery by bracelets.

#### Patriotic deception

**[Variant: Piso]** Tarpeia intended to demand the Sabines’ shields, deprive them of defensive equipment, and deliver them to Romulus. Her messenger defected to Tatius. The shield killing was Tatius’s counter-deception.

Livy preserves a shorter version without the messenger: some said she straightforwardly demanded the arms to be surrendered and was killed when detected. This is not identical in detail to Dionysius’s Pisonian account but belongs to the same exculpatory tradition.

#### Sexual or marital desire

**[Hellenistic/Augustan variant]** Simylus made her desire marriage to a Gallic king. Propertius made her fall in love with Tatius. She imagines herself his wife, imagines Sabine-Roman union, and even offers betrayal as the dowry of her marriage. Propertius does not acquit her; he relocates the destructive appetite from gold to erotic desire.

#### Filial obedience and liberation

**[Variant]** Antigonus’s Tarpeia is Tatius’s daughter held by Romulus. She acts for her father, eliminating betrayal of her own community.

#### Coercion

**[Variant]** Dio’s fragment says she was seized while fetching water and induced to betray the fortress. It does not preserve enough detail to decide whether inducement meant bribery, pressure, or persuasion.

### The bargain

The recurrent formula is “what they wear on their left arms/hands.”

- In the greedy version, Tarpeia means gold bracelets.
- The Sabines exploit the wording by giving her their shields as well.
- In Piso, Tarpeia herself exploits the wording, intending “shields,” but Tatius kills her with those same shields.
- Propertius turns the compact into an erotic and quasi-marital *pactum*.
- In Antigonus, the preserved notice does not make greed central.

The tale is therefore not one stable moral anecdote but a contest over ambiguous language: who understands the bargain, who controls its literal fulfillment, and whether Tarpeia is deceiver, deceived, or both.

### Opening the fortress

**[Common core]** Tarpeia opens or delivers an access point to Tatius and the Sabines. Livy, Dionysius, Propertius, and Plutarch agree that the citadel falls through internal access rather than frontal assault.

**[Ovidian displacement]** Ovid adds or emphasizes Juno’s divine opening of a barred gate. This does not simply erase Tarpeia; it places human treason within the pro-Sabine action of a goddess.

### Death

**[Common core by late Republic]** Tatius and the Sabines throw shields upon Tarpeia, crushing, burying, or suffocating her.

Ancient explanations differ:

- punishment of a despised traitor;
- literal satisfaction of the ambiguous bargain;
- anger at the excessive price;
- concealment of treachery so the citadel appears taken by force;
- neutralization of a Roman counter-agent;
- in Simylus, the same shield death transferred to the Gallic/Boiian setting.

**[Modern invention]** Popular summaries sometimes say Tarpeia herself was thrown from the Tarpeian Rock. The principal ancient accounts instead say she died under shields at or on the hill. The later cliff executions and her burial/name were joined into a moral geography, but Livy does not say the Sabines threw her from the cliff.

### Body, burial, and removal

- **Dionysius/Piso:** she lay buried where she fell, with an honored monument and annual libations.
- **Varro:** she was killed and buried on the hill.
- **Propertius:** begins with her *turpe sepulcrum*, “shameful tomb.”
- **Plutarch:** she was buried there; when a Tarquin dedicated the site to Jupiter, her bones were removed and the hill’s Tarpeian name disappeared, except for the rock.
- The destination of the removed bones is never given.

This contradiction—honored tomb versus shameful grave—is central evidence for Tarpeia’s polyvalence, not an error to be harmonized.

### Historical function

**[Modern reconstruction]** The tale has been interpreted as:

- an aetiology for the name of the Tarpeian Hill or Rock;
- an aetiology for execution of traitors from the rock;
- a warning about the contempt due to traitors;
- a narrative of Roman-Sabine incorporation;
- a gendered warning about uncontrolled female appetite;
- a tale of the instability of contracts and literal language;
- a reflection of a ritual heap of captured arms;
- an adaptation of Mediterranean “maiden betrays city for enemy lover” stories.

These functions are compatible as later uses but cannot all be established as the legend’s origin.

## 4. Relations with gods and other heroes

### Genealogical variants

| Source and date | Father | Civic identity | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fabius/Cincius, late 3rd c. BCE, through Dionysius | Roman commander, unnamed in the fragment | Roman maiden | Betrays for gold |
| Livy 1.11, late 1st c. BCE | Spurius Tarpeius | Roman maiden; ritual water-bearer | Admits Sabines, crushed |
| Propertius 4.4, c. 16 BCE | Tarpeius implied | Roman Vestal | Loves Tatius, betrays Rome |
| Antigonus, uncertain Hellenistic date, through Plutarch | Titus Tatius | Sabine princess detained by Romulus | Acts for father |
| Plutarch, c. 100 CE | Tarpeius | Roman maiden | Greedy traitor |
| Dio fragment, early 3rd c. CE | Not preserved | Roman water-fetcher | Captured and induced |

No securely attested consort or child exists. Propertius gives Tarpeia imagined marriage and imagined Sabine children, not an accomplished union.

### Every documented interaction with a named god or hero

- **Titus Tatius** — Tarpeia bargains with him for access to the citadel; in the greedy version she desires his soldiers’ gold, in Piso she tries to take their shields, in Propertius she loves Tatius personally, and Tatius begins her execution. **Sources:** Fabius/Cincius and Piso through Dionysius 2.38–40; Livy 1.11; Propertius 4.4; Plutarch, *Romulus* 17. **Tier:** early attestation with conflicting Republican and Augustan elaborations.

- **Titus Tatius** — In Antigonus’s alternative genealogy, he is Tarpeia’s father and directs or benefits from her action against Romulus. **Source:** Antigonus, quoted by Plutarch, *Romulus* 17.5. **Tier:** Hellenistic variant of uncertain date.

- **Romulus** — In Piso’s version Tarpeia sends a messenger warning Romulus that she plans to disarm the Sabines; the messenger defects. **Source:** Piso through Dionysius 2.39. **Tier:** Republican patriotic variant.

- **Romulus** — In the Antigonus variant, Romulus holds Tatius’s daughter Tarpeia under compulsion. **Source:** Plutarch, *Romulus* 17.5. **Tier:** Hellenistic variant.

- **Romulus** — Plutarch reports that Tarpeia’s father Tarpeius was later convicted of treason when prosecuted by Romulus; Tarpeia herself does not directly meet Romulus in this version. **Source:** Sulpicius Galba through Juba, quoted by Plutarch 17.5. **Tier:** learned variant; indirect relationship only.

- **Vesta** — Tarpeia serves or is associated with Vesta as a Vestal and fetches water for the goddess; her betrayal violates Vesta’s fire and office. **Sources:** Varro, *De lingua Latina* 5.41; Propertius 4.4.15–18 and following. **Tier:** late-Republican/Augustan elaboration.

- **Silvanus** — Tarpeia draws ritual water in a grove described as Silvanus’s “branching house.” No dialogue or cult partnership is stated. **Source:** Propertius 4.4.3–16. **Tier:** Augustan poetic setting.

- **Jupiter** — Tarpeia’s action enables capture of Jupiter’s ancient threshold; later, according to Plutarch, dedication of the hill to Jupiter causes removal of her bones and eclipse of her place-name. **Sources:** Propertius 4.4.1–2; Plutarch, *Romulus* 18. **Tier:** Augustan poetic interaction and imperial antiquarian systematization.

- **Jupiter Stator** — A fragmentary Festus tradition has been interpreted as locating an image or memorial of Tarpeia in or near his temple. No narrative encounter between Tarpeia and the god is recorded. **Source:** Festus/Paulus, conventionally cited from the damaged entry around p. 496 Lindsay. **Tier:** uncertain cult/topographical testimony.

- **Juno/Saturnia** — After Tarpeia has admitted the Sabines, Juno opens or unbars the gate in support of the Sabine assault. The two act sequentially rather than meeting. **Sources:** Ovid, *Fasti* 1.259–276; compare *Metamorphoses* 14.775–804. **Tier:** Augustan divine elaboration.

- **Venus** — Venus counters the Sabine advance after the gate episode by enlisting local nymphs to flood the way with heated water. Tarpeia and Venus do not meet. **Source:** Ovid, *Metamorphoses* 14.775–804. **Tier:** Augustan divine elaboration; indirect interaction.

- **Aeneas** — No documented interaction. Virgil’s *Tarpeia sedes* is a place through which Evander guides Aeneas, chronologically before Tarpeia’s supposed lifetime. **Source:** *Aeneid* 8.347. **Tier:** topographical allusion, not a pairing.

- **Evander** — No personal interaction; he points out the later Tarpeian site to Aeneas. **Source:** *Aeneid* 8.347. **Tier:** topographical anachronism in prophetic Roman geography.

- **Hersilia** — No interaction is stated. Hersilia and the abducted Sabine women end the war after Tarpeia’s death. **Sources:** Livy 1.11–13; Plutarch, *Romulus* 18–19. **Tier:** adjacent narrative only.

- **Mettius Curtius** — No personal interaction. His battle and plunge into the marsh follow the capture of the fortress. **Source:** Dionysius 2.41–42. **Tier:** adjacent narrative only.

No ancient source pairs Tarpeia with Mars, Remus, Hercules, Aeneas at Lavinium, or the Lupercal twins in a direct action.

## 5. Cult, tomb, rock, and ritual

### The alleged tomb and annual rite

**[Cult claim]** Piso, quoted by Dionysius 2.40.3, states that Tarpeia had a monument where she fell and that Romans annually performed *choai*, liquid offerings, for her. Dionysius considers these honors evidence against the traitor version: neither Romans nor Sabines, he argues, would honor a woman who sold her country.

This is explicit ancient testimony, but several cautions are necessary:

- It reaches us through Dionysius, not Piso’s original text.
- Dionysius’s argument may reflect Piso’s apologetic construction.
- No inscription naming Tarpeia’s tomb has been found.
- No archaeological structure has been securely identified as her tomb.
- Annual libations to a dead person need not imply that she was a goddess; they are compatible with funerary commemoration or hero-like honors.

Jaclyn Neel’s modern re-evaluation argues that Piso’s evidence should not simply be dismissed and that such libations fit Roman practices at ancestral tombs. Her reconstruction of the rite’s form and location remains explicitly speculative. [Neel, “The Tomb of Tarpeia”](https://www.peren-revues.fr/eugesta/1560?lang=en).

### Location disputes

- **Dionysius/Piso:** monument and burial where she fell, on the city’s holiest hill.
- **Varro:** burial on the earlier *mons Tarpeius*.
- **Propertius:** a “shameful tomb” within his poetic Tarpeian landscape.
- **Plutarch:** burial at the future Capitoline sanctuary; bones removed for Jupiter’s dedication.
- **Festus:** fragmentary evidence may imply another commemorative location associated with Jupiter Stator.
- **Modern topographers:** differ over the southwestern Capitol, the northern *arx*, and whether ancient writers themselves conflated distinct locations.

The physical “Tarpeian Rock” cannot be assigned a single universally accepted ancient edge solely from the texts. The traditional identification places it on the southwestern side of the Capitoline above the *vicus Iugarius* and modern Piazza della Consolazione.

### Executions

**[Historically attested institution, not Tarpeia’s biography]** Republican sources associate the *saxum/rupes Tarpeia* with execution by precipitation, especially for treason or offenses against the state. The earliest case narrated at length is Marcus Manlius Capitolinus in Livy 6.20, conventionally dated to 384 BCE, though the account itself is much later.

Tarpeia’s own principal death tradition is crushing by shields. The association between her alleged treason and the cliff’s penal use is an aetiological or moral linkage, not evidence that she was its first executed victim.

### Image or statue

**[Cult claim; insecure]** The fragment attributed to Festus concerning a representation of Tarpeia and Jupiter Stator has sometimes been treated as evidence of an honored image. The damaged textual transmission does not establish its form, date, or whether it was an object of worship.

### Archaeological verdict

- Tomb: unlocated.
- Shrine or *heroon*: unexcavated and unverified.
- Cult inscription: none known.
- Annual rite: literary testimony only.
- Republican coin: securely attested.
- Augustan coin: securely attested.
- Basilica Aemilia relief: securely identified.
- Capitoline Iron Age burials: archaeologically real, but none can be assigned to Tarpeia.

Thus Tarpeia may have received funerary or hero-like commemoration, but “cult of the goddess Tarpeia” exceeds the evidence.

## 6. Historical evolution of the figure

### Possible preliterary formation

**[Modern reconstruction]** The place-name and story may predate surviving literature, but no evidence fixes their order. Possible mechanisms include oral narrative, performance, family traditions of the Tarpeii or Sabine-descended families, and aetiology attached to the Capitoline. Wiseman’s wider work on Roman foundation myth argues that much apparently archaic narrative was shaped through middle-Republican performance and political debate. Applied specifically to Tarpeia, this remains a method and hypothesis, not a recovered original text. [Review of Wiseman’s *Remus*](https://bmcr.brynmawr.edu/1997/1997.05.18/).

### Late third to second centuries BCE: competing annalistic histories

**[Early attestation]** Fabius and Cincius establish the venal version. Piso supplies a counter-history in which Tarpeia is a patriotic agent. Antigonus and Simylus, if correctly dated, show that Greek-language versions could alter her nationality, parentage, enemy, and motive.

There never was a single uncontested Republican Tarpeia.

### 89 BCE: public visual canon

**[Material evidence]** Titurius’s coin makes the shield burial visually canonical. Its image leaves motive unresolved, allowing traitor and heroine interpretations alike.

### First century BCE: antiquarian and moral localization

Varro makes Tarpeia a Vestal and an eponym of the hill. Livy turns the episode into a conspicuously unstable moral exemplum, presenting incompatible rationales. Propertius eroticizes her and converts Roman topography into elegiac tragedy. Virgil makes “Tarpeian” part of Rome’s deep landscape.

### Augustan and early imperial Rome

The Turpilianus coin and Basilica Aemilia relief place Tarpeia within visual programs of foundation history. Ovid adds divine agency. Valerius Maximus narrows her into a cautionary exemplum. These are not simple repetitions: the Augustan period simultaneously monumentalizes, eroticizes, moralizes, and destabilizes her.

### High Empire

Plutarch preserves and judges variants; Florus epitomizes; Dio adds capture and inducement. The rock becomes more culturally prominent as a symbol of sudden fall, state punishment, and the short distance between triumph and ruin.

### Late Antiquity

Servius and other commentators transmit the place-name and story as school antiquarianism. The legend is euhemerized as early Roman history and used to explain Virgilian language. There is no secure evidence that a living Tarpeia rite continued into late antiquity.

An inscription of 259 CE referring to the *mons Tarpeius* and *Dea Virgo Caelestis* shows survival of the hill-name, not worship of Tarpeia herself. Identifying the goddess with Tarpeia would be unsupported syncretism.

### Middle Ages and Renaissance

**[Medieval/early-modern reception]** Tarpeia survived through Livy, Ovid, Plutarch, Valerius Maximus, and Virgilian commentary. Moralizing readers treated her as the exemplary female traitor destroyed by avarice. Humanists recovering Roman topography used her to locate and narrate the Capitol.

Petrarch’s imagined walk through Rome calls her the deceitful maiden buried beneath arms and caught by her own fraud. This is literary topographical reception, not evidence for medieval folk cult.

### Eighteenth and nineteenth centuries

Tarpeia entered neoclassical drama and music. Christoph Kuffner’s tragedy *Tarpeia* received incidental music including Beethoven’s **triumphal march, WoO 2a**, associated with a production in 1813. The dramatic tradition could recast her as lover, patriot, or tragic victim, drawing particularly on Propertius and Piso. [Beethoven work history](https://www.lvbeethoven.it/woo002a/).

Nineteenth-century historians increasingly treated the story as an aetiological myth rather than factual regal history.

### Twentieth and twenty-first centuries

Tarpeia has become a case study in:

- Roman myth-making;
- unstable exemplarity;
- female desire and civic boundaries;
- Roman-Sabine ethnic incorporation;
- the semantics of oath and bargain;
- the political use of coin imagery;
- tomb and hero-cult methodology;
- the relationship between monuments and literary narrative.

Tara S. Welch’s 2015 monograph is the first book-length modern synthesis centered on the whole tradition. [Publisher’s description](https://ohiostatepress.org/books/titles/9780814212813.html).

## 7. Modern reception and reinvention

### Literature and historical fiction

**[Modern reception]** Propertius’s lover-traitor and Piso’s failed patriot provide the principal templates for modern retellings. Historical novels generally choose between greed, love, coercion, and secret patriotism, often presenting one as the hidden truth. That narrative choice is modern; the ancient dossier never resolves the variants.

The recurring proverb-like image of the Capitol beside the Tarpeian Rock—success close to catastrophic disgrace—preserves the place more strongly than the woman’s individual characterization.

### Drama and music

**[Modern reception]** Kuffner’s *Tarpeia* and Beethoven’s 1813 incidental march are the clearest substantial musical-theatrical afterlife. The music does not independently transmit an ancient variant; it belongs to neoclassical historical drama.

No comparably canonical opera centered on Tarpeia has entered the standard international repertory.

### Visual reception

Ancient visual formulations—the kneeling woman, raised hands, two armed men, accumulating shields—have dominated modern illustrations. Reconstructions frequently copy Titurius’s denarius or the Basilica Aemilia relief.

A recurrent modern image of Tarpeia falling from the cliff conflates her shield death with later executions. It is an intelligible reception motif but not the principal ancient account.

### Political use

**[Ancient political use]** Titurius’s Social War-era coin and the Augustan reissue place Tarpeia in periods when Roman identity, Italian integration, and the moral language of loyalty were politically charged. The exact partisan messages cannot be recovered from the types alone.

**[Modern reception; negative finding]** Romanità, nationalism, and Fascism extensively appropriated Rome’s founders, the Capitol, the wolf, the fasces, and imperial monuments. A dedicated search does not establish Tarpeia herself as a major or systematic Fascist symbol. Claims that she was a central Fascist icon should therefore be rejected unless tied to a specific documented work, speech, or monument.

Modern political language occasionally applies “Tarpeia” to a traitorous woman, but such usage is rhetorical inheritance rather than a continuous cult or ideological program.

### Psychological and structuralist readings

- **[Modern reconstruction]** Psychoanalytic and feminist readings emphasize repressed female desire, the gender anomaly of the Vestal, penetration of the city, water vessels and sexuality, or Tarpeia’s collapse of civic and erotic boundaries.
- **[Modern reconstruction]** Georges Dumézil’s *Tarpeia* (1947) placed the story within comparative Indo-European functional structures, contrasting warrior force and wealth. The method was influential but depends on cross-cultural structural correspondences rather than new evidence about Tarpeia.
- **[Modern reconstruction]** Salomon Reinach interpreted the shield burial as myth generated from a rite involving consecrated heaps of captured weapons; Tarpeia became the local spirit or heroine beneath the arms. No excavation confirms such a Tarpeian weapons rite.
- **[Modern reconstruction]** The Rank–Raglan hero pattern and Campbellian monomyth fit Tarpeia poorly: she lacks divine birth, heroic quest, return, kingship, and apotheosis. Applying the monomyth would obscure the distinctive aetiological and civic character of her record.
- **[Modern reconstruction]** Structural parallels with Scylla, Comaetho, Pisidice, and other city-betraying maidens illuminate a widespread narrative type but do not prove borrowing in any particular direction.

### Documented modern inventions

The following widely repeated claims are unsupported or misleading:

- Tarpeia was certainly a historical Vestal of the eighth century BCE.
- She was thrown from the rock by the Sabines.
- Archaeologists have discovered her tomb.
- The Lapis Niger names Tarpeia.
- The Lavinium heroon belonged to her.
- She was demonstrably an Etruscan, Sabine, lunar, earth, or warrior goddess.
- The crescent and star on Titurius’s coin prove her astral divinity.
- Piso’s account reveals what “really happened.”
- Tarpeia was a central icon of Italian Fascism.
- The phrase *arx Tarpeia Capitoli proxima* is itself a securely attested ancient proverb in that exact standard form; its sentiment is ancient, but the familiar formulation has a complex later reception.

## 8. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Historicity

No contemporary regal-period evidence verifies Tarpeia. The Roman-Sabine fusion reflected in the foundation narrative may preserve cultural memory of real interaction between communities, but mapping a named woman and episode onto an eighth-century event is impossible.

Cornell’s archaeological-history method permits early Roman traditions to be examined alongside material development without converting legend into eyewitness history. Tarpeia’s story can therefore be historically informative about later Roman memory even when it cannot prove an event in Romulus’s reign. [Cornell, *The Beginnings of Rome*](https://books.google.com/books/about/The_Beginnings_of_Rome.html?id=UgoOD2XDpzIC).

### Original traitor or original heroine?

The debate cannot be decided simply by counting surviving sources.

- Fabius and Cincius favor greed.
- Piso favors patriotic deception.
- The 89 BCE coin depicts punishment but not motive.
- Varro omits motive while calling her a Vestal and buried eponym.
- Livy presents both.
- Dionysius argues that cult honors support Piso.
- Propertius makes her guilty through love.
- Plutarch rejects exculpatory and alternative ethnic versions.
- Tomb and rite traditions resist complete reduction to disgrace.

Welch argues that Livy’s presentation does not allow a single secure moral conclusion and that visual perspective matters: the same figure can be victim, traitor, enemy, Roman, or Sabine depending on the observer. [Welch, “Perspectives On and Of Livy’s Tarpeia”](https://doi.org/10.54563/eugesta.1069).

### The cult question

Older scholars often reasoned:

1. a tomb and rite existed;
2. therefore Tarpeia was originally a local divinity;
3. the betrayal story later degraded her into a mortal.

Step 1 rests on ancient literary testimony; steps 2–3 are modern reconstruction. Neel’s recent work makes a more limited argument: funerary honors reported by Piso are plausible in Roman practice and need not be discarded merely because most Latin narratives make Tarpeia a traitor. This does not demonstrate goddesshood.

### Ritual-origin theories

Salomon Reinach argued that a tabooed heap of spoils generated the story of a local heroine suffocated beneath weapons. The theory elegantly explains shield burial and cult honors but lacks direct Tarpeian ritual evidence. It belongs to the early twentieth-century “ritual precedes myth” school and should not be reported as fact. [Reinach’s treatment](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/a/aa/Cults%2C_myths_and_religions_%28IA_cultsmythsreligi00reinrich%29.pdf).

### Dumézil and comparative mythology

Dumézil’s 1947 study read Tarpeia through the interaction of Indo-European social functions—particularly warrior force and wealth/fertility. Its strength is structural comparison; its weakness is that similarities among myths can be selected and arranged without establishing direct historical transmission. Later criticism of Dumézil’s broader trifunctional method counsels against treating the reconstruction as an archaic Roman doctrine.

### Etymology and eponymy

The ancient claim “Tarpeia gave her name to the hill” may itself be an aetiology. Conversely, deriving Tarpeia from a place-name or *gens* does not prove the whole narrative was invented at one moment. Personal, family, and topographical names could reinforce one another over centuries.

### Iconographic meaning

The Titurius denarius, Turpilianus denarius, and Basilica Aemilia relief securely represent the shield death. Their moral valence remains unsettled:

- punishment of treachery;
- martyrdom of Piso’s patriot;
- Sabine contribution to Roman identity;
- warning about female transgression;
- exemplum of ambiguous oaths;
- dynastic or moneyer wordplay;
- Augustan foundation ideology.

The images omit the bracelets and bargain, so texts should not automatically dictate what an ancient viewer saw.

### Basilica Aemilia chronology

The frieze has often been dated to an Augustan rebuilding and read beside Augustan moral legislation. Architectural reassessment has allowed lower or alternative dates for portions of the basilica. The relief’s stylistic and programmatic dating therefore remains a scholarly problem; “Augustan propaganda” is an interpretation, not an excavated label.

### Tarpeia as Vestal

Jaclyn Neel argues that scholarship has overprivileged Propertius’s Vestal in reconstructing the myth. Vestal status is securely ancient by Varro and Propertius, but it cannot automatically be projected into Fabius Pictor’s version. [Neel, “Tarpeia the Vestal”](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-roman-studies/article/abs/tarpeia-the-vestal/43019F211963F69A8F7009E4608AD4EA).

### State of the evidence

What survives:

- later quotations of early annalists;
- complete or substantially complete Augustan narratives;
- Hellenistic poetic fragments quoted by Plutarch;
- two securely catalogued coin types;
- a fragmentary monumental relief;
- topographical names;
- literary claims for tomb and rite.

What is lost or uncertain:

- the original texts of Fabius, Cincius, Piso, Antigonus, Simylus, and Sulpicius Galba;
- any archaic narrative;
- the tomb’s location;
- the alleged cult image’s form;
- the date and program of all phases of the Basilica Aemilia frieze;
- whether the place-name preceded the woman;
- whether any historical event underlies the tale.
