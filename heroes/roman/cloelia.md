# Cloelia: Research Dossier

> **Traditional class:** mortal Roman heroine and civic *exemplum* of *virtus*; not a demigod and not securely a recipient of hero cult.  
> **Earliest secure attestation:** the lost *Annales* of Lucius Calpurnius Piso Frugi, written in the later second century BCE, preserved indirectly by Pliny, *Natural History* 34.29. Piso said that Cloelia’s fellow hostages erected her statue after Porsenna released them. No contemporary sixth- or fifth-century BCE inscription, image, tomb, or cult record identifies Cloelia. The often-assigned narrative date, 509/508 BCE, belongs to the later annalistic chronology, not to contemporary evidence. A recent survey confirms Piso, *FRHist* 9 F 22, as the earliest identifiable author to mention her. [Cambridge, “Porsenna, Horatius Cyclops, and Cloelia”](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/classical-quarterly/article/porsenna-horatius-cyclops-and-cloelia-virgil-aeneid-864951/C7876316A4F5647A03E5746D73DCA4BB)

## Evidentiary key

- **Secure early attestation:** evidence securely datable to Republican or early imperial antiquity, without implying that its narrated event is historical.
- **Later canonical elaboration:** an ancient retelling that expands or reshapes the Republican tradition.
- **Learned systematization:** antiquarian, rhetorical, scholastic, or biographical organization of inherited material.
- **Medieval or early-modern accretion:** post-antique expansion of the narrative.
- **Modern scholarly reconstruction:** an interpretation of how the legend, statue, or historical setting arose.
- **Modern invention:** a new relationship, episode, or characterization presented in modern literature, opera, film, or popular retelling.
- **Negative finding:** no evidence was found in the relevant corpus for the claimed category.

## 1. Identification, names, and classification

Cloelia is the adolescent Roman hostage who, in the canonical account, escaped the camp of the Etruscan king Lars Porsenna and crossed the Tiber with other female hostages. Rome returned her to honor the treaty; Porsenna admired her courage, released her, and allowed her to select other hostages for liberation. Roman tradition associated an equestrian statue on or beside the upper *Via Sacra* with her. Her story belongs to the legendary crisis surrounding the expulsion of Rome’s last king, Tarquinius Superbus, and Porsenna’s intervention, conventionally dated to 509/508 BCE. The evidence for Cloelia herself begins centuries later.

She is consistently a **mortal *virgo***—an unmarried girl—not a goddess, demigod, or heroized dead person. Ancient authors apply to her the normally masculine vocabulary of *virtus*, courage or excellence, while repeatedly stressing that she surpassed expectations attached to her sex and age. Dionysius says that Porsenna praised her spirit as superior to both; Valerius Maximus makes her a “light of courage” to men; Seneca uses her statue to rebuke effeminate young men. These are ancient constructions of gendered exemplarity, not biographical observations. [Dionysius 5.33–35](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Dionysius_of_Halicarnassus/5B%2A.html) [Valerius Maximus 3.2.2](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/thayer/l/roman/texts/Valerius_Maximus/3%2A.html) [Cambridge, *Roman Girlhood*](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/69683507BA2DFDADF668DD4E1012DA6C/9781139644440c1_p15-44_CBO.pdf/formal-education-and-socialization-in-virtue.pdf)

### Names and forms

- **Latin:** *Cloelia*, conventionally pronounced approximately *KLOI-lee-a* in reconstructed Classical Latin. This is the feminine form of the nomen *Cloelius*: the tradition gives no praenomen, father, mother, patronymic, or cognomen.
- **Greek:** Κλοιλία, *Kloilía*, in Greek authors such as Plutarch and Polyaenus.
- **Italian:** *Clelia*.
- **French:** *Clélie*.
- **English:** normally *Cloelia*, occasionally *Clelia* in works transmitted through Italian or French.
- Older forms associated with the family name include *Cluilius/Cluilia* and *Cloulius*. Roman genealogical tradition connected the Cloelii with Alba Longa, while still later family mythology derived them from a supposed Trojan companion of Aeneas called Clolius. None of this supplies Cloelia with a documented genealogy.

The name is securely a Roman gentilicial name, but its deeper etymology is uncertain. Derivations from Latin *cluere* (“to be renowned” or, in a different semantic history, “to cleanse”) and associations with *Cloacina* are conjectural. Modern baby-name claims that *Cloelia* simply “means famous” convert an uncertain etymological comparison into a false certainty.

A historically influential theory identifies the heroine’s name with the misunderstood subject of the statue: Venus Equestris, Venus Cloacina, or another female divinity. That is a **modern scholarly reconstruction**, not an ancient identification. The Shrine of Venus Cloacina was a real cult site over the Cloaca Maxima, but no ancient text identifies Cloelia with Cloacina. [Oxford Classical Dictionary](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/61673/chapter-abstract/548770007) [Treccani, “Clelia”](https://www.treccani.it/enciclopedia/clelia_%28Enciclopedia-Italiana%29/) [Shrine of Venus Cloacina](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Gazetteer/Places/Europe/Italy/Lazio/Roma/Rome/_Texts/PLATOP%2A/Sacrum_Cloacinae.html)

No ancient epithet comparable to a divine cult title is securely attached to her. Descriptions such as *virgo*, *puella*, or the “maiden equal to men” are narrative characterizations, not fixed cult epithets.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### Republican evidence

**Lucius Calpurnius Piso Frugi, *Annales*, later second century BCE — secure early attestation, fragmentary.**  
The work is lost. Pliny, *Natural History* 34.29, preserves Piso’s report that the equestrian statue was erected by Cloelia’s fellow hostages after Porsenna returned them. Piso was consul in 133 BCE and censor in 120 BCE; his annalistic work therefore precedes Virgil, Livy, and Dionysius. What the fragment establishes is that by the later second century BCE a statue was being explained through a Cloelia-and-hostages tradition. It does not establish that either the statue or the escape dated to about 508 BCE. [Pliny 34.28–29](https://www.attalus.org/translate/pliny_hn34a.html)

**Annius Fetialis, date uncertain but earlier than Pliny — competing antiquarian tradition.**  
Also preserved only by Pliny 34.29, Annius denied that the equestrian figure represented Cloelia. He identified it as Valeria, daughter of Publicola, the sole survivor after Tarquin’s stratagem destroyed the other hostages. This is not a minor detail: it provides a different heroine, different disaster, and different rationale for the monument.

### Augustan canonical shaping

**Virgil, *Aeneid* 8.646–651, composed approximately 29–19 BCE — secure early extant text.**  
On Aeneas’s shield, Vulcan represents Porsenna demanding Tarquin’s restoration, Horatius Cocles opposing the Etruscans, and “Cloelia, having broken her bonds, swimming the river” (*vinclis innaret Cloelia ruptis*). The line proves that the heroine was recognizable to Virgil’s audience from a compressed allusion. It uniquely evokes literal or figurative “bonds.” It says nothing about companions, return, release, hostage-selection, or the statue. [Virgil, *Aeneid* 8.630–670](https://dcc.dickinson.edu/vergil-aeneid/vergil-aeneid-viii-630-670)

**Livy, *Ab urbe condita* 2.13.6–11, composed in the late first century BCE — canonical narrative.**  
Livy supplies the fullest influential Latin account: Cloelia is one of the hostages; she deceives the guards; leads a column of maidens through hostile missiles; swims the Tiber; is returned when Porsenna threatens to regard the treaty as broken; is released unharmed; chooses the *impuberes*, prepubescent boys, for liberation; and receives an equestrian statue at the top of the *Via Sacra*. Livy’s earliest complete manuscripts are medieval, although the work’s composition is Augustan. [Livy 2.13, Latin](https://vroma.org/vromans/bmcmanus/livy_latin.html) [Livy 2.13, translation](https://vroma.org/vromans/bmcmanus/livy_english.html)

**Dionysius of Halicarnassus, *Roman Antiquities* 5.32–35, published around 7 BCE — parallel Greek canonical narrative.**  
Dionysius says twenty children of leading families were delivered as hostages. The maidens obtained permission to bathe and persuaded their guards to withdraw to preserve their modesty; following Cloelia’s advice and example, they swam across. During their honorable return, Tarquin’s horsemen tried to seize them, but Porsenna’s son rescued the Romans. Porsenna then released all the hostages and gave Cloelia a richly caparisoned warhorse. The senate ordered a bronze statue, erected by the maidens’ fathers. Dionysius says that by his own visit it no longer stood, reportedly because of a neighboring fire. [Dionysius 5.32–35](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Dionysius_of_Halicarnassus/5B%2A.html)

### Early imperial exempla and poetry

**Manilius, *Astronomica* 1.778–781, early first century CE — poetic exemplum.**  
Cloelia appears in a catalogue of Roman greatness as a girl who surpassed men, part of the developing “masculine maiden” interpretation.

**Seneca, *Consolation to Marcia* 16.2, approximately 40 CE — moralized exemplum.**  
Seneca invokes Cloelia seated on an equestrian statue on the *Via Sacra* to shame contemporary young men who travel luxuriously in litters. His present-tense formulation conflicts with Dionysius’s statement that the statue was already gone; either Seneca speaks rhetorically, the traditions concern different locations, or a replacement had been erected.

**Valerius Maximus, *Memorable Deeds and Sayings* 3.2.2, around 30 CE — learned exemplum.**  
Cloelia mounts a horse and crosses rapidly, freeing herself and her country from fear; she becomes an example of *fortitudo* for men. Unlike Livy, Valerius explicitly makes the crossing equestrian. [Valerius Maximus 3.2.2](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/thayer/l/roman/texts/Valerius_Maximus/3%2A.html)

**Pliny the Elder, *Natural History* 34.28–29, completed 77 CE — antiquarian dossier on the statue.**  
Pliny says the equestrian honor was extended to women in Cloelia’s case, remarks on her being represented in a toga, and records the competing Piso and Annius Fetialis explanations. This is the central source for the statue’s disputed identity and dedication. [Pliny 34.28–29](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/L/Roman/Texts/Pliny_the_Elder/34%2A.html)

**Silius Italicus, *Punica* 10.496–497 and 13.829–830, later first century CE — epic exemplum.**  
Silius characterizes Cloelia as despising king, treaty, youth, and river and again stresses her superiority to the constraints of sex.

**Juvenal, *Satires* 8.264–265, late first or early second century CE — satirical exemplum.**  
The swimming maiden is one of the ancestral Roman models contrasted with degraded contemporary nobility. The later scholia explain that Cloelia sent the guards away.

**Florus, *Epitome* 1.4.7, probably Hadrianic period — patriotic condensation.**  
Cloelia escapes custody and “rides” through her ancestral river; Florus calls Horatius, Mucius, and Cloelia Roman prodigies who would seem fabulous if the annals did not record them. His rhetoric does not independently authenticate them. [Florus, Latin](https://www.ancienttexts.org/library/latinlibrary/florus1.html) [Florus, translation](https://www.roman-britain.co.uk/classical-references/florus-the-epitome-of-roman-history/)

### Plutarchan and military-stratagem traditions

**Plutarch, *Publicola* 19, early second century CE — later canonical elaboration.**  
The maidens collectively decide to swim; “some say” Cloelia alone crossed on horseback and encouraged the swimmers. Publicola returns them because their escape might look like Roman fraud. Tarquin’s forces ambush the convoy; Valeria escapes with three attendants, and Arruns, Porsenna’s son, rescues the others. Porsenna gives Cloelia a horse. Plutarch reports that the equestrian statue stood by the *Via Sacra*, although some identified it as Valeria. [Plutarch, *Publicola* 19](https://lexundria.com/plut_publ/19/prr)

**Plutarch, *Bravery of Women* 14, 250C, early second century CE — learned systematization.**  
The bathing ruse and collective swim are retold in a collection organized specifically around female courage.

**Polyaenus, *Stratagems* 8.31, approximately 162 CE — strategic recasting.**  
Cloelia proposes that the women bind their clothes around their heads and swim. Porsenna rewards her with a richly caparisoned horse and sends the companions home. [Polyaenus 8.31](https://www.attalus.org/translate/polyaenus8B.html)

**Pseudo-Aurelius Victor, *De viris illustribus urbis Romae* 13, probably fourth century CE — learned epitome.**  
Cloelia deceives the guards, seizes a conveniently available horse, crosses the Tiber, is returned, and selects the young because they are most exposed to injury. The episode combines features found separately in earlier sources.

**Servius, commentary on *Aeneid* 8.651, late fourth or early fifth century CE — learned systematization.**  
Servius noticed that hostages were not normally chained and offered alternative explanations for Virgil’s *vinclis ruptis*: Cloelia broke confinement or surveillance, or the phrase referred to the broken “bond” of the bridge. Later commentators continued debating whether Virgil imagined literal chains, a poetic equivalent for custody, or the broken bridge. [Packard Humanities Institute, Servius 8.651](https://latin.packhum.org/loc/2349/5/4822)

### Named corpora in which Cloelia is absent

A dedicated search found no Cloelia episode in Ovid’s *Fasti* or *Metamorphoses*. Cassius Dio’s surviving account is fragmentary and is mediated in places through Zonaras; a fragmentary notice reports Porsenna giving the maiden arms and, in some versions, a horse, but adds no securely independent early evidence. The *Fasti Capitolini*, Lapis Niger, Lupercal material, and Lavinium heroon archaeology illuminate early Rome’s chronology, institutions, and heroic memory but do not name Cloelia. They therefore cannot be used as direct evidence for her story, monument, or cult.

## 3. The story and its variants

### The hostage settlement

In Livy, Porsenna makes peace after the Mucius Scaevola episode and receives Roman youths and maidens as hostages. Dionysius specifies twenty children of elite families and names Marcus Horatius’s son and Publicola’s marriageable daughter among them. Plutarch says ten boys and ten girls elsewhere in his narrative tradition. These numbers and identities are variants, not cumulative facts.

Historically, Porsenna’s intervention may preserve a memory of Etruscan domination or a successful occupation of Rome followed by operations farther south. Modern historians are more willing to treat Porsenna and the Etruscan conflict as historically plausible than to accept the individualized heroic episodes. Archaeology and the separate Greek tradition about the Etruscan defeat at Aricia supply context for Porsenna, but not corroboration of Cloelia. [BMCR review of Cornell](https://bmcr.brynmawr.edu/1997/1997.03.26/)

### Escape from the guards

- **Livy:** Cloelia alone is *dux agminis virginum*, “leader of the column of maidens.” She tricks the guards and swims under hostile missiles.
- **Dionysius:** the girls ask to bathe, exploit modesty conventions to make the male guards withdraw, and collectively swim following Cloelia’s advice and example.
- **Plutarch:** the girls collectively conceive the escape; only “some say” Cloelia crossed on horseback.
- **Valerius Maximus, Florus, and Pseudo-Aurelius Victor:** Cloelia rides across.
- **Virgil:** she swims after breaking “bonds.”
- **Polyaenus:** she tells her companions to bind their clothing on their heads before swimming.

Thus “Cloelia swam the Tiber” is canonical but not universal. “Cloelia crossed on horseback” is already ancient, not a Renaissance invention. The horse may have entered the action through attempts to explain an equestrian statue, while the opposite process is also conceivable: a remembered equestrian exploit may have explained the monument.

### Return and second deliverance

Livy makes Roman treaty-faith central. Porsenna threatens to consider the agreement broken unless Cloelia is returned but promises to send her back unharmed; Rome keeps faith, and Porsenna rewards courage. Cloelia is permitted to choose some hostages and selects the youngest boys. Livy explains the choice as both becoming to her virginity and protective of those most vulnerable to *iniuria*, a word that can include violence or sexual violation. The text does not say that any hostage was raped, but it explicitly makes vulnerability to abuse relevant.

In Dionysius and Plutarch, Tarquin’s forces ambush the convoy returning the girls. Dionysius has Porsenna’s son and troops rescue them; Plutarch allows Valeria to escape with three attendants. The ambush proves Tarquin’s bad faith and prompts Porsenna to abandon the Tarquins. This political causality is absent from Livy’s Cloelia episode.

Valerius Maximus radically compresses the outcome: Cloelia’s crossing frees not only herself but Rome from fear. That makes her agency greater than in Livy, where peace has already been negotiated.

### The horse

The horse occupies three incompatible positions:

1. Cloelia seizes or mounts a horse and crosses on it: Valerius Maximus, Florus, Pseudo-Aurelius Victor, and one Plutarchan variant.
2. Porsenna gives her a warhorse after her return: Dionysius, Plutarch, Polyaenus, and the fragmentary Dio tradition.
3. Rome commemorates her with an equestrian statue: Livy, Pliny, Dionysius, Plutarch, and Seneca.

Plutarch explicitly recognizes the interpretive circularity: supporters of the horseback crossing cite Porsenna’s equine gift, while others say the gift merely honored her courage.

### Fate, death, and descendants

No ancient source in the examined corpus relates Cloelia’s later marriage, children, death, burial, apotheosis, or posthumous apparition. Boccaccio expressly says that the ancients did not transmit her parentage. Stories assigning her a husband—especially Horatius Cocles—or romantic life are post-antique inventions.

## 4. Relations with gods and other heroes

### Genealogy

There is no ancient genealogical tree for Cloelia.

- **Parents:** unnamed.
- **Siblings:** none recorded.
- **Consort:** none recorded.
- **Children:** none recorded.
- **Divine descent:** none recorded.
- **Connection to the gens Cloelia:** implied by her name but never narrated in the surviving story.
- **Alban or Trojan ancestry:** belongs to later genealogy of the *gens*, not to a source about the heroine herself.

## 5. Statue, cult, tomb, and ritual

### The equestrian statue

The statue is the only material object consistently attached to Cloelia, but it does not survive and its original identity and date are unresolved.

The literary testimonia disagree:

- **Piso via Pliny:** the returned hostages erected it.
- **Livy:** “the Romans” awarded the new female virtue a new kind of honor; it stood at the top of the *Via Sacra*.
- **Dionysius:** the senate ordered a bronze statue, erected by the maidens’ fathers; it was already destroyed by fire when he investigated.
- **Pliny:** discusses it as an equestrian statue and suggests it might have been among Rome’s earliest publicly erected statues, but then records Piso’s private or collective-hostage dedication.
- **Plutarch:** it stood beside the *Via Sacra* toward the Palatine, but some called it Valeria.
- **Seneca and Pliny:** speak as if the statue or its identification remained current after Dionysius said it had disappeared.

The contradiction has prompted the **modern reconstruction** that a statue destroyed before Dionysius was replaced, perhaps during Augustan monument restoration. No inscription or archaeological find proves that replacement.

Calling it “the earliest equestrian statue of a woman” requires qualification. It is the earliest female equestrian honor claimed in Roman literary tradition. It is not a surviving sixth-century statue, and archaeology has not authenticated its date or subject. Scholars note that a life-sized bronze honorific equestrian monument around 508 BCE would be anomalously early. Proposed solutions are:

1. the honorific statue was erected much later, perhaps around the third century BCE, and retrojected into the foundation-era story;
2. an archaic cult statue of a mounted goddess was reidentified as Cloelia;
3. an old monument represented Valeria;
4. the monument or its subject was substantially reinvented in Republican or Augustan memorial culture.

The hypothesis that it represented Venus Cloacina, Venus Equestris, or Vica Pota is possible but unproved. [“Tagged Exemplarity in Roman Culture”](https://classics.jhu.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/20/2023/11/Tagged-Exemplarity-in-Roman-Culture.pdf) [Oxford Classical Dictionary](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/61673/chapter-abstract/548770007)

### Cult and ritual: negative findings

No ancient evidence documents:

- a temple, altar, *heroon*, sacred grove, priesthood, sacrifice, votive deposit, festival, games, oracle, mystery rite, or calendar day for Cloelia;
- a tomb or relics attributed to her;
- invocation of Cloelia as a divine or protective power;
- a theophoric personal name derived from a cult title of the heroine.

The statue was commemorative or exemplary in the sources, not demonstrably cultic. The nearby cults and monuments of the Roman Forum cannot be transferred to Cloelia without evidence.

The Lapis Niger, Lupercal traditions, Fasti Capitolini, and Lavinium heroon are therefore contextual comparanda only. None names Cloelia or supplies her with cult. The Venus Cloacina sanctuary was genuine, but equating its goddess with Cloelia is a modern explanation of the statue, not evidence of “Cloelia worship.”

## 6. Historical evolution of the figure

### Later second century BCE: annalistic monument-memory

Piso’s fragment shows that the legend was already attached to a statue and hostage-release tradition. The story may have arisen from family memory, annalistic reconstruction, monument interpretation, or some combination. The evidence cannot decide among them.

### Augustan age: integration into Roman national history

Virgil transforms Cloelia into a single emblem on the shield of Aeneas: the young woman who breaks constraint and swims for liberty. Livy builds an extended moral sequence—Horatius, Mucius, Cloelia—in which courage passes from men to a girl, while Roman treaty-faith and Porsenna’s capacity to recognize Roman virtue remain intact. Dionysius gives the episode Greek historiographical and ethical form, emphasizing modest bathing, civic good faith, Tarquinian treachery, and Porsenna’s judgment.

### First and second centuries CE: flexible exemplum

Valerius Maximus inserts her among examples of courage normally dominated by men. Seneca deploys her against luxurious youths. Juvenal and Florus use her as evidence of Rome’s lost ancestral vigor. Pliny turns her statue into a problem in the history of Roman public portraiture. Plutarch preserves variant traditions rather than harmonizing them. Polyaenus makes the escape a stratagem.

The result was not one canonical Cloelia but a reusable figure:

- leader of female companions;
- lone rider;
- swimmer under fire;
- protector of younger boys;
- guarantor of Roman courage;
- possible treaty-breaker whose act must be repaired;
- girl who reveals adult male inadequacy.

### Late antiquity and the Middle Ages

Pseudo-Aurelius Victor and Servius condensed and rationalized the classical material. No medieval cult or sustained vernacular folklore has been securely documented. Her main route into medieval and Renaissance Europe was learned transmission through Livy, Valerius Maximus, Plutarch, and moralized collections of famous women.

### Boccaccio, approximately 1361–1362

Boccaccio’s *De mulieribus claris* 52, “De Cloelia romana virgine,” makes her one of the exemplary women of universal history. He admits that her parents are unknown, infers noble birth from her character and hostage status, and retells the Tarquin–Porsenna setting, the escape, return, and reward. The work converts an ancient civic exemplum into a Christian-era moral biography without turning her into a saint. Donato Albanzani’s Italian translation followed in the late fourteenth century. [Boccaccio, Latin text](https://boccaccio.letteraturaoperaomnia.org/boccaccio_de_mulieribus_claris.html) [Albanzani translation](https://it.wikisource.org/wiki/De_mulieribus_claris/L)

### Renaissance visual culture

Cloelia was particularly suitable for marriage chests: an aristocratic maiden who exercises courage yet preserves chastity, civic loyalty, and concern for vulnerable children.

Guidoccio di Giovanni Cozzarelli’s Sienese cassone panel, *The Legend of Cloelia*, about 1480, shows the surrender of hostages, the women swimming, and their entry into Rome. The Metropolitan Museum identifies Plutarch as the principal narrative source. This is a securely catalogued Renaissance object, not an ancient representation. [Metropolitan Museum](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/436032)

Illustrated manuscripts of Boccaccio also show Cloelia’s flight; one manuscript image makes her cross on horseback, demonstrating reception of the equestrian variant. [Biblissima manuscript record](https://portail.biblissima.fr/fr/ark%3A/43093/ifdata6305a84ab4746ce8ebb49a08d10fdbe72046fda8)

### Seventeenth century: romance and Baroque painting

Madeleine de Scudéry’s ten-volume *Clélie, histoire romaine* (1654–1661) turns the sparse ancient heroine into the protagonist of an immense French *roman précieux*. Ancient Republican names frame contemporary discussions of friendship, love, power, and salon sociability. The famous *Carte de Tendre* belongs to this novel, not to ancient Cloelia tradition. Clélie’s elaborate emotional life is an **early-modern invention**. [Bibliothèque nationale de France](https://catalogue.bnf.fr/ark%3A/12148/cb31341823z) [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/madeleine-scudery/)

Peter Paul Rubens’s *Cloelia Crossing the Tiber*, about 1630–1635, gives the episode a Baroque mythological visual language, including a personified river. It is part of Cloelia’s artistic reception, not iconographic evidence for an ancient cult or original statue.

### Eighteenth century: opera seria

Pietro Metastasio’s *Il trionfo di Clelia* was first set by Johann Adolf Hasse and performed in Vienna on 27 April 1762 for a Habsburg dynastic celebration. It invents romantic and political relationships, notably Cloelia as Horatius’s intended bride, and combines several early-Republican heroes in one theatrical plot.

Gluck’s setting inaugurated Bologna’s Teatro Comunale on 14 May 1763. Josef Mysliveček produced another setting in Turin in 1767; numerous other composers used the libretto. The horseback river-crossing became theatrical spectacle. ### Nineteenth and twentieth centuries

Nineteenth-century histories increasingly separated the probable Etruscan conflict from the exemplary tales. Hypercritical historians treated Cloelia as a pure aetiological invention explaining a misunderstood statue; more cautious scholarship regarded that as possible but not demonstrable.

The Franco-Italian-Yugoslav film *Le vergini di Roma* / *Amazons of Rome* (1961), directed by Carlo Ludovico Bragaglia and Vittorio Cottafavi and starring Sylvia Syms as Clelia, converts the hostage girl into the commander of female warriors. Its Greeks, Gauls, romantic plot, sewer assault, and marriage to the invented Drusco are modern cinematic inventions. ## 7. Modern reception and reinvention

The most consequential reinventions are:

- **Boccaccio, *De mulieribus claris* 52, c. 1361–1362:** moral biography.
- **Cozzarelli cassone, c. 1480:** domestic and nuptial presentation of female courage.
- **Rubens, c. 1630–1635:** Baroque heroic spectacle.
- **Scudéry, *Clélie*, 1654–1661:** vast romance and vehicle for moral-political conversation; the *Carte de Tendre* becomes more famous than the ancient escape.
- **Metastasio/Hasse, 1762; Gluck, 1763; Mysliveček, 1767:** dynastic opera seria and invented romance with Horatius.
- **Le vergini di Roma*, 1961:** sword-and-sandal warrior leader.

Modern scholarship often treats her as a case study in gender performance: Roman authors praise a girl through masculine *virtus*, but her status as an unpenetrated *virgo* allows her temporarily to cross male/female categories without establishing ordinary female political equality. Roller emphasizes that the same exemplum can be deployed positively or negatively depending on the argument. [Cambridge, *Models from the Past*](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/models-from-the-past-in-roman-culture/cloelia/C5A4C9829677608E06E1CB9C0C461DCC)

No well-documented modern extremist appropriation specific to Cloelia was found. Generic use in patriotic lists of early Roman heroes is common, but evidence does not justify attributing to her a distinct nationalist cult comparable to later appropriations of Romulus, Horatius, or Lucretia. Likewise, no major Freudian, Jungian, Rankian, Raglanian, Campbellian, Dumézilian, or Nagyan interpretive tradition specifically centered on Cloelia was located. Applying “monomyth” terminology to her without a scholarly source would be a modern imposition.

## 8. Historiography and contested material

### Was Cloelia historical?

There is no contemporary evidence for an individual Cloelia. The earliest testimony is roughly three and a half centuries later. The central historical question is therefore not whether one can prove that she swam the Tiber, but how early Roman memory, monuments, family traditions, and annalistic writing produced the story.

Three positions recur:

1. **Traditional acceptance:** a historical hostage escape underlies the later elaboration. Its strength is the plausible setting of interstate hostage-taking during a historically credible Etruscan intervention. Its weakness is the absence of contemporary evidence and the story’s exemplary symmetry with Horatius and Mucius.
2. **Aetiology of a monument:** an old equestrian statue of an unknown woman or goddess generated the narrative. The competing ancient identifications as Cloelia or Valeria strongly support uncertainty about the statue; its proposed identification as Venus remains conjectural.
3. **Layered construction:** some memory of hostages or conflict combined with later monument interpretation, family tradition, and literary exemplarity. This is often the most economical model but cannot be proven in detail.

### Did Porsenna capture Rome?

Ancient Roman narrative often softens defeat: Livy’s Porsenna negotiates after admiring individual Roman courage. Other evidence, including Tacitus’s statement that Porsenna imposed restrictions after Rome’s surrender, has encouraged modern historians to infer that Porsenna captured or dominated the city. If so, the heroic sequence may be a later Roman reshaping of defeat. This does not demonstrate that Cloelia was invented, but it changes the political function of her tale: personal *virtus* compensates for collective military failure.

### The monument problem

The strongest evidence that ancient observers themselves were uncertain is Pliny’s preservation of incompatible identifications and Dionysius’s report that the statue was gone. Modern treatments that simply reproduce a photograph of an unrelated Roman equestrian sculpture as “Cloelia’s statue” are modern illustration, not evidence.

The phrase “first equestrian statue erected to a woman” should therefore be rendered:

> Ancient literary tradition represented Cloelia’s monument as an exceptionally early female equestrian statue; neither the object nor an identifying inscription survives, and its date, dedicator, subject, and even continuity through the imperial period are disputed.

### Swimming, riding, and chains

No version can be privileged merely because it is more realistic.

- Piso’s recoverable fragment concerns the statue, not the method of crossing.
- Virgil, the earliest extant poetic notice, says she swims after breaking bonds.
- Livy and Dionysius say she swims.
- Valerius Maximus and Florus make her ride.
- Plutarch explicitly preserves both positions.
- Servius shows that late-antique readers already found Virgil’s chains problematic.

The riding version need not be a late corruption: it is securely early imperial. Conversely, the statue may have stimulated it.

### “Cloelia chose the boys because they faced rape”

Livy says the *impuberes* were the age most exposed to *iniuria*. Sexual abuse is a defensible implication in the context of captive vulnerability, and modern scholarship has emphasized it, but Livy does not narrate an assault. The disciplined formulation is that she selects those considered most vulnerable to violation; actual rape is not attested.

### Misleading modern claims

- **“Cloelia was a princess”:** not anciently attested.
- **“Daughter of a named Roman senator”:** not attested.
- **“Horatius Cocles’s fiancée”:** Metastasian invention.
- **“Founder or priestess of a Cloelia cult”:** no evidence.
- **“Her statue has been excavated”:** false.
- **“Cloelia is another name for Venus Cloacina”:** speculative modern theory, not ancient fact.
- **“The story is confirmed by the Lapis Niger/Fasti Capitolini/Lavinium archaeology”:** false; none names her.
- **“She unquestionably crossed on horseback”:** only one ancient branch of the tradition.
- **“Cloelia means ‘famous’”:** disputed etymology presented as certainty.
