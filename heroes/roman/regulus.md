# Marcus Atilius Regulus: Research Dossier

> **Traditional class:** historical Roman magistrate transformed into a **mortal civic hero and moral exemplum**; never securely attested as a demigod or cult-receiving *heros*.  
> **Earliest secure attestation:** the First Punic War narrative in Polybius, *Histories* 1.29–35, composed in Greek about 150–140 BCE, securely attests Regulus’s African command, failed negotiations, defeat, and capture. It does **not** attest his later embassy to Rome, voluntary return, or torture. The earlier Latin epic *Bellum Punicum* of Naevius, composed in the late third century BCE by a veteran of the war, covered Regulus’s campaign, but its surviving fragments do not securely preserve the parole-and-return story. The earliest surviving evidence for that legend derives from the late-second-century BCE annalist Gaius Sempronius Tuditanus, quoted by Aulus Gellius, *Noctes Atticae* 7.4.

## Evidentiary key

- **[EA] Securely attested early material:** near-contemporary or independent evidence for offices, campaigns, defeat, or capture.
- **[LC] Later canonical elaboration:** the familiar exemplary story, first securely visible generations after the events.
- **[RV] Variant:** a materially different ancient version.
- **[LS] Learned systematization:** antiquarian, rhetorical, scholastic, or genealogical organization.
- **[MA] Medieval or early-modern accretion.**
- **[MR] Modern scholarly reconstruction:** an inference rather than an ancient datum.
- **[MI] Modern invention or unsupported attribution.**
- **[AE] Absence of evidence:** a specifically investigated but unattested claim.

## 1. Identification, names, and classification

Marcus Atilius Regulus was a plebeian Roman consul of the third century BCE, probably the son of the Marcus Atilius Regulus who held the consulship in 294 BCE. He was consul in 267 BCE and, as suffect consul, again in 256 BCE. He commanded part of Rome’s invasion of North Africa during the First Punic War, defeated Carthaginian forces, imposed—or attempted to impose—severe peace terms, and was defeated and captured by the Spartan mercenary commander Xanthippus in 255 BCE. These facts are substantially supported by Polybius and the Augustan consular and triumphal fasti. **[EA]**

The heroic Regulus is a later construction: Carthage supposedly sent him to Rome under oath to obtain peace or a prisoner exchange; instead, he urged the Senate to continue the war, refused family and friends’ entreaties, returned voluntarily to captivity, and died under ingenious torture. By Cicero’s time, he embodied *fides*, *constantia*, *fortitudo*, patriotic self-subordination, and obedience to an oath despite foreseeable death. **[LC]**

### Roman name

**Mārcus Atīlius Rēgulus**, conventionally abbreviated **M. Atilius M. f. L. n. Regulus** in the reconstructed fasti:

- **Marcus**: the common Latin praenomen.
- **Atilius**: nomen of the plebeian *gens Atilia*. Its ultimate etymology is uncertain. A traditional linguistic classification derives it from a hypothetical praenomen *Atius* or the attested Sabine form *Attius* plus a gentilic suffix; this remains an onomastic reconstruction, not an ancient explanation. **[MR]**
- **Regulus**: Latin *rēgulus*, literally “little king,” “petty king,” or “prince,” the diminutive of *rex*. It was a hereditary cognomen in one branch of the Atilii; it was not a royal title awarded to this Marcus. **[EA/LS]**
- The spelling **Attilius** is a later or variant orthography frequently encountered in manuscripts and early-modern works. **[LS]**

Other language forms include Greek **Μᾶρκος Ἀτίλιος Ῥήγουλος** (*Markos Atilios Rhēgoulos*); Italian **Marco Atilio Regolo**; French **Marcus Atilius Régulus** or **Régulus**; Spanish **Marco Atilio Régulo**; and German **Marcus Atilius Regulus**.

### “Serranus”

Virgil, *Aeneid* 6.844, names a *Serranus* found sowing—*te sulco, Serrane, serentem*—among future Roman worthies. Servius and later antiquarians connected this with an Atilius allegedly summoned from agricultural labor. The identification is unstable:

- The agricultural story properly belongs in several sources to **Gaius Atilius Regulus**, consul in 257 and 250 BCE, not securely to Marcus, the captive consul. **[LS]**
- Silius Italicus names Marcus’s son **Serranus**, but no securely historical son of that name is independently known; Marcus did have a historically attested son of the same name who later became consul. **[LC]**
- Ancient derivation from *serere*, “to sow,” is a folk etymology. Modern proposals connect *Serranus* with the Umbrian town Saranum or the Campanian river Sarnus. None identifies Marcus Atilius Regulus securely as “Regulus Serranus.” **[MR]**

### False identifications

Regulus must be distinguished from:

- Marcus Atilius Regulus, consul in 294 BCE, probably his father;
- Gaius Atilius Regulus, consul in 257 and 250 BCE;
- Marcus Atilius Regulus, consul in 227 BCE and later censor, probably his son;
- Gaius Atilius Regulus, consul killed at Telamon in 225 BCE;
- Marcus Aquilius Regulus, the notorious imperial-era informer attacked by Pliny.

No ancient source equates the First Punic War Regulus with Romulus, Aeneas, Hercules, a god, or a hero of Greek cult. Literary comparison with Hercules does not amount to identification. **[AE]**

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### Documentary evidence

The **Fasti Capitolini**, monumental consular lists erected under Augustus and recovered in the Roman Forum in 1546, record Regulus’s suffect consulship in 256 BCE after the death of Quintus Caedicius: *in eius locum factus est M. Atilius M. f. L. n. Regulus II*. They also preserve his first consulship in 267 BCE. The fasti are late documentary witnesses using earlier official lists; they establish magistracies, filiation, and iteration of office, not the embassy or martyrdom. **[EA]**

The **Fasti Triumphales**, likewise an Augustan compilation from earlier records, credit an Atilius Regulus with a triumph over the Sallentini. Later literary tradition associates the 267 BCE campaign with the capture of Brundisium and a vow to Pales. **[EA for triumph; LC for circumstantial narrative]**

No securely identified contemporary portrait inscription, funerary monument, tomb text, dedication, coin portrait, or battlefield inscription of this Regulus survives. **[AE]**

### Principal primary corpus

1. **Naevius, *Bellum Punicum*** — Latin epic, composed in the late third century BCE; surviving only in fragments quoted by later grammarians. Naevius had served in the First Punic War. The poem treated the African expedition, including Roman operations traditionally assigned to Regulus, but the surviving wording does not attest the oath, Roman embassy, or torture. Assignments of particular fragments to Regulus are partly editorial reconstruction. **[EA/MR]**

2. **Polybius, *Histories* 1.29–35** — Greek, about 150–140 BCE. The earliest complete surviving narrative. Regulus campaigns successfully, devastates the countryside, seeks the glory of ending the war before a successor arrives, and proposes intolerable terms. Carthage resumes resistance under Xanthippus; Regulus is defeated and captured. Polybius turns the reversal into a warning against trusting Fortune. He says nothing about a later embassy or torture. **[EA]**

3. **Gaius Sempronius Tuditanus, historical work, fragment quoted by Gellius 7.4** — Latin, probably about 120 BCE. Earliest surviving attribution of a torture tradition: Carthage allegedly administered slow poison before sending Regulus to Rome and, after his return, confined him in darkness before suddenly exposing him to the sun with his eyelids fastened open. Because the work survives only through Gellius, its exact original context cannot be recovered. **[LC]**

4. **Diodorus Siculus, *Bibliotheca historica* 23–24 fragments** — Greek, first century BCE. Diodorus condemns Regulus’s arrogant peace terms and reports the retaliatory torture of Carthaginian prisoners by Regulus’s wife and sons. The extant fragments do not contain the familiar embassy; modern editors expressly note that absence. **[EA/RV]**

5. **Cicero** — Latin, chiefly the 50s–40s BCE:
   - *De officiis* 1.39 and 3.99–115 gives the fullest Republican ethical treatment: Regulus refuses to support exchange, returns because of his oath, and accepts torture.
   - *Pro Sestio* 127, *In Pisonem* 43, *Philippics* 11.9, and *Ad Atticum* 16.11 cite his voluntary return or torture as proverbial.
   - Cicero’s evidence securely establishes that the complete exemplary story was canonical by the mid-first century BCE, not that it happened in 250 BCE. **[LC]**

6. **Livy, *Ab urbe condita*, lost Book 18; *Periocha* 18** — Latin history composed in the late first century BCE, preserved here only through a much later epitome. The summary says that Regulus, sent to Rome by the Carthaginians, advised against peace and prisoner exchange, returned voluntarily, and was killed by torture. Because Book 18 is lost, Livy’s sources and full handling cannot be inspected. **[LC]**

7. **Horace, *Odes* 3.5** — Latin lyric, published in 23 BCE. Regulus rejects a dishonorable exchange lest ransomed captives corrupt Roman military virtue; he separates himself from his wife, children, friends, and Senate and departs as an “illustrious exile.” Horace does not narrate the physical torture. The poem applies Regulus to Augustan problems created by Roman prisoners in Parthia after Carrhae. **[LC/political reinterpretation]**

8. **Virgil, *Aeneid* 6.824–859, with Servius** — Latin epic, 20s BCE; commentary of Servius, early fifth century CE. Virgil’s parade of Roman heroes does not name Marcus Regulus. The nearby *Serranus* generated learned Atilian identifications, but using this as evidence for Marcus’s legend is circular. **[AE/LS]**

9. **Valerius Maximus, *Facta et dicta memorabilia*** — Latin, reign of Tiberius. Regulus appears repeatedly:
   - 1.1.14: the gods permitted Carthaginian cruelty so that his glory would be greater;
   - 4.4.6: his poverty and small estate;
   - 9.2 ext.1: eyelids removed or forced open and death by exposure;
   - other passages connect his family with punishment for perjury.
   Valerius converts him into a catalogued exemplum for rhetorical instruction. **[LC/LS]**

10. **Seneca, *De providentia* 3.4–9** — Latin, mid-first century CE. Regulus’s nailed confinement becomes evidence that adversity tests and displays virtue; the good man can overcome Fortune through endurance. **[LC/philosophical reinterpretation]**

11. **Pliny the Elder, *Natural History* 8.37** — Latin, completed 77 CE. Regulus’s army kills a giant serpent at the Bagradas with siege engines, and its 120-foot skin and jaws are displayed at Rome until the Numantine War. **[LC]**

12. **Silius Italicus, *Punica* 6.62–589** — Latin epic, approximately 80–101 CE. Serranus, presented as Regulus’s son, reaches the veteran Marus after Trasimene. Marus narrates Regulus’s African campaign, the gigantic serpent, embassy, family farewell, return, and death. The episode deliberately epicizes the historical exemplum and supplies otherwise unattested characters, speeches, and family scenes. **[LC]**

13. **Frontinus, *Strategemata*** — Latin, late first century CE. Preserves military stratagems associated with the African war rather than the complete martyr legend. **[LC/LS]**

14. **Florus, *Epitome* 1.18** — Latin, usually dated to the second century CE. Gives a highly compressed, patriotic narrative culminating in imprisonment, starvation, and crucifixion or death in a nail-studded enclosure. **[LC/RV]**

15. **Appian, *Punic Wars* 3–4** — Greek, second century CE. Gives the defeat and capture, the embassy under oath, advice against exchange, return, and death in a spiked enclosure through enforced sleeplessness. Appian also relates Xanthippus’s fate, illustrating how late narratives paired moral reversals. **[LC/RV]**

16. **Aulus Gellius, *Noctes Atticae* 7.4** — Latin, late second century CE. Quotes or paraphrases Tuditanus and Quintus Aelius Tubero: slow poison; darkness; eyelids fastened open; exposure to the sun. Gellius is indispensable because he names earlier authorities now lost. **[LC/LS]**

17. **Cassius Dio, Book 11 fragments, principally through Zonaras and John of Damascus** — Greek, early third century CE with Byzantine transmission. Gives the 120-foot serpent and Regulus’s defeat; other Dionian material supplies purported peace terms. Attribution of every later epitomized detail directly to Dio requires caution. **[LC]**

18. **Pseudo-Aurelius Victor, *De viris illustribus urbis Romae* 40** — Latin, fourth century CE. A schoolbook biography combining campaign, serpent, embassy, return, and torture. **[LS]**

19. **Eutropius, *Breviarium* 2.21–25; Orosius, *Historiae adversus paganos* 4.8–10** — Latin, fourth and early fifth centuries CE. Abbreviated imperial and Christian universal histories perpetuating the narrative. Orosius incorporates it into a providential history of pagan calamity. **[LS/Christian reinterpretation]**

20. **Tertullian, *Ad martyras* 4 and *Apologeticum* 50** — Latin, about 197–200 CE. Regulus, packed into a chest pierced with nails, becomes a pagan anticipation and rhetorical competitor of Christian martyrs: if earthly glory could inspire such endurance, heavenly reward should inspire Christians more. **[LC/Christian appropriation]**

### Corpus named in the brief but not evidence for Regulus

Livy Books 1–2, Dionysius of Halicarnassus, Ovid’s *Fasti* and *Metamorphoses*, Plutarch’s *Romulus* and *Publicola*, the Lapis Niger, the Lupercal, and the Lavinium “heroon” concern Rome’s foundation traditions and early Republic, not Marcus Atilius Regulus. Cornell, Wiseman, Grandazzi, Dumézil, and Horsfall are indispensable for those traditions but do not supply evidence for Regulus’s third-century legend. Their omission from the positive dossier is therefore an evidentiary finding, not an oversight. **[AE]**

## 3. The story and its variants

### Origins and family

The fasti call the 256 consul **M. Atilius M. f. L. n. Regulus**, “Marcus Atilius Regulus, son of Marcus, grandson of Lucius.” This supports descent from the consul of 294 BCE. **[EA]**

No ancient source preserves his birthplace, mother, date of birth, childhood, education, or youth. Claims that he was born in Campania derive from reconstructions of the Atilian family’s regional origin, not testimony about this individual. **[MR]**

He had a wife, unnamed in Diodorus and most sources. Silius calls her **Marcia**, a literary naming first securely attested in the *Punica*. **[LC]** Diodorus says that his widow and sons tortured two Carthaginian captives. A historically attested Marcus Atilius Regulus, later consul and censor, was probably one son; Silius’s **Serranus** is poetic and cannot be securely equated with him. **[EA/LC]**

### First consulship and the Sallentini

The consular and triumphal fasti attest his consulship with Lucius Julius Libo in 267 BCE and a triumph over the Sallentini. Florus says Brundisium, with its famous harbor, was captured and that Pales demanded a temple as the price of victory. A temple vow is therefore literary rather than archaeologically verified. **[EA for office and triumph; LC for the vow]**

### African command

After consul Quintus Caedicius died in office in 256 BCE, Regulus replaced him and shared command with Lucius Manlius Vulso. The Roman fleet defeated Carthage near Cape Ecnomus and transported an army to Africa. Polybius is the earliest detailed authority. **[EA]**

When Vulso returned to Italy, Regulus remained with a reduced force. He captured towns, ravaged Carthaginian territory, and won at Adys. Tunis was occupied, and Carthage faced rebellion or defection among its Numidian neighbors. **[EA]**

A later poverty motif says that Regulus asked to be relieved because an estate manager had died or absconded and his family’s small farm could not be maintained. The Senate supposedly arranged support. This appears in Valerius Maximus and later moralizing accounts and makes the commander an analogue of Cincinnatus. Polybius does not report it. **[LC]**

### The Bagradas serpent

Pliny, Dio’s fragments, Valerius Maximus, and Silius report that Regulus’s army encountered an enormous serpent beside the Bagradas River. It killed soldiers and could be overcome only with artillery; its skin, allegedly 120 Roman feet long, was sent to Rome and displayed publicly. **[LC]**

Variants concern whether the object sent was the whole skin, slough, jaws, or hide and precisely how the animal was killed. Silius expands the incident into a miniature dragon-slaying epic: named soldiers die grotesquely, Regulus rallies the army, and engines destroy the creature. Comparisons with the Hydra and dragon combats place Regulus in a Herculean register, but no source says Hercules assists him or that Regulus is Hercules reborn. **[LC; AE for direct divine interaction]**

The story might preserve memory of a large python, an army rumor, a displayed curiosity, or a literary monster. None is demonstrable. Its origin in Naevius is possible but unproven because no surviving Naevian fragment contains it. **[MR]**

### Failed peace negotiations

Polybius says that Carthage sought terms after its reverses. Regulus, expecting the glory of ending the war, demanded terms so harsh that the Carthaginians preferred continued war. Polybius does not enumerate them. Diodorus similarly condemns his arrogance. **[EA]**

Dio’s transmitted fragments supply detailed demands: surrender of prisoners and deserters, Roman retention or acquisition of territory, indemnity, annual tribute, severe restriction of the Carthaginian navy, and subordination of Carthaginian foreign policy to Rome. The precision belongs to a much later source and may reconstruct what “intolerable terms” meant. **[LC]**

This is the earliest moral Regulus: not yet the martyr of *fides*, but a warning that good fortune breeds overconfidence. Polybius’s explicit lesson is the reversal of Fortune; Diodorus’s is punishment for arrogance. **[EA as historiographical interpretation]**

### Xanthippus, defeat, and capture

The Carthaginians hired the Spartan Xanthippus, who reorganized their deployment around cavalry and elephants. At the battle near Tunis in 255 BCE, the Roman army was destroyed; about 500 men, including Regulus, were captured. Polybius supplies the basic account. **[EA]**

Later texts heighten the contrast between Regulus and Xanthippus or invent a treacherous death for Xanthippus. Those stories do not alter the secure fact of Regulus’s capture. **[LC]**

### Captivity and embassy to Rome

The canonical tradition places the embassy after several years of captivity, usually around 250 BCE after Carthaginian reverses near Panormus. Carthage sends Regulus and envoys to propose peace or exchange of prisoners, requiring him to swear that he will return if negotiations fail. **[LC]**

Cicero says Regulus spoke in the Senate against exchange because the Carthaginian prisoners were useful commanders while he himself was old and expendable. Horace recasts the argument: soldiers who surrendered should not be ransomed lest recovered cowards contaminate Roman courage. Livy’s epitome and later historians combine peace and exchange proposals. **[LC/RV]**

Some versions say Regulus initially refused to enter Rome as a citizen because captivity had deprived him of status, or refused to give a formal senatorial opinion. This legal coloring reflects later Roman debate about *postliminium*, oath, and captive status. **[LS]**

### Family farewell and voluntary return

Horace portrays Regulus lowering his eyes from his wife and little children, treating himself as no longer a citizen, then leaving mourning friends with the calmness of someone departing for ordinary country business. Cicero emphasizes that friends and relatives tried to detain him. Silius dramatizes Marcia blocking or pleading at the threshold. **[LC]**

The oath is the hinge of the Roman tradition. Regulus allegedly held that an oath sworn to an enemy remained binding because Jupiter witnessed oaths and because Roman *fides* could not depend on the beneficiary’s nationality. Cicero uses the story to argue that the honorable and the expedient cannot truly conflict. **[LC/philosophical systematization]**

### Death: incompatible versions

Ancient accounts do not agree:

- **Slow poison before departure; darkness and solar blinding after return:** Tuditanus through Gellius 7.4. Regulus is kept in a dark chamber, then brought into intense sunlight with eyelids fixed open. **[LC]**
- **Eyelids cut off or sewn open:** Cicero’s allusions and Valerius Maximus; the exact bodily technique varies with textual formulation. **[LC/RV]**
- **Spiked chest or cage:** Appian, Silius, pseudo-Aurelius Victor, and Tertullian. Internal nails prevent rest; sleep deprivation kills him. **[LC/RV]**
- **Starvation and crucifixion:** Florus’s compressed version, which may combine distinct traditions. **[LC/RV]**
- **Generic torture:** Livy’s *Periocha* and several abbreviated histories. **[LC]**
- **Death in captivity without the embassy:** compatible with what can be recovered from Diodorus; the date and manner are uncertain. **[EA/RV]**
- **No reported later fate:** Polybius simply leaves him a prisoner after the catastrophe. **[EA/AE]**

The famous nail-studded rolling barrel is a later visual and popular conflation of the spiked chest with wheel or barrel punishments. It is not the stable account of Cicero, Horace, Livy’s epitome, or Polybius. **[MA/MI]**

### Retaliation against Carthaginian prisoners

Diodorus reports that Regulus’s widow and sons confined two Carthaginian captives, **Bodostor** and **Hamilcar**, under brutal conditions. Bodostor died; household slaves alerted magistrates or tribunes, and Hamilcar was rescued. Gellian and later traditions vary the apparatus of torture. **[RV]**

This episode is crucial to the historicity dispute. One modern reconstruction holds that Regulus died naturally or through ordinary neglect in captivity and that his family’s abuse of prisoners produced a defensive Roman counter-story accusing Carthage of first torturing him. The chronological and causal relationship cannot be proved. Diodorus’s preservation of an anti-Atilian episode nevertheless shows that the unanimous patriotic legend is not “the consent of antiquity.” **[MR]**

### Body and burial

No source securely describes the disposition, recovery, ransom, cremation, or burial of Regulus’s body. No ancient tomb has been identified in Carthage or Rome. Claims of a “tomb of Regulus” are unsupported or confuse him with unrelated persons bearing the cognomen. **[AE]**

## 5. Cult, tomb, ritual, and images

### Cult

There is no secure evidence that Regulus received:

- a *heroon*;
- sacrifices;
- games;
- a priesthood;
- an altar;
- votive offerings;
- an oracle;
- a festival;
- relic veneration;
- invocation as a protective hero;
- official deification.

**[AE]**

Roman exemplary commemoration is not automatically hero cult. Regulus lived in rhetoric, education, poetry, and political memory, but the surviving record does not show ritual worship.

### Temple of Pales

Florus’s report that Regulus vowed a temple to Pales after the Sallentine campaign is evidence for Regulus acting as a Roman magistrate toward a deity. The temple’s location and archaeological identification are uncertain, and no excavated structure can securely be labeled “Regulus’s temple of Pales.” **[LC/AE]**

### Tomb and relics

No tomb, cenotaph, shrine, body relic, or securely ancient funerary portrait of the captive consul is known. The absence is especially significant because the legend normally leaves his body at Carthage. **[AE]**

### Ancient portraiture

No surviving ancient portrait can securely be identified as Regulus. Two herms were labeled with his name in modern times, apparently because their physiognomy or supposed Republican appearance seemed appropriate. The inscriptions or identifications are modern and do not establish iconography. **[MI]**

Coins of the Atilii and other Roman portraits have occasionally generated conjectural identifications, but there is no demonstrable lifetime portrait type of Marcus Atilius Regulus. **[MR/AE]**

### Ancient narrative images

Silius Italicus says that Hannibal saw temple-wall pictures at Liternum representing First Punic War scenes and ordered them burned. The episode belongs to epic narrative; it does not supply an extant archaeological image, and it is unclear whether Silius reflects an actual monument. **[LC]**

## 6. Historical evolution of the figure

### 267–255 BCE: historical magistrate and defeated commander

Official lists preserve offices and victory over the Sallentini. Polybius preserves the African expedition, harsh negotiations, catastrophic defeat, and capture. At this stage the moral is principally Greek historiographical reversal: success produces overconfidence, and Fortune overturns it. **[EA]**

### Late third to early second century BCE: epic memory

Naevius’s *Bellum Punicum* made the First Punic War material for Roman national epic. Because the poem survives in fragments, it cannot be shown whether Naevius knew the embassy-and-return legend. **[EA/AE]**

### About 120 BCE: emergence of the martyr narrative

Tuditanus’s lost history, preserved by Gellius, is the earliest datable witness to the elaborate death story. It already knows poisoning, return from Rome, darkness, and solar torture. The legend therefore existed by the late second century BCE, but there is a gap of more than a century after the alleged events. **[LC]**

### First century BCE: canonical civic exemplum

Diodorus preserves a critical or pro-Carthaginian strand: Regulus’s harshness provokes renewed resistance, and his family behaves atrociously toward captives. Cicero, by contrast, repeatedly makes Regulus the supreme case in which oath, state interest, and moral right override personal survival. Livy incorporates the story into Roman national history. Horace gives it enduring poetic form and applies it to the shame of Roman captives in Parthia. **[LC/RV]**

### First century CE: rhetorical, Stoic, and epic expansion

Valerius Maximus organizes Regulus under moral headings; Seneca turns him into a Stoic proof that suffering is not evil; Pliny monumentalizes the serpent; Silius embeds the entire legend within Flavian epic and invents domestic and veteran perspectives. Regulus becomes simultaneously general, dragon slayer, austere farmer, loyal husband who abandons domestic claims, keeper of oaths, and martyr of the state. **[LC/LS]**

### Second to fifth centuries CE: school canon and Christian appropriation

Appian, Florus, Gellius, Dio, and epitomators multiply death variants. Tertullian takes Regulus seriously as a pagan model of bodily endurance, arguing that Christian martyrs possess an even greater cause. Late-antique schoolbooks preserve a compact “life” suitable for declamation. **[LS]**

### Middle Ages

Regulus survives through Cicero, Valerius Maximus, Orosius, late-antique epitomes, and moralized collections of famous men. Boccaccio’s *De casibus virorum illustrium* places him among men brought low by Fortune. Fifteenth-century illuminated manuscripts depict the nail torture, translating conflicting literary accounts into a stable visual scene. **[MA]**

### Early modern Europe

Humanist education made Regulus a standard exemplum of keeping one’s word and sacrificing private interest to the state. Painters, dramatists, and librettists favored the family farewell, return, blinding, or spiked enclosure. His moral absoluteness also made him suitable for debate: admirable fidelity could appear indistinguishable from fanaticism or inhuman civic ideology. **[MA]**

### Modern period

Historical criticism separated the securely attested campaign from the later embassy narrative. Simultaneously, Turner’s *Regulus* made the disputed solar torture one of the legend’s most influential modern visual forms. **[MR/modern reception]**

## 7. Modern reception and reinvention

### Drama and opera

Pietro Metastasio composed the libretto ** *Attilio Regolo* ** during the 1740s. Johann Adolf Hasse’s setting premiered at Dresden in 1750. The work excludes a conventional dominant love intrigue and represents Regulus as a serenely self-mastering Roman whose public duty defeats domestic affection. It substantially shaped the eighteenth-century theatrical Regulus. **[MA]**

Other musical settings and adaptations followed Metastasio’s libretto. These are receptions of the Ciceronian-Horatian hero, not independent evidence for antiquity.

### Painting and graphic art

- **Salvator Rosa, *The Death of Atilius Regulus***, early 1660s: monumentalizes the violent execution tradition. **[MA]**
- Numerous eighteenth-century history paintings show Regulus departing Rome, rejecting his family, or suffering torture. They frequently combine Horace’s farewell with Gellius’s blinding or the late spiked enclosure. **[MA]**
- Fifteenth-century French illumination of Boccaccio’s *De casibus*, Paris, BnF Français 234, fol. 40, depicts the torture with nails. **[MA]**
- **J. M. W. Turner, *Regulus***: first exhibited in Rome in 1828, heavily reworked and exhibited again in London in 1837; oil on canvas, now Tate Britain. Turner reduces Regulus to a barely visible figure within a Carthaginian harbor overwhelmed by white sunlight. The viewer’s effort to look into the glare reenacts the solar blinding. The painting therefore transforms a disputed late-Republican torture variant into an optical experience rather than a conventional history tableau. It entered the Turner Bequest in 1856. **[modern reception]**
- Daniel Wilson and Samuel Bradshaw produced nineteenth-century engravings after Turner, helping stabilize the title and narrative for a wider audience. **[modern reception]**

The claim that Turner straightforwardly painted “Regulus leaving Carthage” or “Regulus leaving Rome” is an interpretive title tradition; modern art history emphasizes the picture’s deliberate narrative elusiveness.

### Political and educational uses

From Cicero onward Regulus could justify uncompromising public duty, rejection of prisoner exchange, and subordination of the individual to the state. Horace used him in the context of Roman captives held by Parthia. Early-modern monarchies and republics alike could appropriate him as a model of incorruptibility and patriotic sacrifice. **[LC/MA]**

In modern civic education he was often paired with Cincinnatus, Mucius Scaevola, Lucretia, the Decii, and Cato as a “Roman virtue” figure. Such anthologies commonly presented the embassy as uncontested history and omitted Polybius’s hostile account of the peace terms and Diodorus’s account of the Atilian family’s torture of prisoners. **[modern reception]**

No major, continuous cult of Regulus in modern nationalism comparable to those surrounding Romulus, Arminius, Vercingetorix, or Leonidas is documented in the material reviewed. His authoritarian potential lies more in generalized praise of obedience and patriotic self-annihilation than in a stable extremist symbol. **[AE]**

### Psychology and comparative mythology

Regulus has not been a major test case for Rank, Raglan, Campbell, Dumézil, or Nagy. Applying a generic “hero’s journey” to him—departure, ordeal, return, death—is a modern template and obscures the actual direction of the evidence: a captured commander was progressively rewritten as an exemplum. **[MR/MI]**

Comparisons with dragon slayers are relevant specifically to the Bagradas episode in Pliny, Dio, and Silius. They do not show that Regulus began as an Indo-European serpent-slaying god or culture hero. No linguistic, cultic, or early narrative evidence supports such a theory. **[AE]**

### Modern inventions

The following frequently circulate as ancient fact but require correction:

- **A nail-studded barrel rolled downhill:** late conflation, not the securely earliest account. **[MI/MA]**
- **A securely identified portrait bust of Regulus:** none exists. Modern inscriptions on herms are not authentication. **[MI]**
- **A tomb or shrine at Carthage:** unattested. **[MI]**
- **Hero cult or formal deification:** unattested. **[MI]**
- **The title “Regulus Serranus” for the captive consul:** conflation of Atilian traditions. **[MI]**
- **Polybius describing the embassy and torture:** false; he is silent. **[MI]**
- **Universal ancient agreement:** false; Polybius omits the story, while Diodorus preserves a sharply different moral tradition. **[MI]**

## 8. Historiography and scholarly debate

### What is historically secure?

There is broad reason to accept that Regulus was a real magistrate, twice consul, victorious in southern Italy, commander in Africa, defeated, and captured. These points rest on the fasti and Polybius rather than on the exemplary tradition. **[EA]**

His consulship cannot reasonably be dismissed merely because the later martyr narrative is legendary. Conversely, his historical office does not authenticate the embassy, oath speech, or torture. The dossier must keep those propositions separate.

### Polybius’s silence

The strongest objection to the canonical story is that Polybius, writing within roughly a century of the war and narrating Regulus’s campaign in detail, says nothing about his mission to Rome or grotesque death. Polybius had an obvious moralizing interest in Regulus but uses him to illustrate Fortune and overconfidence, not *fides*. Diodorus’s extant parallel material likewise lacks the embassy. **[EA]**

Arguments from silence are not conclusive because:

- Polybius’s purpose and selection could omit later captivity;
- the surviving narrative may depend on sources with limited information about prisoners;
- parts of related traditions are fragmentary;
- absence does not prove impossibility.

Nevertheless, the silence is substantial because the embassy would have perfectly suited Polybius’s interest in Roman institutions and moral reversal. **[MR]**

### Dating the legend

The earliest securely recoverable torture authority is Tuditanus, writing about 120 BCE. By Cicero’s time the whole structure—oath, anti-exchange speech, voluntary return, torture—was familiar enough to function without elaborate introduction. The legend therefore crystallized between the mid-third and late-second centuries BCE, but its precise author or occasion is unknown. **[MR]**

Possible contexts proposed by scholars include:

- retrospective justification of Rome’s refusal to ransom captives;
- ideological preparation for or response to debates after Cannae in 216 BCE;
- the Atilian family’s need to answer accusations concerning tortured Carthaginian hostages;
- intensified anti-Carthaginian memory around the destruction of Carthage in 146 BCE;
- annalistic competition in constructing ancestral exempla.

These are plausible historical models, not attested origins. **[MR]**

### Carthaginian and Roman source traditions

Polybius and Diodorus may ultimately preserve material influenced by the pro-Carthaginian historian Philinus of Acragas. Their Regulus is arrogant, strategically shortsighted, and punished by reversal. Roman annalistic tradition turns the same captive into the moral victor over Carthage. The two portraits need not be harmonized: their conflict is evidence for competing memory cultures. **[MR]**

### Torture as counter-propaganda

The hypothesis that Roman accounts of Regulus’s torture developed to excuse or invert the Atilian family’s abuse of Bodostor and Hamilcar remains influential. Its strengths are the existence of the Diodoran counter-story and the progressively spectacular nature of Roman torture accounts. Its weaknesses are uncertainty about Diodorus’s source, chronology, and the possibility that both sets of atrocities were separately invented or exaggerated. **[MR]**

### Legal debate: oath and *postliminium*

Cicero’s discussion belongs to Roman ethical and legal argument. Regulus’s case raised whether a captive recovered citizen status on returning, whether a coerced oath bound him, whether he could speak in the Senate, and whether public utility could excuse perjury. Later juristic references to Regulus are evidence for the story’s use in Roman legal thought, not independent confirmation of the event. **[LS]**

### Regulus as *exemplum*

Modern scholarship emphasizes that a Roman *exemplum* is not simply a biographical anecdote. Repetition in speeches, poems, schools, and monuments allowed each audience to evaluate and reproduce communal values. Regulus’s usefulness depended on extremity: the oath costs him not convenience but family, civic status, body, and life.

The same mechanism allowed criticism. Polybius’s Regulus is the agent of avoidable disaster; Horace’s hero may look uncompromisingly admirable or frighteningly indifferent to ordinary human bonds; Silius tests whether the inherited exemplar remains adequate after the disaster at Trasimene. **[MR]**

### Textual and material limitations

- Livy’s full Book 18 is lost; only the *Periocha* remains.
- Tuditanus and Tubero survive only in quotation or paraphrase.
- Diodorus’s relevant books are fragmentary and excerpted.
- Dio’s early books survive through Byzantine excerpts and epitomes.
- Naevius survives in grammarians’ quotations whose narrative placement is partly conjectural.
- No Punic version of Regulus’s captivity survives.
- No securely identifiable tomb, portrait, or personal inscription supplements the texts.

The surviving corpus is therefore asymmetrical: abundant Roman moral reception, weak contemporary documentation of the disputed episode. **[EA]**

### Discredited or unsupported theories still circulating

- Treating Cicero as an eyewitness-quality historian of the First Punic War.
- Treating every detailed torture as mutually corroborating when later authors may depend on the same annalistic tradition.
- Reading Silius’s Marus, Serranus, speeches, and domestic scene as archival history.
- Assuming that the serpent proves a mythological origin for Regulus.
- Treating absence from Polybius as mathematical proof that every later element is false.
- Treating the fasti’s authentication of the consul as authentication of the martyr legend.

Each collapses a distinct evidence tier. **[MI/MR]**

## 10. Findings

1. A historical Marcus Atilius Regulus is securely attested as consul, commander, defeated general, and captive. **[EA]**
2. The celebrated embassy, oath, return, and torture are not found in Polybius and first become visible more than a century after the capture. **[LC]**
3. The ancient tradition was never uniform. Polybius and Diodorus make Regulus’s arrogance central; Cicero and Horace make his fidelity central; imperial writers compete in elaborating the torture. **[EA/LC/RV]**
4. The giant-serpent exploit is an ancient but late legendary addition, most fully epicized by Silius. **[LC]**
5. The story of the widow’s torture of Carthaginian prisoners is an ancient counter-tradition, not a modern attempt to defame him. **[RV]**
6. No cult, tomb, relic, shrine, ancient portrait type, or apotheosis of Regulus is securely documented. **[AE]**
7. Turner’s painting is not merely an illustration: it makes the spectator undergo the legend’s blinding glare and is a decisive modern reinvention of the figure.
8. The historically responsible formulation is therefore not “Regulus certainly did” or “Regulus never did,” but: **Polybius attests the captured commander; late-Republican Roman memory created the paradigmatic keeper of faith; later literature, Christianity, opera, and painting repeatedly remade him.**
