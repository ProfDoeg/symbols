# Pallas (son of Evander): Research Dossier

**Traditional class:** mortal epic prince and warrior; a heroized literary dead, but not a securely attested recipient of ancient cult.  
**Earliest secure attestation of Pallas specifically as Evander’s son and Aeneas’s young ally:** Virgil, *Aeneid* 8–12, composed c. 29–19 BCE and published after Virgil’s death in 19 BCE.  
**Earlier related but incompatible testimony:** Polybius of Megalopolis, writing in the second century BCE but preserved only through Dionysius of Halicarnassus, knew a Pallas who was Evander’s **grandson**, the son of Heracles and Evander’s daughter Lavinia; he died on the future Palatine and was allegedly buried there. This is evidence for a pre-Virgilian Roman Pallas-etymology, not securely for Virgil’s warrior.

## Evidentiary key

- **[E1: securely attested early material]** A surviving ancient text, inscription, object, or archaeological context whose date and content are reasonably secure.
- **[E2: later canonical elaboration]** A literary development dependent upon, or later than, the earliest recoverable form.
- **[E3: regional variant]** A geographically localized or incompatible traditional version.
- **[E4: learned systematization]** Antiquarian, grammatical, genealogical, or etymological harmonization.
- **[E5: syncretistic identification]** Deliberate equation of distinct figures, cults, or traditions.
- **[E6: medieval or early-modern accretion]** Post-antique material represented as an addition to the ancient story.
- **[E7: folklore]** Popular or legendary material without contemporary documentary corroboration.
- **[E8: modern scholarly reconstruction]** A reasoned modern hypothesis rather than an ancient statement.
- **[E9: modern invention or error]** A demonstrably recent claim, conflation, or unsupported presentation as ancient fact.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### Orientation

**[E1]** In Virgil’s *Aeneid*, Pallas is the only son and dynastic hope of the aged Arcadian king Evander. He lives at Pallanteum, Evander’s settlement on the future site of Rome, and participates with his father in the annual sacrifice commemorating Heracles’ defeat of Cacus. When Aeneas seeks allies against Turnus, Evander entrusts Pallas to him for military instruction. Pallas commands Arcadian cavalry, distinguishes himself in his first battle, kills Halaesus and other Italians, and is then killed in single combat by Turnus. Turnus strips from him a richly decorated sword-belt. Aeneas sends the body back to Evander with a magnificent funeral escort; later, at the poem’s conclusion, the sight of Pallas’s belt on Turnus causes Aeneas to abandon an impulse toward mercy and kill Turnus.

The character is consequently not a “biographical” person but a tightly constructed epic node joining:

1. Arcadian Pallanteum to Rome’s Palatine;
2. Evander’s pre-Trojan settlement to Aeneas’s future Rome;
3. fathers and sons—Evander/Pallas, Anchises/Aeneas, Aeneas/Ascanius, Mezentius/Lausus;
4. hospitality and its obligations;
5. the Homeric pattern Achilles–Patroclus–Hector;
6. the moral and political ambiguity of Rome’s foundation through violence.

### Names and forms

- **Latin:** *Pallas*, genitive *Pallantis*. Virgil also uses the adjective/patronymic **Evandrius**, “Evander’s” or “son of Evander,” at *Aeneid* 10.394 and 10.420. **[E1]**
- **Greek:** Πάλλας, *Pállas*. The name is shared by numerous unrelated mythic figures: Athena Pallas; the Titan Pallas; Pallas son of Lycaon; Pallas son of Pandion; and others. Identity cannot be inferred from the name alone. **[E1]**
- **English:** Pallas; occasionally “Pallant” in older English or French-influenced reception.
- **French:** Pallas or Pallante.
- **Italian:** Pallante.
- **German:** Pallas.
- **Medieval Latin:** *Pallas*, sometimes *Pallantes* through reinterpretation of the Latin inflection. Boccaccio calls him *Pallantes Evandri regis filius*. **[E6]**

No stable personal epithet comparable to “swift-footed Achilles” belongs to him. Virgil instead marks him contextually as *puer* or *iuvenis*, “boy/young man”; *natus* or *natum*, “son”; *spes et solacia nostri*, “our hope and consolation” (*Aeneid* 8.514); and *Evandrius*, “Evander’s.” **[E1]**

### Etymology

The personal name’s remote etymology is uncertain. Proposed derivations of Greek Πάλλας have been associated with πάλλω, “brandish, wield” or “shake,” but the ancient name cluster is too diverse for certainty. **[E8]**

The locally important ancient question was not the remote Indo-European etymology but the relationship among **Pallas**, **Pallantium/Pallanteum**, and **Palatium/Palatine**:

- **Virgil, *Aeneid* 8.51–54:** Evander’s Arcadians derive from an ancestral Pallas, and the settlement is named from him. In Virgil this ancestor is distinct from Evander’s son. **[E1]**
- **Livy 1.5:** the Palatine takes its name from Pallanteum, the Arcadian city from which Evander came. Livy does not make the young warrior Pallas the eponym. **[E1]**
- **Dionysius 1.31:** Evander’s settlers name their Roman settlement after their Arcadian homeland. **[E1]**
- **Polybius as quoted by Dionysius 1.32.1:** Pallantium was named after a boy Pallas, son of Heracles and Evander’s daughter Lavinia, who died and was buried there. **[E1/E3]**
- **Servius on *Aeneid* 8.51:** collects several derivations—from Evander’s grandfather Pallas, his daughter Pallantia, his prematurely dead son Pallas, a son killed in sedition after Evander’s death, or sheep’s bleating, *balatus*. **[E4]**
- **Festus:** likewise preserves alternatives involving grazing or wandering sheep, Pallantia, and Pallas’s burial. **[E4]**
- **Pausanias 8.43–44, second century CE:** Rome’s Pallantium recalls Arcadian Pallantium, where statues of an older Pallas and Evander stood. That Pallas is normally the Arcadian eponym, son of Lycaon, not Virgil’s youth. **[E3]**

Modern historical linguistics does not accept any of these narrative genealogies as proof that the hill was historically named after a person. They are ancient etiology: stories built around resemblance among place-names and heroic names. **[E8]**

### Confusable or competing figures

| Figure | Parentage | Function | Relationship to this dossier |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pallas, son of Evander | Evander; unnamed Sabine mother implied by *Aeneid* 8.510 | Aeneas’s protégé, killed by Turnus | Principal subject; Virgilian |
| Pallas, son of Heracles and Lavinia | Heracles + Evander’s daughter | Dead youth and alleged Palatine eponym | Pre-Virgilian incompatible variant reported from Polybius |
| Pallas, Evander’s ancestor | Usually grandfather or remoter ancestor | Eponym of Pallanteum | Virgil/Servius genealogical figure |
| Pallas, son of Lycaon | Lycaon | Founder/eponym of Arcadian Pallantium | Arcadian figure, not Evander’s son |
| Pallantia | Daughter of Evander; in one account assaulted or impregnated by Heracles | Alternative Palatine eponym | Antiquarian variant |
| Pallas Athena | Zeus; goddess | Frequently called simply Pallas | Entirely distinct; many digital searches conflate them |

There is no ancient equation of Evander’s son with Athena, the Titan, or Pallas son of Lycaon. Such conflations are **[E9]**.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### Earliest evidence

The earliest potentially relevant testimony is a lost statement of **Polybius** (c. 200–118 BCE), quoted by Dionysius of Halicarnassus at *Roman Antiquities* 1.32.1. It concerns a Pallas who died at Rome and whose tomb supposedly named Pallantium, but calls him Evander’s grandson and Heracles’ son. **[E1/E3]** Because Polybius survives here only through Dionysius, the wording is Dionysius’s transmission, not an independently preserved Polybian text.

The earliest secure and complete appearance of **Pallas son of Evander as Aeneas’s ally** is therefore Virgil’s *Aeneid*. No surviving Greek epic, archaic inscription, Republican fasti, or securely identified pre-Virgilian image documents that complete identity. **[E1]**

### Primary corpus

#### Virgil, *Aeneid* 8–12

- **Language:** Latin.
- **Composition:** approximately 29–19 BCE.
- **Textual transmission:** ancient papyrus fragments and late-antique codices survive; the principal complete manuscript tradition is late antique and medieval. The character is not dependent upon a conjectural modern restoration.
- **Role:** principal Arcadian youth in the Italian war and the emotional cause of the epic’s final killing. **[E1]**

Key passages:

- 8.51–54: ancestral Pallas and Pallanteum’s name.
- 8.104–125: Pallas greets Aeneas at the river and escorts him to Evander.
- 8.185–279: Pallas is present at the rites for Heracles and the story of Cacus.
- 8.466–519: Evander entrusts Pallas to Aeneas.
- 8.558–584: departure; Evander prays for his son’s return or his own immediate death.
- 10.362–425: Pallas rallies the Arcadians and kills named opponents, culminating in Halaesus.
- 10.426–438: parallel confrontation of Pallas and Lausus, prevented by Jupiter.
- 10.439–509: Turnus challenges, kills, and strips Pallas.
- 10.510–605: Aeneas’s retaliatory fury and intended human sacrifices to Pallas’s shade.
- 11.26–99: preparation and dispatch of Pallas’s funeral procession.
- 11.139–181: Evander receives the body and demands Turnus’s death.
- 12.938–952: Pallas’s belt decides Turnus’s fate.

#### Ovid, *Fasti* 1.471–586

- **Language:** Latin.
- **Date:** composed and revised approximately 2–8 CE, with later revisions in exile.
- **Role:** Carmenta prophetically addresses Pallas as her grandson: “Pallas, my grandson dear, why don those fatal arms?” She foretells his death and vengeance by no ordinary champion. Ovid presupposes Virgil’s narrative and incorporates it into Augustan sacred chronology. **[E2]**

Ovid’s *Metamorphoses* does not furnish an independent narrative of this Pallas. Occurrences of “Pallas” there normally mean Athena or another namesake. Claims that *Metamorphoses* contains the warrior’s full story are **[E9]**.

#### Dionysius of Halicarnassus, *Roman Antiquities* 1.31–32

- **Language:** Greek.
- **Date:** published beginning c. 7 BCE.
- **Role:** preserves the competing Polybian genealogy and then subjects it to empirical criticism. Dionysius says he saw no tomb of Pallas, heard of no libations to him, and could discover no other cultic observance, although annual sacrifices to Evander and Carmenta were visible. **[E1]**

This is the most important ancient negative evidence concerning Pallas’s cult.

#### Livy, *Ab urbe condita* 1.5

- **Language:** Latin.
- **Date:** late first century BCE.
- **Role:** connects Evander and his Arcadians to the Palatine and derives the name from Arcadian Pallanteum. He does not narrate Virgil’s Pallas. **[E1]**

#### Ovid, *Fasti* 4

Ovid narrates Evander, Heracles, and Cacus and the Arcadian context of Roman ritual, but this is background rather than another developed Pallas narrative. **[E2]**

#### Servius and Servius Danielis, commentary on Virgil

- **Language:** Latin.
- **Date:** core commentary conventionally associated with Servius, late fourth/early fifth century CE; the expanded *Servius Danielis* tradition incorporates additional older and later scholia.
- **Role:** preserves genealogical, topographical, and etymological variants. At 8.51 Servius reports Pallas as Evander’s ancestor, daughter, prematurely dead son, or son killed in a post-Evanderian sedition, and notes the *balatus* etymology. **[E4]**

Servius is invaluable testimony to ancient scholarship but does not make every alternative equally early or historical.

#### Pausanias, *Description of Greece* 8.43–44

- **Language:** Greek.
- **Date:** c. 150–180 CE.
- **Role:** describes Arcadian Pallantium, its connection with Rome, and statues of Pallas and Evander. The Pallas is the Arcadian ancestral figure, not demonstrably Virgil’s slain youth. **[E3]**

#### Silius Italicus, *Punica* 6.628–640

- **Language:** Latin.
- **Date:** late first century CE.
- **Role:** makes the child born from Heracles and Evander’s daughter the first **Fabius**, serving the gens Fabia’s heroic genealogy. This differs from the Polybian/Dionysian version, where that child is Pallas. **[E2/E4]**

#### Statius, *Silvae* 5.3

- **Language:** Latin.
- **Date:** late first century CE.
- **Role:** uses Evander’s anxiety for Pallas and the faithful attendant Acoetes as exempla of paternal or foster-parental devotion. It presupposes Virgil. **[E2]**

#### Plutarch and Cassius Dio

Plutarch’s *Romulus*, *Publicola*, and *Fabius Maximus*, and Cassius Dio’s Roman history preserve parts of the wider Evander, Palatine, Lupercal, and Fabian traditions, but neither supplies an independent full narrative of Pallas son of Evander. Plutarch’s *Fabius* 1 makes the first Fabius a child of Heracles and an Italian woman or nymph; this belongs to the competing Fabian genealogy. **[E4]**

#### Fasti Capitolini and Lapis Niger

Neither the **Fasti Capitolini** nor the **Lapis Niger inscription** names Pallas son of Evander. The Lapis Niger is an archaic Latin ritual/legal inscription, generally dated to the late sixth or early fifth century BCE; its value is evidence for early Roman literacy, kingship, and sacred regulation, not evidence for Pallas. The Capitolian fasti are Augustan chronological monuments concerning Roman magistrates and triumphs. Their invocation as “proof” for Pallas is **[E9]**.

## 3. The story

### Parentage and youth

Virgil names Pallas as Evander’s son but never names his mother. At *Aeneid* 8.510 Evander says Pallas is *mixtus matre Sabella*, “of mixed descent through a Sabine mother.” **[E1]** Any modern assignment of a personal name to her is unsupported.

Pallas is Evander’s dynastic “hope and consolation” and seems to be his only surviving child. The poem repeatedly calls him a youth and makes the Italian battle his first day of major warfare. No ancient source supplies a childhood narrative, tutor, earlier adventure, wife, lover, or child. **[E1; absence finding]**

### Meeting Aeneas

At *Aeneid* 8.102–125, Aeneas approaches Pallanteum while Evander, Pallas, senators, and young men are sacrificing to Heracles. Pallas, bold enough to interrupt the rite, meets the strangers with a spear and asks who they are, what they seek, and whether they bring peace or war. Aeneas identifies himself; Pallas takes his hand and leads him to Evander. **[E1]**

The clasped hands and shared tables establish *hospitium*, hereditary guest-friendship. That bond later governs Evander’s trust, Turnus’s taunt, Aeneas’s guilt, and vengeance.

### Entrusted to Aeneas

At 8.508–519 Evander declares himself too old to command and Pallas partly native through his Sabine mother. He therefore asks Aeneas to act as Pallas’s military teacher:

> *hunc tibi praeterea, spes et solacia nostri, / Pallanta adiungam; sub te tolerare magistro / militiam… adsuescat*  
> “I shall join to you Pallas, our hope and consolation; under you as master let him learn to endure warfare.”

Evander provides two hundred Arcadian horsemen, while Pallas supplies another two hundred in his own name. **[E1]**

At departure, Evander clings to Pallas, foresees danger, and prays either for his safe return or for immediate death before news of disaster. The scene constructs the expedition as both heroic initiation and impending parental catastrophe.

### Pallas in battle

When difficult terrain forces the Arcadians to dismount, they begin to retreat. Pallas rallies them by Evander’s name and their equality as mortal men. **[E1]** His aristeia follows:

- kills Lagus with a spear;
- kills Hisbo with a sword;
- attacks Sthenius and Anchemolus;
- kills or mutilates the twins Thymber and Larides;
- kills Rhoeteus with a missile intended for Ilus;
- prays to the river-god Tiber and kills Halaesus.

The narrator emphasizes that the youth earns genuine martial distinction before dying; he is not merely a helpless victim.

### Pallas and Lausus

Pallas and Lausus—beautiful, similarly aged sons of opposed fathers—press against one another at 10.426–438. Jupiter refuses to let them meet because each is destined to die at the hand of a greater opponent: Pallas by Turnus, Lausus by Aeneas. **[E1]**

This is a deliberate structural pairing. Both sons display *pietas* toward their fathers, and both deaths expose the terrible cost older leaders impose on the young. Pallas and Lausus do not actually exchange blows; statements that one kills or wounds the other are **[E9]**.

### Duel and death

Juturna, Turnus’s divine sister, directs Turnus toward Pallas. Turnus orders everyone else to withdraw, wishes Evander could witness the combat, and claims Pallas as his own opponent. **[E1]**

Pallas replies that either victory with spoils or a glorious death will make him famous. Knowing he is physically overmatched, he prays to Heracles by the obligations of hospitality. Heracles hears but can only weep. Jupiter tells Heracles that everyone has an appointed day and that glory must extend the short span of life.

Pallas’s spear grazes Turnus’s shoulder. Turnus’s spear penetrates the layered shield, cuirass, and chest. Pallas tries unsuccessfully to pull it free; blood and life depart together, and he falls face down on hostile soil.

Turnus stands on the corpse and tells the Arcadians to return Pallas to Evander “as he deserved.” He allows burial but says Aeneas’s hospitality will cost Evander dearly. Turnus then places his left foot on Pallas and tears away the massive baldric. **[E1]**

### The belt

The baldric or sword-belt—*balteus*, with its decorated *cingula*—depicts a “crime”: fifty young men murdered in their bridal chambers on one wedding night. Virgil does not name the Danaids, but ancient and modern commentators generally identify the scene as the daughters of Danaus killing the sons of Aegyptus. The identification is highly probable, not explicit. **[E8]**

The narrator immediately condemns Turnus’s inability to observe limits in success and predicts that he will wish Pallas unharmed and hate both the spoils and the day he took them. The belt is therefore:

- a trophy obtained through normal heroic spoliation;
- an image of bridal murder, recalling the poem’s own contested marriage;
- a sign of Turnus’s triumph and excess;
- a memory-object that transfers Pallas’s claim into the final scene.

Interpretation remains disputed. Some scholars see the belt as furnishing just punishment for Turnus’s boastful desecration; others see it as triggering Aeneas’s relapse into *furor* and making Rome’s foundation morally compromised. These are modern readings of an intentionally difficult scene, not alternative ancient endings. **[E8]**

### Aeneas’s retaliation and human sacrifice

When Aeneas learns of Pallas’s death, he kills indiscriminately in search of Turnus. He captures four sons of Sulmo and four raised by Ufens alive, intending to sacrifice them to Pallas’s shade and drench the funeral flames with captive blood (*Aeneid* 10.517–520). **[E1]**

This is threatened or intended human sacrifice within the epic narrative. Virgil does not narrate its completed performance in Book 11. It should therefore be described neither as an established Roman rite nor as certainly executed. **[E1; evidentiary limit]**

### Funeral and return to Evander

At the beginning of Book 11, Aeneas prepares Pallas’s return:

- the body is washed and laid on a woven bier;
- Aeneas places over it two garments made by Dido;
- Pallas’s arms and captured spoils accompany him;
- enemy captives, destined for sacrifice, are sent in the procession;
- Acoetes, Pallas’s aged attendant, follows;
- Pallas’s horse Aethon walks riderless and weeps;
- a thousand chosen men escort the body.

Virgil compares the dead youth to a flower cut by a maiden’s hand. **[E1]**

At Pallanteum, Arcadians meet the procession with torches. Evander throws himself upon the corpse. He insists that Pallas did not disgrace his ancestry and does not blame the Trojan alliance, but says his life has become hateful and charges Aeneas to bring him Turnus’s death. **[E1]**

Virgil does **not** narrate Pallas’s burial, tomb construction, cremation, or final deposition. Medieval descriptions of an elaborate tomb fill a gap in the ancient poem. **[E6]**

### The ending of the *Aeneid*

In Book 12, Aeneas defeats Turnus. Turnus appeals to Aeneas’s father Anchises, asks that his body be returned to Daunus, and acknowledges defeat. Aeneas pauses and begins to relent. Then he sees Pallas’s belt on Turnus’s shoulder.

Aeneas declares:

> *Pallas te hoc vulnere, Pallas / immolat et poenam scelerato ex sanguine sumit*  
> “Pallas sacrifices you with this wound; Pallas takes punishment from your guilty blood.”

Aeneas drives his sword into Turnus; the epic ends as Turnus’s life flees indignantly among the shades. **[E1]**

The verb *immolat* deliberately gives the killing sacrificial coloring. Whether Aeneas acts as a legitimate avenger, a victim of wrath, or both is the central unresolved ethical dispute in modern Virgilian criticism. The transmitted poem has no ancient sequel in which Pallas returns, is resurrected, or receives apotheosis.

## 4. Relations with gods and other heroes

### Genealogies

#### Virgilian genealogy

```text
Mercury + Carmenta/Carmentis
            │
          Evander + unnamed Sabine woman
                    │
                  Pallas
```

Virgil names Evander’s mother through the Carmenta tradition and describes Pallas’s mother only as Sabine. **[E1]**

#### Polybian/Dionysian alternative

```text
Evander
   │
Lavinia + Heracles
        │
      Pallas
```

Here Pallas is Evander’s grandson and Heracles’ son, dies on the future Palatine, and becomes its alleged eponym. **[E1/E3]**

#### Fabian alternative

```text
Evander
   │
unnamed daughter + Heracles
                 │
               Fabius
```

Silius Italicus and Plutarch connect Heracles’ Italian child with the Fabii rather than with Virgil’s Pallas. **[E2/E4]**

### Documented interactions

The following list distinguishes direct interactions from narrative juxtaposition.

- **Evander:** father; raises him as dynastic hope, entrusts him to Aeneas, embraces him at departure, receives and laments his body. Virgil, *Aeneid* 8.104, 8.466–584; 11.139–181, c. 29–19 BCE. **[E1]**
- **Aeneas:** Pallas greets him, takes his hand, leads him to Evander, becomes his pupil and allied commander; after Pallas’s death Aeneas prepares his funeral and avenges him. *Aeneid* 8.102–125, 8.514–519; 10.510–520; 11.26–99; 12.938–952. **[E1]**
- **Heracles/Alcides:** Pallas attends his commemorative sacrifice and later prays to him for victory over Turnus; Heracles hears and weeps but cannot alter fate. *Aeneid* 8.102–279; 10.460–472. **[E1]**
- **Jupiter:** does not converse with Pallas; prevents Pallas and Lausus from fighting and explains to Heracles that Pallas’s allotted day cannot be changed. *Aeneid* 10.426–438, 10.464–473. **[E1]**
- **Tiber/Thybris:** Pallas prays to the river-god for his missile to penetrate Halaesus and vows the spoils to Tiber’s oak; the god hears. *Aeneid* 10.420–425. **[E1]**
- **Turnus:** enemy in single combat; Turnus kills Pallas, insults his father’s hospitality, and strips the belt; the belt later causes Turnus’s death. *Aeneid* 10.439–505; 12.938–952. **[E1]**
- **Lausus:** opposing young commander and deliberate double of Pallas; they press against one another but Jupiter prevents a duel. *Aeneid* 10.426–438. **[E1]**
- **Halaesus:** enemy killed after Pallas’s prayer to Tiber. *Aeneid* 10.411–425. **[E1]**
- **Lagus:** enemy speared while lifting a rock. *Aeneid* 10.380–384. **[E1]**
- **Hisbo:** attacks after Lagus falls; Pallas kills him with a sword thrust to the lung. *Aeneid* 10.384–387. **[E1]**
- **Sthenius:** directly attacked in Pallas’s aristeia; exact manner of death is compressed. *Aeneid* 10.388. **[E1]**
- **Anchemolus:** directly attacked and killed; identified as having violated his stepmother’s marriage bed. *Aeneid* 10.388–389. **[E1]**
- **Thymber:** twin whose head Pallas severs. *Aeneid* 10.390–396. **[E1]**
- **Larides:** Thymber’s twin; Pallas cuts off his right hand. *Aeneid* 10.390–396. **[E1]**
- **Rhoeteus:** killed by Pallas with a missile while fleeing Teuthras and Tyres; the missile had been aimed at Ilus. *Aeneid* 10.399–404. **[E1]**
- **Ilus:** Pallas’s intended target; Rhoeteus intercepts the cast. *Aeneid* 10.399–404. **[E1]**
- **Arcadian companions:** Pallas commands and rallies them when they retreat. They later carry his body on his shield. *Aeneid* 10.362–379, 10.505–509. **[E1]**
- **Acoetes/Acestes:** aged attendant who accompanies Pallas and follows the funeral procession in grief. *Aeneid* 11.30–33. The manuscripts/commentaries vary over the form and identity; he should not automatically be equated with the Sicilian king Acestes. **[E1/E4]**
- **Aethon:** Pallas’s named horse; follows the bier without ornaments and weeps. *Aeneid* 11.89–90. **[E1]**
- **Carmenta:** grandmother in the Roman genealogy; Ovid makes her prophesy directly to Pallas about his fatal armor and avenger. Ovid, *Fasti* 1.519–524, early first century CE. **[E2]**
- **Juturna:** she guides Turnus toward Pallas, but no direct exchange with Pallas is narrated. *Aeneid* 10.439–440. **[E1; indirect]**
- **Dido:** no living encounter occurs; garments she made for Aeneas cover Pallas’s corpse. *Aeneid* 11.72–77. **[E1; posthumous material connection]**
- **Ascanius/Iulus:** no direct conversation or joint exploit is narrated. Pallas functions structurally as a vulnerable surrogate son beside Aeneas’s surviving son, but that is interpretation. **[E8]**
- **Patroclus and Achilles:** no literal meeting exists. Pallas’s death and Aeneas’s vengeance are literary reworkings of the Patroclus–Achilles pattern. **[E8]**
- **Athena/Minerva:** no interaction. The shared name Pallas has generated confusion, but Virgil’s youth is not the goddess’s child, avatar, or consort. **[E9]**

No consort or child of Pallas son of Evander is named in a reliable ancient source. **[E1; absence finding]**

## 5. Cult, tomb, and ritual

### Ancient cult

The strongest ancient evidence is explicitly negative. Dionysius 1.32.2 says:

- he had not seen Pallas’s tomb at Rome;
- he had not heard of drink-offerings to Pallas;
- he could find no other comparable honor;
- by contrast, he knew annual Roman sacrifices to Evander and Carmenta and had seen their altars.

Thus Pallas was remembered in etiology and epic but is not securely attested as a Roman cult recipient. **[E1]**

The funeral rites in *Aeneid* 11 are narrative representation, not evidence that historical Romans sacrificed to Pallas. Aeneas’s proposed killing of captives is likewise epic action, not proof of a recurring cult. **[E1]**

### Alleged tomb on the Palatine

The Polybian tradition said Evander erected a tomb for his grandson Pallas and named Pallantium after him. Dionysius investigated and rejected the claim’s observable cultic implications. **[E1/E3]**

Servius and Festus preserve a different etiology in which Evander’s son Pallas was buried on the Palatine. **[E4]** No securely identified tomb, inscription, bones, altar, or votive assemblage confirms either Pallas.

### Arcadian Pallantium

Pausanias saw a sanctuary or monument containing statues of Pallas and Evander at Arcadian Pallantium. The Pallas involved is the city’s ancestral eponym, usually son of Lycaon, not demonstrably the Virgilian youth. **[E1/E3]** Antoninus Pius later favored the town because of its claimed ancestral connection with Rome. That imperial privilege illustrates the political value of the Pallantium–Palatine genealogy, but not a cult of Evander’s son.

### Lupercal

Ancient writers explained the Lupercal and Lupercalia as Arcadian institutions brought by Evander and related them to Lycaean Pan. This belongs to Evander’s cultural-hero complex, not directly to Pallas. **[E1/E4]**

The decorated chamber discovered beneath the Palatine in 2007 was publicly proposed as the Lupercal, but specialists disputed that identification on topographical and stylistic grounds. In either case, it contains no evidence naming Pallas. **[E8]**

### Lapis Niger

The Lapis Niger complex documents an archaic Roman sacred area and very early Latin inscription. It neither names Pallas nor confirms an Arcadian Pallanteum. Using it to validate the story of Pallas is an illegitimate leap from “early Rome had sacred institutions” to “this particular epic hero was historical.” **[E9]**

### Lavinium’s heroon

Excavations at Lavinium uncovered a seventh-century BCE elite tomb, reworked in the sixth century and monumentalized as a heroon in the fourth. Ancient visitors associated a local heroon with Aeneas or Anchises. The archaeological sequence is important for understanding how old tombs could be reidentified with founders, but it is not Pallas’s tomb and supplies no evidence for him. **[E1]**

This is nevertheless an instructive parallel: archaeological monuments could acquire heroic identities centuries after construction. It shows how a Pallas-tomb tradition might function, not that such a tomb existed. **[E8]**

### Medieval “discovery” of Pallas’s body

Beginning in twelfth-century testimony, a celebrated legend claimed that Pallas’s gigantic, incorrupt body was discovered near Rome during the reign of an emperor Henry or, in other versions, Frederick Barbarossa. William of Malmesbury’s *Gesta regum Anglorum* and later chronicles describe:

- an enormous corpse surpassing city walls or ordinary men;
- the chest wound made by Turnus;
- an epitaph identifying Pallas as Evander’s son;
- an inextinguishable lamp;
- eventual destruction or dissolution of the body.

The epitaph commonly reads:

> *Filius Evandri Pallas, quem lancea Turni  
> militis occidit, more suo iacet hic.*

“Here lies Pallas, son of Evander, whom the spear of the warrior Turnus killed in its accustomed fashion.”

**[E6/E7]** No contemporary excavation record, surviving object, or inscription corroborates the story. The contradictory discovery dates and miraculous lamp mark it as medieval literary accretion, not ancient archaeology.

The Old French *Roman d’Énéas* and Heinrich von Veldeke’s *Eneit* elaborate Pallas’s tomb. Virgil supplies a procession and lament but no tomb description; medieval authors invented the monument, embalming, and architectural spectacle. **[E6]**

Boccaccio’s *Genealogia deorum gentilium* 12.67 repeats the discovery from Martinus’s chronicle, including the immense wound and perpetual lamp. **[E6]** Early-modern antiquarians and writers on “ever-burning lamps” then treated the story as a marvel; later occult literature recirculated it as fact. **[E7/E9]**

## 6. Historical evolution

### Before Virgil: Republican etiological traditions

**[E1/E8]** By the second century BCE, Roman and Greek scholarship was connecting Evander, Arcadian Pallantium, Heracles, the Palatine, and the Lupercal. The Polybian fragment shows a Pallas already embedded in one Palatine etiology, but as Evander’s grandson. The tradition probably developed through antiquarian attempts to explain Roman names and rites by Greek foundations. This is reconstruction, not proof that an Arcadian colony occupied the Palatine.

### Augustan canonical shaping

**[E1]** Virgil transformed scattered names and foundation traditions into the emotionally central son of Evander. The youth binds pre-Trojan Rome to Aeneas and allows the future Roman landscape to be imagined before Rome. His death also ensures that the poem’s foundation war culminates in morally charged revenge.

Ovid then absorbed the Virgilian Pallas into Carmenta’s prophetic view of Roman history, making the youth’s death one stage in the rise of Augustan world rule. **[E2]**

### Imperial learned tradition

Dionysius preserves and critiques the competing tomb narrative; Silius redirects the Heracles–Evander genealogy toward the Fabii; Statius uses Pallas as an exemplum of paternal grief; Pausanias records Arcadian monuments; Servius assembles incompatible etymologies. **[E2–E4]**

The result was not a single stable genealogy but a family of explanations:

- Palatine from Arcadian Pallantium;
- Pallantium from an ancestral Pallas;
- Palatine from Evander’s son’s grave;
- Palatine from Evander’s grandson’s grave;
- Palatine from Pallantia;
- Palatine from sheep.

### Medieval transformation

Medieval readers knew Pallas primarily through Virgil, school commentary, and vernacular romances. **[E6]** The *Roman d’Énéas* and *Eneit* expanded his funeral into courtly funerary architecture. Chronicle tradition converted the literary hero into an apparently excavated giant with an eternal lamp. This “discovery” served as material confirmation of Virgil for medieval audiences, despite lacking contemporary evidence.

Dante, *Monarchia* 2.3.7, invokes the duel in which Aeneas killed Turnus after seeing Pallas’s belt as part of Rome’s providential history. Pallas remains indirect but indispensable: his token licenses the decisive victory. **[E6]**

### Renaissance and early modern period

Humanist mythography, especially Boccaccio, catalogued Pallas as Evander’s son and transmitted the miraculous tomb. Illustrated Virgil editions depicted his meeting with Aeneas, combat, death, and funeral. **[E6]**

The figure rarely developed an autonomous cult or literary cycle. He remained subordinate to Aeneas and Turnus, but his death offered artists a concentrated subject of youthful beauty, paternal grief, heroic violence, and ambiguous vengeance.

### Modern period

From the nineteenth century onward, Pallas became central to scholarly debate about the *Aeneid*’s politics and ending. Optimistic or “Roman” readings treat his belt as evidence making Turnus’s execution just; pessimistic or “Harvard School” readings stress Aeneas’s rage, the sacrificial vocabulary, and the poem’s refusal of reconciliation. More synthetic readings argue that vengeance is simultaneously intelligible, culturally authorized, and morally terrible. **[E8]**

## 7. Modern reception and reinvention

### Literature and translation

Every major modern translation of the *Aeneid* necessarily reproduces Pallas’s role. Dryden’s *Aeneis* (1697), Conington’s verse translation (1866), C. Day Lewis (1952), Robert Fitzgerald (1983), Robert Fagles (2006), Sarah Ruden (2008/2021), and Shadi Bartsch (2020) differ especially in rendering the last words *Pallas… immolat*. Translation choices—“sacrifices,” “strikes,” “offers,” or “slays”—can soften or intensify the ritual violence. **[E8]**

Giovanni Pascoli’s Latin poetry, notably *Post occasum Urbis* (1907), reworked the medieval discovery of Pallas’s corpse and its lamp. This is a modern literary revival of a medieval invention, not new ancient evidence. **[E6/E8]**

### Visual art

- **Sebastian Brant’s illustrated Virgil (1502):** shows Pallas taking Aeneas by the hand and leading him to Evander. **[E6]**
- **Claude Lorrain, *The Arrival of Aeneas at Pallanteum* (1670s):** depicts the Book 8 meeting in an ideal landscape; surviving related drawings include Aeneas, Evander, and Pallas. **[E6]**
- **Jacques-Henri Sablet, *The Death of Pallas* (1778):** shows Turnus triumphant over the fallen youth and taking his belt; now in the Museum of Fine Arts, Houston. **[E6]**
- **Girodet, illustration of Evander mourning Pallas (1798):** emphasizes paternal lament in Book 11. **[E6]**

No securely identified ancient vase painting, wall painting, statue, or coin can presently be assigned to Virgil’s Pallas with confidence. Unlabeled ancient warriors should not be identified merely because one is young or fallen. **[E1; absence finding]**

### Opera, film, comics, and games

Pallas appears or is referenced in adaptations of the *Aeneid*, but he has not acquired an independent reception comparable to Aeneas, Dido, or Turnus. Operas centered on Dido commonly end before the Italian war and omit him. Later adaptations that compress Books 7–12 often reduce him to the youthful casualty whose belt motivates the finale. **[E8]**

No major modern commercial franchise has established a widely recognized alternative Pallas mythology. Online character lists sometimes conflate him with Athena, the Titan, or the asteroid; those are taxonomic errors rather than reception traditions. **[E9]**

### Political and nationalist use

Pallas belongs to the *Aeneid*’s Roman foundation narrative, and his death has therefore participated indirectly in imperial, Augustan, papal, Fascist, and national appropriations of Virgil. **[E8]** However, no evidence found in the dedicated search establishes an important modern nationalist or extremist cult specifically centered on Pallas son of Evander. Claims of such a movement would require evidence beyond the broader political reception of Aeneas and Rome.

His most important political function remains internal to Virgil: the death of a young allied Italian enables Aeneas to complete Rome’s foundational victory, but the final act looks disturbingly like private vengeance. Modern debate over Pallas is thus debate over whether the poem legitimates Roman violence, mourns its cost, or does both.

### Documented modern inventions

- A surviving ancient “Sword-Belt of Pallas”: **[E9]** none is known.
- A Palatine tomb securely excavated and scientifically identified as Pallas’s: **[E9]** none.
- An eternal lamp recovered from that tomb: **[E6/E7]**, a medieval marvel story.
- Identification of the Lavinium heroon as Pallas’s grave: **[E9]** it was associated with Aeneas or Anchises.
- Pallas as a demigod son of Heracles in Virgil: **[E9]** that belongs to the incompatible Polybian genealogy.
- Pallas resurrected or apotheosized after Book 12: **[E9]** unattested.
- Pallas as Aeneas’s explicitly stated lover: **[E9]** unattested. Modern queer readings may examine emotional or erotic coding, but the ancient poem calls him pupil, ally, guest-friend, and quasi-filial object of care rather than lover.

## 8. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Was Pallas a pre-Virgilian hero?

The answer depends on definition.

- A Pallas connected to Evander and the Palatine existed before Virgil if Dionysius accurately transmits Polybius. **[E1]**
- That Pallas was Evander’s grandson and Heracles’ son, not securely the warrior entrusted to Aeneas. **[E1/E3]**
- Virgil may have combined the Pallas name, Palatine etiologies, Evander’s household, Homeric young-warrior patterns, and the needs of the Italian war. **[E8]**
- Conington’s suggestion that the episode may echo Apollonius’s Lycus sending Dascylus with Jason is a literary hypothesis, not evidence for direct borrowing. **[E8]**

The prudent conclusion is that Virgil probably did not invent every element named Pallas, but the surviving record does not prove that his complete Pallas narrative existed before the *Aeneid*.

### Historicity

There is no evidence that Pallas was a historical prince whose biography Virgil preserved. Neither early inscription nor securely identified burial names him. **[E1]** Archaeology confirms settlement on the Palatine and extensive Greek contacts in archaic Latium; it does not verify Evander’s Arcadian colony or Pallas’s combat with Turnus.

Cornell, Grandazzi, Wiseman, and other historians of early Rome differ over how much historical memory Roman foundation legends may retain. Their debates concern processes—migration, Greek contact, cult transfer, ethnic memory, and antiquarian construction—not whether Virgil’s individual Pallas can be authenticated. **[E8]**

### Pallanteum and Palatine

The similarity of *Pallantium*, *Pallas*, and *Palatium* generated several ancient explanations. Modern scholars generally treat the narratives as etiology rather than reliable linguistic history. **[E8]** The direction of invention may have been:

```text
existing Roman place-name/ritual
          ↓
comparison with Arcadian Pallantium and Lycaean Pan
          ↓
Evander as Arcadian founder
          ↓
multiple Pallas/Pallantia genealogies
          ↓
Virgil’s young Pallas as emotional and political centerpiece
```

This is a reconstruction, not a demonstrated chronological chain.

### Hero cult

Earlier scholarship sometimes inferred a Pallas cult from his supposed tomb or from the general pattern of cult at heroic graves. Dionysius’s explicit denial is decisive against asserting an observed Roman cult in his time. **[E1]** A lost earlier cult remains logically possible but undocumented. The Arcadian statues do not establish worship of Evander’s son.

### The belt and the ending

Modern debate clusters around four positions:

1. **Just punishment:** Turnus forfeits mercy by killing and despoiling Pallas; the belt manifests guilt.
2. **Failure of *pietas*:** Aeneas nearly achieves mercy but surrenders to rage; *furiis accensus* and *immolat* darken the foundation.
3. **Sacrificial completion:** Aeneas acts as Pallas’s ritual avenger, fulfilling Evander’s demand and the poem’s economy of exchanged lives.
4. **Deliberate doubleness:** Roman justice and destructive fury cannot be cleanly separated; Virgil makes the foundation necessary and grievous.

Conte’s study of “Pallas’s belt,” Putnam’s analyses of Virgilian violence and art objects, and Horsfall’s and Tarrant’s commentaries are major reference points. **[E8]**

### The belt’s image

Virgil says it depicts young men foully murdered in bridal chambers on a single wedding night, engraved in gold by Clonus son of Eurytus. Ancient and modern readers identify the Danaids. Debate concerns why Pallas wears an image of criminal female violence and why Turnus appropriates it. Suggested functions include foreshadowing, the corruption of marriage into civil bloodshed, and an art object whose possessors fail to understand its warning. **[E8]**

### Pallas and Patroclus

The Homeric analogy is unmistakable but not exact:

| *Iliad* | *Aeneid* |
|---|---|
| Patroclus fights in Achilles’ place | Pallas fights under Aeneas’s guardianship |
| Hector kills and strips Patroclus | Turnus kills and strips Pallas |
| Achilles returns in wrath | Aeneas enters retaliatory *furor* |
| Achilles kills Hector | Aeneas kills Turnus |
| Hector’s body is abused, then ransomed | Turnus’s fate ends at death; no aftermath survives |

Pallas also resembles Sarpedon, Euphorbus, and other beautiful doomed epic youths. Lausus is his internal Virgilian double. **[E8]**

### Manuscripts and losses

The *Aeneid*’s Pallas narrative is textually secure in the main tradition. The greater evidentiary loss concerns pre-Virgilian sources: Polybius’s relevant passage survives only as quotation; early Roman annalists and antiquarians such as Fabius Pictor and Varro survive fragmentarily. Servius may preserve otherwise lost variants, but their original dates and contexts cannot always be recovered. **[E1]**

### Discredited or unsupported theories still circulating

- that archaeology has “proved” Evander and Pallas historical;
- that the Lapis Niger names either figure;
- that Lavinium’s heroon is Pallas’s grave;
- that Pausanias saw a statue of Virgil’s warrior;
- that the medieval giant corpse was an authentic excavation;
- that the name Pallas proves identity with Athena;
- that Pallas received a well-documented annual Roman cult.

All exceed the evidence. **[E9]**

Dumézil’s comparative work remains influential for archaic Roman religion generally, but a trifunctional Indo-European reading does not independently establish Pallas’s antiquity. Applying the model to this hero is interpretation, not attestation. **[E8]**

The evidentiary balance is unusually clear. Pallas son of Evander is securely a Virgilian mortal hero, subsequently canonical in Roman and European literature. A pre-Virgilian Pallas was already associated with Evander and the Palatine, but in a different genealogy. No ancient cult, securely identified tomb, inscription, or image of Evander’s warrior son is known. His enduring “relic” is literary: the belt taken by Turnus, made into the instrument by which the dead youth determines the final act of Rome’s national epic.
