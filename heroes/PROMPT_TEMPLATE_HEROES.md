# PROMPT_TEMPLATE_HEROES.md

The hero dossier prompt (Anthony, 2026-09-17: "let's have a collection of heroes from different
traditions", separate from the pantheon and from the atlas). tools/codex_dossier_set.py sends the
part after the first `---` with `[NAME]` substituted, then the per-item brief, then DELIVER.txt.
Adapted from the pantheon prompt: the subject is a hero of myth, epic or legend, so the dossier is
the documented record of the figure across texts, images and cult, not a biography.

---

[NAME]

Please conduct a comprehensive, source-driven research dossier on this hero of myth, epic or legend. The subject has no verifiable birth-to-death life, or has one so overgrown by legend that the legend is the subject; what I want is the complete documented record of the figure as it appears in texts, images, cult and belief, from earliest attestation to the present. The hero's existence in story is the subject; the evidence for it, poems, tablets, sagas, vases, tombs, shrines, testimony, is the material. Reconstruct both: what the traditions say the hero was and did, and what the historical record shows about when, where and how people said it.

Hold the evidentiary discipline throughout. For every significant claim, say which source carries it and from when. Distinguish: securely attested early material, later canonical elaboration, regional variant, learned systematization, syncretistic identification, medieval or early-modern accretion, folklore, modern scholarly reconstruction, and modern invention (including material that circulates as ancient but is demonstrably recent). Where sources conflict, on parentage, on a deed's outcome, on the hero's death, give the competing versions with their sources and dates rather than harmonizing them; the variants are the history. Where a historical person may stand behind the legend, treat that as a datable scholarly claim with its evidence and its critics, never as the story's truth. Do not sanitize: rape, infanticide, human sacrifice, betrayal, the political uses of the hero and modern nationalist or extremist appropriation are part of many records and must be stated plainly where documented.

Organize the dossier along these lines, adapting as the figure requires:

1. **Identification, Names and Epithets.** Who the hero is, in one dense orienting passage; then the onomastics: names in every relevant language and script with transliteration, etymologies including disputed ones, epithets and patronymics with their meanings and attestations, name variants across regions and periods, identifications with other figures (interpretatio, syncretism, medieval equations) each as a datable event with a source. State the hero's class as the traditions class it: mortal hero, demigod, heroized dead with cult, culture hero, saint-like figure, epic king.

2. **Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus.** The oldest securely dated evidence, inscription, vase, tablet, hymn, place-name, with what it does and does not say. Then the full corpus of primary texts in which the hero appears, each with language, date of composition (and of the surviving manuscripts where the gap matters) and the hero's role in it: epics, sagas, tragedies, chronicles, genealogies, scholia, folk songs. Treat these as the primary evidence, paired with the critical editions and the scholarship that dates them.

3. **The Story.** The hero's deeds and fate told fully, cycle by cycle: birth and parentage, upbringing, the exploits, the companions, the loves, the crimes, the death and what happened to the body. For each episode: earliest source and date, the significant variants with what each changes and where it appears, the development (early core versus later elaboration, borrowings between traditions), and its function where scholars have established one. Myth is the primary content here.

4. **Relations with Gods and Other Heroes.** Parentage and descent (divine and human), consorts, children, companions, rivals, enemies, patrons among the gods, and the hero's position in the tradition's genealogies, with conflicting trees laid side by side and dated. Then a dedicated subsection listing EVERY documented interaction between this hero and any OTHER named divine being or hero, one entry each: the other figure, the nature of the interaction in one line (combat, alliance, seduction, deception, judgment, gift, theft, rescue, punishment, guidance, joint appearance in a scene), the source and its date, and the evidence tier. Discrete, checkable claims, not narrative prose; do not force an interaction the texts do not state, and note plainly where a famous pairing is a later invention.

5. **Cult, Tomb and Ritual.** Whether the hero received cult and where: hero shrines, tombs and relics (the bones of Orestes at Tegea, the tomb of Achilles at Sigeion), sacrifices and games in the hero's name, the heroon and its archaeology, oracles, the festivals, the theophoric or commemorative naming, the political uses of the tomb; distinguish the archaeologically attested from the literary and the later.

6. **Historical Evolution of the Figure.** A periodized account from first attestation to the present: emergence, canonical shaping, the tradition's own reinterpretations (tragedy, Hellenistic and Roman reworking, medieval romance, the sagas' Christianization, the national epics of the nineteenth century), suppression or euhemerization, and survival in folk practice; make the periodization explicit.

7. **Modern Reception and Reinvention.** The hero's afterlife: literature, opera, painting, film, comics and games that shaped the modern image, with dates; nationalist and political appropriation; psychological and structuralist readings as datable interpretive traditions (Rank, Raglan, Campbell's monomyth and its critics, Dumézil, Nagy); commercial use; documented modern inventions identified as such.

8. **Historiography and Scholarly Debate.** How the figure has been studied: the major works and controversies (historicity, dating of the texts, the hero-cult debates, the oral-formulaic question where relevant, comparativist claims and their critics), the state of the evidence (manuscripts, finds, losses), the discoveries that changed the picture, and discredited theories that still circulate.

9. **Chronology.** A compact timeline of the hero's ATTESTATION and cultic history, not a life: first evidence, key texts, shrines founded and destroyed, rediscoveries, editions, revivals, down to the present, each entry anchored to its evidence.

10. **Sources.**

Search broadly and deeply; prefer the primary texts in critical editions and reliable translations, the epigraphic and archaeological record, and academic scholarship over encyclopedia summaries. Search in the source languages where feasible and in the modern languages of the scholarship, not only English. Look for obscure variants, local cults and details that disappear from summaries. Write without editorial overlay; report what is attested, by whom and when. Favor documentary presentation. Detail over brevity.
