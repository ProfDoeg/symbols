# Cari and Zapana: Research Dossier

> **Traditional class:** rival mortal *sinchis*, *curacas* or regional lords—Cari of Chucuito/Lupaqa and Zapana of Hatuncolla/Qolla—not gods or demigods. Cieza also presents their names as recurring dynastic or official names, so several bearers may have been compressed into a single legendary pair.
>
> **Earliest secure attestation:** Pedro de Cieza de León’s *Crónica del Perú*. Part I, chapters 100–102, was printed in Seville in 1553; Part II, chapters 4 and 41–43, was composed around 1550 but remained unpublished until the nineteenth century. This is evidence for traditions circulating in the central Andes in the mid-sixteenth century, not contemporary documentation of the alleged pre-Inca events.
>
> **Historical finding:** archaeology independently confirms fortified, frequently violent societies in the northern Lake Titicaca basin during approximately 1100–1450 CE, especially after about 1275/1300. It does not identify a particular fortress, grave, artifact, or destruction layer with either Cari or Zapana.

## Evidentiary key

- **[E1: securely attested early record]** A sixteenth-century text or archaeological context securely dated as evidence for what was then reported or practised.
- **[E2: early-colonial variant]** A sixteenth- or seventeenth-century narrative elaboration, contradiction, or learned arrangement.
- **[E3: regional variant]** A tradition attributed to a particular locality or ethnic constituency.
- **[E4: learned systematization]** A colonial or modern attempt to reconcile separate accounts into a coherent history.
- **[E5: folklore]** A demonstrably collected oral tradition.
- **[E6: modern scholarly reconstruction]** An inference from texts, archaeology, linguistics, or comparative analysis.
- **[E7: modern invention or unsupported circulation]** A claim not found in the primary record or contradicted by its chronology.
- **[Ø: absence of evidence]** A specifically investigated connection for which no documentation was located.

## 1. Identification, names and epithets

### Orientation

Cari and Zapana belong to colonial accounts of the fragmented political landscape around Lake Titicaca before and during Inca expansion. In Cieza’s fullest version, Zapana rules from **Hatuncolla**, while Cari, established at **Chucuito**, commands the settlements of Hilave/Ilave, Xulli/Juli, Cepita/Zepita and Pomata. Both seek to become sole lord of the Collao. They solicit the intervention of Viracocha Inca; before the Inca arrives, Cari defeats and kills Zapana at Paucarcolla, then makes a ritual compact with Viracocha. [E1] Cieza expressly says that other men bore the name Zapana and later employs both Cari and Zapana again for leaders of a subsequent Collao rebellion. The safest identification is consequently “remembered ruler names or titles attached to several cycles,” not two securely individualized historical biographies. [Cieza, Part II, chapters 41–43 and 53](https://readingroo.ms/2/5/2/5/25255/25255-h/25255-h.htm).

### Dynastic or personal names?

Cieza’s wording is decisive: under Viracocha Inca, Hatuncolla was held by “un señor llamado Çapana, **como otro que hobo deste nombre**”—“a lord called Zapana, as there had been another of this name.” Part I similarly says Zapana and descendants ruled Hatuncolla. Later, Cari and Zapana appear together again in a rebellion after Inca conquest, although the Zapana of Paucarcolla had supposedly been killed. [E1] Either Cieza preserved several generations, recurrent offices or ancestral names, or his narrative conflated chronologically distinct traditions.

Garcilaso makes recurrence explicit: Cari and Chipana were names inherited by successors “to preserve the memory” of valorous ancestors. [E2] Modern scholarship therefore commonly treats Cari and Zapana as titles, dynastic designations, or recurring ancestral names. [E6] No pre-Hispanic alphabetic or epigraphic text confirms that interpretation.

### Ethnic and political identifications

- **Cari as Lupaqa lord of Chucuito:** explicit or strongly implied in Cieza’s geography; standard in subsequent ethnohistory. [E1/E6]
- **Zapana as Qolla lord of Hatuncolla:** explicit in Cieza. [E1]
- **Zapana = Ruqui Çapana:** a reasonable comparison of Cieza and Betanzos, not an explicit equation in either source. [E6]
- **Zapana = Chuchi Cápac/Colla Cápac:** common modern harmonization, but Sarmiento gives a different name, opponent and conquest sequence. [E4/E6]
- **Cari and Zapana as “kings of an Aymara empire”:** modern systematization. The sources call them *señor*, *sinchi*, *curaca*, *capitán* and, polemically, *tirano*. Archaeology favors competing and variably centralized polities rather than a securely demonstrated unified “Aymara Empire.” [E6/E7]
- **Tiwanaku rulers:** unsupported. Tiwanaku’s state florescence and collapse substantially predate the late pre-Inca fortification horizon and the events assigned to Cari and Zapana. [E7]

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### Earliest evidence

**Cieza, Part I, chapters 100–102 (printed 1553).** [E1] Cieza reports that “many Indians of these Collas” said two great lords, Zapana and Cari, conquered numerous *pucaras*. One of them entered Lake Titicaca and exterminated white, bearded inhabitants of its principal island; Cieza’s Part II assigns that deed specifically to Cari. They then fought the Canas and Canchis before turning their arms against one another and seeking Viracocha Inca’s favor. Chapter 102 records that Zapana and descendants had ruled Hatuncolla. It does **not** supply absolute dates, birth traditions, genealogy, tombs, divine ancestry or cult.

**Cieza, Part II, chapters 4 and 41–43 (composed c.1550; nineteenth-century first publication).** [E1] This is the fullest narrative. Chapter 4 separates an earlier Zapana, conqueror of warrior women near Chungara, from Cari’s migration from the valley of Coquimbo and his massacre of the islanders. Chapters 41–43 tell the rivalry, embassy, Paucarcolla battle, Zapana’s death and Cari’s compact with Viracocha. [Spanish text](https://www.artehistoria.com/contextos/capitulo-xli-3?libro=88130).

The distinction between composition and publication matters. Although Part II became widely available only centuries later, its manuscript testimony belongs to the mid-sixteenth century.

### Requested corpora in which direct attestation was not established

- **Guaman Poma, Murúa and Santa Cruz Pachacuti:** valuable for Collao peoples, Andean ages, Inca conquest and fortified warfare, but no securely verified independent Cari–Zapana episode was found. [Ø]
- **Cabello Valboa’s Naymlap tradition and the Anónimo de Trujillo’s Tacaynamo material:** north-coast traditions about different founding rulers. They do not attest Cari or Zapana. [Ø]
- **Huarochirí Manuscript:** a Quechua ritual-mythic corpus from Huarochirí, not the Titicaca political cycle; no Cari–Zapana episode. [Ø]
- **The *Ollantay* drama:** neither the Valdez nor the Justo Pastor Sahuaraura textual tradition supplies a Cari–Zapana cycle. Its colonial versus pre-Hispanic compositional debate is irrelevant to their first attestation. [Ø]
- **Puquio Inkarrí accounts collected in the 1950s by José María Arguedas and discussed by Alejandro Ortiz Rescaniere:** important evidence for twentieth-century Andean messianic narrative, but no documented genealogical or narrative continuation of Cari and Zapana. [Ø]
- **Argentine northwest “cordillera versions”:** targeted searches found no properly collected oral version naming both figures. Modern surname occurrences and generic Cordilleran folklore are not evidence of a Cari–Zapana tradition. [Ø]
- **Alberto Flores Galindo, *Buscando un Inca*:** indispensable for the modern history of Andean utopia and Inkarrí, but not a primary source for Cari or Zapana. Later commentary sometimes places the pair inside his broader account of Andean political memory; that does not create a Cari–Zapana reception cycle. [E6/Ø]

## 3. The story

### A. Rise of Zapana and the warrior women

Cieza’s Part II, chapter 4 says that a “most valiant lord” named Zapana arose in the Collao and subjected many peoples. Near Chungara, in the Canas region, women living apart from husbands, bearing arms and founding their own settlements, resisted him behind fortifications and breastworks. Zapana captured and killed them, extinguishing their name. Cieza compares them with classical Amazons and immediately cautions that only God knew whether the report was true. [E1/E3]

This episode is not securely connected to the later Zapana killed at Paucarcolla. Cieza’s own reference to repeated Zapana bearers makes an earlier namesake possible. Classical “Amazon” language may be Cieza’s interpretive frame for a local story rather than its indigenous category. [E6]

### B. Cari’s arrival and island war

In the same chapter, Cieza says Cari left the valley of **Coquimbo**, reached Chucuito, founded or ordered new settlements, crossed to Titicaca’s island by raft and exterminated its “white and bearded” people. Part I, chapter 100 leaves ambiguous which of the two lords performed the island deed; Part II assigns it to Cari. [E1/E3]

The story documents a sixteenth-century account of migration, conquest and ethnic replacement. It does not document Europeans, Phoenicians, Vikings, Tiwanaku priest-colonists or another “white” population in pre-Columbian Titicaca. Attempts to use it as proof of transoceanic Caucasian settlement—most visibly in twentieth-century diffusionist “white gods” literature—are modern speculative appropriation. [E7]

No archaeological trail connects the Coquimbo valley to a migration led by Cari. Archaeologists explicitly note the absence of material and linguistic evidence adequate to verify such a movement. [E6]

### C. Expansion through the southwest lakeshore

Cieza’s Part II, chapter 41 reports that Cari made Chucuito his seat and had Hilave, Juli, Zepita and Pomata populated by his command. After sacrifices “to his gods”—rendered polemically by Cieza as “or demons”—he marched against the Canas, defeated them with many deaths, and defeated them again at Lurocachi. [E1]

These are etiological claims about Lupaqa political geography. Colonial inspection records confirm the later importance of Chucuito and its lakeshore towns, but they do not prove that a single man named Cari founded them. [E1/E6]

### D. Rivalry with Zapana

Hearing of Cari’s successes, Zapana gathered allies and vassals to intercept him and seize his booty. Cari learned of the plan and withdrew by another road to Chucuito. Each then resolved to destroy the other and become the one lord of the Collao. [E1]

Both sent ambassadors and rich presents to Viracocha Inca in Cusco. After consulting an oracle, the Inca privately promised help to Cari while telling Zapana merely that they would discuss friendship when he entered the Collao. [E1] The narrative presents Inca power as an external arbiter manipulating a regional rivalry.

### E. Paucarcolla and Zapana’s death

Before the Inca could join Cari, Zapana marched from Hatuncolla to attack him. The armies met near **Paucarcolla**. Cieza claims 150 *guarangas* and more than 30,000 dead; those numbers belong to epic-political narration and cannot be accepted as demographic counts. Cari won; Zapana was killed; Cari seized his camp and wealth and returned to Chucuito. [E1]

No excavated mass grave, named tomb, weapon deposit or securely dated destruction at Paucarcolla has been identified as evidence of this particular battle. [Ø]

### F. Cari and Viracocha’s compact

Viracocha learned that the battle had already ended. Cieza says he was publicly pleased but privately regretted losing the advantage offered by two divided rivals. At Chucuito he offered Cari one of his daughters in marriage. Cari declined because he was old and asked that she be married to a young man instead, while accepting Viracocha as lord and ally. [E1]

They drank from a great gold vessel, placed it on a smooth stone as witness to their agreement, reverenced the Sun, performed a *taqui/areyto*, and deposited the cup in a temple among objects witnessing royal oaths. [E1] This is the only cultic-ritual episode directly involving Cari, but it is a diplomatic rite, not worship of Cari.

### G. Garcilaso’s peaceful variant

Garcilaso relocates the episode much earlier in Inca history and calls Zapana **Chipana**. Cari and Chipana ask the Inca to arbitrate their dispute, visit him peacefully and accept his settlement. The Inca absorbs their lands without Cieza’s lethal battle. Garcilaso explicitly criticizes Cieza’s chronology. [E2]

The versions cannot be harmonized:

- **Cieza:** Viracocha favors Cari; Cari kills Zapana; a strategic compact follows.
- **Garcilaso:** an earlier Inca mediates; both lords survive and submit voluntarily.
- **Sarmiento:** Pachacuti directly defeats Chuchi Cápac of Hatuncolla.
- **Betanzos:** Pachacuti confronts Ruqui Çapana, a lord claiming parity with the Inca.

These are rival constructions of Inca expansion: alliance and exploitation of division; beneficent arbitration; heroic single combat; and imperial contest between peer sovereigns.

### H. The later rebellion

Cieza later says Cari, Zapana, Humalla, the lord of Azángaro and others met at Hatuncolla and Chucuito, drank from one vessel, placed it in a temple, killed Inca governors and *orejones*, and launched rebellion. Túpac Inca defeated the Collas at Pucara and captured Cari, rebuking him for violating the peace made by his “grandfather” Viracocha. [E1]

This cannot involve the already dead Zapana unless:

1. Zapana was a recurring dynastic name or title;
2. Cieza conflated separate chronologies; or
3. the death episode and rebellion derive from incompatible traditions.

Cieza’s text itself supports the first two possibilities but does not resolve them.

### I. Fate of the bodies

- **Zapana:** Cieza says he died at Paucarcolla but says nothing about disposal, burial, mummification, relics or a tomb. [E1/Ø]
- **Cari:** the early victorious Cari survives the Chucuito pact; no death narrative is given. A later Cari is captured and sent toward Cusco for punishment, but his ultimate fate is unstated. [E1/Ø]
- Claims that Zapana’s identifiable remains lie at Sillustani are modern assertions unsupported by an inscribed burial, documented excavation, or primary chronicler. [E7]

## 4. Relations with gods and other heroes

### Genealogical record

No source gives either man a divine parent, human parent, named sibling, named spouse or named child. Viracocha’s proposed daughter is unnamed and Cari refuses the marriage. Garcilaso supplies ancestral succession in the abstract—earlier Cari and Chipana bearers—but no family tree. [Ø]

Betanzos’s Ruqui Çapana calls himself “son of the Sun.” If he is related to the Zapana tradition, this is a royal sacral title rather than a narrated biological union, and the identification remains conjectural. [E1/E6]

## 5. Cult, tomb and ritual

### Cult

No chronicler says Cari or Zapana received sacrifice, prayer, a priesthood, oracle, annual festival, named shrine, divine image or formal hero cult. [Ø] Their case consequently differs from figures such as Viracocha/Tiqsi Wiraqocha or localized *huacas*.

The recurrent use of their names may imply ancestral or dynastic commemoration, but that is not proof of cult. Garcilaso says successors inherited the names to preserve ancestral memory; he does not say the ancestors were worshipped. [E2]

### Rituals involving them

Two political covenants use a vessel placed in a temple:

1. Cari and Viracocha’s Chucuito compact: drinking, solar reverence, dance/song and deposit of a gold cup. [E1]
2. The later Collao rebels’ collective oath: drinking from one vessel and depositing it among sacred things. [E1]

These passages document the sacralization of political agreements. They do not make Cari or Zapana the objects of ritual.

### Sillustani and tomb claims

Sillustani is a major funerary landscape associated with late Qolla and Inca-period elite mortuary practice. Monumental *chullpas*, collective burials, ceramics and architectural phases establish ancestor-focused mortuary activity in the Hatuncolla region. [E1 archaeological context]

No surviving inscription names Zapana; no controlled excavation has identified “Zapana’s body”; and Cieza never says Zapana was taken from Paucarcolla to Sillustani. Tourism and surname websites that declare a specific Zapana burial there are therefore [E7], though they build upon the legitimate general association between Sillustani, Hatuncolla and Qolla elites.

The distinction is:

- **Archaeologically attested:** elite funerary towers and ancestral mortuary activity.
- **Historically plausible:** rulers of the Hatuncolla polity were among people commemorated in the regional mortuary landscape.
- **Unproved:** a particular tower or skeleton is Zapana’s.
- **Modern invention:** confident claims that his identified remains have been found.

### Political use of ancestors

Qolla and Lupaqa mortuary monuments visibly asserted descent, land rights and elite continuity. That broader interpretation supplies an appropriate political context for recurring ruler names but cannot identify the individual occupants or turn every *chullpa* into a royal tomb. [E6]

## 6. Historical evolution

### c.1100–1275: post-Tiwanaku regionalization

After Tiwanaku’s collapse, political life in the basin reorganized into numerous communities and regional groupings. Early hilltop refuge use existed, but intensive fortification was not an immediate, basin-wide consequence of Tiwanaku’s fall. [E1 archaeological record]

### c.1275/1300–1450: fortified landscape

Survey and radiocarbon evidence show a sharp proliferation of *pucaras*, defensive walls and hilltop settlements after about 1275/1300. Conflict, collective defense and factional competition structured the northern basin. Large sites existed, but the settlement pattern does not require a single centralized Qolla kingdom. [E1/E6] Pucarani, for example, is the largest known Late Intermediate Period site in the Qolla region and has been dated broadly to the late pre-Inca/Inca transition, yet it carries no inscription connecting it to Zapana. [Pucarani project](https://www.elizabetharkush.com/past-projects/pucarani).

### Fifteenth century: probable historical setting

Inca expansion into Collasuyu met powerful Titicaca polities. The chronological placement, ruler names and number of campaigns vary radically among chroniclers. A conflict involving Qolla, Lupaqa and Cusco interests is historically plausible; the exact sequence “Cari kills Zapana immediately before Viracocha arrives” is a remembered narrative, not an independently dated event. [E6]

### c.1550–1572: first written formations

Cieza records local Collao reports and Cusco *orejón* traditions; Betanzos records a Quechua-Cusco account; Sarmiento writes under Viceroy Toledo’s program to depict Inca rule as conquest and tyranny. Their political viewpoints differ. [E1] These decades create the documentary Cari–Zapana dossier.

### 1609–1653: moral and imperial harmonization

Garcilaso transforms violent rivalry into willing arbitration by an exemplary Inca and explains the repeated names as ancestral succession. Cobo subsequently systematizes Inca history from earlier written and oral materials. [E2/E4]

### Nineteenth century: national historiography and rediscovery

The belated publication and translation of Cieza’s Part II made its detailed narrative newly influential. Nineteenth-century historians treated Cari and Zapana increasingly as identifiable monarchs in a linear pre-Inca political history. Rulers, “kingdoms” and battles were often described with European monarchical vocabulary. [E4]

### Twentieth century: ethnohistory and archaeology

Ethnohistorians—including John Murra, María Rostworowski, Catherine Julien, John Hyslop and others—situated the figures within Qolla–Lupaqa rivalry, ecological complementarity, colonial provincial organization and Inca expansion. Rostworowski warned that Cieza repeatedly uses Cari and Zapana across different reigns and proposed that they may be regional ruler titles. [E6]

Archaeological work complicated the “two centralized kingdoms” model. Excavated and surveyed fortifications strongly support conflict but also reveal a politically fractured landscape of peer communities rather than a straightforward territorial state ruled from one capital. [E6] A useful synthesis is Ilana Abraham’s review of the Late Intermediate occupation at Pukara, which contrasts chronicles’ unified Qolla polity with archaeological arguments for persistent factional competition. [Abraham chapter](https://pukara.org/wp-content/uploads/2014/06/Abraham_2012_Advances3_ch15.pdf).

### Twenty-first century: educational canon and digital legend

School materials and tourism pages commonly reduce the story to: “Zapana, king of the Qollas, fought Cari, king of the Lupaqas; Cari won with Inca support.” [E4] Online expansions add unsupported identifications with Tiwanaku survivors, Manco Cápac’s migration, a specific Sillustani grave, exact regnal dates or a unitary “Aymara Empire.” [E7]

## 7. Modern reception and reinvention

### Scholarship and educational narrative

Cari and Zapana have had little independent literary, operatic, cinematic, comic-book or game reception. Their principal modern afterlife is historiographical and educational: maps and textbooks use them to personify Qolla–Lupaqa rivalry. [E4]

Some modern Bolivian and Peruvian historical narratives elevate them as rulers of a large pre-Inca Aymara political order. This can function as a corrective to older Cusco-centered histories, but a corrective political purpose does not itself establish centralized imperial institutions or exact territorial boundaries. [E6/E7]

### Archaeological tourism

Tours of Sillustani and Titicaca sometimes connect Zapana directly with named *chullpas* or claim his remains lie there. Those presentations blend real Qolla mortuary archaeology with an unattested personal identification. [E7]

### “White gods” diffusionism

Cari’s massacre of “white and bearded” islanders was recruited into twentieth-century claims that Europeans or other transoceanic settlers civilized the Andes before Columbus. Cieza reports a tradition but supplies no material evidence, dates or ethnicity for the islanders. Archaeology does not validate the racial-diffusionist inference. [E7]

### Inkarrí and Andean utopia

Flores Galindo’s analysis of the “Andean utopia,” Arguedas’s Puquio collections and Ortiz Rescaniere’s work on Inkarrí are relevant to how conquered Andean sovereignty was remembered and reimagined. They do not document Cari or Zapana as forms of Inkarrí. Making the pair messianic returning kings is a modern extrapolation. [Ø/E7]

### Nationalist or extremist appropriation

Targeted searches found regionalist and Aymara-national historical retellings, but no well-documented extremist organization or sustained state cult centered specifically on Cari and Zapana. [Ø] Their most visible political function is as symbols of pre-Inca Aymara sovereignty and of the consequences of internal division. Claims of a fully documented, unified empire ruled successively by “King Zapana” and “King Cari” should be labeled modern nationalist systematization rather than ancient record. [E4/E7]

### Commercial use

Tourism, genealogy and surname sites reproduce the figures, especially Zapana, as an ancestral “king.” No evidence was found for a substantial commercial franchise or globally influential modern adaptation. [Ø]

## 8. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Historicity

**Minimal position:** the mid-sixteenth-century sources probably preserve memories of real political conflict among Qolla, Lupaqa and expanding Inca powers. The geography—Hatuncolla, Chucuito, Paucarcolla, lakeshore settlements—and the archaeological warfare horizon support that context. [E6]

**Strong historicist position:** Cari and Zapana were individual fifteenth-century rulers whose actions occurred substantially as Cieza narrates. Its evidence is Cieza’s proximity to indigenous informants and the narrative’s realistic political geography. Its weaknesses are recurrence of the names, impossible internal chronology, epic numbers and conflict with Betanzos, Sarmiento and Garcilaso. [E6]

**Title/dynasty position:** Cari and Zapana were recurrent titles or ancestral names for Lupaqa and Qolla rulers. Cieza’s “another of this name,” his later reuse of both names, and Garcilaso’s explicit statement about inherited names support it. The objection is that no indigenous administrative or epigraphic record independently defines either as an office. [E6]

**Narrative-type position:** the pair may condense a political pattern—two regional powers, rivalry, appeal to Cusco, Inca divide-and-rule—rather than preserve a single event. This best explains the variants but risks dissolving genuine personal memories into structure. [E6]

### Chronology of Inca conquest

- Cieza connects the duel and alliance with Viracocha Inca, followed by later conquest and rebellion.
- Sarmiento assigns the decisive defeat of Hatuncolla’s Chuchi Cápac to Pachacuti.
- Betanzos likewise centers Pachacuti but names Ruqui Çapana.
- Garcilaso places Cari and Chipana’s submission much earlier and peaceful.

No independent absolute chronology resolves these accounts. Sarmiento’s own regnal chronology contains demonstrable inconsistencies; exact modern dates such as “1438” are retrospective scholarly schemes, not dates found in the Cari–Zapana narratives. [E6]

### Was there a Qolla “kingdom”?

Colonial writers describe powerful lords and large subject territories. Archaeological research, however, finds fortified communities, variable ceramic distributions and peer-polity competition. The label “kingdom” is convenient but may overstate administrative centralization. Aymara *señoríos* should be treated as a spectrum of regional authority, not as equivalent to European kingdoms. [E6]

Research on Pacajes similarly cautions that the named Colla, Lupaqa and Pacajes groupings may have consisted of noncentralized or internally competing polities. [Titicaca archaeology synthesis](https://escholarship.org/content/qt194464mn/qt194464mn_noSplash_0fd94f4a15aa40ab9d748c9154a4bd52.pdf).

### Oral tradition and colonial mediation

Cieza identifies different informant frames: “many Indians of these Collas” and Cusco *orejones*. His categories “tyrant,” “demon,” “Amazon” and “barbarous” are colonial-Christian/classical overlays. [E1] Sarmiento wrote for Toledo and explicitly sought evidence useful to the Spanish crown’s case that Inca authority was tyrannical. Garcilaso, conversely, idealized Inca governance and voluntary incorporation. The differences are political historiography, not noise to be harmonized away.

### Archaeological correlation

Elizabeth Arkush’s work shows that most substantial northern-basin *pucara* building postdates about 1275/1300 and that defenses were repeatedly strengthened. Slingstones and skeletal trauma independently attest violence. [E1] This makes the warlike setting credible but does not prove named battles.

At Pukara, archaeology does not neatly match a chronicle’s image of one unified Qolla state. Scholars have therefore contrasted centralized ethnohistoric narratives with factional archaeological landscapes. [E6]

### Discredited or weak theories still circulating

- Cari’s island enemies were Europeans, Israelites, Vikings or “white gods.” [E7]
- Cari led a demonstrable mass migration from Chilean Coquimbo to Titicaca. [E7]
- Zapana founded or ruled Tiwanaku. [E7]
- The Lagarto *chullpa* or another Sillustani tower is Zapana’s identified tomb. [E7]
- Chuchi Cápac, Ruqui Çapana, Zapana and every later “Zapana” are certainly one man. [E7]
- Cari and Zapana were themselves gods or culture heroes worshipped throughout the Andes. [E7]
- Their story appears in the Huarochirí Manuscript, *Ollantay* or Puquio Inkarrí traditions. [E7]

| Date | Documentary or archaeological event |
|---|---|
| c.1100 CE | Collapse-era transition after Tiwanaku; beginning of the Late Intermediate Period. No Cari or Zapana attestation. |
| c.1275/1300–1450 | Major proliferation of fortified settlements in the northern Titicaca basin; independent evidence for endemic conflict. |
| Fifteenth century, broadly | Likely period of Inca intrusion and annexation in the Titicaca basin; exact ruler sequence disputed. |
| c.1550 | Cieza composes Part II: Zapana and warrior women; Cari’s migration and island massacre; embassy, Paucarcolla battle and Chucuito pact; later rebellion. Earliest surviving detailed record. |
| 1551 | Betanzos records Ruqui Çapana of Hatuncolla opposing Pachacuti. |
| 1553 | Cieza publishes Part I, the earliest printed Cari–Zapana attestation. |
| 1555 | Zárate publishes his history, part of the early corpus used for comparison of regional lordships and titles. |
| 1572 | Sarmiento records Chuchi Cápac/Colla Cápac, not Cari and Zapana, as Pachacuti’s principal Hatuncolla opponent. |
| 1609 | Garcilaso publishes the Cari–Chipana peaceful-arbitration variant and explicitly explains the names as inherited. |
| 1653 | Cobo completes *Historia del Nuevo Mundo*, further systematizing Inca conquest traditions. |
| 1880s | Cieza’s Part II enters wide print circulation; its detailed story becomes much more accessible to modern historians. |
| Late nineteenth–early twentieth century | National histories increasingly present the pair as two identifiable pre-Inca monarchs. |
| Mid-twentieth century | Ethnohistory reframes them within Qolla–Lupaqa political organization; diffusionists misuse the “white and bearded” islanders. |
| 1970s–2000s | Settlement archaeology and Lupaqa/Qolla ethnohistory test the “two kingdoms” model; no personal artifact or tomb is identified. |
| 2005–2020 | Arkush and related projects date and analyze northern Titicaca fortifications, confirming late pre-Inca conflict while emphasizing political fragmentation and collective defense. |
| Twenty-first century | School, tourism and online texts circulate a simplified duel; unsupported Sillustani-tomb, Tiwanaku and “Aymara Empire” claims proliferate. |
| Present | Cari and Zapana remain historically plausible remembered ruler-names embedded in incompatible colonial traditions; neither an individual biography nor a cult can be reconstructed securely. |

## 10. Sources and source assessment

### Primary sources

- **Pedro de Cieza de León**, *Crónica del Perú*, Part I, chapters 100–102, printed 1553; Part II, chapters 4, 41–43 and 53–54, composed c.1550. Foundational source. Cieza distinguishes Collao and Cusco informants but writes through colonial categories.
- **Juan de Betanzos**, *Suma y narración de los incas*, 1551, especially the account of Ruqui Çapana. Important independent nomenclature and Pachacuti-centered chronology.
- **Pedro Sarmiento de Gamboa**, *Historia de los incas*, 1572, chapters 37–38. Toledo-era official history; preserves Chuchi Cápac/Colla Cápac tradition.
- **Inca Garcilaso de la Vega**, *Comentarios reales*, 1609, Book II, chapter 20. Later but explicit Cari–Chipana variant and dynastic-name explanation.
- **Agustín de Zárate**, *Historia del descubrimiento y conquista del Perú*, 1555/1577. Comparative early-colonial historiography.
- **Bernabé Cobo**, *Historia del Nuevo Mundo*, completed 1653. Late colonial synthesis, not an independent contemporaneous report of the alleged events.

### Archaeology and modern scholarship

- Elizabeth Arkush, research on Qolla *pucaras*, warfare and defensive community.
- Ilana Abraham, “The Late Intermediate Period Occupation,” reviewing the divergence between chronicle-based unification and archaeological political fragmentation.
- Titicaca Basin archaeological syntheses concerning Colla, Lupaqa and Pacajes hilltop settlement.
- John Hyslop, archaeological survey of the Lupaqa region.
- María Rostworowski, analyses of Pachacuti, Collao conquest and recurrent ruler names.
- Catherine Julien, critical studies of Inca historiography and provincial organization.
- John Murra, Lupaqa ethnohistory and the “vertical archipelago.”
- Alberto Flores Galindo, *Buscando un Inca*, relevant to later Andean political memory but not an attestation of Cari and Zapana.
- José María Arguedas and Alejandro Ortiz Rescaniere, Puquio Inkarrí collections and analysis; investigated here as a possible reception thread, with no direct Cari–Zapana connection found.

### Final assessment

The securely documented object is not a recoverable life of either man but a layered archive:

1. a mid-sixteenth-century Collao/Cusco memory of rival regional powers;
2. several incompatible conquest chronologies;
3. recurrent ruler names or titles;
4. a late-pre-Hispanic archaeological landscape genuinely marked by fortification and violence;
5. later efforts to transform those materials into a linear history of two kings.

The minimal defensible conclusion is that Cari and Zapana encode remembered Qolla–Lupaqa political rivalry during the age of Inca expansion. Everything more specific—their exact dates, single identities, ancestry, tombs, Tiwanaku connections and modern racialized interpretations—requires labels ranging from scholarly reconstruction to modern invention.
