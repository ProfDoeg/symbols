# Ollantay: Research Dossier

> **Traditional class:** mortal military hero and rebel lord (*apu*) of Antisuyu in a late-colonial Quechua heroic drama; not a demigod, culture hero, epic king, or archaeologically attested heroized dead.  
> **Earliest secure attestation:** the late eighteenth-century textual tradition of the Quechua play *Ollantay*. César Itier dates its composition specifically to **1782** and attributes it to the priest Antonio Valdez; older dates and a pre-Hispanic archetype remain hypotheses. No sixteenth- or seventeenth-century chronicle securely names Ollantay as Pachacútec’s general.

## Evidentiary notation

- **[SEA] Secure early attestation:** evidence contemporary with, or close to, the earliest recoverable Ollantay tradition.
- **[LCE] Late-colonial/early-modern accretion:** eighteenth- or early nineteenth-century manuscript, performance tradition, or interpretation.
- **[CCE] Colonial canonical elaboration:** the story as transmitted by the principal Quechua drama.
- **[RV] Regional or textual variant.**
- **[LS] Learned systematization:** antiquarian, philological, literary, or historical construction.
- **[F] Folklore:** orally or popularly transmitted material documented after the colonial period.
- **[MSR] Modern scholarly reconstruction.**
- **[MI] Modern invention or adaptation.**
- **[NEG] Negative finding:** absence from the relevant record; not proof that no oral antecedent existed.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### Identification

**[CCE]** Ollantay is the protagonist of a three-act Quechua drama set retrospectively in the reigns of the historical Incas Pachakutiq/Pachacútec and Tupaq Yupanqui. The play makes him the exceptionally successful but non-royal general of Antisuyu. He secretly marries or forms a sexual union with Pachakutiq’s daughter Kusi Quyllur, fathers Ima Sumaq, is refused dynastic recognition by the Inca, rebels at Tampu, defeats an imperial army, rules in defiance for approximately ten years, is captured by Rumi Ñawi’s stratagem, and is pardoned and restored by Tupaq Yupanqui. The surviving drama therefore treats him as a **mortal rebel hero**, not as a god.

**[NEG]** There is no independently dated Inca-period inscription, quipu text, tomb, image, genealogy, shrine, sacrifice, or chronicle identifying such a general. The play’s historical sovereigns and geography are real; that does not independently authenticate its protagonist or plot.

### Forms of the name

- **Ollantay / Ullantay** — the usual title and protagonist’s name in modern Spanish and normalized Quechua usage.
- **Ollanta / Ullanta** — common nineteenth-century editorial form, including Clements Markham’s *Ollanta: An Ancient Ynca Drama* (1871).
- **Apu Ollantay / Apu Ollanta** — “Lord Ollantay”; *apu* is a title of high authority and can also designate a powerful sacred mountain, but the title does not make this dramatic character a deity.
- **Ollantaytambo / Ullantay Tampu** — modern Spanish and Quechua forms of the place-name; *tampu* means a lodging, administrative station, or settlement.

The nineteenth-century editions employ highly variable Spanish-based spellings: *Cusi Coyllur*, *Cusi Ccoyllur*, *Yma Sumac*, *Rumi-ñahui*, *Uillac Umu*, and so forth. Normalized modern Quechua commonly gives **Kusi Quyllur**, **Ima Sumaq**, **Rumi Ñawi**, and **Willka Umu**.

### Etymology

**[MSR]** The strongest modern linguistic analysis is Rodolfo Cerrón-Palomino’s “Ollantay: topónimo antes que antropónimo” (2006). He argues that *Ollantay* was originally an **Aymara place-name**, not a Quechua personal name: approximately reconstructed *ulla-nta-wi*, “place from which one looks downward,” hence “lookout/height/overlook.” On this account, phonological adaptation under Quechua produced *Ullantawi > Ullantaw > Ullantay*. The literary author then derived the hero’s anthroponym from the place-name, reversing the popular explanation that the fortress was named after the man. Cerrón-Palomino expressly concludes that the toponym cannot serve as proof of the hero’s historicity ([article and linguistic argument](https://revistas.pucp.edu.pe/index.php/lexis/article/download/2218/2148)).

**[LS—discredited or weak proposals]**

- José Sebastián Barranca (1868) attempted to associate *ulla* with sexual love through *ullu*, “penis.” Pacheco Zegarra and Cerrón-Palomino rejected this as grammatically and pragmatically untenable.
- Fernández Nodal derived the name from *Colla/Qulla* plus Quechua suffixes; this likewise fails regular phonological and grammatical analysis.
- Vicente Fidel López connected it arbitrarily with *Anta*.
- Durand proposed Aymara/“Quechumara” *sulla*, “dew”; Cerrón-Palomino rejects the required sound manipulation.
- Pacheco Zegarra construed final *-y* as a gentilic or possessive element, roughly “that belonging to Ollanta”; Julio Calvo noted possible genitive or vocative interpretations in the drama. These describe how an already opaque form might be resegmented, not its historical derivation.
- Popular translations such as “brave man,” “warrior,” “lover of the princess,” or “inn of Ollantay” are modern folk etymologies.

### Epithets and status terms

- **Apu** — “lord/chief,” attached to Ollantay in the title and dialogue.
- **General of Antisuyu** — his position in the dramatis personae of the manuscript tradition.
- **Qhapaq/cápac Ollantay** — “powerful, eminent, or valiant Ollantay,” a laudatory address.
- **Auqui/Awki** — noble or prince. The play makes Ollantay a promoted noble but simultaneously distinguishes him from Inca blood; precisely how *awki* functions varies among manuscripts and translations.
- **Rebel/traitor** — the Cusco court’s characterization after his flight.
- **Inca** — Ollantay’s Antis acclaim him as their Inca during the rebellion. This is a political elevation inside the story, not evidence that he belonged to the historical royal dynasty.

### The place-name

**[SEA for the place; NEG for the hero]** Sixteenth-century writers such as Cieza de León and Sarmiento de Gamboa call the settlement simply **Tambo**, while other colonial records establish that the place and its architectural complex existed independently of the drama. Archaeology and ethnohistory associate its monumental Inca rebuilding with Pachacútec’s conquest and royal-estate system, not with a documented general Ollantay ([archaeological-historiographical review](https://www.scielo.org.pe/scielo.php?pid=S0254-92122022000100255&script=sci_arttext)).

**[LS]** Ricardo Rojas and other defenders of a prehistoric hero treated “Ollantaytambo” as a commemorative name preserving the rebel’s memory. Cerrón-Palomino reverses the direction: the hero was named from the already existing toponym.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### What the early colonial corpus does—and does not—contain

**[SEA]** Garcilaso de la Vega’s *Comentarios reales* (1609) says that Inca specialists composed performances about past rulers and heroic men. This supports the existence of indigenous dramatic or ceremonial performance in general. It does **not** name Ollantay or preserve his plot.

**[NEG]** Ollantay is not securely named as Pachacútec’s rebel general in:

- Juan de Betanzos, *Suma y narración de los incas* (composed c. 1551);
- Pedro Cieza de León, *Crónica del Perú*, Part II (c. 1553);
- Pedro Sarmiento de Gamboa, *Historia índica* (1572);
- Miguel Cabello Valboa, *Miscelánea antártica* (1586);
- Martín de Murúa’s histories (late sixteenth–early seventeenth centuries);
- Inca Garcilaso de la Vega, *Comentarios reales* (1609);
- Felipe Guaman Poma de Ayala, *Nueva corónica y buen gobierno* (c. 1615);
- Bernabé Cobo, *Historia del Nuevo Mundo* (completed 1653);
- the Quechua *Huarochirí Manuscript* (c. 1608).

These works attest Incas, wars, Antis, royal genealogy, religious practice, and dramatic conventions, but not this hero’s career. Silence is especially material because a decade-long secession and two major imperial campaigns would be conspicuous events. It is not conclusive against an otherwise unattested local tale, but it prevents the drama from being used as straightforward fifteenth-century history.

**[NEG]** Cabello Valboa’s Naymlap material and the *Anónimo de Trujillo* traditions about Tacaynamo belong to north-coast dynastic traditions. Neither text documents Ollantay or an encounter with him.

### Earliest recoverable witnesses

The manuscript history has often been blurred by treating lost exemplars, later copies, and publication dates as one event.

1. **Antonio Valdez tradition, late eighteenth century.**  
   **[SEA/LCE]** Antonio Valdez, a Quechua-speaking priest associated with Sicuani and Tinta, possessed or produced the text from which several later witnesses descend. Markham dated Valdez’s writing-down to about 1770, but this was based on nineteenth-century testimony and a pre-Hispanic-origin model, not on a surviving dated autograph.

2. **Itier’s proposed composition in 1782.**  
   **[MSR]** César Itier argues from contemporary and near-contemporary attributions, Valdez’s political activity, and internal allusions that Valdez wrote the comedy in **1782**. It celebrated the December 1781 peace between Diego Cristóbal Condorcanqui and royal representatives, rejected José Gabriel Túpac Amaru’s claim to sovereignty, and called for reconciliation between the rebellious high provinces and the Cusco valleys that had fought them. This is the most precise historically grounded dating argument presently available ([Itier’s article record and abstract](https://repositorio.pucp.edu.pe/items/1b8e1047-3bf1-4f6e-9aaa-d80131a68336)).

3. **Justiniani manuscript.**  
   **[SEA/LCE]** Justo Pastor Justiniani copied a Valdez-related text. The manuscript, titled *Tragicomedia del Apu Ollantay y Cusi Ccoyllor. Rigores de un padre y generosidad de un rey Ynca*, is held by Peru’s Archivo General de la Nación. Peru’s Ministry of Culture classifies it as an eighteenth-century copy and declared it National Cultural Heritage in 2021 ([official description](https://www.gob.pe/institucion/cultura/noticias/564741-ministerio-de-cultura-declara-patrimonio-cultural-a-la-copia-manuscrita-del-drama-ollantay)). Markham copied the Justiniani text at Laris in 1853.

4. **Dominican I manuscript.**  
   **[LCE]** A text in the Dominican convent at Cusco supplied Johann Jakob von Tschudi’s first complete printed Quechua edition in *Die Kechua-Sprache* (Vienna, 1853). Markham called this witness corrupt and Hispanicized, judgments entangled with his own campaign for pre-Hispanic authenticity.

5. **Sahuaraura manuscript.**  
   **[LCE]** Justo Apu Sahuaraura Inca copied a text around **1838**, probably from the Valdez manuscript family. It is therefore not itself an eighteenth-century manuscript, despite occasional summaries grouping it with the Valdez and Justiniani witnesses. Ippolito Galante published it in 1938. The broader Sahuaraura collection is now held by Peru’s Biblioteca Nacional ([BNP provenance notice](https://www.bnp.gob.pe/declaran-patrimonio-cultural-de-la-nacion-manuscrito-de-la-nobleza-inca-que-regreso-a-peru-despues-de-140-anos/)).

6. **Other witnesses.**  
   **[LCE]** Scholars enumerate a Harmsen/La Paz manuscript, published by Tschudi in his revised 1875 treatment, and a second Dominican manuscript identified in the twentieth century. The relationships among the six principal witnesses are stemmatic and textual questions; divergence shows copying and revision, not automatically a pre-Conquest archetype.

### Publication and translation corpus

- **1837:** *Museo Erudito* of Cusco printed the earliest known public notice, claiming immemorial transmission and Valdez’s writing-down.
- **1851:** Mariano Eduardo de Rivero and Tschudi printed extracts in *Antigüedades peruanas*.
- **1853:** Tschudi printed the first complete Quechua text in *Die Kechua-Sprache*.
- **1868:** José Sebastián Barranca published the first Spanish translation, *Ollanta, o sea la severidad de un padre y la clemencia de un rey*.
- **1871:** Clements R. Markham printed the Justiniani-family Quechua text with English translation and a sustained argument for pre-Hispanic authorship. His edition remains accessible as a primary witness, but its historical and linguistic conclusions must not be accepted uncritically ([complete edition](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Ollanta:_An_Ancient_Ynca_Drama)).
- **1873:** José Fernández Nodal issued a Quechua grammar and version of the drama.
- **1875:** Tschudi published a revised text with German translation and material from the La Paz witness.
- **1876:** Constantino Carrasco produced a Spanish verse paraphrase.
- **1878:** Gavino Pacheco Zegarra published Quechua and French texts with linguistic argument for antiquity ([digitized book record](https://books.google.com/books?id=NFeAAAAAIAAJ)).
- **1890:** Ernst W. Middendorf published Quechua and German texts and argued that an indigenous saga had been reshaped through Spanish theatrical form.
- **1938:** Ippolito Galante produced the first modern-style critical publication of the Sahuaraura witness.
- **1971:** Jesús Lara published *Ollanta: drama quechua del tiempo de los inkas*, defending a substantially Incaic inheritance.
- **1998:** Julio Calvo Pérez published *Ollantay: análisis crítico, reconstrucción y traducción*, collating manuscript evidence and emphasizing the work’s colonial, mestizo, and Spanish-dramatic features ([edition description](https://cbc.org.pe/producto/ollantay-edicion-critica-de-la-obra-anonima-quechua/)).

## 3. The story

The following reconstruction describes the drama’s canonical plot. It is not a biography inferred from the play.

### Antecedents, parentage, and status

**[CCE]** The drama gives Ollantay no birth scene, parents, siblings, ancestry, childhood, or initiation. His lack of royal Inca blood is central: Pachakutiq can reward him as a general and noble but regards marriage to his daughter as an intolerable ascent across dynastic rank.

**[MI]** Modern retellings sometimes call Ollantay a prince by birth, a son of Illapa, or a supernatural Andean titan. None of those genealogies belongs to the principal colonial drama. A 2016 Bolivian novel, for example, makes him a son of Illapa; that is an explicit modern reinvention.

### The secret union with Kusi Quyllur

**[CCE]** At the opening, Ollantay confides in his servant Piki Chaki that he loves Kusi Quyllur (“Joyful/Happy Star”), daughter of Pachakutiq. His words to Willka Umu indicate that she is already “my wife,” that he is bound to her blood, and that her mother knows. The drama is therefore not merely about a distant courtship: the lovers have already formed a forbidden union.

**[CCE]** Willka Umu, high priest and diviner, reads Ollantay’s concealed intention and warns him that pursuing the princess is a crime and will bring disaster. He performs signs with a dried flower and seed to dramatize the warning. Ollantay refuses to abandon her.

**[CCE]** Kusi Quyllur laments Ollantay’s absence to her mother, Anawarki. The drama does not portray the union as rape. It does, however, expose Kusi Quyllur to imprisonment and prolonged abuse once her pregnancy or relationship is discovered.

### Pachakutiq’s refusal

**[CCE]** Ollantay approaches Pachakutiq after recalling his services and asks for Kusi Quyllur. Pachakutiq rebukes him for aspiring above his condition and dismisses him. The rebuff translates a private relationship into a political question: can service override hereditary Inca rank?

**[CCE]** Ollantay leaves Cusco with Piki Chaki, threatens retaliation, and establishes himself at Tampu. The drama does not say that Pachakutiq constructed the fortress for him or that the town was renamed after him.

### Rebellion and the first war

**[CCE]** The Antis acclaim Ollantay as their ruler. His lieutenants include Urqu Waranqa, while Hanku Wayllu appears as an older rebel. Ollantay organizes the approaches to Tampu, orders poisoned arrows prepared, and plans the defense.

**[CCE]** Pachakutiq sends Rumi Ñawi against him. Urqu Waranqa lays an ambush: defenders occupy the heights and roll stones upon the trapped Cusco army. Rumi Ñawi returns defeated. The drama makes Ollantay militarily successful; his ultimate fall comes through deception, not battlefield inferiority.

### Kusi Quyllur and Ima Sumaq

**[CCE]** Kusi Quyllur gives birth to Ollantay’s daughter, Ima Sumaq (“How Beautiful” or “Beautiful One”). Pachakutiq has the mother confined in a subterranean prison within or adjoining the house of chosen women, the *Akllawasi*. The girl is raised in the same institution without being told her mother’s identity.

**[CCE]** The suffering is prolonged and physical: Kusi Quyllur is chained, starved, isolated, and reduced almost to a skeletal or ghostlike condition. The play’s violence is patriarchal and carceral; it contains neither an execution nor sexual assault of her, but it does depict years of cruel detention.

**[CCE]** Ima Sumaq repeatedly hears a hidden woman groaning and pleading for death. Pitu Salla eventually permits her to enter the cell, where mother and daughter recognize one another.

### Succession and Rumi Ñawi’s stratagem

**[CCE]** Pachakutiq dies after approximately ten dramatic years without defeating Ollantay. Tupaq Yupanqui succeeds him. The play’s chronology is literary: it should not be converted into exact dates such as 1461–1471 without an external argument.

**[CCE]** Rumi Ñawi asks for another chance. He mutilates or has himself bloodied, arrives at Tampu pretending that the new Inca has punished and expelled him, and requests refuge. Ollantay accepts him despite the obvious strategic risk.

**[CCE]** During a three-day solar festival, Ollantay’s forces drink heavily. Rumi Ñawi opens the fortress to concealed Cusco troops. Ollantay, Urqu Waranqa, Hanku Wayllu, and their followers are captured. The text presents betrayal and intoxication as the decisive mechanism.

### Judgment, pardon, and restoration

**[CCE]** At Cusco, the prisoners expect death. Tupaq Yupanqui asks his council what punishment they deserve, then reverses the expected sentence: he pardons Ollantay, restores or enlarges his authority, and incorporates the rebel rather than killing him. In some translations, he makes Ollantay temporary governor of Cusco while the Inca campaigns.

**[MSR]** Itier reads this reconciliation politically: in 1782 it could dramatize the reintegration of insurgent Andean provinces after the Túpac Amaru wars, under a legitimate monarch who is merciful once rebellion has ended.

### Recognition

**[CCE]** Ima Sumaq enters the royal presence and begs the Inca to save the imprisoned woman she now knows as her mother. Tupaq Yupanqui, Ollantay, and the court go to the cell. Ollantay recognizes Kusi Quyllur; Tupaq Yupanqui recognizes his sister and niece, orders the chains removed, and sanctions the reunited family.

**[CCE]** Ollantay’s death is not told. There is no canonical account of his burial, corpse, relics, apotheosis, or posthumous cult.

### Conflicting conclusion: pardon or execution

- **[CCE]** The principal drama ends with pardon, restoration, and family reunion.
- **[RV, weakly documented]** A frequently repeated claim says that a Spanish manuscript of 1776 associated a stone “Degolladero” at Tambo with a rebel Ollantay executed under Huayna Cápac. The claim would move the story to another reign and replace reconciliation with death.
- **Evidentiary judgment:** no reliable accessible edition or archival identification of this supposed 1776 manuscript emerged from the dedicated search. The assertion circulates through encyclopedic repetition without adequate shelfmark, transcription, or provenance. It must therefore remain an **unverified reported variant**, not the earliest established attestation and certainly not proof of a “real Ollantay.”

## 4. Relations with gods and other heroes

### Family and political relationships

| Figure | Relationship in the drama | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Kusi Quyllur | Lover or clandestine wife | Principal late-colonial drama |
| Ima Sumaq | Daughter of Ollantay and Kusi Quyllur | Principal drama |
| Pachakutiq | Sovereign, patron, prospective father-in-law, then enemy | Principal drama |
| Anawarki | Kusi Quyllur’s mother; aware of the lovers’ union | Principal drama |
| Tupaq Yupanqui | Kusi Quyllur’s brother; successor who pardons Ollantay | Principal drama |
| Piki Chaki | Servant, confidant, messenger, and comic companion | Principal drama |
| Urqu Waranqa | Rebel lieutenant and successful battlefield commander | Principal drama |
| Hanku Wayllu | Older rebel associated with Ollantay’s party | Principal drama |
| Rumi Ñawi | Imperial rival who loses in battle, infiltrates Tampu, and captures Ollantay | Principal drama |
| Willka Umu | High priest/diviner who warns him, later participates in judgment | Principal drama |

There is no alternate securely transmitted parentage. The drama supplies no parents for Ollantay and no child besides Ima Sumaq.

### Every documented named interaction

All entries below belong to the **[CCE] late-colonial dramatic tradition**, unless otherwise marked. Dates refer to the work’s recoverable late-eighteenth-century form, not its fictional setting.

- **Kusi Quyllur** — Ollantay loves and secretly unites with her; she becomes the mother of his child; after ten years’ separation he recognizes and regains her. Source: *Ollantay*, Acts I and III, recoverable eighteenth-century text.
- **Ima Sumaq** — his daughter, unknown to him during the rebellion, leads him and Tupaq Yupanqui to Kusi Quyllur’s prison. Source: Act III.
- **Pachakutiq/Pachacútec** — Ollantay serves him as general, requests his daughter, is rejected, renounces obedience, and fights his forces. Source: Acts I–II.
- **Tupaq Yupanqui/Cápac Yupanqui** — receives Ollantay as captive, pardons and restores him, then sanctions the reunion with Kusi Quyllur. Source: Act III.
- **Anawarki** — knows about Ollantay’s union with her daughter; she and Ollantay do not have a substantial independent scene together in the received text. Source: Act I.
- **Willka Umu/Uillac Umu** — discovers Ollantay’s concealed love through divination, warns him, refuses to validate the transgression, and later participates in the royal judgment. Source: Acts I and III.
- **Piki Chaki/Piqui Chaqui** — serves as Ollantay’s confidant, skeptical commentator, messenger, and comic foil. Source: throughout Acts I–II.
- **Urqu Waranqa/Urco Huarancca** — accepts command from Ollantay, plans the ambush that defeats Rumi Ñawi, and is captured beside him. Source: Acts II–III.
- **Hanku Wayllu/Hanco Huayllu** — appears as an “old rebel,” joins Ollantay’s political-military coalition, and is captured with it. Source: Acts II–III.
- **Rumi Ñawi/Rumi-Ñahui** — commands Pachakutiq’s expedition, is defeated by Ollantay’s side, later feigns persecution, wins Ollantay’s trust, and captures him during the Raymi. Source: Acts II–III.
- **Apu Maruti** — receives a subordinate deployment in Urqu Waranqa’s battle plan; no direct personal exchange with Ollantay is preserved. Source: Act II.
- **Chara** — subordinate commander named in Urqu Waranqa’s plan; no direct personal interaction with Ollantay is stated. Source: Act II.
- **Inti/Ynti, the Sun** — Ollantay participates in a festival directed toward the Sun and uses solar imagery; no scene presents Inti appearing, speaking, fathering, rescuing, or punishing him. Source: Acts I–III. **Evidence tier: [CCE], ritual reference rather than reciprocal divine encounter.**
- **Pachakamaq/Pachacamac** — Willka Umu says Pachacamac rejoices at sacrificial smoke; Ollantay has no direct interaction with the deity. Source: Act II. **[CCE/NEG for direct encounter].**
- **Illapa/Yllapa** — invoked only as an image for the lightning-like imperial assault in Markham’s translation; no interaction with Ollantay occurs. **[CCE/NEG].**
- **Túpac Amaru II/José Gabriel Condorcanqui** — nineteenth-century accounts claimed the drama was performed before him and that Valdez was his friend. Itier instead reads the work as written after José Gabriel’s execution and as undermining his sovereign claim. No dramatic encounter between Ollantay and Túpac Amaru occurs. **[LCE testimony contested by MSR].**
- **Diego Cristóbal Condorcanqui** — not a character; Itier interprets the reconciliation plot as an allegorical response to his December 1781 peace. **[MSR], not an in-story interaction.**
- **Inkarrí** — no encounter or identification occurs in the drama or in José María Arguedas’s 1950s Puquio materials. The comparison is thematic and modern: both traditions can encode the return or restoration of Andean sovereignty. **[NEG/MSR].**
- **Naymlap and Tacaynamo** — no documented interaction or genealogy links them with Ollantay. **[NEG].**

### Divine status

**[NEG]** No colonial witness identifies Ollantay with Inti, Illapa, an *apu* mountain, Inkarrí, or a Christian saint. Modern use of *Apu Ollantay* as a title must not be converted into divinity. Likewise, Kusi Quyllur’s star-name does not make her a star goddess in the drama.

## 5. Cult, tomb, images, and ritual

### Cult and shrine

**[NEG]** No Ollantay cult is securely documented. There is no identified *huaca*, *mallki*, shrine, priesthood, sacrifice, oracle, pilgrimage, festival, votive deposit, theophoric lineage, or ritual calendar dedicated to him.

The play contains ritual material—solar observance, animal sacrifice, divination, Raymi, coca, ceremonial drinking—but these belong to its representation of the Inca world. They are not evidence of worship directed to Ollantay.

### Tomb and relics

**[NEG]** No tomb, burial, mummy, bone relic, or authenticated funerary monument of Ollantay is known. The alleged “Degolladero” is a place-legend, not an excavated burial or inscribed execution site. Tourist and popular accounts that present architectural features at Ollantaytambo as Ollantay’s residence, prison, throne, or monument are post-textual identifications unless supported by separate archaeological evidence.

### Archaeology of Ollantaytambo

**[SEA for architecture; NEG for personal attribution]** Ollantaytambo is a major archaeological settlement with monumental terraces, temples, waterworks, residential blocks, storage, and defenses. Its Inca-period transformation is associated archaeologically and ethnohistorically with imperial conquest and royal-estate formation. None of its buildings bears an ancient inscription naming the dramatic hero.

The site’s genuine military history includes Manco Inca’s resistance to Spanish forces in 1537. That battle is about a historical sixteenth-century ruler and should not be back-projected onto the fictional rebellion of Ollantay.

### Visual record

**[NEG]** No securely pre-Hispanic portrait of Ollantay exists. Markham reported that in 1837 an Indigenous owner presented a ceramic head said by family tradition to portray Rumi Ñawi; even if the object and report are accepted, that is nineteenth-century provenance for an identification, not a labeled Inca portrait and not an image of Ollantay.

**[LCE/MI]** The hero’s visual corpus begins effectively with illustrated editions, theatrical costume, nationalist painting and stage design, opera, educational illustration, and tourism imagery. Modern performers frequently employ generalized imperial regalia. Those images document reception, not ancient iconography.

## 6. Historical evolution of the figure

### Before the eighteenth century

**[NEG]** There is no secure named Ollantay tradition before the late colonial period. Earlier sources establish the cultural materials from which the drama could draw—Inca royal history, conflict with Antis, Quechua song, court performance, solar rites, and Spanish missionary theater—but not the protagonist.

**[Possible antecedent; MSR]** Martín Lienhard identifies a related narrative structure in Juan de Miramontes y Zuázola’s early seventeenth-century *Armas antárticas*: transgressive love, imperial hierarchy, rebellion, and political reconciliation. This is evidence for a colonial narrative environment, not proof of a lost play bearing Ollantay’s name.

### Late colonial formation, c. 1770–1782

Three models must remain distinct:

1. **Pre-Hispanic composition model [LS].** Markham, Barranca, Pacheco Zegarra, and later Jesús Lara regarded the language, songs, institutions, and lack of overt Christianity as evidence for an Inca drama orally preserved until Valdez wrote it down.
2. **Colonial authorship model [LS/MSR].** Bartolomé Mitre, Ricardo Palma, Middendorf in qualified form, Calvo Pérez, and especially Itier emphasize Spanish dramatic structure, colonial Quechua, manuscript history, and contemporary politics.
3. **Hybrid model [MSR].** An indigenous narrative, songs, or memories of Anti-Inca conflict may have been reshaped in colonial dramatic form. This is plausible but cannot be demonstrated merely from “Andean” content.

**[MSR]** Itier’s 1782 reconstruction supplies a concrete context: after the great rebellion, the merciful successor who reintegrates a defeated provincial rebel could advocate peace without endorsing José Gabriel Condorcanqui’s claim to kingship. On this reading the happy ending is political design, not naïve preservation of fifteenth-century history.

### Nineteenth-century antiquarian canonization

**[LCE/LS]** The 1837 notice transformed a local manuscript tradition into a claim of “immemorial” antiquity. Tschudi, Barranca, Markham, Pacheco Zegarra, and Middendorf then internationalized the text. Their editions preserved indispensable evidence but also made authenticity a contest between “pure Inca relic” and “Spanish imitation.”

Markham’s argument was circular at key points: Garcilaso showed that Inca dramatic performance existed; Areche’s 1781 sentence prohibited Inca memory performances; therefore *Ollantay* must be one of them. The premises make survival possible, but they do not identify this particular play.

### Early- and mid-twentieth-century indigenismo

**[LS/MI]** The play became proof that Peru possessed a national dramatic literature anterior to Europe’s conquest. Its hero shifted from royal rebel to emblem of Indigenous dignity, social mobility, anti-tyranny, and American cultural independence.

José María Arguedas’s 1952 essay emphasized both the Indigenous and Western stylistic components of colonial Quechua drama rather than demanding an uncontaminated origin ([Arguedas article record](https://revistaletras.unmsm.edu.pe/index.php/le/es/article/view/1474)).

### Relation to Inkarrí

**[F/MSR]** Arguedas collected important Inkarrí testimony at Puquio in the 1950s; Alejandro Ortiz Rescaniere later analyzed Andean messianic and mythic structures. Inkarrí concerns the dismembered or absent Inca whose restoration promises a reordered world. Alberto Flores Galindo’s *Buscando un Inca* (1986) studied the recurring political utopia of Inca restoration.

Ollantay is not Inkarrí: he is neither dismembered nor expected to return. Comparison is nevertheless productive because the drama converts rebellion and dynastic rupture into restored just rule. The relationship is **modern comparative interpretation**, not textual identification or syncretism.

### Present status

**[Modern reception]** Ollantay now circulates simultaneously as:

- a canonical Quechua literary figure;
- a school-text “Inca legend”;
- a hero of anti-aristocratic love;
- a symbol of Andean identity;
- an operatic and orchestral subject;
- a tourism narrative attached to Ollantaytambo;
- a modern personal name, particularly familiar through former Peruvian president Ollanta Humala.

These uses do not settle the work’s date or the character’s historicity.

## 7. Modern reception and reinvention

### Literature and theater

- **1868 — Barranca:** first Spanish translation; established the enduring subtitle contrasting paternal severity with royal generosity.
- **1876 — Constantino Carrasco:** Spanish verse paraphrase.
- **1908 manuscript / later publication — Ricardo Rojas:** rewrote the material as an Argentine-American national myth.
- **1939 — Rojas, *Un titán de los Andes*:** explicitly described his work as a restoration of a primordial Andean legend, while creating a new tragic fable.
- **Carlos B. Monsalve:** produced an Argentine *Ollantay* tragedy before or alongside Rojas’s nationalist appropriation.
- **1953 — César Miró and Sebastián Salazar Bondy:** adapted Barranca and Pacheco Zegarra for Peru’s Compañía Nacional de Comedias.

A recent study traces this movement “from colonial epic to modern cultural nationalism,” particularly Ricardo Rojas’s attempt to place an Indigenous-American antiquity beneath Argentine national theater ([comparative reception study](https://www.scielo.sa.cr/scielo.php?pid=S2215-26362023000200007&script=sci_arttext)).

### Opera and music

- **1900 — José María Valle-Riestra, *Ollanta*:** opera with a libretto by Federico Blume y Corbacho, premiered in Lima. It clothed the Indigenous theme in European operatic form.
- **1920 — revised *Ollanta*:** re-staged with a libretto associated with Luis Fernán Cisneros and official support during Augusto B. Leguía’s government. Scholarship reads the revival within elite and state-sponsored Incaist nationalism ([musicological study](https://tesis.pucp.edu.pe/items/8b9a3e68-137b-4cd3-afb6-21b836bfc8e0)).
- **1947–1949 — Alberto Ginastera, *Ollantay*, Op. 17:** a three-movement symphonic poem—“Landscape of Ollantaytambo,” “The Warriors,” and “Death of Ollantay”—premiered by Erich Kleiber and the Teatro Colón orchestra in 1949. Its death ending is a modern Argentine symphonic transformation, not the colonial drama’s conclusion.

### Nationalist uses

**[MI/Political reception]** In Peru, editions and performances helped install the play as evidence of an ancient national literature and a prestigious Inca inheritance. In Argentina, Rojas used it in the *Eurindia* program: a national culture produced by fusion between Indigenous America and European traditions.

**[MSR]** These appropriations can be nationalist and essentializing, but the evidence reviewed here did not establish a documented extremist cult centered specifically on Ollantay. Claims of extremist appropriation should not be manufactured from broader Andean or Inca symbolism.

### Popular and commercial reinvention

**[MI]**

- Tourist storytelling routinely says the fortress “was named for General Ollantay,” often without noting that the toponym likely preceded the character.
- School adaptations simplify the play into a timeless Inca folktale and commonly turn the clandestine union into chaste courtship.
- Some modern works divine-parent Ollantay through Illapa, relocate the plot to Tiwanaku or Copacabana, or kill him heroically.
- Personal-name use—*Ollanta*—derives from the literary and nationalist prestige of the drama, not from proof that it was a common Inca personal name.
- Claims that the play is unquestionably “the oldest theater in America” confuse possible oral antecedents with the date of the surviving work.

### Argentine Northwest and “cordillera” versions

**[Reception rather than securely collected folklore]** Searches for an independently collected Northwest Argentine oral Ollantay cycle produced literary and educational retellings, Quechua-language references, and Rojas’s pan-Andean nationalism, but not a securely documented local oral corpus comparable to the Puquio Inkarrí accounts. Material labeled “folklore of the cordillera” generally descends from printed *Ollantay*, school anthologies, or nationalist rewriting. Until collector, locality, informant, date, and text are supplied, it should be classified as **modern diffusion**, not an autonomous ancient regional variant.

## 8. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Was Ollantay historical?

**Arguments formerly offered for historicity**

- The fortress supposedly preserves his name.
- The setting, Inca institutions, Quechua language, and royal protagonists are historically authentic.
- The chroniclers attest that Inca drama existed.
- Local nineteenth-century tradition claimed the plot represented a true event.
- A war between Cusco and Antis could preserve a political memory.

**Criticisms**

- The name is better explained as a toponym converted into an anthroponym.
- No early chronicler names the hero or his ten-year rebellion.
- Real rulers and places can frame fictional historical drama.
- General evidence for Inca theater is not evidence for this play.
- The manuscripts are colonial and show textual revision.
- The reconciliation plot fits 1781–1782 politics unusually well.

**Present evidentiary conclusion:** Ollantay’s historical existence is unverified. A lost memory of Anti resistance is possible, but no identifiable fifteenth-century individual can presently be recovered behind the dramatic hero.

### Inca or colonial origin?

#### Markham and the pre-Hispanic case

**[LS, 1871]** Markham stressed archaic vocabulary, absence of Spanish loanwords and Christianity, Inca institutions, songs, and testimony that Valdez merely wrote down a performance. He dated the drama internally to the reign of Huayna Cápac, c. 1475–1525.

Problems include his imperfect Quechua, dependence on informants and a limited manuscript base, an overly rigid assumption that a colonial priest would necessarily write Christian theater, and circular use of generalized testimony about Inca performance.

#### Middendorf’s mixed position

**[LS, 1890]** Middendorf identified colonial language, Spanish meters, and European dramatic structure, while allowing an older indigenous saga about warfare between the Incas and Antis. This became an influential hybrid theory.

#### Jesús Lara

**[LS, twentieth century]** Lara defended a strongly Indigenous or Incaic textual inheritance. His work was important in legitimizing Quechua literary history, but its antiquity claims exceed the securely dated manuscript record.

#### Julio Calvo Pérez

**[MSR, 1998]** Calvo’s critical reconstruction treated the drama as a colonial mestizo work, reportedly placing composition broadly between c. 1680 and 1735 and detecting Calderonian influence. His edition is philologically important, although Itier later contested such early dating.

#### César Itier

**[MSR, 2006]** Itier rejects seventeenth- and early-eighteenth-century datings as unsupported. He combines attribution testimony with close political reading to argue for Valdez’s authorship in 1782. His interpretation explains both the succession from severe father to merciful king and the reconciliation of insurgent Antis with Cusco.

### Authorship

Possible attributions have included:

- **Antonio Valdez:** the strongest documentary candidate under Itier’s reconstruction.
- **Anonymous Indigenous or mestizo oral tradition:** possible as a source, not demonstrable as the complete text’s author.
- **Juan de Espinosa Medrano (“El Lunarejo”):** proposed without adequate documentary proof.
- **Collective colonial adaptation of a pre-Hispanic drama:** a longstanding hybrid hypothesis, but difficult to falsify because its lost archetype is undefined.
- **Strict anonymity:** retained by many editions because Valdez’s autograph and explicit authorial declaration are lacking.

### Oral-formulaic and performance questions

Quechua songs and variable manuscripts show that performance and copying affected the text. Yet manuscript divergence by itself does not prove centuries of pre-Hispanic oral transmission. Short lyric passages may be older than the encompassing plot; the date of a song, a narrative motif, a performed scenario, and the extant three-act comedy need not be identical.

### Textual state and losses

- Valdez’s autograph or exemplar is lost or unidentified.
- The Justiniani and Sahuaraura copies belong to related but nonidentical textual lines.
- The Dominican and La Paz witnesses contain corruptions, substitutions, and omissions.
- Nineteenth-century editors normalized spelling, rearranged scenes, and sometimes translated freely.
- Markham’s parallel Quechua-English edition is indispensable but not a modern critical edition.
- Calvo’s 1998 reconstruction remains the major modern collation available in published form.

### Discredited claims that continue to circulate

- **“The fortress proves Ollantay existed.”** Reversed by modern onomastic analysis.
- **“All three principal manuscripts are eighteenth-century.”** Sahuaraura’s copy is approximately 1838.
- **“Areche specifically banned *Ollantay*.”** His sentence suppressed representations commemorating the Incas; it does not name this play.
- **“Túpac Amaru II certainly commissioned or watched it.”** This rests on later testimony and conflicts with Itier’s 1782 dating.
- **“No Spanish words means pre-Conquest authorship.”** Colonial authors could write deliberately classicizing Quechua.
- **“Kusi Quyllur was an Inca star goddess.”** In the play she is a mortal princess with an astral personal name.
- **“Ollantay was son of Illapa.”** Modern fictional genealogy.
- **“His tomb/temple survives at Ollantaytambo.”** No supporting archaeological identification.
- **“The 1776 Degolladero manuscript proves his execution.”** The circulating claim lacks an adequately identified and published primary witness.

## 10. Sources and assessment

### Primary and early editorial witnesses

- The Quechua drama itself, principally as represented by the Justiniani, Dominican, Sahuaraura, and La Paz manuscript families.
- Clements R. Markham’s 1871 Quechua-English edition: valuable for its transcription of the Justiniani-family witness and nineteenth-century testimony, but partisan on antiquity and linguistically superseded.
- José Sebastián Barranca’s 1868 translation: foundational for Spanish reception, accompanied by speculative historicist and etymological argument.
- Gavino Pacheco Zegarra’s 1878 Quechua-French edition: important philological witness and defense of antiquity.
- Garcilaso, Cieza, Sarmiento, Betanzos, Guaman Poma, Murúa, Cobo, and the *Huarochirí Manuscript*: contextual controls. Their failure to name Ollantay prevents their general information about Inca history and performance from being treated as direct attestation.

### Principal modern scholarship

- **César Itier, “Ollantay, Antonio Valdez y la rebelión de Thupa Amaru” (2006):** strongest case for Valdez’s authorship and a precise 1782 political context.
- **Rodolfo Cerrón-Palomino, “Ollantay: topónimo antes que antropónimo” (2006):** strongest linguistic treatment of the name and decisive criticism of the place-name-as-biographical-proof argument.
- **Julio Calvo Pérez, *Ollantay: análisis crítico, reconstrucción y traducción* (1998):** principal modern critical reconstruction.
- **Martín Lienhard, “La épica incaica en tres textos coloniales” (1985):** colonial literary and epic context.
- **José María Arguedas, “El Ollantay. Lo autóctono y lo occidental…” (1952):** influential analysis of stylistic hybridity.
- **Ari Zighelboim:** interpretation of the work’s movement from politically legible late-colonial comedy toward an anonymized “popular legend.”
- **Reception scholarship on Ricardo Rojas and Valle-Riestra:** documents the transformation of Ollantay into a modern pan-Andean and national symbol.

### Final assessment

The documented Ollantay is best understood as a **late-colonial Quechua dramatic hero constructed from Inca historical names, Andean geography and ritual, colonial political experience, and European theatrical form**. The possibility of older songs, local narratives, or memories of Anti resistance remains open, but none presently supplies a securely datable pre-Hispanic Ollantay. His fortress, tomb, cult, divine ancestry, and fifteenth-century military biography are unattested. His real historical life is the history of a text: composed or stabilized in the eighteenth century, copied by Quechua and Inca-descended intellectuals, transformed by nineteenth-century philology, nationalized in Peru and Argentina, and repeatedly returned to oral, theatrical, musical, educational, and touristic circulation.
