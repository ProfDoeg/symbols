# Chuquillanto and Acoytapia: Research Dossier

**Traditional class:** Chuquillanto and Acoytapia are a pair of legendary lovers and etiological mountain figures. Murúa classes Chuquillanto as a **ñusta and daughter/virgin of the Sun** and Acoytapia as a **mortal shepherd from Lares**. Their petrified forms are retrospectively associated with the mountain-wak’as Pitusiray and Sawasiray. Neither is an epic king, historical ruler, or independently attested heroized dead.

**Earliest secure attestation:** Martín de Murúa’s illustrated *Historia del origen y genealogía real de los reyes ingas del Pirú*, conventionally dated 1590 but materially revised after that date, probably in the 1590s (Galvin manuscript, fols. 144r–147v). This is the earliest securely identifiable narrative of the lovers. Murúa’s later *Historia general del Pirú*—completed principally in 1613 and presented in Spain in 1616—repeats it in Book I, chapters 91–92.

**Central evidentiary qualification:** Murúa says that Indigenous people treated the story as an old tradition, that “some make much of it and show memories of it,” and that he had repeatedly seen the two supposed stone figures. That is good evidence that a localized story was circulating around Calca–Yucay in the late sixteenth century. It is not proof that every literary detail in Murúa’s ornate Spanish narration is pre-Hispanic. No securely pre-conquest text, inscription, dated artifact, or archaeological deposit names either lover.

## Evidence labels

- **[EARLY COLONIAL ATTESTATION]** Direct evidence in a sixteenth- or early-seventeenth-century manuscript.
- **[EARLY COLONIAL VARIANT]** A separate colonial account concerning the same landscape or mountain pair.
- **[LATER CANONICAL ELABORATION]** A later edition, translation, anthology, or literary retelling dependent on colonial material.
- **[REGIONAL VARIANT]** A locally reported story that materially changes the plot or identities.
- **[CULT/ARCHAEOLOGY]** Documentary or material evidence for ritual treatment of the mountains or stones.
- **[MODERN SCHOLARLY RECONSTRUCTION]** A modern interpretation, not an assertion made by the primary text.
- **[MODERN INVENTION/ADAPTATION]** A demonstrably modern narrative addition or simplification.
- **[ABSENCE]** A searched corpus contains no documented appearance of the lovers as such.

## 1. Identification, Names and Epithets

### Identification

**[EARLY COLONIAL ATTESTATION]** In Murúa, Chuquillanto is the elder of two exceptionally beautiful “hijas del Sol,” a ñusta living among women dedicated to the Sun. Acoytapia is a young man from Lares who tends the white sacrificial livestock of the Sun on Sawasiray above the Yucay valley. They fall in love, evade the restrictions imposed on the Sun’s women, sleep together, flee discovery, and become two human-shaped stones near Calca. Murúa’s story therefore joins an unequal social pair—sacred royal woman and pastoral servant—and anchors them in visible terrain.

The title “daughter of the Sun” is ambiguous. Murúa treats the women both as daughters of the deity and as women consecrated to his service. The text does not provide a mortal mother, a divine conception, or an Inca genealogy for Chuquillanto. Calling her a “demigoddess” would consequently exceed the source.

### Name forms

**Chuquillanto**

Attested spellings include:

- *Chuquillanto*, *Chuqui Llanto*, *Chiquillanto*—Murúa’s manuscripts and later editions.
- *Chukillanto*—normalized modern spelling.
- *Chuquillantu*—modern Quechua-influenced spelling.
- *Chuquillanto ñusta, hija del Sol*—Murúa’s identifying formula.

**[MODERN SCHOLARLY RECONSTRUCTION]** One proposed analysis takes *chuqi/chuki* as “gold,” “precious metal,” “lance,” or, in some southern Andean usages, an element connected with lightning; *llanthu/llantu* means “shade,” “shadow,” or “shelter.” On that basis scholars have suggested “shadow/shelter of lightning” or a place where lightning—or sometimes the jaguar—takes shelter. These are contextual interpretations, not etymologies furnished by Murúa, and the compound’s exact original sense remains uncertain.

The name also appears elsewhere in Murúa as the alternative name of Mama Guarqui, wife of Huáscar. **[LEARNED/CODICOLOGICAL REASSIGNMENT]** That queen is not securely the same person as the mythical Sun-virgin. Murúa reused or reassigned illustrations and labels while constructing his manuscripts, and the shared name cannot establish identity.

**Acoytapia**

Attested spellings include:

- *Acoytapra*—prominent in the Galvin manuscript’s title.
- *Acoitapia*, *Acoytapia*—Murúa and modern editions.
- *Acoitapra*, *Acoitrapa*—transcriptions and school adaptations.
- *Aqoytapia*—modern normalized form.

**[MODERN SCHOLARLY RECONSTRUCTION]** An interpretation cited in modern landscape studies derives the first element from an old lexicographical *acuy*, “dead body,” and reads the figure as an ancestral or death-associated mountain being. The derivation is speculative: Murúa supplies no etymology, and the variable consonant sequence *-tapia/-tapra/-trapa* has not received a securely demonstrated analysis.

### Titles and descriptive epithets

- **“pastor” / “famoso pastor” / “gran Acoytapra”**—Acoytapia; Galvin manuscript.
- **“natural de los Lares”**—Acoytapia; Murúa.
- **keeper of the “ganado blanco del sacrificio”**—Acoytapia’s office, not a hereditary epithet.
- **“hermosa y discreta Chuquillanto”**—Galvin chapter heading.
- **“ñusta, hija del Sol”**—Chuquillanto’s class and affiliation.
- **“virgen del Sol” or *aclla***—common modern shorthand. Murúa’s actual formulations are more varied: daughter, ñusta, and woman dedicated to the Sun.

### Mountain identifications

**[EARLY COLONIAL ATTESTATION]** Murúa opens by naming two neighboring mountains, Sawasiray and Pitusiray, and says that Acoytapia and Chuquillanto were transformed there. At the end he calls the place *Pitu Sira* and describes two stone figures visible from Huayllabamba and Calca. He does not unambiguously write “Acoytapia is Sawasiray and Chuquillanto is Pitusiray.”

**[MODERN SCHOLARLY RECONSTRUCTION]** Modern writers commonly pair Acoytapia with Sawasiray and Chuquillanto with Pitusiray. The correspondence is plausible, but it is an inference from the paired lovers and paired peaks, not an explicit sentence in Murúa.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### The Galvin Murúa

**[EARLY COLONIAL ATTESTATION]** The earliest witness is Murúa’s *Historia del origen y genealogía real de los reyes ingas del Pirú*, fols. 144r–147v. Its title page bears 1590, but codicological studies show that production and revision continued later, probably into the middle or later 1590s. It is therefore safest to cite it as **“dated 1590; compiled and revised in the 1590s.”**

The story is accompanied by five surviving watercolors at fols. 145v, 146r, 146v, 147r, and 147v. Their subjects include:

1. Chuquillanto’s dream.
2. The lovers together.
3. Their flight.
4. The Calca landscape, labeled with Sawasiray, Pitusiray, Huayllabamba, Calca, Acoytapra, and Chuquillanto.
5. An additional scene within the sequence.

The pictures have been attributed to Felipe Guaman Poma de Ayala in recent scholarship, although the attribution participates in the broader, still-debated reconstruction of artists and scribes in Murúa’s manuscripts. “Guaman Poma illustrated the sequence” is a strong modern attribution, not an artist’s signed declaration.

### The Getty Murúa

**[EARLY COLONIAL ATTESTATION]** Murúa’s substantially rewritten *Historia general del Pirú*, Book I, chapters 91–92, dates principally to 1613, with certification and presentation activity through 1616. It preserves the full Spanish narrative but not the five-watercolor cycle in its earlier arrangement. Differences from the Galvin version are chiefly orthographic, stylistic, and editorial; they do not establish an independent oral variant.

Murúa’s headings explicitly call the tale a *ficción* and *fábula*, while also saying that Indigenous elders transmitted it as an ancient event. Those terms reflect the friar’s colonial-Christian judgment, not necessarily the Indigenous narrators’ genre.

### Independent colonial witnesses to the landscape

**[EARLY COLONIAL VARIANT]** Guaman Poma’s *El primer nueva corónica y buen gobierno* (written approximately 1600–1615) names **Saua Ciray** and **Pitu Ciray** among the major wak’as of Antisuyu. He says sacrifices included children, white rabbits, coca, *mullu* shell, feathers, and llama blood. Elsewhere he reports that Huayna Cápac spared Sawasiray and Pitusiray when other unresponsive idols were broken. Guaman Poma does **not** name Chuquillanto or Acoytapia in his own account and does not narrate their romance.

**[EARLY COLONIAL VARIANT]** Juan de Santa Cruz Pachacuti Yamqui Salcamayhua’s *Relación de antigüedades deste reino del Pirú* (c. 1613) attaches the names Pitusiray and Sawasiray to a different story: a male and female pair associated with Sañuc, sexual transgression, a speaking stone-wak’a, Manco Cápac, and Tonapa’s staff. This is evidence that the mountain names sustained more than one etiological narrative. It should not be harmonized with Murúa’s lovers.

### Later publication history

- **1910 – [LATER CANONICAL ELABORATION]:** Clements Markham summarized or translated the romance in *The Incas of Peru*, calling it a uniquely surviving specimen of genuine Inca literature. That judgment greatly exceeds what textual transmission can prove.
- **1920:** Manuel Beltroy’s Spanish translation of Markham helped circulate the narrative.
- **1938:** Jorge Basadre selected the story for the *Biblioteca de Cultura Peruana*, consolidating it as “Inca” or Quechua literature.
- **1946:** Constantino Bayle’s edition of Murúa made the chronicle and illustrations more widely available.
- **1962–1964:** Manuel Ballesteros Gaibrois published the later manuscript.
- **2004:** Juan Ossio’s facsimile study of the Galvin codex returned attention to the illustrated early witness.
- **2008:** Getty studies established a more precise codicological relationship between the Galvin and Getty manuscripts.
- **2019:** Alicia Maria Houtrouw treated text, pictures, Murúa, and Guaman Poma together.
- **2022:** Paula Martínez Sagredo, Alberto Díaz Araya, and Carlos Mondaca analyzed the dream and sacred geography; Javier Ortiz Luna studied Sawasiray–Pitusiray as landscape, ancestors, water, and fertility.

### Named corpora searched but not witnesses to this pair

**[ABSENCE]**

- Betanzos, Cieza de León, Sarmiento de Gamboa, Cobo, Garcilaso de la Vega, Cabello Valboa, and the Anonymous Trujillo account do not preserve the lovers’ narrative.
- Cabello Valboa’s Naymlap and the Anonymous Trujillo’s Tacaynamo belong to northern coastal dynastic traditions, not this Calca romance.
- The *Huarochirí Manuscript* contains numerous transformations into stone and local wak’a traditions but not Chuquillanto and Acoytapia.
- The manuscripts of *Ollantay*—including those associated with Antonio Valdez and Justo Pastor Sahuaraura—and the long dispute over that drama’s pre-Hispanic versus colonial date do not contain this pair. Similarities involving forbidden unequal love are thematic, not evidence of shared characters.
- Arguedas’s and Ortiz Rescaniere’s Puquio Inkarrí collections of the 1950s, and later discussions by Flores Galindo, concern the returning/dismembered Inca, not these lovers.
- Rostworowski, Zuidema, and Urton supply comparative models for Andean sacred geography, dual organization, myth, and astronomy; they are not primary witnesses to this romance.

## 3. The Story

### Setting and encounter

**[EARLY COLONIAL ATTESTATION: Murúa, 1590s/1613]** Above the fertile Yucay valley, on snowy Sawasiray, Acoytapia tends white animals destined for sacrifice to the Sun. While the animals rest, he plays a flute. Two daughters of the Sun, allowed to roam the highland meadows by day but required to return at night, encounter him.

Overwhelmed by their beauty, Acoytapia kneels because he thinks they cannot be human. They take his arm, seat him, and reassure him. Chuquillanto, the elder, asks his name, birthplace, and parentage. The text never supplies his father’s name or a lineage beyond his being from Lares.

### The *utussi*

Chuquillanto notices a shining silver forehead ornament. Murúa calls its general class *canipu*, while Acoytapia calls this one *utussi*; Murúa says he could not discover the word’s true meaning. At its foot are two minute *aradores* “eating a heart.” The image becomes an erotic emblem: she returns the ornament but metaphorically gives him her heart.

**[MODERN SCHOLARLY RECONSTRUCTION]** *Arador* may designate an agricultural implement, a creature, or an asterism according to context. Modern astronomical-agricultural readings connect the image with plowing, rain, and the seasonal sky. Murúa himself does not make that explanation.

### The palace and its guardians

The women return to a guarded palace. Porters search entrants because women have reportedly smuggled lovers inside belts (*chumpis*) or necklace beads. Chuquillanto refuses supper and retires, consumed by desire.

The household contains women drawn from the empire’s four quarters and four corresponding fountains:

- Sulla Puquío—toward Chinchaysuyu; Murúa glosses it as “spring of pebbles.”
- Llullu Chapuquío—toward Collasuyu; “spring of aquatic plants.”
- Ocorura Puquío—toward Cuntisuyu; “spring of watercress.”
- Siclla Puquío—toward Antisuyu; “spring of frogs.”

Spellings and directional assignments differ in modern normalizations. Later writers sometimes silently replace Murúa’s glosses with alternative Quechua analyses, such as “dew spring” or “blue-flower spring.” Those are philological revisions, not variant narration.

### Dream and oracle

**[EARLY COLONIAL ATTESTATION]** Chuquillanto dreams that a songbird—Murúa’s Spanish calls it a *ruiseñor*, “nightingale”—flies among trees, alights in her lap, speaks, and asks the cause of her sorrow. She says she will die without Acoytapia but fears that the Sun will have her killed if the household discovers her love.

The bird directs her to stand among the four fountains and sing. If all repeat her song, she may safely pursue her desire. She sings a Quechua line transmitted approximately as:

> *micuc, usutu, cuyuc, utussi cusin*

Murúa glosses it approximately: “Plowman/*arador* who are eating the *utussi* that moves—fortunate is it.” The phrase is textually and lexically difficult; fluent modern translations that remove its ambiguity should not be treated as secure.

All four fountains echo her words. Chuquillanto accepts this as authorization.

### Acoytapia’s mother and the walking-stick

Acoytapia meanwhile grieves nearly to death. His unnamed mother in Lares divines his danger. Murúa, writing through a Christian demonological framework, says she learned the cause “through the devil” and calls her one of the women respected as diviners.

She reaches him before sunrise, gathers nettles—said to cure sadness and gladden the heart—and cooks them. When the sisters arrive, Acoytapia hides, on his mother’s instructions, **inside a beautifully painted staff**.

The old woman tells Chuquillanto that the staff came to her by inheritance and that she had once been one of Pachacamac’s beloved women. Chuquillanto asks for it and receives it. She unknowingly carries Acoytapia through the palace inspection.

This is the walking-stick episode named in the brief: the stick does not disguise him by ordinary camouflage; it supernaturally contains him.

### Union, discovery, and flight

Near midnight Acoytapia emerges and calls Chuquillanto by name. She embraces him, covers him with finely worked *lipi* and *cumbi* blankets, and they sleep together. Murúa does not describe force: the episode is narrated as mutual desire, though embedded in radically unequal social roles and mortal danger.

Before dawn he reenters the staff. The next day Chuquillanto takes it into a concealed ravine, where he emerges and rests with his head in her lap. A guard follows because Chuquillanto has gone out alone and raises the alarm.

The lovers expect execution if captured. They flee toward the mountains above Calca, collapse on a rock, awaken to pursuit, and try to run again. Chuquillanto has one sandal in her hand and one on her foot. Looking toward Calca, both become stone.

Murúa states that the two stone figures were still visible from Huayllabamba, Calca, and elsewhere and that he had seen them many times. This is eyewitness testimony to rocks interpreted anthropomorphically—not eyewitness testimony to metamorphosis.

### Competing mountain story

**[EARLY COLONIAL VARIANT: Pachacuti Yamqui, c. 1613]** The Pitusiray–Sawasiray names also appear in a different story involving siblings or a male-female pair who have committed sexual sin, a dangerous stone at Sañuc, Manco Cápac’s golden staff, and petrification or confinement beside the wak’a. This version is more overtly Christianized in its language of carnal sin and hell. It neither names Acoytapia nor Chuquillanto.

### “Mothers of the acllahuasi”

Murúa describes plural women of the Sun supervising the palace, but the romance names only Acoytapia’s mother. **[MODERN ADAPTATION]** Retellings that turn the attendants into individually named “mothers of the acllahuasi,” or give them decisive speaking roles, elaborate beyond the surviving primary text.

### Every documented interaction with a named divine being or hero

- **Inti / the Sun** — Chuquillanto is called his daughter and belongs to his dedicated household; Acoytapia guards the Sun’s white sacrificial livestock; the lovers fear punishment under the Sun’s institutional authority. **Source:** Murúa, Galvin manuscript, 1590s; *Historia general*, 1613. **Tier:** [EARLY COLONIAL ATTESTATION].

- **Pachacamac** — Acoytapia’s mother tells Chuquillanto that she was formerly a beloved woman of Pachacamac and inherited the magic staff through that connection. Pachacamac does not appear or act directly. **Source:** Murúa, 1590s/1613. **Tier:** [EARLY COLONIAL ATTESTATION].

- **Sawasiray** — One of the two named mountains at which the flight and petrification are localized; modern scholarship commonly identifies it with Acoytapia. **Source:** Murúa, 1590s/1613. **Tier:** mountain association securely attested; personal equation [MODERN SCHOLARLY RECONSTRUCTION].

- **Pitusiray** — The other mountain/place of petrification; modern scholarship commonly identifies it with Chuquillanto. **Source:** Murúa, 1590s/1613. **Tier:** mountain association securely attested; personal equation [MODERN SCHOLARLY RECONSTRUCTION].

No direct interaction is documented with Viracocha, Manco Cápac, the Ayar siblings, Tonapa, Pachacútec, Huayna Cápac, Huáscar, Ollantay, Inkarrí, Naymlap, or Tacaynamo. Their appearance in landscape parallels or comparative discussions must not be converted into meetings with the lovers.

## 5. Cult, Stones and Ritual

### What is securely documented

**[EARLY COLONIAL ATTESTATION]** Murúa records:

- a named sacred landscape at Sawasiray–Pitusiray;
- two visible anthropomorphic stones interpreted as the lovers;
- local narration of their transformation;
- continued remembrance around Calca and Huayllabamba;
- the white sacrificial herds of the Sun;
- four symbolically organized springs.

**[CULT/ETHNOHISTORICAL ATTESTATION]** Guaman Poma independently establishes that Sawasiray and Pitusiray were major Antisuyu wak’as. His listed offerings—children, white rabbits, coca, *mullu*, feathers, and llama blood—constitute actual documentary evidence of cult attributed to the Inca period. His account does not say the sacrifices were made specifically to “Chuquillanto and Acoytapia.”

### What is not established

No excavated shrine, dedicatory inscription, named priesthood, festival, oracle, tomb, relic, or securely identified sacrificial deposit has been tied archaeologically to the lovers. No source calls the two stones their graves or says their bodies were buried there. “Tomb of Chuquillanto and Acoytapia” is unsupported.

The modern phrase “sanctuary of the lovers” may describe the landscape devotionally or for tourism; it is not the name of an excavated pre-Hispanic *heroon*.

### Landscape interpretation

**[MODERN SCHOLARLY RECONSTRUCTION]** Scholars relate:

- the four springs to Tawantinsuyu’s fourfold organization;
- the lovers’ pairing to complementary male/female mountain powers;
- the shepherd, white livestock, rain, and irrigated Yucay agriculture;
- *utussi*, *arador*, water, and seasonal constellations;
- petrification to the widespread Andean process by which ancestors become enduring wak’as.

These readings are coherent and productive, but their components do not all appear as an explicit system in Murúa.

## 6. Historical Evolution

### Before the Spanish invasion

**[ABSENCE]** No pre-Hispanic document names the lovers. The antiquity of the mountain cult is plausible from the mountains’ Inca-period status reported by early colonial witnesses, but the antiquity of Murúa’s complete romantic plot cannot be independently demonstrated.

### Late sixteenth century

**[EARLY COLONIAL ATTESTATION]** Murúa records the romance from people he characterizes as Indigenous elders. The Galvin manuscript integrates Spanish prose, Quechua words and song, Andean landscape knowledge, Christian demonological judgments, romance-like plotting, and Indigenous-style images.

This hybridity prevents two opposite simplifications: it is neither securely a verbatim pre-Hispanic tale nor safely dismissible as Murúa’s personal fiction.

### Early seventeenth century

Murúa rewrites the story in the *Historia general*. Guaman Poma documents the mountain wak’as and their sacrifices without the lovers. Pachacuti Yamqui preserves a rival etiological story. The three accounts show a landscape with overlapping traditions, not one stable canon.

### Nineteenth and early twentieth centuries

The manuscript circulated chiefly through copies. Markham’s 1910 treatment detached the narrative from its uncertain transmission and promoted it as authentic Inca literature. Beltroy’s 1920 translation and Peruvian anthologies broadened its audience.

### 1930s–1960s

Basadre’s 1938 selection helped canonize the romance within Peruvian literature. The 1946 and 1962–1964 Murúa editions provided broader access, although editions based on copies and editorial normalization sometimes obscured manuscript variants.

### Late twentieth century

Sabine Dedenbach-Salazar’s 1990 study placed the shepherd theme in Andean literary and pastoral contexts. Structural, hydrological, dualist, and calendrical interpretations multiplied. Most are reconstructions from the narrative’s symbolic organization rather than recovered Indigenous commentaries.

### Twenty-first century

Facsimile and codicological research restored the Galvin manuscript’s pictures and complicated the simple date “1590.” Art historians emphasized collaboration and conflict between Murúa and Indigenous artists, especially Guaman Poma. Educational publishers converted the episode into a concise children’s legend, generally suppressing difficult philology, colonial mediation, sacrifice, and uncertainty.

## 7. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Literary canonization

Markham called the tale effectively unique as surviving Inca literature. Basadre and later Peruvian anthologists treated it as a major early Peruvian story. The classification is historically influential but textually stronger than the evidence permits: the only full surviving wording is Murúa’s colonial Spanish composition, interspersed with Quechua.

A Peruvian literary history has accordingly praised it as indispensable to anthologies of Inca/Quechua literature and of the Peruvian short story. That double classification—Indigenous tradition and colonial literary artifact—is more defensible than assigning it wholly to either side.

### School-reader form

**[MODERN ADAPTATION]** Modern school texts commonly use *Acoitrapa y Chuquillanto*. A documented 2014 Peruvian edition, *La leyenda de la Huacachina; Acoitrapa y Chuquillanto: guía del maestro*, explicitly packages it as children’s literature. Teacher materials present prediction questions, vocabulary, comprehension exercises, and a simplified plot.

Typical alterations include:

- calling the instrument a **quena**, where Murúa merely says *flauta*;
- calling the animals llamas without discussing Murúa’s sacrificial herd;
- omitting the *utussi* and difficult Quechua song;
- replacing the diviner mother’s relation to Pachacamac with a generic “wise old woman”;
- treating the magic staff as a familiar fairy-tale device;
- turning the ending into a sentimental symbol that their love is eternal;
- describing soldiers as the pursuers although Murúa first specifies a female guard and then only an approaching commotion;
- conflating the lovers straightforwardly with the two whole mountains.

These versions belong to modern pedagogy, not independent oral testimony.

### Tourism and local identity

Calca tourism and municipal publications present Pitusiray and Sawasiray as ancestral sacred mountains and repeat the lovers’ legend. This is documented modern heritage-making rooted in genuine colonial testimony to the wak’as, but promotional prose often blurs:

1. colonial testimony to mountain cult,
2. Murúa’s romantic narrative,
3. present-day identifications of particular formations,
4. modern tourism branding.

No substantial, documented nationalist or extremist appropriation of the lovers themselves was located. Broader indigenista and Peruvian national-cultural canonization is evident; extremist use is not.

### Popular visual culture

The five Galvin watercolors remain the foundational visual cycle. Modern illustrations, web retellings, and teaching graphics generally derive from Murúa or from generic “Inca princess and shepherd” imagery. No major opera, feature film, internationally distributed game, or comics franchise centered on the pair was verified.

## 8. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Is it a pre-Hispanic Inca story?

Three positions can be distinguished:

1. **Strong-authenticity position:** Markham and some anthologists called it genuine Inca literature. Its Quechua words, local terrain, fourfold springs, wak’a setting, and Indigenous visual participation support real Andean substance.

2. **Colonial-literary position:** Murúa explicitly calls it *ficción* and narrates it in elaborate Europeanized romance prose. The speaking nightingale, lovesickness, secret entry, magic container, and tragic lovers can be read through early-modern literary conventions.

3. **Hybrid-production position:** Current evidence best supports a localized Andean narrative and sacred landscape reshaped through Murúa’s Spanish prose, Christian categories, manuscript collaboration, and early-modern literary expectations. This position explains both Indigenous specificity and colonial form without pretending to recover an uncontaminated original.

### Date of the first manuscript

The printed or catalogued date 1590 is not the whole answer. Physical and textual work indicates later stages of production; some scholarship dates relevant activity around 1596 or later. The safest bibliographic practice is “Galvin Murúa, title-page date 1590, compiled/revised in the 1590s.”

### Authorship of the pictures

Houtrouw and related scholarship associate the romance illustrations with Guaman Poma. Manuscript studies also recognize multiple artistic hands and complex transfer of pages between Murúa’s projects. Attribution should therefore be expressed as “attributed to Guaman Poma,” not as an uncontested signature.

### Independent cult versus narrative

Guaman Poma’s evidence proves that Sawasiray and Pitusiray were treated as important wak’as in accounts of Inca religion. It does not prove that ritual officiants understood those wak’as primarily as Chuquillanto and Acoytapia. The romance may be one local etiological overlay on a much older paired mountain cult.

### Hydrological and astronomical readings

Modern analyses see a system of water, agriculture, seasonal rain, complementary mountains, and celestial *aradores*. The four springs and the sacrificial-pastoral setting securely support a water-and-landscape dimension. Exact stellar identifications and full ritual-calendar reconstructions remain hypotheses.

### Comparison with *Ollantay*

Both stories concern forbidden love across a social or sacred barrier, but no manuscript makes the characters meet, and no evidence shows that one text directly borrowed from the other. The uncertain dating of *Ollantay* cannot be used to date this romance.

### Argentine northwest “cordillera versions”

A dedicated search did not locate a securely documented Argentine northwestern oral variant naming Chuquillanto and Acoytapia. General Andean tales of mountain lovers, petrified couples, princesses, shepherds, and forbidden unions are abundant, but resemblance is not identity. Until a dated field collection supplies the names or a demonstrable transmission chain, “Argentine version of Chuquillanto and Acoytapia” remains unverified.

### Historicity, tomb, and nationalism

- **Historicity:** no evidence identifies either lover as a historical individual.
- **Tomb:** none attested.
- **Cult of the named lovers:** unproven; cult of Sawasiray and Pitusiray is documented.
- **Etymology:** disputed and partly conjectural.
- **Nationalism:** literary-national canonization exists; political mobilization specifically around the pair was not found.
- **Modern false antiquity:** polished retellings often label their exact prose, dialogue, or moral “an ancient Inca legend.” Only the tradition’s late-sixteenth-century existence is securely demonstrated; the precise age of each motif is unknown.
