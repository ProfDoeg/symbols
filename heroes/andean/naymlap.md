# Naymlap: Research Dossier

> **Traditional class:** **mortal founder-king and culture hero whose concealed death becomes a claim of apotheosis**; dynastic ancestor of Lambayeque’s rulers and institutor of the image-cult of Yampallec. Calling Naymlap simply a “god” collapses stages that the earliest text keeps distinct.
>
> **Earliest secure attestation:** an oral origin tradition collected in Lambayeque, probably in **1581**, and written into Miguel Cabello Valboa’s Spanish *Miscelánea Antártica*, completed in **1586**, pp. 327–330 of the 1951 edition. No presently known pre-Hispanic inscription names Naymlap.
>
> **Essential evidentiary conclusion:** the sixteenth-century tradition is securely documented; the hero’s pre-Hispanic historicity is not. Sicán/Lambayeque archaeology independently establishes a powerful society, monumental ceremonial centers, elite ancestor rites, distinctive religious imagery and climatic upheavals. It does **not** securely identify a particular skeleton, monument or image as Naymlap.

## Evidence labels

- **[EA] Secure early attestation:** directly present in the earliest documentary witness, Cabello Valboa’s account collected about 1581 and completed in 1586.
- **[CE] Colonial elaboration:** a colonial author’s framing, rationalization or later colonial variant.
- **[RV] Regional variant:** a distinguishable local version rather than part of the earliest account.
- **[LS] Learned systematization:** an antiquarian, linguistic or comparative interpretation imposed after collection.
- **[AR] Archaeologically attested:** demonstrated by excavated context or material analysis, but not automatically connected with Naymlap.
- **[MSR] Modern scholarly reconstruction:** a reasoned historical, linguistic, iconographic or structural interpretation.
- **[F] Folklore or continuing social memory:** a postcolonial oral or popular tradition whose antiquity cannot be assumed.
- **[MR] Modern reception or political-cultural reuse.**
- **[MI] Modern invention or unsupported popular claim.**
- **[NEG] Negative finding:** absent from the examined source or corpus.

## 1. Identification, names and epithets

### Orientation

**[EA]** Cabello Valboa calls Naymlap a *señor*, *caudillo*, “father of companies,” and a man of exceptional *valor y calidad*. He arrives by sea with a principal wife, concubines, officials and a large following; establishes a palace or palatial settlement called **Chot**; installs the green-stone image **Yampallec**; rules peacefully; fathers children; and dies. His attendants secretly bury him in his dwelling and tell the population that he grew wings and disappeared. Thus the earliest record presents a human ruler whose court manufactures—or preserves—a story of supernatural departure, not an unambiguously primordial deity. The same account says that Cium hid his own death so that posterity would regard him as “immortal and divine,” making sacralized kingship a theme of the dynasty. [Cabello Valboa, *Miscelánea*, 1586, pp. 327–329](https://es.scribd.com/document/333068744/Cabello-Valboa-1586-Miscelanea-Antartica).

### Recorded forms

- **Naimlap / Naymlap / Nainlap / Nailamp** — **[EA]** variant spellings in the transmitted Cabello Valboa witnesses. The orthography is Spanish and unstable; it is not an indigenous script.
- **Ñamla** — **[RV/CE]** form recorded by Modesto Rubiños y Andrade in 1782.
- **Ñaimlap / Ñaymlap / Naylamp** — **[LS/MR]** normalized modern spellings. *Naylamp* became particularly widespread in Peruvian archaeology, museums, tourism and institutional naming.
- **Nyampal / Ñampal** — **[LS]** Enrique Brüning’s 1922 reconstruction, based on colonial spelling practices and local documentary forms.
- **Yampallec / Yampállec / Llampallec** — **[EA/CE/LS]** name of the green-stone image. Cabello glosses it as “figure and statue of Naymlap.” Later spellings with *Ll-* are editorial or popular adaptations rather than independent early witnesses.
- **Lambayeque / Lanbayeque** — **[EA/MSR]** Cabello derives the valley’s name from Yampallec. Historical linguistics recognizes a probable relationship among the forms but does not regard the precise derivation as solved.

### Etymologies

**[RV/CE]** Rubiños’s 1782 account says *Ñamla* means “ave (o gallina) de la agua”—“water bird” or “water fowl”—in the local Indigenous language. This is the earliest explicit lexical interpretation, but it is two centuries later than Cabello and was written after major language loss.

**[LS]** Brüning argued that the original form was *Nyampal/Ñampal* and that *Yampallec* was morphologically derived from it.

**[MSR]** Urban and Eloranta prefer **Ñaimlap** and examine possible Mochica/Muchik relationships among the hero’s name, Yampallec and the historical place-name *Ñampaxllæc*. They warn that proposed etymologies remain problematic and should not be accepted uncritically. In particular, popular formulas such as “Naymlap certainly means water bird” convert a late gloss into linguistic fact. [Urban and Eloranta, “Ñaimlap, the Birds, and the Sea,” 2017](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/00277738.2017.1304104); [history of northern Peruvian languages](https://www.ey.com/content/dam/ey-unified-site/ey-com/es-pe/insights/growth/documents/ey-historia-lenguas-antiguo-obispado-trujillo.pdf).

**[MSR]** A further analysis interprets a reconstructed form *Ñampaxllæc* through Mochica *paxll-* “become,” yielding something like “one who becomes a bird.” It attractively fits the flight story, but the morphology and reconstructed base are disputed; it is not an ancient gloss.

### Epithets and identifications

**[EA]** Cabello gives no fixed divine epithet. “Lord,” “leader,” and “father of companies” are descriptions, not cult titles.

**[MSR]** “Sicán Deity,” “Sicán Lord,” “winged-eye deity,” and the older popular term *Huaco Rey* are modern archaeological labels for recurrent Lambayeque imagery. They are not ancient names.

**[MSR]** The equation **Sicán Deity = Naymlap** is plausible but unproved. The icon has mask-like or pointed eyes, elaborate headgear and at times avian features; these suit the flight tradition, but no inscription identifies it. Museum catalogues properly use “may be associated with Ñaimlap,” not “is certainly Naymlap.” [Metropolitan Museum of Art](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/316428).

**[MI]** Yampallec is sometimes called Naymlap’s wife. Cabello explicitly says that it represented **Naymlap’s face**; his wife was Ceterni. Turning Yampallec into a goddess-consort contradicts the earliest record.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### The first witness

**[EA]** Cabello began the *Miscelánea Antártica* in 1576 and completed it in 1586. Luis E. Valcárcel dated his Lambayeque collection to Cabello’s journey through the region in 1581. Cabello introduces the account communally: “the natives of Lambayeque say—and the other neighboring peoples of this valley agree.” Martín Farro Chumbi of Túcume and Francisco Puicunsoli Efquemsoli of Jayanca have separately been proposed as informants, but neither attribution is demonstrable from Cabello’s text. [2023 documentary study](https://www.scielo.cl/scielo.php?pid=S0719-26812023000200131&script=sci_arttext).

**[EA]** The surviving manuscript situation matters. The work circulated in multiple codices; the autograph once associated with Joaquín García Icazbalceta was located in Texas, while another manuscript was held in New York. The editors of the 1951 edition reported no substantive difference between them in the relevant narrative. Cabello’s full work remained unpublished until the twentieth century, although excerpts circulated earlier.

### Primary and near-primary corpus

1. **Miguel Cabello Valboa, *Miscelánea Antártica*, Spanish, completed 1586; tradition probably collected 1581.** **[EA]** The sole full early account of Naymlap’s voyage, court, Yampallec, secret burial, alleged flight, dynasty, Fempellec’s transgression and the Chimú takeover.

2. **Modesto Rubiños y Andrade, *Sucesión cronológica o serie historial de los curas de Mórrope y Pacora*, Spanish, written 1782, published 1936.** **[RV/CE]** Calls the hero Ñamla. He flees unnamed islands after military defeat and reaches Lambayeque through providence and maritime ordeal. His settlement is named for him; a coastal huaca receives his wife Soternic’s name. The elaborate court, Yampallec, flight and Fempellec catastrophe disappear. The version emphasizes successful coastal adaptation and territorial legitimacy.

3. **Local oral and popular retellings, nineteenth–twenty-first centuries.** **[F/MR]** Generally descend from the printed Cabello or Rubiños traditions, museum interpretation and regional education. Individual additions require separate proof.

### Relevant corpora in which Naymlap is absent

- **Betanzos, Sarmiento de Gamboa, Cieza de León, Cobo, Garcilaso de la Vega, Guaman Poma and Murúa:** **[NEG]** no independent Naymlap narrative. Cieza’s 1553 description of north-coast lordship—great adobe residences, plazas, dancers, musicians, drinking and polygyny—provides comparative social context but does not name Naymlap.
- **Anonymous History of Trujillo:** **[NEG]** contains the separate Chimú founder **Tacaynamo**, another sea-arriving dynastic ancestor. Similarity is comparative evidence, not an encounter or identity.
- **Huarochirí Manuscript:** **[NEG]** no Naymlap; its named huacas and heroes belong principally to the central Peruvian Huarochirí region.
- **Quechua drama *Ollantay*:** **[NEG]** no Naymlap. The Valdés, Justo Pastor Justiniani and related manuscripts belong to an independent Cuzco dramatic tradition; their dating controversy does not date Naymlap.
- **Inkarrí narratives collected by José María Arguedas and others at Puquio in the 1950s:** **[NEG]** no Naymlap. Structural comparison between returning or transformed rulers may be illuminating, but it is modern comparison, not a shared ancient episode.
- **Argentine northwest and cordilleran folklore:** **[NEG]** no securely documented Naymlap cycle was located. General Andean or maritime-founder parallels should not be presented as regional variants without an actual collected text.

## 3. The story

### Arrival and court

**[EA]** Naymlap comes “from the upper part of this Peru” (*de la parte suprema de este Piru*) in a great fleet of rafts. The phrase has produced incompatible geographical readings: “north,” “south,” “up-coast,” “highland,” and “upper Peru.” Cabello does not name an embarkation point. Modern certainty that he came from Ecuador, Polynesia, Wari territory or a named island exceeds the text.

**[EA]** His principal wife is **Ceterni**; Cabello also gives him many concubines. Forty officers accompany the ruler. Only prominent examples are individually described:

- **Pita Zofi**, conch-shell trumpeter;
- **Ñinacola**, keeper of the litter and chair;
- **Ñinagintue**, drink steward;
- **Fongasigde**, who scatters powdered marine shell where the lord will walk;
- **Occhocalo**, cook;
- **Xam Muchec**, responsible for facial unguents and color;
- **Ollopcopoc**, bath attendant;
- **Llapchillulli**, maker of feather garments and a valued principal.

**[EA]** The list dramatizes a fully differentiated royal household rather than a band of improvised colonists. Whether the expressions are personal names or fossilized occupational titles is a modern linguistic question.

### Foundation of Chot and Yampallec

**[EA]** The fleet lands at the mouth of the river then called **Faquisllanga**. The immigrants abandon the rafts, travel half a league inland and build palaces “in their fashion,” calling the place **Chot**.

**[EA]** There they install with ritual devotion a green-stone *huaca* or idol named **Yampallec**, fashioned with Naymlap’s own face. It is a portable dynastic image brought on the voyage, not a wife or child.

**[MSR]** Chot is frequently identified with **Huaca Chotuna**, partly through name resemblance, location and monumental remains. Brüning initially favored the identification but later reconsidered it. Donnan’s excavations showed long occupation and suitable monumental architecture but found no naming inscription or green idol. Therefore **Chotuna is a candidate for Chot, not its proven equivalent**.

### Rule, death and wings

**[EA]** Naymlap lives and rules for many peaceful years and has many children. No battles, law code, irrigation project or conquest is attributed personally to him.

**[EA/CE ambiguity]** At death, attendants bury him secretly in the room where he had lived “so that his vassals should not understand that death had jurisdiction over him.” They announce that by his own power he took wings and disappeared. Cabello’s phrasing may rationalize an Indigenous transformation account into elite deception; the underlying oral version cannot be recovered with certainty.

**[EA]** First-generation followers abandon their homes, children and grandchildren to search for him, leaving principally the locally born population behind.

**[MI]** Modern retellings add a giant-bird transformation, ascent to heaven, a trail of light, a final voyage in a golden raft or an explicit appointment of Fempellec as immediate heir. Those details are absent from Cabello; **Cium**, not Fempellec, succeeds him.

### Cium and the twelve sons

**[EA]** Naymlap’s eldest son **Cium** marries **Zolzoloñi** and, through her and other concubines, fathers **twelve sons**, each the ancestor of a large family. The “twelve descendants” are therefore Cium’s sons, not twelve sons explicitly fathered by Naymlap.

**[EA]** Three settlement traditions are specified: **Nor** goes to Cinto; **Cala** to Túcume; unnamed brothers establish themselves at Collique and elsewhere. Llapchillulli settles Jayanca with followers.

**[EA]** Cium enters an underground vault and lets himself die so that posterity will believe him immortal and divine. The hidden deaths of father and son make political sacralization—not literal biological immortality—a central motif.

**[Textual tension]** Cabello first says Naymlap’s original followers dispersed in search of him, then retains the original courtier Llapchillulli into the dispersal under Cium. The discontinuity may preserve joined oral segments or regional genealogies rather than a coherent single composition.

### Dynasty and catastrophe

**[EA]** Cabello names the succession after Cium as **Escuñain, Mascuy, Cuntipallec, Allascunti, Nofan Nech, Mulumuslan, Llamecoll, Lanipat-cum, Acunta**, and finally **Fempellec**. Counting Naymlap, Cium, nine intervening rulers and Fempellec produces a twelve-ruler scheme; the text supplies no deeds for the nine middle rulers.

**[EA]** Fempellec attempts to move Yampallec from Chot but cannot. A being Cabello calls “the Demon,” in the form of a beautiful woman, appears and sleeps with him. The text does not name her. Immediately thirty days of unprecedented rain begin, followed by a year of sterility and famine.

**[CE]** “Demon,” “sin,” “lack of continence” and “nefarious union” are Christian moral vocabulary. The precolonial identity of the female figure—deity, huaca, temptress or ritual partner—is unrecoverable.

**[EA]** Priests and leading men decide that the ruler’s offense caused collective suffering. They bind Fempellec hand and foot and throw him into the deep sea. This is the only explicit lethal royal punishment in the cycle. It may be described as execution or regicide; calling it a formally attested sacrificial rite goes beyond Cabello.

**[MSR]** Scholars have associated the thirty-day flood with El Niño/ENSO events. Archaeology and paleoclimate show severe climatic disruptions in northern Peru, but no particular event can securely be identified as “Fempellec’s flood.”

**[EA]** After the dynasty ends, **Chimo Capac** conquers the valley and appoints Pongmassa; Cabello continues with later Chimú and Inca succession. The tale thereby converts political subjugation into the consequence of broken ritual order.

### Rubiños’s variant

**[RV/CE]** In 1782 Ñamla is a defeated island ruler, not an unexplained triumphant voyager. Providence carries his group to Lambayeque after innumerable hardships. Fishing, hunting, fresh water, salt, bows and arrows allow survival. The settlement takes Ñamla’s name and a seaside huaca that of **Soternic**, his wife.

**[RV]** Rubiños omits the winged disappearance, Yampallec and Fempellec. Sium’s palace is supposedly buried by descendants at the Spanish conquest and survives as the “House of the Cacique.” The version is more topographical and Christianized and probably served eighteenth-century claims to territory and noble ancestry. It cannot simply fill gaps in Cabello.

### Every documented interaction with another named divine being or hero

- **Yampallec — [EA]:** Naymlap brings and installs this green-stone huaca bearing his likeness at Chot; Cabello Valboa, collected c. 1581, completed 1586. Yampallec is an image/huaca, not securely an independently personified god.
- **Ceterni — [EA]:** principal wife who accompanies Naymlap’s maritime migration; Cabello Valboa, 1586.
- **Cium — [EA]:** eldest son and successor; inherits Naymlap’s lordship and repeats the concealed-death strategy; Cabello Valboa, 1586.
- **Soternic — [RV]:** wife in Rubiños’s version; her name is given to a coastal huaca; Rubiños y Andrade, 1782.
- **Sium — [RV]:** son/successor in Rubiños; his palace is later said to have been concealed beneath a huaca; 1782.
- **Llapchillulli — [EA]:** valued featherworker/principal in Naymlap’s court and later founder at Jayanca; Cabello Valboa, 1586. The chronology is internally inconsistent.
- **Fempellec — [EA]:** final descendant; attempts to move Naymlap’s Yampallec image, precipitating the catastrophe that ends the line; Cabello Valboa, 1586. He never meets Naymlap personally in the text.
- **Chimo Capac — [EA]:** conquers the leaderless territory after Naymlap’s dynasty ends; Cabello Valboa, 1586. This is dynastic succession, not a personal encounter.
- **Tacaynamo — [NEG/MSR]:** a parallel maritime founder in the Anonymous History of Trujillo; no source makes him meet or identify with Naymlap.
- **Viracocha, Pachacamac, Inti, Pariacaca, Cuniraya, Chaupiñamca, Manco Capac, the Ayar siblings, Ollantay and Inkarrí — [NEG]:** no primary text documents an interaction with Naymlap.
- **Unnamed beautiful female/demon — [EA/CE]:** interacts sexually with Fempellec, not Naymlap; she cannot be entered as a named deity because Cabello supplies no name.

## 5. Cult, tomb and ritual

### Textual cult

**[EA]** The only explicit cult founded by Naymlap is devotion to **Yampallec at Chot**. The image is a *huaca* and dynastic palladium; priests still attend it in Fempellec’s reign. This demonstrates a narrated image-cult, not independent archaeological proof of worship of a deity named Naymlap.

**[EA]** Naymlap’s concealed burial is placed in his own living chamber. The story supplies neither public tomb cult, recurring sacrifice, feast, oracle nor named priesthood dedicated directly to him.

**[NEG]** No bones, relic, tomb inscription or securely identified heroon of Naymlap has been found. Claims that he lies beneath Huaca Chotuna are local belief or archaeological speculation.

### Chotuna–Chornancap

**[AR]** Chotuna and Chornancap are real monumental Lambayeque sites with palaces, platforms, enclosures, murals, domestic and craft areas, burials, and multiple construction phases. Donnan excavated there from 1980 to 1982 and Carlos Wester’s project resumed extensive work in 2006. [Donnan, *Chotuna and Chornancap*](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/0413v2j8).

**[AR/MSR]** Donnan found the archaeological sequence broadly compatible with a long-lived center and later disruptions, but wrote that existing evidence could neither prove the narrative historical nor demonstrate it mythical. Assigning Naymlap to approximately 1150 CE was therefore a conjectural correlation, not a dated individual.

**[AR]** Evidence at the complex includes sacrificial or attendant burials. These establish violent mortuary and ritual practices at the site; they do not show that victims were offered to Naymlap.

### The Chornancap tomb

**[AR]** On 18 October 2011 Wester’s team discovered an intact elite female burial, generally dated to the twelfth–thirteenth centuries CE. The approximately 45–50-year-old woman was seated facing east and accompanied by eight young females and a small camelid. Her assemblage included gold, silver, copper and bimetallic objects, ceramics and an elaborate funerary mask. [Peruvian Ministry of Culture report](https://www.gob.pe/institucion/naylamp/noticias/546454-diez-anos-del-descubrimiento-de-la-gobernante-y-sacerdotisa-de-chornancap).

**[MSR]** She is called the **Governess/Priestess of Chornancap** because of her high-status and ritual insignia. The designation is an archaeological interpretation, not a recovered personal title.

**[NEG]** The tomb is **not Naymlap’s tomb**: the deceased is female, materially dated centuries before Cabello’s record, and unnamed. Nor does it prove that Yampallec was female. Its importance lies in documenting elite female ritual-political authority within Lambayeque society.

**[MSR]** Wester associates the burial’s sea, bird, lunar and female imagery with a broader Lambayeque sacred landscape. This is a significant contextual interpretation, but linking the woman to a particular mythical goddess or episode remains inferential. [Wester, “Poder y género…”](https://dialnet.unirioja.es/servlet/articulo?codigo=8152153).

### Sicán archaeology

**[AR]** Shimada’s Sicán Archaeological Project distinguishes Early Sicán, roughly 750/800–900 CE; Middle Sicán, approximately 900–1100; and Late Sicán after about 1100. Middle Sicán possessed monumental centers around Batán Grande/Pómac, sophisticated metallurgy, hierarchical burials and a widely repeated supernatural icon.

**[AR]** Burning and abandonment affected important monumental buildings around 1100. Material culture also shows continuity through later changes. Neither the transition nor the burning is a direct archaeological transcription of Fempellec’s story. [Shimada, *Cultura Sicán*](https://fundacionbbva.pe/wp-content/uploads/2016/04/libro_000051.pdf); [climatic and cultural sequence](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-3263/12/6/238).

**[MSR]** Shimada treats archaeology as a means of testing broad historical processes behind the legend, while warning against converting it into a literal king-list. The evidence does not support twelve individually identified reigns.

## 6. Historical evolution of the figure

### Before 1581: recoverable context, not textual attestation

**[AR]** Between the eighth and fourteenth centuries CE, Lambayeque/Sicán society developed the monuments, elite mortuary practices, metallurgy and religious art later drawn into Naymlap interpretation.

**[NEG]** No pre-Hispanic writing labels any image or ruler Naymlap. The name’s existence before European contact is probable insofar as Cabello recorded an Indigenous regional tradition soon after conquest, but its age and earlier form cannot be measured.

### c. 1581–1586: colonial fixation

**[EA/CE]** Cabello converts a flexible regional oral complex into a compact universal-history narrative. Indigenous words such as *huaca* coexist with Christian “Demon” and sexual-sin language. The result preserves irreplaceable names and episodes but is not a transcript untouched by colonial interpretation.

**[MSR]** The tradition likely helped Lambayeque elites articulate a prestigious local origin following Chimú, Inca and Spanish domination, epidemic loss, resettlement and climatic disaster. Naymlap represents ordered government; Fempellec embodies ritual-political failure.

### Seventeenth century

**[NEG]** No comparable independent Naymlap narrative has been located. The silence of major Inca chroniclers is important: Naymlap was regional, not a standard member of the Cuzco-centered pantheon.

### 1782: territorialized retelling

**[RV/CE]** Rubiños records a shorter, more Christian and environmentally practical account. It replaces the mysterious origin with defeated island exiles and emphasizes named landscape features, adaptive skill and surviving descendants.

### Nineteenth and early twentieth centuries

**[LS]** Antiquarian publication and philology converted the narrative into a problem of migration, dynastic chronology and etymology. Some writers treated oral genealogy as straightforward political history.

**[LS]** Brüning’s fieldwork, local documentation and linguistic observations connected the story with Chotuna while also exposing the uncertainty of that identification.

### Late twentieth century

**[MSR]** Archaeologists Donnan and Shimada tested possible correlations with material sequences. Structural and ethnohistorical scholarship, including Zuidema and Rostworowski, shifted attention from “Was Naymlap literally real?” to what the narrative says about lineage, sacred kingship, dual organization, political legitimacy and collective memory.

**[MSR]** Rowe and Zuidema were skeptical of reading the dynasty as literal history. Donnan took a cautiously correlationist position; Shimada criticized attempts to force archaeology into a chronicle and emphasized long-term continuities and discontinuities.

### Twenty-first century

**[AR/MR]** Renewed Chotuna–Chornancap excavation and the Priestess’s tomb have strengthened regional interest without identifying Naymlap materially.

**[MR]** Naymlap now functions as an emblem of Lambayeque identity in museum displays, public heritage projects, tourism, schools, business names and the state archaeological unit formerly styled Proyecto Especial Naylamp. This is documented regional commemoration, not survival of an unchanged pre-Hispanic cult.

## 7. Modern reception and reinvention

### Art, museum display and popular imagery

**[MR]** Modern reconstructions commonly show a crowned sea-king arriving on reed rafts, carrying a green idol or transforming into a bird. These pictures synthesize Cabello with excavated Lambayeque regalia; they are not copies of a securely identified ancient portrait.

**[MR/MSR]** The famous gold ceremonial knives popularly called *tumis of Naymlap* bear the recurrent Sicán supernatural figure. “Naymlap” is an interpretive identification. The safer catalogue term is “Sicán Deity” or “Lambayeque supernatural figure.”

**[MR]** Museum and educational videos retell the legend as the origin story of Lambayeque art. Smarthistory’s Denver Art Museum presentation is a representative modern synthesis. [Smarthistory](https://smarthistory.org/ancient-andean-art-in-context-origin-story-legend-of-naymlap/).

### Literature and music

**[MR]** *Naymlap: leyenda novelada sobre el origen de Lambayeque* appeared in 2009. Its form is explicitly modern historical/legendary fiction, not a recovered oral primary text. [Bibliographic record](https://repositorio.uap.edu.pe/jspui/handle/20.500.12990/9623).

**[MR]** Chilean ethnomusicologist Mauricio Vicencio issued the album *Naylamp* in 1993, using reconstructed or replica Andean sonorities. It belongs to modern musical reception.

### Identity and politics

**[MR]** Naymlap’s best-documented political use is regional identity-building, not extremist appropriation. Sixteenth- and eighteenth-century versions could legitimate local noble descent and territorial rights; modern heritage institutions use the name to distinguish Lambayeque’s past from Inca-centered national history.

**[NEG]** No well-documented extremist movement or sustained state-nationalist cult specifically centered on Naymlap was found. Describing ordinary regional commemoration as “nationalist appropriation” without evidence would overstate the record.

### Demonstrable modern distortions

- **[MI]** “Naymlap founded the Sicán kingdom in exactly 750/800/900/1025/1100/1150 CE.” These dates result from modern archaeological correlation.
- **[MI]** “The Chornancap Priestess was Yampallec/Naymlap’s wife.” No inscription or early text supports this.
- **[MI]** “The gold tumi is a portrait of Naymlap.” It depicts a Sicán supernatural or elite figure; the proper name is conjectural.
- **[MI]** “Naymlap came from Polynesia, Easter Island, Japan, Ecuador or a particular Peruvian island.” Cabello names no such homeland.
- **[MI]** “Naymlap personally built the irrigation network.” Neither early version says so.
- **[MI]** “He appointed Fempellec and flew away in a glowing bird form.” Cabello makes Cium the immediate successor and says only that attendants announced wings and disappearance.
- **[MI]** “Yampallec was a green-jade goddess.” Cabello calls it a green-stone likeness of Naymlap; the mineral and gender are not independently established.

## 8. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Historicity

**Literalist position — [MSR]:** the named dynasty, arrival, Chot and catastrophic end preserve historical events. Supporting arguments include the geographical fit of Chotuna, the reality of maritime movement, monumental architecture, post-Moche cultural change and remembered Chimú domination.

**Skeptical position — [MSR]:** the orderly succession, hidden deaths, sacred image, sexual transgression and cosmological flood form a political origin myth rather than a chronicle. The first writing is centuries later; no names are epigraphically confirmed; internal narrative seams suggest compilation.

**Cautious position — [MSR]:** social memories can preserve real landscape, conquest and institutional patterns without preserving a literal founder or chronology. Donnan’s conclusion that the available evidence can neither prove historicity nor prove complete fiction remains methodologically sound.

### Site identification

**For Chotuna = Chot — [MSR]:** similar name, appropriate lower-valley location, monumental scale and long occupation.

**Against certainty — [MSR]:** no Yampallec image, naming inscription or Naymlap tomb; Cabello’s half-league measure cannot be mapped securely because the sixteenth-century river mouth is uncertain; nearby Chornancap and other centers complicate one-to-one matching.

### Iconography

**Identification with Naymlap — [MSR]:** avian characteristics, pointed “winged” eyes, maritime associations and elite dress fit the transformation and sea-arrival motifs.

**Objections — [MSR]:** the icon predates the written narrative by centuries; similar supernatural eye and avian features occur in Moche, Wari and Pachacamac-related traditions; the figure may be a deity, divine ruler, ancestor or changing composite. Its ubiquity does not supply a name.

### Etymology

The “water bird” gloss is historically valuable but late. Reconstructions involving Mochica *ñan/ñam*, *la* and *paxll-* compete and depend on uncertain colonial spelling and a poorly documented extinct language. The resemblance between Naymlap, Yampallec and Lambayeque is significant; exact segmentation remains disputed.

### Structure and political function

**[MSR]** Zuidema read the account principally as a statement about the society that produced it rather than a transparent king-list. The repeated concealment of royal death, branching descent, named settlements and catastrophe support analysis as a charter of authority and territorial organization.

**[MSR]** Rostworowski’s wider work on Andean sacred rulers, huacas, ancestors, gendered divine pairs and dual political organization supplies a comparative framework. It should not be turned into proof that every Andean founder belonged to one uniform system. [IEP edition information](https://fondoeditorial.iep.org.pe/producto/estructuras-andinas-del-poder-ideologia-religiosa-y-politica-obras-completas-vii-ebook/).

### Climate

**[AR/MSR]** ENSO offers a credible environmental referent for extraordinary coastal rain, agricultural failure and famine. Paleoclimate establishes repeated events, not the moral causation or thirty-day duration of Cabello’s tale.

### State of the evidence

- **Texts:** two substantive colonial versions, separated by roughly two centuries.
- **Manuscripts:** Cabello survives through early codices but was published fully only in the twentieth century; Rubiños was written in 1782 and published in 1936.
- **Archaeology:** abundant for Lambayeque/Sicán society, absent for a named Naymlap.
- **Images:** abundant but non-inscribed; iconographic naming remains inferential.
- **Cult:** textual evidence for Yampallec; archaeological evidence for Lambayeque ritual, ancestor veneration and sacrifice; no independently named Naymlap shrine.
- **Losses:** organic ritual objects, local oral performances and most knowledge of Mochica/other northern languages are gone; the green image itself has not been found.

## 10. Sources and assessment

### Primary sources

- Cabello Valboa, Miguel. *Miscelánea Antártica*, completed 1586, especially pp. 327–330 in the 1951 edition. Earliest and indispensable witness; preserves Indigenous names but contains Christian framing.
- Rubiños y Andrade, Modesto. *Sucesión cronológica o serie historial de los curas de Mórrope y Pacora*, 1782; published 1936. Independent late regional variant, not a supplement to Cabello.
- Cieza de León, Pedro. *Crónica del Perú*, 1553. Comparative evidence for north-coast lordship; no Naymlap episode.

### Archaeology and material culture

- Donnan, Christopher B. *Chotuna and Chornancap: Excavating an Ancient Peruvian Legend*. Los Angeles: Cotsen Institute, 2012. Most direct archaeological test of the narrative.
- Donnan, “An Assessment of the Validity of the Naymlap Dynasty,” 1990. Establishes the cautious historicity debate.
- Shimada, Izumi. “Cultural Continuities and Discontinuities on the Northern North Coast of Peru,” 1990; *Cultura Sicán*, 1995. Long-duration archaeological model and criticism of literal correlations.
- Wester La Torre, Carlos. Studies of the Chornancap Governess/Priestess, 2012–2016. Primary excavation interpretation for the elite female tomb.

### Linguistics, memory and interpretation

- Urban, Matthias, and Rita Eloranta. “Ñaimlap, the Birds, and the Sea: Viewing an Ancient Peruvian Legend through the Lens of Onomastics,” 2017. Most careful account of the name problem.
- “La leyenda de Naymlap en la memoria y la identidad de los pueblos de Lambayeque,” *Diálogo Andino* 71, 2023. Close comparison of Cabello and Rubiños and their different colonial settings.
- Rostworowski, María. *Estructuras andinas del poder*, 1983. Comparative framework for sacred authority, huacas, ancestors and political organization.
- Zuidema, R. Tom. 1990 contribution to the Naymlap debate. Structural interpretation: the myth documents social thought rather than supplying an uncomplicated royal chronicle.

### Final assessment

**[EA]** Naymlap is securely a sixteenth-century Lambayeque founder-king tradition, already presented as the origin of a dynasty, sacred image and regional political order. **[AR]** Lambayeque archaeology confirms the kind of complex society presupposed by that tradition. **[NEG]** It does not confirm the man, his twelve-ruler line, his tomb, Yampallec’s surviving image or his portrait in metalwork. **[MSR]** The most defensible reconstruction is therefore neither “pure fiction” nor “verified king”: Naymlap is a colonial-era record of northern Peruvian social memory whose narrative may preserve transformed memories of migration, elite sacralization, regional settlement, climatic catastrophe and conquest. The transformations are themselves the documented history.
