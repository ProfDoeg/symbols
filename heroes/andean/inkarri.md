# Inkarrí: Research Dossier

> **Traditional class:** A post-Conquest Andean **culture hero, creator/founder, defeated Inca king, and eschatological returning ruler**. Individual versions variously call him a god, the Sun’s son, the progenitor of present humanity, an ancient being hostile to present humanity, or a ritual king representing Cusco.
>
> **Earliest secure attestation:** The name and narrative cycle are not securely attested in Inca-period or colonial sources. The first presently identifiable printed notice is a brief tradition in the *Monografía de la provincia de Parinacochas*, published in 1950–1951; the first substantial, securely documented ethnographic collections were made at **Q’ero in 1955** and **Puquio in 1956**. The famous story of a severed head generating a new body is first securely documented in the Puquio material collected by Josafat Roel Pineda and José María Arguedas in 1956—not in an Inca text or sixteenth-century chronicle. [Biblioteca Nacional del Perú](https://bibliotecadigital.bnp.gob.pe/items/9845fe72-1c17-4fc6-a251-3a92256f20fe); [Arguedas text](https://es.scribd.com/document/366785859/Coral-Jose-Maria-Arguedas-pdf); [Peter Elmore review](https://www.scielo.org.pe/scielo.php?pid=S2071-50722023000100012&script=sci_arttext)

## Evidentiary key

- **[EA] Secure early attestation:** the earliest recorded Inkarrí testimony, meaning twentieth-century oral evidence—not necessarily a twentieth-century origin.
- **[F] Folklore:** a dated oral report, performance, or customary practice.
- **[RV] Regional variant:** a geographically localized form that must not be universalized.
- **[CE] Colonial/early-modern antecedent:** an older text, image, execution, drama, or belief later connected with Inkarrí but not itself an attestation of that name or cycle.
- **[LS] Learned systematization:** an interpretation or synthesis created by ethnographers, historians, writers, or artists.
- **[SR] Syncretistic identification:** an equation with a historical ruler, Christian figure, or other mythic being.
- **[MSR] Modern scholarly reconstruction:** a proposed history inferred from separate bodies of evidence.
- **[MI] Modern invention/reinvention:** material presented as traditional or ancient without early documentation, or a modern cultural work knowingly reworking the cycle.
- **[NE] Negative evidence:** an expected connection for which the examined record supplies no direct evidence.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### Orientation

Inkarrí is not a single hero with a stable canonical biography. The name designates a family of Quechua oral narratives and ritual identifications documented principally in southern and central Peru from the mid-twentieth century onward. In one major cluster—Q’ero and related Cusco traditions—he founds Cusco, distributes territory, competes with Qollarrí, and sometimes withdraws toward the eastern forest. In another—Puquio and related Ayacucho traditions—he creates or orders the world, is captured and beheaded by a Spanish king, survives as a living head beneath the earth, regenerates a body, and will return at a final judgment. Elsewhere he may be a dangerous survivor of a former humanity whose return means universal destruction. Treating all these as one ancient “myth of national liberation” suppresses the most important historical fact: the variants disagree about his nature, deeds, enemy, death, and desirability. [Arguedas text](https://es.scribd.com/document/366785859/Coral-Jose-Maria-Arguedas-pdf); [Manuel Gutiérrez Estévez](https://revistas.pucp.edu.pe/index.php/anthropologica/article/download/344/335/1354); [Nelson Manrique review](https://revistas.pucp.edu.pe/index.php/debatesensociologia/article/download/7098/7287)

### Names and etymology

- **Inkarrí, Inkarri, Inkari, Incarri, Inkarriy.** **[F/LS]** Arguedas explained the form in 1968 as a hybrid contraction of Quechua *Inka* and Spanish *rey*: “Inca king.” The final stressed *-rrí* represents the Quechua adaptation/contraction of *rey*, not an independently documented pre-Hispanic divine name. The etymology is highly persuasive but remains a linguistic analysis of a colonial hybrid form. [Arguedas text](https://es.scribd.com/document/366785859/Coral-Jose-Maria-Arguedas-pdf)

- **Inka Qhapaq / Inca Cápac**, “powerful/rich/sovereign Inca.” **[RV]** Used interchangeably with Inkarrí in Q’ero narrations recorded by Thomas Müller in 1979. [Müller and Müller](https://revistas.ucsp.edu.pe/index.php/Allpanchis/article/download/925/721/6362)

- **Qollarrí, Qollarí, Collarrí; Qolla Qhapaq.** **[RV]** Inkarrí’s complementary rival or partner, associated with Qolla territory, Puno, the Altiplano, and in some versions Aymara-speaking populations. The name likewise combines *Qolla* with *rey*. Qollarrí is male in the four late-1970s Q’ero accounts but female in the version published by Óscar Núñez del Prado, demonstrating that gender is variant rather than essential. [Müller and Müller](https://revistas.ucsp.edu.pe/index.php/Allpanchis/article/download/925/721/6362)

- **Españarrí / Españarri**, “Spain-king” or “Spanish king.” **[RV/SR]** Inkarrí’s conqueror in some narratives. The figure can denote the king of Spain, Spanish colonial power collectively, or the Christian-Western order; identifying him specifically with Francisco Pizarro is a later interpretive or narrative choice unless the individual version does so. [Gutiérrez Estévez](https://revistas.pucp.edu.pe/index.php/anthropologica/article/download/344/335/1354)

- **Mistirey / Negrorey.** **[RV/SR]** Names of the third ritual king in the San Pablo Three Kings race; Gutiérrez Estévez interprets this figure as bringing the Inkarrí–Españarrí opposition into a Christian Epiphany performance. *Misti* conventionally denotes a non-Indigenous or Hispanicized person. [Gutiérrez Estévez](https://revistas.pucp.edu.pe/index.php/anthropologica/article/download/344/335/1354)

### Classification in the sources

- **Puquio:** “the first god,” child of the Sun and a wild woman; creator and cosmic organizer. **[EA/RV]**
- **Q’ero:** a hero rather than a god, created by Roal and ancestor of present Indigenous humanity. **[EA/RV]**
- **Q’ero–Cusco competition cycle:** royal founder and territorial culture hero. **[F/RV]**
- **Fuerabamba:** survivor of the pre-solar “gentile” age and enemy of present humanity. **[F/RV]**
- **Modern intellectual synthesis:** anticolonial messiah and national symbol of cultural regeneration. **[LS/MI]**

No secure text gives him a fixed patronymic or a historically continuous dynastic genealogy.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### What the earliest evidence does—and does not—show

The 1950–1951 *Monografía de la provincia de Parinacochas* is bibliographically the earliest identified publication said by later scholarship to contain an Inkarrí notice. Its status should remain qualified until the precise passage, informant, collection date, and wording are established from the scanned volumes. **[EA, qualified]** The bibliographic record securely proves the publication’s date, not by itself every later claim about its Inkarrí passage. [Biblioteca Nacional del Perú](https://bibliotecadigital.bnp.gob.pe/items/9845fe72-1c17-4fc6-a251-3a92256f20fe)

The evidential foundation therefore remains:

1. **Q’ero, 1955.** An expedition directed by Óscar Núñez del Prado collected a foundation story. Early publication supplied Spanish transcriptions but, Arguedas noted in 1968, not the original Quechua. **[EA]**
2. **Puquio, 1956.** Josafat Roel Pineda obtained one account; Arguedas obtained two variants. They were published in *Revista del Museo Nacional* and summarized in “Puquio, una cultura en proceso de cambio” and later essays. These are the earliest secure records of the regenerating head and final return. **[EA]** [Arguedas text](https://es.scribd.com/document/366785859/Coral-Jose-Maria-Arguedas-pdf)
3. **Subsequent collections.** Quinua, Vicos, Urcos, Q’ero, Fuerabamba, Chachas, Pacapauza, Shipetiari, Quero, and other localities produced materially different narratives. **[F/RV]**

### Core Inkarrí corpus

- **Óscar Núñez del Prado, Q’ero collection, 1955; publication 1950s.** Spanish rendering of an oral Quechua account: two humanities; Inkarrí and Qollarrí; foundation and ancestry. **[EA/RV]**
- **José María Arguedas and Josafat Roel Pineda, three Puquio versions, collected 1956.** Creator Inkarrí, Spanish capture, severed head, regeneration, judgment. **[EA/RV]**
- **Efraín Morote Best, Q’ero material, late 1950s.** Foundation-centered rather than principally messianic. **[EA/RV]**
- **Arguedas and Alejandro Ortiz Rescaniere, Vicos and Quinua research, 1963–1965.** Arguedas distinguished narratives dominated by biblical and colonial elements from Q’ero’s foundation account. **[F/RV]**
- **José María Arguedas, “Mitos quechuas post-hispánicos,” 1967/1968.** Comparative learned synthesis of Q’ero, Puquio, Quinua, and Vicos; invaluable but secondary to the individual recordings. **[LS]** [Arguedas text](https://es.scribd.com/document/366785859/Coral-Jose-Maria-Arguedas-pdf)
- **Alejandro Ortiz Rescaniere, “El mito de Inkarrí no es un mito,” 1970, and *De Adaneva a Inkarrí*, 1973.** Structural/comparative reading of Indigenous visions of Peruvian history. The provocative title warns that “myth” is an analytical classification, not an Indigenous genre necessarily isolated from history. **[LS]** [Ortiz Rescaniere archive](https://archivoortizrescaniere.org/tema/mitologia-y-vision-del-mundo/)
- **Juan Ossio, ed., *Ideología mesiánica del mundo andino*, 1973.** The anthology that powerfully established “Andean messianism” as the dominant interpretive frame. **[LS]** [PUCP overview](https://repositorio.pucp.edu.pe/bitstreams/d5a47ed5-97ba-45c5-9a19-b425ac6f47a3/download)
- **Franklin Pease, “Las versiones del mito de Inkarrí,” 1977.** Historical comparison of the growing corpus and its relationship to Andean images of the past. **[LS]** [PUCP repository](https://repositorio.pucp.edu.pe/items/d087e26b-972d-4f4d-8bb4-bc4b6020b9f5/full)
- **Práxides Avendaño de Castro/Alejandro Vivanco, Chachas version, recorded 1976; published 1984.** Inkarrí works irrigation; conflict with the female mountain Coropuna; clay figures “made by Inkarrí” appear in cattle-marking ritual. Quechua text and Spanish translation were printed. **[F/RV]** [Vivanco](https://revistas.pucp.edu.pe/index.php/anthropologica/article/download/656/638/2551)
- **Thomas and Helga Müller, four Q’ero narrations collected 1979–1980; published 1984.** Quechua texts with translations; Inkarrí–Qollarrí competitions, territorial complementarity, three ages, Christian features, and some return motifs. **[F/RV]** [Müller and Müller](https://revistas.ucsp.edu.pe/index.php/Allpanchis/article/download/925/721/6362)
- **Manuel Gutiérrez Estévez, 1984.** Comparative analysis of six Inkarrí–Qollarrí competition stories and the San Pablo ritual. He argues that asymmetric exchange and complementarity may be more central than liberationist messianism. **[LS]** [Gutiérrez Estévez](https://revistas.pucp.edu.pe/index.php/anthropologica/article/download/344/335/1354)
- **Hernán Pantoja narrative, Amazonas, published 1980s.** Proposed as a northern localized transformation of Inkarrí; the identification is scholarly and comparative rather than the narrator’s necessarily explicit equation. **[RV/MSR]** [DOAJ](https://doaj.org/article/b01b74d2622e49ab9932ea3f23e16af6)
- **Shipetiari and Quero versions, published by Aurélie Omer in 2015.** Later additions from Madre de Dios and Cusco showing continued geographic and formal variation. **[F/RV]** [JSTOR](https://www.jstor.org/stable/44475395)

### Colonial and early-modern comparator corpus

None of the following securely names Inkarrí. They document motifs, historical executions, Inca origin traditions, or theatrical memories later brought into Inkarrí studies:

- **Juan de Betanzos, *Suma y narración de los incas*, c. 1551:** Ayar and Viracocha traditions, dynastic accounts, conquest testimony. **[CE/NE]**
- **Pedro Cieza de León, *Crónica del Perú*, 1550s:** Inca origins and conquest history. **[CE/NE]**
- **Pedro Sarmiento de Gamboa, *Historia de los incas*, 1572:** Toledo-era dynastic history. **[CE/NE]**
- **Miguel Cabello Valboa, *Miscelánea antártica*, 1586:** Naymlap tradition of the north coast. Naymlap is a separate founder; no direct Inkarrí interaction is recorded. **[CE/NE]**
- **Martín de Murúa, histories composed c. 1590–1616:** Inca history and an account drawing on testimony about Túpac Amaru I’s execution. **[CE/NE]**
- **Huarochirí Manuscript, Quechua, c. 1598–1608:** regional huaca cycles involving Pariacaca and other powers. Its transformations, territorial conflicts, and sacred landscape provide comparison, not an Inkarrí attestation. **[CE/NE]** [University of Texas/JSTOR edition](https://www.jstor.org/stable/10.7560/730526)
- **Inca Garcilaso de la Vega, *Comentarios reales*, 1609; *Historia general del Perú*, 1617:** Manco Cápac and Mama Ocllo, golden staff, civilizing foundation, conquest. These motifs help explain later comparisons but do not prove a pre-Hispanic Inkarrí. **[CE/NE]**
- **Felipe Guaman Poma de Ayala, *Nueva corónica y buen gobierno*, c. 1615:** illustrated Inca history, conquest, Christian-periodization, and political critique; no named Inkarrí cycle. **[CE/NE]**
- **Bernabé Cobo, *Historia del Nuevo Mundo*, completed 1653:** compilation of Inca religion and history; no named Inkarrí. **[CE/NE]**
- **Anonymous of Trujillo traditions concerning Tacaynamo:** separate north-coast foundation material; no interaction or genealogical relation to Inkarrí is documented. **[CE/NE]**
- ***Ollantay*, Quechua drama:** surviving manuscripts are colonial/republican. The Justiniani copy was said to descend from a Valdez text associated with c. 1770; the Sahuaraura manuscript is dated 1838. César Itier argues for composition around the Túpac Amaru rebellion, approximately 1782; older scholarship defended a pre-Hispanic core. It contains Ollantay, Pachacútec, Cusi Coyllur, and Túpac Yupanqui—not Inkarrí. **[CE/NE]** [Itier article](https://biblat.unam.mx/hevila/HistoricaLima/2006/vol30/no1/2.pdf); [Sahuaraura text record](https://publicaciones.fcnym.unlp.edu.ar/rmlp/article/view/1120)

The historical gap is decisive: **no securely identified sixteenth-, seventeenth-, eighteenth-, or nineteenth-century document records the complete named Inkarrí story**.

## 3. The story: cycles and variants

### A. Puquio creation, defeat, and regeneration

In Arguedas’s 1968 synthesis of the three accounts collected in 1956:

1. The *wamanis*, sacred mountains, are “second gods,” protecting humans and supplying water.
2. Inkarrí is the first god, born from the Sun and a wild woman.
3. He makes what exists on earth.
4. He binds the Sun on Mount Osqonta and confines or controls the wind so that creation can be completed.
5. He throws a golden bar to determine the site of Cusco.
6. The Spanish king captures, tortures, and decapitates him.
7. His head is taken to Cusco and remains alive.
8. Beneath the earth a body grows downward from the head.
9. Because Inkarrí presently lacks power, his laws and will are not obeyed.
10. When the body is complete, he will return and the Last Judgment will occur.
11. Coastal birds supposedly confirm his presence by singing, “In Cusco, the king” and “Go to Cusco.”

All these propositions are **[EA/F/RV]**, attested to the 1956 Puquio collection—not verified history. [Arguedas text](https://es.scribd.com/document/366785859/Coral-Jose-Maria-Arguedas-pdf)

Important cautions:

- The accessible synthesis says **Cusco**, not Lima, holds the head. **[EA]**
- Versions placing the head under Lima’s Government Palace or distributing limbs among Lima, Cusco, and Ayacucho are later regional or popular elaborations and require individual provenance. **[RV]**
- The Puquio account predicts judgment but does not, in Arguedas’s quoted synopsis, spell out the modern formula “he will defeat all Spaniards and restore the Inca Empire.” That formula is part of the later canonical synthesis. **[LS/MI]**

### B. Q’ero foundation and ancestry

Arguedas’s synopsis of the account collected in 1955 gives two humanities:

1. The **ñawpa/awpa**, ancient people, live in dim lunar light and possess enormous strength.
2. The god **Roal** destroys them by drying them with the Sun’s burning light, turning them into *soqa*, mummified ancient beings.
3. Roal creates Inkarrí and Qollarrí, here male and female.
4. They are parents—not creators—of present Indigenous humanity.
5. Inkarrí travels, throws a golden bar, and founds Cusco where it sinks.
6. He populates the world with wise humanity.
7. Q’ero people descend from Inkarrí’s firstborn son.
8. Inkarrí visits Q’ero and finally disappears into the eastern forest, associated in local songs with death.

This is **[EA/RV]**. It contains neither Spanish conquest nor Christian gods in the version Arguedas summarized. His description of it as completely uncontaminated by post-Hispanic material is an interpretive judgment, not proof that the narrative was unchanged from Inca times. [Arguedas text](https://es.scribd.com/document/366785859/Coral-Jose-Maria-Arguedas-pdf)

### C. Inkarrí and Qollarrí: competition and exchange

The later Q’ero/Cusco corpus depicts rival territorial rulers:

- They race or compete in eating.
- Inkarrí rapidly consumes Qollarrí’s cheese or cañihua products; Qollarrí has difficulty with Inkarrí’s toasted broad beans.
- They exchange highland and Cusco-area products.
- Each throws a golden bar toward his respective territory.
- Inkarrí’s bar passes through Rumicolca/Huacarpay and establishes Cusco over a former lake.
- La Raya marks the division between Cusco and Qolla/Puno spheres.
- Inkarrí normally has precedence, but the opposition is complementary rather than a simple extermination of the loser.

These are **[F/RV]**, especially well documented in four narratives recorded in 1979–1980. Gutiérrez Estévez’s claim that their fundamental subject is unequal reciprocity rather than messianic revolution is **[LS]**. [Müller and Müller](https://revistas.ucsp.edu.pe/index.php/Allpanchis/article/download/925/721/6362); [Gutiérrez Estévez](https://revistas.pucp.edu.pe/index.php/anthropologica/article/download/344/335/1354)

### D. Inkarrí and Españarrí

Some narratives introduce Españarrí as the new royal power:

- Españarrí defeats or deceives Inkarrí.
- He imprisons him, cuts off his head, and removes it from the body.
- The separated head and body grow toward reunion.
- Reunion may promise Inkarrí’s restored sovereignty.

This is **[F/RV]**. “Españarrí = Pizarro” or “Españarrí = Charles V” is **[SR]** unless a given narrator says so. Gutiérrez Estévez proposed that the opposition could encode a frustrated search for reciprocal relations between Andean and Spanish orders rather than simply a prophecy of racial war. **[LS]** [Gutiérrez Estévez](https://revistas.pucp.edu.pe/index.php/anthropologica/article/download/344/335/1354)

### E. Urcos and the three ages

A Urcos version recorded by Manuel Marzal combines:

- the age of the Father, associated with pre-Christian “gentiles”;
- the age of the Son, associated with Spanish domination and suffering;
- the future age of the Holy Spirit, when land will return to peasants;
- Inkarrí’s return as the sign of transition.

This is **[F/RV/SR]**. Flores Galindo connected it with Andean *pachacuti*, Christian apocalypse, the mystical body of Christ, and Joachimite three-age history. That genealogy is a historical hypothesis, **[MSR]**, not a demonstrated direct transmission line from Joachim of Fiore to every narrator. [Nelson Manrique review](https://revistas.pucp.edu.pe/index.php/debatesensociologia/article/download/7098/7287)

### F. Fuerabamba: anti-messianic Inkarrí

In the Fuerabamba account recorded by Ricardo Valderrama and Carmen Escalante:

- Inkarrí belongs to the former *gentile* humanity.
- He and companions survive the Sun’s emergence by hiding underground in *Ukhu Pacha*.
- He rejoices when the Sun and Moon weaken.
- His return, accompanied by the gentiles, means present humanity will be crushed or burned at divine judgment.
- Asked whether he desired Inkarrí’s return, the narrator emphatically rejected it.

This is **[F/RV]** and directly disproves the claim that Inkarrí is everywhere a benevolent national liberator. The explanation that hostile local memory reflects communities once subjugated by the Incas is plausible **[MSR]**, not a fact stated by the story. [Nelson Manrique review](https://revistas.pucp.edu.pe/index.php/debatesensociologia/article/download/7098/7287)

### G. Chachas: irrigation and Coropuna

In Práxides Avendaño de Castro’s 1976 narration:

- Inkarrí digs an irrigation channel and conducts water.
- The mountain Coropuna blocks it.
- Coropuna is female and becomes pregnant.
- She demands implements or precious equipment for the child; after Inkarrí’s refusal or denial of paternity, she threatens and emits destructive fire.
- Local cattle-marking ritual includes clay figures described as *Inkarriypa ruwasqan*, “things made by Inkarrí.”

This is **[F/RV]** and shows a creator concerned with water, livestock, and a female mountain power rather than a severed messiah. [Vivanco](https://revistas.pucp.edu.pe/index.php/anthropologica/article/download/656/638/2551)

### H. Paititi and forest withdrawal

Some Cusco/Amazonian versions make Inkarrí withdraw toward Pantiacolla or Paititi after founding Q’ero or Cusco. **[RV]** This should not be retrojected into all versions, and archaeological or treasure-hunting claims about Paititi do not constitute evidence for Inkarrí’s historicity. The earliest Q’ero synopsis says only that he disappears into the great forest. [Study of the Inca’s mythical refuge](https://www.scielo.cl/scielo.php?pid=S0719-26812017000300103&script=sci_arttext)

## 4. Relations with gods and other heroes

### Genealogical alternatives

| Version | Parentage/origin | Partner or counterpart | Descendants/status |
|---|---|---|---|
| Puquio, 1956 | Son of the Sun and an unnamed wild woman | No fixed consort in the synopsis | First god and creator |
| Q’ero, 1955 | Created by Roal | Qollarrí, female in this version | Parents of present Indigenous humanity; Q’ero descend from Inkarrí’s firstborn |
| Later Q’ero, 1979–1980 | Origin often unstated | Qollarrí usually male rival | Territorial rulers representing Cusco and Qolla |
| Chachas, 1976 | Unstated | Coropuna claims he fathered her child; Inkarrí denies it | Disputed child |
| Historical synthesis | Symbolically identified with Atahualpa, Túpac Amaru I, or Túpac Amaru II | None | Returning Inca |
| Fuerabamba | Member/survivor of ancient humanity | Fellow *gentiles* | Enemy of present humanity |

### Documented named interactions

Each line below identifies an interaction, its source/date, and evidence tier.

- **Roal** — Creates Inkarrí and Qollarrí after destroying the former humanity with solar heat; Q’ero account collected 1955, summarized by Arguedas in 1968. **[EA/RV]**
- **Inti / the Sun** — Inkarrí is his son in the Puquio accounts; Inkarrí binds him on Mount Osqonta. In Q’ero, Roal instead uses the Sun as an instrument to destroy the ancients. Puquio 1956 and Q’ero 1955. **[EA/RV]**
- **Qollarrí / Collarrí / Qolla Qhapaq** — Co-created partner and co-parent in one Q’ero version; male rival in later versions; exchanges food, races, and throws a golden bar in territorial competitions. Q’ero 1955 and 1979–1980; comparative corpus published 1984. **[EA/F/RV]**
- **Españarrí / Spanish King** — Captures, tortures or defeats, and beheads Inkarrí; Puquio 1956 and later comparative versions. **[EA/F/RV]**
- **Coropuna** — Female mountain blocks Inkarrí’s irrigation water, claims pregnancy by him, demands goods for the child, and burns the land after his denial; Chachas account recorded 1976. **[F/RV]**
- **Wamanis** — Collective named class rather than individual heroes: mountains are secondary gods and human protectors in the Puquio synthesis; Inkarrí is ranked above them. Puquio 1956. **[EA/RV]**
- **Taytacha / Christian Lord** — In Fuerabamba, the Lord burns or destroys present humanity at the judgment accompanying Inkarrí’s return; collection published before 1988. **[F/RV/SR]**
- **God the Father** — His age is the first of three historical epochs in the Urcos synthesis; twentieth-century collection analyzed by Flores Galindo. **[F/RV/SR]**
- **Jesus Christ / God the Son** — His age corresponds to colonial suffering; in San Pablo’s Epiphany ritual the three kings race to adore the Christ Child. Twentieth-century Urcos tradition and San Pablo ritual documented in 1973/1984. **[F/SR]**
- **Holy Spirit** — The future third age, associated in the Urcos interpretation with restoration of peasant land and Inkarrí’s return. **[F/RV/SR]**
- **Christ Child / Niño Jesús** — Object of worship by Inkarrí, Collarrí, and Mistirey in the San Pablo Three Kings performance. Ritual described from 1973 testimony, analyzed in 1984. **[F/SR]**
- **Mistirey / Negrorey** — Third king racing against Inkarrí and Collarrí in San Pablo; his victory has agricultural consequences. Documented in 1973 testimony and the 1984 study. **[F/RV/SR]**
- **Manco Cápac** — No narrative meeting. Scholars compare Inkarrí’s golden bar, Cusco foundation, solar relationship, and civilizing role with colonial accounts of Manco Cápac. **[MSR/NE]**
- **Mama Ocllo** — No narrative meeting. Qollarrí’s female form was compared with Mama Ocllo in the Manco Cápac foundation tradition. **[MSR/NE]**
- **Viracocha** — No documented encounter. Similar creator and world-ordering traits are comparative, not an attested equation. **[MSR/NE]**
- **Ayar siblings** — No documented interaction. Mountain-altering power, migration, and Cusco foundation are parallel motifs cited by Arguedas from Betanzos, Cieza, and Sarmiento. **[MSR/NE]**
- **Pariacaca, Huallallo Carhuincho, Cuniraya, and other Huarochirí beings** — No documented Inkarrí interaction. **[CE/NE]**
- **Ollantay, Pachacútec, Cusi Coyllur, Túpac Yupanqui** — No interaction in *Ollantay* or the Inkarrí corpus examined. **[CE/NE]**
- **Naymlap** — No interaction; separate north-coast founder. **[CE/NE]**
- **Tacaynamo** — No interaction; separate Chimú founder. **[CE/NE]**
- **Atahualpa** — Not an encounter but a later identification: conquest drama and scholarship place Atahualpa’s death behind Inkarrí. Atahualpa was actually garroted after baptism in 1533, not beheaded, though later dramatic tradition often stages decapitation. **[SR/MSR]**
- **Túpac Amaru I** — Later identification based on his actual public beheading at Cusco in 1572 and the political destruction of Vilcabamba. **[SR/MSR]**
- **Túpac Amaru II / José Gabriel Condorcanqui** — Later identification based on the 1780–1781 rebellion, attempted dismemberment, beheading, and distribution of remains. **[SR/MSR]**
- **Juan Santos Atahualpa** — Some scholarship associates his eighteenth-century Amazonian rebellion and disappearance traditions with the returning-Inca complex; no early secure Inkarrí meeting or identity is demonstrated. **[SR/MSR]**

## 5. Cult, tomb, and ritual

### Tomb and body

There is **no archaeologically identified tomb, relic, mummy, shrine, or *huaca* securely attributable to Inkarrí as a historical person**. **[NE]** Cusco, Lima, subterranean mountains, lagoons, Vilcabamba, and Paititi are mythic locations whose meaning changes by version. The claim that the head physically lies beneath Lima’s Government Palace is folklore, not an excavated burial. The securely summarized Puquio account instead locates the living head in Cusco. [Study of the mythical refuge](https://www.scielo.cl/scielo.php?pid=S0719-26812017000300103&script=sci_arttext)

### Ritual evidence

- **San Pablo, Canchis: Three Kings race.** Each 6 January, performers identified as Inkarrí, Collarrí, and Negrorey/Mistirey ride to the Christ Child. Their order predicts agricultural prosperity: an Inkarrí victory favors Cusco; a Collarrí victory favors Qolla country. This is direct ritual evidence for Inkarrí’s incorporation into a Christian festival, documented through testimony collected in 1973 and analyzed in 1984. **[F/SR]** [Gutiérrez Estévez](https://revistas.pucp.edu.pe/index.php/anthropologica/article/download/344/335/1354)

- **Chachas cattle-marking.** Clay figures set out during the vigil were called “things made by Inkarrí.” This is an association within livestock ritual, not proof of sacrifice or a dedicated Inkarrí cult. **[F/RV]** [Vivanco](https://revistas.pucp.edu.pe/index.php/anthropologica/article/download/656/638/2551)

- **Death-of-Atahualpa dramas.** Annual representations of the Inca’s death occur in several Andean communities. They belong to a related but distinguishable performance cycle. Some end in restoration or implied return; others legitimate conquest or are anti-utopian. They cannot all be classified as Inkarrí ritual. **[F/CE]** [Nelson Manrique review](https://revistas.pucp.edu.pe/index.php/debatesensociologia/article/download/7098/7287)

### What is not documented

No verified evidence was found for:

- a priesthood devoted exclusively to Inkarrí;
- a pre-Hispanic temple bearing his name;
- standardized sacrifices offered directly to him;
- canonical prayers or a pan-Andean feast day;
- an authenticated physical relic;
- uninterrupted cult from Inca times.

The ritual record is local, syncretic, and thin compared with the extensive modern literature about the myth.

## 6. Historical evolution

### Before 1532: possible motifs, no Inkarrí attestation

**[CE/NE]** Inca origin traditions later recorded by Betanzos, Cieza, Sarmiento, Garcilaso, and others contain solar ancestry, civilizing founders, golden staffs or bars, transformed landscapes, sacred centers, and successive humanities. These may have furnished narrative resources. They do not document the named post-Conquest hero.

### 1532–1572: historical catastrophes later absorbed

- **1533:** Atahualpa is executed by garrote at Cajamarca after a judicial proceeding controlled by Pizarro. Later dramas and popular memory frequently turn the death into decapitation. **[CE]**
- **1572:** Túpac Amaru I is publicly beheaded in Cusco after Toledo’s conquest of Vilcabamba. Eyewitness-derived accounts occur in Gonzalo de Oviedo, Baltasar de Ocampo, Murúa, and official documentation. **[CE]** [Colonial Latin American Review](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/10609160701644524)

The composite severed Inca plausibly draws on both deaths, but no surviving 1572 source says that Túpac Amaru’s head began regenerating a body. That connection is **[MSR]**.

### Sixteenth–seventeenth centuries: religious transformation

Taki Onqoy, extirpation records, Christian eschatology, three-age schemes, and beliefs that the Inca might return or be resurrected provide a historical environment for later narratives. Solórzano Pereira reported in 1648 that some believed the Inca would resurrect and guarded rich mines for him. This is important evidence for colonial returning-Inca belief, but it is not yet the named Inkarrí story. **[CE]** [Anthropologica](https://revistas.pucp.edu.pe/index.php/anthropologica/article/download/923/889/3549)

### Eighteenth century: rebellion and dramatic memory

Juan Santos Atahualpa’s movement and disappearance, the Túpac Amaru II rebellion, and performances of Atahualpa’s death intensified the returning-Inca field. José Gabriel Condorcanqui’s failed drawing-and-quartering, beheading, and dispersal of remains supplied an especially close historical analogue to bodily fragmentation. Nevertheless, one must not assume that every rebel followed an Inkarrí prophecy. **[CE/MSR]**

*Ollantay* probably belongs substantially to the colonial period; Itier connects its composition to the Túpac Amaru era. Its merciful dynastic resolution is relevant to Inca political imagination but it does not name Inkarrí. **[CE/NE]**

### Nineteenth to early twentieth centuries

Elite antiquarianism, indigenismo, theatrical performance, and political invocations of the Inca preserved and reshaped images of an ideal precolonial order. Evidence for a continuous, universally expected Inkarrí return is absent. Accusations that peasant movements intended to restore the Inca state were sometimes landlords’ pretexts for repression rather than the peasants’ documented program. **[NE/MSR]** [Nelson Manrique review](https://revistas.pucp.edu.pe/index.php/debatesensociologia/article/download/7098/7287)

### 1950–1968: ethnographic emergence

The Parinacochas notice, Q’ero expedition, and Puquio fieldwork make Inkarrí visible in print. Arguedas observed that at Puquio the tradition was already receding among younger people. Thus its entry into national intellectual life was not a transparent measure of expanding village belief. **[EA]** [Elmore review](https://www.scielo.org.pe/scielo.php?pid=S2071-50722023000100012&script=sci_arttext)

### 1970s–1980s: canonization as “Andean messianism”

Ortiz Rescaniere, Ossio, Pease, Flores Galindo, Manuel Burga, and others made the cycle central to interpretations of conquest memory and Andean historical consciousness. New collections simultaneously demonstrated that many variants were non-messianic, ambivalent, or hostile to Inkarrí. **[LS]**

### 1990s–present: critique, diversification, and cultural circulation

Later work emphasizes regional specificity, interviewer–informant mediation, translation, migration, performance, and the literary production of a “canonical” Inkarrí. New records from Amazonian and Cusco communities show continuing narrative change. The figure now circulates in scholarship, art, fiction, political rhetoric, tourism, internet spirituality, and New Age prophecy. **[LS/MI]**

## 7. Modern reception and reinvention

### Literature and criticism

- **José María Arguedas:** After the 1956 discovery, Inkarrí’s tones and images influenced his interpretation of Andean historical consciousness and have been read in relation to *Los ríos profundos* (1958), his Quechua poetry, and later fiction. Attribution of every resurrection image in his novels directly to Inkarrí remains critical interpretation. **[LS]**
- **Manuel Scorza, *La tumba del relámpago* (1979):** employs Inkarrí to figure a proposed movement from mythic consciousness toward political action. **[MI]**
- **Laura Riesco, *Ximena de dos caminos* (1994):** makes the story morally and emotionally formative for a child protagonist. **[MI]**
- **Santiago Roncagliolo, *Abril rojo* (2006):** uses Inkarrí in a thriller about Ayacucho and postwar violence; critics have noted its construction of exoticized Andean otherness. **[MI]** [Elmore review](https://www.scielo.org.pe/scielo.php?pid=S2071-50722023000100012&script=sci_arttext)
- Inkarrí also appears in Quechua songs, poetry, theater, and contemporary “oraliture”; each composition is evidence of reception, not automatic evidence for ancient belief. **[MI/F]** [Cantos quechuas study](https://dialnet.unirioja.es/servlet/articulo?codigo=8238909)

### Visual art

- **Fernando de Szyszlo, *Inkarri* (1968):** acrylic on wood, 150.5 × 150.5 cm, now in the Blanton Museum of Art. Its red-black abstract field and fragmented anthropomorphic forms gave the modern visual canon one of its most influential images. **[MI]** [Google Arts & Culture](https://artsandculture.google.com/asset/inkarri-fernando-de-szyszlo/bQG3EcunQrSRpQ?hl=en-GB)
- **Juan Javier Salazar, *Algo va’pasar* (1980):** reworks expectations of imminent transformation. **[MI]**
- **Jaime Higa and Eduardo Tokeshi, *Uchuraccay* (1985):** marks a shift from optimistic messianism toward mourning amid Peru’s internal armed conflict. **[MI]** [Elmore review](https://www.scielo.org.pe/scielo.php?pid=S2071-50722023000100012&script=sci_arttext)

### Nationalism and political appropriation

Alberto Flores Galindo’s *Buscando un Inca: identidad y utopía en los Andes* (first published 1986) defined “Andean utopia” as plural projects formed where memory and imagination met to envision the Inca’s return and a reconstructed society. It did not simply claim that every Andean peasant literally awaited a resurrected monarch. **[LS]**

Critics identify several problems:

- the genuinely messianic Urcos account was treated as more representative than non-messianic versions;
- negative Inkarrí traditions and regions without Inkarrí were underweighted;
- the proposed pan-Andean extent—from Quito toward Tucumán—exceeded the strongest evidence;
- post-1920 peasant mobilizations often lack proof that Inkarrí belief directed them;
- the concept sometimes slides from literal Inca restoration to any radical fusion of Andean traditions and Marxism.

These criticisms were clearly articulated in scholarly debate over Flores Galindo and Burga. [Nelson Manrique review](https://revistas.pucp.edu.pe/index.php/debatesensociologia/article/download/7098/7287)

The Velasco government’s official exaltation of Túpac Amaru II helped place Inca and rebel imagery at the center of national culture, but that does not mean the state instituted a literal Inkarrí cult. Likewise, assertions that Sendero Luminoso was simply Inkarrí millenarianism flatten its Maoist doctrine and the documented opposition of many Quechua communities. Attempts to read every insurgency through the myth are **[LS]**, often poorly evidenced.

### Modern inventions and unstable claims

The following should not be represented as ancient pan-Andean doctrine:

- a single prophecy in which Inkarrí’s head is under Lima’s Government Palace, arms under Cusco, and legs in Ayacucho;
- an exact timetable for bodily regeneration;
- the “Eagle and Condor” prophecy as an ancient Inkarrí oracle;
- claims that archaeological finds at Paititi will reveal Inkarrí’s living body;
- seamless identity among Atahualpa, Túpac Amaru I, Túpac Amaru II, Juan Santos Atahualpa, and Inkarrí;
- claims that all Indigenous Peruvians expect restoration of Tawantinsuyu;
- assertions of a pre-Hispanic Inkarrí scripture or temple.

These are **[RV]**, **[LS]**, or **[MI]** depending on provenance—not securely attested Inca doctrine.

## 8. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Was Inkarrí a historical person?

No evidence establishes a separate ruler named Inkarrí. The form means “Inca king,” and narratives fuse offices, creators, founders, and memories of several executed rulers. Inkarrí’s “historicity” lies in the historical experiences encoded and reworked—not in a recoverable individual biography. **[NE/MSR]**

### Is the myth pre-Hispanic?

Three positions must be separated:

1. **Pre-Hispanic survival:** golden bar, solar origin, successive humanities, sacred mountains, and foundation of Cusco descend from Inca traditions. This is credible at the motif level. **[MSR]**
2. **Post-Conquest formation:** Españarrí, decapitation by Spaniards, Last Judgment, biblical ages, and resurrection respond to colonial history and Christian teaching. This is directly supported by narrative content. **[F/MSR]**
3. **Modern canonical synthesis:** the concise story of the decapitated anticolonial Inca who will reunite and restore justice was refined after the 1950s through ethnography, anthologies, art, and politics. Peter Elmore argues that this canon corresponds exactly to none of the original field versions. **[LS]** [Elmore review](https://revista.letras.unmsm.edu.pe/index.php/le/article/download/2469/2564?inline=1)

The best-supported conclusion is neither “pure Inca survival” nor “academic fabrication”: locally transmitted narratives carried older and colonial materials, while twentieth-century intellectual practice selected and combined them into a national myth.

### Atahualpa or Túpac Amaru I?

- **Atahualpa:** supplies the paradigmatic capture of the Inca and the collapse at Cajamarca; death-of-the-Inca drama often makes him beheaded.
- **Túpac Amaru I:** supplies the historical public decapitation in Cusco.
- **Túpac Amaru II:** supplies attempted dismemberment, beheading, and territorial dispersion of body parts.

The composite is historically intelligible, but the earliest Puquio synopsis calls the victim Inkarrí and his killer the Spanish king; it does not require choosing one prototype. **[MSR]**

### Messianism or reciprocity?

- **Ossio, Pease, Ortiz Rescaniere, and Flores Galindo:** emphasized eschatology, conquest memory, and restoration. **[LS]**
- **Gutiérrez Estévez:** emphasized exchange and complementary opposition in Inkarrí–Qollarrí narratives; even Inkarrí–Españarrí could express a demand for reciprocity. **[LS]**
- **Henrique Urbano and critics of pan-Andean messianism:** argued that benevolent messianic Inkarrí is an exception rather than the rule. **[LS]**
- **Elmore:** relocates the canonical messianic version within the history of the Peruvian intellectual field after 1956. **[LS]**

No single model accounts for the whole corpus.

### Oral tradition and mediation

The evidence passed through interviews, translation from Quechua, transcription, editorial selection, and scholarly comparison. Q’ero’s first account circulated initially without its Quechua original; the Müller recordings later supplied bilingual texts. Informants sometimes responded to questions framed by researchers already seeking Inkarrí. This does not invalidate the traditions; it means the published text is a jointly produced historical artifact.

### Geographic extent

Securely documented centers include Ayacucho, Cusco, Apurímac, Ancash, Arequipa, Amazonas, and Madre de Dios. Claims for an unbroken Inkarrí zone from Quito to Tucumán are not demonstrated. Flores Galindo himself described “islands and archipelagos,” while critics found the evidence strongest in southern Peru. The requested Argentine-northwest pass did not locate a securely sourced Argentine oral text explicitly naming Inkarrí comparable to Puquio or Q’ero. Broader returning-Inca, hidden-king, and Andean millenarian traditions certainly crossed modern borders, but that is not identical evidence. **[NE]**

### Tomb and cult controversy

The underground head has encouraged literal searches and touristic localization, but there is no archaeological Inkarrí tomb. San Pablo’s race and Chachas ritual are the strongest cult-like evidence, yet neither establishes a centralized hero cult. **[NE]**

### The record’s principal losses

- the absence of a pre-Conquest phonetic text;
- lack of a securely identified colonial Inkarrí narrative;
- missing or unpublished original Quechua for some first collections;
- imperfect recording of informant biography and elicitation conditions;
- receding local knowledge already observed by Arguedas in 1956;
- uncertain provenance for widely repeated Lima/body-parts versions.
