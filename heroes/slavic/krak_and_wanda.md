# Krak and Wanda: Research Dossier

> **Figure class:** Krak is a legendary mortal ruler, founder, lawgiver, dragon-cycle culture hero, and later “epic king”; Wanda is his legendary mortal daughter and successor, subsequently shaped into a virgin queen, national martyr, and quasi-sacral heroine. Neither has an independently verified biography or securely documented ancient cult.
>
> **Earliest secure attestation of the figures:** Wincenty Kadłubek’s Latin *Chronica Polonorum*, Book I, composed about 1190–1208. Its archetype is lost; the oldest surviving witness is Vienna, Österreichische Nationalbibliothek MS 480, early fourteenth century. The city of Kraków itself is historical and is attested centuries earlier—about 965 in the account preserved from Ibrāhīm ibn Yaʿqūb—but that notice says nothing about Krak or Wanda.
>
> **Corpus correction:** Krak and Wanda are not heroes of the East Slavic *byliny*. They belong to Polish Latin historiography and its later literary, visual, local, and educational traditions. Searches of the Kirsha Danilov, Rybnikov, and A. F. Hilferding/Gilferding corpora; Bailey and Ivanova’s anthology; the Kyiv and Novgorod cycles; the *Primary Chronicle*; Russian *lubok* traditions; Vasnetsov’s *Bogatyrs*; and Rimsky-Korsakov’s *Sadko* produced no Krak-or-Wanda episode. Their inclusion in a “byliny heroes” category is a modern classificatory error.

## Evidence labels

- **[EA] Secure early attestation:** the earliest datable textual, documentary, or archaeological evidence.
- **[MC] Medieval canonical elaboration:** later medieval chronicle development.
- **[RE] Regional or textual variant.**
- **[LS] Learned systematization:** antiquarian, etymological, genealogical, or historiographic construction.
- **[EM] Early-modern accretion.**
- **[F] Folklore:** orally collected belief or performance, not merely a literary story called “folklore.”
- **[MR] Modern scholarly reconstruction:** an argued interpretation, not primary evidence.
- **[MI] Modern invention or adaptation.**
- **[NEG] Negative finding:** absence in a searched or relevant corpus.
- **[ARCH] Archaeological evidence.**

## 1. Identification, Names and Epithets

### Orientation

**[EA]** Kadłubek introduces *Gracchus* as the ruler chosen after fabulous Polish victories against external enemies. He establishes laws, gives civic order priority over private interest, confronts a ravenous monster through his sons, and becomes the eponymous founder of *Gracchovia*. After his death, a son kills his brother and is expelled; Krak’s only daughter, *Vanda*, succeeds. Wanda’s beauty or numinous majesty causes an invading ruler’s army to refuse battle; the invader kills himself while cursing his men to rule beneath women. Kadłubek does **not** make Wanda drown herself.

**[MC]** The *Chronicle of Greater Poland* converts this invasion into a rejected marriage proposal, makes the invader an Alemannic ruler, and adds Wanda’s voluntary death in the Vistula as thanksgiving to pagan gods. Długosz supplies the name Rytigier/Rüdiger, a bridge, an extended sacrificial rationale, named sons Krak and Lech, and physical identifications of the Krak and Wanda mounds. Marcin Bielski’s Polish chronicle of 1597 gives the dragon-killing stratagem to the cobbler Skub/Skuba.

Thus “Krak and Wanda” is not one stable tale. It is a connected dossier whose principal strata are:

1. Kadłubek’s constitutional and dynastic foundation myth;
2. the later medieval rejected-suitor and self-sacrifice story;
3. Długosz’s antiquarian topography and national history;
4. Bielski’s commoner-dragon-slayer version;
5. nineteenth-century patriotic and Romantic national myth;
6. twentieth- and twenty-first-century children’s, civic, touristic, theatrical, and neo-pagan adaptations.

### Disputed etymologies

**[MR] Krak.** The safest linguistic proposition is that *Kraków* is a possessive formation, “Krak’s place,” from the personal name *Krak*. Other proposals connect *krak* with “raven/crow,” an oak or branching object, or a judicial staff/sceptre (*krakula*). The resemblance to Czech Krok has produced theories of shared tradition or literary dependence. None establishes a historical King Krak. Kadłubek himself offers two literary explanations: the city commemorated Gracchus, while an alternative derived its name from the croaking of ravens drawn to the dead monster.

**[MR] Wanda.** Aleksander Brückner influentially argued that Wanda was Kadłubek’s learned creation from *Vandalus/Vandali*. Competing scholarship looks for an older local tale, an Indo-European sovereign-maiden pattern, a parallel to Libuše, or Germanic heroic material behind Rüdiger. These are reconstructions. No pre-Kadłubek inscription, personal name, song, or independent narrative verifies an ancient Wanda.

### Identifications and equations

- **Krak—Czech Krok:** **[MR]** Comparison based on similar names and corresponding founder/judge roles; Cosmas’s Krok is earlier in writing, but direct borrowing is debated.
- **Wanda—Libuše:** **[MR]** Both are daughters and successors of a founder-judge, praised for beauty and wisdom. Wanda lacks Libuše’s developed prophecy and marriage-to-ploughman cycle.
- **Wanda—Vandals/Vistula:** **[LS]** Kadłubek’s explicit learned equation.
- **Rytigier—German heroic Rüdiger:** **[MR]** Długosz’s name has been compared with Rüdiger of German heroic poetry and the *Þiðreks saga* tradition. Resemblance does not demonstrate direct transmission.
- **Krak/Wanda as deities:** **[MI/MR]** Modern mythographic and neo-pagan websites sometimes promote them to pre-Christian gods. No medieval source calls either divine, and no inscription, sanctuary, or contemporary cult-title supports that classification.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### The evidentiary threshold

**[EA]** Ibrāhīm ibn Yaʿqūb’s notice of Kraków, c. 965, proves the place existed as an important settlement; it does not mention its legendary founder.

**[NEG]** Gallus Anonymous’s *Gesta principum Polonorum*, c. 1112–1116, does not name Krak or Wanda. Gallus begins the Polish dynastic sequence with the Popiel–Piast material and explicitly passes over pagan-age persons lost in oblivion. His silence does not disprove an oral tale, but it prevents dating the figures from his work.

**[EA]** Kadłubek’s *Chronica Polonorum*, composed c. 1190–1208 in learned Latin, is the first secure evidence. The original is lost. The oldest extant manuscript is early fourteenth-century Vienna MS 480; numerous fifteenth-century “school” manuscripts transmit the work with Jan of Dąbrówka’s commentary. Marian Plezia’s 1994 *Monumenta Poloniae Historica, series nova*, vol. XI, is the standard critical edition.

### The requested *byliny* corpus

**[NEG]** The eighteenth-century Kirsha Danilov manuscript and its editions, Rybnikov’s 1859–1867 recordings, Hilferding’s 318 Onega songs recorded in 1871, Bailey and Ivanova’s *Anthology of Russian Folk Epics*, and scholarship by Propp and Oinas concern East Slavic epic heroes such as Ilya Muromets, Dobrynya Nikitich, Alyosha Popovich, Svyatogor, Volkh, Sadko, and Vasily Buslayev. They do not document Krak or Wanda.

**[NEG]** Likewise, the Kyiv and Novgorod cycles, the Russian *Primary Chronicle*, Vasnetsov’s *Bogatyrs* (1881–1898), and Rimsky-Korsakov’s *Sadko* (premiered 1897) contain no interaction with Krak or Wanda. They illuminate a different Slavic epic system and should not be used as evidence for this Polish chronicle legend.

## 3. The Story

### A. Krak as founder and lawgiver

**[EA: Kadłubek, c. 1190–1208]** After fabulous wars against Romans or Gauls in Kadłubek’s pseudo-ancient Polish past, the community chooses Gracchus. He argues that a polity cannot subsist without law and creates civic order. The tale functions in Kadłubek’s dialogue as political exemplum: rulership is justified by service to the commonwealth, consent, justice, and law.

**[MC: Długosz, fifteenth century]** Krak becomes a more conventional individual monarch, chronologically placed in Poland’s invented prehistory. Later genealogies treat the fabulous chronology as a literal regnal list. No contemporary annal, coin, inscription, or document confirms such a king.

### B. The city

**[EA]** Kadłubek says a famous city was founded on the monster’s rock and named *Gracchovia* so that Gracchus’s memory would live. He also reports an alternate raven explanation.

**[MR]** The normal legendary-founder process may run from place-name to hero: Kraków existed before the written Krak narrative, and medieval historians often generated eponymous founders. This is more economical than using the story to prove the king, but it remains a reconstruction of how the legend formed.

### C. The Wawel monster

**[EA: Kadłubek]** A savage creature called *holophagus*—“whole-eater” or “all-devourer”—inhabits a rock-cavern. Unless supplied with a fixed number of cattle, it consumes an equivalent number of people. This is the earliest securely attested dragon-cycle episode. Kadłubek does not call the victim a princess and does not introduce a cobbler.

Krak’s sons cannot defeat it by force. They place burning sulphur inside cattle hides and offer them as food. The monster swallows the bait and suffocates or burns from the internal flames.

**[MC: Długosz]** Krak himself devises the trap; the bait contains sulphur, tinder, wax, pitch, and tar. Długosz explicitly situates the cave below the Wawel castle.

**[EM: Bielski, 1597]** The cobbler Skub/Skuba advises King Krok to fill a calfskin with sulphur. The dragon drinks after eating and dies; the cobbler is rewarded. This is the first major Polish-language commoner-slayer form.

**[MI]** The familiar sheep or lamb, fire-breathing dragon, endangered princess Wanda, marriage prize, the dragon drinking the Vistula until it bursts, and the name Dratewka are chiefly modern fairy-tale combinations. They must not be projected into Kadłubek.

### D. Fratricide and succession

**[EA: Kadłubek]** After the monster dies, the younger son murders the elder, claims that the monster killed him, and presents himself as sole victor. The crime is exposed; he is permanently exiled. The people then choose Krak’s sole daughter Wanda.

**[MC: Długosz]** The sons are Krak the Younger and Lech; Długosz reverses or regularizes earlier roles so that Lech kills Krak. Some modern retellings name both brothers already in Kadłubek; that is inaccurate.

### E. Wanda’s rule and the invader

**[EA: Kadłubek]** Wanda is elected because of the people’s love for her dead father. She exceeds all others in beauty, dignity, and prudence. An unnamed “Leman/Alemannic” or German-associated tyrant attacks her realm. When Wanda appears, his troops believe they confront something more than mortal and refuse sacrilegious combat. Their leader kills himself with his sword, declaring that Wanda shall command sea, earth, and air and condemning his men and descendants to female rule.

Kadłubek then explains *Vandalus* and *Vandali* from Wanda. He does not say that the invader proposed marriage, that Wanda drowned, or that her corpse was recovered.

### F. Marriage refusal and drowning

**[MC: *Chronicle of Greater Poland*, thirteenth/fourteenth century]** The enemy seeks marriage; Wanda refuses; the Alemannic ruler invades and is defeated or dies. Wanda voluntarily leaps into the Vistula as an offering of gratitude to the pagan gods. This is the earliest surviving source for her drowning.

**[MC: Długosz, fifteenth century]** The suitor is Rytigier. Wanda has vowed virginity. After victory and thanksgiving, she leaps from a bridge into the Vistula. Her recovered body is carried to the Dłubnia/Mogiła area and buried beneath a great mound.

The popular formula “she drowned rather than marry a German” compresses distinct causal sequences. In the medieval Greater Poland/Długosz tradition, she first refuses, survives the invasion, wins, and then sacrifices herself; she does not simply leap to escape an imminent wedding.

**[MI]** Later literature variously makes Wanda secretly love Rytigier, kill herself from grief, die to protect her people from future invasions, or enter the river as a mystical union with the land. These are authorial interpretations.

### G. Death and body

- **Krak:** **[MC][LS]** Jan of Dąbrówka and Długosz identify the Krzemionki mound as his grave. Kadłubek does not securely describe that physical mound.
- **Wanda, Kadłubek:** **[EA][NEG]** No death or body narrative.
- **Wanda, Greater Poland Chronicle:** **[MC]** Voluntary drowning/sacrifice.
- **Wanda, Dąbrówka commentary:** **[LS]** The gods cause her body to emerge; burial near Mogiła.
- **Wanda, Długosz:** **[MC][LS]** Drowned from a bridge, recovered, buried near the Dłubnia; Długosz himself says the mound claim is more astonishing than credible.

## 5. Cult, Tomb and Ritual

### Krakus Mound

**[ARCH]** Krakus Mound is a monumental earthwork on the Krzemionki ridge. Excavation in 1934–1937 found internal timber or wattle construction, an Avar-type bronze belt fitting in a lower context, later objects near the top, and no securely identified burial. Proposed dates have ranged widely; an early-medieval date, often eighth–tenth century, is plausible but not settled by a royal interment.

**[LS]** Jan of Dąbrówka, c. 1440, is the first surviving writer explicitly to locate Krak’s body there. Długosz repeats the identification.

**[NEG]** Archaeology has not found Krak’s remains, an inscribed grave, royal regalia, or any object bearing his name.

### Wanda Mound

**[EA for monument, not heroine]** A 1222 donation calls the locality *Mogiła sive tumba*; later Cistercian documents use *Claratumba*. The oldest notices do not connect the mound with Wanda.

**[ARCH]** Limited investigations in 1913 and the 1960s did not securely date the mound or reveal Wanda’s burial. Dariusz Niemiec has argued hypothetically for a seventh–eighth-century construction connected with a local power centre and perhaps a ruler’s burial; this is a scholarly model, not an identification of Wanda.

**[LS]** Jan of Dąbrówka first explicitly connects Wanda with the Mogiła mound. Długosz supplies the burial narrative and topographic detail. Sixteenth-century sources report a chapel, statue, or epitaphic tradition, but none constitutes contemporary evidence for Wanda.

**[MI]** In 1890 a monument designed by Jan Matejko—an eagle oriented westward above the name “WANDA,” sword, and distaff—was installed. Its west-facing national symbolism belongs to partition-era commemoration.

### Rękawka

**[EM/F]** The festival associated with the Krakus Mound is first described in surviving writing by Stanisław Sarnicki around 1577; later practice involved throwing eggs, bread, nuts, coins, and other goods from the slope. Scholars have interpreted it as a survival or transformation of rites for the dead, but the written record begins many centuries after the mound’s construction.

**[MR]** The term *Rękawka* is better compared with Slavic words for grave/coffin than explained by the popular tale that mourners carried soil in their sleeves (*rękawy*). Neither etymology proves a cult of Krak personally.

**[MI]** Since 2001 a reconstructed “Traditional Rękawka” has included staged tenth-century Slavic rites and historical reenactment. Ethnographic research treats the neo-pagan and Catholic events as competing or parallel heritage regimes. This is modern ritualization around Krak, not uninterrupted evidence of his ancient worship.

### Cult verdict

**[NEG]** There is no secure pre-Christian or medieval evidence for sacrifices, priests, games, a heroon, oracle, relic cult, feast day, votive inscription, or theophoric naming dedicated specifically to Krak or Wanda.

**[F/MI]** Mound visitation, patriotic commemoration, literary language calling Wanda “saint-like,” garland customs linked retrospectively with her, local place-names, and modern neo-pagan ceremonies demonstrate reception and belief, but not an archaeologically attested ancient hero cult.

## 6. Historical Evolution

### Before c. 1190

**[EA/NEG]** Kraków and the monumental mounds existed; the written figures do not. Cosmas’s Czech Krok-and-Libuše narrative offers an earlier parallel. Gallus’s silence leaves oral prehistory unknowable.

### c. 1190–1208: Kadłubek’s formation

**[EA]** The first extant narrative makes Krak a lawgiving founder and places Wanda within a political meditation on legitimate government, civic consent, inherited prestige, female sovereignty, and foreign aggression. Classical rhetoric and pseudo-antique ethnography are integral, not decorative accidents.

### Thirteenth–fourteenth centuries

**[MC]** Successor chronicles abbreviate, moralize, and reorganize the material. The *Chronicle of Greater Poland* supplies the marriage refusal and drowning; Dzierzwa transmits the monster-fratricide complex. The legend becomes a usable prehistory for an increasingly systematized Polish regnal sequence.

### Fifteenth century

**[LS]** University commentary and Długosz join text to landscape: the two great earthworks become royal graves. Długosz names Rytigier and the sons, expands the pagan sacrifice, and transforms a learned myth into apparently localized antiquarian history while occasionally signaling doubt.

### Sixteenth–seventeenth centuries

**[EM]** Printed chronicles, emblematic genealogies, poems, and illustrations spread the cycle. Bielski’s Skuba shifts heroic intelligence from dynasty to artisan. Learned “Vandalian” ethnogenesis makes Wanda serviceable to national-origin theories until Sarmatian models become dominant.

### Enlightenment and partitions

**[MI]** Critical historians increasingly reject Krak and Wanda as factual monarchs. At the same time, partitions make them more powerful as cultural figures. “Wanda who did not want the German” turns female virginity and self-destruction into allegories of national integrity and resistance to German rule.

### Nineteenth-century national school

**[MI]** Tragedy, painting, opera, youth literature, public monuments, and school narratives canonicalize a merged version: Krak founds Kraków and defeats or commissions the defeat of the dragon; Wanda refuses a German and dies in the Vistula for Poland. Artistic certainty replaces source plurality.

### Twentieth century

**[MI/F]** Children’s books, postcards, tourism, theatre, civic imagery, and education further detach Skuba/Dratewka and the bursting dragon from their late dates. Unlike the Russian *bogatyrs*, Krak and Wanda were not major subjects of Soviet epic canon-building. Vasnetsov’s *Bogatyrs* and Rimsky-Korsakov’s *Sadko* are unrelated.

### Twenty-first century

**[MI/MR]** Kraków presents the dragon, Krak, Wanda, and the mounds as city heritage. Archaeologists continue to debate the mounds’ dates and functions. Theatre, opera, feminist “herstory,” reenactment, fantasy, and neo-paganism reinterpret Wanda variously as sovereign woman, sacrificial victim, patriotic icon, or suppressed goddess. Only the first two roles have medieval textual support, and “goddess” does not.

## 7. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Literature and theatre

- **1794:** Bogusławski’s *Krakowiacy i Górale* uses the anti-foreign refrain about Wanda. **[MI]**
- **1807:** Tekla Łubieńska’s *Wanda* treats national sacrifice in the political atmosphere following Poland’s partitions. **[MI]**
- **1809:** Zacharias Werner’s German *Wanda* internationalizes the heroine. **[MI]**
- **1847–1848:** Norwid’s *Wanda*, dedicated to the mound, interprets her death as mystery and sacrifice rather than a simple xenophobic refusal. **[MI]**
- **1876:** Teofil Lenartowicz’s poem *Wanda*. **[MI]**
- **1887:** Przyborowski’s *Król Krak i królewna Wanda* addresses young readers. **[MI]**
- **1897/1904:** Wyspiański’s two versions of *Legenda* symbolically recombine Kraków’s waters, dynasty, dragon, crime, and national destiny. **[MI]**
- **1938:** Makuszyński and Walentynowicz’s *Wanda leży w naszej ziemi* popularizes the story for children. **[MI]**

### Visual culture

- **Sixteenth century:** Printed chronicles establish recognizable scenes of Wanda’s leap and the early rulers. **[EM]**
- **1855:** Aleksander Lesser paints *The Death of Wanda*; an 1871 Warsaw tableau vivant reproduces it. **[MI]**
- **1859:** Maksymilian Piotrowski paints Wanda’s sacrificial leap. The National Museum identifies the work with Długosz’s version and the partition-era meaning of national self-sacrifice. **[MI]**
- **1890:** Matejko’s monument crowns Wanda Mound. **[MI]**
- **Twentieth–twenty-first centuries:** Book illustration, municipal branding, monuments, souvenirs, cartoons, and dragon attractions make the cycle a commercial emblem of Kraków. **[MI]**

### Music

- **1876:** Dvořák’s five-act *Vanda* adds named lovers, priests, Svantovít, choral paganism, and a large Slavic-German conflict. These are operatic elaborations. **[MI]**
- **2021:** Joanna Wnuk-Nazarowa’s one-hour *Wanda*, drawn from Norwid, premieres in Wawel Castle’s courtyard. **[MI]**
- **[NEG]** Rimsky-Korsakov’s *Sadko* (1897) has no Krak/Wanda material.

### National and political use

**[MI]** During the partitions, Wanda’s refusal became a compact anti-German national allegory. Piotrowski’s painting, Bogusławski’s refrain, Romantic drama, and the west-facing eagle on her mound all participated in this semantic field.

**[MR]** The formula can express resistance to foreign domination, but it also reduces a complex medieval sovereignty story to ethnic endogamy and female self-annihilation. Modern nationalist circulation often quotes the slogan without acknowledging that Kadłubek neither names a German suitor nor kills Wanda.

**[NEG]** No well-documented, distinctive extremist cult of Krak or Wanda comparable to modern extremist appropriations of some Germanic or Norse symbols emerged from the sources reviewed. General nationalist and anti-German uses are well attested; claims of a specific continuous extremist tradition require evidence not presently available.

### Documented modern inventions

- Krak personally killing the dragon: late redistribution of the sons’ deed. **[MC/MI]**
- A single heroic cobbler named Dratewka: twentieth-century literary conflation. **[MI]**
- A princess offered weekly to the dragon: modern fairy-tale motif. **[MI]**
- The dragon exploding after drinking the Vistula: modern popular ending. **[MI]**
- Skuba marrying Wanda: modern storybook closure. **[MI]**
- Wanda drowning simply because she “did not want a German”: compressed modern canonical version. **[MI]**
- Krak and Wanda as *bylina* heroes: modern category error. **[MI]**
- Direct relationships with named Slavic gods: modern invention except in identifiable works such as Dvořák’s opera. **[MI]**

## 8. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Historicity

**[MR]** Older antiquarian and patriotic writers treated Krak and Wanda as early rulers and assigned them dates. Modern source criticism observes that neither appears before Kadłubek, that his account belongs to a deliberately classicizing fabulous history, and that no independent contemporary record corroborates them.

The mounds and early medieval Kraków show that a real political landscape underlies the legend. They do not identify its rulers. Assertions such as “Krak ruled 694–728” are late chronological constructions, not historical data.

### Kadłubek: inventor or transmitter?

Two positions need not be falsely harmonized:

- **Learned-construction emphasis:** Brückner and successors see Wanda, the Vandal etymology, Gracchus, and much of the narrative as Kadłubek’s literary fabrication from classical, biblical, and medieval learning.
- **Traditional-structure emphasis:** Banaszkiewicz, Słupecki, and comparative scholars argue that Kadłubek organized inherited narrative structures—foundation combat, sovereign judge, monstrous disorder, fratricidal succession, and royal maiden—through learned Latin rhetoric.

**Finding:** Kadłubek is the first witness. Any pre-existing oral form is reconstructive because no earlier Polish text preserves it.

### Czech comparison

**[MR]** Cosmas’s Krok and Libuše precede Kadłubek’s Krak and Wanda. The analogies are close enough to demand comparison, but scholarship disputes whether Kadłubek borrowed, both used related Central European traditions, or similar foundation-story structures independently attached to similar place and personal names.

### Comparative dragon theories

**[MR]** Scholars have compared the sulphur bait with other dragon and devouring-monster tales, including Danielic, classical, and international folktale patterns. Such comparisons illuminate motif history but do not prove a single origin. The physical cave beneath Wawel likely helped localize and preserve the story.

### Wanda and ancient exempla

**[MR]** Comparisons have been proposed with classical virgin rulers, sacrificial maidens, women whose presence disarms armies, and Germanic Rüdiger material. These are hypotheses of literary formation. No ancient Greek, Roman, Scandinavian, or German text contains the complete Wanda narrative.

### The mounds

- **Krakus Mound:** early-medieval dating is plausible; burial function remains unproved.
- **Wanda Mound:** still less securely dated; the oldest documents call the place a mound/tomb without Wanda.
- **Astronomical alignments:** published studies note sightlines associated with solar or lunar risings and settings. Alignment alone cannot establish Celtic construction, a calendar function, or dedication to Krak and Wanda.
- **Pagan-cult claims:** possible funerary or ritual contexts are archaeological hypotheses; direct personal cult is unattested.

### Folklore versus literary diffusion

**[F/NEG]** Modern Polish folklore reference works stress that Krak and Wanda are surprisingly rare in independently collected oral tradition. Many “folk” versions recorded by ethnographers derive from chronicles, chapbooks, schoolbooks, or literary retellings. Calling every modern retelling an ancient folktale obscures this feedback loop.

### Manuscripts, editions, and losses

Kadłubek’s autograph is lost. The earliest surviving witness is early fourteenth century; many fifteenth-century copies descend through scholastic use and Jan of Dąbrówka’s commentary. Some manuscripts and archival materials were destroyed in 1944. Plezia’s 1994 critical Latin edition supersedes reliance on nineteenth-century printings, although those remain valuable for reception history.

### Discredited ideas still circulating

- exact seventh- or eighth-century regnal dates for Krak and Wanda;
- proof of their bodies in the mounds;
- a continuous cult documented from pagan antiquity;
- certainty that Wanda was an ancient Slavic goddess;
- the Vandal equation as literal ethnography;
- Celtic astronomical construction inferred solely from alignments;
- Dratewka as the medieval dragon-slayer;
- inclusion in Russian *byliny*.

## 10. Sources and assessment

The strongest primary base is Kadłubek’s *Chronica Polonorum*, read through Plezia’s critical edition and the surviving manuscript record; the *Chronicle of Greater Poland*; Jan of Dąbrówka; Długosz’s *Annales* and *Liber beneficiorum*; Bielski’s *Kronika polska*; and early documents for Mogiła. Archaeological claims should be checked against the excavation history rather than city legends. For reception, the literary and visual works are primary evidence for their own periods, not witnesses to antiquity.

The dedicated contested-material pass supports the following bottom line:

- **Historicity:** no direct evidence for either person.
- **Tombs:** real ancient/early-medieval earthworks; personal identifications first documented in the fifteenth century.
- **Cult:** no securely attested personal cult; later commemorative and reconstructed ritual exists.
- **Dragon:** Kadłubek’s sons, Długosz’s royal plan, Bielski’s Skuba, and modern Dratewka are distinct strata.
- **Wanda’s death:** absent from Kadłubek; first attested in the *Chronicle of Greater Poland*.
- **Etymology:** all major derivations remain disputed; Kadłubek’s Vandal genealogy is learned construction.
- **Nationalism:** strongly documented from the partitions onward, especially through the slogan rejecting “the German.”
- **Byliny:** no attestation; the supplied Russian corpus is irrelevant except as a control demonstrating absence.
- **Modern invention:** goddess status, a continuous pagan cult, exact regnal dates, Dratewka’s medieval antiquity, and named relations with Slavic gods lack early evidence.
