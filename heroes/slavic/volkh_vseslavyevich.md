# Volkh Vseslavyevich: Research Dossier

## Evidentiary key

- **[SEA] Securely attested early material:** an extant text or manuscript securely earlier than the nineteenth-century collecting movement.
- **[LCE] Later canonical elaboration:** a printed, literary, artistic, or educational form that became standard.
- **[RV] Regional or performer variant:** an independently recorded or locally reshaped oral version.
- **[LS] Learned systematization:** antiquarian, folkloristic, philological, or ideological organization of the material.
- **[SYN] Syncretistic identification:** an identification with another figure or tradition, rather than a fact stated by the primary text.
- **[FOL] Folklore:** an orally performed or orally presented tradition whose documentation is later than its claimed setting.
- **[MSR] Modern scholarly reconstruction:** a proposed origin, prototype, ritual meaning, or comparative interpretation.
- **[MI] Modern invention:** a recent narrative or claim presented as ancient without corresponding early evidence.

## 1. Identification, Names and Epithets

**Class:** Volkh Vseslavyevich is, in the attested bylina, a **mortal but supernaturally conceived bogatyr, sorcerer-prince, shapeshifter, hunter, and leader of a druzhina**. He is not securely attested as a god, demigod, saint, heroized dead person, or recipient of cult. “Culture hero,” “totemic ancestor,” “shaman,” and “Indo-European wolf-warrior” are scholarly interpretations, not classifications supplied by the song. **[FOL/MSR]**

**Earliest secure attestation:** the song conventionally titled **“Volkh Vseslavyevich”** in the manuscript collection attributed to Kirsha Danilov, assembled in the Urals after 1742, probably during the 1740s–1760s. The original is lost; the textual basis is an eighteenth-century copy, generally dated to the 1760s–1780s. The collection was first printed incompletely in 1804 and more fully in 1818. Nothing presently establishes that this particular song existed in the Middle Ages in the form recorded by the manuscript. **[SEA]** [Russian historical encyclopedia](https://history.ru/encyclopedia/kirsha-danilov); [National Electronic Library record of the 1818 edition](https://kp.rusneb.ru/item/material/5fcf8be8991f3b9142345a07)

### Names

- **Волх Всеславьевич — Volkh Vseslavyevich.** This is the name in the Kirsha Danilov text of the Indian campaign. *Vseslavyevich* means “son of Vseslav.” The poem, however, names his mother **Marfa Vseslavyevna**, so the patronymic does not yield a coherent human genealogy: mother and son both bear derivations from *Vseslav*. **[SEA/FOL]**
- **Волхв Всеславич — Volkhv Vseslavich.** A learned or normalized variant emphasizing Russian *volkhv*, “sorcerer, pagan diviner.” It is common in scholarship and reference works but is not necessarily the manuscript’s exact form. **[LS]**
- **Вольга Всеславьевич / Вольга Святославич — Vol’ga Vseslavyevich / Vol’ga Svyatoslavich.** These forms appear in related songs, especially the encounter with Mikula. **[FOL/RV]**
- **Вольга Буслаевич / Буславлевич — Vol’ga Buslayevich / Buslavlevich**, and **Ярославьевич — Yaroslavyevich**, are additional patronymic variants. The fluctuation suggests that singers treated the patronymic as a movable epic element; it cannot be used as a reliable genealogy. **[RV]** [Nosov’s onomastic survey](https://russkayarech.ru/sites/default/files/articles/2011/2/2011-2_78-88.pdf)
- English spellings include **Volkh Vseslavyevich**, **Volkh Vseslav’evich**, **Volga Svyatoslavich**, and **Volga Sviatoslavitch**. These are transliteration differences, not separate historical forms.

### Etymologies and disputed identifications

1. **Volkh from *volkhv*, “sorcerer/diviner.”** The semantic fit is obvious because the hero learns “wisdoms” and changes shape. Dal and later folklorists favored this explanation. The deeper etymology of *volkhv* is uncertain; proposals connecting it with muttering or incantation, *Veles/Volos*, animal hair, the dead, Germanic or Finnic terms remain disputed. **[LS/MSR]**

2. **Volkh and *volk*, “wolf.”** Russian *волх* and *волк* differ by one consonant. The resemblance became particularly attractive because the hero turns into a wolf, but resemblance is not proof of common derivation. The Volkh–wolf wordplay is poetically and comparatively important without being a secure etymology. **[MSR]**

3. **Vol’ga as a form derived from Oleg.** Nineteenth- and early-twentieth-century historical-school scholars connected *Vol’ga* with Old Rus’ **Oleg/Ol’g**, often ultimately from Old Norse *Helgi*, “holy.” Forms with initial *v-* occur in medieval East Slavic name transmission, but this does not prove that the epic hero is Oleg. **[MSR]**

4. **Vol’ga from the river Volga or Slavic “moisture.”** Anti-Normanist and hydronymic explanations connected the name with *Volga* and Slavic *vlaga*, “moisture.” These arguments often mixed linguistic inquiry with national historiography; they have not displaced the conventional derivation of historical Oleg from Scandinavian *Helgi*. **[MSR]**

5. **Volkh and Vol’ga: one hero or two.** Vladimir Propp treated Vol’ga as a later social transformation of the archaic Volkh. S. N. Azbelev instead separated the Indian-campaign Volkh from the tribute-collecting Vol’ga and assigned them different proposed historical prototypes. N. N. Nosov observes that the names remain strikingly tied to their plots: **Volkh** in the Indian expedition, **Vol’ga** in the Mikula song. Their similar shapeshifting introductions nevertheless encouraged performers and editors to merge them. **[LS/MSR]** [Nosov](https://russkayarech.ru/sites/default/files/articles/2011/2/2011-2_78-88.pdf)

6. **Vseslav of Polotsk.** Prince Vseslav Bryachislavich of Polotsk, who died in 1101, is the strongest historical “shadow,” not a proved original. The *Primary Chronicle* says he was born “from sorcery” (*ot volkhvovaniia*) and wore the membrane or mark with which he was born; the *Tale of Igor’s Campaign* depicts him ranging by night as a wolf. Those correspondences—sorcery, wolf-form, extraordinary mobility, and the name *Vseslav*—make influence plausible. They do not establish identity, direct descent of the song, or an eleventh-century Volkh bylina. **[SEA/MSR]** [Primary Chronicle text](https://www.gumer.info/bibliotek_Buks/History/drevnruss/povvrlet.php); [parallel Old East Slavic text of the *Tale of Igor’s Campaign*](https://nevmenandr.net/cgi-bin/slovo.py?fragm=159&it=a1&it=bk)

7. **Oleg the Seer.** Vsevolod Miller and other representatives of the historical school associated the expedition with Oleg’s legendary attack on Constantinople and the hero’s wisdom with Oleg’s epithet *Veshchii*, “Prophetic/Seer.” Propp objected that the actual plots scarcely correspond: Volkh’s animal transformations, espionage, mass transformation of his army, extermination of a kingdom, and seizure of its queen have no close counterpart in the chronicle’s Oleg narrative. **[MSR]** [Propp’s discussion](https://www.byliny.ru/biblio/propp/volkh-vseslavevich)

8. **Alexander the Great.** Susana Torres Prieto has proposed a bookish or clerical background: conception by a serpent and an expedition to India resemble medieval Alexander traditions. This is a recent hypothesis about composition, not an attested identification made by singers. **[MSR]** [Torres Prieto 2022](https://slovene.ru/2022_2_Torres_Prieto.pdf)

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### The earliest document

The Kirsha Danilov collection is the first secure evidence for **Volkh Vseslavyevich as a named epic hero**. The manuscript song begins with his portentous birth, then recounts his acquisition of transformations, recruitment and provisioning of a seven-thousand-man druzhina, reconnaissance of the Indian kingdom, infiltration of the city, annihilation of most of its inhabitants, killing of its king, and Volkh’s seizure of the kingdom and queen. **[SEA]** [Manuscript facsimile and diplomatic presentation](https://feb-web.ru/feb/byliny/texts/kir/kir-238-.htm?cmd=p)

The surviving documentary situation matters:

- The collection was made **after 1742**, probably in the Urals. **[SEA]**
- Its lost original was reportedly in Prokopy Demidov’s possession by 1768; the surviving copy is later eighteenth century. **[SEA]**
- The incomplete **1804** edition printed 26 of 71 items; the **1818** edition, edited by Konstantin Kalaidovich, printed 61 and included melodies. **[LCE]**
- A manuscript-based fuller edition followed in **1901**; a facsimile edition appeared in **1977**. **[LCE]**
- The manuscript gives direct evidence for an eighteenth-century song, not for the date at which its plot was invented.

### The Volkh–India corpus

Propp knew the Kirsha text and five later records or reworkings. One recorded from **Agrafena Matveyevna Kryukova** by A. V. Markov demonstrably reflects a book-derived tradition; her daughter Marfa Kryukova subsequently developed the material further. This weakens arguments that every nineteenth- or twentieth-century occurrence independently preserves an ancient oral archetype. **[FOL/LCE]** [Propp](https://www.byliny.ru/biblio/propp/volkh-vseslavevich); [Markov’s *Belomorskie byliny*, 1901](https://www.booksite.ru/fulltext/markov/index.htm)

Torres Prieto goes further: all known variants of the Volkh song may ultimately derive from the Kirsha Danilov publication. If correct, the tradition exemplifies **secondary orality**—a printed song returning to oral performance—rather than an independently documented medieval-to-modern oral chain. The hypothesis is significant but not universally settled; absence of pre-1804 field recordings cannot demonstrate that no oral tradition existed before print. **[MSR]** [Torres Prieto 2022](https://slovene.ru/2022_2_Torres_Prieto.pdf)

### The Vol’ga–Mikula corpus

A related but textually separable song concerns **Vol’ga and Mikula Selyaninovich**:

- Pavel Rybnikov recorded a 157-line performance from Trofim Ryabinin in **1860**, published in *Songs Collected by P. N. Rybnikov* beginning in 1861. **[FOL]**
- Alexander Hilferding recorded Ryabinin again in **1871**, first on Kizhi and again on 4 December in St Petersburg; the latter version grew to 184 lines and became a school-text standard. **[RV/LCE]**
- Rybnikov also recorded the plot from Nikifor Prokhorov and an incomplete prose account from a boatman at Shala. Other northern singers supplied further versions. **[RV]**

The repetitions show both stability and performer adaptation. [Kizhi Museum study of Ryabinin’s versions](https://site.kizhi.karelia.ru/library/ryabinin-2007/476.html); [critical reading text](https://feb-web.ru/feb/byliny/texts/bpu/bpu-094-.htm?cmd=p)

### Medieval comparative texts—not attestations of Volkh

1. **The *Primary Chronicle*.** Compiled in the early twelfth century and surviving in later manuscripts, it records historical Vseslav’s birth “from sorcery,” his campaigns, imprisonment, release by the Kievans in 1068, brief rule in Kiev, and return to Polotsk. It never calls him Volkh Vseslavyevich, never gives him a serpent father, and never sends him to India. **[SEA]**

2. **The *Tale of Igor’s Campaign*.** Usually dated close to Igor’s 1185 campaign, though the only early-modern manuscript was lost in 1812 after the first edition of 1800. It says Vseslav judged people and ordered cities by day but ranged as a wolf at night, reaching Tmutarakan before cockcrow and crossing the path of “great Khors.” This is the strongest medieval literary parallel to Volkh’s werewolf identity. It is not a manuscript of the Volkh bylina. **[SEA]**

3. **The seventeenth-century *Tale of Sloven and Rus and the City of Slovensk*.** This learned foundation legend makes **Volkhov**, son of Sloven, a crocodile-transforming sorcerer associated with the River Volkhov, killed by demons and buried in a mound that later disappears. F. I. Buslaev and others compared him with Volkh. The different name, parentage, locale, deeds, death, and textual history forbid treating them as the same attested figure. **[Medieval/early-modern accretion; SYN]**

### Modern translations and editions

James Bailey and Tatyana Ivanova’s *An Anthology of Russian Folk Epics* (1998; reissued 2015) supplies a scholarly English translation of the Kirsha song with historical and performance context. [Publisher record](https://books.google.com/books/about/An_Anthology_of_Russian_Folk_Epics.html?id=K6dzCQAAQBAJ)

An older English translation appeared in Leonard Magnus’s *The Heroic Ballads of Russia* (1921), while Isabel Hapgood’s earlier anthology also printed the song. These translations helped establish “Volkh Vseslavyevich” as the standard English name but belong to modern reception. **[LCE]** [Magnus scan](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/7/78/The_heroic_ballads_of_Russia_%28IA_heroicballadsofr00magniala%29.pdf); [Hapgood text](https://readingroo.ms/7/1/9/3/71933/71933-h/71933-h.htm)

## 3. The Story

### Conception and birth

In the Kirsha text, the bright moon shines over Kiev as young **Volkh Vseslavyevich** is born. His birth shakes the damp earth, the Indian kingdom, and the blue sea. Fish retreat to the depths, birds fly above the clouds, hoofed animals flee over the mountains, smaller animals enter the woods, and predators withdraw into the pine forests. **[SEA/FOL]**

The fullest popular retellings say that his mother, **Marfa Vseslavyevna**, stepped from a stone onto a fierce serpent, which wound itself around her leg and impregnated her. The manuscript tradition’s serpent is a sexual father, not an opponent defeated by the hero. Descriptions that identify it specifically as **Zmei Gorynych** or **Veles** go beyond the securely attested wording and are editorial or reconstructive. **[FOL; later elaboration where named]**

At roughly an hour and a half old, Volkh speaks in a thunderous voice. He tells his mother not to bind him in swaddling clothes but to clothe him in steel armor, place a golden helmet on his head, and give him a thirty-*pud* lead club. The miraculous infant-warrior motif gives him heroic maturity from birth. **[SEA/FOL]**

### Education and metamorphosis

At seven, or at ten in some normalized translations, Volkh is sent to learn reading and writing. He then learns “wisdoms”:

1. to become a bright falcon;
2. to become a gray wolf;
3. to become a bay aurochs or wild bull with golden horns.

Later in the expedition he assumes additional useful forms, notably a tiny bird or animal for reconnaissance, an ermine to damage weapons, and an ant; he also transforms his entire army into ants. Exact animal lists differ with edition and variant. **[SEA/RV]**

The transformations are literal within the song. Likhachev contrasted them with what he regarded as the poetic wolf imagery of the *Tale of Igor’s Campaign*. The literal-versus-metaphorical distinction is an interpretation: the *Tale* itself does not explain whether its audience was meant to understand Vseslav’s wolf-running as marvel, legend, or poetic figure. **[LS]**

### Formation and support of the druzhina

At fifteen Volkh gathers a **druzhina of seven thousand** men, all said to be fifteen years old. On campaign, he alone remains awake while they sleep. As a wolf he hunts elk, deer, wolves, bears, hares, foxes, sables, and leopards; as a falcon he takes geese, swans, and ducks. He supplies the army with meat, skins, footwear, and clothing. **[SEA/FOL]**

This sequence has supported several incompatible readings:

- a memory of a hunting economy or hunter-culture hero; **[MSR]**
- a totemic ancestor who is simultaneously human and animal; **[MSR]**
- a shamanic master of animal forms; **[MSR]**
- an ideal prince who magically provisions his retinue; **[MSR]**
- an epic dramatization of predatory lordship over nature. **[MSR]**

The song itself explains neither ritual nor social origin.

### Reconnaissance of India

Volkh learns that the Indian king **Saltyk Stavrulyevich** intends to attack or devastate Kiev. He flies ahead as a falcon and enters the Indian realm, then becomes a small animal—commonly an ermine in edited versions—to penetrate the palace and overhear the king and queen. **[SEA/FOL]**

He reaches the armory and destroys the enemy’s military capacity: he gnaws bowstrings, breaks bows or arrows, and disables firearms in versions whose weapon vocabulary is plainly late. The presence of guns is strong evidence that the recorded form cannot simply be an unchanged song from Kievan Rus’. **[SEA; later lexical accretion]**

The target “India” is epic geography, not a reliably locatable campaign. Proposed referents include:

- literal fabulous India;
- Byzantium/Constantinople, through comparison with Oleg;
- the Golden Horde;
- a Turkish sultanate;
- Jerusalem;
- a generalized rich and hostile southern kingdom.

Variants replace the Indian ruler with a Turkish sultan or Golden Horde ruler. Such substitutions reveal continuing adaptation to successive imagined enemies; none proves the original destination. **[RV/MSR]**

### Entry, massacre, and conquest

The Indian city has a white-stone wall, iron gates, copper fastenings, continuous guards, and an opening so small only an ant can pass. Volkh transforms himself and every member of his druzhina into ants, leads them through, and restores them to human form inside. **[SEA/FOL]**

He commands his men to kill “old and young” and to leave no one “for seed,” except seven thousand beautiful maidens. The text thus describes attempted extermination of the male and nonselected population, followed by sexual appropriation of the surviving women. It should not be sanitized as merely “victory over India.” **[SEA]**

Volkh kicks in the palace door, seizes **Saltyk Stavrulyevich**, and, after remarking that kings are supposedly neither beaten nor executed, smashes him against the brick floor until he is reduced to pieces. **[SEA]**

Volkh then takes the king’s young wife, **Elena Alexandrovna**, also associated in the text with the matronymic or title **Azvyakovna**, as his own. His warriors marry the seven thousand selected maidens. Because no consent is described and the marriages follow mass killing and conquest, these unions are coerced war-captivity marriages. **[SEA]**

Volkh occupies the kingship, distributes gold, silver, horses, and cattle—one thousand of each measure or share to every warrior—and the song ends in conquest rather than return, repentance, death, or apotheosis. **[SEA]**

### The Vol’ga–Mikula cycle

If Vol’ga is accepted as a later form of the same hero, a second cycle begins with similar claims that he wants many “wisdoms”: to move as a pike in deep seas, a falcon beneath the clouds, and a gray wolf over open fields. In the song itself he does not employ these powers. **[FOL/RV]**

Prince Vladimir grants Vol’ga three towns—commonly Gurchevets, Krestyanovets, and Orekhovets—and their revenues. Traveling with his druzhina to collect tribute, he hears a plowman before he can reach him. The plowman is **Mikula Selyaninovich**. Mikula’s ordinary-looking labor proves superhuman:

- Vol’ga’s entire force cannot pull Mikula’s plow from the furrow;
- Mikula lifts and throws it easily;
- Vol’ga’s horse cannot match the peasant’s mare;
- Mikula reports having beaten oppressive urban tax collectors;
- Vol’ga invites him to accompany the expedition and, in some versions, appoints him governor.

The song subordinates princely and military force to agrarian power. Soviet interpretation especially favored it as a celebration of the working peasant, although its historical social meaning remains debated. **[FOL/LS]**

Some variants say townsmen have dismantled a bridge and Vol’ga and his force would perish without Mikula. Others conclude in reconciliation and administrative partnership rather than combat. Vol’ga does not kill Mikula; “contest” means an unequal test of strength, horseflesh, and social worth. **[RV]**

### Death and body

No securely documented Volkh or Vol’ga bylina gives the hero a death, tomb, relic, or posthumous fate. The mound and disappearing corpse sometimes attached to “Volkh” belong to **Volkhov, son of Sloven**, in the seventeenth-century Slovensk foundation legend, not to Volkh Vseslavyevich. **[Absence of evidence; correction of SYN]**

## 4. Relations with Gods and Other Heroes

### Genealogical alternatives

| Tradition or theory | Father | Mother | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| Kirsha/Volkh song | An unnamed fierce serpent | Marfa Vseslavyevna | **[SEA/FOL]** |
| Popular retellings | Zmei Gorynych | Marfa Vseslavyevna | **[LCE/MI when presented as exact ancient text]** |
| Mythological reconstruction | Veles/Volos in serpent form | Marfa Vseslavyevna | **[MSR/SYN]** |
| Oleg theory | Historical genealogy of Oleg substituted for the marvelous parentage | not applicable | **[MSR]** |
| Vseslav theory | Volkh is an epic transformation of Vseslav of Polotsk, son of Bryachislav | not applicable | **[MSR]** |
| Vol’ga–Mikula cycle | Common patronymics include Svyatoslavich, Vseslavyevich, Buslayevich, Yaroslavich | usually unstated | **[RV]** |

Volkh’s only attested consort is the conquered **Elena Alexandrovna/Azvyakovna**. No children are named. His companions are a collectively named druzhina, not individually characterized heroes. **[SEA]**

### Every documented named divine or heroic interaction

- **Marfa Vseslavyevna** — mother; receives the newborn’s command to arm him rather than swaddle him. Kirsha Danilov manuscript, after 1742. **[SEA/FOL]**
- **Saltyk Stavrulyevich** — Indian king and enemy; Volkh spies on him, invades his city, and kills him by smashing him against the palace floor. Kirsha Danilov manuscript, after 1742. **[SEA/FOL]**
- **Elena Alexandrovna/Azvyakovna** — wife of Saltyk; Volkh takes her as wife after killing her husband and destroying the kingdom. Kirsha Danilov manuscript, after 1742. **[SEA/FOL]**
- **Mikula Selyaninovich** — superhuman plowman whom Vol’ga cannot overmatch; Mikula’s strength, mare, and plow surpass the prince and his entire druzhina, after which the two become companions or administrative partners. Rybnikov records from 1860 and Hilferding records from 1871. **[FOL/RV]**
- **Prince Vladimir** — in the Vol’ga–Mikula cycle, grants Vol’ga towns from which to collect tribute. Nineteenth-century northern records. **[FOL/RV]**
- **Vseslav of Polotsk** — no narrative interaction. He is a proposed historical prototype or source-shadow, not a character encountered by Volkh. *Primary Chronicle*, early twelfth century; *Tale of Igor’s Campaign*, late twelfth century; identification made by modern scholars. **[SEA comparison/MSR]**
- **Oleg the Seer** — no narrative interaction. Proposed prototype only. **[MSR]**
- **Khors** — no documented interaction with Volkh. In the *Tale of Igor’s Campaign*, historical/legendary Vseslav crosses the path of “great Khors” while running as a wolf. Transferring this encounter to Volkh is a scholarly conflation. **[SEA comparison; no Volkh attestation]**
- **Veles/Volos** — no documented interaction. Identifying the serpent father with Veles is a modern mythological reconstruction. **[MSR/SYN]**
- **Zmei Gorynych** — not securely named as Volkh’s father in the Kirsha text; the identification is a later popular standardization of an unnamed serpent. **[LCE/MI]**
- **Alexander the Great** — no narrative interaction; proposed literary analogue based on serpent paternity and India. **[MSR]**
- **Volkhov son of Sloven** — no narrative interaction; a separate seventeenth-century foundation-legend sorcerer compared by later scholars. **[Early-modern accretion/SYN]**
- **Sadko** — no interaction. Both figures belong to the broad corpus of Russian epics, but they do not share an attested episode. **[Absence of evidence]**
- **Volkhova**, daughter of the Sea King in Rimsky-Korsakov’s *Sadko* — no relation or interaction; similarity of name is accidental or hydronymic, not evidence of identity. **[Modern artistic figure; correction]**
- **Ilya Muromets, Dobrynya Nikitich, and Alyosha Popovich** — no securely attested meeting with Volkh in the core texts. Modern ensemble imagery should not be back-projected into the songs. **[Absence of evidence]**

No named god directly appears in the Kirsha Volkh song. Its marvels operate without a divine patron, prayer, Christian saint, pagan deity, temple, or oracle. **[SEA; absence of evidence]**

## 5. Cult, Tomb and Ritual

There is **no securely documented cult of Volkh Vseslavyevich**:

- no excavated shrine or *heroon*;
- no inscription, votive object, idol, or cult image securely bearing his name;
- no sacrifice, festival, game, oracle, priesthood, sacred calendar, or relic;
- no medieval church condemnation of worship specifically directed to him;
- no documented ancient theophoric naming;
- no securely localized tomb.

This is not proof that no performer or community ever treated him religiously; it is the state of the evidence. **[Absence of evidence]**

Three bodies of material must be kept distinct:

1. **Historical volkhvy.** Medieval chronicles describe *volkhvy*, diviners or sorcerers, including violent conflicts in 1071. These attest a social/religious category, not worship of the epic hero. **[SEA]**

2. **Volkhov son of Sloven.** The early-modern Slovensk legend gives a different sorcerer a mound and disappearing corpse. Treating that mound as Volkh Vseslavyevich’s tomb is conflation. **[Early-modern accretion/SYN]**

3. **Modern Rodnoverie.** Twentieth- and twenty-first-century Slavic Native Faith literature sometimes makes Volkh a bearer or founder of “Veles’s tradition,” supplies childhood episodes absent from the bylina, and embeds him in reconstructed pagan theology. A 1998 *Book of the Native Faith*, for example, gives him an invented seven-year silent childhood and a role in a Veles tradition. This is modern religious mythmaking, not recovered evidence of medieval cult. **[MI]** [Modern Rodnover narrative](https://religion.wikireading.ru/123546)

## 6. Historical Evolution of the Figure

### Eleventh–twelfth centuries: the historical and literary shadow

The historical Vseslav of Polotsk is recorded as a prince with an uncanny birth, political reversals, warfare, captivity, release, and brief rule in Kiev. The *Tale of Igor’s Campaign* turns his speed and reputation into wolf imagery. These texts establish a medieval **Vseslav-the-sorcerous-wolf complex**, but not the existence of the Volkh song. **[SEA]**

### Seventeenth century: learned foundation mythology

The *Tale of Sloven and Rus* creates Volkhov, crocodile sorcerer and eponym of the river. Antiquarians later folded this figure into the Volkh problem. **[Medieval/early-modern accretion; LS]**

### Eighteenth century: first secure Volkh

The Kirsha Danilov manuscript provides the first documentary Volkh Vseslavyevich: serpent-born, magically educated, shapeshifting, hunter-provider, army leader, spy, conqueror, mass killer, and king. Its mixture of archaizing marvels with firearms and imperial enemy geography shows a layered work rather than a transparent survival from one ancient date. **[SEA/FOL]**

### 1804–mid-nineteenth century: print canonization

Publication in 1804 and 1818 made the text available to educated readers and future singers. Romantic nationalism encouraged its classification as ancient national epic. Early mythologists read Volkh as a nature power, storm cloud, solar figure, or pagan residue; historical-school scholars sought Oleg, Vseslav, Olga, or other rulers behind him. **[LCE/LS]**

### 1860–1901: field collection and differentiation

Rybnikov’s and Hilferding’s northern collections established the vitality of bylina performance and canonized **Vol’ga and Mikula**. Markov’s White Sea collection documented a Volkh text from Agrafena Kryukova, but book influence complicated the claim of independent oral survival. **[FOL/RV]**

### Late nineteenth–early twentieth century: national visual culture

Artists and publishers fused epic scholarship with the neo-Russian style. Vrubel’s monumental **“Vol’ga and Mikula”** fireplace, made around 1898–1900 and shown at the Paris Exposition of 1900, presents the warrior and plowman in reconciliation. **[LCE]** [Russian Museum](https://rusmuseumvrm.ru/online_resources/art_gallery/mihail_vrubel/index.php?lang=ru&p=0&page=1&ps=500&show=asc&t=824)

Ivan Bilibin created a major illustration suite for **“Vol’ga”** in 1903–1904, including Vol’ga with his druzhina and scenes with Mikula. These images shaped the modern costume and physiognomy of the hero more strongly than any medieval image, because no medieval image of him is known. **[LCE]** [Russian Museum Bilibin collection](https://rusmuseumvrm.ru/collections/albums/album.php?id=476&lang=ru)

### Soviet period

Soviet folkloristics retained Volkh as an “archaic” hero but often translated myth into socioeconomic development. Propp interpreted the hunter-sorcerer as a remnant of an earlier social world and Vol’ga’s humiliation by Mikula as the later people’s judgment on princely power. Textbooks favored the peasant-positive **Vol’ga and Mikula** over the ethically difficult extermination of India. **[LS]**

Bilibin revisited the material after returning to the USSR and produced **“Vol’ga Svyatoslavovich and Mikula Selyaninovich”** in 1940. Soviet reference works continued to identify the figure as a princely werewolf and character of two byliny. **[LCE]**

### Post-Soviet period

The figure survives in school anthologies, illustrated retellings, fantasy-inflected popular culture, scholarship, and Rodnover mythmaking. Popular versions frequently moralize the Indian expedition: they turn it into defensive liberation, rescue an oppressed queen, omit extermination, or make the foreign population welcome the invaders. Those changes are modern adaptations, not faithful summaries of the Kirsha text. **[MI/LCE]**

## 7. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Literature and translation

- **1804 and 1818 editions:** converted manuscript song into a national literary monument. **[LCE]**
- **V. P. Avenarius, late nineteenth-century literary treatment:** presented Vol’ga as an ancient titan-hunter and treated the Oleg identification with confidence characteristic of the period. **[LCE/LS]**
- **Hapgood and Magnus English versions:** established the hero for Anglophone readers. **[LCE]**
- **Bailey and Ivanova, 1998:** supplied the principal modern scholarly English anthology and restored performance and collection history to the presentation. **[LCE]**

### Visual art

- **Mikhail Vrubel, “Vol’ga and Mikula,” c. 1898–1900:** majolica fireplace; emphasizes accord between warrior and peasant. **[LCE]**
- **Ivan Bilibin, “Vol’ga” illustrations, 1903–1904:** definitive neo-Russian visual cycle. **[LCE]**
- **Bilibin, “Vol’ga Svyatoslavovich and Mikula Selyaninovich,” 1940:** Soviet-period return to the epic. **[LCE]**
- Later illustrators—including Vladimir Konashevich, Nikolai Kochergin, and others—continued to favor the Mikula episode and children’s-book format. **[LCE]**

### Screen

- **“Vol’ga and the Sultan’s Wife” (2010), directed by Aik Khanjyan:** an animated modern adaptation. Its “sultan” framing belongs to the later Turkish form of the hostile-king motif, not necessarily the Kirsha wording. **[MI/LCE]**

### Corrections to commonly asserted reception

- **Viktor Vasnetsov’s *Bogatyrs* (1898)** depicts Ilya Muromets, Dobrynya Nikitich, and Alyosha Popovich—not Volkh. It belongs to the general visual canon that conditions how audiences imagine bogatyrs, but it is not an image of this hero. **[Correction]**
- **Rimsky-Korsakov’s opera *Sadko* (1896)** does not adapt Volkh Vseslavyevich. Its **Volkhova**, daughter of the Sea King and personification of the Volkhov River, is a different figure. **[Correction]**
- There is no secure ancient iconography of Volkh. Images presented online as “ancient Slavic depictions” are modern illustrations unless independently proven otherwise. **[Absence of evidence/MI]**

### Nationalist and political use

Romantic and imperial-era editors made the byliny evidence of a deep Russian national past. Soviet accounts recoded Vol’ga and Mikula as a contrast between princely-military authority and productive peasant labor. Contemporary nationalist and Rodnover writers sometimes present Volkh as an authentically pre-Christian Russian priest-warrior, son or initiate of Veles, or founder of an unbroken esoteric lineage. The documentary record does not substantiate that lineage. **[LS/MI]**

Searches for specific extremist organizational appropriation produced no strong, source-based case in which Volkh Vseslavyevich functions as a central extremist emblem. General nationalist and neo-pagan reuse is documented; stronger claims should not be made without organizational evidence. **[Absence of evidence]**

### Psychological and comparativist readings

Volkh has not generated a large figure-specific Freudian, Rankian, Raglanian, or Campbellian bibliography. His miraculous birth, precocious speech, initiation into transformations, expedition, victory, and kingship can be mapped onto generic hero-pattern schemes, but such mapping is imposed by twentieth-century comparativism. It is not evidence of direct transmission or a universal original myth. **[MSR]**

## 8. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Historicity

The principal proposed prototypes are:

- **Oleg the Seer:** supported by name resemblance, wisdom, princely status, and a distant foreign campaign; opposed by the almost complete difference between narrative episodes.
- **Vseslav of Polotsk:** supported by *Vseslav*, magical birth, wolf imagery, mobility, and Kiev associations; opposed by the song’s serpent parentage, Indian campaign, transformations of an army, and different political story.
- **Princess Olga:** proposed through the name Vol’ga and traditions of tribute collection; generally less influential.
- **A composite hero:** Miller and later scholars allowed material from Oleg, Vseslav, and mythic tradition to accumulate.
- **No recoverable prototype:** Propp emphasized artistic and social transformation rather than decoding every song as disguised chronicle.

No proposal establishes that Volkh was a historical individual. **[MSR]**

### Dating

“Archaic” is not the same as “early attested.” Animal transformation, serpent paternity, cosmic birth portents, and hunter-provider behavior may be typologically ancient, yet their first secure combination under this name is eighteenth century. Conversely, guns and shifting enemies show relatively late reworking. Dating the whole song to prehistoric, pagan, or Kievan times from selected motifs is methodologically circular. **[MSR]**

Torres Prieto’s 2022 argument poses the sharpest recent challenge: the close agreement of variants and their appearance after the Kirsha publication suggest descent from print. This does not date every motif to the eighteenth century, but it prevents use of the later variants as multiple independent witnesses to an ancient song. **[MSR]**

### Mythological and nature-school theories

Nineteenth-century mythologists explained Volkh as storm cloud, thunder hero, solar or atmospheric being. Miller’s storm-cloud interpretation used the shaking world at birth and rapid transformations. These theories are historically important but now generally treated as products of a method that too readily converted narrative details into weather allegory. **[Discredited or strongly qualified MSR]**

### Totemism and the hunting hero

Propp interpreted serpent descent and animal transformation as survivals or artistic reworkings of totemic conceptions, and the hunting episode as reflecting a hunting economy. Later scholars have called Volkh a totemic ancestor or culture hero. These interpretations illuminate the coherence of the animal forms, but there is no archaeological or ritual evidence tying the song to a Volkh clan cult. **[MSR]**

A. K. Sukharev’s 2018 study reconstructs a primordial culture hero and creation-myth complex from the hunting and secret-entry episodes. Its conclusions are an ambitious modern reconstruction, not a newly discovered early source. **[MSR]** [Sukharev 2018](https://www.vestnik-sk.ru/assets/files/Vestnik_2018_2_48.pdf)

### Shamanic interpretation

The alternating bird, wolf, and hoofed-animal forms, nocturnal travel, mastery of animal domains, reconnaissance flight, and collective transformation have encouraged “shamanic” readings. Marta De Michiel’s study of Volkh/Volk treats the transformations alongside Boyan, Vseslav, and Igor as a cluster of metamorphic or shamanic motifs in Old Russian literature. The label is comparative: no primary source calls Volkh a shaman, records a trance, drum, séance, spirit-helper initiation, or historically documented shamanic office. **[MSR]** [De Michiel](https://cris.unibo.it/handle/11585/142055)

### Jakobson, Szeftel, and the Vseslav epos

Roman Jakobson and Marc Szeftel’s **“The Vseslav Epos”**—published in the 1949 volume *Russian Epic Studies* and reprinted in Jakobson’s *Selected Writings IV*—argued for a broad lost epic complex surrounding Vseslav, bringing together the chronicle, the *Tale of Igor’s Campaign*, and Volkh. Its strength is the unusually dense cluster of name, sorcery, wolf, speed, and princely motifs. Its weakness is the documentary gulf between the medieval texts and the eighteenth-century Volkh song. “Lost Vseslav epos” remains a reconstruction, not a recovered work. **[MSR]** [bibliographic record](https://books.google.com/books/about/Selected_Writings.html?hl=en&id=NxyhJcACk5IC&output=html_text)

### Indo-European wolf-warrior theory

Jakobson’s comparisons and later Indo-European scholarship place Volkh near traditions in which youthful warrior bands symbolically become wolves, live outside society, hunt, raid, and return under a prince. The gray-wolf transformation and same-aged druzhina make comparison reasonable. Yet the bylina does not describe a historical initiation society, and similarity cannot determine whether the motif is inherited, borrowed, or independently composed. “Indo-European werewolf warrior” should therefore be labeled a comparative model. **[MSR]**

### “India,” enemy substitution, and ideology

Propp regarded the song’s development as an attempted conversion of a morally alien hunter-conqueror into a defender of Kiev: the enemy could become an Indian king, Golden Horde ruler, or Turkish sultan who first threatened Rus’. This ideological remodeling explains why later retellings emphasize preemption and defense. It also explains why the exterminatory conclusion sits uneasily beside the defensive premise. **[MSR]**

### Oral-formulaic and textual questions

The corpus displays conventional bylina formulas, repeated lines, epic numbers, incremental repetition, and stock geography. These show participation in an oral-poetic system but do not prove that the entire Volkh text was orally composed in the Middle Ages. Printed collections, chapbooks, schoolbooks, public readings, and singers’ memories interacted from the nineteenth century onward. **[FOL/LS]**

### State of the evidence

- No medieval manuscript names Volkh Vseslavyevich.
- The lost Kirsha original is represented by an eighteenth-century copy.
- The earliest publication is 1804; the fuller edition is 1818.
- Later oral records are real performances but may be book-influenced.
- There is no archaeological evidence specifically attached to the hero.
- There is no ancient or medieval image securely depicting him.
- No cultic document, tomb, relic, or ritual is securely associated with him.
- The medieval Vseslav evidence is independent and earlier, but the bridge from Vseslav to Volkh is inferential.
