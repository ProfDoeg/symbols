# Job: Research Dossier

**Traditional class:** In the Hebrew book, Job is a righteous, wealthy mortal outsider and wisdom-drama protagonist—not an Israelite king, demigod, or warrior. Later Jewish sources variously classify him as a historical gentile prophet, an Israelite, or a fictional parabolic figure; Christianity makes him a saintly type of Christ and exemplar of patience; Islam classifies Ayyūb explicitly as a prophet.

**Earliest secure attestation:** The earliest datable textual witness to Job as a named exemplary righteous man is Ezekiel 14:14, 20, conventionally dated to the early sixth century BCE, where “Noah, Daniel/Danel, and Job” could save only themselves by their righteousness. The passage does not narrate Job’s ordeal and does not prove that its Job is the protagonist of the surviving book. The earliest physical witnesses to the book itself are fragmentary Hebrew Job manuscripts and an Aramaic Job translation from Qumran, approximately the second century BCE through the first century CE. No inscription, tomb, contemporary administrative document, or archaeological artifact securely identifies an historical Job.

## Evidence labels

- **[EARLY]** Securely attested biblical or materially preserved ancient evidence.
- **[CANONICAL]** Material in the received Hebrew, Greek, Latin, Christian, or Qurʾānic canon, with textual tradition specified.
- **[VARIANT]** Ancient or regional textual alternative.
- **[SYSTEMATIZATION]** Rabbinic, patristic, scholastic, tafsīr, or comparable learned harmonization.
- **[SYNCRETISM]** Identification of Job with another figure.
- **[ACCRETION]** Medieval or early-modern development.
- **[FOLKLORE]** Local shrine, oral, pilgrimage, or wonder tradition without contemporary evidence for the narrated event.
- **[RECONSTRUCTION]** Modern scholarly inference rather than an explicit ancient claim.
- **[INVENTION]** Demonstrably modern assertion presented as, or easily mistaken for, ancient tradition.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### Orientation

**[CANONICAL]** The Hebrew Job, אִיּוֹב (*ʾIyyōḇ*), is introduced as a blameless and upright man in the land of Uz, “the greatest of all the people of the east” (Job 1:1–3). His wealth is pastoral and household-based. He fears God, intercedes for his children, loses children, property, servants, and health after a challenge in the heavenly court, disputes divine justice with three friends, hears Elihu and YHWH, and receives restored prosperity and another ten children. The book does not supply a father, tribe, genealogy, reign, named wife, grave, or verifiable date.

**[EARLY]** Ezekiel 14 places Job with Noah and Daniel—probably the legendary wise Danel known from Northwest Semitic tradition rather than necessarily the hero of the biblical Book of Daniel—as proverbial righteous men. This establishes circulation of a Job tradition by the sixth century BCE, but not the literary form of the extant book.

### Names and scripts

- Hebrew: **אִיּוֹב**, vocalized *ʾIyyōḇ*.
- Aramaic: forms corresponding to **איוב**, including the Qumran Targum.
- Greek Septuagint: **Ἰώβ**, *Iṓb*.
- Latin Vulgate: **Iob**.
- Syriac: **ܐܝܘܒ**, *ʾIyyōḇ*.
- Arabic Qurʾān: **أَيُّوب**, *Ayyūb*.
- Geʿez and Ethiopic Christian usage: forms derived through the biblical and ecclesiastical traditions.
- European forms include Job, Hiob, Ijob, Giobbe, and Jó; these are transmission forms, not independent ancient personages.

### Etymology

**[RECONSTRUCTION—DISPUTED]** The Hebrew name is unique in the Hebrew Bible. Proposed derivations include:

1. A relation to the Semitic root represented by Hebrew איב, “to be hostile,” yielding interpretations such as “the persecuted/attacked one” or “where is the divine father?” through comparison with West Semitic names.
2. Comparison with second-millennium BCE personal names such as Akkadian *Ayyābum/Ayabum*. Similarity shows that a name of this general form was possible in the ancient Near East; it does not identify the biblical Job.
3. Homiletic interpretation as “enemy” or “one treated as an enemy.” The vocalization and morphological details prevent certainty.

David Clines notes that the name is unattested elsewhere in Hebrew and surveys the ancient Near Eastern comparisons; no proposed etymology is universally accepted. [Clines, *Job 1–20*](https://www.joaomordomo.com/files/books/ebooks/Word%20Biblical%20Commentary%20%28WBC%29%20-%20Vol.%2017%20-%20Job%201-20%20-%20Clines.pdf)

### Titles and epithets

- **“Blameless and upright”** (*tam we-yāšār*): narrator and YHWH, Job 1:1, 1:8, 2:3. **[CANONICAL]**
- **“One who fears God and turns from evil”**: same passages. **[CANONICAL]**
- **“Greatest of all the people of the East”**: Job 1:3. **[CANONICAL]**
- **“My servant Job”**: YHWH in Job 1:8; 2:3; 42:7–8. **[CANONICAL]**
- **Prophet:** implied in rabbinic lists of gentile prophets and explicit in Islam. **[SYSTEMATIZATION/CANONICAL ISLAMIC]**
- **Job the Long-Suffering** or “the Much-Suffering”: Christian liturgical epithet emphasizing endurance. **[ACCRETION]**
- **Saint Job:** Christian calendars, not the classification of the Hebrew book. **[ACCRETION]**
- **The patient Job / ṣabr Ayyūb:** proverbial Islamic figure of patience. **[SYSTEMATIZATION/FOLKLORE]**

### Uz

**[CANONICAL]** Job 1:1 says only “the land of Uz.” Genesis contains persons named Uz; Jeremiah 25:20 mentions kings of Uz; Lamentations 4:21 associates Edom with dwelling in Uz. These passages permitted southern—Edomite or north-Arabian—and northern—Aramean or Hauran—locations.

**[VARIANT]** The Septuagint calls the country **Ausitis** and its appendix places it on the borders of Idumea and Arabia. Later Syrian, Jordanian, Turkish, Iraqi, Omani, Lebanese, and Central Asian localizations are mutually incompatible. They document reception and sacred geography, not Job’s residence.

### Identifications

- **Job = Jobab, king of Edom (Genesis 36:33):** The Septuagint appendix at Job 42:17a–e identifies Job’s former name as Jobab and gives an Edomite genealogy. **[SYNCRETISM; Hellenistic Jewish, probably last centuries BCE]**
- **Job = a descendant of Esau:** Same Greek appendix. **[SYNCRETISM]**
- **Job’s wife = Dinah daughter of Jacob:** Pseudo-Philo’s *Biblical Antiquities*, the *Testament of Job*, Genesis Rabbah, and later traditions connect them in differing ways. This is absent from the Hebrew Book of Job. [Posen Library overview](https://www.posenlibrary.com/guide/dinah-jobs-wife-early-jewish-literature) **[ACCRETION/SYSTEMATIZATION]**
- **Job as an adviser of Pharaoh alongside Balaam and Jethro:** Rabbinic narrative, especially the Exodus interpretive complex; not biblical Job. **[SYSTEMATIZATION]**
- **Job as an Israelite:** One strand in the Babylonian Talmud; others call him a gentile. **[SYSTEMATIZATION—CONFLICTING]**
- **Job as pure fiction:** An opinion preserved in b. Bava Batra 15a and philosophically developed by Maimonides. **[SYSTEMATIZATION]**

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### Earliest literary notice

**Ezekiel 14:14, 20, ca.  early sixth century BCE. [EARLY]** Noah, Daniel/Danel, and Job form a triad of exemplary righteous men. The non-Israelite and archaic associations of all three suit Ezekiel’s rhetoric. The passage supplies no ordeal, friends, heavenly wager, wife, or restoration. Whether Ezekiel knew the prose tale underlying Job or merely a proverbial righteous Job is unknown.

### Hebrew Book of Job

**[CANONICAL; composition disputed]** The received book contains:

1. Prose opening, Job 1–2.
2. Job’s curse of his birth, chapter 3.
3. Three dialogue cycles with Eliphaz, Bildad, and Zophar, chapters 4–27.
4. The wisdom poem, chapter 28.
5. Job’s final defense, chapters 29–31.
6. Elihu’s speeches, chapters 32–37.
7. YHWH’s whirlwind speeches and Job’s replies, 38:1–42:6.
8. Prose epilogue, 42:7–17.

Modern dates range broadly from the monarchic period to the Hellenistic age. A common critical range for the main poetic work is the sixth–fourth centuries BCE, often with final formation in the Persian period. Reasons include linguistic mixture, Aramaisms, intertextual relations with exilic/post-exilic texts, and developed debate over retributive justice. Dating from apparent “patriarchal” customs confuses narrative setting with composition. There is no scholarly consensus on the relative age of every layer. [Overview of the unresolved social and chronological context](https://doaj.org/article/5173c836326342bbae28d24c13387bbc)

The Elihu section is widely—but not unanimously—treated as secondary because Elihu appears neither in the prologue nor epilogue, interrupts the transition from Job’s defense to YHWH, and uses distinctive language. Chapter 28 and the Behemoth/Leviathan material have likewise been assigned to different compositional layers by different scholars. These are **[RECONSTRUCTIONS]**, not manuscript facts: Elihu already occurs in ancient witnesses.

### Material witnesses

**[EARLY]** Qumran yielded Hebrew Job fragments conventionally catalogued as 2Q15 and 4Q99–101, plus **11Q10/11QtgJob**, an extensive Aramaic translation. The fragments demonstrate that Job circulated in more than one linguistic form before the common era. The Hebrew fragments broadly resemble the later Masoretic tradition but do not preserve the whole work. The Aramaic translation is an independent ancient witness, not the Hebrew “original.” A synopsis of 11Q10, Peshitta, and rabbinic Targum illustrates distinct translation strategies. [Oxford study](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid%3A039b549f-3491-4f98-869a-33eba9d04f5a)

### Masoretic Text

**[CANONICAL]** The medieval Masoretic codices preserve the standard Hebrew form. The oldest complete codices are roughly a millennium later than likely composition, though Qumran fragments demonstrate substantial earlier continuity. Job’s Hebrew is notoriously difficult: rare vocabulary, compressed syntax, textual corruption, and deliberate learned or foreign coloring make many verses uncertain.

### Septuagint

**[VARIANT/CANONICAL in Greek Christianity]** The Old Greek Job, translated in the last centuries BCE, is approximately one-sixth shorter than the Masoretic form in its oldest recoverable state, though it also contains expansions. It frequently condenses speeches and sometimes softens Job’s most confrontational language.

Its appendix at 42:17a–e supplies resurrection language, Job’s identification as Jobab, an Edomite genealogy, an Arabian wife, and royal identities for the friends. None occurs in the Masoretic ending, Qumran Targum, Syriac Peshitta, or later rabbinic Targum. The appendix is therefore evidence for Greek-speaking Jewish interpretation, not for Job’s historical pedigree. [Comparison of Greek and Hebrew traditions](https://intertextual.bible/guide/differences-in-septuagint-and-masoretic-traditions)

### Vulgate and Latin tradition

**[CANONICAL Christian]** Jerome translated Job from Hebrew while consulting Greek recensions. The Latin **Iob** became central to Western liturgy, exegesis, funerary language, and the iconography of patience. Latin Job 19:25—*scio enim quod redemptor meus vivat*—was commonly interpreted christologically, although the Hebrew passage is textually difficult and need not predict Jesus in its ancient literary setting.

### Peshitta and Targums

**[VARIANT]** The Syriac Peshitta supplies an important ancient Christian Semitic version. The rabbinic Targum contains interpretive expansions, including traditions about Job’s wife. These versions sometimes converge with 11Q10 in technique but are not simply copies of it.

### Testament of Job

**[ACCRETION; probably Jewish Greek composition, approximately first century BCE–second century CE]** The *Testament of Job* turns the protagonist into a heroic antagonist of Satan. Job, formerly Jobab, destroys a satanic idol, knowingly accepts the resulting attack, distributes his goods, and tells his story from his deathbed. His first wife, commonly called Sitis/Sitidos in modern forms, suffers and dies; Dinah becomes his later wife in a synthesis of genealogical traditions. His daughters receive supernatural cords or sashes, speak angelic languages, and participate in heavenly worship. The work is preserved in Christian manuscripts, and its exact date, provenance, and degree of Christian editing remain debated. Current scholarship continues to defend a Jewish diaspora core with later Christian scribal modification. [Nicholas List on Jewish and Christian features](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/09518207221137064); [Charlesworth collection and introduction](https://eclass.uoa.gr/modules/document/file.php/THEOL264/James%20H.%20Charlesworth%20The%20Old%20Testament%20Pseudepigrapha%2C%20Vol.%201%20Apocalyptic%20Literature%20and%20Testaments%201983.pdf)

### Other Jewish literature

- **1 Enoch:** No narrative interaction with Job. 1 Enoch 60 separately develops Leviathan and Behemoth as female sea and male land monsters reserved for the eschaton. **[ACCRETION; cross-tradition monster reception]**
- **Jubilees:** No surviving independent Job cycle comparable to the *Testament*. Claims that Jubilees narrates the canonical Job story are unsupported. **[ABSENCE OF EVIDENCE]**
- **Life of Adam and Eve:** No substantive Job narrative. **[ABSENCE OF EVIDENCE]**
- **Pseudo-Philo, *Biblical Antiquities*:** Connects Dinah and Job in genealogical expansion. **[ACCRETION]**
- **Josephus, *Antiquities*:** Josephus does not retell Job as he retells much biblical narrative. That silence is significant but cannot reveal whether he rejected, ignored, or assumed the story. **[ABSENCE OF EVIDENCE]**
- **James 5:11, late first century CE:** Invokes “the endurance of Job” and the Lord’s compassionate outcome. This is the clearest New Testament reception. **[CANONICAL Christian]**

### Rabbinic corpus

**[SYSTEMATIZATION]** b. Bava Batra 14b–16b preserves, rather than resolves, incompatible claims:

- Moses wrote Job.
- Job lived in Moses’ time.
- Job lived in the age of the patriarchs, Jacob, the Judges, Ahasuerus, or the return from exile.
- Job was Jewish.
- Job was a gentile prophet.
- Job “never existed and was never created”; the story is a parable.
- Counterargument: the text’s naming of Job’s place suggests a purportedly real setting.

The passage’s value is precisely its pluralism. [Bava Batra 15a](https://www.sefaria.org/Bava_Batra.15a?lang2=en&with=all)

Genesis Rabbah associates Dinah with Job through verbal analogy; Targumic and later aggadic collections expand her identity. Pirqe de-Rabbi Eliezer contributes to the broader genealogical web but should not automatically be cited for every late story later attributed to “the rabbis.” Ginzberg’s *Legends of the Jews* is an influential modern compilation of rabbinic traditions, not itself an ancient witness.

### Christian exegesis

- Origen’s Hexaplaric work exposed divergences between Greek and Hebrew Job. **[SYSTEMATIZATION, third century]**
- John Chrysostom and other Greek Fathers read Job as ascetic and moral exemplar. **[SYSTEMATIZATION]**
- Gregory the Great’s *Moralia in Iob*, composed approximately 578–595, became the monumental Western allegorical and moral commentary: Job could signify Christ, the Church, the righteous soul, and the active/contemplative life. [Dating of the *Moralia*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moralia_in_Job) **[SYSTEMATIZATION]**
- The medieval *Glossa Ordinaria* condensed patristic readings around the biblical text. **[ACCRETION]**
- Thomas Aquinas’s thirteenth-century *Expositio super Iob* treated providence and natural causation in a more literal-philosophical mode. **[ACCRETION]**

### Qurʾān and Islamic literature

**[CANONICAL Islamic; seventh century CE]** Ayyūb appears in Q 4:163 and 6:84 among recipients of revelation and guidance; Q 21:83–84 and 38:41–44 narrate his distress, prayer, divine healing, restored family, and commendable patience. In Q 38 he stamps or strikes the earth and receives cool water for washing and drinking. He must take a bundle in his hand and strike without breaking an oath; the Qurʾān does not name the person concerned or explain the oath.

**[SYSTEMATIZATION]** Tafsīr and *qiṣaṣ al-anbiyāʾ* identify the person as Job’s wife and explain that he had sworn to beat her, often after Satan deceived her or she offended him; the bundle permits a symbolic single blow. Other interpretation treats the bundle medicinally. The violence belongs to later explanation, not the Qurʾān’s explicit wording.

Al-Ṭabarī, al-Thaʿlabī, and the work circulated under al-Kisāʾī amplify Job’s disease, Iblīs’s schemes, his wife’s loyalty, duration of suffering, ancestry, and the spring. These versions vary; they must not be retrojected into the Qurʾān. A scholarly survey notes that later Islamic tradition proposed numerous mutually exclusive locations for Job’s spring. [Vicchio, “The Image of Ayyub”](https://bibleinterp.arizona.edu/articles/Vicchio_Image_Ayyub)

## 3. The story and its variants

### The man of Uz and his family

**[CANONICAL Hebrew]** Job possesses seven sons, three daughters, 7,000 sheep, 3,000 camels, 500 yoke of oxen, 500 female donkeys, and many servants. His children feast in rotation; Job offers burnt offerings in case they have “blessed”—a scribal euphemism for cursed—God inwardly.

The family is conspicuously ungenealogized. This supports a literary presentation of Job as a universal eastern righteous man, although that function is a modern interpretation, not an explicit authorial statement.

### The heavenly court and wager

**[CANONICAL Hebrew]** “The sons of God” present themselves before YHWH, and **ha-śśāṭān**, “the accuser/adversary,” comes among them. This is a title with the definite article, not yet necessarily the fully developed independent Devil of later Judaism and Christianity. YHWH praises Job; the accuser argues that Job fears God only because God protects and rewards him. YHWH permits successive tests, first of possessions and family, then of Job’s body, while initially forbidding harm to Job and later forbidding his death.

Calling this a “wager” accurately describes the narrative challenge but no stake is formally exchanged. The human Job never learns of the court scene.

### The losses

**[CANONICAL Hebrew]** Four reports arrive:

1. Sabeans take the oxen and donkeys and kill the servants.
2. “Fire of God” consumes the sheep and servants.
3. Chaldeans seize the camels and kill the servants.
4. A desert wind collapses the eldest son’s house, killing all ten children.

Job tears his robe, shaves his head, worships, and refuses to charge God with wrongdoing. The second test gives him severe sores “from the sole of his foot to the crown of his head.” He sits among ashes and scrapes himself with pottery.

The popular “dunghill” comes especially from Greek and later iconographic development; the Hebrew says ashes.

### Job’s wife

**[CANONICAL Hebrew]** The unnamed wife says, “Do you still hold fast your integrity? Bless/curse God and die” (2:9). Job replies that she speaks “as one of the foolish women,” but says they should receive adversity as well as good. The narrator says Job did not sin with his lips.

Interpretive possibilities include temptation, despair, bitter irony, or a demand that Job provoke death. The Hebrew text neither names nor kills her.

**[VARIANT Greek]** The Septuagint gives her a much longer lament over dead children, lost labor, wandering, and Job’s diseased body. This humanizes her economic and maternal suffering.

**[ACCRETION]** The *Testament of Job* names and develops the first wife as a tragic, loyal provider whom Satan manipulates; she sells her hair for bread and dies. Later Dinah traditions solve the perceived problem of whether the restored children came from the same wife.

### The three friends

**[CANONICAL Hebrew]** Eliphaz the Temanite, Bildad the Shuhite, and Zophar the Naamathite come to console Job. They sit silently for seven days and nights. After Job curses his birth, their speeches increasingly defend a morally ordered world in which suffering is connected to wrongdoing:

- Eliphaz appeals to experience, visionary revelation, and corrective discipline.
- Bildad appeals to ancestral wisdom and sharply implies that Job’s children died for their sin.
- Zophar is the harshest and claims Job deserves worse than he has received.

Job insists that his suffering is disproportionate, accuses God of hunting or targeting him, calls for an arbiter, demands a legal hearing, and oscillates among despair, protest, hope, and visions of vindication.

The dialogue does not reduce to “Job is patient.” The Job of chapters 3–31 is verbally audacious. James’s later “endurance of Job” and Christian “patience of Job” select one genuine but incomplete aspect of the book.

### Elihu

**[CANONICAL received text; possible later layer]** Elihu son of Barachel the Buzite waits because he is younger, then attacks both Job and the friends. He argues that suffering may instruct, warn, or discipline rather than merely punish past sin; God communicates through dreams, pain, and an angelic mediator. He prepares for the storm but receives neither divine rebuke nor commendation in the epilogue. His absence from the narrative frame supports—but does not prove—the widespread theory that chapters 32–37 are a later addition.

### The whirlwind

**[CANONICAL Hebrew]** YHWH answers “from the whirlwind.” The speeches do not disclose the heavenly challenge or give Job a direct causal explanation. They survey creation: earth, sea, dawn, weather, constellations, wild animals, and creatures beyond human control. The rhetorical force is epistemic and cosmic: Job cannot administer the whole created order.

Job first answers briefly that he is insignificant (40:3–5). At 42:1–6 he retracts or relents “concerning dust and ashes”; whether he “repents,” “is comforted,” or abandons his case depends on a difficult Hebrew phrase. Translations that make Job confess moral wickedness go beyond what is textually certain.

### Behemoth and Leviathan

**[CANONICAL Hebrew]** Behemoth appears in Job 40:15–24 as a mighty grass-eating land creature made alongside Job. Leviathan in 40:25–41:26 is an untamable, armored, fire-breathing sea being. Behemoth is explicitly named only here in the Hebrew Bible; Leviathan occurs elsewhere as a primordial serpent or sea monster.

**[RECONSTRUCTION]** Naturalistic identifications—hippopotamus and crocodile—account for some traits but not all. Mythic readings connect Leviathan with Ugaritic Lotan, the twisting/fleeing serpent defeated by Baal and Anat. The relation of Behemoth to a specific earlier monster is much less secure. [Oxford study of Behemoth and Leviathan](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid%3A7e3b4291-0e09-4eea-9165-eae9deedd6d7/files/s08612p357); [Near Eastern divine-council and Lotan context](https://www.marquette.edu/maqom/clifford.pdf)

**[INVENTION]** Claims that Job describes dinosaurs, dragons as zoological survivals, or advanced scientific knowledge are modern concordist readings. They are not supported by ancient manuscripts, archaeology, or the comparative philology of the passages.

### Verdict and restoration

**[CANONICAL Hebrew]** YHWH tells Eliphaz that he and his two companions have not spoken rightly “as my servant Job has.” Elihu is not mentioned. The friends must offer seven bulls and seven rams; Job prays for them. The text says YHWH “restored Job’s fortunes” when he prayed.

Job receives double the listed livestock, renewed kinship support, seven further sons, and three named daughters: Jemimah, Keziah, and Keren-happuch. The daughters receive inheritance alongside their brothers—an exceptional narrative detail, although the sons remain unnamed. Job lives another 140 years and sees four generations.

The replacement of dead children is morally disturbing to modern readers and is not explained away by the text. Neither restoration nor doubled livestock reverses the deaths. The canonical book does not narrate Job’s death beyond the formula “old and full of days,” nor identify his body or grave.

## 4. Relations with gods and other heroes

### Competing genealogies

| Tradition | Parentage/descent | Wife or wives | Children |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hebrew Job | None given | Unnamed wife | Seven sons and three daughters die; seven sons and Jemimah, Keziah, Keren-happuch follow |
| Septuagint appendix | Identifies Job with Jobab, fifth from Abraham through Esau; father Zare/Zerah in the expanded genealogy | Arabian wife | Biblical children; appendix emphasizes Edomite royal setting |
| *Testament of Job* | Jobab transformed into Job; genealogy varies by manuscript interpretation | First wife Sitis/Sitidos; later Dinah | Biblical family expanded; daughters become inspired heavenly singers |
| Rabbinic traditions | Period and ethnicity vary from gentile patriarchal sage to Israelite contemporary of Moses | Frequently Dinah in one strand | Harmonized with biblical totals in varying ways |
| Islamic learned narratives | Often linked to Esau or Abrahamic genealogies, with substantial variation | Usually Raḥma or another named faithful wife in post-Qurʾānic narratives | Family lost/restored; details vary |
| Qurʾān alone | No genealogy | No named wife | Family restored or doubled; no names |

### Documented interactions with named divine beings and heroic figures

Each line records an explicit interaction, not mere comparison.

- **YHWH / God** — Praises Job in the heavenly court, permits his testing, answers him from the whirlwind, rebukes his friends, accepts Job’s intercession, and restores him. Job 1–2; 38–42, Hebrew book, main composition usually sixth–fourth centuries BCE. **[CANONICAL]**
- **The accuser, *ha-śśāṭān*** — Challenges Job’s disinterested piety and inflicts property loss, bereavement, and disease under divine limits. Job 1–2. **[CANONICAL]**
- **Job’s unnamed wife** — Urges him to bless/curse God and die; receives Job’s rebuke. Job 2:9–10. **[CANONICAL]**
- **Eliphaz the Temanite** — Consoler turned disputant; accuses Job and is later commanded to seek Job’s intercession. Job 2–5; 15; 22; 42. **[CANONICAL]**
- **Bildad the Shuhite** — Disputes with Job, defends traditional retribution, and participates in the closing sacrifice. Job 2; 8; 18; 25; 42. **[CANONICAL]**
- **Zophar the Naamathite** — Disputes with Job, asserts that Job deserves greater punishment, and participates in the closing sacrifice. Job 2; 11; 20; 42. **[CANONICAL]**
- **Elihu son of Barachel the Buzite** — Rebukes Job and the friends and offers a disciplinary theory of suffering. Job 32–37. **[CANONICAL received text; possible later elaboration]**
- **Jemimah** — Job’s restored daughter; receives inheritance. Job 42:14–15. **[CANONICAL]**
- **Keziah** — Job’s restored daughter; receives inheritance. Job 42:14–15. **[CANONICAL]**
- **Keren-happuch** — Job’s restored daughter; receives inheritance. Job 42:14–15. **[CANONICAL]**
- **Behemoth** — Displayed to Job by YHWH as a creature beyond human mastery; Job performs no action toward it. Job 40:15–24. **[CANONICAL]**
- **Leviathan** — YHWH challenges Job to capture or domesticate it; no encounter between Job and the creature is narrated. Job 40:25–41:26. **[CANONICAL]**
- **Noah** — Job is placed beside Noah and Daniel/Danel as an exemplary righteous man; no meeting or action between them. Ezekiel 14:14, 20. **[EARLY; joint rhetorical appearance only]**
- **Daniel/Danel** — Same triadic appearance; no narrative interaction. Ezekiel 14. **[EARLY; identity disputed]**
- **Jobab** — Job is equated with, rather than interacting with, the Edomite king Jobab. Septuagint Job 42:17 appendix. **[SYNCRETISM]**
- **Esau** — Job is made Esau’s descendant in the Septuagint genealogy; no meeting is narrated. **[SYNCRETISM]**
- **Dinah** — Made Job’s wife in Pseudo-Philo, the *Testament of Job*, Genesis Rabbah, and later texts; absent from biblical Job. **[ACCRETION]**
- **Jacob** — Becomes Job’s father-in-law under the Dinah identification; no securely early meeting is narrated. **[ACCRETION]**
- **Pharaoh** — Rabbinic legends place Job among Pharaoh’s advisers; Job’s silence or equivocation over oppression is made the cause of his suffering in some versions. **[SYSTEMATIZATION]**
- **Balaam** — Appears with Job as a fellow adviser to Pharaoh in rabbinic legend; their interaction is collegial or deliberative, not biblical. **[SYSTEMATIZATION]**
- **Jethro** — Fellow adviser who flees after opposing Pharaoh, contrasted with Job’s silence. **[SYSTEMATIZATION]**
- **Moses** — Said in b. Bava Batra 15a to have written Job; other strands make Job his contemporary. No canonical meeting occurs. **[SYSTEMATIZATION]**
- **Abraham** — Rabbinic sources compare or date Job to Abraham; no biblical interaction. **[SYSTEMATIZATION]**
- **Satan in the *Testament of Job*** — Job destroys Satan’s idol; Satan retaliates through disguises, disasters, and attacks on his household. **[ACCRETION]**
- **Sitis/Sitidos** — Job’s first wife in the *Testament*; supports him, is deceived by Satan, and dies after lamenting her children. **[ACCRETION]**
- **Job’s three daughters in the *Testament*** — Job gives them heavenly cords; they speak angelic languages and sing celestial hymns. **[ACCRETION]**
- **Christ** — Job does not meet Jesus in any canonical narrative. Patristic exegesis treats Job as a type of Christ and reads Job 19:25 christologically. **[SYSTEMATIZATION, not narrative interaction]**
- **Allah** — Ayyūb prays; Allah removes his distress, produces healing water, restores his family, and praises his patience. Q 21:83–84; 38:41–44. **[CANONICAL Islamic]**
- **Iblīs** — Later Islamic stories make Iblīs the personal instigator of Ayyūb’s losses and disease. The Qurʾān says “Satan has touched me” but does not narrate the full heavenly wager. **[CANONICAL allusion plus SYSTEMATIZATION]**
- **Ayyūb’s wife, commonly Raḥma in later sources** — Supports him; later stories explain Q 38:44 as an oath to strike her, commuted through a bundle. Name and details are post-Qurʾānic. **[SYSTEMATIZATION]**
- **Gabriel/Jibrīl** — Appears in some later Islamic narratives or images as bearer of divine guidance or relief; no encounter is stated in the Qurʾānic Job passages. **[ACCRETION]**
- **Muhammad** — Job and Muhammad do not meet; Q 4:163 tells Muhammad that revelation was given to Job and other prophets. **[CANONICAL; revelatory comparison only]**

No securely early source gives Job an interaction with Adam, Eve, Enoch, Isaac, Joseph, David, Solomon, Jonah, Aaron, or Jesus. Later compilations sometimes place him in their genealogical or typological company; juxtaposition is not interaction.

## 5. Cult, tomb, relics, and ritual

### Ancient Israelite evidence

**[ABSENCE OF EVIDENCE]** No Iron Age shrine, inscription, votive dedication, sacrifice, festival, priesthood, or tomb can securely be assigned to Job. Unlike some Mediterranean heroes, he is not demonstrably a heroized dead person with an archaeologically attested cult. The book’s sacrifices are performed by Job within the story; they are not evidence of a historical Job cult.

Ezekiel and Qumran attest texts, not worship. General “biblical archaeology” disputes associated with maximalists, minimalists, Finkelstein, or Dever concern the historical value of biblical narratives and Iron Age Israel broadly. They yield no direct Job archaeology. Invoking those debates as if excavations had tested Job’s biography is a category error.

### Jewish practice

**[SYSTEMATIZATION]** Rabbinic discussion reveres and disputes Job but establishes no normative pilgrimage to his grave. The traditional attribution of the book to Moses is literary attribution, not tomb evidence.

### Christian veneration

**[ACCRETION]** Job enters saint calendars: traditionally 6 May in Byzantine churches and 10 May in the Latin martyrological tradition, with other local dates. This is liturgical commemoration, not proof of an ancient Israelite cult. A nineteenth-century Catholic summary records the Greek and Latin dates. [Catholic Encyclopedia, “Job”](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/08413a.htm)

Job 19 became embedded in Christian funerary liturgy, especially through the Latin declaration that his redeemer lives. Gregory’s *Moralia*, lectionaries, manuscripts, church sculpture, and Books of Hours made the diseased Job on ashes or a dung heap an object of moral contemplation.

### Claimed tombs and springs

**[FOLKLORE/REGIONAL VARIANT]** Competing sites include:

- Dhofar/Salalah in Oman.
- The Şanlıurfa/Edessa region in Turkey, with a cave, well, and healing associations.
- Sites in Iraq near al-Ḥillah.
- Hauran sites in Syria.
- Niha in Lebanon, maintained in Druze sacred geography.
- Jordanian localizations.
- Chashma-Ayyūb in Bukhara and other Central Asian springs.

Their multiplicity and late documentation disqualify them as mutually confirming evidence. They reveal the mobility of prophetic sacred geography, especially the attraction of Q 38’s healing spring. The Turkish **Eyüp Sultan** tomb belongs to Abū Ayyūb al-Anṣārī, Muhammad’s companion, not Job; their shared name has caused modern confusion. A survey notes at least several competing Job tombs and ordeal sites. [Overview of the competing claims](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Job_%28biblical_figure%29)

No claimed remains have an authenticated chain of custody to the literary Job. “Tomb of Job” should therefore always be labeled a local attribution.

## 6. Historical evolution

### Sixth–fourth centuries BCE: emergence and literary formation

**[EARLY/RECONSTRUCTION]** Ezekiel knows an exemplary Job. A Hebrew poet or successive writers shape a prose ordeal tale into a sustained contest over innocent suffering, divine justice, human epistemic limits, and the moral adequacy of retribution. Precise dating and layering remain disputed.

Mesopotamian righteous-sufferer works—especially *Ludlul bēl nēmeqi* and the so-called Babylonian Theodicy—provide comparative genres and motifs. They are not “the original Book of Job,” and no direct line of copying has been proved.

### Third–first centuries BCE: textual plurality

**[EARLY/VARIANT]** Hebrew forms circulate; Job is translated into Greek and Aramaic. Greek translators abbreviate and reinterpret. The Jobab genealogy historicizes Job within Genesis. Qumran preserves multiple witnesses. The *Testament of Job* begins its separate development.

### First–sixth centuries CE: exemplary Job

**[SYSTEMATIZATION]** James foregrounds endurance. Rabbinic literature preserves radical plurality over period, ethnicity, authorship, and existence. Christian interpreters read Job through resurrection, Christ, ascetic endurance, and ecclesiology. Greek and Syriac traditions give Job’s wife a larger voice.

### Seventh–tenth centuries: prophet Ayyūb

**[CANONICAL Islamic/SYSTEMATIZATION]** The Qurʾān compresses the story into prayer, healing, patience, family restoration, spring, and oath. Tafsīr and prophets’ tales reconnect this concise Ayyūb to Satanic testing, genealogy, a named wife, and localized landscape.

### Sixth–fifteenth centuries Christian West and Byzantium

**[ACCRETION]** Gregory’s *Moralia* dominates Latin interpretation. Job becomes simultaneously historical saint, Christ-type, Church-type, moral athlete, and sufferer. Illuminated manuscripts show the heavenly court, messengers, dead children, wife, friends, sores, dung heap, and restoration. The Index of Medieval Art records Christian images from late antiquity through the sixteenth century across manuscripts, sculpture, mosaic, and other media. [Index description](https://www.libraries.rutgers.edu/databases/christian_art)

The Chartres north-porch sculpture depicts Job on the dung heap, his wife, and approaching friends—an example of the iconographic synthesis of Job 2 with later visual convention. [Digital Pitt record](https://digital.library.pitt.edu/islandora/object/pitt%3AFCSP22310200)

Job appeared in biblical drama, but claims for a universal, independent medieval “Job mystery play” require local documentation; he was less structurally central to the great English civic cycles than Creation, Passion, and Last Judgment scenes.

### Early modern period

**[ACCRETION]** Reformation translation returned attention to Hebrew while retaining the exemplary patient Job. Art increasingly treated him as a psychologically isolated sufferer. Handel’s *Messiah* (1741) used the Christianized redeemer interpretation of Job 19 in Part III.

### Nineteenth century

**[MODERN REINVENTION]** Historical criticism separated the prose frame, dialogue, wisdom hymn, Elihu, and divine speeches into proposed strata. Comparative Assyriology situated Job among ancient Near Eastern suffering texts. Romantic and post-Romantic artists reclaimed Job as a rebel, visionary, or critic of divine power rather than merely passive saint.

### Twentieth century to present

**[MODERN REINVENTION]** Existential, Holocaust, liberationist, feminist, psychoanalytic, trauma, disability, ecological, and postcolonial readings emphasize different unresolved features. Modern scholarship increasingly studies the coexistence of voices rather than reducing the book to one doctrinal answer.

## 7. Modern reception and reinvention

### William Blake

**[MODERN REINVENTION]** Blake created Job watercolors in 1805–1806, a second set around 1821, and a sequence of twenty-two engravings published in 1826. His border texts and images form an interpretation, not neutral illustration: Job’s conventional piety is transformed through visionary encounter, while musical restoration and renewed family become spiritual re-creation. The British Museum preserves Blake’s copperplates and records their 1826 production. [British Museum plate record](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/P_1919-0528-20)

### Literature, music, and visual art

- Goethe’s heavenly prologue to *Faust* deliberately recalls Job’s divine-adversarial framework. **[MODERN REINVENTION]**
- Archibald MacLeish’s play *J.B.* (1958) relocates Job into the modern world and the aftermath of mass violence. **[MODERN REINVENTION]**
- Joseph Roth’s novel *Hiob* (1930) recasts Job through a Jewish family’s migration, loss, and apparent miracle. **[MODERN REINVENTION]**
- Ralph Vaughan Williams’s *Job: A Masque for Dancing* premiered in 1931, drawing heavily upon Blake. **[MODERN REINVENTION]**
- Numerous Holocaust writers use Job as a language for protest, divine absence, and the inadequacy of compensatory endings. Such uses are analogical, not discoveries about ancient Job.

### Jung

**[MODERN REINVENTION]** C. G. Jung’s German *Antwort auf Hiob* appeared in 1952; the English *Answer to Job* followed in 1954. Jung argues that Job, though powerless, is morally more conscious than Yahweh; the encounter drives a transformation in the God-image culminating in incarnation and continuing human consciousness. This is analytical psychology and speculative theology, not historical exegesis of the book’s composition. [Publication history](https://www.taylorfrancis.com/chapters/edit/10.4324/9781032652047-262/answer-job-jung-hull)

### Argentine and Latin American essay tradition

**[MODERN RECEPTION, one-line orientation]** Argentine writers, especially Jorge Luis Borges in his 1967 Buenos Aires conference on the Book of Job, treated Job as a metaphysical and literary problem—divine scale confronting human demands for ethical explanation—within a wider Latin American essay tradition that uses biblical figures as philosophical rather than confessional interlocutors. The Argentine National Library documents the conference publication. [Biblioteca Nacional Argentina dossier](https://www.bn.gov.ar/uploads/borges-el-judaismo-e-israel.pdf)

### Psychology and comparative hero theory

Rank, Raglan, and Campbell developed hero-pattern schemes emphasizing miraculous birth, separation, ordeal, victory, and return. Job fits these only imperfectly: he has no miraculous birth, youthful quest, monster combat, throne-taking, or heroic death. Applying the “monomyth” to him is a twentieth-century comparative operation, not an ancient classification. Structuralist readings can illuminate oppositions—prosperity/loss, court/ash heap, speech/silence, human/cosmic—but risk flattening textual contradiction.

### Politics and nationalism

**[NEGATIVE FINDING]** Job has been politically invoked as a symbol of a suffering people or nation, including Jewish and Christian communities under catastrophe, but the dedicated search found no stable, major nationalist or extremist Job cult comparable to political appropriations of Joshua, Samson, David, or crusading saints. Claims that Job furnishes archaeological title to territory are unsupported: the setting of Uz remains indeterminate and Job is not presented as conqueror or national founder.

Job’s language is more commonly used to contest state violence and triumphalist theodicy than to authorize conquest. That reception should not be confused with the book’s original historical function.

### Commercial and proverbial use

“Job’s comforters” means people whose consolation worsens suffering; “the patience of Job” derives especially from James 5:11 and later reception. Job’s name appears in hospitals, charities, counseling ministries, artworks, music, and commercial religious products. These uses perpetuate the patient sufferer more than the protesting litigant.

### Modern inventions and recurring misinformation

- “Job is demonstrably the oldest book of the Bible.” **[INVENTION/OVERCLAIM]** The patriarchal-looking setting does not date composition.
- “Moses certainly wrote Job.” **[SYSTEMATIZATION presented as fact]** This is one rabbinic view among several.
- “Archaeologists found Job’s tomb.” **[INVENTION]** Competing shrines lack authenticated evidence.
- “Behemoth is certainly a dinosaur.” **[INVENTION]**
- “Job predicts modern astronomy, meteorology, or the weight of air.” **[INVENTION]** These readings extract modern science from poetic language.
- “Satan makes a bet with God exactly as the Christian Devil.” **[ANACHRONISTIC SYSTEMATIZATION]** The Hebrew figure is the court accuser operating under YHWH’s authority.
- “Job receives his original dead children back.” **[MISREADING]** The Hebrew epilogue gives him later children; it does not narrate resurrection of the first ten.
- “The Qurʾān says Job beat his wife.” **[MISATTRIBUTION]** Q 38:44 gives an unexplained command involving a bundle; wife-beating is a later exegetical explanation.
- “Job’s wife was named Dinah/Raḥma in the earliest text.” **[ACCRETION presented as canonical]**

## 8. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Historicity

The evidence permits three disciplined statements:

1. A figure named Job was proverbial by Ezekiel’s time. **[EARLY]**
2. The surviving book is sophisticated literary composition with multiple textual forms. **[EARLY/RECONSTRUCTION]**
3. No external evidence verifies the protagonist’s possessions, ordeal, family, grave, or lifetime. **[NEGATIVE FINDING]**

An historical sufferer may lie behind the proverb or prose tale, but that possibility is non-falsifiable with present evidence. Rabbinic tradition itself preserves both existence and parable positions. Maimonides states that, whether or not Job existed, the heavenly dialogue is a fiction constructed to examine providence. [*Guide for the Perplexed* III.22](https://www.sacred-texts.com/jud/gfp/gfp158.htm)

### Unity and compositional growth

Major positions include:

- Essential literary unity, with deliberate changes in voice and genre.
- An old prose folktale expanded by a poetic dialogue.
- A principal poem supplemented by chapter 28, Elihu, and/or parts of the divine speeches.
- Multiple editions rather than a simple sequence of interpolations.

The shorter Greek text once encouraged theories of a shorter Hebrew edition. Current work often attributes much of its brevity to the Greek translator’s condensation, although textual history remains debated. No surviving pre-Masoretic manuscript supplies a complete alternative Hebrew Job.

### Genre

Labels include wisdom dialogue, dramatic poem, disputation, lament, lawsuit, theodicy, anti-theodicy, didactic tale, and tragicomedy. “Drama” describes dialogue and staging but there is no evidence that ancient Job was written for theatrical performance. “Theodicy” is useful but incomplete because God never explains the wager to Job.

### The friends: refuted or partly right?

The epilogue says Eliphaz and companions did not speak rightly as Job did. Yet many individual statements by the friends resemble other biblical wisdom. Scholars therefore debate whether the verdict rejects their entire theology, their application of it to Job, their escalating accusations, or their speech about God. The text’s polyphony resists a simple rule that everything Job says is correct and everything the friends say is false.

### Job 19:25

Hebrew *gōʾēl* ordinarily denotes a vindicator or kinsman-redeemer. The passage may express hope for a heavenly advocate, posthumous vindication, or vindication before death; syntax and text are difficult. The explicitly Christian resurrection reading belongs to Septuagintal, Latin, liturgical, and patristic reception. It should not be stated as the uncontested meaning of the Hebrew poem.

### Job 42:6

Possible translations include “I despise/retract [my words] and repent,” “I reject and am comforted,” or “I relent concerning dust and ashes.” The ambiguity affects whether Job submits, changes his mind, rejects his prior case, or receives consolation. Robert Alter and other modern translators foreground the philological uncertainty rather than treating penitence as settled.

### The divine answer

Interpretive schools include:

- God overwhelms Job without answering the justice question.
- The speeches replace anthropocentric retribution with a wider, partly wild creation.
- God validates Job’s honest speech and repudiates pious falsehood.
- Behemoth and Leviathan symbolize controlled chaos.
- The answer is intentionally inadequate, preserving protest.
- Job’s encounter supplies presence rather than propositional explanation.

These are readings of the literary effect, not discoverable biographical events.

### Comparative ancient Near Eastern material

Parallels with *Ludlul bēl nēmeqi*, the Babylonian Theodicy, Sumerian suffering prayers, Ugaritic divine councils, and Lotan establish a shared intellectual and mythic environment. Similarity does not prove that Job translated or plagiarized a single Mesopotamian work. The common internet formula “Job is copied from the Babylonian Job” is discredited oversimplification.

### Oral-formulaic and folklore approaches

The prose frame contains folktale-like repetitions and patterned calamity reports; the poetry uses inherited laments, hymns, legal language, and wisdom forms. Oral antecedents are plausible, but reconstruction of a complete oral “original Job” is speculative.

### Gender criticism

Modern feminist work has recovered the silenced perspectives of Job’s wife, dead daughters and sons, servants, and the replacement children. The Septuagint and *Testament of Job* already expand women’s roles, but they do so differently: the Greek wife becomes a voice of labor and grief; the *Testament* both subjects its first wife to Satanic manipulation and grants the restored daughters extraordinary visionary authority. [Study of women in the *Testament*](https://www.aup-online.com/content/journals/10.5117/NTT1986.40.019.HORS)

### State of the evidence

- Entire Hebrew medieval codices survive.
- Ancient Hebrew Job survives only fragmentarily.
- An extensive but incomplete Aramaic Qumran translation survives.
- Old Greek must be critically reconstructed from manuscripts and later revisions.
- The *Testament of Job* survives through Christian transmission, complicating identification of its earliest form.
- Archaeology illuminates Edom, Persian-period Yehud, scribal culture, and ancient Near Eastern imagery but has produced no Job artifact.
- Digital manuscript work and improved Qumran imaging refine textual readings; they have not verified a historical Job.
