# Daniel: Research Dossier

**Traditional class:** In Jewish tradition Daniel is an exemplary righteous sage and apocalyptic visionary, but ordinarily not one of the *neviʾim* (“prophets”) in the strict rabbinic classification; his book belongs to the *Ketuvim*, the Writings. Christianity classifies him as a prophet, often one of the four “major prophets.” Later Islam generally calls Dāniyāl a prophet, although neither he nor his book is named in the Qurʾan. As a comparative mythic type he is a legendary wise courtier, dream-interpreter, innocent sufferer, monster-slayer and apocalyptic revealer—not a demigod or securely attested historical court official.

**Earliest secure attestation:** The earliest securely datable evidence for the familiar Daniel hero is the literary figure in the Book of Daniel. Its older Aramaic court tales probably circulated during the Persian or early Hellenistic period, while the book’s final apocalyptic form is conventionally dated about 167–164 BCE from its detailed knowledge of Antiochus IV’s persecution and its inaccurate expectation of his end. Manuscript fragments from Qumran, including 4QDanᶜ/4Q114, show that Daniel was copied by the later second century BCE. Ezekiel 14:14, 14:20 and 28:3, conventionally dated to the early sixth century BCE, mention a differently spelled *Danʾel*; whether that figure is the later Daniel, the Ugaritic Danel, or another proverbial sage remains disputed.

## Evidentiary labels

- **[Early text]** securely attested ancient evidence or the earliest recoverable textual layer.
- **[Canonical elaboration]** material in the received Hebrew-Aramaic book but belonging to its later literary framing or apocalyptic edition.
- **[Greek addition]** material transmitted in the Old Greek or Theodotionic Daniel but absent from the Masoretic Text.
- **[Regional variant]** geographically restricted version or localization.
- **[Learned systematization]** rabbinic, patristic, scholastic or later exegetical organization.
- **[Syncretistic identification]** equation with another traditional person or deity.
- **[Medieval/early-modern accretion]** post-antique expansion.
- **[Folklore]** devotional or oral tradition without independent historical confirmation.
- **[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** a critical inference, not a statement made by an ancient source.
- **[Modern invention]** demonstrably recent expansion marketed or circulated as ancient.

## 1. Identification, Names and Epithets

Daniel is the name assigned to a Judean exile who, in Daniel 1–6, preserves dietary purity, interprets royal dreams and portents, survives hostile courtiers and a lions’ pit, and rises under successive Babylonian and “Median” governments. In Daniel 7–12 he becomes the pseudonymous recipient of revelations about the Seleucid crisis, the resurrection of the dead and the end of foreign rule. The stories present him as living from Nebuchadnezzar’s accession through Cyrus’s reign, conventionally the early sixth century BCE. Critical scholarship instead treats that chronology as the narrative setting of traditions written and edited centuries later. The book therefore documents how Hellenistic-period Jews imagined an ideal exile-era sage; it does not constitute a contemporary Babylonian personnel record.

### Names

- **Hebrew:** דָּנִיֵּאל, *Dāniyyēl* or *Dāniʾel*.
- **Aramaic:** דָּנִיֵּאל, conventionally *Dāniyyēl*.
- **Greek:** Δανιήλ, *Daniēl*.
- **Latin:** *Daniel*.
- **Syriac:** ܕܢܝܐܝܠ, *Dāniʾel*.
- **Arabic and Persian:** دانيال, *Dāniyāl*.
- **Geʿez:** ዳንኤል, *Dāniʾel*.

The name is normally parsed as the Semitic verbal element *dān/dānānī*, “judged me” or “is my judge,” plus *ʾēl*, “God”: “God is my judge” or “God has judged.” This is secure as a traditional Hebrew explanation, although exact verbal parsing varies.

Ezekiel writes דנאל, *Dnʾl*, without the internal *yod* found in the Book of Daniel. Because Ugaritic likewise writes *dnʾil*, scholars after the discovery of the Ugaritic tablets compared Ezekiel’s sage with Danel, father of Aqhat. Orthography alone cannot prove identity, since consonantal spellings fluctuate, but the difference is relevant.

### Babylonian court name

Daniel 1:7 says Ashpenaz renamed him **Belteshazzar**, Aramaic בֵּלְטְשַׁאצַּר, Greek Βαλτασαρ. Daniel 4 associates the name with Nebuchadnezzar’s god. Proposed Akkadian reconstructions include *Bēlet-šar-uṣur*, “Lady, protect the king,” or a form of *Balāṭ-šar-uṣur*, “Protect the life of the king.” Neither reconstruction is completely certain; the biblical spelling may already be a distorted foreign name. Belteshazzar must not be confused with **Belshazzar**, the king in Daniel 5.

### Titles and classifications

- **“One of the Judean exiles” / “sons of Judah”** — Daniel 1:6; **[Canonical elaboration]**.
- **Royal or noble youth** — implied by Daniel 1:3–6, but Daniel is not individually said to be a prince; **[Canonical elaboration]**.
- **Wise man, magician or member of the Babylonian learned class** — Daniel 1:17–20; 2:48; 4:9; 5:11; **[Early text]**.
- **“Chief of the magicians”** — Daniel 4:9; this is a court title within the story, not external evidence.
- **“Man greatly beloved/precious”** — Daniel 9:23; 10:11, 19; **[Canonical elaboration]**.
- **Prophet** — Josephus, *Antiquities* 10.266–268; Matthew 24:15; Christian canon and liturgy; **[Learned systematization]**.
- **Not a prophet, but superior as a visionary in certain respects** — Babylonian Talmud, *Megillah* 3a, which says Haggai, Zechariah and Malachi were prophets while Daniel was not, although he saw what they did not; **[Rabbinic systematization]**.
- **Prophet Dāniyāl** — post-Qurʾanic Muslim historiography and *qiṣaṣ al-anbiyāʾ*; **[Medieval Islamic accretion]**.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### The Ezekiel problem

Ezekiel 14:14 and 14:20 names “Noah, Danʾel and Job” as paradigms of righteousness unable to save anyone but themselves; Ezekiel 28:3 sarcastically asks the ruler of Tyre whether he is “wiser than Danʾel.”

Three explanations remain in circulation:

1. **The biblical Daniel:** Traditional Jewish and Christian readers identify Ezekiel’s figure with the exile sage. This creates a chronological difficulty: Ezekiel treats him like Noah and Job, proverbial figures already famous enough to cite, while the Book of Daniel makes him Ezekiel’s contemporary. **[Traditional identification]**

2. **Ugaritic Danel:** The thirteenth-century-BCE Tale of Aqhat depicts Danel as a king or judge who “judges the widow’s case” and defends the orphan, and whose child is imperiled. His antiquity, wisdom, connection with Tyre’s Northwest Semitic cultural world and consonantal name *dnʾil* fit Ezekiel’s list. This became a widespread modern scholarly view after the Ugaritic discoveries. John Day nevertheless stressed that Ezekiel’s Danel and the later book-hero need not be identical even if the Daniel author borrowed the prestigious name. **[Modern scholarly reconstruction]**

3. **Another lost sage:** The surviving Aqhat tablets never explicitly call Danel exceptionally wise, and their polytheistic hero does not precisely match Ezekiel’s righteous exemplar. Ezekiel may have known another version of Danel or a different traditional wise man. **[Modern scholarly caution]**

No surviving source proves that the Daniel of the canonical book existed before its tales. The safest conclusion is that Ezekiel attests a proverbial **Danʾel**, but his identity with the later apocalyptic hero is not secure.

### The Book of Daniel

The Masoretic book has two overlapping structures:

- Daniel 1–6: court tales.
- Daniel 7–12: apocalyptic visions.
- Daniel 1:1–2:4a and 8–12: Hebrew.
- Daniel 2:4b–7: Aramaic.

The Aramaic section itself forms a loose concentric structure: four kingdoms in chapters 2 and 7; deliverances from royal death decrees in chapters 3 and 6; and judgments on Babylonian kings in chapters 4 and 5. The language boundary therefore does not coincide with the tales/visions boundary. Proposed explanations include reuse of an Aramaic tale collection, deliberate bilingual composition, translation from one language into another, and editorial joining of earlier units. None commands universal agreement.

The prevailing critical reconstruction is:

- Individual court tales or a small Aramaic collection developed in Jewish eastern-diaspora settings during the Persian or early Hellenistic centuries.
- Daniel 7 adapted the four-kingdom scheme to Antiochus IV.
- Hebrew visions in chapters 8–12 and a Hebrew introduction brought the work into approximately its present form around 167–164 BCE.
- Daniel 11 follows Seleucid-Ptolemaic history closely through Antiochus’s persecution but predicts a death for Antiochus in or near Palestine that did not occur; Antiochus died in the east in 164 BCE. This “accurate up to the author’s present, inaccurate thereafter” pattern is the central evidence for the date—not merely an assumption that prophecy is impossible. [TheTorah’s discussion of Daniel 7](https://www.thetorah.com/article/daniels-vision-of-the-four-beasts-the-prehistory-of-chanukah) and the [Oxford Research Encyclopedia overview](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/62249/chapter-abstract/551358644) summarize this setting.

Conservative Jewish and Christian scholarship argues for substantial sixth-century authorship, citing Imperial Aramaic features, Persian administrative vocabulary, knowledge of Babylonian customs and the book’s self-presentation. These arguments can support the antiquity of some traditions, but they do not explain as economically the historical sequence and failed expectation in Daniel 11. The presence of Persian and Greek loanwords is consistent with, but does not alone prove, a Hellenistic date.

### Manuscripts and versions

Fragments of Daniel from Qumran preserve portions of eleven chapters; 4Q174 also quotes Daniel 12. These witnesses demonstrate Daniel’s rapid acceptance but not sixth-century authorship. The [Leon Levy Dead Sea Scrolls Digital Library](https://www.deadseascrolls.org.il/explore-the-archive/search?locale=en_US) provides the manuscript archive.

A 2025 radiocarbon-and-handwriting study proposed a broad calibrated range extending approximately 230–160 BCE for 4Q114. Because the range is broad, the material may predate, overlap or postdate the conventional final composition date. Contamination, calibration and the difficulty of applying the handwriting model to its semi-cursive script require caution. Even a manuscript copied before 164 would revise the redactional chronology; it would not make the sixth-century court narrative contemporary testimony. **[Contested modern reconstruction]**

The principal textual forms are:

- **Masoretic Text:** twelve chapters in Hebrew and Aramaic.
- **Old Greek Daniel:** probably produced by the late second or early first century BCE; often freer and substantially different, especially in chapters 4–6.
- **Theodotionic Daniel:** a closer Greek revision that displaced the Old Greek in most Christian use by late antiquity.
- **Papyrus 967:** a third-century-CE witness to the Old Greek, with a different order: Daniel 1–4, 7–8, 5–6, 9–12, Bel, Susanna.
- **Vulgate:** Jerome translated the Hebrew-Aramaic text but retained the Greek additions as chapters 13–14 and the additions to chapter 3, marking their non-Hebrew status.
- **Syriac Peshitta, Coptic, Armenian, Ethiopic and Old Latin versions:** evidence for Christian transmission and local textual history.

### Greek additions

The [NETS critical translation of Bel and the Dragon](https://ccat.sas.upenn.edu/nets/edition/41-bel-nets.pdf) presents both Old Greek and Theodotion from the Göttingen editions.

- **Prayer of Azariah and Song of the Three Young Men:** inserted into Daniel 3. Azariah confesses Israel’s sin in the furnace; an angel cools the fire; the three sing a cosmic hymn. **[Greek addition]**
- **Susanna:** Daniel, presented in Theodotion as a youth, exposes two elders who attempted to coerce Susanna into sex and then falsely accused her of adultery. By separating the witnesses and eliciting contradictory tree names, he prevents her judicial murder. **[Greek addition]**
- **Bel:** Daniel demonstrates that Bel’s priests and families secretly consume the idol’s offerings by scattering ashes on the sealed temple floor. The priests, wives and children are executed and Bel’s temple destroyed. **[Greek addition; contains collective capital punishment]**
- **The Dragon:** Daniel kills a living cult animal with cakes of pitch, fat and hair; opponents place him in a lions’ pit for six days; an angel transports Habakkuk by his hair from Judea to Babylon with food. **[Greek addition]**

Old Greek and Theodotion differ in wording, narrative framing and details—four versus forty sheep in Bel’s rations, for example. “Septuagint Daniel” must therefore not be treated as a single uniform text.

### Related Danielic literature

- **Prayer of Nabonidus, 4Q242:** an Aramaic text in which Nabonidus suffers a seven-year affliction at Teima and a Jewish diviner explains it. Its overlap with Daniel 4 suggests a shared or adapted tradition; it does not prove which text is directly dependent on the other. **[Early parallel]**
- **Pseudo-Daniel, 4Q243–245:** fragmentary Aramaic works placing revelations and historical surveys under Daniel’s name. **[Second Temple Danielic expansion]**
- **1 Enoch:** does not make Daniel a character, but its symbolic history, angelic revealers, heavenly judgment and “son of man” traditions belong to the same developing apocalyptic environment. Direction of dependence in individual motifs is disputed.
- **Jubilees:** does not recount the canonical Daniel. Jubilees 4 contains another Danel in Enoch’s genealogy; equation with the court sage is unwarranted.
- **Life of Adam and Eve and the Testaments of the Twelve Patriarchs:** Daniel is not a narrative character. Similar angelology or apocalypse is contextual, not an interaction.
- **Josephus, *Antiquities* 10.186–281:** retells Daniel 1–6 and the visions, calls Daniel one of the greatest prophets, and claims that he predicted Antiochus Epiphanes and Rome. Josephus also says Daniel built a splendid tower at Ecbatana/Susa where Median, Persian and Parthian rulers were buried. **[First-century-CE Jewish elaboration]**
- **Lives of the Prophets:** assigns Daniel traditions concerning his death or burial in eastern lands; manuscript forms vary. **[Late antique legendary geography]**
- **New Testament:** Matthew 24:15 names “Daniel the prophet”; Mark 13:14 has the “desolating sacrilege” without naming Daniel. Hebrews 11:33’s heroes who “shut the mouths of lions” probably allude to Daniel, but he is not named.
- **Revelation:** extensively reuses Daniel’s beasts, horns, heavenly court and temporal formulas but never makes Daniel a character.

## 3. The Story

### Exile, education and purity

Daniel 1 sets the exile in Jehoiakim’s third year, conventionally 606/605 BCE, when Nebuchadnezzar besieges Jerusalem and removes temple vessels and elite youths. Daniel, Hananiah, Mishael and Azariah receive Babylonian names and three years’ education. Daniel refuses the royal food and wine, requests vegetables and water for ten days, and becomes healthier than the other trainees. God gives all four knowledge, but Daniel receives special insight into dreams.

**Historical difficulty:** The Babylonian Chronicle securely places Nebuchadnezzar’s capture of Jerusalem and Jehoiachin’s deportation in 597 BCE. No surviving Babylonian record confirms the siege described for Jehoiakim’s third year, and Jeremiah’s regnal reckoning differs. The chapter may conflate campaigning, submission and the later deportation. **[Canonical story; historical setting contested]**

The food refusal may concern impurity, meat not slaughtered according to Jewish practice, food offered to gods, or political dependence. The text never states the exact reason. Its function is clear: fidelity under foreign rule produces divine favor without requiring withdrawal from court service.

### Nebuchadnezzar’s statue dream

In Daniel 2 Nebuchadnezzar demands both his forgotten or concealed dream and its interpretation. Daniel and his friends pray; God reveals the secret. A statue with gold head, silver torso, bronze body, iron legs and iron-clay feet is destroyed by a stone “not cut by hands,” which becomes a mountain. Daniel identifies Babylon as the golden head and predicts successive inferior kingdoms followed by God’s everlasting kingdom.

In the Maccabean reading, the sequence is Babylon, Media, Persia and Greece, with the divided Seleucid-Ptolemaic world represented by iron and clay. Later Jewish and Christian interpretation merged Media and Persia, made Greece third and Rome fourth. The latter became the basis of Roman, Byzantine, medieval and modern historicist schemes. Neither list is explicitly supplied in chapter 2; the identifications arise by coordinating it with chapters 7–11.

### The fiery furnace

Daniel 3 concerns **Daniel’s three friends, not Daniel**. Nebuchadnezzar orders officials to worship a giant golden image. Shadrach, Meshach and Abednego refuse. They tell the king that God can save them, “but if not,” they still will not worship the image. They are cast bound into an overheated furnace; the executioners die from the flames. The king sees four unbound figures, the fourth “like a son of the gods,” and promotes the three.

The Masoretic text neither explains Daniel’s absence nor identifies the fourth being by name. The Greek addition makes an angel extinguish the fire and inserts the Prayer of Azariah and Song of the Three. Christian interpretation commonly identified the fourth figure typologically with Christ; that is a patristic reading, not the Aramaic narrative’s explicit statement.

Rabbinic expansions ask where Daniel was and why the three later disappear. *Sanhedrin* 93a–b proposes alternatives: Daniel had been sent away, was fetching fodder, or was absent so that Nebuchadnezzar would not mistakenly attribute rescue to him; traditions also connect the youths’ later fate with execution or miraculous survival. **[Rabbinic systematization]**

### Nebuchadnezzar’s tree and madness

Daniel 4 is an autobiographical royal proclamation in Aramaic. Nebuchadnezzar dreams of a cosmic tree cut down by a heavenly “watcher.” Daniel interprets it: the king will be driven from humanity, live with beasts and eat grass until he acknowledges divine sovereignty. After twelve months the sentence occurs; after “seven times” his reason and kingdom return.

No Babylonian document records such an episode for Nebuchadnezzar. The Prayer of Nabonidus and later traditions about Nabonidus’s long absence at Teima suggest that a story concerning Nabonidus may have been transferred to the better-known Nebuchadnezzar. This is plausible reconstruction, not demonstrated genealogy.

### Belshazzar’s feast and the writing on the wall

Daniel 5 makes Belshazzar hold a banquet using Jerusalem’s temple vessels while praising gods of metal and stone. A disembodied hand writes **מנא מנא תקל ופרסין**, *mĕnēʾ mĕnēʾ tĕqēl ûp̄arsîn*. Daniel interprets the words through puns on weights and verbs:

- *mene*: God has numbered the kingdom.
- *tekel*: Belshazzar has been weighed and found wanting.
- *peres/parsin*: the kingdom is divided and given to the Medes and Persians.

Belshazzar is killed that night and “Darius the Mede” receives the kingdom.

Babylonian sources confirm Belshazzar as Nabonidus’s son and an important royal administrator or co-regent, which vindicated the name as genuinely ancient after it was recovered from cuneiform. They do not call him Nebuchadnezzar’s literal son; “father” can mean predecessor or dynastic ancestor in literary usage, but the story’s succession remains historically confused. Cyrus’s Persians—not an otherwise unknown Darius the Mede—captured Babylon in 539 BCE. Thus the narrative combines authentic eastern names with a schematic succession.

### The lions’ den

Daniel 6 takes place under Darius the Mede. Jealous officials induce the king to issue an irrevocable thirty-day ban on petitions to gods or humans other than himself. Daniel continues praying three times daily toward Jerusalem. He is thrown into a sealed lions’ pit; an angel shuts the lions’ mouths. Darius then casts Daniel’s accusers, their wives and children into the pit, where the lions kill them before they reach the bottom.

The collective execution of families is integral to the story and should not be sanitized. It corresponds to ancient tales of court reversal but is not evidence for a specific Persian legal event. The law of the “Medes and Persians” functions as a narrative device emphasizing the king’s inability to reverse his decree.

Bel and the Dragon supplies a second lions’ den: Daniel remains six days among seven lions, while Habakkuk is supernaturally transported to feed him. Later images sometimes combine details from the two episodes.

### Four beasts and heavenly judgment

Daniel 7 presents four beasts from the sea: a winged lion, a bear, a four-winged and four-headed leopard, and a terrifying ten-horned beast. A “little horn” persecutes the holy ones. The Ancient of Days judges; “one like a son of man” comes with the clouds and receives dominion; the holy ones receive the kingdom.

In its probable Maccabean context, the beasts represent Babylon, Media, Persia and Greece; the little horn is Antiochus IV. “One like a son of man” may symbolize the angelic patron or the collective holy people rather than an individual messiah. Early Christianity identified the figure with Jesus, decisively reshaping the passage’s reception.

### Ram, goat and Antiochus

Daniel 8 explicitly identifies a two-horned ram as Media and Persia and a goat as Greece. The goat’s great horn is its first king, normally Alexander; four horns are successor kingdoms. A little horn attacks the sanctuary and interrupts sacrifice for 2,300 “evenings and mornings.” Whether that means 2,300 days or 1,150 paired evening-and-morning sacrifices has long been disputed.

### The seventy weeks

In Daniel 9, Daniel reads Jeremiah’s seventy-year prophecy, confesses Israel’s sins and receives Gabriel’s reinterpretation: seventy “weeks” are decreed, conventionally seventy weeks of years or 490 years. After seven and sixty-two weeks an “anointed one” is cut off; a ruler destroys or desecrates the city and sanctuary; sacrifice ceases during the final week.

The dominant historical-critical reading associates the cut-off anointed one with the murdered high priest Onias III and the final crisis with Antiochus IV’s suppression of the Jerusalem cult in 167–164 BCE. The arithmetic does not map cleanly onto actual chronology, probably because it schematizes Jeremiah through jubilee symbolism.

Christian interpreters applied the passage to Jesus’s ministry and death, sometimes beginning the count with Persian decrees in Ezra or Nehemiah. Dispensationalism inserts a long gap between the sixty-ninth and seventieth weeks and assigns the last week to a future Antichrist. Neither Jesus nor a chronological gap is named in Daniel 9; these are later confessional constructions.

### Final visions, angels and resurrection

Daniel 10–12 stages conflict between angelic “princes” of Persia and Greece. Gabriel-like revelatory speech—though the speaker is not consistently named—describes wars between the Ptolemaic “king of the south” and Seleucid “king of the north.” Michael protects Daniel’s people. Daniel 12:2 contains the Hebrew Bible’s clearest early statement of a differentiated resurrection: some of the dead awaken to everlasting life, others to shame. Daniel is told to seal the book and is promised that he will “stand” for his allotted portion at the end.

The text never narrates Daniel’s ordinary death, marriage or burial.

### Susanna

Two elders spy on the married Susanna bathing, demand sex and threaten to accuse her of adultery if she refuses. She refuses, is convicted on their testimony and faces death. Young Daniel interrupts, separates them, and asks under which tree the assault occurred. Their incompatible answers expose the lie; they receive the penalty intended for Susanna.

The story condemns attempted rape, abuse of judicial authority and false testimony. Greek wordplay on the tree names and punishments works naturally in Greek, supporting Greek composition or thorough Greek rewriting, although some scholars have proposed a Semitic original.

### Bel and the dragon

Daniel refuses to worship Bel, exposes the priests’ fraud by footprints in ash and destroys the idol and temple. The priests’ wives and children are implicated in the secret consumption and are killed with the priests. Daniel then kills a venerated “dragon” or serpent without a sword by feeding it an explosive or indigestible mixture. These stories recast Daniel as an anti-idolatry detective and monster-slayer. Identifying the dragon straightforwardly with Tiamat is a modern comparative speculation, not something the text says.

## 4. Relations with Gods and Other Heroes

### Genealogy and household

No canonical source names Daniel’s parents, spouse or children. Daniel 1 places him among selected Judean youths, possibly royal or noble, but does not supply a genealogy. Josephus and rabbinic tradition infer royal descent from Isaiah 39:7, in which Hezekiah’s descendants become palace eunuchs. Rabbinic and Christian writers consequently sometimes make Daniel a descendant of Hezekiah and a eunuch. This is inference and legend, not information in Daniel.

A rabbinic identification equates Daniel with **Hatach**, Esther’s intermediary in Esther 4, partly because both are righteous court figures. Other traditions identify Hatach with someone else. **[Rabbinic syncretistic identification]**

### Named interactions

The following records direct interaction or an explicit traditional equation. Mere thematic resemblance is excluded.

- **God of Israel / “God of heaven” / Most High:** gives Daniel wisdom and dream knowledge; reveals mysteries; receives Daniel’s prayers; sends deliverance and promises resurrection — Daniel 1–2, 6, 9–12, final form c. 164 BCE. **[Canonical]**
- **Ashpenaz:** renames and supervises Daniel and his companions — Daniel 1. **[Canonical]**
- **Unnamed palace guard:** negotiates Daniel’s vegetable test — Daniel 1. **[Canonical; not a named figure]**
- **Hananiah/Shadrach:** fellow exile and prayer companion; Daniel secures his promotion — Daniel 1–2. Daniel does not appear with him in the furnace — Daniel 3. **[Canonical]**
- **Mishael/Meshach:** same relationship — Daniel 1–2. **[Canonical]**
- **Azariah/Abednego:** same relationship; in Greek Daniel 3 Azariah prays in the furnace — Daniel 1–3. **[Canonical/Greek addition]**
- **Nebuchadnezzar II:** takes Daniel into court, tests and promotes him; receives Daniel’s interpretations of the statue and tree dreams — Daniel 1–4. **[Early court-tale tradition]**
- **Arioch:** captain whom Daniel persuades not to execute Babylon’s sages; brings him to the king — Daniel 2. **[Canonical]**
- **Belshazzar:** summons Daniel to read the wall; rewards him immediately before his own death — Daniel 5. **[Canonical]**
- **The queen/queen mother:** recommends Daniel to Belshazzar — Daniel 5; unnamed, so no further identification is secure.
- **Darius the Mede:** appoints Daniel, reluctantly consigns him to the lions, witnesses his survival and punishes his enemies — Daniel 6 and 9. **[Canonical; ruler historically unattested]**
- **Cyrus the Persian:** Daniel is said to prosper or remain until his reign; a vision is dated to Cyrus’s third year — Daniel 1:21; 6:28; 10:1. No dialogue occurs in the Masoretic text. **[Canonical]**
- **Gabriel:** interprets the ram-and-goat vision and the seventy weeks — Daniel 8–9. **[Canonical angelic interaction]**
- **Michael:** named as “one of the chief princes” and protector of Daniel’s people; aids the revelatory angel against Persia — Daniel 10–12. Daniel receives information about Michael but no direct dialogue with him is narrated. **[Canonical]**
- **Prince of Persia:** unnamed celestial adversary who obstructs Daniel’s revelatory messenger — Daniel 10. **[Canonical angelology]**
- **Prince of Greece:** celestial power whose coming is foretold — Daniel 10. Daniel does not personally encounter him. **[Canonical angelology]**
- **Jeremiah:** Daniel reads his prophecy of seventy years — Daniel 9:2. This is textual reception, not a personal meeting. **[Canonical]**
- **Ancient of Days:** Daniel sees this enthroned divine figure in vision — Daniel 7. **[Canonical vision]**
- **“One like a son of man”:** Daniel sees the figure receive dominion — Daniel 7. Identification with Jesus is Christian interpretation beginning in the New Testament period; identification with an individual messiah is not explicit in Daniel. **[Canonical vision/later identification]**
- **Susanna:** Daniel rescues her from execution by exposing false witnesses — Susanna, probably Hellenistic; **[Greek addition]**.
- **Joakim:** Susanna’s husband; Daniel’s intervention restores his household — Susanna; they do not converse directly. **[Greek addition]**
- **Bel:** Daniel refuses to worship the idol, exposes its priests and destroys it — Bel 1–22. Bel is Babylonian Marduk under the title Bēl, though the story treats the image as fraudulent — **[Greek addition/syncretistic identification]**.
- **The dragon:** Daniel refuses worship and kills the cult animal — Bel 23–30. It is not named as Tiamat in the source. **[Greek addition]**
- **Habakkuk:** an angel transports him to feed Daniel in the lions’ pit — Bel 33–39. The prophet’s chronological relocation is legendary. **[Greek addition]**
- **Unnamed angel in Bel:** carries Habakkuk by the hair and returns him — Bel 33–39. **[Greek addition]**
- **Jesus:** Daniel never meets Jesus in Jewish scripture. Matthew calls him a prophet and applies his “desolating sacrilege” to a later crisis; Christian art and theology identify Daniel 7’s humanlike figure with Christ. **[Later Christian identification, not ancient narrative interaction]**
- **Hezekiah:** Daniel is made Hezekiah’s descendant through interpretation of Isaiah 39:7 — Josephus and rabbinic tradition. No canonical encounter occurs. **[Learned genealogical construction]**
- **Isaiah:** said to have foretold Daniel’s eunuch status through the oracle to Hezekiah — *Sanhedrin* 93b and patristic commentary. No meeting. **[Rabbinic/patristic construction]**
- **Esther/Hatach:** Daniel is identified with Esther’s messenger Hatach in rabbinic tradition; no biblical text places Daniel and Esther together. **[Rabbinic syncretistic identification]**
- **Ezra:** some tomb traditions place Ezra near Daniel, but no early text narrates an interaction. **[Regional folklore]**
- **ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb:** Islamic conquest narratives say the caliph ordered Daniel’s discovered body reburied secretly and returned or retained its signet — al-Ṭabarī and related traditions, written centuries after the alleged seventh-century event. **[Medieval Islamic accretion]**
- **Abū Mūsā al-Ashʿarī:** said to discover Daniel’s incorrupt body and book at Susa or Tustar — Islamic conquest traditions. **[Medieval historiography]**
- **David:** a modern Iraqi shrine page reports a tradition that Daniel met David, requiring extraordinary longevity; no early Jewish, Christian or Islamic source securely supports it. **[Regional folklore/modern circulation]**
- **Danel and Aqhat:** the canonical Daniel never meets either. The equation of Daniel with Aqhat’s father is a modern scholarly hypothesis about names and tradition, not a narrated interaction. **[Modern reconstruction]**

## 5. Cult, Tomb and Ritual

### Ancient cult

There is no secure archaeological evidence that Jews in the Second Temple period worshipped Daniel as a heroized dead person, sacrificed to him or maintained an authenticated tomb. The biblical book gives no death or burial location. Jewish opposition to worship of human beings also makes “hero cult” an inexact category.

The Talmud mentions a “synagogue of Daniel” near Barnish in *Eruvin* 21a. This is literary evidence for a commemorative place-name or sanctuary, not an excavated tomb. **[Rabbinic attestation]**

Christian devotion classifies Daniel as prophet and saint. His image in catacombs and sarcophagi is funerary and salvific rather than evidence of sacrifices made to him. Eastern churches commemorate Daniel with the three young men, commonly on 17 December; Western calendars have used 21 July or other local dates. Liturgical calendars vary.

### Images

From the third century CE, Daniel between two lions became one of the commonest early Christian deliverance images. He is frequently naked or tunic-clad, frontal, with arms raised in the *orans* posture; two lions replace the story’s unspecified number. The type signified rescue from death, resurrection and the persecuted believer’s hope. A sixth-century synagogue mosaic at Naʿaran shows that the motif also circulated in Jewish art. The [Complutense iconographic survey](https://webs.ucm.es/centros/cont/descargas/documento24825.pdf) documents its early-Christian, Jewish and Romanesque development.

Other established cycles include:

- The three youths in the furnace.
- Susanna and the elders, sometimes treated as an image of the persecuted Church.
- Belshazzar’s feast and the hand writing on the wall.
- Daniel’s visions of beasts.
- Habakkuk feeding Daniel, especially in medieval art.
- Daniel as a bearded prophet holding a scroll in Byzantine and Western prophet series.

### Competing tombs

The multiplicity of tombs is evidence of Daniel’s prestige, not of the body’s location.

- **Susa/Shush, Iran:** The best-known shrine, shared in Muslim and Persian-Jewish memory. Benjamin of Tudela’s twelfth-century itinerary describes a dispute between communities on opposite sides of the river over possession of Daniel’s body and a settlement involving its suspension or periodic allocation. The existing conical shrine is much later. Daniel 8 places a vision at Susa but never says Daniel died there. **[Medieval localization]**
- **Tustar/Shushtar:** Islamic conquest narratives often place discovery of the corpse at al-Sūs or Tustar. The geographical names shift among transmitters. **[Medieval Islamic historiography]**
- **Kirkuk:** A mosque in the citadel contains tombs attributed to Daniel and the three youths. Local tradition predates some modern descriptions, but there is no archaeological authentication. **[Regional cult and folklore]**
- **Samarkand:** Khoja Daniyar’s elongated tomb is associated with Daniel, Danyal or a local saint. A widespread story says Timur transferred relics from Susa. The structure and traditions are medieval or later. **[Regional folklore]**
- **Tarsus:** A tomb discovered or identified in later Islamic tradition is associated with Daniel; its archaeological setting does not identify the occupant. **[Regional localization]**
- **Mosul and Muqdadiyah:** Iraqi shrines have also been assigned to Daniel. Some were damaged or destroyed during recent conflict. **[Regional tradition]**
- **Alexandria:** Nabi Daniel Mosque is connected by name with a later Muslim scholar, Muḥammad Dāniyāl al-Mawṣilī; identifying its crypt as the biblical Daniel’s tomb is erroneous. **[Later misidentification]**

The [Encyclopaedia Iranica account](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/danial-e-nabi/) records the Iranian traditions; recent scholarship on early Islamic relic narratives notes that some accounts said the discovered body was Daniel while others said Darius.

### Islamic relic narrative

Al-Ṭabarī’s conquest history, drawing on earlier transmitters, places the discovery in the 640s. A body lay in a silver coffin; residents used it in drought rites. A ring showed a man between two lions. ʿUmar ordered the body secretly buried so that its site would not become an object of improper veneration. The [English translation of al-Ṭabarī, volume 13](https://ghayb.com/wp-content/uploads/2016/09/Tabari_Volume_13.pdf) preserves this layer.

This is evidence that a Daniel relic legend existed by medieval Islamic historiography. It is not contemporary evidence for the body’s identity or even for every detail of the conquest story.

## 6. Historical Evolution of the Figure

### Sixth century BCE: possible proverbial precursor

Ezekiel invokes Danʾel as a righteous and wise exemplar. If this is Ugaritic Danel or another ancient sage, it supplies a prestigious name later available to Jewish storytellers, not a biography of the courtier.

### Persian to early Hellenistic period

Diaspora court tales likely formed around a Jewish sage who succeeds without abandoning ancestral loyalty. Daniel belongs to the international “wise courtier” family alongside Joseph, Ahiqar and Mordecai, but direct borrowing cannot always be demonstrated. Stories of dream interpretation, calumniated officials and reversal at court gave communities under empire a model of resistance through knowledge, prayer and selective accommodation.

### 167–164 BCE: apocalyptic redaction

The Antiochene persecution converted the court hero into a revealer of history’s divine timetable. The older tales promise that God can humble kings; the visions promise that the whole succession of beastly empires is near its end. The final author writes prophecy under an ancient sage’s name, a standard apocalyptic convention rather than modern signed authorship.

### Late Second Temple Judaism

Qumran copied Daniel and composed additional Danielic revelations. The four-kingdom schema, resurrection hope, angelic interpreters and coded chronology entered Jewish apocalyptic discourse. Josephus emphasized fulfilled prediction and presented Daniel as uniquely precise about future empires.

### Early Christianity

Jesus traditions used Daniel’s “son of man” and “desolating sacrilege”; Revelation rebuilt Daniel’s beasts and heavenly court. Christians read the stone, humanlike heavenly figure and seventy weeks as predictions of Christ. Daniel and the three youths became paradigms of martyrdom without death and therefore common funerary images.

Porphyry of Tyre, in the late third century CE, argued that Daniel was written during Antiochus IV’s reign and described past events as prophecy. Jerome’s early-fifth-century commentary preserves and attacks Porphyry’s argument. Much of Porphyry’s work is otherwise lost. Modern Maccabean dating resembles this ancient diagnosis but rests on a broader linguistic, textual and historical record.

### Rabbinic Judaism

Rabbinic literature praises Daniel but excludes him from the formal succession of prophets. It expands his genealogy, eunuch status, relation to Hatach, prayers and the fate of the three youths. Louis Ginzberg’s *Legends of the Jews* is a twentieth-century synthesis of these older traditions, useful as an index but not itself an ancient witness.

### Late antiquity and the Middle Ages

Daniel’s four empires became a universal-history template. Jews, Byzantine Christians, Syriac Christians and later Muslims reassigned the fourth kingdom according to political circumstances. Rome could be the evil final empire, the providential restraint on Antichrist, Byzantium, an Islamic power or a contemporary enemy. The [study of Daniel’s four-kingdom reception](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0020964316688051) emphasizes that the scheme survived precisely because interpreters could rewrite it.

The Latin *Ludus Danielis* dramatized Belshazzar and Daniel in cathedral culture. The Beauvais version was composed in the late twelfth or early thirteenth century and survives in a thirteenth-century manuscript. It turns Daniel into a Christian prophet whose story culminates in Christ’s advent. [Modern scholarship on the play](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1515/jbr-2025-0006/html) dates and contextualizes this adaptation.

Islam did not canonize Daniel’s book in the Qurʾan, but historians and *qiṣaṣ* authors absorbed biblical, rabbinic and Christian material. Al-Thaʿlabī and al-Kisāʾī treat Dāniyāl within prophetic history. The tales include dream interpretation, lions, Nebuchadnezzar, the recovery of an incorrupt corpse and, in some forms, revival of the dead by Daniel’s prayer. These are *isrāʾīliyyāt* traditions, not Qurʾanic narratives.

### Reformation to nineteenth century

Confessional controversy repeatedly reassigned Daniel’s fourth beast or little horn to Rome, the papacy, Islam, Protestant states or revolutionary powers. Critical scholars developed the Maccabean date, while confessional defenders argued for sixth-century prediction.

William Miller treated the 2,300 evenings-mornings of Daniel 8:14 as 2,300 years and predicted Christ’s return in 1843–1844. The failure culminating on 22 October 1844 became the “Great Disappointment.” Seventh-day Adventism reinterpreted the event as the beginning of a heavenly investigative judgment. The movement’s self-understanding remains deeply Danielic, as surveyed by the [Oxford Handbook of Bible reception](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/34393/chapter-abstract/291661078).

### Twentieth and twenty-first centuries

Historical criticism generally retained a layered, Maccabean final composition, while renewed study of Qumran, Old Greek and Aramaic literature complicated simple single-author models. Conservative interpreters continued to defend sixth-century authorship.

Dispensationalist prophecy charts combine Daniel’s statue, beasts and seventieth week with Revelation, a future Antichrist and a tribulation. Jehovah’s Witnesses and Adventists maintain distinctive historicist schemes. These systems are genuine modern religious receptions; their identifications of present-day nations are not recoverable meanings of the second-century text.

## 7. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Literature, drama, music and visual art

- **Rembrandt, *Belshazzar’s Feast*, c. 1635:** visualizes the panic at the supernatural writing. Its Hebrew/Aramaic inscription draws on Jewish consultation but is arranged unconventionally. [National Gallery record](https://www.nationalgallery.org.uk/paintings/rembrandt-belshazzar-s-feast).
- **Peter Paul Rubens, *Daniel in the Lions’ Den*, c. 1614–1616:** helped fix the modern image of an athletic, nearly naked Daniel surrounded by realistically studied lions.
- **Handel, *Belshazzar*, 1745:** oratorio with a libretto by Charles Jennens, Christianizing the fall of Babylon and expanding the biblical roles.
- **William Walton, *Belshazzar’s Feast*, 1931:** monumental choral setting joining Daniel’s feast with psalmic Babylon imagery; Daniel himself is not its dramatic center.
- **The *Ludus Danielis* revival:** twentieth-century early-music performances turned a manuscript drama into a repertory work sometimes called an ancestor of opera—an evaluative modern label, not a medieval genre description.
- **Film, television and children’s media:** biblical epics, animated Bible series, *VeggieTales* and evangelical videos routinely merge Daniel 6 with details from Bel and the Dragon, give Daniel dialogue absent from scripture, or place him in the furnace scene. Those additions are **[Modern dramatization]**, not ancient variants.
- **Games and comics:** Daniel’s beasts and statue frequently become coded monsters or end-times maps. Direct representations of Daniel remain less common than adaptations of his iconography.

### Political and nationalist use

Daniel’s schemes are political because they portray empire as temporary and judged. Documented uses include:

- Jewish resistance readings under Seleucid rule.
- Christian identification of pagan Rome, Christian Rome, Byzantium, the papacy, Islam or modern superpowers as the fourth kingdom.
- Byzantine and Syriac constructions of a Last Roman Emperor.
- Protestant anti-papal readings.
- British-Israelite and American exceptionalist prophecy charts.
- Christian Zionist systems that assign modern Israel a fixed place in Daniel’s seventieth week.
- Jehovah’s Witness interpretation of the iron-and-clay feet as the divided Anglo-American and contemporary political order; its current presentation appears on the organization’s [Daniel 2 prophecy page](https://www.jw.org/en/library/series/more-topics/political-turmoil-fulfills-bible-prophecy/).

No single modern nation is named in Daniel. Claims that Daniel explicitly predicted the United States, Russia, the European Union, a modern caliphate, NATO or a named contemporary leader are **[Modern inventions or confessional applications]**.

Extremist movements sometimes use Daniel-Revelation imagery to characterize opponents as demonic beasts or to legitimate apocalyptic violence. Such use should be attributed to the specific movement; it is not licensed by the mere presence of beasts in Daniel. Daniel’s protagonists themselves usually resist through prayer, interpretation and noncompliance, not insurgent warfare.

### Psychological and comparative readings

- **Otto Rank:** hero-birth theory has limited application because Daniel has no birth story.
- **Lord Raglan:** a royal-hero pattern likewise fits poorly; treating Daniel as a standard dying-and-rising hero requires omitting most of the dossier.
- **Joseph Campbell:** Daniel can be made to fit a “hero’s journey” only selectively. The tales are repeated court ordeals rather than one departure-initiation-return biography.
- **Georges Dumézil:** no widely accepted trifunctional interpretation governs Daniel scholarship.
- **Structuralist readings:** profitably compare paired tales and the Aramaic chiastic structure, but structure does not prove common authorship.
- **Oral-formulaic approaches:** court-tale motifs may have circulated orally, yet surviving evidence is written and multilingual; a specific oral archetype cannot be reconstructed securely.

The most defensible comparative models are the wise courtier, vindicated innocent, dream interpreter and apocalyptic revealer.

### Modern inventions

The following often circulate as ancient fact but lack early support:

- Daniel was a named Babylonian governor independently recorded in cuneiform.
- Archaeologists have authenticated Daniel’s personal seal.
- A particular claimed tomb has been scientifically identified as Daniel’s.
- Daniel personally founded a specific surviving synagogue or mosque.
- The canonical text says he was castrated; it does not—this is inference from Isaiah and court status.
- Daniel was present in the fiery furnace; the chapter omits him.
- Daniel 7 explicitly names the Antichrist, Rome, the papacy, Islam or a modern state.
- Bel and the Dragon identifies the dragon as Tiamat.
- The Qurʾan names Daniel.
- Recent radiocarbon results prove sixth-century authorship. They do not.

## 8. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Historicity

No Babylonian, Median or Persian administrative document securely names the biblical Daniel. This absence cannot prove that no Jewish courtier named Daniel existed, but it prevents biographical reconstruction.

Authentic features—Belshazzar’s name, eastern court terminology, Persian words and knowledge that royal courts employed dream specialists—show that traditions could preserve historical coloring. Against literal historicity stand:

- The problematic siege date in Daniel 1.
- Belshazzar’s representation as Nebuchadnezzar’s son and king.
- Darius the Mede, unattested between Belshazzar and Cyrus.
- The separate Median empire inserted between Babylon and Persia.
- The extraordinary precision followed by error in Daniel 11.
- The absence of Daniel from contemporary records.
- The close correspondence between the visions and Antiochus IV’s persecution.

“Daniel was entirely invented in 164 BCE” goes beyond the evidence; older tales probably existed. “Daniel’s book is a contemporary sixth-century memoir” likewise exceeds the evidence. The strongest reconstruction is a composite legendary dossier reaching final form in the Maccabean crisis.

### Date and language

Daniel’s Aramaic contains old and continuing Imperial-Aramaic features; language of this kind remained usable after the Persian period. Persian loanwords indicate composition or transmission after Persian administration became influential. Greek terms in Daniel 3 are chiefly musical-instrument names and do not by themselves require a very late date. Hebrew resembles other late biblical Hebrew but is not mechanically datable.

The linguistic evidence permits older components and Hellenistic editing; it is less decisive than Daniel 11’s historical horizon.

### Textual plurality

Old Greek Daniel differs enough from the Masoretic tradition to imply a complex textual history, particularly in chapters 4–6. Theodotion later replaced the Old Greek in mainstream Christianity. Qumran fragments broadly support a text close to the Masoretic form while also documenting spelling and textual variants. The field has therefore moved away from treating “the original Daniel” as a single easily recoverable document. The [open-access reconstruction of Aramaic Daniel](https://brill.com/display/book/9789004521308/9789004521308_webready_content_text.pdf) foregrounds this textual plurality.

### Four kingdoms

The principal schemes are:

| Interpretation | Kingdoms | Historical setting |
|---|---|---|
| Maccabean-critical | Babylon, Media, Persia, Greece | Antiochus IV is the little horn |
| Jewish/Christian Roman | Babylon, Medo-Persia, Greece, Rome | Rome becomes the fourth empire |
| Byzantine/medieval extensions | Same basic sequence, with Rome continuing through Byzantium or successor states | Supports imperial continuity |
| Historicist Protestant | Babylon, Medo-Persia, Greece, Rome/papal phase | Extends prophecy through church history |
| Dispensational futurist | Babylon, Medo-Persia, Greece, Rome, revived final phase | Final week and Antichrist remain future |

Daniel 8 explicitly joins Media and Persia in one ram, whereas Daniel 7’s fourfold sequence is less explicit. This tension is one reason the debate persists.

### Seventy weeks

Jewish readings have variously connected the “anointed ones” with Cyrus, Joshua the high priest, Onias III or other priestly/ruling figures. Christians connected the passage with Jesus. Modern critical scholarship usually interprets it as a schematic rewriting of Jeremiah ending in the Antiochene crisis. Attempts to produce exact modern dates depend on selecting a starting decree, calendar length, treatment of overlapping periods and sometimes an unstated chronological gap.

### Hero-cult and tomb

Older travel literature often reported a “Tomb of Daniel” as though the designation authenticated the burial. Contemporary archaeology distinguishes:

1. an actual building and local devotional practice;
2. documentary evidence for the attribution;
3. proof of the occupant’s identity.

For Daniel, the first two can be established at several sites; the third cannot.

### Archaeology and “minimalism”

The maximalist/minimalist debate about biblical history is only indirectly relevant. Israel Finkelstein and William Dever chiefly debate Iron Age settlement and monarchy, not Daniel’s personal historicity. Archaeology clarifies Babylonian destruction, Persian administration, Hellenistic Judea and Seleucid conflict, but it has not produced Daniel’s dossier. Invoking either scholar as if one “proved” or “disproved Daniel” is misleading.

### Discoveries that changed the field

- Cuneiform recovery of Belshazzar confirmed that the name was not a late fantasy, while simultaneously revealing that he was Nabonidus’s son.
- Ugaritic tablets disclosed Danel of the Aqhat epic, transforming interpretation of Ezekiel’s Danʾel.
- Qumran yielded Daniel manuscripts, Pseudo-Daniel and the Prayer of Nabonidus.
- Papyrus 967 improved access to the Old Greek and revealed its alternative order.
- Digital imaging and radiocarbon testing refined manuscript analysis but did not settle composition by themselves.

### Discredited or overstated theories

- A single Greek word or Aramaic form can date the whole book precisely.
- Darius the Mede has been conclusively identified with Gobryas, Cyaxares II or another known ruler. Each proposal remains conjectural.
- The four kingdoms can only be Rome-centered because all ancient Jews agreed on that reading. Second Temple evidence is more diverse.
- The book’s canonical status proves an early date. Communities can accept recent authoritative works rapidly.
- Qumran’s copies prove a sixth-century autograph. Manuscripts prove circulation, not the date claimed inside the narrative.
- Porphyry invented the Maccabean theory from anti-Christian prejudice. His polemical setting matters, but the theory addresses genuine textual evidence independently assessed in modern scholarship.

## 10. Sources and assessment

The documentary center of the dossier is the Hebrew-Aramaic Book of Daniel, read against its Old Greek and Theodotionic forms. The [Sefaria Daniel text](https://www.sefaria.org/Daniel.1?lang=en) provides the Masoretic-language tradition and modern Jewish translation. The Greek additions require separate comparison because neither “the Septuagint” nor “Daniel 13–14” represents a single original edition.

For modern critical orientation, John J. Collins’s *Daniel* in the Hermeneia series remains foundational; the Anchor Bible Dictionary articles on Daniel and apocalypse, the *Dictionary of Deities and Demons in the Bible*, Carol Newsom’s Old Testament Library commentary, and the Göttingen editions of Joseph Ziegler and Olivier Munnich are central reference works. Robert Alter’s translation is valuable for literary form but is not a full textual-critical commentary. James Kugel’s work illuminates ancient interpretive expansion; Ginzberg compiles rabbinic legend but must always be traced back to its cited source.

The requested pseudepigrapha do not produce a hidden cycle of direct Daniel interactions. Their importance is primarily contextual: they document the apocalyptic, angelological and rewritten-biblical environment in which Daniel was transmitted. Likewise, neither archaeology nor cuneiform currently supplies a verified Daniel biography. Their contribution is to test the book’s political geography, royal sequence and cultural vocabulary.

The resulting evidentiary picture is asymmetric but clear: Daniel is abundantly documented as a literary and religious figure from the Hellenistic age onward; earlier proverbial Danel material may have contributed his name or reputation; no external evidence securely establishes the canonical courtier as a sixth-century individual. His historical reality is the exceptionally consequential record of stories, manuscripts, visions, images, tomb claims and political calendars made in his name.
