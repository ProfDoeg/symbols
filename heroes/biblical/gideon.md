# Gideon: Research Dossier

**Traditional class:** mortal Israelite deliverer and *šōpēṭ* (“judge,” here a charismatic military leader rather than principally a magistrate); later Jewish and Christian traditions also classify him as prophet, exemplary leader, saint, and “hero of faith.” He is never a demigod. No securely attested ancient cult of Gideon is known.

**Earliest secure attestation:** the earliest surviving physical witness naming or narrating Gideon is **4QJudgesᵃ (4Q49)**, a Herodian-period Hebrew manuscript, commonly dated to the late first century BCE, preserving parts of Judges 6:2–6 and 6:11–13. The Gideon tradition itself is substantially older: Judges belongs to the Deuteronomistic historical corpus, whose major formation is usually placed in the late monarchic and exilic periods, with later editing; scholars differ sharply over whether parts of Judges 6–8 preserve an earlier northern Israelite heroic tale. The eleventh–tenth-century BCE inscription **yrbʿl** (“Jerubbaal”) from Khirbet al-Ra‘i is earlier than every biblical manuscript, but it names an otherwise unknown person and cannot securely be identified with Gideon.

## Evidence labels

- **[Early textual tradition]** Material in the Hebrew Bible whose literary formation predates the surviving manuscripts, although its exact compositional date is disputed.
- **[Secure manuscript evidence]** A physically surviving, datable textual witness.
- **[Later canonical elaboration]** A later biblical or canonically received reuse.
- **[Regional/textual variant]** A reading belonging to a particular textual tradition.
- **[Second Temple elaboration]** Jewish interpretation or rewriting from approximately the last centuries BCE through the first century CE.
- **[Rabbinic interpretation]** Talmudic or midrashic interpretation, dated by the compilation containing it.
- **[Patristic/medieval accretion]** Christian typology, commentary, drama, or iconography not asserted by Judges itself.
- **[Islamic reception]** Qur’anic or later Islamic reuse; Gideon himself is not named in the Qur’an.
- **[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** A critical hypothesis, not an ancient report.
- **[Modern reception/invention]** A modern reuse, identification, institution, or claim.

## 1. Identification, Names and Epithets

Gideon is the central figure of Judges 6–8 and the father whose legacy structures the Abimelech story in Judges 9. In the received narrative he is an Abiezrite of the tribe of Manasseh, son of Joash, living at Ophrah. Midianite and allied raiders have reduced Israel to hiding and hunger. A messenger of YHWH calls Gideon while he is beating wheat in a winepress; he protests both YHWH’s apparent abandonment of Israel and his own insignificance. After signs, the destruction of his father’s Baal altar, and the fleece test, he reduces an army to three hundred men, surprises the Midianite camp with trumpets, jars, torches, and shouting, and pursues its kings Zebah and Zalmunna. The story then turns darker: Gideon tortures or kills men of Succoth, kills Penuel’s men, executes the captured kings in vengeance for his brothers, acquires royal wealth and a large household, and makes an ephod that becomes a religious “snare.” He verbally refuses hereditary rule, but his son Abimelech subsequently seizes power after murdering nearly all Gideon’s sons.

### Names

- **גִּדְעוֹן — Gidʿōn / Gideon.**  
  The traditional vocalization is preserved in the Masoretic Text. The name has often been connected with the Hebrew root **גדע, gdʿ**, “cut down, hew, lop off,” producing the conventional “hewer” or “one who cuts down.” That interpretation fits the altar-destruction episode but may be folk-etymological. Simon DeVries proposed “driver”; other analyses treat the name as a diminutive or personal formation whose exact sense is unrecoverable. **[Modern scholarly reconstruction; disputed]**

- **יְרֻבַּעַל — Yərubbaʿal / Jerubbaal.**  
  Judges 6:32 explains it as “Let Baal contend with him,” because Joash challenges Baal to defend his own altar. Grammatically, however, scholars have proposed such senses as “May Baal contend,” “Baal contends,” or—if the verbal element reflects *rbb*—“May Baal be great.” The form contains the divine element **Baal** and is therefore probably older than the hostile explanation attached to it. Judges 7:1 explicitly equates “Jerubbaal, that is Gideon”; Judges 8:29, 35 and most of Judges 9 favor Jerubbaal when discussing his dynasty. **[Early textual tradition; biblical folk etymology versus modern linguistic reconstruction]**

- **יְרֻבֶּשֶׁת — Yərubbešet / Jerubbesheth.**  
  Second Samuel 11:21 uses this form in recalling Abimelech’s death. *Bōšet*, “shame,” replaces *Baal*, as in Ish-bosheth and Mephibosheth, reflecting a scribal or traditional strategy that stigmatized Baal-bearing names. It is not a distinct hero. **[Regional/textual or editorial variant]**

- **Greek:** Γεδεών, *Gedeōn*, and forms of Ἰεροβάαλ, *Ierobaal*. The Septuagint’s Judges survives in two notably different Greek recensions, represented especially by Codex Vaticanus and Codex Alexandrinus. **[Ancient translation tradition, principally last centuries BCE; surviving major codices fourth–fifth centuries CE]**

- **Latin:** *Gedeon*, *Jerobaal/Hierobbaal*. Jerome’s Vulgate renders the name accordingly; Judges 6:32 explains *Jerobaal* from Joash’s challenge to Baal. **[Late antique Christian translation, c. 390–405 CE]**

- **Arabic:** جدعون, *Jidʿūn*, in Arabic biblical and Christian usage. Gideon is not named in the Qur’an. The Qur’anic figure Ṭālūt is normally Saul, not Gideon, although Qur’an 2:249 transfers or independently reproduces a drinking-test motif strongly resembling Judges 7. **[Islamic reception; motif transfer, not secure identification]**

### Epithets and classifications

- **“Mighty warrior” / “valiant warrior”** (*gibbôr heḥāyil*), the messenger’s address in Judges 6:12. In context it may be proleptic, ironic, encouraging, or socially descriptive. **[Early textual tradition]**
- **“The youngest/least”** in his father’s house, from the “weakest” clan in Manasseh, Gideon’s own self-description in Judges 6:15. Translations vary between “youngest” and “least.” **[Early textual tradition]**
- **Son of Joash the Abiezrite**, Judges 6:11; 7:14; 8:13, 29, 32. **[Early textual tradition]**
- **Judge of Israel.** Judges 8:28 says the land rested forty years “in the days of Gideon,” although the standard formula “he judged Israel” is less explicit than for some judges. Josephus makes him a holder of government; later tradition routinely numbers him among the judges. **[Canonical systematization and later reception]**
- **Prophet.** Judges does not call Gideon a *nābîʾ*; an anonymous prophet appears in Judges 6:8. Some Jewish and Christian catalogues nevertheless call Gideon a prophet. **[Later classification]**
- **Saint/holy Gideon.** Christian calendars and patristic writers treat him as an Old Testament righteous man; Ambrose calls him “holy Gideon.” **[Patristic and liturgical reception]**
- **Hero of faith.** Hebrews 11:32 includes Gideon among figures who “through faith conquered kingdoms.” **[Later canonical elaboration, first century CE]**

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Earliest physical evidence

**4QJudgesᵃ (4Q49).** This fragmentary Hebrew scroll preserves Judges 6:2–6 and 11–13 and is the oldest surviving manuscript of Judges. Its surviving text includes the Midianite oppression and the beginning of Gideon’s call. It does **not** preserve the fleece, army selection, battle, kings’ execution, ephod, death, or Abimelech material. The fragment is particularly important because verses 6:7–10—the anonymous prophet’s Deuteronomistic rebuke—appear absent between 6:6 and 6:11. If the reconstruction of the column is correct, this supports the conclusion that the prophetic speech was absent from this textual form and may be a secondary insertion. Scholars caution that fragmentary layout must be reconstructed and that textual difference does not by itself yield a complete compositional history. **[Secure manuscript evidence; text-critical inference]**

Other Qumran Judges manuscripts are sparse. Consequently, most detailed comparison of Gideon’s story must use the medieval Masoretic manuscripts and ancient translations.

### Main primary corpus

1. **Judges 6:1–8:35; 9:1–57 — Biblical Hebrew.**  
   The primary and only extended ancient narrative. The story combines vocation, altar legend, signs, tribal muster, divinely mandated troop reduction, night victory, intertribal negotiation, blood vengeance, anti-monarchic speech, cultic failure, and dynastic catastrophe. Literary formation is disputed: older source critics divided it among J, E, and Deuteronomistic layers; more recent work uses redaction criticism, tradition history, and literary analysis without consensus on recoverable sources. A late monarchic or exilic Deuteronomistic framework is widely recognized, while proposed older local tales remain hypotheses. **[Early textual tradition]**

2. **Judges 9 — Biblical Hebrew.**  
   Gideon is dead but repeatedly named as Jerubbaal; Abimelech appeals to maternal kin at Shechem, murders seventy brothers “on one stone,” and makes himself king. Jotham’s fable and accusation make Gideon’s deliverance the moral debt that Shechem has betrayed. Judges 9 may once have had a literary history partly independent of Judges 6–8; its linkage to Gideon’s family is nevertheless integral to every extant complete Hebrew form. **[Early textual tradition; compositional relation disputed]**

3. **1 Samuel 12:11 — Biblical Hebrew.**  
   Samuel’s historical retrospect says YHWH sent “Jerubbaal” among Israel’s deliverers. Some witnesses or translations differ in the list, and the verse’s textual history is difficult. Its use of Jerubbaal rather than Gideon shows that the Baal-name circulated as the hero’s recognized identity. **[Early textual tradition; textual variants]**

4. **2 Samuel 11:21 — Biblical Hebrew.**  
   Joab recalls how a woman killed “Abimelech son of Jerubbesheth” at Thebez. This is a direct memory of Judges 9:50–54 and preserves the euphemistic *-bosheth* form. **[Early textual tradition; editorial name substitution]**

5. **Isaiah 9:3–4; 10:26 — Biblical Hebrew.**  
   “The day of Midian” and YHWH’s blow against Midian “at the rock of Oreb” presuppose the Gideon victory tradition but do not name Gideon. These passages show that the deliverance from Midian functioned as a reusable national image of miraculous liberation. Their Isaianic contexts are usually assigned to the monarchic prophetic tradition, though the precise dating and editing of individual verses remain debated. **[Early allusive reception]**

6. **Psalm 83:10–12 — Biblical Hebrew.**  
   The psalm asks that enemies be made like Midian, Sisera, and Jabin and names Oreb, Zeeb, Zebah, and Zalmunna. Gideon is unstated, but the enemies’ names presuppose Judges 7–8 or a shared tradition. The psalm’s date is disputed and should not be used as independent proof of an Iron Age Gideon. **[Canonical allusion; date uncertain]**

7. **Septuagint Judges — Koine Greek.**  
   Produced during the Hellenistic period, but surviving substantially in later codices. Judges has unusually divergent Greek forms. The Vaticanus-related and Alexandrinus-related texts differ in wording, names, and sequence; neither should simply be equated with a single pristine “Septuagint.” In Judges 8, for example, Greek manuscripts vary over the Ephraim confrontation’s placement or formulation, illustrating continuing textual fluidity. **[Ancient translation and regional textual variants]**

8. **Vulgate Judges 6–9 — Latin, c. 390–405 CE.**  
   Jerome’s translation transmits *Gedeon* and *Jerobaal*. At Judges 8:16 the Vulgate intensifies the obscure Hebrew wording into Gideon’s “crushing” and “breaking” the men of Succoth with thorns and briers; at 8:17 it states that he destroyed Penuel’s tower and killed the city’s inhabitants. **[Late antique translation]**

9. **Pseudo-Philo, *Liber Antiquitatum Biblicarum* 35–36 — probably Hebrew original, before or around 70 CE; preserved in Latin.**  
   Pseudo-Philo calls Gideon very mighty among his brothers, expands divine and human speeches, and moralizes his decline. It explicitly charges him with making and worshipping idols, while explaining why immediate public rebuke would be misunderstood as Baal’s vengeance for Gideon’s earlier destruction of Baal’s sanctuary. **[Second Temple Jewish rewriting]**

10. **Josephus, *Jewish Antiquities* 5.211–232 — Greek, c. 93/94 CE.**  
    Josephus retells the story for a Greco-Roman audience. He suppresses or softens features damaging to Gideon, praises his virtue and moderation toward Ephraim, and makes his assumption of government more explicit. The result is a political-moral portrait, not an independent historical record. **[First-century Jewish rewriting]**

11. **Hebrews 11:32–34 — Greek, later first century CE.**  
    Gideon appears with Barak, Samson, Jephthah, David, Samuel, and the prophets as examples of faith, military strength, and victory. The passage omits the ephod and dynastic catastrophe. **[Later canonical elaboration]**

12. **Babylonian Talmud, Rosh Hashanah 25b — compiled approximately fifth–sixth centuries CE.**  
    Gideon/Jerubbaal, Samson/Bedan, and Jephthah are called three “light” or less distinguished leaders but are treated as equivalent in authority to Moses, Aaron, and Samuel within their own generations. The point is institutional obedience, not biography. **[Rabbinic interpretation]**

13. **Midrashic literature.**  
    Gideon is less extensively rewritten than Abraham, Moses, or David. Later midrash associates his merit with advocacy for Israel and sometimes explains his election through his defense of the people. Midrash Tanhuma traditions use Abimelech son of Jerubbaal as an example of authority fleeing one who pursues it. Genesis Rabbah is not a continuous Gideon source; Pirqe de-Rabbi Eliezer concentrates on Genesis through parts of the wilderness narrative and supplies no independent Gideon cycle. **[Rabbinic/medieval interpretation; absence is significant]**

14. **Louis Ginzberg, *The Legends of the Jews*, vol. IV, 1913, with notes in vol. VI.**  
    Ginzberg synthesizes scattered aggadic material into continuous narrative. It is a modern scholarly anthology, indispensable for locating reception but not itself an ancient source; its seamless prose must be checked against its notes and underlying rabbinic texts. **[Modern learned systematization]**

15. **Qur’an 2:246–251 — Arabic, early seventh century CE.**  
    Gideon is not named. Ṭālūt, normally identified as Saul, tests his army at a river; most drink, while a small group abstains or takes only a handful. The narrative then proceeds to David and Goliath. The drinking test resembles Judges 7 strongly, but its placement with Saul means it is not evidence that Islam identified Gideon and Ṭālūt as one person. **[Islamic motif transfer]**

16. **Qiṣaṣ al-anbiyāʾ and commentaries, including al-Thaʿlabī (d. 1035) and works attributed to al-Kisāʾī.**  
    These elaborate Ṭālūt, David, and Israelite prophetic history. Where they transmit the river test, they follow the Qur’anic Saul/Talut cycle. Claims that they preserve a separate named Gideon cycle require manuscript-specific demonstration; Gideon is not a Qur’anic prophet. **[Medieval Islamic elaboration; non-identification]**

17. **Patristic and medieval Christian exegesis.**  
    Ambrose’s *On Widows* interprets the fleece’s changing wetness and dryness as Israel receiving revelation and then becoming dry while grace passes to the Gentile Church. Later Latin and Syriac traditions make the dew-filled fleece a figure of Mary conceiving Christ without loss of virginity. The jars become human bodies, the torches divine grace, and the shout confession. None of these meanings appears in Judges. **[Patristic/medieval typology]**

18. **Medieval biblical drama and early-modern plays.**  
    Gideon appeared in biblical cycles and confessional drama, especially through the fleece’s Marian typology and the hero’s caution in testing revelation. Hans Sachs’s *Gedeon* (1540) and related Reformation-era treatments recast him as a model of obedient but discerning leadership. **[Medieval/early-modern accretion]**

### Corpora named in the brief but yielding no Gideon cycle

There is no Gideon narrative in **1 Enoch, Jubilees, the Greek/Latin/Slavonic Life of Adam and Eve, or the Testaments of the Twelve Patriarchs**. Their silence should not be converted into evidence about Gideon’s historicity; it shows only that he was not important to their surviving literary programs. Genesis Rabbah and Pirqe de-Rabbi Eliezer likewise do not supply an independent continuous Gideon tradition. **[Documented absence]**

## 3. The Story

### 3.1 Oppression and the least man in Manasseh

Judges 6:1–6 says Israel does evil and is delivered to Midian for seven years. Midianites, Amalekites, and “people of the east” repeatedly destroy crops and livestock, leaving Israel impoverished. Israelites use mountain refuges, caves, or strongholds—the Hebrew terms are difficult.

An anonymous prophet in 6:7–10 indicts Israel for disobeying YHWH after the exodus. The apparent absence of this unit in 4QJudgesᵃ is the most important early textual variation in the call narrative. **[Possible later Deuteronomistic insertion]**

Gideon is beating wheat **in a winepress**, apparently to conceal the small harvest from Midian. The messenger of YHWH sits under Joash’s tree at Ophrah and calls him “mighty warrior.” Gideon answers that if YHWH is with Israel, the wonders told by “our fathers”—especially the exodus—are conspicuously absent. YHWH or the messenger then commissions him.

The alternation between “messenger of YHWH” and YHWH speaking directly has been interpreted as evidence of combined sources, conventional biblical angelology, or deliberate narrative identification of the messenger with the sender. **[Modern scholarly debate]**

Gideon calls his clan the poorest/weakest in Manasseh and himself the least/youngest in his father’s house. His marginality is therefore part of the canonical characterization, though Joash’s ownership of an altar, sacred pole, livestock, servants, and a substantial household complicates any picture of utter poverty.

### 3.2 The offering and altar

Gideon asks for a sign, prepares a young goat and unleavened cakes from an ephah of flour, and presents them. The messenger touches the food with a staff; fire consumes it, and the messenger disappears. Gideon fears death from seeing the messenger face to face, but YHWH says “Peace to you.” Gideon builds an altar called **YHWH-shalom**.

Judges locates this altar “to this day” at Ophrah of the Abiezrites. This is a literary aetiology for a local sanctuary. Archaeology has not securely located Gideon’s Ophrah or identified its altar. **[Early sanctuary tradition; archaeological non-corroboration]**

### 3.3 Baal’s altar, Asherah, and Jerubbaal

That night YHWH commands Gideon to tear down his father’s altar to Baal and cut down the **Asherah** beside it, then build a proper altar to YHWH and offer a bull using the Asherah wood. The Hebrew description of the bull or bulls is textually awkward. Gideon acts with ten servants at night because he fears his family and townsmen.

The townsmen demand Gideon’s death. Joash responds that Baal should plead for himself; hence Gideon is called Jerubbaal. The episode simultaneously:

- subordinates Baal to YHWH;
- explains the hero’s theophoric name polemically;
- legitimates a YHWH altar at a site associated with older cult;
- depicts Joash, owner or custodian of the Baal installation, protecting his son.

Whether an older hero actually bore a favorable Baal-name and the altar-destruction story was composed to neutralize it is a plausible but unprovable reconstruction. **[Modern scholarly reconstruction]**

### 3.4 Muster and fleece

Midian and its allies camp in the Jezreel Valley. “The spirit of YHWH clothed Gideon,” who blows a trumpet and summons Abiezer, Manasseh, Asher, Zebulun, and Naphtali.

Gideon then asks God for two signs:

1. dew on the wool fleece while the ground remains dry;
2. fleece dry while dew covers the ground.

Both occur. In Judges this is a test of the promise “you will save Israel by my hand,” not a general biblical method for discovering private divine guidance. Modern evangelical language about “putting out a fleece” universalizes a unique narrative sign and is therefore **modern devotional reception**, not an ancient rule.

### 3.5 The three hundred

At the spring of Harod, Gideon begins with 32,000. YHWH orders him to dismiss the fearful; 22,000 leave. At the water, the remainder are divided by how they drink. The Masoretic syntax is famously confusing: three hundred lap with hand to mouth, while the rest kneel. Exactly what bodily posture identifies the chosen group is less clear than popular illustrations suggest.

The stated theological purpose is explicit: Israel must not boast, “My own hand saved me.” The selection is therefore not presented as a test of superior hygiene, alertness, military professionalism, or animal-like ferocity. Those familiar explanations are **later rationalizations** without support in Judges.

### 3.6 Dream, trumpets, jars, and torches

Gideon still fears. God tells him to descend with his servant **Purah** to overhear the camp. A Midianite dreams that a barley loaf overturns a tent; another interprets it as “the sword of Gideon son of Joash,” to whom God has given Midian.

Gideon divides the three hundred into three companies, giving each a trumpet and a jar with a torch concealed inside. At his signal they break the jars, expose the torches, sound the trumpets, and shout, in the Masoretic form, **“A sword for YHWH and for Gideon!”** The camp panics; YHWH sets each enemy’s sword against his fellow.

This is a divinely induced rout, not a technically realistic description of three hundred swordsmen defeating 135,000 in conventional battle. Later military readings emphasize surprise, noise, darkness, intelligence, and psychological warfare. Those factors are narratively present, but calculating the episode as recoverable tactical history exceeds the evidence.

### 3.7 Oreb and Zeeb; confrontation with Ephraim

Israelites from Naphtali, Asher, and Manasseh pursue the enemy. Ephraim seizes the Jordan crossings and kills the princes **Oreb** and **Zeeb**, displaying their heads to Gideon.

Ephraim angrily challenges Gideon for not summoning it earlier. Gideon defuses the dispute through a viticultural proverb: “Is not the gleaning of Ephraim better than the vintage of Abiezer?” Josephus amplifies this into proof of Gideon’s exemplary moderation. **[First-century moralizing elaboration]**

### 3.8 Succoth, Penuel, Zebah, and Zalmunna

Gideon and the three hundred cross the Jordan, exhausted but pursuing **Zebah and Zalmunna**, kings of Midian. Succoth and Penuel refuse bread because the kings have not yet been captured; their refusal may reflect fear of Midianite retaliation rather than simple cowardice.

After defeating the remaining force, Gideon captures the kings. He returns to Succoth, obtains a written list of seventy-seven officials from a young man, and “teaches” or punishes the elders with desert thorns and briers. The Hebrew verb is textually uncertain; translations range from “taught a lesson” to “threshed” or “tore.” At Penuel he destroys the tower and kills the city’s men. The narrative reports these acts without an explicit divine command.

Gideon asks Zebah and Zalmunna about men they killed at Tabor. They answer that the men resembled Gideon, “each like a king’s son.” Gideon identifies them as his full brothers and swears he would spare the kings had they spared them. He orders his firstborn **Jether** to execute them, presumably to humiliate them by death at a boy’s hand and initiate Jether into warrior status. Jether is afraid. The kings tell Gideon to do it himself, and he kills them, taking crescent ornaments from their camels.

This vengeance motive—absent from the initial divine commission—has often been treated as evidence of a separate older clan-feud story. The source division remains hypothetical, but the ethical and motivational shift is real in the received narrative.

### 3.9 Refusal of kingship and quasi-royal practice

Israel asks Gideon: “Rule over us, you, your son, and your grandson,” an explicit proposal for hereditary monarchy. Gideon answers: “I will not rule over you, nor shall my son rule over you; YHWH shall rule over you.”

Yet the following details destabilize the refusal:

- Gideon demands a share of spoil;
- he receives 1,700 shekels of gold plus royal ornaments and purple garments;
- he possesses many wives;
- he fathers seventy sons;
- his Shechemite concubine bears **Abimelech**, whose name can mean “my father is king” or be a conventional royal/theophoric personal name;
- his household becomes a dynasty powerful enough for Abimelech to exploit.

The text thus juxtaposes anti-monarchic words with behavior resembling chieftainship or kingship. Whether this is intentional irony, the joining of favorable and hostile sources, or both remains debated.

### 3.10 The ephod and snare

Gideon requests the captured gold earrings—Judges calls the Midianites “Ishmaelites” in this connection—and makes an **ephod** at Ophrah. “All Israel whored after it there,” and it becomes a snare to Gideon and his household.

What the object was is uncertain:

- a priestly garment;
- a cult image;
- a gold-plated wooden image or oracular apparatus;
- a freestanding sanctuary object associated with divination.

The quantity of gold and the verb “set/placed” at Ophrah encourage the cult-object interpretation, but Judges never describes its shape. Calling it simply an “idol” goes beyond the Hebrew wording, although Pseudo-Philo explicitly interprets Gideon’s action as idolatry.

This is an important internal reversal: the destroyer of Baal’s altar establishes an object that diverts Israel at his own center of power.

### 3.11 Death and Abimelech

Midian is subdued, and the land rests forty years “in Gideon’s days.” Gideon dies “at a good old age” and is buried in the tomb of Joash at Ophrah of the Abiezrites. No cause of death is supplied.

Afterward Israel returns to the Baals, making Baal-berith its god, and fails to show loyalty to Gideon’s house. Abimelech then secures silver from the temple of Baal-berith, hires followers, and kills his seventy brothers on one stone. **Jotham** alone escapes. Shechem makes Abimelech king.

Jotham’s fable of the trees condemns the bramble-king and predicts mutual destruction. Abimelech’s rule ends when a woman at Thebez drops an upper millstone on his skull. He orders his armor-bearer to kill him so it will not be said that a woman killed him; 2 Samuel 11:21 shows that the humiliating version survived anyway.

The story does not say Gideon personally appointed Abimelech. It presents Abimelech’s kingship as a violent exploitation of Gideon’s prestige, wealth, concubinage, and unresolved dynastic legacy.

| **David** | Jointly listed among heroes of faith; no narrative meeting | Hebrews 11:32 | Later canonical association |

| **Moses** | Gideon’s call resembles Moses’ call; rabbinic tradition makes Jerubbaal in his generation comparable in authority to Moses | Literary comparison; b. Rosh Hashanah 25b | Rabbinic interpretation; the narrative does not make them meet |
| **Aaron** | Rabbinic authority comparison places lesser judges against Moses/Aaron/Samuel | b. Rosh Hashanah 25b | Rabbinic interpretation |

There is no documented ancient interaction between Gideon and Deborah, Jael, Joshua, Elijah, or other frequently juxtaposed biblical heroes. Modern illustrated Bibles may place them in a common series, but that is curatorial association.

## 5. Cult, Tomb and Ritual

### Ophrah and its altar

Judges reports:

- Joash’s Baal altar and Asherah at Ophrah;
- Gideon’s altar YHWH-shalom;
- Gideon’s ephod installed there;
- Gideon’s burial in Joash’s tomb there.

These statements show **literary memory of a local sanctuary and ancestral tomb**, not archaeological verification. Gideon’s Ophrah has not been securely identified; suggested locations in the Manassite hill country remain conjectural. The Benjaminite Ophrah mentioned elsewhere need not be the same site.

### Hero cult

No inscription, votive deposit, sacrificial calendar, excavated *heroon*, priesthood, festival, oracle, or securely identified relic demonstrates cult paid to Gideon as a heroized dead person. Judges’ “to this day” formula applies to YHWH-shalom, not to pilgrimage at Gideon’s grave.

The ephod may imply an oracular or pilgrimage center during the story’s imagined past, but the text condemns it and does not say Gideon himself was worshipped. Pseudo-Philo turns it into explicit idolatry. **Conclusion: cult of an object associated with Gideon is literarily attested; cult of Gideon is not.**

### Tomb and relics

Judges 8:32 says Gideon was buried in the tomb of Joash at Ophrah. No authenticated tomb, bones, reliquary, or continuous local cult can be connected to him. Modern sites advertised as Gideon’s tomb rest on identification or devotional tradition rather than excavation tied to an inscription. **[Contested-material finding: literary tomb, no secure archaeological tomb]**

### Commemorative naming

*Gideon/Gidʿon* became a Jewish and Christian personal name. Modern Israeli sites and military organizations use the name, but this is national commemoration, not survival of an ancient cult.

## 6. Historical Evolution of the Figure

### I. Possible pre-literary and northern traditions

**[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** Many scholars infer local northern traditions behind the extant story:

- Abiezrite conflict with trans-Jordanian raiders;
- a sanctuary legend at Ophrah;
- a blood-feud tale involving Zebah and Zalmunna;
- an independent Jerubbaal-Abimelech dynastic tradition at Shechem.

Repetition, conflicting motivations, changes of geography, the two divine signs, and the contrast between charismatic deliverer and vengeful chieftain support some literary growth. Exact source boundaries are not agreed.

### II. Monarchic and Deuteronomistic shaping

The editors of Judges framed Gideon within the recurring sequence of apostasy, oppression, cry, deliverance, and relapse. Anti-Baal polemic, the exodus rebuke, reduction of the army so YHWH alone receives credit, and the warning against monarchy or dynastic ambition give the story its received theological architecture.

Some scholars locate major shaping in the late seventh century BCE; others emphasize exilic, Persian-period, or multiple stages. The prophetic speech’s possible absence in 4QJudgesᵃ is evidence of textual growth but does not date every surrounding unit.

### III. Second Temple remembrance

Isaiah and Psalm 83 made Midian’s defeat paradigmatic. Pseudo-Philo judged Gideon more severely for idolatry. Josephus instead polished him into a prudent, virtuous ruler suitable for Greco-Roman historiography. Hebrews selected the victorious Gideon as an exemplar of faith, omitting his compromised ending.

### IV. Rabbinic reinterpretation

The rabbis did not develop a massive Gideon legend cycle. Their most influential move was institutional: even Gideon, Samson, and Jephthah—less distinguished than Moses, Aaron, and Samuel—must be obeyed when legitimately appointed in their generation. Other aggadic traditions praise Gideon for defending Israel before God. The ambivalence of Judges persisted; Ginzberg’s synthesis retains both merit and failure.

### V. Patristic and medieval Christian transformation

Christian exegesis increasingly detached the fleece from military confirmation and treated it typologically:

- fleece wet, earth dry: revelation first given to Israel;
- fleece dry, earth wet: grace transferred to the gentile Church;
- fleece receiving dew without damage: Mary conceiving Christ while remaining virgin.

This interpretation shaped hymns, sermons, cathedral sculpture, manuscript illumination, and the *Biblia pauperum*. The anti-Jewish form of the wet/dry allegory must be stated plainly: several Christian writers made the dry fleece represent Jewish unbelief or divine rejection.

### VI. Reformation and early-modern drama

Protestant treatments often returned to Gideon as a literal leader who verifies a divine command, opposes idolatry, and defeats tyranny. Catholic art continued Marian fleece typology while also depicting the sacrifice, army selection, and victory. Confessional uses selected different portions of the same story.

### VII. Modern historical criticism

Nineteenth- and early twentieth-century source criticism divided Judges 6–8 into documentary layers. Later scholars challenged overly mechanical divisions and emphasized sophisticated final-form structure, irony, characterization, and editorial art. Gideon increasingly appeared not as an uncomplicated saint but as a deliberately deteriorating figure: hesitant but obedient at first, then vengeful, acquisitive, cultically dangerous, and dynastically disastrous.

### VIII. Modern national and evangelical reuse

Zionist and Israeli military culture often emphasizes the three hundred, reconnaissance, surprise night attack, courage, and victory against numerical superiority. Evangelical Christianity frequently emphasizes weakness made strong, signs, and “laying out a fleece.” The Gideons International turned his name into a global emblem of a small band accomplishing disproportionate results.

## 7. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Literature and drama

- **Hans Sachs, *Gedeon* (1540):** Reformation-era biblical drama presenting careful obedience to divine command. **[Early-modern reception]**
- **Paddy Chayefsky, *Gideon* (Broadway, 1961):** a seriocomic drama about an ordinary, reluctant man confronting God; televised in 1966. **[Modern reinvention]**
- Gideon appears widely in children’s Bibles and evangelical fiction, usually with the troubling Succoth, Penuel, vengeance, polygyny, and ephod episodes omitted. **[Modern selective reception]**

### Music

- **John Christopher Smith, *Gideon* (1769):** an oratorio pasticcio assembled largely from music by George Frideric Handel after Handel’s death. Calling it simply “Handel’s *Gideon*” is misleading; Handel did not compose a complete Gideon oratorio under that title. **[Modern learned/commercial attribution requiring qualification]**
- Marian hymns in Eastern and Western Christianity use “Gideon’s fleece” as a title or type of the Theotokos. **[Liturgical reception]**

### Visual art

Documented subjects include:

- the angel’s call and consumed offering;
- destruction of Baal’s altar;
- the fleece;
- selection of the three hundred;
- breaking jars and revealing torches;
- receipt of Midianite ornaments;
- execution of Zebah and Zalmunna.

A surviving Austrian manuscript fragment of approximately **1420–1430** combines Gideon and the fleece with selection of the army. Medieval *Biblia pauperum* compositions pair the fleece with the Annunciation or Nativity. Maarten van Heemskerck’s sixteenth-century *Gideon and the Fleece* represents the Renaissance continuation of the theme. Later prints by Jan Goeree and others make Gideon’s wringing of the fleece the central visual action.

The Princeton Index of Medieval Art catalogs the subject across manuscripts, sculpture, stained glass, and other media, but database entries establish iconographic occurrence, not historicity.

### The Gideons International

The association traces its beginning to traveling businessmen John H. Nicholson and Samuel E. Hill in 1898–1899; W. J. Knights participated in choosing **“The Gideons”** in 1899. The name represented a small group relying on God to accomplish a large task. Hotel Bible placement began in 1908 at the Superior Hotel in Montana. The organization reports distributing more than two billion Bibles and New Testaments.

This is the single most globally visible modern appropriation of Gideon’s name. “Gideon Bible” means a Bible distributed by that association; it is not a special ancient textual version.

### Political and military appropriation

- **Ha-Gidʿonim/Gideonites:** an early Jewish self-defense group associated with Zikhron Ya‘akov in the late Ottoman period used Gideon’s name as a militant biblical precedent. **[Modern nationalist appropriation]**
- **Orde Wingate and the Special Night Squads, 1938:** Wingate’s biblical imagination and night operations in Mandatory Palestine encouraged comparison with Gideon’s surprise attack. These units participated in violent counterinsurgency during the Arab Revolt; invoking Gideon sacralized or romanticized asymmetric colonial warfare. **[Modern political appropriation]**
- **Gideon Force, 1940–1941:** the British-led force operating with Ethiopian resistance against Italian occupation took the name in the same biblical-military milieu. **[Modern military naming]**
- **Golani Brigade’s 13th “Gideon” Battalion:** officially commemorates Gideon as a model commander associated with intelligence, maneuver, and surprise. **[Modern Israeli military reception]**
- **Operation Gideon’s Chariots, 2025:** Israel used Gideon’s name for a major Gaza operation. This is contemporary military branding, not continuation of an ancient rite or evidence about the ancient figure. **[Modern political appropriation]**

No well-documented extremist movement appears to have made Gideon uniquely central in the manner that some movements appropriate Samson or the Maccabees. Nevertheless, Gideon’s three hundred and divinely authorized small-force violence remain readily usable symbols in militant Christian and nationalist rhetoric. Claims of a particular appropriation require organization-specific evidence.

### Modern inventions and recurrent errors

- **“The drinkers were selected because they remained alert.”** Not stated in Judges.
- **“The lappers were chosen because they did not bow to idols.”** Later homiletic invention.
- **“Gideon had exactly 300 special-forces troops.”** Modern military analogy; Judges presents divine selection, not elite training.
- **“The Jerubbaal inscription proves Gideon existed.”** False. It proves that a compatible personal name existed in the relevant broad period.
- **“Gideon founded monarchy in Israel.”** He refuses the stated proposal, although the text exposes quasi-royal conduct and his son becomes king.
- **“The Qur’an calls Gideon Ṭālūt.”** Misleading. Ṭālūt is Saul in the received Islamic tradition; a Gideon-like motif has been transferred to him.
- **“Gideon’s ephod was certainly a priestly vestment.”** Uncertain; a cult image or oracular object is possible.
- **“His tomb has been found.”** No authenticated discovery supports this.
- **“Putting out a fleece is a biblical command for decision-making.”** It is a modern generalization from a unique story.
- **“Handel composed the oratorio *Gideon*.”** It is a posthumous pasticcio arranged by John Christopher Smith.

## 8. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Historicity

No contemporary inscription names “Gideon son of Joash,” no excavated destruction layer can be tied to his campaign, and no Midianite record describes the event. The historical Gideon is therefore unverified.

Three broad positions recur:

1. **Older-memory position:** Judges preserves genuine memories of decentralized highland society, seasonal raiding, clan warfare, and local charismatic leaders in Iron I. William Dever and other historically inclined archaeologists regard aspects of Judges as socially plausible without treating every episode as literal reportage.

2. **Tradition-history position:** an old Abiezrite or Manassite hero tale may underlie later national and Deuteronomistic framing. Israel Finkelstein has proposed that northern heroic traditions were collected or reshaped under the northern monarchy, sometimes particularly in the age of Jeroboam II. Specific dating remains inferential.

3. **Strongly literary/minimalist position:** the extant narrative is too late, composite, and ideologically shaped to reconstruct a person or event. Social plausibility and old place-names do not establish that Gideon existed.

The responsible conclusion is agnostic: an Iron Age leader may stand behind some traditions, but Gideon as narrated is a literary figure built through centuries of transmission.

### The Jerubbaal inscription

A pottery sherd from Khirbet al-Ra‘i bears the Early Alphabetic sequence **yrbʿl**, published by Christopher Rollston, Yosef Garfinkel, Kyle Keimer, Gillan Davis, and Saar Ganor in 2021. Its archaeological context is approximately the twelfth/eleventh century BCE, with the editors favoring the relevant Iron I horizon.

What it establishes:

- the consonantal name Jerubbaal existed early;
- Baal-theophoric names circulated in the southern Levant;
- biblical preservation of such a name is linguistically plausible.

What it does not establish:

- that the bearer was Gideon;
- that Khirbet al-Ra‘i was Ophrah;
- that the Midianite war occurred;
- that Judges was written at that time.

The site lies far from the expected Manassite setting, and the name need not have been unique. Identification with Gideon is publicity, possibility, or faith claim—not secure epigraphy.

### Composite narrative versus literary unity

Evidence cited for composition from several traditions includes:

- Gideon is commissioned to save Israel, but later says his pursuit is vengeance for brothers;
- two or more signs occur in close succession;
- tribal mobilization and the three hundred do not always align smoothly;
- the Oreb/Zeeb episode and Zebah/Zalmunna pursuit appear partially separable;
- the favorable king-refusal is followed by royal behavior and cultic failure;
- Gideon and Jerubbaal cluster differently across the Gideon and Abimelech stories;
- 4QJudgesᵃ may lack Judges 6:7–10.

Final-form interpreters answer that repetition, deterioration, irony, and narrative doubling are meaningful literary designs. These approaches are not mutually exclusive: an edited composite can also possess deliberate final form.

### Gideon and Abimelech

Some scholars consider Judges 9 an older Shechemite kingship tale secondarily connected to Gideon; others see Judges 6–9 as a consciously shaped unit in which Gideon’s ambiguous quasi-monarchy generates Abimelech’s catastrophe. Judges 8:22–35 functions unmistakably as a bridge in the received text.

### Meaning of the ephod

Older confessional interpretation sometimes tried to preserve Gideon’s orthodoxy by making the ephod merely a commemorative vestment later abused by others. Against this, Judges says Gideon made and installed it, all Israel “whored” after it, and it became a snare to him and his house. Pseudo-Philo goes further and calls his conduct idolatrous. The object’s physical form remains unknown, but the narrator’s condemnation does not.

### Gideon’s moral trajectory

Josephus and Hebrews encourage an idealized Gideon; Judges itself is more ambivalent. Modern literary critics commonly detect decline:

1. fearful but honest protest;
2. obedient action;
3. divinely empowered victory;
4. tactful diplomacy;
5. unauthorized vengeance and civic violence;
6. royal accumulation;
7. cultic failure;
8. dynastic disaster.

Some interpreters instead defend Gideon’s later acts as ancient justice or necessary warfare. The lack of an explicit divine command for the Succoth and Penuel reprisals and the narrator’s ephod verdict make an entirely idealized reading difficult.

### Oral tradition and folklore

The call of the least son, repeated signs, enemy dream, tiny victorious band, panic among enemies, and reluctant youth ordered to execute kings resemble widespread heroic and folktale motifs. Comparative classification can illuminate story form but cannot date the narrative or demonstrate borrowing from a particular foreign tale.

Comparisons with the Spartan three hundred are especially weak: Judges’ number functions through divine reduction and anti-boasting theology, whereas Thermopylae belongs to a different military and literary complex. Direct dependence has not been demonstrated.

### Archaeology of Midian and settlement

Archaeology confirms mobile and semi-mobile populations, long-distance exchange, and cultural formations associated by scholars with “Midianite” or Qurayyah Painted Ware in parts of northwestern Arabia and the southern Levant. It does not identify the enormous army or its named kings. Likewise, Iron I highland settlement provides background for emerging Israel but cannot be mapped directly onto the Gideon narrative.

### State of the evidence

- **Manuscripts:** one highly fragmentary early Hebrew witness relevant to the call, fuller medieval Masoretic codices, two major Greek textual forms, the Vulgate, and later translations.
- **Epigraphy:** one early *yrbʿl* personal name, not linked to Gideon.
- **Archaeology:** no confirmed Ophrah, altar, ephod, battlefield, or tomb.
- **Iconography:** abundant Christian images beginning long after the narrated period; none is ancient Israelite portraiture.
- **Cult:** no secure evidence of Gideon worship.
- **Historical core:** possible but unrecoverable with present evidence.
