# Solomon: Research Dossier

**Traditional class:** mortal human king and culture hero in the Hebrew Bible; divinely favored sage and Temple-founder; in later Judaism and Christianity, inspired author, exorcist, and magician; in Islam, prophet-king (*nabī* and *malik*) commanding winds, animals, jinn, and demons; in Ethiopian tradition, dynastic ancestor.

**Earliest secure attestation:** Solomon is not named in any securely authenticated contemporary tenth-century BCE inscription. His earliest reconstructable attestation is the Solomon narrative in 1 Kings 1–11, whose sources may preserve older court and regnal material but whose surviving literary form belongs principally to the late monarchic, exilic, and perhaps early Persian periods, approximately the seventh–fifth centuries BCE. The earliest surviving physical witness that actually preserves part of his story is the Herodian-period Hebrew manuscript 4QKings (4Q54, roughly first century BCE), containing portions of 1 Kings 7:31–41 and 8:1–9, 16–18. It supplies a reading lost from both the medieval Masoretic Text and the main Septuagint tradition. No securely contemporary object, palace, tomb, seal, or inscription can presently be assigned to Solomon personally. The ninth-century BCE Tel Dan inscription attests a “House of David,” not Solomon.

## Evidence labels

- **[E1: early literary]** securely attested pre-Hellenistic textual tradition, though not necessarily contemporary with the narrated age.
- **[E2: later canonical]** Second Temple-period or later canonical elaboration.
- **[E3: regional variant]** geographically distinctive Jewish, Christian, Islamic, Ethiopian, or folkloric tradition.
- **[E4: learned systematization]** theological, genealogical, demonological, magical, or exegetical synthesis.
- **[E5: medieval/early-modern accretion]** material first securely documented in those periods.
- **[E6: folklore/cult belief]** locally transmitted legend or practice not independently historical.
- **[E7: modern reconstruction]** scholarly historical, archaeological, literary, or psychological proposal.
- **[E8: modern invention/reception]** demonstrably modern fiction, art, political use, or pseudo-history.

These labels date evidence, not religious truth.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

**[E1]** Hebrew **שְׁלֹמֹה**, *Šĕlōmōh* or *Šəlōmōh*, is conventionally connected with the Northwest Semitic root **š-l-m**, “wholeness, well-being, peace.” 1 Chronicles 22:9 explicitly makes the name programmatic: the child will be a “man of rest,” and God will give Israel *šālôm*, peace. This is an internal biblical etymology, not proof of the name’s original formation.

**[E7]** A minority philological proposal treats *Šĕlōmōh* and names such as Absalom as theophoric formations involving the West Semitic deity Šalim/Šalem. The god’s existence is secure, but the inference that Solomon’s name invokes that god is disputed; resemblance within the productive *š-l-m* root does not establish personal devotion to Šalim.

Principal forms are:

Epithets are normally descriptive rather than fixed cult titles:

- **“Son of David”** — **[E1]** 1 Kings 1–11; also the attribution formulas of Proverbs 1:1 and Ecclesiastes 1:1.
- **“King over Israel”** — **[E1]** 1 Kings 4:1.
- **“King in Jerusalem”** — **[E2]** Ecclesiastes 1:1, 12.
- **“Beloved of YHWH” / Jedidiah** — **[E1]** 2 Samuel 12:25.
- **“Wisest of men”** — **[E1]** 1 Kings 4:29–34.
- **“Son of God” in dynastic, not biological, language** — **[E2]** 1 Chronicles 28:6, applying the Davidic adoption formula.
- **Prophet of God** — **[E3]** implicit throughout the Qur’anic cycle and explicit in later Islam.
- **Master of demons** — **[E2/E4]** Josephus, *Testament of Solomon*, rabbinic literature, magical texts.
- **Ancestor of Menelik and the Ethiopian royal house** — **[E3/E4]** *Kəbrä Nägäśt*, in its extant Geʽez form principally fourteenth century.
- **Archetypal magician or goetic king** — **[E5]** medieval and early-modern grimoires, not Kings or the Qur’an.

No ancient source classifies Solomon as a demigod, and no securely documented Israelite hero-cult made him a deified dead king.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### Documentary floor

**[E1/E7]** The canonical Solomon narrative identifies earlier written materials—“the Book of the Acts of Solomon” in 1 Kings 11:41—but that work is lost. Administrative lists, building notices, succession materials, royal annals, folktales of wisdom, and Deuteronomistic theological judgments may have entered Kings at different times. Their dates cannot be read directly from the events they claim to describe.

**[E7]** The received Books of Kings extend through Jehoiachin’s release in Babylon around 561 BCE. The classic Deuteronomistic History model therefore places a major edition in the exilic period, often with an earlier Josianic edition and later additions. Other models distribute the composition and redaction more widely from the late monarchy into the Persian period. No consensus permits dating 1 Kings 1–11 as a unit to Solomon’s purported reign.

**[E1]** The oldest surviving Solomon manuscript is 4QKings, 4Q54. It preserves Temple material in 1 Kings 7–8 and demonstrates that textual plurality preceded the medieval Masoretic codices. The official Dead Sea Scrolls image archive identifies the fragments as 4Q54. The scroll is evidence for the story’s circulation by the late Second Temple period, not evidence that Solomon built the Temple.

### Hebrew Bible and ancient versions

1. **2 Samuel 12:24–25; 1 Kings 1–11** — Hebrew; final forms principally late monarchic through exilic/Persian periods.  
   **[E1]** Birth, succession struggle, executions and consolidations, divine wisdom, judicial wisdom, administration, wealth, Temple and palace, Tyrian alliance, forced labor, Queen of Sheba, harem, apostasy, adversaries, death.

2. **1 Chronicles 3, 22–29; 2 Chronicles 1–9** — Hebrew; commonly dated late Persian or early Hellenistic period, approximately fourth–third centuries BCE.  
   **[E2]** Rewrites Samuel–Kings. David publicly selects and equips Solomon; Solomon builds on Mount Moriah; fire descends at the dedication. Chronicles omits the violent succession, the women’s judgment, foreign wives, apostasy, and adversaries, producing a more unambiguously ideal Temple king. Chronicles’ revision of Kings is shaped by later priestly and cultic concerns.

3. **Psalms 72 and 127** — Hebrew, superscribed *lišlōmōh*, which can mean “of,” “for,” or “concerning Solomon.”  
   **[E1/E2]** Psalm 72 constructs the just, universal royal ideal; Psalm 127 associates building and household prosperity with Solomon. The headings do not establish personal authorship.

4. **Proverbs 1:1; 10:1; 25:1** — Hebrew, composite wisdom anthology.  
   **[E1/E2]** Attributes collections to Solomon, while 25:1 says Hezekiah’s men copied a Solomonic collection—already representing reception and transmission rather than autograph authorship.

5. **Song of Songs 1:1; 3:7–11; 8:11–12** — Hebrew, probably assembled in the Persian or Hellenistic period from older lyric materials.  
   **[E2]** The title associates the collection with Solomon; poems invoke his litter, wedding crown, harem, and vineyard. Whether he is lover, foil, or symbolic royal figure varies by reading. Qumran preserves several copies or fragments.

6. **Ecclesiastes/Qoheleth 1:1, 12; 2:4–11** — Hebrew with linguistic features widely judged postexilic, commonly third century BCE.  
   **[E2]** Never names Solomon, but its “son of David, king in Jerusalem,” extraordinary wisdom, wealth, gardens, singers, and concubines invite a Solomonic persona. Traditional attribution to Solomon is reception, not the critical consensus on authorship. Qumran fragments establish circulation by the second–first centuries BCE.

7. **Wisdom of Solomon** — Greek, probably Alexandria, roughly first century BCE to early first century CE.  
   **[E2/E4]** Anonymous in the text but speaks in the voice of a king and judge who prayed for Wisdom. The title identifies the voice with Solomon; modern scholarship treats it as Jewish Greek pseudepigraphy.

8. **Sirach 47:12–23** — Hebrew original, early second century BCE; Greek translation after 132 BCE.  
   **[E2]** Praises Solomon’s wisdom, songs, parables, wealth, and peaceful reign, then condemns his sexual submission and the division caused by his dynasty.

9. **Septuagint 3 Kingdoms/Reigns 1–11** — Greek translation with a Hebrew Vorlage partly different from the Masoretic Text.  
   **[E2]** The order and contents differ substantially: material about Solomon, Hiram, Pharaoh’s daughter, and Jeroboam is rearranged; the long alternative account at 3 Reigns 12:24a–z is particularly important. Scholarship no longer safely treats every difference as either translator error or simple expansion: MT and Old Greek may represent divergent literary editions of older Hebrew material.

10. **Vulgate 3 Kings** — Latin translation, chiefly Jerome, late fourth–early fifth century CE.  
    **[E4]** Transmits the Hebrew-shaped narrative to western Christianity; numbering differs from modern Protestant 1 Kings.

### Jewish, Christian, and pseudepigraphic witnesses

- **Josephus, *Jewish Antiquities* 8**, 93/94 CE — **[E2/E4]** retells Kings for a Greco-Roman audience, expands Solomon’s science of plants and demons, and claims to have witnessed Eleazar exorcise a demon before Vespasian using a ring, a Solomonic root, the name of Solomon, and attributed incantations. This is the earliest securely dated extended testimony to operational “Solomonic” exorcism.
- **New Testament, Matthew 6:29; 12:42; Luke 11:31; 12:27; Acts 3:11; 5:12; John 10:23** — first century CE. **[E2/E4]** Jesus contrasts Solomon’s glory with lilies and his wisdom with “one greater than Solomon”; the Queen becomes the “queen of the South.” “Solomon’s Portico” names an architectural location, not a verified surviving Solomonic structure.
- **Testament of Solomon** — Greek, composite and difficult to date; constituent material often placed in the early Roman centuries, with later redaction and medieval manuscripts. **[E4]** Michael supplies a ring bearing a divine seal. Solomon summons and interrogates Ornias, Beelzeboul, Asmodeus, Obyzouth, Enepsigos, the seven planetary spirits, and many others; demons build the Temple and disclose the angels or formulas that thwart them. The conclusion makes Solomon’s lust for a Jebusite woman and sacrifice to foreign gods the cause of his fall. Securely dated “Solomon” ring formulas from the Roman period support the antiquity of the exorcistic milieu, but not every episode or the text’s final form.
- **Babylonian Talmud, Gittin 68a–b**, redacted mainly fifth–sixth centuries CE — **[E4]** Solomon captures Ashmedai to obtain the *shamir*, loses ring and throne when Ashmedai tricks him, wanders declaring “I, Qoheleth, was king,” and eventually returns. The story mocks imperial acquisition and teaches mortality as much as magic.
- **Genesis Rabbah**, fifth-century form; **Pirke de-Rabbi Eliezer**, probably eighth–ninth century — **[E4]** enlarge Solomon’s wisdom, throne, animals, Temple, and Queen traditions.
- **Targum Sheni to Esther**, probably late antique or early medieval in its present form — **[E4/E5]** contains an elaborate Solomon–Queen of Sheba cycle, including the hoopoe and riddles, and has complex relations with Islamic versions.
- **Ginzberg, *Legends of the Jews***, 1909–1938 — **[E7]** indispensable synthesis and index to rabbinic legend, but a modern literary compilation, never an ancient primary source.
- **1 Enoch, Jubilees, Life of Adam and Eve, and the patriarchal Testaments** — **absence finding:** none supplies a major independent Solomon cycle comparable to Josephus or the *Testament of Solomon*. Their frequent inclusion in broad pseudepigraphal bibliographies should not be converted into unattested deeds.

### Islamic and Ethiopian corpora

- **Qur’an 2:102; 4:163; 6:84; 21:78–82; 27:15–44; 34:12–14; 38:30–40**, early seventh century CE — **[E3]** Sulaymān is David’s prophetic heir; judges a field dispute; understands birds; commands wind and jinn; hears an ant; tests the hoopoe; writes to the unnamed Queen of Sheba; brings or has brought her throne; converts her from sun worship; and dies leaning on his staff while jinn continue working until a ground-creature gnaws it. Qur’an 2:102 explicitly denies that Solomon disbelieved or taught sorcery: “the devils disbelieved.”
- **al-Ṭabarī, al-Thaʿlabī’s *ʿArāʾis al-majālis*, and works attributed to al-Kisāʾī**, ninth–eleventh centuries and later manuscript traditions — **[E4/E5]** integrate Qur’anic notices with Jewish, Christian, Iranian, and oral materials: the ring, loss and recovery of kingship, Ṣakhr the jinn, Bilqīs’s parentage, glass pavement, hairy legs, magic, world travel, and monumental building.
- **Kəbrä Nägäśt**, Geʽez compilation principally fourteenth century, drawing on earlier biblical, Christian, Arabic, and local materials — **[E3/E4]** names the queen Makədda. Solomon engineers a pledge in which she promises not to take anything from his house; after he makes her thirsty with salted food, taking water releases him from his reciprocal promise, and he sleeps with her. The episode is coercive by modern standards even though the text frames it as legal ingenuity. Their son Bayna Ləḥkəm/Menelik visits Solomon; companions remove the Ark to Ethiopia with divine approval. The story authorizes Ethiopian Israelite election and royal descent.

## 3. The story, cycle by cycle

### Birth, guilt, and double naming

**[E1]** In 2 Samuel 11–12, David has sex with Bathsheba, wife of Uriah, after seeing her bathing; whether modern categories call the initial act rape remains debated because the narrator records no consent and depicts an extreme power imbalance. David then arranges Uriah’s death. Their first child dies after Nathan pronounces judgment. A later son is named Solomon by David or Bathsheba, while Nathan calls him Jedidiah. The text does not say Solomon is the dead infant, and harmonizations that confuse them are erroneous.

**[E2]** 1 Chronicles 3:5 calls his mother Bath-shua and includes Solomon among four sons born to David in Jerusalem. Chronicles suppresses the adultery and killing.

### Succession and bloodshed

**[E1]** 1 Kings 1–2 makes Solomon’s accession contested, not automatic. Adonijah, son of David and Haggith, proclaims himself with Joab and Abiathar. Nathan and Bathsheba prompt the aged David to declare Solomon heir; Zadok anoints him at Gihon. After David’s death Solomon has Adonijah killed for requesting Abishag, Joab killed at the altar, Shimei executed after a travel violation, and Abiathar exiled. Benaiah performs the killings. The narrative can be read simultaneously as legitimation and disclosure of dynastic violence.

**[E2]** Chronicles replaces this intrigue with orderly public selection by David and the assembly. The two versions should not be harmonized: one remembers a dangerous palace coup, the other an ideal liturgical succession.

### Gibeon and wisdom

**[E1]** In 1 Kings 3 Solomon sacrifices at the “great high place” of Gibeon. YHWH appears in a dream and offers a gift. Solomon asks for a “listening heart” to judge; God grants wisdom plus riches and honor. The episode legitimates royal judicial discernment while acknowledging that worship still occurred outside Jerusalem.

**[E2]** 2 Chronicles 1 converts the setting into authorized tabernacle worship and describes the request as wisdom and knowledge. Wisdom of Solomon 7–9 later turns the prayer into a philosophical invocation of personified Wisdom.

### The two women and the child

**[E1]** 1 Kings 3:16–28 describes two prostitutes, each claiming a surviving infant after one baby dies when its mother lies upon it. Solomon orders the living child divided; the woman who relinquishes her claim to save it is adjudged the mother. No child is actually cut. The tale demonstrates knowledge of maternal compassion rather than forensic evidence, and its protagonists’ marginal status magnifies the king’s access to justice.

**[E5/E8]** “Solomon’s judgment” became a generic label for ingenious adjudication, including modern economics’ “King Solomon problem.” Those abstractions are reception, not ancient episodes.

### Kingdom, wealth, forced labor, and slavery

**[E1]** 1 Kings 4–5 and 9–10 depicts twelve provisioning districts, a court consuming enormous daily supplies, cavalry and chariots, maritime trade with Ophir, and dominion “from the River to the land of the Philistines.” It also records levies: 30,000 Israelites rotate to Lebanon; 70,000 bear burdens and 80,000 quarry stone. 1 Kings 9:20–22 distinguishes surviving non-Israelites as forced slave labor while claiming Israelites served as officials and soldiers.

**[E2]** 2 Chronicles 2:16–18 and 8:7–10 keeps the non-Israelite forced-labor distinction and idealizes Israelite administration. Neither archaeology nor independent inscriptions verify the biblical empire’s geographical extent or numbers.

### Temple and palace

**[E1]** 1 Kings 5–8 says Hiram of Tyre supplies cedar, cypress, and craftsmen. Solomon builds the Temple in seven years and his palace complex in thirteen. Hiram/Huram, a bronze worker whose mother is Israelite and father Tyrian, makes the pillars Jachin and Boaz, the Sea, stands, and vessels. At dedication the Ark enters the inner sanctuary, cloud fills the building, Solomon prays, and vast sacrifices follow.

**[E2]** Chronicles adds Mount Moriah, makes David the comprehensive planner and donor, intensifies Levite participation, and has heavenly fire consume the sacrifice. These are later canonical elaborations.

**[E7]** The Temple Mount’s continuous sacred use and political sensitivity prevent ordinary excavation of the claimed sanctuary site. The First Temple is historically plausible in a Jerusalemite monarchy, but no excavated remain can securely be identified as Solomon’s Temple. Comparative Phoenician and Syrian temple architecture shows the biblical description belongs to an intelligible Iron Age tradition; it does not identify the builder.

### Hiram and the disputed cities

**[E1]** 1 Kings 9 says Solomon gives Hiram twenty Galilean towns; Hiram calls them “Cabul,” expressing displeasure. In 2 Chronicles 8, by contrast, Hiram gives towns to Solomon, who rebuilds and settles them. The direction of transfer is reversed, likely to protect Solomon’s prestige and Israelite territorial integrity.

### Queen of Sheba

**[E1]** 1 Kings 10:1–13 and 2 Chronicles 9:1–12 leave the queen unnamed. She hears Solomon’s fame, tests him with hard questions, praises his wisdom and YHWH, exchanges extravagant gifts, and departs. The texts say neither that they marry nor have sex nor produce a child.

**[E7]** Sheba is commonly associated with Saba in southern Arabia, although African Red Sea connections made Ethiopian appropriation possible. No Sabaean inscription identifies this queen or her visit. The biblical account may encode later knowledge of Arabian luxury trade and Persian-period royal ideology.

**[E4/E5]** Jewish and Islamic exegetes name her, invent riddles, and explore whether she is human, demonic, hairy, or sexually anomalous. The Qur’an leaves her unnamed; **Bilqīs** is post-Qur’anic.

**[E3/E4]** The Ethiopian *Kəbrä Nägäśt* makes Makədda Solomon’s sexual partner and Menelik’s mother. Menelik’s removal of the Ark supplies a charter for Ethiopian sacred kingship. This is a medieval national epic, not suppressed evidence from 1 Kings.

### Wives, foreign gods, and fall

**[E1]** 1 Kings 11 gives Solomon 700 royal wives and 300 concubines. Named categories include Pharaoh’s daughter and Moabite, Ammonite, Edomite, Sidonian, and Hittite women. The narrator says they turn his heart toward Ashtoreth of the Sidonians, Milcom of Ammon, Chemosh of Moab, and Molech/Milcom; he builds shrines east of Jerusalem. YHWH therefore promises to tear away most of the kingdom after his death.

**[E7]** The round total of one thousand and Jerusalem’s probable size make the harem historically implausible as a literal household. Literarily it completes a critique anticipated by Deuteronomy 17: kings must not multiply horses, wives, silver, or gold—all things Solomon accumulates.

**[E2]** Chronicles omits the apostasy entirely. Sirach preserves both admiration and condemnation. The *Testament of Solomon* turns the fall into demonological tragedy. Qur’an 2:102 polemically refuses the conclusion that Solomon became an unbelieving sorcerer.

### Adversaries, death, and body

**[E1]** YHWH raises Hadad the Edomite, Rezon son of Eliada, and Jeroboam son of Nebat. Ahijah symbolically tears a garment into twelve pieces and assigns ten tribes to Jeroboam. Solomon attempts to kill him; Jeroboam flees to Egypt.

**[E1]** 1 Kings 11:42–43 says Solomon ruled forty years, died, and was buried “in the city of David his father”; Rehoboam succeeded. Chronicles repeats the formula. No description of illness, murder, corpse, relics, or translation survives in the early sources.

**[E6]** Later sites called Solomon’s tomb, throne, prison, pool, or bed occur across Jerusalem, Iran, Central Asia, and the Islamic world. They attest the geographical reach of his name, not the location of his body.

A famous pairing requiring rejection is **Solomon and Hiram Abiff**. Biblical Kings knows Hiram the king and Hiram the bronze worker but no murdered master architect named “Hiram Abiff.” The murder-and-raising complex belongs to modern Freemasonry, principally eighteenth-century ritual development: **[E8]**.

## 5. Cult, tomb, relics, and ritual

**[E1/E7: negative finding]** There is no secure archaeological or epigraphic evidence that Solomon received an Israelite hero-cult, sacrifices at a tomb, priesthood, games, or a functioning oracle. Biblical religion remembers him as deceased king and Temple builder, not a god or divinized ancestor.

**[E1]** Kings places his burial in the City of David. No excavated tomb can be identified as his, and no authenticated relic or inscription names his grave.

**[E2/E4]** Late-antique Christian pilgrims were reportedly shown a ring associated with Solomon among Jerusalem relics. Such display documents belief and pilgrimage, not provenance. Josephus’s exorcist uses Solomon’s name and an inscribed ring as operative ritual authority.

**[E4/E5]** Jewish, Christian, and Islamic amulets invoke Solomon as the controller of harmful spirits. Pentagrams, hexagrams, knots, divine names, mounted holy riders, and images of a demon subdued beneath the seal appear in multiple traditions. “Seal of Solomon” never denoted one invariant ancient emblem: pentagram and hexagram were interchangeable in medieval usage, and other devices also bore the name. It is therefore incorrect to project the modern Star of David unchanged onto the tenth century BCE.

**[E5]** *Clavicula Salomonis*, the *Hygromanteia*, *Ars Notoria*, and related works ritualize his authority through pentacles, planetary timing, purification, prayers, blades, circles, and conjurations. The principal manuscripts are medieval and early modern. The *Lemegeton* or “Lesser Key,” including the *Goetia*, is a seventeenth-century compilation dependent on identifiable earlier printed and manuscript sources. Its seventy-two demons are not a recovered catalog from Solomon’s court.

**[E3/E6]** Islamic landscapes assign Solomon thrones, prisons, footprints, fountains, walls, and tombs. Takht-e Soleymān in Iran is fundamentally a Sasanian sacred complex later attached to Solomon. Sulayman-Too at Osh accumulated shrines, caves, bodily impressions, fertility practices, and a “grave” of Solomon; the regional cultic complex took much of its documented form in the seventeenth–eighteenth centuries. Women rolling on a polished stone to seek fertility records living sacred geography, not a bodily relic.

**[E6]** Pasargadae’s Tomb of Cyrus was locally called the “Tomb of Solomon’s Mother” before modern identification. The name helped protect and Islamize an imposing pre-Islamic monument; it is not evidence for Solomon’s family.

## 6. Historical evolution

### Tenth century BCE: narrated horizon, not documentary attestation

**[E7]** Conventional biblical chronology places Solomon around 970–931 BCE. No contemporary inscription names him. Archaeology indicates developing highland polities, regional trade, and settlement growth, but the scale and organization of tenth-century Judah remain disputed.

### Seventh–fifth centuries BCE: literary formation

**[E1/E7]** Court traditions, annalistic notices, folktales, Temple ideology, and criticism of kingship were shaped into Kings. Solomon becomes both apex and cause of collapse: wisdom, wealth, and Temple on one side; taxation, forced labor, foreign marriage, and apostasy on the other.

### Fourth–first centuries BCE: ideal king and wisdom author

**[E2]** Chronicles sanitizes and sacerdotalizes him. Proverbs, Song, Ecclesiastes, Psalms, Sirach, and Wisdom progressively turn “Solomon” into an authorizing signature for lawlike wisdom, love poetry, philosophical reflection, royal justice, and revealed Wisdom. Qumran manuscripts prove circulation but not authorship.

### First–sixth centuries CE: exorcist and cosmic ruler

**[E2/E4]** Josephus makes Solomonic exorcism current practice. The *Testament of Solomon*, incantation bowls, gems, and amulets elaborate the seal, ring, demons, angels, astrology, disease, and Temple labor. Rabbinic tradition both magnifies and humiliates the king: he understands creation yet can be displaced by Ashmedai.

### Seventh–eleventh centuries: Islamic prophet-king

**[E3/E4]** The Qur’an exonerates Sulaymān from unbelieving magic and subordinates his powers completely to God. Tafsīr and *qiṣaṣ al-anbiyāʾ* reconnect him to the ring and temporary loss of rule, while expanding Bilqīs, jinn, beasts, travel, architecture, and sacred landscapes. Iranian royal and monstrous geography is absorbed into Solomonic nomenclature.

### Twelfth–seventeenth centuries: dynastic ancestor and grimoire authority

**[E3/E5]** The *Kəbrä Nägäśt* makes him father of Ethiopia’s sacred dynasty. In Europe and the Mediterranean, pseudo-Solomonic books become central technologies of learned magic. The authorial name functions like a patent of primordial wisdom.

### Eighteenth–nineteenth centuries: monarchy, Masonry, Orientalism

**[E8]** Freemasonry reorganizes the Temple story around Hiram Abiff. Handel’s 1748 oratorio *Solomon* presents ordered, prosperous kingship and has often been read in relation to Hanoverian Britain. Romantic and Orientalist painting eroticizes Sheba, monumental architecture, luxury, and judgment. European occult editors repackage manuscript grimoires as ancient secrets.

### Twentieth century to present

**[E7/E8]** Archaeology dismantles easy identifications of “Solomon’s stables” and gates while generating competing chronologies. Ethiopian monarchy continues to claim Solomonic descent until Haile Selassie’s overthrow in 1974. Novels, film, comics, fantasy, anime, and games split the figure into wise king, erotic despot, occult master, demon summoner, or superhuman mage.

## 7. Modern reception and reinvention

- **George Frideric Handel, *Solomon* (1748; premiered 1749)** — **[E8]** oratorio combining Temple dedication, judgment, and Sheba into tableaux of harmonious monarchy. Its celebrated “Arrival of the Queen of Sheba” has acquired an independent concert life.
- **Charles Gounod, *La reine de Saba* (1862)** — **[E8]** opera transforming the Sheba story through romantic rivalry.
- **H. Rider Haggard, *King Solomon’s Mines* (1885)** — **[E8]** imperial adventure fiction; its African treasure geography is modern fantasy, not an ancient location tradition.
- **Edward Poynter, *The Visit of the Queen of Sheba to King Solomon* (1890)** — **[E8]** Victorian Orientalist spectacle.
- **Aleister Crowley’s edition of the *Goetia* (1904)** and popular “Greater/Lesser Key” publishing — **[E8]** modern editorial occultism presenting heterogeneous manuscripts as a usable Solomonic system.
- **Film *Solomon and Sheba* (1959)** — **[E8]** erotic-historical epic starring Yul Brynner and Gina Lollobrigida; it expands romance and warfare far beyond Kings.
- **Rastafari** — **[E3/E8]** receives the Ethiopian Solomonic genealogy through reverence for Haile Selassie, interpreted within varying Rastafari theologies. It is a twentieth-century religious reception of the medieval Ethiopian claim, not independent ancient evidence.
- **Masonic Solomon** — **[E8]** the Temple embodies moral construction, secret craft, and fraternity; claims of institutional descent from actual Temple builders are legendary.
- **Contemporary occultism** — **[E8]** mass-market demon decks, “ancient” seals, online sigil systems, and supposed rings commonly mix the *Testament*, *Clavicula*, *Lemegeton*, Kabbalah, and modern ceremonial magic without manuscript distinctions.
- **Manga/anime and games**, including *Magi* and the *Fate/Grand Order* version of Solomon — **[E8]** recast him as world-system architect, magical singularity, or heroic spirit. These are explicit creative reinventions.
- **Commercial “King Solomon” branding** — **[E8]** mines, wisdom products, jewelry, cannabis strains, and occult supplies exploit associations with limitless wealth, judgment, sexuality, and magic.

### Political appropriation

**[E3/E4]** The Ethiopian Solomonic monarchy used descent from Solomon and Makədda to legitimate rule. The 1955 Ethiopian constitution formally grounded imperial dignity in descent from the dynasty of Menelik I, “son of the Queen of Ethiopia, the Queen of Sheba, and King Solomon of Jerusalem.” This was political theology, not demonstrated genealogy.

**[E7/E8]** Biblical archaeology was entangled with European imperial, Christian restorationist, and Zionist interest in monumental Israelite monarchy. That context does not invalidate excavation data, but names such as “Solomon’s Stables” and “Solomonic gates” often embedded a historical conclusion in the label before the chronology was settled.

**[E8]** Solomon’s Temple remains a charged symbol in Jewish, Christian, and Muslim claims concerning the Temple Mount/Haram al-Sharif. Extremist Temple-rebuilding agendas and anti-Jewish conspiracy theories about Masons or “Solomonic” control are modern political appropriations, not evidence about the Iron Age king.

## 8. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Historicity

**[E7]** Three positions should be distinguished:

1. **Substantial-monarchy reconstruction:** scholars such as William G. Dever and Amihai Mazar accept a tenth-century state of meaningful scale and regard some monumental construction at Gezer, Hazor, or Jerusalem as compatible with the biblical period. Even this does not directly prove that Solomon commissioned a given gate.
2. **Low-chronology or reduced-monarchy reconstruction:** Israel Finkelstein and collaborators shift major northern monumental remains into the ninth-century Omride period and reconstruct tenth-century Jerusalem as the center of a much smaller polity.
3. **Strong minimalism:** some scholars regard David and Solomon principally as later literary constructions. The Tel Dan “House of David” inscription weakened denials that a Davidic dynastic founder was remembered by the ninth century, but it neither names Solomon nor verifies the united empire.

The responsible minimum is: a historical dynastic ruler behind Solomon is possible and accepted by many scholars; his deeds, empire, wealth, Temple, and chronology cannot be independently confirmed as a package.

### “Solomonic” gates and stables

**[E7]** Yigael Yadin connected six-chambered gates at Hazor, Megiddo, and Gezer with 1 Kings 9:15. Similar plans and conventional tenth-century dating seemed to reveal centralized architecture. Finkelstein and David Ussishkin argued that major Megiddo remains belong to the ninth-century Omrides; Amnon Ben-Tor and Mazar defend higher or modified chronologies at Hazor and elsewhere. Dever defends a tenth-century Gezer gate but concedes that archaeology does not inscribe Solomon’s name upon it. Current chronology does not justify collectively calling all three “Solomon’s gates” as an established fact.

Megiddo’s pillared buildings were once announced as “Solomon’s stables.” They were later redated and variously assigned to Ahab, Jeroboam II, or non-equine functions. The episode is a classic warning against naming finds from scripture before stratigraphic demonstration.

### Jerusalem

**[E7]** Jerusalem’s tenth-century scale is central. Proposed monumental structures in the City of David have been assigned by excavators to Davidic or Solomonic state-building, while critics dispute dating, architectural unity, and biblical attribution. The Temple platform itself cannot supply normal excavation controls. Assertions that Solomon’s palace or Temple has been “found” exceed the evidence.

### Textual history

**[E7]** The Masoretic and Greek Solomon narratives are not merely identical stories in different languages. Their rearrangements, additions, omissions, and duplicated notices point to literary growth. 4QKings preserves wording absent from MT and LXX but retained in Chronicles, proving that no single medieval witness monopolizes the earliest recoverable form.

### Authorship of wisdom books

**[E7]** Traditional Solomonic authorship rests on superscriptions, persona, and reputation. Linguistic, literary, and historical analysis dates the final forms of Song and Ecclesiastes centuries later; Proverbs is explicitly composite; Wisdom of Solomon is Greek. “Solomonic” is best understood as an ancient attributional and pseudepigraphic category comparable to Davidic psalmody or Mosaic law.

### Demonology and magic

**[E7]** Josephus proves that Solomon’s name authorized exorcism by the late first century CE. The *Testament of Solomon* documents late-antique demonological synthesis; its exact date remains contested because it is composite and survives in much later manuscripts. Claims that it is Solomon’s own testament or a single first-century book are discredited.

The ring tradition is absent from Kings. Qur’an 2:102 defends Solomon against accusations of sorcery; therefore describing Qur’anic Sulaymān simply as a magician reverses the Qur’an’s polemic. Medieval grimoires later appropriate him precisely as magician.

### Comparative claims

**[E7]** Solomon resembles Near Eastern royal sages, legendary just judges, temple builders, animal-language masters, and magician-kings. Individual parallels can illuminate genres. They do not prove derivation without demonstrable transmission. Frazerian, Rankian, Raglan-style hero-pattern, Jungian, structuralist, and Campbellian readings are twentieth-century interpretive grids; Solomon fits the monomyth poorly because the canonical account is royal historiography and wisdom legend, not a quest-and-return biography.

### Lost and misleading evidence

- The “Book of the Acts of Solomon” is lost.
- No royal inscription of Solomon is known.
- No authenticated “Solomon ivory,” ring, seal impression, or gold mine bears his name.
- The ivory pomegranate once promoted as a First Temple relic has a contested inscription and cannot authenticate Solomon.
- Modern replicas marketed as “Solomon’s ring” reproduce medieval or modern sigils.
- The *Lesser Key of Solomon* is not an ancient translation from Hebrew.
- The Queen of Sheba’s child is absent from Bible and Qur’an.
- No excavated tomb securely belongs to Solomon.
- “Solomon’s Pools” near Bethlehem are principally later waterworks whose current name is a traditional attribution, not an archaeological date.

## 10. Sources and assessment

### Primary texts and critical anchors

- Hebrew Bible: 2 Samuel 11–12; 1 Kings 1–11; 1 Chronicles 3, 22–29; 2 Chronicles 1–9; Psalms 72, 127; Proverbs; Song of Songs; Ecclesiastes.
- Septuagint: 3 Reigns/Kingdoms 1–12, especially the alternative Jeroboam complex at 12:24a–z.
- Dead Sea Scroll 4Q54/4QKings.
- Sirach 47 and Wisdom of Solomon 7–9.
- Josephus, *Jewish Antiquities* 8.
- New Testament passages naming Solomon.
- *Testament of Solomon*, evaluated as a composite pseudepigraphon.
- Babylonian Talmud, Gittin 68a–b.
- Qur’an 2, 4, 6, 21, 27, 34, and 38.
- Medieval *qiṣaṣ al-anbiyāʾ* traditions of al-Thaʿlabī and pseudo-al-Kisāʾī.
- *Kəbrä Nägäśt*, especially chapters 19–94.
- Medieval and early-modern *Hygromanteia*, *Clavicula Salomonis*, *Ars Notoria*, and *Lemegeton* manuscripts.

### Overall evidentiary judgment

**[E7]** Solomon’s documentary profile is inverse to his legendary magnitude. The securely surviving evidence closest to antiquity is literary and several centuries later than the conventional reign. It depicts an internally divided figure: divinely wise yet politically ruthless; Temple-builder yet exploiter of forced labor; guardian of YHWH’s name yet patron of other gods. Chronicles removes the contradiction; wisdom books make him an author; Josephus and the *Testament* make wisdom technological power over demons; the Talmud subjects that power to comic humiliation; the Qur’an converts it into prophetic miracle and defends him from sorcery; Ethiopia makes his sexuality the origin of a sacred dynasty; grimoires turn his name into magical authorship.

The archaeology supports an Iron Age setting containing temples, monumental gates, trade, and emerging monarchies, but it has not recovered Solomon. The attested historical object is therefore not a verified imperial biography but a long, branching record of communities using “Solomon” to think about kingship, justice, sacred architecture, dangerous knowledge, sexuality, empire, divine favor, and the limits of human wisdom.
