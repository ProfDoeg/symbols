# Jonah: Research Dossier

> **Traditional class:** mortal prophet (*nāḇîʾ*), not a demigod or epic king. Judaism, Christianity, and Islam all treat him as a divinely commissioned human messenger; later Jewish and Christian traditions sometimes treat him as a revived, death-transcending, or saint-like holy man.  
> **Earliest secure attestation:** the name **Jonah son of Amittai of Gath-hepher** occurs in 2 Kings 14:25, in a notice set during the reign of Jeroboam II of Israel, conventionally 8th century BCE. The notice is the earliest literary attestation but survives only in much later manuscripts and cannot independently verify Jonah’s existence. The earliest surviving physical witnesses to the Jonah narrative are fragments of the Book of the Twelve from the second and first centuries BCE, including 4Q76, 4Q81, and 4Q82.  
> **Method:** labels in brackets distinguish **[early canonical]**, **[later canonical]**, **[textual variant]**, **[rabbinic elaboration]**, **[Christian interpretation]**, **[Islamic canonical]**, **[Islamic elaboration]**, **[regional tradition]**, **[archaeological]**, **[medieval/early-modern]**, **[folklore]**, **[modern reconstruction]**, and **[modern invention]**.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

Jonah is the recalcitrant Israelite prophet whom the Book of Jonah sends westward in flight from a commission to preach against Nineveh. A divinely sent storm exposes him; foreign sailors cast lots, interrogate him, and reluctantly throw him into the sea; a “great fish” swallows him for three days and nights; after his prayer he is expelled onto dry land. He then announces Nineveh’s overthrow, but humans and animals fast, the city is spared, and Jonah angrily admits that he fled because he knew Israel’s merciful God might relent. A divinely appointed plant, worm, and scorching wind become the instruments of God’s final, unanswered lesson about pity. The Hebrew story never calls the animal a whale, never says Jonah died in it, never names Nineveh’s king, and never records Jonah’s answer to God’s final question.

### Names

- **Hebrew:** יוֹנָה בֶן־אֲמִתַּי, *Yônāh ben-ʾĂmittay*, “Jonah son of Amittai” (2 Kings 14:25; Jonah 1:1). *Yônāh* is the ordinary Hebrew word for “dove” or “pigeon.” Symbolic readings—Jonah as unstable “dove,” Israel’s “silly dove” in Hosea 7:11, or a homing bird that fails to return properly—are interpretations, not an etymology beyond the lexical meaning. **Amittai** is related to אמת, *ʾemet*, “truth, reliability,” often glossed “my truth” or “truthful”; “son of truth” as an ironic literary characterization is a modern or homiletic inference. [early canonical; modern reconstruction] [Recent discussion of the dove imagery](https://doi.org/10.3390/rel15040502).

- **Greek:** Ἰωνᾶς, *Iōnas*, in the Septuagint and New Testament. The Greek fish is κῆτος, *kētos*, a large sea-creature or sea monster, not a zoologically specific whale. [textual transmission]

- **Latin:** *Ionas/Jonas* in the Vulgate; hence medieval and early-modern English **Jonas**, alongside **Jonah**. The Vulgate’s *piscis grandis* remains “great fish.”

- **Syriac:** ܝܘܢܢ, *Yawnān/Yonān*, in the Peshitta and Syriac Christian tradition.

- **Arabic:** يُونُس, *Yūnus*, the Qurʾanic form; also ذو النون, *Dhū al-Nūn*, “the man/possessor of the fish,” in Qurʾan 21:87, and صاحب الحوت, *ṣāḥib al-ḥūt*, “companion of the fish,” in 68:48. [Islamic canonical]

### Titles and classifications

- **“The prophet”**: 2 Kings 14:25 explicitly calls Jonah *hannāḇîʾ*, while the Book of Jonah presents him performing a prophet’s commission without giving him the title in its narrative opening. [early canonical]
- **“Servant” of YHWH**: 2 Kings 14:25 calls him God’s servant. [early canonical]
- **“Son of Amittai”**: patronymic in both 2 Kings and Jonah, the literary device that makes their identification natural within the canon.
- **“One of the messengers”**: Qurʾan 37:139, placing Yūnus within Islamic prophethood. [Islamic canonical]
- **“The man of the fish”**: Qurʾan 21:87. [Islamic canonical]
- **Jonas as a type of Christ**: not an identity but a Christian typological equation beginning in Matthew 12:39–41. [Christian interpretation]
- **Widow’s resurrected son**: Jonah’s equation with the boy revived by Elijah in 1 Kings 17 appears in postbiblical Jewish and Christian traditions, not in either biblical passage. [rabbinic/late-antique elaboration]

No securely ancient source identifies Jonah as a demigod, divine incarnation, founder-king, or recipient of an Israelite hero cult.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### 2.1 The earliest literary notice: 2 Kings 14:23–27

The Deuteronomistic history reports that Jeroboam II restored Israel’s border “from Lebo-hamath as far as the Sea of the Arabah” according to YHWH’s word spoken through “his servant Jonah son of Amittai, the prophet, who was from Gath-hepher.” [early canonical]

What it establishes within the narrative world:

- a northern Israelite prophet named Jonah;
- the patronymic Amittai;
- origin at Gath-hepher in Galilee;
- association with Jeroboam II;
- a favorable oracle concerning Israel’s territorial restoration.

What it does **not** establish:

- a journey to Nineveh;
- a fish episode;
- the composition or authorship of the Book of Jonah;
- Jonah’s birth, death, or tomb;
- independent historical corroboration.

The notice is set in the 8th century BCE, but Kings reached its surviving literary form later, commonly in exilic or post-exilic editing. Jonah is unattested in contemporary Assyrian royal inscriptions. Jeroboam II is historically plausible within the securely attested Neo-Assyrian geopolitical setting, but that contextual plausibility does not authenticate Jonah personally.

### 2.2 The Book of Jonah

The Hebrew book consists of forty-eight verses in four chapters and belongs to the Book of the Twelve.

**Date.** Traditional Jewish and Christian attribution made the eighth-century prophet author and protagonist. Most modern critical scholarship instead dates the literary work after the fall of Nineveh in 612 BCE, often to the Persian or early Hellenistic period, approximately the late 6th through 4th centuries BCE. Arguments include its linguistic mixture, apparently retrospective use of Assyria, literary affinities with later biblical prose, universal theology, and reuse of older scriptural language. Language alone does not yield an exact date; recent scholarship therefore tends toward a broad post-monarchic range rather than a single year. [A recent research survey](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1476993X221150655) and [a review of the linguistic dispute](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-anglican-studies/article/linguistic-periodization-of-the-book-of-jonah-eb-pusey-and-modern-scholarship/E7578598AD5B5D2D372E5B91CAA64EDB).

**Genre.** Proposals include prophetic novella, didactic tale, parable, comedy, parody, satire, and “metaprophetic” fiction. The hyperbolic city, extraordinarily responsive foreigners and animals, absurdly successful five-word Hebrew sermon, and petulant prophet sustain comic or satirical readings. Yet no ancient colophon calls it satire, and its targets remain disputed: xenophobia, narrow nationalism, prophetic self-importance, rigid theories of prediction, or the audience itself. “The sages accidentally canonized a joke they misunderstood” is speculation, not documented canon history.

**Psalm.** Jonah 2:3–10 is a thanksgiving psalm embedded in the prose. Its speaker describes deliverance as already accomplished even while inside the fish. Some scholars regard it as an older independent psalm inserted by an editor; others read the disjunction as deliberate irony. No manuscript preserves a Jonah narrative without it, so interpolation remains literary reconstruction.

### 2.3 Material witnesses

Fragments of several copies of the Twelve preserve Jonah:

- **4Q76/4QXIIᵃ**, usually dated c. 150–125 BCE, preserves portions of Jonah 1–3.
- **4Q81/4QXIIᶠ**, roughly second–first century BCE, preserves portions of Jonah 1.
- **4Q82/4QXIIᵍ**, approximately late first century BCE, preserves parts of all four chapters.
- **8ḤevXIIgr**, the Greek Minor Prophets scroll from Naḥal Ḥever, first century BCE/CE, preserves parts of Jonah 2–4.
- **MurXII/Mur88**, Wadi Murabbaʿat, first–second century CE, preserves extensive Hebrew Jonah material.

These prove circulation within the Twelve by the late Second Temple period. They are not eyewitness records of an eighth-century mission. [Witness summary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Book_of_Jonah).

The medieval **Masoretic Text** is the principal Hebrew base. The **Septuagint**, translated before the Christian era, and the **Vulgate**, produced by Jerome around 390–405 CE, transmit consequential variants.

### 2.4 Significant textual variants

- **Jonah 3:4:** MT and later Hebrew witnesses give “forty days”; the Septuagint gives **three days**. Augustine interpreted both symbolically rather than correcting either, connecting three to resurrection and forty to Christ’s post-resurrection period. [textual variant; patristic systematization] [Septuagint study](https://ccat.sas.upenn.edu/ioscs/bulletin/volumes/bioscs20.pdf).

- **Jonah 4:6, קִיקָיוֹן, *qîqāyôn*:** the plant’s identification is uncertain. LXX has κολοκύνθη, “gourd”; Aquila reportedly used κισσός, “ivy”; Jerome transliterated/explained the unfamiliar plant but used *hedera*, “ivy,” in the Vulgate. Augustine reported that a North African congregation nearly rioted when its bishop read Jerome’s unfamiliar “ivy” in place of the established “gourd.” This is evidence for communal attachment to inherited translation, not botanical identification. [Augustine–Jerome correspondence](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/1102075.htm).

- **Fish:** Hebrew דָּג גָּדוֹל, *dāg gādôl*, “great fish.” Jonah 2:1 uses the morphologically feminine *dāgâ*, which later interpreters developed into a transfer from a male to a pregnant female fish. The grammatical alternation itself does not narrate two animals. [rabbinic elaboration built on grammar]

### 2.5 Other ancient and medieval textual witnesses

- **Sirach 49:10**, c. 2nd century BCE, refers collectively to the Twelve Prophets but does not recount Jonah.
- **Tobit 14**, in variant forms, cites either Jonah or Nahum concerning Nineveh, demonstrating textual instability and knowledge of prophetic traditions.
- **Josephus, *Antiquities* 9.208–214**, c. 93/94 CE, retells Jonah as national history. He connects the Nineveh mission to the prophet of Jeroboam’s reign and says he recounts what he found in the Hebrew books. [learned harmonization]
- **Matthew 12:38–41; 16:4** and **Luke 11:29–32**, late first century CE, make Jonah a sign. Matthew explicitly compares the fish’s three days and nights to the Son of Man in the earth; Luke emphasizes Jonah himself as a sign to Nineveh and its repentance. [later canonical Christian] [Parallel text](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew+12%3A38-41%2CLuke+11%3A29-32&version=ESV).
- **3 Maccabees 6:8**, probably first century BCE/CE, recalls Jonah’s wasting in the sea monster as one item in a catalogue of deliverances.
- **Lives of the Prophets**, a Jewish-origin Greek work with extensive Christian transmission, probably compiled in the first centuries CE, supplies origins, family movements, death, and burial otherwise absent from Scripture. Its earliest stable Greek manuscript is sixth century. [late-antique legendary biography]
- **Mishnah Taʿanit 2:1**, compiled c. 200 CE, cites Nineveh to argue that God saw not sackcloth and fasting but changed conduct.
- **Babylonian Talmud**, especially Taʿanit and Yevamot, preserves exegetical traditions; the custom of reading Jonah at Yom Kippur’s afternoon service is already attested in the Babylonian Talmud.
- **Genesis Rabbah 98**, fifth-century form with older materials, debates Jonah’s tribal affiliation.
- **Pirke de-Rabbi Eliezer 9–10, 33**, probably eighth or ninth century, gives the elaborate maritime and Leviathan cycle.
- **Yalkut Shimoni**, thirteenth century, and the composite **Midrash Jonah** collect further traditions.
- **Ginzberg, *Legends of the Jews* (1909–1938)** synthesizes rabbinic material; it is an invaluable index to traditions but a modern compilation, not an ancient source.
- **Qurʾan 4:163; 6:86; 10:98; 21:87–88; 37:139–148; 68:48–50**, early seventh century CE, names Yūnus, the people of Yūnus, and the “man of the fish.”
- **Al-Ṭabarī**, d. 923; **al-Thaʿlabī**, d. 1035; and works attributed to **al-Kisāʾī**, probably medieval, integrate Qurʾanic notices with biblical, exegetical, and legendary materials in *tafsīr* and *qiṣaṣ al-anbiyāʾ*. [Islamic learned elaboration]

The named pseudepigrapha **1 Enoch, Jubilees, Life of Adam and Eve, and Testaments of the Twelve Patriarchs contain no sustained Jonah narrative**. Their absence is important: Jonah did not acquire an extensive Second Temple pseudepigraphic cycle comparable to Enoch, Adam, or the patriarchs.

## 3. The Story, Episode by Episode

### 3.1 The historical oracle

In 2 Kings 14:25 Jonah predicts Israel’s territorial recovery under Jeroboam II. The narrator attributes the recovery to divine compassion despite the king’s wrongdoing. [early canonical]

Rabbi Akiva’s interpretation in Yevamot 98a limits God’s speech to Jonah to the two commissions explicitly counted in Jonah 3:1. It consequently reads the Kings notice analogically rather than as a third separate prophecy. Other traditions simply treat all notices as episodes in one prophetic career. [rabbinic systematization]

### 3.2 Commission and flight

YHWH orders Jonah to “arise, go to Nineveh, that great city, and cry against it.” Jonah instead descends to Joppa and pays passage to Tarshish. Tarshish is not securely identified here; proposals include Tartessos in Iberia and other western Mediterranean destinations. Its narrative function is unequivocal: movement away from the eastern destination and “from the presence of YHWH.” [early canonical]

Later Jewish interpretation says Jonah feared that Gentile repentance would expose Israel’s failure, or that a conditional prophecy would make him appear false. Pirke de-Rabbi Eliezer adds that he sought a region where the divine presence was thought not to rest. These motives expand Jonah 4:2; they are not stated at the outset by the biblical narrator. [rabbinic elaboration]

### 3.3 Storm, prayers, and lot

YHWH hurls a wind; the sailors cry to their individual gods and throw cargo overboard, while Jonah sleeps below. The captain wakes him. Lots identify Jonah. He calls himself a Hebrew who fears YHWH, “God of heaven, who made the sea and dry land,” and admits flight. The sailors row toward shore before reluctantly throwing him overboard; the sea stops, and they fear YHWH, sacrifice, and vow vows. [early canonical]

The foreigners act more responsibly than the prophet: they pray, seek information, attempt rescue, and fear killing an innocent man. Whether this constitutes satire of prophetic or Israelite self-importance remains interpretive.

Pirke de-Rabbi Eliezer makes the crew representatives of the **seventy nations**, each holding his god, and dramatizes repeated partial immersions before they finally release Jonah. [rabbinic elaboration] [Text](https://www.sefaria.org/Pirkei_DeRabbi_Eliezer.3-11).

### 3.4 Great fish and three days

YHWH “appoints” a great fish. Jonah remains in its belly three days and nights. The story describes divinely sustained confinement, not chewing, digestion, or Jonah’s death. The common “whale” is a translation and iconographic convention.

The psalm invokes Sheol, the deep, seaweed, mountains, and the barred earth. These can describe near-death poetically; Christian interpretation later made the episode a descent-and-resurrection type.

Later Jewish cycles greatly expand it:

- the fish was created or appointed during creation;
- its eyes were windows and a jewel illuminated the interior;
- it carried Jonah through the cosmic ocean, showing him the Reed Sea, foundations of earth, Sheol, Gehinnom, and the place beneath the Temple;
- the fish feared being eaten by **Leviathan**;
- Jonah promised to bring Leviathan to the eschatological banquet, displayed Abraham’s covenantal seal, and drove it away;
- exegetes turned the grammatical change from masculine fish to feminine fish into a transfer to a cramped pregnant fish that forced Jonah to pray.

These are medieval rabbinic narrative exegesis, chiefly Pirke de-Rabbi Eliezer and later compilations, not suppressed verses of ancient Jonah. [Expanded text](https://www.sefaria.org/Pirkei_DeRabbi_Eliezer.10.1-10).

### 3.5 Prayer and expulsion

Jonah’s psalm thanks God, promises sacrifice, and concludes that deliverance belongs to YHWH. God speaks to the fish; it vomits Jonah onto dry land. No canonical text names the landing place. Locations advertised as Jonah’s landing are regional traditions.

### 3.6 Nineveh’s repentance

At the renewed commission Jonah goes to Nineveh. The city is called “great to God” and a “three-days’ walk.” He enters one day and proclaims: “Forty days more, and Nineveh is overthrown” (LXX: three days). The verb *nehpāket* can mean physically overturned or transformed, enabling later wordplay, but the immediate warning is destructive.

Ninevites believe God, proclaim a fast, and wear sackcloth. Their unnamed king leaves his throne, covers himself with sackcloth, and sits in ashes. His decree requires humans and animals to fast, wear sackcloth, cry out, and abandon violence. God sees their deeds and relents. Neither Assyrian records nor archaeology corroborates a citywide royal conversion or animal fast.

The story’s **“king of Nineveh”** is historically imprecise: Neo-Assyrian monarchs were kings of Assyria, and Nineveh did not become the principal royal capital until after the historical setting suggested by Jeroboam II. Apologetic comparisons with plagues, eclipses, or temporary Assyrian weakness establish only possible background, not evidence that Jonah caused a repentance.

The Qurʾan makes the “people of Yūnus” uniquely profitable believers whose faith averts punishment (10:98). [Islamic canonical] [Arabic and translation](https://quran.com/en/jonas/98).

### 3.7 Anger, plant, worm, and question

Jonah is furious because God relents. He says this is why he fled: he knew YHWH was gracious, compassionate, slow to anger, abundant in loyalty, and relenting from harm—a citation-like reversal of Israel’s traditional confession of mercy. Jonah asks to die.

Outside the city he builds a booth and waits. God appoints the *qîqāyôn* plant, then a worm that destroys it, and finally a scorching east wind. Jonah again asks for death. God contrasts Jonah’s pity for a plant he neither cultivated nor sustained with divine pity for Nineveh’s more than 120,000 morally uncomprehending people “and many animals.”

The book ends with God’s question. It supplies no repentance, conversion, death, or answer for Jonah. Liturgical and homiletic continuations that make him assent close a deliberately open ending.

### 3.8 Family, later career, death, and burial variants

Scripture gives only Amittai as father and Gath-hepher as place of origin.

Competing postbiblical accounts include:

- **Tribe of Zebulun:** inferred from Gath-hepher’s territory. [rabbinic]
- **Tribe of Asher:** another rabbinic assignment; harmonizers made his father Zebulunite and mother Asherite.
- **Son of the widow of Zarephath revived by Elijah:** Pirke de-Rabbi Eliezer 33 and related traditions. Genesis Rabbah reasons that the widow was non-Israelite because Elijah touched the child. [rabbinic elaboration]
- **Disciple of Elisha and anointer of Jehu:** medieval Jewish commentary identifies the unnamed “son of the prophets” in 2 Kings 9 with Jonah. Scripture does not. [learned identification]
- **Long life:** more than 120 or 130 years in chronographic traditions.
- **Never died:** Ecclesiastes Rabbah reportedly says the widow’s son did not die, contingent upon his identification with Jonah.
- **Lives of the Prophets:** Jonah returns with his mother to the region of Tyre, later settles near the Oak of Deborah, dies, and is buried in a cave associated with Kenaz. Recensions differ. [late-antique legendary biography]
- **Mosul/Nineveh, Mashhad/Gath-hepher, Halhul, and other tombs:** mutually incompatible local claims without recoverable human remains securely attributable to Jonah. [regional sacred geography]

### 4.2 Every documented interaction with another named divine being or hero

Named collectives and anonymous persons—the sailors, captain, fish, king, Ninevites, worm—are excluded because the requested cross-reference requires named figures.

- **YHWH / the God of Israel** — commissions Jonah twice, sends the storm, appoints fish/plant/worm/wind, hears his prayer, spares Nineveh, and questions him. **Sources:** 2 Kings 14:25; Jonah 1–4, texts composed/transmitted in the first millennium BCE. **Tier:** early canonical within Israelite tradition.

- **Jeroboam II** — Jonah’s oracle is said to authorize or predict the king’s restoration of Israel’s border. **Source:** 2 Kings 14:23–27, narrative setting eighth century BCE; later literary transmission. **Tier:** early canonical; possible historical context, no independent attestation of interaction.

- **Elijah** — later identified as the prophet who revived Jonah when Jonah was the widow of Zarephath’s dead son. **Sources:** Pirke de-Rabbi Eliezer 33 and related medieval traditions; 1 Kings 17 itself never names the child. **Tier:** rabbinic elaboration.

- **Elisha** — later made Jonah’s prophetic master; some commentators identify Jonah with the unnamed messenger sent by Elisha to anoint Jehu. **Source:** commentary on 2 Kings 9 and later Jewish legend. **Tier:** learned identification, absent from the canonical account.

- **Jehu** — Jonah allegedly anoints him when identified with the unnamed prophetic emissary of 2 Kings 9. **Source:** medieval Jewish commentary. **Tier:** learned identification.

- **Leviathan** — the fish tells Jonah it is due to be eaten by Leviathan; Jonah confronts or frightens Leviathan and vows to bring it to the righteous. **Source:** Pirke de-Rabbi Eliezer 10, c. eighth–ninth century. **Tier:** rabbinic elaboration; Leviathan is not named in biblical Jonah.

- **Jesus** — invokes Jonah as the only “sign” for his generation; Matthew equates Jonah’s three days in the fish with the Son of Man’s time in the earth, while Luke makes Jonah himself a sign to Nineveh. **Sources:** Matthew 12:39–41; 16:4; Luke 11:29–32, late first century CE. **Tier:** later canonical Christian interpretation.

- **The Son of Man** — explicitly compared with Jonah in Matthew 12:40. Whether the phrase is simply Jesus’ self-reference or an apocalyptic title is a separate New Testament question. **Tier:** Christian canonical typology.

- **Muhammad** — no Qurʾanic scene depicts a meeting; Muhammad is said to have received revelation like Noah and Jonah (Qurʾan 4:163), and Qurʾan 68:48 warns Muhammad not to be like the “companion of the fish” in impatient distress. **Tier:** Islamic canonical comparison, not narrative interaction.

- **Addās** — in Ibn Isḥāq/Ibn Hishām’s prophetic biography, the Christian servant Addās recognizes Muhammad after Muhammad identifies Jonah son of Amittai as his brother-prophet. Jonah is discussed, not physically present. **Tier:** early Islamic sīra, learned comparison rather than interaction.

No secure source records Jonah interacting with Adam, Eve, Noah, Abraham, Isaac, Jacob, Moses, Aaron, David, Solomon, Isaiah, Jeremiah, Ezekiel, Daniel, Enoch, Job, or the Twelve Patriarchs. Modern charts that insert such relationships are genealogical or thematic constructions.

## 5. Cult, Tomb, Ritual, and Images

### 5.1 Jewish ritual

Jonah is the haftarah at the **afternoon service of Yom Kippur**. The Babylonian Talmud already attests the selection, making this a late-antique or earlier Jewish liturgical tradition. Its themes are repentance, the efficacy of changed conduct, divine mercy, and the possibility that non-Israelites may repent more promptly than Israel. The liturgical reading commonly appends Micah 7:18–20, supplying words of mercy after Jonah’s unanswered ending. [History and interpretation](https://www.thetorah.com/article/silencing-jonah-the-zealot-on-yom-kippur).

Jonah had no securely documented First Temple sacrifice, priesthood, annual Israelite festival, or archaeologically identified *heroon*. Yom Kippur reading is veneration of a scriptural text, not sacrifice to Jonah.

### 5.2 Christian commemoration and imagery

The “sign of Jonah” made him one of the most frequent biblical figures in early Christian funerary art:

- catacomb paintings from approximately the late second through fourth centuries;
- carved sarcophagi, lamps, gold glass, and mosaics;
- a common three-scene cycle: Jonah cast into the sea, expelled by the monster, and reclining beneath the plant;
- the reclining Jonah often borrows the pose of the pagan Endymion, illustrating formal continuity rather than worship of Endymion;
- the cycle signifies deliverance from death, Christ’s resurrection, and by extension the deceased Christian’s hoped-for resurrection.

A Roman sarcophagus fragment dated to the end of the third century depicts Jonah and the sea monster. [Princeton University Art Museum](https://artmuseum.princeton.edu/art/collections/objects/33917). The broader Index of Medieval Art catalogs Jonah across manuscripts, sculpture, mosaics, and other media. [Index description](https://www.libraries.rutgers.edu/databases/christian_art).

Jonah appears in Orthodox, Catholic, Coptic, Syriac, and Armenian calendars, though dates differ. This is saint-like liturgical commemoration, not evidence of a historical tomb.

### 5.3 Mystery plays and medieval typology

Medieval biblical drama staged the storm, sailors, monster, Nineveh, and repentance. English cycle traditions such as the York, Chester, and Towneley-related biblical theatre use prophets and prefigurations within salvation history, though surviving cycle contents differ and not every cycle preserves an independent Jonah play. Illustrated *Biblia pauperum* traditions juxtapose Jonah’s expulsion or confinement with Christ’s entombment and resurrection. [medieval Christian typology]

### 5.4 Tomb traditions

#### Gath-hepher/Mashhad

A tomb near Mashhad in Galilee has been identified locally with Jonah, fitting 2 Kings’ Gath-hepher provenance. Medieval Jewish travelers report a Jonah shrine in the region. Archaeology has not authenticated a burial belonging to Jonah son of Amittai. [regional tradition]

#### Mosul: Nebi Yunus

Tell Nebi Yunus is one of ancient Nineveh’s principal mounds. A Christian monastery was established over Assyrian palace remains in the first millennium CE and was later converted into an Islamic shrine and mosque containing Jonah’s reputed tomb. The shrine became important to local Muslims and was also respected in Christian and wider regional memory.

ISIL demolished the mosque and shrine on **24 July 2014**, condemning the tomb cult as illegitimate and simultaneously exploiting and damaging the archaeological mound. The destruction was an extremist political-theological act against local religious heritage, not archaeological proof for or against Jonah. [Digital recovery project](https://orbilu.uni.lu/handle/10993/65528).

Looting tunnels exposed remains of an Assyrian palace, including monumental inscriptions and reliefs. Those remains establish Neo-Assyrian occupation and royal building, not Jonah’s grave. [Heidelberg investigation](https://ninive.uni-heidelberg.de/ny_tunnels_en.html).

#### Other claims

Halhul near Hebron and several locations in the Levant possess competing Jonah/Yūnus tomb traditions. Their plurality is itself evidence of mobile sacred geography. None has a securely proven chain of custody to an eighth-century body.

### 5.5 Islamic veneration

Yūnus is honored as a prophet. Mosques, shrines, villages, and personal names commemorate him. His supplication from Qurʾan 21:87—“There is no deity but You; glory be to You; indeed I was among the wrongdoers”—has a major devotional life independent of tomb pilgrimage. Sunni and Shiʿi traditions both honor Yūnus, although attitudes to visitation of prophet shrines vary.

## 6. Historical Evolution

### Eighth–sixth centuries BCE: name and remembered oracle

The Jonah of 2 Kings belongs to northern prophetic memory connected to Jeroboam II. The dating of the written notice is later than its narrative setting, and no contemporary inscription names him.

### Sixth–third centuries BCE: literary transformation

A writer appropriated “Jonah son of Amittai” for a tale about prophecy, flight, foreign piety, repentance, and divine freedom. The exact date and ideological opponent cannot be fixed. The book was incorporated into the Twelve before its Qumran witnesses.

### Second century BCE–first century CE: scriptural circulation

Qumran and Judaean Desert manuscripts attest Hebrew and Greek Jonah. Sirach’s Twelve, Tobit’s Nineveh traditions, 3 Maccabees, Josephus, and the Gospels show expanding authority and interpretation.

### First–fourth centuries CE: typology and funerary salvation

Matthew’s death/resurrection analogy and Luke’s repentance sign anchor Christian exegesis. Catacomb and sarcophagus imagery turns Jonah into a visual emblem of deliverance from death. Jewish fasting traditions cite Nineveh as behavioral repentance.

### Fourth–sixth centuries: translation controversy and commentary

Jerome’s Hebrew-based translation of *qîqāyôn* as ivy disrupts the familiar gourd and provokes Augustine’s defense of communal textual usage. Patristic commentators make the fish, expulsion, plant, and Nineveh a system of Christological, ecclesiological, and moral types.

### Seventh–tenth centuries: Qurʾanic prophet and rabbinic sea voyager

The Qurʾan reframes Yūnus around prophetic impatience, supplication, election, and the exceptional salvation of his people. Pirke de-Rabbi Eliezer expands the fish episode into cosmic travel and combat with Leviathan. Neither development is evidence for an original lost chapter.

### Eleventh–fifteenth centuries: *qiṣaṣ*, shrines, glosses, and image cycles

Islamic storytellers combine Qurʾan, exegesis, and inherited biblical lore. Christian glosses stabilize allegorical readings; manuscript and monumental art spreads Jonah’s iconography. Regional tombs acquire pilgrimage histories.

### Sixteenth–eighteenth centuries: confessional exegesis and natural history

Reformation and Catholic commentators debate literal history, translation, and typology. Early naturalists speculated about whale species and whether a human could survive swallowing—questions produced by treating “great fish” as zoological reportage. They are not questions the Hebrew tale itself resolves.

### Nineteenth century: philology, comparative myth, and literary monument

Historical criticism separates the eighth-century setting from the book’s later composition. Comparative scholars propose links with Greek sea-monster myths, including Heracles, Perseus, and Jason. Shared motifs are real at a broad level, but direct literary dependence has not been demonstrated. Melville gives Jonah a defining place in the modern maritime imagination.

### Twentieth–twenty-first centuries: literary plurality, archaeology, and contested heritage

Form, narrative, feminist, psychoanalytic, ecological, postcolonial, and humor studies displace the older fixation on whale biology. Jonah becomes a figure of trauma, vocation, evasion, nationalism, divine mercy, ecological kinship, and the ethics of enemies. The 2014 destruction of Nebi Yunus and subsequent archaeological documentation make Jonah part of contemporary debates about extremism, plural heritage, looting, and reconstruction.

## 7. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Literature

- **Herman Melville, *Moby-Dick* (1851), chapters 8–9:** Father Mapple’s ship-shaped pulpit and sermon retell Jonah as the supreme lesson in obeying God rather than one’s own judgment. Mapple’s Jonah tries “to flee from God,” and his deliverance follows repentance and submission. The sermon foreshadows the novel’s doomed maritime resistance while not simply equating Ahab with Jonah. [Primary text](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/2489/2489-h/2489-h.htm).

- **Carlo Collodi, *The Adventures of Pinocchio* (1881–1883):** Pinocchio’s encounter with the giant Dogfish and reunion with Geppetto participates in the Jonah-like swallowed-and-restored motif, although the immediate literary lineage also includes folktale and adventure traditions.

- Twentieth-century writers repeatedly employ Jonah as the fugitive intellectual, reluctant witness, survivor, or enclosed consciousness. A modern survey traces this reception across novels and poetry. [Study](https://www.mdpi.com/2077-1444/13/7/661).

### Music, theatre, and film

- Medieval liturgical drama and mystery plays established Jonah as a spectacle of storm, monster, repentance, and resurrection.
- Giacomo Carissimi’s seventeenth-century oratorio **Jonas** belongs to the Latin sacred dramatic tradition.
- The story became a staple of illustrated children’s Bibles, animation, Sunday-school theatre, and religious film.
- **Jonah: A VeggieTales Movie** (2002) translates the story into comic animation and explicitly moralizes compassion and second chances.
- Pinocchio films, especially Disney’s **Pinocchio** (1940), helped fuse the popular image of biblical Jonah with a literal whale, even though Collodi’s creature was a *pesce-cane* and biblical Hebrew says “great fish.”

### Visual art

Major examples include:

- early Christian catacomb cycles and sarcophagi;
- medieval manuscript illumination and sculptural typology;
- Michelangelo’s monumental **Jonah** over the altar of the Sistine Chapel (c. 1511–1512), positioned in relation to resurrection and Last Judgment imagery;
- Pieter Lastman and other early-modern painters depicting Jonah and the whale;
- Gustave Doré’s biblical illustrations (1860s);
- Marc Chagall’s twentieth-century biblical works.

The familiar enormous whale with a horizontal mouth is largely an iconographic convention. Ancient Christian monsters often have coiling bodies and dragon-like heads; Islamic painting may instead show a large fish amid angels and vegetation.

### Psychology and comparative mythology

- **Otto Rank**, *The Myth of the Birth of the Hero* (1909), helped establish psychoanalytic comparison of enclosure, abandonment, and rebirth, though Jonah does not fit the royal-child pattern cleanly.
- **Lord Raglan**, *The Hero* (1936), constructed a recurring heroic biography. Jonah matches few of its royal and sacrificial criteria; forcing him into the full pattern erases the tale’s prophetic specificity.
- **Joseph Campbell**, *The Hero with a Thousand Faces* (1949), used “the belly of the whale” as the threshold of inward transformation in the monomyth. This is a powerful modern abstraction, not an ancient technical meaning of Jonah’s fish. Jonah’s continuing anger after expulsion also resists a neat completed transformation.
- Structuralist and psychoanalytic readings treat sea, fish, and expulsion as death/rebirth symbols. These are interpretive traditions, not recoverable authorial explanations.
- Comparisons with Jason, Perseus, Heracles, Gilgamesh, and Indian fish legends identify motifs rather than proving a single source. Claims that “Jonah is simply a rewritten pagan solar myth” exceed the evidence.

### Political and nationalist uses

Jonah has been mobilized in competing ways:

- as a critique of exclusive nationalism, because God pities an enemy imperial city;
- as a defense of missionary universalism;
- as a figure of Israel’s election and prophetic responsibility;
- as a local emblem of Mosul and Nineveh’s layered Assyrian, Christian, and Islamic past;
- in extremist iconoclasm, when ISIL characterized shrine devotion as illicit and demolished Nebi Yunus;
- in heritage reconstruction, where Iraqi and international projects treat the site as both an archaeological mound and a living locus of memory.

No single ancient reading licenses the modern slogan that Jonah is straightforwardly “anti-nationalist.” That formulation is a modern political-theological interpretation of the book’s discomfort with mercy toward Assyria.

### Modern inventions and recurrent errors

- **“The Bible says whale”** — false for Hebrew Jonah; “whale” is a later translation/artistic convention.
- **“Jonah was proven by Assyrian records”** — false; no known contemporary Assyrian text names him or records his mission.
- **“Archaeologists found Jonah’s bones in Mosul”** — unsupported.
- **“The fish was Leviathan”** — absent from biblical Jonah; Leviathan enters the expanded rabbinic story as a separate monster threatening the fish.
- **“The Nineveh eclipse proves Jonah”** — modern apologetic conjecture. The Bur-Sagale eclipse of 763 BCE is real; its connection to Jonah is not documented.
- **“Jonah died and was resurrected inside the fish”** — possible Christian typological inference, not stated by Jonah 1–2.
- **“Jonah wrote the Book of Jonah”** — traditional attribution, but the book is anonymously narrated and never claims Jonah’s authorship.
- **“All ancient Jews read Jonah literally”** — too broad; ancient sources use it as Scripture but do not provide a modern genre theory.
- **“Satire is proven fact”** — overstated. Comic, ironic, parodic, and satirical features are strongly argued; the precise genre and target remain debated.

## 8. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### 8.1 Are the two Jonahs the same?

The canonical books encourage identification through identical name and patronymic. Traditional interpretation therefore constructs one career. Critical scholarship distinguishes:

1. a remembered prophet in 2 Kings;
2. the narrator’s later literary appropriation of his name;
3. the protagonist as a deliberately shaped character.

The historical prophet may have supplied the name, but there is no evidence that he experienced the book’s adventures. Conversely, absence of external attestation cannot prove that no eighth-century Jonah existed.

### 8.2 Historicity

**Traditional-historical position:** the book recounts a miraculous but real mission; the Kings notice supplies its historical anchor; Jesus’ appeal to Jonah is treated as confirmation.

**Critical-literary position:** the tale’s hyperbole, imprecise Assyrian title, miraculous chain, stylized universal response, theological dialogue, and later Hebrew favor didactic fiction or novella. Jesus’ use demonstrates first-century scriptural reception, not independent archaeological corroboration.

**Qualified position:** the named prophet may be historical while the story is a later literary legend. This best preserves the difference between the two evidence sets without claiming more than either proves.

Archaeological disputes associated with Israel Finkelstein, William Dever, and “minimalism” concern how biblical texts may be used to reconstruct ancient Israel. They do not provide a direct archaeological test of the fish episode. Nineveh and the Neo-Assyrian Empire are abundantly historical; that does not make every story set there historical.

### 8.3 Date and language

Older arguments treated Aramaisms or allegedly late vocabulary as decisive proof of a fourth- or third-century date. Current work is more cautious because authors can archaize, dialects coexist, and the book is short. Nevertheless, the aggregate linguistic and literary evidence commonly favors post-exilic composition. A sixth-to-fourth-century range remains more defensible than precision to a particular decade.

### 8.4 Satire, parody, or prophetic critique?

Evidence for comic/satirical reading includes:

- everything “great”: city, wind, storm, fear, fish, displeasure;
- pagan sailors behave reverently while the prophet sleeps;
- animals repent in sackcloth;
- the shortest oracle produces the greatest prophetic success;
- Jonah resents the mercy implicit in Israel’s own creed;
- he pities vegetation more readily than a city;
- God grants him repeated object lessons but the text withholds his reform.

Against reducing it to satire:

- humor does not exclude religious seriousness;
- the book’s prayer and creedal language connect it deeply to worship;
- its object may be human moral rigidity rather than a specific group;
- “anti-Jewish satire” can reproduce Christian supersessionist stereotypes by making Jonah stand for supposedly legalistic Judaism.

### 8.5 Universalism and particularism

A common interpretation treats Jonah as universalist opposition to Ezra-Nehemiah’s separation from foreigners. No explicit citation links those books, and relative dating is uncertain. The tale simultaneously retains Israel’s God, prophet, scripture, and election while extending divine compassion to foreigners and animals. “Universalist” is useful if it does not erase those particularities.

### 8.6 Sources and analogues

Proposed sources include:

- Elijah and Elisha narratives;
- Jeremiah’s conditional prophecy;
- Joel’s repentance language;
- Psalms of sea distress;
- flood and sea-monster traditions;
- Greek stories of Perseus, Heracles, and Jason.

Verbal and thematic biblical intertexts are firmer than claims of direct dependence on a particular Greek myth. Motif similarity is not proof of borrowing.

### 8.7 State of evidence

Secure evidence consists of:

- the Kings notice;
- the anonymous Hebrew book;
- Second Temple manuscript fragments;
- ancient translations;
- early Jewish, Christian, and Islamic reception;
- datable artworks and shrine buildings.

Missing evidence includes:

- a contemporary inscription naming Jonah;
- an Assyrian report of Nineveh’s conversion;
- the author’s identity;
- an authenticated body or tomb;
- a manuscript preserving an earlier fishless or psalmless narrative;
- an ancient declaration of the book’s genre as satire.

### Critical controls

Robert Alter’s translation usefully preserves the Hebrew tale’s compression and repetitions. Jack M. Sasson’s Anchor Bible commentary remains central for philology and comparative evidence; the *Anchor Bible Dictionary*, *Encyclopaedia Judaica*, *Dictionary of Deities and Demons in the Bible*, James Kugel’s history of biblical interpretation, critical editions of the Twelve, and the *Encyclopaedia of Islam* provide controls on later synthesis. None should be substituted for the dated primary witnesses.

The documentary conclusion is necessarily layered: an eighth-century setting attaches to a briefly named northern prophet; a later anonymous literary masterpiece turns that name into an anti-heroic prophetic figure; Jewish, Christian, and Islamic communities then make Jonah respectively a teacher of repentance, a sign of resurrection, a divinely rescued messenger, a cosmic sea voyager, a saint-like intercessory memory, and a claimant to multiple tombs. The layers cannot be collapsed without losing the history.
