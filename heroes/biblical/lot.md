# Lot and his wife: Research Dossier

> **Traditional class:** Lot is a mortal patriarchal ancestor in Genesis; “righteous Lot” in early Christianity; and **Lūṭ, a prophet and messenger** in Islam. His unnamed wife is a mortal cautionary figure—disobedient in Genesis, an archetype of apostasy in the Qurʾan, and a named betrayer or grieving mother in later exegesis. Neither received securely attested ancient hero cult.
>
> **Earliest secure attestation:** The earliest surviving material witnesses are fragmentary manuscripts of Genesis from the Judean Desert, beginning perhaps in the third or second century BCE. No presently identified Qumran fragment preserves Genesis 19:26 itself. The underlying Lot cycle in Genesis 11–14 and 18–19 is older than those manuscripts, but its stages cannot be dated securely; most current models place Genesis’s final literary formation in the exilic or Persian periods, while allowing older narrative traditions. The Greek Pentateuch, including the Lot story, was translated in the third century BCE.  
>
> **Evidence labels used below:** **[Early textual]** securely attested ancient text; **[Later canonical]** later scriptural reuse; **[Variant]** textual or regional alternative; **[Rabbinic]**, **[Patristic]**, **[Islamic exegetical]**, **[Medieval]**, **[Folklore]**, **[Archaeological]**, **[Modern reconstruction]**, and **[Modern invention]** identify the evidence’s historical layer rather than judge its religious truth.

## 1. Identification, names and epithets

Lot is **לֹוט, Lōṭ** in the Masoretic Hebrew text, **Λώτ, Lōt** in the Septuagint, *Lot* in Latin, Syriac **ܠܘܛ, Lōṭ**, and Qurʾanic Arabic **لُوط, Lūṭ**. **[Modern philology]** Hebrew lexicography traditionally relates the name to the root לו״ט, “wrap, cover,” and the noun לוֹט, “covering/veil” (compare Isaiah 25:7). “Veil” is therefore a possible wordplay or folk etymology, not a demonstrated historical derivation of the personal name. The similar Edomite clan-name Lotan, לֹוטָן, does not establish identity with Lot.

Genesis first defines him genealogically as **Lot son of Haran**, grandson of Terah, and consequently Abram’s brother’s son—normally “nephew” in English (Genesis 11:27, 31; 12:5). **[Early textual]** The collective expressions “children/sons of Lot” in Deuteronomy 2:9, 19 and Psalm 83:8 designate Moab and Ammon and presuppose the ancestor genealogy.

No biblical epithet accompanies his name. **[Later canonical]** 2 Peter 2:7–8 calls him δίκαιος Λώτ, “righteous Lot,” three times, making righteousness his most consequential Christian epithet. **[Islamic canonical]** The Qurʾan calls Lūṭ one of the **mursalīn**, “messengers” (Q 37:133), and says God gave him **ḥukm** and **ʿilm**, “judgment and knowledge” (21:74).

Lot’s wife is unnamed in the Hebrew Bible, Septuagint, Vulgate, New Testament, and Qurʾan. Later names are separate traditions:

- **ʿIddit/Idit, עִדִּית**: Midrash Tanhuma; the word can mean choice or superior land. **[Rabbinic]**
- **ʿIrit, עִירִית**, and spellings rendered Edith/Edith or Iris: *Pirkei de-Rabbi Eliezer* 25, generally dated to the eighth or ninth century. **[Medieval Jewish]**
- **Ado/Edith**: post-biblical compilatory variants, including the medieval *Book of Jasher* tradition. **[Medieval]**
- **Wāʿila/Wālihā**: extra-Qurʾanic Muslim nomenclature; sources disagree and sometimes exchange the names assigned to Noah’s and Lot’s wives. **[Islamic exegetical]**

Her common designation “Lot’s wife”—Hebrew **אֵשֶׁת־לוֹט**, Greek **ἡ γυνὴ Λώτ**, Latin *uxor Lot*, Arabic **امرأة لوط, imraʾat Lūṭ**—is relational, not an epithet.

Later traditions identify biblical Lot and Qurʾanic Lūṭ as the same figure. **[Syncretistic identification]** This equation is already intrinsic to the Qurʾan’s late-antique scriptural discourse, although the Qurʾanic characterization is not simply a translation of Genesis: Lūṭ is a preaching prophet, and Genesis’s incest sequel disappears.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### Formation and manuscripts

**[Modern reconstruction]** Genesis is composite literature. Proposed dates for its component traditions range widely from the monarchic period through the Persian period; many scholars place its final form in the fifth or fourth century BCE. Genesis 14 is especially disputed and is often treated as a relatively independent, sometimes late, insertion. The conclusion warranted by the evidence is not “Lot lived in the Bronze Age,” but “Judean authors and transmitters placed Lot in the ancestral age.”

About twenty-five fragmentary Genesis manuscripts survive from the Judean Desert, dating from approximately the third century BCE to the second century CE. The oldest may be 4QpaleoGen^m. The surviving Genesis–Exodus manuscript 4Q11 preserves Exodus 19:24–25, not Genesis 19; similar chapter numbering has occasionally produced misleading internet claims. The known Genesis fragments do not supply an independent ancient witness to the pillar verse. [The current manuscript overview gives the oldest witnesses and their dates.](https://www.jstor.org/stable/jj.18137929)

The Septuagint Pentateuch was translated in Ptolemaic Egypt, conventionally in the third century BCE. Its Genesis 19:26 reads: **καὶ ἐπέβλεψεν ἡ γυνὴ αὐτοῦ εἰς τὰ ὀπίσω, καὶ ἐγένετο στήλη ἁλός**, “and his wife looked toward the things behind, and became a pillar of salt.” **[Early textual variant]** This makes the woman herself the grammatical subject of “became” more explicit than the terse Hebrew sequence. [Greek text and translation.](https://openlxx.com/genesis/19/)

The Vulgate has **respiciensque uxor eius post se versa est in statuam salis**, “and his wife, looking behind herself, was turned into a statue of salt.” **[Late-antique translation]** *Statua* encouraged a visibly anthropomorphic reading. [Vulgate text.](https://vulgate.net/xgenesis19)

### Hebrew Bible

The complete primary corpus concerning Lot is:

- **Genesis 11:27–32; 12:4–5**: genealogy and migration from Ur toward Haran and Canaan. **[Early textual]**
- **Genesis 13**: Abram and Lot become too wealthy for their herdsmen to coexist; Abram offers the choice; Lot selects the well-watered Jordan plain and moves toward Sodom. The narrator immediately calls Sodom’s inhabitants exceedingly wicked. **[Early textual]**
- **Genesis 14**: eastern kings capture Lot in the war of the kings; Abram mounts a household expedition, rescues him and his property, and subsequently encounters Melchizedek. **[Early textual, composition disputed]**
- **Genesis 18:16–33**: YHWH discloses Sodom’s impending judgment to Abraham; Lot is not present, but his residence motivates Abraham’s intercession. **[Early textual]**
- **Genesis 19**: angels, attempted gang rape, escape, wife’s transformation, Zoar, cave, intoxication and father–daughter incest, and the births of Moab and Ben-ammi. **[Early textual]**
- **Deuteronomy 2:9, 19**: Moabites and Ammonites are “sons of Lot,” whose territories Israel must not seize. **[Later canonical reuse]**
- **Psalm 83:8**: “children of Lot” participate in a poetic enemy coalition. **[Later canonical reuse]**

Other Hebrew-Bible condemnations of Sodom—Isaiah 1 and 13; Jeremiah 23 and 49–50; Amos 4:11; Zephaniah 2:9; Ezekiel 16:49–56—do not narrate an interaction with Lot. Ezekiel identifies Sodom’s offenses as pride, abundance, ease, failure to aid the poor, and “abominations.” These passages show that the city’s scriptural significance was broader than one sexual offense.

### Second Temple Jewish literature

- **Wisdom of Solomon 10:6–8**, Greek, probably first century BCE or CE: Wisdom rescues “a righteous man” fleeing the destruction of the five cities; a smoking wasteland and salt pillar remain as witnesses. Lot and his wife are unnamed but unmistakable. **[Later canonical/deuterocanonical]**
- **Jubilees 13 and 16**, Hebrew composition normally dated to the second century BCE and preserved substantially in Ethiopic, with Hebrew fragments at Qumran: retells Lot’s separation, capture, rescue, and Sodom’s destruction. It says “we saved Lot” because God remembered Abraham and stresses sexual pollution, but does not retell the wife’s transformation or the incest in equivalent detail. **[Second Temple elaboration]**
- **1 Enoch**, the *Life of Adam and Eve*, and most *Testaments of the Twelve Patriarchs* contain analogies to Sodom or sexual corruption rather than an independent Lot cycle. Claims that these works preserve a full alternative biography of Lot are unsupported. **[Absence finding]**
- **Testament of Naphtali 3**, in its complicated Greek/Hebrew transmission, invokes the men of Sodom as a warning against disorder; Lot is not a narrative protagonist. **[Second Temple/Christian transmission]**

### Josephus

Josephus, *Jewish Antiquities* 1.170–206, written c. 93/94 CE, paraphrases Genesis. He makes Lot’s wife’s looking continuous and curious—she kept turning to observe the city despite the prohibition—and states of the pillar, “I have seen it, and it remains to this day.” **[Early Roman Jewish elaboration/testimony]** This is evidence that a formation was being identified as the wife in Josephus’s time, not proof that the formation was a transformed human. [Loeb text.](https://s3.amazonaws.com/loebolus/L242.pdf)

### New Testament

- **Luke 17:28–32**, late first century CE: Jesus compares the sudden revelation of the Son of Man to Lot’s departure and commands, “Remember Lot’s wife.” The wife becomes an eschatological warning against turning back to possessions or former life. **[Later canonical typology]**
- **2 Peter 2:6–9**, probably late first or early second century: calls Lot righteous and distressed by lawlessness; his rescue proves God can save the godly from trial. **[Later canonical moralization]**
- **Jude 7** recalls Sodom and Gomorrah’s sexual immorality and punishment but does not name Lot. **[Later canonical reuse]**

### Rabbinic corpus

- **Genesis Rabbah 41–42, 50–51**, compiled in Roman Palestine around the fifth century, portrays Lot ambivalently: he learned hospitality from Abraham, but chose Sodom knowingly; God remembered his silence when Abraham represented Sarah as his sister. At 51:5 his wife borrows salt from neighbors specifically to expose the guests and is punished “with salt.” [Primary passage.](https://www.sefaria.org/Bereshit_Rabbah.51.5?lang=en&with=all)
- **Babylonian Talmud, Sanhedrin 109a–b**, redacted roughly fifth–sixth centuries, supplies grotesque examples of Sodomite injustice, especially hostility to strangers and the poor. [Primary passage.](https://www.sefaria.org/Sanhedrin.109b-110a)
- **Babylonian Talmud, Berakhot 54b** requires a blessing on seeing places of miracles and specifically a blessing at Lot’s wife; **Niddah 70b** treats the salt pillar as not imparting corpse impurity. **[Rabbinic ritual geography]**
- **Pirkei de-Rabbi Eliezer 25**, eighth–ninth century: names the wife Irit, says compassion for married daughters made her look back, and says she saw the Shekhinah and became salt; cattle lick the pillar by day and it regrows by morning. **[Medieval Jewish elaboration/folklore]**
- **Rashi on Genesis 19:26**, eleventh century, canonizes the measure-for-measure salt explanation in medieval Jewish commentary. [Text and translation.](https://voices.sefaria.org/sheets/738374)
- Louis Ginzberg’s *Legends of the Jews* (1909–1938) is a modern scholarly synthesis of rabbinic and medieval legends, not itself an ancient source. Its narrative of four daughters, hostile wife, salt, and Lot’s moral failings must be traced to its notes. [Digital edition.](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/1493/pg1493-images.html)

### Qurʾan and Islamic literature

Lūṭ appears by name twenty-seven times. The cycles are Q 7:80–84; 11:69–83; 15:57–77; 21:71–75; 26:160–175; 27:54–58; 29:26–35; 37:133–138; 54:33–39; and 66:10. [Concordance.](https://corpus.quran.com/search.jsp?q=lem%3AluwT+pos%3Apn)

**[Islamic canonical]** The Qurʾan makes Lūṭ a prophet sent to his people. He denounces their unprecedented **fāḥisha**, lusting after men rather than women, approaching males, “cutting the road,” and committing wrongdoing in assemblies (the emphases vary by sura). The guests are angelic messengers. The people demand them; Lūṭ offers “my daughters,” whose meaning—biological daughters or the community’s women under prophetic fatherhood—became exegetically disputed. The angels save his household except his wife, “one of those who stayed behind.” Stones of baked clay destroy the offenders. There is no pillar of salt and no father–daughter incest.

Q 66:10 makes Noah’s wife and Lot’s wife examples for unbelievers: marriage to righteous servants cannot save them because they “betrayed” their husbands. **[Islamic canonical]** Classical exegesis normally denies adultery and identifies her betrayal as unbelief and disclosure of the guests. Al-Ṭabarī, d. 923, transmits Ibn ʿAbbās’s explanation that she pointed out the guests. [Arabic text and commentary.](https://quran.ksu.edu.sa/tafseer/tabary/sura66-aya10.html)

Al-Thaʿlabī’s *ʿArāʾis al-majālis* (eleventh century) and the tales attributed to al-Kisāʾī expand the episode with names, signals used by the wife, descriptions of the cities, and angelic punishment. **[Islamic exegetical/qiṣaṣ]** These works combine Qurʾanic exegesis, transmitted reports and *isrāʾīliyyāt*; their additions are medieval, not missing Qurʾanic verses.

## 3. The story and its variants

### Kinship and migration

**[Early textual]** Genesis 11 makes Haran the father of Lot and brother of Abram and Nahor. Terah takes Abram, Sarai and Lot from Ur toward Canaan but stops in Haran. Abram subsequently brings Lot into Canaan (12:5). No mother, birth story, childhood or age is recorded.

**[Rabbinic]** Later interpreters make Lot Abraham’s disciple, servant-like companion, and imitator of his hospitality. These are character reconstructions from his migration and conduct, not biblical episodes.

### The choice of Sodom

In Genesis 13, Abram and Lot’s wealth causes pastoral conflict. Abram permits Lot first choice. Lot sees the Jordan district “like the garden of YHWH, like Egypt,” chooses it, and pitches toward Sodom. **[Early textual]** The narrator juxtaposes attractive landscape and moral danger. Lot is neither explicitly condemned for greed nor praised; both readings are interpretive. Studies of his characterization stress a progression of separation: “toward Sodom” in 13:12, resident in Sodom in 14:12, seated in its gate in 19:1. [Study of Genesis 13’s reception.](https://etheses.durham.ac.uk/id/eprint/11380/)

### Capture and rescue

Genesis 14’s coalition of eastern kings defeats the cities of the plain and captures Lot and his goods. Abram arms 318 retainers, pursues the captors, and retrieves Lot, the people and property. **[Early textual, disputed literary layer]** Nothing says Lot fought. Jubilees repeats the rescue. Later claims that Lot was a military hero are unsupported.

### Angels, hospitality and the mob

Genesis 19 calls the visitors “two angels,” although Lot treats them initially as travelers. He bows, presses hospitality upon them, prepares a feast and unleavened bread. The males of Sodom, “young and old, all the people,” surround the house and demand that Lot bring the visitors out so “we may know them.” **[Early textual]** In context, the demand is attempted gang rape and domination of strangers, not a consensual relationship.

Lot offers his two virgin daughters instead: “do to them as you please.” **[Early textual]** This is an offer of sexual violence. The narrator records no consent and gives no explicit approval. The visitors pull Lot inside and strike the assailants with **sanwērîm**, disabling blindness or dazzlement.

**[Qurʾanic transformation]** Lūṭ instead confronts his people as their prophet. His “these are my daughters” (Q 11:78; 15:71) generated two major readings: his literal daughters offered in lawful marriage, or the women of his community, since a prophet is metaphorically their father. The Qurʾan does not narrate him handing women to the mob.

### Flight and the wife

The Genesis angels command the family to flee, not stop in the plain, and not look behind. Lot hesitates; the men seize his hand, his wife’s hand and his daughters’ hands “because of YHWH’s compassion.” Lot negotiates permission to flee to the small city Bela/Zoar. **[Early textual]**

Genesis 19:24–26 says YHWH rains sulfur and fire “from YHWH out of heaven”; the wife looks back **מֵאַחֲרָיו, mēʾaḥărāyw**, “from behind him,” and becomes **נְצִיב מֶלַח, nəṣîḇ melaḥ**, “a pillar/standing formation of salt.” The text does not state her motive, name, speech, emotional state, or whether she was warned personally rather than as part of the group.

Competing explanations must remain separate:

- Disobedience or attachment to Sodom: inferred by Luke 17 and common Christian commentary. **[Later canonical interpretation]**
- Betrayal by exposing the guests through a request for salt: Genesis Rabbah 51:5. **[Rabbinic]**
- Compassion for married daughters left behind and accidental sight of the Shekhinah: *Pirkei de-Rabbi Eliezer* 25. **[Medieval Jewish]**
- Physical transformation into a continuing monument: Septuagint, Vulgate, Wisdom and Josephus. **[Ancient reception]**
- Ibn Ezra’s grammatical alternative, reported by later commentators: the land, rather than her body, became a salt formation. **[Medieval learned variant]**
- In the Qurʾan, the wife never escapes: she remains among the condemned, with unbelief and betrayal replacing the backward glance and salt. **[Islamic canonical variant]**

### Zoar, cave and daughters

After first insisting that Zoar will save him, Lot becomes afraid and leaves it for a mountain cave with his daughters. The daughters say there is no man “in the land” to continue humanity’s way. They intoxicate their father on successive nights and have intercourse with him without his awareness; both conceive. **[Early textual]**

The text depicts reproductive sexual assault of an incapacitated man. It does not say Lot initiates the sex, though later interpreters argue over whether his second intoxication implies complicity. The daughters’ belief may mean that all humanity has perished, that no acceptable men remain, or that they are isolated from marriage prospects.

The elder names her son **Moab, מוֹאָב**, explained by the narrative as “from father,” a Hebrew folk etymology; the younger names hers **Ben-ammi, בֶּן־עַמִּי**, “son of my kin/people,” ancestor of Ammon. **[Early textual ethnogenesis]** The episode is a polemical kinship story about Israel’s Transjordanian neighbors but also makes them Abrahamic relatives. Deuteronomy’s protection of their territories complicates any simple anti-Moabite reading. A linguistic study treats both names as literary explanations of incestuous descent. [Study.](https://elitzurbarashersiegal.huji.ac.il/sites/default/files/elitzurbarashersiegal/files/an_explanation_of_the_etiology_of_the_n_0.pdf)

Genesis says nothing further about Lot’s death or burial. **[Absence finding]** Stories assigning him a lifespan, a death in the Hijaz, or burial at Bani Naʿim are post-biblical local or Islamic traditions.

## 4. Relations with God, angels and other heroes

### Genealogical alternatives

**Genesis genealogy [Early textual]:**

```text
Terah
├── Abram/Abraham ── Sarah
├── Nahor
└── Haran
    └── Lot ── unnamed wife
        ├── elder daughter ── Lot → Moab → Moabites
        └── younger daughter ── Lot → Ben-ammi → Ammonites
```

Genesis 19 mentions two daughters “who are here” and sons-in-law. Whether the men are already married to other daughters or betrothed to the two present daughters is grammatically disputed.

**Genesis Rabbah and later Jewish legend [Rabbinic/Medieval]:** Lot had four daughters—two married and left in Sodom, two betrothed and rescued. This resolves the sons-in-law problem and explains the wife’s backward glance but is not explicit in Genesis.

**Qurʾanic genealogy [Islamic canonical]:** Lūṭ is linked to Ibrāhīm—Q 29:26 says Lūṭ believed him—and has a wife and a household. The Qurʾan does not name his father, daughters, descendants, Moab or Ammon and omits the incest genealogy.

### Documented interactions: one line per named divine being or heroic figure

- **YHWH/God** — delivers Lot because he remembers Abraham; destroys the cities; in Islamic reception appoints Lūṭ as prophet and saves him except for his wife. Genesis 19:16, 29, Persian-period final form; Q 7 etc., seventh century CE. **[Early textual / Islamic canonical]**
- **Abraham/Abram/Ibrāhīm** — uncle, migration companion, senior herdsman, rescuer and indirect intercessor; Lūṭ believes in Ibrāhīm in Q 29:26. Genesis 11–14, 18–19; Jubilees 13, 16; Qurʾan 29:26. **[Early textual / Second Temple / Islamic canonical]**
- **Sarah/Sarai** — travels with Lot from Haran; no direct conversation is preserved. Genesis 11:31; 12:5. Genesis Rabbah says Lot’s silence concerning Abraham’s “she is my sister” claim later merits rescue. **[Early textual / Rabbinic]**
- **Terah** — grandfather and leader of the migration from Ur to Haran. Genesis 11:31. **[Early textual]**
- **Haran** — Lot’s father; no narrated interaction, since Haran dies before Terah’s migration. Genesis 11:27–31. **[Early textual; absence of interaction]**
- **Chedorlaomer** — coalition leader whose forces take Lot captive after defeating Sodom. Genesis 14:5, 12, 17. **[Early textual, literary date disputed]**
- **Amraphel** — allied king in the campaign resulting in Lot’s capture; no personal meeting stated. Genesis 14:1, 9. **[Early textual, disputed]**
- **Arioch** — allied king in the campaign resulting in Lot’s capture; no personal meeting stated. Genesis 14:1, 9. **[Early textual, disputed]**
- **Tidal** — allied king in the campaign resulting in Lot’s capture; no personal meeting stated. Genesis 14:1, 9. **[Early textual, disputed]**
- **Bera king of Sodom** — Lot resides in his city and is taken during its defeat; no direct dialogue between them survives. Genesis 14:2, 12, 17. **[Early textual, disputed]**
- **Birsha king of Gomorrah** — participant in the war leading to Lot’s capture; no direct encounter. Genesis 14:2. **[Early textual, disputed]**
- **Shinab king of Admah** — same indirect wartime connection. Genesis 14:2. **[Early textual, disputed]**
- **Shemeber king of Zeboiim** — same indirect wartime connection. Genesis 14:2. **[Early textual, disputed]**
- **Melchizedek** — appears to Abraham after Abraham rescues Lot, but the text records no meeting with Lot. Genesis 14:18–20. **[Early textual; famous association only indirect]**
- **Mamre, Eshcol and Aner** — Abraham’s allies in the expedition that rescues Lot; Genesis does not narrate direct speech with Lot. Genesis 14:13, 24. **[Early textual]**
- **Moab** — Lot’s son and grandson by the elder daughter; no later interaction is narrated. Genesis 19:37. **[Early textual ethnogenesis]**
- **Ben-ammi** — Lot’s son and grandson by the younger daughter; no later interaction is narrated. Genesis 19:38. **[Early textual ethnogenesis]**
- **Jesus** — invokes Lot’s departure and commands hearers to remember Lot’s wife; no story-world meeting is claimed. Luke 17:28–32, late first century. **[Later canonical typology]**
- **Noah/Nūḥ** — Lot’s wife is paired with Noah’s wife as an example of unbelief in Q 66:10; no personal meeting. **[Islamic canonical learned pairing]**
- **The Shekhinah** — Lot’s wife turns, sees the descending divine presence, and becomes salt in *Pirkei de-Rabbi Eliezer* 25. **[Medieval Jewish elaboration]**

The biblical angels are unnamed. Identifying them as Michael, Gabriel and Raphael belongs to later exegetical systematization; Genesis 18 has three visitors with Abraham, while Genesis 19 has two angels in Sodom, but the text does not name or individuate them.

## 5. Cult, tomb, pillar and ritual

### No demonstrated ancient hero cult

No Bronze- or Iron-Age inscription, sanctuary dedication, votive deposit, priesthood or festival names Lot or his wife. No securely excavated ancient tomb contains an identifying inscription. **[Archaeological absence]** Lot was remembered through scripture and sacred geography, not through a demonstrable Israelite hero cult.

### The salt pillar

Wisdom 10:7 already treats a salt pillar as visible testimony. Josephus c. 93/94 says he saw it. Irenaeus and Tertullian accepted its continuing existence; some Christian texts added the extraordinary claim that it menstruated, emphasizing a still-female living body. **[Ancient belief]**

Egeria, traveling in 384, was told the pillar had disappeared beneath Dead Sea waters and could no longer be seen. The Piacenza pilgrim, c. 570, rejected the claim that animals diminished it and said it remained as before. [Study of the competing pilgrim traditions.](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/harvard-theological-review/article/abs/lots-wife-on-the-border/DC9EB0B4C8AD97446AF5222C170B6A4A)

Benjamin of Tudela, c. 1170, located it two parasangs from the Dead Sea and reported that sheep continually licked it but it regained its shape. [Itinerary.](https://robscholtemuseum.nl/wp-content/uploads/2017/10/Itinerary-of-Benjamin-of-Tudela.pdf) Later travelers variously claimed to see it, repeated hearsay, or found nothing. These reports almost certainly do not all identify the same formation.

The currently promoted “Lot’s Wife” on Mount Sedom is a natural halite column with an anhydrite cap, left by the collapse and erosion of a salt-cave wall. **[Archaeological/geological]** A geological study proposed that its formation about four millennia ago could have generated the story. That is an etiological hypothesis, not proof of a Genesis event or proof that ancient witnesses pointed to this particular column. [Geological paper.](https://www.igs.org.il/_files/ugd/d5b382_3d4209a044634714893b4a0e2d21101e.pdf)

### Lot’s Cave at Deir ʿAin ʿAbata

At the southeastern Dead Sea, near modern Ṣāfī/Zoar in Jordan, a cave became identified as Lot’s refuge. Archaeology has documented a Byzantine monastic and pilgrimage complex, conventionally called the Sanctuary of Agios Lot, with a basilica above the cave and occupation extending into the early Islamic period. **[Archaeological late-antique cultic commemoration]** The remains attest Christian veneration of the narrative’s location, not Lot’s Bronze-Age occupancy.

### Bani Naʿim and Nabi Lūṭ

Bani Naʿim east of Hebron contains the Mosque and Maqām of Nabi Lūṭ, with a cenotaph revered as the prophet’s tomb; nearby Maqām al-Nabī Yaqīn is connected with Abraham’s view of the destruction and local footprints or prayer traditions. **[Medieval/modern Islamic sacred geography]** Islamic-period inscriptions, endowments and restoration establish sustained veneration, but not the identity of buried remains. UNESCO’s Palestinian tentative-list documentation describes the two monuments and their living significance. [UNESCO entry.](https://whc.unesco.org/en/tentativelists/6952/)

These sites have also entered the politics of archaeology, conservation and territorial control in the West Bank. Modern attempts to classify, excavate or “protect” them can privilege competing Israeli or Palestinian historical claims. That is a political afterlife of the sacred landscape, not evidence for Lot’s historicity.

## 6. Historical evolution

### Persian or early Hellenistic literary consolidation

**[Modern reconstruction]** Older local tales—an ancestor of Moab and Ammon, a destroyed-city legend, hospitality under threat and an etiological salt formation—may lie behind Genesis, but they cannot be separated and dated with confidence. In Genesis’s received form, Lot functions as Abraham’s collateral relative: separated from the promised line yet repeatedly preserved through Abraham.

### Hellenistic and Second Temple interpretation

The Septuagint clarifies the wife’s transformation. Wisdom makes the physical landscape a witness. Jubilees emphasizes sexual corruption and divine judgment. The story consequently becomes simultaneously ethnographic, moral and apocalyptic.

### Early Roman Judaism and Christianity

Josephus presents the pillar as eyewitness geography. Luke turns the wife into an eschatological imperative; 2 Peter makes Lot unequivocally righteous. Early Christian interpreters confront the morally awkward daughters through allegory: Irenaeus, *Against Heresies* 4.31, excuses Lot’s ignorance and the daughters’ belief that humanity had ended, then interprets the two daughters as two churches receiving life from one Father. [Primary text.](https://logoslibrary.org/irenaeus/heresies/431.html)

Origen acknowledges that readers condemn the incest but insists on figurative meanings; Clement of Alexandria uses the wife as a warning against returning to sensual life. Augustine repeatedly treats her immobility as the consequence of looking back: she “seasons” later Christians as salt while warning monks, celibates and converts against retreat. [Augustine’s sermon.](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/160346.htm)

### Rabbinic reshaping

Palestinian and Babylonian rabbis shift the emphasis from sex alone to Sodom’s institutional cruelty toward strangers and the poor. Lot remains morally mixed: hospitable because Abraham trained him, yet compromised by his chosen city and his offer of daughters. His wife’s salt becomes measure-for-measure punishment; later tradition humanizes her as a mother looking for abandoned children.

### Qurʾanic transformation

The Qurʾan makes Lūṭ an authoritative prophet rather than an ambiguous collateral patriarch. It suppresses the incest sequel, repeatedly stages prophetic warning and rejection, and gives the wife a theological role as unbeliever within a prophet’s household. The “people of Lūṭ” becomes a transhistorical moral category.

In classical Islamic jurisprudence, **liwāṭ**, named from Lūṭ, becomes a term for male–male anal intercourse. **[Learned linguistic development]** This usage has profoundly shaped Muslim legal and political reception but is not the meaning of the personal name.

### Medieval Jewish, Christian and Islamic worlds

Midrash, *qiṣaṣ al-anbiyāʾ*, Christian glosses and sermons supply names, motives, signals and moral explanations. The *Glossa Ordinaria* transmits patristic readings of Sodom as the world, flight as conversion and backward vision as relapse. Medieval manuscript illumination and monumental mosaics commonly show an angel leading Lot and daughters while the wife freezes behind them; the twelfth-century Monreale Cathedral mosaic is a prominent example.

Mystery-play cycles dramatized the broad biblical history, but Lot never achieved the independent dramatic prominence of Noah or Abraham. Where the episode appeared, spectacular destruction and the visible salt-body naturally dominated. Claims of a single standard medieval “Lot play” are overstated; the material survives unevenly across cycles, paraphrases and visual programs.

### Reformation and early modernity

Protestant and Catholic commentators alike used Luke’s “Remember Lot’s wife” as a warning against apostasy. Visual art increasingly concentrated on the cave scene, allowing painters to combine biblical history, landscape destruction, intoxication and eroticized female nudity. This altered popular memory: “Lot and his daughters” became a major art subject even where the wife disappeared from the canvas.

### Modernity

Historical criticism identified the Moab/Ammon story as ethnological genealogy and the pillar as an etiological legend. Archaeology tested proposed locations for Sodom but produced no inscribed identification. Modern writers, especially women poets, reversed the traditional moral gaze and treated the wife’s backward look as grief, memory, resistance or human solidarity.

## 7. Modern reception and reinvention

### Visual art

- **Albrecht Altdorfer and the Danube-school tradition**, early sixteenth century: panoramic burning landscapes place family drama against cosmic destruction. **[Early-modern reception]**
- **Lucas van Leyden, *Lot and His Daughters*, engraving, 1530**: integrates flight, burning cities and cave incest. **[Early-modern reception]**
- **Peter Paul Rubens, *Lot and His Daughters*, 1613–14**: monumental sensual treatment of intoxication and incest. [Metropolitan Museum record.](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/729898)
- **Artemisia Gentileschi, *Lot and His Daughters*, c. 1630** and **Francesco Furini, c. 1634**: Baroque psychological and erotic treatments; neither is documentary evidence for ancient costume or behavior. [Prado discussion of Furini.](https://www.museodelprado.es/en/the-collection/art-work/lot-and-his-daughters/703e2792-decb-4f67-9085-0b318dd9a0eb)

A recurring art-historical problem is the aesthetic transformation of sexual assault and incest into erotic spectacle. Modern museum catalogues increasingly identify that violence instead of describing the daughters merely as seductresses.

### Literature and poetry

- **Anna Akhmatova, “Lot’s Wife,” 1924**: rehabilitates the wife’s turn as attachment to a destroyed home and asks who will mourn her. **[Modern literary reinvention]**
- **Wisława Szymborska, “Lot’s Wife,” published in *A Large Number*, 1976**: multiplies possible motives—curiosity, grief, rebellion, fatigue—refusing the singular explanation denied by Genesis itself. **[Modern literary reinvention]**
- **Jeanette Winterson, *Oranges Are Not the Only Fruit*, 1985**, and numerous later feminist and queer readings use the wife as a figure for resisting compulsory forward movement. **[Modern reception]**
- The comparison with Orpheus and Eurydice is widespread and thematically suggestive—fatal backward looking—but no ancient source identifies the traditions or proves direct borrowing. **[Modern comparativist analogy]**

### Film and performance

- **Michael Curtiz, *Sodom und Gomorrha*, 1922**: silent spectacle freely inventing characters and political plots. **[Modern invention]**
- **Robert Aldrich, *Sodom and Gomorrah*, 1962**: presents Lot as leader of a Hebrew community, supplies named daughters and extensive political romance, and makes his wife a developed dissenter; these are screenplay inventions. [Film summary and production data.](https://www.imdb.com/title/tt0056504/)
- **James Sibley Watson and Melville Webber, *Lot in Sodom*, 1933**: avant-garde film whose stylized male bodies and spectacle have been read through queer cinema history. **[Modern reinvention]**

### Political and sexual discourse

“Sodom” and “sodomy,” and in Arabic *liwāṭ*, became legal and polemical categories detached from the narrative’s full field of violence, inhospitality, arrogance and neglect of the poor. Modern conservative movements use Lūṭ to condemn LGBTQ people; queer Muslim and Christian interpreters counter that the narrative concerns attempted rape, coercion, humiliation of strangers and social injustice rather than consensual same-sex relationships. **[Modern contested reception]** Neither side should be retrojected as the uncontested meaning of every ancient layer: Genesis and the Qurʾan have different wording and rhetorical agendas.

No significant modern nationalist movement treats Lot himself as an exclusive ethnic founder comparable to Abraham, Moses or a national epic hero. **[Absence finding]** Political appropriation instead concentrates on territorial archaeology around Sodom, Palestinian Islamic shrines, and culture-war uses of “Sodom.”

### Commercial and popular invention

Tourism markets the natural Mount Sedom pillar as the literal wife, often without distinguishing geological identification from scriptural belief. Commercial “Sodom tours,” salt souvenirs, cartoons and memes reduce the story to the pun “salty.” **[Modern commercial folklore]** Claims that a specific modern photograph shows the same object seen by Josephus are unprovable because ancient reports do not supply a uniquely traceable location or morphology.

## 8. Historiography and scholarly disputes

### Historicity

Older “biblical archaeology” often sought a Bronze-Age disaster that could verify Genesis. William F. Albright and later excavators looked toward the southern Dead Sea sites Bab edh-Dhraʿ and Numeira, whose Early Bronze destruction and cemeteries seemed suggestive. Their ancient names remain unknown; chronological and geographical problems prevent secure identification with Sodom or Gomorrah.

Maximalist-leaning scholars such as Kenneth Kitchen and William Dever allow that patriarchal traditions may preserve early social or geographical memories while rejecting simplistic artifact-to-verse equations. Minimalist and revisionist scholars—including Thomas L. Thompson, Niels Peter Lemche, Philip Davies and, in a more archaeological register, Israel Finkelstein—stress that no contemporary evidence verifies the patriarchs and that Genesis reflects first-millennium literary and ideological worlds. The sound evidentiary conclusion is agnostic: archaeology documents regional settlements and destructions, not Lot.

### Tall el-Hammam

Steven Collins proposed Tall el-Hammam northeast of the Dead Sea as Sodom. A 2021 *Scientific Reports* article further claimed a Tunguska-like airburst destroyed the city and may have generated the biblical tradition. **[Contested modern reconstruction]** Specialists criticized its excavation context, mineral identifications, image handling and impact physics. In April 2025 the journal retracted the paper, stating that methodological and interpretive errors left its conclusions unreliable. [Retracted article and notice.](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-021-97778-3) The retracted airburst hypothesis must not be cited as archaeological confirmation of Genesis.

### Composition and literary function

Hermann Gunkel classified the wife’s transformation as an etiological legend explaining a Dead Sea formation and detected layered growth in Genesis 19. Later literary scholars emphasize the chapter’s tightly linked themes of seeing, hospitality, thresholds, delay and reversal. Source division remains disputed; assigning verse 26 mechanically to a named documentary source is less secure than older handbooks imply.

Genesis 19 also resembles Judges 19: a host confronts a mob demanding a male guest and offers women instead. Scholars debate literary dependence and deliberate intertextuality. Judges produces gang rape, death and civil war; Genesis substitutes angelic intervention and cosmic destruction. The parallel demonstrates a biblical convention of sexualized violence against outsiders, not historical duplication.

### Lot’s moral character

Interpretation oscillates among three poles:

1. **Compromised opportunist:** chooses fertile Sodom, hesitates, offers daughters and becomes drunk.
2. **Righteous host:** protects strangers at mortal risk; 2 Peter makes this explicit.
3. **Dependent beneficiary:** Genesis 19:29 says he is rescued because God remembers Abraham.

The received Genesis permits all three observations but does not harmonize them.

Modern readings of Genesis 19:30–38 likewise conflict. Traditional defenses make the daughters innocent survivalists and Lot unconscious. Suspicious readings note that the narrative is transmitted from a patriarchal perspective and ask whether Lot manipulated isolation or alcohol. Such reconstructions can illuminate narrative gaps but must be labeled hypotheses; the explicit grammatical action belongs to the daughters.

### Salt, geology and testimony

The Dead Sea region naturally produces salt formations, bitumen, sulfurous odors and dramatic seismic landscapes. Those facts make an etiological origin plausible but do not identify a single historical disaster. Josephus’s “I saw it” is strong evidence for first-century sacred geography; it does not authenticate metamorphosis.

### Comparative mythology

Comparisons include:

- Baucis and Philemon: hospitable hosts spared while an inhospitable community is destroyed.
- Orpheus and Eurydice: prohibited backward look with fatal consequence.
- Niobe: grieving woman petrified.
- Flood and city-destruction traditions from Mesopotamia.

These are typological parallels. Direct borrowing is unproved unless a demonstrable transmission path and distinctive shared details can be shown.

### Textual state and losses

Genesis survives in medieval Masoretic codices, ancient translations, Samaritan Pentateuch witnesses and fragmentary Judean Desert manuscripts. The textual stability of the short wife verse is substantial, but its syntax remains terse. Jubilees survives fully only in Ethiopic, while its Hebrew fragments confirm its ancient Jewish origin. Many rabbinic and *qiṣaṣ* traditions survive in manuscripts centuries later than their attributed authorities, requiring separation of work date, reported speaker and manuscript date.

## 10. Conclusions and source assessment

The securely recoverable record begins not with a historical Lot but with texts and manuscripts. Genesis preserves an intentionally ambiguous collateral patriarch: Abraham’s nephew and beneficiary, brave host, chooser of Sodom, offerer of daughters, survivor, intoxicated father and eponymous ancestor of neighboring peoples. His wife receives only one Hebrew verse, but that narrative vacancy generated an unusually rich afterlife.

The first interpretive transformations are already ancient. The Septuagint makes the physical transformation clearer; Wisdom and Josephus turn the landscape into testimony; Luke turns her into an imperative; 2 Peter turns Lot into “the righteous”; rabbis turn salt into reciprocal justice or maternal grief; the Qurʾan turns Lot into a prophet and his wife into an unbeliever while removing both salt and incest. Christian allegory, Jewish midrash, Islamic *qiṣaṣ*, pilgrimage and visual art are not corruptions to be discarded: they are successive, datable forms of the figure.

There is no securely identified ancient tomb, body, inscription, contemporary record or cult of Lot. The late-antique and Islamic shrines are historically real places of devotion but cannot authenticate the ancestral narrative. The currently named pillar is natural halite; whether a similar formation helped generate Genesis 19:26 remains plausible but unproved. Archaeological destructions around the Dead Sea do not identify Sodom, and the widely publicized Tall el-Hammam airburst paper was retracted.

The dossier’s central negative finding is therefore important: the surviving evidence documents more than two millennia of belief about Lot and his wife, but it does not independently document their existence as Bronze-Age persons. Their demonstrable history is the history of a text, a landscape repeatedly identified, and Jewish, Christian, Islamic and modern communities continually deciding what it means to leave—and what it means to look back.
