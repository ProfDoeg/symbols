# Malinalxochitl: Research Dossier

> **Traditional class:** a migration-era *nahualli* or dangerous ritual specialist, founding ancestress of the Malinalca and mother of Copil; colonial and modern writers also call her a goddess. She is not securely attested as a mortal “hero,” a ruler with a recoverable biography, or a recipient of an independently documented cult.
>
> **Earliest secure attestation:** the separation of the Malinalca from the migrating Mexica is recorded by the anonymous *Historia de los mexicanos por sus pinturas*, compiled in Spanish about 1530–1540 from Indigenous pictorial and oral materials. It does not supply the developed personal story. The earliest surviving explicit account of Malinalxochitl as Huitzilopochtli’s sorceress sister is Diego Durán’s *Historia de las Indias de Nueva España*, completed in 1581. The cognate accounts in the *Crónica mexicana* (about 1598) and Nahuatl *Crónica Mexicayotl* (probably composed or revised around 1609) preserve a fuller common tradition, conventionally associated with the lost “Crónica X.”

## Evidentiary key

- **[E1: early colonial witness]** Sixteenth-century pictorial or alphabetic evidence derived from Indigenous informants or documents. “Early” here means early colonial, not demonstrably preconquest.
- **[E2: later canonical elaboration]** Late-sixteenth- or early-seventeenth-century narrative in which the developed legend is explicit.
- **[E3: regional or genealogical variant]** A local, non-Tenochca, or homonymous tradition not safely combinable with the principal legend.
- **[E4: learned systematization]** A colonial or modern attempt to identify, harmonize, or classify figures.
- **[E5: modern scholarly reconstruction]** A historical, structural, political, astronomical, or archaeological interpretation rather than a statement in a primary narrative.
- **[E6: modern reception or invention]** A recent retelling, image, identification, or unsupported enlargement of the ancient record.
- **[Negative finding]** A requested category for which no primary evidence has been identified.

## 1. Identification, names and epithets

Malinalxochitl belongs principally to the Mexica migration cycle. Durán, Tezozómoc’s related chronicles, Tovar, and Cristóbal del Castillo describe her as the elder sister of Huitzilopochtli who traveled with the emigrants, harmed or frightened them through sorcery, and was abandoned asleep at her brother’s direction. She and those left with her settled at Texcaltepec/Malinalco; she bore Copil, who later attempted to avenge her against Huitzilopochtli and the Mexica. Copil’s extracted heart became part of the foundation mythology of Mexico-Tenochtitlan. **[E2]** The fullest Nahuatl-Spanish text is available in the UNAM edition of the [*Crónica Mexicayotl*](https://historicas.unam.mx/publicaciones/publicadigital/libros/cronica/008c_04_02_cronicamexicayotl.pdf).

### Name

- **Classical Nahuatl:** *Malīnalxōchitl*; the chronicles frequently shorten it to *Malinalxoch* and manuscripts vary in vowel length, spelling, and doubled consonants: *Malinalxoch*, *Mallinalxoch*, *Malinálxoch*, *Malinalxóchitl*. **[E2]**
- **Components:** *malīnalli* + *xōchitl*. *Xōchitl* means “flower.” *Malīnalli* denotes twisted grass, grass or fiber used for cordage, and is connected with *malina*, “to twist.” Accordingly, responsible glosses include “malinalli-flower,” “flower of twisted grass,” or “flower of the cordage grass.” “Grass flower” is serviceable but imprecise. The lexical attestations are collected by UNAM’s [Gran Diccionario Náhuatl](https://gdn.iib.unam.mx/diccionario/malinalxochitl/188900). **[E1/E4]**
- The meanings “black-grass flower,” “desert flower,” and “goddess of desert insects” circulate online but are not literal translations established by the cited colonial narratives. **[E6]**
- **Malinalco:** normally parsed from *malīnalli* plus locative *-co*, “at/in the place of malinalli.” The chronicles instead give a narrative etiology: the place was called Malinalco because Malinalxoch came or ruled there. Those explanations need not be linguistically identical. The narrative etiology occurs explicitly in the *Crónica Mexicayotl*. **[E2]**

### Descriptive titles in the chronicles

The Nahuatl *Crónica Mexicayotl* applies several agent nouns to her:

- *tēyōllocuāni*, “eater of people’s hearts”;
- *tēcotzanāni*, “taker/eater of people’s calves”;
- *tēīxcuepāni*, approximately “turner/distorter of people’s faces or sight”;
- *teōtlaxīliāni*, a difficult term associated in this context with magical attack or casting things upon people;
- *tēcochmāmāni*, one who carries people away while they sleep;
- expressions saying she caused people to eat snakes and owls and summoned venomous or filthy creatures.

The close verbal agreement between the Nahuatl *Crónica Mexicayotl* and Spanish *Crónica mexicana* indicates a shared written ancestor, not several independent confirmations. Gabriel Kruell prints the parallel texts and discusses their manuscript relationship in [“La Crónica Mexicáyotl: versiones coloniales”](https://nahuatl.historicas.unam.mx/index.php/ecn/article/download/77713/68759/228645). **[E2; textual dependence]**

The Spanish *Crónica mexicana* expands the catalogue: her gaze kills, she consumes hearts and calves, deceives sight, carries sleepers away, brings vipers or other snakes, and employs scorpions, centipedes, and spiders; she may transform herself into a bird or other animal. Thus the thread that she “commanded scorpions and snakes” is substantially attested, but “commanded” simplifies a range of claims: she summons, handles, or sends dangerous animals against victims. **[E2]**

### Status and disputed identifications

The primary texts move between human and more-than-human categories. The Nahuatl account says *amo tlācatl*, “she was not human/a person,” calls her a great *nahualli* or witch, yet treats her as sister, mother, migrant leader, and place-founder. Tovar says she intended to make herself worshipped as a god. Modern summaries often flatten this ambiguity into “Aztec goddess.” **[E2/E4]**

Miguel León-Portilla identified Malinalxochitl with Coyolxauhqui and treated the names as different designations of Huitzilopochtli’s hostile sister. That is a **modern learned equation**, not an explicit equation in the principal migration narrative; his formulation appears in [*El Templo Mayor en la historia sagrada de los mexicas*](https://historicas.unam.mx/publicaciones/publicadigital/libros/obras_leon_portilla/434/434_04_05_templomayor.pdf). The two have analogous narrative positions—older sister opposed to Huitzilopochtli—but different sons, episodes, names, and textual careers. They should not be silently merged. **[E4/E5]**

A woman named Malinalxochitl also occurs in Acolhua genealogy as a daughter of Tlotzin and wife of a lord of Coatlichan. The *Mapa Tlotzin* tradition places a named Malinalxochitl among Chichimec-Acolhua ancestors. This is a homonymous genealogical woman, not securely the Mexica sorceress. The document is described by UNAM’s [Mapa Tlotzin catalogue](https://www.iifilologicas.unam.mx/wikfil/index.php/Tlotzin%2C_Mapa). **[E3]**

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### What the earliest evidence establishes

The colonial *Historia de los mexicanos por sus pinturas*, compiled approximately 1530–1540, says that the Tarascans and Malinalca separated from the migrating Mexica, placing the Malinalca separation after Tzompanco. It does not preserve the developed episode of the sleeping sorceress, her sibling relationship, or the animal catalogue. Federico Navarrete discusses this distinction in [“El camino migratorio de los mexicas”](https://historicas.unam.mx/publicaciones/publicadigital/libros/origenes/origen005.pdf). **[E1]**

The *Tira de la Peregrinación* or Codex Boturini, conventionally dated to the first half of the sixteenth century, represents the migration and associated peoples pictorially. Its opening group includes a Malinalca place/people sign among communities associated with the departure. It does **not securely portray or label the woman Malinalxochitl**. Reading every Malinalca glyph as her portrait is therefore unwarranted. **[E1; negative finding for personal iconography]**

### Core corpus

1. **Anonymous, *Historia de los mexicanos por sus pinturas*, Spanish, ca. 1530–1540.** Separation of the Malinalca as a group, without the full personal legend. **[E1]**

2. **Codex Boturini/Tira de la Peregrinación, pictorial-Nahuatl tradition, probably ca. 1530–1540.** Visual migration history; evidence for Malinalca collective identity, not an unambiguous portrait of Malinalxochitl. **[E1]**

3. **Diego Durán, *Historia de las Indias de Nueva España e islas de Tierra Firme*, Spanish, completed 1581.** Book I, early migration chapters: the emigrants complain to Huitzilopochtli about his sorceress sister; he orders them to leave her asleep; she laments the betrayal, settles and becomes the foundress of Malinalco, and bears Copil. Durán adds that Malinalca were still considered witches because they inherited the arts of their founding lady. The manuscript and its component dates are described by the [Biblioteca Nacional de España](https://www.bne.es/es/colecciones/manuscritos/manuscritos-historicos-genealogicos/historia-indias-nueva-espana). **[E2]**

4. **Juan de Tovar, *Relación del origen de los indios que habitan esta Nueva España*, Spanish, ca. 1580s.** A close cognate: she is an extremely harmful sorceress who wished eventually to be worshipped; Huitzilopochtli contrasts conquest through her enchantment with his own program of conquest by weapons and courage. Tovar, Durán, and the Tezozómoc complex are not independent witnesses at every point. **[E2]**

5. **Hernando Alvarado Tezozómoc or the textual complex attached to him, *Crónica mexicana*, Spanish, ca. 1598.** The fullest Spanish description of her magical assaults and animals, her abandonment, the foundation of Malinalco, Copil’s revenge, and his death. The complete work is catalogued and reproduced by the [Biblioteca Virtual Miguel de Cervantes](https://www.cervantesvirtual.com/portales/hagiografia_catalana/obra/cronica-mexicana-escrita-hacia-el-ano-de-1598-929707/). **[E2]**

6. **Tezozómoc/Chimalpahin textual complex, *Crónica Mexicayotl*, Nahuatl, probably ca. 1609 in its surviving form.** It preserves the crucial Indigenous-language terminology and dates Copil’s return to 1 Calli, glossed by the colonial editor as 1285. The surviving text is transmitted in the *Codex Chimalpahin* manuscript complex; the familiar 1949/1975/1998 editions descend from later copies, while modern criticism privileges Cambridge MS 374. Authorship and the extent of Chimalpahin’s intervention remain disputed. **[E2]**

7. **Cristóbal del Castillo, migration histories, Nahuatl, late sixteenth century; preserved in later copies.** Belongs to the broader migration corpus and supplies related material about Mexica origins and the Copil conflict, but its exact relation to the “Crónica X” family requires source criticism. **[E2]**

8. **Chimalpahin’s histories, Nahuatl and Spanish, early seventeenth century.** Preserve or copy related material, sometimes placing Malinalxochitl and Copil within wider central-Mexican regional chronology. **[E2/E3]**

### Named corpora in which she is absent or marginal

- **Sahagún’s *Historia general*/Florentine Codex, completed 1577:** indispensable for Mexica religion and the Coatepec-Coyolxauhqui myth, but not a principal witness to the personal Malinalxochitl migration cycle. Sahagún’s statements about Malinalca/Ocuilteca sorcery are contextual, not an independent full biography. **[Negative finding]**
- **Anales de Cuauhtitlan, mid-sixteenth-century Nahuatl annals:** important for Toltec and Chichimec history and for movements into Ocuillan/Malinalco, but no securely identified developed Malinalxochitl story was found in the searchable UNAM text. The text is available through [Temoa/UNAM](https://temoa.iib.unam.mx/cuauhtitlan-anales). **[Negative finding]**
- **Leyenda de los Soles, 1558 manuscript:** contains cosmogonic and heroic cycles used comparatively by Graulich, but it is not a primary narrative of Malinalxochitl. **[Negative finding]**
- **Historia Tolteca-Chichimeca, ca. 1547–1560:** records a place named Popocatépetl Malinalo near Ocopipilla/Xillotepec. This may illuminate competing geography, but does not itself securely narrate Huitzilopochtli’s sister. **[E1/E3]**
- **Ixtlilxochitl:** his Acolhua histories and associated pictorial-genealogical tradition contain homonymous Malinalxochitl figures and regional material; these must not be merged automatically with the Mexica figure. **[E3]**
- **Nicholson’s *Topiltzin Quetzalcoatl* (2001), Davies’s Toltec studies, and Carrasco’s Quetzalcoatl scholarship:** methodological and comparative works on Toltec traditions, source stratification, and historicity, not primary evidence that Malinalxochitl was a “Toltec hero.” Nicholson’s book is described by the [Smithsonian catalogue](https://www.si.edu/object/topiltzin-quetzalcoatl-once-and-future-lord-toltecs-hb-nicholson%3Asiris_sil_716672). **[E5]**

## 3. The story

### Migration and conflict

Malinalxochitl accompanies the emigrants as Huitzilopochtli’s elder sister. No source supplies a birth story, childhood, or securely named parents for her. Claims that Coatlicue was her mother are extrapolated from Huitzilopochtli’s Coatepec genealogy or modern pantheon-building, not stated in the core migration account. **[E2; negative finding for parentage]**

In Durán, the people complain that her enchantments are intolerable. Huitzilopochtli speaks through a priest’s dream and orders the company to depart secretly while she and her attendants sleep. In the *Crónica Mexicayotl*, the narrator foregrounds her nonhuman wickedness and magical predation; the whole elder generation or guardians leave her sleeping. The *Crónica mexicana* gives Huitzilopochtli an ideological speech: her office is enchantment, whereas his commission is war, bow, arrows, shield, courage, and imperial expansion. These are variants of agency:

- the people petition Huitzilopochtli: Durán;
- Huitzilopochtli rejects her because of her nature and office: *Crónica Mexicayotl*;
- the god contrasts magic with armed conquest: *Crónica mexicana*/Tovar.

They should not be harmonized into a single verbatim event. **[E2]**

Her powers include killing through sight, consuming hearts or calves, confusing perception, magically moving sleeping people, transforming into animals, and employing snakes, scorpions, centipedes, and spiders. These are accusations voiced by the victorious migration tradition, not an ethnographic inventory verified independently. **[E2]**

### Abandonment and foundation

On waking, Malinalxochitl mourns and denounces her brother’s furtive betrayal. She asks her remaining elders or guardians where they can go, since the land is already occupied. The group settles at Texcaltepec or Texcaltepeticpac, subsequently identified as Malinalco. Some summaries say she was abandoned “at Malinalco,” while the fuller texts make settlement there follow abandonment at an unspecified point on the road. **[E2]**

The *Crónica Mexicayotl* explicitly says the town bore her name because she arrived and ruled there. Durán calls her its *señora y fundadora*. This is a charter myth of Malinalca separation and identity. It is not archaeological proof that one woman founded the historical settlement. **[E2/E5]**

The geography is contested. Most reception identifies her foundation with present-day Malinalco in the State of Mexico. Tezozómoc’s Texcaltepec may instead point toward another location closer to Coatepec; the *Historia Tolteca-Chichimeca* knows a Popocatépetl Malinalo near Ocopipilla and Xillotepec. A 2020 INAH study presents this alternate geographical reconstruction and interprets the legend as memory of political division within migrating groups: [*Arqueología* 62](https://revistas.inah.gob.mx/index.php/arqueologia/article/download/19313/20792/41373). **[E3/E5]**

### Mother of Copil

Malinalxochitl is already pregnant in one version when the group reaches Texcaltepec; she later gives birth to Copil. The father is not named in the core accounts. Assertions assigning her a divine consort or a particular husband lack support from these texts. **[E2; negative finding]**

She educates Copil in resentment or charges him to avenge her. When grown, Copil travels among communities of the Basin—versions name or imply Azcapotzalco, Tlacopan, Coyoacan, Xochimilco, Chalco, and Colhuacan—warning them against the Mexica and organizing resistance. The *Crónica Mexicayotl* makes him a powerful *nahualli*, though “perhaps not so much as his mother.” **[E2]**

### Copil’s death and the foundation sign

Huitzilopochtli recognizes his nephew and confronts him. Copil identifies himself through Malinalxochitl and says he will destroy his uncle because he abandoned his mother asleep. Huitzilopochtli’s followers seize him at Tepetzinco. He is decapitated, his chest opened, and his heart removed; the head is set at the place called Acopilco. Huitzilopochtli gives the heart to the priest or god-bearer Cuauhtlequetzqui, who casts it into the reeds at a place associated with Quetzalcoatl’s former seat. From it, or at its resting place depending on the version, grows the nopal that marks Mexico-Tenochtitlan. **[E2]**

The familiar national emblem—eagle on cactus—therefore has in this narrative complex a suppressed bodily prehistory: the cactus is rooted in the sacrificed heart of Malinalxochitl’s son. The *Codex Azcatitlan* depicts Copil and Huitzilopochtli in the foundation sequence, but that is evidence for Copil’s visual reception, not an identified portrait of his mother. The relevant pictorial folio is indexed in the [Codex Azcatitlan collection](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Codex_Azcatitlan). **[E1/E2]**

### Fate of Malinalxochitl

No core source describes her death, burial, apotheosis, disappearance, or the treatment of her body. She falls out of the narrative once Copil undertakes vengeance. **[Negative finding]**

## 4. Relations with gods and other heroes

### Genealogical alternatives

| Relationship | Attestation | Assessment |
|---|---|---|
| Elder sister of Huitzilopochtli | Durán, Tovar, *Crónica mexicana*, *Crónica Mexicayotl*, late 16th–early 17th c. | Core relationship, though dependent texts share an ancestor. **[E2]** |
| Mother of Copil | Same core narrative family | Secure within the legend. **[E2]** |
| Mother-in-law/grandmother through Copil’s daughter Atzcatl Xochtzin | *Crónica Mexicayotl* names Copil’s daughter | Later genealogical elaboration. **[E2]** |
| Daughter of Coatlicue | Not stated in the core Malinalxochitl cycle | Modern harmonization from Huitzilopochtli’s separate genealogy. **[E6]** |
| Sister or identity of Coyolxauhqui | Structural analogy; León-Portilla and later retellings sometimes equate them | Learned systematization, not explicit early identity. **[E4/E5]** |
| Daughter of Tlotzin and wife of Tlacotzin of Coatlichan | Acolhua genealogical tradition/Mapa Tlotzin | Probably a homonym, not the migration sorceress. **[E3]** |

### Every documented interaction with a named divine or heroic figure

- **Huitzilopochtli** — Her elder/younger brother and migration rival. He hears complaints about her, orders or permits her abandonment while asleep, rejects enchantment in favor of warfare, later confronts and kills her son Copil. Durán (1581), Tovar (ca. 1580s), *Crónica mexicana* (ca. 1598), *Crónica Mexicayotl* (ca. 1609). **[E2]**
- **Copil** — Her son. She bears him at or after the settlement of Texcaltepec/Malinalco; his attack on the Mexica avenges her abandonment. *Crónica mexicana* and *Crónica Mexicayotl*, late 16th–early 17th c.; Durán’s cognate account. **[E2]**
- **Atzcatl Xochtzin** — Copil’s daughter and thus Malinalxochitl’s granddaughter. She is named in the *Crónica Mexicayotl*, but no direct meeting or action between the two women is narrated. **[E2; kinship only]**
- **Coyolxauhqui** — No direct meeting is narrated. Identification or pairing rests on modern comparison of two hostile elder-sister cycles. **[E4/E5; famous pairing not early]**
- **Coatlicue** — No direct interaction is documented in the core cycle. Making Coatlicue Malinalxochitl’s mother is a modern genealogical harmonization. **[E6]**
- **Quetzalcoatl/Topiltzin Quetzalcoatl** — No direct interaction with Malinalxochitl. Quetzalcoatl enters only indirectly when Copil’s heart is cast near a place where Quetzalcoatl was said to have rested. *Crónica Mexicayotl*, ca. 1609. **[E2; indirect only]**
- **Cuauhtlequetzqui** — No direct interaction with Malinalxochitl. He receives Copil’s heart from Huitzilopochtli. *Crónica Mexicayotl*, ca. 1609. **[E2; indirect only]**
- **Tlotzin and Tlacotzin of Coatlichan** — Interactions belong to a distinct Acolhua woman named Malinalxochitl, not securely to the sorceress. **[E3]**

No reliable primary source documents Malinalxochitl interacting with Tezcatlipoca, Tláloc, Xochiquetzal, Cihuacoatl, the Centzon Huitznahua, or the historical Topiltzin Quetzalcoatl. **[Negative finding]**

## 5. Cult, tomb, temple and ritual

### Cult

There is no securely identified preconquest temple, festival, priesthood, feast-day, sacrificial rite, image-type, *ixiptla*, hymn, or offering dedicated specifically to Malinalxochitl. Tovar’s assertion that she sought to be adored as a god is a narrative accusation, not evidence that the cult was established. Durán’s designation of her as Malinalco’s founding lady demonstrates ancestral prestige but not a documented liturgical institution. **[Negative finding]**

INAH’s public description calls Malinalxochitl a goddess of sorcery and explains Malinalco as “where Malinalxochitl is worshipped,” but expressly cautions that the archaeological zone takes its name from the town and has **no demonstrated connection with rituals consecrated to her**: [INAH, Zona Arqueológica de Malinalco](https://www.inah.gob.mx/zonas/135-zona-arqueologica-malinalco). This official caution is decisive against identifying every building at the site as “her temple.” **[E5]**

### Malinalco and its Mexica sanctuary

Malinalco was a Matlatzinca-region city conquered by Mexica forces under Axayacatl in campaigns of approximately 1474–1476. It then served as a tributary center, military garrison, protector of routes and an aqueduct, and a center for elite military rites. Its rock-cut Cuauhcalli or Cuauhtinchan complex was built or expanded under late Mexica rule, probably principally under Ahuizotl and Motecuhzoma II rather than Axayacatl personally. It contains eagle, jaguar/ocelot, and serpent imagery connected with military initiation and state religion. INAH summarizes the conquest and site functions in [Lugares INAH: Malinalco](https://lugares.inah.gob.mx/en/node/4437). **[Archaeologically and historically attested; not a Malinalxochitl cult]**

The distinction is essential:

1. the legend says Malinalxochitl founded Malinalco;
2. the historical town was conquered by Tenochtitlan centuries later in the narrative chronology;
3. the surviving monumental sanctuary is a late imperial Mexica military complex;
4. no inscription or securely identified image dedicates that complex to Malinalxochitl.

Calling the Cuauhcalli “the temple of Malinalxochitl” is therefore a modern conflation. **[E6 when stated as fact]**

### Tomb and relics

No tomb, bones, relics, funerary urn, or burial attributed to Malinalxochitl is recorded in the colonial corpus or securely identified archaeologically. **[Negative finding]**

### Templo Mayor

Malinalxochitl herself has no identified shrine at the Templo Mayor. The connection is mythic and genealogical: her son’s heart generates or marks the nopal foundation site, while Huitzilopochtli’s temple later occupies the sacred center. Archaeologically, the Templo Mayor was dedicated principally to Huitzilopochtli and Tláloc, with many subsidiary buildings; INAH’s overview is [here](https://templomayor.inah.gob.mx/historia/zona-arqueologica-del-templo-mayor). No excavated offering has been securely labelled “Malinalxochitl’s.” **[E2 myth; negative archaeological finding]**

## 6. Historical evolution

### Before 1521: recoverable limits

Malinalca communities, the settlement of Malinalco, and Mexica conquest of the region are historical. Whether a preconquest narrative already personified their separation as Malinalxochitl is plausible but not directly demonstrable because no surviving preconquest inscription or securely identified image names her. **[E5]**

### ca. 1530–1560: collective migration memory

Early colonial pictorial and prose histories record Malinalca among migrating or separating peoples. The *Historia de los mexicanos por sus pinturas*, Codex Boturini, and related migration pictorials establish the collective framework before the surviving full biography. **[E1]**

### 1580–1610: canonical narrative shaping

Durán, Tovar, the *Crónica mexicana*, and *Crónica Mexicayotl* produce the recognizable figure: elder sister, dangerous sorceress, abandoned sleeper, founder, mother of Copil. Their verbal agreements probably reflect a common Nahuatl history rather than four separate oral traditions. The “Crónica X” hypothesis explains Durán and Tezozómoc through a lost common source; Kruell alternatively proposes an earlier lost form of Tezozómoc’s *Crónica Mexicayotl*. **[E2/E5]**

The story serves several political functions visible in the texts:

- distinguishing Mexica armed conquest from rival ritual power;
- converting a schism among migrants into divinely mandated history;
- explaining Malinalca identity and their reputation for sorcery;
- making resistance to the Mexica originate in the vengeance of a rejected woman;
- incorporating that resistance physically into Tenochtitlan through Copil’s heart.

These are interpretations grounded in narrative structure, not proof of a particular historical incident. **[E5]**

### Colonial period after 1610

The figure survives chiefly through manuscripts, copies, and antiquarian histories rather than a documented continuing public cult. Christian authors translate *nahualli* and related Indigenous categories as “witch,” “sorceress,” “devil,” or “evil woman,” imposing moral vocabulary that cannot simply be projected backward as Indigenous theology. Yet the underlying Nahuatl text itself also presents her as dangerous and *tlahueliloc*, so colonial demonization is not the only source of negative characterization. **[E2/E5]**

### Nineteenth and twentieth centuries

Printed editions—Durán from the nineteenth century, Tezozómoc’s *Crónica mexicana* in 1878, and Adrián León’s bilingual *Crónica Mexicayotl* in 1949—made the cycle broadly accessible. Twentieth-century synthesis increasingly classified her as a “goddess,” connected her to Coyolxauhqui or lunar symbolism, and interpreted her as a displaced female cult leader, rival clan deity, or memory of political secession. **[E4/E5]**

Michel Graulich’s “Las brujas de las peregrinaciones aztecas” collects the core witnesses and analyzes Malinalxochitl alongside other hostile women in migration mythology: [Estudios de Cultura Náhuatl](https://nahuatl.historicas.unam.mx/index.php/ecn/article/download/78245/69199/230895). Graulich treats the episode comparatively and structurally; his conclusions are scholarship, not additional ancient testimony. **[E5]**

### Present

Malinalxochitl remains a civic-historical symbol at Malinalco and appears in museum education, popular mythology sites, neopagan art, and feminist revision. These revivals often reverse the chronicles’ moral polarity, presenting her as an independent ritual woman suppressed by militaristic patriarchy. That is a legitimate modern interpretation, but it is not itself an ancient variant unless supported by a primary source. **[E6]**

## 7. Modern reception and reinvention

Modern reception is smaller and less securely catalogued than that of Coyolxauhqui or La Malinche.

- **Local identity:** municipal and tourism narratives routinely call her Malinalco’s founding goddess. This develops the colonial place-foundation legend into civic heritage. **[E6]**
- **Museum/public history:** the Museo del Templo Mayor has staged educational dramatizations titled *Corazón de Cópil*, including Malinalxochitl’s dispute with Huitzilopochtli. This is theatrical reception, not reconstruction of a known preconquest drama. **[E6]**
- **Feminist/decolonial reading:** modern writers recast the abandonment as suppression of autonomous female ritual authority. This builds upon the gendered opposition in the sources but changes their evaluative standpoint. **[E5/E6]**
- **Coyolxauhqui equation:** modern diagrams and popular accounts sometimes call Malinalxochitl a lunar deity or “another name” for Coyolxauhqui. The structural parallel is real; the identity is not securely primary. **[E4/E6]**
- **Coatlicue genealogy:** internet pantheons frequently list her as Coatlicue’s daughter. No such parentage occurs in the core Malinalxochitl accounts. **[E6]**
- **Fixed iconography:** modern pictures showing a woman with a scorpion, snakes, black flowers, or a specific costume are imaginative syntheses from her animal catalogue. No securely authenticated preconquest portrait with these attributes is known. **[E6]**
- **National symbolism:** her clearest relation to Mexican national imagery is indirect, through Copil’s heart and the nopal of Tenochtitlan. She herself has not been a major state-nationalist hero comparable to Cuauhtémoc. Claims of extensive nationalist or extremist appropriation were not substantiated in the searched academic and institutional record. **[Negative finding]**
- **Commercial media:** scattered fantasy, game, social-media, and neopagan usages exist, but no single film, opera, novel, comic, or game was found that demonstrably reshaped her international image. **[Negative finding; avoid padding with unverified titles]**

## 8. Historiography and debate

### Was she historical?

No contemporaneous biography, dated inscription, burial, or portrait supports an individual historical Malinalxochitl. Historical reconstructions instead treat the story as possible memory of:

- a schism between migrant groups;
- displacement of one *calpolli* or priestly faction by Huitzilopochtli’s faction;
- Tenochca conquest or ideological subordination of Malinalca communities;
- transformation of a collective ethnonym into a female ancestor.

Rudolf van Zantwijk proposed that the Huitznahuac faction displaced a Chalman-associated group; later scholars have related the story to factional politics and the Mexica’s change of name or identity. These are testable interpretive models, not recovered events. **[E5]**

### “Crónica X” and independence of witnesses

Durán, Tovar, and the two chronicles share sequence and distinctive wording. Robert Barlow’s “Crónica X” model posited a lost Indigenous history used by Durán and Tezozómoc. Later philology complicates both attribution and direction of dependence. Kruell argues that the Nahuatl *Crónica Mexicayotl* and Spanish *Crónica mexicana* descend from a common Nahuatl text, possibly an earlier version of Tezozómoc’s work; others assign more of the surviving compilation to Chimalpahin. Consequently, agreement among these works increases confidence that the tradition circulated by the late sixteenth century, but it cannot be counted as four independent attestations. **[E5]**

### Sorceress or deity?

The sources call her sister of a god, “not human,” a transformer and *nahualli*, while also treating her as a woman who sleeps, grieves, becomes pregnant, rules, and bears a child. “Goddess” and “mortal priestess” are both reductive if asserted exclusively. Tovar’s statement that she intended to become worshipped suggests contested sacral authority, but not necessarily an established temple cult. **[E2/E5]**

### Gender and colonial vocabulary

One interpretation sees her demonization as the product of friars or patriarchal Tenochca historiography. The record supports caution but not a simple reversal:

- Durán and Tovar use Christianizing language.
- The Nahuatl *Crónica Mexicayotl* itself calls her *amo tlacatl* and *tlahueliloc* and supplies the predatory titles.
- The account is preserved through elite male, largely Tenochca-oriented textual transmission.
- Her own speech presents abandonment as treachery and preserves her grievance.

Thus “evil witch” is not neutral description, but neither is “benevolent healing goddess” an attested older version. **[E2/E5/E6]**

### Malinalxochitl and Coyolxauhqui

Gillespie, Graulich, León-Portilla, and others have observed that both cycles place an elder female relative against Huitzilopochtli and end by legitimating his supremacy. Structural comparison is strong. Literal identity is weak: the Malinalxochitl cycle concerns migration, abandonment, Malinalco, motherhood, and Copil; the Coyolxauhqui cycle concerns Coatlicue’s pregnancy, Coatepec, the Centzon Huitznahua, dismemberment, and the Templo Mayor’s sacrificial program. **[E5]**

### Archaeological limits

The late Mexica sanctuary at Malinalco confirms the town’s imperial ritual importance, not the biography or cult of its legendary foundress. The Templo Mayor confirms Huitzilopochtli’s state cult and the material performance of foundation/conquest ideology, but no find securely names Malinalxochitl. Archaeology contextualizes the legend; it has not verified her as an individual. **[Negative finding]**

## 10. Conclusions

The secure documentary nucleus is narrow but coherent: a Malinalca separation existed in early colonial migration history; by 1581 Malinalxochitl had become—or was first preserved to us as—the elder sorceress sister of Huitzilopochtli, abandoned through deception, founder of Malinalco, and mother of the avenger Copil. The expanded animal catalogue, her son’s mutilation, and the casting of his heart into the future sacred center belong to the late-sixteenth/early-seventeenth-century canonical narrative family.

What cannot presently be established is equally important. There is no secure preconquest written attestation of her personal name, no uncontested ancient portrait, no named parents or consort, no account of her death, no tomb, no archaeologically demonstrated temple or cult, and no proof that Malinalco’s surviving rock-cut sanctuary was hers. Her equation with Coyolxauhqui, classification as a moon goddess, descent from Coatlicue, and fixed scorpion-and-snake iconography are learned or modern constructions. The surviving legend is nevertheless not mere decorative folklore: it explains collective separation, encodes the defeated party’s grievance, opposes female-associated magical coercion to Huitzilopochtli’s program of armed empire, and makes the sacrificed body of Malinalxochitl’s son foundational to Tenochtitlan itself.
