# Chimalman: Research Dossier

## Evidentiary key

- **[EA] Secure early attestation:** sixteenth-century Indigenous or Indigenous-informed manuscript evidence, securely associated with the name or figure.
- **[LC] Later canonical elaboration:** a later text consolidating or expanding an inherited narrative.
- **[RV] Regional or lineage variant:** a tradition belonging to a particular polity, ethnic history, or manuscript family.
- **[LS] Learned systematization:** an antiquarian, clerical, or scholarly attempt to reconcile disparate traditions.
- **[SA] Syncretistic identification:** an explicit or implicit equation with a Christian or other religious figure.
- **[CA] Colonial accretion:** material first securely documented after the conquest, whether Indigenous, Christian, or hybrid.
- **[F] Folklore:** documented post-conquest oral or popular tradition.
- **[MSR] Modern scholarly reconstruction:** an analytical proposition, not a statement made directly by a primary source.
- **[MI] Modern invention or unsupported circulation:** a recent claim lacking an identified primary attestation.

## 1. Identification, names, and class

**Class:** Chimalman is best classified not as a recoverable historical “hero” with a biography, but as a **legendary ancestral woman and heroic mother**, appearing in two partly independent narrative complexes:

1. the Toltec–Chichimec cycle, where she confronts the hunter-conqueror Mixcoatl, becomes the mother of Ce Acatl Topiltzin Quetzalcoatl, and dies in childbirth; and
2. the Mexica migration cycle, where a woman named Chimalma leaves Aztlan and appears among the bearers or custodians of the Mexica sacred bundles.

Modern reference works frequently call her an “Aztec goddess of fertility, life, death, and rebirth.” That classification is **[MI/LS]**, not established by the early sources: no surviving ritual calendar, temple list, hymn, sacrificial prescription, or archaeological dedication securely documents an independent cult of “the goddess Chimalman.”

**Earliest secure attestation:** the earliest surviving material evidence is probably the **Codex Boturini/Tira de la Peregrinación**, generally dated to the early or middle sixteenth century, whose opening migration scene includes a shield-glyph woman identified through comparison with the alphabetic migration histories as Chimalma **[EA]**. The earliest extended narrative is the Nahuatl **Leyenda de los Soles**, copied in 1558, fols. 80–81 of the manuscript conventionally called the *Codex Chimalpopoca* **[EA]**. No presently known pre-conquest inscription securely names her.

### Name

The principal forms are:

- **Chīmalmān / Chimalman**, the form in the *Leyenda de los Soles*. The final *-n* is real and cautions against treating the name as an uncomplicated compound of *chīmalli*, “shield,” and *māitl*, “hand.”
- **Chīmalmā / Chimalma**, the common form in the migration histories and much modern scholarship.
- Spanish orthographies include **Chimalman**, **Chimalma**, and occasionally **Chimalmatl**.

A pictorial shield serves as her identifying sign in migration manuscripts. That makes an association with Nahuatl *chīmalli*, “shield,” secure at the level of scribal interpretation **[EA]**. “Shield-hand,” ubiquitous in modern summaries, is only a proposed etymology **[LS]**. The University of Oregon’s Nahuatl Dictionary specifically notes that the final *n* in *Chimalman* makes derivation from *māitl*, “hand,” problematic. A more cautious gloss is therefore “Chimalman, a name represented by a shield,” not “Shield-Hand” as a settled translation.

The story in which she catches Mixcoatl’s arrows invites a folk-etymological reading of her as a woman whose hand functions like a shield. The surviving *Leyenda*, however, does not say that this deed bestowed the name upon her.

### Titles and epithets

No stable ancient epithet comparable to *Topiltzin* or *Ce Acatl* is attached to Chimalman. The sources identify her relationally:

- “the woman Chimalman”;
- “the woman of Huitznahuac”;
- mother of Ce Acatl/Topiltzin;
- in the migration corpus, an Aztlan woman and sacred-bundle bearer.

“Goddess of fertility,” “patroness of life and death,” and “guide of rebirth” are modern encyclopedic titles **[MI]** unless a more specific primary citation is supplied.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### 2.1 Codex Boturini / Tira de la Peregrinación

**Language and medium:** pictorial Nahua manuscript with alphabetic annotations; amatl paper; probably produced c. 1530–1540, though datings vary within the sixteenth century.

**Role:** in the opening migration sequence a woman marked by a shield sign accompanies the departure from Aztlan. Comparison with the *Crónica Mexicayotl* identifies her as Chimalma. In the expanded reading of the scene, Chimalma, Apanecatl, Cuauhcoatl, and Tezcacoatl are the four *teomamaque*, bearers of sacred bundles **[EA/RV]**.

**What it does not establish:** the pictorial manuscript does not identify this migration woman as Mixcoatl’s wife or Quetzalcoatl’s mother. The equation of the migration Chimalma with the Toltec-cycle mother is possible but not stated in the document.

### 2.2 Leyenda de los Soles

**Language and date:** Nahuatl; manuscript copied in 1558, probably from earlier pictorial and oral materials. It is preserved with the *Anales de Cuauhtitlan* in the manuscript called the *Codex Chimalpopoca*.

**Role:** this is the fullest primary account of Chimalman **[EA]**. Mixcoatl invades Huitznahuac; Chimalman confronts him naked, lays down her shield and weapons, and evades or catches four arrows. On a subsequent encounter they have intercourse; she becomes pregnant. She gives birth to Ce Acatl and dies four days later. Quilaztli-Cihuacoatl rears the child. The same source later calls Chimalman Mixcoatl’s wife and gives Topiltzin a fifty-six-year span.

This account is not a virgin-birth narrative. It expressly says that Mixcoatl lay with her and she became pregnant.

### 2.3 Anales de Cuauhtitlan

**Language and date:** Nahuatl annals compiled in the sixteenth century, conventionally dated around the 1570s but incorporating older annalistic materials; the surviving manuscript was lost after being copied and photographed.

**Role:** in its Topiltzin genealogy, Chimalman conceives Quetzalcoatl after swallowing a *chalchihuitl*, a precious greenstone or jade bead **[EA/RV]**. This is the principal early source for the non-sexual conception. The narrative belongs to a different textual stratum from the *Leyenda de los Soles* and should not be harmonized with it.

### 2.4 Crónica Mexicayotl

**Language and textual history:** Nahuatl history conventionally attributed in substantial part to Hernando Alvarado Tezozomoc, c. 1598, but surviving in a manuscript copied and edited by Domingo Chimalpahin, with additional material by Alonso Franco and Chimalpahin **[LC]**.

**Role:** states that the migrating Mexica brought from Aztlan-Chicomoztoc “a woman named Chimalma” while taking Huitzilopochtli/Tetzahuitl with them. It supplies the alphabetic name that helps identify the shield-marked woman in migration codices **[RV]**.

### 2.5 Related migration manuscripts

The Codices Azcatitlan and Aubin recount the Mexica migration in forms related to Boturini. Their relevance is comparative: they document the changing visual and alphabetic representation of migration leaders and sacred bundles. Elizabeth Hill Boone and later scholars have emphasized that these manuscripts are histories constructed for particular colonial audiences rather than photographic records of a twelfth-century journey **[MSR]**.

### 2.6 Historia Tolteca-Chichimeca

**Language and date:** Nahuatl and pictorial; compiled at Cuauhtinchan, probably between 1547 and 1560, partly for a colonial land dispute.

It narrates Toltec-Chichimec migrations, conquests, genealogies, and land claims, but it does not supply a clear independent Chimalman episode comparable to the *Leyenda*. Its importance is contextual: it shows how sixteenth-century Nahua communities used “Toltec” and “Chichimec” descent narratives to authorize territory and government **[EA/context]**. Claims that it contains a full Chimalman biography require a folio citation; none was located.

### 2.7 Sahagún

Bernardino de Sahagún’s *Historia general de las cosas de Nueva España* / *Florentine Codex*, compiled principally in the mid-sixteenth century and completed by 1577, is fundamental for Quetzalcoatl, Toltec traditions, ritual, and the Tenochtitlan sacred precinct. It does **not**, in the standard Quetzalcoatl narrative, preserve the detailed Mixcoatl–Chimalman encounter, arrow-catching episode, or jade conception.

Accordingly, appeals to “Sahagún’s story of Chimalman” should be treated cautiously **[absence of evidence]**. Sahagún’s Toltec material cannot automatically be combined with the *Leyenda* genealogy.

### 2.8 Durán

Diego Durán’s *Historia de las Indias de Nueva España e Islas de Tierra Firme* was composed in the later sixteenth century; its component treatises date approximately 1570–1581. Durán preserves extensive traditions about Mexica migration, Huitzilopochtli, Topiltzin Quetzalcoatl, and Tula. No secure independent Chimalman episode comparable to the *Leyenda* was located in the work **[absence of evidence]**.

### 2.9 Ixtlilxochitl

Fernando de Alva Ixtlilxochitl’s histories, written in the early seventeenth century and culminating in the unfinished *Historia de la nación chichimeca*, reorganize Toltec and Chichimec history from a Tetzcocan dynastic perspective **[LC/LS]**. They are important for the later historicization of Topiltzin, but do not provide the earliest evidence for Chimalman.

### 2.10 Historia de los mexicanos por sus pinturas and Motolinía-related traditions

These early colonial compilations preserve related Mixcoatl, Camaxtli, Toltec, and Quetzalcoatl genealogies. Michel Graulich’s synopsis shows that the sources disagree over whether Topiltzin’s father is Mixcoatl, Camaxtli, Totepeuh, or another named ancestor and whether Chimalman is a second wife **[RV/LS]**. These are parallel genealogical constructions, not independent confirmation of a single family history.

## 3. The story

### 3.1 The armed woman of Huitznahuac

**Earliest source:** *Leyenda de los Soles*, 1558 manuscript **[EA]**.

Mixcoatl goes to conquer Huitznahuac. Chimalman comes out to meet him. She places her shield on the ground, sets down her arrows and *atlatl*, removes skirt and shift, and stands naked. Mixcoatl shoots four times:

1. the first arrow passes over her as she bends;
2. the second passes beside her;
3. she catches the third in her hand;
4. the fourth passes between her legs.

Mixcoatl withdraws. Chimalman hides in a red cave. When Mixcoatl returns and cannot find her, he kills women of Huitznahuac while searching. The women then tell Chimalman that he is killing her companions because of her, and she comes out.

This is not a harmless courtship scene. It combines invasion, lethal coercion against other women, nudity, missile combat, and eventual sexual possession. The text’s verb after their renewed confrontation states that Mixcoatl took her and lay with her; it does not narrate a formal marriage ceremony or clearly articulate Chimalman’s consent.

The often repeated modern formulation that “she caught all five arrows” is **[MI]**. The *Leyenda* has four shots and distinct responses. “Five arrows caught in her bare hand” conflates or embellishes the passage.

### 3.2 Sexual conception and death in childbirth

**Source:** *Leyenda de los Soles*, 1558 **[EA]**.

On the second encounter Chimalman again stands exposed and Mixcoatl again shoots. Afterward he lies with her, and she becomes pregnant. Four days after the child is born, Chimalman dies. The child’s calendrical name is **Ce Acatl**, “One Reed.”

The text then says that **Quilaztli, Cihuacoatl**, raises the infant. Later the child goes with his father, Mixcoatl, campaigns or learns warfare, and after Mixcoatl’s murder by the Centzon Mixcoa seeks his father’s remains and installs them in a temple at Mixcoatepetl.

Chimalman’s death serves narratively to detach the child from his birth mother and transfer him to a supernatural foster mother. It also establishes Topiltzin as a posthumously bereaved avenger **[MSR as to function]**.

### 3.3 Jade conception

**Source:** *Anales de Cuauhtitlan*, sixteenth-century annalistic compilation **[EA/RV]**.

Chimalman conceives Topiltzin Quetzalcoatl because she swallows a *chalchihuitl*, a precious greenstone. No human father is required in this episode as transmitted. Greenstone was associated throughout central Mexico with preciousness, water, fertility, breath, and life; interpreting this particular stone as Venus, semen, maize, or a sacramental host is modern exegesis unless tied to an explicit passage **[MSR]**.

This version conflicts with the *Leyenda*, which explicitly gives Mixcoatl as sexual father. They are not successive moments of one story.

### 3.4 Marriage and chronological notice

The *Leyenda de los Soles* later states: “Mixcoatl had lived thirty-nine years. The name of his wife is Chimalman. Topiltzin lasted fifty-six years.” It then dates Topiltzin’s departure from Tollan and death in Tlapallan **[EA]**.

This compact notice integrates Chimalman into a pseudo-annalistic royal chronology. It does not prove that any of the named persons lived for those exact periods.

### 3.5 Aztlan migration

**Sources:** Boturini and related migration pictorials; *Crónica Mexicayotl* **[EA/RV]**.

A woman Chimalma accompanies the migration from Aztlan-Chicomoztoc. In the Boturini interpretive tradition she is one of four *teomamaque*, custodians or bearers of divine bundles. The *Crónica Mexicayotl* says the migrants brought her from Aztlan while taking Huitzilopochtli with them.

This figure may represent a founding ancestress, priestly office, corporate group, or eponym. The sources do not identify her as the mother of Topiltzin. Treating both women as one immortal goddess is a modern harmonization **[LS/MI]**.

### 3.6 “Three sisters” and celestial annunciation

Modern mythographies sometimes say Chimalman was one of three sisters; that the creator appeared as Citlalatonac; or that a celestial messenger announced her conception. The accessible early Chimalman passages do not support this narrative. Its form closely resembles Christian annunciation and comparative “virgin hero” templates. Unless an exact colonial manuscript passage is produced, it belongs under **[MI/SA]**, not pre-conquest tradition.

Likewise, claims that Chimalman was the sister of Coatlicue and Xochitlicue, daughter of Tlaltecuhtli and Tlalcihuatl, or mother of Xolotl circulate widely online but were not verified in the named early corpus. They should not be presented as ancient fact.

### 4.2 Every securely documented named interaction

- **Mixcoatl:** invades Chimalman’s country, shoots four arrows at her, searches for her, kills Huitznahuac women to force her appearance, has intercourse with her, and fathers Ce Acatl; later called her husband. *Leyenda de los Soles*, copied 1558. **[EA]**

- **Apanecatl:** fellow migration leader or sacred-bundle bearer in the Boturini interpretive tradition; no dialogue or kinship is stated. Codex Boturini, sixteenth century. **[EA/RV]**
- **Cuauhcoatl:** fellow migration leader or sacred-bundle bearer; no further personal interaction stated. Codex Boturini, sixteenth century. **[EA/RV]**
- **Tezcacoatl:** fellow migration leader or sacred-bundle bearer; no further personal interaction stated. Codex Boturini, sixteenth century. **[EA/RV]**
- **Centzon Mixcoa:** they murder Mixcoatl after Chimalman’s death; their only relation to her is as killers of her child’s father. *Leyenda de los Soles*, 1558. **[EA]**

- **Xochitlicue:** no secure early interaction located; alleged sisterhood is **[MI]**.
- **Xolotl:** no early source located making him Chimalman’s son. **[MI]**
- **Tezcatlipoca:** no early Chimalman episode securely documents his persuading others to murder Mixcoatl. That plot belongs to modern composite retellings **[MI]**.
- **Citlalatonac:** no securely identified early passage documents an annunciation to Chimalman. **[MI/SA]**

## 5. Cult, tomb, and ritual

### Independent cult

No securely identified source documents:

- a temple dedicated to Chimalman;
- a calendrical feast in her name;
- a priesthood, cult image, or impersonator;
- sacrifices offered specifically to her;
- an oracle, sacred spring, or pilgrimage;
- a tomb, relics, or translated bones;
- theophoric naming demonstrably derived from her cult.

This is a substantive negative finding. Sahagún’s description of the Tenochtitlan sacred precinct lists many structures; archaeology has identified temples to Huitzilopochtli, Tlaloc, Ehecatl-Quetzalcoatl, Xochipilli, and other beings, but no structure securely assigned to Chimalman. Templo Mayor archaeology therefore provides religious context, not proof of her cult.

### Ritual implications in the stories

The migration Chimalma’s role as *teomama* is ritual in a broad sense: sacred bundles embodied patron powers and their bearers held priestly-political authority **[EA/RV]**. That is evidence for a ritual office in the migration narrative, not necessarily worship of Chimalma herself.

The Huitznahuac episode has been interpreted as a mythic representation of Chichimec conquest, hunting, sexual capture, dynastic marriage, or union between northern hunter-warriors and southern sedentary populations **[MSR]**. The text does not label it a performed rite.

### Tomb and death

Only the *Leyenda* states Chimalman’s death, four days after childbirth. It says nothing about treatment of her body, burial place, shrine, apotheosis, or relics. Claims that she was “deified because she died in childbirth” are plausible extrapolations from Nahua beliefs about women who died in labor, but no Chimalman-specific cult text was located **[MSR/MI]**.

## 6. Historical evolution

### Possible pre-conquest substrate

The surviving stories presuppose pre-conquest names, calendrical structures, landscape traditions, and political identities. Nonetheless, no extant pre-conquest inscription names Chimalman. Any ninth- or tenth-century date assigned to her as a historical woman is **[MSR]**, normally derived by treating annalistic dates as literal biography.

### c. 1530–1560: pictorial migration and Toltec-Chichimec records

The Codex Boturini presents Chimalma as a migration ancestress or bundle bearer. The *Historia Tolteca-Chichimeca* demonstrates the wider environment in which migration and conquest traditions legitimated colonial-era land rights and noble status.

### 1558: narrative consolidation

The *Leyenda de los Soles* records the violent Huitznahuac encounter, pregnancy, childbirth death, fosterage by Cihuacoatl, and Topiltzin’s paternal vengeance. This is the central documentary witness.

### Later sixteenth century: alternate conception and annalistic Topiltzin

The *Anales de Cuauhtitlan* supplies the swallowed-jade conception. Rather than a corrupted copy of the *Leyenda*, it is evidence of a different genealogy and mode of supernatural birth.

### c. 1598–seventeenth century: migration historicization

The *Crónica Mexicayotl* names Chimalma among those brought from Aztlan and incorporates her into a Mexica national history. Ixtlilxochitl and other historians further systematize Toltec rulers and dynasties, while Christian chronology encourages the treatment of legendary figures as ancient kings.

### Colonial Christian comparison

The non-sexual jade conception invited comparison with the Virgin Mary. Such comparison is **[SA/CA]**, not neutral recovery of Nahua doctrine. Christian writers and later antiquarians repeatedly treated Quetzalcoatl as a distorted Christ, apostle, or Saint Thomas; his mother could consequently be recast as a Mexican Virgin.

The crucial distinction is textual:

- *Leyenda de los Soles*: sexual conception by Mixcoatl;
- *Anales de Cuauhtitlan*: ingestion of greenstone;
- later Christian/comparative retellings: virginal annunciation.

Calling “the virgin Chimalman” an uncontested Aztec belief erases the earliest detailed episode.

### Nineteenth and early twentieth centuries

Antiquarians translated the *Codex Chimalpopoca*, organized Nahua beings into Greco-Roman-style pantheons, and often classified any named supernatural woman as a “goddess.” Cecilio Robelo’s mythological dictionary belongs to this systematizing phase **[LS]**.

### Late twentieth and twenty-first centuries

Scholars increasingly treated Toltec and migration accounts as political narratives created by different communities rather than fragments of a single lost chronicle. Nicholson’s source catalogue, Davies’s historical criticism of “Toltec empire,” Carrasco’s analysis of Quetzalcoatl and sacred kingship, Graulich’s comparative reconstruction, León-Portilla’s literary treatment, Boone’s study of pictorial histories, and Susan Gillespie’s work on dynastic ideology all contributed to this shift **[MSR]**.

Popular websites simultaneously expanded Chimalman into a fertility and rebirth goddess with an extensive divine family. Those claims frequently cite one another rather than a folio or critical edition **[MI]**.

## 7. Modern reception and reinvention

Chimalman remains secondary in literature and visual culture compared with Quetzalcoatl, Coatlicue, or Malintzin. Her main modern afterlives are:

- retellings of Quetzalcoatl’s birth;
- illustrated surveys of “Aztec gods”;
- comparative-mythology presentations of a virgin-born culture hero;
- neo-Toltec and esoteric narratives treating Quetzalcoatl as a Mexican Christ;
- educational presentations of women in the Aztlan migration.

David Adams Leeming’s 1998 *Mythology: The Voyage of the Hero* presents Chimalman as a virgin and one of three sisters visited by an all-father in the form of Citlalatonac **[LS/MI]**. This is a comparative heroic-birth synthesis, not a transparent translation of the *Leyenda*.

Online profiles commonly add:

- fertility, life/death, and rebirth portfolios;
- sisterhood with Coatlicue and Xochitlicue;
- parentage from Tlaltecuhtli and Tlalcihuatl;
- Xolotl as a second son;
- Tezcatlipoca engineering Mixcoatl’s death;
- five arrows rather than four.

Until matched to exact early passages, these are **[MI]**.

There is no documented major nationalist cult centered specifically on Chimalman. Aztlan became politically important in Mexican and Chicano nationalisms, but the symbolic emphasis fell on the migrating Mexica collectively, Huitzilopochtli, the eagle-and-cactus sign, and the homeland Aztlan. Chimalma’s presence in the migration record should not be inflated into evidence that she herself became a nationalist emblem.

No substantial modern extremist appropriation centered on her was located.

## 8. Historiography and debate

### Was Chimalman historical?

No direct archaeological or epigraphic evidence identifies a historical woman Chimalman. The historicist position arises from reading Topiltzin as a tenth-century ruler and his genealogy as dynastic memory **[MSR]**. Critics point to:

- incompatible genealogies;
- supernatural conception;
- calendrical names;
- duplication of actors across mythic and historical registers;
- manuscripts produced centuries after the supposed events;
- political functions of Toltec ancestry in Postclassic and colonial societies.

The safest conclusion is not that she “never existed,” which cannot be proved, but that the dossier concerns a documented legendary figure for whom no independently verifiable historical biography survives.

### One Chimalman or two?

There are at least two source roles:

1. mother of Topiltzin in the Toltec-Mixcoatl cycle;
2. Aztlan migration woman and bundle bearer.

A single underlying ancestress is possible **[MSR]**, but the manuscripts never explicitly equate them. A repeated prestigious name, a lineage title, or deliberate appropriation of a Toltec ancestral name by Mexica historiography are equally possible.

### Goddess, mortal, or office-holder?

The Toltec story treats her as “woman” and “wife,” though she inhabits a mythic genealogy. The migration sources can be read as depicting a priestly bearer or founding ancestress. The sources do not give her a divine portfolio. “Goddess” is therefore an interpretive classification, not a source quotation.

### Sexual violence and romanticization

Older retellings frequently transform invasion and lethal coercion into courtship: Mixcoatl “falls in love,” Chimalman playfully catches arrows, and they marry. The *Leyenda* is harsher. Mixcoatl is conquering her land; he shoots at her; when she hides, he kills women associated with her; and only afterward takes her sexually. Whether the Nahuatl verb implies consensual union cannot be settled from euphemistic translation alone, but “romance” suppresses the coercive structure.

### Virgin-birth debate

The evidence supports a genuine non-sexual conception variant—the swallowed *chalchihuitl*—but not a single canonical virgin-birth dogma. Christian comparisons may have shaped colonial reception; they do not automatically prove that the jade story itself was invented by friars. Conversely, similarity to Mary does not establish a pre-Christian “Mexican annunciation.” Both diffusionist certainty and apologetic claims of exact Christian anticipation exceed the evidence.

### Tula and the Toltecs

Nigel Davies treated the Toltec traditions against archaeology and questioned expansive reconstructions of a historical empire. Nicholson catalogued the Topiltzin source complex and resisted reducing it either to literal biography or pure invention. Carrasco emphasized sacred kingship and the ideological use of Quetzalcoatl. Graulich read the narratives comparatively as transformations of cosmogonic and solar-maize myths. These approaches illuminate Chimalman but do not produce consensus on her historicity.

### Manuscript condition and losses

The *Codex Chimalpopoca* manuscript disappeared from the Museo Nacional in 1949; scholarship depends on earlier copies, editions, and photographs. Consequently, readings must be checked across the Velázquez, Lehmann, Bierhorst, and other editions. The loss particularly matters when arguments turn on spelling, foliation, or a single Nahuatl verb.

## 10. Sources and assessment

### Primary texts and critical editions

- *Leyenda de los Soles*, fols. 80–81: indispensable source for the Mixcoatl encounter, four arrows, pregnancy, death, and Cihuacoatl.
- *Anales de Cuauhtitlan*: source for swallowed-greenstone conception.
- Codex Boturini: earliest surviving visual attestation of the migration Chimalma.
- *Crónica Mexicayotl*: alphabetic identification of the Aztlan woman and migration context.
- *Historia Tolteca-Chichimeca*: comparative evidence for the political construction of Toltec-Chichimec migration and land history.
- Sahagún and Durán: important controls on what the larger sixteenth-century corpus does and does not say.
- Ixtlilxochitl: later Tetzcocan systematization, not an early independent witness to Chimalman’s principal episodes.

### Principal modern studies

- H. B. Nicholson, *Topiltzin Quetzalcoatl: The Once and Future Lord of the Toltecs* (2001): comprehensive source catalogue; essential for keeping distinct manuscript traditions apart.
- Nigel Davies, *The Toltecs*: historical and archaeological criticism of literalized Toltec chronicles.
- David Carrasco, *Quetzalcoatl and the Irony of Empire* (1982): sacred kingship and ideological transformation.
- Michel Graulich, *Los reyes de Tollan*: comparative analysis of conflicting Topiltzin genealogies.
- Elizabeth Hill Boone and later migration-codex scholarship: pictorial histories as purposeful historical arguments.
- Susan Gillespie, *The Aztec Kings*: dynastic and political construction of Mexica history.
- Miguel León-Portilla: Nahuatl literary and philosophical framing, valuable but sometimes more synthesizing than strictly source-separating.

### Bottom line

The securely documented Chimalman is both richer and narrower than the modern “goddess” profile. She is:

- an armed woman of Huitznahuac who survives Mixcoatl’s arrows;
- the mother of Ce Acatl Topiltzin in a narrative involving conquest, coercion, sex, childbirth, and death;
- alternatively, the woman who conceives after swallowing precious greenstone;
- and, in another historical tradition, an Aztlan migration woman associated with the sacred bundles.

There is no secure evidence for her own temple, cult, tomb, festival, fertility portfolio, or extensive divine family. The variants—not their harmonization—constitute her documented history.
