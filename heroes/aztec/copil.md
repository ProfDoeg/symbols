# Copil: Research Dossier

> **Traditional class:** Copil is a mortal, mytho-historical migration hero and sacrificial enemy of the Mexica. The sources do not class him as a god or demigod, although his divine maternal kinship, magical powers, sacrificial death, and transformation into the foundation-sign of Tenochtitlan place him at the boundary between heroic ancestor, enemy champion, and etiological victim.
>
> **Earliest secure named attestation:** the Spanish-language *Historia de los mexicanos por sus pinturas*, compiled from Indigenous pictorial and oral materials probably in the 1530s, less than two decades after the conquest. It says that Copil opposed Huitzilopochtli and that his extracted heart marked the place where the Mexica were to settle. No securely pre-Conquest inscription or image names him.
>
> **Central evidentiary conclusion:** Copil’s narrative is early colonial documentation of a probably pre-Conquest Mexica tradition, not a verifiable biography. Pre-Conquest and early-colonial images independently attest the eagle–cactus–stone emblem, but only colonial texts explicitly derive the cactus from Copil’s heart. No securely identified tomb, shrine, priesthood, festival, sacrifice, or independent cult of Copil is known.

## Evidence labels used

- **[EA] Securely attested early material:** sixteenth-century testimony or material culture preserving demonstrably early traditions.
- **[CE] Colonial canonical elaboration:** developed sixteenth- or early-seventeenth-century narrative.
- **[RV] Regional or source-family variant.**
- **[LS] Learned systematization:** antiquarian, clerical, or scholarly harmonization.
- **[SM] Syncretistic identification.**
- **[MA] Medieval or early-modern accretion:** in this dossier, chiefly colonial-era additions or reinterpretations.
- **[F] Folklore or living popular tradition.**
- **[MSR] Modern scholarly reconstruction.**
- **[MI] Modern invention or unsupported modern amplification.**
- **[NEG] Negative finding:** the claim or figure is absent from the specified evidence.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### Orientation

**[CE]** Copil belongs to the Mexica migration cycle. In its fullest surviving form, his mother Malinalxochitl—an elder sister or female counterpart of Huitzilopochtli—is abandoned by the migrating Mexica because of her dangerous sorcery. She settles at or near Malinalco and bears Copil. When grown, Copil learns how his mother was treated and seeks revenge: he travels among the peoples surrounding the Basin of Mexico, urges them to destroy the Mexica at Chapultepec, and prepares to watch their defeat. Huitzilopochtli discovers the conspiracy and orders his followers to strike first. Copil is killed at Tepetzinco; his head is placed on a hill, while his heart is carried or thrown into the lake among reeds and rushes. From it grows the stone-rooted nopal on which the eagle appears, indicating the site of Mexico-Tenochtitlan.

The fullest witnesses are Diego Durán’s *Historia de las Indias de Nueva España e islas de Tierra Firme* and the Nahuatl *Crónica Mexicáyotl*. The earlier *Historia de los mexicanos por sus pinturas* contains the essential heart-and-foundation episode but not all the developed narrative.

### Name and spelling

- **Copil / Cópil** — conventional Spanish spelling.
- **Copil** — alphabetic Nahuatl form in the colonial texts.
- **Copilli** — often substituted in modern retellings, but philologically this is also a common noun meaning a high or conical headdress, cap, crown, or diadem.
- **Cohuil** — a spelling in the surviving Spanish text of Tezozómoc’s *Crónica mexicana*, generally corrected or understood as Copil.
- **Copil Tlaciuhqui / Tlaciuhque** — “Copil the diviner/soothsayer,” a modern or late learned expansion frequently repeated online. It is not established as an ancient formal epithet by the core early witnesses and should not be cited without a precise manuscript locus. **[MI/LS]**

The UNAM *Gran Diccionario Náhuatl* treats *Copil* as a personal name in the narrative and separately records *copilli* as a conical headdress. Durán glossed *copil* as “gorro cónico,” but that lexical association does not prove that the hero’s name was originally an epithet meaning “crown.” [UNAM, *Gran Diccionario Náhuatl*](https://gdn.iib.unam.mx/diccionario/copil)

### Etymology

**[EA/uncertain]** The only secure lexical comparison is with Classical Nahuatl *copilli*, “conical cap, high headdress, diadem.” The loss or omission of final absolutive *-li* in names is possible, but the sources do not themselves explain the hero as “the Crown” or “the Crowned One.”

**[MI]** Modern spiritual and nationalist retellings often expand this into “royal crown,” “royal blood,” a matrilineal principle, or a cosmic union of feminine and masculine powers. Those meanings are interpretations of the modern era, not translations attested in Durán, Tezozómoc, the *Historia de los mexicanos*, or surviving migration codices.

### Toponyms associated with the name

- **Acopilco**: the *Crónica Mexicáyotl* explains the place through Copil’s severed head at Tepetzinco. This is a mythic etiology, not proof of a historical burial. **[CE]**
- **Copilco** and similarly formed place-names may use the common noun or a personal name; resemblance alone cannot establish a cult or death-place of the hero. **[NEG/MSR]**
- **Tenochtitlan** is connected by the narratives with *tenochtli*, the stone-growing prickly pear: *tetl*, “stone,” plus *nochtli*, “prickly pear.” Deriving *Tenochtitlan* directly from Copil’s personal name is therefore incorrect. **[MI]**

### Identifications

Copil was not equated in the early sources with Quetzalcoatl, Tezcatlipoca, the Moon, or another pan-Mesoamerican deity. **[NEG]**

The recurring modern description of Copil as “lunar,” or as a son whose heart reunites with his mother the Moon, depends on interpreting Malinalxochitl as a lunar being. It is a modern symbolic reading, not an explicit identification made in the Copil narrative. **[MSR/MI]**

Calling Copil “Toltec” is also misleading. His story is Mexica and Malinalca and belongs to the Basin migration cycle. Toltec memory enters because the *Crónica Mexicáyotl* makes the heart pass from a stone seat associated with Quetzalcoatl’s departure from Tollan; this links Mexica possession of the island to Toltec antiquity but does not make Copil a Toltec hero. **[CE/MSR]**

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### Earliest secure evidence

#### *Historia de los mexicanos por sus pinturas*

**[EA]** This anonymous Spanish account was probably composed in the 1530s, perhaps from work associated with Andrés de Olmos, using Indigenous paintings and interpreters. The surviving copy is later, so there is a manuscript gap, but the work is generally accepted as one of the earliest post-Conquest compilations of Mexica sacred history.

Its Copil sequence says, in substance, that Copil was a relative and opponent of Huitzilopochtli; Huitzilopochtli ordered his killing; Copil’s heart was buried or deposited at the place where the Mexica were to reside; and the settlement received its name after the tuna growing from a stone whose roots emerged from where the heart had been placed. This is the earliest securely dated named witness to the etiological chain:

> Copil → extracted heart → designated ground → stone-grown nopal → Tenochtitlan.

The text does **not** establish Copil’s historical existence, furnish archaeological coordinates for the deposited heart, or attest a cult.

### Relevant pictorial evidence

#### Codex Mendoza, fol. 2r

**[EA]** Produced in New Spain about 1541–1542, the Codex Mendoza depicts the foundation of Tenochtitlan through an eagle on a flowering or fruiting nopal growing from a stone, surrounded by water and the city’s four divisions. Copil is neither pictured as a named human figure nor identified in the gloss.

It corroborates the early centrality of the eagle–nopal–stone emblem but does not, by itself, prove the heart narrative.

#### Codex Boturini, or *Tira de la Peregrinación*

**[EA]** Probably painted in the first half of the sixteenth century in a strongly Indigenous pictorial idiom. It recounts the migration from Aztlan through Chapultepec but is incomplete and ends before the complete foundation sign. Its surviving pages do not explicitly name or portray the developed Copil episode. The official INAH study calls it an early migration history but stresses its incompleteness. [INAH, Codex Boturini overview](https://www.gob.mx/cultura/es/articulos/el-codice-boturini-del-peregrinaje-de-los-aztecas-a-la-fundacion-de-mexico-tenochtitlan?idiom=es)

**[NEG]** It is therefore inaccurate to say that the surviving Boturini manuscript directly depicts Copil’s heart becoming the nopal.

#### Codex Azcatitlan

**[CE/RV]** This later-sixteenth-century pictorial history contains a foundation scene interpreted in modern cataloguing as Copil and Huitzilopochtli. The identification relies partly on comparison with the alphabetic chronicles. It is valuable corroboration of the visual narrative but not an independently glossed portrait securely naming Copil.

#### Codex Mexicanus

**[CE]** A composite colonial manuscript made by several painters and writers, probably across the later sixteenth century. Its migration annals culminate in the nopal before the eagle’s arrival in the year 2 House, commonly correlated with 1325. Modern analysis stresses that the manuscript is a palimpsest whose sections and additions were made by multiple hands. [Castañeda de la Paz and Oudijk, *El Códice Mexicanus*](https://historicas.unam.mx/publicaciones/publicadigital/libros/727_01/727_01_04_00_introduccion.pdf)

The pictorial sequence may presuppose the Copil story, but the image is not equivalent to a named textual attestation.

### Principal alphabetic accounts

#### Diego Durán, *Historia de las Indias de Nueva España e islas de Tierra Firme*

**[CE]** Written principally in the 1570s and completed around 1581; first printed only in the nineteenth century. Durán gives the most expansive Spanish narrative:

1. Malinalxochitl is Huitzilopochtli’s sister and a feared sorceress.
2. The Mexica abandon her while she sleeps.
3. She bears Copil and teaches him her magical arts.
4. Copil promises to avenge her.
5. He moves from town to town, “moving the hearts” of neighboring peoples against the Mexica.
6. Huitzilopochtli orders a pre-emptive attack at Tepetzinco.
7. Copil is killed and his heart removed.
8. The heart is cast among the reeds.
9. A nopal grows from it, and the eagle on that nopal later marks Tenochtitlan.

Durán’s Spanish wording is searchable through the UNAM lexicographic corpus. [UNAM quotation from Durán](https://gdn.iib.unam.mx/diccionario/copil/188806)

Durán’s descriptions of Malinalxochitl and Copil as “witches” must be read as colonial Christian vocabulary applied to Indigenous *nahual* and divinatory powers. **[MA]**

#### Fernando Alvarado Tezozómoc, *Crónica mexicana*

**[CE]** Composed in Spanish near the end of the sixteenth century, conventionally around 1598. Its migration account closely resembles Durán and has long been assigned with it to the hypothetical “Crónica X” source family.

The chronicle says Malinalxochitl was left asleep because she used dangerous arts and “ate” living hearts; she settled at Texcaltepec and bore Cohuil/Copil. The adult son confronts the Mexica tradition and becomes the enemy whose death precedes the foundation.

Shared wording between Durán and Tezozómoc is evidence of textual or source relationship, not two fully independent historical testimonies. **[MSR]**

#### *Crónica Mexicáyotl*

**[CE]** A Nahuatl chronicle traditionally attributed to Fernando Alvarado Tezozómoc, composed or compiled in the early seventeenth century and transmitted through the papers and copying activity of Domingo Chimalpahin. The surviving textual history is complicated; it should not be treated as an untouched pre-Conquest document.

Its Copil narrative is the most topographically and ritually detailed:

- Malinalxochitl tells Copil that his maternal uncle Huitzilopochtli abandoned her.
- Copil establishes himself near Texcaltépetl/Zoquitzinco.
- He rallies surrounding groups against the Mexica at Chapultepec.
- Huitzilopochtli informs his priestly carriers.
- Mexica agents reach Copil at Tepetzinco.
- He is killed, decapitated, and opened at the chest.
- His head is set on Tepetzintli, etiologically associated with Acopilco.
- Cuauhtlequetzqui/Cuauhcoatl carries and throws the heart from a red-and-black stone seat associated with Quetzalcoatl.
- The heart falls among reeds and rushes and germinates as the nopal of Tenochtitlan.

The relevant Nahuatl passage includes:

> *Niman ocacique in Copil in oncan Tepetzinco … conanilli in iyollo …*  
> “Then they came upon Copil there at Tepetzinco … they took his heart …”

A modern INAH exposition prints the passage and translation. [INAH digital Codex Boturini study, “La gesta de Cópil”](https://codiceboturini.inah.gob.mx/approaches_text.php?textID=5)

The chronicle also uses the name Copil for one of the ancestral *teomamaque*, bearers of the deity bundle, in a separate list. Whether this is the same figure, a namesake, or evidence of conflated traditions is uncertain. It must not automatically be folded into the enemy-son narrative. **[RV]**

### Other named corpora in the brief

#### *Anales de Cuauhtitlan*

**[NEG]** Compiled in Nahuatl around 1570, surviving through the lost Chimalpopoca manuscript and copies. It is indispensable for Toltec and central-Mexican history but does not carry the developed Copil migration narrative in its standard published text. Its silence prevents using it as independent confirmation.

#### *Leyenda de los Soles*

**[NEG]** Written in Nahuatl in 1558 and transmitted with the Chimalpopoca materials. It concerns cosmic ages, divine conflicts, and the creation of humanity; Copil is not a protagonist in its surviving text.

#### *Historia Tolteca-Chichimeca*

**[NEG/analogical]** Produced at Cuauhtinchan in the mid-sixteenth century, probably 1547–1560. It does not tell Copil’s story. Scholars compare its Cholula foundation imagery, cactus symbolism, territorial rites, and Toltec-Chichimec political organization with Mexica foundation traditions, but these are structural analogies, not Copil attestations.

#### Bernardino de Sahagún, *Historia general* / Florentine Codex

**[NEG]** Compiled through successive stages between the 1540s and 1570s. Sahagún preserves extensive material on Huitzilopochtli, Tenochtitlan, ritual, and Mexica history, but not the full Copil revenge-and-heart narrative. Claims that “Sahagún tells the Copil legend” generally conflate his evidence for related gods and symbols with Durán or Tezozómoc.

#### Fernando de Alva Ixtlilxochitl

**[NEG/secondary context]** His early-seventeenth-century histories preserve Acolhua and Toltec traditions and material on the Basin’s political past. They are relevant to competing histories of settlement but are not a primary detailed witness to Copil’s deeds.

#### Chimalpahin

**[CE/RV]** Chimalpahin’s annals contain related migration, foundation, and divine-list material, including a passage naming Copil among beings formerly treated as gods. His version helps document seventeenth-century Nahua reception but does not supersede the narrative in the *Crónica Mexicáyotl*. The relevant dictionary corpus records a Nahuatl list including “Huitzilopochtli, Copil, Quetzalpatzactli … Quetzalcohuatl, Tepuztecatl,” followed by the Christianizing observation that some had only been humans and were now dead. [Nahuatl Dictionary, Chimalpahin passage](https://nahuatl.wired-humanities.org/node/201092)

## 3. The story

### A. Malinalxochitl’s abandonment

**Earliest developed witnesses:** Durán, 1570s; Tezozómoc, late sixteenth century; *Crónica Mexicáyotl*, early seventeenth century. **[CE]**

Malinalxochitl travels with the Mexica and Huitzilopochtli. The migrants complain that she harms people through sorcery. Huitzilopochtli declares that her powers and purposes are incompatible with his own martial mission and orders the group to leave secretly while she sleeps.

- In Durán, her dangerous magic and the missionaries’ moral vocabulary dominate.
- In the *Crónica mexicana*, she is accused of killing people merely by looking at them and of consuming hearts.
- In the *Crónica Mexicáyotl*, the separation belongs to a kinship rupture: a brother abandons a sister, generating the grievance inherited by her son.

**[MSR]** Scholars read the episode as mythologizing a split between related migrating communities or cult groups, possibly Mexica and Malinalca. This is plausible political interpretation, not demonstrated event-history.

### B. Copil’s birth and father

**[CE/RV]**

- The *Crónica Mexicáyotl* makes Malinalxochitl settle with Chimalcuauhtli, ruler of Malinalco or a related locality, and makes him Copil’s father.
- The *Crónica mexicana* says Malinalxochitl was already pregnant when she settled near Texcaltepec; in that passage Copil’s father is not identified.
- Durán centers the mother–son relationship and does not make paternal descent narratively important.

These accounts cannot be harmonized into a secure genealogy. Chimalcuauhtli is the father only in the tradition that names him.

### C. Education and magical character

**[CE]** Durán says Malinalxochitl teaches Copil her *malas mañas y hechicerías*, “evil tricks and sorceries.” This is the source for calling him a sorcerer. The stronger modern claims that he was an “astronomer,” “astrologer,” initiated priest of a lunar cult, or master of a formal esoteric school lack a cited early locus. **[MI]**

### D. The vow of vengeance

**[CE]** When Malinalxochitl tells her son that Huitzilopochtli deserted her, Copil promises to find and destroy his uncle and the uncle’s people. The motive is consistently filial revenge. The story’s emotional motor is the mother’s tears and the son’s anger.

### E. Mobilization against the Mexica

**[CE/RV]** Copil travels through the settlements around the lake, denounces the Mexica as destructive and dangerous, and urges their neighbors to unite against them at Chapultepec. Durán describes him metaphorically as “moving” or “setting fire to” people’s hearts.

The exact coalition varies with modern reconstruction. Lists commonly include Tepanec, Xochimilca, Chalca, Colhua, and other Basin peoples, but not every surviving account gives the same list. The secure core is that Copil incites neighboring peoples collectively; exhaustive ethnic lists should be attributed to their individual source.

**[MSR]** A historical reading sees this as a transformed memory of conflict between newly arrived Mexica and established Basin communities. There is no independent contemporary record naming a commander Copil.

### F. Huitzilopochtli’s counter-command

**[CE]** Huitzilopochtli learns of the plot and speaks through the *teomamaque* or priests carrying his bundle. He orders the Mexica to kill Copil before the coalition attacks.

This is not a fair duel between heroes. It is a divinely commanded pre-emptive killing and sacrificial dismemberment.

### G. Capture and death

**[CE/RV]**

In the *Crónica Mexicáyotl*, Copil is reached at Tepetzinco. The syntax can make Huitzilopochtli the commanding or acting subject, but within the migration narrative the god ordinarily acts through his priestly representatives. Copil is killed, decapitated, and cut open; his heart is removed.

Durán similarly places him on a hill awaiting the Mexica’s destruction. The Mexica climb to him, kill him, and bring his heart to Huitzilopochtli’s agents.

**Variant:** some modern retellings call the event death “in battle.” The primary narrative more strongly resembles surprise, capture, execution, and ritual dismemberment. **[MI if stated as a formal duel]**

### H. The head

**[CE/RV]** The *Crónica Mexicáyotl* says Copil’s head was placed on Tepetzintli and uses it to explain Acopilco. This detail is not equally prominent in all accounts.

No excavated skull has been identified as Copil’s. Acopilco is a narrative toponymic association, not an authenticated tomb. **[NEG]**

### I. The heart and Cuauhtlequetzqui

**[EA/CE]** Huitzilopochtli instructs the priest or leader Cuauhtlequetzqui, also called Cuauhcoatl in the *Crónica Mexicáyotl*, to carry the heart into the reeds. From a red-and-black stone or stone seat associated with Quetzalcoatl’s passage toward Tlillan Tlapallan, he throws it into the lake.

The relevant command in the Nahuatl account is approximately:

> *… oncan timoquetzaz in toconmayahuiz in yehuatl in iyollo in Copil*  
> “… there you shall stand and cast the heart of Copil.”

The heart falls among *tollin* and *acatl*, reeds and rushes. It germinates or changes into the foundation nopal.

**Variant:** the *Historia de los mexicanos* says the heart was buried or deposited where the Mexica would settle. The *Crónica Mexicáyotl* emphasizes throwing it across the wetland. These should remain distinct.

### J. Nopal, eagle, and city

**[EA/CE]** The prickly pear grows from a stone at the place marked by Copil’s heart. Huitzilopochtli tells the migrants that an eagle will be found on it; they recognize the site and name it Tenochtitlan.

The eagle’s prey varies in early imagery and texts:

- some early representations show an eagle alone;
- some show it holding a bird;
- the Teocalli of Sacred Warfare shows the eagle associated with *atl-tlachinolli*, “water and burned earth,” a metaphor for war;
- the now-standard snake becomes dominant in colonial and national heraldry.

Therefore “Copil’s heart produced the cactus on which the eagle devoured a snake” combines the Copil tradition with one particular later form of the emblem. The serpent is not stable across the earliest witnesses. **[MA/LS]**

### K. What happened to Copil after death?

The story grants continuing generative force to his heart but does not describe his soul’s destination, resurrection, apotheosis, or enthronement in an underworld. **[NEG]**

Calling him a god after death is unsupported. A seventeenth-century Christianized list can say that Copil had been revered as a god, but no corresponding pre-Conquest sanctuary, feast, or divine iconography is securely identifiable.

## 4. Relations with gods and other heroes

### Genealogical alternatives

| Relationship | Version | Source and date | Tier |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mother | Malinalxochitl | Durán, 1570s; *Crónica mexicana*, c. 1598; *Crónica Mexicáyotl*, early 1600s | CE |
| Father | Chimalcuauhtli, ruler associated with Malinalco | *Crónica Mexicáyotl*, early 1600s | RV/CE |
| Father | Unnamed; Malinalxochitl already pregnant | *Crónica mexicana*, c. 1598 | RV/CE |
| Maternal uncle | Huitzilopochtli | Durán, Tezozómoc, *Crónica Mexicáyotl* | CE |
| Wife/consort | Xicomoyahualtzin in a reconstructed historical-genealogical tradition | colonial genealogical material as analyzed by modern historians | RV/MSR |
| Daughter | a younger Xicomoyahualtzin, in the same genealogical reconstruction | colonial genealogical material | RV/MSR |
| Children in core myth | none named | principal Copil narratives | NEG |

The wife-and-daughter material should not be silently added to the revenge myth. It belongs to attempts to reconstruct Copil as a local political actor or lineage figure.

### Documented interactions with every named divine being or hero

1. **Malinalxochitl** — Mother and instructor; tells Copil of Huitzilopochtli’s abandonment, provoking his vow of revenge. Durán, 1570s; *Crónica mexicana*, c. 1598; *Crónica Mexicáyotl*, early seventeenth century. **[CE]**

2. **Chimalcuauhtli** — Named as Copil’s father in the *Crónica Mexicáyotl* genealogy; no deed jointly performed with Copil is narrated. Early seventeenth century. **[RV/CE]**

3. **Huitzilopochtli** — Maternal uncle, target of Copil’s vengeance, supernatural discoverer of his plot, and commander of his killing and the disposal of his heart. *Historia de los mexicanos por sus pinturas*, 1530s; Durán, 1570s; Tezozómoc, late sixteenth/early seventeenth century. **[EA/CE]**

4. **Cuauhtlequetzqui / Cuauhcoatl** — Mexica priest or leader who, under Huitzilopochtli’s order, receives, carries, and casts Copil’s heart into the reeds. *Crónica Mexicáyotl*, early seventeenth century. **[CE]**

5. **Quetzalcoatl** — No face-to-face interaction. The heart is cast from or near a red-and-black stone seat said to be where Quetzalcoatl rested during his departure toward Tlillan Tlapallan. *Crónica Mexicáyotl*, early seventeenth century. **[CE; indirect spatial association only]**

6. **Tenoch** — The founder-leader participates in the migration and recognition of the foundation sign in related accounts, but the core texts do not securely narrate a personal encounter between Tenoch and living Copil. Their pairing as duelists or personal rivals is a later harmonization. **[NEG/LS]**

7. **Atl-Tenoch** — In a separate *Crónica Mexicáyotl* list, Atl-Tenoch and a man named Copil both appear among ancestral leaders or deity-bearers. The text does not establish that this bearer Copil is Malinalxochitl’s son. Early seventeenth century. **[RV; possible namesake]**

8. **Xomimitl** — Appears among priestly or migration leaders and in events around the foundation, but no unambiguous direct encounter with the enemy Copil is stated in the core episode. **[NEG]**

9. **Tláloc** — Modern structural interpretations connect the lake, nopal, and watery foundation with Tláloc and the Templo Mayor’s rain-god half, but no primary Copil narrative states a meeting or transaction between Copil and Tláloc. **[NEG/MSR]**

10. **Coyolxauhqui** — Copil’s dismemberment and opposition to Huitzilopochtli have been compared with Coyolxauhqui’s defeat at Coatepec. No early source says that they meet, are siblings, are identical, or act together. **[NEG/MSR]**

11. **Centzon Huitznahua** — Modern comparison links Copil’s anti-Mexica coalition to Huitzilopochtli’s stellar enemies at Coatepec. No direct interaction with Copil is narrated. **[NEG/MSR]**

12. **Xicomoyahualtzin** — In reconstructed genealogical material, a woman of this name is Copil’s consort and mother of a daughter likewise named Xicomoyahualtzin. This relationship is absent from the canonical revenge narrative. **[RV/MSR]**

No other named god or hero has a securely documented direct interaction with Copil in the examined corpus.

## 5. Cult, tomb, and ritual

### Cult

**[NEG]** There is no securely documented pre-Conquest cult devoted specifically to Copil:

- no temple securely named for him;
- no priesthood;
- no annual festival;
- no calendrical feast;
- no established iconographic costume;
- no dedicatory inscription;
- no securely identified offering addressed to him;
- no oracle;
- no prescribed sacrifice “to Copil.”

Chimalpahin’s Christianizing list of beings formerly “made gods” includes Copil, but this isolated statement does not establish the institutional form, location, or antiquity of such worship.

Copil is better described as a sacralized figure within the foundation narrative than as a god with a demonstrated cult. **[CE/MSR]**

### Head-place and alleged tomb

The *Crónica Mexicáyotl* places his severed head on Tepetzintli and associates the location with Acopilco. This is literary-toponymic evidence. **[CE]**

No excavated burial has been identified as Copil’s; no chain of custody exists for purported bones, skull, or heart relics. **[NEG]**

### Cerro del Elefante sculpture

A damaged stone figure reportedly found at Cerro del Elefante in Hidalgo has been proposed by some researchers as a representation of Copil. The object’s archaeological context is poor, its date is uncertain, and the iconographic identification is hypothetical. The published study explicitly describes the proposal as susceptible to revision in light of empirical evidence. [“El ‘Cópil’ del cerro del Elefante”](https://www.revistas.inah.gob.mx/index.php/arqueologia/article/download/15654/16682/)

It cannot presently be used as proof of a Copil shrine or historic burial. **[MSR]**

### Templo Mayor and the foundation axis

The Templo Mayor, built in successive stages in the sacred precinct of Tenochtitlan, materialized the city’s divine center and was divided between Huitzilopochtli and Tláloc. Archaeology securely attests eagle imagery, heart sacrifice, military symbolism, and the deliberate recreation of sacred geography. [INAH, Templo Mayor](https://lugares.inah.gob.mx/en/node/4463)

It does not securely attest Copil by name. No excavated offering has been demonstrated to be “Copil’s heart,” and no structure can be identified as his heroon.

**[MSR]** The nopal growing from his heart nevertheless supplies a mythic charter for the same sacred center: the body of an enemy becomes the territorial root of the Mexica capital. The story converts conquest and sacrifice into emplacement.

### Ritual pattern

Copil’s death follows recognizable sacrificial actions—capture or execution, opening of the chest, extraction of the heart, decapitation, and spatial deposition of body parts. **[CE]** Whether the narrative records a particular historical sacrifice or retrospectively models the past through later Mexica sacrificial practice cannot be decided.

His heart is not a passive relic. In Nahua thought the heart, *yōllōtl*, was associated with vitality, intention, cognition, and animating force. The transformation of an enemy heart into the city’s living plant is therefore culturally coherent. A UNAM study cites the *Historia de los mexicanos* specifically for Huitzilopochtli’s instruction to deposit Copil’s heart at the future dwelling-place. [UNAM, *El nahualismo*](https://historicas.unam.mx/publicaciones/publicadigital/libros/el/ELN003.pdf)

## 6. Historical evolution of the figure

### Possible pre-Conquest formation: before 1519

**[MSR]** The eagle–nopal–stone emblem was already a state symbol in late Mexica Tenochtitlan. Monumental and pictorial evidence securely predating or closely following the conquest makes it unlikely that the entire foundation complex was invented by Spanish chroniclers.

What remains unproven is whether the named Copil narrative existed in precisely its surviving form before 1519. The absence of a securely pre-Conquest text naming him leaves room for late pre-Conquest reformulation or early colonial elaboration.

### First colonial documentation: 1530s–1550s

**[EA]** The *Historia de los mexicanos por sus pinturas* supplies the named heart-and-foundation episode in the 1530s. Codex Mendoza makes the eagle–nopal–stone emblem visible by about 1541–1542. Other early pictorial histories preserve the migration route but vary in their treatment of the foundation.

This is the firm documentary threshold.

### Late-sixteenth-century canonical shaping

**[CE]** Durán and Tezozómoc turn the compact etiological episode into a drama of sibling rupture, magical danger, filial revenge, coalition-building, divine intelligence, execution, and foundation.

Their agreement is important but partly genealogical: both probably drew on related Indigenous historical materials or the hypothetical lost “Crónica X.”

### Early-seventeenth-century Nahua elaboration

**[CE]** The *Crónica Mexicáyotl* gives the narrative its richest Nahuatl topography and ritual sequencing. It also binds the new capital to Toltec antiquity by placing Quetzalcoatl’s abandoned stone seat within the heart’s trajectory.

Chimalpahin’s copying and compilation preserved material while also subjecting it to Christian historical judgment.

### Colonial and antiquarian reception

**[MA/LS]** Colonial writers increasingly organized heterogeneous pictorial and oral traditions into linear chronicles. Christian vocabulary recast Malinalxochitl and Copil as witches, false gods, or mortal deceivers.

Eighteenth- and nineteenth-century collectors—Boturini, León y Gama, Aubin, Goupil and others—dispersed, copied, catalogued, and eventually institutionalized the manuscripts. The original Chimalpopoca manuscript later disappeared, making copies and editions indispensable but complicating textual certainty.

### National period

**[LS]** Independent Mexico appropriated the eagle, nopal, stone, lake, and eventually serpent as national heraldry. Copil himself remained largely implicit: his sacrificed heart survives beneath the visible emblem without usually being named.

The modern state symbol therefore contains a mythic structure older than the nation-state while also reflecting nineteenth- and twentieth-century heraldic standardization.

### Twentieth- and twenty-first-century reinvention

**[MSR/F/MI]** Archaeologists, ethnohistorians, and philologists recovered the Copil episode as a key to Mexica sacred geography. Popular and neo-Indigenous accounts increasingly treat him as a lunar prince, matriarchal initiate, suppressed legitimate heir, astrologer, or emblem of an older peaceful spirituality. Those interpretations may carry modern religious or political value but exceed the colonial evidence.

## 7. Modern reception and reinvention

### National emblem

The eagle-on-nopal motif became a principal emblem of Mexican collective identity and appears in successive national seals and flags. The current state emblem does not label the stone or cactus as Copil’s body, but scholarship and popular retelling reconnect the emblem with his heart.

A Mexican government account describes the eagle and nopal as the pre-Hispanic component of a national emblem later combined with Spanish and liberal-European elements. [Secretaría de Cultura, “La bandera nacional”](https://www.gob.mx/cultura/articulos/bandera-nacional?idiom=es)

**[MSR]** Copil’s modern political significance is thus mostly metonymic: he is the hidden sacrificial substrate of a publicly visible national symbol.

### Literature and public history

- Schoolbooks, municipal histories, museum exhibitions, and illustrated retellings reproduce the heart–nopal episode, usually without the source variants. **[F/LS]**
- The 2009 collective volume *Corazón de Cópil*, prepared in memory of ethnologist Barbro Dahlgren, examines written traditions concerning the sacred precinct and foundation of Tenochtitlan. It is scholarship and commemoration, not an ancient title. **[MSR]**
- Gutiérre Tibón’s *Historia del nombre y de la fundación de México* (1975) helped popularize symbolic readings of Copil, the heart, the stone, the nopal, and the city-name. **[MSR]**

No Copil-centered opera, internationally canonical novel, major feature film, or ancient dramatic cycle comparable to Greek heroic reception was identified in the examined scholarly record. **[NEG]**

### Neo-Indigenous and esoteric reinterpretation

Contemporary accounts sometimes claim that:

- Malinalxochitl is simply “the Moon”;
- Copil represents matriarchy or the “feminine current”;
- his heart reconciles red and blue cosmic waters;
- *Copilli* means royal blood rather than a headdress;
- Copil was an astrologer initiated in a secret Malinalco school;
- his death records the patriarchal suppression of a peaceful lunar religion.

These claims are not stated by the primary sources. Some combine real Nahua symbols with twentieth- or twenty-first-century esoteric synthesis. **[MI]**

### Nationalist use

The broader foundation image has repeatedly been used to naturalize territorial unity: the capital and nation are portrayed as destined by prophecy. Copil’s death can thereby disappear behind the triumphant emblem. **[MSR]**

Conversely, revisionist retellings sometimes cast him as an Indigenous dissident or victim of Mexica imperial ideology. This is a legitimate modern ethical rereading but not a recoverable statement of Copil’s own politics. **[MSR]**

No well-documented extremist cult centered specifically on Copil was found. **[NEG]**

### Comparative hero theories

Copil can be compared with sacrificial foundation figures, dismembered culture heroes, divine nephews, or enemy bodies transformed into landscapes. Rank, Raglan, Campbell, Dumézil, and structuralist approaches provide comparative frameworks, but none constitutes primary evidence for Copil. Assigning him to a universal “hero’s journey” obscures the fact that he is defeated and appropriated by the founders rather than returning as victorious hero. **[MSR]**

## 8. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Myth versus event

The central debate is not whether Copil’s story is “true” or “false,” but how much political memory may have been incorporated into its mythic form.

#### Historical-memory hypothesis

**[MSR]** Some scholars interpret Copil as a transformed memory of a Malinalca, Toluca-region, or other local leader who opposed Mexica settlement at Chapultepec. Supporting considerations include:

- the narrative’s dense Basin topography;
- known conflicts between Mexica migrants and established communities;
- genealogical traditions assigning Copil a consort or descendants;
- the political realism of coalition-building;
- place-names associated with his body.

#### Criticisms

- no contemporary inscription names him;
- the sources were written two to three centuries after the conventional thirteenth-century setting;
- places and dates disagree;
- the story is structured by kinship, sacrifice, divine command, and etiological transformation;
- toponyms may generate stories rather than preserve events;
- Durán and Tezozómoc are not wholly independent.

The safest conclusion is that a historical conflict may underlie the story, but no individual historical Copil is recoverable. **[MSR]**

### Dating the legendary action

The *Crónica Mexicáyotl* tradition has been correlated with the year 1 House, sometimes converted to 1285, while the foundation sign is conventionally assigned to 2 House/1325. These Gregorian conversions depend on calendar correlations and chronicle reconstruction.

Neither date is an attestation date. Copil is first documented in the sixteenth century. Presenting “1285” as his verified death date is a category error. **[MI if unqualified]**

### The “Crónica X” problem

Durán’s *Historia* and Tezozómoc’s *Crónica mexicana* share sequences and wording that led Robert Barlow and later scholars to posit a lost common source, the “Crónica X.”

- If correct, it may represent Indigenous historical material earlier than either surviving chronicle.
- It remains reconstructed, not extant.
- Shared dependence reduces the value of Durán and Tezozómoc as independent witnesses.

**[MSR]**

### Pictorial versus alphabetic testimony

Elizabeth Hill Boone and later codex scholars emphasize that painted histories do not merely illustrate alphabetic narratives; they organize time, place, movement, and political legitimacy through distinct conventions. [Boone, *Stories in Red and Black*](https://utpdistribution.com/9780292719897/stories-in-red-and-black/)

Applied to Copil, this means:

- an eagle-nopal emblem cannot automatically be expanded into every detail in Durán;
- absence of a named human figure does not show that the associated oral tradition was unknown;
- later alphabetic narration may unpack, alter, or harmonize pictorial signs.

### Toltec linkage

The *Crónica Mexicáyotl* locates the casting of the heart at a stone associated with Quetzalcoatl’s departure. Modern scholars interpret this as a strategy for rooting Mexica sovereignty in Toltec sacred history.

H. B. Nicholson’s source-critical study of Topiltzin Quetzalcoatl is relevant methodologically and to the Toltec background, but it is not a monograph about Copil. Nigel Davies’s work on the Toltecs is likewise contextual. Neither licenses calling Copil a Toltec king. [Smithsonian catalogue for Nicholson](https://www.si.edu/object/topiltzin-quetzalcoatl-once-and-future-lord-toltecs-hb-nicholson%3Asiris_sil_716672)

### Symbolic interpretations

Michel Graulich and other comparativists have connected the heart, cactus, eagle, sacrifice, and foundation with wider Mesoamerican ideas of cosmic renewal, warfare, and solar nourishment. Such interpretations illuminate the narrative’s grammar but are not additional attestations of Copil.

The comparison with Coyolxauhqui is especially attractive:

- both oppose Huitzilopochtli;
- both are defeated and dismembered;
- bodily remains mark sacred space;
- both conflicts establish Huitzilopochtli’s supremacy.

Yet the sources never identify Copil with Coyolxauhqui, and the genders, kinship relations, settings, and narrative outcomes differ. **[MSR]**

### Archaeological debate

Archaeology securely confirms:

- the antiquity of eagle, cactus, heart, and warfare imagery;
- the Templo Mayor’s Huitzilopochtli–Tláloc dual structure;
- the ritual deposition of human and animal remains;
- repeated rebuilding of Tenochtitlan’s sacred center.

It does not confirm:

- Copil as an individual;
- his execution at a particular hill;
- a heart deposited beneath the foundation;
- his head at Acopilco;
- a Copil shrine.

The Cerro del Elefante identification is a hypothesis weakened by uncertain archaeological context. **[MSR]**

### Discredited or unsupported claims still circulating

1. **Copil was an Aztec “prince” in the later dynastic sense.** The early sources do not give him a Tenochtitlan royal office. **[MI]**
2. **He was a demigod because his mother was a goddess.** The sources’ categories are less systematic and do not call him a demigod. **[LS/MI]**
3. **He was a Toltec hero.** Unsupported. **[MI]**
4. **The Codex Boturini depicts his heart becoming the cactus.** The surviving codex is incomplete and does not explicitly do so. **[MI]**
5. **His excavated tomb or body has been found.** No. **[NEG]**
6. **The serpent in today’s arms is an unchanged component of the oldest foundation sign.** No; early prey and speech/war signs vary. **[MI]**
7. **The name means “royal blood.”** Lexically unsupported; the secure comparison is a conical headdress or diadem. **[MI]**
8. **Copil was an astronomer or astrologer.** No secure primary locus found. **[MI]**
9. **The story proves an ancient matriarchal war against patriarchy.** This is modern ideological reconstruction. **[MI]**
10. **Nicholson’s 2001 book identifies Copil as Topiltzin Quetzalcoatl.** It does not. **[MI]**

## 10. Sources

### Primary and early colonial sources

- Anonymous. *Historia de los mexicanos por sus pinturas*, probably 1530s. Earliest named Copil account; Spanish compilation from Indigenous paintings and testimony.
- Codex Mendoza, c. 1541–1542, fol. 2r. Foundation emblem; no named Copil.
- Codex Boturini / *Tira de la Peregrinación*, first half of the sixteenth century. Migration pictorial ending before the complete foundation.
- Codex Azcatitlan, later sixteenth century. Pictorial migration and foundation tradition.
- Codex Aubin, sixteenth- to seventeenth-century compilation. Migration annals and foundation material.
- Codex Mexicanus, composite later-sixteenth-century manuscript. Migration and foundation sequence.
- Durán, Diego. *Historia de las Indias de Nueva España e islas de Tierra Firme*, 1570s–c. 1581.
- Alvarado Tezozómoc, Fernando. *Crónica mexicana*, c. 1598.
- Alvarado Tezozómoc/Chimalpahin textual tradition. *Crónica Mexicáyotl*, early seventeenth century.
- Chimalpahin Quauhtlehuanitzin, Domingo. Nahuatl annals and historical collections, early seventeenth century.
- *Anales de Cuauhtitlan*, c. 1570. Relevant negative evidence.
- *Leyenda de los Soles*, 1558. Relevant negative evidence.
- *Historia Tolteca-Chichimeca*, c. 1547–1560. Comparative foundation material; no Copil narrative.
- Sahagún, Bernardino de. *Historia general de las cosas de Nueva España* / Florentine Codex, sixteenth century. Context and negative evidence.
- Alva Ixtlilxochitl, Fernando de. Historical works, early seventeenth century. Context and negative evidence.
