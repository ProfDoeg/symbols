# Huemac: Research Dossier

> **Traditional class:** legendary Toltec *tlahtoāni* (ruler), royal priest, culture-failure hero, and after death an immortal or otherworldly lord of Cincalco. He is not securely attested as a demigod or as the recipient of an independently documented hero cult.
>
> **Earliest secure attestation:** no contemporary Toltec-period inscription or image names Huemac. His earliest surviving documentary attestations are sixteenth-century colonial Nahua traditions: most importantly the mid-sixteenth-century *Historia Tolteca-Chichimeca*, Sahagún’s Nahua materials culminating in the *Florentine Codex* of 1577, and the *Anales de Cuauhtitlan* and *Leyenda de los Soles*, preserved in the now-missing manuscript conventionally called the *Codex Chimalpopoca*. These sources may preserve older oral and pictorial traditions, but their surviving texts postdate Tula’s abandonment by roughly four centuries.

## Evidentiary key

- **[SEA] Securely attested early material:** securely dated early-colonial Indigenous-language testimony or demonstrably pre-Conquest archaeology. It does **not** mean that the narrated event is historically verified.
- **[LCE] Later canonical elaboration:** a later but influential colonial retelling.
- **[RV] Regional variant:** a tradition belonging to a particular community or historiographic lineage.
- **[LS] Learned systematization:** a chronicler’s or scholar’s attempt to reconcile chronologies and identities.
- **[SI] Syncretistic identification:** equation with another Indigenous or Christian figure.
- **[EMA] Early-modern accretion:** colonial additions, Christianizing interpretation, or antiquarian harmonization.
- **[F] Folklore or living popular tradition.**
- **[MSR] Modern scholarly reconstruction:** an inference rather than a statement made by a primary source.
- **[MI] Modern invention or unsupported modern assertion.**

## 1. Identification, names, and titles

### Orientation

**[SEA]** In the major Nahua accounts Huemac is a human ruler of Tollan, usually its last ruler, under whom famine, factional war, sacrifice, portents, and dispersal terminate the Toltec age. The *Anales de Cuauhtitlan* makes him a former minister of Quetzalcoatl who becomes ruler after Tlilcoatzin; the *Historia Tolteca-Chichimeca* makes him a foundling whose demands provoke war between Tolteca-Chichimeca and Nonoalca-Chichimeca; the *Leyenda de los Soles* makes his refusal of maize offered by the Tlaloque the immediate mythical cause of a four-year famine. These are related but irreducible narratives.

**[SEA/LCE]** His ending likewise divides. The *Anales* says that, seven years after the dispersal, he hanged himself in the cave of Cincalco at Chapultepec. The *Historia Tolteca-Chichimeca* says his enemies dragged him from the cave and shot him with arrows. The *Leyenda de los Soles* says only that he “entered Cincalco,” language compatible with disappearance or translation rather than an ordinary death. By the late-sixteenth-century Durán–Tezozómoc tradition, Huemac still lives as Cincalco’s inaccessible lord and Motecuhzoma II seeks admission to his realm.

### Name and orthography

- **Huēmac / Uemac / Vemac / Huémac / Hueymac / Huehmac. [SEA/LCE]** These are alphabetic colonial spellings of the same name. Sixteenth-century orthography commonly writes initial /w/ with *u*, *v*, or *hu*. *Huemac* is not an Aztec-script spelling; it is an alphabetic form.
- **“The one of great hands” or “Great/Powerful Hand.” [LS]** This traditional interpretation connects *huē(y)-*, “great,” with *mā(itl)*, “hand,” and is recorded or repeated by Ixtlilxochitl, later lexicography, and the UNAM *Gran Diccionario Náhuatl*, which cautiously gives “possibly ‘the one with great hands.’”
- **“Great Gift.” [MSR]** Dana Leibsohn and Camilla Townsend understand the name in the *Historia Tolteca-Chichimeca* as “Big Gift,” an ironic reference to the abandoned child whom Tezcatlipoca gives the two communities so that he will destroy their concord. This reading explains the story’s wordplay better than “Great Hand,” but it should not be imposed on every Huemac tradition.
- **Handprint etiology. [EMA]** Ixtlilxochitl reports that some said Huemac received the name because he impressed his hands into rock “as if in soft wax”; others interpreted it as “great or powerful hand.” This is a seventeenth-century explanatory legend, not archaeological evidence for a Huemac handprint.
- **Atecpanécatl / Atempanécatl. [SEA]** The *Anales de Cuauhtitlan* calls this Huemac’s “royal sovereign name.” The form is variously normalized; a plausible sense is “person of the water-side/water-palace precinct.” It is a title or accession name, not proof of a separate person.
- **Tlahtoāni of Tollan. [SEA]** The *Anales*, Sahagún, and later chronicles class him as ruler.
- **Minister or priest of Quetzalcoatl. [SEA]** The *Anales* says Huemac served Quetzalcoatl but lost that status after sexual relations with beings identified in the Christianized translation as “demonesses,” manifestations or agents of Yaotl/Tezcatlipoca.
- **Lord of Cincalco. [LCE]** Durán and the related *Crónica Mexicana* tradition treat him as the enduring ruler of a paradisal or subterranean realm accessible near Chapultepec.
- **“Father of the children of the clouds.” [LCE/SI]** Durán says he saw a painting of “Topiltzin/Hueymac” with a long robe and large hat, captioned *padre de los hijos de las nubes*. Because Durán explicitly merges Huemac and Topiltzin here, this is evidence for colonial identification, not a secure independent epithet of the last king.

### Identification with Quetzalcoatl and Topiltzin

**[SEA]** The *Anales de Cuauhtitlan* normally distinguishes Ce Acatl Topiltzin Quetzalcoatl from Huemac: Quetzalcoatl belongs to an earlier episode; Huemac later serves his priestly institution and eventually rules. Its king list places several rulers between them.

**[LCE/SI]** Durán instead writes of a great lord called Topiltzin, also “Father,” whom the Mexica called Hueymac. Ixtlilxochitl similarly applies Quetzalcoatl and Huemac to a virgin, holy lawgiver who came from the east, taught fasting, erected a cross, and was associated with rain and health.

**[EMA/SI]** The saintly, celibate, cross-bearing Huemac-Quetzalcoatl is a colonial Christianizing construction. It cannot be retrojected unchanged into pre-Conquest belief.

**[MSR]** Paul Kirchhoff, H. B. Nicholson, Nigel Davies, Michel Graulich, Susan Gillespie, Alfredo López Austin, and later scholars have variously treated Topiltzin and Huemac as distinct rulers, overlapping figures, doubled first-and-last kings, offices, or transformations within a cyclical Tollan narrative. No consensus permits their historical identification.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### No contemporary Huemac document

**[MSR]** Excavation confirms that Tula, Hidalgo, was a large Early Postclassic city. Recent synthesis of 68 radiocarbon and seven archaeomagnetic dates indicates that Tula Grande and the Tollan-phase city were abandoned before Aztec II settlement; later Mexica-period activity at the ruins shows sustained interest in the site. None of this archaeology identifies Huemac by name.

**[MSR]** Therefore “Huemac reigned from 994 to 1070,” “1098 to 1162,” or similar Gregorian dates are correlations imposed on colonial year-counts. They are not contemporary dates. Differences arise from where scholars anchor repeated 52-year calendar cycles.

### Principal sources

1. **Historia Tolteca-Chichimeca**, Nahuatl with pictorial writing, Cuauhtinchan, mid-sixteenth century; surviving manuscript on European paper, about fifty folios, now BnF Fonds Mexicain 46–58. **[SEA/RV]**  
   It presents the Cuauhtinchan Tolteca-Chichimeca past. Huemac is an abandoned child found and raised by Toltecs, described as Tezcatlipoca’s offering or trace. As a young ruler he demands a woman with exceptionally broad buttocks, rejects the Nonoalca’s candidate, causes rupture between the allied communities, flees to Cincalco, and is captured, shot, and killed. This is the oldest securely datable surviving Huemac-bearing document presently identifiable.

2. **Bernardino de Sahagún and Nahua collaborators, *Historia general* / Florentine Codex**, Nahuatl and Spanish; research begun in the 1540s–1550s, final illuminated manuscript completed in 1577. **[SEA]**  
   Book 3, in the Tollan cycle, distinguishes Huemac from Quetzalcoatl. Huemac’s daughter sees the naked stranger Tohueyo—Tezcatlipoca in disguise—becomes violently lovesick, and is married to him. Huemac then attempts to dispose of the socially contemptible son-in-law in war, but Tohueyo wins. Subsequent spectacles and deceptions help destroy Tollan. The narrative is a divine-subversion tale, not a court chronicle in the modern sense.

3. **Anales de Cuauhtitlan**, Nahuatl annals compiled in the sixteenth century, often dated around 1570; preserved in the *Codex Chimalpopoca*. **[SEA/RV]**  
   It supplies the fullest annalistic Huemac cycle: accession as Atecpanécatl; marriage to Coacueye; priesthood of Quetzalcoatl; seduction by Tezcatlipoca/Yaotl’s female forms; famine; sacrifice of Huemac’s children; Ixcuinanme rites; warfare and flaying; collapse in year 1-Flint; attempted cave entry; migration; and suicide at Cincalco in 7-Rabbit. The manuscript photographed for the 1945 facsimile subsequently disappeared, probably in the late 1940s; modern work depends on photographs, copies, and earlier transcriptions.

4. **Leyenda de los Soles**, Nahuatl, written in 1558 according to its closing notice and preserved as another section of the *Codex Chimalpopoca*. **[SEA]**  
   It contains the ballgame with the Tlaloque, the substitution of maize ears and leaves for jade and quetzal feathers, Huemac’s refusal, four years of famine, the sacrifice of the Mexica girl Quetzalxotzin, the restoration of rain, Toltec disappearance, and Huemac’s entrance into Cincalco. Its temporal structure transfers world succession from Toltecs to Mexica.

5. **Diego Durán, *Historia de las Indias de Nueva España e islas de Tierra Firme***, Spanish, historical treatise completed in 1581; autograph manuscript preserved in Madrid. **[LCE/SI]**  
   Durán identifies Topiltzin and Hueymac in his early Tollan discussion. Near the Conquest he narrates Motecuhzoma II’s attempt to escape to Cincalco. Envoys ask Huemac to receive Motecuhzoma as his servant; Huemac initially refuses, requires penance, and finally arranges to meet him, but a supernatural warning delivered through a commoner or divine representative prevents the flight.

6. **Hernando Alvarado Tezozómoc, *Crónica Mexicana***, Spanish, conventionally dated c. 1598 and closely related to the lost “Crónica X.” **[LCE]**  
   Its Cincalco account is more expansive than Durán’s: Motecuhzoma believes Cincalco offers immortality, abundant food, flowers, and contentment in Huemac’s company. He sends flayed human skins as gifts and offers to serve as Huemac’s sweeper. Huemac demands to know why the Mexica ruler deserves admission.

7. **Crónica Mexicáyotl**, Nahuatl compilation associated with Tezozómoc, Alonso Franco, and Chimalpahin; composed and recopied across the late sixteenth and early seventeenth centuries. **[LCE/RV]**  
   It belongs to the Mexica migration and dynastic corpus and preserves Tollan/Chapultepec frameworks, but it is not an independent early account of the complete Huemac cycle. Its complicated authorship and Chimalpahin’s chronological corrections make it unsafe to cite as a single eyewitness voice.

8. **Domingo Chimalpahin, *Memorial breve acerca de la fundación de Culhuacan***, Nahuatl, early seventeenth century. **[RV/LCE]**  
   It makes Totepeuh of Culhuacan Huemac’s father. Because other sources make Totepeuh the father of Topiltzin, this genealogy contributed to later Huemac–Quetzalcoatl equations.

9. **Fernando de Alva Ixtlilxochitl, historical relations and *Historia de la nación chichimeca***, Spanish, early seventeenth century. **[LCE/EMA/SI]**  
   His Texcocan-oriented synthesis presents variant Toltec genealogies and Christianizes Quetzalcoatl/Huemac as a righteous eastern teacher. Its synchronisms and etymologies are important evidence for colonial historiography, not transparent Toltec history.

10. **Juan de Torquemada, *Monarquía indiana***, Spanish, 1615. **[LS/EMA]**  
    Torquemada systematizes earlier missionary and Indigenous traditions and makes Huemac a contemporary of Quetzalcoatl in some passages. His work is secondary to the sixteenth-century Nahua records.

### Sources often invoked but not direct Huemac attestations

- **Codex Boturini / Tira de la Peregrinación**, early-colonial pictorial migration history. **[SEA—negative finding]** It depicts Mexica movement through Tollan and Chapultepec but does not securely label Huemac. It supplies geographical and political memory, not a portrait or biography.
- **Other migration codices. [SEA—negative finding]** They demonstrate the importance of Tollan, Colhuacan, and Chapultepec in Mexica origin history, but apparent figures at these places should not be named Huemac without a gloss or a demonstrable textual correspondence.
- **Templo Mayor archaeology. [SEA—negative finding]** Finds demonstrate Mexica collection and ritual reuse of older objects and an ideological interest in Tollan. No excavated offering securely names or represents Huemac.

## 3. The story

### The foundling who divides Tollan

**[SEA/RV]** In the *Historia Tolteca-Chichimeca*, Toltecs find Huemac as a child, take him in, and raise and educate him. The manuscript describes him as Tezcatlipoca’s offering, creation, or trace, destined to cause the Tolteca-Chichimeca and Nonoalca-Chichimeca to destroy one another.

**[SEA/RV]** As a young man Huemac demands that the Nonoalca guarding his house obtain a woman whose buttocks are four hand-spans wide. He rejects the woman they bring. The Nonoalca take the rejection as an intolerable demand or insult; factional conflict follows. Toltecs cry for Huemac’s death. He flees to the cave of Cincalco, but his enemies seize him, bring him out, shoot him with arrows, and kill him. The inhabitants conceal Quetzalcoatl’s belongings before abandoning Tollan.

**[MSR]** The tale explains political dissolution through a disastrous “gift,” sexual entitlement, and failed reciprocal service. Leibsohn and Townsend read “Big Gift” as deliberate irony. This interpretation fits this source particularly well but need not govern unrelated versions.

### Accession, Coacueye, and loss of priestly status

**[SEA/RV]** The *Anales de Cuauhtitlan* says Tlilcoatzin dies in 9-Rabbit and Huemac succeeds him with the royal name Atecpanécatl. Shortly before accession he married Coacueye, described as a *mocihuaquezqui*—literally a woman who “raises herself up” like a warrior, a term associated with women dying in childbirth—and as having a back or hips a fathom wide.

**[SEA]** Quauhtli, a fire-priest from Xicococ, sits on Quetzalcoatl’s mat and throne as Quetzalcoatl’s image and keeper of the gods. Huemac replaces him as Quetzalcoatl’s minister. Yaotl/Tezcatlipoca and female beings deceive or sexually engage Huemac; after intercourse with them he ceases to be Quetzalcoatl’s minister.

**[MSR]** Christian translations call these beings “devils” and “demonesses.” The underlying Nahua scheme concerns Tezcatlipoca’s transformative and adversarial powers; “demon” records the colonial translator’s theology, not necessarily the narrators’ category.

### Huemac’s daughter and the naked Tohueyo

**[SEA]** In Sahagún’s Book 3, Tezcatlipoca takes the form of a naked Huastec or foreigner, Tohueyo, selling green chile in Tollan’s marketplace. Huemac’s daughter sees his exposed penis, becomes physically ill with desire, and can be cured only by union with him. Huemac has Tohueyo found and orders him to sleep with her; marriage follows.

**[SEA]** Toltec nobles resent the ruler’s daughter marrying a socially despised stranger. Huemac sends Tohueyo into battle with dwarfs, hunchbacks, pages, and other persons whom he expects to be killed. Tohueyo instead destroys the enemy and returns victorious. Huemac must receive and honor him.

**[SEA]** Tezcatlipoca/Tohueyo then stages ruinous wonders: music and dancing that draw crowds to death, a tiny dancing figure that causes a lethal crush, a corpse whose stench kills, deceptive labor, and other portents. Versions distribute these acts differently between Tezcatlipoca’s names and disguises.

**[MSR]** The episode is not evidence that a historical Huemac’s daughter married an identifiable Huastec. It dramatizes foreignness, sexuality, royal misjudgment, hierarchy reversed, and divine destruction.

### Famine and the sacrifice of Huemac’s children

**[SEA/RV]** The *Anales* records seven years of famine beginning in 7-Rabbit. Supernatural powers demand Huemac’s legitimate children. He leaves the children at Xochiquetzalayapan, Huitzcoc, and Xicococ “to pay” with them. The text associates this with the beginning of extensive human sacrifice.

**[SEA]** The source does not say that Huemac personally kills the children; it does say that he delivers them as sacrificial payment. Any softened paraphrase obscures the episode.

### Ixcuinanme, warfare, flaying, and collapse

**[SEA/RV]** The *Anales* says the Ixcuinanme arrive from the Huastec region with captives and inaugurate arrow sacrifice at Tollan. Yaotl begins war against Nextlalpan, urges the Toltecs to kill captives, and introduces flaying. An Otomi woman is seized, flayed, and her skin worn by Xiuhcozcatl; the text makes this the beginning of Tótec’s wearing of human skin.

**[SEA/RV]** The annalist contrasts this with Ce Acatl Quetzalcoatl’s time, when Quetzalcoatl allegedly rejected human sacrifice. Under Huemac, the destructive rites attributed to the “devils” flourish. This is the annalist’s moral and ritual periodization, not archaeological proof that human sacrifice began at that moment.

### The ballgame against the Tlaloque

**[SEA]** In the *Leyenda de los Soles*, Huemac plays ball against the Tlaloque, the rain-associated plurality belonging to Tlaloc. The wager is named as jade beads (*chalchihuites*) and quetzal feathers. Huemac wins.

**[SEA]** The Tlaloque bring tender maize ears and their fresh green husks, treating these as their jade and feathers. Huemac refuses: those are not the precious objects he demanded. The Tlaloque pay literal jade and feathers but announce that they will hide “our jade”—the rain, greenness, and maize—and that the Toltecs will suffer four years.

**[SEA]** Frost, heat, and drought destroy crops, trees, maguey, and even stones. Toltecs starve. At Chapultepec a Tlaloc priest emerges from water and gives a Toltec maize to carry to Huemac. The gods demand the young Mexica girl Quetzalxotzin, daughter of Tozcuecuex.

**[SEA]** Huemac sends Chiconcohuatl and Cuetlachcohuatl to request her. After four days of fasting and mourning, the Mexica take the child to Pantitlan and sacrifice her. The Tlaloque put her heart and foods in her father’s gourd; rain resumes. The episode plainly includes child sacrifice and a transfer of fertility and historical succession from Toltec to Mexica.

**[MSR]** Graulich and other comparativists interpret Huemac’s error as mistaking the material sign for agricultural substance: he chooses elite treasure over maize. This is persuasive interpretation, not an explicit ancient gloss. The narrative simultaneously makes him culpable and makes divine powers demand a child’s death.

### Flight and failed entry to the cave

**[SEA/RV]** In 1-Flint, according to the *Anales*, Tollan breaks apart. At Cincoc Huemac sacrifices a man named Ce Coatl, described as the image or offering of his people, in an attempt to enter a cave on the road to Tlamacazcatzinco. Entry is refused. Huemac’s wife Cuauhnene gives birth at a place consequently named Cuauhnec.

**[SEA/RV]** Yaotl separates followers who remain with him from the departing Toltecs. The migrants pass through Chapultepec and Colhuacan and disperse to Cholula, Tehuacan, Cozcatlan, Nonohualco, Coixtlahuaca, and other places. The itinerary converts Tollan’s collapse into a charter for later communities.

### Death, disappearance, or survival

- **Arrow execution. [SEA/RV]** *Historia Tolteca-Chichimeca*: Huemac is pulled from Cincalco and shot dead.
- **Suicide by hanging. [SEA/RV]** *Anales de Cuauhtitlan*: after seven years’ wandering, he sees that no Toltecs remain, weeps, and hangs himself “from despair” in Cincalco’s cave at Chapultepec, in 7-Rabbit.
- **Entrance into Cincalco. [SEA]** *Leyenda de los Soles*: in 1-Flint the Toltec disappears and Huemac enters Cincalco. No method of death is given.
- **Immortal lord. [LCE]** Durán and Tezozómoc: Huemac still presides over Cincalco when Motecuhzoma II seeks him shortly before the Spanish invasion.
- **Holy traveler identified with Quetzalcoatl. [EMA/SI]** Ixtlilxochitl and Durán sometimes replace the failed last king with a celibate lawgiver who departs eastward.
- **Historical date. [MSR]** Proposed equivalents range across the eleventh and twelfth centuries; 1162 is a frequently cited correlation of 7-Rabbit, while older systems give 1070 or other dates. None is independently confirmed.

### Every documented named divine or heroic interaction

- **Tezcatlipoca:** Huemac is called Tezcatlipoca’s offered foundling or trace, destined to divide the two Tollan communities; *Historia Tolteca-Chichimeca*, mid-16th c. **[SEA/RV]**

- **Tezcatlipoca / Titlacahuan / Tohueyo:** disguised as a naked foreign chile seller, he makes Huemac’s daughter desire him, marries her, survives Huemac’s attempt to have him killed, and helps destroy Tollan; Sahagún, Book 3, 1577. **[SEA]**
- **Yaotl (“Enemy,” a name/aspect of Tezcatlipoca):** deceives Huemac, instigates war and sacrifice, and separates followers during the Toltec dispersal; *Anales de Cuauhtitlan*. **[SEA/RV]**
- **Quetzalcoatl:** Huemac serves as his minister and later loses that office; *Anales de Cuauhtitlan*. **[SEA/RV]**
- **Ce Acatl Topiltzin Quetzalcoatl:** distinguished from Huemac as the earlier, anti-sacrifice ruler; *Anales de Cuauhtitlan*. **[SEA/RV]**
- **Ce Acatl Topiltzin Quetzalcoatl:** identified with Huemac as one holy ruler; Durán, 1581, and Ixtlilxochitl, early 17th c. **[SI/EMA]**
- **Tlaloque:** play ball with Huemac, lose, offer maize as jade and feathers, and punish his rejection with four years of agricultural disaster; *Leyenda de los Soles*, 1558 manuscript tradition. **[SEA]**
- **Tlaloc:** his underwater priest restores maize and conveys the divine demand for Quetzalxotzin’s sacrifice; *Leyenda de los Soles*. The text stages interaction through Tlaloc’s priest rather than a face-to-face appearance by a singular Tlaloc. **[SEA]**
- **Ixcuinanme:** arrive at Tollan and inaugurate arrow sacrifice during Huemac’s reign; *Anales de Cuauhtitlan*. No direct conversation with Huemac is reported. **[SEA/RV]**
- **Tótec/Xipe Totec:** the *Anales* associates the first wearing of flayed skin by Tótec with Huemac’s period, but does not narrate Huemac meeting the god. **[SEA/RV]**
- **Coacueye:** Huemac’s broad-hipped warrior-woman wife; *Anales de Cuauhtitlan*. **[SEA/RV]**
- **Cuauhnene:** wife who gives birth during the exodus and gives her name to Cuauhnec; *Anales de Cuauhtitlan*. **[SEA/RV]**
- **Quauhtli:** fire-priest whose Quetzalcoatl office Huemac takes over; *Anales de Cuauhtitlan*. **[SEA/RV]**
- **Ce Coatl:** human representative or victim sacrificed by Huemac when attempting cave entry; *Anales de Cuauhtitlan*. **[SEA/RV]**
- **Quetzalxotzin:** child requested through Huemac and sacrificed at Pantitlan to restore fertility; *Leyenda de los Soles*. **[SEA]**
- **Tozcuecuex:** father of Quetzalxotzin, from whose community Huemac requests the child; *Leyenda de los Soles*. **[SEA]**
- **Chiconcohuatl and Cuetlachcohuatl:** Huemac’s messengers to obtain Quetzalxotzin; *Leyenda de los Soles*. **[SEA]**
- **Tlilcoatzin:** Huemac succeeds him as ruler of Tollan; *Anales de Cuauhtitlan*. **[SEA/RV]**
- **Nauhyotzin:** leads the Colhua departure in the same collapse sequence; *Anales de Cuauhtitlan*. A close kinship with Huemac is not securely stated there. **[SEA—negative qualification]**
- **Motecuhzoma II:** asks Huemac to admit him to Cincalco as servant; Huemac initially rejects him, later sets conditions or a meeting, and the escape is prevented; Durán, 1581, and *Crónica Mexicana*, c. 1598. **[LCE]**
- **Huitzilopochtli:** migration traditions link the Mexica god’s people to succession after Tollan, but no primary Huemac account securely narrates a direct Huemac–Huitzilopochtli meeting. **[SEA—absence of direct interaction]**
- **Mixcoatl:** later Toltec reconstructions place Mixcoatl, Topiltzin, and Huemac in one dynastic “saga,” but no early source used here documents a direct Huemac–Mixcoatl encounter. **[MSR—famous reconstructed pairing]**

## 5. Cult, tomb, cave, and ritual

### Cincalco and Chapultepec

**[SEA]** Cincalco literally evokes the “place/house of maize ears” (*cin-tli*, maize ear; *cal-li*, house; locative *-co*). In the Huemac narratives it is a cave-associated destination at or near Chapultepec, a place of death, disappearance, fertility, and continued existence.

**[LCE]** Durán sometimes writes *Cicalco* and glosses it as a “place of rabbits,” producing a lunar/fertility interpretation. This spelling and gloss should not erase the better-attested maize-house etymology.

**[LCE]** The Motecuhzoma episode shows that Cincalco remained a potent royal destination in conquest-era memory: Huemac can receive or reject a living king, and the realm promises food, flowers, water, and freedom from ordinary death.

### Tomb and relics

**[SEA—negative finding]** No excavated tomb, bones, funerary deposit, or inscribed relic has been securely identified as Huemac’s. The *Anales* names a cave as the place of hanging; the *Historia Tolteca-Chichimeca* places an execution at a cave; neither supplies an archaeologically verified burial.

**[MI]** Claims that a presently visible Chapultepec tunnel, audiorama cave, or elevator passage is demonstrably “Huemac’s tomb” are modern identifications unless supported by excavation and stratigraphy. They may preserve local place-lore but are not verified archaeology.

### Cult

**[SEA—negative finding]** No primary source records a temple dedicated specifically to Huemac, a priesthood bearing his name, calendrical feast, regular sacrifice to him, named oracle, sacred games in his honor, or theophoric personal names demonstrating a formal Huemac cult.

**[LCE]** Motecuhzoma’s embassies, gifts of flayed skins, penitential preparations, and intended entry into Huemac’s realm are ritualized communication with an otherworldly ruler. They establish belief in Huemac’s continuing power, but not necessarily a public cult institution.

**[MSR]** “Heroized dead” is therefore possible as an analytical description of the Cincalco Huemac, but stronger claims that he was worshipped as a god exceed the evidence.

### Images

**[LCE]** Durán says he saw a painting of the merged Topiltzin-Hueymac dressed in a long robe and broad hat. The surviving Durán manuscript contains colonial miniatures influenced by Indigenous pictorial conventions. Its figure is evidence for sixteenth-century reception, not a contemporary Toltec portrait.

**[SEA—negative finding]** No Tula sculpture—the so-called Atlanteans, chacmools, relief warriors, or other monumental images—can securely be identified as Huemac. Modern captions that do so are speculative.

## 6. Historical evolution of the figure

### Early Postclassic Tula, approximately ninth–twelfth centuries

**[SEA archaeology/MSR identification]** Archaeology documents Tula’s growth, monumental center, neighborhoods, long-distance exchange, burning, contraction, and eventual abandonment. It does not document Huemac. Linking a particular destruction layer, drought, faction, or ruler to his stories is a hypothesis.

### Pre-Conquest memory, indirectly visible

**[MSR]** Sixteenth-century traditions are too numerous and structurally diverse to be inventions of a single friar. They probably draw on pre-Conquest oral performances, annals, migration paintings, place traditions, and elite genealogies. Their exact pre-Conquest wording and date cannot be recovered.

### Mid-sixteenth-century Indigenous redactions

**[SEA]** The *Historia Tolteca-Chichimeca* uses Huemac to explain ethnic rupture and migration. Sahagún’s informants place him inside a Tezcatlipoca-driven fall-of-Tollan cycle. The *Leyenda de los Soles* makes him the ruler whose failure to recognize maize transfers fertility and historical precedence to the Mexica. The *Anales* makes his reign the threshold between Quetzalcoatl’s abstinent order and sacrifice, famine, war, and dispersal.

### Late sixteenth-century conquest historiography

**[LCE]** Durán and Tezozómoc join Huemac’s ancient fall to Motecuhzoma’s final crisis. The last Mexica ruler contemplates withdrawing from history into the domain of the last Toltec ruler. The analogy makes imperial collapse repeat itself.

### Seventeenth-century Christian and dynastic synthesis

**[EMA/SI]** Ixtlilxochitl turns Huemac/Quetzalcoatl into a celibate moral teacher with cross-like doctrine. Chimalpahin and other annalists place Huemac within Culhua genealogy. Torquemada reconciles missionary histories. Huemac becomes both failed king and proto-Christian sage depending on the author’s purpose.

### Nineteenth and early twentieth centuries

**[LS]** National and antiquarian historians—Clavijero’s successors, Orozco y Berra, Chavero, Brasseur de Bourbourg, and others—often treated annalistic year signs as straightforward chronology and produced precise reigns. Huemac entered linear “Toltec empire” histories as an ordinary last monarch.

**[MSR]** Archaeological identification of Tula, Hidalgo, as the Tollan most relevant to Mexica memory strengthened historicist readings, but the excavation did not verify the chronicles’ named individuals.

### Later twentieth century to present

**[MSR]** Davies’s *The Toltecs: Until the Fall of Tula* (1977) reconstructed a broad Mixcoatl–Topiltzin–Huemac saga while acknowledging contradictions. Dewey’s 1983 study emphasized Huemac’s supernatural transitions and Cincalco. Gillespie, Graulich, Carrasco, López Austin, Boone, Nicholson, Leibsohn, and others increasingly treated Tollan narratives as political, ritual, migration, and cyclical history rather than recoverable court biography.

**[F/MI]** Popular retellings now commonly combine the ballgame, four-year famine, fall of Tula, 1162 suicide, and immortal reign in one continuous biography. That synthesis is readable but not source-faithful: it harmonizes incompatible endings and chronologies.

## 7. Modern reception and reinvention

### Scholarship and public history

**[MSR]** Miguel León-Portilla popularized the formulation that Toltec history and myth are inseparable and presented Huemac in broad histories of Nahua thought and Mesoamerica. Nicholson’s 2001 *Topiltzin Quetzalcoatl* is primarily about Topiltzin but remains indispensable because it catalogues the very sources in which Huemac is distinguished from or assimilated to him.

**[MSR]** Elizabeth Hill Boone’s work on migration histories interprets pictorial migration documents as performative histories and political charters, warning against reading the Boturini group merely as illustrated itineraries. This matters because later summaries sometimes insert Huemac into pictorial scenes that do not label him.

**[MSR]** Dana Leibsohn’s 2009 study of the *Historia Tolteca-Chichimeca* recovered Huemac’s function as a destructive foundling and analyzed the interaction of alphabetic and pictorial history. Camilla Townsend’s 2024 discussion used the “Big Gift” irony to illustrate what close Nahuatl reading can recover.

### Indigenous-language and community adaptation

**[F/modern literary adaptation]** The bilingual work *Uemac: Los gigantes del Mezquital*, promoted electronically by Mexico’s Instituto Nacional de los Pueblos Indígenas, reworks Toltec collapse and the “Uemac” as defenders of freedom and dignity in an Otomi/Mezquital setting. It is a documented modern cultural adaptation, not a newly discovered ancient Huemac tradition.

### Nationalism and politics

**[MSR—negative finding]** Broad Mexican national narratives have repeatedly appropriated “Toltec” civilization as ancestral grandeur. However, this research found no comparably well-documented state cult, mass nationalist movement, or extremist organization centered specifically on Huemac. His political use is secondary to Quetzalcoatl, the Mexica rulers, and Tula’s monuments.

**[MI]** Assertions that Huemac was a historical “emperor of all Anahuac,” that his cave conceals a national redeemer awaiting return, or that named modern populations descend directly from his personal followers require evidence and should not be presented as ancient fact.

### Esotericism

**[MI/F]** Neo-Aztec, New Age, and esoteric websites often turn Cincalco into a precisely mapped astral paradise, assign Huemac secret initiations, or harmonize him with universal “ascended masters.” Unless they cite a colonial passage accurately, these are modern religious constructions.

### Literature and popular media

**[MSR—negative finding]** Huemac has not developed a major internationally canonical afterlife in opera, cinema, comics, or commercial games comparable to Quetzalcoatl. His strongest modern presence is in histories of the Toltecs, educational retellings of the ballgame, fiction or poetry using Cincalco, local cultural projects, and online mythology compendia.

## 8. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Was Huemac historical?

**Historicist position. [MSR]** Older scholars and some modern reconstructions accept a historical last ruler behind the accounts. Supporting considerations are the annalistic king list, named migration routes, multiple community traditions, the real abandonment of Tula, and the persistence of Chapultepec place-memory.

**Critical position. [MSR]** The evidence is entirely retrospective; the earliest manuscripts are separated from Tula’s florescence by centuries; sources disagree on origin, genealogy, relation to Quetzalcoatl, deeds, chronology, and death; and Huemac’s stories possess conspicuous mythic structures. Gillespie and Graulich argue that attempts to extract a single biography may misunderstand the cyclical and political work of Tollan history.

**Assessment. [MSR]** A historical ruler or office may stand behind some material, but no available method securely isolates that person. “Legendary ruler represented as historical by colonial Nahua sources” is more accurate than either “fictional god” or “verified king.”

### One Huemac or several?

**[MSR]** The foundling destroyed by Nonoalca, Quetzalcoatl’s fallen minister, father of the Tohueyo-desiring daughter, ballplayer against the Tlaloque, suicide at Chapultepec, immortal Cincalco lord, and saintly Quetzalcoatl-double need not originate in one biography. The shared name and collapse setting encouraged amalgamation.

**[LS]** Kirchhoff’s 1955 “Quetzalcoatl, Huemac y el fin de Tula” made the relationship a central chronological problem. Later studies have variously separated the figures by a century, made them contemporaries, or understood them as complementary first-and-last rulers.

### What was “Tollan”?

**[MSR]** *Tōllān*, “place of reeds,” could designate Tula, Hidalgo, but also function as a category for an exemplary great city. Archaeology supports a strong Mexica interest in the ruins of Tula, Hidalgo, and makes it a principal referent. It does not prove that every Tollan narrative describes that site alone.

### What caused Tula’s collapse?

**[SEA archaeology]** Settlement history indicates contraction and abandonment, with burning in parts of the monumental center. Environmental stress, political fragmentation, migration, and regional reorganization are archaeological and historical questions.

**[SEA narrative]** The sources explain collapse through Tezcatlipoca’s deceptions, Huemac’s sexual and political failures, human sacrifice, famine, refusal of maize, factional conflict, and divine withdrawal.

**[MSR]** These narrative causes cannot simply be matched one-to-one with a drought layer or burned building. Myth may preserve memories of stress while reorganizing them into moral and cosmic history.

### Ballgame interpretation

**[MSR]** The Huemac–Tlaloque contest joins political sovereignty, agricultural fertility, precious-green symbolism, and ritual exchange. Maize and jade share greenness and preciousness; Huemac insists on literal elite wealth and loses the life-giving referent. Structural interpretations are strong because the text itself contrasts the objects, but claims about a particular historical game are unsupported.

### Cincalco

**[MSR]** Interpretations include maize-filled paradise, cave of the dead, fertility realm, royal refuge, and passage between cosmic levels. The sources support overlapping meanings. They do not support identifying it simply with Mictlan or locating its complete geography on a modern park map.

### Manuscript problems

**[SEA]** The original *Codex Chimalpopoca* is missing. The 1945 Velázquez facsimile and earlier copies are therefore crucial. Translation is consequential: Christian terms such as “devil” can conceal Indigenous divine names, and manuscript damage or normalization can alter personal and place names.

**[SEA]** The *Historia Tolteca-Chichimeca* is a composite Nahuatl-pictorial manuscript made for Cuauhtinchan political history. Reading its Huemac episode apart from its community agenda produces false universality.

### Discredited or weak claims that still circulate

- **“Huemac is archaeologically proved to have reigned in exact Gregorian years.” [MI]**
- **“The Atlantean statues at Tula portray Huemac.” [MI]**
- **“Huemac’s authenticated tomb or skeleton has been found in Chapultepec.” [MI]**
- **“The Codex Boturini explicitly pictures and names Huemac.” [MI]**
- **“Every source calls Huemac Quetzalcoatl.” [MI]** Several early sources distinguish them.
- **“Every source says he committed suicide.” [MI]** One has arrow execution; another merely entrance into Cincalco.
- **“The Toltecs certainly formed the unitary empire described by later chronicles.” [MI]** Tula was a major polity, but the scale and structure of a “Toltec Empire” remain debated.
- **“Huemac founded human sacrifice as a datable historical institution.” [MI]** The *Anales* makes that a moral-periodizing claim; archaeology documents sacrifice in Mesoamerica long before the proposed reign.
- **“Motecuhzoma actually met Huemac physically in 1519.” [MI]** Durán and Tezozómoc narrate attempted supernatural communication, not a verifiable meeting.
