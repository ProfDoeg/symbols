# Garshasp (Keresaspa): Research Dossier

**Traditional class:** mortal, superhuman dragon-slaying warrior; bearer of the royal Glory; culture-protecting hero; in Middle Persian eschatology, an undead or prematurely resurrected champion of the Renovation; in New Persian epic, a king and world-travelling ancestor of the house of Rostam. No source makes him a god, and no historically verifiable person can be recovered behind him.

**Earliest secure attestation:** the Younger Avestan heroic tradition, especially *Yasna* 9.10–11 and the Kərəsāspa digression in *Zamyād Yašt* 19.38–44. These passages belong to an orally composed Old Iranian corpus whose absolute dating remains disputed—broadly first millennium BCE, with some heroic matter possibly older—but whose surviving manuscripts are medieval and early modern. The oldest extant Khordeh Avesta manuscript is Jm4 (1352 CE); the principal complete Yašt codex F1 dates to 1591. Accordingly, “Avestan” below identifies linguistic and compositional strata, not surviving artifacts from the first millennium BCE. [Encyclopaedia Iranica, “Yašts”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/yashts/)

## Evidence labels

- **[EA] Securely attested early material:** the Avestan corpus, allowing for disputed internal chronology and late manuscript transmission.
- **[LC] Later canonical elaboration:** Middle Persian Zoroastrian exegesis and cosmology, principally ninth–tenth-century redactions preserving older material.
- **[RV] Regional variant:** especially Sistanian traditions.
- **[LS] Learned systematization:** priestly, genealogical, chronological, or historiographical reconciliation.
- **[SI] Syncretistic identification:** equation with Sām, Narīmān, biblical figures, Adamic history, or other systems.
- **[MA] Medieval or early-modern accretion:** New Persian epic, chronicles, romances, manuscript illustration.
- **[F] Folklore:** orally or locally reported belief not securely traceable to the early corpus.
- **[MSR] Modern scholarly reconstruction:** linguistic, comparative, historical, or literary inference.
- **[MI] Modern invention or reinvention:** modern fiction, nationalism, popular culture, or material incorrectly presented as ancient.

## 1. Identification, names and epithets

Kərəsāspa first appears as a member of the Sāma clan and the son of Θrita, a prodigiously strong but morally imperfect human champion. He inherits the third portion of Yima’s fugitive *xvarənah*, kills dragons, giants, robbers, and cosmic rebels, and avenges his brother. Middle Persian tradition calls him Karsāsp, often identifies or compounds him with Sām and Sām ī Narīmān, condemns him for an offense against sacred Fire, preserves his body in supernatural sleep, and assigns him the future killing of the escaped Dahāg. New Persian literature turns Garšāsp into several partly incompatible figures: a Jamšidian ancestor of Narīmān, Sām, Zāl, and Rostam; a Pishdadian ruler; a commander or treasurer at royal courts; and, in Asadi Tusi’s *Garšāsp-nāma*, an immense travelling conqueror whose adventures have little narrative contact with the Avestan corpus beyond the name and dragon-slaying. [Encyclopaedia Iranica, “Karsāsp”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/karsasp/)

### Names and forms

- **Avestan:** 𐬐𐬆𐬭𐬆𐬯𐬁𐬯𐬞𐬀, conventionally transliterated **Kərəsāspa-**; older scholarship often writes **Keresāspa/Keresaspa**. **[EA]**
- **Middle Persian/Pahlavi:** **Karsāsp/Kirsāsp**, written variably `<klsʾsp>`, `<glsʾsp>`, `<glyšʾsp>`, `<klyšsp>`, and `<klšsp>` in manuscripts. Some shorter forms may be scribal misreadings. **[LC]**
- **New Persian:** **Garšāsp/Garshasp/Garshāsb**, گرشاسپ or گرشاسب; **Karšāsp** is philologically closer to the old initial consonant. Arabic-script historical texts sometimes preserve initial *k-*. **[MA]**
- **Compound identities:** **Sāmān Karsāsp**, **Sām Karsāsp**, and **Sām ī Narīmānān** in Middle Persian sources. These are not stable personal names but evidence of the fusion and subsequent splitting of heroic personae. **[LC/SI]**

The most defensible etymology is Avestan *kərəsa-* “lean, meager” plus *aspa-* “horse”: “he whose horses are lean/meager.” The Old Indic comparison is *kṛśāśva*. The meaning is incongruously modest for the “strongest of strong men,” but Iranian heroic names often preserve archaic horse compounds. The change from Karsāsp to Garšāsp is unexplained. A proposed influence from **Gar-šāh**, “mountain king,” an epithet of Gayōmard, is explicitly conjectural. **[MSR]**

### Epithets and attributes

- **nairiiō.manah / naire.manah**, “manly-minded,” “heroic-minded”: his characteristic Avestan epithet. It later contributed to the independent name **Narīmān**, though the historical pathway is not wholly recoverable. **[EA/MSR]**
- “Strongest of strong men after Zarathustra”: *Yašt* 19.38. The comparison gives religious priority to Zarathustra while conceding supreme secular strength to the warrior. **[EA]**
- **gaēsu**, Pahlavi **gēswar**, *Yasna* 9.10: probably a description of his hair, perhaps “long-haired” or “wearing braids,” but the meaning is uncertain. Pahlavi commentators disagreed: Māhwindād associated it with being *Tāzīg* (“Arab”), while Māhgušasp objected that Turks also wore *gēs*. **[EA/LS]**
- **gadauuara**, “club-bearing”: *Yasna* 9.10. Later clubs are called “arm-smashing” (*arm-zadār*), “ox-headed” (*gāw-sār*), and “victorious” (*pērōzgar*). **[EA/LC]**
- **Sāmahe**, “of the Sāma [clan]”: the Avestan family designation. It should not automatically be read as saying that his father was the later epic hero Sām. **[EA]**

### Identity problems

By late antiquity, Kərəsāspa’s designations had become separate figures. The third-century Manichean *Book of Giants* already has giant brothers called Sām and Narīmān in Middle Persian, and Sāhm and Pātsāhm in Sogdian. That is evidence that splitting was underway by the third century CE, not proof that either giant was originally Kərəsāspa. **[SI/MSR]**

The New Persian Garšāsp who briefly succeeds Zav in some *Šāh-nāma* manuscripts is likewise not securely identical with every other Garšāsp. Khaleghi-Motlagh judges the few verses about this reign secondary; the anachronistic “Kayanid crown” and their absence from important witnesses and dependent historians support interpolation. **[MA/MSR]**

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### Avestan evidence

The Avestan texts were orally composed and transmitted. Their relative antiquity rests on language, poetics, geography, and religious development, not on early manuscripts. Exact dates such as “1200 BCE” or “600 BCE” should therefore not be attached to individual Kərəsāspa episodes as settled facts.

1. **Yasna 9.10–11, Hōm Yašt** — Younger Avestan; first millennium BCE in linguistic-compositional terms. Θrita, of the family of Sāma, is the third primordial presser of Haoma. His reward is two sons, Uruuāxšaiia and Kərəsāspa. Kərəsāspa is club-bearing and kills the horned, horse-devouring, man-devouring serpent. He mistakes its immense back for ground, cooks a noon meal upon it, and flees when the heated creature rises and overturns the pot. This is the earliest full kernel of the cooking episode. **[EA]**

2. **Yašt 5.38, Ābān Yašt** — Kərəsāspa sacrifices to Arədvī Sūrā Anāhitā and requests victory over the golden-heeled Gaṇdərəβa in the Vourukaša Sea. The goddess grants it. **[EA]**

3. **Yašt 13.61 and 13.136, Frawardīn Yašt** — his body is guarded by 99,999 fravašis; his own fravaši is invoked for resistance against enemy armies, thieves, and robbers. These terse notices are the Avestan basis for his preserved body and future availability, though 13.61 does not itself narrate his awakening or killing Dahāg. **[EA]**

4. **Yašt 15.27–28, Rām Yašt** — Kərəsāspa petitions Vayu for victories including that over Gaṇdərəβa and over Hitāspa, his brother’s killer. **[EA]**

5. **Yašt 19.38–44, Zamyād Yašt/Kayān Yasn** — the fullest early catalogue. Kərəsāspa receives the third departure of Yima’s Glory and kills the serpent Srvara, Gaṇdərəβa, the nine sons of Pathana, the progeny of Nivika and Dāštayāna, Hitāspa, Varəšava son of Dāna, Pitaona with many pairikās, Arəzošamana, and Snāuuiδka. The latter boasts that when grown he will make earth a wheel and heaven a chariot and compel the Good and Evil Spirits to draw it. Kərəsāspa kills him before he can do so. The Kərəsāspa section may be a secondary minstrel’s digression within the Glory hymn, but it remains Avestan heroic material, not a medieval invention. [Avestan text, Geldner basis](https://www.avesta.org/ka/yt19.htm); [Darmesteter translation](https://www.avesta.org/ka/yt19sbe.htm) **[EA/MSR]**

6. **Vīdēvdād 1.9** — in the catalogue of lands and their counter-creations, the Evil Spirit creates the pairikā Xnąθaitī against Vaēkərəta; Kərəsāspa is associated with her. The obscure Avestan notice does not plainly say “idol worship.” That is a Middle Persian interpretation. **[EA]**

### Middle Persian Zoroastrian corpus

Most works survive in redactions or manuscripts much later than the Sasanian period, although they preserve Sasanian exegesis and lost Avestan-nask material.

- **Dēnkard 7.1.32; 9.15; 9.23.6; 9.36** — ninth–tenth-century encyclopedic redaction. It places Karsāsp in sacred history; explains his warrior function and share of Yima’s Glory; epitomizes the lost *Sūdgar Nask* account of his soul, sin, and judgment; mentions the arm-smashing club; and preserves contradictory traditions about whether he accepted the religion. **[LC/LS]**
- **Bundahišn 29.8–9; 31.17–18; 33.42; 35–36** — cosmological compilation, principally ninth century with later additions. It explains his supernatural sleep, offense against the religion, protection by fravašis, awakening, killing of Dahāg, and genealogical positions. Chapter 35’s genealogy from Ferēdūn’s son Tūz is a very late addition drawing on Islamic-era history. **[LC/LS/MA]**
- **Mēnōy ī Xrad 26.49–53; 61.20–21** — probably sixth-century material in a later Middle Persian recension. It lists the cosmic benefit of his killings and locates his preserved body near Damāvand under 99,999 fravašis. **[LC]**
- **Dādestān ī Dēnīg 16.5; 36.84** — answers by the ninth-century priest Manuščihr. It cites the Avestan guarded-body passage and says Sāmān Karsāsp will be raised early to smash Dahāg with his ox-headed club. **[LC/LS]**
- **Zand ī Wahman Yasn 9.16–22** — medieval Middle Persian apocalypse drawing on older traditions. Srōš and Nēryōsang awaken Karsāsp, who kills the escaped Ažidahāg. **[LC]**
- **Wizīdagīhā ī Zādspram 32.4; 35.6** — late ninth century. It connects the Avestan robbers with later highwaymen and discusses Karsāsp among the sleepers in snow. **[LC/LS]**
- **Pahlavi Rivāyat accompanying the Dādestān ī Dēnīg**, chapters 18f, 48, 54 — a major full narrative of his deeds, offense against Fire, postmortem hearing, sleep, and eschatological function. **[LC]**
- **Māh ī Frawardīn Rōz ī Hordad 31** — says Sām ī Narīmānān kills Dahāg and temporarily rules the seven continents before Kay Husrōy. **[LC/SI]**
- **Jāmāsp-nāmag/Ayādgār ī Jāmāspīg** — Karsāsp offers Ažidahāg conversion and joint rule; when Dahāg accepts only after being struck, it is too late. **[LC]**

### New Persian and Islamic-era corpus

- **Abu’l-Moʾayyad Balḵī, lost *Ketāb/Aḵbār-e Garšāsp*** — probably tenth century. Known through the *Tārīḵ-e Sīstān* and possibly Asadi. Its precise relation to Asadi’s source cannot be proved. **[MA]**
- **Ferdowsi, *Šāh-nāma*** — completed ca. 1010; critical edition by Djalal Khaleghi-Motlagh is textually preferable to the older Mohl and Moscow traditions. Garšāsp is a minor and unstable figure: Jamšid’s descendant, courtier, treasurer, commander, ancestor claimed by Rostam, and in secondary manuscript verses Zav’s successor. Dick Davis’s translation is readable but abridged in places; Warner and Warner is complete but based on an older text. **[MA]**
- **Asadi Tusi, *Garšāsp-nāma*** — completed 458 AH/1066 CE at Nakhchivan and dedicated to the otherwise obscure Abū Dolaf. Its oldest dated complete manuscript used by the standard editor is British Library Or. 2780, copied in 800/1397. Habib Yaghma’i’s 1938 edition remains the only complete critical edition; Huart translated the first part into French in 1926, Massé the remainder in 1951, and Kanus-Credé translated it into German in 1996–98. [Encyclopaedia Iranica, “Garšāsp-nāma”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/garsasp-nama/) **[MA]**
- **Ṭabarī, *History*, early tenth century** — treats Garšāsp within universal legendary history and makes him a descendant of Ferēdūn’s son Tūz/Tūr. **[LS/MA]**
- **Balʿamī, Persian adaptation of Ṭabarī, later tenth century** — calls Garšāsp Zav’s vizier and a descendant of Afrēdūn. **[MA]**
- **Ṯaʿālebī, *Ḡorar*, ca. 1000** — omits Garšāsp while developing Sām son of Narīmān as Manūčehr’s generalissimo and governor, showing that the Garšāsp–Sām equation was never universal. **[MA]**
- **Anonymous *Tārīḵ-e Sīstān*, core eleventh century with continuations** — makes Garšāsp founder of Sistan, preserves a local genealogy and the Karkuy fire tradition, and Islamizes his virtues. **[RV/MA]**
- **Mojmal al-tawāriḵ, ca. 1126** — gives the line Rostam–Zāl–Sām–Narīmān–Garšāsp–Aṯreṭ, with marriages to royal women of Egypt and Rūm. **[LS/MA]**
- **Persian Rivāyats, Ṣad dar-e Bondaheš 20, Dinkard-nāma, Persian Bahman-nāma and Jāmāsp-nāma** — late medieval and early-modern Zoroastrian retellings, especially important for his judgment and final combat. **[MA/LS]**
- **Mirḵᵛānd, *Rawżat al-ṣafāʾ*, late fifteenth century** — adds biblical synchronisms and makes Garšāsp’s mother a descendant of Benjamin son of Jacob. **[SI/MA]**
- **Borzū-nāma**, probably composed between the eleventh and thirteenth centuries in multiple layers — belongs to the later Sistanian epic cycle but concerns Rostam’s grandson Borzū. Garšāsp’s relevance is genealogical, not that of an active protagonist; it cannot independently establish the Avestan genealogy. **[MA]**

The Sasanian **Xwadāy-nāmag** is lost. Later Arabic and Persian royal histories drew on versions of Iranian national history associated with it, but no surviving fragment lets us isolate a “Karsāsp chapter.” Statements that a particular New Persian episode “comes from the Xwadāy-nāmag” are reconstructions, not direct documentation. **[MSR]**

## 3. The story, cycle by cycle

### A. Birth and inherited potency

In *Yasna* 9, Θrita of the Sāma clan is the third mortal to press Haoma. Haoma rewards him with Uruuāxšaiia and Kərəsāspa. The passage explains the hero’s birth as a ritual reward and places him in a sequence after Yima and Θraētaona but before Zarathustra. **[EA]**

*Yašt* 19 supplies a different source of power: when Yima loses the royal Glory through falsehood, it departs three times in bird form. Mithra takes the first share, Θraētaona the second, and Kərəsāspa the third. The sequence places the hero inside Iranian sacral kingship without explicitly calling him king. **[EA]**

The *Dēnkard* systematizes this as the warrior share of Yima’s *xwarrah*: Karsāsp receives the “word” belonging to the warrior estate. That is priestly functional analysis, not explicit Avestan doctrine. **[LC/LS]**

### B. The horned serpent and the overturned meal

In *Yasna* 9.11 and *Yašt* 19.40, the serpent is enormous, horned, yellow, venomous, and a devourer of horses and men. Kərəsāspa mistakes its back for ground and sets a brass cooking vessel upon it at noon. Heat wakes the serpent; it rises and overturns the boiling water, and the startled hero springs back. He nevertheless kills it. The text does not say that he knowingly used the dragon as a stove. **[EA]**

Later Zoroastrian exegesis makes the cooking fire central to his guilt. In the *Ṣad dar-e Bondaheš*, the overturned vessel extinguishes or injures Fire, under the guardianship of Ard Wahišt. This offense delays his admission to paradise. Whether this is an inherited part of the same myth or exegetical development from *Yasna* 9.11 cannot be determined. **[LC/MSR]**

Asadi’s dragon episode is narratively different. The fourteen-year-old Garšāsp accepts Żaḥḥāk’s challenge, equips himself against poison, and kills a mountain-sized dragon. It preserves the general identity “Garšāsp the dragon-slayer,” but not the Avestan cooking plot. **[MA]**

### C. Gaṇdərəβa/Gandarw

In *Yašt* 5, Kərəsāspa sacrifices to Anāhitā for victory over the golden-heeled Gaṇdərəβa in the Vourukaša Sea; *Yašts* 15 and 19 register the victory. **[EA]**

The *Pahlavi Rivāyat* expands the fight: Gandarw drags Karsāsp into the sea; they struggle until Karsāsp grips and strips the skin from below the monster’s feet, binds him in it, and overcomes him. Āxrūrag appears as Karsāsp’s friend or helper only in this late full narrative, though an Āxrūra has an independent Avestan attestation. **[LC]**

### D. The catalogue of enemies

*Yašt* 19.41–44 credits Kərəsāspa with killing:

- nine members of Pathana’s brood;
- the broods of Nivika and Dāštayāna;
- Hitāspa, here “golden-crowned”;
- Varəšava son of Dāna;
- Pitaona, accompanied by many pairikās;
- Arəzošamana;
- Snāuuiδka.

The identities and narrative contexts of several are lost. It is methodologically unsafe to reconstruct complete adventures from their epithets alone. **[EA]**

Snāuuiδka’s boast is unusually complete: while still a child, he says that as an adult he will make earth a wheel, heaven a chariot, and force Spəṇta Mainiiu and Aŋra Mainiiu to pull it. Kərəsāspa kills him before maturity. The episode casts the hero as defender of cosmic order against a would-be usurper. **[EA]**

### E. Uruuāxšaiia’s death and vengeance

Kərəsāspa’s brother Uruuāxšaiia is killed by Hitāspa. Kərəsāspa kills Hitāspa and drags him behind his chariot; the notices occur in *Yasna* 9.10 and *Yašts* 15.28 and 19.41. The vengeance is among the clearest personal motives in the early corpus. **[EA]**

### F. Robbers, Kamak, and the Wind

*Yašt* 13.136 invokes his fravaši against armies and robbers. The *Pahlavi Rivāyat* and Persian Zoroastrian texts turn this into the killing of giant highwaymen, seven in the *Ṣad dar*. **[EA → LC]**

The *Mēnōy ī Xrad* adds the bird Kamak; the *Ṣad dar-e Bondaheš* explains that its wings were so vast that they blocked rain. **[LC/MA]**

In the *Pahlavi Rivāyat*, demons deceive the Wind into attacking Karsāsp. Its violence threatens the earth, but Karsāsp persuades it to resume its created duty of sustaining heaven and earth. This is a Middle Persian cosmic tale with no secure Avestan Kərəsāspa episode behind it. **[LC]**

### G. Sin, judgment, and exclusion from paradise

The sources give several incompatible sins:

1. *Vīdēvdād* 1.9 associates him obscurely with Xnąθaitī. **[EA]**
2. Its Pahlavi translation interprets Xnąθaitī as “witch-desire,” then “idol worship,” and makes Karsāsp its first practitioner. **[LC/LS]**
3. *Bundahišn* 31.17–18 equates this with Sām’s demon worship; another authority says his fault was failing to tie the sacred girdle. **[LC/LS]**
4. *Bundahišn* 29.8 says Sām became unconscious because he scorned the Mazdayasnian religion. **[LC]**
5. The dominant *Pahlavi Rivāyat* narrative says he offended or killed Fire by overturning the cooking vessel. **[LC]**
6. *Dēnkard* passages disagree on whether he rejected or accepted the religion. **[LC]**

In the judgment story, Ohrmazd summons Karsāsp’s fierce soul from hell at Zarathustra’s request. Karsāsp pleads that his killings preserved creation: had even one great monster survived, the Resurrection could not occur. Fire refuses him paradise, but Gōšurūn, the soul of beneficent animal creation, refuses to let him return to hell. Zarathustra promises to care for Fire; Karsāsp is assigned to *hamēstagān*, the intermediate station, or in the *Ṣad dar* is pardoned by Ard Wahišt. These are variant outcomes, not stages that can confidently be combined. **[LC/MA]**

### H. Sleep, body, soul, and the end time

*Yašt* 13.61 says only that 99,999 fravašis guard his body. Middle Persian sources elaborate:

- *Bundahišn* 29 places the unconscious Sām/Karsāsp in the plain of *Pēšyānsēy, protected by 10,000 fravašis, under snow and amid wormwood. A Turk called *Nōhīn shot him while he slept, and the demoness Būšāsp overcame him. **[LC]**
- *Mēnōy ī Xrad* uses 99,999 guardians and locates the body near Damāvand. **[LC]**
- *Pahlavi Rivāyat* 54 says he possesses a body but no soul, unlike some other immortals; another passage speaks explicitly of his soul awakening. The contradiction is textual and theological. **[LC]**
- Zādspram’s inclusion of him among sleepers under snow may have been influenced by the *Šāh-nāma* story of Kay Ḵosrow’s companions disappearing in snow; this is scholarly inference, not consensus fact. **[MSR]**

At the end time, Dahāg escapes his chains at Damāvand. In the *Zand ī Wahman Yasn*, Fire, Water, and Plant appeal to Ohrmazd; Srōš and Nēryōsang call Karsāsp four times, and he rises and kills Ažidahāg with his club. The *Pahlavi Rivāyat* first asks Frēdōn’s soul, which declares itself unable, then awakens Karsāsp. *Dādestān ī Dēnīg* says he is raised prematurely and smashes Dahāg. The *Bundahišn* calls him the first of the living world raised from death. **[LC]**

Later Persian variants moralize the encounter. In the *Bahman-nāma*, Sōšāns first forces Sām to accept the religion; Sām then offers Dahāg the same choice. Dahāg tempts him to serve Ahrimen and seize heaven, but Sām refuses and kills him. In the Persian *Jāmāsp-nāma*, Dahāg accepts and evil departs. In the Middle Persian *Jāmāsp-nāmag*, he accepts only after being struck, too late to save himself. **[MA]**

### I. Asadi’s independent epic life

Asadi supplies a coherent mortal biography that should not be back-projected into the Avesta:

Jamšid, fleeing Żaḥḥāk, fathers Tūr on the daughter of Kūrang, king of Zābolestān. The line continues Tūr–Šēdāsp–Ṭovorg–Šam–Eṯreṭ–Garšāsp. Born seven hundred years after Tūr while Żaḥḥāk still rules, the young hero kills the dragon; restores the displaced Indian king against Bahū; visits Sarandīb and Adam’s footprint; debates cosmology and religion with Brahmans; tours marvel-filled islands; woos a princess of Rūm; restores his father against Kabul; builds Sistan; campaigns through North Africa and Cordova; succeeds his father in Zābolestān; adopts his nephew Narēmān; submits to Ferēdūn after Żaḥḥāk’s fall; campaigns in Turan and China; sees the birth of Sām; fights the king of Tangier; kills a final dragon; returns home and dies. **[MA]**

The philosophical Brahmans voice Islamic Neoplatonic learning rather than documented Indian doctrines. Sarandīb’s sacred footprint is interpreted as Adam’s, not the Buddha’s. The poem is therefore an Islamic Persian universal epic, not a fossilized Avestan saga. **[MA/LS]**

## 4. Relations with gods, heroes, and named beings

### Competing genealogies

| Source and date | Genealogy | Tier |
|---|---|---|
| *Yasna* 9, Younger Avestan | Θrita of the Sāma clan → Uruuāxšaiia and Kərəsāspa | **[EA]** |
| Asadi, 1066 | Jamšid → Tūr → Šēdāsp → Ṭovorg → Šam → Eṯreṭ → Garšāsp; Garšāsp adopts Narēmān → Sām → Zāl → Rostam | **[MA]** |
| *Tārīḵ-e Sīstān*, eleventh century | broadly Asadi’s Jamšidian line, but Kūrang is sometimes Garšāsp’s son and Narīmān his grandson | **[RV/MA]** |
| Ṭabarī, early tenth century | Ferēdūn → wicked son Tūz/Tūr → intermediate generations → Garšāsp | **[LS/MA]** |
| *Mojmal*, ca. 1126 | Garšāsp → Narīmān → Sām → Zāl → Rostam | **[LS/MA]** |
| *Šāh-nāma*, ca. 1010 | Garšāsp is “son/seed of Jamšid”; Rostam’s pedigree has manuscript variants Hōšang/Karšāsb/Karīmān | **[MA]** |
| Mirḵᵛānd, late fifteenth century | Garšāsp’s mother descends from Benjamin son of Jacob | **[SI/MA]** |

No Avestan text names a wife or child of Kərəsāspa. His placement at the head of Rostam’s lineage is medieval Sistanian and national-epic genealogy. It is central to later tradition but not securely early.

### Documented interactions: one figure per entry

- **Θrita:** father; his Haoma sacrifice produces Kərəsāspa and Uruuāxšaiia. *Yasna* 9.10–11; Younger Avestan. **[EA]**
- **Uruuāxšaiia:** brother; Kərəsāspa avenges his killing by Hitāspa. *Yasna* 9.10; *Yašts* 15.28, 19.41. **[EA]**
- **Haoma:** divine plant/person speaks of Θrita’s sacrifice and its heroic offspring. *Yasna* 9. **[EA]**
- **Yima/Jamšid:** Kərəsāspa receives the third portion of Yima’s departed Glory; later pedigrees make Garšāsp his descendant. *Yašt* 19.34–38; *Šāh-nāma* and Asadi, eleventh century. **[EA/MA]**
- **Kavi Xvarənah, the royal Glory:** cleaves to Kərəsāspa and enables heroic strength. *Yašt* 19.38–44. **[EA]**
- **Mithra:** takes the first portion of Yima’s Glory; Kərəsāspa takes the third. They share a sequence but do not personally meet. *Yašt* 19.35–38. **[EA; no direct interaction]**
- **Θraētaona/Ferēdūn:** takes the second share of Yima’s Glory; later captures but cannot kill Dahāg, making Karsāsp’s future role necessary. *Yašt* 19.36–38; *Bundahišn* 29; *Pahlavi Rivāyat* 48. **[EA/LC]**
- **Arədvī Sūrā Anāhitā:** receives Kərəsāspa’s sacrifice and grants victory over Gaṇdərəβa. *Yašt* 5.37–39. **[EA]**
- **Vayu/Wāy:** receives his sacrifice and grants requested victories; a separate *Dēnkard* episode has Kay Husrōy riding Wāy past Karsāsp. *Yašt* 15.27–28; *Dēnkard* 9.23. **[EA/LC]**

## 5. Cult, tomb, and ritual

There is no securely identified archaeological tomb of Garšāsp, no excavated heroon attributable to him, no attested sacrificial calendar in his name, and no inscription proving an organized hero cult. Claims of “Garshasp’s tomb” require local documentation and cannot presently be treated as ancient evidence. **Absence of evidence is significant here.**

What is attested is more limited:

- Kərəsāspa appears in liturgical texts. His fravaši is invoked in *Yašt* 13.136, and the power that attached to him is praised in *Yašt* 19. This is ritual commemoration within Zoroastrian worship, not evidence of a separate shrine cult. **[EA]**
- The *Tārīḵ-e Sīstān* reports that the Karkuy/Karkōy fire was dedicated to Garšāsp and that his place of worship was later restored by Kay Ḵosrow and Rostam. It attributes verses praising him to the site and cites Abu’l-Moʾayyad’s lost *Ketāb-e Garšāsp*. This is an eleventh-century Sistanian literary claim about a sacred fire, not archaeological proof that Garšāsp received cult there. **[RV/MA]**
- Zoroastrian personal names derived from Kərəsāspa/Garšāsp, including the modern Parsi name Kersasp, demonstrate commemorative naming but not worship. **[LC–modern]**
- Damāvand and the plain *Pēšyānsēy are mythic locations of his sleep in Middle Persian cosmography. No recoverable grave is described: his protected body is eschatological, not a relic available to devotees. **[LC]**

## 6. Historical evolution

### Old Iranian heroic formation

The earliest recoverable Kərəsāspa is not a king with a biography but a cluster of praise-formulas: Haoma-born champion, bearer of Glory, avenger, serpent-killer, sea-monster killer, and destroyer of named adversaries. His myths belong to an eastern Iranian oral geography and heroic vocabulary. **[EA]**

### Zoroastrian moral and eschatological recasting

Priestly tradition found the violent hero indispensable but religiously compromised. His strength becomes the warrior portion of sacred order; the overturned cooking fire becomes a grave sin; his admission to paradise becomes a juridical problem; and his preserved body solves the future problem created when Frēdōn binds rather than kills Dahāg. This produces an unusually complex moral figure: savior of creation, offender against Fire, and necessary end-time executioner. **[LC]**

### Late-antique fusion and fission

Kərəsāspa’s clan and epithet generated or became entangled with Sām and Narīmān. By the Manichean *Book of Giants*, Sām and Narīmān could already function independently. Middle Persian Zoroastrian books frequently call the eschatological hero Sāmān Karsāsp; New Persian epic instead arranges Garšāsp, Narīmān, and Sām into successive generations. **[SI/MSR]**

### National history and Islamization

Arabic and Persian historians placed Garšāsp into numbered dynasties, biblical chronology, and the universal history from Adam to Islam. Regional Sistanian tradition made him founder of Sistan and exemplar of local nobility. The *Tārīḵ-e Sīstān* retrofits his descendants with Muslim virtues and predicts Sistan’s early acceptance of Islam. **[RV/LS/MA]**

### New Persian epic

Ferdowsi marginalizes the figure while preserving conflicting genealogical traces. Asadi responds by supplying the missing epic in 1066. His Garšāsp is an Islamic-era cosmopolitan conqueror: India, Sarandīb, Rūm, Cordova, China, North Africa, marvel literature, Neoplatonic instruction, and dynastic ancestry are joined in one poem. De Blois emphasizes that beyond name and dragon-slaying there is little direct contact between Asadi’s narrative and the Avestan/Pahlavi Kərəsāspa cycle. **[MA/MSR]**

### Manuscript and visual culture

The most securely identified premodern Garšāsp image located in a museum catalogue is a Safavid folio from a *Garšāsp-nāma*, Shiraz, ca. 1580, showing Garšāsp slaying a dragon in ink, opaque watercolor, and gold. [Smithsonian National Museum of Asian Art](https://asia-archive.si.edu/object/S1986.274/) **[MA]**

A Delhi Sultanate *Šāh-nāma*, ca. 1450, contains an image catalogued as “Garshasp kills the dragon,” now associated with the Rietberg Museum. [Image record](https://www.worldhistory.org/image/11707/persian-hero-garshasp/) **[MA]**

Caution is essential: museum and popular searches frequently return **Gushtāsp/Goštāsp** slaying the dragon, a different hero. The famous Great Mongol and Shah Tahmasp *Šāh-nāmas* contain celebrated dragon combats by Gushtāsp, Rostam, Esfandiyār, and Bahrām Gūr. These are not automatically images of Garšāsp. No securely catalogued Garšāsp scene in either celebrated manuscript was established in this research. The similarity of Latin spellings “Garshasp/Gushtasp” is a persistent modern source of misidentification. **[MSR/MI]**

## 7. Modern reception and reinvention

Modern scholarship and popular culture generally recover Garšāsp through three channels: Zoroastrian textual study, Persian epic nationalism, and fantasy media.

- European Avestan editions and translations—from Westergaard and Spiegel through Darmesteter, Geldner, Lommel, Hintze, Humbach, and Ichaporia—made Kərəsāspa’s early dossier philologically accessible. Geldner’s 1886–96 edition remains foundational; Hintze’s 1994 and Humbach–Ichaporia’s 1998 studies transformed the treatment of *Yašt* 19. **[MSR]**
- Yaghma’i’s 1938 *Garšāsp-nāma* edition established the complete Persian text from several manuscripts. French and German translations widened its reception, but no comparably complete standard English translation has displaced reliance on summaries. **[MSR]**
- Iranian cultural nationalism often places Garšāsp among pre-Islamic national heroes. The evidence located supports heritage revival rather than a distinct, documented extremist Garšāsp cult. Claims of systematic far-right appropriation should not be made without a traceable organization, publication, or campaign. **[modern reception; negative finding]**
- **Garshasp: The Monster Slayer**, developed in Iran and released in 2011, turns him into a God-of-War-style action hero fighting demons after Ferēdūn binds Aži Dahāka. Its stolen ancestral mace, revenge plot, monsters, and “Temple of the Dragon” expansion freely remix Iranian names and motifs. They are modern game narrative, not translations of Asadi or the Avesta. [Official game site](https://garshasp.com/) **[MI]**
- Scholarship on Iranian video games interprets *Garshasp* as cultural-memory work and a vehicle for representing Persian heritage, while also noting that its mechanics and fantasy aesthetic are transnational rather than a reconstruction of ancient Iran. [Šisler and Mohseni, chapter abstract](https://www.taylorfrancis.com/chapters/edit/10.4324/9781315394183-7/revolution-reloaded-vit-sisler-ebrahim-mohseni) **[MSR]**

No major opera, canonical Western dramatic cycle, or internationally influential psychological school centered specifically on Garšāsp was found. Generic application of Rank, Raglan, Campbell, or Jung to him is modern comparativism unless tied to an identifiable publication. Likewise, calling him “the Persian Hercules” is a useful modern analogy at most, not an ancient syncretistic identification.

## 8. Historiography and contested questions

### Was there a historical Garšāsp?

No contemporary inscription, administrative record, datable tomb, coin, or archaeological context identifies a historical person behind Kərəsāspa. The earliest evidence is poetic-religious. Historical interpretations are therefore conjectures about social memory, not biographical recovery. **[MSR]**

### How old is the myth?

The linguistic stratum is Younger Avestan, but the individual tales may be older than their surviving formulation. Indo-Iranian comparisons—especially serpent combat and the name’s Old Indic cognates—support inherited structures, not a precise date or a single historical event. The extant manuscripts are more than a millennium later than the likely composition. **[MSR]**

### Is he the same as Sām and Narīmān?

The evidence shows both identification and separation. Avestan Kərəsāspa belongs to the Sāma clan and bears *naire.manah*; Pahlavi texts use Sāmān Karsāsp; early Manichean evidence has Sām and Narīmān separately; New Persian genealogies make Garšāsp, Narīmān, and Sām different generations. The most economical reconstruction is progressive reanalysis of inherited names and epithets, but no single family tree is original in every detail. **[MSR]**

### Is Asadi’s epic an ancient story preserved intact?

No. Asadi names a written book of Garšāsp’s adventures, probably related to Abu’l-Moʾayyad’s lost work, and the *Tārīḵ-e Sīstān* shares substantial material. Yet Asadi’s plot incorporates Islamic universal history, Adam lore, marvel geography, and Neoplatonic discourse. Apart from name and dragon-slaying, its contacts with the known Avestan and Pahlavi narratives are slight. [Encyclopaedia Iranica, “Asadī Ṭūsī”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/asadi-tusi/) **[MSR]**

### Did Kərəsāspa kill Aži Dahāka in the Avesta?

Not in the extant Avestan text. *Yašt* 19.36–37 credits Θraētaona with defeating Aži Dahāka; Kərəsāspa follows as the third recipient of Glory and kills other adversaries. His killing of the released Dahāg is explicit only in Middle Persian eschatology. Presenting it as an Avestan exploit collapses two chronological layers. **[EA versus LC]**

### Did he die, sleep, or remain bodiless?

- Asadi: he returns home and dies. **[MA]**
- *Yašt* 13: his body is guarded. **[EA]**
- *Bundahišn*: he is unconscious under supernatural protection. **[LC]**
- *Pahlavi Rivāyat* 54: body without soul. **[LC]**
- Other Rivāyat passages: his soul is judged and later awakened. **[LC]**
- *Dādestān ī Dēnīg*: raised from death before the general resurrection. **[LC]**

These are genuine doctrinal and narrative variants.

### What was his sin?

Idolatry, following a pairikā, failing to wear the sacred girdle, scorning the religion, and extinguishing Fire are all documented. The fire episode’s emergence from the cooking-pot story is plausible but unprovable. The dossier should not select one as “the original sin.” **[EA/LC/MSR]**

### Dumézil and comparative functionalism

The division of Yima’s Glory among Mithra, Θraētaona, and Kərəsāspa has been read in tripartite functional terms—religious sovereignty, warrior force, and prosperity—and the *Dēnkard* explicitly makes Karsāsp a warrior recipient. Wikander connected him with an Indo-Iranian warrior band; Mary Boyce criticized aspects of that reconstruction. Similarities to Indra–Vṛtra are real at the level of heroic dragon combat but do not establish borrowing, identity, or a historical Indo-Iranian prototype in every narrative detail. **[MSR]**

### Oral-formulaic and textual questions

The Kərəsāspa catalogue in *Yašt* 19 may be a bardic interpolation into the hymn’s progression of royal Glory. Its digressive position and formulaic structure support that view; it does not follow that the material is late. Conversely, Asadi’s recurrent invocations of a *dehqān* or *mōbad* do not prove direct oral composition, because he explicitly names a written source. **[MSR]**

### Cult and tomb controversy

The Karkuy tradition documents medieval Sistanian memory around a sacred fire, but no excavated shrine can be assigned to Garšāsp. Damāvand is a cosmological location, not an attested tomb. Internet claims of an ancient “Garshasp tomb” or continuous cult remain unsubstantiated unless accompanied by epigraphy, stratigraphy, or a reliable historical description. **[negative finding]**

### Modern inventions and recurrent errors

- Equating every dragon-slayer called Gushtasp, Garshasp, or Goshtasp in museum metadata is erroneous. **[MI]**
- Treating Asadi’s travels to Cordova and Sarandīb as historical Iranian expeditions is literalization of epic. **[MI]**
- Calling the game’s stolen mace and revenge narrative “from the Avesta” is false; they are modern adaptation. **[MI]**
- Presenting the entire Pahlavi apocalypse as textually present in *Yašt* 13 is anachronistic. **[MI]**
- The statement that Garšāsp is unambiguously “the last Pishdadian king in Ferdowsi” overlooks the secondary manuscript status of his reign and the historian tradition that omits it. **[MA/MSR]**

## 10. Sources and assessment

### Primary and critical sources

- *Avesta*: *Yasna* 9; *Yašts* 5, 13, 15, 19; *Vīdēvdād* 1. Geldner’s text remains foundational; for *Yašt* 19, Hintze and Humbach–Ichaporia supersede Darmesteter on many philological points. Darmesteter’s public-domain translation is useful for orientation but should not be treated as the final authority.
- *Dēnkard* 7 and 9; *Bundahišn*; *Mēnōy ī Xrad*; *Dādestān ī Dēnīg*; *Zand ī Wahman Yasn*; Zādspram; *Pahlavi Rivāyat*; *Māh ī Frawardīn Rōz ī Hordad*; *Jāmāsp-nāmag*.
- Ferdowsi, *Šāh-nāma*, ed. Djalal Khaleghi-Motlagh; translations by Dick Davis and Arthur and Edmond Warner, used with their different textual and completeness limitations.
- Asadi Tusi, *Garšāsp-nāma*, ed. Habib Yaghma’i, 1938; French translations by Clément Huart and Henri Massé; German translation by H. Kanus-Credé.
- Ṭabarī; Balʿamī; *Tārīḵ-e Sīstān*; *Mojmal al-tawāriḵ*; Ṯaʿālebī; Mirḵᵛānd; Persian Rivāyats.

### Major modern scholarship

- Ehsan Yarshater, “Iranian National History,” in *The Cambridge History of Iran*, III/1, 1983, especially the Karsāsp synthesis.
- Prods Oktor Skjærvø, “Karsāsp,” *Encyclopaedia Iranica*: presently the most concentrated source-critical guide to the entire textual dossier.
- François de Blois, “Garšāsp-nāma,” *Encyclopaedia Iranica*.
- Almut Hintze, *Der Zamyād-Yašt: Edition, Übersetzung, Kommentar*, 1994.
- Helmut Humbach and Pallan R. Ichaporia, *Zamyād Yasht: Yasht 19 of the Younger Avesta: Text, Translation, Commentary*, 1998.
- Olga M. Davidson, *Poet and Hero in the Persian Book of Kings*: important for performance, heroic tradition, and epic poetics, though not a monograph on Garšāsp.
- Mahmoud Omidsalar’s studies of the *Šāh-nāma* manuscript and oral traditions: important methodological counterweight to treating every late variant as either corruption or ancient survival.
- Mary Boyce’s criticism of Wikander’s warrior-band reconstruction: important in limiting overly confident Indo-European functional explanations.

The record supports a long-lived hero-complex rather than a single continuous biography. The securely early core is a Haoma-born, Glory-bearing monster-slayer. Middle Persian theologians made his violence both sinful and cosmically indispensable. Sistanian and New Persian poets transformed him into a founder, king, traveller, and ancestor of Rostam. Modern fantasy has selected the monster-slayer and discarded most of the moral and eschatological tension. Those transformations—not an invented seamless life—constitute the documented history of Garšāsp.
