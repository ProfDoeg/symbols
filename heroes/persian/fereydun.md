# Fereydun (Thraetaona): Research Dossier

**Traditional class:** Avestan dragon-slaying mortal hero and possessor of royal Glory; in Middle Persian religious history and the Persian national epic, a primordial **Pishdadian culture hero and universal king**, not a god or demigod. No securely documented independent hero-cult or historically identifiable individual lies behind him.

**Earliest secure attestation:** **Θraētaona Āθβiiāni** in the Younger Avestan tradition—especially *Yasna* 9.7–8 and *Yašts* 5.33–35, 15.23–24, and 19.36–37. These compositions are conventionally assigned to the first millennium BCE, but their precise dates are disputed and they survive only through much later Zoroastrian manuscript transmission; the oldest surviving Avestan manuscript is probably K7a, late thirteenth century CE, while the oldest pure *Yašt* codex is F1 of 1591. The early texts call him the son or heir of Āθβiia’s house, say that he possessed the royal *xᵛarənah*, sacrificed for victory, defeated the three-headed Aži Dahāka, and rescued Dahāka’s two women. They do **not** yet narrate the cow Barmāya, Kāva’s rebellion, the ox-headed mace, imprisonment on Damāvand, the tripartite division of the world, or the death of Iraj. [*Yašts*: transmission and dating](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/yashts/); [Avestan manuscript record](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/codices-hafnienses/).

## Evidence labels used below

- **[EA] Securely attested early material:** the Avestan corpus.
- **[MS] Middle Persian learned systematization:** late antique material preserved in eighth–tenth-century Zoroastrian compilations.
- **[LC] Later canonical elaboration:** Ferdowsi’s *Šāhnāma* and closely related national-history traditions.
- **[RV] Regional or folkloric variant.**
- **[IS] Islamic-era learned identification or accretion.**
- **[MR] Modern scholarly reconstruction:** comparative, historical, linguistic, or structural inference.
- **[MI] Modern invention or adaptation.**

Dates attached to Avestan passages distinguish the probable period of composition from the much later manuscripts preserving them.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

Fereydun is the New Persian form of an old Iranian dragon-slayer. In the Avesta, Θraētaona is a heroic man of the house of Āθβiia who acquires the royal Glory and defeats Aži Dahāka, a three-mouthed, three-headed, six-eyed being created by Angra Mainyu. In Middle Persian Zoroastrian literature he becomes Frēdōn, one of the primordial rulers: avenger of Yima, binder of Dahāg on Mount Damāvand, progenitor of three territorial lineages, physician, and benefactor of civilization. In Ferdowsi’s *Šāhnāma*, completed around 1010, Fereydun is a just Pishdadian king, son of Ābtin and Farānak, nourished by the marvelous cow Barmāya, allied with the rebel blacksmith Kāva, conqueror of Zahhāk, divider of the world among Salm, Tur, and Iraj, and grieving patriarch whose family settlement generates the Iran–Turan feud. Aḥmad Tafażżolī’s synthesis remains the most economical source map for these transformations. [Tafażżolī, “Ferēdūn”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/feredu/).

### Names and scripts

- **Avestan:** 𐬚𐬭𐬀𐬉𐬙𐬀𐬊𐬥𐬀, **Θraētaona-** (often normalized *Thraētaona*). **[EA]**
- **Patronymic/house epithet:** **Āθβiiāni**, “of Āθβiia’s house/clan”; *Yasna* 9.7 treats Āθβiia as his father. **[EA]**
- **Middle Persian, Book Pahlavi:** **Frēdōn**; Manichaean Middle Persian has the same general form. **[MS]**
- **Arabicized medieval forms:** **Afrīdūn**, **Farīdūn**. **[IS]**
- **New Persian:** فریدون, **Fereydūn/Farīdūn**, with modern spellings Fereydoun, Feridun, Faridun, Freydun, and Faredoon. **[LC/modern orthography]**
- **Epic dynastic epithet:** **Pīšdādī/Pishdadian**, placing him among the primordial “first-law” kings. **[MS/LC]**
- Middle Persian texts call him **purr-pērōzgar**, “very victorious,” and the *Dēnkard* calls him **tagīg**, “valiant,” and **zōrīg**, “powerful.” **[MS]**

### Etymology and the Trita problem

The name is securely inherited from Proto-Iranian *Θraitauna-*. Its further analysis is debated.

1. **Patronymic/derivative theory [MR].** Because Θraētaona and Θrita/Trita both contain the Indo-Iranian numeral base “three,” Bruce Lincoln and others reconstructed an earlier *Trito- and interpreted Θraētaona approximately as a derivative or “son of Trita.” Tafażżolī presents this as a suggestion, not a textual fact.

2. **Separated-brothers theory [MR].** Āθβiia corresponds linguistically to Vedic Āptya. Avestan Θrita and Vedic Trita Āptya are healers or ritual figures, while Θraētaona inherits the dragon combat. Mary Boyce therefore proposed that Iranian tradition differentiated two members of an old *Āptya family—one physician, one warrior—and subsequently transferred medical power to Θraētaona.

3. **Not simple identity.** Vedic **Trita Āptya** and Avestan **Θraētaona Āθβiiāni** are cognate figures within a shared Indo-Iranian complex, but no Vedic text calls Trita “Fereydun,” and the surviving Indian and Iranian narratives differ. The Rigvedic Trita tradition involves Vṛtra/Viśvarūpa, cattle, Soma, a well, and cooperation or rivalry with Indra; the Iranian tradition makes Θraētaona the personal conqueror of Aži Dahāka. Treating them as one unchanged hero is **modern harmonization**, not primary evidence. [Iranica, “Aždahā”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/azdaha/azdaha-i-in-old-and-middle-iranian/).

### Later identifications

Islamic chronographers sought to integrate Iranian sacred history with biblical-Qurʾanic universal history:

- **Noah:** al-Bīrūnī reports an equation of Fereydun with Noah, ca. 1000. **[IS]**
- **Abraham:** al-Maqdisī compares or identifies him with Abraham, late tenth century. **[IS]**
- **Nimrod:** al-Dīnawarī records an equation with Nimrod, ninth century. **[IS]**
- Al-Maqdisī’s story that he and another child were twins in the womb, followed by a miraculous birth, is generally understood as an adaptation of Jacob-and-Esau material. **[IS]**

These equations are mutually incompatible learned synchronisms; they are not Iranian variants of his original name.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### Avestan dossier

The Avestan texts are oral-liturgical compositions of differing ages. Their conventional chapter numbers belong to the transmitted canon, and their medieval manuscripts are not contemporaneous records of an Iron Age person.

- **Yasna 9.7–8, Hōm Yašt:** Āθβiia is the second primordial Haoma-presser and receives as his reward the birth of Θraētaona, who “smote” Aži Dahāka. The following verses separately name Θrita, father of Urvaḵšaya and Kərəsāspa; this is important evidence that Θraētaona and Θrita were not simply identical within the received Avesta. **[EA]** [Text](https://www.avesta.org/yasna/yasna.htm).
- **Yašt 5.33–35, Ābān Yašt:** in four-cornered Varəna, Θraētaona offers Arədvī Sūrā Anāhitā one hundred stallions, one thousand cattle, and ten thousand sheep; he asks to overcome Dahāka and release the women Saŋhavāc and Arənawāc. The goddess grants the boon. **[EA]** [Text](https://www.avesta.org/ka/yt5sbe.htm).
- **Yašt 15.23–24, Rām Yašt:** the same petition and victory are addressed to Vāyu, who grants the boon. **[EA]** [Text](https://www.avesta.org/ka/yt15sbe.htm).
- **Yašt 19.36–37, Zamyād Yašt:** Θraētaona receives the second portion of the Glory that departed from Yima and smites Dahāka. At 19.92, the Glory borne by Θraētaona is recalled “when Aži Dahāka was killed.” **[EA]** [Text](https://www.avesta.org/ka/yt19sbe.htm).
- **Yašt 5.61–65:** Θraētaona transforms or hurls the otherwise obscure Vafra Navāza into the air as a bird; Anāhitā later rescues him. The text gives no motive for Θraētaona’s act. **[EA]**
- **Vendīdād 1.17:** Varəna is the fourteenth good land and the birthplace of Θraētaona, “who smote Aži Dahāka.” Later Pahlavi commentary identifies Varəna with the Caspian/Damāvand region, but that localization is not secure for the Avestan geographical name. **[EA; later gloss]** [Text](https://www.avesta.org/vendidad/vd1sbe.htm).
- **Yašt 13.131 and Vendīdād 7.58:** his *fravaši* is invoked against itch, fever, bodily humors, incontinence, and serpent-created afflictions. This underlies his later reputation as healer. **[EA]**
- **Vendīdād 20:** Θrita, not Θraētaona, is explicitly the first physician to receive healing remedies. Later tradition transfers this function to Fereydun. **[EA/MS]** [Text](https://www.avesta.org/vendidad/vd20sbe.htm).

The Avestan record provides no parents beyond Āθβiia, no mother, childhood, cow nurse, mace, rebel army, sons, world division, or death.

### Middle Persian corpus

- **Bundahišn**, a Zoroastrian cosmological and genealogical compilation redacted in the ninth century from earlier materials: makes Frēdōn son of Pūrtōrā the Āspiyān and descendant of Yim; says he avenged Yim; names his brothers Barmāyūn and Katāyūn; names his sons Salm, Tūz/Tūč, and Ērik/Airik; and gives a more elaborate genealogy after Ērik’s murder. The oldest substantially complete manuscripts are sixteenth-century. **[MS]** [Text and translation](https://www.avesta.org/mp/bundahis.html); [manuscript history](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/bundahisn-primal-creation/).
- **Dēnkard**, principally ninth-century compilation with final redaction in the tenth century: preserves religious-historical traditions in which Ohrmazd stops Frēdōn from killing Dahāg because demonic creatures would escape his body; Frēdōn binds him instead. The archetype named in later colophons was copied at Baghdad in 1020, while the principal surviving manuscript is later and damaged. **[MS]** [Text history](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/denkard/).
- **Mēnōg ī xrad**, probably sixth-century material in a later redaction: says Fereydun was initially immortal but Ahriman made him mortal. **[MS]**
- **Pahlavi Rivāyat:** adds that his mortality followed either contempt for Ohrmazd or another moral failure; it also preserves Dahāg’s imprisonment until the end time, when Garšāsp kills him. **[MS]**
- **Zand ī Wahman Yasn and related apocalyptic literature:** place Dahāg’s escape and Garšāsp’s final victory in the eschaton. Fereydun’s own role has ended with the binding. **[MS]**

### Islamic and Persian national-history corpus

- **Al-Dīnawarī, *al-Aḵbār al-ṭewāl*, ninth century; al-Ṭabarī, *Taʾrīḵ*, completed 915; al-Masʿūdī, tenth century; Ḥamza al-Eṣfahānī, tenth century; al-Maqdisī, late tenth century; al-Thaʿālibī, *Ḡorar*, ca. 1020; and Balʿamī’s Persian adaptation of Ṭabarī, tenth century** transmit differing forms of the Iranian king list derived, directly or indirectly, from Sasanian royal histories. They preserve Dahāka’s defeat, world division, sons, and numerous learned synchronisms. **[IS/LC]**
- The lost Sasanian **Xwadāy-nāmag**, compiled in Middle Persian before the Arab conquest and subsequently translated or adapted, probably supplied the regnal framework linking primordial myth to royal history. Its text does not survive; its exact contribution to any individual Fereydun episode must therefore be reconstructed from later witnesses. **[MR]** [Iranica on Sasanian royal literature](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/belles-lettres-sasanian-iran/).
- **Ferdowsi, *Šāhnāma***, completed in its final form around 1010: the fullest canonical narrative. Its earliest dated surviving manuscript is Florence MS Magl. III.24 of 9 May 1217, containing the poem’s first half. Djalal Khaleghi-Motlagh’s eight-volume critical edition, 1987–2005/07, collates the earliest manuscripts; Arthur and Edmond Warner’s nine-volume English translation, 1905–25, is complete but based on older editions; Dick Davis’s 2006 translation, revised in 2016, is literary and selective rather than a critical apparatus. [Florence manuscript](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/italy-viii-persian-manuscripts-2/); [Khaleghi-Motlagh edition](https://persianheritagefoundation.org/what-we-do/publications/persian-text-series/); [Davis edition](https://books.google.com/books/about/Shahnameh.html?id=wyjaCwAAQBAJ).
- **Asadī Ṭūsī, *Garšāsp-nāma***, completed 1066: Garšāsp first serves Zahhāk, then acknowledges Fereydun after Zahhāk’s defeat; Garšāsp and Narēmān campaign in Turan and China and send a captive ruler to Fereydun. Its oldest dated complete manuscript is British Library Or. 2780, copied 1397. **[medieval epic accretion]** [Corpus notice](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/garsasp-nama/).
- **Borzū-nāma**, probably an accretive epic compiled in forms between the eleventh and later medieval centuries, concerns Rostam’s descendants, not Fereydun’s personal cycle. Mentions of Fereydun are genealogical or proverbial; it supplies no primary early evidence about him. **[medieval accretion]** [Corpus notice](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/borzu-nama-2-epic-poem/).
- The **Haft Laškar** tradition gives a late variant in which Fereydun captures Zahhāk, Zahhāk escapes during Fereydun’s absence, and Garšāsp captures him again. This contradicts both Ferdowsi and the standard Pahlavi eschatology. **[late epic variant]**

## 3. The story and its development

### A. Avestan core: Glory, dragon, women

Yima loses his royal *xᵛarənah* after embracing falsehood. In *Yašt* 19.35–38, the Glory leaves three times: Mithra obtains the first portion, Θraētaona the second, and Kərəsāspa the third. Θraētaona’s possession of it makes him the most victorious man after Zarathustra. He defeats Aži Dahāka, described as three-mouthed, three-headed, six-eyed, immensely perceptive, and the strongest corporeal Lie created by Angra Mainyu. **[EA]**

In *Yašts* 5 and 15, Θraētaona does not win unaided: he sacrifices to Anāhitā and Vāyu, requesting victory and the release of Saŋhavāc and Arənawāc. The women are called Dahāka’s “wives” in the old translation, though the Iranian expression need not imply a freely contracted marriage. They are captives whom the hero takes from a monstrous aggressor. **[EA]**

The verb translated “smote” can denote violent defeat and *Yašt* 19.92 plainly speaks of the occasion “when Aži Dahāka was killed.” Nothing in the surviving Avestan notices says that he was spared or chained. **[EA]**

### B. Killing becomes imprisonment

The *Dēnkard* changes the outcome: Fereydun begins to kill Dahāg, but Ohrmazd warns that innumerable noxious creatures will emerge from the corpse. He therefore binds and imprisons him on Damāvand. The Pahlavi apocalyptic tradition says Dahāg will escape near the end of time and be slain by the resurrected Garšāsp. **[MS]**

This is not a harmless expansion. It transfers Dahāg’s final death from Fereydun’s primordial victory to an eschatological combat and explains why evil persists. Tafażżolī, following Boyce, calls it a late Zoroastrian scholastic elaboration of the simpler Avestan killing.

Ferdowsi adopts imprisonment. Fereydun strikes Zahhāk with the ox-headed mace and prepares to kill him, but the angel Sorūš tells him that Zahhāk’s appointed time has not arrived. Fereydun binds him with lion hide, carries him to Damāvand, and pins him within the mountain. **[LC]**

Al-Ṭabarī contains evidence for both strands: one form implies death; another repeats imprisonment. The versions should not be harmonized into “he almost killed him” unless the individual source says so.

### C. Birth, persecution, and the cow Barmāya

In Ferdowsi, Zahhāk dreams that a young man bearing a cow-headed mace will overthrow him. Astrologers identify the unborn or infant Fereydun. His father Ābtin, descended from Jamshid, is seized and killed to feed Zahhāk’s shoulder-serpents. His mother **Farānak** hides the child.

For three years the marvelous, multicolored cow **Barmāya/Barmāyeh** nurses him in a meadow. Warned of danger, Farānak carries him to Mount Alborz, where a pious hermit raises him. Zahhāk’s men discover and kill Barmāya and devastate the pasture. At sixteen or eighteen, depending on textual reading and retelling, Fereydun descends, learns his ancestry, and demands vengeance. **[LC]**

The named cow and detailed childhood are absent from the Avesta. Comparative scholars have suggested that Saŋhavāc and Arənawāc replaced cattle recovered from a serpent in an older Indo-Iranian myth, or conversely that later Persian tradition reintroduced cattle through Barmāya. That is a reconstruction, not a surviving narrative. **[MR]**

Some Middle Persian genealogies instead make Frēdōn the son of Pūrtōrā Āspiyān and give him brothers named Barmāyūn and Katāyūn. The resemblance between **Barmāyūn** and the cow **Barmāya** may reflect redistribution of names across traditions; it does not justify treating the cow as his brother or vice versa.

### D. Kāva’s revolt and the ox-headed mace

In Ferdowsi, the blacksmith **Kāva** denounces Zahhāk after nearly all his sons have been sacrificed. He tears up the document falsely attesting Zahhāk’s justice, raises his leather apron as a banner, and leads the people to Fereydun. The apron becomes the royal **Derafš-e Kāviān**. Fereydun is thus the divinely favored king, but Kāva supplies the public insurrection. **[LC]**

Fereydun draws a model for a mace ending in a buffalo or ox head and has smiths forge it. He crosses the Arvand River, takes Zahhāk’s palace, frees Arnavāz and Shahrnāz, and defeats the returning tyrant. The ox-headed mace is not named in the Avesta. It is a canonical Persian epic attribute and subsequently an emblem of legitimate Iranian kingship. **[LC]**

Archaeology establishes that horned and bovine mace-heads existed in Iran before Ferdowsi—including a Sasanian three-bull mace head—but no excavated mace can be identified as “Fereydun’s.” Later Islamic ceremonial maces deliberately cited the epic type; an eighteenth-century example is inscribed “Fereydun-as-Sultan.” **[archaeological object; later reception]** [British Museum mace](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/W_1938-1110-1); [Gardner Museum mace](https://www.gardnermuseum.org/isgm-rooms/modals/art-object/7073/nojs).

### E. Kingship and Mehragān

After Zahhāk’s overthrow, Ferdowsi makes Fereydun assume the crown at the beginning of the month Mehr and establish or renew **Mehragān**. Fires are lit, incense burned, wine drunk, and the world is restored through justice. He reigns five hundred years. **[LC]**

Al-Bīrūn, ca. 1000, likewise records that Mehragān commemorated Fereydun’s victory and Zahhāk’s confinement. The festival itself is much older and principally connected with Mithra/Mehr; the victory story is a historical explanation attached to it, not proof that Fereydun originally founded the rite. **[IS]** [Mehragān dossier](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/mehragan/).

### F. Marriage and sons

Ferdowsi says Fereydun’s envoys obtain three daughters of the king of Yemen—often called **Sarv**—for his three sons. The brides are not consistently named in the oldest textual strata. In later naming traditions they become Arzū, Māh, and Sahī, with manuscript and retelling variation. Fereydun tests the couples by appearing as a dragon; the sons respond differently, enabling him to discern their characters and assign the names Salm, Tur, and Iraj. **[LC]**

Another widespread tradition makes the rescued **Arnavāz and Shahrnāz** Fereydun’s consorts. Ferdowsi’s genealogy is textually complicated: the three sons are born from two women, but manuscripts and summaries differ over maternity. No Avestan text calls either captive his wife or names any child.

### G. Division of the world

Fereydun partitions his empire:

- **Salm**, eldest: Rūm and the western lands.
- **Tur/Tūz**, second: Turan, China, and the eastern or northeastern lands.
- **Iraj/Ērik**, youngest and favored: Iran and the central/best portion of the world, together with the throne and royal insignia.

**[MS/LC]**

The *Bundahišn* already has Salm, Tūz, and Airik; Ferdowsi turns the partition into an extended moral and political tragedy. This is not a record of a historical Bronze Age division of Eurasia. It is mythic ethnography, systematized in late antique Iranian royal ideology and reshaped by medieval geography. Reading Salm as the literal ancestor of Romans, Tur as the biological ancestor of all Turks, and Iraj as the first historical Iranian is a medieval genealogical schema, not population history.

### H. Murder of Iraj and origin of the Iran–Turan feud

Salm and Tur resent Iraj’s superior inheritance. Iraj voluntarily goes to them, rejects war, and offers to surrender his kingship, but Tur kills him—by dagger, blow, or decapitation according to textual form—and sends the severed head to Fereydun. Ferdowsi describes Fereydun’s lament, blindness or failing sight from grief in some readings, and curse upon the murderers. **[LC]**

The *Bundahišn* gives a harsher genealogical variant: Salm and Tūz kill Airik and his sons; Fereydun hides Airik’s daughter, whose line continues through further daughters until Manuščihr is born. Ferdowsi makes Iraj’s daughter Māhāfarīd the mother or ancestress through whom **Manuchehr** descends. **[MS/LC]**

Fereydun raises Manuchehr and sends him to avenge Iraj. Manuchehr defeats and kills Tur and Salm; their heads are returned to Fereydun. The old king mourns rather than celebrates the destruction of his line. This cycle supplies the national epic with a genealogy for the antagonism of Iran and Turan, although later Turanian enemies—especially Afrāsīāb—belong to additional narrative strata. The war is not the consequence of a documented prehistoric treaty; it is an etiological epic history.

### I. Death and body

The Avesta says nothing of Θraētaona’s death or burial. **[absence of evidence]**

- *Mēnōg ī xrad* says he was once immortal but Ahriman made him mortal. **[MS]**
- The Pahlavi *Rivāyat* attributes mortality to contempt for Ohrmazd; al-Thaʿālibī instead connects it with his unjust preference for Iraj. **[MS/IS variants]**
- Ferdowsi says that after Manuchehr’s victory Fereydun eventually dies, is prepared according to royal custom, and is placed in a **dakhma/tomb**. The poem supplies a literary funeral, not a location that can be archaeologically identified. **[LC]**
- No securely excavated tomb, body, relic, or funerary inscription of Fereydun is known. Claims that Damāvand is “Fereydun’s tomb” confuse Zahhāk’s legendary prison with Fereydun’s burial and are **modern error**.

### Documented interactions: divine beings and heroes

Each line records an explicit interaction, not merely shared membership in a genealogy.

- **Āθβiia/Athwya** — father whose Haoma pressing is rewarded by Θraētaona’s birth; *Yasna* 9.7–8, Younger Avestan. **[EA]**
- **Haoma/Hōm** — divine plant-being recounts the circumstances of his birth; *Yasna* 9.7–8. Θraētaona himself is not said there to press Haoma. **[EA]**
- **Aži Dahāka/Dahāg/Zahhāk** — adversary defeated and apparently killed in the Avesta; spared, bound, and imprisoned in Middle Persian and Ferdowsian versions; *Yasna* 9, *Yašts* 5, 15, 19; *Dēnkard*; *Šāhnāma*. **[EA/MS/LC]**
- **Angra Mainyu/Ahriman** — creates Dahāka against the material world; later makes Fereydun mortal; *Yašt* 19 and *Mēnōg ī xrad*. No early text describes a face-to-face battle. **[EA/MS]**
- **Arədvī Sūrā Anāhitā** — receives Θraētaona’s immense sacrifice and grants victory over Dahāka and rescue of the women; *Yašt* 5.33–35. **[EA]**
- **Vāyu** — receives the same request and grants it; *Yašt* 15.23–24. **[EA]**
- **Xᵛarənah/Farr, the royal Glory** — leaves Yima and adheres to Θraētaona, enabling victorious kingship; *Yašt* 19.36–37, 92. This is a sacral power, not consistently a personified god. **[EA]**
- **Yima/Jamshid** — Θraētaona receives one portion of Yima’s departed Glory; later traditions make Fereydun his descendant and avenger. *Yašt* 19; *Bundahišn*; *Šāhnāma*. **[EA/MS/LC]**
- **Mithra** — receives the first portion of Yima’s Glory immediately before Θraētaona receives the second; *Yašt* 19.35–36. This is succession in a list, not a meeting. **[EA]**
- **Kərəsāspa/Garšāsp** — receives the third portion of Yima’s Glory after Θraētaona; in Pahlavi eschatology ultimately kills the Dahāg whom Fereydun bound; in Asadī’s 1066 epic submits to King Fereydun. **[EA/MS/medieval epic]**
- **Ohrmazd/Ahura Mazda** — in the *Dēnkard*, commands Fereydun not to kill Dahāg because creatures would escape the corpse; in the Avestan passages Ahura Mazda narrates or authorizes the cosmic order but does not personally issue that command. **[MS; not EA]**
- **Sorūš/Sraoša** — in Ferdowsi, intervenes as Fereydun is about to kill Zahhāk and orders imprisonment on Damāvand. **[LC]**
- **Saŋhavāc/Šahrnāz** — captive of Dahāka whom Θraētaona asks to release; later freed from Zahhāk’s palace and attached to Fereydun’s household. *Yašts* 5 and 15; *Šāhnāma*. **[EA/LC]**
- **Arənawāc/Arnavāz** — the second captive released under the same circumstances. **[EA/LC]**
- **Vafra Navāza** — Θraētaona hurls or transforms him into a bird; Anāhitā returns him safely to earth; *Yašt* 5.61–65. Motive and wider story are lost. **[EA]**
- **Θrita/Trita** — separately named Avestan healer and Haoma figure; cognate and structurally related, but no primary text stages an interaction with Θraētaona. **[EA; relationship MR]**
- **Farānak** — mother who hides and protects Fereydun, entrusts him to Barmāya and the Alborz hermit, then celebrates his coronation; *Šāhnāma*, ca. 1010. **[LC]**
- **Ābtin** — father killed under Zahhāk; his death motivates vengeance; *Šāhnāma*. **[LC]**
- **Barmāya/Barmāyeh** — miraculous cow that nurses the child and is killed by Zahhāk; *Šāhnāma*. A named animal, not a goddess. **[LC]**
- **Kāva/Kāveh** — raises the rebellion and leads the people to Fereydun; Fereydun adopts Kāva’s apron as the royal banner; *Šāhnāma*. **[LC]**
- **Kiyānuš and Barmāya** — companions or brothers-in-arms who accompany Fereydun in some *Šāhnāma* manuscripts and later narratives. **[LC/textually variable]**
- **Salm** — eldest son; receives the western lands, conspires in Iraj’s murder, and is ultimately killed by Manuchehr; *Bundahišn*, national histories, *Šāhnāma*. **[MS/LC]**
- **Tur/Tūz** — second son; receives Turan and China, kills Iraj in Ferdowsi, and is killed by Manuchehr. **[MS/LC]**
- **Iraj/Airik/Ērik** — favored youngest son; receives Iran, seeks peace with his brothers, and is murdered. **[MS/LC]**
- **Manuchehr/Manuščihr** — descendant of Iraj whom Fereydun conceals or raises, recognizes as heir, and commissions or blesses as avenger. **[MS/LC]**
- **Sarv, king of Yemen** — father of three princesses whom Fereydun obtains for his sons; Ferdowsi, ca. 1010. **[LC]**
- **Narēmān** — Garšāsp’s adopted heir; campaigns under Fereydun’s overlordship in Asadī’s *Garšāsp-nāma*, 1066. **[medieval epic accretion]**
- **Faḡfūr of China** — defeated by Garšāsp and brought captive to Fereydun; *Garšāsp-nāma*, 1066. **[medieval epic accretion]**

There is no primary-source interaction between Fereydun and Indra, Vṛtra, Cyrus, or a historical Achaemenid king. Such pairings are comparative or modern.

## 5. Cult, tomb, and ritual

### Cult status

No temple, *heroon*, priesthood, votive inscription, sacrifice calendar, or independently documented cult devoted specifically to Θraētaona/Fereydun has been identified. He appears inside Zoroastrian liturgy because worshippers recite texts in which he sacrifices to gods and because his *fravaši* is therapeutically invoked. That is religious veneration within a larger ritual system, but it is not evidence for an autonomous “cult of Fereydun.”

The sacrifices of one hundred horses, one thousand cattle, and ten thousand sheep in *Yašts* 5 and 15 are actions **performed by the hero** within hymn narrative. They are not evidence that historical worshippers sacrificed those quantities **to him**.

### Healing and magical practice

*Yašt* 13.131 invokes his *fravaši* against illnesses, especially those conceptualized as serpent-produced. Manichaean Middle Persian incantations later include Frēdōn among efficacious “names of power,” and Islamic authors remember him as an inventor or master of medicine. **[EA/MS/IS]** This is the clearest approach to practical devotion, but the evidence supports an apotropaic name and healing reputation, not a temple cult.

### Damāvand

Damāvand is the literary and folkloric prison of Zahhāk. Medieval accounts locate Fereydun’s birth near Var/Varak in Larijan and identify Varəna with the Caspian highlands. They also associate local caves, wells, noises, smoke, and sulphurous emissions with the chained monster. **[MS/IS/RV]** [Damāvand in mythology](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/damavand/damavand-ii-in-iranian-mythology/).

No excavated cave on Damāvand can be demonstrated to be Zahhāk’s prison; no associated structure is securely a shrine of Fereydun. “Takht-e Fereydun” and similar mountain place-names attest continuing memory, not an ancient sanctuary.

### Mehragān

Ferdowsi and al-Bīrūnī connect Mehragān with Fereydun’s victory and accession. Modern Zoroastrian and Iranian celebrations sometimes retell that episode. Nevertheless, Mehragān is a festival of Mehr/Mithra and the autumn season; Fereydun functions as its royal etiological hero, not its divine recipient. In 2024 UNESCO inscribed **“Mehregan/Mihragan and its associated cultural practices”** for Iran and Tajikistan; nomination materials mention Fereydun as the hero of Good’s victory. That constitutes documented modern heritage transmission, not proof of a prehistoric Fereydun cult. [UNESCO nomination material](https://ich.unesco.org/doc/src/43549-EN.pdf).

### Material attributes

Actual bovine mace heads from Sasanian and later Islamic Iran show that the form was culturally real. Later kings, warriors, and Zoroastrian priests carried ceremonial examples; the object had become a generalized emblem of Iranian authority and victory. Connecting every archaeological bull mace directly to Fereydun would be circular: the artifacts rarely name him, while the literary association is explicit only in medieval and later evidence.

## 6. Historical evolution of the figure

### I. Indo-Iranian prehistory: reconstructed, not directly attested

Comparative linguistics links Θraētaona Āθβiiāni with Trita Āptya and with a broader Indo-Iranian pattern involving a “third” hero, intoxicating sacred drink, cattle or women, and combat with a multiheaded serpent. Lincoln reconstructs a Proto-Indo-European “third man” myth; Boyce reconstructs differentiated warrior and healer brothers. **[MR]** Neither reconstruction is an ancient written source, and the Proto-Indo-European figure *Trito is a scholarly model, not a recovered personal name from an inscription.

### II. Younger Avestan heroic-liturgical phase, first millennium BCE

Θraētaona is a mortal exemplar of victorious sacral kingship. His narrative is compressed into ritual allusions: genealogy, sacrifices, royal Glory, Dahāka, captive women, and the Vafra Navāza fragment. The audience apparently knew stories no longer preserved. **[EA]**

### III. Sasanian and early Islamic systematization, third–tenth centuries CE

Priestly redaction turns the defeated dragon into an eschatological prisoner; Frēdōn enters linear world history, acquires a dynasty, brothers, sons, inventions, cities, fire-temple foundations, and a role in the geographic ordering of peoples. The lost *Xwadāy-nāmag* tradition organizes mythic kings into the prehistory of Iranian monarchy. **[MS]**

The tripartition becomes political ethnogenesis: Salm, Tūz, and Ērik represent westerners, Turanians, and Iranians. This layer reflects Sasanian and post-Sasanian conceptions of *Ērānšahr*, not necessarily the geography of the Avestan poets.

### IV. Arabic and New Persian universal history, ninth–eleventh centuries

Muslim historians preserve Iranian narrative while synchronizing it with prophets and biblical genealogies. Fereydun may be Noah, Abraham, or Nimrod; his capital may be placed at Babylon; miracles and twin-birth material enter the story. **[IS]**

### V. Ferdowsi’s canonical shaping, ca. 1010

Ferdowsi gives the cycle its enduring literary architecture:

1. Zahhāk’s nightmare;
2. Ābtin’s death and Farānak’s flight;
3. Barmāya’s nursing and death;
4. Kāva’s uprising;
5. construction of the ox-headed mace;
6. conquest of Zahhāk’s palace;
7. Sorūš’s intervention and imprisonment on Damāvand;
8. just kingship and Mehragān;
9. testing and naming of the sons;
10. division of the world;
11. Iraj’s murder;
12. Manuchehr’s vengeance;
13. Fereydun’s grief and death.

The king is neither flawless nor merely triumphant. His partiality and division of the realm become the conditions of dynastic catastrophe; his final image is the bereaved ruler who outlives his sons.

### VI. Secondary epics and regional legend, eleventh–nineteenth centuries

Asadī places Garšāsp under Fereydun’s sovereignty. Later epics, chronicles, romances, and oral traditions attach new wars, foundations, magical acts, and localities to him. Caspian historical works place his birth at Var near Larijan; Damāvand stories treat volcanic noises and fumes as signs of Zahhāk. **[medieval accretion/RV]**

### VII. Illustrated royal manuscript culture, fourteenth–seventeenth centuries

The Great Mongol *Šāhnāma*, ca. 1330s, emphasizes Fereydun’s mourning and royal tragedy. A surviving folio shows him visiting Iraj’s palace in grief. [Great Mongol folio](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File%3AFaridun_goes_to_Iraj%E2%80%99s_palace_and_mourns%2C_Folio_from_the_Great_Mongol_Shahnama.jpg).

The Shah Tahmasp *Šāhnāma*, produced at Tabriz ca. 1520s–1530s, monumentalizes the cycle: Zahhāk hears the prophecy of Fereydun; Fereydun attacks with the ox-headed mace; Salm and Tur receive his reply. These paintings visualize Safavid kingship through a pre-Islamic epic past. [Metropolitan Museum folio](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/452113); [Aga Khan Museum folio](https://collections.agakhanmuseum.org/collection/artifact/salm-and-tur-receive-the-reply-of-faridun-and-manuchihr-akm495); [Smithsonian battle image](https://asia-archive.si.edu/learn/shahnama/f1996-2/).

### VIII. Modern national-symbolic phase, nineteenth century to present

Qajar images and ceremonial maces turn Fereydun into an immediately legible emblem of royal justice. Twentieth-century Iranian nationalism elevated the *Šāhnāma* as national patrimony, especially during the 1934 Ferdowsi millennium and Pahlavi state-building. This was a broad appropriation of Ferdowsi and pre-Islamic kingship; evidence for a distinct state “cult of Fereydun” is absent.

Contemporary political discourse often maps Zahhāk onto tyranny, Kāva onto popular revolt, and Fereydun onto legitimate national renewal. This use occurs among monarchists, secular nationalists, democrats, and opponents of the Islamic Republic; it is not exclusive to a single party. Conversely, ethno-nationalist readings can literalize Salm, Tur, and Iraj as immutable races. That racialization is a modern ideological use of medieval mythic ethnography, not ancient population science.

## 7. Modern reception and reinvention

### Literature and scholarship

Translations by the Warners and Davis made the Fereydun cycle accessible in English, while critical work by Khaleghi-Motlagh re-established it from the manuscript tradition. Modern children’s books and graphic retellings commonly simplify the family tragedy into “good Iranian liberator defeats foreign tyrant,” reducing the ambiguity of the Iraj episode.

Structuralist and comparativist interpretations include:

- **Georges Dumézil:** read Indo-European myth through tripartite social functions; Fereydun’s tripartition invites such comparison, although direct derivation remains debated. **[MR]**
- **Bruce Lincoln:** reconstructed *Trito, a “third” man who recovers cattle from a serpent; influential but necessarily hypothetical. **[MR]**
- **Mary Boyce:** reconstructed a divided Indo-Iranian Trita/Θraētaona complex and emphasized the transfer of healing functions. **[MR]**
- **Rank, Raglan, and Campbell:** Fereydun can be fitted retrospectively to persecuted-child or monomyth schemes, but none of those schemes is primary evidence, and fitting motifs to a universal checklist does not date them.
- **Dick Davis, Olga Davidson, Mahmoud Omidsalar, and Ehsan Yarshater:** treat the *Šāhnāma* as a work of oral-derived epic art, manuscript transmission, and national tradition rather than as a transparent historical chronicle.

### Visual arts

Four images have been especially durable:

1. the infant with Barmāya;
2. the young king with ox-headed mace;
3. Zahhāk bound on Damāvand;
4. the aged Fereydun mourning Iraj.

The Great Mongol and Shah Tahmasp manuscripts did not merely illustrate a fixed story; they recoded it for Ilkhanid and Safavid courts through contemporary clothing, architecture, physiognomy, and royal ceremony.

### Film, animation, comics, and games

- **The Legend of Mardoush**, Iranian stop-motion trilogy produced 2002–05, adapts Zahhāk’s rise and Fereydun’s upbringing, including Farānak and the Alborz refuge. **[MI/adaptation]**
- **The Last Fiction**, directed by Ashkan Rahgozar and released theatrically in 2018, reimagines the Zahhāk cycle as dark fantasy; Ashkan Khatibi voices Fereydun. Its characters and chronology are intentionally redesigned and must not be cited as ancient tradition. **[MI]**
- Modern *Shahnameh* comics and illustrated children’s series translate the cycle into superhero and graphic-novel conventions. **[MI]** [Modern comic project](https://theshahnameh.com/).
- Online game and fan communities sometimes turn Fereydun into a summonable warrior, assign him personal guilt over later heroes, or give him invented international genealogies. These are demonstrably contemporary inventions, even when they use Avestan spellings. **[MI]**

No opera or internationally canonical feature film centered solely on Fereydun has shaped his modern image to the degree that manuscript painting and Iranian animation have.

## 8. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Historicity

No contemporary inscription, administrative document, royal monument, coin, or datable archaeological horizon identifies a ruler Θraētaona/Fereydun. The earliest evidence is poetic-religious myth. Consequently, “Fereydun was a historical king” is unproved.

Abolala Soudavar has proposed that aspects of Fereydun reflect **Cyrus the Great**. The suggested parallels include legitimate overthrow of a tyrant, royal *farr*, and remembered imperial geography. **[MR, minority proposal]** The objections are fundamental: Θraētaona is linguistically and mythologically older than Cyrus; the Avestan dragon combat has no specific Achaemenid setting; and no ancient source identifies Cyrus with him. At most, later royal history could have colored an older hero.

### Dating and transmission

The largest evidentiary gap is between the Avestan compositions and their manuscripts. Oral and priestly transmission preserved archaic language, but it also leaves individual narrative layers hard to date. The Avestan passages cannot automatically be labeled “Sasanian” merely because the canon was organized under Sasanian patronage; equally, their precise placement in the second or first millennium BCE remains debated.

The *Šāhnāma* presents another gap: Ferdowsi completed it ca. 1010, but the earliest dated manuscript is 1217. Thousands of variant readings accumulated. Khaleghi-Motlagh’s edition is therefore essential; popular web texts and nineteenth-century translations should not be treated as a stable autograph.

### Killing versus binding

This is the best-documented transformation:

- Avestan: Θraētaona smites, and *Yašt* 19.92 speaks of Dahāka being killed. **[EA]**
- Pahlavi: Ohrmazd forbids the killing; Dahāg is bound until Garšāsp’s eschatological intervention. **[MS]**
- Ferdowsi: Sorūš stops the killing and directs imprisonment. **[LC]**
- Some Arabic histories preserve both outcomes. **[IS]**
- Late epic: Zahhāk may escape and be recaptured by Garšāsp. **[late variant]**

The popular assertion that “the Avesta says Zahhāk cannot die” is misleading. That rationale belongs to later exegetical tradition.

### Trita and Θraētaona

Phonology, patronymics, and shared motifs establish a genuine comparative problem. They do not establish that every deed of Vedic Trita once belonged to Fereydun. The received Avesta itself distinguishes Θraētaona son of Āθβiia from Θrita of the Sāma family in adjacent *Yasna* passages.

### Origin of Iran and Turan

The Iraj/Tur genealogy was historically powerful, but it is not a record of ethnogenesis. “Turanian” in the Iranian epic is not consistently synonymous with the later linguistic category “Turk,” and Ferdowsi’s imagined geography combines inherited Iranian myth with late antique and Islamic world geography. Modern racial or nationalist literalization is methodologically discredited.

### Cult and tomb

The search for a hero-cult produces a negative result. Liturgical mention, invocation of a *fravaši*, Mehragān etiologies, and Damāvand folklore are real evidence of belief; none establishes a dedicated sanctuary or physical tomb. Claims to possess Fereydun’s grave, remains, or mace lack archaeological documentation.

### Oral-formulaic and epic composition debates

The national epic preserves old narrative material but is not a word-for-word survival of an Achaemenid “original.” Scholars differ over the respective contribution of oral minstrel tradition, Sasanian court compilations, the prose *Šāhnāma* of Abū Manṣūr, Daqīqī, and Ferdowsi’s literary shaping. The lost *Xwadāy-nāmag* prevents a simple source stemma.

### Discredited or weakly supported claims still circulating

- **“Fereydun was Cyrus/Astyages.”** A modern hypothesis, not consensus.
- **“Trita and Fereydun are exactly the same person.”** Overstates cognation.
- **“The Avesta says he chained Zahhāk on Damāvand.”** The location and chaining come from Pahlavi commentary and later literature.
- **“Barmāya is an ancient Iranian cow-goddess.”** No primary source calls her a goddess.
- **“His ox-headed mace has been excavated.”** Bovine maces exist; none has Fereydun provenance.
- **“His tomb is on Damāvand.”** No archaeological or early textual evidence.
- **“The division among Salm, Tur, and Iraj records actual ancient national borders.”** Mythic geography.
- **“He founded the primordial cult of Mehragān.”** Later festival etiology.
- **“Zahhāk was Arab and Fereydun Persian in the earliest myth.”** The ethnic and political coding belongs to later national-epic development; Avestan Dahāka is a demonic serpent-being.
