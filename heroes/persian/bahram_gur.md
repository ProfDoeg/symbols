# Bahram Gur: Research Dossier

## Evidentiary key

- **[EARLY]** Secure contemporary or near-contemporary evidence: coins, fifth-century material culture, and late-antique Roman, Syriac, or Armenian testimony.
- **[HIST]** Historically plausible reconstruction from multiple sources, distinguished from the romance.
- **[SASANIAN TRADITION]** Material probably derived, directly or indirectly, from lost Sasanian royal historiography or court narrative, especially the *Khwadāy-nāmag* tradition, but preserved only in Islamic-period texts.
- **[CANONICAL]** The classical Persian literary form, especially Ferdowsi’s *Shāhnāma* and Neẓāmi’s *Haft Peykar*.
- **[VARIANT]** A competing textual or regional version.
- **[SYSTEMATIZATION]** A learned astrological, moral, genealogical, or cosmographical organization imposed on inherited narrative.
- **[ACCRETION]** Medieval or early-modern expansion.
- **[FOLKLORE]** Local oral tradition, place-legend, or proverbial survival not independently verified as ancient.
- **[RECONSTRUCTION]** A modern scholarly inference.
- **[MODERN INVENTION]** A recent attribution or elaboration unsupported by the premodern record.

## 1. Identification, names, epithets, and class

**Traditional class:** mortal historical king transformed into an **epic king, exemplary hunter, lover, warrior, and justice-restoring culture hero**. He is not a demigod and is not securely attested as a heroized dead receiving cult.

**Earliest secure attestation:** the coins issued in the reign of the Sasanian king Wahrām V, conventionally dated **420–438 CE**, bearing his Middle Persian royal name and titles. These establish the ruler, not the later epithet *Gur* or the romance. A fifth-century gilded silver hunting plate may already allude to the celebrated gazelle feat, but identification of unnamed royal hunters is interpretive rather than epigraphically certain. A securely proven “Bahram Gur” cult or tomb is absent.

Bahram Gur is the New Persian literary form of the historical Sasanian **Wahrām V**, son of Yazdegerd I. His reign and coins belong to history; the Arab childhood, lion ordeal, fabulous hunts, disguised wanderings, Indian marriages, seven planetary princesses, and disappearance into earth belong to successive strata of royal legend.

### Names and scripts

- **Middle Persian:** 𐭥𐭫𐭧𐭫𐭠𐭭, conventionally transliterated **Warahrān**, **Wahrām**, or **Wahrān**. The unvocalized Pahlavi spelling appears as *wlhlʾn*. **[EARLY]**
- **New Persian:** **بهرام**, *Bahrām*; with epithet **بهرام گور**, *Bahrām-e Gūr* or *Bahrām Gur*. **[CANONICAL]**
- **Arabic historiography:** **بهرام جور**, commonly *Bahrām Jūr*, Arabic *j* reflecting Persian *g* in the epithet. **[SASANIAN TRADITION]**
- **Armenian:** forms related to **Vrām/Vahagn** occur in the Iranian-name tradition; the royal name is normally rendered *Vram*. **[EARLY/TRANSLITERATION]**
- **Greek and Latin:** forms such as **Bararanes**, **Vararanes**, and **Gororanes** occur in late-antique and later transmission. **[EARLY/MEDIEVAL TRANSLITERATION]**
- **Georgian:** **Baram**. **[REGIONAL]**
- Modern spellings include Bahram, Bahrām, Behram, Vahram, Varahran, Warahran, Wahram, and Bahrom.

The name descends from Old Iranian **\*Vṛθragna**, Avestan **Vərəθraγna**, the divinity whose name means approximately “smiting resistance/obstruction” and hence “victory.” It developed through Middle Persian *Warahrān/Wahrām* into New Persian *Bahrām*. The name was also applied to the planet Mars. This etymology is philologically secure; it does **not** establish that the king was worshipped as Vərəθraγna. The identification of the literary hunter’s supernatural prowess with the victory god is a modern comparative interpretation of royal ideology, not an explicit statement in the narratives. [Encyclopaedia Iranica, “Bahrām”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/bahram-the-name-of-six-sasanian-kings/) and [“Hunting in Iran”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/hunting-in-iran/).

### The epithet *Gur*

New Persian **گور**, *gūr*, means the Asiatic wild ass or onager. The usual medieval explanations are:

1. he was exceptionally devoted to hunting onagers;
2. he earned the name through a spectacular shot involving an onager and a lion;
3. his gazelle/onager feats epitomized royal mastery of the hunt.

These explanations occur only in the later Persian and Arabic narrative tradition. The epithet is not securely demonstrated on his coins. **[SASANIAN TRADITION/CANONICAL]**

Persian also has a homonymous **گور**, *gūr*, “grave.” Poets exploited the pun: the king who spent his life “taking onagers” was finally “taken by the grave.” This becomes central to Neẓāmi, lyric poetry, and the quatrain attributed to ʿOmar Khayyām. **[MEDIEVAL LITERARY ACCRETION]**

Claims that *Gur* instead meant “great,” “hero,” or that Bahram received it for enforcing Zoroastrian exposure of corpses are not supported by the principal medieval sources and should be treated as **modern folk etymology**.

### Royal titles

His coins place him within standard Sasanian royal theology as a Mazda-worshipping, divine king of kings of Iranians and non-Iranians whose *čihr*—lineage, image, or radiance—is from the gods. This is royal titulature, not evidence that Bahram himself was regarded as a god. His crown has stepped crenellations and a prominent crescent; some reverse types place the royal bust amid the flames of the fire altar. [British Museum coin, 420–438](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/C_1920-0515-192); [Encyclopaedia Iranica, “Bahrām V Gōr”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/bahram-the-name-of-six-sasanian-kings/bahram-v/).

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### 2.1 Contemporary record

#### Coinage, 420–438

**[EARLY]** Silver drachms and rarer gold and copper issues are the firmest contemporary evidence. They identify Wahrām as king, display his crown and fire altar, and document a functioning imperial mint system. They do not call him “Gur,” depict Azadeh, recount a lion ordeal, or record seven wives. Sasanian coinage is therefore evidence for the historical ruler and royal representation, not by itself for the romance. [Iranica, “Sasanian Coinage”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/sasanian-coinage/).

#### Roman ecclesiastical histories, fifth century

**[EARLY, EXTERNAL]** Socrates Scholasticus, *Ecclesiastical History* 7.18, and Theodoret, *Ecclesiastical History* 5.37, written within decades of the events, associate the Persian king’s accession period with persecution of Christians and the Roman–Persian war of 421–422. Their interests are ecclesiastical and polemical. They know a Persian monarch at war with Theodosius II; they do not know the lion ordeal, seven beauties, or Bahram’s disguised adventures.

#### Armenian and Syriac traditions

**[EARLY/NEAR-CONTEMPORARY]** Armenian historiography preserves his role in the final suppression of the Arsacid Armenian monarchy: Artašēs/Ardashir was removed and Armenia moved toward marzbān government. Syriac martyr traditions preserve Christian suffering under Yazdegerd I and Bahram, but theological narrative and chronology require source criticism. These witnesses counter the uniformly genial king of later Persian romance.

### 2.2 The possible early visual narrative

A gilded silver plate now in the Metropolitan Museum, dated approximately **400–500 CE**, shows a mounted royal archer altering the apparent sex of gazelles by shooting away horns and supplying false ones with arrows. The museum interprets it as the earliest surviving image of the feat later narrated of Bahram and Azadeh. If the identification is right, the story circulated in or close to Bahram’s own century, long before Ferdowsi. The vessel has no narrative inscription naming either figure, however; royal hunting imagery was conventional, and attribution remains iconographic. **[EARLY OBJECT; IDENTITY RECONSTRUCTED]** [Metropolitan Museum educational catalogue](https://www.metmuseum.org/-/media/files/learn/for-educators/publications-for-educators/art-of-the-ancient-near-east.pdf).

A later Sasanian silver plate in the Hermitage, dated to the late sixth or early seventh century, explicitly catalogued as Bahram and Azadeh, shows the king and harpist on a camel among gazelles. It demonstrates the pre-Islamic antiquity of this pictorial type, though again the identification comes from the scene rather than a naming inscription. **[LATE SASANIAN ICONOGRAPHY]** [Hermitage Museum](https://hermitagemuseum.org/digital-collection/97899).

### 2.3 Lost Sasanian royal tradition

The Middle Persian **Khwadāy-nāmag**, “Book of Lords/Kings,” compiled and revised during the late Sasanian period, does not survive. Arabic translations and adaptations, including a version associated with Ebn al-Moqaffaʿ in the eighth century, are also lost. It is often invoked as the source of Islamic accounts of Bahram, but Jaakko Hämeen-Anttila has shown that “the *Khwadāy-nāmag*” should not be imagined as a single recoverable text behind every agreement: Arabic and Persian authors combined multiple written and oral sources. **[LOST SOURCE; MODERN RECONSTRUCTION]** [Hämeen-Anttila, *Khwadāynāmag: The Middle Persian Book of Kings*](https://www.research.ed.ac.uk/files/137623854/H_meenAnttila2018_Khwadaynamag_The_Middle_Persian_Book_of_Kings.pdf).

### 2.4 Islamic historical corpus

- **Dīnavarī, *al-Akhbār al-ṭiwāl*, late ninth century:** Arabic universal history drawing on Iranian material. Bahram is a warrior-king in a dynastic succession. **[SASANIAN TRADITION]**
- **Ṭabarī, *Taʾrīkh al-rusul wa’l-mulūk*, completed early tenth century:** the principal Arabic narrative of the reign, preserving the upbringing with the Lakhmids, contested accession, wars, government, musicians, and variant reports. Its Sasanian material is several centuries later than the reign. **[SASANIAN TRADITION]**
- **Balʿamī’s Persian adaptation of Ṭabarī, later tenth century:** a rewriting and expansion, not a word-for-word translation. **[REGIONAL/CANONICAL PROSE]**
- **Ḥamza al-Eṣfahānī, *Taʾrīkh sinī mulūk al-arż wa’l-anbiyāʾ*, ca. 961:** preserves regnal and Iranian narrative traditions, including material later connected with Bahram’s importation of Indian musicians. **[SASANIAN TRADITION]**
- **Ṯaʿālebī, *Ḡorar aḵbār molūk al-Fors*, ca. 1000:** Arabic literary history close in narrative world to Ferdowsi. It calls the harpist Azādvār and says the hunting incident was painted at Khawarnaq. **[CANONICAL PARALLEL]**
- **Ebn al-Balḵī, *Fārs-nāma*, early twelfth century:** condensed Persian dynastic account. **[LATER HISTORIOGRAPHY]**
- **Neẓām al-Molk, *Siyāsat-nāma*, ca. 1086–1092:** uses Bahram and the corrupt minister Rāst-Rawšan/Rāst-Raviš as a mirror-for-princes exemplum. **[DIDACTIC ACCRETION]**
- **Mojmal al-tawāriḵ wa’l-qeṣaṣ, 1126:** preserves death and localizing variants. **[VARIANT]**
- Later compilers, including Nowayrī and Ḥamd-Allāh Mostawfī, repeat or reorganize the material. **[LATE ACCRETION]**

### 2.5 Ferdowsi’s *Shāhnāma*

Ferdowsi completed the New Persian *Shāhnāma* around **1010 CE**. Bahram’s reign is one of its largest Sasanian royal narratives. The oldest surviving complete textual witnesses are centuries later; the Florence manuscript of 1217 is the oldest substantial dated witness. Djalal Khaleghi-Motlagh’s eight-volume critical edition, first published 1987–2008, uses the earliest manuscripts and is the preferred Persian text. [Persian Heritage Foundation edition record](https://persianheritagefoundation.org/what-we-do/publications/persian-text-series/).

The Warners’ English *Shahnama*, volume VII, remains complete but reflects an older text; Dick Davis’s *Shahnameh* is more readable but abridged and therefore insufficient for cataloguing every Bahram episode. The complete older translation can be consulted in [Warner and Warner, volume VII](https://persian.packhum.org/text/006801287).

Ferdowsi gives:

- Yazdegerd’s long childlessness and Bahram’s auspicious birth;
- his education at the Arab court;
- Azadeh and the gazelle;
- recovery of the crown between lions;
- justice, feasting, and tax remission;
- disguised encounters with poor and rich subjects;
- fabulous hunts and monster-killing;
- the eastern campaign against the khāqān;
- travel to India and marriage to Šangol’s daughter;
- the bringing of *lūrī* musicians from India;
- succession arrangements and natural death.

This is not a fifth-century biography. It is the canonical epic presentation of ideal and defective kingship. **[CANONICAL]**

### 2.6 Neẓāmi’s *Haft Peykar*

Neẓāmi of Ganja completed *Haft Peykar*—“Seven Portraits,” figuratively “Seven Beauties”—probably in **1197**. It survives as part of his *Ḵamsa*. Ritter and Rypka’s 1934 edition, based on fifteen manuscripts divided into two families, remains a major critical text. [Iranica, “Haft Peykar”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/haft-peykar/).

Neẓāmi acknowledges Ferdowsi but normally abbreviates material Ferdowsi had treated and constructs a new moral and astrological architecture:

- Bahram sees portraits of seven princesses in a sealed chamber at Khawarnaq;
- he marries all seven;
- seven domes correspond to seven climes, planets, weekdays, and colors;
- each wife tells an embedded story;
- sensual and astrological immersion permits a wicked minister to ruin the kingdom;
- seven victims expose the oppression;
- Bahram restores justice and abandons the planetary pleasure-system;
- he disappears while pursuing an onager into a cave.

This is a deliberate literary systematization, not a record of seven historical queens. A complete older translation is available in [C. E. Wilson, *The Haft Paikar*](https://persian.packhum.org/text/017601040/all).

### 2.7 Later romances

- **Amir Ḵosrow Dehlavī, *Hašt Behešt*, 1302:** an Indian Persian response to Neẓāmi, reorganizing the frame around eight paradises and adding or altering tales. Bahram again disappears while hunting. **[LITERARY ACCRETION]**
- Ottoman Turkish, Chaghatay, Kurdish, Georgian, and Indo-Persian retellings transmitted elements of the cycle, though no single recension represents a stable popular “original.”
- **ʿAwfī, *Lobāb al-albāb*, early thirteenth century**, and Dawlatshah’s fifteenth-century *Taḏkerat al-šoʿarāʾ* make Bahram the first Persian poet. The Persian verses ascribed to him are linguistically much later than the fifth century. **[PSEUDEPIGRAPHIC ACCRETION]** [Iranica, women-poets project](https://poets.iranicaonline.org/article/tazkirahs-of-premodern-women-poets-2/).

### 2.8 Corpora named in the brief but not containing this hero

The **Avesta**, including *Yasht* 14 to Vərəθraγna and *Yasht* 19 with older Iranian heroes such as Thraētaona/Fereydun and Kərəsāspa/Garshasp, contains no Bahram Gur. The **Bundahišn** and **Dēnkard** preserve cosmology, religious learning, and heroic genealogical material, but no developed Bahram Gur cycle. Asadi Tusi’s eleventh-century **Garšāsp-nāma**, the **Borzu-nāma**, and other secondary heroic epics concern different heroes. Their absence is important: Bahram Gur belongs to late Sasanian dynastic memory and Islamic Persian romance, not the ancient Avestan heroic age.

## 3. The story, episode by episode

### 3.1 Parentage and birth

**Historical minimum [EARLY]:** Bahram was a son of Yazdegerd I and succeeded after the crisis following Yazdegerd’s death in 420. His coins establish the reign but not his mother.

**Maternal variants:**

- Later Persian tradition calls his mother **Šōšanduxt**, supposedly daughter of the Jewish exilarch. This genealogy is preserved in late sources and has been treated as possible evidence for Jewish connections at court, but it is not contemporaneously verified. **[SASANIAN TRADITION/CONTESTED]**
- Ferdowsi and Neẓāmi concentrate instead on Yazdegerd’s childlessness, prayer, astrologers, and the auspicious son. **[CANONICAL]**
- Neẓāmi explicitly has Yazdegerd pray to God/Ahura Mazda after twenty years without an heir. **[SYSTEMATIZATION]**

### 3.2 Arab upbringing at Ḥīra—or Yemen

Ṭabarī and related sources send the prince to the Lakhmid Arab court at **al-Ḥīra**, ruled in the narrative by Noʿmān and/or his son Monḏer. A political fosterage, exile following disagreement with his father, and literary idealization of desert upbringing have all been proposed. No contemporary document confirms the childhood. **[SASANIAN TRADITION; HISTORICITY POSSIBLE, UNPROVEN]**

Ferdowsi makes the Arab court a school for letters, warfare, hunting, and rule. Neẓāmi relocates it, as medieval Persian literature often does, to **Yemen** and foregrounds the fabulous palace **Khawarnaq**. The Yemen setting is geographical romance, not evidence against the historical location of Ḥīra. **[CANONICAL VARIANT]**

### 3.3 Khawarnaq

At the Arab court, Bahram inhabits the palace Khawarnaq, constructed for Noʿmān by the architect **Senemmār**. The broader legend says the patron had Senemmār thrown from the completed palace so he could never reproduce it. Bahram is not always the agent in this killing. Neẓāmi uses Khawarnaq as the place where Bahram discovers the portraits of the seven women he is destined to marry. **[ACCRETION/SYSTEMATIZATION]**

### 3.4 Azadeh and the gazelles

In Ferdowsi and Ṯaʿālebī, Bahram takes the Roman slave-musician **Āzāda/Āzādvār** hunting on his camel. She challenges him to:

- turn a male gazelle into a female by shooting off its horns;
- turn a female into a male by planting two arrows like horns;
- make another lift its hoof to its ear, then pin hoof and ear together.

Bahram performs the shots. Instead of praising him, Azadeh pities the animals and calls the feat demonic. Enraged, he throws her from the camel and has the animal trample her to death. The episode plainly represents royal violence against an enslaved woman; later aesthetic treatments frequently omit or soften the killing. **[CANONICAL; VIOLENCE]** [Iranica, “Āzāda”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/azada-name/).

Ṯaʿālebī adds that Monḏer ordered the episode painted at Khawarnaq. **[CANONICAL PARALLEL]**

In Neẓāmi, the woman is renamed **Fetna**, “Temptation.” Bahram orders her killed, but the officer entrusted with the task hides her. She trains by carrying a growing calf upstairs every day; when it is a full-grown ox she demonstrates that repeated practice, not magic, explains astonishing skill. Bahram recognizes the moral, spares her, and marries or restores her. **[TWELFTH-CENTURY MORAL REVISION]** Neẓāmi deliberately replaces Ferdowsi’s irreversible murder with education in clemency.

In Amir Ḵosrow the musician becomes **Delārām**, and the story is further reorganized. **[INDO-PERSIAN ACCRETION]**

### 3.5 The succession and crown between two lions

After Yazdegerd’s death, the magnates kill or exclude his sons and select a Sasanian prince named **Ḵosrow**. Bahram returns with Arab support. In the Persian romance he proposes that the crown and royal robe be placed between two lions: whoever can take them has divine royal fortune. Khosrow withdraws; Bahram kills the lions and takes the crown. **[SASANIAN TRADITION/CANONICAL]**

No contemporary source records this ordeal. It visualizes **xwarrah**, divinely sanctioned royal fortune, and turns a contested accession supported by Lakhmid force into a providential test. It may preserve the memory of real opposition by nobles while fictionalizing its resolution. **[RECONSTRUCTION]**

### 3.6 Kingship, pleasure, and justice

The later historical and epic sources praise Bahram for remitting taxes and debts during celebrations, promoting musicians, hunting, drinking, and making royal joy available to his subjects. They also repeatedly warn that pleasure without supervision produces oppression. **[SASANIAN TRADITION/CANONICAL]**

Ferdowsi’s disguised wanderings expose contrasting social worlds:

- **Lombak**, a hospitable water-carrier, entertains the unknown king generously despite poverty; Bahram rewards him.
- **Barāhām/Brahām**, a wealthy Jewish host in the story, treats the stranger miserably and is punished. The episode uses an ethically charged and potentially anti-Jewish contrast between generous poor man and miserly rich Jew; it should not be treated as documentary evidence about Jewish life.
- Bahram visits peasants, shepherds, millers, shoemakers, jewelers, and landed nobles, testing hospitality and the effect of government.
- **Barzīn’s daughters Māhāfarīd, Farānak, and Šanbalīd** sing, play harp, and dance; Bahram marries all three. **[CANONICAL]** [Iranica, “Barzīn”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/barzin-from-pahlavi-burzen-the-name-of-several-figures-in-the-sah-nama/).
- **Ārزو/Ārzū**, daughter of the jeweler Māhyār, entertains him with music and becomes another love or wife. **[CANONICAL]**

These are king-in-disguise exempla, not recoverable court records.

### 3.7 Fabulous hunts and monsters

Ferdowsi gives Bahram escalating demonstrations of royal archery:

- the gazelle transformations;
- killing lions threatening flocks;
- killing an onager and lion with a single shot in some versions;
- killing a dragon that has devoured a youth;
- killing a wolf, rhinoceros/unicorn, and other extraordinary beasts in variant or interpolated passages.

Some episodes are textually unstable. Princeton’s scene index explicitly identifies the “monkey-lion” episode as an interpolation in parts of the manuscript tradition. **[CANONICAL WITH INTERPOLATIONS]** [Princeton *Shāhnāma* scene index](https://www.princeton.edu/~shahnama/scenes.html).

The hunt simultaneously encodes aristocratic pastime, military readiness, mastery of nature, and royal charisma. Modern comparison associates Bahram’s prowess with Mithra and Vərəθraγna, but the poems do not say that either god appears and helps him. **[MODERN ICONOLOGICAL RECONSTRUCTION]**

### 3.8 Eastern campaign and the khāqān

Ṭabarī and Persian sources report an eastern victory over “Turks,” a khāqān, or Hephthalites/Kidarites. Ferdowsi elaborates the campaign: Bahram feigns weakness or departs secretly, surprises the khāqān, defeats and sometimes captures him, establishes a frontier marker, installs a ruler, and appoints his brother **Narsī** in Khorasan. **[HISTORICAL CORE WITH EPIC ELABORATION]**

Modern scholarship generally locates the historical conflict among the Kidarite/Hunnic powers active northeast of Iran rather than assuming Ferdowsi’s ethnic-geographic labels map directly onto fifth-century peoples. The precise campaign sequence is disputed because numismatic, Armenian, and later Persian evidence do not produce a single chronology.

### 3.9 Rome and Christians

Late-antique Christian sources present Bahram as continuing persecution begun after Christians destroyed a Zoroastrian fire sanctuary. War with Theodosius II followed in 421–422 and ended in a negotiated peace. **[EARLY]**

Persian epic reverses the emphasis: Bahram is a victorious or magnanimous universal sovereign receiving Roman embassies. **[CANONICAL POLITICAL MEMORY]** Neither perspective should be used alone. The Christian sources have polemical interests; the national epic magnifies Iranian kingship.

### 3.10 India, Šangol, and Sepinud

In Ferdowsi, Bahram travels to India, often incognito, enters the court of King **Šangol**, displays strength and monster-killing, and marries Šangol’s daughter **Sepinud/Sapinud**. Šangol may alternate between host, suspicious father-in-law, and opponent. Bahram escapes or returns with the princess. **[CANONICAL ROMANCE]**

Later genealogies sometimes make Sapinud the mother of Yazdegerd II, but contemporary evidence for the marriage is lacking. **[GENEALOGICAL ACCRETION]**

### 3.11 The Indian musicians

Ṭabarī, Ḥamza, Ferdowsi, and later authors say Bahram asked the Indian king for thousands of musicians—often **10,000 or 12,000 lūrīs**—so poorer Iranians could enjoy music. Bahram gave them animals and grain; they consumed the resources, failed as farmers, and wandered as entertainers. **[SASANIAN TRADITION/CANONICAL]**

The story has repeatedly been presented as proof that the Roma migrated from India under Bahram. That inference is untenable: medieval *lūrī/lūlī/zott* labels do not transparently denote the later European Roma, the account is written centuries after Bahram, and linguistic reconstruction of Romani migrations cannot be derived from it. It is evidence for a medieval Iranian origin-story about itinerant musicians, not a passenger list from the fifth century. **[DISCREDITED MODERN HISTORICAL USE]**

### 3.12 The seven beauties

Neẓāmi makes seven royal women embodiments of the world’s seven climes:

1. **Furak/Nurak**, Indian princess — Saturday, Saturn, black.
2. **Yaghmā Nāz**, daughter of the khāqān — Sunday or one of the eastern climes in variant ordering.
3. **Nāz-Parī**, princess associated with Khwarazm/Turkistan — green.
4. **Nasrīn-Nūš**, Slavonic princess — red or the corresponding planetary pavilion.
5. **Āzarbin/Āzaryūn**, Maghrebi princess — sandalwood.
6. **Homāy**, daughter of the Roman Caesar — blue.
7. **Dirostī**, Iranian princess of the Kayānian house — Friday, Venus, white.

Names, colors, regions, and sequence vary among manuscripts and translations; the stable structure is seven women, climes, days, planets, and colors. **[SYSTEMATIZATION]**

Each queen tells an autonomous tale. These include the black-clad city, the king who resists marriage, stories of fidelity and betrayal, the cruel or riddle-setting princess ancestral to the later Turandot complex, and the final white tale of restraint. Scholars have not traced all seven to demonstrably earlier written sources. The cycle is therefore Neẓāmi’s literary construction from heterogeneous tale material rather than an inherited Sasanian biography.

The poem’s moral movement is not simply ascent through occult wisdom. Bahram’s absorption in sensual and astrological order lets his minister oppress the realm. Seven injured subjects reveal the truth; Bahram punishes the minister, repairs injustice, and abandons or converts the pleasure-domes. Justice, not submission to the stars, completes his kingship. **[CANONICAL INTERPRETATION]**

### 3.13 Death and disappearance

The variants cannot be harmonized:

- **Ferdowsi, ca. 1010:** Bahram appoints his heir and dies naturally, in his sleep. **[CANONICAL VARIANT]**
- **Some Arabic and Persian chronicles:** he drowns, sinks in a marsh, or falls into a deep pit while hunting. **[SASANIAN-DERIVED/LOCAL VARIANTS]**
- **Neẓāmi, 1197:** pursuing an onager, he enters a cave and vanishes. His mother has the ground excavated for forty days, but no body is found. **[CANONICAL ROMANCE]** [Wilson translation, conclusion](https://persian.packhum.org/text/017601040/5).
- **Amir Ḵosrow, 1302:** disappearance during the hunt is retained. **[INDO-PERSIAN ACCRETION]**
- Local traditions identify different pits, caves, marshes, and deserts as the site. **[FOLKLORE]**

The missing corpse allows the punning identity of hunter, hunted animal, and grave: Bahram seeks *gūr* and receives *gūr*. No archaeologically authenticated burial has been found.

### 4.2 Every documented interaction with another named divine being or hero

The word “hero” is applied here broadly to named royal and legendary actors. Ordinary named courtiers and relatives are included because the dossier requires cross-referencing; no divine encounter is invented.

- **Ahura Mazda/Ohrmazd** — Yazdegerd prays for a child in Neẓāmi; Bahram is the resulting child, but Bahram does not converse with the deity. *Haft Peykar*, 1197. **[LEARNED RELIGIOUS FRAME]**

Apparent associations in modern illustrations or summaries usually confuse homonymous Bahrams or join unrelated sections of the Iranian heroic corpus.

## 5. Cult, tomb, and ritual

### Cult

No securely identified temple, *heroon*, sacrificial calendar, priesthood, oracle, annual festival, or votive inscription is dedicated to **Bahram Gur as a dead hero**. He should not be placed in the category of Achilles, Orestes, or a saint with an archaeologically demonstrable tomb cult.

The name **Bahrām** occurs extensively in Zoroastrian religious usage because it is the Middle/New Persian name of the victory divinity Vərəθraγna. **Ātaš Bahrām**, the highest grade of Zoroastrian sacred fire, is dedicated to or named for Victory; it is not a cult of King Bahram V. Temples called “Bahrām” therefore cannot be assigned to Bahram Gur without specific evidence. **[CRITICAL DISTINCTION]**

Likewise, the “Bahram fire temple” or “Bahram Gur fire temple” names attached in tourism and local nomenclature to ruins near Ray and elsewhere do not by themselves prove royal foundation or worship of the king. **[LATE TOPONYMIC ATTRIBUTION]**

### Tomb and body

No excavated burial has been authenticated as his. The textual record instead emphasizes contradictory endings and, increasingly, absence of a body:

- natural death in Ferdowsi;
- marsh, pit, or drowning in chronicles;
- cave disappearance in Neẓāmi;
- local “Bahram’s grave,” “Bahram’s pit,” and “Bahram’s cave” traditions.

Neẓāmi’s statement that the excavated ground was called Bahram’s sepulchre is an etiological literary passage, not an archaeological report. **[CANONICAL ETIOLOGY]**

The modern **Bahrām-e Gūr Protected Area** in Fars preserves the royal-hunter association and protects Persian onagers, but it is modern conservation geography, not evidence for an ancient shrine.

### Ritual and commemorative practice

Bahram’s durable quasi-ritual presence lies in:

- recitation of the *Shāhnāma*;
- manuscript patronage by courts;
- paintings used as royal and ethical exempla;
- proverbial allusions to the onager/grave pun;
- the name Bahrām in personal naming.

These are literary and commemorative practices, not a cult of the dead king.

## 6. Historical evolution

### 420–438: historical king and royal image

Coins and external histories attest a Sasanian monarch confronting succession instability, Christian persecution and Roman war, Armenia’s reorganization, and an eastern threat. Royal hunting imagery was already central to Sasanian sovereignty. Whether the gazelle trick was already attached specifically to him is likely but not epigraphically certain.

### Fifth–seventh centuries: court memory

The silver hunting plates indicate circulation of a recognizable gazelle-and-musician scene before the Islamic period. Court narrative probably remembered Bahram as mounted archer, patron of pleasure, and victorious king. The exact contents of any Sasanian written biography are lost.

### Eighth–tenth centuries: translation and historiography

Middle Persian royal histories were translated and recombined in Arabic. Dīnavarī, Ṭabarī, Ḥamza, Balʿamī, and Ṯaʿālebī preserve increasingly elaborate traditions: Lakhmid upbringing, disputed succession, musicians, hunts, justice, and eastern victories. The label “from the *Khwadāy-nāmag*” cannot be applied mechanically to every episode.

### ca. 1010: Ferdowsi’s canonical epic king

Ferdowsi joins chronicle, romance, hunt tale, social exemplum, and mirror-for-princes material. Bahram becomes a mobile king who tests his realm personally, rewards generosity, punishes failure, defeats beasts and foreign kings, loves many women, and nevertheless dies as a mortal.

### Eleventh–twelfth centuries: moralization

Neẓām al-Molk uses him to expose ministerial corruption. Neẓāmi then transforms the cycle into an erotic, cosmological, and anti-fatalist education in kingship. Fetna corrects the cruelty of the Azadeh story; the seven pavilions teach that aesthetic and astrological perfection without justice is ruinous.

### Thirteenth–fifteenth centuries: Persianate expansion

Amir Ḵosrow’s *Hašt Behešt* carries the cycle into Indo-Persian literature. Biographical dictionaries invent Bahram as the first New Persian poet. Illustrated ceramics, metalwork, and manuscripts spread scenes far beyond any single text.

### Sixteenth–eighteenth centuries: imperial manuscript culture

Safavid, Mughal, Ottoman, and Deccan ateliers repeatedly paint the hunt, lion ordeal, and seven pavilions. Bahram functions as an image of princely refinement and warning: hunting and love authenticate sovereignty only when governed by justice.

### Nineteenth century: European encounter

European editions and translations of Ferdowsi, Neẓāmi, and the *Rubāʿiyāt* introduce Bahram as “the great Hunter.” Edward FitzGerald’s versions of Khayyām turn him into a familiar emblem of vanished magnificence.

### Twentieth–twenty-first centuries

Bahram persists in Iranian and Azerbaijani theater, ballet, painting, fiction, popular editions, museum displays, and nationalist discussions of pre-Islamic kingship. His best-known modern image—the seven-domed lover-king—derives chiefly from Neẓāmi, not the fifth century.

## 7. Modern reception and reinvention

### Poetry and the Khayyām problem

A famous Persian quatrain reads:

> آن قصر که جمشید در او جام گرفت  
> آهو بچه کرد و روبه آرام گرفت  
> بهرام که گور می‌گرفتی همه عمر  
> دیدی که چگونه گور بهرام گرفت

“See how Bahram, who caught onagers all his life, was taken by the grave.” The poem uses *gūr* as onager and grave.

Its attribution to **ʿOmar Khayyām** is insecure. No authorial manuscript of Khayyām’s quatrains exists, and modern Persian scholarship also attributes the poem to the thirteenth-century writer Sadīd-al-Dīn Aʿvar. It must be cited as **attributed to Khayyām**, not securely his. [Ganjoor attribution notice](https://ganjoor.net/khayyam/robaee/sh7).

FitzGerald’s *Rubáiyát* recasts the motif:

> “And Bahrám, that great Hunter—the Wild Ass  
> Stamps o’er his Head, but cannot break his Sleep.”

This is a nineteenth-century English poetic reinvention, not a translation of a securely authenticated single Khayyām text. [FitzGerald/Heron-Allen text](https://persian.packhum.org/text/008801110).

### Manuscript painting

The most recurrent scenes are:

- Bahram and Azadeh/Fetna hunting;
- Bahram taking the crown between the lions;
- Bahram killing a dragon or lions;
- Bahram entertained incognito;
- Bahram in one of the seven colored pavilions.

A preliminary index records at least thirty-two Bahram scenes in illustrated *Shāhnāma* manuscripts. **[DOCUMENTED RECEPTION]**

The **Great Mongol Shāhnāma**, made probably at Tabriz around 1330, gives unusual prominence to Sasanian kings as models for Ilkhanid sovereignty. Its Bahram paintings belong to a program by which Mongol rulers inserted themselves into Iranian kingship. The manuscript was dismantled by the dealer Georges Demotte in the early twentieth century, damaging the original narrative sequence. [National Museum of Asian Art](https://asia.si.edu/whats-on/exhibitions/mongol-shahnama/).

The **Shah Tahmasp Shāhnāma**, produced in the early sixteenth-century Safavid court, likewise made ancient kingship serve contemporary imperial culture. Not every detached Bahram painting marketed under its name actually belongs to that manuscript; folio provenance must be checked individually. [Aga Khan Museum overview](https://remastered.agakhanmuseum.org/themes/the-courageous-spirit/objects/shah-tahmasp-shahnameh/index.html).

Mina’i bowls of the late twelfth and early thirteenth centuries show Bahram and Azadeh on camelback. One Metropolitan Museum bowl visualizes the moment before her fatal criticism. [Metropolitan Museum bowl, 12th–13th century](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/451388). Such ceramics demonstrate popular recognition of the scene but cannot independently prove its fifth-century details.

### European visual transmission

A forthcoming study identifies plate 36 in Johannes Stradanus’s *Venationes* of about 1596 as a European representation of Bahram’s hunting feat, possibly transmitted through Persian manuscripts collected by the Vecchietti brothers. If accepted, it documents a much earlier European reception than the nineteenth-century translations. **[MODERN SCHOLARLY IDENTIFICATION]**

### Ballet and stage

Azerbaijani composer **Gara Garayev** composed the ballet *Seven Beauties* in 1947–48 for Neẓāmi’s 800th-anniversary celebrations; it premiered in Baku on **7 November 1952**. It translates Neẓāmi’s erotic-political allegory into Soviet Azerbaijani national culture. Later productions revise choreography and plot, so “the ballet” is not a fixed transcription of *Haft Peykar*. **[MODERN REINVENTION]**

### Turandot

The cruel or riddle-setting princess in Neẓāmi’s fourth embedded tale is an early relative of the narrative that, after later Persian retelling and Pétis de la Croix’s European adaptation, produced Carlo Gozzi’s *Turandot* and ultimately Puccini’s opera. Bahram himself disappears from these descendants. Calling Puccini’s *Turandot* a “Bahram Gur opera” would therefore be misleading: the transmission concerns an embedded tale, not the hero.

### Nationalism and political use

The *Shāhnāma* became a major resource for modern Iranian national identity, particularly under the Pahlavi monarchy, which emphasized pre-Islamic imperial continuity. Bahram participates in that general gallery of Sasanian kings, but evidence for a distinctive political “cult of Bahram Gur” is lacking. Modern nationalist presentations often:

- treat epic victories as literal military history;
- suppress Christian persecution;
- present the seven-clime ruler as evidence of ancient Iranian universalism;
- romanticize the killing of Azadeh as courtly hunting;
- conflate Vərəθraγna, the planet Mars, and the historical king.

These are selective receptions, not the consensus of the sources. Modern scholarship on *Shāhnāma* nationalism warns against projecting nineteenth- and twentieth-century nationhood directly into a medieval dynastic epic. [Iranica, “Šāh-nāma”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/sah-nama/).

No well-documented extremist organization appears to have made Bahram Gur personally a primary emblem. Claims of such appropriation require case-specific evidence; the broader use of pre-Islamic Iranian kings by monarchist, secular-nationalist, or ethnonationalist discourse should not automatically be assigned to him.

### Commercial and popular uses

His name is now attached to restaurants, publications, tourism sites, wildlife areas, and decorative goods. Images frequently combine a generic Sasanian hunter, Neẓāmi’s seven domes, and later miniature costume. These composites are **modern branding**, not reconstructions of fifth-century appearance.

## 8. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Was he historical?

Yes: Wahrām V is securely attested by coins and external historical sources. The disputed issue is not his existence but how much of Bahram Gur’s biography can be projected back into his reign. The safest division is:

- ruler, reign, dynastic succession, wars, and coinage: historical;
- precise childhood narrative and lion ordeal: possible memories embedded in later legend;
- Azadeh scene: perhaps old, given Sasanian imagery, but not independently biographical;
- seven princesses and planetary domes: Neẓāmi’s twelfth-century literary architecture;
- cave disappearance and known grave-sites: legend and folklore.

### Dating the romance

The fifth-century hunting plate is the crucial discovery. If its accepted identification is correct, a recognizable version of the gazelle feat existed around Bahram’s lifetime. It does not prove Ferdowsi’s entire story, Azadeh’s name, or her death. The later Hermitage plate confirms continued Sasanian circulation.

Richard Ettinghausen’s “Bahram Gur’s Hunting Feats or the Problem of Identification” stressed the danger of identifying every royal hunter as Bahram merely because later literature made him the archetypal hunter. Crown form, date, medium, and exact narrative action must all be considered. **[ONGOING ICONOGRAPHIC DEBATE]**

### The *Khwadāy-nāmag* problem

Older scholarship often treated later authors as witnesses to a single Sasanian book. Recent work emphasizes multiple editions, translations, oral tales, court records, and Islamic-period rewriting. Agreement between Ṭabarī and Ferdowsi may indicate shared Iranian tradition, but not verbatim dependence on a recoverable archetype.

### Historicity of Arab fosterage

The Lakhmid court was a real Sasanian client power, and Arab military assistance in a succession struggle is plausible. Yet the detailed curriculum, palace adventures, and dialogue are literary. Neẓāmi’s Yemen relocation demonstrates how geography moved under romance conventions.

### The lions and *xwarrah*

The lion ordeal is often read as dramatization of divine royal fortune. This explains its narrative function but does not prove an actual accession ritual. No contemporary inscription records it.

### Bahram, Vərəθraγna, and Mithra

The name unquestionably derives from Vərəθraγna. Hunting and victory imagery can share the symbolism of divine power. But a strong claim that Bahram Gur is a euhemerized god, incarnation of Vərəθraγna, or Mithraic savior exceeds the texts. The poems present a mortal king.

### The seven domes: astrology or ethics?

Earlier interpretation sometimes treated *Haft Peykar* chiefly as an astrological ascent. The poem unquestionably deploys planets, colors, weekdays, climes, and the number seven. François de Blois argues, however, that the domes nearly destroy Bahram: the poem ultimately subordinates the stars to justice and moral choice. [Iranica, “Haft Peykar”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/haft-peykar/).

### Roma-origin theory

The musicians-from-India story has been cited as the first migration of the Roma. Its chronology, terminology, and narrative character cannot bear that conclusion. Linguistics supports an Indian origin for Romani, but not identification of Ferdowsi’s entire group with proto-Roma or a migration ordered by Bahram. **[DISCREDITED AS A LITERAL HISTORICAL PROOF]**

### First Persian poet

ʿAwfī and Dawlatshah’s attribution makes Bahram the first New Persian poet. The language of the quoted verse is anachronistic; it is a medieval attempt to give Persian poetry a royal pre-Islamic founder. **[PSEUDEPIGRAPHIC]**

### Death and tomb

The proliferation of mutually exclusive places and mechanisms—sleep, drowning, marsh, well, pit, cave—shows legendary localization. None has archaeological confirmation. The absence of a body becomes productive literary symbolism rather than evidence that a particular local site is genuine.

### Editions and translation issues

- Khaleghi-Motlagh is preferable to the Moscow edition for Persian textual work.
- Warner and Warner is full but stylistically archaic.
- Davis is literary and accessible but abridged.
- Ritter–Rypka remains fundamental for *Haft Peykar*.
- Wilson’s English translation is complete but linguistically dated.
- Museum labels often state “Bahram Gur” more confidently than iconographic evidence permits.
- Internet summaries routinely combine Ferdowsi, Neẓāmi, Ṭabarī, and modern historical reconstruction without marking the centuries between them.

### Texts that should not be recruited

The Avesta, *Bundahišn*, *Dēnkard*, *Garšāsp-nāma*, and *Borzu-nāma* provide comparative Iranian religious or heroic background but do not document Bahram Gur’s deeds. Omidsalar and Davidson’s work on Persian epic can illuminate oral transmission and heroic poetics, but Bahram’s historical-romance cycle should not be forced into the same prehistoric heroic stratum as Rostam or Garshasp.

## 10. Conclusions on the state of the evidence

The historical Wahrām V is secure. The literary Bahram Gur is an unusually clear example of legend growing around a documented ruler without entirely replacing him.

The evidence supports four main stages:

1. **Fifth-century king and hunter-image:** coins, wars, Armenian policy, and perhaps an already recognizable gazelle-shooting tale.
2. **Sasanian and early Islamic royal memory:** Arab fosterage, contested succession, eastern victory, musicians, liberality, and exemplary rule.
3. **Classical Persian canon:** Ferdowsi’s hunter-king and Neẓāmi’s seven-pavilion lover who must learn justice.
4. **Persianate and modern afterlife:** manuscript painting, ceramics, Indo-Persian imitation, Khayyām/FitzGerald’s mortality emblem, ballet, nationalism, museums, and tourism.

The dossier yields several firm negative findings:

- no Bahram Gur in the Avesta or ancient heroic yashts;
- no securely attested interaction with a god;
- no authenticated hero cult, shrine, relic, or tomb;
- no historical evidence for seven planetary wives;
- no warrant for treating the lūrī-musician story as a literal record of Roma migration;
- no basis for calling every unnamed Sasanian royal hunter Bahram;
- no secure attribution of the famous *gūr/grave* quatrain to ʿOmar Khayyām.

What survived was not a biography but a flexible record of Persian kingship: victory made visible through archery, pleasure limited by justice, royal fortune proved against lions, and worldly magnificence finally caught by the grave.
