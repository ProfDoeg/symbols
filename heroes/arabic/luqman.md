# Luqman: Research Dossier

> **Traditional class:** A mortal, divinely endowed sage and culture hero—not unambiguously a prophet; in later Arabic, Persian, and Turkish tradition also a fabulist, exemplary enslaved sage, physician, herbalist, and sometimes saint-like holy man. A separate or partly conflated pre-Islamic figure, Luqmān ibn ʿĀd, is a legendary king and archetypal long-lived man.
>
> **Earliest secure attestation:** The name and legend of Luqmān/Lubad occur in poetry attributed to the late-sixth-century poet al-Nābigha al-Dhubyānī, transmitted in later written collections; this is the earliest literary stratum, but the poems’ pre-Islamic wording must be distinguished from the dates of their Islamic-period recension. The earliest independently fixed text is Qurʾān 31:12–19, proclaimed in the early seventh century CE, where Luqmān is a wise monotheist counseling his unnamed son.

## Evidentiary key

- **[E1: securely attested early material]** Pre-Islamic poetic material with an established early attribution, or the seventh-century Qurʾānic text.
- **[E2: early Islamic reception]** Traditions attributed to seventh–eighth-century authorities but preserved in later compilations.
- **[E3: later canonical elaboration]** Classical tafsīr, chronicles, adab, and *qiṣaṣ al-anbiyāʾ*.
- **[E4: regional variant]** A geographically restricted literary or oral form.
- **[E5: learned systematization or syncretism]** Deliberate genealogical, philosophical, biblical, or Aesopic equation.
- **[E6: medieval or early-modern accretion]** Material first documented in medieval manuscripts or early printing.
- **[E7: folklore or living belief]** Oral legend, healing lore, shrine attribution, or popular practice not independently historical.
- **[E8: modern scholarly reconstruction]** A historical-critical hypothesis, not an ancient identification.
- **[E9: modern invention or unsupported attribution]** A recent claim, quotation, image, or synthesis lacking an identifiable premodern source.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Orientation

Luqmān is not the protagonist of a continuous ancient biography. He is a name around which several initially separable bodies of tradition accumulated:

1. **Luqmān the pre-Islamic sage or long-lived man**, especially Luqmān ibn ʿĀd, whose life is measured by seven vultures and whose last bird is Lubad.
2. **The Qurʾānic Luqmān**, a recipient of divine *ḥikma*, “wisdom,” who counsels his son in Qurʾān 31:12–19.
3. **The exegetical Luqmān**, variously a free Arab, Nubian, Ethiopian, Egyptian, Israelite, carpenter, tailor, shepherd, judge, enslaved man, prophet, or non-prophetic saintly sage.
4. **The Aesopic Luqmān**, assigned animal fables and physical and biographical traits derived partly from Aesop traditions.
5. **The Persian and Turkish Luqmān/Lokman**, an exemplary ascetic and eventually a master physician and herbalist, sometimes credited with discovering or nearly discovering a cure for death.

The documentary record does not permit these figures to be reduced confidently to one historical person. The history of Luqmān is instead the history of their convergence.

### Names and scripts

- Arabic: **لُقْمَان**, *Luqmān*.
- With epithet: **لُقْمَان الْحَكِيم**, *Luqmān al-Ḥakīm*, “Luqmān the Wise.” **[E3]**
- Pre-Islamic/antiquarian patronymic: **لُقْمَان بْن عَاد**, *Luqmān ibn ʿĀd*. “ʿĀd” can designate the vanished Arabian people and, in later genealogical narration, an ancestor. **[E1–E3]**
- Persian: **لقمان حکیم**, *Loqmān-e Ḥakīm*.
- Ottoman and modern Turkish: **Lokman Hekim**.
- Early-modern European spellings: *Locman*, *Loqman*, *Lokman*.
- Last vulture: **لُبَد**, *Lubad* or *Lubadh*, conventionally understood as “the abiding/staying one.” **[E1–E3]**

The epithet *al-Ḥakīm* is semantically productive. Arabic *ḥakīm* can mean wise man, philosopher, or physician; Persian and Turkish reception exploited the medical sense, helping transform the scriptural sage into **Lokman Hekim**, archetypal doctor. This is semantic reception, not evidence that the Qurʾānic figure practiced medicine. The Turkish Islamic Encyclopedia traces the extensive use of his name in medical and herbal literature. **[E4–E7]** ([TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi](https://islamansiklopedisi.org.tr/lokman))

### Etymology

Arabic lexicographical interpretation connects *Luqmān* with the root **l-q-m**, “to swallow, eat in morsels,” and *luqma*, “morsel.” That is a traditional Arabic etymologization, not a demonstrated historical derivation. Some grammarians treated the name as foreign, pointing to its diptotic behavior; others accepted it as an Arabic proper name. **[E3, E5]**

Proposed equations with **Alcmaeon**, **Balaam**, **Aḥiqar**, or an otherwise lost foreign name are comparative hypotheses, not settled etymologies:

- **Aḥiqar:** Luqmān’s father-to-son instruction, proverbial authority, and some individual maxims resemble the Aḥiqar tradition. Modern scholarship generally explains this as participation in, and sometimes borrowing from, a common Near Eastern wisdom repertoire—not proof that *Luqmān* is a linguistic form of *Aḥiqar*. **[E8]**
- **Balaam/Bela son of Beor:** Islamic genealogies making Luqmān the son of Bāʿūrāʾ/Beor encouraged learned comparison or confusion with Balaam son of Beor and the Edomite king Bela. This is medieval genealogical systematization. **[E5]**
- **Alcmaeon of Croton:** Mahmoud Muftić and, more recently, Juan Cole have entertained an equation with the Greek philosopher-physician Alcmaeon. Phonetic resemblance and thematic parallels are insufficient to establish identity; recent scholarship continues to discuss rather than accept it as a result. **[E8]**
- **Aesop:** The two names are not cognate. The equation is narrative and literary: traits and fables belonging to Aesop were transferred to Luqmān. **[E5–E6]**

A recent synthesis appropriately treats Luqmān, Aḥiqar, Solomon, and Aesop as intersecting representations rather than a single recoverable person. ([Pietruschka, “Doomed Power and Eternal Wisdom”](https://www.mdpi.com/2077-1444/16/10/1301))

### Traditional classification

The Qurʾān calls Luqmān neither prophet nor king. It states that God gave him *ḥikma*. Most classical exegetes therefore classified him as a righteous mortal sage; a minority, especially a report attributed to ʿIkrima, called him a prophet. **[E2–E3]** The majority position is not proof about a historical individual, but it is the dominant internal classification.

Luqmān ibn ʿĀd belongs to another class: legendary long-lived ancestor, king, and culture hero attached to the destroyed people of ʿĀd. Later compilers sometimes distinguished him explicitly from Luqmān the Wise; others allowed their qualities to merge.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Pre-Islamic poetic evidence

Poetry attributed to **al-Nābigha al-Dhubyānī** (active in the later sixth century) uses Lubad, the last of Luqmān’s vultures, as an image of time finally overcoming even proverbial longevity. This presupposes an audience familiar with the legend. **[E1, with transmission caveat]** A scholarly discussion identifies Lubad specifically as the last of the seven birds whose lives measured Luqmān’s. ([Brill, *The Cooing of the Dove and the Cawing of the Crow*](https://brill.com/display/book/9789004499287/BP000009.xml))

Other early Arabic verses invoke Luqmān alongside ʿĀd, Iram, and Dhū Jadan as an emblem of vanished antiquity, power, and fame. The verses are attributed to pre-Islamic poets but survive through Abbasid-era collectors. Their value is real but not identical to a contemporary inscription: they attest an early poetic tradition as transmitted, not a securely excavated sixth-century text. **[E1/E2]**

No securely identified pre-Islamic inscription, coin, statue, or archaeological monument naming Luqmān is presently known. That absence matters: the earliest record is literary.

### The Qurʾān

**Qurʾān 31:12–19**, early seventh century, is the first securely fixed extended portrayal. **[E1]** Its contents are limited and precise:

- God gave Luqmān wisdom and charged him to be grateful (31:12).
- Luqmān admonishes his unnamed son not to associate partners with God (31:13).
- Verses 14–15 interrupt or expand the address with divine instruction concerning parents, pregnancy, nursing, and the limits of obedience to polytheist parents.
- Luqmān tells his son that even a mustard seed hidden in a rock, heaven, or earth will be brought forth by God (31:16).
- He orders prayer, commanding right, forbidding wrong, and patient endurance (31:17).
- He condemns contemptuous behavior and arrogant walking (31:18).
- He recommends moderation in gait and voice; the most disagreeable voice is that of donkeys (31:19).

The text supplies no birthplace, ethnicity, occupation, wife, personal name for the son, date, death, tomb, medical practice, animal fables, or encounter with David. ([Qurʾānic Arabic Corpus, 31:12](https://corpus.quran.com/translation.jsp?chapter=31&verse=12))

Qurʾānic manuscript evidence independently anchors the sura’s textual history. A manuscript catalogued by Corpus Coranicum, palaeographically dated broadly to 700–900 CE and radiocarbon-tested, preserves Q 29:18–31:22, including most of the Luqmān passage. Later Abbasid fragments preserve the sura’s conclusion. **[E1 manuscript evidence]** ([Corpus Coranicum manuscript 522](https://corpuscoranicum.de/en/manuscripts/522?sura=31&verse=24); [Cambridge MS Add.1145](https://corpuscoranicum.de/en/manuscripts/1576/page/1v?sura=33&verse=12))

### Early and classical exegesis

#### Muqātil ibn Sulaymān

The eighth-century commentary attributed to **Muqātil ibn Sulaymān** (d. 767) is among the earliest continuous tafsīrs. It participates in genealogical expansion: Luqmān becomes related to Job, reportedly the son of Job’s maternal uncle. **[E2]** This is already a move from the Qurʾān’s unnamed sage into biblical sacred history.

#### Al-Ṭabarī

**Al-Ṭabarī** (d. 923), *Jāmiʿ al-bayān*, on 31:12–19, preserves multiple earlier reports rather than a single biography. They include the positions that Luqmān was:

- a black or Nubian enslaved man;
- an Ethiopian carpenter;
- a tailor or shepherd in other transmitted versions;
- a righteous servant rather than a prophet;
- exceptionally thoughtful, reserved, and observant;
- granted wisdom because of moral qualities rather than genealogy.

Al-Ṭabarī’s work is a tenth-century compilation whose named reports often claim seventh- or eighth-century transmitters. Those dates must not be collapsed: the attribution is early Islamic; the extant synthesis is medieval. **[E2–E3]** ([Arabic text of al-Ṭabarī on 31:12](https://quran.com/ms/31%3A12/tafsirs/ar-tafsir-al-tabari))

#### Al-Thaʿlabī and al-Kisāʾī

**Al-Thaʿlabī** (d. 1035/36), *ʿArāʾis al-majālis fī qiṣaṣ al-anbiyāʾ*, gives Luqmān a place within stories of sacred history. It records competing lineages, including:

- nephew or relative of Job;
- Luqmān son of Bāʿūrāʾ, son of Nāḥūr/Nahor, son of Tāraḥ/Terah;
- an enslaved black man of Nubian or Ethiopian origin;
- a contemporary of David.

It also transmits the story that angels offered Luqmān rule or judgment; he declined the burden unless divinely commanded, and David consequently received the vicegerency while Luqmān received wisdom without that trial. **[E3]**

The Arabic *Qiṣaṣ al-anbiyāʾ* attributed to **al-Kisāʾī**, surviving in medieval manuscripts and difficult to date precisely in its received form, further embeds Luqmān in prophetic story cycles. It belongs to the accretive storytelling tradition, not to independent early evidence. **[E3]**

### Adab, proverbs, and wisdom anthologies

From the Abbasid period onward, sayings introduced as “Luqmān said to his son” proliferated in *adab*, ascetic, educational, and proverbial collections. Among the important repositories are:

- Ibn Qutayba’s *ʿUyūn al-akhbār*;
- Ḥunayn ibn Isḥāq’s or the tradition transmitted under his name as *Ādāb al-falāsifa*;
- al-Maydānī’s proverb collection;
- al-Ghazālī’s ethical writings;
- Ibn ʿArabī’s *Fuṣūṣ al-ḥikam*, which devotes a chapter to Luqmānic wisdom.

The formula became an attributional frame: attaching a maxim to Luqmān authenticated it as ancient wisdom. The mere appearance of “Luqmān said” therefore does not date the saying to pre-Islamic Arabia. **[E3–E5]**

### *Kitāb al-Muʿammarīn*

Abū Ḥātim al-Sijistānī (d. 862 or 869), *al-Muʿammarūn wa-l-waṣāyā*, is the major early compilation concerning Arab long-lived persons. It gives Luqmān ibn ʿĀd an immense lifespan—versions include 560 years and 3,500 years—and makes the lives of seven vultures its measure. **[E3]** The book is a ninth-century witness to the developed legend, not a record of biological longevity. ([National Library of Israel catalogue](https://www.nli.org.il/ar/books/NNL_ALEPH990018708970205171/NLI))

### The Arabic fables

The oldest securely dated substantial fable collection attributed to Luqmān is a **1299 Christian Arabic manuscript**, written in a Coptic milieu and containing forty-one fables. Most derive from Greek and Syriac Aesopic material, especially a Syriac recension associated with Syntipas/Synthipas; all but a small minority have Greek Aesopic counterparts. **[E6, syncretistic attribution]**

This chronology is decisive. Older Arabic literature knows Luqmān as a speaker of proverbs and wise maxims but does not securely document the received forty-one-fable book as his composition. Calling him “the Arabic Aesop” describes the medieval and early-modern reception, not an established ancient authorship. ([Al-Qantara study](https://al-qantara.revistas.csic.es/index.php/al-qantara/article/download/695/782?inline=1))

Thomas Erpenius printed *Locmani sapientis fabulae et selecta quaedam Arabum adagia* at Leiden in **1615**, with Arabic, Latin translation, and notes. This publication made the Aesopic Luqmān widely known in European orientalism and language instruction. **[E6]** ([1615 edition record](https://books.google.cz/books?hl=cs&id=NnUTAAAAQAAJ&lr=))

Joseph Derenbourg’s nineteenth-century edition and translation further canonized “Fables de Loqman.” These editions are witnesses to reception and manuscript scholarship, not proof of ancient Luqmānic authorship.

### Persian and Turkish corpus

- **Saʿdī, *Golestān* (1258):** Luqmān says that he learned courtesy from the discourteous—he avoided whatever he found offensive in them. **[E4/E6]**
- **Saʿdī, *Būstān*:** Luqmān appears as moral authority and exemplary sage. **[E4/E6]**
- **Rūmī, *Mathnawī* (thirteenth century):** Luqmān is an enslaved but inwardly free ascetic, beloved by his master and distinguished from dishonest fellow slaves; other passages use him as the voice of spiritual discernment. **[E4/E6]**
- **Ibn ʿArabī, *Fuṣūṣ al-ḥikam* (thirteenth century):** Luqmān becomes a bearer of mystical *ḥikma*, with Qurʾān 31 interpreted metaphysically. **[E5/E6]**
- **Ottoman *Pendnāme-i Lokmân Hekim* literature:** Collections of paternal counsels, sometimes translated or adapted from Persian, place Luqmān within advice literature. One Ottoman work published in 1324 AH/1906–07 versified thirty-four counsels from a Persian *Pendnāma*. **[E4/E6]**
- **Turkish and Uyghur oral literature:** Lokman is prophet, sage, healer, and seeker of immortality. **[E4/E7]** ([Uyghur legends study](https://dergipark.org.tr/tr/pub/egetdid/article/380264))

## 3. The Story

### 3.1 Luqmān ibn ʿĀd and the seven vultures

In the long-life legend, Luqmān belongs to the primordial Arabian people of ʿĀd. Some versions make him a leader or king; later Yemenite antiquarian narratives make him Ḥimyarite. God, or the gods in reconstructed pre-Islamic framing, permits him to choose a prodigious lifespan. He chooses the successive lives of seven vultures or eagles rather than a fixed number of years. He raises one bird at a time; when one dies, he takes another. The seventh and last is Lubad. When Lubad can no longer rise, Luqmān knows that his own allotted term has ended. **[E1 poetic allusion; E3 narrative form]**

The earliest poetic evidence alludes to Lubad but does not narrate the entire episode. The choice, rearing, number seven, and detailed death scene survive in Islamic antiquarian literature. It is therefore methodologically wrong to project every ninth-century detail into the sixth-century poem.

A modern hypothesis by Jan Retsö connects the birds with Arabian mantic practice. That is an interpretive reconstruction, not an attested explanation supplied by the old texts. **[E8]**

Later historical and genealogical authors add wives, kingship, adventures, and even marital betrayal. One report makes the long-lived Luqmān repeatedly marry unfaithful women and attempt to seclude a young wife, who nevertheless meets another man. Such stories belong to misogynistic medieval anecdotal development, not the Qurʾānic sage cycle. **[E3]**

### 3.2 Luqmān receives wisdom

The Qurʾān’s first statement is theological rather than biographical: “We gave Luqmān wisdom: be grateful to God” (31:12). Gratitude benefits the grateful; God is self-sufficient. **[E1]**

Later reports explain the gift:

- He was habitually truthful, silent except for good purpose, morally observant, and contemplative. **[E2–E3]**
- He frequented rulers and judges to learn from their successes and failures. **[E3]**
- Angels offered him judicial or royal office. He declined because judging people was a grave temptation, accepting only if directly commanded and divinely protected. God therefore granted wisdom; David accepted rule. **[E3]**
- Some sources say he slept when angels poured wisdom into him; others emphasize acquired ethical discernment. These are variants, not complementary facts.

### 3.3 The counsels to his son

The Qurʾānic cycle consists of a tightly constructed parental exhortation:

1. **Monotheism:** Do not associate partners with God; association is a great wrong.
2. **Parents:** Human beings owe gratitude and care to parents, especially the mother who bore and nursed the child; nevertheless, parents must not be obeyed in demands for polytheism.
3. **Divine omniscience and judgment:** A deed the weight of a mustard seed, even hidden in stone or the cosmos, will be produced by God.
4. **Practice:** Establish prayer.
5. **Public ethics:** Command what is right and forbid what is wrong.
6. **Fortitude:** Endure what befalls you.
7. **Humility:** Do not turn your cheek contemptuously from people or walk arrogantly.
8. **Moderation:** Be measured in gait and lower the voice; the donkey’s braying is the most disagreeable voice.

Only portions of 31:12–13 and 31:16–19 are grammatically presented as Luqmān’s direct speech. Verses 14–15 are divine instruction inserted into the sequence. Later retellings often present the whole block as one uninterrupted paternal testament; that is a harmonizing reception. **[E1 versus E3]**

The son remains unnamed in the Qurʾān. Later names include **Thārān/Tārān**, **Anʿum**, **Mashkam**, and other manuscript-dependent forms. No one name has early authority. **[E3 variants]**

### 3.4 The enslaved African sage

Early exegetical reports describe Luqmān as a black Nubian, Ethiopian, or Upper Egyptian enslaved man, sometimes with thick lips and flat or fissured feet. Professions vary: carpenter, tailor, shepherd. **[E2–E3]**

These descriptions serve several functions in the transmitted literature:

- Wisdom is shown to be God’s gift rather than a consequence of lineage, wealth, free status, or conventional beauty.
- Anecdotes rebuke anti-black contempt by naming Luqmān among exemplary black persons.
- The physical description converges with the Greek *Life of Aesop*, whose hero is enslaved, physically marked, initially underestimated, and verbally brilliant.

The African and enslaved characterization is old within Islamic exegesis but not stated by the Qurʾān. It should neither be erased as embarrassing nor converted into a verified biography. **[E2–E3; Aesopic convergence E5]**

### 3.5 Heart and tongue

In a celebrated anecdote, Luqmān’s master orders him to slaughter an animal and bring its two best parts. He brings the heart and tongue. Ordered later to bring the worst parts, he again brings heart and tongue: when good, nothing is better; when corrupt, nothing is worse. **[E3/E5]**

The episode belongs also to Aesopic biography. Its attribution to Luqmān is evidence of literary transfer, not independent proof that Luqmān and Aesop were one person.

### 3.6 Luqmān and David

Medieval Islamic tradition makes Luqmān a contemporary of **Dāwūd/David**:

- Luqmān visits David and watches him fashion armor, refraining from asking until the completed object explains itself.
- David praises him for having received wisdom while being spared the ordeal of kingship or judgment.
- Luqmān serves as judge before David, learns from him, or becomes his counselor or vizier, depending on the source.
- Some philosophical chronographies extend Luqmān’s life to one thousand years so he can encounter David.

None of this occurs in the Qurʾān. The cycle integrates the independent sage into biblical-Islamic sacred chronology. **[E3/E5]**

### 3.7 Aesopic fables

The medieval collection assigns Luqmān familiar animal fables: foxes, wolves, lions, hares, dogs, birds, and human types act out compact moral lessons. The corpus is substantially Aesopic and Syriac-Christian in transmission. **[E6]**

The tale of **Luqmān, his son, and the donkey**—in which every arrangement of riders attracts criticism, proving the impossibility of pleasing everyone—is documented in an Arabic version transmitted by Ibn Ṭāwūs in the thirteenth century. It is ATU 1215. It is not in the forty-one-fable collection and cannot automatically be assigned to ancient Aesop; medieval Latin and Christian comparanda complicate its route. **[E6]** ([Al-Qantara](https://al-qantara.revistas.csic.es/index.php/al-qantara/article/download/695/782?inline=1))

### 3.8 Persian ascetic stories

In Saʿdī, Luqmān’s wisdom comes from observing bad conduct and refusing to imitate it. In Rūmī, he is an enslaved man whose spiritual refinement contrasts with other servants’ greed or deceit. When his master gives him bitter food, he accepts it gratefully because he has received so many sweet gifts from the same hand; this becomes an allegory for accepting divine trial. **[E4/E6]**

These stories reinterpret his Qurʾānic gratitude through Persian Sufi ethics. They are not forgotten portions of an ancient Arabian biography.

### 3.9 The physician and lost cure for death

Turkish folklore turns *Ḥakīm/Hekim* into “physician.” Lokman learns the virtues of every plant; plants may speak and announce their medicinal uses. He discovers a remedy for death, but the prescription is lost—blown into a river, dropped from a bridge, swallowed by water, or destroyed through divine intervention. In some versions Khidr or an angel prevents humanity from attaining immortality. **[E4/E7]**

In Şahmaran-related folklore, Lokman’s healing wisdom becomes entangled with the death and medicinal body of the serpent queen Şahmaran. The configurations vary regionally; a single canonical story does not exist. **[E4/E7]**

### 3.10 Death and body

There is no Qurʾānic account of Luqmān’s death. The long-lived Luqmān dies with Lubad; the sage Luqmān simply disappears from the scriptural account. Medieval and modern local traditions place his grave in several mutually incompatible locations, including:

- the vicinity east of the Sea of Galilee/Tiberias;
- Palestine, including traditions around Ramla or Bethlehem;
- northern Iraq or the Mosul region;
- al-Ḥusayniyya north of Baghdad;
- sites in Anatolia and elsewhere associated with Lokman Hekim.

Multiplicity is evidence that these are commemorative attributions, not a recoverable burial history. **[E3–E7]**

## 4. Relations with God, Divine Beings, and Other Heroes

### Genealogical variants

| Tradition | Lineage or status | Source stratum | Tier |
|---|---|---|---|
| Qurʾānic | No parentage supplied | Qurʾān 31:12–19, seventh century | E1 |
| Arabian | Luqmān ibn ʿĀd, belonging to primordial ʿĀd | Early poetry; later antiquarian narrative | E1/E3 |
| Joban I | Son of Job’s sister | Report attributed to Wahb ibn Munabbih; al-Thaʿlabī | E2/E3 |
| Joban II | Son of Job’s maternal uncle | Muqātil; later tafsīr | E2/E3 |
| Abrahamic/biblical | Son of Bāʿūrāʾ/Beor, son of Nahor, son of Terah/Āzar | Report attributed to Ibn Isḥāq; later tafsīr | E2/E3/E5 |
| Nubian | Black Nubian enslaved man | Reports from Mujāhid, Saʿīd ibn al-Musayyab, Ibn ʿAbbās; preserved in tafsīr | E2/E3 |
| Ethiopian | Ethiopian enslaved carpenter | Report attributed to Ibn ʿAbbās | E2/E3 |
| Egyptian | Enslaved man from Upper Egypt | Later exegetical harmonization | E3 |
| Ḥimyarite king | Ancient South Arabian ruler | Yemenite antiquarian/genealogical tradition | E3/E5 |

The genealogies cannot all be historical. Their value lies in showing successive attempts to locate a famous but under-specified sage.

No securely early wife is named. The Qurʾān mentions a son but not his name or mother. Long-lived-man stories add wives; Persian and Turkish stories usually retain only master, servants, pupils, or symbolic companions.

### Documented interactions: one line per named being or hero

- **Allah/God** — God grants Luqmān wisdom and commands gratitude; Luqmān instructs his son not to associate partners with God and teaches divine omniscience and judgment. *Qurʾān* 31:12–19, early seventh century. **[E1]**
- **Luqmān’s unnamed son** — Recipient of the monotheistic, ethical, and social counsels. *Qurʾān* 31:13, 16–19. Although not divine or heroic, he is the cycle’s principal human counterpart. **[E1]**
- **Dāwūd/David** — Luqmān observes David making armor, advises or learns from him, and is contrasted with him over kingship/judgment; absent from the Qurʾān and developed in tafsīr and *qiṣaṣ*. Al-Thaʿlabī and related medieval sources, eleventh century onward. **[E3/E5]**
- **Angels** — Offer Luqmān rule or judgment, question his refusal, or communicate the gift of wisdom. Al-Thaʿlabī and related narrative traditions. **[E3]**
- **Ayyūb/Job** — No securely narrated personal encounter; Luqmān is made Job’s nephew or cousin in competing genealogies. Reports attributed to Wahb and Muqātil, preserved in medieval exegesis. **[E2/E3/E5]**
- **Ibrāhīm/Abraham** — No direct interaction; Luqmān is inserted into Abraham’s extended genealogy through Nahor and Terah. Ibn Isḥāq-attributed genealogy in later tafsīr. **[E2/E3/E5]**
- **Āzar/Terah** — Genealogical ancestor only, not a narrative companion. Later tafsīr. **[E3/E5]**
- **Balaam son of Beor** — No early story of an encounter. Luqmān’s constructed patronymic Bāʿūrāʾ prompted medieval or modern identification/confusion. **[E5/E8]**
- **Aḥiqar** — No text stages a meeting. The relationship is comparative and intertextual: paternal wisdom, proverbial sayings, and motifs circulate between or within a common Near Eastern repertoire. **[E8]**
- **Aesop** — No ancient meeting. Luqmān receives Aesop’s slave biography, physical traits, heart-and-tongue anecdote, and fables through Syriac and Arabic transmission. Identification becomes explicit or functional in medieval and early-modern fable culture. **[E5/E6]**
- **Solomon/Sulaymān** — No securely documented personal interaction. Comparison rests on both figures’ divinely given wisdom and on later wisdom literature. **[E5/E8]**
- **Empedocles** — Some Islamic philosophical histories claim that Empedocles accompanied or learned from Luqmān. This is a learned attempt to build a pre-Socratic transmission genealogy, not early Greek evidence. **[E5]**
- **Alcmaeon of Croton** — No ancient interaction. Modern identification based on name and philosophical context remains speculative. **[E8]**
- **Al-Khiḍr/Khidr** — In some Turkish and Central Asian immortality or healing tales, Khidr parallels, assists, or supersedes Lokman; individual versions vary and are late. **[E4/E7]**
- **Şahmaran** — In Anatolian and Kurdish-Turkish folklore, Lokman acquires medical knowledge in a story involving the serpent queen’s death or healing flesh. This is a regional folkloric conjunction, not Qurʾānic tradition. **[E4/E7]**
- **Iblīs/Satan** — Dialogues circulated online or in late popular preaching are not part of the securely identified early corpus unless tied to a specific manuscript or edition. Famous modern “Luqmān and Satan” conversations should be treated as late devotional literature or unverified attribution. **[E9 unless sourced]**
- **Muḥammad** — The Qurʾān presents Luqmān within Muḥammad’s proclamation, but no canonical text narrates a historical meeting; Luqmān belongs to the exemplary past. **[E1]**

There is no documented interaction with Dhū l-Qarnayn, the Companions of the Cave, ʿAntar, Majnūn, Sinbad, or other heroes named in the broader Arabic-Islamic dossier program. Their inclusion alongside Luqmān in modern surveys reflects genre grouping, not story-world contact.

## 5. Cult, Tomb, Image, and Ritual

### Cult

There is no securely attested pre-Islamic temple, sacrificial cult, priesthood, festival, oracle, or hero shrine dedicated to Luqmān. He was proverbial and literary, but a Greek-style hero cult cannot be inferred from fame alone. **[Absence of E1 archaeological evidence]**

Likewise, no Qurʾānic or early Islamic ritual is prescribed in his name. Reciting Sūrat Luqmān, teaching its parental counsels, or naming children Luqmān are forms of scriptural reception, not evidence of an autonomous cult.

### Tombs and shrines

Multiple tombs are attributed to Luqmān. A Baghdad-area grave in al-Ḥusayniyya is locally identified as his; other traditions place him near Tiberias, in the Levant, or in northern Iraq. Modern reports themselves acknowledge the conflict. **[E7]** ([report on Iraqi and Levantine claims](https://www.aljazeera.net/misc/2021/12/1/%D8%A3%D9%88%D8%A7%D8%B9%D8%AF%D9%83-%D8%A8%D8%A7%D9%84%D9%88%D8%B9%D8%AF-%D9%88%D8%A3%D8%B3%D9%82%D9%8A%D9%83-%D9%8A%D8%A7%D9%83%D9%85%D9%88%D9%86-%D9%85%D8%A7-%D9%82%D8%B5%D8%A9-%D9%87%D8%B0%D8%A7))

None has published archaeological evidence linking a burial to a datable historical Luqmān. They are best classified as **attributed shrines**. Their significance is reception-historical: communities localized an otherwise placeless sacred sage.

### Healing belief

In Turkish popular culture, Lokman Hekim is invoked as the ancestor or patron-like exemplar of physicians, pharmacists, and herbalists. Medical books, pharmacies, institutions, journals, and health products use his name. This is cultural commemoration rather than a formally constituted saint cult. **[E4/E7]**

The association is sufficiently strong that Turkish studies discuss him as a functional successor to older healing gods or culture heroes, but such comparisons are interpretive and do not prove direct cultic continuity. ([“Türk Kültüründe Lokman Hekim”](https://dergipark.org.tr/en/pub/tkhcbva/article/1157288))

### Images

No securely identified ancient portrait of Luqmān survives. Islamic manuscripts may illustrate fables, prophetic histories, or wisdom tales, but an image labeled “Luqmān” documents the manuscript’s own period, not his appearance.

A further source of confusion is **Seyyid Lokman**, the sixteenth-century Ottoman court historian whose lavishly illustrated chronicles were produced with painters including Nakkaş Osman. Miniatures in his books are not representations of Luqmān the Sage merely because “Lokman” appears in catalogue descriptions. **[E9 correction]**

Modern illustrations commonly portray Luqmān as an elderly black or African sage instructing a boy, or as a turbaned herbal physician. These images synthesize Qurʾānic counseling, exegetical ethnicity, and Turkish medical folklore. They are modern iconography, not copies of an ancient type. **[E9]**

## 6. Historical Evolution

### Phase I: Pre-Islamic Arabian proverbial antiquity

By the poetic stratum associated with al-Nābigha, Luqmān/Lubad represented extreme longevity and the inevitable victory of time. Other verse placed him among ʿĀd and Iram, figures of primordial Arabian grandeur. **[E1 with oral-transmission caveat]**

Whether “wise Luqmān” and “long-lived Luqmān ibn ʿĀd” were already one character cannot be established. Early Islamic scholars themselves inherited enough ambiguity to distinguish or combine them.

### Phase II: Qurʾānic canonization, early seventh century

The Qurʾān selected Luqmān not for longevity, kingship, or fables but for God-given wisdom and paternal monotheistic teaching. It transformed a recognized Arabian sage into a witness to Qurʾānic ethics while retaining his mortal status. **[E1]**

The address participates in an ancient Near Eastern “father to son” instructional form seen in Egyptian instructions, Proverbs, Aḥiqar, and other wisdom texts. Similarity of form establishes literary-cultural context, not direct copying from one uniquely identified book. Saqib Hussain’s recent study treats the passage as Qurʾānic *ḥikma* and rationally accessible virtue. **[E8]** ([Oxford Academic](https://academic.oup.com/book/62229/chapter-abstract/550259049))

### Phase III: Early Islamic identification, seventh–ninth centuries

Transmitters supplied what the Qurʾān omitted: color, origin, occupation, moral habits, lineage, and prophetic status. The most influential image was the black enslaved craftsman whose wisdom defeated social prejudice. Simultaneously, antiquarians systematized Luqmān ibn ʿĀd and the seven vultures. **[E2–E3]**

### Phase IV: Classical synthesis, tenth–thirteenth centuries

Al-Ṭabarī displayed variant reports. Al-Thaʿlabī and *qiṣaṣ* literature integrated Luqmān with Job, Abrahamic genealogy, David, angels, and sacred kingship. Adab writers multiplied counsels. Philosophers and mystics made him an ancient transmitter of universal wisdom. **[E3/E5]**

This is also the period in which Aḥiqar- and Aesop-like material increasingly attached to him.

### Phase V: Aesopic canonization and Persianization

The 1299 Christian Arabic fable manuscript demonstrates that Luqmān had become the named author of an Aesopic collection. Meanwhile, Saʿdī and Rūmī reshaped him as an ascetic and spiritually free slave. **[E4/E6]**

The same century therefore preserves different Luqmāns: Qurʾānic father, Sufi exemplar, slave-sage, and animal fabulist.

### Phase VI: Ottoman medicalization and European orientalism

In Ottoman culture, *Lokman Hekim* became the proverbial physician and herbalist. Advice books and medical recipes used his name. **[E4/E6/E7]**

Erpenius’s 1615 edition simultaneously established Luqmān in European Arabic studies as an oriental Aesop. Later editions treated the fables as elementary Arabic reading matter, reinforcing a misleading impression that they were an ancient book by the Qurʾānic sage. **[E6]**

### Phase VII: Modern educational, national, and commercial uses

Modern Muslim educational literature foregrounds Luqmān as the ideal father and Qurʾānic pedagogue. Turkish cultural discourse foregrounds a native or Islamic ancestor of medicine. African and African-diasporic readings may emphasize the honored black sage, drawing legitimately on early exegetical reception while sometimes presenting that reception as Qurʾānic fact.

There is no major documented extremist appropriation specific to Luqmān comparable to the political deployment of warrior or conquest heroes. Political uses are generally ethical and pedagogical: family authority, filial conduct, religious education, and claims that Islamic civilization anticipated holistic medicine. **[E7–E9]**

## 7. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Literature and education

Luqmān’s counsels are repeatedly adapted into children’s books, sermons, parenting manuals, school curricula, illustrated Qurʾānic stories, and collections of “wisdom quotes.” Modern retellings commonly merge Qurʾān, al-Ṭabarī, Ibn Kathīr, fables, and Turkish folklore without indicating their dates. **[E9 when undifferentiated]**

A source-critical reading should ask of every maxim:

1. Is it actually in Qurʾān 31?
2. If not, what is its earliest tafsīr, adab, Persian, or fable witness?
3. Is the wording stable?
4. Is it a modern motivational paraphrase?

Statements circulated as “Luqmān said” frequently have no cited premodern text. Attribution by name alone is not evidence.

### Medicine and institutional naming

“Lokman Hekim” names Turkish hospitals, universities, journals, pharmacies, herbal preparations, television or educational productions, and health businesses. This derives from the late physician persona, not the Qurʾānic passage. **[E7/E9]**

The medicalization may be culturally traditional, but claims that Luqmān historically founded medicine, discovered anesthesia, wrote surviving pharmacology, or possessed a literal universal cure are unverified unless tied to a datable text. **[E9]**

### Film, television, visual art, and performance

Modern religious animation and television depict the father-son counsels. Turkish storytellers dramatize Lokman’s search for immortality and the loss of the prescription in a river. Contemporary Şahmaran adaptations occasionally include or echo the Lokman healing complex, though many omit him.

Unlike ʿAntar, Alexander, or Majnūn, Luqmān has not generated one internationally dominant filmic image. His modern visual form remains distributed among religious illustration, children’s media, medical emblems, and folkloric art.

### Race and representation

The black Luqmān can function as a counterexample to racial hierarchy: early exegetical reports explicitly use his wisdom to answer contempt for blackness. Yet modern images sometimes reproduce caricatural traits derived from the grotesque *Life of Aesop*. Responsible reception must distinguish the tradition’s anti-status moral from the physiognomic stereotypes through which medieval writers expressed it.

### Modern inventions requiring rejection or qualification

- “Luqmān wrote the forty-one fables in antiquity.” **Unsupported; the collection’s secure manuscript evidence is medieval.**
- “The Qurʾān says Luqmān was Ethiopian/Nubian.” **False; tafsīr says this, not the Qurʾān.**
- “The Qurʾān calls Luqmān a prophet.” **False; it says he received wisdom.**
- “Archaeology has found Luqmān’s grave.” **Unsupported.**
- “Luqmān was certainly Aesop, Alcmaeon, Balaam, or Aḥiqar.” **Not established.**
- “Ottoman miniatures by Lokman depict the sage.” **Often a confusion with the author Seyyid Lokman.**
- “All sayings addressed ‘O my son’ are Qurʾānic.” **False; the corpus expanded enormously after the Qurʾān.**
- Images advertised as “authentic portraits of Luqmān” are modern devotional or commercial art. **[E9]**

## 8. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### One Luqmān or several?

Older reference works often spoke of two or three Luqmāns: the long-lived ʿĀdite, the Qurʾānic sage, and the fabulist. More recent work studies how traditions bearing the same name interacted without assuming either complete identity or absolute separateness.

The strongest historical conclusion is modest: a Luqmān figure was proverbial in pre-Islamic Arabian poetic memory; the Qurʾān presents a sage of that name; later transmitters combined him with Arabian antiquity, biblical genealogy, African slave-sage traditions, Aḥiqar, Aesop, and the physician archetype.

### Historicity

No contemporary birth, office, inscription, or grave record permits a historical biography. The poetic and Qurʾānic attestations demonstrate that the **figure and name circulated**, not that any particular medieval biography is factual.

A historical sage may stand behind the tradition, but that proposition is presently non-falsifiable. Assertions that Luqmān lived in David’s reign, ancient Nubia, Egypt, Yemen, or Greece depend on late identification systems.

### Authenticity of the pre-Islamic poetry

The al-Nābigha material is earlier in attribution and poetic setting than the Qurʾān, but the extant dīwāns were collected and written in the Islamic period. Scholars therefore use such poems critically: an allusion embedded in an archaic poem is good evidence for an early legend, but its precise sixth-century form is less secure than an inscription or papyrus.

### Luqmān and Aḥiqar

Comparisons include:

- father-to-son instruction;
- emphasis on disciplined speech;
- animal and donkey imagery;
- maxims shared with Proverbs, Sirach, and other Near Eastern traditions.

Nöldeke and subsequent scholars traced transfers among Aḥiqar, Aesop, and Luqmān. Current discussion emphasizes a common and mobile wisdom archive rather than a simple one-source borrowing model. The Aḥiqar story itself survives in multiple languages and recensions, making linear dependency difficult to prove. ([Ahiqar research project](https://ahiqar.uni-goettingen.de/))

### Luqmān and Aesop

The case for literary influence is strong:

- both become ugly or physically marked slaves;
- both defeat social superiors through wisdom;
- both receive the heart-and-tongue anecdote;
- medieval Arabic Luqmān fables correspond extensively to Greek and Syriac Aesop.

The case for personal identity is weak. No early Greek source calls Aesop Luqmān, and no early Arabic source securely assigns the received fable book to Luqmān. The most economical explanation is medieval transfer and convergence. **[E8 reconstruction supported by E6 manuscripts]**

### African origin

The Nubian/Ethiopian identity is an early Islamic tradition, not a modern fabrication, but it is post-Qurʾānic. Scholars debate whether it originated independently, reflected social memory of African sages and slaves, or arose under Aesopic influence. The evidence supports the antiquity of the Islamic portrayal but cannot decide the ethnicity of a historical Luqmān.

### Prophecy

The majority exegetical view denies prophecy because the Qurʾān assigns wisdom, not revelation or messengerhood. A minority report attributed to ʿIkrima calls him a prophet. Later popular practice sometimes adds *ʿalayhi al-salām*, a formula associated with prophets, without resolving the classical dispute. The variants should remain visible.

### Fable-manuscript discoveries

The 1299 Coptic Christian Arabic manuscript is a critical datum: it locates the received Luqmān fables within medieval Christian Arabic and Syriac-Aesopic transmission. Modern studies of Garshuni manuscripts continue to reveal additional Aesopic and non-Aesopic material, showing that “the Luqmān fables” were not one immutable authorial book. ([Ben-Gurion University research record](https://cris.bgu.ac.il/en/publications/neue-%C3%A6sopische-und-andere-fabeln-in-einer-arabischen-garshunihand/))

### Tomb debate

Competing grave claims lack archaeological confirmation. Medieval geographical statements and modern custodial testimony document belief, but not bodily identification. The multiplicity of tombs is best understood as localization of sacred memory.

### Medical tradition

The physician role is historically important in Persianate and especially Turkish reception. It is not discredited as folklore; it is discredited only when converted into a literal ancient medical biography. The shift is linguistically intelligible through *ḥakīm/hekim* and culturally reinforced by tales of speaking medicinal plants and lost immortality.

## 9. Chronology of Attestation and Reception

| Date | Documentary event |
|---|---|
| Late sixth century CE, traditional attribution | Al-Nābigha al-Dhubyānī alludes to Lubad, last of Luqmān’s seven vultures; earliest poetic stratum, preserved in later recension. |
| Early seventh century | Qurʾān 31:12–19 presents Luqmān as recipient of divine wisdom and counselor of his son. |
| Seventh–eighth centuries, reports preserved later | Mujāhid, ʿIkrima, Saʿīd ibn al-Musayyab, Wahb ibn Munabbih, Ibn Isḥāq, and Muqātil are credited with divergent accounts of prophecy, African origin, slavery, occupation, and genealogy. |
| Eighth–ninth centuries | Antiquarian and adab traditions increasingly distinguish or combine Luqmān the sage and Luqmān ibn ʿĀd. |
| Ninth century | Abū Ḥātim al-Sijistānī records the long-lived Luqmān and seven-vulture legend in *al-Muʿammarūn*. |
| c. 700–900 manuscript range | Early Qurʾānic manuscript catalogued by Corpus Coranicum preserves Q 29:18–31:22, including the Luqmān passage. |
| Early tenth century | Al-Ṭabarī compiles major exegetical variants on Q 31:12–19. |
| Early eleventh century | Al-Thaʿlabī embeds Luqmān in *qiṣaṣ al-anbiyāʾ*, biblical genealogy, angelic dialogue, and the David cycle. |
| Eleventh–thirteenth centuries | Adab, proverb, philosophical, and mystical literature greatly expands his sayings. |
| 1258 | Saʿdī completes the *Golestān*, including Luqmān as proverbial moral observer. |
| Thirteenth century | Rūmī’s *Mathnawī* develops the enslaved ascetic Luqmān; Ibn ʿArabī interprets Luqmānic wisdom metaphysically. |
| Thirteenth century | Ibn Ṭāwūs transmits an Arabic “Luqmān, his son, and the donkey” tale. |
| 1299 | Coptic Christian Arabic manuscript records forty-one fables attributed to Luqmān, largely Aesopic through Syriac transmission. |
| Later medieval period | Persian and Turkish advice literature, healing lore, and immortality legends proliferate. |
| 1615 | Thomas Erpenius publishes the Arabic-Latin *Locmani sapientis fabulae* at Leiden. |
| Seventeenth–nineteenth centuries | Reprints, translations, and school editions establish Luqmān as “the Arabic Aesop” in Europe. |
| 1850 | Derenbourg publishes an influential European edition and French translation of the fables. |
| 1906–07 | An Ottoman *Pendnāme-i Lokmân Hekim* versifies Persian counsels and is printed at Istanbul. |
| Twentieth century | Scholarship distinguishes Qurʾānic, ʿĀdite, Aḥiqar, Aesopic, Persian, and Turkish strata; Turkish medicine adopts Lokman Hekim as a cultural emblem. |
| Twenty-first century | Manuscript, folklore, Qurʾānic, and intertextual studies increasingly analyze convergence rather than search for one historical biography; unattributed online sayings and synthetic “life stories” proliferate. |

### Critical scholarship

Especially important are the *Encyclopaedia of Islam* article by B. Heller and Y. K. Stillman; Dmitri Gutas on Arabic wisdom literature; Theodor Nöldeke on Aḥiqar; studies of Aesopic Arabic and Garshuni manuscripts; the Turkish Islamic Encyclopedia entry; recent work by Ute Pietruschka on intertwined Luqmān representations; and Saqib Hussain’s study of Luqmān within Qurʾānic wisdom and late-antique moral discourse.

The most secure final assessment is therefore:

**Luqmān is demonstrably an early Arabian legendary sage, canonically reshaped by the Qurʾān as a grateful monotheist and ethical father. Everything beyond those counsels—the ʿĀdite kingship, seven vultures in full narrative form, African slave biography, Davidic setting, biblical genealogy, Aesopic fables, Sufi asceticism, medical mastery, immortality remedy, and competing tombs—is historically important reception, but each belongs to a distinguishable later evidentiary stratum.**
