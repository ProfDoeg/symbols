# Al-Khidr: Research Dossier

## Evidentiary key

- **[E1: securely attested early material]** Qurʾānic text and early, well-attested hadith or tafsīr.
- **[E2: later canonical elaboration]** Classical tafsīr, universal history, qiṣaṣ al-anbiyāʾ, and authoritative medieval theology.
- **[E3: regional variant]** A geographically localized literary or cultic form.
- **[E4: learned systematization]** Sufi, philosophical, theological, or literary interpretation consciously organizing older material.
- **[E5: syncretistic identification]** Identification or shared cult with another named sacred figure.
- **[E6: medieval/early-modern accretion]** Material added in romances, hagiographies, and later narrative cycles.
- **[E7: folklore/lived belief]** Oral traditions, visitation, festivals, vows, healing, and reports of encounters.
- **[E8: modern scholarly reconstruction]** Historical-critical explanations not asserted by the premodern sources themselves.
- **[E9: modern invention or reuse]** Recent literary, political, commercial, psychological, or internet-era construction.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

**Traditional class:** Al-Khiḍr is variously classified as a **prophet** (*nabī*), divinely instructed **servant of God**, **walī** or saint, immortal guide, helper of travelers, master of hidden knowledge, and—especially in regional religion—a saint-like culture hero governing water, fertility, vegetation, and liminal spaces. The traditions do not agree on his rank. **[E1]**

**Earliest secure attestation:** the earliest secure textual nucleus is Qurʾān 18:60–82, conventionally dated to the early seventh century CE. It describes an unnamed “servant from among Our servants” whom God has granted mercy and knowledge and whom Moses follows. It does **not** give the servant the name al-Khiḍr, say that he drank the Water of Life, identify him with Elijah or Saint George, or declare him immortal. Those are post-Qurʾanic identifications and expansions. The identification is explicit in early Islamic tradition preserved in the hadith and tafsīr corpora. **[E1]**

### Name and spellings

- Arabic: **الخَضِر**, commonly vocalized *al-Khaḍir* or *al-Khiḍr*; also الخِضْر, *al-Khiḍr*. The consonantal root **ḫ-ḍ-r** denotes greenness or verdure. *Al-Khaḍir* is therefore “the Green/Verdant One.” **[E1/E2]**
- Persian: خضر, *Khiżr, Kheżr, Ḵeżr*; honorific **خواجه خضر**, *Khwāja Khiżr*, “Master/Lord Khidr.” **[E3]**
- Turkish: **Hızır**; the compound **Hıdırellez/Hıdrellez** joins Hızır and İlyas. **[E3/E7]**
- Urdu and South Asian Persianate usage: **Khizr**, **Khwāja Khizr**, **Khizr-i Rāh** (“Khizr of/the Guide of the Path”). **[E3/E9]**
- Balkan and other localized forms include *Hidir, Ederlez,* and *Erdelezi*, particularly in spring-festival terminology. **[E3/E7]**

A hadith preserved in *Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī* explains the epithet etiologically: al-Khaḍir was so named because barren or white ground turned green after he sat upon it. Al-Bukhārī died in 256/870; the extant collection thus supplies a secure ninth-century textual attestation to both the name and its association with miraculous greening. **[E1]**

The derivation from Arabic greenness is linguistically transparent and already traditional. Attempts to derive the name from Greek **Glaukos**, Utnapishtim/Hasisatra, the Ugaritic craftsman-god Kothar-wa-Khasis, or an Arabic root meaning “to be present” are modern comparative proposals or speculative etymologies; none has displaced the ordinary Arabic explanation. **[E8]**

### Personal names and genealogies

Later authors attempted to convert the epithet into a biography. Forms include **Balyā/Baliyā ibn Malkān**, sometimes extended through Fāliq, ʿĀbir, Shāliḥ, Arfakhshadh, Sām, and Noah. Other reports made him a descendant of Cain, a son of Adam, an Arab, a Persian, a son of Pharaoh’s daughter, or a contemporary and follower of Abraham. Ibn Ḥajar reportedly collected approximately ten competing identifications. These are mutually incompatible medieval genealogical constructions, not independent evidence for a historical person. **[E2/E6]**

Al-Ṭabarī’s *History* preserves a form in which Baliyā son of Malkān belongs to the era of the Persian king Afrīdūn, identified there with an elder Dhū al-Qarnayn; another strand makes his father a believer who emigrated with Abraham. Al-Ṭabarī does not harmonize all such reports, and neither should the dossier. **[E2]**

### Identifications with other figures

- **The unnamed servant of Qurʾān 18:** nearly universal in post-Qurʾanic Islamic exegesis; explicit in hadith and early tafsīr. **[E1]**
- **Elijah/Ilyās:** sometimes separate companion, annual meeting-partner, twin, or complementary double; elsewhere an outright identification. Al-Ṭabarī also transmits a version keeping them distinct—a Persian Khidr and Israelite Elijah who meet annually. **[E2/E5]**
- **Alexander’s cook Andreas/Glaukos:** a comparative and narrative equation produced by Islamic Alexander traditions from the revived-fish and Water-of-Life motifs. In the Greek and Syriac antecedents the cook, not a figure named Khidr, finds the life-giving water. **[E6/E8]**
- **Saint George/al-Khaḍr:** locally shared identity in the Levant and Anatolia, particularly at shrines visited by Christians and Muslims. Shared worship is documented; descent from an ancient unitary “Green Man” or Baal cult is not. **[E5/E7]**
- **Jhūlelāl/Uderolāl/Zinda Pīr/Sheikh Tāhir:** overlapping river-saint complexes in Sindh. Different communities can identify, distinguish, or translate the figures differently. **[E3/E5/E7]**
- **Idrīs, Jesus, the Hidden Imam, and the “Men of the Unseen”:** grouped with Khidr as deathless or occulted sacred beings in medieval mystical cosmologies, without necessarily being identified as the same person. **[E4]**
- Proposed identifications with **Melchizedek**, **Enoch**, **Elisha**, pre-Islamic Iranian divinities, or a Canaanite vegetation deity remain learned or comparative hypotheses unless a particular source explicitly makes the equation. **[E4/E8]**

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### Qurʾān 18:60–82

**Language:** Arabic.  
**Date:** early seventh century; surviving manuscript evidence belongs to the early Islamic Qurʾanic transmission.  
**Role:** unnamed teacher of Moses. **[E1]**

The passage has two linked movements:

1. Moses and his unnamed young attendant travel toward the “junction of the two seas.” A fish they carry escapes into the sea at a rock; the attendant initially forgets to tell Moses.
2. Moses meets a servant endowed with divine mercy and knowledge. The servant warns that Moses cannot be patient. Moses promises obedience but protests when the servant damages a boat, kills a *ghulām*—a boy or youth—and repairs a wall without payment in an inhospitable settlement. The servant then explains the hidden purposes.

The text calls the companion only **ʿabdan min ʿibādinā**, “a servant from among Our servants.” Its final words—“I did not do it on my own command”—became central to the argument that the servant was a prophet acting upon revelation. **[E1/E4]**

### Hadith

The Moses narrative is elaborated in reports preserved by al-Bukhārī. Moses, after answering that he is the most learned person, is directed to a servant at the junction of the seas; his attendant is named **Yūshaʿ ibn Nūn**, Joshua son of Nun. The servant is named al-Khaḍir, recognizes Moses as the Moses of Israel, and the episode becomes a lesson in intellectual humility. Another Bukhārī report explains the name through miraculous greening. **[E1]**

These collections are ninth-century written witnesses transmitting claims through earlier isnāds. They establish what mature hadith tradition taught, not direct seventh-century observation of a historical Khidr.

### Early tafsīr

- **Muqātil ibn Sulaymān** (d. 150/767): among the earliest substantially surviving continuous commentaries. His work represents an eighth-century stage in which the Qurʾanic narratives were already being supplied with names, settings, and explanatory details. Its surviving textual transmission is later than its attributed composition and must be critically assessed. **[E1/E2]**
- **Al-Ṭabarī**, *Jāmiʿ al-bayān* and *Tārīkh al-rusul wa-l-mulūk* (author d. 310/923): collects competing reports about the servant, Moses, Joshua, genealogy, Dhū al-Qarnayn, the River of Life, and Khidr’s antiquity. The method preserves variants rather than producing a single biography. **[E2]**
- **Al-Māwardī** (d. 450/1058): lists several possible meanings of the “mercy” granted to Khidr—prophethood, grace, perfect obedience, or prolonged life. **[E4]**
- **Al-Baghawī** (d. 516/1122) and **al-Bayḍāwī** (d. 719/1319): witnesses to the continuing dispute over saint versus prophet. Al-Bayḍāwī argues that the mercy is revelation and prophethood; reports attributed to al-Baghawī present the contrary position as widely held. **[E4]**

### Qiṣaṣ al-anbiyāʾ

- **Al-Thaʿlabī**, *ʿArāʾis al-majālis fī qiṣaṣ al-anbiyāʾ* (author d. 427/1035): places Khidr within an expanded narrative history of the prophets, drawing Qurʾanic, exegetical, biblical, and storytelling materials together. **[E2/E6]**
- **Al-Kisāʾī**, *Qiṣaṣ al-anbiyāʾ*, generally attributed to an author of roughly the eleventh century: supplies embroidered prophetic biographies, including Khidr, beyond the Qurʾan’s terse notices. The attribution and exact date are less secure than the existence of the medieval textual tradition. **[E2/E6]**

### Alexander literature

The Greek Alexander Romance’s β-recension and Syriac Alexander materials contain a salted or cooked fish that revives in life-giving water. Alexander’s cook, commonly called Andreas in the Greek tradition, discovers or enters the spring while Alexander ultimately fails to obtain immortality. The Syriac metrical Alexander poem, usually dated to late antiquity or the early seventh century but surviving in later manuscripts, gives the fish a diagnostic role in locating the Water of Life. **[E8: pre-Islamic/late-antique comparandum]**

Islamicate Alexander texts progressively make Khidr Alexander’s guide, minister, cook, or prophetic companion:

- al-Ṭabarī preserves the River-of-Life narrative in a Persian universal-historical framework. **[E2]**
- Firdawsī’s *Shāhnāma* (completed ca. 1010) participates in the Persian Alexander tradition. **[E6]**
- Niẓāmī’s *Iskandarnāma* (late twelfth century) gives sophisticated Persian poetic form to Iskandar’s quest and Khidr’s successful access to immortality. **[E4/E6]**
- Later Arabic *Sīrat al-Iskandar* traditions can title Khidr Alexander’s vizier and make the Water-of-Life adventure a major narrative component. **[E6]**

It is therefore inaccurate to say simply that “the Qurʾan tells how Khidr guided Alexander to the Water of Life.” The Qurʾan places the Moses and Dhū al-Qarnayn narratives in the same sura but does not join their protagonists.

### Sufi and Persianate corpus

- ʿAṭṭār (ca. 1145–1221), especially the *Ilāhī-nāma* and *Manṭiq al-ṭayr*, uses Khidr and the Water of Life as figures for inspired knowledge and the seeker’s path. **[E4]**
- Ibn ʿArabī (1165–1240) treats Khidr as living initiator and liminal master. His accounts of investiture became foundational for later claims of Khidrian initiation. **[E4]**
- Rūmī (1207–1273), in the *Mathnawī*, turns Moses and Khidr into a model for disciple and spiritual guide while preserving the peril of judging divine wisdom by appearances. **[E4]**
- Persian ghazal and romance repeatedly use Khidr, darkness, the guide, and the Water of Life as metaphors for poetic speech, love, eternal meaning, and spiritual instruction. **[E4]**

The source brief’s references to *Sīrat ʿAntar*, the *Muʿallaqāt*, Laylā and Majnūn, Sinbad, and the *Thousand and One Nights* belong to a wider Arabian-hero corpus; they are not primary Khidr witnesses merely by belonging to that corpus. No interaction between Khidr and ʿAntara, Laylā, Majnūn, or Sinbad should be asserted without a particular text.

## 3. The story

### Moses’ search

Moses travels until he reaches the junction of two seas, even if the journey should take years. His young attendant carries a fish. At a rock the fish takes its way into the sea in a wondrous fashion; forgetfulness delays the report. Moses recognizes the place as their destination and retraces the route. **Earliest source:** Qurʾān 18:60–65. **[E1]**

Hadith identifies the attendant as Joshua and explains that Moses was sent because he had failed to attribute ultimate knowledge to God. This turns the episode into an explicit correction of prophetic self-confidence. **[E1]**

Later geography located the “two seas” at numerous real or symbolic sites—Mediterranean and Red Sea, Bosporus, Persian Gulf, or the meeting of exoteric and esoteric knowledge. None is fixed by the Qurʾan. **[E3/E4]**

### The compact of silence

Moses asks to follow the servant and learn some of his right guidance. The servant predicts that Moses cannot patiently endure acts whose meaning he does not know. Moses promises patience and obedience. The condition is that Moses ask no question until the servant himself explains. **Qurʾān 18:66–70. [E1]**

Sufi interpretation commonly reads Moses as bearer of the revealed law’s outward judgment and Khidr as bearer of divinely bestowed inward knowledge. This is a medieval hermeneutic, not a distinction stated in those terms by the Qurʾan. **[E4]**

### The boat

Khidr damages a boat after its owners admit them aboard. Moses protests that he has endangered its passengers. Khidr later explains that the boat belonged to poor working people and that a king was seizing every sound vessel; the defect preserved it for its owners. **Qurʾān 18:71, 79. [E1]**

Variants concern how the boat was damaged—removing or loosening a plank, piercing it, or otherwise making a repairable defect. These are exegetical elaborations. **[E2]**

### The killing of the boy

Khidr kills a *ghulām*. Moses protests in unmistakably moral and legal language: he has killed a pure or innocent soul without retaliation for another killing. Khidr later says the boy’s parents were believers and “we feared” that he would burden them with rebellion and unbelief; God intended to replace him with someone purer and more compassionate. **Qurʾān 18:74, 80–81. [E1]**

This is killing, not symbolic correction, in the surface narrative. Later exegetes variously:

- treated the victim as a prepubescent child;
- understood *ghulām* as an older youth;
- supplied a divine command or prophetic revelation;
- alleged that the youth was already a criminal;
- emphasized divine foreknowledge of his future oppression or unbelief;
- read the episode as a unique divine dispensation unavailable for human imitation.

Those explanations are mutually non-identical and later than the Qurʾanic text. The episode generated the central theological problem: whether divinely granted knowledge may suspend ordinary legal judgment. Classical Sunni legal theology generally denied that private claims of Khidrian inspiration authorize violation of revealed law. **[E2/E4]**

### The wall

In a settlement whose people refuse hospitality, Khidr repairs a collapsing wall without demanding payment. Moses suggests that he could have taken wages. Khidr explains that the wall belonged to two orphan boys; treasure beneath it had been left by their righteous father, and God intended the boys to recover it at maturity. **Qurʾān 18:77–82. [E1]**

Exegetes speculated about the town, the nature of the treasure, and the father’s identity. These names and locations are not Qurʾanic facts. **[E2]**

### Separation

After the third protest Khidr ends the companionship and supplies the promised *taʾwīl*, disclosure of the acts’ true outcome. He states that he did not act on his own authority. The Qurʾan says nothing further about his life or death. **[E1]**

### Alexander and the Water of Life

In the developed romance, Alexander enters the Land of Darkness seeking the Water of Life. Khidr, serving as guide or advance scout, recognizes the spring when a dried or salted fish revives, drinks or bathes, and becomes immortal. Alexander cannot recover the spring. **[E2/E6]**

The motif’s documented earlier relatives are the Greek/Syriac Alexander cook-and-fish story. Modern scholarship generally sees a literary relationship among the Qurʾanic fish episode, late-antique Alexander materials, and later Islamic exegesis, but disagrees about exact direction, intermediary forms, and whether the Qurʾanic Moses episode directly reworks the Alexander story. Brannon Wheeler particularly cautions that later exegetes—not the Qurʾan itself—made the explicit Water-of-Life and Alexander linkage. **[E8]**

### Immortality and appearances

Reports that Khidr remains physically alive until the end of time are post-Qurʾanic. Medieval Sufis and authorities including Ibn al-Ṣalāḥ and al-Nawawī accepted or favored continued life, appealing especially to accumulated encounter testimony. Hadith critics including Ibn al-Jawzī, Ibn Kathīr, and Ibn Ḥajar rejected the relevant longevity reports as inauthentic; other polemical lists attach Ibn Taymiyya and Ibn al-Qayyim to the rejection. Qurʾān 21:34 and the hadith that nobody then alive would remain after one hundred years were used against immortality. **[E4]**

Accordingly:

- “Khidr is immortal” is a major medieval belief. **[E2/E4/E7]**
- “The Qurʾan says Khidr is immortal” is false.  
- No securely attested tomb can be reconciled straightforwardly with the simultaneously widespread belief that he never died.

## 4. Relations with God, prophets, heroes, and saints

### Genealogical position

No genealogy appears in the Qurʾan or sound core hadith. Later trees make Khidr:

1. **Baliyā ibn Malkān**, descendant of Noah through Shem and Arpachshad;
2. a man of Abraham’s generation whose father followed Abraham;
3. a descendant of Cain;
4. a direct son of Adam;
5. son of Pharaoh’s daughter;
6. Persian, Arab, or Israelite.

These should be treated as competing medieval efforts to insert a free-floating sacred figure into sacred history. **[E2/E6]**

## 5. Cult, shrines, tombs, and ritual

Khidr’s cult is anomalous if defined narrowly as worship at the grave of a dead saint: many devotees regard him as alive and mobile. His sacred geography therefore favors **maqāms**—stations, places of appearance, footprints, caves, springs, riverbanks, islands, sea-coasts, and prayer sites—rather than a single defensible tomb. **[E7]**

### Levant and Iraq

Muslim visitors have shared several Saint George sanctuaries, including al-Khaḍr near Bethlehem and other sites in Palestine, Jordan, and the Antioch/Hatay region. Healing, fertility, protection, vows, and relief from mental illness are recurring functions. These practices document convergence of cult; they do not prove that George and Khidr began as one ancient deity. **[E5/E7]**

At Karak in Transjordan, control of an al-Khidr sanctuary became the focus of serious Christian–Muslim and tribal conflict in 1937–1939. The Mandate-era dispute became a national political issue, and the government attempted to regulate or nationalize the symbolic territory. This is a documented political use of Khidr’s shrine rather than an ancient mythic event. **[E9]**

Baghdad has a Khidr–Elias complex beside the Tigris, illustrating the recurrent pairing of the two immortals and Khidr’s connection to waterways. Claims that such buildings contain his body belong to shrine tradition and conflict with the living-Khidr belief. **[E3/E7]**

### Anatolia and the Balkans: Hıdırellez

Hıdırellez is observed annually around 6 May as the coming of spring. Its name compounds Hızır and İlyas, protectors respectively associated with earth and water in the official UNESCO description. Rituals seek fertility, prosperity, protection of crops and animals, healing, marriage, and fulfilled wishes. Practices include outdoor meals, greenery, fires, water, written or modelled wishes, and visits to sacred places. **[E7]**

Türkiye and North Macedonia successfully nominated the celebration to UNESCO’s Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity in 2017. UNESCO registration documents a living community tradition; it does not certify any proposed ancient origin. **[E9]**

The coincidence between Ottoman Hıdırellez, Orthodox Saint George’s spring feast, and Balkan Đurđevdan/Erdelezi facilitated mutual influence. A single uninterrupted pagan “Green Man” survival is a much stronger claim than the evidence permits. **[E5/E8]**

### Iran, Afghanistan, and Central Asia

Khidr/Kheżr is associated with vegetation, water, deserts, rescue, navigation, eschatology, and seasonal renewal. Places where he allegedly prayed or appeared become local sanctuaries. Persian proverbial and poetic tradition says that spring follows his footsteps. **[E3/E7]**

### South Asia

In Sindh, **Khwāja Khizr** became patron of the Indus, sailors, boatmen, traders, and water-carriers. Shrines may depict or imagine him upon a fish. The island shrine near Rohri/Sukkur is traditionally associated with Khwāja Khizr or Zinda Pīr; one published study reports a date of 952 CE attached to the site, but that date should be treated as a shrine-historical claim requiring epigraphic verification, not as proof that the extant complex is unchanged from the tenth century. **[E3/E7]**

Jhūlelāl, Uderolāl, Daryā Shāh, Zinda Pīr, Sheikh Tāhir, and Khwāja Khizr form a contested interreligious complex:

- Hindu Sindhis may treat Jhūlelāl as a manifestation of Varuṇa and river savior.
- Muslims may identify the saint as Sheikh Tāhir or Khwāja Khizr.
- Shared sites do not mean all worshippers accept an identical theology.
- A colonial-period dispute over shrine control at Sukkur reportedly led to a separate mainland Hindu site in the late nineteenth century. **[E3/E5/E7]**

### Morocco and the western Islamic world

Khidr belief is broadly present in the Maghreb through Sufi storytelling, reported apparitions, maritime protection, saint veneration, and the widespread designation of sacred sites as Khidr stations. The present search did not locate sufficiently strong documentation for a single Moroccan “tomb of Khidr” comparable to the named Levantine and Sindhi cases. Claims of an unbroken chain of Khidr shrines “from Morocco to India” are best understood as a description of geographic diffusion, not evidence of one centrally organized cult. **[E7; absence of securely localized evidence noted]**

### Images

Persian and Mughal manuscripts commonly show Khidr in green garments, at the Water of Life, accompanying Alexander, or standing/riding upon a fish. A seventeenth-century Mughal image in the Victoria and Albert Museum’s Small Clive Album shows the developed fish-riding iconography. The Qurʾan never describes Khidr’s appearance or puts him on a fish; the visual type belongs to Persianate and South Asian reception. **[E6]**

Ananda Coomaraswamy’s 1934 study, “Khwāja Khadir and the Fountain of Life in the Tradition of Persian and Mughal Art,” was formative in cataloguing this iconography, though some of its broad comparative conclusions reflect early twentieth-century perennialist scholarship. **[E8]**

## 6. Historical evolution

### 1. Qurʾanic nucleus, early seventh century

An unnamed divinely taught servant instructs Moses through three morally disturbing acts whose concealed outcomes vindicate divine knowledge. No genealogy, greenness, immortality, Alexander, Elijah, Saint George, shrine, or cult appears. **[E1]**

### 2. Naming and exegetical stabilization, eighth–tenth centuries

Early tafsīr and hadith identify the servant as al-Khaḍir, name Joshua, explain the epithet through miraculous vegetation, and frame the journey as Moses’ lesson in humility. Al-Ṭabarī preserves genealogies and begins integrating Khidr into universal history. **[E1/E2]**

### 3. Prophetic biography and romance, tenth–thirteenth centuries

Qiṣaṣ collections convert allusions into expansive sacred history. Arabic and Persian Alexander traditions attach Khidr to Iskandar and the Water of Life. The successful immortal guide contrasts with the king whose political power cannot conquer death. **[E2/E6]**

### 4. Sufi transformation, twelfth–fifteenth centuries

Khidr becomes an initiator outside ordinary institutional chains, a living guide, revealer of inward knowledge, and patron of solitary seekers. ʿAṭṭār, Ibn ʿArabī, Rūmī, and later Persian poets establish the imagery of the guide, darkness, spring, and living water. **[E4]**

Ibn ʿArabī recounts an investiture near Mosul in 601/1204: ʿAlī ibn Jāmiʿ, who claimed investiture by Khidr, clothed Ibn ʿArabī in the same place and spiritual state. Some retellings compress this into “Khidr personally gave Ibn ʿArabī the mantle”; the surviving account includes an intermediary and describes the garment in one version as a small cotton cap. **[E4]**

### 5. Localization and shared cult, medieval–early modern periods

Shrines and appearance-sites proliferate from the Balkans and Levant through Iran and Central Asia to Sindh. Khidr merges locally with Elijah, Saint George, river saints, and spring festivals. The fusion is not uniform: distinct figures can share a shrine without becoming doctrinally identical. **[E3/E5/E7]**

### 6. Early-modern visual canon

Safavid, Ottoman, and Mughal art standardizes the green-robed sage and aquatic guide. South Asian images place Khidr on a fish, making visible his mastery of water and the Fountain of Life. **[E6]**

### 7. Reformist contestation, nineteenth–twentieth centuries

Modernist and hadith-oriented critics attack claims of present bodily life, shrine cults, and supernatural meetings as weakly transmitted or superstitious. Sufis and popular devotees continue citing the abundance of encounter testimony. Colonial administration and emerging nation-states regulate shared shrines and communal ownership. **[E8/E9]**

### 8. Contemporary survival

Khidr remains active in sermons, dream reports, Sufi initiation narratives, Hıdırellez celebrations, shared pilgrimage sites, poetry, children’s literature, visual culture, and online esotericism. UNESCO recognition has reframed Hıdırellez as heritage as well as devotion. **[E7/E9]**

## 7. Modern reception and reinvention

### Muhammad Iqbal

Iqbal’s Urdu poem **“Khizr-i Rāh”** was recited in Lahore in April 1922 and published in *Bāng-i darā* in 1924. The poet questions Khidr about life, government, labor, capital, and the Muslim world after the First World War. Khidr becomes a political oracle diagnosing imperialism, capitalism, the Ottoman collapse, and Muslim decline. This is a deliberate modern reuse of the guide—not an inherited episode from medieval legend. **[E9]**

Because Iqbal later became Pakistan’s national poet, the poem also participates in modern Muslim political self-definition. It should not, however, be reduced to a simple Pakistan-nationalist tract: its initial context was pan-Islamic, anti-imperial, and pre-Partition. **[E9]**

### Visual and museum reception

Modern museum catalogues and educational writing frequently reproduce Mughal Khidr-on-a-fish imagery as the representative picture of the figure. This can obscure the fact that the iconography is regional and early modern rather than Qurʾanic. **[E9]**

### Comparative religion and the “Green Man”

Twentieth-century writers often treated Khidr as the Islamic form of a universal vegetation spirit or “Green Man,” connecting him to Osiris, Tammuz, Baal, Glaukos, Saint George, Elijah, or pre-Islamic river gods. Shared green, aquatic, death-and-renewal imagery makes comparison possible; it does not establish direct descent. The grandest continuity theories exceed the datable evidence. **[E8]**

### Psychology and perennialism

Jungian and perennialist receptions treat Khidr as the Self, the inner guide, the archetypal “wise old man,” or a transreligious initiator. Such readings belong to modern depth psychology and esotericism. They are not recoveries of the Qurʾan’s original historical meaning. **[E9]**

### Contemporary polemic

The killing of the boy is now invoked by Muslim apologists, ex-Muslim critics, Islamophobic writers, philosophical ethicists, and reformist interpreters. Some critics present it as approval for killing unbelieving children; classical interpretation generally treated it as a unique act performed under divine command, not a legal precedent. Conversely, apologetic retellings that silently turn the child into an already-convicted adult conceal the Qurʾan’s deliberately shocking presentation. **[E9]**

Claims circulating online that Khidr is straightforwardly the Canaanite Baal, an extraterrestrial, a time traveler, or the occult ruler of the world are modern speculative constructions unless tied to an identifiable older text. **[E9]**

## 8. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Is Khidr a historical person?

There is no independent contemporary record of a human teacher named al-Khidr living with Moses or Alexander. Historical-critical scholarship therefore studies the figure’s textual formation rather than reconstructing a birth-to-death life. Medieval genealogies demonstrate attempts to historicize him, not confirmation that they succeeded. **[E8]**

### Sources of the Qurʾanic narrative

A. J. Wensinck and other early comparativists divided the story among three antecedent complexes:

1. the Alexander Romance’s revived fish and Water of Life;
2. Gilgamesh/Utnapishtim traditions of the distant immortal;
3. Jewish stories in which Elijah performs inexplicable acts later explained to a human companion.

The model remains influential but requires qualification. The familiar Elijah–Rabbi Joshua ben Levi story survives in relatively late forms and may itself reflect Islamic influence. Roger Paret and others have instead emphasized a Christian monastic tale attributed to John Moschus in which an anonymous angel performs troubling acts and later explains them. Exact literary dependence remains disputed. **[E8]**

The strongest result is motif-level, not a complete stemma: late-antique Near Eastern traditions already combined distant travel, revived fish, hidden wisdom, paradoxical divine justice, and death-defying water. The Qurʾan gives these materials a distinctive Moses-centered theological form. Later Muslim exegetes then reconnected the passage explicitly to Alexander. **[E8]**

### Was he prophet or saint?

Arguments for prophethood:

- the servant receives knowledge directly from God;
- he acts “not on my own command”;
- killing an apparently innocent person would require revelation;
- Moses, himself a prophet, temporarily becomes his student.

Arguments for sainthood:

- the Qurʾan calls him “servant,” not *nabī* or *rasūl*;
- God can grant inspiration and saintly knowledge without prophethood;
- later Sufi experience places him among the *awliyāʾ*.

Other traditions call him an angel or liminal celestial being. No consensus can be projected backward into the Qurʾan. **[E2/E4]**

### Is he alive?

The evidence separates cleanly:

- Qurʾān 18 does not say.
- The Water-of-Life story explains immortality only in later tradition.
- Sufi encounter testimony strongly sustained belief that he remained alive.
- Hadith critics found no sound report proving indefinite bodily life.
- Claimed tombs and continued appearances coexist because shrines can mark presence rather than burial.

Thus “alive” is a major doctrinal and devotional position, while “historically demonstrated immortal” is not. **[E4/E7/E8]**

### Antinomianism and authority

The Moses–Khidr story posed a persistent problem: can inward knowledge override law? Sufi writers used Khidr to legitimate knowledge outside formal instruction, but mainstream jurists generally denied that anyone after revelation could cite private inspiration to damage property or kill. Critics of Sufism saw Khidrian claims as a route to imposture; Sufis replied that genuine Khidr encounters do not abolish Muḥammad’s law. **[E4]**

### Shrine studies and syncretism

Older scholarship sometimes treated shared shrines as survivals of an unchanged pagan cult. More recent anthropology emphasizes practice: communities may share space, ritual technologies, and a wonder-working personality while maintaining different narratives and identities. The Karak conflict shows that shared sanctity can generate cooperation or competition depending on property, tribe, administration, and national politics. **[E8/E9]**

### State of the evidence

- The Qurʾanic text is the secure starting point.
- Hadith and early tafsīr survive through manuscript traditions compiled well after the Qurʾan.
- Al-Ṭabarī is exceptionally valuable because he preserves divergent reports rather than only a polished legend.
- Qiṣaṣ manuscripts are numerous, mobile, and often unstable in attribution.
- Alexander materials exist in Greek, Syriac, Arabic, Persian, Ethiopic, and other recensions; dates of composition and surviving manuscripts must not be conflated.
- Shrine antiquity is often asserted through local memory; masonry, inscriptions, documentary ownership, and present cult may belong to different centuries.
- Encounter accounts document belief and religious authority, not externally verifiable meetings with an immortal.

## 10. Conclusions

The documented figure begins not as a green-robed immortal or Alexander’s guide but as the unnamed recipient of divine knowledge in Qurʾān 18. Hadith gives him a name and the greening miracle; tafsīr supplies Joshua, genealogies, locations, and theology; qiṣaṣ and universal history place him in prophetic time; Alexander romance gives him the Water of Life; Sufism makes him a living initiator; regional cult makes him protector of water, roads, crops, and supplicants; shared shrines bring him into practical equivalence with Elijah, Saint George, and Sindhi river saints; modern poetry makes him a political oracle.

There is no single coherent “life of Khidr.” His contradictions are the record:

- unnamed servant and universally recognized Khidr;
- prophet and saint;
- historical man and celestial intermediary;
- killer of a youth and embodiment of mercy;
- subordinate servant of God and teacher of Moses;
- separate from Elijah and identical with him;
- Alexander’s guide but absent from the Qurʾanic Alexander episode;
- buried at numerous shrines and still bodily alive;
- guardian of Islamic esoteric knowledge and shared saint of several religions.

The secure historical subject is not an independently verifiable immortal traveler. It is the unusually durable tradition that continually gave an anonymous Qurʾanic teacher a name, a color, an infinite lifespan, a global sacred geography, and the authority to appear whenever ordinary knowledge reached its limit.
