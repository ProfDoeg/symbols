# Dhul-Qarnayn: Research Dossier

> **Traditional class:** In the Qur’an, a divinely empowered, just ruler and culture hero; later exegetes variously class him as a righteous mortal king, prophet, angel-like being, universal monarch, or Alexanderian epic hero. He has no independently recoverable biography.  
> **Earliest secure attestation:** Qur’an 18:83–98, recited in Arabic in the early seventh century CE and incorporated into the canonical Qur’anic text. The passage names him only by the epithet **ذُو ٱلْقَرْنَيْنِ** (*Dhū l-Qarnayn*, “Possessor of the Two Horns/Two-Horned One”). No securely pre-Islamic Arabic inscription or manuscript presently attests that name.  
> **Evidence labels used below:** **[Early]** securely attested early material; **[Canonical]** Qur’anic or subsequently normative Islamic material; **[Late-antique parallel]** related Alexander material not itself Islamic; **[Exegesis]** tafsīr or transmitted report; **[Medieval elaboration]** romance, geography, *qiṣaṣ*, or epic; **[Regional variant]** locally or linguistically bounded tradition; **[Syncretism]** equation with another figure; **[Modern reconstruction]** scholarly historical hypothesis; **[Modern invention]** recent claim lacking early evidence.

---

## 1. Identification, Names and Epithets

Dhul-Qarnayn is the unnamed ruler of Qur’an 18:83–98. God has “established” him in the earth and given him “a means to everything.” He follows successive “ways” or “means” to the western limit, where he finds the sun setting in a dark or muddy spring; to the eastern limit, where it rises upon a people having no shelter from it; and finally between two mountain barriers. There a scarcely intelligible people ask protection from **Yaʾjūj and Maʾjūj**, Gog and Magog. Refusing tribute, he mobilizes their labor, fills the pass with iron blocks, heats them, and pours molten *qiṭr*—usually interpreted as copper—over them. The completed barrier cannot be scaled or pierced, but Dhul-Qarnayn declares it temporary: when God’s promise arrives, God will level it. The Qur’an supplies no personal name, ancestry, homeland, dates, consort, offspring, death, or tomb. Its interest is exemplary sovereignty subordinated to God, not biography. The Arabic text is accessible beginning at [Qur’an 18:83](https://quran.com/fa/al-kahf/83).

### Name and grammar

- **Arabic:** ذُو ٱلْقَرْنَيْنِ, *Dhū l-Qarnayn*. *Dhū* means “possessor of” or “one characterized by”; *al-qarnayn* is the oblique dual of *qarn*.

- **Literal sense:** “Possessor of the Two Horns,” conventionally “the Two-Horned One.” *Qarn* can also mean an extremity, generation, age, or epoch, enabling later explanations such as “lord of the two ends [east and west]” or “man of two ages.” The grammatical form by itself does not decide which explanation generated the epithet.

- **Common transliterations:** Dhū al-Qarnayn, Dhu’l-Qarnayn, Dhul-Qarnayn, Zulqarnain, Zülkarneyn; Persian **ذوالقرنین** (*Dhu’l-Qarnayn*) and **اسکندر** (*Iskandar/Eskandar*); Turkish *Zülkarneyn*; Urdu *Zulqarnain*.

### Explanations of the epithet

1. **Actual or represented horns.** **[Late-antique parallel / Exegesis]** Alexander was represented with the ram horns of Zeus-Ammon on Hellenistic coinage. The Syriac Alexander Legend says God caused “horns of iron” to grow for Alexander’s conquest of kingdoms. This is the strongest contextual explanation if the Qur’anic figure is the late-antique legendary Alexander.

2. **The two extremities of the world.** **[Exegesis]** Al-Ṭabarī (d. 923) and later commentators report that he was so named because he reached the eastern and western “horns” or ends of the inhabited earth.

3. **Two wounds on his temples.** **[Exegesis]** Reports attributed to ʿAlī say that his people struck one side of his head, God restored him, and they struck the other; hence “two-horned.” Al-Baghawī preserves a version in which he dies twice and is revived.

4. **Two locks or braids of hair.** **[Exegesis]** A report attributed to al-Ḥasan and transmitted by Ibn ʿAsākir explains *qarnayn* as two plaits.

5. **A horned crown or two hornlike protuberances.** **[Exegesis]** Medieval commentators record both.

6. **Two generations or ages.** **[Learned systematization]** He allegedly lived through two *qurūn* or ruled in two epochs. The currently circulating claim that the epithet “really” means only “Possessor of Two Epochs” is **[Modern reconstruction]**, not an established correction of the traditional translation.

7. **East and west, light and darkness, or two domains.** **[Medieval elaboration]** These symbolic readings arise from his journeys rather than independent early evidence.

### Classifications

- **Righteous king:** the majority tendency in tafsīr; **[Canonical interpretation]**.
- **Prophet:** inferred by some from God’s direct address in 18:86; **[Exegesis]**.
- **Angel:** a minority report; **[Exegesis]**.
- **Inspired but non-prophetic servant:** attributed especially to ʿAlī in Sunni and Shiʿi transmissions; **[Exegesis]**.
- **Alexander the Great:** already explicit in the eighth-century tafsīr attributed to Muqātil ibn Sulaymān, which glosses him as “Alexander Caesar”; dominant in much medieval romance and historical writing; **[Syncretistic identification]**.
- **Ṣaʿb ibn Dhī Marāthid, a Himyarite Tubbaʿ:** developed in Yemeni genealogical tradition; **[Regional variant]**.
- **Cyrus the Great:** primarily a nineteenth- and twentieth-century Muslim proposal, popularized by Sayyid Aḥmad Khān and Abul Kalam Azad; **[Modern reconstruction]**.
- **Afrīdūn/Fereydun or an “elder Alexander”:** chronological harmonizations placing him in Abraham’s age; **[Medieval learned systematization]**.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### The Qur’an

**Qur’an 18:83–98** is the earliest secure attestation. Traditional chronology assigns Sūrat al-Kahf to Mecca, while some Islamic verse lists assign portions, including 18:83–101, to Medina. Modern relative chronologies generally retain a Meccan setting for the sura but cannot date this pericope to an exact year. Claims that it was certainly revealed in 615–619, or certainly inserted after 630, exceed the direct evidence.

The early Birmingham Qur’an leaves contain 18:17–31, not the Dhul-Qarnayn passage; they nevertheless prove early circulation of the same sura and its present sequence with Suras 19–20. The parchment was radiocarbon-dated to 568–645, but parchment age is not identical to writing date. The contents are documented by the [University of Birmingham](https://www.birmingham.ac.uk/facilities/cadbury/birmingham-quran/faqs). A more complete early witness containing the end of Sura 18 is the late-eighth-century Codex Mashhad. Thus:

- the figure is securely early seventh-century literary material;
- no extant manuscript of 18:83–98 has been demonstrated to predate the Qur’anic proclamation itself;
- absence of those verses from the Birmingham fragment is simply a consequence of which leaves survive.

### Qur’anic story, verse by verse

- **18:83–84:** questioners ask about Dhul-Qarnayn; God has established him and granted him means. **[Early/Canonical]**
- **18:85–88:** western journey; sun appears or is found setting in an *ʿayn ḥamiʾa*, “muddy/dark spring.” A canonical reading, *ḥāmiya*, gives “hot spring.” He is authorized to punish or treat a people well, and announces punishment for wrongdoing and reward for faith. **[Early/Canonical; textual reading variant]**
- **18:89–91:** eastern journey; he reaches people with no shelter from the sun. **[Early/Canonical]**
- **18:92–98:** journey between two barriers; construction of an iron-and-*qiṭr* rampart against Gog and Magog; acknowledgment that it is divine mercy and will fall at the promised time. **[Early/Canonical]**
- **18:99–102:** apocalyptic transition: peoples surge together, the trumpet sounds, and judgment follows. Dhul-Qarnayn is no longer an actor. **[Canonical context]**

### Late-antique Alexander corpus

The Qur’anic account belongs in the documentary environment of the Alexander Romance, but the components must be dated separately.

- **Greek Alexander Romance, Pseudo-Callisthenes:** core composition conventionally placed around the third century CE, with earlier Hellenistic materials. It supplies Alexander’s fantastic travels, marvels, letters, world limits, and quest traditions, but its oldest forms do **not** contain the Qur’an’s complete Gog-and-Magog barrier episode.

- **Josephus, *Jewish War* 7.7.4 and *Antiquities* 1.6.1:** first century CE. Josephus associates a pass or iron gate with Alexander and identifies Magog’s descendants with Scythians, but does not narrate the Qur’anic construction episode. **[Late-antique precursor, not direct equivalent]**

- **Syriac Alexander Romance:** a Syriac translation/adaptation, probably sixth–seventh century, perhaps mediated through Middle Persian. It is textually distinct from the short Christian Legend.

- **Neṣḥānā d-Aleksandrōs**, the Syriac Christian Legend or “Victory/Exploit of Alexander”: the closest parallel. Alexander, a monotheistic world king, travels to the setting and rising places of the sun and builds an iron-and-bronze gate against Hunnic peoples identified with Gog and Magog. God will open it at the appointed end. E. A. Wallis Budge published the Syriac and English translation in 1889; the relevant “Christian Legend” occupies pp. 144–158 in the [modern digital edition](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.31826/9781463208899/html).

- **Dating dispute:** Nöldeke, Reinink, and Kevin van Bladel placed the extant Legend about 629–630, interpreting its anti-Persian prophecy as Heraclian propaganda. Van Bladel consequently treated it as the Qur’anic story’s immediate source or near-immediate Vorlage. Tommaso Tesei formerly accepted that date but now argues that a substantial form arose under Justinian, 527–565, with a Heraclian prophecy added around 629–630. His case is developed in *The Syriac Legend of Alexander’s Gate* (2023), summarized in the [publisher’s book record](https://books.google.com/books/about/The_Syriac_Legend_of_Alexander_s_Gate.html?id=x6bVEQAAQBAJ). **The relationship is strong; the direction and precise stage of transmission remain debated.**

- **Syriac Alexander Poem/Homily:** formerly attributed to Jacob of Serugh (d. 521), but generally treated as pseudonymous and later, probably seventh century. It combines the barrier with the Water of Life cycle.

- **Pseudo-Methodius:** Syriac apocalypse, probably c. 690, which develops Alexander’s enclosure of northern nations and the last Roman emperor. It is later than the Qur’an and therefore a reception parallel, not the Qur’anic source.

### Tafsīr and Islamic narrative corpus

- **Muqātil ibn Sulaymān, *Tafsīr* (d. 767):** earliest extant sustained Qur’anic commentary; explicitly calls Dhul-Qarnayn Alexander. **[Early exegesis]**

- **Al-Ṭabarī, *Jāmiʿ al-bayān* (completed early tenth century):** preserves mutually incompatible reports: Roman youth and founder of Alexandria; righteous king; stories from Wahb ibn Munabbih; explanations of the epithet; and extensive ethnographic detail. Al-Ṭabarī records rather than fully harmonizes them. His Arabic commentary on 18:83 is available [here](https://quran.ksu.edu.sa/tafseer/tabary/sura18-aya83.html).

- **Al-Ṭabarī, *History of Prophets and Kings* (completed 915):** separates or duplicates traditions: Dhul-Qarnayn “the Elder,” associated with Abraham and sometimes Afrīdūn, and the later Macedonian Alexander. Al-Khidr commands the elder king’s vanguard in one recension. **[Learned harmonization]**

- **Ibn Hishām, *Kitāb al-Tījān* (“Book of Crowns,” transmitted under his name, ninth century):** presents Yemeni traditions and argues that Dhul-Qarnayn was the Himyarite Ṣaʿb ibn Dhī Marāthid. **[Regional variant]**

- **Al-Thaʿlabī, *ʿArāʾis al-majālis fī qiṣaṣ al-anbiyāʾ* (early eleventh century):** places Dhul-Qarnayn among prophetic history and elaborates journeys, angels, Khidr, and the Water of Life. **[Medieval canonical elaboration within *qiṣaṣ* literature]**

- **Al-Kisāʾī, *Qiṣaṣ al-anbiyāʾ*:** an unstable medieval compilation attributed to al-Kisāʾī; manuscript recensions vary. It is evidence for later story circulation, not for the seventh-century form.

- **Al-Qurṭubī (d. 1273):** inventories the competing classifications and names. He notes that the report making Dhul-Qarnayn a young Roman who founded Alexandria has a weak chain; his commentary is preserved [online](https://quran.ksu.edu.sa/tafseer/qortobi/sura18-aya83.html).

- **Ibn Kathīr (d. 1373):** sharply distinguishes the Qur’anic righteous Dhul-Qarnayn from Alexander son of Philip, the pupil of Aristotle. He posits an earlier Alexander in Abraham’s age. **[Theological harmonization]**

### Arabic and Persian romances

- **Qissat Dhī l-Qarnayn / Ḥadīth Dhī l-Qarnayn:** medieval Arabic and Hispano-Arabic Alexander narratives. One *Qissa* recension gives four alternative deaths, including death at Dūmat al-Jandal. These are not hadith in the canonical technical sense.

- **Firdawsī, *Shāhnāma* (completed c. 1010):** Iskandar is not simply an alien Macedonian. He becomes the son of the Iranian Dārāb and Philip’s daughter Nāhīd, hence half-brother of Dārā; he conquers Iran, visits the Kaʿba, travels through marvel-filled lands, searches unsuccessfully for the Water of Life, and builds the barrier. **[Persian epic syncretism]**

- **Anonymous Persian *Iskandar-nāma*:** probably eleventh century in its earliest form, surviving in later manuscripts. It expands Iskandar into a Muslim universal king, explorer, and wonder hero.

- **Niẓāmī Ganjavī, *Iskandar-nāma* (late twelfth century):** two poems, the *Sharaf-nāma* and *Iqbāl-nāma*. Iskandar progresses from conqueror to philosopher-king and prophet-like sage. The corpus and its textual instability are summarized by [Encyclopaedia Iranica](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/eskandar-nama-of-nezami/).

- **Amīr Khusraw, *Āʾīna-yi Iskandarī* (1299):** reshapes Alexander as an exemplary sovereign and inventor.

- **Jāmī, *Khirad-nāma-yi Iskandarī* (fifteenth century):** makes him a frame for philosophical wisdom.

The *Sīrat ʿAntar*, the *Muʿallaqāt*, Layla and Majnun traditions, and *The Thousand and One Nights* do not constitute primary Dhul-Qarnayn sources. Their inclusion in a general “Arabian heroes” bibliography must not be mistaken for evidence about this figure.

## 3. The Story

### A. Qur’anic cycle

#### Commission and power

God has given Dhul-Qarnayn earthly power and *sabab*, a means, route, or instrument. The text never describes a coronation, army, parents, miraculous birth, or divine descent. Later claims that he possessed supernatural clouds, ropes to heaven, or universal linguistic knowledge are **[Exegesis]**, not Qur’anic statements.

#### Western journey

He follows a route until he finds the sun setting “in a muddy/dark spring” (*ʿayn ḥamiʾa*) or, under the canonical reading *ḥāmiya*, “a hot spring.” The verse states what he “found” or perceived at the world’s western limit. Medieval cosmographers alternately literalized the spring and interpreted it perspectivally as the ocean horizon.

A people live there. God—or an angel speaking for God in some exegesis—gives him a choice: punish or treat them well. Dhul-Qarnayn establishes a judicial rule: the wrongdoer is punished and returned to God; the believer who acts rightly receives a good reward. **The Qur’an does not say he massacres the people, forcibly converts them, or conquers a named kingdom.**

#### Eastern journey

At the rising place of the sun he finds a people for whom God has made no protection against it. The Qur’an does not name or morally condemn them. Tafsīr supplies huts, tunnels, nakedness, primitive conditions, or peoples who hide in water; these are **[Later ethnographic elaborations]**.

#### The northern or intermontane journey

The text does not say “north,” although late-antique and medieval geography places the barrier northward. Between two *saddayn*—barriers or mountains—he encounters a people who scarcely understand speech. They describe Gog and Magog as *mufsidūn fī l-arḍ*, corrupters in the land, and offer tribute for a barrier.

Dhul-Qarnayn refuses payment: what God has established him in is better. He asks only for labor or strength. He piles iron blocks between the mountain sides, orders bellows until the mass becomes fire, and pours *qiṭr* over it. Gog and Magog cannot climb or pierce it.

His final speech denies permanent human mastery: “This is a mercy from my Lord”; when God’s promise comes, God will reduce it to rubble. The adjoining apocalyptic verses describe universal upheaval and judgment.

### B. Alexanderian cycle

The Syriac Legend supplies a closely matching itinerary: Alexander prays to the one God, travels to the fetid sea at the setting sun and the land of sunrise, reaches a mountain pass, constructs an iron-and-bronze gate, and inscribes predictions concerning the release of northern peoples. Differences are as important as similarities:

- the Syriac hero is explicitly Alexander; the Qur’an withholds the name;
- the Legend identifies and catalogs enclosed Hunnic nations; the Qur’an names only Gog and Magog;
- the Legend’s gate bears detailed political prophecies; the Qur’an replaces them with a concise statement of divine eschatological sovereignty;
- the Qur’an adds the just-ruler judgment scene and refusal of tribute;
- the Syriac text’s prophecy serves Roman imperial ideology; the Qur’an subordinates the ruler and barrier to God.

Thus “the Syriac Legend of c. 630 was the source” is a major scholarly thesis, not an uncontested datum. The demonstrable result is a shared narrative complex; direct literary dependence on the extant Syriac recension is probable to many specialists but cannot be physically proved by a lost intermediary.

### C. Water of Life cycle

The Qur’an never says Dhul-Qarnayn sought immortality and never names Khidr in his story. The preceding Qur’anic episode, 18:60–82, concerns Moses and an unnamed divine servant later called al-Khidr. Medieval storytelling fused that servant with Alexander’s cook or guide from the Romance.

In al-Ṭabarī, al-Thaʿlabī, Persian epics, and later *qiṣaṣ*, Dhul-Qarnayn learns from an angel of the Water of Life in the Land of Darkness. Khidr guides the vanguard, discovers the spring when a dead or salted fish revives, drinks or bathes, and becomes immortal; Dhul-Qarnayn misses it. A long version gives the advising angel the name **Rafāʾīl/Raphael** or **Ranaqīl**. Ibn Kathīr records the tale but treats its transmission cautiously. **[Medieval elaboration derived from Alexander-romance material]**

### D. Birth and parentage variants

| Version | Parentage | Source and date | Tier |
|---|---|---|---|
| Qur’anic | None supplied | Qur’an 18, early 7th c. | **Early/Canonical** |
| Macedonian | Philip II; later romance often makes Nectanebo the secret father | Greek and Syriac Alexander Romance, 3rd c. onward | **Late-antique romance** |
| Persian-national | Dārāb of Iran and Nāhīd, daughter of Philip; Alexander and Dārā are half-brothers | Firdawsī, c. 1010; later romances | **Persian syncretism** |
| Himyarite | Ṣaʿb ibn Dhī Marāthid, a Tubbaʿ, with invented South Arabian genealogy | Ibn Hishām’s *Tījān*, 9th c.; al-Hamdānī, 10th c.; Nashwān, 12th c. | **Regional variant** |
| Abrahamic “elder Alexander” | Lineages variously run through Greek, Persian, or biblical ancestors | al-Ṭabarī and later harmonizers | **Learned systematization** |
| Cyrus | Cambyses’ Achaemenid son, identified through Danielic ram symbolism | modern Muslim interpreters, 19th–20th c. | **Modern reconstruction** |

### E. Death and body

- **Qur’an:** entirely silent. No death, grave, relic, embalming, or posthumous cult. **[Early negative finding]**
- **Historical Alexander:** died at Babylon in 323 BCE; body eventually associated with Alexandria. This is evidence about Alexander III, not automatically Dhul-Qarnayn.
- **Romance:** Alexander dies after omens and failed quests; details vary by recension.
- **Firdawsī and Niẓāmī:** death becomes a moral demonstration that universal conquest cannot defeat mortality.
- **Arabic *Qissa* variants:** include death at Dūmat al-Jandal and other locations. **[Medieval accretion]**
- No claimed “tomb of Dhul-Qarnayn” has archaeological documentation connecting it to the Qur’anic figure.

## 4. Relations with Gods and Other Heroes

### Genealogical position

The Qur’an gives no genealogy. Consequently every tree is secondary:

```text
Qur’an:
    [no parents] — Dhul-Qarnayn — [no consort or children]

Macedonian historical/romance:
    Philip II (or secretly Nectanebo) + Olympias
                         |
                     Alexander

Persian epic:
    Dārāb + Nāhīd, daughter of Philip
             |
          Iskandar —— half-brother of Dārā

Himyarite:
    invented Tubbaʿ genealogy
             |
    Ṣaʿb ibn Dhī Marāthid = Dhul-Qarnayn
```

No Qur’anic evidence supports a consort, children, demigod ancestry, or descent from Zeus-Ammon. The Persian and Arabic romances sometimes supply wives, dynastic relations, or royal kin, but these belong to their Iskandar cycles.

### Documented interactions

Each line records a direct interaction or explicit relationship, not merely a modern identification.

- **God/Allah** — grants Dhul-Qarnayn power and means; authorizes judgment at the western limit; Dhul-Qarnayn attributes the barrier to divine mercy and its destruction to God’s promise. Qur’an 18:84–88, 98, early seventh century. **[Early/Canonical]**

- **Yaʾjūj (Gog)** — named with Magog as a corrupting people against whom he constructs the barrier. Qur’an 18:94–97. No face-to-face meeting is narrated. **[Early/Canonical]**

- **Maʾjūj (Magog)** — same interaction and limitation. Qur’an 18:94–97. **[Early/Canonical]**

- **The unnamed people at the western spring** — Dhul-Qarnayn judges them by wrongdoing versus faith and righteous action. Qur’an 18:86–88. **[Early/Canonical]**

- **The unnamed eastern people** — he reaches them; no combat or speech is reported. Qur’an 18:90–91. **[Early/Canonical]**

- **The scarcely intelligible people between the barriers** — they request a defensive work and offer tribute; he rejects payment but directs their labor. Qur’an 18:93–96. **[Early/Canonical]**

- **Alexander the Great** — no interaction: medieval authors identify the two figures. This is an equation, not a meeting. Muqātil, eighth century onward. **[Syncretism]**

- **Al-Khidr** — minister, guide, or vanguard commander; finds the Water of Life that eludes Dhul-Qarnayn. Al-Ṭabarī’s *History*, al-Thaʿlabī, Firdawsī, anonymous *Iskandar-nāma*, tenth–thirteenth centuries. **[Medieval elaboration]**

- **Raphael/Rafāʾīl or Ranaqīl** — angelic companion who tells him of the Water of Life and, in some versions, gives him a wondrous stone. Ibn ʿAsākir material repeated by Ibn Kathīr; *qiṣaṣ* recensions. **[Medieval elaboration; unstable name]**

- **Abraham/Ibrāhīm** — Dhul-Qarnayn allegedly embraces Abraham’s faith and circumambulates the Kaʿba with him. Reports cited by al-Azraqī and later Ibn Kathīr. **[Medieval chronological harmonization]**

- **Ishmael/Ismāʿīl** — appears with Abraham in Kaʿba-building or circumambulation reports. Later historical-exegetical transmission. **[Medieval elaboration]**

- **Moses/Mūsā** — no Qur’anic meeting. Medieval texts sometimes conflate or coordinate the preceding Moses–servant episode with the Dhul-Qarnayn cycle. **[Later harmonization]**

- **Elijah/Ilyās** — joins Khidr in some Persian illustrated and literary traditions; a Safavid *Fālnāma* painting places Khidr and Ilyas beside Iskandar at the wall. **[Regional artistic syncretism]**

- **Dārā/Darius** — adversary and, in the Persian epic genealogy, half-brother; Iskandar defeats him and encounters him dying after betrayal by his own men. Firdawsī, c. 1010, drawing on Iranian national tradition. **[Persian epic]**

- **Philip/Filfūs** — maternal grandfather or putative father depending recension. Firdawsī and Persian romances. **[Persian epic]**

- **Nāhīd** — mother in the *Shāhnāma* cycle, daughter of Philip and wife of Dārāb. Firdawsī, c. 1010. **[Persian epic]**

- **Aristotle** — counselor and philosopher associated with Iskandar in Arabic-Persian wisdom literature. The Qur’an never mentions him; Ibn Taymiyya and Ibn Kathīr use the historical Alexander’s association with Aristotle to distinguish him from the earlier righteous Dhul-Qarnayn. **[Medieval learned tradition]**

- **Porus/Fūr** — Indian king fought by Iskandar in the *Shāhnāma* and Alexander romances. **[Alexanderian epic, not Qur’anic]**

- **Queen Qaydāfa** — politically outwits or tests Iskandar in Persian romance. Firdawsī and later *Iskandar-nāmas*. **[Persian epic]**

- **Solomon/Sulaymān** — no narrative meeting. Exegetes sometimes list Solomon and Dhul-Qarnayn as the two believing universal kings, opposed to Nimrod and Nebuchadnezzar. **[Learned classification, not interaction]**

- **Nimrod and Nebuchadnezzar** — paired contrastively with him as unbelieving world kings; no direct encounter. Tenth-century exegetical tradition. **[Learned classification]**

No securely early source records interaction with a goddess, consort, biological child, or named companion other than figures added by the Alexander and *qiṣaṣ* traditions.

## 5. Cult, Tomb and Ritual

### Cult

There is no evidence of a distinct ancient or medieval **cult of Dhul-Qarnayn** comparable to a Greek hero cult:

- no excavated *heroon* securely dedicated to him;
- no dedicatory inscriptions;
- no independently attested sacrifice, priesthood, annual festival, oracle, or relic cult;
- no securely identified tomb;
- no Qur’anic command to invoke him.

This negative finding must be distinguished from the cults and civic commemorations of historical Alexander, and from shrines connected with al-Khidr.

### Devotional reception

Sūrat al-Kahf is widely recited, especially on Friday, but this is devotion to Qur’anic revelation as a whole, not a cult of Dhul-Qarnayn. Sermons use him as a model of power, justice, engineering, and humility before God. Children’s narratives and popular preaching often call him a prophet, although the classical classification remained disputed.

### Walls as material identifications

Several real fortifications were interpreted as his barrier:

- **Caucasus/Derbent:** the Sasanian walls and gates at Darband were repeatedly called Alexander’s Gate or Dhul-Qarnayn’s barrier. The structures are historical late-antique defenses, not works of Alexander III.

- **Darial Pass:** another Caucasian candidate in Christian and Muslim geography.

- **Gorgan Wall:** occasionally proposed because of its scale and northern defensive function; archaeologically Sasanian.

- **Great Wall of China or Yumenguan:** medieval itineraries and modern speculation sometimes identify these with the barrier. No inscription connects their construction to Dhul-Qarnayn.

- **Central Asian passes:** localized through itineraries derived from Alexander lore.

### Sallām the Interpreter

Ibn Khurradādhbih’s ninth-century *Kitāb al-masālik wa-l-mamālik* reports that Caliph al-Wāthiq, alarmed by a dream that the wall had opened, sent the multilingual Sallām with fifty men. Sallām traveled through Caucasian and Central Asian courts, crossed ruined and foul-smelling lands, and found an enormous iron gate maintained by hereditary guardians. They struck its lock on Mondays and Thursdays to warn Gog and Magog that guards remained. The Arabic report begins in the digitized [Ibn Khurradādhbih text](https://www.masaha.org/book/view/3950/page/168).

The expedition is traditionally dated c. 842–845. Its historicity is disputed. Van Donzel and Schmidt argue that the itinerary contains geographically recoverable elements and may terminate near China’s Jade Gate, while its architecture and ethnography are literary. Their study’s scope is summarized by [Brill/Google Books](https://books.google.com.ng/books?id=_xqwCQAAQBAJ). Later geographers largely reproduce Ibn Khurradādhbih rather than provide independent eyewitness confirmation.

## 6. Historical Evolution of the Figure

### Before Islam: formation of the Alexander complex

From Hellenistic royal imagery onward, Alexander acquired Ammon’s horns, cosmic conquest, journeys beyond ordinary geography, and associations with remote gates. Jewish and Christian apocalyptic traditions added Gog and Magog, while Syriac writers Christianized Alexander into a servant of the one God. **The exact compound corresponding to the Qur’an is late antique, not a report from Alexander’s lifetime.**

### Early seventh century: Qur’anic reformulation

The Qur’an condenses the cosmic Alexander material into three journeys and a moral-political lesson. It suppresses Alexander’s name, pagan divine parentage, and dynastic ambition; emphasizes justice, refusal of tribute, cooperative labor, and the temporary character of imperial technology.

### Eighth–tenth centuries: identification and diversification

Muqātil identifies him as Alexander. Al-Ṭabarī preserves Roman, Persian, biblical, and Arabian accounts. Yemeni scholars promote Ṣaʿb ibn Dhī Marāthid. The multiplication of an “elder” and “younger” Alexander solves the problem that the Qur’anic king is pious whereas the historical Macedonian was polytheist and lived after the conventionally dated Abraham.

### Ninth–twelfth centuries: geography and *qiṣaṣ*

Sallām’s supposed expedition turns apocalyptic geography into Abbasid state intelligence. Al-Thaʿlabī and related collections integrate Dhul-Qarnayn with prophets, angels, Khidr, the Water of Life, and marvelous peoples. The wall is localized successively in the Caucasus, Central Asia, and China.

### Persian epic synthesis

Firdawsī Iranianizes Alexander genealogically while retaining the trauma of Persia’s defeat. Niẓāmī turns him into an ideal ruler, philosopher, seeker, and prophet-like world sage. The figure is no longer merely the builder of one wall but a vehicle for kingship theory, cosmology, travel literature, and ethical instruction. The development of the Persian romance is surveyed by [Encyclopaedia Iranica](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/eskandar-nama/).

### Mongol to early-modern period

The apparent eruption of northern peoples gave Gog-and-Magog traditions renewed political force. Persian, Ottoman, Mughal, and Central Asian manuscripts repeatedly pictured Iskandar supervising the wall. Timur and other rulers adopted Alexanderian universal-conquest imagery; occasional use of “Dhul-Qarnayn” as a royal title is political imitation, not evidence for the Qur’anic hero’s historicity.

### Modern period

Historical criticism reconnected the Qur’anic account with the Syriac Alexander corpus. At the same time, modern Muslim interpreters increasingly rejected Alexander because the historical conqueror was pagan, proposing Cyrus or an unknown righteous ruler. Iranian religious nationalism made Cyrus especially attractive because it could unite Qur’anic legitimacy with pre-Islamic national heritage.

## 7. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Art and material reception

The most substantial visual record represents **Iskandar as Dhul-Qarnayn**, not an independently iconographed Qur’anic hero.

- **Tabriz, c. 1330–36:** an Ilkhanid *Shāhnāma* folio shows Iskandar building the iron rampart; now in the Smithsonian. The museum identifies the object and date in [its catalogue](https://asia-archive.si.edu/learn/shahnama/s1986-104/). **[Medieval visual reception]**

- **Iran, c. 1460:** a Timurid *Shāhnāma* image shows engineers, bellows, mortar, and barrier construction; [University of Michigan Museum of Art](https://umma.umich.edu/objects/sikandar-builds-a-barrier-against-yajuj-and-majuj-from-the-shahnama-of-firdausi-1963-1-66/).

- **Qazvin, c. 1550–60:** the monumental *Fālnāma* image places Iskandar with haloed Khidr and Ilyas while demons work at the wall. **[Safavid syncretistic iconography]**

- **Mughal India, c. 1590–1600:** Alexander supervises the brazen wall in a painting probably from Niẓāmī’s *Khamsa*, catalogued by the [British Museum](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/A_1982-0529-0-1).

These paintings sometimes racialize or monsterize Gog and Magog as naked savages, demons, or grotesques. That iconography reflects medieval boundary-making and should not be projected into the Qur’an, which calls them corruptors but gives no physical description.

### Nationalism and politics

- **Iranian Cyrus identification:** Sayyid Aḥmad Khān and especially Abul Kalam Azad argued that Daniel’s two-horned ram, Cyrus’s east-west empire, biblical praise of Cyrus, and a winged, horned figure at Pasargadae identified Cyrus as Dhul-Qarnayn. The theory was adopted by some South Asian and Shiʿi commentators, including Ṭabāṭabāʾī, and became a means of reconciling Islamic sacred history with Iranian nationalism. Eliezer Merhavy analyzes this as “religious appropriation” of Cyrus in modern Iran; the political setting is summarized in [Ali Ansari’s study](https://research-repository.st-andrews.ac.uk/bitstream/handle/10023/23694/Ansari_2021_A_royal_romance_JRAS_AAM.pdf%3Bjsessionid%3D5CD63C1D10D35A076367DA3757A481C8?sequence=1).

- **Yemeni/Himyarite pride:** the Ṣaʿb identification made universal monarchy indigenous to South Arabia and used the prevalence of *Dhū-* titles in ancient Yemen as supporting rhetoric. No ancient South Arabian inscription naming Ṣaʿb “Dhul-Qarnayn” is known.

- **Islamic leadership discourse:** sermons and educational media present him as an ideal governor who rejects private profit, uses technology defensively, and credits God. This is a legitimate reception of Qur’an 18 but not historical evidence.

- **Apocalyptic politics:** Gog-and-Magog language has been mapped onto Turks, Mongols, Khazars, Europeans, Russians, Jews, Chinese, and modern geopolitical enemies. Such equations are period-specific polemic. The Qur’an does not identify a contemporary ethnicity.

### Modern media and commercial use

Dhul-Qarnayn appears mainly in Qur’anic animation, illustrated religious books, online videos, lectures, and apocalyptic popular literature. Unlike Alexander, he has not generated a stable international film or game iconography under his Qur’anic name. Modern images commonly borrow a horned helmet, Macedonian armor, Cyrus costume, or generic Islamic royal dress; none is an ancient portrait of the Qur’anic figure.

The personal name **Zulqarnain/Zülkarneyn** is common in Muslim societies. This is commemorative naming, not hero cult.

### Documented modern inventions

- “NASA/satellite imagery has found the exact wall”: **[Modern invention]** unless tied to an archaeologically published structure.
- “The barrier is invisible, underwater, at the North Pole, or in another dimension”: **[Modern invention/speculation]**.
- “Gog and Magog are certainly the Khazars, Mongols, Chinese, Russians, Jews, or a present nation”: **[Modern political identification]**, unattested in the Qur’an.
- “Cyrus is scholarly consensus”: **[Modern misstatement]**. Cyrus is influential confessional interpretation, not the dominant historical-critical conclusion.
- “The Syriac text is unquestionably from 630 and copied word-for-word by the Qur’an”: **[Overstatement]**. Dating and transmission remain debated, and the texts differ significantly.
- “Dhul-Qarnayn’s tomb has been discovered”: **[Unsupported modern claim]**.

## 8. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Was Dhul-Qarnayn Alexander?

**Evidence for an Alexanderian figure:**

1. Alexander’s two horns in royal iconography.
2. Alexanderian journeys to the world’s western and eastern limits.
3. an iron/bronze northern gate;
4. Gog and Magog as enclosed nations;
5. apocalyptic destruction of the gate;
6. monotheistic Christian transformation before or around Islam;
7. early Muslim identification by Muqātil and sustained Iskandar tradition.

Taken together, these make a legendary Alexander referent the dominant historical-critical explanation.

**Objections and qualifications:**

- the Qur’an never says “Alexander”;
- the historical Alexander was polytheist;
- the exact Syriac Legend’s date relative to the Qur’an is disputed;
- a common oral or lost written source remains possible;
- Qur’anic reshaping is theological, not simple reproduction;
- early Muslim exegetes preserved multiple identifications rather than unanimity.

The academically defensible formulation is: **Dhul-Qarnayn is best understood as the Qur’an’s reworked version of a late-antique Alexanderian world-ruler, not as a biography of Alexander III.**

### The c. 630 source thesis

Nöldeke and van Bladel regarded the extant Syriac Legend as Heraclian, c. 629–630, and as the immediate source behind Qur’an 18. Tesei’s earlier article also emphasized the chronological problem; the article is available as [“The Prophecy of Dhū-l-Qarnayn”](https://almuslih.org/wp-content/uploads/Library/Tesei%2C%20T%20-%20The%20Prophecy.pdf). Tesei’s 2023 monograph now assigns the Legend’s underlying political-narrative structure to Justinian’s reign, with later Heraclian updating. This reduces, though does not eliminate, the chronological difficulty.

No surviving sixth- or seventh-century manuscript settles the recension’s earliest form. The oldest manuscript evidence is later than both proposed composition dates. Dating therefore rests on internal political allusions, intertextual relationships, and reconstruction.

### Cyrus

Azad’s case combines:

- Daniel 8’s two-horned ram representing Media and Persia;
- Cyrus’s eastward and westward campaigns;
- Isaiah’s depiction of Cyrus as God’s anointed instrument;
- a horned figure at Pasargadae;
- Cyrus’s reputation for tolerance;
- proposed Caucasian fortification links.

Critics answer that:

- Daniel’s ram represents kingdoms and does not call Cyrus Dhul-Qarnayn;
- the Pasargadae relief is not a secure named portrait of Cyrus and its horns belong to composite royal iconography;
- Cyrus is not associated in ancient evidence with an iron-and-copper Gog-and-Magog barrier;
- the Qur’anic itinerary closely follows Alexander lore;
- Cyrus’s personal monotheism is not securely established in the form modern apologetic argument requires.

Thus Cyrus remains **[Modern confessional reconstruction]**, possible as theological identification but weak as literary history.

### Himyarite king

The Ṣaʿb theory is historically valuable as evidence for medieval Yemeni identity formation. Arguments include the South Arabian royal prefix *Dhū-*, poetry attributed to pre-Islamic Arabs, and reports assigned to Wahb ibn Munabbih or Kaʿb al-Aḥbār. Problems include late attestation, doubtful poetic authenticity, invented genealogies, and lack of epigraphic confirmation. It is a genuine medieval variant, not a securely recovered pre-Islamic king.

### The barrier

Historical candidates—Derbent, Darial, Gorgan, China—demonstrate reception history, not identification. The Qur’anic structure is built between mountains from iron and poured *qiṭr*; none of the candidates precisely matches all features or bears a contemporary Dhul-Qarnayn inscription. Real frontier works probably helped audiences localize an inherited cosmic barrier.

### Historicity

No external evidence verifies that one ruler made the three Qur’anic journeys and built the described barrier. Historical Alexander, Cyrus, Sasanian engineers, and Himyarite kings may each have contributed imagery, but “the historical Dhul-Qarnayn” is not presently recoverable. Historicity claims should therefore be separated:

- **Alexanderian literary identity:** strongly supported.
- **Identity with Alexander III as portrayed by contemporary historians:** contradicted by the legendary itinerary and theology.
- **Cyrus:** modern reconstruction without barrier evidence.
- **Ṣaʿb:** medieval regional legend without ancient attestation.
- **Unknown righteous king:** theologically possible but historically unfalsifiable.

### Finds and editions that changed discussion

- Budge’s 1889 edition made the Syriac Alexander materials widely accessible.
- Nöldeke’s 1890 study established the classical Syriac–Qur’anic comparison.
- modern study of Sasanian frontier walls undermined attribution of Caucasian defenses to Alexander;
- van Bladel’s 2008 article renewed the c. 630 source argument;
- van Donzel and Schmidt’s 2010 study re-evaluated Sallām’s journey;
- Tesei’s 2014 article and 2023 monograph reopened the chronology and proposed a Justinianic core.

## 10. Sources and Evidentiary Conclusions

### Primary-source hierarchy

1. **Qur’an 18:83–98** is the only early source that directly attests a figure called Dhul-Qarnayn.
2. The **Syriac Alexander Legend** is the closest late-antique narrative parallel; its exact composition history is contested.
3. Early tafsīr, beginning with **Muqātil** and becoming extensive in **al-Ṭabarī**, documents how Muslims identified and expanded the figure.
4. **Ibn Hishām, al-Thaʿlabī, al-Kisāʾī, Ibn Khurradādhbih, Firdawsī, Niẓāmī**, and the Arabic/Persian romances record medieval diversification.
5. Walls, manuscripts, and paintings document belief and representation, not the Qur’anic hero’s historical existence.

### Firm conclusions

- Dhul-Qarnayn is first securely known from the early seventh-century Qur’an.
- The name is an epithet, conventionally “the Two-Horned One.”
- The Qur’an classifies him functionally as a God-favored, just universal ruler, without resolving whether he is a prophet.
- The narrative has exceptionally close structural relations to late-antique Alexander lore.
- Classical Islamic tradition was diverse: Alexander was widespread, but Himyarite, Persian, and “elder Alexander” alternatives are genuinely medieval.
- Cyrus is principally a modern identification.
- The Qur’an does not connect Dhul-Qarnayn with Khidr, Abraham, Aristotle, or the Water of Life; those links are later.
- No independent archaeological evidence confirms his barrier, grave, relics, or cult.
- The historical record is therefore a history of texts, images, geography, exegesis, and political memory—not a recoverable birth-to-death life.
