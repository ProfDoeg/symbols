# The Seven Sleepers (the Companions of the Cave): Research Dossier

**Traditional class:** A collective of mortal confessors and miracle-working saint-like heroes: Christian martyr-confessors and witnesses to bodily resurrection; in Islam, righteous monotheistic youths made an *āya*, a divine sign, rather than prophets. Later Christian and Muslim practice treats them as protective holy figures.

**Earliest secure attestation:** The earliest securely datable, substantially preserved literary witness is the Syriac metrical homily on the Youths of Ephesus by Jacob of Serugh (c. 451–521), composed in the late fifth or early sixth century. It presupposes an already circulating narrative. A now-lost fifth-century Greek account, sometimes attributed to Bishop Stephen of Ephesus in 448–451, is a modern scholarly reconstruction, not an extant primary source. Archaeology confirms that the purported Ephesian cave became a Christian cemetery and pilgrimage complex in late antiquity; it does not verify the miraculous sleep.

## Evidentiary key

- **[EARLY]** Securely attested material through the seventh century.
- **[CANONICAL]** Scriptural or subsequently normative formulation.
- **[ELABORATION]** Later literary, exegetical, or hagiographic expansion.
- **[REGIONAL]** Local textual, cultic, or topographical variant.
- **[SYSTEMATIZATION]** Learned attempt to reconcile or organize variants.
- **[SYNCRETISM]** Deliberate identification with another tradition or figure.
- **[FOLKLORE]** Oral or popular practice not demonstrably part of the earliest text.
- **[RECONSTRUCTION]** Modern scholarly inference.
- **[INVENTION]** Modern or demonstrably late claim circulated as ancient.

## 1. Identification, Names and Epithets

### Identification

**[EARLY]** In the late-antique Christian legend, several Christian youths of Ephesus refuse pagan sacrifice during the persecution of the emperor Decius, hide in a cave, are enclosed there, and awaken under the Christian emperor Theodosius II. Their reappearance disproves people who deny the resurrection of the body. Jacob of Serugh’s late-fifth/early-sixth-century Syriac homily is the earliest securely datable substantial witness by a named author; Syriac historical recensions associated with Pseudo-Zacharias and John of Ephesus follow during the sixth century. The legend’s specifically Ephesian setting is therefore already fixed before Islam. [Oxford Cult of Saints database](https://portal.sds.ox.ac.uk/articles/online_resource/E02525_The_Homily_memr_on_the_Youths_of_Ephesus_the_Seven_Sleepers_of_Ephesus_S00287_is_written_in_Syriac_during_the_late_5th_early_6th_c_by_Jacob_of_Serugh_c_451-521_It_retells_the_story_of_the_young_men_who_fell_asleep_in_a_cave_during_th/13822904); [Encyclopaedia Iranica](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/ephesus/).

**[CANONICAL]** Qur’an 18:9–26 calls them *aṣḥāb al-kahf wa-l-raqīm*, “the companions/people of the cave and al-Raqīm.” It neither names Ephesus nor Decius nor any youth, and deliberately declines to settle their number by ordinary human speculation. It does mention their dog, their silver money, the dispatch of one companion to buy food, the disputed proposal to build over them, and “three hundred years, and they added nine.”

### Collective names

- **Greek:** οἱ ἑπτὰ κοιμώμενοι, *hoi hepta koimōmenoi*, “the Seven Sleepers”; also ἅγιοι ἑπτὰ παῖδες, “the Holy Seven Youths.” **[ELABORATION]** These are Byzantine and liturgical designations; the number is not uniform in the earliest eastern recensions.
- **Latin:** *Septem Dormientes Ephesi* or *Septem Dormientes apud Ephesum*, “the Seven Sleepers at Ephesus”; *pueri septem*, “seven youths.” **[ELABORATION]**
- **Syriac:** ܛܠܝ̈ܐ ܕܐܦܣܘܣ, *ṭalyē d-Efesos*, “the youths of Ephesus”; ܐܚ̈ܐ ܕܡܝܟ̈ܐ, approximately *aḥē dmīkhē*, “the sleeping brothers.” **[EARLY]**
- **Arabic:** أَصْحَابُ ٱلْكَهْف, *Aṣḥāb al-Kahf*, “Companions of the Cave”; أَهْلُ ٱلْكَهْف, *Ahl al-Kahf*, “People of the Cave”; أَصْحَابُ ٱلْكَهْفِ وَٱلرَّقِيم, *Aṣḥāb al-Kahf wa-l-Raqīm*. **[CANONICAL]**
- **Persian:** اصحاب کهف, *Aṣḥāb-e Kahf*. **[ELABORATION]**
- **Turkish:** *Yedi Uyurlar*, “Seven Sleepers,” and *Eshab-ı Kehf/Ashab-ı Kehf*. **[REGIONAL]**
- **Tunisian Arabic:** *Rgūd al-Sabʿa* or *al-Sabʿa Rgūd*, “the Seven Sleepers.” **[REGIONAL/FOLKLORE]**

### *Al-Raqīm*

**[CANONICAL]** Qur’an 18:9 joins *al-raqīm* to “the cave” without defining it. The noun belongs to the Arabic root r-q-m, “to mark, inscribe, write, number.”

**[ELABORATION]** Tafsīr preserves mutually incompatible identifications: a written tablet containing the youths’ names or story; their village; their valley; their mountain; a book or register; or a separate group. Some popular sources make *al-Raqīm* the dog’s name. No early consensus exists, and the modern Jordanian identification of al-Raqīm with al-Rajīb depends partly on phonetic resemblance and local sacred geography, not on a Qur’anic geographical statement.

### Personal names

There is no authoritative cross-traditional roster.

**[EARLY]** Gregory of Tours’ sixth-century Latin version gives forms conventionally normalized as:

1. Maximianus  
2. Malchus  
3. Martinianus  
4. Constantinus  
5. Dionysius  
6. Johannes  
7. Serapion  

**[REGIONAL]** Other Greek and Latin lists substitute Maximilianus for Maximianus, add Antonius, or use Iamblichus/Jamblichus where Gregory has Malchus.

**[EARLY/REGIONAL]** Syriac recensions sometimes count eight, the additional member being a watcher or shepherd. Their names vary considerably among manuscripts.

**[ELABORATION]** Arabic exegetical and popular rosters include such forms as:

- Maksalīnā or Maksimilīnā
- Marnūsh or Marṭūnus
- Mishlīnā
- Yamlīkhā, Tamlikhā, or Yamnikhā
- Dabarnūsh
- Shādhanūsh or Sāzanūsh
- Kafashṭaṭyūsh
- sometimes additional names, depending on the recension

The correspondences are imperfect but several are plainly Arabicized reflexes of Greek or Syriac names: Maksimilīnā/Maximilianus, Marṭūnus/Martinianus, and Yamlīkhā/Iamblichus. Bartłomiej Grysa’s comparative survey emphasizes that number, order, and spelling change across Syriac and Arabic recensions. [Grysa, comparative study](https://almuslih.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/11/Grysa-B-The-Legend-of-the-Seven-Sleepers-.pdf).

### Qitmir

**[CANONICAL]** The Qur’an mentions a dog stretched at the cave entrance but does not name it.

**[ELABORATION]** Classical and later Islamic tradition usually calls the dog قِطْمِير, *Qiṭmīr*—with Qatmīr, Kitmir, or Kıtmir as transliterations. Qur’anic Arabic *qiṭmīr* elsewhere means the thin membrane on a date stone (Q 35:13), something of negligible worldly value. Whether the dog’s name was coined from that word cannot be demonstrated.

**[REGIONAL]** Some sources instead call the dog al-Raqīm; the sixth-century *De situ terrae sanctae* tradition has been transmitted with a dog named Viricanus. Neither is Qur’anic.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### The earliest recoverable layer

**[RECONSTRUCTION]** Ernest Honigmann argued in 1953 that Bishop Stephen of Ephesus composed a Greek account around 448–451, in the context of controversy over bodily resurrection. The proposal explains the Ephesian setting and the awakening under Theodosius II, but the supposed text is lost and its authorship cannot be checked. It must not be presented as the earliest surviving source.

**[EARLY]** Jacob of Serugh’s Syriac *memrā* is the first securely attributable substantial text. Jacob died in 521. The homily already contains the stable Christian structure: persecution, refusal of idolatry, retreat to the cave, God-given sleep, awakening generations later, and testimony to bodily resurrection. There is not yet a complete modern critical edition of this homily, a serious limitation on claims about its exact earliest form. [Oxford Cult of Saints database](https://portal.sds.ox.ac.uk/articles/online_resource/E02525_The_Homily_memr_on_the_Youths_of_Ephesus_the_Seven_Sleepers_of_Ephesus_S00287_is_written_in_Syriac_during_the_late_5th_early_6th_c_by_Jacob_of_Serugh_c_451-521_It_retells_the_story_of_the_young_men_who_fell_asleep_in_a_cave_during_th/13822904).

The transmission is unusually large: catalogues enumerate more than two hundred medieval witnesses across Latin, Greek, Syriac, Arabic, Ethiopic, Coptic, Armenian, Old English, and Middle Irish, besides Sogdian, Persian, and Turkic adaptations. These totals are catalogue-dependent, not a fixed canon.

### Qur’an and Islamic corpus

**[CANONICAL]** Qur’an 18:9–26, probably proclaimed in the early seventh century, is the earliest Arabic witness. Its version is allusive: the audience is treated as already knowing that there is a disputed story. It changes the center of gravity from named Ephesian martyrs to anonymous young monotheists whose preservation demonstrates God’s guidance, knowledge, and power to raise the dead.

**[ELABORATION]** Muḥammad ibn Jarīr al-Ṭabarī’s *Jāmiʿ al-bayān* (completed before his death in 923) gathers earlier reports rather than offering one uniform story. It supplies Ephesus/Afsūs, Daqyānūs, individual names, extended speeches, the young men’s worship, and numerous explanations of *al-Raqīm*. In one transmitted account Yamlīkhā is entrusted with provisions and later enters the transformed city. [Al-Ṭabarī on Q 18:10](https://tafseerquran.com/en/tafsir/18/10).

**[ELABORATION]** Al-Thaʿlabī’s early-eleventh-century *ʿArāʾis al-majālis fī qiṣaṣ al-anbiyāʾ* and the work attributed to al-Kisāʾī expand the narrative as sacred history. Their speeches, names, court scenes, shepherd, dog, and precise geography are not independent late-antique evidence. They document medieval Islamic reception. A Leiden edition of al-Kisāʾī’s Arabic text appeared in 1922. [Digitized al-Kisāʾī edition](https://ar.wikisource.org/wiki/%D9%85%D9%84%D9%81%3A%D9%82%D8%B5%D8%B5_%D8%A7%D9%84%D8%A3%D9%86%D8%A8%D9%8A%D8%A7%D8%A1_%D9%84%D9%84%D9%83%D8%B3%D8%A7%D8%A6%D9%8A.pdf).

**Scope correction:** Luqmān, Dhū al-Qarnayn, Moses’ unnamed servant conventionally identified as al-Khiḍr, and the Companions occur in Sūrat al-Kahf or the surrounding Qur’anic corpus, but they do not interact in the text. *Sīrat ʿAntar*, the *Muʿallaqāt*, Laylā and Majnūn, the Alexander romances, and the *Thousand and One Nights* are relevant to a broader set of Arabic-hero dossiers, not primary sources for the Seven Sleepers. Treating them as part of this hero’s corpus would create false evidence.

## 3. The Story

### A. Christian cycle

#### Persecution and refusal

**[EARLY]** Jacob of Serugh and the sixth-century Syriac and Latin traditions place the youths at Ephesus under Decius, emperor from 249 to 251. They are ordered to sacrifice to pagan gods and refuse. Some recensions make them noble courtiers or sons of leading families; that social rank is hagiographic characterization, not independently documented biography.

**[VARIANT]** Accounts disagree over whether Decius initially releases them to reconsider, whether they distribute their wealth to the poor, and whether they flee spontaneously or after formal interrogation.

#### Retreat to the cave

**[EARLY]** The youths withdraw to a cave on or near Mount Anchilos/Celion, names varying with language and manuscript. One member—Malchus, Iamblichus, Yamlīkhā, or a corresponding name—is responsible for obtaining food and news.

**[ELABORATION]** Some eastern versions add a shepherd who joins the youths. A watcher or shepherd helps produce the total of eight in several Syriac recensions.

#### Enclosure and sleep

**[EARLY]** Decius discovers or is told where they are and seals the cave, intending them to die. In some accounts two Christian officials secretly deposit a lead or bronze tablet naming the youths and recording the event. They fall into a sleep indistinguishable from death.

**[THEOLOGICAL FUNCTION]** The oldest developed Christian versions make their preservation answer denial of the bodily resurrection. They are not simply rescued fugitives: their unchanged bodies are living proofs that God can restore the dead.

#### Discovery under Theodosius II

**[EARLY]** During the reign of Theodosius II (408–450), the landowner or his workmen open the cave to obtain stone or construct an enclosure. Light enters, and the youths wake believing they have slept one night.

One youth goes to Ephesus with an old coin to buy food. He sees crosses or Christian signs, hears Christ’s name used publicly, and is bewildered. The vendor treats the obsolete coin as evidence of a hidden treasure. Officials question him; his story leads clergy, magistrates, townspeople, and eventually the emperor to the cave.

#### The coin

**[EARLY]** The old coin is present in Christian witnesses before Islam and functions as the recognition device: ordinary material culture reveals that centuries have passed. Its identification as a specific “medal of Decius” is a later antiquarian sharpening.

**[CANONICAL]** Qur’an 18:19 mentions their silver money (*wariqikum*) and the errand to buy the purest food but does not say that the coin’s antiquity exposes them. That connection is supplied by the known legend and later tafsīr.

#### Testimony and second death

**[EARLY]** The awakened youths address the emperor and confirm the resurrection of the same body. They then die or return to their repose. Theodosius wishes to place them in golden shrines, but in several versions they direct that they remain in the cave or be buried humbly. A church is established over or by the cave.

#### Duration

Christian sources do not agree:

- approximately 196 years, if enclosure under Decius is counted to c. 447;
- 187 years in the version reproduced by Edward Gibbon;
- about 200 years in several Syriac summaries;
- larger totals, including more than 300 years, in some eastern recensions.

No historical chronology can make every total agree.

### B. Qur’anic cycle

**[CANONICAL]** Qur’an 18:9–26 gives the following sequence:

1. The youths take refuge in the cave and pray for mercy and right guidance.
2. God “strikes upon their ears” for a number of years—that is, places them in profound sleep.
3. God later raises them to demonstrate which disputing party could best calculate their stay.
4. They are youths who believe in their Lord; God increases their guidance and strengthens their hearts.
5. They reject their people’s gods and withdraw to the cave.
6. The sun is described as inclining away from the cave at rising and passing them at setting while they lie in an open space.
7. They appear awake although sleeping; God turns them right and left.
8. Their dog stretches its forelegs at the entrance.
9. An observer would flee in terror.
10. On waking, they debate how long they have remained—“a day or part of a day.”
11. They send one of their number with silver money to obtain pure food discreetly.
12. They fear being stoned or forced back into the persecutors’ creed.
13. God makes their case known so that people may know that God’s promise and the Hour are true.
14. The discoverers dispute what to build over them; those who prevail say they will build a place of worship.
15. People dispute their number: three plus the dog, five plus the dog, or seven plus the dog.
16. The text commands: “My Lord knows best their number”; only a few know.
17. They remain “three hundred years, and they added nine,” followed by the injunction that God knows best how long they stayed.

The narrative never calls them “seven” in the narrator’s own unqualified voice. The conventional Islamic number seven rests on the placement and rhetoric of 18:22 as understood by exegetes, not on an explicit statement “there were seven.”

#### Three hundred and nine years

**[CANONICAL]** The phrase “three hundred years, and they added nine” is Qur’anic.

**[SYSTEMATIZATION]** Medieval commentators often explain the nine as the difference between 300 solar years and approximately 309 lunar years. That explanation is mathematically plausible in round numbers but is an exegetical harmonization, not stated in the verse itself. Other commentators construe the entire figure as reported human speech before “God knows best.”

### C. Later Islamic narrative

**[ELABORATION]** Tafsīr and *qiṣaṣ al-anbiyāʾ* identify the tyrant as Daqyānūs, the city as Afsūs/Ephesus, and the food-buyer as Yamlīkhā or Tamlikhā. They add court debates, conversion narratives, the names of all companions, a shepherd’s accession, and Qitmir’s speech or faithful following.

**[FOLKLORE]** Qitmir becomes exemplary among animals admitted to paradise. Lists of “animals of paradise” vary and are not Qur’anic.

**[FOLKLORE]** The companions’ names are copied on houses, letters, boats, weapons, amulets, and talismanic shirts so that their bearer may be protected as the youths were protected. Nineteenth-century European descriptions report Qitmir’s name being written on letters sent across the sea; this is evidence for then-current belief, not for early Islam.

## 4. Relations with Gods and Other Heroes

The Sleepers have no parentage, consorts, or children in the primary traditions. The collective’s identity depends on shared confession rather than genealogy. No divine paternity or demigod status is claimed.

## 5. Cult, Tomb and Ritual

### Ephesus

**[ARCHAEOLOGICALLY ATTESTED]** The site traditionally called the Cemetery or Grotto of the Seven Sleepers lies near Ephesus. Excavation in 1926–1927 exposed a Roman-period burial complex transformed into a late-antique Christian cemetery and pilgrimage sanctuary, with a church, catacomb chambers, numerous burials, and pilgrim material. The definitive excavation report was Camillo Praschniker and Franz Miltner, *Das Cömeterium der Sieben Schläfer* (1937). [Digitized excavation report](https://digi.ub.uni-heidelberg.de/diglit/fie_bd4_2/0009?navmode=struct).

**[CULTIC INFERENCE]** The density of Christian burials near the sanctuary shows that people sought burial *ad sanctos*, close to holy figures. The archaeological complex substantiates a late-antique cult site identified with the Sleepers. It does not identify seven original bodies or demonstrate the legend’s event.

**[EARLY LITERARY CULT]** *De situ terrae sanctae*, c. 518–531, reports an Ephesian church associated with the Sleepers. Gregory of Tours treats them as martyrs. Thus the local cult is securely present by the early sixth century and probably arose in the fifth.

### Feast days

**[REGIONAL]** Byzantine churches commemorate the seven youths commonly on 4 August, with another commemoration connected with 22 October in portions of the eastern calendar. Western calendars traditionally placed them on 27 July; the Old Irish *Félire Óengusso* commemorates them on 7 August. Calendar differences document independent cultic transmission, not different historical deaths.

### Relics and tombs

**[EARLY LEGEND]** The cave at Ephesus is their tomb in the Christian narrative.

**[REGIONAL]** Numerous later caves and elongated graves claim the same identity. None contains remains securely identified as those of the legendary youths. “Tomb of the Sleepers” therefore means a cultic attribution unless an archaeological statement specifies otherwise.

### Afşin, Turkey

**[REGIONAL]** The Eshab-ı Kehf complex on Bencilus Hill near Afşin includes a cave and an Islamic-Ottoman religious and charitable complex. Turkish authorities placed it on UNESCO’s Tentative List in 2015. The nomination argues that the cave’s orientation matches Qur’an 18:17 and that a worship building fulfills 18:21. These are confessional/site-traditional identifications, not archaeological proof that the Qur’anic story occurred there. The nomination itself acknowledges many competing caves, including Ephesus, Tarsus, Lice, and sites outside Turkey. [UNESCO Tentative List](https://whc.unesco.org/en/tentativelists/6037).

### Al-Raqīm/al-Rajīb, Jordan

**[REGIONAL]** A rock-cut funerary cave at al-Rajīb near Amman, with Byzantine-period Christian features and a nearby cemetery, is promoted as Kahf al-Raqīm. Excavation confirms an ancient burial cave and Christian use; it does not identify the occupants as the Companions. A Greek cross on the façade supports Christian occupation, while later Muslim worship makes the site a genuinely shared sacred place. The identification with Qur’anic *al-Raqīm* is later sacred geography. [Comparative Ephesus–Jordan study](https://ejoss.euras-edu.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/EJOSS-Nisan-2023-3-1-4.Makale.pdf).

### Chenini, Tunisia

**[REGIONAL/FOLKLORE]** The Mosque of the Seven Sleepers at Chenini is associated with long graves, a cave, and the *Rgūd al-Sabʿa*. Architectural study finds a vernacular structure with probable Ottoman-period influences but no archaeological or historical evidence that connects the site to late-antique Ephesus. Its significance lies in living southern Tunisian tradition. [Aida Ladhari’s architectural study](https://al-sabil.tn/2026/09/14/fr-aida-ladhari-mosquee-des-sept-dormants-a-chenini-tataouine-etude-architecturale-et-historique/).

### Protection charms

**[ARCHAEOLOGICALLY/MUSEUM ATTESTED]** The names of the Sleepers appear in Christian and Islamic protective contexts. A Coptic formulary assigns their names a healing or protective role; comparable names occur in the eighth-century anchorite’s grotto at Pachoras in Nubia. [Coptic Magical Papyri project](https://www.coptic-magic.phil.uni-wuerzburg.de/index.php/text/kyp-t-1480/).

**[MUSEUM ATTESTED]** The Kelsey Museum holds a twentieth-century Cairo lead amulet with a magic square, the Sleepers, and the invocation *Yā Ḥafīẓ*, “O Protector.” [Kelsey Museum](https://exhibitions.kelsey.lsa.umich.edu/pearls/objects/teardrop.html).

**[MUSEUM ATTESTED]** Islamic talismanic manuscripts and objects employ the story and names on the principle that recitation or possession extends God’s protection of the youths to the bearer. One Romanian collection manuscript places the Sleepers and Qitmir on its final leaf. [Discover Islamic Art](https://islamicart.museumwnf.org/database_item.php?id=object%3BEPM%3Brm%3BMus21%3B24%3Ben); [Metropolitan Museum overview](https://www.metmuseum.org/fr/essays/amulets-and-talismans-from-the-islamic-world).

## 6. Historical Evolution of the Figure

### Before c. 450: analogues, not the legend itself

**[COMPARATIVE RECONSTRUCTION]** Greek Epimenides, Jewish Ḥoni ha-Meʿaggel, and the Abimelech/Baruch tradition supply older examples of extraordinary sleep and awakening. These parallels show that the motif was available; they do not prove direct descent or establish an original “Seven Sleepers” text.

### c. 450–550: formation

**[EARLY]** The specifically Christian-Ephesian legend emerges in the fifth century and is securely visible in Jacob of Serugh by the early sixth. Its decisive innovation is theological: preserved bodies answer doubt about bodily resurrection.

**[RECONSTRUCTION]** Honigmann connected its composition with Bishop Stephen of Ephesus and resurrection controversy under Theodosius II. Syriac-priority scholars instead argued that the earliest literary form arose in Syriac. Because the hypothetical earliest Greek text is lost and the surviving Syriac tradition is textually diverse, priority remains debated.

### Sixth century: geographic and linguistic expansion

**[EARLY]** Syriac ecclesiastical histories, Gregory of Tours, and the pilgrim Theodosius establish eastern and western circulation. Gregory’s acknowledgment of a Syrian interpreter is direct evidence of transmission across languages.

### Early seventh century: Qur’anic recasting

**[CANONICAL]** Sūrat al-Kahf removes the named imperial chronology, leaves the youths anonymous, includes the dog prominently, warns against fruitless dispute over numbers, and makes the entire episode a sign of God’s knowledge and the coming Hour. Sidney Griffith argues that the Qur’an could allude rather than narrate because the Christian lore was already part of its audience’s religious world. [Griffith, “Christian Lore and the Arabic Qur’an”](https://www.sbl-site.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/11/Reeves.pdf).

### Eighth–thirteenth centuries: canonization and expansion

**[ELABORATION]** Byzantine menologia, Latin martyrologies, al-Ṭabarī, al-Thaʿlabī, al-Kisāʾī, Christian Arabic translations, and vernacular European poems stabilize particular rosters while multiplying local detail. Jacobus de Voragine’s *Golden Legend* becomes the dominant western version.

**[ICONOGRAPHIC]** Two notable Arabic manuscript images dated 1236–1237 and 1250 depict the sleeping youths; the later of these belongs to the illustrated *Maqāmāt* tradition associated with al-Wāsiṭī. Such pictures show that Islamic visual culture did not uniformly avoid representing the scene. [Thierry Zarcone, “How to represent the miracle of the Seven Sleepers”](https://www.cini.it/wp-content/uploads/2022/12/Zarcone-2022-The-Seven-Sleepers-1.pdf).

### Late medieval–early modern period: localization

**[REGIONAL]** Caves, shrines, and tombs from Anatolia and the Levant to North Africa and Central Asia acquire Sleepers traditions. The plurality is itself evidence of diffusion. It is not evidence that the group traveled among these places.

**[HISTORIOGRAPHIC]** Cardinal Cesare Baronio questioned the legend’s historicity while editing the Roman Martyrology in the late sixteenth century. Skepticism is therefore not merely a modern secular reaction.

### Eighteenth–nineteenth centuries: antiquarian critique and comparison

**[EARLY MODERN RECEPTION]** Edward Gibbon retold the legend in chapter 33 of *The History of the Decline and Fall of the Roman Empire* (volume III, 1781), calling it a “memorable fable.” His Jamblichus offers an obsolete coin of Decius and is suspected of finding treasure. Gibbon’s conjecture that Muḥammad learned the story while trading in Syria is not documented biography; it reflects eighteenth-century source criticism and polemic. [Gibbon, chapter 33](https://www.ccel.org/g/gibbon/decline/volume1/chap33.htm).

**[RECONSTRUCTION]** Johann Koch’s *Die Siebenschläferlegende* (1883) and Michael Huber’s *Die Wanderlegende von den Siebenschläfern* (1910) treated it as a migrating folktale. This comparativist approach illuminated transmission but could flatten the different theological functions of each recension.

### Twentieth–twenty-first centuries

**[SCHOLARLY/CULTIC REINVENTION]** Louis Massignon collected Christian and Islamic texts, images, shrines, and rituals, then used the Sleepers as an emblem of Christian-Muslim encounter.

**[LIVING CULT]** Eastern Christian commemoration, Qur’anic recitation, shrine visitation, amulets, and local cave traditions continue. Their continuation establishes present belief and ritual, not late-antique historicity.

## 7. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Literature and theatre

**[MODERN REINVENTION]** Tawfīq al-Ḥakīm’s Arabic play *Ahl al-Kahf* (1933) transforms the legend into philosophical drama about time, love, cultural displacement, and the impossibility of returning to one’s lost world. Its importance belongs to modern Egyptian theatre, not to medieval *qiṣaṣ*. [Digitized Arabic edition information](https://www.safahat.org/books/18292682/).

**[MODERN RECEPTION]** John Buchan alludes humorously to the Sleepers in *The Three Hostages* (1924). Mark Twain parodies the legend in *The Innocents Abroad* (1869). These works presuppose the western proverbial sense of an extraordinarily long sleeper.

**[MODERN REINVENTION]** Kader Abdolah reworks the martyrdom and cave motif in modern Dutch-Iranian literary contexts; scholarship treats this as another transformation rather than survival of an unchanged tale. [Cambridge chapter abstract](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/martyrdom/martyrdom-of-the-seven-sleepers-in-transformation-from-syriac-christianity-to-the-quran-and-to-the-dutchiranian-writer-kader-abdolah/F5D75E0024FD02880E33F235C923B915).

### Visual art

**[MEDIEVAL RECEPTION]** Byzantine icons show the youths lying together in the cave, sometimes as parallel wrapped bodies. Western manuscripts of the *Golden Legend* depict Decius ordering the cave sealed, the discovery of the old coin, and the emperor approaching the saints. Persian, Arabic, Ottoman, and South Asian manuscripts sometimes show the sleepers and dog together, though aniconic forms—names, calligraphy, diagrams, and talismanic squares—are equally important.

### Massignon and Vieux-Marché

**[SYNCRETISM/MODERN INVENTION]** In 1954 Louis Massignon attached an explicitly Islamic-Christian gathering to the Breton *pardon* at the chapel of the Seven Saints/Sleepers at Vieux-Marché. He treated the Breton seven saints and the Ephesian/Qur’anic sleepers as a bridge between faiths. The equation is not an ancient Breton tradition demonstrated by early evidence; it is Massignon’s learned and devotional construction.

**[POLITICAL USE]** Massignon instituted the pilgrimage amid French decolonization and immediately before the Algerian War, hoping for a “serene peace” in Algeria and North Africa. It survives as an interreligious event, although ethnographic scholarship notes the asymmetry of Islam being invited into a primarily Catholic framework. [Official Massignon site](https://louismassignon.fr/idees-debats/idees-forces/pelerinage/); [Vieux-Marché pilgrimage history](https://septdormants-levieuxmarche.fr/Le-reveil-des-Sept-Dormants.html).

### Tourism, heritage, and national claims

**[MODERN POLITICAL/ECONOMIC USE]** Turkey, Jordan, and Tunisia promote local caves or shrines as heritage and pilgrimage destinations. UNESCO nomination language for Afşin, state development around al-Rajīb, and cultural-tourism presentation at Chenini convert regional belief into heritage claims. Archaeology supports the antiquity of some structures, but no site possesses evidence capable of identifying the legendary sleepers.

No securely documented major extremist appropriation comparable to the use of some warrior heroes was found. The politically significant uses are instead interfaith diplomacy, national heritage competition, municipal tourism, and claims to scriptural landscape.

### Modern inventions and errors

- **[INVENTION]** “The Qur’an explicitly says there were seven youths.” It records competing claims and says God knows best.
- **[INVENTION]** “The Qur’an names the dog Qitmir.” It does not.
- **[INVENTION]** “Archaeologists found the seven bodies and a dog’s skeleton, proving the story.” Ancient burials or animal remains do not provide such an identification.
- **[INVENTION]** “The Afşin/Jordanian cave’s orientation proves it is the Qur’anic cave.” Orientation is compatible with selective readings of 18:17 but cannot establish identity.
- **[INVENTION]** “Three hundred solar years equal exactly 309 lunar years.” The conversion is approximate; the interpretive principle is medieval, not stated in the verse.
- **[INVENTION]** “All Christian versions lacked a dog until Islam.” A dog is absent from important early accounts, but the *De situ* textual tradition has one; manuscript chronology and interpolation must be assessed individually.
- **[INVENTION]** “The Breton seven saints were originally the Ephesian Sleepers.” The firm identification is Massignon’s twentieth-century syncretistic program.

## 8. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Greek or Syriac origin

**[DEBATE]** Ignazio Guidi, Theodor Nöldeke, Arthur Allgeier, and others argued for Syriac priority because the earliest substantial surviving witnesses are Syriac and because the tradition flourished there. Michael Huber, Paul Peeters, and Ernest Honigmann argued for a lost Greek original from Ephesus.

The responsible conclusion is narrower: the earliest extant substantial form is Syriac, while the Ephesian setting and certain names make prior Greek composition plausible. A lost Greek original cannot be cited as though it survived.

### Historicity

**[DEBATE]** Honigmann attempted to recover a historical event behind the narrative: a fifth-century appearance or discovery at Ephesus interpreted as miraculous and then retrojected into the Decian persecution.

**[CRITIQUE]** No contemporary record from the reign of Decius identifies the youths; no securely contemporary document from Theodosius II records their awakening; the names and number vary; and the narrative closely serves a fifth-century resurrection controversy. Archaeology establishes a cult complex but not the claimed event. The historical record therefore supports the existence of a late-antique Ephesian cult and story, not a third-century entombment followed by literal awakening.

### Function: bodily resurrection

**[STRONG RECONSTRUCTION]** The awakening under Theodosius II when the church is troubled by denial of resurrection makes apologetic function explicit in Christian versions. The Qur’an retains that function—“that they might know God’s promise is true and there is no doubt concerning the Hour”—while stripping away much ecclesiastical detail.

### Direct textual borrowing versus oral milieu

**[DEBATE]** Older scholarship often said the Qur’an “borrowed” directly from Jacob of Serugh or another fixed Syriac text. More recent work emphasizes intertextuality and a multilingual oral environment: no surviving pre-Islamic version contains every Qur’anic feature in precisely the Qur’anic arrangement. The shared narrative is certain; the exact transmission channel—written Syriac, Greek-mediated oral performance, Arabic Christian retelling, or several channels—remains unproved.

### The dog

**[DEBATE]** The Qur’anic dog may reflect a Christian variant, oral expansion, a transformation of the shepherd/watcher figure, or an otherwise lost recension. It should not automatically be called Muḥammad’s invention, as Gibbon did, because the full pre-Qur’anic textual stemma is unavailable. Gibbon also confused *al-Raqīm* with the dog.

### Number

**[DEBATE]** Seven became dominant in Greek and Latin cult, while several Syriac sources have eight and the Qur’an preserves three, five, and seven as disputed counts. Later commentators infer seven from the rhetoric of 18:22. The very label “Seven Sleepers” privileges one branch of a plural tradition.

### Archaeology and sacred geography

Excavation at Ephesus changed the debate by demonstrating that the story was attached to a real and important late-antique Christian burial and pilgrimage complex. It did not establish that the complex began as the tomb of seven Decian confessors.

The caves at Afşin, Tarsus, al-Rajīb, Chenini, and elsewhere document localization: communities map a portable sacred story onto local terrain. They should be compared as reception history, not ranked by devotional arguments presented as archaeological proofs.

### State of the evidence

- No third-century inscription names the group.
- The hypothetical foundational Greek account is lost.
- Jacob of Serugh’s homily lacks a complete modern critical edition.
- Syriac, Greek, Latin, Arabic, Coptic, Ethiopic, Armenian, Georgian, Sogdian, and vernacular manuscripts preserve divergent recensions.
- The gap between composition and many surviving manuscripts complicates claims based on a single late copy.
- Ephesus supplies the strongest evidence for an early cult, not for the miracle itself.
- Islamic material is unusually rich in commentary, illustrated manuscripts, talismans, and living shrine traditions.
