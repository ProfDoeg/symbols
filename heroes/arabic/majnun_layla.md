# Majnun Layla (Qays ibn al-Mulawwah): Research Dossier

**Traditional class:** mortal Bedouin lover-poet and “hero of sentiment”; later an exemplary ʿUdhrī lover, wilderness ascetic, Sufi type of the annihilated lover, and—at particular South Asian sites—a saint-like focus of popular visitation. He is not a demigod, prophet, Qurʾanic hero, or securely attested recipient of an ancient hero cult.

**Earliest secure attestation:** the earliest surviving connected account is Ibn Qutayba’s Arabic *Kitāb al-Shiʿr wa-l-shuʿarāʾ* (“Book of Poetry and Poets”), composed in the ninth century, probably before Ibn Qutayba’s death in 276/889. It attributes poems and a legendary biography to “Majnun,” but already offers alternative lineages and therefore does not securely establish a seventh-century individual. Abu al-Faraj al-Iṣfahānī’s much larger *Kitāb al-Aghānī*, compiled in the tenth century, preserves conflicting identities, genealogies, stories, poems, and death traditions. These are the earliest documentary strata—not contemporary records of Qays.

## Evidentiary key

- **[E1: securely attested early material]** A datable early or medieval text securely contains the claim; this does not certify the narrated event as historical.
- **[E2: later canonical elaboration]** A coherent literary development, especially Niẓāmī’s Persian romance of 584/1188.
- **[E3: regional variant]** A localized Persian, Turkic, Kurdish, Pashto, Urdu, Indian, or other adaptation.
- **[E4: learned systematization/syncretism]** Genealogical, mystical, medical, theological, or literary-critical ordering of earlier material.
- **[E5: medieval or early-modern accretion]** An episode or attribution appearing well after the presumed seventh-century setting.
- **[E6: folklore/belief]** Oral, commemorative, shrine, or popular tradition whose existence is documentable but whose historical claim is not.
- **[E7: modern scholarly reconstruction]** A modern inference rather than a primary-source statement.
- **[E8: modern invention/reinvention]** A modern creative, commercial, political, or falsely historicized claim.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Orientation

Majnun Layla is the protagonist created by the conjunction of poems attributed to an elusive early-Islamic Arabic lover and prose *akhbār*—anecdotal reports constructed around those verses. In the oldest surviving literary biography he is a young man of the Banū ʿĀmir whose public passion for Layla leads her family to bar him, whereupon his reason deteriorates, he rejects clothing and society, associates with wild animals, and speaks coherently chiefly when Layla is mentioned. The ninth- and tenth-century sources disagree about his name, descent, Layla’s descent, their first meeting, her husband, and his death. **[E1]** In 1188 Niẓāmī of Ganja converted these discontinuous Arabic reports into a Persian narrative poem of about 4,600 couplets. His Majnun becomes an aristocratic child, poet, animal-protector, wilderness sovereign, and ascetic whose earthly fixation approaches mystical self-annihilation. **[E2]** Subsequent Persianate and Turkic authors made that version the principal international form of the legend. [Ibn Qutayba’s extant Arabic text](https://ablibrary.net/book_content/b/5737/15); [Encyclopaedia Iranica](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/leyli-o-majnun-narrative-poem/).

### Names and scripts

- **Arabic:** قَيْس بْن المُلوَّح, *Qays ibn al-Mulawwaḥ*; مَجْنُون لَيْلَى, *Majnūn Laylā*; مَجْنُون بَنِي عَامِر, *Majnūn Banī ʿĀmir* (“the madman of the Banū ʿĀmir”). **[E1]**
- **Persian:** لیلی و مجنون, *Laylī o Majnūn* or *Leylī o Majnūn*. Niẓāmī puts Layli first in the title; Amir Khusraw reverses it in *Majnūn o Laylī*. **[E2]**
- **Ottoman/Azeri Turkish:** ليلى و مجنون; modern *Leylâ ve Mecnûn*, *Leyli və Məcnun*. **[E3]**
- **Urdu:** لیلیٰ مجنوں, *Lailā Majnūn*. **[E3]**
- European spellings include *Majnun, Majnūn, Madjnun, Medjnun, Mejnun, Mecnun* and *Layla, Laila, Leyla, Leila, Leyli*.

**Majnūn** is the passive participle of Arabic *janna*, “to cover/conceal”; literally it means “one possessed or covered by a jinn,” hence “mad” or “demented.” In the legend it is an epithet earned through love, not securely a personal name or medical diagnosis. **[E1/E4]** Ibn Sallām, as quoted in *al-Aghānī*, says he could swear the lover was not inherently insane but became distraught when Layla was married and hope disappeared. The work elsewhere compares his condition to a *lawtha*, a disturbance or touch of madness. [The relevant *Aghānī* passage](https://ablibrary.net/book_content/b/1682/32?lang=fa).

**Majnūn Laylā** most naturally means “Layla’s madman” or “the one made mad by Layla,” not the frequently offered loose modern gloss “madly in love with Layla.” **[E1/E7]**

**Qays** is an old Arabic masculine name. Its ordinary lexical associations include measuring or comparison, but the sources do not provide a story-etymology for it; elaborate symbolic derivations should therefore not be presented as ancient tradition. **[E7]**

**al-Mulawwaḥ / Mulawwiḥ / Mülevvah** is transmitted with vocal and spelling variation. It is principally a patronymic in this dossier, “son of al-Mulawwaḥ,” not an independently verified family name. **[E1]**

**Laylā** (ليلى) is an Arabic feminine name related to *layl*, “night”; Arabic lexicography also associates *laylā* with the darkness or intoxication of wine. Persian poets exploited “night,” darkness, black hair, and the dark beloved, but no early source says that the woman was invented solely as an etymological allegory. **[E4]**

### Conflicting identities and genealogies

The following are variants, not pieces of one recoverable family tree:

1. **Qays ibn al-Mulawwaḥ ibn Muzāḥim**, of Banū Jaʿda/Banū ʿĀmir ibn Ṣaʿṣaʿa: the name that became canonical. **[E1]**
2. **Qays ibn Muʿādh**, assigned by genealogical material to Banū ʿUqayl: an early alternative. **[E1]**
3. **Muʿādh ibn Kulayb**, a “madman” who loved Layla; *al-Aghānī* also says Muzāḥim ibn al-Ḥārith shared this Layla’s love. **[E1]**
4. Later notices report **Bakhtarī ibn al-Jaʿd**, **Mahdī ibn al-Mulawwaḥ**, and other names. **[E4]**
5. *Al-Aghānī* preserves a report that an Umayyad youth loved a woman, concealed himself by attributing his poetry to “Majnun,” manufactured stories for the persona, and that later transmitters enlarged them. **[E1]** This is ancient skepticism, not a modern debunking.
6. Layla is usually **Laylā bint Mahdī al-ʿĀmiriyya**, sometimes given the *kunya* Umm Mālik or Umm ʿAmr; another *Aghānī* report makes her Laylā bint Mahdī ibn Saʿīd of Banū al-Ḥarīsh. **[E1]**

The long, seamless pedigrees now circulating to ʿAdnān and Ishmael are learned genealogical harmonizations. No contemporary inscription supports them. **[E4]**

### Classifications

- **Early Arabic:** poet, lover, disturbed wanderer, semi-historical tribal figure. **[E1]**
- **ʿUdhrī literary classification:** model of chaste, constant, fatal love—what modern scholarship calls “heroism of sentiment rather than action.” **[E4/E7]**
- **Niẓāmian Persianate classification:** tragic romance hero, inspired poet, ascetic, and king or protector of animals. **[E2]**
- **Sufi classification:** the lover emptied of self in the Beloved; Layla may signify divine beauty and Majnun the seeker. This is an interpretation, not his classification in the earliest Arabic reports. **[E4/E5]**
- **South Asian shrine belief:** saint-like patron of faithful lovers at an attributed joint tomb. **[E6]**

There is no ancient equation with Luqman, Dhū al-Qarnayn, al-Khiḍr, the Sleepers, ʿAntar, Alexander, or Sinbad. Their Qurʾanic, *qiṣaṣ*, epic, or romance corpora are irrelevant to Majnun’s primary attestation. **Absence of evidence is the finding.**

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Earliest surviving Arabic layer

#### Ibn Qutayba, *Kitāb al-Shiʿr wa-l-shuʿarāʾ*

**Language/date:** Arabic; ninth century, before 276/889. Survives in much later manuscripts and modern editions, including Aḥmad Muḥammad Shākir’s edition. **[E1]**

**Content:** poems attributed to Majnun are embedded in a connected account: uncertain lineage; love of Layla from youth; exclusion by her people; mental derangement; intervention by Nawfal ibn Musāḥiq; pilgrimage to the Kaʿba; wilderness wandering; wild animals; and death. The text says he tore off clothing, understood nothing unless Layla was named, and then spoke accurately and recited poetry. Nawfal clothes him and unsuccessfully attempts to secure Layla because her clan meets them armed and rejects entry even at the cost of bloodshed. [Arabic text](https://ablibrary.net/book_content/b/5737/15). **[E1]**

**What it does not prove:** it is roughly two centuries later than the presumed setting; it contains no contemporary document, securely dated autograph poem, inscription, or eyewitness record. Its alternative lineages advertise uncertainty.

#### Abu al-Faraj al-Iṣfahānī, *Kitāb al-Aghānī*

**Language/date:** Arabic; compiled in the tenth century, before the author’s death in 356/967. **[E1]**

**Role:** the largest early dossier, occupying dozens of pages in volume II of standard editions. Its chains of transmitters, variant readings, musical notices, and contradictions preserve the formation of the legend rather than one authorized biography.

Principal materials include:

- rival identities for “Majnun”; **[E1]**
- childhood or youthful courtship, versus a version in which he hears of an accomplished woman, dresses elegantly, and visits her as an adult; **[E1]**
- reciprocal love and swoons; **[E1]**
- the father’s rejected proposal and a rival suitor; **[E1]**
- Layla threatened into choosing Ward ibn Muḥammad despite Majnun’s offer of fifty red camels; [Arabic report](https://ablibrary.net/book_content/b/1682/17); **[E1]**
- another husband described as a wealthy man of Thaqīf; [Arabic report](https://ablibrary.net/book_content/391/44); **[E1]**
- the lover’s desert withdrawal and partial lucidity when Layla is mentioned; **[E1]**
- secret messages and encounters; **[E1]**
- poems set to music, an important reminder that *al-Aghānī* is a book of songs as well as biography; **[E1]**
- death on rough ground among black stones, with a written scrap reportedly found on the body. [Arabic text](https://www.masaha.org/book/view/2567/page/456). **[E1]**

Contrary to a common conflation, the accessible *Aghānī* death report says he was found dead on rough ground among black stones. It does **not** securely establish the famous Niẓāmian tableau in which he dies embracing Layla’s grave. The grave-death is canonical in Niẓāmī and later retellings. **[E1 versus E2]**

#### Other Arabic witnesses

- **Al-Jāḥiẓ, *al-Bayān wa-l-tabyīn***, ninth century: scattered citations or allusions, not a complete romance. **[E1]**
- **Al-Masʿūdī, *Murūj al-dhahab***, tenth century: additional anecdotes in an adab/historical setting. **[E1]**
- **Ibn al-Nadīm, *al-Fihrist***, late tenth century: bibliographical evidence relevant to collections and attribution. **[E1]**
- **Abū Bakr al-Wālibī, *Dīwān Qays ibn al-Mulawwaḥ al-Majnūn***: a collected corpus transmitted under al-Wālibī’s name. It preserves important poetry and *akhbār*, but it is a retrospective compilation, internally heterogeneous, and cannot be treated as a seventh-century authorial book. **[E4/E5]**
- **Dāwūd al-Anṭākī, *Tazyīn al-aswāq fī akhbār al-ʿushshāq***, early modern: an influential compilation of lovers’ stories, downstream from the medieval tradition. **[E5]**

The attributed *Dīwān* contains poems of differing styles and overlaps with the repertoires of other love poets. Attribution poem by poem remains uncertain. **[E7]**

### Persian corpus

#### Pre-Niẓāmian allusions

Rudakī, Manūchihrī, Bābā Ṭāhir, Sanāʾī, and other Persian poets use Majnun and Layla as proverbial lovers before 1188. The allusions show that the names were culturally intelligible, but not that a full Persian romance already existed. Iranica counts fewer than ten such allusions in Persian *dīvāns* before Niẓāmī. **[E1/E4]**

#### Niẓāmī, *Laylī o Majnūn*

**Language/date:** Persian, completed 584/1188; third romance of Niẓāmī’s *Khamsa*, about 4,600 couplets, written at the request of the Shirvanshah Akhsitān. **[E2]** Critical editions include Waḥīd Dastgirdī (1934), A. A. Alizade (1965), Behruz Tharvatiyān (1985), and Barāt Zanjānī (1990). [Iranica’s editions and bibliography](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/leyli-o-majnun-narrative-poem/).

Niẓāmī’s references to anonymous transmitters seventeen times are probably literary authentication rather than recoverable citations. He combines Arabic *akhbār* with Persian romance structures and motifs derived in part from ʿAyyūqī’s *Varqa o Gulshāh*. **[E2/E7]**

#### Major Persian and Persianate recastings

- **Amir Khusraw Dihlavī, *Majnūn o Laylī*, 1299:** first major imitation; adapts the narrative to Indo-Persian and Chishti-Sufi concerns. **[E3/E5]**
- **Jāmī, *Laylī o Majnūn*, 1484:** within the *Haft Awrang*; strengthens the allegory of divine beauty. **[E3/E5]**
- **Maktabī Shīrāzī, 1481; Hātifī, late fifteenth/early sixteenth century; Hilālī, Qāsimī and others:** numerous *naẓīras*. Hātifī adds Majnun’s earlier attraction to beautiful women, Nawfal himself falling for Layla, a magically paralyzed rival, walking in snow, and the ransomed cypress. These are not early Arabic episodes. **[E3/E5]**
- Surveys enumerate 59 to 80 Persian or Persianate imitations, depending on criteria. **[E7]**

### Turkic corpus

- **Chaghatay:** ʿAlī Shīr Navāʾī’s *Laylī va Majnūn*, 1484. **[E3]**
- **Ottoman Turkish:** Hamdullah Hamdī, 1499; numerous subsequent versions. **[E3]**
- **Azeri/Ottoman Turkish:** Fużūlī’s *Leylâ vü Mecnûn*, commonly dated 1535–36, about 3,096 couplets. It fuses human tragedy, lyric virtuosity, and mystical aspiration and became the dominant Turkic version. **[E3/E5]**
- Turkish scholarship records many manuscript, theatrical, *meddah*, children’s, and popular adaptations. [TDV overview and bibliography](https://islamansiklopedisi.org.tr/leyla-ve-mecnun). **[E3/E6]**

### Urdu and South Asian corpus

Urdu versions ordinarily descend through Persian rather than directly from the Arabic reports. They localize landscape, manners, costume, and sometimes Qays’s ancestry so thoroughly that he may be called Indian. Early Deccani examples include Shaykh Aḥmad Gujarātī’s fragmentary poem and Muḥammad ibn Aḥmad ʿAzīz’s 1636 adaptation of Hātifī. [TDV Urdu survey](https://islamansiklopedisi.org.tr/leyla-ve-mecnun#4-urdu-edebiyati). **[E3]**

## 3. The Story: Episodes and Variants

### Birth, parentage, and first meeting

**Arabic stratum:** no secure birth date. Modern dates such as 24/645 are calculations attached to a presumed historical person, not early documentary facts. **[E7/E8]** Qays is usually made a Banū ʿĀmir tribesman; Ibn Qutayba and *al-Aghānī* transmit rival lineages. In one version Qays and Layla grow up together and converse among other youths; in later popular summaries they herd animals together. Another *Aghānī* report says he was already fond of women, heard of Layla’s beauty and cultivation, dressed in his best clothes, perfumed himself, took a fine camel and sword, and visited her. [Variant Arabic report](https://ablibrary.net/book_content/b/1682/36). **[E1]**

**Niẓāmī:** Qays is the long-awaited son of a childless, aristocratic chief. He and Layli meet at school. Both details Persianize and urbanize the Bedouin material. **[E2]**

### Love becomes public

In the Arabic *akhbār*, Qays’s poetry names Layla openly. Her family regards the publicity as dishonor and restricts access; the poems both express love and create the social barrier. **[E1/E7]** Later moralizing summaries often say an Arab father could not marry a daughter to a man who had publicly eroticized her. This is plausible within the story’s honor logic, but it should not be universalized into an unchanging “Arab law.” **[E7]**

Niẓāmī makes public recitation the outward sign of all-consuming obsession. Qays sees everything through Layli and becomes “Majnun.” **[E2]**

### Proposal, rival, and forced choice

In one *Aghānī* report Majnun offers fifty red camels; Ward ibn Muḥammad offers ten camels and a herdsman. Layla’s family ostensibly permits her to choose but threatens mutilation if she does not choose Ward, so she accepts him unwillingly. **[E1]** Another strand gives her a wealthy Thaqafī husband. **[E1]** Niẓāmī calls the husband **Ibn Salām**, portrays him as a princely Arab suitor, and has Layli preserve her virginity by resisting consummation. **[E2]**

The claim that every version makes Layla a lifelong virgin is false. It is crucial in the Persian romance’s chastity structure but not demonstrable across all Arabic variants. **[E7]**

### Madness and wilderness

Ibn Qutayba’s Majnun tears his clothes, plays with earth, gives irrelevant answers, and recovers lucidity when Layla is named. **[E1]** *Al-Aghānī* contains both “real madness” narratives and Ibn Sallām’s rationalizing judgment that despair, not congenital insanity, produced his condition. **[E1/E4]**

He abandons ordinary food, clothing, kinship, and status; roams Najd and sometimes, in later reports, the Hijaz or Syrian borderlands; recites verse; communes with gazelles, wolves, ravens, and other animals. **[E1/E5]**

Niẓāmī greatly enlarges this theme:

- Majnun releases trapped gazelles because their eyes recall Layli. **[E2]**
- He becomes surrounded by predatory and gentle animals living in peace. **[E2]**
- His emaciation, nakedness, fasting, silence, insomnia, and solitude parallel deliberate ascetic disciplines, although love rather than choice imposes them. **[E2/E4]**
- He becomes a symbolic “king of animals,” a sovereignty gained by renouncing human sovereignty. **[E2]**

The common modern picture of Majnun continuously writing poems in desert sand is a later pictorial and popular condensation; it should not be treated as the earliest securely attested act. **[E5/E8]**

### Nawfal’s intervention

Ibn Qutayba’s Nawfal ibn Musāḥiq encounters the naked Majnun, clothes him, promises to obtain Layla, and withdraws when her armed kin refuse and bloodshed threatens. Majnun accuses him of breaking his promise. **[E1]**

Niẓāmī transforms the official into a chivalric Persian-style chief. Nawfal fights two battles for Majnun. The lover paradoxically prays for Layli’s people rather than his own ally because love overrides faction. **[E2]** This enlarged war is not evidence for a seventh-century battle.

### The Meccan pilgrimage

Majnun’s father takes him to the Kaʿba, expecting him to pray for deliverance from love. Majnun instead asks God to increase or perpetuate his love. **[E1]** In literary function, the episode makes passion a devotional absolute and prepares mystical interpretation. **[E4/E7]** It does not mean Islam recognized Layla as divine.

### Meetings and messages

Arabic reports vary between direct conversations, Layla’s nocturnal visit at the request of Majnun’s mother, sudden encounters ending in a swoon, intermediaries, and exchanged verses. In one *Aghānī* account Layla tells him to fear God and preserve his life; he answers that God has decreed her for another while afflicting him with her love. [Arabic report](https://ablibrary.net/book_content/b/1682/31). **[E1]**

In another report Majnun unexpectedly encounters Layla’s departing camp, faints, and she refuses a public stop that would disgrace her, but sends a maid to say that she would sacrifice herself if she could cure him. [Arabic report](https://ablibrary.net/book_content/391/50). **[E1]**

Niẓāmī’s lovers exchange letters and meet at a distance to recite poetry, without touching. When Ibn Salām dies, Layli becomes legally available, but Majnun can no longer accept the embodied woman: his interior Layli has displaced her physical presence. **[E2/E4]**

### Parents’ deaths

Niẓāmī narrates the father’s visits to the wilderness and death, followed by the mother’s death; Majnun mourns but does not return to society. **[E2]** *Al-Aghānī* transmits a poem in which Majnun laments his father and says the father died before his complete derangement. [Arabic report](https://ablibrary.net/book_content/b/1682/54?lang=ar). **[E1]**

### Layla’s death

The Arabic material is not a single stable account. Some later Arabic compilations have Layla die first and Majnun seek her grave; other reports concentrate on his own death without constructing Niẓāmī’s bridal-funeral scene. **[E1/E5]**

Niẓāmī has Layli die of grief after Ibn Salām’s death and be buried dressed as a bride. **[E2]**

### Majnun’s death and body

The variants must remain separate:

1. **Found on rough stony ground:** *al-Aghānī* reports Majnun found dead between or among black stones; family and Layla’s father attend, and a scrap bearing verses is found. **[E1]**
2. **Food left untouched:** a woman or kinswoman who regularly leaves him food finds it uneaten; searchers locate his body in a stony valley. **[E1/E5]**
3. **Death on Layla’s grave:** in Niẓāmī, Majnun learns of Layli’s death, reaches her tomb, prays for death, embraces the grave, and dies there. His body remains guarded by animals before burial beside her; a dream reveals the lovers enthroned together in Paradise. **[E2]**
4. **Modern harmonization:** “He died in 68/688 on Layla’s grave” joins an uncertain annalistic date to Niẓāmī’s canonical ending. No contemporary record verifies either component. **[E8]**

## 4. Relations with Named Figures

### Genealogical alternatives

| Person | Version | Source/status |
|---|---|---|
| Majnun | Qays ibn al-Mulawwaḥ ibn Muzāḥim, Banū ʿĀmir/Jaʿda | Ibn Qutayba; *al-Aghānī* **[E1]** |
| Majnun | Qays ibn Muʿādh, associated with Banū ʿUqayl | early genealogical variant **[E1]** |
| Majnun | Muʿādh ibn Kulayb | *al-Aghānī* alternative mad lover **[E1]** |
| Layla | Laylā bint Mahdī al-ʿĀmiriyya; sometimes cousin of Qays | dominant Arabic tradition **[E1]** |
| Layla | daughter of Mahdī ibn Saʿīd of Banū al-Ḥarīsh | *al-Aghānī* variant **[E1]** |
| Husband | Ward ibn Muḥammad al-ʿUqaylī | *al-Aghānī* **[E1]** |
| Husband | unnamed wealthy Thaqafī | *al-Aghānī* variant **[E1]** |
| Husband | Ibn Salām | Niẓāmī **[E2]** |

No securely attested child of Majnun appears in the central tradition. **Absence of evidence.**

### Every documented interaction with another named divine being or heroic/narrative figure

- **Layla/Layli:** reciprocal childhood or adult love; poetry, separation, messages, swoons, distant meetings, and—in Niẓāmī—posthumous union. Ibn Qutayba, ninth century; *al-Aghānī*, tenth century; Niẓāmī, 1188. **[E1/E2]**
- **God/Allāh:** Majnun prays at the Kaʿba not to be cured but to have his love increased; he repeatedly appeals to divine decree. Ibn Qutayba and later Arabic tradition; Niẓāmī. **[E1/E2]** This is devotion addressed to God, not interaction with a pagan deity.
- **Nawfal ibn Musāḥiq:** befriends, clothes, and promises to help Majnun; fails before armed resistance. Ibn Qutayba, ninth century. **[E1]**
- **Niẓāmī’s Nawfal:** becomes a chivalric war leader who attacks Layli’s tribe; Majnun supports Layli’s side emotionally. Niẓāmī, 1188. **[E2]**
- **Ward ibn Muḥammad:** rival suitor selected under coercion; *al-Aghānī*, tenth century. **[E1]**
- **The Thaqafī husband:** Majnun encounters or questions Layla’s husband in some Arabic reports; *al-Aghānī*, tenth century. **[E1]**
- **Ibn Salām:** marries Layli but cannot consummate the marriage; his death removes the legal barrier but not Majnun’s interior separation. Niẓāmī, 1188. **[E2]**
- **Majnun’s father, al-Mulawwaḥ:** seeks the marriage, takes his son to Mecca, visits or counsels him, and dies; Arabic *akhbār* and Niẓāmī. **[E1/E2]**
- **Majnun’s mother:** seeks Layla’s intervention and visits her son; Arabic reports and Niẓāmī. **[E1/E2]**
- **Layla’s father, Mahdī:** refuses the match because of scandal, madness, or tribal honor; appears at or after Majnun’s death in one *Aghānī* report. **[E1]**
- **Salām Baghdādī:** seeks Majnun in the wilderness to hear and transmit his poetry; Niẓāmī. **[E2]**
- **Zayd:** intermediary who carries news, including Ibn Salām’s death, and later dreams of the lovers in Paradise. Niẓāmī, 1188. **[E2]**
- **Zaynab:** paired with Zayd in an inset love story that mirrors and comments on the principal pair. Niẓāmī, 1188. **[E2]**
- **King of Marv:** appears in Niẓāmī’s didactic inset about attachment to dogs and altered perception. **[E2]**
- **Ḥallāj:** no narrative meeting. Later Sufi readers compare “I am Layli and Layli is I” with the ecstatic language attributed to Ḥallāj. This is a learned typological pairing, not an interaction. **[E4]**
- **Luqman, Dhū al-Qarnayn/Alexander, al-Khiḍr, the Sleepers, ʿAntar, and Sinbad:** no interaction in Majnun’s early Arabic or Niẓāmian corpus. Famous juxtaposition within surveys of Islamic heroes does not create a textual encounter. **Absence of evidence.**

## 5. Cult, Tomb, and Ritual

### Arabia

No excavated *heroon*, inscribed grave, relic, ancient sacrifice, festival, oracle, or institutional cult of Qays/Majnun is securely attested in Arabia. **[E7]** Saudi cultural-memory projects connect him with Najdi places such as al-Tūbād and al-Ghayl, but appropriately treat the record as a mixture of literature and place-memory rather than archaeological identification. [Saudi Memory](https://saudimemory.com/people/173). **[E6/E8]**

Poems naming places can sustain a literary geography; they cannot by themselves authenticate a tomb or an individual’s itinerary.

### Niẓāmī’s literary tomb

Niẓāmī says the adjoining graves become a place of pilgrimage and concludes with a dream of the lovers united as king and queen in Paradise. This is a feature of the 1188 poem, partly comparable to the lovers’ graves in Persian romance, not proof of an operating twelfth-century shrine. [Iranica’s plot analysis](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/leyli-o-majnun-narrative-poem/). **[E2]**

### Binjaur, Rajasthan

A small mausoleum at Binjaur near Anupgarh in Rajasthan is locally identified as the joint tomb of Laila and Majnu. Couples and newlyweds visit for blessings, offer cloths and prayers, and attend an annual June fair. [NDTV report](https://www.ndtv.com/india-news/rajasthan-remembers-laila-majnu-420892); [site description](https://www.theislamicheritage.com/detail/LAILA-MAJNU-KA-MAZAR). **[E6]**

The practice is genuine contemporary folklore and ritual. The identification is geographically incompatible with the early Arabic setting and unsupported by inscriptional or archaeological evidence linking the grave to a seventh-century Arabian couple. It is therefore **not** the historical tomb of an established Qays but a South Asian localization of the legend. **[E6/E7]**

This is the closest documented phenomenon to “cult”: a shared Muslim/Hindu-adjacent popular love shrine rather than formal hero worship or canonical Islamic sainthood.

## 6. Historical Evolution

### Presumed seventh-century setting

Later sources place Qays under the Umayyads, most often in mid-to-late first/seventh-century Najd, and variously date his death between 65/685 and 80/699. **[E1/E7]** No contemporary papyrus, inscription, coin, chronicle entry, or securely transmitted autograph poem confirms him.

### Eighth-century formation

Modern reconstruction proposes that poetic fragments and oral lover-anecdotes crystallized during the second/eighth century. A report already attributed to ʿAwāna ibn al-Ḥakam denies that the persona existed, while another explanation makes “Majnun” a pseudonym protecting an Umayyad lover. **[E1/E7]** Thus the historicity dispute belongs to the tradition’s early history.

### Ninth–tenth centuries: Arabic consolidation

Ibn Qutayba first supplies an extant connected biography. *Al-Aghānī* then creates an archive rather than a uniform story: variant chains, names, songs, rival accounts, and skeptical notices coexist. **[E1]**

Majnun becomes the paradigmatic ʿUdhrī lover—chaste, constant, eloquent, socially destroyed by separation. “ʿUdhrī” here names a literary ethic and genre; it does not prove that every attributed poem originated among Banū ʿUdhra. **[E4/E7]**

### Eleventh–twelfth centuries: Persian proverb and mystical availability

Persian poets use the pair as familiar emblems. Aḥmad Ghazālī employs Majnun anecdotes in *Sawāniḥ*; Sanāʾī and later ʿAṭṭār use him to reason about love, self-loss, and perception. **[E4/E5]**

### 1188: Niẓāmī’s canonical reconstruction

Niẓāmī’s decisive innovations are narrative continuity, schoolroom love, aristocratic parentage, expanded war, animal kingship, resistant virgin wife, letters, cosmic prayers, the deaths of both parents, Layli’s bridal burial, Majnun’s death on her grave, joint burial, pilgrimage, and paradisal reunion. **[E2]**

The poem does not simply translate Arabic folklore; it makes a Persian ethical and aesthetic romance from it.

### Thirteenth–sixteenth centuries: Persianate expansion

Amir Khusraw, Jāmī, Navāʾī, Hamdī, Hātifī, and Fużūlī repeatedly alter point of view, mystical intensity, local setting, and subsidiary plot. **[E3/E5]** References multiply sharply: Iranica counts 106 in Rūmī’s *Dīvān-i Shams*, illustrating how Majnun becomes a reusable sign rather than only a protagonist.

### Early modern visual canon

Safavid, Ottoman, Mughal, Deccani, and Rajput artists make several scenes iconic:

- the children at school;
- the proposal and tribal battle;
- the skeletal Majnun among animals;
- Layli visiting the wilderness ascetic;
- the final meeting or grave.

A 1517 folio depicts the lovers at school in a thoroughly Persian architectural setting. [LACMA object record](https://collections.lacma.org/object/32643). **[E5]** A British Museum folio shows a naked Majnun on a rocky outcrop among animals. [British Museum](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/W_1938-1210-0-2). **[E5]** A Rajasthani painting of about 1790–1810 reimagines the pair as a woman visiting an ascetic. [British Museum](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/A_1880-0-2272). **[E3/E5]**

These images document reception, not portraits of historical persons.

### Nineteenth century: print and translation

James Atkinson’s abridged English verse translation appeared in 1836. Lithographed and printed Arabic, Persian, Urdu, and Turkic editions detached the story from elite manuscript circulation and expanded popular access. An 1881 Arabic printing of *Qiṣṣat Qays ibn al-Mulawwaḥ al-ʿĀmirī al-maʿrūf bi-Majnūn Laylā* documents this print afterlife. [NYU digital copy](https://sites.dlib.nyu.edu/viewer/books/nyu_aco000290/1). **[E5]**

### Twentieth–twenty-first centuries

The figure moves into drama, opera, ballet, cinema, popular music, television, tourism, psychology, and national heritage. These works often treat Niẓāmī’s synthesis as “the ancient original,” obscuring the earlier Arabic plurality. **[E8]**

## 7. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Literature and drama

- **Aḥmad Shawqī, *Majnūn Laylā/Qays Laylā*, 1931:** Arabic verse drama drawing on *al-Aghānī*, translated into English in the 1930s and again later. **[E8]**
- **Louis Aragon, *Le Fou d’Elsa*, 1963:** transfers the Majnun model to “Elsa” and the fall of Granada; indebted especially to the Persian/Jāmī tradition rather than a recreation of seventh-century Arabia. [AUC study](https://fount.aucegypt.edu/faculty_journal_articles/3168/). **[E8]**
- **André Miquel, *Laylâ, ma raison*, 1984:** modern French poetic-fictional return to the Arabic material. **[E8]**
- Modern Arabic, Persian, Turkish, Urdu, Indonesian, and English novels continue to use “Majnun” for obsessive, socially impossible, or mystical love. **[E8]**

### Opera and music

- **Uzeyir Hajibeyli, *Leyli and Majnun*, Baku, 1908:** a foundational Azerbaijani mugham opera joining European operatic form with mugham performance. Azerbaijani cultural institutions commemorate it as the first opera of the Muslim East; the formulation is a national cultural claim, not a property of the medieval legend. [Azerbaijan UNESCO Commission](https://www.unesco.az/en/articles/intangible_cultural_heritage/azerbaijani-mugham). **[E8]**
- **Qara Qarayev, symphonic poem, 1947**, and subsequent Soviet ballets and films extended its musical-national canon. **[E8]**
- **Derek and the Dominos, “Layla,” 1970:** Eric Clapton encountered the story through Ian Dallas, later Abdalqadir as-Sufi, and identified Majnun’s unavailable beloved with his own passion for Pattie Boyd, then married to George Harrison. The borrowing is the name and emotional paradigm, not a retelling of Niẓāmī’s plot. [Recording history](https://www.soundonsound.com/techniques/classic-tracks-derek-dominos-layla); [contemporary documentation of Boyd as the song’s inspiration](https://apnews.com/article/bfa3d41917e2c5222065ae5c0836660d). **[E8]**
- Claims that “Layla” proves a direct unbroken line from seventh-century Qays to rock music should be replaced by the documented chain: medieval Arabic legend → Niẓāmī → modern translation/retelling → Ian Dallas → Clapton. **[E7]**

### Cinema

Iranian films titled *Layli va Majnun* appeared in 1937, 1956, and 1970. Indian cinema repeatedly adapted *Laila Majnu* from the silent era onward; a 2018 Hindi film relocates Qais and Laila to contemporary Kashmir. **[E8]** These are reinventions of an already Persianized and South-Asianized romance, not evidence for its Arabian origins.

### Modern visual culture

Manuscript paintings established the thin, nearly naked Majnun surrounded by harmonious animals as his most persistent image. Modern monuments, tiles, metro panels, book jackets, illustrations, and tourism campaigns repeat that iconography. **[E5/E8]**

### National and political appropriation

The underlying legend is Arabic; Niẓāmī wrote its canonical romance in Persian while living in Ganja; Fużūlī wrote a major Turkic version; Hajibeyli created the Azerbaijani opera. These are distinct, cumulative facts.

Soviet and post-Soviet institutions promoted Niẓāmī as an Azerbaijani national poet, while Iranian and much international scholarship classifies him by the language and literary system of his work as a Persian poet. Treating either modern nationality as a transparent twelfth-century identity is anachronistic. **[E7/E8]** State-sponsored anniversaries, museums, monuments, and UNESCO-linked commemorations demonstrate modern heritage politics, not exclusive ownership of Majnun.

No documented extremist appropriation specific to Majnun comparable to the militant use of some warrior heroes emerged in the reviewed evidence. His political utility has principally been cultural-national, humanist, romantic, and occasionally countercultural. **Absence of evidence for a significant extremist cult.**

### Psychological readings

Modern psychiatry and popular psychology diagnose Majnun retrospectively as erotomanic, obsessive, psychotic, depressive, anorectic, or “limerent.” **[E8]** The medieval Arabic category *majnūn* mixes possession, madness, poetic inspiration, and social judgment and cannot be mapped confidently onto a modern diagnosis. Michael Dols’s work on madness in medieval Islamic society is useful for historical semantics; it cannot clinically diagnose a possibly fictional seventh-century person. **[E7]**

Structuralist and comparative readings class the story with separated lovers, courtly love, “Romeo and Juliet,” or a universal monomyth. The comparison can clarify plot functions but cannot establish borrowing without historical links. The widespread slogan “the Romeo and Juliet of the East” is modern marketing; Niẓāmī precedes Shakespeare, but both works have distinct sources and structures. **[E8]**

## 8. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Did Qays exist?

**Position A: a historical kernel.** I. Yu. Krachkovsky’s 1946 study argued that early citations and the formation of the tradition support some historical lover-poet or cluster of such poets behind Majnun. Later scholars commonly call him “semi-historical.” **[E7]**

**Position B: literary pseudonym or composite.** Early Muslim transmitters themselves report that “Majnun” never existed, or that an Umayyad lover placed his poems under an invented name and attached stories to them. Multiple identities, unstable genealogy, interchangeable lover motifs, late documentation, and heterogeneous poetry support a composite-persona hypothesis. **[E1/E7]**

**Position C: historical question undecidable and secondary.** Asʿad Khairallah and later literary scholars emphasize the way anecdotes interpret poems and poems authorize anecdotes. The recoverable object is the Majnun tradition, whether or not one Qays existed. **[E7]**

**Assessment:** no evidence presently permits a verified birth-to-death biography. A historical lover-poet is possible; “Qays ibn al-Mulawwaḥ, born 645 and died 688” is not securely demonstrated.

### Dating and textual growth

The approximately two-century gap between the presumed events and Ibn Qutayba is fundamental. Oral transmission is possible but cannot be reconstructed simply from later chains. **[E7]** The Arabic reports are “frame stories” around attributed verses; prose context may be later than the poems, and the poems themselves may have migrated among poets.

Niẓāmī is not the first witness to the pair but is the first surviving author to create the internationally canonical continuous romance. **[E1/E2]**

### Authenticity of the Dīwān

Abū Bakr al-Wālibī’s recension preserves a valuable medieval Majnun corpus, but:

- the language and style are not uniform;
- lines occur under other poets’ names;
- anecdotes motivate verses too neatly;
- later copyists and compilers expanded the corpus;
- no authorial manuscript exists.

Accordingly “Majnun’s poem” means “a poem transmitted under Majnun’s name,” unless an attribution can be independently strengthened. **[E7]**

### Arabic versus Persian priorities

Older national-literary histories sometimes describe Niẓāmī as merely versifying an existing Arabic romance. Modern comparison shows that he used Arabic motifs but imposed a new architecture, borrowed Persian romance conventions, expanded Nawfal’s role, created major subsidiary episodes, and transformed the ending. **[E7]**

Conversely, calling the whole story “Persian folklore” erases its Arabic *akhbār* and poetry. The defensible formulation is: **Arabic legendary-poetic nucleus; Persian canonical synthesis; multilingual Persianate afterlife.**

### Sufi interpretation

Majnun was usable for mystical comparison before and after Niẓāmī. Aḥmad Ghazālī, Sanāʾī, ʿAṭṭār, Rūmī, Jāmī, and Fużūlī progressively turn the lover’s loss of self into a model of *fanāʾ*, annihilation in the Beloved. **[E4/E5]**

Two cautions follow:

1. The earliest Arabic story is not simply a coded Sufi allegory.
2. Niẓāmī’s poem occupies the boundary between human and mystical love; reducing Layli to “God” removes the social, erotic, and gendered tragedy.

### Gender and coercion

The romance has often been celebrated as pure love while Layla’s confinement, threats, coerced marriage, reputational exposure, and reduced freedom are minimized. *Al-Aghānī* explicitly preserves a threat of mutilation in the Ward episode. **[E1]** Niẓāmī gives Layli emotional constancy and sexual resistance but less mobility and poetic publicity than Majnun. **[E2/E7]** The record contains no rape by Majnun; some versions make the husband attempt marital possession, which Layli resists.

### Tomb debate

- **Literary evidence:** Niẓāmī’s joint grave and pilgrimage. **[E2]**
- **Arabic death report:** body on rough stony ground. **[E1]**
- **Rajasthan tomb:** documented living folklore, not archaeologically linked to seventh-century Arabia. **[E6]**
- **Saudi place-memory:** meaningful cultural geography, not a verified burial. **[E6]**

Presenting any modern site as “the discovered tomb” is unsupported.

### Discredited or weak claims still circulating

- exact dates 645–688 as established facts; **[E8]**
- a contemporary seventh-century written *Dīwān*; **[E8]**
- death on Layla’s grave as the unambiguous *Kitāb al-Aghānī* account; **[E8]**
- the Rajasthan grave as archaeological proof; **[E8]**
- Niẓāmī as author of the original Arabic legend; **[E8]**
- the lovers as Qurʾanic figures; **[E8]**
- Layla and Majnun as an Islamic married saint-couple with an authenticated relic cult; **[E8]**
- a simple diagnosis of schizophrenia, erotomania, or jinn possession; **[E8]**
- a direct historical encounter with al-Khiḍr, Alexander, ʿAntar, or other heroes in the item brief. **[E8]**
