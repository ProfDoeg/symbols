# Dietrich von Bern: Research Dossier

**Traditional class:** mortal heroic king and exile; in the German poems an epic king and monster-fighting champion, in the *Nibelungenlied* an elder counselor and survivor, and in Christianized traditions a morally ambiguous hero carried away by a demonic horse. He is neither a god nor a demigod, and there is no securely attested cult of Dietrich as a heroized dead man.

**Earliest secure attestation:** the Old High German *Hildebrandslied*, copied at Fulda about 830–840 from an older exemplar. It explicitly names *Theotrihhe* and says Hildebrand fled east with Dietrich from Otacher/Odoacer. The Rök runestone in Östergötland, conventionally dated around 800, may be slightly earlier, but its supposed reference to “Þjóðríkr the bold” is disputed and cannot presently be treated as secure.

## Evidence labels used

- **[EA] Secure early attestation:** an early source unquestionably names Dietrich/Theodoric in the heroic tradition.
- **[LC] Later canonical elaboration:** a major medieval literary formulation.
- **[RV] Regional variant:** a geographically or linguistically localized version.
- **[LS] Learned systematization:** antiquarian, chronicle, or encyclopedic harmonization.
- **[SI] Syncretistic identification:** explicit equation with another person or tradition.
- **[MA] Medieval or early-modern accretion:** late development without secure early evidence.
- **[F] Folklore:** orally reported or etiological tradition.
- **[MSR] Modern scholarly reconstruction:** inference rather than primary testimony.
- **[MI] Modern invention or demonstrably modern popular elaboration.
- **[C] Contested evidence or interpretation.**

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

Dietrich von Bern is the legendary transformation of the historical Ostrogothic king Theoderic the Great, ruler of Italy from 493 to 526, but the legendary record is not a distorted biography that can simply be corrected back into history. It is an autonomous heroic tradition. Its defining narrative reverses Theoderic’s actual career: historical Theoderic entered Italy as an invader, defeated and killed Odoacer, and established his kingdom; legendary Dietrich is driven from his hereditary realm, spends decades as an exile with the Hunnish king Etzel/Attila, and eventually returns. Ermanaric, Attila, Odoacer, Gothic Italy, Burgundian legend, giant tales, dwarf narratives, and Christian accounts of Theoderic’s damnation were progressively combined around him.

### Names

| Form | Language/source | Meaning and status |
|---|---|---|
| **Theodericus / Theodoricus** | Latin historical and ecclesiastical sources, sixth century onward | Latinized Germanic royal name. |
| **Þiudareiks**, conventionally reconstructed | Gothic | Usually analyzed as *þiuda* “people, nation” + *reiks* “ruler”; thus “ruler of the people.” The exact Gothic spelling is reconstructed, not preserved as the hero’s name in a Gothic manuscript. **[MSR]** |
| **Theotrih / Theotrihhe** | *Hildebrandslied*, c. 830–840 | Old High German form; earliest unambiguous written naming of the legendary exile. **[EA]** |
| **Dietrich** | Middle High German | Regular later German development of the name, not a separate person. |
| **Dietrich von Bern** | Middle High German heroic poetry | “Dietrich of Bern”; *Bern* normally means Verona in this corpus. **[LC]** |
| **Þjóðrekr** | Eddic poetry | Old Norse form in *Guðrúnarkviða III* and prose framing associated with *Guðrúnarkviða II*. **[RV]** |
| **Þiðrekr af Bern** | Old Norse *Þiðreks saga* | Central Scandinavian prose form. **[LC/RV]** |
| **Didrik / Didrik av Bern** | Old Swedish *Didrikskrönikan* | Fifteenth-century Swedish adaptation. **[RV/MA]** |
| **Thidrek, Thidrekssaga** | Modern normalized scholarly usage | Modern spelling conventions, not a distinct medieval identity. |
| **Dietrich der Berner / von Berne** | German poems | Geographic epithet, “the man/lord from Bern.” |
| **Amelung** | Later heroic and Heldenbuch usage | Associates Dietrich with the Amal dynasty; historically Theoderic was an Amal. Its deployment inside a comprehensive heroic genealogy is later systematization. **[LS/MA]** |

### What is “Bern”?

In the dominant medieval German understanding, *Bern* is Verona: medieval German also used *Welschbern* for Verona. This identification fits the historical Theoderic’s Italian setting, although Ravenna, not Verona, was his principal capital. **[SI/LC]**

Heinz Ritter-Schaumburg argued in the 1980s that the saga’s Bern was Bonn, which occasionally bore Latinizing forms such as *Verona*, and that Þiðrekr was a forgotten Lower-Rhenish ruler rather than Theoderic. This is a **modern minority reconstruction**, not an attested medieval identification. It depends upon relocating Rome to Trier and numerous saga place-names to northern Germany. Mainstream philology rejects the method because it treats a thirteenth-century compilation’s mobile heroic geography as literal late-antique reportage and gives the derivative fifteenth-century Swedish version unusual priority. **[MSR/C]**

### Identifications

- **Dietrich = Theoderic the Great:** already presupposed by the name, Verona, Odoacer, Amal associations, and medieval historiography; explicitly systematized by chroniclers. **[SI]**
- **Dietrich = a Bonn-based petty king:** Ritter-Schaumburg’s modern hypothesis; unattested in ancient or medieval evidence. **[MSR/C]**
- **Dietrich as an Odinic or deified ruler on the Rök stone:** proposed especially by twentieth-century Germanic-religion scholarship, notably Otto Höfler; dependent on a contested reading and unsupported by independent cult evidence. **[MSR/C]**
- **Dietrich as a leader of the Wild Hunt:** occasionally inferred from infernal-rider imagery and later folklore. The medieval sources more securely show a damned or penitent supernatural rider, not an established identity with Wodan/Odin. **[MA/F/C]**

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

## The Rök stone

The Rök runestone, Ög 136, was raised by Varinn for his dead son Vémóðr in Östergötland, Sweden, probably around 800. The traditional interpretation reads a stanza approximately as:

> “Then ruled Theodoric the bold, prince of sea-warriors, over the shores of the Hreið-sea; now he sits armed on his Gothic horse, shield strapped, prince of the Mærings.”

On that reading, the stone would be the earliest evidence for heroic Theoderic in Scandinavia. **[EA/C]**

That reading is no longer secure. Bo Ralph argued in 2007 that the relevant runes need not contain the personal name Þjóðríkr; Per Holmberg and collaborators subsequently interpreted the passage as part of riddling discourse about the sun, cosmic threat, and the renewal of light. Their 2020 study relates the inscription to Eddic wisdom poetry and memories of climatic catastrophe. Klaus Johan Myrvoll’s 2026 reassessment defends the traditional Theodoric interpretation on syntactic and metrical grounds and disputes the newer reading. The disagreement includes whether *raiþ* means “rode” or “ruled.” The stone therefore supplies **possible but contested evidence**, not the earliest secure naming.

## Earliest unambiguous witness: the *Hildebrandslied*

The *Hildebrandslied* survives on two reused blank leaves of a Kassel theological codex, written at Fulda by two scribes around 830–840. Its mixed Old High German/Old Saxon linguistic form points to copying and adaptation from an older exemplar. Hadubrand says that his father Hildebrand went east with “Theotrih” because of Otacher’s hostility. Dietrich never enters the dramatic scene, but his exile organizes the entire tragedy. The poem does not name Attila, narrate Dietrich’s birth, or say how Dietrich regained his kingdom. **[EA]**

The first modern edition was published by Johann Georg von Eckhart in 1729. Chemical attempts to improve the manuscript’s legibility subsequently damaged portions of it.

## Early external and allusive evidence

- ***Waldere***, Old English, surviving in two tenth- or eleventh-century manuscript fragments: Waldere invokes an episode in which Widia rescued Theodoric from captivity among monsters. This is early evidence for a monster-captivity story and for Dietrich’s connection with Widia/Wittich. **[EA]**
- ***Deor***, Old English, preserved in the late-tenth-century Exeter Book: its obscure *Þeodric* stanza says that Þeodric possessed the Mærings’ stronghold for thirty years. Identification with legendary Theoderic is traditional but the stanza’s syntax and referent remain debated. **[EA/C]**
- **Rök stone**, c. 800: possible Theodoric, as discussed above. **[C]**

## Eddic material

- ***Guðrúnarkviða III***, Old Norse heroic poem, probably twelfth century or earlier but surviving in the thirteenth-century *Codex Regius*: Guðrún is accused by Herkja of sleeping with Þjóðrekr. Guðrún clears herself by ordeal. The poem places Þjóðrekr at Atli’s court and says that he lost thirty warriors. **[RV]**
- ***Guðrúnarkviða II***: Guðrún’s lament is addressed to Þjóðrekr only in its prose framing. The poem itself neither names him nor unmistakably establishes an addressee. The prose editor probably imported the situation from *Guðrúnarkviða III*. **[RV/C]**
- Dietrich does **not** appear as an actor in *Atlakviða*, *Atlamál*, or the Sigurð poems proper, although their characters were later joined to his cycle.

## German high-medieval corpus

- ***Nibelungenlied***, composed c. 1200; principal manuscripts early thirteenth century: Dietrich is the pre-eminent exile at Etzel’s court. He tries to restrain the conflict, mourns his retainers, captures Hagen and Gunther, and hands them alive to Kriemhild. **[LC]**
- ***Nibelungenklage***, c. 1200–1220: elaborates the mourning and aftermath and anticipates Dietrich’s eventual departure with Hildebrand and Herrat. **[LC]**
- ***Alpharts Tod***, thirteenth century, incomplete: young Alphart, one of Dietrich’s warriors, fights Witege and Heime and is treacherously killed. **[LC]**
- ***Dietrichs Flucht***, late thirteenth century, 10,152 rhyming couplets: Dietrich loses his realm to Ermrich/Ermanaric, reaches Etzel, and repeatedly sacrifices men and wealth in attempts to return. Five principal manuscripts survive, the earliest around 1300. **[LC]**
- ***Rabenschlacht***, late thirteenth century: Dietrich fights at Raben/Ravenna; the young Etzel sons Ort and Scharpfe and Dietrich’s brother Diether are killed by Witege. Dietrich pursues Witege in furious grief. It commonly circulates with *Dietrichs Flucht*. **[LC]**
- ***Dietrich und Wenezlan***, thirteenth-century fragment: Dietrich fights the Polish king Wenezlan. **[RV/LC]**

## “Fantastical” or *aventiurehaft* Dietrich epics

The division between “historical” and “fantastical” epics is modern scholarship’s classification, not a medieval genre label.

- ***Eckenlied***, attested from the early thirteenth century: Dietrich fights the giant Ecke, kills him after a long duel, and subsequently meets Fasolt. Multiple versions significantly alter the conclusion and Dietrich’s degree of guilt. **[LC]**
- ***Goldemar***, attributed to Albrecht von Kemenaten, c. 1230, surviving only in fragments: Dietrich confronts the dwarf king Goldemar. **[LC]**
- ***Laurin***, probably thirteenth century, in extensively varying later manuscripts: Dietrich and companions invade dwarf-king Laurin’s rose garden; Laurin captures them before they escape and overcome him. Later versions add reconciliation and Walberan. **[LC/RV]**
- ***Virginal***, thirteenth century in several divergent redactions: an inexperienced Dietrich and Hildebrand assist Queen Virginal against the heathen Orkise, giants, and dragons; Dietrich is captured and rescued. **[LC]**
- ***Sigenot***, thirteenth century: the giant Sigenot defeats and imprisons Dietrich; Hildebrand ultimately kills Sigenot and frees him. The poem likely expands the older captivity motif but Sigenot himself has no early attestation. **[LC/MA]**
- ***Rosengarten zu Worms***, thirteenth century: Kriemhild stages contests between Dietrich’s heroes and the Burgundian/Siegfried group. Dietrich reluctantly fights Siegfried; in prominent versions his rage and fiery breath help overcome him. **[LC]**
- ***Wunderer***, probably fifteenth century: young Dietrich at Etzel’s court saves a maiden from the cannibalistic Wunderer. Its conclusion connects him with the demonic horse and perpetual dragon combat. **[MA]**

## Scandinavian synthesis

***Þiðreks saga af Bern*** was compiled in Old Norse, probably at the Norwegian royal court in Bergen around 1250. Its oldest substantial manuscript, Stockholm Perg. fol. nr 4, dates about 1275–1300; Icelandic paper manuscripts preserve further recensions, and the Old Swedish *Didrikskrönikan* is a fifteenth-century adaptation.

It is the only medieval work to fashion almost the entire material into a continuous life of Þiðrekr. It incorporates the stories of Velent/Wieland, Viðga/Witege, Sigurðr/Siegfried, the Niflungs, Attila, Ermanaric, Walther, Herburt, King Isung, and the Wilzen. The work repeatedly claims German oral or written informants. Scholars agree on substantial Low German input but dispute whether the compiler translated a lost Low German prose work or assembled shorter oral and written materials in Norway. **[LC/RV/C]**

## Late systematization

- **Heldenbuch-Prosa**, appearing with manuscript and printed Heldenbücher from the late fifteenth century: constructs a providential history of the entire heroic age, ending with Dietrich as its last survivor. **[LS/MA]**
- ***Jüngeres Hildebrandslied***, attested from the fifteenth century and widely printed in the sixteenth: gives the father-son combat a reconciliatory ending, unlike the unresolved older poem. **[MA/F]**
- **Old Swedish *Didrikskrönikan***, fifteenth century: abbreviates, rationalizes, and geographically reshapes the Old Norse saga. **[RV/MA]**

## Texts named in the comparative brief but not direct Dietrich sources

Absence is important here:

- ***Völsunga saga*** does not make Dietrich an actor. Its Sigurðr–Brynhildr–Guðrún tradition overlaps with material incorporated into *Þiðreks saga*, but it is not independent evidence for Dietrich.
- ***Hervarar saga*, *Ragnars saga loðbrókar*, and *Gautreks saga*** do not provide a Dietrich cycle.
- **Saxo Grammaticus’s *Gesta Danorum*** does not supply a substantive Dietrich narrative.
- ***Beowulf*** does not name Dietrich. Its references to Hama, the Brosing necklace, Eormenric, Widia’s world, giants, and dragon fighting belong to the wider heroic environment. Tolkien’s “Beowulf: The Monsters and the Critics” concerns the poetic function of monsters in *Beowulf*, not evidence for Dietrich.
- ***Grettis saga*** is useful comparatively for strongman, outlaw, revenant, and monster motifs but supplies no secure Dietrich interaction.
- ***Egils saga*** and the Icelandic family sagas generally do not document Dietrich’s deeds.
- The **Skaldic Poetry of the Scandinavian Middle Ages** corpus does not furnish a secure early skaldic Dietrich poem.

## 3. The Story

## Parentage and youth

In *Þiðreks saga*, King Þetmarr/Thetmar of Bern and Queen Odilia are Þiðrekr’s parents. Hildebrand becomes his tutor and weapons-master. As a youth, Þiðrekr kills the giantess Hild and the giant Grim, with the dwarf Alfrikr’s help, and acquires the helmet Hildegrim and sword Nagelring. He later gains the horse Falke through Heime. **[LC/RV]**

German poems usually presuppose rather than narrate his upbringing. *Dietrichs Flucht* calls Dietmar his father and gives him a younger brother, Diether. The historical Theoderic’s father was Theodemir, one of the Amal brothers; legendary Dietmar transparently preserves that name, but the narrative relationships should not be projected back as biography. **[SI/LC]**

## The hero-band

The saga makes Dietrich a magnet for challengers and companions. He fights or tests Heime, Viðga, Þetleifr, and other champions, then incorporates them into his following. The “twelve heroes” configuration is clearest in the later synthesis and resembles courtly and Carolingian heroic groupings; it is not securely attested in the ninth-century material. **[LC/MA]**

## Monster combats

The older *Waldere* allusion already knows Theodoric imprisoned among monsters and rescued by Widia. Thirteenth-century poems repeatedly vary this structure:

- In *Eckenlied*, Ecke seeks Dietrich to prove his own fame. Dietrich kills him only after a brutal, exhausting duel; some versions emphasize Dietrich’s reluctance and Ecke’s refusal to desist.
- In *Sigenot*, Sigenot defeats and imprisons Dietrich; Hildebrand saves him.
- In *Virginal*, Dietrich fights giants and dragons, is captured, and must be rescued by his allies.
- In *Laurin*, Dietrich destroys the boundary of Laurin’s rose garden, refuses or contests its toll, is magically overcome, and becomes a prisoner before escaping.
- In *Þiðreks saga*, the Grim episode, giant combats, and dragon episodes are distributed through the synthetic “life.”

These tales do not portray an invariably victorious superhero. Dietrich’s susceptibility to captivity, his dependence on Hildebrand and companions, and the danger of uncontrolled anger are recurrent features.

## Laurin and the dwarfs

In the principal *Laurin* tradition, the dwarf king’s rose garden is protected by a golden thread and threatened by anyone who enters. Dietrich and Witege violate it. Laurin’s belt gives him immense strength, and his cloak can conceal him. Laurin captures Dietrich and his companions in his mountain realm; Künhilt and her brother Dietleib help release them. Laurin is defeated and, in some versions, taken to Bern.

Later South Tyrolean folklore makes the red evening glow on the Rosengarten mountains the betrayed reflection of Laurin’s roses. That picturesque explanation is **folkloric and modernly popularized**, not evidence for the thirteenth-century poem’s original formation.

## Fire-breath

Dietrich’s fiery breath is medieval, not a modern fantasy invention, but it is not part of the earliest record. In *Rosengarten zu Worms*, Dietrich grows terrifyingly hot or breathes fire during his reluctant combat with Siegfried; the heat overcomes Siegfried’s horn-hard skin or forces his submission, depending on recension. In *Þiðreks saga*, fire or extreme heat issues from Þiðrekr when his rage is aroused, notably in combat. **[LC]**

The motif is variously explained as heroic superhumanity, rage externalized, or adaptation of dragon-like qualities. No source makes him a god or literally gives him draconic parentage. **[MSR]**

## Exile

The common legendary structure is:

1. Dietrich rules Bern.
2. His uncle or kinsman Ermanaric/Ermrich, assisted by the evil counselor Sibich/Sifka, attacks or dispossesses him.
3. Dietrich flees with Hildebrand and surviving retainers.
4. Etzel receives him.
5. Dietrich remains at the Hunnish court for decades.
6. Campaigns to regain his realm end in costly victories or failures.
7. He eventually returns after Ermanaric’s death or defeat.

The *Hildebrandslied* preserves an older configuration: Dietrich and Hildebrand flee from **Otacher/Odoacer**, not Ermanaric. The substitution of Ermanaric as principal persecutor is therefore a development, not the original form demonstrable from the earliest German text. **[EA versus LC]**

Historical chronology is inverted. Ermanaric died around 375; Attila died in 453; Theoderic was born around 454; Odoacer was killed by Theoderic in 493. Frutolf of Michelsberg already observed around 1100 that vernacular songs made men contemporaries whom Jordanes placed in different periods. He called attention to, rather than solved, the contradiction. **[LS]**

## Etzel’s court and the Burgundian catastrophe

In the *Nibelungenlied*, Dietrich is already an exile at Etzel’s court. He warns the arriving Burgundians, attempts mediation, and initially stays outside the fighting. After all his men except Hildebrand are killed, Dietrich enters the hall. He defeats Hagen and Gunther separately, binds them, and delivers them alive to Kriemhild. She has Gunther beheaded and kills Hagen herself; Dietrich neither orders nor performs those executions. Hildebrand then kills Kriemhild. **[LC]**

In *Þiðreks saga*, the Niflung downfall is integrated differently. Þiðrekr fights on Attila’s side; Hǫgni survives long enough to father a posthumous avenger, Aldrian, who later entombs Attila with the Niflung treasure. This is a distinct Scandinavian-Low German configuration. **[RV]**

The Eddic *Guðrúnarkviða III* preserves another fragment: Þjóðrekr has lost thirty men and is accused of sexual relations with Guðrún. Guðrún undergoes an ordeal and is vindicated; Herkja is punished. No German Dietrich epic makes this accusation central. **[RV]**

## *Dietrichs Flucht*

The poem gives Dietrich a political and ethical crisis. Ermrich expands by treachery and violence; Dietrich’s followers repeatedly ransom him and support renewed campaigns. Helche supplies treasure to reward his warriors. The narrator’s famous excursus condemns arbitrary princely rule and contrasts Dietrich’s loyal reciprocity with Ermrich’s tyranny. It has been associated with a poet named Heinrich der Vogler, although differences between *Flucht* and *Rabenschlacht* prevent confident common authorship. **[LC/C]**

## The Battle of Ravenna

In *Rabenschlacht*, Dietrich campaigns from Etzel’s court against Ermrich at Raben, the heroic form of Ravenna. The young Ort and Scharpfe, Etzel’s sons, and Diether, Dietrich’s brother, are placed under protection but enter combat. Witege kills them. Dietrich pursues Witege to the sea; Witege escapes through supernatural or extraordinary assistance depending on the version. The military result is overshadowed by Dietrich’s grief and failure as guardian. **[LC]**

*Þiðreks saga* relocates or renames the corresponding battle as Gransport/Gronsport and integrates Viðga’s shifting allegiance differently. The correspondence is strong, but neither text is simply a transcription of the other. **[RV]**

## Return

In *Þiðreks saga*, the aged Þiðrekr eventually recovers his realm and remains a warrior into old age. The *Nibelungenklage* anticipates his departure after the catastrophe. German historical epics concentrate more on exile and attempted return than on a triumphant settled reign.

## Death and supernatural ending

There is no single canonical death.

- ***Þiðreks saga*:** its last episodes recount the aging king’s final combats and vengeance; the transmitted saga does not present the same fully developed Christian hell-ride as late German texts. **[LC/RV]**
- **Ecclesiastical Theoderic tradition:** Pope Gregory the Great’s *Dialogues* (late sixth century) reports a vision in which the historical Theoderic is thrown into the volcanic crater of Vulcano as punishment for killing Boethius’s father-in-law Symmachus and Pope John I. This is damnation propaganda concerning the Arian king, later available for combination with heroic Dietrich. **[EA for hostile Theoderic reception; SI for Dietrich]**
- **San Zeno reliefs, Verona, c. 1138–1140:** a foolish king, *regem stultum*, hunts with horn, falcon, and hounds; a deer lures him toward a demonic destination. These reliefs are widely interpreted as Theoderic’s infernal ride. **[MA/iconographic]**
- **Later German tradition:** Dietrich, sometimes naked from his bath, mounts a suddenly appearing black horse and is carried away. In hostile readings he goes to hell; in *Wunderer* he remains alive until Judgment Day, fighting dragons as divinely imposed penance. **[MA]**
- **Heldenbuch-Prosa:** Dietrich is the last survivor of the heroic age and disappears rather than receiving an ordinary heroic burial. **[LS/MA]**
- **Wild-Hunt folklore:** later observers sometimes assimilated the mounted, never-resting Dietrich to the Wild Hunt. The identification is secondary and cannot establish pre-Christian Dietrich worship. **[F/C]**

No securely identified medieval “tomb of Dietrich von Bern” exists.

## 4. Relations with Gods and Other Heroes

## Competing genealogies

| Tradition | Father | Mother | Brother/close kin | Usurper |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Historical Theoderic | Theodemir | Ereleuva/Erelieva | historical Amal kin | none corresponding to the legendary story |
| *Dietrichs Flucht* / *Rabenschlacht* | Dietmar | usually undeveloped | Diether | uncle Ermrich |
| *Þiðreks saga* | Þetmarr | Odilia | saga genealogy extends through Samson and Bern’s royal house | Erminrik |
| Heldenbuch tradition | Amal/Amelung genealogy systematized | variable | Diether and broad heroic kinship | Ermenrich |

The apparent correspondence Theodemir → Dietmar/Þetmarr supports derivation from Theoderic tradition, but the full genealogies are literary constructions.

## Consorts and children

Dietrich normally has no prominent dynastic son. In *Þiðreks saga* he marries Herrad/Herrat, a relative of King Erminrik in the saga’s political network. German texts also connect Herrat with his final return. The absence of a celebrated heir assists the late Heldenbuch presentation of Dietrich as the last hero, but this is literary patterning, not evidence of ritual celibacy.

## Documented interactions with named divine beings and heroes

The following inventory separates direct textual encounters from later association. “Hero” includes named heroic kings, warriors, dwarfs, giants, and legendary women within the corpus.

- **Hildebrand** — tutor, weapons-master, chief counselor, exile companion, and rescuer; *Hildebrandslied* c. 830–840, *Nibelungenlied* c. 1200, Dietrich epics and *Þiðreks saga* thirteenth century. **[EA/LC]**
- **Hadubrand** — no direct Dietrich encounter; Hadubrand knows Dietrich as the lord for whom his father fled; *Hildebrandslied*, c. 830–840. **[EA]**
- **Odoacer/Otacher** — persecutor from whom Dietrich and Hildebrand flee in the *Hildebrandslied*; historical victim of Theoderic is narratively reversed. **[EA/SI]**
- **Widia/Witege/Viðga/Wittich** — rescues Dietrich from monsters in *Waldere*; later companion, rival, deserter, and killer of Diether and Etzel’s sons; *Waldere*, tenth/eleventh century, then German epics and *Þiðreks saga*. **[EA/LC]**
- **Ermanaric/Ermrich/Erminrik** — usurping uncle or kinsman who drives Dietrich into exile; *Dietrichs Flucht*, *Rabenschlacht*, *Þiðreks saga*, thirteenth century. **[LC]**
- **Sibich/Sifka** — malicious counselor whose intrigue helps produce persecution and war; German heroic tradition and *Þiðreks saga*. **[LC]**
- **Etzel/Atli/Attila** — host and patron during exile, military ally; Eddic setting, *Nibelungenlied*, *Dietrichs Flucht*, *Rabenschlacht*, *Þiðreks saga*. **[LC/RV]**
- **Helche/Erka** — Etzel’s queen; protects, finances, or supports Dietrich and his men; *Dietrichs Flucht* and related tradition. **[LC]**
- **Ort and Scharpfe** — Etzel’s sons entrusted to Dietrich and killed by Witege; *Rabenschlacht*, late thirteenth century. **[LC]**
- **Diether** — Dietrich’s younger brother, killed by Witege at Ravenna/Gransport; *Rabenschlacht* and *Þiðreks saga*. **[LC]**
- **Kriemhild/Grimhild/Guðrún** — Dietrich warns and opposes her in the *Nibelungenlied*; she receives his captives; in *Guðrúnarkviða III* she is falsely accused of sleeping with Þjóðrekr; in *Þiðreks saga* their relationship belongs to the Niflung catastrophe. **[LC/RV]**
- **Herkja** — accuses Guðrún of adultery with Þjóðrekr; Guðrún’s ordeal proves the charge false; *Guðrúnarkviða III*. **[RV]**
- **Gunther/Gunnarr** — Dietrich captures him alive after the hall battle; *Nibelungenlied*, c. 1200. In the saga tradition they fight on opposing sides in the Niflung catastrophe. **[LC/RV]**
- **Hagen/Hǫgni** — Dietrich defeats and binds him in the *Nibelungenlied*; saga versions place them on opposite sides. **[LC/RV]**
- **Siegfried/Sigurðr** — famous duel in *Rosengarten zu Worms*, where Dietrich’s rage and heat overcome him; also encountered in *Þiðreks saga*. The duel is not in the early Sigurð poems or *Völsunga saga*. **[LC; famous pairing absent from early Norse sources]**
- **Brünhild/Brynhildr** — belongs to the heroic complex incorporated into *Þiðreks saga* but has no major securely independent Dietrich episode. **[LC/RV]**
- **Rüdiger of Bechelaren** — fellow leader at Etzel’s court; Dietrich warns the Burgundians and later laments Rüdiger’s death; *Nibelungenlied*. **[LC]**
- **Volker** — enemy in the hall battle and killer/opponent of Dietrich’s men; *Nibelungenlied*. **[LC]**
- **Wolfhart** — Dietrich’s fierce retainer; fights the Burgundians and dies after killing Giselher; *Nibelungenlied*. **[LC]**
- **Giselher** — Burgundian prince killed by Wolfhart during the conflict Dietrich initially avoids; *Nibelungenlied*. **[LC]**
- **Alphart** — young retainer whose death by Witege and Heime is a major loss to Dietrich; *Alpharts Tod*. **[LC]**
- **Heime/Heimir** — challenges Dietrich, is defeated or recruited, later deserts or opposes him in some traditions; *Þiðreks saga*, *Alpharts Tod*, and related poems. **[LC]**
- **Þetleifr/Dietleib** — joins Dietrich’s champion-band; assists in the Laurin tradition; *Þiðreks saga*, *Biterolf und Dietleib*, *Laurin*. **[LC]**
- **Biterolf** — heroic associate and father of Dietleib; *Biterolf und Dietleib* and connected Dietrich tradition. **[LC]**
- **Ecke** — giant challenger killed by Dietrich; *Eckenlied*, early thirteenth century onward. **[LC]**
- **Fasolt/Fasold** — Ecke’s brother or companion; confronts Dietrich and in some versions joins or reaches an accommodation with him; *Eckenlied*, *Þiðreks saga*. **[LC/RV]**
- **Sigenot** — giant who defeats and imprisons Dietrich; *Sigenot*, thirteenth century. **[LC]**
- **Eggerich** — dwarf associated with Sigenot’s story; *Sigenot*. **[LC/MA]**
- **Hild and Grim** — giant pair defeated in Dietrich’s youth; *Þiðreks saga*, thirteenth century, with allusive connections in German poems. **[LC/RV]**
- **Alfrikr/Alberich** — dwarf who assists Dietrich against Grim and supplies heroic equipment; *Þiðreks saga*. **[LC/RV]**
- **Laurin** — dwarf king whose rose garden Dietrich violates; captor, enemy, and in reconciliatory versions subordinate or ally; *Laurin*, thirteenth century and later. **[LC]**
- **Walberan** — Laurin’s powerful kinsman; initially threatens Dietrich’s party and later reaches settlement; later *Laurin/Walberan* continuation. **[MA]**
- **Künhilt** — captive or bride in Laurin’s mountain who assists Dietrich’s party’s escape; *Laurin*. **[LC]**
- **Virginal** — queen whom Dietrich assists against Orkise and monsters; *Virginal*. **[LC]**
- **Orkise** — heathen antagonist oppressing Virginal; killed or overcome by Dietrich’s side; *Virginal*. **[LC]**
- **Wunderer** — cannibalistic pursuer killed by Dietrich; *Wunderer*, probably fifteenth century. **[MA]**
- **The nameless maiden in *Wunderer*** — rescued by Dietrich and gives him a blessing connected with his final fate. **[MA]**
- **King Isung** — ruler whose champions compete against Dietrich’s heroes; *Þiðreks saga*. **[LC/RV]**
- **Sigurd Svein** — Isung’s champion and counterpart of Siegfried in portions of the saga complex; fights within the tournament cycle; *Þiðreks saga*. **[LC/RV]**
- **Wenezlan** — Polish king who fights Dietrich; *Dietrich und Wenezlan*, thirteenth-century fragment. **[LC]**
- **Wieland/Velent** — no major direct early encounter; as Viðga’s father and maker of Mimung, his craftsmanship affects Dietrich’s combats; *Þiðreks saga*. **[LC/RV]**
- **Odin/Wodan** — no secure direct interaction in the medieval Dietrich corpus. An Odinic interpretation of the Rök stone or infernal ride is modern and contested. **[MSR/C]**
- **The Christian God** — in *Wunderer*, Dietrich’s continuing dragon combat is interpreted as penance until Judgment Day; divine action is reported rather than dramatized as a face-to-face encounter. **[MA]**
- **The Devil/demonic horse** — carries Dietrich away in Christianized ending traditions and the infernal-hunt iconography; San Zeno c. 1138–1140 and later German accounts. **[MA]**
- **Dragons** — unnamed monsters fought in *Virginal*, *Þiðreks saga*, and the *Wunderer* ending. They are creatures, not separately named divine beings. **[LC/MA]**

No secure text records Dietrich meeting Thor, Freyr, Loki, Baldr, or the valkyries. Modern illustrations that place him generically among “Norse gods” are inventions.

## 5. Cult, Tomb, and Ritual

There is **no secure evidence that Dietrich von Bern received cult**.

No excavated *heroon*, votive deposit, sacrificial calendar, priesthood, festival, oracle, relic translation, or theophoric naming practice can be assigned to the legendary hero. The historical Theoderic had a monumental mausoleum at Ravenna, constructed in the early sixth century, but that royal tomb is not evidence of heroic cult. Medieval removal or loss of the king’s body and later stories about his damnation do not convert the mausoleum into a Dietrich shrine.

The Rök stone is commemorative and riddling, not a shrine. Even if its traditional Theodoric reading is accepted, it would prove remembrance of a famous ruler, not sacrifice or worship. Höfler’s claim that it reflected an apotheosis or Germanic ruler cult exceeds the inscription’s evidence. **[MSR/C]**

The San Zeno reliefs are Christian monumental imagery: they admonish through the punishment of a foolish or heretical king. They are not devotional images of Dietrich. **[MA]**

Verona, Ravenna, the Rosengarten mountains, and locations claimed in later tourism preserve memory and place-association. None has yielded archaeological proof of a Dietrich cult or legendary tomb.

## 6. Historical Evolution of the Figure

### c. 500–600: Theoderic remembered

Contemporary and near-contemporary sources present the historical king in radically different ways. Cassiodorus and the *Variae* articulate legitimate Roman-Gothic kingship; Jordanes places him in Gothic history; the *Anonymus Valesianus* becomes hostile in its latter portion; Boethian and papal traditions remember executions and religious conflict. Gregory the Great’s late-sixth-century vision sends Theoderic to volcanic punishment. These are antecedents to, not yet the complete Dietrich legend. **[historical substrate]**

### c. 800–1050: Heroic exile and monster tradition

The contested Rök stone may show Scandinavian knowledge around 800. The *Hildebrandslied* securely shows by c. 830–840 an exile tradition involving Dietrich, Hildebrand, and Odoacer. *Deor* and *Waldere* show an English-speaking heroic environment in which Theodoric of the Mærings and captivity among monsters could be invoked allusively. The stories were already assumed knowledge rather than newly explained biography. **[EA]**

### Eleventh–twelfth centuries: Criticism and Christianization

Frutolf around 1100 recognizes that heroic song contradicts learned chronology. The San Zeno portal around 1138–1140 visualizes the infernal Theoderic hunt. Eddic tradition places Þjóðrekr at Atli’s court and makes him the object of an adultery accusation. Heroic, historical, and ecclesiastical memories coexist rather than replace one another. **[RV/LS/MA]**

### c. 1200–1300: Canonical expansion

The *Nibelungenlied* makes Dietrich the ethically weighty exile and last great opponent of Hagen and Gunther. The German Dietrich epics develop two overlapping modes: wars of exile and return, and monster/dwarf adventures. *Þiðreks saga* creates the fullest synthetic life, drawing much of continental heroic legend into Dietrich’s orbit. **[LC]**

### Fourteenth–sixteenth centuries: Proliferation and closure

Manuscripts, illustrated Heldenbücher, the *Jüngeres Hildebrandslied*, Old Swedish *Didrikskrönikan*, and printed epics transmit increasingly systematized cycles. The Heldenbuch-Prosa explains why the heroes disappeared and makes Dietrich the final survivor. Christian eschatology transforms endless heroic combat into penance. **[LS/MA]**

### Seventeenth–eighteenth centuries: Decline and rediscovery

Printed popular versions continued into the seventeenth century, but living German heroic song diminished. Antiquarians recovered manuscripts; Eckhart printed the *Hildebrandslied* in 1729. The figure shifted from broad entertainment toward philological monument.

### Nineteenth century: National philology

Germanists edited the epics as remnants of a national heroic antiquity. Romantic and nationalist scholarship sought lost original lays, tribal memories, or unified epics behind the manuscripts. Unlike the *Nibelungenlied*, the Dietrich cycle never acquired one universally canonical modern national poem. Felix Dahn’s *Ein Kampf um Rom* (1876) popularized the historical Theoderic and Gothic Italy, influencing the imaginative field without retelling Dietrich’s legendary exile cycle. **[modern reception]**

### Twentieth century: Ideology and genre reassessment

Völkisch and National Socialist scholarship sometimes treated Germanic heroic literature as racial-national inheritance. Otto Höfler’s leader-cult theories connected Theoderic, the Rök stone, warrior associations, and sacral kingship. These claims must be separated from the primary evidence; the Rök inscription does not demonstrate a Dietrich cult.

Postwar scholarship increasingly studied the surviving texts as thirteenth-century literary works shaped by courtly romance, manuscript culture, and changing political ethics, rather than as transparent fragments of Migration-Age history.

### Twenty-first century: Material, textual, and runological revision

Digital manuscripts, manuscript censuses, new fragments such as an additional *Virginal* witness, and full-text corpora have sharpened transmission history. The Rök reinterpretation displaced Theodoric from what had long seemed the oldest witness; the 2026 counterargument shows that the matter remains open. Recent work on the “captivity among monsters” motif stresses variation across *Waldere*, *Virginal*, *Sigenot*, and *Laurin* instead of reconstructing a single lost original.

## 7. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Literature and scholarship

- **Felix Dahn, *Ein Kampf um Rom* (1876):** historical novel about Ostrogothic Italy; enormously influential in German images of the Goths and Theoderic, but not a faithful Dietrich epic. **[modern reinvention]**
- **Romantic and national editions:** nineteenth-century editors attempted to recover a German national heroic past through the *Hildebrandslied*, *Nibelungenlied*, and Dietrich poems.
- **J. R. R. Tolkien:** discussed the Germanic heroic world in scholarship and creatively reused its names and atmospheres. His “Beowulf: The Monsters and the Critics” (1936) does not establish Dietrich material in *Beowulf*. The name “Théodred” and Rohirric heroic diction evoke Germanic naming systems but are not adaptations of Dietrich’s narrative. **[MI where direct equations are claimed]**
- **Modern retellings and fantasy novels:** frequently combine Dietrich’s historical and legendary lives, treat fire-breath as an innate magical power, or introduce Norse gods. Such combinations are modern unless tied to the medieval passages identified above.

### Visual art

- Medieval manuscript title images show Dietrich fighting giants, dwarfs, or human opponents; illustrated witnesses include *Sigenot*, *Eckenlied*, *Laurin*, *Rosengarten*, *Virginal*, and *Wunderer*.
- Nineteenth-century historicist artists such as Johannes Gehrts illustrated Þiðrekr and the dwarf Alfrikr in a Romantic Germanic mode. These images strongly shaped modern visual expectations but are not reconstructions of medieval costume. **[MI]**
- South Tyrolean tourism and illustration made Laurin’s rose garden and the alpenglow legend more prominent than they were in early textual transmission. **[F/MI]**

### Film and popular media

Cinema adaptations of the Nibelung story often omit Dietrich because they concentrate on Siegfried and Kriemhild. Fritz Lang’s *Die Nibelungen* (1924) does not reproduce the full second-half Dietrich role of the medieval poem. Later screen retellings freely redistribute Hagen’s defeat, Kriemhild’s death, and Etzel’s court. **[MI where presented as medieval plot]**

A German-language *Dietrich von Bern* comic by Peter Wiechmann and Rafael Méndez appeared in the *Fix und Foxi Extra* environment from 1981 to 1983 and has subsequently been republished. It is a modern adventure synthesis, not a transmitted medieval cycle.

Dietrich or Theoderic appears intermittently in strategy games, role-playing games, comics, and online mythology catalogues. Common modern inventions include:

- making him a literal fire dragon or dragon-descendant;
- assigning him divine parentage;
- treating the black horse as his regular heroic mount—his ordinary saga horse is Falke;
- identifying him unambiguously with Odin’s Wild Hunt;
- presenting Ritter-Schaumburg’s Bonn localization as established history;
- calling the Rök stone uncontested proof of Dietrich;
- inserting him into *Beowulf*, *Völsunga saga*, or Viking religion as an active character.

### Nationalist and extremist appropriation

Dietrich was available to German nationalism as a supposedly pan-German heroic king, but his political reception was less central than Arminius, Siegfried, the *Nibelungenlied*, or Wagner’s gods. Nineteenth-century and völkisch philology nevertheless elevated him as “the foremost German hero,” often treating linguistic Germanic unity as racial or national continuity.

Under National Socialism, Germanic heroic scholarship, warrior-band theory, and ideas of sacral leadership could be made congenial to racial-national ideology. Höfler’s interpretation of Theoderic as an object of leader cult and his use of Rök evidence belong to this intellectual setting. It would be inaccurate, however, to claim a single large official “Dietrich cult” or a major Nazi state ritual devoted specifically to him. **Absence of evidence is material here.**

## 8. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

## Was there a historical Dietrich distinct from Theoderic?

The mainstream position is that Dietrich ultimately derives from Theoderic the Great. The evidence is cumulative:

- equivalent name;
- Bern = Verona;
- Dietmar/Þetmarr ≈ Theodemir;
- association with Odoacer;
- Italian and Ravenna settings;
- Amal/Amelung identity;
- medieval historiographers’ own equation.

The differences—exile with Attila, persecution by Ermanaric, northern geography, and reversed victory over Odoacer—are evidence of legendary transformation, not proof of another king.

Ritter-Schaumburg’s Bonn theory treats geography in *Þiðreks saga* as an encoded historical itinerary and the Old Swedish version as independently valuable. Critics respond that heroic narratives systematically move persons and places; that the Swedish text is generally derivative; and that no independent late-antique evidence supplies the proposed Bonn ruler. The theory remains popular outside mainstream medieval studies partly because it promises archaeological localization of famous stories. **[MSR/C]**

## How old is the exile legend?

The *Hildebrandslied* proves it existed by the early ninth century. Its Odoacer configuration is closer in names to Theoderic’s actual Italian wars but reverses agency. Whether the reversal arose through oral narrative mechanics, confusion with another Theoderic, political sympathy for displaced followers, or lost historical circumstances cannot be demonstrated. Attempts to reconstruct a complete sixth-century “original Dietrich lay” are speculative. **[MSR]**

## Oral tradition versus written composition

The early allusions presuppose circulating stories, and *Þiðreks saga* explicitly invokes German reports. Yet no continuous sixth- or seventh-century poem survives. Older scholarship often treated each thirteenth-century epic as debris from ancient lays. Current work gives more weight to literate composition, courtly romance, manuscript redaction, and intertextual borrowing while allowing oral transmission.

The saga’s “translation hypothesis” posits a large lost Low German source. The “compilation hypothesis” sees a Norwegian compiler combining shorter Low German oral and written materials. The lack of the supposed German master text favors caution; recurrent detailed correspondences nevertheless show real continental sources.

## “Historical” versus “fantastical” epics

This useful modern distinction separates exile-war poems from dwarf-and-giant adventures, but it can mislead. Monster captivity is already attested in *Waldere*, centuries before the surviving *Sigenot* or *Virginal*. Conversely, the supposedly historical poems are not reliable chronicles. Recent scholarship therefore treats both groups as evolving literary traditions.

## The Rök controversy

- **Traditional interpretation, from Bugge through Wessén:** the stone names Theodoric, ruler/rider of the Hreið-sea, now seated on his Gothic horse.
- **Ralph, 2007:** the supposed name results from questionable segmentation; the passage need not refer to Theodoric.
- **Holmberg, Gräslund, Sundqvist, and Williams, 2020:** the monument presents riddles about cosmic light, Ragnarök-like danger, and renewal, not a catalogue of Migration-Age heroes.
- **Myrvoll, 2026:** the traditional stanza is metrically and syntactically defensible; objections to the Theodoric reading are overstated.

Accordingly, claims such as “the Rök stone proves Theoderic was worshipped in Viking Sweden” compound two uncertainties: the name itself and the leap from mention to cult.

## The infernal ride

The hostile Christian tradition around historical Theoderic is early: Gregory’s volcanic damnation story dates to the late sixth century. The visual hunt at San Zeno is twelfth-century. Later heroic texts convert damnation into ambiguous survival and penance. Scholarship disputes whether Germanic Wild Hunt material lies behind the image or whether Christian moral narrative is sufficient. No pre-Christian source describes Dietrich’s black horse ride. **[C]**

## Fire-breath

The motif is genuinely medieval in the *Rosengarten* and saga tradition, but its precise mechanics vary: fiery breath, heat, smoke, or rage. Treating it as a stable physiological ability across all Dietrich texts is a modern simplification.

## Cult theory

Höfler and allied scholars inferred charismatic ruler cult and warrior-society traditions. The absence of ritual sites, sacrifices, invocations, or devotional objects specifically naming Dietrich makes “cult” much less secure than “heroic fame.” Modern histories of scholarship have also shown how twentieth-century national and racial frameworks affected readings of the Rök stone.

## Manuscripts and losses

The evidence is uneven:

- *Hildebrandslied*: one unique, damaged witness.
- *Waldere*: two fragments.
- *Dietrich und Wenezlan* and *Goldemar*: fragmentary.
- *Nibelungenlied*: many manuscripts, but significant recension variation.
- Individual Dietrich epics: often highly unstable, with short and long redactions.
- *Þiðreks saga*: one damaged medieval Norwegian parchment codex plus related Icelandic and Swedish witnesses.
- Lost Low German materials are inferred but not extant.

The tradition’s variability is therefore primary evidence, not textual noise to be harmonized away.

- Heldenbuch-Prosa and printed Heldenbücher.

### Major scholarly orientations

Joachim Heinzle’s work remains fundamental for distinguishing and interpreting the historical and fantastical Dietrich epics while respecting their manuscript variation. Elisabeth Lienert’s editions and studies provide the essential framework for the historical epics. Theodore M. Andersson’s work on the Brynhild tradition clarifies the relationship among the *Nibelungenlied*, *Þiðreks saga*, Eddic poetry, and *Völsunga saga*. Jan de Vries, Gabriel Turville-Petre, Rudolf Simek, and later Eddic scholarship supply the Scandinavian literary and religious contexts. The *Klaeber Beowulf* tradition, Andy Orchard, and Tolkien remain relevant for the wider Old English heroic world, but none makes *Beowulf* a Dietrich source. Recent manuscript catalogues and the *Handschriftencensus* are indispensable because the redactions often differ more substantially than narrative summaries admit.
