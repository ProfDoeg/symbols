# Egil Skallagrimsson: Research Dossier

**Traditional class:** mortal Icelandic poet-warrior, farmer and saga hero—not a demigod, culture hero, saint, or demonstrably heroized cult-dead. Modern descriptions such as “Viking poet” and “antihero” summarize his saga persona, not a medieval cultic category.

**Earliest secure attestation:** Egil is named in skaldic verses attributed to him and conventionally dated to the tenth century, but no surviving witness is contemporary: the poetry was transmitted in manuscripts written centuries later. The earliest physical witness to his narrative is the Old Norse fragment **AM 162 A fol. θ**, copied around the middle of the thirteenth century. *Landnámabók*, surviving in late-thirteenth- and fourteenth-century recensions, independently—or interdependently—names Egil among the leading men of western Iceland. Consequently, “tenth-century poet” is a defensible historical reconstruction, not a directly contemporary attestation.

## Evidentiary key

- **[EA] Secure early attestation:** physically early manuscript or substantially independent evidence.
- **[CP] Contemporary or plausibly early poetry:** verse attributed to Egil and possibly tenth-century, but preserved only much later.
- **[LC] Later canonical elaboration:** principally *Egils saga*, composed in the thirteenth century.
- **[RV] Regional or manuscript variant.**
- **[LS] Learned medieval systematization.**
- **[MA] Medieval or early-modern accretion.**
- **[F] Folklore or continuing local tradition.**
- **[MSR] Modern scholarly reconstruction or diagnosis.**
- **[MI] Modern invention or reception.**
- **[NEG] Negative finding:** the specified corpus or evidence class does not attest the claim.

## 1. Identification, names and epithets

**[LC]** Old Norse **Egill Skalla-Grímsson**, modern Icelandic **Egill Skallagrímsson**, means “Egill, son of Skalla-Grímr.” *Skalla-Grímsson* is a patronymic, not a hereditary surname. The father’s name is normally divided **Skalla-Grímr**, “Bald-Grímr”: *skalli/skalla-* refers to baldness, while *Grímr* is a common masculine personal name. The saga itself does not supply a learned etymology.

**[MSR]** The etymology of **Egill** is uncertain. It is sometimes connected with Old Norse *agi*, “awe, terror, discipline,” or treated as a diminutive involving Proto-Germanic *ag-*; confident internet translations such as “terror” are therefore too strong. It is also distinct from *egg*, “edge,” despite popular association with weapons.

**[LC]** The saga calls him a **skáld**, poet, and repeatedly presents him as a warrior, raider, shipmaster, landholder, litigant and chieftain. “Poet-Viking” is an accurate modern compound description, but not his fixed medieval epithet.

**[LC]** His immediate genealogy is:

- Kveld-Úlfr Bjálfason → Skalla-Grímr Kveldúlfsson + Bera Yngvarsdóttir → Þórólfr and Egill.
- Egill + Ásgerðr Bjarnardóttir → Þorgerðr, Bera, Böðvarr, Gunnarr and Þorsteinn.

The genealogy is supplied principally by *Egils saga* and partly by *Landnámabók*. It must not be converted into a verified modern pedigree merely because later Icelandic genealogies reproduce it.

**[NEG]** No medieval source equates Egil with an Eddic or Continental Germanic hero. He is not Egill/Agilaz the archer of *Þiðreks saga* and the Franks Casket, nor the legendary Yngling king Egill. Similarity of name is not identity.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### Poetry attributed to Egil

The [Skaldic Poetry of the Scandinavian Middle Ages database](https://skaldic.org/) and its associated textual database catalogue the following corpus:

- **Lausavísur**, forty-eight occasional stanzas or fragments attributed to Egil. **[CP/LC]** Individual stanzas may preserve tenth-century compositions, but their situations and speakers are fixed by the thirteenth-century saga.
- **Hǫfuðlausn**, “Head-Ransom,” conventionally twenty stanzas plus refrain, in end-rhymed *runhent*. **[CP disputed]** Its unusual rhyme and imperfect manuscript transmission have generated doubts about its tenth-century integrity. The poem and its performance story survive in the B/C textual tradition, not in the principal Möðruvallabók text.
- **Sonatorrek**, “Irreparable Loss of Sons,” twenty-five stanzas. **[CP disputed]** Often accepted as substantially old because of diction, metre and pagan theology, but its sole complete witness is late and difficult; several stanzas are corrupt.
- **Arinbjarnarkviða**, praise of Arinbjǫrn, twenty-five surviving stanzas. **[CP disputed]** It supports a tradition of Egil’s dependence on Arinbjǫrn but survives incompletely.
- Shorter or fragmentary attributions including **Berudrápa**, **Aðalsteinsdrápa** and **Skjaldardrápa**. **[CP/LC]** Attribution and extent vary.

No autograph, tenth-century inscription or contemporary foreign notice names Egil. The poetry is therefore “early material plausibly embedded in later books,” not unmediated documentation.

### *Egils saga Skalla-Grímssonar*

**[EA/LC]** The saga was composed in Old Norse-Icelandic, probably in western Iceland, most often dated approximately **1220–1240**. Its events are set from the later ninth to the late tenth century. The nearly three-century interval is fundamental.

Its transmission has three principal groups:

- **A/M redaction:** first represented by AM 162 A fol. θ, about 1250; chiefly by **Möðruvallabók, AM 132 fol.**, copied around 1330–1370. M has the fullest continuous prose but omits *Hǫfuðlausn* and *Sonatorrek*.
- **B/W redaction:** chiefly the fourteenth-century Wolfenbüttel manuscript, Herzog August Bibliothek **Cod. Guelf. 9.10 Aug. 4to**; it preserves *Hǫfuðlausn*.
- **C/K redaction:** known principally through seventeenth-century paper copies of a lost medieval exemplar; it alone preserves the complete *Sonatorrek*.  

The redactions differ in prose, ordering and distribution of verse. A modern composite *Egils saga* is consequently an editorial construction, not the text of any single medieval codex. The University of Copenhagen’s [A-redaction edition](https://nors.ku.dk/publikationer/trykte_serier/editiones/egils_saga-a/) and Bjarni Einarsson’s [Viking Society edition](https://vsnr.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/11/Egils_saga.pdf) document this problem.

### Other medieval evidence

- **[EA/LC] *Landnámabók***, compiled and repeatedly revised from the twelfth through fourteenth centuries, names Egil, his family and descendants. One recension calls him one of the greatest chieftains of western Iceland after sixty years of settlement. Its agreements with *Egils saga* are not wholly independent: scholars have demonstrated borrowing and cross-contamination between settlement-book and saga traditions.
- **[LC] *Heimskringla*** and other kings’ sagas overlap with Egil’s royal setting and occasionally preserve material related to his poetry, but do not give a second independent biography.
- **[LC] *Gunnlaugs saga ormstungu*** concerns Egil’s descendants, especially his granddaughter Helga the Fair; it extends the family’s literary memory rather than Egil’s deeds.
- **[LC] *Laxdæla saga*** shares descendants and western-Icelandic genealogies through Egil’s daughter Þorgerðr and grandson Kjartan.

### Corpora named in the brief that do **not** attest this Egil

**[NEG]** Egil Skallagrimsson does not appear in the *Poetic Edda’s* heroic lays, *Vǫlsunga saga*, *Hervarar saga*, *Ragnars saga loðbrókar*, *Gautreks saga*, Saxo Grammaticus’ *Gesta Danorum*, *Beowulf*, the *Nibelungenlied*, or *Þiðreks saga*. Nor is he a character in *Grettis saga*. These works are indispensable comparanda for Germanic heroic tradition, outlaw narrative and legendary historiography, but they are not primary witnesses to Egil. Klaeber, Tolkien, Orchard, Andersson, Rowe, Simek and Turville-Petre therefore supply comparative method and context, not attestations of him.

## 3. The story

### Ancestry, settlement and childhood

**[LC]** *Egils saga* begins before Egil, with Kveld-Úlfr and Skalla-Grímr’s conflict with Haraldr hárfagri, the killing of Egil’s uncle Þórólfr, vengeance against the king’s men, and the family’s migration to Iceland. Skalla-Grímr establishes Borg in Mýrar.

**[LC]** Egil is baptized with water in the pre-Christian naming rite, grows exceptionally fast, is dark-haired, ugly, verbally precocious and difficult. At three he travels uninvited to a feast and composes a verse. These are characterization motifs, not childhood records.

**[LC]** At six or seven, during a ball game, the older Grímr Heggsson humiliates him. Egil returns with an axe and kills Grímr. The saga’s chapter 40 makes this his first human killing. Modern retellings often say simply that he killed someone for cheating; the actual sequence includes physical defeat and public mockery. No earlier independent source confirms it.

**[LC]** Soon afterward Skalla-Grímr, in a berserk-like rage during another game, kills Egil’s companion Þórðr and threatens Egil. Egil retaliates by killing his father’s favored steward. Father and son cease speaking for the winter. The episode establishes both inherited violence and Egil’s resistance to paternal authority.

### Voyaging, raiding and feud

**[LC]** Egil sails with his brother Þórólfr, fights, trades and raids around Norway and the Baltic, and enters a feud with King Eiríkr blóðøx and Queen Gunnhildr. The saga attributes numerous killings to him, including killings in drunken disputes, combat and revenge. It does not present him as a morally purified national hero.

**[LC]** At Atley he is given poisoned ale. Egil cuts his palm, carves runes on the drinking horn and reddens them with blood; the horn bursts. He then kills Bárðr, the host responsible. This is the source of “runes against poison.” The narrative combines rune magic, poetic utterance and violence; there is no archaeological object or contemporary account.

**[LC]** During the inheritance conflict over Ásgerðr’s property, Egil kills Atli inn skammi in formal combat by biting through his throat after weapons prove ineffective. The grotesque deed is saga narrative, not securely early evidence.

**[LC]** After Eiríkr and Gunnhildr obstruct his legal claim, Egil raises a **níðstöng**—a hazel pole bearing a horse’s head—against them and against Norway’s *landvættir*, carving the curse in runes. This is the sole detailed source for the famous act. It does not demonstrate a standardized historical ritual.

### Brunanburh/Vínheiðr and Æthelstan

**[LC with historical frame]** The saga has Egil and Þórólfr serve King Aðalsteinn, Æthelstan of England, in a large battle at **Vínheiðr**, commonly associated with the historically attested Battle of Brunanburh in 937. Þórólfr dies; Egil avenges him and receives rings and two chests of silver.

**[MSR]** The identification with Brunanburh is plausible but not exact: the saga’s geography, armies and chronology do not map cleanly onto English sources. Neither the *Anglo-Saxon Chronicle* poem nor other contemporary English evidence names Egil or Þórólfr. His participation therefore remains a saga claim embedded in a real war.

### The head-ransom

**[LC/CP disputed]** Shipwreck brings Egil into Eiríkr’s power at York. Arinbjǫrn advises him to compose praise overnight. Gunnhildr allegedly sends a shape-shifting swallow to distract him; Arinbjǫrn guards the window. Egil performs *Hǫfuðlausn* before Eiríkr, who spares him because killing the author after receiving the praise would be dishonorable.

The story explicitly compares Egil with the earlier poet Bragi, who supposedly bought his life from King Bjǫrn with a poem. Thus the episode may be literary typology as well as remembered practice. Eiríkr’s rule at York is historical, but the saga’s chronology conflicts with its continued treatment of Æthelstan as alive. The praise poem may contain old material; the dramatic performance story is thirteenth-century narrative.

### Family, grief and *Sonatorrek*

**[LC]** Egil marries Ásgerðr, his brother’s widow, after a dispute about eligibility and inheritance. The saga gives them five children.

**[LC/CP]** His son Gunnarr dies of illness; Böðvarr drowns in Borgarfjörður. Egil carries Böðvarr’s body to Skalla-Grímr’s mound and resolves to starve himself. His daughter Þorgerðr deceives him by saying she will fast with him while chewing seaweed and then asks for water; she persuades him to compose. The result is *Sonatorrek*.

**[CP disputed]** The poem mourns the collapse of the family line, reproaches the sea powers for Böðvarr’s death, and addresses Óðinn as both betrayer and giver of poetry. Egil finally accepts that poetic power enables him to articulate grief. Its religious world is substantially pagan, but the complete text’s late manuscript preservation precludes certainty that every stanza is tenth-century.

### Old age, silver and death

**[LC]** In old age Egil becomes blind and dependent at Mosfell. He proposes taking Æthelstan’s two chests of English silver to the Alþingi and scattering the coins at the Law Rock so that the crowd will fight over them. His family prevents the journey.

**[LC/F]** He then rides out with two enslaved men belonging to Grímr, the silver and a horse. He returns without the men or treasure and says he killed the slaves and concealed the silver. The saga reports guesses—near hot springs or pits south of the river—and “mound-fire” seen there. It does not disclose a location. Treasure maps and claims of modern discovery are modern inventions unless supported by an authenticated find.

**[LC]** Egil dies after an illness and is buried with weapons and clothing in a mound at Tjaldanes. After conversion, Þórdís is said to have transferred him to a church at Hrísbrú. When that church is later removed, unusually large bones are found beneath the altar. Priest Skafti Þórarinsson strikes the enormous, ridged, heavy skull with an axe hammer; it whitens but neither dents nor cracks. The bones are reburied at the outer edge of Mosfell churchyard.

The saga does **not** say that someone tried to execute living Egil with an axe and failed. “A skull too hard for an axe” refers to a posthumously identified skull in chapter 86.

## 4. Relations with gods and other heroes

### Divine interaction

- **Óðinn — [CP]** In *Sonatorrek*, Egil says he had trusted Óðinn, but the god broke friendship; nevertheless Óðinn gave him poetic skill and the ability to expose deceivers. This is the only direct, named divine relationship in Egil’s attributed poetry.
- **Rán — [CP]** *Sonatorrek* represents the sea as taking Böðvarr and speaks within the mythic vocabulary of Rán/Ægir’s realm; depending on reconstruction of corrupt stanzas, the divine naming is less secure than the poem’s explicit address to Óðinn.
- **Landvættir of Norway — [LC]** Egil curses the unnamed land-spirits through the horse-headed *níðstöng*, ordering them to wander until Eiríkr and Gunnhildr leave Norway. They are a class, not individually named gods.
- **A supernatural swallow/hamhleypa — [LC]** The saga implies that Gunnhildr’s magic sends or assumes the bird-form that hinders composition. It does not identify a named deity.
- **[NEG]** No source records Egil meeting Thor, Freyr, Loki, a Valkyrie, elf, dwarf, or named giant. Modern illustrations that place him socially among the Norse gods are inventions.

### Named heroic and royal figures

- **Skalla-Grímr Kveldúlfsson — [LC]** Father, violent antagonist, landholding predecessor and eventual recipient of Egil’s burial care.
- **Kveld-Úlfr — [LC]** Grandfather; Egil never meets him in the narrative, which uses his feud and migration as dynastic prehistory.
- **Þórólfr Skalla-Grímsson — [LC]** Elder brother, companion in travel and war; killed at Vínheiðr, then avenged and lamented by Egil.
- **Haraldr hárfagri — [LC]** Adversary of Egil’s grandfather and father; no direct Egil–Haraldr encounter is securely narrated.
- **Eiríkr blóðøx — [LC/CP]** Royal enemy who condemns Egil but spares him after *Hǫfuðlausn*.
- **Gunnhildr konungamóðir — [LC]** Persistent enemy, alleged magic-worker and opponent of clemency at York.
- **Arinbjǫrn Þórisson — [LC/CP]** Protector and friend; shelters Egil, guards his composition and negotiates the head-ransom. Addressee of *Arinbjarnarkviða*.
- **Aðalsteinn/Æthelstan — [LC/CP disputed]** Employer and reward-giver; receives Egil after Vínheiðr and gives silver. No contemporary English source confirms the relationship.
- **Hákon Aðalsteinsfóstri — [LC]** King under whom Egil pursues property claims and political settlement.
- **Bragi inn gamli — [LC/LS]** Precedent invoked by Arinbjǫrn for saving one’s head through an overnight praise poem; no encounter with Egil.
- **Berserkers Ljót inn bleiki and Atli inn skammi — [LC]** Opponents defeated by Egil, the latter killed by throat-biting.
- **Ölvir hnúfa — [LC]** Older poet and family associate; part of the poetic-social inheritance surrounding Egil.
- **Böðvarr Egilsson — [LC/CP]** Son whose drowning precipitates *Sonatorrek*.
- **Þorgerðr Egilsdóttir — [LC]** Daughter who prevents Egil’s suicidal fast and elicits the elegy.
- **Þorsteinn Egilsson — [LC]** Son with whom Egil has a difficult relationship but whom he ultimately assists in legal conflict.
- **Kjartan Óláfsson — [LC genealogical]** Grandson named in the saga’s concluding family survey and a principal hero of *Laxdæla saga*; no substantial Egil–Kjartan adventure is recorded.
- **Gunnlaugr ormstunga and Hrafn Önundarson — [LC genealogical]** Later poets who contend over Egil’s granddaughter Helga; they do not interact with Egil.
- **Grettir Ásmundarson, Sigurðr Fáfnisbani, Brynhildr, Guðrún Gjúkadóttir, Beowulf, Þiðrekr and the Nibelung heroes — [NEG]** No documented interaction. Juxtaposition belongs to modern comparative study.

## 5. Cult, tomb, relics and ritual

**[NEG]** There is no evidence that Egil received pagan or Christian cult: no shrine, dedicatory inscription, votive deposit, annual sacrifice, feast, oath, oracle, sacred games, healing rite or medieval prayer is attached to him. Calling his grave a *heroon* would import a Greek category unsupported in Iceland.

**[LC]** The saga nevertheless constructs a potent mortuary history: mound burial, translation into a church, privileged burial under an altar, examination of the skull and reburial at the churchyard edge. This is remembrance of an exceptional ancestor, not proof of worship.

**[MSR]** Excavations by the Mosfell Archaeological Project identified a conversion-period church and cemetery at Hrísbrú consistent with the sort of landscape described by the saga. An empty grave in the small chancel is suggestive but cannot be identified as Egil’s. No excavated bones have been authenticated as his. A mound locally called “Egil’s mound” proved to be a natural moraine. Per Holck’s excavation report expressly states that Egil’s remains were not found.

**[F/MI]** Borg, Borgarnes, Mosfell and alleged treasure locations operate as commemorative landscapes and tourist sites. Modern statues and place-markers are memorial reception, not continuations of a documented medieval cult.

## 6. Historical evolution

### Possible tenth-century nucleus

**[CP/MSR]** A poet named Egil may have composed some surviving stanzas and may have belonged to the Borg lineage. The verse’s complex metre, personal voice and historical names are positive evidence; late transmission, corruption, conventional diction and possible saga-era imitation are the controls.

### Twelfth- and thirteenth-century formation

**[LC]** Genealogy, settlement tradition, royal history, local place-lore and attributed poetry were organized into *Egils saga*. Its author shaped an ancestral feud into a study of kingship, law, property, poetry, inherited temperament, paganism and conversion.

### Fourteenth- to seventeenth-century textual plurality

**[RV]** Scribes produced A, B and C forms with materially different verse corpora. The famous three long poems never coexisted completely in the principal medieval A manuscript. Seventeenth-century copies preserved material from now-lost medieval exemplars; a seventeenth-century *rímur* version further adapted the story.

### Antiquarian print culture

**[MA]** The saga was first printed at Hrappsey in **1782**, followed by a second edition in 1809. Printing stabilized a composite figure assembled from variant witnesses.

### Nineteenth- and twentieth-century canonization

**[MI]** National philology elevated Egil as an exemplary early Icelandic poet and forceful individual. The first complete English translation, W. C. Green’s *The Story of Egil Skallagrimsson*, appeared in **1893**; E. R. Eddison’s translation followed in 1930. Sigurður Nordal’s 1933 *Íslenzk fornrit* edition became especially influential.

### Twenty-first century

**[MSR/MI]** Digital manuscript access and the Skaldic Project have made textual variation more visible, while popular culture often collapses the historical hypothesis, saga character and modern Viking stereotype into one “real Viking.”

## 7. Modern reception and reinvention

- **1893 — [MI]** W. C. Green’s English translation presented the saga as an Icelandic family history, encouraging biographical reading.
- **1930 — [MI]** E. R. Eddison’s literary English translation further established Egil in Anglophone heroic reading.
- **1933 — [MSR]** Sigurður Nordal’s critical edition and introduction strongly influenced arguments for historicity, poetic authenticity and Snorri’s authorship.
- **1963 — [MI]** Anne Marie Carl-Nielsen’s sculpture *Egill Skallagrímsson reiðir lík Böðvars sonar síns til haugs Skallagríms*—Egil carrying Böðvarr’s body—was installed in Skallagrímsgarður, Borgarnes. It monumentalizes the grieving father rather than the killer.
- **1990 — [MI]** Poul Anderson’s novel *Mother of Kings* retold the feud from a historical-fantasy perspective centered on Gunnhild.
- **1995 — [MSR popularization]** Jesse Byock’s “Egil’s Bones” in *Scientific American* popularized the Paget’s-disease hypothesis.
- **Modern music and internet culture — [MI]** Translations of Egil’s childhood verse circulate as “My Mother Told Me.” Some performances detach it from the saga and present it as an anonymous Viking-age song; musical settings and English lyrics are modern even when based on an attributed stanza.
- **Viking branding — [MI]** Egil’s image and name appear in tourism, reenactment, beer and popular “warrior-poet” culture. These uses are commercial reception, not inherited cult.

**Nationalism and extremism.** Nineteenth- and twentieth-century Nordic and Germanic medievalism frequently recruited “Vikings” as racial or national ancestors. Egil participates in that broad symbolic economy, particularly as a hypermasculine, pagan, self-sufficient Northerner. Evidence for a distinctive organized political “cult of Egil,” however, is lacking. Claims that he himself was a proto-nationalist are anachronistic: the saga’s loyalties are household, kinship, friendship and negotiated allegiance, not the modern nation-state. Far-right uses of generalized Viking imagery are documented, but attribution of a particular extremist use to Egil requires a specific artifact or statement.

## 8. Historiography and contested material

### Did Egil exist?

**[MSR]** A restrained historical model accepts a tenth-century poet and landholder behind the saga because of the attributed verse, coherent western-Icelandic genealogies and *Landnámabók*. Critics emphasize that every full witness is medieval, genealogy can be literary and the saga can manufacture contexts around inherited or pseudonymous verse. The correct conclusion is neither “proved fictional” nor “biography confirmed”: a historical nucleus is probable to many scholars, while nearly every deed remains unverified.

### Did Snorri Sturluson write *Egils saga*?

**[MSR]** Snorri is a serious but unproved candidate. Supporting arguments include:

- his descent from Egil’s family;
- his residence and interests in western Iceland;
- knowledge of skaldic poetics and Norwegian royal history;
- stylistic and thematic comparisons with *Heimskringla* and the *Edda*;
- a plausible composition date during Snorri’s lifetime.

Counterarguments include:

- no medieval manuscript names an author;
- statistical style tests are inconclusive;
- *Egils saga* exists in substantially different redactions;
- “author” may conceal compilation, revision and scribal reshaping;
- knowledge of western traditions was not exclusive to Snorri.

Torfi Tulinius argues for Snorri or his close milieu while retaining caution; Guðrún Nordal stresses that the extant saga cannot simply be treated as one author’s stable work. “Probably by Snorri” should therefore be labeled a scholarly attribution, never a manuscript fact.

### Authenticity of the poems

**[MSR]** Linguistic and metrical archaism favors a tenth-century core in parts of *Sonatorrek* and the lausavísur. Against this stand late manuscripts, textual damage, editorial reconstruction, conventional skaldic diction and the capacity of thirteenth-century Icelanders to imitate older forms. *Hǫfuðlausn’s* end rhyme has been treated both as a remarkable early experiment influenced by English/Irish verse and as grounds for later dating. Authenticity must be assessed poem by poem and sometimes stanza by stanza.

### Paget’s disease

**[MSR]** Byock and others diagnosed polyostotic Paget’s disease from the saga’s large, heavy, corrugated, impact-resistant skull and Egil’s old-age blindness, deafness, headache or imbalance. Paget’s can thicken and deform cranial bone.

The objections are decisive for evidentiary classification:

- no authenticated skeleton exists;
- the description was written centuries after Egil’s presumed death;
- literary gigantism and ancestral resemblance may explain it;
- symptoms are nonspecific;
- one medical critique notes that Egil’s supposed travel history does not support the proposed fluoride-related model.

Thus Paget’s is an ingenious **palaeopathology-by-proxy hypothesis**, not a diagnosis. Claims of bipolar disorder, acromegaly or other syndromes are still less demonstrable.

### Tomb and treasure

**[LC/F/MSR]** Archaeology supports medieval settlement, church and burial activity at Hrísbrú, not the identification of an individual grave. The saga’s “Egil’s mound,” translated bones and hidden silver have generated local searching, but no authenticated remains or hoard has been found. “Egil’s silver discovered” remains modern invention unless provenance and archaeological publication emerge.

### Magic and belief

**[LC]** The poison-runes, healing-runes, curse-pole, land-spirits and Gunnhild’s sorcery document what a thirteenth-century saga writer could narrate about a pagan-age poet. They cannot without corroboration be treated as eyewitness ethnography of tenth-century ritual.

### Discredited or unsupported claims still circulating

- **[MI]** Egil was a berserker by formal status: the saga gives him violent rages and monstrous feats but does not consistently classify him as *berserkr*.
- **[MI]** His skull has been recovered and medically tested: false.
- **[MI]** His treasure has been found: false.
- **[MI]** He is an Eddic hero or appears in *Beowulf*: false.
- **[MI]** Snorri’s authorship is certain: false.
- **[MI]** Every poem in the saga is an authenticated tenth-century composition: false.
- **[MI]** “My Mother Told Me” in its familiar modern musical form is an ancient Viking song: the attributed stanza is medievally transmitted; the tune and most popular arrangements are modern.

## 10. Sources and assessment

### Primary texts and editions

- *Egils saga Skalla-Grímssonar*, ed. Bjarni Einarsson, Viking Society for Northern Research, 2003: the principal accessible critical Old Norse edition, with manuscript introduction.
- *Egils saga Skalla-Grímssonar*, Guðni Jónsson text: useful searchable Old Norse text; its composite presentation should not be mistaken for one manuscript.
- *Skaldic Poetry of the Scandinavian Middle Ages*, vol. V, ed. Margaret Clunies Ross et al.: current critical treatment of the poems and their manuscript witnesses.
- *Landnámabók*: important genealogical and settlement corroboration, qualified by demonstrable literary interdependence.
- Möðruvallabók, AM 132 fol.: chief A-redaction witness and material evidence for fourteenth-century saga reception.

### Scholarship

Sigurður Nordal remains foundational but reflects an earlier confidence in authorial unity and historical recovery. Bjarni Einarsson and the Copenhagen editorial project expose the redactional evidence. Torfi H. Tulinius offers the strongest recent Snorri-centered interpretation while stopping short of proof. Guðrún Nordal’s textual arguments caution against equating an archetype or one proposed author with the heterogeneous extant saga. Jesse Byock’s medical and archaeological work is productive when treated as hypothesis; later “palaeopathology by proxy” criticism correctly stresses the absence of a body.

Comparative works by R. I. Page, Rudolf Simek, E. O. G. Turville-Petre, Theodore M. Andersson, Carolyne Larrington, Andy Orchard, Christopher Tolkien and scholars of *fornaldarsögur* illuminate Germanic heroic conventions. They do not turn Egil into a participant in the Völsung, Nibelung, Eddic or *Beowulf* cycles.

The resulting documentary profile is unusually clear: Egil is possibly a tenth-century poet and landholder, securely a major thirteenth-century saga figure, never securely a cult hero, and thereafter a continually edited, diagnosed, monumentalized and commercialized image of the violent poet.
