# Gunnar (Gunther): Research Dossier

> **Traditional class:** mortal heroic king—specifically king of the Burgundians/Gjúkungar/Niflungar and a tragic epic hero. He is never securely classed as a god or demigod, and no cult of a heroized Gunnar is attested.
>
> **Earliest secure attestation:** the historical Burgundian ruler appears as Greek **Γυντιάριος** (*Gyntiarios*) in Olympiodorus of Thebes’ account of events in 411–413, composed in the early fifth century but surviving only through Photius’ ninth-century epitome. The earliest independently surviving near-contemporary statement is Prosper of Aquitaine’s mid-fifth-century *Chronicle*, which names **Gundicarius**, king of the Burgundians, and records his destruction with his people by Huns. The earliest secure *heroic-literary* witness is Old English **Gūðhere** in *Widsith*, preserved in the late-tenth-century Exeter Book but of disputed earlier composition. The earliest securely identifiable images of the legendary Gunnar are Scandinavian carvings with harp and serpents from the twelfth century; proposed ninth–eleventh-century identifications remain disputed.

## Evidence key

- **[SEA] Securely attested early material:** contemporary or near-contemporary historical evidence, early manuscript evidence, or an unambiguous early image.
- **[LCE] Later canonical elaboration:** a medieval work that became a principal literary version.
- **[RV] Regional variant:** a distinct Scandinavian, German, Anglo-Saxon, or other local version.
- **[LS] Learned systematization:** an antiquarian, genealogical, mythographic, or scholarly arrangement of inherited material.
- **[Syn] Syncretistic identification:** an explicit equation of figures or combination of traditions.
- **[MA] Medieval/early-modern accretion:** an episode or relationship first documented substantially later.
- **[F] Folklore:** vernacular or popular transmission not demonstrably ancient in its recorded form.
- **[MSR] Modern scholarly reconstruction:** an inference about lost traditions, history, or transmission.
- **[MI] Modern invention/reinvention:** a demonstrably modern narrative, religious, artistic, or commercial construction.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### Identification

**[SEA/MSR]** Gunnar/Gunther is the legendary reflex of the historically attested Burgundian king Gundaharius/Gundicarius, but the two records must not be collapsed. Roman evidence gives a ruler involved in imperial politics in 411–413 and killed with many Burgundians by Hunnic forces in approximately 436–437. Heroic traditions turn that political catastrophe into several incompatible stories: a Burgundian king in Old English praise poetry; an avaricious Rhineland king defeated by Walter; the oath-brother and brother-in-law who helps betray Sigurd/Siegfried; the doomed keeper of the Niflung treasure who dies in Atli’s snake-pit; or the German king who survives until Kriemhild’s revenge at Etzel’s court. The historical identification is compelling because of the name, ethnicity, royal status, and Hunnic destruction, but individual legendary episodes are not historical testimony.

### Name forms

| Language/tradition | Form | Attestation and status |
|---|---|---|
| Greek | **Γυντιάριος**, *Gyntiarios* | Olympiodorus, early fifth century, preserved in Photius: **[SEA]** historical ruler. |
| Latin | **Gundicarius**, **Gundaharius**, later **Guntharius** | Prosper’s *Chronicle*; *Lex Burgundionum*; medieval Latin epic: **[SEA/LCE]** according to source. |
| Proto-Germanic reconstruction | ***Gunþiharjaz** or ***Gunþaharjaz** | Linguistic reconstruction, not an attested spelling: **[MSR]**. |
| Old English | **Gūðhere/Guðhere** | *Widsith* and *Waldere*: **[SEA/RV]**. |
| Old Norse | **Gunnarr**, usually **Gunnarr Gjúkason** | Eddic poetry, Snorri, *Völsunga saga*, *Þiðreks saga*: **[LCE/RV]**. |
| Middle High German | **Gunther** | *Nibelungenlied*, *Rosengarten*, *Biterolf und Dietleib*: **[LCE/RV]**. |
| Modern German | **Gunther**, **Günther** | Literary and personal-name continuations. |
| Wagnerian | **Gunther**, ruler of the Gibichungs | *Götterdämmerung*, completed 1874: **[MI]** as a deliberately synthesized character. |

**[SEA/MSR] Etymology.** The first element belongs to Germanic *gunþ-* “battle, conflict,” reflected in Old Norse *gunnr/guðr* and Old English *gūð*. The second is normally connected with *harjaz* “army, host; warrior,” yielding approximately “battle-warrior” or “battle-army.” The familiar derivation of Old Norse *Gunnarr* directly as *gunnr* + *-arr* is broadly sound, but the historical shape of *-arr* is less transparent than popular name books imply: scholarship also discusses convergence with elements meaning “defender” or “spear.” “Bold warrior” is therefore an interpretive gloss, not a literal uniquely established translation. The uncertainty is documented in Scandinavian onomastic work on *-arr* compounds.

### Patronymics, dynastic names, and titles

- **Gunnarr Gjúkason**, “Gunnar, son of Gjúki”: Eddic and saga usage, **[LCE/RV]**.
- **Gjúkungar**, “descendants/sons of Gjúki”: Gunnar’s family in the Norse corpus, **[LCE]**.
- **Niflungar/Nibelungen:** Gunnar’s dynasty or treasure-bearing company in Norse and German reception. The referent shifts between lineage, people, and possessors of the hoard; it is not a stable ethnic label, **[LCE/RV]**.
- **King of the Burgundians:** Roman records, *Widsith*, and German tradition, **[SEA/LCE]**.
- **King of the Niflungs:** Scandinavian prose, **[LCE/RV]**.
- **Gibichung:** Wagner’s dynastic label for Gunther’s household, taken from the Gibica/Gjúki name complex but reorganized, **[MI]**.

No stable ancient epithet comparable to “dragon-slayer” belongs to Gunnar. “Gunnar in the snake-pit” is a modern descriptive label grounded in medieval narrative and image.

### Identifications and equations

- **Gundaharius = Gūðhere = Gunnarr = Gunther:** **[MSR]** the standard historical-philological identification, based on regular name correspondence, royal Burgundian status, and Hunnic destruction. It does not prove narrative identity in every episode.
- **Gibica = Gifica = Gjúki/Gibeche:** **[MSR]** a parallel dynastic equation encouraged by the names and traditions. The *Lex Burgundionum* lists Gibica, Gundomar, Gislaharius, and Gundahar as royal predecessors but does not call them father and sons or brothers.
- **Gundomar/Godomar = Gernot or Gothorm; Gislaharius = Giselher:** **[MSR]** plausible name-driven legendary reorganization, not a relationship supplied by the law code.
- **Atli/Etzel = Attila:** **[Syn/medieval tradition]** an old heroic identification. Historically, Attila was not necessarily the commander present at Gundaharius’ destruction; Prosper says “the Huns,” not Attila.
- **Hagen = Högni:** **[Syn/MSR]** functional and phonological equation across German and Norse systems, but the kinship changes radically.
- **Brynhild = Brunhild:** **[Syn]** traditional equation, with major narrative divergence.
- **Gunnar = Ragnar Loðbrók because both die in snake-pits:** not attested as an identification. At most Ragnar’s death may imitate or reuse the Gunnar motif, **[MSR]**; treating them as one hero is **[MI]**.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### Historical record

1. **Olympiodorus of Thebes, *History*, early fifth century; surviving in Photius, ninth century.**  
   **[SEA]** Γυντιάριος, described as Burgundian *phylarch*, and Goar the Alan support the usurper Jovinus in 411. This establishes a named Burgundian ruler and political actor. It says nothing about Sigurd, Brynhild, treasure, snakes, Worms, or kinship with Gibica.

2. **Prosper of Aquitaine, *Chronicle*, final redactions in the 440s–450s.**  
   **[SEA]** Prosper reports that Aetius first crushed *Gundicarius*, king of the Burgundians, granted peace when he begged for it, and that shortly afterward Huns destroyed him “with his people and stock.” The annal’s transmitted placement varies because of Prosper’s chronological systems, producing modern dates of 436 or 437. It supplies the catastrophe behind the legend, not the later banquet or snake-pit.

3. **Hydatius, *Chronicle*, mid-fifth century.**  
   **[SEA]** Hydatius says rebellious Burgundians were defeated by Romans under Aetius. He does not name Gundaharius in the relevant notice. This corroborates the conflict but not the developed legend.

4. **Gallic chronicles of 452 and 511.**  
   **[SEA]** These continue the record of the Burgundian defeat. Their brevity does not validate later narrative details.

5. ** *Lex Burgundionum*, principally under Gundobad, late fifth/early sixth century.**  
   **[SEA/LS]** The royal prologue names Gibica, Gundomar, Gislaharius, and Gundahar among ancestral royal lawgivers/predecessors. It neither locates them at Worms nor says that Gibica fathered the other three. The later heroic family is a reorganization of this remembered royal nomenclature, not a genealogy quoted from the law.

### Earliest heroic and literary witnesses

6. **Old English *Widsith*, Exeter Book, copied c. 960–990; composition disputed.**  
   **[SEA/RV]** The fictive travelling poet says that among the Burgundians “Gūðhere gave me a splendid treasure … no sluggish king.” The poem also knows Gifica and Gislhere, but does not explicitly construct the later family. It presents Guðhere as a paradigmatic generous ring-giver. Claims that this passage was composed in the sixth or seventh century remain **[MSR]**; the manuscript is the secure terminus.

7. **Latin *Waltharius*, probably ninth or tenth century; manuscripts from the tenth century onward.**  
   **[RV/MA]** Guntharius is a Frankish king at Worms, not explicitly Burgundian. Greedy for Walter’s Hunnic treasure, he pursues him with twelve warriors despite Hagen’s warnings. Walter kills the company; in the final grotesque combat Walter loses a hand, Hagen an eye and teeth, and Gunther a leg. The poem preserves a different heroic complex, not the Hunnic destruction.

8. **Old English *Waldere*, fragments copied c. 1000.**  
   **[SEA/RV]** Guðhere is again Walter’s aggressor; Hildegyth says he wrongly began the feud and refused offered treasure. Waldere challenges him and refers to his attempt to make Hagena attack first. Because the poem is fragmentary, its ending cannot be reconstructed with certainty.

9. **Eddic heroic poetry, chiefly preserved in Codex Regius GKS 2365 4to, c. 1270, with poems of varying earlier dates.**  
   The relevant corpus includes:

   - *Grípisspá*: Gunnar within the prophecy of Sigurd’s future, **[LCE]**.
   - *Brot af Sigurðarkviðu*: fragment of the betrayal and aftermath, **[LCE]**.
   - *Sigurðarkviða in skamma*: Brynhild drives Gunnar toward Sigurd’s death and prophesies Gunnar’s snake-pit, **[LCE]**.
   - *Helreið Brynhildar*: Brynhild recounts the deceptive wooing, **[LCE]**.
   - *Guðrúnarkviða I–III*: Gunnar in Gudrun’s family and the consequences of Sigurd’s death, **[LCE]**.
   - *Oddrúnargrátr*: Gunnar’s clandestine love with Oddrún, harp summons, snake-pit, and the deadly snake as Atli’s mother, **[MA/RV]**, usually dated around the eleventh century.
   - *Atlakviða*: probably among the older Eddic heroic lays—often assigned to the ninth or tenth century, though exact dating and place are disputed. Gunnar refuses to reveal the gold and strikes the harp by hand in the serpent enclosure, **[SEA within the Norse literary tradition]**.
   - *Atlamál in grœnlenzku*: a longer domesticized version, usually regarded as later; Gunnar plays with his toes because his hands are bound, **[LCE/RV]**.
   - *Guðrúnarhvöt* and *Hamðismál*: retrospective references to the deaths of Gunnar and Högni, **[LCE]**.
   - The prose *Dráp Niflunga*, placed between poems by the Codex Regius compiler, systematizes the cycle, **[LS]**.

10. **Skaldic evidence.**  
    **[SEA but fragmentary]** Verses conventionally assigned to Bragi Boddason’s ninth-century *Ragnarsdrápa* appear to allude to the Niflung catastrophe, Gunnar guarding the gold, Högni’s heart, and the serpent enclosure. Their survival is through Snorri’s thirteenth-century quotation, so ninth-century authorship and exact reconstruction remain debated. They are important possible evidence that the death-cycle circulated before the Eddic manuscripts.

11. **Snorri Sturluson, *Prose Edda*, c. 1220.**  
    **[LS/LCE]** *Skáldskaparmál* retells the Sigurd–Gjúkung cycle to explain poetic diction: Gjúki and Grímhild are parents of Gunnar, Högni, and Guðrún; Sigurd helps Gunnar win Brynhild; the family kills Sigurd; Atli kills the brothers for the gold; Gunnar dies among snakes. Snorri is a systematizer and witness to medieval tradition, not a neutral preservation of a single ancient original.

12. ***Völsunga saga*, composed in Iceland or a Norse-Icelandic environment c. 1250–1270; sole complete medieval manuscript NKS 1824 b 4to, c. 1400.**  
    **[LCE]** This is the fullest Norse prose synthesis. It drew on surviving and now-lost Eddic poetry; its account of the great lacuna in Codex Regius is therefore invaluable but cannot simply be backdated to the Viking Age.

13. ***Þiðreks saga af Bern*, Norway, c. 1250, with divergent manuscripts/redactions.**  
    **[RV/Syn]** A Norse compilation claiming German oral sources. It combines continental relationships and names with Scandinavian motifs. Gunnar and his brothers participate in Sigurd’s betrayal and the Niflung expedition; Gunnar is captured by Osid and placed in a tower—in one redaction explicitly a snake-tower—where he dies. It is evidence for thirteenth-century synthesis, not a transparent transcript of lost Low German songs.

14. ***Nibelungenlied*, composed c. 1200, probably in the Passau/Austrian-Bavarian region; principal manuscripts A, B, and C are thirteenth century.**  
    **[LCE/RV]** Gunther rules at Worms with Gernot and Giselher. Siegfried helps him defeat and sexually subdue Brunhild; Gunther assents to Siegfried’s murder and Hagen’s appropriation of the treasure. At Etzel’s court he fights loyally with his men, is captured by Dietrich, and is beheaded by Kriemhild. This is not the snake-pit tradition.

15. ***Nibelungenklage*, early thirteenth century.**  
    **[MA/LS]** A sequel lamenting and interpreting the catastrophe, transmitted with the *Nibelungenlied* in several manuscripts.

16. ***Rosengarten zu Worms*, thirteenth century, securely attested by c. 1300.**  
    **[RV/MA]** Gunther belongs to the twelve Worms champions who defend Kriemhild’s rose garden against Dietrich’s company. This adventurous Dietrich epic rearranges the cast and is not part of Gunnar’s early biography.

17. ***Biterolf und Dietleib*, second half of the thirteenth century.**  
    **[RV/MA]** Gunther, Gernot, and Hagen attack Dietleib; a subsequent campaign against Worms ends in reconciliation. Again the figure is absorbed into Dietrich epic.

18. **Later *Heldenbuch* traditions, fifteenth–sixteenth centuries.**  
    **[MA/LS]** These organize disparate heroic figures into genealogical and chronological systems, often smoothing contradictions that the earlier texts preserve.

### Requested corpora in which the dossier’s Gunnar is absent or only indirect

- ***Beowulf***, manuscript c. 1000, composition disputed: **[SEA—negative finding]** neither Gunnar nor Gunther appears. The poem’s Sigemund episode and heroic-poetic setting show cognate legendary culture, but they do not document an interaction with Gunnar. Klaeber’s edition and Tolkien’s “Beowulf: The Monsters and the Critics” illuminate heroic tradition generally, not Gunnar specifically.
- ***Hervarar saga ok Heiðreks***: **[LCE—negative finding]** no securely identifiable Gunnar Gjúkason episode. Its Migration-Age figures and Hunnic/Gothic conflict are comparative material, not Gunnar evidence.
- ***Gautreks saga***: **[LCE—negative finding]** no securely identifiable Gunnar/Gunther of the Burgundians.
- **Saxo Grammaticus, *Gesta Danorum*, c. 1200:** **[LCE—negative finding]** no unambiguous account of Gunnar Gjúkason. Names resembling Gunnar are insufficient identification.
- ***Ragnars saga loðbrókar*, late thirteenth century:** **[MA/related tradition]** Gunnar does not act, but the saga follows *Völsunga saga* in NKS 1824 b 4to through Aslaug, daughter of Sigurd and Brynhild. Ragnar’s death in a snake-pit recalls Gunnar and may be literary reuse.
- ***Grettis saga*, *Egils saga*, and other Icelandic family sagas:** **[negative finding]** their numerous historical or saga-age men named Gunnarr are not Gunnar Gjúkason. Treating Gunnar of Hlíðarendi in *Njáls saga*, Gunnar Þiðrandabani, or other Icelandic Gunnars as this hero is false conflation.
- **“Gunnar’s tune” (*Gunnars slagur*):** preserved as a later Icelandic musical/antiquarian tradition associated with the harp scene, **[F/MA]**; it is not a surviving fifth-century or Viking-Age melody.

## 3. The story, cycle by cycle

### A. Historical catastrophe

**[SEA]** Gundaharius is a Burgundian ruler by 411, when he helps elevate Jovinus. After Jovinus’ defeat, Burgundians occupied territory west of the Rhine under Roman arrangements. In 435/436 they came into conflict with Aetius. Prosper says Aetius defeated Gundicarius, granted peace, and that Huns soon destroyed the king, his people, and his lineage.

**[MSR]** The customary date “437” reflects one chronological reading; 436 is also used. “Battle of Worms” is not the language of the contemporary sources. The precise location of Gundaharius’ kingdom is debated, and Worms is inferred largely from later literary geography. The Roman evidence does not establish that Attila personally commanded the Huns.

### B. The generous Burgundian king

**[SEA/RV]** *Widsith* remembers Gūðhere not as villain or doomed victim but as a generous ring-giver. Nothing in the surviving passage joins him to Sigurd, Brynhild, Atli, or a treasure curse.

### C. The Walter cycle

**[RV]** In *Waltharius*, Gunther hears that Walter and Hildegund have escaped Attila carrying treasure. Despite Hagen’s counsel and Walter’s offer of compensation, Gunther pursues with twelve men. Walter kills the others in successive combats. Gunther and Hagen then attack together: Walter cuts off Gunther’s leg, Hagen cuts off Walter’s hand, and Walter disfigures Hagen. The three survivors exchange grim jokes.

**[RV]** *Waldere* presents Guðhere as the unjust aggressor who refused treasure, but its fragmentary state prevents certainty about the outcome.

This episode is independent of the Brynhild wooing and the fall at Etzel’s court. Later heroic compilations put Walter, Hagen, Gunther, Dietrich, and Siegfried in a common world, but that is medieval cross-cycling, not an original unified biography.

### D. Gunnar, Brynhild, and Sigurd: the Norse cycle

**[LCE] Parentage and household.** In the Eddic–*Völsunga saga* system Gunnar is son of Gjúki and Grímhild, brother of Guðrún and Högni; Gothorm is a younger brother or half-brother in prose systematizations. The historical *Lex Burgundionum* does not provide these relationships.

**[LCE] Oath-brotherhood.** *Völsunga saga* says Sigurd comes to Gjúki’s court, drinks Grímhild’s potion, forgets Brynhild, marries Guðrún, and swears brotherhood with Gunnar and Högni. This oath makes the later killing both kin-murder and oath-breaking.

**[LCE/RV] Winning Brynhild.** Gunnar seeks Brynhild, who will marry only the man able to cross her encircling fire. Gunnar tries Sigurd’s horse Grani, but it refuses him. Gunnar and Sigurd exchange shapes; Sigurd, appearing as Gunnar, rides through the flames, spends three nights beside Brynhild with the sword Gram between them, and obtains her promise. He takes back the ring Andvaranaut and later resumes his own form. Gunnar consequently marries Brynhild through deception.

This precise shape-changing and fire-crossing belongs to Norse prose synthesis and related poetic allusions. It is not the *Nibelungenlied*’s athletic contest.

**[LCE] Disclosure.** During a quarrel over precedence while bathing, Guðrún tells Brynhild that Sigurd, not Gunnar, crossed the fire and shows the ring as proof. Brynhild experiences the marriage as fraud and sexual dishonor.

**[LCE] Demand for Sigurd’s death.** Brynhild presses Gunnar to kill Sigurd, sometimes alleging that Sigurd violated the sword-separated bed or presenting his continued existence as intolerable. Gunnar cannot directly break the sworn brotherhood. In *Völsunga saga* he and Högni induce Gothorm—who had not sworn the oath—to kill Sigurd, fortifying or maddening him with wolf-and-serpent flesh. Sigurd kills Gothorm as he dies.

**[LCE] Responsibility.** Gunnar is therefore not necessarily the hand that kills Sigurd, but he plans or authorizes the killing. Calling it merely “Hagen’s murder” imports the German version; calling Gunnar innocent sanitizes the Norse one.

**[LCE] Brynhild’s death.** Brynhild kills herself and orders a funeral pyre beside Sigurd, while prophesying Gunnar’s eventual death at Atli’s hands. Gunnar survives her.

### E. Gunther, Brunhild, and Siegfried: the German cycle

**[LCE/RV] Courtship bargain.** In the *Nibelungenlied*, Siegfried seeks Kriemhild. Gunther agrees to the marriage if Siegfried helps him win Brunhild, queen of Iceland/Isenstein.

**[LCE/RV] Athletic deception.** Brunhild requires a suitor to defeat her in spear-casting, stone-throwing, and leaping, on pain of death. Wearing the invisibility cloak *Tarnkappe*, Siegfried secretly supplies the strength while Gunther appears to compete.

**[LCE/RV] Wedding-night coercion and sexual violence.** Brunhild resists consummation, overpowers Gunther, binds him, and hangs him from a wall peg. On the next night Siegfried, again invisible and acting for Gunther, wrestles her into submission and takes her girdle and ring; Gunther then consummates the marriage. The poem presents this within courtly-marital logic, but the episode is plainly coercive and culminates in sex obtained through deception and force.

**[LCE/RV] Queens’ quarrel and murder.** Kriemhild exposes Siegfried’s role and displays Brunhild’s ring and girdle. Hagen proposes murder to restore Brunhild’s and Gunther’s honor. Gunther assents, participates in the false hunting expedition, and helps conceal responsibility. Hagen spears Siegfried at a spring.

**[LCE/RV] Treasure.** Hagen later sinks the Nibelung hoard in the Rhine. Gunther knows the hiding place and shares responsibility for denying Kriemhild her property. Unlike the Norse Gunnar, he is not primarily its lone heroic guardian until the final scene.

### F. The fall before Atli/Etzel

#### *Atlakviða*

**[SEA within the Norse poetic corpus]** Atli invites Gunnar and Högni to his court with deceitful promises. Their sister Guðrún warns them, but they ride through Myrkviðr into a mythicized landscape. Atli demands Sigurd’s/Niflung gold.

Gunnar refuses to reveal the treasure until Högni’s heart is shown to him. The Huns first cut out the coward Hjalli’s heart; Gunnar detects the trembling organ. They then cut out Högni’s living heart. Seeing it steady, Gunnar declares that he alone now knows where the Rhine’s metal lies and that Atli will never obtain it.

Atli has Gunnar placed among serpents. The poem’s transmitted text says Gunnar struck the harp **with his hand**. It does not say “with his toes.” He dies keeping the secret.

#### *Atlamál*

**[LCE/RV]** This longer version gives Gunnar a wife, Glaumvör, whose ominous dreams warn him. Gunnar and Högni go despite warnings. The killing becomes a more domestic feud. Gunnar’s hands are bound, so he plays the harp **with his toes**. This is the secure literary source for the famous detail.

#### *Oddrúnargrátr*

**[MA/RV]** Gunnar and Oddrún, Atli’s sister, are clandestine lovers because Atli refuses their marriage. Atli discovers the affair and destroys Gunnar and Högni. Oddrún is away preparing a feast for Geirmund when Gunnar plays the harp to summon her; she hears it from afar but arrives too late. Most snakes are lulled, but Atli’s mother, transformed into a serpent, bites Gunnar through the heart. This shape-shifting mother-serpent is a distinctive late variant, not stated in *Atlakviða*.

#### *Völsunga saga*

**[LCE]** The saga combines the treasure motive, Högni’s unshaken heart, Gunnar’s toe-playing, and the fatal adder. Guðrún attempts to aid or avenge her brothers, then kills her sons by Atli, serves their flesh and blood to him, kills him with Högni’s son Niflung, and burns the hall. These acts belong to Guðrún’s revenge cycle, not Gunnar’s deeds.

#### *Nibelungenlied*

**[LCE/RV]** Kriemhild marries Etzel specifically to acquire power for revenge. Gunther and the Burgundians accept Etzel’s invitation despite warnings. At the Hunnish court, violence escalates into mass slaughter. Gunther fights with his followers; Dietrich finally captures Gunther and Hagen and hands them to Kriemhild. She demands the hoard from Hagen. Hagen refuses while any of his lords lives. Kriemhild has Gunther beheaded and shows Hagen the head; Hagen exults that now only he and God know the treasure’s location. Kriemhild then kills Hagen and is herself cut down by Hildebrand.

Thus German Gunther dies by his sister’s order, not by Etzel’s snake-pit; Hagen, not Gunther, becomes the last keeper of the hoard’s secret.

#### *Þiðreks saga*

**[RV/Syn]** Gunnar is captured during the Niflung battle by Osid, Atli’s nephew. Grímhild urges his disposal; he is thrown into a tower, explicitly a snake-tower in one redaction, and dies. This hybridizes continental revenge relationships with the Scandinavian death motif.

### G. The body

- **Historical Gundaharius:** no identified body, grave, relic, or securely excavated tomb, **[SEA—absence]**.
- ***Atlakviða*, *Atlamál*, *Oddrúnargrátr*:** death in the serpent enclosure; no developed burial cult, **[LCE]**.
- ***Nibelungenlied*:** decapitation in Etzel’s hall; subsequent lament and commemoration are literary, **[LCE]**.
- Claims to possess Gunnar/Gunther’s bones, grave, or royal tomb are unsupported, **[MI unless new archaeological evidence is supplied]**.

## 4. Relations with gods and other heroes

### Competing genealogies

| Tradition | Parents | Siblings | Spouse/lover | Children |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Roman/legal record | Not stated | Not stated | Not stated | Not stated |
| Eddic poetry | Usually Gjúki’s son; mother often unstated in verse | Högni, Guðrún; other siblings vary | Brynhild; Oddrún as forbidden lover in *Oddrúnargrátr* | None securely central |
| Snorri/*Völsunga saga* | Gjúki + Grímhild | Högni, Guðrún; Gothorm variably half-brother; Gullrönd/Guðný in some systematizations | Brynhild; later Glaumvör before Atli expedition in the saga’s narrative system | A son may appear in some later genealogical traditions; no consistent canonical line |
| *Nibelungenlied* | Dancrat + Ute | Gernot, Giselher, Kriemhild | Brunhild | A son named Siegfried is mentioned after the marriages, but never becomes a major actor |
| *Rosengarten* A | Gibeche’s son | Gernot, unnamed third brother, Kriemhild | Not central | Not central |
| Wagner | Gibich + Grimhilde | Gutrune; Hagen is maternal half-brother, son of Alberich | Intended husband of Brünnhilde through deception | None |

The law code’s four kings are not a securely attested family tree. The heroic trees are later arrangements.

## 5. Cult, tomb, and ritual

### Cult

**[SEA—negative finding]** No inscription, sanctuary, altar, sacrificial calendar, temple, sacred grove, oracle, priesthood, votive deposit, or named festival securely documents worship of Gunnar/Gunther. No medieval text calls him a god or records sacrifice to him.

**[MSR]** His inclusion on church portals and baptismal fonts demonstrates cultural and possibly moral or typological usefulness, not cult. The story could be read alongside Daniel in the lions’ den or as an image of steadfastness and salvation, but interpretation varies.

### Tomb and relics

**[SEA—negative finding]** No grave of Gundaharius has been identified. Worms possesses extensive medieval and modern Nibelung commemoration but no authenticated Gunther tomb. Claims that a local mound, church feature, or excavation revealed a Nibelung hero require individual provenance; none currently establishes Gunnar’s burial.

**[MSR]** Worms may have lain within or near the historical Burgundian sphere, but the earliest text explicitly placing Gunther at Worms is the much later *Waltharius*, where he is called a Frank. Later localization is not archaeological proof of a royal capital.

### Images

1. **Oseberg cart carving, ninth century:** a man among serpentine forms has been proposed as Gunnar. The identification is highly contested because there is no harp and serpents occur in many iconographic contexts, **[contested MSR]**.
2. **Klinte Hunninge I, Stenkyrka Smiss I, Ardre VIII, and other Gotland stones, ninth–eleventh centuries:** bound or snake-surrounded figures have been connected with Gunnar. Aðalheiður Guðmundsdóttir argues that a harp was not essential to every variant; critics stress ambiguity, **[contested MSR]**.
3. **Kirk Andreas cross, Isle of Man, c. 1000:** proposed as Gunnar, but Loki is another identification, **[contested MSR]**.
4. **Södermanland 40, Västerljung, eleventh century:** a bound figure amid serpents is relatively plausibly Gunnar because of its iconographic environment and neighboring Sigurd material, but the absence of an identifying inscription leaves caution necessary, **[probable MSR]**.
5. **Hylestad stave-church portal, late twelfth or c. 1200:** Gunnar plays the harp with his feet in the snake-pit; context within a Sigurd cycle makes identification secure, **[SEA image]**.
6. **Austad portal; Heddal pew; Uvdal and Kravik portals; Näs/Lockne or Norum-area baptismal fonts, twelfth–fourteenth centuries:** harp-playing serpent scenes securely continue the motif, **[LCE material evidence]**.
7. **Mo drinking horn, fourteenth/fifteenth century:** late material continuation, **[MA]**.

The ecclesiastical settings show Christian accommodation of heroic imagery. They do not prove survival of pagan ritual.

### Commemorative naming and civic use

**[LCE/F]** Gunnar became a common Scandinavian personal name; individual bearers cannot be presumed named specifically for the hero. Onomastic scholarship has suggested that the hero’s fame may have reinforced the name’s popularity, but this is not directly demonstrable for each inscription.

**[MI]** Worms’ museums, festivals, statues, tourist routes, and *Nibelungen-Festspiele* are modern civic commemoration. They are reception, not continuous cult.

## 6. Historical evolution

### 411–sixth century: political ruler and remembered catastrophe

**[SEA]** Gundaharius enters Roman history in the crisis of Jovinus and dies in the Burgundian disaster of 436/437. The *Lex Burgundionum* preserves his name in an ancestral royal list. No narrative cycle is yet documented.

### Sixth–ninth century: oral heroic formation

**[MSR]** During this undocumented interval, the annihilation was converted into heroic memory. The historical king’s name, Burgundian identity, and destruction by Huns survived, while chronology and agency changed. Attila’s fame absorbed an event perhaps carried out by Hunnic auxiliaries before or early in his sole rule. Kinship and treasure themes attached the catastrophe to other Migration-Age traditions.

### Ninth–eleventh century: divergent poetic worlds

**[SEA/RV]** *Widsith* knows the generous Burgundian Guðhere; *Waltharius* knows a greedy Frankish Gunther at Worms; Eddic and perhaps skaldic traditions know the doomed Niflung ruler. These are already divergent, not stages of a recoverable single “original saga.”

The snake-pit, heart-test, and guarded treasure become central in Scandinavia. The Brynhild–Sigurd marriage catastrophe joins Gunnar’s fall, although the exact order and mechanism vary.

### c. 1200–1300: canonical medieval syntheses

**[LCE]** The *Nibelungenlied* recasts the material in courtly Christian terms: Gunther is a compromised feudal king, Hagen dominates the murder and treasure plot, and Kriemhild becomes the agent of the final destruction.

**[LS/LCE]** Snorri, *Völsunga saga*, and *Þiðreks saga* systematize older poems and oral materials differently. The *Rosengarten* and *Biterolf* insert Gunther into the expanding Dietrich universe.

### Fourteenth–sixteenth century: manuscript expansion and decline

**[MA/LS]** Late manuscripts, *Heldenbücher*, prose summaries, images, and popular plays continue to reorganize the cast. The *Nibelungenlied* then largely disappears from active literary circulation by the sixteenth century.

### 1755–nineteenth century: rediscovery and national epic

**[MI/MSR]** The recovery of a *Nibelungenlied* manuscript at Hohenems in 1755 begins modern reception. Editors and Romantic philologists construct the poem as a German national epic. The historical Gundaharius becomes evidence in arguments that the epic embodies authentic national memory. This stimulated serious textual study but also encouraged uncritical euhemerism and nationalist ownership.

### 1848–1874: Wagnerian synthesis

**[MI]** Wagner’s *Ring des Nibelungen* combines Eddic poetry, *Völsunga saga*, German heroic material, mythography, and his own political-philosophical design. Gunther is neither the Eddic Gunnar nor simply the *Nibelungenlied* king: he is the weak Gibichung ruler manipulated by Hagen, seeking marriage with Brünnhilde for prestige. Hagen kills him during the struggle for the ring.

### Twentieth century to present

**[MI]** Stage, cinema, novels, comics, fantasy games, tourism, and neopagan or medievalist culture reproduce selective Gunthers: weak king, betrayed oath-brother, tragic Burgundian, or harp-playing martyr. Modern versions frequently merge mutually exclusive German and Norse plots.

## 7. Modern reception and reinvention

### Literature and scholarship

- **Friedrich de la Motte Fouqué, *Der Held des Nordens*, 1808–1810:** Romantic dramatic adaptation, **[MI]**.
- **Friedrich Hebbel, *Die Nibelungen*, 1861:** dramatic trilogy emphasizing political and psychological conflict, **[MI]**.
- **William Morris and Eiríkur Magnússon, *Völsunga Saga* translation, 1870; Morris, *The Story of Sigurd the Volsung*, 1876:** major Anglophone mediation of Gunnar’s Norse form, **[MI reception]**.
- **J. R. R. Tolkien, *The Legend of Sigurd and Gudrún*, composed principally in the 1920s–1930s, published 2009:** modern alliterative reconstruction, explicitly not a newly discovered ancient poem, **[MI]**.
- **Theodore M. Andersson, *The Legend of Brynhild* (1980):** influential comparative reconstruction of the legend’s development, **[MSR]**, not primary evidence.

### Wagner

**[MI]** In *Götterdämmerung*, Gunther lives with Gutrune and half-brother Hagen. Hagen’s potion makes Siegfried forget Brünnhilde and woo her in Gunther’s form. Gunther participates in the deception and murder plot, then quarrels with Hagen over the ring. Hagen kills him. Wagner removes the snake-pit and substantially changes kinship, motive, metaphysics, and culpability.

### Visual art, theatre, and film

- **Henry Fuseli, *Brunhild Watching Gunther Suspended from the Ceiling*, 1807:** visualizes the *Nibelungenlied* wedding-night humiliation, **[MI reception]**.
- **Fritz Lang and Thea von Harbou, *Die Nibelungen*, 1924:** Theodor Loos plays a wavering, dependent Gunther; the film monumentalizes the national epic while giving it Weimar-era visual form, **[MI]**.
- **Harald Reinl, *Die Nibelungen*, 1966–1967:** postwar color-film retelling, **[MI]**.
- **Television and film versions including *Dark Kingdom/Ring of the Nibelungs* (2004):** freely hybridize Wagner, Norse saga, and German epic, **[MI]**.
- **Worms *Nibelungen-Festspiele*, founded 2002:** annually rework the epic in civic theatre, **[MI]**.

### Nationalist and extremist appropriation

**[MI/political reception]** From the late eighteenth century, the *Nibelungenlied* was elevated into a German national epic. Nineteenth-century “bardic nationalism” treated anonymous oral growth as the collective voice of a primordial German nation.

**[MI]** Chancellor Bernhard von Bülow coined the political expression **Nibelungentreue** in 1909 for unconditional German loyalty to Austria-Hungary. The model was the Burgundians’ refusal to surrender Hagen even when that loyalty ensured their destruction. Gunther thereby became part of a political vocabulary of fatal solidarity.

**[MI]** National Socialism appropriated the Nibelung complex, particularly loyalty, sacrifice, and annihilatory endurance. Hermann Göring compared the encircled German army at Stalingrad to the doomed Nibelungs. This does not make the medieval poem Nazi; it documents a modern ideological reading.

**[MI]** Contemporary white-nationalist and ethnonationalist pagan milieus sometimes appropriate an imaginary homogeneous “Germanic” heroic past. Evidence supports appropriation of the wider Norse/Nibelung complex, not a distinctive modern cult centered on Gunnar. Claims that Gunnar embodies an ancient racial religion are modern constructions.

### Psychology and comparative mythology

Gunnar has not generated a discrete interpretive school comparable to Oedipus. He is used within broader heroic-pattern studies:

- **Otto Rank, *The Myth of the Birth of the Hero* (1909):** Gunnar does not fit the birth-pattern centrally; applying it to him requires selective abstraction, **[MSR]**.
- **Lord Raglan, *The Hero* (1936):** Gunnar scores poorly against the ritual-hero schema; he lacks miraculous birth, divine parentage, and cult, **[MSR]**.
- **Joseph Campbell, *The Hero with a Thousand Faces* (1949):** Gunnar’s story does not neatly instantiate a monomyth; labeling him a standard Campbellian quest hero is a modern analytic imposition, **[MSR/MI when marketed as ancient structure]**.
- **Georges Dumézil:** Indo-European functional comparisons illuminate heroic ideology in general, but no consensus derives Gunnar directly from a reconstructed Indo-European deity, **[MSR]**.
- **Oral-formulaic and performance approaches:** plausibly explain multiformity and recomposition but cannot recover exact fifth-century songs, **[MSR]**.

### Commercial and popular invention

Modern fantasy frequently makes Gunther a warrior prince, villain, dragon-linked king, or playable unit. Such works often give him magic, divine descent, or children absent from medieval sources. These are **[MI]** unless explicitly traceable to a named medieval witness. In particular:

- a “Gunnar rune” or ancient sigil uniquely belonging to the hero is unattested;
- an ancient cult of Gunnar as a snake or music god is unattested;
- the toe-played harp is medieval Norse, not a fifth-century historical report;
- calling Wagner’s plot “the original myth” is false;
- identifying every snake-pit image as Gunnar is methodologically unsafe.

## 8. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Historicity

**Consensus:** a historical Gundaharius existed. The convergence of Olympiodorus, Prosper, and the Burgundian law code is strong.

**Debate:** how directly the heroic figure preserves him. Name, kingship, Burgundian association, and destruction by Huns are persuasive. The treasure, Brynhild, Sigurd, oath, harp, snake-pit, and family tree are unattested historically.

**Debate:** Worms. Later literature consistently makes Worms important, but fifth-century sources do not name it as Gundaharius’ capital. Archaeological and political reconstructions allow a Middle Rhine realm, yet “kingdom of Worms” remains an inference.

**Discredited but persistent theory:** detailed heroic narrative is encrypted fifth-century history. Modern scholarship instead treats the texts as successive literary transformations.

### Dating the poetry

*Atlakviða* is often called ninth-century and among the oldest Eddic poems. Linguistic archaism, meter, and narrative economy support relative antiquity, but estimates range later, and the “Greenlandic” label is itself problematic. Codex Regius c. 1270 is the secure physical witness.

*Oddrúnargrátr* is generally later, often placed in the eleventh century, partly because Oddrún’s role and romance are otherwise secondary. Exact dating remains inferential.

Bragi’s *Ragnarsdrápa* may supply ninth-century allusions, but attribution and the reconstruction of verses quoted centuries later require caution.

### Relationship between German and Norse versions

Earlier scholarship often sought a single original *Nibelungensage*. Current work emphasizes multiform transmission. The German and Norse traditions share a character-network and catastrophic skeleton but reorganize motive:

- German: dynastic honor, Hagen’s loyalty, Kriemhild’s revenge.
- Norse: cursed treasure, oath-breaking, Gunnar and Högni’s resistance, Guðrún’s revenge on Atli.
- *Þiðreks saga*: hybrid intermediary, but not necessarily a simple bridge.

Andersson’s reconstruction of the Brynhild legend remains influential; its stages are scholarly hypotheses, not lost manuscripts.

### Oral tradition and manuscripts

The time between fifth-century events and surviving heroic manuscripts makes oral transmission unavoidable as a general explanation but unrecoverable in detail. “Oral” does not mean unchanged. Performers could combine cycles, shift kinship, localize settings, and moralize motives.

Major losses shape the dossier:

- Olympiodorus survives only indirectly.
- *Waldere* survives in two fragments.
- Codex Regius has the “great lacuna” in the Sigurd cycle.
- *Völsunga saga* sometimes paraphrases poetry now lost.
- Eddic poems survive in late manuscripts whose readings can be corrupt.
- *Þiðreks saga* redactions disagree.
- The *Nibelungenlied* exists in multiple versions rather than one authorially fixed text.

### Iconographic debate

Earlier art history often identified any bound man among snakes as Gunnar. Guðmundsdóttir has argued for a broader Gunnar iconography without a harp and for links among Gotlandic stones, Viking-Age art, and later church carvings. The countervailing principle is that snakes are widespread motifs and can represent punishment, legend, biblical material, or decorative cosmology. Secure identification requires a distinctive attribute or narrative context; the harp provides the strongest marker.

### Cult debate

There is no positive evidence for Gunnar cult. Heroic images in churches, memorial stones, and drinking equipment indicate narrative fame. Interpreting them as remnants of worship confuses representation with ritual.

### Major modern scholarship

- **Gustav Neckel/Hans Kuhn:** foundational critical work on the Eddic poems.
- **Ursula Dronke:** detailed textual and literary study of the heroic Edda.
- **E. O. G. Turville-Petre:** heroic poetry, myth, and medieval Norse tradition.
- **Rudolf Simek:** reference synthesis on Norse myth and heroic material.
- **Theodore M. Andersson:** comparative development of the Brynhild and Nibelung traditions.
- **Edward R. Haymes and Susann Samples; Victor Millet:** Germanic heroic legend and the Nibelung tradition.
- **Aðalheiður Guðmundsdóttir:** principal modern study of Gunnar’s snake-pit iconography.
- **Klaeber and Andy Orchard:** essential for the Old English heroic environment; *Beowulf* itself does not attest Gunnar.
- **J. R. R. Tolkien:** influential literary and philological readings of Germanic heroic verse; his creative Sigurd–Gudrún poems are modern compositions.
- **Elizabeth Ashman Rowe and the *Stories for All Time* project:** manuscript and genre work on *fornaldarsögur*.
- **Skaldic Poetry of the Scandinavian Middle Ages project:** critical database for skaldic fragments, attributions, and textual contexts.
