# Signy: Research Dossier

> **Traditional class:** mortal legendary heroine of the Völsung cycle—royal daughter, unwilling dynastic bride, architect of blood-feud vengeance, incestuous mother of an avenger, and self-chosen victim of the concluding hall-burning. She is not classed as a goddess, demigoddess, valkyrie, saint, or cult-receiving heroic dead.
>
> **Earliest secure attestation:** the named Völsung heroine **Signý Vǫlsungsdóttir** is first securely attested in the anonymous Old Norse-Icelandic *Vǫlsunga saga*, composed probably in the later thirteenth century, conventionally c. 1250–1270. Its only complete medieval manuscript is the Icelandic parchment **NKS 1824 b 4to**, written c. 1400. Older evidence—most importantly *Beowulf*’s Sigemund and Fitela and the Eddic Sinfjǫtli material—establishes the antiquity of adjacent members of the heroic cycle, but neither securely names Signý nor preserves her complete story. The first part of *Vǫlsunga saga*, including Signý’s narrative, has no extant Eddic poem as its direct parallel. [Store norske leksikon](https://snl.no/V%C7%ABlsunga_saga); [Gottfried-Portal manuscript description](https://gottfried.unistra.fr/nibelungen/islandische-und-norrone-fassungen/volsungasaga/)

## Evidentiary key

Every substantive item below is assigned one of these categories:

- **[E] Securely attested early comparative material:** evidence older than *Vǫlsunga saga* that genuinely belongs to the Sigemund–Fitela/Sinfjǫtli or broader Völsung complex, but does not necessarily attest Signý herself.
- **[C] Later canonical medieval account:** the named heroine’s narrative in *Vǫlsunga saga* and closely dependent medieval witnesses.
- **[V] Regional or independent variant:** a divergent medieval tradition or a different heroine bearing the same name.
- **[L] Learned systematization:** genealogical, antiquarian, editorial, or scholarly combination of originally separate evidence.
- **[A] Medieval or early-modern accretion:** later manuscript, rímur, chronicle, or antiquarian development.
- **[F] Folklore:** orally transmitted ballad or place-lore, chiefly concerning the distinct Hagbarðr-and-Signý heroine.
- **[R] Modern scholarly reconstruction:** a reasoned inference, not a surviving medieval statement.
- **[M] Modern reception or invention:** post-medieval adaptation, conflation, creative rewriting, or unsupported popular claim.
- **[N] Negative finding:** no secure evidence located in the specified corpus.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### Identification

**[C]** Signý is the daughter of King Vǫlsungr, twin sister of Sigmundr, and wife of the Gautish king Siggeirr in *Vǫlsunga saga*, chapters 2–8. After foreseeing danger in the marriage, she is nevertheless given to Siggeirr. When he kills Vǫlsungr and all but one of her brothers, Signý preserves Sigmundr, engineers a long revenge, orders Sigmundr to kill her two sons by Siggeirr after they fail tests of courage, exchanges appearances with a sorceress, sleeps incognito with Sigmundr for three nights, and bears Sinfjǫtli. Sigmundr and Sinfjǫtli eventually burn Siggeirr’s hall. Offered escape, Signý reveals Sinfjǫtli’s parentage and voluntarily returns to the fire, saying that everything she did was directed toward Siggeirr’s death. [*Völsunga saga*, chapters 5–8](https://morrisarchive.lib.uiowa.edu/volsunga-saga---chapter-5); [chapter 6](https://morrisarchive.lib.uiowa.edu/volsunga-saga---chapter-6); [chapter 7](https://morrisarchive.lib.uiowa.edu/volsunga-saga---chapter-7); [chapter 8](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/V%C3%B6lsunga_Saga/Chapter_8)

She is therefore best described as a **mortal heroic woman and feud-avenger**, not a supernatural being. Her ability to assume another appearance is obtained from an unnamed *seiðkona*, not presented as an inherent divine power.

### Name

**Old Norse-Icelandic:** **Signý**, conventionally normalized *Signý*; genitive *Signýjar* in modern Icelandic.

**Modern forms:** Icelandic and Faroese *Signý*; Norwegian and Danish *Signy* or *Signe*; Swedish *Signe*; English scholarship commonly *Signy*.

**[L]** The conventional etymology divides the dithematic name as **Sig-** < Old Norse *sigr*, “victory,” plus **-ný**, feminine of *nýr*, “new” or possibly “young”: hence “new victory,” or less mechanically “young/new [woman] of victory.” The Swedish Institute for Language and Folklore gives *Sig-* “victory” + *-ný* “new”; Swedish reference tradition also allows “young” for the second member. [Institutet för språk och folkminnen](https://www.isof.se/utforska/kunskapsbanker/lar-dig-mer-om-arets-namn-och-handelser/namn/signe); [NE name entry](https://www.ne.se/uppslagsverk/encyklopedi/l%C3%A5ng/signe)

The translation “new victory” is a modern compositional gloss, not a medieval interpretation of the character. Nothing in the saga explicitly makes Signý’s name prophetic.

### Epithets and patronymics

**[C]** The saga ordinarily identifies her relationally: **Signý, daughter of Vǫlsungr**, Sigmundr’s sister, or Siggeirr’s wife. No stable cultic epithet or heroic byname comparable to *Fáfnisbani* (“Fáfnir’s slayer”) is attached to her.

**[M]** “Signy the Volsung,” “Signy Volsungsdaughter,” “the avenging queen,” and similar English phrases are useful modern descriptors, not securely attested medieval epithets.

### Distinct figures who must not be merged

1. **Signý Vǫlsungsdóttir** — the subject of this dossier. **[C]**

2. **Signý/Sygne, daughter of Sigar, lover of Hagbarðr** — a separate legendary heroine. Her fullest medieval account is Saxo Grammaticus, *Gesta Danorum* VII, composed in Latin around 1200. She and Hagbarðr pledge fidelity; after his capture she arranges to burn her chamber and hang herself when she believes he has been executed. Saxo calls her *Sygne*. **[V]** [Saxo, Latin text](https://la.wikisource.org/wiki/Gesta_Danorum/Liber_VII); [English translation](https://sacred-texts.com/neu/saxo/saxo07.htm)

3. **Signý in *Illuga saga Gríðarfóstra*** — daughter of the troll-woman Gríðr, released from enchantment and married to Illugi. This is another person in a late legendary saga. **[V]**

4. **The proposed Signý among the Scyldings** — the name of Hroðgar’s sister is lost in *Beowulf* line 62. Kluge proposed an Old English restoration such as *Signeow*; later Scandinavian sources call a corresponding woman Signý, while other reconstruction identifies her as Yrsa. This is conjectural and not the Völsung heroine. **[R]**

5. **Sigyn, Loki’s wife** — phonetic resemblance does not establish identity. Her Old Norse name, textual role, and genealogy are different. Equating Signý with Sigyn is a modern error. **[N/M]**

6. **Wagner’s Sieglinde** — not simply Signý under a German spelling. She is a nineteenth-century composite based principally on Signý’s status as Siegmund’s twin and incestuous partner and Hjǫrdís’s position as Sigurðr’s mother. **[M]** [University of Texas Wagner source guide](https://www.laits.utexas.edu/wagner/ringsources/diewalkure.html)

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### The older comparative horizon

#### *Beowulf*

**[E]** The Old English poem *Beowulf*, probably composed sometime between the eighth and early eleventh centuries and preserved in London, British Library, Cotton Vitellius A.xv, c. 1000, contains the earliest surviving recognizable relative of the Völsung material. At lines 874–900 a scop celebrates **Sigemund, son of Wæls**, and his *nefa* **Fitela**. The pair kill giants; Sigemund later kills a dragon and takes its treasure.

What *Beowulf* establishes:

- Sigemund and Fitela were already figures of heroic legend in the Old English-speaking world. **[E]**
- Fitela is called Sigemund’s *nefa*, normally “nephew,” consistent with Sinfjǫtli being his sister’s son. **[R/E]**
- The passage supplies an early cognate of Vǫlsungr in **Wæls/Wælsing**. **[E]**

What it does **not** establish:

- It does not name Signý. **[N]**
- It does not state that Fitela is also Sigemund’s biological son. **[N]**
- It does not narrate incest, Siggeirr’s treachery, the child tests, or the hall-burning. **[N]**
- It assigns the dragon exploit to Sigemund, whereas later Norse tradition makes Sigurðr the principal dragon-slayer. This is genuine variation, not a mistake to be harmonized. **[V]**

The identification Fitela = Sinfjǫtli is a very strong philological comparison, but the deduction that *Beowulf*’s audience necessarily knew the complete thirteenth-century Signý story remains reconstruction. [Oxford digital *Beowulf* edition](https://editions.mml.ox.ac.uk/editions/beowulf/); [discussion of lines 874–902](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/anglo-saxon-england/article/abs/some-difficulties-in-beowulf-lines-874902-sigemund-reconsidered/84A9726FDE9978915D1CA4DF5D2B484F)

#### The Poetic Edda

**[E/N]** The Eddic heroic corpus attests Sigmundr and Sinfjǫtli but does not preserve a poem narrating Signý’s life.

- *Helgakviða Hundingsbana I* contains Sinfjǫtli’s flyting with Guðmundr and presupposes his heroic career. Signý is not an actor in the poem. **[E/N]**
- *Frá dauða Sinfjǫtla*, a prose bridge in Codex Regius of the Poetic Edda, names Sigmundr’s sons Sinfjǫtli, Helgi, and Hámundr, then narrates Borghildr’s poisoning of Sinfjǫtli. It does not name Sinfjǫtli’s mother. **[E/N]** [Old Norse text](https://heimskringla.no/wiki/Gr%C3%ADpissp%C7%AB%CC%81); [Danish translation](https://heimskringla.no/wiki/Om_Sinfj%C3%B8tles_d%C3%B8d)
- References to Sigmundr, Sinfjǫtli, and Völsung genealogy elsewhere in heroic poetry establish the surrounding cycle but not Signý’s own story. **[E/N]**

The absence matters: the full Signý episode is not “in the Poetic Edda.” It is plausible that the saga compiler used lost verse, but this cannot convert a lost hypothetical poem into an extant early attestation.

### *Vǫlsunga saga*

**[C]** This is the foundational and effectively sole medieval narrative witness for Signý Vǫlsungsdóttir.

- **Language:** Old Norse-Icelandic prose with verse insertions.
- **Composition:** commonly placed in the later thirteenth century, perhaps c. 1250–1270.
- **Principal manuscript:** NKS 1824 b 4to, c. 1400, where it is followed by *Ragnars saga loðbrókar*.
- **Secondary transmission:** roughly fifty later paper manuscripts, principally seventeenth to nineteenth century.
- **Role:** chapters 2–8 give Signý’s ancestry, marriage, warnings, preservation of Sigmundr, killing of her children, conception of Sinfjǫtli, and death.

The saga’s later sections closely paraphrase extant Eddic lays. Its opening Signý section does not: Store norske leksikon expressly notes that the first part is not preserved in known poems. The compiler may have had lost poems, oral narratives, prose traditions, or some combination. **[R]**

A four-line verse about Sigmundr and Sinfjǫtli cutting through the stone prison is embedded in the prose. Scholars including Finnur Jónsson and Peter Andreas Munch took its presence and the elevated final speech as traces of a lost Signý/Sigmundr lay. That is plausible but unprovable. **[R]**

### Snorri Sturluson’s *Edda*

**[L/N]** *Skáldskaparmál*, composed c. 1220, knows the Völsung heroic world and mentions Siggeirr as Vǫlsungr’s son-in-law in some textual contexts, but it does not preserve Signý’s revenge biography. Its lists of legendary couples include **Hagbarðr and Signý**, the other Signý. Those references must not be transferred to Vǫlsungr’s daughter. [Faulkes’s critical edition of *Skáldskaparmál*](https://vsnr.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/Edda-2_skaldskaparmal_combined.pdf)

### *Vǫlsungarímur*

**[A]** The late-medieval Icelandic *Vǫlsungarímur* recast the early Völsung narrative in rímur form. Their value is as evidence of continuing medieval reception and variation, not as an independent Viking-Age record. The verse “Signý fekk þat nauða” represents her coerced or grievous marriage in this later poetic transmission. [Old Norse text](https://www.heimskringla.no/wiki/V%C3%B6lsungar%C3%ADmur_I-VI)

### German and Norwegian analogues

#### *Nibelungenlied*

**[N/V]** The Middle High German *Nibelungenlied*, composed around 1200 and surviving in thirteenth-century manuscripts, belongs to the wider Siegfried–Burgundian complex. It does not contain Signý Vǫlsungsdóttir, Sinfjǫtli, or her revenge cycle. Its Siegfried is not made the product of Sigmund–Signý incest.

#### *Þiðreks saga af Bern*

**[N/V]** The Old Norse *Þiðreks saga*, compiled in Norway in the thirteenth century from predominantly German narrative material, has Sigmundr, Sisibe, and Sigurðr/Siegfried traditions but no equivalent of the Völsung Signý plot. Sisibe is not Signý.

#### *Ragnars saga loðbrókar*

**[N/L]** This saga follows *Vǫlsunga saga* in NKS 1824 b 4to, connecting Sigurðr and Brynhildr’s daughter Áslaug to Ragnarr. Signý belongs genealogically to the prehistory of that constructed continuum but does not act in *Ragnars saga*.

### Other corpora named in the brief

- ***Hervarar saga ok Heiðreks***: no Signý Vǫlsungsdóttir episode. **[N]**
- ***Gautreks saga***: no such episode. **[N]**
- ***Grettis saga Ásmundarsonar***: no Signý; comparisons between Grettir’s encounters and *Beowulf* concern the Grendel complex, not her. **[N/R]**
- ***Egils saga Skalla-Grímssonar***: no Signý Völsung heroine. **[N]**
- **Other Icelandic family sagas:** women named Signý occur as historical-literary saga persons, but none is identified as Vǫlsungr’s daughter or a survival of her cult. **[N]**
- **Skaldic Poetry of the Scandinavian Middle Ages database:** no securely datable early skaldic stanza narrates her deeds. **[N]**

These absences demonstrate how narrowly her biography is transmitted despite the broad diffusion of related Sigmund–Sigurðr material.

## 3. The story

### Ancestry and birth

**[C]** *Vǫlsunga saga* gives a divine-royal genealogy:

**Óðinn → Sigi → Rerir → Vǫlsungr → the twins Sigmundr and Signý**

Rerir’s wife conceives Vǫlsungr through a fertility apple delivered by Hljóð after Rerir and his wife pray to Óðinn and Frigg. Vǫlsungr later marries Hljóð. Signý and Sigmundr are their eldest children and twins; nine further sons follow.

This makes Signý a remote descendant of Óðinn, not a demigoddess. Óðinn is her great-great-grandfather within the saga’s learned heroic genealogy.

### The marriage to Siggeirr

**[C]** Siggeirr, king of Gautland, asks for Signý. Vǫlsungr and his sons approve; Signý is reluctant and says her foreknowledge tells her that no good will result. Her father refuses to break the agreement, and she marries Siggeirr.

At the wedding, a one-eyed stranger—identifiable within the saga as Óðinn—enters the hall and drives a sword into the great tree Barnstokkr. Only Sigmundr can draw it. Siggeirr offers to buy the sword; Sigmundr refuses, provoking resentment. The event makes the marriage feast the point at which supernatural dynastic favor and human hostility intersect.

The saga does not say Signý selected Siggeirr, loved him, or consented enthusiastically. It also does not stage a physical abduction. “Forced marriage” is a reasonable modern description of her constrained position, but the medieval text frames it as paternal enforcement of a pledged dynastic match.

### Warning and massacre

**[C]** Siggeirr departs angrily and later invites Vǫlsungr and his sons to Gautland. Signý secretly meets the arriving Völsungs and warns that her husband has assembled overwhelming forces and intends treachery. She advises retreat. Vǫlsungr refuses because flight would violate his heroic honor; he also orders Signý back to her husband.

Siggeirr’s army kills Vǫlsungr and captures his ten sons. Signý requests that they not be killed immediately but placed in stocks. Siggeirr agrees because he interprets delay as added torture.

### The she-wolf and Sigmundr’s survival

**[C]** Each night an enormous she-wolf eats one brother. Before the tenth night Signý sends a trusted servant with honey to place on Sigmundr’s face and in his mouth. The wolf licks the honey; Sigmundr bites its tongue and kills it while breaking the stocks.

The narrator adds a variant formula: **“some men say”** the wolf was Siggeirr’s mother, transformed by witchcraft. This is an explicitly marked intra-saga variant:

- Giant predatory she-wolf: narrative baseline. **[C]**
- Siggeirr’s shape-changing mother: attributed alternative. **[V]**

Signý then helps Sigmundr establish a hidden underground dwelling and supplies him.

### The deaths of Signý’s sons by Siggeirr

**[C]** Signý sends her elder son by Siggeirr, aged ten, to Sigmundr as a prospective avenger. The child is afraid to knead flour because something living is inside the sack. Sigmundr declares him unfit. Signý says, in substance, “take and kill him; why should he live longer?” Sigmundr does so. The same sequence occurs with her second son.

Before sending the boys, Signý tests them by sewing their clothing or gloves through skin and flesh. They cry out and fail in her judgment. This is deliberate maternal complicity in child-killing. The saga neither conceals nor exonerates it.

The sons remain unnamed in the surviving text. They therefore cannot receive separate named interaction entries below.

### Shape-exchange, sexual deception, and incest

**[C]** Signý meets an unnamed woman skilled in magic and asks to exchange appearances. The sorceress takes Signý’s place beside Siggeirr, who does not detect the substitution. Signý, wearing the other woman’s form, goes to Sigmundr’s earth-house and claims to be a lost traveler.

Sigmundr desires the apparent stranger; the two share a bed for three nights. Signý then returns and resumes her own appearance. She gives birth to Sinfjǫtli.

The episode is incest, but it is also sexual deception: Sigmundr consents to sex with the woman he perceives, not knowingly with his sister. The saga assigns the disguise and reproductive plan to Signý. Calling the act simply a mutual “affair” obscures that textual fact.

No surviving source says that Signý becomes physically pregnant while still magically transformed, that the sorceress is a goddess, or that Siggeirr knowingly approves the exchange.

### Sinfjǫtli’s testing

**[C]** Signý sews Sinfjǫtli’s garment through skin and flesh. Unlike his half-brothers, he does not flinch. When she tears it away with skin attached, he minimizes the pain as something Vǫlsungr would scarcely have felt.

Sent to Sigmundr before age ten, he kneads the sack’s living contents into the dough. Sigmundr identifies the creature as a deadly serpent. Sinfjǫtli can withstand venom externally, though not yet ingest it. The episode marks him as doubly Völsung in body and temperament before Sigmundr learns his parentage.

### Outlaw years and wolf transformation

**[C]** Sigmundr and Sinfjǫtli find two sleeping skin-changers with wolf pelts. Putting on the pelts traps them in lupine form until the appointed interval ends. They range separately through the forest, kill men, and communicate by howling. Sigmundr wounds Sinfjǫtli severely after the younger man disregards their agreed limit on opponents; a raven-borne leaf restores him in the saga’s account.

Signý does not accompany their wolf exploits. Modern summaries that make her part of a werewolf trio are inventions.

### Capture, burial, and escape

**[C]** Sigmundr and Sinfjǫtli enter Siggeirr’s hall but are discovered by two young children of Signý and Siggeirr. Signý urges the intruders to kill the children so they cannot report them; Sigmundr refuses, but Sinfjǫtli kills them. It is not entirely clear whether these are additional children beyond the two earlier sons, a narrative duplication, or an inconsistency produced by compilation. The saga should not be forcibly regularized.

Siggeirr captures the two Völsungs and entombs them alive in a stone structure divided by a slab. Signý has a bundle of straw thrown inside before closure; it contains Sigmundr’s sword. Father and son cut through the stone, escape, and pile wood around the sleeping hall.

### The burning hall and Signý’s death

**[C]** Sigmundr and Sinfjǫtli ignite the hall. Siggeirr asks who is responsible; Sigmundr identifies himself and his sister’s son.

Sigmundr calls Signý out and offers her honor and compensation. She refuses. Her final declaration supplies the saga’s retrospective explanation:

- she caused her sons by Siggeirr to be killed because they were unfit to avenge Vǫlsungr;
- she approached Sigmundr in disguise;
- Sinfjǫtli is the child of both Vǫlsungr’s son and daughter;
- everything was done to bring about Siggeirr’s death;
- she does not wish to live afterward;
- although she was not glad to marry Siggeirr, she will now die with him.

She kisses Sigmundr and Sinfjǫtli, returns to the burning hall, and dies with Siggeirr and his men.

The text presents a voluntary final act, but its meaning is not reduced to one motive. It may simultaneously complete the feud, acknowledge responsibility for incest and filicide, fulfill the marital bond she never chose, and remove a woman whose actions have transgressed every normal kinship role. Those functional interpretations are scholarly; the saga itself gives vengeance completed and a refusal to live longer.

### Every documented interaction with a named god or hero

The list is deliberately restrictive. Merely occupying the same genealogy is not counted as an interaction.

- **Vǫlsungr — father and daughter:** he accepts Siggeirr’s proposal despite Signý’s foreboding; later rejects her warning to retreat and commands her to return to her husband. *Vǫlsunga saga*, chs. 3 and 5, late thirteenth century. **[C]**

- **Siggeirr — husband and enemy:** he marries Signý, resents Sigmundr, betrays and kills her father and brothers, unknowingly sleeps with the substituted sorceress, captures Sigmundr and Sinfjǫtli, and dies when Signý’s revenge culminates in the hall-burning; Signý returns to die beside him. *Vǫlsunga saga*, chs. 3–8. **[C]**

- **Sigmundr — twin brother, protected fugitive, deceived sexual partner, and fellow avenger:** Signý saves him from the she-wolf, hides and provisions him, sends her sons to him, approaches him in another woman’s appearance, conceives Sinfjǫtli by him, supplies his buried sword, reveals their son’s parentage, kisses him farewell, and dies. *Vǫlsunga saga*, chs. 5–8. **[C]**

- **Sinfjǫtli — son and instrument of vengeance:** Signý tests him, sends him to Sigmundr, protects or assists him during the infiltration and burial episodes, reveals his true parentage, and kisses him before returning to the fire. *Vǫlsunga saga*, chs. 7–8. **[C]**

- **Óðinn — joint appearance without direct dialogue:** disguised, he enters Signý’s wedding feast and plants the sword in Barnstokkr, initiating the sword conflict between Sigmundr and Siggeirr. The saga records no direct speech between Óðinn and Signý. *Vǫlsunga saga*, ch. 3. **[C]**

No direct interaction is documented between Signý and Frigg, Hljóð after Signý’s birth, Sigurðr, Helgi, Brynhildr, Guðrún, Loki, Þórr, or any named valkyrie. **[N]**

The sorceress, trusted servant, Siggeirr’s mother, and Signý’s children by Siggeirr are not individually listed because the saga does not name them.

## 5. Cult, tomb, image, and ritual

### Cult

**[N]** No medieval source describes sacrifice, prayer, oath-taking, festival, shrine, priesthood, oracle, sacred spring, or hero-cult offered to Signý Vǫlsungsdóttir.

**[N]** No archaeological structure has been securely identified as her grave, her hall, or a sanctuary dedicated to her.

**[N]** No runic inscription securely identifies or invokes this heroine. Historical women bore the same personal name; a name alone cannot establish reference to the legendary figure.

### Images

**[N]** No surviving Viking-Age or medieval image is securely identifiable as Signý. Scandinavian and British carvings interpreted as episodes from the Völsung cycle—such as Sigurðr killing Fáfnir or roasting the dragon’s heart—concern Sigurðr and Reginn, not Signý.

Scenes such as a woman at a burning hall, a bound man and wolf, or an incestuous conception would require accompanying inscription or an unusually exact narrative ensemble before identification could be secure. No such identification has scholarly consensus.

### Tomb traditions belonging to the other Signý

**[F/A]** “Signý’s chamber,” “Signý’s mound,” wells, cairns, and associated monuments in Halland, Blekinge, Uppland, Närke, and Denmark belong to the **Hagbarðr-and-Signý** place-lore, not the Völsung heroine.

At Asige in Halland, “Hagbard’s Gallows” consists of monumental standing stones and mounds dating to the Bronze Age, long before the surviving medieval legend. Modern archaeology treats the medieval attribution as later cultural association, not proof that Hagbarðr or Signý was buried there. The site remains a focus of local theatrical reenactment. **[F/A]** [*Antiquity* archaeological study](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/hagbards-gallows-in-context-dating-a-bronze-age-monument-in-southwestern-sweden/425DAA1716AEABF1148AB74CCB0DE829)

Assigning those monuments to Vǫlsungr’s daughter is a double conflation: it merges two Signýs and treats folklore attached to prehistoric remains as contemporary archaeology. **[M]**

### Naming

**[A/M]** Signý continued as a Scandinavian personal name and survives as Signy/Signe. That constitutes reception of a traditional name, not proof of cult. Its modern Norwegian popularity was strongest roughly from 1880 to 1950, overlapping the Nordic literary revival, but individual namings cannot normally be attributed specifically to the Völsung heroine without documentary evidence. [Nordic Names](https://www.nordicnames.de/wiki/Signy)

## 6. Historical evolution of the figure

### Phase I: early Germanic heroic background, before c. 1000

**[E]** *Beowulf* demonstrates an early Sigemund–Fitela heroic pairing. Fitela’s description as Sigemund’s nephew is compatible with, but does not prove, the later brother-sister genealogy. The dragon exploit and lineage differ from later Norse organization.

**[N/R]** A Signý-like sister may have existed in oral tradition, but she is not named in the surviving Old English poem. Reconstruction of her full role at this stage exceeds the evidence.

### Phase II: Eddic and skaldic heroic transmission, approximately ninth–thirteenth centuries

**[E]** Eddic poetry preserves Sinfjǫtli as Sigmundr’s son and heroic companion and preserves his later death. The poetry does not preserve Signý’s revenge narrative.

**[R]** The prose saga’s verse fragment, compact episode structure, and formal final declaration have encouraged reconstruction of one or more lost lays. The exact contents, dates, and order of such lays are unknowable.

### Phase III: thirteenth-century saga synthesis

**[C]** *Vǫlsunga saga* gives the first complete, named Signý. It organizes diverse heroic materials into a dynastic history extending from Óðinn through Sigurðr, the Burgundians, Atli, and the descendants who connect to Ragnarr loðbrók.

Within that synthesis, Signý performs several structural tasks:

- preserves the Völsung line after catastrophe;
- creates a biologically “double” Völsung avenger;
- links vengeance, reproductive control, and kinship transgression;
- provides a first hall-burning that anticipates later destruction in the cycle;
- demonstrates that heroic loyalty to natal kin can annihilate marriage and motherhood.

These are literary functions inferred from the compiled narrative. **[R]**

### Phase IV: late-medieval rímur and manuscript transmission

**[A]** *Vǫlsungarímur* versifies the early cycle. Later manuscripts reproduce and sometimes modernize the saga, but no independent medieval witness supplies a radically different ending for Vǫlsungr’s daughter.

The Hagbarðr-and-Signý tradition, meanwhile, develops independently in Saxo, ballads, chronicles, local place-lore, and antiquarian geography. Similarities—an aristocratic Signý, a burning chamber, voluntary death—may have encouraged popular conflation, but common motifs do not establish identity. **[V/R]**

### Phase V: antiquarian recovery, seventeenth–eighteenth centuries

**[A/L]** Editors and antiquarians catalogued legendary sagas and attempted to locate their narratives in physical landscapes. In the Hagbarðr tradition, prehistoric monuments received narrative names. The Völsung Signý did not acquire a comparably documented localization.

### Phase VI: Romantic and national revival, nineteenth century

**[M]** Norse heroic literature became material for Scandinavian and British national-cultural projects.

- C. C. Rafn included *Vǫlsunga saga* in *Fornaldar Sögur Nordrlanda* (1829–30), consolidating its place in a printed “ancient northern” corpus.
- William Morris and Eiríkr Magnússon published an English translation, *Völsunga Saga: The Story of the Volsungs and Niblungs*, in 1870.
- Morris’s *The Story of Sigurd the Volsung and the Fall of the Niblungs* appeared in 1876–77. It expands Signý emotionally, but retains her calculated incest and death.
- Richard Wagner’s *Die Walküre* premiered in 1870. Its Sieglinde combines Signý with Hjǫrdís and transforms the reproductive plot: the twins recognize and embrace their incestuous love, and their child is Siegfried rather than Sinfjǫtli.

Morris explicitly promoted the Völsung story as a northern counterpart to the Trojan cycle and used the language of a shared “race.” That is a documented Victorian cultural-national framing, not a medieval self-description. [Morris historical introduction](https://morrisarchive.lib.uiowa.edu/introduction-historical-sigurd)

### Ibsen and the Scandinavian revival

The requested “Signy of Ibsen” requires correction.

**[M/N]** Henrik Ibsen did not dramatize Signý Vǫlsungsdóttir under that identity. His early medievalist play *Gildet paa Solhoug* (*The Feast at Solhaug*), first staged in 1856, contains a young woman named **Signe**, sister of Margit and beloved of Gudmund. She does not enact the Völsung plot: she neither marries Siggeirr, conceives Sinfjǫtli, kills children, nor dies in a burning hall. [Ibsen text](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/66060/66060-h/66060-h.htm)

Ibsen’s *Hærmændene paa Helgeland* (*The Vikings at Helgeland*), written in 1857 and staged in 1858, participates more broadly in saga revival but its principal women are Hjørdis and Dagny, not Signý. **[M/N]**

The accurate conclusion is therefore:

- Ibsen belongs to the nineteenth-century Scandinavian revival of saga-inflected drama. **[M]**
- His Signe at Solhaug is a namesake within medievalizing fiction, not an adaptation of Vǫlsungr’s daughter. **[M/N]**
- Claims that Ibsen wrote “the Signy of the Völsungs” appear to arise from name and revival-context conflation. **[M]**

### Phase VII: twentieth–twenty-first-century literary reception

**[M]** J. R. R. Tolkien composed *Völsungakviða en nýja*, “The New Lay of the Völsungs,” probably in the 1920s or 1930s; it was published posthumously in *The Legend of Sigurd and Gudrún* in 2009. Its second section is titled “Signý.” Tolkien compresses and consciously reconstructs a poetic version of the cycle; it is not a translation of a recovered medieval lay. [Tolkien Estate synopsis](https://www.tolkienestate.com/writing/the-legend-of-sigurd-and-gudrun-a-prose-account-of-the-story/)

**[M]** Arthur Gilchrist Brodeur’s “Vengeance,” published in *Adventure* on 30 June 1925, retells the Signý–Sigmundr revenge.

**[M]** Modern translations by R. G. Finch (1965), Jesse Byock (1990), Kaaren Grimstad, and others have made the saga widely accessible and have increasingly emphasized its manuscript and comparative context.

**[M]** The Netflix series *Ragnarok* (2020–2023) includes a modern teenage character named Signy. Popular fan theories identify her with Sif or other mythic women, but the series character is not a faithful adaptation of Signý Vǫlsungsdóttir, and the medieval sources do not identify Signý with Sif.

## 7. Modern reception and reinvention

### William Morris

**[M]** Morris’s 1870 translation with Eiríkr Magnússon was central to Anglophone knowledge of Signý. His 1876–77 epic poem enlarges the saga’s speeches and psychology. In Morris, Signý’s incest is not romantic passion but a purposeful sacrifice to continue and avenge the Völsung line. He gives her more developed farewells to Sigmundr and Sinfjǫtli. [William Morris Archive](https://morrisarchive.lib.uiowa.edu/exhibits/show/poetry/sigurdthevolsung); [editorial notes](https://morrisarchive.lib.uiowa.edu/sigurd-notes)

Morris’s racial-national rhetoric—calling the saga a northern equivalent to Troy and a story for “our race”—belongs to Victorian medievalism. It should not be projected backward onto the saga.

### Wagner

**[M]** Wagner’s **Sieglinde** is the most influential indirect reinvention:

- Signý contributes the twin-sister relationship and incest with Sigmund/Siegmund.
- Hjǫrdís contributes motherhood of Sigurðr/Siegfried.
- Siggeirr is replaced as husband by Hunding, borrowed and reconfigured from the Helgi material.
- Signý’s magical disguise, calculated breeding program, child killings, long revenge, and suicide with Siggeirr are removed.
- Wagner turns dynastic vengeance into a drama of spontaneous erotic recognition and conflict with Wotan’s law.

Calling Sieglinde simply “the German Signy” erases this composite construction.

### Tolkien

**[M]** Tolkien’s “Signý” section deliberately creates the sort of continuous alliterative lay that the medieval record no longer supplies. He preserves the unwanted marriage, wolf episode, incestuous conception, revenge, and death but makes selective changes, including the characterization of Signý’s disguised form. It is an authored twentieth-century synthesis published in 2009.

Tolkien’s celebrated 1936 essay “Beowulf: The Monsters and the Critics” changed *Beowulf* studies by insisting that the poem be studied as art rather than mined chiefly for fragmentary history. It does not constitute evidence that *Beowulf* named Signý. [Tolkien essay overview](https://www.medievalists.net/2012/12/beowulf-the-monsters-and-the-critics/)

### Visual art

**[M]** Nineteenth-century Nordic art frequently depicts **Hagbarðr and Signý**, especially the doomed lovers and burning chamber. Works captioned merely “Signy” must be identified by scene: a hanged lover, maidens, or a burning maiden’s chamber normally signals Sigar’s daughter, not Vǫlsungr’s.

No single canonical visual type emerged for Vǫlsungr’s daughter comparable to the horned-helmet valkyrie image of Wagnerian popular culture.

### Psychological, feminist, and structural readings

These are histories of interpretation, not recoveries of the saga author’s conscious doctrine.

- **[R] Psychoanalytic/Jungian:** incestuous twins and their exceptional child have been read as a union of divided psychic principles or a *coniunctio*. Such readings depend on twentieth-century analytical categories.
- **[R] Rank/Raglan/Campbell:** Sinfjǫtli or Sigurðr can be mapped onto heroic-birth patterns, but Signý’s story does not neatly follow Campbell’s later standardized monomyth. Treating her as a “goddess mentor” is modern schema-imposition.
- **[R] Dumézilian/comparativist:** efforts to place the Völsungs in Indo-European functional structures have produced suggestive comparisons but no independent evidence for Signý’s historicity or cult.
- **[R] Gender and feud studies:** recent scholarship reads Signý as an extreme example of the conflict between loyalty to natal kin, marital obligation, reproductive agency, and maternal duty. Her capacity to command killing does not mean the saga grants her stable political sovereignty; her power operates covertly and ends in self-destruction.
- **[R] Consent-centered reading:** the text presents Signý as constrained in marriage while making Sigmundr the target of sexual deception. Both facts can coexist; reducing the story either to female empowerment or to simple victimhood loses the saga’s deliberate moral violence.

### Nationalism and extremist use

**[M]** The Völsung complex broadly entered German and Nordic nationalist discourse through philology, Wagner, racial romanticism, and ideas of ancestral heroic culture. Morris also used “race” rhetoric in an Anglo-Northern cultural sense.

**[N]** No evidence located supports a distinctive political cult, emblem, extremist organization, or nationalist ritual centered specifically on Signý Vǫlsungsdóttir. General appropriation of Wagner, runes, Odinism, or the Völsungs must not be misreported as Signý-specific activity.

Likewise, interpreting her attempt to produce a “pure” double-Völsung child as medieval racial eugenics is anachronistic. The saga stresses lineage, inherited courage, and feud fitness; modern biological-racial ideology is a later overlay. **[M/R]**

### Commercial and popular invention

Common modern errors include:

- “Signý is a Norse goddess.” **[M; false]**
- “Signý is another name for Sigyn or Sif.” **[M; false]**
- “The Poetic Edda tells her complete story.” **[M; false]**
- “She and Sigmundr knowingly fall in romantic love.” **[M; contradicted by the saga’s disguise]**
- “She transforms herself through her own divine power.” **[M; the sorceress performs the exchange]**
- “She is Siegfried’s mother.” **[M; true only of Wagner’s composite Sieglinde; in the saga Sigurðr’s mother is Hjǫrdís]**
- “Signý’s tomb or chamber has been excavated.” **[M; confusion with Hagbarðr place-lore]**
- “Ibsen wrote a tragedy about Völsung Signý.” **[M; unsupported conflation with *The Feast at Solhaug* and the larger saga revival]**

## 8. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Was there a lost Signý lay?

Older scholars often argued yes. The evidence cited is:

1. the short verse embedded in the stone-prison episode;
2. the saga section’s compact episodic construction;
3. Signý’s rhetorically concentrated final speech;
4. *Beowulf*’s much earlier knowledge of Sigemund and Fitela;
5. the saga compiler’s demonstrable use of poetry elsewhere.

**[R]** These points make earlier poetic tradition probable in a general sense. They do not prove a single unified “Lay of Signý,” its exact date, or that the saga preserves it without alteration. The compiler may have combined verse fragments, prose legend, international tale motifs, and authorial transitions.

### Signý’s incest and the Fitela problem

*Beowulf* calls Fitela Sigemund’s nephew; *Vǫlsunga saga* makes Sinfjǫtli both nephew and son. Two main positions follow:

- **Continuity model [R]:** *Beowulf* uses the socially acceptable or genealogically sufficient “nephew” while presupposing the same incest story.
- **Development model [R]:** the incestuous paternity is a later Norse elaboration; *Beowulf* knew only an uncle-nephew team.

The surviving wording cannot decide the matter. Claims that the Old English poet “censored” incest are possible but unprovable.

### Source and motif comparisons

Scholars have compared Signý’s story with:

- sibling incest used to intensify dynastic purity;
- the avenging sister or daughter;
- child-testing and monstrous food;
- concealed identity in sexual encounters;
- shape-shifting and substitution;
- the loyal or fatal wife returning to a burning hall;
- the destructive conflict between natal and marital kin.

**[R]** Comparanda can clarify narrative type, but resemblance does not establish borrowing without linguistic, chronological, and transmission evidence.

The similarity between Völsung Signý’s fiery death and Hagbarðr’s Signý burning with her maidens may represent shared northern narrative motifs, analogical influence, or coincidence. The women’s genealogies, lovers, enemies, and plots remain distinct. **[V/R]**

### Historicity

**[N]** No securely identifiable historical woman stands behind Signý Vǫlsungsdóttir.

Historical events lie behind other parts of the larger cycle: the destruction of the Burgundian kingdom in 436 and the memory of Attila and Ermanaric were transformed into heroic legend. That historical sediment concerns later Völsung/Niflung material, not evidence for Signý’s existence.

Attempts to date Signý to the Migration Period by assigning dates to Sigmundr, Siggeirr, or the Geats mistake narrative setting for attestation. **[M]**

### Pagan survival versus Christian literary work

The saga preserves pre-Christian gods, magical transformations, vengeance ethics, and legendary genealogies. Yet its extant form is the work of a Christian Icelandic literary environment centuries after conversion.

Two extremes should be rejected:

- everything supernatural is a transparent survival of pagan ritual; **[unsupported]**
- everything is a thirteenth-century author’s free invention with no older tradition; **[inconsistent with comparative evidence]**

The defensible position is layered: early heroic names and relationships, lost oral or poetic transmission, and thirteenth-century Christian-Icelandic compilation. **[R]**

### Manuscripts and editions

The text’s vulnerability is substantial. A late-thirteenth-century composition survives completely in only one medieval manuscript copied around 1400, followed by numerous much later paper copies. There is therefore no contemporary authorial manuscript and no early manuscript family through which every reading can be independently controlled.

Major editorial milestones include:

- C. C. Rafn, *Fornaldar Sögur Nordrlanda* I, 1829;
- Sophus Bugge and nineteenth-century Eddic editions, which sharpened comparative work;
- Magnus Olsen, *Vǫlsunga saga ok Ragnars saga loðbrókar*, 1906–08;
- R. G. Finch’s facing-text edition and translation, 1965;
- Jesse Byock’s English translation, 1990;
- Kaaren Grimstad’s manuscript-based edition and translation.

The online Morris translation is historically important but should not replace consultation of a normalized Old Norse critical text.

### Oral-formulaic and compilation questions

The saga’s mixture of prose, remembered verse, formulaic speech, and parallels to extant heroic lays shows that “oral” and “literary” are not mutually exclusive categories. Its compiler could receive material through written Eddic exemplars, written or oral lost verse, and prose storytelling.

The exact balance in the Signý section remains disputed because its presumed poetic source has not survived. **[R]**

### State of the evidence

What is secure:

- the late-thirteenth-century saga tradition;
- the c. 1400 principal manuscript;
- the full plot as transmitted there;
- earlier independent Sigemund–Fitela and Sigmundr–Sinfjǫtli evidence;
- absence of cultic and archaeological corroboration.

What is probable but unrecoverable:

- older oral or poetic materials underlying at least parts of the narrative.

What is speculative:

- a historical Signý;
- a specific lost poem reconstructable scene by scene;
- a Signý cult;
- identification of anonymous images or monuments with her;
- equivalence with Hagbarðr’s Signý, Sigyn, Sif, or Wagner’s Sieglinde.

### Evidentiary conclusion

Signý’s documentation is simultaneously rich and narrow. Her saga narrative is extraordinarily detailed, but it rests essentially on a single late-thirteenth-century composition surviving in a manuscript copied around 1400. Earlier sources securely attest the heroic world around her—Sigemund, Fitela/Sinfjǫtli, Wæls/Völsung—but not her name or complete deeds. Nothing securely establishes her as a historical woman, object of cult, occupant of a tomb, or subject of medieval art.

Her later power comes from reinterpretation: medieval rímur, Romantic translation, Morris’s northern epic, Wagner’s composite Sieglinde, Tolkien’s deliberately new lay, and modern readings of gender, coercion, kinship, incest, reproductive strategy, and revenge. The documented medieval Signý is neither a goddess nor a passive tragic bride. She is the human strategist through whom the Völsung family survives its first annihilation—and through whose vengeance her husband, children, and finally she herself are destroyed.
