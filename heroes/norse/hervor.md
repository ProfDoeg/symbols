# Hervör: Research Dossier

> **Class:** Hervör is a **mortal legendary heroine** of Old Norse heroic literature, not a demigoddess or securely attested recipient of cult. The tradition contains **two different women named Hervör**: (1) Angantýr’s daughter, a Viking leader who summons her dead father and inherits Tyrfingr; and (2) her granddaughter, Heiðrekr’s daughter, a Gothic commander killed fighting the Huns.  
> **Earliest secure attestation:** the first Hervör and the grave-awakening episode are securely preserved in *Hervarar saga ok Heiðreks*, composed in Iceland during the thirteenth century and first extant in the early-fourteenth-century *Hauksbók* recension. Claims that *Hervararkviða* itself is tenth- or eleventh-century are linguistic and metrical reconstructions, not manuscript attestations. The older Old English poem *Widsith* preserves material connected with the saga’s Gothic-Hunnish cycle, but **does not name Hervör**.

## Evidentiary key

- **[SEA] Securely attested early material:** a statement actually present in a medieval witness or independently early source.
- **[LCE] Later canonical elaboration:** the thirteenth-century saga synthesis or a later recension of it.
- **[RV] Regional or textual variant:** an alternative confined to one recension, ballad, *ríma*, or regional retelling.
- **[LS] Learned systematization:** antiquarian genealogy, historical geography, or editorial reconstruction.
- **[MA] Medieval/early-modern accretion:** later manuscript, ballad, *ríma*, or antiquarian addition.
- **[F] Folklore:** orally circulating or ballad material whose surviving record is later.
- **[MSR] Modern scholarly reconstruction:** an inference about lost poems, historical background, gender, function, or dating.
- **[MI] Modern invention or adaptation:** a post-medieval work materially rewriting the tradition.
- **[NEG] Negative finding:** no relevant attestation was located in the stated corpus.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### The two Hervörs

**Hervör I, daughter of Angantýr.** [LCE] In the saga she is born after the death of her father, grows up at the court of her maternal grandfather Bjarmarr, prefers weapons to textile work, becomes an outlaw and then a Viking, adopts the masculine name **Hervarðr**, takes command of a ship, lands alone on Sámsøy, summons Angantýr from his barrow, and forces him to surrender the family sword Tyrfingr. She later abandons raiding, marries Hǫfundr, and becomes the mother of Angantýr and Heiðrekr. The saga does not narrate her death.

**Hervör II, daughter of Heiðrekr.** [LCE] The first Hervör’s granddaughter is explicitly described as a **skjaldmær**, “shield-maiden.” Fostered by Fróðmarr in the R recension, she commands a Gothic frontier fortress near Myrkviðr and dies with most of her force during the first Hunnish assault. She never possesses Tyrfingr in the medieval saga.

These women must not be conflated. Peter Nicolai Arbo’s frequently reproduced *Hervors død* depicts **Hervör II’s battlefield death**, not the barrow-visiting Hervör I.

### Forms of the name

- **Old Norse:** *Hervǫr*, normalized modern Icelandic *Hervör*; genitive *Hervarar*. The saga title means “Saga of Hervör and Heiðrekr.”
- **Masculine identity/name in the saga:** *Hervarðr*. [SEA] R, H, and U change the name when Hervör joins the Vikings. H and U also employ masculine singular pronouns, while the company containing Hervarðr is referred to by a masculine plural rather than the mixed-gender neuter. A recent grammatical study argues that this is stronger evidence than the conventional paraphrase “a woman disguised as a man” acknowledges. That is a modern interpretation, not an assertion that medieval Iceland possessed a modern trans identity category. [MSR] [Postmedieval study](https://doi.org/10.1057/s41280-026-00423-3)
- **Modern Scandinavian forms:** Icelandic *Hervör*; Swedish *Hervor*; Norwegian and Danish *Hervor*; Faroese usage occurs principally through the ballad tradition.
- **English forms:** *Hervor*, *Hervör*; older translations often anglicize the associated names as *Angantyr*, *Heithrek/Heidrek*, and *Tyrfing*.
- **German:** *Hervör* or *Hervor*; Russian *Хервёр*; Italian and Spanish usually retain *Hervör*.

### Etymology

The first element is securely Germanic **\*harja- / Old Norse *herr***, “host, army.” The second is disputed:

1. “army-defender,” connecting *-vǫr* with a feminine formation related to *verja*, “defend,” or Proto-Germanic *warjaz*, “defender”; or
2. “army-wary/careful,” connecting it with Old Norse *varr*, “wary, watchful.”

The Swedish national encyclopedia records both alternatives; neither should be presented as certain. [MSR] [NE name entry](https://www.ne.se/uppslagsverk/skola/avancerad/hervor)

The masculine *Hervarðr* is related in form and meaning but is not simply a scribal misspelling of *Hervǫr*: the narrative uses it at the point when the character assumes male equipment, social position, and Viking identity.

### Epithets and classifications

Neither Hervör has a stable Homeric-style epithet.

- **Hervör I:** modern “shield-maiden” is common but textually imprecise. The saga describes her weapon training, male attire, Viking service, and command, but does not itself call her *skjaldmær*. [SEA/MSR]
- **Hervör/Hervarðr:** the paired form is increasingly used in scholarship to represent the saga’s name and pronoun shift. [MSR]
- **Hervör II:** *skjaldmær*, “shield-maiden,” is textually warranted. [SEA]
- **“The Waking of Angantýr” / “Hervör’s Incantation”:** modern titles for the embedded poem conventionally called *Hervararkviða*. [LS]
- **Not Hervör alvitr:** *Hervör alvitr* in *Vǫlundarkviða* is a different figure, one of three supernatural swan-maidens. *Alvitr* is variously glossed “all-wise” or “otherworldly/strange being.” The shared personal name does not identify her with either saga Hervör. [NEG]

There is no medieval equation of Hervör with a goddess, valkyrie, saint, or historical queen.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### What is actually earliest?

The evidence has three different chronological levels:

1. **Older Germanic story-material:** [SEA for the cycle, not Hervör] *Widsith*, surviving in the late-tenth-century Exeter Book but generally believed to contain considerably older poetry, names figures and conflicts comparable to Heiðrekr, Angantýr, Hlöðr, Sifka, and the Gothic-Hunnish war. The proposed correspondences include Old English *Heaþoric*, *Incgenþeow*, *Hliþe*, and *Sifecan*. *Widsith* does not name Hervör or Tyrfingr. The relationship therefore supports the antiquity of part of the **dynastic war-cycle**, not the early existence of the barrow heroine. [Cambridge study](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/pmla/article/widsith-and-the-hervararsaga/C05021AC3C2F005A9D871B366D414932)

2. **The lost poetic strata:** [MSR] *Hervararkviða*, *Hlǫðskviða*, and portions of the Gestumblindi riddles are metrically and stylistically distinguishable from the saga prose. Scholars commonly regard them as older poems incorporated by a thirteenth-century compiler. Proposed dates range widely—often the tenth through twelfth centuries for *Hervararkviða*, and still earlier oral roots for *Hlǫðskviða*—but no manuscript of any poem predates the saga witnesses.

3. **Secure Hervör attestation:** [SEA] *Hervarar saga ok Heiðreks* in *Hauksbók*, AM 544 4to, copied in the early fourteenth century, conventionally c. 1302–10 or somewhat later within the compilation process. The saga itself is normally assigned to the thirteenth century. The manuscript breaks off during the Gestumblindi riddles.

### The main saga witnesses

The three principal recension families are H, R, and U:

- **H — *Hauksbók*, AM 544 4to:** early fourteenth century. It is the earliest surviving Hervör witness but a conflated recension. Later paper copies, especially AM 281 4to and AM 597b 4to, preserve portions now lost from H.
- **R — GKS 2845 4to:** Iceland, c. 1440–60; fols. 61v–73v. It is defective, with an internal lacuna and loss of the conclusion during the Gothic-Hunnish material, but is generally judged the most conservative surviving recension. [University of Copenhagen manuscript record](https://fasnl.net/manuscripts/245)
- **U — Uppsala University Library R 715 and related seventeenth-century paper witnesses:** a substantially rewritten form with a new opening, expanded genealogy, and material influenced by later *rímur* and saga transmission. It cannot be used indiscriminately to fill R or H as though all three were identical. [LCE/MA]

Jón Helgason’s 1924 *Heiðreks saga: Hervarar saga ok Heiðreks konungs* remains fundamental because it prints the recensions separately rather than hiding their differences. Gabriel Turville-Petre’s 1956 student edition, introduced by Christopher Tolkien, and Christopher Tolkien’s 1960 edition and translation are still important. The modern peer-reviewed verse edition is Hannah Burrows’s contribution to *Skaldic Poetry of the Scandinavian Middle Ages* VIII (2017). The project’s notes retain manuscript and stanza-level distinctions. [Skaldic Project](https://skaldic.org/db.php?id=13345&if=default&table=notes)

### Contents of *Hervarar saga*

The saga is a prosimetrum—a prose narrative incorporating verse—and joins several originally separable narrative units:

1. the forging and curse of Tyrfingr;
2. Arngrímr and his twelve berserker sons;
3. the duel on Sámsøy and Hjálmarr’s death-song;
4. Hervör’s upbringing and *Hervararkviða*;
5. the career of Heiðrekr;
6. Óðinn’s riddle contest with Heiðrekr, *Gátur Gestumblinda*;
7. Heiðrekr’s sons Angantýr and Hlöðr and the Gothic-Hunnish war, including *Hlǫðskviða*;
8. in later witnesses, a Swedish royal genealogy.

The result is not a biography of Hervör: she is the central figure only in one major movement, while Tyrfingr, inheritance, and the family line unify the whole.

### Corpus audit requested in the brief

- **Poetic Edda:** [NEG] Neither saga Hervör appears in the Codex Regius heroic collection. *Hervararkviða* is “Eddic” in metre and genre and has sometimes been printed in expanded modern Edda anthologies, but it is transmitted in *Hervarar saga*. *Hervör alvitr* in *Vǫlundarkviða* is unrelated.
- **Vǫlsunga saga:** [NEG] No saga Hervör. It provides a comparative cursed-treasure, sword, genealogy, and heroic-woman tradition, not a witness to her story.
- **Ragnars saga loðbrókar:** [NEG] No Hervör episode. Its relevance is generic and manuscript-cultural: it is another thirteenth-century *fornaldarsaga* joining older heroic verse and retrospective genealogy.
- **Gautreks saga:** [NEG] No Hervör. Relevant comparatively to legendary-heroic prosimetrum and the recycling of heroic personages.
- **Saxo Grammaticus, *Gesta Danorum*, c. 1200:** [NEG] Saxo preserves other warrior women and Scandinavian heroic legends but not the Hervör–Tyrfing episode.
- **Beowulf:** [NEG] No Hervör, Angantýr, or Tyrfingr. Connections are comparative—barrows, ancestral weapons, revenant spaces, Gothic/Geatic memory, and heroic ethics. Klaeber’s critical edition remains the standard point of textual reference.
- **Grettis saga:** [NEG] No Hervör. Grettir’s barrow encounter with Kárr and acquisition of a weapon provide a useful analogue but not evidence of borrowing in either direction.
- **Egils saga Skalla-Grímssonar:** [NEG] No Hervör; occasionally relevant to women, inheritance, weapons, and saga prosimetrum, but not a Hervör witness.
- **Nibelungenlied:** [NEG] No Hervör. It belongs to the broader Germanic heroic complex but does not transmit her.
- **Þiðreks saga:** [NEG] No Hervör episode. It preserves Scandinavian prose arrangements of continental heroic legend; analogies must not be turned into identity.
- **Icelandic family sagas generally:** [NEG] no independent Hervör tradition has been demonstrated.
- **Faroese and Danish ballads:** [F/MA] *Hjálmar og Angantýr*, *Arngríms synir*, *Gátu ríma*, Danish *Angelfyr og Helmer kamp*, and Swedish *Kung Speleman* preserve or rework portions of the larger cycle. They are valuable reception witnesses, not proof of a pre-saga Hervör cult.
- **Ormars rímur**, probably fifteenth century: [MA] adapts a related pattern in which Ormarr approaches his dead father’s mound to obtain a sword, apparently through a lost prose intermediary. It demonstrates medieval reuse of the barrow-inheritance complex.

A detailed bibliography of manuscripts, editions, translations, and scholarship is maintained by the University of Copenhagen’s *Stories for All Time* project. [Fornaldarsögur bibliography](https://fasnl.net/bibliography/17)

## 3. The story

### Tyrfingr before Hervör

[LCE] In the saga’s usual opening, King Svafrlami forces the dwarfs Dvalinn and Durinn to forge a sword. It will cut iron and stone, never rust, and ensure victory, but the dwarfs curse it: once drawn it must kill; it will perform three great evils; and it will kill Svafrlami himself. Arngrímr later obtains the sword—after Svafrlami’s death in most forms—and gives it to his son Angantýr.

The exact opening varies. U expands and rationalizes portions of the genealogy; R and H differ over names and sequence. The curse’s details belong to the saga synthesis and are not independently attested in Viking-Age evidence.

### Angantýr, Tófa, and Hervör’s birth

[LCE] Angantýr is one of Arngrímr’s twelve berserker sons. Before the brothers’ fatal expedition he fathers a child with **Tófa**, daughter of Earl Bjarmarr. The brothers challenge Hjálmarr and Ǫrvar-Oddr on Sámsøy. All twelve berserkers are killed; Hjálmarr is mortally wounded, while Oddr survives and arranges the burials. Angantýr is placed in a mound with Tyrfingr.

Tófa gives birth to a daughter and names her Hervör. The saga does not give Hervör a miraculous or divine conception. Her posthumous birth supplies the inheritance problem that drives her encounter with the dead.

### Childhood and outlawry

[LCE] Hervör is brought up by Bjarmarr. The prose characterizes her as strong, unruly, and more interested in bow, shield, and sword than sewing and embroidery. When restrained, she goes into the woods and robs or kills travellers.

A slave or thrall insults her by calling her the daughter of a slave or exposing her paternal ancestry—the wording varies—and tells her that Angantýr lies in a mound with treasure. She responds not with shame but by asserting her right to the paternal inheritance.

Modern readings divide here:

- Carol Clover’s influential “functional son” model treats Hervör as filling the male heir’s role until the lineage can be regularized through marriage. [MSR]
- Torfi Tulinius connects the saga’s inheritance conflicts with thirteenth-century Icelandic legal and ideological concerns. [MSR]
- Later gender studies emphasize magic, spatial liminality, the name *Hervarðr*, and the manuscripts’ masculine grammar, arguing that “cross-dressing woman” is an incomplete description. [MSR]
- None of these readings is itself the medieval story’s declared meaning.

### Hervarðr the Viking

[SEA/LCE] Hervör equips herself as a man, takes the name Hervarðr, joins Vikings, and eventually becomes their leader after the previous captain dies. The story therefore grants her actual command rather than merely a brief disguise.

The prose contains an important double movement: gendered identity changes with material practice and social space, but after the sword quest the saga later makes Hervör resume a conventionally aristocratic female life. Whether that is “restoration,” coercive closure, or one of several valid identities is a modern interpretive dispute.

### The waking of Angantýr

[SEA within the saga witness] Hervör sails to Sámsøy. Her companions refuse to accompany her onto the haunted island. At sunset she encounters a shepherd and asks for the mounds; terrified by fires around the graves, he flees. Hervör proceeds alone.

The prose yields to *Hervararkviða*, a dialogue in *fornyrðislag*. She calls upon Angantýr and all eleven of his brothers by name, threatens to curse or torment them if they withhold Tyrfingr, and demands the sword as her lawful paternal inheritance.

The mounds open and flames surround them. Angantýr initially warns her away:

- Tyrfingr will bring destruction upon her family;
- she is alone among the dead;
- the sword is dangerous to possess;
- in some readings or textual forms, he questions whether she is really his daughter.

Hervör refuses every warning and accuses the dead of cowardice or deception. Angantýr finally delivers Tyrfingr from the mound and prophesies that her descendants will destroy one another through it. Hervör leaves in triumph.

The poem ends with her reflecting that she seemed to move “between worlds” while fires burned around her. That phrase has become central to supernatural and gender-liminal readings, but it first describes the barrow encounter, not a modern psychological diagnosis.

### Return, marriage, and children

[LCE] Hervör rejoins her crew and raids successfully. She eventually returns to Bjarmarr, adopts aristocratic women’s work and dress, and marries **Hǫfundr**, son of King Guðmundr of Glæsisvellir. Their sons are **Angantýr** and **Heiðrekr**.

Tyrfingr passes to Heiðrekr. During a quarrel or violent episode Heiðrekr draws it and kills his brother Angantýr, fulfilling one stage of the curse. Hervör prevents Hǫfundr from imposing complete dispossession or permanent exile on Heiðrekr and helps secure his patrimony. The text thus does not make her disappear entirely when she ceases raiding: she remains an influential mother and dynastic actor.

No medieval witness narrates Hervör I’s death, grave, apotheosis, or posthumous cult.

### Heiðrekr and the riddles of Gestumblindi

Hervör does **not** participate in the riddle contest, but it belongs to the saga named for her and is indispensable to the textual history.

[LCE] Heiðrekr has decreed that an offender can save himself by posing riddles the king cannot answer. A man called Gestumblindi sacrifices to Óðinn for help. Óðinn assumes Gestumblindi’s appearance and challenges Heiðrekr with a sequence of riddles about the natural world, human crafts, animals, and myth.

The contest ends when Óðinn asks what he whispered into Baldr’s ear before Baldr was placed on the pyre—the same unanswerable divine-secret strategy seen in *Vafþrúðnismál*. Heiðrekr recognizes Óðinn and strikes at him with Tyrfingr. Óðinn escapes as a falcon, losing tail-feathers in some versions, and curses or predicts Heiðrekr’s death by slaves.

The riddles vary materially among H, R, and U. H, supplemented by early copies of its now-lost leaves, preserves the largest collection. Nineteenth-century editors sometimes printed Heiðrekr’s prose answers as verse, an editorial reconstruction not warranted by all manuscripts. [Skaldic and manuscript discussion](https://skaldic.org/db.php?id=13345&if=default&table=notes)

### Hervör II and the Gothic-Hunnish war

[LCE with older poetic material] Heiðrekr fathers several children by different women. His successors include the legitimate Gothic king **Angantýr**, the Hunnish-associated **Hlöðr**, and **Hervör II**. In R she is fostered in England by Fróðmarr; U gives expanded maternal and genealogical information.

After Heiðrekr’s murder, Hlöðr asks Angantýr for half the Gothic inheritance. Angantýr offers immense treasure and a substantial territorial settlement, but not equal kingship. The aged Gizurr Grytingaliði insults Hlöðr as the son of a bondwoman and a bastard. Hlöðr returns to his maternal grandfather Humli, and the Huns mobilize.

Hervör commands a frontier fort at **Myrkviðr**, with her foster-father or companion **Ormarr**. The Hunnish host overwhelms the defenders. Hervör and most of her force are killed; Ormarr escapes to inform Angantýr. The king laments that the Huns have treated his excellent sister “unbrotherly.”

The main battle follows. The Goths defeat the Huns, and Angantýr kills Hlöðr with Tyrfingr. Thus the war converts a disputed inheritance into fratricidal catastrophe.

### Historical background of the war

[MSR] *Hlǫðskviða* contains archaic-looking names and an east-European geography involving Goths, Huns, the Danube, Myrkviðr, and the “Gothic people.” Scholars have variously connected it with:

- fourth-century Gothic-Hunnish conflict;
- Ermanaric-era traditions;
- battles remembered in *Widsith*;
- Scandinavian reshaping of continental Gothic material;
- Carpathian, Vistula, Black Sea, or Ukrainian-steppe geography.

No consensus permits identification of either Hervör with a historical person. The poem telescopes migrations, geography, names, and dynastic motifs. Its historical value lies in the transmission and Scandinavian reimagining of Gothic memory, not as a literal battle report.

## 4. Relations with gods and other heroes

### Genealogies

#### Hervör I

```text
Arngrímr
└── Angantýr ── Tófa, daughter of Bjarmarr
    └── Hervör/Hervarðr ── Hǫfundr, son of Guðmundr
        ├── Angantýr (killed by Heiðrekr with Tyrfingr)
        └── Heiðrekr
            ├── Angantýr, Gothic king
            ├── Hlöðr, half-brother and Hunnish claimant
            └── Hervör II
```

#### Important uncertainty

The saga gives Heiðrekr several marriages and sexual relationships, and the recensions do not present his children’s maternity uniformly. U’s expanded opening and genealogies are later evidence, not automatic corrections to R or H. In the story of Hlöðr, Heiðrekr’s seizure and impregnation of the Hunnish woman Sifka is coercive and should be described plainly: the tradition makes sexual violence part of the disputed succession. [RV/LCE]

## 5. Cult, tomb, and ritual

### Cult

**[NEG] No cult of Hervör is attested.** There is:

- no dedicated shrine or *hof*;
- no inscription invoking her;
- no named priesthood;
- no sacrifice, festival, game, or oracle;
- no reliably identified relic or grave;
- no medieval testimony that she was worshipped, heroized, or invoked in magic;
- no archaeological object securely identifying her.

Her classification is therefore **literary mortal heroine**, not “heroized dead with cult.”

### The barrows on Sámsøy

The saga locates Angantýr and his brothers’ mounds on Sámsøy, conventionally identified with modern Samsø in Denmark. [LCE] This is a narrative geography, not an excavated identification. Samsø contains prehistoric monuments, but no mound has been archaeologically demonstrated to be “Angantýr’s tomb,” and none can substantiate Hervör’s visit.

The episode does preserve medieval ideas about dangerous burial mounds:

- the dead retain knowledge and property;
- grave-fire marks supernatural wealth or an open mound;
- the heir may demand goods from the dead;
- weapons transmit both lineage and catastrophe.

Those ideas are evidence for medieval literary and belief history, not for the literal raising of a corpse.

### Ritual comparison

Hervör’s summons is often compared with *grógaldr*, necromancy, *seiðr*, mound-breaking, and saga encounters with revenants. The text does not name her rite *seiðr*. Calling Hervör a “witch” or *vǫlva* is therefore interpretation, although the terrified shepherd treats her approach to the graves as uncanny. [MSR]

Heiðrekr’s court does contain a separate ritual: a boar dedicated to Freyr is led into the hall at Yule and men swear vows on its bristles. [RV] That passage concerns Heiðrekr, not a cult of Hervör.

## 6. Historical evolution of the figure

### Possible early heroic matter: before c. 1000

[MSR] The Gothic-Hunnish conflict and some personal names probably derive from an older Germanic heroic complex. *Widsith* is the earliest independent evidence for related figures. Hervör herself is absent, and no runic inscription or Migration-Age image names her.

### Poetic formation: approximately tenth–twelfth centuries

[MSR] A separate *Hervararkviða* may have circulated before the prose saga. Its compact dialogue, metre, refrain-like summonses, and concentration on the barrow support separate composition. Dating it precisely is impossible. “Tenth century” should be cited as a hypothesis, not a fact.

### Saga synthesis: thirteenth century

[SEA/LCE] An Icelandic compiler joins the Tyrfingr curse, Sámsøy duel, Hervör poem, Heiðrekr biography, riddles, Gothic-Hunnish lay, and royal genealogy. In this form Hervör’s acquisition of Tyrfingr becomes the hinge between the berserker generation and Heiðrekr’s royal line.

### Manuscript redaction: fourteenth–seventeenth centuries

- H produces a learned, conflated early-fourteenth-century form.
- R, copied c. 1440–60, retains what most editors regard as a more conservative version but is defective.
- U extensively rewrites the story and incorporates later matter.
- *Hervarar rímur*, ballads, and related narratives reshape individual episodes. [MA/F]
- Copying continued into the nineteenth century, evidence of antiquarian and literary interest rather than uninterrupted cult. Jeffrey Scott Love documents four redactional and derivative traditions. [Reception study](https://www.nordistik.uni-muenchen.de/forschung/publikationen/mns/band14/index.html)

### Swedish Gothicism: seventeenth century

Olaus Verelius published *Hervarar saga på gammal Götska* at Uppsala in 1672, with Old Norse text, Swedish interpretation, and Latin notes. [LS] This was among the first printed saga editions and helped recruit the saga into Swedish Gothicism—the claim that Sweden was heir and homeland of the ancient Goths.

Verelius did serious philological and runological work by the standards of his time, but the national framework encouraged the treatment of legendary royal genealogies as Swedish history. His edition is thus both a textual milestone and a political antiquarian appropriation. The surviving preparatory manuscript, National Library of Sweden Isl. papp. fol. 121, dates to 1671. [Manuscript catalogue](https://manuscripta.se/ms/101438)

### Romantic nationalism: eighteenth–nineteenth centuries

Translations by Thomas Percy and other British antiquaries circulated the barrow poem as “Runic poetry.” Scandinavian Romanticism turned Hervör into a visually dramatic Northern heroine. The tradition increasingly emphasized:

- fearless confrontation with death;
- “Nordic” female heroism;
- ancestral inheritance;
- Gothic national antiquity;
- the doomed magical sword.

These are selective modern emphases, not evidence of how a pre-Christian community regarded her.

### Twentieth and twenty-first centuries

Philological work shifted attention from a single reconstructed original to recension, manuscript context, gender, inheritance, and reception. Modern popular culture often labels Hervör a “Viking shield-maiden,” merges the two Hervörs, or presents Tyrfingr as her lifelong personal weapon. Those are simplifications or inventions.

## 7. Modern reception and reinvention

### Literature and translation

- **1703–05:** George Hickes printed and translated portions of *Hervararkviða* in his *Linguarum veterum septentrionalium thesaurus*. [LS]
- **1763:** Thomas Percy included saga poetry in *Five Pieces of Runic Poetry*, helping create the English “Runic sublime.” [MI/LS]
- **1862 and later editions:** Leconte de Lisle’s “L’Épée d’Angantyr” in *Poèmes barbares* adapts the grave scene into French Parnassian poetry. [MI]
- **1900:** Beatrice Barmby incorporated a close verse rendering of *Hervararkviða* into *Gísli Súrsson: A Drama*. [MI]
- **1912:** E. M. Smith-Dampier’s *The Norse King’s Bridal* rendered the poem in archaizing English. [MI]
- **1921:** Nora Kershaw published an English H-based translation in *Stories and Ballads of the Far Past*. [LS]
- **1960:** Christopher Tolkien published *The Saga of King Heidrek the Wise*, based mainly on R with clearly signalled supplements. [LS]
- **1988–2021:** translations by Régis Boyer, Marcello Meli, Lars Lönnroth, Rudolf Simek and collaborators, and Jackson Crawford reflect continued international reception.

### Visual art

- **Constantin Hansen, 1872, *Hervør henter sværdet Tyrfing i Angantyrs høj***: [MI] the canonical Romantic image of Hervör at the fiery mound.
- **Peter Nicolai Arbo, *Hervors død*:** [MI] depicts the second Hervör’s death in the Hunnish battle. Arbo’s work belongs to nineteenth-century Norwegian national Romantic history painting, not medieval iconography.
- Numerous nineteenth-century book illustrations depict Hjálmarr, Ǫrvar-Oddr, Angantýr, and the grave scene. No medieval image has been securely identified as Hervör.

### Opera

Wilhelm Stenhammar’s ** *Tirfing*, Op. 15**, with libretto by Anna Boberg, was composed in 1897–98 and premiered at the Royal Opera in Stockholm on 9 December 1898, conducted by Stenhammar. [MI] It radically rewrites the saga: Hervör/Hervardur must conceal her sex, Gullväg desires her while believing her male, and Hervör kills the invented beloved Vidar. These are operatic inventions shaped by Wagnerian music drama and fin-de-siècle gender conflict, not medieval variants. [Swedish Musical Heritage](https://www.swedishmusicalheritage.com/composers/stenhammar-wilhelm/SMH-W358-Tirfing_after_Hervardursagan_Norse_mythological_music_drama_in_two_act)

### Tolkien, Mirkwood, and Rohan

Several distinctions are necessary.

1. **Documented knowledge:** J. R. R. Tolkien knew *Hervarar saga* and the wider Gothic heroic tradition. Christopher Tolkien later edited and translated the saga and published “The Battle of the Goths and the Huns” in 1955–56. [SEA for scholarly engagement]

2. **Mirkwood:** Old Norse *Myrkviðr*, “dark wood,” occurs in *Hlǫðskviða* and other Eddic poems. “Mirkwood” was already used in English by Sir Walter Scott and William Morris before Tolkien. Tolkien’s Mirkwood therefore belongs to a documented Germanic literary word-tradition, but it is not demonstrably borrowed from Hervör alone. [MSR]

3. **Riddle contest:** Bilbo and Gollum’s contest belongs to the widespread riddle-game tradition. *Gátur Gestumblinda* is a relevant Norse analogue known to Tolkien, but claiming it as the sole direct source exceeds the evidence.

4. **Rohan:** The Rohirrim are textually and linguistically constructed primarily through Old English, especially *Beowulf*, Mercian language, hall culture, mounted war, elegy, and Tolkien’s historical imagination. Scholars note resonances with *Hlǫðskviða*: Gothic horsemen, a frontier at Mirkwood, doomed resistance, and a warrior woman. A direct equation “Hervör became Éowyn” is unproved. Éowyn’s battlefield role has multiple literary antecedents and a different narrative outcome. [MSR]

5. **Barrows and swords:** Tolkien’s barrow-wights and recovered blades resonate with Old Norse barrow stories, including Hervör and Grettir, but no exclusive one-to-one derivation is demonstrable.

Tolkien’s 1936 lecture “Beowulf: The Monsters and the Critics” changed modern evaluation of Germanic heroic poetry by insisting that monsters and legendary structure were central artistic material, not debris obscuring history. That approach indirectly legitimized reading *Hervarar saga* as literature rather than mining it only for Gothic facts.

### Games, comics, and commercial culture

“Tyrfing,” “Hervor,” and related names recur in fantasy games, manga, anime, card games, and weapon catalogues. [MI] These adaptations commonly:

- make Tyrfingr an unrestricted magical weapon;
- combine Hervör I’s sword quest with Hervör II’s battlefield death;
- convert her into a valkyrie;
- remove the marriage and inheritance framework;
- sexualize “shield-maiden” imagery;
- connect Tyrfingr to unrelated Sigurðr/Nibelung characters.

Unless an adaptation cites the saga closely, these are modern inventions rather than variants of medieval tradition.

### Political appropriation

- **Swedish Gothicism:** securely documented through Verelius and the seventeenth-century antiquarian environment. [LS]
- **Nineteenth-century Nordic nationalism:** Hervör and the saga’s Goths contributed to a generalized heroic Northern past in art, scholarship, and music. [MSR]
- **Modern extremist appropriation:** Norse heroic imagery and runes have unquestionably been appropriated by racist and neo-Nazi movements, but the dedicated searches conducted for this dossier found **no well-documented, distinctive extremist cult or political program centered specifically on Hervör**. [NEG] It would be irresponsible to infer one merely because Tyrfingr and shield-maidens circulate in neo-pagan and metal subcultures.

## 8. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Is Hervör historical?

No source identifies either Hervör with a verifiable historical woman. [NEG] Historical reconstruction has concentrated instead on the Gothic-Hunnish poem’s names and geography.

Older scholarship frequently tried to map every king and battlefield onto fourth- or fifth-century history. Proposed locations range from the Danube and Carpathians to the Vistula and Black Sea. Such work can identify ancient-looking place-name traditions but repeatedly mistakes poetic geography for a map. The strongest conclusion is that the cycle preserves transformed memory of continental Germanic conflict.

### How old is *Hervararkviða*?

Arguments for an early date include metre, diction, compact dramatic form, and its detachable structure. Counterweights are:

- all witnesses are late medieval;
- oral-style verse can be composed late;
- scribes and compilers altered stanzas;
- no independent quotation securely dates the poem;
- “archaic” language can be preserved or imitated.

Accordingly, “probably older than the prose frame” is safer than a precise century.

### One saga or a compilation?

The work’s shifts of setting, generation, style, and verse strongly indicate compilation. Yet the compiler imposed real unity through Tyrfingr, inheritance, kin-killing, and repeated failures of succession. Treating the work as random fragments misses this thirteenth-century design; treating it as a single ancient epic erases its strata.

### H, R, and U

Christopher Tolkien and Jón Helgason established the importance of keeping the recensions distinct. Alaric Hall subsequently argued that stylistic change between redactions also creates different historiographical meanings: recension is interpretation, not mere corruption. [Hall bibliography and access](https://arlima.net/eh/hervarar_saga_ok_heidreks.html)

R is normally closest to the lost archetype, but “closest” does not mean complete or always best. H sometimes preserves material lost in R; U occasionally transmits old readings amid extensive modernization. An eclectic modern narrative must label every supplementation.

### Gender

Major modern approaches include:

- **maiden-king or warrior-woman:** compares Hervör with women who reject marriage and masculine authority;
- **functional son:** emphasizes patrilineal inheritance;
- **ritual/magical liminality:** reads weapons, barrows, seiðr-like action, and the movement between worlds;
- **queer and transmasculine readings:** emphasize *Hervarðr*, male pronouns, masculine collective grammar, and the material production of identity.

The text does not actually call Hervör I a *meykongr* or *skjaldmær*. Conversely, reducing the masculine name and grammar to meaningless disguise also suppresses textual evidence. The interpretive dispute should remain open.

### Oral-formulaic and prosimetric questions

The verse may authenticate prose events by presenting itself as inherited speech. Modern work on *fornaldarsögur* argues that embedded poetry can imitate the evidentiary function of skaldic verse in kings’ sagas while claiming the anonymous authority of Eddic tradition. That does not prove every stanza is ancient; it shows how medieval compilers made narratives sound inherited.

### The riddles

The Gestumblindi collection is the only extensive medieval Old Norse riddle sequence of its kind. Its parallels include *Vafþrúðnismál*, Anglo-Saxon riddles, wisdom contests, and international riddle types. Debate concerns:

- which riddles belonged to the archetype;
- whether answers were prose or verse;
- whether Óðinn’s final unanswerable question was original;
- how scribes expanded or reordered the collection.

### Textual losses and discoveries

- H is physically defective, but seventeenth-century copies preserve portions made before further loss.
- R has an internal lacuna and loses the end of the saga.
- U survives only in early-modern paper witnesses.
- The recovery and comparison of derivative *rímur*, ballads, copies, and summaries have shown that material sometimes travelled outside the three familiar recensions.
- Love’s 2013 reception study replaced simple stemmatic narratives with a broader history of active rewriting. [University of Copenhagen record](https://researchprofiles.ku.dk/en/publications/the-reception-of-hervarar-saga-ok-hei%C3%B0reks-from-the-middle-ages-t/)

### Discredited or unsupported claims still circulating

- **“Hervör was a historical Viking woman.”** Unsupported; the saga is centuries later and set before the Viking Age in parts.
- **“Her grave has been found on Samsø.”** Unsupported.
- **“She was worshipped as a goddess or valkyrie.”** Unsupported and partly caused by confusion with Hervör alvitr.
- **“The Poetic Edda contains her complete story.”** False in manuscript terms; the poem is transmitted in the saga.
- **“Both Hervörs are one woman.”** False.
- **“Hervör II inherited Tyrfingr and died wielding it.”** Not the medieval saga’s account.
- **“The saga is a literal chronicle of the fourth century.”** Methodologically obsolete.
- **“Tolkien simply copied Hervör as Éowyn.”** An attractive but unproved one-to-one claim.
- **“Hervör I is explicitly called a shield-maiden.”** Not in the principal text; the term belongs explicitly to Hervör II.
