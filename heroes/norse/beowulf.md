# Beowulf: Research Dossier

> **Traditional class:** Mortal monster-slaying hero, aristocratic retainer, and—after fifty years of rule—epic king of the Geats. The poem never calls him a god, demigod, saint, or recipient of cult.  
> **Earliest secure attestation:** The unique Old English text in the Nowell Codex, British Library, Cotton MS Vitellius A XV, copied by two scribes around the year 1000, conventionally within approximately 975–1025. Proposed seventh-, eighth-, ninth-, tenth-, or early-eleventh-century dates for the poem’s composition remain scholarly reconstructions, not separately attested texts.  
> **Central evidentiary fact:** Beowulf the Geat is directly attested in only this poem. Scandinavian stories of Bǫðvarr bjarki, Icelandic monster tales about Grettir, archaeological halls at Lejre, and historical notices concerning Hygelac illuminate the poem’s legendary environment but do not independently attest Beowulf himself.

## Evidentiary labels

- **[E1: securely attested early material]** The extant medieval manuscript, its readings, or an independently dated early source.
- **[E2: later medieval parallel or canonical elaboration]** Medieval material later than the Beowulf manuscript, including Scandinavian analogues.
- **[E3: regional variant or cognate tradition]** A story resembling Beowulf but not naming him or demonstrably descending from his poem.
- **[E4: learned systematization or syncretistic identification]** Medieval or modern equation of separate figures.
- **[E5: folklore]** A tale-type or orally derived motif proposed from comparative evidence.
- **[E6: modern scholarly reconstruction]** A hypothesis about composition, history, archaeology, religion, or common ancestry.
- **[E7: modern reception or invention]** Translation, adaptation, political appropriation, or new narrative presented after the medieval rediscovery.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### Orientation

**[E1]** In the poem conventionally called *Beowulf*, the hero is a **Geat**—Old English *Gēat*, plural *Gēatas*—the son of Ecgþeow and an unnamed daughter of the Geatish king Hrēðel. He serves his maternal uncle Hygelac, sails to Denmark with chosen retainers, kills Grendel and Grendel’s unnamed mother, returns to the Geats, eventually becomes king, rules for fifty years, and dies after killing a treasure-guarding dragon with Wiglaf’s help. The poem ends with his cremation, barrow, and public lament, not with apotheosis or cult. The entire direct record is the 3,182-line Old English poem preserved in Cotton MS Vitellius A XV; an online diplomatic and critical presentation is supplied by the [Electronic Beowulf](https://ebeowulf.uky.edu/criticaledition.html).

### Name and manuscript spellings

- **Bēowulf / Beowulf. [E1]** Normalized Old English form, conventionally marked with a long first vowel. The first manuscript scribe generally writes *Beowulf*; the second, who takes over during line 1939, uses forms including *Biowulf*. These are scribal spellings, not evidence for two heroes.
- **Biuuulf. [E1, disputed identification]** A seventh-century personal name found in the Durham *Liber Vitae* has been invoked by Leonard Neidorf as evidence that a cognate personal name circulated before the poem’s extant manuscript. Even if the identification and dating are accepted, the entry supplies only a name: it does not identify its bearer as the epic hero or recount any deed.
- **“Bee-wolf,” hence “bear.” [E6]** The traditional analysis divides Old English *bēo*, “bee,” and *wulf*, “wolf,” interpreting the compound as a poetic circumlocution for the animal that raids bees or hives. It is famous and semantically attractive, particularly beside proposed bear traditions, but no independent Old English common noun *bēowulf* meaning “bear” is attested. It therefore should not be reported as a settled lexical fact.
- **“Wolf of Bēow.” [E6]** Andy Orchard and, with different onomastic arguments, Leonard Neidorf and Chenyun Zhu have proposed that the first element is the name **Bēow**, associated with grain and compared with Old Norse Byggvir, making the name structurally comparable to theophoric names such as *Þórólfr*, “Thor-wolf.” The argument stresses that “bee” is otherwise implausible as the first element in a Germanic male dithematic name. Critics note that Bēow/Byggvir is obscure and that no narrative relationship between the hero and a grain divinity is stated in the poem. The dispute is surveyed in the [Neidorf–Zhu study](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/355538360_The_Germanic_Onomasticon_and_the_Etymology_of_Beowulf%27s_Name) and subsequent discussion of [minor Germanic deities and the name](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/0895769X.2022.2103636).
- **Bēow-wulf versus Bēo-wulf. [E6]** These analyses are mutually exclusive reconstructions; manuscript spelling alone does not decide between them.
- **Bǫðvarr bjarki, “battle-Bǫðvarr/little bear.” [E4]** This is the Scandinavian champion of Hrólfr kraki, not a medieval spelling or translation of Beowulf. His identification as Beowulf’s cognate, transformed descendant, or common-source relative is modern comparative scholarship. The later sources actually call him Biarco, Bodvarus, or Bǫðvarr bjarki.

### Titles, roles, and descriptive formulas

The poem does not give Beowulf a stable divine epithet system. Its descriptions are social and heroic:

- **Hygelāces þegn**, “Hygelac’s thane/retainer.” **[E1]**
- **Gēata cempa**, “champion of the Geats.” **[E1]**
- **Weder-Gēata leod / Wederas’ king or prince. [E1]** “Weders” is a poetic ethnonym for the Geats.
- **Ecgþēowes bearn**, “Ecgtheow’s son.” **[E1]**
- **mǣg Hygelāces**, “kinsman of Hygelac.” **[E1]**
- **hringa fengel**, “lord of rings,” and comparable royal formulas after his accession. **[E1]**
- In the closing judgment he is praised as the mildest or gentlest of men, kindest to his people, and most eager for fame, depending on the translation adopted for lines 3180–82. **[E1]**

### Distinguishing the two Beowulfs

**[E1]** The poem’s prologue names **Bēowulf/Bēow**, son of Scyld Scefing and ancestor of Hrothgar. That Danish dynastic figure is not the Geatish hero. Editors sometimes print the ancestor’s name as *Bēow* because the genealogy otherwise conflicts with related West Saxon pedigrees, but the manuscript problem remains debated. Humfrey Wanley already distinguished the two figures; the history of his reading is documented by the [Electronic Beowulf transcript archive](https://ebeowulf.uky.edu/transcripts_collations.html).

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### The unique manuscript

**[E1]** Cotton MS Vitellius A XV is a composite volume. Its latter component, the Nowell Codex, contains *The Life of Saint Christopher*, *The Wonders of the East*, *Alexander’s Letter to Aristotle*, *Beowulf*, and *Judith*. The grouping has encouraged the defensible observation that monsters, distant wonders, threatened bodies, and extraordinary adversaries link several contents; it does not prove that a single planner composed them.

The manuscript’s principal facts are:

- Two contemporary scribes copied *Beowulf*. Scribe A wrote through line 1939; Scribe B took over in the middle of that line and also copied *Judith*. **[E1]**
- Neil Ker’s influential palaeographical range was approximately 975–1025; narrower or slightly later ranges have been argued, but the manuscript is securely a product of around 1000. **[E1/E6]**
- The first scribe’s section and the second’s work differ in script, spelling, and some linguistic habits. This proves a change of copyist, not a change of author. The British Library catalogue confirms the two hands in its description of [Cotton MS Vitellius A XV, ff. 94r–209v](https://searcharchives.bl.uk/catalog/041-001102975).
- The poem has no medieval title and no named author. “Beowulf” is a modern conventional title taken from its protagonist. **[E1]**
- The language is predominantly Late West Saxon in its copied form but contains forms and metrical features interpreted as residues of earlier dialect or transmission. Whether those features date the poem itself is disputed. **[E1/E6]**

### Damage, transcripts, and recovery

- **1563. [E1]** Laurence Nowell wrote his name in the codex, establishing its early-modern possession.
- **Late seventeenth century–1705. [E1]** Humfrey Wanley examined and transcribed parts of it, describing it as a “most noble text written in poetry.” His copies preserve letters subsequently lost.
- **1731. [E1]** Fire at Ashburnham House damaged the Cotton collection. *Beowulf* survived, but heat and later deterioration caused its margins to crumble.
- **1787 and approximately 1789–90. [E1]** At Grímur Jónsson Thorkelin’s initiative, an amanuensis—probably James Matthews—and then Thorkelin made the transcripts now called Thorkelin A and B. Together they preserve roughly two thousand letters subsequently lost or obscured. Their exact copying dates and their individual reliability have been reconsidered, but their documentary value is unquestioned; see the study of the [Thorkelin transcripts](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/anglo-saxon-england/article/abs/what-use-are-the-thorkelin-transcripts-of-beowulf/303F53EDFEEE2EB06F5091E862B06DAE).
- **1815. [E7]** Thorkelin published the *editio princeps*, with a Latin translation, as a work about ancient Danish deeds.
- **1820. [E7]** N. F. S. Grundtvig published *Bjowulfs Drape*, the first complete translation into a modern language, Danish.
- **1833/1835 and 1837. [E7]** John Mitchell Kemble issued improved Old English editions, followed by the first complete modern-English translation in 1837.
- **1845. [E1]** The manuscript was remounted in paper frames. This preserved fragile leaves but concealed parts of their edges.
- **1882. [E7]** Julius Zupitza’s facsimile gave scholars systematic photographic access.
- **1922; revised through 1950. [E7]** Friedrich Klaeber’s *Beowulf and the Fight at Finnsburg* became the standard scholarly edition.
- **2008; 2014. [E7]** R. D. Fulk, Robert E. Bjork, and John D. Niles issued “Klaeber 4,” followed by a revised impression. It remains the principal critical edition; its bibliographical identity is recorded by [Beowulf’s Afterlives](https://beowulf.dh.tamu.edu/detail?id=260).
- **Electronic era. [E7]** Kevin Kiernan’s *Electronic Beowulf*, now in its fifth major edition, supplies multispectral images, transcripts, and layered editorial readings.

### Composition date: what is secure and what is disputed

The **terminus ante quem** is the copying of the manuscript around 1000. No securely dated fragment of the poem predates it.

1. **Early-date position, especially late seventh or eighth century. [E6]**  
   Proponents cite archaic metre, phonology, name forms, linguistic changes apparently misunderstood by the extant scribes, knowledge of early Scandinavian traditions, and historical conditions in which an English audience might readily understand continental dynasties. R. D. Fulk has argued for approximately 725; Leonard Neidorf for the late seventh or early eighth century. These features may show old language or inherited formulas rather than the date at which the surviving poem became a unified composition.

2. **Broad agnostic position. [E6]**  
   After the 1980 Toronto conference and the 1981 volume *The Dating of Beowulf*, many scholars emphasized that linguistic, historical, and cultural clues rarely yield a unique date. A defensible documentary formulation is: composed after the Anglo-Saxon conversion began and no later than the extant manuscript, with earlier oral and textual material possible but unrecoverable in exact form.

3. **Late-date position, late tenth or early eleventh century. [E6]**  
   Kevin Kiernan argued that the extant codex stands close to composition and associated it with the political world culminating in Cnut’s reign. His case combines codicology, corrections, textual development, and the poem’s Anglo-Scandinavian setting. Critics accept much of his manuscript work without accepting that the scribes participated in composition.

4. **Unity versus accretion. [E6]**  
   Nineteenth-century “dissection” theories divided the poem into lays or interpolated strata. Twentieth-century literary criticism increasingly treated it as a designed whole. A 2019 computational study claimed stylistic support for single authorship; a 2021 response argued that the method was confounded by topic and could not establish unity. The criticism appears in [Nature Human Behaviour](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41562-021-01222-5). Neither result identifies an author or dates the poem.

### Primary corpus: direct and comparative

#### Direct witness

- ***Beowulf*. [E1]** Old English alliterative poem, 3,182 conventional lines; composition disputed from roughly the later seventh to early eleventh century; unique manuscript around 1000. It is the sole text that names Beowulf the Geat and recounts his career.

#### Independent or cognate historical background

- **Gregory of Tours, *Historia Francorum* III.3. [E1]** Written in the later sixth century. It reports that the Danish king Chlochilaichus led a sea raid into Frankish territory and was killed. Grundtvig’s identification of Chlochilaichus with the poem’s Hygelac is widely accepted because the names, royal status, seaborne raid, location, and death correspond, although Gregory calls him Danish rather than Geatish.
- ***Liber Historiae Francorum*. [E1]** Early eighth century; retells the raid and in some witnesses identifies the ruler as king of the Goths.
- ***Liber Monstrorum*. [E1]** Usually dated to the eighth century; names King Huiglaucus of the Getae and describes his prodigious bones displayed on a Rhine island. This is legendary elaboration around Hygelac, not evidence for Beowulf.
- ***Widsith*. [E1, relative dating disputed]** Old English heroic catalogue preserved in the tenth-century Exeter Book. It names several peoples and figures found in *Beowulf*, but not Beowulf the hero.
- **Scandinavian Scylding traditions. [E2/E3]** Saxo, the lost *Skjöldunga saga* as known through later epitomes, *Hrólfs saga kraka*, and *Bjarkarímur* preserve Hróarr/Hrothgar, Helgi/Halga, Hrólfr/Hrothulf, Aðils/Eadgils, and related traditions in altered genealogies. They do not name the Geatish hero Beowulf.

#### Analogues, not additional Beowulf texts

- **Saxo Grammaticus, *Gesta Danorum*, Books II–III. [E2/E3]** Completed in the early thirteenth century. Biarco comes from abroad to King Rolvo, kills Agnerus and a giant bear, helps Hialto, and dies loyally with Rolvo. Similarities to Beowulf coexist with fundamental differences.
- **Lost *Skjöldunga saga*. [E2/E3]** Probably around 1200, surviving through later Latin summaries; includes Bodvarus in the Scylding world.
- ***Hrólfs saga kraka*. [E2/E3]** Present form commonly placed in the late fourteenth or early fifteenth century; surviving manuscripts are later. Bǫðvarr bjarki and Hǫttr/Hjalti confront a monstrous visitor at Hrólfr’s hall; Bǫðvarr also possesses a bear-associated double or battle-spirit.
- ***Bjarkarímur*. [E2/E3]** Approximately 1400; develops Bǫðvarr’s story with a she-wolf and bear.
- ***Grettis saga Ásmundarsonar*. [E2/E3]** Fourteenth-century Icelandic saga. The Sandhaugar episode has Grettir defend a hall against a male troll and pursue a troll-woman to a waterfall cave. The saga’s present form is centuries later than *Beowulf*. Shared tradition, folktale convergence, and literary borrowing have all been proposed; direct dependence remains unproved. A source-language instructional extract dates the received saga to the [fourteenth century](https://lrc.la.utexas.edu/eieol/norol/30).
- **Poetic Edda, *Vǫlsunga saga*, *Hervarar saga*, *Ragnars saga loðbrókar*, *Gautreks saga*, *Þiðreks saga*, *Nibelungenlied*, *Egils saga*, and other family sagas. [Finding]** These are essential corpora for Germanic heroic tradition but do not attest Beowulf. Comparisons involving Sigurðr/Siegfried, Starkaðr, dragons, cursed treasure, feuds, or hall culture are typological unless a particular dependence is demonstrated.
- **Skaldic corpus. [Finding]** No securely attributed medieval skaldic stanza names the Geatish Beowulf. Bjarkamál traditions concern Bjarki and Hrólfr, not Beowulf under an attested alternate name.

## 3. The story

All line references in this section are to the conventional numbering of the poem.

### Birth, ancestry, and obscure youth

**[E1: *Beowulf*, chiefly lines 371–375, 459–472, 2425–2537]** Beowulf is the son of **Ecgþeow**, a Wægmunding, and the unnamed daughter of King **Hrēðel** of the Geats. Ecgtheow killed Heatholaf of the Wulfings and could not settle the resulting feud because his own people feared war. Hrothgar, newly ruling the Danes, paid compensation and obtained oaths. This earlier service creates the reciprocity behind Hrothgar’s welcome of Beowulf.

Beowulf is fostered in Hrethel’s household. Later reminiscence says that the Geats once considered him slack or unpromising and that he received little honour in youth. The poem does not describe his birth, mother’s name, childhood adventures, marriage, or children.

### The Breca episode

**[E1: lines approximately 499–606]** At Heorot, Unferth accuses Beowulf of foolishly losing a seven-night swimming contest to **Breca**. Beowulf answers that the two swam together by youthful agreement, armed against sea beasts; storms separated them, he killed nine monsters, and currents carried him to the land of the Finns. He then attacks Unferth for killing his own brothers.

The poem does not provide an independent narrator’s verdict on every detail. “Swimming match,” prolonged armed sea-voyage, and boastful contest are translation-dependent descriptions. The scene functions as a formal challenge to Beowulf’s reputation and as his self-authentication before the Danish court.

### Voyage to Denmark and arrival at Heorot

**[E1: lines approximately 194–490]** Learning that Grendel has attacked Heorot for twelve years, Beowulf chooses fourteen companions according to the common reading—fifteen men including himself—and sails to Denmark. The coastguard questions them; Wulfgar carries their request to Hrothgar. Beowulf publicly offers to fight without sword or shield because Grendel uses no weapon.

The poem calls Heorot a splendid royal hall built by Hrothgar. It does not locate it with archaeological coordinates. Identifying it with the great halls excavated at Lejre is a modern reconstruction.

### Grendel

**[E1: lines approximately 702–836]** Grendel enters Heorot, kills and devours Beowulf’s retainer Hondscio, and reaches for Beowulf. The hero seizes him. Their struggle wrecks the hall; the other Geats’ swords cannot bite because Grendel is protected against weapons. Beowulf tears off his arm and shoulder. Grendel escapes to die in his mere, and the arm is displayed under Heorot’s roof.

The poem’s narrator places Grendel among the exiled progeny of **Cain** and repeatedly identifies him as an enemy of God. This is not an interaction between Beowulf and Cain; it is Christian genealogy assigned to the monster.

### Celebration, Sigemund, and warnings

**[E1: lines approximately 837–1250]** A Danish poet immediately compares Beowulf’s deed with Sigemund’s dragon-slaying and contrasts it with the disastrous rule of Heremod. Hrothgar symbolically adopts Beowulf “in heart,” gives treasure, and compensates Hondscio’s family. Wealhtheow presents gifts and asks Beowulf to be kind to her sons.

The Finnsburg lay sung during the celebration concerns Hnæf, Hildeburh, Finn, Hengest, and a destructive feud. Beowulf does not participate in that embedded story.

### Grendel’s mother

**[E1: lines approximately 1251–1784]** Grendel’s unnamed mother attacks Heorot in vengeance, killing Hrothgar’s counsellor Æschere and recovering Grendel’s arm. Beowulf follows tracks to the mere. Unferth lends him **Hrunting**. The female monster seizes him and carries him to her underwater or subaqueous hall. Hrunting fails; Beowulf discards it. She throws him down and attempts to kill him with a knife, but his mail protects him. He sees a gigantic sword, uses it to behead her, then beheads Grendel’s corpse. The blade melts from the monsters’ blood, leaving the hilt.

Calling the mother a “witch,” “hag,” or seductive demon is interpretive. The poem describes her with terms including *ides*, a word that can mean “woman” or “lady,” as well as hostile compounds. It gives her neither personal name nor sexual relationship with Beowulf. Later films that make her Beowulf’s lover are modern inventions.

### Hrothgar’s sermon and the return home

**[E1: lines approximately 1700–2199]** Hrothgar warns Beowulf against pride, using Heremod as negative exemplum, and reminds him that strength and prosperity end. Beowulf returns Hrunting to Unferth without insult. Hrothgar and Beowulf part emotionally.

Back in Geatland, Beowulf reports to Hygelac and Hygd. He recounts Hrothgar’s planned marriage of Freawaru to Ingeld and predicts that the peace will fail when an old Heathobard incites vengeance over ancestral weapons. He gives Hygelac, Hygd, and their heirs his Danish treasures and receives land, a hall, and a princely seat.

### Service under Hygelac and the Frisian disaster

**[E1: scattered retrospective passages, especially lines 1202–1214, 2354–2379, 2490–2509]** Beowulf serves Hygelac during a disastrous continental raid. Hygelac is killed. Beowulf says that he crushed the Frankish champion **Dæghrefn** to death in his arms and escaped by swimming with captured armour.

**[E1 plus independent historical background]** Gregory of Tours’ Chlochilaichus was killed during a Frankish coastal raid in the early sixth century. This strongly anchors the poem’s Hygelac material in remembered history but does not independently authenticate Beowulf’s participation.

### Succession and the Swedish-Geatish wars

**[E1: lines approximately 2200–2396 and 2425–2537]**

- Hygd offers Beowulf the throne because she doubts young Heardred’s capacity. Beowulf declines and supports Heardred.
- The Swedish princes Eadgils and Eanmund, sons of Ohthere, flee from their uncle Onela and receive shelter from Heardred.
- Onela invades. His champion Weohstan kills Eanmund; Heardred is killed.
- Onela allows Beowulf to become king. Later Beowulf supports Eadgils with warriors and weapons; the poem says Eadgils repays Onela in battle, conventionally understood as killing him.

Several of these Scandinavian rulers recur under cognate names in later Norse sources—Aðils, Óttarr, Áli—but the sources reorganize genealogy, motivation, and participants. They confirm a circulating legendary complex, not every detail of Beowulf’s account.

### Fifty-year reign and the dragon

**[E1: lines approximately 2200–2820]** After fifty years of Beowulf’s rule, an unnamed fugitive or slave steals a cup from a dragon’s barrow-hoard. The dragon burns settlements, including Beowulf’s hall. Beowulf orders an iron shield and resolves to fight.

The treasure originated with the last survivor of a vanished people, who deposited it in the earth. The poem provides no named original owner and no curse spoken by a supernatural being. Modern claims that Beowulf’s dragon is literally Fáfnir or that its treasure is the Nibelung hoard are syncretistic inventions.

At the barrow Beowulf enters with eleven chosen companions and the guide. His sword **Nægling** fails. The retainers flee except **Wiglaf**, son of Weohstan. Wiglaf rebukes them, enters the fight, and is burned when his wooden shield fails. Beowulf wounds the dragon; Nægling breaks; the dragon bites his neck. Wiglaf strikes its underside, and Beowulf uses a knife to cut it open. The dragon dies, but its venom kills Beowulf.

### Death, treasure, and funeral

**[E1: lines approximately 2790–3182]** Beowulf asks Wiglaf to bring out the treasure so that he may see what he has won for his people. He gives Wiglaf his gold collar, helmet, mail-coat, and rings and acknowledges that he has no son. He asks for a barrow on the headland at **Hronesnæs**, “Whale’s Headland,” to be called *Bēowulfes biorh*, Beowulf’s barrow.

Wiglaf reveals the death and denounces the deserters. A messenger predicts attacks by Franks, Frisians, and Swedes, explaining that the king’s death removes the Geats’ protector. The dragon is pushed from the cliff into the sea. The Geats:

1. build a pyre hung with armour;
2. cremate Beowulf;
3. include the hoard’s remains in a large barrow built over ten days;
4. place the barrow on a headland visible to sailors;
5. have twelve nobles ride around it, reciting a lament and praising him.

The closing funeral is accessible in a line-numbered translation of [lines 3137–3182](https://www.poetryintranslation.com/PITBR/English/BeowulfPart9.php).

**Absence of evidence:** The poem does not say that Beowulf rises from the dead, becomes a god, receives sacrifice, answers prayers, or continues as a barrow-dwelling spirit. It does not name his wife, because no wife is attested, and it explicitly leaves him without a son.

## 4. Relations with gods and other heroes

### Genealogy

```text
Wægmunding line                         Geatish royal line

Wægmund? (eponymous ancestor)           Hrēðel, king of the Geats
          |                              ├── Herebeald
       Ecgþeow ───────── unnamed daughter├── Hæþcyn
                    |                    ├── Hygelac ── Hygd
                 Beowulf                 |             └── Heardred
                                         └── unnamed daughter ── Ecgþeow
```

The poem does not expressly make Wægmund an individual ancestor; “Wægmunding” is a dynastic designation. Wiglaf, Weohstan, and Ecgtheow belong to that group, making Wiglaf and Beowulf kinsmen, although their precise genealogical link is unstated.

### Consorts and descendants

- **Wife:** none named or securely implied. **[Finding]**
- **Lover:** none attested. **[Finding]**
- **Children:** none; at death Beowulf expressly says he has no son to receive his armour. **[E1]**
- **Hygd as wife:** the conjecture that kingship would have included marriage to Hygd is a modern inference, not a statement in the poem.
- **Grendel’s mother as sexual partner:** invented by modern adaptations, most prominently the 2007 film. **[E7]**

### Bǫðvarr bjarki: cognate, counterpart, or coincidence?

**[E2/E3]** The later Scandinavian champion resembles Beowulf in being a foreign or overseas warrior who reaches a Danish Scylding court, performs extraordinary combats around the royal hall, becomes the king’s champion, participates in Swedish dynastic affairs, and bears a potentially ursine name.

The variants matter:

- **Saxo:** Biarco kills Agnerus and a giant bear; he makes Hialto drink the bear’s blood.
- ***Hrólfs saga kraka*:** Bǫðvarr transforms cowardly Hǫttr by making him drink a monster’s blood, then arranges for him to receive credit for the killing. In Hrólfr’s final battle, a bear-like being fights while Bǫðvarr sits in trance.
- ***Bjarkarímur*:** the enemies include a she-wolf and a bear.

**[E6]** Older scholarship sometimes reconstructed a single Bjarki-Beowulf original. Axel Olrik and Larry Benson emphasized that the closest resemblances arise only when features from several late sources are combined. Recent work has reopened a more limited common-tradition case: a “proto-Bjarki” or Scylding monster-slayer may lie behind both traditions. The strongest recent statement still begins conditionally—“assuming” the parallels are not coincidence or later transmission—and therefore remains reconstruction, not identification; see [*In Defence of Böðvarr bjarki*](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11061-023-09792-7).

## 5. Cult, tomb, and ritual

### Cult

**Finding: no attested cult.**

No medieval inscription, sacrificial calendar, shrine, priesthood, votive deposit, healing tradition, invocation, theophoric personal name, or Christian feast identifies Beowulf as a recipient of worship. Neither the poem nor any Scandinavian source presents him as divine. Describing him as a “heroized dead” in the technical Greek-cult sense would exceed the evidence.

Literary descriptions of pagan Danish temple worship in the poem concern desperate Danes appealing to unnamed idols before Beowulf arrives. Beowulf neither conducts nor receives that worship.

### The literary barrow

**[E1]** The poem provides a highly specific funeral tradition: cremation, armour on the pyre, a woman’s lament, construction of a headland barrow over ten days, deposition of the dragon treasure, and twelve mounted nobles circling the mound. This is evidence for what the poet or tradition imagined as a royal Geatish funeral—not evidence that archaeologists have found the grave.

The treasure is returned to the earth and said to be as useless to people as it had been before. The funeral can be read as magnificent commemoration, political anxiety, or indictment of failed treasure circulation; those are interpretations of the same attested rite.

### Skalunda and other claimed tombs

**[E6]** Birger Nerman proposed that the large barrow at **Skalunda** in Västergötland might be Beowulf’s grave, connecting the nearby place-name Aranäs with the poem’s *Earnanæs* and treating the hero as a Geatish ruler. The proposal lacks:

- an inscription naming Beowulf;
- grave goods uniquely corresponding to the poem;
- a securely demonstrated identity between Aranäs and *Earnanæs*;
- independent evidence locating Beowulf’s kingdom or death at Skalunda.

The equation is therefore a modern archaeological-historical conjecture, not an excavated identification. Its circulation as “Beowulf’s tomb” turns an uncertain possibility into a modern legend. Nerman’s proposal is summarized in the account of [Skalunda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skalunda), but the evidentiary basis does not permit secure attribution.

### Heorot and Lejre

**[E1]** Danish archaeology has uncovered successive monumental halls at **Gammel Lejre**, a major elite centre with nearby barrows and wetlands. These discoveries make the landscape broadly compatible with later traditions locating the Scyldings at Hleiðr/Lejre.

**[E6]** No hall bears an inscription “Heorot”; no find identifies Hrothgar, Beowulf, or Grendel. John D. Niles argued that archaeology anchors the poem more firmly to a specific kind of Danish royal site while simultaneously showing how much of its story remains mythical. A review of *Beowulf and Lejre* stresses both the hall-and-barrow setting and the poem’s continuing fantasy: [The Medieval Review](https://scholarworks.iu.edu/journals/index.php/tmr/article/view/16565).

### Images and iconography

**Finding:** No securely medieval image is known to depict Beowulf by name. The manuscript is text, not an illustrated heroic cycle. Migration- and Viking-period images of warriors, beasts, halls, dragons, or animal-headed fighters may illuminate visual culture but cannot be catalogued as Beowulf iconography without an identifying inscription or uniquely diagnostic narrative sequence.

## 6. Historical evolution of the figure

### I. Sixth-century remembered world

**[E1/E6]** The independently recorded death of Chlochilaichus/Hygelac places part of the legendary background in the early sixth century. Swedish-Geatish dynastic names also have later Scandinavian cognates. There is no sixth-century source for Beowulf himself.

### II. Oral and poetic formation, date unknown

**[E5/E6]** The poem’s formulaic diction, inherited heroic names, digressions, and traditional scenes imply deep participation in Old English oral-poetic practice. That does not establish that the complete surviving poem was orally improvised. Formulaic language occurs in demonstrably literate Old English works.

Friedrich Panzer’s 1910 comparison with the international **Bear’s Son Tale** treated the Grendel sequence as a folktale in which an extraordinarily strong hero defeats a first monster and follows another into an underworld-like lair. Later “Two-Troll” studies refined this comparison. The tale-type can explain structural affinity, but it neither dates the episode nor proves that Beowulf originally was a bear.

### III. Christian literary shaping

**[E1/E6]** The received poem is explicitly Christian in narration: one Creator governs events; Grendel descends from Cain; pagan idol worship is condemned; pride and transience dominate Hrothgar’s sermon. Yet the characters inhabit a pre-Christian Scandinavian world of cremation, feud, oath, vengeance, and heroic fame.

Three principal readings remain in tension:

1. the poet Christianized older pagan material while admiring it;
2. the poem critiques the self-consuming heroic economy;
3. it presents Beowulf as an ethically exemplary, even quasi-Christological protector while stopping short of sainthood.

The poem never names Christ, the Trinity, a saint, baptism, church, or Eucharist. Beowulf thanks God but receives a non-Christian cremation. “Saint Beowulf” is consequently an interpretive analogy, not a traditional classification.

### IV. Manuscript canonization around 1000

**[E1]** Copying the poem into the Nowell Codex preserved one textual form. There is no evidence of wide medieval circulation: no second manuscript, medieval translation, quotation explicitly ascribed to *Beowulf*, or commentary survives.

### V. Parallel Scandinavian reshaping, thirteenth–fifteenth centuries

**[E2/E3]** Saxo, *Skjöldunga saga*, *Hrólfs saga kraka*, *Bjarkarímur*, and *Grettis saga* preserve analogous halls, kings, monster-killers, bear associations, and Swedish warfare. These sources should be read as independent late witnesses to related northern story-worlds, not as missing chapters of *Beowulf*.

### VI. Antiquarian rediscovery, 1563–1815

**[E7]** Nowell, Wanley, Cotton, Thorkelin, and early editors converted a nearly invisible manuscript into a historical and philological object. Wanley foregrounded dynastic history; Thorkelin presented it as ancient Danish history.

### VII. National epic formation, nineteenth century

**[E7]** Danish, German, and English scholars competed over the poem’s cultural ownership. Grundtvig placed it within Danish antiquity; English philology increasingly made it the beginning of “English literature,” even though its hero, geography, and dynasties are Scandinavian. The expression “national epic” is therefore a nineteenth-century reception category, not a medieval title.

### VIII. Literary revaluation after 1936

**[E7]** J. R. R. Tolkien delivered “Beowulf: The Monsters and the Critics” to the British Academy on 25 November 1936. He argued that the monsters are structurally central and that scholars had too often quarried the poem for history while treating its literary design as an embarrassment. The lecture did not discover the poem or end historical study, but it transformed critical priorities. Its date and publication history are documented by the [Tolkien Estate](https://www.tolkienestate.com/scholarship/tom-shippey-the-monsters-and-the-critics-and-other-essays/).

### IX. Global translation and popular culture

**[E7]** Twentieth- and twenty-first-century translation, fantasy literature, comics, performance, games, and films converted Beowulf from a specialist Old English text into an internationally recognizable monster-slayer. This modern Beowulf is often more individualistic, muscular, sexually active, pagan, and psychologically conflicted than the medieval figure.

## 7. Modern reception and reinvention

### Translations and literary retellings

- **William Morris and A. J. Wyatt, 1895. [E7]** An aggressively archaizing English translation; influential but often criticized for obscurity.
- **Francis B. Gummere, 1909. [E7]** Alliterative translation widely reprinted and reused in later illustrated adaptations.
- **Tolkien, 1936. [E7]** “The Monsters and the Critics” established the poem’s monsters and elegiac structure as central literary subjects.
- **John Gardner, *Grendel*, 1971. [E7]** Retells the Danish story from Grendel’s existentially alienated viewpoint; Gardner’s Beowulf becomes an unnerving, almost mythic opponent.
- **Michael Crichton, *Eaters of the Dead*, 1976. [E7]** Recasts the Grendel material as an encounter between Ibn Fadlan and a surviving archaic people. Its pseudo-documentary apparatus is modern fiction, not evidence for a historical explanation of Grendel.
- **Seamus Heaney, *Beowulf: A New Verse Translation*, 1999. [E7]** A major poetic and commercial event. Heaney connected Old English *þolian*, “endure,” with Ulster dialect “thole” and used an Irish-inflected English register. The translation was acclaimed for force and accessibility but debated for its regional voice and departures from literal syntax. Its scholarly reception is surveyed in [*Translating Beowulf*](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/translating-beowulf/00392B0FF4E14C43C0DB826160C60CAB/listing).
- **J. R. R. Tolkien, *Beowulf: A Translation and Commentary*, 2014. [E7]** Posthumously edited by Christopher Tolkien. This prose translation and lecture material should not be confused with the 1936 essay.
- **Maria Dahvana Headley, *Beowulf: A New Translation*, 2020. [E7]** Uses contemporary slang and a forceful vernacular voice, beginning with “Bro!” Its language is deliberate modern reinvention, not a claim about literal Old English diction.

Tolkien’s own fiction also absorbed the poem: Meduseld recalls Heorot; Théoden’s funeral recalls Beowulf’s mounted mourners; the thief’s cup and Smaug’s retaliation in *The Hobbit* closely rework the dragon episode. These are literary transformations, not continuations of medieval Beowulf tradition.

### Film and television

- ***Grendel Grendel Grendel*, 1981. [E7]** Animated adaptation derived from Gardner’s novel.
- ***The 13th Warrior*, 1999. [E7]** Film of Crichton’s *Eaters of the Dead*; rationalizes monsters as human enemies.
- ***Beowulf*, 1999, directed by Graham Baker. [E7]** Moves the story into a post-apocalyptic techno-feudal setting and gives Beowulf supernatural parentage. Both are modern inventions.
- ***Beowulf & Grendel*, 2005, directed by Sturla Gunnarsson. [E7]** Humanizes Grendel, supplies a history of grievance, and reduces the poem’s overt supernatural framework.
- ***Grendel*, 2007 television film. [E7]** Action-fantasy retelling.
- ***Beowulf*, 2007, directed by Robert Zemeckis, screenplay by Neil Gaiman and Roger Avary. [E7]** Motion-capture feature. It makes Grendel the son of Hrothgar and Grendel’s mother, turns the mother into a seductive shape-shifter, has Beowulf lie about killing her, makes him her lover and the dragon’s father, and relocates him to the Danish throne. None of these relationships occurs in the poem. The official film page identifies the motion-capture production and cast: [Paramount Pictures](https://www.paramountpictures.com/movies/beowulf).

### Comics, games, and commercial hero

**[E7]** Beowulf has appeared in DC and Marvel comics, Gareth Hinds’s 2007 graphic novel, role-playing games, interactive fiction, video games, heavy-metal music, operas, rock operas, and branded entertainment. A continuously expanded bibliography records hundreds of translations and adaptations; the breadth demonstrates modern canonization, not an unbroken folk cult.

### Nationalist and racial appropriation

**[E7]** The poem became entangled with nineteenth-century Romantic nationalism and the construction of “Anglo-Saxon” racial ancestry. Its Old English language allowed English institutions to call it a national epic, while Danish and German scholars claimed its Scandinavian and pan-Germanic material. These claims routinely blurred language, ethnicity, nation, and race.

Twentieth-century Germanic and Anglo-Saxonist movements sometimes recruited Beowulf into narratives of heroic northern masculinity, warrior hierarchy, and racial antiquity. Nazi-era Germanic studies and later Odinist or white-nationalist milieus could incorporate the poem into a larger myth of pan-Germanic inheritance. That political use does not demonstrate that the poem teaches modern racial nationalism: its peoples are kin-linked, feud-bound dynasties, not modern nations or biological races. A scholarly conference report explicitly framed this problem as “Beowulf: Prince of the Geats, Nazis, and Odinists” and described the use of history, linguistics, and folklore to construct pan-Germanic identity in the [Old English Newsletter](https://www.oenewsletter.org/OEN/archive/OEN41_3.pdf).

Conversely, Heaney’s Northern Irish framing, postcolonial readings, feminist retellings, and translations centered on displacement or hybridity have contested exclusive English or racial ownership.

### Psychological and structuralist readings

- **Friedrich Panzer, 1910. [E6]** Comparative folktale analysis: the Grendel sequence reflects the Bear’s Son pattern.
- **Lord Raglan, *The Hero*, 1936. [E6]** His ritual-hero scheme is sometimes applied to Beowulf, but Beowulf poorly fits several central items: miraculous birth, divine parentage, threatened infancy, royal marriage, and mysterious disappearance are absent.
- **Tolkien, 1936. [E6/E7]** Mythic-elegiac structure: youth versus age, external monsters versus historical doom, and the heathen heroic world under Christian judgment.
- **F. P. Magoun, 1953. [E6]** Oral-formulaic analysis treated the poem as the product of oral composition using inherited formulas.
- **Joseph Campbell, *The Hero with a Thousand Faces*, 1949. [E6]** Modern teaching and popular media often map Beowulf onto the “monomyth.” Campbell did not supply evidence for the poem’s historical development, and the three-combat, fifty-year structure resists a simple single “hero’s journey.”
- **Georges Dumézil. [Finding]** Tripartite Indo-European ideology has influenced Germanic comparative mythology, but no accepted Dumézilian reconstruction establishes Beowulf as a god or as the embodiment of one social function.
- **Otto Rank. [Finding]** Rank’s birth-of-the-hero pattern has limited application because the poem supplies no miraculous birth or childhood exposure.
- **Gregory Nagy. [Finding]** Nagy’s work on oral poetics and hero traditions offers comparative concepts, but Beowulf has no documented cult comparable to Greek heroes; analogy cannot create one.
- **Later criticism. [E6]** Major approaches include Christian typology, feud economics, gift exchange, kingship, gender, disability, trauma, ecology, animal studies, and critiques of heroic competence. They are datable interpretations of the poem, not recovered medieval beliefs.

## 8. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Historicity

The strongest historical correspondence is **Hygelac**, not Beowulf. Gregory of Tours’ Chlochilaichus anchors a royal raid remembered in the poem. The identification was a foundational discovery by Grundtvig, but it proves that the poem incorporated a historical event, not that its protagonist existed. A Danish historical study properly warns that *Beowulf* remains poetry rather than a historical chronicle: [“Beowulf, Hygelac og Chlochilaichus”](https://tidsskrift.dk/historisktidsskrift/article/view/56158).

There is no contemporary annal, inscription, genealogy, coin, grave marker, or foreign chronicle naming Beowulf the Geat. A historical warrior behind him is possible but presently untestable.

### The Geats

The conventional identification is with the **Gautar** of southern Sweden, especially Västergötland. Alternatives have included Gotlanders and other Gothic or Jutish groups. Linguistic correspondences and the poem’s geography favour the Gautar identification, but the poem’s political map is legendary and cannot be transferred wholesale onto modern Sweden.

### Swedish-Geatish wars

The poem contains a coherent dynastic sequence involving Ongentheow, Ohthere, Onela, Eadgils, and Eanmund. Later Scandinavian sources remember Óttarr, Aðils, Áli, and related Scylding-Swedish conflicts in changed forms. This supports the antiquity of some names and rivalries. It does not independently confirm Beowulf’s intervention, which appears only in the Old English poem.

### Pagan poem or Christian poem?

Earlier theories sometimes imagined a pagan lay later interpolated by a monk. The received text, however, integrates Christian diction throughout its narration, characterization, genealogies, and moral design. Whether that integration belongs to an early Christian poet, successive redaction, or late composition remains debated. No manuscript evidence physically separates “pagan” and “Christian” strata.

### Oral composition and literacy

Magoun’s oral-formulaic thesis emphasized formulas and traditional themes. Critics observed that formula density cannot by itself distinguish oral improvisation from literate composition trained in traditional verse. The current cautious position is that the poem participates deeply in oral-derived tradition, while the surviving artifact is an elaborately written and copied text. John Miles Foley’s continuum between traditionality and textuality avoids a false binary.

### The monsters and the critics

Tolkien’s 1936 intervention rejected the treatment of Grendel and the dragon as childish matter surrounding historically valuable digressions. He argued that the monsters provide the poem’s large structure: Beowulf confronts destructive evil in youth and mortality itself in age. His lecture became enormously influential, though later critics have restored attention to politics, feud, gender, manuscript context, and material history.

### Beowulf and Bǫðvarr bjarki

The positions are:

1. **Identity or direct derivation:** older scholarship sometimes treated them as versions of one hero.
2. **Common legendary ancestor:** a shared Scylding monster-slayer or “proto-Bjarki.”
3. **Selective resemblance:** the apparent analogue is assembled from mutually differing late Scandinavian sources.
4. **Folkloric convergence:** hall defence and monster combat independently draw on international tale patterns.

Recent scholarship can defend common ancestry, but the source gap and variant distribution prevent secure equation.

### Beowulf and Grettir

Guðbrandur Vigfússon and later scholars linked Grendel’s mere and Grettir’s Sandhaugar fight. Proposed explanations include:

- a common oral Two-Troll tale;
- Icelandic knowledge of an English story;
- broad folktale coincidence.

Magnús Fjalldal showed how tentative parallels hardened into assumed relationship without decisive transmission evidence. The “late *Grettis saga* version of Beowulf” formulation should therefore be treated as historiography, not fact.

### Archaeology

Lejre’s halls demonstrate elite Danish centres of the appropriate broad period and setting. Sutton Hoo and other rich burials illuminate the poem’s material imagination—helmets, rings, weapons, ships, and royal display—but none is Beowulf’s grave or treasure. Skalunda remains unverified. Archaeology can establish cultural plausibility; it cannot name literary actors without diagnostic evidence.

### Textual losses and editorial reconstruction

The manuscript’s burned and crumbling edges make *Beowulf* unusually dependent on early transcripts and editorial emendation. Editors distinguish:

- letters visible in the manuscript;
- readings preserved only by Wanley or the Thorkelin transcripts;
- conjectural restorations;
- normalization of spelling;
- interpretive emendation.

Translations often conceal this hierarchy by presenting a smooth continuous poem. The [Electronic Beowulf critical edition](https://ebeowulf.uky.edu/criticaledition.html) is especially valuable because it exposes rather than suppresses the layers.

### Discredited or unproved theories still circulating

- **“Beowulf is certainly a sixth-century historical king.”** Unsupported; only the poetic character is attested.
- **“His tomb has been found at Skalunda.”** Unsupported modern identification.
- **“He was worshipped as a god.”** No evidence.
- **“Beowulf literally means bear.”** A disputed etymological inference, not an attested dictionary gloss.
- **“Bǫðvarr bjarki is simply Beowulf’s Norse name.”** No medieval source says so.
- **“Christian monks merely added a few pious lines to an otherwise recoverable pagan poem.”** No manuscript strata establish this.
- **“The poem was written by two authors because there are two scribes.”** Copying hands do not equal composing authors.
- **“Grendel’s mother seduced Beowulf and bore the dragon.”** Invention of the 2007 film.
- **“The poem is the oldest surviving English text.”** False; earlier Old English inscriptions, glosses, laws, and poems survive. It is among the most substantial early English poems.
- **“It is a Viking poem.”** It concerns Scandinavian ancestors and was later compared with Norse literature, but its extant language is Old English and its manuscript was produced in Anglo-Saxon England.
