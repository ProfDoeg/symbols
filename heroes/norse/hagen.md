# Hagen (Högni): Research Dossier

> **Traditional class:** a mortal heroic warrior: in the German tradition Gunther’s formidable Burgundian vassal and counsellor; in the principal Norse Niflung tradition Högni is Gunnar’s brother; in *Þiðreks saga* he is their supernatural, illegitimate half-brother. No medieval source calls him a god, demigod, saint, or cult-receiving hero.  
> **Earliest secure attestation:** Latin **Hagano** in the *Waltharius*, probably composed in the ninth or tenth century and certainly represented by a surviving late-tenth-century fragment. An earlier-looking Old English **Hagena** occurs in *Widsith*, but neither the poem’s date nor the identity of that ruler with the Nibelung Hagen is secure.  
> **Central evidentiary result:** “Hagen/Högni” is not a biography recoverable behind the texts. It is a changing role in several intersecting heroic traditions. The murderer of Siegfried at a spring, concealer of the Rhine hoard, Danube-prophecy traveller, and victim of Kriemhild’s sword belongs chiefly to the Middle High German *Nibelungenlied*. The Högni whose heart is cut out while he laughs belongs to the Norse Atli cycle. Wagner combined and radically rewrote both.

## Evidentiary labels

- **[EA—secure early attestation]** Early textual evidence whose existence and approximate date are reasonably secure.
- **[LC—later canonical elaboration]** A medieval work that became a principal version but postdates the story’s earliest recoverable stage.
- **[RV—regional variant]** A form peculiar to, or especially characteristic of, one linguistic or regional tradition.
- **[LS—learned systematization]** An antiquarian, genealogical, or encyclopedic arrangement of inherited materials.
- **[SI—syncretistic identification]** A deliberate equation or combination of originally distinct figures or traditions.
- **[MA—medieval/early-modern accretion]** A demonstrably later addition or retelling.
- **[F—folklore]** Post-medieval popular localization, oral tradition, or belief.
- **[MSR—modern scholarly reconstruction]** A hypothesis inferred from comparison, not a statement of a primary source.
- **[MI—modern invention]** A modern artistic or popular creation sometimes mistaken for inherited legend.
- **[NEG—negative finding]** No relevant primary evidence has been identified.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### Identification

Hagen is the German form, Högni the normalized modern Scandinavian form, and **Hǫgni** the usual normalized Old Norse spelling of a major figure in Germanic heroic tradition. The forms cannot simply be treated as one stable character.

In the *Nibelungenlied* (composed about 1200), **Hagene von Tronege** is a non-royal but exceptionally powerful vassal and counsellor of the Burgundian kings Gunther, Gernot, and Giselher. He engineers and personally commits Siegfried’s murder, takes Kriemhild’s treasure and sinks it in the Rhine, recognizes the doomed nature of the journey to Etzel, and becomes the principal Burgundian combatant in the catastrophe at the Hunnish court. Kriemhild finally decapitates him with Siegfried’s sword. **[LC/RV]**

In the Eddic poems, **Hǫgni** is normally one of Gjúki’s sons and therefore Gunnar’s brother, not his vassal. He participates in the conspiracy against Sigurðr, but the actual killer is commonly the younger brother Guthormr. Later Atli has Högni’s heart cut from his living body; he laughs rather than cry out. Gunnar, not Högni, is the last guardian of the hoard’s secret. **[EA/RV]**

In *Þiðreks saga af Bern*, Högni occupies an intermediate position: he is the maternal half-brother of Gunnar and the other Niflungs, conceived when an **álfr**, a supernatural being, sleeps with their mother. He kills Sigurðr himself by stabbing him from behind and later fathers Aldrian during the Niflungs’ last battle. **[LC/RV]**

The Latin *Waltharius* supplies still another Hagen: **Hagano**, a Frankish noble hostage at Attila’s court, friend of Waltharius, and later unwilling ally of Guntharius against him. He loses an eye in the concluding duel but survives. This is early evidence for Hagen’s attachment to Gunther/Gunnar and to an Attila-Walter story-world, not evidence for the later Siegfried murder. **[EA]** The manuscript history includes a fragment probably from the late tenth century and fuller manuscripts from the eleventh century onward ([Harvard *Waltharius* manuscript history](https://waltharius.fas.harvard.edu/Manuscript_History); [Gottfried Portal, *Waltharius*](https://gottfried.unistra.fr/nibelungen/lateinische-fassungen/waltharius/)).

| Swedish | **Högne/Högni** | Scholarly and literary forms. |

The name belongs to a Germanic name family reconstructed with forms such as *Hagano-* or *Haganōn-*, but its precise etymology is unsettled. Connections proposed with Germanic *hagan-*—“enclosure, hedge, fenced place”—are linguistically more plausible than romantic equations with a god, animal-demon, or historical foreign title. Henry Kratz reviewed derivations from *hag*, “thorn/bush,” a supposed breeding bull or boar, the Turkic title *khan/khagan*, Aetius, and Eugenius, finding the historical and mythological equations weak; he tentatively interpreted the name as “one begotten in the brush,” hence “bastard,” to explain the *Þiðreks saga* birth story. That semantic construction is ingenious but remains a **modern hypothesis**, not a medieval gloss. **[MSR]** ([Kratz, “The Etymology of the Name Hagen”](https://ans-names.pitt.edu/ans/article/download/380/379/762)).

### Bynames, titles, and descriptive formulas

- **von Tronege / von Trony / von Tronie**: the *Nibelungenlied* manuscripts vary. B and C characteristically have *Tronege*; A often has *Trony*. Attempts to locate it at modern Dhronecken, Troyes, or elsewhere remain conjectural. The poem treats it as a territorial byname, but its author’s intended geography is unrecoverable. **[LC/MSR]**
- **Aldrianes/Dankrates sun**: Hagen’s father is called Aldrian in important *Nibelungenlied* readings; manuscript and redactional variation connects the royal father Dancrât/Dankrat with this genealogy. The relationship is not uniform across witnesses. **[LC]**
- **Gjúkason**: implicit or explicit Norse patronymic status as son of Gjúki; Högni is one of the Gjúkungar/Niflungar. **[EA/RV]**
- **elf-son/álfr’s son**: *Þiðreks saga* alone gives the supernatural paternity. **[LC/RV]**
- **“grim,” “fierce,” “faithful,” “devilish”**: these are descriptions or critical characterizations, not stable ancient cult epithets.
- **“of Troy”**: *Waltharius* calls Hagano *Troiana stirpe creatus*, “born of Trojan stock.” That is a learned medieval ethnogenealogical convention, not the origin of “Tronje” and not evidence that Hagen was Trojan. **[LS]**

### Necessary separation of homonyms

Old Norse literature contains several men called Högni who are not the Niflung:

1. **Högni, father of Sigrún**, enemy of Helgi Hundingsbani in the Helgi lays and *Völsunga saga*.
2. **Högni, father of Hildr**, participant in the everlasting Hjaðningavíg battle; corresponding names occur in Snorri, Saxo, and other traditions.
3. Various historical or pseudo-historical Högnis in *Hervarar saga*, *Ragnars saga*, *Gautreks saga*, *Egils saga*, *Grettis saga*, and other sagas.

A name match is not an identification. The requested fornaldarsögur and family sagas are valuable controls precisely because they demonstrate how common the name became. They do not all preserve episodes about the Niflung Högni. **[NEG]**

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### Earliest evidence

#### *Widsith*

The Old English catalogue poem *Widsith*, preserved only in the Exeter Book, copied in the later tenth century, lists rulers and peoples from Germanic heroic memory. Its **Hagena** is associated with the Holmryce. Because the entry gives no Gunther, Sigurd, Burgundian court, or Atli episode, identity with Hagen/Högni cannot be demonstrated. Proposed identity rests on comparative name matching. **[EA? / MSR]**

#### *Waltharius*

The earliest secure narrative Hagen is **Hagano** in the Latin hexameter *Waltharius*. Its composition is debated—often placed in the ninth or first half of the tenth century—while the earliest surviving fragment is probably late tenth century. Hagano, Waltharius, and Hiltgunt are hostages under Attila. Hagano escapes when he hears that Gibicho has died and Guntharius rules. When Waltharius later escapes with Hiltgunt and treasure, Guntharius pursues him. Hagano initially refuses to fight his old companion; taunted by Guntharius and bound by loyalty, he joins the final two-against-one combat. Waltharius loses a hand, Guntharius a leg, and Hagano an eye. They reconcile through grim joking. **[EA]**

The work attests:

- Hagen’s bond with a Gunther figure;
- friendship with Walter;
- a past at Attila’s court;
- divided loyalty and mutilating combat.

It does **not** attest Siegfried, Kriemhild, the Rhine hoard, Danube women, or Hagen’s death.

### The Eddic heroic poems

Most survive in **Codex Regius GKS 2365 4to**, copied about 1270, from older exemplars. Individual poems may be much older, but dates such as “ninth century” describe scholarly linguistic and metrical estimates, not surviving manuscripts. *Atlakviða* has often been dated around 900, though estimates extend into the eleventh century ([discussion of dating and manuscript](https://uni.hi.is/adalh/files/2013/01/Speculum.pdf)).

Relevant poems include:

- **Grípisspá**: prophetic synopsis of Sigurðr’s fate; Högni belongs to the Gjúkung complex. **[EA/LS]**
- **Brot af Sigurðarkviðu**: fragment of a Sigurðr lay; Gunnar and Högni debate the killing. The preserved version places Sigurðr’s death in bed, although prose links surrounding the Eddic corpus acknowledge competing forest/outdoor versions. **[EA]**
- **Sigurðarkviða in skamma**: Brynhildr drives Gunnar toward Sigurðr’s death; Gunnar consults Högni; Guthormr is induced to kill Sigurðr. **[EA]**
- **Guðrúnarkviða I–II**: Högni participates in the family tragedy and reports or is implicated in Sigurðr’s killing. **[EA]**
- **Atlakviða in grœnlenzka**: Gunnar and Högni go to Atli despite warning; Högni fights, is captured, and laughs while his heart is cut out. **[EA]**
- **Atlamál in grœnlenzku**: a longer, more domestic and expansive treatment of the Atli catastrophe. Högni has a wife, Kostbera, and sons; her dreams and rune-reading warn him. **[LC/RV]**
- **Oddrúnargrátr**: Oddrún’s lament recalls Gunnar, Högni, Atli, and the death sequence. **[EA/LC]**
- **Guðrúnarhvöt/Hamðismál**: the aftermath belongs to Guðrún’s later marriages and sons; Högni is remembered rather than acting. **[EA]**

### *Völsunga saga*

Composed in Iceland, usually in the later thirteenth century, and surviving in the fifteenth-century manuscript **Ny kgl. Saml. 1824 b 4to**, *Völsunga saga* turns Eddic verse and related prose traditions into a continuous dynastic narrative. Högni is Gjúki and Grímhildr’s son, brother of Gunnar, Guthormr, and Guðrún. He counsels against treachery but participates in the brothers’ oath-breaking plot. Guthormr kills Sigurðr. Later Högni fights Atli’s men, is captured, and has his heart cut out. **[LC/LS]**

### *Þiðreks saga af Bern*

Composed in Norway in the mid-thirteenth century from traditions the compiler repeatedly associates with German oral report, *Þiðreks saga* is not simply a translation of the *Nibelungenlied*. Its Högni is born when an elf fathers a child on Queen Oda, making him the Niflungs’ maternal half-brother. He is physically dark and formidable. He spears Sigurðr between the shoulders after Gunnar cannot overcome him. At Atli’s court, Högni survives longest, fathers Aldrian on an unnamed or differently named woman in versions of the episode, and dies of wounds after arranging the son’s survival. Aldrian later kills Atli by sealing him in the treasure cave. **[LC/RV]** A linguistic reader summarizes the half-elf birth and the back-stabbing of Sigurðr ([Barnes and Faulkes reader](https://theswissbay.ch/pdf/Books/Linguistics/Mega%20linguistics%20pack/Indo-European/Germanic/Old%20Norse%2C%20A%20New%20Introduction%20to%20%5BPart%201%20-%20Grammar%2C%20Part%202%20-%20Reader%2C%20Part%203%20-%20Glossary%20and%20Index%20of%20Names%5D%20%28Barnes%20%26%20Faulkes%29.pdf)).

### *Nibelungenlied*

The anonymous Middle High German epic was composed around 1200, probably in the Danube-Austrian cultural sphere. It survives in more than thirty manuscripts and fragments. Principal manuscripts A, B, and C are thirteenth-century witnesses; the three principal codices were entered in UNESCO’s Memory of the World register in 2009 ([German UNESCO Commission](https://www.unesco.de/staette/nibelungenlied/)). The work has 39 *Aventiuren* in the familiar organization.

Hagen’s role is central:

- old enough to recognize Siegfried and recount his earlier exploits;
- Gunther’s counsellor;
- planner and killer of Siegfried;
- confiscator and concealer of the Nibelung treasure;
- interpreter of the Danube women’s prophecy;
- commander in the journey to Etzel;
- slayer of Ortlieb and many Huns;
- last major Burgundian captive;
- decapitated by Kriemhild.

The full Middle High German and historical editions, plus early translations, are catalogued by the [Gottfried Portal](https://gottfried.unistra.fr/nibelungen/deutsche-fassungen/nibelungenlied/). A public-domain English translation is available through [Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/7321/7321-h/7321-h.htm).

### *Nibelungenklage*

The *Klage*, composed soon after the *Nibelungenlied* and transmitted with it in many manuscripts, narrates lamentation, burial, and transmission of the news. It interprets responsibility more explicitly, often redistributing blame and defending Christian or courtly moral order. Hagen is dead and functions as an object of judgment and mourning. **[LC]**

### Other German texts

- **Biterolf und Dietleib**, thirteenth century: places Hagen in a wider German heroic network. **[MA]**
- **Rosengarten zu Worms**, thirteenth century: Hagen fights in Kriemhild’s rose garden tournament, a story independent of the canonical murder-and-revenge sequence. **[MA/RV]**
- **Das Lied vom Hürnen Seyfrid**, surviving in a sixteenth-century print tradition: radically alters Siegfried’s youth and dragon story; it is not evidence for the early Hagen narrative. **[MA]**
- **Volksbuch and theatrical adaptations**: early-modern transformations rather than ancient witnesses. **[MA/F]**

### Snorri, Saxo, and the Hildr-Högni cycle

Snorri Sturluson’s *Skáldskaparmál* (about 1220) explains poetic diction through the Hjaðningavíg: Högni is father of Hildr; Heðinn abducts her; Hildr resurrects the dead each night so the battle continues. Saxo Grammaticus’s Latin *Gesta Danorum* (written about 1200) gives the corresponding Hithinus/Höginus story. These works attest a **different Högni**, notwithstanding early scholarship’s attempts to unite every Hagen/Högni. **[LS/NEG]**

### *Beowulf*, *Grettis saga*, *Egils saga*, and the fornaldarsögur

Klaeber’s *Beowulf* and Tolkien’s “Beowulf: The Monsters and the Critics” matter methodologically for Germanic heroic legend, oral inheritance, and the danger of mining poetry merely for history. They do not attest the Niflung Hagen. Likewise, *Beowulf*’s historical-legendary kings and Finnsburg material do not provide an episode involving him. **[NEG]**

*Grettis saga* and *Egils saga Skallagrímssonar* are family sagas composed centuries after their ostensible settings. They preserve heroic vocabulary, revenant narratives, poetry, and occasional persons bearing common names, but no demonstrable Niflung-Hagen episode. **[NEG]**

*Hervarar saga*, *Ragnars saga loðbrókar*, *Gautreks saga*, and related fornaldarsögur include Högnis or material sharing the broad legendary world, but their names must be checked individually. The Copenhagen [Stories for All Time bibliography](https://fasnl.net/bibliography/) documents their manuscripts and textual histories. A Högni in one of these texts is not automatically Gunnar’s brother. **[NEG/MSR]**

## 3. The story, cycle by cycle

## 3.1 The Walter cycle: hostage, friend, reluctant opponent

In *Waltharius*, Hagano is given to Attila as a hostage in place of the infant Guntharius. He and Waltharius become distinguished Hunnish warriors. After escaping home, Hagano tries to dissuade Guntharius from attacking Waltharius for his treasure. When the king’s followers fall, Guntharius shames Hagano into helping him. The resulting duel mutilates all three protagonists but ends in reconciliation. **[EA]**

This version makes Hagen’s defining problem conflicting obligations: friendship to Waltharius versus service to Gunther. His eventual choice anticipates the later German Hagen’s uncompromising loyalty, but *Waltharius* neither condemns him as a murderer nor kills him.

## 3.2 The Norse Sigurðr cycle

Gjúki and Grímhildr have Gunnar, Högni, Guðrún, and—depending on source and genealogy—Guthormr. Sigurðr marries Guðrún and becomes the brothers’ sworn companion. Through disguise or shape exchange he assists Gunnar in winning Brynhildr. When Guðrún reveals the deception, Brynhildr demands Sigurðr’s death. **[EA/LC]**

The brothers’ positions differ:

- In *Brot af Sigurðarkviðu*, Gunnar and Högni confer. Högni recognizes Sigurðr’s value and the horror of oath-breaking but does not prevent the murder. The fragment’s death scene belongs to a bed-killing version. **[EA]**
- In *Sigurðarkviða in skamma*, Gunnar incites Guthormr because Gunnar and Högni have sworn oaths to Sigurðr. Guthormr, who has not sworn them, kills Sigurðr and is himself killed. **[EA]**
- In *Völsunga saga*, this logic is systematized: Högni argues against killing Sigurðr, but the brothers drug or excite Guthormr into doing it. **[LC/LS]**
- In *Þiðreks saga*, Högni himself thrusts a spear between Sigurðr’s shoulder blades during a hunt or outdoor expedition, then helps conceal the deed. **[LC/RV]**
- In the *Nibelungenlied*, Hagen himself stabs Siegfried at a spring. **[LC/RV]**

Thus “Hagen killed Siegfried” is true of the German and *Þiðreks saga* branches, but false if asserted as the uniform Eddic account.

## 3.3 The spring murder in the *Nibelungenlied*

Hagen persuades Gunther that Siegfried poses a danger after the quarrel between Kriemhild and Brünhild. He manufactures a false war and asks Kriemhild how he may protect Siegfried. Trusting him, she sews a cross on the vulnerable place in Siegfried’s garment. The hunting party later races to a spring. Hagen has removed or withheld other drink; when Siegfried bends to drink, Hagen takes Siegfried’s spear and drives it through the marked spot. He then has the corpse laid outside Kriemhild’s chamber. **[LC]**

The violence is explicitly treacherous. The poem nevertheless gives Hagen virtues recognized by its heroic system—courage, foresight, prowess, and loyalty to his lords. Its moral force comes partly from the coexistence of heroic excellence and criminal betrayal.

## 3.4 The hoard in the Rhine

After Siegfried’s death, Kriemhild gains control of the Nibelung treasure and distributes wealth to attract allies. Hagen persuades the kings that her patronage threatens them. The treasure is taken from her; Hagen sinks it in the Rhine at **Lôche**, while the kings are absent, intending that he and they might retrieve it later. The location dies with them. **[LC]**

In the Norse Atli poems the hoard’s hiding is different. Gunnar knows its location and will not disclose it while Högni lives. When shown Högni’s heart, Gunnar declares that the secret is now his alone. A late Eddic prose summary says Gunnar threw or hid the gold in the Rhine, illustrating cross-contamination between German and Norse geography. **[EA/LC]**

Accordingly, the familiar picture of Hagen personally sinking the hoard in the Rhine is specifically the German canonical version, not a universal Nibelung tradition.

## 3.5 Danube prophecy and attempted killing of the chaplain

On the journey to Etzel, Hagen encounters supernatural women bathing in the Danube region, steals their clothes, and forces them to prophesy. One, Hadburg, initially promises success to regain the garments; another corrects her: all the Burgundians will die except the king’s chaplain. Hagen attempts to falsify the prophecy by throwing the chaplain into the flooded Danube and pushing him under with an oar. The chaplain survives through divine aid and returns to shore. Hagen then knows the prophecy is true, destroys the ferry, and nevertheless continues. **[LC]**

This episode presents no cult of Hagen. It dramatizes knowledge of fate, an attempted murder of a cleric, and the hero’s conscious advance into annihilation.

## 3.6 The Atli/Etzel catastrophe: Norse version

Atli covets the Niflungs’ gold and invites Gunnar and Högni to his court. Guðrún sends a warning, including a rune-cut token, but the message is altered or misunderstood. In *Atlamál*, Högni’s wife Kostbera correctly reads the damaged runes and interprets ominous dreams; he dismisses the warnings. **[EA/LC]**

The brothers fight after reaching Atli. Högni is captured. Atli’s men first cut out the trembling heart of the slave or cook Hjalli to deceive Gunnar. Gunnar recognizes it as a coward’s heart. Högni’s heart is then cut from him alive; he laughs without weeping. Gunnar recognizes the fearless heart and refuses to disclose the treasure. He is cast into a snake pit and dies. Guðrún later kills her sons by Atli, serves him their flesh and blood, kills him, and burns the hall in some versions. **[EA]**

Högni therefore dies before Gunnar and never meets Kriemhild’s executioner’s stroke in this branch.

## 3.7 Etzel’s hall: German version

Kriemhild marries Etzel and eventually invites her brothers to Hungary, intending revenge. Hagen suspects the trap. Dietrich of Bern and others warn him, but the Burgundians remain armed and defiant. Violence begins around the feast. Hagen kills Kriemhild and Etzel’s young son **Ortlieb**—a deliberate killing of a child—by striking off his head so that it falls into Kriemhild’s lap. **[LC]**

The fighting escalates into the burning of the hall. Hagen and Volker defend a doorway and conduct a mass slaughter. Rüdiger, bound by contradictory oaths, fights the Burgundians and dies. Dietrich finally captures Gunther and Hagen separately and delivers them alive to Kriemhild, expecting her to spare them. She instead has Gunther beheaded and shows his head to Hagen, hoping to force disclosure of the hoard. Hagen rejoices that only he now knows its location. Kriemhild takes Siegfried’s sword Balmung and cuts off Hagen’s head. Hildebrand, outraged that a woman has killed such a warrior, kills Kriemhild. **[LC]**

The famous “last stand” is therefore not Hagen’s isolated final combat. It is a collective Burgundian destruction whose ethical architecture includes revenge, broken hospitality, child-killing, compelled loyalty, and the violent policing of gender.

## 3.8 *Þiðreks saga*: survival through a son

In *Þiðreks saga*, the Niflungs’ destruction also occurs at Atli’s court, but Högni’s ending differs. Mortally wounded, he fathers a son, Aldrian, to preserve revenge. The child is hidden and later seals Atli inside the treasure cave, where he dies. **[LC/RV]**

This converts Högni from a sterile last defender into progenitor of vengeance and makes Atli, rather than Kriemhild/Grímhildr, the principal final victim.

## 5. Cult, tomb, and ritual

### Cult

No secure inscription, sanctuary, altar, sacrifice, festival, priesthood, votive deposit, or medieval testimony documents worship of Hagen/Högni. No source calls him a god or a hero who receives offerings. **[NEG]**

This distinguishes him sharply from Greek cult heroes. His function is literary and memorial: he belongs to heroic song, manuscript, performance, political myth, and modern civic commemoration—not demonstrable pre-Christian cult.

### Tomb and relics

No securely attested medieval tomb, relic, or burial place belongs to Hagen. Claims attaching him to Rhine localities, Worms, Dhronecken/Tronje, or the treasure’s supposed resting place are literary geography, antiquarian speculation, tourism, or folklore unless supported by independent archaeological evidence. None has yielded an inscribed or datable “Hagen tomb.” **[NEG/F]**

The *Nibelungenklage* narrates treatment and mourning of the dead after Etzel’s catastrophe, but a literary burial account is not archaeological evidence. **[LC]**

### Ritual within the narratives

Ritualized acts do occur inside the stories:

- oath-brotherhood between Sigurðr and the Gjúkungar;
- hospitality and its catastrophic breach at Atli/Etzel’s court;
- funeral lament and burial in the *Klage*;
- oath-taking over weapons in Wagner;
- Wagner’s invented ceremonial summons of the Gibichung vassals and proposed sacrifices to Wotan, Fricka, and Donner.

These are narrative rituals, not evidence that audiences performed a cult of Hagen. **[LC/MI]**

### Place-memory and commemoration

Worms and Rhine tourism materially commemorate the *Nibelungenlied*, and modern monuments, museums, festivals, public art, and treasure legends localize Hagen. These document reception from the nineteenth century onward, not ancient worship. **[F/MI]**

## 6. Historical evolution of the figure

### Fifth-century event horizon

Roman chronicles report the defeat of the Rhine Burgundians and King Gundaharius by Aetius and Hunnic auxiliaries in 436/437. The *Lex Burgundionum* later preserves royal ancestral names resembling Gibica, Gundomar, Gislaharius, and Gundaharius. This is strong evidence that the Burgundian-destruction tradition retained distorted names and a real catastrophe ([Oxford Classical Dictionary, “Burgundians”](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/61673/chapter-abstract/548508952?login=false); [historical-source discussion](https://www.nibelungenlied-gesellschaft.de/03_beitrag/bender/fs13_bend.html)). **[Historical evidence]**

There is, however, no fifth-century Hagen. Attila did not personally destroy the Burgundians in the manner of Atli/Etzel’s feast, and historical Theoderic belongs to a later generation. The heroic tradition compressed generations and transferred agency. **[MSR]**

### Eighth–tenth centuries: name catalogues and *Waltharius*

*Widsith* may show an early Hagena remembered as a ruler, but identification remains insecure. *Waltharius* gives the first secure narrative Hagen: hostage, friend, and Gunther’s warrior. **[EA]**

### Ninth–eleventh centuries: early Norse heroic poetry

If the conventional early dating of *Atlakviða* is accepted, Högni’s fearless heart-death was established centuries before the surviving manuscript. The Eddic Sigurðr lays developed his position as Gunnar’s brother and implicated conspirator rather than universal principal murderer. **[EA/MSR]**

### About 1200: German canonical shaping

The *Nibelungenlied* reorganized inherited heroic material according to high-medieval courtly, Christian, and political concerns. Hagen became Gunther’s vassal, the central strategist of both halves, Siegfried’s murderer, keeper of the hoard, interpreter of prophecy, and almost superhuman defender at Etzel’s hall. The poet neither simply celebrates nor simply condemns him. **[LC]**

### Early thirteenth century: explanatory and moral supplements

The *Klage* supplied mourning, burial, news transmission, and retrospective judgment. Snorri and Saxo systematized other Högni cycles, especially the Hjaðningavíg, helping later readers conflate names that their stories keep separate. **[LC/LS]**

### Mid-to-late thirteenth century: Scandinavian synthesis

*Þiðreks saga* reconciled German and Nordic material without erasing contradiction: Högni is both sibling and outsider through his elf paternity; he personally kills Sigurðr; his son perpetuates revenge. *Völsunga saga* imposed prose continuity on Eddic lays and related traditions, assigning Guthormr the killing while retaining Högni’s complicity and Atli-death. **[LC/LS/RV]**

### Late medieval and early-modern proliferation

Tournament poems, prose books, prints, chronicles, and localizations detached Hagen from any single coherent cycle. Manuscript redactors altered motivation and moral emphasis. The heroic corpus did not possess one fixed medieval canon. **[MA]**

### Eighteenth and nineteenth centuries: rediscovery and national epic

Modern German philology and Romanticism recovered and edited the *Nibelungenlied*. Early editors including Bodmer, von der Hagen, Lachmann, and others transformed a manuscript tradition into a printed national classic. The work came to be styled a “German Iliad.” Grimm’s engagement helped make medieval heroic poetry evidence for an imagined national antiquity, although the politics and textual scholarship were not uniform ([Cambridge, *Brothers Grimm and the Making of German Nationalism*](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/brothers-grimm-and-the-making-of-german-nationalism/prince-of-germany/8D3B67CB0D9927149A6D459307E17403)). **[MSR/modern reception]**

### Wagner, 1848–1876

Wagner drafted the project after the revolutions of 1848–49, completed *Götterdämmerung*’s score in 1874, and premiered the full *Ring* at Bayreuth in 1876 ([Berlin State Opera](https://www.staatsoper-berlin.de/de/veranstaltungen/goetterdaem-merung.11030/)). He fused Norse names and cosmology with German episodes.

His Hagen is:

- the unloved son of Alberich and Grimhilde;
- Gunther and Gutrune’s half-brother;
- a bass role marked by grim, low orchestral sonority;
- awake while others sleep;
- manipulator of the forgetfulness potion, marriage plot, and hunt;
- Siegfried’s spear-murderer;
- claimant to the ring;
- killed when he lunges into the Rhine after it.

This is a powerful modern synthesis, not a recovery of an ancient original. **[MI]**

### Twentieth century to present

Philology increasingly emphasized variant traditions, manuscript redaction, performance, and the instability of the “Nibelung cycle.” Hagen continued in novels, theatre, opera productions, comics, film, television, games, festivals, and tourism, usually filtered through either the *Nibelungenlied* or Wagner.

## 7. Modern reception and reinvention

### Literature and theatre

- **Friedrich de la Motte Fouqué, *Der Held des Nordens* (1808–10):** Romantic dramatic adaptation drawing on Nordic material. **[MI]**
- **Friedrich Hebbel, *Die Nibelungen* (1861):** trilogy in which Hagen’s loyalty, fatalism, and antagonism receive modern psychological and national framing. **[MI]**
- **William Morris, *The Story of Sigurd the Volsung and the Fall of the Niblungs* (1876):** English poetic recasting of Norse sources; Högni remains within the Gjúkung family. **[MI]**
- **J. R. R. Tolkien, *The Legend of Sigurd and Gudrún***, composed largely in the 1920s–30s and published in 2009: a modern alliterative reconstruction, not a translated lost Eddic poem. **[MI]**

### Opera

Wagner’s Hagen dominates modern imagery. His paternity from Alberich, obsession with the Ring, and final drowning-lunge are Wagnerian constructions. *Götterdämmerung* premiered on 17 August 1876, with Gustav Siehr as Hagen ([Corago performance record](https://corago.unibo.it/opera/Z000032554); [Wagner libretto](https://www.naxos.com/PDF/RingCycle/Libretti/4_Gotterdammerung_libretto_mini-site.pdf)). **[MI]**

### Visual art and monuments

Medieval *Nibelungenlied* manuscripts are primarily textual witnesses; there is no securely identified early pagan iconography of Hagen. Viking-Age and Romanesque Sigurðr carvings—such as the Ramsund carving and Hylestad portal—depict parts of Sigurðr’s youth, not securely Hagen/Högni. Assigning an unidentified armed figure to Högni without an inscription is unwarranted. **[NEG]**

Modern illustrators and stage designers supplied the now-familiar visual Hagen: dark-armoured, spear-bearing, broad and grim. Josef Hoffmann’s 1876 Bayreuth designs helped monumentalize Wagner’s version. Municipal monuments around Worms and Rhine tourism belong to modern commemoration. **[MI]**

### Film

- **Fritz Lang, *Die Nibelungen* (1924):** Hagen, played by Hans Adalbert Schlettow, is visually severe and politically charged; the two-part film helped fix the image of the implacable warrior in Weimar-era mass culture. **[MI]**
- **Harald Reinl, *Die Nibelungen* (1966–67):** colour spectacle recasting the epic for post-war popular cinema. **[MI]**
- **Uli Edel, *Ring of the Nibelungs/Dark Kingdom* (2004):** freely hybridizes Wagnerian, Norse, and German elements. **[MI]**
- Numerous filmed *Ring* productions continually reinterpret Wagner’s Hagen through race, class, industrialization, fascism, capitalism, and family trauma. These readings document performance history, not medieval meanings.

### Nationalist and extremist appropriation

“Nibelung loyalty” (*Nibelungentreue*) became a political slogan for unconditional loyalty unto destruction. Before and during the First World War it described Germany’s bond with Austria-Hungary; later nationalist rhetoric applied the doomed steadfastness to soldiers and political obedience. **[modern political appropriation]**

After 1918, the “stab-in-the-back” myth could invert the medieval murder: Siegfried became the allegedly undefeated German army, while Hagen’s rearward spear thrust was mapped onto democrats, socialists, or Jews falsely blamed for defeat. The analogy is modern antisemitic and anti-democratic propaganda, not the poem’s allegory ([Textpraxis discussion](https://textpraxis.ivv1.uni-muenster.de/stefan-tomasek-vermittlungswege-mittelhochdeutscher-texte)). **[MI/political abuse]**

National Socialism exploited the Nibelung complex. In January 1943 Hermann Göring compared the encircled soldiers at Stalingrad to the Nibelungs fighting in their burning hall. Hagen could be celebrated as ruthless loyalty or displaced into the role of treacherous “stabber,” depending on propagandistic need. Recent scholarship describes the Nazi period as a high point of nationalist instrumentalization ([University of Bayreuth record](https://eref.uni-bayreuth.de/id/eprint/87725/)). **[modern political appropriation]**

The name and imagery persist in far-right milieus, including the German extremist martial-arts event **Kampf der Nibelungen**. Such use is contemporary ideological branding, not continuity of medieval cult or belief. **[MI]**

### Psychology and comparative mythology

Hagen has attracted archetypal readings as shadow, trickster, resentful outsider, oath-bound retainer, or embodiment of death. These are modern interpretive vocabularies.

- **Otto Rank** and later psychoanalytic writers approached heroic cycles through birth, family conflict, and the hero’s double. Applying that model to Hagen is interpretation, not primary evidence.
- **Lord Raglan’s hero-pattern** fits Hagen poorly: Hagen is not consistently a royal child, wonder-born culture founder, or cult-receiving dead hero.
- **Joseph Campbell’s monomyth** centers the questing hero and does not explain Hagen’s changing structural role particularly well.
- **Georges Dumézil-style trifunctional comparison** has occasionally encouraged mapping heroic figures onto Indo-European social functions, but no consensus identifies Hagen as the survival of a specific deity or function.
- Structuralist readings productively examine exchange—women, oaths, treasure, hospitality, and vengeance—but do not demonstrate an ancient ritual origin.

All such claims require labeling as **[MSR]**.

### Commercial and popular reinvention

Hagen/Högni appears in fantasy novels, role-playing games, manga, anime, comics, metal music, and games. Common modern inventions include:

- treating “Hagen, son of Alberich” as universal mythology;
- making him literally a demon, orc, dark elf, or dwarf;
- giving him magical powers not present in the medieval texts;
- merging him with Högni father of Hildr or Högni father of Sigrún;
- presenting a named historical Hagen at Gundaharius’s court;
- locating the Rhine treasure through pseudo-archaeological certainty.

These are creative uses, not recovered tradition. **[MI]**

## 8. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Historicity

The Burgundian defeat of 436/437 and the name Gundaharius are historical. Hagen is not independently attested in fifth-century records. No reliable evidence identifies him with Aetius, Aetius’s son, the Alan Goar/Eochar, Eugenius, or another historical contemporary. Those equations depend on loose phonetic resemblance and role substitution. Kratz’s survey records and criticizes several of them. **[MSR, largely discredited]**

The defensible historical formulation is therefore:

1. a historical Burgundian catastrophe contributed to the legendary complex;
2. some royal names preserve distant historical memory;
3. Hagen’s individual historicity is unproved;
4. the narrative’s Attila, Theoderic, and Burgundian synchronism is chronologically impossible.

### One Hagen or several?

Older scholarship sometimes sought a single original Hagen behind:

- Hagano of *Waltharius*;
- Högni the Niflung;
- Högni father of Hildr;
- Högni father of Sigrún;
- the *Widsith* Hagena;
- later saga namesakes.

Shared name, warrior type, and proximity to heroic legend permit comparison but not automatic identity. Current evidentiary discipline favors separate dossiers unless narrative relationships connect them. **[MSR]**

### Brother versus vassal

The difference is fundamental, not a copying error:

- Norse Högni: Gunnar’s full brother.
- *Þiðreks saga*: supernatural maternal half-brother.
- *Nibelungenlied*: Gunther’s vassal, with his own brother Dankwart.
- Wagner: Alberich’s son and Gunther’s maternal half-brother.

Kratz argued that half-brother status might be older than either full brotherhood or pure vassalage because it explains both intimacy and separation. That is a comparative reconstruction, not an established stemma. **[MSR]**

### Who kills Sigurd?

The traditions assign the deed differently:

- Guthormr: central Eddic and *Völsunga saga* version.
- Högni/Hagen: *Þiðreks saga* and *Nibelungenlied*.
- Location: bed in one Eddic strand; forest/outdoors in another; spring in the German poem.

The Codex Regius prose itself knows incompatible accounts, showing that variation is medieval, not merely modern confusion ([study of the death traditions](https://lbsrafhladan20.landsbokasafn.is/bitstream/handle/10802/8295/Preprint-online.pdf?sequence=1)). **[EA/LC]**

### Source direction and oral tradition

It is unsafe to draw a simple line from one lost “original epic” to every surviving text. The *Nibelungenlied*, Eddic poems, *Völsunga saga*, and *Þiðreks saga* preserve overlapping but independently reshaped materials. *Þiðreks saga* expressly invokes German reports, yet its exact written and oral sources remain debated.

Oral-formulaic approaches explain how episodes, names, and scenes can persist while relationships change. They do not license reconstruction of a complete lost Proto-Nibelung poem word for word. **[MSR]**

### Dating the Eddic poems

Statements such as “*Atlakviða* was written in Greenland in the ninth century” combine incompatible claims. The manuscript title calls it Greenlandic; Greenland was not Norse-settled until the late tenth century; linguistic dating of the poem varies. The secure datum is its late-thirteenth-century manuscript witness. Earlier composition is a scholarly judgment, strong in broad terms but imprecise in year. **[EA/MSR]**

### Etymology

Proposals include:

- derivation from *hagan-* “enclosure/hedge”;
- a byname associated with thorniness;
- “begotten in the brush,” hence bastard;
- bull or boar symbolism;
- the title *khagan*;
- Aetius or Eugenius;
- mythic equation with Höðr.

Only the Germanic lexical connection has broad phonological plausibility; the specific semantic story remains uncertain. The animal, foreign-title, and personal-name equations lack sufficient evidence. **[MSR]**

### “Tronje”

Suggested identifications with Dhronecken, Troyes, or other places are not settled. Medieval spellings prove the byname, not its modern map location. Tourist certainty exceeds the sources. **[LC/MSR/F]**

### Heroic ethics

Older national-romantic criticism often treated Hagen as the embodiment of ancient Germanic loyalty. Later readings emphasize that his loyalty entails deceit, murder, theft, attempted drowning of a priest, killing a child, and enabling collective destruction. Conversely, reducing him to a modern villain ignores the poems’ admiration for his courage and endurance. The contradiction is generated by the texts themselves. **[MSR grounded in LC]**

### Manuscripts, editions, and discoveries

- The late-tenth-century *Waltharius* fragment prevents dating the poem solely from later complete codices.
- The Codex Regius preserves the central Eddic heroic sequence but also a major lacuna in the Sigurðr material.
- The *Nibelungenlied*’s A, B, and C versions differ enough that “the text” must be treated editorially.
- Nineteenth-century editions established accessibility but sometimes imposed hypothetical originals.
- Modern manuscript catalogues and digital facsimiles increasingly expose variation that older normalized translations concealed.

### Discredited or unsupported theories still circulating

- Hagen was a documented historical general at Worms. **Unsupported.**
- He was Aetius, Aetius’s son, Goar, or an Alan *khagan*. **Speculative and generally rejected.**
- He was originally the god Höðr or a boar-shaped vegetation demon. **No direct evidence.**
- “Von Tronje” securely means modern Dhronecken or Troyes. **Unproved.**
- Archaeologists have found Hagen’s grave or the Nibelung hoard. **No accepted evidence.**
- The Sigurðr carvings depict Högni. **No secure identification.**
- Wagner faithfully dramatized the *Nibelungenlied*. **False: he constructed a new synthesis dominated by Norse material and his own inventions.**
