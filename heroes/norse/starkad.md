# Starkad (Starkaðr): Research Dossier

**Traditional class:** legendary mortal warrior and court poet of giant ancestry; in some witnesses effectively a demigod-like, superhuman champion, but never securely attested as a recipient of hero-cult. Medieval authors also classify him as an ancient skald.

**Earliest secure attestation:** the name **Starkaðr konungr**, a brother of Höðbroddr, occurs in the prose framework of *Helgakviða Hundingsbana II*, preserved in the *Codex Regius* of the Poetic Edda, GKS 2365 4to, copied about 1270. The underlying Helgi material may be older, conventionally placed somewhere between the tenth and twelfth centuries, but neither this king’s identity with the famous long-lived Starkad nor an earlier date for the prose notice is demonstrable. The earliest securely datable **extended** Starkad narrative is therefore Saxo Grammaticus’s Latin *Gesta Danorum*, probably completed around 1208–1218. [Securely attested medieval material.]

---

## 1. Identification, Names and Epithets

Starkad is the paradigmatic old warrior of medieval Scandinavian legend: gigantic, hideous, prodigiously strong, scar-covered, learned in poetry and martial ethics, repeatedly loyal to kings yet fated to betray or kill them. His unusually long career connects otherwise separate heroic generations—from Víkar and the early Uppsala kings through Frotho, Ingeld and the Brávellir combatants. This temporal excess is explained either by Odin’s grant of three human lifetimes or, in rationalizing traditions, by the conflation of several warriors. His defining contradiction is itself traditional: Odin gives prowess, poetry, wealth and victory, while Thor imposes social exclusion, wounds, childlessness and three shameful crimes.

### Names

- **Old Norse:** *Starkaðr*, variant *Stǫrkuðr*; normalized modern Icelandic *Starkaður*. The oblique stem produces spellings such as *Starkaði*. [Securely attested medieval material.]
- **Latin:** *Starcatherus*, *Starcather*, occasionally modernized *Starkaterus*, in Saxo and later Latin writers. [Later canonical elaboration.]
- **Danish:** *Stærkodder*, *Starkodder*; Swedish/Norwegian *Starkad*, *Starkader*. [Medieval and early-modern reception.]
- **Epithets:** *inn gamli*, “the Old”; *inn sterki*, “the Strong”; *Stórvirksson*, “son of Stórvirkr”; and, for the giant ancestor, *Áludrengr*, an obscure byname often rendered “Ala-warrior.” [Later canonical elaboration/regional variant.]
- *Skáldatal* calls him **Starkaðr inn gamli** and puts him first among royal poets; a related prose notice says his poems were “the oldest that people knew” and that he composed about Danish kings. This is a thirteenth-century learned classification, not proof of historical authorship. [Learned systematization.]

### Etymology

The first element is transparently related to Old Norse *sterkr/starkr*, “strong, rigid, harsh.” The second element is disputed.

1. A straightforward personal-name interpretation treats *Starkaðr* as an old compound whose second member had become opaque; nineteenth-century Scandinavian scholars glossed it approximately “strongly acting.” [Modern scholarly reconstruction.]
2. Sophus Bugge proposed that *Starkaðr* contracted a phrase meaning **“the strong Heaðobard”**, linking him with the Heaðobard-Danish conflict known from *Beowulf*. *Klaeber’s Beowulf* records this proposal but also notes competing explanations. No Old English form naming Starkad survives. [Disputed modern reconstruction.]
3. Attempts to extract historical nationality or tribal identity solely from the name are therefore insecure.

### One Starkad or several?

The sources preserve at least three incompatible systems:

- **One prodigiously long-lived warrior:** Saxo’s preferred narrative. Odin gives him three lifetimes. [Later canonical elaboration.]
- **Giant ancestor plus human descendant:** the U-version of *Hervarar saga* makes an eight-armed Starkaðr Áludrengr abduct Álfhildr and be killed by Thor; their descendants include another Starkad, son of Stórvirkr. [Regional variant/learned genealogy.]
- **Conflation of independent heroes:** modern source criticism, especially Axel Olrik, separated a mythical giant, a Víkar warrior and a Danish heroic-age counselor. This explains chronological and genealogical contradictions economically but remains reconstruction, not an attested native doctrine. [Modern scholarly reconstruction.]

Saxo expressly contrasts a supposedly credible report that Starkad came from the country east of Sweden occupied in Saxo’s time by Estonians with a *fabulosa et vulgaris opinio*—a “fabulous popular opinion”—that made him giant-born and many-armed. Saxo nevertheless records the latter in detail. Olaus Magnus, *Historia de gentibus septentrionalibus* (1555), relocates or ethnically reclassifies him as *Starchaterum Thauestum*, “Starkad the Tavastian.” [Medieval rationalization; early-modern accretion.]

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Earliest witnesses

1. **The Helgi tradition.** The prose before *Helgakviða Hundingsbana II* names King Starkaðr as one of Granmarr’s sons and Höðbroddr’s brother. Helgi kills Granmarr’s sons. The surviving witness is the c.1270 *Codex Regius*; the poem is composite and older than that manuscript, but the age of this particular prose genealogy is uncertain. It does not mention extra arms, Odin’s gifts, Víkar, three crimes or a long life. [Securely attested medieval material.]

2. **Vetrliði Sumarliðason’s Thor stanza.** Snorri’s *Skáldskaparmál*, composed c.1220 and surviving chiefly in manuscripts from c.1300 onward, quotes a verse attributed to the late-tenth-century Icelandic skald Vetrliði praising Thor for striking or overcoming several giants, including Starkad. If the attribution and text are sound, this is the oldest datable poetic association of a Starkad with Thor; it does not itself identify that adversary with Víkar’s champion. [Potentially securely attested early material, preserved only in a later manuscript.]

3. **Saxo Grammaticus, *Gesta Danorum*, Books VI–VIII.** Latin, composed c.1208–1218; the principal manuscript tradition is later because Saxo’s autograph is lost. This is the first complete surviving Starkad cycle and includes his origins, Víkar’s killing, service to several kings, poems and speeches, shameful deeds, Brávellir and death. [Securely attested medieval textual evidence; later canonical elaboration of the legend.]

### Texts named in the brief but not direct Starkad witnesses

- ***Beowulf***, surviving in London, British Library, Cotton Vitellius A.xv, c.1000, contains the Danish-Heaðobard conflict and the old warrior who incites renewed vengeance, but **does not name Starkad**. Identifying him with that old warrior is a comparative hypothesis, not textual fact.
- Tolkien’s “Beowulf: The Monsters and the Critics” (1936) transformed the study of *Beowulf*’s literary unity but offers no independent Starkad attestation.
- ***Völsunga saga*** does not make Starkad part of its continuous Sigurðr biography; the encounter belongs instead to *Norna-Gests þáttr*.
- The ***Nibelungenlied*** does not name him.
- ***Þiðreks saga af Bern*** does not supply a secure Starkad episode.
- ***Ragnars saga loðbrókar***, ***Egils saga Skallagrímssonar***, ***Grettis saga*** and the principal Íslendingasögur do not document Starkad’s deeds. Resemblances between Starkad, Grettir or Egill—great strength, ugliness, poetry, social alienation—are comparative readings.
- There is consequently no basis for presenting Starkad as a pan-Germanic hero directly attested in Old English or Middle High German texts. [Absence of evidence.]

## 3. The Story

### Giant ancestry and the extra arms

The U-prologue of *Hervarar saga* tells of **Starkaðr Áludrengr**, an eight-armed giant. During a dísablót at King Álfr’s court, he abducts Álfr’s daughter Álfhildr while she is reddening the sanctuary with sacrificial blood. Álfr invokes Thor; Thor kills Starkad and returns Álfhildr. In this genealogy Álfhildr bears Grímr by Starkad; Grímr fathers Stórvirkr; Stórvirkr fathers the later Starkad. [Regional variant.]

Saxo instead collapses the beings: his Starcatherus himself descends from giants and originally has six arms. Thor tears off four, leaving him with a human two-armed shape. Saxo calls this a popular fable contrary to reason, yet preserves it. [Learned rationalization preserving mythic material.]

*Gautreks saga* does not narrate the amputation directly. Its Starkad says that men see on him *jötunkuml átta handa*, “the giant-signs of eight hands,” from which Hlórriði—Thor—deprived his giant ancestor. Thus:

- **Six arms, four removed:** Saxo.
- **Eight arms in the ancestral complex:** *Hervarar saga* and the language of *Gautreks saga*.
- **Mutilated hero versus mutilated ancestor:** unresolved variation, not two stages of a verifiable life.

### Parentage and upbringing

In *Gautreks saga*, Starkad is the son of **Stórvirkr**, a warrior associated with King Haraldr of Agðir. Stórvirkr abducts Unnr, daughter of Freki; her brothers kill both parents, and young Starkad is fostered at Haraldr’s court alongside Víkar. After Haraldr is killed by Herþjófr, Víkar and Starkad eventually avenge him. [Later canonical Icelandic elaboration.]

Saxo names Starkad’s father Storwerk/Storuerkus in the human genealogy but also reports giant descent and an eastern birthplace. His chronology and relationships differ sufficiently that a single family tree cannot be imposed upon both sources.

### Service to Víkar

*Gautreks saga* makes Víkar and Starkad foster-brothers. Starkad becomes Víkar’s foremost champion, high-seat companion, counselor and coastal defender. The embedded *Víkarsbálkr* credits them with victories over Herþjófr, Sísarr of Kiev, Geirþjófr and Friðþjófr. Starkad fights without mail and hews with both hands. Víkar gives him a three-mark gold ring; Starkad gives Víkar the island Þruma. He says he served him for fifteen summers. [Later canonical elaboration supported inside the saga by attributed verse.]

The verses are not eyewitness testimony. Finnur Jónsson dated *Víkarsbálkr* no earlier than the thirteenth century and rejected Axel Olrik’s linguistic case for a twelfth-century date. Modern study treats the prose-and-verse complex as medieval legendary historiography, though individual motifs may be older. [Modern textual criticism.]

### Odin’s gifts and Thor’s curses

When contrary winds detain Víkar’s fleet, lots show that Odin demands a man hanged; Víkar’s lot repeatedly appears. That night Starkad’s foster-father **Hrosshárs-Grani**—“Horsehair-Grani”—takes him to an island assembly. The foster-father occupies the twelfth seat and is greeted as Odin. Odin and Thor then alternate decrees:

| Odin’s gift | Thor’s counter-curse |
|---|---|
| Three human lifetimes | One shameful deed in each lifetime |
| The best weapons and clothes | No land or landed possession |
| Abundant movable wealth | He will never think he has enough |
| Victory and excellence in every battle | A severe wound in every battle |
| Poetry composed as quickly as speech | He will not remember his poems |
| Esteem among the noblest and best | Hatred among common people |
| — | No son or daughter; his line ends |

Thor’s first curse is motivated by Álfhildr, Starkad’s paternal grandmother, having preferred a “dog-wise giant” to Thor. [Later canonical Icelandic elaboration.]

Saxo has a simpler scheme. Odin makes Starkad strong and poetically gifted so that he can kill Víkar and grants **three human lifetimes conditional on three detestable deeds**. Saxo does not reproduce the complete alternating list. [Parallel canonical variant.]

### Víkar’s sacrifice

In *Gautreks saga*, Odin/Hrosshárs-Grani demands Víkar as payment. He gives Starkad a spear that appears to be a reed. The king’s advisers agree to stage only a memorial imitation of sacrifice:

- a thin pine branch for the gallows;
- calf intestines for the noose;
- a reed for the spear.

Starkad puts the loop around Víkar, touches him with the reed and says, **“Nú gef ek þik Óðni”**—“Now I give you to Odin.” The reed becomes a spear and passes through the king; the intestines become a strong withy; the branch springs upright and hangs him. Starkad becomes hated and an exile. In *Víkarsbálkr* he calls this the deed that grieves him most. [Later canonical elaboration.]

Saxo removes much of the miracle. A wicker noose unexpectedly tightens and strangles Wicar; Starkad finishes the dying king with his sword. Saxo explicitly rejects the alternative report that pliant withies became iron-hard. The killing is simultaneously a hanging and a stabbing, the combination associated elsewhere with Odinic victims; this supports interpretation of the episode as narrative ritual, but it does not prove that an historical Víkar was sacrificed. [Medieval rationalization; modern ritual interpretation.]

### Warrior, poet and moral censor

After Víkar, the Icelandic saga sends Starkad to Eiríkr and Alrekr at Uppsala and then on Viking expeditions. Saxo greatly expands the itinerary. Starkad fights in Denmark, Sweden, Norway, Saxony, Russia and the eastern Baltic; kills champions including Wisinus and Hama; serves Frotho; protects or admonishes young rulers; and repeatedly condemns luxury, cooks, actors, effeminacy and foreign courtly manners.

His most famous Saxonian role concerns **Ingeld**. Starkad sees Ingeld feasting with the sons of Swerting, whose father killed Ingeld’s father Frotho. Through a long invective or exhortatory poem, he shames Ingeld into killing them. This resembles the unfinished Danish-Heaðobard vengeance foreseen in *Beowulf*, where an unnamed old warrior awakens a young man’s resentment over inherited weapons. The shared plot establishes related heroic tradition; it does not establish that *Beowulf*’s speaker “is Starkad.” [Comparative reconstruction.]

Saxo repeatedly makes Starkad a spokesman for austere, archaic masculinity. The speeches are also Saxo’s Latin literary compositions, shaped by classical rhetoric and Christian moralizing. They cannot simply be back-translated into lost pagan lays.

### The three shameful deeds

The tradition agrees on a triadic moral fall but not on every detail.

1. **Víkar’s killing:** present in both Saxo and *Gautreks saga*.
2. **Flight or failure after a king’s death:** in Saxo’s scheme Starkad abandons the field after the death of his king Olo/Áli; the act violates the retainer’s duty. Some reconstructions order and identify the second crime differently because Saxo’s narrative is dispersed.
3. **Killing a king for money:** Starkad accepts gold to murder King Olo/Áli or another royal lord, depending on the reconstruction of Saxo’s sequence. His eventual death is arranged as expiation and vengeance.

Dumézil summarized the offenses as violations internal to the warrior’s relation to sovereignty: sacrificing one’s king, deserting the king’s cause, and selling the king’s life. That elegant triad is an interpretation of Saxo’s structure, not wording shared by every source. [Modern scholarly reconstruction.]

### Sigurðr and the lost teeth

*Norna-Gests þáttr* moves Starkad into the Völsung cycle. The very old Starkad encounters Sigurðr Fáfnisbani; Sigurðr strikes him with Gramr and knocks out two teeth. A later antiquarian tradition claimed that one enormous tooth weighed seven ounces. The encounter is absent from the Poetic Edda’s Sigurðr lays and *Völsunga saga* proper. [Late medieval cross-cycle elaboration; later folklore.]

### Brávellir

In Saxo VIII and *Sögubrot*, the elderly Starkad fights at the legendary Battle of Brávellir, conventionally placed by medieval genealogists in the eighth century but not securely historical. He fights for the Danish king Harald Wartooth against Sigurðr Hring and receives severe wounds. Saxo’s battle catalogue and the Norse fragment overlap but do not provide contemporary evidence for an eighth-century battle. Starkad’s participation is one mechanism by which his three lifetimes join separate heroic ages. [Later legendary historiography.]

### Old age and death

Saxo gives the only full death narrative. Starkad is ancient, nearly blind and eager to die by a worthy hand. Carrying gold obtained through his final betrayal, he encounters **Hatherus/Haðr**, whose father he had killed. Starkad offers Hather the money and his sword in return for decapitation, satisfying both vengeance and payment. He tells Hather that jumping between the falling head and trunk will make him invulnerable. Saxo declines to decide whether this is instruction or a final attempt to crush his killer beneath the enormous corpse.

Hather decapitates him but wisely does not jump. Starkad’s severed head bites the earth. Hather buries the body on the field called **Rølung/Roljung**. [Later canonical elaboration.]

No source documents a competing natural death. Later “grave” and sword localizations depend on this story, not on an excavated named burial.

## 4. Relations with Gods and Other Heroes

### Genealogical variants

**Hervarar–Gautreks system**

```text
Starkaðr Áludrengr (giant) + Álfhildr, daughter of Álfr
                         │
                       Grímr
                         │
                    Stórvirkr
                         │
                 Starkaðr Stórvirksson
```

Some recensions compress or alter intermediate generations. Thor kills the giant Starkad in *Hervarar saga*; the later Starkad inherits his giant marks in *Gautreks saga*. [Regional variant.]

**Saxo’s compressed system**

```text
Giants
  │
Starcatherus / Starkad, son of Storwerk
(six-armed until Thor removes four; granted three lives by Odin)
```

[Learned systematization and conflation.]

Starkad has no securely attested wife, son or daughter. *Gautreks saga* makes this childlessness a direct curse from Thor.

## 5. Cult, Tomb and Ritual

### Cult

No inscription, excavated sanctuary, votive object, sacrificial deposit, priesthood, festival or medieval testimony securely documents **worship of Starkad**. He is never demonstrably a cult hero in the manner of a localized divine or ancestral recipient. His relation to ritual is narrative:

- Thor mutilates or kills his giant form.
- Odin determines his fate.
- Starkad performs Víkar’s hanging-and-spearing.
- Álfhildr is abducted during a dísablót.

The Víkar episode may preserve knowledge of sacrificial symbolism, especially dedication to Odin by hanging and spear, but it is not evidence for a “cult of Starkad.” H. M. Chadwick’s older formulation of Starkad as a figure of “the cult of Othin” describes mythic affiliation, not an archaeologically identified cult. [Modern scholarly interpretation.]

### Tomb

Saxo says Hather buried Starkad at Rølung. He also reports a stone dented by Starkad’s seated weight. These are literary place-legends. No excavated barrow has yielded an inscription or securely proven association with him.

The late-thirteenth-century *Annales Ryenses* reportedly localized Starkad’s sword beneath the bridge at **Boilyngh**, visible when water was low. Later writers associated the death-place with the Rönne river or northwestern Skåne. Sixteenth-century tradition knew “Starkkarl’s stones” near Vegeholm. These are successive localizations of a literary hero, not cumulative archaeological confirmation. [Medieval and early-modern accretion.]

### Folklore

Axel Olrik and Arthur Stille collected Starkad place-legends in northwestern Skåne. Magnus Brostrup Landstad reported that a Starkad story was still remembered in upper Telemark in the nineteenth century, though the tradition was already fading. Giant teeth, footprint or body-impression stones and putative graves belong to familiar landscape-folklore mechanisms. [Folklore.]

## 6. Historical Evolution of the Figure

### Possible tenth-century mythic residue

The Vetrliði stanza, if correctly transmitted and attributed, associates Thor with striking Starkad by the late tenth century. It probably concerns a giant adversary, not necessarily Víkar’s retainer. No contemporary inscription, image or prose biography survives. [Potential securely attested early material.]

### Eleventh–twelfth-century oral and poetic formation

Comparative source criticism reconstructs multiple lays: a giant/Thor narrative, a Víkar lay, Danish royal poetry and a warrior’s old-age cycle. Axel Olrik assigned some lost Starkad poems to the eleventh and twelfth centuries. Because these poems survive only through Saxo’s Latin paraphrases or later saga verse, their number, wording and dates remain hypothetical. [Modern reconstruction.]

### Early thirteenth century: Saxo’s monumental synthesis

Saxo joins mythic, Danish, Swedish and Norwegian materials into one long life. His Starkad is simultaneously admired and condemned: a defender of martial loyalty, an enemy of luxury and an instrument of pagan deception. Saxo euhemerizes Thor and Odin as magicians who falsely obtained divine honor, rejects some marvels, but retains the three lifetimes and prodigious feats. [Christian learned systematization.]

### Thirteenth–fourteenth centuries: Icelandic diversification

*Gautreks saga* organizes the Víkar material around a divine þing and alternating decrees. *Hervarar saga* splits giant and hero genealogically. *Sögubrot* inserts him at Brávellir. *Norna-Gests þáttr* connects him to Sigurðr. *Skáldatal* canonizes him as an ancient poet. These witnesses do not preserve one stable saga but a network of reusable heroic modules. [Regional variants and learned synthesis.]

### Late medieval and early-modern localization

Annals, chronicles and landscape traditions place his sword, grave and stone-impressions in Skåne. Olaus Magnus reproduces Starkad for a European Latin readership, illustrates him as a northern warrior and identifies him as Tavastian. Printed editions of Saxo stabilize *Starcatherus* as a learned antiquarian figure. [Medieval/early-modern accretion.]

### Nineteenth and early twentieth centuries

National philology treated Starkad as evidence for ancient Danish or pan-Germanic heroic poetry. Scholars sought historical tribal conflicts behind Saxo and *Beowulf*, separated multiple Starkads and reconstructed lost lays. Popular national mythologies retold him as “the Nordic Heracles.” These reconstructions often predated stricter manuscript and oral-traditional analysis. [Modern nationalism and scholarship.]

### Late twentieth century to present

Dumézil made Starkad a major comparative Indo-European warrior, while saga scholarship increasingly emphasized redaction, genre, prosimetrum and medieval historiography. Fantasy fiction and games detach the extra-armed giant from the morally divided royal retainer, often making him simply a gigantic being killed by Thor. [Modern reinvention.]

## 7. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Scholarship as reception

Georges Dumézil’s *Mythe et épopée II* (1971), translated in part as *The Stakes of the Warrior* (1983), compares Starkad with the Indian Śiśupāla and interprets him through Indo-European functional ideology. Odin represents sovereignty and magical-intellectual power; Thor partly represents the physically forceful and socially broader order. Starkad becomes the “stake” in their contest, embodying the greatness and structural sins of the warrior function. The three offenses destroy the warrior’s proper relation to his king.

The comparison is influential but is not a recovered Proto-Indo-European text. Critics question selection of parallels, dependence on late Scandinavian sources and the risk that a tripartite model predetermines the reading. Dumézil’s political biography and the later appropriation of trifunctional ideology by parts of the European radical right also require separation from the evidentiary merits of any particular comparison. [Modern scholarly reconstruction and contested intellectual reception.]

James Milroy’s “Starkaðr: An Essay in Interpretation” (1974–77) reads the saga as a criticism of excessive civilization and luxury and a celebration of endurance under moral strain. Later scholars have instead emphasized textual plurality, Christian authorship and the narrative work performed by attributed verse.

### Literature and visual art

- Olaus Magnus’s *Historia de gentibus septentrionalibus* (1555) includes an early printed visualization of Starkad in battle. It illustrates sixteenth-century reception, not ancient iconography.
- Nineteenth- and early-twentieth-century Scandinavian mythological handbooks and illustrated heroic histories turned him into a national-age super-warrior.
- Poul Anderson’s *Ensign Flandry* (1966) names a fictional planet **Starkad**; this is nominal borrowing, not adaptation of the saga.
- Bernard King’s fantasy trilogy—*Starkadder* (1985), *Vargr-Moon* (1986) and *Death-Blinder* (1988)—freely reworks Norse materials around a Starkad figure.
- *God of War* (2018) mentions **Starkaðr the Mighty** as a Jötunn with eight arms; Mímir says Thor tore off six and killed him. This merges and simplifies the *Hervarar saga*, Saxo and *Gautreks saga* variants. [Modern invention.]

No major opera, canonical feature film or continuous iconographic tradition comparable to those of Siegfried or Beowulf was located. Claims that modern fantasy images reproduce an ancient visual type are unsupported: no securely identified pre-Christian image of Starkad exists.

### Political appropriation

Starkad participated indirectly in nineteenth-century Danish and Scandinavian heroic nationalism through Saxo editions, national literary histories and claims for an ancient national epic. Searches for a distinct modern extremist cult, organization or widely documented white-supremacist emblem centered on Starkad produced no reliable evidence. General far-right use of Norse names and Odinism does not justify attributing a specific Starkad appropriation without documentation. Dumézil’s work has been appropriated by “Euro-nationalist” theorists, but that is reception of trifunctionalism, not evidence that Starkad himself is a standard extremist symbol. [Absence of evidence; contested modern reception.]

## 8. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Historicity

No contemporary annal, inscription, datable grave or external record establishes a historical Starkad. Medieval genealogists place him across periods too distant for one ordinary lifespan, and the three-life explanation is internal to legend.

Three broad scholarly positions have appeared:

1. **Historical kernel:** one or more remembered warriors or court poets accumulated marvels.
2. **Mythic origin:** a giant or Odinic warrior was historicized into royal chronicle.
3. **Composite literary figure:** independent giant, Víkar, Danish-retainer and Brávellir materials were joined because the same or similar name circulated.

The third describes the textual evidence most cautiously, but none is provable as the figure’s “real origin.” [Modern scholarly debate.]

### Dating *Víkarsbálkr*

Older scholars sometimes treated the verses as ancient testimony authenticated by prose. Finnur Jónsson dated the poem no earlier than the thirteenth century and rejected Olrik’s placement in the twelfth. Current analysis of fornaldarsaga prosimetrum warns that verse can simulate historical authority and need not predate its prose setting by centuries. [Textual-critical debate.]

### Saxo and lost lays

Saxo says or implies that Starkad composed songs and renders long speeches in Latin verse. Scholars widely accept that he knew vernacular traditions, but it is impossible to recover exact lost Old Norse originals mechanically from his classicizing Latin. His rhetoric, Christian polemic and political interest in Danish monarchy actively reshape the material. [State of evidence.]

### *Beowulf* and the Heaðobards

The strong parallel is structural:

- a Danish king has married into or accommodated an enemy family;
- inherited weapons recall an unresolved killing;
- an old warrior rekindles vengeance;
- Ingeld abandons peace.

Saxo names the inciter Starkad; *Beowulf* does not. Bugge’s “strong Heaðobard” etymology and the identification of the unnamed *Beowulf* warrior with Starkad are ingenious but unconfirmed. Klaeber’s apparatus records the theory rather than establishing it. Tolkien’s work supports reading *Beowulf* as a poem, not mining it uncritically as a historical index. [Contested comparative reconstruction.]

### Myth and ritual

Víkar’s simultaneous hanging and spearing resembles Odin’s self-sacrifice in *Hávamál* and other narrative dedications to Odin. Scholars have therefore read the episode as an Odinic sacrificial complex or sacral-kingship narrative. The source, however, is a Christian-period legendary saga. It attests medieval knowledge or construction of such symbolism, not the actual performance of this sacrifice at a named archaeological site. [Modern reconstruction.]

### Dumézil

Dumézil’s comparison illuminates the patterned opposition of Odin’s gifts and Thor’s curses and the three royal betrayals. Objections include:

- dependence on late, internally divergent sources;
- converting flexible narrative variation into a single inherited structure;
- selective comparison with Śiśupāla;
- uncertain mapping of Thor onto a fixed functional position;
- insufficient allowance for Saxo’s and the sagas’ medieval literary agendas.

His interpretation remains important as historiography, not settled origin-history.

### Manuscripts and losses

- The *Codex Regius* securely anchors the Helgi name by c.1270.
- Saxo’s original manuscript is lost; medieval fragments and later copies preserve the text.
- *Gautreks saga* is especially problematic because its most extensive witnesses are late paper manuscripts and its short and long recensions differ.
- *Hervarar saga* survives in distinct R, H and U textual forms; the Starkad genealogy belongs particularly to prologue material whose form varies.
- *Sögubrot* is, as its name indicates, fragmentary.
- No archaeological discovery has converted Starkad from legendary dossier to historical biography.

### Discredited or overstated claims still circulating

- “Starkad is named in *Beowulf*”: false.
- “His eight arms are agreed by all sources”: false; Saxo gives six, other traditions eight or ancestor-based traces.
- “Thor tore off six arms in the medieval texts”: this is a modern harmonization of an eight-armed figure with survival of two; Saxo explicitly says four were torn from a six-armed body.
- “The reed accidently became a spear”: misleading; in *Gautreks saga* Odin knowingly gives the disguised spear and orders the killing.
- “Starkad’s grave has been found”: unsupported.
- “*Víkarsbálkr* is Starkad’s eyewitness autobiography”: medieval attribution, not verified authorship.
- “The legendary chronology proves an eighth-century lifespan”: circular use of medieval genealogies.
- “Starkad had an ancient cult”: no evidence located.
