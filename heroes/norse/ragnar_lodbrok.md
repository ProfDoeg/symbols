# Ragnar Lodbrok: Research Dossier

**Traditional class:** mortal heroic king and Viking war-leader; more specifically a legendary Danish–Swedish *sagnkonge* (“legendary king”), serpent-slayer, dynastic ancestor, and father of conquerors. He is never securely attested as a god, demigod, saint, or cult-receiving hero.

**Earliest secure attestation:** Ari Þorgilsson’s Old Icelandic *Íslendingabók*, composed c. 1122–1133, twice names **Ragnarr loðbrók**: first as father of Ívarr, who “caused St Edmund, king of the English, to be killed,” and then in Ari’s genealogy through Ragnar’s son Sigurðr. The work survives only in seventeenth-century paper copies. Earlier, c. 1070s witnesses mention a father named **Lothbrocus/Lodparchus** but do not call him Ragnar; a ninth-century *Ragnarsdrápa* addresses a Ragnar, but its identification with Ragnar Lodbrok is a later attribution, not a secure contemporary attestation. [Íslendingabók text](https://is.wikisource.org/wiki/%C3%8Dslendingab%C3%B3k); [Árni Magnússon Institute manuscript account](https://www.arnastofnun.is/is/greinar/islendingabok-am-113-g-fol); [Skaldic Poetry Project on *Ragnarsdrápa*](https://skaldic.org/db.php?id=1132&if=default&table=text)

## Evidence labels used

- **[EARLY]** securely attested material of the Viking Age or approximately contemporary external evidence.
- **[EARLY?]** material possibly early in origin but preserved or attributed only later.
- **[CANONICAL]** the principal medieval literary formation of the Ragnar legend, chiefly c. 1200–1400.
- **[REGIONAL]** a geographically distinctive variant.
- **[SYSTEMATIZATION]** learned genealogical or historiographic organization of disparate traditions.
- **[ACCRETION]** later medieval or early-modern enlargement.
- **[FOLKLORE]** oral, ballad, or local tradition not demonstrably early.
- **[RECONSTRUCTION]** a modern scholarly historical or comparative hypothesis.
- **[INVENTION]** a demonstrably modern creation or conflation.

## 1. Identification, Names and Epithets

Ragnar is the organizing center of a medieval Scandinavian legendary complex, not a recoverable biography. In its mature Icelandic form he is Ragnarr, son of Sigurðr hringr, ruler of Denmark and Sweden; slayer of the serpent guarding Þóra; husband first of Þóra and later of the disguised Völsung princess Áslaug/Kráka; father of Ívarr the Boneless, Bjǫrn Ironside, Hvítserkr, Rǫgnvaldr, and Sigurðr Snake-in-the-Eye; and a defeated invader of Northumbria who dies in King Ælla’s snake-pit. Saxo Grammaticus offers a divergent Danish synthesis: Regnerus has several wives—including Lagertha, Thora, Suanlogha, and a daughter of Esbern—and a much larger, internally inconsistent set of sons. Neither version is contemporary with the purported ninth-century events.

### Names

| Form | Language and period | Meaning/status |
|---|---|---|
| **Ragnarr** | Old Norse | A dithematic Germanic personal name, normally analyzed from *ragin-* “counsel, decision of the gods/powers” and *-hari/*-herr “army, warrior.” It is an ordinary personal name and cannot by itself identify the legendary figure. |
| **Ragnarr loðbrók** | Old Icelandic, securely in Ari c. 1122–1133 | The canonical Norse form. |
| **loðbrók / loðbróka** | Old Norse byname or name-form | Conventionally “shaggy/hairy breeches”: *loð* “shag, fleece” + *brók* “breeches.” The saga explains it through fur garments boiled in pitch and worn against Þóra’s serpent. The grammatical and transmission history is disputed: some manuscript language points toward a feminine **Loðbróka**, and Rory McTurk proposed that the composite Ragnar-Loðbrók legend arose by joining a Ragnar to a separate female or taboo-name tradition. | **Lodparchus** | Adam of Bremen, c. 1070s | Father of the exceptionally cruel *Inguar*. This may reflect metathesis or textual transmission of Loðbrók, perhaps through William, but again Adam supplies no Ragnar. **[EARLY PRECURSOR/SYSTEMATIZATION]** The dependence of Adam upon William is debated. ### Patronymics and titles

- **Ragnarr Sigurðarson**, “Ragnar son of Sigurðr,” follows from the Icelandic designation of Sigurðr hringr as his father. **[CANONICAL]**
- **Danakonungr**, king of the Danes, and sometimes ruler of both Denmark and Sweden. **[CANONICAL/SYSTEMATIZATION]**
- Later popular “King Ragnar of Norway” is usually **[INVENTION]** or confusion. The central medieval sources make Denmark and/or Sweden his principal realms.
- No medieval source uses “Ragnar Lothbrok” as a modern family surname shared by his household.

### Syncretistic identifications

1. **Ragnar = the patron of Bragi’s *Ragnarsdrápa*.** Snorri Sturluson makes this equation in the thirteenth century. The surviving poem names Ragnar and “Sigurðr’s son,” making the equation possible, but modern editors treat it as uncertain. **[MEDIEVAL ATTRIBUTION / EARLY?]** [Skaldic Project](https://skaldic.org/db.php?id=1132&if=default&table=text)

2. **Ragnar = Reginheri, Paris raider of 845.** Common from nineteenth-century historicist scholarship onward; supported principally by the name and appropriate general milieu, opposed by the lack of the byname, kinship, snake-pit, or saga exploits in contemporary records. **[RECONSTRUCTION]**

3. **Ragnar as composite.** Modern scholars have treated the literary hero as an aggregation of Reginheri, traditions about Björn, Ívarr/Ímar, Halfdan, Ubba, a Loðbróka figure, and generic serpent-slayer and death-song motifs. **[RECONSTRUCTION]**

4. **Ragnar as descendant/in-law of the Völsungs.** The Icelandic saga joins him through Áslaug to Sigurðr Fáfnisbani and Brynhildr. This is a medieval literary-genealogical bridge, not an independently attested historical kinship. **[CANONICAL/SYSTEMATIZATION]**

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### The earliest evidence

#### Ninth-century records

**[EARLY] Reginheri and Paris, 845.** The *Annals of St-Bertin* report that 120 Northman ships reached Paris and that Charles the Bald paid 7,000 pounds of silver for their departure; the annal’s basic account does not establish a Ragnar Loðbrók. The *Annals of Xanten* name a leader Reginheri and report his death by divine punishment later in 845. Other hagiographic narratives about the raid embroider the Vikings’ subsequent disease and panic. These are evidence for a historical Reginheri, not for Þóra, Áslaug, Ragnar’s sons, or a Northumbrian snake-pit. [Annals of St-Bertin translation](https://sourcebooks.web.fordham.edu/source/843bertin.asp); [Ferdinand Lot’s source study](https://www.persee.fr/doc/bec_0373-6237_1909_num_70_1_448366)

**[EARLY?] Bragi Boddason’s *Ragnarsdrápa*.** Fragments preserved in Snorri’s *Edda* describe mythic scenes on a shield given by a patron Ragnar: Hamðir and Sǫrli, Hjaðningavíg, Þórr fishing for the Midgard Serpent, and Gefjon’s ploughing of Zealand. If Bragi and the verses are ninth-century, they are exceptionally early testimony to a Ragnar, but the surviving attribution and identification with Loðbrók are thirteenth-century. The poem does not narrate hairy breeches, Þóra’s serpent, Áslaug, the sons, or the snake-pit. **It is therefore not the earliest secure attestation of “Ragnar Lodbrok.”** [Critical text and commentary](https://skaldic.org/db.php?id=1132&if=default&table=text)

**[EARLY] The Great Army and its leaders, 865 onward.** The *Anglo-Saxon Chronicle* records a *micel/micel hæþen here*, a “great heathen army,” entering East Anglia, taking York, and campaigning through the English kingdoms. Contemporary or near-contemporary traditions attest leaders including Halfdan; later English recensions and hagiography name Ingware/Hinguar and Ubba. They do not say that the army came to avenge Ragnar. [Chronicle timeline](https://www.anglo-saxons.net/hwaet/?do=get&from=865&to=880&type=chron)

**[EARLY] Ímar in Irish annals.** The *Annals of Ulster* record Ímar as “king of the Northmen of all Ireland and Britain” at his death in 873. The later identification of Ímar with Ívarr the Boneless is plausible but disputed; the contemporary Irish annals do not name Ragnar as his father. [Discussion of Ímar and the Irish record](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%8Dmar)

#### Tenth- and eleventh-century precursors

- **Abbo of Fleury, *Passio sancti Eadmundi*, c. 985–987:** Hinguar and Hubba murder Edmund; Ragnar is absent. Abbo says his information came through Archbishop Dunstan from an old armor-bearer. **[EARLY HAGIOGRAPHIC PRECURSOR]** [Translation](https://torrencia.org/edmund/lattrans.html); [study of the dating](https://digital.library.leeds.ac.uk/14116/1/LSE2005.pdf)
- **William of Jumièges, *Gesta Normannorum ducum*, c. 1050s–1070:** in a redaction, *Bier Coste de Fer* is son of *Lothburcus/Lothbrocus*. **[EARLY PRECURSOR]**
- **Adam of Bremen, *Gesta Hammaburgensis*, c. 1070s:** calls Inguar son of Lodparchus; Ragnar is absent. **[EARLY PRECURSOR/SYSTEMATIZATION]**
- These witnesses show that a father-Loðbrók tradition was attached to famous Viking leaders before Ari, but not conclusively that “Ragnar Lodbrok” was already the unified hero.

### Corpora named in the brief: actual relationship to Ragnar

- **Poetic Edda:** Ragnar does not appear as an active character. The heroic poems supply Sigurðr, Brynhildr, Guðrún, Gunnarr, and the older snake-pit motif. *Atlakviða* and *Oddrúnargrátr* place Gunnarr in a snake-pit; this is an important possible literary antecedent to Ragnar’s death. **[COMPARATIVE CONTEXT, NOT DIRECT ATTESTATION]**
- **Völsunga saga:** connects Ragnar indirectly through Áslaug. **[CANONICAL LINKAGE]**
- **Beowulf:** contains no Ragnar Lodbrok. Its Danish and Geatish heroic world, genealogy, gift exchange, feud, and monster combat are comparative Germanic context only. Klaeber’s edition and Tolkien’s “Beowulf: The Monsters and the Critics” do not make Ragnar a character. **Absence of direct evidence is the finding.**
- **Nibelungenlied:** contains Siegfried and Brünhild traditions but no Ragnar or Áslaug. Its relationship is collateral through the wider Sigurðr/Siegfried complex. **[COMPARATIVE, NOT GENEALOGICAL EVIDENCE]**
- **Þiðreks saga:** reworks continental heroic material around Sigurðr and Brynhildr but does not document a meeting with Ragnar. **[COMPARATIVE]**
- **Grettis saga, Egils saga, the family sagas generally:** no independent Ragnar narrative. Their value lies in genealogical memory, allusion, saga poetics, and reception of legendary ancestry.
- **Gautreks saga:** belongs to the same *fornaldarsaga* environment and intersecting heroic genealogies; it is not a principal witness for Ragnar’s deeds.
- The Icelandic saga corpus and *Landnámabók* nevertheless contain numerous genealogical references to Ragnar’s kin. Hauksbók places its Ragnar material within a program of Icelandic settlement history and prestigious descent. [Study of Hauksbók genealogy](https://api.repository.cam.ac.uk/server/api/core/bitstreams/96c2d651-37cc-43d4-a555-23a863a8cb98/content)

## 3. The Story

## 3.1 Birth, ancestry, and succession

**Icelandic canonical version.** Ragnar is son of **Sigurðr hringr**, ruler of Denmark and Sweden. Through *Hervarar saga* and related genealogies, Sigurðr descends from Randvér and the expansive dynasty of Ívarr víðfaðmi. Ragnar succeeds after Sigurðr’s death. **[CANONICAL/SYSTEMATIZATION]**

**Saxo’s version.** Regnerus is son of Siward Ring and grandson of Ingeld. Saxo situates him in a continuous Danish royal history and gives him an implausibly extensive imperial career. **[REGIONAL/SYSTEMATIZATION]**

There is no securely contemporary evidence for Ragnar’s parentage, accession, or joint rule of Denmark and Sweden.

## 3.2 Þóra and the serpent

In *Ragnars saga*, Jarl **Herruðr** of Gautland gives his daughter **Þóra borgarhjǫrtr** a small snake. It grows around her bower until it becomes a dragon-like menace. Herruðr promises Þóra to whoever kills it. Ragnar has woolly breeches and a cloak made and boiled in pitch, attacks with spear and shield, kills the serpent, and wins Þóra. His protective clothing produces the explanatory byname **loðbrók**, “shaggy breeches.” **[CANONICAL]**

In Saxo, **Herodd**, king of the Swedes, is troubled by serpents reared by his daughter **Thora**. Ragnar obtains a woolen mantle and very hairy thigh-coverings, freezes them, kills the serpents with spear and sword, and marries Thora. **[REGIONAL VARIANT]**

The episode belongs to the widespread “hero wins maiden by slaying a serpent” pattern. Its use as literal etymology for the byname is narrative aetiology, not linguistic proof.

Þóra bears Ragnar **Eiríkr** and **Agni** in *Ragnars saga*. She dies of illness. Saxo distributes Ragnar’s sons differently and makes Thora the mother of Rathbarthus, Dunwat, Siward, Biorn, Agnar, and Ivar. The lists should not be harmonized.

## 3.3 Áslaug/Kráka/Randalín

In the saga complex, **Áslaug** is daughter of **Sigurðr Fáfnisbani** and **Brynhildr Buðladóttir**. After her parents’ deaths, the fosterer **Heimir** conceals her inside a harp. At Spangereid, the peasant couple **Áki** and **Gríma** murder Heimir for presumed treasure, discover the child, call her **Kráka** (“crow”), and raise her in poverty while concealing her beauty. **[CANONICAL LINKAGE TO THE VÖLSUNGS]**

Ragnar’s men see Kráka bathing. Ragnar orders her to come:

- neither clothed nor naked,
- neither fasting nor fed,
- neither alone nor accompanied.

She arrives wrapped in a fishing net, with her hair covering her, having bitten an onion, and accompanied by a dog. Ragnar recognizes her cleverness and later marries her. This is a form of the international “clever peasant girl” tale. **[CANONICAL/FOLKTALE MOTIF]**

When Ragnar proposes to marry **Ingibjǫrg**, daughter of King **Eysteinn beli**, Áslaug reveals her lineage and predicts that her unborn son will bear a serpent image in his eye. **Sigurðr ormr-í-auga** is born with the mark, vindicating her. Ragnar abandons the Swedish marriage. He calls Áslaug **Randalín** when she accompanies the sons to war. **[CANONICAL]**

Saxo does not know this Áslaug/Kráka marriage. His wives are differently arranged. Consequently, “Lagertha then Þóra then Áslaug” is a modern combination of Saxo and Icelandic materials, not one medieval source’s sequence.

## 3.4 The sons

### *Ragnars saga*

- By Þóra: **Eiríkr** and **Agni**.
- By Áslaug: **Ívarr inn beinlausi**, **Bjǫrn járnsíða**, **Hvítserkr**, **Rǫgnvaldr**, and **Sigurðr ormr-í-auga**.

Ívarr’s “bonelessness” is not medically explained in the saga with certainty. Interpretations as disability, impotence, extraordinary flexibility, or misunderstood epithet are modern hypotheses.

Bjǫrn’s “Ironside” signifies exceptional protection or toughness. The eleventh-century *Bier Coste de Fer* tradition is earlier than the complete Ragnar saga but does not securely prove the later genealogy.

Sigurðr’s snake-mark makes the Völsung ancestry visible on his body. It is a literary dynastic credential.

### Saxo

Saxo assigns Ragnar a larger roster, including **Fridleif**, daughters whose names are not preserved in the same way, **Ubbe**, **Rathbarthus**, **Dunwat**, **Siward**, **Biorn**, **Agnar**, **Ivar**, **Regnald**, **Hwitserk**, and **Erik**, with maternal assignments that contradict the Icelandic saga. **[REGIONAL SYSTEMATIZATION]**

### Historical caution

Ímar/Ingware, Halfdan, Ubba, and a Björn are individually plausible or securely attested Viking-Age actors. Their collective status as brothers and Ragnar’s sons is a later genealogical claim. Medieval tradition may have made Ragnar the father of famous leaders in the same way heroic genealogies regularly concentrate dispersed achievements into one dynasty.

## 3.5 Eysteinn beli and the enchanted cow

In *Ragnars saga*, Ragnar’s sons Eiríkr and Agni attack King **Eysteinn** after Ragnar’s rejected marriage arrangement. Eysteinn deploys the sacred or magical cow **Sibilja**, whose bellowing breaks enemy morale. Eiríkr is captured and chooses death by being raised on spear-points; Agni dies in battle. Áslaug/Randalín leads her own sons in vengeance; Sibilja is killed and Eysteinn defeated. Young Sigurðr participates precociously. **[CANONICAL FANTASTIC EPISODE]**

This episode has no contemporary historical corroboration. It serves dynastic and heroic functions: Þóra’s sons are removed, Áslaug’s sons inherit narrative centrality, and Áslaug demonstrates martial leadership.

## 3.6 Warfare and empire

*Krákumál* places its speaker in a geographically expansive heroic world: combats near the Baltic, Britain, Scotland, and continental shores. It opens with the refrain **“Hjoggum vér með hjörvi”**, “We struck with the sword,” and accumulates remembered battles rather than presenting a reliable itinerary. **[REGIONAL HEROIC POETRY]**

Saxo expands Ragnar into a near-world conqueror who fights or rules in Denmark, Sweden, Norway, Britain, Ireland, Saxony, the Baltic, Bjarmaland, and the “Hellespont.” He uses brazen horses on wheels against an eastern army and promulgates military laws. **[LEARNED EPIC SYSTEMATIZATION]** These campaigns combine geography, heroic motifs, annalistic fragments, and names of historically separate Viking leaders.

The Icelandic saga is more interested in marriage, sons, and vengeance than in Ragnar’s detailed western campaigns. Neither medieval account knows the television series’ invention that Ragnar personally discovered navigation to England or led the 793 Lindisfarne raid.

## 3.7 The final expedition and snake-pit

In *Ragnars saga*, Ragnar becomes jealous of his sons’ fame and sails to England with only two ships, despite Áslaug’s warning. She gives him a protective silken garment. King **Ælla** defeats his force; Ragnar, refusing to identify himself, is placed in a pit of snakes. The garment initially protects him, so it is removed. As he dies he says, in substance, that the piglets would grunt if they knew how the old boar suffered. Ælla realizes too late that his captive is Ragnar. **[CANONICAL]**

The saga does **not** embed the full twenty-nine-stanza *Krákumál* as the verbatim speech at this point. The popular “death song sung while dying” joins the saga’s death scene to the independent poem.

In *Krákumál*, the speaker remembers his battles, welcomes death, says Óðinn is preparing benches, and anticipates drinking ale with the Æsir. The famous English formula “laughing shall I die” is a translation/receptional condensation of the final heroic posture rather than an exact neutral prose statement. **[LATE TWELFTH-CENTURY POETIC DRAMATIZATION]**

Saxo likewise has Ælla capture Ragnar and kill him among snakes, but embeds the death in a Christian providential frame: Ragnar’s contempt for Christianity brings just punishment. Saxo also has Ragnar’s sons avenge him by cutting an eagle into Ælla’s back and salting the wound. **[REGIONAL/CHRISTIANIZED SYSTEMATIZATION]**

There is no ninth-century evidence for Ragnar dying in Northumbria, for Ælla executing him, for snakes, for a protective garment, or for the death speech. The snake-pit had an older heroic precedent in the death of Gunnarr at Atli’s hands in Eddic and Völsung tradition. Medieval Scandinavian images of a man among snakes generally identify Gunnarr, often through his harp; they cannot simply be reassigned to Ragnar. [Study of Gunnarr and snake-pit imagery](https://uni.hi.is/adalh/files/2013/01/Speculum.pdf)

## 3.8 Revenge and the Great Army

The historical *Anglo-Saxon Chronicle* says a great heathen army entered East Anglia in 865, wintered there, took horses, and seized York in 866; Ælla and Osberht died attacking York in 867. It does **not** say that Ragnar’s sons raised the army in vengeance. **[EARLY]**

Abbo’s c. 985–987 *Passio* makes Hinguar and Hubba killers of Edmund but supplies no Ragnar motive. Ari, writing c. 1122–1133, explicitly calls Ívarr Ragnar’s son. The full revenge structure is therefore later than the campaigns it explains.

In *Ragnars saga*, Ragnar’s sons first attack Ælla directly and are defeated. Ívarr then uses cunning: he asks for as much land as an ox-hide can cover, cuts it into a long thong, acquires a stronghold—identified with York—and builds political support before his brothers return. Ælla is defeated. **[CANONICAL]**

In *Ragnarssona þáttr* and Saxo, the sons perform the “blood eagle” on Ælla. The precise nature—and historicity—of the blood eagle is disputed; later prose may have elaborated compressed poetic imagery into anatomical torture. Even the modern anatomical defense of its physical possibility does not turn the Ragnar episode into historical fact. [Speculum study](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/10.1086/717332)

Thus:

- Great Army: **historical**.
- Named leaders such as Halfdan and, in later English witnesses, Ingware and Ubba: **historically grounded**.
- All leaders as biological sons of Ragnar: **later tradition**.
- Army formed specifically to avenge Ragnar: **medieval legendary explanation**.
- Blood-eagle execution of Ælla: **later literary tradition, historically unverified**.

## 3.9 The body

No reliable source records Ragnar’s burial, bones, relics, or transfer of his body. The saga’s snake-pit narrative ends with death and vengeance, not an archaeologically locatable grave. Claims to know “Ragnar’s grave” or the physical site of Ælla’s pit are **[MODERN INVENTION/TOURISTIC LOCALIZATION]** unless supported by evidence independent of the saga.

## 4. Relations with Gods and Other Heroes

## 4.1 Conflicting family structures

### Icelandic saga genealogy

```text
Sigurðr hringr
└── Ragnarr loðbrók
    ├── with Þóra borgarhjǫrtr
    │   ├── Eiríkr
    │   └── Agni
    └── with Áslaug/Kráka/Randalín
        ├── Ívarr inn beinlausi
        ├── Bjǫrn járnsíða
        ├── Hvítserkr
        ├── Rǫgnvaldr
        └── Sigurðr ormr-í-auga
```

Áslaug’s ancestry:

```text
Sigurðr Fáfnisbani + Brynhildr Buðladóttir
└── Áslaug/Kráka/Randalín
    └── children with Ragnar
```

**Date/status:** *Völsunga saga* plus *Ragnars saga*, thirteenth-century composition, manuscript c. 1400. **[CANONICAL]**

### *Ragnarssona þáttr*

The tale largely retains Sigurðr hringr as father and Áslaug’s major sons but rearranges events and concentrates upon the sons’ conquests and dynastic descendants. It connects Sigurðr Snake-in-the-Eye’s line to later Danish and Norwegian kings. **[SYSTEMATIZATION]**

### Saxo’s genealogy

Saxo gives Ragnar wives and partners including **Lagertha**, **Thora**, **Suanlogha**, and **Esbern’s unnamed daughter**. The maternal structure differs fundamentally from the Icelandic account. **[REGIONAL SYSTEMATIZATION]**

## 5. Cult, Tomb, and Ritual

### Cult

No secure archaeological, epigraphic, liturgical, or narrative evidence establishes an ancient cult of Ragnar Lodbrok.

There is no attested:

- *hof*, *hörgr*, or *heroon* dedicated to Ragnar;
- sacrificial calendar or feast in his name;
- oracle attached to him;
- authenticated relic;
- prehistoric or Viking-Age votive inscription invoking him;
- cult-title identifying him as a god;
- securely ancient grave bearing his name.

This negative finding matters. Ragnar is a heroic and genealogical figure, not demonstrably a heroized dead person with organized cult.

### Tomb and snake-pit

No known grave can be attributed to Ragnar. Viking-Age mounds popularly associated with Ragnar or his sons generally depend on later local identification. The Björn Ironside mound tradition on Munsö, for example, concerns Björn and remains an attribution rather than proof of Ragnar’s genealogy. [Ekerö heritage discussion](https://www.upplevekero.se/se--gora/vara-varldsarv/birka--hovgarden/bjorn-jarnsida)

No excavated structure in Northumbria is securely Ælla’s snake-pit. Modern maps, tourist claims, and game locations are **[INVENTION]**.

### Ritual within the stories

The texts nevertheless depict or imply ritual culture:

- *Krákumál* imagines post-mortem reception by Óðinn and drinking with the Æsir. **[MEDIEVAL POETIC REPRESENTATION]**
- Ragnar’s warfare is framed by vows, funerary honor, feasting, and revenge obligations.
- The blood eagle is presented in later texts as vengeance, and elsewhere in Old Norse literature can be interpreted as an offering to Óðinn; this does not prove a historical Ragnar ritual.
- Ragnar’s sons and descendants function genealogically as charter ancestors. That is social memory and political legitimation, not cult in the strict sense.

### Images

No Viking-Age image is securely identifiable as Ragnar. Snake-pit scenes on Gotland stones, church carvings, and textiles are normally assigned to **Gunnarr**, especially where a harp appears. Reidentifying them as Ragnar because he also dies among snakes is unwarranted.

Securely identified Ragnar imagery is principally modern:

- Hugo Hamilton, Scandinavian-history illustrations, 1830.
- August Malmström, *King Ælla’s Messenger before Ragnar Lodbrok’s Sons*, 1857.
- Mathias Skeibrok, *Ragnar Lodbrok in the Snake Pit*, sculpture, 1877.
- Malmström, *Ragnar Lodbrok in the Snake Pit*, drawing, 1870s, and illustrations for *Sagan om Ragnar Lodbrok*, 1880.
- Kristian Zahrtmann, snake-pit treatment, 1915.

These works visualize the literary legend during Scandinavian Romantic and national-historical culture; they are not ancient iconographic evidence. [Nationalmuseum record](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File%3AAugust_Malmstr%C3%B6m_-_Ragnar_Lothbrok_in_the_Snake_Pit_-_NMH_153-1929_-_Nationalmuseum.jpg); [overview of Scandinavian treatments](https://scandinavism.com/2020/03/28/in-the-snake-pit/)

## 6. Historical Evolution of the Figure

### I. Ninth century: separate historical persons and possible poetic patron

The documentary record contains Reginheri in Francia, Björn-like leaders, Ímar/Ingware, Halfdan, and Ubba. Bragi may have praised a patron Ragnar. None is called “Ragnar Lodbrok” in an uncontested contemporary witness, and no contemporary source assembles them into the saga family.

### II. Late tenth to eleventh century: the father of famous invaders

Abbo makes Hinguar and Hubba central villains in Edmund’s martyrdom. William’s textual tradition calls Björn son of Lothbrocus; Adam calls Inguar son of Lodparchus. The Loðbrók-name is becoming a genealogical explanation for known Viking leaders, though Ragnar is not yet explicitly joined to it.

### III. Early twelfth century: the composite name becomes secure

Ari writes **Ívarr Ragnarssonr loðbrókar** and claims descent through Sigurðr Ragnarsson. Ragnar now functions both as father of the Edmund-killer and as Icelandic genealogical ancestor. The historical invasion age has become a chronological and genealogical framework for Icelandic identity.

### IV. Late twelfth to early thirteenth century: poetic and Danish monumentalization

*Krákumál* constructs the dying warrior who remembers a lifetime of battle and expects Óðinn’s hall. Saxo turns Ragnar into an imperial Danish king and integrates multiple wives, sons, campaigns, and legends into national history, while Christian providence condemns his pagan violence.

### V. Thirteenth to fourteenth century: Icelandic canonical synthesis

*Völsunga saga* and *Ragnars saga* are transmitted together, making Áslaug the bridge between the Völsungs and Viking conquest. The serpent-slaying marriage, clever-maiden test, enchanted cow, death in England, and sons’ revenge form the best-known narrative. *Ragnarssona þáttr*, Hauksbók, and *Landnámabók* extend the dynasty into Scandinavian kingship and Icelandic settlement genealogy.

### VI. Later medieval and early-modern transmission

Annals, chronicles, and genealogies repeat, synchronize, or expand the story. Contradictions are often treated as chronological puzzles rather than evidence of variant traditions. Learned historians increasingly search for a historical Ragnar behind the texts.

### VII. Seventeenth and eighteenth centuries: antiquarian “Runic” hero

Northern antiquarianism prints and translates *Krákumál*. The poem’s imagined pagan death becomes evidence for supposedly ancient Scandinavian character. Thomas Percy’s “The Dying Ode of Regner Lodbrog” in *Five Pieces of Runic Poetry* (1763; versions circulated from 1761) strongly shapes British Gothic and Romantic ideas of fearless Vikings. The label “Runic” is antiquarian branding; *Krákumál* survives in manuscripts, not as a rune-stone inscription.

### VIII. Nineteenth century: national-romantic Ragnar

Danish, Swedish, Norwegian, and British artists and poets render the snake-pit, messenger, wives, and sons. Adam Oehlenschläger’s *Regnar Lodbrok* (1848) reshapes the death-song into a Danish heroic-national poem. Visual artists turn Ragnar into a muscular national ancestor. **[MODERN REINVENTION]** [Study abstract on Oehlenschläger and Percy](https://www.efrome.it/fileadmin/res/PDF_Flyers_Affiches_Programmes/Moyen_Age/Programma_completo_con_abstract.pdf)

### IX. Twentieth-century scholarship

Philology shifts attention from “the real Ragnar” to manuscript relationships, oral tradition, motifs, genealogical politics, and source development. Scholars including Hilda Ellis Davidson, Peter Fisher, Rory McTurk, and Elizabeth Ashman Rowe emphasize the composite and changing tradition. The historical Reginheri remains a possible contributor, not an identified biography.

### X. Twenty-first century: global screen hero

The television series *Vikings* (2013–2020) makes Ragnar an inquisitive farmer, navigator, antihero, brother of Rollo, companion of Floki, lover of Lagertha and Áslaug, raider of Lindisfarne and Paris, and conflicted observer of Christianity. This is a deliberate fusion of medieval legend with several centuries of Viking-Age history. Its Ragnar is now more culturally familiar than the saga king.

## 7. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Literature and antiquarianism

- **Thomas Percy, 1760s:** *Krákumál* becomes the “Dying Ode,” a foundational English image of the fearless, Valhalla-seeking Norse warrior. **[EARLY-MODERN/ROMANTIC REINVENTION]**
- **Hugh Downman:** cheaper and frequently reprinted treatments helped popularize Ragnar beyond Percy’s scholarly readership. [Review of reception scholarship](https://scholarworks.iu.edu/journals/index.php/tmr/article/view/33402)
- **Adam Oehlenschläger, *Regnar Lodbrok* (1848):** Danish Romantic national epic that recasts Ragnar as warrior-poet.
- Popular histories and children’s retellings progressively turn variant traditions into a single linear “life.”

### Painting and sculpture

Nineteenth-century artworks select two theatrical moments: heroic exposure in the snake-pit and the sons’ controlled reactions to the death-message. They embody Romantic ideals of masculinity, nation, fatalism, and ancestral character. None documents medieval appearance, clothing, tattoos, or physiognomy.

### Film, television, comics, and games

- **The Vikings** (1958), with Ernest Borgnine as Ragnar, loosely uses the Ragnar–Ælla cycle. **[INVENTION/ADAPTATION]**
- **Vikings** (2013–2020), created by Michael Hirst, stars Travis Fimmel as Ragnar. Hirst explicitly treats the material as drama rather than documentary. The series combines legendary Ragnar with Reginheri’s Paris association, the 793 Lindisfarne raid, Rollo, later political figures, and invented domestic psychology. [Television Academy interview](https://www.televisionacademy.com/features/news/online-originals/real-thing); [TIME interview](https://time.com/82983/vikings-season-2-finale/)
- Series inventions include Ragnar as the originating western navigator, Rollo as his brother, Floki as his intimate collaborator, Athelstan as his central Christian friend, and a single coherent chronology joining events separated by generations. Ragnar’s tattoos and much of the visual “Viking” identity are modern design.
- **Assassin’s Creed Valhalla** (2020) treats Ragnar’s death-place and lost warriors as discoverable game-world history. This is **[MODERN INVENTION]**, not archaeological evidence.
- Numerous strategy and mobile games use “Ragnar Lothbrok” as a standardized Viking king, often visually modeled more on Travis Fimmel than on pre-2013 art.

### Nationalism and extremist appropriation

Romantic scholarship and art used Ragnar to construct Scandinavian—and sometimes broader “Teutonic” or “Northern”—ancestry. Such appropriation is historically datable and should not be projected backward as the saga’s original political program.

Modern white-nationalist and far-right subcultures appropriate Viking symbols, imagined racial ancestry, Valhalla rhetoric, and television-derived warrior aesthetics. Ragnar can appear within that symbolic field, but he is not as uniquely diagnostic as symbols such as runes or the valknut. A Ragnar image, quotation, or tattoo is therefore not by itself proof of extremist affiliation. Scholarship on modern Viking reception explicitly distinguishes broad popular enthusiasm from racial-nationalist appropriation. [Review discussing white-nationalist appropriation](https://scholarworks.iu.edu/journals/index.php/tmr/article/view/33402)

### Commercial image

“Ragnar” now sells alcohol, clothing, jewelry, beard products, fitness programs, and neo-Viking tourism. Common modern features—shaved-side hair, elaborate scalp tattoos, black leather armor, “Lothbrok” as a family surname—derive chiefly from screen and fantasy design. **[MODERN INVENTION]**

### Psychological and structural readings

Ragnar fits several recurrent heroic patterns: serpent-slayer wins maiden; hidden princess solves impossible condition; father is surpassed by sons; failed final expedition triggers filial vengeance; hero turns death into performance. Rankian, Raglanian, Campbellian, and structuralist models can map those motifs, but no such model establishes historicity or recovers a single prehistoric original. Dumézilian tripartite readings are similarly comparative hypotheses. Oral-traditional analysis explains variation better than a forced “monomyth” when it preserves the specific manuscript and political settings of each version.

## 8. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

## 8.1 Was Ragnar historical?

### Case for a historical core

- Reginheri is securely recorded in 845 and bears a linguistically corresponding name.
- His social setting—Danish or Scandinavian elite warfare in Francia—fits part of the later Ragnar image.
- Bragi’s possibly ninth-century poem addresses a Ragnar and calls him Sigurðr’s son.
- Björn, Ívarr/Ímar, Halfdan, and Ubba resemble later Ragnarsons.
- Traditions making famous raiders sons of a Loðbrók existed by the later eleventh century.

### Case against identification

- No ninth-century source writes “Ragnar Loðbrók.”
- Reginheri’s recorded Paris expedition and reported death in 845 conflict with the later Northumbrian snake-pit.
- The 845 leader cannot straightforwardly father and personally precede every leader later concentrated into the family without substantial chronological and genealogical construction.
- *Ragnarsdrápa*’s identification depends on Snorri’s later attribution.
- “Lothbrocus/Lodparchus” appears before the explicit Ragnar-Loðbrók compound and may once have denoted another or even a female figure.
- The saga’s central episodes are international heroic and folktale motifs.

**Present evidentiary judgment:** a historical Ragnar or Reginheri may have contributed to the legend, but the documented Ragnar Lodbrok is a medieval composite. Statements that “Ragnar definitely lived” or that Reginheri “was Ragnar Lodbrok” exceed the evidence.

## 8.2 Did Ragnar raid Paris in 845?

The raid is historical; the identification is not secure. The *Annals of St-Bertin* document the fleet, Paris, Charles, and payment. The *Annals of Xanten* supply Reginheri. The later Ragnar sources do not preserve the Paris raid as the central saga episode. Thus “Ragnar raided Paris” is shorthand for a disputed modern equation, not what the contemporary annal itself says.

## 8.3 Was the Great Army revenge for Ragnar?

No contemporary source states the motive. The chronology—army arrives in 865, Ælla dies in 867—allowed later tradition to connect the campaign to a prior execution. Ari already knows Ívarr as Ragnar’s son by the early twelfth century; the full revenge plot is developed later. The legend may preserve family propaganda, but that cannot be demonstrated.

## 8.4 Dating *Krákumál*

The poem has often been romanticized as Ragnar’s ninth-century composition. Linguistic, metrical, geographical, and transmission evidence instead favors the late twelfth century, probably in the Norse Atlantic/Scottish islands. It is an artful medieval dramatic monologue, not stenography from a snake-pit. The title itself and its association with Kráka have a complicated manuscript history. [Skaldic Project commentary](https://skaldic.org/db.php?id=1020&if=default&table=text)

## 8.5 Dating and structure of *Ragnars saga*

The saga is often dated to the thirteenth century, sometimes around 1230. Its earliest complete manuscript is NKS 1824 b 4to, c. 1400, where it follows *Völsunga saga*. The physical sequence is evidence for deliberate continuity between the Völsung and Ragnar cycles. A fragment in AM 147 4to and later paper manuscripts show further transmission. [Fornaldarsögur manuscript study](https://etheses.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/24017/1/Rowbotham%2C%20T.%20P.%20-%20Fornaldars%C3%B6gur%2C%20Prosimetrum%2C%20and%20History-Writing%20in%20Medieval%20Iceland.pdf)

## 8.6 Oral tradition versus literary composition

The tales undoubtedly use orally transmissible motifs and verses, but “the saga is ancient oral history written down unchanged” is untenable. The extant work is a literate medieval composition shaped by:

- the manuscript pairing with *Völsunga saga*;
- Icelandic genealogical claims;
- knowledge of English saint tradition;
- Scandinavian royal historiography;
- folktale structures;
- heroic verse inserted or cited as authority.

Oral-formulaic theory helps explain variation and reusable scenes; it does not license dating every narrative element to the Viking Age.

## 8.7 The Loðbróka hypothesis

McTurk argued that the composite name emerged from Ragnar plus a separate **Loðbróka**, possibly a female name associated with fertility or taboo-name practice. Supporting points include grammatical forms such as “sons of Loðbróka” and the earlier appearance of Loðbrók-like father names without Ragnar. Critics regard parts of the reconstruction as speculative and question whether the name evidence requires a goddess or distinct female tradition. **Status: influential but contested modern reconstruction.**

## 8.8 The blood eagle

Older scholarship alternately treated it as an authentic Viking execution or a misunderstanding of skaldic diction. Recent interdisciplinary work argues that such a procedure is anatomically possible while emphasizing that textual possibility and historical occurrence are different questions. Ragnar’s revenge episode remains late and uncorroborated.

## 8.9 Manuscripts and losses

- *Íslendingabók*: original lost; known through seventeenth-century copies.
- *Ragnars saga*: earliest complete witness NKS 1824 b, c. 1400.
- *Völsunga saga* and *Ragnars saga* share that codex, creating a material literary sequence.
- *Ragnarssona þáttr*: preserved in Hauksbók, associated with Haukr Erlendsson and his genealogical interests.
- *Ragnarsdrápa*: only fragments quoted in later prose.
- *Krákumál*: later manuscript transmission; title and attribution vary.
- Contemporary Danish narrative records for the alleged lifetime are absent.

## 8.10 Discredited or unsupported claims still circulating

- Ragnar’s dates can be precisely given as a birth and death: **unsupported**.
- Every named Ragnarson was his biological child: **unsupported**.
- The Paris raider is certainly the saga hero: **unsupported**.
- The Great Army’s documented motive was revenge: **unsupported**.
- A particular mound is Ragnar’s grave: **unsupported**.
- Snake-pit church carvings depict Ragnar rather than Gunnarr: usually **misidentification**.
- *Krákumál* is a Viking-Age song composed and performed by Ragnar during execution: **late attribution treated as fact**.
- Ragnar was a historical Norwegian farmer who discovered England: **television invention**.
- “Ragnar Lothbrok” names an ancient hereditary clan: **modern surname-like misunderstanding**.

- Rory McTurk, *Studies in Ragnars saga loðbrókar and Its Major Scandinavian Analogues* (1991).

The evidentiary conclusion is deliberately narrower than the legend: Ragnar Lodbrok is securely a named figure by the early twelfth century and a fully developed hero by c. 1200–1300. Reginheri, the Great Army, Ímar, Halfdan, Ubba, and other ninth-century actors are historical evidence around which the legend formed; none supplies a verifiable birth-to-death Ragnar. The serpent-slayer, Völsung marriage, imperial campaigns, Northumbrian snake-pit, death-song, and filial revenge are the medieval documented tradition—and are historically important precisely as tradition.
