# Gudrun (Kriemhild): Research Dossier

**Class:** Mortal human heroine—princess, queen, widow, avenger, and dynastic survivor—in German and Old Norse heroic legend. No medieval source treats her as a goddess, demigoddess, valkyrie, saint, or heroized dead receiving cult.

**Earliest secure attestation:** Kriemhilt in the Middle High German *Nibelungenlied*, composed about 1200, preserved in manuscripts beginning in the second quarter of the thirteenth century. Claims that individual Old Norse poems about Guðrún were composed earlier may be philologically plausible, especially for *Atlakviða*, but their securely datable principal witness is the Icelandic Codex Regius, GKS 2365 4to, written about 1270. The earliest surviving Scandinavian prose synthesis is Snorri Sturluson’s *Skáldskaparmál*, conventionally dated about 1220.

**Evidence labels used below**

- **[SEA]** securely attested early material
- **[LCE]** later canonical elaboration
- **[RV]** regional variant
- **[LS]** learned systematization or antiquarian synthesis
- **[SI]** syncretistic identification
- **[MEA]** medieval or early-modern accretion
- **[F]** folklore
- **[MSR]** modern scholarly reconstruction
- **[MI]** modern invention or adaptation

## 1. Identification, Names and Epithets

### Orientation

Gudrun/Kriemhild is not one character preserved in a continuous biography but the changing female center of the Nibelung–Völsung heroic complex. **[SEA/LCE]** In Scandinavian tradition, **Guðrún Gjúkadóttir** is the daughter of Gjúki and Grímhildr, wife of Sigurðr and afterward Atli, sister of Gunnarr and Hǫgni, and mother—under mutually inconsistent genealogies—of Sigmundr, Svanhildr, Atli’s sons Erpr and Eitill, and Jónakr’s sons Hamðir, Sǫrli, and sometimes Erpr. She mourns Sigurðr, is compelled or persuaded to marry Atli, warns her brothers of Atli’s treachery, and after their deaths kills her children by Atli, makes him consume their flesh and blood, kills him, and burns his hall. Later poems prolong her story through Svanhildr’s death and the doomed revenge of Hamðir and Sǫrli.

**[SEA]** In the *Nibelungenlied*, **Kriemhilt** is the Burgundian princess at Worms, sister of Gunther, Gernot, and Giselher; she marries Siegfried, is widowed when Hagen murders him with Gunther’s connivance, and later marries Etzel so that she can draw the Burgundians to his court and avenge Siegfried. After killing Hagen with Siegfried’s sword, Balmung, she is cut down by Hildebrand. Thus the two great branches invert the principal revenge:

- Old Norse Guðrún avenges her **brothers upon her second husband Atli**.
- German Kriemhild avenges her **first husband Siegfried upon her brothers and Hagen**.

This is not a superficial discrepancy to be harmonized. It is the principal evidence for the legend’s divergent development.

### Names

- **Old Norse:** *Guðrún*, normalized *Guðrún*; manuscript spellings vary. The patronymic is *Gjúkadóttir*, “Gjúki’s daughter.”  
  **[MSR]** The transparent compound is usually analyzed as *guð-* “god/divine being” plus *rún* “secret, confidential lore; rune.” The name therefore means approximately “divine secret/lore,” not simply “battle-rune.” Nothing in the heroic narratives makes Guðrún a magician by virtue of this etymology.

- **Middle High German:** *Kriemhilt*, with manuscript variants including *Chriemhilt*, *Kriemhild*, and related spellings; modern German **Kriemhild**.  
  **[MSR]** The second element is Germanic *hildjō-* “battle.” The first element is disputed. Proposals connect it with forms meaning “mask/helmet,” “grim,” or a personal-name stem; older popular etymologies such as “helmeted battle-maiden” are not secure. The Upper German *Kr-* form corresponds broadly to northern *Grímhildr*, but in Scandinavian narrative **Grímhildr is Guðrún’s mother**, not Guðrún herself.

- **Old Norse continental compilation:** *Grímhildr* in *Þiðreks saga*.  
  **[RV/SI]** Here the northern compiler uses the name cognate with German Kriemhild for the Niflung princess, while the native Scandinavian Völsung cycle assigns *Grímhildr* to Guðrún’s mother.

- **Wagnerian German:** *Gutrune*.  
  **[MI]** Richard Wagner created this form for *Der Ring des Nibelungen*. His Gutrune is a drastically reduced fusion of Norse Guðrún and German Kriemhild: Gunther’s sister, recipient of Siegfried’s potion-induced love, but neither the great widow-avenger of the *Nibelungenlied* nor Atli’s filicidal destroyer.

### Epithets and descriptive formulas

- **Guðrún Gjúkadóttir**, “Guðrún, Gjúki’s daughter”: recurrent Old Norse patronymic. **[SEA/LCE]**
- **Gjúka borin**, “born of Gjúki”: *Hamðismál* 1. **[SEA]**
- **dróttning**, “queen,” and equivalent royal designations: saga prose and Eddic framing. **[LCE]**
- **Kriemhilt diu schœne**, “beautiful Kriemhild,” and comparable courtly descriptions: *Nibelungenlied*. **[SEA]**
- “Widow of Sigurðr/Siegfried,” “wife of Atli/Etzel,” and “sister of Gunnarr/Gunther” are functional descriptors rather than stable cult titles. **[SEA]**
- There is no securely attested ancient or medieval divine epithet, cult title, or theophoric title for her. **[SEA: absence]**

### Identification with Ildico

**[SEA historical evidence]** Jordanes, *Getica* 254, written in 551 and explicitly attributing the report to the fifth-century historian Priscus, says that Attila married a beautiful young woman named **Ildico** and died after the wedding feast from blood flowing into his throat. Ildico is found alive, veiled, and weeping; Jordanes does not accuse her of murder. [Jordanes, *Getica* 254](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Jordanes/Getica/D%2A.html)

**[MSR]** Since Atli/Etzel transparently continues the name Attila and Kriemhild/Grímhild contains *-hild*, scholars have repeatedly proposed that Attila’s bride Ildico contributed to the legendary queen. This is possible but unprovable. The source supplies neither a Burgundian genealogy, marriage to a dragon-slayer, revenge for brothers, filicide, nor the killing of Attila. “Ildico was the historical Kriemhild” is therefore shorthand for a reconstruction, not an attested identity.

**[MSR]** Attempts to derive aspects of Kriemhild from the sixth-century Merovingian queens Brunhild and Fredegund rest on patterns of dynastic marriage, feud, and vengeance rather than name identity or direct textual transmission. They remain comparative hypotheses.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### What the earliest evidence establishes

#### The *Nibelungenlied*

**[SEA]** The earliest securely dated work naming the heroine is the anonymous Middle High German *Nibelungenlied*, composed around 1200, probably in the Danube region. It consists in its usual division of 39 *Âventiuren*. Its first stanza of narrative introduces Kriemhild as a beautiful Burgundian maiden; its last catastrophe follows from her revenge. The work relates her falcon dream, marriage to Siegfried, quarrel with Brünhild, Siegfried’s murder, seizure of the Nibelung treasure, marriage to Etzel, invitation of the Burgundians, destruction of both courts, execution of Hagen, and death at Hildebrand’s hands. UNESCO entered its principal manuscript witnesses in the Memory of the World register in 2009. [German UNESCO Commission](https://www.unesco.de/staette/nibelungenlied/)

The earliest complete or nearly complete witnesses—conventionally A, B, and C—date from the thirteenth century. Their endings and moral emphases differ. The poem’s approximately contemporary composition date is firmer than any proposed date for an individual Eddic heroic poem.

#### Snorri’s *Skáldskaparmál*

**[LCE/LS]** Snorri Sturluson’s *Edda*, conventionally dated about 1220, gives the earliest datable Scandinavian prose synopsis of the Gjúkung catastrophe. In *Skáldskaparmál*, the story explains poetic circumlocutions for gold: Guðrún is Sigurðr’s widow and Atli’s wife; Atli kills Gunnarr and Hǫgni for the treasure; Guðrún kills her sons, serves their blood and hearts to Atli, kills him, and burns the hall. [*Skáldskaparmál* 50 synopsis and text](https://northerntales.se/en/eddas/prose-edda/skaldskaparmal/50/)

#### Codex Regius

**[SEA manuscript evidence]** GKS 2365 4to, the Codex Regius of the Poetic Edda, was copied about 1270. It preserves the principal Guðrún poems and is the secure terminus ante quem for their extant forms. The manuscript’s date is palaeographic; individual poems may be older, but their dates cannot simply be equated with the Migration-period events they fictionalize. A modern diplomatic electronic edition is maintained by the Arnamagnæan institutions. [Codex Regius electronic edition](https://eae.ku.dk/q?p=eae%2Fvols%2Ftext%2F1)

### The Old Norse poetic corpus

All the following are preserved principally in Codex Regius, about 1270, although proposed composition dates range from the ninth to the thirteenth century.

- **Grípisspá**: prophesies Sigurðr’s marriage to Guðrún, the deception of Brynhildr, Sigurðr’s death, and Guðrún’s later marriage to Atli. **[LCE or earlier oral material; date disputed]**
- **Brot af Sigurðarkviðu**: fragment of Sigurðr’s death; Guðrún is the bereaved wife. **[SEA manuscript; composition disputed]**
- **Guðrúnarkviða I**: Guðrún sits speechless beside Sigurðr’s corpse until Gullrǫnd uncovers it; her grief breaks into lament. **[SEA manuscript]**
- **Sigurðarkviða in skamma**: compact account of Sigurðr’s death, Brynhildr’s responsibility, Guðrún’s grief, and Brynhildr’s suicide. **[SEA manuscript]**
- **Helreið Brynhildar**: Brynhildr’s posthumous self-defense; Guðrún belongs to the disputed marriage exchange. **[SEA manuscript]**
- **Guðrúnarkviða II / Guðrúnarkviða hin forna**: Guðrún narrates her sufferings to Þjóðrekr; includes Grímhildr’s potion and pressure to marry Atli. The title “old” is medieval, not a secure date. **[SEA manuscript; putative early material]**
- **Guðrúnarkviða III**: Herkja accuses Guðrún of sleeping with Þjóðrekr; Guðrún clears herself in a boiling-water ordeal, and Herkja is punished. **[LCE/RV]**
- **Oddrúnargrátr**: Oddrún recalls Gunnarr and the destruction of her kin; Guðrún is part of the background genealogy. **[SEA manuscript]**
- **Atlakviða in grœnlenzka**: Atli invites and kills Guðrún’s brothers; she kills her sons and serves Atli their hearts and blood, then kills him and burns the hall. Its “Greenlandic” rubric need not mean composition in Greenland. It is often judged archaic, but exact dating remains contested. **[SEA manuscript; often reconstructed as early]**
- **Atlamál in grœnlenzku**: a longer, more domestic and discursive retelling; Guðrún sends warning runes, fights, kills her sons, serves them to Atli, and kills him with Hniflungr. **[LCE/RV]**
- **Guðrúnarhvǫt**: after Svanhildr’s death, Guðrún incites Hamðir and Sǫrli against Jǫrmunrekkr and laments her own history. **[SEA manuscript]**
- **Hamðismál**: the sons depart after Guðrún’s incitement, kill their half-brother Erpr, mutilate Jǫrmunrekkr, and are stoned to death. Guðrún frames their mission but does not accompany them. **[SEA manuscript; often considered to contain archaic material]**

A freely accessible dual-language scholarly edition makes the contradiction over Guðrún’s sons explicit: one prose genealogy counts Erpr as Jónakr’s son, while the following poem handles his status differently. [Edward Pettit, ed. and trans., *The Poetic Edda*](https://skaldskeep.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/08/PDF-The-Poetic-Edda-Edward-Pettit.pdf)

### Prose sagas and compilations

- **Völsunga saga**, late thirteenth century; chief manuscript witness Ny kgl. Saml. 1824 b 4to, around 1400. **[LCE/LS]** It synthesizes Eddic poems and lost material into a family saga. Chapters 27–44 cover Guðrún’s dreams, the forgetting drink given to Sigurðr, marriage, queenly quarrel, Sigurðr’s death, mourning, marriage to Atli, destruction of her brothers, murder of her sons and Atli, failed suicide, marriage to Jónakr, Svanhildr’s death, and the last revenge. [UCLA contents and edition information](https://viking.ucla.edu/publications/books/volsungs_penguin.html)

- **Þiðreks saga af Bern**, Norwegian compilation around the mid-thirteenth century, based substantially on continental—especially Low German—heroic traditions. **[RV/LS]** Its heroine is Grímhildr, daughter of Aldrian and Oda. After Sigurðr’s murder she marries Atli and engineers the Niflung catastrophe. Þiðrekr ultimately kills her. The saga combines German revenge motivation with northern names and independent variants; it is not merely a prose copy of the *Nibelungenlied*.

- **Nornagests þáttr**, late thirteenth or fourteenth century. **[MEA/LS]** Its long-lived narrator recalls Sigurðr and the Gjúkungs, offering antiquarian linkage rather than a distinct Guðrún biography.

- **Ragnars saga loðbrókar**, probably thirteenth century. **[MEA]** It links Ragnar’s wife Áslaug/Kráka genealogically to Sigurðr and Brynhildr. Guðrún is displaced from this ancestry rather than made Áslaug’s mother; the connection enlarges the Völsung family’s legendary prestige.

- **Hervarar saga ok Heiðreks**, versions from the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries. **[RV]** It preserves Gothic and Hunnic heroic matter, including Heiðrekr and Angantýr, but not a continuous Guðrún narrative. Its importance is comparative: it shows independent northern transformations of Migration-period names and Gothic–Hunnic conflict.

- **Gautreks saga**, probably late thirteenth century, and other *fornaldarsögur*. **[MEA]** These demonstrate the later legendary-saga environment in which heroic families were cross-linked, but they are not independent witnesses to Guðrún’s central story.

- **Saxo Grammaticus, *Gesta Danorum***, composed around 1200–1220. **[RV/LS]** Saxo preserves a version of the Hamdir/Sørli–Ermanaric material, but not the full Sigurðr–Guðrún–Atli biography. It is evidence for an independently circulating revenge complex.

### Continental German corpus

- **Nibelungenlied**, c. 1200: primary German canonical account. **[SEA]**
- **Nibelungenklage**, early thirteenth century, transmitted with the epic: interprets and mourns the catastrophe, assigns responsibility, and narrates the dissemination of the news. **[LCE]**
- **Rosengarten zu Worms**, thirteenth century in several redactions: Kriemhild sponsors or provokes a tournament between her Worms champions and Dietrich’s heroes. **[RV/MEA]** This combative, courtly Kriemhild is not situated after Siegfried’s murder.
- **Biterolf und Dietleib**, mid-thirteenth century, surviving in the sixteenth-century *Ambraser Heldenbuch*: places Kriemhild in an expanded Dietrich–Etzel heroic world and modifies the feuding relationships. **[MEA]**
- **Lied vom Hürnen Seyfrid**, preserved in print from 1530, probably drawing on later medieval material: Kriemhild is rescued by the horn-skinned Seyfrid from a dragon. **[MEA/F]** It substantially rewrites her role and is not evidence for the earliest Nibelung story.
- **Heldenbuch-Prosa**, late fifteenth century: learned prose organization of German heroic ages and genealogies. **[LS/MEA]**
- **Der Marner**, mid-thirteenth century, mentions popular heroic subjects including what Kriemhild “betrayed,” valuable evidence that multiple versions circulated beyond the surviving epics. **[SEA reception evidence]**
- **Danish and Faroese ballad traditions**, recorded much later: transform the Nibelung material in oral-song environments. **[F/early-modern documentation]**

### Named works in the brief that do not independently attest her

- **Beowulf**, manuscript c. 1000, poem of disputed earlier date: contains Sigemund, Heremod, Hama, Eormanric-linked treasure, Franks, Frisians, and other heroic matter, but neither Guðrún nor Kriemhild. **[SEA: absence]** Klaeber’s edition and Tolkien’s 1936 “Beowulf: The Monsters and the Critics” matter to Germanic heroic-poetry method, not as witnesses to her.
- **Grettis saga**, **Egils saga**, and the other *Íslendingasögur*: may employ heroic diction, quotation, naming, lament conventions, and analogies familiar from Eddic poetry, but they do not supply an independent Gudrun–Kriemhild narrative. **[LCE: reception/analogy, not biography]**
- **Skaldic Poetry of the Scandinavian Middle Ages project:** indispensable for checking genuine skaldic attestations. No securely dated Viking-Age skaldic stanza provides an independent narrative of Guðrún comparable to the Eddic poems. **[SEA: absence]**

## 3. The Story

## 3.1 Parentage and childhood

**[SEA German]** In the *Nibelungenlied*, Kriemhild grows up at Worms under her mother Uote and brothers Gunther, Gernot, and Giselher. Her father Dancrât is already dead or narratively marginal. She dreams that two eagles kill a falcon she has reared; Uote interprets the falcon as the husband she will lose. Kriemhild initially rejects love, but the audience knows the dream prefigures Siegfried.

**[LCE Norse]** In Eddic and saga tradition, Guðrún is the daughter of Gjúki and the potion-wise Grímhildr, and sister of Gunnarr and Hǫgni. Gutþormr is variably a full brother, half-brother, or younger maternal brother. *Völsunga saga* gives Guðrún ominous dreams that Brynhildr interprets as foretelling Sigurðr, his death, and Guðrún’s later marriage.

No source supplies a detailed supernatural birth or childhood exploit.

## 3.2 Sigurðr/Siegfried, Brynhildr/Brünhild, and the forgetting potion

**[LCE Norse]** In *Grípisspá*, *Guðrúnarkviða II*, and *Völsunga saga*, Grímhildr arranges Sigurðr’s attachment to Guðrún. The saga makes the mechanism explicit: Sigurðr drinks a potion that causes him to forget Brynhildr. He marries Guðrún; through disguise or shape exchange he then helps Gunnarr cross the fire and win Brynhildr.

**[Variant]** The precise degree of Guðrún’s knowledge and agency differs. In the saga she is chiefly the beneficiary of her mother’s scheme, not its designer. The famous “potion of forgetting” is securely medieval Norse narrative, not historical evidence and not part of the *Nibelungenlied*.

**[SEA German]** In the *Nibelungenlied*, there is no forgetting potion. Siegfried comes to Worms seeking Kriemhild and willingly marries her after helping Gunther win Brünhild through deception and physical coercion. The marriages are a political exchange. Kriemhild later possesses Brünhild’s ring and girdle, objects Siegfried obtained when he overpowered Brünhild.

## 3.3 The queens’ quarrel

**[SEA German]** Kriemhild and Brünhild quarrel over which husband has higher rank and therefore which queen should enter the cathedral first. Kriemhild declares that Siegfried, not Gunther, mastered Brünhild and displays the ring and girdle. The public humiliation prompts Hagen’s plan to kill Siegfried.

**[LCE Norse]** *Völsunga saga* relocates the quarrel to bathing and precedence in the river. Guðrún reveals that Sigurðr crossed the fire in Gunnarr’s shape and shows Brynhildr’s ring. Brynhildr demands Sigurðr’s death. Both traditions make the women’s dispute reveal male fraud, but neither makes the quarrel an isolated cause: the deceptive bride-winning has already created the catastrophe.

## 3.4 Sigurðr/Siegfried’s death and Guðrún’s lament

**[SEA German]** Hagen tricks Kriemhild into marking the vulnerable place on Siegfried’s clothing by claiming he wishes to protect him. Hagen then spears Siegfried at a spring during a hunt. The corpse is placed outside Kriemhild’s chamber. At the bier she identifies Hagen when the wounds bleed in his presence—the ordeal of the bier.

**[Old Norse variants]**

- In *Brot af Sigurðarkviðu*, Sigurðr appears to be killed outdoors.
- In *Sigurðarkviða in skamma* and *Völsunga saga*, Gutþormr kills him in bed; Sigurðr throws his sword and cuts the killer in two.
- Some witnesses make Guðrún and Sigurðr’s young son Sigmundr another victim.
- *Guðrúnarkviða I* concentrates not on the murder mechanics but on traumatic silence: Guðrún cannot weep until Gullrǫnd uncovers Sigurðr’s body.
- *Guðrúnarkviða II* turns the widow’s memory into an autobiographical catalogue of losses.

## 3.5 Widowhood and the treasure

**[SEA German]** Kriemhild remains at Worms. She gains the Nibelung treasure and distributes wealth so liberally that Hagen fears she will acquire armed followers. With Gunther’s permission he sinks the treasure in the Rhine. This confiscation deepens her grievance.

**[RV Norse]** The treasure is primarily the reason Atli desires the Gjúkungs. Gunnarr and Hǫgni hide it before their fatal journey. Guðrún is not its dispossessed owner in the German manner.

## 3.6 Marriage to Atli/Etzel

**[LCE Norse]** In *Guðrúnarkviða II*, Grímhildr gives Guðrún a drink of forgetfulness and uses threats, gifts, and dynastic pressure to obtain her marriage to Atli. Guðrún resists because she foresees disaster. In *Völsunga saga*, the settlement is similarly coerced.

**[SEA German]** Etzel’s emissary Rüdiger proposes marriage. Kriemhild initially objects because Etzel is a heathen, while she is Christian, but accepts after recognizing that his power can serve her revenge. Etzel is not Siegfried’s murderer and is largely ignorant of her plan. Their son is Ortlieb.

## 3.7 The warning to the brothers

**[SEA/LCE Norse]**

- In *Atlakviða*, Guðrún sends a ring entwined with wolf hair as warning.
- In *Atlamál*, she carves runes, but the messenger Vingi alters them; Kostbera nevertheless recognizes danger.
- The brothers knowingly proceed, motivated by courage, fatalism, or refusal of dishonor.

**[SEA German]** Kriemhild intends the invitation itself as the trap. Hagen and Uote foresee danger, but the Burgundians come with a large retinue. Here she is not the warning sister but the architect of their destruction.

## 3.8 Deaths of Gunnarr and Hǫgni

**[SEA/LCE Norse]** Atli attacks his guests for the hoard. Guðrún fights beside her brothers in *Atlakviða* and *Atlamál*. Hǫgni’s heart is cut out while he laughs; Gunnarr refuses to reveal the treasure until he sees that heart, then says he alone knows the secret. He is cast into a snake pit and plays a harp with his toes before a snake kills him.

**[SEA German]** Gunther and Hagen survive the hall battle and are captured by Dietrich. Kriemhild demands the treasure. Hagen refuses while Gunther lives; she has Gunther beheaded and brings Hagen the head. Hagen answers that now only God and he know the treasure’s location. Kriemhild kills him with Balmung.

## 3.9 Filicide, cannibal meal, and Atli’s death

This is the most violent Scandinavian episode and must not be transferred to the German Kriemhild.

**[SEA manuscript tradition]** *Atlakviða* says Guðrún kills Atli’s sons, fashions drinking vessels from their skulls, mixes their blood with drink, and serves their roasted hearts to Atli. After telling him what he has consumed, she kills him and burns the hall. An accessible translation preserves the poem’s account of the slaughter and revenge. [*Atlakviða*, “The Lay of Atli”](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Elder_Edda_and_the_Younger_Edda/Elder_Edda/The_Lay_of_Atli)

**[LCE/RV]** *Atlamál* names the sons Erpr and Eitill and lets them speak before their mother kills them. Atli consumes their hearts and drinks from skull vessels. Guðrún and Hniflungr kill him. [*Atlamál*](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Poetic_Edda_%28tr._Bellows%29/Atlamol_en_Gr%C3%B6nlenzku)

**[LCE]** *Völsunga saga* follows this outline: Guðrún cuts the boys’ throats, prepares their flesh, kills Atli with Hǫgni’s son Niblung, and burns the hall. [*Völsunga saga*, ch. 39](https://archive.sacred-texts.com/neu/vlsng/vlsng41.htm)

**[Comparative reconstruction]** The child-meal resembles the Procne–Tereus and Atreus–Thyestes story types. Direct borrowing is possible but not demonstrable merely from resemblance.

## 3.10 Kriemhild’s German death

**[SEA]** Kriemhild kills the bound Hagen herself. Hildebrand declares it shameful that so great a warrior should be slain by a woman and cuts her to pieces or kills her with his sword. The poem does not allow her triumph or reconciliation. Dietrich and Etzel survive to mourn. Some manuscript redactions and the *Nibelungenklage* redistribute blame, but the killing remains.

**[RV]** In *Þiðreks saga*, Grímhildr is killed by Þiðrekr during the battle after he recognizes her treachery. The details do not exactly reproduce the *Nibelungenlied*.

## 3.11 Jónakr, Svanhildr, and the final revenge

**[SEA/LCE Norse only]** After killing Atli, Guðrún attempts to drown herself, but the sea carries her to Jónakr’s land. She marries him. Her daughter Svanhildr—normally Sigurðr’s daughter—is married to Jǫrmunrekkr. Through Bikki’s malicious counsel she is accused of a relationship with Randvér and trampled by horses.

Guðrún incites Hamðir and Sǫrli to avenge Svanhildr. They kill Erpr on the journey, depriving themselves of the helper who would have cut off Jǫrmunrekkr’s head. They sever the king’s hands and feet but are stoned to death after Óðinn-like advice identifies stones as the means to defeat them. Guðrún’s own death is not securely narrated. *Guðrúnarhvǫt* ends in lament and longing for death, not a witnessed burial.

## 4. Relations with Gods and Other Heroes

### Competing genealogies

| Tradition | Parents | Siblings | Husbands | Children |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| *Nibelungenlied*, c. 1200 **[SEA]** | Dancrât and Uote | Gunther, Gernot, Giselher | Siegfried; Etzel | Siegfried’s unnamed son in some interpretation/tradition; Ortlieb by Etzel |
| Eddic/Völsung **[SEA/LCE]** | Gjúki and Grímhildr | Gunnarr, Hǫgni, Gutþormr; Gullrǫnd in some sources | Sigurðr; Atli; Jónakr | Sigmundr and/or Svanhildr by Sigurðr; Erpr and Eitill by Atli; Hamðir and Sǫrli, and variably Erpr, by Jónakr |
| *Þiðreks saga* **[RV]** | Aldrian and Oda | Gunnar, Gernoz, Gisler; Hǫgni as half-brother | Sigurðr; Atli | genealogy varies by redaction |
| Wagner **[MI]** | Gibichung family; mother unnamed in the drama | Gunther; half-sister of Hagen | Siegfried | none |

### Every documented interaction with another named god or hero

The entries distinguish direct interaction from genealogy or reported association.

- **Sigurðr/Siegfried** — Husband; receives or gives marital loyalty depending on branch; his death produces her defining lament and German revenge. *Nibelungenlied* c. 1200; Eddic poems, manuscript c. 1270; *Völsunga saga*, late 13th c. **[SEA/LCE]**
- **Gjúki** — Father in northern genealogy; no substantial dramatic exchange survives. Eddic prose and *Skáldskaparmál*, 13th c. **[LCE]**
- **Grímhildr** — Mother who interprets or manipulates marriages, prepares forgetting drinks, and presses Guðrún to marry Atli. *Guðrúnarkviða II*; *Völsunga saga*. **[SEA manuscript/LCE]**
- **Dancrât** — Father in the German genealogy; no surviving direct dramatic interaction. *Nibelungenlied*. **[SEA: genealogical]**
- **Uote** — Mother; interprets Kriemhild’s falcon dream and later dreams of danger before the Burgundians’ journey. *Nibelungenlied*. **[SEA]**
- **Gunnarr/Gunther** — Brother; participates in Sigurðr’s death in the northern branch and authorizes or tolerates Hagen’s action in the German; later victim either of Atli, despite Guðrún’s warning, or of Kriemhild’s revenge. Eddic poems; *Völsunga saga*; *Nibelungenlied*. **[SEA/LCE]**

## 5. Cult, Tomb and Ritual

### Cult

**[SEA: negative finding]** No inscription, shrine, *hof*, Christian church dedication, sacrificial calendar, votive object, priesthood, oracle, healing rite, relic, or festival securely attests worship of Guðrún/Kriemhild. Medieval texts consistently classify her as a mortal princess and queen.

### Tomb and relic claims

**[SEA: negative finding]** No archaeologically identified grave can be connected to her. Unlike figures assigned literary tombs or translated relics, Gudrun/Kriemhild receives no stable medieval tomb cult.

**[F/modern local commemoration]** Worms and other places associated with the *Nibelungenlied* have monuments, festival sites, tourist routes, and modern commemorative naming. These materialize literary memory, not ancient worship.

### Ritual structures inside the stories

- Kriemhild’s bier ordeal, in which Siegfried’s wounds bleed before Hagen, is a medieval juridical-religious motif. **[SEA literary ritual]**
- Guðrún’s boiling-water ordeal in *Guðrúnarkviða III* belongs to medieval ordeal culture, probably Christian-period narrative, not demonstrable pagan cult. **[LCE]**
- Funeral lament, cremation, oath-taking, gift exchange, marriage negotiation, and memorial feasting are literary social practices, not a cult of the heroine. **[SEA/LCE]**
- Modern neopagan claims that Guðrún was historically worshipped as a goddess, valkyrie, “dark mother,” or patron of runes have no medieval documentary or archaeological basis. **[MI]**

## 6. Historical Evolution of the Figure

### Fifth and sixth centuries: historical names without the heroine’s story

**[SEA historical]** Attila dies in 453 after marrying Ildico. The Burgundian king Gundahar had been defeated with his people by Hunnic forces under Roman command in 436. Jordanes’ Ermanaric–Sunilda narrative, written in 551, supplies a separate Gothic revenge complex underlying Svanhildr and her brothers. These events and names are raw historical comparanda, not a recoverable biography of Guðrún.

### Oral heroic formation, approximately sixth–eleventh centuries

**[MSR]** Scholars reconstruct oral poems combining Burgundian destruction, Attila, the dragon-slayer, the wife of Attila, and Ermanaric. No surviving manuscript from this period names Guðrún or Kriemhild. The absence of Guðrún from *Beowulf* and Viking-Age skaldic verse is important: the wider heroic world was known, but her particular story cannot be directly documented there.

### Around 1200: German canonical shaping

**[SEA]** The *Nibelungenlied* reshapes the material for a Christian courtly audience. Kriemhild moves from sheltered princess to widow, wealthy patron, dispossessed queen, calculating avenger, and finally transgressive executioner. Her conflict is embedded in hospitality, feudal loyalty, marriage diplomacy, Christianity versus heathendom, and competing forms of *triuwe*.

### Early to late thirteenth century: northern systematization

**[LCE/LS]** Snorri summarizes the treasure catastrophe; Codex Regius preserves mutually inconsistent poems; *Völsunga saga* constructs a continuous genealogy from materials that never formed a single internally consistent original. The northern branch makes Guðrún both victim and destroyer across three marriages and two revenge cycles.

### Thirteenth–sixteenth centuries: expansion and popular recombination

**[RV/MEA]** *Þiðreks saga*, *Rosengarten*, *Biterolf und Dietleib*, *Heldenbuch-Prosa*, and *Hürnen Seyfrid* detach Kriemhild from one fixed plot. She becomes tournament sponsor, rescued maiden, dynastic node, or generalized dangerous queen.

### Seventeenth–eighteenth centuries: recovery and antiquarian editing

**[LS]** Codex Regius came to learned notice in seventeenth-century Iceland and was presented to the Danish king in 1662. The German epic, after limited medieval transmission, was rediscovered for modern scholarship in the eighteenth century. Editorial recovery encouraged the false idea that scholars had uncovered a single original national epic rather than divergent medieval texts.

### Nineteenth century: national epic, philology, theater, and opera

**[MSR/MI]** German Romantic and nationalist scholarship elevated the *Nibelungenlied* as a national epic. Friedrich Hebbel’s dramatic trilogy *Die Nibelungen* appeared in 1861–62 and psychologized Kriemhild’s transformation. Wagner’s *Ring*, completed in 1874 and first performed as a cycle in 1876, redistributed Guðrún/Kriemhild’s functions: Gutrune becomes a relatively passive bride, while Brünnhilde inherits the final agency, grief, and world-consuming act.

### Twentieth century to present

**[MI/political appropriation]** Kriemhild remained central in drama, film, novels, illustration, festivals, comics, and games. Nationalist and Nazi readings emphasized heroic loyalty and catastrophic endurance more often than Kriemhild’s critique of male violence, but the entire Nibelung complex—including its women—was available for völkisch myth-making. Postwar retellings frequently foreground coercion, gender, trauma, and Kriemhild’s exclusion from lawful justice.

## 7. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Literature and drama

- **Friedrich de la Motte Fouqué, Nibelung dramas, 1808–10** — Romantic reconstruction. **[MI]**
- **Friedrich Hebbel, *Die Nibelungen*, 1861–62** — Kriemhild’s psychology and revenge dominate a modern tragic trilogy. **[MI]**
- **William Morris, *The Story of Sigurd the Volsung and the Fall of the Niblungs*, 1876** — English poetic synthesis centered on the northern materials. **[MI]**
- **J. R. R. Tolkien, *The Legend of Sigurd and Gudrún*, composed probably in the 1920s–30s, published 2009** — deliberately modern alliterative poems based on Eddic matter, not a recovered medieval text. **[MI]**
- Modern novels repeatedly split between sympathetic trauma-survivor, monstrous avenger, and feminist resistance figure. These are interpretations, not additional medieval variants. **[MI]**

### Wagner’s Gutrune

**[MI]** In *Götterdämmerung*, Gutrune is Gunther’s sister and Hagen’s half-sister. Hagen prompts her to give Siegfried a drink that makes him forget Brünnhilde and desire her. She marries Siegfried, discovers the deception too late, and mourns him. She does not avenge him, kill Gunther or Hagen, marry Attila, murder children, or burn the hall. Wagner assigns the final immolation to Brünnhilde. A detailed comparison of Wagner’s sources identifies Gutrune as derived from both Kriemhild and Guðrún while noting Wagner’s invented relationships and scenes. [University of Texas Wagner source guide](https://www.laits.utexas.edu/wagner/ringsources/gotterdammerung.html)

### Visual art

- **Hundeshagen Codex, c. 1440** — the only extensively colored illustrated manuscript of the *Nibelungenlied*; scenes include Kriemhild’s dream, discovery of Siegfried’s body, and killing of Hagen. **[MEA]** [Bavarian Academy manuscript database](https://kdih.badw.de/datenbank/handschrift/96/0/1)
- **Johann Heinrich Füssli/Fuseli, 1807** — Kriemhild confronts Hagen with the Nibelung ring in a Romantic, theatrical mode. **[MI]**
- **Peter Cornelius, Nibelung fresco designs, early nineteenth century**, and **Julius Schnorr von Carolsfeld’s Munich Nibelung frescoes, 1820s–60s** — monumental national-Romantic iconography; Kriemhild is alternately Marian bride and anguished widow. **[MI]**
- **Emil Lauffer, *Kriemhild’s Accusation*, 1879** — monumental historicist painting. **[MI]**
- **Arthur Rackham’s illustrations for Wagner’s *Ring*, 1910–11** — visualize Gutrune, not the medieval heroine in an evidential sense. **[MI]**
- Modern statues and fountains at Worms are municipal literary commemoration. **[MI]**

### Film and television

- **Fritz Lang, *Die Nibelungen*, 1924:** Part I, *Siegfried*; Part II, *Kriemhilds Rache*. The second film makes Kriemhild a severe, monumental avenger and uses architecture and mass choreography to turn the epic into modern myth. **[MI]**
- **Harald Reinl, *Die Nibelungen*, 1966–67** — color spectacle that softens and rearranges medieval motivations. **[MI]**
- Numerous German television, stage, and festival productions reinterpret Kriemhild through gender politics and the memory of nationalism. **[MI]**

### Political appropriation

**[MSR/modern political history]** From the late eighteenth century, the *Nibelungenlied* was increasingly presented as an indigenous German national epic. “Nibelung loyalty” (*Nibelungentreue*) became a political metaphor for unconditional alliance and self-destructive fidelity; after the First World War the complex became entangled with the “stab-in-the-back” myth, and National Socialism exploited Nibelung endurance and racialized Germanic heroism. Göring’s 1943 Stalingrad rhetoric invoked the Nibelungs’ annihilation. This appropriation usually privileged Hagen, Siegfried, and the doomed Burgundian band, but Kriemhild’s revenge supplied the internal catastrophe. Modern scholarship explicitly treats the nationalist and National Socialist inheritance as reception, not the medieval poem’s timeless political message. [Goethezeitportal overview](https://www.goethezeitportal.de/wissen/projektepool/rezeption-nibelungen/politische-rezeption-der-nibelungen.html) [University of Bayreuth research record](https://eref.uni-bayreuth.de/id/eprint/87725/)

### Psychology and comparative mythology

- **Otto Rank and early psychoanalytic myth study:** family romance, incest, rivalry, and hero birth/death patterns were applied broadly to heroic legend; such schemes do not constitute historical evidence for Guðrún. **[MSR]**
- **Lord Raglan:** hero-pattern morphology poorly fits Guðrún because it privileges male royal biographies. **[MSR]**
- **Joseph Campbell:** the monomyth can redescribe parts of her suffering but suppresses the tradition’s incompatible endings and is not a dating method. **[MSR]**
- **Georges Dumézil:** trifunctional Indo-European comparison influenced Germanic studies, but no consensus reconstruction derives Guðrún from a single Indo-European goddess or function. **[MSR]**
- **Structuralist and feminist readings:** have productively examined exchange of women, feud, speech, grief, and the denial of legal remedy. They are modern interpretations, not latent “original myths.” **[MSR]**

### Commercial and popular culture

Kriemhild/Gudrun appears in fantasy novels, manga, anime, role-playing and mobile games, often as a warrior queen or magical “Avenger.” Weapons, elemental powers, armor, divine status, and romantic pairings not found in the medieval texts are modern inventions. **[MI]** Any claim that she historically possessed a named demonic sword of her own, ruled an underworld, or was worshipped as a revenge goddess should be rejected unless explicitly presented as adaptation.

## 8. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### One heroine or a conflation?

**[MSR]** Traditional scholarship often treated Guðrún and Kriemhild as regional forms of one inherited heroine. That remains useful at the level of the shared narrative network, but it can conceal major differences. Northern Guðrún’s mother is Grímhildr, while continental Kriemhild herself bears the cognate name. Northern Atli is a treacherous killer of her brothers; German Etzel is largely innocent of the plot. A better formulation is that the texts preserve related but repeatedly recomposed roles.

### Historicity

**[MSR]** The historical anchors—Gundahar, Burgundian destruction, Attila, Ildico, and Ermanaric—do not prove a historical woman who experienced the combined plot. Jordanes’ Ildico neither kills Attila nor belongs to the Burgundian royal family. The centuries separating events from manuscripts permitted extensive recombination. “Kriemhild was Ildico” remains an attractive name-and-role hypothesis, not a fact.

### Dating Eddic poetry

**[MSR]** Linguistic archaism, meter, inherited vocabulary, geographical references, intertextuality, and worldview have produced dates ranging from the Viking Age to the thirteenth century. The only firm common point for most Guðrún poems is the c. 1270 manuscript. Thorvaldsen emphasizes that dating oral-derived Eddic poetry cannot be reduced to manuscript date or presumed pagan content. [Cambridge, “The Dating of Eddic Poetry”](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/handbook-to-eddic-poetry/dating-of-eddic-poetry/A0F5C950CBEC52D0D522A1CBAC71D388)

### The “older northern version” problem

Older scholarship frequently called the Scandinavian revenge-on-Atli plot the “older” form and the German revenge-on-brothers plot “younger.” **[MSR]** That may describe a reconstruction based on perceived historical fit—Attila kills Burgundians, then a wife kills Attila—but surviving textual chronology does not straightforwardly confirm it: the *Nibelungenlied* is earlier than the Codex Regius manuscript, and Snorri is only slightly later than the German poem. Direction of borrowing and recomposition must be argued episode by episode.

### Oral-formulaic and performance questions

**[MSR]** Repetition, formulaic diction, variable episodes, and contradictions support a long history of oral performance. They do not license reconstruction of a single word-for-word Migration-period poem. Codex Regius is an organized literary artifact; the *Nibelungenlied* is a sophisticated courtly composition; *Völsunga saga* is an authorial prose synthesis.

### The missing lacuna

**[SEA manuscript fact]** Codex Regius has a physical lacuna in the Sigurðr cycle. Modern editions use *Völsunga saga*, quotations, and conjecture to describe missing material, but no editor can reproduce the lost leaves verbatim. Claims to possess “the complete original Edda story” are therefore false.

### Andreas Heusler, Theodore Andersson, and narrative stratification

**[MSR]** Heusler’s influential models reconstructed stages from short heroic lays to expansive epic. Theodore M. Andersson’s *The Legend of Brynhild* analyzes the competing northern and German structures and the transfer of roles among Brynhild, Guðrún, and Kriemhild. Such models clarify narrative development but remain reconstructions from late evidence.

### Simek, Turville-Petre, Orchard, Tolkien, and Rowe

- **Rudolf Simek** supplies lexical and mythological reference work but must not be used to turn heroic mortals into cult gods. **[MSR]**
- **E. O. G. Turville-Petre** situates heroic poetry within Old Norse religion and literature while recognizing Christian manuscript mediation. **[MSR]**
- **Andy Orchard’s *Critical Companion to Beowulf*** maps the broader Germanic heroic lexicon; *Beowulf* remains contextual, not a Gudrun witness. **[MSR]**
- **Tolkien’s “Beowulf: The Monsters and the Critics” (1936)** changed evaluation of heroic poetry as art rather than historical quarry; his later Gudrun poems are creative reconstruction. **[MSR/MI]**
- **Elizabeth Ashman Rowe’s work on *fornaldarsögur*** stresses their manuscript, political, and late-medieval contexts, correcting the habit of treating them as transparent pagan survivals. **[MSR]**
- **The Skaldic Project** provides edited, attributed skaldic corpora and guards against mislabeling anonymous Eddic verse as securely Viking-Age testimony. **[MSR/editorial]**

### Gender and ethics

**[MSR]** Scholarship divides over whether Kriemhild is monstrous, heroic, victimized, or all three. The texts themselves sustain incompatible evaluations. She exposes male deceit, loses husband and property, and is denied effective justice; she also engineers mass death. Northern Guðrún suffers coercive marriage and fraternal slaughter, then murders innocent children. Neither condemnation nor exoneration should erase the narrated violence.

### State of evidence

- No pre-1200 text securely names her.
- No inscription or archaeological object securely depicts or dedicates itself to her.
- The German and northern branches survive in manuscripts produced in Christian literate societies.
- The Eddic corpus is internally contradictory.
- The *Nibelungenlied* also exists in materially different manuscript redactions.
- The fifteenth-century Hundeshagen illustrations are evidence for late-medieval reception, not Migration-period costume or events.
- The Ildico identification is plausible but unverifiable.
- Cult, shrine, tomb, and relic claims are unsupported.
