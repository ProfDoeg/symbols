# Atli (Etzel, the Attila of legend): Research Dossier

**Traditional class:** mortal epic king: ruler of the Huns, husband or patron of heroic figures, and—depending on the tradition—treacherous treasure-seeker, bereaved host, imperial protector, or doomed victim. He is not a demigod or cult hero.

**Earliest secure legendary attestation:** Old English **Ætla**, “ruler of the Huns,” in *Widsith* 18, preserved in the late tenth-century Exeter Book. The surviving manuscript is secure; claims that this line or the poem’s catalogue substantially preserves sixth-, seventh-, or eighth-century oral material remain scholarly reconstructions. The historical Attila is, of course, documented independently in fifth- and sixth-century Greek and Latin historiography.

## Evidentiary key

- **[EA] Secure early attestation:** contemporary historical testimony or the earliest surviving legendary witness.
- **[LC] Later canonical elaboration:** a major medieval literary shaping of inherited material.
- **[RV] Regional variant:** a geographically or linguistically distinctive version.
- **[LS] Learned systematization:** chronicle, genealogy, antiquarian synthesis, or scholarly harmonization.
- **[SA] Syncretistic identification:** explicit fusion or equation with another figure or tradition.
- **[MA] Medieval/early-modern accretion:** development not secure in the oldest evidence.
- **[F] Folklore:** locally reported popular tradition without corresponding early documentary proof.
- **[MSR] Modern scholarly reconstruction:** an inference from comparison, linguistics, or historical context.
- **[MI] Modern invention or reuse:** modern artistic, political, commercial, or pseudohistorical construction.

## 1. Identification, names and epithets

Atli is the Old Norse legendary transformation of Attila, the fifth-century king of the Huns; Etzel is the corresponding Middle High German form, and Ætla the Old English form. These names do not denote a stable literary personality. *Widsith* remembers Ætla simply as a pre-eminent ruler. The Eddic lays make Atli Guðrún’s avaricious husband, who invites her brothers Gunnar and Hǫgni to his court, kills them for their treasure, and is killed by Guðrún. The *Nibelungenlied* makes Etzel a wealthy, hospitable and generally blameless widower whose second wife Kriemhild engineers the Burgundians’ destruction at his court. German Dietrich poetry makes him the generous protector of Dietrich von Bern. The Norwegian *Þiðreks saga* incorporates both favorable and hostile components and ultimately has him starved to death through the vengeance of Hǫgni’s son.

### Etymology

The traditional Germanic derivation takes *Attila* from Gothic or another East Germanic language: *atta*, “father,” plus diminutive *-ila*, hence “little father” or “dear father.” It is phonologically straightforward and has long been the majority explanation. Competing Turkic derivations connect the name with *at* “horse,” *atlı* “horseman,” or an alleged title meaning “oceanic/universal ruler”; M. Snædal and others have objected that no proposed Turkic analysis has achieved consensus. A 2025 proposal instead derives it from a Yeniseian/Arinic adjective meaning approximately “rather quick.” These are etymological hypotheses, not proofs of Attila’s ethnicity or the Hunnic language. **[MSR]** A recent linguistic survey also cautions that the historical name may have coalesced with an independently possible Germanic *Atli*, sometimes related to Old Norse *atall*, “fierce.” The safe conclusion is that Germanic speakers naturalized an already famous royal name; the ultimate origin remains disputed.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### Historical substrate

The historical Attila ruled jointly with Bleda and then alone until 453. Priscus visited his court in 449 and wrote a Greek history now surviving only in fragments and excerpts. **[EA]** Priscus is therefore the principal near-contemporary witness but does not tell the later Nibelung story.

Jordanes, *Getica* 254–258, written c. 551 and explicitly attributing the account to Priscus, reports that Attila married the beautiful **Ildico** after numerous other wives; after the wedding feast he was found dead from a hemorrhage while Ildico wept beside him. Jordanes then describes the ceremonial lament and secret burial with gold, silver and iron, followed by the killing of those who performed the burial. **[EA]** Jordanes does not accuse Ildico of murder, connect her to the Burgundians, identify a river burial, or mention Gunnar, Hǫgni, Guðrún or the Nibelung treasure. The familiar “three coffins under a diverted river” story is a later amplification of Jordanes’ three burial metals. [Jordanes, *Getica*](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Jordanes/Getica/D%2A.html)

The Burgundian disaster belongs to a separate historical event. Hydatius and the *Gallic Chronicle of 452* attribute the destruction of the Rhine Burgundians and death of their king Gundaharius in 436/437 to Aetius and Hunnic auxiliaries—not demonstrably to Attila personally. **[MSR]** The legendary fusion of Gundaharius/Gunnar’s death with Attila’s court is therefore historical condensation, not a recoverable report of one event.

### Germanic and Norse corpus

1. **Old English *Widsith***, Old English; surviving in the Exeter Book, c. 960–990. Ætla rules the Huns at line 18 and later appears within the poem’s panoramic heroic geography. **[EA]** Nothing is said about his wife, death, treasure, snake pit or Burgundian guests. The poem’s earlier oral history and proposed eighth-century shape remain disputed. [Old English Poetry Project text](https://oldenglishpoetry.camden.rutgers.edu/widsith/)

2. **Latin *Waltharius***, probably ninth or tenth century, preserved in medieval manuscripts. Attila receives Walter of Aquitaine, Hildegund of Burgundy and Hagen as noble hostages. He treats Walter favorably and makes him a commander; Walter and Hildegund escape with treasure. **[RV/LC]** This is the hospitable imperial Attila tradition, not the Eddic murder plot. [Harvard digital *Waltharius*](https://waltharius.fas.harvard.edu/Main_Page)

3. **The Poetic Edda**, Old Norse. Its principal witness, Reykjavík GKS 2365 4to, the Codex Regius, was written c. 1270–1280. Individual poems are older but rarely datable with precision; ninth-century dates for *Atlakviða* and eleventh-century dates for *Oddrúnargrátr* or *Atlamál* are scholarly judgments, not manuscript dates. A modern electronic edition identifies the manuscript and its palaeographical dating. [Codex Regius electronic edition](https://eae.ku.dk/q?p=eae%2Fvols%2Ftext%2F1)

   Relevant items include:

   - *Brot af Sigurðarkviðu*: Atli is part of Brynhild’s dynastic world and the future Guðrún story is anticipated. **[LC]**
   - *Sigurðarkviða in skamma*: Brynhild identifies Atli as her brother; Guðrún’s later marriage to him is foretold. **[LC]**
   - *Helreið Brynhildar*: Brynhild recalls her kin and tragic relations. **[LC]**
   - *Guðrúnarkviða II*: Guðrún narrates how her family compelled her to marry Atli; he dreams of disaster and she interprets the dreams. **[LC]**
   - *Guðrúnarkviða III*: Atli’s concubine or serving woman Herkja accuses Guðrún of adultery with Þjóðrekr; Guðrún proves herself by ordeal, and Herkja is punished. **[LC/MA]**
   - *Oddrúnargrátr*: Atli’s sister Oddrún recounts her forbidden love for Gunnar; Atli obstructs their marriage and is implicated in Gunnar’s death. **[LC/RV]**
   - *Atlakviða in grœnlenzka*: the compact principal Atli lay—invitation, warning, betrayal, treasure demand, deaths of Hǫgni and Gunnar, Guðrún’s revenge. Its “Greenlandic” rubric may be a scribal displacement; an early, perhaps ninth- or tenth-century composition remains widely argued but not secure. **[LC/MSR]** [Old Norse text](https://heimskringla.no/wiki/Atlakvi%C3%B0a)
   - *Atlamál in grœnlenzku*: a much longer domestic and psychological retelling, often placed near 1100 and more plausibly connected with Greenland. It adds wives’ dreams, children, dialogue and household realism. **[LC/RV/MSR]**
   - Prose before *Guðrúnarhvöt* and *Guðrúnarhvöt* itself: Guðrún survives her killing of Atli and enters a third marriage. **[LC]**

4. **Hátalykill inn forni**, Old Norse skaldic catalogue by Rǫgnvaldr Kali Kolsson and Hallr Þórarinsson, c. 1140s. It briefly invokes Gunnar’s snake-pit death under Atli. **[EA/LC]** This is an independent twelfth-century witness that the episode circulated before the Codex Regius was written.

5. **Snorri Sturluson’s *Edda***, especially *Skáldskaparmál*, c. 1220. It systematizes the Niflung/Völsung material for poetic instruction. **[LS]**

6. ***Vǫlsunga saga***, Old Norse prose, conventionally c. 1250–1270, surviving in the fifteenth-century manuscript NKS 1824 b 4to. Chapters 34–41 synthesize Eddic material: forced marriage, altered rune-message, prophetic dreams, journey, battle, heart-cutting, snake pit, cannibal feast, murder and hall-burning. **[LC/LS]** Its prose cannot automatically be treated as older than the poems it paraphrases. [Saga contents and textual orientation](https://www.viking.ucla.edu/others/SagaVolsungstoc.html)

7. ***Norna-Gests þáttr***, probably late thirteenth century. A learned prose integration of Völsung material into a Christian royal setting; Atli belongs to the remembered heroic past. **[LS/MA]**

8. ***Þiðreks saga af Bern***, Old Norse prose composed in Norway, usually c. 1250, professedly from German oral reports; oldest substantial manuscript Stockholm Perg. fol. 4, c. 1275–1300. It calls the king Atli, locates him in **Susat/Soest**, gives him a separate ancestry, makes his court the convergence point for numerous heroes, recounts the Niflungs’ fall, and supplies a distinctive revenge-death for Atli. **[RV/LC/LS]**

9. ***Nibelungenlied***, Middle High German, composed c. 1200; principal vellum manuscripts date from the thirteenth century. Etzel dominates the second half as Kriemhild’s second husband and host of the Burgundians. Manuscript recensions *AB and *C alter emphases; none makes Etzel the deliberate architect of the massacre. **[LC/RV]** The major manuscripts and the attached *Nibelungenklage* are documented in the UNESCO nomination dossier. [UNESCO manuscript dossier](https://media.unesco.org/sites/default/files/webform/mow001/germany_song_of_the_nibelungs.pdf)

10. ***Nibelungenklage***, Middle High German, early thirteenth century and commonly transmitted after the *Nibelungenlied*. It narrates the mourning, dissemination of news and aftermath; strikingly, it says no one knows what ultimately became of Etzel. **[LC]**

11. **German Dietrich epics**, principally thirteenth century:

   - *Dietrichs Flucht*: Etzel and Helche shelter the exiled Dietrich and finance his campaigns. **[RV/LC]**
   - *Rabenschlacht*: Etzel’s sons Orte and Scharpfe accompany Dietrich and are killed by Witege; Etzel and Helche grieve and ultimately forgive Dietrich. **[RV/LC]**
   - *Biterolf und Dietleib*: Etzel is an imperial patron and military overlord. **[RV/LC]**
   - *Rosengarten zu Worms*, *Alpharts Tod* and related Dietrich material place Etzel within the same heroic geopolitical system, although his narrative importance varies. **[RV/MA]**
   - Late Heldenbuch prose attempts to arrange the contradictory heroic cycles into history. **[LS/MA]**

12. **Hungarian chronicles**, especially Simon of Kéza’s *Gesta Hunnorum et Hungarorum* (c. 1282–1285) and the fourteenth-century Hungarian chronicle tradition, recast Attila as an ancestral ruler within a Hun–Hungarian succession. Some assimilate German heroic names and Etzelburg traditions. **[SA/LS]** These texts concern Attila’s broader medieval legendary afterlife but are not sources for the original Eddic plot.

### Sources named in the brief that do not attest this Atli

- ***Beowulf*** does not name Ætla/Atli/Etzel. Its relevance is comparative: it preserves the same broad Migration-Age heroic-memory environment and illustrates how historical peoples and kings were recomposed in vernacular poetry. Tolkien’s “Beowulf: The Monsters and the Critics” concerns the poem’s poetic integrity, not Atli. **Absence is the finding.**
- ***Grettis saga*** has important *Beowulf* analogues but no identifiable Attila-Atli. **Absence.**
- ***Egils saga* and the Íslendingasögur** contain men called Atli because it was an ordinary Scandinavian personal name; those persons must not be merged with the Hunnic king. **False-positive names.**
- ***Hervarar saga*** contains war between Goths and Huns but no secure Atli/Attila character. Its Hun king is Humli. **Absence of this figure.**
- ***Ragnars saga loðbrókar*, *Gautreks saga* and Saxo Grammaticus** contain names and motifs adjacent to heroic legend but do not provide an independent dossier episode for Attila-Atli. **Absence.**
- The Skaldic Poetry of the Scandinavian Middle Ages database is useful for *Hátalykill* and name-searching, but unrelated historical skalds named Atli are not the legendary king.

## 3. The story, cycle by cycle

### A. The historical death transformed

**[EA]** Jordanes’ version: Attila marries Ildico, celebrates heavily, suffers a fatal hemorrhage in bed, and is found dead while she mourns. His warriors lament him and bury him secretly.

**[MSR]** A widespread scholarly model holds that three memories converged:

1. the Burgundian king Gundaharius died in the Roman-Hunnic destruction of 436/437;
2. Attila died in 453 beside a Germanically named bride, Ildico;
3. a rumor or poetic reinterpretation made the bride responsible for his death.

This model explains—but cannot document step by step—how the bride became the avenger of slain Burgundian kinsmen. The equation **Ildico → Kriemhild/Grímhild → Guðrún** is philologically attractive because *Ildico* may represent Germanic *Hildikō*, “little Hild,” but the names are not identical and the surviving early historical source does not call Ildico Burgundian or murderous. **[MSR]** It is therefore best described as a likely seed or catalyst, not a demonstrated biography of the legendary heroine.

### B. *Atlakviða*: gold, betrayal and vengeance

**[LC; perhaps early within the Eddic corpus]**

Atli sends Knéfrǫðr to invite Gunnar and Hǫgni to his hall, promising weapons, wealth and lands. Guðrún covertly sends a warning, including a ring wound with wolf-hair, but the brothers travel despite ominous signs. At Atli’s stronghold Guðrún warns them; battle follows.

Atli demands the gold associated with Sigurðr and the Gjúkungs. Hǫgni’s heart is cut out. Gunnar refuses to reveal the treasure until he knows Hǫgni is dead, because thereafter he alone possesses the secret. Gunnar is thrown into a snake enclosure; the poem’s surviving form associates his death with serpents, while the elaborate harp-playing belongs more clearly to later tellings.

Guðrún kills her and Atli’s sons, has their skulls made into drinking vessels, serves their blood and roasted hearts to their father, and only afterward tells him what he has consumed. She then kills Atli in bed and burns the hall. The poem neither moralizes the cannibalism away nor excuses Atli’s torture and murder. It makes dynastic hospitality a trap and Guðrún’s maternal violence the final mechanism of blood vengeance.

### C. *Atlamál*: domestic expansion

**[LC/RV; probably eleventh or early twelfth century]**

The same plot becomes a long household tragedy. Atli’s invitation is mediated by **Vingi**, whose treachery is made explicit. Hǫgni’s wife **Kostbera** reads the damaged runes and dreams of danger; Gunnar’s wife **Glaumvǫr** has parallel dreams. The men reinterpret or dismiss the omens.

Atli’s household fights Gunnar and Hǫgni’s party. Guðrún arms herself and joins her brothers in battle. The executioners first cut out the heart of the coward **Hjalli** as a test or deception; Gunnar recognizes it as a coward’s heart because it trembles. Hǫgni laughs as his own heart is cut out. Gunnar is killed by serpents after playing a harp with his toes.

Guðrún kills the boys **Erpr** and **Eitill**, serves Atli their flesh, and uses their skulls as cups. In this version she and **Hniflungr**, Hǫgni’s son, stab Atli. The hall is burned afterward. **Variant:** *Atlakviða* centers Guðrún’s solitary vengeance; *Atlamál* supplies a male co-avenger and far more domestic motivation.

### D. Other Eddic retrospections

- ***Guðrúnarkviða II*:** Grímhild and Guðrún’s brothers press her into marrying Atli with treasure and dynastic argument. Atli dreams that his sons are plants or animals destroyed; Guðrún’s interpretations partly conceal the coming reality. **[LC]**
- ***Guðrúnarkviða III*:** Herkja accuses Guðrún of sleeping with Þjóðrekr. Atli confronts Guðrún. She undergoes a boiling-water ordeal and is vindicated; Herkja fails and is drowned in a bog. This is a Christian judicial motif attached to the heroic court. **[MA]**
- ***Oddrúnargrátr*:** Oddrún says she and Gunnar loved each other, but Atli would not allow the match. Brynhild was instead married into the Gjúkung family. The poem relocates part of the tragedy into Atli’s control of women’s marriages. **[LC/RV]**
- **After Atli:** Guðrún attempts suicide by entering the sea but is carried to King Jónakr’s land, marries him, and has further children. Atli’s death is thus an episode within Guðrún’s much longer tragic cycle, not its conclusion. **[LC]**

### E. *Vǫlsunga saga*

**[LC/LS, thirteenth century]**

The saga joins the poems into continuous prose. Atli is Buðli’s son and Brynhild’s brother. Guðrún is induced to marry him after Sigurðr’s death. Atli covets Sigurðr’s gold and also alleges vengeance for Sigurðr against her brothers. Guðrún’s runic warning is altered by Vingi. Kostbera and Glaumvǫr’s dreams are elaborated. Gunnar and Hǫgni kill Vingi once he confesses his deceit, then fight Atli’s army.

The saga fully develops the two-heart episode, Gunnar’s toe-harping, and the serpent that finally bites his heart. Guðrún and Hǫgni’s son Niflungr stab Atli in bed and burn the hall. **Variant:** the saga makes Atli’s pretext—avenging Sigurðr—more explicit while retaining greed as his central motive.

### F. *Nibelungenlied*: gracious Etzel and the Burgundian catastrophe

**[LC/RV, c. 1200]**

Etzel is the widowed king of a vast Hunnish realm. His first wife **Helche** has died. He sends **Rüdiger of Bechelaren** to seek the hand of Kriemhild, Siegfried’s widow. She hesitates because Etzel is a pagan; Rüdiger’s personal oath and the prospect of power make the marriage acceptable. Etzel is even represented as potentially open to baptism. They have a son, **Ortlieb**.

After years at Etzel’s court, Kriemhild persuades him to invite her brothers Gunther, Gernot and Giselher and their vassal Hagen. Etzel receives them generously. He does not know that Kriemhild intends vengeance for Siegfried. Tension becomes violence through Kriemhild’s inducements, Blœdel’s attack on the Burgundian squires, and Hagen’s retaliation. Hagen decapitates the child Ortlieb before his parents. Etzel grieves and becomes a participant in the war only after his hospitality and household have been destroyed.

Rüdiger is trapped between sworn service to Etzel and bonds of friendship and marriage with the Burgundians. Dietrich remains Etzel’s guest but finally captures Gunther and Hagen. Kriemhild has Gunther killed and herself beheads Hagen with Siegfried’s sword. Hildebrand then kills her. Etzel survives amid universal bereavement.

This is not a softened version of *Atlakviða* in which only a few details differ. Responsibility is structurally reversed:

| Eddic/Norse Atli | *Nibelungenlied* Etzel |
|---|---|
| Invites the brothers to seize their gold | Invites them at Kriemhild’s request, without knowing her full plan |
| Orders Hǫgni’s heart cut out | Does not torture Hagen for the treasure |
| Orders Gunnar’s snake-pit death | Attempts to behave as host and peacemaker until violence overwhelms the court |
| Is fed his murdered sons by Guðrún | Sees his son Ortlieb murdered by Hagen |
| Is killed by his wife | Survives Kriemhild, who is killed by Hildebrand |
| Embodies greed and breached kinship | Embodies wealth, hospitality and tragic political helplessness |

The *Nibelungenklage* further rehabilitates survivors and messengers and leaves Etzel’s eventual fate unknown.

### G. *Þiðreks saga*

**[RV/LC/LS, thirteenth century]**

The saga makes Atli a king in Susat/Soest and draws heroic figures from disparate centuries into one world. He is son of King **Ósíð/Osid** in the saga’s constructed genealogy. He marries **Erka**, corresponding broadly to German Helche/Herrat traditions, and becomes lord and protector of Þiðrekr.

In the Niflung episode, Grímhild—not a quietly grieving Guðrún—is active in provoking conflict. The Niflungs fight Atli’s men at Susat. Gunnar is imprisoned and killed by serpents; Hǫgni is mortally wounded but fathers a posthumous avenger, **Aldrian**, on a woman before dying. Grímhild is killed by Þiðrekr after her conduct makes the destruction irretrievable.

The saga then supplies an ending unknown to the *Nibelungenlied*: Aldrian tells Atli that Hǫgni’s treasure is concealed in a mountain or cave, entices him inside, seals the exit, and leaves him to starve. **[RV]** Here Atli’s greed finally produces his death, but Guðrún is no longer his killer.

### H. German Dietrich and Walter cycles

In *Waltharius*, Attila is an imperial conqueror who accepts noble children as hostages, raises them honorably and trusts Walter with military command. Walter and Hildegund deceive him and escape with treasure. Attila’s reaction is anger and dismay, not the calculated extermination of their families.

In *Dietrichs Flucht*, Etzel and Helche receive the dispossessed Dietrich, provide troops and wealth, and function as ideal patrons. In *Rabenschlacht*, their sons Orte and Scharpfe die while campaigning with Dietrich. The bereaved parents initially condemn but eventually forgive him. **[RV]** These poems preserve the “good Etzel” particularly strongly.

## 4. Relations with gods and other heroes

### Genealogical systems

| Tradition | Parents/siblings | Consorts | Children |
|---|---|---|---|
| Historical record | Father Mundzuk; brother Bleda | Numerous wives; Ildico named at his death | Ellac, Dengizich, Ernak and others in historical sources |
| Eddic/*Vǫlsunga saga* | Son of Buðli; brother of Brynhild and Oddrún; Bekkhild also belongs to Buðli’s family in the saga | Guðrún; Herkja is a concubine/household woman in one poem | Erpr and Eitill |
| *Nibelungenlied* | Parentage not narratively important | First Helche, then Kriemhild | Ortlieb by Kriemhild |
| Dietrich epics | Dynastic background varies or is secondary | Helche | Orte and Scharpfe |
| *Þiðreks saga* | Son of Ósíð/Osid in its learned heroic genealogy | Erka; relationship to Grímhild traditions differs by recension | Children vary across the saga complex |

The incompatible pedigrees are part of the evidence. Buðli is not Mundzuk under a securely documented name-change; nor are Erpr, Eitill, Ortlieb, Orte and Scharpfe demonstrably transformations of Attila’s historical sons.

### Every documented named interaction

The following entries consolidate repeated versions but identify materially distinct relationships. No surviving Atli/Etzel narrative gives him a direct encounter with Óðinn, Þórr, Freyr, Týr, Loki, a valkyrie acting as a deity, or the Christian God. Divine patronage sometimes attributed to him in modern mythological summaries is therefore **not attested**.

- **Bleda** — Historical co-ruler and brother; later historical sources say Attila killed him, but Bleda has no substantial role in the Atli/Etzel heroic cycle. Priscus fragments/Jordanes, fifth–sixth century. **[EA; historical, not heroic]**
- **Ildico** — Historical final bride, found mourning beside his body; later scholarship associates her with the avenging legendary wife. Jordanes *Getica* 254, c. 551. **[EA/MSR]**
- **Guðrún Gjúkadóttir** — Wife compelled into marriage; warns her brothers; fights Atli’s forces; kills their sons, feeds them to him, and kills him. Eddic lays, probably ninth–twelfth centuries in composition, manuscript c. 1270; *Vǫlsunga saga*, c. 1250–1270. **[LC]**
- **Kriemhild** — Second wife; uses Etzel’s court and forces to avenge Siegfried; predeceases him. *Nibelungenlied*, c. 1200. **[RV/LC]**
- **Grímhild** — In Norse tradition, Guðrún’s mother and a broker of her marriage to Atli; in *Þiðreks saga*, the corresponding woman helps precipitate the Niflung conflict. Eddic poems, *Vǫlsunga saga*, *Þiðreks saga*. **[LC/RV]**
- **Brynhild** — Atli’s sister in the Eddic/Völsung genealogy; her death helps motivate his asserted grievance against the Gjúkungs. *Sigurðarkviða in skamma*, *Vǫlsunga saga*. **[LC]**
- **Oddrún** — Atli’s sister; he prevents or fails to sanction her union with Gunnar. *Oddrúnargrátr*, commonly dated eleventh century, manuscript c. 1270. **[LC/RV]**
- **Buðli** — Atli’s father in Norse genealogy. Eddic poems and *Vǫlsunga saga*. **[LC]**
- **Bekkhild** — Atli’s sister or close Buðlung kinswoman in *Vǫlsunga saga*’s genealogy. Thirteenth century. **[LC/LS]**
- **Sigurðr** — Atli’s brother-in-law through Brynhild and Guðrún; Atli invokes vengeance for Sigurðr while seeking his treasure. Eddic cycle and *Vǫlsunga saga*. **[LC]**
- **Siegfried** — Deceased predecessor as Kriemhild’s husband; Etzel does not meet him in the *Nibelungenlied*, and Kriemhild’s loyalty to Siegfried motivates the catastrophe. *Nibelungenlied*, c. 1200. **[RV/LC; indirect interaction only]**
- **Gunnar** — Brother-in-law and victim; Atli demands the treasure and has him killed in a serpent pit. Eddic lays and *Vǫlsunga saga*. In *Þiðreks saga*, Atli imprisons him and serpents kill him. **[LC/RV]**
- **Gunther** — German counterpart of Gunnar; Etzel welcomes him as guest and relative, and Dietrich captures him after the hall battle. Kriemhild has him killed. *Nibelungenlied*, c. 1200. **[RV/LC]**
- **Hǫgni** — Brother-in-law and victim; Atli orders his heart cut out. Eddic lays and *Vǫlsunga saga*. **[LC]**
- **Hagen** — German counterpart/relative of Hǫgni; Etzel receives him, witnesses or learns of his killing of Ortlieb, and fights against him. *Nibelungenlied*, c. 1200. **[RV/LC]**
- **Gernot** — Burgundian guest and Kriemhild’s brother; dies at Etzel’s court. *Nibelungenlied*. **[RV/LC]**
- **Giselher** — Burgundian guest, Kriemhild’s brother, and Rüdiger’s prospective son-in-law; dies in the battle. *Nibelungenlied*. **[RV/LC]**
- **Guthormr** — Guðrún’s brother and Sigurðr’s killer; belongs to the kin-group whose actions underpin Atli’s asserted vengeance but has no developed encounter with Atli. Eddic/Völsung material. **[LC; indirect]**
- **Erpr and Eitill** — Atli and Guðrún’s sons; murdered by their mother and served to Atli as food and drink. *Atlakviða*, *Atlamál*, *Vǫlsunga saga*. **[LC]**
- **Ortlieb** — Etzel and Kriemhild’s young son; Etzel presents him to the Burgundians, after which Hagen decapitates him. *Nibelungenlied*. **[RV/LC]**
- **Hjalli** — Atli’s servant or swineherd whose trembling heart is initially cut out in place of Hǫgni’s. *Atlamál* and *Vǫlsunga saga*. **[LC/MA]**
- **Knéfrǫðr** — Atli’s envoy, who delivers the invitation to Gunnar and Hǫgni. *Atlakviða*. **[LC]**
- **Vingi** — Atli’s treacherous messenger; alters or defeats Guðrún’s warning and is killed by the brothers. *Atlamál*, *Vǫlsunga saga*. **[LC/MA]**
- **Kostbera** — Hǫgni’s wife; tries to prevent the fatal journey by reading Guðrún’s runes and interpreting dreams. Atli is the threatened danger rather than her direct interlocutor. *Atlamál*, *Vǫlsunga saga*. **[LC]**
- **Glaumvǫr** — Gunnar’s wife; dreams of the catastrophe at Atli’s court and warns him. *Atlamál*, *Vǫlsunga saga*. **[LC]**
- **Hniflungr/Niflungr** — Hǫgni’s son; helps Guðrún kill Atli in expanded Norse versions. *Atlamál*, *Vǫlsunga saga*. **[LC]**
- **Herkja** — Woman of Atli’s household who accuses Guðrún of adultery; Atli orders or accepts the ordeal, after which Herkja is drowned. *Guðrúnarkviða III*. **[MA]**
- **Þjóðrekr/Þiðrekr/Dietrich von Bern** — Guest and champion at Atli/Etzel’s court. In *Guðrúnarkviða III* he is the alleged partner in Guðrún’s adultery; in *Þiðreks saga* and German epics Etzel protects him; in the *Nibelungenlied* Dietrich captures Gunther and Hagen. **[RV/LC]**
- **Hildebrand** — Dietrich’s retainer at Etzel’s court; kills Kriemhild after she kills Hagen. *Nibelungenlied*. **[RV/LC]**
- **Rüdiger of Bechelaren** — Etzel’s envoy, vassal and mediator; arranges the marriage to Kriemhild, receives the Burgundians, and dies fighting them under conflicting oaths. *Nibelungenlied*, *Klage*, Dietrich epics. **[RV/LC]**
- **Helche** — Etzel’s first wife in German tradition; remembered as an exemplary queen and, in Dietrich poetry, mother of Orte and Scharpfe. *Nibelungenlied* retrospectively; *Dietrichs Flucht*, *Rabenschlacht*. **[RV/LC]**
- **Erka** — *Þiðreks saga*’s corresponding royal wife, daughter of Osantrix. Thirteenth century. **[RV/SA]**
- **Blœdel/Blödelin** — Etzel’s brother in the *Nibelungenlied*; Kriemhild induces him to attack the Burgundian squires, beginning the massacre. **[RV/LC]**

- **Ósíð/Osid** — Atli’s father in *Þiðreks saga*’s constructed genealogy. **[RV/LS]**
- **Osantrix** — King connected by marriage and warfare to Atli in *Þiðreks saga*. **[RV/LC]**

## 5. Cult, tomb and ritual

### Cult

No securely identified shrine, *hof*, *hörgr*, *heroon*, altar, dedicatory inscription, recurring sacrifice, priesthood, sacred game or oracle of Atli/Etzel is known. He is an epic king, not a heroized dead with demonstrable cult. **[Documented absence]**

Modern references to a “cult of Attila” generally mean political or ancestral veneration, particularly in Hungarian national mythology, not archaeologically documented pre-Christian worship. Medieval Hungarian chronicles made Attila a predecessor of Hungarian kings; nineteenth- and twentieth-century nationalism intensified that ancestral use. **[LS/MI]**

### Tomb and burial

Jordanes is the sole substantial ancient narrative source for the funeral. He reports ceremonial display, three metals, secret burial and the killing of the grave-workers. He does not identify a site. No excavated tomb has been securely attributed to Attila. [Survey of the tomb evidence](https://www.livescience.com/where-is-attila-the-hun-buried)

Claims for burial:

- **Under the Tisza or another diverted river:** **[F/MA]** later elaboration; absent from Jordanes.
- **Triple nested gold, silver and iron coffins:** **[MA/F]** rationalizes Jordanes’ metals into coffins.
- **Buda:** the twelfth-century *Kaiserchronik* places Attila’s burial there. **[MA/LS]**
- **Specific Hungarian, Romanian or Transcarpathian mounds:** **[F/MI]** repeatedly asserted, never confirmed archaeologically.
- **Catastrophe if the grave is opened:** **[F/MI]** modern treasure-lore, not an ancient attestation.

The Eddic Atli receives no cultic burial. In some readings the hall-burning is effectively his cremation, but it is Guðrún’s destructive revenge, not a prescribed funerary rite.

### Images and material culture

No surviving Migration-period image can securely be identified as the legendary Atli. Scandinavian images of Gunnar in the snake pit—on medieval carved objects or church art—attest reception of the Niflung episode, but usually show Gunnar and serpents, not a recognizable Atli. **[MA]** They must not be labeled “portraits of Attila.”

The major *Nibelungenlied* manuscripts are textual books rather than a consistent medieval iconographic cycle. Later illustrated manuscripts, prints and paintings visualize Etzel using the costume and monarchy of their own periods. **[MA]** Such images document reception, not Attila’s appearance.

## 6. Historical evolution of the figure

### 449–sixth century: historical memory

Priscus’ eyewitness court account and Jordanes’ derivative death narrative establish Attila as a real ruler whose court included many peoples and whose death occurred beside Ildico. **[EA]** Ecclesiastical historiography increasingly casts him as a providential scourge; Germanic heroic memory can instead remember an immensely rich patron.

### Ninth–tenth centuries: first secure heroic literature

*Waltharius* presents the generous imperial captor and patron. *Widsith* ranks Ætla first among famous rulers in its catalogue. **[EA/RV]** Neither source contains the complete Nibelung catastrophe.

### Ninth–twelfth centuries: Scandinavian tragic formation

The Eddic tradition turns Atli into Guðrún’s husband and the murderer of her brothers. Greed for treasure, the treacherous invitation and revenge by a bride/widow organize disparate Migration-period memories into kin tragedy. *Atlakviða* represents the compressed form; *Atlamál* expands it into domestic narrative. **[LC/MSR]**

### c. 1140–1300: literary consolidation

*Hátalykill* confirms the snake-pit episode’s circulation. The *Nibelungenlied* produces the courtly, hospitable Etzel; the *Klage* reflects on the aftermath. *Vǫlsunga saga* harmonizes Eddic poems into prose. *Þiðreks saga* assembles German heroic reports into an enormous narrative world. Dietrich epics make Etzel an exemplary patron and grieving father. **[LC/LS]**

### Later Middle Ages: chronicle and national genealogy

German Heldenbücher regularize the cycles; Hungarian chroniclers join Huns and Hungarians and place Attila inside a national succession. **[LS/SA]** Christian chroniclers alternate between providential scourge, pagan emperor, convert-at-the-last-moment, ancestor and model king.

### Early modern period: antiquarian survival

Printed Heldenbücher, chronicles and vernacular retellings keep Etzel visible, but the medieval heroic poems cease to occupy the cultural center. The figure is increasingly split between “historical Attila the barbarian” and “Etzel of heroic poetry.”

### Eighteenth–nineteenth centuries: rediscovery and national epic

The rediscovery and editing of the *Nibelungenlied* turned it into a candidate German national epic. Romantic philology treated German and Norse witnesses as fragments of a recoverable primordial saga. Karl Lachmann’s attempt to divide the poem into separate lays was enormously influential but is no longer accepted in its strong form. **[MSR]**

Etzel himself was less useful as a German national hero than Siegfried or Hagen: he was foreign, Hunnish and comparatively passive in the *Nibelungenlied*. Nevertheless, his court supplied the stage for the “heroic loyalty unto destruction” later politicized as *Nibelungentreue*.

### Twentieth–twenty-first centuries

Nationalist and National Socialist rhetoric appropriated the Nibelungs’ doomed resistance. Hermann Göring’s 1943 Stalingrad speech notoriously compared the encircled German army to the Nibelungs in the burning hall. **[MI]** That use selected Hagen-like endurance, not Etzel’s hospitality, from the poem.

Hungarian Turanist and nationalist traditions have meanwhile treated Attila as an ancestral eastern ruler. Their claims belong to medieval chronicle genealogy and modern identity politics, not to archaeology or historical linguistics. **[LS/MI]**

Modern fantasy, film and games often conflate historical Attila, Norse Atli, German Etzel and Wagner’s Nibelung world. **[MI]** That fusion is convenient but textually false.

## 7. Modern reception and reinvention

### Literature and scholarship-shaped creative work

- **Friedrich de la Motte Fouqué, *Der Held des Nordens* (1808–1810):** Romantic reworking of the northern heroic cycle. **[MI]**
- **Friedrich Hebbel, *Die Nibelungen* (1861):** dramatic trilogy based principally on the German tradition; Etzel remains the bereaved ruler whose court becomes the site of revenge. **[MI]**
- **William Morris, *The Story of Sigurd the Volsung and the Fall of the Niblungs* (1876):** English poetic recreation favoring the northern version and its Atli. **[MI]**
- **J. R. R. Tolkien, *The Legend of Sigurd and Gudrún*:** twentieth-century English alliterative poems published posthumously in 2009. Tolkien constructs a deliberate modern synthesis; it is not a translated lost Eddic poem. **[MI]**
- **Modern historical novels about Attila:** usually draw more on Priscus and Roman warfare than on Atli/Etzel. When they add Ildico’s deliberate revenge for Burgundian kin, they adopt the medieval legendary synthesis. **[MI]**

### Opera and music

Richard Wagner’s *Der Ring des Nibelungen* (premiered complete in 1876) is routinely associated with the Nibelung legend, but **Atli/Etzel is not a character in the tetralogy**. Wagner ends with Siegfried and Brünnhilde material before the Atli/Etzel marriage and Burgundian destruction. Calling Wagner’s Ring “the story of Atli” is a modern conflation. **[MI]**

Giuseppe Verdi’s *Attila* (1846) concerns the historical-conqueror legend—Rome, invasion and assassination—not the Eddic Atli or hospitable Etzel. **[MI]**

### Film and visual media

- **Fritz Lang, *Die Nibelungen* (1924), Part II, *Kriemhilds Rache*:** Etzel, played by Rudolf Klein-Rogge, is rendered through expressionist “eastern” spectacle. The film greatly influenced modern visual stereotypes of the Hunnish court. **[MI]**
- **Harald Reinl’s two-part *Die Nibelungen* (1966–1967):** retells the German cycle and includes Etzel’s court as the arena of Kriemhild’s revenge. **[MI]**
- **Television films and fantasy adaptations** frequently splice *Vǫlsunga saga*, *Nibelungenlied* and Wagner. Atli may disappear, become “Attila,” or inherit traits from both murderous Atli and innocent Etzel. **[MI]**
- Games using “Attila” chiefly depict the historical warlord. Unless Guðrún, Gunnar, Hǫgni, the treasure or the Nibelung court appears, these are not receptions of Atli in the strict sense.

### Political appropriation

The *Nibelungenlied* became strongly associated with German national identity after its Romantic rediscovery. Scholarship traces a peak in nationalist and National Socialist deployment, followed by post-war suspicion and more recent feminist, media-critical and fantasy reinterpretation. [Modern political reception overview](https://www.goethezeitportal.de/wissen/projektepool/rezeption-nibelungen/politische-rezeption-der-nibelungen.html)

Attila’s Hungarian ancestral status likewise developed through medieval chronicles and modern Turanism. It should not be confused with a continuous pagan “Attila cult.” A scholarly collection explicitly distinguishes the medieval myth of Scythian origin from later cultivation of Attila as national ancestor. [Klaniczay, “The Myth of Scythian Origin and the Cult of Attila”](https://energy.ceu.edu/sites/default/files/publications/klaniczay-attila_0.pdf)

### Modern inventions to reject

- Atli as a Norse god or son of Óðinn: **unattested modern invention**.
- A historically identified Atli shrine in Scandinavia: **unattested**.
- A securely discovered grave of Attila beneath the Tisza: **false**.
- Jordanes’ statement that Ildico murdered Attila: **false attribution**; Jordanes reports hemorrhage and mourning.
- Triple nested coffins and river diversion as Priscus’ eyewitness description: **later amplification**.
- Wagner’s appearance of Etzel in the *Ring*: **false**.
- Treating every saga character named Atli as Attila: **onomastic conflation**.
- Treating either *Atlakviða* or the *Nibelungenlied* as a factual report of events in 453: **genre error**.

## 8. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Historicity and the “seed” of legend

No serious dispute exists that historical Attila stands behind the names Ætla, Atli and Etzel. The dispute concerns how much actual fifth-century information survived. The secure correspondences are few:

- the ruler’s name;
- his association with the Huns;
- enormous wealth and an international court;
- death in connection with a bride;
- the broader historical presence of Hunnic forces in the Burgundian catastrophe.

Everything beyond that is literary transformation. The conflation of 436/437 and 453 is evident because the historical Gundaharius and Attila did not die in one encounter. The legendary synchronization of Attila with Theoderic the Great is even more conspicuous: Attila died in 453, whereas Theoderic was born around 454. **[MSR]**

### Did Ildico kill Attila?

- **For a legendary connection:** her Germanic-looking name, her presence at the deathbed, later reports or rumors of murder, and the northern tale of the wife who kills Atli.
- **Against treating it as history:** the earliest full account says hemorrhage, depicts Ildico mourning, and gives no revenge motive. No contemporary evidence makes her Gundaharius’ sister or daughter.
- **Judgment:** Ildico’s wedding-night presence plausibly stimulated the revenge legend, but her murder of Attila is unverified. **[MSR]**

### Which version is older?

Nineteenth- and early twentieth-century scholarship often assumed that the terse, savage Norse form was closest to an original Migration-Age lay and that the courtly German poem softened it. More recent work is less linear. The Scandinavian and continental traditions could independently reorganize shared material; archaic style does not make every *Atlakviða* detail fifth-century. Conversely, the favorable Etzel of *Waltharius* and German Dietrich poetry may preserve a genuinely old memory of Attila as a lavish patron among eastern Germanic retainers. **[MSR]**

Matthias Hardt’s comparative formulation—that eastern Germanic memories favored a noble gift-giving ruler while western traditions associated him with Burgundian destruction—is useful as a model, but the extant sources are later mixtures, not pristine tribal archives. [Hardt, “Attila–Atli–Etzel”](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/49619314_Attila_Atli_Etzel_Uber_den_Wandel_der_Erinnerung_an_einen_Hunnenkonig_im_europaischen_Mittelalter)

### Dating the Eddic poems

The Codex Regius dates only the writing of the surviving manuscript. Linguistic archaism, meter, narrative compression and intertextual relationships have supported early dates for *Atlakviða*. Yet oral poetry can preserve, imitate or revise archaic language. Current method therefore presents ranges and relative dating rather than calling it a securely ninth-century document. A handbook treatment emphasizes precisely these methodological limits. [“The Dating of Eddic Poetry”](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/handbook-to-eddic-poetry/dating-of-eddic-poetry/A0F5C950CBEC52D0D522A1CBAC71D388)

The manuscript calls both *Atlakviða* and *Atlamál* Greenlandic. Greenlandic origin is widely considered credible for *Atlamál*, in part because of its social and environmental vocabulary; the heading of *Atlakviða* may be scribal transfer or confusion. Neither conclusion is certain.

### Oral-formulaic and compositional questions

The poems use inherited alliterative meters and formulae, but “oral” does not mean unchanged. *Atlamál* shows extensive literary expansion; *Vǫlsunga saga* demonstrably composes prose from verse and other traditions. The *Nibelungenlied* is a sophisticated written courtly epic drawing on oral heroic material, not a stenographic record of a single ancient song.

The *Þiðreks saga*’s claim to report German men’s stories is valuable evidence for thirteenth-century transmission, but not a guarantee that every episode derives unchanged from oral antiquity. Its geography—especially Susat/Soest—and its systematic synchronization have generated repeated attempts to relocate the entire heroic tradition to northern Germany. Those reconstructions remain disputed.

### Heusler’s stemmata and reconstruction

Andreas Heusler and other comparatists reconstructed lost lays and family trees of versions. Such models clarified relationships but sometimes turned hypothetical intermediaries into quasi-documents. The lost “original Nibelung lay” is not an extant source. **[MSR]**

### Tolkien, Andersson and comparative interpretation

Tolkien valued the tragic architecture of the northern cycle and wrote his own alliterative synthesis, but his creative poem is not evidence for an ancient version. Theodore M. Andersson’s *The Legend of Brynhild* treats the Sigurð–Brynhild–Guðrún complex comparatively and demonstrates how no surviving witness can simply be declared the original whole. E. O. G. Turville-Petre and Rudolf Simek remain useful for mythological and literary context, but Atli belongs to heroic legend, not the divine mythological system.

### Beowulf comparisons

Klaeber’s *Beowulf*, Tolkien’s criticism and Orchard’s companion illuminate heroic diction, legendary geography and the treatment of Migration-Age history. They do not attest Atli. Attempts to insert Attila into *Beowulf* on the ground that both works remember Germanic antiquity are unsupported.

### State of the evidence

- Priscus: fragmentary and mediated by later excerptors.
- Jordanes: complete enough to preserve a funeral narrative, but writing about a century later and reshaping Priscus.
- *Widsith*: one late tenth-century manuscript.
- Eddic Atli poems: principally one late thirteenth-century manuscript.
- *Vǫlsunga saga*: composition thirteenth century, principal complete manuscript fifteenth century.
- *Nibelungenlied*: numerous manuscripts and fragments, allowing recension comparison.
- *Þiðreks saga*: multiple redactions and manuscripts; textual variation affects reconstruction.
- Archaeology: abundant evidence for Hunnic-period material culture, but no object securely tied to the legendary Atli and no identified Attila tomb.
