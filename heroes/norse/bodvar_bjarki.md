# Bödvar Bjarki: Research Dossier

> **Traditional class:** mortal legendary champion and royal retainer, endowed in late Icelandic tradition with inherited animal power and an external bear-double; not a god, demigod, king, or securely attested cult hero.  
> **Earliest secure attestation:** Biarco/Bjarki in the Scandinavian heroic tradition underlying Saxo Grammaticus’s *Gesta Danorum*, Book II, written in Latin around 1200–1216. A few Old Norse stanzas ascribed to *Bjarkamál in fornu* (“The Old Lay of Bjarki”) may preserve an earlier, possibly tenth-century poem, but neither the poem’s original date nor the attribution of all the surviving or Latinized verses is secure. The roughly contemporary, now-lost *Skjöldunga saga* named a Norwegian champion *Bodvarus* in Hrólfr’s service.  
> **Documentary finding:** there is no securely identified pre-Christian inscription, image, shrine, tomb, sacrifice, festival, or other cultic evidence for Bödvar Bjarki.

## Evidentiary key

- **[EA] Secure early attestation:** a securely dated early textual or material witness.
- **[LC] Later canonical elaboration:** a developed medieval literary form that became authoritative.
- **[RV] Regional or textual variant:** a materially different version in another witness.
- **[LS] Learned systematization:** antiquarian, historiographical, or scholarly ordering of traditions.
- **[SI] Syncretistic identification:** equation with another independently attested figure.
- **[MA] Medieval or early-modern accretion:** a feature first documented substantially later than the earliest evidence.
- **[F] Folklore:** an orally transmitted tale-type or folkloric comparison, not proof of a specific ancient narrative.
- **[MSR] Modern scholarly reconstruction:** a hypothesis inferred from comparisons among witnesses.
- **[MI] Modern invention or adaptation:** material created in modern scholarship, fiction, art, games, or commerce.

“Early” throughout means early in the surviving record of the legend, not contemporary with an assumed sixth-century hero.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

Bödvar Bjarki is the paramount champion of the legendary Danish king Hrólfr kraki. In Saxo he is Biarco, a foreign or insular warrior who wins renown by killing Agnerus and later dies defending Rolvo. In the lost *Skjöldunga saga* he was *Bodvarus Norwegus*, “Bödvar the Norwegian.” In *Bjarkarímur*, composed around 1400, and the late-medieval *Hrólfs saga kraka*, he is a Scandinavian prince who comes to Hrólfr’s court from abroad, defeats monsters, creates the warrior Hjalti out of the cowardly Hǫttr, participates in Scandinavian dynastic warfare, and dies beside his king at Hleiðargarðr/Lejre. Only *Hrólfs saga kraka* supplies the fully developed bear-cursed father, three animal-marked brothers, dragon-like Yule monster, blood initiation, and fighting spirit-bear. [EA/LC/MA] The principal witnesses and their relationships are summarized in Tom Grant’s reassessment and the Copenhagen manuscript account of *Skjöldunga saga*. [Grant, “In Defence of Böðvarr bjarki”](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11061-023-09792-7); [University of Copenhagen, *Skjöldunga saga* manuscript](https://manuscript.ku.dk/motm/skjldunga-saga/).

### Forms of the name

- **Old Norse:** *Bǫðvarr bjarki* in normalized spelling; modern Icelandic usually *Böðvar Bjarki*. *Bǫðvarr* is a masculine personal name built from *bǫð*, “battle,” and probably *-varr*, conventionally interpreted as “warrior/defender” or as part of a dithematic name. The easy gloss “warlike” is serviceable but not a complete morphological demonstration. [LC/LS]

- **Old Norse nickname:** *bjarki*, literally “little bear,” “bear-cub,” or an affectionate/diminutive derivative of *bjǫrn*, “bear.” In the developed saga it is treated as his cognomen and retrospectively explained by his bear ancestry and bear manifestation. [LC/MA]

- **Latin:** *Biarco* in Saxo; *Bodvarus* in Arngrímur Jónsson’s Latin excerpt of the lost *Skjöldunga saga*. These are Latinized forms rather than separate figures. [EA/RV]

- **English and Scandinavian modernizations:** Bodvar/Bödvar/Bøthvar/Bjarke/Biarke, and combinations such as Bodvar Bjarki or Bøthvar Bjarke. These are editorial or linguistic normalizations. [LS]

### Was “Bödvar” originally a name or an epithet?

Saxo has Biarco receive *belliger*—“warlike,” “warrior”—as a cognomen after killing Agnerus. This prompted the important reconstruction that the hero was originally simply Bjarki, while *bǫðvar* was a genitive noun in a phrase such as *bǫðvar Bjarki*, “Bjarki of battle” or “Battle-Bjarki,” later reanalyzed as the personal name *Bǫðvarr*. [MSR] Grant notes this possibility but does not treat it as demonstrated fact; the medieval Icelandic prose tradition already understands *Bǫðvarr* as the forename and *bjarki* as the nickname. [Grant](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11061-023-09792-7).

### Identifications with other heroes

- **Bödvar Bjarki = Beowulf:** first proposed in nineteenth-century comparison and developed by Gísli Brynjúlfsson, W. W. Lawrence, Friedrich Panzer, R. W. Chambers, and others. This is not a medieval identification. The sources never say that Bjarki is Beowulf. [SI/MSR]

- **Bödvar and Beowulf as descendants of a common “proto-Bjarki”:** a modern reconstruction under which independent English and Scandinavian traditions drew on an earlier Scandinavian monster-slaying champion associated with the Scyldings and Swedish wars. Grant’s 2023 study cautiously reopens this model. [MSR]

- **Bödvar as a berserker:** *Hrólfs saga kraka* includes him among Hrólfr’s champions and surrounds him with bear imagery, but it does not straightforwardly call his final bear manifestation a conventional fit of *berserksgangr*. Calling him “a berserker who shapeshifts” collapses several distinct saga concepts. [LC/MSR]

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### The earliest evidence

#### *Bjarkamál in fornu*

The lost *Old Lay of Bjarki* is customarily dated to the tenth century, principally on literary-historical grounds. Six Old Norse stanzas or partial stanzas survive through quotation in medieval works, notably *Heimskringla* and Snorri’s *Edda*. Snorri reports that Þormóðr Kolbrúnarskáld recited the old lay before the battle of Stiklestad in 1030; that story occurs in a work compiled around 1230 and is not a contemporary record of the performance. [EA with disputed dating] The fragments call Hrólfr’s retainers to combat, praise loyalty and liberality, and mention the enemies of Aðils. They do not preserve Bödvar’s birth, monster fights, Hǫttr’s transformation, or bear-double. [Old Norse fragments](https://www.heimskringla.no/wiki/Fragmenter_af_det_gamla_Bjarkamal); [*Heimskringla* episode](https://www.heimskringla.no/wiki/XIV._Tormod_kveder_Bjarkem%C3%A5let).

Saxo’s long Latin *Bjarkamál*, 298 hexameters placed in Book II, is not a manuscript of the Old Norse poem. It is a learned Latin refashioning, expansion, and classicizing paraphrase. Its dialogue between Hialto and Biarco probably preserves genuine heroic matter, but no critical procedure can recover the lost poem line for line from Saxo. [EA/LS]

#### *Skjöldunga saga*

The lost Old Norse *Skjöldunga saga* was probably written around 1180–1200 and revised around 1250. It is now chiefly recoverable through Arngrímur Jónsson’s Latin *Rerum Danicarum fragmenta* of 1596 and through borrowings in other works. It named *Bodvarus Norwegus* as a champion of Rolfo and associated him with Hjalti and the king’s Swedish warfare. [EA transmitted through an early-modern epitome] Because Arngrímur abbreviated and sometimes combined sources, his wording cannot automatically be assigned verbatim to the lost saga. [University of Copenhagen](https://manuscript.ku.dk/motm/skjldunga-saga/).

#### Saxo Grammaticus

Saxo wrote *Gesta Danorum* in Latin in the late twelfth and early thirteenth centuries, completing it after 1208 and probably by about 1216. Book II contains Biarco’s combat with Agnerus, his service to Rolvo, his relationship with Hialto, and their last stand. [EA] Saxo’s oldest complete textual basis is the 1514 Paris printing from a now-lost manuscript; fragments survive from around Saxo’s lifetime, though not a complete independent Book II manuscript. The standard modern edition is Karsten Friis-Jensen’s Latin text with Peter Fisher’s English translation. [Latin Book II](https://la.wikisource.org/wiki/Gesta_Danorum/Liber_II); [Friis-Jensen and Fisher bibliographic record](https://books.google.com/books/about/Gesta_Danorum.html?id=MbK6BwAAQBAJ).

### Full primary corpus relevant to Bjarki

1. **Old Norse *Bjarkamál in fornu*** — composition often placed in the tenth century but uncertain; fragments transmitted in thirteenth-century works and later manuscripts. Bjarki’s name is associated with the lay by medieval attribution and by Saxo’s Biarco/Hialto dialogue. [EA, date disputed]

2. **Lost Old Norse *Skjöldunga saga*** — c. 1180–1200, with a younger redaction c. 1250; surviving mainly in Arngrímur’s 1596 Latin rendering. Names *Bodvarus Norwegus*, places him in Rolfo’s service, and preserves participation in Swedish dynastic conflict. [EA/RV]

3. **Saxo, Latin *Gesta Danorum* II** — c. 1200–1216. Provides the earliest substantial extant narrative: Biarco kills Agnerus, marries Rolvo’s sister Rutha, helps defeat Aðislus, and dies at Rolvo’s last battle after being awakened by Hialto. He threatens Othinus if the god is present among the enemy. [EA/RV]

4. **Snorri Sturluson, *Edda*, especially *Skáldskaparmál*** — c. 1220. Names Bǫðvarr bjarki and Hjalti among Hrólfr’s champions and quotes material associated with the old lay. It supplies no full Bjarki biography. [EA]

5. ***Heimskringla*, *Óláfs saga helga*** — c. 1230. Says Þormóðr recited *Bjarkamál in fornu* before Stiklestad. This attests the poem’s medieval fame and exemplary function, not the historical existence of Bjarki. [EA/LS]

6. ***Bjarkarímur*** — eight-ríma Icelandic cycle, c. 1400, incomplete at the end. It independently preserves much material also found in *Hrólfs saga kraka*, probably through dependence by both on forms of *Skjöldunga saga*. Its monsters are a man-eating wolf and a bear; its geography and ancestry differ from the saga’s. [LC/RV] [Finnur Jónsson’s 1904 edition record](https://openlibrary.org/books/OL6578829M/Hro%CC%81lfs_saga_Kraka_og_Bjarkari%CC%81mur); [Grant, “Beow in Scandinavia”](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/anglo-saxon-england/article/beow-in-scandinavia/C4BEBA0CDB1DFD02D265F5C2280A578E).

7. ***Hrólfs saga kraka ok kappa hans*** — compiled in its surviving form around the late fourteenth or early fifteenth century. A saga was recorded at Möðruvellir in 1461, but all extant complete manuscripts are seventeenth-century or later; the text has undergone substantial scribal modernization and variation. Desmond Slay’s 1960 edition remains fundamental. [LC/MA] [Old Norse text](https://www.heimskringla.no/wiki/Hr%C3%B3lfs_saga_kraka_ok_kappa_hans); [manuscript record](https://icelandicscribesproject.com/manuscripts/cph-ams/am-284-4to/); [Cambridge teaching text](https://www.asnc.cam.ac.uk/spokenword/on_hrolfs.php?d=inf).

8. **Related prose and poetic witnesses:** later legendary-historical compilations, modern editions of Arngrímur, and antiquarian reconstructions preserve or discuss material derived from the texts above. They are not independent attestations merely because they repeat Bjarki’s story. [LS]

### Texts in the item brief that do not contain Bödvar Bjarki

The following are context or comparanda, not members of the Bjarki primary corpus:

- The **Poetic Edda’s heroic poems** do not name Bödvar Bjarki.
- ***Völsunga saga*** does not contain him.
- ***Hervarar saga ok Heiðreks***, ***Ragnars saga loðbrókar***, and ***Gautreks saga*** do not contain him.
- ***Þiðreks saga*** and the ***Nibelungenlied*** do not contain him.
- ***Egils saga*** contains an unrelated Böðvarr, Egill’s son; this is not Bjarki.
- The Icelandic family sagas contain animal doubles, berserkers, monster combats, and men with names such as Böðvarr or Bjarki, but no securely intended appearance by Hrólfr’s champion.
- ***Grettis saga*** supplies celebrated monster-fight analogues to *Beowulf* and the Bear’s Son tale; it does not name Bödvar Bjarki.

Failure to separate these bodies of material has generated false “complete Germanic cycle” genealogies in popular summaries. [Documentary negative finding]

## 3. The story

### A. Birth from a bear-cursed father

In *Hrólfs saga kraka*, King Hringr of Upplönd has a son, Bjǫrn. Hringr’s new wife Hvít, described as a woman from Finnmark—that is, within medieval Norse ethnographic language, Sámi or Finnic—attempts to seduce her stepson. When Bjǫrn refuses, she curses him to become a bear by day and regain human form at night. [LC/MA]

Bjǫrn lives with his beloved Bera, whose name itself means “she-bear.” He foretells his death and instructs her not to eat his flesh. Hringr’s men kill the bear without realizing that it is the prince. Hvít then compels Bera to eat some of the bear meat. The union produces three sons:

1. Fróði, with elk-like lower parts or feet;
2. Þórir, with a dog’s foot;
3. Bǫðvarr, outwardly human but with the greatest potential.

[LC/MA] The episode’s patterned animal names and bodies are conspicuously literary: Bjǫrn “bear,” Bera “she-bear,” Bjarki “little bear.” It may partly be an etiological story devised to explain the hero’s name and animal power. That is a modern inference, not something the narrator admits. [MSR]

Bödvar later kills Hvít in vengeance. From his father’s hiding place he obtains weapons, including a sword that only he can draw or wield properly. His brothers cannot use the inheritance in the same way. [LC/MA]

**Variants:** Saxo knows none of this genealogy and calls Biarco insular or foreign; *Skjöldunga saga* merely calls Bodvarus Norwegian. *Bjarkarímur* relocates or restructures his family in eastern Scandinavia and gives his grandfather Bjórr a setting connected with Åland. The bear-father narrative therefore cannot be securely projected back into the earliest Bjarki tradition. [RV]

### B. Visits to his brothers

Bödvar first finds Elg-Fróði, a superhuman robber or wilderness-dweller. The brothers test strength, exchange counsel, and establish a magical token by which Fróði may know how Bödvar dies: earth, water, or blood placed in a footprint will disclose the manner of death. Bödvar then visits Þórir Houndsfoot, who has become king in Gautland. [LC/MA]

This itinerary explains why the Norwegian hero comes to Denmark from among the Gautar—one of the parallels later emphasized in comparison with Beowulf, a Geat who sails to the Danish Scylding court. [LC/MSR]

### C. Arrival at Hrólfr’s hall and adoption of Hǫttr

At Hleiðargarðr, Bödvar encounters Hǫttr, a terrified boy whom the retainers torment by throwing bones into a heap around him. Bödvar pulls him out, seats him beside himself, and protects him. He kills or maims one of the bullying retainers and compels the court to accept Hǫttr’s presence. [LC/MA]

The episode makes Bödvar not merely a monster-killer but a maker of heroes: he recognizes potential in a socially abject dependent and overturns the hall’s abusive hierarchy. Modern ritual readings regard Hǫttr’s movement from bone-pile victim to weapon-bearing champion as an initiation sequence. No medieval source identifies it as the reenactment of a historical cult ritual. [MSR]

### D. The Yule monster and dragon’s blood

For two successive winters an unidentified creature has attacked Hrólfr’s lands during Yule. The king forbids his men to challenge it because prior attempts have been fatal. Bödvar secretly goes out and kills it. The saga’s terminology is unstable and deliberately monstrous: it is a *dýr* or *tröll*, sometimes represented in translations and modern retellings as a dragon-like winged beast. [LC/RV]

Bödvar then makes Hǫttr drink the creature’s blood and eat some of its heart. The consumption gives Hǫttr strength and courage. Bödvar props up the carcass, lets Hǫttr stage a public “killing,” and prompts Hrólfr to award him a sword and the new name Hjalti, probably “hilt.” [LC/MA]

Thus Bödvar kills the monster, but Hjalti receives the socially recognized victory. The deception is benevolent and initiatory: Bödvar converts private transformation into public heroic status. [LC/MSR]

**Variants:** Saxo has no Hǫttr blood-initiation. His Biarco and Hialto fight a human warrior and a bear; *Bjarkarímur* has a she-wolf or wolf and then a bear; *Hrólfs saga* has a living monster and its reanimated or displayed body. Grant argues that despite these differences all three versions preserve a structural pair of monster encounters, hostile reception at the court, and royal recognition. Olrik, Olson, Benson, and Andersson treated the late saga’s resemblance to *Beowulf* as secondary. [RV/MSR] [Grant](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11061-023-09792-7).

### E. Agnarr/Agnerus

In Saxo, Agnerus, son of Ingellus, challenges Biarco. Agnerus’s sword breaks on him; Biarco kills him, admires his smiling death, acquires the title *belliger*, and takes a trophy. He later marries Rutha, Rolvo’s sister and Agnerus’s widow or intended bride in Saxo’s configuration. [EA/RV]

*Bjarkarímur* also has Bjarki kill Agnarr in battle and preserves the motif of the fallen warrior smiling. The lost *Skjöldunga saga* appears to have included a comparable killing. *Hrólfs saga kraka* does not preserve the episode in this form. [RV]

### F. Swedish dynastic war

The Scandinavian witnesses associate Bjarki with the conflict involving Aðils and Áli, counterparts of Eadgils and Onela in *Beowulf*. The direction of allegiance varies:

- Saxo’s Biarco helps Rolvo defeat Aðislus.
- Other Norse configurations place Bjarki in Hrólfr’s expedition against Aðils or connect him with Áli’s side.
- Grant emphasizes that the names, alliances, and sequence have shifted during transmission; the stable point is Bjarki’s participation in Scylding–Swedish conflict. [EA/RV/MSR]

This is one of the strongest arguments for an old connection with the legendary world of *Beowulf*, but it does not demonstrate that Bjarki and Beowulf were once one historical person. [MSR]

### G. Hrólfr, Aðils, and the gold of Fyrisvellir

In *Hrólfs saga*, Hrólfr and his champions travel to Uppsala to obtain property due from Aðils. They endure magical ordeals, including fire. A disguised old man named Hrani advises them; he is recognizable to the audience as Óðinn. Hrólfr rejects a gift of weapons from him, after which the king’s luck is compromised. [LC]

During their escape, Hrólfr scatters Aðils’s gold over Fyrisvellir, causing the pursuing Swedes to dismount and gather it. Bödvar participates as one of the champions, but the episode’s central hero is Hrólfr. [LC] It should not be transformed into a separate Bjarki exploit.

### H. The death at Lejre

Hrólfr’s half-sister Skuld and her husband Hjörvarðr rebel and launch a night attack on Hleiðargarðr, traditionally identified with Lejre on Zealand. Skuld employs sorcery and supernatural forces. [LC]

Bödvar appears to remain asleep or entranced inside the hall while an immense bear fights close to Hrólfr, crushing men and horses and proving invulnerable to ordinary weapons. Hjalti repeatedly calls for Bödvar. When he finally awakens and enters battle in human form, the bear vanishes; Bödvar rebukes Hjalti because the awakening deprived the king of more powerful aid. [LC/MA]

The text does not technically say that Bödvar’s flesh transformed on the battlefield. It presents the bear as his externalized fighting power, *hamr*, spirit-double, or animal manifestation while his body remains seated. “Shapeshifter” is therefore an approximate modern label. [LC/MSR]

Bödvar and Hjalti fight to the death beside Hrólfr. In Saxo, Hialto likewise rouses the apparently sleeping Biarco, but no fighting bear is described; their long exchange forms Saxo’s Latin *Bjarkamál*. Biarco suspects that Othinus is moving among the enemy and says that, could he see the god, he would attack him. Both champions knowingly choose death with their lord. [EA/RV]

### I. The body and burial

*Hrólfs saga* says that after the battle the dead king and champions received mounds, each with a weapon, and that Hrólfr was buried with the sword Skǫfnungr. [LC] This is a literary burial tradition. It does not identify an excavated mound as Bödvar’s, supply an unbroken local cult, or permit archaeological attribution.

No securely documented relics, exhumation, translation of bones, or named Bjarki mound are known. [Documentary negative finding]

### Every documented interaction with a named god or heroic figure

The list distinguishes direct narrative interaction from mere comparative identification.

- **Aðils / Aðislus / Eadgils** — Bjarki participates in Scylding conflict with the Swedish king and, in developed Norse narrative, accompanies Hrólfr’s confrontation with him. *Gesta Danorum* II, c. 1200–1216; *Skjöldunga saga*, c. 1180–1200 through later epitome; *Hrólfs saga*, late 14th/early 15th century. **[EA/RV/LC]**

- **Agnarr / Agnerus** — Biarco/Bjarki kills him after Agnarr’s sword breaks; Agnarr dies smiling. Saxo, c. 1200–1216; *Bjarkarímur*, c. 1400; probably *Skjöldunga saga*. **[EA/RV]**

- **Áli / Onela** — Bjarki is connected with the Swedish war involving Áli and Aðils; the exact allegiance varies in transmission. *Skjöldunga saga*, Saxo, *Bjarkarímur*, and later saga matter. **[EA/RV]**

- **Bera** — mother and protector; she lives with Bjǫrn in bear form, bears Bödvar and his brothers, and directs them toward their paternal inheritance. *Hrólfs saga*, late medieval. **[LC/MA]**

- **Bjǫrn, son of Hringr** — father; cursed into bear form, sires Bödvar by night, foretells his own death, and leaves weapons for his sons. *Hrólfs saga*. **[LC/MA]**

- **Elg-Fróði** — elder brother; tests Bödvar’s strength, exchanges counsel with him, and establishes a magical death-token. *Hrólfs saga*. **[LC/MA]**

- **Hjalti / Hialto, formerly Hǫttr** — protégé and companion; Bödvar rescues him from abuse, makes him consume monster blood and heart, stages his heroic victory, and later fights beside him. Hjalti’s awakening of Bödvar ends the spirit-bear’s intervention. Saxo preserves the last-stand companionship but not the initiation; *Hrólfs saga* preserves both. **[EA/LC/RV]**

- **Hringr** — paternal grandfather and king of Upplönd; absent during Hvít’s curse and later receives Bera’s sons. *Hrólfs saga*. **[LC/MA]**

- **Hjörvarðr / Hiarwarthus** — rebel commander and husband of Skuld; his attack causes the deaths of Hrólfr, Bödvar, and the champions. Saxo and *Hrólfs saga*. **[EA/RV/LC]**

- **Hrani** — disguised old counsellor, narratively identifiable as Óðinn; advises Hrólfr’s party during the Uppsala expedition. Bödvar is present among the champions, but no private dialogue between Hrani and Bödvar is preserved. *Hrólfs saga*. **[LC]**

- **Hrólfr kraki / Rolvo** — lord and king; receives Bödvar into service, rewards his deeds, and is defended by him until death. In Saxo Biarco also becomes Rolvo’s brother-in-law. All major witnesses. **[EA/LC/RV]**

- **Hvít** — sorcerous step-grandmother; curses Bjǫrn, forces Bera to eat bear flesh, and is killed by Bödvar in vengeance. *Hrólfs saga*. **[LC/MA]**

- **Óðinn / Othinus** — in Saxo’s last battle Biarco suspects Othinus is helping the enemy and threatens to strike him if he can identify him; in *Hrólfs saga*, Bödvar accompanies Hrólfr during dealings with Hrani/Óðinn. No source makes Óðinn Bödvar’s father or personal patron. Saxo c. 1200–1216; *Hrólfs saga* late medieval. **[EA/RV/LC]**

- **Rutha** — Rolvo’s sister, whom Biarco marries after Agnerus’s death in Saxo. *Gesta Danorum* II. **[EA/RV]**

- **Skuld** — sorcerous half-sister of Hrólfr; organizes the rebellion and magical army in whose attack Bödvar dies. *Hrólfs saga*; a differently framed rebellion appears in Saxo. **[LC/RV]**

- **Þórir Houndsfoot** — brother and king in Gautland; receives Bödvar before Bödvar continues to Denmark. *Hrólfs saga*. **[LC/MA]**

- **Hrólfr’s other named champions**—Svipdagr, Beigaðr, Hvítserkr, Hrómundr, Haklangr, Harðrefill, Haki, Vǫttr, Starólfr, and others according to the list used—fight jointly with Bödvar but generally have no individually narrated exchange with him. *Hrólfs saga* and Snorri’s *Edda*. **[EA/LC]**

- **Beowulf** — no interaction. They never co-appear in a medieval narrative; equation or common derivation is modern comparative scholarship. **[MSR, not attested interaction]**

- **Grendel, Grendel’s mother, Wiglaf, Hrothgar, Grettir, Sigurðr, Brynhildr, Starkaðr, Þiðrekr, and the Nibelung heroes** — no documented interaction. Resemblances or shared legendary environments do not create narrative meetings. **[Documentary negative finding]**

No named goddess directly interacts with Bödvar in a surviving source. [Documentary negative finding]

## 5. Cult, tomb, and ritual

### Cult

There is no inscription naming Bödvar Bjarki, no consecrated *hof* or *hörgr*, no literary notice of sacrifice to him, no festival bearing his name, and no securely ancient invocation or theophoric name formed from him. [Documentary negative finding]

The title “hero” is consequently literary and social, not proof of the technical Greek-style category “heroized dead with cult.” Hrólfr’s retainers celebrate his loyalty and martial fame; that is commemoration inside heroic poetry, not evidence of worship.

### Tomb

The saga’s mound burials for Hrólfr and his champions belong to its conclusion. Archaeological discoveries at Gammel Lejre—including large halls from the sixth to tenth centuries—establish Lejre as a major elite center and make the setting historically intelligible. They do not identify Bödvar, Hrólfr, or Heorot as excavated individuals or buildings. [Archaeological context/negative identification]

Assertions that “Bödvar’s tomb is at Lejre” convert literary geography into archaeological fact and should be classified as **[MI]** unless tied to a demonstrable post-medieval local naming tradition.

### Ritual interpretations

Scholars including Mary Danielli, Margaret Arent, Jens Peter Schjødt, and later interpreters have read the Hǫttr episode as a warrior initiation: social humiliation, symbolic death among bones, monster combat, consumption of blood and heart, acquisition of courage, new weapon, and new name. [MSR] Other work connects Bödvar’s bear manifestation with berserker ideology, animal warriors, *hamr* belief, or Óðinnic war bands.

These comparisons may illuminate the saga’s symbolic grammar. They do not prove that a historical initiation ceremony involving dragon blood was ever conducted, still less that there was a “cult of Bödvar Bjarki.” The relevant doctoral synthesis explicitly treats such reconstructions as structures inferred from literature. [Matveeva dissertation](https://eprints.nottingham.ac.uk/33637/1/phd_matveeva_2.pdf).

## 6. Historical evolution of the figure

### I. Possible oral and poetic formation: before c. 1200

[MSR] A Bjarki lay or group of lays probably circulated before Saxo. The surviving fragments show a heroic ethos centered on waking, gift-giving, battle, and dying with one’s lord. Snorri’s story that *Bjarkamál* was performed in 1030 shows how thirteenth-century Icelanders understood the poem’s antiquity and function; it does not date the composition conclusively.

At this stage the securely recoverable Bjarki is a champion of Hrólfr, associated with Hjalti, the king’s final battle, and enemies connected with Aðils. His bear ancestry and Hǫttr’s initiation remain unattested.

### II. Danish and Icelandic historiography: c. 1180–1230

[EA/LS] *Skjöldunga saga* incorporated Bjarki into a dynastic history of the Danish Scyldings; Saxo classicized the material in Latin national history. Saxo makes Biarco the exemplary retainer whose loyalty outlasts the king’s fortune. His *Bjarkamál* combines northern heroic matter with Latin epic style and moral rhetoric.

Snorri treats Bjarki and Hjalti as famous members of Hrólfr’s retinue. The tradition is already plural: Saxo, *Skjöldunga saga*, and Snorri do not agree in every name or deed.

### III. Icelandic expansion: c. 1300–1450

[LC/RV] *Bjarkarímur* converts dynastic legend into the elaborate metrical form of Icelandic *rímur*. Around the same period, the surviving form of *Hrólfs saga kraka* combines court legend, wonder tale, genealogy, monster narrative, magical travel, and last-stand epic.

[MA] This is the first documentation of the complete bear-cursed lineage and the spirit-bear explanation for Bjarki’s sleep. The saga also gives the Hǫttr-to-Hjalti story its fullest form.

### IV. Manuscript and antiquarian survival: 1461–1800

A copy of *Hrólfs saga* was recorded at Möðruvellir in 1461, but it is lost. The surviving manuscripts are seventeenth-century and later. [Transmission evidence] Arngrímur Jónsson’s 1596 Latin epitome preserved otherwise lost portions of *Skjöldunga saga*. Saxo’s 1514 printing ensured Biarco’s place in learned Danish history.

Seventeenth- and eighteenth-century antiquaries printed the *Bjarkamál* fragments and treated them as monuments of northern antiquity. Herder’s and other translations inserted the lay into European Romantic discussions of national poetry. [LS]

### V. Romantic nationalism and comparative philology: nineteenth century

[LS/MSR] Danish, Icelandic, German, and English scholars sought common Germanic origins for *Beowulf*, Saxo, and the sagas. Gísli Brynjúlfsson’s 1852 comparison brought Bjarki decisively into *Beowulf* criticism. Bjarki became evidence in attempts to reconstruct an ancestral heroic world shared by Scandinavia and Anglo-Saxon England.

This was often nationalizing scholarship, but no evidence located in the dedicated search establishes a distinctive political cult of Bjarki comparable to later appropriations of Odin, the Vikings generally, Arminius, or the Nibelungs. Claims of specific extremist appropriation require a named organization, publication, emblem, or event; none was located. [Documentary negative finding]

### VI. Twentieth-century folktale and literary debate

Friedrich Panzer’s *Studien zur germanischen Sagengeschichte I: Beowulf* (1910) assembled more than two hundred “Bear’s Son” tales and argued that *Beowulf*’s monster story developed from this international tale complex. Bödvar, the son of bear-associated parents and monster-slaying visitor to a royal hall, was a major comparator. [MSR/F]

R. W. Chambers accepted Bjarki’s importance. Axel Olrik and Oscar Olson objected that the strongest correspondences occur in late Icelandic witnesses. Larry Benson’s “The Originality of *Beowulf*” (1970) argued that only late developments of the Bjarki story look substantially like *Beowulf*. Theodore Andersson treated Benson’s critique as largely decisive. [MSR]

Tolkien regarded the Bear’s Son background as illuminating but did not reduce *Beowulf* to a folktale. His 1936 “The Monsters and the Critics” instead insisted that the poem’s monsters were integral to its artistic design. In his later published translation materials and *Sellic Spell*, Tolkien explored how a folktale-like monster story might stand behind the literary epic. [MSR/MI where Tolkien retells]

### VII. Twenty-first-century reassessment

Recent work by John Niles, Tom Shippey, Francis Leneghan, Tom Grant, Leonard Neidorf, and others has reconsidered the Scandinavian evidence. Grant argues that earlier critics privileged Saxo as a pure original and dismissed incompatible later witnesses as corruption; when the full corpus is compared structurally, the foreign champion, hostile reception, paired monster combats, royal recognition, and Swedish intervention remain surprisingly stable. [MSR]

This supports possible common legendary inheritance, not identity between Bjarki and Beowulf and not the historical existence of either hero. [Grant](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11061-023-09792-7).

## 7. Modern reception and reinvention

### Editions and translations

Finnur Jónsson’s *Hrólfs saga kraka og Bjarkarímur* (1904) made both Icelandic narratives readily available for modern comparison. Desmond Slay’s 1960 critical edition and manuscript study established the late, complex textual transmission. Jesse Byock’s 1998/1999 English translation popularized the saga for modern readers. [LS]

### Literature

- **Poul Anderson, *Hrolf Kraki’s Saga* (1973):** a fantasy novel synthesizing Saxo, the Icelandic saga, *Beowulf*, *Widsith*, and other Norse material. Anderson reconciles contradictions that the medieval sources leave unresolved; the result is an influential modern harmonization, not a translation. [MI] [Publication record](https://www.45cat.com/book/title/hrolf-krakis-saga).

- **J. R. R. Tolkien, *Sellic Spell*** — composed in the twentieth century and published posthumously in 2014 with Tolkien’s *Beowulf* translation. It is an invented folktale-style reconstruction of material imagined behind *Beowulf*, informed by Bear’s Son comparisons including Bjarki. It is not an ancient tale recovered by Tolkien. [MI]

### Visual art

Nineteenth- and early-twentieth-century Nordic illustrators, including Lorenz Frølich and Louis Moe, pictured Hrólfr’s last battle and Bödvar’s bear manifestation. Such images strongly shaped the now-familiar visual formula of a seated or sleeping warrior whose gigantic bear-double fights outside. They are modern narrative illustrations, not reproductions of Viking Age iconography. [MI]

No securely identified medieval carving, bracteate, picture stone, tapestry, or manuscript miniature can be assigned specifically to Bödvar Bjarki. Images of animal warriors or bear-headed figures provide comparative context only. [Documentary negative finding]

### Games and commercial adaptation

- **Brawlhalla, Bödvar:** the game’s “Unconquered Viking” is born to a bear father, fights giants and gods, and forces his way into Valhalla. The official biography borrows the name and bear-son idea but invents Grothnar, the Witch Queen, the Giant Wars, and most of the character’s narrative. [MI] [Official character page](https://www.brawlhalla.com/legends/bodvar).

- **God of War Ragnarök (2022):** an optional berserker named Bödvar the Fierce belongs to the game’s fictionalized group of Hrólfr’s berserkers. The spectral boss and its particular history are modern inventions using saga names. [MI]

- **Modern “Bödvar Saga” collectible coins:** Germania Mint explicitly creates a new vengeance-and-redemption protagonist loosely associated with the legendary name. It should not be cited as Norse tradition. [MI] [Manufacturer’s description](https://germaniamint.com/series/bodvar-saga/).

- **Online role-playing and anime-style adaptations:** representations that classify Bjarki as a summonable “Saber” or “Berserker,” give him game statistics, or supply adventures with gods and monsters are modern fan or commercial taxonomies. [MI]

### Nationalist and extremist appropriation

Nineteenth-century publication of Danish and Icelandic heroic tradition participated in broader projects of cultural nationalism and the construction of a Germanic antiquity. [LS] Bjarki was useful primarily as a comparative link between Danish Scylding legend and England’s *Beowulf*.

The dedicated search did not locate documented use of Bjarki himself as an emblem of a named fascist, neo-Nazi, white-nationalist, or ethnonationalist movement. That absence matters: general extremist interest in Vikings or berserkers cannot be transferred to this hero without evidence. Conversely, modern commercial claims that his stories were “well known among all Germanic peoples” exceed the geographically and chronologically limited textual record. [Documentary negative finding/MI]

## 8. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Historicity

No contemporary sixth-century document names Bödvar Bjarki. The earliest sources are separated from their dramatic setting by roughly six centuries. Lejre was a real elite center, and the Scylding world preserves names that recur across English and Scandinavian traditions; this makes a background in Migration-period dynastic memory plausible. It does not make Bjarki a verified historical person. [MSR]

Attempts to identify him with an unnamed retainer buried at Lejre, with Beowulf, or with another documented warrior are speculative. There is no body, inscription, synchronistic chronicle entry, coin, or genealogy close enough to establish identity.

### Dating *Bjarkamál*

The traditional tenth-century date rests on diction, meter, presumed heroic context, and Snorri’s Stiklestad story. Against certainty stand the poem’s fragmentary transmission and Saxo’s radical Latin recasting. A responsible formulation is therefore “possibly tenth century; securely known through thirteenth-century witnesses.” [Contested MSR]

### The relationship among Saxo, *Skjöldunga saga*, *Bjarkarímur*, and *Hrólfs saga*

Older scholarship often treated Saxo as the earliest and purest version, with the Icelandic monster episodes as late accretions. More recent analysis stresses that:

1. Saxo abbreviates and classicizes;
2. the roughly contemporary *Skjöldunga saga* disagreed with him;
3. *Bjarkarímur* and *Hrólfs saga* probably preserve material through common or related lost sources rather than one merely copying the other;
4. oral and written variants may coexist without forming a simple ladder from “pure” to “corrupt.”

[MSR] The stemma remains partly unrecoverable because *Skjöldunga saga* is lost and the extant *Hrólfs saga* manuscripts are late.

### Beowulf and Bjarki

#### Case for relationship

- both are foreign champions entering the Danish Scylding court;
- both face hostility or suspicion upon arrival;
- both defeat a sequence of monstrous opponents;
- both receive a sword or royal reward;
- both are connected with Geatland/Gautland;
- both intervene in Swedish dynastic conflict involving Aðils/Eadgils and Áli/Onela;
- both have bear-associated names or later bear interpretations;
- Hjalti’s final loyalty has been compared with Wiglaf’s.

These are cumulative correspondences, not an attested equation. [MSR]

#### Criticism

Olrik, Olson, Benson, and Andersson emphasize that:

- Saxo’s early substantial account lacks the saga’s Yule monster and bear-double;
- *Bjarkarímur* and the prose saga disagree on the monsters;
- many supposed similarities belong to widespread heroic and folktale patterns;
- the late Icelandic saga may have accumulated motifs independently;
- “bear” etymologies for *Beowulf* remain disputed.

Benson’s sharp conclusion was that the Bjarki legend resembles *Beowulf* mainly in its latest forms. [MSR] Andersson’s survey presents this critique in detail. [Andersson PDF](https://public.gettysburg.edu/~cfee/courses/English313/Complete%20Course%20Readings/Andersson.pdf).

#### Recent defense

Grant replies that the early-versus-late model is too linear and that Saxo cannot be presumed complete. Different monsters—warrior and bear; wolf and bear; living and dead troll—can occupy the same paired-combat structure. The independent *Skjöldunga saga* also preserves features missing from Saxo. He proposes that an older proto-Bjarki may have supplied the *Beowulf* poet with a foreign Scylding champion, without claiming that the surviving saga is the poem’s source. [MSR] [Grant](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11061-023-09792-7).

### Panzer’s Bear’s Son theory

Panzer’s 1910 comparative study assembled a vast international corpus, now usually divided between ATU 301, “The Three Stolen Princesses,” and related Strong John types. The common complex includes a hero of extraordinary strength, sometimes born of or raised by a bear, companions, descent into a monster realm, betrayal, rescue, and monster combat. [F/MSR]

Bödvar supplies unusually concentrated bear imagery but lacks important parts of ATU 301. *Beowulf* likewise lacks the stolen-princess and underworld-companion plot. Critics therefore object that Panzer could generate similarity by combining flexible motifs from many variants. The Bear’s Son theory remains heuristically valuable but is not a demonstrated lost text or single line of transmission. A contemporary review already criticized the method for treating absent or discordant elements too freely. [Review of Panzer](https://zenodo.org/records/1553168/files/article.pdf).

### Ritual and shamanic readings

The spirit-bear has been explained as:

- a *fylgja*, an accompanying animal double;
- an external *hamr* or projected shape;
- a berserker manifestation;
- evidence of animal-warrior ideology;
- a literary explanation of Bjarki’s name;
- a convergence of several originally independent bear traditions.

[MSR] “Shamanic trance” is an interpretive label, especially tempting because the saga makes Hvít a woman from Finnmark and northern literature stereotypes Sámi figures as magicians. The saga never uses a modern anthropological term equivalent to shamanism, and direct derivation from Sámi ritual has not been demonstrated.

### Oral-formulaic and literary approaches

The *Bjarkamál* fragments belong to a performative heroic-poetic world: awakening formulae, exhortation, catalogues of retainers, praise of generosity, and the ideal of surviving fame. Saxo transforms this into classicizing Latin hexameter. *Hrólfs saga* then embeds related material in prose and dialogue. The transmission therefore crosses oral poetry, Latin historiography, vernacular saga, and *rímur*; no one medium gives unmediated access to an original legend.

### State of the evidence

What is securely possessed:

- six or so Old Norse poetic fragments attributed to *Bjarkamál*;
- Saxo’s Book II through the printed and fragmentary manuscript tradition;
- excerpts and derivatives of lost *Skjöldunga saga*;
- the incomplete *Bjarkarímur* cycle;
- numerous but late manuscripts of *Hrólfs saga kraka*;
- contextual archaeology at Lejre, not personal archaeology for Bjarki.

What is lost:

- the complete Old Norse *Bjarkamál*;
- the original and younger complete redactions of *Skjöldunga saga*;
- the recorded 1461 *Hrólfs saga* manuscript;
- the conclusion of *Bjarkarímur*;
- any early independent witness to the bear-father and spirit-bear episodes.
