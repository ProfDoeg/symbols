# Lagertha: Research Dossier

> **Traditional class:** mortal legendary warrior-woman (*skjaldmær* in later descriptive terminology), noblewoman, wife and military ally of the legendary Danish king Ragnar/Regner, and finally ruler in her own right. Saxo calls her a *perita bellandi femina* and compares her to an Amazon; he does not make her divine, semi-divine, or a recipient of hero cult.
>
> **Earliest secure attestation:** Saxo Grammaticus, *Gesta Danorum* IX.4, composed in Latin in Denmark approximately 1200–1219. No Viking-Age inscription, skaldic stanza, archaeological object, or independently transmitted saga securely names Saxo’s Lagertha. The oldest surviving complete witness to her passage is therefore not a medieval manuscript but Christiern Pedersen’s Paris editio princeps of 1514; the surviving c.1200 Angers fragment belongs to Book I, not Book IX. The figure’s putative ninth-century setting is part of Saxo’s legendary chronology, not the date of the evidence.

## Evidentiary key

- **[SEA] Securely attested early material:** what Saxo’s medieval text actually says, without asserting that its events happened.
- **[LCE] Later canonical elaboration:** a later work that extends or substantially reshapes Saxo.
- **[RV] Regional variant:** a cognate or parallel tradition, not automatically the same figure.
- **[LS] Learned systematization:** antiquarian, mythographic, historiographic, or philological organization of traditions.
- **[SI] Syncretistic identification:** an argued equation with another figure.
- **[MEA] Medieval or early-modern accretion:** post-Saxo material presented as history or legend.
- **[F] Folklore:** orally transmitted or folkloric material, where documented.
- **[MSR] Modern scholarly reconstruction:** a modern inference rather than a primary-source statement.
- **[MI] Modern invention:** a demonstrably modern narrative, image, title, relationship, or attribution.

## 1. Identification, Names and Epithets

### Orientation

**[SEA]** Lagertha exists securely as a literary character only in Book IX of Saxo Grammaticus’s *Gesta Danorum*. After the Swedish king Frø kills the Norwegian king Siward and consigns the women of Siward’s family to a brothel, they join Ragnar’s avenging army dressed as men. Among them is **Lathgertha**, a noble maiden and exceptionally capable fighter. Ragnar credits his victory to her, survives the bear and dog she places outside her dwelling, and marries her. They have two unnamed daughters and a son, Fridleif. Ragnar later divorces her for Thora. When he subsequently requests military assistance, Lagertha arrives with her husband, son, and 120 ships, saves Ragnar’s failing army by attacking the enemy rear, and afterward kills her second husband with a spearhead concealed beneath her gown and takes his power for herself. The complete episode appears in [*Gesta Danorum* IX](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Danish_History/Book_IX), lines corresponding to IX.4.1–11 in modern divisions.

Saxo does not call her a goddess, valkyrie, queen of Kattegat, mother of Björn Ironside, or “Earl Ingstad.” Those are scholarly comparisons or modern inventions.

### Names and forms

| Form | Language/period | Status and explanation |
|---|---|---|
| **Lathgertha** | Medieval Latin, Saxo | **[SEA]** The standard reading of Saxo’s name. Latin orthography renders a Scandinavian or Germanic name but does not preserve an independently attested Old Norse original. |
| **Ladgerda, Ladgertha, Ladgerd** | Early-modern and modern European editions | **[MEA/LS]** Editorial and transliteration variants arising in the printed reception of Saxo. |
| **Lagertha, Lagerta** | Modern Danish, Norwegian, English and international use | **[LS/MI]** Modern conventional forms, now especially reinforced by television. |
| **Hlaðgerðr** | Normalized Old Norse reconstruction | **[MSR]** The usual philological back-formation from *Lathgertha*. It should not be cited as Saxo’s spelling or as a form attested for Ragnar’s wife in an Old Norse manuscript. |
| **Hlaðgerður** | Modern Icelandic form | **[LS]** Modern continuation or normalization of *Hlaðgerðr*. |

The Norwegian name dictionary explanation derives reconstructed **Hlaðgerðr** from *hlað*, “ornamental band/headdress,” and *gerðr*, “enclosure/protection.” It explicitly describes *Lagertha* as the Latinization of a corresponding Old Danish name rather than as a transparently preserved Viking-Age spelling ([Store norske leksikon](https://snl.no/Lagertha_-_kvinnenamn)).

**[MSR—disputed]** Other proposed analyses connect the first element with *hlaða*, “to load, pile, or weave,” with the place-name **Hlaðir** (Lade), or with a poetic reinterpretation of continental Germanic names such as **Liutgard/Luitgarde**. Hilda Ellis Davidson noted the Frankish-name possibility; it is an etymological and source-history proposal, not evidence that a Frankish Luitgarde was Lagertha.

### Titles and epithets

Saxo supplies no fixed patronymic or mythic epithet.

- **“A skilled Amazon”** (*perita … Amazon*) — **[SEA/LS]** Saxo’s classicizing comparison, *Gesta Danorum* IX.4.2. “Amazon” places her within Latin historiographic vocabulary for warrior women; it need not preserve a Scandinavian title.
- **“Shield-maiden” / *skjaldmær*** — **[LS]** An accurate modern generic classification, but not the word used in Saxo’s Latin passage.
- **“Noble among the barbarians”** — **[SEA]** Saxo’s statement of her rank.
- **Queen, Earl, Earl Ingstad, Queen of Kattegat** — **[MI]** Titles belonging principally to *Vikings* (2013–2020), not to Saxo.
- **Lothbrok/Lodbrok as her surname** — **[MI]** Ragnar’s sobriquet has been converted into a modern family name. No medieval source calls her “Lagertha Lothbrok.”

### Proposed identifications

#### Þorgerðr Hǫlgabrúðr

**[SI/MSR—contested]** Nora Kershaw Chadwick regarded an identification of Lagertha with **Þorgerðr Hǫlgabrúðr** as highly probable; Davidson treated it more cautiously as possible. The proposed chain is:

1. reconstructed *Hlaðgerðr* resembles Þorgerðr’s name and may evoke Hlaðir, seat of Jarl Hákon Sigurðarson;
2. Saxo locates Lagertha in Gaulardal/Gauldal, in the Trondheim-region landscape associated in later literature with Hákon and Þorgerðr;
3. Lagertha circles or “flies” to the enemy rear in battle;
4. in versions of the Hjørungavágr tradition, Þorgerðr and Irpa aid Hákon from the storm above the battle.

The equation remains conjectural. The Þorgerðr texts do not call her Hlaðgerðr, marry her to Ragnar, give her Fridleif, or tell the bear-and-dog and concealed-spearhead episodes. Conversely, Saxo never names Þorgerðr or Irpa in Lagertha’s story.

Þorgerðr’s own medieval corpus includes *Jómsvíkinga saga*, *Þorleifs þáttr jarlsskálds*, *Njáls saga*, *Harðar saga*, Snorri’s *Edda*, and material incorporated into *Flateyjarbók*. The earliest relevant Þorgerðr traditions are approximately contemporary with or later than Saxo rather than independent Viking-Age documentation. A modern survey cautions that none of the extant Þorgerðr narratives is early enough by itself to authenticate a pre-Christian cult ([*Saga of the Jómsvikings*, source discussion](https://dokumen.pub/the-saga-of-the-jomsvikings-a-translation-with-full-introduction-9783110627169-9781580443111.html)).

#### Valkyries and Kára

**[MSR]** Judith Jesch and Davidson connected Saxo’s *circumvolare*—“to fly/circle around”—with valkyrie imagery, especially Kára, who flies over Helgi Haddingjaskati’s battle in swan form in *Hrómundar saga Gripssonar*. This is a motif comparison, not an ancient identification. Saxo describes Lagertha as a human maiden, wife, mother, and ruler.

#### Hlaðgerðr of the Hlaðeyjar

**[RV]** A Hlaðgerðr appears in the late legendary *Hálfdanar saga Brönufóstra*, conventionally dated around or after 1300. She rules the Hlaðeyjar and supplies Halfdan with twenty ships. The shared reconstructed name, rulership, and naval assistance make comparison legitimate, but the saga does not identify her as Ragnar’s wife. Its Hlaðgerðr belongs to a different genealogy and story cycle. The Old Norse text is available in the [*Hálfdanar saga Brönufóstra* edition](https://norroen.info/src/forn/halfdanb/on.html).

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Saxo and the material text

**[SEA]** Saxo wrote the sixteen-book *Gesta Danorum* under the patronage of Archbishop Absalon and dedicated the completed work to Absalon’s successor Anders Sunesen. Because the preface reflects Danish gains made in 1208 and the work was apparently complete by approximately 1219, Book IX’s safest date is **c.1200–1219**, not “the ninth century.”

The manuscript situation requires unusual caution:

- The **Angers fragment**, four parchment leaves dated c.1200–1210 and plausibly Saxo’s working copy or work produced under his direction, preserves part of Book I only. It proves the existence and working development of Saxo’s text around 1200 but does **not** physically preserve Lagertha’s passage ([Royal Danish Library catalogue](https://permalink.kb.dk/permalink/2006/manus/525/)).
- No complete medieval manuscript survives. Later fragments together represent only a small part of the work.
- The first complete surviving witness is **Christiern Pedersen’s 1514 Paris edition**, printed from a now-lost manuscript. Consequently, all modern readings of Book IX depend substantially on that printed witness. The transmission history and rediscovery of the Angers leaves are summarized by the [Danish history portal](https://danmarkshistorien.lex.dk/Angers-fragmentet%2C_ca._1200).
- The current major bilingual critical edition is Karsten Friis-Jensen, ed., Peter Fisher, trans., *Saxo Grammaticus: Gesta Danorum—The History of the Danes*, 2 vols. (Oxford, 2015). Earlier foundational editions include Jørgen Olrik and Hans Ræder’s Latin text (1931) and Fisher’s translation with Davidson’s commentary (1979–80).

Oliver Elton’s 1894 translation is freely accessible through [Wikisource](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Danish_History/Book_IX) and [Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/1150/1150-h/1150-h.htm), but critical work should check it against Friis-Jensen’s Latin and Fisher’s modern translation.

### Named corpora in which Lagertha does not appear

**[SEA—negative finding]** The following major medieval Germanic corpora contain warrior women, Ragnar material, heroic genealogies, or useful comparanda, but no identifiable Lagertha episode:

- the **Poetic Edda**, including the Helgi, Sigurðr, Guðrún, and Brynhildr poems;
- *Völsunga saga*;
- *Hervarar saga ok Heiðreks*, *Gautreks saga*, and the core legendary sagas normally grouped as *fornaldarsögur*;
- *Ragnars saga loðbrókar* and *Ragnarssona þáttr*;
- *Beowulf*;
- *Grettis saga*;
- *Egils saga Skalla-Grímssonar*;
- the Icelandic family sagas as a corpus;
- the *Nibelungenlied*;
- *Þiðreks saga*;
- the surviving edited skaldic corpus.

This absence is decisive for the dossier’s shape: Lagertha is **not** a pan-Germanic heroine documented across Eddic, Anglo-Saxon, Icelandic, and German cycles. She is a character peculiar to Saxo’s version of Ragnar’s legend.

The Icelandic *Ragnars saga* gives Ragnar other wives—especially Þóra and Áslaug/Kráka—and knows nothing of Lagertha. A nineteenth-century edition already noted explicitly that Saxo’s Lathgertha was unknown to the Icelandic saga tradition ([*Saga om Konung Ragnar Lodbrok*](https://www.heimskringla.no/wiki/Saga_om_Konung_Ragnar_Lodbrok_och_hans_S%C3%B6ner)).

### What the comparative corpus can and cannot do

**[MSR]** *Beowulf*, the Eddic lays, *Völsunga saga*, *Nibelungenlied*, and *Þiðreks saga* illuminate the transmission and recombination of Germanic heroic legend. Tolkien’s “Beowulf: The Monsters and the Critics,” Klaeber’s edition, Orchard’s companion, Andersson’s *The Legend of Brynhild*, and scholarship on oral-formulaic transmission show why a medieval literary character cannot simply be projected backward into an undifferentiated “Germanic mythology.” None supplies evidence for Lagertha herself.

Similarly, scholarship by Rowe and others on the *fornaldarsögur* explains how legendary sagas combine learned writing, oral traditions, romance, genealogy, and retrospective visions of a pagan past. This makes the distinct Hlaðgerðr of *Hálfdanar saga Brönufóstra* an important comparator, but not an independent witness to Saxo’s marriage story.

## 3. The Story

### 3.1 Captivity, sexual violence, and mobilization

**[SEA]** Saxo’s narrative begins when **Frø**, king of Sweden, kills **Siward**, king of Norway. Frø then binds the wives of Siward’s relatives in a brothel and exposes them to public sexual violation. Saxo says some have already suffered assault, while others fear it. Ragnar travels to Norway to avenge his grandfather Siward.

The women enter Ragnar’s camp in men’s clothing, declaring that they prefer death to sexual violation. Lagertha is one of them. Saxo therefore makes her martial identity emerge directly from war, dynastic defeat, and sexual enslavement.

No source gives Lagertha’s parents, childhood, exact age, or earlier deeds.

### 3.2 First battle

**[SEA]** Saxo calls her a skilled Amazon who, although a maiden, possesses a man’s courage. She fights in the front rank “with her hair loose over her shoulders.” Her hair reveals her sex only amid the battle; everyone marvels at her deeds. Ragnar defeats and kills Frø but declares that the victory was won through the strength of one woman.

**[MSR]** The exposed hair performs several literary functions: revelation of sex, visual spectacle, and the juxtaposition of feminine appearance with conventionally masculine courage. Comparisons with Roman epic warrior women and Amazons are plausible because Saxo’s prose is highly classicizing. The detail is nonetheless securely Saxo’s, even if its ultimate source cannot be identified.

### 3.3 Courtship, bear, and dog

**[SEA]** Learning that Lagertha is nobly born, Ragnar sends messengers to court her. She inwardly rejects him but pretends to consent and places a bear and dog at her doorway. Ragnar crosses the sea, leaves his followers in Gaulardal, and approaches alone. He spears one animal and strangles the other by the throat. Saxo then states that he obtains the maiden as the “prize” of the danger.

The episode is coercive in its narrative grammar: Lagertha attempts to keep him away; Ragnar treats the lethal obstacle as a marriage test. Saxo records no spoken consent after the animals are killed.

**[MSR]** The animal-guarded bride resembles folktale and heroic courtship tests, but no securely documented Lagertha folktale predating Saxo survives. It should not be assigned an Aarne–Thompson type or ritual origin without a specific demonstration.

### 3.4 Marriage and children

**[SEA]** Ragnar and Lagertha have:

- two daughters, whose names Saxo says were not transmitted;
- **Fridleif**, their son.

They live in peace for three years. Saxo gives no separate adventures for the daughters and no interaction between them and their mother.

### 3.5 Divorce

**[SEA]** Ragnar later desires **Thora**, daughter of King Herodd, and divorces Lagertha. Saxo says he distrusts Lagertha because he remembers the dangerous animals she placed at her doorway. The divorce is Ragnar’s act; the text reports no proceeding, settlement, or reaction from Lagertha.

The Icelandic Ragnar tradition also contains Þóra’s serpent story, but it does not preface it with a marriage to Lagertha. Saxo has attached his singular Lagertha cycle to a more widely distributed Ragnar–Þóra narrative.

### 3.6 The fleet of 120 ships

**[SEA]** When Jutish and Scanian opponents support **Harald** against Ragnar, Ragnar sends to Norway for assistance. Although Ragnar has divorced her, Lagertha’s “early love” remains firm. She sails to him with her unnamed second husband, Fridleif, and **120 ships**.

The text does not say that Lagertha personally commands every ship, but it presents the contingent as aid that she brings.

### 3.7 The turning of the battle

**[SEA]** Ragnar’s army begins to collapse after **Siward Snake-Eye**, one of Ragnar’s sons by another woman, is wounded. Lagertha, “of matchless spirit though delicate in frame,” circles to the enemy rear, takes it by surprise, and transfers panic from Ragnar’s force to Harald’s. Harald is routed.

**[MSR—disputed reading]** Saxo’s *circumvolare* may mean a rapid encircling maneuver. Jesch stresses its literal aerial potential and reads Lagertha through valkyrie analogues. That reading is suggestive but does not turn the passage into an unambiguous statement that she physically flies.

### 3.8 Murder of the second husband and seizure of power

**[SEA]** After returning home, Lagertha kills her husband at night with a spearhead hidden in her gown. She then appropriates “his whole name and sovereignty” because, according to Saxo’s hostile authorial comment, she prefers to rule alone rather than share the throne.

The husband is never named. Saxo supplies no motive beyond her desire for undivided rule and gives no legal or political aftermath.

This episode should not be softened into an accidental death or defensive killing: in Saxo it is deliberate nocturnal murder followed by seizure of the husband’s rulership.

### 3.9 Death and body

**[SEA—negative finding]** No medieval source records:

- Lagertha’s later reign;
- the location, duration, or character of her government;
- reconciliation with Ragnar;
- old age or manner of death;
- funeral, cremation, burial mound, tomb, bones, relics, or afterlife.

Any detailed account of her death—including the television character’s killing by Hvitserk and elaborate funeral—is modern invention.

## 4. Relations with Gods and Other Heroes

### Genealogies

#### Saxo’s genealogy

```text
Siward, king of Norway
└── unnamed child
    └── Ragnar/Regner
        ├── marriage 1: Lagertha
        │   ├── unnamed daughter
        │   ├── unnamed daughter
        │   └── Fridleif
        └── later wives/partners in Saxo
            ├── Thora
            ├── Swanloga
            └── unnamed daughter of Esbern
```

The precise intermediate relationship between the Norwegian Siward and Ragnar is not supplied in the Lagertha passage beyond Ragnar calling Siward his grandfather.

#### Icelandic Ragnar genealogy

**[RV]** *Ragnars saga loðbrókar* makes Þóra and Áslaug/Kráka central wives and assigns Ragnar’s famous sons through those unions. Lagertha is absent. The two traditions should be displayed beside one another, not combined.

| Early warrior wife | Lagertha | None |
| Children of that union | Fridleif and two unnamed daughters | Not applicable |
| Þóra | Later wife after Lagertha | Early canonical wife |
| Áslaug/Kráka | Saxo instead has Swanloga in the relevant position | Central wife and mother of famous sons |
| Björn’s mother | Not Lagertha | Commonly Áslaug/Kráka in saga reception |

## 5. Cult, Tomb and Ritual

### Lagertha herself

**[SEA—negative finding]** There is no attested Lagertha cult.

No inscription, place-name securely connected to worship, sacrificial calendar, shrine, *hof*, *hörgr*, image, votive object, priesthood, funeral game, oracle, relic, sacred tomb, or medieval testimony of prayer to Lagertha has been identified. Saxo neither heroizes nor deifies her after death.

**[MSR]** Gaulardal’s geographical reality does not convert it into a Lagertha cult center. Saxo locates her dwelling there; he does not say that she was worshipped there.

No burial—Birka grave Bj.581 or otherwise—can responsibly be called “Lagertha’s tomb.” Bj.581 contained a biologically female individual with an exceptional weapon assemblage and has been interpreted as the burial of a high-ranking warrior. It is in tenth-century Birka, Sweden, bears no name, and has no evidentiary link to Saxo’s character. The excavation’s modern reassessment is important to the historical question of women and martial status but not to Lagertha’s identity ([Price et al., *Antiquity* 93, 2019](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/viking-warrior-womenreassessing-birka-chamber-grave-bj581/7CC691F69FAE51DDE905D27E049FADCD)).

### The Þorgerðr complication

**[SI/MSR]** Several medieval texts attribute cult, idols, offerings, or supernatural battle-aid to Þorgerðr Hǫlgabrúðr. *Jómsvíkinga saga* associates her with Hákon jarl’s desperate invocation and the sacrifice of his son before the battle of Hjørungavágr; later royal-saga material describes images and their destruction under Christian kings. Modern scholars have variously interpreted her as goddess, giantess, local tutelary being, divine bride, or dead ancestral protectress. A concise scholarly overview notes the proposed ancestral-cult interpretation ([Store norske leksikon](https://snl.no/Torgerd_H%C3%B8lgabrud)).

These traditions belong to Þorgerðr. They become evidence for Lagertha’s cult only if the contested identification is assumed in advance. The responsible finding is therefore:

- Þorgerðr has a medieval literary cult record;
- Lagertha does not;
- the proposed identity is a modern reconstruction, not a cultic attestation.

## 6. Historical Evolution of the Figure

### I. Possible pre-Saxo inputs: before c.1200

**[MSR]** Saxo may have drawn on oral Ragnar traditions, Norwegian place-knowledge, heroic tales of warrior women, valkyrie motifs, Latin Amazon literature, or some combination. No recoverable poem or tale can be demonstrated as his exact Lagertha source.

The putative Þorgerðr background belongs here only as a hypothesis. It cannot be described as a securely reconstructed “original myth.”

### II. Saxo’s canonical formation: c.1200–1219

**[SEA]** Saxo produces the only medieval Lagertha narrative. His figure combines:

- sexually threatened dynastic woman;
- cross-dressed combatant;
- spectacular Amazon;
- dangerous bride;
- wife and mother;
- loyal former wife;
- decisive military commander;
- husband-killer and autonomous ruler.

Saxo admires her courage but condemns her usurpation. That double construction—marvelous bravery and socially threatening female autonomy—is central to the evidence.

### III. Medieval transmission: thirteenth–fifteenth centuries

**[MEA]** No independent Lagertha saga emerged. Saxo was excerpted and abridged, notably in the *Compendium Saxonis*. Meanwhile, Icelandic Ragnar texts developed without her. This divergence establishes two separate medieval Ragnar traditions rather than one complete “canon.”

The Hlaðgerðr of *Hálfdanar saga Brönufóstra* may show that the reconstructed name and motif of a female ruler providing ships circulated elsewhere, but identity with Saxo’s heroine is unproved.

### IV. Printed antiquarian survival: 1514–eighteenth century

**[LS/MEA]** Pedersen’s 1514 edition preserved the complete Latin account after the loss of the full medieval manuscripts. Subsequent Latin editions and vernacular translations made Lathgertha available to Danish antiquarian and national historiography. Variant spellings proliferated through translation.

### V. Nordic theatrical nationalism and Romanticism: 1789–nineteenth century

**[MI]** Christen Henriksen Pram’s five-act *Lagertha* (1789) transformed Saxo’s short episode into drama. Vincenzo Galeotti’s *Lagertha*, with music by Claus Schall and verses by Pram, premiered at Copenhagen’s Royal Theatre on 30 January 1801. It was billed as a pantomimic tragedy mixed with song and is treated as the first major “Nordic” ballet, helping shift Danish theater from classical Mediterranean subjects toward a national heroic past ([Royal Danish Ballet history](https://lex.dk/Den_Kongelige_Ballet); [1801 bibliographic record](https://danskforfatterleksikon.dk/1850bib/gvincenzogaleotti.htm)).

This reception belongs to Romantic cultural nationalism. It is not evidence of Viking-Age popular worship or continuous folklore.

### VI. Illustrated Viking romanticism: late nineteenth–early twentieth centuries

**[MI]** Morris Meredith Williams’s battle image for Eleanor Hull’s *The Northmen in Britain* (1913) gave Lagertha a durable visual form: a heroic, visibly feminine warrior dominating a battlefield. The image is an imaginative reconstruction, not an archaeological portrait ([Wikimedia Commons record](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File%3ALathgertha_by_Morris_Meredith_Williams.png)).

### VII. Television-era reinvention: 2013–2020

**[MI]** Michael Hirst’s *Vikings* made Lagertha, played by Katheryn Winnick, a principal character through all six seasons. The program combines a few Saxonian premises with extensive invention:

- Ragnar’s first wife and a shield-maiden: broadly Saxonian;
- mother of Björn and Gyda: invented and genealogically incompatible with the medieval traditions;
- farmer in “Kattegat”: invented;
- Earl Ingstad, ruler of Hedeby, and Queen of Kattegat: invented;
- abusive husband Sigvard and lover Kalf: invented reshaping of Saxo’s unnamed second husband;
- relationships with Ecbert, Astrid, and others: invented;
- killing Áslaug: invented;
- death at Hvitserk’s hand and ship-like funeral spectacle: invented.

The broadcaster’s own character page calls her Ragnar’s first wife, an earl, and a fighter in the shield wall, illustrating how the television construction is marketed as a coherent identity ([History Channel](https://www.history.com/shows/vikings/cast/lagertha)).

### VIII. Present popular culture

**[MI]** The television image—blonde hair, leather armor, side-shaved hairstyles, facial markings, and queenly titles—now dominates fan art, costumes, tattoos, merchandise, naming, online “Viking history,” and commercial statues. This is a reception of Winnick’s characterization more than of Saxo’s brief Latin narrative.

## 7. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Literature and theater

- **1789 — Pram, *Lagertha*. [MI]** Dramatic expansion of Saxo.
- **1801 — Galeotti, Schall, and Pram, *Lagertha*. [MI]** Ballet/pantomimic tragedy; the most historically significant pre-television adaptation.
- **Nineteenth-century histories and popular retellings. [LS/MI]** These commonly converted Saxo’s legendary chronology into apparent biography and regularized *Lathgertha* as *Lagertha* or *Ladgerda*.

### Image

- **1913 — Morris Meredith Williams. [MI]** Imaginary battle illustration for Hull’s *The Northmen in Britain*.
- **2013 onward — photographic and digital image of Winnick’s Lagertha. [MI]** This supplanted the earlier classical-Amazon and Romantic-Nordic visual modes.

No securely identified medieval image depicts Lagertha. Viking-Age pictures of armed women, valkyries, or female riders cannot be labeled portraits of her.

### Film and television

The decisive reception event is *Vikings* (History, later distributed internationally), beginning in 2013. Contemporary promotion presented Lagertha as a powerful shield-maiden, earl, and feminist television character. Those descriptions document the twenty-first-century character, not Viking history.

### Feminist reception

**[MI/MSR]** Television audiences and popular criticism have treated Lagertha as an icon of female agency: warrior, survivor of domestic abuse, sexual subject, commander, and queen. The reading has a genuine basis in Saxo’s assignment of tactical success and autonomous rule to her, but modern adaptations suppress or rewrite his condemnation and provide political motives, emotional interiority, and relationships absent from the medieval text.

The modern image is not ideologically simple. It can celebrate female authority while continuing a long visual tradition in which the warrior woman remains conventionally beautiful and sexually available. Recent work on ancient-DNA publicity notes that reconstructions of the Birka woman have been influenced by both nineteenth-century art and the screen image of Lagertha ([Critical Perspectives on Ancient DNA](https://umu.diva-portal.org/smash/get/diva2%3A1943568/FULLTEXT01.pdf)).

### Nationalist and extremist appropriation

**[LS/MI]** Lagertha’s 1789–1801 revival participated in Danish-Nordic cultural nationalism: Saxo’s material supplied a native heroic antiquity for the national stage.

**[MI—negative specific finding]** Modern white-nationalist and neo-Nazi movements have extensively appropriated generic Viking, runic, and Norse religious imagery. In the material located for this dossier, however, Lagertha herself is not documented as a standardized extremist emblem comparable to the appropriated Othala rune, Tyr rune, or certain versions of Thor’s hammer. Claims that she is an established ancient nationalist symbol should therefore not be made without organization-specific evidence.

Conversely, the television character frequently functions as an individualized feminist or “Viking woman” emblem. Commercial use does not establish religious devotion or political affiliation.

### Commercial reception

**[MI]** Lagertha is marketed through licensed and unlicensed posters, replica shields and axes, jewelry, costumes, figurines, busts, tattoos, and AI/chat characters. Much of the merchandise depicts Winnick rather than a textually reconstructed Saxonian heroine. Calling such objects “Norse symbols of Lagertha” obscures their origin in twenty-first-century production design.

### Famous modern inventions

| Circulating claim | Finding |
|---|---|
| Lagertha was a historically documented ninth-century Norwegian queen | **[MI/unsupported]** Saxo alone places her in legendary history, writing more than three centuries after the supposed setting. |
| Archaeologists found Lagertha’s grave | **[MI/false attribution]** No named burial is connected with her. Bj.581 is not her tomb. |
| Lagertha was Björn Ironside’s mother | **[MI]** Television genealogy. |
| Lagertha ruled Kattegat | **[MI]** Kattegat is a sea area; the program’s polity is fictional. |
| Lagertha bore a double-Fehu rune as her historical emblem | **[MI]** Television-era design, not attested medieval heraldry. |
| She died at Hvitserk’s hand | **[MI]** Television plot. Saxo gives no death. |
| She was definitely Þorgerðr in human form | **[SI/MSR]** A scholarly hypothesis, not a fact stated by any primary text. |
| A runic inscription names her | **[MI/unsupported]** None is known. |
| She was worshipped as a goddess | **[MI or conflation]** No Lagertha cult is attested; the claim normally transfers Þorgerðr evidence across a disputed identification. |

## 8. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Historicity

The central historical fact is not that Lagertha lived, but that Saxo narrated her around 1200.

**Evidence against confident historicity:**

1. no contemporary or near-contemporary annal, inscription, poem, charter, grave inscription, or coin names her;
2. no Icelandic Ragnar source knows her;
3. her episode uses highly literary structures: Amazon comparison, cross-dressing, animal-guarded bridal chamber, miraculous battlefield reversal, and concealed weapon;
4. Saxo’s legendary Books I–IX freely organize myth, heroic legend, oral material, learned Latin models, and royal genealogy.

**Evidence sometimes offered in favor:**

1. Gauldal is a real Norwegian region;
2. women could possess property and authority in medieval Scandinavia;
3. literary and archaeological evidence does not exclude female participation in violence;
4. a biologically female individual received the elite weapon burial Bj.581.

None of those points identifies Lagertha. The responsible verdict is **historicity unverified and presently untestable**, not “proved false” and not “probably historical.”

### Saxo’s warrior women

Scholars disagree on the proportions of Scandinavian tradition and Latin literary modeling in Saxo’s warrior women.

- **Classicizing interpretation:** Saxo’s “Amazon” vocabulary and Latin education encourage comparison with Amazons and Virgil’s Camilla.
- **Norse-traditional interpretation:** valkyries, shield-maidens, maiden-kings, and saga warrior women supply indigenous narrative contexts.
- **Gender-ideological interpretation:** Saxo admires martial courage while depicting female rejection of marriage or male control as social inversion. Lagertha’s final killing and “presumption” let him contain the admiration within a moral condemnation.
- **Synthetic position:** classical form, Scandinavian names and places, inherited heroic motifs, and Saxo’s own political theology need not be mutually exclusive.

### “Flight” at the second battle

**[MSR—open]** The verb translated “flew round” can denote speed and encirclement. Jesch’s valkyrie reading draws attention to a possible supernatural resonance; a purely tactical translation remains possible. The passage does not explicitly say that Lagertha rises into the air or changes shape.

### The Þorgerðr hypothesis

#### Supporting arguments

- phonetic/onomastic association between reconstructed *Hlaðgerðr*, Hlaðir, and Þorgerðr;
- shared Trondheim-region geography;
- female supernatural or quasi-supernatural battle assistance;
- aerial or storm-associated movement;
- traditions connecting Þorgerðr with Hákon and his wife Þóra.

#### Objections

- no primary text equates the names;
- Þorgerðr’s core relationships, sister Irpa, cult image, sacrifices, and weather magic are missing from Lagertha;
- Lagertha’s marriage to Ragnar, children, animal test, divorce, and husband-murder are missing from Þorgerðr;
- the extant Þorgerðr sources are themselves medieval literary constructions;
- similarities may arise from a broad battle-helper or warrior-woman motif rather than common identity;
- place-name and name-element reasoning is not sufficient to establish derivation.

The equation remains useful for source criticism but should never be silently converted into biography.

### The Frankish-name hypothesis

**[MSR]** Davidson recorded the possibility that *Lathgertha/Hlaðgerðr* reflects a Scandinavian adaptation or poetic translation of continental **Liutgard/Luitgarde**. This could point toward imported Frankish narrative material. No specific Frankish tale matching the full Lagertha episode has been demonstrated, so the theory is weaker as narrative reconstruction than as an onomastic possibility.

### Female-warrior archaeology

The 2017 genomic identification of the Bj.581 individual as biologically female and the 2019 reassessment of the grave changed debate over Viking-Age gender and warfare. The grave includes an unusually complete weapon set, horses, and gaming pieces. Scholars continue to debate precisely what grave goods demonstrate about lived profession and social identity, although the sex determination and single interment are secure.

The find makes categorical statements that “no Viking-Age woman could have held martial status” untenable. It does not validate Saxo’s individual stories or identify the occupant as Lagertha. The distinction between **historical possibility** and **personal identification** is essential.

### Oral tradition and the limits of reconstruction

Unlike Eddic heroic poems or skaldic verses with analyzable meter and diction, the Lagertha episode has no surviving poetic antecedent. Oral-formulaic models may explain how heroic tales circulated, but they cannot date an unattested Lagertha lay or reconstruct its wording.

Saxo sometimes says he uses Danish oral memory, inscriptions, or Icelandic learning elsewhere. He does not cite a source for Lagertha. Claims that her story is “an ancient saga passed down unchanged” exceed the evidence.

### Source losses and discoveries

The chief loss is the disappearance of the complete medieval manuscripts behind the 1514 edition. The 1863 discovery and 1877 identification of the Angers fragment transformed understanding of Saxo’s authorial revision but did not supply an earlier Lagertha witness. Modern critical editions have improved the Latin text and contextualization; they have not uncovered independent corroboration.

### Discredited or unsupported ideas that continue to circulate

- treating Saxo as a contemporary Viking-Age chronicler;
- dating Lagertha’s first attestation to the ninth century;
- citing reconstructed *Hlaðgerðr* as an independently surviving saga character married to Ragnar;
- equating every armed female burial with a named shield-maiden;
- presenting the Þorgerðr hypothesis as settled;
- using *Vikings*’ genealogy and death scene as saga material;
- claiming a known tomb, rune, shield-device, or cult for Lagertha.

### Assessment of the record

Lagertha’s dossier is unusually compact at its medieval center and unusually expansive in modern reception. One medieval author supplies essentially the entire ancient-looking biography. Everything else falls into one of four classes:

1. transmission and adaptation of Saxo;
2. comparative figures such as Þorgerðr, Kára, and the second Hlaðgerðr;
3. historical and archaeological evidence about women, power, and warfare that establishes possibilities but not identity;
4. modern expansion, above all Pram’s drama, Galeotti’s ballet, and *Vikings*.

The securely supportable conclusion is therefore not that Lagertha was or was not a historical woman. It is that **the documented Lagertha begins as Saxo’s learned medieval construction, perhaps assembled from materials now only partly recoverable, and becomes a much larger heroine through modern Nordic theater and global television.**
