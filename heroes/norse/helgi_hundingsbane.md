# Helgi Hundingsbane: Research Dossier

**Traditional class:** mortal legendary warrior-prince and epic king; in the Eddic redaction a Völsung/Ylfing, lover of a valkyrie, slain hero admitted to Valhöll, and returning dead man. No source calls him a god or demigod, and no securely identified cult is attested.

**Earliest secure attestation:** the earliest approximately datable literary attestation is Saxo Grammaticus’s Latin *Gesta Danorum*, Book II, composed about 1185–1208, where the Danish king **Helgo** kills **Hundingus** and consequently receives a Hunding-slayer surname. Whether this Helgo is Helgi Hundingsbani, the Danish Scylding Helgi/Halga, or Saxo’s fusion of them is disputed. The earliest surviving unambiguous witness to **Helgi Hundingsbani as the son of Sigmundr and lover of Sigrún** is the Icelandic Codex Regius of the Poetic Edda, GKS 2365 4to, copied about 1270; its poems are older than the manuscript, but their individual dates remain disputed. [Secure medieval attestation; disputed identification]

## Evidentiary key

- **[SEA] Securely attested early material:** what a medieval witness actually says, without implying that its narrated events are historical.
- **[LCE] Later canonical elaboration:** medieval synthesis or expansion demonstrably later than the underlying poems.
- **[RV] Regional variant:** a materially different Danish, Icelandic, Swedish, or other localized form.
- **[LS] Learned systematization:** genealogical, reincarnational, euhemerizing, or editorial connection imposed by a medieval compiler.
- **[SI] Syncretistic identification:** equation of originally separable figures or cycles.
- **[MEA] Medieval/early-modern accretion:** material not demonstrably part of the oldest recoverable tradition.
- **[F] Folklore:** post-medieval oral or customary tradition, where documented.
- **[MSR] Modern scholarly reconstruction:** a hypothesis, not primary evidence.
- **[MI] Modern invention or adaptation:** modern artistic, religious, political, or commercial material.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Orientation

Helgi Hundingsbani is the protagonist of two heroic compositions in the Codex Regius: *Helgakviða Hundingsbana in fyrri* and *Helgakviða Hundingsbana ǫnnur*. In their received Icelandic framework he is the son of Sigmundr Völsungsson and Borghildr of Brálundr, the brother or half-brother of Sinfjötli, and therefore—by the genealogy developed elsewhere in the Völsung cycle—the half-brother of Sigurðr Fáfnisbani. He kills King Hundingr and Hundingr’s sons, loves the valkyrie-princess Sigrún, defeats her unwanted fiancé Höðbroddr, and is later killed by Sigrún’s brother Dagr with a spear supplied by Óðinn. In the second lay he enters Valhöll, rides back to his burial mound for one night with Sigrún, and is afterward said in prose to have been reborn as Helgi Haddingjaskati. [SEA for the Codex Regius record; LS for the full genealogy and reincarnation sequence]

The poems also call Helgi and his people **Ylfingar**, “Wolfings,” creating a real textual difficulty: an old heroic kindred called Ylfingar existed independently of the Völsungs. The prose before the second lay resolves this by declaring Sigmundr’s relatives both *Vǫlsungar ok Ylfingar*. Many scholars instead infer that an originally independent Ylfing Helgi was attached secondarily to the Völsung genealogy. That is a reconstruction, not something proved by the poems’ prehistory. [SEA: Ylfing designation; LS: prose equation; MSR: secondary Völsung attachment] [Eddukvæði II discussion](https://dokumen.pub/eddukvi-ii.html); [Acker and Larrington, *Revisiting the Poetic Edda*](https://www.perlego.com/book/1674798/revisiting-the-poetic-edda-essays-on-old-norse-heroic-legend-pdf)

### Names and forms

- **Old Norse:** *Helgi Hundingsbani*. *Helgi* is conventionally related to *heilagr*, “holy, sacred, inviolable,” ultimately Germanic *hailagaz*. Older lexicography treats *helgi* as the contracted definite form, “the holy/sacred one”; a stricter name-etymology may allow an earlier sense “fortunate, blessed.” Neither etymology establishes that the hero was worshipped. [Philological inference] [Cleasby–Vigfusson, *heilagr*](https://cleasby-vigfusson-dictionary.vercel.app/word/heilagr)
- **Old Norse epithet:** *Hundings-bani*, literally “Hundingr’s slayer/bane.” *Bani* denotes a killer or cause of death. In both Eddic and saga narrative the epithet is etiological: Helgi receives it because he kills Hundingr. [SEA]
- **Latinized Saxo:** *Helgo*; *Hundingus*; Saxo says that killing Hunding brought Helgo an *agnomen*, a surname, and later killing Hothbrodus another cognomen. [RV; possible SI]
- **Old English cognate:** *Hālga/Halga*, the name of Healfdene’s son and Hrothgar’s brother in *Beowulf*. This is linguistically equivalent to Helgi, but *Beowulf* never calls Halga Hunding-slayer and gives none of the Sigrún narrative. Identifying him with Helgi Hundingsbani depends principally on Saxo’s fusion-like Danish genealogy. [SEA for Halga; MSR/SI for identity]
- **Modern Scandinavian:** Danish/Norwegian/Swedish *Helge Hundingsbane* or Swedish *Helge Hundingsbane/Hundingsban(e)*.
- **English:** Helgi Hundingsbane, Helgi Hunding-slayer, Helgi the Slayer of Hunding.
- **German:** Helgi/Helge der Hundingstöter or Hundingstödter.

### Other designations

- **Sigmundar sonr**, “son of Sigmundr”: explicit in *Helgakviða Hundingsbana I* 6 and in the prose framework. [SEA]
- **Ylfinga niðr**, “scion/descendant of the Ylfings”: *Helgakviða Hundingsbana I* 5 or 15 depending on editorial numbering, with further Ylfing references in both lays. [SEA]
- **Vǫlsungr/Vǫlsung:** supplied by the manuscript titles and prose genealogy, then fully integrated in *Völsunga saga*. [SEA/LCE]
- **Haddingjaskati:** the prose conclusion of the second lay says that the reborn Helgi was called *Helgi Haddingjaskati*, approximately “Helgi, lord/champion of the Haddingjar.” This is not an epithet applied to Hundingsbani during his narrated life. [LS]
- **Helgi Hjǫrvarðsson:** not a patronymic variant of Hundingsbani in the verse. He is a parallel Helgi with different parents and lover. The prose compilers connect them through rebirth. [SEA as separate hero; LS as reincarnation]

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### 2.1 Saxo Grammaticus

**Text:** *Gesta Danorum*, Book II.  
**Language:** Latin.  
**Composition:** approximately 1185–1208.  
**Transmission:** the original is lost; the earliest fragments are later thirteenth century, with the full text dependent on early printed transmission.

Saxo makes Helgo a son of Halfdan and brother of Ro (Hróarr/Hrothgar), thus placing him in the Danish Scylding dynasty. Helgo conquers the Slavs, rapes Thora and fathers Urse/Yrsa, kills the Saxon prince Hundingus in single combat near Stad, receives his Hunding-slayer name, and later avenges Ro by killing the Swedish king Hothbrodus. Saxo consequently combines the two signature victories—over Hunding and Hothbrodd—with the genealogy of the Danish Helgi/Halga known from the Scylding tradition. [SEA for Saxo’s account; RV/SI relative to the Edda] [Saxo, English translation](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/1/1c/The_first_nine_books_of_the_Danish_history_of_Saxo_Grammaticus_%28IA_cu31924089406593%29.pdf)

Saxo has no Sigrún, no Dagr killing Helgi with Óðinn’s spear, no mound reunion, and no reincarnation chain. His Helgo instead commits rape and incest: after raping Thora and fathering Urse, he later has sex with Urse without recognizing her as his daughter, fathering Roluo Kraki. These acts belong to Saxo’s Scylding Helgo narrative and must not silently be imported into the Eddic Helgi-Sigrún story. [RV]

### 2.2 The Codex Regius Helgi corpus

**Manuscript:** Reykjavík, GKS 2365 4to, the Codex Regius of the Poetic Edda, copied in Iceland about 1270. It is the sole medieval manuscript preserving the three Helgi compositions in this sequence:

1. *Vǫlsungakviða*, now called *Helgakviða Hundingsbana in fyrri*.
2. *Helgakviða Hjǫrvarðssonar*.
3. *Vǫlsungakviða in forna*, now called *Helgakviða Hundingsbana ǫnnur*.

The Árni Magnússon Institute’s electronic edition provides a diplomatic transcription of the manuscript. [SEA] [Codex Regius electronic edition](https://eae.ku.dk/q?p=eae%2Fvols%2Ftext%2F1)

#### *Helgakviða Hundingsbana in fyrri* (HH I)

**Form:** 56 or, in some numbering systems, 55/58 stanzas, predominantly *fornyrðislag*.  
**Role:** continuous praise-poem narrative: miraculous birth, norns, royal maturation, killing Hundingr and his sons, encounter with Sigrún, sea expedition, flyting, battle at Frekasteinn, death of Höðbroddr, victory and union.

Its unusually continuous narration, elaborate compounds, kennings, and court-praise atmosphere have often produced a relatively late date. Bellows proposed no earlier than the second quarter of the eleventh century; recent linguistic work has sometimes put its received form later, even in the late twelfth century. Such dates concern the extant composition, not necessarily every traditional episode embedded in it. [MSR] [Pettit’s dual-language edition](https://books.openbookpublishers.com/10.11647/obp.0308.12.pdf); [Bellows’s introduction](https://sacred-texts.com/neu/poe/poe19.htm)

#### *Helgakviða Hundingsbana ǫnnur* (HH II)

**Form:** 51 numbered stanzas interspersed with substantial prose.  
**Role:** a prosimetric compilation of episodes: Helgi’s disguise after spying on Hundingr; Sigrún’s declaration of love; the Höðbroddr campaign; Helgi’s marriage; Dagr’s vengeance; Valhöll; Helgi’s return to the mound; Sigrún’s death; reincarnation notice.

The manuscript itself marks stanzas 13–16 as belonging to an older *Vǫlsungakviða in forna*. Scholars have therefore frequently regarded HH II as a compilation of old dialogue lays or fragments connected by prose, but the number, age, and original boundaries of those pieces are debated. Tenth-century datings belong to older scholarship; recent linguistic datings can be later. [SEA for its composite transmitted form; MSR for reconstructed constituent lays] [Pettit’s HH II edition](https://books.openbookpublishers.com/10.11647/obp.0308.14.pdf)

#### *Helgakviða Hjǫrvarðssonar*

This is a prosimetric compilation about a different Helgi: son of Hjǫrvarðr and Sigrlinn, named and protected by the valkyrie Sváfa, killer of Hróðmarr, and eventually killed by Álfr after Helgi’s brother Heðinn has sworn—under supernatural compulsion—to possess Sváfa. Its concluding prose says Helgi and Sváfa were reborn; the prose before HH II says Sigmundr’s son was named after this earlier Helgi. The verse itself does not say that Helgi Hjǫrvarðsson became Hundingsbani. [SEA for parallel narrative; LS for reincarnation] [Text and critical introduction](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Poetic_Edda_(tr._Bellows)/Helgakvitha_Hjorvarthssonar)

### 2.3 *Völsunga saga*

**Language:** Old Norse prose.  
**Composition:** usually placed in the later thirteenth century, often around 1260–70.  
**Principal medieval manuscript:** NKS 1824 b 4to, about 1400, followed by *Ragnars saga loðbrókar*. [LCE] [Manuscript catalogue](https://fasnl.net/manuscripts/756)

Chapters 8–9 synthesize HH I and parts of HH II. The saga makes Helgi and Hámundr sons of Sigmundr and Borghildr, has norns prophesy Helgi’s fame, and makes him go raiding at fifteen with Sinfjötli. He kills Hundingr and four named sons—Álfr, Eyjólfr, Hervarðr, and Hagbarðr—meets Sigrún, defeats Höðbroddr, marries her, acquires his kingdom, and then disappears from the saga. The saga proper does **not** narrate Dagr’s killing of Helgi, the mound-return, or reincarnation. Nineteenth-century English presentations sometimes append Eddic verse after the saga, creating the false impression that those episodes occur in its prose text. [LCE; modern editorial conflation] [Old Norse critical text](https://elias.rhi.hi.is/e-books/V%C3%B6lsunga%20saga.pdf)

### 2.4 *Hrómundar saga Gripssonar* and the lost *Káruljóð*

The prose ending to HH II cites a lost *Káruljóð*, “Lay of Kára,” for the next incarnation: Helgi Haddingjaskati and Kára Hálfdansdóttir. The extant *Hrómundar saga Gripssonar* descends through seventeenth-century prose from earlier *rímur*, themselves related to a lost medieval saga. Its Helgi Haddingjaskati fights Hrómundr while Kára, in swan form, magically protects him; Helgi accidentally strikes her, loses the protection, and Hrómundr kills him. This is important comparative evidence, but it is not an independently secure biography of Hundingsbani. [MEA; possible survival of lost medieval heroic material]

### 2.5 Sources named in the brief but not direct witnesses

- **Beowulf:** names **Halga**, Healfdene’s son and Hrothgar’s brother, but not Hunding, Sigrún, or any Hundingsbani episode. Its manuscript is about 1000, and the poem is earlier, making Halga an early cognate hero-name but not a secure attestation of Helgi Hundingsbani. [SEA; disputed SI]
- **Klaeber’s *Beowulf* and Tolkien’s “Beowulf: The Monsters and the Critics” or *Finn and Hengest*:** important for Old English heroic tradition and Scylding history, but neither converts Halga into Hundingsbani through primary evidence. [MSR]
- **Hervarar saga, Ragnars saga loðbrókar, Gautreks saga, Grettis saga, Egils saga, and the Icelandic family sagas:** no direct Helgi Hundingsbani narrative. Their relevance is comparative—heroic genealogy, mound-dead, supernatural lights, revenants, or the manuscript context of the fornaldarsögur. [Negative finding]
- **Nibelungenlied and Þiðreks saga:** transmit continental Sigurðr/Siegfried and Niflung material, but not Helgi Hundingsbani. They demonstrate how weakly Helgi’s cycle travelled beyond Scandinavia compared with Sigurðr’s. [Negative finding]
- **Skaldic corpus:** no securely identified Viking-Age skaldic poem about Helgi Hundingsbani survives. Sophus Bugge argued that eleventh-century skalds imitated phrases from HH I, but dependence and direction are philological inferences, not direct named attestations. [MSR]
- **Snorri’s Edda:** does not narrate Hundingsbani’s life. *Kálfsvísa* includes a *skati Haddingja*, sometimes compared with Helgi Haddingjaskati, but does not name Hundingsbani. [SEA; uncertain identification]

## 3. The Story

### 3.1 Birth, fate, and naming

In HH I 1–4 Borghildr bears Helgi at Brálundr while eagles cry and holy waters fall from the heavenly mountains. Norns arrive at night, determine that he will become the most famous ruler, and stretch golden fate-threads east, west, and north. Ravens immediately anticipate carrion from the infant warrior’s future battles. Sigmundr returns from war, names him Helgi, gives him estates—Hringstaðir, Sólfjǫll, Snæfjǫll, Sigarsvellir, Hringstaðr, Hátún, Himinvangar—and a sword. [SEA: HH I 1–9]

*Völsunga saga* condenses this: norns promise that he will be the most famous king; Sigmundr brings a leek, names him, grants Hringstaðir, Sólfjǫll, and a sword, and wishes him success in the Völsung line. [LCE]

HH II instead begins with prose genealogy and fosterage: Sigmundr and Borghildr name their son after Helgi Hjǫrvarðsson, and Hagall fosters him. This explicit naming after the previous Helgi is part of the prose compiler’s reincarnation scheme. [LS]

### 3.2 Hundingr and the disguise

HH II’s prose says Hundingr and Sigmundr were rival kings whose families killed one another’s kin. Helgi secretly spies in Hundingr’s hall. On leaving, he provocatively sends Hœmingr a message revealing that the supposed Hamall was Helgi. Hundingr’s men search Hagall’s house; Helgi disguises himself in a bondwoman’s clothing and grinds grain. Blindr notices that the “maid” has fierce eyes and breaks the millstone, but Hagall excuses the strength by claiming she is a valkyrie whom Helgi captured. Helgi escapes to a warship. [SEA: HH II prose and stanzas 1–4]

HH I has no disguise episode. It simply says the young ruler does not spare the sword, kills Hundingr—who had long ruled lands and men—and is thereafter called Hundingsbani. The prose saga likewise has Helgi encounter and kill Hundingr during a campaign. [SEA/LCE]

A popular modern claim that Helgi “avenges his father” by killing Hundingr is not supported by the received Eddic genealogy: Sigmundr remains alive and participates in Helgi’s naming. The feud is between families, but Hundingr is not said to have killed Sigmundr. [Modern error]

### 3.3 Hundingr’s sons

HH I names Hundingr’s sons as Eyjólfr, Álfr, Hjǫrvarðr, and Hávarðr, with others depending on interpretation of the battle passage. Helgi kills them near Logafjǫll and receives a challenge concerning compensation; he refuses peace. *Völsunga saga* instead names Álfr, Eyjólfr, Hervarðr, and Hagbarðr. These lists should not be harmonized. [SEA: HH I; LCE variant: saga]

HH II’s opening prose names Hœmingr as another son of Hundingr but does not say that Helgi kills him. The later *Völsunga saga* preserves Lyngvi as a son of Hundingr in Sigmundr’s subsequent story, again showing that “all Hunding’s sons” is not a stable literal census. [SEA/LCE]

### 3.4 First meeting with Sigrún

In HH I, after the battle, light flashes from Logafjall and helmeted women ride through the sky and sea; Sigrún identifies herself. Helgi asks whether the valkyries will return home with the warriors. She replies that her father Högni has promised her to Höðbroddr, son of Granmarr, whom she detests. Helgi promises to oppose the marriage. [SEA: HH I 15–20]

HH II preserves a more intimate fragment: Sigrún seeks Helgi at Sefafjǫll, embraces and kisses him, and says she loved him before seeing him. The prose expressly calls her a valkyrie, but the exact relationship between that label and every verse fragment is disputed. [SEA; possible LS in prose designation]

The saga stages the meeting after Helgi kills Hundingr’s sons. Sigrún and mounted women meet him by a wood; she refuses an invitation to drink, explains the coerced betrothal, and says she would rather live with Helgi than any king. [LCE]

### 3.5 Fleet and flyting

Helgi calls forces to Rauðabjǫrg and gathers ships from Hedinsey and Nǫrvasund. The fleet sails through a destructive storm. In HH I, valkyries protect the ships; in the saga, Sigrún leads them to safe harbor at Gnípalundr. [SEA/LCE]

At the enemy shore, Sinfjötli and Guðmundr, one of Granmarr’s sons, conduct a vicious flyting. They accuse each other of cowardice, castration, bestiality, sexual submission, witchcraft, and bearing or fathering wolves. Helgi finally orders them to stop and fight. These accusations are weapons of masculine humiliation, not reliable reports of past events. [SEA; genre-qualified evidence]

### 3.6 Battle at Frekasteinn and Höðbroddr’s death

Höðbroddr summons Granmarr’s sons, Högni, and allied rulers. At Frekasteinn Helgi’s army attacks; Sigrún and valkyries protect him. HH I describes mass slaughter and ends with Sigrún praising Helgi while wolves and ravens consume the dead. [SEA]

HH II’s prose states that Helgi kills Höðbroddr, Högni, Granmarr, and all Högni’s sons except Dagr. Its verse gives Sigrún’s encounter with the dying Höðbroddr and her grief that Helgi has killed her father and most of her kin. Helgi tells her that fate caused the conflict and tries to console her. [SEA]

The saga has Helgi personally kill Höðbroddr beneath his standard, after which Sigrún thanks him and he takes both kingdom and bride. It omits the tragic cost to Sigrún’s family emphasized in HH II. [LCE]

### 3.7 Marriage and children

HH II’s prose says Helgi marries Sigrún and that they have sons, but none is named. No extant source supplies a secure genealogical continuation from those sons. *Völsunga saga* says only that Helgi “got” or married Sigrún and became a famous king. [SEA/LCE]

### 3.8 Dagr’s vengeance and Helgi’s death

HH II’s prose says Dagr, Sigrún’s surviving brother, swears oaths to Helgi. Desiring vengeance for his father, he sacrifices to Óðinn, who lends him a spear. Dagr meets Helgi at Fjǫturlundr and kills him. He then tells Sigrún. [SEA: HH II prose]

The verse has Dagr say that he unwillingly brought grief upon his sister: “Óðinn alone causes all evil,” because the god placed strife-runes between the kin. Sigrún curses Dagr’s ship, horse, and sword, then rejects the compensation he offers. The text does not describe a fair duel; Dagr’s oath-breaking vengeance is central. [SEA]

No securely transmitted passage states that Helgi is killed specifically by “Dagr’s spear.” It is Óðinn’s spear placed in Dagr’s hands. Nor does *Völsunga saga* narrate this death at all. [Textual correction]

### 3.9 Valhöll and Hundingr’s humiliation

After Helgi’s death, HH II 38–39 places him in Valhöll. Óðinn offers him authority alongside himself, and Helgi orders the dead Hundingr to perform degrading chores: foot-washing, fire-making, dog-binding, horse-tending, and pig-feeding. The scene preserves enmity after death and depicts Helgi as exalted among Óðinn’s warriors. It does not prove a historical cult. [SEA]

### 3.10 Return to the mound

A maid sees Helgi and a large retinue riding toward his burial mound and asks whether Ragnarök or an illusion is occurring. Helgi says she is seeing neither: the warriors have been permitted to ride home. Sigrún enters the mound. Helgi is bloody, corpse-cold, and wet with wounds; he says her tears fall burning upon his breast. Sigrún prepares a bed and sleeps in his arms. Before dawn he must ride the blood-red road and return west across the bridge of heaven before the cock awakens the victorious host. [SEA: HH II 40–50]

The prose says Sigrún’s maid returned the next evening, but Helgi did not. Sigrún soon died from sorrow. The text describes one night, not a continuing revenant marriage, and contains no claim that Sigrún dug up or reanimated his corpse. [SEA]

### 3.11 Rebirth

The prose conclusion says ancient people believed in rebirth, though the compiler dismisses such belief as *kerlingavilla*, “old women’s delusion.” It then declares that Helgi and Sigrún were reborn as Helgi Haddingjaskati and Kára Hálfdansdóttir, citing the lost *Káruljóð*. The preceding Helgi poem similarly says Helgi Hjǫrvarðsson and Sváfa were reborn. Thus the received sequence is:

**Helgi Hjǫrvarðsson + Sváfa → Helgi Hundingsbani + Sigrún → Helgi Haddingjaskati + Kára.**

Only the prose framework states the chain; it cannot automatically be projected into the independent verse traditions. [LS]

## 4. Relations with Gods and Other Heroes

### Genealogical alternatives

**Eddic/Völsung genealogy [SEA/LCE]**

Völsungr → Sigmundr + Borghildr → Helgi Hundingsbani and Hámundr.  
Sigmundr + Signý → Sinfjötli, making Sinfjötli both Helgi’s paternal half-brother and first cousin in *Völsunga saga*.  
Sigmundr + Hjǫrdís → Sigurðr Fáfnisbani, making Sigurðr Helgi’s paternal half-brother by saga systematization.

**Ylfing hypothesis [SEA/MSR]**

The verse calls Helgi an Ylfing. Scholars therefore reconstruct an earlier Helgi belonging to an independent Wolfing dynasty, later made Sigmundr’s son. No complete alternative parental genealogy survives. 

**Saxo/Scylding genealogy [RV/SI]**

Halfdan → Ro and Helgo; Helgo + Thora → Urse; Helgo + Urse → Roluo Kraki. This is the Danish Halga/Helgi genealogy, fused in Saxo with the killings of Hunding and Hothbrodd.

**Reincarnation genealogy [LS]**

Helgi Hjǫrvarðsson/Sváfa → Helgi Hundingsbani/Sigrún → Helgi Haddingjaskati/Kára. This is serial identity, not biological descent.

### Documented interactions, one per named figure

- **Álfr, son of Hundingr** — Helgi kills him in battle. HH I and *Völsunga saga* ch. 9, poem probably eleventh–twelfth century; saga late thirteenth century. [SEA/LCE]
- **Eyjólfr** — Helgi kills this son of Hundingr. HH I; *Völsunga saga* ch. 9. [SEA/LCE]
- **Hjǫrvarðr/Hervarðr, son of Hundingr** — killed by Helgi; spelling/list varies between poem and saga. [SEA/LCE]
- **Hávarðr/Hagbarðr** — killed by Helgi; poem and saga preserve different names. [SEA/LCE]
- **Hœmingr** — Hundingr’s son receives Helgi’s taunting message after the spying episode; not securely killed by him. HH II prose/stanza 1. [SEA]
- **Hundingr** — enemy king killed by Helgi; afterward subjected to menial service by Helgi in Valhöll. HH I 10; HH II; *Völsunga saga* ch. 9; Saxo Book II. [SEA/RV/LCE]
- **Sigmundr** — father in the Eddic prose, HH I, and saga; names and endows Helgi in HH I/saga. [SEA/LCE]
- **Borghildr** — mother in HH I, HH II prose, and saga. [SEA/LCE]
- **Hámundr** — full brother in *Völsunga saga*; no direct adventure with Helgi survives. [LCE]
- **Sinfjötli** — brother/half-brother and military companion; conducts the flyting on Helgi’s behalf. HH I, HH II, *Völsunga saga*. [SEA/LCE]
- **Sigurðr Fáfnisbani** — half-brother only by the combined saga genealogy; the Helgi lays never stage a meeting and the Sigurðr poems do not mention Helgi. [LS; negative interaction finding]
- **Hagall** — Helgi’s foster-father, shelters him from Hundingr’s search party. HH II prose. [SEA]
- **Hamall** — Hagall’s son, whose identity Helgi uses while spying; they are foster-brothers by implication. HH II prose/stanza 1. [SEA]
- **Blindr inn bǫlvísi** — detects the disguised Helgi’s strength at the mill but is misled by Hagall. HH II prose/stanzas 2–4. [SEA]
- **Sigrún Hǫgnadóttir** — valkyrie-princess, lover and wife; asks Helgi to prevent her forced marriage, protects him, bears unnamed sons, mourns him, and spends one night with him in his mound. HH I and HH II; saga ch. 9 omits the death reunion. [SEA/LCE]
- **Högni** — Sigrún’s father; promises her to Höðbroddr and is killed in Helgi’s war according to HH II prose. [SEA]
- **Dagr Hǫgnason** — Helgi spares him and accepts his oath; Dagr later kills him at Fjǫturlundr with Óðinn’s spear. HH II. [SEA]
- **Höðbroddr Granmarsson** — rival suitor and enemy; Helgi kills him at Frekasteinn. HH I, HH II, saga; Saxo relocates Hothbrodus into Swedish-Danish royal war. [SEA/RV/LCE]
- **Granmarr** — Höðbroddr’s father and enemy king, killed in HH II’s battle summary; in the saga he participates in the hostile mobilization. [SEA/LCE]
- **Guðmundr Granmarsson** — enemy prince; exchanges insults with Sinfjötli and reports Helgi’s invasion. Direct personal combat with Helgi is not stated. HH I/HH II. [SEA]
- **Starkaðr** — listed among enemy allies in HH I’s mobilization; no individual duel with Helgi is narrated. [SEA]
- **Hringr and Hringr’s sons** — summoned as enemies in the Höðbroddr campaign; no discrete meeting survives. HH I/saga. [SEA/LCE]
- **Leifr** — Helgi’s shipmaster, asked to count the assembled fleet in *Völsunga saga*. [LCE]
- **Óðinn** — receives Dagr’s sacrifice, lends him the fatal spear, receives Helgi in Valhöll, and offers him authority there. HH II prose and stanzas 33, 38. [SEA]
- **The norns (*nornir*)** — unnamed collective who spin Helgi’s fate at birth. HH I 2–4; saga ch. 8. No source securely names one as Skuld in this scene. [SEA/LCE]
- **Valkyries/helmet-maidens** — unnamed group accompanying Sigrún and protecting Helgi’s fleet and army. HH I. [SEA]
- **Helgi Hjǫrvarðsson** — earlier namesake; no narrated meeting. Prose declares Hundingsbani was named after him and implies rebirth. [LS]
- **Sváfa** — no meeting with Hundingsbani; identified by the prose reincarnation scheme as Sigrún’s previous existence. [LS]
- **Helgi Haddingjaskati** — no meeting; declared to be Hundingsbani reborn. HH II concluding prose. [LS]
- **Kára Hálfdansdóttir** — no meeting under Hundingsbani’s name; declared to be Sigrún reborn and Helgi Haddingjaskati’s valkyrie partner. [LS]
- **Hrómundr Gripsson** — kills Helgi Haddingjaskati, not Helgi Hundingsbani as such, in the later surviving saga tradition. [MEA]
- **Kára’s swan-form** — Haddingjaskati accidentally strikes the protecting Kára, enabling Hrómundr to kill him; a late witness to the purported next incarnation. [MEA]
- **Halga of *Beowulf*** — no documented interaction; proposed identity with Helgi is a scholarly equation. [MSR]
- **Hrothgar/Hróarr** — Saxo makes Ro Helgo’s brother; the Eddic Hundingsbani never meets him. [RV/SI]
- **Yrsa/Urse** — Saxo’s Helgo rapes her mother Thora, fathers Urse, and later unknowingly fathers Hrólfr with Urse; absent from the Eddic Hundingsbani cycle. [RV/SI]
- **Hrólfr Kraki/Roluo** — son of Saxo’s Helgo and Urse; no place in the Eddic Hundingsbani genealogy. [RV/SI]

## 5. Cult, Tomb, and Ritual

### Cult

No inscription, cult title, temple, sacrifice record, dedicatory object, place-name securely tied to worship of Helgi Hundingsbani, or excavated *heroon* has been identified. No medieval source says that historical communities sacrificed to him or celebrated games or a festival in his name. His name’s “holy” etymology cannot substitute for cult evidence. [Negative finding]

Dagr’s sacrifice is to **Óðinn**, not to Helgi. Helgi’s admission to Valhöll makes him one of the heroic dead in the poem; it does not show a cult of Helgi. [SEA]

### Tomb and return

HH II says Helgi was buried in a mound and returned to it with a mounted retinue. The mound is a narrative location, not an archaeologically localized tomb. No securely identified “Helgi’s mound” has yielded evidence connecting it with the poem. [SEA; negative archaeological finding]

The scene can be compared with Old Norse mound-dead traditions and with legal prohibitions on sacrifice at mounds or necromantic “sitting out.” Such comparisons establish a cultural vocabulary for interaction with the dead, not that the poem records a rite actually performed for Helgi. [MSR] [Sanmark, “Living On: Ancestors and the Soul”](https://arcanelandscape.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/05/Sanmark-Living-On-Ancestors-the-Soul-Chapter_8.pdf)

### Ritual-hypothesis debate

A recurrent reconstruction makes the successive Helgis forms of a sacred, ritually killed king whose union with a valkyrie or sovereignty goddess guaranteed tribal fortune. Its supporting features are the name “holy one,” supernatural female lovers, repeated deaths, rebirth, and close-kin killers. None independently documents a ritual, sanctuary, priesthood, or sacrifice. It remains a comparativist model, not an attested cult. [MSR]

## 6. Historical Evolution of the Figure

### Possible early heroic material, before c. 1000

Hundingr-, Hǫðbroddr-, Ylfing-, and Baltic/Danish place-name elements may preserve traditions older than the surviving poems. Attempts to localize Brálundr, Frekasteinn, Hedinsey, Varinsfjǫrðr, and other places have produced Swedish, Danish, southern Baltic, Norwegian, and insular theories. No localization yields archaeological proof of Helgi. [MSR]

### Tenth–twelfth centuries: poetic formation

Older scholarship often dated substantial HH II fragments to the tenth century and HH I to the eleventh. Newer linguistic analyses sometimes date HH II to the late eleventh and HH I’s received form to the late twelfth century. The responsible conclusion is a range: traditional matter may be older, while the extant poems underwent composition and recomposition before their thirteenth-century copying. [MSR]

### About 1200: Saxo’s Danish national history

Saxo turns Helgo into a Danish sea-king and legislator, combining the Scylding genealogy with victories over Hunding and Hothbrodd. His *Gesta Danorum* explicitly glorifies Danish royal antiquity and converts heroic legend into sequential national history. [RV/LS]

### About 1270: Codex Regius systematization

The Icelandic compiler orders the three Helgi works before the Sigurðr poems and uses prose to create a reincarnation chain. He also identifies Völsungs and Ylfings and names Hundingsbani after Hjǫrvarðsson. The compilation preserves contradiction rather than fully erasing it. [LS]

### Late thirteenth century: *Völsunga saga*

The saga turns disjointed verse traditions into dynastic prose. It firmly incorporates Helgi into the Völsung family but truncates his story after marriage, leaving the death and mound return outside its continuous narrative. [LCE]

### Later Middle Ages and early modern period

The lost *Káruljóð*, lost *Hrómundar saga*, *Griplur*, and extant early-modern *Hrómundar saga* transmission carried the Haddingjaskati/Kára branch. Helgi Hundingsbani himself did not develop the broad German continental afterlife enjoyed by Sigurðr. [MEA]

### Seventeenth–nineteenth centuries: recovery and editions

The Codex Regius entered learned circulation in the seventeenth century. Editions by scholars including P. H. Resen, Sophus Bugge, and Gustav Neckel made the lays accessible; nineteenth-century Romantic scholarship tried to reconstruct their homeland, original poems, and historical kernels. [Modern scholarly reception]

## 7. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Literature and drama

- Friedrich de la Motte Fouqué’s *Heldenspiele* (1818) presents a trilogy: *Helgi der Hiorwardsohn*, *Helgi der Hundingstödter*, and *Helgi der Haddingenheld*. It deliberately dramatizes the medieval reincarnation sequence. [MI] [Fouqué bibliography](https://de.wikisource.org/wiki/Friedrich_Heinrich_Karl_Baron_de_la_Motte_Fouqu%C3%A9)
- Adam Oehlenschläger’s *Helge* belongs chiefly to the Danish Helgi/Hrólf Kraki–Yrsa tradition, not a faithful dramatization of the Sigrún cycle. Its title has nevertheless encouraged conflation. [MI/SI]
- Henrik Ibsen wrote the poem “Helge Hundingsbane,” a nineteenth-century literary reception rather than transmitted folklore. [MI] [Ibsen text](https://www.ibsen.uio.no/DIKT_Diktht%7CDHelgeHundingsbane.pdf)
- William Morris and Eiríkr Magnússon’s 1870 English *Völsunga Saga* appended Eddic material, helping Anglophone readers encounter the full Helgi tragedy while also blurring the boundary between saga and poem. [Modern editorial reception]

### Visual art

- Johannes Gehrts’s **“Helgi and Sigrun”** (1901) visualizes the romantic warrior-valkyrie pair. [MI]
- Ernest Wallcousins’s **“Hundingbane’s Return to Valhalla”** (published 1912) helped establish the modern image of Helgi as an armored, mounted revenant. [MI]
- Robert Engels’s untitled **Helgi Hundingsbane and Sigrún** illustration, published in Rudolf Herzog’s *Germaniens Götter* (1919), gives Sigrún spear and shield beside the sword-bearing hero. [MI] [Wikimedia record](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File%3AHelgi_Hundingsbane_and_Sigr%C3%BAn_by_Robert_Engels.jpg)

No securely identified medieval image depicts Helgi Hundingsbani. Viking-Age and medieval Sigurðr carvings must not be relabeled as Helgi merely because both entered the Völsung compilation. [Negative finding]

### Nationalism and politics

Saxo’s version already serves medieval Danish royal historiography. Nineteenth-century Scandinavian and German Romantic writers subsequently treated Eddic heroic poetry as ancestral national literature. Helgi’s reception, however, was markedly smaller than that of Sigurðr/Siegfried, the Nibelungs, or the gods. [LS/modern reception]

Modern Germanic-neopagan and ethnonationalist websites sometimes treat Helgi as an ancient sacral king or historical tribal champion. One far-right esoteric venue has published an ideological commentary on his return from death. This documents appropriation, not ancient doctrine. No evidence found shows Helgi functioning as a major state, party, or mass-movement emblem. [MI; contested-material finding]

### Psychology and comparative mythology

- Otto Rank’s hero-birth pattern and Lord Raglan’s ritual-hero scheme can accommodate aspects of Helgi—portentous birth, royal status, violent death—but neither supplies historical evidence for him. [MSR]
- Joseph Campbell’s monomyth can redescribe the story but obscures its composite transmission and variant endings. [MSR]
- Georges Dumézil’s trifunctional method influenced study of Indo-European heroic structures, yet no consensus Dumézilian identification of Helgi has displaced textual analysis. [MSR]
- Oral-formulaic and performance-centered scholarship, especially Joseph Harris’s study of parallel passages in the Helgi poems, treats repetition as evidence of compositional practice rather than necessarily of corrupt copying. [MSR]
- Gender-focused scholarship reads the Sinfjötli–Guðmundr flyting as policing heroic masculinity and examines how Sigrún’s desire simultaneously drives the heroic victory and destroys her kin. [MSR] [David Clark, *Gender, Violence, and the Past*](https://academic.oup.com/book/4283/chapter-abstract/146163419)

### Commercial culture

Helgi appears in modern myth encyclopedias, neopagan instructional material, online games, and character databases, but no film, opera, comic, or major commercial game centered on Hundingsbani was securely documented in the sources reviewed. Claims that Wagner’s *Ring* dramatizes Helgi are false: Wagner adapted the Sigurðr/Siegfried-Nibelung complex, not the Helgi-Sigrún plot. [Negative finding; MI correction]

## 8. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Dating and composition

The central disagreement is whether HH II preserves very early fragments while HH I is a late courtly reworking, or whether both received compositions are substantially medieval. Metrics, diction, kennings, prose joins, and inter-poem parallels provide relative evidence, but no contemporary author attribution or external date exists. Manuscript date must therefore remain distinct from hypothesized composition date. [MSR]

### One Helgi, three Helgis, or a recurrent type?

The medieval prose says one heroic pair was repeatedly reborn. Modern scholarship offers three main approaches:

1. distinct heroes linked by a compiler;
2. regional variants of a common Helgi legend;
3. recurrent manifestations of a sacral-hero pattern.

Only the existence of distinct transmitted narratives and the compiler’s reincarnation statement is secure. Original unity and ritual origin are reconstructions. [LS/MSR]

### Danish Halga versus Ylfing Helgi

Saxo’s Helgo has the same name and kills Hunding and Hothbrodd, while *Beowulf* supplies an early Scylding Halga. This makes some connection probable at the level of medieval legend. It does not show whether Saxo preserved an original Danish Hundingsbani, fused two Helgis, or historicized circulating Eddic-like material. The Eddic Ylfing designation pulls in a different dynastic direction. [RV/MSR]

Sophus Bugge argued for a Danish/British development of the poems and extensive Celtic, English, and Frankish influence. Earlier Swedish and Norwegian homeland theories competed with it. Subsequent scholarship has retained particular comparisons but rejected confidence in a single recoverable geographic origin. [Historiography] [Bugge, *The Home of the Eddic Poems*](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/e/ee/The_home_of_the_Eddic_poems%3B_with_especial_reference_to_the_Helgi-lays_%28IA_cu31924007972262%29.pdf)

### Völsung incorporation

The strongest internal evidence for secondary incorporation is the clash between “son of Sigmundr/Völsung” and “Ylfing scion,” together with Helgi’s absence from the Eddic poems centered on Sigurðr. Against this, the received poems themselves already contain Sigmundr and Sinfjötli; the pre-Völsung Helgi can only be reconstructed, not read in a surviving complete poem. [SEA/MSR]

### Sigrún and Brynhildr

Both are valkyrie-like women attached to Völsung heroes, but Sigrún is not Brynhildr under another name in any medieval source. Similarities may reflect shared heroic-poetic conventions or influence during Völsung compilation. Andersson’s work on the Brynhild legend is therefore comparative context, not evidence that the women are identical. [Negative finding/MSR]

### Historicity

No contemporary chronicle, inscription, coin, datable battlefield, grave, or genealogy verifies Helgi Hundingsbani as a historical person. Proposals connecting him to Migration-Age Danish or Swedish rulers depend on name equivalence, dynastic legend, and place-name localization. The prudent classification is legendary hero, possibly containing memories of dynastic conflict but without an isolable historical individual. [Negative finding/MSR]

### State of the evidence

The core survives in one late-thirteenth-century poetic manuscript and one approximately 1400 saga manuscript, plus Saxo’s differently structured Latin account. The lost *Káruljóð*, possible lost Helgi lays, and lost medieval *Hrómundar saga* prevent reconstruction of the complete medieval tradition. Modern editions necessarily number, title, normalize, and sometimes rearrange material more decisively than the manuscript does. [Textual-history finding]

### Discredited or unsupported claims still circulating

- Helgi has a historically excavated tomb: unsupported. [MI]
- Helgi was demonstrably worshipped as a god: unsupported. [MI]
- Sigrún is his “mistress” rather than wife: HH II prose says they married. [Modern mistranslation]
- Helgi avenged Sigmundr by killing Hundingr: contradicted by the received sequence. [Modern error]
- *Völsunga saga* narrates the mound reunion: false; modern editions append the lay. [Modern editorial confusion]
- *Beowulf* tells Helgi Hundingsbani’s story: false; it only names Halga. [Modern conflation]
- Helgi Hjörvarðsson, Hundingsbani, and Haddingjaskati are demonstrably one pre-Christian deity: the manuscript compiler links them by rebirth, but deity status is unattested. [Overstated reconstruction]
