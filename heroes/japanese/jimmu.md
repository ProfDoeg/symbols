# Emperor Jimmu: Research Dossier

> **Traditional class:** divine-descended mortal founder-king, conquering culture hero, first *tennō*, and—especially since the nineteenth century—imperial ancestor worshipped as a kami. He is not securely recoverable as a historical individual.
>
> **Earliest secure attestation:** the completed text of the *Kojiki* (古事記, 712 CE), followed by *Nihon shoki* (日本書紀, 720). No contemporary inscription, archaeological object, foreign record, or securely pre-eighth-century image names him. The earliest surviving *Kojiki* manuscript is the Shinpukuji copy of 1371–1372; the oldest surviving *Nihon shoki* manuscript is a ninth-century copy of Book 10, not the Jimmu book. The figure is therefore securely attested as an early-eighth-century textual construction, while the traditions incorporated into that construction may be older but cannot be dated independently. [Nara National Museum](https://www.narahaku.go.jp/exhibition/special/feature_exhibition/201206_kojiki/); [Kokugakuin University](https://kojiki.kokugakuin.ac.jp/kojiki/%E5%A4%A9%E5%9C%B0%E5%88%9D%E7%99%BA/); [Nara National Museum, *Nihon shoki* manuscript](https://www.narahaku.go.jp/english/collection/p-1190-0.html)

## Evidentiary key

- **[EA] Securely attested early material:** what an early source actually says, normally the *Kojiki* (712) or *Nihon shoki* (720), without assuming the narrated event happened.
- **[CE] Later canonical elaboration:** later texts extending or regularizing the chronicles.
- **[RV] Regional variant:** a locally preserved or localized tradition.
- **[LS] Learned systematization:** antiquarian, theological, genealogical, or chronological synthesis.
- **[MA] Medieval or early-modern accretion.**
- **[F] Folklore:** tradition documented in popular or local circulation but not demonstrably ancient.
- **[MSR] Modern scholarly reconstruction:** an inference rather than a primary tradition.
- **[MI] Modern invention or reinvention:** a demonstrably modern representation, institution, identification, or political interpretation.

These labels classify attestations, not truth-values. An [EA] claim is early evidence for a story, not necessarily evidence that the story’s events occurred.

## 1. Identification, names and epithets

### Orientation

**[EA]** The *Kojiki* presents Kamu-Yamato-Iware-biko as the divine-descended leader who leaves the region called Hyūga, journeys east with his brothers, loses Itsuse after attacking Yamato from the sunward west, circles through Kii and Kumano, receives heavenly aid, defeats or accepts the submission of Yamato’s rulers, establishes a palace at Kashihara, marries Isukeyori-hime, and heads the first imperial genealogy. The *Nihon shoki* turns this episodic conquest legend into Book 3 of an annalistic national history: it supplies a long reign, dates, speeches, military appointments, sacrifices, an accession in 660 BCE, and a death and burial chronology. The latter’s title “first emperor” describes his position inside the eighth-century court’s constructed sequence, not a contemporaneously documented seventh-century-BCE office.

**[MSR]** Modern historical scholarship generally treats Jimmu as legendary because the only narrative witnesses are texts compiled more than a millennium after the assigned date, because the chronology is artificial and impossibly elongated, and because no archaeological or independent textual evidence identifies him. That conclusion does not prove that every geographical memory or conquest motif in the cycle was invented in 712; it means that no recoverable individual can securely be separated from the literary founder.

### Names

- **神倭伊波礼毘古命 — Kamu-Yamato-Iware-biko-no-Mikoto.** **[EA: *Kojiki*, 712]** The principal *Kojiki* form. *Kamu* means “divine/numinous”; *Yamato* locates or affiliates him with the Yamato polity; *biko/hiko* means “male child/prince.” *Iware* is generally treated as a Yamato place-name. A complete translation such as “Divine Prince of Yamato-Iware” is safer than speculative semantic etymologies of *Iware*. [Kokugakuin University Digital Museum](https://d-museum.kokugakuin.ac.jp/eos/detail/?id=9379)

- **神日本磐余彦天皇 — Kamu-Yamato-Iware-biko no Sumera-mikoto.** **[EA: *Nihon shoki*, 720]** The graph 日本 is read *Yamato* here; 磐余 writes *Iware*. The expanded chronicle form includes the sovereign title *sumera-mikoto*.

- **神日本磐余彦火火出見天皇 — Kamu-Yamato-Iware-biko-Hohodemi no Sumera-mikoto.** **[EA/LS: *Nihon shoki*, 720]** A fuller form associating him with the dynastic *Hohodemi* name. This must not be confused without qualification with his grandfather Hiko-hohodemi/Hoori.

- **若御毛沼命 — Waka-mikenu-no-Mikoto.** **[EA: *Kojiki*, 712]** “Young Mikenu,” although the internal analysis of *mikenu* is disputed.

- **豊御毛沼命 — Toyo-mikenu-no-Mikoto.** **[EA: *Kojiki*, 712]** An alternate name, with *toyo*, “abundant/rich.”

- **狭野尊/狭野命 — Sano-no-Mikoto.** **[CE/LS]** A childhood-name tradition emphasized in later genealogical and local accounts; it is associated especially with Sano in southern Kyūshū.

- **始馭天下之天皇 — Hatsukuni-shirasu Sumera-mikoto**, approximately “the sovereign who first ruled the realm.” **[EA/LS: *Nihon shoki*]** The chronicles also apply a comparable “first ruler” formula to Sujin, producing a famous double-foundation problem. Scholars have variously read this as evidence for combined genealogies, distinct senses of “first ruling,” or successive constructions; it cannot by itself identify Jimmu with Sujin. A dedicated comparative study is catalogued by the National Diet Library. [NDL Search](https://ndlsearch.ndl.go.jp/books/R000000004-I7726777)

- **神武天皇 — Jinmu Tennō.** **[CE/LS: later eighth century]** The Chinese-style posthumous name means approximately “Divine Martial Emperor.” It does not appear as his living name in the founding narrative. Medieval tradition explicitly knew it as a later, Chinese-language designation: Kitabatake Chikafusa wrote that *Kamu-Yamato-Iware-biko* was the ancient Japanese name and *Jinmu* a later name established according to Chinese usage. The attribution of early imperial posthumous names to Ōmi no Mifune, traditionally in 762–764, is plausible learned tradition but not contemporary documentation for every individual naming act. [*Jinnō shōtōki* text](https://ja.wikisource.org/wiki/%E7%A5%9E%E7%9A%87%E6%AD%A3%E7%B5%B1%E8%A8%98)

### Class and identifications

**[EA]** In the chronicles, Jimmu is human enough to marry, wage war, age, die, and be buried, but belongs to an unbroken divine genealogy. He is not said to become a kami in the *Kojiki* story. “Demigod” is only a rough comparative label; the indigenous classification is an imperial descendant of the heavenly kami.

**[CE/MA]** Genealogical works treated him as the hinge between the divine age and the age of human emperors. *Jinnō shōtōki* (composed 1339, subsequently revised) makes him “the first sovereign of the human age” and uses the succession from Amaterasu through Jimmu to defend imperial, and particularly Southern Court, legitimacy.

**[MI]** Equations of Jimmu with the Chinese voyager Xu Fu/Jofuku, extraterrestrials, Israelites, or other foreign founders are not ancient Japanese traditions. Nineteenth- and twentieth-century diffusionist speculation sometimes connected Jimmu’s migration with Xu Fu or continental migrants, but no early source identifies them and no contemporary evidence supports the equation.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### What the earliest evidence does—and does not—establish

**[EA]** The *Kojiki* was presented in 712 under Empress Genmei and is the earliest extant work naming and narrating the founder. Its Jimmu cycle is in the middle scroll. The surviving text depends on manuscripts much later than composition: the Shinpukuji manuscript was copied in 1371–1372. That gap requires textual criticism but does not justify redating the whole work to the fourteenth century. [Kokugakuin manuscript catalogue](https://museum.kokugakuin.ac.jp/files/user/2020_kojiki_list.pdf)

**[EA]** The *Nihon shoki*, submitted in 720 by Prince Toneri and collaborators, is the first imperially commissioned national history. Written largely in literary Chinese and organized annalistically, it makes Jimmu the subject of Book 3. The original and its lost genealogical volume do not survive; nevertheless, Heian manuscripts and the long record of court lectures show early transmission. The oldest surviving *Nihon shoki* codex fragment is ninth century and contains Ōjin’s book rather than Jimmu’s. [Nara National Museum](https://www.narahaku.go.jp/collection/_test/ja/1190-0.html)

**Absence finding:** no excavated seventh-century-BCE inscription, weapon, palace record, bronze mirror, tomb inscription, Chinese chronicle, or Korean record names Jimmu, Iwarebiko, his expedition, or a 660 BCE Yamato enthronement.

### Primary corpus

1. **[EA] *Kojiki* (古事記), 712; mixed Sino-Japanese written register.**  
   Jimmu’s names and descent; migration from Hyūga; Itsuse’s death; Kumano collapse and the heavenly sword; Yatagarasu; Ukashi; Yoshino beings; fighting at Osaka; Nigihayahi’s submission; marriage, children, death-age, and burial. The cycle contains songs conventionally called *Kume uta*.

2. **[EA] *Nihon shoki* (日本書紀), 720; classical Chinese.**  
   The fullest early witness: genealogy, expedition dated from 667 BCE, Shiotsutsu’s advice, itinerary, battles, dreams, ritual manufacture, Yatagarasu, the golden kite, accession dated 660 BCE, appointments, queen and children, reign, death, and burial.

3. **[CE] *Kogo shūi* (古語拾遺), submitted by Inbe no Hironari in 807.**  
   A ritual-clan memorandum covering myth and early court history. It supplements official myth-history and advances Inbe claims. It is important for the consolidation of court ritual tradition but does not provide an independent contemporary witness to Jimmu. [University of Vienna overview](https://religion-in-japan.univie.ac.at/kami/Kogo_sh%C5%ABi)

4. **[CE/LS] *Sendai kuji hongi* (先代旧事本紀), probably ninth–tenth century in its extant form.**  
   Books 6–7 retell imperial beginnings while magnifying Nigihayahi, Umashimaji and Mononobe genealogies. Much is drawn from the earlier chronicles and *Kogo shūi*, so its distinctive Mononobe material must not automatically be treated as an independent prehistoric source. The text’s present form contains post-807 material and an Engishiki reference. [Text and dating overview](https://sendaikujihongi.seisaku.bz/)

5. **[CE/LS] *Engishiki* (延喜式), compiled 905–927.**  
   Its mortuary and ritual regulations preserve the official name and location-description of Jimmu’s mausoleum, “northeast of Mount Unebi.” This is secure evidence for tenth-century state remembrance, not archaeological proof of the occupant.

6. **[MA] *Shaku Nihongi* (釈日本紀), late thirteenth century.**  
   Urabe Kanekata’s commentary preserves quotations, court-lecture traditions and interpretations of the chronicles, including the learned account of Chinese-style imperial names.

7. **[MA] Kitabatake Chikafusa, *Jinnō shōtōki* (神皇正統記), 1339 and revisions.**  
   Integrates Jimmu into a moral-political history of legitimate imperial descent and explicitly distinguishes the ancient Japanese and later Chinese-style names.

8. **[MA/LS] Hayashi Gahō and the Mito school’s *Dai Nihon shi* project, seventeenth century onward.**  
   Neo-Confucian histories regularized the imperial sequence and debated chronology, morality and tomb locations.

9. **[MA/LS] Motoori Norinaga, *Kojiki-den* (古事記伝), 1798 completed.**  
   A foundational philological commentary restoring ancient readings and privileging the *Kojiki*. Norinaga also supported a different candidate for Jimmu’s tomb from the site eventually designated.

10. **[MI] Meiji state proclamations, school histories, shrine publications and liturgies, 1870s–1945.**  
    They converted chronicle time into civic time, taught Jimmu as national founder, fixed a public iconography and institutionalized Kashihara as a national sacred center.

**Corpus exclusion:** the *Heike monogatari*, *Gikeiki*, *otogizōshi*, *Nihon ryōiki*, *Konjaku monogatari*, Noh and kabuki repertories named in the broader Japanese-heroes brief are indispensable for other heroes but do not constitute a significant independent Jimmu corpus. Their absence is itself useful: unlike Yoshitsune, Benkei, Momotarō or Urashima, Jimmu’s major elaboration occurred in chronicle commentary, imperial ritual, antiquarianism, schooling and modern state commemoration rather than a large medieval theatrical cycle.

## 3. The story

### Descent and birth

**[EA]** Both chronicles place Jimmu at the end of the heavenly line:

Amaterasu → Ame-no-Oshihomimi → Ninigi → Hiko-hohodemi/Hoori → Ugayafukiaezu + Tamayori-hime → Jimmu.

Counting generations varies according to whether Amaterasu and intermediate figures are included, which explains modern descriptions ranging from “great-grandson” to more remote descendant. In the narrative genealogy his father is Ugayafukiaezu and his mother Tamayori-hime, daughter of the sea deity Watatsumi. The Imperial Household Agency still presents these parents in its official mausoleum register. [Imperial Household Agency](https://www.kunaicho.go.jp/visit/ryobo/001.html)

**[EA]** The *Nihon shoki* calls him the fourth son. The *Kojiki* names the brothers Itsuse, Inahi and Mikenu; ordering and name forms vary. Neither early chronicle gives a verifiable birthplace in the modern biographical sense.

### Decision to go east

**[EA: *Kojiki*]** Jimmu and his elder brother Itsuse deliberate over where best to govern the realm and set out east from Takachiho in Hyūga. The route passes through the Hayasui Channel, Tsukushi and Aki to Kibi, where the expedition remains for years.

**[EA: *Nihon shoki*]** At age forty-five, Jimmu declares the east the land’s center after hearing Shiotsutsu-no-oji’s report that a heavenly descendant had already descended there. The chronicle embeds the expedition in calendrical notices and political speeches absent from the shorter *Kojiki*.

**[MSR]** The story has been interpreted as a memory of population movement from northern or southern Kyūshū into the Kinai, as an eighth-century charter mapping allies and subjected groups onto the landscape, or as a literary reversal of the heavenly descent. None of these reconstructions establishes a single migration led by Jimmu.

### Defeat at Yamato and Itsuse’s death

**[EA]** The invaders enter the Osaka region and fight Tomi/Naga-sune-hiko. Itsuse is struck by an arrow.

**[EA]** Itsuse concludes that descendants of the sun goddess should not fight while facing eastward—against the sun. The army therefore withdraws and turns south so that it can approach Yamato with the sun behind it. Itsuse dies from the wound and is buried at a place identified by the chronicles in Kii.

**Function [MSR]:** the defeat makes solar descent operational rather than merely genealogical. Victory requires correct cosmic orientation, not only military strength.

### Kumano: poison, dream and heavenly sword

**[EA: *Kojiki*]** At Kumano, a great bear appears and disappears; Jimmu and his army collapse under a deity’s noxious influence. Takakuraji brings a sword disclosed through a dream. The sword causes the hostile deities to fall and the army to awaken.

**[EA: *Kojiki*]** Takakuraji reports that Amaterasu and Takagi/Takamimusubi asked Takemikazuchi to descend. Takemikazuchi declined to descend personally and sent down the sword that had subdued the land, placing it in Takakuraji’s storehouse.

**[EA: *Nihon shoki*]** The same complex is retold with different allocation of heavenly initiative. The divine sword later known as Futsu-no-mitama became associated with Isonokami Shrine. The chronicles’ sword is an object within the story; claims to possess the identical prehistoric weapon are matters of shrine tradition rather than independently verifiable provenance.

### Yatagarasu

**[EA]** A giant crow, Yatagarasu (八咫烏), is sent to guide the expedition through the Kumano–Yoshino mountains. In the *Kojiki*, Takagi-no-kami is the sender; the received *Nihon shoki* narrative assigns the revelatory instruction to Amaterasu. This difference should not be harmonized. [Kokugakuin University](https://www.kokugakuin.ac.jp/en/article/145336)

**[CE/LS]** Neither early chronicle says that this crow has three legs. The now-standard three-legged form emerged through identification with the continental solar crow and is attested in learned Heian tradition, conventionally associated with the *Wamyō ruijushō* around 930. The common statement “Jimmu’s three-legged crow appears in the *Kojiki*” is therefore a retrojection.

### Ukashi and the crushing trap

**[EA]** At Uda, the elder Ukashi professes submission but builds a hall fitted with a trap. The plot is exposed by his younger brother.

**[EA: *Kojiki*]** Jimmu’s followers force the elder Ukashi into his own trap; he is crushed, killed, dismembered, and the army celebrates with a song. The violence is explicit and should not be softened into a merely bloodless “pacification.”

**[EA: *Nihon shoki*]** The same betrayal and reversal appear with expanded military roles and ritualized feasting. The younger Ukashi submits and is rewarded.

### Yoshino and local beings

**[EA]** Guided through Yoshino, Jimmu encounters figures emerging from river, well and rock environments: the fish-trap or cormorant-associated Nihe-motsu-no-ko, the luminous-tailed Ihika, and a “child of the rock-pusher.” They identify themselves as local deities or subjects and submit.

**[MSR]** These encounters have been read as a charter connecting Yamato sovereignty to specialist and regional groups of Yoshino. The bizarre bodies—especially tails—belong to the texts’ boundary imagery; they are not ethnographic descriptions that can securely identify an ancient “race.”

### Earth-spiders and feast killings

**[EA]** The chronicles call some resisters *tsuchigumo*, “earth-spiders,” a derogatory category for unsubdued local people rather than evidence for literal monsters. In the *Kojiki*, eighty tailed earth-spiders in an Osaka cave are defeated.

**[EA: *Nihon shoki*]** Jimmu uses deception against hostile groups: enemies are invited to a feast, individual soldiers are assigned victims, and a song signals the attack. This is a narrated massacre under hospitality’s cover, not merely ceremonial incorporation.

### Sacred clay and sacrifice

**[EA: *Nihon shoki*]** Following a dream, Jimmu sends disguised agents to obtain clay from sacred Mount Kagu. Vessels are made without a potter’s wheel, and he sacrifices to heavenly and earthly gods. The chronicle depicts ritual mastery as a prerequisite of conquest.

**[EA]** Jimmu appoints Michi-no-omi to oversee worship of Takamimusubi and performs divination and ritual. There is animal or offering language in the ritual complex, but no reliable source here documents human sacrifice by Jimmu; claims that the expedition instituted human sacrifice should not be inferred from its killings.

### Naga-sune-hiko, Nigihayahi and the golden kite

**[EA]** Naga-sune-hiko protests that he already serves a heavenly descendant, Nigihayahi, who descended with heavenly insignia and married his sister. This is a serious complication: Jimmu is not the only figure claiming heavenly authorization in Yamato.

**[EA: *Kojiki*]** Nigihayahi recognizes Jimmu’s heavenly status, presents the celestial tokens, and submits. The text does not foreground a golden kite.

**[EA: *Nihon shoki*]** A golden kite (*kinshi*, 金鵄) alights on Jimmu’s bow during battle. Its radiance blinds or dazzles the enemy. Naga-sune-hiko shows Nigihayahi’s heavenly tokens, but Jimmu replies that many heavenly descendants exist. When Naga-sune-hiko refuses submission, Nigihayahi kills him and joins Jimmu.

**[EA versus CE]** The golden kite and Yatagarasu are separate birds in the *Nihon shoki*: one guides the route, the other produces battlefield radiance. Modern images often merge them or place an ambiguously golden, sometimes three-legged bird on Jimmu’s bow.

### Kashihara and enthronement

**[EA: *Nihon shoki*]** Jimmu surveys the Kashihara region by Mount Unebi, builds a palace, takes the formal queen, and accedes on the first day of the first month in a *kanoto-tori* year. The chronicle’s traditional computation equates this with 660 BCE; modern conversion produced 11 February.

**[EA]** The speech associated with founding includes the phrase conventionally paraphrased as “cover the eight directions and make them one dwelling” (*hakkō wo ōite ie to nasan*). It concerns the construction of a capital in the chronicle; **hakkō ichiu** (八紘一宇) as a compact slogan is a modern coinage, not Jimmu’s verbatim ancient motto.

**[MSR]** The 660 BCE date has no external confirmation. The extreme reign lengths before the more historically accessible rulers show that the compilers extended the dynasty backward through schematic chronology. Proposals involving a 1,260-year calendrical cycle are scholarly reconstructions of the compilers’ arithmetic, not dates preserved independently from the seventh century BCE.

### Wives and children

**[EA]** Before Yamato, Jimmu has a consort called Ahiratsu-hime/Ahira-hime and sons including Tagishimimi; forms and the number of sons differ between the chronicles.

**[EA: *Kojiki*]** His Yamato queen Isukeyori-hime is daughter of the god Ōmononushi, who approaches her mother in the form of a red-painted arrow entering the privy or water channel. Jimmu courts her after seven maidens are pointed out. Their sons include Kamuyai-mimi and Kamu-nunakawamimi, the future Suizei.

**[EA: *Nihon shoki*]** The queen Himetataraisuzu-hime is instead genealogically attached to Kotoshironushi through a similarly divine conception complex. This is a substantive parentage variant linking the dynasty to different Yamato divine houses.

### Death and burial

**[EA: *Kojiki*]** Jimmu dies aged 127 and is buried north of Mount Unebi, on or near Kashihara’s Oak-tail ridge.

**[EA: *Nihon shoki*]** He dies after a reign of seventy-six years at the age of 127 or 137 according to textual and chronological traditions; burial follows at the Unebi-yama northeast mausoleum. Traditional dates give 585 BCE.

**[MSR]** Such ages and dates are parts of the chronicle system, not biological or archaeological evidence.

**[MI]** The present Imperial Household Agency tomb is a round mound at Ōkubo, Kashihara, designated in 1863 after competing locations had been advanced. Official administration calls it Jimmu’s *Unebiyama no ushitora no sumi no misasagi*; archaeology has not authenticated it as a seventh-century-BCE royal burial. A 2002 Diet record acknowledges four competing late-Edo candidates and explains that the current site was selected from topography, place-names and chronicle descriptions. [National Diet record](https://www.shugiin.go.jp/internet/itdb_kaigirokua.nsf/html/kaigirokua/004115420020408001.htm)

## 4. Relations with gods and other heroes

### Genealogical alternatives

| Relationship | *Kojiki* (712) | *Nihon shoki* (720) | Classification |
|---|---|---|---|
| Heavenly ancestress | Amaterasu through Oshihomimi and Ninigi | Same broad line, with variant divine genealogies in the preceding books | [EA] |
| Father | Ugayafukiaezu | Ugayafukiaezu | [EA] |
| Mother | Tamayori-hime, daughter of the sea god | Tamayori-hime | [EA] |
| Brothers | Itsuse, Inahi, Mikenu | Corresponding brothers with variant name forms and some different fate details | [EA] |
| Earlier consort | Ahiratsu-hime | Ahira-tsu-hime, with variant titulature | [EA] |
| Yamato queen | Isukeyori-hime, daughter of Ōmononushi | Himetataraisuzu-hime, daughter of Kotoshironushi | [EA conflict] |
| Successor | Kamu-nunakawamimi/Suizei | Suizei | [EA] |

### Documented interactions, one per named figure

“Interaction” below requires direct narrative contact, communicated assistance, conflict, marriage, or formal submission. Mere ancestry is identified separately.

- **Itsuse-no-Mikoto** — Brother and co-leader; fights beside Jimmu, is wounded by Naga-sune-hiko’s forces, advises reversing the solar direction, and dies during withdrawal. *Kojiki* 712; *Nihon shoki* 720. **[EA]**

- **Inahi-no-Mikoto** — Brother; associated with the expeditionary generation, though the chronicles vary over when and how he enters the sea and returns to his maternal sea lineage. *Kojiki* 712; *Nihon shoki* 720. **[EA; variant chronology]**

- **Mikenu/Mikeirinu-no-Mikoto** — Brother; disappears into the sea or goes to Tokoyo in the chronicle variants. *Kojiki* 712; *Nihon shoki* 720. **[EA; variant chronology]**

- **Shiotsutsu-no-oji** — Advises Jimmu in the *Nihon shoki* that the east contains a fine land and another heavenly descendant, prompting the expedition. *Nihon shoki* 720. **[EA; absent in this role from the *Kojiki* account]**

- **Usatsuhiko** — Receives and feeds Jimmu’s party at Usa. *Kojiki* 712; *Nihon shoki* 720, with elaboration. **[EA]**

- **Usatsuhime** — Receives the expedition with Usatsuhiko; the *Nihon shoki* connects her by marriage to a follower. *Kojiki* 712; *Nihon shoki* 720. **[EA]**

- **Sao-netsu-hiko/Shiinetsuhiko** — Pilot and guide encountered at sea; becomes an expeditionary retainer and in the *Nihon shoki* undertakes the disguised Mount Kagu clay mission. *Kojiki* 712; *Nihon shoki* 720. **[EA]**

- **Naga-sune-hiko/Tomi-biko** — Principal Yamato enemy; his forces kill Itsuse. In the *Nihon shoki* he disputes which heavenly descendant possesses legitimate tokens and is killed by Nigihayahi. *Kojiki* 712; *Nihon shoki* 720. **[EA]**

- **Amaterasu Ōmikami** — Divine ancestress; in the *Nihon shoki* communicates protective guidance and sends Yatagarasu, while the *Kojiki* distributes immediate action differently among Amaterasu, Takagi and Takemikazuchi. *Kojiki* 712; *Nihon shoki* 720. **[EA; variant agency]**

- **Takagi-no-kami/Takamimusubi** — Sends Yatagarasu in the *Kojiki* and is the deity whom Jimmu publicly worships in the *Nihon shoki*. *Kojiki* 712; *Nihon shoki* 720. **[EA]**

- **Takemikazuchi** — Declines to descend again but sends the land-subduing sword to Jimmu through Takakuraji. *Kojiki* 712; parallel *Nihon shoki* tradition. **[EA]**

- **Takakuraji** — Receives the divine sword through a dream and delivers it, reviving Jimmu’s force. *Kojiki* 712; *Nihon shoki* 720. **[EA]**

- **Yatagarasu** — Heavenly guide from Kumano through the mountains; also acts as messenger to enemies in the expanded chronicle. *Kojiki* 712; *Nihon shoki* 720. **[EA]** Three-legged appearance: **[CE/LS]**.

- **Nihe-motsu-no-ko** — Local earthly deity/person encountered fishing in Yoshino; submits and explains that he came to meet the heavenly deity. *Kojiki* 712. **[EA]**

- **Ihika** — Luminous-tailed local figure emerging from a well in Yoshino; acknowledges Jimmu. *Kojiki* 712; related *Nihon shoki* localization. **[EA]**

- **Iwa-oshi-waku-no-ko** — “Child of the rock-pusher,” a tailed Yoshino figure who meets and submits to Jimmu. *Kojiki* 712. **[EA]**

- **Eukashi, elder Ukashi** — Feigns submission and prepares a crushing trap; Jimmu’s men force him into it and kill him. *Kojiki* 712; *Nihon shoki* 720. **[EA]**

- **Otoukashi, younger Ukashi** — Exposes his brother’s plot, submits, and receives a place among the victor’s adherents. *Kojiki* 712; *Nihon shoki* 720. **[EA]**

- **Michi-no-omi** — Chief retainer and ritual-military agent; executes stratagems and is appointed master of Takamimusubi’s worship. *Nihon shoki* 720. **[EA]**

- **Ōkume-no-Mikoto** — Warrior-retainer, participant in the Ukashi confrontation, and intermediary in Jimmu’s courtship of Isukeyori-hime. *Kojiki* 712. **[EA]**

- **Nigihayahi-no-Mikoto** — Rival heavenly descendant already in Yamato; recognizes Jimmu’s tokens and submits. In the *Nihon shoki* he kills Naga-sune-hiko. *Kojiki* 712; *Nihon shoki* 720. **[EA]**

- **The golden kite (*kinshi*)** — Alights on Jimmu’s bow and blinds the hostile army with radiance. *Nihon shoki* 720. **[EA]** It is not named as an individual deity and is not Yatagarasu in this source.

- **Ahiratsu-hime** — Pre-Yamato consort and mother of Tagishimimi and, in some forms, another son. *Kojiki* 712; *Nihon shoki* 720. **[EA]**

- **Isukeyori-hime/Himetataraisuzu-hime** — Yamato queen whom Jimmu courts and marries; mother of the successor’s branch. *Kojiki* 712; *Nihon shoki* 720. **[EA; name and parentage variants]**

- **Tagishimimi** — Son from the earlier union; survives Jimmu and later attempts to eliminate the younger heirs. The murderous succession episode occurs after Jimmu’s death. *Kojiki* 712; *Nihon shoki* 720. **[EA]**

- **Kamu-nunakawamimi/Suizei** — Son and eventual successor; direct father-son relation, with decisive action against Tagishimimi narrated after Jimmu’s death. *Kojiki* 712; *Nihon shoki* 720. **[EA]**

- **Kamuyai-mimi** — Son who participates in the posthumous Tagishimimi crisis but yields precedence to his braver brother. *Kojiki* 712; *Nihon shoki* 720. **[EA]**

- **Umashimaji/Umashimami** — Son of Nigihayahi and Mononobe ancestor. Later *Sendai kuji hongi* tradition gives him a magnified ritual relationship to Jimmu’s court. Ninth–tenth-century text. **[CE/LS]**

### Named deities related genealogically but not directly encountered

- **Ōmononushi** — *Kojiki* father of Jimmu’s queen; no direct meeting with Jimmu is narrated. **[EA]**
- **Kotoshironushi** — *Nihon shoki* father of the queen; no direct meeting with Jimmu is narrated. **[EA variant]**
- **Watatsumi** — Maternal great-grandfather; no direct interaction. **[EA]**
- **Ninigi, Hoori/Hiko-hohodemi, Oshihomimi** — ancestors, not companions in the expedition. **[EA]**
- **Susanoo, Yamato Takeru, Momotarō, Yoshitsune and Benkei** — no early or canonical interaction with Jimmu. Pairings in modern pantheon charts, games or popular illustration are **[MI]** unless a particular modern work is cited.

## 5. Cult, tomb and ritual

### Ancient and medieval record

**[EA/CE]** The chronicles give Jimmu a burial place near Mount Unebi. Later state ritual treated the tomb as an imperial mausoleum. The *Engishiki* (927) lists its location and administrative dimensions. This proves official tenth-century commemoration, not that officials possessed an authenticated seventh-century-BCE grave.

**[CE]** The *Nihon shoki* records an early imperial concern with Jimmu’s mausoleum in the late seventh century. Scholarship has argued that founder-mausoleum rites became significant during Tenmu’s court, close to the period in which the dynastic histories were being organized. This is evidence for a founder cult in the state-forming centuries, not for Jimmu’s assigned lifetime.

**[MA]** As central mortuary administration declined, the physical identification was lost or contested. Early-modern antiquarians proposed several sites around Unebi. Motoori Norinaga favored Maruyama; other scholars supported the place called Jimbu-den/Misanzai.

### The modern tomb

**[MI]** During the Bunkyū mausoleum restorations, the shogunate and court selected the present site in 1863. The reasons recorded in later official testimony were correspondence to “northeast of Unebi,” a surviving mound, and suggestive place-names. Four candidates still existed at the time. The designation was therefore an antiquarian-political judgment, not an excavation-based identification. [Diet testimony](https://www.shugiin.go.jp/internet/itdb_kaigirokua.nsf/html/kaigirokua/004115420020408001.htm)

**[MI]** The Imperial Household Agency now maintains the site as a round imperial mound and identifies Jimmu as the first emperor. Access and excavation are restricted. Limited construction monitoring has recovered historic-period material, but nothing publicly reported authenticates a Jimmu burial. [Imperial Household Agency](https://www.kunaicho.go.jp/visit/ryobo/001.html)

### Kashihara Jingū

**[MI]** Kashihara Jingū was not an excavated archaic shrine rediscovered intact. Local loyalists petitioned for a shrine on the supposed palace site; the Meiji emperor approved it, donated former Kyoto palace buildings, and the shrine was enshrined on 2 April 1890. Its deities are Jimmu and his queen. [Kashihara Jingū history](https://kashiharajingu.or.jp/about)

**[MI]** As a *kanpei taisha*, it became a top-ranked institution of State Shinto and a performative “birthplace of Japanese history.” Ritual forms associated with the Jimmu cycle included revived Kume dance, local Kuzu performance and newly composed imperial kagura. [Karli Shimizu, “Kashihara Jingū and a Japanese Secularity”](https://eprints.lib.hokudai.ac.jp/repo/huscap/all/94670/Karli_Shimizu.pdf)

### Festivals

- **Kigensai, 11 February.** **[MI]** Kashihara’s chief annual festival commemorates the accession and is conducted in the presence of an imperial envoy. The shrine reports attendance numbering in the thousands. [Kashihara Jingū](https://kashiharajingu.or.jp/event/%E7%B4%80%E5%85%83%E7%A5%AD)

- **Jinmu Tennō-sai, 3 April.** **[MI]** A modern imperial and shrine observance of Jimmu’s traditional death date, developed during the late Tokugawa–Meiji restructuring of imperial rites.

- **Kume-mai.** **[CE/MI]** Court dance linked by learned tradition to the expedition’s warrior songs; modern performances are revivals within later ritual systems, not unchanged survivals demonstrable from 660 BCE.

There is no secure evidence for a continuously operating popular Jimmu shrine at Kashihara from prehistoric antiquity through the medieval period.

## 6. Historical evolution of the figure

### Before 712: unattested horizon

**[MSR]** The cycle probably drew on genealogies, place traditions, court songs and clan traditions circulating before compilation. Their precise age cannot be established. The absence of Jimmu from contemporary writing before 712 prevents recovery of an original oral form.

### 712–720: canonical formation

**[EA]** The *Kojiki* shaped a genealogical and mythic founder; the *Nihon shoki* transformed him into an annalistically dated conquering monarch. The latter’s Chinese historiographical idiom, formal speeches and chronology supplied the basis for later “biographical” retellings.

### Eighth–tenth centuries: court systematization

**[CE/LS]** Chinese-style posthumous nomenclature, court lectures, clan memorials, the *Kogo shūi*, *Sendai kuji hongi* and *Engishiki* stabilized his name, relation to ritual lineages and mausoleum. Mononobe-oriented tradition enlarged Nigihayahi and Umashimaji without displacing Jimmu’s sovereignty.

### Medieval period

**[MA]** Jimmu remained important primarily in genealogical and legitimacy discourse rather than becoming a major warrior-romance protagonist. *Jinnō shōtōki* used him to articulate an unbroken sacred succession during the Northern–Southern Court conflict. Medieval relic claims concerning ancient imperial clothing or regalia belong to court memory; reported destruction of supposed Jimmu garments in the 1333 palace fire prevents verification.

### Early modern period

**[MA/LS]** Confucian historiography, Mito scholarship and *kokugaku* turned Jimmu into an object of textual criticism, moral interpretation and territorial antiquarianism. Norinaga’s philology elevated the *Kojiki* and helped recover its archaic readings. Competing tomb identifications show that the physical location was not continuously certain.

### 1863–1890: physical and civic reconstruction

**[MI]** Designation and monumental repair of the tomb began in the political crisis of the late shogunate. Meiji government then made Jimmu’s accession the origin of national chronology, established civic holidays and founded Kashihara Jingū.

### 1890–1945: imperial founder and mobilizing symbol

**[MI]** School history, mass prints, ceremonies, tourism, army symbolism and national holidays taught Jimmu as a historical founder. His bow, golden bird, beard and archaizing military dress became recognizable iconography. The divine-line narrative naturalized rule by the modern imperial house and was extended to colonial subjects.

### 1945–1966: delegitimation and dispute

**[MI/MSR]** Defeat, occupation reform and the abolition of State Shinto removed Jimmu from compulsory civic orthodoxy. Kigensetsu was abolished under the 1948 Holiday Law. Postwar textbooks and mainstream historians treated the early imperial narrative as mythology or later political construction rather than literal national history.

### 1966–present: symbolic holiday, shrine cult and contested memory

**[MI]** The holiday returned by legislation as *Kenkoku kinen no hi*, “National Foundation Day,” first observed in 1967. The inserted *no* avoids formally declaring that 11 February is the empirically known date of the state’s foundation. Government language describes the day as recalling the nation’s foundation and cultivating love of country without legally affirming Jimmu’s historicity. [Cabinet Office](https://www8.cao.go.jp/chosei/shukujitsu/gaiyou/kaku.html)

**[MI]** Kashihara and other shrines continue ritual commemoration. Conservative organizations treat the date as continuity with the imperial foundation; leftist, secular and historians’ groups criticize it as a revived State Shinto symbol. The argument is therefore about public memory and constitutional religion as much as about ancient chronology.

## 7. Modern reception and reinvention

### Visual art

**[MI]** Earlier securely dated images are scarce; Jimmu’s now-familiar bodily appearance is overwhelmingly modern. Tsukioka Yoshitoshi’s 1880 print in *Mirror of Famous Generals of Great Japan* shows a bearded martial founder with long bow and golden bird. It helped establish the iconic warrior-emperor. [Yoshitoshi print](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File%3AEmperor_Jimmu.jpg)

**[MI]** Meiji “Eastern Expedition” paintings and prints represented conquest in Western-influenced perspective and heroic anatomy. Adachi Ginkō, Kobayashi Eitaku and others made episodes of the chronicles available as national history imagery. The Agency for Cultural Affairs records an Eitaku expedition painting as a Meiji work. [Cultural Heritage Online](https://online.bunka.go.jp/db/heritages/detail/432381)

**[MI]** Nakamura Fusetsu’s 1916 *Enthronement of Emperor Jimmu* monumentalized Kashihara in a modern historical-painting idiom. The costumes are artistic reconstructions, not documentation of seventh-century-BCE regalia.

**[MI]** Votive paintings, school illustrations, postcards and stamps repeated three motifs: bow, bird and eastern-conquest landscape. Modern works often confuse the golden kite with three-legged Yatagarasu.

### Education and print culture

**[MI]** Meiji and pre-1945 school histories narrated Jimmu’s expedition and accession as the beginning of national history, while the Imperial Rescript on Education and emperor-centered moral education supplied the ideological environment. Unlike Momotarō, whose standardized schoolbook tale became a mass children’s narrative, Jimmu was fixed through national history, ethics, ceremony and calendrical instruction.

**[MI]** Kashihara Jingū published a manga, *Jinmu Tennō go-ichidaiki*, in 2016 to popularize the expedition on the 2,600th anniversary of his traditional death. This is a modern devotional retelling, not a newly discovered source. [Kashihara Jingū manga notice](https://kashiharajingu.or.jp/news/%E6%A9%BF%E5%8E%9F%E3%81%A0%E3%82%88%E3%82%8A/402.html)

**[MI]** Yasuhiko Yoshikazu’s manga *Jinmu* and modern historical novels imaginatively naturalize the chronicle material, adding characterization and political motives. Such works document contemporary reception only.

### Kigensetsu and national chronology

**[MI]** In 1872–1873 the government adopted Jimmu’s accession as the epoch of the imperial calendar and established Kigensetsu. Initial calendrical confusion produced 29 January; from 1874 the observance was fixed on 11 February through conversion of the chronicle’s lunar date.

**[MI]** The Meiji Constitution was promulgated on Kigensetsu, 11 February 1889, deliberately joining modern constitutional statehood to mythical foundation.

### The 2,600th anniversary, 1940

**[MI]** Because 660 BCE counted as imperial year 1, 1940 was celebrated as *kōki* 2600. State bodies, municipalities, newspapers, shrines, corporations and colonial administrations organized ceremonies, exhibitions, monuments, songs, travel and consumer goods. Kenneth Ruoff characterizes it as one of Imperial Japan’s most comprehensive commemorations and as the climax of “unbroken imperial line” ideology. [Kenneth Ruoff, *Imperial Japan at Its Zenith*](https://www.jstor.org/stable/10.7591/j.ctt1287cnm)

**[MI]** Celebration did not consist only of austere militarism: it also drove domestic tourism, commercial products, civic construction and entertainment. That consumer dimension does not erase its operation within an empire then waging war in China.

### *Hakkō ichiu*

**[MI]** Nichirenist thinker Tanaka Chigaku coined the compact expression *hakkō ichiu* from the *Nihon shoki* passage. By the late 1930s it was detached from its immediate capital-building context and made a slogan for imperial unity and expansion.

**[MI]** The 1940 tower in Miyazaki—originally the *Hakkō ichiu* tower, later renamed Peace Tower—materialized this reading. Scholarship documents a shift from Jimmu as benevolent domestic unifier to precedent for holy war and imperial mission. [Walter Edwards, “Forging Tradition for a Holy War”](https://online.ucpress.edu/jjs/article-abstract/29/2/289/201381/Forging-Tradition-for-a-Holy-WarThe-Hakko-Ichiu)

### Wartime media

**[MI]** Jimmu’s own cycle appeared in commemorative songs, prints, maps, pageants and news media. The better-known wartime *Momotarō* animated propaganda films use another hero; they belong to the same myth-nationalist media environment but are not adaptations of Jimmu. Conflating them would obscure how different heroic traditions were assigned different propaganda functions.

### Contemporary politics

**[MI]** National Foundation Day remains an official public holiday. A 2026 prime-ministerial message repeated the statutory formulation about recalling foundation and fostering love of nation, but did not assert that Jimmu was a verified historical person. [Prime Minister’s Office](https://japan.kantei.go.jp/104/statement/202602/11message.html)

**[MI]** Shrine organizations and nationalist groups continue to speak of accession and “2,686 imperial years” as sacred-national chronology. Critics—communist, secularist, teachers’ and historians’ organizations among them—describe the date as scientifically groundless and politically continuous with Kigensetsu. The two positions concern the same modern commemoration but use different standards: cultic-symbolic truth versus empirical history.

## 8. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Historicity

**Mainstream position [MSR]:** Jimmu cannot be verified as a historical individual. The assigned seventh-century-BCE setting precedes the emergence of the Yamato royal order evidenced by later Yayoi and Kofun archaeology; the narratives first appear in eighth-century court texts; names, speeches and dates are literary; and the ages and reigns are schematic.

**Qualified-migration position [MSR]:** Some historians have proposed that the eastward journey preserves a memory of population movement, maritime expansion or a Kyūshū-linked group entering the Kinai. The route’s geographical realism makes this possible as a tradition-history hypothesis, but it does not prove the protagonist, date or single campaign.

**Late-Yamato conquest position [MSR]:** Other reconstructions move a possible background into the third or fourth century, when archaeological evidence demonstrates expanding political centralization. This supplies a plausible social context, not a textual identification.

**Literalist position [LS/MI]:** Shrine apologetics and nationalist histories sometimes argue that absence of evidence does not disprove Jimmu or that later archaeological discoveries may vindicate the chronicles. The logical possibility is true but does not convert the narrative into verified history.

### Tsuda Sōkichi

**[MSR]** Tsuda’s *Kojiki oyobi Nihon shoki no shinkenkyū* (1919) and related studies argued through source criticism that the early imperial narratives were constructed to express the political ideas of later Yamato monarchy rather than preserve reliable biographies of the earliest emperors.

**[MI]** Wartime authorities prosecuted Tsuda and restricted his books; postwar scholarship elevated him into a symbol of suppressed critical history. Recent historiography cautions that a simplified “Tsuda proved all myth false” image obscures both his nuanced arguments and subsequent archaeological work. [Japan Review](https://japanreview.jp/issues/34/2554/)

### Jimmu and Sujin

**[MSR]** Both are called forms of “first ruler of the realm,” while the intervening eight emperors have little narrative beyond genealogy. This led to theories that Sujin was the true first king, that Jimmu duplicates Sujin, or that separate traditions of beginnings were combined. Similar titles are evidence of compositional difficulty, not proof that the two names denote one person.

### Date construction

**[MSR]** The 660 BCE accession is rejected as historical chronology. Explanations invoke sexagenary cycles, Chinese calendrical ideas and the desire to make the dynasty sufficiently ancient. No surviving compiler’s memorandum states exactly how the date was calculated, so any single formula should remain labeled reconstruction.

### Nigihayahi and rival legitimacy

**[MSR]** Nigihayahi’s prior heavenly descent shows that the chronicles preserved or accommodated a rival lineage, later associated particularly with the Mononobe. The scene resolves the contradiction by making the rival acknowledge Jimmu. Claims that it proves a specific ethnic invasion or that Nigihayahi was “the real first emperor” go beyond the evidence.

### Tomb debate

**[MA/MI]** The tomb’s location was debated among early-modern scholars and formally selected only in 1863. Archaeologists cannot treat present designation as proven provenance, and access restrictions impede testing. The broader imperial-tomb controversy concerns whether mausolea should remain inviolable dynastic religious sites or be investigated as cultural and archaeological properties. Legal-historical scholarship notes that very few ancient imperial tomb attributions are considered secure by archaeologists. [Imperial tomb study](https://droit.cairn.info/revue-revue-du-droit-public-2020-1-page-191?lang=fr&tab=texte-integral)

### Archaeological overclaims

**Contested claim [MSR]:** A recent publication interprets figures on third-century bronze mirrors as Jimmu’s warriors and the eastern expedition. The objects themselves are ancient; identifying their imagery with a named eighth-century textual episode is interpretive, not an inscriptional identification. Its conclusion that Jimmu’s campaign is thereby “firmly” historicized has not displaced the mainstream source-critical position. [German Archaeological Institute catalogue](https://publications.dainst.org/books/dai/catalog/book/2056)

### Yatagarasu

**[MSR]** The three-legged bird is a classic case of iconographic back-projection. The eighth-century texts say “Yatagarasu/great crow,” while later learned culture identifies it with the continental three-legged solar crow. Modern sporting and shrine emblems make the later form seem primordial.

### State Shinto and the “invented tradition” question

**[MSR]** The texts and some court rites are genuinely ancient or medieval; Kashihara Jingū, Kigensetsu, the standardized warrior portrait, mass national schooling and *hakkō ichiu* mobilization are modern. “Invented tradition” therefore does not mean that Meiji officials invented Jimmu from nothing. It means that they selected old texts, fixed disputed places and dates, created institutions and presented the resulting synthesis as immemorial continuity.

### State of the evidence

- No original *Kojiki* or *Nihon shoki* manuscript survives.
- The earliest complete *Kojiki* witness is more than six centuries later than composition.
- Early *Nihon shoki* manuscripts survive only in parts, though their age and lecture tradition securely establish the work long before the late medieval period.
- The original *Nihon shoki* genealogical scroll is lost.
- No contemporary Jimmu inscription or securely attributable portrait exists.
- The designated tomb has not supplied public archaeological confirmation.
- The strongest evidence concerns the eighth-century construction and later reception of the founder, not a seventh-century-BCE life.

## 10. Sources and assessment

### Primary and critical-text priorities

- *Kojiki*, middle scroll, Jimmu sections. The Chamberlain translation is historically important but linguistically dated; it should be checked against modern critical Japanese editions such as the Shinpen Nihon Koten Bungaku Zenshū, Nihon Shisō Taikei or Nihon Koten Bungaku Taikei texts. An accessible Chamberlain text preserves the complete episode sequence. [Chamberlain translation contents](https://sacred-texts.com/shi/kj/index.htm?chaptersPage=3)

- *Nihon shoki*, Book 3. W. G. Aston’s translation remains useful but dated; modern editions and translations should control readings, especially names, variant notes and calendrical language.

- *Kogo shūi*, *Sendai kuji hongi* Books 6–7, *Engishiki* mortuary and ritual sections, *Shaku Nihongi*, and *Jinnō shōtōki*.

- Motoori Norinaga, *Kojiki-den*, for early-modern philology and tomb debate—not as an independent witness to prehistory.

### Major modern research directions

- Tsuda Sōkichi: textual construction of early imperial history.
- John S. Brownlee: Japanese historians and national myths.
- Klaus Antoni: *kokutai*, myth, State Shinto and postwar transformation.
- Kenneth J. Ruoff: the 1940 anniversary as political, consumer and imperial culture.
- Walter Edwards: Jimmu, *hakkō ichiu* and the transformation of founding myth for holy war.
- Takagi Hiroshi and Tōike Noboru: modern imperial mausolea, commemoration and the manufacture of national historical space.
- Karli Shimizu: Kashihara Jingū as modern sacred and ostensibly civic space.
- Recent scholarship on Tsuda: caution against reducing complex source criticism to a simple mythology-versus-history binary.

### Concluding evidentiary judgment

**[EA]** Emperor Jimmu is securely real as an eighth-century textual founder: the first ruler in the court’s canonical genealogy, a solar descendant who conquers Yamato through warfare, deception, ritual and divine aid.

**[MSR]** He is not securely recoverable as a seventh-century-BCE person. No contemporary document, inscription, grave or image bridges the millennium separating the traditional date from the chronicles.

**[MA/MI]** His historical force nevertheless became concrete. Medieval legitimists made him the opening human sovereign; early-modern philologists argued over his language and grave; nineteenth-century officials assigned him a tomb, shrine, calendar and holiday; artists gave him a body; schools gave him a national audience; and the 1940 state made his expedition an imperial world-political precedent.

The dossier’s central distinction is therefore not “real or unreal,” but between two separately documentable histories: an unverifiable life placed in 711–585 BCE by the *Nihon shoki* system, and a richly verifiable history of people writing, depicting, worshipping and politically mobilizing Jimmu from 712 CE to the present.
