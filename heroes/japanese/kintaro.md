# Kintarō: Research Dossier

**Traditional class:** Kintarō is a **legendary wonder-child and culture hero who becomes a mortal warrior**, Sakata no Kintoki, in the service of Minamoto no Yorimitsu/Raikō. Some modern shrines treat Kintoki as a protective kami of children and health, but no medieval evidence establishes a continuous ancient hero-cult.

**Earliest secure attestation:** the oldest securely datable textual witness to the underlying figure is **“□□ no Kintoki” (□□公時), one of three retainers of Minamoto no Yorimitsu, in *Konjaku monogatari-shū* 28.2**, compiled in the early twelfth century, probably ca. 1120. That story knows an adult eastern warrior named Kintoki; it says nothing about Ashigara, a mountain-witch mother, bears, an axe, the name Kintarō, or the Shuten-dōji expedition. The composite “golden boy” recognizable today is an **early-modern creation**, assembled principally in seventeenth- and eighteenth-century puppet drama, popular books, theater, and prints.

## Evidence labels

- **[EA] Secure early attestation:** pre-Edo evidence securely attached to Kintoki.
- **[MC] Medieval canonical elaboration:** medieval narrative or pictorial expansion.
- **[EMA] Early-modern accretion:** Edo-period theater, books, prints, or learned synthesis.
- **[RV] Regional variant:** a geographically localized tradition, irrespective of claimed antiquity.
- **[F] Folklore:** orally collected or locally transmitted material whose date of origin is uncertain.
- **[LS] Learned systematization:** an antiquarian or scholarly attempt to produce a continuous biography.
- **[MSR] Modern scholarly reconstruction:** an argument by a modern researcher, not a primary tradition.
- **[MI] Modern invention or standardization:** Meiji or later creation, recasting, or demonstrably recent claim.
- **[CR] Cultic/ritual reception:** worship, festival, votive, or calendrical practice.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

Kintarō is not the protagonist of an ancient Japanese epic. He is the youthful form retrospectively assigned to a warrior called Kintoki, remembered as a retainer of Minamoto no Yorimitsu and later incorporated into the “Four Heavenly Kings” (*shitennō*) who destroy Shuten-dōji. The familiar child—red-skinned, bob-haired, nearly naked except for a red bib marked 金, carrying an axe, befriending animals and wrestling a bear on Mount Ashigara—is mostly the product of Edo-period performance and visual culture, subsequently naturalized by Meiji children’s books and song.

### Names

- **□□公時, Kintoki** — [EA], *Konjaku monogatari-shū* 28.2, early twelfth century. The surname is lost in the manuscript lacuna. The text calls him one of Yorimitsu’s three formidable eastern retainers alongside Taira no Sadamichi and Taira no Suetake. It does not call him Sakata.
- **坂田公時, Sakata no Kintoki** — [MC], securely present in the medieval Shuten-dōji tradition, including the fourteenth-century *Ōeyama ekotoba*. 公時 literally combines “public/ducal” and “time,” but here functions as a personal name; attempts to extract mythic meaning from the characters are speculative.
- **坂田金時, Sakata Kintoki** — [EMA]. 金時 substitutes 金, “gold,” for 公. This orthographic transformation helped make possible the name and visual rhetoric of “Golden Boy.” It is not the spelling in the earliest witness.
- **金太郎, Kintarō** — literally “Gold Tarō” or conventionally “Golden Boy.” *Tarō* is a common masculine name/form for an eldest son. The *Kokushi daijiten* account finds the name in the dance piece *Kantan sono no kikuchō*, performed at the Nakamura-za in 1809; ordinary-language citations become plentiful only in the nineteenth century. Thus “Kintarō was Kintoki’s historical childhood name” is not an early fact but retrospective legend.
- **怪童丸 / 快童丸, Kaidōmaru** — “monstrous/wondrous boy” or, with the favorable homophone 快, “vigorous boy.” [EMA] Chikamatsu Monzaemon’s *Komochi Yamauba* (1712) uses 快童丸. The punning spellings circulated in theater, picture books, and prints.
- **坂田民部金時, Sakata Minbu Kintoki** — [EMA], *Sukune Akutarō* (1664), a Kinpira-jōruri text, where he is the father of the hero Sakata Kinpira.
- **坂田宿禰公時, Sakata no Sukune Kintoki** — [EMA], *Kiyohara Udaishō* (1677), where Yorimitsu bestows the name upon a prodigious youth brought from Ashigara by an ogress.
- **金時 / 公時, Kintoki** — the two spellings continue side by side. Modern references often silently normalize the medieval 公時 to 金時, obscuring the chronology.

### “Sakata” and disputed etymologies

No surviving early record proves the origin of the surname. Later writers and local historians have associated it with:

- **Sakata in Ōmi Province**, especially the old Sakata District; [RV/LS] this supports theories that he belonged to an Ōmi lineage.
- **Sakata/Yamagata and other same-sounding places**; [RV] these are local origin claims without early corroboration.
- **酒田, “sake-field,”** used in some early-modern materials; orthographic variation should not be treated as proof of birthplace.

“Kintoki” became a productive color word: the hero’s bright red body contributed to *kintoki* as a designation for red-colored foods and objects, including *kintoki-mame*. That semantic afterlife is early-modern or modern reception, not evidence for an ancient solar or thunder deity.

### Status and divine identifications

In the medieval *Ōeyama ekotoba*, an old man explains the warriors’ **honji**, or Buddhist “original grounds”: Yorimitsu is a manifestation of Daiitoku Myōō, Tsuna of Tamonten, **Kintoki of Jikokuten**, and other retainers of the remaining directional kings. [MC/syncretistic identification] This is a medieval Buddhist equation, not parentage and not evidence that Kintoki began as Jikokuten.

The comparison between Yorimitsu’s four retainers and the Buddhist **Four Heavenly Kings** is itself a systematizing metaphor. The complete standard quartet—Watanabe no Tsuna, Sakata no Kintoki, Usui Sadamitsu, and Urabe no Suetake—does not occur as such in the earliest *Konjaku* notice.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### The first witness: *Konjaku monogatari-shū* 28.2

**Language:** classical Japanese with mixed logographic and phonographic notation.  
**Composition:** early twelfth century, conventionally ca. 1120; surviving textual transmission is later.  
**Evidence:** [EA].

The opening names three warriors serving “Settsu Governor Minamoto no Yorimitsu”: Taira no Sadamichi, Taira no Suetake, and “□□ no Kintoki.” They are handsome, skilled, brave, prudent eastern warriors. During the return day of the Kamo festival, they borrow a carriage, conceal themselves as if they were women, are violently jostled because they are unused to carriage travel, become motion-sick, vomit, lose their caps, and are mocked.

This is valuable precisely because it is unheroic. It establishes:

1. a Kintoki remembered as Yorimitsu’s retainer;
2. association with eastern warriors;
3. no preserved surname;
4. no childhood narrative;
5. no supernatural strength;
6. no Ashigara;
7. no Shuten-dōji;
8. no fixed group of four.

The story therefore cannot prove that the later Sakata Kintoki was historical. It proves only that by the early twelfth century a storyteller knew—or claimed to know—an adult retainer of that name.

### What is not in the corpus

The *Kojiki* and *Nihon shoki* contain no Kintarō or Sakata Kintoki. Their heroic cycles concern figures such as Yamato Takeru and provide comparative context, not witnesses to this legend. Likewise, *Heike monogatari* and *Gikeiki* are indispensable for later warrior heroes such as Yoshitsune and Benkei but are not primary Kintarō texts. *Nihon ryōiki* and *Konjaku* are important setsuwa corpora, but only the latter supplies the early Kintoki notice. The Meiji Yoshitsune-as-Genghis-Khan theory and wartime Momotarō films are parallel examples of modern heroic invention and propaganda; they are not parts of Kintarō’s documentary corpus.

## 3. The story and its variants

### 3.1 Parentage and birth

No early or medieval source preserves Kintoki’s childhood.

#### The yamauba’s child

In seventeenth-century Kinpira-jōruri, Kintoki is attached to Ashigara and to a yamauba or ogress. *Sukune Akutarō* (1664) says that while Yorimitsu was concealed at Ashigara, a yamauba presented her child to him. *Kiyohara Udaishō* (1677) makes the youth sixteen or seventeen when an ogress brings him out and Yorimitsu names him. [EMA]

Chikamatsu’s *Komochi Yamauba* (1712) gives the best-known theatrical genealogy. Yaegiri, associated with the courtesan tradition and the warrior Sakata Kurandō Tokiyuki, becomes a mountain woman; their prodigious child is Kaidōmaru. Chikamatsu combines the Noh yamauba, Kinpira heroic genealogy, and a wonder-child spectacle. [EMA]

#### Red dragon and mountain woman

*Zen-Taiheiki* (preface 1803) presents a more cosmic birth. An old woman tells Yorimitsu that while sleeping on the mountain she dreamed a **red dragon** came and coupled with her; the resulting son is the youthful-looking twenty-one-year-old he sees. Yorimitsu recognizes the pattern of miraculous rulers and recruits him. [LS/EMA] Some retellings soften this into dream-conception; others state directly that Kintoki is the son of a dragon and a yamauba.

Claims that the father was **Raijin**, a thunder god, circulate widely online and in modern popular mythology. The securely traceable early-modern source found in this investigation specifies a red dragon, not Raijin. The Raijin equation should therefore be labeled [F/MI] unless a particular regional source is supplied.

#### Human parents: Tokiyuki and Yaegiri

Ashigara traditions make the father **Sakata Kurandō Tokiyuki** and the mother **Yaegiri**, sometimes a daughter of the immensely wealthy “Forty-Thousand Chōja.” In the modern Minamiashigara account, Yaegiri marries into the Sakata family, returns to Jizōdō during a family conflict, and gives birth there. In a collected Oyama tradition, Tokiyuki kills himself in the capital; Yaegiri reaches a waterfall, becomes a yamauba, and bears Kintarō. [RV/F] These are not compatible with the red-dragon version and should not be fused with it.

### 3.2 Childhood on Ashigara

The stable popular cycle is:

- brought up in the mountains;
- superhumanly strong from infancy;
- able to communicate or play with animals;
- wrestles a bear, and in some versions a boar;
- rides the bear as though practicing horsemanship;
- uproots trees or breaks rocks;
- carries an axe;
- sometimes bridges a ravine with a felled tree;
- sometimes fights or captures a giant carp.

The documentary order matters. Bear/boar sumō is explicit in *Komochi Yamauba* (1712). Animal companionship, the axe, carp, scarlet skin, and the simplified red bib were multiplied in eighteenth- and nineteenth-century picture books and prints. The canonical song beginning “まさかりかついで金太郎 / 熊にまたがりお馬の稽古” (“Kintarō carrying an axe / astride a bear practicing riding”) was published in 1900 and made that cluster nationally mnemonic. [EMA → MI]

The famous bib marked 金 should not be projected into medieval art. It is a visual label and theatrical/print costume. Redness may signify vigor, auspiciousness, childhood, theatrical visibility, or supernatural force; claims that it proves descent from a particular ancient red or thunder deity are [MSR] unless tied to a demonstrable historical text.

### 3.3 Recruitment by Yorimitsu

The recruitment is absent from *Konjaku*, where Kintoki is already a retainer. Early-modern drama creates an origin scene at Ashigara. Depending on the text:

- a yamauba offers her child to Yorimitsu (*Sukune Akutarō*, 1664);
- an ogress leads out an adolescent prodigy and Yorimitsu names him (*Kiyohara Udaishō*, 1677);
- Yorimitsu encounters an old woman and twenty-one-year-old “boy,” hears the red-dragon conception, and names him Sakata Kintoki (*Zen-Taiheiki*, 1803).

Some later retellings give Usui Sadamitsu the role of discoverer or scout. Unless a specific recension is named, “Yorimitsu found Kintarō” describes the standardized synthesis, not one immutable ancient account.

### 3.4 Shuten-dōji

The oldest extant developed Shuten-dōji narrative is the *Ōeyama ekotoba*, late fourteenth or early fifteenth century. Yorimitsu and Fujiwara no Yasumasa receive an imperial command. Yorimitsu takes Tsuna, Kintoki, Sadamitsu, and Suetake; Yasumasa takes Kiyohara no Munenobu. The party prays at great shrines, disguises itself as mountain ascetics, receives supernatural assistance, enters the oni’s residence, tricks him with sake, and attacks while he is incapacitated. [MC]

Kintoki’s particular prominence varies. In some recensions he performs a dance so effectively that the suspicious oni are disarmed; later texts and images make him one of the fighters against Shuten-dōji’s retainers. The famous statement “Kintarō killed Shuten-dōji” is imprecise: standard medieval and Edo narratives generally credit **Yorimitsu with decapitating Shuten-dōji**, while Kintoki participates in the expedition and fighting. Modern franchises may transfer the decisive blow to Kintoki; that is [MI].

The story includes abduction, imprisonment, cannibalism, deception and killing. The warriors knowingly intoxicate or poison the oni, bind him, and attack him in sleep. Narratives moralize this as an imperial rescue mission, but the mechanics should not be sanitized.

### 3.5 Other adult exploits

Later “Four Heavenly Kings” cycles attach Kintoki to:

- the defeat of the **tsuchigumo**;
- battles with demons and brigands;
- grotesque night attacks on Yorimitsu;
- comic strength contests;
- exploits of his son Kinpira.

The important limitation is that the early *Tsuchigumo sōshi* focuses on Yorimitsu and Tsuna. Generic statements that “Kintoki is in the original Tsuchigumo Scroll” exceed that evidence. Edo prints and later recensions do explicitly place the standardized quartet in the battle; Kuniyoshi-era imagery even shows Kintoki pinning a monster with a *goban*. [EMA]

### 3.6 Death and body

There is no securely early death account.

- **Shōō/Katsuta tradition:** Kintoki allegedly fell ill while marching toward Kyushu and died in 1010 or 1011, aged fifty-five, at present-day Shōō, Okayama. Kurikara Shrine is said to contain or enshrine his spirit. [RV/F]
- **Ashigara tradition:** after Yorimitsu’s death, he visited his master’s grave for three months, returned to Ashigara, and disappeared. [RV/F]
- **Kawanishi tradition:** a grave associated with Kintoki is shown in the region connected with the Seiwa Genji. [RV]
- **No death:** many children’s narratives end with recruitment or fame.

No excavated, securely dated grave, relic, contemporary funerary inscription, or authenticated set of bones identifies Sakata Kintoki. Exact dates such as birth in 956, recruitment on 1 March 976, Shuten-dōji’s defeat in 990, and death on 15 December 1010 are products of later chronological systematization and local heritage presentation, not contemporary annals.

## 4. Relations with gods and other heroes

### Genealogical variants

| Version | Mother | Father | Witness |
|---|---|---|---|
| Yamauba’s offered child | unnamed yamauba | unstated | *Sukune Akutarō*, 1664 [EMA] |
| Ogress’s prodigious youth | unnamed ogress | unstated | *Kiyohara Udaishō*, 1677 [EMA] |
| Yaegiri–Tokiyuki | Yaegiri, later yamauba | Sakata Kurandō Tokiyuki | *Komochi Yamauba*, 1712; regional traditions [EMA/RV] |
| Red-dragon conception | old mountain woman/yamauba | red dragon encountered in a dream | *Zen-Taiheiki*, 1803 [LS/EMA] |
| Forty-Thousand Chōja lineage | Yaegiri, chōja’s daughter | a Sakata man/Tokiyuki | Minamiashigara local synthesis [RV/F] |
| Raijin’s son | yamauba | Raijin | recent popular circulation; no securely identified early source [MI/F] |

A son, **Sakata Kinpira**, belongs to the seventeenth-century Kinpira-jōruri cycle. Kinpira’s fame was so great that *kinpira* became associated with toughness and, eventually, with the dish *kinpira gobō*. Kinpira should not be inserted into Kintoki’s early genealogy.

### Documented interactions with named divine beings and heroes

Each line records an interaction, not merely an association.

- **Minamoto no Yorimitsu/Raikō** — Kintoki is his retainer in *Konjaku monogatari-shū* 28.2, early twelfth century [EA]; accompanies him against Shuten-dōji in *Ōeyama ekotoba*, late fourteenth/early fifteenth century [MC]; is recruited and named by him in seventeenth-century drama and *Zen-Taiheiki* [EMA/LS].
- **Taira no Sadamichi** — fellow Yorimitsu retainer and companion in the disastrous carriage outing, *Konjaku* 28.2, early twelfth century [EA]. He is not automatically identical with Usui Sadamitsu.
- **Taira/Urabe no Suetake** — fellow retainer in *Konjaku* 28.2 [EA]; fellow member of the later Shuten-dōji quartet in *Ōeyama ekotoba* [MC].
- **Watanabe no Tsuna** — fellow warrior in the Shuten-dōji expedition, *Ōeyama ekotoba*, late fourteenth/early fifteenth century [MC]; repeatedly paired in later Four Kings cycles [EMA].
- **Usui Sadamitsu** — fellow member of Yorimitsu’s quartet in *Ōeyama ekotoba* [MC]; in some later recruitment traditions acts as discoverer or intermediary [EMA/RV].
- **Fujiwara no Yasumasa/Hōshō** — co-commander of the imperial Shuten-dōji expedition, *Ōeyama ekotoba* [MC].
- **Kiyohara no Munenobu** — member of Yasumasa’s contingent in *Ōeyama ekotoba* [MC].
- **Shuten-dōji** — Kintoki enters his residence in disguise, participates in deception and intoxication, and fights in the extermination expedition, *Ōeyama ekotoba* and later recensions [MC/EMA].
- **Ibaraki-dōji** — enemy lieutenant suspected of challenging the disguised warriors; Kintoki participates in the opposing expedition, later *Shuten-dōji* recensions [MC/EMA]. No early independent Kintoki–Ibaraki duel is securely established.
- **Hachiman of Iwashimizu** — Kintoki travels with the party that seeks divine aid; the deity or emissary assists the expedition, *Ōeyama ekotoba* and *otogizōshi* recensions [MC].
- **Hiyoshi deity** — Yorimitsu’s contingent prays at Hiyoshi in *Ōeyama ekotoba* [MC]; Kintoki is a member of the expedition, though the prayer is led by Yorimitsu.
- **Sumiyoshi Myōjin** — divine helper, appearing in disguised form in Shuten-dōji recensions and providing guidance/aid [MC].
- **Kumano Gongen** — divine helper associated with the disguised guides and the expedition [MC].
- **Jikokuten** — Kintoki is identified as Jikokuten’s manifestation in the *honji* explanation attached to the *Ōeyama ekotoba* tradition [MC/syncretistic identification].
- **Daiitoku Myōō** — indirect relation: Yorimitsu is declared Daiitoku’s manifestation in the same *honji* scheme, establishing the sacred hierarchy under which Kintoki fights [MC].
- **Tamonten** — indirect relation: Tsuna is declared Tamonten’s manifestation in the same scheme [MC].
- **Red dragon** — supernatural father in the conception narrative, *Zen-Taiheiki*, 1803 [EMA/LS].
- **Yamauba/Yaegiri** — mother, guardian and eventually beautiful maternal partner in seventeenth-century drama and Utamaro’s prints [EMA].
- **Sakata Kurandō Tokiyuki** — human father in *Komochi Yamauba* and related Yaegiri traditions, 1712 onward [EMA].
- **Sakata Kinpira** — Kintoki is his father in Kinpira-jōruri, mid-seventeenth century onward [EMA].
- **Tsuchigumo** — adversary only in later expanded Four Kings recensions and Edo images [EMA]; the early *Tsuchigumo sōshi* does not securely give Kintoki a named individual role.
- **Abe no Seimei** — no direct meeting is stated in the principal early Kintoki corpus; Seimei is mentioned in *Ōeyama ekotoba* as protecting the capital from the oni [MC, indirect].
- **Yamato Takeru, Yoshitsune, Benkei, Momotarō and Urashima Tarō** — no premodern narrative interaction found. Their pairing with Kintarō occurs through anthologies, toy sets, “Japanese heroes” groupings, and modern media [MI].
- **Raijin** — no securely traced early interaction; “father of Kintarō” is a later unsourced identification unless tied to a specified local record [MI/F].

## 5. Cult, tomb, and ritual

### No demonstrated medieval hero-cult

Unlike some ancient or classical heroes, Kintoki has no securely excavated medieval *heroon*, early votive inscription, state cult, or documented sequence of sacrifices. The evidence begins as narrative, not cult. Consequently, descriptions of him as an “ancient Japanese god” are unsupported.

### Kintoki Shrine, Hakone

Kintoki Jinja near Mount Kintoki in Sengokuhara enshrines Sakata Kintoki and is presently associated with children’s strength and health. Its modern festival on 5 May includes children’s events and sumō. [CR] The mountain, formerly also called Inohana-yama, and the shrine materially anchor the legend in Ashigara; neither proves that the Heian retainer was born there.

### Minamiashigara sacred landscape

Jizōdō and its surroundings preserve a dense legendary geography:

- Yaegiri’s or Kintarō’s dwelling site;
- Kabuto-ishi and Taiko-ishi, “play stones”;
- Yūhi-no-taki, said to provide his first bath;
- Yaegiri’s sitting stone;
- place-names, springs and mountain routes;
- a local yamauba image.

These are [RV/F/CR]. They document the localization and commemoration of the story, but presently available evidence does not date the physical features’ Kintarō associations to the Heian period.

### Shōō, Okayama

Kurikara Shrine is locally associated with Kintoki’s death and spirit, while the town holds a Kintoki festival. [RV/CR] The shrine and festival are genuine material and social facts; the claimed 1010/1011 death is not independently contemporary evidence.

### Boys’ Day and May dolls

During the Edo period, the fifth-month festival developed from protective seasonal practices and martial display into elaborate domestic celebration. By the middle Edo period, doll shops were selling armor, warriors, **Kintarō**, Momotarō, Issun-bōshi and Ushiwakamaru figures. Kintarō’s strength made him a wish-image for robust male children. Late Edo and Meiji popularization spread clay, papier-mâché and other regional Kintarō dolls. [CR/EMA]

Modern **Tango no Sekku/Kodomo no Hi** displays still include Kintarō, usually with axe, bear or red bib. This is domestic calendrical commemoration and protective aspiration, not evidence of an unchanged ancient Kintoki cult.

## 6. Historical evolution

### I. Early warrior memory: early twelfth century

A storyteller remembers Kintoki as a capable but comically embarrassed eastern retainer of Yorimitsu. There is no wonder-child. [EA]

### II. Medieval demon-slaying system: fourteenth–sixteenth centuries

The Shuten-dōji tradition places Sakata no Kintoki within a sacred imperial expedition. The quartet is modeled as Yorimitsu’s Four Heavenly Kings; Buddhist *honji* interpretation makes Kintoki a manifestation of Jikokuten. [MC]

### III. Kinpira and the invention of childhood: seventeenth century

Kinpira-jōruri turns Kintoki into an ancestral strongman and father. Plays dated 1664 and 1677 connect him to Ashigara, a yamauba or ogress, extraordinary youth and recruitment by Yorimitsu. [EMA]

### IV. Chikamatsu and theatrical synthesis: 1712

*Komochi Yamauba* supplies Yaegiri, Kaidōmaru and animal sumō, joining the Noh mountain woman to popular heroic genealogy. This is the decisive stage in producing the wonder-child. [EMA]

### V. Print icon: eighteenth–early nineteenth centuries

Picture books and prints stabilize the muscular red child, axe, bear, carp and mountain mother. Antiquarians such as the compiler of *Zen-Taiheiki* construct exact dates and a continuous biography. “Kintarō” overtakes older Kaidōmaru forms. [EMA/LS]

### VI. Utamaro’s maternal reinvention: ca. 1795–1806

Kitagawa Utamaro produced more than fifty known or attributed Yamauba-and-Kintarō prints, especially ca. 1801–1804. They transform the terrifying old mountain woman into an erotically attractive, affectionate mother nursing, bathing, feeding, writing while disciplining, or playing with a red-bodied child. A Met example, *Yamauba Playing with Kintarō*, is dated ca. 1795; Philadelphia preserves *Yamauba Nursing Kintarō*, ca. 1800.

These are not illustrations of an ancient fixed story. They are urban Edo reworkings of maternity, beauty and unruly childhood. The respectable mythic title also permitted artists to explore exposed breasts, physical intimacy and maternal bodies during tightening print censorship. [EMA]

### VII. Meiji national childhood: 1890s–1900

School and commercial print culture detach the child’s adventures from the adult warrior cycle. Iwaya Sazanami’s *Kintarō*, no. 20 of *Nihon mukashibanashi*, appeared on 23 April 1896. Ishihara Wasaburō and Tamura Torazō’s “Kintarō” in *Yōnen shōka* (1900) fixed the remembered sequence: axe, bear-riding, horsemanship, animal sumō and Ashigara. [MI]

Unlike Momotarō, Kintarō was not as systematically used as a conqueror of overseas enemies. His chief didactic uses were strength, health, bravery, loyalty and successful entry into warrior service. Modern claims that every familiar detail descends from oral antiquity ignore the demonstrable role of print and schooling.

### VIII. Mass media and political mobilization: twentieth century

Kitayama Seitarō’s animated *Kintarō* opened in 1918; the film is lost. [MI] The figure entered postcards, advertising, toys, manga and cinema.

A striking 1940–41 men’s undergarment in the Metropolitan Museum combines Kintarō carrying a bear and Momotarō imagery with the flags of Fascist Italy and Nazi Germany, anchors, helmets and other military motifs. This is direct evidence that familiar folk heroes could inhabit the visual vocabulary of Axis nationalism. It does not prove that the Kintarō legend was originally nationalist.

### IX. Heritage mascot and global franchise character: postwar to present

Ashigara municipalities promote competing “birthplace” identities through statues, festivals, signs, foods and mascots. Kintarō also appears in anime, manga, games and character naming. Examples include the lost 1918 film, the 2001 Production I.G OVA *Kai Doh Maru*, the *Fate* franchise’s blond, motorcycle-associated Sakata Kintoki, and *Record of Ragnarok*. These works selectively recombine red-dragon descent, yamauba motherhood and demon-slaying. [MI]

## 7. Modern reception and reinvention

### Art and commercial imagery

Utamaro’s intimate maternal series had enormous influence on the visual rehabilitation of Yamauba. Later artists, including Kuniyoshi and Yoshitoshi, preferred violent or spectacular Kaidōmaru/Kintoki subjects: giant carp, bears, oni and supernatural battles.

The red bib bearing 金 is now a self-identifying costume used by dolls, mascots, advertising figures and children in staged festival photographs.

### Kintarō candy

**Kintarō-ame** is a cylindrical assembled candy in which every cross-section shows the same face. Kintarō Ame Honten’s own company history says founder Kikumatsu began as a street seller in Minowa in the early Meiji period; the second-generation Ken’ichirō learned patterned-candy technique and, near the end of Meiji, produced the Kintarō face and sold it nationally with the slogan “A little Kin-san came out of the candy.” [MI/commercial]

Therefore the frequently repeated claim that face-patterned Kintarō candy is an Edo-period institution is not established by the producer’s documented history. “Kintarō-ame” later became a metaphor for standardized, identical products or people.

### Psychology and comparative mythology

Kintarō can readily be fitted to modern hero-pattern schemes: extraordinary conception, wilderness childhood, precocious strength, animal mastery, recognition and incorporation into elite society. Such fits are [MSR], not proof of diffusion from Heracles, Gilgamesh or an Indo-European prototype.

- **Rank’s birth-of-the-hero pattern:** applicable only selectively; the child is not consistently exposed, threatened by a king, or restored to royal parents.
- **Raglan’s hero pattern:** weak fit because Kintarō lacks a stable kingship, ritual death or sacrificial succession.
- **Campbell’s monomyth:** recruitment can be read as departure from the mountain into society, but the canonical child story often ends before a full quest.
- **Structuralist reading:** mountain/wilderness, yamauba/female, animal and raw strength are opposed to capital/court, male lordship, warrior discipline and social name. Recruitment converts dangerous liminal force into imperial service. This is a productive interpretation of the early-modern synthesis, not a statement made by the tales.
- **Folkloristic reading:** Torii Fumiko’s documentary work traces Kintarō’s formation through the dramatization of earlier narrative elements, then their migration into picture books and modern children’s culture. This evidential model is stronger than attempts to derive every trait from a single prehistoric deity.

### Nationalism and extremism

Kintarō’s schoolbook virtues could be integrated into modern ideals of healthy, obedient, martial boyhood. The 1940–41 Axis-pattern garment supplies concrete wartime evidence. However, no evidence found supports describing Kintarō as a unique or central symbol of contemporary Japanese extremist movements. Modern municipal branding is primarily heritage tourism. The absence of documented major present-day extremist appropriation is itself a finding.

### Famous false antiquities and conflations

- “Kintarō appears in the *Kojiki* or *Nihon shoki*”: **false**.
- “The *Konjaku* tells how a yamauba raised him”: **false**; its Kintoki is an adult retainer in a comic tale.
- “Kintarō was certainly a historical samurai born in 956”: **unproved learned/local chronology**.
- “His childhood name was historically Kintarō”: **retrospective early-modern equation**.
- “He personally beheaded Shuten-dōji in the medieval tale”: **normally false**; Yorimitsu delivers the central blow.
- “Raijin is his ancient canonical father”: **unverified later circulation**; the documented learned source gives a red dragon.
- “The grave at Shōō proves his life dates”: **false as historical method**; it proves a local commemorative tradition.
- “Kintarō-ame is securely Edo in origin”: **not supported by the manufacturer’s history**, which places the face version near the end of Meiji.

## 8. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Historicity

The case for a historical Kintoki is minimal but not empty: *Konjaku*, written roughly a century after Yorimitsu’s lifetime, names a Kintoki among his retainers and presents no supernatural biography. That may preserve social memory of a real eastern warrior.

Against certainty:

- the surname is lost;
- no contemporary court diary or administrative record securely identifies him;
- the oldest account is literary and comic;
- the standard quartet is later;
- the entire childhood is absent;
- exact birth, recruitment and death dates enter only through much later synthesis.

The defensible conclusion is: **a real retainer may underlie the name, but the evidence cannot verify Sakata Kintoki’s biography; Kintarō is unquestionably a later legendary construction.**

### Dating and textual transmission

The *Konjaku* passage is early twelfth century, not a contemporary tenth-century record. The Shuten-dōji cycle survives in multiple Ōeyama and Ibuki families; characters, shrine visits, oni origins and individual deeds shift among manuscripts and prints. “The Shuten-dōji story” is therefore not a single stable text.

The *Ōeyama ekotoba* establishes Kintoki in the demon-slaying quartet before Edo childhood literature. The 1522 *Shuten-dōji emaki*, attributed to Kanō Motonobu with prestigious calligraphers, witnesses further early-sixteenth-century visual elaboration.

### Formation of the child hero

The principal modern documentary thesis, associated especially with Torii Fumiko’s 1990 article and 2002 monograph *Kintarō no tanjō*, is that the familiar child formed by cumulative transformation:

1. Kintoki the retainer;
2. Kintoki the Shuten-dōji warrior;
3. Kintoki as father in Kinpira-jōruri;
4. Ashigara/yamauba recruitment;
5. Kaidōmaru’s animal strength in *Komochi Yamauba*;
6. picture-book and ukiyo-e standardization;
7. Meiji children’s canon.

This model is supported by datable textual change. It does not require either a wholly invented Edo figure or a perfectly preserved prehistoric oral archetype.

### Yamauba interpretation

Scholars variously read Yamauba as dangerous mountain other, abandoned or marginal woman, fertility/maternal power, performer’s transformation, or embodiment of the mountain outside settled order. Foster’s work cautions that yōkai categories are historically made and remade across oral narrative, theater, visual art and commerce. Reider’s study of Shuten-dōji similarly treats oni narratives as changing ideological and textual formations. Neither supports collapsing all mountain women into one ancient goddess.

### Local evidence and tourism

Oyama, Minamiashigara, Hakone, Ōmi and Shōō preserve meaningful local traditions. Their stones, waterfalls, graves and shrines are evidence for reception and localization. They cannot be dated merely by the age of the natural feature or by a legend’s claimed Heian year. Much of the current public chronology was invigorated by twentieth-century local research, education and tourism—for example, Oyama credits Furumi Kazuo’s 1931 *Study of Sakata Kintoki* and Fukaya Hakudō’s 1932 *Kintoki ga kataru* with its modern birthplace campaign.

### State of the evidence

- No authenticated autograph or contemporary Kintoki document survives.
- The earliest surname is missing because of a manuscript lacuna.
- Medieval picture scrolls provide named, visual narrative evidence but are already centuries after Yorimitsu.
- Many Edo texts survive in rare printed or manuscript copies requiring recension-specific study.
- The 1918 animated film is lost.
- Ukiyo-e documentation is unusually rich, allowing individual stages of the visual type to be dated.
- Modern municipal and commercial websites are useful evidence for living reception, but not independent verification of Heian events.
