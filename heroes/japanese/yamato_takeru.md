# Yamato Takeru: Research Dossier

> **Traditional class:** mortal imperial prince and conquest hero; after death, a transformed/heroized dead and shrine *kami*. Some texts grant him quasi-imperial or divine status, but neither early court chronicle says that he reigned.
>
> **Earliest secure attestation:** the substantially contemporary cluster formed by the *Kojiki* (古事記, presented in 712), *Nihon shoki* (日本書紀, completed in 720), and the early-eighth-century provincial *fudoki*, especially the *Hitachi no kuni fudoki* (常陸国風土記, conventionally dated c. 714–718). No securely identified inscription, tomb object, or image naming him predates these texts. The earliest physical manuscripts are much later: the oldest complete *Kojiki* witness is the Shinpukuji manuscript of 1371–1372, while the earliest surviving *Nihon shoki* manuscript is a ninth-century fragment of Book X—not the Yamato Takeru narrative in Book VII. [Kokugakuin University](https://jmapps.ne.jp/kokugakuin/det.html?data_id=78816); [Nara National Museum](https://www.narahaku.go.jp/english/collection/p-1190-0.html)

## Evidentiary key

Every substantive item below is marked by one of these categories:

- **[E1: securely attested early material]** Eighth-century text or demonstrably ancient material.
- **[E2: early variant/local tradition]** Eighth-century provincial or alternate textual form.
- **[C: later canonical elaboration]** A court, shrine, liturgical, or learned formulation later accepted as canonical.
- **[M: medieval/early-modern accretion]** Material first documented after the ancient corpus.
- **[F: folklore/regional tradition]** Local tradition whose antiquity cannot be established from its present record.
- **[R: modern scholarly reconstruction]** A modern hypothesis rather than a fact narrated by the sources.
- **[I: modern invention/reinvention]** Modern literature, ideology, art, entertainment, or pseudo-ancient material.

Dates assigned inside the chronicles—97, 110, 113/114 CE and the like—belong to their retrospective chronology. They are not independent contemporary dates for the hero.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

Yamato Takeru is the violent younger prince Ousu, son of the legendary Emperor Keikō, who acquires the name “Takeru of Yamato” from a dying Kumaso opponent. The *Kojiki* makes him a feared and increasingly isolated son repeatedly dispatched by his father; the *Nihon shoki* recasts him as a willing imperial agent, “a god in human form,” whose conquests legitimate Yamato rule over western and eastern peoples. He survives death as a white bird and as the *kami* of later shrines. The figure is therefore at once conquest hero, tragic exile, ancestor of Chūai’s imperial line, and ideological image of territorial incorporation. [E1] [Kojiki excerpts](https://orias.berkeley.edu/yamato-text-excerpts); [Nihon shoki, Book VII](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Nihongi/Book_VII)

### Names

- **倭男具那命 / 倭男具那王 — Yamato Oguna no Mikoto / Yamato Oguna no Miko:** an early personal designation in the *Kojiki*, literally something like “Yamato young man/boy.” [E1]
- **日本童男尊 / 日本童男 — Yamato Oguna no Mikoto / Yamato Oguna:** corresponding *Nihon shoki* spellings. [E1]
- **小碓命 / 小碓尊 — Ousu no Mikoto:** “Prince Little Mortar,” paired with the elder/twin Ōusu, “Great Mortar.” The literal sense is clear, but no securely established historical explanation of the pair’s names exists. [E1]
- **倭建命 — Yamato Takeru no Mikoto:** the *Kojiki* spelling. 倭 denotes Yamato/Japan; 建 here represents *takeru*, “brave, fierce, martial one.” [E1]
- **日本武尊 — Yamato Takeru no Mikoto:** the *Nihon shoki* spelling, using 武, “martial valor.” The honorific 尊 is especially elevated. [E1]
- **日本武皇子 — Yamato Takeru no Miko:** “Prince Yamato Takeru,” used in the *Nihon shoki*. [E1]
- **倭武天皇 / Yamato Takeru no Sumeramikoto:** “Emperor Yamato Takeru,” the striking title in the *Hitachi fudoki*. It is a local textual representation, not evidence that he historically reigned. [E2] [Kokugakuin’s *Fudoki* account](https://jmapps.ne.jp/kokugakuin/det.html?data_id=78822)
- **白鳥 / shiratori; 白千鳥 / shirochidori:** “white bird” and, in the *Kojiki*, more specifically a great white shorebird or plover-like bird. These are postmortem forms, not personal names. [E1]

The chronicles themselves supply the name etiology: the defeated Kumaso/Kawakami Takeru declares that no one in the west equals the prince and transfers the title *takeru* to him. In the *Kojiki* two Kumaso brothers confer it; in the *Nihon shoki* a single chieftain, Torishikaya/Kawakami Takeru, does so. The scene is literary charter, not independent proof of etymology. [E1]

### Status and later identifications

The *Nihon shoki* has Keikō call him outwardly his child but “in reality a God,” while the *Hitachi fudoki* calls him emperor. The *Kojiki* calls Ototachibana his *kisaki*, an imperial consort-title, and uses elevated death language. These usages demonstrate exceptional textual status, not an otherwise lost reign. [E1/E2] [Encyclopedia of Shinto](https://d-museum.kokugakuin.ac.jp/eos/detail/id=9726)

No ancient source identifies him as Susanoo, Ōkuninushi, or another god. Comparisons with Susanoo—the violent younger son, possessor of a sword associated with Kusanagi—and with Ōkuninushi are modern comparative interpretations. They are not ancient syncretistic equations. [R] [Kanda Norishiro’s comparison](https://glim-re.repo.nii.ac.jp/record/2388/files/gakushuinjyoudai_4_18_23.pdf)

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### The ancient corpus

1. **The *Kojiki*, middle book, 712.** Written in a mixed use of Chinese characters to represent Japanese language and meaning; attributed in its preface to Ō no Yasumaro’s transcription of Hieda no Are’s recitation. Yamato Takeru’s cycle includes the brother killing, Kumaso disguise, Izumo deception, eastern campaign, Kusanagi, burning field, Ototachibana, Ashigara, Miyazu, Ibuki, death songs, and white-bird transformation. Its coherent tragic design is the fullest literary form of the legend. [E1]  
   The autograph is lost. The oldest complete witness, copied by the monk Ken’yū in 1371–1372, is separated from the asserted compilation by roughly 660 years. [Shinpukuji manuscript record](https://www.norinagakinenkan.com/pages/197/)

2. **The *Nihon shoki*, Book VII, 720.** An imperially commissioned history in literary Chinese. Its annalistic Keikō chronology gives the prince’s Kumaso expedition to the traditional year 97 and eastern expedition to 110. Unlike the *Kojiki*, it first gives Keikō an extensive Kyushu campaign; Yamato Takeru then suppresses a Kumaso resurgence and volunteers for the east. The narrative emphasizes imperial command, the emperor’s spiritual power, submission, administrative incorporation, captives, and hereditary service groups. [E1] [Book VII](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Nihongi/Book_VII)  
   The original is lost. A ninth-century Book X fragment is the oldest surviving copy of any portion; the oldest complete thirty-book copy is early modern. [National Archives of Japan](https://www.archives.go.jp/exhibition/digital/rekishitomonogatari/contents/02.html)

3. **The *Hitachi no kuni fudoki*, c. 714–718.** A provincial gazetteer preserving numerous local place-name traditions. Its Yamato Takeru is an “emperor” who travels, observes terrain, names places, hunts, visits springs, and behaves as a beneficent local sovereign. It does not simply reproduce the tragic *Kojiki* hero or the subordinate *Nihon shoki* commander. [E2] [study of the variant](https://www.kci.go.kr/kciportal/ci/sereArticleSearch/ciSereArtiView.kci?sereArticleSearchBean.artiId=ART002375501)

4. **Other *fudoki*.** Yamato Takeru travel traditions occur fragmentarily outside Hitachi; Hizen preserves a journey tradition, whereas Bungo and Hizen more often assign Kyushu inspection and conquest to Keikō. These local records show competition between a royal father’s itinerary and his son’s expanding itinerary. [E2]

5. **The *Kogo shūi* (古語拾遺), 807.** This Inbe-clan memorial discusses sacred regalia and ritual traditions but adds no comparably full independent Yamato Takeru cycle. Its relevance lies chiefly in the developing authoritative history of Kusanagi and court ritual. [C]

6. **The *Engishiki*, compiled 905–927.** It records the institutional shrine world within which Takebe and other cult sites were classified. It is evidence for Heian-period cult administration, not for the prince’s second-century existence. [C]

7. **Medieval chronicles, shrine origin narratives, commentaries, and war tales.** They transmit and reinterpret the ancient account, but Yamato Takeru is not a central actor in the *Heike monogatari*, *Gikeiki*, *Nihon ryōiki*, *Konjaku monogatari*, or the Momotarō/Urashima/Kintarō *otogizōshi* cycles named in the comparative brief. Those corpora belong to other heroes. Their silence is important: Yamato Takeru’s principal textual base remains the *Kiki–fudoki* complex and shrine tradition, not medieval warrior biography.

8. **Motoori Norinaga, *Kojiki-den*, 1790–1822 publication.** Norinaga’s philological recovery of the *Kojiki* helped elevate its Yamato Takeru from one chronicle figure into a privileged embodiment of ancient Japanese emotion and language. His work belongs to early-modern *kokugaku*, not ancient testimony. [M]

### What is not attested

There is no securely contemporary inscription naming Yamato Takeru; no archaeologically identified weapon belonging to him; no securely excavated grave inscription; and no independent second- to fifth-century record of his campaigns. Consequently, the eighth-century texts establish that the figure and cycle were documented by that time—not that the events occurred on the chronicles’ traditional dates.

## 3. The story: episodes and variants

### Parentage and the brother killing

**The *Kojiki* version [E1].** Ousu and Ōusu are sons of Keikō and Inabi no Ōiratsume of Harima. When Ōusu stops attending the emperor’s morning and evening meals, Keikō tells Ousu to “teach” him his duty. Ousu reports that he seized his brother in the privy, crushed him, tore off his limbs, wrapped them in matting, and threw them away. Keikō, frightened by the youth’s ferocity, sends him west. The killing is explicit fratricide and dismemberment. [Kojiki text](https://orias.berkeley.edu/yamato-text-excerpts)

**The *Nihon shoki* version [E1].** It omits the murder. Ōusu remains alive, later hides rather than accept the eastern commission, and receives Mino as his domain. Yamato Takeru then volunteers. This is not a minor omission: it converts a feared, disposable son into the loyal hero of imperial expansion. [Book VII](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Nihongi/Book_VII)

### Kumaso and the woman’s disguise

**The *Kojiki* [E1].** Still youthful, Ousu borrows his aunt Yamatohime’s upper garment and skirt, lets down his hair, hides a sword against his body, and enters the feast of two Kumaso Takeru brothers as an attractive young woman. The brothers seat “her” between them. He stabs the elder through the chest and pursues the younger, driving the sword through his buttocks before ripping him open “like a ripe melon.” The younger confers the name Yamato Takeru. This is assassination through gender disguise and erotic deception, followed by spectacular violence.

**The *Nihon shoki* [E1].** At sixteen, accompanied by named warriors, he infiltrates the feast of one chief, Torishikaya or Kawakami Takeru. The intoxicated chief takes the disguised youth by the hand, seats him beside himself, and offers him drink. The prince stabs him in the chest; the dying chief confers the title. The prince’s men then kill the whole band, “leaving not a chewer.” The text thus combines deceptive killing with collective extermination. [Book VII, traditional year 97](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Nihongi/Book_VII)

No early source calls this a consensual seduction. Modern children’s retellings often suppress the buttock wound, dismemberment, sexual danger, and extermination. That sanitization is modern reception. [I]

### Izumo Takeru

Only the *Kojiki* gives this full episode. Yamato Takeru befriends Izumo Takeru, secretly makes a wooden imitation sword, persuades him to exchange swords after bathing in the Hi River, then challenges and kills him when the false blade cannot be drawn. He sings mockingly about the bladeless sword. [E1] The episode doubles the Kumaso naming structure but makes Yamato Takeru the betrayer of sworn friendship. The *Nihon shoki* omits it.

### Appointment to the eastern campaign

**The *Kojiki* [E1].** Immediately after returning, he is sent without an army to subdue the “unsubmissive people” and “savage deities” of the twelve eastern roads. He weeps to Yamatohime at Ise that his father must want him dead. She gives him the Grass-Quelling Sword and a bag to open in emergency.

**The *Nihon shoki* [E1].** Ministers hesitate; Ōusu refuses; Yamato Takeru volunteers. Keikō gives him a battle-axe and praises him as a divinely sent savior of the monarchy. The prince attributes success to the imperial spirit and promises persuasion first, force second. Yamatohime gives Kusanagi but no emergency bag in the main text. This is the clearest ideological divergence between the chronicles.

### Kusanagi and the burning field

The sword’s earlier divine story lies outside Yamato Takeru’s cycle: Susanoo finds the sword Ama-no-Murakumo in the tail of the slain Yamata no Orochi and presents it to Amaterasu. It reaches Yamatohime and Ise before she gives it to the prince. [E1: mythic genealogy of the object]

**The *Kojiki* [E1].** A ruler of Sagamu lures him into a moor and sets the grass on fire. Yamato Takeru opens Yamatohime’s bag, finds a fire-striker, cuts the grass with the sword, starts a counter-fire, escapes, and exterminates and burns the rulers. The text already calls the weapon the “Grass-Quelling Sword”; it does not derive the name here.

**The *Nihon shoki* main account [E1].** Suruga brigands lure him with a report of abundant deer and burn the moor. He uses a fire-drill to set a counter-fire, escapes, and burns the band. The place becomes Yaketsu.

**The *Nihon shoki* alternate account [E1 variant].** The sword Ama-no-Murakumo moves by itself and mows the grass; therefore it is renamed Kusanagi, “Grass-Mower.” This famous supernatural action belongs explicitly to an alternate version, not the main annal. [Book VII](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Nihongi/Book_VII)

The frequent claim that Yamato Takeru indisputably renamed the sword after personally mowing grass harmonizes these versions and should be avoided.

### Ototachibana’s sea sacrifice

**The *Kojiki* [E1].** At Hashirimizu a crossing deity raises waves. Ototachibana, called Yamato Takeru’s *kisaki*, offers herself in his place so he can complete the imperial mission. She spreads eight layers each of sedge mats, hides, and silk over the water, sits upon them, and sinks. The sea becomes calm. Seven days later her comb washes ashore and is enclosed in a tomb. Her song recalls the prince’s concern for her during the Sagamu fire.

**The *Nihon shoki* [E1].** After Yamato Takeru boasts that the sea is so small he could jump across, a storm strikes. Ototachibana is called a concubine and daughter of Oshiyama no Sukune. She diagnoses the Sea God’s will and plunges directly into the waves as a ransom for his life. The storm stops. The text does not give the layered mats, comb tomb, or her fire-field poem here. [Book VII](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Nihongi/Book_VII)

Calling this episode human sacrifice is accurate at the narrative level: a woman offers her life to appease a deity and obtain safe passage. Whether any historical rite lies behind it is unknown.

### The east, captives, and “Azuma”

In the *Nihon shoki*, Emishi chiefs submit after seeing the prince’s magnificent mirrored ship; he pardons them, but takes their chiefs as captives and attendants. Later they are presented to Ise, removed for disorder, resettled, and made ancestors of Saeki service groups. [E1] The passage is an imperial ethnography of allegedly uncivilized eastern peoples, not neutral description.

Both chronicles connect his cry *Azuma wa ya*—“Ah, my wife!”—with the name Azuma, eastern Japan. In the *Kojiki* the cry occurs at Ashigara after he kills a white-deer-shaped pass deity with a piece of wild chive. In the *Nihon shoki* it occurs at Usui Pass as he looks southeast and remembers Ototachibana. These are competing place-name etiologies. [E1]

### Mountain gods and death

**The *Kojiki* [E1].** After leaving Kusanagi with Miyazu in Owari, Yamato Takeru boasts that he will seize the god of Mount Ibuki bare-handed. A huge white boar appears. He assumes it is merely the god’s messenger and postpones killing it, but it is the god itself. Because he “raises words”—a boastful verbal declaration—the god sends heavy icy rain that confounds and weakens him. No text says straightforwardly that the god administers literal poison; “poison” is a later paraphrase of the divine affliction. He briefly revives at a spring, then progressively loses the use of his legs. Place-names explain his stages of collapse. At Nobono he sings of Yamato, homecoming clouds, and the sword left beside Miyazu, then dies.

**The *Nihon shoki* [E1].** The Ibuki god appears as a giant serpent. The prince mistakes it for a messenger, strides over it, and is struck by cloud, ice rain, mist, and illness. His death speech emphasizes grief that he cannot return to serve the emperor; Kibi no Takehiko transmits it. The father sincerely mourns him. The tragic father–son alienation of the *Kojiki* is absent.

The *Nihon shoki* separately gives a white-deer mountain god in Shinano; a white dog guides the lost prince out. Modern retellings sometimes fuse the Shinano deer, Ibuki boar, and Ibuki serpent.

### Corpse, tombs, and white bird

**The *Kojiki* [E1].** Family members build a tomb at Nobono. The dead prince becomes an enormous white *chidori*, flies to the shore, and is pursued through fields and sea while mourners sing four funeral songs. The bird stops at Shiki in Kawachi; another tomb is made, but it flies away to heaven. The text says the songs continued in imperial funerals.

**The *Nihon shoki* [E1].** Officials bury him at Nobono. A white bird flies from the tomb; the coffin contains only clothing. It stops at Kotobiki Plain in Yamato and then Furuchi in Kawachi, and tombs are made at all three places. Only clothes and official cap are deposited. Keikō creates the Takeru-be service group to perpetuate his fame. [Book VII](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Nihongi/Book_VII)

Thus there are two tombs in the *Kojiki* story but three in the *Nihon shoki*. Neither account licenses identifying every later “white bird tomb” as an actual burial.

## 4. Relations with gods and other heroes

### Genealogies

**Shared central genealogy [E1]:**

Amaterasu → imperial divine descent → Keikō + Inabi no Ōiratsume → Ōusu and Ousu/Yamato Takeru → Chūai → imperial succession through the lineage associated with Jingū and Ōjin.

The divine ancestry belongs to the chronicles’ imperial genealogy; it is not a historically demonstrated family tree.

**Major *Nihon shoki* consorts and children [E1]:**

- Futaji-irihime, imperial princess: Ineyoriwake, Emperor Chūai, Nuno-oshi-irihime, Wakatake.
- Kibi no Anato no Takehime, daughter of Kibi no Takehiko: Takemiko and Towokiwake.
- Ototachibanahime, daughter of Oshiyama no Sukune: Wakatakehiko.
- Miyazuhime: wife at Owari in the narrative; no child is assigned to her in this list.

The *Kojiki* genealogy has its own expanded set of unions and descendants tied to regional lineages. The lists function as charters for houses and service groups and should not be merged into a single biographical family.

### Every named divine or heroic interaction in the principal early corpus

- **Emperor Keikō:** father and commander; fears and repeatedly dispatches him in the *Kojiki*, praises and commissions him in the *Nihon shoki*. *Kojiki* 712; *Nihon shoki* 720. **[E1]**
- **Ōusu no Mikoto:** elder/twin brother; dismembered by Ousu in the *Kojiki*, remains alive and refuses the eastern command in the *Nihon shoki*. 712/720. **[E1, conflict]**
- **Yamatohime no Mikoto:** paternal aunt and Ise priestess; lends women’s clothing before the Kumaso killing in the *Kojiki* and gives Kusanagi before the eastward expedition in both chronicles. 712/720. **[E1]**
- **Kumaso Takeru, elder brother:** enthralled by the disguised prince and stabbed at the feast. *Kojiki*, 712. **[E1]**
- **Kumaso Takeru, younger brother:** pursued, stabbed through the buttocks, confers the name Yamato Takeru, then is ripped open. *Kojiki*, 712. **[E1]**
- **Torishikaya/Kawakami Takeru:** sole Kumaso chieftain in the *Nihon shoki*; drinks with the disguised prince, is stabbed, and bestows the title. 720. **[E1 variant]**
- **Izumo Takeru:** made a friend, deceived with a wooden sword, challenged, and killed. *Kojiki*, 712. **[E1; absent from *Nihon shoki*]**
- **Amaterasu Ōmikami:** no direct conversation; her Ise sanctuary is worshiped, and her transmitted sword reaches him through Yamatohime. *Kojiki* 712; *Nihon shoki* 720. **[E1, indirect]**
- **Susanoo no Mikoto:** no personal meeting; Susanoo is the sword’s earlier finder and donor in the divine-age narrative. *Kojiki* 712; *Nihon shoki* 720. **[E1, object-chain only]**
- **Yamata no Orochi:** no interaction with Yamato Takeru; the sword later given to him was found in Orochi’s tail. *Kojiki* 712; *Nihon shoki* 720. **[E1, indirect; famous pairing is not a meeting]**
- **Miyazuhime:** pledged bride and later wife; he leaves Kusanagi at her dwelling before Ibuki. *Kojiki* 712; *Nihon shoki* 720. **[E1]**
- **Ototachibanahime:** consort/concubine who gives her life to calm the sea and save him. *Kojiki* 712; *Nihon shoki* 720. **[E1]**
- **Deity of the Hashirimizu crossing / Sea God:** raises the storm; Ototachibana is sacrificed in Yamato Takeru’s place. *Kojiki* 712; *Nihon shoki* 720. **[E1]**
- **Deity of Ashigara Pass:** appears as a white deer and is killed with wild chive. *Kojiki*, 712. **[E1]**
- **Mountain god of Shinano:** appears as a white deer, is blinded/killed with garlic; its disturbance causes the prince to lose his way. *Nihon shoki*, 720. **[E1 variant]**
- **White dog of Shinano:** spontaneously guides him from the mountain. *Nihon shoki*, 720. Its divine status is implied by function but not expressly named. **[E1; classification uncertain]**
- **God of Mount Ibuki:** appears as the god itself in boar form in the *Kojiki* but as a serpent mistaken for its messenger in the *Nihon shoki*; sends icy rain and mortal illness. 712/720. **[E1, conflict]**
- **Malignant deity of Ana Ferry:** killed during the western return. *Nihon shoki*, 720. **[E1]**
- **Malignant deity of Kashiha Ferry:** killed for afflicting travelers with poisonous vapor. *Nihon shoki*, 720. **[E1]**
- **Kibi no Takehiko:** companion and messenger who reconnoiters Koshi and reports the hero’s last words. *Nihon shoki*, 720. **[E1]**
- **Ōtomo no Takehi:** eastern-expedition companion and recipient of the Yuki-be. *Nihon shoki*, 720. **[E1]**
- **Misukitomomimitake:** Kibi prince assigned as companion in the *Kojiki*. 712. **[E1]**
- **Emperor Chūai:** son and genealogical successor; no surviving principal narrative scene of personal interaction with his father. *Kojiki* 712; *Nihon shoki* 720. **[E1; genealogy only]**
- **Ōnamuchi/Ōkuninushi:** co-enshrined at Takebe Taisha only after the shrine’s later relocation tradition; no ancient narrative meeting. **[C, cultic juxtaposition]** [Takebe Taisha](https://takebetaisha.jp/about/)
- **Kunitokotachi:** later co-enshrined with Yamato Takeru and Ototachibana at Ōtori Jinja; no narrative interaction. **[C]** [Ōtori shrine history](https://www.ootorijinja.or.jp/%E7%94%B1%E7%B7%92/)

## 5. Cult, tomb, relic, and ritual

### Kusanagi and Atsuta

The *Nihon shoki* says explicitly that Kusanagi, formerly worn by Yamato Takeru, was “now” at Atsuta in Owari. This is the earliest textual connection between the hero, sword, and Atsuta. [E1] Atsuta Shrine’s present tradition says Miyazuhime enshrined the sword there after his death and lists Yamato Takeru, Miyazuhime, and Takeinadanemikoto among its associated deities. [C] [Atsuta Jingū](https://www.atsutajingu.or.jp/jingu/about/enshrined.html)

The sword venerated at Atsuta is a sacred regalia object, not an archaeologically inspected and dated weapon proven to have belonged to an individual Yamato Takeru.

### Takebe Taisha

Takebe Taisha in Ōmi venerates Yamato Takeru as principal deity. Shrine tradition assigns its first establishment to Keikō’s reign and relocation to Seta in 676, but the safely datable evidence is the shrine’s historical and *Engishiki* status, not the legendary CE 116 foundation. [C] The annual Senkōsai/Funakōsai on 17 August sends a portable shrine on the Seta River in a fleet, interpreted as reenacting the hero’s sea campaign. The boat procession was revived in 1915, so its present form must not be projected intact into antiquity. [C/I] [Japan Heritage](https://japan-heritage.bunka.go.jp/ja/culturalproperties/result/6883/); [Ōtsu historical plan](https://www.city.otsu.lg.jp/material/files/group/16/rekimachikeikakusyo_syusei_r7.pdf)

### Ōtori/Ōtori faith

Ōtori Jinja and Ōtori Taisha traditions say the hero’s spirit appeared or landed as a white bird and was worshiped as Tori Myōjin. This is a cultic localization of the chronicle transformation. Sakai City notes an alternative interpretation: the shrine may originally have worshiped the ancestor of the Ōtori lineage. [C/R] [Sakai cultural-property record](https://www.city.sakai.lg.jp/kanko/rekishi/bunkazai/bunkazai/shokai/bunya/kenzobutu/ootoritaisya.html)

### The “three tombs”

The *Nihon shoki* supplies three literary tombs: Nobono in Ise, Kotobiki in Yamato, and Furuchi in Kawachi. The *Kojiki* supplies Nobono and Shiki in Kawachi. These are part of the white-bird narrative before they are archaeological identifications. [E1]

- **Nobono Ōtsuka Kofun, Kameyama:** designated by the Imperial Household in 1879 as Yamato Takeru’s Nobono tomb. The designation is Meiji-era administrative attribution, not an excavated identification. [C/I] [Kameyama Historical Museum](https://kameyamarekihaku.jp/content/21theme/zone02/shousai2.html)
- **Shiratori-ryō/Lightly Maenoyama Kofun, Habikino:** presently designated as his Kawachi white-bird mausoleum. Its mound belongs to the Middle Kofun period and forms part of a much later monumental landscape than the chronicles’ traditional first-century dates. Literary and archaeological identities therefore do not coincide automatically. [C/R] [Habikino City](https://www.city.habikino.lg.jp/soshiki/shougaigakushu/bunka-sekai/bunkazainikansurukoto/bunkazai_shokai/kofun_chuki/2379.html)
- **Kotobiki Plain:** a literary second stopping place in the *Nihon shoki*; there is no securely excavated, inscribed tomb establishing Yamato Takeru’s occupancy. **Absence of evidence is the finding.**

### Funerary ritual

The *Kojiki* says the four songs sung while mourners pursue the white bird continued to be performed at imperial great funerals. This is early evidence for compilers connecting the hero’s lament cycle with court mortuary performance. It is not proof that the songs originated at a historical funeral of Yamato Takeru. [E1]

### Geographic cult and folklore

Shrines and place traditions along putative eastern routes—Yaizu, Hashirimizu, Ashigara, Usui, Shinano, Owari, Ibuki, and Nobono—localize episodes and often claim foundation by the prince. The *Hitachi fudoki* proves that this localization process was already active in the eighth century; individual modern shrine-foundation dates require separate evidence. [E2/F]

A documented twentieth-century Mie–Gifu–Shiga tradition says that when a coffin was opened, separate white birds flew toward Yamato and Atsuta. That is folklore collected in the modern period, not an omitted ancient chronicle passage. [F] [Nichibunken folklore database](https://www.nichibun.ac.jp/YoukaiCard/1232892.html)

## 6. Historical evolution

### Before 712: hypothetical formation

No extant text fixes the legend’s beginnings. Scholars commonly infer that the written cycle combined:

- court genealogies;
- conquest traditions associated with Yamato expansion;
- regional route and place-name tales;
- funeral songs;
- sword-regalia tradition;
- heroic disguise and deception tales;
- the white-bird soul motif.

This decomposition is a reconstruction, not an excavated oral original. [R]

### 712–720: divergent canonical shaping

The *Kojiki* fashions an emotionally isolated, dangerous prince whose strength makes his father fear him and whose final songs turn conquest into tragic longing. The *Nihon shoki* suppresses the fratricide, supplies imperial speeches, ethnographic descriptions of Kumaso and Emishi, captives, service groups, and loyal last words. The first centers estrangement and lament; the second centers monarchy and incorporation. [E1]

### Early eighth-century provincial localization

The *Hitachi fudoki* makes him a ruler who tours, surveys, names, and improves the landscape. This is neither merely “more historical” nor simply corrupt: it documents how provincial elites inserted a mobile imperial hero into local geography. [E2]

### Heian institutionalization

Shrine worship, Kusanagi’s status at Atsuta, Takebe’s place in the shrine system, genealogical claims, and court commentary increasingly turned the narrative hero into a protective *kami* and ancestor. The *Engishiki* world is evidence for institutional cult centuries after the story’s alleged events. [C]

### Medieval transformation

Shrine-temple complexes, pilgrimage, local foundation legends, genealogies, and performance sustained the figure, but medieval warrior literature did not make him a protagonist on the scale of Yoshitsune or Benkei. Medieval reception was geographically dispersed and cultic rather than dominated by one new epic. [M]

### Early-modern philology and visual culture

*Kokugaku* scholars, especially Motoori Norinaga, privileged the *Kojiki*’s language and emotional tragic hero. Printed commentaries and illustrated books made the cycle more widely available. Edo and Meiji artists depicted the woman-disguise assassination, burning field, Ototachibana’s sacrifice, Ibuki, and the white bird. [M]

### Meiji to 1945: imperial hero

The modern state’s school history and State Shinto framework could present Yamato Takeru as an early agent of national unification and model of sacrificial obedience. That image preferentially resembles the *Nihon shoki*’s loyal commander, though the *Kojiki* supplied emotional scenes. The description of Kumaso and Emishi as rebels or “barbarians” mapped neatly onto a national narrative of center conquering periphery. [I]

Meiji mausoleum designations materially fixed uncertain story geography: Nobono was officially assigned in 1879; the Kawachi tumulus was similarly integrated into imperial commemoration. These acts were state historicization, not archaeological discoveries. [I]

The “Meiji Memorial” Yamato Takeru statue at Kenrokuen, erected to honor soldiers killed in the 1877 Satsuma Rebellion, is frequently described as an early modern Japanese bronze monument. It converts a legendary conqueror into a figure of military commemoration. [I] [research report](https://kaken.nii.ac.jp/ja/file/KAKENHI-PROJECT-17K02425/17K02425seika.pdf)

### Post-1945 to present

Postwar scholarship sharply separated chronicle narrative from verifiable political history, while literature, manga, tourism, shrine festivals, kabuki, film, anime, and games continued to use the hero. Recent school materials often pair the *Kojiki* and *Nihon shoki* to teach textual comparison rather than treating the campaigns as literal history. [R/I] [modern textbook study](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/nihonbungaku/64/1/64_2/_article/-char/ja/)

## 7. Modern reception and reinvention

### Literature and scholarship-inflected fiction

- **Ivan Morris, *The Nobility of Failure* (1975):** casts Yamato Takeru as the first in a series of admired tragic failures. This influential English-language framing privileges the *Kojiki*’s doomed hero. [I/R]
- **Kuroiwa Jūgo, *Shiratori no Ōji Yamato Takeru* series (from 1990):** historical fiction reconstructing the legend within an imagined ancient political world. [I] [Chiba library bibliography](https://www.library.city.chiba.jp/news/pdf/20160813hanamigawadanchi_kikaku_list.pdf)
- **Yasuhiko Yoshikazu, *Yamato Takeru* manga:** reframes the campaigns as conflict among historically imagined polities; later collected in bunkobon editions. [I] [publisher record](https://www.chuko.co.jp/bunko/2019/10/206794.html)

### Theatre and performance

- **Umehara Takeshi and Ichikawa Ennosuke III, *Yamato Takeru*, 1986:** the first “Super Kabuki,” premiered at Shinbashi Enbujō on 4 February 1986. It transforms the ancient materials into spectacular modern drama and makes the hero a compassionate opponent of oppressive power. It is not a traditional medieval kabuki script. [I] [National Theatre](https://www2.ntj.jac.go.jp/dglib/modules/kabuki_dic/entry.php?entryid=1329); [Kabuki repertory guide](https://enmokudb.kabuki.ne.jp/repertoire_en/4443/)
- **Regional kagura:** modern performance traditions dramatize the Kumaso disguise and name transfer. Their scripts are living regional theatre, not independent ancient witnesses. [F/I] [Hiroshima Kagura](https://www.rccbc.co.jp/event/kagura/en/programs/yamato-takeru-no-mikoto.html)
- There is no major securely medieval canonical Noh play entitled *Yamato Takeru* comparable in repertory status to *Atsumori* or *Yashima*. Later shrine *shin-nō*, new Noh, and local performance titles should not be backdated. **Absence of evidence is material here.**

### Film, animation, comics, and games

- **Toho film *Yamato Takeru* / *Orochi, the Eight-Headed Dragon* (1994):** freely combines Yamato Takeru with Susanoo, Tsukuyomi, and Orochi into fantasy cinema. A climactic personal conflict with Tsukuyomi is a modern invention. [I]
- **Television anime *Yamato Takeru* (1994, 37 episodes):** science-fiction series set in the twenty-fifth century, featuring giant robot Susanoo and antagonist Tsukuyomi. It borrows names rather than retelling the chronicles. [I] [Nippon Animation](https://www.nippon-animation.co.jp/work/1692/)
- **Stan Sakai, *Usagi Yojimbo: Grasscutter* (1997–1998):** incorporates the Kusanagi transmission into a modern comics narrative. [I]
- **Fate/Samurai Remnant (2023):** identifies the Saber-class servant as Yamato Takeru and reworks Kusanagi and the hero’s gender presentation within the *Fate* franchise. The chronicle disguise does not make the ancient figure canonically female or nonbinary; the game’s presentation is modern creative interpretation. [I] [official game page](https://www.koeitecmoamerica.com/games/fate-samurai-remnant/)

### Political appropriation

The imperial-state reading treated western and eastern campaigns as primordial national unification, naturalized Yamato sovereignty, and represented resistance as rebellion against an already legitimate center. Modern nationalist retellings sometimes continue to cite the chronicles’ retrospective CE dates as straightforward history. That is methodologically unsound: the dates derive from the eighth-century regnal scheme.

Conversely, the simple claim that the story is nothing but Meiji propaganda is also false. The documented narrative is eighth-century; what is modern is its conversion into linear national history, its monumental fixing, and selective emphasis on filial obedience and territorial unity.

No specific contemporary extremist organization can responsibly be assigned a Yamato Takeru cult without documented evidence. Broad assertions that “the far right worships Yamato Takeru” exceed the evidence reviewed here.

## 8. Historiography and contested material

### Historicity

There is no independent proof that a single historical prince performed the western and eastern circuits. Modern historians commonly treat the figure as legendary or composite. Proposed historical kernels include memories of fourth- or fifth-century Yamato expansion, multiple royal commanders, lineage traditions, and local conquest leaders. [R]

A recurrent proposal connects Yamato Takeru with **Wakatakeru**, the royal name inscribed on the fifth-century Inariyama and Eta Funayama swords and commonly associated with Emperor Yūryaku. The similarity of *takeru* is real, but it does not identify the two figures: *takeru* is a title/name element, the chronology differs, and no inscription names Yamato Takeru or recounts his cycle. [R] The debate is catalogued as “Yamato Takeru and Great King Wakatakeru,” not settled identity. [National Institute of Japanese Literature index](https://ronbun.nijl.ac.jp/kokubun/00237982)

Postwar claims that he is entirely invented and claims that archaeology has “proved” his campaigns are both stronger than the evidence permits. The defensible conclusion is narrower: the written hero is securely attested in the early eighth century; possible earlier political memories cannot presently be isolated with confidence.

### The route as historical memory

Some scholars read the geographic route as preserving stages of Yamato penetration into eastern Japan. Others emphasize that it is a literary chain of place-name etymologies and cult localizations assembled from heterogeneous traditions. Archaeology documents changing political connections and keyhole-tomb distribution, but it does not label those processes as Yamato Takeru’s personal expedition. [R]

### Kojiki priority and literary form

It is often said that the *Kojiki* preserves the “older” version. Individual motifs may indeed be older, but the extant *Kojiki*’s tragic unity may also reflect its own sophisticated compilation. The *Nihon shoki* is not simply a later copy: it preserves alternate accounts and independent political reshaping. Textual comparison must proceed episode by episode. [R] [University of Tokyo study summary](https://www.l.u-tokyo.ac.jp/postgraduate/database/2020/12393.html)

### Funeral songs

The death-cycle songs may predate their prose setting, and scholars have treated them as an old lament complex later attached to the hero. That remains literary reconstruction. The *Kojiki* is the earliest securely datable witness assigning them to Yamato Takeru and court funerals. [E1/R]

### Tomb controversy

Nobono Ōtsuka and Shiratori-ryō are genuine kofun, but “genuine ancient mound” does not mean “identified occupant.” Their official Yamato Takeru designations are nineteenth-century administrative decisions founded on textual geography and local tradition. Lack of an identifying inscription, restricted excavation, and chronological problems prevent verification. [R/I]

### Kusanagi controversy

Kusanagi’s physical identity is religiously asserted, not archaeologically demonstrated. The *Nihon shoki* proves that an authoritative eighth-century tradition located the sword at Atsuta. It cannot prove that the present unseen sacred object is the same physical artifact or that it was wielded by a historical prince. [E1/C]

### Comparative myth

Scholars have compared:

- Yamato Takeru and Susanoo as violent marginal sons connected with Kusanagi;
- Yamato Takeru and Ōkuninushi as heroes subjected to repeated ordeals;
- the white-bird transformation with Eurasian swan-soul and hero-apotheosis motifs;
- the woman-disguise assassination with cross-dressing warrior tales;
- the cycle with heroic-pattern schemes associated with Rank, Raglan, or Campbell.

These approaches may reveal structural analogies, but none establishes direct borrowing without transmission evidence. Campbellian “monomyth” readings are especially prone to flatten the decisive difference between the hostile father in the *Kojiki* and laudatory sovereign in the *Nihon shoki*. [R]

### Pseudo-ancient texts

The *Hotsuma Tsutae* and other supposed *jindai moji* documents are sometimes circulated as pre-*Kojiki* testimony about Yamato Takeru. Their surviving textual history is early modern or later; mainstream scholarship does not accept them as prehistoric records. Their elaborate Yamato Takeru material is therefore **[M/I]**, not an earlier attestation. A modern study can analyze their ideology without authenticating their asserted antiquity. [study of *Hotsuma Tsutae*](https://www.mdpi.com/2077-1444/13/3/199)

### Scope correction to the comparative brief

Antoni, Reider, Foster, Kawamoto, McCullough, the Yoshitsune–Genghis Khan invention, and wartime Momotarō films are valuable for a multi-hero Japanese dossier but do not supply primary Yamato Takeru evidence. Importing them here as though they documented his cycle would create false associations. In particular:

- Yoshitsune-as-Genghis-Khan is a modern invention concerning Yoshitsune, not Yamato Takeru.
- *Momotarō no Umiwashi* and *Momotarō: Umi no Shinpei* are wartime Momotarō propaganda films, not Yamato Takeru adaptations.
- *Heike monogatari* and *Gikeiki* document other heroic traditions.

## 10. Sources

### Primary texts and critical-text foundations

- Ō no Yasumaro, *Kojiki*, 712, middle book. For accessible English extracts, Basil Hall Chamberlain’s 1882 translation; modern work should check it against Japanese critical editions because its vocabulary and ethnonyms are dated.
- Prince Toneri and court compilers, *Nihon shoki*, 720, Book VII, Keikō and Yamato Takeru annals. Accessible English: W. G. Aston, *Nihongi*, 1896.
- *Hitachi no kuni fudoki*, c. 714–718; fragmentary provincial gazetteer.
- Other surviving *fudoki*: Harima, Bungo, Hizen, and Izumo, eighth century.
- Inbe no Hironari, *Kogo shūi*, 807.
- *Engishiki*, compiled 905–927.
- Motoori Norinaga, *Kojiki-den*, composed 1764–1798 and published serially 1790–1822.

### Selected scholarship and documentary resources

- Mori Mizue, “Yamatotakeru,” *Encyclopedia of Shinto*: compact comparison of the two chronicles, names, genealogy, and divine status.
- Hong Seong-Mok, “*Hitachi-no-kuni fudoki* and Emperor Yamatotakeru” (2018): the local sovereign and landscape-ruler variant.
- Kanda Norishiro, “Yamato Takeru and Ōkuninushi”: comparative literary analysis, not proof of identity.
- Kobayashi Masami, “Pioneering the History of Acceptance of Yamato Takeru’s Cultural Resources” (KAKEN project report): modern monuments and regional reception.
- Imperial Household Agency, municipal museums, and cultural-property databases for the history—not the truth—of mausoleum designation.
- Atsuta Jingū, Takebe Taisha, and Ōtori shrine institutional histories, used as evidence for their current and received traditions rather than independent confirmation of ancient events.
