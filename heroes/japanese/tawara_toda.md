# Tawara Tōda (Fujiwara no Hidesato): Research Dossier

> **Traditional class:** A mortal warrior subsequently magnified into a dragon-helper, monster-slayer, culture hero, ancestral founder of warrior houses, and—at specific modern shrines—a heroized dead person worshipped as a kami. He is not a demigod in the earliest sources.
>
> **Earliest secure attestation:** Fujiwara no Hidesato (藤原秀郷) appears as a living provincial warrior in records of Taira no Masakado’s defeat in **940**, especially the near-contemporary *Shōmonki* and the annalistic notices preserved in the *Nihon kiryaku*. These sources know the historical combatant but do **not** attest the Seta Bridge serpent, Dragon Palace, giant centipede, inexhaustible rice bale, or the explanation “Lord Rice-Bale.” The earliest presently secure extended centipede narrative is in Book 15 of the fourteenth-century *Taiheiki*.

## Evidentiary key

- **[E1: securely attested early material]** Tenth- or early-eleventh-century documentary or near-contemporary narrative evidence.
- **[E2: later canonical elaboration]** A major medieval literary formulation that became authoritative.
- **[E3: regional variant]** Locally recorded oral, ritual, or topographical tradition.
- **[E4: learned systematization]** Genealogy, commentary, antiquarian synthesis, or retrospective historical arrangement.
- **[E5: syncretistic identification]** Deliberate equation with a deity, Buddhist protector, ancestor, or another legendary figure.
- **[E6: medieval/early-modern accretion]** Material added in later medieval manuscripts, *otogizōshi*, printed books, drama, or Edo commentary.
- **[E7: folklore]** Oral or customary material whose antiquity cannot normally be established from the tale itself.
- **[E8: modern scholarly reconstruction]** An argument made by modern historians or folklorists.
- **[E9: modern invention/reinvention]** Modern adaptation, shrine construction, propaganda, tourism, entertainment, or a recent claim presented as tradition.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### Orientation

**[E1/E2]** Fujiwara no Hidesato was a tenth-century warrior based in Shimotsuke Province, active under Emperor Suzaku. In 940 he and Taira no Sadamori defeated the rebel Taira no Masakado. Later literature turned this already formidable warrior into **Tawara Tōda**, the fearless archer who walks across a colossal serpent on Seta Bridge, enters an aquatic dragon realm, kills the giant centipede threatening it, receives inexhaustible treasures, and subsequently overcomes an invulnerable, magically duplicated Masakado. The documentary Hidesato and the literary Tawara Tōda overlap, but they are not evidentially interchangeable.

### Names and forms

- **藤原秀郷 — Fujiwara no Hidesato.** **[E1]** The historical name in the Masakado record. *Fujiwara* is the clan name; *Hidesato* is the personal name. Contemporary evidence does not establish his birth or death year.

- **田原藤太 — Tawara/Tahara Tōta or Tōda.** **[E2/E4]** This is the older and philologically less fanciful written form. *Tōta* is conventionally explained as an abbreviated name meaning “Fujiwara’s eldest son”: 藤太, *Tōta*, compressing *Fujiwara no Tarō*. The pronunciation *Tōda* is also established.

- **俵藤太 — Tawara Tōta/Tōda.** **[E2/E6]** Here 田原, “Tahara,” is replaced by the homophone 俵, “straw rice bale.” The medieval story makes the inexhaustible bale the cause of the name: because the Dragon King’s gift never runs out, Hidesato becomes “Tawara Tōda.” That is an etiological pun inside the legend, not proof of the historical name’s origin.

- **俵藤太秀郷 / 田原藤太秀郷 — Tawara/Tahara Tōta Hidesato.** **[E2/E6]** Compound literary forms identifying the legendary hero explicitly with Fujiwara no Hidesato.

- **My Lord Bag of Rice / My Lord Bag-O’-Rice.** **[E9]** Victorian English renderings that literalize 俵. Basil Hall Chamberlain’s *Japanese Fairy Tale Series* version appeared in 1887; Yei Theodora Ozaki’s “My Lord Bag of Rice” appeared in *The Japanese Fairy Book* in 1903. These titles helped fix the rice-bale etymology for Anglophone readers.

### Competing etymologies

1. **Rice-bale gift etymology.** **[E2/E6]** The *Taiheiki* explicitly says that the inexhaustible contents of the tied bale caused him to be called 俵藤太.

2. **Place-name plus birth-order/title.** **[E4/E8]** Edo antiquarian Takizawa Bakin, approved on this point by Minakata Kumagusu in 1916, argued that 田原 referred to a place—variously said to be in Shimotsuke, Yamato, or Ōmi—and 藤太 meant “Fujiwara Tarō”; 俵 and the rice-bale story were then secondary wordplay. The conflicting proposed Tahara locations expose the weakness of treating any one local tradition as demonstrated biography.

3. **Residence near Uji/Tahara.** **[E4/E9]** The modern foundation narrative of Karasawayama Shrine says that the young Hidesato lived at Tahara near the Ōmi–Yamashiro boundary and was consequently called Tahara Tōta. This is shrine tradition, not tenth-century documentation.

The safest conclusion is that **田原藤太 is attested as a medieval literary name and 俵藤太 is a punning reshaping of it**; the exact historical referent of 田原 remains unresolved.

### Status and identifications

- **Mortal provincial warrior:** **[E1]** The earliest and most secure classification.
- **Peerless archer and martial ancestor:** **[E2/E4]** Medieval warrior literature and later genealogies made him a founder of eastern martial lineages.
- **Dragon-helper and monster-slayer:** **[E2]** Canonically established by the *Taiheiki*.
- **Protector favored by Hachiman and the Dragon King:** **[E2/E6]** A literary-religious formulation, not divine parentage.
- **Heroized kami at Karasawayama Shrine:** **[E9]** Instituted in the Meiji period, with the shrine founded in 1883 and raised to *bekkaku kanpeisha* rank in 1890.
- **Founder-ancestor of the Hidesato-line Fujiwara houses:** **[E4]** Claimed by the Oyama, Yūki, Sano, Fujiwara-Ashikaga, and other houses; the fourteenth-century *Sonpi bunmyaku* also connected the Ōshū Fujiwara to him. Such pedigrees served medieval lineage politics and cannot all be projected into the tenth century.

There is no early identification of him as a demigod, incarnation of Hachiman, Buddhist saint, or dragon-descendant.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### The historical record

#### *Shōmonki* (将門記)

**[E1]** Probably written not long after the rebellion of 935–940, although the surviving text is incomplete and transmitted through later manuscripts. It is the principal narrative source for Masakado’s uprising. It presents Hidesato as the experienced strategist whose alliance with Taira no Sadamori makes Masakado’s defeat possible. The historical core is an alliance of regional warrior leaders, not a solitary fairy-tale duel.

The chronicle reports that Hidesato and Sadamori attacked Masakado’s base and that Masakado died in battle on the fourteenth day of the second month of Tengyō 3. His fatal missile is given providential coloring—effectively a heavenly arrow—but the chronicle does not say that Hidesato learned Masakado’s magical weak point from a lover, faced six doubles, or shot an iron-bodied sorcerer.

#### *Nihon kiryaku* (日本紀略)

**[E1]** A later Heian annalistic compilation preserving earlier court records. Notices for 940 report:

- on the fifth day of the third month, Hidesato’s messenger reported Masakado’s killing;
- on the ninth, Hidesato, described as a Shimotsuke official, received junior fourth rank, lower grade;
- in the fourth month his messenger presented Masakado’s head;
- later that year he received the governorship of Shimotsuke; *Fusō ryakki* additionally says he concurrently received Musashi.

These notices securely attest Hidesato’s leading role and unusually high reward. They do not attest Tawara Tōda’s supernatural adventures.

#### Other early historical notices

- **929, *Fusō ryakki*. [E1, though preserved in a later compilation]** Shimotsuke requested measures against Hidesato’s violent or disorderly activities. This complicates the later image of an uncomplicated imperial loyalist.
- **947, *Teishinkōki-shō*. [E1]** Hidesato reportedly informed the court, through Minamoto no Takaakira, that Masakado’s brothers were plotting rebellion. This is among the latest reasonably secure notices associated with him.
- Claims that he died at age ninety-one, in a named year, or on the date later used for his shrine festival belong to retrospective tradition, not secure contemporary biography.

### Early and medieval narrative corpus

#### *Konjaku monogatari-shū*, Book 25

**[E2]** Compiled around the early twelfth century. Its Masakado story calls the warrior **Tahara Tōta Hidesato** and makes Hidesato and Sadamori central to the suppression of the “New Emperor.” It knows the warrior-name but not, in the received story, the developed Seta-Bridge/centipede cycle. This is a crucial terminus: the name precedes the securely attested monster tale.

#### *Genpei jōsuiki* and related war-tale traditions

**[E2/E6]** Later medieval versions of the Genpei war narratives retrospectively insert episodes in which Hidesato considers joining Masakado or approaches him to test his character. Such passages develop the hero as a cunning judge of men. They should not be substituted for the tenth-century record.

#### *Taiheiki*, Book 15, “The Miidera Battle and the Temple Bell, with the Matter of Tawara Tōda”

**[E2]** Composed through the fourteenth century and surviving in variant textual forms. This is the earliest secure extended centipede account. It appears as an aetiology of Miidera’s bell:

1. Hidesato encounters a twenty-*jō* serpent stretched across Seta Bridge.
2. Unafraid, he walks over its back.
3. A strange little man appears and asks him to destroy an enemy.
4. Hidesato follows him beneath Lake Biwa into a jeweled palace.
5. At night an enormous centipede approaches from the direction of Mount Hira, its innumerable legs appearing like thousands of torches.
6. Two arrows rebound from its iron-hard body.
7. Hidesato wets the third arrow with saliva and drives it through the creature’s brow and throat.
8. The dragon being rewards him with a sword, silk, armor, a tied bale, and a bronze bell.
9. The silk and bale never run out; the bale explains the spelling 俵藤太.
10. Hidesato gives the bell to Miidera.

The text itself hesitates over causation: the third shot may work because saliva acts like poison, or because all three arrows struck the same point. That uncertainty belongs to the primary text and should be preserved.

#### *Tawara Tōda monogatari* / *Tawara Tōda emaki*

**[E2/E6]** A Muromachi-period *otogizōshi* known through divergent manuscripts, illustrated scrolls, and early-modern printed books. The composition and textual stemma remain disputed. Yukio Ōshima divided the materials broadly into A and B textual families; some simpler illustrated witnesses may preserve a form not dependent upon the full Masakado romance and have been argued to predate the standard *Taiheiki* relationship, although this is not settled.

Major surviving developments include:

- the bridge-serpent becomes a beautiful woman or **dragon woman**, rather than the *Taiheiki*’s strange little man;
- she visits Hidesato at his lodging after testing him on the bridge;
- the enemy is fixed at **Mount Mikami**, not Mount Hira;
- the monster becomes large enough to wind around the mountain seven and a half times;
- Hidesato invokes **Hachiman Daibosatsu**, wets the final arrow, and kills the centipede;
- the centipede is sometimes cut apart and discarded;
- gifts are distributed in two stages: silk, an inexhaustible rice bale, and a bronze vessel, followed at the palace by armor, sword, and bell;
- the Miidera donation is expanded into temple origin material;
- a second half incorporates Masakado’s rebellion, supernatural body, doubles, secret weak point, defeat, and Hidesato’s rewards.

The *otogizōshi* therefore joins what were initially separable bodies of material: the dragon-realm monster tale, Miidera’s bell legend, and the Masakado war cycle.

### Manuscripts, illustrated books, and editions

- **Early illustrated witnesses. [E2/E6]** A “simple” old scroll lacking some later Masakado material has sometimes been assigned before the Nanbokuchō period, but dating and its relation to the *Taiheiki* remain debated.
- **Chester Beatty *Tawara Tōda monogatari emaki*. [E6]** An important richly illustrated witness, reproduced in a modern facsimile.
- **Ibaraki Prefectural Museum scrolls. [E6]** Three Edo-period volumes: the first shows the centipede and Dragon Palace; the middle and lower volumes show the Masakado campaign, including Taira no Nakamon’s episode and Masakado’s mounted death.
- **Early Edo woodblock and manuscript books. [E6]** These made the story widely accessible as *otogizōshi*.
- **Modern critical texts. [E8]** Important editions include Matsumoto Takanobu’s *Otogizōshi-shū* (Shinchō Nihon Koten Shūsei, 1980) and the *Shin Nihon Koten Bungaku Taikei: Muromachi monogatari-shū*, lower volume (1992).

### Negative corpus findings

**[E1/E8]** Hidesato/Tawara Tōda does not appear in the *Kojiki* or *Nihon shoki*: these eighth-century works predate him and concern a different heroic stratum, including Yamato Takeru. He is likewise not a character in the *Heike monogatari* cycle of Yoshitsune and Benkei, the *Gikeiki*, or the early *Nihon ryōiki*. Comparisons with Momotarō, Kintarō, Urashima Tarō, Yoshitsune, or Benkei concern genre and reception, not direct textual interaction. No classical Noh play centered on Tawara Tōda has been securely identified in the standard repertory.

## 3. The story

### A. Parentage and youth

**[E4]** Later genealogies make Hidesato a descendant of Fujiwara no Uona, usually the son of a Shimotsuke official named Murao. Some traditions name a Kashima-family woman as his mother. These are retrospective pedigrees, not facts supplied by the tenth-century battle record.

**[E4/E7]** His birthplace or childhood home is variously placed in Shimotsuke, an Ōmi Tahara estate, Yamato, or Tahara near Uji. The disagreement is historically important: local and genealogical communities each attached the hero to their landscape.

No early source supplies a miraculous birth, divine father, exposed infancy, supernatural tutor, or childhood exploit.

### B. The serpent on Seta Bridge

**[E2: *Taiheiki*, fourteenth century]** A serpent twenty *jō* long blocks the bridge. Its blazing eyes resemble two suns, its horns a winter wood, its iron teeth interlock, and its red tongue seems to emit flame. Other travelers would collapse in terror; Hidesato walks directly across its back without looking behind him.

**[E6: *Tawara Tōda monogatari*]** The bridge encounter becomes a test deliberately staged by a dragon woman or the Dragon King’s daughter. After Hidesato passes, she assumes human form and visits him, commonly at a post-station lodging.

**[E3/E7]** Local retellings relocate details, call the being an old man, the Dragon King, Otohime, or a dragon princess, and disagree about whether the bridge is Seta/Karahashi or another bridge. Modern retellings often collapse all of these into “the Dragon King disguised as a serpent.”

### C. Entry into the Dragon Palace

**[E2: *Taiheiki*]** The strange little man leads Hidesato through Lake Biwa for more than fifty *chō*. They enter a palace of lapis-lazuli sands, jeweled pavements, vermilion halls, golden fittings, and silver columns. The host dresses in formal court attire and seats Hidesato as an honored guest.

**[E6: *otogizōshi*]** Some witnesses delay the palace visit until after the centipede has been killed. The dragon woman first rewards Hidesato on land and later conducts him to a more elaborate underwater court.

**[E8]** Minakata Kumagusu compared this complex with Indian Buddhist dragon-princess rescue stories, Chinese dragon-palace literature, snake–centipede enmity traditions, and international grateful-animal tales. These are comparative arguments about motifs, not proof that one surviving Indian or Chinese story was the direct source.

### D. The giant centipede

**[E2: *Taiheiki*]** After midnight, wind, rain, and lightning announce the enemy. What appear to be two rows of thousands of burning torches are the centipede’s legs, while its body resembles an island. It approaches from Mount Hira.

Hidesato uses an immense five-man bow and three specially prepared arrows. The first two rebound like arrows striking iron. He spits on the third arrow and shoots the same point between the eyes. It passes through to the throat; the lights vanish and the creature falls with an earth-shaking crash.

**[E6: standard *Tawara Tōda monogatari*]** The mountain is normally **Mikami**, east of Lake Biwa. The centipede winds around it seven and a half times. Hidesato ties on a white headband, prays to Hachiman, and fires. “Seven and a half turns” versus his “eight-wrap” headband generates an early-modern folk pun explaining *hachimaki*, “headband”; this is not in the early historical record.

**[E3/E7]** Variants make saliva inherently poisonous to centipedes; others credit repeated shots, Hachiman’s aid, or a specially empowered arrow. Some identify the resulting sword as **Mukademaru** or **Mukadegiri**, but objects now bearing such names cannot thereby be authenticated as Hidesato’s tenth-century weapon.

### E. Gifts and the name

**[E2: *Taiheiki*]** The gifts are:

- a sword;
- one roll of silk;
- one suit of armor;
- a tied bale;
- a bronze temple bell.

The silk and contents of the bale never diminish. Hidesato stores the inexhaustible productive treasures but gives the Buddhist bell to Miidera. The bale produces his name 俵藤太.

**[E6: *otogizōshi* variants]** The dragon woman may first present inexhaustible silk, rice, and a bronze cooking vessel; the Dragon King subsequently adds armor, a sword, and the bell. Later summaries frequently reduce these to “three treasures” or make the rice bale the only memorable object.

**[E4/E8]** The inexhaustible bale converts the historical warrior into a provider of food and prosperity. That interpretation is strengthened in modern media, but there is no evidence of an ancient agrarian cult of “the rice-bale god.”

### F. Miidera and the bell

**[E2]** Hidesato donates the Dragon Palace bell to Onjōji/Miidera. The *Taiheiki* uses this to introduce the bell later dragged away by Benkei. The temple still identifies the so-called Benkei Bell with the object of the Tawara Tōda legend, while describing the surviving bell as Nara-period workmanship. Thus:

- the bell is a genuine early object;
- its identification as a Dragon Palace gift is a medieval legend;
- Hidesato’s donation is not demonstrated by a tenth-century inscription;
- Benkei’s removal of the bell belongs to a different legendary cycle joined through the object.

### G. Masakado: historical battle and legendary duel

#### Historical stratum

**[E1]** In 940 Hidesato allied with Taira no Sadamori. Their forces attacked Masakado’s base and defeated his remaining army. The *Shōmonki* credits Hidesato’s mature tactical ability; court annals show that Hidesato’s reward exceeded Sadamori’s. Masakado was killed by an arrow in battle, his head was sent to Kyoto, and Hidesato received junior fourth rank and provincial office.

The evidence does not securely identify Hidesato personally as the archer of the fatal shot. “Hidesato killed Masakado” is correct in the collective military sense; a precise individual shot belongs to narrative elaboration.

#### Legendary stratum

**[E6: *Tawara Tōda monogatari*]** Masakado is over seven feet tall, his fivefold body is metal or gold, one eye has two pupils, and six beings exactly resembling him accompany him—seven Masakados in all. Only the real Masakado casts a shadow when facing sun or lamplight. His body is invulnerable except near the ear or temple.

Hidesato feigns service to Masakado and becomes intimate with **Kozaishō no Tsubone**, who discloses the doubles and vulnerable point. Hidesato then recognizes and shoots the real Masakado. This changes a provincial coalition and confused battle into an intelligence story in which erotic access, deception, and secret knowledge determine victory.

**[E3/E7]** Regional variants substitute **Kikyō no Mae** for Kozaishō. She is variously Masakado’s lover, Hidesato’s lover, betrayer, or coerced informant. Some variants make the doubles straw figures; the real Masakado alone exhales visible breath in winter. Others say a Narita Fudō talisman defeats his transformations. These motifs are not in the *Shōmonki* and should not be described as eyewitness history.

**[E2/E6]** The *Taiheiki*’s separate “Enemies of the Realm” passage already gives Masakado an extraordinary or iron body, providing one source for the *otogizōshi* elaboration.

### H. End, death, and body

**[E1]** No secure contemporary record gives Hidesato’s death date or burial.

**[E4/E7]** Later traditions assign extreme longevity, often ninety-one years, and identify burial places or memorial mounds. These cannot be harmonized into a historical death narrative.

**[E3/E9]** The “Fujiwara no Hidesato tomb” at Shin-Yoshimizu in Sano is a municipally designated traditional site. Archaeological reporting notes that the roughly three-metre-high, five-metre-radius mound may instead be an older kofun, called the Tōmyōji Kofun. It has not yielded evidence securely identifying Hidesato as its occupant.

### Every documented interaction with another named divine being or hero

- **Taira no Masakado (平将門):** Hidesato and Sadamori defeat his rebellion; later romance turns the campaign into infiltration and the shooting of an iron-bodied original among six doubles. *Shōmonki* and *Nihon kiryaku*, c. 940/later annalistic transmission; *Tawara Tōda monogatari*, Muromachi–Edo witnesses. **[E1; E6]**

- **Taira no Sadamori (平貞盛):** Military ally against Masakado; the early record makes their coalition essential, while later Hidesato-centered works subordinate Sadamori. *Shōmonki*, tenth century; *Konjaku monogatari-shū*, c. 1120; *Tawara Tōda monogatari*. **[E1/E2/E6]**

- **Dragon King / dragon deity (龍神・龍王):** Tests or recruits Hidesato, hosts him in the underwater palace, asks him to kill the centipede, and rewards him. *Taiheiki* Book 15, fourteenth century; expanded *Tawara Tōda monogatari*. **[E2/E6]**

- **Dragon woman / Dragon King’s daughter (龍女):** Female transformation of the bridge serpent; petitions Hidesato, entertains him, and gives or mediates the gifts. *Tawara Tōda monogatari* and illustrated witnesses, Muromachi–Edo. **[E6]**

- **Otohime (乙姫):** Some Edo and modern visual catalogues identify the dragon woman with Otohime. This naming is not uniform in the medieval text and is a later equation with the familiar Dragon Palace princess. **[E5/E6/E9]**

- **Hachiman Daibosatsu (八幡大菩薩):** Hidesato invokes Hachiman before his decisive arrow in expanded versions. *Tawara Tōda monogatari* and later performance retellings. **[E6]**

- **Emperor Suzaku (朱雀天皇):** Historical sovereign under whom the rebellion occurred; later story frames Hidesato’s commission and reward as direct imperial recognition. Annals, 940; *Tawara Tōda monogatari*. **[E1/E6]**

- **Taira no Nakamon / Nakamon no Tayū (平仲文):** Appears in the Masakado portion of illustrated *Tawara Tōda* witnesses as part of the campaign narrative. Edo-period scroll recension. **[E6]**

- **Kozaishō no Tsubone (小宰相局):** Hidesato gains the secret of Masakado’s true body and vulnerable spot through an intimate relationship with her. Standard expanded *Tawara Tōda monogatari*. **[E6]**

- **Kikyō no Mae (桔梗前):** Regional substitute for Kozaishō; betrays or reveals Masakado’s secret to Hidesato. Recorded regional folklore. **[E3/E7]**

- **Benkei (弁慶):** No face-to-face meeting occurs. Their cycles are connected by the Miidera bell: Hidesato allegedly donates it; Benkei later drags it to Mount Hiei. *Taiheiki* Book 15 and Miidera legend. **[E2]** This is an object-mediated relationship, not a joint adventure.

- **Minamoto no Yoshitsune:** No documented interaction. Association arises only because Benkei and Yoshitsune share a separate heroic cycle. **[E1–E9: absence]**

- **Yamato Takeru, Momotarō, Kintarō, Urashima Tarō:** No documented interaction. They are comparative Japanese heroes, not characters in Tawara Tōda’s corpus. **[Absence]**

- **Giant centipede / Ōmukade:** Supernatural enemy slain for the dragon household. Although not consistently given a personal name, it is the hero’s principal mythic antagonist. *Taiheiki* Book 15 onward. **[E2/E6]**

No securely attested medieval source pairs Tawara Tōda with Susanoo, Raijin, Fūjin, or a named Buddhist bodhisattva other than his invocation of Hachiman Daibosatsu.

## 5. Cult, tomb, relics, and ritual

### Karasawayama Shrine

**[E9]** Karasawayama Shrine in Sano is the clearest institutional cult of Hidesato. It is not a surviving tenth-century hero shrine.

- Descendants and former retainers of the Sano house organized the Tōmeikai around 1880.
- Construction was authorized in 1881.
- A provisional sanctuary was completed and Hidesato enshrined in 1883.
- The government posthumously promoted him to senior third rank in 1883.
- The shrine became a *bekkaku kanpeisha*, a state-supported category honoring loyal or nationally meritorious persons, in 1890.
- Hidesato received senior second rank in 1918.
- The present main buildings largely date from 1908–1924.

The shrine therefore embodies **Meiji loyalist ancestor-making**: the victor over the self-proclaimed “New Emperor” was converted into an exemplary imperial servant. Claims that Hidesato himself built Karasawayama Castle are traditional; archaeological work dates the castle remains no earlier than approximately the fifteenth century.

### Ryūōgū-Hidesato Shrine and Unjūji, Seta

**[E3/E6]** At the east end of Seta Bridge, Ryūōgū-Hidesato Shrine jointly commemorates the Seta River dragon deity and Hidesato. Nearby Unjūji preserves a centipede memorial hall. These sites anchor the narrative physically to the bridge landscape. Their traditions are valuable evidence of local reception, but not archaeological proof of a tenth-century monster event.

### Miidera’s bell

**[E2/E6]** Miidera displays the so-called Benkei Bell and repeats the legend that Hidesato brought it from the Dragon Palace and donated it after killing the centipede. The bell is materially ancient, but no inscription demonstrates supernatural origin or Hidesato’s ownership. It is a genuine relic around which two hero legends accumulated.

### Tomb and bodily relics

**[E3/E9]** The Sano mound identified as Hidesato’s grave is commemorative and municipally protected as a traditional site. Archaeologists have proposed that it is an earlier kofun. No excavated remains securely identify Hidesato, and no authenticated bones or contemporary funerary inscription are known.

### Arms and armor

**[E4/E6]** Swords called Mukademaru or Mukadegiri, armor called *Hiraiya no yoroi* (“arrow-avoiding armor”), and arrowheads attributed to the centipede battle survive in treasury and family traditions. Their provenances express lineage memory; the attribution to Hidesato or the Dragon King is legendary unless supported independently. One reputed centipede sword was presented to Ise Shrine in 1765—evidence for an Edo cult of ancestry, not for tenth-century possession.

### Performance and commemorative ritual

- **Nagashi/Shikimi “Tawara Tōda’s Centipede Extermination.” [E9]** A festival performance at Otomiya Shrine in Nagasaki, performed every eight years on 29 October. The municipal record says it was devised as entertainment for a Sino-Japanese War victory celebration and later became a shrine offering. Its explicit modern origin is exceptionally well documented.
- **Shinjō Festival float, 2023. [E9]** A float dramatized Tawara Tōda and Otohime, including the modern synthesis that the dragon deity later helped him defeat Masakado.
- **Ōtsu processional performance. [E6]** A 1787 festival register already lists a Tawara Tōda *nerimono*, showing established Edo-period public dramatization.
- **Iwami kagura, “Mikamiyama.” [E9]** A creative modern kagura adaptation rather than an ancient liturgical survival.

No securely documented medieval annual sacrificial cult, oracle, funeral games, or human/animal sacrifice to Hidesato is known.

## 6. Historical evolution

### 940–c. 1000: provincial commander

**[E1]** Hidesato enters the record as a politically ambiguous but effective provincial warrior: earlier accused of disorder, later indispensable to the court. His defeat of Masakado and high reward established the historical capital from which the legend grew.

### Eleventh–twelfth centuries: warrior exemplar and named “Tahara Tōta”

**[E2]** *Konjaku monogatari-shū* turns the rebellion into exemplary warrior narrative and already uses the Tahara Tōta name. There is still no securely attested centipede.

### Thirteenth–fourteenth centuries: lineage ancestor and bell legend

**[E2/E4]** Eastern houses increasingly used Hidesato ancestry to authorize status. The *Taiheiki* attached him to Seta Bridge, Lake Biwa’s dragon realm, the centipede, inexhaustible treasures, and Miidera’s bell. The monster episode is introduced to explain a famous temple object, not as part of a continuous biography.

### Muromachi period: composite hero

**[E2/E6]** *Tawara Tōda monogatari* fused the dragon episode and Masakado cycle. The Dragon King acquired a daughter or female manifestation; Mount Mikami displaced Mount Hira; Hachiman entered the shot; Masakado acquired a metal body and doubles; a woman supplied the fatal intelligence. The hero became brave, pious, cunning, sexually persuasive, and divinely favored.

### Edo period: print, theatre, local history

**[E6]** Illustrated scrolls, *nara ehon*, woodblock books, popular histories, festival tableaux, jōruri, and kabuki spread the story. A kabuki/jōruri title “Tawara Tōta,” associated with *Ware tatsu ōjō shiki no utedai*, is recorded for a Nagoya premiere in 1732. No evidence supports treating Tawara Tōda as a major independent classical Noh role.

Antiquarians also began separating pun from history. Bakin’s view that “Tahara Tōta” preceded “Rice-Bale Tōta” is an early philological demythologization.

### Nineteenth century: ukiyo-e hero and imperial loyalist

**[E6/E9]** Artists made the supernatural spectacle visually canonical:

- Utagawa Kuniyoshi, **Kusatsu: Tawara Tōda and the Dragon Woman**, from *Fifty-three Pairings for the Tōkaidō*, 1845–46.
- Kuniyoshi, **The Dragon Palace: Three Gifts Presented to Tawara Tōda Hidesato**, triptych, fourth month of 1858.
- Tsukioka Yoshitoshi, **Tawara Tōta Hidesato and the Dragon Princess of Seta**, from *One Hundred Ghost Stories of China and Japan*, 1865.
- A triptych titled **Hidesato kills the great centipede at Seta Bridge; the dragon deity rejoices**, 1867.
- Yoshitoshi’s **Former Taiheiki: Sōma Palace**, 1868, joins Hidesato’s image to the Masakado/Takiyasha visual world.

From 1880 onward the Sano commemoration movement transformed him into a state-sanctioned loyalist kami. This was not the recovery of a continuous ancient shrine.

### International fairy-tale era

**[E9]** Chamberlain’s 1887 “My Lord Bag-O’-Rice” and Ozaki’s 1903 version detached the centipede story from the more morally ambiguous Masakado infiltration. The result was a self-contained children’s tale: courage, monster-slaying, gratitude, and inexhaustible food. Modern English retellings often derive more from this Victorian redaction than from the *Taiheiki* or full *otogizōshi*.

### Twentieth and twenty-first centuries

**[E8/E9]** Folklorists analyzed snake–centipede opposition, dragon-palace analogues, grateful-animal structures, agricultural plenty, and possible metalworking symbolism. Local museums and tourism organizations now present a double figure—historical commander and legendary centipede-slayer—while games recast the inexhaustible bale as a magical ability.

## 7. Modern reception and reinvention

### Scholarship and children’s literature

- **Minakata Kumagusu, “Tahara Tōta’s Journey to the Dragon Palace,” 1916. [E8]** A vast comparative survey linking the story with Asian dragon, snake, centipede, poison, treasure, and underwater-palace motifs. Its breadth is foundational, but many parallels establish resemblance rather than direct transmission.
- **Chamberlain, 1887; Ozaki, 1903. [E9]** These versions established “My Lord Bag of Rice” in English-language children’s literature.
- **Japanese school culture. [E9]** Tawara Tōda circulated through readers and children’s anthologies, but unlike Momotarō he was not the central standardized hero of Meiji–wartime national textbooks. Claims that Tawara Tōda was used in the wartime Momotarō propaganda films confuse separate heroic traditions.

### Visual art

The dominant modern image—archer in the foreground, enormous segmented body coiling around a dark mountain, dragon princess nearby—is substantially an Edo/Meiji print convention. Kuniyoshi and Yoshitoshi did not merely illustrate a fixed ancient story; they selected and stabilized its most theatrical components.

### Theatre and folk spectacle

The hero appears in kabuki/jōruri titles, Edo procession records, local shrine dances, floats, creative kagura, and modern rakugo programs. The evidence supports broad performative circulation but not a major unbroken Noh cult-drama.

### Digital entertainment

**[E9]** *Fate/Grand Order* introduced Tawara Tōta as an Archer-class “Servant.” His design emphasizes appetite, archery, and the inexhaustible rice gift; the game places him with Xuanzang, the Knights of the Round Table, and other figures he never meets in premodern Japanese texts. These interactions are franchise inventions. The game launched in 2015, and its Camelot story later received animated film adaptations.

### Political appropriation

**[E9]** The strongest documented political appropriation is Meiji state commemoration:

- Hidesato was selected as the loyal suppressor of an imperial rebel.
- Karasawayama Shrine’s founders included descendants, former retainers, and political patrons.
- Court ranks were posthumously bestowed in 1883 and 1918.
- The shrine’s elevation to *bekkaku kanpeisha* placed him among state-honored loyal subjects.

The Nagasaki centipede performance was explicitly invented for a Sino-Japanese War victory celebration, then ritualized locally. These examples show how a monster-slayer could be mobilized for loyalty and military victory. No comparably documented modern extremist movement centered on Tawara Tōda was found.

### Modern inventions commonly misrepresented as old

- “The historical Hidesato was called Rice-Bale Lord because of a real inexhaustible rice sack.” **Modern literalization of a medieval etiology.**
- “Karasawayama Shrine is his ancient family shrine.” **False: founded in 1883.**
- “Karasawayama Castle was archaeologically built by Hidesato.” **Traditional claim contradicted by present archaeological dating.**
- “The Sano mound has been proven to contain him.” **False: identification unproved; it may be a kofun.**
- “Otohime is the uniform medieval name of the bridge serpent.” **False: a later familiarizing identification.**
- “He personally fired the historically documented arrow that killed Masakado.” **Not established by the early evidence.**
- “The standard story comes from the *Kojiki*.” **False: it appears many centuries later.**
- “Tawara Tōda appears in the wartime Momotarō films.” **Conflation with another hero.**

## 8. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Was Hidesato historical?

Yes, in the limited documentary sense. **[E1/E8]** His participation in the 940 suppression, rank, and offices are multiply attested. What is not recoverable is a complete biography: birth, childhood, marriage, exact parentage, death, and burial remain dependent on much later sources.

The better historiographical question is not “did he exist?” but **which features belong to the tenth-century commander and which to the Tawara Tōda construct?**

### How early is the centipede story?

The conservative answer is the fourteenth-century *Taiheiki*. A possibly older-looking illustrated recension has led some scholars to question simple one-way dependence, but no tenth-century document attests the episode. Assertions that *Konjaku monogatari-shū* contains the fully developed centipede story are erroneous; it supplies Tahara Tōta and the Masakado narrative, not the received Dragon Palace cycle.

### *Taiheiki* versus *Tawara Tōda monogatari*

Yukio Ōshima and subsequent textual scholarship stress multiple recensions rather than a single stable work. Important divergences include:

| Motif | *Taiheiki* | Expanded *Tawara Tōda monogatari* |
|---|---|---|
| Serpent’s human form | Strange little man | Beautiful woman/dragon woman |
| Palace visit | Before battle | Often after battle |
| Mountain | Hira | Mikami |
| Rewards | Five principal objects | Often two-stage and differently enumerated |
| Hachiman prayer | Not central in quoted version | Explicit |
| Masakado cycle | Elsewhere in work | Integrated second half |
| Name explanation | Inexhaustible bale | Repeated and amplified |

### Origin of the name

The rice-bale explanation is internally attested but etiological. Bakin and Kumagusu’s place-name theory is plausible, yet the competing Tahara locations remain unresolved. The claim that *tōta* abbreviates “Fujiwara Tarō” is philologically conventional but still a retrospective explanation rather than a signed tenth-century self-designation.

### What does the centipede “mean”?

Several modern interpretations circulate:

- **Snake-versus-centipede zoological opposition. [E8]** Supported by East Asian lore in which centipedes overcome snakes.
- **Water deity versus mountain force. [E8]** Fits the Lake Biwa/Mikami topography.
- **Agrarian provider myth. [E8]** Supported by the inexhaustible rice but less clearly by the earliest version’s broader gift list.
- **Mining or ironworking conflict. [E8]** The centipede’s many legs can resemble miners, and mountain/metal associations invite the theory, but no primary text says that the monster represents miners or a conquered metalworking population. It should be presented as symbolic reconstruction, not hidden historical fact.
- **Political allegory of Masakado. [E8]** Similarities—iron bodies, shooting a vulnerable spot—may have encouraged the fusion, but the *Taiheiki* does not identify the centipede as Masakado.

### Genealogy and descent

Medieval pedigrees made Hidesato an exceptionally productive ancestor. The connection to the Ōshū Fujiwara is attested in the fourteenth-century *Sonpi bunmyaku*, but interdisciplinary work treats it as a later genealogical construction, not as direct contemporary proof. The same caution applies to the many Kantō houses claiming him.

### Tomb and relic debates

The material record is strongest where it contradicts literal tradition:

- the “tomb” lacks identifying evidence and may be an earlier kofun;
- the Karasawayama castle remains are centuries later than Hidesato;
- the Miidera bell is ancient but lacks proof of Dragon Palace or Hidesato provenance;
- attributed swords and armor document later family belief and patronage, not necessarily tenth-century ownership.

### State of the evidence

The dossier rests on an uneven archive:

1. sparse court notices;
2. an incomplete near-contemporary war narrative;
3. twelfth-century exemplary tales;
4. fluid fourteenth-century war-tale texts;
5. divergent Muromachi and Edo picture manuscripts;
6. genealogies produced by families with claims to validate;
7. authentic monuments bearing later legends;
8. modern folklore records that seldom establish the age of oral material.

Consequently, precise labels are more reliable than a harmonized “life.”

## 10. Sources and assessment

### Primary and near-primary textual foundations

- *Shōmonki*. The indispensable near-contemporary narrative of Masakado’s rebellion. Best used through critical Japanese editions and Judith N. Rabinovitch’s English translation, *Shōmonki: The Story of Masakado’s Rebellion* (1986).
- *Nihon kiryaku*, Tengyō 3 entries. Annalistic evidence for reports, rank, head presentation, and appointment.
- *Fusō ryakki*. Later compilation preserving the 929 and rebellion-era notices.
- *Teishinkōki-shō*. Notice for 947.
- *Konjaku monogatari-shū*, Book 25. Early literary Hidesato/Tahara Tōta and Masakado tradition.
- *Taiheiki*, Book 15. Earliest secure full centipede narrative and Miidera-bell aetiology.
- *Tawara Tōda monogatari*. Multiple Muromachi–Edo recensions; must not be cited as though it were one invariant text.
- *Sonpi bunmyaku*. Important evidence for later genealogical construction.

### Critical editions and scholarship

- Matsumoto Takanobu, ed., *Otogizōshi-shū*, Shinchō Nihon Koten Shūsei, 1980.
- Ichiko Teiji et al., eds., *Muromachi monogatari-shū*, lower volume, Shin Nihon Koten Bungaku Taikei, 1992.
- Ōshima Yukio, “The Textual Formation of the *Otogizōshi Tawara Tōda monogatari*,” 1985.
- Araki Hiroyuki, study of the formation and dynamics of the Tawara Tōda tradition, 1985.
- Hirota Osamu, study of the *Tawara Tōda emaki*, 1989.
- Matsuda Nobufumi, study of Onjōji’s bell tradition, 1999.
- Noguchi Minoru, *Densetsu no shōgun Fujiwara no Hidesato*, 2001.
- Minakata Kumagusu, “Tahara Tōta ryūgū-iri no hanashi,” 1916.
- Tochigi Prefectural Museum, *Fujiwara no Hidesato and His Descendants: Historical Fact and Legend*, exhibition and object list, 2025.

### Final assessment

The record supports four propositions with different degrees of confidence:

1. **Fujiwara no Hidesato was a historical tenth-century provincial commander who played a leading role in defeating Taira no Masakado.**
2. **“Tahara Tōta” was attached to him by the early twelfth century, before the securely attested centipede story.**
3. **The familiar serpent, centipede, Dragon Palace, endless rice, and Miidera-bell cycle is a medieval literary construction first securely visible in the fourteenth-century *Taiheiki* and substantially reshaped by *otogizōshi*.**
4. **His cult as an enshrined loyalist kami is principally a Meiji creation, while today’s popular hero is an accumulation of medieval literature, Edo visual culture, Victorian fairy-tale editing, local ritual, museum heritage, and digital reinvention.**

The history is therefore not the biography of an ancient monster-slayer. It is the documented transformation of a politically ambiguous provincial warrior into an ideal archer, dragon-helper, food-giver, clan ancestor, loyalist deity, fairy-tale hero, and game character.
