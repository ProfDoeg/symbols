# Empress Jingū: Research Dossier

> **Traditional class:** a mortal imperial consort, inspired ruler, military culture-hero and heroized dead; subsequently a kami of the Sumiyoshi and Hachiman cult-complexes and a protective mother associated with pregnancy and childbirth. She is not originally a goddess or demigod in the eighth-century chronicles.
>
> **Earliest secure attestation:** the *Kojiki* (古事記, completed 712 CE), where she is Okinaga-tarashi-hime and receives divine possession, conquers Silla while pregnant, bears the future Emperor Ōjin, and defeats his rival half-brothers. The *Nihon shoki* (日本書紀, 720), the near-contemporary provincial *fudoki*, and no securely contemporary third- or fourth-century inscription constitute the earliest documentary stratum. The traditional dates 169–269 CE are the *Nihon shoki*’s retrospective chronology, not independently verified life-dates.

## Evidentiary labels

- **[E1: securely attested early material]** A statement demonstrably present in the *Kojiki* (712), *Nihon shoki* (720), an early *fudoki*, or relevant archaeological/epigraphic evidence. “Securely attested” dates the testimony, not the event narrated.
- **[E2: later canonical elaboration]** A development in medieval authoritative chronicles, shrine histories, Hachiman texts, or canonical commentaries.
- **[E3: regional variant]** A locally transmitted shrine, place-name, relic or ritual tradition.
- **[E4: learned systematization/syncretism]** A genealogical, Buddhist, Shintō, Confucian or historiographical arrangement imposed after the early narratives.
- **[E5: medieval or early-modern accretion]** A story absent from the eighth-century witnesses and first securely visible in later literature or visual culture.
- **[E6: folklore]** Oral or customary tradition documented locally without an early textual warrant.
- **[E7: modern scholarly reconstruction]** A modern argument, not primary evidence.
- **[E8: modern invention/reinvention]** A modern image, ideological claim, commercial representation or story presented as new, or sometimes misrepresented as ancient.

## 1. Identification, Names and Epithets

### Orientation

**[E1]** The *Kojiki* makes Okinaga-tarashi-hime the wife of Tarashi-nakatsu-hiko (Emperor Chūai) and mother of Homuda-wake, the future Emperor Ōjin. When gods possess her and promise a treasure-rich western country, Chūai refuses to believe them and dies. She conducts further divination, mobilizes a fleet, delays childbirth with stones attached at her waist, reaches Silla through supernatural maritime assistance, accepts its submission, returns to Kyūshū, gives birth, and defeats princes who contest her son’s succession. The text does not give her an independent reign; its Jingū narrative lies within the Chūai–Ōjin succession.

**[E1]** Book IX of the *Nihon shoki* expands this into a formally annalistic “administration” of sixty-nine years. It depicts her as priest-medium, commander and posthumous imperial regent, though it calls her *Ōkisaki* or empress-consort and later “Grand Empress.” It gives traditional dates equivalent to 169–269 CE, an age of one hundred, and burial at Saki. The chronology is internally artificial: fourth-century Korean diplomatic material is placed in the third century, and excerpts from Chinese histories concerning Himiko are inserted under Jingū’s annals.

The *Kojiki* genealogical commentary associates *Okinaga* with a place and lineage, while later antiquarians such as Motoori Norinaga took *tarashi* as an honorific meaning “perfect” or “complete.” That explanation is learned philology rather than a securely recoverable second-century etymology. The early imperial names containing *tarashi* may themselves have been regularized during later genealogical construction.

### Descent and status

**[E1]** In the *Nihon shoki*, she is the daughter of Okinaga no Sukune no Miko and Katsuraki no Taka-nuka-hime, and a great-great-granddaughter or remote descendant of Emperor Kaika, depending on how the compressed genealogy is counted. The *Kojiki* supplies a fuller maternal line descending from the immigrant prince Ame-no-Hiboko through Tajima figures. That Korean-connected ancestry creates an important irony beside the conquest legend, but it is still legendary genealogy, not evidence of Jingū’s ethnicity.

**[E1]** She is:

- Chūai’s imperial consort;
- a divinely possessed medium who speaks the gods’ commands;
- mother and political guardian of Homuda-wake/Ōjin;
- commander or supreme sponsor of the western expedition;
- administrator during the interval before Ōjin’s accession;
- a deceased royal assigned a mausoleum.

**[E2–E4]** Later Hachiman doctrine places her beside Ōjin/Hachiman and a female deity as one of a triad, or makes her the divine mother of Hachiman. Sumiyoshi traditions likewise enshrine her with the three Sumiyoshi sea gods. These are datable cultic incorporations, not her original ontological class.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### What the oldest evidence is—and is not

No surviving coin, inscription, tomb text, Korean record or securely excavated object names Jingū as a contemporary ruler. **The oldest securely dated evidence for the figure is literary and early eighth-century.** This leaves a documentary gap of roughly four centuries between the traditional setting and the texts.

The fourth-century Seven-Branched Sword (*shichishitō*) at Isonokami Shrine is relevant to the world later placed inside her annals, but its inscription addresses a ruler of Wa rather than Jingū. Its most-discussed date, 369, and the *Nihon shoki*’s placement of a seven-branched sword in Jingū year 52 are evidence of Baekje–Wa diplomacy and of the chronicle’s chronological construction, not direct proof of Jingū.

### Primary textual corpus

#### *Kojiki* — 712, Japanese in mixed Chinese/phonographic notation

**[E1]** The relevant sections are:

- Genealogy under Emperor Kaika: ancestry of Okinaga-tarashi-hime.
- Chūai sections XCV–XCIX: marriage and children; divine possession; Chūai’s disbelief and death; purification and renewed oracle; mobilization; Silla expedition; pregnancy stones; return and fishing omen.
- Section C: civil war against Kagosaka and Oshikuma.
- Sections CI–CII: Homuda-wake’s encounter with the deity Izasa-wake and Jingū’s “waiting-liquor” song.
- Section CIII: her death at one hundred and burial at Tatanami in Saki, in a passage traditional commentators suspected was added from the *Nihon shoki*.
- Ōjin sections: her ancestry from Ame-no-Hiboko.

The surviving manuscripts are much later than 712; the oldest substantially complete textual witnesses are medieval. The date 712 belongs to the compilation reported in Ō no Yasumaro’s preface, not to an autograph manuscript.

#### *Nihon shoki* — 720, Classical Chinese

**[E1]** Book VIII introduces the divine promise of the western treasure-land and Chūai’s death. Book IX, devoted to Okinaga-tarashi-hime, is the most extensive source:

- parentage, beauty and intelligence;
- ritual interrogation of the gods;
- campaigns against Kumaso, Hashiro Kuma-washi and Tabura-tsu-hime;
- the Matsura fishing omen and sacred rice-field;
- martial transformation and expedition;
- childbirth-delaying stone;
- surrender of Silla and purported submission of Goguryeo and Baekje;
- birth of Homuda;
- defeat of Oshikuma;
- formal administration;
- diplomatic annals, including Chinese-history excerpts and Korean records;
- death and burial.

The *Nihon shoki* explicitly preserves “one version says” variants. These should not be collapsed into a single biography.

#### Provincial *fudoki* — ordered in 713; surviving texts and fragments compiled chiefly in the early eighth century

**[E1/E3]** The *Harima no kuni fudoki*, probably completed by about 716, and the *Hizen no kuni fudoki* preserve numerous local Jingū/Ōjin traditions, including marches, place-name explanations, encounters with local rulers or “earth-spiders,” stones and omens. Harima survives uniquely through the much later Sanjōnishi manuscript; Hizen is fragmentary. They are early evidence for the geographical spread and localization of the legend, but their individual episodes must not automatically be projected into a third-century life.

#### Chinese dynastic histories

The *Wei zhi* account of the Wa people, compiled in the later third century within Chen Shou’s *Sanguozhi*, describes Queen Himiko and her successor Iyo/Toyo. **[E1, external contemporary tradition]** It never names Okinaga-tarashi-hime or Jingū.

**[E1]** The *Nihon shoki* inserts paraphrased or quoted Chinese notices concerning a Wa queen into Jingū’s annals. This establishes that the 720 compilers aligned their chronological framework with the Chinese queen material; it does not establish that Himiko and Jingū were one historical woman.

#### Korean histories and quoted Baekje records

The extant *Samguk sagi* was compiled in 1145 from earlier material. It does not corroborate a third-century conquest of all three kingdoms by a ruler named Jingū. The *Nihon shoki* also quotes a lost “Baekje record,” apparently compiled by Korean literati available to the Yamato court. Its fourth-century diplomatic names and events are often more chronologically plausible than the surrounding Jingū dating.

#### Medieval Hachiman corpus

- *Hachiman Usa-gū gotakusen-shū* (八幡宇佐宮御託宣集), compiled in the early fourteenth century: **[E2/E5]** systematizes Usa oracles and the Hachiman/Jingū sacred history.
- *Hachiman gudōkun* (八幡愚童訓), late thirteenth to early fourteenth century, in distinct A and B recensions: **[E2/E5]** retells her expedition as proof of Hachiman’s protection against foreign enemies, in the ideological atmosphere created by the Mongol invasions of 1274 and 1281.
- Hachiman origin scrolls (*Hachiman engi emaki*), medieval through early modern: **[E2/E5]** visualize Jingū’s campaign and Ōjin’s emergence as Hachiman.
- *Taiheiki*, fourteenth century: **[E5]** participates in the medieval Azumi-no-Isora/tide-jewel complex.
- *Mizukagami*, conventionally dated to the late twelfth century: **[E5]** is among the earlier narrative histories elaborating the tide-jewel conquest.

#### Early-modern corpus

- Illustrated Hachiman books and shrine histories;
- Hayashi Razan’s and Tokugawa historiographers’ historical discussions;
- Arai Hakuseki and other rationalizing or euhemerizing histories;
- the Mito school’s *Dai Nihonshi*, which excluded Jingū from the numbered imperial succession and treated her as regent rather than the fifteenth sovereign;
- women’s conduct literature such as *Honchō jokan*, which could praise her as wife, mother and ruler while disciplining her transgressive military authority;
- shrine records at Mekari, Kashii, Umi, Sumiyoshi, Hakozaki and related regional sites.

#### Corpus exclusions

The *Heike monogatari*, *Gikeiki*, otogizōshi concerning Momotarō, Kintarō or Urashima, and the principal Yoshitsune–Benkei cycle are essential to other Japanese hero dossiers but are not foundational Jingū sources. Likewise, *Nihon ryōiki* and *Konjaku monogatari-shū* do not provide an independent full Jingū cycle comparable to the *Kojiki*, *Nihon shoki* or Hachiman texts. Their absence is significant: Jingū’s strongest medieval transmission ran through shrine-origin literature, war ideology and visual Hachiman narrative, not through a single vernacular epic devoted to her.

## 3. The Story

### 3.1 Parentage, marriage and pregnancy

**[E1: *Kojiki*, 712]** Okinaga-tarashi-hime descends maternally from Ame-no-Hiboko, marries Chūai, and bears Homu-ya-wake and Homuda-wake/Ōjin. The text says a flesh-like pad on Homuda’s arm marked, even in the womb, that he would rule countries.

**[E1: *Nihon shoki*, 720]** Her parents are Okinaga no Sukune no Miko and Katsuraki no Taka-nuka-hime. She becomes Chūai’s empress in his second year. Her pregnancy is already under way when the gods declare that the unborn child will possess the promised country.

These are incompatible with a literal ordinary chronology: the child’s gestation is extended through the overseas expedition, and later tradition enormously magnifies the interval. The supernatural pregnancy is central, not an embarrassment to be removed.

### 3.2 The divine promise and Chūai’s death

**[E1: *Kojiki*]** While Chūai prepares to attack Kumaso at Kashii, Jingū becomes possessed. Through her the gods promise a western land rich in gold and silver. Chūai climbs or looks west, sees only sea, denounces the gods as liars and stops playing his lute. In darkness the instrument resumes sounding; Takeuchi no Sukune lights a fire and finds Chūai dead. Further divination identifies the gods and says the promised country will belong to the child in Jingū’s womb.

**[E1: *Nihon shoki* principal account]** Chūai refuses the oracle and dies prematurely as divine punishment. Jingū conducts purification and seven days and nights of ritual inquiry while Takeuchi plays the lute and Igatsu no Omi acts as *saniwa*, the interpreter/testing official.

**[E1: *Nihon shoki* alternative account]** The gods speak through several local mediums before speaking through Jingū. Chūai objects to surrendering his ship and rice-field and mocks the oracle’s language; the gods condemn him and reserve the land for his unborn child.

The sources thus disagree over the ritual staging and named media but agree on the structure: female possession, male disbelief, death, renewed consultation, and transfer of divine favor to mother and fetus.

### 3.3 Identification of the gods

The *Nihon shoki* names:

1. Tsuki-sakaki-idzu-no-mitama-amazakuru-Muka-tsu-hime, understood by Aston and later commentators as an elaborate designation connected with Amaterasu at Ise;
2. an incompletely identified deity dwelling at Aha in Oda;
3. Kotoshironushi;
4. Uwa-tsutsu-no-o, Naka-tsutsu-no-o and Soko-tsutsu-no-o, the three Sumiyoshi sea gods.

**[E1]** The gods’ plurality is important. The early text does not say simply that “Ryūjin gave Jingū magic jewels.” That familiar simplification belongs to the medieval layer.

### 3.4 Kyūshū campaigns and local opponents

**[E1: *Nihon shoki*]** Kamo no Wake subdues the Kumaso. Jingū then marches against Hashiro Kuma-washi, a powerful winged or bird-like rebel who plunders the people, and kills him. She puts to death Tabura-tsu-hime, characterized as a *tsuchigumo* (“earth-spider”), after which Tabura-tsu-hime’s brother Natsuha flees.

“Tsuchigumo” is court rhetoric for resistant local peoples as well as a monster-word. The passage records the Yamato text’s representation of conquest; it does not establish that its targets were literally nonhuman.

### 3.5 The Matsura fishing omen

**[E1: both chronicles; local variant in Hizen traditions]** At the Matsura River, Jingū bends a needle into a hook, pulls thread from her garment, baits it with rice and catches an *ayu*. She takes this as an omen of success.

The *Nihon shoki* derives Matsura from her exclamation *mezurashi* (“marvellous/strange”), an etymology rejected even by early modern and modern philologists. Both chronicles state that local women continued the ritual fishing in the first ten days of the fourth month; the *Nihon shoki* adds that men could not catch fish. This is unusually valuable evidence that the compilers understood a women’s regional custom through the Jingū legend.

### 3.6 Sacred rice-field and water channel

**[E1: *Nihon shoki*]** Jingū consecrates and cultivates a rice-field. A rock blocks its irrigation channel. Takeuchi offers a sword and mirror and prays; thunder and lightning split the rock, producing the Sakuta Channel. The episode makes military preparation, agriculture and ritual sovereignty continuous.

### 3.7 Martial transformation

**[E1: *Nihon shoki*]** Jingū washes her hair and asks that it divide spontaneously if the campaign will succeed. It parts into male-style bunches. She declares that, though a “feeble woman,” she will temporarily borrow a man’s outward appearance and adopt “manly counsels,” taking personal responsibility for failure.

This is the source’s gendered rhetoric, not a neutral report. Medieval and modern readers alternately emphasized the cross-gender commander, the obedient divine medium, or the maternal consort.

### 3.8 The pregnancy stones

**[E1: *Kojiki*]** To restrain her womb, Jingū takes stones and wraps or binds them at the waist of her skirt. The child is born only after she returns to Tsukushi. The text connects the stones with place-names Tsukushi and Umi.

**[E1: *Nihon shoki*]** She places a stone at her loins and prays that delivery occur in this land on the day she returns. The compiler says the stone remained beside a road in Ito District.

**[E3/E6]** Later Kyūshū traditions multiply the objects into *chinkai-seki* or *tsukinobe-no-ishi*, identifying particular stones at shrines in Itoshima, Iki, Kyoto and elsewhere. No geological object can be authenticated as Jingū’s stone. The securely early point is the narrated use of a stone or stones and an eighth-century claim that a local relic existed.

### 3.9 The expedition to Silla: early versions

#### *Kojiki*

**[E1]** Great and small fish carry or surround the ship; a following wind drives the fleet, and a wave washes it into Silla. The ruler, terrified, submits and promises to serve as the imperial “horse-groom” or provider until the world ends. Jingū plants her staff at his gate and makes Silla an imperial possession. The text tells no pitched naval battle and names neither Goguryeo nor Baekje as conquered.

#### *Nihon shoki*

**[E1]** The rough spirit of the deity leads the fleet; the gentle spirit guards Jingū. The Wind God raises a favorable wind, the Sea God raises waves, and great fish surround the ships. The tide floods deep into Silla. Its king, styled Phasa Mikeun in Aston’s transcription, sees a divinely supported fleet, binds himself, submits maps and registers, and promises annual tribute, slaves, combs and whips. Jingū refuses a suggestion to kill a ruler who has surrendered, seals the treasure stores, plants her spear at the palace gate and accepts a hostage and eighty treasure ships.

The chronicle then says the rulers of Goguryeo and Baekje submit without fighting, producing the retrospective category “the three Han.” This is the textual origin of the later slogan *sankan seibatsu* or “conquest/subjugation of the Three Han.” It is not corroborated as a single third-century campaign by Korean records or archaeology.

The *Nihon shoki* nevertheless contains genuine-looking fourth-century Korean diplomatic materials elsewhere in Jingū’s annals. Their presence shows that the compilers attached remembered Baekje–Wa relations to the legendary regency. It does not verify the opening conquest scene.

### 3.10 Tide jewels: the medieval battle

**[E5]** Neither the *Kojiki* nor the *Nihon shoki* gives Jingū the ebb-tide and flood-tide jewels. In the early myths those jewels belong to the Hoori/sea-god cycle.

Medieval Hachiman texts transfer them to Jingū. In versions represented by the *Hachiman gudōkun*, *Hachiman Usa-gū gotakusen-shū*, *Taiheiki* and related shrine narratives:

1. Jingū or Takeuchi summons the marine deity Azumi-no-Isora.
2. Ashamed of barnacles, oysters or abalone clinging to his face, Isora initially remains underwater.
3. Sacred music or dance draws him out.
4. Isora visits or mediates with the Dragon King—variously named or localized—and obtains the ebb and flood jewels.
5. Jingū throws the ebb jewel into the sea; the water retreats and the Korean force advances on the exposed seabed.
6. She then uses the flood jewel; the returning sea drowns or overwhelms the enemy.
7. Survivors submit.

This violent annihilation is an elaboration of the chronicles’ supernatural inundation. It became influential because medieval Hachiman writers used Jingū’s victory to prefigure Japan’s divine rescue from the Mongol fleets. Modern retellings that attribute the jewels directly to the *Kojiki* or *Nihon shoki* are demonstrably wrong.

**[E3/E6]** Mekari Shrine and local traditions at Kanju and Manju islands claim that the jewels, their substitutes or commemorative forms were deposited nearby. Hakozaki’s *tama-seseri* ball-taking rite has sometimes been explained through the jewels, but sources also give other origins. These are local cult etiologies, not proof that physical tide-control jewels existed.

### 3.11 Return and birth of Ōjin

**[E1: *Nihon shoki*]** Jingū returns from Silla and gives birth to Homuda in Tsukushi on the fourteenth day of the twelfth month. The place is called Umi because of the birth. The present Umi Hachimangū identifies itself as that site and venerates Jingū and Ōjin as mother-and-child deities, with particular emphasis on safe childbirth.

**[E1: *Kojiki*]** The birth takes place after the return; the child’s names include Homuda-wake and Ōtomo-wake. The text later describes Jingū brewing “waiting-liquor” for him.

**[E2/E4]** Ōjin’s later identification with Hachiman turns the pregnancy into the gestation of a god. Jingū consequently becomes both heroic mother and theotokos-like divine mother within Buddhist-Shintō Hachiman discourse.

### 3.12 Succession war

**[E1: both chronicles]** Chūai’s sons Kagosaka and Oshikuma see Jingū’s newborn as a threat and prepare an ambush.

- In the *Kojiki*, a boar uproots Kagosaka’s tree and devours him during an omen-hunt. Oshikuma fights on. Jingū’s commander Takefurukuma feigns surrender, has his men conceal spare bowstrings, then rearms and defeats the enemy.
- In the *Nihon shoki*, Kagosaka dies after an inauspicious hunt; Oshikuma and his commander Isachi are defeated by Takeuchi no Sukune and Takefurukuma. Oshikuma and Isachi drown themselves at Seta; their bodies later appear at Uji.

Jingū herself is politically decisive but does not personally fight the final battle in the surviving accounts.

### 3.13 Government, death and body

**[E1: *Nihon shoki*]** Ministers honor her as Grand Empress and reckon the year 201 as the first of her administration. Subsequent annals record diplomacy, tribute and court affairs. She dies in traditional 269 at age one hundred and is buried at Saki no Tatanami no Ike-no-e no misasagi.

**[E1: *Kojiki*]** Her rule is not a separate reign. A parenthetical note says she dies aged one hundred and is buried at Tatanami in Saki; classical commentators suspected this note was imported from the *Nihon shoki*.

There is no early narrative of apotheosis immediately after death, no authenticated body or relic, and no contemporary epitaph.

## 4. Relations with Gods and Other Heroes

### Genealogical alternatives

#### *Nihon shoki* line

Emperor Kaika  
→ an imperial descendant line  
→ Okinaga no Sukune no Miko + Katsuraki no Taka-nuka-hime  
→ **Okinaga-tarashi-hime / Jingū**

#### *Kojiki* maternal immigrant line

Ame-no-Hiboko  
→ Tajima-morosuku  
→ Tajima-hine  
→ Tajima-hinaraki  
→ Tajima-hitaka  
→ Taka-nuka-hime of Katsuraki + Okinaga no Sukune no Miko  
→ **Okinaga-tarashi-hime / Jingū**

The exact generations and spellings vary. **[E1]** Both are eighth-century court genealogies; neither is a verified biological tree.

### Consort and children

- **Emperor Chūai / Tarashi-nakatsu-hiko:** husband in both chronicles.
- **Homuda-wake / Emperor Ōjin / Hachiman:** son and intended successor.
- **Homu-ya-wake:** another child in the *Kojiki* genealogy; absent or differently handled in standard *Nihon shoki* presentations.
- **Kagosaka and Oshikuma:** Chūai’s sons by Ōnakatsu-hime and rivals to Jingū’s child, not Jingū’s sons.

## 5. Cult, Tomb and Ritual

### Mausoleum

**[E1]** The chronicles assign Jingū a tomb at Tatanami/Saki. The Imperial Household Agency designates the large keyhole-shaped Gosashi Kofun in the Saki tomb group, Nara, as **Saki no Tatanami no Ike-no-e no Misasagi**, her mausoleum.

**[Archaeological finding]** The mound is about 270–275 metres long and is conventionally dated to the late fourth or early fifth century. That date fits the era of large Yamato kofun but not the *Nihon shoki*’s death date of 269. The occupant is unknown. “Jingū’s tomb” is therefore an official attribution, not an archaeological identification.

In 2008 representatives of archaeological and historical societies received rare permission to enter the outer area for inspection. No burial chamber, inscribed body or object identifying Jingū was recovered. The archaeological mound and the literary hero’s grave-tradition must remain separate findings.

### Sumiyoshi worship

**[E1]** The *Nihon shoki* says the three Sumiyoshi gods accompanied the expedition and directed the establishment of worship for their rough spirits at Yamada in Anato. It also associates their gentle spirit with the royal vessel and later settlement at the Sumiyoshi locality.

**[E2/E3]** Sumiyoshi Taisha in Osaka now enshrines the three Sumiyoshi gods and Jingū as four principal deities. Shimonoseki Sumiyoshi Shrine associates itself with the rough spirits’ establishment, while Hakata Sumiyoshi Shrine incorporates Jingū in its broader shrine complex. These institutions preserve cultic interpretations of the chronicle; their claimed foundation dates cannot be treated as contemporary inscriptions.

### Hachiman worship

**[E2/E4]** At Usa Jingū, Jingū occupies the third sanctuary alongside Hachiman/Ōjin and Hime Ōkami. Numerous Hachiman shrines reproduce a triad of Ōjin, Jingū and a female deity, although some substitute Chūai, Tamayori-hime or Takeuchi no Sukune. The variation itself shows that “the Hachiman triad” was not a single immutable ancient arrangement.

Hachiman’s eighth-century rise as a state-protective deity and Buddhist bodhisattva eventually transformed Jingū from a dead imperial heroine into the divine mother of a universal protector. Medieval texts then used her overseas victory as Hachiman’s first paradigm of repelling or subduing foreign enemies.

### Childbirth cult

At Umi Hachimangū in Fukuoka, Jingū and Ōjin are worshipped as mother and child. **[E3]** The shrine identifies the area as Ōjin’s birthplace and maintains *koyasu-ishi*, “safe-childbirth stones,” which worshippers borrow or dedicate. This practice is a living religious development from the birth narrative; individual stones are not archaeologically authenticated relics.

Kashii-gū worships Chūai and Jingū and localizes Chūai’s death and Jingū’s oracular activity. Hakozaki-gū and numerous north-Kyūshū shrines preserve campaign, landing, fishing, stone, armor, banner or route traditions. Their density establishes the strength of regional remembrance, not the literal itinerary of a verified expedition.

### Matsura women’s fishing

**[E1]** Both chronicles expressly state that women of Matsura reenacted Jingū’s omen-fishing annually. This is the earliest described ritual attached directly to her. Whether the practice began with the legend, was absorbed by it, or supplied material from which the story was made cannot be determined.

### Tide-jewel and maritime rites

**[E3/E6]** Mekari Shrine traditions connect nearby Kanju and Manju islands with the deposited tide jewels. Hakozaki’s *tama-seseri* has been explained as commemorating the Dragon King’s presentation of the jewels, but the documentary record contains multiple etiologies. Such rites demonstrate the fertility of the medieval story, not an eighth-century possession by Jingū.

### Relics

Claimed relics include:

- pregnancy stones;
- a maternity sash or abdominal band;
- a spear or staff;
- helmets, bows or garments;
- tide jewels or their ritual substitutes;
- footprints and landing stones.

All are **[E3/E6]** unless supported by a datable object history. No surviving artifact has a contemporary inscription establishing personal ownership by Jingū.

## 6. Historical Evolution of the Figure

### I. Before 712: recoverable background, not a biography

**[E7]** The narrative may preserve memories of:

- female ritual authority comparable in broad form to the third-century Chinese description of Himiko;
- fourth-century Wa–Baekje alliance and conflict involving Silla and Goguryeo;
- court absorption of north-Kyūshū maritime traditions;
- Okinaga-lineage and immigrant genealogies;
- women’s Matsura ritual;
- succession struggles surrounding an early Yamato ruler later remembered as Ōjin.

No method securely recombines these into one historical queen.

### II. 712–720: court canonical formation

The *Kojiki* places the story within dynastic succession; the *Nihon shoki* expands it into an annalistic regency and embeds Chinese and Korean records. The resulting figure legitimates:

- the fetus who will become Ōjin;
- divine punishment for resistance to oracle;
- court supremacy over Kyūshū peoples;
- westward maritime dominion;
- the ritual authority of the imperial woman;
- the subordination of foreign rulers within a Yamato-centered world.

### III. Eighth–twelfth centuries: localization and cult incorporation

Provincial traditions attach Jingū to rivers, stones, roads and shrines. Hachiman’s rise gives her son a divine identity and her maternity cosmic importance. Sumiyoshi traditions cast her expedition as the foundational demonstration of maritime protection. Court and shrine genealogies increasingly coordinate what were once heterogeneous local traditions.

### IV. Late twelfth–fourteenth centuries: military-Buddhist transformation

The *Mizukagami*, Hachiman oracles, *Hachiman gudōkun*, shrine-origin scrolls and *Taiheiki* add:

- Azumi-no-Isora;
- Dragon Palace;
- ebb and flood jewels;
- deliberate drowning of foreign troops;
- assertions of Japan’s sacral superiority;
- typological linkage between Jingū’s victory and the defeat of the Mongol invasions.

This is the decisive shift from miraculous voyage and submission to magical naval battle.

### V. Fifteenth–eighteenth centuries: diffusion, print and historical argument

Hachiman scrolls and illustrated books disseminate the scene. Confucian historiography disputes whether a woman should count as sovereign. Mito scholarship’s *Dai Nihonshi* removes her from the numbered imperial line, helping establish the enduring category “regent.” Women’s biographies domesticate her as chaste wife and exemplary mother even while preserving her command.

Edo-period discussions of Korea used the conquest both to imagine old Japanese authority abroad and to debate relations with Chosŏn. It would be anachronistic to treat every early-modern retelling as Meiji colonial propaganda; the political meanings varied.

### VI. Meiji to 1945: national icon and imperial precedent

The restored imperial state made Jingū exceptionally useful:

- she embodied unbroken imperial genealogy;
- her conquest supplied an ancient precedent for Japanese authority in Korea;
- her divinely protected pregnancy joined expansion to dynastic motherhood;
- her gender could be rendered nonthreatening by emphasizing service to husband and son;
- her image could represent the state while avoiding a portrait of the living emperor.

Her story entered historical readers and visual culture. Colonial and military discourse invoked the “Three Han conquest” and related claims of ancient domination when rationalizing Japan’s expansion and the 1910 annexation of Korea. The ancient texts did not prescribe modern annexation; imperial ideologues selected and modernized them.

### VII. 1945–present: repudiation, ritual survival and reanalysis

Defeat discredited the conquest heroine in academic and public history. Textual criticism increasingly treated her as legendary or composite. She disappeared from official sovereign lists but remained in shrines, childbirth practice, local heritage, art collections, historical fiction and popular mythology.

Recent scholarship has reopened the subject through gender history, cult history, regional tradition, Korean–Japanese memory and image analysis—without requiring belief in a historical conquest. One important correction is that Meiji imagery did not simply make her increasingly warlike: some widely distributed portraits suppress combat and convert her into an imperial mother.

## 7. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Currency

**[E8]** The “Jingū notes” (*Jingū Kōgō-satsu*) were Japanese government paper money, not Bank of Japan notes. The first denominations were issued beginning in 1881; the Bank of Japan was not founded until 1882 and began issuing its own notes in 1885.

Italian engraver Edoardo Chiossone designed the portrait, reportedly using a contemporary woman associated with the Printing Bureau as a physical model because no historical likeness existed. Consequently the familiar face is a nineteenth-century fabrication.

Jingū is commonly described as the **first woman depicted on modern Japanese paper currency**. The qualification matters: she appeared on imperial-government notes, and the historical portrait was invented. The Bank of Japan’s Currency Museum records five denominations—10 yen, 5 yen, 1 yen, 50 sen and 20 sen—and dates the series from 1881. The one-yen note began circulation in January 1881; other denominations followed.

### Prints and painting

- **Tsukioka Yoshitoshi, 1879:** *The Fifteenth Generation: Empress Jingū*, from *Concise Illustrated History of Great Japan*. **[E8]** It shows her in an emphatically hybrid costume, including Victorian-style elements, inscribing a seaside rock with her bow while Takeuchi and followers watch. Calling her “fifteenth generation” preserves the older numbering later rejected.
- **Gankō/Gankyō, nineteenth century:** hanging-scroll images group Jingū, Takeuchi and the child Ōjin/Hachiman.
- **Nagano Yasunosuke, 1898:** an *ema* depicts Jingū, Takeuchi and infant Ōjin. **[E8]** This votive composition makes maternal dynastic continuity, rather than combat, the center.
- Medieval and early-modern Hachiman scrolls supplied the visual grammar: armored mother, infant, minister, ships, sea deities and tide jewels.

A quantitative iconographic study of 268 works dated between 1725 and 1999 found that her image repeatedly changed with gender norms and political conditions. The apparently timeless “warrior empress” is itself historically variable.

### Postage and state imagery

High-value Japanese stamps and related state graphics reproduced the Chiossone-type face. These were modern national portraits of a woman who had no recoverable physiognomy. State use made her recognizable while turning legend into apparent documentary history.

### Education and empire

Meiji and imperial school materials presented the expedition as national history. Colonial textbooks could frame intervention in Korea as assistance requested by ancient Japanese possessions or allies. The story supported a chain of assertions—ancient conquest, continuing affinity, restoration of an old order—that exceeded both the early chronicles and the evidence.

Modern nationalist websites and popular books still cite the Seven-Branched Sword, the Gwanggaeto Stele or fourth-century Wa warfare as if each independently proved Jingū’s personal conquest. This is **[E8: modern conflation]**. Those materials attest relations and warfare involving Wa; none names her.

### Literature, theatre and performance

Jingū never acquired a single dominant early-modern drama comparable to the canonical Yoshitsune or Sugawara plays. She nevertheless appeared in historical dance, kabuki-derived pageants, shrine performance, didactic biographies and illustrated books. A documented modern kabuki-dance program in 1979 includes a role explicitly named Jingū Kōgō. Such performances belong to continuing reception, not an ancient dramatic repertoire.

### Film, manga and games

She appears episodically in modern historical manga, myth anthologies and strategy or deity-collection games. These works characteristically select one of four models:

1. armored pregnant conqueror;
2. shaman-queen and possible Himiko;
3. divine mother of Hachiman;
4. tide-jewel-wielding sea heroine.

Where a game presents her as a literal goddess, dragon-summoner or historically established conqueror of all Korea, that classification is **[E8]**, albeit built from identifiable medieval motifs.

### Modern false antiquity

The following circulating claims are not early tradition:

- “The *Kojiki* says Ryūjin gave Jingū the tide jewels.” — False; the Jingū jewel episode is medieval.
- “The Seven-Branched Sword bears Jingū’s name.” — False.
- “Korean chronicles record Jingū conquering the peninsula.” — False.
- “Her banknote is a genuine portrait.” — False.
- “The officially designated kofun proves her existence.” — False; its occupant is unidentified.
- “Jingū is universally counted as Japan’s fifteenth emperor.” — False; the numbering was disputed and modern official lists exclude her.
- “She was Himiko.” — A historical hypothesis, not an attested identity.

## 8. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Historicity

From the nineteenth century through the imperial period, historians often assumed a historical Jingū while correcting the chronology. Early Western translator W. G. Aston recognized that the Korean sections were displaced and argued that some fourth-century diplomatic material had a factual nucleus.

Tsuda Sōkichi’s source criticism, especially from 1919–1930, treated the pre-Ōjin imperial narratives as late dynastic constructions rather than reliable biographies. Postwar scholarship broadly accepted that the canonical Jingū could not be treated as a verified ruler.

Naoki Kōjirō developed a composite-figure theory: Jingū’s image drew on later reigning empresses such as Suiko, Kōgyoku/Saimei and Jitō. **[E7]** This explains the eighth-century court model but does not by itself identify every narrative component’s origin.

Chizuko Allen argued in 2003 for reconsidering the possibility that traditions of a female shaman-ruler underlay the later construction. Her case emphasizes the combination of mediumship, rulership, north-Kyūshū geography and early patterns of female sacred authority. It is a revisionist historical reconstruction, not proof that the chronicle’s single protagonist lived.

### Jingū and Himiko

#### Case for connection

- The *Nihon shoki* places excerpts concerning the Chinese-recorded Wa queen within Jingū’s annals.
- Both figures are female rulers with sacred or mediumistic authority.
- The artificially lengthened chronology makes Jingū contemporary with Himiko.
- Both operate in traditions strongly connected to northern Kyūshū and overseas diplomacy.

#### Case against identity

- The Chinese sources name Himiko and Iyo/Toyo; the Japanese chronicles name neither in Jingū’s story.
- Himiko dies around 247/248, while Jingū’s annals continue to 269.
- Chinese sources describe internal Wa politics and diplomacy with Wei, not a conquest of Silla.
- Jingū is inserted into Yamato imperial genealogy as Chūai’s wife and Ōjin’s mother; Himiko’s kinship position is different and obscure.
- The *Nihon shoki*’s use of Chinese quotations may be the compilers’ synchronizing strategy rather than preserved identity.
- Other Japanese figures, including Yamato-totohi-momoso-hime and Yamatohime, have also been proposed as Himiko.

**Finding:** Jingū = Himiko is possible only as a modern reconstruction of literary formation; it is not a conclusion compelled by the evidence.

### Chronology and the “120-year correction”

Several Korean-related entries in Jingū’s annals align more plausibly when moved forward by two sexagenary cycles, or 120 years. The seven-branched sword episode placed at traditional 252 then approaches the late 360s/early 370s; Baekje rulers named in the annals also belong to the fourth century.

This correction demonstrates chronological manipulation in the *Nihon shoki*. It does not license moving every Jingū story forward exactly 120 years or identifying the corrected ruler as a historical Jingū. Narrative mythology, local traditions and diplomatic archives may have been combined at different stages.

### The Seven-Branched Sword

The inscription is difficult and damaged. Major readings date it to 369, though alternative dates such as 408 have been argued. Scholars also dispute whether its language implies a gift between equals, a Baekje grant to a subordinate Wa ruler, or diplomatic courtesy.

What can safely be said:

- it is a real Baekje-associated prestige weapon;
- it provides material evidence of fourth- or early fifth-century Baekje–Wa relations;
- the *Nihon shoki* records a comparable sword in Jingū’s annals;
- it neither names Jingū nor verifies her invasion.

### Was there a conquest of Korea?

There is no evidence for a single third-century Japanese conquest of Silla, Baekje and Goguryeo under Jingū. Fourth- and fifth-century sources do show warfare and alliances among Wa, Baekje, Gaya polities, Silla and Goguryeo. The Gwanggaeto Stele refers to Wa forces in the peninsula around the turn of the fifth century, but its damaged syntax, perspective and political language are contested.

The historically responsible formulation is therefore: **the Jingū conquest legend probably absorbed memories or records of real transmarine relationships and warfare, but the narrative of one pregnant empress subduing the “Three Han” is uncorroborated.**

### Tomb debate

The designated Gosashi Kofun’s late fourth-/early fifth-century archaeological date has encouraged attempts to connect it to a corrected Jingū. This is circular unless the occupant can be independently identified. No inscription or excavated assemblage names her, and restricted access has limited investigation.

### Hero-cult and gender debate

Older treatments often saw only a militarist warrior or a mother-goddess. Recent gender-history research shows that these images coexisted and were politically managed. Premodern texts could find female sovereignty admirable; Meiji ideology could celebrate her while recoding her as a wife acting for Chūai and a mother acting for Ōjin. Her banknote portrait’s femininity and stillness are therefore as ideological as armored battle pictures.

### Nationalism and scholarly reaction

Because Jingū was intensely used by empire, postwar scholars sometimes avoided the legend or reduced it to fabrication. More recent work by Yoshida Shūsaku, Sagara Mikako and others studies the full transmission—regional, medieval, visual and colonial—without validating imperial claims.

The opposite error now appears in some revisionist popular history: alleging that postwar criticism “suppressed” proof of Jingū. Archaeological evidence for ancient contact is then substituted for evidence of her identity. The fact that scholarship has political contexts does not make an unnamed sword or mound name Jingū.

### State of the evidence

- No contemporary Jingū inscription.
- No securely identified remains.
- Two early eighth-century court chronicles.
- Early-eighth-century provincial traditions preserved in much later manuscripts.
- Lost Korean/Baekje materials quoted selectively by the *Nihon shoki*.
- A real fourth-century diplomatic and military background.
- Abundant medieval Hachiman elaboration.
- Extensive shrine, ritual and image traditions.
- Modern ideological reuse on a national scale.

## 10. Sources

### Primary texts and editions

- *Kojiki*, completed 712. Relevant portions are the Kaika genealogy and Chūai sections XCV–CIII. Basil Hall Chamberlain’s translation is dated and sometimes tendentious but makes the full public-domain text accessible. Modern critical editions include Kurano Kenji et al., *Nihon koten bungaku taikei: Kojiki*, and the Shōgakukan *Shinpen Nihon koten bungaku zenshū* edition.
- *Nihon shoki*, completed 720, Books VIII–IX. W. G. Aston’s 1896 translation remains accessible; modern Japanese critical editions in the Iwanami and Shōgakukan series provide the necessary textual apparatus.
- *Harima no kuni fudoki* and *Hizen no kuni fudoki*, early eighth century, surviving in later manuscript transmission. Michiko Yamaguchi Aoki’s *Records of Wind and Earth* is a principal English translation of the extant *fudoki*.
- Chen Shou, *Sanguozhi*, “Account of the Wa” in the *Wei zhi*, late third century.
- Seven-Branched Sword inscription, Isonokami Shrine, conventionally dated 369 but disputed.
- *Hachiman gudōkun*, late thirteenth/early fourteenth century, A and B recensions.
- *Hachiman Usa-gū gotakusen-shū*, early fourteenth century.
- *Taiheiki*, fourteenth century.
- Hachiman origin scrolls and shrine histories.

### Selected scholarship

- Chizuko Allen, “Empress Jingū: A Shamaness Ruler in Early Japan,” *Japan Forum* 15.1 (2003), pp. 81–98.
- Tsuda Sōkichi, *Kojiki oyobi Nihon shoki no shin kenkyū* (1919) and subsequent studies of Japan’s ancient narratives.
- Ross Bender, *The Political Meaning of the Hachiman Cult in Ancient and Early Medieval Japan* (1980).
- Yoshida Shūsaku, *Yorikuru kotoba to denshō: takusen, Jingū Kōgō, chiiki* (2008).
- Sagara Mikako, studies of the formation and transformation of Jingū’s early-modern image.
- Melanie Trede, “Banknote Design as a Battlefield of Gender Politics and National Representation in Meiji Japan,” in *Performing “Nation”* (2008).
- Recent gender-history work reassessing Jingū’s iconography and the coexistence of martial, political and maternal types.
- Gina Barnes, *Himiko and Japan’s Elusive Chiefdom of Yamatai* (2007), for the archaeological and historiographical context of Himiko rather than proof of an identification with Jingū.
