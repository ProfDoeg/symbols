# Kaguya-hime: Research Dossier

> **Traditional class:** an otherworldly/celestial heroine (*tennin* or moon-being), temporarily embodied as a human foster-daughter; not a mortal hero, demigod, historical princess, or heroized dead. She has generated local legends and commemorative festivals, but no securely attested premodern cult.
>
> **Earliest secure attestation:** Kaguya-hime’s complete story is preserved in the anonymous *Taketori monogatari* 竹取物語, composed in the late ninth or early tenth century, certainly before its treatment as an established classic in the “E-awase” 絵合 chapter of *Genji monogatari* (ca. 1008). No manuscript near the date of composition survives: the oldest complete witness bears a 1592 colophon. An eighth-century *Man’yōshū* poem about a “Taketori no Okina” and the woman 仙媛 is possible antecedent material, but it does not name Kaguya-hime or narrate her story. [Japan Search](https://jpsearch.go.jp/en/gallery/ndl-rEG4P3boPx8), [National Institute of Japanese Literature](https://www.nijl.ac.jp/etenji/bungakushi/contents/detail/detail02-01_004.html), [University of Virginia Japanese Text Initiative](https://jti.lib.virginia.edu/japanese/taketori/index.html)

## Evidence labels

- **[EA] Secure early attestation:** Heian-period text or a demonstrably early reference to it.
- **[LC] Later canonical transmission:** medieval or early-modern witness preserving the received literary text.
- **[RV] Regional variant:** locally documented departure from that text.
- **[LS] Learned systematization:** antiquarian, philological, theological, or symbolic interpretation.
- **[MA] Medieval/early-modern accretion:** later retelling, illustration, or performative development.
- **[F] Folklore:** oral or locally presented traditional narrative whose date of origin is not securely fixed.
- **[MSR] Modern scholarly reconstruction:** inference rather than an explicit statement in an early source.
- **[MI] Modern invention or adaptation:** demonstrably modern elaboration, identification, or use.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

Kaguya-hime is the luminous miniature girl found by a bamboo cutter inside a bamboo internode. Reared on earth, she reaches adult size in approximately three months, receives the ceremonial name **Nayotake no Kaguya-hime**, rejects five aristocratic suitors through impossible treasure-quests, refuses incorporation into the emperor’s household, and finally returns involuntarily to the lunar capital. The received tale leaves her lunar offense unspecified. Its human episodes make her an active refuser of marriage, while its ending makes her subject to a superior celestial authority. **[EA]**

### Names

| Form | Script and transliteration | Meaning and evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Kaguya-hime | かぐや姫, *Kaguya-hime* | Conventional modern spelling, “Princess/Lady Kaguya.” *Hime* means a high-born young woman or princess. **[LC]** |
| Nayotake no Kaguya-hime | なよ竹のかぐや姫, *Nayotake no Kaguya-hime* | Full name bestowed by Mimuroto no Inbe no Akita: approximately “Kaguya-hime of the supple/slender bamboo.” **[EA]** |
| Kaguya-hime | 赫映姫, 輝夜姫 and other retrospective spellings | Graphic representations emphasizing radiance. The kana text does not establish one original kanji etymology. **[LC/MA]** |
| Kaguya-hime no monogatari | かぐや姫の物語 | “The Tale of Kaguya-hime,” a historical alternative title. **[LC]** |
| Taketori no Okina no monogatari | 竹取翁物語 | “The Tale of the Old Bamboo Cutter,” another expanded title. **[LC]** |

“Kaguya” is normally associated with brilliance—compare *kagayaku*, “to shine”—and the narrative repeatedly makes the child and her bamboo radiate. That semantic association is strong; derivations from a hypothetical root simultaneously meaning light and shadow remain philological proposals, not a gloss supplied by the tale. **[MSR]** Japanese lexicography also records debate over whether the older pronunciation was *Kakuya* or *Kaguya*. [Kotobank](https://kotobank.jp/word/%E3%81%8B%E3%81%90%E3%82%84%E5%A7%AB-43833)

### The *Kojiki* namesake

The *Kojiki* (712) names **Kaguya-hime-no-mikoto** 迦具夜比売命 as a daughter of Ōtsutsukitarine-no-miko and consort of Emperor Suinin. **[EA, separate figure]** She is not found in bamboo, does not confront five suitors, and does not return to the Moon. Similarity of name and later geographical arguments have encouraged identification with the literary heroine, especially in local-origin claims, but no early source makes that equation. It is therefore a **[LS/MSR] syncretistic identification**, not evidence that Kaguya-hime was a historical imperial consort. [Kokugakuin University’s *Kojiki* database](https://kojiki.kokugakuin.ac.jp/shinmei/kaguyohime/)

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### Antecedents and dating

1. **The *Man’yōshū*, poem 3791 (compiled in substantially final form in the eighth century):** its headnote introduces an old bamboo cutter, *Taketori no Okina*, who encounters nine women and exchanges a long poem with them. **[EA antecedent]** The shared designation may indicate a preexisting bamboo-cutter narrative complex, but the poem has no infant in bamboo, five suitors, lunar princess, or Fuji ending. It cannot be treated as an attestation of Kaguya-hime herself.

2. ***Taketori monogatari*, late ninth–early tenth century:** **[EA]** anonymous, in Japanese prose interspersed with *waka*. Suggested authors—including Ki no Haseo, Minamoto no Shitagō, Henjō, an Inbe author, or an anti-Tenmu political faction—are unsupported or disputed attributions. The names and ranks of several suitors resemble seventh-century courtiers, but that supports satirical or pseudo-historical construction, not eyewitness reporting. The University of Virginia provides the Japanese text based on a modern edited text. [Japanese Text Initiative](https://jti.lib.virginia.edu/japanese/taketori/index.html)

3. **Tenth-century intertexts:** *Yamato monogatari* and *Utsuho monogatari* contain material generally read as alluding to the tale. **[EA]** Their exact chronological relation to successive forms of *Taketori* remains a textual-historical question.

4. ***Genji monogatari*, “E-awase,” ca. 1008:** calls the bamboo cutter “the ancestor/parent of the coming-into-being of tales” (*monogatari no idekihajime no oya*). **[EA]** This proves that a *Taketori* narrative was already canonical at the eleventh-century court; it does not prove the exact wording of the 1592 witness. [NIJL](https://www.nijl.ac.jp/etenji/bungakushi/contents/detail/detail02-01_004.html)

5. ***Konjaku monogatarishū* 31.33, early twelfth century:** “How the Old Bamboo Cutter Found and Raised a Girl” abbreviates and alters the story. Its heroine is a nonhuman being who ultimately returns to the sky/heavenly realm rather than being described consistently through the received lunar geography. **[MA/variant textual tradition]** [Primary Japanese text](https://yatanavi.org/text/k_konjaku/k_konjaku31-33)

6. ***Kaidōki*, 1223:** refers to a Kaguya tradition in the Mount Fuji context. **[MA]** It helps document the medieval circulation of Fuji-centered versions, but does not authenticate any modern locality’s claim to be the historical birthplace.

### Manuscripts and editions

The archetype is lost. The oldest complete manuscript generally recognized is the former Mutō Motonobu manuscript in Tenri Central Library, with a colophon dated Tenshō 20 (1592). Early movable-type editions followed around 1596–1615. Consequently, more than six centuries separate composition from the oldest complete copy. **[LC]** [Japan Search](https://jpsearch.go.jp/en/gallery/ndl-rEG4P3boPx8)

Early-modern transmission included illustrated scrolls, luxury *Nara ehon*, and printed books. A late-seventeenth-century three-volume illustrated set in the Metropolitan Museum combines ink, color, gold and silver and retains the full bamboo-child, suitor, emperor, and lunar-return sequence. **[MA]** [Metropolitan Museum](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/45361) The Imperial Household collection likewise preserves an early-Edo *Taketori no Okina narabi ni Kaguya-hime emaki*. [Imperial Household Agency](https://shoryobu.kunaicho.go.jp/Toshoryo/Detail/1000275910002)

## 3. The story and its variants

### The luminous child

**[EA]** An old bamboo cutter—named in the received text as Sanuki no Miyatsuko/Marō, with textual variation in the first element—sees one bamboo shining at its base. Inside is a girl about three *sun* tall. He takes her home and places her in his wife’s care. Thereafter he repeatedly finds gold in bamboo, becoming wealthy. The child grows into a full-sized woman in roughly three months.

Nothing in this opening says that she was biologically born from the plant. The bamboo functions as her point of terrestrial manifestation. Modern “bamboo-born princess” summaries are reasonable shorthand, but literal botanical birth is not stated. **[MI simplification]**

### Naming and seclusion

**[EA]** The cutter summons Mimuroto no Inbe no Akita, who names her Nayotake no Kaguya-hime. A coming-of-age ceremony is celebrated. Her beauty becomes famous even though she is kept secluded; men gather around the house and eventually abandon their vigils except for five persistent aristocrats.

### The five suitors and the tasks

The received text associates the suitors with historically plausible names and court ranks. That gives the satire a courtly target but does not make their encounters historical. **[EA literary fiction; MSR historical models]**

| Suitor | Required treasure | Outcome in *Taketori monogatari* |
|---|---|---|
| Prince Ishitsukuri 石作皇子 | The Buddha’s stone begging bowl from India | He takes an ordinary soot-darkened bowl from a Japanese mountain temple. Its failure to shine exposes it; Kaguya rejects him. The episode produces wordplay concerning shamelessness and the bowl’s “radiance.” |
| Prince Kuramochi 車持皇子 | A jeweled branch from Mount Hōrai, with silver roots, golden stem, and jewels for fruit | He secretly commissions artisans to fabricate it, then narrates a false overseas adventure. The craftsmen arrive demanding unpaid wages and expose him. |
| Abe no Miushi/Mimura-ji 阿倍御主人 | The fire-rat’s robe from China, supposedly incombustible | He buys an expensive supposed robe through a foreign-trade intermediary. Kaguya tests it in fire; it burns, proving the fraud. |
| Ōtomo no Miyuki 大伴御行 | A five-colored jewel from a dragon’s neck | He orders retainers to obtain it, abuses them when they fail, and finally sails himself. A storm terrifies him into abandoning the quest; his men ridicule the enterprise. |
| Isonokami no Marotari/Maro 石上麻呂足 | The cowry shell carried by a swallow as a childbirth charm | He devises a hazardous retrieval operation in a storehouse, falls while reaching into a nest, grasps swallow dung rather than a shell, and is gravely injured. In the received account he dies after Kaguya sends a poem. |

The objects draw on Buddhist, Chinese, Daoist, maritime, dragon, and childbirth lore. Attempts to map all five systematically to the Five Phases (*wuxing*) are **[LS/MSR]**; published scholarship has not reached agreement on the correspondences. [J-STAGE study](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/seia/47/0/47_KJ00009759659/_article/-char/en)

Modern children’s versions commonly call all five “princes,” omit the craftsmen’s labor dispute, remove physical abuse, and let every suitor survive. Those are **[MI]** simplifications. Mika Saitō’s reception study also shows that modern manga and children’s adaptations enlarge the foster-mother’s role and often make Kaguya herself directly assign the tasks; in the premodern text the cutter conveys the demands after negotiating with her. [Saitō 2021](https://jll.pitt.edu/ojs/JLL/article/download/153/316)

### The emperor

**[EA]** The emperor hears of Kaguya and sends a court woman to inspect her. Kaguya refuses to appear. He then arranges to visit during a hunt and attempts to have her carried away. She becomes shadow-like or loses her visible substantiality, proving that she is not an ordinary terrestrial woman. He desists.

They exchange poems and letters for approximately three years. The text gives this relationship greater duration and mutuality than the suitor episodes, but Kaguya still refuses palace service or marriage. Statements that the emperor “won her heart” are interpretive; the source records affectionate correspondence and her farewell letter, not a consummated romance. **[EA with later romantic amplification]**

### Revelation, defense, and return

**[EA]** Near the full moon of the eighth month, Kaguya repeatedly weeps while looking at the Moon. She tells her foster-parents that she belongs to the lunar capital and was sent to earth because of an offense or karmic fault; the nature of that fault is never disclosed. Claims that she refused a lunar king, committed a named crime, or was sent to learn human feeling are **[MI/MSR]** unless tied to a particular adaptation.

The emperor sends an officer, Takano no Ōkuni, and two thousand guards. On the fifteenth night the lunar company arrives in overwhelming light. Doors open without human action; weapons and resistance become useless. A figure described as apparently their king or leader summons the cutter. He says Kaguya’s term of punishment is over and reproaches the old man’s grief by pointing to the wealth granted during her stay.

Kaguya writes farewell letters. She leaves clothing for her foster-parents and a letter and immortality elixir for the emperor. A celestial attendant offers her the elixir and then places the **feather robe** (*ama no hagoromo*) on her. She asks for time because the robe will erase or transform her earthly feelings, but it is put on and she loses worldly attachment. She departs in the celestial vehicle.

This is not a heroic death: no corpse, grave, relic, or apotheosis is described. Her departure reverses embodiment and earthly affiliation. **[EA]**

### Fuji and the elixir

**[EA]** The emperor, unwilling to live forever without her, asks what mountain is nearest heaven. He sends Kaguya’s letter and the elixir with Tsuki no Iwakasa and many warriors to the summit of Suruga’s mountain, where they are burned. The text plays on:

- *fushi* 不死, “deathlessness/immortality”; and
- *fuji* 富士 or the multitude of warriors (*fu* “abundant” + *shi* “warriors,” a literary pun).

The received ending explicitly says the smoke still rises. This is narrative aetiology, not reliable historical etymology: “Fuji” is attested independently, and the tale offers wordplay rather than linguistic derivation. The claim that volcanic smoke precisely dates composition is suggestive but insecure; literary “still smoking” need not be a contemporaneous observation. **[EA story; MSR dating argument]** A modern scholarly translation of the Fuji passage is reproduced by Chuo University. [Chuo University](https://www.chuo-u.ac.jp/uploads/2022/03/7479_THE_LITERATURE_OF_Mt.FUJI_Japanese_Classical_Literature.pdf)

### Major regional variant: Fuji’s immortal woman

Fuji City preserves/promotes a version in which Kaguya identifies herself not primarily as a moon princess but as an immortal woman or *sennyo* of Mount Fuji, ascends the mountain, and disappears there. **[RV/F]** This reverses the literary geography: Fuji changes from the place where humans burn her rejected immortality to her own supernatural home. Fuji City links the version to the medieval *Fuji engi* tradition and local names such as Hina/Himena and Kaguyahime, but the locality’s modern “birthplace” campaign is not proof that this version antedates *Taketori monogatari*. [Fuji City](https://www.city.fuji.shizuoka.jp/4005230000/p003475.html), [Fuji Kaguya-hime Museum](https://museum.city.fuji.shizuoka.jp/exhibition/detail/292)

## 4. Relations with divine beings and other heroes

### Kinship and genealogy

The tale supplies no personal name for Kaguya’s biological parents, no divine genealogy, no spouse, and no children. The cutter and his wife are foster-parents. The lunar ruler and company exercise jurisdiction over her but are not called her parents. **[EA]**

The *Kojiki* genealogy—Ōtsutsukitarine → Kaguya-hime-no-mikoto, consort of Suinin—belongs to the separate namesake. Importing it into the bamboo-cutter story creates a modern or learned synthetic pedigree. **[LS/MI]**

## 5. Cult, tomb, and ritual

### Negative finding

No securely documented ancient or medieval Kaguya-hime cult, *heroon*, grave, relic, sacrificial rite, oracle, or shrine foundation appears in the early textual record. The story supplies no dead body and therefore no tomb. Searches for a “Kaguya-hime tomb” yield local commemorative or origin traditions rather than an archaeologically identified burial. **[Absence of evidence]**

### Local commemorative landscapes

- **Hiro陵/Kōryō, Nara:** promotes itself as Kaguya-hime’s home through the cutter’s disputed name *Sanuki no Miyatsuko*, Sanuki Shrine, nearby place-names, and the *Kojiki* namesake’s Yamato genealogy. **[LS/RV/modern heritage construction]** The municipality itself distinguishes oral tradition from the authored literary tale. [Kōryō Town](https://www.town.koryo.nara.jp/0000000019.html)
- **Fuji City:** preserves Taketorizuka (“Bamboo-Cutter Mound”), Kaguyahime/Himena place-name traditions, museum displays, the Fuji-return narrative, and modern Kaguya-branded festivities. **[F/RV/MI commemoration]**
- **Other claimants:** Kyoto’s Kyōtanabe and Mukō, Sanuki in Kagawa, Kurashiki, Takehara, and Satsuma have participated in modern Kaguya-hime origin networks or “summits.” Their mutually incompatible claims are evidence of reception and regional identity-making, not multiple archaeological attestations of one historical woman. **[MI heritage politics]**

These activities are civic folklore, tourism, and commemoration—not proof of worship in the technical sense.

## 6. Historical evolution

### Eighth century: possible narrative material

The *Man’yōshū* bamboo-cutter poem and older heavenly-maiden, found-child, magical-wife, immortality, and impossible-task motifs supply comparanda. **[EA antecedents/MSR]** They do not reconstruct a complete oral “original.”

### Late ninth–early tenth century: literary synthesis

*Taketori monogatari* combines vernacular prose and poetry, folktale patterns, court satire, Chinese-Buddhist-Daoist treasure lore, marriage politics, and an extraterrestrial or celestial departure. **[EA]** The five suitors’ courtly ranks and quasi-historical names make elite pretension and failed masculine acquisition central to its comedy.

### Eleventh–thirteenth centuries: canonization and abbreviation

*Genji* canonized the work as an ancestor of fictional tales. *Konjaku* converted it into a shorter exemplary narrative and blurred “Moon” into a more general heavenly realm; *Kaidōki* participated in Fuji-centered recollection. **[EA/MA]**

### Sixteenth–nineteenth centuries: manuscript, image, print

The received text survives in late manuscripts and movable-type editions. Illustrated handscrolls and *Nara ehon* made its set pieces—glowing bamboo, treasure presentations, imperial guards, lunar procession—visually canonical. The Met’s late-seventeenth-century volumes and numerous early-Edo copies demonstrate elite pictorial reception. **[LC/MA]**

Tsukioka Yoshitoshi’s 1888 print in *One Hundred Aspects of the Moon* helped define the modern pictorial Kaguya: the tiny radiant figure emerging from bamboo under the cutter’s gaze. **[MI reception]**

### Meiji to 1945: children’s tale and national education

European-language translations began in the late nineteenth century; F. V. Dickins’s *The Old Bamboo-Hewer’s Story* appeared in English in 1888. **[MI translation history]** The tale was progressively abridged and standardized for children. National-school textbook treatment turned the difficult court satire and ambiguous lunar punishment into a recognizable fairy tale. Research on wartime teaching shows that textbook “Kaguya-hime” participated in militarized national education while simultaneously fixing the modern children’s-story form. **[MI/political appropriation]** [Saitō study of wartime pedagogy](https://icu.repo.nii.ac.jp/record/5400/files/ACS48_05%E6%96%89%E8%97%A4.pdf)

Unlike Momotarō, Kaguya-hime did not become a comparably direct mascot of imperial conquest. Claims of a major autonomous “Kaguya nationalist cult” are not supported by the evidence located; her political use was primarily curricular, imperial-centered, and mediated through school canon formation.

### Postwar to present: agency, ecology, gender, and space

Postwar retellings increasingly center Kaguya rather than the cutter, strengthen her foster-mother, psychologize her punishment, and interpret her marriage refusal through feminist or anti-patriarchal frameworks. **[MI]** The tale’s lunar origin also made her an unusually apt emblem for Japanese space science.

## 7. Modern reception and reinvention

### Film and performance

- **1935 – *Kaguya-hime*:** Yoshitsugu Tanaka’s live-action musical, with cinematography and special effects by Eiji Tsuburaya. A shortened print was rediscovered decades later. **[MI]**
- **1987 – *Taketori monogatari* / *Princess from the Moon*:** Kon Ichikawa’s science-fiction-inflected film recasts the celestial machinery in cinematic terms. **[MI]**
- **1994 – *Sailor Moon S: The Movie*:** borrows the snow/lunar princess complex rather than reproducing the medieval text. **[MI]**
- **Modern Noh:** “Kaguya-hime” exists as a **new Noh**, not as a securely attested medieval item in the classical repertory. It dramatizes her anguish and lunar return using Noh conventions. **[MI]** [Performance description](https://www.nohbutai.com/contents/05/02ka/1kaguyahime.htm)
- **2014/2015 – Numajiri Ryūsuke, *Bamboo Princess*:** modern Japanese opera, first concert performance in 2014 and staged in Japan in 2015. **[MI]** [Biwako Hall](https://www.biwako-hall.com/performance/bambooprincess1124)

No evidence located establishes an old canonical Kabuki or medieval Noh “Kaguya-hime” equivalent to the major traditional repertoires named elsewhere in the brief. Modern productions that mix Noh, Kabuki, dance, opera, or contemporary theatre should be labeled new compositions.

### Takahata’s Kaguya

Isao Takahata’s *Kaguya-hime no monogatari* (Studio Ghibli, released 23 November 2013) is the most influential modern reinterpretation. It retains the bamboo discovery, rapid growth, wealth, five tasks, emperor, lunar retrieval, robe, and farewell, but adds a rustic childhood, the boy Sutemaru, Kaguya’s forced courtly education, emotional revolt, attempted escape, and an explicit longing for earthly embodied life. Sutemaru is **[MI]**, not a lost ancient lover. The film makes the Moon’s painless purity oppressive and human transience precious—an interpretation compatible with the old ending but not explicitly stated by it. [Studio Ghibli](https://www.ghibli.jp/works/kaguyahime/)

The film’s loose brushwork and unfinished-line aesthetic evoke handscroll and ink-painting traditions without reproducing a particular ancient Kaguya manuscript. It received an Academy Award nomination and wide international recognition; its modern characterization should not be retrojected into the Heian text.

### The lunar probe

Japan’s SELENE lunar explorer was officially nicknamed **KAGUYA**, while its two subsatellites were called **OKINA** and **OUNA**, after the old man and woman of the tale. **[MI scientific commemoration]** Launched on 14 September 2007, KAGUYA conducted global lunar observations and was deliberately impacted on 11 June 2009 after mission completion. Its “return to the Moon” consciously restaged the narrative at the level of public symbolism; it did not encode a belief that the tale records ancient space travel. [JAXA mission history](https://global.jaxa.jp/projects/sas/selene/topics.html), [JAXA final images](https://global.jaxa.jp/press/2009/06/20090619_kaguya_hdtv_e.html)

### Comics, animation, and games

- *Kaguya-sama: Love Is War* (manga 2015–2022) uses Kaguya and suitor/task names as a modern naming matrix; it is not a continuation of the old narrative. **[MI]**
- *Touhou Project*’s Hourai/Kaguya material turns the immortality elixir and lunar exile into an elaborate game cosmology. **[MI]**
- *Naruto*’s Kaguya Ōtsutsuki borrows name, lunar/celestial associations, and bamboo-related name play but is a newly invented antagonist and progenitor. **[MI]**
- Numerous anime, manga, games, advertisements, tourist mascots, and commercial products use “Kaguya” as shorthand for a beautiful, inaccessible, or lunar woman. These are reception evidence, not variants of the premodern text.

## 8. Historiography and contested material

### Historicity

There is no contemporaneous chronicle, inscription, administrative record, or excavated object identifying the literary Kaguya-hime as a human person. The text presents her as nonhuman and belongs to fictional *monogatari*. Connections to the *Kojiki* consort or to a local ancient princess are **[MSR/LS]**, built from name resemblance and place-name interpretation. They have not displaced the literary classification.

### Date and authorship

The usual late-ninth/early-tenth-century date rests on language, intertextual relations, institutional references, the historical coloring of the suitors, and recognition by later Heian works. Volcanic-smoke dating is weaker. The anonymous author’s sex, faction, and identity remain unknown; the common reconstruction of a male, Chinese-literate court intellectual is plausible but not directly attested. **[MSR]**

### Sources of the plot

Three positions recur:

1. **Indigenous oral-tradition emphasis:** found-child, bamboo, heavenly maiden, marriage-test, and local Fuji materials developed within Japan.
2. **Continental-source emphasis:** Hōrai, fire-rat cloth, Buddhist relics, dragon jewels, immortality medicine, and mid-autumn lunar imagery derive from Chinese, Buddhist, and broader Asian narrative reservoirs.
3. **Literary-synthesis position:** an author deliberately combined multiple inherited motifs with invented court satire.

The third best fits the mixed evidence, but no lost oral archetype or single Chinese source has been demonstrated. Similar Tibetan or Chinese bamboo-girl stories are comparanda; resemblance alone does not establish the direction or date of borrowing. **[MSR]**

### The suitors as historical persons

Several names approximate figures around Emperor Tenmu’s court, and three or more have often been assigned historical “models.” This is useful evidence for satire and dating, not proof that historical nobles pursued an actual Kaguya. The fictive Prince Kuramochi has, for example, been connected with Fujiwara no Fuhito through reconstructed correspondences; this remains **[MSR]**, not an explicit identification in the text.

### Kaguya’s offense

The lunar leader says her punishment has ended, but the text never identifies the offense. Theories—refusing a lunar ruler, violating celestial law, needing to acquire compassion, or choosing earthly desire—are **[MSR or MI]**. Takahata turns this blank into a psychological and ethical center; that is powerful reception, not recovered ancient doctrine.

### Marriage refusal and gender

The story plainly depicts repeated male attempts to acquire or compel Kaguya and her successful resistance until the lunar authority overrides her. Modern feminist readings emphasize her refusal of aristocratic marriage and the emperor. Other scholars stress that she remains constrained by foster-father, court, and lunar patriarchy. Both observations arise from the text; labeling her a modern feminist heroine without qualification is **[MI interpretive reframing]**.

### Cult and tomb claims

No verified premodern shrine or excavated tomb has been connected to the literary heroine. Taketorizuka, Sanuki Shrine associations, Kaguya place-names, and “birthplace” campaigns document local reception. Their age must be established individually; the existence of an old mound or shrine does not itself date its Kaguya explanation. **[F/LS/MI]**

### Fuji etymology

The tale’s *fushi/fuji* and “many warriors” explanations are literary puns. Treating either as modern historical linguistics is discredited. **[EA aetiology, not factual etymology]**

### “Ancient astronaut” claims

Assertions that Kaguya-hime records an alien visitation, spacecraft landing, or prehistoric lunar mission are modern pseudohistory. The narrative uses celestial vehicles, overpowering light, immortality medicine, and a lunar polity, but no ancient source frames these as technological reportage. JAXA’s spacecraft name is reception, not confirmation. **[MI]**
