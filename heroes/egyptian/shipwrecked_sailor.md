# The Shipwrecked Sailor: Research Dossier

> **Traditional class:** Anonymous mortal protagonist and royal expeditionary official; a literary survivor-hero, not a demigod, epic king, culture hero, or cult-receiving heroic dead. “The Shipwrecked Sailor” is a modern descriptive title, not his personal name or an ancient title.
>
> **Earliest secure attestation:** Papyrus Hermitage 1115, formerly called Papyrus St Petersburg or Leningrad 1115, written in hieratic Middle Egyptian and generally dated palaeographically and linguistically to the early or middle Twelfth Dynasty, approximately 2000–1900 BCE. It is the only surviving manuscript of the story. James P. Allen calls it the oldest surviving Egyptian story; a physically lost opening remains possible. [Securely attested early material; modern scholarly dating]

## Evidentiary key

- **[Early]** Securely attested Middle Kingdom evidence in Papyrus Hermitage 1115.
- **[Later attestation]** A possible ancient allusion or reuse later than the surviving manuscript.
- **[Reconstruction]** Modern philological, historical, geographical, literary, or religious interpretation.
- **[Comparandum]** A separate work compared typologically; no direct dependence is established unless expressly stated.
- **[Modern reception]** A modern translation, adaptation, illustration, or educational use.
- **[Modern invention]** A detail absent from the ancient manuscript or an unsupported presentation of conjecture as fact.
- **[Negative finding]** A requested category for which no evidence has been identified.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Identification

**[Early]** The protagonist is an unnamed Egyptian official designated in the framing narrative as a *šmsw*—conventionally “follower,” “attendant,” or “retainer.” He addresses an unnamed *ḥꜣty-ꜥ*, a high official and expedition leader, after their party has returned past Wawat and Bigga at Egypt’s southern frontier. The official fears the royal audience awaiting him. The follower therefore tells how, on an earlier voyage to the sovereign’s mining country, he alone survived a shipwreck, reached a marvellous island, encountered a gigantic divine serpent, received a prophecy and gifts, and returned to royal reward. The narrative is consequently not an independently narrated “life” of a sailor but an exemplary first-person story embedded in a courtly frame. [Allen, *Middle Egyptian Literature*, 2015; UCL Digital Egypt]

**[Early]** The protagonist’s heroic quality is endurance, persuasive speech, ritual propriety, and successful return—not divine descent or martial conquest. The serpent’s central maxim is that one who perseveres will embrace his children, kiss his wife, see his home, and die in his own town. [Papyrus Hermitage 1115, lines 132–136]

### Ancient designations

- **šmsw**, commonly transliterated *shemsu*: “follower,” “attendant,” or royal retainer. The manuscript’s heading begins *ḏd in šmsw iqr*, approximately “Recitation/speech by an able follower.” Allen describes *šmsw* as a low-ranking royal-attendant title. It is an office, not a name. [Early]
- **šmsw iqr**: “able,” “excellent,” or “competent follower.” This is the nearest thing to an epithet applied to him in the text. [Early]
- **z**, “man,” and ordinary first-person pronouns occur in speech; no personal name, patronymic, birthplace, or genealogy is supplied. [Early; negative finding]
- The labels **“Shipwrecked Sailor,” “Shipwrecked Mariner,” “Naufragé,” “Schiffbrüchiger,” “Потерпевший кораблекрушение,”** and equivalents are modern editorial titles derived from the adventure. No ancient title is preserved. [Modern scholarly convention]
- Calling him simply a **“sailor”** is convenient but socially reductive: in the frame he is a royal follower and participant in state expeditions, not identified by the Egyptian occupational title for an ordinary shipman. [Reconstruction]

### Other important names and expressions

- **jw pn n kꜣ**, *iw pen en ka*: “this island of ka.” The noun *kꜣ* can denote a person’s vital force and is associated with nourishment. Gardiner interpreted the phrase as suggesting an unreal or “phantom” island; Claude Vandersleyen and Allen emphasize the primary sense of life-sustaining vitality because the island contains everything necessary for life. “Island of the Soul,” “Island of the Spirit,” “Island of the Ka,” and “Enchanted Island” are therefore interpretive translations, not exact synonyms. [Early phrase; disputed modern interpretation]
- **wꜣḏ-wr**, *Wadj-wer*, literally “Great Green,” is the body of water crossed in the earlier voyage. Whether this denotes the Red Sea specifically in this passage is debated; the mining expedition and Punt-like products make an eastern or southern maritime setting plausible, but the tale does not provide coordinates. [Early; reconstruction]
- The serpent calls himself **ḥqꜣ Pwnt**, “ruler/lord of Punt.” Punt is named, but the island is not straightforwardly identified as Punt: the serpent distinguishes his own abundance from the sailor’s proposed gifts and says that he possesses the myrrh associated with Punt. [Early]
- **Ameny son of Amenaa**, conventionally *Imny sꜣ Imn-ꜥꜣ*, appears only in the colophon as the “scribe excellent of fingers.” He is the copyist claimed by the manuscript, not the sailor and not securely the story’s original author. The precise vocalization and even interpretation of the names vary among editions because Egyptian normally omits vowels. [Early colophon; philological convention]

### Syncretisms and identifications

**[Negative finding]** The manuscript never names the serpent as Re, Atum, Amun, Osiris, or another specific god. His beard, golden body, lapis-lazuli brows, supernatural knowledge, and the language of *bꜣw* (“manifest power/impressiveness”) identify him as divine or godlike; assigning him a particular divine identity is modern reconstruction.

**[Reconstruction]** The serpent’s seventy-five-member family has been compared with the seventy-five invocations or forms associated with Re in the New Kingdom *Litany of Re*. This can illuminate the story’s learned religious number-symbolism, but the later funerary text does not name the sailor or prove that the serpent “is Re.” [John Baines, 1990; UCL Digital Egypt]

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Papyrus Hermitage 1115

**[Early]** The sole manuscript is Papyrus Hermitage 1115 in the State Hermitage Museum, St Petersburg. Its archaeological provenance is unknown. Vladimir Golénischeff identified it in the Imperial Hermitage collection around 1880, presented the work at the Fifth International Congress of Orientalists in Berlin in 1881, issued an early account and translation, and published major editions in 1912–1913. Adolf Erman produced a hieroglyphic transcription in 1906. [Modern recovery and publication history]

**[Early]** The manuscript contains 189 numbered textual positions. Columns 1–123 are vertical; lines 124–176 run horizontally across six pages; columns 177–189 return to vertical writing. Black ink carries most of the text, with red ink marking many divisions. The change of direction occurs as the serpent begins its story within the sailor’s story. Its purpose is unknown. [Allen 2015]

**[Reconstruction]** Grammar and palaeography support approximately 2000–1900 BCE. Some summaries call it an early Twelfth-Dynasty copy; UCL describes it more broadly as a late Middle Kingdom papyrus. These formulations should not be silently harmonized: absolute dating of Middle Kingdom literary hands is imprecise, and date of composition need not equal date of the surviving copy. Andréas Stauder’s linguistic work supports an early Middle Egyptian literary setting, while exact regnal attribution remains unproved.

**[Early/contested preservation]** The story begins abruptly with the able follower speaking. Andrey Bolshakov’s physical examination argued that the manuscript is incomplete and that an introduction has been lost. Others have treated the abrupt opening as deliberate *in medias res*. The extant first column is therefore secure; whether it was originally the composition’s first column is disputed. [Bolshakov 1993; Allen 2015; UCL Digital Egypt]

**[Early]** The colophon says that the text has come “from its beginning to its end, as found in writing,” copied by the “scribe excellent of fingers, Ameny son of Amenaa.” That formula claims faithful copying. It does not demonstrate authorship, eyewitness testimony, or historicity. The colophon may also play on *iqr*, “excellent/able,” used at the story’s beginning of the follower. [Papyrus, columns 186–189; Rendsburg 2000]

### Full primary corpus for this figure

1. **Papyrus Hermitage 1115**, Middle Egyptian in hieratic, approximately 2000–1900 BCE or somewhat later within the Middle Kingdom: the only complete narrative source and only source that depicts the protagonist directly. [Early]
2. **A Ramesside text discussed by William Kelly Simpson in 1958**, which may allude verbally to both *The Shipwrecked Sailor* and *The Eloquent Peasant*. Because this is an allusion rather than another copy, it supports later familiarity with phrasing but supplies no new deed, name, genealogy, or variant biography. [Later attestation]
3. **Another probable later citation identified by James P. Allen (2008)** is noted by Allen’s 2015 edition. Again, it is evidence for textual afterlife, not an independent story-cycle. [Later attestation]

### The surrounding Egyptian corpus

The works named in the brief establish Egyptian literary context but do not add episodes to this protagonist:

- **The Story of Sinuhe**, Middle Egyptian, composed in the Middle Kingdom and surviving in numerous Middle and New Kingdom copies: another first-person return narrative concerned with foreignness, royal favor, homecoming, and burial in Egypt. [Comparandum]
- **The Eloquent Peasant**, Middle Kingdom composition: an elaborate demonstration of effective speech before authority. [Comparandum]
- **Papyrus Westcar**, Berlin 3033, copied in the Second Intermediate Period in Middle Egyptian: court tales about marvels and kingship. It does not mention the sailor. [Comparandum; negative finding]
- **The Tale of Two Brothers**, Papyrus d’Orbiney, New Kingdom/Late Egyptian: a different wonder tale with named protagonists Anubis and Bata. It does not mention the sailor. [Comparandum; negative finding]
- **Wenamun**, Papyrus Pushkin 120, early first millennium BCE copy of a Late Egyptian narrative: a troubled official voyage involving trade, diplomacy, and foreign vulnerability. It does not mention the sailor. [Comparandum; negative finding]
- **The Demotic Setne Khamwas stories**, Ptolemaic and Roman-period manuscripts: learned tales about Prince Khaemwaset, magic, books, death, and retribution. Neither the texts nor Khaemwaset’s monuments at Saqqara document the shipwrecked sailor. [Comparandum; negative finding]
- **Herodotus, Book II**, fifth century BCE, and the fragments attributed to **Manetho**, third century BCE, do not name or recount this hero. They cannot be used to supply his history. [Negative finding]

### Principal editions and translations

- Golénischeff, *Le conte du Naufragé* (1912) and photographic publication of Hermitage papyri (1913).
- A. M. Blackman, *Middle-Egyptian Stories*, Part I (1932).
- Miriam Lichtheim, *Ancient Egyptian Literature*, vol. I (1973).
- William Kelly Simpson et al., *The Literature of Ancient Egypt* (successive editions beginning 1972).
- Richard B. Parkinson, *The Tale of Sinuhe and Other Ancient Egyptian Poems 1940–1640 BC* (1997).
- John L. Foster, *The Shipwrecked Sailor: A Tale from Ancient Egypt* (1998).

## 3. The Story

### A. The framing expedition

**[Early]** The preserved text opens after an expedition has returned safely to the southern frontier. The follower tells the high official to steady his heart: the mallet has been taken up, the mooring post struck, the prow-rope set ashore, thanksgiving offered, and the crew returned without loss. They have passed Wawat and Bigga. Yet the leader apparently faces danger because the mission has failed or because his report to the king may be badly received. The exact failure is not described in the surviving text—possibly because the opening is missing. [Columns 1–21]

**[Early]** The follower recommends measured speech at court: let the official answer when addressed and speak without stammering, because “a man’s mouth saves him.” The official initially rebuffs him. The sailor responds by offering a precedent: what happened to him on an earlier royal mission. [Columns 11–23]

### B. Voyage and shipwreck

**[Early]** The follower says that he was journeying to the sovereign’s mining country and descending or sailing upon the Great Green. His vessel was 120 cubits long and forty cubits wide and held 120 sailors “of the select of Egypt.” They had seen sky and land and were more stout-hearted than lions; they could predict storm before it arrived. [Columns 23–38]

**[Early]** A gale nevertheless arose before landfall. The wind repeatedly lifted waves eight cubits high. A piece of timber or wave saved the speaker, but the ship died and none of its occupants survived besides him. No divine agent is named as causing the storm. [Columns 31–41 and repeated account at 89–110]

**[Variant in the same text]** The sailor tells the shipwreck twice—first to his superior and then to the serpent. The repeated versions are closely related but not mechanically identical. Gary Rendsburg treats the patterned repetition and variation as deliberate literary workmanship. [Early textual variation; modern literary analysis]

### C. Three days alone and the island’s abundance

**[Early]** A wave carries him to an island. He spends three days alone, his heart as his companion, sheltering beneath vegetation. He then searches for food and finds figs, grapes, vegetables, green and ripe sycamore figs, melons, fish, and birds: “there was nothing that was not inside it.” [Columns 39–52]

**[Early]** After eating, he makes fire with a fire-stick and offers a burnt offering to “the gods.” The divinities are plural and unnamed. The offering precedes the serpent’s appearance but the text does not explicitly say that it summons him. [Columns 52–56]

### D. The golden serpent

**[Early]** A noise like thunder follows. Trees crack, the earth shakes, and the sailor first thinks a wave of the Great Green is approaching. He uncovers his face and sees a serpent thirty cubits—about 15.7 metres—long, with a beard longer than two cubits. Its body is plated or covered with gold; its brows are genuine lapis lazuli. It bends forward over him. Beard, gold, lapis, gigantic scale, and supernatural speech are divine attributes. [Columns 56–66; Allen 2015]

**[Early]** The serpent asks who brought him. Unless the sailor answers, it threatens to make him know himself as ashes or as one “who is not seen.” The terrified sailor cannot answer and loses consciousness. The serpent carries him unharmed in its mouth to its dwelling, places him down, and repeats the question. The carrying is not a biting or attempted consumption; the sailor later says nothing was taken from him. [Columns 67–88]

**[Early]** The sailor recounts the shipwreck. The serpent replies that “the god” caused him to live and brought him to this “island of ka,” full of every good thing. The god is grammatically singular but unnamed; the serpent does not say “I brought you.” He predicts that the sailor will remain four months before a ship crewed by men he knows arrives. The sailor will return home and die in his own town. [Columns 111–123]

### E. The serpent’s bereavement

**[Early]** The serpent then supplies the third narrative level. He once lived on the island with siblings and offspring, seventy-five serpents in total. He also mentions a “little daughter” whom he had obtained or who had come to him; the difficult wording does not securely establish that she was a human visitor, a divine daughter, or a separate species. [Lines 124–129]

**[Early]** A star descended, and the serpent’s family rose or burned in fire while he was absent. He found them as one heap of corpses and says that he “died for them,” an expression of devastating grief rather than a literal death followed by resurrection. [Lines 129–132]

**[Modern invention]** Retellings sometimes say the falling star killed only the girl, that the girl herself fell from heaven, or that she was “turned to ashes.” The ancient syntax instead places the serpent’s family as the victims of the descending star/fire; the little daughter’s precise relation to that disaster remains textually difficult. Astronomical identification with a historical meteorite or comet is unsupported.

**[Early]** The serpent converts his loss into consolation: if the sailor perseveres and controls his heart, he will embrace his children, kiss his wife, see his house, and live among his family. His counsel mirrors the follower’s attempt to console the expedition leader. [Lines 132–136]

### F. Promise, laughter, and the island’s disappearance

**[Early]** The sailor prostrates himself and promises to report the serpent’s power to the king. He will send oils, resins, temple incense, sacrificial animals, and a ship laden with Egypt’s riches, treating the serpent as a foreign divine ruler worthy of royal exchange and cultic gifts. [Lines 136–148]

**[Early]** The serpent laughs. The sailor is not rich in myrrh, it says; the serpent is lord of Punt and possesses the island’s incense. Only one promised oil may be lacking. More decisively, once the sailor departs he will never see the island again: it will become water. [Approximately lines 149–160]

**[Translation history]** “Become water” is the secure claim. “Sink,” “vanish,” “move away,” “turn into waves,” and “phantom island” are translations or narrative expansions. The papyrus does not describe the physical process from an external narrator’s viewpoint. [Early phrase; modern renderings]

### G. Rescue, gifts, and royal reward

**[Early]** As predicted, the sailor sees a ship approaching and recognizes its crew. He climbs a tree to observe it, tells the serpent, and receives a farewell blessing: he is to return home, see his children, and make the serpent’s name good in his town. [Lines 160s]

**[Early]** The serpent supplies a cargo of aromatic products and luxuries, including myrrh, oils, aromatic woods or resins, eye-paint, giraffe tails, ivory, hounds, monkeys, baboons, and other valuables. Exact identifications of several Egyptian commodity terms remain uncertain. [Lines 163–169]

**[Early]** The sailor prostrates himself and praises the island’s lord. Once aboard, he looks back or gives praise from shore; the prediction that the island cannot be revisited frames the departure. The voyage home lasts two months. [Lines 169–176]

**[Early]** He presents the cargo to the king. He is appointed a follower and receives servants or dependants. This creates a circularity: the frame calls him a follower, while his remembered adventure ends with his appointment to that status. Whether this is literal career history or crafted narrative closure cannot be determined. [Lines 174–180]

### H. The failed consolation

**[Early]** Returning to the frame, the follower asks the high official to listen because listening benefits people. The superior answers, approximately: “Do not act too clever, my friend. Does one give water to a bird at dawn on the morning when it is to be slaughtered?” The tale ends immediately, followed by the colophon. [Lines 180–189]

**[Reconstruction]** The ending has two principal readings:

1. The superior rejects the consolation: a successful survival story cannot help a man expecting punishment before the king.
2. The superior criticizes its timing or rhetorical excess rather than its truth; consolation offered too late is useless.

The manuscript does not report the superior’s royal audience, punishment, pardon, or death.

## 4. Relations with Gods and Other Heroes

### Genealogy and household

**[Early]** No parentage, ancestry, birthplace, or childhood is supplied. The sailor says that he has a wife, children, and siblings or kin at home, but none is named, numbered, or made an independent character. [Negative finding]

**[Early]** His ship had 120 sailors; the rescuing vessel contains sailors he knows. None is named. The superior, king, serpent, and serpent’s family are likewise anonymous. [Negative finding]

### Every documented interaction with another named divine being or hero

| Other figure | Interaction | Source and date | Evidence tier |
|---|---|---|---|
| **None** | No named god, goddess, mythic hero, legendary king, or other named heroic figure interacts with the sailor in the surviving tale. | Papyrus Hermitage 1115, approximately 2000–1900 BCE | **Secure negative finding** |

For completeness, the following interactions are real but fail the “named being” criterion:

- **The great serpent:** interrogates, threatens, physically carries, consoles, prophesies to, hosts, enriches, and dismisses the sailor. The serpent is divine in appearance and is “lord of Punt,” but has no personal name. [Early]
- **“The god” (*nṯr*):** according to the serpent, saves the sailor and brings him to the island. The deity is singular but unnamed. [Early]
- **“The gods” (*nṯrw*):** receive the sailor’s burnt offering; no individual deity is specified. [Early]
- **The unnamed king:** receives the sailor and his cargo and rewards him. No pharaoh’s throne name or personal name occurs. [Early]
- **The unnamed high official:** hears and rejects or qualifies the sailor’s consolation. [Early]

**[Modern invention]** Identifying the unnamed king as Amenemhat I, the serpent as Re-Atum, the little daughter as Maat, or the sailor as a historical expedition leader creates famous pairings that the manuscript does not state.

## 5. Cult, Tomb, and Ritual

### Cult and tomb

**[Negative finding]** No temple, *heroon*, tomb, relic, priesthood, festival, game, oracle, sacrifice, votive object, theophoric name, or commemorative place is attested for the shipwrecked sailor. No excavated burial has been connected to him. The requested searches for “cult” and “tomb” return interpretation of the tale and unrelated shipwrecked sailors, not evidence for worship of this figure.

**[Negative finding]** The sailor is not described as becoming divine, receiving posthumous offerings, or founding a cult. The serpent foretells an ordinary desired Egyptian fate: death in his own town, where proper burial would be possible.

### Ritual inside the story

- **Burnt offering:** the sailor makes fire and gives an offering to the unnamed gods after finding food. [Early]
- **Prostration:** he lies on his belly and touches the ground before the serpent, treating it as a divine or royal superior. [Early]
- **Promised temple commodities:** he offers oils, resins, incense, animals, and royal shipment of valuables. [Early]
- **Praise at departure:** he praises or thanks the island’s lord. [Early]
- **Royal presentation:** he hands the serpent’s gifts to the king and is rewarded. [Early]

**[Reconstruction]** These actions use recognizable Egyptian ritual and courtly behavior, but a literary depiction of ritual is not evidence that Egyptians historically worshipped either character.

## 6. Historical Evolution of the Figure

### Middle Kingdom emergence, approximately 2000–1900 BCE

**[Early]** The figure enters the record fully formed in one sophisticated manuscript. The nested construction—official hears sailor, sailor hears serpent—links three threatened speakers and three uses of narrative consolation. The work belongs beside *Sinuhe* and *The Eloquent Peasant* as elite Middle Egyptian literature concerned with speech, royal service, danger abroad, and restoration at home.

**[Reconstruction]** Historical expedition practice underlies the fiction. Ships, crews, mines, Wawat, exotic cargoes, and Punt trade belong to the political economy of Middle Kingdom royal expeditions. Archaeological work at Red Sea ports such as Wadi Gawasis has strengthened the historical plausibility of long-distance state voyages. It does not identify the sailor or prove his island existed.

### New Kingdom afterlife

**[Later attestation]** Simpson identified a Ramesside verbal allusion to *The Shipwrecked Sailor* and *The Eloquent Peasant*. Allen notes two probable later citations in total. These indicate some textual afterlife, but the contrast with *Sinuhe*, preserved in many school copies and ostraca, is striking: no second manuscript of this tale survives.

**[Reconstruction]** The New Kingdom *Litany of Re* supplies a numerical-theological comparandum for seventy-five serpents/forms. Direction of borrowing is not demonstrable from the shared number alone.

### Late, Greco-Roman, and medieval Egypt

**[Negative finding]** Neither Herodotus II, surviving Manethonian fragments, Demotic narrative cycles, nor Coptic literature is known to continue the sailor as a named character. There is no documented ancient canonization, saint-like transformation, euhemerization, or regional cult.

**[Comparandum]** The *Odyssey*, conventionally shaped in the late eighth or seventh century BCE, shares storm, shipwreck, solitary survival, marvellous islands, supernatural hosts, first-person storytelling, gifts, and homecoming. These are genuine motifs for comparison. No surviving intermediary or verbal correspondence proves that Homer borrowed this tale.

**[Comparandum]** The voyages of Sindbad entered the Arabic *Thousand and One Nights* tradition much later, probably through layered medieval transmission. Shipwrecks, islands, monsters, wealth, and return make Sindbad a useful analogue. Calling the Egyptian sailor “the ancestor of Sindbad” is a metaphor for typological priority, not a demonstrated genealogy.

### Nineteenth-century rediscovery

**[Modern scholarship]** Golénischeff’s recognition and publication transformed an isolated papyrus into a widely translated work of “world literature.” The modern hero-title dates from this editorial afterlife, not ancient Egyptian naming practice.

### Twentieth and twenty-first centuries

**[Modern reception]** Anthologies by Lichtheim, Simpson, Parkinson, Foster, Quirke, and Allen established the story in university teaching. Digital hieroglyphic editions now permit line-by-line comparison, while illustrated children’s books recast the anonymous official as a friendly adventure hero.

## 7. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Editions, literature, and visual retellings

- **1895:** W. M. Flinders Petrie included an English retelling in *Egyptian Tales*. Its accessible narrative helped establish the work among English readers. [Modern reception]
- **1973:** Lichtheim’s *Ancient Egyptian Literature*, vol. I, provided a standard scholarly English translation. [Modern reception]
- **1997:** Parkinson published a literary translation in *The Tale of Sinuhe and Other Ancient Egyptian Poems*. [Modern reception]
- **1998:** John L. Foster’s *The Shipwrecked Sailor: A Tale from Ancient Egypt*, illustrated by Lyla Pinch Brock, treated it as an illustrated poetic work. [Modern reception]
- **2000:** Tamara Bower’s *The Shipwrecked Sailor: An Egyptian Tale with Hieroglyphs* retold and illustrated it in an Egyptianizing visual style. Bower calls the place the “Island of the Soul,” identifies the serpent as “Prince of Punt,” and makes sailor and serpent friends—effective adaptations, but not exact reproductions of the manuscript’s terminology or initially threatening encounter. [Modern reception and invention]
- **1997:** The educational computer game *Nile: An Ancient Egyptian Quest* included a narrated retelling among its historical and mythological sequences. [Modern reception]
- **2012:** Joyce Tyldesley’s *Stories from Ancient Egypt* included a children’s retelling. [Modern reception]
- **Online audio and teaching editions:** Storynory, Reading Is Fundamental, university lesson plans, and digital Egyptian-language corpora continue its circulation. [Modern reception]

### “Oldest sea-marvel tale”

**[Qualified reconstruction]** Allen calls it the oldest surviving Egyptian story. It is also one of the earliest surviving extended shipwreck-and-marvellous-island narratives. “The world’s oldest sea story,” “first fantasy,” or “first adventure story” are genre-dependent promotional formulations, not archaeological facts: earlier myths and voyage motifs exist, while the boundaries of “story,” “fantasy,” and “sea tale” are modern.

### Psychological and structural readings

**[Reconstruction]** The tale readily fits the later hero-journey sequence—departure, liminal isolation, supernatural encounter, gift, return—but applying Campbell’s twentieth-century monomyth does not show that the Egyptian author possessed that theory.

**[Reconstruction]** Rite-of-passage interpretations treat the wreck, island, initiation by the serpent, gifts, and reintegration as separation, liminality, and return. The pattern is illuminating but remains a modern analytic model.

**[Reconstruction]** Baines rejects a simple one-to-one allegory. He reads the work as complex written literature drawing on elite religious knowledge while playing with oral, anonymous, proverbial, and folk-like forms. The work can support theological, courtly, didactic, comic, and pessimistic readings without any one exhausting it.

### Politics, nationalism, and extremist appropriation

**[Negative finding]** The dedicated searches identified no documented nationalist or extremist movement organized around the shipwrecked sailor himself. Broader Afrocentric, Egyptian nationalist, or regional claims about Punt sometimes recruit the tale as evidence for national antiquity, especially in modern arguments locating Punt in a particular present-day state. Those debates concern the geography and ownership of ancient heritage, not an ancient political cult of the hero.

**[Modern invention]** Online claims that the falling star records a specific impact event, that the serpent is an extraterrestrial or dinosaur, or that the tale proves direct descent into Homer, Sindbad, or biblical serpent stories are unsupported by the manuscript and lack an established transmission chain.

## 8. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Date and manuscript state

**[Consensus with limits]** The language and hand place the manuscript in the Middle Kingdom, usually around 2000–1900 BCE. A more exact reign is not secure. Composition could precede the surviving copy.

**[Contested]** Bolshakov argued from physical examination that the beginning is lost. The abrupt initial grammar also prompted debate, but deliberate dramatic opening remains possible. The papyrus’s assertion that it runs “from beginning to end” belongs to the colophon and does not by itself settle whether the physical roll later lost material.

### Prose or verse

**[Contested]** Foster and Allen scan the composition as narrative verse organized chiefly in couplets, with tercets and other groupings. Other scholarship has resisted imposing metrical or verse segmentation on Egyptian prose. The red divisions, parallelism, repetitions, and stress patterns are real; the modern categories “prose poem,” “verse,” and “prose” do not map perfectly onto Middle Egyptian practice.

### Oral folktale or elite written literature

**[Contested]** Anonymous characters, formulaic repetition, a huge talking animal, patterned numbers, and a marvel-island resemble folktale. Yet the nested architecture, wordplay, court ideology, expedition terminology, and esoteric religious imagery imply expert scribal composition. Baines’s influential synthesis is that the text is sophisticated written literature which adopts and manipulates oral or folk-like presentation.

### Meaning of *jw pn n kꜣ*

- **Gardiner:** a “phantom” or unreal island. [Historical scholarly interpretation]
- **Vandersleyen/Allen:** an island of *ka* as vital sustenance, explicitly correlated with its inexhaustible food. [Modern philological interpretation]
- **Funerary/theological readings:** a liminal afterlife landscape, primeval mound, or place outside normal geography. [Reconstruction]
- **Geographical readings:** a fictionalized Red Sea or Punt-world location grounded in expedition experience. [Reconstruction]

These readings need not be mutually exclusive at the literary level, but none licenses placing the island on a modern map.

### Identity of the serpent

**[Early]** The serpent is a divine being and ruler of Punt; the manuscript supplies no personal name.

**[Reconstruction]** Solar identification draws on gold and lapis divine flesh, beard, fiery family catastrophe, the number seventy-five, and the island’s emergence/disappearance. Proposals include solar creator, primordial god, or composite divine ruler. The text’s deliberate anonymity prevents secure identification.

### Geography and historicity

**[Early]** Wawat and Bigga occur in the frame; the earlier destination is the king’s mining region; the serpent is lord of Punt; the cargo resembles southern and Red Sea trade goods.

**[Reconstruction]** These details anchor the fiction in genuine expedition culture. They do not make the narrative an expedition report. No named commander, dated reign, harbour inscription, shipwreck deposit, or island archaeology corroborates the episode.

**[Negative finding]** No historical individual has been persuasively identified behind the protagonist. Historicizing him as an officer under Sankhkare Mentuhotep III or Amenemhat I extrapolates from periods of expedition activity.

### The serpent’s daughter and falling star

**[Contested philology]** Translation of the “little daughter” clause is difficult. She may be one member of the family, a specially mentioned child, or an outsider brought to the serpent. Retellings that turn her into a heavenly maiden exceed the text.

**[Reconstruction]** The falling star can function as divine fire, celestial catastrophe, or literary image of uncontrollable loss. Meteorite and comet explanations are possible in nature but unverified historically.

### The ending

**[Contested]** Optimistic readings emphasize that storytelling, perseverance, and correct speech saved the sailor. Pessimistic readings emphasize that his story fails to help the superior. Ironical readings see the follower’s excessive cleverness undermining his own maxim about apt speech. The superior’s bird proverb intentionally prevents uncomplicated moral closure.

### State of the evidence

**[Secure]** One manuscript; unknown excavation provenance; no ancient illustration; no associated archaeological context; no independently named protagonist; possibly two later textual echoes; no cult evidence.

**[Modern scholarly problem]** Allen noted in 2015 that the original papyrus still lacked a fully satisfactory modern publication and worked from Golénischeff’s reproductions, including his record of red ink. Digital transcriptions are useful but remain dependent on earlier facsimiles and editorial judgments.

### Evidentiary conclusions

**[Secure]** The complete ancient record of the protagonist consists of one Middle Kingdom narrative manuscript plus probable later verbal echoes. He has no ancient personal name, genealogy, birth narrative, cult, tomb, relic, iconography, independent historical attestation, or documented interaction with a named god or hero.

**[Secure]** The tale itself records a royal follower surviving shipwreck, meeting an anonymous divine serpent on the Island of Ka, hearing the serpent’s catastrophe, receiving a prediction and Punt-like gifts, returning to Egypt, and gaining royal reward.

**[Reconstruction]** Solar theology, rite of passage, expedition ideology, afterlife geography, primeval-island symbolism, oral folktale background, and links with the *Odyssey* or Sindbad are interpretive frameworks. Several are persuasive contextual readings; none changes the single-source evidentiary foundation.

**[Modern invention]** A named pharaoh, a named sailor, a securely identified serpent-god, an excavated island or tomb, a historical meteor impact, direct descent into Homer or Sindbad, and an ancient cult of the sailor are not documented.
