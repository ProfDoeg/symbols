# The Two Brothers (Anpu and Bata): Research Dossier

**Traditional class:** In *The Tale of the Two Brothers*, Anubis/Anpu and Bata begin as apparently mortal farming brothers, but Bata possesses divine strength, converses with gods, dies and revives repeatedly, changes bodily form, and becomes pharaoh; Anubis restores the dead and ultimately succeeds him. They are therefore best classified as **semi-divine folktale heroes and royal culture-heroes whose names and functions draw upon two locally associated gods**, not as historical kings with recoverable biographies.

**Earliest secure attestation of the pair as story-heroes:** Papyrus d’Orbiney, British Museum EA 10183, a nineteen-column Late Egyptian hieratic manuscript copied by the scribe Inena/Ennana in the later Nineteenth Dynasty, approximately 1215 BCE. It is the sole surviving ancient copy and may be close in date to the composition. The papyrus’s paleography, associated manuscripts, colophon and an appended reference to the future Seti II place it around the Amenmesse–Seti II transition. Its likely ancient provenance is Saqqara, although older cataloguing also associated it broadly with Memphis. [British Museum](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/Y_EA10183-6); [Thesaurus Linguae Aegyptiae](https://thesaurus-linguae-aegyptiae.de/object/PE3MKOGYSJAXXEPOYMROUIJ3UA)

## Evidentiary key

- **[E] Secure early attestation:** the Ramesside manuscript itself or evidence demonstrably preceding or contemporary with it.
- **[L] Later Egyptian attestation:** later pharaonic, Demotic, Ptolemaic or Roman evidence.
- **[V] Variant or regional tradition:** an independently attested local or narrative variant.
- **[S] Scholarly reconstruction:** a modern interpretation, comparison or historical hypothesis rather than an explicit ancient statement.
- **[F] Folklore classification or later folklore:** a modern comparative category or demonstrably later oral/literary analogue.
- **[M] Modern reception or invention:** a modern edition, adaptation, conflation or unsupported claim.

## 1. Identification, Names and Epithets

### The brothers

**[E]** The manuscript opens: two brothers have “the same mother and the same father”; the elder is **Anubis**, the younger **Bata**. Anubis owns a household and has an unnamed wife; Bata lives with him “in the manner of a son,” makes clothing, ploughs, harvests and tends cattle. Bata is exceptionally beautiful or physically excellent and has “the strength/virility of a god” in him. The text thus deliberately blurs human and divine status without stating that the brothers are incarnated gods. [Lichtheim translation](https://staffsites.sohag-univ.edu.eg/uploads/316/1540445761%20-%20Lichtheim,%20Two%20Brothers.pdf)

### Anubis / Anpu

- **Egyptian:** conventionally transliterated **jnpw** or **ỉnpw**; older popular Egyptological vocalizations include **Anpu**, **Anup**, **Inpu** and **Anupu**.
- **Greek:** Ἄνουβις, **Anoubis**, source of English **Anubis**.
- **[E]** The d’Orbiney manuscript uses the Egyptian divine/personal name conventionally rendered “Anubis.” “Anpu” is not a preserved ancient vocalization but a modern Egyptological reading.
- **[S]** The etymology of *jnpw* is unresolved. Proposed connections with royal-child, decay or putrefaction vocabulary have not produced a secure consensus and should not be presented as settled.
- **[E]** No patronymic is supplied: only “elder brother,” “brother of the same father and mother,” and functional descriptions.
- **[E]** In the story he is never explicitly titled “god Anubis.” His actions nevertheless echo the mortuary god: he finds Bata’s displaced heart, reconstitutes him with water, and restores life.
- **[S]** Calling the character a “euhemerized Anubis” is an interpretation. The name and mortuary parallel support it, but the text’s domestic setting and human grammar leave deliberate ambiguity.

### Bata

- **Egyptian:** normally transliterated **Bꜣtꜣ**, often printed **Bata**, **Bataï**, **Bati**, **Batu** or older **Bitau**.
- **[E]** The text’s distinctive epithet is **“Bata, Bull of the Ennead”**, spoken by the assembled gods. This simultaneously marks potency, bovine association and divine favor.
- **[E]** Bata later becomes an extraordinary bull, but the narrative does not say merely that he is “the bull-god Bata in disguise.”
- **[S]** The name’s etymology remains uncertain. Attempts to derive it directly from *bꜣ*, “ba/spirit,” are attractive beside the detachable-heart episode but are not linguistically established.
- **[S]** Older scholarship sometimes identified Bata with an Old Kingdom deity written *Bt* and even claimed First-Dynasty evidence. The readings and identity are disputed; they cannot displace Papyrus d’Orbiney as the first secure attestation of **this narrative hero**.
- **[L]** A divine Bata, lord of Saka, appears in the religious traditions of Middle Egypt and in the Ptolemaic Papyrus Jumilhac. The latter depicts Bata as a bull carrying the mummy of Osiris and identifies him in a Sethian mythic complex. This is evidence for a god bearing the same name, not a second copy of Bata’s folktale biography. [Louvre catalogue](https://collections.louvre.fr/en/ark:/53355/cl010003518); [“Bata, maître de Saka”](https://www.persee.fr/doc/rhr_0035-1423_1949_num_136_1_5664)

### Collective and editorial titles

- **[E]** The ancient opening has no surviving formal title equivalent to the modern one.
- **[M]** *The Tale/Story of the Two Brothers*, *Anpu and Bata*, *Das Zweibrüdermärchen*, *Conte des deux frères* and *The Brothers Bata and Anubis* are modern editorial titles.
- **[M]** “The oldest fairy tale in the world,” made especially prominent by Susan Tower Hollis, is a modern promotional description, not an ancient genre label. It is also contestable because other early narrative and magical tales predate the papyrus.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Papyrus d’Orbiney

**[E]** British Museum EA 10183 consists of ten mounted frames preserving the sole known copy. It is written in literary Late Egyptian hieratic, with rubrics in red and the main text in black. The colophon names Inena/Ennana as writer or owner and associates the work with treasury scribes Kagab and Hori/Hari. It threatens anyone who maligns the book with opposition from Thoth. A secondary notation referring to Seti-Merenptah while still crown prince helps place the copy shortly before or around Seti II’s accession. [British Museum](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/Y_EA10183-6); [TLA catalogue and bibliography](https://tla.digital/object/PE3MKOGYSJAXXEPOYMROUIJ3UA)

The manuscript does **not** establish:

- that Anpu or Bata were historical brothers;
- that Bata was an actual viceroy or pharaoh;
- that the story records a particular royal scandal;
- that its surviving copy was a temple ritual script;
- or that every motif originated in the Nineteenth Dynasty.

### Ancient primary corpus

1. **Papyrus d’Orbiney, P. BM EA 10183** — Late Egyptian; late Nineteenth Dynasty, approximately 1215 BCE. **[E]** Complete narrative source for the brothers.

2. **Papyrus Jumilhac, Louvre E 17110** — Middle Egyptian religious composition in a Ptolemaic manuscript, 332–30 BCE. **[L/V]** Contains local religious mythology of the seventeenth and eighteenth Upper Egyptian nomes, including Anubis, Osiris, Seth and a bull-form Bata. It helps establish the independent divine background of the names but does not retell d’Orbiney’s plot. [Louvre](https://collections.louvre.fr/en/ark:/53355/cl010003518)

3. **Earlier and contemporary attestations of the gods Anubis and Bata** — **[E/S]** Anubis is abundantly attested from the Old Kingdom onward. Evidence for Bata as a local bull deity becomes clearer in the New Kingdom, including Ramesside material, but proposed much earlier occurrences remain philologically disputed. These records concern divine cult, not the two story-characters as a paired heroic biography.

### Texts named in the comparative brief but not part of the brothers’ corpus

- **The Shipwrecked Sailor, Sinuhe and the Eloquent Peasant** — Middle Egyptian compositions conventionally assigned to the Middle Kingdom, though surviving manuscripts may be later. **[E, contextual]** They illuminate Egyptian narrative prose, exile, return, eloquence and divine marvels; neither Anpu nor Bata appears.
- **Westcar Papyrus** — composition often placed in the Middle Kingdom, surviving in a Second Intermediate Period manuscript. **[E, contextual]** Royal wonder tales and miraculous births offer genre parallels, not an appearance by the brothers.
- **Wenamun, Papyrus Pushkin 120** — late New Kingdom/early Third Intermediate Period manuscript. **[E, contextual]** Its Levantine setting is relevant to Egyptian literary geography; it contains neither brother.
- **Setne Khamwas stories** — Demotic narratives in Ptolemaic and Roman manuscripts. **[L, contextual]** They show later Egyptian supernatural fiction and the transformation of a historical prince into a legendary magician. Neither Anpu nor Bata participates.
- **Herodotus, *Histories* II**, fifth century BCE — **[L, absence]** No Anpu-and-Bata tale.
- **Manetho**, third century BCE, surviving only in later excerpts — **[L, absence]** No securely preserved notice of the brothers.
- **Khaemwaset’s Saqqara monuments** — historical/archaeological evidence for Ramesses II’s son, relevant to the later Setne hero but not evidence for Anpu and Bata.

The distinction matters: the named works form a history of Egyptian prose and legendary narrative, but expanding them into an “Anpu and Bata cycle” would be a modern invention.

### Editions and translations

- Émile de Rougé announced and substantially analyzed the text in **1852**.
- The British Museum purchased it from Elizabeth d’Orbiney in **1857**.
- Samuel Birch’s *Select Papyri in the Hieratic Character*, part II (**1860**), published facsimiles.
- Charles E. Moldenke produced a hieratic/hieroglyphic edition and translation in **1898**. [Digitized edition](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:The_tale_of_the_two_brothers%2C_a_fairy_tale_of_ancient_Egypt%3B_the_d%27Orbiney_papyrus_in_hieratic_characters_in_the_British_Museum%3B_the_hieratic_text%2C_the_hieroglyphic_transcription_%28IA_cu31924026909618%29.pdf)
- Alan H. Gardiner’s *Late-Egyptian Stories* (**1932**) remains the foundational hieroglyphic transcription.
- Miriam Lichtheim, *Ancient Egyptian Literature*, vol. II (**1976 edition history beginning 1970s**), and Edward Wente in William Kelly Simpson’s *The Literature of Ancient Egypt* provide standard English translations.
- Susan Tower Hollis’s monographs (**1990; expanded 2008**) integrate philology, religion and folklore.
- Wolfgang Wettengel (**2003**) proposed a detailed Ramesside ideological interpretation.

## 3. The Story

Everything in this section is **[E] Papyrus d’Orbiney, approximately 1215 BCE**, unless another tier is stated.

### Household and attempted seduction

Anubis has a house and wife; Bata performs the household’s agricultural labor. His cattle speak and guide him to good pasture. During ploughing, Anubis sends him home for seed. Anubis’s unnamed wife notices Bata carrying five sacks of grain, praises his strength, seizes him and proposes that they spend an hour in bed; she promises fine clothing.

Bata becomes furious “like an Upper Egyptian leopard.” He refuses because she is like a mother and Anubis like a father. He promises not to expose her.

### False accusation and attempted fratricide

Fearing disclosure, the woman smears herself with fat or grease to imitate injuries, leaves the house dark, and pretends to vomit. She reverses the encounter: Bata, she claims, propositioned and beat her. She tells Anubis that she will die if Bata lives.

Anubis sharpens or takes his spear and waits behind the stable door to kill Bata without questioning him. Two cattle warn Bata. He sees Anubis’s feet and flees.

### Divine judgment and self-castration

Bata appeals to **Pre-Harakhty/Re-Horakhty** to distinguish wicked from just. The god creates a crocodile-filled body of water between the brothers. At sunrise Bata states his case and swears by the sun god.

He then cuts off his penis with a reed knife and throws it into the water, where a catfish swallows it. He collapses in weakness. The act is not a euphemism: it is self-mutilation intended as an extreme proof of sexual innocence and a severing of his former domestic and reproductive identity.

Anubis grieves. Bata instructs him to return home, tend the cattle and watch for a life-token: fermenting beer and spoiled wine will announce Bata’s death. Anubis kills his wife and throws her body to the dogs. The tale supplies no trial, speech of defense or burial for her.

### The Valley of the Pine/Cedar and the external heart

Bata leaves for the **Valley of the Pine**—often rendered “Valley of the Cedar,” a literary Levantine landscape. He announces that he will place his heart atop the tree’s flower or cone. If the tree is felled, Anubis must search for the heart and put it in cool water.

**[S]** Translators differ between pine and cedar because of botanical and lexical problems. “Cedar flower” is conventional, but the text need not describe a scientifically exact species or literal blossom.

Bata hunts, builds a mansion and establishes a solitary household. The Ennead encounters him and pities him. Re-Horakhty orders Khnum to make him a wife. Khnum fashions a woman more beautiful than any other, with the “fluid” or essence of every god in her. The Seven Hathors immediately predict: “She will die by the knife.”

Bata tells her his heart’s location and warns her not to leave the house because the sea may seize her; he says that he cannot rescue her because he is “a woman like you,” an explicit reference to his mutilation.

### The sea, hair and Pharaoh

The anthropomorphic sea pursues the woman. A tree catches a lock of her hair, which the sea carries to Egypt. Its divine perfume clings to Pharaoh’s laundry. Learned royal scribes identify it as belonging to a daughter of Re-Horakhty containing the essence of every god.

Pharaoh sends expeditions. Bata kills the first party except for one messenger. A larger force, including charioteers and a woman supplied with jewelry, successfully brings Bata’s wife to Egypt. Pharaoh makes her **Great Lady**.

Whether she goes voluntarily is not stated clearly enough to erase the coercive setting: an armed royal expedition removes her after Bata kills the first envoys.

### First death and resurrection

At Pharaoh’s prompting, the woman reveals that Bata can be killed by cutting the tree containing his heart. Soldiers fell it; Bata dies immediately.

Anubis receives the life-token: beer ferments excessively and wine turns bad. He travels to the valley, finds Bata’s corpse and searches beneath the tree for more than three years. In the fourth year he finds the heart in a fruit. Cool water revives it; Bata’s body twitches. Anubis gives him the water to drink, the heart returns to its place, and the brothers embrace.

The story describes restoration through the heart, not preservation of a disembodied “soul” in the later European sense. “External soul” is the comparative folklorist’s label.

### The bull

Bata transforms himself into a magnificent bull. Anubis rides him to Pharaoh, receives silver and gold, and returns home. Pharaoh honors the bull with offerings.

In the kitchen, Bata reveals himself to his former wife: she has killed him, but he lives as the bull. She extracts an oath from Pharaoh and demands the bull’s liver. Despite distress, Pharaoh orders a great sacrifice. Bata is slaughtered.

**[E]** This is genuine animal sacrifice inside the story. It is not evidence for a historical festival in which Egyptians sacrificed “Bata.”

### The two persea trees

As the dead bull is carried away, two drops of blood fall beside Pharaoh’s portal and grow overnight into two great **persea** trees. Pharaoh makes offerings to them.

One tree tells the Great Lady that it is Bata and recalls her betrayals. She again obtains an oath and asks that both trees be cut down for furniture. As she watches, a splinter flies into her mouth. She swallows it and immediately becomes pregnant.

### Rebirth, kingship and judgment

She bears a son whom Pharaoh loves. He appoints the boy Viceroy of Kush and then crown prince. When Pharaoh “flies to heaven,” the reborn Bata becomes king.

Bata assembles the officials, reveals everything and has the woman judged. The surviving wording says the officials assent but is damaged or terse concerning the exact execution; many translations infer that the Seven Hathors’ prediction of death by the knife is fulfilled. The dossier should therefore distinguish **secure judgment** from **probable but textually less explicit execution**.

Bata summons Anubis and appoints him heir or prince of the entire land. Bata reigns thirty years, dies, and Anubis succeeds him “on the day of death.”

### Structural development

The narrative falls into two connected complexes:

1. the false accusation, separation and vindication of the brothers;
2. the external heart, divinely made wife, serial deaths and royal rebirth.

**[S]** Scholars have therefore proposed composite growth from originally independent tales. Others emphasize verbal links, life-tokens, repeated betrayal and the brothers’ final succession as evidence of deliberate unity. Since only one ancient copy survives, neither an original oral form nor separate written sources can be reconstructed securely.

## 4. Relations with Gods and Other Heroes

### Family and social tree

**Papyrus d’Orbiney [E]:**

```text
unnamed father + unnamed mother
│
├── Anubis/Anpu — husband of unnamed first wife
│
└── Bata — husband of divinely fashioned unnamed woman
           │
           └── reborn as that woman’s son by Pharaoh’s household
```

The woman is simultaneously Bata’s wife, betrayer and gestational mother. Pharaoh treats the reborn child as his son, but the child is generated by a splinter of Bata rather than ordinary paternity. The story gives Bata no independently born child.

**Papyrus Jumilhac [L/V]:** Anubis, Bata/Seth, Osiris and Horus belong to a local theological network, not the domestic genealogy above. It must not be harmonized with the story into a single family tree.

## 5. Cult, Tomb and Ritual

### Cult

**[E/L]** There is no evidence for a joint hero cult of “Anpu and Bata” founded because of the tale. There is, however, independent worship of the gods whose names they bear:

- Anubis had an ancient and widespread mortuary cult.
- Bata was a local bull deity associated with Saka/Cusae and Middle Egyptian nome theology.
- Later local theology connects Anubis and Bata/Seth in the protection, theft and recovery of Osirian remains.

The ritual record therefore supports **a divine substrate for the names**, not worship of the brothers as deceased folktale kings.

### Offerings within the story

**[E, narrative only]** Pharaoh offers to the marvelous bull and later to the two persea trees. These scenes represent recognition of divine manifestation inside the fiction. They cannot by themselves prove an external cult of Bata’s bull and tree transformations.

### Tombs and relics

**[E, absence]** No ancient tomb, mummy, relic, shrine or royal monument has been securely identified as belonging to the story-heroes.

**[M, false conflation]** The celebrated “Tomb of the Two Brothers” excavated by Flinders Petrie at Rifeh in 1907 contained the Middle Kingdom burials of two real men, Nakhtankh and Khnum-nakht. It has no evidentiary connection to Anpu and Bata. The shared label is accidental. [UCL site index](https://www.ucl.ac.uk/museums-static/digitalegypt/archaeology/sitesindex.html)

### Images

- **[E]** Papyrus d’Orbiney is a written manuscript, not an illustrated narrative cycle.
- **[L]** Papyrus Jumilhac contains religious vignettes, including bull-form Bata carrying a mummy.
- **[S]** Earlier attempts to read agricultural or funerary tomb scenes as illustrations of d’Orbiney are speculative unless inscriptions name the episode. Resemblance among bulls, trees, offerings and agricultural work is insufficient.

## 6. Historical Evolution of the Figure

### Before c. 1215 BCE: divine and narrative prehistory

**[E/S]** Anubis’s cult is ancient; Bata’s securely recoverable local cultic profile is thinner and debated. Speaking-animal tales, divine birth, exile and royal succession already existed in Egyptian literature. A pre-d’Orbiney oral tale is plausible but not directly documented.

### Late Nineteenth Dynasty: canonical surviving form

**[E]** Inena’s manuscript supplies the only complete ancient narrative. It integrates rural labor, sexual accusation, self-castration, Levantine geography, divine creation, transformations and royal succession.

### Ptolemaic period: local theological systematization

**[L/V]** Papyrus Jumilhac records a priestly, geographically organized mythology. Bata is brought into Sethian and Osirian theology; Anubis becomes an active defender of Osiris. This is learned local systematization, not proof that every Jumilhac episode already underlay the Ramesside tale.

### Greek and Roman authors

**[L, absence]** Herodotus and the surviving Manethonian tradition do not preserve the brothers’ narrative. No Greco-Roman prose retelling can presently bridge d’Orbiney to later European folktales.

### Medieval and early-modern period

**[F, absence/direct transmission unproved]** Eurasian tales contain treacherous wives, life-tokens, external souls, animal and tree transformations, and rebirth. No manuscript chain demonstrates uninterrupted transmission of the complete Egyptian story through the medieval period.

### Nineteenth century onward

**[M]** Decipherment transformed the papyrus into a landmark of “world literature.” Editions increasingly standardized “Anpu and Bata,” compared the wife with Potiphar’s wife, and called the work a fairy tale. Later scholarship restored its Egyptian religious and royal dimensions.

## 7. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Folklore classification

**[F]** The tale gave its name to the **Batamärchen** and is now associated with **ATU 318**, “The Faithless Wife” or “Batamärchen.” Thompson motif **K2111**, “Potiphar’s Wife,” describes the rejected woman’s false accusation. The classification is analytic: d’Orbiney contains more episodes than any single ATU synopsis. [ATU 318 database](https://folkloredatabase.com/db_atu.php?atu=318)

**[F/S]** Balkan and wider Eurasian tales repeat parts of the sequence: a treacherous wife or princess, a hero killed in animal form, blood producing trees, the trees being cut, and survival in a chip, seed or bird. Similarity demonstrates motif circulation, not automatically descent from the Egyptian manuscript.

### Joseph and Potiphar’s wife

**[S]** Genesis 39 and d’Orbiney share a clear narrative pattern:

1. a socially powerful married woman propositions a younger man;
2. he refuses;
3. she reverses the accusation;
4. the husband or master acts against the accused.

Differences are equally important: Bata is the husband’s brother and voluntarily promises silence; the Egyptian accuser fabricates bodily injuries; Anubis intends immediate killing; Bata proves innocence by self-castration; Joseph is an enslaved foreigner whose garment functions as supposed evidence and who is imprisoned.

The Egyptian manuscript is earlier than surviving biblical manuscripts and probably earlier than the composition of the Joseph novella. That chronology makes Egyptian influence possible but does not prove direct borrowing. The motif is widely distributed, and current biblical scholarship often dates the Joseph story within a broader first-millennium literary environment rather than making it a direct copy of d’Orbiney. [Schipper on the Egyptian background of Joseph](https://www.mohrsiebeck.com/en/article/the-egyptian-background-of-the-joseph-story-101628hebai-2019-0007/); [comparative discussion](https://access.archive-ouverte.unige.ch/access/metadata/0ce046b0-0cd5-4482-bb78-73e368c25709/download)

**[M, discredited]** Moldenke’s suggestion that Moses might have studied the papyrus at a “University of Heliopolis” is nineteenth-century biblical romanticism without historical evidence. There is no evidence identifying Moses as the author of Genesis 39 or showing access to this manuscript.

### Psychological and structural readings

- **[S]** Frazerian and external-soul approaches treated Bata’s heart-tree as a survival of primitive belief.
- **[S]** Psychoanalytic readings have emphasized castration, sexual rivalry, maternal substitution and rebirth.
- **[S]** Alan Dundes interpreted the reversed sexual accusation through “projective inversion”; Susan Hollis challenged reductions that detach the episode from Egyptian religion and narrative structure. [Dundes abstract](https://www.proquest.com/scholarly-journals/projective-inversion-ancient-egyptian-tale-two/docview/198451384/se-2)
- **[S]** Rank, Raglan and Campbell provide general heroic-pattern models, but none supplies direct evidence for the tale’s composition. The story only partly fits monomythic schemes: Anubis, not Bata, performs the crucial recovery; Bata’s kingship comes through repeated object, animal, vegetal and uterine rebirth.
- **[S]** Structuralist readings commonly identify cycles of household order, rupture, death, restoration and royal order. Useful as analysis, this is not an ancient Egyptian commentary.

### Literature, visual culture and performance

**[M]** The work’s strongest documented modern reception is editorial and educational rather than a major opera, film or game franchise:

- nineteenth-century translations by de Rougé, Birch, Petrie and Moldenke;
- inclusion in anthologies of Egyptian and world tales;
- modern children’s retellings, often sanitizing the castration, killings and sexual coercion;
- museum display and digital facsimile;
- the British Museum’s 2022–23 *Hieroglyphs: Unlocking Ancient Egypt* exhibition, where the manuscript was displayed. [British Museum literary feature](https://www.britishmuseum.org/blog/page-turners-literature-ancient-egypt)

No well-documented major opera, canonical feature film, nationalist state cult, extremist appropriation or sustained commercial franchise centered specifically on Anpu and Bata was located in the dedicated search. Claims that they are major figures in present-day popular “Egyptian mythology” often derive from modern myth compendia rather than continuous reception.

### Common modern inventions and distortions

- **[M]** Giving Anubis’s wife a personal name unsupported by the papyrus.
- **[M]** Identifying Bata’s wife unequivocally as Hathor. Hollis and others have explored Hathoric qualities, but the text leaves her unnamed.
- **[M]** Saying Anpu and Bata were pharaohs known from king lists.
- **[M]** Treating the Rifeh “Two Brothers” burial as their tomb.
- **[M]** Calling Bata’s removed organ merely a symbolic “sacrifice of masculinity”; the text describes physical self-castration.
- **[M]** Making the wife’s death explicit in places where the final wording is not fully preserved.
- **[M]** Treating Papyrus Jumilhac as a direct sequel.
- **[M]** Presenting ATU 318 as proof that every European variant descended linearly from Egypt.
- **[M]** Equating Bata automatically with Baal, Osiris, Horus or Joseph. These are comparanda or proposed syncretisms, not interchangeable identities.

## 8. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Is it myth, folktale, fairy tale or literature?

The answer depends on the feature being described.

- **[E]** The text uses named Egyptian gods and divine transformations.
- **[F]** Its motifs fit international folktale catalogues.
- **[S]** Its kingship sequence and carefully repeated deaths support reading it as elite literary composition.
- **[S]** “Mythic folktale,” “folkloric myth” and “literary fairy tale” are modern hybrid descriptions. No single label exhausts the work.

### Human heroes or gods?

- **Divine-origin position [S]:** Anubis and Bata were local gods reduced or translated into human brothers. Their names, Bata’s bull epithet and forms, Anubis’s revivification, and Jumilhac parallels support this.
- **Literary-character position [S]:** The characters operate initially as humans and need not be literal divine incarnations. Egyptian personal names could be theophoric or identical with divine names; the story intentionally moves from ordinary farm life toward divine kingship.
- **Best evidentiary conclusion:** the text activates divine associations without giving a doctrinal identity statement.

### Unity or composite tale?

- **Composite theory [S]:** the Potiphar-wife episode and the transformation/rebirth sequence can circulate independently and may have been joined.
- **Unity theory [S]:** betrayal, life-tokens, repeated recognition speeches, vengeance and succession join the halves; the movement from household to cosmos to kingship is architectonic.
- **Limit:** a single manuscript cannot reveal earlier redactional layers with certainty.

### Royal propaganda and historical allegory

Wolfgang Wettengel proposed that the tale legitimized the Ramesside dynasty and the introduction or elevation of an Asiatic storm-god/Baal complex, reading Bata’s journey from the cedar country and the royal ending politically. Susan Hollis and others have connected it more broadly with the troubled succession after Merneptah.

Thomas Schneider argued that Nineteenth-Dynasty politics and Ugaritic/West Asian motifs may be relevant but objected that explicit promotion of Baal is implausible; Bata’s name and cult are Egyptian, and the text never names Baal. [Schneider](https://austriaca.at/0xc1aa500e_0x001e8a83.pdf)

**Conclusion [S]:** the date makes contemporary royal resonance possible, especially the prominence of crown prince, Viceroy of Kush and legitimate succession. No ancient gloss equates Anubis and Bata with Seti II, Amenmesse or named historical rivals. “Political satire” and “dynastic charter” remain hypotheses.

### The cedar landscape and West Asian influence

**[S]** The Levantine tree-valley, divine journey and displaced-heart motif have prompted comparison with West Asian cedar traditions and storm-god mythology. Noga Ayali-Darshan argues that this part of the tale incorporates foreign motifs and literary geography. That does not make Bata “really Baal,” nor does it establish dependence on a single surviving West Asian text. [Research summary](https://cris.iucc.ac.il/en/publications/the-background-of-the-cedar-forest-tradition-in-the-egyptian-tale-3/)

### Sex, violence and gender

The manuscript plainly includes sexual solicitation, a false accusation of attempted sexual assault, intended fratricide, self-castration, summary killing of Anubis’s wife, mass killing of envoys, armed abduction, repeated engineered deaths, animal sacrifice and judicial revenge.

Modern summaries often make the first woman a stock “wicked temptress” while minimizing Anubis’s willingness to kill without inquiry. Conversely, describing her allegation as evidence of a historical Egyptian “rape-hoax culture” exceeds what one literary composition can establish. The scene documents a narrative motif and gendered anxiety, not crime statistics.

### Oral tradition

**[S]** Repetition, formulaic transitions and international motifs are consistent with oral storytelling, but the surviving artifact is an elite scribal manuscript. There is no recording of an ancient performance and no recoverable oral archetype. “Copied from a peasant folktale” is possible, not demonstrated.

### State of the evidence

- One ancient narrative manuscript.
- No securely identified ancient narrative illustration.
- No second recension with which to reconstruct variants.
- Separate cultic evidence for the gods Anubis and Bata.
- A much later local theological witness, Papyrus Jumilhac.
- Extensive modern comparative evidence, but no proven continuous transmission chain.

This extreme asymmetry means that the tale is both unusually complete as a story and exceptionally fragile as evidence for its own earlier history.

### Evidentiary conclusion

The documentary core is narrow but exceptionally rich. One near-complete Ramesside manuscript records the brothers’ entire heroic narrative; independent religious sources confirm that their names carried divine and regional associations; a Ptolemaic priestly text preserves a different Bata–Anubis theological constellation. Everything beyond those points—oral archetypes, direct descent into Genesis or European folktale, historical royal allegory, Baal identification, a tomb, continuous cult, or real kingship—is a modern reconstruction of varying plausibility, not an additional ancient chapter of the brothers’ lives.
