# Setne Khamwas (Khaemwaset): Research Dossier

**Traditional class:** mortal royal hero and learned magician, modeled on a securely historical prince and priest; not demonstrably deified, although probably commemorated at Saqqara and treated in later literature as a sage with supernatural powers.

**Earliest secure attestation:** contemporary Nineteenth-Dynasty reliefs and inscriptions naming Prince **Khaemwaset**, son of Ramesses II, beginning in the thirteenth century BCE. The earliest surviving narrative attestation of **Setne Khamwas** is the early-Ptolemaic Demotic manuscript Cairo CG 30646, conventionally called *Setne I*, probably third century BCE. No surviving Ramesside text calls the historical prince “Setne” as a personal name or attributes the tales’ magic to him.

## Evidentiary key

- **[EA] Securely attested early material:** contemporary or near-contemporary inscriptions, objects, architecture, or documentary texts.
- **[LC] Later canonical elaboration:** ancient literary tradition substantially later than the historical prince.
- **[RV] Regional or textual variant.**
- **[LS] Learned systematization:** ancient or modern organization of disparate evidence.
- **[SI] Syncretistic identification.**
- **[MA] Medieval or early-modern accretion.**
- **[F] Folklore or orally inferred tradition.**
- **[MSR] Modern scholarly reconstruction.**
- **[MI] Modern invention or adaptation.**

Dates are BCE/CE unless stated otherwise.

## 1. Identification, names and epithets

### Orientation

Setne Khamwas is the fictionalized form of **Khaemwaset**, fourth son of Ramesses II and second son of Queen Isetnofret, historically a *sem*-priest and eventually high priest of Ptah at Memphis. Contemporary monuments associate him with royal ritual, Apis-bull burials, the Memphite necropolis, and inscriptions placed on much older royal monuments. Nearly a millennium later, Demotic storytellers transformed this conspicuously bookish, necropolis-associated prince into **Setne**, an inquisitive magician who reads tomb inscriptions, violates a learned ghost’s burial to obtain a book written by Thoth, suffers an erotic and dynastic nightmare, restores the violated dead, and later follows his miraculous son Si-Osire through the underworld. The fiction preserves the historical matrix—Ramesses II, Memphis, Ptah, old tombs, writing—but not a biography.

### Names

- **Ḫꜥ-m-Wꜣst**, conventionally vocalized **Khaemwaset**, **Khaemweset**, **Khaemuas**, **Khamwese**, French **Khâemouaset**, German **Chaemwese**: “He who appears in Thebes” or “Manifest in Thebes.” The elements are *ḫꜥ*, “appear/rise in glory,” *m*, “in,” and *Wꜣst*, Thebes. **[EA]**
- Hieroglyphic spelling commonly represented as **N28-G17-S40-X1-O49**, transliterated *ḫꜥ-m-wꜣst*. Orthographic variation does not imply different persons. **[EA/LS]**
- **stne**, **Setne**, **Setna**, **Satni**, **Setme**: the Demotic story-name. The usual explanation derives it from the priestly title *stm/setem* or *sem*, held by Khaemwaset. The papyrus tradition appears to treat it as part of his name. Some philological discussion cautions against assuming that every Demotic occurrence was still understood as a title rather than a fossilized name. **[LC/MSR]**
- **Setne Khamwas**, “Setne Khaemwaset”: a modern conventional compound used to distinguish the literary hero from the historical prince. **[LS]**

### Titles and classifications

Contemporary monuments give Khaemwaset titles including:

- **“King’s son”** and sometimes “eldest king’s son,” the latter capable of denoting senior status rather than literal primogeniture. **[EA]**
- ***sem*-priest of Ptah** and ultimately **greatest of the directors of craftsmen**, the standard title translated “high priest of Ptah.” **[EA]**
- **Iunmutef**, “pillar of his mother,” a ritual role represented by the sidelock and leopard skin and associated with filial service to a divine or divinized king. **[EA]**
- Crown prince late in Ramesses II’s exceptionally long reign, after older brothers had died; he predeceased Ramesses and never ruled. **[EA/MSR]**

“**First Egyptologist**,” “first archaeologist,” and “restorer of the pyramids” are modern honorifics, not ancient titles. They capture his demonstrable attention to older monuments but impose modern disciplinary and preservationist values. Current reassessment emphasizes that his inscriptions also advertised Ramesses II and himself, renewed ritual memory, and could coexist with dismantling older structures for stone. [UCL’s Digital Egypt](https://www.ucl.ac.uk/museums-static/digitalegypt/saqqara/khaemwaset.html) explicitly warns against equating his activity with modern archaeology; Price similarly interprets it as monumental self-presentation and ritual participation. **[MSR]**

### Identifications

No ancient source identifies Setne/Khaemwaset with a god. His literary wisdom and magical reputation resemble those of Imhotep, and Khaemwaset’s monuments furnish the earliest known occurrence of Imhotep’s epithet “son of Ptah,” but equivalence between the two men is not attested. **[EA/MSR]**

There is likewise no documented ancient equation with Hermes Trismegistus. The association arises indirectly through Thoth, sacred books, and later Western occultism. **[MI]**

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### Contemporary record of the historical prince

Khaemwaset appears in Nineteenth-Dynasty royal-family scenes, priestly monuments, statues, Apis-burial records, and inscriptions on older monuments. A British Museum statue, EA 947, dated about 1260 BCE, portrays him carrying standards bearing Ramesses II’s names; its texts invoke Atum and Osiris. This is material evidence for the prince’s priestly and filial presentation, not for the later magical stories. **[EA]**

His secondary inscriptions occur at or are reported from:

- the pyramid of Unas;
- Djoser’s Step Pyramid;
- Userkaf’s pyramid;
- Shepseskaf’s mastaba;
- Sahure’s pyramid;
- Niuserre’s sun temple;
- Pepi I’s pyramid;
- probably additional monuments at Dahshur and Giza.

The best-preserved formula says in substance that Khaemwaset caused the former king’s name to endure because he greatly desired to make splendid the monuments of earlier kings whose fabric had decayed. The inscription establishes an ancient concern with names, ritual memory, and monuments; it does not by itself prove modern-style excavation or conservation. **[EA]**

At the Serapeum, Khaemwaset was involved in Apis burials and probably in creating or reorganizing the “Lesser Vaults” around Ramesses II’s year 30. Numerous shabtis bearing his name were found in the burial connected with that Apis. Whether these objects show that Khaemwaset himself was buried there is disputed below. **[EA/MSR]**

### The literary Setne corpus

#### 2.1 *Setne I*: Setne and Naneferkaptah

- **Object:** Cairo Egyptian Museum CG 30646; Trismegistos 55857.
- **Language/script:** Egyptian in Demotic script.
- **Manuscript date:** early Ptolemaic, commonly placed in the third century BCE.
- **Provenance:** usually reported as Theban.
- **State:** six columns/pages; the first two pages are lost. The surviving text begins during Ahwere’s account of her proposed marriage to Naneferkaptah.
- **Role:** Setne is a learned prince, tomb-reader, magician, transgressor, sexual aggressor within a supernatural illusion, and ultimately restorer of the dead.
- **Evidence:** **[LC]**

The widely printed opening in which Setne learns of the Book of Thoth, searches with his foster brother Inaros, and enters Naneferkaptah’s tomb is a reconstruction from the surviving action, not preserved text. Lichtheim marks it explicitly as reconstructed. The surviving papyrus itself should therefore not be cited verbatim for every detail of that opening.

The colophon says the work was copied in a regnal “year 15,” first month of winter, but the ruler’s name is lost. It dates the copy internally without permitting a unique absolute year.

#### 2.2 *Setne II*: Setne and Si-Osire

- **Object:** British Museum EA 10822, formerly P. Lond. 604; Trismegistos 48854.
- **Language/script:** Demotic Egyptian on the verso.
- **Manuscript date:** first century CE. Greek land accounts on the recto are dated to Claudius’s year 7, 46–47 CE, giving a firm terminus for reuse.
- **Provenance:** bought at Aswan; the Greek accounts mention a Crocodilopolis probably in the Panopolite nome. TLA cautiously proposes the Nile valley between Thebes and Elkab, perhaps Gebelein.
- **State:** beginning damaged; the preserved story starts around Mehusekhe’s conception of Si-Osire.
- **Role:** Setne is father and pupil. Si-Osire, rather than Setne, conducts the decisive supernatural acts.
- **Evidence:** **[LC/RV]**

The [British Museum catalogue](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/Y_EA10822-2) and [Thesaurus Linguae Aegyptiae](https://thesaurus-linguae-aegyptiae.de/object/SM6PFKDQGFDA3AYO43M3F6JNKA) securely establish the manuscript’s first-century date.

#### 2.3 Ptolemaic “find-notes”

Copies of Book of the Dead spells 166–167 contain notices crediting Khaemwaset with finding texts “under the head of a blessed spirit west of Memphis.” These do not narrate Setne I, but they place Khaemwaset inside a first-millennium scribal mythology of textual discovery in the Memphite necropolis. **[LC]**

#### 2.4 Corpus boundaries

Only the two substantial Demotic Setne narratives above securely survive as Setne stories. References to a large, recoverable “Setne cycle” are reasonable as a generic designation but should not be mistaken for evidence of a complete ancient series. Papyrus survival is accidental, and oral antecedents are possible but unprovable. **[MSR/F]**

### Texts named in the brief that do not contain Setne

The following establish the history of Egyptian prose narrative but do **not** mention Khaemwaset or Setne:

- *Sinuhe*, *The Shipwrecked Sailor*, and *The Eloquent Peasant*: Middle Kingdom narratives, principally in Middle Egyptian.
- the *Westcar Papyrus*: surviving Hyksos-period or early-New-Kingdom manuscript of tales set among Old Kingdom kings and magicians, probably based on earlier compositions;
- *The Tale of Two Brothers*, Papyrus d’Orbiney: New Kingdom, later Nineteenth Dynasty;
- *Wenamun*, Papyrus Pushkin 120: early first millennium manuscript, traditionally treated as a report or literary narrative;
- Herodotus, *Histories* II, fifth century BCE;
- Manetho’s Egyptian history, early third century BCE, surviving only through later excerpts.

Their relevance is comparative: learned magicians, royal courts, embedded tales, divine books, travel, historical memory, and the use of earlier kings as narrative settings. They cannot fill lacunae in either Setne papyrus. **[EA for the manuscripts; LS for comparison]**

Herodotus’s story of Sethos, priest of Hephaestus/Ptah, was grouped with the Setne tales by F. Ll. Griffith because all concern Memphite priestly wisdom, but Sethos is not Setne Khamwas. Manetho likewise supplies no secure Setne episode. **[LS]**

## 3. The story

## Cycle I: the Book of Thoth, Naneferkaptah, and Tabubu

### Entry into the tomb

In the reconstructed beginning, Setne learns that a book written by Thoth lies in the tomb of the ancient prince Naneferkaptah. He enters the tomb with his foster brother Inaros and sees the book shining. Naneferkaptah, his sister-wife Ahwere, and their son Merib appear as effective dead. Because pages 1–2 are lost, the precise ancient wording and some mechanics of the entry remain unknown. **[LC/MSR]**

### Ahwere’s embedded history

The preserved manuscript begins with Ahwere recounting her family. She and her brother Naneferkaptah are children of a Pharaoh Mernebptah. Their father initially opposes their sibling marriage and proposes marriages to generals’ children, but yields; Ahwere bears Merib.

Naneferkaptah’s consuming interest is walking through the Memphite necropolis and reading royal-tomb inscriptions and House-of-Life stelae. An old priest tells him about Thoth’s book, charging silver and tax-free priestly stipends for the information.

The book, he says, lies in the river at Coptos inside nested boxes of iron, copper, juniper, ivory and ebony, silver, and gold, guarded by reptiles and an immortal serpent. Its first spell gives comprehension of celestial, aquatic, and terrestrial creatures; its second reveals Re with his Ennead, the moon, and the stars. **[LC]**

### Theft of Thoth’s book

Naneferkaptah, Ahwere, and Merib sail south. At Coptos they worship Isis and Harpocrates. Naneferkaptah magically animates wax sailors, parts the water with sand, neutralizes the reptiles, repeatedly kills the immortal serpent so it cannot revive, and takes the book. He and Ahwere read its spells.

Thoth complains to Re. Divine retribution follows: Merib and then Ahwere fall into the river and drown; Naneferkaptah recovers and buries both at Coptos. He himself later drowns, with the book bound to his body, and is buried at Memphis. The family’s separation between Coptos and Memphis motivates the conclusion. **[LC]**

### Setne’s contest with the dead

Naneferkaptah warns Setne, then plays a board game with him. Each loss drives Setne farther into the ground. Setne calls to Inaros, who obtains Ptah’s amulets and magical books from Pharaoh. Revived or strengthened by them, Setne seizes the book and leaves the tomb. Naneferkaptah predicts that he will return it carrying a forked stick and a burning brazier as signs of humiliation. **[LC]**

### Tabubu

Setne sees the beautiful Tabubu in Ptah’s temple precinct. She is identified as daughter of a prophet of Bastet of Ankhtawi. He offers money for sex and offers to settle any legal complaint she has. She insists that he come to her luxurious house at Bubastis so that she will not be treated as a street prostitute.

There, Tabubu demands legal transfer of Setne’s property to her. She next demands that his children consent, then that they be killed so they cannot contest the deed. Setne agrees and, within the vision, hears or sees their deaths. When he finally tries to have sex with her, she vanishes or transforms; he finds himself naked and sexually exposed, conventionally understood as attempting intercourse with a vessel or object. Pharaoh reveals that the children remain alive. **[LC]**

The episode does not portray consensual romance. Setne initiates a coercive proposition backed by royal power; Tabubu’s counter-demands expose his readiness to dispossess and murder his family for sexual access. Whether Tabubu is an autonomous ghost, Naneferkaptah’s magical creation, or part of an illusion controlled by Naneferkaptah is not made completely explicit. A recent reading notes that even the apparent Pharaoh in the awakening scene may be Naneferkaptah in disguise. **[LC/MSR]**

### Restitution

Setne returns the book with the predicted forked stick and brazier. Naneferkaptah asks him to retrieve Ahwere and Merib from Coptos. Setne sacrifices to Isis and Harpocrates and searches the cemetery. Naneferkaptah appears disguised as an old priest and identifies their burial beneath a police chief’s house. Setne dismantles and then rebuilds the house, transports the bodies to Memphis, and reunites the family in Naneferkaptah’s tomb. **[LC]**

The story’s resolution is therefore not simply “return the stolen book”: Setne repairs the violated geography of burial and restores a dead family’s bodily unity.

## Cycle II: Si-Osire, the underworld, and the Nubian magician

### Miraculous conception

Setne’s wife **Mehusekhe/Meheweskhe** is childless. In the damaged opening she follows dream instructions involving a plant or gourd and conceives by Setne. Setne dreams that the child must be called **Si-Osire**, “Son of Osiris,” and that he will perform marvels. **[LC]**

There is no secure historical evidence that the actual Khaemwaset had a wife of this name or a son Si-Osire. **[LC, not EA]**

### The two funerals and descent to Amenti

Setne sees a rich man’s magnificent funeral and a poor man carried out in a mat. He assumes the rich man will fare better after death. Si-Osire says the opposite and physically conducts him through seven halls of Amenti.

Setne sees frustrated and punished souls, including people unable to reach food and a man whose eye serves as a door-pivot. In the seventh hall, Osiris sits enthroned, with Anubis and Thoth supervising judgment and the balance. Ammit destroys those whose evil outweighs their good; the righteous join blessed spirits, while balanced souls serve Sokar-Osiris.

The splendidly clothed spirit beside Osiris is the poor man, rewarded for his deeds and given the rich man’s burial equipment. The rich man is the tortured door-pivot. Si-Osire’s explicit teaching is that earthly funerary display does not override moral judgment. **[LC]**

Greek parallels such as Tantalus, Sisyphus, and Ocnus have been proposed for some punishments, but the judgment scene is deeply Egyptian in personnel and structure. Direct borrowing, shared eastern-Mediterranean motifs, and local adaptation remain distinguishable hypotheses rather than facts. **[MSR]**

### The sealed letter

At twelve Si-Osire surpasses the House-of-Life scribes. A Nubian envoy challenges Pharaoh: if no Egyptian can read a sealed letter without opening it, Nubia will proclaim Egypt’s humiliation. Setne cannot answer and despairs; Si-Osire demonstrates remote reading on his father’s books and then reads the letter publicly. **[LC]**

### The embedded ancient contest

The letter recounts events 1,500 years earlier. A Nubian magician, Hor son of the Nubian woman, magically abducted Pharaoh Si-Amun to Nubia and had him beaten. The Egyptian scholar-magician Hor son of Pa-neshe appealed to Thoth at Hermopolis, copied a divine book revealed in a dream, protected Pharaoh, and retaliated against the Nubian ruler.

The two magicians then competed at Pharaoh’s court with fire, darkness, a vast stone vault, magical boats, transformations, and counterspells. Hor son of Pa-neshe defeated but spared his opponent after he swore not to return for 1,500 years. **[LC]**

The story’s hostile ethnic vocabulary and its portrayal of Nubia as Egypt’s magical rival are integral to the text. They express a Greco-Roman-period Egyptian contest narrative, not neutral ethnography and not reliable evidence for a specific Ramesside war. **[LC]**

### Si-Osire’s identity and disappearance

Si-Osire reveals that the envoy is the returning Nubian magician and that he himself is Hor son of Pa-neshe, permitted by Osiris to be reborn through Setne’s household. He burns the Nubian magician and disappears “as a shadow.” Setne mourns. Mehusekhe later bears another son, named Wesy-Ment-Hor in Griffith’s uncertain reading. Setne thereafter makes offerings to the spirit of Hor son of Pa-neshe. **[LC]**

“Reincarnation” is convenient shorthand, but the text describes a deceased magician obtaining permission from Osiris to return temporarily, enter conception, be born, complete a mission, and vanish. It is not a systematic Egyptian doctrine of repeated universal rebirth. **[LC/MSR]**

### The hero’s death

The tales give no death of Setne. The historical Khaemwaset disappears from contemporary evidence before Ramesses II’s death, commonly assigned to approximately the king’s regnal year 55, around 1225 BCE. No securely identified body has been recovered. **[EA/MSR]**

## 4. Relations with gods and other heroes

### Family and competing genealogical levels

| Figure | Historical record | Literary record |
|---|---|---|
| Ramesses II/Usermaatre | Father of Khaemwaset; contemporary monuments **[EA]** | Father and Pharaoh of Setne in *Setne I–II* **[LC]** |
| Isetnofret | Mother of the historical Khaemwaset **[EA]** | Named in some translations’ explanatory material, not central to the surviving tales |
| Mehusekhe | No secure historical attestation as Khaemwaset’s wife | Setne’s wife and mother of Si-Osire in *Setne II* **[LC]** |
| Si-Osire | No historical son securely identified | Miraculous son; incarnation of Hor son of Pa-neshe **[LC]** |
| Wesy-Ment-Hor(?) | No secure historical counterpart | Later son born after Si-Osire’s disappearance; reading uncertain **[LC/RV]** |
| Inaros | Not established as Khaemwaset’s foster brother historically | Setne’s foster brother and assistant in *Setne I* **[LC]** |

### Documented named interactions

Each line records an explicit encounter, cultic act, or relationship—not merely shared presence in Egyptian religion.

- **Ramesses II/Usermaatre** — father and sovereign; supplies Ptah’s amulets, warns Setne to return the book, and authorizes the Coptos expedition. *Setne I*, Cairo CG 30646, third century BCE. **[LC]**
- **Inaros** — foster brother/assistant; accompanies the tomb expedition in the reconstructed opening and carries messages and amulets. *Setne I*, third century BCE; opening partly reconstructed. **[LC/MSR]**
- **Naneferkaptah** — dead rival, game opponent, punisher, and eventual reconciled beneficiary; Setne steals and returns his book and reunites his family. *Setne I*, third century BCE. **[LC]**
- **Ahwere** — ghostly narrator and victim of the book’s history; warns Setne and is later transferred by him from Coptos to Memphis. *Setne I*, third century BCE. **[LC]**
- **Merib** — dead child of Naneferkaptah and Ahwere; Setne retrieves his body. *Setne I*, third century BCE. **[LC]**
- **Tabubu** — apparent priestly woman/supernatural seductress; bargains with Setne and subjects him to a punitive illusion. *Setne I*, third century BCE. **[LC]**
- **Ptah** — Setne walks and acts in Ptah’s Memphite temple; Ptah’s amulets restore or protect him; the historical Khaemwaset was Ptah’s *sem*-priest and high priest. Ramesside monuments; *Setne I–II*. **[EA/LC]**
- **Thoth** — author and owner of the stolen divine book; his complaint causes punishment in the embedded story. In *Setne II*, his book and dream instruction empower Hor son of Pa-neshe. *Setne I*, third century BCE; *Setne II*, first century CE. **[LC]**
- **Re/Pre** — receives Thoth’s complaint about Naneferkaptah; Setne later sees or is compared with Re’s radiance in the tomb. *Setne I*, third century BCE. **[LC]**
- **The Ennead** — revealed by the Book of Thoth’s second spell; no personal dialogue with Setne. *Setne I*, third century BCE. **[LC]**
- **Isis of Coptos** — Setne offers an ox, goose, wine, burnt offering, and libation before searching for Ahwere and Merib. *Setne I*, third century BCE. **[LC]**
- **Harpocrates** — receives the same offerings beside Isis. *Setne I*, third century BCE. **[LC]**
- **Bastet of Ankhtawi** — Tabubu is daughter of her prophet and invokes that priestly household; Setne does not meet the goddess. *Setne I*, third century BCE. **[LC]**
- **Neith** — Ahwere invokes Neith’s curse against the priest who disclosed the book; Setne has no direct interaction. *Setne I*, third century BCE. **[LC]**
- **Si-Osire/Hor son of Pa-neshe** — Setne’s miraculous son, guide through Amenti, savior of Egypt, and teacher; Setne later honors his spirit. *Setne II*, first century CE. **[LC]**
- **Osiris** — Setne sees him enthroned during the underworld journey; Osiris authorizes Hor son of Pa-neshe’s return. *Setne II*, first century CE. **[LC]**
- **Anubis** — Setne witnesses him participating in judgment at Osiris’s left. *Setne II*, first century CE. **[LC]**
- **Thoth in Amenti** — Setne sees him record the weighing of deeds. *Setne II*, first century CE. **[LC]**
- **Ammit/Ama** — described as destroying the condemned; Setne witnesses the judgment system, not a personal attack. *Setne II*, first century CE. **[LC]**
- **Sokar-Osiris** — balanced souls are assigned to his service; no direct dialogue. *Setne II*, first century CE. **[LC]**
- **Amun/Amen of Meroe** — patron invoked by the Nubian antagonists; Si-Osire addresses the envoy about Amun’s power. *Setne II*, first century CE. **[LC]**
- **Ra-Horakhty** — Egypt is called his footstool or beautiful horizon during Si-Osire’s denunciation of the envoy; no direct encounter. *Setne II*, first century CE. **[LC]**
- **Atum of Heliopolis** — invoked in the embedded magician’s oath; the historical Khaemwaset’s British Museum statue also contains a prayer to Atum. Statue EA 947, about 1260 BCE; *Setne II*, first century CE. **[EA/LC]**
- **Imhotep** — historical Khaemwaset venerates him in a monument carrying an early “son of Ptah” formulation; no meeting appears in either Setne tale. Ramesside evidence. **[EA]**
- **Osiris** — historical Khaemwaset’s statue EA 947 bears prayers to Osiris; this is cultic devotion, not narrative adventure. About 1260 BCE. **[EA]**

No ancient source pairs Setne with Hermes Trismegistus, Moses, Cleopatra, or a named Greek hero. Such pairings are modern comparative or fictional constructions. **[MI]**

## 5. Cult, tomb and ritual

### Apis and the Serapeum

Khaemwaset’s strongest cultic context is his historical office in the Memphite theology of Ptah and his responsibility for Apis-bull burials. The Lesser Vaults’ organization and monuments from Ramesses II’s reign associate him directly with funerary ritual for the living Apis as a divine manifestation. **[EA]**

Shabtis, jewelry, and a human-faced gold mask bearing or associated with Khaemwaset’s name were found by Auguste Mariette in or near a disturbed Serapeum chamber. Earlier investigators therefore proposed that the prince was buried among the Apis bulls. Later reassessment interprets the wrapped, masked deposit as connected with an Apis burial, and intrusive or dedicatory objects need not mark the owner’s grave. **[MSR]**

### North Saqqara monument

Waseda University excavations northwest of the Serapeum uncovered a roughly 25-by-30-meter stone monument with portico, corridor, cult room, and fragments of a red-granite false door depicting Khaemwaset. This is securely his monument. Calling it his tomb is less secure: no burial chamber or identified body proves that function. Price therefore calls it a chapel or ka-chapel and states that there is no firm evidence it served as his tomb. **[EA/MSR]**

Late Period votive pottery, faience, bronzes, and later graffiti at or around the monument indicate continued activity and memory. A nearby false-door fragment naming Khaemwaset was found with first-millennium material. This supports posthumous commemoration but not necessarily divine cult. **[EA/MSR]**

### Was he worshipped?

The evidence supports three different levels:

1. **Historical ritual authority:** certain. He officiated within established cults. **[EA]**
2. **Posthumous commemoration at Saqqara:** plausible to probable from later activity, votives, find-notes, and literary fame. **[EA/MSR]**
3. **Formal deification equal to Imhotep or a temple cult as a god:** not demonstrated. Michel Chauveau emphasizes that no dedicated shrine, statue dedication, or votive object securely proves treatment as a deity. **[MSR]**

The claim that “his cult survived into Hellenistic times” is therefore too categorical unless “cult” is used loosely for commemoration.

### Offerings in the tale

At the end of *Setne II*, Setne continually makes offerings and libations to the spirit or *genius* of Hor son of Pa-neshe. This is an explicitly literary instance of cult for a heroic dead magician; it is not archaeological evidence for a historical cult of Si-Osire. **[LC]**

## 6. Historical evolution of the figure

### Thirteenth century BCE: prince, priest, monument-maker

The contemporary Khaemwaset is a royal son embedded in Ramesses II’s court and monumental program. His identifiable activities concern Ptah, Apis, royal jubilees, ancestor-monuments, and self-commemoration. Nothing contemporary makes him a magician. **[EA]**

### First millennium BCE: remembered discoverer and necropolis sage

Later find-notes associate him with recovery of funerary texts near Memphis. Activity at his Saqqara monument suggests durable local memory. How continuously that memory was transmitted across the intervening centuries cannot be demonstrated. **[LC/MSR]**

### Ptolemaic period: transgressive antiquarian hero

*Setne I* converts expertise in old tombs and writings into a moral danger. Setne resembles Naneferkaptah: both roam cemeteries reading, seek forbidden knowledge, and disrupt the dead. The tale both celebrates scribal mastery and subordinates it to divine ownership, proper burial, and restraint. **[LC]**

### Roman period: father, witness, and national scholar

In *Setne II*, Setne becomes less transgressive. He is a respected but limited scholar whose son surpasses him, reveals just judgment, and defeats a Nubian challenger. The story folds Egyptian scribal learning, underworld ethics, and national rivalry into a tale written under Roman rule. **[LC/MSR]**

### Late antiquity to early modernity

No securely documented continuous medieval Setne tradition is known. Egyptian scripts and the Demotic texts ceased to be readable; the tales did not pass into a demonstrated Arabic or European Setne cycle. Similar motifs in later Jewish, Christian, Greek, or Arabic stories require case-by-case comparison and cannot establish direct descent merely through resemblance. **[MSR]**

### Nineteenth and twentieth centuries

Decipherment and publication restored Setne to circulation. Griffith’s 1900 *Stories of the High Priests of Memphis* presented both tales together with Herodotus’s Sethos and provided Demotic text, translation, commentary, and facsimiles. Lichtheim, Simpson/Ritner, Hoffmann/Quack, and Agut-Labordère/Chauveau subsequently supplied more reliable translations and philology. **[MSR]**

## 7. Modern reception and reinvention

- **1902 — J.-H. Rosny aîné, “Les Aventures de Setnê.”** A French literary adaptation freely reworks the cycle. **[MI]**
- **1918 — Sax Rohmer, *Brood of the Witch-Queen*.** Its fictional Book of Thoth and mummy-magic plot substantially echo *Setne I*. **[MI/MSR]**
- **1932 — Universal’s *The Mummy*.** The “Scroll of Thoth” that resurrects Imhotep is a modern cinematic object. Scholarship traces the premise through Rohmer and ultimately to the Setne story, although the prop itself used a reproduction of a Book of the Dead papyrus. **[MI]**
- **Modern occult “Books of Thoth.”** Tarot, Hermetic, Theosophical, and New Age works using this title are not the book stolen by Naneferkaptah. The Demotic scholarly composition now conventionally titled *Book of Thoth* is also a separate ancient text, its modern title supplied by editors. **[MI/LS]**
- **2002 — *Age of Mythology*.** A character called Setna appears as a priest of Osiris, not the historical high priest of Ptah. **[MI]**
- **2018 — *Assassin’s Creed Origins: The Curse of the Pharaohs*.** Setna appears in a supernatural Egyptian setting. **[MI]**
- Museum exhibitions and popular histories increasingly present Khaemwaset as “the first Egyptologist.” This has made the historical prince more famous but often flattens the difference between ancient ritual renovation, dynastic appropriation, salvage, and modern conservation. **[MSR/MI]**

No specific nationalist or extremist movement centered on Setne Khamwas was located in the evidence reviewed. Modern Egyptian “Pharaonist” discourse sometimes celebrates ancient scientific and cultural achievement, but assigning Khaemwaset a particular political appropriation without a source would be conjectural. **Absence of documented evidence is the finding.**

Likewise, Rank, Raglan, Campbell, Dumézil, and Nagy did not establish a major Setne-specific interpretive school. One can map the tales onto “hero journey” or comparative-magician patterns, but doing so is modern analysis, not an attested ancient structure. **[MSR]**

## 8. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Historicity

The historical core is exceptionally secure: Khaemwaset is abundantly attested by contemporary monuments. The question is not whether he existed, but how much of the later characterization preserves historical memory. Memphis, Ptah, Ramesses II, the necropolis, and interest in old inscriptions align with the monuments. The magical contests, Book of Thoth, Tabubu, underworld journey, Mehusekhe, and Si-Osire are known only from much later fiction. **[EA versus LC]**

### “First Egyptologist”

Older scholarship treated the restoration inscriptions as evidence of disinterested antiquarianism. More recent work stresses:

- earlier Egyptians also restored and reused monuments;
- Khaemwaset’s inscriptions insert his and Ramesses II’s names into prestigious ancient landscapes;
- ritual renewal and ancestor cult mattered more than preservation in the modern sense;
- some older complexes were simultaneously quarried.

“First Egyptologist” remains a useful popular metaphor only when these differences are stated. **[MSR]**

### Tomb controversy

Competing proposals are:

- **Serapeum burial:** based on shabtis, jewelry, a mask, and the prince’s close association with Apis burials.
- **North Saqqara monument as tomb:** based on its ownership, false door, scale, and funerary form.
- **North Saqqara monument as commemorative/ka-chapel only:** favored by the absence of a demonstrated burial and by its ritual architecture.
- **Tomb near, but not inside, the Serapeum:** supported by architectural fragments and the density of Khaemwaset material.

No identified mummy or sealed, inscribed burial resolves the issue. Claims that “Khaemwaset’s tomb has been found in the Serapeum” should be labeled obsolete or disputed. **[MSR]**

### Dating the stories

The manuscripts, not hypothetical oral originals, provide the secure dates:

- Cairo CG 30646: early Ptolemaic, probably third century BCE.
- BM EA 10822: first century CE, after 46–47 CE if the Demotic verso postdates the Greek recto as expected.

The tales may contain older motifs or antecedents, but assigning either complete composition to the Ramesside period lacks evidence. **[EA/MSR]**

### Setne’s name

Griffith’s derivation of *Setne* from Khaemwaset’s *sem/setem* title remains standard. The caution is that within the Demotic narrative it functions like a personal name. Describing it simply as a “corruption” can obscure normal linguistic development and literary reanalysis. **[MSR]**

### Tabubu’s identity

She has been read as:

- a real priestly woman caught within Naneferkaptah’s spell;
- a ghost or magical apparition;
- Naneferkaptah’s constructed instrument;
- a punitive mirror of Setne’s appetites;
- a dangerous-woman literary type.

The tale establishes the episode as deceptive punishment but does not conclusively specify her independent ontology. **[LC/MSR]**

### Underworld influences

The moral reversal of rich and poor has often been compared with the later story of the rich man and Lazarus; futile punishments invite Greek comparisons. The Egyptian papyrus is roughly contemporary with early Roman-period Mediterranean traditions. Similarity does not determine a one-way borrowing route, and the judgment tableau itself is unmistakably Egyptian. **[MSR]**

### Egypt and Nubia

Early translations often reproduce racialized terminology such as “Negress” and “Ethiopian.” More recent translation distinguishes Egyptian ethnonyms, personal epithets, and hostile narrative rhetoric. The story plainly demeans the Nubian challenger and dramatizes Egyptian superiority, but it was composed in a world where Egypt and Kush had centuries of reciprocal political, religious, and intellectual contact. It is literary ideology, not a factual account of Nubian magic. **[LC/MSR]**

### State of the evidence

The corpus is small and damaged:

- two opening pages of *Setne I* are lost;
- *Setne II* begins imperfectly and contains many uncertain readings;
- the historical tomb remains unidentified;
- no ancient illustrations of the literary episodes are securely known;
- surviving portraits show the historical priest, not scenes of Setne’s fictional adventures.

The principal change in recent scholarship has come less from discovery of new Setne narratives than from re-examination of Khaemwaset’s Saqqara landscape, secondary inscriptions, and later activity at his monument.
