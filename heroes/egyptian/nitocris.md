# Nitocris (the legend): Research Dossier

**Traditional class:** legendary mortal queen and avenging ruler; in Manetho, a king of Egypt; in modern fiction, increasingly a supernatural “ghoul-queen.” She is not securely attested as a heroized dead person, demigoddess, culture hero, or recipient of cult.

**Earliest secure attestation of the legendary queen:** Herodotus, *Histories* 2.100, Greek, composed about 440–420 BCE. He reports an Egyptian priestly story in which Nitocris succeeds her murdered brother, drowns his killers at a subterranean banquet, and kills herself in a chamber filled with ashes. No surviving Old Kingdom inscription, contemporary monument, image, tomb, or cult record securely names this woman.

**Evidence labels used below**

- **[EARLY DOCUMENT]** securely attested ancient evidence predating Herodotus, but not necessarily evidence for his heroine.
- **[CLASSICAL TRADITION]** Greek or Roman literary testimony.
- **[LATER SYSTEMATIZATION]** chronography, king-list reconstruction, or learned elaboration.
- **[VARIANT]** a competing version that should not be harmonized with another.
- **[MODERN RECONSTRUCTION]** a scholarly inference rather than an ancient statement.
- **[MODERN INVENTION]** modern fiction or pseudo-history.
- **[ABSENCE]** a searched corpus or evidence class that supplies no attestation.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

Nitocris is the Greek **Νίτωκρις, Nítōkris**, an Egyptian queen known first from Herodotus. His story classifies her as a mortal native Egyptian woman who became sovereign after Egyptians murdered her unnamed royal brother. Her identity as a Sixth-Dynasty ruler, daughter of Pepi II, wife or sister-wife of Merenre II, last monarch of the Old Kingdom, or owner of a pyramid is not in Herodotus. Those propositions result from later king-list correlations and modern reconstruction.

### Epithets

Herodotus gives the Egyptian Nitocris no formal epithet.

The Manethonian epitome preserved by George Syncellus through Julius Africanus calls her, in the surviving Latin rendering, **“omnium aetatis suae virorum fortissima et mulierum formosissima, flava rubris genis”**: braver than all the men of her age, most beautiful of women, fair/blond with rosy cheeks. This is a third-century BCE learned description surviving only in much later excerptors; it is not an Old Kingdom title. [LATER SYSTEMATIZATION]

Eratosthenes’ king list, surviving through Apollodorus and Syncellus, reportedly glosses her name as **“Athena is victorious”** or “Athena the victorious.” This is Greek interpretatio of Egyptian Neith as Athena and a learned name explanation, not an Egyptian epithet securely applied to the Herodotean figure. [SYNCRETISTIC IDENTIFICATION; LATER SYSTEMATIZATION]

### Disambiguation

At least four figures must be kept separate:

1. Herodotus’s avenging Egyptian queen.
2. Manetho’s Sixth-Dynasty Nitocris and alleged pyramid builder.
3. The male **Netjerkare/Neitiqerty Siptah** reconstructed from Ramesside king lists.
4. Psamtik I’s historical daughter Nitocris, Divine Adoratrice of Amun.

A fifth is Herodotus’s Babylonian Nitocris. Similar names alone do not establish identity.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### Evidence before Herodotus

#### Turin King List

The fragmentary Turin Royal Canon is a Ramesside papyrus, usually assigned to the Nineteenth Dynasty, compiled roughly nine centuries after the end of the Sixth Dynasty. It formerly appeared to contain a feminine-looking name *nt-ỉqr.ty*, which was treated as native confirmation of Nitocris.

Kim Ryholt’s 2000 reconstruction, based on papyrus fibres and fragment placement, showed that this entry continues with **Siptah**, a masculine name. Since royal entries carrying two names normally give a prenomen and nomen, he identifies the king as male. The corresponding position in the Abydos list contains **Netjerkare**. Ryholt therefore concludes that the apparent “Nitocris” is a corrupted *Netjerkare*, not a queen. His paper’s conclusion is categorical: the supposed female ruler “can be shown to be non-existent.” [EARLY DOCUMENT; MODERN RECONSTRUCTION]

What the Turin list does establish is the existence of a damaged Ramesside textual tradition from which a Nitocris-like form could develop. It says nothing about a sister avenging a murdered brother, a flooded chamber, suicide, beauty, or a pyramid.

#### Abydos King List

The Abydos list made under Seti I gives **Netjerkare** after Nemtyemsaf II in the equivalent position. It represents a separate Ramesside king-list tradition and supports Ryholt’s corruption hypothesis. It supplies no woman, revenge narrative, kinship, or cult. [EARLY DOCUMENT]

#### Contemporary Old Kingdom evidence

No securely identified Sixth-Dynasty inscription, seal, titulary, relief, tomb, pyramid complex, or administrative document attests a reigning woman named Nitocris. Percy Newberry’s proposed identification of Queen Neith’s funerary material with a royal prenomen was rejected after W. Stevenson Smith re-examined the inscription and read Pepi II’s prenomen **Neferkare**, not a throne name assumed by Nitocris. [ABSENCE; DISPUTED MODERN RECONSTRUCTION]

### Herodotus, *Histories* 2.100

**Language:** Ionic Greek.  
**Composition:** approximately 440–420 BCE.  
**Manuscripts:** medieval; the earliest substantial witnesses are more than a millennium later, although Herodotus’s work was quoted in antiquity.

Herodotus says Egyptian priests read from a papyrus a succession of 330 kings: eighteen Ethiopians, one native Egyptian woman, and the remainder Egyptian men. The woman’s name was Nitocris, “the same as the Babylonian” queen.

Her brother had been king. Egyptians killed him and then gave the kingship to her. To avenge him, she constructed a long underground chamber, ostensibly to inaugurate it. She invited the Egyptians whom she knew to have been principally responsible for the murder. While they feasted, she released the river through a large concealed channel. After completing the revenge, she threw herself into a chamber full of ashes so that she would escape retaliation.

This is the complete earliest story. Herodotus does not name the brother, the murderers, her parents, spouse, child, capital, dynasty, reign length, tomb, pyramid, or gods. “Nile” is implicit in “the river” within the Egyptian setting, but the Greek text does not name the river in the episode. [CLASSICAL TRADITION]

### Herodotus’s Babylonian Nitocris, 1.185–187

This different queen straightens or diverts the Euphrates through canals, creates a defensive basin, builds a bridge, and erects a tomb above a busy city gate. Its inscription invites a future king in financial need to open it. Darius opens it and finds no treasure, only a rebuke for greed.

The two Nitocris stories share water engineering, concealed spaces, royal intelligence, and deception. Whether Herodotus or his sources transferred motifs between them cannot be demonstrated. Alexandre Tourraix treats the Babylonian tomb story as an **apatē**, a structured deception serving Herodotus’s moral and imperial narrative. [CLASSICAL TRADITION; SCHOLARLY INTERPRETATION]

### Manetho

**Original:** *Aegyptiaca*, Greek, early third century BCE, conventionally associated with Ptolemy I or II.  
**Survival:** not in Manetho’s autograph or a continuous ancient manuscript, but in epitomes transmitted chiefly by Julius Africanus, third century CE, Eusebius, fourth century CE, and George Syncellus, about 800 CE.

In Africanus’s version of Dynasty VI, Nitocris is a woman-king, exceptionally brave and beautiful, fair or blond with rosy cheeks, who reigned twelve years. She is said to have built the **third pyramid**. Eusebius’s version also makes her a queen and pyramid builder but differs in chronological details. [LATER SYSTEMATIZATION; VARIANT]

The third large pyramid at Giza is archaeologically and epigraphically assigned to Fourth-Dynasty king Menkaure, centuries before the end of Dynasty VI. Manetho’s assertion is therefore evidence for a Ptolemaic-era attribution, not for Nitocris’s actual construction of it.

Petrie proposed that confusion between the names **Menkare**, **Menkaure**, and Nitocris helped generate the pyramid attribution. Gregory Nagy instead emphasizes the broader Greek-Egyptian complex linking Nitocris, Rhodopis, rosy beauty, courtesans, and female pyramid builders. Both are modern explanations of a demonstrably late attribution. [MODERN RECONSTRUCTION]

### Eratosthenes

A list of thirty-eight “Theban kings,” attributed to Eratosthenes of Cyrene and said to have been translated from Egyptian sacred records, survives through Apollodorus and Syncellus. It includes Nitocris as a queen and gives a Greek interpretive meaning involving victorious Athena. The transmission is exceptionally indirect, and its historical usefulness is disputed. It supplies neither Herodotus’s revenge nor independent contemporary evidence. [LATER SYSTEMATIZATION]

### Egyptian literary corpora named in the brief

A dedicated check of the principal tale collections finds no Nitocris:

- **The Tale of Sinuhe**, composed in the Middle Kingdom and preserved in Middle- and New-Kingdom manuscripts: no Nitocris. [ABSENCE]
- **The Shipwrecked Sailor**, Middle Egyptian, generally Middle Kingdom: no Nitocris. [ABSENCE]
- **The Eloquent Peasant**, Middle Kingdom: no Nitocris. [ABSENCE]
- **Papyrus Westcar/King Cheops and the Magicians**, surviving copy normally dated to the Second Intermediate Period, with debated Middle-Kingdom composition: no Nitocris. Its royal and magical tale-world is relevant genre context, not evidence for her. [ABSENCE]
- **The Tale of Two Brothers**, Papyrus d’Orbiney, literary Late Egyptian, copied about 1215 BCE under or near Seti II: no Nitocris. [ABSENCE]
- **Wenamun**, Papyrus Pushkin 120, narrative set near the end of the Twentieth Dynasty and preserved in a possibly later copy: no Nitocris. [ABSENCE]
- **Setne Khamwas I and II**, Demotic tales surviving in Ptolemaic and Roman papyri and translated by scholars including Robert Ritner: no Nitocris. [ABSENCE]
- **Khaemwaset’s monuments at Saqqara:** no attested connection with Nitocris. [ABSENCE]

Lichtheim, Simpson, Parkinson, and Ritner are therefore essential for defining the Egyptian narrative environment and checking its contents, but none supplies a missing indigenous Nitocris tale.

## 3. The story

### Parentage and accession

Herodotus says only that Nitocris had a brother who was king. Egyptians killed him and handed sovereignty to her. No parent is named. [CLASSICAL TRADITION]

The identification of the brother with **Merenre Nemtyemsaf II**, and of Nitocris as a daughter of **Pepi II** and Queen **Neith**, is a modern genealogical construction made by correlating Herodotus with later king lists. Herodotus never makes those identifications. No contemporary document identifies a royal daughter of Pepi II as the avenging Nitocris. [MODERN RECONSTRUCTION]

Claims that she was the wife of Merenre II, or simultaneously his sister and wife, are still later harmonizations. They are not in Herodotus or the surviving Manethonian epitomes. [MODERN RECONSTRUCTION/MODERN POPULAR ACCRETION]

### Murder of the brother

In Herodotus the killers are “Egyptians” or subjects, and Nitocris knows which were most responsible. No motive is provided. Later retellings commonly transform them into courtiers, nobles, conspirators, or priests. Those specifications are not ancient evidence. [CLASSICAL TRADITION versus MODERN INVENTION]

### The subterranean banquet and drowning

Nitocris orders a large underground room made. Claiming that she is inaugurating it, she invites the principal killers to a feast. During the meal she admits the river through a great concealed conduit. Herodotus does not say that the room lies beneath a pyramid, palace, temple, or amphitheatre; those settings are later additions. [CLASSICAL TRADITION]

The episode combines royal revenge, banquet treachery, hidden engineering, and the Nile’s destructive power. Proposed ritual, folktale, or historical interpretations remain hypotheses because no Egyptian version survives. [MODERN RECONSTRUCTION]

### Suicide

Herodotus says she throws herself into an **οἴκημα σποδοῦ πλέον**, a room or chamber full of ashes, in order to escape punishment. Translators and retellers sometimes turn this into a “burning room,” flames, or live cremation. The Greek specifies ashes, not explicitly a blazing fire. [CLASSICAL TRADITION; TRANSLATION VARIANT]

Manetho’s epitome does not preserve the revenge or suicide. Its silence cannot prove that Manetho did not know them, only that they are absent from the surviving summary.

### The pyramid

Manetho’s epitome says Nitocris built “the third pyramid.” The natural later interpretation is the third principal pyramid at Giza, that of Menkaure. Archaeology contradicts literal authorship by a Sixth-Dynasty queen. [LATER SYSTEMATIZATION]

Alternative efforts to rescue the statement—interpreting it as the third pyramid at another necropolis, a restoration, reuse, or a queen’s subsidiary pyramid—have not produced a monument securely inscribed for the legendary Nitocris. [MODERN RECONSTRUCTION]

### Beauty and complexion

Only the Manethonian tradition calls her exceptionally beautiful, brave, fair/blond, and rosy-cheeked. Herodotus supplies no physical description. Nagy connects the rosy complexion with **Rhodopis**, whose Greek name means “rosy-faced,” and with rival traditions assigning a pyramid to a woman. This is an analysis of tale formation, not proof that Nitocris and Rhodopis were once one historical person. [LATER SYSTEMATIZATION; MODERN RECONSTRUCTION]

### Death and body

Herodotus records suicide but says nothing about recovery, embalming, burial, tomb, mummy, or cult. The pyramid tradition first appears in the surviving Manethonian material. A mummy, sarcophagus, or tomb attributed online to “Nitocris” ordinarily belongs to the historical Divine Adoratrice or to another namesake, not securely to the Herodotean queen. [ABSENCE]

## 4. Relations with gods and other heroes

### Competing genealogies

| Relationship | Source | Status |
|---|---|---|
| Unnamed king as brother | Herodotus 2.100, fifth century BCE | Secure within the earliest legend. |
| Merenre Nemtyemsaf II as brother | Correlation of Herodotus with Manetho and king lists | Modern reconstruction. |
| Pepi II as father | Modern dynastic harmonization | Not stated by an ancient narrative source. |
| Queen Neith as mother | Modern dynastic harmonization | Unproved; often confused with the name’s reference to goddess Neith. |
| Merenre II as husband | Modern popular genealogy | No secure ancient attestation. |
| Children | None | No ancient source names a child. |

### Every documented ancient interaction with another named god or hero

- **Unnamed royal brother** — Nitocris succeeds and avenges him after Egyptians murder him; Herodotus 2.100, c. 440–420 BCE. **[CLASSICAL TRADITION]**
- **Neith** — no narrative interaction. Neith occurs only in the proposed etymology “Neith is excellent” and in later genealogical confusion with Queen Neith. **[ABSENCE]**
- **Athena** — no narrative interaction. Eratosthenes’ transmitted name gloss equates or translates Neith through Athena. **[SYNCRETISTIC IDENTIFICATION]**
- **Menkaure** — no ancient encounter. His pyramid is reassigned to Nitocris by Manetho’s tradition; proposed name-confusion is modern scholarship. **[LATER SYSTEMATIZATION]**
- **Rhodopis** — no ancient meeting or relationship. Their traditions compete or overlap as tales of beautiful women associated with pyramids; identification is scholarly reconstruction. **[MODERN RECONSTRUCTION]**
- **Babylonian Nitocris** — no interaction. Herodotus merely says the Egyptian woman bore the same name. **[CLASSICAL COMPARISON]**
- **Merenre Nemtyemsaf II** — no source explicitly naming him as the brother survives; the pairing is modern identification. **[MODERN RECONSTRUCTION]**

Thus, the earliest legend contains no named god, spouse, child, companion, enemy, or patron.

### Named interactions created in modern reception

- **Khephren/Khafre** — Lovecraft makes Nitocris the underground consort of Khephren, ruling hybrid mummies; “Imprisoned with the Pharaohs/Under the Pyramids,” 1924. **[MODERN INVENTION]**
- **Osiris** — in Tennessee Williams’s “The Vengeance of Nitocris,” the brother’s impiety toward Osiris helps cause his death; *Weird Tales*, August 1928. **[MODERN INVENTION]**
- **Horus, Medjed, and Anubis** — magical associations and powers in *Fate/Grand Order*, launched in Japan in 2015. **[MODERN INVENTION]**
- **Ozymandias/Ramesses II** — ally and superior in *Fate/Grand Order*’s Camelot narrative. **[MODERN INVENTION]**
- **Bedivere, Mash Kyrielight, Hassan of the Hundred Faces, and the Lion King/Rhongomyniad** — rescue, combat, alliance, or opposition in the same game narrative. **[MODERN INVENTION]**

## 5. Cult, tomb, ritual, and images

### Cult

No inscription records sacrifices, priesthood, festival, oracle, games, votive dedications, or a **heroon** for the legendary Nitocris. No ancient author says she was worshipped or heroized. [ABSENCE]

The historical Divine Adoratrice Nitocris participated in the cult of Amun and possessed estates and officials. That evidence belongs to Psamtik I’s daughter and cannot be transferred to the legendary queen. The Adoption Stela records the historical Nitocris playing the sistrum before Amun. [EARLY DOCUMENT; NAMESAKE]

### Tomb and pyramid

No excavated tomb has been securely attributed to the legendary Nitocris. Manetho’s “third pyramid” is a literary attribution contradicted by the Menkaure complex’s evidence. Newberry’s Saqqara hypothesis depended on disputed readings that did not survive re-examination. [ABSENCE; DISPROVED OR UNSUPPORTED RECONSTRUCTION]

The Babylonian Nitocris’s elevated tomb in Herodotus 1.187 belongs to the different Babylonian figure. It is frequently conflated with the Egyptian queen in popular retellings. [CLASSICAL TRADITION; CONFLATION]

### Images

No secure ancient portrait or narrative scene of Herodotus’s Nitocris is known. Objects labeled “Nitocris” in museum catalogues may depict or name the historical Divine Adoratrice. Modern illustrations of an Egyptian queen, banquet, flooded chamber, mirror, jackal ears, or supernatural underworld are reception images, not ancient iconography. [ABSENCE]

### Political use

Unlike many Egyptian royal figures, Nitocris has no well-documented ancient tomb-politics or nationalist cult. Modern presentations sometimes celebrate her as “the first woman pharaoh,” but that formula depends on accepting the disputed historical reconstruction and overlooks earlier securely documented royal women and the distinction between regency, queenship, and kingship. [MODERN POPULAR RECONSTRUCTION]

No substantial, source-based record of extremist appropriation was located. [ABSENCE]

## 6. Historical evolution

### Ramesside period, thirteenth century BCE

The Turin and Abydos king lists preserve divergent forms for a male successor of Nemtyemsaf II: *nt-ỉqr.ty Siptah* and *Netjerkare*. These are the earliest documents relevant to the later name, but they do not contain the queen or legend. [EARLY DOCUMENT]

### Late Period, seventh–sixth centuries BCE

A historical royal woman named **Nitocris/nt-jqr.t**, daughter of Psamtik I, becomes Divine Adoratrice of Amun. Her prominence demonstrates that the name was real and prestigious. It does not establish identity with a Sixth-Dynasty monarch. [EARLY DOCUMENT]

### Fifth century BCE

Herodotus records the first secure avenging-queen narrative. His juxtaposition with the Babylonian namesake places both women within a historiographical interest in formidable queens, hydraulic works, deception, and reversal. [CLASSICAL TRADITION]

### Hellenistic Egypt, third century BCE

Manetho positions Nitocris in Dynasty VI, praises her beauty and courage, gives a reign, and attributes the third pyramid to her. Eratosthenes’ transmitted list supplies a Greek divine-name interpretation. The figure is becoming a learned component of Egyptian universal chronology. [LATER SYSTEMATIZATION]

### Roman and Byzantine transmission

Manetho’s original disappears; Africanus, Eusebius, and Syncellus preserve inconsistent epitomes. Herodotus remains the narrative source. Details acquire apparent authority through repetition despite the widening chronological distance from the alleged reign. [LATER SYSTEMATIZATION]

Josephus does **not securely identify Nitocris as the Queen of Sheba**. In *Antiquities* 8.165–175 he calls that queen a ruler of Egypt and Ethiopia; the name **Nicaule** and equation with Nitocris are bound up with later textual and editorial tradition, notably Whiston’s conjectural note. Treating “Nicaule/Nitocris” as an ancient identification is unsafe. [DISPUTED ACCRETION]

### Early modern Egyptology

Before hieroglyphic decipherment and archaeological control, Manetho and classical authors structured Egyptian chronology. Nitocris consequently appeared as a historical Sixth-Dynasty queen in standard histories. [EARLY-MODERN ACCRETION]

### Nineteenth and early twentieth centuries

Scholars correlated the apparent Turin form with Herodotus and Manetho. Petrie proposed name confusion involving Menkare and Menkaure; Newberry defended a historical female ruler and sought a Saqqara monument. These theories gave the legend a historical biography—Pepi II’s daughter, Merenre II’s sister or wife, last queen of Dynasty VI—that the earliest texts lack. [MODERN RECONSTRUCTION]

### Late twentieth century to present

Closer physical examination of the Turin papyrus culminated in Ryholt’s reconstruction of **Neitiqerty Siptah** as a male king and the apparent female name as corruption of **Netjerkare**. Academic presentations increasingly distinguish the Herodotean legend from the male king-list entry, while popular histories continue to present Nitocris as the first female pharaoh. [MODERN RECONSTRUCTION versus POPULAR SURVIVAL]

## 7. Modern reception and reinvention

### Literature and theatre

- **Lord Dunsany, “The Queen’s Enemies,” 1917:** a stage adaptation of the banquet-revenge pattern. The enemies are dramatically individualized and the Herodotean anecdote becomes theatrical suspense. [MODERN INVENTION]
- **George Griffith, *The Mummy and Miss Nitocris: A Phantasy of the Fourth Dimension*, 1906:** combines reincarnation, occult science, Egyptomania, and a modern woman bearing the queen’s identity. [MODERN INVENTION]
- **H. P. Lovecraft and Harry Houdini, “Imprisoned with the Pharaohs,” 1924:** Nitocris becomes a subterranean “ghoul-queen,” consort of Khephren and ruler of nonhuman mummies. Nothing in Herodotus or Manetho makes her a ghoul, sorceress, or wife of Khephren. [MODERN INVENTION]
- **Tennessee Williams, “The Vengeance of Nitocris,” written 1927 and published in *Weird Tales*, August 1928:** expands the murder into sacrilege, priests, sacred fires, hyena sacrifice, an amphitheatre, and lurid revenge. These are Williams’s additions. [MODERN INVENTION]
- **Naguib Mahfouz, *Rhodopis of Nubia* (Arabic, 1943):** uses Nitocris within a fictional Sixth-Dynasty court and connects the historical-romance environment with Rhodopis and Merenre. [MODERN INVENTION]
- **Brian Lumley, “The Mirror of Nitocris”:** creates a cursed or supernatural mirror associated with her and extends the Lovecraftian tradition. No ancient “Mirror of Nitocris” exists. [MODERN INVENTION]

### Games and popular media

The Japanese mobile role-playing game **Fate/Grand Order** introduced Nitocris as a summonable “Caster,” later “Assassin” and “Avenger” variants. Its jackal imagery, underworld mirror, association with Medjed and Anubis, magical pharaoh status, and partnership with Ozymandias combine Egyptological motifs, Herodotean guilt and suicide, Lovecraftian horror, and game mythology. These representations have strongly shaped current online imagery but are wholly modern. [MODERN INVENTION]

Tabletop *Call of Cthulhu* material and Cthulhu-Mythos fiction similarly inherit Lovecraft’s ghoul-queen rather than an Egyptian tradition.

### Modern false antiquities and recurring errors

The following circulate as ancient but are unsupported:

- that Nitocris possessed a magical mirror;
- that she was buried alive in a pyramid;
- that her mummy or sarcophagus has been found;
- that she ruled an underground army of mummies;
- that she married Khephren;
- that she interacted with Anubis, Horus, Medjed, or Osiris;
- that Herodotus names Pepi II, Merenre II, or Queen Neith;
- that the Turin King List straightforwardly records a female Sixth-Dynasty pharaoh;
- that the historical Divine Adoratrice’s monuments are evidence for the legendary queen.

All are modern inventions, conflations, or obsolete reconstructions.

## 8. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Historicity

Three broad positions have existed:

1. **Historical queen:** Herodotus, Manetho, and the apparent Turin name preserve a Sixth-Dynasty female ruler. Newberry and numerous twentieth-century royal histories adopted versions of this view.
2. **Historical core, legendary narrative:** a real female ruler may underlie the later revenge tale, though the drowning and suicide are folklore. This remains possible in the weak sense that missing evidence cannot disprove every ephemeral reign, but no contemporary attestation supports it.
3. **Textual phantom:** the female ruler arose from corruption of male **Netjerkare Siptah**, later elaborated by Greek and Egyptian storytelling. Ryholt’s fibre-based reconstruction provides the strongest documentary case for this position.

The third presently best explains the king lists, but it cannot by itself determine when or how the revenge folktale formed.

### Dating and dependence

Herodotus predates Manetho by roughly a century and a half. Manetho may have drawn upon Egyptian king-list traditions containing the corrupted name, upon Herodotus, or upon overlapping oral and temple traditions. The lost original prevents a secure dependency analysis.

Herodotus attributes his information to priests reading a papyrus. That is evidence for how he frames his inquiry, not independent survival of the papyrus or proof that its contents dated to the Old Kingdom.

### The pyramid controversy

Archaeology securely assigns the third major Giza pyramid to Menkaure. Proposed explanations for Manetho include:

- confusion of **Menkaure** with later **Menkare**;
- conflation with Rhodopis or another female pyramid-builder story;
- appropriation or restoration rather than construction;
- epitomator corruption.

No proposal is demonstrable from the lost Manethonian text. The correct documentary conclusion is that Manetho’s transmitted tradition says Nitocris built it, while the monument says Menkaure did.

### Tale-type and gender

Herodotus presents Nitocris within a series of exceptional royal women. Modern readings have examined the reversal by which a woman punishes male political violence through concealed hydraulic engineering. Such literary readings illuminate Herodotus’s narrative but cannot establish an Egyptian ritual or matriarchal historical episode.

### State of the evidence

The evidence is unusually fragile:

- no contemporary Sixth-Dynasty record;
- one relevant but damaged Ramesside papyrus;
- a separate Ramesside monumental list;
- Herodotus preserved through medieval manuscripts;
- Manetho lost and known through epitomes separated by centuries;
- Eratosthenes known through still later intermediaries;
- no securely associated image, tomb, or cult installation.

The major discovery was not a new monument but Ryholt’s physical rearrangement and reading of the Turin fragments. It changed the apparent native “queen” into a male royal entry.

### Discredited or unsafe claims still repeated

- Newberry’s claimed royal reading in Queen Neith’s chapel is not confirmed.
- Josephus’s “Nicaule” should not be confidently cited as an independent Nitocris witness.
- Eratosthenes is not contemporary Egyptian evidence.
- Manetho’s pyramid attribution cannot override the Menkaure complex.
- A reign dated precisely to 2184–2181 BCE gives a false impression of certainty.
- Lists calling Nitocris “the first female pharaoh” often convert disputed reconstruction into fact.

| Third–fourth centuries CE | Africanus and Eusebius epitomize Manetho with differing regnal data. |
| c. 800 CE | Syncellus preserves substantial remains of the earlier chronographic traditions. |
| Nineteenth century | Decipherment and archaeology permit comparison of classical accounts with Egyptian king lists and the Giza monuments. |
| 1894 | Petrie articulates a Menkare/Menkaure name-confusion explanation. |
| 1906 | George Griffith publishes *The Mummy and Miss Nitocris*. |
| 1917 | Dunsany publishes “The Queen’s Enemies.” |
| 1924 | Lovecraft’s “Imprisoned with the Pharaohs” invents the ghoul-queen. |
| 1928 | Tennessee Williams publishes “The Vengeance of Nitocris.” |
| 1943 | Percy Newberry argues for a historical Sixth-Dynasty queen. |
| 1943 | Mahfouz publishes *Rhodopis of Nubia*. |
| 2000 | Ryholt reconstructs the Turin fragments and identifies the entry as male Netjerkare/Neitiqerty Siptah. |
| 2015 onward | *Fate/Grand Order* popularizes a magical, jackal-associated Nitocris derived partly from modern horror. |
| Present | Academic work generally treats the female ruler as doubtful or legendary; popular works continue both the “first female pharaoh” and supernatural traditions. |

## 10. Conclusions

The documented Nitocris tradition begins not with an Old Kingdom queen’s monument but with Herodotus’s fifth-century BCE account of a namelessly situated avenger. Manetho subsequently anchors her to Dynasty VI, supplies beauty and courage, and gives her the third pyramid. Neither author is confirmed by a contemporary inscription.

The earlier Ramesside king lists are relevant but do not securely attest her. Ryholt’s reconstruction offers a strong documentary genealogy for the name: a male **Netjerkare Siptah** was corrupted into a feminine-looking *Neitiqerty*, after which later historians understood the entry as a woman. Whether that error alone generated Herodotus’s revenge tale is unknown.

There is no securely attested ancient cult, tomb, mummy, iconography, divine genealogy, spouse, child, or named supernatural interaction. The figure’s richest later existence is literary: Dunsany’s avenging queen, Griffith’s reincarnated occult heroine, Lovecraft’s ghoul-queen, Williams’s revenge protagonist, Lumley’s magical mirror, and *Fate/Grand Order*’s underworld pharaoh. Those inventions are part of Nitocris’s documented history, but not evidence for ancient Egyptian belief.
