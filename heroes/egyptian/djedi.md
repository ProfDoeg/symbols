# Djedi the magician: Research Dossier

> **Class:** **Mortal wonder-worker and sage-magician**—in the surviving tale, explicitly a *nḏs*, a non-royal “commoner,” not a god, demigod, king, or cult hero. A controversial modern reconstruction identifies him with an Old Kingdom deified man of the same name, but the connection is unproved.
>
> **Earliest secure attestation:** **Papyrus Berlin P 3033, the Westcar Papyrus**, columns 6–9, a hieratic manuscript assigned by the Berlin collection to the **Seventeenth Dynasty, Second Intermediate Period, approximately the seventeenth–sixteenth centuries BCE**. Its Djedi episode is set fictionally in the reign of Khufu, roughly a millennium earlier. Proposed Middle Kingdom dates apply to the composition or an earlier form of it, not to a surviving Djedi manuscript. The papyrus is the only secure ancient textual witness to Djedi’s story. [Berlin State Museums](https://search.smb.museum/object/obj-792727?page=56&sammlungen=%C3%84gyptisches+Museum+und+Papyrussammlung); [Alexandria transcription and translation](https://inscriptionslibrary.bibalex.org/Presentation/Monument.aspx?INS_ID=13&Lang=en&MON_ID=2857).

## Evidentiary key

- **[A: securely attested early material]** Direct evidence from an ancient manuscript, inscription, or object whose date and identity are reasonably secure.
- **[B: later ancient elaboration or reception]** Ancient but later evidence, including Greek, Roman, and Demotic literary traditions.
- **[C: modern scholarly reconstruction]** An inference or interpretation argued from ancient evidence but not stated by it.
- **[D: disputed identification]** A scholarly proposal with substantive counterarguments.
- **[E: modern reception or invention]** Modern creative, popular, commercial, or pseudohistorical material.
- **[Ø: absence of evidence]** No presently documented evidence in the searched textual, archaeological, iconographic, or cultic record.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### Orientation

**[A]** Djedi is the extraordinarily old magician whom Prince Hordjedef introduces to Khufu in the fourth episode of the Westcar Papyrus. Unlike the preceding wonders, which Khufu’s sons narrate as remote events under earlier kings, Djedi is said to be alive in Khufu’s own time. Hordjedef brings him from **Djed-Sneferu**, and at court Djedi refuses Khufu’s proposal to use a condemned human being in a decapitation experiment. He instead restores the severed heads of birds and a bull. Questioned about the *ṯnw nꜣ n(y) jpwt n(y)t wnt n(y)t Ḏḥwty*—traditionally “the number of the secret chambers of the sanctuary of Thoth”—he says that he does not know the number but knows where the relevant object or information is kept. He then prophesies that the eldest of three children in the womb of Ruddjedet, children of Ra, will obtain it and that the three will assume kingship. The following episode narrates their divine-assisted births, but Djedi does not reappear. [Westcar, cols. 6–9](https://rhbarnhart.net/Westcar_trans_web.html).

The text calls Djedi a **commoner**, not a priest. Modern descriptions such as “lector-priest,” “sage,” “soothsayer,” and “court magician” are functional classifications inferred from his magical knowledge, speech, and affinities with other Egyptian learned magicians; they are not securely preserved personal titles in the Djedi passage.

### Egyptian name and spellings

- **Egyptian:** conventionally transliterated **Ḏdj**, sometimes normalized **Dedi**, **Djedi**, **Djedj**, or **Djedji**. The papyrus spelling is conventionally represented by two *ḏd*-pillar signs and a reed-leaf phonetic complement, commonly catalogued as **R11–R11–M17**.
- **Proposed meaning:** “the enduring one,” “he who endures,” or “the stable/enduring.” **[C]** This is a modern lexical interpretation from the consonantal root associated with enduring or stability; the papyrus supplies no etymological gloss.
- **Greek or Latin form:** **[Ø]** Djedi is not named by Herodotus, Manetho’s surviving epitomes, Diodorus, or other known classical authors.
- **Demotic or Coptic continuation:** **[Ø]** No securely identified later spelling of this particular literary character is known.
- **“Djedi of Djed-Sneferu”:** a useful modern designation, but the text says that a man named Djedi **lives at** *Ḏd-Snfrw*. It is therefore a residence, not an attested epithet or patronymic. Djed-Sneferu is associated with the Dahshur/Sneferu pyramid region.
- **“The magician”:** a modern descriptive epithet. The story says that he knows how to join a severed head and that he speaks “words of magic” (*ḏdwt.f m ḥkꜣ*); it does not give him a formal title equivalent to “Magician Djedi.”
- **“The first magician” or “first recorded stage magician”:** **[E]** a modern popular-history label. The manuscript records a fictional wonder, not a dated public performance by a historically documented conjurer.

### Distinct persons and disputed identifications

1. **Prince Djedi, son of Rahotep and Nofret.**  
   **[A for the prince; D for the equation]** A Fourth-Dynasty prince named Djedi is represented in the Meidum tomb complex of his parents. Modern popular accounts sometimes suggest that he inspired the Westcar magician. The shared personal name and broad Fourth-Dynasty setting are the entire case; the papyrus does not call its Djedi a prince and instead calls him a *nḏs*. No inscription connects the prince with magic, Djed-Sneferu, Hordjedef, or the prophecy. The identification is therefore possible only in the weak sense that an author could reuse a known old name, not established biography.

2. **The deified Old Kingdom Djedi of Saqqara inscriptions.**  
   **[D]** Hans Goedicke proposed in 1955 that Old Kingdom inscriptions mentioning a distinguished or divine **Djedi** refer to a deified private man and further suggested that this might be the magician remembered in Westcar. Julia Troche revived and substantially developed the first part of that argument in 2021: two monuments reportedly use a divine determinative after the name and two attest a priesthood or favored status associated with Djedi. However, no monument calls this Djedi a magician, connects him with Khufu’s court, or relates any Westcar deed. Troche explicitly calls the literary identification equivocal. Fischer and Daoud instead read the disputed sign group as a manifestation or epithet of Ptah, *ḏd-šps(s)*, rather than the name of a deified man. [Goedicke 1955](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/030751335504100106); [Troche 2021, chapter 6](https://www.jstor.org/stable/10.7591/j.ctv1dp0v6k).

3. **Thoth/Djehuty.**  
   **[E]** Some modern esoteric websites derive Djedi’s name from **Djehuty**, the Egyptian name of Thoth, and make Djedi a form, priest, or encoded manifestation of that god. The consonantal spellings are different, and neither the Westcar text nor a securely identified ancient source makes this equation. Djedi knows something concerning Thoth’s sanctuary; that does not make him Thoth.

4. **Star Wars’ “Jedi.”**  
   **[E]** The resemblance between *Djedi* and *Jedi* has generated a modern derivation claim, but no primary evidence from George Lucas establishes it. Contemporary etymological references treat “Jedi” as an invented 1977 term; proposals involving Japanese *jidaigeki* also circulate. The Egyptian derivation should be labeled unproved modern speculation. [Etymonline](https://www.etymonline.com/word/Jedi).

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### The Westcar Papyrus

**Object:** Berlin P 3033, conventionally “Papyrus Westcar” or “King Cheops and the Magicians.”

**[A] Manuscript facts**

- Material: papyrus, inscribed in hieratic.
- Language: literary or classical Middle Egyptian, with linguistic and scribal features used variously in dating it.
- Present location: Egyptian Museum and Papyrus Collection, Berlin.
- Museum date: Seventeenth Dynasty, Theban, Second Intermediate Period.
- Provenance: unknown. Henry Westcar acquired it in Egypt in 1823–1824; Karl Richard Lepsius received it in England in 1838–1839; it entered the Berlin collection after Lepsius’s death.
- Condition: beginning lost; surviving roll contains twelve columns; the ending has sometimes been called incomplete, although some scholars argue that the apparent ending is deliberate.
- Unique witness: no second ancient copy of the Djedi episode is known.
- Djedi episode: approximately columns 6.22–9.21. The birth episode follows in columns 9.21–12.26.

The Berlin catalogue treats the work as a politically inflected literary parable about the transition from the Fourth to the Fifth Dynasty, but that is a curatorial interpretation, not a colophon identifying author, date, or purpose. [Berlin State Museums](https://search.smb.museum/object/obj-792727?page=56&sammlungen=%C3%84gyptisches+Museum+und+Papyrussammlung).

### Date of manuscript versus date of composition

- **[A]** The surviving physical manuscript is normally assigned to the late Middle Kingdom or, more often now, the Second Intermediate Period, broadly the eighteenth–sixteenth centuries BCE; the Berlin catalogue specifies the Seventeenth Dynasty.
- **[C]** A Twelfth- or Thirteenth-Dynasty composition is frequently proposed from language, genre, and comparison with Middle Kingdom court tales.
- **[C]** Some scholars allow that individual tales or oral motifs may be older than the extant composition.
- **[Ø]** There is no Fourth- or Fifth-Dynasty manuscript of the tale and no contemporary record of Djedi’s performance or prophecy.
- **[C]** The Old Kingdom setting therefore cannot itself date the composition. Harold Hays showed that the author sometimes sacrifices Old Kingdom historical accuracy to literary design and argued that the work is not conscientious dynastic history. [Hays, “The Historicity of Papyrus Westcar”](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/48345193_The_Historicity_of_Papyrus_Westcar).

### Structure of the surviving work

1. **Fragmentary first tale:** set under Djoser/Netjerikhet; magician’s name lost. Modern identification with Imhotep is conjectural.
2. **Second tale, told by Khafre:** the lector-priest Webaoner creates a wax crocodile to punish his wife’s lover under King Nebka.
3. **Third tale, told by Baufre:** Djadjaemankh folds or displaces the water of Sneferu’s lake to recover a rower’s pendant.
4. **Fourth tale, told by Hordjedef:** the living Djedi is summoned to Khufu, performs restorations, and prophesies the coming kings.
5. **Birth tale:** Ruddjedet bears three children of Ra with the assistance of Isis, Nephthys, Meskhenet, Heket, and Khnum.

The shift from narrated past marvels to a living wonder-worker is explicit. Hordjedef criticizes reliance on tales of the dead because truth cannot be distinguished from falsehood and offers someone whom Khufu can test in the present. This is part of the tale’s own play with evidence, not proof that Djedi existed outside it.

### Editions and translations

Major points in the modern textual history include:

- **Adolf Erman, 1890:** *Die Märchen des Papyrus Westcar*, the foundational publication and translation.
- **A. M. Blackman:** transcription and translation of *The Story of King Kheops and the Magicians*; W. V. Davies published a revised edition in 1988.
- **Miriam Lichtheim, 1973/2000:** “King Cheops and the Magicians,” *Ancient Egyptian Literature*, vol. I, pp. 215–222.
- **R. B. Parkinson, 1997:** *The Tale of Sinuhe and Other Ancient Egyptian Poems, 1940–1640 BC*, offering a literary translation and extensive notes.
- **William Kelly Simpson, ed., 2003:** *The Literature of Ancient Egypt*, 3rd ed., pp. 13–24.
- **Verena M. Lepper, 2008:** *Untersuchungen zu pWestcar*, a full philological and literary reanalysis.
- **Laura Parys, 2017:** *Le récit du Papyrus Westcar*, with updated transcription, translation, philological commentary, and an argument for the unity of the work and its Fifth-Dynasty legitimation theme. [Publisher’s description](https://www.safran.be/en/proddetail.php?prod=TEG01).

A searchable online transcription based substantially on Blackman permits line-by-line checking, but it is an independent teaching resource and should not replace a critical edition. [Online Westcar transcription](https://rhbarnhart.net/Westcar_trans_web.html).

### The wider Egyptian tale corpus named in the brief

These texts contextualize Djedi but do **not** name him:

- **The Tale of Sinuhe:** **[A]** Middle Kingdom composition, abundantly copied in papyri and ostraca; a courtier’s exile and return. It establishes the broader classical narrative world of royal authority, eloquent protagonists, and historically named kings. **Djedi is absent.** [UCL Sanehat corpus](https://www.ucl.ac.uk/museums-static/digitalegypt/literature/sanehat/index.html).
- **The Shipwrecked Sailor:** **[A]** late Middle Kingdom manuscript, Hermitage Papyrus 1115; a framed tale of a sailor and a divine serpent. **Djedi is absent.**
- **The Eloquent Peasant:** **[A]** Middle Kingdom manuscripts; a peasant wins justice through formally crafted speeches. **Djedi is absent.**
- **Papyrus d’Orbiney, Tale of the Two Brothers:** **[A]** British Museum EA 10183, copied by the scribe Inena around 1215 BCE under Seti II; a New Kingdom narrative of Anubis and Bata involving divine transformation, betrayal, death, restoration, and kingship. It shows the longevity of Egyptian wonder-narrative but offers no Djedi tradition. [British Museum](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/Y_EA10183-6).
- **Wenamun:** **[A]** an early first-millennium manuscript generally assigned to the late New Kingdom/early Third Intermediate Period, narrating an envoy’s journey to obtain timber. It does not mention Djedi and is relevant only to the changing form of literary historical narrative.
- **Setne Khamwas I and II:** **[B]** Demotic stories preserved in Ptolemaic and Roman-period papyri. Setne I survives primarily in Cairo CG 30646 and concerns Khaemwaset’s theft of a magical book written by Thoth from Naneferkaptah’s tomb; Setne II survives principally on British Museum Papyrus 604 verso and features the prodigious child-magician Si-Osire. These are genuine later Egyptian magician stories, but neither identifies its magician with Djedi. They attest continuity of the learned-magician and forbidden-book complex, not continuous transmission of Djedi as a character. [Lichtheim’s Setne I translation](https://www.attalus.org/egypt/setne.html); [Lichtheim, *Ancient Egyptian Literature* III](https://www.ancientportsantiques.com/wp-content/uploads/Documents/PLACES/Egypt-Libya/EgyptianLiteratureVol.III-Lichtheim1980.pdf).
- **Khaemwaset’s Saqqara monuments:** **[A]** Historical inscriptions and monuments establish Ramesses II’s son Khaemwaset as a prince, high priest, and participant in restoration and mortuary activity. They do not attest Djedi. They matter because later Demotic fiction transformed an historical learned prince into Setne the magician, providing a documented analogy for—but not proof of—a historical Djedi behind Westcar.
- **Herodotus II.124–127:** **[B]** fifth century BCE, over a millennium after the Westcar manuscript. Herodotus reports traditions portraying Cheops/Khufu as impious, closing temples, compelling labor, and prostituting his daughter to obtain money. Djedi is absent. [Herodotus II](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0126%3Abook%3D2&force=y).
- **Manetho:** **[B]** third century BCE, surviving only through much later epitomes. The Fourth-Dynasty king Suphis/Khufu is said to have been hostile to the gods, later to have repented, and to have written a sacred book. Djedi is absent. The transmission through Africanus, Eusebius, and Syncellus is textually unstable and must not be retrojected into Westcar as though it were the tale’s source. [Eusebius’ Chronicle](https://www.tertullian.org/fathers/eusebius_chronicon_02_text.htm).

## 3. The story

### 3.1 Introduction at Khufu’s court

**[A: Westcar 6.22–7.8]** Prince Hordjedef declares that accounts about people of the past cannot reliably be sorted into truth and falsehood, whereas someone presently alive under Khufu can be examined. He names **Djedi**, residing in **Djed-Sneferu**.

Djedi is 110 years old. He consumes daily 500 loaves, a shoulder or side of beef, and 100 jars of beer. The quantities mark prodigious vitality rather than providing credible biographical data. The age of 110 was an Egyptian ideal of completed longevity; its use here characterizes Djedi as a perfectly aged sage.

Hordjedef attributes three capacities to him:

1. joining a severed head;
2. causing a lion to follow him while its tether trails on the ground;
3. knowing the *ṯnw* of the *jpwt* of Thoth’s sanctuary.

Only the first is subsequently demonstrated. **[Ø]** Djedi never performs the lion feat in the surviving text.

### 3.2 Khufu’s interest in Thoth’s sanctuary

**[A: Westcar 7.5–8]** Khufu has spent time seeking the *jpwt* of the sanctuary or enclosure of Thoth so that he might make something similar for his own *ꜣḫt*, literally “horizon” and here commonly understood as his royal tomb or pyramid.

The often repeated statement that Djedi “knew the number of the secret chambers of Thoth” requires qualification:

- Hordjedef says he knows their *ṯnw*, “number,” “measure,” or possibly specifications.
- Djedi later denies knowing the number itself and says that he knows **where it is**.
- The key term *jpwt* has been translated “secret chambers,” “shrines,” “locks,” “sealed things,” “plans,” “measurements,” or a body of hidden knowledge.
- Djedi locates the sought thing in a flint chest in a room called “Inventory” at Heliopolis.
- **[C]** Gardiner’s influential architectural interpretation makes Khufu seek plans or dimensions for his pyramid.
- **[C]** Goedicke instead read the passage as metaphysical or legitimating knowledge.
- **[C]** Jenny Berggren’s survey showed that the phrase remains lexically disputed and that translating it as literal rooms is not certain. [Berggren summary](https://id.scribd.com/document/256503308/Ipwt-Westcar).

Thus “secret chambers of Thoth” is a conventional translation, not an archaeologically verified chamber complex.

### 3.3 Hordjedef fetches Djedi

**[A: Westcar 7.9–8.8]** Khufu orders Hordjedef himself to bring Djedi. Hordjedef travels south by boat and then in a luxurious ebony palanquin with gold-mounted poles. He finds Djedi lying on a mat at the threshold of his house or enclosure. One servant supports or massages his head and another rubs his feet.

Hordjedef greets him with elaborate blessings and invites him to enjoy royal food and burial among his ancestors. Djedi accepts. He asks that a boat be supplied for his children and books or pupils—translation varies with the damaged and compact wording. He travels in Hordjedef’s vessel while his dependants and belongings follow in other boats.

This is the story’s only representation of Djedi’s household. **[Ø]** No spouse, parent, named child, teacher, or lineage is given.

### 3.4 Audience and refusal of human decapitation

**[A: Westcar 8.9–17]** In the palace’s columned hall, Khufu asks why Djedi has not previously been brought before him. Djedi answers with a courtly maxim: “He who is summoned comes”; the king has summoned him, and he has come.

Khufu asks whether Djedi can join a severed head. On receiving confirmation, the king orders that a prisoner under sentence be brought and executed for the demonstration. Djedi refuses:

> “Not to a human, sovereign, my lord. Surely it is not commanded to do such a thing to the noble flock.”

The “noble flock” is humanity as the gods’ or god’s flock. Djedi does not rebel against kingship; he respectfully invokes a superior norm and proposes or accepts animal subjects. The episode establishes a moral and ritual limit to the king’s command. It is also the sharpest contrast between Djedi and Khufu in the tale.

The text does **not** say that Djedi beheads the human or secretly rescues one. Modern summaries that say he “revived a condemned prisoner” are false.

### 3.5 Restoration of the animals

**[A: Westcar 8.18–9.1]**

1. A **Nile goose** is brought and decapitated. Its body is placed on the west side of the hall and its head on the east. Djedi pronounces his words of magic. Head and body move toward each other; once reunited, the goose stands and cackles.
2. A second bird, whose species is uncertain and is variously rendered “goose,” “waterfowl,” or *khetaa*-bird, undergoes the same operation.
3. A **bull** is brought and its head is cast to the ground. Djedi speaks his magic, and the bull stands behind him with its tether fallen or trailing.

The verbs describe restored living movement in narrative terms. They do not explain a technique and cannot establish an historical illusion. The popular designation “the first documented magic trick” confuses the date of a literary manuscript with the date of an actual performance.

### 3.6 The hidden knowledge

**[A: Westcar 9.1–8]** Khufu then asks about the report that Djedi knows the number of the *jpwt* of Thoth’s sanctuary. Djedi answers carefully: he does not know their number, but knows the place where it is. It lies in a flint chest in the room called “Inventory” at Heliopolis.

When Khufu tells him to bring it, Djedi says that he is not the one who will do so. The **eldest of three children in Ruddjedet’s womb** will bring it to the king. This restriction makes the coming royal line, not Djedi or Khufu, the authorized mediator of hidden Heliopolitan knowledge.

### 3.7 Prophecy of the three kings

**[A: Westcar 9.8–18]** Djedi identifies Ruddjedet as the wife of a *wab*-priest of **Ra, lord of Sakhbu**, pregnant with three children of Ra. They will exercise kingship or an “excellent office” throughout Egypt, and the eldest will become high priest of Ra at Heliopolis.

Khufu becomes distressed. Djedi reassures him that the succession will not be immediate: first Khufu’s son, then that son’s son, then one of the three children. This postponement softens the dynastic threat.

Djedi predicts the birth date: day 15 of the first month of the *peret* season. Khufu regrets that the sandbanks of the Two-Fish Channel impede his journey to the temple of Ra at Sakhbu. Djedi promises to make four cubits of water appear over the sandbanks. The text does not narrate him performing this hydrological marvel.

Khufu assigns Djedi to Hordjedef’s household and orders provisions: 1,000 loaves, 100 jars of beer, one ox, and 100 bundles of vegetables. Djedi’s episode ends there.

### 3.8 The birth episode and the Fifth Dynasty

**[A: Westcar 9.21–12.26]** Djedi is absent from the subsequent birth scene. Ra sends Isis, Nephthys, Meskhenet, Heket, and Khnum, disguised as itinerant performers or midwives, to assist Ruddjedet. Three boys are born with royal bodily signs; the divine visitors conceal royal crowns in a sack of barley, from which royal sounds later issue.

The children’s names are literary forms conventionally equated with:

- **Userkaf**
- **Sahure**
- **Neferirkare Kakai**

**[C]** The equation is convincing at the level of royal name-play, but the story’s claim that the first three Fifth-Dynasty kings were triplet sons of one priest’s wife is not corroborated by contemporary genealogy. Archaeological and inscriptional evidence gives a different and incomplete dynastic picture; Sahure, for example, is associated with Queen Neferhetepes in contemporary evidence.

### 3.9 Fate, death, and body

**[Ø]** No ancient source narrates Djedi’s death, burial, tomb, mummification, apotheosis, return, or posthumous adventures. He is alive when his episode closes, provisioned in Hordjedef’s household. Any complete “life of Djedi” extending beyond this point is modern invention or conjecture.

### Figures in the birth tale but not interacting with Djedi

These deities act in fulfillment of the prophecy, but the text gives Djedi no meeting, speech, or joint scene with them:

- **Isis:** assists the births and names or addresses the infants.
- **Nephthys:** assists Ruddjedet.
- **Meskhenet:** pronounces royal destinies.
- **Heket:** hastens or assists delivery.
- **Khnum:** gives bodily health or attends the births.

Their connection with Djedi is structural, not an interaction. Calling them his allies or patrons would go beyond the text.

### Famous but unattested pairings

- **Djedi and Imhotep:** **[Ø/E]** never paired in an ancient source.
- **Djedi and Setne Khamwas:** **[Ø/E]** never paired; they belong to separate literary periods.
- **Djedi and Prince Djedi:** **[D]** possibly the same name, not an attested meeting or identity.
- **Djedi and the Jedi:** **[E]** modern word-association, not an ancient relation.
- **Djedi and Thoth as teacher or incarnation:** **[E]** no ancient evidence.

## 5. Cult, tomb, and ritual

### What the Westcar text supplies

**[A]** The tale attributes magic, secret knowledge, a moral prohibition, prophecy, and promised control of canal water to Djedi. It does not describe worship of him, offerings at his tomb, a priesthood serving him, a shrine, oracle, festival, relic, statue, or ritual performed in his name.

**[Ø]** No tomb can securely be assigned to the Westcar magician.

**[Ø]** No securely identified ancient image represents the Westcar Djedi. The papyrus is text-only; later generic illustrations of an elderly magician, severed goose, or court performance are reconstructions.

### The disputed Saqqara cult

A dedicated contested-material review substantially complicates the categorical assertion that there was “no Djedi cult.”

**Evidence for a deified person named Djedi:**

Julia Troche assembles four Old Kingdom objects:

1. **“Goedicke’s Stela”:** reportedly from the antiquities market, unprovenanced and now difficult to locate, bearing a formula “favored by Djedi”; dated by Goedicke to the late Sixth Dynasty.
2. **Stela of Wsr:** from the Teti cemetery at Saqqara, dated to the late Sixth Dynasty or First Intermediate Period; invokes “favored by Djedi.”
3. **False door of Ptahshepses, British Museum EA 682:** interpreted as giving Djedi’s name with a divine determinative and evidence of priestly service.
4. **Cairo Stela CG 1565:** Sixth Dynasty, Saqqara; interpreted as naming a priest of Djedi and writing Djedi with a divine standard.

**[C]** Troche argues that these establish a localized cult of a deified private man named Djedi, probably beginning in the Fifth Dynasty and continuing into the Sixth Dynasty or First Intermediate Period.

**Counterarguments:**

- Henry George Fischer and Khaled Daoud read the relevant sign group not as the personal name **Djedi** plus determinative but as **Djed-shepses**, a form or designation of Ptah.
- The “Goedicke Stela” lacks secure provenance and adequate published documentation.
- No cult object calls its Djedi a magician.
- No tomb or body of this deified Djedi has been identified.
- The Westcar manuscript postdates the proposed cult evidence by many centuries.

**Finding:** **[D]** A cult of a deified Old Kingdom figure named Djedi is a serious modern scholarly reconstruction, not mere internet invention. Its identification with the Westcar magician remains unproved. The proper formulation is: **possible cult of a same-named deified man; no securely attested cult of Djedi-as-Westcar-magician.** [Troche](https://www.jstor.org/stable/10.7591/j.ctv1dp0v6k); [Goedicke](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/030751335504100106).

### Ritual significance of the wonders

**[C]** Djedi’s spoken *ḥkꜣ* belongs to an Egyptian conceptual world in which magic, religion, medicine, and temple knowledge were overlapping rather than opposed categories. Robert Ritner’s study cautions against treating Egyptian magic as secular trickery detached from ritual authority. [Ritner, *The Mechanics of Ancient Egyptian Magical Practice*](https://isac.uchicago.edu/publications/mechanics-ancient-egyptian-magical-practice).

The decapitation-restoration sequence may dramatize command over life and bodily integrity, while the refusal to use a human marks an ethical or cosmic boundary. It is not, however, presented as a public rite, initiation, resurrection liturgy, or sacrifice to Djedi.

## 6. Historical evolution of the figure

### Fourth Dynasty setting, c. twenty-sixth century BCE

**[A for Khufu and Hordjedef; not for Djedi’s deeds]** Khufu and Hordjedef are historical royal figures. The Great Pyramid and contemporary inscriptions attest Khufu; Hordjedef is archaeologically attested and later remembered as a sage. The magician’s presence at their court is not contemporaneously documented.

**[D]** A historical prince Djedi and possibly a deified man Djedi existed in the broader Old Kingdom record. Neither can securely be made the Westcar magician.

### Fifth–Sixth Dynasties

**[D]** The disputed Saqqara material may attest worship of a deified private Djedi. If the reading is correct, it supplies the only plausible pre-literary social memory from which the character could have developed. It still supplies none of his narrative deeds.

### Middle Kingdom or early Second Intermediate Period

**[C]** The Westcar composition, or an earlier form, is often placed here. Djedi is shaped as the culminating living magician in a carefully ordered court-tale cycle. The narrative moves from entertainments about former kings to prophecy concerning future kings.

### Seventeenth Dynasty / Second Intermediate Period

**[A]** Berlin P 3033 provides the first secure physical attestation. Djedi is already a fully formed character: aged sage, restorer of animals, guardian of restricted knowledge, moral corrector of Khufu, and dynastic prophet.

### New Kingdom

**[A/B]** Egyptian literature continued to cultivate transformations, magical speech, divine intervention, and restored bodies, notably in the *Tale of Two Brothers*. Ramesside scribal culture copied and canonized Middle Egyptian classics. No surviving New Kingdom manuscript names Djedi, so direct popularity cannot be measured.

### Late, Ptolemaic, and Roman Egypt

**[B]** The Setne Khamwas stories preserve a later type of learned Egyptian magician concerned with Thoth’s book, tombs, dangerous knowledge, visions, and prodigious children. They are legitimate comparanda but do not continue Djedi’s personal story.

### Greek and Manethonian representations of Khufu

**[B]** Herodotus’ fifth-century BCE Cheops is markedly tyrannical: temple-closer, forced-labor king, and exploiter of his daughter. Manetho’s much later and fragmentarily preserved Suphis is hostile to the gods but later repentant and author of a sacred book. Neither mentions Djedi.

**[C]** Westcar is often placed at the beginning of this “bad Khufu” tradition because its king proposes killing a prisoner for display and seeks restricted divine knowledge. Yet the contrast must not be overstated. Khufu accepts Djedi’s refusal, converses with him, worries over succession, seeks access to Ra’s temple, and rewards him generously. Hays’s interpretation is more precise: literary oppositions criticize royal attitudes and contrast them with the piety attributed to a coming line; the work is not simply an anti-Khufu pamphlet.

### Medieval and early-modern periods

**[Ø]** No secure Arabic, Coptic, medieval European, or early-modern tradition continuing Djedi by name has been identified. Medieval Arabic pyramid legends about antediluvian kings, Hermes, treasure, and hidden chambers belong to a broad pyramid legendary complex; attaching Djedi to them without a named textual witness is modern synthesis.

### Nineteenth century to present

- **1823–1824:** Westcar acquires the papyrus.
- **1838–1839:** Lepsius receives it.
- **1886:** Berlin accession after Lepsius’s death.
- **1890:** Erman publishes the first major edition.
- **Twentieth century:** Djedi becomes standard in anthologies of Egyptian literature and histories of magic.
- **Twenty-first century:** he becomes the namesake of a pyramid-exploration robot, while online retellings increasingly misstate the story as an eyewitness record of the first stage illusionist.

## 7. Modern reception and reinvention

### Egyptological and literary anthologies

**[E, responsible reception]** Lichtheim, Simpson, Parkinson, Lepper, and Parys made Djedi accessible as a major character of Egyptian narrative literature. Their presentations differ in translation, dating, textual unity, and political interpretation, but none treats the performance as verified Fourth-Dynasty history.

### Histories of magic and illusion

**[E]** Djedi is frequently advertised as “the world’s first magician” or the first performer of a documented trick, conventionally dated to “2700 BCE.” This compresses three different dates:

1. the tale’s fictional Fourth-Dynasty setting;
2. a possible Middle Kingdom composition;
3. the Second Intermediate Period surviving manuscript.

It also converts narrated *ḥkꜣ* into modern stage illusion. The Westcar text offers no cups-and-balls performance. Claims that Djedi performed the game depicted in the Beni Hasan tomb of Baqet III are a modern conflation of two unrelated pieces of evidence.

### The Djedi Project

**[E, documented modern commemoration]** Around 2009–2011 an international robotics project investigating the narrow shafts of the Great Pyramid’s so-called Queen’s Chamber named its robot **Djedi**. The name deliberately evoked the magician associated with Khufu and hidden architectural knowledge. The robot’s exploration was published in the *Journal of Field Robotics* in 2013. This is the clearest modern institutional reuse of the figure. [RTVE, 2010](https://www.rtve.es/noticias/20100812/djedi-nuevo-robot-explorara-pasadizos-gran-piramide/346010.shtml).

Popular descriptions sometimes say Khufu consulted Djedi “when deciding where to place” or “when planning” the pyramid. That is a modernization of Westcar: the text says Khufu sought something analogous to Thoth’s *jpwt* for his *ꜣḫt*, not that Djedi designed or sited the Great Pyramid.

### Literature, film, opera, comics, and games

**[Ø]** Djedi has not acquired a securely documented major independent reception comparable to Imhotep, Moses, Merlin, or Circe. Modern novels about Khufu, Hordjedef, or Egyptian magic may adapt Westcar motifs, but the searches conducted here did not establish a canonical opera, major film, sustained comic-book tradition, or widely distributed game centered on Djedi. Generic claims that he “appears throughout modern fantasy” should therefore be treated cautiously.

### Nationalism and political appropriation

**[Ø]** No well-documented Egyptian nationalist, imperial, fascist, racial-nationalist, or extremist cult of Djedi was found. Modern celebration of him as evidence that “Egypt invented magic” participates in cultural-priority discourse, but that is not by itself an organized nationalist appropriation.

### Esoteric and pseudohistorical reuse

**[E]** Modern pyramid-occult literature often turns the *jpwt* passage into evidence for:

- an actual lost subterranean “Hall of Records”;
- secret plans of the Great Pyramid;
- Thoth’s hidden technological archive;
- antediluvian science;
- Djedi as an initiated priest of Thoth;
- supernatural proof that unknown chambers remain in Khufu’s pyramid.

None follows securely from the wording. The passage is literary, the key nouns are disputed, and Djedi says that the sought object is in Heliopolis, not inside Khufu’s pyramid.

### Psychological and structuralist readings

**[C]** Djedi can be fitted retrospectively to modern hero-pattern schemes—wise old man, culture hero, prophetic mediator, or threshold guardian—but no Rank, Raglan, Campbell, Dumézil, or Nagy theory is necessary to explain the actual Egyptian evidence. He has no narrated birth, childhood trial, quest, combat, marriage, death, or return, so treating him as a complete Campbellian monomyth hero requires supplying most of the pattern from outside the text. Literary readings focused on frame narrative, royal ideology, piety, restricted knowledge, and the transition between dynasties are better anchored in the manuscript.

## 8. Historiography and scholarly debate

### 8.1 History or fiction?

Earlier scholarship sometimes mined Westcar for Old Kingdom dynastic history. The prophecy appeared to preserve a popular memory that Userkaf, Sahure, and Neferirkare were brothers whose rise displaced Khufu’s line.

**[C, now heavily qualified]** The text may preserve awareness of a conceptual break between dynasties, but it cannot be read as a reliable genealogy. Hays argues that its author selected and altered historical material for literary effect and that the work addresses proper and improper royal attitudes rather than reporting the historical rise of solar religion. Contemporary evidence already shows strong solar ideology in the Fourth Dynasty, undermining a simple narrative in which the Fifth Dynasty newly introduced it. [Hays](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/48345193_The_Historicity_of_Papyrus_Westcar).

### 8.2 Propaganda, legitimation, or literary retrospect?

Three broad positions overlap:

1. **Dynastic legitimation:** the miraculous births authorize the first three Fifth-Dynasty rulers as sons of Ra.
2. **Retrospective literary theology:** a much later author uses the famous transition to explore kingship, piety, and divine selection.
3. **Historical allusion to the author’s own period:** Detlef Franke tentatively related the three royal brothers to Thirteenth-Dynasty succession; this remains conjectural.

Parys argues strongly for the work’s unity and for glorification of the future Fifth-Dynasty kings. Hays accepts political and ethical meaning but rejects treating the tale as a historical account of the solar cult’s rise.

### 8.3 Is Khufu condemned?

- **For a negative reading:** he proposes executing a human for entertainment, covets restricted knowledge, and grieves at another dynasty’s future.
- **Against a simple villain reading:** he respects Djedi’s refusal, asks questions rather than seizing the object, seeks Ra’s temple, and rewards Djedi. The succession is also delayed through Khufu’s son and grandson.
- **Later comparison:** Herodotus and the Manethonian fragments portray a much more unambiguously impious Cheops/Suphis. They show the later evolution of Khufu’s legend but do not prove that Westcar intended every later accusation.

### 8.4 What are the *jpwt*?

This is the central philological dispute:

- “secret chambers” or “rooms”;
- “shrines” within Thoth’s enclosure;
- “locks” or sealed objects;
- plans, measurements, or specifications;
- esoteric knowledge connected with legitimate succession.

The grammar links *ṯnw*, a term of counting or measure, to *jpwt*, but Djedi’s answer relocates the referent into a flint chest in an Inventory room. That narrative movement may indicate a document, model, or legitimating object rather than literal chambers. The evidence does not decide the matter.

### 8.5 Did Djedi really restore severed animals?

**[A for the narration; Ø for the event]** The manuscript unquestionably narrates the restorations. There is no corroborating eyewitness account, physical evidence, or explanation of method. Attempts to reconstruct a stage trick belong to modern illusion history. Conversely, dismissing the episode as “merely a trick” imposes a modern secular category on Egyptian *ḥkꜣ*.

### 8.6 Was Djedi historical?

The evidence permits four increasingly speculative positions:

1. **Secure:** Djedi is a literary character in P Berlin 3033.
2. **Possible:** the author reused an Old Kingdom personal name.
3. **Disputed:** memories of a deified Old Kingdom Djedi helped form the character.
4. **Unsupported:** the Westcar narrative records an actual court performance and verbatim prophecy.

The historical Prince Djedi and disputed deified Djedi show that the name is not inherently fictional. Neither verifies the story.

### 8.7 Folklore, “novel,” or court literature?

Erman and Sethe emphasized popular or folkloric wonder tales. Later scholars have stressed sophisticated composition: repeated royal settings, escalating marvels, narrative nesting, temporal progression, and a climax in dynastic prophecy. “Novel” can describe its unity but is a modern genre label. “Cycle,” “court tales,” and “Tales of Wonder” are safer.

### 8.8 Relationship to later literature

Comparisons have been proposed between Westcar and:

- Moses’ contest with Egyptian magicians;
- the parting of water;
- Joseph’s age of 110 and objects placed in sacks;
- divine-birth traditions, including later Egyptian royal birth cycles and Greek myths.

These are comparative literary arguments, not direct Djedi attestations. Demonstrating common motifs does not establish borrowing; chronology, transmission, genre, and exact verbal correspondence must be shown. A recent proposal relating the sacks in Westcar and Genesis explicitly acknowledges the absence of evidence that Westcar was widely copied during the relevant later period. [Fuller 2022](https://jbqnew.jewishbible.org/assets/Uploads/503/jbq_503_fullewestcarpapyrus.pdf).

### 8.9 State of the evidence

- One ancient manuscript preserves Djedi.
- Its beginning is lost; its ending and completeness remain debated.
- No author or ancient title survives.
- No secure ancient image of Djedi survives.
- No securely identified tomb or relic survives.
- Possible Old Kingdom cult evidence concerns a same-named figure whose identity is disputed.
- Later Egyptian magician literature supplies analogies, not a continuous Djedi corpus.
- The discovery or publication of another Westcar manuscript would be decisive, but none is presently known.

### Final finding

Djedi’s **complete secure ancient record** consists of one literary episode and the prophecy leading into the following birth tale. Within it he is a mortal, non-royal sage-magician whose authority exceeds the king’s in magic, moral restraint, and access to hidden knowledge. He restores animals, refuses a human killing, foretells Ra’s three future kings, promises to open a waterway, and then disappears from the record. A same-named deified Old Kingdom person may have possessed a localized cult, but identifying that person with the magician remains a contested modern reconstruction. There is no secure tomb, portrait, genealogy, death tradition, medieval continuation, or independent ancient sequel for Djedi.
