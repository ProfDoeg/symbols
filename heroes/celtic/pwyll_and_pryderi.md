# Pwyll and Pryderi: Research Dossier

> **Traditional class:** Pwyll and Pryderi are presented in the surviving medieval Welsh tradition as mortal, pre-Arthurian rulers and heroes of Dyfed who interact with Annwn, not explicitly as gods or demigods. Pwyll becomes *Pen Annwfn*, “Head/Chief of Annwn,” through alliance and substitution with Arawn; Pryderi succeeds him as territorial lord. Their frequent modern classification as Celtic deities is a scholarly or religious reconstruction, not the Mabinogi’s stated classification.
>
> **Earliest secure attestation:** Pryderi’s earliest securely datable physical witness is probably the *Englynion y Beddau* (“Stanzas of the Graves”) in the Black Book of Carmarthen, copied before or around 1250, although the verse itself has often been dated linguistically to the ninth or tenth century. A fragment of the Second or Third Branch in Peniarth 6, also thirteenth-century, supplies early prose evidence for Pryderi. Pwyll’s earliest surviving physical attestations are the fourteenth-century Book of Taliesin, where *Preiddeu Annwfn* names “Pwyll and Pryderi,” and the mid-fourteenth-century White Book of Rhydderch, which preserves the First Branch in full. The tales and poems were composed earlier than these witnesses, but proposed dates—roughly the ninth to twelfth centuries for the relevant verse and approximately 1050–1225 for the Four Branches—are scholarly datings, not manuscript dates. [National Library of Wales: Black Book of Carmarthen](https://www.library.wales/discover-learn/digital-exhibitions/manuscripts/the-middle-ages/the-black-book-of-carmarthen), [National Library of Wales: Book of Taliesin](https://www.library.wales/discover-learn/digital-exhibitions/manuscripts/the-middle-ages/book-of-taliesin), [National Library of Wales: White Book of Rhydderch](https://www.library.wales/discover-learn/digital-exhibitions/manuscripts/the-middle-ages/white-book-of-rhydderch).

## 1. Identification, names and epithets

### Orientation

**[Later canonical elaboration: Four Branches, probably late eleventh–twelfth century; surviving chiefly in fourteenth-century manuscripts]** Pwyll is lord of the seven cantrefs of Dyfed. After unlawfully displacing another hunter’s hounds from a kill, he discovers that the hunter is Arawn, ruler of Annwn. Pwyll exchanges shape and place with Arawn for a year, defeats Arawn’s rival Hafgan, and observes chastity toward Arawn’s unnamed queen. He thereafter receives the title *Pwyll Pen Annwfn*. He later wins Rhiannon from Gwawl through the stratagem called “Badger in the Bag.” Their abducted infant is recovered by Teyrnon Twryf Liant and ultimately named Pryderi.

Pryderi, first fostered as Gwri Wallt Euryn, succeeds Pwyll, joins Bendigeidfran’s disastrous Irish expedition, and is one of seven survivors who return to Britain. In the Third Branch he gives Dyfed and his widowed mother Rhiannon to his companion Manawydan, is magically imprisoned with Rhiannon, and is liberated by Manawydan. In the Fourth, Gwydion obtains Pryderi’s pigs of Annwn through illusory barter, provokes war, and kills him in single combat by “strength and valour and magic and enchantment.” He is the only person named in all four Branches, although in the First he appears principally as an infant and child and in the Second as a member of the expedition and surviving company. [Middle Welsh text of the Four Branches](https://titus.uni-frankfurt.de/texte/etcs/celt/mcymr/pkm/pkmt.htm).

### Names

- **Pwyll**, Middle Welsh *Pwyll*. The ordinary Welsh noun means “sense, deliberation, discretion, prudence.” Treating the character as the personification “Prudence” is a plausible literary observation but not an etymological proof of divine origin. **[Secure lexical fact; mythological interpretation disputed.]**
- **Pwyll Pendeuic Dyuet**, modern *Pwyll Pendefig Dyfed*: “Pwyll, prince/noble ruler of Dyfed.” It is the opening designation in the White Book text: *Pwyll, Pendeuic Dyuet, a oed yn arglwyd ar seith cantref Dyuet*—“Pwyll, Prince of Dyfed, was lord over the seven cantrefs of Dyfed.” **[Secure manuscript attestation, mid-fourteenth century.]** [TITUS transcription](https://titus.uni-frankfurt.de/texte/etcs/celt/mcymr/pkm/pkm001.htm).
- **Pwyll Pen Annwfn / Pen Annwfyn**: “Pwyll, Head/Chief of Annwn.” The First Branch says this name displaced his former designation after his successful year in Annwn. *Pen* can mean head, chief, or leader; it does not by itself mean “god.” **[Later canonical elaboration.]**
- **Pryderi fab Pwyll**: “Pryderi son of Pwyll.” This is the regular patronymic.
- **Pryderi** is explained inside the First Branch by Rhiannon’s *pryder*, “care/anxiety”: when she hears that the recovered child may be hers, she says that, if true, she will be delivered from her anxiety; Pendaran then declares the boy well named Pryderi. The name has consequently been glossed “care,” “anxiety,” or “worry.” A proposed connection with *pryd*, “form/appearance,” or with *Prydain*, “Britain,” is an older scholarly alternative but is not demanded by the tale. **[Canonical folk etymology versus disputed philological reconstruction.]**
- **Gwri Wallt Euryn**, approximately “Gwri of the Golden Hair,” is the foster-name given to the recovered child because his hair was like gold. Manuscripts and translations vary between *Gwri*, *Gweir*, and related forms. Identification of this Gwri with **Gweir ap Gwystyl**, the prisoner in *Preiddeu Annwfn*, is W. J. Gruffydd’s and related scholars’ reconstruction, not an identification made by the Mabinogi. **[Modern scholarly reconstruction.]**
- **Pryderi, lord/king of Dyfed**: the text generally uses *arglwydd*, lord, rather than a uniformly modern conception of “king.” In the Fourth Branch his power has expanded to twenty-one cantrefs: seven of Dyfed, seven of Morgannwg, four of Ceredigion, and three of Ystrad Tywi. **[Canonical prose attestation.]**
- **One of the Three Powerful Swineherds of the Island of Britain**: a Welsh Triad names Pryderi as the keeper of Pendaran Dyfed’s swine at Glyn Cuch. The triadic tradition survives in manuscripts beginning in the thirteenth century, but individual triads cannot automatically be assigned that early. **[Medieval learned systematization.]** [Rachel Bromwich’s critical edition description](https://press.uchicago.edu/ucp/books/book/distributed/T/bo20348942.html).

### Identifications and equations

- **Pwyll = an underworld god or Welsh Pluto.** Nineteenth- and early twentieth-century mythographers inferred this from *Pen Annwfn*. A 1912 Catholic Encyclopedia could state as fact that Pwyll was the Welsh counterpart of Pluto. No inscription, temple, sacrifice, or ancient image establishes that equation. **[Obsolete learned systematization/syncretistic identification.]**
- **Pryderi/Gwri = Mabon ap Modron/Maponos.** Gruffydd and other comparativists connected the lost child, supernatural growth, imprisonment, mother, and possible forms *Gwri/Gweir*. The analogies are real, but no medieval text says “Pryderi is Mabon.” **[Modern scholarly reconstruction, disputed.]**
- **Rhiannon = Epona or *Rigantona*; Pryderi = her divine son.** This influential reconstruction combines the horse-associated Rhiannon with the Gallo-Roman horse goddess Epona and derives Rhiannon from reconstructed Brittonic *Rīgantonā*, “Great/Divine Queen.” It may preserve historical relationships, but the Mabinogi does not call Rhiannon Epona or a goddess. **[Modern comparative reconstruction.]**
- **Pwyll = Conn Cétchathach; Pryderi = Peredur/Percival.** John Carey and other comparativists have explored Irish and Grail correspondences. These are proposed lines of transmission or structural analogy, not medieval identifications. **[Modern scholarly reconstruction.]**
- **Pwyll as a “Celtic god of the underworld.”** This wording entered modern reference works and was used by the International Astronomical Union when it approved the name Pwyll for a crater on Europa in 1997. It is a modern classificatory simplification, not a securely attested ancient cult-title. [USGS–IAU Gazetteer](https://planetarynames.wr.usgs.gov/Feature/4878).

[National Library of Wales](https://www.library.wales/discover-learn/digital-exhibitions/manuscripts/the-middle-ages/the-black-book-of-carmarthen). ### Principal primary corpus

1. **The Four Branches of the Mabinogi** — anonymous Middle Welsh prose; commonly dated between the later eleventh and later twelfth centuries, although proposals range about 1050–1225; complete in the White and Red Books.

   - **First Branch**, conventionally *Pwyll Pendefig Dyfed*: Pwyll’s substitution for Arawn, defeat of Hafgan, acquisition of the title *Pen Annwfn*, wooing of Rhiannon, humiliation of Gwawl, disappearance and recovery of the child, and naming of Pryderi.
   - **Second Branch**, conventionally *Branwen ferch Llŷr*: Pryderi joins Bendigeidfran’s expedition to Ireland and is one of seven survivors.
   - **Third Branch**, *Manawydan fab Llŷr*: Pwyll is dead before it begins. Pryderi brings Manawydan into Dyfed, arranges his marriage to Rhiannon, enters an enchanted fort despite Manawydan’s warning, and is imprisoned with Rhiannon until Manawydan compels Llwyd Cil Coed to release them.
   - **Fourth Branch**, *Math fab Mathonwy*: Pryderi owns pigs sent from Annwn by Arawn; Gwydion tricks him out of them and kills him in single combat.

2. **Englynion y Beddau** — “Stanzas of the Graves”; Middle Welsh verse, some probably composed in the ninth or tenth century, surviving principally in the Black Book of Carmarthen. It assigns Pryderi a grave at Aber Gwenoli. This conflicts with the Fourth Branch’s Maen Tyfiawg/Tywynawg above Y Felenrhyd. **[Secure variant, not to be harmonized.]**

3. **Preiddeu Annwfn** — “The Spoils of Annwn,” Book of Taliesin. Arthur and three shiploads raid otherworldly forts. The poem says Gweir was held at Caer Siddi *trwy ebostol pwyll a phryderi*. *Ebostol* has been translated “epistle,” “message,” “story,” “spite,” or “treachery”; the poem therefore establishes a relationship among the names, not a recoverable prose episode. It also mentions the richly ornamented cauldron of *Pen Annwfn*, but does not explicitly say that this chief is Pwyll. [Text and discussion](https://d.lib.rochester.edu/camelot/text/preiddeu-annwn.html).

4. **“Song before the Sons of Llyr” / related Book of Taliesin poem** — Pryderi and Manawydan know Caer Siddi, where age and illness are absent and a fruitful fountain flows. It provides an independent poetic association between Pryderi, Manawydan, and the Otherworld. **[Secure manuscript; obscure early poetic tradition.]**

5. **Welsh Triads, Triad 26 in standard editions** — Pryderi is one of the Three Powerful Swineherds, keeping Pendaran Dyfed’s seven swine at Glyn Cuch; the episode is not identical to the Fourth Branch, where the pigs belong to Pryderi and came from Arawn. **[Regional or learned variant.]**

6. **Culhwch ac Olwen** — anonymous Middle Welsh Arthurian prose, probably twelfth century in its surviving form. Pwyll is included in or associated with the enormous catalogue of Arthur’s court; neither he nor Pryderi performs a narrative action in the quest. The identification of catalogue-name Gware/Gwri Wallt Euryn with Pryderi is possible but not explicit. The surviving witnesses are the White and Red Books. [Textual bibliography](https://www.culhwch.info/bibliography.html).

7. **Medieval bardic poetry** — Einion fab Gwalchmai, Cynddelw Brydydd Mawr, Hywel Foel ap Griffri, and Dafydd ap Gwilym employ Pryderi as a legendary reference. Dafydd’s *tir Pryderi*, “Pryderi’s land,” makes him an emblem of Dyfed. These are receptions of the established legend, not independent biographies.

### Sources named in the item-specific brief but not in this tradition

The following source audit is essential because the brief calls the pair “Celtic (Irish and Welsh) heroes” and names Irish corpora:

- The **Táin Bó Cúailnge**, **Aided Óenfir Aífe** (“Death of Aífe’s Only Son”), **Fled Bricrenn**, and **Longes mac nUislenn** belong to the Irish Ulster Cycle. Neither Pwyll nor Pryderi appears in them.
- **Acallam na Senórach**, **Macgnímartha Finn**, and **Tóraigheacht Dhiarmada agus Ghráinne** belong to the Irish Fenian/Finn Cycle. Neither appears in them.
- Neither is a character in the Irish **Dindshenchas**.
- Dáithí Ó hÓgáin’s work on Fionn and Joseph Falaky Nagy’s *The Wisdom of the Outlaw* concern the Fenian tradition, not a Pwyll–Pryderi corpus.
- James Macpherson’s **Ossian** publications and their authenticity controversy belong to eighteenth-century Scottish-Gaelic reception of Finn/Oisín materials. They did not transmit Pwyll or Pryderi.

These absences do not preclude comparative study: fosterage, supernatural birth, otherworld journeys, heroic feasts, and swine narratives have Irish analogues. They do preclude treating the Irish works as primary attestations of these two Welsh figures. The online *Acallam* text, for example, opens with Oisín and Caílte as survivors of the Fianna, not Pryderi. [Acallam na Senórach text](https://iso.ucc.ie/Acallamh-senorach/Acallamh-senorach-text.html).

## 3. The story

### Cycle I: Pwyll, Arawn, and Hafgan

**[Later canonical elaboration: First Branch]** While hunting at Glyn Cuch, Pwyll drives his own hounds onto a stag already killed by a strange pack—white dogs with red ears—and drives the other pack away. Arawn identifies himself as lord of Annwn and demands reparation.

Arawn proposes that the men exchange shapes and kingdoms for a year and a day. Pwyll must meet Hafgan, Arawn’s rival. Arawn warns him to strike only once: Hafgan habitually asks an opponent to strike again, after which he recovers.

Pwyll rules Annwn prosperously in Arawn’s form. He shares Arawn’s bed nightly but neither embraces nor speaks intimately to Arawn’s queen. At the appointed ford he strikes Hafgan nearly in two. When Hafgan asks for a second blow, Pwyll refuses; Hafgan’s followers submit. Pwyll and Arawn resume their own forms. Arawn discovers Pwyll’s chastity from his wife, and their friendship is strengthened. Because Pwyll has united the two realms under Arawn, the text says he is thereafter called **Pwyll Pen Annwfn**.

This is not a story of Pwyll dying or becoming permanent ruler of the dead. Annwn is portrayed as a prosperous court closely resembling an aristocratic human realm, not explicitly as hell. Equating it straightforwardly with a classical underworld is a later interpretive choice.

### Cycle II: Rhiannon and Gwawl

At Gorsedd Arberth, Pwyll sees Rhiannon riding a pale-white horse. His men cannot overtake her although her horse appears to amble. Only when Pwyll calls to her does she stop. She says she has sought him because she prefers him to Gwawl fab Clud, to whom others intend to give her.

A year later Pwyll visits the court of her father Hyfaidd Hen. An unknown petitioner asks him for a gift; Pwyll rashly promises anything in his power, whereupon the stranger reveals himself as Gwawl and claims Rhiannon and the feast. Rhiannon devises a remedy rather than accepting Pwyll’s loss as final.

At a second feast one year later Pwyll, disguised as a petitioner, asks Gwawl to fill a magical or inexhaustible bag with food. When Gwawl enters or presses into it to stamp the food down, Pwyll’s hidden followers close it. They strike and kick the bag while asking, “What is in the bag?” and receiving the answer “a badger”; hence the game “Badger in the Bag.” Gwawl renounces Rhiannon and promises peace.

The tale presents severe humiliation and collective beating but does not say that Gwawl is killed. The Third Branch later condemns Pwyll’s action as an “ill-advised deed,” supplying an internal reinterpretation: Llwyd Cil Coed enchants Dyfed partly to avenge his friend Gwawl.

### Cycle III: the vanished child

After a period of childlessness, Pwyll’s nobles ask him to take another wife. He grants Rhiannon another year; she conceives and bears a son at Arberth.

Six women set to watch mother and child fall asleep. The infant vanishes. To save themselves, they kill a puppy, smear its blood and bones on the sleeping Rhiannon, and accuse her of devouring her baby. The text therefore contains a fabricated accusation of maternal infanticide and cannibalism, not an actual killing by Rhiannon.

Pwyll refuses to repudiate Rhiannon but accepts a penance proposed by advisers. For years she sits by the court’s mounting-block, recounts the charge to travelers, and offers to carry them on her back. This degradation supplies the narrative’s horse imagery; whether it preserves the memory of a horse-goddess ritual is unproved.

At Teyrnon Twryf Liant’s court in Gwent, a prized mare foals every May Eve, but each foal disappears. Teyrnon keeps watch, sees a huge claw reach through the window, severs the arm, and finds an infant outside. The same night the mare’s foal survives. Teyrnon and his wife foster the child as **Gwri Wallt Euryn**, who grows at an extraordinary rate.

When the child’s resemblance to Pwyll becomes unmistakable, Teyrnon returns him. Rhiannon says her *pryder*, care or anxiety, would be ended if he were truly hers; Pendaran Dyfed declares **Pryderi** an appropriate name. Pwyll rewards Teyrnon and commits Pryderi to Pendaran’s further fostering. The Branch closes with Pryderi excelling in appearance and games, Pwyll’s death, and Pryderi’s succession.

The creature that removed child and foal is never named or explained. Identifying it with a particular god, witch, or animal is modern invention.

### Cycle IV: Ireland and the seven survivors

**[Second Branch]** Pryderi is among Bendigeidfran’s British host that crosses to Ireland after Branwen, married to King Matholwch, is abused and beaten. The expedition ends in reciprocal slaughter. Efnisien destroys the Irish warriors restored by the magic cauldron but dies doing so; the mortally wounded Bendigeidfran orders his head removed and carried to Britain.

Seven men survive with the head: **Pryderi, Manawydan, Taliesin, Ynawg, Gruddieu, Heilyn**, and **Gilfaethwy**. They spend seven years at Harlech and eighty years at Gwales in an enchanted feast before opening a forbidden door and returning to bury the head in London.

Pryderi is not the expedition’s leader and has no individually narrated combat. Calling this “Pryderi’s Irish campaign” is reasonable shorthand but exceeds his stated role.

### Cycle V: the enchantment of Dyfed

**[Third Branch]** Pwyll is dead. Pryderi has married Cigfa, daughter of Gwyn Glohoyw. He offers Manawydan friendship, his mother Rhiannon as wife, and the use of Dyfed.

When Pryderi, Manawydan, Rhiannon, and Cigfa sit at Gorsedd Arberth, thunder and mist descend. All other people, domestic animals, houses, and settlements disappear from Dyfed. After living by hunting, Pryderi and Manawydan travel to England and successively practice saddlery, shield-making, and shoemaking. Their superior craftsmanship provokes local guildsmen to violence; Manawydan repeatedly restrains Pryderi from fighting and returns with him to Dyfed.

A white boar leads their hounds to an enchanted fort. Inside, Pryderi sees a golden bowl held by chains. Despite Manawydan’s warning, he touches it; his hands and feet adhere, and he loses speech. Rhiannon reproaches Manawydan for abandoning him, enters the fort, and is similarly trapped. The fort vanishes.

Manawydan and Cigfa cultivate wheat, but supernatural mice destroy the crops. Manawydan catches a pregnant mouse and prepares to hang it. A scholar, priest, and bishop successively offer ransom. The bishop is Llwyd Cil Coed in disguise; the mouse is his pregnant wife. Manawydan refuses release until Llwyd restores Pryderi and Rhiannon, lifts the enchantment permanently, and renounces revenge.

Llwyd explains that he acted for Gwawl, avenging the “Badger in the Bag” humiliation inflicted by Pwyll. Thus Pryderi bears retaliation for his father’s deed although he had not been born when it occurred.

### Cycle VI: the pigs and Pryderi’s death

**[Fourth Branch]** Pryderi now rules twenty-one cantrefs in southern Wales. Arawn has sent him pigs from Annwn. In the Triad’s variant, however, Pryderi keeps Pendaran Dyfed’s pigs at Glyn Cuch; the sources should not be collapsed.

Gwydion proposes obtaining the animals for Math. Disguised as bards, Gwydion, Gilfaethwy, and ten companions visit Pryderi. Gwydion entertains the court as master storyteller and requests the pigs. Pryderi explains that a compact with his people prevents him from giving or selling them until they have doubled their number.

Gwydion exploits the wording: he magically produces twelve horses, twelve hounds, golden fittings, and shields from fungus and exchanges them for the pigs. Because the illusion will last only until the next day, he drives the pigs rapidly north. Place-name explanations—Mochdref, Mochnant, and others—mark their route. These are medieval narrative etymologies, not independent proof of the journey.

When the false animals revert, Pryderi raises Dyfed and pursues. The war devastates both sides. Pryderi asks that the dispute be settled between himself and Gwydion, since Gwydion caused it. In single combat Gwydion defeats and kills him “through strength and valour and aggression and magic and enchantment.”

The prose says Pryderi was buried at **Maen Tyfiawg/Maen Tywynawg above Y Felenrhyd**. *Englynion y Beddau* instead puts his grave at **Aber Gwenoli**. Neither site has yielded an archaeologically identified burial of Pryderi. The contradiction is primary evidence for separate local traditions.

## 4. Relations with gods and other heroes

### Genealogical and marital record

| Relationship | Four Branches’ account | Variant or reconstruction |
|---|---|---|
| Pwyll’s parents | Not named | Later pedigrees supplying ancestors are antiquarian or genealogical accretions |
| Pwyll’s wife | Rhiannon ferch Hyfaidd Hen | Gruffydd’s suggestion that Pwyll, Arawn, or Manawydan may represent competing fathers or consorts is reconstruction |
| Pwyll’s son | Pryderi | Theories that Arawn, Manawydan, or another otherworld being was Pryderi’s “original” father are modern reconstructions |
| Pryderi’s mother | Rhiannon | Comparison with Modron, mother of Mabon, is scholarly reconstruction |
| Pryderi’s fosterers | Teyrnon and his unnamed wife initially; Pendaran Dyfed later | Triad emphasizes Pendaran and his pigs |
| Pryderi’s wife | Cigfa ferch Gwyn Glohoyw | No child is named |
| Pryderi’s stepfather/companion | Manawydan fab Llŷr after marriage to Rhiannon | No source says Manawydan biologically fathers Pryderi |

### Documented named interactions

“Early” below means early poetic or triadic tradition, not a contemporary record of an historical event.

#### Pwyll

- **Arawn** — Pwyll offends his hunt, exchanges shape and realm with him for a year, kills Hafgan on his behalf, and becomes his enduring ally. *First Branch*, probably late eleventh–twelfth century; White Book witness c. 1350. **[Later canonical elaboration.]**
- **Hafgan** — Pwyll, disguised as Arawn, strikes him once in formal combat and refuses the restorative second blow. *First Branch*. **[Later canonical elaboration.]**
- **Rhiannon** — Pwyll encounters her at Gorsedd Arberth, follows her marriage plan, temporarily loses her through his rash promise, then marries her. *First Branch*. **[Later canonical elaboration.]**
- **Gwawl fab Clud** — Pwyll accidentally grants him Rhiannon, then traps and has him beaten in the bag until he renounces her. *First Branch*; condemned retrospectively in the Third. **[Later canonical elaboration with internal reinterpretation.]**
- **Hyfaidd Hen** — Pwyll is entertained at his court and marries his daughter Rhiannon there. *First Branch*. **[Later canonical elaboration.]**
- **Teyrnon Twryf Liant** — Teyrnon restores Pwyll’s abducted son; Pwyll promises him maintenance and reward. *First Branch*. **[Later canonical elaboration.]**
- **Pendaran Dyfed** — confirms the child’s name and receives Pryderi for fostering. *First Branch*. **[Later canonical elaboration.]**
- **Pryderi** — father and recovered son; Pwyll has him fostered and Pryderi succeeds him after his death. *First Branch*. **[Later canonical elaboration.]**
- **Gweir ap Gwystyl** — Gweir’s imprisonment at Caer Siddi occurs *trwy ebostol Pwyll a Phryderi*. The nature of *ebostol* is uncertain; “through the treachery/spite/message/story of Pwyll and Pryderi” are competing translations. *Preiddeu Annwfn*, uncertain composition, fourteenth-century manuscript. **[Secure obscure poetic association.]**
- **Arthur** — Pwyll is a name in Arthur’s courtly orbit or catalogue in *Culhwch ac Olwen*; no deed shared by them is narrated. Probably twelfth century, fourteenth-century manuscripts. **[Medieval Arthurian assimilation.]**
- **Llwyd Cil Coed** — no direct meeting is narrated. Llwyd later avenges Pwyll’s treatment of Gwawl on Pwyll’s family and land. *Third Branch*. **[Indirect canonical relation; not a direct interaction.]**
- **Math, Gwydion, Bendigeidfran, Branwen, Taliesin** — no direct interaction with Pwyll is narrated in the Four Branches. Famous groupings of them as a single “Welsh pantheon” are modern systematization.

#### Pryderi

- **Pwyll** — father, recovered at his court; Pryderi inherits Dyfed after Pwyll’s death. *First Branch*. **[Later canonical elaboration.]**
- **Rhiannon** — mother; falsely accused after his disappearance; restored when he returns; later imprisoned with him and released. *First and Third Branches*. **[Later canonical elaboration.]**
- **Teyrnon Twryf Liant** — rescues Pryderi as an infant, fosters him as Gwri Wallt Euryn, recognizes him, and returns him. *First Branch*. **[Later canonical elaboration.]**
- **Pendaran Dyfed** — names or confirms the name Pryderi and becomes his foster-father; a Triad makes Pryderi keeper of Pendaran’s pigs. *First Branch* and Triad 26. **[Canonical plus learned variant.]**
- **Cigfa ferch Gwyn Glohoyw** — wife; shares the emptied Dyfed but is not imprisoned with him. *Third Branch*. **[Later canonical elaboration.]**
- **Bendigeidfran** — Pryderi joins his rescue expedition and later helps carry the severed head. *Second Branch*. **[Later canonical elaboration.]**
- **Branwen** — Pryderi belongs to the expedition mounted in response to her abuse; no individual conversation is recorded. *Second Branch*. **[Joint narrative appearance.]**
- **Matholwch** — Pryderi is on the opposing British side in the war against the Irish king; no individual combat between them is narrated. *Second Branch*. **[Joint antagonism.]**
- **Efnisien** — fellow member of the British expedition; Efnisien’s actions determine the catastrophe, but no individual exchange with Pryderi is recorded. *Second Branch*. **[Joint appearance.]**
- **Nisien** — fellow member of Bendigeidfran’s company; no individual exchange recorded. *Second Branch*. **[Joint appearance.]**
- **Manawydan fab Llŷr** — fellow survivor, friend, guest and eventual stepfather; receives Dyfed and Rhiannon through Pryderi; warns Pryderi against the enchanted fort and later engineers his release. *Second and Third Branches; Book of Taliesin poem*. **[Canonical interaction with early poetic analogue.]**
- **Taliesin** — one of the seven survivors accompanying Bendigeidfran’s head; no individual conversation recorded. *Second Branch*. **[Joint canonical appearance.]**
- **Ynawg, Gruddieu, Heilyn, Gilfaethwy** — fellow members of the seven survivors. *Second Branch*. **[Joint canonical appearance.]**
- **Llwyd Cil Coed** — enchants Dyfed and imprisons Pryderi to avenge Gwawl; ultimately releases him under Manawydan’s coercion. *Third Branch*. **[Later canonical elaboration.]**
- **Gwawl fab Clud** — no direct meeting: Pryderi suffers Llwyd’s vengeance for what Pwyll did to Gwawl before Pryderi’s birth. *Third Branch*. **[Indirect canonical relation.]**
- **Arawn** — sends the otherworld pigs owned by Pryderi; whether directly to Pryderi or first through Pwyll varies in later retellings. *Fourth Branch*. **[Canonical gift without narrated meeting.]**
- **Gwydion fab Dôn** — entertains and deceives Pryderi, exchanges magical counterfeits for his pigs, provokes war, and kills him by martial and magical means. *Fourth Branch*. **[Later canonical elaboration.]**
- **Gilfaethwy fab Dôn** — accompanies Gwydion in the deception and resulting war; he had earlier been a fellow survivor in the Second Branch. *Second and Fourth Branches*. **[Canonical interaction.]**
- **Math fab Mathonwy** — ruler of the opposing northern host; Pryderi’s war draws Math away from Goewin. No personal meeting or combat between Math and Pryderi is narrated. *Fourth Branch*. **[Joint antagonistic episode.]**
- **Gweir ap Gwystyl** — obscurely imprisoned through the *ebostol* of Pwyll and Pryderi. *Preiddeu Annwfn*. **[Secure but obscure poetic association.]**
- **Arthur** — no explicit interaction. Modern retellings sometimes place Pryderi in Arthur’s company by identifying him with Gweir/Gwri; the identification is not stated in *Culhwch*. **[Modern reconstruction.]**
- **Mabon ap Modron, Maponos, Peredur/Percival, Fionn, Cú Chulainn** — no medieval text records a meeting or identity. All pairings are comparative scholarship or modern invention.

## 5. Cult, tomb, image and ritual

### Cult

No securely identified ancient or medieval cult of Pwyll or Pryderi is known.

There is:

- no dedication to either in the surviving Romano-British epigraphic record;
- no securely excavated shrine or *heroon*;
- no recorded sacrifice, priesthood, festival, oracle, sacred games, relic cult, or continuous pilgrimage;
- no demonstrably ancient iconography labeling either figure;
- no historical tomb cult attached to either alleged grave.

Consequently, “Pwyll was worshipped as god of the underworld” or “Pryderi was a god of youth reborn” must be labeled **modern reconstruction**, not cult history. The positive evidence is literary: titles, otherworld interactions, supernatural events, and comparative motifs.

### Graves and places

- **Maen Tyfiawg/Tywynawg above Y Felenrhyd** — the Fourth Branch’s explicit burial place for Pryderi. **[Medieval literary claim.]**
- **Aber Gwenoli** — *Englynion y Beddau* locates Pryderi’s grave here, where waves strike the land. **[Independent early poetic/localizing variant.]**
- **Narberth/Arberth** — associated with Pwyll’s court, Pryderi’s birth, and the supernatural mound. The standing ruins of Narberth Castle are Norman and later; the Royal Commission judges that the castle may occupy an earlier Welsh administrative site, but this does not prove Pwyll’s court. [Royal Commission on the Ancient and Historical Monuments of Wales](https://rcahmw.gov.uk/on-the-trail-of-pryderi-king-of-dyfed/).
- **Glyn Cuch, Gorsedd Arberth, Gwales, Harlech, London, Mochdref, Mochnant, Y Felenrhyd** — real or proposed landscapes organize the literary geography. Place-name correspondence shows the story was mapped onto Wales; it does not independently verify events.
- **“Pryderi’s grave” as archaeology** — neither literary location has produced an inscribed burial or datable object linking it to the character. **Absence of evidence is the finding.**

### Images

The medieval manuscripts are textual witnesses, not a surviving ancient iconographic cycle for Pwyll and Pryderi. Known visual representations are post-medieval illustrations:

- Lady Charlotte Guest’s nineteenth-century editions established visual settings for the English-reading public.
- Ernest Wallcousins illustrated Pryderi’s death for Charles Squire’s *Celtic Myth and Legend, Poetry and Romance* (1905). **[Modern illustration.]**
- Later fantasy covers, children’s books, character art, heritage trails, and digital art derive from the prose narratives rather than from ancient cult images.
- The 1997 naming of the bright-rayed crater **Pwyll** on Europa is scientific commemoration, not religious evidence.

### Modern religious practice

Some contemporary Druid, Celtic-reconstructionist, and eclectic Pagan practitioners address Pwyll as a deity or venerate him as an Annwn-related power. This is **documented modern religious reception**, but no continuous line from an ancient cult has been demonstrated. Pryderi is less prominent in modern devotion and more often treated as a mythic prince or divine-son analogue.

## 6. Historical evolution of the figures

### Possible early poetic and local traditions: approximately ninth–eleventh centuries

Pryderi appears to have circulated independently of the surviving prose synthesis: his grave was localized; he was remembered as a swineherd; and Pwyll and Pryderi were connected with Gweir’s otherworld imprisonment. These notices do not reconstruct a single lost biography. **[Secure early poetic residue; synthesis hypothetical.]**

### Formation of the prose Mabinogi: approximately 1050–1225

An anonymous author or redactor organized diverse courtly, legendary, onomastic, and wonder-tale materials into four coordinated Branches. The sequence turns Pwyll’s actions into causes of Pryderi’s fortunes: alliance with Annwn produces the pigs; humiliation of Gwawl causes Dyfed’s enchantment; succession joins father and son; and the Fourth Branch supplies Pryderi’s death.

Whether this was one author’s composition or a harmonizing redaction of previously independent tales remains disputed. The polished cross-references support deliberate literary design; variant graves, pig ownership, and poetic allusions support pre-existing materials.

### Thirteenth–fourteenth centuries: manuscript canonization

The Black Book fixes the grave tradition. Peniarth fragments attest circulation of Branch material. The White Book and Red Book preserve the full cycle. The Book of Taliesin places the names within an enigmatic learned-poetic landscape of Annwn and Caer Siddi.

Christian vocabulary—invocations of God, confession, priests, bishops, and moralized courtly conduct—exists alongside otherworld marvels. Calling the prose a mere Christian disguise over an intact pagan scripture is too simple; it is medieval Christian Welsh literature using inherited legendary materials.

### Later Middle Ages

Triads and court poetry reduce Pryderi to memorable classifications and territorial allusion: swineherd, dead hero, exemplar of Dyfed. Pwyll enters the Arthurian catalogue in *Culhwch ac Olwen*, but neither becomes central to continental Arthurian romance.

### Sixteenth–eighteenth centuries

Manuscripts continued to be copied and collected, but Pwyll and Pryderi never acquired the international profile of Arthur, Merlin, or Tristan. Antiquarian attempts to arrange a coherent “British mythology” began reclassifying the figures as survivals of pagan gods.

The Macphersonian Ossian controversy did not concern them. Any claim that Macpherson preserved Pwyll/Pryderi material is false.

### Nineteenth-century recovery

Lady Charlotte Guest’s bilingual *Mabinogion* installments, published 1838–1849 and subsequently collected and revised, made the stories broadly accessible in English. The collective title “Mabinogion” was extended beyond the four texts properly called branches of the *Mabinogi*. Guest’s work was a major act of preservation and reception but also helped detach the tales from Welsh-language performance.

Victorian Celtic comparativism interpreted Pwyll as an underworld god and Pryderi as a displaced young divinity. These readings intersected with efforts to demonstrate the antiquity and dignity of Welsh culture, but they do not amount to a documented political cult of either figure.

### Twentieth–twenty-first centuries

Critical editions by Ifor Williams, R. L. Thomson, and others shifted discussion toward manuscript language and narrative form. Gwyn and Thomas Jones, Jeffrey Gantz, Patrick Ford, and Sioned Davies produced influential English translations.

Pwyll and Pryderi became figures of fantasy, children’s literature, Welsh heritage interpretation, Pagan reconstruction, environmental campaigns, and planetary nomenclature. No well-documented extremist appropriation specifically centered on either figure emerged in the material surveyed. General assertions about “Celtic racial religion” should not be imputed to these characters without a particular organization, text, and date.

## 7. Modern reception and reinvention

### Editions and translation

- **William Owen Pughe**, late eighteenth–early nineteenth century, prepared translations and probably influenced the conventional individual titles. These titles can misleadingly imply four discrete biographies.
- **Lady Charlotte Guest, 1838–1849; revised 1877** — her English translation and notes created the international “Mabinogion” canon. [1877 edition record](https://onlinebooks.library.upenn.edu/webbin/book/lookupid?key=olbp22605).
- **Ifor Williams, *Pedeir Keinc y Mabinogi*, 1930; second edition 1951** — a foundational Middle Welsh edition.
- **Gwyn Jones and Thomas Jones, 1948/1949 onward** — highly influential English translation.
- **Patrick K. Ford, 1977** — literary translation emphasizing Welsh tale tradition.
- **Sioned Davies, 2007** — performance-sensitive modern translation with pronunciation and explanatory material.
- **R. L. Thomson, 1957 and later revised edition** — detailed critical work on *Pwyll Pendeuic Dyuet*.

### Fiction

- **Evangeline Walton, *The Virgin and the Swine* (1936; reissued as *The Island of the Mighty*, 1970)** retells the Fourth Branch and therefore Pryderi’s dispossession and death.
- **The Children of Llyr** (1971), **The Song of Rhiannon** (1972), and **Prince of Annwn** (1974) completed Walton’s sequence. *Prince of Annwn* retells Pwyll’s Branch; *The Song of Rhiannon* expands Pryderi and Manawydan’s Third Branch. Walton psychologizes and systematizes social and religious conflicts absent from the source. **[Modern literary invention.]** [Publication history](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evangeline_Walton).
- **John Cowper Powys, *Porius* (1951)** imaginatively embeds the Four Branches and their supposed author in a late-antique Wales. **[Modern metafiction.]**
- **Lloyd Alexander, *The Chronicles of Prydain* (1964–1968)** borrows names and motifs rather than retelling the Branches. Its King Pryderi is a proud warlord who joins Arawn and dies attempting to seize the Book of Three. This betrayal is Alexander’s invention, not a medieval Pryderi episode. **[Modern invention.]**
- Numerous contemporary fantasy novels, role-playing settings, comics, and online mythographies promote Pwyll to “god of the underworld” and Pryderi to “god of swine” or “god of youth.” Such portfolios are not attested in medieval texts.

### Visual and public reception

- Guest’s illustrated editions and Wallcousins’s 1905 “Death of Pryderi” established romantic-medieval imagery.
- The Royal Commission’s “Trail of Pryderi” maps the legend onto historic landscapes while identifying him as mythological.
- In 2026 the Eryri National Park used a performer representing Pryderi in an anti-litter/environmental campaign. This is civic heritage symbolism, not cult. [Eryri campaign](https://eryri.gov.wales/visit/snowdon/plastic-free-yr-wyddfa/bringing-legends-to-life-to-inspire-change/).
- **Pwyll crater**, approved by the IAU in 1997, is a 45-kilometre named feature on Europa. The gazetteer’s description “Celtic god of the underworld” records twentieth-century reception rather than settling the medieval figure’s status.

### National and political uses

The Four Branches have supported Welsh cultural recovery through publication, education, tourism, and Welsh-language identity. Pwyll and Pryderi function as specifically Welsh, rather than generically “Celtic,” figures in that process.

No source located documents a sustained nationalist organization, state cult, or extremist movement organized specifically around Pwyll or Pryderi. The responsible conclusion is therefore:

- **Welsh cultural-national reception:** documented at the level of canon formation, education, heritage, and reclamation.
- **Specific extremist appropriation:** not demonstrated.
- **Generic racialist “Celtic pantheon” claims:** present in some nineteenth- and twentieth-century mythography, but must not be assigned to either hero without a cited user or organization.

## 8. Historiography and scholarly debate

### The “Pryderi saga”

W. J. Gruffydd proposed that the Four Branches grew from a lost four-part “Saga of Pryderi,” aligned with Irish narrative categories: conception/birth, youthful deeds, imprisonment or expulsion, and death. Pryderi’s appearance in every Branch and the sequence from birth to death support the perception of continuity.

Critics answer that:

- Pryderi is marginal in much of the Second Branch;
- the Fourth Branch’s main narrative moves onward to Math, Gwydion, Arianrhod, Lleu, and Blodeuwedd;
- the proposed Irish four-stage model was imposed more rigidly than the evidence allows;
- the extant work’s artistry deserves analysis as composition rather than as debris from a better lost saga.

Modern scholarship generally treats “Pryderi saga” as an influential hypothesis, not an established recovered text.

### One author, redactor, or accumulated cycle?

Cross-references—Gwawl’s humiliation causing the Third Branch enchantment, Annwn’s pigs connecting Pwyll and Pryderi, and Pryderi’s life arc—suggest deliberate coordination. Contradictory grave traditions and the Triad’s different ownership of the pigs show that independent materials existed. The evidence permits a gifted redactor using varied tradition; it does not identify that person.

### Dating

The manuscript dates are secure; composition dates are not. Linguistic, legal, social, and literary comparisons have produced estimates from the mid-eleventh into the early thirteenth century. “Ancient Celtic text” should not imply an Iron Age written original. Conversely, fourteenth-century manuscripts do not prove fourteenth-century invention.

### Historicity

No contemporary chronicle, genealogy securely anchored to historical rulers, inscription, coin, or archaeological context verifies Pwyll or Pryderi as historical princes. Dyfed, Gwent, Gwynedd, and the political geography are real; legendary placement within them is not biography.

Attempts to manufacture dynasties for Pwyll from late pedigrees or to identify Pryderi with a historical Demetian ruler are speculative. The lack of named ancestry for Pwyll is especially unlike securely transmitted royal genealogy.

### Divinity and euhemerization

Older scholars frequently assumed that every supernatural Mabinogi figure was an euhemerized deity. Pwyll’s Annwn title, Rhiannon’s horse associations, and Pryderi’s supernatural childhood make divine antecedents possible. They do not demonstrate:

- that the extant figures were ever worshipped;
- that a coherent “Welsh pantheon” existed in the form modern charts present;
- that Pwyll was originally Arawn;
- or that Pryderi was simply Maponos under another name.

The sound formulation is: **the narratives may preserve transformed mythological material; the cultic identities reconstructed from it remain hypotheses.**

### Paternity

The Four Branches consistently make Pwyll Pryderi’s father. Gruffydd and other scholars have suggested that Pwyll’s chastity in Arawn’s bed, Rhiannon’s otherworld character, and Manawydan’s later marriage preserve conflicting versions in which Arawn or Manawydan fathered the child. No extant medieval source explicitly gives either alternative. These are reconstructions, not competing surviving pedigrees.

### The meaning of *ebostol*

The phrase *trwy ebostol Pwyll a Phryderi* is among the dossier’s genuinely unresolved textual problems. Depending on emendation and semantics, Pwyll and Pryderi may have sent a message, told a tale, practiced deception, or caused Gweir’s imprisonment through hostility. It cannot safely be converted into a complete lost adventure.

### Grave variants

The Fourth Branch’s inland Maen Tyfiawg/Y Felenrhyd and the grave stanzas’ coastal Aber Gwenoli are irreconcilable on present evidence. Claims that one is a cenotaph, that the body was moved, or that two Pryderis existed are unsupported harmonizations.

### “Celtic” versus Welsh and Irish

Pwyll and Pryderi are Brittonic/Welsh figures. Comparison with Irish literature is methodologically legitimate because the languages and traditions are related and medieval Wales and Ireland interacted. Combining their corpora as if all “Celtic mythology” were one canon erases source boundaries. The item brief’s Ulster, Fenian, Dindshenchas, Ó hÓgáin, Nagy, and Ossian materials belong in comparative or negative-control discussion, not the primary dossier.

### Major modern scholarship

- W. J. Gruffydd, *Rhiannon: An Inquiry into the Origins of the First and Third Branches of the Mabinogi* (1953): influential but highly reconstructive.
- Andrew Welsh, “The Traditional Narrative Motifs of the Four Branches of the Mabinogi”: formal and narrative criticism.
- Proinsias Mac Cana, *Branwen Daughter of Llŷr*: mythological and structural study.
- Patrick Sims-Williams: linguistic and historical work on the dating and transmission of medieval Welsh literature.
- John Carey: comparative Irish-Welsh and Grail hypotheses.
- Juliette Wood and later Mabinogi scholars: reception, folklore, and critique of reductive pagan reconstructions.

### Final evidentiary assessment

What is secure is a medieval Welsh documentary history:

- Pryderi was already known in poetic, grave, and swineherd traditions outside the complete prose cycle.
- Pwyll and Pryderi were associated with Annwn before or alongside the surviving prose synthesis.
- The Four Branches deliberately construct an intergenerational cycle from Pwyll’s encounter with Arawn to Pryderi’s death.
- Pryderi is the only named figure present in all four Branches.
- The grave tradition and pig tradition preserve genuine variants.
- No historical lives, ancient cults, temples, sacrifices, or identified burials can be demonstrated.

What remains reconstruction:

- Pwyll as a pre-Christian god identical with Pluto, Arawn, Manawydan, or a historical ruler;
- Pryderi as simply Mabon/Maponos, Gweir, Peredur, or a historical prince;
- alternative divine paternity;
- a recoverable original “Pryderi saga” in four Irish-style stages;
- an unbroken Pagan cult;
- the placement of either figure in the Ulster or Fenian cycles.

The documentary subject is therefore not two forgotten biographies but a changing Welsh legendary complex: poetic allusion, localized grave lore, courtly prose, learned classification, manuscript canon, antiquarian “pantheon,” fantasy reinvention, and modern heritage.
