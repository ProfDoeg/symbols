# Conall Cernach: Research Dossier

**Traditional class:** mortal heroic warrior of the Ulaid and member of Conchobar mac Nessa’s court; genealogical ancestor in medieval learned tradition. He is not securely attested as a god, demigod, saint, historical king, or recipient of ancient hero-cult.

**Earliest secure attestation:** Conall first becomes visible in texts whose language contains Old Irish material conventionally assigned to roughly the eighth or ninth century, notably *Táin Bó Fraích*, *Fled Bricrenn*, *Scéla Mucce Meic Dá Thó*, and early strata of *Togail Bruidne Dá Derga*. None survives in an eighth-century manuscript. The earliest physical witnesses are chiefly *Lebor na hUidre*, compiled about 1100, and the Book of Leinster, about 1152–61. *Táin Bó Fraích* has specifically been dated linguistically to the first half of the eighth century. There is no contemporary Iron Age inscription, image, grave, shrine, or annal naming Conall. [**Secure early literary material; manuscript transmission distinguished from inferred composition date.**]

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

Conall Cernach is the foremost Ulaid warrior after—or, in several tales, independently of—Cú Chulainn. His characteristic roles are champion, feud-killer, taker of heads, defender of Ulster honour, foster-kinsman and avenger of Cú Chulainn, and eventually an aged exile maintained at Cruachu by his Connacht enemies. Medieval genealogists also made him an ancestor of several Irish peoples. The documentary record is therefore not a biography but a shifting assemblage: relatively early heroic prose, later death-tales and onomastic explanations, political genealogies, Welsh borrowing, Scottish Gaelic ballad attribution, antiquarian comparison, and modern popular mythology.

### Names

- **Old and Middle Irish:** *Conall Cernach*, genitive *Conaill Chernaig*. Modern Irish spelling commonly *Conall Cearnach*. Latin-script manuscripts only; no ancient epigraphic spelling of this hero is known. [**Secure medieval attestation.**]
- **Conall:** generally derived from Common Celtic *Cuno-walos*, approximately “hound/wolf-strong” or “strong as a hound,” with Welsh *Cynwal* as the expected cognate. This is a linguistic etymology of the personal name, not evidence that every historical or legendary Conall is the same figure. John Rhys’s further comparison with the Norse Vali belonged to nineteenth-century solar mythology and is not accepted as proof of common identity. [**Modern linguistic reconstruction; obsolete comparativism distinguished.**]
- **Cernach:** commonly translated “victorious,” “triumphant,” or “having victories.” Medieval *Cóir Anmann* §252 expressly glosses *cern* as *búaid*, “victory,” but also supplies mutually incompatible learned explanations: *cern níad*, “strong man”; Latin *cerno*, “I see,” because a remarkable eye saw equally by night and day; an excrescence or angular swelling on his head as large as a shield-boss, received in Alba during a quarrel over a woman; or something done by a hornet/beetle during his birth. These are medieval name-legends, not independent etymological evidence. [*Cóir Anmann*, composite Middle Irish onomastic compilation preserved in late manuscripts: **learned systematization/later canonical elaboration**.]
- **Conall Claenbráigdech**, “Conall of the crooked neck”: in *Cóir Anmann* §251, his maternal uncle Cet puts the infant under his heel and crushes or bends the neck without breaking the spine; the mother rebukes Cet’s treachery, and the text derives *Conall* by wordplay from *con-fell*, “hound-treachery.” [**Later medieval etymological legend**, not scientific etymology.]
- **“Conall of the hundred trophies/pledges”** appears in the metrical *Dindshenchas* poem on Carn Conaill. [**Later learned poetic epithet.**]
- **“The Victorious”** is Kuno Meyer’s conventional English rendering of *Cernach*. “The Horned” is not a secure medieval translation.

### The Cernunnos question

Anne Ross and some earlier comparativists associated *Cernach* with the Gaulish divine name **Cernunnos**, drawing attention to possible “horn/head” vocabulary, Conall’s unusual bodily description, and the serpent in *Táin Bó Fraích* that harmlessly enters his belt. [**Modern scholarly reconstruction**, especially Ross, *Pagan Celtic Britain*, 1967.]

Against this:

1. No Irish medieval text identifies Conall with Cernunnos.
2. The medieval glossators explain *cernach* principally through victory, strength, sight, a cranial swelling, or wordplay—not through antlers.
3. Cernunnos is an independently attested continental deity; Conall is presented as a mortal Ulaid warrior.
4. The serpent episode says nothing about horns or divine identity.
5. Similarity of sounds is insufficient to establish survival or incarnation.

The equation “Conall Cernach is the Irish Cernunnos” is therefore **unproved modern comparativism**. Its widespread presentation online as ancient Celtic teaching is **modern invention**.

### Welsh form

In the list of Arthur’s retainers in *Culhwch ac Olwen*, the White Book and Red Book readings represented by **Choruil/Corfil Berfach** have been emended to **Conul Bernach** and identified with Conall Cernach. The surrounding list also contains distorted forms of Conchobar, Cú Roí, Fergus, and Lóegaire Búadach. Patrick Sims-Williams treats this as part of the demonstrable Irish material in the Welsh tale. It is a borrowed name and catalogue appearance, not a Welsh narrative cycle about Conall. [*Culhwch ac Olwen*, composition conventionally c. 1100, manuscripts c. 1350 and c. 1400: **regional literary borrowing**.]

No secure occurrence of this Conall has been found in the *Book of Taliesin*. Assertions that Taliesin preserves a developed Welsh Conall cycle are unsupported.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### Documentary limits

The heroic action is conventionally imagined around the beginning of the Common Era, but those dates derive from medieval synchronization and pseudo-history. Conall “belongs to Old Irish literature rather than to history,” as the *Dictionary of Irish Biography* succinctly states. No inscription, coin, contemporary chronicle, or excavated object identifies him.

*Lebor na hUidre* is the earliest extant substantial Irish-language literary manuscript, copied around 1100 at Clonmacnoise. The Book of Leinster dates principally to the mid-twelfth century. Older linguistic forms demonstrate earlier textual ancestry, but do not establish either an eighth-century manuscript of the extant wording or an Iron Age historical Conall.

### Texts named in the brief but not part of Conall’s primary corpus

- *Acallam na Senórach*, *Macgnímartha Finn*, *Tóraigheacht Dhiarmada agus Ghráinne*, Ó hÓgáin’s *Fionn mac Cumhaill*, and Nagy’s *The Wisdom of the Outlaw* concern the Fenian tradition. They illuminate Irish heroic narrative and later reception but do not document an active Fenian career for Conall Cernach. A passing comparison to his blow against Cet is not a narrative interaction. [**Absence finding.**]
- James Macpherson’s *Fingal* (1761), *Temora* (1763), and the Ossian controversy transformed the reception of Finn/Oisín and conflated Irish heroic chronologies, but Macpherson did not transmit an ancient Conall Cernach epic. [**Modern reception context, not primary Conall evidence.**]
- Patrick Ford’s *Ystoria Taliesin* concerns a different Welsh textual complex. It supplies no independent biography of Conall. [**Absence finding.**]

## 3. The story

### Conception and childhood

In *Cóir Anmann* §251, Finnchóem longs for a child. A druid advises her to drink from a well; she swallows a worm and conceives Conall. The infant’s maternal uncle Cet mac Mágach places him beneath his heel, damaging his neck, after which druids predict that he will kill more than half the men of Connacht and never be without a Connachtman’s head at his belt. [**Later medieval birth legend/onomastic systematization.**]

The same passage’s attempt to derive *Conall* from Cet’s “hound-treachery” is punning folklore. It cannot override the comparative Celtic etymology of the name.

Conall is normally son of **Amargen Iarngiunnach**, a Ulaid poet-warrior, and **Finnchóem**, sister of Deichtine; this makes him first cousin and foster-brother of Cú Chulainn. Some genealogical arrangements differ in intermediate ancestry and in Finnchóem’s relation to Cathbad and Conchobar. [**Medieval genealogical tradition; not historical pedigree.**]

### Fráech’s cattle raid

In *Táin Bó Fraích*, Fráech asks Conall to help recover his wife, three sons, and cattle. They track the raiders through Alba and Britain to the Alps or an exotic continental landscape. An Irish woman at the captors’ fort recognizes Conall as “the best warrior among the Ulaid,” embraces him, and explains that the fort’s destruction has been prophesied for him. A serpent guards the enclosure; it enters Conall’s belt and later leaves without either harming the other. Conall and Fráech sack the fort and recover the captives and cattle. [**Secure early literary material, probably 8th-c. textual origin.**]

Nothing in the tale calls the serpent Cernunnos, makes Conall antlered, or declares him divine.

### The champion’s portion

In *Scéla Mucce Meic Dá Thó*, warriors of Ulster and Connacht dispute who deserves the champion’s portion. Cet humiliates successive Ulaid by recalling earlier defeats. Conall arrives and answers Cet’s record of violence. When Cet says that his brother Anlúan would defeat Conall were he present, Conall throws Anlúan’s severed head onto the table. He wins the portion, after which battle erupts. [**Secure early literary material.**]

In *Fled Bricrenn*, Bricriu separately promises the champion’s portion to Conall, Lóegaire, and Cú Chulainn, provoking rivalry among the men and their wives. Tests at Cruachu and with Cú Roí demonstrate Cú Chulainn’s superiority. Conall is formidable but refuses or fails the final beheading ordeal that Cú Chulainn accepts. [**Secure early core with composite development.**]

These tales should not be harmonized into one contest: the first establishes Conall as Ulster’s answer to Connacht; the second subordinates him to Cú Chulainn.

### Connla, Aífe’s son

In *Aided Óenfir Aífe*, Connla arrives in Ireland under prohibitions against revealing his name or refusing combat. Condere cannot persuade him to desist. Conall declares that Ulster’s honour shall not be taken while he lives, confronts the boy, and is thrown down and bound with his own shield straps. Cú Chulainn then fights and kills Connla despite Emer’s recognition of him. Conall neither kills the boy nor prevents the filicide. [**Medieval canonical tradition.**]

### Deirdre and the sons of Uisliu

The compact early *Longes mac nUislenn* does not make Conall a principal rescuer. In the much later *Oidheadh Chloinne Uisneach*, Conall trains the sons, hears the supernatural roar associated with their peril, or participates in expanded vengeance material. Those episodes belong to an early-modern romantic redaction and must not be projected into the Old Irish tale. [**Early-modern accretion.**]

### Dá Derga’s hostel

*Togail Bruidne Dá Derga* places Conall among King Conaire’s defenders. The watchman’s catalogue describes him with asymmetrical red and white cheeks, differently coloured eyes, abundant curled golden hair, a red shield called “Bricriu,” a great spear, and terrifying destructive capacity. He is expected to defend all seven doors and kill three hundred in his first attack.

During the destruction, Conall does kill three hundred and repeatedly drives the raiders away. He leaves only after Conaire has died, with approximately 150 spear wounds in the shield arm and the other arm mangled. His father Amargen initially reproaches him for surviving his lord, but acknowledges that Conall’s wounds prove his fidelity. [**Secure early/composite literary material.**]

This is a decisive counterexample to any simple “biography”: Conall survives a destruction tale whose main king dies, allowing him to migrate into other cycles of vengeance and death.

### Mes Gedra and Conchobar’s brain-ball

In the battle tradition surrounding *Cath Ruis na Ríg* and the death of Conchobar, Conall fights the Leinster king Mes Gedra. Because Mes Gedra has lost a hand, Conall binds one of his own arms and fights one-handed, then kills and beheads him. Mes Gedra’s brain is mixed with lime into a trophy ball. Cet later steals or obtains it and shoots it into Conchobar’s head. [**Medieval linked-tale complex.**]

The surgeons leave the ball embedded; Conchobar lives under restrictions until it bursts or dislodges. Christianized death-tales synchronize his death with the Crucifixion. That synchronism is **medieval Christian historiography**, not evidence that Conall or Conchobar lived in the first century.

### Cú Chulainn’s death and vengeance

In *Aided Con Culainn*/*Brislech Mór Maige Muirthemne*, Conall and Cú Chulainn have sworn that whichever survives will avenge the other before sunset. After Lugaid mac Con Roí kills and decapitates Cú Chulainn, Conall pursues him.

Lugaid has lost a hand; Conall again binds one hand to make the combat equal. When neither prevails, Conall’s horse **Deirg Drúchtach** tears flesh from Lugaid’s side, enabling Conall to behead him. He brings the head back—later retellings specify delivery to Emer. In connected traditions he also kills **Erc**, one of Cú Chulainn’s killers, and carries Erc’s head to Tara. The “Achall” *Dindshenchas* says Erc’s sister Achall dies of grief when Conall brings the head, explaining her mound and place-name. [**12th-c. attested developed tradition; later localization.**]

The sources therefore support the requested thread: Conall is Cú Chulainn’s avenger and carries Lugaid’s head. They do not support modern sanitized accounts that omit the trophy-taking.

### Cet, Bélchú, and the continuing feud

In *Aided Cheit maic Mágach*, Cet returns from an Ulster raid bearing three heads. Conall tracks him through snow; they fight, Cet dies, and Conall collapses badly wounded. Bélchú of Breifne finds him and considers killing both “wolves” responsible for Ireland’s devastation, but instead carries Conall home to recover so that a fair killing may later bring honour.

When Bélchú’s sons plan to murder Conall in bed, Conall overhears them, exchanges sleeping places with Bélchú, and lets the sons kill their own father. Conall then kills and beheads all three. Keating’s seventeenth-century synthesis preserves this plot. [**Later canonical elaboration, then early-modern compilation.**]

This is heroic cunning by the saga’s standards, but it is also deliberate exploitation of intended murder and intrafamilial killing.

### Ailill and Conall’s death

In *Goire Conaill Chernaig*, an aged, debilitated, grief-stricken, and leprous Conall goes to Cruachu after Conchobar and Cú Chulainn are dead. Despite his lifelong blood feud with Connacht, Ailill and Medb support him.

Medb asks Conall to kill Ailill, ostensibly as vengeance because Ailill had killed Fergus mac Róich, and in a context involving Ailill’s sexual infidelity. Conall spears Ailill. He then flees, but Connacht pursuers under a son of Cet overtake him at a ford. Conall kills many, is himself killed, and his severed head is taken. The surviving versions are likely thirteenth-century rewritings of a lost older tale already listed in the Book of Leinster. [**Later medieval death-tale.**]

A local Cavan tradition identifies the ford with Ballyconnell and a nearby cairn with his grave. Other *Dindshenchas* material places a **Carn Conaill** elsewhere and may concern either the hero or genealogically connected namesakes. The locations cannot be reconciled as one historical burial.

## 4. Relations with gods and other heroes

### Genealogical alternatives

**Common heroic genealogy**

- Amargen Iarngiunnach + Finnchóem  
  - Conall Cernach  
    - named sons in different sources include Irél/Irial Glúnmár, Uaithne Find, and others incorporated into dynastic pedigrees.

Finnchóem is commonly sister of Deichtine, making Conall and Cú Chulainn maternal cousins; she is also Cú Chulainn’s foster-mother, making them foster-brothers. [**Medieval heroic genealogy.**]

**Dynastic genealogy**

Rawlinson B 502, the Book of Ballymote, Book of Lecan and related tracts make Conall an ancestor of some or all of:

- Conaille Muirthemne;
- Dál nAraidi;
- Uí Echach Cobo;
- Sogain;
- Loígis or Loíches;
- selected Scottish or Pictish-associated kindreds.

The pedigrees conflict. One account derives Conaille from Conall Anglonnach son of Dedu; another attaches that ancestor to the Ulaid; another makes Conall Casdamail a seventh-generation descendant of Conall Cernach; yet another simply declares the royal line Conall’s descendants. Eoin MacNeill interpreted these contradictions as political adoption of a prestigious heroic eponym by historically unrelated populations. [**Medieval political genealogy; modern historical analysis.**]

The Black Book of Clanranald, in its early-modern Scottish learned milieu, invokes the Clann Rughraighe and Conall’s vengeance to support claims concerning the Red Hand. This documents appropriation of Ulster epic ancestry, not biological descent.

### Documented interactions with named figures

The following inventory covers the identified Conall corpus above. “Interaction” means an explicit meeting, kinship, combat, alliance, command, protection, killing, or shared scene—not merely appearance in the same modern index.

- **Aífe:** Conall confronts her unnamed-at-first son Connla after she sends him to Ireland; *Aided Óenfir Aífe*, medieval recension. [**Canonical elaboration.**]
- **Ailill mac Máta:** receives aged Conall at Cruachu; Conall later kills him at Medb’s prompting; *Goire Conaill Chernaig*, surviving c. 13th-c. rewriting. [**Later medieval.**]
- **Achall:** Conall does not fight her, but his delivery of Erc’s head causes her fatal grief; “Achall,” metrical *Dindshenchas*, 11th–12th c. [**Learned localization.**]
- **Amargen Iarngiunnach:** father; receives the mutilated Conall after Dá Derga and tests his fidelity; *Togail Bruidne Dá Derga*. [**Early/composite.**]
- **Anlúan mac Mágach:** Conall kills and carries his head, producing it in the champion’s-portion dispute; *Scéla Mucce Meic Dá Thó*. [**Secure early literary material.**]
- **Bélchú of Breifne:** rescues Conall intending later honourable combat; Conall’s deception causes Bélchú’s sons to kill him; *Aided Cheit*. [**Later canonical.**]
- **Bélchú’s three sons:** attempt to murder Conall; inadvertently kill their father; Conall kills and beheads them; *Aided Cheit*. [**Later canonical.**]
- **Blaí/Bricriu:** Bricriu separately incites Conall, Cú Chulainn, and Lóegaire to seek the champion’s portion; *Fled Bricrenn*. [**Early core.**]
- **Cathbad:** appears as druid and genealogical relative in Ulaid materials; no secure independent master–pupil episode with Conall in the earliest corpus. [**Genealogical association; direct interaction uncertain.**]
- **Cet mac Mágach:** maternal uncle and lifelong adversary; injures infant Conall in *Cóir Anmann*; disputes honour in *Scéla Mucce*; is finally killed by Conall in *Aided Cheit*. [**Early rivalry plus later birth/death elaboration.**]
- **Conaire Mór:** Conall is his favoured guest and defender at Dá Derga’s hostel but cannot save him; *Togail Bruidne Dá Derga*. [**Early/composite.**]
- **Conchobar mac Nessa:** Conall serves his court and campaigns; later traditions make Conchobar a foster-brother or close kinsman; Conall’s Mes Gedra trophy becomes the weapon that kills Conchobar; numerous Ulster tales. [**Canonical heroic relationship.**]
- **Condere mac Echach:** after Condere fails to turn Connla away, Conall accepts the challenge; *Aided Óenfir Aífe*. [**Canonical elaboration.**]
- **Connla:** Conall challenges him for Ulster’s honour, is defeated, thrown, and bound; *Aided Óenfir Aífe*. [**Canonical elaboration.**]
- **Cormac Con Loinges:** named with Conall in Ulaid and Glenmasan material; shared allegiance rather than a major two-person episode. [**Medieval shared-scene evidence.**]
- **Cú Chulainn:** cousin/foster-brother, rival in *Fled Bricrenn*, fellow Ulaid champion and sworn mutual avenger; Conall avenges his death by killing Lugaid and Erc. [**Central early and canonical relationship.**]
- **Cú Roí mac Dáiri:** judges or engineers the beheading test in which Conall fails and Cú Chulainn succeeds; *Fled Bricrenn*. [**Early core.**]
- **Deirg Drúchtach:** Conall’s horse bites Lugaid and makes victory possible; *Aided Con Culainn*. The horse is a named heroic animal, not securely a deity. [**Canonical elaboration.**]
- **Erc:** Conall kills and beheads him in vengeance for Cú Chulainn; *Aided Con Culainn* complex and “Achall” *Dindshenchas*. [**Canonical/learned localization.**]
- **Emer:** in later versions Conall brings her Lugaid’s head; she also shares the “Lay of the Heads” dialogue attributed to Conall in Scottish tradition. [**Later medieval/Scottish reception.**]
- **Fergus mac Róich:** fellow Ulaid hero turned Connacht exile; Conall later kills Ailill partly to avenge Fergus; *Goire Conaill Chernaig*. In *Visio Tnugdali*, Fergus and Conall are paired in punishment. [**Later medieval.**]
- **Finnchóem:** mother; miraculous conception and naming appear in *Cóir Anmann*; also Cú Chulainn’s foster-mother. [**Learned birth legend.**]
- **Fráech:** Conall accompanies him abroad, sacks the fort, and recovers his family and cattle; *Táin Bó Fraích*. [**Secure early literary material.**]
- **Fráech’s wife and three sons:** rescued by Conall and Fráech; *Táin Bó Fraích*. [**Secure early literary material.**]
- **Lóegaire Búadach:** rival claimant to the champion’s portion; *Fled Bricrenn*. [**Early core.**]
- **Lugaid mac Con Roí:** killer of Cú Chulainn; Conall fights him with one hand bound, aided by his horse, kills and beheads him; *Aided Con Culainn*. [**Canonical elaboration.**]
- **Mac Cécht:** both defend Conaire in Dá Derga’s hostel, Mac Cécht seeking water while Conall guards the king; *Togail Bruidne Dá Derga*. [**Early/composite.**]
- **Medb:** hosts aged Conall, uses him to control and finally kill Ailill; *Goire Conaill Chernaig*. She is also an adjudicating queen in *Fled Bricrenn*. [**Early role plus later death-tale.**]
- **Mes Gedra:** Conall fights him one-handed, kills him, and takes his head/brain; battle and death-tale complex. [**Medieval canonical.**]
- **Náisi and the sons of Uisliu:** no major direct relationship in the compact early exile tale; later *Oidheadh Chloinne Uisneach* makes Conall their instructor or avenging associate. [**Early-modern accretion.**]
- **Serpent of the foreign fort:** enters and leaves Conall’s belt without hostility; *Táin Bó Fraích*. It is unnamed and never identified as Cernunnos. [**Secure early literary material; modern divine equation rejected.**]

### Divine beings

No securely transmitted tale makes Conall the child, incarnation, lover, opponent, or chosen protégé of a named Irish god. He appears among mortal heroes. Assertions that he is Cernunnos, Lugh, a solar god, or a divine horned serpent-master are **modern reconstructions or inventions**, not documented medieval interactions.

## 5. Cult, tomb, and ritual

### Cult

No securely identified shrine, *heroon*, altar, votive deposit, sacrifice, festival, priesthood, oracle, or cult-title of Conall Cernach is known. Medieval texts celebrate and localize him, but literary fame and genealogical descent are not themselves cult.

There are no ancient images securely identified as Conall. Modern illustrations based on his description in *Togail Bruidne Dá Derga* are reception, not iconographic witnesses from pagan Ireland.

### Tomb claims

1. **Carn Conaill:** A metrical *Dindshenchas* poem says that “Conall was buried with his father” beneath the cairn that consequently bore his name. The poem mixes heroic and genealogical landscape lore and does not provide archaeological authentication. [**Medieval learned localization.**]
2. **Ballyheady/Ballyconnell, County Cavan:** local tradition places Conall’s death at a ford near Ballyconnell and his burial in a cairn at Ballyheady. The place-name *Béal Átha Conaill* is attested in late medieval annalistic form, but “Conall” alone does not prove identification with the hero. Archaeology identified prehistoric cairn material and a Middle Bronze Age cist, chronologically incapable of verifying the literary warrior. The Schools’ Collection recorded twentieth-century belief that a found skeleton was Conall’s. [**Folklore attached to prehistoric archaeology.**]
3. **Other Conall place-names:** Ireland contains many places and people named Conall. Attribution to Conall Cernach requires an explicit text; name resemblance alone is insufficient.

There is therefore **tomb tradition but no authenticated tomb and no demonstrated ancient cult**.

## 6. Historical evolution

### c. 700–900: early literary formation

Old Irish tales construct Conall as an autonomous champion: Fráech’s indispensable ally, Cet’s superior at the feast, a rival whom Cú Chulainn must surpass, and an extraordinary defender at Dá Derga. His hostility to Connacht, head-taking, loyalty, bodily strangeness, and concern with honour are already central. [**Secure early literary layer, not contemporary history.**]

### c. 1000–1200: compilation and canonical integration

The heroic corpus is copied into LU and the Book of Leinster. Redactors coordinate Conall with Cú Chulainn’s biography, Conchobar’s court, the Táin, the sons of Uisliu, and Conaire’s destruction. The death of Cú Chulainn generates Conall’s great avenging role. *Dindshenchas* poets embed him in named Irish landscapes.

### c. 1100–1300: regional transfer and death-tales

*Culhwch ac Olwen* imports a distorted form of his name into Arthur’s court. Irish death-tales complete heroic biographies: Conall kills Cet, Bélchú, Ailill, Lugaid, and Erc, then dies at Connacht hands. *Goire Conaill Chernaig* also converts the invincible youth into a disabled, leprous dependent, introducing a deliberately bitter ending.

Christian moralization is visible when heroic killers appear in hell traditions or when the Ulster chronology is synchronized with Christ.

### c. 1200–1600: genealogical and Scottish appropriation

Learned genealogists attach Conall to politically useful populations. Conflicting pedigrees reveal construction rather than a stable inherited family tree. The Book of the Dean of Lismore attributes a heroic lay to him, while the Black Book of Clanranald invokes his vengeance within learned Scottish claims about Ulster ancestry and the Red Hand.

### c. 1600–1800: compilation, romance, and Ossianic disturbance

Keating synthesizes death-tale material. Early-modern versions of Deirdre’s story enlarge Conall’s role. Scottish Gaelic ballad collections preserve speech-poems and heroic catalogues.

Macpherson’s Ossianic publications do not preserve a Conall epic but rearrange the popular understanding of Gaelic antiquity, merge heroic generations, and privilege Oisín as supposed ancient author. Conall’s attribution as an “author” in Scottish manuscript tradition arose similarly from mistaking a speaking character for the historical composer.

### c. 1800–1950: editions, nationalism, and comparative mythology

Windisch, Stokes, Meyer, Thurneysen, Gwynn and others publish texts that had previously been accessible chiefly in manuscripts. Conall becomes usable in cultural-national revival as an emblem of Ulster heroic strength.

Nineteenth-century solar mythology identified him as a personified sun and compared him with Norse Vali. Early twentieth-century scholarship often projected the sagas directly onto an Irish Iron Age. Margaret Dobbs’s 1920 study gathered central-Irish place traditions but confidently assigned the heroic setting to the early Iron Age in a way later literary criticism treats more cautiously.

### 1950–present: literary-historical reassessment

Later scholarship emphasizes redaction, manuscript context, political genealogy, and medieval literary composition. Arguments that saga society transparently preserves Iron Age reality have been challenged: narrative forms and ideological concerns often fit the learned culture of first-millennium and high-medieval Ireland.

Thomas Kinsella’s *The Táin* (1969) created a powerful modern composite English reading, incorporating selected *remscéla*. Cecile O’Rahilly’s editions and translations of Recension I (1976) and the Book of Leinster recension (1967) preserve the distinction between manuscript versions more clearly. Conall’s limited role in the central raid must not be inflated from his larger satellite corpus.

## 7. Modern reception and reinvention

### Literature and translation

- Scholarly translations by Stokes, Meyer, Thurneysen, O’Rahilly, Kinsella, Gantz and others made Conall available outside specialist manuscript study. [**Modern reception.**]
- Yeats’s *On Baile’s Strand* (1904) and “Cuchulain’s Fight with the Sea” adapt the Connla tragedy. Conall is not their principal protagonist, but the source tale’s sequence includes his earlier defeat by Connla. [**Modern literary adaptation.**]
- Modern myth dictionaries, retellings, fantasy fiction, role-playing material, comics, and online games frequently present him as Cú Chulainn’s lieutenant, “the Irish Hercules,” or a horned-god survival. Such formulations should be evaluated individually; none is an ancient title.

### Political and genealogical use

- Medieval claims of descent from Conall are political identity-making, especially among Conaille and Cruithin/Pictish-associated populations. [**Medieval political genealogy.**]
- Scottish learned material connected him with Clann Rughraighe and Red Hand traditions. [**Early-modern appropriation.**]
- The broad use of Ulster-cycle heroes in Irish revivalism and regional heritage does not mean every modern Ulster emblem specifically represents Conall. Claims that the Ulster Red Hand arose when Conall placed a bloody hand on a white banner lack support in the medieval Conall corpus and appear to be **modern invention**.
- No substantial, well-documented modern extremist cult centered on Conall was found. That negative finding should not be converted into a claim that no individual has ever used his image politically.

### Psychological and comparativist readings

- **Solar mythology:** John Rhys interpreted Conall as a sun hero and paralleled his vengeance with Norse myth. [**Late-19th-c. theory; now methodologically obsolete.**]
- **Cernunnos survival:** Anne Ross proposed an affinity based on name and imagery. [**20th-c. comparativist hypothesis, unproved.**]
- **Heroic-biography models:** Rank, Raglan and Campbell were not working from a securely unified “life of Conall.” Applying their templates requires assembling late birth, deed, and death traditions that medieval witnesses never present as one continuous biography. [**Modern interpretive construction.**]
- **Dumézilian/Indo-European comparison:** potentially useful for structures of sovereignty and warfare, but no trifunctional reading establishes Conall’s historicity or divinity.
- **Oral-traditional interpretation:** older scholarship often treated stylistic archaism as direct survival from the Iron Age. Later critics stress written composition, redaction, and monastery-based transmission. Oral antecedents remain plausible but cannot be dated simply from the fictional setting.

### Common modern inventions

- “Conall was the Irish incarnation of Cernunnos.” — **Not attested.**
- “Archaeologists found Conall’s body at Ballyheady.” — **Folkloric identification of prehistoric remains, not an archaeological conclusion.**
- Exact birth and death years around the first century CE. — **Pseudo-historical calculation.**
- A continuous Welsh Conall saga in the *Mabinogion* or *Book of Taliesin*. — **Unsupported; only a likely borrowed catalogue-name in *Culhwch*.**
- Membership in Finn’s Fianna or participation in the pursuit of Diarmuid and Gráinne. — **Conflation with other figures named Conall/Conán.**
- Ancient worship as a horned god. — **No cult evidence.**

## 8. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Historicity

No source contemporaneous with an alleged historical Conall exists. The earliest recoverable evidence is literary and several centuries later. The most defensible description is “legendary hero of the Ulaid.” Attempts to reconstruct an individual warrior behind him are hypotheses without corroborating inscriptional, annalistic, or archaeological evidence.

### Dating and oral tradition

The language of *Táin Bó Fraích* and other tales supports early medieval textual ancestry. It does not date every episode equally, and an early linguistic layer is not proof of unchanged oral transmission from pre-Christian Ireland. J. P. Mallory and other critics have emphasized that the Ulster Cycle is better studied as evolving medieval literature than mined uncritically for Iron Age ethnography.

### Was Conall once the chief Ulaid hero?

His independence in *Táin Bó Fraích* and *Scéla Mucce Meic Dá Thó*, and the absence or comparative weakness of Cú Chulainn in some Conall-centred tales, have prompted the reconstruction that Conall may once have occupied a more central heroic position before Cú Chulainn’s literary ascendancy. This is a plausible history-of-tradition model, not a datum stated by the manuscripts. *Fled Bricrenn* visibly stages the hierarchy by making Conall formidable but inferior to Cú Chulainn.

### Genealogical history

MacNeill demonstrated that pedigrees attaching several populations to Conall contradict one another. Their evidentiary value lies in medieval political self-definition, not Bronze or Iron Age descent. The Conaille’s similar name made Conall especially attractive as an eponym.

### Cernunnos

The case rests on phonetic resemblance, uncertain semantics, and selected imagery. The absence of a medieval identification, Conall’s mortal classification, and the multiplicity of native explanations weigh against treating the theory as established. It remains a comparativist proposal useful mainly as a case study in modern reconstruction.

### The death tradition

The Book of Leinster already lists Conall’s death among the canonical *aideda*, but the extant narrative survives only in two much later manuscripts and was rewritten from older material. Consequently:

- the existence of a medieval Conall death-tale is secure;
- its lost early wording is unrecoverable;
- Ballyconnell localization is later place tradition;
- no physical grave can be derived from the narrative.

### State of the evidence

Important witnesses are damaged, composite, or late. LU contains work by several hands and later interpolators; the Book of Leinster reorganizes inherited material; *Fled Bricrenn* ends defectively in LU; the death of Cú Chulainn is preserved in a twelfth-century form and much later copies; Conall’s own death survives in two divergent witnesses. Critical editions necessarily reconstruct relationships among manuscripts and should not be mistaken for diplomatic reproductions of a single ancient original.

## 10. Sources and assessment

### Primary texts and editions

The documentary foundation consists principally of:

- *Táin Bó Fraích*, especially the Book of Leinster and related manuscript traditions.
- *Scéla Mucce Meic Dá Thó*, ed. Rudolf Thurneysen.
- *Fled Bricrenn*, LU, Egerton 93, Leiden and Edinburgh witnesses.
- *Togail Bruidne Dá Derga*, especially Eleanor Knott’s and related recension-based editions.
- *Táin Bó Cúailnge*, ed./trans. Cecile O’Rahilly, Recension I and Book of Leinster recension; Thomas Kinsella’s translation is a deliberately composite modern literary version.
- *Aided Óenfir Aífe*.
- *Aided Con Culainn*/*Brislech Mór Maige Muirthemne*.
- *Goire Conaill Chernaig i Crúachain ocus Aided Ailella ocus Conaill Chernaig*, ed./trans. Kuno Meyer.
- *Aided Cheit maic Mágach* and Keating’s later retelling.
- *Cóir Anmann* §§251–53.
- The metrical *Dindshenchas*, particularly “Achall” and “Carn Conaill.”
- *Culhwch ac Olwen* for the Welsh borrowed name.
- Medieval Irish genealogical tracts and early-modern Scottish compilations.

### Modern scholarship

MacNeill’s analysis remains fundamental for recognizing constructed, contradictory population pedigrees. Ross is the datable source for the influential modern Cernunnos comparison. Sims-Williams provides the strongest context for the Irish names in *Culhwch*. Modern manuscript and literary criticism supersedes the older assumption that the tales straightforwardly report Iron Age history. Ó hÓgáin, MacKillop, Nagy and Ford are valuable comparative or reference works, but their discussions must not be used to import Fenian or Taliesin material into Conall’s corpus without a textual occurrence.

The firmest conclusions are therefore negative as well as positive: Conall is richly documented as a medieval literary hero, political ancestor, landscape figure, and object of later comparative interpretation; he is not securely documented as a historical person, ancient god, cult-recipient, or occupant of an archaeologically identified grave.
